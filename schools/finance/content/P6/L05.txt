@article
A compliance officer once told me, "My job is to make it easy to do the right thing and hard to do the wrong thing." That is a good description of a **compliance program**: the policies, people and checks through which a firm follows the law and its own ethics. In this lesson you learn who writes the rules and what a program has to contain.

### The regulators

| Body | Role |
|---|---|
| SEC (Securities and Exchange Commission) | Federal regulator of securities markets, public-company disclosure, investment advisers and investment companies |
| FINRA | Self-regulatory organization for broker-dealers; writes and enforces rules, runs licensing exams, and is overseen by the SEC |
| State securities regulators (and NASAA, their association) | Register smaller advisers and individual representatives and enforce state securities laws; NASAA develops the Series 63, 65 and 66 exams, which FINRA administers |
| CFTC and NFA | Regulate futures, swaps and other derivatives; the NFA is the industry self-regulator |
| Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC | Bank supervision and deposit insurance |
| CFPB | Consumer financial protection; its scope and activity have been changing since 2025, so check current developments |

Outside the US the roles are similar: a national securities regulator, a self-regulator or exchange rules, and a code of conduct. Many firms also operate under several regimes at once.

### The laws behind the rules

The **Securities Act of 1933** governs the offering of new securities. The **Securities Exchange Act of 1934** regulates trading, creates the SEC and contains the main anti-fraud rule used against insider trading (Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5). The **Investment Company Act of 1940** regulates mutual funds, and the **Investment Advisers Act of 1940** regulates advisers, who owe clients a **fiduciary duty** (care and loyalty, as the SEC has interpreted it). The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002** tightened public-company reporting and governance, and the **Dodd-Frank Act of 2010** added oversight of systemic risk, derivatives and a whistleblower program, which can pay awards of typically 10% to 30% of sanctions above \$1 million. Broker-dealers recommending to retail customers follow **Regulation Best Interest** and deliver **Form CRS**.

### Elements of an adviser's compliance program

Under the SEC's compliance rule for advisers (Rule 206(4)-7), a registered adviser must:

1. Adopt **written policies and procedures** reasonably designed to prevent violations of the Advisers Act.
2. **Review them at least annually** for adequacy and effectiveness.
3. Designate a **chief compliance officer** (CCO) who is competent and knowledgeable and has authority.

The policies normally cover portfolio management and allocation, trading and best execution, a **code of ethics** and personal trading (Week 3), insider-trading controls, marketing and performance advertising (the SEC's marketing rule), custody of client assets, privacy and cybersecurity, anti-money-laundering where it applies (the extension of AML program rules to advisers has had its effective date postponed, so check current status), business continuity, and **books and records**. Advisers also file and update **Form ADV** at least annually (within 90 days of fiscal year-end), deliver the Part 2A brochure to clients and, for retail clients, Form CRS.

Two quick tests show how this works in practice.

- **Registration.** An adviser with roughly \$100 million or more of **regulatory assets under management (RAUM)** generally registers with the SEC; smaller advisers generally register with their state (there is a buffer around the threshold; confirm current rules). RAUM counts securities portfolios for which the adviser gives **continuous and regular supervisory or management services**, whether or not it has discretion.
- **Retention.** Under the Advisers Act books-and-records rule (Rule 204-2) most records are kept for **five years from the end of the fiscal year in which the last entry was made**, the first two years in an appropriate office of the adviser. Broker-dealers follow different periods.

### Material nonpublic information (MNPI)

Information is **material** if a reasonable investor would likely consider it important in making an investment decision, or if it would likely move the price. It is **nonpublic** if it has not been disseminated to the marketplace. Standard II(A) says you may not act or cause others to act on MNPI, and in the United States, trading on MNPI obtained or used in breach of a duty is illegal under the anti-fraud rules, as is **tipping**. Under **Regulation FD** public companies may not selectively disclose material information to analysts or investors; they must release it broadly.

The **mosaic theory** says analysts may combine public information with immaterial nonpublic pieces to reach a conclusion that is itself not material to others. A mosaic is legitimate research; a single tile of MNPI is not.

If you receive possible MNPI: **stop trading** in that security, tell compliance, let compliance place it on a restricted or watch list, and do not pass it on. Firms build **information barriers** ("firewalls") between units that hold inside information (investment banking, say) and units that trade or publish research.

@example
title: SEC or state registration
problem: Willow Bend Advisory manages \$74.0 million of portfolios on a discretionary basis and gives continuous supervisory services to another \$38.5 million of client accounts where the client approves each trade. It also mails an occasional newsletter to prospects. Using a general threshold of \$100 million, where does it register?
step: RAUM includes securities portfolios for which it gives continuous and regular supervisory or management services, discretionary or not: $74.0 + 38.5 = \$112.5$ million. The newsletter is not an advisory account and is not counted.
step: Compare to the threshold: \$112.5 million is above \$100 million (and above the top of the buffer band, about \$110 million).
step: Conclusion: it generally registers with the SEC, files Form ADV and adopts a compliance program under Rule 206(4)-7. It should confirm current rules and any state notice filings.
answer: RAUM \$112.5 million; generally SEC registration.

@example
title: A tip from a golf course
problem: Analyst Dani Moreau plays golf with a friend who is an executive at Quillfeather Biotech. The friend says, "Keep this between us: our drug trial failed, and we will announce it Friday." Dani's fund owns the stock. What should Dani do?
step: Is it material? A failed drug trial for a biotech company is very likely important to investors and to the price. Is it nonpublic? It will not be announced until Friday.
step: Duties. Standard II(A) prohibits acting or causing others to act on MNPI, and a sale would be trading on inside information; the friend's disclosure may also breach a duty.
step: Action. Dani should not sell the shares, tell the portfolio manager or tip anyone; she should report the situation to compliance, which will restrict the security and review what to do.
step: Afterward, trading can resume once the information is public.
answer: Do not trade or tip; report to compliance so that the stock is restricted (Standard II(A)).

@mistakes
- Thinking a compliance program is only a binder of policies; it also needs a CCO, annual review, training and testing.
- Counting only discretionary accounts in regulatory assets under management; non-discretionary accounts with continuous and regular supervisory services also count.
- Assuming information passed in confidence "off the record" is fine to trade on if you did not ask for it; receiving MNPI is enough to trigger the duty to stop.
- Applying broker-dealer retention periods to adviser records, or the reverse; check which rule applies.
