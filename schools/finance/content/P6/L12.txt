@article
When I started, the most advanced tool on the desk was a spreadsheet. Today a first-year analyst can use code to read a thousand filings overnight, and tools that write text and code on request. The tools change; the duties do not. This final lesson shows how the Standards apply to technology and how to plan a career in which the tools keep moving.

### Where technology is used

- **Research.** Natural-language processing of filings and call transcripts, data pipelines, summaries, and code written with AI assistance.
- **Portfolio construction and trading.** Quantitative signals, optimization, algorithmic execution, rebalancing and tax-loss harvesting at scale.
- **Client service.** Chat assistants, personalization, automated reporting and onboarding checks.
- **Compliance and risk.** Surveillance of communications and trades, screening, anomaly detection and reporting.
- **Markets and infrastructure.** Faster settlement (US stock trades settle T+1), digital assets and tokenization, and a federal framework for payment stablecoins, the GENIUS Act of July 18, 2025.

### The Standards still apply

| Use | Standard | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Relying on a model or AI-generated analysis | V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis | Validate: check sources, test on data the model has not seen, know its limits; never publish an unverified figure |
| Telling clients how a tool is used | V(B) Communication | Describe the role and limitations in plain language; separate fact from the tool's output |
| Pasting client or firm data into an outside tool | III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality | Use approved tools under contract with confidentiality protections; do not enter client identities or nonpublic information into unapproved tools |
| Claims about your technology | I(C) Misrepresentation | Do not exaggerate what the system does; regulators have brought enforcement actions against advisers for misleading claims about AI use ("AI washing") |
| Using generated text or code | I(C) | Attribute and check, since copied or fabricated content is your responsibility |
| Overseeing staff using tools | IV(C) Supervisors | Write a usage policy, train, monitor and audit |
| Keeping the audit trail | V(C) Record Retention | Retain prompts, versions, data and outputs that support a recommendation, with electronic communications rules |

### Model risk

A model is a simplified description of reality that can be wrong. Supervisory guidance on model risk management (US bank regulators have long described it) asks firms to maintain an inventory of models, validate them independently, document assumptions and limits, monitor performance and have a human accountable for the result. Typical failures are **overfitting** (a model that fits history and fails on new data), **data snooping** (testing many ideas and reporting the best), **bias** in training data, **hallucinated** citations or numbers from generative tools, **concept drift** (relationships that change), and **opaque** models that cannot be explained to a client or a regulator.

### Measuring a screening tool

Suppose a surveillance model flags communications for review. Let TP be true problems it flags, FP false alarms, and FN real problems it misses.

$$\text{precision} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{recall} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}.$$

Precision says how much of the flagged work is real; recall says how much of the real problems the model finds. Increasing one often lowers the other, so a compliance team chooses a balance.

### The false-discovery trap

If you test $n$ independent ideas that have no real effect, each at a 5% significance level, the probability that at least one looks "significant" is $1 - 0.95^{n}$. With $n = 20$ it is 64.2%. This is why analysts who test many signals must report how many they tried and test the result on new data.

### Regulation and the future

Rules are moving. The SEC proposed a rule on conflicts from predictive data analytics in 2023 and later withdrew it, while it continues to use existing anti-fraud and disclosure rules for AI. Customer-information safeguards in Regulation S-P were amended in 2024 to require incident response programs, with phased compliance dates. The European Union's AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force in 2024 with obligations that phase in over several years. Because details change, check current sources before relying on any summary.

What will be scarce for people? Tools do routine tasks well. What remains valuable is **judgment under uncertainty**, understanding what is not in the data, client trust, communication, and ethics. Learn the tools well enough to supervise them, and keep the skill to check their work by hand.

### A five-year plan

Write a plan with a theme per year (for example, year 1 foundations and licenses, year 2 specialization and the Level II exam, year 3 ownership of a coverage area, year 4 mentoring and the charter, year 5 broader responsibility), with measurable goals, skills, credentials, relationships and checkpoints. Revisit it every six months, because roles and tools will change.

@example
title: Evaluating a surveillance model
problem: A compliance team's model flags 200 emails in a month for review; 14 of them contain real policy breaches. A separate sample review finds that there were 20 real breaches in the month. Compute precision and recall, and interpret them.
step: True positives: 14. False positives: $200 - 14 = 186$. False negatives: $20 - 14 = 6$.
step: Precision: $14/200 = 7.0\%$. Only 7 of 100 flagged emails are real breaches, so reviewers spend most of their time on false alarms.
step: Recall: $14/20 = 70.0\%$. The model catches 70% of real breaches and misses 30%.
step: Interpretation: the tool saves reading but still misses real breaches, so it supplements rather than replaces human review (Standard IV(C)), and its thresholds should be tuned and documented.
answer: Precision 7.0%; recall 70.0%.

@example
title: Disclosing the use of AI in client reports
problem: A firm uses a generative tool to draft first versions of client commentary, which analysts edit. An analyst proposes saying nothing about it and pasting client holdings into a public chatbot for speed. What should the firm do?
step: Confidentiality. Pasting client holdings into a public tool risks Standard III(E); use only approved tools with contractual protections.
step: Diligence. The analyst remains responsible for each fact and number (V(A)), so every figure is checked against source data.
step: Communication and honesty. The firm should accurately describe how it uses the tool where that is material to clients (V(B), I(C)) and not claim more automation or intelligence than it has.
step: Supervision and records. A usage policy, training, review and retention of drafts and sources (IV(C), V(C)).
answer: Use approved tools only, verify every output, describe use accurately, supervise and keep records (III(E), V(A), V(B), I(C), IV(C), V(C)).

@mistakes
- Treating an AI tool's answer as a reasonable basis without checking sources; a fabricated number is still your error (Standard V(A)).
- Entering client data into tools that the firm has not approved.
- Reporting the best of many tested signals without saying how many were tried.
- Overstating what a product's AI does in marketing; this is a misrepresentation (Standard I(C)).

@practice num
prompt: A researcher tests 20 independent trading signals that have no real predictive power. Each test uses a 5% significance level. What is the probability that at least one of the 20 looks statistically significant by chance? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 64.2
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (1 - 0.95 ** 20) * 100
hint: Find the probability that none of the 20 tests is significant, then subtract from 1.
solution: The chance that a single test is not significant is 0.95, so all 20 are not significant with probability $0.95^{20} = 0.3585$. The chance of at least one false discovery is $1 - 0.3585 = 64.2\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: A start-up that your firm is considering as a vendor says its product is "fully AI-driven, with no human involvement" in constructing portfolios. In your due diligence, you find that analysts manually override many of its trades. In four to six sentences, explain the Standards issues and what the firm should require before using or marketing this product.
hint: Consider misrepresentation, diligence on a third-party tool, and what clients are told.
solution: The claim is misleading, which is a problem under Standard I(C) for the vendor and, if the firm repeats it to clients, for the firm too; regulators have acted against exaggerated AI claims. Standard V(A) requires the firm to carry out diligence on a third-party model before relying on it: documentation of methods and data, validation on new data, performance and failure analysis, and understanding of the human overrides. Standard V(B) requires the firm to describe accurately to clients how the product is used and its limits. The firm should require corrections to the vendor's claims, written confirmation of controls and data protection (III(E)), and a monitoring plan before it uses or markets the product.
rubric: Identifies the misleading claim as a misrepresentation issue (Standard I(C)).
rubric: Requires diligence on the third-party tool: documentation, validation and review of human overrides (Standard V(A)).
rubric: Says the firm must describe the tool accurately to clients and requires controls and monitoring.
