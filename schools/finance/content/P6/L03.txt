@article
Every firm has conflicts. A manager is paid more when assets grow, a broker earns more when clients trade, an analyst may own a stock she covers. The profession does not pretend conflicts can be removed; it asks you to **disclose** them, **manage** them and never let them decide a client's outcome. The small firms I have worked with fail here not because they are dishonest but because they never wrote the rules down.

### Standard VI(A): Disclosure of Conflicts

You must make full and fair disclosure of all matters that could reasonably impair your independence and objectivity or interfere with your duties to clients, prospects and your employer. The disclosure has to be **prominent, in plain language and specific enough** that a reader understands the conflict. Typical items are ownership of stock you recommend, compensation tied to a product, a board seat at a company you cover, a relationship between your employer and an issuer, or a family member working at a company you analyze. Disclosure to clients is for them to weigh your advice; disclosure to your employer lets it manage the risk. Disclosure is necessary but not sufficient: some conflicts must be avoided altogether.

### Standard VI(B): Priority of Transactions

Investment transactions for **clients and your employer come before** transactions in which you have a personal beneficial interest. The order is clients first, employer second, you last. Three behaviors are forbidden in practice:

- **Front running**: trading for yourself (or someone you favor) ahead of a client order you know is coming, to profit from the price move the order causes.
- **Trading on the back of recommendations**: buying before you release a buy recommendation to clients, or selling ahead of a sell.
- **Using client trades to benefit yourself** in any other way, such as buying an initial public offering (IPO) allocation that should have gone to clients.

The profit from front running is the price gap you capture:

$$\text{gain} = \text{shares} \times (P_{\text{sell}} - P_{\text{buy}}),$$

and every cent of it is borne by clients, who pay a higher price because the order was preceded by yours.

### Standard VI(C): Referral Fees

If you pay or receive anything of value for referring a client or prospect, you must disclose it to your employer, to the client or prospect, and to anyone else who needs to know **before** the service is provided, with the nature of the arrangement and its value. This lets the client judge whether the referral is objective.

### The personal-trading policy

Firms put these standards into a written policy. A good one has these parts:

| Element | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Definition of covered persons and accounts | Includes the employee, household members and accounts the employee controls or has a beneficial interest in |
| Pre-clearance | The compliance officer approves a trade before it is placed, and approval expires quickly |
| Restricted and watch lists | Blocks trading in securities where the firm has client orders, research in progress or inside information |
| Blackout and holding periods | No trading around client trades or a research release; a minimum holding period discourages short-term trading |
| IPO and private-placement approval | Prior written approval for limited offerings and new issues |
| Reporting | Holdings and transactions are reported on a schedule |
| Monitoring and sanctions | Compliance reviews reports against client trades, and breaches have stated consequences |

In the United States, an investment adviser's **code of ethics** under the Investment Advisers Act (Rule 204A-1) must require its "access persons" to report holdings when they join and annually, and to report transactions quarterly, and must require pre-approval of IPOs and limited offerings. The firm's compliance officer reviews the reports. A small firm can meet the principle with a few pages and one accountable person.

@example
title: Spotting and pricing front running
problem: A trader at Linden Row Asset Management learns that clients are about to buy 250,000 shares of Calloway Marine, a thinly traded stock priced near \$27.40. Before entering the client order, he buys 3,000 shares in his own account at \$27.40. The client purchases push the price to \$28.15, where he sells his shares. What did he gain, which Standard did he violate, and what should the firm do?
step: The gain is $3{,}000 \times (28.15 - 27.40) = 3{,}000 \times 0.75 = \$2{,}250$ (ignoring commissions).
step: He placed his own interest ahead of the clients' orders and profited from information about their trades, a breach of Standard VI(B), Priority of Transactions. It also breaches III(A), Loyalty, Prudence, and Care.
step: The firm should have its compliance officer investigate, remove the gain, apply the sanctions in its policy, and review whether the pre-clearance and blackout rules were enforced.
answer: \$2,250; Standard VI(B) (and III(A)); investigate, disgorge and sanction.

@example
title: Disclosing a holding
problem: An analyst's published note recommends a buy on Fenwick Dairy. She owns 1% of its outstanding shares through a family trust and her spouse is an employee of its supplier. She plans to say nothing because "it is public record". What should she do?
step: Duties. Standard VI(A) requires disclosure of matters that could reasonably impair independence or objectivity, to clients, prospects and the employer.
step: Facts that matter. Her ownership and her spouse's employment are specific, material to how a reader would weigh the buy rating.
step: Action. Tell her employer and compliance and disclose both facts prominently in the note in plain language. The employer may also decide that she should not cover the company.
answer: Disclose both interests in the note and to her employer under Standard VI(A); the employer may reassign coverage.

@mistakes
- Believing that disclosure alone makes a conflict acceptable. Some conflicts, such as trading ahead of clients, cannot be cured by disclosure.
- Burying a conflict in a long footnote or using vague wording ("may have interests"); the standard asks for prominent, specific, plain-language disclosure.
- Leaving out household members' accounts from a personal-trading policy.
- Disclosing a referral fee only after the client has hired the manager; VI(C) asks for disclosure before services are provided.

@practice self
prompt: A small advisory firm of eight people has no personal-trading rules. Draft a one-page personal-trading policy for it (about 8 to 10 numbered provisions) that covers who and which accounts are covered, pre-clearance, restricted securities, blackout around client trades and research, holding period, IPOs and private placements, reporting, monitoring and sanctions.
hint: Use the table of policy elements in the lesson as your outline, and make each provision something compliance can actually check.
solution: 1. Covered persons: all employees and their household members; covered accounts: any in which they have a beneficial interest or discretion. 2. Pre-clearance: every trade in a covered security needs written approval from the chief compliance officer (CCO) before it is placed; approval is good for one trading day. 3. Restricted list: no trading in a security on the restricted list (client orders pending, research in progress, or inside information). 4. Blackout: no personal trades in a security on a day when the firm has an unexecuted client order in it, or for three trading days before and after a research rating change. 5. Holding period: positions held at least 30 days, with exceptions approved by the CCO. 6. IPOs and private placements: not permitted without prior written approval. 7. Reporting: holdings within 10 days of joining and annually; transactions quarterly, with brokerage statements sent to the CCO. 8. Monitoring: the CCO compares personal trades with client and research activity, and another partner reviews the CCO's own trades. 9. Sanctions: warnings, disgorgement of profits and dismissal for repeated or serious breaches. 10. Annual written certification by each employee and review of the policy.
rubric: Defines covered persons and accounts to include household members and accounts with beneficial interest.
rubric: Includes pre-clearance and restricted-list or blackout provisions that put clients and research first (Standard VI(B)).
rubric: Includes reporting and monitoring, and who reviews the compliance officer's own trades.
rubric: States specific, checkable provisions (periods, approvals, sanctions) rather than generalities.

@quiz self
prompt: An analyst at your firm wants to buy shares in a company's IPO for her personal account. Her firm's clients may also seek an allocation of the same offering. In four or five sentences, say what she should do and why, naming the Standard.
hint: Clients and the employer come before the analyst's personal interest.
solution: She should not buy unless the firm's policy allows it and compliance gives prior written approval. Standard VI(B), Priority of Transactions, puts clients' and the employer's transactions ahead of personal ones, and a limited supply of IPO shares means that her purchase could reduce what clients receive. Firms commonly bar access persons from buying IPOs for this reason, and the US code of ethics rule requires pre-approval of IPO purchases. If clients are fully served and compliance approves in writing, she may proceed and disclose the holding as needed under VI(A).
rubric: Names Standard VI(B), Priority of Transactions, and says clients and the employer come before personal trades.
rubric: Says she needs prior approval from compliance and ties the point to limited IPO supply.
rubric: Mentions disclosure of the resulting holding or conflict (Standard VI(A)).
