@article
Welcome to the part of the job that no model can do for you. Over a career you will build valuations, but clients will trust you because of how you behave when nobody is checking. I have sat on both sides of the table, as the analyst with a tempting shortcut and as the reviewer who found it. The Code and Standards exist so that you can recognize the moment before it happens.

### What the Code and Standards are

The **CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct** apply to every CFA Institute member and every CFA candidate, wherever they work. The current text took effect on January 1, 2024, and it is explained in the Standards of Practice Handbook (12th edition, 2024). The Code of Ethics is short. In my words, it asks you to act with integrity, competence, diligence and respect toward the public, clients, employers and colleagues; to put the integrity of the profession and your clients' interests ahead of your own; to use reasonable care and independent judgment; to practice and encourage ethical conduct; to promote the integrity and viability of the global capital markets; and to keep your professional competence current.

The **Standards** turn those principles into seven groups:

| Group | Subject | Parts |
|---|---|---|
| I | Professionalism | Knowledge of the Law; Independence and Objectivity; Misrepresentation; Misconduct; Competence |
| II | Integrity of Capital Markets | Material Nonpublic Information; Market Manipulation |
| III | Duties to Clients | Loyalty, Prudence, and Care; Fair Dealing; Suitability; Performance Presentation; Preservation of Confidentiality |
| IV | Duties to Employers | Loyalty; Additional Compensation Arrangements; Responsibilities of Supervisors |
| V | Investment Analysis, Recommendations, and Actions | Diligence and Reasonable Basis; Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients; Record Retention |
| VI | Conflicts of Interest | Disclosure of Conflicts; Priority of Transactions; Referral Fees |
| VII | Responsibilities as a CFA Institute Member or CFA Candidate | Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs; Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program |

The Code and Standards are professional ethics, not law. A violation can lead to a sanction through CFA Institute's Professional Conduct Program, ranging from a private censure to a suspension or the loss of the charter, even when no law was broken. Weeks 1 to 4 of this course walk through all seven groups. This week is Standard I.

### Standard I in plain words

- **I(A) Knowledge of the Law.** Know and follow the laws, rules and regulations that apply to you, including the Code and Standards. When the law and the Code differ, follow the **stricter** one. If you learn that a colleague or client is breaking a rule, you may not knowingly take part or help; you step away and tell your supervisor or compliance function.
- **I(B) Independence and Objectivity.** Keep your judgment free of influence from gifts, pressure or relationships. Modest, widely known items are different from a paid trip, a large gift or a promise of business. Disclosure of a gift does not cure a gift that compromises you.
- **I(C) Misrepresentation.** Do not make false or misleading statements about your work, your credentials, your results or a security. This includes **plagiarism**: presenting another person's research, charts or words as your own without credit. Using a source is fine when you attribute it and have checked it.
- **I(D) Misconduct.** Do not commit acts of dishonesty, fraud or deceit, or conduct that harms your professional reputation or competence. Purely private matters are not covered unless they reflect on your honesty or fitness for the work.
- **I(E) Competence.** Keep and improve the skills your work requires, and do not take on tasks you cannot do competently. You need not be an expert in everything, but you must know your limits and get help.

### A method for any dilemma

1. **Facts.** Write down what is known and what is assumed, and who could be harmed.
2. **Duties.** Which Standards apply, and to whom do you owe each duty (clients, employer, markets, the profession)?
3. **Conflicts.** Where does your interest, your employer's, or a client's pull the other way?
4. **Options.** List at least three actions, including doing nothing.
5. **Decision.** Pick the action that satisfies every Standard, name the Standard in your reasoning, and say what you would document.

Most violations in practice are not dramatic. They are small, rationalized steps. A good test is whether you would be comfortable if your action were described on the front page of a newspaper and read by a client.

@example
title: A trip paid by the issuer
problem: Priya is a research analyst at a small asset manager in a market where local rules do not restrict analysts from accepting travel from companies they cover. The chief financial officer of Alder Freight, a company she rates, offers to fly her and two colleagues to a plant visit, with first-class flights and three nights at a resort. She is about to publish an updated rating. What should she do under the Standards?
step: Facts. The travel is lavish and offered by the company she covers, just before she publishes. The local law allows it.
step: Duties. Standard I(A) says to follow the stricter of law and Code, so permissive local law is no defense. Standard I(B) asks her to protect her independence and objectivity.
step: Conflict. A resort stay and first-class flights could reasonably be seen as influencing her rating, and the timing makes that worse.
step: Options. Accept as offered; decline everything; attend the plant visit but pay for her own commercial-class travel and lodging (or have her employer pay). The third option keeps the useful access without the compromise.
step: Decision. She should decline the paid package and, with her employer's approval, pay her own way for the visit, because the Code's independence requirement is stricter than the local law. She should tell her supervisor about the offer.
answer: Decline the paid trip, pay her own way (or have her firm pay), and report the offer, citing Standards I(A) and I(B).

@example
title: A chart that looks familiar
problem: Tomás, a new analyst, copies a market-share chart and two paragraphs of industry analysis from a broker's published report into his firm's client note and removes the broker's name. Which Standard does this violate, and how should he fix it?
step: Facts. The text and chart were produced by someone else and are presented as his firm's own work.
step: Duties. Standard I(C) prohibits misrepresentation, which includes plagiarism, and Standard I(D) warns against deceit.
step: What would have been acceptable. Quoting or summarizing with a clear source, or building his own analysis from the underlying public data.
step: Fix. Withdraw or correct the note, attribute the source or replace the material with his own work, and tell his supervisor.
answer: Standard I(C) (misrepresentation through plagiarism); attribute or replace the material and correct the note.

@mistakes
- Treating legal compliance as the finish line: when local law is weaker than the Code, the stricter Code governs.
- Thinking that disclosing a compromising gift or trip fixes the problem; Standard I(B) is about whether your judgment can be influenced, not only about telling people.
- Assuming plagiarism only means copying text; charts, models and ideas count if presented as your own.
- Reading Standard I(D) as covering every private act; it concerns dishonesty, fraud, deceit and conduct that reflects on your professional fitness.

prompt: A portfolio manager works in a country whose securities law permits an activity that the CFA Institute Standards of Professional Conduct prohibit. Under Standard I(A), Knowledge of the Law, what should the manager do?
choice: Follow the local law, because it is binding where the manager works
choice*: Follow the stricter of the local law and the Code and Standards, so avoid the activity
choice: Follow the Code and Standards only when working for a firm that is a CFA Institute member
choice: Engage in the activity but disclose it to clients in writing
hint: Standard I(A) names a rule for conflicts between the law and the Code.
solution: Standard I(A) requires members and candidates to follow the stricter of applicable law and the Code and Standards. Because the Code is stricter here, the manager should avoid the activity; disclosure does not make a prohibited activity acceptable.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of the following actions would violate Standard I(C), Misrepresentation? Select all that apply.
choice*: Including a competitor's forecast table in a client report without credit, as if the analyst had built it
choice*: Telling a prospect that a strategy has "never had a losing year" when it lost money in one calendar year
choice: Summarizing a third-party industry study in a report, naming the author and the study
choice*: Describing yourself in a pitch book as a CFA charterholder when you have only passed Level II
choice: Telling a client that a stock's forecast is uncertain and may be wrong
hint: Look for statements that are false, misleading or that present someone else's work as your own.
solution: Uncredited use of a competitor's table is plagiarism, claiming a perfect record that is untrue is a false statement, and calling yourself a charterholder before earning the charter is a misrepresentation of credentials. Citing a source and honestly describing uncertainty are good practice.

@practice mc
prompt: A broker offers a buy-side analyst a modest branded pen and a seat at a widely attended, public industry conference. Under Standard I(B), Independence and Objectivity, which statement is most accurate?
choice: Any item of any value is acceptable if the analyst discloses it to the client afterward
choice: The analyst must refuse every item from every broker, including a conference seat and a pen
choice*: Items of nominal value and generally available events are unlikely to impair independence, but gifts of significant value that could influence judgment should be declined or approved and disclosed under firm policy
choice: Only gifts to the portfolio manager matter, not gifts to analysts
hint: The test is whether the offer could reasonably be expected to compromise your independent judgment.
solution: Standard I(B) is about whether your judgment can be influenced. Nominal, widely available items rarely do; valuable or targeted ones can, and the firm's gift policy and disclosure to the employer help manage them.

@practice self
prompt: Marisol, an analyst at a regional investment firm, learns that her supervisor has been rounding the firm's reported composite returns up by 0.3 percentage points a year "to match the competition". She has not been asked to do anything about it. Write a short resolution (4 to 6 sentences) that names the Standard or Standards involved and says what she should do.
hint: Think about both what the firm is saying to clients and what Marisol may not do even if she is not the one rounding.
solution: Rounding returns up is a false statement to clients and prospects, which violates Standard I(C), Misrepresentation, and Standard III(D), Performance Presentation (fair, accurate and complete presentation), and it is dishonesty under I(D). Standard I(A) says Marisol must not knowingly participate in or assist the violation and should dissociate from it. She should document what she observed, raise it with her supervisor if she believes that is safe and effective, and otherwise go to the compliance officer or senior management so the reports can be corrected. If nothing is done she should keep her own work free of the inflated numbers and consider whether she can remain associated with the reporting.
rubric: Identifies the false return reporting as a violation of Standard I(C) (and names III(D) or I(D)).
rubric: Cites Standard I(A): she must not knowingly participate in or assist the violation and should dissociate.
rubric: Recommends specific steps: document, escalate to compliance or senior management, and ensure the reports are corrected.
rubric: Does not suggest ignoring the issue because she is not personally changing the numbers.

@practice self
prompt: Write a five-step analysis, using the method from this lesson (facts, duties, conflicts, options, decision), for this case. Devika is asked by her manager to prepare a valuation of a thinly traded company in an industry she has never covered. The deadline is tomorrow, and the client expects a confident recommendation. Name the Standard or Standards you rely on.
hint: Standard I(E) is about competence; consider options that let the work get done honestly.
solution: Facts: unfamiliar industry, tight deadline, a confident recommendation expected, a thinly traded stock whose price is a weak guide. Duties: Standard I(E), Competence, requires skills and knowledge for the task; Standard I(C) forbids overstating confidence; Standard V(A) requires diligence and a reasonable basis. Conflicts: pressure from the manager and the client's expectation against her duty not to overstate what she knows. Options: write the recommendation anyway; refuse; tell the manager the limits, ask for more time or an experienced reviewer, and present a range with stated assumptions. Decision: the third option. She should say what she can and cannot support in the time, get help, and avoid projecting more certainty than the analysis supports, documenting the sources and assumptions.
rubric: Applies all five steps in order with facts specific to the case.
rubric: Names Standard I(E), Competence, and ties it to getting help or more time.
rubric: Rejects overstating confidence by citing I(C) or V(A).
rubric: Chooses an option that is honest and workable (seek help, state limits, present a range) rather than refusing or bluffing.

@quiz mc
prompt: Ines, a CFA candidate, writes on a business card: "Ines Varga, CFA". She has passed Level II but not yet earned the charter. Which standard is most directly relevant?
choice: Standard II(A), Material Nonpublic Information
choice*: Standard VII(B), Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program
choice: Standard IV(A), Loyalty to employers
choice: Standard III(E), Preservation of Confidentiality
hint: The question concerns how a person may refer to the designation and the program.
solution: Using the letters "CFA" after a name signals that she holds the charter, which she does not. Standard VII(B) governs references to the designation and the program, and it also implicates Standard I(C), Misrepresentation. She may truthfully say she has passed Level II.

@quiz self
prompt: A senior analyst at your firm tells you, "The regulator doesn't require us to disclose that issuers pay for our analysts' site visits, so there is nothing to worry about." In four or five sentences, explain whether the senior analyst is right, naming the Standards involved, and say what you would suggest the firm do.
hint: Compare what the law requires with what Standards I(A) and I(B) require.
solution: The senior analyst is not right. Standard I(A) says to follow the stricter of law and Code, so absence of a legal requirement does not settle the question. Standard I(B), Independence and Objectivity, asks analysts to keep their judgment free of influence, and issuer-paid trips can compromise it. I would suggest that the firm adopt a policy under which the firm or the analyst pays for travel, limits gifts and hospitality, and requires disclosure and approval of anything beyond nominal value.
rubric: States that the senior analyst is wrong because legal minimums do not set the standard (Standard I(A), stricter rule).
rubric: Identifies Standard I(B) and explains how issuer-paid travel threatens independence.
rubric: Proposes a concrete policy step such as self-paid or firm-paid travel, gift limits, or pre-approval and disclosure.
