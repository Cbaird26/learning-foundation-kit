@article
Performance is the number clients remember, so it is also the number most tempting to dress up. The easiest ways to cheat are to show only your best accounts, to report returns that depend on when clients added money, or to change the benchmark after the fact. The **Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS)** are the industry's answer: voluntary, ethical standards that make performance reports **fair, comparable and complete**. The current edition is **GIPS 2020**. Standard III(D) of the Code requires fair, accurate and complete performance presentation; following GIPS is a recognized way to meet it. (Note that from the February 2027 CFA exams, GIPS is no longer a separate Level I reading, but it remains part of professional practice.)

### What GIPS covers

GIPS are compliance standards for **firms**, applied to all of a firm's relevant business. The 2020 edition has separate sections for firms that manage separate accounts (using **composites**), firms that manage **pooled funds** (which prepare a GIPS Pooled Fund Report), and **asset owners**. A firm that claims compliance must meet *all* applicable requirements; there is no "partial compliance". The claim must use the prescribed wording, which in substance says that the firm claims compliance with GIPS and has prepared and presented the report in compliance with GIPS. **Verification** by an independent third party (firm-wide) is optional, and a firm may not say a composite has been verified: verification is of the firm, not of a composite.

**Composites.** A composite is the aggregation of the portfolios managed under the same mandate, strategy or objective. All actual, fee-paying, discretionary portfolios must be included in at least one composite, so a firm cannot leave out poor performers. Composite definitions are fixed in advance and applied consistently; portfolios move between composites only for documented reasons.

### Returns

GIPS require **time-weighted returns** (TWRs), which remove the effect of client contributions and withdrawals the manager does not control. Returns are **total returns** (income plus capital gains), with trade-date accounting and accrued income. Portfolios are valued at least monthly and on the date of **large cash flows** (a firm sets its own definition). A TWR over sub-periods $i = 1, \ldots, n$ is

$$R_i = \frac{V_i^{\text{end}} - V_i^{\text{begin}} - CF_i}{V_i^{\text{begin}}}, \qquad R_{\text{TWR}} = \prod_{i=1}^{n}(1 + R_i) - 1,$$

where each sub-period ends just before a large flow, values are measured at that date, and a flow is added to the next sub-period's starting value. Returns for periods longer than one year are annualized as $(1 + R_{\text{cum}})^{1/y} - 1$.

When a valuation at every flow is not available, an approximation is the **Modified Dietz** return:

$$R_{\text{MD}} = \frac{V^{\text{end}} - V^{\text{begin}} - \sum CF_j}{V^{\text{begin}} + \sum w_j\, CF_j}, \qquad w_j = \frac{CD - D_j}{CD},$$

where $CD$ is the number of days in the period, $D_j$ the day on which flow $j$ occurs, and a deposit is a positive $CF$. Money-weighted (internal) rates of return depend on the client's timing, so they measure the client's experience, not the manager's skill.

### What a composite report must show

A GIPS composite report presents the composite's annual returns, **gross-of-fees or net-of-fees** (a firm must show one of the two, and may show both; net-of-fees means after actual investment management fees), and a **benchmark** return, with the number of portfolios, **composite assets**, **total firm assets** (all as of each annual period end; composite assets as a percentage of firm assets may be added but cannot replace the firm total), and a measure of **internal dispersion** (such as the asset-weighted standard deviation, equal-weighted standard deviation, range or interquartile range) when the composite holds six or more portfolios for the full year. It shows the **three-year annualized ex-post standard deviation** of monthly returns for both the composite and the benchmark when 36 months of data exist. The report gives a composite description, the fee schedule, the currency and the compliance statement, and presents at least five years of compliant performance (or since inception), building up to ten.

**Composite return** is the asset-weighted average of the portfolio returns, using beginning-of-period assets (or beginning assets plus weighted flows):

$$R_c = \sum_i w_i R_i, \quad w_i = \frac{A_i}{\sum A_i}.$$

The **asset-weighted standard deviation** of the portfolios' returns is $\sqrt{\sum_i w_i (R_i - R_c)^2}$.

@example
title: A time-weighted return with a large deposit
problem: A portfolio starts a quarter at \$10.0 million. Just before a client deposit of \$5.0 million it is worth \$10.6 million, and it ends the quarter at \$16.1 million. Compute the time-weighted return, and compare it with the naive return that ignores the deposit.
step: Sub-period 1, before the deposit: $R_1 = (10.6 - 10.0)/10.0 = 6.00\%$.
step: After the deposit the portfolio is worth $10.6 + 5.0 = 15.6$ million. Sub-period 2: $R_2 = (16.1 - 15.6)/15.6 = 3.205\%$.
step: Link: $R_{\text{TWR}} = 1.0600 \times 1.03205 - 1 = 9.397\%$.
step: A naive return that divides the ending value by the starting balance plus the deposit, $16.1/15.0 - 1 = 7.33\%$, understates the result, because the manager earned 6.00% on the smaller balance and 3.21% on the larger one. The time-weighted 9.40% measures the manager's skill whatever the timing of the deposit.
answer: 9.40%

@example
title: A composite return and dispersion
problem: A composite has six portfolios with beginning assets (in millions) and returns for the year: 42 at 7.1%, 28 at 6.4%, 20 at 8.3%, 10 at 5.2%, 18 at 6.9% and 12 at 7.7%. Compute the asset-weighted composite return and the asset-weighted standard deviation.
step: Total assets: $42 + 28 + 20 + 10 + 18 + 12 = 130$. Weights: 32.31%, 21.54%, 15.38%, 7.69%, 13.85%, 9.23%.
step: Composite return: $R_c = 0.3231(7.1) + 0.2154(6.4) + 0.1538(8.3) + 0.0769(5.2) + 0.1385(6.9) + 0.0923(7.7) = 7.02\%$.
step: Squared deviations weighted by assets: $\sum w_i (R_i - R_c)^2 = 0.6364$, so the dispersion is $\sqrt{0.6364} = 0.80\%$.
step: Six portfolios are held for the full year, so dispersion is a required item in the report. The report would show 7.02% (gross or net, as stated), 0.80% dispersion, six portfolios and \$130 million of composite assets.
answer: Composite return 7.02%; asset-weighted standard deviation 0.80%.

@mistakes
- Using the money-weighted return to describe a manager's performance; client deposits and withdrawals distort it.
- Averaging sub-period returns instead of linking them geometrically.
- Cherry-picking: leaving a weak portfolio out of a composite, or adding a strong one after the fact.
- Saying a firm is "GIPS compliant except for ..." or that a composite is "GIPS verified": the standards allow no partial compliance, and verification is of the firm as a whole.

hint: Link the three annual returns geometrically, as growth factors.
hint: Take the cube root of the product of $(1 + R)$ and subtract 1.
solution: $(1.082)(0.965)(1.110) = 1.15898$, so the cumulative return is 15.90% and the annualized return is $1.15898^{1/3} - 1 = 5.04\%$. The simple average of 5.23% overstates it.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio begins a 30-day month at \$4,000,000 and ends at \$4,390,000. On day 5 the client withdraws \$100,000, and on day 20 the client deposits \$300,000 (flows occur at the end of the day). Compute the Modified Dietz return for the month, weighting each flow by the fraction of the month it was invested. Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.73
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (4390000 - 4000000 - (300000 - 100000)) / (4000000 + 300000 * (30 - 20) / 30 - 100000 * (30 - 5) / 30) * 100
hint: The numerator is ending value minus beginning value minus net flows.
hint: The weights are $(30 - 20)/30$ for the deposit and $(30 - 5)/30$ for the withdrawal, which enters with a negative sign.
solution: Net flows are $+300{,}000 - 100{,}000 = 200{,}000$, so the gain is $4{,}390{,}000 - 4{,}000{,}000 - 200{,}000 = 190{,}000$. The denominator is $4{,}000{,}000 + 300{,}000(10/30) - 100{,}000(25/30) = 4{,}016{,}667$. The return is $190{,}000/4{,}016{,}667 = 4.73\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: A client asks why you report a time-weighted return for the account instead of the money-weighted return that reflects her own deposits and withdrawals. In four or five sentences, explain the difference and which is the better measure of your skill as manager, and when the money-weighted return is still useful.
hint: Ask who controls the timing of cash flows.
solution: The time-weighted return removes the effect of deposits and withdrawals by measuring each sub-period and linking them, so it reflects the manager's investment decisions; the manager does not control when the client adds or removes money. The money-weighted return (internal rate of return) weights the performance by the amounts invested at each time, so a client who adds money before a poor stretch gets a lower figure even if the manager did well. GIPS therefore require time-weighted returns for composites. The money-weighted return is still useful for the client's own experience and for assets where the manager controls the cash flows, such as private-equity funds.
rubric: Explains that the time-weighted return removes the effect of client cash flows and measures the manager.
rubric: Explains that the money-weighted return depends on the timing and size of flows.
rubric: Names when the money-weighted return is still useful, such as judging the client's own outcome.
