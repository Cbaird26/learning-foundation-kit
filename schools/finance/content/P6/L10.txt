@article
In investing, a resume is a small piece of research: a set of claims about you that a skeptical reader will check. That is useful for you, because everything you learned about evidence in this course applies. Be specific, quantify, and never overstate.

### The one-page resume

For early-career roles a single page is the norm. Use this order: contact line, **education** (degree, honors, relevant coursework, GPA if strong), **experience** (most recent first), **projects** (valuation models, stock pitches, code), **credentials** (CFA Program status, licenses), and **skills** (Excel, Python, SQL, data tools). Use a plain, scannable format. Many employers use applicant tracking systems that search for keywords, so mirror the language of the posting where it is true.

**Bullets.** Each bullet should be: strong verb + what you did + how + result. Compare "Responsible for valuation models" with "Built a discounted cash flow model for six retail companies in Python, cutting update time from 45 to 12 minutes (a 73% reduction)." The second claim is specific and checkable. Quantify with percentages, dollar amounts, counts and time saved, and compute them accurately. The percent change is

$$\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100.$$

### Describing credentials correctly

The Code and Standards apply to your resume. Under Standard I(C) (Misrepresentation) and Standard VII(B) (Reference to CFA Institute, the CFA Designation, and the CFA Program) you may say you are a **candidate in the CFA Program** or that you passed a given level; you may not imply that you hold the charter, use the designation "CFA" after your name, or suggest that passing levels gives a partial designation. A practical wording is "Passed Level I of the CFA Program (2026); registered for Level II." The same rule of accuracy covers dates, titles, grades, licenses and the scope of your role. Employers check, and misstatements are a common reason for withdrawn offers and later dismissal.

### Networking

Networking in finance is the practice of learning from people and being useful to them. A good request is brief, specific and respects time: introduce yourself in two sentences, say what you admire about the person's work, and ask for 15 to 20 minutes to learn about the role. Prepare questions, listen, thank them the same day and follow up when you act on advice. Keep to the Standards: never ask for or accept confidential or material nonpublic information, and follow gift and entertainment rules when you work for a firm. Use professional communities such as local CFA societies, university alumni groups and industry events. Keep a simple tracker of contacts and dates.

### Interviews

Expect three kinds of questions.

- **Behavioral.** "Tell me about a time you...": answer with a story using **situation, task, action, result** (STAR), under two minutes, naming your own actions and the measurable outcome. Prepare three stories that between them show analysis, teamwork, handling pressure, an ethical choice and a mistake you learned from.
- **Technical.** Expect accounting (how the three statements connect), valuation (walk through a discounted cash flow, compare multiples), and markets. Practice speaking each aloud. If you do not know, say how you would find out.
- **Fit and judgment.** Why this role and firm, and what would you do if a client or colleague asked you to do something questionable? Here your ethics preparation shows: name the principle and the escalation.

For a **stock pitch**, state the recommendation, the thesis in two or three points, the valuation and the main risks. Bring one idea you can defend. Remember the lessons of Week 4: separate fact from opinion, and label assumptions. Keep your answers neutral about employers: describe what you want to learn and contribute, not criticisms of other firms.

### Preparing

Read the firm's public materials (filings, annual reports, the posting), practice aloud with a friend, prepare questions of your own about the team and how success is judged, and follow up with a short thank-you note. If you receive an offer, ask for the time to consider it and compare the full package, not just the base salary.

@example
title: A resume bullet from raw facts
problem: Your internship notes say: "Helped with the monthly performance report. Before: 3 analysts spent 20 hours a month in Excel; after the Python template: 6 hours." Turn this into a resume bullet and compute the percentage change in hours.
step: Percent change in hours: $(6 - 20)/20 = -70\%$, a 70% reduction. Check the figure: 20 hours down to 6 saves 14 hours.
step: Identify your own contribution. If you wrote the template, say so; do not claim the whole team's result as yours.
step: Bullet: "Built a Python template for the monthly performance report, cutting preparation time from 20 to 6 hours (70%) across a team of three analysts."
step: Be ready to explain it in an interview: how the template works and how you measured the hours.
answer: 70% reduction; "Built a Python template ... cutting preparation time from 20 to 6 hours (70%)".

@example
title: A STAR story about an ethical choice
problem: Outline a STAR story for an interview question "Tell me about a time you faced an ethical issue."
step: Situation. On a student investment team, a teammate wanted to cite a competitor's chart without credit in the final pitch the night before it was due.
step: Task. As the person editing the deck, I needed to protect the team's integrity and its deadline.
step: Action. I explained that using the chart without credit would be plagiarism (Standard I(C) in the professional setting), offered to credit the source and add our own comparison from public filings, and we finished by midnight.
step: Result. The pitch cited sources, drew a question about our own comparison and placed in the top three; I now check attribution on every slide.
answer: A STAR outline with a specific situation, my own actions, a measurable result and a lesson.

@mistakes
- Writing duties instead of results ("responsible for...") and leaving out numbers.
- Overstating credentials, for example writing "CFA Level II" as if it were a designation or placing "CFA" after your name before earning the charter.
- Sending the same generic networking message to everyone, or asking a new contact for a job in the first message.
- Telling stories in which "we" did everything, so the interviewer cannot tell what you did.

prompt: Write three interview stories in STAR form (situation, task, action, result), each under 150 words: one on analysis or problem solving, one on teamwork or conflict, and one on an ethical choice or a mistake you learned from. Use real or realistic experiences.
hint: Make the action about what you did, and finish with a result you can measure and a lesson.

rubric: Gives three distinct stories matching the three categories.
rubric: Each story uses situation, task, action and result and focuses on the learner's own actions.
rubric: At least two results include a number or concrete outcome.
rubric: The ethical or mistake story names a principle or lesson and avoids blaming others.

@quiz self
prompt: A potential mentor at a firm you admire agrees to a 20-minute call. In the middle of the call he casually mentions a client's unannounced acquisition. In four or five sentences, say what you should do and why.
hint: Think about Standard II(A) and how to handle the information and the conversation.
solution: I should not use or share the information. An unannounced acquisition is probably material and nonpublic, and Standard II(A) prohibits acting on it or causing others to act. I would politely steer the conversation back, avoid repeating the information, not trade in related securities, and, if I work for a firm, report the conversation to compliance so the security can be restricted. I would also be careful to keep the mentor from disclosing more, since passing the information could harm his own position.
rubric: Recognizes the information as likely material and nonpublic and cites Standard II(A).
rubric: Says not to trade or pass it on, and steers the conversation away.
rubric: Mentions reporting to compliance where applicable.
