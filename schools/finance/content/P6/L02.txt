@article
Last week was about your own character. This week is about the people you owe things to. In my first year as an analyst, a senior colleague told me, "The client's interest comes first, your employer's second, yours third." That ordering is a simplification, but it is a good way to remember the shape of Standards III and IV.

### Standard III: Duties to Clients

**III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care.** You act for the benefit of your clients and put their interests before your employer's and your own. You exercise the care a prudent person would, and you manage client assets and, where you hold them, proxies in the client's interest. Using client brokerage commissions to buy products or services that benefit the manager rather than the client is a classic breach. The standard applies to a fiduciary relationship, and a client's account is a clear one. Where there is no fiduciary relationship, you still owe honesty and care.

**III(B) Fair Dealing.** You treat all clients fairly when you share recommendations or change them, and when you trade. This does not mean every client gets identical results, because clients have different mandates. It means no group is systematically favored. The usual safeguards are a written allocation policy, **pro rata allocation** of a partially filled block order in proportion to the amounts each account requested, and releasing a change in recommendation to all affected clients in a reasonable time rather than calling the favorite ones first.

**III(C) Suitability.** When you advise a client or manage a portfolio, first learn the client's circumstances: investment objectives, return and risk tolerance, constraints such as time horizon, liquidity, taxes, legal limits and unique needs. Record them (an investment policy statement is the usual tool), update them regularly (at least annually is common practice), and judge each action in the context of the **whole portfolio**, not each holding alone. For a manager running a mandate, the duty is to invest consistently with the stated mandate.

**III(D) Performance Presentation.** Performance claims must be fair, accurate and complete. Week 7 covers the GIPS standards, which are the way many firms meet this duty.

**III(E) Preservation of Confidentiality.** Client information is kept confidential. There are three exceptions: the information concerns illegal activity, disclosure is required by law, or the client gives permission. Even then, share only what is needed.

### Standard IV: Duties to Employers

**IV(A) Loyalty.** Do not harm your employer or deprive it of your skills and judgment. You may prepare to leave, but you may not solicit the firm's clients or take its records and files before you go; once you have left, you may compete unless an agreement restricts it. Standard IV(A) does not require silence about wrongdoing: acting to protect clients or the integrity of the markets takes priority over loyalty to an employer engaged in violations.

**IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements.** If anyone other than your employer will pay or reward you for work related to your job (a bonus from a client for a particular outcome, referral gifts, an outside consulting fee), you need the employer's **written consent** first.

**IV(C) Responsibilities of Supervisors.** If you supervise others you must make reasonable efforts to detect and prevent violations: adopt and maintain compliance procedures, communicate them, and follow up. If the firm has no workable procedures, a supervisor should decline to accept supervisory responsibility until it does.

### Allocating a block

Suppose accounts request shares in a block order, and only part is filled. Each account's fill is its share of the total request, times the shares filled:

$$\text{fill}_i = \frac{\text{requested}_i}{\sum \text{requested}} \times \text{shares filled}.$$

Round to the trading lot size and handle remainders by a documented rule. Allocating after you see which account would profit most is a violation, whatever the intent.

@example
title: Splitting a partial fill
problem: A manager places a combined buy order for 100,000 shares of a stock on behalf of three accounts that requested 20,000, 50,000 and 30,000 shares. The broker fills only 40,000 shares at a single average price. How many shares does each account receive under pro rata allocation?
step: Total requested: $20{,}000 + 50{,}000 + 30{,}000 = 100{,}000$. Shares filled: $40{,}000$, so each account gets 40% of what it asked for.
step: Account 1: $0.40 \times 20{,}000 = 8{,}000$. Account 2: $0.40 \times 50{,}000 = 20{,}000$. Account 3: $0.40 \times 30{,}000 = 12{,}000$.
step: Check: $8{,}000 + 20{,}000 + 12{,}000 = 40{,}000$ and every account pays the same average price. If the manager had given all 40,000 shares to the account that expected to profit most, that would breach Standard III(B).
answer: 8,000, 20,000 and 12,000 shares.

@example
title: Moonlighting for a client
problem: Ravi is a portfolio manager at Cedar Point Advisors. A wealthy client offers him a personal fee of \$30,000 a year to "keep an eye on" the client's family trust on weekends, using the research he writes for the firm's accounts. He has not told his employer. What should he do?
step: Duties. Standard IV(B) requires the employer's written consent before accepting compensation from a client for work related to his job. Standard IV(A) requires that he not harm his employer by diverting its work product.
step: Conflict. Using firm research for a private paid arrangement could also shortchange other clients (III(B)) and may conflict with the firm's own fee agreement with this client.
step: Options. Accept quietly; decline; disclose the proposal in writing to his employer and proceed only with written consent and on terms that do not disadvantage other clients.
step: Decision. He should disclose in writing and obtain written consent, or decline. Accepting quietly violates IV(B).
answer: Disclose in writing and get the employer's written consent (Standard IV(B)), or decline; do not accept quietly.

@mistakes
- Treating fair dealing as equal results for every client: different mandates produce different holdings, but no client may be systematically disadvantaged in allocation or recommendations.
- Thinking verbal permission from a supervisor satisfies Standard IV(B), which asks for the employer's written consent.
- Assuming loyalty to the employer means keeping quiet about client harm; protecting clients and market integrity comes first.
- Revealing client details to a spouse, friend or the press because the information is "not secret"; confidentiality has only three exceptions (illegal activity, legal requirement, client permission).

answer: 21000
unit: shares

hint: First compute the share of the total request that the 30,000-share account represents.

solution: The four requests total $10{,}000 + 20{,}000 + 20{,}000 + 30{,}000 = 80{,}000$ shares and the fill is 70% of that, so the account receives $0.70 \times 30{,}000 = 21{,}000$ shares.

prompt: Resolve each of these five workplace dilemmas in one or two sentences, naming the Standard. (1) A portfolio manager gives a favorite account the best-priced fills from a block and the rest to others. (2) An advisor puts a retired client's emergency savings into a single illiquid private fund because the fund pays the advisor's firm a higher fee. (3) An analyst's friend at a rival firm asks which stocks the client's fund holds. (4) A manager receives a gift card from a client after a strong quarter and does not mention it to the firm. (5) A supervisor sees that a junior trader has no written guidance on trade allocation but does nothing because "nothing has gone wrong yet".

solution: (1) Violates III(B), Fair Dealing: allocate pro rata or by a written, consistently applied policy, not by favoritism. (5) Violates IV(C), Responsibilities of Supervisors: put written procedures in place and monitor them rather than waiting for a failure.

rubric: Identifies the conflict of interest in case (2): the higher fee to the firm.
rubric: Keeps each resolution short and specific rather than generic.

@quiz self
prompt: You are the new head of compliance at a ten-person advisory firm. The firm has no written procedure for how a supervisor should respond when a junior analyst reports a possible violation. Write a short procedure (five to seven sentences or steps) and name the Standard that makes this a supervisor's responsibility.
hint: Standard IV(C) expects reasonable procedures, communication and follow-up.
solution: Standard IV(C) requires supervisors to make reasonable efforts to detect and prevent violations, which includes adopting and communicating procedures. Procedure: (1) any employee may report a possible violation verbally or in writing to a supervisor or directly to the compliance officer; (2) the recipient records the report the same day; (3) compliance reviews it within a set number of business days, with support from legal counsel if needed; (4) the supervisor takes steps to stop any continuing violation; (5) the reporter is protected from retaliation; (6) the outcome and the corrective action are documented; and (7) the procedure is reviewed annually and training is repeated.
rubric: Names Standard IV(C), Responsibilities of Supervisors.
rubric: Includes specific procedural elements: how to report, who reviews, timing, and documentation.
rubric: Includes protection from retaliation and a way to stop the violation.
