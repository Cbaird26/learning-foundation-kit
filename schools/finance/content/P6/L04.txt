@article
When you write your first research note, you will feel pressure from three directions: a portfolio manager who wants a particular answer, a company that would like a friendlier rating, and your own wish to sound certain. Standard V and the research-independence rules are the guardrails for those moments.

### Standard V(A): Diligence and Reasonable Basis

Before you take investment action or make a recommendation, you need a **reasonable and adequate basis**, supported by appropriate research and investigation. In practice that means independent judgment, thorough work proportional to the question, and awareness of what you do not know. You may use research from others, but you must make reasonable efforts to confirm that it is sound: check the author's assumptions, the data and the process. A model that you cannot explain is not a reasonable basis. Quantitative models, screens and now AI tools fall under the same duty (Week 12).

### Standard V(B): Communication with Clients and Prospective Clients

Tell clients how you reach your recommendations: the basic format and general principles of your process, and any significant changes to it. Disclose the important limitations and risks of your approach. Use judgment about which factors are important and include them. Above all, separate **fact** from **opinion**. "Revenue grew 11% last year" is a fact you can source; "the company will keep growing at that rate" is an opinion that rests on assumptions you must state.

A recommendation should say what you expect and why. One quantity is an **expected total return**, computed from a price target $P_T$, the current price $P_0$ and dividends $D$ expected over the horizon:

$$\text{expected return} = \frac{P_T + D}{P_0} - 1.$$

Report the horizon, the method behind $P_T$ (for example a discounted cash flow valuation or a target multiple) and what would change your mind.

### Standard V(C): Record Retention

Keep records that support your analysis, recommendations, actions and communications with clients and prospects, in a form that can be reproduced. The Handbook recommends keeping them at least **seven years** unless local law requires longer. The records belong to the firm.

### Independence rules for research

Standards alone do not remove the pressure on analysts, so regulators have added rules. In the United States, **Regulation AC** requires research reports and public appearances to include a **certification** that the views expressed accurately reflect the analyst's personal views, and a statement of whether any part of the analyst's pay was, is or will be related to specific recommendations or views. FINRA's research rule (Rule 2241) requires broker-dealers to separate research from investment banking pressure: for example, banking staff may not approve or direct research content, an analyst's pay may not be tied to a specific banking deal, and there are limits on analysts' trading and on communications around offerings (quiet periods). Reports also disclose conflicts such as the firm's ownership of the stock and the **rating distribution**: the percentage of covered companies rated buy, hold and sell, so readers can see if ratings skew positive.

The mosaic theory in Standard II(A) is also relevant to research: you may combine public information and non-material nonpublic information to form a view, but you may not trade or recommend on material nonpublic information (Week 5 returns to this).

### A research-specific addendum to a trading policy

Personal-trading rules (Week 3) need extra provisions for analysts: no personal trade in a covered security shortly before or after a rating change; no trade contrary to the analyst's own published rating; disclosure of any holding in a covered company; and a hold on trading while a report is in draft or under review.

@example
title: Stating an expected return and what drives it
problem: An analyst's discounted cash flow model gives Brightwater Utilities a 12-month price target of \$60.00. The stock trades at \$52.00 and is expected to pay \$2.40 in dividends over the year. She writes "Strong buy, the stock will rise 15%." Is the statement adequate, and what should she write?
step: Compute the expected total return: $(60.00 + 2.40)/52.00 - 1 = 62.40/52.00 - 1 = 0.20 = 20.0\%$. The price return alone is $60/52 - 1 = 15.4\%$, and dividends add the rest.
step: Her statement mixes a price return with an unqualified forecast, and presents an opinion as a fact. Standard V(B) asks her to separate fact from opinion and explain what drives the number.
step: Rewrite: "Our DCF-based 12-month target is \$60.00, which with expected dividends of \$2.40 implies a total return of about 20%. This rests on our assumption of 3% annual revenue growth and stable allowed returns; higher interest rates or an adverse regulatory decision are the main risks."
step: Support it. She should keep the model, inputs and sources in the file (Standard V(C)).
answer: Expected total return 20.0%; restate it as an opinion with assumptions and risks (Standards V(A), V(B), V(C)).

@example
title: Relying on someone else's model
problem: A portfolio manager asks Keiko, an analyst, to use a vendor's quantitative score to rank 300 stocks for a client. Keiko does not know how the score is built, and the vendor will not share details. What should she do?
step: Duties. Standard V(A) requires a reasonable and adequate basis, and permits reliance on others' research only after reasonable efforts to confirm it is sound.
step: Facts. She cannot examine inputs or methods, so she cannot confirm the score is sound. Standard V(B) would also require her to explain the process to clients.
step: Options. Use it unchanged; refuse; test it (back-test, check for obvious bias, compare to her own analysis), get documentation, and use it only as one input with disclosure of the limits.
step: Decision. Testing and documenting before relying on it, and disclosing that the score is vendor-supplied and what is known about it.
answer: Test and document the score, use it only as one input, and disclose its limits (Standards V(A), V(B)).

@mistakes
- Quoting a price target as a certainty rather than an opinion with stated assumptions and risks.
- Forgetting dividends when computing expected total return from a price target.
- Treating a third party's recommendation or a vendor's model as a reasonable basis without checking its inputs and assumptions.
- Deleting working files and models after publication; supporting records must be retained (seven years is the recommended minimum).

@practice self
prompt: A small firm's personal-trading policy has pre-clearance, a restricted list and quarterly reporting, but nothing specific to its three research analysts. Write four research-specific provisions to add to the policy, and for each name the Standard it supports.
hint: Think about timing around ratings, contrary trades, holdings in covered companies and reports in draft.
solution: (1) No personal trade in a covered security during the three trading days before and after a rating or target change (Standards VI(B), II(A)). (2) No personal trade contrary to the analyst's own current published rating, such as selling a stock rated buy, without compliance approval (Standards VI(B), V(B)). (3) Analysts must disclose any holding in a covered company to compliance and in the research report (Standard VI(A)). (4) Once a report is in draft, covered securities are on a watch list and analysts may not trade them until it is published (Standards VI(B), II(A)). Compliance reviews analysts' trades against the rating history each quarter.
rubric: Gives at least four distinct provisions specific to research staff (timing around ratings, contrary trades, holdings disclosure, reports in draft).
rubric: Names an appropriate Standard for each provision (VI(B), VI(A), II(A), V(B)).
rubric: Provisions are concrete and checkable, with specific windows or approvals.
rubric: Includes a monitoring step in which compliance checks trades against research activity.

@quiz self

solution: I should not change the rating to suit the banking relationship.
