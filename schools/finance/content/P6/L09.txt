@article
When people ask me how to get into investing, they usually mean one job, "analyst", as if it were a single thing. It is a family of jobs with different clients, rhythms and pay structures. Choosing among them early saves years. This lesson is a map; Week 10 shows how to present yourself for the roles you choose.

### Three sides of the industry

**Buy side.** Firms that invest money for themselves or on behalf of clients: asset managers (mutual funds, exchange-traded funds and separate accounts), hedge funds, pension funds, endowments and foundations, insurers, sovereign wealth funds, family offices, and private equity and venture capital firms. Their research is private and supports investment decisions.

**Sell side.** Firms that provide services to the buy side and to issuers, usually investment banks and broker-dealers: **research** (publishing analysis and recommendations), **sales and trading** (execution, market-making and client coverage) and **investment banking** (raising capital and advising on mergers and acquisitions).

**Advisory and other.** Wealth management and financial planning, consulting, corporate finance and treasury, credit rating agencies, banks' credit and risk functions, and the growing set of data, quantitative and technology roles at all of the above.

### Roles in investment management

| Role | What you do | Skills that matter |
|---|---|---|
| Research analyst (buy side) | Study companies or sectors, build valuation models, pitch ideas to the portfolio manager | Financial statement analysis, valuation, writing, judgment under uncertainty |
| Portfolio manager | Decide what to hold and in what size within a mandate; answer for the results and for risk | Decision-making, risk management, communication with clients |
| Trader | Execute orders at good prices, manage market impact and liquidity | Market structure, speed, discipline |
| Risk analyst | Measure and report portfolio risk, test limits | Statistics, programming, communication |
| Performance and operations analyst | Calculate returns (GIPS), reconcile holdings, manage data | Accuracy, attention to detail, systems |
| Client service and relationship manager | Report to clients, win and retain mandates | Listening, writing, product knowledge |
| Quantitative researcher | Build and test systematic strategies | Statistics, programming, data |

### Research and banking

A **sell-side research analyst** covers a group of companies, publishes models and notes, answers client questions and works under independence rules (Week 4). The role rewards clear writing and frequent communication. A **credit analyst** at a bank, insurer or rating agency studies a borrower's ability to repay, using the same financial statement tools with a focus on downside protection.

In **investment banking** the usual ladder is analyst, associate, vice president, director and managing director. Analysts build financial models, valuation and presentation materials for mergers and acquisitions and for debt and equity offerings; hours are long and the work is deadline driven. Bankers often hold the Series 79 registration (Week 6). Many bankers later move to private equity, corporate development or the buy side.

### How an asset manager earns money

Most asset managers charge a **fee as a percentage of assets under management (AUM)**. Revenue is

$$\text{revenue} = \text{AUM} \times \text{fee rate}.$$

This ties the firm's income to market levels and client flows: when markets fall, AUM and revenue fall together, even if headcount does not. This is why asset managers watch costs and why hiring slows after market declines. Hedge funds and some other vehicles also charge a **performance fee**, a share of profits, which makes income more variable. Compensation typically combines salary and a bonus linked to the firm's results and to individual contribution. Details differ widely by firm and region, so look up current surveys rather than relying on a number from a book.

### Credentials and paths

Entry routes include internships, rotation programs, analyst programs, a master's degree in finance, and lateral moves from accounting, consulting, engineering or data science. Credentials valued across the industry include the **CFA charter** (passing three levels, 4,000 hours of relevant experience over at least 36 months, membership and references), the CPA for accounting-heavy roles, and licenses for client-facing roles (Week 6). Programming in Python and SQL, fluency in spreadsheets and clear writing help in nearly every role.

### Choosing

Ask three questions: **What do I like doing** (reading filings, building models, talking to clients, executing trades, working with data)? **What pace and structure suit me** (deadline-driven deals, daily market hours, long-term research)? **What does the path require** (a license, a degree, a portfolio of work)? Then test your answers by speaking with people in the role.

@example
title: Revenue and profit of an asset manager
problem: An asset manager runs \$3.2 billion at an average fee of 0.55% and has an operating margin of 35%. What are its annual revenue and operating profit?
step: Revenue: $3{,}200{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0055 = \$17{,}600{,}000$, or \$17.6 million.
step: Operating profit: $0.35 \times 17.6 = \$6.16$ million.
step: Sensitivity: if markets drop 10% and AUM falls with them (no flows), revenue falls 10% to \$15.84 million. If costs do not change, profit falls by \$1.76 million to \$4.40 million, a decline of about 29%, because of operating leverage.
answer: Revenue \$17.6 million; operating profit \$6.16 million.

@example
title: Matching a person to roles
problem: Nadia enjoys reading filings and building spreadsheets, dislikes daily deadlines and wants to write and defend a view over months. She has completed the CFA Level I exam. Which roles should she explore first, and which less?
step: Her interests (filings, models, long-horizon views, written argument) align with buy-side or sell-side equity research and credit analysis.
step: Her dislike of daily deadlines and execution pressure points away from trading and, for most firms, from deal-driven investment banking.
step: Credentials: the CFA Program fits research roles; sell-side research also needs analyst registration (Series 86 and 87), which her employer would sponsor.
step: First step: talk to analysts in two or three of those roles, then choose a portfolio of written work to show.
answer: Research (buy side or sell side) and credit analysis first; trading and banking less.

@mistakes
- Treating "analyst" as one job: buy-side research, sell-side research, banking and risk analysts have different work and incentives.
- Assuming fee-based revenue is stable; when markets fall, AUM and revenue fall even if clients stay.
- Choosing a path only by pay and prestige, without testing the daily work and hours.
- Applying for a role without checking whether it requires a license or registration.

@quiz self
prompt: A friend says, "Investment banking and equity research are basically the same job because both use financial models." In four or five sentences, explain how the two roles differ in output, client and incentives.
hint: Compare what each produces, who it serves and how independence rules apply.
solution: Both roles build models, but the output differs. A banker produces transaction work: valuation analysis, marketing materials and execution of mergers or financings for issuers who pay the firm, so the work is deadline driven and tied to completing deals. A research analyst publishes an independent view of companies for investors; the rating must not be directed by banking, and independence rules apply (Standard I(B), FINRA Rule 2241). Their licensing differs too (Series 79 for bankers and Series 86 and 87 for analysts), and the hours, rhythm and career paths differ.
