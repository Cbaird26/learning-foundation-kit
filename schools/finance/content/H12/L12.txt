@article
Rights limit what governments may do. The first ten amendments, the **Bill of Rights** (1791), originally limited only the national government. The Fourteenth Amendment (1868) later became the basis for applying most of them to state and local governments, a process called **incorporation**.

### Major rights

- **First Amendment:** "Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances." Courts have held that speech rights have limits, such as incitement to imminent lawless action, and that the government may not favor a religion or punish religious practice without strong reasons. Americans disagree about where exactly the lines fall.
- **Second Amendment:** concerns the right to keep and bear arms; courts and citizens debate its scope.
- **Fourth through Eighth Amendments:** protect against unreasonable searches, require grand juries, bar double jeopardy and self-incrimination, guarantee due process, jury trials, the right to counsel and protection from cruel and unusual punishments. In *Gideon v. Wainwright* (1963), the Court held that states must provide lawyers to defendants who cannot afford them in serious criminal cases. In *Miranda v. Arizona* (1966), it required warnings before custodial questioning.
- **Ninth and Tenth Amendments:** the Ninth says that listing some rights does not deny others, and the Tenth reserves powers to the states and the people.

### Who can vote

The Constitution originally left voting rules mostly to states, and early voters were mainly white men, often with property. Amendments widened the electorate: the **Fifteenth** (1870, race), the **Nineteenth** (1920, sex), the **Twenty-fourth** (1964, poll taxes in federal elections) and the **Twenty-sixth** (1971, age 18). Congress passed the Indian Citizenship Act in 1924 and the Voting Rights Act in 1965. Even after these laws, some Native Americans, Black Americans and others faced barriers for years.

### How elections work

Federal elections are run mostly by states and localities. Parties choose nominees through **primaries** and caucuses. For president, the **Electoral College** has 538 electors; a candidate needs a majority, 270, to win. Each state has electors equal to its House seats plus two senators, and the District of Columbia has three. Almost all states award all their electors to the statewide winner; Maine and Nebraska divide theirs. If no candidate wins a majority, the House chooses among the top candidates. Supporters of the Electoral College say it gives weight to states and encourages broad coalitions; critics say it can elect a candidate who loses the national popular vote and gives some voters more influence than others. Others propose compromises or keep the current system.

### Contested questions

Americans debate several election rules. Voter identification: supporters say it protects confidence in elections; critics say it can burden eligible voters who lack documents. Early and mail voting: supporters say it increases access; critics raise concerns about verification. Campaign finance: some argue that spending limits protect against corruption, while others argue that limits restrict free speech. A careful student states each position in terms its holders would accept and checks the claims against evidence.

### Civic participation

Citizenship involves more than voting. People serve on juries, pay taxes, follow laws, contact officials, petition, join community groups, run for office, volunteer, work on campaigns and follow reliable news. Many policies that affect daily life are made by local governments, such as school boards and city councils, so local participation matters.

@example
title: Counting toward 270
problem: A candidate has won states with 232 electoral votes. How many more are needed to win the presidency, and why 270?
step: There are 538 electors. A majority is more than half, so the candidate needs $\frac{538}{2} + 1 = 270$ votes.
step: Subtract what the candidate already has: $270 - 232 = 38$ more votes.
step: If no candidate reaches 270, the House of Representatives chooses among the top candidates, with each state delegation casting one vote.
step: Notice the difference between this count and the national popular vote; the two usually agree but not always.

@mistakes
- Thinking the Bill of Rights always applied to the states; incorporation came gradually through court decisions applying the Fourteenth Amendment.
- Assuming a right is absolute; courts balance rights with other interests under legal tests.
- Stating one side of an election-rules debate as established fact; present each side's strongest argument and the evidence.
