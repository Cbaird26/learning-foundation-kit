@article
A lender's problem is simple to state: some borrowers will not repay. The art is to say how many, how much will be lost, and how uncertain that is. Credit risk measurement breaks the question into three parts.

### The three building blocks and their conventions

- **Probability of default (PD)**: the probability that the obligor defaults within the stated horizon, **one year unless stated otherwise**. "Default" needs a definition (for example, a payment more than 90 days past due or an unlikely-to-pay judgment); different institutions define it differently, so state it.
- **Exposure at default (EAD)**: the dollar amount owed at the moment of default. For a term loan it is the outstanding balance; for a credit line it includes the amount expected to be drawn before default: $\text{EAD} = \text{drawn} + \text{CCF} \times \text{undrawn}$, where the **credit conversion factor** CCF is the fraction of the undrawn commitment expected to be drawn.
- **Loss given default (LGD)**: the fraction of EAD that is lost if default occurs, after recoveries and net of workout costs: $\text{LGD} = 1 - \text{recovery rate}$. Secured loans have lower LGD than unsecured ones. LGD is a **fraction of EAD** (a number between 0 and 1) in this course, and losses are positive numbers.

### Expected loss

If PD, LGD and EAD are independent,

$$\text{EL} = \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD}.$$

For a book of loans, expected losses add: $\text{EL}_{book} = \sum_i \text{PD}_i\,\text{LGD}_i\,\text{EAD}_i$. Expected loss is a cost of doing business: lenders price it into interest rates and hold **provisions** against it. Under accounting standards for expected credit losses (CECL in US GAAP, IFRS 9 internationally) the allowance is built from such estimates: IFRS 9 recognizes 12-month expected losses for loans whose credit quality has not deteriorated significantly and lifetime expected losses for those that have, while CECL uses lifetime expected losses from the start.

### Unexpected loss

Actual losses fluctuate around EL. For a single loan with fixed EAD and LGD, the loss is $\text{EAD}\times\text{LGD}$ with probability PD and zero otherwise: a Bernoulli variable, so the **unexpected loss** (the standard deviation of the loss) is

$$\text{UL} = \text{EAD}\times\text{LGD}\times\sqrt{\text{PD}(1-\text{PD})}.$$

If LGD is random with independent standard deviation $\sigma_{LGD}$, then $\text{UL} = \text{EAD}\sqrt{\text{PD}\,\sigma_{LGD}^2 + \text{LGD}^2\,\text{PD}(1-\text{PD})}$. Unexpected losses do **not** add across loans unless defaults are uncorrelated; correlation across borrowers (a recession hits many at once) is what makes the tail of a loan book heavy. Banks hold capital against that tail, as Week 9 shows.

### Default probabilities over time

If a borrower has a constant annual PD $p$ and years are independent, the probability of surviving $T$ years is $(1-p)^T$, so the **cumulative PD** is $1 - (1-p)^T$. Equivalently, with a constant hazard rate $\lambda$, $\text{PD}(T) = 1 - e^{-\lambda T}$. Ratings agencies publish migration matrices because real PDs change as ratings change. **Point-in-time** PDs follow the current economic cycle, **through-the-cycle** PDs average over it.

### From spreads: the credit triangle

A bond's credit spread compensates for expected default loss, so for small values $s \approx \lambda \times \text{LGD}$, giving $\lambda \approx s/\text{LGD}$. This is a market-implied PD under the risk-neutral measure and includes a risk premium, so it overstates the real-world probability of default.

### From the firm's balance sheet: the Merton model

The **Merton model** treats equity as a call option on the firm's assets with strike equal to the face value of its zero-coupon debt $D$ due at $T$. If asset value $V$ follows geometric Brownian motion with drift $\mu$ and volatility $\sigma$, the real-world default probability, the chance that $V_T < D$, is $N(-d)$, where the **distance to default** is

$$d = \frac{\ln(V/D) + (\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}.$$

Its virtues are intuition and use of market information; its weaknesses are the single debt maturity, the unobservable asset value and volatility, and default only at maturity.

@example
title: Expected loss on a loan with a credit line
problem: Larkspur Outfitters has \$8,000,000 drawn on a facility with a further \$2,000,000 undrawn. The CCF is 50%, the one-year PD is 2.0% and the LGD is 45% of EAD. Find the EAD, the expected loss and the unexpected loss (LGD fixed).
step: $\text{EAD} = 8{,}000{,}000 + 0.5 \times 2{,}000{,}000 = \$9{,}000{,}000$.
step: $\text{EL} = 0.02 \times 0.45 \times 9{,}000{,}000 = \$81{,}000$.
step: $\text{UL} = 9{,}000{,}000 \times 0.45 \times \sqrt{0.02 \times 0.98} = 4{,}050{,}000 \times 0.14 = \$567{,}000$.
step: UL is about seven times EL: for a single risky loan the uncertainty dwarfs the average.
answer: EAD \$9,000,000; EL \$81,000; UL about \$567,000.

@example
title: Merton default probability
problem: A firm has assets worth 120, zero-coupon debt with face value 100 due in one year, asset volatility 25% and expected asset return 8% a year (real-world). Find the one-year default probability from the Merton model.
step: $\ln(120/100) = 0.18232$ and $\mu - \tfrac12\sigma^2 = 0.08 - 0.03125 = 0.04875$.
step: $d = (0.18232 + 0.04875)/(0.25 \times 1) = 0.9243$.
step: $\text{PD} = N(-0.9243) = 0.1777$, about 17.8%.
answer: About 17.8%.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the undrawn commitment: using only the drawn balance as EAD.
- Using the recovery rate where LGD is needed. LGD is $1 - $ recovery.
- Adding unexpected losses of loans as if they were independent. Only variances add, and only under zero correlation.
- Reading a credit-spread-implied default probability as the real-world probability: it includes a risk premium.

prompt: A borrower has a constant annual default probability of 3% and defaults in different years are independent. What is the probability that it defaults within five years? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 14.13
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1 - (1 - 0.03) ** 5) * 100
hint: Find the probability of surviving all five years.
solution: $P(\text{survive}) = 0.97^5 = 0.8587$, so $\text{cumulative PD} = 1 - 0.8587 = 14.13\%$. Simply multiplying $5 \times 3\% = 15\%$ overstates it because it double counts paths with more than one default year.

model:
```python
def expected_loss_book(loans):
    """Sum of PD * LGD * EAD, with EAD = drawn + ccf * undrawn."""
    total = 0.0
    for drawn, undrawn, ccf, pd, lgd in loans:
        ead = drawn + ccf * undrawn
        total += pd * lgd * ead
    return total
```
