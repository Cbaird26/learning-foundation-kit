@article
A bank's liabilities are mostly short-term deposits and its assets are loans. An insurer is the reverse in one respect: it takes money first (premiums) and pays later, perhaps decades later. That gap creates a pool of investable funds, called **float**, and a different set of risks.

### The main risks of an insurer

- **Underwriting risk**: claims turn out higher than the premiums assumed. **Premium risk** is the risk that claims on new business exceed the price; **reserve risk** is the risk that the **reserves** already set aside for reported and unreported claims prove inadequate (adverse development). For life insurers, underwriting risk includes mortality, longevity (people live longer than assumed) and **lapse** risk (policyholders surrender at unexpected rates).
- **Catastrophe risk**: events such as hurricanes, earthquakes and pandemics produce correlated claims that break the independence on which pooling relies.
- **Market risk**: interest rates, equity prices, credit spreads and property prices change the value of the investment portfolio. For long-dated liabilities, **interest-rate risk** takes the form of an asset-liability mismatch (below).
- **Credit risk**: bond issuers and **reinsurers** may default.
- **Liquidity risk**: a surge of claims, surrenders or collateral calls on derivatives forces asset sales at poor prices.
- **Operational and model risk**: from claims processing to actuarial models (Week 7).

### Underwriting results

The **combined ratio** is the loss ratio plus the expense ratio:

$$\text{combined ratio} = \frac{\text{incurred losses} + \text{expenses}}{\text{earned premiums}}.$$

Below 100% the insurer earns an underwriting profit; above 100% it relies on investment income. Adverse reserve development raises incurred losses.

### Aggregate claims

For a portfolio with a Poisson number of claims, mean $\lambda$ a year, and independent severities $X$ with mean $m$ and second moment $E[X^2]$, aggregate claims $S$ satisfy (as in Week 7)

$$E[S] = \lambda m, \qquad \text{Var}(S) = \lambda\,E[X^2].$$

When $\lambda$ is large, a normal approximation gives a quantile $E[S] + z_c\sqrt{\text{Var}(S)}$. The approximation is poor when severities are very heavy-tailed or $\lambda$ is small. An exponential severity with mean $m$ has $E[X^2] = 2m^2$.

### Reinsurance

**Reinsurance** transfers part of an insurer's risk to a reinsurer. In **quota share** reinsurance the reinsurer takes a fixed percentage of every policy's premiums and losses. In **excess-of-loss** reinsurance the reinsurer pays the part of a loss above a **retention** up to a **limit**:

$$\text{recovery} = \min\left(\max(\text{loss} - \text{retention},\, 0),\ \text{limit}\right),$$

and the insurer keeps the rest. Reinsurance cuts the insurer's tail risk and capital needs but adds reinsurer credit risk and has a cost.

### Asset-liability mismatch

A life insurer may have long-dated liabilities (annuities and whole-life policies) of greater duration than its assets. The surplus (assets minus liabilities) changes by

$$\Delta S \approx -D_A\,\Delta y\,A + D_L\,\Delta y\,L,$$

using modified durations $D_A, D_L$ and market values $A, L$ and a parallel yield change $\Delta y$. If liabilities have the longer duration and values are similar, falling yields hurt the surplus, because liabilities rise more than assets. Insurers manage this by duration matching, cash-flow matching and derivatives, with limits.

### Capital standards

**Solvency II** (European Union) sets a **Solvency Capital Requirement (SCR)** calibrated to a **99.5% value at risk of basic own funds over one year**, so that an insurer should be able to absorb the losses of a 1-in-200 year event. A lower **Minimum Capital Requirement** triggers the strongest supervisory action. The **solvency ratio** is eligible own funds divided by the SCR. In the United States, state regulators apply the NAIC's **risk-based capital (RBC)** formulas, which assign charges to underwriting, asset, credit and other risks; the company's total adjusted capital is compared to the authorized control level, with action levels as the ratio falls (Week 10). The two regimes differ in design and detail, and both change, so check current rules. Accounting for insurance contracts follows IFRS 17 in much of the world.

title: Aggregate claims and a capital quantile
problem: An insurer expects $\lambda = 2{,}000$ claims a year. Claim sizes are exponential with mean \$5,000. Use the compound Poisson formulas and a normal approximation to find the mean, the standard deviation and the 99.5th percentile of annual aggregate claims.
step: Mean: $E[S] = 2{,}000 \times 5{,}000 = \$10{,}000{,}000$.
step: $E[X^2] = 2 \times 5{,}000^2 = 5 \times 10^{7}$, so $\text{Var}(S) = 2{,}000 \times 5 \times 10^{7} = 10^{11}$ and the standard deviation is $\$316{,}228$.
step: With $z_{0.995} = 2.5758$, the 99.5th percentile is $10{,}000{,}000 + 2.5758 \times 316{,}228 = \$10{,}814{,}549$.
step: The capital needed above the expected claims at this level is about \$814,549.
answer: Mean \$10,000,000; standard deviation \$316,228; 99.5th percentile about \$10,814,549.

@example
title: Excess-of-loss reinsurance
problem: A catastrophe contract pays the loss above a retention of \$1,000,000 up to a limit of \$2,000,000 (that is, \$2,000,000 excess of \$1,000,000). A storm causes a loss of \$3,200,000. What does the reinsurer pay, and what does the insurer keep?
step: Loss above the retention: $3{,}200{,}000 - 1{,}000{,}000 = \$2{,}200{,}000$.
step: The limit caps the recovery at \$2,000,000.
step: The insurer keeps $3{,}200{,}000 - 2{,}000{,}000 = \$1{,}200{,}000$: the retention plus the \$200,000 above the exhausted layer.
answer: Reinsurer pays \$2,000,000; insurer keeps \$1,200,000.

@mistakes
- Treating a combined ratio of 98% as a total profit measure: it ignores investment income and the cost of capital.
- Using the standard deviation of one claim in place of $\sqrt{\lambda E[X^2]}$ for the aggregate.
- Forgetting that the insurer keeps the loss above the top of a reinsurance layer.
- Assuming that rising interest rates always help or always hurt: it depends on the durations of the assets and the liabilities.

model:
```python
import math
from statistics import NormalDist


def aggregate_quantile(lam, mean_sev, second_moment_sev, confidence):
    """Normal-approximation quantile of compound Poisson aggregate claims."""
    mean = lam * mean_sev
    std = math.sqrt(lam * second_moment_sev)
    return mean + NormalDist().inv_cdf(confidence) * std
```
hint: Compute the mean and the variance of the aggregate first.
hint: Use the square root of the variance, which is $\lambda$ times the second moment, not the variance of a single claim.
solution: The aggregate mean is $\lambda m$ and its variance is $\lambda E[X^2]$. Add $z$ standard deviations, with $z$ from `NormalDist().inv_cdf(confidence)`. At confidence 0.5 the quantile $z$ is zero and the function returns the mean, which the third test checks; the last test checks that a portfolio with no expected claims has no aggregate loss.

solution: Assets rise by $5.0 \times 0.005 \times 800 = 20$ million and liabilities rise by $8.0 \times 0.005 \times 700 = 28$ million. The surplus changes by $20 - 28 = -8$ million, because the liabilities have the longer duration.
