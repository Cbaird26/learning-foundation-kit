@article
VaR answers a question about ordinary bad days under the statistical patterns of the past. Stress testing asks a different question: what would happen to us if something extreme but plausible occurred, including something that has not happened before? The two are complementary. VaR is calibrated to data and is comparable across firms and days; stress tests explore the tail, nonlinearities, and breaks in correlations that data-based measures miss.

### Types of stress test

- **Sensitivity analysis** shocks one risk factor by a stated amount (yields up 200 basis points, equity prices down 20%), holding everything else fixed.
- **Historical scenarios** replay a past episode (the 1987 stock market crash, the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis) on today's positions.
- **Hypothetical scenarios** are constructed narratives, often macroeconomic: a deep recession with rising unemployment, falling house prices and a sharp drop in equity prices. The macro path is translated into shocks to each risk factor.
- **Reverse stress tests** start from an outcome that would be unacceptable (the bank fails, capital falls below a requirement) and work backward to find the scenarios that would cause it. They expose hidden concentrations.

### How to design a stress test

1. **Define the purpose and the metric**: loss, capital ratio, liquidity coverage, or covenant breach.
2. **Choose the scenario**: severe but plausible, internally consistent (equities do not collapse while credit spreads tighten without a reason), and relevant to the firm's own vulnerabilities. Severity and plausibility trade off.
3. **Map to risk factors**: translate the narrative into shocks to prices, rates, spreads, volatilities and correlations.
4. **Revalue and aggregate**: for a single-factor shock to a linear position, $\Delta V = V \times \text{shock}$; for nonlinear positions use full revaluation, not a delta approximation. Include second-round effects (funding costs, counterparty failures, collateral calls) where material.
5. **Report and act**: compare the result to risk appetite and limits, and decide on actions: hedge, reduce, raise capital, or accept. A stress test that never changes a decision is not working.

### Stressed P&L

For positions with market values $V_i$ and fractional shocks $s_i$, the stressed profit and loss is

$$\Delta V = \sum_i V_i\, s_i,$$

with losses shown as negative P&L in this lesson's tables (elsewhere in this course, losses are positive numbers; say which). A small caution: shocks that are consistent with one another matter more than the shock to any single factor.

### Post-stress capital

For a bank, the key output is the capital ratio after the stress. In simplified form,

$$\text{CET1 ratio}_{stressed} = \frac{\text{CET1} - \text{after-tax stress loss}}{\text{stressed RWA}},$$

where CET1 is common equity tier 1 capital and RWA is risk-weighted assets, which usually grow in a recession as asset quality deteriorates (Week 9). In the United States the Federal Reserve runs annual supervisory stress tests of the largest banks under a severely adverse scenario that it publishes; the results feed each bank's stress capital buffer requirement. The European Banking Authority runs EU-wide stress tests periodically. Scenarios, rules and results change every year, so check the regulators' current publications.

### Limits of stress testing

Scenarios reflect the imagination of their designers, probabilities are not attached to them, and firms may judge them too mild when they are under pressure. A stress test is a conversation about vulnerability, not a forecast.

@example
title: A hypothetical scenario on a four-asset portfolio
problem: A portfolio has these positions and scenario shocks: equities \$5,000,000 (shock -30%), corporate bonds \$3,000,000 (-8%), government bonds \$2,000,000 (+4%), commodities \$1,000,000 (-25%). Find the stressed P&L in dollars and as a share of the \$11,000,000 portfolio.
step: Equities: $5{,}000{,}000 \times (-0.30) = -\$1{,}500{,}000$. Corporate bonds: $3{,}000{,}000 \times (-0.08) = -\$240{,}000$.
step: Government bonds: $2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.04 = +\$80{,}000$. Commodities: $1{,}000{,}000 \times (-0.25) = -\$250{,}000$.
step: Total: $-1{,}500{,}000 - 240{,}000 + 80{,}000 - 250{,}000 = -\$1{,}910{,}000$.
step: As a share of the portfolio: $-1{,}910{,}000/11{,}000{,}000 = -17.36\%$. The government bond gain offsets only a small part of the loss.
answer: Stressed P&L of -\$1,910,000, or -17.36% of the portfolio.

@example
title: Post-stress capital ratio
problem: A bank has CET1 capital of \$12,000 million and RWA of \$100,000 million. In a severely adverse scenario, its pre-tax losses are \$4,500 million, the tax rate on losses is 25%, and RWA rises to \$104,000 million. What is the stressed CET1 ratio? If the bank must keep a CET1 ratio of at least 7.0% (a 4.5% minimum plus a 2.5% conservation buffer, Week 9), does it?
step: After-tax loss: $4{,}500 \times (1 - 0.25) = \$3{,}375$ million.
step: Stressed CET1: $12{,}000 - 3{,}375 = \$8{,}625$ million.
step: Ratio: $8{,}625/104{,}000 = 8.29\%$, compared with the starting ratio of $12{,}000/100{,}000 = 12.0\%$.
step: The stressed ratio is 1.29 percentage points above 7.0%, so the bank stays above the requirement in this scenario, although it uses a large share of its cushion.
answer: 8.29%, above the 7.0% requirement.

@mistakes
- Using only historical scenarios. The next crisis rarely repeats the last one.
- Choosing shocks that are individually severe but inconsistent with each other.
- Forgetting that RWA, and therefore the denominator of the capital ratio, also changes under stress.
- Treating stress results as probabilities or as the worst case. They are plausible illustrations, not bounds.

import math
book = {"equity": 1_000_000, "credit": 500_000, "rates": 250_000}

model:
```python
def stress_pnl(positions, shocks):
    """Total P&L: sum of market value times shock, with missing shocks = 0."""
    return sum(value * shocks.get(name, 0.0)
               for name, value in positions.items())
```
hint: Loop over the positions, not over the shocks, so that shocks for assets you do not hold are ignored.
hint: Use `shocks.get(name, 0.0)` to treat a missing shock as zero.
solution: For each held asset multiply its market value by its shock (zero if there is none) and add up. Looping over the positions rather than the shocks is what makes unheld assets irrelevant. The test with a shock on an asset that is not held catches solutions that raise an error or add the shock anyway.
