@article
Market risk is measured in two complementary ways. **Statistical measures** (volatility, correlation, beta) describe how a portfolio's returns have varied. **Sensitivities** (duration, delta, beta) describe how much the value moves for a small move in one risk factor. Value at risk, next week, combines the two.

### Returns and volatility

The **simple return** over a period is $r_t = P_t/P_{t-1} - 1$ (ignoring dividends), and the **log return** is $\ln(P_t/P_{t-1})$. Log returns add across periods, which makes them convenient for scaling over time. The **volatility** $\sigma$ is the standard deviation of returns, quoted per period; the sample standard deviation divides by $n-1$.

### Scaling volatility: the square-root-of-time rule

If returns in successive periods are independent and identically distributed (iid) with constant variance, the variance of an $h$-period return is $h$ times the one-period variance, so

$$\sigma_h = \sigma_1 \sqrt{h}.$$

A daily volatility becomes annual by multiplying by $\sqrt{252}$ (252 trading days is the usual convention; some desks use 250 or 260). The rule assumes **no autocorrelation** and **constant volatility**. Volatility clusters in real markets and returns can trend or mean-revert, so the rule is an approximation that can understate risk over long horizons in crises. The mean also scales with $h$, not with $\sqrt h$; for short horizons it is small relative to $\sigma\sqrt h$ and is often set to zero.

### Portfolio volatility

For two assets with weights $w_1, w_2$ (summing to 1), volatilities $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$,

$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2.$$

With $\rho < 1$ the portfolio volatility is below the weighted average of the volatilities: this is **diversification**. For many assets the general form is $\sigma_p^2 = \mathbf{w}^{\top}\Sigma\mathbf{w}$, where $\Sigma$ is the covariance matrix.

### Beta

The **beta** of an asset against a market index is $\beta = \text{Cov}(r_i, r_m)/\text{Var}(r_m)$. It is the sensitivity of the asset's return to the market return: a beta of 1.3 means that a 1% market move is associated, on average, with a 1.3% move in the asset.

### Sensitivities for bonds and options

For a bond with price $P$, modified duration $D_{mod}$ and convexity $C$, a small yield change $\Delta y$ changes the price by

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{mod}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}C(\Delta y)^2.$$

The **DV01** (dollar value of an 01) is the price change for a one basis point (0.0001) rise in yield: $\text{DV01} \approx D_{mod} \times P \times 0.0001$. For an option with delta $\Delta$ and gamma $\Gamma$, $\Delta V \approx \Delta\,\Delta S + \tfrac12\Gamma(\Delta S)^2$, where $\Delta S$ is the change in the underlying price. These approximations are accurate for small moves and deteriorate for large ones, which is one reason for stress testing.

@example
title: Volatility of a two-fund portfolio
problem: A portfolio holds 60% in an equity fund with annual volatility 20% and 40% in a bond fund with annual volatility 12%. The correlation between them is 0.3. What is the portfolio's annual volatility?
step: Weighted squares: $0.6^2 \times 0.20^2 = 0.0144$ and $0.4^2 \times 0.12^2 = 0.002304$.
step: Cross term: $2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.3 \times 0.20 \times 0.12 = 0.003456$.
step: Variance is $0.0144 + 0.002304 + 0.003456 = 0.02016$.
step: Volatility is $\sqrt{0.02016} = 14.20\%$, below the weighted average of 16.8%, because the correlation is less than 1.
answer: 14.20% a year.

@example
title: Price change from a yield move
problem: A bond priced at \$102.40 per 100 of par has modified duration 6.8 and convexity 55. Yields rise by 50 basis points. Estimate the new price with duration alone and then with duration and convexity.
step: Duration term: $-6.8 \times 0.005 \times 102.40 = -\$3.4816$.
step: Convexity term: $\tfrac12 \times 55 \times 0.005^2 \times 102.40 = +\$0.0704$.
step: Duration only: $102.40 - 3.4816 = \$98.92$. With convexity: $102.40 - 3.4816 + 0.0704 = \$98.99$.
step: Convexity helps the holder: the true loss from a yield rise is slightly smaller than duration alone predicts.
answer: About \$98.92 with duration alone and \$98.99 with convexity.

@mistakes
- Applying the square-root rule to a volatility without noting that it needs iid returns; it also scales variance, not the mean.
- Adding the volatilities of two assets to get portfolio volatility. Only variances (with the covariance term) combine.
- Using $\Delta y$ in percent where the formula needs a decimal (50 basis points is 0.005, not 0.5).
- Treating a duration-based loss estimate as exact for large yield moves.

prompt: A stock's daily return volatility is 1.4%. Assuming iid returns and 252 trading days a year, what is its annualized volatility? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 22.22
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 1.4 * 252 ** 0.5
hint: Multiply the daily volatility by the square root of the number of days.
solution: $\sigma_{annual} = 1.4\% \times \sqrt{252} = 1.4\% \times 15.8745 = 22.22\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A risk analyst scales a daily volatility to a 10-day volatility by multiplying by $\sqrt{10}$. Which assumption is required for this to be exact?
choice: Daily returns are normally distributed with zero mean
choice*: Daily returns are independent over time with the same variance
choice: Daily returns are perfectly correlated from one day to the next
choice: The portfolio is rebalanced to equal weights every day
hint: The variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of the variances.
solution: The variance of the 10-day return equals ten times the daily variance only if the daily returns are uncorrelated (independent) and have the same variance. Normality is not needed for the scaling of the standard deviation, and perfect correlation would make volatility scale by 10, not by $\sqrt{10}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Over the past two years an asset's returns and the market index's returns have given a covariance of 0.0312 and a market variance of 0.0225 (both annualized). What is the asset's beta? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 1.39
tol: 0.01
check: 0.0312 / 0.0225
hint: Beta is covariance with the market divided by market variance.
solution: $\beta = 0.0312/0.0225 = 1.3867 \approx 1.39$. A 1% market move is associated with about a 1.39% move in the asset.
