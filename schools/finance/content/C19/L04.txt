@article
VaR draws a line and says nothing about what lies beyond it. Two portfolios can have the same 99% VaR of \$1 million while one loses \$1.1 million on a bad day beyond the line and the other loses \$20 million. **Expected shortfall (ES)**, also called conditional VaR or tail VaR, measures what happens in the tail.

### Definition and conventions

For a continuous loss distribution, with losses positive and confidence level $c$ over a stated horizon,

$$\text{ES}_c = E\left[\,L \mid L \ge \text{VaR}_c\,\right] = \frac{1}{1-c}\int_c^1 \text{VaR}_u\,du.$$

It is the average loss on the worst $(1-c)$ share of outcomes. By construction $\text{ES}_c \ge \text{VaR}_c$.

**Normal formula.** If the loss over the horizon is normal with mean $\mu_L$ and standard deviation $\sigma_L$ (losses positive),

$$\text{ES}_c = \mu_L + \sigma_L\,\frac{\varphi(z_c)}{1-c},$$

where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density and $z_c = \Phi^{-1}(c)$. With zero mean, the ratio $\varphi(z_c)/(1-c)$ is 2.0627 at 95%, 2.3378 at 97.5% and 2.6652 at 99%, against $z_c$ of 1.6449, 1.9600 and 2.3263.

**Sample (historical) ES.** Convention used in this course: with $n$ sorted losses, let $k = \lceil (1-c)\,n \rceil$ and take the average of the $k$ largest losses. With $n = 200$ and $c = 0.95$, $k = 10$.

### Why ES is preferred

A risk measure is **coherent** if it is monotonic, translation-invariant, positively homogeneous and **subadditive**: merging portfolios never increases measured risk, $\rho(A+B) \le \rho(A) + \rho(B)$. ES is coherent; VaR satisfies the first three but is not subadditive in general (so it can penalize diversification for some non-normal loss distributions). In the Basel market-risk framework known as the Fundamental Review of the Trading Book (FRTB), ES at the 97.5% level, scaled up for the liquidity horizons of different risk factors, replaces 10-day 99% VaR for internal models. For a normal distribution these two are almost the same size (ES at 97.5% is 2.34 standard deviations; the 99% VaR is 2.33), but ES responds to fat tails where VaR does not. Implementation dates and details vary by jurisdiction, so check the current status.

### Backtesting

A VaR model is only useful if it is tested against outcomes. A **backtest** compares each day's loss with the VaR forecast made the previous day and counts **exceptions** (days when the loss exceeded VaR). If the model is right, the number of exceptions $x$ in $N$ days is binomial with parameter $p = 1 - c$, so the expected count is $Np$. At 99% over 250 days, expect 2.5 exceptions.

**Basel traffic lights.** In the Basel backtesting framework for 99% one-day VaR over the most recent 250 trading days: 0 to 4 exceptions is the **green zone**, 5 to 9 the **yellow zone** (the supervisor may raise the capital multiplier), and 10 or more the **red zone** (the model is presumed to be flawed).

**Kupiec proportion-of-failures test.** The likelihood-ratio statistic

$$LR = -2\ln\!\left[(1-p)^{N-x}p^{x}\right] + 2\ln\!\left[\left(1-\tfrac{x}{N}\right)^{N-x}\left(\tfrac{x}{N}\right)^{x}\right]$$

is approximately chi-square with 1 degree of freedom under the null that the true exception probability is $p$. The 5% critical value is 3.841. A rejection means too many or too few exceptions. Too few can also be a problem: it indicates an overly conservative, capital-hungry model. The test does not check whether exceptions cluster in time, which needs additional tests.

@example
title: Expected shortfall under a normal model
problem: Bellwether Foods' portfolio is worth \$10,000,000 with a daily return volatility of 1.2% and a zero mean. Assuming normal returns, compute the 99% one-day VaR and ES (losses positive).
step: $\text{VaR} = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.012 \times 2.32635 = \$279{,}162$.
step: $\text{ES} = 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.012 \times 2.66521 = \$319{,}826$ (the factor $\varphi(z_c)/(1-c)$ is 2.6652 to four decimals).
step: ES exceeds VaR by about 14.6%: when the 1-in-100 day arrives, the average loss on such days is about \$319,826 under the model.
answer: VaR \$279,162; ES \$319,826.

@example
title: A Kupiec test
problem: A bank's 99% one-day VaR model produced 7 exceptions in 250 days. Test at the 5% level whether the model's exception rate is consistent with 1%.
step: The expected number of exceptions is $250 \times 0.01 = 2.5$, and the observed frequency is $7/250 = 2.8\%$.
step: Compute $LR = -2\ln[0.99^{243}\,0.01^{7}] + 2\ln[(0.972)^{243}(0.028)^{7}] = 5.50$.
step: Since $5.50 > 3.841$, reject the null (the p-value is about 0.019). The model understates risk.
step: Under the Basel traffic lights, 7 exceptions place the model in the yellow zone, which is consistent with the test's warning.
answer: LR = 5.50, reject at 5%; the model understates risk.

@mistakes
- Quoting ES without its confidence level, or comparing an ES at 97.5% with a VaR at 99% without noting what each means.
- Averaging all losses above the sample VaR but using the wrong number of observations: the tail count is $\lceil (1-c)n \rceil$.
- Thinking that fewer exceptions than expected is always good. A model that never fails may hold too much capital against risk it does not have.
- Treating the Kupiec test as proof of a good model: it only checks the exception count, not their timing or size.

model:
```python
import math


def expected_shortfall(losses, confidence):
    """Average of the k largest losses, k = ceil((1 - confidence) * n)."""
    ordered = sorted(losses, reverse=True)
    k = math.ceil(round((1 - confidence) * len(ordered), 9))
    return sum(ordered[:k]) / k
```
hint: Sort a copy in descending order so that the largest losses come first.
hint: Compute $k$ with `math.ceil`, take the first `k` elements and average them.
solution: Sort the losses from largest to smallest, find the tail count $k$ (rounding the product first to avoid floating-point noise at exact integers), and average the first $k$. A typical error is to average the losses above the VaR value without counting correctly, which gives the wrong $k$ when the product $(1-c)n$ is an integer or when $n$ is small.

prompt: A VaR model at 99% confidence is backtested over 250 independent trading days, and an exception occurs on any day with probability 1% if the model is correct. What is the probability of observing 5 or more exceptions (the yellow or red zone)? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 10.78
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1 - sum(comb(250, i) * 0.01 ** i * 0.99 ** (250 - i) for i in range(5))) * 100
hint: Use the binomial distribution with $N = 250$ and $p = 0.01$; it is easier to find $P(X \le 4)$ first.
solution: $P(X \ge 5) = 1 - \sum_{i=0}^{4}\binom{250}{i}0.01^i\,0.99^{250-i} = 1 - 0.8922 = 10.78\%$. Even a correct model has about one chance in nine of leaving the green zone, which is why the zones are a warning and not a verdict.

@quiz num
prompt: A bank's 99% one-day VaR model had 9 exceptions in 500 trading days. Compute the Kupiec proportion-of-failures statistic $LR$ against the null exception probability $p = 0.01$. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 2.61
tol: 0.01
check: -2 * (491 * log(0.99) + 9 * log(0.01)) + 2 * (491 * log(1 - 9 / 500) + 9 * log(9 / 500))
hint: Here $N = 500$, $x = 9$, and the observed frequency is $9/500 = 1.8\%$.
hint: Plug into $LR = -2[(N-x)\ln(1-p) + x\ln p] + 2[(N-x)\ln(1-x/N) + x\ln(x/N)]$.
solution: The null part is $491\ln(0.99) + 9\ln(0.01) = -4.9347 - 41.4465 = -46.3812$ and the alternative part is $491\ln(0.982) + 9\ln(0.018) = -8.9185 - 36.1565 = -45.0750$. Then $LR = -2(-46.3812) + 2(-45.0750) = 2.61$. Since $2.61 < 3.841$, the model is not rejected at 5%.
