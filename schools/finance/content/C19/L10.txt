@article
You cannot predict whether your house will burn down this year, but an insurer who covers 100,000 houses can predict, quite closely, how many will. Insurance is the business of **pooling**: many exposed people pay small, certain premiums, and the insurer pays the few large losses. It converts risk that is hard for one person to bear into a cost that is easy to budget.

### Why pooling works

If $n$ policyholders each have independent, identically distributed annual losses with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, the average loss per policy has mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$. By the **law of large numbers**, as $n$ grows the average becomes predictable. Independence is the key assumption: a hurricane or pandemic hits many policyholders at once and breaks it, which is why insurers limit or reinsure catastrophe exposure.

**Insurable risks** usually share these features: many similar exposure units; a loss that is definite, accidental and measurable in dollars; losses that are not catastrophic to the whole pool; a premium that is affordable relative to the loss; and the insurer able to estimate frequency and severity. **Adverse selection** (people with higher risk are more likely to buy) and **moral hazard** (insured people take less care) threaten the pool, and underwriting, deductibles and exclusions manage them.

### Principles of insurance

- **Insurable interest**: the buyer must stand to suffer a financial loss from the event insured against (you cannot insure your neighbor's house).
- **Indemnity**: property and liability insurance restores the policyholder to the pre-loss financial position, no better; the policy limit caps the payment.
- **Utmost good faith**: the applicant must disclose material facts honestly, and the insurer must deal fairly.
- **Subrogation**: after paying a claim, the insurer takes over the policyholder's right to recover from a party at fault.
- **Contribution**: when several policies cover the same loss, the insurers share it; the policyholder cannot collect more than the loss.

Life insurance is generally treated differently for indemnity: the amount is a stated sum, because a life cannot be restored in money.

### Products

**Property and casualty (P&C)** insurance covers property damage and liability: auto, homeowners, commercial property, general liability, workers' compensation. **Life insurance** includes term insurance (a death benefit for a fixed period), whole life and universal life (permanent coverage with a savings component), and **annuities** (a stream of payments, often guarding longevity risk). **Health insurance** covers medical costs. **Reinsurance** is insurance for insurers (Week 11).

### Policy terms

A **deductible** is the loss the policyholder bears first. A **limit** is the most the insurer pays. In property insurance a **coinsurance clause** requires the owner to carry insurance of at least a stated percentage of the property's value; if the amount carried is lower, the owner bears part of every loss. A common convention, used in this lesson:

$$\text{payment} = \min\left(\text{limit},\ \frac{\text{carried}}{\text{required}} \times \text{loss} - \text{deductible}\right),\qquad \text{required} = \text{coinsurance \%} \times \text{value},$$

with the ratio capped at 1 and the deductible subtracted after the coinsurance penalty. Policy wording varies, so real contracts may differ. (In health insurance, "coinsurance" means something else: the percentage of covered costs the insured pays after the deductible.)

### Pricing

The **pure premium** is the expected loss per policy: frequency times severity. The **gross premium** loads for expenses and profit. With fixed expense $F$ per policy and a variable expense and profit load $v$ as a fraction of the gross premium,

$$G = \frac{\text{pure premium} + F}{1 - v}.$$

The **loss ratio** is losses over premiums, the **expense ratio** is expenses over premiums, and the **combined ratio** is their sum; below 100%, the underwriting is profitable before investment income.

### Regulation

In the United States, insurance is regulated primarily by the states rather than the federal government, which Congress affirmed in the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945. State insurance departments license insurers, review forms and, in many lines, rates, examine financial condition and enforce **risk-based capital** requirements. The National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) coordinates model laws and statutory accounting. State **guaranty associations** pay some claims of failed insurers, within limits that vary by state. The Federal Insurance Office within the Treasury (created in 2010) monitors the sector but is not a general regulator. In the European Union, the **Solvency II** regime has applied since 2016; Week 11 describes its capital standard. Insurers' own financial statements use IFRS 17 for insurance contracts in many jurisdictions outside the United States (effective 2023), and US GAAP inside it. Details change, so check current rules.

@example
title: From pure premium to gross premium
problem: A pet insurer expects a claim frequency of 0.04 per policy per year with an average severity of \$6,500. Fixed expenses are \$40 per policy, and variable expenses and profit together are 27% of the gross premium. Find the pure premium and the gross premium.
step: Pure premium: $0.04 \times 6{,}500 = \$260$.
step: Add fixed expense: $260 + 40 = \$300$.
step: Divide by $1 - 0.27 = 0.73$: $G = 300/0.73 = \$410.96$.
step: Check: 27% of \$410.96 is \$110.96, and \$410.96 - \$110.96 = \$300 covers losses and fixed expense.
answer: Pure premium \$260; gross premium \$410.96.

@example
title: A coinsurance penalty
problem: A building is worth \$500,000. The policy requires 80% coinsurance and carries \$300,000 of coverage, with a \$1,000 deductible. A covered loss of \$100,000 occurs. How much does the insurer pay?
step: Required coverage: $0.80 \times 500{,}000 = \$400{,}000$.
step: Ratio carried to required: $300{,}000/400{,}000 = 0.75$, so the insurer pays 75% of the loss: $0.75 \times 100{,}000 = \$75{,}000$.
step: Subtract the deductible: $75{,}000 - 1{,}000 = \$74{,}000$, which is below the \$300,000 limit.
step: The owner bears \$26,000 of the loss, \$25,000 of it a penalty for underinsurance.
answer: \$74,000.

@mistakes
- Dividing by the variable load in the wrong way: the load is a fraction of the gross premium, so divide by $1 - v$, do not multiply by $1 + v$.
- Applying the coinsurance penalty when the amount carried meets the requirement (the ratio is capped at 1).
- Assuming insurance pays more than the loss (the indemnity principle forbids it).
- Assuming pooling removes all risk. It removes only the diversifiable part; correlated losses such as catastrophes remain.

@practice num
prompt: An insurer has 2,500 independent policies, each with an annual loss that has a standard deviation of \$12,000. What is the standard deviation of the average loss per policy? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 240
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 12000 / sqrt(2500)
hint: The standard deviation of an average of $n$ independent losses is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: $12{,}000/\sqrt{2{,}500} = 12{,}000/50 = \$240$, one-fiftieth of the individual standard deviation. Quadrupling the pool would only halve it again.
