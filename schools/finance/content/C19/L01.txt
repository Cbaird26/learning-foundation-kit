@article
A bakery owner can be sure of one thing about next year: not everything will go as planned. An oven may fail, a flour supplier may raise prices, a customer may not pay. **Risk** is exposure to a loss, or to an outcome worse than expected, whose likelihood and size are uncertain. Risk management does not remove uncertainty. It makes the uncertainty visible, measured and deliberate, so that the firm takes the risks it is paid to take and avoids or shares the rest.

### Risk is not the same as loss

A **loss** is an outcome. A **risk** is the possibility of that outcome. Finance usually works with risks whose probabilities can at least be estimated from data or judgment. Outcomes that cannot be assigned probabilities at all are sometimes called Knightian uncertainty, and good risk managers keep that humility in mind: every model in this course is an estimate.

### The main categories of financial risk

| Category | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Market risk** | Loss from changes in market prices: equity prices, interest rates, exchange rates, commodity prices, volatilities | A bond fund falls when yields rise |
| **Credit risk** | Loss because a borrower or counterparty fails to pay what it owes | A company defaults on a loan |
| **Liquidity risk** | Inability to meet cash needs when due (funding liquidity), or inability to sell an asset quickly without a large price concession (market liquidity) | A fund cannot sell a thinly traded bond without cutting the price |
| **Operational risk** | Loss from inadequate or failed internal processes, people and systems, or from external events | A payment is sent twice because of a software error |
| **Model risk** | Loss from decisions based on a model that is wrong, misused or built on bad data | A pricing model understates the value of a guarantee |
| **Business (strategic) risk** | Loss from poor strategic decisions or changes in the competitive environment | Customers move to a rival product |

The categories overlap. A bank's loan loss is credit risk, but if the credit model was flawed it is also model risk, and if the bank cannot fund itself while it waits for repayment it is also a liquidity problem.

### The risk management process

Most frameworks describe the same loop:

1. **Identify** the risks: workshops, loss histories, process maps, checklists.
2. **Measure** them: likelihood and size, using the tools of the following weeks.
3. **Treat** them: choose a response (below).
4. **Monitor and report**: track key indicators, test whether the treatment works, and revisit the list.

### Frequency, severity and expected loss

Two numbers describe many risks. **Frequency** is the expected number of loss events per year, and **severity** is the average loss per event. When events are rare enough that frequency is close to the annual probability of one event, the **expected annual loss** is

$$\text{EL} = \text{frequency} \times \text{severity}.$$

Expected loss is the average cost of the risk, not the worst case. Risks with the same EL can behave very differently: a frequent small loss is predictable, while a rare catastrophic loss may be the one that ends the firm. That is why later weeks measure the tail as well.

### The four basic treatments

- **Avoid**: do not take the risk (do not enter the business).
- **Reduce (mitigate)**: lower the frequency or the severity with controls such as sprinklers, backups or diversification.
- **Transfer**: shift the financial consequence to another party by buying insurance, hedging with derivatives, or signing a contract that allocates the loss.
- **Retain (accept)**: bear the loss yourself, ideally funded by reserves or capital.

A control is worthwhile, in expected-value terms, when the reduction in expected loss exceeds its cost. Expected value is only part of the decision: a firm may pay to transfer a rare loss that it cannot afford to retain, even when the premium exceeds the expected loss.

@example
title: Ranking three risks by expected loss
problem: Larkspur Outfitters faces three risks. A warehouse fire has an annual probability of 0.015 and a severity of \$2,400,000. A key supplier failure happens 0.10 times a year on average and costs \$350,000. A data breach happens 0.20 times a year and costs \$500,000. Compute each expected annual loss and the total.
step: Fire: $0.015 \times 2{,}400{,}000 = \$36{,}000$.
step: Supplier failure: $0.10 \times 350{,}000 = \$35{,}000$.
step: Data breach: $0.20 \times 500{,}000 = \$100{,}000$.
step: Total expected annual loss is $36{,}000 + 35{,}000 + 100{,}000 = \$171{,}000$. The data breach has the largest EL even though the fire has the largest severity.
answer: Fire \$36,000; supplier \$35,000; breach \$100,000; total \$171,000.

@example
title: Is a control worth its cost?
problem: Larkspur considers a sprinkler upgrade costing \$9,000 a year that cuts the annual fire probability from 0.015 to 0.006. The fire severity is \$2,400,000. What is the net expected annual benefit?
step: Expected loss before the upgrade is $0.015 \times 2{,}400{,}000 = \$36{,}000$.
step: Expected loss after the upgrade is $0.006 \times 2{,}400{,}000 = \$14{,}400$.
step: The reduction in expected loss is $36{,}000 - 14{,}400 = \$21{,}600$.
step: Net benefit is $21{,}600 - 9{,}000 = \$12{,}600$ a year, so the control passes the expected-value test.
answer: \$12,600 a year.

@mistakes
- Treating expected loss as the worst case. It is an average; the tail is measured separately.
- Mixing up frequency (events per year) with severity (cost per event) and multiplying the wrong pair.
- Putting every risk in one category. A single event often has market, credit, liquidity and operational aspects.
- Assuming that transferring a risk removes it. Insurance moves the loss but leaves the insurer's credit risk and any exclusions.

@practice num
prompt: Two independent events can each cause a loss in a given year: a plant outage with probability 5% and a cyber incident with probability 8%. What is the probability that at least one of them occurs in the year? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 12.6
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (1 - (1 - 0.05) * (1 - 0.08)) * 100
hint: It is easier to find the probability that neither event occurs.
hint: With independence, $P(\text{neither}) = (1 - 0.05)(1 - 0.08)$.
solution: $P(\text{neither}) = 0.95 \times 0.92 = 0.874$, so $P(\text{at least one}) = 1 - 0.874 = 12.6\%$. Adding 5% and 8% would give 13%, which double counts the case where both occur.
