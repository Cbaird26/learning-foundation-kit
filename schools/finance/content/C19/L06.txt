@article
Two ways to lose money have little to do with price moves. A counterparty can fail to honor a contract that is worth money to you, and you can find yourself unable to turn assets into cash at a fair price when you need it. Both were central in the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis, and in the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in March 2023 a run by depositors, many uninsured, drained its cash faster than it could sell assets.

### Counterparty credit risk

**Counterparty credit risk (CCR)** is the risk that the other party to a derivative or repurchase agreement defaults while the contract has positive value to you. It differs from lending risk in three ways. The exposure is **contingent** (your claim exists only when the contract's market value is positive), it is **uncertain** (it moves with market prices), and it is **two-sided** (the contract can be an asset for one side and a liability for the other, and either side can default).

The **current exposure** to one trade is $\max(V, 0)$, where $V$ is the mark-to-market value to you.

**Netting.** Under a legally enforceable master agreement (for example an ISDA Master Agreement), if the counterparty defaults all trades are closed out and only the net amount is owed. The exposure to a netting set is $\max\left(\sum_i V_i, 0\right)$, never more than the sum of the positive values $\sum_i \max(V_i, 0)$.

**Collateral.** Margin agreements require the party owing the net amount to post collateral. Collateral reduces exposure, but not to zero: there is a delay between a default and the sale of collateral during which values move (the margin period of risk), and the collateral itself can lose value.

**Central clearing.** A central counterparty (CCP) stands between the two sides of a standardized contract, collects margin and mutualizes losses in a default, concentrating counterparty risk in the CCP.

### Credit valuation adjustment

**CVA** is the market price of counterparty credit risk: the expected loss, discounted, from the counterparty's default over the life of the contract. A common discretized form, with exposure assumed independent of default, is

$$\text{CVA} = \text{LGD} \times \sum_{i=1}^{n} DF_i \times EE_i \times PD_i,$$

where $EE_i$ is the **expected (positive) exposure** in period $i$, $PD_i$ is the **marginal** default probability in period $i$ (the probability of default during that period, not the cumulative one), $DF_i$ is the discount factor, and LGD is the fraction of exposure lost on default. This is a unilateral CVA: it ignores the possibility that you default yourself. **Wrong-way risk** arises when exposure and default are positively dependent (a counterparty that sells you protection against its own country's default, for example): then the independence assumption understates CVA. Basel III added a capital charge for CVA risk after the crisis, because most of the losses in 2007 to 2009 came from fair-value losses on counterparties' deteriorating credit rather than from outright defaults.

### Liquidity risk

**Funding liquidity risk** is the risk of being unable to meet cash obligations as they fall due without unacceptable losses. **Market liquidity risk** is the risk that a position cannot be sold quickly without moving the price. The two interact: a funding squeeze forces fire sales, and fire sales depress prices and trigger margin calls.

**Liquidity-adjusted VaR.** A simple adjustment adds the cost of unwinding at the bid-ask spread. With a relative quoted spread $s$ (as a fraction of the mid price) and position value $V$, selling at the bid costs half the spread:

$$\text{LVaR} = \text{VaR} + \tfrac{1}{2}\,s\,V.$$

This is an exogenous-liquidity adjustment: it assumes the position is small enough that selling does not move the price. Large positions also require a longer liquidation horizon, which raises VaR through the square-root-of-time scaling of Week 2.

**Basel III liquidity standards.** The **liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)** is

$$\text{LCR} = \frac{\text{stock of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA)}}{\text{total net cash outflows over the next 30 calendar days under stress}} \ge 100\%,$$

where net outflows are stressed outflows minus stressed inflows, with inflows capped at 75% of outflows. The **net stable funding ratio (NSFR)** is available stable funding divided by required stable funding over a one-year horizon, also with a minimum of 100%. Supervisors also monitor funding concentration and contingency funding plans.

@example
title: Computing a CVA
problem: A dealer has an interest-rate swap with a counterparty. In each of the next three years the expected positive exposure is \$2.0 million, \$3.1 million and \$2.5 million, the counterparty's marginal default probabilities are 1.0%, 1.2% and 1.5%, and the discount factors are 0.980, 0.955 and 0.930. LGD is 60%. Compute the CVA, assuming exposure and default are independent.
step: Year 1: $0.010 \times 2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.980 = \$19{,}600$.
step: Year 2: $0.012 \times 3{,}100{,}000 \times 0.955 = \$35{,}526$.
step: Year 3: $0.015 \times 2{,}500{,}000 \times 0.930 = \$34{,}875$. The sum is \$90,001.
step: Multiply by LGD: $\text{CVA} = 0.60 \times 90{,}001 = \$54{,}001$, the expected cost of the counterparty's credit risk, to be deducted from the swap's value.
answer: CVA is about \$54,001.

@example
title: The effect of netting
problem: A bank has four trades with one counterparty under a netting agreement, with mark-to-market values (in millions of dollars) of +5.0, -3.0, +2.0 and -1.5. Compare the exposure with and without netting.
step: Without netting, the exposure is the sum of the positive values: $5.0 + 2.0 = \$7.0$ million.
step: With netting, the exposure is $\max(5.0 - 3.0 + 2.0 - 1.5,\,0) = \$2.5$ million.
step: Netting reduces the exposure by \$4.5 million, but only if close-out netting is legally enforceable in the counterparty's jurisdiction.
answer: \$7.0 million without netting and \$2.5 million with netting.

@mistakes
- Using cumulative default probabilities in the CVA sum where marginal (per-period) probabilities belong.
- Counting every trade's positive value in the exposure when a netting agreement applies, or applying netting where it is not enforceable.
- Treating collateral as eliminating exposure; delays and collateral price changes leave a residual.
- Adding the full bid-ask spread (instead of half) to the liquidation cost of a position valued at mid.

prompt: A dealer has four trades with one counterparty with mark-to-market values (in millions of dollars) of +6.0, -2.5, +1.5 and -4.0, covered by an enforceable netting agreement. By how much, in millions of dollars, does netting reduce the exposure compared with summing the positive values? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 6.5
tol: 0.1
check: sum(x for x in [6.0, -2.5, 1.5, -4.0] if x > 0) - max(sum([6.0, -2.5, 1.5, -4.0]), 0)
hint: Compute the sum of the positive values, then the net value of all four trades.
solution: Without netting the exposure is $6.0 + 1.5 = 7.5$ million. The net value is $6.0 - 2.5 + 1.5 - 4.0 = 1.0$ million, positive, so the netted exposure is 1.0 million. The reduction is 6.5 million.

@practice num
prompt: A bank has HQLA of 540 (million dollars), stressed cash outflows of 900 and stressed cash inflows of 300 over the next 30 days. Inflows are counted up to a cap of 75% of outflows. What is the LCR? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 90.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 540 / (900 - min(300, 0.75 * 900)) * 100
hint: First check whether the inflow cap binds: 75% of 900 is 675.
hint: Net outflows are outflows minus the counted inflows.
solution: The cap is $0.75 \times 900 = 675$, above the inflows of 300, so all 300 count. Net outflows are $900 - 300 = 600$ and $\text{LCR} = 540/600 = 90.0\%$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following statements about counterparty and liquidity risk are correct?
choice*: Counterparty credit exposure on a derivative is contingent: it exists only when the contract's value to you is positive
choice*: Enforceable close-out netting can substantially reduce exposure to a counterparty
choice*: Funding liquidity risk and market liquidity risk can reinforce each other through fire sales and margin calls
choice: Posting collateral removes all counterparty credit risk
choice: The liquidity coverage ratio measures a bank's capital adequacy
hint: Collateral is subject to delays and price changes, and the LCR concerns cash, not capital.
solution: Derivative exposure is $\max(V, 0)$, netting cuts it to the net positive value, and funding stress can force sales that deepen market illiquidity. Collateral reduces but does not eliminate exposure, and the LCR is a liquidity measure, not a capital measure.
