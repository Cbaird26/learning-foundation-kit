@article
Not every loss comes from a market move or a borrower. A trader keys in the wrong quantity, a server fails during month-end processing, an employee commits fraud, a hurricane floods a data center. Risk managers also worry about the opposite problem: the numbers they use to measure risk may themselves be wrong.

### Operational risk

The Basel Committee defines **operational risk** as the risk of loss resulting from inadequate or failed internal processes, people and systems, or from external events. The definition includes legal risk and excludes strategic and reputational risk. The Committee classifies loss events into seven **event types**:

1. Internal fraud
2. External fraud
3. Employment practices and workplace safety
4. Clients, products and business practices
5. Damage to physical assets
6. Business disruption and system failures
7. Execution, delivery and process management

Operational losses are usually **low frequency, high severity** at the extreme end: many small errors and a few giant ones. Data are scarce for the largest events, and risks are hard to diversify.

### The loss distribution approach

A standard quantitative model combines two distributions for each risk class:

- **Frequency**: the number of events $N$ in a year, often Poisson with mean $\lambda$, so $P(N = k) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$.
- **Severity**: the size $X$ of each loss, often lognormal: $\ln X$ is normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Then $E[X] = e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$ and $E[X^2] = e^{2\mu + 2\sigma^2}$.

The **aggregate annual loss** is $S = X_1 + \dots + X_N$. If severities are independent of each other and of $N$ (a compound Poisson process),

$$E[S] = \lambda\,E[X], \qquad \text{Var}(S) = \lambda\,E[X^2].$$

A tail measure such as the 99.9% quantile of $S$ is usually found by Monte Carlo simulation. The expected loss is the cost of doing business, while the tail is what capital must cover. The estimate is highly sensitive to the heaviness of the severity tail, which is hard to measure from few observations.

### Regulation of operational risk capital

Under the Basel framework, operational risk is part of the Pillar 1 minimum capital requirement alongside credit and market risk. Earlier approaches, including advanced internal-model approaches, were replaced in the final Basel III reforms by a single standardized approach, which scales a measure of a bank's income (the business indicator) by a factor and a multiplier that reflects the bank's own loss history. Implementation timing varies by jurisdiction, so check the current rules.

### Model risk

**Model risk** is the potential for adverse consequences from decisions based on incorrect or misused model outputs. It arises from:

- **Model error**: wrong assumptions, a poor functional form, coding mistakes (a spreadsheet formula that ignores some rows).
- **Parameter and data error**: noisy estimates, stale data, data that omit a crisis.
- **Misuse**: applying a model outside the range for which it was built, or ignoring its limitations.

Because most risk measures are proportional to an estimated volatility, a mis-estimated volatility scales the error one for one. If a parametric VaR uses $\sigma_{used}$ but the true volatility is $\sigma_{true}$, the VaR is understated by the fraction $1 - \sigma_{used}/\sigma_{true}$.

In the United States, the Federal Reserve and the OCC issued supervisory guidance on model risk management in April 2011 (known as SR 11-7). Its main themes: sound model **development, implementation and use**; independent **validation** (conceptual soundness, ongoing monitoring, and outcomes analysis such as backtesting); and **governance** with a model inventory, documentation and clear roles. A core principle is **effective challenge**: critical analysis by informed people with the incentives and authority to push for change. Model risk cannot be eliminated. It is managed with simple benchmark models, conservative adjustments for uncertainty, limits on use, and human judgment.

@example
title: A loss-distribution estimate
problem: For one operational risk class, annual event counts are Poisson with $\lambda = 8$, and loss severities are lognormal with $\mu = 10$ and $\sigma = 1.2$. Find the expected severity, the expected annual aggregate loss and its standard deviation.
step: $E[X] = e^{10 + 1.2^2/2} = e^{10.72} = \$45{,}252$.
step: $E[S] = 8 \times 45{,}252 = \$362{,}015$.
step: $E[X^2] = e^{2(10) + 2(1.44)} = e^{22.88}$, so $\text{Var}(S) = 8\,e^{22.88}$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{8\,e^{22.88}} = \$262{,}950$.
step: The standard deviation of the annual total is not far below the expected loss, which signals a heavy right tail: some years will be much worse than average.
answer: E[X] about \$45,252; E[S] about \$362,015; standard deviation about \$262,950.

@example
title: The cost of a mis-estimated volatility
problem: A desk computes a 99% one-day parametric VaR (zero mean, losses positive) on an \$8,000,000 portfolio using a daily volatility of 0.9%, but the true volatility is 1.2%. How much is the VaR understated?
step: Reported VaR: $8{,}000{,}000 \times 2.3263 \times 0.009 = \$167{,}497$.
step: Correct VaR: $8{,}000{,}000 \times 2.3263 \times 0.012 = \$223{,}329$.
step: Understatement: $223{,}329 - 167{,}497 = \$55{,}832$, which is $1 - 0.9/1.2 = 25\%$ of the correct VaR.
step: A 25% error in the volatility input becomes a 25% error in capital and in limits.
answer: Understated by about \$55,832, or 25%.

@mistakes
- Assuming that operational risk includes reputational and strategic risk. The Basel definition excludes them.
- Using $e^{\mu}$ as the mean of a lognormal severity. The mean is $e^{\mu + \sigma^2/2}$, which is larger.
- Believing that validated models are safe. Validation reduces model risk but does not remove it.
- Computing the standard deviation of aggregate loss as $\sqrt{\lambda}\,\sigma_X$; with random counts the correct variance uses $\lambda\,E[X^2]$.

@practice num
prompt: A control failure happens a random number of times a year, modeled as Poisson with mean 3. What is the probability of at least two failures in a year? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 80.09
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1 - exp(-3) * (1 + 3)) * 100
hint: Find $P(N = 0)$ and $P(N = 1)$ and subtract them from 1.
solution: $P(N=0) = e^{-3} = 0.0498$ and $P(N=1) = 3e^{-3} = 0.1494$. So $P(N \ge 2) = 1 - 0.1991 = 80.09\%$.

@practice num
prompt: A risk model for a \$5,000,000 portfolio uses a daily volatility of 1.0%, but the true daily volatility is 1.6%. By what percent is the parametric VaR (at any confidence level, zero mean) understated, as a percentage of the correct VaR? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 37.5
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (1 - 1.0 / 1.6) * 100
hint: VaR is proportional to the volatility used, so the confidence level cancels.
solution: The reported VaR is proportional to 1.0 and the correct VaR to 1.6, so the understatement is $1 - 1.0/1.6 = 37.5\%$ of the correct VaR. For example, at 99% the reported VaR is \$116,317 against a correct \$186,108.
