@article
A bank funds loans and securities mostly with deposits and other borrowing, and only a thin layer with its owners' money. When loans go bad, that layer of **capital** absorbs the losses before depositors and other creditors do. Bank capital rules set how thick the layer must be relative to the risk the bank takes. Because bank failures can spread through the financial system, the rules are set internationally and applied by national supervisors.

### Who sets the rules

The **Basel Committee on Banking Supervision**, hosted by the Bank for International Settlements, writes international standards that national authorities then adopt in their own law. In the United States the Federal Reserve, the OCC and the FDIC write and enforce capital rules. The Committee's accords are known as Basel I (1988, an 8% total capital ratio against credit risk), Basel II (2004, adding market and operational risk and the three pillars) and Basel III (agreed after the 2007 to 2009 crisis, with further reforms finalized in 2017). Adoption dates and national details differ, and in the United States the final stage of Basel III has been subject to proposals and revisions, so check the regulators' current rules.

### The components of capital

- **Common equity tier 1 (CET1)**: common shares and retained earnings, less regulatory deductions such as goodwill and other intangibles. It is the highest-quality, loss-absorbing capital.
- **Additional tier 1 (AT1)**: perpetual instruments with no obligatory payments that can absorb losses (by conversion or write-down) while the bank is still operating.
- **Tier 2**: supplementary capital, such as subordinated debt with an original maturity of at least five years, that absorbs losses in resolution.

**Tier 1 capital** = CET1 + AT1. **Total capital** = tier 1 + tier 2.

### Risk-weighted assets

A \$100 loan to a government is not as risky as a \$100 unsecured loan to a small business. **Risk-weighted assets (RWA)** scale each exposure by a **risk weight**:

$$\text{RWA}_{credit} = \sum_i \text{exposure}_i \times \text{risk weight}_i.$$

Under the **standardized approach**, regulators prescribe weights by exposure class (and, depending on the jurisdiction and Basel version, by external credit rating or other features). Under the **internal ratings-based (IRB)** approach, banks with supervisory approval estimate PD (and, in the advanced approach, LGD and EAD, as in Week 5) and a supervisory formula turns them into a risk weight. The final Basel III reforms also introduced an **output floor**, which limits how far internally modeled RWA can fall below standardized RWA (72.5% of the standardized figure, phased in). Risk weights in this lesson's problems are **hypothetical**; real weights vary by jurisdiction and exposure.

Market risk and operational risk are added using capital charges converted to RWA: since the minimum total capital ratio was 8%, $\text{RWA} = 12.5 \times \text{capital charge}$ ($1/0.08 = 12.5$). Total RWA is the sum of credit, market and operational RWA.

### The ratios and Basel III minimums

$$\text{CET1 ratio} = \frac{\text{CET1}}{\text{RWA}}, \quad \text{Tier 1 ratio} = \frac{\text{Tier 1}}{\text{RWA}}, \quad \text{Total capital ratio} = \frac{\text{Total capital}}{\text{RWA}}.$$

The Basel III minimums are 4.5% (CET1), 6% (tier 1) and 8% (total capital) of RWA. On top of the minimum, banks must hold:

- a **capital conservation buffer** of 2.5% of RWA in CET1,
- a **countercyclical capital buffer** set by national authorities, between 0% and 2.5%, raised when credit is growing too fast,
- a **surcharge for global systemically important banks (G-SIBs)** that rises with the bank's systemic importance.

Falling into the buffer range does not close a bank, but it restricts dividends, share buybacks and bonuses.

The **leverage ratio** is a simple backstop that ignores risk weights: $\text{Leverage ratio} = \text{Tier 1 capital}/\text{total exposure}$, with a Basel III minimum of 3% (higher for G-SIBs). It guards against models that make risk weights too low.

### The three pillars

**Pillar 1** sets minimum capital for credit, market and operational risk. **Pillar 2** is supervisory review: the bank assesses its own capital needs and supervisors may require more. **Pillar 3** is market discipline through public disclosure of risks and capital.

@example
title: Capital ratios for a bank
problem: A bank has these credit exposures with hypothetical risk weights (in millions of dollars): government bonds 20,000 at 0%; interbank deposits 10,000 at 50%; residential mortgages 40,000 at 35%; corporate loans 50,000 at 100%. Its market-risk capital charge is 800 and its operational-risk capital charge is 1,200. CET1 is 9,400, AT1 is 1,500 and tier 2 is 2,600. Compute RWA and the three capital ratios.
step: Credit RWA: $0 + 5{,}000 + 14{,}000 + 50{,}000 = 69{,}000$.
step: Market RWA: $800 \times 12.5 = 10{,}000$. Operational RWA: $1{,}200 \times 12.5 = 15{,}000$. Total RWA is $94{,}000$.
step: CET1 ratio $= 9{,}400/94{,}000 = 10.00\%$. Tier 1 $= 10{,}900$ and the ratio is $11.60\%$. Total capital $= 13{,}500$ and the ratio is $14.36\%$.
step: All three exceed the minimums of 4.5%, 6% and 8% by wide margins.
answer: RWA 94,000; CET1 ratio 10.00%, tier 1 ratio 11.60%, total capital ratio 14.36%.

@example
title: Distance from the buffer requirement
problem: The bank above has total RWA of 94,000 and a CET1 ratio of 10.0%. Suppose its CET1 requirement is the 4.5% minimum, a 2.5% conservation buffer, a hypothetical 1.0% G-SIB surcharge and a hypothetical 0.5% countercyclical buffer. How much CET1 capital does it hold above the full requirement, and what leverage ratio does it have if total exposure is 130,000?
step: Requirement: $4.5\% + 2.5\% + 1.0\% + 0.5\% = 8.5\%$.
step: Headroom: $(10.0\% - 8.5\%) \times 94{,}000 = 1{,}410$ million.
step: Leverage ratio: Tier 1 $/$ exposure $= 10{,}900/130{,}000 = 8.38\%$, well above the 3% minimum.
answer: CET1 headroom of 1,410 million; leverage ratio 8.38%.

@mistakes
- Dividing capital by total assets instead of risk-weighted assets for the risk-based ratios.
- Adding capital charges to RWA without multiplying by 12.5 (or dividing RWA by 12.5 when converting back).
- Counting all capital as equal. Only CET1 counts toward the CET1 ratio.
- Thinking the buffers are hard minimums that trigger closure. They trigger restrictions on distributions, with supervisory consequences.
