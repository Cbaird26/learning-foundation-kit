@article
Over the past eleven weeks you measured risks one at a time. Real firms face them together, and the largest failures were rarely a measurement problem alone. They were failures of governance, incentives and culture that let known risks grow unchecked. **Enterprise risk management (ERM)** is the practice of managing all of a firm's material risks together, in line with its strategy and objectives.

### Frameworks

The COSO framework, *Enterprise Risk Management: Integrating with Strategy and Performance* (2017), organizes ERM into five components: governance and culture; strategy and objective-setting; performance; review and revision; and information, communication and reporting. **ISO 31000** is an international standard of risk management principles and guidelines. Neither is a regulation; both are widely used as references. Large banks supervised in the United States must also have a board-level risk committee and a chief risk officer, and similar requirements exist elsewhere.

### Risk appetite, limits and indicators

- **Risk capacity** is the maximum risk the firm can bear without breaching regulatory or financial constraints.
- **Risk appetite** is the amount and type of risk the board is willing to take to pursue its strategy, expressed in a **risk appetite statement** with measurable metrics (for example, a CET1 ratio above a target in a stress scenario, or a one-day 99% VaR below a number).
- **Limits** convert appetite into daily constraints for business units (VaR limits, concentration limits, single-name exposure limits). **Limit utilization** is the current risk measure divided by the limit.
- **Key risk indicators (KRIs)** are early-warning metrics with thresholds (green, amber, red): failed trades, staff turnover, overdue audit findings, limit breaches.

### Governance: the three lines model

The Institute of Internal Auditors' Three Lines Model (2020) describes who does what. The **first line** (business management) owns and manages risk. The **second line** (risk management and compliance) provides expertise, sets policies, monitors and challenges. The **third line** (internal audit) gives independent assurance to the board. The **board** sets the appetite and oversees the whole. Independence matters: the people who measure and limit risk must not be paid solely on the profit of the activity they monitor.

### Risk-adjusted performance

A business that earns high returns with high hidden risk looks good until the risk materializes. **RAROC (risk-adjusted return on capital)** charges for expected loss and divides by the capital that risk requires:

$$\text{RAROC} = \frac{\text{revenue} - \text{costs} - \text{expected loss}}{\text{economic capital}}.$$

(This course's convention is pre-tax; some firms use after-tax versions.) **Economic capital** is the capital needed to absorb unexpected losses at a confidence level chosen to match the firm's target credit standing, typically a very high level such as 99.9% over one year. A unit creates value if its RAROC exceeds the firm's **hurdle rate**.

### Allocating risk: component VaR

Portfolio VaR is not the sum of stand-alone VaRs, so how much of it does each position cause? For parametric normal VaR with zero mean, let $a_i = V_i\sigma_i$ be the dollar volatility of position $i$ and $\sigma_p$ the portfolio dollar volatility. The **component VaR** of position $i$ is

$$\text{CVaR}_i = z_c\,\frac{a_i\sum_j \rho_{ij}a_j}{\sigma_p},$$

and by Euler's theorem $\sum_i \text{CVaR}_i = z_c\,\sigma_p = \text{VaR}$ exactly. A position's share of the total can be larger or smaller than its share of the capital invested: a hedge has a negative or small component.

### Culture

**Risk culture** is the shared set of attitudes and behaviors toward risk: whether staff feel free to raise concerns, whether bad news travels upward, whether pay rewards risk-taking without penalties for losses. Well-documented failures show recurring themes. In 1998 a highly leveraged hedge fund, Long-Term Capital Management, needed a private-sector recapitalization after correlations that its models had treated as stable moved together. In 2008 the insurer AIG, through a financial products unit that had sold large amounts of credit protection through credit default swaps, faced collateral calls that it could not meet, and received federal assistance. Lehman Brothers, which failed in September 2008, relied on short-term repurchase funding against illiquid assets and large real estate positions. In March 2023 Silicon Valley Bank failed after losses on a large securities portfolio, a concentration of uninsured deposits and a rapid run. In each case, models did not capture the interaction of leverage, liquidity and concentration, and governance did not impose a limit that would have forced action in time.

@example
title: RAROC for two business units
problem: A bank's corporate lending unit has revenue of \$85 million, operating costs of \$40 million, expected credit losses of \$12 million and economic capital of \$150 million. Its trading unit has revenue of \$50 million, costs of \$30 million, expected loss of \$6 million and economic capital of \$70 million. The hurdle rate is 12%. Compute each RAROC.
step: Lending: $(85 - 40 - 12)/150 = 33/150 = 22.0\%$.
step: Trading: $(50 - 30 - 6)/70 = 14/70 = 20.0\%$.
step: Both exceed the 12% hurdle. Trading has a smaller profit in dollars but about the same return per unit of capital.
answer: Lending 22.0%; trading 20.0%; both above the hurdle.

@example
title: Component VaR
problem: A book has two positions: \$6,000,000 in asset A with a daily volatility of 1.0%, and \$4,000,000 in asset B with a daily volatility of 2.0%, with correlation 0.25. Using the 99% one-day parametric VaR with zero mean (losses positive), find the portfolio VaR and each position's component VaR.
step: Dollar volatilities: $a_A = 60{,}000$ and $a_B = 80{,}000$. The portfolio variance is $60{,}000^2 + 80{,}000^2 + 2(0.25)(60{,}000)(80{,}000) = 1.24\times 10^{10}$, so $\sigma_p = \$111{,}355$.
step: Portfolio VaR $= 2.3263 \times 111{,}355 = \$259{,}051$.
step: $\text{CVaR}_A = 2.3263 \times (60{,}000^2 + 0.25 \times 60{,}000 \times 80{,}000)/111{,}355 = 2.3263 \times 43{,}105 = \$100{,}278$.
step: $\text{CVaR}_B = 2.3263 \times (80{,}000^2 + 0.25 \times 60{,}000 \times 80{,}000)/111{,}355 = 2.3263 \times 68{,}250 = \$158{,}773$. The sum is \$259,051, and A causes 38.7% of the risk with 60% of the money.
answer: VaR \$259,051; component VaRs \$100,278 (A) and \$158,773 (B).

@mistakes
- Defining risk appetite as a vague aspiration. It needs metrics, limits and owners.
- Letting the people who take the risk also be the only people who measure and limit it.
- Using a hurdle rate comparison without charging for expected loss and for the capital the risk requires.
- Allocating portfolio VaR in proportion to stand-alone VaR or to money invested, which ignores correlations.

@practice self
prompt: Choose one well-documented risk failure from the course (for example the failure of Silicon Valley Bank in 2023, or the 2008 crisis episode at AIG or Lehman Brothers). In about 200 words, explain what risks were taken, which risk measures or controls failed or were missing, which lesson from this course applies, and one change in governance that could have helped.
hint: Identify the risk categories involved, then ask which limit, measure, stress test or incentive should have raised a flag.
solution: Silicon Valley Bank (March 2023): the bank's deposits grew very fast and were concentrated in uninsured balances from a connected group of depositors. It invested heavily in long-dated securities, so a rise in interest rates left large unrealized losses (interest-rate risk and market risk) while its funding was short-term and prone to a run (liquidity risk). Measures and controls: interest-rate risk was understated or left unhedged, and a stress test with a rise in rates combined with deposit outflows would have shown the vulnerability (Week 8); a liquidity coverage test (Week 6) should have flagged the concentration. Lessons: risk categories interact, and stress tests must combine market and liquidity shocks. Governance change: an empowered chief risk officer and board risk committee with limits on duration and on uninsured-deposit concentration, with breaches escalated to the board.
