Suppose two professionals each help you invest. One earns a percentage of the assets she manages for you. The other earns a commission each time you buy a product. Both may say "I'm here to help you," but the law asks different things of them. This lesson compares the regimes. It is general education, not legal advice, and the rules have been changing, so check current sources.

### Investment advisers and the fiduciary duty

The **Investment Advisers Act of 1940** regulates people paid to give securities advice. **Section 206** is an antifraud provision, and the Supreme Court held in *SEC v. Capital Gains Research Bureau* (1963) that it reflects a **federal fiduciary duty**. In its 2019 Commission Interpretation, the SEC described the duty as having two parts that apply to the whole relationship:

- **Duty of care**: give advice in the client's **best interest** based on a reasonable understanding of the client's objectives; seek **best execution** of trades; and provide advice and monitoring over the course of the relationship, at a frequency consistent with the agreement.
- **Duty of loyalty**: do not place the adviser's interests ahead of the client's; eliminate or fully and fairly **disclose** conflicts so that the client can give **informed consent**.

The duty can be shaped by agreement, but it cannot be waived entirely, and a client's consent does not allow fraud.

**Registration.** Advisers with about \$100 million or more in assets under management (AUM) generally register with the SEC (a buffer band applies around the threshold), and smaller advisers generally register with their state. Registered advisers file **Form ADV**; Part 2A (the "brochure") describes services, fees, conflicts and disciplinary history in plain English, and clients get it. The **Compliance Rule** (Rule 206(4)-7) requires written policies, an annual review and a chief compliance officer; other rules cover a code of ethics (Rule 204A-1), custody of client assets (Rule 206(4)-2) and marketing (Rule 206(4)-1).

### Broker-dealers and Regulation Best Interest

A **broker-dealer** buys and sells securities for customers and its own account and has historically earned commissions or markups. Broker-dealers are regulated by the SEC and FINRA. **Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI)**, effective June 30, 2020 (Exchange Act Rule 15l-1), requires that when a broker-dealer **recommends** a securities transaction or investment strategy (including a type of account) to a **retail customer**, it must act in the retail customer's **best interest at the time of the recommendation, without placing its own financial or other interest ahead** of the customer's. A **retail customer** is a natural person (or that person's representative) who uses the recommendation primarily for personal, family or household purposes. Reg BI has four component obligations:

1. **Disclosure**: before or at the recommendation, disclose in writing the material facts about the relationship, fees and conflicts.
2. **Care**: exercise reasonable diligence, care and skill to understand the recommendation's risks and costs and have a reasonable basis to believe it is in the customer's best interest, considering reasonably available alternatives. Cost is a factor but not the only one.
3. **Conflict of interest**: establish policies to identify and at least disclose and mitigate conflicts, and to eliminate sales contests and quotas for specific products.
4. **Compliance**: establish, maintain and enforce policies reasonably designed to achieve compliance.

Reg BI governs recommendations, not an ongoing duty to monitor unless the firm agrees to monitor. It is not the same as an adviser's fiduciary duty, a point on which commentators disagree: supporters say it raised the broker standard well above prior suitability and fits a transaction-based business model, while critics say it leaves ambiguity about what "best interest" means in practice and does not impose the full fiduciary duty of loyalty and continuing care.

### Suitability and other standards

**FINRA Rule 2111** (suitability) still applies to recommendations not covered by Reg BI, such as those to institutional customers. It has three parts: **reasonable-basis** suitability (the product suits at least some investors), **customer-specific** suitability (it suits this customer's profile), and **quantitative** suitability (the recommended trading, taken together, is not excessive). **Know-your-customer** (FINRA Rule 2090) requires the firm to know essential facts about each customer. Under **Form CRS** (the relationship summary of two pages or fewer), both broker-dealers and advisers must give retail investors a plain-English summary of services, fees, conflicts and disciplinary history. Many firms are **dual registrants**, acting as an adviser in some accounts and a broker in others, which makes it important to say which hat they are wearing. Retirement accounts may also fall under ERISA's fiduciary rules and Department of Labor rules, which have been repeatedly revised and litigated, and some states have their own standards.

@example
title: Applying Reg BI to a recommendation
problem: A registered representative of a broker-dealer recommends to a retired customer that she buy a proprietary fund that pays the firm a higher sales charge, though a comparable fund with lower costs and a similar strategy is available. The representative does not mention the alternative. Does this raise Reg BI issues?
step: The customer is a natural person investing for personal purposes, and a securities recommendation is being made, so Reg BI applies.
step: Care obligation: the representative must consider reasonably available alternatives. Ignoring a comparable lower-cost fund suggests no reasonable basis for concluding that the proprietary fund is in the customer's best interest unless some other feature justifies it.
step: Conflict obligation: the firm earns more from the proprietary fund, which is a conflict that must at least be disclosed and mitigated. Disclosure alone does not satisfy the care obligation or the "do not place the firm's interest ahead" requirement.
step: Conclusion: unless the representative can document a customer-specific reason (for example a feature the cheaper fund lacks), the recommendation likely breaches Reg BI.
answer: Likely a Reg BI breach under the care and conflict-of-interest obligations.

@mistakes
- Saying that a broker-dealer owes the same fiduciary duty as an adviser. Reg BI is a distinct standard focused on recommendations, though it is higher than the old suitability rule.
- Believing disclosure alone cures any conflict. Under both regimes the recommendation must still be in the client's best interest.
- Applying Reg BI to institutional customers. It covers recommendations to retail customers; FINRA Rule 2111 applies to others.
- Assuming the cheapest option is always required. Cost is an important factor but a broker or adviser may recommend a costlier product if it better fits the client's objectives.

solution: Recommending an account type to a retail customer is itself a recommendation, so when acting as a broker-dealer the firm must satisfy Reg BI, and when acting as an adviser it owes the fiduciary duty; the firm must tell the client which role it is playing (Form CRS). Under the care obligation the representative must understand the client's goals, expected trading and the costs of each account, and have a reasonable basis to believe the account is in her best interest considering the alternatives. A buy-and-hold investor who trades rarely may pay more in a commission account's embedded costs or less depending on the pricing, so the firm should compare expected total costs for this client's pattern. The compensation difference is a conflict, so the firm must disclose it and have policies to mitigate it (for example, no incentive that rewards steering clients to the more profitable account). A compliant process uses a documented comparison of costs and services, explains the differences to the client in plain language, records the reasons for the recommendation, and does not place the firm's higher compensation ahead of the client's interest. @quiz self
prompt: An adviser receives a payment from a custodian each time clients' assets are held there, and recommends that custodian to clients. Write a short paragraph on what the adviser's fiduciary duty requires about this conflict, including what a good disclosure would say.
hint: Think about full and fair disclosure and informed consent.
solution: The payment gives the adviser an incentive to recommend that custodian, so under the duty of loyalty the adviser must either eliminate the conflict or give full and fair disclosure that is specific enough for clients to give informed consent. A good disclosure says plainly that the adviser receives payments from the custodian, explains how the payments are calculated, states that this creates an incentive to recommend that custodian, and describes how clients can choose another custodian or ask questions. The duty of care still requires the adviser to have a reasonable basis to believe the custodian serves clients' interests, considering price, execution quality and services, rather than relying on disclosure alone. The disclosure belongs in Form ADV Part 2A and should be understandable to the client.
