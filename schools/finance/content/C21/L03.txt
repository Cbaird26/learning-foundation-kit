@article
After the 1929 crash and the Great Depression, Congress concluded that investors had bought securities on the basis of little or false information. It chose a **disclosure** philosophy: rather than judging whether an investment is wise, the law requires truthful, complete information and punishes fraud. Two statutes form the core. This is general education, not legal advice.

### The Securities Act of 1933: the primary market

The **1933 Act** regulates the **offer and sale of securities by issuers**, mainly when a company first sells to the public. Its central rule is **Section 5**: no one may offer or sell a security through interstate commerce unless a **registration statement** is on file (before offers) and effective (before sales), and buyers receive a **prospectus**. Companies usually register on Form S-1 (or a shelf registration under Rule 415 for later sales). The SEC reviews the filing for disclosure, not merit, and registration does not mean the SEC endorses the securities.

Liability under the 1933 Act:

- **Section 11**: anyone who signed a registration statement containing a material misstatement or omission can be liable to buyers. The issuer is essentially strictly liable. Directors, underwriters and experts can avoid liability by proving a **due diligence** defense.
- **Section 12(a)(1)**: a buyer may rescind a sale made in violation of Section 5.
- **Section 12(a)(2)**: liability for misstatements in a prospectus or oral communication in a public offering.
- **Section 17(a)**: a general antifraud rule enforced by the SEC (courts have not recognized a private right of action).

### Exemptions from registration

Registration is costly, so the Act exempts many offerings:

| Exemption | Key features |
|---|---|
| Section 4(a)(2) | Private offering to investors who can fend for themselves; no public offering |
| Regulation D, Rule 506(b) | Unlimited accredited investors plus up to 35 sophisticated non-accredited purchasers; **no general solicitation**; non-accredited purchasers must receive specified disclosure |
| Regulation D, Rule 506(c) | General solicitation allowed, but **all purchasers must be accredited** and the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify |
| Regulation A | Small public offerings with a lighter process: Tier 1 up to \$20 million and Tier 2 up to \$75 million in 12 months |
| Regulation Crowdfunding | Up to \$5 million in 12 months through a registered funding portal or broker |
| Rule 144A | Resales to qualified institutional buyers |
| Regulation S | Offers outside the United States |

Dollar limits are adjusted from time to time; check the SEC's current rules. An **accredited investor** (Rule 501(a)) includes an individual with income above \$200,000 (\$300,000 with a spouse or partner) in each of the last two years and an expectation of the same, or with net worth above \$1 million excluding the primary residence, or a holder in good standing of a Series 7, 65 or 82 license; entities with over \$5 million in assets; and directors and executive officers of the issuer. Securities sold in exempt offerings are usually **restricted**: resale needs registration or an exemption such as Rule 144, which generally imposes a holding period of six months for securities of reporting companies and one year otherwise.

### The Securities Exchange Act of 1934: the secondary market

The **1934 Act** created the **SEC** and regulates the trading of securities after issue. It requires registration of **national securities exchanges, broker-dealers and clearing agencies**; requires public companies to file **periodic reports** (Section 13, Form 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K); regulates **proxy solicitation** (Section 14); and requires insiders to report holdings and trades under **Section 16**. Section 16(b) makes **officers, directors and holders of more than 10%** of a class of registered equity securities return to the company any profit from a purchase and sale (or sale and purchase) of that security within **less than six months**, with no need to prove misuse of information. Profit is computed by matching the **lowest-priced purchase** with the **highest-priced sale** within the window, which can produce "profit" larger than the insider's actual gain.

### Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5

**Section 10(b)** and **Rule 10b-5** are the broadest antifraud provisions: it is unlawful, in connection with the purchase or sale of any security, to employ a scheme to defraud, make a material misstatement or omission, or engage in a deceptive practice. A private plaintiff must prove: (1) a **material** misrepresentation or omission (or deceptive act); (2) **scienter**, meaning intent to deceive or severe recklessness; (3) a connection with a purchase or sale; (4) **reliance**; (5) **economic loss**; and (6) **loss causation**. In open markets, courts presume reliance on the market price (the fraud-on-the-market theory) unless the defendant rebuts it. The SEC need not prove reliance or loss. Private actions are subject to the Private Securities Litigation Reform Act of 1995 and generally must be brought within two years of discovery and five years of the violation.

@example
title: Choosing a registration exemption
problem: Larkspur Robotics, a private company, wants to raise \$4 million. It plans to sell stock to 14 accredited investors it already knows and to 3 friends of the founder who are not accredited but have business experience. It does not plan any advertising. Could it use Rule 506(b)? What would change if it advertised the offering online?
step: Rule 506(b) permits unlimited accredited investors and up to 35 non-accredited purchasers who, alone or with a purchaser representative, have enough knowledge and experience to evaluate the investment. Three non-accredited, experienced friends fit.
step: Rule 506(b) forbids general solicitation, and Larkspur plans none. It must give the non-accredited purchasers specified disclosure comparable to a registration statement, and file a notice on Form D after the first sale.
step: If Larkspur advertised online, it would be using general solicitation. It could then use Rule 506(c) only if **every** purchaser is accredited, and it would have to take reasonable steps to verify each one's status, so the three friends could not participate unless they qualify.
answer: Yes under Rule 506(b) with disclosure to the non-accredited buyers; advertising would require Rule 506(c) and only accredited, verified purchasers.

@example
title: Short-swing profit under Section 16(b)
problem: Hartwell Dynamics' chief financial officer buys 4,000 shares at \$12.50 in January and another 4,000 shares at \$15.00 in February. In April she sells 6,000 shares at \$20.00. All trades fall within six months. How much must she disgorge to the company?
step: Section 16(b) matches the lowest-priced purchases with the highest-priced sales. Start with the \$12.50 shares: 4,000 matched at a gain of \$7.50 each is \$30,000.
step: The remaining 2,000 shares sold are matched with the next-lowest purchase, \$15.00: $2{,}000 \times (\$20.00 - \$15.00) = \$10{,}000$.
step: Total recoverable profit: $\$30{,}000 + \$10{,}000 = \$40{,}000$. Her intent and her access to information are irrelevant.
answer: \$40,000.

@mistakes
- Saying the SEC approves or guarantees registered securities. Registration is about disclosure, not merit.
- Mixing up the Acts: the 1933 Act governs the issuer's offering; the 1934 Act governs trading, exchanges, broker-dealers and periodic reporting.
- Forgetting that Rule 506(b) bans general solicitation and that Rule 506(c) requires every purchaser to be a verified accredited investor.
- Averaging the purchase prices in a Section 16(b) problem instead of matching lowest-in with highest-out.

The stock rose 30% over three weeks. Investor Alder bought 1,000 shares at the inflated price, and when the truth came out the price fell 45%. Alder sues under Rule 10b-5. hint: Remember the fraud-on-the-market presumption of reliance.
solution: Alder must show a material misrepresentation: the statement that all tests were passed was false, and a reasonable investor would consider test results important (the price reaction supports materiality). Scienter: the chief executive knew two tests had failed, so intent or severe recklessness is shown. Connection with a purchase or sale: Alder bought shares after the statement. Reliance: Dunmore is exchange-listed with an efficient market, so Alder can invoke the fraud-on-the-market presumption, which Dunmore may try to rebut by showing, for example, that the price would have been the same without the statement or that Alder did not rely on the market price. Loss causation: Alder must show the decline was caused by the revelation of the truth rather than by other events, such as market-wide declines; this is likely the point on which Dunmore will concentrate. The weakest point for Alder is therefore loss causation or any rebuttal of reliance, while Dunmore's weakest points are materiality and scienter given what the executive knew.
