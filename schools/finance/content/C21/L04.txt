@article
If you start a bank, a brokerage, a hedge fund and an insurer, you will deal with different regulators for each. The US financial system grew by layers, often after crises, so supervision is **fragmented** by type of firm and by activity. The practical skill is to ask two questions: *what is the firm's charter or registration*, and *what activity is it carrying out*? This is general education; agencies' scope and activity change, so check current sources.

### Securities and derivatives

**The SEC** (Securities and Exchange Commission) was created by the Securities Exchange Act of 1934. It supervises securities exchanges, broker-dealers, investment advisers, mutual funds, public company disclosure and, under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act, the **PCAOB** (the audit regulator). It writes rules, examines firms, and brings civil enforcement actions in court or administratively; criminal cases are brought by the Department of Justice. It has up to five commissioners, appointed by the President with Senate consent, no more than three from the same political party.

**FINRA** (Financial Industry Regulatory Authority) is a **self-regulatory organization (SRO)** under Section 15A of the Exchange Act, formed in 2007 by merging the NASD and the New York Stock Exchange's member regulation. It is a private, nonprofit body, not a government agency, but it is supervised by the SEC. It licenses and tests registered representatives, writes rules (the SEC approves them), examines broker-dealers, disciplines members and runs an arbitration forum for customer disputes. Other SROs include the exchanges and the **MSRB** (municipal securities).

**The CFTC** (Commodity Futures Trading Commission) regulates futures, options on futures and, since the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act, most **swaps**, under the Commodity Exchange Act. The SEC regulates **security-based swaps**. The **NFA** (National Futures Association) is the futures industry's SRO. Because the SEC and CFTC divide similar products, the two agencies share rulemaking and coordinate on overlaps.

### Banking

Banks operate in a **dual banking system**: a bank has either a federal or a state charter.

| Agency | Main responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Federal Reserve** | Supervises bank holding companies, financial holding companies and state-chartered banks that are Fed members; conducts monetary policy; lender of last resort |
| **OCC** (Office of the Comptroller of the Currency) | Charters and supervises national banks and federal savings associations |
| **FDIC** (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation) | Insures deposits (\$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category); supervises state nonmember banks; resolves failed banks |
| **NCUA** | Charters and insures federal credit unions |
| **State banking departments** | Charter and supervise state banks |
| **CFPB** | Consumer financial protection; its scope has been changing since 2025, so check current |

The **Glass-Steagall Act** of 1933 separated commercial and investment banking; the **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act** of 1999 repealed its core separation and allowed financial holding companies to combine banking, securities and insurance, supervised by the Fed as umbrella supervisor, with each subsidiary regulated by its **functional regulator** (the SEC for a broker-dealer, a state insurance commissioner for an insurer). After the 2007–2009 crisis, the **Dodd-Frank Act** of 2010 created the **Financial Stability Oversight Council (FSOC)**, the CFPB, the Volcker Rule limits on proprietary trading, and the Orderly Liquidation Authority. **Insurance** is regulated mainly by the states (McCarran-Ferguson Act, 1945). The **FinCEN** bureau of the Treasury administers anti-money laundering rules, and OFAC administers sanctions (Lesson 8).

### Digital assets

The **GENIUS Act** (July 18, 2025) created a federal framework for **payment stablecoins**: permitted issuers (subsidiaries of insured banks, federally qualified nonbank issuers and certain state-qualified issuers) must hold high-quality reserves backing each coin one for one, publish reserve information and honor redemption. Rules and effective dates are being implemented by regulators, so check current status. Whether other crypto assets are securities, commodities or something else remains subject to ongoing legislation and litigation.

### Independent agencies and *Trump v. Slaughter*

Congress has often made financial regulators "independent": commissioners serve fixed terms and may be removed only for cause. In *Humphrey's Executor v. United States* (1935) the Supreme Court upheld such limits for the FTC. On June 29, 2026, in ***Trump v. Slaughter***, the Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor*, holding that the President may remove FTC commissioners at will. The Court treated the Federal Reserve as distinct and left its independence in place. How the decision applies to other agencies, including financial regulators with similar structures, is still being worked out.

The debate is real. Supporters of presidential control argue that executive power belongs to an elected President, so agencies that wield it should answer to him, giving voters accountability. Supporters of independence argue that insulation from short-term politics allows steady, expert, evidence-based supervision and reassures markets, especially for monetary policy, and that Congress may design agencies to resist partisan swings. Learners should be able to state both positions fairly.

@example
title: Who regulates this firm?
problem: A national bank (Marlowe National) owns a broker-dealer subsidiary that sells stocks to retail customers, and an affiliate that trades interest-rate swaps with corporations. Identify the main regulators of each piece.
step: Marlowe National is a national bank: its primary supervisor is the OCC, the FDIC insures its deposits, and the Federal Reserve supervises its parent holding company. The CFPB has consumer-protection authority for larger banks (check current scope).
step: The broker-dealer subsidiary is regulated by the SEC and by FINRA (as a member SRO), under the functional regulation principle, regardless of the bank affiliation.
step: The swaps affiliate trades swaps with corporations. Swaps are generally regulated by the CFTC (security-based swaps by the SEC), with NFA membership if it registers as a swap dealer, and prudential rules apply through the banking regulators for bank-affiliated dealers.
answer: OCC, FDIC and Federal Reserve for the bank group; SEC and FINRA for the broker-dealer; CFTC (and NFA) for swaps.

@example
title: Deposit insurance coverage
problem: At one insured bank, Rhea has \$180,000 in a savings account and \$120,000 in a certificate of deposit, both in her sole name, and \$300,000 in a joint account with her spouse. What is insured and uninsured?
step: Single-ownership deposits at the same bank are added: \$180,000 + \$120,000 = \$300,000 against the \$250,000 limit, so \$250,000 is insured and \$50,000 is not.
step: The joint account is a separate ownership category. Each co-owner is insured up to \$250,000, so \$300,000 split equally gives \$150,000 each, fully insured.
step: Total insured: \$250,000 + \$300,000 = \$550,000. Uninsured: \$50,000.
answer: \$550,000 insured and \$50,000 uninsured.

@mistakes
- Calling FINRA a government agency. It is a private self-regulatory organization overseen by the SEC.
- Saying that one regulator supervises each firm: large financial groups have several, by subsidiary and activity.
- Believing that SIPC or FDIC insurance covers investment losses. FDIC insures deposits at banks; SIPC covers customers' missing cash and securities when a brokerage fails, not market losses.
- Stating that *Trump v. Slaughter* settled the independence of every financial regulator. The Court distinguished the Federal Reserve, and application elsewhere is still being worked out.
