@article
Finance puts people in positions of trust over other people's money and information, with incentives attached. That combination creates recurring dilemmas. Learning to recognize each one's pattern is the first control. This lesson is general education, not legal advice; the cases are fictional unless described as general history.

### Conflicts of interest

A **conflict of interest** exists when a person's own interests, or duties to someone else, could bias judgment owed to a client or employer. The standard responses are to **avoid** the conflict, **disclose** it fully, **manage** it (information barriers, independent review, no incentive to favor one outcome) and, where the conflict is too serious, **decline**. Disclosure helps only if the client can understand and act on it.

A historic example is **research analyst conflicts**: when analysts' pay and careers depend on investment banking relationships, pressure to issue favorable ratings arises. In 2003 regulators and ten large firms reached a global settlement over this problem. Rules now include **Regulation AC** (analysts must certify that their views are their own and that no compensation is tied to a specific recommendation) and **FINRA Rule 2241** (research analyst conflicts), which separate research from investment banking.

### Common dilemmas and their rules

| Practice | What it is | Why it is wrong |
|---|---|---|
| **Front-running** | Trading for oneself (or tipping others) ahead of a known pending client order | Uses the client's information for personal gain; may violate Rule 10b-5, FINRA rules (such as Rule 5270 for block orders) and fiduciary duty |
| **Churning** | Excessive trading in a client's account, controlled by the broker, mainly to generate commissions | Puts the broker's pay ahead of the client's interest; violates Rule 10b-5, FINRA rules and Reg BI's quantitative element |
| **Cherry-picking** | Waiting to see how trades perform, then allocating winners to favored (or the adviser's own) accounts and losers to others | Unfair allocation; a breach of fiduciary duty and Advisers Act antifraud provisions |
| **Window dressing** | Buying winners or selling losers just before a reporting date to make holdings look better | Creates a misleading impression |
| **Misleading performance** | Cherry-picked periods, undisclosed fees, hypothetical results presented as actual | Violates antifraud rules and the SEC's Marketing Rule |
| **Pressure-driven reporting** | Managing earnings or reserves to meet targets | Misleads investors; can violate securities laws |
| **Gifts and entertainment** | Lavish hospitality that could influence decisions | Compromises independence; firms set limits and approval rules; public officials face stricter limits |

**Soft dollars.** Section 28(e) of the Exchange Act gives money managers a safe harbor to pay more than the lowest commission for brokerage and **research** services if they determine in good faith that the commission is reasonable relative to the value of the services, and the research assists the manager's investment decisions. Managers must disclose soft-dollar practices, and using client commissions to pay for the manager's overhead falls outside the safe harbor.

**Personal trading.** Firms adopt codes of ethics requiring employees to report holdings and trades, **pre-clear** trades, observe holding periods and avoid trading ahead of clients.

### Measuring excessive trading

Two common indicators for churning:

- **Turnover rate** = annual purchases ÷ average account equity. Arbitrators and courts have often treated rates of about four as suggesting excessive trading and of about six as strong evidence, though the right level depends on the objective and the client.
- **Cost-to-equity ratio** = total commissions and costs ÷ average account equity: the annual return the account must earn just to break even on costs.

These figures indicate a problem; they do not prove it, because the legal test also considers control over the account and intent.

### Writing a resolution

A good resolution is more than a verdict. Use this outline: **Facts** (known and unknown); **Stakeholders and interests**; **Rules and duties** (law, policy, professional standards); **Options** (including ones that dissolve the dilemma: ask, disclose, decline, escalate); **Analysis** (what the frameworks and tests say, the strongest argument against your choice); **Decision**; and **Safeguards** (what to document and what controls would prevent repetition).

@example
title: Is this account being churned?
problem: A broker has discretionary control of a retiree's account with average equity of \$150,000. During the year the broker made purchases totaling \$720,000, and the account paid \$13,500 in commissions and costs. The client's stated objective is capital preservation with modest income. Compute the turnover rate and the cost-to-equity ratio and interpret them.
step: Turnover rate: $\$720{,}000 / \$150{,}000 = 4.8$.
step: Cost-to-equity: $\$13{,}500 / \$150{,}000 = 9.0\%$. The account must earn about 9% a year just to break even after costs.
step: A turnover of 4.8 is high, a cost-to-equity of 9% is very high, and both conflict with a preservation objective. They are strong indicators of excessive trading, and the broker's control of the account supports the control element of a churning claim. Whether churning occurred also depends on intent (scienter) and on whether the trades had a legitimate rationale.
answer: Turnover 4.8; cost-to-equity 9.0%; strong indicators of churning.

@example
title: Cherry-picking
problem: An adviser buys 10,000 shares in one block for several clients in the morning and does not assign them to accounts until the close. By then the stock is up. She allocates most shares to her spouse's account and to a favored client, and the rest to other clients at the day's price. What is the problem and how should allocation work?
step: The adviser waited until she saw the result before allocating, giving herself the option of keeping winners for favored accounts and assigning losers to others. This is cherry-picking.
step: It breaches her fiduciary duty of loyalty (favoring herself and one client) and her disclosure duties, and violates Advisers Act antifraud provisions.
step: The fix is a written allocation policy under which shares are assigned to accounts **before** the order is placed, or by a fair, documented formula such as pro rata, with exceptions reviewed by compliance.
answer: Cherry-picking; allocate before execution or by a fair documented formula.

@mistakes
- Treating disclosure as a cure-all. If the client cannot understand or act on it, or if the practice harms the client regardless, disclosure is not enough.
- Concluding churning from high turnover alone. The test also needs control and intent, though high turnover and cost-to-equity are strong indicators.
- Believing "everyone does it" is a defense. It is a classic rationalization.
- Writing a resolution that gives a verdict with no options, no counterargument and no safeguards.

Deepika, a junior research analyst, receives a gift basket worth about \$400 and two tickets to a major sporting event from the CFO of a company she covers, just before she must decide whether to downgrade the stock. Her firm's policy requires approval for gifts and disclosure of any gift above a modest amount.

solution: Facts: Deepika received a \$400 basket and tickets from the CFO of a covered company, shortly before a rating decision; policy requires approval and disclosure of larger gifts; we do not know whether the CFO intends to influence her. Stakeholders: investors who rely on her research, her firm, the CFO's company and Deepika. Rules: firm gift policy; professional standards of independence and objectivity (such as the CFA Standard I(B) on independence); FINRA rules limiting gifts that influence employees of others and Reg AC and FINRA Rule 2241 on research independence. Options: keep quietly; accept after approval; return the gifts and report. Analysis: keeping quietly breaches policy and creates an appearance of buying favor even if her view is honest; accepting after approval may be allowed only if it fits policy limits, but the timing before a rating decision creates a visible conflict; returning with a polite note and reporting protects independence, and the publicity test favors it. The strongest argument against returning is that it may offend a relationship, which the firm can handle through compliance. Decision: report the gifts to compliance immediately, return them (or have compliance decide), and recuse from the decision or have a colleague review it if compliance directs. Safeguards: document the steps, remind covered companies of the firm's gift policy, and adopt a blackout on accepting gifts near rating changes.

@practice self
prompt: An adviser's trade-allocation practice lets her decide which accounts get a block of shares after the day's close, and her own account keeps outperforming clients' accounts by wide margins. Write a short compliance review (200 to 250 words) explaining the red flags, how you would test for cherry-picking, and what controls to recommend.
hint: Think about comparing allocation timing, returns by account and who benefits.
hint: Controls include pre-allocation and exception review.
solution: The red flags are that allocation occurs after prices are known and that the adviser's own account consistently outperforms clients, which suggests she assigns winners to herself. To test, compare allocation timestamps with execution timestamps, calculate first-day returns of the trades allocated to each account type, and run statistical comparisons of the adviser's and favored accounts' results against other clients' results; a pattern that is unlikely to occur by chance is strong evidence. Review communications and any changes to allocation statements. Controls: require allocation instructions to be recorded before or at the time of the order; use a pro rata or documented formula; require compliance to review exceptions and any late changes; restrict the adviser's personal trading and require pre-clearance; monitor returns by account and report to the chief compliance officer; and train staff on the issue. Disclose the allocation policy in Form ADV.
rubric: Identifies the red flags (post-trade allocation, own-account outperformance).
rubric: Describes concrete tests for cherry-picking.
rubric: Recommends specific controls (pre-allocation, formula, exception review, pre-clearance).

@quiz self
prompt: A portfolio manager at a firm is allowed to trade personally. She wants to buy a small-cap stock that her funds are about to buy in size. Write a short resolution (150 to 200 words): what should she do, and what firm rules should govern her?
hint: Think front-running and the code of ethics.
solution: She should not buy the stock until her funds have finished their trading and any required waiting period has passed, because trading ahead of client orders is front-running and a breach of her duty of loyalty. Her firm's code of ethics should require pre-clearance of personal trades, which compliance should deny when the stock is on the funds' trading list or being bought. The code should also require reporting holdings and trades, apply holding periods and ban trading during pending client orders. If she believes the stock is attractive for herself, she may buy it only after the clients are done and compliance approves, or decide not to buy. Clients' interests come first: the fund's price impact should not benefit her. She should document the request and the outcome.
rubric: Identifies front-running and the duty of loyalty.
rubric: Describes the code-of-ethics controls (pre-clearance, reporting, holding period, restrictions).
rubric: Reaches a clear decision that puts clients first.
