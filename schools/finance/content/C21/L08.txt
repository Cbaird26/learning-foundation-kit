@article
**Money laundering** is making money from crime look legitimate. **Sanctions** are legal restrictions on dealing with specified countries, groups or people for national security or foreign policy reasons. Financial firms are the front line for both: they are required to know their customers, watch for suspicious activity and screen for sanctioned parties. This is general education, not legal advice.

### How laundering works

Launderers typically move through three stages:

1. **Placement**: getting cash into the financial system (for example by depositing small amounts or buying a business that handles cash).
2. **Layering**: moving the money through many transactions, accounts or countries to hide its origin.
3. **Integration**: bringing the money back as apparently legitimate wealth (real estate, business income, investments).

The same machinery can move clean money for **terrorist financing**.

### The legal framework

The **Bank Secrecy Act (BSA, 1970)** requires financial institutions to keep records and report certain transactions to **FinCEN** (the Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, a bureau of the Treasury). The **USA PATRIOT Act (2001)** expanded it, requiring customer identification and banning relationships with shell banks, and the **Anti-Money Laundering Act of 2020** modernized the system. Money laundering itself is a federal crime (18 U.S.C. §§ 1956 and 1957). Banks, broker-dealers, futures commission merchants, mutual funds, money services businesses and others are covered institutions, and **FINRA Rule 3310** requires member firms to maintain AML programs.

An **AML program** must have: (1) internal policies, procedures and controls; (2) a designated **AML compliance officer**; (3) ongoing employee training; (4) **independent testing**; and (5) **customer due diligence (CDD)**, which since 2018 includes identifying the **beneficial owners** of legal-entity customers (each person owning 25% or more, and one person who controls the entity). The **customer identification program (CIP)** requires a firm to collect and verify at least a name, date of birth, address and identification number before opening an account.

### Reports

- **Currency transaction report (CTR)**: a financial institution files a CTR for each deposit, withdrawal, exchange or payment involving **more than \$10,000 in currency** in a single business day. Multiple cash transactions by or for the same person in a business day are **aggregated**. Nonfinancial businesses report cash payments over \$10,000 on Form 8300.
- **Structuring** (31 U.S.C. § 5324) is breaking up transactions to avoid a report. It is a crime even if the money is legal.
- **Suspicious activity report (SAR)**: required when a firm knows or suspects that a transaction of at least a threshold amount (generally \$5,000 for banks and broker-dealers, \$2,000 for money services businesses) involves illegal funds, is designed to evade the BSA, or has no apparent lawful purpose. A SAR is generally due within **30 calendar days** of initial detection (up to 60 if no suspect has been identified). Filers have a **safe harbor** from civil liability for reporting, and **SARs are confidential**: it is illegal to tell the subject ("tipping off").

Red flags include structuring, activity that does not fit the customer's profile, rapid movement of funds in and out, transactions with high-risk jurisdictions, unusual cash intensity, and customers who refuse to supply information.

### Sanctions and OFAC

The **Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC)** of the Treasury administers sanctions under statutes such as the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). Some programs target countries; others target individuals and entities placed on the **Specially Designated Nationals and Blocked Persons (SDN) List**. US persons must **block** (freeze) property of listed parties and report it to OFAC, and may not deal with them. Under OFAC's **50 Percent Rule**, an entity owned **50% or more in the aggregate**, directly or indirectly, by one or more blocked persons is itself blocked, even if it is not on the list. Civil liability is **strict**: a violation can be penalized without proof that the firm knew, though knowledge and a good compliance program affect penalties. Willful violations can be criminal (up to 20 years in prison and a \$1 million fine under IEEPA). Sanctions are separate from AML: AML asks whether funds are suspicious, while sanctions ask whether the *party* is prohibited.

**Beneficial ownership transparency.** The Corporate Transparency Act of 2021 created a reporting regime for beneficial ownership of companies. In March 2025 FinCEN issued an interim final rule exempting domestic companies and US persons from the reporting requirement, limiting it to certain foreign companies registered to do business in the United States; the rules have changed more than once, so check current status. Banks' CDD obligations continue.

@example
title: Aggregation and structuring
problem: A convenience store owner deposits \$9,000 in cash on Monday, \$9,500 on Tuesday and \$8,800 on Wednesday, each time in a separate branch of the same bank. He says a friend told him deposits above \$10,000 are "reported." Is a CTR required? Is there a SAR concern?
step: A CTR is required only if currency transactions exceed \$10,000 in a business day. Each day's total is under \$10,000, so no CTR is triggered.
step: The pattern, three deposits just under \$10,000 on consecutive days in different branches, plus the owner's comment about reporting, suggest intent to evade the reporting rule, which is **structuring**. The owner may commit a crime (31 U.S.C. § 5324) even though the funds may be legitimate.
step: The bank should file a SAR on the suspicious pattern, within 30 calendar days of detection, and must not tell the customer. Whether the owner is prosecuted is for the authorities.
answer: No CTR, but the pattern is suspected structuring and warrants a SAR.

@example
title: The 50 Percent Rule
problem: Halden Shipping is owned 30% by Blocked Person X, 15% by Blocked Person Y, 20% by an unlisted investor and 35% by various unlisted holders. Is Halden Shipping blocked?
step: Add the interests of the blocked persons in the aggregate: $30\% + 15\% = 45\%$.
step: The threshold is 50% in the aggregate. At 45%, Halden is not automatically blocked under the 50 Percent Rule.
step: The firm should still use enhanced due diligence: a blocked person with 45% may control the company in practice, and OFAC may take a different view if control exists. Changes in ownership (for example if Y buys 5% more) could cross the threshold.
answer: Not automatically blocked (45%), but it warrants enhanced diligence.

@mistakes
- Believing structuring is legal if the money is clean. Structuring to avoid a CTR is itself a crime.
- Telling a customer that a SAR was filed ("tipping off"). SARs are confidential.
- Looking only at the list: under the 50 Percent Rule, an entity not on the SDN List may still be blocked.
- Treating sanctions screening as part of fraud detection only. Sanctions compliance is strict-liability, and is separate from AML.

@practice self
prompt: Compare a fiduciary standard and a suitability standard in a one-page memo (250 to 350 words) addressed to a new compliance trainee. Cover what each requires, whom each applies to, how each handles conflicts of interest, and one example in which the two would lead to different outcomes.
hint: Use the adviser's duties of care and loyalty, and Reg BI and FINRA Rule 2111 for the other side.
hint: Think of a recommendation that is suitable but not the best available option.
solution: A fiduciary standard, under the Advisers Act, requires an investment adviser to act in the client's best interest over the whole relationship through a duty of care (advice based on the client's goals, best execution, monitoring as agreed) and a duty of loyalty (not placing the adviser's interest ahead of the client's, eliminating or fully disclosing conflicts for informed consent). A suitability standard, traditionally FINRA Rule 2111, asks whether a recommendation is reasonably suitable for the customer given the customer's profile (reasonable-basis, customer-specific and quantitative suitability) and is not a promise to put the customer's interest first; it still applies to recommendations outside Reg BI. Regulation Best Interest has raised the standard for broker-dealer recommendations to retail customers to the customer's best interest at the time of the recommendation, without putting the firm's interest ahead, but it is tied to recommendations and does not impose the same continuing duty. On conflicts, the fiduciary must eliminate or fully disclose and obtain informed consent, whereas under suitability the focus is on the recommendation's fit, and disclosure was historically less central. Example: a product that pays the firm a higher commission fits the client's risk profile but costs more than a similar product that is also available. It could be suitable, but a fiduciary (or a broker under Reg BI's care obligation) would have to consider the cheaper alternative and the conflict, and would likely not recommend the costlier product without a client-specific reason.

rubric: Explains whom each standard applies to and how each handles conflicts.

@quiz self
prompt: In a paragraph, explain how anti-money laundering rules differ from sanctions rules, and why a firm needs both screening and transaction monitoring.
hint: One asks about suspicious funds, the other about prohibited parties.
solution: AML rules, based on the Bank Secrecy Act, require firms to know their customers, monitor for suspicious transactions and report them (CTRs and SARs). The question is whether funds appear to come from or be meant for crime. Sanctions rules, administered by OFAC, prohibit dealing with specified countries and with listed parties (and entities they own 50% or more), regardless of whether the money looks suspicious, and civil liability is strict. A transaction can raise one without the other: a clean-looking payment to a blocked entity violates sanctions, while a payment to an unsanctioned party can be a layering step. Screening checks names, ownership and countries against sanctions lists at onboarding and on each payment; monitoring looks at behavior over time to spot structuring and unusual patterns. Both are needed because each catches problems the other cannot.
