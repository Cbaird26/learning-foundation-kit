@article
Markets work when investors can trust the numbers. The US system relies on **mandatory disclosure**: public companies must file standardized reports with the SEC, which anyone can read free on **EDGAR**. This lesson explains what must be filed, what counts as important enough to disclose, and the controls that make the reports reliable. It is general education, not legal advice.

### The reporting cycle

Companies with registered securities file reports under Section 13 of the 1934 Act:

| Report | Content | Deadline (after period end) |
|---|---|---|
| **Form 10-K** | Annual report: business, risk factors, MD&A, audited financial statements | 60 days (large accelerated filers), 75 days (accelerated), 90 days (all others) |
| **Form 10-Q** | Quarterly report with unaudited financial statements | 40 days (large accelerated and accelerated), 45 days (others) |
| **Form 8-K** | Current report of major events (earnings releases, mergers, executive departures, bankruptcy, auditor changes) | Generally four business days after the event |
| **Proxy statement** | Director elections, executive pay, shareholder proposals (Section 14) | Before the annual meeting |

**Large accelerated filers** have a public float of \$700 million or more; **accelerated filers** have \$75 million to under \$700 million. Rules about reporting frequency can change, so check the SEC for current requirements. Financial statements follow **GAAP**; the format and content requirements are in Regulation S-X (financial statements) and Regulation S-K (everything else).

### Materiality

A fact is **material** if there is a substantial likelihood that a reasonable investor would consider it important in deciding how to act, or that it would significantly alter the "total mix" of information (*TSC Industries v. Northway*, 1976). For uncertain events such as a merger negotiation, materiality depends on **probability and magnitude** (*Basic v. Levinson*, 1988). There is no fixed percentage. A common starting rule of thumb is about 5% of a line item such as pre-tax income, but SEC staff guidance (Staff Accounting Bulletin 99) stresses that a number below 5% can still be material for **qualitative** reasons, for example if it turns a loss into a profit, hides a failure to meet covenants or analysts' expectations, or involves illegal acts or management's compensation.

### Fair disclosure and non-GAAP measures

**Regulation FD** (Fair Disclosure, 2000) prohibits public companies from selectively disclosing material nonpublic information to securities professionals and shareholders who are likely to trade, such as analysts and investors. If the disclosure is intentional, the company must make public disclosure at the same time (for example by Form 8-K or a broadly accessible webcast). If it is unintentional, the company must make it public promptly, generally within 24 hours or before the next market open, whichever is later. Regulation FD does not cover ordinary-course communications with customers or suppliers, or with people who owe a duty of confidence.

**Non-GAAP measures** such as "adjusted EBITDA" are allowed in earnings releases and filings only with Regulation G and S-K Item 10(e) safeguards: show the most directly comparable GAAP measure with equal or greater prominence and a reconciliation, and do not label recurring costs as non-recurring in a misleading way. **Forward-looking statements** receive a statutory safe harbor (PSLRA, 1995) when identified as such and accompanied by meaningful cautionary language, unless made with actual knowledge of falsity.

### Sarbanes-Oxley and clawbacks

Congress passed the **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX)** after accounting frauds at several large companies:

- **Section 302**: the CEO and CFO certify each periodic report is accurate and that they maintain disclosure controls.
- **Section 404**: management assesses **internal control over financial reporting**, and most larger companies also need an auditor's attestation.
- **Section 906**: knowingly certifying a false report is a crime.
- The **PCAOB** registers and inspects audit firms; **auditors** are restricted from certain non-audit services and must be overseen by an independent **audit committee**.
- **Section 304** lets the SEC recover bonuses and trading profits from the CEO and CFO after a restatement caused by misconduct. More broadly, **Rule 10D-1** (under Dodd-Frank, carried out through stock exchange listing standards in effect since late 2023) requires listed companies to recover **incentive-based compensation** received by current and former executive officers during the three preceding fiscal years that exceeds what they would have received after an **accounting restatement**. Recovery is required without any finding of fault and is calculated before taxes.

Note that the SEC's 2024 climate-related disclosure rule is **not in effect**: the SEC voted in March 2025 to stop defending it. Companies may still have to disclose climate risks that are material under existing rules, and the EU's sustainability reporting rules (CSRD) have been simplified, so check current requirements.

@example
title: Is the loss of a customer material?
problem: Bellwether Foods earns \$40 million before tax. A customer representing 4% of revenue (about \$2.2 million of pre-tax income) has told Bellwether it will not renew, and the loss will make this the first year in which Bellwether misses the earnings target that triggers executive bonuses. Is the loss material?
step: Quantitative: \$2.2 million is 5.5% of \$40 million pre-tax income, just above the common 5% rule of thumb, so it is a starting signal of materiality but not a conclusion.
step: Qualitative: the loss makes a bonus target be missed, affecting management compensation, and signals a trend in customer relations. Both factors push toward materiality even if the percentage were smaller.
step: Probability and magnitude: the customer has said it will not renew, so the probability is high and the magnitude is significant.
step: Conclusion: the loss is likely material. Materiality alone does not create a duty to disclose, but a duty arises when a specific requirement applies (an item of Form 8-K, or a known trend or uncertainty that MD&A in the next 10-Q or 10-K must discuss) or when silence would make the company's other statements misleading. Here the next periodic report will almost certainly need to discuss it, and the company should document its reasoning.
answer: Likely material (5.5% of pre-tax income and qualitative factors).

@example
title: A clawback after a restatement
problem: Nimbus Components pays its CEO a bonus of 0.5% of reported operating income. Reported operating income was \$80 million, but a restatement lowered it to \$62 million. How much must the CEO return under a Rule 10D-1 policy, ignoring taxes?
step: Original bonus: $0.005 \times \$80{,}000{,}000 = \$400{,}000$.
step: Recalculated bonus using the restated figure: $0.005 \times \$62{,}000{,}000 = \$310{,}000$.
step: Excess incentive pay: $\$400{,}000 - \$310{,}000 = \$90{,}000$, recoverable regardless of whether the CEO was at fault or took part in the error.
answer: \$90,000.

@mistakes
- Treating a 5% threshold as a safe harbor. Small items can be material for qualitative reasons.
- Thinking Regulation FD stops all conversations with analysts. It prohibits selective disclosure of material nonpublic information, not discussions of public or immaterial information.
- Assuming a clawback requires proof of misconduct. Rule 10D-1 recovery after a restatement is no-fault.
- Presenting an adjusted (non-GAAP) figure without the comparable GAAP measure and a reconciliation.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 250 words, explain Regulation FD to a new investor-relations employee: what it prohibits, what a compliant earnings call looks like, and two things the employee may still do.
hint: Think about public access to the call and what counts as material nonpublic information.
solution: Regulation FD prohibits a public company from giving material nonpublic information selectively to securities professionals and likely traders, such as analysts and large holders, before telling the public. If the disclosure is intentional, the company must disclose publicly at the same time; if accidental, it must do so promptly, generally within 24 hours or before the next market open, whichever is later. A compliant earnings call is announced in advance, accessible to the public (by webcast or dial-in, with notice by press release), and uses the same information that appears in the release. The employee may still discuss information that is already public, may give analysts help in understanding public information (as part of the mosaic of an analyst's work), may speak with customers and suppliers in the ordinary course, and may disclose to people who have agreed to keep it confidential, such as lawyers or underwriters. Anything material and not yet public should be shared only through a broad channel such as an 8-K.
rubric: States the prohibition and the timing rules (simultaneous for intentional, prompt for unintentional).
rubric: Describes a compliant earnings call (advance notice, public access).
rubric: Lists accurate permitted activities (public information, ordinary-course business, confidentiality agreements).

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, in a paragraph, why the SEC's disclosure-based system needs both the Sarbanes-Oxley certification requirements and an independent audit, and what each contributes.
hint: One provides personal accountability for management, the other independent verification.
solution: Disclosure only helps investors if the numbers are reliable. Under Section 302 the CEO and CFO personally certify that reports are accurate and that controls exist, and under Section 906 knowingly false certification is a crime, which gives management a personal stake in accuracy. An independent audit adds verification from an outside party that is supervised by the PCAOB and overseen by an audit committee, so errors that management misses or hides are more likely to be found. Certification works through management's accountability, and the audit through independence and skepticism; neither alone is enough, since managers can err or mislead and auditors can only test samples.

rubric: Explains the audit's independence, PCAOB oversight and audit committee.
