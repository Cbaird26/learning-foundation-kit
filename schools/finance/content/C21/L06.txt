@article
Is it illegal to trade on information other people do not have? Sometimes. A portfolio manager who studies public filings and trades before the crowd is doing her job; a chief financial officer who sells before announcing a loss is committing a crime. The difference lies in the **source of the information** and the **duty** attached to it. This is general education, not legal advice.

### No statute, but clear rules

No federal statute defines "insider trading." The law developed under **Section 10(b)** of the 1934 Act and **Rule 10b-5**, which prohibit fraud and deception in connection with securities trading, through court decisions. Possessing a trading advantage is not itself unlawful: liability requires a **breach of a duty** of trust and confidence while trading on (or tipping) **material nonpublic information (MNPI)**. Information is *material* if a reasonable investor would consider it important (Lesson 5) and *nonpublic* until it has been disseminated to the market.

### Three theories of liability

1. **Classical theory** (*Chiarella v. United States*, 1980): a corporate insider (officer, director, employee, and temporary insiders such as outside lawyers and accountants) who trades the company's securities on MNPI breaches a duty to the company's shareholders. The duty is to disclose or abstain from trading.
2. **Misappropriation theory** (*United States v. O'Hagan*, 1997): a person who trades on confidential information in breach of a duty owed to the **source of the information** (for example an employer or client) defrauds the source, even though the person owes nothing to the company whose shares are traded. A lawyer at the acquirer's firm who buys the target's stock before a deal is announced is a typical case.
3. **Tipper-tippee liability** (*Dirks v. SEC*, 1983; *Salman v. United States*, 2016): an insider who gives MNPI to another person (the tipper) breaches a duty if the tip is made for a **personal benefit**, which includes a gift of confidential information to a trading relative or friend. The tippee is liable if he knew or should have known of the breach. A tipper who tips and a tippee who trades both face liability, and the chain can continue.

Under **Rule 10b5-2**, duties of trust and confidence can arise from agreements to keep information confidential, from a history of sharing confidences, or between close family members. **Rule 14e-3** separately bans trading on MNPI about a tender offer once substantial steps toward it have been taken, even without a breach of duty.

### Trading plans and the mosaic theory

Under **Rule 10b5-1**, a trade is made "on the basis of" MNPI if the trader was **aware** of it when trading. An **affirmative defense** exists for trades under a written plan adopted in good faith when the person was not aware of MNPI. Amendments effective in 2023 added conditions: a **cooling-off period** before trading starts (for directors and officers, the later of 90 days after adoption or two business days after the company files its report for the quarter in which the plan was adopted, up to a maximum of 120 days; 30 days for others), a certification by directors and officers that they are not aware of MNPI, limits on overlapping plans and single-trade plans, and good faith throughout the plan.

The **mosaic theory** holds that an analyst may combine public information with pieces of immaterial nonpublic information (channel checks, industry visits) into a conclusion that is itself not MNPI. The line is crossed when one piece is material and came from a source breaching a duty.

### Penalties

- **SEC civil remedies**: disgorgement of profit gained or loss avoided, plus a civil penalty of up to **three times** that amount (Section 21A), and officer-and-director bars. Tippers can be penalized for the profits their tippees gain.
- **Criminal**: up to 20 years in prison and a fine of up to \$5 million for individuals (up to \$25 million for entities) under Section 32(a) of the 1934 Act, and securities fraud under 18 U.S.C. § 1348. The Department of Justice prosecutes.
- **Private**: Section 20A gives people who traded at about the same time in the opposite direction a claim for damages.

### Market manipulation

Manipulation means artificially affecting price or the appearance of trading. Key provisions are **Section 9(a)(2)** of the 1934 Act (a series of transactions raising or depressing the price of a registered security to induce others to trade), Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, and the **Commodity Exchange Act**, which Dodd-Frank amended to prohibit **spoofing**. Common practices:

- **Wash trades and matched orders**: trading with oneself or a colluding party to create false volume.
- **Marking the close**: trades near the close to push the closing price.
- **Spoofing and layering**: placing orders with the intent to cancel them before execution, to mislead others about supply or demand.
- **Pump-and-dump**: promoting a thinly traded security with false statements while selling holdings into the buying.

@example
title: A chain of tips
problem: Dr. Tran, CFO of Alder Biotech, tells her brother-in-law Joel that Alder's drug trial failed, before the public announcement. Joel buys put options and mentions the news to his golf partner Kim, who also buys puts. Alder's price falls 35% after the announcement. Who is liable?
step: Tran is a corporate insider and the information (a failed trial) is material and nonpublic. Telling a relative who may trade is a gift of confidential information, which satisfies the personal benefit test (*Salman*), so Tran breached her duty as tipper.
step: Joel knew the information came from the CFO and that disclosing it was improper, and he traded on it, so he is liable as a tippee.
step: Kim is a remote tippee. She is liable only if she knew or should have known that the information came from an insider in breach of duty. Facts about what Joel told her matter: if Joel said it came from "my wife's sister, the CFO, before it's public," she likely knew.
step: Tran may face penalties based on Joel's and Kim's profits, and all three may face disgorgement, civil penalties up to three times the profit, and criminal liability if the DOJ proves willfulness.
answer: Tran (tipper) and Joel (tippee) are liable; Kim is liable if she knew or should have known the information was improperly obtained.

@example
title: Computing the civil penalty
problem: A tippee sold 20,000 shares at \$50.00 shortly before an announcement dropped the price to \$38.50. What is the loss avoided, and what is the maximum SEC civil penalty under Section 21A, in addition to disgorgement?
step: Loss avoided: $20{,}000 \times (\$50.00 - \$38.50) = \$230{,}000$.
step: Maximum civil penalty: three times the loss avoided, $3 \times \$230{,}000 = \$690{,}000$.
step: The SEC may also seek disgorgement of the \$230,000 and other remedies. A court has discretion and the penalty can be lower.
answer: Loss avoided \$230,000; maximum civil penalty \$690,000 (plus disgorgement).

@mistakes
- Saying that trading on any nonpublic information is illegal. Liability requires MNPI and a breach of a duty (or a tip received from a breach).
- Thinking only company employees can be liable. Outsiders can be liable under the misappropriation and tippee theories.
- Treating a Rule 10b5-1 plan as protection no matter when it was adopted. A plan adopted while aware of MNPI, or changed in bad faith, gives no defense.
- Calling all aggressive trading manipulation. Manipulation requires an intent to mislead the market or distort price.

@practice self
prompt: A trading firm places and quickly cancels large buy orders for a mid-cap stock many times a day while selling its own shares into the price bumps. Write a short analysis (150 to 250 words) of why this may be spoofing or manipulation, what evidence a regulator would seek, and what legitimate explanations the firm might offer.
hint: Focus on intent and order-cancellation patterns.
hint: Remember that cancelling orders is normal in itself.
solution: The pattern suggests layering or spoofing: orders placed with the intent to cancel before execution create a false impression of demand and lift the price, allowing the firm to sell into it. Spoofing is expressly prohibited in commodity markets under the Commodity Exchange Act, and in securities the SEC and DOJ use Section 9(a)(2), Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5. A regulator would look for intent: the high cancel-to-fill ratio, timing of cancellations just after the opposite-side trades, order sizes far larger than the firm's ordinary trades, internal communications and the firm's profits. The firm might explain that cancelling is normal because market makers update quotes as prices move, that the orders were genuine and cancelled when conditions changed, and that algorithms manage risk. Because intent is the dividing line, the evidence of purpose, not cancellation alone, decides the case.
rubric: Identifies layering or spoofing and the false impression of demand.
rubric: Names evidence of intent a regulator would seek.
rubric: Gives fair legitimate explanations and notes that intent is the dividing line.

@quiz self
prompt: Sasha overhears two strangers on a train discussing in detail an unannounced takeover of Pelham Corp. She buys Pelham shares that afternoon. Explain in one paragraph whether insider trading liability is likely, and what additional facts could change your answer.
hint: Ask whether any duty was breached by Sasha or by the people she overheard.
solution: Liability is unlikely on these facts. Sasha owes no duty to Pelham's shareholders or to the source, and the strangers' conversation was not a tip to her for a personal benefit, so there is no breach of duty that she inherits as a tippee. Insider trading law requires a breach of duty, not merely possession of nonpublic information. The answer would change if Sasha knew that the speakers were insiders disclosing the information improperly and she could be treated as a tippee, if she had a duty of confidentiality to the speakers or their firm (for example if she worked for one of them), or if the speakers deliberately spoke to be overheard in exchange for a benefit. Rule 14e-3 could also apply if the takeover is a tender offer and substantial steps have been taken, because it does not require a breach of duty.
rubric: Concludes that liability is unlikely because no duty was breached.
rubric: Identifies facts that could change the result (knowledge of improper source, a duty to the speakers, deliberate tip).
rubric: Mentions Rule 14e-3 for tender offers or explains that possession alone is not enough.
