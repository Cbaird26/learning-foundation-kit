@article
Companies cannot sign contracts or trade securities themselves; people do it for them. **Agency law** decides when a person's acts bind the firm, and **business organization law** decides who bears the loss when the firm fails. Both matter in finance, where a trader, a broker or a board member acts for someone else every day. This is general education, not legal advice.

### Agency

An **agency** is a relationship in which an **agent** acts on behalf of and under the control of a **principal**. The agent owes the principal **fiduciary duties**: loyalty (no self-dealing, no secret profit), obedience to lawful instructions, and care. A principal is bound by an agent's contract when the agent had authority:

- **Actual authority** comes from the principal's words or conduct *toward the agent*. It can be **express** (a written instruction) or **implied** (reasonably necessary to carry out the task).
- **Apparent authority** comes from the principal's words or conduct *toward the third party*, which lead the third party reasonably to believe the agent is authorized, even if the agent is not. A secret limit the third party never heard of does not defeat it.
- **Ratification** occurs when a principal, knowing the material facts, accepts the benefit of an unauthorized act or fails to repudiate it.

Under **respondeat superior**, an employer is liable for torts an employee commits within the scope of employment. A firm can also be liable for an employee's fraud when the employee acted with actual or apparent authority.

### Forms of business organization

| Form | Owner liability | Federal income tax | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sole proprietorship | Unlimited | Owner's return | No separate legal entity |
| General partnership | Unlimited; partners are jointly and severally liable | Pass-through | Each partner is an agent of the partnership |
| Limited partnership | General partners unlimited; limited partners capped at investment | Pass-through | Limited partners may not control management |
| LLC | Members generally limited to their investment | Pass-through by default; may elect corporate tax | Flexible, set by operating agreement |
| C corporation | Shareholders limited to their investment | Corporation pays tax (federal rate 21%), then shareholders pay tax on dividends | Easiest to raise outside equity |
| S corporation | Shareholders limited to their investment | Pass-through | At most 100 eligible shareholders and one class of stock |

**Double taxation** is the C corporation's cost: profit is taxed at the corporate level and again when paid out as dividends. Pass-through entities avoid the first tax; owners report their share of profit whether or not it is distributed. (State taxes and many deductions are ignored here.)

### Corporate governance and duties

Shareholders own the corporation and elect a **board of directors**, which oversees the business; **officers** run it day to day. Directors and officers owe the corporation two core fiduciary duties: the **duty of care** (act with the diligence of a reasonably prudent person, on an informed basis) and the **duty of loyalty** (put the corporation's interests ahead of personal interests, and disclose and cleanse conflicted transactions). Under Delaware law, which governs many large public companies, directors also owe a duty of **good faith**, and the board has a duty of oversight (the *Caremark* line of cases) to make a good-faith effort to put a reporting system in place and respond to red flags.

The **business judgment rule** presumes that directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith and in the honest belief that the decision served the company. A court will not second-guess a decision made that way, even if it turns out badly. The presumption is lost when there is a conflict of interest, bad faith or no real information-gathering, and the directors must then prove the transaction was **entirely fair**.

### Limited liability and its limits

Limited liability means creditors can reach the entity's assets, not the owners' homes. A court may **pierce the corporate veil** and hold owners personally liable when the entity is a sham: owners mix personal and company funds (**commingling**), leave the company grossly **undercapitalized**, ignore formalities, or use the entity to commit fraud. Courts do this sparingly, and the facts are weighed together rather than by any one factor.

@example
title: Does the agent bind the principal?
problem: Ashgrove Boats' written policy lets its purchasing assistant, Dana, sign purchases up to \$5,000. For two years Ashgrove let Dana order up to \$60,000 of parts from Teller Marine Supply, and paid every invoice. Dana now orders \$85,000 of engines from Teller, who has never seen the policy. Ashgrove refuses to pay. Is Ashgrove bound?
step: **Actual authority.** Dana's express authority is \$5,000. There is no sign that Ashgrove told Dana to order \$85,000, so Dana lacked actual authority for this order.
step: **Apparent authority.** For two years, Ashgrove's own conduct toward Teller (letting Dana order up to \$60,000 and paying) led Teller reasonably to believe Dana could place orders of that size. An \$85,000 order is larger than anything before, which weakens Teller's belief that Dana could place it.
step: **Ratification.** If Ashgrove keeps the engines or accepts delivery knowing the facts, it ratifies. If it promptly rejects the order and returns the goods, it does not.
step: **Conclusion.** Ashgrove is clearly bound up to the level it had allowed (\$60,000 pattern). The \$85,000 order is a close question: Teller argues the pattern created apparent authority for engine purchases; Ashgrove argues the jump in size put Teller on notice to check. Ashgrove's safest move is to act promptly to repudiate and return the engines.
answer: Bound for orders in the established range; the \$85,000 order is a close question turning on apparent authority and prompt repudiation.

@example
title: The cost of double taxation
problem: Fennimore Software, a C corporation, earns \$200,000 before tax. It pays all after-tax profit to its single shareholder as a dividend. Assume a 21% federal corporate rate, a 15% federal tax on the dividend and no state taxes. How much does the shareholder keep, and what is the combined tax rate? How does that compare with a pass-through owner taxed at 24% on the same profit?
step: Corporate tax: $0.21 \times \$200{,}000 = \$42{,}000$, leaving \$158,000 to distribute.
step: Dividend tax: $0.15 \times \$158{,}000 = \$23{,}700$, so the shareholder keeps $\$158{,}000 - \$23{,}700 = \$134{,}300$.
step: Combined tax: $(\$42{,}000 + \$23{,}700)/\$200{,}000 = 32.85\%$.
step: A pass-through owner taxed at 24% keeps $0.76 \times \$200{,}000 = \$152{,}000$. In this simplified case the pass-through form keeps \$17,700 more, but real choices also turn on deductions, retained earnings, state taxes and the need to raise outside capital.
answer: Shareholder keeps \$134,300 (combined tax 32.85%); the pass-through owner keeps \$152,000.

@mistakes
- Believing an agent's lack of actual authority ends the matter. Apparent authority, created by the principal's own conduct toward the third party, can still bind the principal.
- Assuming "limited liability" is absolute. Commingling, undercapitalization and fraud can lead a court to pierce the veil.
- Treating all partners as having limited liability; only limited partners in a limited partnership (and members of an LLC) do.
- Saying the business judgment rule protects any bad decision. It protects informed, good-faith, disinterested decisions, not conflicted or uninformed ones.

prompt: Two founders, one contributing \$80,000 and the other building the software, plan a small startup. They hope to raise outside investment in about three years but will not distribute profits in the first two. Write a short memo (200 to 300 words) comparing an LLC and a C corporation for them on liability, taxes and fundraising, and say which questions they should ask a lawyer and an accountant before choosing.
hint: Consider what happens to taxes if profits are retained rather than distributed, and what outside investors expect.
hint: Be balanced: each form has advantages here.
solution: Both forms give the founders limited liability, so personal assets are generally protected unless they personally guarantee debts, commingle funds or commit fraud. An LLC is a pass-through by default, so profits and losses flow to the founders' returns; early losses may offset other income, but founders may owe tax on profits they leave in the company. A C corporation pays its own 21% federal tax and shareholders pay tax again only when dividends are paid, so retained profits are taxed once, at the corporate rate. For fundraising, a C corporation with standard classes of stock is what many outside investors expect, while an LLC can convert later but conversion has tax and legal cost. An LLC offers flexibility in dividing control and profit through its operating agreement. The founders should ask a lawyer about the operating agreement or bylaws, equity vesting for the founder building the software, and conversion mechanics, and ask an accountant how their projected profits, any early losses and state taxes affect each form. Neither choice is clearly better without those facts.
rubric: Compares liability protection correctly (limited for both, with the usual exceptions).
rubric: Compares tax treatment accurately: pass-through by default for the LLC, corporate-level tax then dividend tax for the C corporation.
rubric: Addresses fundraising expectations and flexibility, and presents the choice in balance.
rubric: Lists concrete questions for a lawyer and an accountant, and treats the memo as general information rather than a final recommendation.
