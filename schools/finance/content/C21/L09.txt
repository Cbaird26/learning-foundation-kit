@article
Consumers face firms that know more, have lawyers, and write the contract. **Consumer financial protection law** responds with rules about what must be disclosed, what practices are banned, and what remedies consumers have. Its authors disagree about how much regulation is best (see the end of this lesson). This is general education, not legal advice; the scope and activity of the **CFPB** have been changing since 2025, so check current sources.

### Who enforces

The **Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB)** was created by Title X of the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010 to write and enforce rules under most federal consumer financial laws, supervise larger banks and many nonbank lenders, and take consumer complaints. In 2024 the Supreme Court upheld the Bureau's funding mechanism (a transfer from the Federal Reserve) in *CFPB v. Community Financial Services Association of America*. Since 2025 the Bureau's staffing, rules, guidance and enforcement priorities have changed substantially and been the subject of litigation, so learners should check current status. Other enforcers are the **Federal Trade Commission**, the **banking regulators**, **state attorneys general** and state regulators (whose role is often larger when federal activity is smaller), and private plaintiffs where statutes allow suits and class actions.

### Core statutes

| Statute (regulation) | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Truth in Lending Act, TILA (Reg Z)** | Requires clear disclosure of the **APR** and finance charges and other credit terms; gives a three-business-day right to rescind certain home-secured loans on the principal dwelling; caps a cardholder's liability for unauthorized credit card use at \$50; mortgage ability-to-repay rules |
| **Equal Credit Opportunity Act, ECOA (Reg B)** | Bans discrimination in credit on race, color, religion, national origin, sex, marital status, age (if the applicant can contract), receipt of public assistance, or good-faith exercise of consumer-protection rights; requires an adverse action notice with reasons |
| **Fair Credit Reporting Act, FCRA** | Governs credit reports: accuracy, permissible purposes, consumer access, dispute investigations (generally 30 days), and adverse action notices; most negative items drop off after seven years and bankruptcies after up to ten |
| **Fair Debt Collection Practices Act, FDCPA (Reg F)** | Restricts third-party debt collectors: no harassment or false threats, no calls at inconvenient times (before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. local time), a validation notice and a right to dispute the debt within 30 days |
| **Electronic Fund Transfer Act, EFTA (Reg E)** | Protects debit card and electronic transfer users; limits liability for unauthorized transfers and sets error-resolution procedures |
| **Real Estate Settlement Procedures Act, RESPA (Reg X)** | Mortgage settlement disclosures, limits on kickbacks, servicing rules |
| **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act** | Requires financial institutions to give privacy notices and safeguard customer information |

### UDAAP

Dodd-Frank bans **unfair, deceptive or abusive acts or practices (UDAAP)** in consumer financial products. An act is **unfair** if it causes or is likely to cause substantial injury consumers cannot reasonably avoid and that is not outweighed by benefits; **deceptive** if a representation, omission or practice is likely to mislead a reasonable consumer in a material way; and **abusive** if it materially interferes with a consumer's ability to understand terms or takes unreasonable advantage of the consumer's lack of understanding, inability to protect his or her interests, or reasonable reliance on the firm. The FTC Act and state laws have similar standards.

### Regulation E liability tiers

For an unauthorized debit or electronic transfer, a consumer's liability depends on timing:

- **\$50 at most** if the consumer notifies the institution within two business days of learning of the loss or theft.
- **Up to \$500** if the consumer notifies later than two business days (but within 60 days of the statement showing the transfer): the liability is the lesser of \$500 or the sum of (a) \$50 or the amount of unauthorized transfers before the end of two business days, whichever is less, and (b) the amount of unauthorized transfers after two business days and before notice.
- **More**, potentially unlimited for transfers made after 60 days from the statement date if the institution could have stopped them had the consumer notified in time.

Institutions must investigate errors reported within 60 days and generally complete the investigation within 10 business days, or give a provisional credit and finish within 45 days (up to 90 days in certain cases).

### The policy debate

Supporters of strong consumer rules argue that disclosure and bans on abusive practices correct information and bargaining imbalances and protect people who can least absorb losses. Critics argue that heavy regulation raises compliance costs, can reduce access to credit for the riskiest borrowers (for example by limiting small-dollar loans), and that disclosure overloads consumers. Many economists favor tailoring rules to evidence of harm. Both views are part of the current discussion.

@example
title: The APR of a short-term loan
problem: A lender charges a \$15 fee on a \$100, 14-day loan. What is the APR, computed as the fee divided by the amount borrowed, annualized by 365/14?
step: The fee rate for the period is $15/100 = 15\%$ for 14 days.
step: Annualize by multiplying by $365/14 = 26.0714$ periods in a year: $15\% \times 26.0714 = 391.07\%$.
step: A fee that sounds small, 15%, is an APR near 391%. That is why TILA requires the APR: it lets consumers compare the cost of loans of different lengths.
answer: 391.07%.

@example
title: Reg E liability
problem: Dana loses her debit card on Monday and learns of it that day. Unauthorized transfers are made: \$90 on Tuesday, \$600 on Thursday and \$300 on Friday morning. She reports the loss on Friday afternoon. What is her liability under Regulation E?
step: Two business days after learning end at the close of Wednesday. Unauthorized transfers within that period: \$90 (Tuesday). After it and before notice: \$600 + \$300 = \$900.
step: Part (a): the lesser of \$50 or \$90 is \$50. Part (b): \$900. The sum is \$950.
step: Liability is the lesser of \$500 or \$950, so \$500. Had she reported by Wednesday, her liability would have been at most \$50.
answer: \$500.

@mistakes
- Treating a "small" fee as a low-cost loan. Compare the APR, which reflects the cost per year.
- Assuming a debit card has the same \$50 cap as a credit card in all cases. For debit cards the cap depends on reporting within two business days.
- Believing debt collectors may say anything to collect a time-barred or disputed debt. The FDCPA prohibits false, deceptive or harassing conduct.
- Assuming the CFPB is the only enforcer. State attorneys general, the FTC and private lawsuits also matter, and the CFPB's activity is changing.

hint: The consumer can dispute the debt and file complaints.
solution: The FDCPA and Regulation F govern third-party debt collectors. A call at 6:30 a.m. is before 8 a.m. in the consumer's time zone, which is presumed inconvenient. A threat of arrest for non-payment of a consumer debt is a false or deceptive threat, since failing to pay a civil debt is not a crime, and it is an abusive practice. The consumer should request written validation, dispute the debt in writing (keeping copies), keep records of the calls, check credit reports for the account, and may file complaints with the CFPB, the FTC and the state attorney general. The consumer may sue for actual damages, statutory damages of up to \$1,000, and attorney's fees under the FDCPA. This is general education; consumers can contact a legal aid organization or consumer attorney.
