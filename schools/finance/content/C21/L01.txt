@article
Every financial promise you will meet in this course, from a loan agreement to a brokerage account agreement, is a contract sitting inside a legal system. When a deal goes wrong, the question is rarely "was it fair?" but "what did the law and the contract require?" This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the method for answering that question. It is general education, not legal advice.

### Where US law comes from

US law has four main sources, arranged in a hierarchy:

1. **Constitutions.** The US Constitution is supreme. Under the Supremacy Clause, valid federal law overrides conflicting state law. Each state also has its own constitution.
2. **Statutes.** Laws passed by Congress or a state legislature, such as the Securities Act of 1933 or the Uniform Commercial Code (UCC), which each state adopts as its own law.
3. **Administrative regulations.** Rules written by agencies under power Congress has delegated, such as the SEC's rules under the securities laws. A rule that goes beyond the agency's statute can be struck down.
4. **Common law.** Law made by judges in deciding cases. Courts follow **precedent** (*stare decisis*): earlier decisions of a higher court in the same jurisdiction bind later courts.

Courts are organized as trial courts, appeals courts and a supreme court, in both the federal and the state systems. Most contract disputes are state-law matters. Many commercial contracts also choose a **governing law** (for example "the law of the State of New York") and a forum, or require **arbitration** instead of a court case.

### What makes a contract

A **contract** is a promise or set of promises the law will enforce. Courts look for:

- **Mutual assent**: an **offer** (a definite proposal that invites acceptance) and an **acceptance**. At common law the acceptance must mirror the offer; a reply that changes terms is a counteroffer.
- **Consideration**: something of value bargained for and exchanged, such as money, goods, services or a promise to do or refrain from something. A bare promise to give a gift is not enforceable.
- **Capacity**: the parties must be able to understand the deal. Contracts of minors are generally voidable at the minor's option.
- **Legality**: the purpose and subject matter must be lawful.

Even a contract with every element can be avoided for **misrepresentation, duress, undue influence, mistake or unconscionability**.

### Common law versus the UCC

Contracts for services, land and most intangibles follow state **common law**. Contracts for the sale of **goods** (movable things) follow **UCC Article 2**, which is more flexible: it allows a contract despite open terms, lets merchants' confirmations form a contract, and in section 2-207 relaxes the mirror-image rule for the "battle of the forms."

### Writing, parol evidence and the statute of frauds

Most contracts can be oral, but the **statute of frauds** requires a signed writing for certain categories: sales of goods for \$500 or more (UCC 2-201), sales of land, promises to pay another's debt (suretyship), contracts that by their terms cannot be performed within one year, and a few others. An email can satisfy the writing requirement if it shows a contract and is signed (a typed name will do). The **parol evidence rule** keeps out earlier or side agreements that contradict a final written contract.

### Remedies

The usual goal is **expectation damages**: the money that puts the injured party where full performance would have. For a buyer of goods who "covers" by buying substitute goods after the seller breaks the contract, damages are the cover price minus the contract price, plus incidental costs, less expenses saved (UCC 2-712). A seller who resells after the buyer breaks recovers the contract price minus the resale price plus incidental costs (UCC 2-706). Other remedies are **reliance** damages, **restitution** (the value of a benefit conferred), and **specific performance** (an order to perform, for unique goods or land). Punitive damages are generally not awarded for breach of contract, and the injured party must take reasonable steps to **mitigate** its loss.

### Briefing a case

A **case brief** is a short, disciplined analysis. Use **FIRAC**: **F**acts (only the legally relevant ones), **I**ssue (the question the court must answer, phrased with the facts), **R**ule (the statute or doctrine), **A**pplication (apply the rule to these facts, addressing the other side's best argument) and **C**onclusion. Keep the application tied to facts rather than to what feels fair.

@example
title: Briefing a goods contract dispute
problem: Bellwether Foods emailed Larkspur Outfitters: "We will buy 2,000 insulated lunch totes at \$9 each, delivery by March 1. - R. Okafor, Purchasing." Larkspur replied: "Agreed, will ship by March 1." Two days later Bellwether wrote that it had changed its mind. Larkspur resold the totes elsewhere at \$7.50 each, with no incidental costs. Brief the dispute and find Larkspur's damages.
step: **Facts and issue.** Offer by email, acceptance by email, then repudiation by Bellwether. Issue: is there an enforceable contract for the sale of goods worth \$18,000 even though neither party signed a formal document?
step: **Rule.** UCC Article 2 governs because totes are goods. Under UCC 2-201 a contract for \$500 or more needs a writing signed by the party to be charged. A seller who resells after a buyer's breach recovers contract price minus resale price plus incidental damages (UCC 2-706).
step: **Application.** Bellwether's email states the quantity, price and date and carries a typed name that shows intent to sign, so it is a signed writing against Bellwether. Larkspur's reply is an unconditional acceptance. Bellwether's change of mind is a repudiation, so it breached.
step: **Damages.** $2{,}000 \times (\$9.00 - \$7.50) = \$3{,}000$.
step: **Conclusion.** An enforceable contract existed and Bellwether breached; Larkspur recovers \$3,000 plus any incidental costs, less expenses saved.
answer: Enforceable under UCC 2-201; damages of \$3,000.

@example
title: Cover damages
problem: Halloran Packaging agrees to buy 5,000 shipping cartons from Tern Corrugated at \$10.00 each. Tern repudiates before delivery. Halloran buys 5,000 equivalent cartons from another supplier at \$11.40 each and pays \$450 in extra freight and search costs. What are Halloran's expectation damages?
step: Halloran is the buyer of goods, so UCC 2-712 (cover) applies: damages are the cover price minus the contract price, plus incidental costs.
step: Price difference: $5{,}000 \times (\$11.40 - \$10.00) = \$7{,}000$.
step: Add incidental costs of \$450 to get \$7,450. Halloran put itself in the position the contract would have, so it is not paid the whole contract price.
answer: \$7,450.

@mistakes
- Treating a bare promise or a gift as a contract: without bargained-for consideration there is nothing to enforce.
- Applying common law rules to a sale of goods (or the reverse). Always ask first: goods, or services and land?
- Computing damages as the whole contract price instead of the difference that puts the injured party in the position performance would have.
- Writing a brief that retells every fact and never states the issue as a question the rule can answer.

@practice self
prompt: Brief the following fictional case in FIRAC form (no more than 200 words).

Quillfeather Advisory, a consulting firm, orally agreed with Marsh Hollow Dairy to run a cost-reduction project over 18 months for \$12,000. Quillfeather worked for seven months, then Marsh Hollow ended the arrangement and refused to pay. Quillfeather sued for breach. Marsh Hollow raised the statute of frauds. The state follows the common law rule that a contract that by its terms cannot be performed within one year must be in a signed writing, and it allows restitution for the value of benefits conferred.
hint: Decide first whether the contract, by its own terms, could have been performed within one year.
hint: Consider what remedy remains if the contract itself cannot be enforced.
solution: Facts: Quillfeather and Marsh Hollow made an oral 18-month consulting contract for \$12,000; Quillfeather performed for seven months before Marsh Hollow ended it. Issue: Is the oral contract unenforceable under the statute of frauds because it cannot be performed within one year, and can Quillfeather still recover? Rule: contracts that by their terms cannot be performed within one year require a signed writing; a party who confers a benefit under an unenforceable contract may recover its value in restitution. Application: the stated term is 18 months, so performance could not be completed within a year and the one-year provision applies; there is no signed writing, so Quillfeather cannot sue on the contract for the unpaid balance. Quillfeather may argue partial performance, but this state recognizes only restitution. The value of seven months of work is recoverable. Conclusion: the contract is unenforceable, but Quillfeather may recover the reasonable value of its services in restitution. Some states treat full performance by one side as removing the bar, so the outcome depends on the jurisdiction.
rubric: States the facts that matter (oral, 18 months, seven months performed, refusal to pay) without padding.
rubric: Identifies the one-year provision of the statute of frauds as the controlling rule and applies it to the 18-month term.
rubric: Concludes that the contract is unenforceable but that restitution for the value of services already provided remains available.
rubric: Acknowledges that the result can differ across states or exceptions.

@practice self
prompt: Redfern Builders agreed in a signed contract to build a warehouse for Calloway Logistics for \$900,000. Midway through, Redfern's crew hit unexpected buried concrete that made excavation far more costly. Redfern said it would stop unless paid \$40,000 more, and Calloway signed a written promise to pay it. After the warehouse was finished, Calloway refused to pay the extra \$40,000. Write a one-page analysis for Calloway's lawyer weighing each side's best argument. State whether this is a sale of goods or a services contract and how that matters.
hint: Ask what Redfern gave in exchange for the extra \$40,000 that it had not already promised.
hint: Consider the argument that the buried concrete was unforeseen and the modification fair, and consider duress.
solution: This is a construction services contract, so state common law applies, not UCC Article 2 (under UCC 2-209 a good-faith modification of a goods contract needs no new consideration). Calloway's best argument is the preexisting duty rule: Redfern already had a duty to build the warehouse for \$900,000, so a promise to do only that gave Calloway no new value and the \$40,000 promise lacks consideration. It can add that Redfern's threat to stop work may be economic duress if Calloway had no practical alternative. Redfern's best argument is the modification exception recognized by many courts and by the Restatement (Second) of Contracts: where unanticipated circumstances arise and the new terms are fair and equitable, a modification is enforceable without new consideration. Buried concrete, unforeseen by both parties, fits that exception, and the \$40,000 reflects added cost. Calloway signed the modification in writing and Redfern completed the project on the modified terms, which also supports enforcement. On balance, a court that follows the strict preexisting duty rule would side with Calloway; a court that follows the modern modification exception would likely side with Redfern, so the governing state's law and the evidence on foreseeability and fairness decide the case.
rubric: Classifies the contract as services (common law), not a sale of goods, and notes the relevance of UCC 2-209 only by contrast.
rubric: Explains the preexisting duty rule and why the \$40,000 promise may lack consideration.
rubric: Presents Redfern's counterargument (unforeseen difficulty, fair modification) and the duress issue in balance.
rubric: Reaches a reasoned conclusion that depends on the governing law and the facts.

@quiz self
prompt: Pinecrest Mills emailed Harbor Grain: "We offer 400 pallets of oat flour at \$11 per pallet, delivery April 10." Harbor Grain replied on its own purchase form: "We accept. Any dispute will go to binding arbitration." Both are merchants in a state that has adopted the UCC, and Pinecrest never agreed to arbitrate. In one paragraph, say whether a contract exists and whether the arbitration clause is part of it.
hint: Decide whether UCC 2-207 or the common law mirror-image rule applies.
hint: Ask whether Harbor Grain's acceptance was expressly conditional on Pinecrest agreeing to arbitrate, and whether the new term is a material alteration.
solution: Oat flour is a good and both parties are merchants, so UCC 2-207 applies rather than the mirror-image rule. Harbor Grain's reply is a definite and seasonable expression of acceptance and is not expressly conditional on assent to the added term, so a contract exists on Pinecrest's terms. Between merchants, an additional term becomes part of the contract unless the offer limits acceptance to its terms, the term materially alters the contract, or the offeror objects. Courts generally treat an arbitration clause as a material alteration because it removes the right to go to court, so it does not become part of the contract. The result is a contract for 400 pallets at \$11 without the arbitration clause; a few courts reason differently on arbitration, so the answer is the general rule rather than a certainty.
rubric: Applies UCC 2-207 (goods between merchants) instead of the common law mirror-image rule, and concludes a contract exists.
rubric: Applies the additional-terms test (material alteration, objection, limit in the offer) to the arbitration clause.
rubric: Concludes the arbitration clause is generally excluded and notes that courts can differ.
