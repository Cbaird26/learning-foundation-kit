@article
Rules do not enforce themselves. Two firms with identical policies can behave very differently, because **culture**, meaning what people believe will be rewarded, tolerated and punished, decides which rules matter. Compliance is how a firm turns rules into daily behavior; whistleblowing is the safety valve when it fails. This is general education, not legal advice.

### Culture and tone

A firm's culture is set by the **tone at the top** (what leaders say and do), the **tone in the middle** (what managers actually reward when targets are tight), and the incentives built into pay. A bonus plan that pays only for revenue, a manager who laughs off a policy, or a team that punishes bad news will override a code of conduct. Regulators now examine culture directly: they ask who was promoted, who was fired, and what happened to the last person who raised a concern.

### Elements of an effective program

The **U.S. Sentencing Guidelines** (Chapter 8) describe an effective compliance and ethics program with seven elements: (1) standards and procedures to prevent and detect misconduct; (2) oversight by high-level personnel and adequate resources; (3) due care in giving substantial authority to individuals; (4) communication and **training**; (5) **monitoring, auditing** and a system for reporting that allows anonymity or confidentiality; (6) consistent **incentives and discipline**; and (7) prompt **response and remediation** when misconduct is found. The Department of Justice's guidance on evaluating corporate compliance programs asks three questions: is the program well designed, is it adequately resourced and empowered, and does it work in practice? Typical features are a **risk assessment**, written policies, training, a hotline, investigations, third-party due diligence, and testing of controls. The SEC, in its 2001 *Seaboard* report, lists factors it weighs in giving credit to firms that police themselves: self-policing before discovery, **self-reporting**, **remediation** and **cooperation**.

Firms organize responsibility in **three lines of defense**: the first line is the business units that own risk and must follow the rules; the second line is **risk management and compliance**, which sets policy, monitors and advises; and the third line is **internal audit**, which independently tests the first two and reports to the board. A **chief compliance officer** needs authority, access to the board and independence from revenue pressure. In Delaware, directors have a **duty of oversight** (*Caremark*, and *Marchand v. Barnhill*, 2019): they must make a good-faith effort to ensure a reporting system exists for mission-critical risks and must respond to red flags.

### Whistleblowing

A **whistleblower** reports suspected wrongdoing. Law protects and sometimes rewards people who do:

- **Sarbanes-Oxley Section 806** (18 U.S.C. § 1514A) protects employees of public companies and certain contractors who report suspected securities fraud, mail or wire fraud, or violations of SEC rules, to a supervisor, a federal agency or Congress, from discharge or retaliation. A complaint is filed with the Department of Labor (OSHA) within **180 days**; remedies include reinstatement, back pay and damages. **Section 301** requires audit committees to set up procedures for receiving complaints, including **anonymous** ones from employees.
- **Dodd-Frank Section 21F** created the **SEC whistleblower program**: an individual who voluntarily provides **original information** that leads to a successful enforcement action with sanctions over **\$1 million** may receive **10% to 30%** of the money collected. In *Digital Realty Trust v. Somers* (2018) the Supreme Court held that the Dodd-Frank anti-retaliation protection covers only people who have reported to the SEC. The CFTC runs a similar program.
- **SEC Rule 21F-17** prohibits any action, such as a confidentiality agreement clause, that impedes a person from communicating with the SEC about possible violations.
- The **False Claims Act** lets private persons (**relators**) sue on behalf of the government for fraud against government programs (*qui tam*) and share in the recovery (generally 15% to 25% if the government joins the case and 25% to 30% if it does not).

Many compliance professionals encourage employees to report **internally first**, and a program that handles reports well can catch problems early; the law does not require internal reporting before going to a regulator. Supporters of whistleblower awards say they find hidden frauds and offset the personal and career risk of reporting. Critics say that large awards may lead people to bypass internal channels, undermining compliance programs, and may produce low-quality tips. Empirical evidence and policy opinions differ, and many programs encourage internal reports through award provisions.

@example
title: A controller sees revenue pulled forward
problem: Nina, the controller of a public company, notices that her chief financial officer instructed the team to record \$3 million of next quarter's sales in this quarter's revenue, enough to meet analysts' expectations. She raised it with the CFO, who told her to "drop it." What are her options and protections?
step: **Facts and issue.** Possible improper revenue recognition and a false periodic report: a potential securities fraud and a breach of the CFO's certification duties (Sarbanes-Oxley Section 302).
step: **Internal options.** Report to the audit committee through the complaint procedure required by SOX Section 301 (anonymously if she wishes) or to the chief compliance officer or general counsel. Document facts and keep copies of only what she is entitled to hold.
step: **External options.** She may report to the SEC (including anonymously through an attorney). Reporting to the SEC with original information that leads to a sanction over \$1 million could entitle her to an award of 10% to 30%.
step: **Protections.** SOX Section 806 protects her from retaliation for reporting to a supervisor or the SEC; if she is retaliated against, she must file with OSHA within 180 days. Dodd-Frank retaliation claims require that she has reported to the SEC. Her employer may not use a confidentiality agreement to block communications with the SEC (Rule 21F-17).
step: **Conclusion.** Each route has costs and benefits, so she should consider consulting an employment lawyer. An internal report to the audit committee, with a record, is a common first step.
answer: Report internally to the audit committee and/or externally to the SEC; SOX Section 806 protects both, and Dodd-Frank retaliation protection requires an SEC report.

@example
title: Whistleblower award range
problem: An SEC enforcement action based on a whistleblower's original information results in \$8.4 million in monetary sanctions collected. What is the range of the whistleblower's possible award?
step: The sanctions exceed \$1 million, so the award program applies.
step: Minimum: $10\% \times \$8{,}400{,}000 = \$840{,}000$.
step: Maximum: $30\% \times \$8{,}400{,}000 = \$2{,}520{,}000$. The SEC decides within the range using factors such as the significance of the information and the whistleblower's assistance.
answer: \$840,000 to \$2,520,000.

@mistakes
- Assuming a code of conduct is enough. Without monitoring, incentives and consequences, it is only paper.
- Thinking a whistleblower must always report internally first. The law protects reporting to regulators, though internal reporting is often encouraged.
- Believing Dodd-Frank retaliation protection covers every internal reporter. After *Digital Realty*, it covers those who reported to the SEC (SOX Section 806 covers internal reports).
- Using confidentiality agreements that prevent employees from talking to the SEC; Rule 21F-17 prohibits this.

@quiz self
prompt: In one paragraph, explain how a firm's incentive system can undermine its compliance program, and give two changes that would reduce the risk.
hint: Think of targets, bonuses and what managers actually reward.
solution: If bonuses and promotions depend only on revenue or volume, employees learn that results matter more than how they are achieved, and a policy that conflicts with a target will be ignored or bent, which regulators see as a cultural failure. Managers who reward the top producers and dismiss red flags send the same signal. Changes include adding conduct and compliance measures to scorecards and pay decisions (including deferral and clawback of bonuses after misconduct or restatements), removing incentives tied to specific products where conflicts arise, protecting compliance staff with independence and board access, and consistently disciplining high performers who break rules. Tracking how often concerns are raised and resolved gives leaders evidence of whether the system works.
