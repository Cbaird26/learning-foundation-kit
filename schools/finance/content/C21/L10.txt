@article
Most ethical failures in finance do not look like cartoon villainy. They look like a manager rounding a number up, a banker softening a warning, or a salesperson talking only about the upside. Frameworks help you slow down, see the issue, and give reasons beyond "it felt fine." This is general education; the frameworks are tools for reasoning, not formulas that settle every case.

### Law is a floor

**Law** sets minimum standards backed by sanctions. **Ethics** asks what we *should* do, which can go beyond what we *must* do. An act can be legal but unethical (a lawful but misleading sales pitch that exploits a customer's confusion) and, rarely, illegal but defended on ethical grounds. Professionals are expected to comply with the law **and** to meet ethical standards, such as a firm's code of conduct or the CFA Institute Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (which, for example, require a member to follow the stricter of the law and the Standards when they differ).

### Four frameworks

| Framework | Core question | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Consequentialism (utilitarianism)** | Which option produces the greatest net benefit for everyone affected? | Looks at real outcomes; flexible | Hard to measure; can justify harming a minority; invites self-serving estimates |
| **Deontology (duty and rights)** | What duties do I have, and what rights must I respect, regardless of the outcome? | Protects individuals; firm rules such as honesty and keeping promises | Duties can conflict; rigid |
| **Virtue ethics** | What would a person of good character (honest, fair, courageous, prudent) do? | Focuses on habits and culture | Gives less specific guidance |
| **Justice and fairness** | Is the process fair, and would I accept the rule if I did not know whose position I would occupy? | Addresses distribution and procedure | Competing ideas of fairness |

A thoughtful decision-maker uses several lenses. When they agree, the choice is easy. When they conflict, the disagreement itself shows what is at stake.

### Practical tests

- **The law test:** is it legal, and consistent with firm policy?
- **The publicity ("front page") test:** would I be comfortable if the decision and my reasons were reported publicly?
- **The reversibility test:** would I accept this if I were the customer or the counterparty?
- **The consistency test:** would I be comfortable if everyone in my position acted this way?

### An ethical decision framework

1. **Identify the facts** and what is unknown.
2. **Identify the stakeholders** (clients, colleagues, the firm, shareholders, counterparties, the market, the public) and their interests and rights.
3. **Identify the ethical and legal issues** (duties owed, conflicts of interest, rules).
4. **List the options**, including ones that avoid the dilemma, such as asking questions, disclosing, or declining.
5. **Evaluate each option** through the frameworks and tests.
6. **Decide, act and document**, escalating where appropriate.
7. **Review** the outcome and what could prevent a repeat.

### Why good people err

Behavioral research shows predictable pressures: **incentives** (bonuses that reward results only), **conflicts of interest** (where self-interest can bias judgment even in honest people), **ethical fading** (the moral side of a decision disappears when it is framed as a business or technical question), **rationalization** ("everyone does it," "it's only temporary"), **conformity and obedience** to authority, and **loss aversion** (people take more risk to avoid a certain loss). Controls, culture and independent challenge matter because intention alone is not enough.

### The purpose of the firm: a debate

Two views are commonly discussed. The **shareholder primacy** view holds that managers owe their main duty to maximize long-run shareholder value within the law, because shareholders hold residual claims and clear duties keep managers accountable. The **stakeholder** view holds that firms should weigh the interests of employees, customers, communities and others as well, because their well-being drives lasting value and fairness. Many writers argue that over the long run the two often coincide, while others stress that real conflicts arise. Delaware law, which governs many corporations, generally frames directors' duties as owed to the corporation and its stockholders but gives boards wide room under the business judgment rule to consider other constituencies when they relate to long-term value.

@example
title: Four lenses on a softened rating
problem: Priya, a research analyst, thinks Orrin Tools' stock deserves a "sell." Her manager asks her to change it to "hold," because the firm's investment bankers hope to win a stock offering from Orrin. What would each framework say?
step: **Facts and stakeholders.** Priya's honest view, the manager's request, a conflict of interest between research and banking; stakeholders are investors who rely on the research, Priya, the firm and Orrin.
step: **Consequentialist.** A misleading rating harms investors who rely on it and, if discovered, the firm's reputation; the benefit is a banking fee. Counting all stakeholders, the net benefit is likely negative.
step: **Deontological.** Priya has a duty of honesty to the readers and, under the firm's and regulators' rules, a duty to keep research independent of banking pressure. The request asks her to violate that duty whatever the outcome.
step: **Virtue and publicity tests.** An honest, courageous analyst would not misstate her opinion, and the decision would not survive being reported on the front page.
step: **Decision.** Keep the rating, document the request, and escalate to compliance. Regulations also restrict banking influence on research analysts, so escalating protects both Priya and the firm.
answer: All lenses point toward keeping the honest rating and escalating.

@example
title: Expected value is not the whole answer
problem: A firm can market a product honestly and earn \$2.0 million profit, or market it misleadingly and earn \$6.0 million. If the misleading marketing is detected (probability 25%), the firm pays a \$30 million penalty and loses \$10 million of future business. Compare the expected profit of each option and say what the calculation leaves out.
step: Honest marketing: \$2.0 million.
step: Misleading marketing: $\$6.0\text{ million} - 0.25 \times (\$30\text{ million} + \$10\text{ million}) = 6.0 - 10.0 = -\$4.0$ million.
step: The honest option wins even on a cold expected-value basis. But the calculation omits the harm to customers misled, the firm's duty not to deceive them and the culture it teaches. A purely financial calculation can also be gamed by choosing a low detection probability, so honest ethics does not depend on the numbers.
answer: Honest: +\$2.0 million; misleading: -\$4.0 million expected; the calculation ignores harm to customers and duty.

@mistakes
- Assuming that "it's legal" ends the ethical analysis. Law is a floor.
- Treating utilitarianism as the pursuit of the firm's profit only; it counts the well-being of everyone affected.
- Picking the framework that gives the answer you want. Use several and notice disagreements.
- Skipping the facts: many ethical errors begin by not asking what is actually known.

@practice self
prompt: Analyze this fictional consumer complaint using legal rules and ethical reasoning (250 to 350 words). A customer writes: "On March 3, I saw on my February 28 statement three debit card purchases totaling \$1,500 that I did not make. I still have my card and never lost it. I reported this by phone on March 20. The bank refused to refund me, saying I should have kept my card secure, and charged \$35 overdraft fees on two later purchases." Identify the facts, the rules, the likely violations, the remedies and the ethical issues.
hint: Regulation E covers unauthorized electronic transfers and sets error-resolution procedures.
hint: Consider the 60-day reporting rule, whether the card was lost, and whether consumer negligence changes liability.
solution: Facts: three unauthorized debit transactions (\$1,500) appeared on a statement dated February 28; the customer still holds the card and reported on March 20, within 60 days of the statement; the bank refused a refund blaming the customer and charged two overdraft fees. Rules: the Electronic Fund Transfer Act and Regulation E govern unauthorized electronic transfers. A consumer who reports within 60 days of the statement is generally not liable for unauthorized transfers when the card was not lost or stolen (the tiered \$50/\$500 limits are tied to loss or theft of the access device), and an institution may not treat consumer negligence as the basis for imposing greater liability. The bank must investigate promptly (generally within 10 business days, or provide a provisional credit and finish within 45 days) and give results in writing. Likely violations: refusing the refund on the stated ground, and failing to follow error-resolution steps; the overdraft fees caused by the unauthorized transfers should also be reimbursed, and the pattern may raise UDAAP concerns. Remedies: the consumer should send a written dispute, keep records, and file complaints with the CFPB and the state regulator; EFTA allows actual and statutory damages and attorney's fees. Ethical issues: the bank puts its own interest ahead of a customer's legal right, shifts blame, and profits from fees triggered by the fraud; under deontology it breaches duties of honesty and fairness, a utilitarian view counts the harm to the customer and trust in the bank, and the publicity test is failed. This is general education; the specific facts determine the result.
rubric: Identifies Regulation E, the 60-day timing and that consumer negligence is not a basis for refusal.
rubric: Names the likely violations and the remedies available, including complaint channels.
rubric: Gives a coherent ethical analysis using at least two frameworks or the publicity test.
