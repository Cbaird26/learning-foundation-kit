@project
title: Compliance memo on an enforcement case
overview: You will analyze a described (fictional) enforcement case against a broker-dealer, identify the laws and rules broken, explain what each regulator did and why, and write a four-page memo to the firm's board recommending controls and ethical practices to prevent a repeat. The case combines anti-money laundering failures, insider trading, a best-interest failure and interference with whistleblowers, so it draws on most of the course. This is a learning exercise based on invented facts, not legal advice.
goal: Match each fact in a multi-issue case to the statute, rule and regulator that govern it, naming each correctly.
goal: Explain the purpose and likely consequences of each regulatory action in plain, balanced language.
goal: Write a concise, well-organized compliance memo whose recommendations address root causes in controls, incentives and culture.
step: Read the case file in the materials twice. On the second reading, highlight each fact that could be a violation and list unknown facts you would want to investigate.
step: Build a table with four columns: fact, law or rule (statute, SEC or FINRA rule, regulation), regulator or enforcer, and why it applies. Cover at least the AML failures, the insider trading, the product recommendations to retirees and the treatment of the compliance employees.
step: For each regulator action in the case file, write two or three sentences explaining the legal basis, the remedy (penalty, disgorgement, undertakings, criminal charges) and the purpose it serves (deterrence, restitution, reform).
step: Identify root causes using the lessons on culture and incentives: which controls were missing or not working, which incentives pulled toward misconduct, and what the leadership signals were. Separate individual wrongdoing from firm-level failures.
step: Write the recommendations: for each root cause give a specific control or ethical practice (who owns it, how it works, how the board would know it is working). Include at least one recommendation each on AML, trading and information barriers, Regulation Best Interest, compensation, and speak-up channels.
step: Draft the memo (about four pages, 1,600 to 2,000 words) with these headings: Summary; Facts; Laws and rules implicated; Regulators' actions; Root causes; Recommendations; How the board will measure progress. Include one balanced paragraph on the main counterargument to your recommendations (for example cost or effect on sales).
step: Revise: check every statute and rule name against the course lessons, remove any claim the case file does not support, and mark unknowns clearly.
material: **Case file (fictional).** Calloway Harbor Securities, Inc. ("Calloway") is an SEC-registered broker-dealer, a FINRA member and a subsidiary of a bank holding company. It has 900 registered representatives, serves retail and institutional customers, and has an investment banking division advising on mergers. The following facts were established in the regulators' orders. All names and numbers are invented.

1. **Anti-money laundering.** Over 22 months, Eastgate Trading LLC, a company that listed a mailbox address and refused to name its owners, received about \$14 million in wires from five countries and sent about 96% of each wire onward within two days. Customer-onboarding staff recorded that the owner would "not provide ownership information," but the account stayed open. Calloway's AML compliance officer worked part time on AML, the firm's AML program had no independent test for three years, and no suspicious activity report (SAR) was filed on Eastgate. A 2023 internal audit memo recommending a review of foreign wires was not acted on.
2. **Insider trading.** Mr. Reyes, a Calloway investment banker on the team advising Tamarack Foods on its planned acquisition of Birchwood Dairy, told his college roommate, Mr. Lund, in a casual phone call that "something big is coming for Birchwood." Mr. Lund bought Birchwood call options and, on the announcement, made \$310,000 in profit. Mr. Lund then told a coworker, who bought Birchwood shares and made \$40,000. Calloway's restricted list did not include Birchwood until the day of the announcement, and bankers were not required to pre-clear personal or family trades in options.
3. **Retail recommendations.** Calloway's compensation grid paid representatives three times the usual commission on a proprietary structured note with a seven-year term. A sales contest called "Notes November" gave trips to the top sellers. In a review of 1,100 sales, regulators found that 64% went to customers aged 65 or older, many of whom had written objectives of preservation of capital and need for liquidity within five years. Comparable lower-cost alternatives were available but were not discussed in the files. Calloway's Form CRS was delivered, but the supervisory system did not test whether recommendations were in customers' best interest.
4. **Compliance staff and culture.** The chief compliance officer, Ms. Okoye, wrote to the CEO that the notes were "unsuitable for most retirees" and that the contest created a conflict. Two months later she was moved to a "special projects" role. A compliance analyst who asked to speak to the SEC was told that his separation agreement and employee handbook required him to "notify the firm before communicating with any government agency about the firm's business."
5. **Regulators' actions.** The **SEC** found that Calloway willfully violated Regulation Best Interest, failed to file SARs as required of broker-dealers (Exchange Act Rule 17a-8), failed to establish and enforce policies to prevent misuse of material nonpublic information (Exchange Act Section 15(g)), and violated SEC Rule 21F-17 through its agreement and handbook language. It censured the firm, ordered it to cease and desist, ordered disgorgement of \$7 million of commissions to be returned to customers, imposed a civil penalty of \$16 million and required the firm to retain an independent compliance consultant for three years. **FINRA** fined the firm \$5 million for supervisory and AML program failures and suspended the responsible supervisors. **FinCEN** assessed a \$9 million civil penalty for BSA violations. The **Department of Justice** charged Mr. Reyes and Mr. Lund criminally with securities fraud. The firm's earlier internal audit and CCO letters were cited as evidence that senior management knew of the problems.
deliverable: A four-page compliance memo (about 1,600 to 2,000 words) to the board of Calloway Harbor Securities with the seven headings above.
deliverable: The fact-law-regulator table from step 2, as an appendix, with every legal name checked.
rubric: Correctly identifies the law, rule and enforcer for each of the four problem areas, and distinguishes the firm's liability from the individuals' liability (for example the criminal charges against the banker and his friend).
rubric: Explains each regulator's action accurately, including purpose and remedy, and separates SEC, FINRA, FinCEN and DOJ roles.
rubric: Root cause analysis names specific control, incentive and cultural failures, supported by facts in the case file, without adding unsupported facts.
rubric: Recommendations are specific, with an owner and a way to measure whether each works, and address AML, information barriers, Reg BI, compensation and speak-up channels.
rubric: The memo is clear, balanced (includes a counterargument), organized under the required headings and treated as general education rather than legal advice.
safety: The case and all names are invented; do not substitute a real firm or person's name or present your analysis as a statement about any real company. For any real case you want to study, use the regulator's published orders and court documents and attribute them.
tutor: Which facts in this case belong to the firm's liability and which only to the individuals, and why does the difference matter?
tutor: How would you test whether a firm's best-interest and compensation controls actually work, rather than only exist on paper?
tutor: What is the strongest argument against my recommendations on compensation, and how should the memo answer it?

@review
### Week by week

1. **Legal system and contracts.** Constitution, statutes, regulations, case law; the Supremacy Clause. Contract elements: offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, legality. UCC Article 2 for goods versus common law for services and land. Statute of frauds. Expectation damages and cover damages (UCC 2-712). FIRAC case briefs.
2. **Agency and organizations.** Actual authority, apparent authority, ratification, respondeat superior. Sole proprietorship, partnership, LLC, S and C corporations. Double taxation, directors' duties of care and loyalty, the business judgment rule, veil piercing.
3. **Securities law.** 1933 Act (Section 5 registration, Section 11, exemptions including Regulation D, Regulation A and crowdfunding); 1934 Act (SEC, periodic reporting, Section 10(b) and Rule 10b-5, Section 16(b)).
4. **Regulators.** SEC, FINRA (an SRO), CFTC and NFA, Federal Reserve, OCC, FDIC, NCUA, states; Dodd-Frank, Gramm-Leach-Bliley, the GENIUS Act; *Trump v. Slaughter*.
5. **Disclosure.** Forms 10-K, 10-Q and 8-K; materiality (*TSC*, *Basic*); Regulation FD; SOX Sections 302, 404 and 906; clawbacks.
6. **Insider trading and manipulation.** Classical, misappropriation and tipper-tippee theories; Rule 10b5-1; Section 21A penalties; wash trades, spoofing, pump-and-dump.
7. **Advisers and broker-dealers.** Advisers Act fiduciary duty; Form ADV and Form CRS; Regulation Best Interest and its four obligations; FINRA Rule 2111.
8. **AML and sanctions.** Placement, layering, integration; CIP, CDD, CTRs over \$10,000, structuring, SARs, tipping off; OFAC and the 50 Percent Rule.
9. **Consumer protection.** CFPB (check current status), TILA, ECOA, FCRA, FDCPA, EFTA and Regulation E, UDAAP.
10. **Ethics frameworks.** Consequentialism, deontology, virtue and justice; law as a floor; the decision framework; incentives and ethical fading.
11. **Dilemmas.** Conflicts, front-running, churning, cherry-picking, soft dollars; writing a resolution.
12. **Culture and whistleblowing.** Sentencing Guidelines program elements, three lines of defense, *Caremark*; SOX Section 806, Dodd-Frank Section 21F (10% to 30% of sanctions over \$1 million), Rule 21F-17.

### Key formulas and numbers

- Cover damages = (cover price - contract price) x units + incidental costs.
- Section 16(b): match lowest purchase with highest sale within less than six months.
- Maximum civil penalty for insider trading = 3 x (profit gained or loss avoided), plus disgorgement.
- Tiered advisory fee: apply each rate only to the assets in its tier. Cost-to-equity = costs / average equity; turnover = purchases / average equity.
- Reg E: \$50 within two business days; up to \$500 later; APR of a short loan = (fee / amount) x (365 / days).
- Whistleblower award = 10% to 30% of the sanctions collected, when the action's sanctions exceed \$1 million.

- Can you explain the difference between a fiduciary duty and Reg BI without overstating either?
- Can you decide an insider trading case using the three theories?
- Can you write a resolution with facts, stakeholders, rules, options, a decision and safeguards?

### Common errors

Calling FINRA a government agency; treating disclosure as a cure for any conflict; ignoring apparent authority; treating a 5% materiality rule as a safe harbor; assuming insider trading needs a company employee; forgetting that structuring is a crime even with clean money; confusing AML with sanctions; stating that rules are certain when they are changing (CFPB, climate disclosure, digital assets).

@exam self
prompt: Fictional case. Larkwood Capital, a registered investment adviser with \$600 million in assets, allocates block trades to client accounts after the close. Over two years the personal account of its founder has outperformed clients' accounts by a wide margin on those trades. A junior analyst raised the pattern with the chief compliance officer, who is the founder's brother-in-law, and was told to "stop looking for problems." The analyst is considering reporting to the SEC. Write a memo (350 to 500 words) that (a) identifies the laws and rules implicated, (b) explains what the SEC could do, (c) explains in general terms, and in balance, the protections and options open to the analyst, and (d) recommends three controls for the firm.
hint: Use the Advisers Act fiduciary duty, cherry-picking, and the whistleblower provisions.
hint: Consider both the compliance officer's conflict and the analyst's choices of internal versus external reporting.
solution: Laws and rules: Cherry-picking by an adviser breaches the Advisers Act fiduciary duty of loyalty and care and the antifraud provisions (Section 206), and the lack of an allocation policy and independent compliance suggests failure under the Compliance Rule (Rule 206(4)-7) and the code of ethics rule. The SEC could investigate through its enforcement and examination staff, seek disgorgement of the founder's gains, civil penalties, a cease-and-desist order, industry bars, an independent compliance consultant and, with the DOJ, criminal charges if fraud is shown. The analyst's options: escalate in writing to another independent channel (the board or outside counsel, since the compliance officer is conflicted) or report to the SEC. A report to the SEC with original information can lead to an award of 10% to 30% of sanctions above \$1 million, and Dodd-Frank anti-retaliation protection applies to those who have reported to the SEC; SOX Section 806 protects employees of public companies and certain contractors (not necessarily a private adviser), so protections differ and the analyst should consult an employment lawyer. Rule 21F-17 means the firm cannot lawfully bar the analyst from contacting the SEC. The costs are career risk and the possibility that the facts have an innocent explanation, which an investigation would test. Controls: pre-trade allocation by written policy; independent review of allocation by someone who does not report to the founder, with statistical testing of returns by account; a chief compliance officer with independence and board access, and a confidential reporting channel and no-retaliation policy.
rubric: Identifies the fiduciary duty, cherry-picking, the Compliance Rule and the whistleblower rules accurately.
rubric: Describes SEC remedies and the analyst's options and protections in balance, including that protections differ by statute.
rubric: Recommends three specific controls that address the allocation practice and the compliance officer's conflict.
