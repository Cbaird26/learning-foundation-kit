@article
Week 5 showed that with perfect markets, leverage is irrelevant. In practice, two forces tilt the choice: interest on debt is **tax-deductible**, which favors debt, and high debt raises the chance and cost of financial distress, which argues against it. This week quantifies both.

### Modigliani-Miller with corporate taxes

Interest reduces taxable income, so each dollar of interest saves $t$ dollars of tax, where $t$ is the corporate tax rate (the rate that applies to the marginal dollar of income; examples here use 25%, an approximation of the US federal rate of 21% plus state taxes). If a firm borrows an amount $D$ permanently at rate $r_D$, the annual tax saving is $t\,r_D D$ and, discounted at $r_D$ (the tax shield is as risky as the debt),

$$PV(\text{tax shield}) = \frac{t\, r_D D}{r_D} = tD.$$

So, for permanent debt,

$$V_L = V_U + tD.$$

Value rises linearly with debt, and the cost of capital falls. With constant debt the cost of equity is (**MM Proposition II with taxes**)

$$r_E = r_0 + \frac{D}{E}(1 - t)(r_0 - r_D),$$

where $r_0$ is the unlevered cost of capital, and the WACC falls as debt is added:

$$\text{WACC} = r_0\left(1 - t\,\frac{D}{V}\right).$$

In beta terms the **Hamada equation**, assuming debt has zero beta, is $\beta_L = \beta_U\left[1 + (1 - t)\frac{D}{E}\right]$. Use it to unlever a comparable firm's beta and relever it at the target's leverage. These formulas assume permanent debt (a constant dollar amount); other policies, such as a constant debt ratio, give slightly different results.

A tax shield is only valuable if the firm has taxable income to shield. Firms with tax losses cannot use it, and the tax code limits the deductibility of interest: in the United States the deduction for business interest is generally capped (section 163(j)) at 30% of adjusted taxable income; under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, for tax years beginning after December 31, 2024, that income is again computed before depreciation and amortization (an EBITDA-like measure, as in 2018 to 2021). These details change often, so check current rules. Personal taxes matter as well: if investors pay more tax on interest than on equity returns, part of the corporate advantage is lost (the Miller model).

### Financial distress costs

The MM tax result says firms should borrow almost without limit, which they do not. A reason is **financial distress costs**:

- **Direct costs:** legal, accounting and administrative fees of a bankruptcy or restructuring.
- **Indirect costs:** lost customers who fear the firm will fail, suppliers who demand cash on delivery, lost key employees, and underinvestment because managers and creditors disagree about risky projects; assets sold at fire-sale prices.

The costs are not paid every year; their **present value** is the probability of distress times the cost if it happens, discounted. It rises with debt, slowly at first and then faster.

### The trade-off theory

The **trade-off theory** says firm value is

$$V_L = V_U + PV(\text{tax shield}) - PV(\text{distress costs}),$$

so the optimal debt level maximizes $V_L$, where the marginal tax benefit equals the marginal expected distress cost (and the WACC is minimized). Firms with stable cash flows and tangible assets (utilities, for instance) can borrow more than firms with volatile cash flows and intangible assets (early-stage technology firms), whose assets lose value in distress.

An alternative is the **pecking-order theory**: because managers know more than outsiders, new equity signals overvaluation, so firms prefer internal funds first, then debt, and equity last. It predicts that profitable firms borrow *less*, because they fund investment from retained earnings.

@example
title: A tax shield and the cost of capital
problem: An unlevered firm, Granite Peak Stores, is worth \$1,000 million, with an unlevered cost of capital of 10%. It issues \$400 million of permanent debt at 5%. The tax rate is 25%. Find the levered value, the value of equity, the cost of equity and the WACC.
step: Tax shield: $tD = 0.25 \times 400 = \$100$ million. So $V_L = 1{,}000 + 100 = \$1{,}100$ million, and equity is $1{,}100 - 400 = \$700$ million.
step: Cost of equity: $r_E = 0.10 + (400/700)(0.75)(0.10 - 0.05) = 0.10 + 0.02143 = 12.14\%$.
step: WACC with weights $E/V = 700/1{,}100$ and $D/V = 400/1{,}100$: $0.6364(12.14\%) + 0.3636(5\%)(0.75) = 7.727\% + 1.364\% = 9.09\%$.
step: Check with the shortcut: $r_0(1 - tD/V) = 10\%(1 - 0.25 \times 0.3636) = 9.09\%$. The WACC fell from 10% to 9.09%, which is why the value rose.
answer: $V_L = \$1{,}100$ million; equity \$700 million; $r_E = 12.14\%$; WACC $= 9.09\%$.

@example
title: Finding the optimal debt level
problem: A firm with $V_U = \$1{,}000$ million and a 25% tax rate considers permanent debt of various sizes. Analysts estimate the present value of distress costs (illustrative figures) as shown. Which debt level maximizes firm value?

| Debt $D$ (\$ million) | PV tax shield $tD$ | PV distress costs | $V_L$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 | 0 | 1,000 |
| 200 | 50 | 5 | 1,045 |
| 400 | 100 | 20 | 1,080 |
| 600 | 150 | 60 | 1,090 |
| 800 | 200 | 140 | 1,060 |

step: For each row, $V_L = 1{,}000 + tD - PV(\text{distress costs})$. For $D = 600$: $1{,}000 + 150 - 60 = 1{,}090$.
step: Value rises to 1,090 at \$600 million of debt, then falls to 1,060 at \$800 million as the distress costs grow faster than the tax shield.
step: Between \$600 and \$800 million, the extra tax shield is 50 but the extra distress cost is 80, so the marginal cost exceeds the marginal benefit.
answer: Debt of \$600 million (value \$1,090 million).

@mistakes
- Using $V_L = V_U + tD$ for debt that is not permanent, or for a firm without taxable income to shield.
- Forgetting the $(1 - t)$ factor in Proposition II with taxes, or using the pretax cost of debt in the WACC.
- Using the Hamada equation without first unlevering the comparable firm's beta at the comparable's own leverage.
- Treating the optimum as the point of maximum debt or as zero debt, rather than the point where marginal tax benefit equals marginal distress cost.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these are financial distress costs, as used in the trade-off theory? Select all that apply.
choice*: Legal and administrative fees in a bankruptcy
choice*: Customers leaving because they doubt the firm will honor warranties
choice*: Suppliers demanding cash on delivery
