@article
When a company earns more cash than it can invest at attractive returns, it must decide what to do with it: hold it, repay debt, or return it to shareholders. **Payout policy** covers how much to distribute and in which form: **cash dividends** or **share repurchases** (buybacks).

### Mechanics

A dividend has four dates: the **declaration date** (the board announces the amount), the **ex-dividend date** (buyers on or after this date do not receive the dividend), the **record date** (the company lists the holders of record) and the **payment date**. Since US stock trades began settling one business day after the trade (T+1, May 2024), the ex-dividend date is the same as the record date. On the ex-dividend date the stock price falls by about the dividend, since cash has left the firm.

In a repurchase, the firm buys its own shares in the open market, by a tender offer at a stated price, or through a negotiated purchase. Repurchased shares are retired or held as treasury stock, so the **number of shares outstanding falls**. Since 2023, US corporations pay a 1% federal excise tax on the net value of shares they repurchase; rules and rates change, so check the current law.

### Irrelevance in perfect markets

Modigliani and Miller (1961) showed that, with no taxes or transaction costs and a fixed investment policy, payout policy does not affect value. The firm's value comes from its investments. Paying a dividend of \$2 per share lowers the share price by \$2, leaving the shareholder with the same wealth; an investor who prefers cash can sell shares (**homemade dividends**) and one who prefers shares can reinvest the dividend. Repurchases are the same trade seen differently: the firm distributes cash and the share count drops, so the price per share does not change when shares are bought at fair value.

### Why payout policy may matter

- **Taxes.** If dividends are taxed more heavily than capital gains, investors prefer buybacks. US tax law has taxed qualified dividends and long-term capital gains at the same rates, so the gap depends on the investor (see tax facts for the current year); a marginal investor who pays tax at rate $\tau_d$ on dividends and $\tau_g$ on gains is indifferent if the price falls on the ex-dividend date by $D(1-\tau_d)/(1-\tau_g)$.
- **Signaling.** Managers know more than investors. A dividend increase or a buyback can signal confidence in future cash flow. Firms smooth dividends because cutting a dividend sends a bad signal (Lintner).
- **Clienteles.** Investors with different tax positions and income needs choose firms with matching policies.
- **Agency costs.** Cash retained by the firm can be wasted on poor projects. Paying it out forces management back to the capital market (week 11).
- **Flexibility.** Regular dividends are expected to continue; buybacks are discretionary and used for temporary cash.

### Buybacks, EPS and value

A buyback financed with cash usually raises EPS because fewer shares share the earnings, but the firm also loses the income from the cash spent (after tax). EPS rises when the **earnings yield** $E/P$ exceeds the after-tax return forgone on the cash. A higher EPS does not by itself create value: if the shares are bought at their fair price, wealth is unchanged. A buyback at a price above fair value transfers wealth from the remaining shareholders to those who sell; at a price below fair value it does the reverse.

### Payout measures

The **dividend payout ratio** is dividends divided by net income; the **total payout ratio** adds repurchases. A **residual dividend policy** pays out only what remains after funding all positive-NPV investments at the target capital structure.

@example
title: A dividend and a buyback are equivalent
problem: Calder Marine has 100 million shares at \$50, so its equity is worth \$5,000 million. It will distribute \$200 million, either as a dividend or as a buyback at \$50. No taxes. Find the share price after each.
step: Dividend: the firm pays \$2 a share, \$200 million in all. Equity falls to $5{,}000 - 200 = \$4{,}800$ million over 100 million shares, an ex-dividend price of \$48. A holder has a share worth \$48 plus \$2 in cash: \$50.
step: Buyback: at \$50 the firm retires $200/50 = 4$ million shares, leaving 96 million.
step: Equity is again \$4,800 million, so the price is $4{,}800/96 = \$50$.
step: Shareholders who sell receive \$50 a share; those who stay hold shares worth \$50. Either way each holder's wealth is \$50 per original share.
answer: Ex-dividend price \$48 (plus the \$2 dividend); buyback price \$50 with 96 million shares left. Wealth is the same.

@example
title: EPS accretion from a buyback
problem: Marlowe Textiles earns net income of \$500 million with 100 million shares at \$50 (P/E of 10). It spends \$1,000 million of cash, which earned 4% pretax (tax rate 25%), to repurchase shares at \$50. Compute EPS before and after.
step: Before: $\text{EPS} = 500/100 = \$5.00$.
step: Shares repurchased: $1{,}000/50 = 20$ million, leaving 80 million.
step: Lost after-tax income: $1{,}000 \times 0.04 \times (1 - 0.25) = \$30$ million, so net income falls to \$470 million.
step: After: $\text{EPS} = 470/80 = \$5.875$, up 17.5%. The earnings yield $E/P = 10\%$ exceeds the 3% after-tax return forgone, so EPS rises. The share price should not rise from this alone, because the firm is also smaller.
answer: EPS rises from \$5.00 to \$5.875.

@mistakes
- Believing that a repurchase automatically creates value because EPS goes up; the lost interest income and the fair price paid offset the gain.
- Forgetting that the share price falls by about the dividend on the ex-dividend date, and calling it a loss.
- Ignoring the foregone after-tax interest on the cash used when computing post-buyback EPS.
- Treating a stock split or a stock dividend as a payout of cash: no cash leaves the firm and value is unchanged.
