@article
A DCF values a company from its own cash flows. **Relative valuation** asks instead what the market pays for similar companies, and applies that price per unit of earnings, sales or book value to the company you are valuing. It is quick and reflects market sentiment, which is also its weakness: if peers are overpriced, so is your answer.

### The common multiples

| Multiple | Numerator | Denominator | Belongs to |
|---|---|---|---|
| P/E | Price per share (or equity value) | EPS (or net income) | Equity |
| P/B | Equity value | Book value of equity | Equity |
| EV/EBITDA | Enterprise value | EBITDA | All capital |
| EV/EBIT | Enterprise value | EBIT | All capital |
| EV/Sales | Enterprise value | Revenue | All capital |

**Consistency rule:** the numerator and the denominator must belong to the same group of investors. EBITDA is earned before interest, so it supports all capital providers and must be paired with enterprise value; net income is what remains for shareholders, so it pairs with equity value. EV divided by net income mixes the two and is meaningless. After you apply an enterprise multiple, move from EV to equity value by subtracting net debt and other claims (week 2).

### The comparable-company method

1. Choose **comparable firms** with similar business mix, growth, margins and risk. No two firms are identical, so judgment is needed.
2. Compute each peer's multiple on the same basis: all trailing (last twelve months) or all forward (next year's forecast), the same fiscal calendar, and the same accounting for leases, one-off items and stock compensation.
3. Summarize with the **median** (robust to outliers) or the mean. For ratios with price in the numerator (such as P/E), the **harmonic mean** (the reciprocal of the average of the reciprocals, i.e. of earnings yields) is often better because it avoids a few very high multiples dominating.
4. Apply to the target's metric, and adjust for differences in growth and risk.

Present the results from several multiples as a range, as the project does.

### What justifies a multiple

A multiple is a compressed DCF. For a stock with constant growth $g$ in dividends, cost of equity $r$, payout ratio $b$ (the share of earnings paid out) and return on equity ROE, retaining $1 - b$ of earnings to grow earnings at $g = (1 - b)\,\text{ROE}$ gives the **justified forward P/E**:

$$\frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{b}{r - g} = \frac{1 - g/\text{ROE}}{r - g}.$$

A higher multiple is justified by higher growth *with* returns above the cost of equity, lower risk (lower $r$) and higher ROE. When ROE equals $r$ the formula gives $1/r$ regardless of growth: growth that earns only the cost of equity creates no value. The trailing multiple is the forward multiple times $(1+g)$. The same logic gives similar drivers for EV/EBITDA: growth, reinvestment needs, tax rate and WACC.

### Limits

Peer multiples vary with the market's mood, with accounting differences (leases, depreciation) and with leverage. EV multiples are less distorted by leverage than P/E. Negative or near-zero earnings make P/E useless; EV/Sales then serves, but it ignores margins.

@example
title: Valuing a target with three peer multiples
problem: Harborview Tools has revenue of \$900 million, EBITDA of \$180 million, net income of \$80 million, net debt of \$420 million and 60 million shares. Five peers trade at these multiples (all trailing). Value Harborview per share with the median of each multiple.

| Peer | EV/EBITDA | P/E | EV/Sales |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aldermere | 8.0 | 14.0 | 1.4 |
| Brightwater | 9.5 | 16.5 | 1.8 |
| Coldharbor | 7.2 | 12.8 | 1.2 |
| Dunmore | 10.1 | 18.2 | 2.0 |
| Eastfield | 8.8 | 15.0 | 1.6 |

step: Medians (the middle value of five): EV/EBITDA 8.8, P/E 15.0, EV/Sales 1.6.
step: EV/EBITDA: $EV = 8.8 \times 180 = 1{,}584$; equity $= 1{,}584 - 420 = 1{,}164$; per share $1{,}164/60 = \$19.40$.
step: P/E: $\text{EPS} = 80/60 = 1.3333$, so price $= 15.0 \times 1.3333 = \$20.00$.
step: EV/Sales: $EV = 1.6 \times 900 = 1{,}440$; equity $= 1{,}440 - 420 = 1{,}020$; per share $= \$17.00$. The three methods give a range of \$17.00 to \$20.00.
answer: \$19.40 (EV/EBITDA), \$20.00 (P/E) and \$17.00 (EV/Sales) per share.

@example
title: A justified P/E
problem: A stable company earns a return on equity of 15% and grows earnings at 4% a year by retaining part of its earnings. Its cost of equity is 10%. Find the justified forward and trailing P/E, and compare with a firm whose ROE equals its cost of equity.
step: The payout ratio is $b = 1 - g/\text{ROE} = 1 - 0.04/0.15 = 0.7333$.
step: Forward P/E $= b/(r - g) = 0.7333/0.06 = 12.22$. Trailing P/E $= 12.22 \times 1.04 = 12.71$.
step: If ROE equals the cost of equity of 10% with the same 4% growth, $b = 1 - 0.04/0.10 = 0.60$ and the forward P/E is $0.60/0.06 = 10.0 = 1/r$.
step: The 2.2 turns of extra P/E come entirely from earning 15% instead of 10% on retained earnings.
answer: Forward 12.22, trailing 12.71; with ROE equal to $r$ the forward P/E is 10.0.

@mistakes
- Pairing enterprise value with net income (or equity value with EBITDA): the numerator and the denominator must belong to the same investors.
- Forgetting to subtract net debt (and other claims) after applying an enterprise-value multiple to get a price per share.
- Averaging trailing multiples for some peers and forward multiples for others, or mixing fiscal calendars and lease treatments.
- Treating the peer median as the answer without asking whether the target has similar growth, margins and risk.

model:
```python
def implied_price(metric, multiple, shares, net_debt=0.0, enterprise=True):
    """Value per share implied by an equity or enterprise-value multiple."""
    value = multiple * metric
    if enterprise:
        value -= net_debt
    return value / shares
```
hint: Compute `multiple * metric` first; only an enterprise multiple needs the net debt step.
solution: Multiply the metric by the multiple. For an enterprise multiple, subtract net debt (adding cash when net debt is negative) to reach equity value; for an equity multiple, skip that step. Divide by the number of shares.

@quiz num
prompt: Four comparable companies trade at P/E multiples of 10, 12, 15 and 30. Compute the harmonic mean of these multiples to two decimal places.
answer: 14.12
unit: times
tol: 0.01
check: 4 / (1 / 10 + 1 / 12 + 1 / 15 + 1 / 30)
hint: The harmonic mean is the number of observations divided by the sum of the reciprocals.
hint: The reciprocals are 0.1, 0.0833, 0.0667 and 0.0333.
solution: The sum of reciprocals is $0.1 + 0.08333 + 0.06667 + 0.03333 = 0.28333$, so the harmonic mean is $4/0.28333 = 14.12$. The arithmetic mean, 16.75, is pulled up by the P/E of 30.

solution: A lower $r$ shrinks the denominator $r - g$, which raises the multiple. Higher growth raises it when ROE exceeds $r$, because the extra growth earns more than it costs. A higher cost of equity, lower growth with ROE above $r$, or a lower ROE at the same growth all reduce it.
