@article
A **merger** combines two firms into one; an **acquisition** is the purchase of one firm (the **target**) by another (the **acquirer** or bidder). Deals are **horizontal** (competitors), **vertical** (supplier and customer) or **conglomerate** (unrelated). They can be friendly (agreed by the target's board) or hostile (taken directly to shareholders in a tender offer or a proxy contest).

### Motives

A deal creates value only if the combined firm is worth more than the two separate firms:

$$\text{Synergy } S = V_{A+T} - (V_A + V_T).$$

- **Operating synergies:** cost savings from economies of scale, eliminating duplicate functions or purchasing power; revenue gains from cross-selling or complementary products; access to technology.
- **Financial synergies:** the target's unused tax losses, greater debt capacity, a lower cost of capital.
- **Market power:** higher prices through less competition (which antitrust authorities examine).

Other motives do not create value by themselves. **Diversification** across unrelated businesses: shareholders can diversify more cheaply on their own, and conglomerates often trade at a discount. **Empire building** and managerial **hubris** serve managers rather than owners (week 11). Buying a firm because it looks cheap helps only if the buyer really knows better than the market.

### Valuing the target and the synergies

Value the target **standalone**, $V_T$, using the DCF and multiples methods of weeks 1–4, ideally at its market price as a first check, then value the synergies separately, discounting the after-tax synergy cash flows at a rate that fits their risk. For example, annual pretax cost savings $C$ that last indefinitely, after tax at $t$:

$$S = \frac{C(1 - t)}{r}, \qquad \text{or } S = \frac{C(1-t)}{r - g} \text{ if the first-year saving } C(1-t) \text{ grows at } g.$$

Deduct one-time **integration costs** and **transaction costs**. The value of the target to the acquirer is $V_T + S$. This is the **walk-away price**.

### Who gains: the premium

The **premium** is the amount paid above the target's unaffected market value:

$$\text{premium} = P_{\text{offer}} - P_{\text{unaffected}}, \qquad \text{premium \%} = \frac{P_{\text{offer}}}{P_{\text{unaffected}}} - 1.$$

In a **cash** deal at total price $P$:

$$\text{NPV to acquirer} = (V_T + S) - P - \text{costs} = S - \text{premium} - \text{costs}, \qquad \text{gain to target} = \text{premium}.$$

Together the two gains equal $S$ less costs. The acquirer should not pay more than $V_T + S$, and bidding up to that price gives all the synergy to the target's shareholders. Competition among bidders and the target's bargaining power are why empirical studies generally find that target shareholders gain large premiums while acquirers' gains are small, on average close to zero.

### Cash versus stock

In a **stock** deal, the target's shareholders receive acquirer shares at an **exchange ratio**: shares of the acquirer per target share. Offer value per target share is the ratio times the acquirer's price. The target holders now own a fraction

$$\alpha = \frac{\text{new shares issued}}{\text{acquirer's old shares} + \text{new shares}}$$

of the combined firm, so they share in the synergy and in any overvaluation or error in it. A cash offer puts the whole risk that synergies do not appear on the acquirer. A stock offer shares it. Acquirers tend to pay in stock when they believe their stock is highly valued.

### Accretion and dilution

A deal is **accretive** if pro forma EPS exceeds the acquirer's standalone EPS and **dilutive** if it is lower. Pro forma EPS is combined net income (plus after-tax synergies and less after-tax financing costs) divided by combined shares. EPS accretion is not value creation: it follows from the relative P/E ratios, and only synergies and a fair price create value.

### Accounting, law and process

Under US GAAP (ASC 805) and IFRS (IFRS 3) an acquisition is recorded with the **acquisition method**: the target's identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value, with **goodwill** for any excess of the price over their fair value. Large deals require antitrust review: in the United States, premerger notification under the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act and review by the Department of Justice or the Federal Trade Commission (thresholds and rules change; check current law), and securities-law disclosure to the SEC.

@example
title: Pricing a cash bid
problem: Ridgeline Brands (the acquirer) considers buying Tarn Valley Foods. Tarn Valley has 50 million shares at an unaffected price of \$20, so its standalone value is \$1,000 million. The merger would save \$40 million of pretax costs a year, permanently; the tax rate is 25% and the savings are discounted at 10%. Transaction costs are \$20 million. Ridgeline offers \$24 per share in cash. Find the synergy, the premium, each side's gain and the maximum price per share.
step: Synergy: $S = 40(1 - 0.25)/0.10 = 30/0.10 = \$300$ million.
step: The offer is $24 \times 50 = \$1{,}200$ million. The premium is $(24 - 20) \times 50 = \$200$ million, or 20%.
step: NPV to Ridgeline: $300 - 200 - 20 = \$80$ million. Gain to Tarn Valley's holders: \$200 million.
step: Walk-away price: $(V_T + S)/50 = 1{,}300/50 = \$26$ per share; net of costs, $(1{,}300 - 20)/50 = \$25.60$. Paying more than that would give Ridgeline a negative NPV.
answer: $S = \$300$ million; premium \$200 million (20%); NPV to the acquirer \$80 million; maximum price about \$25.60 per share.

@example
title: The same deal paid in stock
problem: Suppose Ridgeline has 200 million shares at \$25 (market value \$5,000 million). It pays Tarn Valley's holders with 0.96 Ridgeline shares per Tarn Valley share instead of cash, so the offer is worth $0.96 \times 25 = \$24$ per share at the pre-deal price. The synergy is \$300 million, and ignore transaction costs. Find the combined value, share price and the gain to each group.
step: New shares: $0.96 \times 50 = 48$ million. Total shares: $200 + 48 = 248$ million. Target holders own $48/248 = 19.35\%$.
step: Combined value: $5{,}000 + 1{,}000 + 300 = \$6{,}300$ million. New price: $6{,}300/248 = \$25.40$.
step: Target holders receive $48 \times 25.40 = \$1{,}219.4$ million for a business worth \$1,000 million, a gain of \$219.4 million (more than the \$200 million premium, because they share in the synergy).
step: Ridgeline holders own 200 million shares worth $200 \times 25.40 = \$5{,}080.6$ million, a gain of \$80.6 million. The two gains sum to the \$300 million synergy.
answer: Price \$25.40; the target gains \$219.4 million and the acquirer \$80.6 million.

@mistakes
- Paying a premium larger than the value of the synergies, which hands the acquirer a negative NPV.
- Counting a synergy that shareholders could achieve alone (diversification) or that is not credible.
- Treating the stock offer's headline value as fixed: the value received depends on the combined firm's share price after the deal.
- Reading EPS accretion as proof of value creation.

choice*: Eliminating duplicate head-office functions of the two firms, saving costs the firms could not save separately.
choice: Diversifying into an unrelated industry to reduce the firm's earnings volatility.

@practice num
prompt: Linden Co. will pay \$900 million in cash for a target with a standalone value of \$800 million. The present value of the synergies is \$150 million and the acquirer will pay \$10 million of transaction costs. What is the NPV of the deal to the acquirer's shareholders, in millions of dollars?
answer: 40
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 800 + 150 - 900 - 10
hint: NPV equals the value received minus the price paid minus costs.
solution: The acquirer receives value $800 + 150 = 950$ and pays \$900 million plus \$10 million in costs, so $\text{NPV} = 950 - 900 - 10 = \$40$ million. If the target's pre-bid market value equals its \$800 million standalone value, its holders gain the \$100 million premium.

prompt: An acquirer with 160 million shares priced at \$60 offers a target's shareholders \$30 per target share in acquirer stock. The target has 40 million shares. After the exchange, what percentage of the combined company do the former target shareholders own? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 11.11
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (40 * 30 / 60) / (160 + 40 * 30 / 60) * 100
hint: The exchange ratio is the offer divided by the acquirer's price.
hint: The ratio is 0.5, so the acquirer issues 20 million shares.
solution: The ratio is $30/60 = 0.5$ acquirer shares per target share, so 20 million new shares are issued. The target holders own $20/(160 + 20) = 11.11\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: An acquirer has net income of \$600 million and 200 million shares (EPS \$3.00). It buys a target with net income of \$90 million by issuing 48 million new shares. The deal produces pretax cost savings of \$20 million a year (tax rate 25%). Ignoring financing costs and one-time costs, what is the pro forma EPS to the nearest cent?
answer: 2.84
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (600 + 90 + 20 * (1 - 0.25)) / (200 + 48)
hint: Add the target's income and the after-tax synergies to the acquirer's income.
hint: Combined income is $600 + 90 + 15 = 705$ over 248 million shares.
solution: After-tax savings are $20 \times 0.75 = 15$, so combined net income is \$705 million. With $200 + 48 = 248$ million shares, pro forma EPS is $705/248 = \$2.84$, a dilution of 5.2% from \$3.00. Without the synergies EPS would be \$2.78.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about paying for an acquisition in cash versus stock are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: In a cash deal the acquirer's shareholders bear all of the risk that the synergies do not appear.
choice*: In a stock deal the target's shareholders share in both the synergies and the risk that they do not appear.
choice*: The value a target's shareholders receive in a stock deal depends on the acquirer's share price after the deal.
choice: A stock deal removes the need to value the synergies, because the two firms simply combine.
choice: A cash deal always gives the acquirer a higher NPV than a stock deal.
hint: Think about who owns the combined firm after each type of deal.
solution: In a cash deal the target holders are paid in full and leave, so the acquirer bears the synergy risk; in a stock deal they own part of the combined firm, so they share it, and what they receive depends on its post-deal price. Synergies must still be valued to decide the price, and neither payment method dominates for the acquirer.
