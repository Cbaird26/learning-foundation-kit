@article
A **leveraged buyout (LBO)** is the acquisition of a company mostly financed with debt, usually by a private equity sponsor who contributes a modest amount of equity. The target's own assets and cash flows secure the debt, which the company then repays from its free cash flow. If the sponsor can sell the company after a few years for more than it paid, the equity gets a high return because it is a small slice of the capital structure.

### Why LBOs can work

LBO targets are typically stable businesses with predictable cash flow, low capital needs, and room to improve margins. Leverage helps in three ways: it magnifies the return on equity (week 5), it creates an **interest tax shield** (week 6), and the discipline of debt service pushes management to cut waste (the agency argument of week 11). It also raises the **risk of financial distress**, and the more debt, the less room for error.

### Sources and uses

Every LBO begins with a table in which total **uses** (what must be paid for) equals total **sources** (where the money comes from):

| Uses | Sources |
|---|---|
| Purchase of equity and refinancing of existing debt (purchase enterprise value) | Senior debt (bank loans, usually floating-rate, often benchmarked to SOFR) |
| Transaction and financing fees | Subordinated or high-yield debt |
| Minimum cash to the balance sheet | Sponsor equity |

Sponsor equity is the plug: $\text{equity} = \text{uses} - \text{debt}$. Leverage is quoted as a multiple of EBITDA (for example, total debt of 5.0 times EBITDA), together with **interest coverage**, EBITDA divided by cash interest.

### Exit and returns

After the holding period, the sponsor sells the company (or lists it). Using an exit multiple of EBITDA:

$$\text{exit EV} = \text{exit multiple} \times \text{EBITDA}_{\text{exit}}, \qquad \text{exit equity} = \text{exit EV} - \text{net debt at exit}.$$

Net debt at exit is the entry debt minus the debt repaid from the company's cash flow after interest and tax over the holding period. With no interim payments to the sponsor:

$$\text{MOIC} = \frac{\text{exit equity}}{\text{entry equity}}, \qquad \text{IRR} = \text{MOIC}^{1/n} - 1,$$

where $n$ is the holding period in years. If the sponsor also receives interim dividends, the IRR solves $\sum_t CF_t/(1+\text{IRR})^t = 0$ for the whole stream of cash flows.

### Sources of value creation

Equity gain, exit equity minus entry equity, splits into:

- **EBITDA growth:** $(\text{EBITDA}_{\text{exit}} - \text{EBITDA}_{\text{entry}}) \times \text{entry multiple}$.
- **Multiple expansion** (or contraction): $(\text{exit multiple} - \text{entry multiple}) \times \text{EBITDA}_{\text{exit}}$.
- **Debt paydown:** the reduction of net debt.
- **Fees** (a cost) enter with a negative sign: the sponsor pays them at entry.

Skilled sponsors do not rely on multiple expansion, since a multiple at exit equal to the entry multiple is a conservative assumption.

### Risks and limits

Higher leverage raises the IRR when things go well and reduces or eliminates the equity when they do not: a 15% fall in EBITDA, with the same multiple, can wipe out much of the equity of a firm that began at 6 times EBITDA debt. Lenders restrict the firm with **covenants** and require interest coverage minimums. Regulators and rating agencies watch leveraged lending; the rules and tax limits on interest deductions (week 6) change over time.

@example
title: A five-year buyout
problem: A sponsor buys Redstone Components, with EBITDA of \$100 million, at 9.0 times EBITDA. Transaction fees are \$20 million. Debt is 5.0 times EBITDA: \$350 million of senior debt at 7% and \$150 million of subordinated debt at 10%. After five years EBITDA is \$130 million, the exit multiple is 9.0 and \$150 million of debt has been repaid from free cash flow. Compute sources and uses, the first-year interest coverage, MOIC and IRR.
step: Uses: purchase $9.0 \times 100 = \$900$ million plus fees \$20 million = \$920 million. Sources: debt \$500 million, so sponsor equity is $920 - 500 = \$420$ million.
step: First-year cash interest: $0.07 \times 350 + 0.10 \times 150 = 24.5 + 15 = \$39.5$ million. Coverage: $100/39.5 = 2.53$ times.
step: Exit: $EV = 9.0 \times 130 = \$1{,}170$ million; net debt $= 500 - 150 = 350$; exit equity $= 1{,}170 - 350 = \$820$ million.
step: $\text{MOIC} = 820/420 = 1.952$ and $\text{IRR} = 1.952^{1/5} - 1 = 14.3\%$.
answer: Equity \$420 million; coverage 2.53 times; MOIC 1.95; IRR 14.3%.

@example
title: Where did the return come from?
problem: For the Redstone buyout above (entry: 9.0 times EBITDA of \$100 million, fees \$20 million, equity \$420 million; exit: EBITDA \$130 million, \$150 million of debt repaid, exit equity \$820 million), split the \$400 million equity gain into its sources, then recompute the return if the exit multiple is only 8.0.
step: EBITDA growth: $(130 - 100) \times 9.0 = \$270$ million. Multiple change: $(9.0 - 9.0) \times 130 = 0$. Debt paydown: \$150 million. Fees: $-\$20$ million.
step: Total: $270 + 0 + 150 - 20 = \$400$ million, which equals $820 - 420$.
step: At an 8.0 exit multiple: $EV = 8.0 \times 130 = 1{,}040$; exit equity $= 1{,}040 - 350 = \$690$ million; $\text{MOIC} = 690/420 = 1.643$; $\text{IRR} = 1.643^{0.2} - 1 = 10.4\%$.
step: Losing one turn of multiple cut the IRR by about four points: leverage magnifies a change in value, and a change of \$130 million in EV is a \$130 million change in equity of only \$420 million.
answer: \$270 million from EBITDA growth, \$150 million from debt paydown, \$20 million of fees; with an 8.0 exit multiple the IRR is 10.4%.

@mistakes
- Using exit enterprise value as the sponsor's proceeds without subtracting the net debt at exit.
- Ignoring fees, so that the sponsor equity is understated.
- Computing the IRR from the gain (equity profit) rather than from the ratio of exit equity to entry equity.
- Assuming multiple expansion, or debt paydown that ignores interest and tax, in the base case.

model:
```python
def lbo_returns(entry_ebitda, entry_multiple, fees, leverage,
                exit_ebitda, exit_multiple, debt_repaid, years):
    """Sponsor MOIC and IRR for a buyout with no interim distributions."""
    debt = leverage * entry_ebitda
    equity = entry_multiple * entry_ebitda + fees - debt
    exit_equity = exit_multiple * exit_ebitda - (debt - debt_repaid)
    if exit_equity <= 0:
        return (0.0, -1.0)
    moic = exit_equity / equity
    return (moic, moic ** (1 / years) - 1)
```
hint: Compute the debt and sponsor equity first, then the exit equity.
hint: Check the zero-or-negative exit equity case before dividing and taking the root.
solution: Debt is leverage times EBITDA; equity is the purchase price plus fees minus debt. Exit equity is the exit enterprise value less the debt still outstanding. If it is not positive the sponsor loses everything; otherwise MOIC is exit over entry equity and the IRR is its $n$th root minus one.
