@article
Accounting profit is not the cash a company can hand to its investors. A growing retailer can report a profit while spending more on new stores and inventory than it earns. **Free cash flow** is the cash an operating business generates after paying taxes and after making the investments needed to sustain its growth. It is the raw material of every discounted cash flow valuation in this course, so it must be measured carefully and consistently.

### Free cash flow to the firm

**Free cash flow to the firm (FCFF)** is the cash flow available to *all* capital providers, lenders and shareholders, after operating taxes and reinvestment:

$$\text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}(1 - t) + \text{D\&A} - \text{CapEx} - \Delta\text{NWC}.$$

Here EBIT is earnings before interest and taxes, $t$ is the tax rate applied to operating income, $\text{EBIT}(1-t)$ is **NOPAT** (net operating profit after tax), D&A is depreciation and amortization (a non-cash charge, so it is added back), CapEx is capital expenditure on long-lived assets, and $\Delta\text{NWC}$ is the increase in operating net working capital. An increase in working capital absorbs cash, so it is subtracted.

Three conventions matter throughout this course:

- **Operating taxes.** NOPAT taxes EBIT as if the firm had no debt. The interest tax shield is not in FCFF; it is captured later in the after-tax cost of debt inside the WACC. Counting it in both places double-counts it.
- **Operating net working capital** is operating current assets (receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses) minus operating current liabilities (payables, accrued expenses). It excludes cash and marketable securities, and it excludes short-term borrowing, because those are financing items.
- **Cash costs stay in EBIT.** Stock-based compensation, lease cost under ASC 842 or IFRS 16 and similar items are treated the same way every year, and the same way in the forecast as in the history.

### Three routes to the same number

Starting from net income $NI$ with interest expense $I$ in the income statement:

$$\text{FCFF} = NI + \text{D\&A} + I(1 - t) - \text{CapEx} - \Delta\text{NWC}.$$

Starting from cash flow from operations $CFO$ (under US GAAP, interest paid is an operating cash flow, so $CFO$ is already after interest):

$$\text{FCFF} = CFO + I(1 - t) - \text{CapEx}.$$

Adding back $I(1-t)$ undoes the after-tax interest that net income and $CFO$ deducted. The three formulas agree when the tax rate on EBIT is the tax rate actually paid.

### Free cash flow to equity

**Free cash flow to equity (FCFE)** is what remains for shareholders after lenders are paid:

$$\text{FCFE} = \text{FCFF} - I(1 - t) + \text{net borrowing},$$

where net borrowing is new debt issued minus debt repaid. FCFE is discounted at the cost of equity, FCFF at the WACC; each must be matched with its own discount rate (week 2).

### Why reinvestment matters

A company that cuts CapEx below depreciation will show higher FCFF today but a shrinking asset base. When you forecast, link reinvestment to growth: faster growth needs more CapEx and working capital. Week 3 develops this link.

@example
title: One company, three routes
problem: Larkspur Outfitters has EBIT of \$100 million, interest expense of \$20 million, a tax rate of 25% on all income, D&A of \$40 million, CapEx of \$55 million, and operating net working capital that rose by \$8 million. It borrowed a net \$10 million. Find FCFF by two routes, then FCFE.
step: From EBIT: $\text{NOPAT} = 100(1 - 0.25) = 75$. Then $\text{FCFF} = 75 + 40 - 55 - 8 = \$52$ million.
step: Build net income. Pretax income is $100 - 20 = 80$, tax is $0.25 \times 80 = 20$, so $NI = 60$. Then $CFO = NI + \text{D\&A} - \Delta\text{NWC} = 60 + 40 - 8 = 92$.
step: From CFO: $\text{FCFF} = CFO + I(1 - t) - \text{CapEx} = 92 + 20(0.75) - 55 = 92 + 15 - 55 = \$52$ million. Both routes agree.
step: Convert to equity: $\text{FCFE} = 52 - 15 + 10 = \$47$ million. Lenders received after-tax interest of 15, but the new borrowing of 10 added to the cash available to shareholders.
answer: FCFF = \$52 million by both routes; FCFE = \$47 million.

@example
title: Working capital from two balance sheets
problem: Bellwether Foods reports the balances below (millions of dollars). Its EBIT is \$60 million, its tax rate is 30%, D&A is \$25 million and CapEx is \$35 million. Find FCFF for the year.

| Item | Start of year | End of year |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts receivable | 90 | 99 |
| Inventory | 70 | 76 |
| Cash | 30 | 41 |
| Accounts payable | 55 | 58 |
| Accrued expenses | 15 | 17 |
| Short-term bank debt | 20 | 12 |

step: Pick the operating items only. Cash and short-term bank debt are financing items and are left out.
step: Operating net working capital: start $= 90 + 70 - 55 - 15 = 90$; end $= 99 + 76 - 58 - 17 = 100$. So $\Delta\text{NWC} = +10$, a use of cash.
step: NOPAT $= 60(1 - 0.30) = 42$.
step: $\text{FCFF} = 42 + 25 - 35 - 10 = \$22$ million. Including the rise in cash or the repayment of bank debt would have mixed financing flows into an operating measure.
answer: \$22 million.

@mistakes
- Subtracting interest expense (or the full interest bill) in FCFF: interest is a payment to a capital provider, so it stays out of FCFF.
- Including cash and short-term debt in net working capital, which treats financing changes as operating investment.
- Forgetting to add back D&A, or adding it back twice when starting from net income that already has it added in CFO.
- Mixing up the sign of $\Delta\text{NWC}$: a rise in working capital reduces cash flow.

@practice num
prompt: A firm has FCFF of \$150 million. It pays interest of \$30 million, its tax rate is 25%, and during the year it repaid a net \$20 million of debt (net borrowing is $-20$). Compute its FCFE in millions of dollars to one decimal place.
answer: 107.5
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 150 - 30 * (1 - 0.25) + (-20)
hint: Lenders receive after-tax interest of $I(1 - t)$.
hint: Net borrowing is negative here, so it reduces cash available to shareholders.
solution: $\text{FCFE} = 150 - 30(0.75) - 20 = 150 - 22.5 - 20 = \$107.5$ million.

@practice num
prompt: Hallam Tools reports the following operating data, in millions of dollars. Use a tax rate of 30% on EBIT, D&A of \$60 million and CapEx of \$75 million. EBIT is \$180 million. Compute FCFF to one decimal place.

| Item | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Accounts receivable | 200 | 230 |
| Inventory | 150 | 165 |
| Cash | 40 | 90 |
| Accounts payable | 120 | 135 |
| Accrued expenses | 30 | 34 |
| Short-term debt | 50 | 70 |

answer: 85
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 180 * 0.7 + 60 - 75 - ((230 + 165 - 135 - 34) - (200 + 150 - 120 - 30))
hint: Operating net working capital is receivables plus inventory minus payables and accrued expenses.
hint: Operating NWC rises from 200 to 226, so $\Delta\text{NWC} = 26$; NOPAT is $180 \times 0.7$.
solution: Operating NWC is $200 + 150 - 120 - 30 = 200$ in year 1 and $230 + 165 - 135 - 34 = 226$ in year 2, so $\Delta\text{NWC} = 26$. NOPAT is $180 \times 0.7 = 126$. $\text{FCFF} = 126 + 60 - 75 - 26 = \$85$ million. Cash and short-term debt are financing items and are ignored.

@quiz num
prompt: Under US GAAP a company reports cash flow from operations of \$310 million (after interest paid), interest expense of \$36 million, CapEx of \$205 million and a tax rate of 24%. Compute its FCFF in millions of dollars to two decimal places.
answer: 132.36
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 310 + 36 * (1 - 0.24) - 205
hint: Interest paid is inside CFO under US GAAP, so CFO is after the interest.
hint: Add back the after-tax interest, then subtract CapEx.
solution: $\text{FCFF} = CFO + I(1-t) - \text{CapEx} = 310 + 36(0.76) - 205 = 310 + 27.36 - 205 = \$132.36$ million.
