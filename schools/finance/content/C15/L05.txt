@article
How should a firm finance its assets: with equity, with debt or with a mix? Before looking at taxes and bankruptcy, it helps to ask when the answer *does not matter*. The Modigliani-Miller analysis (1958) shows that, under ideal conditions, it does not. Their result is the benchmark against which every real-world reason for choosing leverage is measured.

### The perfect capital market assumptions

The **Modigliani-Miller (MM) propositions** assume: no taxes, no transaction or bankruptcy costs, symmetric information (managers know nothing investors do not), investors and firms borrow at the same rate, and the firm's investment policy (its operating cash flows) is fixed regardless of how it is financed. The first proposition then follows from a no-arbitrage argument.

### Proposition I: value is independent of financing

$$V_L = V_U,$$

where $V_L$ is the value of a levered firm (debt plus equity) and $V_U$ the value of an otherwise identical unlevered firm. The value of a firm is determined by its assets' cash flows, like cutting a pizza into more slices without changing its size. **Homemade leverage** is the proof: an investor who wants the risk of a levered share can borrow personally and buy the unlevered firm's shares, replicating the levered payoff. If the levered firm sold for more, arbitrageurs would sell it and buy the replicating portfolio, so prices cannot differ.

Because the firm's value and its cash flows are unchanged, so is its overall cost of capital: with no taxes, the WACC is constant and equals the **unlevered cost of capital** $r_0$ (also written $r_A$, the required return on the firm's assets).

### Proposition II: leverage raises the cost of equity

The WACC equals $r_0$ at every leverage ratio:

$$r_0 = \frac{E}{V}\,r_E + \frac{D}{V}\,r_D.$$

Solving for the cost of equity gives

$$r_E = r_0 + \frac{D}{E}\,(r_0 - r_D).$$

Debt looks cheap ($r_D < r_E$), but substituting debt for equity makes the remaining equity riskier, and the cost of equity rises exactly enough to offset the cheaper debt. The formula holds with $r_D$ the expected return on debt even when debt is risky.

In beta terms (using the CAPM, $r = r_f + \beta(r_m - r_f)$ for each claim):

$$\beta_E = \beta_A + \frac{D}{E}\,(\beta_A - \beta_D),$$

where $\beta_A$ is the asset (unlevered) beta and $\beta_D$ the debt beta. If debt is riskless, $\beta_D = 0$.

### Effects on EPS and ROE

Leverage magnifies the effect of operating results on shareholders. Suppose EBIT varies; interest is a fixed charge. At high EBIT, a levered firm has a higher EPS and ROE than an unlevered firm, and at low EBIT a lower one. There is a **breakeven EBIT** where the two have equal EPS. Higher expected EPS from leverage does not mean higher value: it is compensation for higher risk, so the P/E falls.

@example
title: The cost of equity rises with leverage
problem: Ridgeline Foods has an unlevered cost of capital of 10% and a cost of debt of 5%. There are no taxes. Its debt-to-equity ratio is 0.5. Find the cost of equity and confirm that the WACC equals 10%.
step: Proposition II: $r_E = r_0 + (D/E)(r_0 - r_D) = 0.10 + 0.5(0.10 - 0.05) = 12.5\%$.
step: With $D/E = 0.5$, the weights are $E/V = 1/1.5 = 2/3$ and $D/V = 1/3$.
step: $\text{WACC} = (2/3)(0.125) + (1/3)(0.05) = 0.08333 + 0.01667 = 10\%$.
step: Leverage raised the cost of equity from 10% to 12.5%, and the WACC did not change.
answer: $r_E = 12.5\%$; WACC $= 10\%$.

@example
title: EPS and ROE with and without debt
problem: Ashgrove Industries has assets worth \$1,000 million and 100 million shares at \$10. A levered alternative would issue \$400 million of debt at 5% (interest \$20 million) and repurchase 40 million shares at \$10, leaving 60 million shares. There are no taxes. EBIT is \$80 million, \$120 million or \$160 million with probabilities 25%, 50% and 25%. Compare EPS and ROE, and find the cost of equity of the levered firm.

| EBIT (\$ million) | Unlevered EPS | Levered EPS | Unlevered ROE | Levered ROE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80 | 0.80 | 1.00 | 8.0% | 10.0% |
| 120 | 1.20 | 1.67 | 12.0% | 16.7% |
| 160 | 1.60 | 2.33 | 16.0% | 23.3% |

step: Unlevered EPS is $\text{EBIT}/100$. Levered EPS is $(\text{EBIT} - 20)/60$: for example $(120 - 20)/60 = 1.667$.
step: ROE is net income over equity. Unlevered equity is \$1,000 million, levered equity is $1{,}000 - 400 = \$600$ million, so levered ROE at EBIT 120 is $100/600 = 16.7\%$.
step: Expected EBIT is $0.25(80) + 0.5(120) + 0.25(160) = 120$, so the unlevered cost of capital is $120/1{,}000 = 12\%$.
step: Proposition II: $r_E = 0.12 + (400/600)(0.12 - 0.05) = 16.67\%$, which matches the expected levered ROE. The spread between good and bad outcomes also widened (ROE from 10% to 23.3% versus 8% to 16%): leverage added risk, and the extra expected return is exactly its price.
answer: Expected levered ROE and cost of equity are both 16.67%; unlevered, 12%. The share price stays at \$10 under Proposition I.

@mistakes
- Believing that because debt is cheaper than equity, replacing equity with debt lowers the WACC. Without taxes the cost of equity rises to offset it.
- Keeping the cost of equity fixed when leverage changes, which produces a falling WACC.
- Using book values for $D$ and $E$ in the leverage ratio instead of market values.
- Treating a higher EPS from leverage as a gain in shareholder value.

prompt: A firm's unlevered cost of capital is 9% and its cost of debt is 4%. With no taxes and a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.8, what is its cost of equity under Modigliani-Miller Proposition II? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 13.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (0.09 + 0.8 * (0.09 - 0.04)) * 100
hint: $r_E = r_0 + (D/E)(r_0 - r_D)$.
hint: The premium over $r_0$ is $0.8 \times 5\%$.
solution: $r_E = 9\% + 0.8(9\% - 4\%) = 9\% + 4\% = 13.0\%$.

@practice num
prompt: An unlevered firm has an asset beta of 0.8. It then adopts a capital structure with a debt-to-equity ratio of 0.6 and a debt beta of 0.2. Assume no taxes, a risk-free rate of 4% and a market risk premium of 5% (hypothetical figures). Compute the cost of equity from the CAPM using the levered equity beta. Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 9.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (0.04 + (0.8 + 0.6 * (0.8 - 0.2)) * 0.05) * 100
hint: First find the equity beta, $\beta_E = \beta_A + (D/E)(\beta_A - \beta_D)$.
hint: $\beta_E = 0.8 + 0.6(0.6) = 1.16$; then use $r_f + \beta_E \times \text{MRP}$.
solution: $\beta_E = 0.8 + 0.6(0.8 - 0.2) = 1.16$. The CAPM gives $r_E = 4\% + 1.16 \times 5\% = 9.8\%$.

prompt: An unlevered firm has 100 million shares and no debt. A levered alternative would have debt with annual interest of \$20 million and 60 million shares. There are no taxes. At what EBIT, in millions of dollars, do the two structures give the same EPS?
answer: 50
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 20 * 100 / (100 - 60)
hint: Set $\text{EBIT}/100 = (\text{EBIT} - 20)/60$.
hint: Cross-multiply: $60\,\text{EBIT} = 100\,\text{EBIT} - 2{,}000$.
solution: $\text{EBIT}/100 = (\text{EBIT} - 20)/60$ gives $60\,\text{EBIT} = 100\,\text{EBIT} - 2{,}000$, so $\text{EBIT} = 2{,}000/40 = \$50$ million. At that EBIT the return on assets equals the cost of debt, so leverage neither helps nor hurts EPS.

@quiz num
prompt: A firm has an unlevered cost of capital of 12% and borrows at 7%. It has \$300 million of debt and \$700 million of equity at market value. Ignoring taxes, what is its cost of equity? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 14.14
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (0.12 + (300 / 700) * (0.12 - 0.07)) * 100
hint: The debt-to-equity ratio is $300/700$.
solution: $r_E = 12\% + (300/700)(12\% - 7\%) = 12\% + 2.143\% = 14.14\%$.
