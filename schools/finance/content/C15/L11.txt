@article
In a small firm the owner decides everything and bears the consequences. In a large corporation, shareholders (the **principals**) hire managers (the **agents**) to run the firm, and the two groups have different interests. This is the **agency problem**. **Agency costs** are the sum of the monitoring costs paid by the principal, the bonding costs paid by the agent, and the residual loss from decisions that still differ from what the principal would choose.

### Three conflicts

1. **Shareholders versus managers.** Managers may prefer a quiet life, perks, empire building through value-destroying acquisitions (week 9), or keeping cash in the firm instead of paying it out. The **free cash flow** argument says that managers with surplus cash tend to invest it in negative-NPV projects. Their pay may reward short-term earnings or sales growth that may not match long-term value. Debt and payouts reduce the free cash flow they control (weeks 6 and 7).
2. **Shareholders versus creditors.** Shareholders hold a residual claim that behaves like a call option on the firm's assets (week 12), so for a firm with debt they can gain from risk at creditors' expense:
   - **Asset substitution:** swapping safe projects for riskier ones. Equity gains from the upside while creditors bear more of the downside.
   - **Underinvestment (debt overhang):** a positive-NPV project may be rejected because most of its gain would go to creditors.
   - **Excessive dividends** or additional borrowing that weaken creditors' claims. Covenants in loan contracts exist to limit these.
3. **Controlling versus minority shareholders.** A shareholder with control through a pyramid structure or **dual-class shares** may extract private benefits (related-party transactions) at the expense of others. With dual-class shares the votes and cash flow rights differ: Class A with one vote per share and Class B with ten votes, for instance, can give insiders a majority of the votes with a minority of the cash flows.

### Governance mechanisms

- **The board of directors** hires, pays and removes the CEO. **Independent directors**, separate audit, compensation and nominating committees, and a separation of the roles of chair and CEO are intended to improve monitoring.
- **Executive compensation** aligns pay with shareholder value through stock, options and performance shares with vesting and holding requirements. Poorly designed pay creates its own problems: a bonus based only on revenue growth rewards growth that destroys value. Since the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010, public companies hold non-binding shareholder votes on executive pay (**say-on-pay**), and since 2023 US exchange listing standards (SEC Rule 10D-1) require companies to adopt **clawback** policies to recover incentive pay erroneously awarded when financial statements are restated.
- **Disclosure and audit.** After the failures of the early 2000s, the Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 required chief executives and chief financial officers to certify financial statements, independent audit committees, management's assessment of internal control over financial reporting (Section 404), and created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to oversee auditors.
- **Shareholder rights.** One share-one vote, annual director elections, proxy access and the ability to vote against directors. Under **cumulative voting** a shareholder can concentrate all votes on one candidate, helping minorities win seats. Under straight voting, the majority elects the whole board.
- **The market for corporate control.** Underperforming firms can be taken over, so managers have an incentive to perform. Defenses such as **staggered boards** and **poison pills** (shareholder rights plans) make hostile takeovers harder; they can protect shareholders by increasing bargaining power or protect managers from accountability, and research and opinion differ.
- **Activist investors** buy stakes and press for change, such as asset sales, buybacks or board seats.
- **Creditors and the market.** Covenants, credit ratings and the need to raise capital on public markets discipline managers.

### Beyond shareholders

Whether firms should maximize shareholder value alone or balance the interests of other stakeholders (employees, customers, communities) is debated. Supporters of shareholder primacy argue that clear objectives make managers accountable; supporters of stakeholder approaches argue that long-run value depends on those relationships. Rules on sustainability reporting and board diversity are changing; check current requirements.

### Cumulative voting arithmetic

With $S$ voting shares, $N$ seats up for election and cumulative voting, a holder needs more than $nS/(N+1)$ shares to be sure of electing $n$ directors, so the minimum number of whole shares is $\lfloor nS/(N+1) \rfloor + 1$.

@example
title: Asset substitution
problem: Cliffside Marine has assets that will be worth \$120 million next year if it keeps its safe strategy. It owes creditors \$100 million (face value) due next year. Management could switch to a risky strategy in which the assets will be worth \$200 million or \$40 million with equal probability (the same \$120 million on average). For clarity ignore discounting. Compare the value of equity and debt under each strategy.
step: Safe: assets are worth \$120 million for certain. Creditors receive \$100 million, equity gets \$20 million.
step: Risky, good case (probability 0.5): assets \$200 million; creditors receive \$100 million; equity gets \$100 million.
step: Risky, bad case (probability 0.5): assets \$40 million; creditors receive only \$40 million and equity gets zero (limited liability).
step: Expected values: equity $= 0.5(100) + 0.5(0) = \$50$ million, debt $= 0.5(100) + 0.5(40) = \$70$ million. Equity rose by \$30 million and debt fell by \$30 million, while total value (120) is unchanged. Shareholders gain at creditors' expense, which is why lenders write covenants.
answer: Equity \$20 million (safe) versus \$50 million (risky); debt \$100 million versus \$70 million.

@example
title: Votes versus cash flow rights
problem: A company has 100 million Class A shares (one vote each) and 40 million Class B shares (ten votes each). The founders hold all of the Class B shares and no Class A. Find the founders' share of the cash flows and of the votes.
step: Cash flow rights: $40/(100 + 40) = 28.57\%$ of the dividends and equity value.
step: Votes: Class B has $40 \times 10 = 400$ million votes and Class A has 100 million votes, total 500 million. The founders hold $400/500 = 80\%$ of the votes.
step: The founders control the firm while bearing less than 29% of the economic consequences of their decisions. This gap, a **wedge**, increases the room for private benefits.
answer: 28.57% of the cash flows and 80% of the votes.

@mistakes
- Treating shareholders as a group with identical interests: controlling holders, minority holders and creditors may have different incentives.
- Assuming that more independent directors or stronger incentive pay always raises value; design and enforcement matter.
- Forgetting limited liability when valuing equity: in the bad case equity receives zero, not a negative amount.
- Treating takeover defenses as always bad or always good for shareholders; the evidence is mixed.

prompt: A company has 300 million Class A shares (one vote each) and 50 million Class B shares (ten votes each). Its founders hold all of the Class B shares and 20 million Class A shares. What percent of the total votes do the founders control?
answer: 65
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (50 * 10 + 20) / (300 + 50 * 10) * 100
hint: Count votes, not shares: Class B shares carry ten votes each.
solution: The founders have $50 \times 10 + 20 = 520$ million votes out of $300 + 500 = 800$ million, which is 65%. Their cash flow rights are $(50 + 20)/350 = 20\%$.

@practice num
prompt: A firm owes creditors \$80 million due in one year (ignore discounting). It can choose a risky project under which its assets will be worth \$150 million or \$30 million with equal probability. What is the expected value of the equity (in millions of dollars) under the risky project? Equity holders have limited liability.
answer: 35
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 0.5 * (150 - 80) + 0.5 * max(30 - 80, 0)
hint: Equity receives the assets minus the debt, but never less than zero.
solution: In the good case equity gets $150 - 80 = 70$; in the bad case assets of 30 are below the debt of 80, so equity gets 0. The expected value is $0.5(70) + 0.5(0) = \$35$ million. With a safe project worth \$90 million for certain the equity would get only \$10 million.

@quiz self
prompt: A company's chief executive receives an annual bonus based entirely on the growth of revenue, and the firm has large cash balances. In four or five sentences, explain the agency problem this may create and describe two governance mechanisms that could reduce it.
hint: Think about which projects a revenue-only bonus rewards, and what to do with surplus cash.
solution: A revenue-based bonus pays the CEO for growth whatever it costs, so with plenty of cash the CEO may buy low-return acquisitions or expand into projects with negative NPV; this is the manager-shareholder conflict and the overinvestment of free cash flow. A compensation committee of independent directors could link the bonus to measures of value creation such as ROIC above the cost of capital or economic profit, paid in stock that vests over several years. The firm could also pay out surplus cash through dividends or buybacks, so that new investments have to compete for funds in the capital market, and a clawback policy would recover pay if results are later restated.
rubric: Identifies the conflict as managers versus shareholders: growth for its own sake or overinvestment of free cash flow.
rubric: Proposes a mechanism that changes pay to reward value creation, such as economic profit or ROIC measures, vesting stock or clawbacks.
rubric: Proposes a second mechanism such as paying out surplus cash, independent board oversight, or the discipline of the market for corporate control.
