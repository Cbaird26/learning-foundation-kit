@article
Debt turns a business model into a levered one: interest is a fixed claim ahead of shareholders, and repayment drains cash. The debt schedule answers three questions each year. How much must the company repay? How much more can it repay? What interest does that produce?

### The schedule

For each debt tranche (a term loan, bonds, a revolver), keep a roll-forward:

$$\text{Ending} = \text{Beginning} - \text{Mandatory repayment} - \text{Optional repayment} + \text{Draws}.$$

**Mandatory repayment** (scheduled amortization or a maturity) comes from the debt note: it must be paid. **Optional repayment** is the discretionary prepayment the model assumes from spare cash. A **revolver** (revolving credit facility) is a borrowing line that the model draws when cash would otherwise fall below the minimum and repays first when cash is spare.

### Cash available and the sweep

A **cash sweep** sends all spare cash to repaying debt. Spare cash is what remains after keeping a **minimum cash balance** the business needs to operate:

$$\text{Cash available for debt repayment} = \text{Beginning cash} + \text{Cash flow before debt repayment} - \text{Minimum cash}.$$

After the mandatory repayment, the sweep is the rest, but never more than the balance outstanding and never negative:

$$\text{Optional repayment} = \max\bigl(0,\ \min(\text{Available} - \text{Mandatory},\ \text{Balance after mandatory})\bigr).$$

If available cash is smaller than the mandatory payment, the model shows a shortfall that must be funded by the revolver, new debt or a dividend cut. A sweep assumption is a forecasting convention: real companies choose whether to prepay, and loan agreements may require a share of excess cash flow to be swept.

### Interest and the circularity

Interest expense equals the rate times a balance. Two conventions:

- **Beginning balance**: $\text{Interest} = r \times \text{Beginning}$. Simple, and it creates no circularity, but ignores repayments during the year.
- **Average balance**: $\text{Interest} = r \times (\text{Beginning} + \text{Ending})/2$. More accurate, but circular: interest lowers net income, which lowers cash flow, which lowers repayment, which changes the ending balance, which changes interest.

Spreadsheet models often enable iterative calculation, or add a switch to break the loop when it blows up. In Python, solve it by iteration: guess the interest, compute the repayment, recompute interest, and repeat until the change is negligible. Because each pass multiplies the error by about $r/2$, convergence is fast. When all spare cash is swept, the fixed point is also solvable by algebra: with cash flow before interest $CF$, repayment $X = CF - I$ and $I = r(B - X/2)$, so $X = (CF - rB)/(1 - r/2)$.

### Building it right

Use the sign convention that repayments reduce debt and cash. Put the mandatory repayment first and the sweep second. Test the edges: zero cash, a balance already smaller than the scheduled payment, and cash flow so large it repays everything. Taxes are left out of the schedules in this lesson's small examples for clarity; interest is tax deductible, which the full model in week 8 handles.

@example
title: A year of the debt schedule
problem: Granite Pass Industries starts the year with a \$500 million term loan at 6%. Cash at the start of the year is \$40 million, the minimum cash balance is \$30 million, cash flow before debt repayment (already after interest) is \$90 million, and the mandatory repayment is \$25 million. Find the sweep and ending debt, and the interest on the beginning and average balances.
step: Cash available for debt repayment: $40 + 90 - 30 = 100$.
step: After the mandatory \$25 million, \$75 million is left, well below the \$475 million outstanding, so the optional repayment is \$75 million.
step: Ending debt: $500 - 25 - 75 = 400$.
step: Interest on the beginning balance: $0.06 \times 500 = 30.0$. On the average balance: $0.06 \times (500 + 400)/2 = 0.06 \times 450 = 27.0$.
answer: Sweep of \$75 million; ending debt of \$400 million; interest of \$30.0 million (beginning) or \$27.0 million (average).

@example
title: Iterating on the circularity
problem: A company has a \$400 million loan at 5% and \$120 million of cash flow before interest. All of it, after interest, repays debt, and cash starts and ends at the minimum. Interest is charged on the average balance. Find the interest and the repayment.
step: Start with a guess, interest on the beginning balance: $I_0 = 0.05 \times 400 = 20$. Then repayment is $120 - 20 = 100$.
step: Recompute on the average balance: $I_1 = 0.05 \times (400 + 300)/2 = 17.5$, so repayment becomes $102.5$.
step: Again: $I_2 = 0.05 \times (400 + 297.5)/2 = 17.4375$, repayment $102.5625$; the next pass gives $17.4359$. The changes shrink by about a factor of 40 each time.
step: The algebra confirms it: $X = (120 - 0.05 \times 400)/(1 - 0.025) = 100/0.975 = 102.564$ and $I = 120 - 102.564 = 17.436$.
answer: Interest of about \$17.44 million; repayment of about \$102.56 million.

@mistakes
- Letting the sweep repay more than the outstanding balance, so debt goes negative.
- Forgetting the minimum cash balance, so the model spends cash the business needs.
- Treating a mandatory repayment as optional (or the reverse), so a shortfall hides inside a positive sweep.
- Computing interest on the ending balance, which understates interest in a year of repayment.

model:
```python
def optional_repayment(cash_begin, cash_flow, min_cash, mandatory, balance):
    """Cash sweep after the mandatory repayment."""
    available = cash_begin + cash_flow - min_cash
    sweep = min(available - mandatory, balance - mandatory)
    return max(0, sweep)
```
hint: Available cash is beginning cash plus cash flow minus the minimum cash balance.
hint: Take the smaller of the spare cash and the balance left after the mandatory payment, then floor it at zero.
solution: Spare cash after the mandatory payment is `available - mandatory`; the sweep cannot exceed the balance that remains, `balance - mandatory`, so take the minimum of the two and floor the result at 0 with `max`. In the last test the available cash is 50 + 50 - 20 = 80, so the spare cash after the mandatory payment is 80 - 10 = 70, but only 60 - 10 = 50 of debt remains, so the sweep is 50.

model:
```python
def debt_schedule(beginning, rate, mandatory, cash_flows):
    """Ending debt balance for each year with a cash sweep."""
    balance = beginning
    ending = []
    for cash_flow in cash_flows:
        interest = rate * balance
        required = min(mandatory, balance)
        spare = cash_flow - interest - required
        optional = max(0, min(spare, balance - required))
        balance = balance - required - optional
        ending.append(balance)
    return ending
```
hint: Compute interest on the beginning balance first, then the mandatory payment capped at the balance.
hint: The sweep is the spare cash after interest and the mandatory payment, floored at zero and capped at what remains.
solution: Each year: interest on the beginning balance, mandatory payment capped at the balance, spare cash after both, and an optional repayment bounded between 0 and the remaining balance. The test with a cash flow of 60 shows the shortfall case: the cash flow does not cover interest and the mandatory payment, the sweep is zero, and the balance still falls by the mandatory 100.

model:
```python
def average_balance_interest(beginning, rate, cash_flow):
    """Interest on the average balance, solved by iteration."""
    interest = rate * beginning
    for _ in range(200):
        repayment = cash_flow - interest
        new_interest = rate * (beginning - repayment / 2)
        if abs(new_interest - interest) < 1e-12:
            interest = new_interest
            break
        interest = new_interest
    return interest, cash_flow - interest
```
hint: Start with interest on the beginning balance as the first guess.
hint: Each pass: repayment = cash flow minus interest, then new interest is the rate times the average of beginning and ending balances.
solution: Guess interest, compute the repayment as the cash flow less that interest, recompute interest on the average of `beginning` and `beginning - repayment`, and repeat until the change is tiny. The result agrees with the algebraic solution $X = (CF - rB)/(1 - r/2)$ used in the last test.
