@article
The model produces forecasts. Valuation turns them into a price. Two families of methods dominate: **discounted cash flow** (DCF), which values the company as the present value of the cash it will generate, and **multiples**, which value it relative to how the market prices similar companies. A good analyst runs both and asks why they disagree.

### Free cash flow to the firm

**Free cash flow to the firm** (FCFF) is the cash the operations generate for all capital providers, before any payment to lenders:

$$\text{FCFF} = \text{EBIT}(1 - t) + \text{D\&A} - \text{Capex} - \Delta\text{NWC}.$$

$\text{EBIT}(1-t)$ is **NOPAT**, the after-tax operating profit as if the company had no debt. D&A is added back as non-cash; capex and the increase in net working capital are cash uses. Interest is not subtracted: the discount rate already compensates lenders.

### Discounting

The discount rate is the **weighted average cost of capital**:

$$\text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V}\,k_e + \frac{D}{V}\,k_d\,(1 - t),$$

with $E$ and $D$ the market values of equity and debt, $V = E + D$, $k_e$ the cost of equity (often from the CAPM) and $k_d$ the pre-tax cost of debt. Assume cash flows arrive at the end of each year (a mid-year convention would discount each by half a year less and raise the value):

$$\text{PV of explicit flows} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{\text{FCFF}_t}{(1+\text{WACC})^t}.$$

### Terminal value

Beyond the forecast horizon, use a **terminal value** (TV) at the end of year $N$. The **Gordon growth** method assumes cash flow grows at a constant rate $g$ forever:

$$\text{TV}_N = \frac{\text{FCFF}_N (1+g)}{\text{WACC} - g}, \qquad g < \text{WACC}.$$

The **exit multiple** method sets $\text{TV}_N = \text{EBITDA}_N \times$ a multiple drawn from comparable companies. Discount either back $N$ years. Terminal value is often 60-80% of enterprise value, so always report its share, and keep $g$ at or below the long-run growth of the economy. A powerful check: convert one method into the other (the **implied exit multiple** from Gordon growth, or the implied growth rate from the multiple) and ask whether it is believable.

### From enterprise value to a share price

**Enterprise value** (EV) is the value of the operations. Equity value is what belongs to shareholders:

$$\text{Equity value} = \text{EV} - \text{Debt} + \text{Cash} \;(- \text{preferred stock} - \text{noncontrolling interests}),$$

and the price per share divides by **diluted shares** outstanding (including in-the-money options and convertible securities). Be consistent: if leases are debt in EV, include them in debt.

### Multiples

A multiple scales a company's metric by a peer-based ratio. **EV/EBITDA** gives EV, so subtract net debt to reach equity; **P/E** (price to earnings) gives the price directly: $\text{Price} = \text{P/E} \times \text{EPS}$. Choose peers with similar growth, margins, risk and capital intensity; the median is steadier than the mean; and state whether the multiple is on last-twelve-months or forecast earnings. A multiple is a statement about what the market pays today, not about what is right, so a DCF and a multiple that agree reinforce each other, and one that disagrees points to an assumption worth examining.

All numbers in this lesson belong to fictional companies.

@example
title: Free cash flow
problem: Westmark Cement has EBIT of \$180 million, a 25% tax rate, D&A of \$40 million, capex of \$60 million and an increase in net working capital of \$4 million. Find FCFF.
step: NOPAT $= 180 \times (1 - 0.25) = 135$.
step: Add D&A: $135 + 40 = 175$.
step: Subtract capex and the working capital increase: $175 - 60 - 4 = 111$.
answer: FCFF of \$111 million.

@example
title: A three-year DCF
problem: Forecast FCFF is \$100 million, \$110 million and \$120 million in years 1 to 3. WACC is 9% and terminal growth is 3%. Net debt is \$300 million and there are 50 million diluted shares. Find the value per share.
step: Discount the explicit flows: $100/1.09 = 91.74$, $110/1.09^2 = 92.58$ and $120/1.09^3 = 92.66$, which sum to \$276.99 million.
step: Terminal value at year 3: $120 \times 1.03/(0.09 - 0.03) = 123.6/0.06 = 2060$. Its present value is $2060/1.09^3 = 1590.70$.
step: Enterprise value $= 276.99 + 1590.70 = \$1{,}867.69$ million. The terminal value is $1590.70/1867.69 = 85\%$ of EV, a high share worth flagging.
step: Equity value $= 1867.69 - 300 = 1567.69$, and per share $= 1567.69/50 = \$31.35$.
answer: About \$31.35 per share.

@mistakes
- Subtracting interest from FCFF (it belongs in free cash flow to equity), or using a cost of equity to discount FCFF.
- Using a terminal growth rate at or above the WACC, which makes the Gordon formula meaningless or negative.
- Forgetting to discount the terminal value, or discounting it $N-1$ years.
- Converting EV to equity value by adding debt instead of subtracting it, or ignoring cash, leases and noncontrolling interests.

```python
def fcff(ebit, tax_rate, da, capex, delta_nwc):
    """Free cash flow to the firm."""
    nopat = ebit * (1 - tax_rate)
    return nopat + da - capex - delta_nwc
```
hint: NOPAT is EBIT after tax, before interest.

solution: Compute NOPAT, add depreciation, and subtract capex and the working capital increase. A negative `delta_nwc` means working capital fell, which releases cash, so subtracting it adds to FCFF.

```python
import math
assert math.isclose(dcf_ev([50], 0.10, 0.02), 625.0, rel_tol=1e-12)
expected = 80 / 1.08 + 90 / 1.08 ** 2 + 90 * 1.02 / 0.06 / 1.08 ** 2

```
model:
```python
def dcf_ev(fcfs, wacc, g):
    """Enterprise value from explicit flows and a Gordon terminal value."""
    if g >= wacc:
        raise ValueError("growth must be below the discount rate")
    pv_explicit = sum(
        cash / (1 + wacc) ** year for year, cash in enumerate(fcfs, start=1)
    )
    terminal = fcfs[-1] * (1 + g) / (wacc - g)
    return pv_explicit + terminal / (1 + wacc) ** len(fcfs)
```
hint: Discount the flow in year `t` by `(1 + wacc) ** t`, counting years from 1.
hint: The terminal value is built from the last flow and is discounted by the same factor as the last flow.
solution: Sum the discounted flows (years numbered from 1), compute the terminal value from the last flow, and discount it `len(fcfs)` years. Raise `ValueError` first when `g >= wacc`. The zero-growth test is a perpetuity of 10 at 5%, worth 200, the same as a three-year annuity plus a perpetuity that starts after it.

```python
def implied_exit_multiple(fcff_last, ebitda_last, wacc, g):
    """EV/EBITDA multiple implied by a Gordon-growth terminal value."""
    if g >= wacc:
        raise ValueError("growth must be below the discount rate")
```
