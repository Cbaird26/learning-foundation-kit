@article
Before you forecast anything you must know what the company has told investors. US public companies file reports with the SEC, and the free EDGAR database holds them all. A new analyst's most valuable skill is knowing where in a 150-page document the answer lives.

### The filings that matter

| Filing | What it is | Audited? |
|---|---|---|
| **10-K** | Annual report: business, risks, MD&A, full financial statements | Yes |
| **10-Q** | Quarterly report for each of the first three fiscal quarters | No (reviewed) |
| **8-K** | Current report of a material event: earnings release, an acquisition, an executive departure | No |
| **DEF 14A** | Proxy statement: board, executive pay, shareholder votes | No |

The fourth quarter has no 10-Q: its numbers are inside the 10-K. For a large accelerated filer the 10-K is due 60 days after the fiscal year end and the 10-Q 40 days after the quarter end; smaller companies get more time.

### Navigating a 10-K

The 10-K follows a fixed structure of **Items**. The ones you will use most: **Item 1 Business** (segments, customers, competition), **Item 1A Risk Factors**, **Item 7 MD&A** (management's discussion and analysis: why results changed, liquidity, critical accounting estimates), **Item 8 Financial Statements and Notes** (the statements and the footnotes on revenue, debt, leases, taxes and segments). Read the notes: they hold the detail the statements summarize, and changes in accounting policy are announced there.

### Earnings releases and calls

On the day results are announced the company files an 8-K with the press release and usually hosts a call: prepared remarks, then analyst questions. Listen for **guidance** (management's forecast ranges), changes in tone, and what management declines to answer. Companies also report **non-GAAP** measures such as adjusted EBITDA; SEC rules require a reconciliation to the most comparable GAAP measure, and your job is to read what was excluded and ask whether it recurs. Regulation FD means the company may not give some investors material information that others lack, so the call is open to all.

### Cleaning the data

Reported data need work before they enter a model. Three tasks recur.

- **Parsing**: amounts appear as `$1,234`, `(1,234)` for negatives, or a dash for zero.
- **Discrete quarters**: a 10-Q cash flow statement is **year to date**, so the quarter's figure is the difference between successive cumulative figures. Q4 equals the full year minus the nine-month figure.
- **Last twelve months (LTM)**: the most recent four quarters, a figure that stays current between 10-Ks:

$$\text{LTM} = \text{FY} + \text{YTD}_{\text{current}} - \text{YTD}_{\text{prior}}.$$

```python
ytd = [100.0, 230.0, 390.0]          # cumulative figures from three 10-Qs
quarters = [ytd[0]] + [b - a for a, b in zip(ytd, ytd[1:])]
print(quarters)                      # [100.0, 130.0, 160.0]
```

Always tie out: the four discrete quarters must add to the 10-K's annual figure. Real data may be restated, so note the filing date of each figure.

@example
title: Last twelve months
problem: Cedar Ridge Foods reported fiscal 2025 revenue of \$840.0 million. Through the first nine months, revenue was \$655.0 million in fiscal 2026 and \$610.0 million in fiscal 2025. Find LTM revenue at the end of the nine months of fiscal 2026.
step: The LTM window is the last four quarters: the fourth quarter of 2025 plus the nine months of 2026.
step: The fourth quarter of 2025 is $840.0 - 610.0 = 230.0$.
step: Add the nine months of 2026: $230.0 + 655.0 = 885.0$.
step: The formula gives the same: $840.0 + 655.0 - 610.0 = 885.0$.
answer: LTM revenue is \$885.0 million.

@example
title: What guidance implies
problem: On the call, Cedar Ridge's management guides fiscal 2026 revenue to \$960 million to \$980 million. Nine-month revenue was \$710 million and the prior-year fourth quarter was \$232 million. What growth does the guidance imply for the fourth quarter?
step: Implied Q4 revenue is the guidance minus the nine-month figure: low $960 - 710 = 250$, high $980 - 710 = 270$.
step: Growth against the prior-year fourth quarter: low $250/232 - 1 = 7.8\%$, high $270/232 - 1 = 16.4\%$.
step: Compare with recent quarters. If the first nine months grew near 8%, the top of the range assumes a clear acceleration, a claim to test on the call.
answer: Implied Q4 revenue of \$250-270 million, or growth of 7.8% to 16.4%.

@mistakes
- Treating a 10-Q cash flow figure as a single quarter when it is cumulative for the year to date.
- Taking a non-GAAP figure at face value without reading the reconciliation and what was excluded.
- Mixing figures from different filings without checking whether the earlier period was restated.
- Reading only the financial statements and skipping the notes, where debt terms, leases and accounting changes are explained.

model:
```python
def parse_amount(text):
    """Convert a printed financial amount to a float."""
    cleaned = text.strip().replace("$", "").replace(",", "").strip()
    if cleaned in ("", "-", chr(8211), chr(8212)):
        return 0.0
    if cleaned.startswith("(") and cleaned.endswith(")"):
        return -float(cleaned[1:-1])
    return float(cleaned)
```
hint: Clean the text first (dollar sign, commas, spaces), then handle the special cases.
hint: Check for the dash characters before calling `float`, and strip the parentheses before negating.
solution: Strip spaces, remove `$` and commas, return `0.0` for blank or dash text, negate the number inside parentheses, and otherwise call `float`. A leading minus sign such as `-7` works with `float` directly, so the lone hyphen is the only special case.

tests:
```python
assert quarterly_from_ytd([90, 200, 330, 470]) == [90, 110, 130, 140]
assert quarterly_from_ytd([50]) == [50]
assert quarterly_from_ytd([]) == []

```
model:
```python
def quarterly_from_ytd(ytd):
    """Discrete quarterly figures from cumulative year-to-date figures."""
    quarters = []
    previous = 0
    for cumulative in ytd:
        quarters.append(cumulative - previous)
        previous = cumulative
    return quarters
```
hint: Each quarter is the cumulative figure minus the previous cumulative figure.
hint: For the first quarter the previous cumulative figure is 0.
solution: Keep the previous cumulative value, starting at 0, and append the difference for each entry. Starting `previous` at 0 makes the first quarter equal to the first cumulative figure, and a cumulative figure that falls (as in the last test) correctly gives a negative quarter.

@quiz code
prompt: Write `ltm_from_quarters(quarters)` that returns the sum of the most recent four values in the list `quarters` (oldest first). If there are fewer than four values, raise `ValueError`. For example, `ltm_from_quarters([10, 20, 30, 40, 50])` is `140`.
starter:
```python
def ltm_from_quarters(quarters):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert ltm_from_quarters([10, 20, 30, 40, 50]) == 140
assert ltm_from_quarters([5, 5, 5, 5]) == 20
assert ltm_from_quarters([1.5, 2.5, 3.5, 4.5, 5.5, 6.5]) == 20.0
assert raises(ValueError, ltm_from_quarters, [1, 2, 3])
assert raises(ValueError, ltm_from_quarters, [])
```
model:
```python
def ltm_from_quarters(quarters):
    """Last twelve months: the sum of the latest four quarters."""
    if len(quarters) < 4:
        raise ValueError("need at least four quarters")
    return sum(quarters[-4:])
```
hint: Check the length before summing.
hint: A negative slice, `quarters[-4:]`, takes the last four items.
solution: Raise `ValueError` when fewer than four quarters exist, and otherwise sum the last four with `quarters[-4:]`. Using `quarters[:4]` would add the oldest four, the classic off-end mistake.
