@article
A pitch is a conversation with a skeptical audience that has little time. The portfolio manager or investment committee will decide in minutes whether your idea deserves more of theirs. The goal is not to win an argument; it is to give them what they need to decide, and to show that you know where your idea is weak.

### The five-minute structure

1. **The call (30 seconds):** the stock, the rating, the target and the upside. State it before anything else.
2. **The business (45 seconds):** what the company does and how it makes money, in plain words.
3. **The thesis (90 seconds):** two or three points on why the stock will move. Include your **variant perception**: where your view differs from the market's, and why you are right.
4. **Valuation (60 seconds):** the method, the scenarios and the sensitivity.
5. **Catalysts and timing (30 seconds):** the dated events that could bring the market to your view.
6. **Risks and what would prove you wrong (45 seconds):** the main risks, their size, and the evidence that would make you change your mind.

Pair it with a one-page summary: header, thesis, a small estimates table, valuation and risks. Rehearse with a timer; five minutes means about 700 spoken words.

### What is priced in

The market price already reflects a set of expectations. A **reverse DCF** asks what growth rate makes the DCF equal the market's enterprise value. For a Gordon-growth model $EV = \text{FCFF}_0(1+g)/(\text{WACC} - g)$; solving for $g$ gives

$$g = \frac{EV \times \text{WACC} - \text{FCFF}_0}{EV + \text{FCFF}_0}.$$

If the implied $g$ is 2% and you believe 4% is achievable, you have a clear variant view; if the implied $g$ is already 6%, the market expects a lot and your thesis must explain why it will get more.

### Risk and reward

Compare the upside to the bull case with the downside to the bear case. The **risk-reward ratio** is

$$\frac{\text{Bull value} - \text{Price}}{\text{Price} - \text{Bear value}}.$$

A ratio above one means the potential gain exceeds the potential loss; the ratio does not say how likely each is. The **break-even probability** is the probability $p$ of the bull case (with the bear case otherwise) at which the expected value equals today's price:

$$p = \frac{\text{Price} - \text{Bear}}{\text{Bull} - \text{Bear}}.$$

If you believe the bull case is likelier than $p$, the idea has a positive expected return under a two-scenario view. This is a framework for reasoning, not a promise.

### Defending the idea

Expect three kinds of questions: about facts ("what was last quarter's gross margin?"), about the model ("what happens to the target if margin is 100 basis points lower?"), and about judgment ("why is the market wrong?"). Know the answers from your sensitivity table. Concede a good point quickly and quantify its effect; defend a good point with evidence and cite the filing. Never bluff: "I don't know, I'll check and send you the figure today" is a professional answer. Before presenting, do a **pre-mortem**: imagine the idea failed in a year and write the three most likely reasons. Those become your risk section.

@example
title: Risk, reward and break-even
problem: A stock is at \$40. Your bull value is \$58 and your bear value is \$34. Find the risk-reward ratio and the break-even probability of the bull case.
step: Upside to the bull case is $58 - 40 = 18$ and downside to the bear case is $40 - 34 = 6$.
step: The risk-reward ratio is $18/6 = 3.0$.
step: Break-even probability: $p = (40 - 34)/(58 - 34) = 6/24 = 25\%$.
step: Check: $0.25 \times 58 + 0.75 \times 34 = 14.5 + 25.5 = 40$. If you think the bull case is more than 25% likely, the two-scenario expected value exceeds the price.
answer: Risk-reward ratio of 3.0; break-even probability of 25%.

@example
title: What the market price implies
problem: A company has enterprise value of \$1,000 million, last year's FCFF of \$60 million and a WACC of 9%. What perpetual growth rate does the market price imply?
step: Use $g = (EV \times \text{WACC} - \text{FCFF}_0)/(EV + \text{FCFF}_0)$.
step: Numerator: $1000 \times 0.09 - 60 = 30$. Denominator: $1000 + 60 = 1060$.
step: $g = 30/1060 = 2.83\%$.
step: Check: $60 \times 1.0283/(0.09 - 0.0283) = 61.70/0.0617 = 1000$. If your model supports faster growth, say so and show the evidence; if it does not, the stock may already price in what you expect.
answer: Implied growth of about 2.83% a year.

@mistakes
- Opening with background and reaching the recommendation in the last minute.
- Calling the risk-reward ratio a probability: it measures sizes of gain and loss, not likelihoods.
- Bluffing an answer instead of saying what you know, what you do not, and when you will find out.
- Listing risks that are generic ("the economy") instead of those that could actually break this thesis, with their size.

```python
def risk_reward(price, bull, bear):
    """Bull upside per unit of bear downside."""
    downside = price - bear
    if downside <= 0:
        return None
    return (bull - price) / downside
```
hint: Compute the downside first and check it before dividing.
hint: Return `None` when the downside is zero or negative.
solution: The downside is `price - bear`; when it is not positive the ratio is undefined, so return `None` before dividing. A bull value equal to the price gives a ratio of zero: no upside at all.

model:
```python
def implied_growth(ev, fcff, wacc):
    """Perpetual growth rate implied by the market enterprise value."""
    return (ev * wacc - fcff) / (ev + fcff)
```
hint: Multiply out `ev * (wacc - g) = fcff * (1 + g)` and collect the terms in `g`.
hint: The result is `g = (ev * wacc - fcff) / (ev + fcff)`.
solution: From $EV(\text{WACC} - g) = \text{FCFF}(1+g)$ we get $EV \cdot \text{WACC} - \text{FCFF} = g(EV + \text{FCFF})$, so the code returns that ratio. The round-trip test plugs the growth rate back into the Gordon formula and recovers the enterprise value.

model:
```python
def breakeven_probability(price, bull, bear):
    """Probability of the bull case that makes the expected value equal price."""
    if bull <= bear or price < bear or price > bull:
        raise ValueError("need bear <= price <= bull and bull > bear")
    return (price - bear) / (bull - bear)
```
hint: Validate the inputs first.
hint: Solve $p \cdot \text{bull} + (1 - p) \cdot \text{bear} = \text{price}$ for $p$.
solution: Setting $p\,\text{bull} + (1-p)\,\text{bear} = \text{price}$ gives $p = (\text{price} - \text{bear})/(\text{bull} - \text{bear})$. A price outside the range between bear and bull cannot be an expected value of the two outcomes, so the function raises an error.
