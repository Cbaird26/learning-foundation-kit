@article
Revenue tells you how big the company is; **margins** tell you how much of each dollar the owners keep. Most forecast errors that matter come from margin assumptions, not revenue, because a small change in margin on a large revenue base is a large change in profit.

### The operating income statement

Work down the statement and forecast each line with a method suited to its behavior.

| Line | Typical forecast method |
|---|---|
| Revenue | Drivers (week 4) |
| Cost of goods sold (COGS) | Percent of revenue, or unit cost times units |
| Gross profit | Revenue minus COGS; gross margin = gross profit / revenue |
| SG&A (selling, general and administrative) | Fixed part plus a percent of revenue |
| R&D | Percent of revenue or a budget growing with headcount |
| EBITDA | Gross profit minus operating expenses before D&A |
| D&A (depreciation and amortization) | From the schedules (week 6) |
| EBIT (operating income) | EBITDA minus D&A |

EBIT margin is EBIT divided by revenue. Interest and taxes come later (weeks 7 and 8).

### Fixed and variable costs

A **variable cost** moves with volume (materials, sales commissions); a **fixed cost** does not, within a range (rent, salaried staff, a plant's overhead). Most costs are mixed, so the model uses

$$\text{Cost} = \text{Fixed} + v \times \text{Revenue},$$

where $v$ is the variable cost as a fraction of revenue. This matters because when revenue grows, fixed costs are spread over more sales and margins rise; when it falls, they drop quickly.

### Operating leverage

The **contribution margin** is revenue minus variable costs. The **degree of operating leverage** (DOL) measures how much EBIT moves for a 1% move in revenue:

$$\text{DOL} = \frac{\%\Delta \text{EBIT}}{\%\Delta \text{Revenue}} = \frac{\text{Contribution margin}}{\text{EBIT}}.$$

The second form holds for a small change with fixed costs unchanged. A DOL of 3 means a 10% revenue gain lifts EBIT about 30%, and a 10% fall cuts it about 30%. High operating leverage (an airline, a software firm with a large development budget) makes profits volatile. Related is the **incremental margin**, $\Delta\text{EBIT}/\Delta\text{Revenue}$: the profit earned on each extra dollar of sales.

### Checking margins against reality

Test every margin against three anchors: the company's own history (and why it moved: price, mix, input costs, scale), competitors, and the industry. A forecast that lifts the EBIT margin five points in two years needs a named cause, such as a plant closing or a price increase already announced. Watch for one-time items (restructuring charges, litigation settlements) and decide whether to forecast them; most models set them to zero going forward, and say so.

### A first income statement in code

```python
def ebit_for(revenue, cogs_pct, sga_fixed, sga_pct, da):
    gross = revenue * (1 - cogs_pct)
    ebitda = gross - (sga_fixed + sga_pct * revenue)
    return ebitda - da

print(ebit_for(400, 0.62, 60, 0.08, 20))   # 40.0
```

Amounts are in millions of dollars throughout this lesson, and every company is fictional.

@example
title: Margins and operating leverage
problem: Ashgrove Cycles has revenue of \$400 million. COGS is 62% of revenue, SG&A is \$60 million fixed plus 8% of revenue, and D&A is \$20 million. Find EBIT, then EBIT if revenue rises 10%, and the DOL.
step: At \$400 million: gross profit $= 400 \times 0.38 = 152.0$. SG&A $= 60 + 0.08 \times 400 = 92.0$. EBITDA $= 152.0 - 92.0 = 60.0$, EBIT $= 60.0 - 20 = 40.0$, a 10.0% EBIT margin.
step: At \$440 million: gross profit $= 440 \times 0.38 = 167.2$. SG&A $= 60 + 35.2 = 95.2$. EBITDA $= 72.0$, EBIT $= 52.0$, an 11.8% margin.
step: EBIT rose $52.0/40.0 - 1 = 30\%$ on revenue growth of 10%, so the DOL is $30/10 = 3.0$.
step: Check with the contribution form: contribution margin $= 400 - 248 - 32 = 120$, and $120/40 = 3.0$.
answer: EBIT of \$40.0 million rising to \$52.0 million; DOL of 3.0.

@example
title: Incremental margin
problem: Using the Ashgrove figures, what is the incremental EBIT margin on the extra \$40 million of revenue, and why does it exceed the average margin?
step: EBIT rose from \$40.0 million to \$52.0 million, so $\Delta\text{EBIT} = 12.0$.
step: Revenue rose by \$40 million, so the incremental margin is $12.0/40 = 30\%$.
step: Each extra dollar of sales brings 30 cents of contribution (100% - 62% - 8%) and no extra fixed cost, so the incremental margin (30%) exceeds the average EBIT margin (10%).
answer: 30%.

@mistakes
- Forecasting every cost line as a flat percent of revenue, which hides operating leverage and understates the swing in profit.
- Applying a historical margin without asking what caused it, so one-time gains or charges become permanent.
- Computing DOL with EBITDA instead of EBIT, or mixing up the contribution margin with the gross margin.
- Forgetting that D&A is a cost between EBITDA and EBIT, so EBIT is overstated if D&A is left out.

```python
def operating_profit(revenue, cogs_pct, sga_fixed, sga_pct, da):
    """Gross profit, EBITDA and EBIT."""
    gross_profit = revenue * (1 - cogs_pct)
    sga = sga_fixed + sga_pct * revenue
    ebitda = gross_profit - sga
    ebit = ebitda - da
    return gross_profit, ebitda, ebit
```
hint: Gross profit is revenue times one minus the COGS percentage.
hint: SG&A has a fixed part and a variable part; EBIT is EBITDA minus `da`.
solution: Compute gross profit, then SG&A as fixed plus percent of revenue, subtract SG&A to get EBITDA, and subtract D&A to get EBIT. With zero revenue the fixed costs remain, so EBITDA is negative: that is operating leverage working in reverse.

model:
```python
def dol(revenue_before, ebit_before, revenue_after, ebit_after):
    """Degree of operating leverage from two observations."""
    revenue_change = revenue_after / revenue_before - 1
    if revenue_change == 0:
        return None
    ebit_change = ebit_after / ebit_before - 1
    return ebit_change / revenue_change
```
hint: Compute each percentage change as after divided by before, minus 1.
hint: Guard against dividing by a zero revenue change.
solution: Divide the EBIT percent change by the revenue percent change, returning `None` when revenue is unchanged. In the third test revenue falls 10% and EBIT falls 30%, which is also a DOL of 3: leverage works in both directions.

```python
def total_costs(revenues, fixed, variable_pct):
    """Fixed plus variable cost for each revenue level."""
    return [fixed + variable_pct * revenue for revenue in revenues]
```
