@article
A great company in a terrible industry usually disappoints, and an average company in a great industry often does fine. So the analyst starts with the industry: how big is it, how fast is it growing, who competes, and what limits profits?

### Market size and growth

Start with **market size**, the annual revenue (or units) of the whole industry, and project it. A **top-down** estimate starts from a broad figure (all households, all hospital beds) and applies penetration and spending per unit. A **bottom-up** estimate multiplies customers by revenue per customer. Estimate both and reconcile them. Once you have a size $S_0$ and a growth rate $g$, the size after $t$ years is $S_t = S_0(1+g)^t$. When you know two sizes, the **compound annual growth rate** is

$$\text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{S_t}{S_0}\right)^{1/t} - 1.$$

A company's **market share** is its revenue divided by the market's revenue. A company can grow faster than its market only by gaining share.

### Concentration

How many firms compete matters for pricing. Two measures:

- The **concentration ratio** $CR_n$ is the combined market share of the $n$ largest firms.
- The **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index** (HHI) sums the squares of every firm's share, with shares in percentage points:

$$\text{HHI} = \sum_i s_i^2.$$

A monopoly has an HHI of 10,000; $N$ equal firms have $10{,}000/N$. Antitrust agencies use the HHI to screen mergers, and their thresholds have changed over time (the 2023 US Merger Guidelines treat a market above 1,800 as highly concentrated), so check the current guidelines. For an analyst, a rising HHI suggests rising pricing power, but concentration alone proves nothing: a market with few firms can still be fiercely competitive.

### The five forces

The standard framework, **Porter's five forces**, asks what determines long-run industry profitability:

| Force | High when... |
|---|---|
| Threat of new entrants | Capital needs, scale economies and brand barriers are low |
| Bargaining power of suppliers | Few suppliers, no substitute inputs, high switching costs |
| Bargaining power of buyers | Few large buyers, standard products, low switching costs |
| Threat of substitutes | Other products meet the same need at a similar price |
| Rivalry among competitors | Many similar firms, slow growth, high fixed costs, exit barriers |

Strong forces compress profits. Pair the forces with the **industry life cycle** (embryonic, growth, shakeout, mature, decline): growth industries reward volume, mature industries reward cost discipline and share.

@example
title: Concentration of a fictional industry
problem: Five firms in regional ferry services have market shares of 35%, 25%, 20%, 12% and 8%. Find $CR_3$ and the HHI.
step: $CR_3$ adds the three largest shares: $35 + 25 + 20 = 80\%$.
step: Square each share in percentage points: $35^2 = 1225$, $25^2 = 625$, $20^2 = 400$, $12^2 = 144$, $8^2 = 64$.
step: Add them: $\text{HHI} = 1225 + 625 + 400 + 144 + 64 = 2458$.
step: With five equal firms the HHI would be 2,000, so this industry is more concentrated than five equal competitors, because its leaders are larger than the tail.
answer: CR3 = 80%; HHI = 2,458.

@example
title: Projecting a market
problem: A market is worth \$8.0 billion today and you expect 7% annual growth. How large is it in 5 years?
step: Use $S_t = S_0(1+g)^t$ with $S_0 = 8.0$, $g = 0.07$, $t = 5$.
step: $1.07^5 = 1.402552$.
step: $S_5 = 8.0 \times 1.402552 = \$11.22$ billion.
answer: About \$11.22 billion.

@mistakes
- Using shares as decimals in the HHI (0.35 squared) and then comparing the result with thresholds quoted on the 0-10,000 scale.
- Adding growth rates instead of compounding them when projecting several years.
- Assuming a high-share firm has pricing power without checking buyer power, substitutes and entry barriers.
- Confusing market size (revenue of the whole industry) with the company's revenue when computing share.

model:
```python
def project_market(size, growth, years):
    """Market size at the end of years 1..years."""
    sizes = []
    for _ in range(years):
        size = size * (1 + growth)
        sizes.append(size)
    return sizes
```
hint: Multiply the size by `1 + growth` once per year and record each result.
hint: The starting size is year 0 and must not appear in the output list.
solution: Loop `years` times, update `size` by `size * (1 + growth)`, and append the new value. Starting the list with the original size would give the wrong length and shift every year by one.

```python
def concentration_ratio(shares, n):
    """Combined share of the n largest firms."""
    return sum(sorted(shares, reverse=True)[:n])
```
hint: Sort the shares from largest to smallest first.
hint: Slice the first `n` items and add them; slicing past the end is safe in Python.
solution: Sort in descending order, take the first `n` shares with a slice, and sum them. A slice longer than the list simply returns the whole list, which handles `n` larger than the number of firms.
