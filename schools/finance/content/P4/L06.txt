@article
Schedules are the supporting calculations beneath the three statements: working capital, PP&E and depreciation (this week), and debt (next week). They turn operating assumptions into the balance sheet and the cash flows, and they are where models usually go wrong, because the logic is simple but the bookkeeping is long.

### Working capital from days ratios

**Operating net working capital** is $\text{AR} + \text{Inventory} - \text{AP}$ (accounts receivable plus inventory minus accounts payable), excluding cash and debt. Three **days** ratios (using 365 days) describe it:

$$\text{DSO} = \frac{\text{AR}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 365, \quad \text{DIO} = \frac{\text{Inventory}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365, \quad \text{DPO} = \frac{\text{AP}}{\text{COGS}} \times 365.$$

DSO is days sales outstanding, DIO days inventory outstanding, DPO days payables outstanding. Here the balances are year-end figures and the ratios are measured on them; some analysts use average balances, so state your choice. The **cash conversion cycle** is $\text{DSO} + \text{DIO} - \text{DPO}$: the days cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers.

To forecast, hold the days constant (or move them for a stated reason) and solve for the balances: $\text{AR} = \text{Revenue} \times \text{DSO}/365$, and likewise for inventory and payables. The cash flow effect is the change: **an increase in net working capital is a use of cash**, so the cash flow statement shows $-(\text{NWC}_t - \text{NWC}_{t-1})$.

### Capital spending and PP&E

**Capex** is spending on long-lived assets. Forecast it as a percent of revenue, or split it into **maintenance capex** (keeping capacity) and **growth capex** (adding it). A growing company that forecasts capex well below depreciation forever is quietly shrinking its asset base. The PP&E roll-forward is

$$\text{PP\&E}_{\text{end}} = \text{PP\&E}_{\text{begin}} + \text{Capex} - \text{Depreciation}.$$

### The depreciation waterfall

Under straight-line depreciation an asset costing $C$ with life $L$ years and no salvage value yields $C/L$ of depreciation each year. Since each year's capex adds a new layer, total depreciation in any year is the sum of the layers still alive: a **waterfall**. In this course a layer starts depreciating in the year of purchase, with a full year's charge (many companies use a half-year or mid-month convention; state yours). Existing assets are forecast separately, often as the current depreciation running off.

| Year | Capex | Layer 1 | Layer 2 | Layer 3 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100 | 20 | | | 20 |
| 2 | 100 | 20 | 20 | | 40 |
| 3 | 100 | 20 | 20 | 20 | 60 |

(These are \$ millions with a 5-year life.) Depreciation climbs for five years and then levels off when old layers expire. Accounting depreciation is a non-cash expense: it reduces EBIT and taxes but cash leaves when the capex is paid.

@example
title: Working capital from days
problem: Fernhill Pottery has revenue of \$600 million and COGS of \$360 million. Its DSO is 45 days, DIO 60 days and DPO 40 days. Find AR, inventory, payables, net working capital and the cash conversion cycle.
step: $\text{AR} = 600 \times 45/365 = 73.97$.
step: $\text{Inventory} = 360 \times 60/365 = 59.18$ and $\text{AP} = 360 \times 40/365 = 39.45$.
step: $\text{NWC} = 73.97 + 59.18 - 39.45 = 93.70$ (millions of dollars).
step: The cash conversion cycle is $45 + 60 - 40 = 65$ days.
answer: NWC of \$93.70 million and a cycle of 65 days.

@example
title: A PP&E roll-forward
problem: Fernhill begins the year with PP&E of \$500 million. Depreciation on existing assets is \$50 million, and it spends \$100 million of capex, depreciated over 5 years starting in the year of purchase. Find ending PP&E.
step: Depreciation on the new capex is $100/5 = 20$.
step: Total depreciation is $50 + 20 = 70$.
step: $\text{PP\&E}_{\text{end}} = 500 + 100 - 70 = 530$.
answer: Ending PP&E of \$530 million.

@mistakes
- Treating a rise in inventory or receivables as a source of cash; it ties up cash.
- Mixing the base: DSO uses revenue, but DIO and DPO use COGS.
- Letting depreciation continue after an asset's life ends, so layers are depreciated more than their cost.
- Setting capex below depreciation for a growing business with no explanation, so the asset base and future revenue capacity shrink.

model:
```python
def depreciation_schedule(capex, life):
    """Straight-line depreciation waterfall."""
    schedule = []
    for year in range(len(capex)):
        total = 0.0
        first_layer = max(0, year - life + 1)
        for layer in range(first_layer, year + 1):
            total += capex[layer] / life
        schedule.append(total)
    return schedule
```
hint: In each year, add up the layers that are still being depreciated.
hint: A layer bought in year `s` is alive in years `s` through `s + life - 1`.
solution: For each year, loop over the capex layers from `year - life + 1` (or the first year) through the current year and add `capex / life` for each. The `max(0, ...)` keeps the index valid early on, and the lower bound is what stops old layers after their life ends; the test with life 2 checks it.

model:
```python
def ppe_rollforward(begin, capex, depreciation):
    """Ending PP&E for each year."""
    balances = []
    for spend, dep in zip(capex, depreciation):
        begin = begin + spend - dep
        balances.append(begin)
    return balances
```
hint: Update the balance each year and carry it forward.
hint: Pair each year's capex with that year's depreciation using `zip`.
solution: Loop over the paired lists, set the balance to beginning plus capex minus depreciation, record it, and let it serve as next year's beginning. Recomputing every year from the original beginning balance would ignore earlier years.
