@article
An **integrated model** forecasts the income statement, the balance sheet and the cash flow statement together, so that a change in one flows consistently to the others. Its great virtue is a built-in audit: if the balance sheet balances, many errors cannot be hiding. If not, something is wrong and you must find it.

### How the statements connect

- **Net income** from the income statement flows to **retained earnings** on the balance sheet (less dividends) and starts the operating section of the cash flow statement.
- **Depreciation** reduces net income and PP&E, and is added back in operating cash flow because it uses no cash.
- **Changes in working capital** (receivables, inventory, payables) come from the balance sheet and enter operating cash flow with the sign "increase in an asset uses cash; increase in a liability provides cash".
- **Capex** is an investing outflow that raises PP&E.
- **Debt repayments, new borrowing, dividends and share issues** are financing flows that change debt and equity.
- **Cash on the balance sheet** equals the prior cash plus the net change in cash from the cash flow statement. Cash is not a plug you force to balance the sheet; it is computed from the cash flow statement, and the balance check tests your work.

### A one-year model

Assume a prior balance sheet with cash, accounts receivable (AR), PP&E, accounts payable (AP), debt and equity, and these steps for the forecast year:

1. $\text{COGS} = \text{Revenue} \times \text{COGS\%}$; $\text{EBIT} = \text{Revenue} - \text{COGS} - \text{SG\&A} - \text{D\&A}$.
2. $\text{Interest} = r \times \text{Beginning debt}$; $\text{EBT} = \text{EBIT} - \text{Interest}$; $\text{Tax} = t \times \text{EBT}$ if EBT is positive, else 0; $\text{NI} = \text{EBT} - \text{Tax}$.
3. $\text{AR} = \text{Revenue} \times \text{DSO}/365$; $\text{AP} = \text{COGS} \times \text{DPO}/365$.
4. $\text{CFO} = \text{NI} + \text{D\&A} - \Delta\text{AR} + \Delta\text{AP}$; $\text{CFI} = -\text{Capex}$; $\text{CFF} = -\text{Debt repayment} - \text{Dividends}$.
5. $\text{Cash}_{\text{end}} = \text{Cash}_{\text{begin}} + \text{CFO} + \text{CFI} + \text{CFF}$.
6. $\text{PP\&E}_{\text{end}} = \text{PP\&E}_{\text{begin}} + \text{Capex} - \text{D\&A}$; $\text{Debt}_{\text{end}} = \text{Debt}_{\text{begin}} - \text{Repayment}$; $\text{Equity}_{\text{end}} = \text{Equity}_{\text{begin}} + \text{NI} - \text{Dividends}$.

Then check: $\text{Cash} + \text{AR} + \text{PP\&E} = \text{AP} + \text{Debt} + \text{Equity}$.

### Why it balances

Starting from a balanced sheet, each flow changes one asset and one claim, or two assets, or two claims, by the same amount. Net income raises equity and (through the cash flow statement) cash or working capital. Capex raises PP&E and lowers cash. A repayment lowers debt and cash. Because every cash flow item is the mirror of a balance sheet change, the sheet balances. When it does not, the difference tells you the culprit. A difference equal to depreciation suggests D&A was handled on one side only; a difference equal to a dividend suggests it was subtracted from cash but not equity, or twice; a difference equal to the change in a working capital item suggests a sign error.

### Debugging and tolerances

Test with `assert`, and compare floats with a tolerance: `abs(assets - claims) < 1e-6`. Check the opening balance sheet first. Then isolate by changing one assumption at a time (set capex to zero, dividends to zero) until you find the line that breaks it. Keep sign conventions explicit: label each cash flow as positive for cash in and negative for cash out.

@example
title: One year, three statements
problem: Using the balance sheet above (in millions of dollars: cash 50, AR 100, PP&E 400, AP 60, debt 200, equity 290), the forecast year has revenue 1,100; COGS 60% of revenue; SG&A 220; D&A 40; capex 60; tax rate 25%; interest rate 5% on beginning debt; DSO 36.5 days; DPO 36.5 days; debt repayment 30; dividends 20. Integrate it.
step: Income statement: COGS $= 660$; EBITDA $= 1100 - 660 - 220 = 220$; EBIT $= 220 - 40 = 180$; interest $= 0.05 \times 200 = 10$; EBT $= 170$; tax $= 42.5$; NI $= 127.5$.
step: Working capital: $\text{AR} = 1100 \times 36.5/365 = 110$ and $\text{AP} = 660 \times 36.5/365 = 66$. So $\Delta\text{AR} = +10$ and $\Delta\text{AP} = +6$.
step: Cash flows: CFO $= 127.5 + 40 - 10 + 6 = 163.5$; CFI $= -60$; CFF $= -30 - 20 = -50$. Net change in cash $= +53.5$, so ending cash is $103.5$.
step: Other balances: PP&E $= 400 + 60 - 40 = 420$; debt $= 170$; equity $= 290 + 127.5 - 20 = 397.5$.
step: Check: assets $= 103.5 + 110 + 420 = 633.5$; claims $= 66 + 170 + 397.5 = 633.5$. It balances.
answer: Net income of \$127.5 million; ending cash of \$103.5 million; total assets and claims of \$633.5 million.

@example
title: Finding the error
problem: A model's balance sheet shows total assets of \$640 million and total liabilities plus equity of \$600 million. The model's depreciation expense is \$40 million. Where should you look first?
step: The imbalance is \$40 million, exactly equal to depreciation, and assets are too high.
step: Cash comes from the cash flow statement, which adds back depreciation, so cash should be right. PP&E being too high suggests that the roll-forward added capex but did not subtract depreciation.
step: Fix it: $\text{PP\&E}_{\text{end}} = \text{PP\&E}_{\text{begin}} + \text{Capex} - \text{D\&A}$, then rerun the check.
answer: The PP&E roll-forward most likely omits depreciation.

@mistakes
- Plugging cash (or any line) to force the balance sheet to balance, which hides the error instead of fixing it.
- Getting the working capital sign wrong: an increase in receivables is a use of cash, an increase in payables a source.
- Forgetting to add depreciation back in operating cash flow, or to subtract it from PP&E.
- Comparing floats with `==` in the balance check instead of a small tolerance.

```python
def cash_from_operations(net_income, da, ar_begin, ar_end, ap_begin, ap_end):
    """Operating cash flow with working capital adjustments."""
    change_ar = ar_end - ar_begin
    change_ap = ap_end - ap_begin
    return net_income + da - change_ar + change_ap
```
hint: An increase in receivables uses cash; an increase in payables provides it.
hint: Compute each change as end minus begin, then subtract the AR change and add the AP change.
solution: Operating cash flow is `net_income + da - (ar_end - ar_begin) + (ap_end - ap_begin)`. In the second test receivables fall by 10 (a source of cash) and payables fall by 5 (a use), so 80 + 10 + 10 - 5 = 95.

    """True if assets equal liabilities plus equity within tol."""
    assets = bs["cash"] + bs["ar"] + bs["ppe"]
    claims = bs["ap"] + bs["debt"] + bs["equity"]

model:
```python
def integrate_year(bs, a):
    """Roll a balance sheet forward one year."""
    cogs = a["revenue"] * a["cogs_pct"]
    ebit = a["revenue"] - cogs - a["sga"] - a["da"]
    interest = a["rate"] * bs["debt"]
    ebt = ebit - interest
    tax = a["tax_rate"] * ebt if ebt > 0 else 0.0
    net_income = ebt - tax
    ar = a["revenue"] * a["dso"] / 365
    ap = cogs * a["dpo"] / 365
    cfo = net_income + a["da"] - (ar - bs["ar"]) + (ap - bs["ap"])
    cfi = -a["capex"]
    cff = -a["repayment"] - a["dividends"]
    return {
        "cash": bs["cash"] + cfo + cfi + cff,
        "ar": ar,
        "ppe": bs["ppe"] + a["capex"] - a["da"],
        "ap": ap,
        "debt": bs["debt"] - a["repayment"],
        "equity": bs["equity"] + net_income - a["dividends"],
        "net_income": net_income,
    }
```
hint: Do the income statement first, then working capital, then the cash flow statement.
hint: Cash is the prior cash plus CFO, CFI and CFF; PP&E, debt and equity each have their own roll-forward.
solution: Follow the six steps from the article: income statement down to net income, AR and AP from days, the three cash flow sections, then the roll-forward of every balance sheet line. The tests confirm that the balance sheet balances without any plug, which is the real proof the links are right.
