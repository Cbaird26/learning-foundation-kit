@article
Revenue is the top of the model, and every other line hangs from it, so an error here travels everywhere. The mistake beginners make is to grow last year's revenue by a guessed percent. Professionals forecast the **drivers** that create revenue, so the forecast can be argued with, tested and updated.

### Driver trees

Write revenue as a product of things you can observe:

- **Volume times price**: $\text{Revenue} = \text{Units} \times \text{Average selling price (ASP)}$.
- **Subscription**: $\text{Revenue} = \text{Average subscribers} \times \text{ARPU}$, where ARPU is average revenue per user per period.
- **Retail**: sales per store times average stores, split into **same-store sales** (stores open at least a year) and new stores.
- **Segments**: forecast each business separately and add them.

When two drivers each grow, revenue growth compounds: $(1+g_{\text{units}})(1+g_{\text{price}}) - 1$, a bit more than the sum of the two growth rates.

### The subscriber roll-forward

For recurring-revenue businesses the key schedule is the **roll-forward**:

$$\text{Ending} = \text{Beginning} \times (1 - \text{churn}) + \text{Gross additions}.$$

**Churn** is the fraction of beginning subscribers who leave in the period. Revenue uses the average of beginning and ending subscribers, because additions and departures happen throughout the period:

$$\text{Revenue} = \frac{\text{Beginning} + \text{Ending}}{2} \times \text{ARPU} \times \text{months in period}.$$

### Making the assumptions defensible

Each driver needs evidence: its own history from the filings (10-K, 10-Q, and the supplemental data in the earnings release), management guidance, the industry (week 2) and a comparison with competitors. Then run three sanity checks. First, does implied **market share** stay plausible? A forecast that has the company grow 15% a year for ten years in a market growing 4% implies steadily rising share. Second, is growth consistent with capacity (stores, plants, salespeople)? Third, is the pattern consistent with history, and if it breaks, do you have a reason?

### In code

Keep assumptions in a dictionary and have a function turn them into numbers. You can then change one assumption and rerun, which is exactly what sensitivity analysis (week 10) needs.

```python
assumptions = {"units": 500_000, "asp": 240.0,
               "unit_growth": 0.06, "asp_growth": 0.03}

def next_revenue(a):
    units = a["units"] * (1 + a["unit_growth"])
    asp = a["asp"] * (1 + a["asp_growth"])
    return units * asp

print(round(next_revenue(assumptions)))   # 131016000
```

@example
title: Volume and price
problem: Brightwater Appliances sold 500,000 units at an average price of \$240 last year. You forecast 6% unit growth and 3% price growth. Find next year's revenue and revenue growth.
step: Last year's revenue was $500{,}000 \times 240 = \$120.0$ million.
step: Next year's units are $500{,}000 \times 1.06 = 530{,}000$ and the price is $240 \times 1.03 = \$247.20$.
step: Revenue is $530{,}000 \times 247.20 = \$131.016$ million.
step: Growth is $131.016/120.0 - 1 = 9.18\%$, equal to $1.06 \times 1.03 - 1$, not simply $6\% + 3\%$.
answer: Revenue of \$131.016 million, growth of 9.18%.

@example
title: One quarter of a subscriber model
problem: Northlight Streaming starts the quarter with 2.00 million subscribers, adds 0.30 million gross, and loses 4% of its beginning subscribers. ARPU is \$11.50 a month. Find ending subscribers and the quarter's revenue.
step: Ending subscribers: $2.00 \times (1 - 0.04) + 0.30 = 1.92 + 0.30 = 2.22$ million.
step: Average subscribers: $(2.00 + 2.22)/2 = 2.11$ million.
step: Revenue: $2.11 \times 11.50 \times 3 = \$72.795$ million for the three months.
step: The same roll-forward repeated each quarter, with the ending subscribers becoming the next beginning, gives the full-year forecast.
answer: 2.22 million ending subscribers; revenue of \$72.795 million.

@mistakes
- Forecasting revenue growth as one rounded percentage with no drivers behind it.
- Using ending subscribers instead of the average to compute revenue, which overstates revenue in a growing base.
- Adding growth rates (6% + 3% = 9%) instead of multiplying the growth factors.
- Mixing periods: applying a monthly ARPU to a quarter without multiplying by three, or an annual churn rate to a quarterly roll-forward.

model:
```python
def forecast_revenue(units, price, unit_growth, price_growth, years):
    """Revenue for years 1..years from unit and price growth."""
    revenues = []
    for _ in range(years):
        units = units * (1 + unit_growth)
        price = price * (1 + price_growth)
        revenues.append(units * price)
    return revenues
```
hint: Update units and price each year, then record their product.
hint: The current year's revenue is not in the output, only years 1 through `years`.
solution: Inside the loop grow `units` and `price` by their own rates and append `units * price`. Because each variable is updated before the append, year 1 already reflects one year of growth, and a zero-year forecast returns an empty list.

model:
```python
def subscriber_revenue(begin, gross_adds, churn, arpu):
    """Quarterly revenue from a subscriber roll-forward."""
    revenues = []
    for adds in gross_adds:
        end = begin * (1 - churn) + adds
        average = (begin + end) / 2
        revenues.append(average * arpu * 3)
        begin = end
    return revenues
```
hint: Compute the ending count first, then the average of beginning and ending.
hint: After recording the revenue, the ending count becomes the next quarter's beginning count.
solution: For each quarter, apply churn to the beginning count and add the gross additions, average the two counts for revenue, multiply by three months of ARPU, and carry the ending count forward. Using the ending count for revenue, or forgetting to carry it forward, are the typical errors.

model:
```python
def segment_total(segments, years):
    """Total revenue of all segments for years 1..years."""
    totals = []
    for year in range(1, years + 1):
        totals.append(sum(rev * (1 + g) ** year for rev, g in segments))
    return totals
```
hint: Segment revenue in year $t$ is its current revenue times $(1+g)^t$.
hint: For each year, add the segments together.
solution: For year `t` from 1 to `years`, sum `rev * (1 + g) ** t` across segments. Each segment compounds at its own rate, so adding the growth rates first or using one blended rate would give the wrong total.
