@article
**Electric current** is the flow of electric charge, usually electrons moving through a wire. It is measured in **amperes (A)**. Current flows only when there is a complete loop, called a **circuit**, and an energy source pushes the charges around it.

### Voltage, current and resistance

- **Voltage** ($V$, in volts) is the "push" the battery gives each unit of charge. A typical AA cell supplies $1.5\ \mathrm{V}$.
- **Current** ($I$, in amperes) is how much charge flows each second.
- **Resistance** ($R$, in ohms, $\Omega$) is how much a material opposes the flow of current. Thin wires, long wires and a bulb's filament have more resistance.

**Ohm's law** connects them:

$$V = IR \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad I = \frac{V}{R}, \quad R = \frac{V}{I}.$$

The **electric power** (the rate at which energy is used) is

$$P = IV,$$

in watts (W). Conductors, such as copper, let charges flow easily. Insulators, such as rubber and plastic, do not.

### Circuit parts

A simple circuit has a **source** (battery), **conducting wires**, a **load** (a bulb or motor) and often a **switch**. An open switch breaks the loop, so the current stops.

### Series circuits

In a **series** circuit there is only one path, so the same current flows through every part. Resistances add:

$$R_\text{total} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots$$

Adding more bulbs in series increases the total resistance, so the current drops and each bulb is dimmer. If one bulb is removed or burns out, the loop breaks and **all** bulbs go out.

### Parallel circuits

In a **parallel** circuit there are several branches. Each branch gets the full battery voltage, so each bulb shines at the same brightness as if it were alone. The total resistance is **smaller** than any branch resistance:

$$\frac{1}{R_\text{total}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \cdots$$

The total current is the sum of the branch currents. If one bulb is removed, the other branches keep working. This is why household outlets are wired in parallel.

@example
title: Ohm's law and total resistance
problem: A $12\ \mathrm{V}$ battery is connected to a bulb of resistance $4\ \Omega$. Find the current. Then find the current if a second $4\ \Omega$ bulb is added in series.
step: Use $I = V/R$: $I = 12 \div 4 = 3\ \mathrm{A}$.
step: In series the resistances add: $R_\text{total} = 4 + 4 = 8\ \Omega$.
step: $I = 12 \div 8 = 1.5\ \mathrm{A}$.
step: The current halves, so each bulb is dimmer than before.

@mistakes
- Adding the branch resistances in a parallel circuit. The total resistance is less than the smallest branch.
- Thinking that current is used up by the first bulb. The same current flows through every part of a series loop.
- Forgetting that a circuit must be a complete loop.
- Using milliamps or kilohms without converting to amperes and ohms.

check: 1 / (1 / 6 + 1 / 3)
tol: 1%
unit: Ω
hint: Add the reciprocals: $1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2$.
hint: $1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2$.
solution: $\frac{1}{R} = \frac16 + \frac13 = \frac12$, so $R = 2\ \Omega$.

@practice self
prompt: Describe a circuit diagram (with a battery, a switch and a bulb) in words, and explain what happens to the current and to the bulb when the switch is open and when it is closed.
solution: The battery's positive terminal is connected by a wire to one side of the switch, the other side of the switch is wired to the bulb, and the bulb is wired back to the battery's negative terminal, making one loop. With the switch open, the loop is broken, so no current flows and the bulb is off. With the switch closed, the loop is complete, so current flows through the bulb and the bulb lights up.
