@article
The binomial model gets closer to a continuous-time model as the steps shrink. In the limit, the stock price follows **geometric Brownian motion**, and the European option price has a closed form: the **Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM)** formula.

### Assumptions

- The stock price follows geometric Brownian motion: $\ln(S_T/S_0)$ is normal with variance $\sigma^2 T$, with constant volatility $\sigma$.
- The risk-free rate $r$ (continuously compounded) and any dividend yield $q$ are constant.
- Trading is continuous, with no transaction costs or taxes, and shares can be shorted and divided freely.
- There are no arbitrage opportunities. The option is **European**.

None holds exactly; the model is a benchmark that traders adjust (for example through implied volatility, Week 10).

### The formula

With $S_0$ the spot price, $K$ the strike, $T$ the time to expiration in years, $r$ and $q$ continuously compounded:
$$d_1 = \frac{\ln(S_0/K) + (r - q + \sigma^2/2)\,T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}},\qquad d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}.$$
$$c = S_0 e^{-qT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2),\qquad p = Ke^{-rT}N(-d_2) - S_0e^{-qT}N(-d_1),$$
where $N(\cdot)$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function. Setting $q=0$ gives the formula for a non-dividend stock.

### Interpreting the terms

- $N(d_2)$ is the **risk-neutral probability** that the option finishes in the money, $P^*(S_T>K)$. The second term $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$ is the present value of the strike paid, weighted by that probability.
- $e^{-qT}N(d_1)$ is the call's **delta** (Week 9): the number of shares in the replicating portfolio. The first term is the present value of the stock received.
- A call equals a leveraged long position in the stock, $S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1)$, financed by borrowing $Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$.

### Other underlyings

For a stock index, $q$ is the index's dividend yield. For a currency option, with the domestic rate $r$ and the foreign interest rate $r_f$, use $q = r_f$ (this is the Garman-Kohlhagen form of the model); the price is in domestic currency per unit of foreign currency. For a futures option, $q = r$ and $S_0$ is replaced by the futures price (Black's model).

### Checking your work

The model satisfies put-call parity, $c - p = S_0e^{-qT} - Ke^{-rT}$, so compute one price and check the other. Comparative statics: the call rises with $S_0$, $\sigma$ and $r$ (and with $T$ when $q=0$) and falls with $K$ and $q$; the put rises with $K$, $\sigma$ and $q$ and falls with $S_0$ and $r$. Volatility raises both calls and puts, since option holders gain from large moves and lose nothing from large adverse moves. **Volatility is the only input that cannot be observed directly.**

In Python, `NormalDist().cdf(x)` from the `statistics` module, or `0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))`, computes $N(x)$.

@example
title: Pricing a call and a put
problem: A stock trades at \$100 and pays no dividends. Price a European call and put with strike \$105 and 6 months to expiration, if $r = 4\%$ (continuously compounded) and $\sigma = 25\%$.
step: $\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.25\sqrt{0.5} = 0.176777$. Then $d_1 = [\ln(100/105) + (0.04 + 0.03125)\times 0.5]/0.176777 = (-0.048790 + 0.035625)/0.176777 = -0.0745$ and $d_2 = -0.0745 - 0.1768 = -0.2513$.
step: $N(d_1) = 0.4703$ and $N(d_2) = 0.4008$. The risk-neutral probability of finishing above \$105 is about 40.1%.
step: $Ke^{-rT} = 105\,e^{-0.02} = 102.9209$. Call: $c = 100 \times 0.4703 - 102.9209 \times 0.4008 = \$5.78$.
step: Put: $N(-d_2) = 0.5992$ and $N(-d_1) = 0.5297$, so $p = 102.9209 \times 0.5992 - 100 \times 0.5297 = \$8.70$.
step: Parity check: $c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0 = 5.78 + 102.92 - 100 = 8.70$. It matches the put.
answer: Call about \$5.78; put about \$8.70.

@example
title: A put on a stock index
problem: An index stands at 2,000 with a continuous dividend yield of 2%. Price a European put with strike 1,950 and 3 months (0.25 years) to expiration, with $r = 5\%$ and $\sigma = 18\%$ (all continuously compounded). The index multiplier is 1.
step: $\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.09$. $d_1 = [\ln(2000/1950) + (0.05 - 0.02 + 0.0162)\times 0.25]/0.09 = (0.025318 + 0.011550)/0.09 = 0.4096$ and $d_2 = 0.3196$.
step: $N(-d_2) = N(-0.3196) = 0.3746$ and $N(-d_1) = N(-0.4096) = 0.3411$.
step: $p = 1950\,e^{-0.0125} \times 0.3746 - 2000\,e^{-0.005} \times 0.3411 = 1925.8 \times 0.3746 - 1990.0 \times 0.3411 = \$42.77$.
answer: About 42.77 index points (\$42.77 with a \$1 multiplier).

@mistakes
- Using the percent volatility as a whole number (25 instead of 0.25), or a monthly volatility with $T$ in years.
- Forgetting $\sigma^2/2$ in $d_1$, or using $r$ instead of $r - q$ for an index.
- Computing $N(d_2)$ when the formula needs $N(-d_2)$ for the put, or the reverse.
- Using the real-world expected return of the stock anywhere in the formula: it does not appear.

model:
```python
import math


def _norm_cdf(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))


def bsm_price(S, K, T, r, sigma, q=0.0, kind="call"):
    """Black-Scholes-Merton price of a European call or put."""
    sd = sigma * math.sqrt(T)
    d1 = (math.log(S / K) + (r - q + sigma ** 2 / 2) * T) / sd
    d2 = d1 - sd
    if kind == "call":
        return S * math.exp(-q * T) * _norm_cdf(d1) - K * math.exp(-r * T) * _norm_cdf(d2)
    return K * math.exp(-r * T) * _norm_cdf(-d2) - S * math.exp(-q * T) * _norm_cdf(-d1)
```
hint: Compute `d1` and `d2` first, then combine the cumulative normal values.
hint: For a put use `-d2` and `-d1` inside the cumulative normal, and swap the signs of the two terms.
solution: Implement $N(x)$ with `math.erf`, compute $d_1$ and $d_2$ with the dividend-adjusted drift $r - q$, then apply the call or put formula. The last test uses put-call parity as an independent check that does not depend on the first formulas being right.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all statements that are true in the Black-Scholes-Merton model for a European option on a stock.
choice*: The stock's expected return does not appear in the pricing formula.
choice*: A higher volatility increases the price of both a call and a put with the same strike.
choice*: A call's price satisfies put-call parity with the put on the same stock, strike and expiration.
choice: The model assumes that volatility changes with the stock price level, producing a skew.
choice: $N(d_1)$ is the risk-neutral probability that a call finishes in the money.
hint: Recall how the model is built from replication, and what $N(d_2)$ versus $N(d_1)$ mean.
solution: The model prices by replication under risk-neutral valuation, so the expected return of the stock is irrelevant. Volatility raises both calls and puts because the payoffs are convex. Parity is a no-arbitrage identity, which the model respects. BSM assumes constant volatility (observed skews contradict it), and the in-the-money probability is $N(d_2)$, while $N(d_1)$ is the call's delta.
