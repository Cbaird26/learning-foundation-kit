@article
A bakery worries that the price of wheat will jump before it buys next season's flour. A farmer worries it will fall before harvest. Both can agree today on a price for a future trade, and each is then protected from the risk the other fears. That agreement is a **derivative**: a contract whose value depends on (is *derived* from) the value of an **underlying** asset, rate or index, such as a commodity price, a stock, an interest rate, an exchange rate or a stock index.

### The four building blocks

- A **forward contract** is an agreement to buy or sell an underlying at a fixed **delivery price** on a future date. The **long** agrees to buy; the **short** agrees to sell. Nothing is paid up front, and both parties are obligated.
- A **futures contract** is a standardized forward traded on an exchange. It is **marked to market** daily and guaranteed by a clearinghouse (Week 3).
- An **option** gives the holder the *right but not the obligation* to trade. A **call** is the right to buy at the **strike price** $K$; a **put** is the right to sell at $K$. The buyer pays a **premium** to the **writer** (seller), who takes the obligation. A **European** option can be exercised only at expiration; an **American** option at any time up to expiration.
- A **swap** is an agreement to exchange a series of cash flows, for example fixed-rate interest for floating-rate interest (Week 4).

Forwards, futures and swaps are **forward commitments**: both sides must perform. Options are **contingent claims**: only the writer is obligated.

### Payoffs at expiration

Let $S_T$ be the underlying price at expiration $T$ and $F_0$ the forward's delivery price. Payoff per unit:

| Position | Payoff at $T$ | Profit |
|---|---|---|
| Long forward | $S_T - F_0$ | same (no premium) |
| Short forward | $F_0 - S_T$ | same |
| Long call | $\max(S_T - K,\,0)$ | payoff $-$ premium |
| Short call | $-\max(S_T - K,\,0)$ | premium $-$ payoff |
| Long put | $\max(K - S_T,\,0)$ | payoff $-$ premium |
| Short put | $-\max(K - S_T,\,0)$ | premium $-$ payoff |

A forward's payoff is linear and can be positive or negative for both sides. An option buyer's loss is capped at the premium, while the writer's gain is capped at the premium. Derivatives are a **zero-sum game** before costs: the long's gain is the short's loss.

### Where derivatives trade

**Exchange-traded** derivatives (futures and many options) have standardized terms, a transparent price, and a **central counterparty** (clearinghouse) that stands between buyer and seller, so each party's credit risk is to the clearinghouse. **Over-the-counter (OTC)** derivatives (most forwards and swaps) are negotiated privately and can be customized, but each side bears **counterparty credit risk**. After the 2007–2009 financial crisis, laws such as the Dodd-Frank Act in the United States (and similar rules in Europe) required many standardized swaps to be cleared through central counterparties and reported to trade repositories; the CFTC regulates futures and most swaps, and the SEC regulates securities-based swaps. US dollar interest-rate swaps now reference **SOFR** (the Secured Overnight Financing Rate), which replaced USD LIBOR after the LIBOR panel ended in 2023.

### Uses, and leverage

**Hedgers** reduce an existing risk; **speculators** take on risk hoping to profit from a view; **arbitrageurs** exploit price differences between related assets. Derivatives are **leveraged**: the **notional value** (the underlying quantity times its price) is far larger than the cash needed to hold the position. A futures contract with notional \$250,000 might need only \$12,000 in margin, so a 1% move in the underlying changes the margin account by about 21%. Leverage magnifies gains and losses alike, and notional value is *not* the amount at risk.

@example
title: A bakery's wheat forward
problem: Brindle Bakeries agrees to buy 10,000 bushels of wheat in three months at a forward price of \$6.20 per bushel. What does the bakery gain or lose if the spot price at delivery is \$7.10? If it is \$5.40?
step: The bakery is the long, so its payoff per bushel is $S_T - F_0$.
step: At $S_T = 7.10$: payoff $= (7.10 - 6.20) \times 10{,}000 = \$9{,}000$. The bakery pays \$6.20 for wheat worth \$7.10.
step: At $S_T = 5.40$: payoff $= (5.40 - 6.20) \times 10{,}000 = -\$8{,}000$. The bakery pays \$6.20 for wheat worth \$5.40.
step: In both cases the bakery's cost of wheat is fixed at \$62,000 once the forward gain or loss is added to what it pays in the spot market. The seller's payoff is the exact opposite.
answer: Gain of \$9,000 at \$7.10; loss of \$8,000 at \$5.40.

@example
title: Leverage in a futures position
problem: A stock-index futures contract has a multiplier of \$50 per index point. The index stands at 4,800 and the initial margin is \$12,000 per contract. What is the contract's notional value, and what is the return on margin if the index rises 1%?
step: Notional value $= 50 \times 4{,}800 = \$240{,}000$.
step: A 1% rise is $0.01 \times 4{,}800 = 48$ points, so the long gains $48 \times 50 = \$2{,}400$.
step: Return on margin $= 2{,}400 / 12{,}000 = 20\%$.
step: A 1% fall loses the same \$2,400, which is 20% of the margin. The leverage ratio is $240{,}000 / 12{,}000 = 20$.
answer: Notional \$240,000; a 1% rise gives a 20% return on margin.

@mistakes
- Treating the notional value as the amount at risk or the amount paid: a forward costs nothing at initiation, and a futures contract requires only margin.
- Forgetting the premium when computing an option's profit (payoff and profit differ), or subtracting it for the writer instead of adding it.
- Thinking an option holder is obligated to exercise: only the writer has an obligation; the holder has a choice.
- Mixing up the long and short sides: the long forward gains when the underlying rises; the long put gains when it falls.

prompt: One equity call option contract covers 100 shares. You buy one contract on Marlow Industries with a strike of \$50 for a premium of \$3.20 per share. At expiration the stock is at \$58. What is your profit on the contract, in dollars, after the premium?
answer: 480
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (max(58 - 50, 0) - 3.20) * 100
hint: First find the payoff per share, then subtract the premium paid per share.
solution: Payoff per share $= \max(58 - 50, 0) = 8$. Profit per share $= 8 - 3.20 = 4.80$, so profit per contract $= 4.80 \times 100 = \$480$.

@quiz num
prompt: You write (sell) one put option contract on 100 shares of a stock, with a strike of \$40, and receive a premium of \$2.50 per share. At expiration the stock is at \$34. What is your profit or loss on the contract, in dollars? Enter a loss as a negative number.
answer: -350
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (2.50 - max(40 - 34, 0)) * 100
hint: The writer collects the premium and pays out the put's payoff.
solution: The put's payoff to the holder is $\max(40 - 34, 0) = 6$ per share, which the writer pays. Profit per share $= 2.50 - 6 = -3.50$, so the loss on the contract is $-3.50 \times 100 = -\$350$.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says, "A derivative with a notional value of \$10 million means we could lose \$10 million." In a few sentences, explain why notional value overstates, and in some cases understates, the amount at risk.
hint: Compare the notional value with the payoff formula of the contract.
solution: Notional value is the size of the underlying exposure used to scale payments, not the amount that changes hands. A forward or swap exchanges only the difference between the agreed price or rate and the market, so the loss is the adverse change in value, which is usually a fraction of the notional. A bought option can lose at most its premium. However, a leveraged position can lose more than the margin posted, and a written call has no upper limit on its loss, so notional can also understate risk when the underlying moves enormously.
rubric: States that payments depend on the change in the underlying relative to the contract terms, so the loss is a fraction of the notional.
rubric: Notes that a long option's loss is capped at the premium.
rubric: Notes that leverage or an uncovered written option can produce losses larger than the margin or premium, so risk is not simply bounded by the notional.
