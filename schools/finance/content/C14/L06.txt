@article
Options are priced by models in later weeks, but some relationships hold regardless of any model, because their violation would allow arbitrage. They are your first tool for checking whether a quoted price is even plausible.

**Conventions in this lesson.** Options are European unless marked American. The risk-free rate $r$ is continuously compounded and constant; there are no transaction costs; $S_0$ is the current stock price, $K$ the strike, $T$ the time to expiration in years, $c$ and $p$ the European call and put prices on the same stock with the same $K$ and $T$.

### Put-call parity

Compare two portfolios, **A** (a *fiduciary call*): one European call plus a zero-coupon bond paying $K$ at $T$, costing $c + Ke^{-rT}$; and **B** (a *protective put*): one European put plus one share, costing $p + S_0$. At expiration:

| | $S_T \le K$ | $S_T > K$ |
|---|---|---|
| A: call + bond | $0 + K = K$ | $(S_T - K) + K = S_T$ |
| B: put + share | $(K - S_T) + S_T = K$ | $0 + S_T = S_T$ |

Both are worth $\max(S_T, K)$, so their prices today must match:

$$c + K e^{-rT} = p + S_0.$$

Rearranged: $p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$ and $c = p + S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$. Parity also shows that a long call, short put and a bond are a **synthetic** long position in the stock: $c - p = S_0 - Ke^{-rT}$.

**With dividends.** If the stock pays known dividends with present value $D$ before expiration, the stock portfolio gives up the dividends, so $c + D + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$. With a continuous dividend yield $q$: $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-qT}$.

**Arbitrage when parity fails.** If $c + Ke^{-rT} > p + S_0$, portfolio A is too expensive: sell the call, buy the put, buy the stock, and borrow or invest so the net cash flow at $T$ is zero. The riskless profit today is the amount of the violation. If the inequality goes the other way, reverse every position.

### Bounds on option prices

Using no-arbitrage arguments (and $c\ge 0$, $p\ge 0$), for a non-dividend stock:

| | Lower bound | Upper bound |
|---|---|---|
| European call | $\max(S_0 - Ke^{-rT},\,0)$ | $S_0$ |
| European put | $\max(Ke^{-rT} - S_0,\,0)$ | $Ke^{-rT}$ |
| American call | $\max(S_0 - K,\,0)$ | $S_0$ |
| American put | $\max(K - S_0,\,0)$ | $K$ |

An American option is worth at least as much as the same European option, and at least its intrinsic value, since it can be exercised immediately. The European call's lower bound is tighter than its intrinsic value because the strike is paid later, at $T$. For a put the opposite holds: $Ke^{-rT}-S_0$ lies below $K-S_0$, which is why an American put can be worth more than a European put.

### Early exercise

**Calls on a non-dividend stock.** Since $c \ge S_0 - Ke^{-rT} > S_0 - K$ whenever $r>0$, the European call (and hence the American call) is worth more than its intrinsic value. Selling the option is better than exercising, so an American call on a non-dividend stock is never exercised early and has the same value as a European call. With dividends, exercising just before a large ex-dividend date can be optimal.

**Puts.** A deep in-the-money put may be worth exercising early: the strike received now earns interest, and the gain is capped (a stock cannot fall below zero). When the European lower bound $Ke^{-rT} - S_0$ is less than the intrinsic value $K - S_0$, the American put can be worth more than its European counterpart. Parity holds exactly only for European options; for American options it becomes an inequality.

@example
title: Finding a put price from parity
problem: A stock trades at \$45 and pays no dividends. A 3-month (0.25-year) European call with strike \$45 trades at \$2.30. The continuously compounded rate is 4%. What is the no-arbitrage price of the European put with the same strike and expiration?
step: Parity: $p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$.
step: $Ke^{-rT} = 45\,e^{-0.04\times0.25} = 45 \times 0.990050 = \$44.5522$.
step: $p = 2.30 + 44.5522 - 45 = \$1.8522$, about \$1.85.
step: Any other price lets a trader lock in a profit with the call, put, stock and bond.
answer: About \$1.85.

@example
title: Why an American put may be exercised early
problem: A stock is at \$20, a put has strike \$50 and one year to expiration, and the rate is 5%. Compare the European put's lower bound with the intrinsic value.
step: Intrinsic value $= 50 - 20 = \$30$.
step: European lower bound $= 50\,e^{-0.05} - 20 = 47.5615 - 20 = \$27.56$.
step: A European put may trade as low as about \$27.56 (below intrinsic value), but an American put is worth at least \$30, since it can be exercised for \$30 immediately.
step: The holder who exercises receives \$50 now and can earn interest on it; waiting can add little because the stock cannot fall by more than \$20. So early exercise is plausible.
answer: The European bound (\$27.56) is below intrinsic value (\$30), so an American put can be worth more than its European twin and may be exercised early.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to discount the strike: parity uses $Ke^{-rT}$, not $K$.
- Applying parity to American options as an equality; it holds exactly only for European options.
- Subtracting dividends at their future value, or forgetting dividends altogether when the stock pays them before expiration.
- Believing that early exercise of a call on a non-dividend stock can be optimal; the option's time value (including the interest on the strike) is lost.

model:
```python
import math


def parity_gap(call, put, spot, strike, r, T, q=0.0):
    """Call + PV(strike) minus put + discounted spot (zero if parity holds)."""
    return call + strike * math.exp(-r * T) - put - spot * math.exp(-q * T)
```
hint: Discount the strike at `r` and the spot price at `q`.
hint: Combine the four terms with the signs given in the prompt.
solution: Add the call and the discounted strike, then subtract the put and the dividend-discounted spot. With `q=0` and `r=0` the gap is just `call - put - spot + strike`, which the third test checks.
