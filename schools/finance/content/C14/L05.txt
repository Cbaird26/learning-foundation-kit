@article
An option's payoff at expiration is a *kinked* line: flat on one side of the strike and sloping on the other. Combining options with each other and with the underlying stock lets you bend that line into almost any shape. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the arithmetic for the most common shapes. All options here are European and held to expiration; prices are per share, and one US equity option contract covers 100 shares. Premiums are paid when the position is opened, and the time value of the premium is ignored when computing profit.

### Single options

For a call with strike $K$, premium $c$, and underlying price $S_T$ at expiration:

- Long call: payoff $\max(S_T-K,0)$, profit $\max(S_T-K,0)-c$, breakeven $S_T = K + c$.
- Short call: profit $c-\max(S_T-K,0)$, with unlimited loss as $S_T$ rises.

For a put with premium $p$:

- Long put: payoff $\max(K-S_T,0)$, profit $\max(K-S_T,0)-p$, breakeven $S_T = K - p$.
- Short put: profit $p-\max(K-S_T,0)$; the largest loss is $K-p$ (when $S_T=0$).

A call is **in the money** if $S>K$, **at the money** if $S\approx K$ and **out of the money** if $S<K$ (reverse for a put). The **intrinsic value** is the payoff if exercised now, $\max(S-K,0)$ for a call; the rest of the premium is **time value**.

### Strategies

Strategies are sums of their legs' profits, so work leg by leg and add.

| Strategy | Construction | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Protective put | Long stock + long put | Loss floored at $S_0 - K + p$; upside kept, less $p$ |
| Covered call | Long stock + short call | Upside capped at $K$; premium cushions small falls |
| Bull call spread | Long call at $K_1$ + short call at $K_2>K_1$ | Profit rises from $-(c_1-c_2)$ to $(K_2-K_1)-(c_1-c_2)$ |
| Long straddle | Long call and long put, same $K$ | Profits from a large move either way; loss at most $c+p$ |
| Long butterfly | Long $K_1$ call, short two $K_2$ calls, long $K_3$ call ($K_2$ midway) | Peak profit at $K_2$; loss limited to the net premium |
| Collar | Long stock + long put at $K_p$ + short call at $K_c>K_p$ | Loss floored and profit capped; often little or no net premium |

**Reading a payoff diagram in words.** Describe it by its flat parts and its slopes. A long straddle at $K=100$ with total premium \$10 is worth \$0 at $S_T=100$ (loss \$10), and slopes up with slope $+1$ to the right of 100 and slope $-1$ to the left, crossing zero profit at 90 and 110.

**Why trade spreads?** A bull spread costs less than a single call, in exchange for capping the gain. A straddle is a bet on volatility rather than direction. A butterfly is a low-cost bet that the price will end near the middle strike. A collar can protect a holding at little cost, giving up the upside above the call's strike. Strategy profits do not account for early exercise, dividends, commissions or taxes.

@example
title: A bull call spread
problem: A trader buys a call with strike 50 for \$6.00 and sells a call with strike 60 for \$2.00, same expiration. Find the maximum profit, maximum loss and breakeven, and the profit at $S_T = 70$ and $S_T = 54$.
step: Net premium paid $= 6.00 - 2.00 = \$4.00$.
step: Maximum loss (for $S_T \le 50$) $= \$4.00$. Maximum profit (for $S_T \ge 60$) $= (60-50) - 4.00 = \$6.00$.
step: Breakeven: the profit in the sloping region is $(S_T - 50) - 4$, which is zero at $S_T = 54$.
step: At $S_T=70$ the spread's profit is $(20-6) - (10-2) = 14 - 8 = \$6.00$. At $S_T = 54$: $(4-6)-(0-2) = 0$, the breakeven.
answer: Maximum profit \$6.00, maximum loss \$4.00, breakeven 54; profit \$6.00 at 70 and \$0 at 54.

@example
title: A long straddle
problem: A trader buys a call (strike 100, premium \$5.50) and a put (strike 100, premium \$4.50). Find the breakevens and the profit if $S_T = 120$.
step: Total cost $= 5.50 + 4.50 = \$10.00$.
step: For $S_T > 100$, profit is $S_T - 100 - 10$, zero at $S_T = 110$. For $S_T < 100$, profit is $100 - S_T - 10$, zero at $S_T = 90$.
step: At $S_T = 120$: call payoff 20, put payoff 0, so profit $= 20 - 10 = \$10.00$.
step: The most the trader can lose is \$10.00, at $S_T = 100$.
answer: Breakevens 90 and 110; profit \$10.00 at 120.

@mistakes
- Confusing payoff with profit: forgetting to subtract the premium paid (or to add the premium received) when finding profit and breakevens.
- Putting the call's breakeven below the strike: a long call breaks even at $K + c$, a long put at $K - p$.
- Applying the wrong sign to a short leg when summing a spread or butterfly: a short option's profit is the premium minus the payoff.
- Treating a straddle as a bet on direction: it loses if the price ends near the strike, whichever way it moves.

model:
```python
def strategy_profit(legs, final_price):
    """Total profit per share of a list of option legs at expiration."""
    total = 0.0
    for kind, strike, premium, quantity in legs:
        if kind == "call":
            payoff = max(final_price - strike, 0.0)
        else:
            payoff = max(strike - final_price, 0.0)
        total += quantity * (payoff - premium)
    return total
```
hint: Compute the payoff of each leg from its `kind`, then subtract the premium and multiply by `quantity`.
hint: A short leg has a negative `quantity`, so the same formula gives the premium minus the payoff.
solution: Loop over the legs, compute the call or put payoff, subtract the premium paid per share, and scale by the signed quantity. Using signed quantities means long and short legs, and multiples such as `-2`, need no special cases. The butterfly test checks that the middle leg counts twice.

@quiz num
prompt: You own a stock bought at \$40 and buy a put with a strike of \$38 for \$1.20 per share (a protective put). What is the maximum loss per share on the combined position at expiration? Give it in dollars to two decimal places as a positive number.
answer: 3.20
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (40 - 38) + 1.20
hint: Below the strike, each dollar the stock falls is exactly offset by the put.
solution: For $S_T \le 38$ the position's profit is $(S_T - 40) + (38 - S_T) - 1.20 = -2 - 1.20 = -\$3.20$. The maximum loss is \$3.20 per share however far the stock falls.
