@article
**Exotic options** are options whose payoffs are more complicated than the plain call or put. Many are **path-dependent**: the payoff depends on the whole price path, not only on the final price.

### Common exotics

| Option | Payoff at expiration $T$ | Path-dependent? |
|---|---|---|
| Cash-or-nothing digital call | $Q$ if $S_T > K$, else 0 | No |
| Asset-or-nothing digital call | $S_T$ if $S_T > K$, else 0 | No |
| Barrier (knock-out / knock-in) | Vanilla payoff, but only if the barrier was never (or was) touched | Yes |
| Asian (arithmetic average price call) | $\max(\bar S - K, 0)$, $\bar S$ = average of the monitored prices | Yes |
| Floating-strike lookback call | $S_T - \min_t S_t$ | Yes |

A **digital** is priced by the BSM building blocks: the cash-or-nothing call is worth $Qe^{-rT}N(d_2)$ and the asset-or-nothing call is worth $S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1)$ (a vanilla call is their difference with $Q = K$). **Barrier** options are cheaper than vanilla options because they can be cancelled (knock-out) or may never start (knock-in). With the same strike, barrier and expiration (and no rebate), **in-out parity** holds: a knock-in plus a knock-out equals the vanilla option, because on every path exactly one of the two is alive. **Asian** options have lower volatility than the final price (an average moves less), so an arithmetic Asian call is cheaper than the vanilla call on the same underlying and strike. Most of these have no simple closed form, so we simulate.

### Monte Carlo under risk-neutral valuation

Option prices are expected discounted payoffs under the risk-neutral measure, where the stock follows geometric Brownian motion with drift $r - q$. For a payoff that depends only on $S_T$, simulate the exact terminal price
$$S_T = S_0\exp\!\left[\left(r - q - \tfrac12\sigma^2\right)T + \sigma\sqrt{T}\,Z\right],\qquad Z\sim N(0,1).$$
The key steps:

1. Draw $N$ independent standard normals $Z_1,\dots,Z_N$.
2. Compute the payoff $X_i$ for each simulated price.
3. Estimate the price: $\hat V = e^{-rT}\,\bar X$.

For a path-dependent payoff, simulate the path in steps of $\Delta t = T/m$: $S_{t+\Delta t} = S_t\exp[(r - q - \sigma^2/2)\Delta t + \sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}\,Z]$, one new $Z$ for each step, then compute the payoff from the monitored prices.

**Accuracy.** The estimate has **standard error** $\text{SE} = e^{-rT}\,s_X/\sqrt{N}$, where $s_X$ is the sample standard deviation of the payoffs. An approximate 95% confidence interval is $\hat V \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$. The error falls only like $1/\sqrt{N}$: to halve it you need four times as many paths. Use the drift $r - q$, never the stock's real-world expected return, because pricing is risk-neutral.

**Variance reduction.** *Antithetic variates* use both $Z$ and $-Z$ for each draw, which cancels some of the noise because the two payoffs are negatively correlated for monotone payoffs. A *control variate* subtracts a correlated quantity with a known expected value (for an Asian option, the vanilla option or a geometric-average Asian option, whose prices are known) to reduce the variance. **Limits:** Monte Carlo handles path-dependence easily, but American early exercise needs additional methods (such as least-squares regression), and the answer is random: a different set of draws gives a slightly different price.

@example
title: A small Monte Carlo estimate
problem: Price a European call with $S_0 = 100$, $K = 105$, $r = 5\%$, $\sigma = 20\%$, $T = 1$ (no dividends) using five standard normal draws: $-1.2, -0.4, 0.3, 0.9, 1.5$. Give the estimate and its standard error.
step: The terminal price is $100\exp[(0.05 - 0.02) + 0.2Z] = 100\,e^{0.03 + 0.2Z}$: for the five draws, 81.06, 95.12, 109.42, 123.37 and 139.10.
step: Payoffs $\max(S_T - 105, 0)$: 0, 0, 4.417, 18.368 and 34.097. The average is 11.376.
step: Discount: $\hat V = e^{-0.05} \times 11.376 = \$10.82$.
step: The sample standard deviation of the payoffs is 14.768, so $\text{SE} = e^{-0.05}\times 14.768/\sqrt5 = \$6.28$. With five draws the standard error is huge: the BSM price is \$8.02, within one standard error of the estimate.
answer: Estimate \$10.82 with standard error \$6.28 (the exact BSM value is \$8.02).

@example
title: Path dependence
problem: A stock is at \$100 and its prices at the end of months 1 to 6 are 104, 98, 107, 110, 112 and 115. Find the payoffs of a European call with strike \$100, an arithmetic Asian call with strike \$100 averaging the six monthly prices, an up-and-out call with strike \$100 and barrier \$120 (monitored at the six dates), and a floating-strike lookback call (minimum over the six monthly prices).
step: European call: $\max(115 - 100, 0) = \$15$.
step: Asian call: the average price is $(104+98+107+110+112+115)/6 = 107.67$, so the payoff is $\$7.67$.
step: Up-and-out call: no monitored price reached 120, so the option is alive and pays the vanilla payoff, \$15.
step: Lookback call: $S_T - \min = 115 - 98 = \$17$.
answer: European \$15; Asian \$7.67; up-and-out \$15; lookback \$17.

@mistakes
- Simulating with the stock's real-world expected return instead of the risk-free rate (minus any dividend yield).
- Leaving out the $-\sigma^2/2$ term in the exponent of $S_T$, which biases the simulated mean price upward.
- Forgetting to discount the average payoff, or discounting each payoff at the wrong time.
- Thinking that 10 times more paths gives 10 times more accuracy: the standard error falls with $\sqrt{N}$.

model:
```python
import math


def mc_price(S0, K, r, sigma, T, draws, kind="call", antithetic=False):
    """Discounted average payoff over given standard normal draws."""
    zs = list(draws)
    if antithetic:
        zs = zs + [-z for z in draws]
    total = 0.0
    for z in zs:
        s_t = S0 * math.exp((r - sigma ** 2 / 2) * T + sigma * math.sqrt(T) * z)
        if kind == "call":
            total += max(s_t - K, 0.0)
        else:
            total += max(K - s_t, 0.0)
    return math.exp(-r * T) * total / len(zs)
```
hint: Build the list of draws first (adding the negatives when `antithetic` is true), then average the payoffs over that whole list.
hint: Divide by the total number of payoffs, which doubles when `antithetic` is true.
solution: Compute the terminal price for every draw, take the call or put payoff, average over all payoffs (twice as many with antithetic draws), and discount once at the end. The antithetic test uses a single draw: the pair $z = 0.8$ and $-0.8$ gives the average of 8.4790 and 0.0, which is 4.2395.
