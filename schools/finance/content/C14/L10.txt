@article
Volatility $\sigma$ is the one BSM input you cannot read from a screen. There are two ways to get it: look backward at how the price has moved (**historical** or **realized** volatility), or look at what option prices say about the future (**implied** volatility).

### Historical volatility

From prices $P_0, P_1, \dots, P_n$ observed at equal intervals, compute continuously compounded (log) returns
$$R_i = \ln\frac{P_i}{P_{i-1}},\qquad i = 1,\dots,n.$$
The sample standard deviation of the returns, $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (R_i - \bar R)^2}$, is the volatility per period. Volatility scales with the square root of time (returns are assumed independent), so **annualized volatility** is $\sigma = s\sqrt{m}$, where $m$ is the number of periods per year: about 252 for daily data (trading days), 52 for weekly and 12 for monthly. Historical volatility describes the past; the sample period matters, and volatility itself changes over time.

### Implied volatility

The BSM price is an increasing function of $\sigma$ (vega is positive). Given an observed option price, the **implied volatility** is the unique $\sigma$ at which the model price equals the market price. There is no closed-form inverse, so solve numerically:

- **Bisection:** bracket $\sigma$ between a low and a high value and repeatedly halve the interval, keeping the half in which the model price crosses the market price. It always converges for a price within the no-arbitrage bounds.
- **Newton's method:** update $\sigma \leftarrow \sigma - (c(\sigma) - c_{\text{mkt}})/\text{vega}(\sigma)$, which converges fast near the solution but can fail when vega is tiny (deep in or out of the money).

An implied volatility is a convenient way to quote prices: it translates a price into a model-based measure of the market's expected volatility, which can be compared across strikes and maturities. A price at or below the lower no-arbitrage bound, or at or above the upper bound, has no implied volatility. The Cboe Volatility Index (VIX) is a model-free measure of the 30-day implied volatility of S&P 500 index options.

### Smiles and skews

If BSM held, options on the same underlying and maturity with different strikes would share one implied volatility. In practice they do not. Plotting implied volatility against strike gives a **smile** (higher at both ends) for currency options, or a **skew** (downward-sloping: higher implied volatility for low strikes) for equity indexes, a pattern that became pronounced after the 1987 crash. The skew means out-of-the-money puts cost more than constant-volatility BSM predicts, consistent with demand for crash protection and with return distributions that have fatter left tails than the lognormal. Implied volatility also varies with maturity (the **term structure**). These patterns show that volatility is not constant and the lognormal assumption is imperfect, which motivates local-volatility and stochastic-volatility models.

### Realized versus implied: the hedging connection

A trader who sells a call at implied volatility $\sigma_{\text{imp}}$ and delta-hedges it earns, roughly, the premium minus the cost of hedging. If realized volatility turns out lower than $\sigma_{\text{imp}}$, the trader tends to profit; if higher, to lose. A delta-hedged *long* option makes money when realized volatility exceeds the volatility paid for it. Hedging at discrete intervals adds noise: the profit on any single path is random, and it concentrates around its expected value only as rebalancing becomes more frequent.

**Simulating a hedge.** A hedge simulation sells one call at the BSM price (volatility $\sigma$), buys $\Delta_0 = N(d_1)$ shares with the proceeds plus borrowing, and at each later date sets the share holding to the new delta, financing the trades from a cash account that earns $r$. At expiration it pays the option's payoff and sells the shares; what remains is the profit.

@example
title: Historical volatility from daily prices
problem: Daily closing prices of a stock are 100.00, 101.50, 100.80, 102.30, 103.00, 101.90. Find the annualized historical volatility using 252 trading days.
step: Log returns: $\ln(101.5/100) = 0.01489$; $\ln(100.8/101.5) = -0.00692$; $\ln(102.3/100.8) = 0.01477$; $\ln(103/102.3) = 0.00682$; $\ln(101.9/103) = -0.01074$.
step: Mean return $\bar R = 0.003764$. The squared deviations sum to $0.000579$, so the sample variance is $0.000579/4 = 0.0001447$ and $s = 0.012028$ (1.2028% a day).
step: Annualize: $\sigma = 0.012028 \times \sqrt{252} = 0.012028 \times 15.8745 = 0.1909$.
answer: About 19.09% a year.

@example
title: Backing out an implied volatility
problem: A 6-month European call with strike \$100 on a non-dividend stock at \$100 costs \$8.00. The rate is 5% (continuously compounded). Find the implied volatility.
step: Try $\sigma = 0.25$: the BSM price is \$8.26, too high. Try $\sigma = 0.24$: the price is \$7.985, slightly too low. So $\sigma$ lies between 24% and 25%.
step: Interpolate (or run Newton's method, with vega about 27.5): $\sigma \approx 0.24 + 0.01 \times (8.00 - 7.985)/(8.26 - 7.985) = 0.2405$.
step: Refine by bisection: the solution is $\sigma = 24.05\%$. Check: BSM at 0.2405 gives \$8.00.
answer: About 24.05%.

@mistakes
- Annualizing with the wrong factor: daily volatility needs $\sqrt{252}$ (trading days), not 252, and weekly volatility needs $\sqrt{52}$.
- Computing volatility from simple price changes rather than log returns, or using the population divisor $n$ when the problem asks for a sample.
- Expecting a single implied volatility for all strikes: smiles and skews are the norm.
- Reading implied volatility as a forecast that is guaranteed to be realized; it is a model-dependent market price and includes a risk premium.

model:
```python
import math


def _cdf(x):
    return 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))


def delta_hedge_pnl(prices, K, r, sigma, T):
    """Final profit of a discretely delta-hedged short call."""
    n = len(prices) - 1
    dt = T / n

    def d1(S, tau):
        drift = (r + sigma ** 2 / 2) * tau
        return (math.log(S / K) + drift) / (sigma * math.sqrt(tau))

    S0 = prices[0]
    x1 = d1(S0, T)
    x2 = x1 - sigma * math.sqrt(T)
    call = S0 * _cdf(x1) - K * math.exp(-r * T) * _cdf(x2)
    shares = _cdf(x1)
    cash = call - shares * S0
    for i in range(1, n + 1):
        cash *= math.exp(r * dt)
        if i < n:
            new_shares = _cdf(d1(prices[i], T - i * dt))
            cash -= (new_shares - shares) * prices[i]
            shares = new_shares
    return cash + shares * prices[n] - max(prices[n] - K, 0.0)
```
hint: Track two things: the share holding and the cash balance. Start with cash equal to the call premium minus the cost of the initial shares.
hint: In each step first grow the cash by `exp(r * dt)`, then (if it is not the last date) rebalance: buying shares reduces cash by the number bought times the price.
solution: At every date accrue interest on cash, then trade shares to the new delta and pay for them from cash. At expiration settle the option and sell the shares at the final price. The profit is small relative to the premium: it is the hedging error from rebalancing only at discrete dates, plus the effect of realized volatility differing from `sigma`. The first test has a single step, so the position is never rebalanced.

model:
```python
import math


def _bsm(S, K, T, r, sigma, kind):
    sd = sigma * math.sqrt(T)
    d1 = (math.log(S / K) + (r + sigma ** 2 / 2) * T) / sd
    d2 = d1 - sd
    cdf = lambda x: 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(x / math.sqrt(2)))
    if kind == "call":
        return S * cdf(d1) - K * math.exp(-r * T) * cdf(d2)
    return K * math.exp(-r * T) * cdf(-d2) - S * cdf(-d1)


def implied_vol(price, S, K, T, r, kind="call"):
    """Implied volatility by bisection; ValueError outside the bounds."""
    pv_k = K * math.exp(-r * T)
    if kind == "call":
        lower, upper = max(S - pv_k, 0.0), S
    else:
        lower, upper = max(pv_k - S, 0.0), pv_k
    if not lower < price < upper:
        raise ValueError("price outside no-arbitrage bounds")
    lo, hi = 1e-6, 5.0
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if _bsm(S, K, T, r, mid, kind) > price:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2
```
hint: The model price increases with volatility, so if the price at `mid` is above the market price, the answer lies below `mid`.
hint: Check the bounds first, before the loop.
solution: Reject prices that are outside the no-arbitrage bounds, since no volatility can produce them. Then bisect: because the BSM price rises with volatility, a model price above the market price means the implied volatility is in the lower half, and otherwise in the upper half. After 100 halvings the interval is far narrower than the test tolerance.
