@article
Derivatives are tools, and a tool does the job it is used for. A firm that uses them to remove a risk it does not want to bear is **hedging**; a firm that uses them to take on new risk is **speculating**. The same contract can be either, depending on the exposure behind it.

### The risk-management process

1. **Identify and measure the exposure.** What price, rate or exchange rate moves the firm's cash flows or value, by how much, and when? (For example, a bakery buying 10,000 bushels of wheat in three months, or a US exporter receiving euros in six months.)
2. **Set the objective.** Fix a price completely, or only set a floor, or reduce the variance? How much of the exposure to hedge?
3. **Choose the instrument and hedge ratio** (below), considering cost, basis risk, liquidity and accounting treatment.
4. **Monitor and evaluate.** Compare hedged and unhedged results across scenarios, rebalance, and review counterparty exposure and cash needs for margin.

### Which instrument?

| Instrument | What it does | What it gives up |
|---|---|---|
| Forward or future | Fixes the price (a linear payoff) | The benefit of a favorable move; futures also create cash flows from margin calls |
| Purchased option (put to protect a seller, call to protect a buyer) | Sets a floor or cap while keeping the upside | The premium, paid up front |
| Collar (long put, short call) | A floor and a cap at little or no net premium | The upside above the call strike |
| Interest-rate swap | Converts floating-rate debt into fixed (or the reverse) | Benefit if rates move favorably; credit exposure to the counterparty |
| Currency swap | Converts debt or cash flows between currencies, with principal exchange | The same, plus the credit exposure on the principal |

**Forward versus option.** A forward is free but locks the rate in both directions; a put costs a premium but the firm keeps the benefit if the market moves in its favor. Neither is "better": the choice depends on whether the firm needs certainty or wants to keep upside, and on the premium.

### Hedging a stock portfolio with index futures

To change a portfolio's beta from $\beta$ to a target $\beta^*$, trade
$$N = \frac{(\beta^* - \beta)\,P}{F\,m},$$
index futures contracts, where $P$ is the portfolio value, $F$ the futures price and $m$ the contract multiplier ($Fm$ is the value of one contract). A negative $N$ means sell. This treats the futures as having a beta of 1 and ignores basis risk and tailing. It lowers market risk but not the risk specific to the stocks held.

### Derivatives can create risk

- **Basis risk:** the hedge instrument does not move one-for-one with the exposure.
- **Liquidity risk:** futures are marked to market, so a hedge that is economically sound can still produce large cash outflows when prices move.
- **Counterparty risk:** an over-the-counter counterparty may fail. Central clearing and collateral reduce it.
- **Model and operational risk:** wrong volatilities or models, weak controls, unauthorized trading. The failure of Barings Bank in 1995, caused by unauthorized futures positions that controls failed to catch, is a classic example of operational failure.
- **Leverage and concentration:** the 2007–2009 financial crisis showed the danger when large sold positions, such as credit default swaps sold by AIG, produced collateral demands that the seller could not meet.

Responses include position limits and Greek limits, independent risk functions, stress tests, and regulation: in the United States the Dodd-Frank Act (2010) brought central clearing of many standardized swaps, trade reporting and margin for uncleared swaps, overseen by the CFTC for most swaps and the SEC for security-based swaps. For accounting, hedge accounting under ASC 815 (US GAAP) and IFRS 9 lets qualifying hedges recognize gains and losses on the hedge and the hedged item in the same period, subject to documentation and effectiveness requirements.

This is general education, not investment advice.

@example
title: Hedging euro receipts three ways
problem: A US exporter will receive 5,000,000 euros in 6 months. The spot rate is 1.10 dollars per euro, dollar interest rates are 4% and euro rates 2% (continuously compounded). A 6-month euro put with strike 1.08 costs 0.02 dollars per euro (ignore the interest on the premium). Compare the dollar receipts if the spot rate in six months is 1.00, 1.10 or 1.20.
step: Forward rate: $F = 1.10\,e^{(0.04-0.02)\times 0.5} = 1.11106$. Selling euros forward locks in $5{,}000{,}000 \times 1.11106 = \$5{,}555{,}276$ whatever the spot rate.
step: No hedge: receipts are $5{,}000{,}000 \times S_T$: \$5,000,000, \$5,500,000 and \$6,000,000.
step: Put hedge: receipts $= 5{,}000{,}000\times\max(S_T, 1.08) - 5{,}000{,}000\times 0.02$. At 1.00: $5{,}400{,}000 - 100{,}000 = \$5{,}300{,}000$; at 1.10: $5{,}500{,}000 - 100{,}000 = \$5{,}400{,}000$; at 1.20: $6{,}000{,}000 - 100{,}000 = \$5{,}900{,}000$.
step: The forward gives the same receipts in every scenario; the put sets a floor of \$5,300,000 and keeps most of the upside, at the cost of the premium; the unhedged position gains or loses the full exchange-rate move.
answer: Forward: \$5,555,276 in all cases. Put: \$5.3M, \$5.4M, \$5.9M. No hedge: \$5.0M, \$5.5M, \$6.0M.

@example
title: Reducing a portfolio's beta with futures
problem: A \$50 million equity portfolio has a beta of 1.2. A manager wants to cut the beta to 0.4 using index futures with a price of 5,000 and a multiplier of \$50. How many contracts should be traded?
step: One contract is worth $5{,}000 \times 50 = \$250{,}000$.
step: $N = (0.4 - 1.2)\times 50{,}000{,}000/250{,}000 = -160$.
step: The manager sells 160 contracts. If the market falls 10%, the portfolio loses about 12% of \$50 million (\$6.0 million), while the short futures gain about $160 \times 250{,}000 \times 10\% = \$4.0$ million, so the net loss is about \$2.0 million, which is 4% of the portfolio: a beta of 0.4.
answer: Sell 160 contracts.

@mistakes
- Treating a derivative position as automatically a hedge: it is a hedge only if it offsets an actual exposure.
- Comparing a forward hedge with an option hedge without counting the option's premium.
- Selling index futures to raise beta (or buying to lower it): the sign of $N = (\beta^*-\beta)P/(Fm)$ gives the direction.
- Ignoring the cash needed for margin calls on futures hedges, which can strain liquidity even when the hedge works economically.

model:
```python
import math


def put_hedged_revenue(final_price, quantity, strike, premium, r=0.0, T=0.0):
    """Revenue of a producer hedged with purchased puts."""
    sale = quantity * final_price
    payoff = quantity * max(strike - final_price, 0.0)
    cost = quantity * premium * math.exp(r * T)
    return sale + payoff - cost
```
hint: Total revenue is the sale at the market price, plus the put's payoff, minus the premium carried forward.
hint: The put's payoff is `max(strike - final_price, 0)` per unit.
solution: The sale gives `quantity * final_price`, the put adds `quantity * max(strike - final_price, 0)`, and the premium, which was paid at the start, is worth `exp(r * T)` times more at expiration. Revenue therefore never falls below `quantity * (strike - premium * exp(r * T))`, which the first test shows.

@quiz self
prompt: A treasurer says, "We bought futures to hedge our fuel costs, but when prices fell sharply we had to pay large margin calls, even though the hedge was doing exactly what it was supposed to do." In a short paragraph, explain why this can happen and name two steps the firm could take to manage it.
hint: Think about marking to market, and about the difference between economic value and cash flow.
solution: Futures are marked to market daily: when fuel prices fall, the long futures position loses value and the loss must be paid in cash through variation margin, immediately. The economic offset, lower fuel purchase costs, arrives only later when the fuel is actually bought, so the hedge creates a timing mismatch and a liquidity (funding) risk even though it is effective economically. The firm can keep committed credit lines or cash reserves to meet margin calls, scale the hedge to what it can fund through stress scenarios, use a forward or swap with a bank (with a negotiated collateral threshold), or use purchased options, which need no margin after the premium is paid.
rubric: Explains that daily marking to market forces cash payments when the hedge loses value, before the offsetting savings on the physical purchase are realized.
rubric: Identifies this as a liquidity or funding risk (a timing mismatch), not a failure of the hedge's economics.
rubric: Names two valid steps, such as liquidity reserves or credit lines, a smaller hedge ratio, forwards with negotiated collateral terms, or options.
