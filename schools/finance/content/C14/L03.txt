@article
A forward is settled once, at delivery. A **futures contract** is settled every day. At the end of each trading day the exchange sets a **settlement price**, and every position is **marked to market**: the day's gain is credited to the long's account and debited from the short's (or the reverse if the price fell). Because gains and losses are paid daily, a trader's loss can never pile up unnoticed, and the clearinghouse that guarantees the contract is exposed to at most one day's price move.

### Margin

To open a position a trader deposits **initial margin**, a performance bond rather than a down payment. Each day the settlement gain or loss moves the **margin account balance**. If the balance falls below the **maintenance margin**, the trader receives a **margin call** and must deposit **variation margin**. In the convention used in this lesson the call restores the balance to the *initial* margin level (not merely to the maintenance level). A trader who does not pay is closed out. Gains above initial margin may be withdrawn.

For a position of $n$ contracts with contract size $Q$, a price change $\Delta F$ changes the account by $n\,Q\,\Delta F$ for a long (the negative of this for a short).

### Futures versus forwards

Futures are standardized (size, grade, delivery months), exchange-traded and marked to market daily, so credit risk is small. Forwards are customized, settled at maturity, and carry counterparty risk. If interest rates are constant and the same for all maturities, futures and forward prices for the same delivery date are equal; when rates are random they can differ slightly. Most futures positions are closed out with an offsetting trade before delivery rather than settled by delivery.

### Hedging and the basis

A **short hedge** (sell futures) protects a producer or an owner of the asset against a price fall. A **long hedge** (buy futures) protects a buyer against a price rise. The **basis** is
$$b_t = S_t - F_t,$$
the spot price minus the futures price. It converges to zero (or close to it) at delivery, but before then it varies. A short hedger who sells at $F_0$ and closes out at $t_2$, when the spot price is $S_2$ and the futures price is $F_2$, realizes
$$S_2 + (F_0 - F_2) = F_0 + b_2.$$
The price is fixed except for the uncertain basis $b_2$: this is **basis risk**. It is larger when the asset hedged differs from the asset underlying the futures (**cross hedging**, such as jet fuel with heating oil futures) or when the hedge ends before delivery.

### The minimum-variance hedge ratio

The **hedge ratio** $h$ is the number of units of futures sold per unit of exposure. Let $\Delta S$ and $\Delta F$ be the changes in spot and futures prices (in the same currency units) over the hedge horizon, with standard deviations $\sigma_S$, $\sigma_F$ and correlation $\rho$. The hedged position changes by $\Delta S - h\,\Delta F$, whose variance is
$$\sigma_S^2 + h^2\sigma_F^2 - 2h\rho\sigma_S\sigma_F.$$
Setting the derivative with respect to $h$ to zero gives
$$h^* = \rho\,\frac{\sigma_S}{\sigma_F} = \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta F)}{\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)},$$
which is the slope of a regression of $\Delta S$ on $\Delta F$. With $Q_A$ units of exposure and futures contracts of size $Q_F$ each, the number of contracts is
$$N^* = h^*\,\frac{Q_A}{Q_F},$$
rounded to a whole number. The fraction of the exposure's variance removed is the regression's $R^2 = \rho^2$, called the **hedge effectiveness**. When percentage changes are used instead of dollar changes the ratio is applied to values (spot value divided by futures value of one contract); in this lesson all changes are in dollars per unit. For long hedges and short hedges alike, $h^*$ is positive when $\rho>0$.

@example
title: A margin account
problem: A trader buys 2 wheat futures contracts, each for 5,000 bushels, at \$6.00. Initial margin is \$3,000 per contract and maintenance margin is \$2,250 per contract. Settlement prices on three days are \$5.90, \$5.75 and \$5.80. Margin calls restore the account to the initial margin. Track the account.
step: Start: balance $= 2 \times 3{,}000 = \$6{,}000$; maintenance level $= 2 \times 2{,}250 = \$4{,}500$. A contract position changes by $2 \times 5{,}000 = 10{,}000$ bushels times the price change.
step: Day 1: price $6.00 \to 5.90$, change $-\$0.10$, so $-\$1{,}000$. Balance $\$5{,}000 \ge 4{,}500$: no call.
step: Day 2: price $5.90 \to 5.75$, change $-\$0.15$, so $-\$1{,}500$. Balance $\$3{,}500 < 4{,}500$: margin call to restore \$6,000, so the trader deposits **\$2,500**.
step: Day 3: price $5.75 \to 5.80$, change $+\$0.05$, so $+\$500$. Balance $= 6{,}000 + 500 = \$6{,}500$. The \$500 above initial margin may be withdrawn.
answer: A margin call of \$2,500 on day 2; the balance after day 3 is \$6,500.

@example
title: Hedge ratio for jet fuel
problem: A charter airline will buy 500,000 gallons of jet fuel in three months. It hedges with heating oil futures (42,000 gallons each). Over three months the standard deviation of the jet fuel price change is \$0.12 per gallon, of the futures price change \$0.15 per gallon, and their correlation is 0.90. Find the hedge ratio, the number of contracts and the hedge effectiveness.
step: $h^* = \rho\,\sigma_S/\sigma_F = 0.90 \times 0.12/0.15 = 0.72$.
step: $N^* = 0.72 \times 500{,}000/42{,}000 = 8.57$, so buy (long hedge) **9 contracts**.
step: Effectiveness $= \rho^2 = 0.81$: about 81% of the variance of the cost of fuel is removed. The remaining 19% is basis risk from cross hedging.
answer: $h^* = 0.72$; 9 long contracts; 81% effectiveness.

@mistakes
- Using a margin call that restores only the maintenance level when the problem states that calls restore initial margin, or computing the call from the total loss rather than the current balance.
- Taking $h^* = \sigma_S/\sigma_F$ without the correlation: the hedge ratio is the product of the correlation and the volatility ratio.
- Reporting $\rho$ as the hedge effectiveness instead of $\rho^2$.
- Selling futures to hedge a future purchase: a buyer who fears a rising price hedges with a long position.

model:
```python
import statistics


def min_variance_hedge_ratio(spot_changes, futures_changes):
    """Slope of spot changes regressed on futures changes."""
    covariance = statistics.covariance(spot_changes, futures_changes)
    variance = statistics.variance(futures_changes)
    return covariance / variance
```
hint: The ratio is $\operatorname{Cov}(\Delta S,\Delta F)/\operatorname{Var}(\Delta F)$.
hint: `statistics.covariance(x, y)` and `statistics.variance(x)` both use the sample (n - 1) divisor, so the divisors cancel.
solution: Divide the covariance of the spot and futures changes by the variance of the futures changes; this is the regression slope. Dividing the spot variance instead, or swapping the arguments, gives a different ratio, which the test with `[1, 2, 3, 4]` against `[2, 4, 6, 8]` catches.

@quiz self
prompt: In a few sentences, explain how daily marking to market and margin accounts reduce the credit risk of a futures contract compared with a forward contract.
hint: Think about how large an unpaid loss can become, and who guarantees performance.
solution: With marking to market, each side pays its losses to the clearinghouse every day, so at most about one day's price move is ever unpaid. The clearinghouse is the counterparty to every trade, and margin deposits are held as a performance bond; if a trader fails to meet a margin call, the position is closed out and the margin covers the loss. A forward is settled only at maturity, so the loss on a position can accumulate for months and the loser may default, with the other side bearing the credit loss unless collateral has been posted.
rubric: States that gains and losses are settled daily, so unpaid exposure is limited to about one day's move.
rubric: Mentions margin as a deposit or performance bond that covers losses and triggers calls.
rubric: Contrasts with a forward, which settles only at maturity so credit exposure accumulates.
