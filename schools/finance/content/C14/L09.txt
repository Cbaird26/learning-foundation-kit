@article
An option's price depends on the stock price, time, volatility and the interest rate. The **Greeks** measure how sensitive the price is to each input. Traders use them to understand a position's exposures and to hedge it.

**Conventions.** The formulas below are for European options under BSM, with $r$ and $q$ continuously compounded and $T$ in years. $\varphi(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}$ is the standard normal density, and $d_1$, $d_2$ are as in Week 8. Greeks are per share and are partial derivatives with respect to the input in its natural units: **vega** per 1.00 (100 percentage points) of volatility, **theta** per year, **rho** per 1.00 of interest rate. Divide by 100 for vega per volatility point, by 365 for theta per calendar day, and by 100 for rho per percentage point.

### The Greeks

| Greek | Meaning | Call | Put |
|---|---|---|---|
| Delta $\Delta = \partial V/\partial S$ | Price change per \$1 in the stock | $e^{-qT}N(d_1)$ | $e^{-qT}[N(d_1)-1]$ |
| Gamma $\Gamma = \partial^2 V/\partial S^2$ | Change in delta per \$1 in the stock | $\dfrac{e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)}{S\sigma\sqrt{T}}$ | same as call |
| Vega $\partial V/\partial\sigma$ | Price change per unit of volatility | $S e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}$ | same as call |
| Theta $\partial V/\partial t$ (calendar time) | Price change as time passes | $-\dfrac{S e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{T}} - rKe^{-rT}N(d_2) + qSe^{-qT}N(d_1)$ | $-\dfrac{S e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\sigma}{2\sqrt{T}} + rKe^{-rT}N(-d_2) - qSe^{-qT}N(-d_1)$ |
| Rho $\partial V/\partial r$ | Price change per unit of interest rate | $KTe^{-rT}N(d_2)$ | $-KTe^{-rT}N(-d_2)$ |

Calls have delta between 0 and 1 and puts between $-1$ and 0 (they differ by $e^{-qT}$, from put-call parity). Gamma and vega are positive for long calls and puts, and largest for options near the money. Theta is usually negative for a long option: time decay.

### Using delta and gamma

For a small move $\Delta S$ in the stock, the **delta-gamma approximation** is
$$\Delta V \approx \Delta\cdot\Delta S + \tfrac12\,\Gamma\,(\Delta S)^2.$$
Delta alone is a linear (tangent) approximation; gamma corrects for the curvature. A long option has positive gamma, so the true change exceeds the delta estimate whichever way the stock moves.

### Delta hedging

A **delta-neutral** portfolio has zero delta, so its value is insensitive to small moves in the stock. To hedge a **short** position in $n$ options each with delta $\Delta$, **buy** $n\Delta$ shares: the loss on the options when the stock rises is offset by the gain on the shares. The hedge holds only for small moves and for a short time, since delta changes with $S$ and with time; the hedger must **rebalance** by trading shares. Hedging a short option costs: you buy shares as the price rises and sell as it falls (buy high, sell low), and this cost is the price the hedger collects as the premium if the realized volatility equals the volatility used for pricing.

### Gamma and vega

To control the curvature, add another option. A **delta-gamma neutral** position uses an option with a different gamma: choose its quantity to cancel the portfolio gamma, then adjust the shares to cancel the delta (shares have zero gamma). Vega can be offset similarly with options of different maturities. A delta-hedged long option is **long gamma** and **short theta**: it profits from large moves, and pays time decay otherwise.

@example
title: The Greeks of an at-the-money call
problem: A stock is at \$100 with no dividends. A European call has strike \$100, 0.5 years to expiration, $r = 5\%$ and $\sigma = 20\%$. Find its price, delta, gamma and vega.
step: $\sigma\sqrt{T} = 0.141421$; $d_1 = (0 + (0.05 + 0.02)\times 0.5)/0.141421 = 0.2475$ and $d_2 = 0.1061$.
step: $N(d_1) = 0.5977$, $N(d_2) = 0.5422$. Price: $100\times0.5977 - 100\,e^{-0.025}\times 0.5422 = 59.77 - 52.88 = \$6.89$.
step: Delta $= N(d_1) = 0.5977$. Density: $\varphi(0.2475) = 0.3869$.
step: Gamma $= \varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt{T}) = 0.3869/(100 \times 0.141421) = 0.02736$.
step: Vega $= S\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T} = 100 \times 0.3869 \times 0.7071 = 27.36$ per 1.00 of volatility, or 0.274 per volatility point.
answer: Price \$6.89, delta 0.598, gamma 0.0274, vega 27.4 (0.274 per volatility point).

@example
title: Hedging a short call position
problem: A dealer sells 20 contracts (100 shares each) of a call with delta 0.60 at a stock price of \$80. The stock then rises \$1 and the delta becomes 0.62. How many shares should the dealer hold initially, and how many more should the dealer buy after the rise?
step: Options: $20 \times 100 = 2{,}000$ shares' worth. Initial hedge: $2{,}000 \times 0.60 = 1{,}200$ shares bought.
step: After the rise the delta is 0.62, so the hedge should be $2{,}000 \times 0.62 = 1{,}240$ shares.
step: The dealer buys 40 more shares, at a higher price: this "buy high" effect is the cost of hedging negative gamma. Gamma $\approx (0.62 - 0.60)/1 = 0.02$ per option.
answer: Hold 1,200 shares at first and buy 40 more after the rise.

@mistakes
- Hedging a short call by selling shares: the dealer is short the call and must buy $n\Delta$ shares.
- Mixing units: vega per 1.00 of volatility versus per point, theta per year versus per day.
- Assuming a delta hedge is permanent: delta changes with the stock price and with time, so the hedge needs rebalancing.
- Claiming that gamma has the opposite sign for puts: long puts and long calls both have positive gamma and vega.

model:
```python
import math


def call_greeks(S, K, T, r, sigma, q=0.0):
    """Delta, gamma and vega (per 1.00 of volatility) of a European call."""
    sd = sigma * math.sqrt(T)
    d1 = (math.log(S / K) + (r - q + sigma ** 2 / 2) * T) / sd
    cdf = 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(d1 / math.sqrt(2)))
    pdf = math.exp(-d1 ** 2 / 2) / math.sqrt(2 * math.pi)
    disc = math.exp(-q * T)
    delta = disc * cdf
    gamma = disc * pdf / (S * sd)
    vega = S * disc * pdf * math.sqrt(T)
    return delta, gamma, vega
```
hint: Only $d_1$ is needed for these three Greeks.
hint: The dividend yield enters through the factor $e^{-qT}$ and through the drift $r - q$ in $d_1$.
solution: Compute $d_1$ with the drift $r - q$, then delta is $e^{-qT}N(d_1)$, gamma is $e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt{T})$ and vega is $Se^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt{T}$. The test with $q = 0.03$ catches a missing dividend adjustment.
