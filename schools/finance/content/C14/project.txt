### Week by week

1. **Markets and instruments.** Forwards, futures, options, swaps; long and short; payoffs; exchange-traded versus OTC; clearing; hedgers, speculators, arbitrageurs; leverage.
2. **Forwards.** $F_0 = S_0e^{rT}$; income, yield, storage and currency adjustments; cash-and-carry arbitrage; forward value $V_t = S_t - F_0e^{-r(T-t)}$.
3. **Futures.** Marking to market, margin calls, basis risk, $h^* = \rho\sigma_S/\sigma_F$, $N^* = h^*Q_A/Q_F$, effectiveness $\rho^2$.
4. **Swaps.** Par swap rate $R = m(1-Z_n)/\sum Z_i$, swap value as a bond difference, currency swaps, SOFR.
5. **Option payoffs.** Payoff versus profit, breakevens, covered call, protective put, spreads, straddle, butterfly, collar.
6. **Parity and bounds.** $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0$, dividend adjustment, bounds, early exercise.
7. **Binomial pricing.** $p = (e^{r\Delta t} - d)/(u-d)$, replication, backward induction, American options, $u = e^{\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t}}$.
8. **Black-Scholes-Merton.** $d_1$, $d_2$, call and put formulas, $N(d_2)$, dividends and currencies.
9. **Greeks.** Delta, gamma, vega, theta, rho; delta-gamma approximation; delta and gamma hedging.
10. **Volatility.** Log returns, annualization, implied volatility, smile and skew, hedge simulation.
11. **Exotics and Monte Carlo.** Digitals, barriers (in-out parity), Asians, simulation, standard error $s/\sqrt N$.
12. **Risk management.** Instrument choice, beta hedging $N = (\beta^*-\beta)P/(Fm)$, liquidity, basis and counterparty risks.

### Key formulas

- Forward: $F_0 = (S_0 - I)e^{rT}$, $S_0e^{(r-q)T}$, $S_0e^{(r_d - r_f)T}$.
- Parity: $c + Ke^{-rT} = p + S_0e^{-qT}$.
- BSM: $c = S_0e^{-qT}N(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}N(d_2)$.
- Delta (call) $= e^{-qT}N(d_1)$; gamma $= e^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)/(S\sigma\sqrt T)$; vega $= Se^{-qT}\varphi(d_1)\sqrt T$.

### Common errors

- Mixing continuous and discrete compounding, or annual and periodic rates.
- Forgetting the premium in option profit, or the discounting of the strike in parity.
- Using real-world probabilities in risk-neutral pricing.
- Using volatility as a percent number, or the wrong annualization factor.
- Reading notional value as the amount at risk, and ignoring margin cash flows.

prompt: A stock trades at \$100 and pays no dividends. The continuously compounded rate is 4% and the volatility is 25%. (a) Find the BSM price of a 1-year European call with strike \$100. (b) Use put-call parity to find the price of the put with the same strike and expiration. (c) A long straddle consists of buying both. What does the straddle cost? Give the cost of the straddle in dollars to two decimal places.
answer: 19.75
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: bsm(100, 100, 1, 0.04, 0.25) + (bsm(100, 100, 1, 0.04, 0.25) + 100 * exp(-0.04) - 100)
hint: Compute $d_1 = (0.04 + 0.03125)/0.25 = 0.285$ and $d_2 = 0.035$ first.
hint: Parity gives $p = c + Ke^{-rT} - S_0$; the straddle costs $c + p$.
solution: $N(d_1) = N(0.285) = 0.6122$ and $N(d_2) = N(0.035) = 0.5140$, so $c = 100 \times 0.6122 - 96.0789 \times 0.5140 = \$11.84$. Parity: $p = 11.84 + 96.08 - 100 = \$7.92$. Straddle cost $= 11.84 + 7.92 = \$19.75$ (using unrounded values). The straddle breaks even if the stock ends above about \$119.75 or below about \$80.25 (ignoring interest on the premium).

@exam num
prompt: A dealer has sold 100 contracts (100 shares each) of a 3-month European call with strike \$60 on a non-dividend stock. The stock is at \$60, the rate is 4% (continuously compounded) and the volatility is 30%. The dealer is delta hedged. The stock then rises immediately to \$61 (with no time passing and the same volatility). How many additional shares must the dealer buy to be delta neutral again? Round to the nearest whole share.
answer: 431
unit: shares
check: round(10000 * (ncdf((log(61 / 60) + (0.04 + 0.045) * 0.25) / (0.3 * sqrt(0.25))) - ncdf((log(60 / 60) + (0.04 + 0.045) * 0.25) / (0.3 * sqrt(0.25)))))
hint: The dealer is short $100 \times 100 = 10{,}000$ options. Find the call's delta, $N(d_1)$, at \$60 and at \$61.
solution: At \$60: $d_1 = (0.04 + 0.045)\times 0.25/0.15 = 0.1417$ and $\Delta = N(0.1417) = 0.5563$. At \$61: $d_1 = (0.016529 + 0.02125)/0.15 = 0.2519$ and $\Delta = N(0.2519) = 0.5994$. The delta rose by 0.0431, and the dealer's short position of 10,000 options needs $10{,}000 \times 0.0431 = 431$ more shares. This is close to gamma $\approx 0.0439$ per option, so $10{,}000 \times 0.0439 \times 1 \approx 439$ to first order. The dealer buys after the price has risen: the cost of being short gamma.

prompt: A US firm has a \$40 million floating-rate loan costing SOFR plus 2.00% a year, and it will receive 10 million euros in one year. The EUR/USD spot rate is 1.08, the continuously compounded dollar rate is 4% and the euro rate is 2.5%. A bank offers (i) a swap in which the firm pays a fixed 4.10% and receives SOFR on \$40 million, and (ii) a one-year forward to sell euros. Compute the loan's all-in annual interest cost after the swap in dollars, compute the forward rate and the dollars received from the forward, and explain in a short paragraph one risk that remains after these two hedges and one thing the firm gives up.
hint: SOFR cancels in the swap. Use the covered interest parity formula $F = Se^{(r_d - r_f)T}$ for the forward.
solution: After the swap the firm pays SOFR + 2.00% on the loan, pays 4.10% fixed and receives SOFR, so its all-in rate is 6.10%, or $0.061 \times 40{,}000{,}000 = \$2{,}440{,}000$ a year (ignoring day counts). The forward rate is $1.08\,e^{(0.04-0.025)\times1} = 1.08 \times 1.015113 = 1.0963$ dollars per euro, so the firm receives $10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.0963 = \$10{,}963{,}221$ in a year whatever the spot rate. Remaining risks include counterparty credit risk on the bank, because the swap and forward are over-the-counter contracts, and the risk that the euro receipt is smaller or later than expected, leaving the forward larger than the exposure. The firm gives up any benefit if SOFR falls or if the euro strengthens: both gains are offset by the hedges.
