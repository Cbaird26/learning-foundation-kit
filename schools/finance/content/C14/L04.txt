@article
A company has a loan whose interest floats with a short-term rate, but its revenues are stable, so it would rather pay a fixed rate. Instead of refinancing, it can enter an **interest-rate swap**: it pays a fixed rate to a counterparty and receives the floating rate, which cancels the floating payments on its loan.

### The plain-vanilla interest-rate swap

In a **plain-vanilla (fixed-for-floating) swap** one party, the **fixed-rate payer** (or "pay-fixed"), pays a fixed rate $R$ on a **notional principal** $N$ each period and receives a floating rate; the other party does the reverse. The notional is never exchanged: it only scales payments, and usually only the **net** amount changes hands. With payment frequency $m$ per year, the fixed payment is $N R/m$ and the floating payment is $N L/m$, where $L$ is the floating rate that applies to the period. Since the replacement of USD LIBOR (its panel ended in June 2023), the main USD floating reference rate is **SOFR**, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate. Swaps referencing SOFR typically pay overnight SOFR compounded over the period (in arrears); in this lesson, to keep the arithmetic simple, the floating rate for each period is a given rate $L$, and period lengths are exactly $1/m$ year.

### The par swap rate

At initiation the swap has zero value, so the fixed rate $R$ makes the present value of the fixed payments equal to that of the floating payments. Two facts do the work:

1. A floating-rate bond that pays the floating rate is worth its par value on any reset date (single-curve assumption: forecast and discount rates are the same curve).
2. The fixed leg is a fixed-rate bond.

With discount factors $Z_1,\dots,Z_n$ for the payment dates, and $n$ periods of $1/m$ year, the per-period fixed rate satisfies $\frac{R}{m}\sum Z_i + Z_n = 1$, so
$$R = m\,\frac{1 - Z_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} Z_i}.$$
A discount factor from an annually compounded spot rate $s_t$ is $Z_t = 1/(1+s_t)^t$.

### Valuing a swap after initiation

Immediately after a payment date, the value to the fixed-rate payer is
$$V_{\text{pay fixed}} = N\left[\,1 - Z_n - \frac{R}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{n} Z_i\right],$$
using the discount factors for the remaining payments. This is the value of the floating-rate bond received ($N$ just after a payment date) minus the value of the fixed-rate bond paid ($N\frac{R}{m}\sum Z_i + N Z_n$). The fixed payer gains when market swap rates rise above $R$, as it then pays less than the new market fixed rate, and loses when they fall. The other side's value is $-V$.

### Currency swaps

A **currency swap** exchanges principal and interest in two currencies. At initiation the two notionals are exchanged at the spot rate $S_0$ (domestic units per foreign unit), then each side pays interest on the notional it received, and at maturity the notionals are re-exchanged at the same rate. The interest can be fixed-for-fixed, fixed-for-floating or floating-for-floating. Unlike an interest-rate swap, the notionals are exchanged, which is why a currency swap has larger credit exposure.

A party that receives domestic-currency payments and pays foreign-currency payments values the swap, in domestic currency, as
$$V = B_d - S_t\,B_f,$$
where $B_d$ is the present value of the domestic-currency bond it receives (coupons plus principal, discounted at domestic rates), $B_f$ is the present value of the foreign-currency bond it pays (discounted at foreign rates), and $S_t$ is the current spot rate.

### Uses and credit risk

Swaps let borrowers convert floating debt to fixed (or the reverse), match assets and liabilities, and raise funds in the currency in which they have the cheapest access and swap into the one they need. The exposure to default is the swap's **current value to the in-the-money party**, not the notional. Many swaps are now centrally cleared under rules adopted after the 2007–2009 financial crisis.

@example
title: The fixed rate of a 3-year swap
problem: Annually compounded spot rates are 3.0% (1 year), 3.4% (2 years) and 3.8% (3 years). Find the fixed rate of an annual-pay 3-year plain-vanilla swap.
step: Discount factors: $Z_1 = 1/1.03 = 0.970874$; $Z_2 = 1/1.034^2 = 0.935317$; $Z_3 = 1/1.038^3 = 0.894145$.
step: Sum of discount factors: $0.970874 + 0.935317 + 0.894145 = 2.800336$.
step: $R = (1 - 0.894145)/2.800336 = 0.105855/2.800336 = 0.037801$.
step: The swap rate is 3.7801%. It lies a little below the 3-year spot rate (3.8%) because earlier payments are discounted at lower rates.
answer: 3.78% (3.7801%).

@example
title: A semiannual settlement
problem: A swap has notional \$50 million and semiannual payments. The fixed rate is 3.9% and the floating rate for the current period is 4.2%. What does the fixed-rate payer pay and receive, and what is the net?
step: Fixed payment: $50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.039/2 = \$975{,}000$.
step: Floating payment: $50{,}000{,}000 \times 0.042/2 = \$1{,}050{,}000$.
step: The fixed payer receives \$1,050,000 and pays \$975,000, so it receives a net **\$75,000**; the notional is not exchanged.
answer: The fixed payer receives a net \$75,000.

@mistakes
- Believing the notional principal changes hands in an interest-rate swap (it does only in currency swaps).
- Treating the fixed rate as an annual rate when payments are semiannual: multiply by $m$ in $R = m(1-Z_n)/\sum Z_i$ and use $R/m$ for each payment.
- Valuing the swap's exposure at its notional instead of its (usually small) current market value.
- Mixing up the sign: when market rates rise, a pay-fixed swap gains value.

choice*: It increases, because the company is paying a rate lower than the new market fixed rate.

model:
```python
def par_swap_rate(discount_factors, periods_per_year=1):
    """Annualized par fixed rate from payment-date discount factors."""
    final = discount_factors[-1]
    annuity = sum(discount_factors)
    return periods_per_year * (1 - final) / annuity
```
hint: The sum of all the discount factors is the annuity factor; the last factor is the maturity factor.
hint: Multiply by `periods_per_year` at the end to annualize the per-period rate.
solution: The per-period rate is $(1 - Z_n)/\sum Z_i$; multiplying by the payment frequency annualizes it. The test with two semiannual factors catches the common error of forgetting the factor $m$.
