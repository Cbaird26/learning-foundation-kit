@article
A factory building, a patent and an internally developed software platform are all long-lived resources, but accounting treats them very differently. This lesson follows the asset from the day it is recognised through its useful life, and marks where the two frameworks part ways.

### Cost and depreciation

Under **IAS 16** an item of property, plant and equipment (PP&E) is recognised if future economic benefits are probable and cost is reliably measurable. Cost includes the purchase price (net of discounts, plus non-refundable taxes), directly attributable costs to bring the asset to working condition, and the initial estimate of dismantling and restoration costs. US GAAP (ASC 360) is similar. Depreciation allocates the depreciable amount (cost less residual value) systematically over the useful life. Common methods are straight-line, declining balance and units of production:

$$\text{Straight-line depreciation} = \frac{\text{cost} - \text{residual value}}{\text{useful life}}, \qquad \text{DDB depreciation}_t = \frac{2}{N}\times \text{opening carrying amount}_t,$$

where $N$ is the useful life in years and double-declining-balance (DDB) never takes the carrying amount below the residual value. **IAS 16 requires component depreciation**: each significant part with a different useful life is depreciated separately, such as a building's structure, roof and heating system. US GAAP permits component depreciation but does not require it.

### Revaluation

IAS 16 offers a choice, by class of assets, between the **cost model** and the **revaluation model**, which carries the asset at fair value less later depreciation, with revaluations frequent enough that the carrying amount does not differ materially from fair value. A revaluation increase goes to OCI as a **revaluation surplus**, unless it reverses an earlier decrease on the same asset recognised in profit or loss; a decrease goes to profit or loss, unless it reverses a prior surplus. **US GAAP does not permit upward revaluation of PP&E** (ASC 360 uses historical cost, less impairment). IAS 40 lets an IFRS reporter measure investment property at fair value through profit or loss; US GAAP has no equivalent.

### Capitalised borrowing costs

Both **IAS 23** and **ASC 835-20** require interest on debt to be capitalised for a **qualifying asset**, one that takes a substantial period to get ready for use (a plant under construction, for example). The amount is:

$$\text{Capitalised interest} = \text{weighted average accumulated expenditures} \times \text{capitalisation rate},$$

limited to the interest actually incurred. Expenditures are weighted by the fraction of the year they were outstanding. A difference: under IFRS, investment income earned on the temporary investment of specific borrowings is deducted from the capitalised amount; US GAAP generally does not offset it.

### Intangible assets

**IAS 38** requires an intangible asset to be identifiable, controlled and expected to give future benefits. Purchased intangibles are capitalised. For internally generated intangibles, **research is expensed**, and **development is capitalised only if the entity can demonstrate** technical feasibility, its intention and ability to complete and to use or sell the asset, how it will generate probable future benefits, adequate resources, and the ability to measure the expenditure reliably. Internally generated brands, mastheads, publishing titles, customer lists and goodwill are never recognised. **US GAAP expenses research and development as incurred** (ASC 730), with narrow exceptions such as certain software costs (ASC 985-20 and ASC 350-40). An intangible with a finite life is amortised; one with an indefinite life is not amortised but is tested for impairment at least annually. **Goodwill** acquired in a business combination is not amortised under IFRS 3 and IAS 36; US GAAP does the same for public companies, but a private company may elect to amortise it, generally over ten years or less (ASU 2014-02). Revaluation of intangibles under IAS 38 is allowed only where an active market exists, which is rare.

@example
title: Component depreciation
problem: Cairn Properties buys a building for \$5,000,000 made up of structure \$3,200,000 (40-year life), roof \$800,000 (20 years) and heating and cooling plant \$1,000,000 (10 years). Residual values are nil. Compare annual depreciation by components with depreciating the whole building over 40 years.
step: Structure: $3{,}200{,}000 / 40 = \$80{,}000$. Roof: $800{,}000 / 20 = \$40{,}000$. Plant: $1{,}000{,}000 / 10 = \$100{,}000$.
step: Component depreciation is $80{,}000 + 40{,}000 + 100{,}000 = \$220{,}000$ a year.
step: The single-asset approach gives $5{,}000{,}000 / 40 = \$125{,}000$, so IAS 16 depreciation is \$95,000 higher in the early years because the short-lived parts are written off faster.
answer: \$220,000 a year by components, against \$125,000 for the whole building.

@example
title: Revaluing a machine
problem: Dornoch Mills bought a machine on 1 January for \$1,000,000. It uses straight-line depreciation over 10 years with a residual value of \$100,000. At the end of year 2 the fair value is \$900,000 and the company uses the IAS 16 revaluation model. Record the revaluation and find depreciation for year 3.
step: Annual depreciation is $(1{,}000{,}000 - 100{,}000) / 10 = \$90{,}000$. After two years accumulated depreciation is \$180,000 and the carrying amount is \$820,000.
step: The increase to fair value is $900{,}000 - 820{,}000 = \$80{,}000$, recognised in OCI as a revaluation surplus.
step: Entry (eliminating accumulated depreciation against the cost): debit Accumulated depreciation 180,000; credit Machine 100,000; credit Revaluation surplus (OCI) 80,000. The machine now has a carrying amount of \$900,000.
step: Depreciation now covers the remaining 8 years with the same residual value: $(900{,}000 - 100{,}000)/8 = \$100{,}000$ in year 3 instead of \$90,000. Under US GAAP no revaluation is recorded and depreciation stays \$90,000.
answer: Revaluation surplus of \$80,000 in OCI; year 3 depreciation of \$100,000.

@mistakes
- Depreciating land, which is not depreciated because it has an unlimited life.
- Recording a revaluation increase in profit or loss instead of OCI (unless it reverses a previous decrease on the same asset).
- Weighting each expenditure for capitalised interest by the whole year instead of the part of the year it was outstanding.
- Capitalising research costs, or capitalising development costs under US GAAP, outside the narrow software exceptions.
