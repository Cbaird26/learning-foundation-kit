@project
title: Reporting comparison: one IFRS reporter and one US GAAP reporter
overview: You will choose one company that reports under IFRS and one that reports under US GAAP in the same industry, find five accounting policy differences in their notes, restate one key ratio on a comparable basis, and write a three-page memo on how the differences affect analysis. The work is a learning exercise, not a recommendation to buy or sell any security.
goal: Read the accounting policy notes of two real annual reports and identify differences that come from the frameworks rather than from the businesses.
goal: Restate one key ratio so that the two companies are comparable, showing every adjustment and its assumptions.
goal: Explain in plain, professional prose how the differences change an analyst's conclusions.
step: Choose an industry with several large reporters under both frameworks (for example retail, airlines, consumer goods or telecommunications), then pick one IFRS reporter and one US GAAP reporter of similar size. Use their latest annual reports; US filers and foreign private issuers file on SEC EDGAR (Form 10-K and Form 20-F), and other IFRS reporters publish annual reports on their investor pages. Look up all data yourself, because it changes.
step: Read the summary of significant accounting policies and the notes on revenue, inventories, property and equipment, intangibles, leases, income taxes, pensions and financial instruments of both companies. List every policy that differs, whether or not the difference stems from the frameworks.
step: From your list, pick five differences that come from the frameworks. Candidates include LIFO against FIFO or average cost, development cost capitalisation, revaluation of property and equipment, lessee lease expense (IFRS 16 against ASC 842 operating leases), pension cost presentation, reversal of impairments and inventory write-downs, the classification of interest paid in cash flows, and the IFRS 18 presentation changes that will apply from 2027.
step: Choose one ratio (for example operating margin, return on assets, inventory turnover, debt to equity or EBITDA to interest) and compute it from reported figures for both companies. Then restate one company, or both, on a comparable basis using the techniques of this course (for example the LIFO reserve, capitalising operating leases, or removing capitalised development costs). Write each adjustment as a formula with its source note and the assumptions you made, including the tax rate.
step: Draft the memo (three pages at most): purpose and companies; the five differences, with note references and why each arises; the ratio before and after the restatement in a table; what changes in the analysis; and limits (data you could not adjust, judgement involved). Use the standard names (IFRS 16, ASC 842, IAS 2, ASC 330, and so on).
step: Check every number against the filings, confirm that debits and credits in any entries you show balance, and re-read the memo for statements not supported by the notes.
material: **Worked pattern for the ratio restatement (invented data).** Use this fictional pair to practise before you start on the real companies. Larkspur Outfitters reports under IFRS and Bellwether Foods under US GAAP (using LIFO). Figures in millions of dollars.

| Item | Larkspur (IFRS, FIFO) | Bellwether (US GAAP, LIFO) |
|---|---|---|
| Cost of sales | 4,800 | 4,950 |
| Closing inventory | 1,020 | 700 |
| Opening inventory | 980 | 650 |
| LIFO reserve, closing | n/a | 240 |
| LIFO reserve, opening | n/a | 190 |
| Tax rate | 25% | 25% |

To compare inventory turnover, convert Bellwether to FIFO: closing inventory $700 + 240 = 940$, opening inventory $650 + 190 = 840$, cost of sales $4{,}950 - (240 - 190) = 4{,}900$. FIFO turnover is $4{,}900 / \frac{940 + 840}{2} = 5.51$ times, against 7.33 times as reported on a LIFO basis; Larkspur's turnover is $4{,}800 / 1{,}000 = 4.80$ times, so the FIFO comparison narrows the apparent gap.
material: **Memo checklist.** Each of the five differences should answer four questions: which standards apply (for example IAS 2 and ASC 330); what each company's note says; which direction the difference moves reported profit, assets or leverage; and whether you were able to adjust for it. State clearly when you used an estimate rather than a disclosed number.
deliverable: A spreadsheet or notebook with the reported figures, the adjustments, the formulas and the restated ratio for both companies, with sources noted.
deliverable: A memo of at most three pages for an investment committee that is not familiar with the differences between IFRS and US GAAP.
rubric: Five genuine framework differences are identified, each tied to a standard (IFRS and ASC references) and to a note in the filings.
rubric: The ratio restatement is arithmetically correct, uses disclosed data, and states every assumption (such as the tax rate) and its effect.
rubric: The memo explains, in correct accounting language, how each difference changes the analysis, and it does not give a buy or sell recommendation.
rubric: The memo discusses at least one limit of the comparison and distinguishes disclosed figures from estimates.
rubric: Writing is clear and professional, within three pages, with numbers that match the spreadsheet.
safety: This is a learning exercise based on public filings. It is not investment advice and should not be used to decide whether to buy, sell or hold a security. Standards and company policies change; check the current text of each standard and the latest filings.
tutor: I restated a ratio by adding the LIFO reserve to inventory. What else should change in the balance sheet and in the income statement, and how do I handle the tax effect?
tutor: How can I tell whether a difference in two companies' notes comes from IFRS versus US GAAP, or simply from different business choices?
tutor: My IFRS reporter capitalises development costs. How can I estimate what its profit and equity would be if it followed US GAAP?

## Review guide for week 13

### Key ideas by week

1. **Framework and standard setters.** Relevance and faithful representation are the fundamental qualities; the IASB framework defines assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. The IASB writes IFRS; the FASB writes US GAAP under SEC authority; foreign private issuers may file IFRS without reconciliation.
2. **Presentation.** IFRS 18 (from 1 January 2027) adds operating, investing and financing categories and the subtotals operating profit and profit before financing and income taxes; US GAAP has no categories. ASU 2023-07 and ASU 2024-03 add segment and expense disclosures.
3. **Revenue.** Five steps; allocate by standalone selling price; constraint on variable consideration; over-time recognition by input or output methods.
4. **Receivables and inventories.** Expected credit losses (IFRS 9 simplified approach, CECL); FIFO, average and LIFO; lower of cost and NRV; IFRS reverses write-downs and prohibits LIFO.
5. **PP&E and intangibles.** Component depreciation and revaluation (IFRS only); capitalised interest; development costs capitalised under IAS 38, expensed under US GAAP.
6. **Impairment.** IAS 36 recoverable amount and reversals (not goodwill); US GAAP undiscounted recoverability test, fair value loss, no reversal.
7. **Leases.** IFRS 16 single lessee model against ASC 842 finance and operating leases; lessor finance and sales-type leases.
8. **Income taxes.** Temporary differences, DTA and DTL, IAS 12 recognises a DTA only when use is probable while ASC 740 uses a valuation allowance (more likely than not), uncertain positions, ASU 2023-09.
9. **Pensions.** Net liability; IAS 19 net interest and OCI remeasurements; ASC 715 expected return and amortisation.
10. **Instruments and provisions.** IFRS 9 classification and ECL stages; effective interest; IAS 37 against ASC 450.
11. **Equity, share-based pay, EPS.** Grant-date fair value, forfeitures, treasury stock method, if-converted method.
12. **Changes and errors.** Policy changes and errors retrospective; estimates prospective.

### Key formulas

- Equity change = income − expenses + contributions − distributions.
- Allocation: price × SSP / total SSP.
- Lease liability = payment × annuity factor; interest = opening liability × rate.
- Recoverable amount = max(fair value less costs of disposal, value in use).
- Deferred tax = temporary difference × enacted rate.
- FIFO COGS = LIFO COGS − change in LIFO reserve.
- Treasury stock method shares = N(1 − K/P).

### Self-check

- Can you give the IFRS and US GAAP treatment for each topic above, and a number for each?
- Can you write the journal entry and say where each amount appears in the statements?
- Can you explain why a ratio changes when LIFO, development costs or leases are restated?

### Common errors

- Mixing the dates: IFRS 18 applies from 1 January 2027, ASU 2024-03 from annual periods beginning after December 15, 2026.
- Using the coupon rate instead of the effective rate, or the periodic payment instead of principal reduction.
- Reversing a goodwill impairment, or any impairment under US GAAP.
- Forgetting the tax effect in retrospective adjustments.

prompt: A US company uses LIFO. It reports cost of sales of \$6,000 thousand, opening inventory of \$850 thousand, closing inventory of \$900 thousand, an opening LIFO reserve of \$220 thousand and a closing LIFO reserve of \$300 thousand. To compare it with an IFRS reporter, restate to FIFO and compute inventory turnover (FIFO cost of sales divided by average FIFO inventory). Give it in times, to two decimal places.
answer: 5.22
unit: times
tol: 0.01
check: (6000 - (300 - 220)) / (((850 + 220) + (900 + 300)) / 2)
hint: FIFO cost of sales is LIFO cost of sales less the change in the reserve; FIFO inventory is LIFO inventory plus the reserve.
solution: FIFO cost of sales is $6{,}000 - 80 = 5{,}920$. FIFO opening inventory is $1{,}070$ and closing inventory is $1{,}200$, an average of \$1,135. Turnover is $5{,}920/1{,}135 = 5.22$ times, below the 6.86 times that the LIFO figures give.

@exam self
prompt: Aster Industries (IFRS) and Birch Industries (US GAAP) are similar manufacturers. Aster reports pre-tax profit of \$500,000. This year it capitalised development costs of \$40,000 that meet the IAS 38 criteria, and its amortisation of previously capitalised development costs was \$25,000. Its capitalised development asset has a carrying amount of \$120,000 at year end. Assume the tax rate is 25%, that development costs are deductible for tax when incurred, and that Aster carries a deferred tax liability on the asset. Write a short memo to an analyst that (a) restates Aster's pre-tax and after-tax profit to a US GAAP basis, (b) states the effect on equity, and (c) names the standards and the differences that explain it.
hint: Under US GAAP (ASC 730) the \$40,000 would be expensed and the \$25,000 of amortisation would not exist.
solution: Under US GAAP, research and development is expensed as incurred (ASC 730), while IAS 38 capitalises development that meets its six criteria. (a) Restated pre-tax profit is $500{,}000 - 40{,}000 + 25{,}000 = \$485{,}000$, which is \$15,000 lower. After tax at 25% the profit reduction is $15{,}000 \times 0.75 = \$11{,}250$. (b) The \$120,000 development asset would not exist, and the related deferred tax liability of \$30,000 would also be removed, so equity is lower by $120{,}000 - 30{,}000 = \$90{,}000$. (c) IAS 38 against ASC 730 causes the difference. Because Aster's capitalised amount (\$40,000) exceeds its amortisation (\$25,000), IFRS profit is higher in a growing programme, and the IFRS balance sheet carries an asset, so leverage and return on assets also differ. The analyst should also compare cash flow classification, since capitalised development appears in investing activities under IFRS but R&D is an operating cash outflow under US GAAP.
rubric: Computes restated pre-tax profit of \$485,000 and after-tax profit lower by \$11,250.
rubric: Computes the equity reduction of \$90,000 (asset of \$120,000 less deferred tax liability of \$30,000).
rubric: Names IAS 38 (capitalise development when criteria are met) and ASC 730 (expense as incurred) and explains the difference in profit, equity and cash flow classification.
