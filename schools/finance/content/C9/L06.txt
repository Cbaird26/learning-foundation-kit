@article
Depreciation spreads an asset's cost over its life. Impairment is different: it recognises that an asset is worth less than its carrying amount now, whatever the schedule says. The rules for when to test, how to measure the loss and whether it can be undone are among the biggest differences between IFRS and US GAAP.

### IAS 36: one step with recoverable amount

At each reporting date an IFRS reporter assesses whether there is any indication that an asset may be impaired (a market decline, obsolescence, plans to restructure or an adverse change in use). Goodwill, intangibles with indefinite lives and intangibles not yet available for use are tested at least annually regardless of indications. An asset is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds its **recoverable amount**:

$$\text{Recoverable amount} = \max\left(\text{fair value less costs of disposal},\ \text{value in use}\right),$$

and the **impairment loss** is the carrying amount minus the recoverable amount, recognised in profit or loss (or against the revaluation surplus for a revalued asset). **Value in use** is the present value of the future cash flows expected from the asset's continuing use and eventual disposal, discounted at a rate that reflects current market assessments of the time value of money and the risks of the asset. Where an asset does not produce largely independent cash inflows, test it with the smallest group that does, a **cash-generating unit (CGU)**.

### US GAAP: a two-step test for long-lived assets

For property, plant and equipment and finite-life intangibles held and used, ASC 360-10 uses a **recoverability test** first. The asset group is tested when indicators exist. If the carrying amount exceeds the **undiscounted** cash flows expected from use and disposal, the group fails; the loss is then the carrying amount minus **fair value**. If undiscounted cash flows are at least equal to the carrying amount, no loss is recorded even if fair value is lower. Because the first hurdle is undiscounted, US GAAP recognises impairments less often than IFRS, but once triggered the loss can be larger than under IAS 36 (it is measured against fair value, not the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use). An asset held for sale is measured at the lower of carrying amount and fair value less costs to sell under both frameworks.

### Goodwill

IAS 36 tests goodwill at the CGU or group of CGUs expected to benefit from the combination. If the unit's recoverable amount is below its carrying amount (including goodwill), the loss is allocated **first to reduce goodwill to zero, then to the other assets of the unit pro rata to their carrying amounts**. US GAAP tests goodwill at the **reporting unit** level. An entity may first make an optional qualitative assessment; if it goes to the quantitative test, the loss is the excess of the reporting unit's carrying amount over its fair value, **limited to the carrying amount of goodwill** (ASU 2017-04 removed the old second step). Other assets of the unit are tested first under ASC 360. Indefinite-lived intangibles are tested against fair value (ASC 350-30).

### Reversals

**IAS 36 requires reversal** of an impairment loss (other than goodwill) if the estimates used to determine the recoverable amount have changed. The carrying amount is raised, but not above what it would have been, net of depreciation, had no loss been recognised. **Goodwill impairments are never reversed.** **US GAAP prohibits reversal** of impairments of assets held and used: the written-down amount is a new cost basis.

### Summary

| | IFRS (IAS 36) | US GAAP (ASC 360, ASC 350) |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger for long-lived assets | Indication of impairment | Indication of impairment |
| Test | Carrying amount versus recoverable amount | Carrying amount versus undiscounted cash flows |
| Loss | Carrying amount less recoverable amount | Carrying amount less fair value |
| Reversal | Required, except goodwill | Prohibited |
| Goodwill level | CGU or group of CGUs | Reporting unit |

@example
title: Impairment under both frameworks
problem: Penrose Fabrication has an asset (a single asset group and CGU) with a carrying amount of \$500,000. Fair value is \$400,000 and costs of disposal are \$10,000. The asset is expected to generate \$95,000 a year for 5 years with no disposal proceeds, and the appropriate discount rate is 9%. Compute the impairment loss under IFRS and US GAAP.
step: IFRS value in use: $95{,}000 \times \frac{1 - 1.09^{-5}}{0.09} = 95{,}000 \times 3.88965 = \$369{,}517$. Fair value less costs of disposal is $400{,}000 - 10{,}000 = \$390{,}000$.
step: Recoverable amount is the higher, \$390,000. The IFRS loss is $500{,}000 - 390{,}000 = \$110{,}000$.
step: US GAAP recoverability: undiscounted cash flows are $95{,}000 \times 5 = \$475{,}000$, below the carrying amount of \$500,000, so the asset fails.
step: The US GAAP loss is carrying amount less fair value: $500{,}000 - 400{,}000 = \$100{,}000$. The frameworks give different losses, \$110,000 under IFRS and \$100,000 under US GAAP.
answer: IFRS loss \$110,000; US GAAP loss \$100,000.

@example
title: Goodwill in a cash-generating unit
problem: A CGU has goodwill of \$200 thousand, property, plant and equipment of \$600 thousand and intangible assets of \$200 thousand (carrying amounts, total \$1,000 thousand). Its recoverable amount is \$650 thousand. Allocate the IAS 36 loss and state the US GAAP goodwill loss if the reporting unit's fair value is also \$650 thousand.
step: The loss is $1{,}000 - 650 = \$350$ thousand.
step: Goodwill is reduced first, by \$200 thousand, to zero. The remaining \$150 thousand is allocated pro rata to the other assets, which total \$800 thousand.
step: PP&E bears $150 \times 600/800 = \$112.5$ thousand and the intangibles bear $150 \times 200/800 = \$37.5$ thousand.
step: Under US GAAP the excess of carrying amount over fair value is \$350 thousand but the goodwill loss is capped at the \$200 thousand of goodwill; the other assets are tested separately under ASC 360 and are not written down by this test.
answer: IFRS: goodwill \$200, PP&E \$112.5 and intangibles \$37.5 (thousands); US GAAP goodwill loss \$200 thousand.

@mistakes
- Comparing carrying amount with fair value alone under IFRS: the benchmark is the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use.
- Using discounted cash flows in the US GAAP recoverability test, which uses undiscounted cash flows.
- Reversing a goodwill impairment, or reversing an impairment under US GAAP: the first is prohibited under IFRS and the second under US GAAP.
- Reversing an IFRS impairment up to the recoverable amount without the cap: the carrying amount cannot exceed what it would have been without the loss.

model:
```python
def impairment_losses(carrying, undiscounted_cf, fair_value, costs_to_sell,
                      value_in_use):
    """Return (IFRS loss, US GAAP loss) for an asset held and used."""
    recoverable = max(fair_value - costs_to_sell, value_in_use)
    ifrs_loss = max(0, carrying - recoverable)
    if undiscounted_cf >= carrying:
        us_loss = 0
    else:
        us_loss = max(0, carrying - fair_value)
    return ifrs_loss, us_loss
```
hint: Compute the IFRS recoverable amount with `max`, then subtract it from the carrying amount, with a floor of zero.
hint: For US GAAP, check the undiscounted cash flows against the carrying amount first.
solution: The IFRS loss comes from one comparison with the higher of fair value less costs to sell and value in use. The US GAAP loss needs the recoverability test first: only if undiscounted cash flows fall short of the carrying amount is the loss measured against fair value. The fourth test shows that the frameworks can disagree even in direction: value in use above carrying amount means no IFRS loss, while the failed recoverability test produces a US GAAP loss.
