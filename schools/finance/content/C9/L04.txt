@article
Receivables and inventories are the two largest working capital assets for many companies, and both rest on estimates: how much of what customers owe will be collected, and what the stock on the shelf will be worth when sold.

### Receivables and expected credit losses

Under both frameworks a company recognises an allowance for expected credit losses on trade receivables from the day it records them. **IFRS 9** uses a simplified approach for trade receivables without a significant financing component: the allowance equals **lifetime expected credit losses**. **CECL** (the current expected credit loss model in **ASC 326-20**) also measures lifetime expected losses from initial recognition for assets at amortised cost. For trade receivables the two models give similar answers, and both are commonly applied with a **provision matrix** (an aging schedule with a loss rate for each age bucket, adjusted for current conditions and reasonable forecasts).

Journal entries use an allowance (contra-asset) account:

| Event | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Set up or increase the allowance | Credit loss expense | Allowance for expected credit losses |
| Write off a specific account | Allowance for expected credit losses | Trade receivables |

The expense each period is the amount needed to bring the allowance to its required balance: required allowance minus the allowance balance before adjustment (a credit balance).

### Inventory cost and measurement

Inventory cost includes purchase cost and conversion costs to bring items to their present location and condition; storage, abnormal waste and selling costs are expensed. When prices change, a **cost flow assumption** decides which costs go to cost of goods sold:

- **FIFO**: the oldest costs go to cost of goods sold, and ending inventory carries the latest costs.
- **Weighted average**: total cost of goods available divided by total units gives one average unit cost.
- **LIFO**: the latest costs go to cost of goods sold. **LIFO is permitted under US GAAP (ASC 330) and prohibited by IAS 2.** In the United States, tax rules require a company that uses LIFO for tax to use it in its financial statements as well.

Inventory is measured at the **lower of cost and net realisable value (NRV)**, where NRV is the estimated selling price less estimated costs of completion and sale. This applies under IAS 2 and, since ASU 2015-11, under ASC 330 for FIFO and average cost. Companies using LIFO or the retail inventory method under US GAAP still apply the older **lower of cost or market** test. The frameworks differ on recoveries: **IAS 2 requires reversal** of a write-down, up to the original cost, when NRV recovers; **US GAAP prohibits reversal**, because the written-down amount becomes the new cost basis.

### Converting LIFO to FIFO

A LIFO company discloses its **LIFO reserve**: FIFO inventory minus LIFO inventory. To compare it with an IFRS reporter:

$$\text{FIFO inventory} = \text{LIFO inventory} + \text{LIFO reserve},$$

$$\text{FIFO COGS} = \text{LIFO COGS} - (\text{ending reserve} - \text{beginning reserve}).$$

When prices rise, the reserve grows and FIFO profit is higher. Pre-tax income rises by the change in the reserve, and cumulative retained earnings rise by the reserve times (1 − tax rate), with the tax effect shown as a deferred tax liability. Reducing LIFO inventory layers, a **LIFO liquidation**, matches old low costs with current sales and inflates profit.

title: A provision matrix
problem: Aldine Wholesale has trade receivables of \$800,000, aged with the loss rates below. The allowance account has a credit balance of \$9,200 before adjustment. What is the credit loss expense, and what is the entry?
step: Multiply each bucket by its rate: current \$600,000 at 1% is \$6,000; 1 to 30 days past due \$120,000 at 4% is \$4,800; 31 to 60 days \$50,000 at 10% is \$5,000; over 60 days \$30,000 at 30% is \$9,000.
step: The required allowance is $6{,}000 + 4{,}800 + 5{,}000 + 9{,}000 = \$24{,}800$.
step: The expense is the required allowance less the existing credit balance: $24{,}800 - 9{,}200 = \$15{,}600$.
step: Entry: debit Credit loss expense 15,600; credit Allowance for expected credit losses 15,600. Net receivables are $800{,}000 - 24{,}800 = \$775{,}200$.
answer: Credit loss expense \$15,600; allowance \$24,800.

@example
title: FIFO, weighted average and LIFO
problem: Bellwether Foods has 100 units at \$20 in opening inventory, buys 200 units at \$22, then 150 units at \$25, and sells 320 units. Compute cost of goods sold (COGS) and ending inventory under each method (periodic system).
step: Goods available: $100 \times 20 + 200 \times 22 + 150 \times 25 = 2{,}000 + 4{,}400 + 3{,}750 = \$10{,}150$ for 450 units. Ending units are $450 - 320 = 130$.
step: FIFO: the oldest 320 units are sold, which is 100 at 20, 200 at 22 and 20 at 25: COGS $2{,}000 + 4{,}400 + 500 = \$6{,}900$. Ending inventory is 130 units at \$25, or \$3,250.
step: Weighted average: unit cost $10{,}150 / 450 = \$22.5556$. COGS is $320 \times 22.5556 = \$7{,}217.78$ and ending inventory is $\$2{,}932.22$.
step: LIFO: the latest costs go first, which is 150 at 25 and 170 at 22: COGS $3{,}750 + 3{,}740 = \$7{,}490$. Ending inventory is 100 at 20 and 30 at 22, or \$2,660.
step: With rising prices FIFO gives the lowest COGS and highest profit, LIFO the reverse. Each COGS plus ending inventory equals \$10,150.
answer: FIFO COGS \$6,900; average \$7,217.78; LIFO \$7,490.

@mistakes
- Recording write-offs as an expense: a write-off debits the allowance and does not change profit, because the expense was recognised when the allowance was set up.
- Setting the expense equal to the required allowance without subtracting the balance already in the allowance account.
- Using the selling price as NRV without deducting the costs of completion and sale.
- Reversing an inventory write-down under US GAAP, which prohibits it, or failing to reverse it under IFRS, which requires it up to the original cost.
