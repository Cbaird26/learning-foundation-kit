@article
Every accounting standard is an answer to a prior question: what is financial reporting for, and what counts as an asset, a liability or an expense? A **conceptual framework** is the set of ideas standard setters use to write consistent standards and that preparers use when no standard covers a transaction. In this course you will meet the same transactions twice, once under IFRS Accounting Standards and once under US GAAP, so you need to know where each framework comes from.

### Who sets the rules

The **IASB** (International Accounting Standards Board) issues IFRS Accounting Standards. It is overseen by the trustees of the **IFRS Foundation**, and the **IFRS Interpretations Committee** answers application questions. More than 100 jurisdictions require or permit IFRS for listed companies.

In the United States the **FASB** (Financial Accounting Standards Board) writes US GAAP, which is organised in the **Accounting Standards Codification (ASC)**; changes arrive as **Accounting Standards Updates (ASUs)**, such as ASU 2023-08 on crypto assets. The **SEC** has legal authority over public company reporting and recognises the FASB's standards as authoritative. A US domestic issuer must use US GAAP. A **foreign private issuer** may file IFRS financial statements, as issued by the IASB, without reconciling them to US GAAP. The two boards ran joint projects after the 2002 Norwalk Agreement, so revenue (IFRS 15 and ASC 606) is largely converged, while leases (IFRS 16 and ASC 842), financial instrument impairment (IFRS 9 and CECL in ASC 326) and inventories (LIFO) are not. IFRS is often called principles-based and US GAAP more detailed, with industry guidance and some bright lines; the contrast is one of degree.

### Objective and qualitative characteristics

The IASB's *Conceptual Framework for Financial Reporting* (revised in 2018) says the objective of general purpose financial reporting is to give existing and potential investors, lenders and other creditors information useful in deciding whether to provide resources to the entity. The 2018 revision restored explicit references to **stewardship** (management's accountability for the resources entrusted to it) and to **prudence**, defined as the exercise of caution when making judgements under uncertainty; prudence supports neutrality and does not call for deliberate understatement. FASB Concepts Statement No. 8 sets out a similar objective.

Useful information has two **fundamental** characteristics:

- **Relevance**: information that can make a difference to decisions, through predictive value, confirmatory value or both. **Materiality** is an entity-specific aspect of relevance.
- **Faithful representation**: complete, neutral and free from error, so that the numbers depict the economic substance of what they claim to depict.

Four **enhancing** characteristics raise usefulness: **comparability**, **verifiability**, **timeliness** and **understandability**. The underlying assumption is **going concern**.

### The elements

| Element | IASB framework definition (2018) |
|---|---|
| Asset | A present economic resource controlled by the entity as a result of past events |
| Liability | A present obligation of the entity to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events |
| Equity | The residual interest in the assets after deducting all liabilities |
| Income | Increases in assets, or decreases in liabilities, that increase equity, other than contributions from holders of equity claims |
| Expenses | Decreases in assets, or increases in liabilities, that decrease equity, other than distributions to holders of equity claims |

An **economic resource** is a right that has the potential to produce economic benefits. The accounting equation follows: assets = liabilities + equity, and the change in equity over a period equals income minus expenses plus contributions minus distributions. The FASB's Concepts Statement No. 6 defines the same elements in somewhat different words (an asset is a probable future economic benefit obtained or controlled as a result of past transactions or events).

### Measurement

**Historical cost** records the price paid. **Current value** bases include **fair value**, **value in use** (the present value of the cash flows an entity expects from using an asset) and **current cost**. IFRS 13 and ASC 820 define fair value in the same way: the price that would be received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability in an orderly transaction between market participants at the measurement date. Both rank inputs in a three-level hierarchy, from quoted prices in active markets (Level 1) to unobservable inputs (Level 3). The Framework does not override a specific standard: if the two conflict, the standard prevails.

@example
title: Does the item meet the definition?
problem: Tallis Freight has three items at year end. (1) A truck it owns and operates. (2) A press release announcing that next year it may refurbish its depots, with no commitment to anyone. (3) A signed order for trucks it will receive and pay for next month. Which are an asset or a liability today under the IASB framework?
step: Apply the asset definition to item 1: Tallis controls the truck, a right to economic benefits, and it arose from a past purchase. The truck is an asset.
step: Apply the liability definition to item 2: a liability needs a present obligation arising from a past event. An announcement of a possible plan creates no obligation to another party, so no liability exists.
step: Item 3 is an executory contract: neither side has yet performed. Tallis has a right to the trucks and an obligation to pay, but the two are interdependent, and the combined right and obligation is generally not recognised until one party performs.
answer: The truck is an asset; the possible refurbishment is not a liability; the unperformed order is not yet recognised.

@example
title: What changed in equity?
problem: Kestrel Mills' equity was \$300,000 at the start of the year. At year end its assets are \$820,000 and its liabilities \$470,000. During the year owners contributed \$20,000 and the company paid dividends of \$15,000. What were income minus expenses for the year (total comprehensive income)?
step: Closing equity is assets minus liabilities: $820{,}000 - 470{,}000 = \$350{,}000$.
step: Equity changed by $350{,}000 - 300{,}000 = \$50{,}000$.
step: Remove the transactions with owners: contributions added \$20,000 and distributions took away \$15,000, so they net to a \$5,000 increase.
step: Income minus expenses is the rest: $50{,}000 - 20{,}000 + 15{,}000 = \$45{,}000$.
answer: \$45,000.

@mistakes
- Treating comparability as a fundamental characteristic: the fundamental ones are relevance and faithful representation; comparability, verifiability, timeliness and understandability enhance usefulness.
- Counting an owner's contribution as income, or a dividend as an expense: both are transactions with holders of equity claims and sit outside income and expenses.
- Believing that IFRS needs judgement while US GAAP is a mechanical rulebook: both frameworks need judgement, and US GAAP simply has more specific guidance in many areas.
- Assuming US companies may use IFRS: US domestic issuers must use US GAAP, while foreign private issuers may use IFRS as issued by the IASB.

@practice self
prompt: Marlow Retail's board announced in a press release that it intends to refurbish all of its stores next year at an expected cost of \$4 million. No contract has been signed, no supplier has been engaged and no one outside the company has been promised anything. Does Marlow have a liability at year end under the IASB framework? Explain in three or four sentences, using the definition of a liability.
hint: List the parts of the liability definition and test each one.
solution: No. A liability is a present obligation to transfer an economic resource as a result of past events. Marlow has stated an intention, but it owes nothing to anyone: no past event has created an obligation, and the company could still change its plan without paying a penalty. Intentions and plans, however firm, are not obligations, so Marlow records nothing; the cost will be recognised when the work is done or a binding commitment arises.
rubric: States the liability definition, including a present obligation and a past event.
rubric: Concludes that no liability exists because an intention to spend creates no obligation to another party.
rubric: Notes that the cost is recognised later, when an obligation arises or the asset is acquired.
