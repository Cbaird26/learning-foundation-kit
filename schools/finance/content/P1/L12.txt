@article
Knowing the material and passing the exam are related but different skills. The second one is trained by taking whole exams under real conditions, then studying your own mistakes. This week you do that.

### The blueprint and the clock

Level I has 180 multiple-choice questions with three answer choices, delivered in two sessions of 2 hours 15 minutes, 90 questions each. That is **1.5 minutes per question**. For the 2026 exams the topic weights are Ethical and Professional Standards 15-20%, Quantitative Methods 6-9%, Economics 6-9%, Financial Statement Analysis 11-14%, Corporate Issuers 6-9%, Equity Investments 11-14%, Fixed Income 11-14%, Derivatives 5-8%, Alternative Investments 7-10% and Portfolio Management 8-12%. **The weights, the format and the rules change every year, and the Level I weights change again from February 2027** (Ethics 10-15%, Quantitative Methods 11-14%, and GIPS no longer a separate reading); confirm everything at cfainstitute.org. A practice exam of 180 questions that follows these weights would have about 31 ethics, 13 quantitative methods, 13 economics, 22 financial statement analysis, 13 corporate issuers, 22 equity, 22 fixed income, 11 derivatives, 15 alternative investments and 18 portfolio management questions.

### Taking a full-length exam

**Conditions.** Sit in two timed blocks of 135 minutes with a break, no notes, and only an approved calculator (check the current list at cfainstitute.org; the policy covers specific models). Practice with the same calculator until its time value of money, cash flow and statistics functions are automatic.

**Pacing.** Use checkpoints every 30 questions: question 30 by about minute 45, question 60 by minute 90, question 90 by minute 120, leaving about 15 minutes for flagged questions.

**First pass.** Read the last sentence of the stem first so you know what is asked. Compute only what is needed. If a question is not under control within about two minutes, eliminate what you can, choose, flag and move on. With three choices and no penalty for wrong answers, **never leave a question blank**. Easy points are worth the same as hard points: do not spend four minutes on one calculation.

**Second pass.** Return to flagged questions in order of how close you were. Do not change an answer without a reason: change it only when you can name the specific error you made the first time.

**Eliminating answers.** In ethics, remove choices that do nothing, conceal or over-react. In quantitative questions, estimate the sign and size first: a bond price change from a 1% rise in yield cannot be positive, a probability cannot exceed 1. In financial statement analysis, check direction with a quick example. In portfolio questions, check whether the measure uses total or systematic risk.

### The error log

After each practice exam, log **every** miss and every lucky guess with four fields: topic, the concept, the **cause** and the fix. Useful cause categories: *knowledge gap* (did not know the rule), *formula error*, *arithmetic or unit slip*, *misread the question*, *time pressure*, *changed a right answer*, and *trap* (fell for a distractor). Count the log by topic and by cause, not just by score. A topic with a low accuracy and a high weight deserves more hours than one with a similar accuracy and a low weight. A cause such as "misread the question" calls for a technique change (underline what is asked), not more content study.

### The final weeks

Space your reviews: revisit a topic after one day, one week and one month. Mix topics in practice sets, because the real exam does not announce the topic. Keep a one-page sheet of formulas and rules you keep missing. Stop taking new practice exams in the last day or two; rest, check logistics and sleep.

@example
title: Turning a result into a plan
problem: In a 90-question session, you finish with 12 minutes to spare but miss 31 questions. Your misses include 9 of 15 ethics questions, mostly by choosing "do nothing" answers, and 8 of 20 fixed income questions, mostly through arithmetic slips. What changes do you make?
step: Pace: finishing 12 minutes early while missing many questions shows that time was not the main problem, so slow down slightly on first pass and use leftover time to recheck calculations.
step: Ethics: the cause is a decision error (choosing passive answers), so drill cases using the five-step framework and practise eliminating choices that do nothing.
step: Fixed income: the cause is arithmetic slips, so practise the calculator routines for bond pricing and duration and always estimate the answer's size and sign first.
step: Re-test with a fresh mixed set after one to two weeks to confirm that the causes have been fixed.
answer: Use the spare time to check work; drill ethics decisions; automate bond calculator routines; retest in one to two weeks.

@mistakes
- Spending most of the study time on favorite topics instead of on the low-accuracy, high-weight ones that the error log reveals.
- Rushing the early questions and running out of time, or spending too long on early questions and guessing the last twenty.
- Leaving questions blank, or changing correct answers without a reason.
- Reading a practice score without classifying the misses by cause, which hides fixable habits.

answer: 8.24
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1 + 0.08 / 4) ** 4 - 1) * 100

prompt: A stock is expected to pay out 45% of its earnings, earnings are expected to grow at 4% a year indefinitely, and the required return is 9%. What is the justified forward P/E ratio (price divided by next year's earnings)? Give it to one decimal place.
answer: 9.0
unit: times
tol: 0.1
check: 0.45 / (0.09 - 0.04)
hint: Justified forward P/E $= (D_1/E_1)/(r - g)$, and $D_1/E_1$ is the payout ratio.
solution: $\text{P/E} = 0.45/(0.09 - 0.04) = 0.45/0.05 = 9.0$.

@quiz mc
prompt: In a period of rising prices with stable inventory quantities, how do the reported results of a company using LIFO compare with those of an otherwise identical company using FIFO?
choice: Higher net income and higher taxes paid
choice*: Lower net income, lower taxes paid and a lower reported inventory balance
choice: Lower cost of goods sold and higher inventory
hint: LIFO charges the most recent, higher costs to cost of goods sold.
solution: Under LIFO, cost of goods sold uses the latest (higher) costs, so gross profit and net income are lower and taxes are lower, while the older, lower costs remain in inventory, so the balance sheet shows lower inventory.

@quiz num
prompt: A bond portfolio has a modified duration of 4.5. Using only duration, what is the estimated percentage change in its value if yields fall by 40 basis points? Give the answer as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 1.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 4.5 * 0.40
hint: A fall in yields raises prices, by modified duration times the yield change.
solution: $\%\Delta P \approx -4.5 \times (-0.0040) = +1.8\%$.
