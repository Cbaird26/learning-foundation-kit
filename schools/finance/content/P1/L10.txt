@article
Derivatives (5-8%) and Alternative Investments (7-10%) together carry 12-18% of the 2026 Level I exam (weights change each year; see cfainstitute.org). Both topics are lighter on arithmetic than they look: the core is a few no-arbitrage identities and a clear sense of who pays whom.

### Forwards, futures and swaps

A **forward contract** is a customized agreement to buy or sell an asset at a set price at a future date; a **futures contract** is standardized, exchange-traded and **marked to market** daily with a clearinghouse, so counterparty risk is low. Pricing by no-arbitrage (cost of carry): with a risk-free rate $r$ and time $T$ in years, $F_0 = S_0(1 + r)^T$ for an asset with no income or cost; with income (dividends or coupons) whose present value is $PV(I)$ and storage costs $PV(C)$, $F_0 = (S_0 - PV(I) + PV(C))(1+r)^T$. The long gains if the asset price ends above the forward price. A **swap** is a series of forwards: in a plain-vanilla interest rate swap, one party pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate (such as SOFR); the **payer of fixed** gains when rates rise, because the floating payments received go up while the fixed payments stay the same. Swaps are settled on net amounts.

### Options

A call gives the right to buy and a put the right to sell at the strike $K$. At expiration: call value $= \max(S_T - K, 0)$, put value $= \max(K - S_T, 0)$. The **long call profit** is $\max(S_T - K, 0) - c_0$ with breakeven $K + c_0$; the **short** option has the mirror payoff. A **protective put** (stock plus put) limits the loss; a **covered call** (stock plus short call) gives up upside for premium income. **Put-call parity** for European options on a non-dividend asset: $c_0 + K/(1+r)^T = p_0 + S_0$, so $p_0 = c_0 - S_0 + K/(1+r)^T$. American options are worth at least as much as European ones. The **binomial model** values an option by replication or risk-neutral probabilities: $\pi = (1 + r - d)/(u - d)$ and $c_0 = [\pi c_u + (1-\pi)c_d]/(1+r)$. Option value rises with volatility and, for a call, with the underlying price, time and the risk-free rate; a put's value rises as the underlying falls.

### Alternatives

**Hedge funds** use strategies such as long/short equity, event-driven, relative value and global macro; fees are often a **management fee** plus an **incentive fee** on profits, sometimes above a hurdle rate or a high-water mark. **Private equity** includes leveraged buyouts and venture capital; limited partners commit capital, the general partner draws it down, and returns show a **J-curve** (negative early, positive later); carried interest is the general partner's share of profits. Evaluate with IRR and with multiples such as total value to paid-in capital. **Real estate** is valued by the **income approach**: under direct capitalization, $\text{Value} = \text{NOI}/\text{cap rate}$, where NOI is net operating income before financing and taxes; the sales comparison and cost approaches are the alternatives. Appraisal-based returns look smoother than market returns. **Commodities** are accessed by futures, whose returns come from price changes, the roll yield (positive in backwardation, negative in contango) and collateral yield. **Digital assets** (cryptocurrencies, stablecoins, tokens) record ownership on distributed ledgers; they carry price, custody and regulatory risk, and in the United States payment stablecoins are covered by a federal framework (the GENIUS Act, July 2025). Alternatives usually involve illiquidity, high fees, valuation uncertainty and limited transparency.

### Technique

For option payoffs, draw the profit at expiration against the final stock price, and mark the strike and the breakeven. For alternatives, first ask: what is the fee base, what is the hurdle and what is illiquid?

@example
title: A forward price and a call's profit
problem: (a) A stock is priced at 100 and will pay a dividend of 2 in six months. The annual risk-free rate is 3% (compounded annually). Find the one-year forward price. (b) An investor buys a call with strike 40 for a premium of 3. Find the profit if the stock is at 48 at expiration, and the breakeven price.
step: (a) The present value of the dividend is $2/1.03^{0.5} = 1.97$.
step: $F_0 = (100 - 1.97) \times 1.03 = 100.97$.
step: (b) The call is worth $\max(48 - 40, 0) = 8$ at expiration, so the profit is $8 - 3 = 5$ per share.
step: The breakeven is $K + c_0 = 40 + 3 = 43$; below 40 the loss is capped at the premium of 3.
answer: (a) Forward price 100.97; (b) profit 5, breakeven 43.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to subtract the present value of dividends (or add storage costs) in a forward price.
- Mixing up the payer of fixed and the receiver of fixed: the payer of fixed gains when rates rise.
- Dropping the premium from a profit calculation, or using the strike instead of the strike plus premium for a call's breakeven.
- Applying a hurdle rate or high-water mark to the wrong base when computing hedge fund fees.

prompt: A European call on a non-dividend-paying stock has a strike of \$102 and one year to expiration, and trades at \$6.50. The stock is at \$100 and the annual risk-free rate is 5%. What is the price of the European put with the same strike and expiration, using put-call parity? Round to the nearest cent.

lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 6.50 - 100 + 102 / 1.05
hint: $p_0 = c_0 - S_0 + K/(1+r)^T$.
hint: The present value of the strike is $102/1.05 = 97.14$.
solution: $p_0 = 6.50 - 100 + 97.14 = \$3.64$.

@practice mc
prompt: An investor buys a share for \$50 and buys a put option on it with a strike of \$45 for a premium of \$2. If the put expires and the investor holds the stock to expiration, what is the maximum loss per share?
choice: \$5, which is the distance between the purchase price and the strike
choice*: \$7, which is the distance between purchase price and strike plus the premium
choice: \$50, because the share could fall to zero
hint: The put guarantees a selling price of the strike, but the premium has been paid.
solution: Below \$45 the put offsets further losses, so the stock position loses at most $50 - 45 = 5$, and the \$2 premium adds to the loss, for a maximum of \$7 per share.

@practice num
prompt: A hedge fund earned a gross return of 18% on its beginning assets of \$100 million. It charges a 2% management fee on beginning assets and a 20% incentive fee on profits after the management fee, with no hurdle rate. What is the investor's net return? Give the answer as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 12.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (18 - 2) * (1 - 0.20)
hint: Deduct the management fee first, then the incentive fee on what remains.
solution: Profit after the management fee is $18\% - 2\% = 16\%$. The incentive fee is $0.20 \times 16\% = 3.2\%$, so the net return is $16\% - 3.2\% = 12.8\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: A stock trades at \$50. In one period it can rise by 20% or fall by 10%, and the one-period risk-free rate is 3%. What is the value today of a one-period European call with a strike of \$52, using risk-neutral probabilities? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 3.37
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: ((1.03 - 0.9) / (1.2 - 0.9)) * (50 * 1.2 - 52) / 1.03
hint: Compute the risk-neutral probability of the up move: $(1 + r - d)/(u - d)$.
hint: The call pays 8 in the up state and 0 in the down state.
solution: $\pi = (1.03 - 0.90)/(1.20 - 0.90) = 0.4333$. The call pays $60 - 52 = 8$ if up and 0 if down, so $c_0 = 0.4333 \times 8/1.03 = \$3.37$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A company enters a plain-vanilla interest rate swap in which it pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate. Immediately after the swap begins, market interest rates rise. What is the effect on the value of the swap to the company?
choice: Its value falls, because the company pays a rate that is now below the market
choice*: Its value rises, because the floating payments it receives will increase while the fixed payments it makes stay the same
choice: Its value is unchanged, because the swap's notional amount is not exchanged
hint: Which leg adjusts to the market, and which leg is fixed?
solution: The payer of fixed receives floating payments that reset higher as rates rise, while its fixed obligation is unchanged, so the swap gains value for the fixed-rate payer. The notional is not exchanged, but its size drives the net payments.
