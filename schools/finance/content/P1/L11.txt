### Portfolio return and risk

The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of its assets' returns: $E(R_p) = \sum w_i E(R_i)$. For two assets, the variance is

$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2\,w_1 w_2\,\rho_{12}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2,$$

where $\rho_{12}$ is the correlation. Risk falls as correlation falls: with $\rho = 1$ there is no diversification benefit, with $\rho < 1$ the portfolio's standard deviation is less than the weighted average of the standard deviations. Covariance $= \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2$. **Systematic risk** (market risk) cannot be diversified away; **unsystematic risk** (firm-specific) can.

### Capital allocation, CAPM and performance

Combining the risk-free asset with the tangency (optimal risky) portfolio gives the **capital allocation line**, with slope equal to the **Sharpe ratio**: $\text{Sharpe} = (R_p - R_f)/\sigma_p$. The **CAPM** says expected return depends only on beta: $E(R_i) = R_f + \beta_i[E(R_M) - R_f]$, plotted as the **security market line** (SML). **Portfolio beta** is the weighted average of the betas. Performance measures: the **Treynor ratio** $(R_p - R_f)/\beta_p$ (for well-diversified portfolios), the **Sharpe ratio** (total risk), **Jensen's alpha** $\alpha = R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_M - R_f)]$, and M-squared. **Roy's safety-first criterion** prefers the portfolio with the largest $(E(R_p) - R_L)/\sigma_p$, where $R_L$ is the minimum acceptable return; it minimizes the probability of falling short under normality.

### Planning: the investment policy statement

The portfolio management process is: plan (client objectives, constraints, the IPS), execute (asset allocation, security selection, implementation) and feedback (monitoring, rebalancing and performance evaluation). The **IPS** states **return objectives** (required return and desire) and **risk tolerance**, which has two parts: **ability** (financial capacity: time horizon, wealth, liquidity needs) and **willingness** (attitude). When they conflict, the portfolio should reflect the **lower**. Constraints are remembered as time horizon, liquidity, taxes, legal and regulatory, and unique circumstances. Strategic asset allocation sets long-run weights; tactical allocation deviates temporarily; rebalancing restores the target. Passive, active and smart-beta approaches differ in cost and tracking error. Costs and fees reduce net returns, and fintech tools (robo-advisers, algorithmic trading, machine learning) change delivery.

### Behavioral finance and ESG

Biases fall into **cognitive errors** (conservatism, confirmation, representativeness, anchoring, illusion of control, hindsight), which can be reduced by better information, and **emotional biases** (loss aversion, overconfidence, self-control, status quo, endowment, regret aversion), which are harder to correct and are usually accommodated. The **disposition effect** is the tendency to sell winners too early and hold losers too long, driven by loss aversion and regret avoidance; **mental accounting** treats money in separate buckets. ESG approaches include exclusionary screening, integration, thematic investing and engagement; their definitions, disclosure rules and regulation vary and are changing, so check current rules.

### Technique

For two-asset variance questions, compute the three terms separately and take the square root at the end. For performance rankings, state which risk measure applies: total risk (Sharpe) or systematic risk (Treynor and alpha).

@example
title: Portfolio risk, Sharpe ratio and alpha
problem: A portfolio is 50% in asset X (expected return 6%, standard deviation 10%) and 50% in asset Y (expected return 10%, standard deviation 20%). The correlation is 0.5 and the risk-free rate is 3%. Separately, a portfolio with beta 1.3 earned 10.5% when the risk-free rate was 3% and the market earned 8%. Find the first portfolio's standard deviation and Sharpe ratio, and the second portfolio's alpha.
step: Expected return $= 0.5(6\%) + 0.5(10\%) = 8\%$.
step: Variance $= 0.25(100) + 0.25(400) + 2(0.25)(0.5)(10)(20) = 25 + 100 + 50 = 175$, so $\sigma_p = 13.23\%$ (less than the 15% weighted average).
step: Sharpe ratio $= (8 - 3)/13.23 = 0.38$.
step: Required return of the second portfolio $= 3 + 1.3(8 - 3) = 9.5\%$, so $\alpha = 10.5 - 9.5 = +1.0\%$.
answer: Standard deviation 13.23%; Sharpe 0.38; alpha +1.0%.

@mistakes
- Averaging standard deviations to get a portfolio's risk (this works only when the correlation is exactly 1).
- Using the Treynor ratio for a portfolio that is not well diversified, or the Sharpe ratio with beta in the denominator.
- Treating the client's stated willingness to take risk as the whole risk tolerance, ignoring the ability to bear it.
- Forgetting the square root when converting variance to standard deviation, or mixing percent and decimal units in the covariance term.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio holds 60% in stock fund A (standard deviation 18%) and 40% in bond fund B (standard deviation 8%). The correlation between them is 0.30. What is the portfolio's standard deviation? Give the answer as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 12.15
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: sqrt((0.6 * 18) ** 2 + (0.4 * 8) ** 2 + 2 * 0.6 * 0.4 * 0.30 * 18 * 8)
hint: Compute each of the three variance terms in percent-squared units.
hint: $(10.8)^2 + (3.2)^2 + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.30)(18)(8)$, then take the square root.
solution: The terms are $116.64 + 10.24 + 20.74 = 147.62$; $\sqrt{147.62} = 12.15\%$, well below the 14.0% weighted average of the two standard deviations.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio returned 10.4% with a standard deviation of 14.2%. The risk-free rate was 2.5%. What is the Sharpe ratio? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.56
tol: 0.01
check: (10.4 - 2.5) / 14.2
hint: The Sharpe ratio is excess return per unit of total risk.
solution: $(10.4 - 2.5)/14.2 = 7.9/14.2 = 0.556 \approx 0.56$.

@practice mc
prompt: A client has a high willingness to take risk but needs most of her portfolio within two years to buy a house. Which statement about the client's risk tolerance in the investment policy statement is most appropriate?
choice*: Her overall risk tolerance should be set by her lower ability to take risk, so the portfolio should be conservative
choice: Her overall risk tolerance should follow her high willingness, since the client's attitude determines the mandate
choice: Her overall risk tolerance should be the average of her willingness and ability
hint: Ability and willingness are separate; what happens when they differ?
solution: The short time horizon and liquidity need limit her ability to bear losses. When ability and willingness conflict, the lower governs, so the IPS should call for a conservative portfolio; the adviser should also document this and discuss it with the client under Standard III(C).

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio has 40% in stock A (beta 0.8), 35% in stock B (beta 1.1) and 25% in stock C (beta 1.5). What is the portfolio beta? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 1.08
tol: 0.01
check: 0.40 * 0.8 + 0.35 * 1.1 + 0.25 * 1.5
hint: Portfolio beta is the weighted average of the betas.
solution: $0.40(0.8) + 0.35(1.1) + 0.25(1.5) = 0.320 + 0.385 + 0.375 = 1.08$.

@practice mc
prompt: An investor wants to minimize the probability that the portfolio's return falls below 2%. Portfolio A has an expected return of 9% and a standard deviation of 12%. Portfolio B has an expected return of 11% and a standard deviation of 18%. Which should the investor choose under Roy's safety-first criterion?
choice*: Portfolio A, because its safety-first ratio of 0.58 exceeds portfolio B's ratio of 0.50
choice: Portfolio B, because its expected return is higher
choice: Neither, because the criterion requires the threshold to be equal to the risk-free rate
hint: Compute (expected return minus the threshold) divided by the standard deviation for each.
solution: $\text{SFRatio}_A = (9 - 2)/12 = 0.583$ and $\text{SFRatio}_B = (11 - 2)/18 = 0.50$. The higher ratio means a lower probability of falling below 2% if returns are normal, so choose portfolio A.
