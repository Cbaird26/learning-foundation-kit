### Time value and returns

With a stated annual rate $r_s$ compounded $m$ times a year, the future value of $PV$ after $N$ years is $FV = PV(1 + r_s/m)^{mN}$, and the effective annual rate is $(1 + r_s/m)^m - 1$. For an ordinary annuity (payments at the end of each period), $PV = PMT\,\frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r}$; for an annuity due, multiply by $(1+r)$.

The **holding period return** is $(P_1 - P_0 + D)/P_0$. Over several periods:

- **Arithmetic mean** $= \frac{1}{n}\sum R_i$ is the best estimate of a single future period's return.
- **Geometric mean** $= \left[\prod (1+R_i)\right]^{1/n} - 1$ is the compound growth rate and is never larger than the arithmetic mean.
- **Time-weighted return** links sub-period returns and removes the effect of the timing of cash flows, so it measures the manager. The **money-weighted return** is the internal rate of return of the investor's cash flows, so it reflects the timing of deposits and withdrawals.

### Statistics and probability

For a sample, the standard deviation uses $n-1$: $s = \sqrt{\sum (X_i - \bar X)^2/(n-1)}$. For any distribution, Chebyshev's inequality says at least $1 - 1/k^2$ of observations lie within $k$ standard deviations of the mean. The coefficient of variation is $s/\bar X$. A positively skewed distribution has mean > median > mode.

The **normal distribution** is symmetric and fully described by its mean and standard deviation; the standardized value is $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$. Roughly 68%, 95% and 99% of observations lie within 1, 2 and 2.58 standard deviations.

Probability rules: $P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(AB)$; $P(AB) = P(A|B)P(B)$; expected value $E(X) = \sum p_i x_i$; covariance and correlation $\rho = \text{Cov}(X,Y)/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$. **Bayes' formula** updates a prior:

$$P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)\,P(A)}{P(B|A)P(A) + P(B|\bar A)P(\bar A)}.$$

### Hypothesis testing

State $H_0$ and $H_a$, choose the significance level $\alpha$ (the probability of a **Type I error**, rejecting a true null), compute the test statistic and compare with the critical value, or compare the **p-value** with $\alpha$: reject $H_0$ if $p < \alpha$. A **Type II error** is failing to reject a false null; power is $1 - P(\text{Type II})$. For a mean with unknown variance, $t = (\bar X - \mu_0)/(s/\sqrt n)$ with $n-1$ degrees of freedom.

### Regression and data

In simple linear regression $Y = b_0 + b_1 X + \varepsilon$, the slope test is $t = \hat b_1/s_{b_1}$ with $n-2$ degrees of freedom, and $R^2 = \text{SSR}/\text{SST}$ is the share of variation in $Y$ explained by $X$ (in simple regression, the squared correlation). Watch for **heteroskedasticity** and **serial correlation**, which can understate standard errors. In machine learning, **supervised** learning fits labeled data and **unsupervised** learning finds structure without labels; an **overfit** model fits noise and performs poorly out of sample.

### Technique

Use the calculator's power key and memory; write the setup before computing. For return questions ask "arithmetic or geometric, time- or money-weighted?" before anything else.

@example
title: A growing deposit and a compound return
problem: (a) A saver deposits \$3,000 at 6% a year compounded monthly. How much is it worth after 5 years? (b) A fund returns +10%, -20% and +30% in three years. Find its geometric mean annual return.
step: (a) The periodic rate is $0.06/12 = 0.005$ and there are $5 \times 12 = 60$ periods.
step: $FV = 3000 \times 1.005^{60} = 3000 \times 1.348850 = \$4{,}046.55$.
step: (b) Growth factors are $1.10$, $0.80$ and $1.30$; their product is $1.144$.
step: The geometric mean is $1.144^{1/3} - 1 = 1.0459 - 1 = 4.59\%$, below the arithmetic mean of 6.67%.
answer: (a) \$4,046.55; (b) about 4.59% a year.

@mistakes
- Using the arithmetic mean to describe compound growth over several years; the geometric mean is the growth rate that matches terminal wealth.
- Using $n$ instead of $n-1$ for a sample standard deviation, or $n-1$ instead of $n-2$ degrees of freedom in a simple regression.
- Reading a p-value as "the probability that the null is true" or a 5% test as "95% sure".
- Ignoring base rates in Bayes problems: a very accurate test can still produce mostly false positives when the event is rare.

@practice num
prompt: A researcher tests H0: mean monthly excess return = 0 using 25 monthly observations. The sample mean is 0.9% and the sample standard deviation is 2.4%. What is the t-statistic? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 1.88
tol: 0.01
check: 0.9 / (2.4 / sqrt(25))
hint: The standard error is $s/\sqrt{n}$.
hint: Divide the mean (minus the hypothesized value of 0) by the standard error.
solution: The standard error is $2.4/\sqrt{25} = 0.48$, so $t = 0.9/0.48 = 1.875 \approx 1.88$ with 24 degrees of freedom.

@practice mc
prompt: A researcher tests whether a strategy's mean excess return differs from zero at the 5% significance level. The test produces a p-value of 0.03. Which conclusion is correct?
choice*: Reject the null hypothesis, because the p-value is below the significance level
choice: Fail to reject the null hypothesis, because the p-value is not below 0.01
choice: Reject the null hypothesis, which means there is a 97% probability that the null is false
hint: Compare the p-value with the chosen significance level.
solution: The p-value is the smallest significance level at which the null can be rejected. Since 0.03 is less than 0.05, reject. A p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true or false, so the 97% statement is a misreading.

@practice num
prompt: Three percent of firms in a sector commit accounting fraud. A screening model flags 90% of fraudulent firms and also wrongly flags 10% of firms that are not fraudulent. A firm is flagged. What is the probability that it is fraudulent? Give the answer as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 21.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 0.03 * 0.90 / (0.03 * 0.90 + 0.97 * 0.10) * 100
hint: Find the probability of being flagged: the fraud route plus the false-alarm route.
hint: Divide the probability of "fraud and flagged" by the probability of "flagged".
solution: $P(\text{flag}) = 0.03(0.90) + 0.97(0.10) = 0.027 + 0.097 = 0.124$, so $P(\text{fraud}|\text{flag}) = 0.027/0.124 = 21.8\%$. Most flagged firms are false alarms because fraud is rare.

import math
assert math.isclose(annualized_return([0.01] * 12, 12), 1.01 ** 12 - 1, rel_tol=1e-9)
assert math.isclose(annualized_return([0.10, -0.10], 1), 0.99 ** 0.5 - 1, rel_tol=1e-9)

model:
```python
def annualized_return(returns, periods_per_year):
    """Geometric annualized return from periodic returns (decimals)."""
    growth = 1.0
    for r in returns:
        growth *= 1 + r
    return growth ** (periods_per_year / len(returns)) - 1
```
hint: Start a running growth factor at 1.0 and multiply by `1 + r` for each return.
hint: Raise the total growth factor to `periods_per_year / len(returns)`, then subtract 1.
solution: Linking returns multiplies growth factors, which handles losses correctly; the exponent converts the total over `len(returns)` periods into one year. An arithmetic average would overstate the result when returns are volatile, which the second test checks.
