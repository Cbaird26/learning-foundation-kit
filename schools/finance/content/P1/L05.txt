@article
Financial Statement Analysis carries 11-14% of the 2026 Level I exam (weights change each year; see cfainstitute.org), and it is split here across two weeks: this week covers the statements and the accounting choices behind them, next week covers ratios, taxes and quality of reporting.

### The statements and their links

The **income statement** reports revenue, expenses and net income for a period. The **balance sheet** reports assets, liabilities and equity at a date, with $\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Equity}$. The **cash flow statement** explains the change in cash through operating, investing and financing activities. They link: net income increases retained earnings, and the cash flow statement reconciles net income to the change in cash.

Under the **indirect method**, cash flow from operations (CFO) starts with net income, adds back noncash charges (depreciation, amortization), removes gains and adds losses on asset sales (those cash flows belong in investing), and adjusts for working capital: an **increase** in a current operating asset (receivables, inventory) is a use of cash, and an increase in a current operating liability (payables) is a source. US GAAP puts interest paid and received and dividends received in CFO and dividends paid in financing; IFRS allows interest and dividends received in either CFO or investing, and interest and dividends paid in CFO or financing.

### Revenue recognition

ASC 606 and IFRS 15 use the same **five-step model**: identify the contract; identify the performance obligations; determine the transaction price; allocate it to the obligations (by relative standalone selling prices); recognize revenue when (or as) each obligation is satisfied, which can be over time (for example a subscription, or a long-term construction contract with progress measured by cost incurred) or at a point in time (when control transfers).

### Inventory

Under rising prices and a stable or growing inventory, **FIFO** gives the lowest cost of goods sold (COGS), highest income and highest ending inventory; **LIFO** gives the highest COGS, lowest income and lowest taxes; weighted-average costing lies between. **LIFO is allowed under US GAAP but not under IFRS.** Companies using LIFO report a **LIFO reserve** (FIFO inventory minus LIFO inventory). To convert to FIFO, $\text{COGS}_{\text{FIFO}} = \text{COGS}_{\text{LIFO}} - \Delta\,\text{LIFO reserve}$ and $\text{Inventory}_{\text{FIFO}} = \text{Inventory}_{\text{LIFO}} + \text{LIFO reserve}$. A **LIFO liquidation** (selling old, low-cost layers) boosts income artificially. Inventory is reported at the lower of cost and net realizable value (US GAAP: for FIFO and average cost; lower of cost or market for LIFO), and writedowns can be reversed under IFRS but not under US GAAP.

### Long-lived assets

**Straight-line** depreciation is $(\text{cost} - \text{salvage})/\text{life}$. **Units of production** uses output relative to total expected output. The **double-declining balance** method applies twice the straight-line rate to beginning book value (ignoring salvage until the book value approaches it): year-1 expense $= 2/\text{life} \times \text{cost}$. Impairment: under US GAAP, an asset held for use is tested for recoverability using undiscounted cash flows, and any loss is written down to fair value and **cannot be reversed**; under IFRS, impairment compares carrying amount with the recoverable amount, and **reversals are allowed** (except for goodwill). Goodwill is not amortized under either framework for public companies; it is tested for impairment. (US private companies may elect to amortize it, but exam questions assume the public-company rules.)

### Leases

Under ASC 842 a lessee records a right-of-use asset and lease liability for leases over 12 months; a **finance lease** shows depreciation and interest, while an **operating lease** shows one straight-line lease cost. IFRS 16 has a single lessee model, like a finance lease, so interest and depreciation are reported for nearly all leases and operating cash flow and EBITDA rise relative to the old treatment.

### Technique

Draw the cash-flow signs before computing. For inventory problems, write the layers, then check your answer against the direction rule (rising prices: LIFO COGS is larger).

@example
title: Cash flow from operations and a FIFO conversion
problem: A firm reports net income of 410, depreciation of 90, a gain on sale of equipment of 25, an increase in receivables of 40, a decrease in inventory of 30 and an increase in payables of 15. Find CFO. Separately, a LIFO-using company has COGS of 5,000 and a LIFO reserve that rose from 120 to 150. Find its FIFO COGS.
step: Start with net income 410 and add back depreciation: $410 + 90 = 500$.
step: Remove the gain, because the sale proceeds are an investing inflow: $500 - 25 = 475$.
step: Working capital: receivables up is a use ($-40$), inventory down is a source ($+30$), payables up is a source ($+15$): $475 - 40 + 30 + 15 = 480$.
step: FIFO COGS $= 5{,}000 - (150 - 120) = 4{,}970$.
answer: CFO = 480; FIFO COGS = 4,970.

@mistakes
- Adding an increase in receivables or inventory to CFO; an increase in an operating asset consumes cash.
- Leaving a gain on sale in CFO instead of subtracting it (and reporting the proceeds in investing).
- Converting from LIFO to FIFO with the level of the LIFO reserve instead of its change when adjusting COGS.
- Saying LIFO gives the highest income in a period of rising prices; it gives the lowest, along with the lowest taxes.

prompt: A retailer begins the period with 200 units at \$10 each and buys 300 more units at \$12 each. It sells 350 units. Using the periodic LIFO method, what is cost of goods sold? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 4100
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 300 * 12 + 50 * 10
hint: LIFO sells the most recently purchased units first.
hint: All 300 units at \$12 are sold, plus 50 of the older units at \$10.
solution: COGS $= 300 \times 12 + 50 \times 10 = 3{,}600 + 500 = \$4{,}100$. FIFO would give $200 \times 10 + 150 \times 12 = \$3{,}800$, lower because prices rose.

@practice mc
prompt: On January 1 a company receives \$1,200 in cash for a 12-month software subscription that gives the customer continuous access throughout the year. The company has one performance obligation. How is the revenue recognized under ASC 606 and IFRS 15?
choice: All \$1,200 on January 1, because the cash was received
choice*: \$100 a month over the year, as access is provided, with the unearned portion a liability until then
choice: All \$1,200 on December 31, when the contract ends
hint: Revenue follows the satisfaction of the performance obligation, not the receipt of cash.
solution: The customer receives and consumes the benefit over time, so the obligation is satisfied over time and revenue is recognized ratably. The cash received in advance creates a contract liability (unearned revenue) that falls by \$100 each month.

@practice num
prompt: A machine costs \$120,000, has a salvage value of \$12,000 and a six-year life. Using the double-declining balance method (rate = twice the straight-line rate), what is depreciation expense in year 2? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 26667
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: (120000 - (2 / 6) * 120000) * (2 / 6)
hint: The DDB rate is $2/6 = 33.3\%$ of beginning book value.
hint: Year-1 depreciation is $0.3333 \times 120{,}000 = 40{,}000$; book value at the start of year 2 is \$80,000.
solution: Year-1 depreciation is \$40,000, leaving a book value of \$80,000; year-2 depreciation is $80{,}000 \times 1/3 = \$26{,}667$ (salvage does not enter until the book value would fall below it).

prompt: A firm reports net income of \$540 million, depreciation of \$120 million, a gain on sale of equipment of \$15 million, an increase in accounts receivable of \$60 million, a decrease in inventory of \$35 million and an increase in accounts payable of \$20 million. Using the indirect method, what is cash flow from operations? Give your answer in millions of dollars.
answer: 640
lead: \$
tol: 0.5
check: 540 + 120 - 15 - 60 + 35 + 20
hint: Add noncash charges, subtract gains, then adjust for each working capital change by its effect on cash.
solution: $540 + 120 - 15 - 60 + 35 + 20 = \$640$ million. The receivables increase uses cash; the inventory decrease and payables increase provide it.

@practice num
prompt: A company uses LIFO and reports COGS of \$8,400 thousand. Its LIFO reserve was \$300 thousand at the start of the year and \$380 thousand at the end. What would COGS have been under FIFO, in thousands of dollars?
answer: 8320
lead: \$
tol: 0.5
check: 8400 - (380 - 300)
hint: Use the change in the LIFO reserve, not its ending level.
hint: A rising reserve means LIFO COGS exceeds FIFO COGS.
solution: $\text{COGS}_{\text{FIFO}} = 8{,}400 - (380 - 300) = \$8{,}320$ thousand. The reserve grew because prices rose, so FIFO COGS is lower and FIFO income higher by 80 before tax.

@quiz num
prompt: A machine costs \$90,000 and has a salvage value of \$6,000. It is expected to produce 420,000 units over its life, and it produces 75,000 units this year. Using the units-of-production method, what is depreciation expense for the year? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 15000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: (90000 - 6000) / 420000 * 75000
hint: Find the depreciable base, then the cost per unit.
hint: The cost per unit is \$0.20.
solution: The depreciable base is $90{,}000 - 6{,}000 = 84{,}000$, or $0.20$ per unit; $0.20 \times 75{,}000 = \$15{,}000$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A company records an impairment loss on equipment held for use. Two years later the equipment's recoverable amount rises well above its written-down carrying amount. Which statement is correct?
choice: Neither IFRS nor US GAAP permits the loss to be reversed
choice*: IFRS permits the loss to be reversed (up to the carrying amount without impairment), while US GAAP does not for assets held for use
choice: US GAAP permits reversal, while IFRS does not
hint: Compare the two frameworks' treatment of reversals.
solution: Under IFRS an impairment loss on an asset other than goodwill can be reversed, limited to what the carrying amount would have been without the impairment. Under US GAAP the written-down amount becomes the new cost basis for assets held for use, so reversal is not allowed.
