### Pricing and yield

A bond's price is the present value of its coupons and principal at its yield to maturity (YTM): $P = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C}{(1+y)^t} + \frac{F}{(1+y)^n}$, where $y$ is the periodic yield and $n$ the number of periods. US corporate bonds pay semiannual coupons and quote a **bond-equivalent yield** (twice the semiannual rate); the effective annual yield is $(1 + y_{\text{semi}})^2 - 1$. Price and yield move in opposite directions. A bond sells at a **premium** when the coupon rate exceeds the yield, at **par** when equal and at a **discount** when below, and the price converges to par at maturity (constant-yield price path). Other measures: **current yield** = annual coupon / price; yield to call for callable bonds; yield to worst; and for floating-rate notes, the quoted margin over a reference rate (SOFR is the main US dollar reference rate since LIBOR ended).

### Term structure

**Spot rates** discount single cash flows. Forward rates follow from no arbitrage: $(1 + z_2)^2 = (1 + z_1)(1 + f_{1,1})$, so the one-year rate one year ahead is $f_{1,1} = (1 + z_2)^2/(1 + z_1) - 1$. A normal yield curve slopes upward. Theories: the pure expectations theory (forward rates equal expected future spot rates), liquidity preference (forward rates include a term premium) and market segmentation. A **bond's price from spot rates** discounts each cash flow at its own spot rate.

### Interest rate risk

**Macaulay duration** is the weighted average time to receipt of cash flows, using present values as weights. **Modified duration** $= \text{Macaulay}/(1 + y)$ (periodic yield) estimates the percentage price change for a small change in yield: $\%\Delta P \approx -\text{ModDur} \times \Delta y$. **Convexity** corrects for the curvature: $\%\Delta P \approx -\text{ModDur}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,\text{Convexity}\,(\Delta y)^2$, which is positive for an option-free bond, so duration alone overstates losses and understates gains. **Effective duration** $= (P_- - P_+)/(2\,P_0\,\Delta y)$ is used when cash flows depend on the yield (callable bonds, mortgage-backed securities). Duration is higher for longer maturity, lower coupon and lower yield. A callable bond has negative convexity when yields fall, because the issuer calls.

### Credit and securitization

Credit risk has two parts: **default probability** and **loss given default** (so expected loss $=$ PD $\times$ LGD). Investment-grade bonds are rated BBB- or higher (Baa3 by Moody's). Analysts use the four Cs (capacity, collateral, covenants, character) and ratios such as debt to EBITDA. The **credit spread** compensates for default risk and liquidity. **Securitization** pools loans (mortgages, auto loans, credit cards) into a special purpose entity that issues securities; credit enhancement includes subordination, overcollateralization and reserve accounts. In a **mortgage-backed security**, prepayment risk takes two forms: **contraction risk** (prepayments rise when rates fall) and **extension risk** (prepayments slow when rates rise). Collateralized mortgage obligations redistribute these risks among tranches.

### Technique

Always match the periodic yield with the period: semiannual coupon, semiannual yield, twice the years. For duration questions, check the sign and the order of magnitude: a modified duration near 6 means roughly 6% change per 1% change in yield.

@example
title: Price, duration and a price estimate
problem: A 3-year bond pays a 6% annual coupon (par 100) and its yield to maturity is 5%. Find its price and modified duration, and estimate the percentage price change if the yield rises by 1 percentage point.
step: Price $= 6/1.05 + 6/1.05^2 + 106/1.05^3 = 5.714 + 5.442 + 91.567 = 102.72$.
step: Macaulay duration $= \frac{1(5.714) + 2(5.442) + 3(91.567)}{102.72} = 2.84$ years.
step: Modified duration $= 2.84/1.05 = 2.70$.
step: $\%\Delta P \approx -2.70 \times 0.01 = -2.70\%$; the price falls to about 99.95 (the exact price at 6% is 100.00, so convexity adds a little).
answer: Price 102.72; modified duration 2.70; price falls about 2.70%.

@mistakes
- Using the annual yield with semiannual coupons (or forgetting to double the number of periods).
- Mixing up the direction: when yields rise, prices fall, and more for a longer-duration bond.
- Applying the duration approximation to a large yield change without a convexity adjustment, or using convexity with the wrong sign.
- Treating contraction and extension risk as the same: contraction risk rises when rates fall, extension risk when they rise.

model:
```python
def price_bond(face, coupon_rate, ytm, years, freq=2):
    """Price of a bond on a coupon date, discounting at ytm / freq."""
    periods = round(years * freq)
    coupon = face * coupon_rate / freq
    rate = ytm / freq
    price = 0.0
    for t in range(1, periods + 1):
        price += coupon / (1 + rate) ** t
    return price + face / (1 + rate) ** periods
```
hint: The coupon per period is `face * coupon_rate / freq` and the periodic yield is `ytm / freq`.
hint: Sum the discounted coupons in a loop, then add the discounted face value at the last period.
solution: Convert everything to the period: the number of periods is `years * freq`, the coupon is the annual coupon divided by `freq`, and the periodic yield is `ytm / freq`. The price is the present value of each coupon plus the face value, which the zero-coupon test and the par-bond test check.
