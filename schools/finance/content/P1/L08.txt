### Market organization

A **market order** executes immediately at the best available price (certainty of execution, not of price). A **limit order** executes only at the limit price or better (certainty of price, not of execution): a buy limit below the market waits. A stop order becomes a market order when a trigger price is reached. In a **margin purchase**, an investor borrows part of the cost: the initial margin requirement is the equity share, and a **margin call** occurs when equity falls below the maintenance margin. Margin amplifies gains and losses. In a **short sale**, the investor borrows shares, sells them, and later buys them back; the short seller's loss is unlimited in principle. Stock trades settle T+1 in the United States (since May 28, 2024). Markets differ as quote-driven (dealer) or order-driven; market integrity depends on transparency and surveillance, and trading venues include exchanges, alternative trading systems and dark pools.

### Indexes

A **price-weighted index** sums the prices and divides by a divisor: $\text{Index} = \sum P_i/D$. It is affected by stock splits, so the divisor is adjusted to keep the index unchanged: $D_{\text{new}} = \sum P_{\text{new}}/\text{Index}_{\text{old}}$. A **market-capitalization-weighted** index weights each stock by its market value (large stocks dominate); a float-adjusted index uses only freely traded shares; an **equal-weighted** index gives each stock the same weight and needs rebalancing, with a bias toward small stocks. Index construction affects the benchmark's return.

### Market efficiency

**Weak form**: prices reflect all past market data, so technical analysis cannot earn abnormal returns. **Semi-strong form**: prices reflect all public information, so fundamental analysis cannot either. **Strong form**: prices reflect all information, including private; evidence that insiders earn abnormal returns contradicts the strong form but not the semi-strong form. Anomalies (value, size, momentum, calendar effects) may be compensation for risk, data mining or limits to arbitrage. Behavioral finance studies biases such as overconfidence and loss aversion.

### Valuation

The **dividend discount model**: $V_0 = \sum D_t/(1 + r)^t$. For constant growth, the **Gordon growth model** gives $V_0 = D_1/(r - g)$, with $D_1 = D_0(1 + g)$ and $r > g$. Sustainable growth is $g = b \times \text{ROE}$, where $b$ is the retention rate. The justified forward P/E is $V_0/E_1 = (D_1/E_1)/(r - g)$.

The **cost of equity** from the CAPM is $r = r_f + \beta\,(E[R_M] - r_f)$.

**Multiples**: P/E, P/B, P/S, and enterprise-value multiples. Enterprise value $\text{EV} = \text{market capitalization} + \text{debt} + \text{preferred stock} + \text{minority interest} - \text{cash and equivalents}$, and EV/EBITDA compares companies with different capital structures. Equity value from an EV multiple is $\text{EV} - \text{net debt}$.

**Residual income**: earnings above the equity charge. $\text{RI}_t = E_t - r\,B_{t-1}$, and $V_0 = B_0 + \sum \text{RI}_t/(1+r)^t$; with residual income growing at a constant $g$, $V_0 = B_0 + \text{RI}_1/(r - g)$. It works well when dividends are zero or cash flows are negative.

### Technique

On valuation questions, write the period of each cash flow before discounting; the most common errors are using $D_0$ instead of $D_1$ and mixing percents with decimals. For efficiency questions, ask: what information set does the evidence use?

@example
title: Enterprise value and a peer multiple
problem: A company has a market capitalization of 1,800, debt of 700, cash of 200, EBITDA of 350 and 150 shares outstanding. Peers trade at 7.0 times EV/EBITDA. What is the company's current EV/EBITDA, and what value per share does the peer multiple imply?
step: $\text{EV} = 1{,}800 + 700 - 200 = 2{,}300$.
step: Current multiple $= 2{,}300/350 = 6.57$ times, below the peers' 7.0.
step: Implied EV at the peer multiple $= 7.0 \times 350 = 2{,}450$.
step: Implied equity value $= 2{,}450 - (700 - 200) = 1{,}950$, or $1{,}950/150 = 13.00$ per share.
answer: 6.57 times now; the peer multiple implies 13.00 a share.

@mistakes
- Using $D_0$ rather than $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$ in the Gordon growth model, or applying it when $g \ge r$.
- Forgetting to adjust a price-weighted index divisor after a stock split.
- Concluding that evidence of insider profits refutes semi-strong efficiency; it refutes strong-form efficiency.
- Using gross debt rather than net debt when moving between enterprise value and equity value.

answer: 46.67
lead: \$

check: 2.00 * 1.05 / (0.095 - 0.05)

@practice num
prompt: A price-weighted index contains three stocks priced at \$40, \$25 and \$60, and the divisor is 3. The \$60 stock then splits 2-for-1 (so its price becomes \$30). What is the new divisor, to two decimal places, that keeps the index unchanged?
answer: 2.28
tol: 0.01
check: (40 + 25 + 30) / ((40 + 25 + 60) / 3)
hint: The old index value is the sum of prices divided by 3.
hint: Divide the new price sum by the old index value.
solution: The old index is $125/3 = 41.667$. The new price sum is $40 + 25 + 30 = 95$, so $D_{\text{new}} = 95/41.667 = 2.28$.

@practice mc
prompt: A study finds that corporate insiders consistently earn abnormal profits by trading in their own company's stock, but that analysts using only public information cannot earn abnormal profits. This evidence is most consistent with which conclusion about market efficiency?
choice: Markets are strong-form efficient and semi-strong-form inefficient
choice*: Markets are semi-strong-form efficient but not strong-form efficient
choice: Markets are weak-form inefficient and semi-strong-form efficient
hint: Which information set earns abnormal profits, and which does not?
solution: If analysts using public information cannot beat the market, prices reflect public information (semi-strong efficiency). Insiders profiting from private information means prices do not reflect all information, so the strong form fails.

@practice num
prompt: The risk-free rate is 3.0%, the expected market return is 8.5% and a stock has a beta of 1.2. What is the stock's required return under the CAPM? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 9.6
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 3.0 + 1.2 * (8.5 - 3.0)
hint: The market risk premium is the market return minus the risk-free rate.
solution: The market risk premium is $8.5\% - 3.0\% = 5.5\%$, so $r = 3.0\% + 1.2 \times 5.5\% = 9.6\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: A company has a book value of \$25 per share and is expected to earn \$3.50 per share next year. The cost of equity is 10%, and residual income is expected to grow at 3% a year forever from next year's level. What is the value per share using the residual income model? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 39.29
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 25 + (3.50 - 0.10 * 25) / (0.10 - 0.03)
hint: Residual income is earnings minus the equity charge, $r \times B_0$.
hint: $\text{RI}_1 = 3.50 - 0.10 \times 25 = 1.00$.
solution: $\text{RI}_1 = 3.50 - 2.50 = 1.00$; $V_0 = 25 + 1.00/(0.10 - 0.03) = 25 + 14.29 = \$39.29$.

@quiz mc
prompt: An investor places a limit order to buy shares at \$48 when the stock is trading at \$50 with the best ask at \$50.05. Which statement is correct?
choice: The order executes immediately at \$50.05, because limit orders always execute at the best ask
choice*: The order waits and executes only if the price falls to \$48 or lower, so execution is not certain
choice: The order is converted into a market order if it is not filled within one day
hint: A limit price is the worst price the investor will accept.
solution: A buy limit order executes only at the limit price or better. With the best ask above the limit, the order rests in the book; the investor has price certainty but not execution certainty.
