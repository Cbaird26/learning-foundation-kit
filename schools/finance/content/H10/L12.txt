@article
Speaking and listening are communication skills you can practice like any other. A strong talk is planned, rehearsed and focused on the audience.

### Plan around audience and purpose

Ask: Who is listening? What do they already know? What do I want them to understand or do? Your purpose (inform, persuade, or both) shapes your content. Limit yourself to **one main message** and **two to four supporting points**; a five-minute talk at a comfortable pace is roughly 600 to 750 words.

### Structure

- **Opening**: an attention-getter (a short story, surprising fact or question), the topic and why it matters, and a preview of your points.
- **Body**: each point stated clearly, supported by evidence or examples, with **transitions** ("Next," "The second reason is...").
- **Closing**: restate the main message and end with a memorable final thought or call to action.

Speaking is not reading an essay aloud. Use **shorter sentences**, repetition for emphasis, and plain words.

### Delivery

- **Voice**: speak loudly enough to be heard at the back, vary pitch and volume, and slow down on key points. Use **pauses** deliberately.
- **Pace**: nerves make people speed up; practice at a pace slightly slower than conversation.
- **Eye contact**: look at people, not your notes or the screen.
- **Body language**: stand steadily, use natural gestures, and avoid pacing or fidgeting.
- **Fillers**: reduce "um" and "like" by pausing silently instead.
- **Notes**: use brief key-word cards or an outline, not a full script.

### Visual aids

Slides support the talk; they do not replace it. Use **few words, large type, high contrast** and one idea per slide. Never read slides aloud word for word. Cite sources for images and data.

### Rehearsal

Practice aloud several times, time yourself, and rehearse in front of a friend. Prepare for questions by guessing what the audience will ask.

### Listening

**Active listening** means giving full attention, noting the main message and supporting points, and withholding judgment until the speaker finishes. Take brief notes, and ask **clarifying** questions ("Do you mean...?") and **probing** questions ("What evidence supports that?"). Listen critically for appeals, evidence, bias and fallacies.

### Group discussion

In a discussion, build on others' ideas, refer to specific evidence, disagree respectfully and make sure everyone can speak.

@example
title: Planning a five-minute talk
problem: You must give a five-minute talk on why teenagers need more sleep. Outline it.
step: Choose one main message: Teens need more sleep to learn and stay healthy.
step: Choose three points: (1) biology shifts teens' sleep timing, (2) lack of sleep hurts learning and mood, (3) simple habits help.
step: Plan the opening and close: open with a question ("When did you last wake up feeling rested?"), close by returning to the question with a call to action.
step: Estimate length: about 120 words for the opening and close and about 150 words per point is around 600 words, roughly five minutes at a normal pace.

@mistakes
- Reading a full script instead of speaking from brief notes.
- Putting paragraphs of text on slides and reading them aloud.
- Speaking too fast when nervous.
- Listening only for what you want to rebut instead of what the speaker is saying.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: Outline a five-minute talk on a topic from this course. Write your main message, two or three points with evidence, an attention-getting opening, and a closing line. Then list two delivery goals for rehearsal.
hint: One main message, short points, and a plan to time yourself.
solution: Main message: Careful word choice makes writing clearer. Opening: a vague sentence versus a revised one, read aloud to the audience. Point 1: precise verbs replace weak modifiers (example: "very tired" to "exhausted"). Point 2: cutting filler and redundancy shortens a sentence without losing meaning (example: "in order to" to "to"). Point 3: connotation affects tone (thrifty versus cheap). Closing: return to the opening pair and ask which one the audience would rather read. Delivery goals: slow down on key points and look at the audience instead of the cards.
rubric: Has one clear main message and two or three supported points.
rubric: The opening is attention-getting and the closing returns to the message.
rubric: Lists two specific delivery goals.
