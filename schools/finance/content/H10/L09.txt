@article
A **literary analysis essay** makes an argument about how a text creates meaning. It is not a plot summary and not a review. Your reader has read the work; your job is to show something about it they might have missed.

### The thesis

A **thesis** is one or two sentences that state your interpretive claim. A good thesis is **arguable** (a reasonable reader could disagree), **specific**, and **supported by the text**. Compare:

- Weak: "Macbeth is about ambition." (a topic everyone accepts)
- Stronger: "In *Macbeth*, Shakespeare uses images of sleep to show that guilt destroys the peace ambition promises."

A strong thesis often names the technique and the effect: *By [technique], the author shows [meaning].*

### Structure

- **Introduction**: hook, brief context (title, author), thesis.
- **Body paragraphs**: each develops one reason supporting the thesis.
- **Conclusion**: restate the thesis in fresh words and explain why it matters; do not introduce a new argument.

### The body paragraph: a model

Use a pattern such as **claim, evidence, analysis, link**:

1. **Topic sentence** states the paragraph's claim, tied to the thesis.
2. **Evidence**: a short, relevant quotation or detail, introduced with a signal phrase.
3. **Analysis**: explain *how* the evidence supports the claim, examining specific words or devices. Analysis should be longer than the quotation.
4. **Link**: connect back to the thesis.

### Embedding quotations

Use short quotations woven into your own sentence: When Lady Macbeth asks whether her hands will "ne'er be clean," her language reveals guilt that she cannot repress. Use a **signal phrase** ("the narrator observes," "she insists") and the exact words in quotation marks. For poetry, mark line breaks with a slash. In MLA style, cite the author's last name and page number (or act, scene and line for plays) in parentheses; writing about literature uses the **present tense** ("Shakespeare shows," not "showed").

### Revising

Check: Does each paragraph prove the thesis? Is each quotation followed by analysis? Have you avoided retelling the plot? Could you cut any sentence that only summarizes?

@example
title: Strengthening a thesis
problem: Turn the weak thesis "The Great Gatsby is about the American Dream" into an arguable, specific thesis. Use only this fact: the novel's green light is Gatsby's symbol of desire.
step: Identify the problem: a topic everyone agrees on, with no claim about how or why.
step: Add technique: the green light as a symbol.
step: Add the interpretation: the symbol shows the dream as always out of reach.
step: Thesis: "By making the green light a symbol of what Gatsby can see but never touch, Fitzgerald suggests that the American Dream is built on longing that cannot be satisfied."

@mistakes
- Writing a thesis that merely states a fact or topic.
- Letting quotations stand alone with no analysis.
- Summarizing plot instead of interpreting.
- Switching tense: using past tense for events in a story's present.

@practice self
prompt: Write one body paragraph (five to seven sentences) arguing that the setting in this original passage creates a lonely mood: "The station was empty except for one flickering lamp. Rain tapped the tin roof. Anna checked the timetable twice, though no train was due." Use a topic sentence, a quotation, and analysis.
hint: Make a claim, quote a short phrase, then explain what the word choice makes readers feel.
solution: The empty station sets a lonely, uncertain mood. The narrator notes that the station is "empty except for one flickering lamp," a detail that stresses absence while the unsteady light suggests fragile comfort. Rain "tapped the tin roof," a small repeating sound that fills the silence rather than relieving it. Anna's act of checking the timetable "twice, though no train was due" shows how loneliness makes her cling to routine. Together these details make the station a place of waiting without hope, which supports the story's mood of isolation.
rubric: Begins with a clear topic sentence that makes a claim.
rubric: Embeds at least one accurate quotation with a signal phrase.
rubric: Analyzes word choice or imagery and uses present tense.
