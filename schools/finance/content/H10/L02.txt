@article
A poem compresses meaning into a small space, so every choice carries weight: a word, a line break, a rhyme. Start by asking who is speaking, to whom and why.

### Speaker and situation

The **speaker** is the voice in the poem, not automatically the poet. Identify the situation (what is happening, where, when) and the speaker's attitude, or **tone**.

### Imagery and figurative language

**Imagery** appeals to the senses. **Figurative language** says one thing in terms of another:

- **Simile**: comparison using *like* or *as* ("the road was like a ribbon of ash").
- **Metaphor**: direct comparison ("the road was a ribbon of ash").
- **Personification**: giving human qualities to non-human things.
- **Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration. **Symbol**: a concrete thing that stands for an idea.

### Sound

- **Alliteration**: repeated initial consonant sounds ("silent, slipping snow").
- **Assonance**: repeated vowel sounds in nearby words. **Consonance**: repeated consonants.
- **Rhyme**: matching end sounds, labeled by letters (ABAB, AABB). **Onomatopoeia**: words that imitate sounds.

### Meter and form

**Meter** is the pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables. A **foot** is one unit of the pattern. An **iamb** is unstressed-stressed (da-DUM); **iambic pentameter** has five iambs, ten syllables per line. A **trochee** is the reverse (DUM-da). **Free verse** has no fixed meter or rhyme but still uses rhythm and line breaks deliberately.

Common forms: a **sonnet** has 14 lines. The Shakespearean sonnet rhymes ABAB CDCD EFEF GG and often ends with a couplet that comments on the rest; the Petrarchan sonnet has an octave and sestet. A **stanza** is a group of lines. A **turn** (volta) is a shift in thought or tone, often signaled by a word such as "but."

### A method: read three times

1. First read: get the literal situation. Who, what, where?
2. Second read: mark images, figures, sound patterns, and shifts.
3. Third read: ask how these choices create the poem's meaning, then state it in a sentence.

Always connect technique to effect. "The poem uses alliteration" is only a label; "the repeated *s* sounds make the snowfall feel hushed" is analysis.

@example
title: Scanning a line
problem: Mark the stresses in the line "The silent snow lay soft upon the hill" and name the meter.
step: Count syllables: The-si-lent-snow-lay-soft-up-on-the-hill, which is 10 syllables.
step: Mark stress: the SI-lent SNOW lay SOFT up-ON the HILL. The pattern alternates unstressed and stressed.
step: Each pair is an iamb, and there are five pairs, so the line is iambic pentameter.
step: Effect: the even, steady rhythm and the soft *s* sounds suit the quiet scene.

@mistakes
- Treating the speaker as the poet without evidence.
- Naming devices (simile, alliteration) without explaining their effect.
- Confusing metaphor and simile: a simile uses *like* or *as*.
- Assuming a poem must rhyme; free verse is real poetry.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: Choose a poem of 8 to 16 lines that you have permission to mark up (a free reading passage works). Annotate it for speaker, two images or figures, one sound device and the turn, then write a three-sentence statement of its meaning.
hint: Annotate first, then turn your margin notes into one statement of meaning.
solution: A model annotation names the speaker and situation, circles images and figures with margin notes of their effect (for example, "personification makes the wind menacing"), marks an alliteration or assonance pattern, notes where the tone shifts, and ends with three sentences such as: "The speaker looks back on a childhood home with longing. Warm sensory images give way after the turn to bare, cold images, showing that the place no longer exists as remembered. The poem suggests that memory preserves what time takes away."
rubric: Annotations identify speaker, two images or figures, a sound device and the turn.
rubric: Each annotation explains an effect, not only a label.
rubric: The final statement claims a meaning that the annotations support.
