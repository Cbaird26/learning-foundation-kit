@article
A stock's **intrinsic value** is the present value of the cash flows its owner will receive, discounted at a rate that reflects their risk. If intrinsic value exceeds the market price, the stock looks cheap, provided the inputs are right. This lesson is an overview; a full course on equity valuation would develop each method and its adjustments.

### The dividend discount model

A shareholder receives dividends $D_1, D_2, \dots$ at the end of each year and may sell the stock later. Since the future price is itself the present value of later dividends, the stock's value today is

$$V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{D_t}{(1+r)^t},$$

where $r$ is the required return on equity, usually from the CAPM ($r = r_f + \beta[E(R_M) - r_f]$). This is the **dividend discount model** (DDM). All rates here are annual and dividends arrive at year-end.

### Gordon growth

If dividends grow forever at a constant rate $g$ with $g < r$, the sum collapses to the **Gordon growth model**:

$$V_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g} = \frac{D_0(1+g)}{r - g}.$$

Rearranged, the implied growth rate for a given price is $g = r - D_1/P_0$. The value is very sensitive to $r - g$: when the two rates are close, a small change in either moves the price a lot.

The growth rate comes from reinvestment. If the firm retains the fraction $b$ of earnings (the **retention ratio**; the **payout ratio** is $1-b$) and earns a return on equity $ROE$ on its new investment, then the **sustainable growth rate** is

$$g = b \times ROE.$$

### Multistage growth

Few firms grow at one rate forever. A **multistage model** forecasts dividends explicitly for $n$ years and then assumes a stable growth rate $g_L$ afterwards. The terminal value at time $n$ is $TV_n = D_{n+1}/(r - g_L) = D_n(1+g_L)/(r - g_L)$, and

$$V_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{D_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{TV_n}{(1+r)^n}.$$

### Multiples

Practitioners also value stocks by **multiples** applied to comparable firms. The **price-to-earnings** ratio (P/E) divides price by earnings per share; the DDM implies a *justified* forward P/E:

$$\frac{P_0}{E_1} = \frac{1 - b}{r - g}.$$

Enterprise value ($EV$ = market value of equity plus debt minus cash) divided by EBITDA allows comparison across firms with different leverage. To convert $EV$ to the value of equity per share, subtract net debt and divide by shares outstanding:

$$P = \frac{EV - \text{net debt}}{\text{shares outstanding}}.$$

Other multiples include price-to-book and EV/sales. Multiples are quick and use the market's pricing of similar firms, but they inherit its errors, and no two firms are truly comparable in growth, risk and accounting.

### Free cash flow approaches

Firms that pay no dividends are valued by discounting **free cash flow to equity** (FCFE) at the cost of equity, or **free cash flow to the firm** (FCFF) at the weighted average cost of capital and then subtracting debt. The mechanics mirror the DDM. Whichever model you use, the forecast of growth and the discount rate drive the answer; always test the result with a range of reasonable inputs. Use EDGAR for a real company's filings.

@example
title: Gordon growth and its sensitivity
problem: A firm just paid an annual dividend of \$2.00. Dividends are expected to grow at 4% forever and the required return is 9%. Find the value, and the value if growth were 5%.
step: $D_1 = 2.00(1.04) = \$2.08$, so $V_0 = 2.08/(0.09 - 0.04) = \$41.60$.
step: With $g = 5\%$: $D_1 = 2.00(1.05) = \$2.10$ and $V_0 = 2.10/(0.09 - 0.05) = \$52.50$.
step: A one-point rise in growth raises the value by 26%, because the denominator $r - g$ shrank from 5% to 4%.
answer: \$41.60 at 4% growth and \$52.50 at 5%.

@example
title: A two-stage dividend discount model
problem: A firm just paid \$1.50. Dividends grow 12% a year for three years and 4% a year after that. The required return is 10%. Value the stock.
step: Dividends: $D_1 = 1.68$, $D_2 = 1.8816$, $D_3 = 2.1074$.
step: Present values at 10%: $1.68/1.1 = 1.5273$, $1.8816/1.21 = 1.5550$, $2.1074/1.331 = 1.5834$, a total of \$4.6656.
step: Terminal value at year 3: $D_4 = 2.1074(1.04) = 2.1917$ and $TV_3 = 2.1917/(0.10 - 0.04) = \$36.528$. Its present value is $36.528/1.331 = \$27.444$.
step: $V_0 = 4.666 + 27.444 = \$32.11$. About 85% of the value comes from the terminal value, which is why the long-run growth assumption matters so much.
answer: \$32.11.

@mistakes
- Using $D_0$ instead of $D_1$ in the numerator of the Gordon growth model.
- Choosing a growth rate that is greater than or equal to the required return; the formula then gives a meaningless or negative value.
- Forgetting to discount the terminal value back from year $n$ to today.
- Applying a P/E from one firm to another with very different growth or risk, or applying EV/EBITDA and then forgetting to subtract net debt.

model:
```python
def ddm_value(dividends, terminal_growth, r):
    """Multistage dividend discount model with a Gordon terminal value."""
    if terminal_growth >= r:
        raise ValueError("terminal growth must be below the required return")
    n = len(dividends)
    pv_dividends = sum(d / (1 + r) ** t for t, d in enumerate(dividends, start=1))
    terminal_value = dividends[-1] * (1 + terminal_growth) / (r - terminal_growth)
    return pv_dividends + terminal_value / (1 + r) ** n
```
hint: The first dividend is at time 1, so use `enumerate(dividends, start=1)`.
hint: The terminal value is as of year `n`, so divide it by `(1 + r) ** n`.
solution: Discount each explicit dividend by its year, compute the Gordon terminal value at year $n$ from the last dividend, discount that back $n$ years and add. With a single dividend and constant growth the result equals the one-stage Gordon value, which the second test checks.
