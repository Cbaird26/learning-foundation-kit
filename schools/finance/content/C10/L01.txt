@article
An economy's wealth is its **real assets**: land, buildings, machines, knowledge and the skills of its workers. **Financial assets** are claims on the income those real assets generate. A share of stock is a claim on a firm's profits, a bond is a claim on promised payments from a borrower, and a derivative's value depends on another asset's price. Investing means giving up consumption today in exchange for claims that you hope will pay more later, and the whole subject of this course is how to measure, price and manage the risk in that bargain.

### Asset classes

| Asset class | What it is | Typical role |
|---|---|---|
| Cash equivalents | Treasury bills, bank deposits, money market instruments | Liquidity, lowest risk |
| Fixed income | Bonds and loans with promised payments | Income, moderate risk |
| Equities | Ownership claims on firms | Growth, higher risk |
| Derivatives | Options, futures, swaps | Hedging, leverage |
| Real assets and alternatives | Real estate, commodities, private equity, hedge funds | Diversification, illiquidity |

Higher expected return generally comes with higher risk, but the link is only an average relationship. Later weeks make it precise.

### Markets and intermediaries

In the **primary market** a firm sells new securities to raise capital, for example in an initial public offering (IPO), usually with an investment bank underwriting the sale. In the **secondary market** investors trade existing securities with one another; the issuer receives nothing from these trades. A **broker** acts as your agent and earns a commission; a **dealer** trades for its own account and quotes a **bid** (the price it will pay) and an **ask** (the price at which it will sell). The **bid-ask spread** is a cost of trading: a round trip buys at the ask and sells at the bid. US stock trades settle one business day after the trade (T+1, since May 28, 2024).

A **market order** executes at the best available price now. A **limit order** executes only at your stated price or better, so it controls price but risks not trading. The SEC regulates securities markets and public company disclosure, FINRA is the self-regulatory organization for broker-dealers, and SIPC protects brokerage customers' assets if a member firm fails (not against market losses).

### Market indexes

An index summarizes a market. A **price-weighted index**, such as the Dow Jones Industrial Average, sums the prices of its stocks and divides by a **divisor**. After a stock split the divisor is reset so that the index level does not jump:

$$\text{Index} = \frac{\sum_i P_i}{D}, \qquad D_{\text{new}} = \frac{\sum_i P_i^{\text{after split}}}{\text{Index before split}}.$$

A **value-weighted** (market-capitalization-weighted) index weights each stock by price times shares outstanding; the S&P 500 is value-weighted, using only the shares available to the public (float-adjusted). With a base level $I_0$,

$$I_1 = I_0 \cdot \frac{\sum_i P_{i,1} N_i}{\sum_i P_{i,0} N_i}.$$

### Buying on margin and selling short

Buying on margin means borrowing from your broker to buy more stock. Under Federal Reserve Regulation T the initial margin is 50% of the purchase price; **margin** is your equity divided by the market value of the position. Leverage magnifies returns in both directions. If you buy at $P_0$ with initial margin $m_0$, and the maintenance margin is $m_m$, a margin call occurs when the price falls to

$$P^{*} = \frac{P_0 (1 - m_0)}{1 - m_m}.$$

In a **short sale** you borrow shares, sell them, and later buy them back to return them. You profit if the price falls, owe any dividends paid while you are short, and have unlimited potential loss, because a price has no upper bound.

This is general education, not investment advice.

@example
title: A price-weighted index through a split
problem: A price-weighted index holds three stocks priced at \$30, \$50 and \$70, and starts with a divisor of 3. Stock B (\$50) then splits 2-for-1. The next day the prices are \$32, \$27 and \$68. Find the index at each stage.
step: Before the split the index is $(30 + 50 + 70)/3 = 50.00$.
step: After the split B trades at \$25, so the sum is $30 + 25 + 70 = 125$. To keep the index at 50, the new divisor is $125/50 = 2.5$.
step: Next day: $(32 + 27 + 68)/2.5 = 127/2.5 = 50.80$.
step: The split changed the weight of stock B, not the index level. The index rose 1.6%, from 50.00 to 50.80.
answer: Index 50.00 before the split, divisor 2.5 after, and 50.80 the next day.

@example
title: Return on a margin purchase
problem: You buy 200 shares at \$40 with 50% initial margin, borrowing the rest at 6% a year. One year later the price is \$46 and there were no dividends. Find your return on equity and the price that triggers a margin call at a 30% maintenance margin.
step: The position costs $200 \times 40 = \$8{,}000$. Your equity is \$4,000 and the loan is \$4,000.
step: After one year the shares are worth $200 \times 46 = \$9{,}200$. You repay $4000 \times 1.06 = \$4{,}240$, leaving \$4,960.
step: Return on equity: $(4960 - 4000)/4000 = 24\%$, against 15% for the unlevered stock price gain.
step: Margin call price: $P^{*} = 40(1 - 0.5)/(1 - 0.30) = \$28.57$.
answer: 24% return on equity; margin call at about \$28.57.

@mistakes
- Dividing by the old divisor after a split, so the index falls just because a stock split.
- Computing the margin return on the whole position instead of on your own equity, or forgetting the interest on the loan.
- Thinking that the issuer receives the money when you buy a share on the secondary market.
- Treating a short seller's loss as limited to the amount invested.

model:
```python
def margin_call_price(price, initial_margin, maintenance_margin):
    """Price at which equity / market value falls to the maintenance margin."""
    loan_per_share = price * (1 - initial_margin)
    return loan_per_share / (1 - maintenance_margin)
```
hint: Per share, the loan is `price * (1 - initial_margin)` and does not change with the stock price.
hint: Margin equals (P - loan) / P; set it equal to the maintenance margin and solve for P.
solution: Setting $(P - L)/P = m_m$ gives $P = L/(1 - m_m)$, where $L$ is the loan per share. With no loan, $L = 0$ and the formula returns 0 automatically.
