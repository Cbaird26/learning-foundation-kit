@article
A fund returned 14% last year. Is that good? It depends on the risk taken and the benchmark: a fund that earned 14% with the market's risk in a year the market earned 17% was disappointing. **Risk-adjusted performance measures** compare a return with the risk that produced it. All measures in this lesson use annualized arithmetic mean returns and annualized standard deviations (sample statistics) unless stated, and all rates below are hypothetical.

### Reward-to-risk ratios

Let $\bar R_p$ be the portfolio's mean return, $\bar r_f$ the risk-free rate, $\sigma_p$ its standard deviation, $\beta_p$ its beta, and $\bar R_M$, $\sigma_M$ the market's.

- **Sharpe ratio**: excess return per unit of *total* risk, $S_p = (\bar R_p - \bar r_f)/\sigma_p$. Use it to judge a portfolio that is the investor's whole risky holding, since total risk matters then.
- **Treynor ratio**: excess return per unit of *systematic* risk, $T_p = (\bar R_p - \bar r_f)/\beta_p$. Use it to judge a portfolio that is one component of a well-diversified portfolio, where idiosyncratic risk is diversified away.
- **Jensen's alpha**: the return in excess of the CAPM, $\alpha_p = \bar R_p - [\bar r_f + \beta_p(\bar R_M - \bar r_f)]$, in return units.
- **M-squared** ($M^2$): the return of the portfolio after mixing it with T-bills (or leveraging it) to have the market's standard deviation, minus the market's return:

$$M^2 = \bar r_f + S_p\sigma_M - \bar R_M.$$

$M^2$ ranks portfolios like the Sharpe ratio but in percentage points, which are easier to read.

### Measures against a benchmark

For an active manager with returns $R_{p,t}$ and a benchmark's $R_{b,t}$, the **active return** is $R_{p,t} - R_{b,t}$, the **tracking error** is the sample standard deviation of the active returns, and the **information ratio** is

$$IR = \frac{\text{mean active return}}{\text{tracking error}}.$$

It measures how much active return the manager earned per unit of the active risk taken. The **Sortino ratio** replaces the standard deviation with the downside deviation below a minimum acceptable return.

### Why the rankings differ

Fund rankings can differ across measures because they penalize different risks. A fund with a low beta but large idiosyncratic risk looks good by Treynor ratio but poor by Sharpe ratio. For a ranking to be meaningful, all funds must be compared with a common benchmark and over a common period, and a single track record is a small sample: the standard error of a mean annual return over five years is the standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{5}$, so luck and skill are hard to separate.

### Performance attribution

A fund's active return can be split by asset class. Let $w_{p,i}$ and $w_{b,i}$ be the portfolio and benchmark weights and $R_{p,i}$, $R_{b,i}$ the returns in class $i$, with $R_b$ the total benchmark return. The (Brinson) effects are

$$\text{Allocation} = \sum_i (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{b,i} - R_b), \qquad \text{Selection} = \sum_i w_{b,i}(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i}),$$

$$\text{Interaction} = \sum_i (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i}),$$

and they sum to $R_p - R_b$.

### Reporting standards

The **Global Investment Performance Standards (GIPS)**, 2020 edition, are voluntary, ethical standards for presenting investment performance fairly. They generally require time-weighted returns for composites of similar portfolios, full disclosure and consistent calculation, so that clients can compare managers.

@example
title: Ranking three funds
problem: The risk-free rate is 3% and the market has a mean return of 10% and a standard deviation of 15%. Rank funds A, B and C by the Sharpe ratio, the Treynor ratio and Jensen's alpha.

| Fund | Mean return | Standard deviation | Beta |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 14% | 22% | 1.3 |
| B | 11% | 14% | 0.7 |
| C | 9% | 20% | 0.5 |
step: Sharpe: A $= 11/22 = 0.500$; B $= 8/14 = 0.571$; C $= 6/20 = 0.300$. Ranking: B, A, C.
step: Treynor (percent per unit of beta): A $= 11/1.3 = 8.46$; B $= 8/0.7 = 11.43$; C $= 6/0.5 = 12.00$. Ranking: C, B, A.
step: Alpha: A $= 14 - (3 + 1.3 \times 7) = 1.9\%$; B $= 11 - (3 + 0.7 \times 7) = 3.1\%$; C $= 9 - (3 + 0.5 \times 7) = 2.5\%$. Ranking: B, C, A. Also $M^2$ for B is $3 + 0.571(15) - 10 = 1.57\%$.
step: Fund C is first by Treynor because its beta is low, but last by Sharpe because most of its risk is idiosyncratic. If C is one holding among many in a diversified portfolio, the Treynor ratio and alpha are the relevant measures; if C is the investor's entire risky portfolio, the Sharpe ratio is.
answer: Sharpe: B, A, C. Treynor: C, B, A. Alpha: B, C, A.

@example
title: Attribution of an active return
problem: A portfolio holds 60% stocks and 40% bonds; the benchmark holds 50% and 50%. The portfolio's returns are 8% in stocks and 3% in bonds; the benchmark's are 6% and 2%. Find the active return and its components.
step: $R_p = 0.6(8) + 0.4(3) = 6.0\%$ and $R_b = 0.5(6) + 0.5(2) = 4.0\%$, so the active return is $2.0\%$.
step: Allocation: $(0.6 - 0.5)(6 - 4) + (0.4 - 0.5)(2 - 4) = 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.4\%$ from overweighting the better-performing asset class.
step: Selection: $0.5(8 - 6) + 0.5(3 - 2) = 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5\%$ from picking better securities within each class.
step: Interaction: $(0.1)(2) + (-0.1)(1) = 0.1\%$. The total is $0.4 + 1.5 + 0.1 = 2.0\%$.
answer: Allocation 0.4%, selection 1.5%, interaction 0.1%, total 2.0%.

@mistakes
- Using the Sharpe ratio to judge a fund that is a small slice of a diversified portfolio, or the Treynor ratio for a fund that is an investor's entire portfolio.
- Forgetting to subtract the risk-free rate, so a high-return, high-risk fund looks better than it is.
- Comparing funds over different periods or against different benchmarks.
- Reading a few years' alpha as proof of skill without comparing it with its standard error.

model:
```python
def rank_funds(funds, rf, market_mean):
    """Rank funds by Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio and Jensen's alpha."""
    scores = {"sharpe": {}, "treynor": {}, "alpha": {}}
    for name, (mean, sd, beta) in funds.items():
        scores["sharpe"][name] = (mean - rf) / sd
        scores["treynor"][name] = (mean - rf) / beta
        scores["alpha"][name] = mean - (rf + beta * (market_mean - rf))
    return {
        measure: sorted(values, key=lambda n: (-values[n], n))
        for measure, values in scores.items()
    }
```

solution: Build one dictionary of scores per measure and sort the fund names by score in descending order. The three measures can rank the same funds differently: a fund with low beta but high total risk does well by Treynor ratio and badly by Sharpe ratio, as fund R shows in the first test.

@quiz num
prompt: A portfolio holds 75% stocks and 25% bonds; its benchmark holds 60% and 40%. The portfolio returned 9% in stocks and 3% in bonds, and the benchmark's stock and bond returns were 6% and 2%. What is the allocation effect, $\sum_i (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{b,i} - R_b)$, where $R_b$ is the total benchmark return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 0.60
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (0.75 - 0.60) * (6 - (0.6 * 6 + 0.4 * 2)) + (0.25 - 0.40) * (2 - (0.6 * 6 + 0.4 * 2))
hint: First find the total benchmark return $R_b = 0.6(6) + 0.4(2)$; allocation compares each class's benchmark return with it.
solution: $R_b = 0.6(6) + 0.4(2) = 4.4\%$. Allocation is $(0.75 - 0.60)(6 - 4.4) + (0.25 - 0.40)(2 - 4.4) = 0.24 + 0.36 = 0.60\%$: the portfolio overweights the asset class that beat the benchmark's total return and underweights the one that did not. The portfolio's own returns do not enter the allocation effect.
