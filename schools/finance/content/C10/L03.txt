@article
You hold one stock whose return swings widely. Add a second stock whose returns do not move in lockstep with the first. On a day the first falls, the second may rise, and the portfolio's return varies less than either stock alone. **Diversification** is this effect, and its size depends on how the assets move together.

In this lesson the means, standard deviations and correlations are given parameters, treated as population values for one year (annual, arithmetic). Estimating them from samples comes later.

### Two assets

Let asset 1 have expected return $\mu_1$ and standard deviation $\sigma_1$, asset 2 have $\mu_2$ and $\sigma_2$, and let their **covariance** be $\sigma_{12} = \rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2$, where $\rho_{12}$ is their **correlation** ($-1 \le \rho_{12} \le 1$). A portfolio with weights $w_1$ and $w_2 = 1 - w_1$ has

$$E(R_p) = w_1\mu_1 + w_2\mu_2, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2.$$

Expected return is a weighted average, but the standard deviation is not. Only when $\rho_{12} = 1$ is $\sigma_p = w_1\sigma_1 + w_2\sigma_2$; for any $\rho_{12} < 1$, $\sigma_p$ is smaller, which is the benefit of diversification. At $\rho_{12} = -1$ the risk can be eliminated completely, with $w_1 = \sigma_2/(\sigma_1+\sigma_2)$.

### The minimum-variance portfolio

Setting the derivative of $\sigma_p^2$ with respect to $w_1$ to zero gives the weight of the **global minimum-variance portfolio** of two assets:

$$w_1^{\text{GMV}} = \frac{\sigma_2^2 - \sigma_{12}}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\sigma_{12}}.$$

When $\rho_{12}$ is low enough, this portfolio has less risk than even the safer asset. The weight can fall outside 0 to 1, which means a short position.

### Many assets

For $n$ assets with weights $w_i$ and covariances $\sigma_{ij}$ (where $\sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2$),

$$\sigma_p^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n} w_i w_j \sigma_{ij}.$$

There are $n$ variance terms but $n(n-1)$ covariance terms, so for large $n$ the covariances dominate. For an equal-weighted portfolio ($w_i = 1/n$) with average variance $\bar\sigma^2$ and average covariance $\bar\sigma_{ij}$,

$$\sigma_p^2 = \frac{1}{n}\bar\sigma^2 + \left(1 - \frac{1}{n}\right)\bar\sigma_{ij}.$$

As $n \to \infty$, $\sigma_p^2 \to \bar\sigma_{ij}$. The part of risk that vanishes is **idiosyncratic** (firm-specific, **diversifiable**) risk; the part that remains is **systematic** (market, **non-diversifiable**) risk. With all pairwise correlations equal to $\rho$ and equal standard deviations $\sigma$, $\sigma_p \to \sigma\sqrt{\rho}$: with $\sigma = 30\%$ and $\rho = 0.25$, no number of assets can push risk below 15%.

The expected return of a risky asset is therefore not rewarded for its idiosyncratic risk, a point the CAPM will make precise.

@example
title: A 60/40 portfolio of two assets
problem: Asset 1 has $\mu_1 = 10\%$ and $\sigma_1 = 20\%$; asset 2 has $\mu_2 = 6\%$ and $\sigma_2 = 12\%$; the correlation is 0.3. Find the expected return and standard deviation of a portfolio with 60% in asset 1, and the global minimum-variance portfolio.
step: Expected return: $0.6(10\%) + 0.4(6\%) = 8.4\%$.
step: Variance: $0.6^2(0.20)^2 + 0.4^2(0.12)^2 + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.20)(0.12) = 0.0144 + 0.002304 + 0.003456 = 0.020160$.
step: Standard deviation: $\sqrt{0.02016} = 14.20\%$, compared with the weighted average of standard deviations, $0.6(20) + 0.4(12) = 16.8\%$. Diversification removes 2.6 percentage points of risk.
step: Minimum variance: $\sigma_{12} = 0.3(0.20)(0.12) = 0.0072$, so $w_1 = (0.0144 - 0.0072)/(0.04 + 0.0144 - 0.0144) = 0.18$ and $w_2 = 0.82$.
step: Its standard deviation is $11.45\%$, lower than either asset's 20% or 12%.
answer: 60/40: expected return 8.4%, standard deviation 14.20%. Minimum variance: 18% in asset 1, 82% in asset 2, standard deviation 11.45%.

@example
title: Risk of an equal-weighted portfolio
problem: Each of $n$ stocks has a standard deviation of 30%, and every pair has a correlation of 0.25. Find the standard deviation of an equal-weighted portfolio for $n = 4$ and $n = 100$.
step: The common covariance is $0.25 \times 0.30^2 = 0.0225$ and the variance is $0.09$.
step: For $n = 4$: $\sigma_p^2 = 0.09/4 + (3/4)(0.0225) = 0.0225 + 0.016875 = 0.039375$, so $\sigma_p = 19.84\%$.
step: For $n = 100$: $\sigma_p^2 = 0.0009 + 0.99(0.0225) = 0.023175$, so $\sigma_p = 15.22\%$.
step: Going from 4 to 100 assets cuts risk only from 19.84% to 15.22%, close to the 15% floor $\sigma\sqrt{\rho}$.
answer: 19.84% for four stocks and 15.22% for one hundred.

@mistakes
- Averaging the standard deviations of the assets to get the portfolio's standard deviation; only the expected return averages.
- Forgetting the factor 2 on the covariance term, or using the correlation where the covariance belongs.
- Believing that diversification can eliminate all risk; correlation among assets leaves a floor of systematic risk.
- Putting weights that do not sum to one into the formulas.

hint: Which risk is specific to individual firms and tends to cancel out across many of them?
solution: Firm-specific shocks are not perfectly correlated and average out, so idiosyncratic risk shrinks. The common movement shared by all stocks, driven by economy-wide factors, cannot be diversified away.

model:
```python
import math


def portfolio_stdev(weights, cov):
    """Standard deviation of a portfolio from weights and a covariance matrix."""
    n = len(weights)
    variance = 0.0
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            variance += weights[i] * weights[j] * cov[i][j]
    return math.sqrt(variance)
```
hint: The variance is a double sum over every pair of assets, including each asset with itself.
hint: Add `weights[i] * weights[j] * cov[i][j]` for all `i` and `j`, then take the square root after the loops.
solution: Looping over all ordered pairs $(i, j)$ counts each covariance twice off the diagonal, which is exactly the factor 2 in the two-asset formula, and the diagonal gives the variances. Averaging the assets' standard deviations would be a typical wrong answer.
