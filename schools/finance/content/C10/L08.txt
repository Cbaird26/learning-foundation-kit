@article
If prices fully reflect available information, then no one can consistently earn returns above those justified by risk using that information. This is the **efficient market hypothesis** (EMH). Its logic is competition: if a piece of public information implied a profit, traders would act on it until the price moved and the profit vanished. The hypothesis does not say that prices are always right or that no one profits from trading; it says that price changes come from new information, so they are unpredictable.

### Three forms

| Form | Prices reflect | Implication |
|---|---|---|
| Weak | Past prices and volume | Technical analysis cannot earn abnormal returns |
| Semi-strong | All public information | Fundamental analysis of public data cannot earn abnormal returns |
| Strong | All information, public and private | Even insiders cannot earn abnormal returns |

Strong-form efficiency is not expected to hold: trading on material nonpublic information is illegal precisely because it is profitable. A related point, the **Grossman-Stiglitz paradox**, is that markets cannot be perfectly efficient, since then no one would pay to gather information, yet prices reflect information only because some people gather it.

### Testing efficiency

**Weak form.** If prices follow a **random walk**, successive returns are uncorrelated. The first-order autocorrelation of returns $r_t$ is

$$\hat\rho_1 = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{n}(r_t - \bar r)(r_{t-1} - \bar r)}{\sum_{t=1}^{n}(r_t - \bar r)^2},$$

and under independence its standard error is about $1/\sqrt{n}$, so values beyond $\pm 1.96/\sqrt{n}$ are significant at the 5% level.

**Semi-strong form: event studies.** To see how fast prices absorb news, estimate a normal return model (often the market model $E(R_{i,t}) = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{M,t}$ from a window before the event), then compute the **abnormal return** and its sum over the event window, the **cumulative abnormal return**:

$$AR_t = R_{i,t} - (\hat\alpha_i + \hat\beta_i R_{M,t}), \qquad CAR = \sum_{t=1}^{T} AR_t.$$

If the residual standard deviation is $\sigma_\varepsilon$ per day, the standard error of a $T$-day CAR for one firm is $\sigma_\varepsilon\sqrt{T}$; for the average CAR of $N$ firms with cross-sectional standard deviation $s$, the t-statistic is $\overline{CAR}/(s/\sqrt{N})$. In an efficient market, abnormal returns appear on the announcement day and are about zero afterwards.

**Testing a trading rule.** To test whether a rule beats the market, compute the difference between the rule's returns and the benchmark's in each period, $d_t = R_{\text{rule},t} - R_{\text{mkt},t}$, and its t-statistic $t = \bar d/(s_d/\sqrt{n})$, with $s_d$ the sample standard deviation. A rule must also beat the benchmark **after** transaction costs and risk adjustment.

### Anomalies and the joint hypothesis problem

An **anomaly** is a pattern in average returns that a pricing model cannot explain. Well-known candidates include the **size** effect (small stocks outperforming), the **value** effect (high book-to-market stocks outperforming), **momentum** (past winners continuing to outperform past losers over 3 to 12 months), **post-earnings-announcement drift** (prices continuing to move in the direction of an earnings surprise) and calendar effects such as the January effect. Their strength changes across periods and markets, and publication tends to weaken them.

Every test of efficiency is a **joint hypothesis**: you test that markets are efficient *and* that your model of expected returns is right. A significant abnormal return could mean market inefficiency or a faulty benchmark, such as a CAPM that omits value and size. Explanations of anomalies divide into **risk-based** (the premium compensates for risk the model misses) and **behavioral** (investor biases such as overconfidence and under-reaction to news, combined with **limits to arbitrage**: costs, short-sale constraints and the risk that mispricing persists).

### Avoiding self-deception

Test many rules on one data set and some will look significant by chance: with 20 independent tests at the 5% level, the probability that at least one appears significant even if none is real is $1 - 0.95^{20} = 64\%$. This is **data snooping**. Guard against it with out-of-sample tests, a rule specified before looking at the data, **look-ahead bias** (using information not yet available at the time of the trade), **survivorship bias** (testing only stocks that still exist), and honest transaction costs.

@example
title: An event study
problem: Using the market model with $\hat\alpha = 0.05\%$ and $\hat\beta = 1.2$ per day, find the abnormal returns and CAR for five days around an announcement, and test the CAR if the daily residual standard deviation is 1.5%.

|Day|-2|-1|0|+1|+2|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|Market|0.4%|-0.2%|1.0%|0.3%|-0.6%|
|Stock|0.6%|0.1%|3.1%|0.8%|-0.2%|
step: Expected return each day is $0.05\% + 1.2 R_M$: $0.53\%, -0.19\%, 1.25\%, 0.41\%, -0.67\%$.
step: Abnormal returns are stock minus expected: $0.07\%, 0.29\%, 1.85\%, 0.39\%, 0.47\%$. The big jump is on the announcement day.
step: $CAR = 0.07 + 0.29 + 1.85 + 0.39 + 0.47 = 3.07\%$.
step: Standard error $= 1.5\%\sqrt{5} = 3.35\%$, so $t = 3.07/3.35 = 0.92$: for one firm over five days, the CAR is not statistically significant, although it is large. Averaging across many firms reduces the noise.
answer: CAR 3.07%, t-statistic 0.92 (not significant).

@example
title: Does a rule beat the market?
problem: A trading rule's monthly returns exceeded the market's by an average of 0.40% over 60 months, with a sample standard deviation of the monthly differences of 3.0%. Is this significant at the 5% level (critical value about 2.0)? What if 20 such rules had been tried?
step: Standard error: $3.0\%/\sqrt{60} = 0.387\%$.
step: $t = 0.40/0.387 = 1.03$, below the critical value of about 2.0, so the outperformance is not statistically significant.
step: If 20 independent rules were tried and the best one reported, the chance that at least one would look significant at the 5% level by luck alone is $1 - 0.95^{20} = 64\%$, so even a significant t-statistic would deserve suspicion.
answer: t = 1.03, not significant.

@mistakes
- Concluding from a large average return that the rule works, without comparing it with its standard error.
- Ignoring the joint hypothesis problem: an abnormal return measured against the wrong benchmark is not proof of inefficiency.
- Reporting only the best of many backtested rules, or testing in the same data used to choose the rule.
- Confusing efficiency with "prices are always right" or "nobody can ever profit".

model:
```python
import math
import statistics


def rule_vs_market(rule_returns, market_returns):
    """Mean excess return of a rule over the market and its t-statistic."""
    diffs = [r - m for r, m in zip(rule_returns, market_returns)]
    mean_diff = statistics.mean(diffs)
    s = statistics.stdev(diffs)
    if s == 0:
        return mean_diff, None
    return mean_diff, mean_diff / (s / math.sqrt(len(diffs)))
```
hint: Test the differences period by period, not the two return series separately.
hint: Use `statistics.stdev` for the sample standard deviation and divide it by the square root of the number of periods.
solution: The null hypothesis is that the mean difference is zero, so form the series of differences, compute its mean and sample standard deviation, and divide the mean by its standard error. A constant difference has zero standard deviation, so the function returns `None` rather than dividing by zero.
