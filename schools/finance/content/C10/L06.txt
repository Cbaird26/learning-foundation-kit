@article
The CAPM says how expected returns relate to beta. To use it we must *estimate* beta from data. The **single-index model** gives the standard way: it says each stock's return is driven by the market plus a stock-specific surprise.

### The single-index model

Using **excess returns** (return minus the risk-free rate in the same period), $R_{i,t}^e = R_{i,t} - r_{f,t}$ and $R_{M,t}^e = R_{M,t} - r_{f,t}$, the model is

$$R_{i,t}^e = \alpha_i + \beta_i R_{M,t}^e + e_{i,t},$$

where $e_{i,t}$ has mean zero and is uncorrelated with the market and with other stocks' residuals. The intercept $\alpha_i$ is **Jensen's alpha**: the average return in excess of what the CAPM predicts for that beta. If the CAPM holds, $\alpha_i = 0$. The regression line through the scatter of stock excess returns against market excess returns is the **security characteristic line**.

### Variance decomposition

Because the residual is uncorrelated with the market,

$$\sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_{e,i}^2.$$

The first term is **systematic** risk and the second is **firm-specific** risk. The share of variance explained by the market is the regression's **R-squared**:

$$R^2 = \frac{\beta_i^2\sigma_M^2}{\sigma_i^2} = \rho_{iM}^2.$$

For an equal-weighted portfolio of many stocks the residuals average out, so portfolio variance is essentially $\beta_p^2\sigma_M^2$: the model also cuts the number of estimates needed from $n(n+1)/2$ variances and covariances to $n$ betas, $n$ residual variances and the market's variance.

### Estimating beta

Given $n$ observations of excess returns, ordinary least squares gives

$$\hat\beta = \frac{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)(y_t - \bar y)}{\sum_t (x_t - \bar x)^2} = \frac{s_{xy}}{s_x^2}, \qquad \hat\alpha = \bar y - \hat\beta\,\bar x,$$

where $x$ is the market excess return and $y$ the stock's. The sample covariance and variance can both divide by $n-1$ or both by $n$; the ratio is the same. The residual standard deviation divides the sum of squared residuals by $n-2$, because two parameters were estimated.

Beta depends on choices that you must state: the **return interval** (daily, weekly, monthly), the **length of the sample** (often five years of monthly returns or two years of weekly returns) and the **market proxy** (a broad index). Different choices give different betas for the same stock. Betas have a **standard error**, so a beta estimated from 60 monthly observations is a range, not a point.

### Adjusted beta

Betas estimated from a sample tend to be pulled back toward 1 over time, partly because extreme estimates contain more error. A common correction (the **Blume adjustment**) is

$$\beta_{\text{adj}} = \tfrac{2}{3}\hat\beta + \tfrac{1}{3}(1).$$

A raw beta of 1.45 becomes 1.30, and 0.70 becomes 0.80. The weights 2/3 and 1/3 are a convention, not a law.

### Putting it to work

For five stocks you would collect aligned return series, subtract the risk-free rate, run one regression per stock, and report $\hat\alpha$, $\hat\beta$, $R^2$ and a standard error. Annualizing a monthly alpha by multiplying by 12 is an approximation. Since a statistically significant alpha is rare, always ask whether it is larger than its standard error.

@example
title: Estimating alpha and beta from six months
problem: Six monthly excess returns (in percent) for the market and for Larkspur Outfitters are below. Estimate alpha and beta and the R-squared. This sample is tiny and only for practice.

|Month|1|2|3|4|5|6|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|Market|2.0|-1.5|3.0|-0.5|1.0|-2.0|
|Larkspur|3.1|-2.0|4.6|-0.2|1.4|-3.5|
step: Means: $\bar x = 0.3333\%$ and $\bar y = 0.5667\%$.
step: Sums of products and squares of deviations: $\sum(x - \bar x)(y - \bar y) = 30.367$, $\sum(x - \bar x)^2 = 19.833$ and $\sum(y - \bar y)^2 = 47.093$.
step: Beta: $\hat\beta = 30.367/19.833 = 1.531$. Alpha: $\hat\alpha = 0.5667 - 1.531(0.3333) = 0.056\%$ per month, about 0.68% a year if multiplied by 12.
step: R-squared: $30.367^2/(19.833 \times 47.093) = 0.987$, so 98.7% of the variance of Larkspur in these months is explained by the market. The residual standard deviation (dividing by $n - 2 = 4$) is 0.387% per month.
step: The standard error of beta is $0.387/\sqrt{19.833} = 0.087$, and alpha of 0.056% is tiny compared with its own noise.
answer: Beta 1.531, alpha 0.056% per month, R-squared 0.987.

@example
title: Systematic and firm-specific risk
problem: A stock has $\beta = 1.2$, the market's standard deviation is 18% and the stock's residual standard deviation is 25% (annual). Find its total standard deviation, R-squared, and Blume-adjusted beta.
step: Systematic variance: $1.2^2(0.18)^2 = 0.046656$. Residual variance: $0.25^2 = 0.0625$.
step: Total variance: $0.109156$, so $\sigma = 33.04\%$.
step: $R^2 = 0.046656/0.109156 = 42.7\%$, so most of this stock's risk is diversifiable.
step: Adjusted beta: $\tfrac23(1.2) + \tfrac13 = 1.133$.
answer: Total standard deviation 33.04%, R-squared 42.7%, adjusted beta 1.13.

@mistakes
- Regressing raw returns, or using the wrong risk-free rate, so that alpha does not mean excess performance.
- Reading a high R-squared as a high beta: R-squared measures fit and beta measures slope, and they are different numbers.
- Mixing return frequencies, such as daily stock returns with monthly market returns.
- Reporting a small alpha as skill without comparing it with its standard error.

model:
```python
def estimate_all(stock_returns, market_returns, rf):
    """(alpha, beta) of each stock from excess-return least squares."""
    x = [r - rf for r in market_returns]
    mean_x = sum(x) / len(x)
    sxx = sum((v - mean_x) ** 2 for v in x)
    result = {}
    for name, returns in stock_returns.items():
        y = [r - rf for r in returns]
        mean_y = sum(y) / len(y)
        sxy = sum((a - mean_x) * (b - mean_y) for a, b in zip(x, y))
        beta = sxy / sxx
        result[name] = (mean_y - beta * mean_x, beta)
    return result
```
hint: Convert both the market's and the stock's returns to excess returns first.
hint: Compute the market's mean and sum of squared deviations once; then for each stock compute the sum of cross-products.
solution: Beta is the ratio of the cross-product sum to the market's sum of squares, and alpha makes the fitted line pass through the point of means. Running the same steps for every stock gives the five-stock table the block asks for. Forgetting to subtract `rf` gives the right beta but the wrong alpha whenever beta differs from 1.
