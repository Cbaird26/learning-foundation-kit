```python
import random
import math
import statistics

random.seed(2041)
NAMES = ["Alder", "Birch", "Cedar", "Dogwood", "Elm",
         "Fir", "Ginkgo", "Hazel", "Ironwood", "Juniper"]
BETA = [0.6, 0.8, 0.9, 1.0, 1.1, 1.2, 1.4, 0.7, 1.0, 1.3]
ALPHA = [0.001, 0.0005, 0.0, 0.0015, -0.0005, 0.0, 0.002, 0.0005, -0.001, 0.0]
IDIO = [0.030, 0.025, 0.035, 0.040, 0.030, 0.045, 0.050, 0.020, 0.035, 0.055]
RF = 0.002
MONTHS = 180

market = [random.gauss(0.007, 0.045) for _ in range(MONTHS)]
returns = {}
for i, name in enumerate(NAMES):
    returns[name] = [
        RF + ALPHA[i] + BETA[i] * (m - RF) + random.gauss(0.0, IDIO[i])
        for m in market
    ]
```

### Key formulas

- Geometric mean: $\left[\prod(1+R_t)\right]^{1/n} - 1$. Sample variance divides by $n-1$.
- Two assets: $\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\sigma_{12}$.
- Sharpe: $(\mu_p - r_f)/\sigma_p$. Tangency: $\propto \Sigma^{-1}(\mu - r_f\mathbf 1)$.
- SML: $E(R_i) = r_f + \beta_i[E(R_M) - r_f]$, with $\beta_i = \text{Cov}(R_i, R_M)/\text{Var}(R_M)$.
- Variance split: $\sigma_i^2 = \beta_i^2\sigma_M^2 + \sigma_e^2$.
- APT: $E(R_i) = r_f + \sum_k \beta_{ik}\lambda_k$. Gordon: $V_0 = D_1/(r - g)$.
- Bond: $\Delta P/P \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\Delta y + \tfrac12 C_x(\Delta y)^2$.
- Alpha: $\mu_p - [r_f + \beta_p(\mu_M - r_f)]$. $M^2 = r_f + S_p\sigma_M - \mu_M$.

### Common errors

- Averaging standard deviations; using total risk where beta belongs; forgetting the risk-free rate in Sharpe and alpha.
- Using the annual yield as the semiannual yield; $D_0$ in place of $D_1$; growth at or above the discount rate.
- Dividing by the wrong divisor after a split; forgetting interest on a margin loan.
- Declaring skill from a mean without the standard error, or from the best of many tests.

The analyst tried 15 variations of the rule on the same data and reports the best one. Round-trip trading costs reduce the average monthly active return by 0.10%. In a short memo to a client, (a) compute the t-statistic and the annualized information ratio before costs, (b) recompute the t-statistic after costs, (c) state the probability that at least one of 15 independent tests at the 5% level appears significant by chance alone, and (d) explain whether you would believe the result.

(d) Even before costs the t-statistic is below the 5% critical value of about 2, so the outperformance cannot be distinguished from luck; after costs it is weaker, and because the best of 15 variations was reported the chance of a spurious winner is better than even. I would not rely on the rule without an out-of-sample test on new data, a rule fixed in advance, and a risk adjustment for factors such as size, value and momentum.
