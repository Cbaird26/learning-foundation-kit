@article
A bond is a promise: fixed coupon payments and a final repayment of face (par) value. Its price is the present value of those payments, so understanding bonds means understanding present value. Conventions in this lesson: face value 100, coupons paid semiannually, the price computed on a coupon date, and the **yield to maturity** (YTM) quoted as an annual rate compounded semiannually (the bond-equivalent yield), so the periodic yield is $y/2$.

### Pricing

A bond with annual coupon rate $c$, years to maturity $T$ and $n = 2T$ semiannual periods pays a coupon $C = 100c/2$ each period and 100 at the end:

$$P = \sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{C}{(1 + y/2)^k} + \frac{100}{(1 + y/2)^n}.$$

If $y = c$, the price is 100 (**par**); if $y > c$, the bond sells below par (a **discount**); if $y < c$, above par (a **premium**). The YTM is the discount rate that makes the price equal the market price; it has no closed form, so it is found numerically. The **current yield** is the annual coupon divided by the price, which ignores the gain or loss at maturity.

### Price and yield move oppositely

The price-yield curve slopes down and is **convex** (it bends upward): a given fall in yield raises the price more than the same rise in yield lowers it. A 5-year 6% bond is worth 104.38 at a 5% yield, 100.00 at 6% and 95.84 at 7%: the 1-point fall adds 4.38 and the 1-point rise subtracts 4.16. Sensitivity is higher for bonds with **longer maturity**, **lower coupons** and (for a given change) **lower starting yields**.

### Duration and convexity

The **Macaulay duration** is the weighted average time to receipt of the bond's cash flows, with weights equal to the present values of the flows relative to the price. The **modified duration** converts it into a price sensitivity:

$$D_{\text{mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/2}, \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}C_x(\Delta y)^2,$$

where $C_x$ is the bond's **convexity**. The first term is the linear estimate, a tangent line to the price-yield curve; the second corrects for curvature and is always positive, which helps the bondholder in both directions. A zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity.

### Spot rates and the yield curve

Bonds with different maturities have different yields. A **spot rate** $s_t$ is the yield on a zero-coupon bond maturing at $t$; the **yield curve** plots yields against maturity. Pricing with spot rates discounts each cash flow at its own rate. The **implied forward rate** for year 2, as an annual rate, satisfies $(1+s_2)^2 = (1+s_1)(1+f_{1,1})$. Yields also differ across issuers: a **credit spread** compensates for default risk. Current yields are published by the Treasury and on FRED; the figures here are hypothetical.

@example
title: Pricing a bond and finding its duration
problem: A 10-year bond has a 4% coupon paid semiannually. Find its price at a 5% yield, its modified duration, and the price at a 6% yield; compare the actual price change with the duration estimate.
step: At $y = 5\%$: 20 coupons of 2.00 at a periodic yield of 2.5% and a final 100. $P = 92.2054$.
step: The Macaulay duration is 8.2556 years, so $D_{\text{mod}} = 8.2556/1.025 = 8.0542$.
step: At $y = 6\%$: $P = 85.1225$, an actual change of $85.1225/92.2054 - 1 = -7.68\%$.
step: Duration estimate for $\Delta y = +1\%$: $-8.0542 \times 0.01 = -8.05\%$, which overstates the fall because it ignores convexity. With convexity 77.11 the estimate is $-8.054\% + \tfrac12(77.11)(0.01)^2 = -7.67\%$, almost exactly right.
answer: Price 92.21; modified duration 8.05; price at 6% is 85.12 (a fall of 7.68%).

@example
title: Pricing with spot rates and finding a forward rate
problem: One-year and two-year zero-coupon spot rates are 3.0% and 3.5% (annual compounding). Price a two-year bond with a 5% annual coupon (face 100), and find the one-year forward rate one year from now.
step: Discount each cash flow at its own spot rate: $5/1.03 + 105/1.035^2 = 4.8544 + 98.0185 = 102.87$.
step: The forward rate satisfies $1.035^2 = 1.03(1 + f)$, so $1 + f = 1.071225/1.03 = 1.04002$ and $f = 4.00\%$.
step: Forward rates exceed spot rates when the yield curve slopes up. Locking in 3.5% for two years is equivalent to earning 3% in year 1 and 4.00% in year 2.
answer: Price 102.87; forward rate 4.00%.

@mistakes
- Using the annual yield as the periodic yield for semiannual coupons (discounting at 5% instead of 2.5% per period, over 10 periods instead of 20).
- Believing a higher coupon rate means a higher price regardless of the yield: price depends on the coupon relative to the YTM.
- Reporting the modified duration in years as if it were the Macaulay duration; the modified duration measures percent price change per unit of yield change.
- Treating the current yield as the return, ignoring the premium or discount that disappears at maturity.

model:
```python
def price(face, coupon_rate, ytm, years, freq=2):
    """Bond price on a coupon date: PV of coupons plus PV of principal."""
    n = int(round(years * freq))
    coupon = face * coupon_rate / freq
    i = ytm / freq
    pv_coupons = sum(coupon / (1 + i) ** k for k in range(1, n + 1))
    return pv_coupons + face / (1 + i) ** n
```
hint: The number of periods is `years * freq`, the coupon per period is `face * coupon_rate / freq` and the periodic yield is `ytm / freq`.
hint: Sum the discounted coupons for periods 1 to `n`, then add the discounted face value.
solution: Convert everything to the period: $n = T \cdot freq$, coupon $= face \cdot c/freq$ and yield $i = y/freq$, then add the present value of the coupon annuity and of the principal at $n$. Using the annual yield as the periodic yield is the usual error.
