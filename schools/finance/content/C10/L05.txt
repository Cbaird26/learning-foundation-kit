@article
Lesson 4 gave every investor the same risky portfolio, the tangency portfolio. If all investors hold it, then in equilibrium it must be the portfolio of all risky assets weighted by market value, the **market portfolio** $M$. The **capital asset pricing model** (CAPM) follows: it tells us what return the market requires of any single asset, given how that asset contributes to the market portfolio's risk.

### Assumptions

The model assumes investors are rational mean-variance optimizers with the same beliefs and one-period horizon, can borrow and lend at one risk-free rate $r_f$, face no taxes or transaction costs, and can trade all assets, which are infinitely divisible. These assumptions are unrealistic, but the model's logic is a benchmark for the whole subject.

### Beta: the risk that is priced

Adding a small amount of asset $i$ to the market portfolio changes the portfolio's variance by an amount proportional to $\text{Cov}(R_i, R_M)$, not to $\sigma_i^2$. An asset's **beta** is that covariance scaled by the market variance:

$$\beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_M)}{\text{Var}(R_M)} = \rho_{iM}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_M}.$$

The market has $\beta = 1$ and the risk-free asset $\beta = 0$. An asset's idiosyncratic risk can be diversified away, so the market does not pay for it: the risk premium depends only on beta.

### The security market line

In equilibrium the expected return of every asset satisfies the **security market line** (SML):

$$E(R_i) = r_f + \beta_i\left[E(R_M) - r_f\right].$$

The term $E(R_M) - r_f$ is the **market risk premium**, and $\beta_i$ times it is the asset's risk premium. The SML plots expected return against beta; it is a straight line with intercept $r_f$ and slope equal to the market risk premium. The **capital market line** (CML), by contrast, plots expected return against *total* risk $\sigma$ and applies only to efficient portfolios, those that mix $M$ and the risk-free asset:

$$E(R_p) = r_f + \frac{E(R_M) - r_f}{\sigma_M}\sigma_p.$$

An individual stock, with its diversifiable risk, plots below the CML but exactly on the SML.

### Portfolio beta and alpha

Beta is linear: a portfolio's beta is the weighted average $\beta_p = \sum_i w_i\beta_i$, with the risk-free asset contributing 0. The **alpha** of an asset is the difference between its expected return from some forecast and the SML return:

$$\alpha_i = E(R_i) - \left[r_f + \beta_i\left(E(R_M) - r_f\right)\right].$$

In equilibrium alpha is zero. A positive alpha means the asset plots above the SML: it is offering more return than its beta requires (underpriced, if you believe the forecast), and buying pressure should raise its price until alpha is zero.

### Uses and limits

Corporations use the SML to estimate the cost of equity, which sets the hurdle rate for capital budgeting, and investors use it as a benchmark for performance. Empirical tests find that the actual line is flatter than the model predicts: low-beta stocks earn more, and high-beta stocks less, than beta alone implies. The market portfolio cannot be observed (it should include real estate, human capital and every other asset), which is the **Roll critique**. These findings motivated the multifactor models of Lesson 7. Use the CAPM as a framework, not a verdict.

@example
title: Required return and beta from a covariance
problem: The risk-free rate is 3%, the expected market return is 8%, and the market's standard deviation is 18%. A stock has a covariance of 0.0432 with the market. Find its beta and required return.
step: $\text{Var}(R_M) = 0.18^2 = 0.0324$, so $\beta = 0.0432/0.0324 = 1.3333$.
step: Market risk premium: $8\% - 3\% = 5\%$.
step: Required return: $3\% + 1.3333(5\%) = 9.67\%$.
step: A stock with $\beta = 1.3$ would require $3\% + 1.3(5\%) = 9.50\%$, so a higher covariance with the market means a higher required return.
answer: Beta 1.333 and required return 9.67%.

@example
title: Is the stock mispriced?
problem: With $r_f = 3\%$ and $E(R_M) = 9\%$, an analyst forecasts an expected return of 11.5% for a stock with $\beta = 1.2$. Compute its alpha and interpret it.
step: Required return: $3\% + 1.2(9\% - 3\%) = 10.2\%$.
step: Alpha: $11.5\% - 10.2\% = +1.3\%$.
step: The stock plots 1.3 percentage points above the security market line. If the forecast is right, the stock is underpriced: it offers more than its risk requires, and a rise in its price would bring its expected return down to 10.2%.
step: The analyst's forecast is the weak point: a 1.3% alpha is small relative to the error in any forecast of expected returns.
answer: Alpha is +1.3%: the stock is above the SML (underpriced if the forecast is correct).

@mistakes
- Using a stock's total standard deviation instead of its beta to find the required return.
- Using the market return instead of the market risk premium as the slope: the SML is $r_f + \beta(E(R_M) - r_f)$, not $r_f + \beta E(R_M)$.
- Confusing the CML (efficient portfolios, total risk) with the SML (any asset, beta).
- Giving the risk-free asset a beta of 1; its beta is 0 and the market's is 1.

def capm_alpha(forecast_return, risk_free, beta, market_return):

    return forecast_return - required

answer: 7.00
unit: %

hint: The slope of the CML is the market's Sharpe ratio, $(9 - 3)/18$.
solution: The CML slope is $0.06/0.18 = 1/3$, so $E(R_p) = 3\% + (1/3)(12\%) = 7\%$. That equals a portfolio with $\beta = 12/18 = 0.667$: $3\% + 0.667(6\%) = 7\%$.
