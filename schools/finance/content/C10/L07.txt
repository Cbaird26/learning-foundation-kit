@article
The CAPM says one factor, the market, explains differences in expected returns. But stocks seem to respond to several economy-wide forces: growth, inflation, interest rates, credit conditions. A **multifactor model** lets a stock's return depend on several of them.

### The factor model of returns

A $K$-factor model writes the return of asset $i$ as

$$R_i = E(R_i) + \beta_{i1}F_1 + \beta_{i2}F_2 + \cdots + \beta_{iK}F_K + e_i,$$

where each factor surprise $F_k$ has mean zero, $\beta_{ik}$ is the asset's **factor loading** (sensitivity) to factor $k$, and $e_i$ is firm-specific noise uncorrelated with the factors and with other assets' noise. A portfolio of many assets has diluted $e$, so the portfolio's return is driven almost entirely by the factors.

### Arbitrage pricing theory

An **arbitrage** is a strategy that needs no net investment, has no risk, and has a positive payoff. The **arbitrage pricing theory** (APT) argues that if there are no arbitrage opportunities among well-diversified portfolios, then expected returns satisfy

$$E(R_i) = r_f + \beta_{i1}\lambda_1 + \beta_{i2}\lambda_2 + \cdots + \beta_{iK}\lambda_K,$$

where $\lambda_k$ is the **risk premium** of factor $k$: the expected excess return of a portfolio with loading 1 on factor $k$ and 0 on the others. In the one-factor case $E(R_i) = r_f + \beta_i\lambda$. Suppose two well-diversified portfolios have the same beta but different expected returns: buy the one with the higher return and short the other, financing neither, and you earn a sure profit with no factor risk. Investors do this until prices adjust, which restores the linear relation.

The APT differs from the CAPM in assuming no mean-variance preferences and no market portfolio; it does not say what the factors are, and its no-arbitrage argument applies exactly to well-diversified portfolios and only approximately to individual stocks. The CAPM is a one-factor case in which the factor is the market's excess return.

### Fama-French and other factor models

Empirical work identified factors that explain average returns beyond market beta. The **Fama-French three-factor model** regresses a stock's excess return on:

- **Mkt-RF**, the market's excess return;
- **SMB** ("small minus big"), the return of small-capitalization stocks minus large-capitalization stocks;
- **HML** ("high minus low"), the return of high book-to-market (value) stocks minus low book-to-market (growth) stocks.

$$R_i - r_f = \alpha_i + b_i(\text{Mkt-RF}) + s_i\,\text{SMB} + h_i\,\text{HML} + e_i.$$

The **five-factor model** adds **RMW** (robust minus weak profitability) and **CMA** (conservative minus aggressive investment). The **Carhart four-factor model** adds a **momentum** factor (winners minus losers over the past year, often written UMD or WML). Factor returns are published by academic data libraries, and their historical averages are only estimates of the risk premiums.

Whether these premiums are compensation for risk or evidence of mispricing is debated, which Lesson 8 takes up. In a factor regression, alpha is the return not explained by the factors; a manager whose "alpha" disappears once value and size exposure are controlled had earned factor premiums, not skill.

@example
title: Expected return from three factors
problem: The risk-free rate is 3%. A stock has loadings of 1.1 on the market, 0.4 on SMB and -0.2 on HML. The factor risk premiums are 6% (market), 2% (SMB) and 3% (HML). Find the expected return.
step: Market contribution: $1.1 \times 6\% = 6.6\%$.
step: Size contribution: $0.4 \times 2\% = 0.8\%$.
step: Value contribution: $-0.2 \times 3\% = -0.6\%$ (a growth tilt lowers the expected return).
step: Total: $3\% + 6.6\% + 0.8\% - 0.6\% = 9.8\%$.
answer: 9.8%.

@example
title: An arbitrage between two well-diversified portfolios
problem: Assume one factor and $r_f = 4\%$. Well-diversified portfolio A has $\beta = 1.0$ and expected return 10%. Portfolio B has $\beta = 0.5$ and expected return 6.4%. Is there an arbitrage? If so, construct it on \$1,000,000.
step: From A, the factor premium is $\lambda = (10\% - 4\%)/1.0 = 6\%$. The APT return for $\beta = 0.5$ is $4\% + 0.5(6\%) = 7\%$, but B offers only 6.4%: B is overpriced.
step: Short \$1,000,000 of B. Buy \$500,000 of A and lend \$500,000 at the risk-free rate. Net investment is zero.
step: Beta: $0.5(1.0) + 0.5(0) - 1.0(0.5) = 0$, so the position has no factor risk.
step: Expected profit: $0.5(10\%) + 0.5(4\%) - 6.4\% = 0.6\%$ of \$1,000,000, or \$6,000, with no factor risk (and negligible idiosyncratic risk in well-diversified portfolios).
answer: Yes; the expected profit is \$6,000 on a zero-investment, zero-beta position.

@mistakes
- Using the factor's loading alone as the premium instead of loading times factor risk premium, or forgetting to add the risk-free rate.
- Treating factor premiums as certain: they are estimated from history and vary across periods.
- Reading a nonzero alpha as evidence of skill without controlling for exposure to value, size and momentum.
- Saying the APT requires the market portfolio. It does not; that requirement belongs to the CAPM.

                                        {"mkt": 0.05, "smb": 0.02, "hml": 0.04}), 0.084)

model:
```python
def apt_expected_return(rf, loadings, premiums):
    """Risk-free rate plus sum of loading * premium over the factors."""
    total = rf
    for factor, premium in premiums.items():
        total += loadings.get(factor, 0.0) * premium
    return total
```
hint: Loop over the factors that have premiums and look up each loading, defaulting to 0.
hint: `loadings.get(factor, 0.0)` returns 0.0 when the factor is missing.
solution: Start from `rf`, then for each factor add loading times premium, treating a missing loading as zero. Looping over `premiums` (not `loadings`) means a loading on a factor with no stated premium is ignored, which is consistent with the pricing relation.
