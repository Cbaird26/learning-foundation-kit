@article
Most households do not hold individual stocks and bonds directly. They hold shares in a **fund**, a pooled portfolio managed on behalf of many investors. Pooling offers diversification, professional management, liquidity and low minimum investments, in exchange for fees. In the United States, funds that sell shares to the public are mostly regulated as investment companies under the Investment Company Act of 1940, with the SEC as regulator.

### Types of investment companies

| Type | Shares | Price | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Open-end fund** (mutual fund) | Created and redeemed at the fund | Net asset value (NAV), set once a day | Investors buy from and sell to the fund |
| **Closed-end fund** | Fixed number, traded on an exchange | Market price, often not equal to NAV | Can trade at a premium or discount |
| **Unit investment trust** | Fixed portfolio, units outstanding until termination | Near NAV | Not actively managed |
| **Exchange-traded fund** (ETF) | Created and redeemed in large blocks | Exchange price, close to NAV | Trades intraday like a stock |
| **Hedge fund** | Private partnership, limited investors | NAV, with lock-ups | Not registered like mutual funds; flexible strategies and fee structures |

### Net asset value

An open-end fund's **net asset value per share** is

$$\text{NAV} = \frac{\text{market value of assets} - \text{liabilities}}{\text{shares outstanding}}.$$

Investors buy and redeem at the next NAV computed after the market close, so the price does not move during the day. A closed-end fund's premium or discount is $(P - \text{NAV})/\text{NAV}$, where $P$ is its exchange price.

### How ETFs stay near NAV

An ETF has **authorized participants** (large broker-dealers) who may create or redeem shares with the fund in large blocks called creation units, usually by exchanging a basket of the underlying securities rather than cash (**in-kind**). If the ETF's market price rises above the NAV of its basket, an authorized participant buys the basket, delivers it to the fund for new ETF shares and sells them at the higher price; if the price falls below NAV, the process reverses. This arbitrage keeps price close to NAV, in contrast with closed-end funds. Because in-kind redemptions can remove low-basis securities without selling them, ETFs often distribute fewer capital gains than mutual funds; this is general education, not tax advice. ETF investors pay the expense ratio plus trading costs such as the bid-ask spread.

### Costs of investing in funds

- The **expense ratio** is the annual percentage of assets deducted for management, administration and distribution (including any 12b-1 fee).
- A **front-end load** is a sales charge deducted from the purchase amount; a **back-end load** is charged when shares are sold.
- A **hedge fund** commonly charges a management fee on assets plus an incentive fee on profits ("2 and 20": 2% and 20%), sometimes only on returns above a **hurdle** and subject to a **high-water mark**.

Fees are certain; returns are not. To a first approximation a fund's net return equals its gross return minus its expense ratio, and the difference compounds. Over 30 years, a fee of 1% a year instead of 0.1% is the difference between growing by a factor of 5.7 and 7.4 at the same gross return of 7%. Since the average actively managed fund must hold the market in aggregate before costs, its average investor must trail the market after costs; whether individual managers add enough to cover their fees is the subject of Lesson 12.

@example
title: NAV and premium or discount
problem: A fund holds securities worth \$250 million and cash of \$5 million. It owes \$3 million in accrued fees and has 10 million shares outstanding. Find the NAV per share. If the fund is closed-end and trades at \$22.50, what is the premium or discount?
step: Net assets: $250 + 5 - 3 = \$252$ million.
step: $\text{NAV} = 252/10 = \$25.20$ per share.
step: Discount: $(22.50 - 25.20)/25.20 = -10.71\%$.
step: An investor buying at \$22.50 pays 89.29 cents per dollar of assets. If the fund were an ETF, an authorized participant would buy the underlying basket and redeem it for new shares, but a closed-end fund cannot create shares, so the discount can persist.
answer: NAV \$25.20; the fund trades at a 10.71% discount.

@example
title: The long-run cost of fees
problem: You invest \$10,000 for 30 years. The portfolio's gross return is 7% a year before fees (arithmetic: net return equals gross minus expense ratio). Compare a fund with a 1.00% expense ratio and one with 0.10%.
step: Net returns are 6.00% and 6.90% a year.
step: $10{,}000(1.06)^{30} = \$57{,}434.91$ and $10{,}000(1.069)^{30} = \$74{,}016.95$.
step: The difference is \$16,582, or 22% less wealth from the higher-cost fund (57,435 versus 74,017), though the fee difference is only 0.9 percentage points a year.
answer: \$57,434.91 versus \$74,016.95.

@mistakes
- Treating a closed-end fund's price as its NAV, or an open-end fund's price as moving through the day.
- Judging a fee of 1% as small because it is "only 1%": it is a share of assets every year, so it takes a larger share of the return.
- Computing a hedge fund's incentive fee on gross profit instead of on profit after the management fee, or ignoring the hurdle, when the terms say otherwise.
- Assuming that an ETF's market price always equals its NAV exactly; the arbitrage keeps them close, not identical.

model:
```python
def hedge_fund_net(gross, mgmt_fee, incentive_fee, hurdle=0.0):
    """Net return after a management fee and a hurdle-based incentive fee."""
    after_mgmt = gross - mgmt_fee
    incentive = incentive_fee * max(0.0, after_mgmt - hurdle)
    return after_mgmt - incentive
```
hint: Compute the return after the management fee first.
hint: The incentive fee applies only to the part of that return above the hurdle; use `max(0.0, ...)`.
solution: The incentive fee is a call-option-like payoff on the return above the hurdle: it is zero when the return after the management fee is below the hurdle and a fixed fraction of the excess otherwise. With a 2% management fee and 20% incentive fee, an 18% gross return becomes 16% and then 12.8% net; a loss pays no incentive fee but still pays the management fee.
