@article
Suppose a stock starts the year at \$50, pays a \$1 dividend, and ends at \$54. You earned \$5 on \$50 invested. Everything in investments begins with this idea, and with a warning: a single number never describes a risky investment, because you need both what it earned and how much that earning varied.

### Holding-period return

The **holding-period return** (HPR) over one period is

$$R_t = \frac{P_t - P_{t-1} + D_t}{P_{t-1}} = \frac{P_t + D_t}{P_{t-1}} - 1.$$

Here the HPR is $(54 - 50 + 1)/50 = 10\%$. Returns over several periods **link geometrically**: wealth grows by $(1+R_1)(1+R_2)\cdots(1+R_n)$. Returns for different periods are never added to get a multi-period return.

### Two averages

For a sample of $n$ periodic returns, the **arithmetic mean** and the **geometric mean** are

$$\bar{R}_A = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n} R_t, \qquad \bar{R}_G = \left[\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1+R_t)\right]^{1/n} - 1.$$

The geometric mean is the constant per-period return that gives the same ending wealth, so it answers "what did I actually earn per year?". The arithmetic mean is the best estimate of the return of a single future period, which is why expected-return models use it. Unless returns never vary, $\bar{R}_G < \bar{R}_A$, and a useful approximation is

$$\bar{R}_G \approx \bar{R}_A - \tfrac{1}{2}\sigma^2.$$

The more volatile the returns, the bigger the gap. A return of $+50\%$ followed by $-50\%$ has an arithmetic mean of 0% but leaves you with $1.5 \times 0.5 = 0.75$ of your money, a geometric mean of $-13.4\%$ per period.

### Time-weighted and money-weighted returns

When you add or withdraw money during the measurement period, there are two sensible answers. The **time-weighted return** (TWR) links the sub-period returns between cash flows, so it ignores their size and timing; it is the right measure of a manager's skill, because the manager does not control client flows. The **money-weighted return** (MWR) is the internal rate of return of the cash flows, so it is affected by when you added money: it measures what *you* earned. Both are per-period (here annual) effective rates.

### Risk: variance and standard deviation

For returns $R_1,\dots,R_n$ observed as a **sample**, the unbiased variance and standard deviation are

$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(R_t - \bar{R}_A\right)^2, \qquad s = \sqrt{s^2}.$$

(A population variance divides by $n$; use it only when you have the whole population or a given probability distribution.) If returns are independent and identically distributed with periodic mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, then over $k$ periods the mean grows like $k\mu$ and the standard deviation like $\sigma\sqrt{k}$, so monthly figures annualize as $12\mu_m$ and $\sigma_m\sqrt{12}$. That arithmetic approximation is what practitioners usually quote.

With a normal distribution, the probability of a loss is $\Phi(-\mu/\sigma)$. For $\mu = 8\%$ and $\sigma = 20\%$ it is $\Phi(-0.4) = 34.5\%$.

### Real returns

Inflation erodes purchasing power. The exact **real return** from a nominal return $R$ and inflation $i$ is

$$r = \frac{1+R}{1+i} - 1 \approx R - i.$$

Current return and inflation data are on FRED; every figure in this course is hypothetical.

@example
title: Averages and standard deviation of annual returns
problem: A portfolio earned 12%, -8%, 20% and 5% in four consecutive years. Find the arithmetic mean, the geometric mean and the sample standard deviation.
step: Arithmetic mean: $(12 - 8 + 20 + 5)/4 = 7.25\%$.
step: Growth factor: $1.12 \times 0.92 \times 1.20 \times 1.05 = 1.298304$, so the total four-year return is 29.83%.
step: Geometric mean: $1.298304^{1/4} - 1 = 6.744\%$, below the arithmetic mean.
step: Deviations from 7.25: $4.75, -15.25, 12.75, -2.25$. Squared: $22.5625, 232.5625, 162.5625, 5.0625$, which sum to $422.75$.
step: Sample variance is $422.75/3 = 140.917$ (in percent squared), so $s = 11.87\%$. A population formula would divide by 4 and give 10.28%, so always say which one is used.
answer: Arithmetic mean 7.25%, geometric mean 6.74%, sample standard deviation 11.87%.

@example
title: Time-weighted versus money-weighted
problem: You invest \$1,000 at the start of year 1. It is worth \$1,200 at the end of year 1, when you add another \$1,000. At the end of year 2 the account is worth \$2,090. Compute the annual time-weighted and money-weighted returns.
step: Year 1 return is $1200/1000 - 1 = 20\%$. Year 2 starts with \$2,200 and ends at \$2,090, a return of $-5\%$.
step: TWR: $\sqrt{1.20 \times 0.95} - 1 = 6.77\%$ per year.
step: MWR solves $-1000 - \dfrac{1000}{1+r} + \dfrac{2090}{(1+r)^2} = 0$, which gives $r = 2.97\%$ per year.
step: The MWR is lower because most of your money was invested during the bad year. The manager earned 6.77% per year on a dollar kept invested throughout, but your dollars earned less.
answer: TWR 6.77% per year, MWR 2.97% per year.

@mistakes
- Averaging multi-year returns arithmetically when asked what an investor actually earned per year; use the geometric mean.
- Dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$ for a sample standard deviation, without saying so.
- Annualizing a monthly return by multiplying the monthly standard deviation by 12 instead of $\sqrt{12}$.
- Judging a manager by the money-weighted return when the client, not the manager, chose the timing of the cash flows.

model:
```python
def money_weighted_return(flows):
    """Per-period IRR of conventional cash flows, by bisection."""
    def npv(rate):
        return sum(cf / (1 + rate) ** t for t, cf in enumerate(flows))

    low, high = -0.99, 10.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        if npv(mid) > 0:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return (low + high) / 2
```
hint: Define an NPV function of the rate; it is positive at low rates and negative at high rates.
hint: Repeatedly halve the interval `[low, high]`, keeping the half in which the NPV changes sign.
solution: Because the NPV falls as the rate rises, a positive NPV at the midpoint means the IRR is higher, so move `low` up; otherwise move `high` down. After 200 halvings the interval is far narrower than the test tolerance.

answer: 13.86
unit: %
