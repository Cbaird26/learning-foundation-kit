@article
Insurance is a way to swap a small, certain cost (the **premium**) for protection against a large, uncertain loss. Many people each pay a premium, and the insurer pays the few who suffer a loss. This is called **risk pooling**.

### Health insurance

The main terms:

- **Premium**: the regular payment to keep coverage.
- **Deductible**: the amount you pay each plan year before the plan starts sharing costs.
- **Copay**: a flat fee for a service, such as a doctor visit.
- **Coinsurance**: your percentage share of costs after the deductible.
- **Out-of-pocket maximum**: a cap on what you pay in a year for covered, in-network care; the plan pays 100% after it.

Assuming a covered, in-network bill and the plan's allowed amount, your cost is

$$\text{you pay} = \min\Big(\text{deductible} + c\,(\text{bill} - \text{deductible}),\ \text{out-of-pocket max}\Big)$$

when the bill exceeds the deductible, where $c$ is your coinsurance rate (if the bill is smaller than the deductible you pay the whole bill). Premiums are not counted toward the deductible or the maximum.

### Auto insurance

**Liability** coverage pays for damage and injuries you cause to others (and is required in nearly every state). **Collision** pays to repair your own car after a crash, and **comprehensive** covers other damage such as theft or hail. With collision coverage, a claim pays the repair cost minus your **deductible**.

### Renters insurance

A landlord's policy covers the building, not your belongings. **Renters insurance** covers your possessions and your liability. Policies pay either **actual cash value** (replacement cost minus depreciation) or **replacement cost** (the price of a new item); the second costs more but pays more.

### Life insurance

**Life insurance** pays a **death benefit** to beneficiaries. **Term** insurance covers a set period and is simpler and cheaper; permanent insurance covers a lifetime and builds a cash value, at a higher premium. Life insurance matters mostly when someone depends on your income, such as children or a spouse. A person with no dependents may not need it.

### Is insurance worth it?

Suppose there is a 1% chance of a \$20,000 loss in a year. The **expected loss** is $0.01 \times 20{,}000 = \$200$. A premium has to exceed the expected loss, because the insurer has costs and profit, so on average the buyer pays more than the average payout. We insure anyway when the loss would be financially devastating; for small losses you can absorb, a higher deductible and a savings buffer are often better.

All figures here are examples; real plans vary.

@example
title: Who pays the hospital bill?
problem: A plan has a \$1,500 deductible, 20% coinsurance and a \$4,000 out-of-pocket maximum. Find what you pay on an in-network allowed bill of \$10,000, and on one of \$40,000.
step: For \$10,000: deductible \$1,500, plus 20% of the remaining $10{,}000 - 1{,}500 = 8{,}500$, which is \$1,700. You pay $1{,}500 + 1{,}700 = \$3{,}200$, below the maximum, so the plan pays \$6,800.
step: For \$40,000: before the cap, you would owe $1{,}500 + 0.20 \times 38{,}500 = 1{,}500 + 7{,}700 = \$9{,}200$.
step: The out-of-pocket maximum caps it at \$4,000, so the plan pays $40{,}000 - 4{,}000 = \$36{,}000$.
answer: \$3,200 on the \$10,000 bill; \$4,000 on the \$40,000 bill.

@example
title: A collision claim and a renters claim
problem: A repair costs \$3,400 and the policy's collision deductible is \$500. Separately, a stolen laptop would cost \$1,200 to replace new and has depreciated 40%. What does each policy pay if the renters policy pays actual cash value?
step: Collision: the insurer pays the repair minus the deductible, $3{,}400 - 500 = \$2{,}900$.
step: Actual cash value of the laptop: $1{,}200 \times (1 - 0.40) = \$720$.
step: Under a replacement cost policy the renters claim would instead pay about \$1,200 (less any deductible), so the type of coverage matters.
answer: \$2,900 for the repair; \$720 for the laptop at actual cash value.

@mistakes
- Thinking the deductible is paid again for each bill: it is a yearly amount that you meet once per plan year.
- Counting the premium toward the deductible or the out-of-pocket maximum.
- Choosing a plan by the lowest premium alone, without considering the deductible and the maximum.
- Insuring small losses you can afford while skipping coverage for large ones, such as liability.

@quiz self
prompt: A friend says, "My renters policy costs \$18 a month. The chance my apartment is burgled is small, so insurance is a waste of money." In four or five sentences, respond using the ideas of expected loss and risk pooling.
hint: Compare the average cost with the size of the worst loss.
solution: On average, an insurer collects more in premiums than it pays in claims, so for a small chance of loss the expected payout is lower than the premium; your friend is right about that average. But insurance is not meant to make money; it protects against a loss too large to pay from savings, such as replacing all your belongings or paying for damage you cause to others, which is covered by the liability part. Because many customers pool their risk, the few who have a loss are paid from everyone's premiums. An \$18 monthly premium (\$216 a year) is a small price against a loss of thousands of dollars.
rubric: Says insurance premiums exceed the expected loss on average, so the friend is right about averages.
rubric: Explains that the purpose is protection against a large loss the person could not afford.
rubric: Mentions risk pooling and covered items such as belongings or liability.
