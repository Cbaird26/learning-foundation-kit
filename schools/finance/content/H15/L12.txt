@article
Knowing how money works is half of personal finance. The other half is not losing it to people who want it. Scammers target every age group, and the best defense is a short list of habits.

### Red flags of a scam

- **Guaranteed high returns with no risk.** Higher expected return always comes with higher risk (Week 10).
- **Pressure to act now**, or secrecy ("don't tell your bank").
- **Unusual payment methods**: gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency or money sent to a "safe account." Legitimate agencies and companies do not demand these.
- **Unsolicited contact** claiming to be a bank, government agency or employer. **Phishing** uses fake emails, texts or sites to steal passwords and account numbers.
- **Prizes you must pay to claim.**

If something seems off, stop, and contact the organization using a phone number or website you already know, not one given in the message.

### Identity theft

**Identity theft** is using your personal information (Social Security number, account numbers) to open accounts or make purchases. Protect yourself: use unique passwords and multi-factor authentication, check your credit reports (weekly at AnnualCreditReport.com), and consider a **credit freeze**, which is free and blocks new credit in your name until you lift it. If you are a victim, report it to the FTC (IdentityTheft.gov), notify the affected companies, and place a freeze or fraud alert.

### Protections and who helps

For a stolen **credit card**, federal law limits your liability for unauthorized charges to \$50 (and many issuers waive even that). For a lost or stolen **debit card** under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act (Regulation E), the limit depends on how fast you report: \$50 if you report within 2 business days of learning of the loss, up to \$500 after that, and potentially more if you wait more than 60 days after your statement. Debit fraud takes your own cash first, so many people favor credit for online purchases. Report problems promptly and check your issuer's policy. Agencies that help include the **CFPB** (consumer finance; its activity has been changing since 2025, so check its current role), the **FTC**, the **SEC** and **FINRA** (investments and advisers), and your state attorney general.

### Understanding true cost

Compare costs on a yearly basis. A **payday loan** charges a fee per \$100 borrowed for a short term. Assuming a 14-day term and a simple annualization by $365/14$, a \$15 fee per \$100 is an APR of $15\% \times 365/14 = 391.07\%$. Compounded, the effective rate is far higher.

### Decisions: opportunity cost

Every choice has an **opportunity cost**, the value of the next-best use of the money. A habit of \$150 a month costs more than \$150: invested at 7% a year compounded monthly for 10 years, that money would become \$25,962.72. Use the same steps for any decision: define the goal, list the options, compare the total cost over the whole period with the same assumptions, check risks, and decide when you are not rushed.

@example
title: The yearly cost of a payday loan
problem: A lender charges \$15 for each \$100 borrowed for 14 days. Find the APR using the simple annualization $365/14$, and the effective annual rate if the loan is rolled over, with the fee charged again every 14 days for a year ($365/14$ terms).
step: The rate for one 14-day term is $15/100 = 15\%$.
step: Simple annualization: $15\% \times 365/14 = 3.9107$, or an APR of 391.07%.
step: If the loan is renewed for the whole year, the fee is charged every term, and the compounded growth of the debt is $1.15^{365/14} - 1 = 37.24$, which is about 3,724% per year. Compare that with a credit card charging a 24% APR.
answer: About 391% APR (simple annualization), and a much higher effective annual rate if rolled over.

@example
title: A promise that is too good
problem: A promoter promises "a guaranteed 2% return every week." What yearly return does this imply if it compounds for 52 weeks, and what should you conclude?
step: The growth factor over 52 weeks is $1.02^{52} = 2.8003$.
step: The implied yearly return is $2.8003 - 1 = 180.03\%$. A \$1,000 deposit would be \$2,800 after one year, and about \$7,842 after two.
step: No legitimate investment can guarantee this: the promised return is far above what even high-risk assets deliver, so it is a classic warning sign.
answer: 180.03% a year; treat a guarantee like this as a red flag for a scam.

@mistakes
- Trusting a message because it uses a real company's name; contact the company yourself with details you already have.
- Paying by gift card, wire or cryptocurrency on request: these payments are hard or impossible to reverse.
- Assuming credit and debit cards give the same protection; reporting delays can raise your liability on debit cards.
- Comparing loans by the fee per period instead of the annual cost.
