@article
Saving keeps your money safe; **investing** puts it to work in assets that may grow, with the risk that they may also fall. This lesson is general education about how investments work, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.

### The main building blocks

- A **stock** is a share of ownership in a company. Its return comes from price changes and **dividends**. Prices can swing widely.
- A **bond** is a loan to a company or government. The issuer pays interest and returns the principal at maturity. Bonds are usually less volatile than stocks, but their prices move with interest rates and the issuer can default.
- A **fund** pools money from many investors to hold many securities. A **mutual fund** is priced once a day; an **exchange-traded fund (ETF)** trades during the day like a stock. An **index fund** aims to match a market index rather than beat it.

Brokerage accounts in the United States have SIPC protection if a firm fails, but SIPC does not protect against losses from falling prices. Stock trades settle one business day after the trade (T+1).

### Return

The **holding-period return** is

$$R = \frac{\text{ending price} - \text{beginning price} + \text{income}}{\text{beginning price}}.$$

For a series of yearly returns, the **arithmetic mean** is their simple average and the **geometric mean** is the constant yearly rate that gives the same final wealth:

$$\bar{R}_G = \Big[\prod (1 + R_t)\Big]^{1/n} - 1.$$

The geometric mean is the one that describes growth of your money, and it is never above the arithmetic mean. Losses hurt more than equal gains help: a 20% loss needs a $1/0.8 - 1 = 25\%$ gain to get back to even.

### Risk, return and diversification

**Risk** here means how variable returns are, often measured by the standard deviation. Higher expected returns usually come with higher risk; there is no free lunch, and a promise of high returns with no risk is a warning sign (Week 12). **Diversification** means holding many assets that do not all move together. It removes much of the risk that belongs to a single company or industry, but not **market risk**, which affects nearly everything. The expected return of a portfolio is the weighted average of its parts:

$$E[R_p] = w_1 E[R_1] + w_2 E[R_2].$$

### Fees and compounding

A fund's **expense ratio** is its yearly fee as a percent of assets. Assume a fee reduces the return each year by its rate. A small fee difference compounds over decades. Time works similarly in the other direction: at 8% a year, money doubles in about $72/8 = 9$ years (the rule of 72).

All returns here are hypothetical. Historical returns do not predict future returns, and you should look up real fund data and costs from the fund's prospectus and sources such as SEC EDGAR.

@example
title: Average return versus growth
problem: A stock fund returns +8%, -4%, +12% and +5% in four years. Find the arithmetic mean, the geometric mean and the growth of \$1.
step: Arithmetic mean: $(8 - 4 + 12 + 5)/4 = 21/4 = 5.25\%$.
step: Growth of \$1: $1.08 \times 0.96 \times 1.12 \times 1.05 = 1.21928$, so every dollar became \$1.2193 (a total return of 21.93%).
step: Geometric mean: $1.21928^{1/4} - 1 = 5.08\%$. A steady 5.08% a year gives the same final wealth, less than the 5.25% simple average.
answer: Arithmetic 5.25%; geometric 5.08%; \$1 grows to \$1.2193.

@example
title: What a fee can cost
problem: Two index funds earn the same 7% a year before fees. Fund A charges an expense ratio of 0.05% and Fund B charges 1.00%. You invest \$10,000 for 30 years and make no other deposits. Assume the return is a constant 7% before fees and the fee reduces it each year. How do the ending balances compare?
step: Net annual returns are 6.95% for Fund A and 6.00% for Fund B.
step: Fund A: $10{,}000 \times 1.0695^{30} = \$75{,}062.61$.
step: Fund B: $10{,}000 \times 1.06^{30} = \$57{,}434.91$.
step: The 0.95% difference in fees costs $75{,}062.61 - 57{,}434.91 = \$17{,}627.70$, almost a quarter of Fund A's balance.
answer: \$75,062.61 against \$57,434.91, a gap of about \$17,628.

@mistakes
- Averaging yearly returns with the arithmetic mean and calling it your growth rate; use the geometric mean for compound growth.
- Thinking a 20% loss is recovered by a 20% gain (it takes 25%).
- Believing diversification removes all risk; it reduces company-specific risk, not market risk.
- Ignoring fees because they look small: a 1% fee takes a growing slice of your wealth every year.

prompt: A fund returns +5%, +10%, -5% and +2% in four consecutive years. What is its geometric mean annual return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 2.86
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1.05 * 1.10 * 0.95 * 1.02) ** 0.25 - 1) * 100
hint: Multiply the four growth factors and take the fourth root.
solution: The growth factors multiply to $1.05 \times 1.10 \times 0.95 \times 1.02 = 1.119195$. The fourth root is $1.119195^{0.25} = 1.02855$, so the geometric mean is 2.86% a year.
