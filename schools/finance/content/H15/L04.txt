@article
Every paycheck has a tax withheld, but your actual tax bill is settled once a year when you file a **tax return**. The return answers: how much tax do you owe for the year, and how does that compare with what was already withheld?

All figures here are for **tax year 2026** (income earned in 2026, reported in the spring of 2027) for a **single** filer, and tax figures change every year. This is general education, not tax advice.

### The path from income to tax

1. **Gross income**: wages (reported to you on Form **W-2** by each employer), interest and other income.
2. **Adjustments** (for example some retirement contributions) give **adjusted gross income (AGI)**. We will ignore adjustments for simple returns.
3. Subtract the **standard deduction**, a flat amount you may subtract without listing expenses: \$16,100 for a single filer in 2026 (\$32,200 married filing jointly, \$24,150 head of household). You can instead **itemize** if your listed deductions are larger.
4. The result is **taxable income**: $\max(0,\ \text{income} - \text{deduction})$.
5. Apply the **tax brackets** to find the tax, then subtract any **credits**.

### Marginal brackets

Tax brackets are **marginal**: only the income inside each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. For single filers in 2026:

| Taxable income | Rate |
|---|---|
| Up to \$12,400 | 10% |
| \$12,400 to \$50,400 | 12% |
| \$50,400 to \$105,700 | 22% |
| \$105,700 to \$201,775 | 24% |
| \$201,775 to \$256,225 | 32% |
| \$256,225 to \$640,600 | 35% |
| Over \$640,600 | 37% |

Moving into a higher bracket never reduces your take-home pay, because only the dollars above the threshold are taxed at the higher rate. The **marginal rate** is the rate on your next dollar; the **average (effective) rate** is total tax divided by income, and is always lower.

### Deductions versus credits

A **deduction** lowers taxable income, so it saves tax equal to the deduction times your marginal rate. A **credit** lowers the tax itself, dollar for dollar. A \$1,000 credit is worth \$1,000; a \$1,000 deduction in the 22% bracket is worth \$220.

### Refund or balance due

Your employer withholds tax using the Form **W-4** you filled out. At filing time:

$$\text{refund} = \text{tax withheld} - \text{tax owed}\quad(\text{a negative result is a balance due}).$$

A refund is not a bonus: it is your own money returned, without interest. Returns for a calendar year are generally due in mid-April of the next year, on **Form 1040**, filed electronically or on paper with the IRS. Payroll taxes (FICA, Week 1) are separate from income tax.

@example
title: A simple return
problem: Nina is single, earned \$38,000 in wages in 2026 and had \$2,900 of federal income tax withheld. She has no other income and takes the standard deduction. Find her tax and her refund or balance due.
step: Taxable income is $38{,}000 - 16{,}100 = \$21{,}900$.
step: The first \$12,400 is taxed at 10%: $0.10 \times 12{,}400 = \$1{,}240$.
step: The remaining $21{,}900 - 12{,}400 = \$9{,}500$ is taxed at 12%: $0.12 \times 9{,}500 = \$1{,}140$. Total tax is $1{,}240 + 1{,}140 = \$2{,}380$ (an average rate of $2{,}380/38{,}000 = 6.26\%$ of wages).
step: Withholding of \$2,900 exceeds the tax of \$2,380, so the refund is $2{,}900 - 2{,}380 = \$520$.
answer: Tax \$2,380; refund \$520.

@example
title: The marginal rate in action
problem: A single filer has wages of \$70,000 in 2026 and takes the standard deduction. Find the tax. If a raise adds \$1,000 of wages, how much more tax is owed?
step: Taxable income is $70{,}000 - 16{,}100 = \$53{,}900$, which reaches into the 22% bracket.
step: Tax is $1{,}240 + 0.12 \times (50{,}400 - 12{,}400) + 0.22 \times (53{,}900 - 50{,}400) = 1{,}240 + 4{,}560 + 770 = \$6{,}570$.
step: The extra \$1,000 is entirely inside the 22% bracket, so tax rises by $0.22 \times 1{,}000 = \$220$ and the worker keeps \$780 of it (before payroll tax).
answer: Tax \$6,570; the extra \$1,000 adds \$220 of income tax.

@mistakes
- Applying the top bracket rate to all income instead of only the income inside that bracket.
- Forgetting to subtract the standard deduction before using the brackets.
- Treating a refund as extra income: it only returns money that was withheld in excess.
- Confusing a deduction with a credit: a deduction saves only the marginal rate times its amount.

model:
```python
BRACKETS = [
    (12400, 0.10),
    (50400, 0.12),
    (105700, 0.22),
    (201775, 0.24),
    (256225, 0.32),
    (640600, 0.35),
    (float("inf"), 0.37),
]


def income_tax_single(taxable):
    """Federal income tax, single filer, tax year 2026."""
    tax = 0.0
    lower = 0
    for upper, rate in BRACKETS:
        if taxable > lower:
            tax += (min(taxable, upper) - lower) * rate
        lower = upper
    return tax
```
hint: Loop over the brackets in order, remembering where each one starts.
hint: In each bracket tax only the slice `min(taxable, upper) - lower`, and only if `taxable` is above `lower`.
solution: Each bracket taxes the part of income between its lower and upper limits at its own rate. Keeping a `lower` variable and using `min(taxable, upper)` limits the slice to the income that actually reaches the bracket; taxable income of zero or less skips every bracket and returns 0.
