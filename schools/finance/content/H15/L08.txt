@article
Housing is usually the biggest item in a budget. Renting and buying are both reasonable choices, and neither is always cheaper. The goal is to count every cost honestly.

### Renting

A renter pays **rent**, usually fixed for a lease term (often a year) and often rising when the lease renews. There is typically a **security deposit**, returned if you leave the place in good condition, and **renters insurance** (Week 9). The landlord pays for major repairs and property taxes. Renting is flexible: you can move at the end of the lease and you do not risk a drop in a home's value.

### Buying

A buyer usually pays a **down payment**, a share of the price paid in cash, and borrows the rest with a **mortgage**. A common down payment is 20% of the price; with less than 20% down, lenders often require **private mortgage insurance (PMI)**, which protects the lender and adds to the monthly cost. At purchase there are also **closing costs** (fees to complete the sale, often a few percent of the price).

The monthly cost of owning is more than the mortgage payment. The usual list is **PITI**: principal, interest, **property taxes** and **homeowners insurance**, plus **maintenance** (a common guideline is to expect to spend a percent or two of the home's value each year) and utilities. Owners also build **equity** (the home's value minus the mortgage balance) as the loan is paid down, but if prices fall, equity can shrink.

### The mortgage payment

The payment on a fixed-rate loan with principal $P$, monthly rate $i = \text{APR}/12$ and $n$ monthly payments is the same installment formula from Week 6:

$$M = P\,\frac{i}{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}.$$

A 30-year mortgage has $n = 360$. A 15-year mortgage has a higher payment but far less total interest. In the first months nearly all the payment is interest.

### Is it affordable?

A common guideline used by lenders is the **28% rule**: housing costs (PITI) should not exceed about 28% of **gross** monthly income, and total debt payments about 36%. Guidelines are not guarantees. Compare options with the same assumptions: how long you will stay, expected rent increases, and the costs of buying and selling. This is general education, not personal advice, and all rates here are hypothetical.

@example
title: The full monthly cost of a mortgage
problem: A home costs \$300,000. The buyer puts 20% down and finances the rest with a 30-year fixed mortgage at a 6.5% APR (monthly payments at the end of each month). Property tax is \$3,600 a year and homeowners insurance is \$1,200 a year. Find the monthly payment, the first month's interest and principal, and the monthly PITI.
step: Down payment is $0.20 \times 300{,}000 = \$60{,}000$, so the loan is $P = \$240{,}000$. The monthly rate is $i = 0.065/12 = 0.0054167$ and $n = 360$.
step: $M = 240{,}000 \times \dfrac{0.0054167}{1 - 1.0054167^{-360}} = \$1{,}516.96$.
step: First month: interest is $240{,}000 \times 0.0054167 = \$1{,}300.00$, so principal repaid is $1{,}516.96 - 1{,}300.00 = \$216.96$.
step: Add taxes and insurance per month: $3{,}600/12 + 1{,}200/12 = 300 + 100 = \$400$, so PITI is $1{,}516.96 + 400 = \$1{,}916.96$.
answer: Payment \$1,516.96; first month interest \$1,300.00 and principal \$216.96; PITI \$1,916.96.

@example
title: Five years of rent
problem: Rent is \$1,500 a month for the first year and rises 3% at the start of each new year (the monthly rent is constant within a year). What is the total rent over five years?
step: Yearly rent in year $k$ (with $k = 0, 1, \dots, 4$) is $12 \times 1{,}500 \times 1.03^k$.
step: The yearly amounts are \$18,000.00, \$18,540.00, \$19,096.20, \$19,669.09 and \$20,259.16.
step: The sum, before rounding each year, is \$95,564.44 (about \$95,564), which is \$5,564 more than five years at a flat \$1,500 (\$90,000).
answer: About \$95,564 in total rent.

@mistakes
- Comparing only the rent with only the mortgage payment, and ignoring taxes, insurance, maintenance and closing costs.
- Assuming a home always gains value; prices can fall, and selling also has costs.
- Using the 28% rule with net pay instead of gross monthly income.
- Forgetting PMI when the down payment is under 20%, or assuming the interest rate guarantees the total cost.
