@article
Money you need soon should be safe and easy to reach. Banks and credit unions offer accounts built for that, and an **emergency fund** is the job such an account does best.

### Account types

A **checking account** is for spending: you deposit paychecks and pay bills with a debit card, checks or electronic transfers. It usually pays little or no interest. A **savings account** keeps money apart from daily spending and pays interest. Compare accounts by their fees (monthly fees, overdraft fees, minimum balances), access (branches, ATMs, transfers) and rate.

### Interest and APY

Savings rates are quoted as an **APY** (annual percentage yield), which includes compounding: it is the effective annual rate (the same idea as the effective annual rate, EAR). If an account is quoted at a nominal 4.8% compounded monthly, the APY is $(1 + 0.048/12)^{12} - 1 = 4.91\%$. At a 3.6% APY, \$5,000 left for one year earns $0.036 \times 5{,}000 = \$180$. All the rates in this lesson are hypothetical; current rates are published by banks and change with the economy.

### Deposit insurance

In the United States the **FDIC** insures deposits at member banks up to \$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions have equivalent coverage from the NCUA. If a bank fails, insured deposits are protected up to the limit. Amounts above the limit in the same category at the same bank are not insured, and the insurance covers deposits, not investments such as stocks.

### Ways to pay

A **debit card** pulls money directly from your checking account. A **credit card** borrows from the card issuer, and you repay later (Week 5). **ACH** transfers and checks move money between accounts. An **overdraft** occurs when you spend more than your balance; the bank may decline the payment or cover it for a fee, so know your account's policy.

### The emergency fund

An emergency fund covers shocks such as a job loss, a medical bill or a car repair so you do not borrow at a high rate. A common target is three to six months of **essential** expenses (not total spending), kept in a safe, accessible account:

$$\text{target} = \text{monthly essential expenses} \times \text{months of cushion}.$$

Building it takes time. Saving a fixed amount $P$ at the end of each month at a monthly rate $i$ gives a balance of $P\,\dfrac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i}$ after $n$ months. Interest helps, but the deposits do most of the work.

@example
title: How big should the fund be?
problem: Dev's essential expenses are rent \$1,050, utilities \$180, groceries \$310, transportation \$120 and phone \$95 each month. What does a three-month and a six-month fund require?
step: Add the essentials: $1{,}050 + 180 + 310 + 120 + 95 = \$1{,}755$ a month.
step: Three months: $3 \times 1{,}755 = \$5{,}265$. Six months: $6 \times 1{,}755 = \$10{,}530$.
step: Entertainment and other wants are left out, because in an emergency Dev can cut them.
answer: \$5,265 for three months and \$10,530 for six months.

@example
title: Saving \$200 a month
problem: Ana deposits \$200 at the end of each month for 12 months into a savings account with a nominal rate of 4% a year compounded monthly. What is the balance, and how much of it is interest?
step: The monthly rate is $i = 0.04/12 = 0.003333$ and there are $n = 12$ deposits, all at the end of the month.
step: The balance is $200 \times \dfrac{(1.003333)^{12} - 1}{0.003333} = 200 \times 12.22246 = \$2{,}444.49$.
step: Deposits total $12 \times 200 = \$2{,}400$, so the interest is $2{,}444.49 - 2{,}400 = \$44.49$.
answer: \$2,444.49, of which \$44.49 is interest.

@mistakes
- Comparing a nominal rate with an APY directly; convert to the same basis first.
- Sizing the emergency fund from total spending instead of essential spending.
- Assuming FDIC insurance covers each account separately: the limit applies per depositor, per bank and per ownership category, so two single-owner accounts at one bank share one \$250,000 limit.
- Treating a debit card as if it were credit: the money leaves your account immediately and overdrafts can be costly.

@quiz self
prompt: A friend wants to keep an emergency fund in a stock investment because "it will grow faster." In three or four sentences, explain why an emergency fund is usually kept in an insured, easy-to-reach account instead.
hint: Think about when you will need the money and what could happen to the price.
solution: An emergency fund has to be available at full value on short notice, and emergencies, such as a job loss, can happen when markets are down. Stock prices can fall sharply, so selling to cover an emergency could lock in a loss. A savings account is FDIC-insured up to the limit and its balance does not fall, so the money is there when needed, and the lower return is the price of that certainty.
rubric: Says an emergency fund must be accessible quickly and at a stable value.
rubric: Explains that stock prices can fall (possibly at the same time as the emergency), causing a loss when selling.
rubric: Mentions deposit insurance or stable principal as the benefit of a savings account.
