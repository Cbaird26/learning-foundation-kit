@article
Retirement can last decades, so starting small and early beats starting large and late. The tax code encourages retirement saving with special accounts. Rules and limits change yearly, so the 2026 figures below are for that year. This is general education, not tax advice.

### Employer plans and IRAs

A **401(k)** (or **403(b)** for schools and nonprofits) is an employer plan funded by payroll deductions. The employee **deferral limit** is \$24,500 in 2026, with a \$8,000 **catch-up** contribution at age 50 and over (\$11,250 at ages 60 to 63). Many employers add a **match** (Week 1): contributing enough to get the full match is usually the first step because the match is part of your pay. An **IRA** (individual retirement account) is opened by an individual, not through an employer; its limit is \$7,500 in 2026, plus \$1,100 catch-up at 50 and over.

### Traditional versus Roth

- **Traditional** (pre-tax): contributions reduce taxable income now, investments grow tax-deferred, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.
- **Roth**: contributions are made with after-tax money, investments grow tax-free, and **qualified** withdrawals are tax-free.

Neither is always better. If your tax rate is the same when you contribute and when you withdraw, the two give the same after-tax result. A traditional account tends to win if you expect a lower tax rate in retirement, and a Roth if you expect a higher one. Many people split between them.

### Rules to know

Withdrawing before age 59½ generally triggers ordinary income tax plus a 10% additional tax, with some exceptions. Traditional accounts require **required minimum distributions (RMDs)** starting at age 73 (age 75 for people born in 1960 or later). Retirement accounts are separate from your emergency fund (Week 3): the early-withdrawal cost is a reason not to count on them for emergencies.

### The power of time

With level monthly contributions $P$ at the end of each month, a nominal annual return $r$ (so $i = r/12$ monthly) and $n$ months, the balance is

$$FV = P\,\frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i}.$$

Starting earlier gives money more months to compound. These projections assume a constant return, which real markets do not deliver, and the result is in future (not today's) dollars: inflation reduces what the money buys.

@example
title: Saving from age 22 to 67
problem: You contribute \$400 at the end of every month from age 22 to age 67 (45 years). Assume a constant 7% a year compounded monthly, no fees, and ignore taxes and inflation. Find the balance at 67. Then compare with starting at 32 and saving the same amount for 35 years.
step: Monthly rate $i = 0.07/12 = 0.0058333$. Starting at 22: $n = 45 \times 12 = 540$, so $FV = 400 \times \dfrac{1.0058333^{540} - 1}{0.0058333} = \$1{,}517{,}037.87$.
step: Contributions total $400 \times 540 = \$216{,}000$, so growth supplies the rest.
step: Starting at 32: $n = 35 \times 12 = 420$ and $FV = \$720{,}421.84$ on $\$168{,}000$ of contributions.
step: A ten-year delay costs $1{,}517{,}037.87 - 720{,}421.84 = \$796{,}616.03$, although it saves only \$48,000 of contributions.
answer: \$1,517,037.87 starting at 22; \$720,421.84 starting at 32.

@example
title: Roth or traditional at the same tax rate
problem: You are in the 22% bracket. A traditional contribution of \$1,000 before tax costs you the same as a Roth contribution of \$780 (after the 22% tax). Both grow 30 years at 7% a year. Compare the after-tax result if the withdrawal is taxed at 22%, and at 12%.
step: Growth factor: $1.07^{30} = 7.6123$.
step: Roth: $780 \times 7.6123 = \$5{,}937.56$, tax-free.
step: Traditional: $1{,}000 \times 7.6123 = \$7{,}612.26$ before tax. At 22%: $7{,}612.26 \times 0.78 = \$5{,}937.56$, the same as the Roth. At 12%: $7{,}612.26 \times 0.88 = \$6{,}698.79$, more than the Roth.
answer: Equal at \$5,937.56 when the tax rate is unchanged; the traditional account is ahead (\$6,698.79) if the retirement rate is lower at 12%.

@mistakes
- Skipping the employer match, which is free money that is part of your pay.
- Assuming a Roth is always better (or always worse): it depends on the tax rate now versus later.
- Treating retirement accounts as an emergency fund, forgetting the 10% additional tax and income tax on early withdrawals.
- Reading a projection as a guarantee: it assumes a constant return and is in future dollars, not today's.

```python
def retirement_balance(monthly, annual_rate, years):
    """Future value of level end-of-month contributions."""
    months = int(round(years * 12))
    if annual_rate == 0:
        return monthly * months
    i = annual_rate / 12
    return monthly * ((1 + i) ** months - 1) / i
```
hint: The monthly rate is `annual_rate / 12` and the number of payments is `years * 12`.
hint: Use $FV = P\,((1+i)^n - 1)/i$, and handle a zero rate separately to avoid dividing by zero.
solution: Convert years to months and the annual rate to a monthly rate, then apply the future value of an annuity formula. A zero rate would divide by zero, so return the contributions added up (`monthly * months`) instead.

@quiz self
prompt: A 22-year-old in the 12% tax bracket says, "I should pick a Roth account." Explain in four or five sentences how the choice between Roth and traditional depends on taxes, and what the person should think about.
hint: Compare the tax rate on the money now with the tax rate when it is withdrawn.
solution: The two accounts differ only in when tax is paid: a traditional account defers it to withdrawal, while a Roth pays it now. If the tax rate at withdrawal equals the rate now, the after-tax results are the same. A young person in a low (12%) bracket may later be in a higher bracket, which favors the Roth because tax is paid at the low rate now; if their retirement rate is lower, a traditional account would win. Since future rates and tax laws are uncertain, many people hold both, and they should also claim any employer match first.
rubric: Explains that the difference is the timing of tax (now for a Roth, at withdrawal for a traditional account).
rubric: Says the account choice depends on comparing today's tax rate with the expected retirement rate, and that equal rates give equal results.
rubric: Notes that a low bracket now favors a Roth if the rate is likely to rise, and mentions uncertainty or splitting.
