@article
A budget is a plan for your money before the month happens. It answers three questions: how much comes in, where it goes, and what is left. It is not a punishment; it is how you decide on purpose instead of by accident.

### Start with net income

Build a budget from **net pay**, the amount you receive after taxes and deductions, because that is what you can spend. A biweekly paycheck arrives 26 times a year, so monthly net income is

$$\text{monthly net income} = \frac{\text{net per paycheck} \times 26}{12}.$$

In two months of the year you get three paychecks. Many people budget on the twice-a-month equivalent and treat the extra paycheck as a bonus for savings.

### Fixed, variable, needs and wants

**Fixed expenses** are the same each month (rent, a phone plan, a loan payment). **Variable expenses** change (groceries, gas, entertainment). Separately, **needs** are costs you must pay to live and work (housing, food, transportation, insurance, minimum debt payments), while **wants** are everything optional. Some items mix both: groceries are a need, but restaurant meals are a want.

### Cash flow and savings rate

$$\text{cash flow} = \text{income} - \text{expenses},\qquad \text{savings rate} = \frac{\text{amount saved}}{\text{income}}.$$

Positive cash flow is a **surplus**; negative is a **deficit**, which must be covered by savings or debt. A budget works only when the plan covers the deficit before it happens.

### A guideline: 50/30/20

One popular rule of thumb splits net income into 50% needs, 30% wants and 20% savings and debt repayment. It is a starting point, not a law: in a high-rent city needs may take far more than half, and the point of the exercise is to see the trade-offs.

### Small amounts add up

Annualize recurring costs to see their size: a \$4.75 purchase on 5 days a week for 52 weeks costs $4.75 \times 5 \times 52 = \$1{,}235$ a year. Equally, **pay yourself first**: move savings out on payday, so saving does not depend on what is left over. For irregular costs (a yearly insurance bill, holiday gifts) divide the annual amount by 12 and set that aside monthly, which some call a sinking fund.

| Category | Example | Type |
|---|---|---|
| Rent | \$1,050 | Fixed need |
| Groceries | \$310 | Variable need |
| Streaming | \$15 | Fixed want |
| Savings transfer | \$200 | Paid first |

@example
title: A first monthly budget
problem: Lena's net pay is \$2,640 a month. Her expenses are rent \$1,050, utilities \$180, groceries \$310, transportation \$120, phone \$95 and entertainment \$210. Find total expenses, monthly cash flow and the savings rate if she saves the entire surplus.
step: Add the expenses: $1{,}050 + 180 + 310 + 120 + 95 + 210 = \$1{,}965$.
step: Cash flow is $2{,}640 - 1{,}965 = \$675$, a surplus.
step: Savings rate is $675 / 2{,}640 = 0.2557$, or 25.6%, above the 20% guideline.
answer: Expenses \$1,965; surplus \$675 a month; savings rate about 25.6%.

@example
title: Paychecks to a monthly figure
problem: Luis's net pay is \$1,487 per biweekly paycheck. What is his monthly net income for budgeting, and in how many months does he get three paychecks?
step: A year has 26 biweekly paychecks, so annual net pay is $1{,}487 \times 26 = \$38{,}662$.
step: Divide by 12 months: $38{,}662 / 12 = \$3{,}221.83$ (rounded to cents) on average; a paycheck count of 26 divided by 12 gives about 2.17 paychecks a month.
step: Because $26 = 2 \times 12 + 2$, two months a year contain three paychecks.
answer: About \$3,221.83 a month on average; two months a year have three paychecks.

@mistakes
- Budgeting from gross pay instead of net pay, so the plan counts money that never reaches the bank account.
- Multiplying a biweekly paycheck by 2 to get a month: that gives 24 paychecks, not 26.
- Forgetting irregular expenses (annual fees, gifts, car repairs) and then calling the budget a failure.
- Saving "whatever is left," which is often nothing; schedule the savings first.

solution: Paying yourself first means moving a planned amount into savings as soon as you are paid, before spending on wants. With \$2,500 of net income, you might transfer 15%, or \$375, on payday and budget the remaining \$2,125 for needs and wants. Spending expands to fill whatever is available, so "whatever is left" is often zero, while the automatic transfer makes saving a fixed expense that is already done.
