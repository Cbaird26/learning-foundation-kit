@article
When you borrow, lenders want to know how likely you are to repay. They look at your **credit report**, a record of your borrowing and repayment history kept by the three nationwide credit bureaus. You can get a free copy of each at AnnualCreditReport.com, and checking for errors is a good habit.

### Credit scores

A **credit score** summarizes the report in a number. The widely used FICO score ranges from 300 to 850; higher means lower predicted risk. The score weights five factors, roughly:

| Factor | Approximate weight |
|---|---|
| Payment history (paying on time) | 35% |
| Amounts owed (utilization) | 30% |
| Length of credit history | 15% |
| New credit (recent applications) | 10% |
| Credit mix | 10% |

The weights are approximate and the exact formulas are secret. Paying late, maxing out cards or opening many accounts at once all lower a score. A good score helps you qualify for lower interest rates and can matter for renting an apartment or some jobs.

### Credit utilization

**Credit utilization** is the share of your revolving credit limits that you are using:

$$\text{utilization} = \frac{\text{total card balances}}{\text{total credit limits}}.$$

Lower is better; many people aim to keep it well under 30%.

### How credit cards charge interest

A credit card has a **credit limit**, an **APR** (annual percentage rate) and a **billing cycle**. If you pay the **full statement balance** by the due date, new purchases typically have a **grace period** and you pay no interest. If you carry a balance, interest is charged. For simplicity, we assume interest each month equals the **APR divided by 12** times the balance (many cards actually use a daily periodic rate, which gives similar results). A card with a 24% APR charges about $24\%/12 = 2\%$ a month, and the effective annual rate is $(1.02)^{12} - 1 = 26.82\%$.

The **minimum payment** keeps the account in good standing but may barely cover interest. A fixed payment $M$ on a balance $B$ at monthly rate $i$ takes

$$n = \frac{-\ln\left(1 - \dfrac{B\,i}{M}\right)}{\ln(1 + i)}$$

months to pay off, and only if $M > B\,i$. Otherwise the balance never falls.

@example
title: Utilization across two cards
problem: Card A has a \$1,200 balance and a \$4,000 limit. Card B has a \$300 balance and a \$1,000 limit. What is each card's utilization and the overall utilization?
step: Card A: $1{,}200 / 4{,}000 = 30\%$. Card B: $300 / 1{,}000 = 30\%$.
step: Overall: total balances $1{,}200 + 300 = 1{,}500$ divided by total limits $4{,}000 + 1{,}000 = 5{,}000$ gives $1{,}500 / 5{,}000 = 30\%$.
step: Paying Card A down to \$400 would cut overall utilization to $(400 + 300)/5{,}000 = 14\%$.
answer: 30% on each card and 30% overall.

@example
title: Paying down a balance with a fixed payment
problem: You owe \$1,000 on a card with a 24% APR and pay \$50 at the end of every month, with no new purchases. How long until the balance is paid, and how much interest do you pay?
step: The monthly rate is $0.24/12 = 0.02$. The first month's interest is $0.02 \times 1{,}000 = \$20$, so only \$30 of the first payment reduces the balance.
step: Months: $n = -\ln(1 - 1000 \times 0.02/50)/\ln(1.02) = -\ln(0.6)/\ln(1.02) = 25.8$, so 26 payments, with the last one smaller than \$50.
step: Interest paid is the total of all payments minus the \$1,000 borrowed. Working month by month with interest rounded to cents, it is about \$289.88.
answer: 26 months; about \$289.88 of interest on a \$1,000 balance.

@mistakes
- Believing that a credit card is a source of income: it is a loan, and the balance must be repaid with interest.
- Assuming the minimum payment is a good plan: it can stretch repayment out and cost far more interest.
- Closing or applying for many cards without thinking about the effect on length of history, new credit and utilization.
- Thinking that checking your own credit report lowers your score; a check by you does not.

model:
```python
def payoff_months(balance, apr, payment):
    """Months to pay off a card balance with a fixed monthly payment."""
    rate = apr / 12
    if payment <= balance * rate and balance > 0:
        return None
    months = 0
    while balance > 1e-9:
        balance = balance * (1 + rate) - payment
        months += 1
    return months
```
hint: Each month the balance becomes `balance * (1 + rate) - payment`.
hint: Count the months in a loop that stops when the balance is zero or less; check first whether the payment even covers the interest.
solution: Convert the APR to a monthly rate, return `None` if the payment cannot cover the first month's interest, and otherwise repeat "add interest, subtract the payment" while counting months until the balance is gone. At exactly the interest amount (a \$20 payment on \$1,000 at 2% a month) the balance never falls, so `None` is returned.
