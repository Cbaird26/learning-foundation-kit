@article
When the price of a good falls, two things happen to the consumer. The good is now **cheaper relative to other goods**, so she is drawn to substitute toward it. And her money now buys more of everything: she is, in effect, **richer**. Demand responds to both, and separating them explains why demand curves usually slope down and why, in rare cases, they might not.

### The Slutsky decomposition

Let the price of good 1 change from $p_1$ to $p_1'$, with $p_2$ and income $m$ fixed. Let $x_1(p_1, m)$ be demand (with $p_2$ held constant and suppressed). The decomposition goes in three steps:

1. **Original bundle.** At $(p_1, m)$ the consumer buys $x_1^{A} = x_1(p_1, m)$.
2. **Substitution effect.** Take away or add enough income so that the *original bundle is just affordable* at the new price: the **Slutsky income** is
$$m' = m + (p_1' - p_1)\,x_1^{A}.$$
Graphically, rotate the new budget line back until it passes through the original bundle. Demand at $(p_1', m')$ gives $x_1^{S}$, and the **substitution effect (SE)** is $x_1^{S} - x_1^{A}$.
3. **Income effect.** Return the income from $m'$ to the true $m$. Demand moves from $x_1^{S}$ to the final $x_1^{B} = x_1(p_1', m)$, and the **income effect (IE)** is $x_1^{B} - x_1^{S}$.

By construction, **total effect = SE + IE**. Note the sign of the adjustment: when the price falls, $m' < m$, because the consumer's purchasing power has risen and we must remove that gain to isolate the substitution effect.

### Signs of the effects

- The **substitution effect always works against the price change**: when a price falls, the consumer buys at least as much of the good along the rotated budget line. (Reason: every point on the rotated line with *less* of good 1 was affordable at the original prices but not chosen, so it is revealed to be worse than the original bundle.) With perfect complements the SE is zero.
- The **income effect** depends on the good. For a price fall the real income rises: a **normal** good ($\partial x_1/\partial m > 0$) is bought more, so IE reinforces SE; an **inferior** good is bought less, so IE opposes SE.

| Good type | Price falls: SE | IE | Total effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | $+$ | $+$ | $+$ (demand curve slopes down) |
| Inferior, IE smaller | $+$ | $-$ | $+$ (still slopes down) |
| Giffen (inferior, IE larger) | $+$ | $-$ | $-$ (demand curve slopes up) |

A **Giffen good** is an inferior good whose negative income effect outweighs its substitution effect, so the quantity demanded falls when its price falls. It is a theoretical possibility; well-documented cases are rare.

### The Slutsky equation

For a small price change, the same decomposition reads
$$\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial p_1} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial x_1^{h}}{\partial p_1}}_{\text{SE} \le 0} - \; x_1\,\underbrace{\frac{\partial x_1}{\partial m}}_{\text{income}},$$
where $x_1^{h}$ is the Hicksian (compensated) demand, which holds utility rather than the original bundle's affordability fixed. The second term is the income effect: a rise in price lowers real income by about $x_1\,dp_1$ and changes demand by $\partial x_1/\partial m$ per dollar.

@example
title: Decomposing a price fall for a Cobb-Douglas consumer
problem: A consumer has $U = x^{0.5}y^{0.5}$ and income \$120. The price of $y$ is \$2. The price of $x$ falls from \$4 to \$2. Decompose the change in her demand for $x$.
step: Demand is $x = 0.5\,m/p_x$. At the original price: $x^{A} = 0.5(120)/4 = 15$.
step: Slutsky income: $m' = 120 + (2 - 4)(15) = 90$. This is the money needed to buy the old bundle $(15, 30)$ at the new price: $2(15) + 2(30) = 90$.
step: Demand at $(p_x', m') = (2, 90)$: $x^{S} = 0.5(90)/2 = 22.5$. So $SE = 22.5 - 15 = +7.5$.
step: Demand at the true income: $x^{B} = 0.5(120)/2 = 30$. So $IE = 30 - 22.5 = +7.5$, and the total effect is $30 - 15 = 15 = 7.5 + 7.5$.
answer: SE $= +7.5$ units, IE $= +7.5$ units, total $+15$ units: $x$ is a normal good.

@example
title: An inferior good
problem: Suppose a consumer's demand for instant noodles is $x(p, m) = 80 - 5p - 0.1m$, where $p$ is the price in dollars and $m$ is income in dollars. Her income is \$300. The price falls from \$6 to \$4. Decompose the change. (Assume demand is positive and $x$ is the only good whose price changes.)
step: Original demand: $x^{A} = 80 - 30 - 30 = 20$. Final demand: $x^{B} = 80 - 20 - 30 = 30$. Total effect $= +10$.
step: Slutsky income: $m' = 300 + (4 - 6)(20) = 260$.
step: Demand at $(4, 260)$: $x^{S} = 80 - 20 - 26 = 34$. So $SE = 34 - 20 = +14$.
step: $IE = x^{B} - x^{S} = 30 - 34 = -4$. Check: $14 - 4 = 10$.
step: The price fall raises her real income and she buys fewer noodles ($\partial x/\partial m = -0.1 < 0$): an inferior good. But $|IE| < SE$, so it is not a Giffen good.
answer: SE $= +14$, IE $= -4$, total $+10$.

@mistakes
- Evaluating the income effect at the old price. It compares demand at Slutsky income $m'$ with demand at the true income $m$, both at the *new* price.
- Computing the Slutsky income with the new quantity instead of the original bundle: $m' = m + (p_1' - p_1)\,x_1^{A}$ uses the quantity bought before the change.
- Believing that an inferior good must have an upward-sloping demand curve. That needs the income effect to outweigh the substitution effect (Giffen).
- Getting the sign of the Slutsky adjustment wrong: a price fall means the consumer needs less money to afford the old bundle, so $m' < m$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement about a Giffen good is correct?
choice*: It is an inferior good whose income effect outweighs its substitution effect, so its demand curve slopes upward.
choice: It is any inferior good, and every inferior good has an upward-sloping demand curve.
choice: It is a normal good whose substitution effect has the wrong sign.
choice: It has a zero substitution effect, as with perfect complements.
hint: A Giffen good needs the quantity bought to fall when its price falls.
solution: The substitution effect of a price fall is never negative, so for the quantity to fall the income effect must be negative (an inferior good) and larger in size than the substitution effect. Most inferior goods have income effects smaller than their substitution effects and therefore still have downward-sloping demand.

@practice num
prompt: A consumer has $U = x^{0.25}y^{0.75}$ and income \$240. The price of $y$ is \$3. The price of $x$ falls from \$6 to \$4. Her demand for $x$ is $x = 0.25\,m/p_x$. What is the substitution effect on her demand for $x$, in units?
answer: 3.75
tol: 0.01
check: 0.25 * (240 + (4 - 6) * (0.25 * 240 / 6)) / 4 - 0.25 * 240 / 6
hint: Her original demand is $0.25(240)/6 = 10$ units.
solution: $x^{A} = 10$. Slutsky income $m' = 240 + (4 - 6)(10) = 220$. Demand at $(4, 220)$ is $0.25(220)/4 = 13.75$. The substitution effect is $13.75 - 10 = +3.75$ units.

@practice ms
prompt: A price of good 1 falls. Which statements are true under the Slutsky decomposition?
choice*: The substitution effect on good 1 is never negative.
choice*: If good 1 is normal, the income effect reinforces the substitution effect.
choice*: With perfect complements, the substitution effect is zero.
choice: The income effect is always positive.
choice: A Giffen good must be a normal good.
hint: Think about the sign of the income effect for normal and for inferior goods.
solution: A price fall rotates the budget line through the old bundle, and the consumer never moves to fewer units of the cheaper good along it, so the SE is nonnegative. For a normal good the extra real income raises demand, reinforcing the SE. For perfect complements the old bundle remains optimal on the rotated line, so SE $= 0$. The income effect is negative for inferior goods, and a Giffen good must be inferior.

@practice num
prompt: Suppose demand for a good is $x(p, m) = 120 - 6p - 0.2m$, where $m$ is income. Income is \$250 and the price falls from \$8 to \$5. What is the income effect on quantity, in units? Give a negative number if the quantity falls.
answer: -13.2
tol: 0.01
check: (120 - 6 * 5 - 0.2 * 250) - (120 - 6 * 5 - 0.2 * (250 + (5 - 8) * (120 - 6 * 8 - 0.2 * 250)))
hint: Original demand is $120 - 48 - 50 = 22$. Find the Slutsky income, then demand at the new price at that income.
solution: $x^{A} = 22$. $m' = 250 + (5 - 8)(22) = 184$. Demand at $(5, 184)$: $120 - 30 - 36.8 = 53.2$ (so SE $= +31.2$). Demand at $(5, 250)$: $120 - 30 - 50 = 40$. The income effect is $40 - 53.2 = -13.2$ units: the good is inferior.

@quiz num
prompt: A consumer has $U = x^{0.6}y^{0.4}$ and income \$300. The price of $y$ is \$2, and the price of $x$ falls from \$5 to \$3. Her demand for $x$ is $x = 0.6\,m/p_x$. What is the income effect on her demand for $x$, in units? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 14.4
tol: 0.1
check: 0.6 * 300 / 3 - 0.6 * (300 + (3 - 5) * (0.6 * 300 / 5)) / 3
hint: Original demand is $0.6(300)/5 = 36$ units. Find the Slutsky income and demand at it.
solution: $x^{A} = 36$. $m' = 300 + (3 - 5)(36) = 228$. Demand at $(3, 228)$ is $0.6(228)/3 = 45.6$ (SE $= 9.6$). Demand at $(3, 300)$ is $60$. IE $= 60 - 45.6 = 14.4$ units.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why the substitution effect of a price decrease can never reduce the quantity demanded of that good. Use the idea of the old bundle being just affordable.
hint: Compare the rotated budget line with the original budget line.
solution: After the Slutsky adjustment, the new budget line passes through the original bundle $A$ and is flatter in terms of good 1's price (the good is cheaper). Every bundle on the new line that has less of good 1 than $A$ lies inside the original budget set, so it was affordable when $A$ was chosen and the consumer passed it over: $A$ is revealed preferred to those bundles. So she will not choose any of them on the new line, and her choice must contain at least as much of good 1 as $A$. Hence the substitution effect of a price fall is nonnegative.
rubric: Describes the rotated (Slutsky) budget line passing through the original bundle.
rubric: Notes that points on the rotated line with less of good 1 were affordable at the original prices, so the original choice shows they are worse.
rubric: Concludes that the new choice has at least as much of good 1.
