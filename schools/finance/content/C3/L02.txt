@article
Last week gave you two objects: indifference curves (what the consumer wants) and the budget line (what she can afford). Her best affordable bundle is the one on the highest indifference curve that touches the budget set. Because preferences are monotone, she spends all her income, so the optimum lies on the budget line.

### The tangency condition

At an interior optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line: the slopes match, so

$$MRS = \frac{MU_1}{MU_2} = \frac{p_1}{p_2}.$$

The intuition is a pair of exchange rates. The MRS is how many units of good 2 she is *willing* to give up for a unit of good 1; the price ratio is how many she *has to* give up. If $MRS > p_1/p_2$, she values good 1 more than the market charges, so she buys more of it; if $MRS < p_1/p_2$, she buys less. Only at equality is she done. Together with $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = m$ this gives two equations in two unknowns.

The same answer comes from the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = U(x_1, x_2) + \lambda\,(m - p_1x_1 - p_2x_2)$. The first-order conditions $MU_1 = \lambda p_1$ and $MU_2 = \lambda p_2$ imply $MU_1/MU_2 = p_1/p_2$, and $\lambda$ is the marginal utility of an extra dollar of income.

### Cobb-Douglas demand

For $U = x_1^{a}x_2^{1-a}$ with $0 < a < 1$, $MRS = \dfrac{a}{1-a}\dfrac{x_2}{x_1}$. Setting this equal to $p_1/p_2$ gives $p_2x_2 = \dfrac{1-a}{a}\,p_1x_1$. Substituting into the budget constraint, $p_1x_1\left(1 + \dfrac{1-a}{a}\right) = m$, so

$$x_1^* = \frac{a\,m}{p_1}, \qquad x_2^* = \frac{(1-a)\,m}{p_2}.$$

The consumer spends a constant **expenditure share** $a$ of income on good 1 and $1-a$ on good 2, whatever the prices. Hence demand for good 1 does not depend on $p_2$.

### Corner solutions and other preferences

- **Perfect substitutes**, $U = ax_1 + bx_2$: the MRS is the constant $a/b$. If $a/p_1 > b/p_2$ (more utility per dollar from good 1), she spends everything on good 1: $x_1 = m/p_1$, $x_2 = 0$. If the inequality reverses, she buys only good 2; if equal, any bundle on the budget line is optimal.
- **Perfect complements**, $U = \min\{ax_1, bx_2\}$: she buys in fixed proportion, $ax_1 = bx_2$, so the optimum is at the corner of an L-shaped curve. Substitute into the budget line to solve.
- Preferences with a diminishing MRS can still have a **corner solution** (a zero quantity of one good) if at $x_2 = 0$ the MRS is above the price ratio (she would like to trade away even more of good 2, but has none to give). There the tangency condition fails and you must check the boundary.

### From optimum to demand

The optimal bundle as a function of prices and income is the **demand function** $x_1(p_1, p_2, m)$. Varying $p_1$ with the other variables fixed traces the **demand curve**. Varying $m$ traces the **Engel curve**. A good is **normal** if $\partial x_1/\partial m > 0$ and **inferior** if $\partial x_1/\partial m < 0$. Three elasticities summarize the response (percentage change in quantity per percentage change in the variable):

$$\varepsilon_{p} = \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial p_1}\frac{p_1}{x_1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{m} = \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial m}\frac{m}{x_1}, \qquad \varepsilon_{12} = \frac{\partial x_1}{\partial p_2}\frac{p_2}{x_1}.$$

For Cobb-Douglas demand $x_1 = am/p_1$: $\varepsilon_p = -1$, $\varepsilon_m = +1$, $\varepsilon_{12} = 0$.

@example
title: A Cobb-Douglas consumer
problem: Imara has $U(x, y) = x^{0.4}y^{0.6}$, income \$200, and faces $p_x = \$2$ and $p_y = \$5$. Find her optimal bundle and check the tangency condition.
step: Use the expenditure-share form with $a = 0.4$. Spending on $x$ is $0.4 \times 200 = \$80$, so $x^* = 80/2 = 40$.
step: Spending on $y$ is $0.6 \times 200 = \$120$, so $y^* = 120/5 = 24$.
step: Check the budget: $2(40) + 5(24) = 80 + 120 = 200$. It is exhausted.
step: Check the tangency: $MRS = \dfrac{0.4}{0.6}\cdot\dfrac{y}{x} = 0.6667 \times \dfrac{24}{40} = 0.4$ and $p_x/p_y = 2/5 = 0.4$. They match. Her utility is $40^{0.4}\,24^{0.6} = 29.44$.
answer: $(x^*, y^*) = (40, 24)$, with utility about 29.44.

@example
title: Perfect complements
problem: Calvin makes sandwiches from 2 slices of bread to 1 slice of cheese, so his utility is $U(x, y) = \min\{x, 2y\}$, where $x$ is bread slices (\$3 each) and $y$ is cheese slices (\$4 each). His income is \$100. Find his optimal bundle.
step: Perfect complements are consumed in fixed proportion, so at the optimum $x = 2y$: any extra of one good beyond that ratio adds nothing to $U$ but costs money.
step: Substitute into the budget line: $3(2y) + 4y = 100$, so $10y = 100$ and $y = 10$.
step: Then $x = 2y = 20$. Spending: $3(20) + 4(10) = 60 + 40 = 100$. Utility is $\min\{20, 20\} = 20$.
step: Notice that the tangency condition is not used: the indifference curve has a corner, not a slope. A change in $p_x$ changes how much he buys of both goods, because he must keep the 2-to-1 ratio.
answer: $(x^*, y^*) = (20, 10)$.

@mistakes
- Setting $MU_1 = p_1/p_2$ or $MRS = p_2/p_1$: the tangency condition is $MU_1/MU_2 = p_1/p_2$.
- Forcing a tangency solution when the preferences give a corner (perfect substitutes) or a kink (perfect complements). Check which case you are in first.
- Forgetting to check the second condition: the tangency alone does not pin down the quantities; you must also use the budget constraint.
- Assuming a downward-sloping demand curve means the good is normal. Slope in price and the sign of the income effect are different properties; week 3 separates them.

choice: $MU_1/MU_2 = p_2/p_1$
choice: $MU_1 = MU_2$

hint: The slope of the indifference curve is $-MRS$ and the slope of the budget line is $-p_1/p_2$.
solution: At an interior optimum the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line, so the MRS equals the price ratio $p_1/p_2$, which can be written $MU_1/p_1 = MU_2/p_2$: equal marginal utility per dollar. $MU_1 = MU_2$ would hold only if the prices were equal.

@practice num
prompt: The demand for good 1 is $x_1 = 10 + 0.05m$, where $m$ is income. At $m = 400$, what is the income elasticity of demand? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.67
tol: 0.01
check: 0.05 * 400 / (10 + 0.05 * 400)
hint: The elasticity is $\dfrac{\partial x_1}{\partial m}\cdot\dfrac{m}{x_1}$. First find $x_1$ at $m = 400$.
solution: At $m = 400$, $x_1 = 10 + 20 = 30$. So $\varepsilon_m = 0.05 \times 400/30 = 0.667 \approx 0.67$: a normal good (positive) and a necessity (below 1).

model:
```python
def cd_demand(alpha, income, p1, p2):
    """Optimal bundle for U = x1**alpha * x2**(1 - alpha)."""
    x1 = alpha * income / p1
    x2 = (1 - alpha) * income / p2
    return (x1, x2)
```
hint: The consumer spends the fraction `alpha` of income on good 1 and the rest on good 2.
hint: Divide each amount spent by that good's own price.
solution: Spending is `alpha * income` on good 1 and `(1 - alpha) * income` on good 2; dividing by the matching price gives the quantities. The test with prices 6 and 4 catches a solution that divides by the wrong price.
