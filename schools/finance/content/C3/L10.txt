@article
Would you accept a coin flip that pays \$200 on heads and nothing on tails, in exchange for a sure \$100? Both have the same average payoff, yet most people take the sure thing. Insurance, diversification and the pricing of risk in financial markets all depend on explaining this.

### Lotteries and expected value

A **lottery** lists outcomes $x_1, \ldots, x_n$ with probabilities $\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_n$ that sum to 1. Its **expected value** is $E[x] = \sum_i \pi_i x_i$. Expected value alone cannot explain behavior: nobody pays a million dollars for a coin flip that pays two million or zero.

### Expected utility

Suppose the consumer's preferences over lotteries satisfy the axioms of von Neumann and Morgenstern (completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence). Then there is a utility function $u$ over outcomes (here, wealth) such that she ranks lotteries by **expected utility**:

$$EU = \sum_i \pi_i\,u(x_i).$$

Unlike the ordinal utility of weeks 1 and 2, here the *shape* of $u$ matters, because it is averaged. Only positive linear transformations $a + bu$ with $b > 0$ preserve the ranking of lotteries.

### Risk attitudes

Compare the lottery with its expected value received for sure:

- **Risk averse**: $u(E[x]) > E[u(x)]$ for every nondegenerate lottery. This holds if and only if $u$ is **strictly concave** ($u'' < 0$): the extra utility from a dollar falls as wealth rises (Jensen's inequality).
- **Risk neutral**: $u$ is linear.
- **Risk loving**: $u$ is strictly convex.

The **certainty equivalent** (CE) is the sure amount that gives the same utility as the lottery: $u(CE) = EU$, so $CE = u^{-1}(EU)$. The **risk premium** is $E[x] - CE$, what she gives up in expected value to avoid the risk. It is positive for a risk-averse person.

The **Arrow-Pratt coefficient of absolute risk aversion** is $A(w) = -u''(w)/u'(w)$. For small risks, the risk premium is approximately $\tfrac12 A(w)\sigma^2$, where $\sigma^2$ is the variance. Examples: $u = \sqrt{w}$ has $A = 1/(2w)$; $u = \ln w$ has $A = 1/w$; $u = -e^{-aw}$ (constant absolute risk aversion) has $A = a$, and for a normally distributed outcome with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, $CE = \mu - \tfrac12 a\sigma^2$ exactly. This mean-variance form is the bridge to portfolio theory.

### Insurance

A consumer has wealth $w_0$ and faces a loss $L$ with probability $p$. With no insurance her expected utility is $(1 - p)u(w_0) + p\,u(w_0 - L)$. Full insurance at premium $\pi$ gives $u(w_0 - \pi)$ for sure. She buys it if $u(w_0 - \pi) \ge EU$, so the **maximum premium** solves $u(w_0 - \pi_{\max}) = EU$, i.e. $\pi_{\max} = w_0 - CE$. The **actuarially fair premium** is $pL$, the expected loss. Since $\pi_{\max} - pL$ equals the risk premium, a risk-averse person is willing to pay more than the fair premium. If the insurer offers any coverage at a fair price, she chooses **full insurance**: with fair prices, her marginal utility is equalized across states only when final wealth is the same in both.

@example
title: How much will she pay for fire insurance?
problem: A homeowner has wealth \$10,000 and a 20% chance of a \$7,500 fire loss. Her utility is $u(w) = \sqrt{w}$. Find her expected utility without insurance, certainty equivalent, risk premium and maximum premium for full coverage, and compare with the fair premium.
step: Without insurance, wealth is \$10,000 with probability 0.8 and \$2,500 with probability 0.2. $EU = 0.8\sqrt{10000} + 0.2\sqrt{2500} = 0.8(100) + 0.2(50) = 90$.
step: Certainty equivalent: $\sqrt{CE} = 90$, so $CE = 90^2 = \$8{,}100$. Expected wealth is $0.8(10000) + 0.2(2500) = \$8{,}500$, so the risk premium is $8500 - 8100 = \$400$.
step: Maximum premium: she is indifferent when $\sqrt{10000 - \pi} = 90$, so $\pi_{\max} = 10000 - 8100 = \$1{,}900$.
step: The fair premium is $0.2 \times 7500 = \$1{,}500$. She would pay \$400 more than fair (the risk premium), so an insurer charging between \$1,500 and \$1,900 earns an expected profit and she still gains.
answer: $EU = 90$, $CE = \$8{,}100$, risk premium \$400, maximum premium \$1,900 against a fair premium of \$1,500.

@example
title: Ranking gambles with log utility
problem: A consumer with $u(w) = \ln w$ chooses between a sure \$190 and a lottery paying \$90 or \$360 with equal probability. Which does she prefer?
step: Expected value of the lottery: $0.5(90) + 0.5(360) = \$225$, above \$190. A risk-neutral person would take the lottery.
step: Expected utility: $0.5\ln 90 + 0.5\ln 360 = 0.5(4.4998) + 0.5(5.8861) = 5.1930$.
step: Certainty equivalent: $CE = e^{5.1930} = \$180$ (indeed $\sqrt{90 \times 360} = 180$).
step: Because $CE = \$180 < \$190$, she prefers the sure \$190 although its amount is far below the lottery's expected value of \$225. The risk premium of the lottery is $225 - 180 = \$45$.
answer: She prefers the sure \$190 (the lottery's certainty equivalent is \$180).

@mistakes
- Ranking gambles by expected value alone. A risk-averse person's ranking depends on the shape of $u$.
- Computing the certainty equivalent as $E[u(x)]$. $E[u(x)]$ is a utility level; the certainty equivalent is the wealth $u^{-1}(E[u])$.
- Taking the average of the utilities of outcomes as the utility of the average: $E[u(x)] \ne u(E[x])$ unless $u$ is linear.
- Forgetting that the maximum premium is measured from initial wealth: $\pi_{\max} = w_0 - CE$, not $E[x] - CE$.

model:
```python
def risk_premium(outcomes, probs, u, u_inv):
    """Expected value minus certainty equivalent of a lottery."""
    expected_value = sum(p * x for p, x in zip(probs, outcomes))
    expected_utility = sum(p * u(x) for p, x in zip(probs, outcomes))
    certainty_equivalent = u_inv(expected_utility)
    return expected_value - certainty_equivalent
```
hint: The certainty equivalent is `u_inv` applied to the expected utility, not to the expected value.
solution: Compute the expected value of the outcomes, compute the expected utility `sum(p * u(x))`, invert it with `u_inv` to get the certainty equivalent and subtract. A negative result, as in the convex-utility test, means the person is a risk lover.

prompt: A consumer with utility $u(w) = \ln w$ is offered a lottery paying \$50 or \$200 with equal probability. What is her certainty equivalent, in dollars?
answer: 100
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: exp(0.5 * log(50) + 0.5 * log(200))

prompt: A risk-averse consumer is offered full insurance against a random loss at the actuarially fair premium. In four or five sentences, explain why she buys full coverage rather than partial coverage, and why she would also pay more than the fair premium for it.
hint: Compare her wealth in the loss and no-loss states with and without full insurance.
solution: Full insurance at the fair premium gives her the same wealth in both states, namely expected wealth, so she faces no risk. Since the premium equals the expected loss, expected wealth is unchanged. Because $u$ is strictly concave, a sure amount equal to the expected value gives higher expected utility than any risky outcome with that mean, so she prefers it to any partial coverage with the same fair price. Her certainty equivalent for the uninsured position is below expected wealth by the risk premium, so she would pay up to the fair premium plus the risk premium for full coverage.
