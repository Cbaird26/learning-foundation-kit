@article
Why do two people with the same income spend it so differently? Microeconomics answers with a model of one consumer who has **preferences** (what she likes) and faces a **budget constraint** (what she can afford). In weeks 1 and 2 you combine the two to predict what she buys. This week builds the two ingredients.

### Bundles and preferences

A **bundle** $(x_1, x_2)$ lists the quantities of two goods. Write $A \succsim B$ for "A is at least as good as B". The standard assumptions are:

- **Completeness**: for any two bundles, $A \succsim B$ or $B \succsim A$ (or both).
- **Transitivity**: if $A \succsim B$ and $B \succsim C$, then $A \succsim C$. Without it a consumer could be led around a cycle and pay to end where she began.
- **Monotonicity** ("more is better"): a bundle with more of at least one good and no less of the other is strictly preferred.
- **Convexity**: averages are preferred to extremes. If $A \sim B$, any mix of the two is at least as good as either.

An **indifference curve** joins all bundles that the consumer ranks equally. Under these assumptions indifference curves slope downward, never cross (a crossing would violate transitivity or monotonicity), and are bowed toward the origin.

### Utility functions

A **utility function** $U(x_1, x_2)$ assigns a number to each bundle so that $U(A) \ge U(B)$ exactly when $A \succsim B$. Only the **ordering** matters, not the size of the numbers (this is *ordinal* utility). So if $U$ represents some preferences, so does $V = f(U)$ for any strictly increasing function $f$: for example $\ln U$, $U^2$ (when $U > 0$) or $5U + 2$.

Three standard forms:

| Preferences | Utility | Indifference curves |
|---|---|---|
| Cobb-Douglas | $U = x_1^{a} x_2^{b}$, $a, b > 0$ | smooth, bowed toward the origin |
| Perfect substitutes | $U = a x_1 + b x_2$ | straight lines with slope $-a/b$ |
| Perfect complements | $U = \min\{a x_1,\, b x_2\}$ | L-shapes with corners where $a x_1 = b x_2$ |

### The marginal rate of substitution

Along an indifference curve, $dU = MU_1\,dx_1 + MU_2\,dx_2 = 0$, so the slope is $dx_2/dx_1 = -MU_1/MU_2$. The **marginal rate of substitution** is

$$MRS = \frac{MU_1}{MU_2},$$

the number of units of good 2 the consumer will give up for one more unit of good 1, keeping utility constant. For $U = x_1^{a} x_2^{b}$, $MU_1 = a x_1^{a-1}x_2^{b}$ and $MU_2 = b x_1^{a}x_2^{b-1}$, so $MRS = \dfrac{a}{b}\dfrac{x_2}{x_1}$. Convexity means the MRS falls as $x_1$ rises along a curve: the more of good 1 you hold, the less you value one more unit of it.

### The budget constraint

With prices $p_1, p_2 > 0$ and income $m$, the bundles the consumer can afford satisfy

$$p_1 x_1 + p_2 x_2 \le m.$$

Assumptions used throughout: goods are perfectly divisible, prices are the same for every unit, and the consumer takes prices as given. The **budget line** $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = m$ has intercepts $m/p_1$ and $m/p_2$ and slope $-p_1/p_2$ when good 1 is on the horizontal axis: the market's rate of exchange, the **opportunity cost** of good 1 in units of good 2.

- A rise in income shifts the line out in parallel; a fall shifts it in.
- A rise in $p_1$ rotates the line inward around the vertical intercept; the slope becomes steeper.
- A **quantity tax** $t$ per unit of good 1 raises its price to $p_1 + t$. A **subsidy** lowers it. A **lump-sum** tax lowers $m$ and shifts the line in parallel.
- A **ration** (a cap $\bar{x}_1$ on good 1) cuts off the part of the budget set to the right of $x_1 = \bar{x}_1$.

@example
title: Ranking bundles and computing the MRS
problem: Tamsin's preferences over coffee $x$ and books $y$ are represented by $U(x, y) = x^{0.5}y^{0.5}$. Compare the bundles $A = (4, 9)$, $B = (9, 4)$ and $C = (6, 7)$, and find her MRS at $A$ and at $B$.
step: Compute utilities. $U(A) = \sqrt{36} = 6$, $U(B) = \sqrt{36} = 6$, $U(C) = \sqrt{42} = 6.481$.
step: $A$ and $B$ lie on the same indifference curve (utility 6), so she is indifferent between them. $C$ has higher utility, so she strictly prefers $C$ to both. This is convexity at work: $C$ is near the average $(6.5, 6.5)$ of $A$ and $B$.
step: For Cobb-Douglas with $a = b = 0.5$, $MRS = \dfrac{y}{x}$. At $A$: $MRS = 9/4 = 2.25$. At $B$: $MRS = 4/9 = 0.444$.
step: Reading the numbers: at $A$ she has little coffee and would give up 2.25 books for one more coffee; at $B$ she has a lot of coffee and would give up only 0.444 books for one more. The MRS falls as we move down the curve.
answer: She prefers $C$; she is indifferent between $A$ and $B$; $MRS = 2.25$ at $A$ and $0.444$ at $B$.

@example
title: Drawing and shifting a budget line
problem: Aurelio has income \$60 to spend on bus rides $x$ (price \$2) and snacks $y$ (price \$5). Write his budget line, then find the new line if (a) the bus fare rises to \$3, and (b) instead, his income falls to \$45 at the original prices.
step: The budget line is $2x + 5y = 60$. Intercepts: $x = 60/2 = 30$ rides and $y = 60/5 = 12$ snacks. Slope: $-p_x/p_y = -2/5 = -0.4$, so each extra ride costs 0.4 snack.
step: (a) With $p_x = 3$: $3x + 5y = 60$. The $y$-intercept is still 12 but the $x$-intercept falls to $60/3 = 20$. The slope is $-3/5 = -0.6$: rides are now more expensive in terms of snacks.
step: (b) With $m = 45$: $2x + 5y = 45$. Intercepts are $45/2 = 22.5$ rides and $45/5 = 9$ snacks, and the slope is still $-0.4$: a parallel inward shift.
step: Check that the bundle $(10, 8)$, which cost $2(10) + 5(8) = 60$, is affordable on the original line, unaffordable on line (a) (it costs $30 + 40 = 70$), and unaffordable on line (b) (cost 60 against income 45).
answer: Original: intercepts 30 and 12, slope $-0.4$. (a) intercepts 20 and 12, slope $-0.6$. (b) intercepts 22.5 and 9, slope $-0.4$.

@mistakes
- Treating utility numbers as cardinal: saying a bundle with $U = 12$ is "twice as good" as one with $U = 6$. Only the ranking is meaningful.
- Putting the price ratio upside down: the slope of the budget line (good 1 on the horizontal axis) is $-p_1/p_2$, not $-p_2/p_1$.
- Reading a parallel shift into a price change: a change in one price rotates the budget line, and only a change in income (or a lump-sum tax) shifts it in parallel.
- Thinking that rescaling utility changes the MRS. The ratio $MU_1/MU_2$ is the same for $U$ and for any strictly increasing transformation of $U$, because the indifference curves are the same; that is why the MRS, unlike marginal utility itself, has economic meaning.

@practice mc
prompt: A consumer has income $m$ and faces prices $p_1$ and $p_2$. Good 1 is on the horizontal axis and good 2 on the vertical axis. What is the slope of her budget line $p_1x_1 + p_2x_2 = m$?
choice*: $-p_1/p_2$
choice: $-p_2/p_1$
choice: $-m/p_1$
choice: $+p_1/p_2$
hint: Solve the budget equation for $x_2$ and read off the coefficient on $x_1$.
solution: $x_2 = m/p_2 - (p_1/p_2)\,x_1$, so the slope is $-p_1/p_2$: the number of units of good 2 given up for each extra unit of good 1. The vertical intercept is $m/p_2$; $-m/p_1$ is not a slope at all.

@practice num
prompt: A consumer's preferences are represented by $U(x, y) = x^{0.3}y^{0.7}$. What is her marginal rate of substitution $MU_x/MU_y$ at the bundle $(x, y) = (10, 20)$? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.86
tol: 0.01
check: (0.3 / 0.7) * (20 / 10)
hint: For $U = x^a y^b$ the MRS is $(a/b)(y/x)$.
solution: $MRS = \dfrac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot\dfrac{20}{10} = 0.4286 \times 2 = 0.857 \approx 0.86$ units of $y$ per unit of $x$.

@practice ms
prompt: Preferences over bundles $(x, y)$ with $x, y > 0$ are represented by $U = xy$. Which of these utility functions represent the same preferences?
choice*: $V = \ln x + \ln y$
choice*: $V = 5xy + 2$
choice: $V = x + y$
choice: $V = \min\{x, y\}$
hint: Two utility functions represent the same preferences if one is a strictly increasing function of the other.
solution: $\ln x + \ln y = \ln(xy)$ and $5xy + 2$ are both strictly increasing functions of $xy$, so they rank every pair of bundles as $U$ does. $x + y$ ranks $(1, 9)$ (value 10) above $(4, 4)$ (value 8) while $xy$ ranks them the other way (9 against 16), and $\min\{x, y\}$ ranks $(2, 2)$ above $(1, 9)$ (2 against 1) while $xy$ ranks $(1, 9)$ above $(2, 2)$ (9 against 4).

@practice num
prompt: A consumer has $U(x, y) = \sqrt{xy}$. The bundle $(5, 12)$ lies on one of her indifference curves. How many units of $y$ must she hold to be equally well off if she holds only 2.5 units of $x$? Give the answer to one decimal place.
answer: 24
tol: 0.1
check: (5 * 12) / 2.5
hint: Utility at $(5, 12)$ is $\sqrt{60}$; on this curve $xy = 60$.
solution: Utility is $\sqrt{5 \cdot 12} = \sqrt{60}$, so the indifference curve is $xy = 60$ or $y = 60/x$. At $x = 2.5$: $y = 60/2.5 = 24$.

@quiz num
prompt: A consumer has income \$300. Good 1 costs \$8 per unit before a tax of \$2 per unit, which she pays; good 2 costs \$5 per unit with no tax. After the tax, how many units of good 2 must she give up to buy one more unit of good 1? (Goods are divisible, and she spends all her income.)
answer: 2
tol: 0.01
check: (8 + 2) / 5
hint: The tax raises the price of good 1 to \$10.
solution: The after-tax price of good 1 is \$10, so the slope of the budget line is $-10/5 = -2$: one more unit of good 1 costs two units of good 2. Income does not enter the slope.

@quiz self
prompt: In three or four sentences, explain why two indifference curves of a consumer whose preferences are transitive and monotone cannot cross.
hint: Suppose two curves cross at a bundle $Z$. Pick one bundle on each curve, one of which has more of both goods than the other.
solution: Suppose curve 1 (utility level $u_1$) and curve 2 (level $u_2 \neq u_1$) cross at $Z$. Then $Z$ is indifferent to every bundle on curve 1 and to every bundle on curve 2, so by transitivity every bundle on curve 1 is indifferent to every bundle on curve 2. But curve 1 contains a bundle with more of both goods than some bundle on curve 2 (or the other way round), and monotonicity says it must be strictly preferred. That contradiction shows that indifference curves cannot cross.
rubric: Assumes two curves cross at a common bundle and uses transitivity to conclude that all bundles on both curves are indifferent to each other.
rubric: Uses monotonicity to show that some bundle on one curve must be strictly preferred to another on the other curve.
rubric: States the contradiction clearly and concludes that the curves cannot cross.
