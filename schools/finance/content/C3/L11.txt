@article
The competitive model assumes everyone knows what is being traded. Often one side knows more: a seller knows her car's history, a driver knows how carefully he drives. **Asymmetric information** can make markets shrink or disappear even when trade would benefit both sides.

### Adverse selection: hidden type

**Adverse selection** arises when one party has private information about its *type* before the contract, and the uninformed party's offers attract the worst types.

**The market for lemons.** Buyers cannot tell good used cars from bad ones ("lemons"). Suppose a fraction $s$ of cars are good. A good car is worth $v_G^{S}$ to its seller and $v_G^{B}$ to a buyer; a lemon is worth $v_L^{S}$ and $v_L^{B}$, with $v^{B} > v^{S}$ for both: trade is efficient. Unable to tell the types apart, buyers will pay at most the average value of a car they might get:

$$\bar{v}^{B} = s\,v_G^{B} + (1 - s)\,v_L^{B}.$$

Owners of good cars sell only if the price is at least $v_G^{S}$. So good cars are offered only if $\bar{v}^{B} \ge v_G^{S}$, that is, if

$$s \ge s^{*} = \frac{v_G^{S} - v_L^{B}}{v_G^{B} - v_L^{B}}.$$

Below this threshold, good cars leave the market, buyers learn that only lemons are offered, and the price falls to the lemon level. The gains from trade on good cars are lost: a market failure.

**Insurance.** If an insurer cannot distinguish low-risk from high-risk customers and charges the pooled fair premium, low-risk customers find it expensive and some drop out. The remaining pool is riskier, the premium must rise, more low-risk customers leave: a "death spiral" in the extreme. Suppose a loss of \$10,000 has probability 10% for low-risk and 30% for high-risk customers in equal numbers. The pooled fair premium is $0.2 \times 10{,}000 = \$2{,}000$. If low-risk customers value coverage at no more than \$1,800, they leave; the premium for the remaining high-risk pool must be \$3,000.

### Remedies for adverse selection

- **Signaling** (by the informed party): an action that is costly in a way that differs by type. In Spence's job-market model, suppose productivity is \$40,000 (low) or \$80,000 (high) a year and a year of education costs the low type \$20,000 and the high type \$10,000. Employers pay \$80,000 to anyone with at least $e$ years of education and \$40,000 to others. Measure money in thousands of dollars below. The low type will not imitate if $80 - 20e \le 40$, i.e. $e \ge 2$; the high type signals if $80 - 10e \ge 40$, i.e. $e \le 4$. Any $e \in [2, 4]$ years separates the types.
- **Screening** (by the uninformed party): offering a menu of contracts that types sort themselves into, such as insurance with different deductibles, or **warranties**, which cost the seller of a lemon more than the seller of a good car.
- **Reputation, certification, disclosure rules and mandatory pooling** (such as compulsory insurance).

### Moral hazard: hidden action

**Moral hazard** arises when, after a contract, one party's *action* is unobserved and the contract changes its incentive to take care. A fully insured driver has less reason to avoid accidents; a manager paid a fixed salary has less reason to work hard. This is a **principal-agent problem**: the principal cannot observe the agent's effort, so pay or coverage must tie to something observable.

Remedies: **deductibles** and **coinsurance** (so the insured bears part of the loss), **monitoring** and **experience rating** (premiums that depend on claims), and **performance pay** for agents, which trades incentives against risk-sharing because pay that varies with results exposes a risk-averse agent to risk.

| | Adverse selection | Moral hazard |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden | Type or quality | Action or effort |
| Timing | Before the contract | After the contract |
| Example | Risky drivers buy more insurance | Insured drivers take less care |
| Typical remedy | Screening, signaling, mandatory pooling | Deductibles, monitoring, incentive pay |

@example
title: When does the lemons market unravel?
problem: A used-car market has good cars (owner values at \$10,000, buyers at \$12,000) and lemons (owner values at \$4,000, buyers at \$5,000). Buyers cannot tell them apart. Find the lowest share of good cars $s^{*}$ at which good cars still trade, and what happens if $s = 0.3$.
step: Buyers' maximum price for a random car is $12000s + 5000(1 - s) = 5000 + 7000s$.
step: Owners of good cars sell only at a price of \$10,000 or more, so good cars trade only if $5000 + 7000s \ge 10000$, giving $s \ge 5/7 = 0.714$.
step: At $s = 0.3$: buyers will pay at most $5000 + 7000(0.3) = \$7{,}100$. That is below \$10,000, so owners of good cars withdraw.
step: Buyers then expect only lemons and will pay at most \$5,000. Lemons sell at a price between \$4,000 and \$5,000. The \$2,000 gain from each good-car trade is lost.
answer: Good cars trade only if $s \ge 5/7 \approx 71.4\%$; at $s = 0.3$ only lemons are sold.

@example
title: How big a deductible restores care?
problem: A homeowner faces a fire loss of \$10,000. If she takes a precaution costing her \$600 (in time and effort), the probability of a loss is 5%; otherwise 20%. The insurer cannot observe her precaution. What deductible makes her take the precaution if the policy pays the loss above the deductible $D$? Assume she compares expected dollar amounts, ignoring risk aversion, and $D \le \$10{,}000$.
step: The precaution is socially worthwhile: it cuts the expected loss by $(0.20 - 0.05)(10000) = \$1{,}500$, which exceeds its cost of \$600.
step: With full insurance she bears none of the loss, so she saves nothing by taking care and skips the precaution (moral hazard).
step: With deductible $D$ she bears $D$ in a loss. The precaution cuts her expected out-of-pocket cost by $(0.20 - 0.05)D = 0.15D$.
step: She takes it if $0.15D \ge 600$, that is $D \ge \$4{,}000$. A deductible of at least \$4,000 restores her incentive; a larger deductible would also leave her with more risk, which a risk-averse person dislikes, so the insurer picks the smallest deductible that works.
answer: A deductible of at least \$4,000.

@mistakes
- Calling every information problem "moral hazard". If the hidden fact is a fixed characteristic known before the deal, it is adverse selection; if it is an action taken after the deal, it is moral hazard.
- Computing the lemons price from the average over *all* cars when only some types are still being offered. The price must reflect the types that stay in the market.
- Assuming a signal must be useful in itself. A signal can work purely because it is cheaper for the high type to send, as the education example shows.
- Treating a high deductible as costless: it restores incentives but exposes the insured to risk.

@practice num
prompt: Good cars are worth \$8,000 to owners and \$10,000 to buyers; lemons are worth \$3,000 to owners and \$4,000 to buyers. Buyers cannot tell types apart. What is the lowest share of good cars at which owners of good cars are still willing to sell, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 66.67
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (8000 - 4000) / (10000 - 4000) * 100
hint: Set the buyers' average value $s(10000) + (1 - s)(4000)$ equal to \$8,000.
solution: $10000s + 4000(1 - s) = 8000$ gives $6000s = 4000$, so $s = 2/3 = 66.67\%$. Below this share good cars are withdrawn.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these are standard responses to moral hazard in insurance?
choice*: A deductible that the insured pays before coverage starts
choice*: Coinsurance, where the insured pays a share of each loss
choice*: Premiums that rise after claims (experience rating)
choice: Offering full coverage at a single flat premium with no monitoring
choice: Charging every customer the average premium
hint: Ask whether the measure makes the insured bear part of the consequences of her own actions.
solution: Deductibles, coinsurance and experience rating all link the insured's costs to losses she can influence. Full coverage with no monitoring removes any incentive for care, which is what creates the moral hazard. A single average premium is a response to adverse selection (pooling), and does not address the incentive to take care.

She compares expected dollar amounts and takes the precaution when it lowers her expected out-of-pocket cost by at least its cost. What is the smallest deductible that induces it, in dollars?
answer: 6000
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 900 / (0.25 - 0.10)
hint: In a loss she pays $D$. The precaution cuts the probability of a loss by 0.15.
solution: The expected saving is $(0.25 - 0.10)D = 0.15D$. Setting $0.15D \ge 900$ gives $D \ge 900/0.15 = \$6{,}000$.

@quiz num
prompt: An insurer sells a policy covering a \$20,000 loss to a pool in which 60% of customers are low-risk (5% chance of loss) and 40% are high-risk (25% chance). It cannot tell them apart and charges a premium equal to the pool's expected loss per customer. What is the premium, in dollars?
answer: 2600
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (0.6 * 0.05 + 0.4 * 0.25) * 20000
hint: Find the average probability of loss in the pool first.
solution: The average loss probability is $0.6(0.05) + 0.4(0.25) = 0.03 + 0.10 = 0.13$, so the pooled fair premium is $0.13 \times 20{,}000 = \$2{,}600$. If the low-risk customers leave, the premium for the high-risk pool alone would be $0.25 \times 20{,}000 = \$5{,}000$.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why a market for used cars can collapse when buyers cannot observe quality, and describe one remedy and how it works.
hint: Think about which sellers stay in the market when the price reflects average quality.
solution: When buyers cannot tell good cars from lemons, they will pay only for average quality. Owners of good cars, whose cars are worth more than that price to them, withdraw, so average quality falls, the price falls further, and in the extreme only lemons are left even though trade in good cars would have benefited both sides. A warranty is a remedy: it costs the seller of a lemon far more than the seller of a good car, so offering one credibly signals quality, and buyers will pay a higher price for warranted cars. Independent inspection or certification works by making quality observable.

rubric: Concludes that gains from trade are lost, possibly leaving only lemons.
rubric: Names a remedy (warranty, certification, inspection or reputation) and explains why it works.
