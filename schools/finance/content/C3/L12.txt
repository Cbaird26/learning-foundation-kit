@article
Households earn income by selling the services of the factors they own: labor, capital and land. So the prices of factors, wages, rents and returns on capital, determine the distribution of income. A firm's demand for a factor is **derived demand**: it hires only because the factor helps produce something it can sell.

### Demand for labor

A firm that sells output at price $p$ and hires labor at wage $w$ earns profit $\pi = p\,f(L) - wL$ (capital fixed). Hiring one more unit of labor adds $MP_L$ units of output, which sell for $MR \cdot MP_L$. This is the **marginal revenue product** $MRP_L = MR \cdot MP_L$. In a competitive output market $MR = p$ and it is the **value of the marginal product**, $VMP_L = p\,MP_L$. The firm hires up to the point where the extra revenue equals the extra cost:

$$MRP_L = \text{marginal factor cost}.$$

For a firm that takes the wage as given, marginal factor cost is $w$, so it hires where $p\,MP_L = w$. Because $MP_L$ diminishes, the $VMP_L$ curve slopes downward and is the firm's labor demand curve. The same logic for capital rented at rate $r$ gives $p\,MP_K = r$.

### Labor supply

A worker divides her time between work and leisure. A higher wage raises the reward for working (a **substitution effect** toward work) but also makes her richer and she may want more leisure (an **income effect**). If the income effect dominates at high wages, the individual labor supply curve bends backward. Market labor supply usually slopes upward because higher wages also draw new workers into the market. The competitive wage is where labor demand equals labor supply.

### Factor payments and shares

If production has constant returns to scale and factor markets are competitive, each factor is paid its marginal product and the payments exhaust output (**Euler's theorem**): $wL + rK = pq$. For $q = AK^{a}L^{1-a}$ labor's share of revenue is the exponent $1 - a$ and capital's share is $a$. A payment to a factor above its opportunity cost is an **economic rent**.

### Monopsony

A **monopsony** is the only buyer in a factor market, so it faces the upward-sloping market supply curve $w(L)$. To hire another worker it must raise the wage for everyone, so the **marginal factor cost** exceeds the wage:

$$MFC = \frac{d(wL)}{dL} = w + L\frac{dw}{dL} > w.$$

It hires where $MRP_L = MFC$ and pays the wage from the supply curve at that quantity. The result: employment and the wage are both lower than in a competitive market, and the wage is below $MRP_L$. A **minimum wage** set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage can *raise* employment, because it makes the marginal factor cost equal to the wage up to the point where supply meets the floor. A minimum wage above the competitive wage reduces employment in a competitive market, since a floor above the equilibrium wage cuts the quantity of labor demanded. These are model predictions: how much market power employers actually have in a given labor market, and so which model fits, is an empirical question on which studies disagree, and the policy also involves value judgments about who gains and who loses.

### The distribution of income

The **Lorenz curve** plots the cumulative share of income held by the poorest fraction of households; the diagonal is perfect equality. The **Gini coefficient** is the area between the diagonal and the curve divided by the area under the diagonal. It is 0 for perfect equality and approaches 1 as one household holds nearly all the income. For $n$ households with incomes $x_i$ and mean $\mu$,

$$G = \frac{1}{2n^2\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}|x_i - x_j|.$$

Income differences reflect differences in **human capital** (education and experience), compensating differentials for unpleasant or risky work, ownership of capital, inheritance, discrimination, luck, and taxes and transfers. Economists describe these facts; how much redistribution is desirable involves a value judgment about the trade-off between equity and efficiency, on which reasonable people disagree.

@example
title: How many workers does a competitive firm hire?
problem: A firm's output is $q = 20\sqrt{L}$ (capital fixed). It sells at \$5 per unit and pays \$10 per labor-hour. How much labor does it hire, and what are its revenue and wage bill?
step: $MP_L = dq/dL = 10L^{-0.5}$, so $VMP_L = 5 \times 10L^{-0.5} = 50L^{-0.5}$.
step: Set $VMP_L = w$: $50/\sqrt{L} = 10$, so $\sqrt{L} = 5$ and $L = 25$ hours.
step: Output is $q = 20(5) = 100$ units and revenue is $5 \times 100 = \$500$. The wage bill is $10 \times 25 = \$250$, so \$250 is left to pay for capital and other costs.
step: Check at $L = 26$: $VMP_L = 50/\sqrt{26} = 9.80 < 10$, so the 26th hour costs more than it brings in. At $L = 24$: $VMP_L = 10.21 > 10$, so more hours are still worth hiring. The two sides balance at $L = 25$.
answer: $L = 25$ hours, revenue \$500, wage bill \$250.

@example
title: Monopsony and the minimum wage
problem: A firm is the only employer in a town. Labor supply is $w = 2 + 0.5L$ and the marginal revenue product is $MRP = 20 - 0.5L$, with $w$ in dollars per hour and $L$ in thousands of hours. Find employment and the wage under monopsony and in a competitive market. What happens with a minimum wage of \$11?
step: Total labor cost is $wL = 2L + 0.5L^2$, so $MFC = 2 + L$. Monopsony: $20 - 0.5L = 2 + L$ gives $L = 12$. The wage is $w = 2 + 0.5(12) = \$8$, while $MRP = 20 - 6 = \$14$.
step: Competitive market: $20 - 0.5L = 2 + 0.5L$ gives $L = 18$ and $w = \$11$.
step: Minimum wage of \$11: the firm now pays \$11 for each hour up to $L = 18$ (where supply meets the floor), so $MFC = 11$ in that range. It hires until $MRP = 11$, which gives $20 - 0.5L = 11$ and $L = 18$.
step: Compared with monopsony, the wage rises from \$8 to \$11 and employment rises from 12 to 18 thousand hours. The floor sits exactly at the competitive wage here; a floor above \$11 would reduce employment below 18.
answer: Monopsony: $L = 12$, $w = \$8$. Competitive: $L = 18$, $w = \$11$. A \$11 floor yields $L = 18$ at $w = \$11$.

@mistakes
- Setting $w = MRP$ for a monopsony. A monopsonist hires where $MRP = MFC$ and pays the lower wage on the supply curve.
- Using $MP_L$ instead of $MRP_L = MR \cdot MP_L$ (the value of the marginal product) when comparing with the wage.
- Concluding that any minimum wage raises employment because it can in monopsony: above the competitive wage, employment falls.
- Reading a high Gini coefficient as a measure of poverty. It measures dispersion of incomes, not their level.

@practice num
prompt: A competitive firm's output is $q = 12\sqrt{L}$ (capital fixed). It sells at \$8 per unit and pays a wage of \$12 per hour. How many hours of labor does it hire to maximize profit?
answer: 16
tol: 0.01
check: (8 * 6 / 12) ** 2
hint: $MP_L = 6L^{-0.5}$. Set $p \cdot MP_L = w$.
solution: $VMP_L = 8 \times 6L^{-0.5} = 48/\sqrt{L}$. Setting $48/\sqrt{L} = 12$ gives $\sqrt{L} = 4$, so $L = 16$ hours.

@practice num
prompt: A monopsonist faces labor supply $w = 4 + 0.25L$ and has marginal revenue product $MRP = 40 - 0.5L$. What wage does it pay, in dollars per hour?
answer: 13
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 4 + 0.25 * ((40 - 4) / (0.5 + 0.5))
hint: Total labor cost is $wL = 4L + 0.25L^2$, so $MFC = 4 + 0.5L$. Set $MRP = MFC$.
solution: $40 - 0.5L = 4 + 0.5L$ gives $L = 36$. The wage is read from the supply curve: $w = 4 + 0.25(36) = \$13$, while $MRP = 40 - 18 = \$22$.

@practice num
prompt: Four households have incomes of \$10,000, \$20,000, \$30,000 and \$40,000. What is the Gini coefficient of this distribution, using $G = \frac{1}{2n^2\mu}\sum_i\sum_j|x_i - x_j|$ (summing over all ordered pairs)? Give a decimal.
answer: 0.25
tol: 0.001
check: sum(abs(a - b) for a in [10, 20, 30, 40] for b in [10, 20, 30, 40]) / (2 * 4 ** 2 * 25)
hint: The mean is \$25,000. The sum over ordered pairs of $|x_i - x_j|$ is twice the sum over distinct pairs.
solution: Absolute differences over distinct pairs (in thousands): 10, 20, 30, 10, 20, 10, totaling 100, so the ordered-pair sum is 200. Then $G = 200/(2 \times 16 \times 25) = 0.25$.

@practice num
prompt: A competitive labor market has labor demand $w = 35 - L$ (this is the marginal revenue product curve) and labor supply $w = 5 + 0.5L$, with $L$ in thousands of workers and $w$ in dollars per hour. A binding minimum wage of \$18 is imposed. How many thousand workers are employed?
answer: 17
tol: 0.01
check: 35 - 18
hint: At \$18, employment is limited by the smaller of the quantity demanded and the quantity supplied.
solution: Without the floor, $35 - L = 5 + 0.5L$ gives $L = 20$ and $w = \$15$, so the \$18 floor binds. Labor demanded at \$18 is $L = 35 - 18 = 17$ and labor supplied is $(18 - 5)/0.5 = 26$. Employment is the smaller, 17 thousand, and 9 thousand workers are unemployed.

@quiz num
prompt: A competitive firm has the production function $q = K^{0.3}L^{0.7}$ and pays each factor its marginal revenue product. Its annual revenue is \$1,000,000. What is its wage bill, in dollars?
answer: 700000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 0.7 * 1000000
hint: With a Cobb-Douglas production function and competitive factor markets, each factor receives a share of revenue equal to its exponent.
solution: $wL = p\,MP_L\,L = p\,(0.7q/L)L = 0.7pq = 0.7 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = \$700{,}000$. Capital receives 30%, or \$300,000, and payments exhaust revenue.

@quiz self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain how a minimum wage can raise employment in a monopsonistic labor market, and why this result does not hold in a competitive labor market.
hint: Compare the monopsonist's marginal factor cost with and without the wage floor.
solution: A monopsonist must raise the wage for all workers to hire one more, so its marginal factor cost lies above the supply curve and it hires fewer workers and pays less than the competitive wage. A minimum wage between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage makes the wage a fixed price for each worker up to the point where supply meets the floor, so the marginal factor cost equals the wage there and the firm hires more workers. In a competitive market the equilibrium wage already equals marginal revenue product, so a floor above it only reduces the quantity of labor demanded and employment falls.
rubric: Explains that marginal factor cost exceeds the wage for a monopsonist, so employment is below the competitive level.
rubric: Explains that a floor in the right range makes marginal factor cost equal to the wage, raising employment.
rubric: Says that in a competitive market a binding floor above the equilibrium wage cuts the quantity demanded.
