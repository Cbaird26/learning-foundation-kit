@article
Markets coordinate millions of decisions without a planner. When are the outcomes good, and when can they be improved? We need a yardstick and a list of the conditions under which markets meet it.

### Surplus

- **Consumer surplus (CS)**: the area between the demand curve and the price, up to the quantity bought. It is the total amount buyers would have paid minus what they pay.
- **Producer surplus (PS)**: the area between the price and the supply curve, up to the quantity sold: revenue minus variable cost.
- **Total surplus** $= CS + PS$ (plus any tax revenue, as it is spent on something).

For linear curves these areas are triangles. In a competitive market the equilibrium quantity maximizes total surplus: it is the point where the marginal benefit to buyers equals the marginal cost to sellers.

### Efficiency

An allocation is **Pareto efficient** if nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off. The **first fundamental theorem of welfare economics** says that a competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient, provided there are no externalities, no public goods, no market power and no asymmetric information, and markets exist for everything that matters. **Market failure** is the failure of one of these conditions. Efficiency is not fairness: many efficient allocations leave some people with very little.

### Deadweight loss of taxes and price controls

A tax or price control moves the quantity away from the efficient level. The lost surplus, which no one gains, is the **deadweight loss (DWL)**. For a per-unit tax $t$ that reduces the quantity from $Q^{*}$ to $Q_t$ with linear curves,

$$DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}\,t\,(Q^{*} - Q_t).$$

A **binding price ceiling** below equilibrium reduces the quantity traded to what suppliers will sell at the ceiling price. If the units are allocated to the buyers who value them most, the DWL is the triangle between demand and supply over the units no longer traded.

### Externalities

An **externality** is a cost or benefit imposed on others outside the market. With a **negative externality**, the social marginal cost is $MSC = MPC + MEC$, where $MPC$ is the private marginal cost and $MEC$ the marginal external cost. The market produces where demand $= MPC$, too much from society's view; the efficient quantity has demand $= MSC$. A **Pigouvian tax** equal to $MEC$ at the efficient quantity makes producers face the social cost. The **Coase theorem**: if property rights are clearly assigned and bargaining is costless, the parties will reach an efficient outcome whoever holds the rights (the distribution differs). With many parties or high transaction costs, bargaining fails and government action may help. Positive externalities (such as vaccination) lead to too little production, and a subsidy equal to the marginal external benefit can correct this.

### Public goods

A **public good** is **non-rival** (one person's use leaves as much for others) and **non-excludable** (people cannot be prevented from using it). Because consumers can free-ride, private markets underprovide it. Since everyone consumes the same quantity, society's marginal benefit is the **vertical** sum of individual marginal willingness to pay, and the efficient quantity satisfies the **Samuelson condition**:

$$\sum_{i} MRS_i = MC.$$

**Common resources** (a fishery) are rival but non-excludable and tend to be overused; **club goods** are non-rival but excludable.

@example
title: Surplus and the deadweight loss of a tax
problem: Demand is $P = 100 - Q$ and supply is $P = 10 + 2Q$. Find equilibrium and surplus. Then a tax of \$15 per unit is levied on sellers. Find the quantity, the prices buyers pay and sellers receive, tax revenue and the deadweight loss.
step: Equilibrium: $100 - Q = 10 + 2Q$ gives $Q = 30$ and $P = \$70$. $CS = \tfrac12(100 - 70)(30) = 450$; $PS = \tfrac12(70 - 10)(30) = 900$; total surplus $= \$1{,}350$.
step: With the tax, sellers' supply price becomes $P_s = 10 + 2Q + 15 = 25 + 2Q$. Then $100 - Q = 25 + 2Q$ gives $Q_t = 25$. Buyers pay $100 - 25 = \$75$ and sellers keep $75 - 15 = \$60$.
step: Tax revenue $= 15 \times 25 = \$375$. New $CS = \tfrac12(100 - 75)(25) = 312.5$ and $PS = \tfrac12(60 - 10)(25) = 625$.
step: $CS + PS + \text{revenue} = 312.5 + 625 + 375 = 1{,}312.5$, so $DWL = 1{,}350 - 1{,}312.5 = \$37.50$. Check with the formula: $\tfrac12(15)(30 - 25) = 37.5$.
answer: $Q_t = 25$, buyers pay \$75, sellers receive \$60, revenue \$375, deadweight loss \$37.50.

@example
title: A negative externality and a Pigouvian tax
problem: In the market above (demand $P = 100 - Q$, private marginal cost $MPC = 10 + 2Q$) each unit also imposes a constant external cost of \$12 on neighbors. Find the efficient quantity, the deadweight loss of the unregulated market, and the Pigouvian tax.
step: Social marginal cost: $MSC = 10 + 2Q + 12 = 22 + 2Q$. Efficient quantity: $100 - Q = 22 + 2Q$ gives $Q^{*} = 26$ (price paid by buyers \$74).
step: The unregulated market produces $Q = 30$ (price \$70), four units more than the efficient quantity. At $Q = 30$, $MSC = 22 + 60 = 82$ exceeds the marginal benefit of \$70 by \$12.
step: $DWL = \tfrac12(12)(30 - 26) = \$24$.
step: A per-unit tax of \$12 shifts the producers' marginal cost to $22 + 2Q$, so the market now chooses $Q = 26$: the tax equals the marginal external cost and makes producers internalize it.
answer: $Q^{*} = 26$, deadweight loss \$24, Pigouvian tax \$12 per unit.

@mistakes
- Treating tax revenue as part of the deadweight loss. Revenue is a transfer to the government; only the surplus lost beyond it is deadweight loss.
- Summing demand curves horizontally for a public good. Because all consume the same quantity, add the marginal benefits vertically.
- Thinking that removing all externality is efficient. The efficient quantity of a polluting good is not zero; it equates marginal benefit and *marginal social* cost.
- Equating efficiency with fairness, or the first welfare theorem with a claim that any market outcome is desirable.

@practice num
prompt: Demand is $P = 90 - Q$ and supply is $P = 30 + Q$. A price ceiling of \$40 is imposed and is enforced; suppose the units that are sold go to the buyers who value them most. What is the deadweight loss, in dollars?
answer: 400
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * ((90 - (40 - 30)) - 40) * ((90 - 30) / 2 - (40 - 30))
hint: Quantity supplied at the ceiling is $40 - 30$. The unrealized trades lie between this quantity and the free-market quantity.
solution: Equilibrium is $Q = 30$ at a price of \$60. At \$40 sellers supply $Q = 10$, where the demand price is \$80 and the supply price is \$40. The lost trades form a triangle of height $80 - 40 = 40$ and base $30 - 10 = 20$, so $DWL = \tfrac12(40)(20) = \$400$.

@practice num
prompt: Two consumers have marginal willingness to pay for a public good of $MB_1 = 20 - Q$ and $MB_2 = 30 - Q$ dollars per unit (for $Q$ at most 20). The good can be produced at a constant marginal cost of \$26 per unit. What quantity is efficient?
answer: 12
tol: 0.01
check: (20 + 30 - 26) / 2
hint: Add the marginal benefits vertically: $MB = (20 - Q) + (30 - Q)$.
solution: The Samuelson condition is $MB_1 + MB_2 = MC$: $50 - 2Q = 26$, so $Q = 12$. At that quantity the benefits are \$8 and \$18, which sum to \$26.

@quiz num
prompt: The market demand for a good is $P = 90 - Q$ and private marginal cost is $MPC = 10 + Q$. Production imposes a marginal external cost of $MEC = 0.5Q$ on others. What per-unit Pigouvian tax, in dollars, induces the efficient quantity? (Evaluate the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity.)
answer: 16
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * ((90 - 10) / (1 + 1 + 0.5))
hint: Efficient quantity: demand equals $MSC = 10 + Q + 0.5Q$.
solution: $90 - Q = 10 + 1.5Q$ gives $Q^{*} = 32$. The tax equals the marginal external cost there: $0.5 \times 32 = \$16$. (Check: with the tax, $90 - Q = 26 + Q$ gives $Q = 32$.)

@quiz self
prompt: A factory's smoke damages a nearby laundry. In four or five sentences, state the Coase theorem and explain what condition would make private bargaining fail and call for a government remedy.
hint: Think about property rights and the cost of bargaining.
solution: The Coase theorem says that if property rights are well defined and the parties can bargain at no cost, they will reach an efficient outcome whichever party holds the rights: the factory and laundry will agree on the amount of smoke that maximizes their joint surplus (the party who holds the right is paid or pays accordingly, so who is richer differs). It fails when transaction costs are high, when property rights are unclear, or when there are many affected parties who each have an incentive to free-ride on the others' bargaining. In those cases a Pigouvian tax, a regulation or a tradable permit system may be needed.
rubric: States that with clear property rights and costless bargaining the parties reach an efficient outcome regardless of who holds the rights.
rubric: Names at least one reason bargaining fails (transaction costs, unclear rights, many parties or free-riding).
rubric: Mentions a government remedy such as a tax, a regulation or tradable permits.
