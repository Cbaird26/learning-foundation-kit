@article
Between monopoly and perfect competition lies **oligopoly**: a few firms, each large enough that its choices affect the others. Each firm must predict what the others will do, so we need **game theory**, the analysis of strategic interaction. A game has **players**, **strategies** and **payoffs**.

### Nash equilibrium

A strategy is **dominant** if it gives a higher payoff than every other strategy whatever the opponent does. A strategy profile is a **Nash equilibrium** if each player's strategy is a **best response** to the others': no player can gain by changing her own strategy alone. A dominant-strategy equilibrium is always a Nash equilibrium; the converse is false.

To find the pure-strategy equilibria of a matrix game, mark the row player's best payoff in each column and the column player's best payoff in each row. Cells with both marks are equilibria.

The **prisoners' dilemma** shows that equilibrium need not be efficient. Two firms choose High or Low price; payoffs (row, column) are in millions of dollars:

| | Column: High | Column: Low |
|---|---|---|
| **Row: High** | (10, 10) | (2, 14) |
| **Row: Low** | (14, 2) | (5, 5) |

Low is dominant for each firm (14 > 10 and 5 > 2), so the only Nash equilibrium is (Low, Low) with payoffs (5, 5), although (High, High) would give both 10.

### Mixed strategies

Some games have no equilibrium in pure strategies, or several. A **mixed strategy** randomizes over pure strategies. In a mixed equilibrium each player randomizes so as to make the *opponent* indifferent between the strategies she mixes over. Every finite game has at least one Nash equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies.

### Cournot competition

In the **Cournot model** firms choose quantities simultaneously. With inverse demand $P = a - bQ$, $Q = q_1 + q_2$, and constant marginal cost $c$, firm 1 maximizes $(a - b(q_1 + q_2) - c)\,q_1$. The first-order condition gives the **reaction (best-response) function**

$$q_1 = \frac{a - c - b\,q_2}{2b},$$

and symmetrically for firm 2. At the intersection (the Nash equilibrium), $q_1 = q_2 = \dfrac{a - c}{3b}$. Compared with the monopoly quantity $\dfrac{a-c}{2b}$ (total) and the competitive quantity $\dfrac{a-c}{b}$, the Cournot total $\dfrac{2(a-c)}{3b}$ lies between them. With $n$ symmetric firms each produces $\dfrac{a-c}{(n+1)b}$.

In the **Bertrand model** firms with identical products and the same constant marginal cost $c$ choose prices; the only equilibrium has $P = c$ and zero profit, even with two firms. In the **Stackelberg model** one firm commits to its quantity first; the leader produces $\dfrac{a-c}{2b}$ and the follower $\dfrac{a-c}{4b}$, and the leader earns more.

### Repeated games and collusion

If firms meet repeatedly, a **grim trigger strategy** ("price High until the rival deviates, then price Low forever") may sustain cooperation. Let $\pi_c$ be the per-period collusive profit, $\pi_d$ the one-period profit from deviating, $\pi_n$ the profit in the one-shot Nash equilibrium, and $\delta$ the discount factor per period. Cooperating forever is worth $\pi_c/(1 - \delta)$; deviating gives $\pi_d + \delta\pi_n/(1 - \delta)$. Cooperation is an equilibrium if

$$\frac{\pi_c}{1 - \delta} \ge \pi_d + \frac{\delta\,\pi_n}{1 - \delta} \iff \delta \ge \frac{\pi_d - \pi_c}{\pi_d - \pi_n}.$$

For the table above, $\pi_c = 10$, $\pi_d = 14$, $\pi_n = 5$, so $\delta \ge 4/9 = 0.444$: firms must value the future enough.

@example
title: Pure and mixed equilibria in a standards game
problem: Two firms, Fern and Gale, choose a standard S or T. Payoffs (Fern, Gale) are: (S, S) = (4, 3); (T, T) = (2, 4); and (0, 0) if they choose different standards. Find all Nash equilibria.
step: Pure equilibria. If Gale plays S, Fern's best response is S (4 against 0). If Gale plays T, Fern's best response is T (2 against 0). By symmetry of the argument for Gale, (S, S) and (T, T) are both Nash equilibria. Mismatches are not, since either player gains by switching.
step: Mixed equilibrium. Let Fern play S with probability $p$. Gale is indifferent when $3p = 4(1 - p)$, so $p = 4/7$.
step: Let Gale play S with probability $q$. Fern is indifferent when $4q = 2(1 - q)$, so $q = 1/3$.
step: Expected payoffs: Fern earns $4q = 4/3$ and Gale earns $3p = 12/7$, both below what either gets in the pure equilibria (4 and 3 in (S, S); 2 and 4 in (T, T)): coordination fails sometimes in the mixed equilibrium.
answer: Pure: (S, S) and (T, T). Mixed: Fern plays S with probability $4/7$, Gale plays S with probability $1/3$; expected payoffs $4/3$ and $12/7$.

@example
title: Cournot duopoly
problem: Two firms with constant marginal cost \$10 face market demand $P = 100 - Q$. Find the Cournot equilibrium, and compare with monopoly and perfect competition.
step: Firm 1's profit is $(90 - q_1 - q_2)q_1$. The first-order condition is $90 - 2q_1 - q_2 = 0$, so $q_1 = (90 - q_2)/2$. By symmetry $q_2 = (90 - q_1)/2$.
step: Solve: $q_1 = q_2 = q$ gives $q = (90 - q)/2$, so $q = 30$. Total $Q = 60$, price $P = 100 - 60 = \$40$, and each firm earns $(40 - 10)(30) = \$900$.
step: Monopoly: $Q = 45$, $P = \$55$, profit \$2,025 (so \$1,012.50 each if shared equally). Perfect competition: $P = MC = \$10$, $Q = 90$, profit zero.
step: Cournot lies between: price \$40 against \$55 and \$10; industry profit \$1,800 against \$2,025 under monopoly. Each firm would prefer the cartel outcome, but if firm 2 produces 22.5 firm 1's best response is 33.75, not 22.5: the cartel is not a Nash equilibrium.
answer: $q_1 = q_2 = 30$, $P = \$40$, profit \$900 each.

@mistakes
- Looking for the highest payoffs instead of mutual best responses. A Nash equilibrium need not be efficient.
- Choosing your own mixing probabilities to make *yourself* indifferent. In a mixed equilibrium your probabilities are set so that the *other* player is indifferent between her pure strategies.
- Calling a game's only equilibrium a "dominant-strategy equilibrium" when no strategy is dominant.
- Using the monopoly condition $MR = MC$ in the Cournot model without subtracting the rival's output: each firm's marginal revenue is $a - 2bq_i - bq_j$.

model:
```python
def pure_nash(payoffs):
    """Return the pure-strategy Nash equilibria of a two-player matrix game."""
    n_rows = len(payoffs)
    n_cols = len(payoffs[0])
    result = []
    for i in range(n_rows):
        for j in range(n_cols):
            row_pay, col_pay = payoffs[i][j]
            row_best = all(payoffs[k][j][0] <= row_pay for k in range(n_rows))
            col_best = all(payoffs[i][k][1] <= col_pay for k in range(n_cols))
            if row_best and col_best:
                result.append((i, j))
    return result
```
hint: For each cell, compare the row player's payoff with the other rows in the same column.
hint: Then compare the column player's payoff with the other columns in the same row. Keep the cell if both comparisons hold.
solution: A cell is an equilibrium when the row player's payoff is at least as high as in every other row of that column and the column player's payoff is at least as high as in every other column of that row. Looping over rows then columns produces the list already sorted. The ties test shows that equal payoffs do not count as profitable deviations.
