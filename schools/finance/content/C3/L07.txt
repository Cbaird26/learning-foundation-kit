@article
A competitive firm takes the price as given. A **monopolist**, the only seller in its market, faces the whole market demand curve and chooses a point on it. To sell more, it must lower the price on *every* unit, so selling an extra unit brings in less than its price. That single fact drives everything in this lesson.

### Marginal revenue

Let inverse demand be $P(Q)$. Revenue is $R(Q) = P(Q)\,Q$ and

$$MR = \frac{dR}{dQ} = P + Q\,\frac{dP}{dQ} < P.$$

For linear inverse demand $P = a - bQ$: $R = aQ - bQ^2$ and $MR = a - 2bQ$. The marginal revenue curve has the same intercept as demand and **twice the slope**. In terms of the price elasticity of demand $\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\dfrac{P}{Q}$, $MR = P\left(1 + \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)$, so MR is positive only where demand is elastic ($|\varepsilon| > 1$).

### Profit maximization and market power

The monopolist maximizes $\pi = R(Q) - C(Q)$, so it sets

$$MR(Q) = MC(Q),$$

and reads the price from the demand curve at that quantity. Using $MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon)$, the rule rearranges to the **Lerner index** of market power:

$$L = \frac{P - MC}{P} = \frac{1}{|\varepsilon|}.$$

The less elastic the demand, the higher the markup. The monopolist always operates on the elastic part of demand, and there is no "supply curve" for a monopolist: the quantity it supplies depends on the demand curve's shape, not only on its cost curve.

### Welfare loss

A competitive market would produce where $P = MC$. The monopolist produces less and charges more. Consumers lose surplus, and part of that is transferred to the monopolist as profit; the rest, the **deadweight loss**, is lost by everyone. With linear demand and constant marginal cost, the deadweight loss is the triangle between the demand curve and the $MC$ line, from the monopoly quantity to the competitive quantity:

$$DWL = \tfrac{1}{2}\,(P_m - MC)\,(Q_c - Q_m).$$

A **natural monopoly** has economies of scale over the whole relevant range, so one firm is the cheapest way to serve the market. Regulators may then cap prices at average cost (which gives zero economic profit) or at marginal cost (which needs a subsidy).

### Price discrimination

A monopolist can earn more by charging different customers different prices. This needs (1) market power, (2) the ability to tell customers or groups apart, and (3) no resale between them.

- **First-degree** (perfect): charge each customer her maximum willingness to pay. Output equals the competitive quantity and there is no deadweight loss, but the monopolist takes all the surplus.
- **Second-degree**: the price depends on the quantity or version bought (quantity discounts, two-part tariffs: a fixed fee plus a per-unit price).
- **Third-degree**: different prices for identifiable groups. With constant marginal cost the firm sets $MR_A = MR_B = MC$ in each market, equivalently $P_i\left(1 - \dfrac{1}{|\varepsilon_i|}\right) = MC$. The group with the less elastic demand pays the higher price.

@example
title: Monopoly pricing
problem: A monopolist faces $P = 100 - 2Q$ and has constant marginal cost \$20 and no fixed cost. Find its price, quantity, profit, the Lerner index and the deadweight loss.
step: $MR = 100 - 4Q$. Setting $MR = MC$: $100 - 4Q = 20$, so $Q_m = 20$. Price from demand: $P_m = 100 - 40 = \$60$.
step: Profit $= (60 - 20)(20) = \$800$. Elasticity at the optimum: $\varepsilon = \dfrac{dQ}{dP}\dfrac{P}{Q} = (-0.5)\dfrac{60}{20} = -1.5$. Lerner index $= (60 - 20)/60 = 0.667 = 1/1.5$.
step: Competitive benchmark: $P = MC$ gives $100 - 2Q = 20$, so $Q_c = 40$. Deadweight loss $= \tfrac12(60 - 20)(40 - 20) = \$400$.
step: Consumer surplus falls from $\tfrac12(100 - 20)(40) = 1{,}600$ to $\tfrac12(100 - 60)(20) = 400$. Of the \$1,200 lost, \$800 becomes profit and \$400 is deadweight loss.
answer: $Q = 20$, $P = \$60$, profit \$800, Lerner index $2/3$, deadweight loss \$400.

@example
title: Third-degree price discrimination
problem: A firm sells in two separated markets. Market A has $P_A = 80 - 2Q_A$ and market B has $P_B = 60 - Q_B$. Marginal cost is a constant \$10. Find the discriminating prices and profit, and compare with a single price. Assume no resale between markets and no fixed cost.
step: Market A: $MR_A = 80 - 4Q_A = 10$, so $Q_A = 17.5$ and $P_A = 80 - 35 = \$45$. Profit $= (45 - 10)(17.5) = \$612.50$.
step: Market B: $MR_B = 60 - 2Q_B = 10$, so $Q_B = 25$ and $P_B = \$35$. Profit $= (35 - 10)(25) = \$625$. Total profit $= \$1{,}237.50$. The higher price is in A, where demand is less elastic at the optimum ($|\varepsilon_A| = 45/(2 \times 17.5) = 1.29$ against $|\varepsilon_B| = 35/25 = 1.4$).
step: A single price: with both markets served (price below \$60), $Q = \dfrac{80 - P}{2} + (60 - P) = 100 - 1.5P$. Inverse demand is $P = 66.67 - 0.667Q$, so $MR = 66.67 - 1.333Q = 10$ gives $Q = 42.5$ and $P = \$38.33$.
step: Profit at the single price is $(38.33 - 10)(42.5) = \$1{,}204.17$, below \$1,237.50. Discrimination raises profit by about \$33.33 here.
answer: $P_A = \$45$, $P_B = \$35$, profit \$1,237.50 against \$1,204.17 with one price.

@mistakes
- Setting price equal to marginal revenue instead of reading the price from the demand curve once $MR = MC$ gives the quantity.
- Using the demand slope for the $MR$ slope: for linear demand, $MR$ is twice as steep.
- Saying monopoly profit is the deadweight loss. Profit is a transfer from consumers; the deadweight loss is the surplus that disappears.
- Charging a higher price in the market with the higher elasticity. The less elastic market (smaller $|\varepsilon|$) pays more.

answer: 475
lead: \$

@practice mc
prompt: A profit-maximizing monopolist with constant marginal cost faces demand with price elasticity $\varepsilon$ at its chosen price. Which expression equals its Lerner index $(P - MC)/P$?
choice*: $1/|\varepsilon|$
choice: $|\varepsilon|$
choice: $1 - |\varepsilon|$
choice: $MC/P$
hint: Start from $MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon) = MC$.
solution: $MR = P(1 + 1/\varepsilon) = MC$ gives $P - MC = -P/\varepsilon = P/|\varepsilon|$ (as $\varepsilon < 0$), so $(P - MC)/P = 1/|\varepsilon|$. $MC/P$ is $1 - 1/|\varepsilon|$, not the Lerner index.

@practice num
prompt: A monopolist faces inverse demand $P = 120 - 3Q$ and has constant marginal cost of \$30 (and no fixed cost). Using the competitive output ($P = MC$) as the benchmark, what is the deadweight loss of monopoly, in dollars?
answer: 337.5
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * ((120 - 3 * 15) - 30) * ((120 - 30) / 3 - 15)
hint: Monopoly: $Q_m = 15$, $P_m = 75$. Competitive: $120 - 3Q = 30$.
solution: $Q_c = (120 - 30)/3 = 30$. $DWL = \tfrac12(P_m - MC)(Q_c - Q_m) = \tfrac12(75 - 30)(30 - 15) = \$337.50$.

@practice num
prompt: A firm with constant marginal cost of \$10 and no fixed cost sells in two separated markets with no resale. Demand in market A is $P = 90 - Q$ and in market B is $P = 50 - Q$. It sets the profit-maximizing price in each. What is its total profit, in dollars?
answer: 2000
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (90 - (90 - 10) / 2 - 10) * ((90 - 10) / 2) + (50 - (50 - 10) / 2 - 10) * ((50 - 10) / 2)
hint: In each market $MR = a - 2Q$; set it equal to 10.
solution: Market A: $90 - 2Q = 10$ gives $Q = 40$, $P = 50$, profit $(50 - 10)(40) = 1{,}600$. Market B: $50 - 2Q = 10$ gives $Q = 20$, $P = 30$, profit $(30 - 10)(20) = 400$. Total \$2,000.

@quiz num
prompt: A monopolist has constant marginal cost of \$30 and faces a demand curve with constant price elasticity $\varepsilon = -2.5$. What price maximizes its profit? Give the answer in dollars.
answer: 50
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 30 / (1 - 1 / 2.5)
hint: Use $P(1 - 1/|\varepsilon|) = MC$.
solution: $P(1 - 1/2.5) = 0.6P = 30$, so $P = \$50$. The Lerner index is $(50 - 30)/50 = 0.4 = 1/2.5$.

@quiz self
prompt: List the three conditions needed for a monopolist to price discriminate, and explain in two or three sentences why, in third-degree discrimination, the group with the less elastic demand pays the higher price.
hint: Use $P_i(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_i|) = MC$ for each group.
solution: The firm needs market power, a way to identify or sort customers into groups, and a way to prevent resale between the groups. With a common marginal cost, profit maximization sets $P_i(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_i|) = MC$ in each market. The factor $(1 - 1/|\varepsilon_i|)$ is smaller when $|\varepsilon_i|$ is small, so the price must be higher there to satisfy the same equation: customers who respond less to price are charged the higher markup.
rubric: Names market power, the ability to separate customers or groups, and the absence of resale.
rubric: Uses the condition $MR_i = MC$ or the inverse-elasticity rule in each market.
rubric: Concludes that the market with smaller $|\varepsilon|$ has the higher price.
