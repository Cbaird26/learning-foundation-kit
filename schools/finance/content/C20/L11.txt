@article
Mean-variance theory (the portfolio model of your prerequisite course) treats an investor as one decision maker who holds a single portfolio on the efficient frontier, caring only about its mean and variance. Real households often hold a money-market account, a few individual stocks bought on a tip, a diversified fund and a lottery ticket at the same time. **Behavioral portfolio theory (BPT)** describes such investors.

### Layers of goals

In BPT an investor's portfolio is a **pyramid of layers**, each tied to a goal with its own **aspiration level** and tolerated probability of falling short. The bottom, **safety**, layer protects against poverty (essential spending, emergencies) and is held in safe assets; upper layers pursue **aspirations** (a larger home, bequests, wealth) with riskier, often skewed assets. The investor is risk averse in the safety layer and risk seeking in the aspiration layer, which fits the fourfold pattern of prospect theory (week 2). Because the layers are separate mental accounts, the investor does not look at covariances across them, so the whole portfolio may be inefficient in mean-variance terms: it may be under-diversified, hold lottery-like stocks, prefer local or familiar stocks (**home bias**), and use naive **1/n diversification** (splitting equally across the options offered, regardless of their risk). The theory is a descriptive one; whether the resulting portfolios are mistakes depends on the investor's true goals, and that is a question for the adviser and client.

### Safety-first

Roy's **safety-first criterion** picks the portfolio that minimizes the probability of falling below a threshold return $H$ that the investor must not miss. If portfolio returns are normal with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$,

$$P(R < H) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{H - \mu}{\sigma}\right) = \Phi(-SFR), \qquad SFR = \frac{\mu - H}{\sigma},$$

where $\Phi$ is the standard normal cumulative distribution function and $SFR$ is the **safety-first ratio**. Minimizing the shortfall probability means maximizing $SFR$. It looks like the Sharpe ratio with the threshold in place of the risk-free rate. It matches the single-goal logic of a safety layer and relies on normality; real returns have fat tails, so the formula understates tail risk.

### Costs of mistakes: time-weighted and money-weighted returns

A fund's **time-weighted return (TWR)** ignores the timing of an investor's deposits and withdrawals; the **money-weighted return** is the investor's internal rate of return (IRR) on her actual cash flows. If investors add money after strong returns and withdraw after weak ones, their money-weighted return is below the fund's time-weighted return: a **behavior gap**. Studies of mutual fund investors typically find money-weighted returns below time-weighted returns, though the size of the gap depends on the methodology, the funds and the period, and some researchers find it much smaller than industry reports suggest.

### Behavioral biases and the adviser

Advisers separate **cognitive biases** (errors in how information is processed, such as anchoring, base-rate neglect and availability) from **emotional biases** (feelings that shape choices, such as loss aversion, regret and overconfidence). Cognitive biases are often reduced by better information and education, so the adviser **moderates** the bias. Emotional biases are more deeply held, and education often fails, so the adviser may **adapt** the plan to them (for example, a lower-risk portfolio for an investor who would panic at a loss) rather than try to remove them. Advisers have duties here: a registered investment adviser owes a fiduciary duty, broker-dealers follow Regulation Best Interest, and under the CFA Institute's Standards, suitability (Standard III(C)) requires understanding the client's needs and circumstances, and communicating clearly (Standard V(B)). Advisers have biases of their own. This is general education and not a recommendation of any investment.

@example
title: Choosing with safety-first
problem: An investor must not fall below a 4% return. Portfolio A has mean 6% and standard deviation 8%, Portfolio B has mean 9% and standard deviation 15%, and Portfolio C has mean 7% and standard deviation 10%. Returns are normal. Which portfolio minimizes the probability of falling short?
step: Safety-first ratios: $A = (6-4)/8 = 0.250$, $B = (9-4)/15 = 0.333$, $C = (7-4)/10 = 0.300$.
step: Shortfall probabilities: $\Phi(-0.250) = 0.4013$, $\Phi(-0.333) = 0.3694$ and $\Phi(-0.300) = 0.3821$.
step: Portfolio B has the highest ratio and the lowest probability of falling below 4%, 36.9%, even though it is the most volatile.
step: Under a different threshold the order can change, which is why the threshold, the goal, must come from the client. Even the best of these portfolios falls short of 4% more than a third of the time under these assumptions.
answer: Portfolio B (SFR 0.333, shortfall probability 36.9%).

@example
title: The behavior gap
problem: A fund returns +30% in year 1 and -20% in year 2. An investor puts in \$1,000 at the start and adds \$9,000 at the end of year 1, after the strong year. Compare the fund's annualized time-weighted return with the investor's money-weighted return.
step: Time-weighted: $\sqrt{1.30 \times 0.80} - 1 = \sqrt{1.04} - 1 = 1.98\%$ a year.
step: Cash flows: $-1{,}000$ at time 0, $-9{,}000$ at time 1, and a final value at time 2 of $(1{,}000 \times 1.30 + 9{,}000) \times 0.80 = \$8{,}240$.
step: The IRR solves $1{,}000(1+r)^2 + 9{,}000(1+r) = 8{,}240$: with $x = 1 + r$, $x = [-9{,}000 + \sqrt{9{,}000^2 + 4(1{,}000)(8{,}240)}]/2{,}000 = 0.8376$, so $r = -16.24\%$.
step: The fund earned about 2% a year while the investor lost 16% a year: she held little in the good year and a lot in the bad one. This is an extreme, constructed case: real gaps are far smaller.
answer: Time-weighted return 1.98% a year; money-weighted return $-16.24\%$ a year.

@mistakes
- Using the safety-first ratio with the risk-free rate as the threshold, or minimizing the ratio instead of maximizing it.
- Reading $\Phi(-SFR)$ as a probability of loss: it is the probability of falling below the threshold $H$, which may be positive or negative.
- Treating the BPT investor as irrational because of layers: the layers can reflect real goals, and the adviser must check them before changing them.
- Comparing the fund's time-weighted return with the investor's experience, or using an arithmetic average of the yearly returns instead of the geometric one.

model:
```python
from statistics import NormalDist


def best_safety_first(portfolios, threshold):
    """Return (name, P(R < threshold)) for the best safety-first portfolio."""
    shortfalls = {
        name: NormalDist(mean, std).cdf(threshold)
        for name, (mean, std) in portfolios.items()
    }
    best = min(shortfalls, key=shortfalls.get)
    return best, shortfalls[best]
```
hint: The shortfall probability of a portfolio is `NormalDist(mean, std).cdf(threshold)`.
hint: Compute it for every portfolio and pick the minimum with `min(..., key=...)`.
solution: Compute each portfolio's shortfall probability from the normal cumulative distribution at the threshold, and return the name with the smallest value. The second test uses a higher threshold, so the ranking changes: the threshold is part of the investor's goal, not a technical detail.
