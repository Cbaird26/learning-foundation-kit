@article
Corporate finance usually assumes rational managers and rational investors. Behavioral corporate finance relaxes one of the two assumptions at a time, and the two relaxations lead to different predictions.

### Irrational investors, rational managers

If market prices can be wrong and managers know it, managers can act to benefit existing shareholders.

- **Market timing.** Issue equity when the market overvalues it and repurchase when it undervalues it. Evidence: equity issuance rises after high stock returns and valuations, and firms that issue equity or go public have, on average, earned lower long-run returns than comparable firms. Measuring long-run abnormal returns is statistically difficult and the results depend on the benchmark, so this evidence is debated. Some researchers also argue that a firm's capital structure reflects the cumulative history of its market timing.
- **Catering.** Managers give investors what they currently pay a premium for. For example, firms are more likely to initiate dividends when dividend-paying stocks trade at a premium.
- **Mergers financed with stock.** A firm whose shares are overvalued can use them as currency to buy real assets. In such a deal, the acquirer's existing holders gain at the expense of the target's holders, who receive overvalued shares. Merger waves have coincided with high market valuations, which fits this view, though other explanations (shocks to industries, financing conditions) also fit.

### Rational investors, irrational managers

Here the market is efficient, but managers' judgments are biased, so they make poor investment and financing choices.

- **Overconfidence and the hubris hypothesis.** A manager who overestimates her ability overestimates the synergies from a takeover, and so pays too much. The bidder's gain is
$$\text{Acquirer NPV} = \text{Synergies} - \text{Premium}, \qquad \text{Premium} = \text{Price paid} - \text{Target's standalone market value}.$$
A deal can destroy value for acquirer shareholders and still be completed, because the manager believes the synergies are larger than they are. Evidence: on average, acquirer shareholders earn announcement returns near zero or slightly negative in deals for public targets, while target shareholders earn large premiums (often 20 to 40%) and combined value changes are positive on average; stock-financed deals do worse for acquirers than cash deals. Studies have also found that executives who hold stock options long after they are deep in the money (a proxy for overconfidence) make more acquisitions and do worse on them, and that their investment is more sensitive to internal cash flow. Proxies are debated, and agency problems such as empire building also predict overpaying, so evidence does not cleanly separate overconfidence from other motives.
- **Optimism and overinvestment.** Managers who think their projects are better than they are invest too much, especially when they have internal cash.

### Debiasing managers' decisions

Processes can reduce errors. Ask for the **outside view**: the base rate of synergies realized by similar deals, instead of the story of this deal. Set a **walk-away price** before negotiation. Hold a **pre-mortem**: assume the deal failed in three years and write down why. Have an independent team estimate synergies, and align compensation with long-term results so the executive does not gain from the announcement alone.

### Stock-financed mergers with misvalued shares

Suppose the acquirer has $N_A$ shares with market price $P_A$ but true value per share $V_A$, and the target has $N_T$ shares priced and truly valued at $P_T$. The acquirer offers $x$ of its shares per target share, issuing $xN_T$ new shares. With no synergies, the true value per share of the combined firm is

$$V = \frac{N_A V_A + N_T P_T}{N_A + x N_T},$$

and the gain to the acquirer's old shareholders is $N_A V - N_A V_A$. The target's holders lose the same amount. If $V_A < P_A$ the stock is overvalued and the acquirer's old holders gain, provided the premium is not so large that $xV_A > P_T$; if the acquirer's stock were undervalued, they would lose.

@example
title: The premium and the NPV of a deal
problem: Hartwell Corp offers \$1,120 million in cash for Larkfield Inc, whose standalone market value is \$800 million. Hartwell's CEO says the deal creates \$400 million of synergies, but a careful study suggests realistic synergies of \$100 million. Find the premium and the NPV to Hartwell's shareholders under each estimate.
step: The premium is $1{,}120 - 800 = \$320$ million, or 40% of the target's market value.
step: With the CEO's \$400 million of synergies, the NPV is $400 - 320 = +\$80$ million: the deal looks attractive.
step: With the \$100 million realistic estimate, the NPV is $100 - 320 = -\$220$ million.
step: The deal breaks even only if synergies are at least \$320 million, which is $320/100 = 3.2$ times the realistic estimate. A manager who overestimates her ability can sincerely believe the first number and approve a deal that destroys value.
answer: Premium \$320 million; NPV $+\$80$ million (CEO) against $-\$220$ million (realistic).

@example
title: Paying with overvalued stock
problem: An acquirer has 100 million shares priced at \$60 but worth \$45 each. It buys a target with 20 million shares priced and truly valued at \$30 by offering 0.6 acquirer shares per target share. There are no synergies. Who gains and who loses, and by how much?
step: The acquirer issues $0.6 \times 20 = 12$ million shares, so there are 112 million shares afterward. The offer is worth $0.6 \times 60 = \$36$ per target share at market prices, a 20% premium.
step: The true value of the combined firm is $100 \times 45 + 20 \times 30 = 4{,}500 + 600 = \$5{,}100$ million, or $5{,}100/112 = \$45.54$ per share.
step: The acquirer's old holders hold 100 million shares worth $100 \times 45.54 = \$4{,}553.6$ million against \$4,500 million before: a gain of \$53.6 million.
step: Target holders receive 12 million shares worth $12 \times 45.54 = \$546.4$ million for a business worth \$600 million: a loss of \$53.6 million. The merger transfers value without creating any, and it looks like a 20% premium to the target's holders.
answer: The acquirer's holders gain \$53.6 million; the target's holders lose \$53.6 million.

@mistakes
- Measuring the premium against the acquirer's price or the offer price instead of the target's standalone market value.
- Counting the premium as value created: it is a transfer to target holders, and the acquirer gains only if synergies exceed it.
- Assuming that a stock-financed offer has a premium equal to the quoted one: its value depends on what the acquirer's shares are truly worth.
- Treating the overconfidence and agency explanations of overpaying as the same: they have different remedies (debiasing processes against better incentives and governance).

@practice self
prompt: Hawthorne Industries (market value \$6,000 million) offers \$1,450 million in cash for Linden Labs (standalone market value \$1,000 million). Its CEO claims \$700 million of synergies. In three earlier acquisitions Hawthorne announced synergies of \$500, \$400 and \$600 million and realized \$120, \$50 and \$200 million. The CEO has exercised stock options only at expiration in each of the last five years. Analyze the deal: compute the premium, the NPV under the CEO's claim and under the company's track record, name the behavioral concern, and propose two steps to reduce the risk of error.
hint: Scale the CEO's claim by the ratio of realized to announced synergies in the earlier deals.
solution: The premium is $1{,}450 - 1{,}000 = \$450$ million. With the CEO's claim, the NPV is $700 - 450 = +\$250$ million. The track record says that $370/1{,}500 = 24.7\%$ of announced synergies were realized, so a realistic estimate is $0.247 \times 700 = \$173$ million and the NPV is $173 - 450 = -\$277$ million. The pattern (synergies repeatedly overstated, and options held to expiration, a commonly used proxy for overconfidence) is consistent with managerial overconfidence and the hubris hypothesis, though agency motives such as empire building cannot be ruled out from these facts. Two steps: use the outside view, with an independent team estimating synergies from the base rate of past deals, and set a walk-away price in advance (here, an offer above about \$1,173 million has a negative NPV even at the track-record synergies). A pre-mortem and compensation tied to realized synergies are also good answers.
rubric: Computes the premium (\$450 million) and both NPVs (about +\$250 million and about -\$277 million).
rubric: Identifies overconfidence or hubris, citing the track record or the option-holding proxy, and notes that other motives are possible.
rubric: Proposes two concrete debiasing steps, such as the outside view, an independent estimate, a walk-away price, a pre-mortem or aligned compensation.
