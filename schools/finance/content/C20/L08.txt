@article
An **anomaly** is a pattern in average returns that a standard asset pricing model (such as the CAPM) cannot explain. Anomalies are interesting to behavioral finance because some patterns look like what mistaken beliefs and limits to arbitrage would produce. They are also interesting to skeptics, because a pattern can come from risk the model leaves out, from chance, or from data mining.

### Momentum and reversal

**Momentum** is the tendency of stocks that have performed well over the past 3 to 12 months (winners) to keep outperforming those that have performed badly (losers) over the next 3 to 12 months. The standard signal is the cumulative return from month $t-12$ to month $t-2$, skipping the most recent month because of short-run reversal:

$$\text{MOM}_{12\text{-}1} = \prod_{s=t-12}^{t-2}(1 + r_s) - 1.$$

A long-short portfolio that buys the top decile and shorts the bottom decile has earned positive average returns in US data over many decades and in many other markets, on the order of a percent a month in much historical data, though it is volatile, has suffered severe crashes (notably when markets rebounded after a decline), and is smaller after trading costs. **Long-run reversal** is the opposite pattern over 3 to 5 years: past long-term losers tend to outperform past winners.

### Value and growth

The **value premium** is the finding that stocks with high ratios of book value to market value (cheap stocks) have, on average, earned more than low book-to-market (growth) stocks in historical US data. It has been weaker in some recent periods, and its size depends on how value is measured.

### Explanations: behavioral and risk-based

| Model | Mechanism | Predicts |
|---|---|---|
| Conservatism and representativeness (BSV) | Investors under-react to individual news, then over-extrapolate after streaks | Momentum, then reversal |
| Overconfidence and self-attribution (DHS) | Overconfident investors overreact to private information and confirming news | Momentum, then reversal |
| Gradual information diffusion (Hong–Stein) | News reaches investors slowly; momentum traders then overshoot | Momentum, then reversal |
| Extrapolation of growth | Investors overpay for past growth and underpay for past losers | Value premium |

Risk-based alternatives argue that value stocks are riskier (financial distress, bad times when wealth matters most) and that momentum profits compensate for risk that varies over time. Evidence is mixed: the value and momentum premiums are not fully explained by standard risk factors, but risk-based models can fit them with additional factors, and transaction costs, short-sale constraints and data-snooping may account for some of the apparent gains. Neither side has won.

### Bubbles

A **bubble** is a sustained rise in prices that departs widely from fundamental value and then collapses. Defining fundamental value is the problem: for a stock with an expected next-year dividend $D_1$, required return $r$ and constant growth $g$, the Gordon growth value is

$$P = \frac{D_1}{r - g},$$

with $g < r$. A price of 150 for a stock with $D_1 = 3$ and $r = 9\%$ implies $g = r - D_1/P = 7\%$ forever, almost the discount rate, which is a high bar.

The **dot-com bubble** is a documented episode. In the late 1990s, prices of internet and technology stocks rose rapidly. Many newly listed companies had large first-day price rises, and valuations rested on traffic and user growth rather than on earnings. The Nasdaq Composite peaked in March 2000 at about 5,000, roughly double its level a year before, and by October 2002 had fallen about 78%. Behavioral ideas applied to the episode include extrapolation of past returns, overconfidence, herding (week 9) and limits to arbitrage: shorting richly priced technology stocks was risky and costly, and studies of institutional filings suggest that many hedge funds held technology stocks through the run-up and reduced their positions before the crash. Controlled laboratory markets, in which the fundamental value of an asset is known to every participant, also frequently produce prices that rise above fundamental value and then crash, although experience reduces the effect.

Not everyone agrees on what a bubble is. Some researchers argue that large price rises can follow from rational learning about uncertain new technology, and that it is hard to know in advance that prices are too high. Others point to the size of the later fall as evidence of mispricing. The fair summary is that large boom-and-bust episodes are factual, while their causes are debated.

@example
title: A 12-1 momentum signal
problem: A stock had these monthly returns from month $t-12$ to $t-2$ (11 months): 4%, 2%, -1%, 5%, 3%, -2%, 1%, 6%, 2%, -1%, 3%. Compute its 12-1 momentum signal. If the return in month $t-1$ was +9%, should it be included?
step: Convert each return into a growth factor: 1.04, 1.02, 0.99, 1.05, 1.03, 0.98, 1.01, 1.06, 1.02, 0.99, 1.03.
step: Multiply: $1.04 \times 1.02 \times 0.99 \times 1.05 \times 1.03 \times 0.98 \times 1.01 \times 1.06 \times 1.02 \times 0.99 \times 1.03 = 1.2394$.
step: The momentum signal is $1.2394 - 1 = 23.94\%$.
step: The +9% return of month $t-1$ is skipped: the most recent month tends to reverse in the short run, so the standard signal leaves it out.
answer: The signal is 23.94%; the most recent month is not included.

@example
title: What a price implies, and how far a bubble falls
problem: (a) A stock with $D_1 = \$3$ and $r = 9\%$ has a price of \$150. What constant growth rate does the price imply, and what would the price be if growth were 4%? (b) After a 78% fall, how large a gain is needed to return to the starting level?
step: (a) $g = r - D_1/P = 0.09 - 3/150 = 0.07$: a 7% growth rate forever. With $g = 4\%$, $P = 3/(0.09 - 0.04) = \$60$.
step: The price of \$150 needs growth of 7% a year in perpetuity, only two points below the required return; the Gordon price is extremely sensitive to $g$ when $g$ is close to $r$.
step: (b) After a fall of 78% the value is $0.22$ of the start, so the required gain is $1/0.22 - 1 = 3.545$, or 354.5%.
step: Losses are asymmetric: the larger the fall, the more disproportionate the gain required to recover.
answer: (a) 7% growth; \$60 at 4% growth. (b) A gain of about 354.5%.

@mistakes
- Including the most recent month in the 12-1 signal, or using only 12 months of returns without skipping a month.
- Treating a documented anomaly as a certain trading profit: costs, crashes and sample periods matter.
- Calling any large price rise a bubble: a bubble claim needs an estimate of fundamental value, which is uncertain.
- Treating risk-based and behavioral explanations as exclusive: both can contribute, and researchers disagree about the mix.

model:
```python
def momentum_rank(monthly_returns):
    """Rank stocks by 12-1 momentum, highest first; ties by name."""
    def signal(returns):
        growth = 1.0
        for r in returns[:11]:
            growth *= 1 + r
        return growth - 1

    signals = {name: signal(r) for name, r in monthly_returns.items()}
    return sorted(signals, key=lambda name: (-signals[name], name))
```
hint: The signal uses `returns[:11]`, which leaves out the last month.
hint: Multiply `1 + r` over those months, then sort by the negative signal and then by name.
solution: Compound the first eleven monthly returns for each stock, subtract 1, and sort. Sorting with the key `(-signal, name)` puts the highest signal first and breaks ties alphabetically. A solution that includes the twelfth month ranks B above A in the first test, which is the typical mistake.
