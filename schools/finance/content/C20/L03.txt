@article
A screening model flags a small-cap stock as a likely "multibagger", and the stock does look like past winners: fast sales growth, a charismatic story. Surely it will win? The question is not how much the stock resembles winners, but how many flagged stocks actually win. People answer the first question and call it the second. That substitution is the core of a **heuristic**: a rule of thumb that replaces a hard judgment with an easier one. Heuristics are fast and often accurate; they produce **biases** when the environment punishes the shortcut.

### Representativeness

**Representativeness** judges the probability that an item belongs to a category by how closely it resembles the category's typical member. Three standard consequences:

- **Base-rate neglect.** Judging by resemblance ignores how common the category is. The correct benchmark is Bayes' rule:
$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \text{not } H)\,\bigl(1 - P(H)\bigr)},$$
where $H$ is the hypothesis, $E$ the evidence, and $P(H)$ the **base rate** (prior).
- **The conjunction fallacy.** A detailed story can look more representative while being less probable. Since $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$, "she is a bank analyst who covers energy stocks" can never be more probable than "she is a bank analyst". Evidence for the fallacy is strong in vignette studies, though how often subjects commit it depends on wording; frequency formats ("how many of 100 people?") reduce it.
- **Belief in the law of small numbers.** People expect short sequences to look random, so they see streaks as unlikely. In fair, independent trials, streaks are common: the probability that 20 independent flips of a fair coin contain a run of at least 5 heads is about 25%. The **gambler's fallacy** is the belief that after a streak the opposite outcome is "due"; the **hot-hand belief** is the opposite belief that a streak will continue. Whether athletes truly have hot hands is debated: early studies found none, but a 2018 reanalysis found a statistical bias in those tests and estimated modest effects. In markets, investors who extrapolate recent returns are treating a streak as informative.

### Availability

**Availability** judges frequency or probability by how easily examples come to mind. Vivid, recent, emotional or heavily reported events are overestimated: after a market crash, crash probabilities seem high; after a long calm, they seem low. Investors also favor stocks that attract attention (large moves, news coverage) because those are available, a point that becomes important for trading in week 9.

### Anchoring

**Anchoring** is estimating a quantity by starting from an initial value (the **anchor**) and adjusting, usually insufficiently. A simple model: with anchor $A$ and true value $T$,

$$\text{estimate} = A + \theta\,(T - A), \qquad 0 \le \theta < 1,$$

where $\theta$ is the fraction of the gap the person closes; $\theta = 1$ is the unbiased case. Anchors can be irrelevant (a random number seen earlier), which is how experiments show the effect. In markets, candidate anchors include a purchase price, an IPO price, last year's earnings, round numbers and the 52-week high; stocks priced near their 52-week high have in some studies earned higher subsequent returns, consistent with investors under-reacting to good news when it would push prices through the anchor.

### What the evidence does and does not say

These effects are replicated in laboratory and survey studies, and some have been found in professionals. They are not proof that every market price is wrong, and heuristics can also be efficient responses to limited time and information. The practical lesson is to compare the intuitive answer with a computed benchmark whenever one exists.

@example
title: Base rates and a stock screen
problem: Among small-cap stocks in an invented universe, 4% will double within three years. A screen flags 75% of the eventual doublers and also flags 15% of the stocks that will not double. A stock is flagged. What is the probability that it doubles?
step: Set $H$ = "doubles" with $P(H) = 0.04$; $P(\text{flag} \mid H) = 0.75$ and $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{not } H) = 0.15$.
step: Numerator: $0.75 \times 0.04 = 0.030$. The flagged non-doublers: $0.15 \times 0.96 = 0.144$.
step: Posterior: $P(H \mid \text{flag}) = 0.030/(0.030 + 0.144) = 0.1724$.
step: So only about 17% of flagged stocks double, not the 75% suggested by "the screen catches most winners". The low base rate means most flagged stocks are false positives.
answer: 17.24%

@example
title: Insufficient adjustment from an anchor
problem: A stock debuted at an IPO price of \$50. An analyst's careful valuation puts its fair value at \$80, but a fund manager who is anchored on the IPO price estimates the value after adjusting only 60% of the way. What is the estimate, and what is the percentage error relative to fair value?
step: The gap is $T - A = 80 - 50 = 30$.
step: The estimate is $A + \theta(T - A) = 50 + 0.6 \times 30 = \$68$.
step: The error is $68 - 80 = -\$12$, which is $12/80 = 15\%$ below fair value.
step: With $\theta = 0.6$ the manager closes only 60% of the gap, so the estimate stays below \$80 even though the information points there; under-reaction to information and anchoring look alike in prices.
answer: Estimate \$68, 15% below the fair value of \$80.

@mistakes
- Answering "how well does the evidence match the hypothesis?" instead of "how probable is the hypothesis, given the base rate?"
- Concluding that a more detailed scenario is more likely: adding a condition can only keep a probability the same or lower it.
- Believing a fair, independent process must alternate: a streak does not change the next probability (the gambler's fallacy), and its absence in short samples is not evidence of a hot hand.
- Reading the anchoring formula backward: $\theta$ is the share of the gap that is closed, so a larger $\theta$ means a smaller bias.

@practice num
prompt: In an invented sample, 3% of firms will restate their earnings. A red-flag model flags 80% of the firms that restate and 10% of those that do not. A firm is flagged. What is the probability it will restate? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 19.83
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 0.03 * 0.80 / (0.03 * 0.80 + 0.97 * 0.10) * 100
hint: The flagged restaters are $0.03 \times 0.80$ of all firms; the flagged non-restaters are $0.97 \times 0.10$.
hint: Divide the flagged restaters by all flagged firms.
solution: $0.024/(0.024 + 0.097) = 0.1983$, about 19.83%: most flagged firms do not restate.

@practice num
prompt: An active fund beats its benchmark in any month with probability 0.5, independently of other months. It has just beaten the benchmark six months in a row. What is the probability, as a percent, that it beats the benchmark in each of the next four months?
answer: 6.25
unit: %
check: 0.5 ** 4 * 100
hint: Independence means the past six months do not change the probability of any future month.
solution: $0.5^4 = 0.0625$, or 6.25%. The six-month streak is irrelevant to the next four months; the gambler's fallacy and the hot-hand belief both treat it as relevant.

@practice mc
prompt: Dara is 34, studied environmental science and follows renewable-energy companies as an equity analyst. Which statement is more probable?
choice*: Dara works at an asset management firm.
choice: Dara works at an asset management firm and covers energy stocks.
choice: The two statements are equally probable because Dara matches both.
choice: The second statement is more probable because it is more representative of her background.
hint: Ask whether the second statement describes a subset of the first.
solution: Anyone who works at an asset management firm and covers energy stocks also works at an asset management firm, so $P(A \cap B) \le P(A)$. The second statement can be more representative of Dara's profile and still be less probable; choosing it is the conjunction fallacy.

@practice num
prompt: An analyst is shown an irrelevant starting value of \$45 for a company whose true fair value is \$75. Her final estimate is \$58. Using $\text{estimate} = A + \theta(T - A)$, what fraction $\theta$ of the gap did she close? Give the answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.43
tol: 0.01
check: (58 - 45) / (75 - 45)
hint: The gap is $T - A$ and she moved $58 - 45$ of it.
solution: $\theta = (58 - 45)/(75 - 45) = 13/30 = 0.43$. She adjusted less than half of the way, so her estimate is still strongly pulled toward the anchor.

@practice num
prompt: A fair coin is flipped 10 times. What is the probability, as a percent to two decimal places, that the sequence contains a run of at least four heads in a row?
answer: 24.51
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: sum('1111' in format(i, '010b') for i in range(1024)) / 1024 * 100
hint: There are $2^{10} = 1024$ equally likely sequences. Count those that contain four consecutive heads.
hint: Count sequences by where the run of four first appears, or build a recursion on the current run length. The count is 251.
solution: Of the 1024 sequences, 251 contain four consecutive heads, so the probability is $251/1024 = 24.51\%$. Streaks that look "too orderly" to be random are common in short random sequences.

prompt: In an invented market, 6% of stocks will fall more than 50% in the next year. A distress model flags 90% of the stocks that will fall and 20% of those that will not. A stock is flagged. What is the probability, as a percent to two decimal places, that it falls more than 50%?
answer: 22.31

solution: $0.054/(0.054 + 0.188) = 0.2231$, or 22.31%. Even a model that catches 90% of the disasters has most of its flags on stocks that do not fall.
