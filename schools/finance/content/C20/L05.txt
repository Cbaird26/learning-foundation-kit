@article
You hold \$3,000 in a savings account earning 1.5%, "my vacation money", while you carry a \$3,000 credit card balance at 22% interest. Money is **fungible**: a dollar in one place buys the same as a dollar in another, so paying down the card would raise your wealth. Yet many people keep the accounts apart. **Mental accounting** is the set of cognitive operations people use to organize, evaluate and keep track of financial activities. It is a descriptive model of how people actually manage money, and it contradicts the standard-finance assumption that wealth is one pool.

### Three parts of mental accounting

- **Which outcomes are grouped.** People label money as "salary", "windfall", "retirement" or "play money" and spend each differently. The **house money effect** is greater risk taking after a gain, as if the gain were not yet one's own.
- **How outcomes are valued.** Gains and losses are evaluated with a value function (week 2). **Hedonic editing** is the hypothesis that people combine or separate outcomes to make themselves feel best: with a concave gain branch and convex loss branch, it is better to **segregate gains** ($v(a) + v(b) > v(a+b)$) and **integrate losses** ($v(-a) + v(-b) < v(-a-b)$, so one combined loss hurts less than two separate ones). For a small gain with a larger loss, **netting** the two usually beats separating them. The evidence for hedonic editing is mixed, and people do not always edit in the predicted way.
- **How often accounts are evaluated.** Checking performance often exposes investors to more frequent losses (see below). A related error is the **sunk cost** fallacy: letting money already spent and unrecoverable influence a decision that should depend only on future costs and benefits.

### Narrow framing and myopic loss aversion

**Narrow framing** is evaluating each decision separately rather than as part of a total portfolio. Take a gamble that wins \$200 or loses \$100 with probability 0.5 each, and assume a simple loss-averse evaluation: gains count at face value and losses are multiplied by $\lambda = 2.25$. A single play has value $0.5(200) - 0.5(2.25)(100) = -12.5$: rejected. Two plays evaluated together have outcomes $+400$ (probability 0.25), $+100$ (0.5) and $-200$ (0.25), so $V = 0.25(400) + 0.5(100) - 2.25(0.25)(200) = 37.5$: accepted. Loss aversion plus a short evaluation period make a favorable gamble look bad. **Myopic loss aversion** is the combination of loss aversion with frequent evaluation. In experiments, subjects who see results less often take more risk. It has also been proposed as an explanation of the equity premium puzzle: investors who check their portfolios about once a year and weigh losses about twice as heavily as gains would demand a large premium to hold stocks. As an explanation of the premium, it remains debated.

### Framing

The **framing effect** is a change in choice caused by a logically equivalent change in the description of the options. In the classic experiments, people chose the sure option when outcomes were described as lives saved and the gamble when the same outcomes were described as lives lost. The same logic applies to a plan described as "saves 200 of 600 jobs" or as "loses 400 of 600 jobs". Standard theory requires **description invariance**: equivalent descriptions should give the same choice. Framing effects have been found in many studies, though their size varies by context and population, and some replications find smaller effects than the classic experiments.

### Dividends and homemade dividends

A stock priced at \$100 pays a \$4 dividend, so its price falls to about \$96 when it goes ex-dividend. An investor with 100 shares holds \$400 in cash and \$9,600 in stock. Without the dividend, she could sell 4 shares at \$100 to raise \$400 and hold 96 shares worth \$9,600: the same wealth. Such a **homemade dividend** shows that, ignoring taxes and transaction costs, the form of the payout does not matter. Yet many investors treat dividends as spendable "income" and shares as untouchable "capital". Mental accounting is one explanation of this preference; taxes, signaling and investor clienteles are others, and researchers still debate their relative importance.

@example
title: Evaluating a gamble one play at a time or in a bundle
problem: A gamble wins \$200 or loses \$100 with probability 0.5 each. An investor evaluates outcomes with a simple loss-averse rule: gains at face value, losses multiplied by $\lambda = 2.25$. Find the value of five plays evaluated together, compared with one play.
step: Let $k$ be the number of wins in five plays, with probability $\binom{5}{k}(0.5)^5$. The net outcome is $200k - 100(5-k)$.
step: The outcomes for $k = 0, 1, \ldots, 5$ are $-500, -200, +100, +400, +700, +1{,}000$, with probabilities $1/32, 5/32, 10/32, 10/32, 5/32, 1/32$.
step: Losses (weighted by $\lambda$): $2.25\,[(1/32)(500) + (5/32)(200)] = 2.25 \times 46.875 = 105.47$. Gains: $(10/32)(100) + (10/32)(400) + (5/32)(700) + (1/32)(1000) = 31.25 + 125 + 109.375 + 31.25 = 296.875$.
step: $V_5 = 296.875 - 105.469 = 191.41 > 0$, accepted, compared with $V_1 = -12.5$ for a single play. The same person rejects one play and accepts five evaluated together.
answer: $V_5 = 191.41$ against $V_1 = -12.5$.

@example
title: Segregating gains and integrating losses
problem: With $\alpha = \beta = 0.88$ and $\lambda = 2.25$, would the person prefer to receive two gains of \$100 and \$50 separately or as one \$150 gain, and two losses of \$100 and \$50 separately or as one \$150 loss?
step: Gains: $v(100) + v(50) = 57.54 + 31.27 = 88.81$ against $v(150) = 82.22$. Because the value function is concave, two separate gains give more total value.
step: Losses: $v(-100) + v(-50) = -129.47 - 70.36 = -199.83$ against $v(-150) = -184.99$. Because the loss branch is convex, one combined loss hurts less.
step: So the hedonic editing hypothesis predicts that people prefer to segregate gains (two small gifts rather than one) and integrate losses (one bill rather than two).
answer: Gains separate (88.81 against 82.22); losses combined ($-184.99$ against $-199.83$).

@mistakes
- Calling any two separate budgets "mental accounting": the fallacy is letting the label change a decision when the dollars are fungible.
- Counting sunk costs as a reason to continue: only future costs and benefits should matter.
- Evaluating the repeated gamble's value by multiplying the single-play value by the number of plays; with loss aversion on the total, the value is not proportional.
- Treating framing as just a wording issue: the point is that equivalent descriptions of the same outcomes change the choice.

solution: Gains: $0.25(480) + 0.5(140) = 190$. Losses: $0.25(200) = 50$, multiplied by 2.5 gives 125. $V = 190 - 125 = 65$. A single play has value $120 - 125 = -5$, so bundling flips the decision.

@quiz num
prompt: A gamble wins \$150 or loses \$100 with probability 0.5 each. An investor counts gains at face value and multiplies losses by $\lambda = 2$. What is the value of three independent plays evaluated together?
answer: 18.75
tol: 0.01
check: sum(comb(3, k) / 8 * (150 * k - 100 * (3 - k) if 150 * k - 100 * (3 - k) >= 0 else 2 * (150 * k - 100 * (3 - k))) for k in range(4))
hint: With $k$ wins in three plays, the net outcome is $150k - 100(3-k)$ and has probability $\binom{3}{k}/8$.
solution: With $k$ wins the net outcome is $150k - 100(3-k)$: $-300$ for $k=0$, $-50$ for $k=1$, $+200$ for $k=2$ and $+450$ for $k=3$, with probabilities $1/8, 3/8, 3/8, 1/8$. Gains: $(3/8)(200) + (1/8)(450) = 131.25$. Losses: $2[(1/8)(300) + (3/8)(50)] = 112.5$. $V = 131.25 - 112.5 = 18.75$.
