@article
Suppose you are offered a coin flip: win \$40,000 or lose \$20,000. The expected gain is \$10,000, yet many people with savings of \$100,000 decline. Is that irrational? Standard finance says no: a sensible person weighs gains and losses by how much they add to well-being, not by dollars. Behavioral finance asks a different question: when do real choices depart from that benchmark, in what direction, and with what consequences for prices?

### Expected utility

A **lottery** assigns probabilities $p_i$ to outcomes (here, final wealth levels $W_i$). An **expected utility** maximizer ranks lotteries by

$$EU = \sum_i p_i\, u(W_i),$$

where $u$ is a **utility function** over wealth. If $u$ is increasing and **concave** ($u'' < 0$), the investor is **risk averse**: extra wealth helps less the more she has. The **certainty equivalent** $CE$ is the sure wealth that gives the same utility as the lottery, $u(CE) = EU$, and the **risk premium** is the expected wealth minus the certainty equivalent, $E[W] - CE$. A risk-averse person has $CE < E[W]$. Two common forms are logarithmic utility, $u(W) = \ln W$, and power (constant relative risk aversion) utility, $u(W) = W^{\alpha}$ with $0 < \alpha < 1$.

### The axioms

Expected utility is the right representation of preferences exactly when they satisfy four axioms:

- **Completeness**: any two lotteries can be ranked (or are equally good).
- **Transitivity**: if $A \succ B$ and $B \succ C$, then $A \succ C$.
- **Continuity**: if $A \succ B \succ C$, some mixture of $A$ and $C$ is exactly as good as $B$.
- **Independence**: if $A \succ B$, then for any lottery $C$ and any $q \in (0,1)$, mixing both with $C$ preserves the ranking: $qA + (1-q)C \succ qB + (1-q)C$.

Together with Bayesian updating of beliefs (the **rational expectations** assumption in market models), these define **standard finance** behavior. They are **normative** in two senses: many people accept them as how one should choose, and violating them can expose you to losses. Whether people actually follow them is a **descriptive** question.

### Where choices depart

The **Allais paradox** tests independence. In many experiments a large share of subjects choose $A$ over $B$ and also $D$ over $C$ in the lotteries below (amounts in millions of dollars). The share varies with the payoffs and the way the problem is posed, but the pattern is well replicated.

| Lottery | Outcomes |
|---|---|
| $A$ | 1 for sure |
| $B$ | 5 with probability 0.10; 1 with 0.89; 0 with 0.01 |
| $C$ | 1 with probability 0.11; 0 with 0.89 |
| $D$ | 5 with probability 0.10; 0 with 0.90 |

The **Ellsberg paradox** shows a similar tension for ambiguity: many people prefer bets with known odds to bets with unknown odds, which no single set of probability beliefs can justify under expected utility.

### Two pillars

Critics of behavioral finance reply, with Friedman's "as if" argument, that markets only need the marginal trader to be rational, and that rational traders will arbitrage away the mistakes of others. Behavioral finance answers with two pillars. The first is **psychology**: systematic, predictable departures from the standard model (the rest of this course). The second is **limits to arbitrage** (week 7): arbitrage is risky and costly, so mispricing can persist. Both pillars are needed. Biased individuals matter for prices only if arbitrage cannot offset them, and a limit to arbitrage matters only if someone creates the mispricing.

Be careful about what is evidence. Experiments, brokerage records and surveys document departures from the axioms; they do not by themselves show that markets are inefficient, and the debate over how much prices are affected remains open.

@example
title: Certainty equivalent under log utility
problem: Your wealth is \$100,000 and you have logarithmic utility $u(W) = \ln W$. You are offered a fair-looking flip: win \$40,000 with probability 0.5 or lose \$20,000 with probability 0.5. Find the certainty equivalent of your wealth after the flip and the risk premium. Would you accept?
step: Final wealth is \$140,000 or \$80,000, so $E[W] = 0.5(140{,}000) + 0.5(80{,}000) = \$110{,}000$.
step: Expected utility: $EU = 0.5\ln(140{,}000) + 0.5\ln(80{,}000) = 0.5(11.8494) + 0.5(11.2898) = 11.5696$.
step: Invert the utility: $CE = e^{11.5696} = \$105{,}830$.
step: The risk premium is $110{,}000 - 105{,}830 = \$4{,}170$. Since $CE = \$105{,}830 > \$100{,}000$, this investor accepts: the flip is worth about \$5,830 to her, even though a smaller stake of wealth would make her decline.
answer: CE of about \$105,830 and a risk premium of about \$4,170; she accepts.

@example
title: Why A with D breaks independence
problem: In the Allais lotteries above, show that an expected utility maximizer who prefers $A$ to $B$ must also prefer $C$ to $D$, and find how concave a power utility $u(x) = x^{\alpha}$ would have to be to prefer $A$ to $B$.
step: Normalize $u(0) = 0$. Then $A \succ B$ means $u(1) > 0.89\,u(1) + 0.10\,u(5)$, which simplifies to $0.11\,u(1) > 0.10\,u(5)$.
step: $C \succ D$ means $0.11\,u(1) > 0.10\,u(5)$: the same inequality. So expected utility allows the choices $(A, C)$ or $(B, D)$, never $(A, D)$.
step: The common $0.89$ chance of 1 in $A$ and $B$ is the "common consequence" that independence says to ignore; people seem to value the certainty of $A$ more than that.
step: For $u(x) = x^{\alpha}$, preferring $A$ needs $5^{\alpha} < 1.1$, or $\alpha < \ln 1.1 / \ln 5 = 0.0592$. Utility would have to be almost flat, far more concave than typical estimates of risk aversion.
answer: Expected utility forces the pair of choices (A, C) or (B, D); with power utility, A over B needs $\alpha < 0.0592$.

@mistakes
- Maximizing expected wealth instead of expected utility, then calling every risk-averse choice irrational.
- Taking the certainty equivalent as $e^{EU}$ for a power utility, or the square of $EU$ for logarithmic utility: invert the utility function you actually have.
- Treating a documented departure from the axioms as proof that prices are wrong; behavioral finance needs both biased traders and limits to arbitrage.
- Reading "descriptive" as "irrational": a descriptive model reports how people choose, and says nothing yet about whether they should.

@practice mc
prompt: A subject chooses a sure \$3 million over a gamble (10% chance of \$7 million, 89% chance of \$3 million, 1% chance of nothing), and then chooses a 10% chance of \$7 million over an 11% chance of \$3 million. Which axiom of expected utility do these two choices violate?
choice: Completeness
choice: Transitivity
choice: Continuity
choice*: Independence
hint: Both pairs share a common 89% consequence in one version and a common 89% chance of nothing in the other.
solution: Under expected utility, the first choice implies $0.11\,u(3) > 0.10\,u(7)$ and the second implies the reverse. Replacing the common 89% consequence changes the ranking, which violates independence; each choice by itself is complete and transitive.

@practice num

@quiz self
prompt: A skeptic says: "If some investors are irrational, rational investors will trade against them and the mistakes will vanish, so behavioral finance cannot matter for prices." In four or five sentences, explain how a behavioral finance researcher would reply, using the two pillars.
hint: Ask what makes arbitrage work, and what happens if it is risky or costly.
solution: The skeptic's argument assumes arbitrage is cheap and safe. The first pillar says investors' departures from the standard model are systematic rather than random, so their errors can point the same way and push prices together. The second pillar, limits to arbitrage, says that rational traders face fundamental risk, noise-trader risk, short-sale constraints, transaction costs and the risk of investor withdrawals, so they take limited positions and mispricing can persist. Both pillars are needed: biased trading creates the pressure, and limits to arbitrage stop it from being fully offset. How large the price effects are remains an empirical question.

rubric: Names limits to arbitrage and gives at least two specific limits (for example fundamental risk, noise-trader risk, short-sale constraints, costs).
rubric: Concludes that both are needed for persistent mispricing and notes that the size of the effect is an empirical question.
