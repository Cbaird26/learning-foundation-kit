@project
title: Bias audit
overview: Study one real investment decision or market episode and audit it for behavioral biases. Identify the biases with evidence, explain the price or performance effects with the course's models, and propose a process that would reduce the errors. The result is a three-page paper.
goal: Identify at least three biases or limits to arbitrage in a real decision or episode, with specific evidence for each.
goal: Explain the price or performance effects with at least two models from the course, using numbers.
goal: Propose a decision process that reduces the errors and say how you would test that it works.
step: Choose the subject. Options: a market episode with documented prices (for example the dot-com bubble, the 1987 crash, or a stock-financed merger wave), a public company's acquisition, or a historical investor decision (a purchase, a hold-or-sell decision, an IPO). If you study a real public company, use its filings on SEC EDGAR (the proxy statement, Form 8-K, annual report and merger documents) and current data that you look up yourself; the analysis is a learning exercise, not a recommendation. If you prefer a ready-made case, use the Birchfield case in the materials below.
step: Build a fact sheet. Record dates, prices, amounts, who decided, what information was available at the time, and what happened afterward. Separate facts from your interpretations and note your sources.
step: Audit for biases. Use the checklist in the materials: for each bias you claim (for example overconfidence, anchoring, the disposition effect, mental accounting, herding), quote or cite the evidence, say what the unbiased benchmark would be (a Bayesian posterior, an NPV with realistic synergies, a PGR/PLR comparison), and note any alternative, non-behavioral explanation.
step: Quantify. Compute at least two numbers with the course's models: for example the NPV of a deal under the decision maker's synergy estimate and under a realistic one, a prospect theory value of the choice faced, a Gordon growth price implied by a market price, a momentum signal, a stub value or a cascade probability. Show formulas and inputs.
step: Link to prices. Explain which limits to arbitrage (fundamental risk, noise trader risk, costs and short-sale constraints, agency problems) allowed any mispricing to persist, or argue that none did. Say what remains debated about the episode.
step: Propose a process. Design three or four specific controls (for example an outside-view base rate, a pre-set walk-away price, a pre-mortem, an independent estimate, a default or commitment device, a decision journal) and say what each addresses, who runs it and how you would measure whether it worked.
step: Write the three-page paper (about 1,200 to 1,500 words plus a table or two) and have another person check that every claim is tied to evidence.
material: **Checklist for the audit.** For each row, write the evidence from your case or "not found".

| Behavior | Question to ask | Unbiased benchmark |

Use these facts for the audit, and answer: what is the premium, what is the NPV under the CEO's synergy estimate and under the realized-to-announced ratio, which biases are present, and which controls would you add?

## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Rational choice.** Expected utility $\sum p_i u(W_i)$; certainty equivalent and risk premium; the four axioms; Allais and Ellsberg; behavioral finance rests on psychology and limits to arbitrage.
2. **Prospect theory.** Value function $v(x) = x^{\alpha}$ for gains and $-\lambda(-x)^{\beta}$ for losses ($\alpha = \beta = 0.88$, $\lambda = 2.25$ in the 1992 estimates); weighting function $w(p) = p^{\gamma}/(p^{\gamma} + (1-p)^{\gamma})^{1/\gamma}$; fourfold pattern.
3. **Heuristics.** Representativeness (base-rate neglect, conjunction fallacy, gambler's fallacy), availability, anchoring $A + \theta(T - A)$; Bayes' rule.
4. **Overconfidence and self-attribution.** Calibration $z = (\text{hits} - nc)/\sqrt{nc(1-c)}$; trading cost $T \times 2c$; biased Bayesian updating.
5. **Mental accounting and framing.** Fungibility, narrow framing, myopic loss aversion, hedonic editing, homemade dividends.
6. **The disposition effect.** $PGR = RG/(RG + PG)$ and $PLR = RL/(RL + PL)$; tax cost; realization utility; debated explanations.
7. **Limits to arbitrage.** Fundamental risk, noise trader risk, costs and short-sale constraints, agency problems; stub value $M_{parent} - f M_{sub}$.
8. **Anomalies and bubbles.** 12-1 momentum, long-run reversal, value premium, Gordon growth value $D_1/(r - g)$, dot-com episode.
9. **Sentiment and herding.** Closed-end fund discount, cascades, LSV measure $H = |B/(B+S) - \bar p| - AF$.
10. **Corporate behavioral finance.** Market timing and catering; hubris: Acquirer NPV = synergies $-$ premium; stock-financed deals with misvalued shares.
11. **Behavioral portfolio theory and advice.** Layers of goals; $SFR = (\mu - H)/\sigma$ and $P(R < H) = \Phi(-SFR)$; time-weighted against money-weighted return; moderate cognitive and adapt to emotional biases.
12. **Choice architecture.** Defaults, automatic enrollment and escalation, $B_N = \sum c_t S_t (1+i)^{N-t}$; evidence and limits of nudges.

## Self-check

- I can compute a certainty equivalent, a prospect theory value, a Bayesian posterior, PGR and PLR, and a stub value without notes.
- I can say, for each bias, what the unbiased benchmark is.
- I can explain why a documented bias does not by itself imply a mispriced market.
- I can say where the evidence is debated: loss aversion size, hot hand, momentum and value explanations, bubbles, nudge effect sizes.

## Common errors

- Applying the value function to final wealth instead of gains and losses.
- Ignoring base rates, or computing the posterior with the wrong likelihood.
- Dividing realized gains by all realized trades (or comparing counts of gains and losses sold) instead of dividing by realized plus paper gains.
- Forgetting that arbitrage is risky: convergence is not guaranteed before capital runs out.
- Calling premiums value created, and treating all large price falls as proof of a bubble.
- Treating nudge effects as equal to those of defaults.

prompt: An investor bought a stock at \$100; it now trades at \$80. She can sell now (a sure loss of \$20 relative to her purchase price), or hold, in which case the stock is equally likely to be worth \$110 or \$50 at her decision date. Using prospect theory with the purchase price as the reference point ($\alpha = \beta = 0.88$, $\lambda = 2.25$, $\gamma = 0.61$ for gains and $0.69$ for losses), by how much does the prospect value of holding exceed the value of selling? Give the difference to two decimal places.
answer: 2.66
tol: 0.01
check: (0.5 ** 0.61 / (0.5 ** 0.61 + 0.5 ** 0.61) ** (1 / 0.61)) * 10 ** 0.88 + (0.5 ** 0.69 / (0.5 ** 0.69 + 0.5 ** 0.69) ** (1 / 0.69)) * (-2.25 * 50 ** 0.88) - (-2.25 * 20 ** 0.88)
hint: Holding is a mixed gamble relative to the purchase price: $+10$ with probability 0.5 and $-50$ with probability 0.5.
hint: $V_{hold} = w^{+}(0.5)\,v(10) + w^{-}(0.5)\,v(-50)$ and $V_{sell} = v(-20)$, where $w^{+}(0.5) = 0.4206$ and $w^{-}(0.5) = 0.4540$.
solution: $v(10) = 7.59$, $v(-50) = -70.36$ and $v(-20) = -2.25 \times 20^{0.88} = -31.41$. $V_{hold} = 0.4206(7.59) + 0.4540(-70.36) = -28.75$. The difference is $-28.75 - (-31.41) = 2.66 > 0$, so she holds: the convexity of the loss branch makes the loser worth holding, the logic often used for the disposition effect (a debated link).

prompt: Cardiff Systems offers \$1,170 million in cash for Dunmore Labs, whose standalone market value is \$900 million. Cardiff's CEO claims \$600 million of synergies. In three earlier deals Cardiff announced synergies of \$400, \$500 and \$300 million and realized \$80, \$150 and \$60 million. Scale the CEO's claim by the ratio of total realized to total announced synergies in the earlier deals. What is the NPV of the deal to Cardiff's shareholders on that basis, in millions of dollars (negative if negative)?
answer: -125
lead: \$
check: 600 * (80 + 150 + 60) / (400 + 500 + 300) - (1170 - 900)
hint: The premium is the price paid minus the target's standalone value.
hint: The realized-to-announced ratio is $290/1{,}200$.
solution: The premium is $1{,}170 - 900 = \$270$ million. The ratio of realized to announced synergies is $290/1{,}200 = 24.2\%$, so realistic synergies are $0.2417 \times 600 = \$145$ million and the NPV is $145 - 270 = -\$125$ million. Under the CEO's claim the NPV would be $+\$330$ million: the gap is the overconfidence in the claim.

@exam self
prompt: Case (invented). Elena, 52, has \$400,000 in a retirement account, all in the stock of her employer, which she bought gradually and which has fallen 30% from its peak. She says: "I'll sell when it gets back to where it was. My employer is a great company, I read every article about it, and I know it better than any analyst." She checks the balance daily, has moved in and out of the stock four times this year, and feels bad about the fall because "my account is my retirement." Write an audit of about 300 words: name at least four biases with the evidence in the case, compute the percentage gain needed to get back to the peak, say what an unbiased benchmark would be for her allocation decision, and propose a process with two controls that you would use as her adviser.
hint: Use the disposition effect, anchoring, overconfidence, narrow framing and myopic loss aversion, and home bias.
solution: Biases and evidence: (1) Anchoring or a reference point on the peak ("sell when it gets back"), which is a break-even rule tied to the disposition effect: she holds a loser to avoid realizing a loss. (2) Overconfidence, since she thinks she knows the company better than any analyst, and focuses on articles that confirm her view (availability and confirmation). (3) Mental accounting and home bias, since the whole account is in the employer's stock: familiarity is mistaken for safety. (4) Narrow framing and myopic loss aversion, since she checks the balance daily, and trades four times a year, which also adds costs and can reflect overconfidence. The gain needed to recover from a 30% fall is $1/0.70 - 1 = 42.9\%$. The benchmark is a diversified portfolio chosen for her goals, horizon and capacity for loss, where the decision to hold the stock depends on its expected return and risk from today's price, not on her purchase price or its peak; concentration in her employer also ties her human capital and her savings to the same risk. Process: (a) a written policy that limits any single stock, including her employer's, to a set percentage of the portfolio, with a staged sale schedule (a pre-commitment that does not require her to decide each time); (b) a review of the account only quarterly, plus a decision journal and a pre-mortem before any trade. Tax effects of selling should be checked; this is general education, not advice.
rubric: Names at least four biases with evidence from the case (for example disposition effect, anchoring, overconfidence, home bias, narrow framing).
rubric: Computes the required gain correctly (42.9%).
rubric: States that the allocation decision should depend on future expected return and risk, goals and diversification, not on the purchase price or peak.
rubric: Proposes two specific controls (a concentration limit or staged sale, less frequent review, a decision journal, a pre-mortem).
