@article
In earlier weeks, each investor's bias was a private matter. Prices change only if many investors err in the same direction at the same time, and arbitrage is limited. **Investor sentiment** is the belief, shared by many investors, about future cash flows and risk that is not justified by the facts at hand. It is the channel through which individual biases can become price effects. **Herding** is the tendency of investors to follow the actions of others.

### Measuring sentiment

There is no direct measure, so researchers use proxies:

- The **closed-end fund discount**: $\text{discount} = (\text{NAV} - \text{price})/\text{NAV}$, where NAV is net asset value per share. Because individual investors hold many of these funds, discounts that widen and narrow together have been used as a sentiment gauge.
- **IPO activity**: the number of IPOs and their first-day returns, which have averaged double-digit percentages in the United States, tend to rise when investors are optimistic.
- **Surveys** of individual investors, trading volume and the issuance of equity relative to debt.
- **Implied volatility** indexes, such as the VIX (the market's expectation of S&P 500 volatility over the next 30 days from option prices), are sometimes read as fear gauges. Find current levels at the data provider, not in this lesson.
- **Composite indexes** that combine several of these proxies into one number.

### What sentiment does to returns

Theory, building on limits to arbitrage, predicts that sentiment matters most for assets that are hard to value and hard to arbitrage: young, small, volatile, unprofitable or non-dividend-paying stocks. Studies find that after periods of high sentiment these stocks tend to earn lower subsequent returns than safer stocks, and the reverse after low sentiment. The result is sensitive to how sentiment is measured and the sample used, so it is best read as supportive evidence, not as a settled rule.

### Herding

**Intentional herding** means ignoring one's own information and imitating others. Reasons include an **informational cascade** (others' actions reveal information, so it is rational for each individual to follow them), **reputation** (a manager who is wrong with the crowd is blamed less than one who is wrong alone) and **compensation** based on relative performance. **Spurious herding** means that investors act alike because they react to the same public information, with no imitation at all. Distinguishing them is hard in data; this is why many statements about herding are debated.

### An informational cascade

Suppose a project's value is High or Low with equal prior probability. Traders move in sequence. Each gets a private signal that is correct with probability $q > 0.5$ and observes earlier actions (adopt or reject), not their signals. The first trader follows her signal. If the first two traders both adopt, then each has revealed a good signal. The third trader's posterior odds that the value is High are the prior odds times the likelihood ratios of all signals she can infer:

$$\text{posterior odds} = \left(\frac{q}{1-q}\right)^{2} \times \left(\frac{1-q}{q}\right) \quad \text{if her own signal is bad.}$$

The two inferred good signals outweigh her one bad signal, so she adopts regardless of her private information. Her action reveals nothing, and everyone after her faces the same inference: a **cascade**. Cascades can start on little information and can be wrong. They are fragile: a small amount of new public information can end them.

### Measuring herding among institutions

The LSV measure compares, for a stock in a quarter, the fraction of institutions that buy, $B/(B+S)$, with the average fraction of buyers across all stocks that quarter, $\bar p$:

$$H = \left|\frac{B}{B+S} - \bar p\right| - AF,$$

where $AF$ is the expected value of the first term if institutions traded independently with probability $\bar p$ of buying (computed from the binomial distribution). A positive $H$ means more clustering than chance. Studies of US institutional investors typically find small average values, with larger values in small stocks, and researchers disagree about whether such clustering reflects imitation or reaction to shared information. Evidence of retail trading that moves together on social media, such as the January 2021 trading in heavily shorted stocks, shows how coordination can occur, but how much it affects prices in general is debated.

@example
title: How often is a cascade wrong?
problem: A project's value is High or Low with equal prior probability. Each trader's private signal is correct with probability $q = 0.7$. Traders move in sequence and see earlier actions. (a) What is the third trader's posterior probability of High if the first two adopted and her own signal is bad? (b) If the true value is Low, what is the probability that the first two traders both adopt, starting an incorrect cascade?
step: (a) Each adoption reveals a good signal, with likelihood ratio $0.7/0.3 = 2.333$. Her own bad signal has ratio $0.3/0.7 = 0.4286$. Posterior odds $= 2.333^2 \times 0.4286 = 2.333$.
step: The posterior probability is $2.333/(1 + 2.333) = 0.70$. She still adopts, because 70% in favor of High beats her prior of 50%.
step: (b) If the value is Low, each signal is wrong (good) with probability 0.3, and each of the first two traders adopts only after a good signal. So the probability is $0.3 \times 0.3 = 0.09$.
step: In 9% of such situations the cascade starts on a wrong signal, and everyone who follows is wrong. The cascade is rational for each person given what they see, and yet inefficient for the group.
answer: (a) 70%; (b) 9%.

@example
title: The LSV herding measure
problem: In one quarter, 10 institutions trade a stock: 8 buy and 2 sell. The average fraction of buyers across all stocks that quarter is $\bar p = 0.5$. Compute the LSV measure $H$.
step: The observed deviation is $|8/10 - 0.5| = 0.30$.
step: Under independent trading, the number of buyers $B \sim$ Binomial($10$, $0.5$). The expected deviation is $AF = \sum_{k=0}^{10}\binom{10}{k}0.5^{10}\,|k/10 - 0.5| = 0.1230$.
step: $H = 0.30 - 0.1230 = 0.177$.
step: A value of 0.177 means that 8 buyers out of 10 is much more lopsided than independent trading would typically produce (about 12.3 percentage points of fraction of buyers). It does not tell us why the institutions agreed: imitation or shared information.
answer: $H = 0.177$.

@mistakes
- Reading any positive LSV value as proof of imitation: spurious herding (reaction to shared information) gives the same pattern.
- Forgetting the adjustment factor $AF$, which is positive even when traders act independently.
- Treating a cascade as irrational: each trader acts rationally on what she sees; the inefficiency is that private signals stop being revealed.
- Using a sentiment proxy as if it were a forecast: the evidence relates sentiment to subsequent returns on average and for particular kinds of stocks.

@practice num
prompt: Signals are correct with probability 0.8 and the prior probability of High is 50%. A trader sees three earlier traders adopt (each revealing a good signal) and her own signal is bad. What is her posterior probability that the value is High, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 94.12
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (4 ** 3 * 0.25) / (1 + 4 ** 3 * 0.25) * 100
hint: A good signal multiplies the odds by $0.8/0.2 = 4$; a bad signal by $0.2/0.8 = 0.25$.
solution: Posterior odds $= 4^3 \times 0.25 = 16$, so $P = 16/17 = 94.12\%$. She adopts despite her own bad signal.

@practice num
prompt: Eight institutions trade a stock in a quarter, and 7 buy and 1 sells. The average fraction of buyers across stocks is $\bar p = 0.5$. Compute the LSV measure $H = |B/(B+S) - \bar p| - AF$, where $AF$ is the expected value of $|B/8 - 0.5|$ if $B \sim$ Binomial($8$, $0.5$). Give $H$ to three decimal places.
answer: 0.238
tol: 0.001
check: abs(7 / 8 - 0.5) - sum(comb(8, k) * 0.5 ** 8 * abs(k / 8 - 0.5) for k in range(9))
hint: The observed deviation is $|7/8 - 0.5| = 0.375$.
hint: Compute $AF = \sum_{k=0}^{8}\binom{8}{k}0.5^8|k/8 - 0.5|$, which is about 0.137.
solution: $AF = 0.1367$, so $H = 0.375 - 0.1367 = 0.238$.

@quiz num
prompt: Signals are correct with probability 0.6 and the prior probability of High is 50%. A trader sees two earlier traders adopt (each revealing a good signal) and her own signal is bad. What is her posterior probability of High, as a percent?
answer: 60
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.5 ** 2 / 1.5) / (1 + 1.5 ** 2 / 1.5) * 100
hint: A good signal multiplies the odds by $0.6/0.4 = 1.5$; a bad signal by $0.4/0.6$.
solution: Posterior odds $= 1.5^2 \times (0.4/0.6) = 2.25 \times 0.6667 = 1.5$, so $P = 1.5/2.5 = 60\%$. She adopts.
