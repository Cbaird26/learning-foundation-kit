@article
Ask someone for a range of next year's inflation that they are 90% sure will contain the truth. If they are calibrated, nine ranges in ten should do so. In many studies the hit rate is far lower, often somewhere between 40% and 70%: the ranges are too narrow. **Overconfidence** is this excess faith in one's own knowledge and judgment, and it is among the best-documented findings in the psychology of judgment.

### Three kinds of overconfidence

- **Overprecision**: too narrow an interval around an estimate, or too much certainty that one's estimate is right (the most robust form).
- **Overestimation**: believing one's own ability, performance or control is higher than it is (the illusion of control belongs here).
- **Overplacement** (better-than-average): believing one ranks above others; most people rate themselves above average on skills such as driving, and the effect shrinks or reverses for hard tasks.

Evidence has limits. Overconfidence depends on the task: the **hard-easy effect** is that people are most overconfident on hard questions and may be underconfident on easy ones. Results come mostly from questionnaires and may reflect how the question is posed, so researchers also use behavior in markets.

### Calibration as a binomial test

If a forecaster gives $n$ intervals at confidence level $c$ and the intervals are calibrated and independent, the number of hits is Binomial($n$, $c$), with mean $nc$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{nc(1-c)}$. A hit count far below $nc$ (measured by $z = (\text{hits} - nc)/\sqrt{nc(1-c)}$) is evidence of overprecision.

### Overconfidence and trading

If investors overestimate the precision of their private information, they trade too much: each believes the other side of the trade is mistaken. Trading is costly (commissions, spreads, market impact and, in taxable accounts, taxes), so gross returns can be fine and net returns poor. For **turnover** $T$ (the fraction of portfolio value sold and replaced in a year) and a cost $c$ per unit traded on each side, annual cost is $T \times 2c$. Studies of US discount brokerage accounts in the early 1990s found that households that traded the most earned substantially lower net returns than those that traded the least, even though their pre-cost performance was not clearly worse; men traded more than women and earned lower net returns in one well-known study. These studies show a link between trading and net returns in particular samples. They do not by themselves prove that overconfidence is the cause: other motives for trading (liquidity needs, taxes, entertainment) also matter.

### Self-attribution bias

**Biased self-attribution** means crediting successes to one's skill and failures to bad luck. It makes confidence grow over time even if skill does not. A benchmark is Bayes' rule. Let $s$ be the prior probability that an investor is skilled, where a skilled investor has a success probability $p_1$ in each period and an unskilled one has $p_0 < p_1$. The posterior odds after an outcome are the prior odds times the **likelihood ratio**: success multiplies the odds by $p_1/p_0$, and failure by $(1-p_1)/(1-p_0)$. A biased updater might weight a failure's likelihood ratio by a fraction $\phi < 1$ (so its effect on odds is $[(1-p_1)/(1-p_0)]^{\phi}$), or ignore it altogether ($\phi = 0$). Models of this kind predict overreaction to private signals, short-run momentum and long-run reversal, topics for week 8. The model is a theory about updating: it matches some experimental and survey evidence, but how strongly it drives market prices is debated.

### Finding biases in a story

The week's deliverable is to read an investor story and name its biases, with the evidence from the text. Look for: intervals and certainty claims (overprecision), "I knew it" after wins (self-attribution), frequent trading (overconfidence with costs), neglect of how often a signal is right (base-rate neglect, week 3), and anchoring on a purchase price.

@example
title: Testing calibration
problem: A forecaster gives 90% confidence intervals for 20 economic quantities, and 12 of the intervals contain the true value. How likely is a result this low or lower if she is calibrated and the intervals are independent?
step: Under calibration, hits $\sim$ Binomial($20$, $0.9$): mean $= 20(0.9) = 18$ and standard deviation $= \sqrt{20(0.9)(0.1)} = 1.342$.
step: The observed hit count is 6 below the mean: $z = (12 - 18)/1.342 = -4.47$.
step: The exact probability of 12 or fewer hits is $\sum_{k=0}^{12}\binom{20}{k}0.9^k 0.1^{20-k} = 0.000416$, about 1 in 2,400.
step: Her hit rate is 60%, not 90%. Such a result would almost never come from a calibrated forecaster, so her intervals are too narrow.
answer: About 0.04% (z = -4.47): her intervals are overprecise.

@example
title: Updating beliefs about skill
problem: An investor's prior probability of being skilled is 0.5. A skilled investor has a 70% chance of a profitable year, an unskilled one 50%. After three profitable years and three unprofitable years, what is the Bayesian posterior? What if she credits successes fully but counts each failure at half weight ($\phi = 0.5$)?
step: Likelihood ratios: success $0.7/0.5 = 1.4$; failure $0.3/0.5 = 0.6$. Prior odds are $1$.
step: Bayesian: odds $= (1.4 \times 0.6)^3 = 0.84^3 = 0.5927$, so $P = 0.5927/1.5927 = 0.372$. Equal numbers of wins and losses leave her less likely to be skilled than before.
step: Biased: odds $= 1.4^3 \times (0.6^{0.5})^3 = 2.744 \times 0.4648 = 1.2755$, so $P = 1.2755/2.2755 = 0.560$.
step: The biased investor ends more confident than she started (56%) even though the evidence points the other way (37%). With $\phi = 0$ she would reach $1.4^3/(1+1.4^3) = 73.3\%$.
answer: Bayesian posterior 37.2%; biased posterior 56.0%.

@mistakes
- Treating overconfidence as one thing: overprecision, overestimation and overplacement are measured differently and do not always appear together.
- Computing trading costs on turnover once instead of on both the sale and the purchase.
- Concluding that high turnover proves overconfidence: link the evidence to costs and net returns, and consider other motives.
- Giving failures a larger weight than successes in a Bayesian update, or forgetting that a failure should lower the odds of skill.

If she is calibrated and the intervals are independent, how many standard deviations is the hit count below its expected value? Give $z$ to two decimal places (as a negative number).
answer: -5.27
tol: 0.01
check: (26 - 40 * 0.9) / (40 * 0.9 * 0.1) ** 0.5

@practice num
prompt: An investor earns a gross return of 9% a year before trading costs. Each year she sells and replaces 150% of her portfolio (turnover of 150%). Each sale and each purchase costs 0.4% of the amount traded. What is her annual return after costs, in percent?
answer: 7.8
unit: %
check: 9 - 1.5 * (0.4 + 0.4)
hint: A year's trades are 150% of portfolio value sold and 150% bought.
hint: Cost $= T \times 2c = 1.5 \times 0.8\%$.
solution: Annual cost is $1.5 \times (0.4\% + 0.4\%) = 1.2\%$, so the net return is $9\% - 1.2\% = 7.8\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: A skilled investor has a 70% chance of a profitable year and an unskilled one 50%. Your prior is that the investor is skilled with probability 0.5. She has two profitable years and one unprofitable year. What is the Bayesian posterior probability that she is skilled? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 54.04
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.4 ** 2 * 0.6) / (1 + 1.4 ** 2 * 0.6) * 100
hint: Success multiplies the odds by $0.7/0.5 = 1.4$ and failure by $0.3/0.5 = 0.6$.
solution: Posterior odds $= 1.4^2 \times 0.6 = 1.176$, so $P = 1.176/2.176 = 0.5404$, or 54.04%.

@quiz self
prompt: Name the main bias in each short story and cite the evidence. (1) Mira trades 20 times a month and says her stock picks "always work out"; she remembers her gains vividly and attributes her few losses to bad news. (2) Jonas read that a stock is up 400% in three years and bought it because the company "looks like Amazon did"; he never checked how many similar firms collapsed. (3) Priya sold her fund at \$62 only after it climbed back to her purchase price of \$62, although she believed it was overvalued at \$55.
hint: Story 1 combines two ideas from this week; story 2 is from week 3; story 3 uses a reference point.
solution: (1) Overconfidence (high turnover, belief that picks always work) and self-attribution bias (successes credited to skill, losses to bad news); the cost is trading costs and a failure to learn. (2) Representativeness with base-rate neglect: the stock resembles a famous winner, but Jonas ignored the base rate of how many look-alike firms fail. (3) Anchoring on the purchase price as a reference point (and a reluctance to realize a loss, which week 6 calls the disposition effect): she waited to break even at \$62 even though she judged the stock overvalued.
