@article
In the efficient-markets argument, a mispriced security is a free lunch: rational traders buy it if it is cheap and sell it if it is expensive, and their trades push the price back. The **law of one price** says identical assets should trade at identical prices. In its textbook form, **arbitrage** is a strategy that requires no capital, bears no risk and earns a positive profit. Real arbitrage is different: **relative-value arbitrage** buys a cheap asset and shorts a similar (not identical) one, and it is risky, costly and often done with other people's money. This week explains why that matters, and it completes the second pillar of behavioral finance.

### Why arbitrage is limited

- **Fundamental risk.** A cheap stock is hard to hedge perfectly. There is rarely a perfect substitute, so the arbitrageur carries risk from news that hits the asset she holds and not the one she shorts.
- **Noise trader risk.** **Noise traders** are investors whose demand is driven by sentiment rather than information. Mispricing can get worse before it gets better: if a cheap asset becomes cheaper, the arbitrageur takes a loss, and she may be forced to close the position before prices converge. The risk is the largest when the arbitrageur's horizon is short.
- **Implementation costs.** Commissions, bid-ask spreads and market impact reduce profits. **Short-sale constraints** matter for overpriced assets: shares must be borrowed, borrow fees can be large, supply can be scarce, and the lender can recall the shares. Overpricing is therefore harder to correct than underpricing.
- **Agency problems.** Arbitrage is often delegated to funds that are judged on performance. If a trade loses money in the short run, investors may withdraw capital exactly when the mispricing is largest and the trade is most attractive. The arbitrageur knows this and takes smaller positions; so mispricing can persist and even deepen. Leverage amplifies the problem because lenders issue margin calls when losses mount.

### Evidence

Evidence for limits comes from cases in which law-of-one-price violations lasted. When a parent company owns most of a subsidiary's shares, the parent's value should be at least the value of its stake. In March 2000, a technology company sold a small share of a subsidiary in an IPO and planned to distribute the rest to its own shareholders. For a period, the subsidiary's market value exceeded the whole parent's market value, which implied that the parent's remaining business had a negative value. Shares of the subsidiary were scarce and expensive to borrow, which limited shorting. Twin shares of two companies that had agreed to split cash flows in a fixed ratio traded at prices that deviated from that ratio, at times by tens of percent, for years. Closed-end funds frequently trade at discounts or premiums to the value of their holdings. In 1998, a large hedge fund heavily leveraged in convergence trades lost most of its capital when spreads widened sharply before converging; its lenders, urged by the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, agreed to a private recapitalization.

Limits to arbitrage do not mean that all prices are wrong, and they do not show why mispricing arises in the first place. They show that the "arbitrage will fix it" defense has holes. Evidence on how large and persistent mispricing is varies by market, and the debate over its size continues.

### The stub value

If a parent owns a fraction $f$ of a subsidiary, then the **implied stub value** of the parent's other businesses is

$$\text{Stub} = M_{\text{parent}} - f \times M_{\text{sub}},$$

where $M$ denotes market capitalization. A negative stub is an apparent violation of limited liability, since an equity cannot be worth less than zero.

### The net return of a convergence trade

For a trade that expects the price gap to close by $g$ percent of the position, the net return over a holding period is $g$ minus transaction costs minus the borrow fee paid over the period. The return per unit of risk (an information ratio or Sharpe-like ratio) is the net expected return divided by the volatility of the spread. If the ratio is low, a risk-averse arbitrageur takes only a small position.

@example
title: A negative stub
problem: Parent Corp owns 90% of Sub Inc. Sub Inc has a market capitalization of \$6,000 million and Parent Corp of \$4,800 million. Find the implied stub value, and say what the pair of prices implies about the market.
step: The value of Parent's stake in Sub is $0.9 \times 6{,}000 = \$5{,}400$ million.
step: The implied stub is $4{,}800 - 5{,}400 = -\$600$ million.
step: An equity cannot be worth less than zero, so the prices imply an error. A trader could buy Parent Corp's shares and short the number of Sub Inc shares that those Parent shares represent (a 90% stake, scaled to the size of the position), and gain if the prices converge, for example if the parent distributes the Sub shares.
step: The trade is not riskless. If Sub shares are hard or expensive to borrow, the short leg may be unavailable or costly, and the gap can widen before it closes. That is why the mispricing is not simply competed away.
answer: Stub of $-\$600$ million, a mispricing that costs and risks keep in place.

@example
title: Net return and leverage risk
problem: A fund expects a 5% price gap to close within a year on a pair of positions with \$100 long and \$100 short. The short leg's borrow fee is 2% a year and round-trip trading costs are 0.5% of the position. The fund has capital of \$20. (a) What is the expected net profit, per \$100 on each leg? (b) If the gap first widens by 3 points, what is the loss on the fund's capital?
step: (a) Gross profit is 5% of \$100 = \$5. Borrow fee is \$2 and trading costs are \$0.50, so net profit is $5 - 2 - 0.5 = \$2.50$, or 12.5% on the \$20 of capital.
step: (b) A widening of 3 points on a \$100 position is a mark-to-market loss of \$3, which is $3/20 = 15\%$ of capital.
step: The expected gain after costs on capital is 12.5%, but the interim loss is 15%. If investors or lenders force her to close, she realizes the loss and misses the convergence.
answer: (a) \$2.50 net per \$100, or 12.5% of capital; (b) a 15% loss on capital before convergence.

@mistakes
- Treating relative-value trades as true arbitrage: they have fundamental risk, and the profit comes only if prices converge before the arbitrageur is forced out.
- Forgetting that short-sale constraints make overpricing harder to correct than underpricing.
- Computing the loss of a leveraged spread against the position's size instead of against the capital.
- Concluding from limits to arbitrage that prices are always wrong: limits explain persistence, but they require a source of mispricing.
