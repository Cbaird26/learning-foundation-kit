@article
Cafeterias place fruit at eye level because people pick what is close. Every decision is made in some environment, and someone designed it, whether deliberately or not. **Choice architecture** is the design of the environment in which people make choices. A **nudge** is any feature of that design that predictably changes behavior without forbidding any option or significantly changing economic incentives. **Libertarian paternalism** is the view that institutions may steer people toward options that make them better off, as judged by themselves, while keeping freedom of choice. Its critics reply that the designer's view of "better off" may be wrong, and that the steering lacks transparency.

### Why defaults work

A **default** is what happens if the person does nothing. It is powerful for three reasons: **inertia and procrastination** (acting has effort costs and the benefits are in the future), **implicit endorsement** (people infer the default is what experts or the employer recommend), and **loss aversion with the status quo as the reference point** (leaving the default feels like a loss from it). In retirement plans, switching from **opt-in** (employees must sign up) to **automatic enrollment** (employees are enrolled unless they opt out) has raised participation sharply. In a well-known study of one large US company, participation among new hires rose from about 37% to about 86%. But defaults are sticky in a second way: many participants stay at the default contribution rate and the default investment fund, so a default rate that is too low can lower the savings of employees who would have chosen a higher rate by themselves.

### Escalation and other tools

**Automatic escalation** raises the contribution rate on a schedule, often with pay raises, so that the increase does not feel like a cut in take-home pay. It uses present bias, the preference for immediate rewards, as a tool: the employee commits now to save more later. Other tools include **active choice** (requiring a decision), **simplification**, **reminders**, **commitment devices** and **framing**. Employers also match contributions, and a default below the match threshold leaves free money unclaimed. In the United States, SECURE 2.0 (2022) requires most new 401(k) and 403(b) plans, for plan years starting after 2024, to enroll eligible employees automatically at a rate of at least 3% and at most 10%, escalating by 1 percentage point a year to at least 10% (and at most 15%), with exceptions for existing plans, small employers and new businesses. Plan rules and limits change: the 2026 employee deferral limit for 401(k)s is \$24,500 (with catch-up contributions for ages 50 and over), and learners should check current rules. This is general education, not tax or investment advice.

### What the evidence says, and its limits

Defaults have large and replicated effects on enrollment in pension plans and, in many studies, on organ donation registration and other choices. Across all nudges the picture is more mixed. A study of 126 randomized trials run by two government nudge units found an average effect of about 1.4 percentage points, compared with about 8.7 points in a sample of published academic papers, which points to publication bias and to the difficulty of scaling effects. Meta-analyses that correct for publication bias disagree on how large the average nudge effect is. The effects also vary across people and settings, and some research (for example, on "choice overload") finds that too many options reduce choice in some settings but not on average. A nudge can also be harmful when the default does not fit people. The fair summary is that defaults are strong in settings with inertia, while nudges as a class are smaller than first reported.

### Ethics and design

A good default is **transparent** (people can see that it is a default and why), **easy to opt out of**, chosen for the people's welfare (not the designer's profit), and **tested** with measurement of outcomes. **Sludge** is the opposite: friction that makes good choices harder, such as long opt-out forms. Designers should also ask who is hurt: if some employees have high-interest debt or little cash, the default could cause harm, so plans offer easy opt-outs and sometimes lower starting rates.

### Computing the effect of a default

Let salary in year $t$ be $S_t = S_1(1+g)^{t-1}$, the contribution rate be $c_t$ and the net return $i$. With contributions at the end of each year, the balance evolves as $B_t = B_{t-1}(1+i) + c_t S_t$, so $B_N = \sum_{t=1}^{N} c_t S_t (1+i)^{N-t}$.

@example
title: Flat defaults against escalation
problem: An employee earns \$50,000 in year 1, with raises of 3% a year, and expects a 5% net return. Contributions are made at the end of each year. Compare the balance after 30 years under (a) a flat 3% default, (b) a flat 6% default, and (c) a default that starts at 3% and rises by 1 point a year up to 10%.
step: For each year $t$ compute $S_t = 50{,}000 \times 1.03^{t-1}$ and the contribution $c_t S_t$; then $B_{30} = \sum c_t S_t\,1.05^{30-t}$.
step: (a) A flat 3% gives $B_{30} = \$142{,}101$.
step: (b) A flat 6% doubles the contribution each year and gives $B_{30} = \$284{,}202$.
step: (c) With escalation the rate is 3%, 4%, ..., 10% in year 8 and 10% afterward, giving $B_{30} = \$418{,}188$.
step: Escalation from the same starting rate is about three times as large as the flat 3% default. A low flat default is the worst of the three, which is why the default's level matters as much as enrollment.
answer: (a) \$142,101; (b) \$284,202; (c) \$418,188.

@example
title: A default below the employer match
problem: An employer matches 50% of the first 6% of pay that an employee contributes. The employee earns \$60,000 and is automatically enrolled at 3% and never changes it. How much employer match does she receive in a year, and how much does she leave unclaimed compared with contributing 6%?
step: At 3%, she contributes $0.03 \times 60{,}000 = \$1{,}800$ and the match is $0.5 \times 1{,}800 = \$900$.
step: At 6%, she contributes \$3,600 and the match is $0.5 \times 3{,}600 = \$1{,}800$.
step: She leaves $1{,}800 - 900 = \$900$ a year of employer match unclaimed.
step: A default at the match threshold (6%) would capture it; a low default turns inertia into a lost benefit. The designer must weigh a higher default against the risk that some employees opt out and the cost of reduced take-home pay.
answer: Match of \$900 at 3%; \$900 a year unclaimed compared with 6%.

@mistakes
- Treating a nudge as a ban or a mandate: a nudge keeps every option open and does not change prices significantly.
- Looking only at participation: the default contribution rate and fund also matter, because many participants never change them.
- Quoting the effect of defaults as the effect of all nudges: the average effect across studies is smaller, and published effects are larger than those in large trials.
- Ignoring who is hurt: a default that helps most people can harm those whose circumstances differ, so opt-out and monitoring are part of the design.

model:
```python
def retirement_balance(salary, raise_rate, start_rate, step, cap,
                       annual_return, years):
    """Balance after `years` of end-of-year contributions with escalation."""
    balance = 0.0
    for year in range(years):
        rate = min(cap, start_rate + step * year)
        balance = balance * (1 + annual_return) + salary * rate
        salary *= 1 + raise_rate
    return balance
```
hint: Use a loop over the years; the rate in loop index `year` (starting at 0) is `min(cap, start_rate + step * year)`.
hint: Update the balance as `balance * (1 + annual_return) + salary * rate`, and then raise the salary.
solution: Each year, the old balance earns the return and the year's contribution is added at the end, so it earns nothing that year; the salary rises afterward. The rate follows the escalation schedule and is capped. The test with a zero return and a cap checks the cap, and the two-year test checks the end-of-year timing, which a start-of-year solution gets wrong (23.1 instead of 21.0).
