@article
Two stocks in your account were each bought for \$50. One now trades at \$65 and the other at \$38. You need cash. Which do you sell? Many investors sell the winner and keep the loser, hoping it will "come back". The **disposition effect** is this tendency to sell assets that have gained in value (winners) too readily and to hold assets that have lost value (losers) too long, relative to a purchase price that serves as the reference point.

### Measuring it: PGR and PLR

Selling more winners than losers is not enough, because an investor may simply own more winners. The standard measure compares what was sold with what could have been sold. On each day that an investor sells a stock in an account, look at every other stock held in that account on that day:

- A **realized gain** is a stock sold at a price above its purchase price; a **paper gain** is a stock held that day at a price above its purchase price that was not sold. A **realized loss** and a **paper loss** are defined the same way below the purchase price.
- The **proportion of gains realized** is
$$PGR = \frac{\text{realized gains}}{\text{realized gains} + \text{paper gains}},$$
and the **proportion of losses realized** is
$$PLR = \frac{\text{realized losses}}{\text{realized losses} + \text{paper losses}}.$$
- An unbiased investor with no reason to treat gains and losses differently would have $PGR \approx PLR$. The disposition effect appears as $PGR > PLR$, often reported as the ratio $PGR/PLR$.

Positions at exactly the purchase price are ignored. In the best-known study (about 10,000 accounts at a US discount brokerage, 1987–1993) the PGR was about 0.15 and the PLR about 0.10, a ratio of about 1.5. Later work found the pattern across many countries and markets, among individual investors, in housing sales (owners resist selling below what they paid), and, usually in smaller magnitudes, among professional investors. Experience reduces but does not eliminate it.

### Is it costly?

In a taxable account, selling a loser lets you use the loss to offset capital gains (and up to \$3,000 a year of ordinary income), while selling a winner triggers tax. Realizing losses and deferring gains is the opposite of the disposition pattern. For tax year 2026, long-term capital gains are taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on taxable income, and an additional 3.8% net investment income tax applies at higher incomes (this is general education, not tax advice). Investors who sell winners also give up the momentum those winners tend to have: in the study above, the winners sold outperformed the losers kept over the following year by several percentage points. Outside of December, investors' sales are not well explained by tax-loss selling.

### Explanations

- **Prospect theory.** The value function is concave for gains (risk averse: lock in the gain) and convex for losses (risk seeking: gamble to get back to even). This argument has a limit: if the reference point is the purchase price and the value function is applied to the investor's total wealth, the theory does not always predict a disposition effect, and some models predict the opposite. The link is debated.
- **Realization utility.** Investors may get utility from the act of realizing a gain (pride) or suffer from realizing a loss (regret), with a purchase price as the reference point. Models of realization utility generate a disposition effect directly.
- **Mental accounting.** Each stock is a separate account, and closing an account at a loss means admitting a loss.
- **Beliefs.** If investors believe prices mean-revert, they hold losers and sell winners. Empirically, winners continue to do well in the short run, so the belief is not supported by returns.
- **Rational reasons.** Rebalancing (sell winners that have grown overweight) and transaction costs can create some selling of winners; studies controlling for these find the effect remains.

@example
title: Computing PGR and PLR
problem: Over a sample of sale days in an invented account, the counts are: realized gains 24, paper gains 96, realized losses 12, paper losses 108. Compute PGR, PLR and the ratio, then test whether the difference is likely to be due to chance.
step: $PGR = 24/(24 + 96) = 0.20$ and $PLR = 12/(12 + 108) = 0.10$, so $PGR/PLR = 2.0$. The investor realized gains twice as readily as losses.
step: A two-proportion $z$ test with $n_1 = 120$ gain-days and $n_2 = 120$ loss-days uses the pooled proportion $\hat p = (24 + 12)/240 = 0.15$.
step: $SE = \sqrt{0.15 \times 0.85 \times (1/120 + 1/120)} = 0.0461$, so $z = (0.20 - 0.10)/0.0461 = 2.17$.
step: $z = 2.17$ is above 1.96, so the difference is significant at the 5% level, with a caveat: the paper positions on different days are not independent, so this naive test overstates significance, and real studies adjust for that.
answer: PGR = 0.20, PLR = 0.10, ratio 2.0, $z = 2.17$.

@example
title: The tax cost of selling the winner
problem: An investor must raise cash. She can sell stock W, a long-term holding with an unrealized gain of \$8,000, or stock L, a long-term holding with an unrealized loss of \$8,000. Her long-term capital gains rate is 15%, and she has no other gains. Compare the tax result of selling W with the tax result of selling L (ignore the \$3,000 ordinary-income offset for this comparison).
step: Selling W realizes a gain of \$8,000 and tax of $0.15 \times 8{,}000 = \$1{,}200$ now.
step: Selling L realizes a loss of \$8,000 and creates no tax today. The loss can be carried forward to offset future gains, worth up to $0.15 \times 8{,}000 = \$1{,}200$ when used.
step: The difference is about \$1,200 of tax paid now compared with \$1,200 of potential future tax saved, which is a swing of up to \$2,400 in favor of selling L. Keeping L in the hope of "getting back to even" gives up that benefit. Taxes are one consideration: the investment case for each stock matters too.
answer: Selling W costs \$1,200 of tax now; selling L saves up to \$1,200 later, a swing of up to \$2,400.

@mistakes
- Measuring the effect by comparing the number of winners sold with the number of losers sold, without dividing by how many winners and losers were held.
- Counting a position at exactly its purchase price as a gain or a loss.
- Calling a $PGR$ above $PLR$ proof of prospect theory: other mechanisms, such as realization utility, also predict it, and prospect theory applied to total wealth need not.
- Assuming that selling winners is always irrational: rebalancing and costs explain some of it, but they do not account for the whole pattern.

model:
```python
def pgr_plr(positions):
    """Return (PGR, PLR) from (return, sold) tuples; None if undefined."""
    gains = [sold for ret, sold in positions if ret > 0]
    losses = [sold for ret, sold in positions if ret < 0]
    pgr = sum(gains) / len(gains) if gains else None
    plr = sum(losses) / len(losses) if losses else None
    return pgr, plr
```
hint: Separate the positions into gains (return above 0) and losses (return below 0), keeping only the `sold` flags.
hint: The proportion realized is the number of `True` flags divided by the length of the list; guard against an empty list.
solution: Split the positions into gain flags and loss flags (zero returns drop out of both lists). Each proportion is the count of `True` values divided by the number of positions in that group, which is `sum(flags) / len(flags)` because `True` counts as 1; an empty group returns `None` rather than dividing by zero.
