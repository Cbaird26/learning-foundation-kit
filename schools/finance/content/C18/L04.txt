@article
A **forward contract** fixes today an exchange rate for a currency exchange on a future date. Its price is not a guess about the future: it follows from today's spot rate and interest rates, because an investor can lock in the same result in two different ways.

### Covered interest rate parity

Let $S$ be the spot rate and $F$ the forward rate, both in **price currency per base currency**. Take the base currency as the foreign currency (EUR) and the price currency as the domestic currency (USD), so $S = 1.1000$ means USD 1.1000 per EUR. Let $i_d$ be the domestic (USD) interest rate and $i_f$ the foreign (EUR) interest rate, both for the period of the contract. You have USD 1 to invest for the period. Route one: invest at home and get $1 + i_d$ in USD. Route two: convert to $1/S$ EUR at spot, invest abroad to get $(1 + i_f)/S$ EUR, and sell those EUR forward at $F$ to get $F(1 + i_f)/S$ USD. With no risk in either route, they must give the same USD. This is **covered interest rate parity** (CIP):

$$\frac{F}{S} = \frac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f}.$$

### Conventions

The formula needs the rate for exactly the contract period, so three conventions matter.

- **Simple interest with a day count.** Money-market rates are quoted as annual rates with simple interest for periods up to a year. For a contract of $d$ days, the period rate is $i \times d/B$, with basis $B = 360$ (ACT/360, used for USD and EUR) or $365$ (ACT/365, used for GBP). Then $F = S \times \dfrac{1 + i_d\, d/B_d}{1 + i_f\, d/B_f}$. Each currency has its own basis.
- **Annual compounding** over $T$ years: $F = S\left(\dfrac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f}\right)^{T}$.
- **Continuous compounding:** $F = S\,e^{(r_d - r_f)T}$.

**Forward points** are $F - S$, quoted in pips (units of the fourth decimal place for most pairs). The currency with the **higher interest rate trades at a forward discount**: if $i_d > i_f$ then $F > S$, so the foreign currency (the base) trades at a forward premium and the domestic one at a discount.

### Covered interest arbitrage

If the quoted forward differs from the CIP value, borrow in one currency, lend in the other and lock the exchange rate: a risk-free profit with no net investment. If the quoted $F$ is above the CIP value, the base currency is overpriced forward: borrow domestic, buy the base at spot, invest it, and sell it forward. If it is below, do the opposite. Transaction costs (bid-ask spreads and credit lines) create a band around the CIP value inside which no arbitrage is possible. Since 2008, differences called the cross-currency basis have appeared at times, mostly when dollar funding was scarce, so CIP holds closely but not perfectly.

**Uncovered interest parity** is a different claim: that the expected future spot rate equals the forward rate. It is a hypothesis about expectations, not an arbitrage, and lesson 6 tests it.

All rates here are invented; current interest rates come from central banks and data services.

@example
title: Pricing a 90-day forward
problem: The spot rate is EUR/USD = 1.1000. The 90-day USD interest rate is 5.00% and the 90-day EUR rate is 3.00%, both quoted ACT/360 with simple interest. Find the 90-day forward rate and the forward points.
step: Period rates for 90 days on a 360 basis: USD $0.05 \times 90/360 = 0.0125$; EUR $0.03 \times 90/360 = 0.0075$.
step: $F = 1.1000 \times 1.0125/1.0075 = 1.10546$.
step: Forward points: $1.10546 - 1.1000 = 0.00546$, or 54.6 pips. USD rates are higher, so the euro trades at a forward premium.
answer: F = 1.1055 (about 54.6 pips above spot).

@example
title: A covered interest arbitrage
problem: EUR/USD spot is 1.1000. One-year interest rates (annual compounding) are 5% in USD and 3% in EUR. A bank quotes a one-year forward at 1.1250. Is there an arbitrage? Show it for USD 1,000,000 and give the profit.
step: The CIP forward is $1.1000 \times 1.05/1.03 = 1.12136$. The quoted 1.1250 is higher, so EUR is overpriced forward.
step: Now: borrow USD 1,000,000 at 5%, which will require USD 1,050,000 at year-end. Convert at spot: $1{,}000{,}000/1.1000 = \text{EUR } 909{,}090.91$. Invest at 3%. Sell the year-end proceeds forward at 1.1250.
step: At year-end: EUR $909{,}090.91 \times 1.03 = 936{,}363.64$, sold forward for USD $936{,}363.64 \times 1.1250 = 1{,}053{,}409.09$.
step: Repay USD 1,050,000. The profit is USD 3,409.09, locked in with no exchange-rate risk and no money of your own.
answer: Yes; profit USD 3,409.09 at the end of the year.

@mistakes
- Putting the rates the wrong way round: the domestic rate belongs in the numerator when the rate is price (domestic) currency per base (foreign) currency.
- Using annual rates for a 90-day contract without scaling by days over the basis.
- Applying one day-count basis to both currencies, when GBP uses 365 and USD and EUR use 360.
- Thinking the forward rate is a forecast of the future spot rate: CIP is a no-arbitrage relation, not a prediction.

model:
```python
def forward_rate(spot, i_d, i_f, days, basis_d=360, basis_f=360):
    """Covered interest parity forward rate with simple interest."""
    domestic = 1 + i_d * days / basis_d
    foreign = 1 + i_f * days / basis_f
    return spot * domestic / foreign
```
hint: Compute one period growth factor for each currency, using its own basis.
hint: Multiply the spot rate by the domestic factor and divide by the foreign factor.
solution: Each currency's growth factor is 1 plus its annual rate times days over its own basis. Covered interest parity puts the domestic factor over the foreign factor, multiplied by the spot rate. With zero days both factors are 1, so the forward equals the spot.
