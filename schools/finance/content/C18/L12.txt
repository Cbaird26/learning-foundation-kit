@article
A **currency crisis** is a sharp fall in a currency's value, or a large loss of reserves and a steep rise in interest rates to avoid one. Crises are most common under pegs and managed rates, because a fixed rate that markets stop believing must be defended with reserves or abandoned.

### First-generation models: unsustainable policies

In the first models of crises, a government runs persistent deficits that the central bank finances by creating money, while it promises a fixed exchange rate. Reserves fall steadily. Rational investors foresee that reserves will run out, so they attack **before** exhaustion, in a **speculative attack** at the date when the **shadow exchange rate** (the rate that would prevail if reserves were gone and the currency floated) reaches the peg. The attack is sudden, though the cause, inconsistent policy, was gradual. A naive projection of exhaustion is reserves divided by the annual loss; the attack comes sooner.

### Second-generation models: self-fulfilling crises

Here the government weighs the costs of defending the peg (high interest rates, unemployment, a weak banking system) against the benefits of keeping it. If investors expect devaluation, the cost of defense rises, so the government is more likely to give up, which validates the expectation. Crises can therefore be **self-fulfilling**: the same fundamentals can support the peg or a crisis, depending on expectations.

### Third-generation models: balance sheets

These emphasize **currency mismatch**: firms, banks and governments borrow in foreign currency but earn in local currency. A devaluation raises the local value of foreign debt, damages balance sheets and can trigger bank runs and a collapse in lending, deepening the crisis. A **sudden stop** of capital inflows magnifies it.

### Warning indicators

Common early-warning indicators include:

- reserves relative to **short-term external debt** (debt due within a year); a ratio below 1 means the country could not repay it from reserves, and many analysts use 1 as a rule of thumb;
- reserves in **months of imports**: reserves divided by monthly imports;
- real exchange rate overvaluation, large current account deficits, rapid credit growth, and a banking sector holding foreign-currency liabilities.

No indicator predicts timing reliably.

### Defending a peg

With covered interest parity and risk neutrality, if investors expect a devaluation of size $\delta$ (the percentage rise in local currency per foreign currency) with probability $q$ within the year, the expected depreciation is $q\delta$ and uncovered parity requires

$$1 + i_d = (1 + i_f)(1 + q\,\delta).$$

The interest rate needed to keep investors willing to hold local-currency assets rises sharply as $q$ rises, which hurts firms and banks. A government can defend with reserves, interest rates, capital controls or IMF support; or let the currency float.

### Three episodes

- **The 1992 ERM crisis.** Several European currencies were held within bands in the Exchange Rate Mechanism. German reunification led to high German interest rates, while other members faced weak growth. In September 1992 the United Kingdom and Italy suspended their participation after heavy reserve sales and sharp interest rate increases failed to hold their currencies.
- **Mexico 1994.** The peso was devalued in December 1994 and then floated. Short-term government debt linked to the dollar, falling reserves, political shocks and rising US interest rates all contributed.
- **Asia 1997.** Thailand floated the baht in July 1997 after months of pressure; the crisis spread to other East Asian economies. Contributing factors were pegs to the dollar, large short-term foreign-currency borrowing, current account deficits and weaknesses in financial supervision. Several countries then turned to the IMF.

These events are summarized; the causes are debated, and the models above emphasize different parts of each case. All numbers below are invented.

@example
title: Defending a peg
problem: The one-year foreign interest rate is 4%. Investors think there is a 30% chance that within a year the local currency will be devalued by 25% (the price of foreign currency rises 25%). What one-year local interest rate makes investors indifferent to holding local-currency assets?
step: Expected depreciation: $q\delta = 0.30 \times 0.25 = 7.5\%$.
step: $1 + i_d = 1.04 \times 1.075 = 1.118$, so $i_d = 11.8\%$.
step: The government must pay a 7.8-point premium over the foreign rate to hold the peg. If the probability rises to 60%, the expected depreciation is 15% and the rate needed is $1.04 \times 1.15 - 1 = 19.6\%$.
answer: 11.8% (and 19.6% if the probability is 60%).

@example
title: The balance-sheet effect
problem: A local firm has assets of 1,800 million local currency units (all in local currency) and owes USD 100 million. The peg is 10 local units per USD. After a devaluation to 15, what happens to the firm's equity, if the local value of its assets does not change?
step: Before: debt $= 100 \times 10 = 1{,}000$ million, so equity $= 1{,}800 - 1{,}000 = 800$ million.
step: After: debt $= 100 \times 15 = 1{,}500$ million, so equity $= 1{,}800 - 1{,}500 = 300$ million.
step: Equity falls by $500/800 = 62.5\%$. Many firms in this position at once can lead to defaults and bank losses.
answer: Equity falls from 800 to 300 million (-62.5%).

@mistakes
- Thinking a crisis starts only when reserves reach zero: speculative attacks occur earlier.
- Believing one model explains every crisis: the three generations stress different causes.
- Treating a reserve ratio as a forecast: indicators signal vulnerability, not timing.
- Forgetting that devaluation helps exporters and raises the local value of foreign-currency debt: the balance-sheet effect can dominate.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose one currency crisis (the 1992 ERM crisis, Mexico in 1994 or Asia in 1997). In six to eight sentences, explain its main causes using at least two of the three generations of models, and say one lesson for policy.
hint: Say which exchange-rate regime was in place, what weakness investors saw, and how the crisis spread.
solution: Taking Asia in 1997: several economies had pegs to the US dollar and attracted large short-term foreign-currency borrowing by banks and firms, creating currency mismatches (third-generation). Current account deficits and signs of overvaluation, together with property and credit booms and weak supervision, made investors doubt the pegs (first-generation elements). Once Thailand abandoned its peg in July 1997, investors reassessed neighboring countries, and expectations of devaluation raised the cost of defense, so the attacks fed on themselves (second-generation). Depreciation raised the local value of dollar debts, hurting balance sheets, causing defaults and sudden stops of capital. A policy lesson is to hold adequate reserves relative to short-term foreign debt, supervise currency mismatches, and allow more exchange rate flexibility. The causes are still debated, and this is a summary, not a complete account.
