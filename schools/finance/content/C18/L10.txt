@article
A US investor who holds only US stocks owns a fraction of the world's investable assets. Adding foreign assets can lower risk, because returns in different economies are not perfectly correlated. But a foreign asset brings a second source of return and risk: the exchange rate.

### Return in the home currency

Let $R_\ell$ be the return on a foreign asset in its **local currency** and $g = S_1/S_0 - 1$ the change in the value of the foreign currency in home currency terms, with $S$ in **home currency per foreign currency** (USD per EUR for a US investor). Over one period,

$$1 + R_d = (1 + R_\ell)(1 + g), \qquad R_d = R_\ell + g + R_\ell\, g \approx R_\ell + g.$$

A foreign stock that rises 8% in euros gives a US investor more than 8% if the euro appreciates, and less if it falls.

### Risk in the home currency

For the home-currency return of the foreign asset, with local volatility $\sigma_\ell$, currency volatility $\sigma_g$ and correlation $\rho_{\ell g}$ between local return and currency change, the variance is approximately

$$\sigma_d^{2} \approx \sigma_\ell^{2} + \sigma_g^{2} + 2\rho_{\ell g}\,\sigma_\ell\,\sigma_g.$$

(The cross-product term $R_\ell g$ is small and ignored.) Currency risk adds to volatility unless the correlation is negative enough to offset it.

### Diversification

For a portfolio with weight $w$ on asset 1 and $1 - w$ on asset 2, volatilities $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$,

$$\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^{2}\sigma_1^{2} + (1 - w)^{2}\sigma_2^{2} + 2w(1 - w)\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}.$$

Unless $\rho = 1$, $\sigma_p$ is below the weighted average of volatilities. The **minimum-variance weight** on asset 1 is

$$w^{*} = \frac{\sigma_2^{2} - \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^{2} + \sigma_2^{2} - 2\rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2}.$$

This formula gives a weight between 0 and 1 only when $\rho < \sigma_2/\sigma_1$ and $\rho < \sigma_1/\sigma_2$. In practice, **correlations tend to rise during global market stress**, so the benefit shrinks when it is most needed. Investors also show **home bias**, holding more domestic assets than a global-market-weight portfolio would, because of familiarity, costs, taxes and currency risk. This is education, not investment advice.

### Hedged returns

An investor can hedge the currency with forward contracts. If the end value of the foreign investment could be hedged exactly, the return in USD would be locked at

$$R_{\text{hedged}} = (1 + R_\ell)\frac{F}{S_0} - 1 = (1 + R_\ell)\frac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f} - 1 \approx R_\ell + (i_d - i_f),$$

using covered interest parity (lesson 4). So hedging replaces the unknown currency change with the **interest differential**: hedging a low-rate currency back to a high-rate home currency adds return; hedging a high-rate currency costs it. In practice the end value is unknown, so some currency exposure remains.

All figures are invented.

@example
title: Local and home-currency returns
problem: A US investor buys euro-area shares. Over a year the shares return 8% in euros. What is the return in USD if the euro (a) appreciates 3% and (b) depreciates 5% against the dollar?
step: (a) $1 + R_d = 1.08 \times 1.03 = 1.1124$, so $R_d = 11.24\%$.
step: (b) $1 + R_d = 1.08 \times 0.95 = 1.026$, so $R_d = 2.60\%$.
step: The currency moved the USD return from 8% to 11.24% in one case and 2.60% in the other. The approximation $R_\ell + g$ gives 11% and 3%, close but not exact: the cross-product matters when rates are large.
answer: (a) 11.24%; (b) 2.60%.

@example
title: Risk and diversification
problem: A US investor considers adding euro-area shares. The shares have local volatility 18% and the euro has volatility 9% against the dollar, with correlation 0.2 between local return and currency change. US shares have volatility 15%, and the correlation between US shares and the euro-area shares in USD is 0.6. What are the volatility of the euro-area shares in USD and of a 50/50 portfolio?
step: Home-currency variance of the euro-area shares: $0.18^{2} + 0.09^{2} + 2(0.2)(0.18)(0.09) = 0.0324 + 0.0081 + 0.00648 = 0.04698$, so $\sigma_d = 21.67\%$.
step: Portfolio variance: $0.5^{2}(0.15)^{2} + 0.5^{2}(0.2167)^{2} + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.6)(0.15)(0.2167) = 0.005625 + 0.011745 + 0.009754 = 0.027124$ approximately.
step: $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.027124} = 16.47\%$. The weighted average of the two volatilities is 18.33%, so diversification lowers risk by about 1.9 points, even though the foreign asset is more volatile than the domestic one.
answer: 21.67% for the euro-area shares in USD; 16.47% for the 50/50 portfolio.

@mistakes
- Adding the local return and the currency change and stopping there: the exact home-currency return multiplies the growth factors.
- Using the exchange rate in the wrong direction: for a US investor in euro assets, $g$ uses USD per EUR.
- Believing a hedge removes all risk and costs nothing: the hedged return embeds the interest differential, and the end value is uncertain.
- Assuming correlations are constant: they usually rise when markets are stressed.

model:
```python
import math


def portfolio_vol(w, vol1, vol2, rho):
    """Volatility of a two-asset portfolio with weights w and 1 - w."""
    variance = ((w * vol1) ** 2 + ((1 - w) * vol2) ** 2
                + 2 * w * (1 - w) * rho * vol1 * vol2)
    return math.sqrt(max(variance, 0.0))
```
hint: Write the variance first, with the squared terms and the covariance term.
hint: Take the square root; guard against a tiny negative value caused by float rounding when the variance is zero.
solution: Add the two squared weighted volatilities and twice the weighted covariance, then take the square root. With perfect negative correlation and the right weights, the variance is zero up to rounding, so the model guards against a tiny negative number before taking the root.

@quiz self
prompt: A US investor says: "Foreign stocks are safer for me because they diversify my portfolio, so the currency does not matter." In four or five sentences, explain what is right and what is wrong about this claim, and how a forward hedge changes the picture.
hint: Think about both diversification and the extra volatility from the currency.
solution: Foreign stocks do diversify, because their returns are not perfectly correlated with US stocks, so adding them can lower portfolio volatility. But the currency matters: in home-currency terms the foreign stock's variance includes the currency's variance and covariance, so unhedged volatility can exceed local volatility, and exchange-rate moves can add or subtract several points of return. A forward hedge replaces the currency change with the interest differential and reduces volatility, but it costs the interest differential when the foreign rate is higher, and it cannot hedge the unknown end value exactly. Correlations also tend to rise in crises, so diversification is weaker when it is most needed.
