@article
The **foreign exchange (FX) market** is where currencies are traded. It is mostly an over-the-counter market of dealers (large banks) trading with each other and with clients around the clock on business days, so there is no single exchange price. The Bank for International Settlements publishes a triennial survey of its turnover, which is the place to look for current volumes.

### Quote conventions

A quote is written **base/price**, for example EUR/USD = 1.0800, meaning **one unit of the base currency (EUR) costs 1.0800 units of the price currency (USD)**. This course always uses that convention: price currency per base currency.

- A **direct quote** states the price of a unit of foreign currency in the investor's home currency. For a US investor, EUR/USD = 1.0800 is direct.
- An **indirect quote** states the price of a unit of home currency in foreign currency. For the same investor, USD/EUR = 0.9259 is indirect.

The two are reciprocals: $S(\text{USD/EUR}) = 1/S(\text{EUR/USD})$. Which one is "direct" depends on whose home currency it is.

### Bid and ask

A dealer quotes two rates for the base currency: the **bid**, at which the dealer buys the base, and the **ask** (or offer), at which the dealer sells it. The bid is always below the ask. For EUR/USD = 1.0848/1.0852 you sell EUR to the dealer at 1.0848 USD and buy EUR from the dealer at 1.0852 USD. The **spread** is the ask minus the bid; expressed relative to the midpoint $m = (b + a)/2$, the **percentage spread** is $(a - b)/m$. A spread of 0.0004 on a four-decimal quote is 4 **pips**.

When you invert a quote, bid and ask swap: $\text{bid}(\text{USD/EUR}) = 1/\text{ask}(\text{EUR/USD})$ and $\text{ask}(\text{USD/EUR}) = 1/\text{bid}(\text{EUR/USD})$. A **spot** trade settles in two business days for most currency pairs (one for USD/CAD).

### Cross rates

If A/B and C/B are quoted (both against B), the cross rate A/C comes from dividing:

$$\text{bid}(A/C) = \frac{\text{bid}(A/B)}{\text{ask}(C/B)}, \qquad \text{ask}(A/C) = \frac{\text{ask}(A/B)}{\text{bid}(C/B)}.$$

To sell A for C through B you sell A for B at the bid, then buy C with B at the ask. Without spreads, $S(A/C) = S(A/B)/S(C/B)$. If the quote is B/C, you multiply instead.

### Triangular arbitrage

If a directly quoted cross rate differs from the one implied by two other rates, you can profit by trading around the triangle. Ignoring spreads and transaction costs, start in one currency, convert through the three currencies, and finish in the starting currency. Profit above your starting amount, risk-free, means an **arbitrage**. In practice such gaps last for fractions of a second and are smaller than the spreads.

### Percentage changes

If EUR/USD moves from $S_0$ to $S_1$, the euro (the base) changes by $S_1/S_0 - 1$. The dollar's change against the euro is not the opposite: it is $S_0/S_1 - 1$. A fall from 1.25 to 1.15 is an 8.00% depreciation of the euro but an 8.70% appreciation of the dollar.

All rates in this course are invented. For current rates use a central bank or a data service such as FRED.

@example
title: A cross rate with spreads
problem: A dealer quotes EUR/USD 1.0800/1.0804 and USD/JPY 150.00/150.05 (JPY per USD). What are the dealer-implied bid and ask for EUR/JPY?
step: Here both quotes chain through USD, so multiply. EUR/JPY bid: sell EUR for USD at 1.0800, then sell USD for JPY at 150.00, giving $1.0800 \times 150.00 = 162.000$.
step: EUR/JPY ask: buy EUR with USD at 1.0804, and buy USD with JPY at 150.05, giving $1.0804 \times 150.05 = 162.114$.
step: The implied quote is EUR/JPY 162.000/162.114, a spread of 0.114 JPY.
answer: EUR/JPY 162.000/162.114.

@example
title: A triangular arbitrage
problem: Ignore spreads. EUR/USD = 1.1000, GBP/USD = 1.3000, and a bank quotes EUR/GBP = 0.8400. Starting with USD 1,000,000, is there an arbitrage, and how much is the profit?
step: The implied EUR/GBP is $1.1000/1.3000 = 0.84615$. The quoted 0.8400 is lower, so EUR is cheap in GBP.
step: Buy GBP with USD: $1{,}000{,}000/1.3000 = \text{GBP } 769{,}230.77$.
step: Buy EUR with GBP at 0.8400 GBP per EUR: $769{,}230.77/0.8400 = \text{EUR } 915{,}750.92$.
step: Sell EUR for USD at 1.1000: $915{,}750.92 \times 1.1000 = \text{USD } 1{,}007{,}326.01$.
step: The profit is about USD 7,326, with no risk and no net investment beyond the starting dollars.
answer: Yes: USD 1,000,000 becomes USD 1,007,326, a profit of about USD 7,326.

@mistakes
- Inverting a bid-ask quote without swapping bid and ask: the inverted bid is 1 over the original ask.
- Computing the dollar's percentage move as the negative of the euro's: the change is $S_0/S_1 - 1$, not $-(S_1/S_0 - 1)$.
- Multiplying when you should divide (or the reverse) for a cross rate: check which currency is the base in each quote.
- Starting a triangular arbitrage around the triangle in the wrong direction, which gives a loss; compare the quoted cross with the implied one first.

model:
```python
def cross_quote(bid_ab, ask_ab, bid_cb, ask_cb):
    """Bid and ask of A/C implied by A/B and C/B quotes."""
    return bid_ab / ask_cb, ask_ab / bid_cb
```
hint: To sell A for C you sell A for B at one rate, then buy C with B at another.
hint: Divide the A/B bid by the C/B ask for the bid, and the A/B ask by the C/B bid for the ask.
solution: The dealer's bid is the worst rate for the client selling A: sell A for B at the bid, then buy C at the ask, so the bid is `bid_ab / ask_cb`. The ask pairs the two opposite rates. Dividing this way guarantees that the cross bid is never above the cross ask.
