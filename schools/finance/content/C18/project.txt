@review
## Course review: International finance

### Week by week

1. **Balance of payments.** Current account, capital account, financial account and errors sum to zero; $CA = S - I$; the NIIP is a stock.
2. **Regimes.** Floats, pegs, boards and unions; gold standard, Bretton Woods, post-1973; the impossible trinity; sterilized and unsterilized intervention.
3. **FX market.** Quotes as price currency per base currency; direct and indirect; bid-ask and inversion; cross rates; triangular arbitrage; percentage changes.
4. **Interest rate parity.** Covered interest parity, day counts and forward points; covered interest arbitrage.
5. **PPP.** Law of one price, absolute and relative PPP, real exchange rate, the international Fisher effect.
6. **Forecasting.** Fundamental, technical and market-based forecasts; ME, MAE and RMSE; the carry trade and the forward premium puzzle.
7. **Exposure.** Transaction, economic and translation exposure; the current rate and temporal methods.
8. **Hedging.** Forward, money market and option hedges; natural hedges.
9. **Capital budgeting.** Foreign currency and home currency methods; the parity-consistent discount rate; country risk.
10. **Portfolios.** Home-currency returns, volatility, diversification, hedged returns.
11. **Sovereign risk.** Spreads, implied default probabilities, country risk premiums, debt dynamics.
12. **Currency crises.** Three generations of models, warning indicators, the cost of defending a peg.

### Key formulas

- $CA + KA + FA + EO = 0$ (FA as net inflow); $CA = S - I$.
- $F = S\,\dfrac{1 + i_d\,d/B_d}{1 + i_f\,d/B_f}$ (price-currency rate on top).
- $S_1/S_0 = (1 + \pi_d)/(1 + \pi_f)$; $q = S P_f/P_d$.
- $1 + R_d = (1 + R_\ell)(1 + g)$; $\sigma_p^{2} = w^2\sigma_1^2 + (1-w)^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w(1-w)\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$.
- $1 + r_f = (1 + r_d)(1 + i_f)/(1 + i_d)$.
- $p = \dfrac{1 - (1 + r_f)/(1 + y)}{1 - R}$; $d_{t+1} = d_t(1 + r)/(1 + g) - pb_t$.

### Self-check

- I can state the quote convention before using any rate.
- I can price a forward and find an arbitrage in either direction.
- I can compute a hedge cost under three methods and explain which one fits which goal.
- I can value a foreign project in two currencies and reconcile them.
- I can name the causes of a crisis using at least two models.

### Common errors

- Mixing sign conventions in the balance of payments, or the direction of a quote.
- Using annual rates for short contracts, or one day count for both currencies.
- Forgetting to swap bid and ask on inversion, or to carry an option premium to maturity.
- Discounting foreign flows at the home rate, or converting at spot when rates differ.
- Treating CIP as a forecast of the future spot rate.

@exam self
prompt: An emerging economy has a fixed exchange rate to the US dollar and free capital movement. Its current account deficit is 7% of GDP, financed mainly by short-term dollar borrowing from foreign banks. Reserves cover 1.8 months of imports and are 60% of short-term external debt. Domestic banks lend in local currency to local firms. In a memo of eight to ten sentences, assess the country's vulnerability to a currency crisis: which of the three generations of models apply, what the balance-of-payments and reserve numbers imply, what would happen to balance sheets after a devaluation, and what policies could reduce the risk.
hint: Link the deficit and its financing to the balance of payments, use the reserve ratios, and think about currency mismatches.
solution: The country is vulnerable. A deficit of 7% of GDP means a net capital inflow of the same size, here mostly short-term dollar borrowing that can reverse in a sudden stop (a first-generation concern: policies are hard to sustain with a fixed rate and reserves that cannot cover obligations). Reserves cover only 1.8 months of imports and 60% of short-term external debt, below the rule-of-thumb ratio of 1, so reserves could not repay the debt due within a year, which invites a speculative attack and a second-generation self-fulfilling crisis, since defending the peg would require very high interest rates. A devaluation would raise the local value of the banks' dollar liabilities, while their assets are in local currency, so equity falls and defaults rise: the third-generation balance-sheet channel. The trinity says the country cannot keep the peg, free capital flows and an independent monetary policy. Policies that reduce risk include building reserves and lengthening the debt, limiting currency mismatches and short-term foreign borrowing through supervision, allowing more exchange-rate flexibility, and using temporary capital-flow measures and IMF support if needed.
rubric: Links the 7% deficit to a capital inflow and identifies the risk from short-term foreign-currency financing.
rubric: Interprets both reserve ratios (1.8 months of imports; 60% of short-term debt) as inadequate.
rubric: Applies at least two model generations correctly, including the balance-sheet effect of devaluation on banks.
rubric: Names relevant policies (reserves, debt maturity, mismatch supervision, flexibility, capital-flow measures).
