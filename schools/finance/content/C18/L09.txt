@article
A US firm considers a factory in the euro area. The project will produce cash flows in euros, but the firm's shareholders care about dollars. There are two ways to value it, and they must give the same answer if the assumptions behind them agree.

### Cash flows to value

As in any capital budgeting, use **incremental cash flows after tax**, at the time they occur. For a foreign project add three questions. Which cash flows reach the **parent** (the project's flows, or only those that can be remitted)? What do **foreign and home taxes** do (the foreign corporate tax, withholding taxes on dividends, and credits in the home country)? What happens at the end (terminal value, working capital)? Use the cash flows in the **currency in which they arise**.

### Method 1: foreign currency method

1. Forecast the project's cash flows in foreign currency (EUR).
2. Discount them at the **foreign-currency discount rate** $r_f$.
3. Convert the resulting NPV to USD at the **spot rate** today.

### Method 2: home currency method

1. Forecast the cash flows in EUR and the exchange rate $S_t$ for each year.
2. Convert each flow to USD at $S_t$.
3. Discount the USD flows at the **home-currency discount rate** $r_d$.

### Making the methods consistent

If the exchange-rate forecast follows interest rate parity (or relative PPP), $S_t = S_0\left(\dfrac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f}\right)^{t}$, where $i_d$ and $i_f$ are the risk-free rates. A flow $C_t$ in EUR is worth $C_t S_t$ in USD at time $t$. Discounting at $r_d$ gives $C_t S_0 (1+i_d)^t(1+i_f)^{-t}(1+r_d)^{-t}$. For both methods to agree, the foreign discount rate must satisfy

$$1 + r_f = \frac{(1 + r_d)(1 + i_f)}{1 + i_d}.$$

This is the CIP/IFE relation in a discount rate: the risk premium over the risk-free rate is built into both rates, and the foreign rate is lower when foreign interest rates are lower. A common error is to discount EUR flows at the USD rate $r_d$ and then convert at spot, or to convert at today's spot and discount at $r_d$ while ignoring the expected exchange-rate change.

### Country risk and other adjustments

**Country risk** (expropriation, blocked funds, war, changes in tax rules) can enter in two ways. A **risk premium** added to the discount rate lowers the value of later cash flows more than earlier ones. **Scenario analysis** adjusts the cash flows directly (for example, a probability-weighted loss if funds are blocked) and fits a specific event better. Insurance and local borrowing also reduce the exposure. The discount rate should reflect the project's systematic risk, which may differ from the parent's; lesson 11 covers country risk premiums.

All numbers are invented; use the parent's actual filings (SEC EDGAR) and current market data to build a real analysis. This is a learning exercise, not a recommendation.

@example
title: One project, two methods
problem: A US firm's euro-area project costs EUR 10,000,000 now and pays EUR 4,000,000, 5,000,000 and 5,500,000 at the ends of years 1 to 3. The USD discount rate is 10%, risk-free rates are 4% (USD) and 2% (EUR), and the spot rate is EUR/USD = 1.1000. Value the project both ways.
step: Foreign discount rate: $1 + r_f = 1.10 \times 1.02/1.04 = 1.078846$, so $r_f = 7.8846\%$.
step: EUR NPV: $-10{,}000{,}000 + 4{,}000{,}000/1.078846 + 5{,}000{,}000/1.078846^{2} + 5{,}500{,}000/1.078846^{3} = \text{EUR } 2{,}383{,}634$. In USD at spot: $2{,}383{,}634 \times 1.1000 = \text{USD } 2{,}621{,}998$.
step: Home method: forecast rates $S_t = 1.1000 \times (1.04/1.02)^t$ give 1.1216, 1.1436 and 1.1660 for years 1 to 3. USD flows: $-11{,}000{,}000$, $4{,}486{,}275$, $5{,}717{,}801$ and $6{,}412{,}906$.
step: Discount at 10%: the NPV is USD 2,621,998, the same as the foreign currency method.
answer: NPV = EUR 2,383,634 = USD 2,621,998 under both methods.

@example
title: Adding a country risk premium
problem: For the project above, management adds a 3-point country risk premium to the USD discount rate (13%). Value the project again by the foreign currency method, keeping the 4% and 2% risk-free rates and the spot rate of 1.1000.
step: New foreign discount rate: $1 + r_f = 1.13 \times 1.02/1.04 = 1.108269$, so $r_f = 10.8269\%$.
step: EUR NPV: $-10{,}000{,}000 + 4{,}000{,}000/1.108269 + 5{,}000{,}000/1.108269^{2} + 5{,}500{,}000/1.108269^{3} = \text{EUR } 1{,}720{,}452$.
step: In USD: $1{,}720{,}452 \times 1.1000 = \text{USD } 1{,}892{,}497$. The premium reduces the NPV by about USD 730,000 but the project stays attractive.
answer: NPV about USD 1,892,497 with the risk premium, against USD 2,621,998 without.

@mistakes
- Discounting foreign currency flows at the domestic discount rate, then converting at spot.
- Converting every future flow at today's spot rate when interest rates differ across the two countries.
- Mixing nominal and real: a EUR cash flow forecast in nominal euros needs a nominal discount rate.
- Counting cash flows the parent cannot remit, or ignoring withholding taxes on remittances.

model:
```python
def domestic_npv(flows, spot, i_d, i_f, r_d):
    """NPV in home currency using interest-parity forecast exchange rates."""
    total = 0.0
    for t, flow in enumerate(flows):
        rate = spot * ((1 + i_d) / (1 + i_f)) ** t
        total += flow * rate / (1 + r_d) ** t
    return total
```
hint: Loop over the flows with their time index, and compute the forecast rate for each time.
hint: Each term is the flow times the forecast rate, divided by $(1 + r_d)^t$.
solution: For each period, forecast the exchange rate with interest rate parity, convert the foreign flow to the home currency and discount it at the home rate. Summing the discounted terms gives the NPV. When the home and foreign rates are linked by the parity relation, this equals the foreign currency method.
