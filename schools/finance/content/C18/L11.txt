@article
A company can go bankrupt and a court can take its assets. A **sovereign** government cannot be forced into bankruptcy in the same way: creditors have limited recourse. Whether a government pays depends on its **ability** to pay (tax base, growth, reserves) and its **willingness** to pay (politics, the cost of losing market access). This is **sovereign risk**.

### Currency of the debt

A government that borrows in its **own currency** can always pay nominally, because its central bank can create that currency, but it may do so at the price of inflation, so default can take the form of inflation or restructuring. Debt in **foreign currency** must be paid with foreign currency the country earns or borrows. Many emerging economies have borrowed mostly in foreign currency, a pattern often called **original sin**, which makes currency depreciation a threat to debt sustainability (lesson 12).

### Ratings and spreads

Rating agencies assign sovereign credit ratings. **Investment grade** means BBB- or higher on the S&P and Fitch scales (Baa3 or higher on Moody's); lower ratings are speculative grade. A sovereign's **spread** is the yield on its foreign-currency bond minus the yield on a risk-free bond of the same currency and maturity. **Credit default swap** (CDS) premiums give a similar market view.

If a one-year zero-coupon bond is risk-free at rate $r_f$ and the sovereign bond yields $y$, with **recovery rate** $R$ (the fraction of value recovered in default) and a **risk-neutral default probability** $p$, then the expected payoff $1 - p(1 - R)$ discounted at $r_f$ equals the bond's price:

$$\frac{1 - p(1 - R)}{1 + r_f} = \frac{1}{1 + y}, \qquad p = \frac{1 - (1 + r_f)/(1 + y)}{1 - R}.$$

A rough rule is $\text{spread} \approx p \times (1 - R)$. The probability here is a market-implied, risk-neutral measure: it includes compensation for risk, so it usually exceeds the real-world probability of default.

### Country risk premium and cost of equity

Practitioners often add a **country risk premium** (CRP) to the cost of equity of firms in risky countries. One method scales the sovereign default spread by the relative volatility of the country's equity market to its bond market:

$$\text{CRP} = \text{default spread} \times \frac{\sigma_{\text{equity}}}{\sigma_{\text{bond}}}, \qquad r_e = r_f + \beta \times \text{ERP}_{\text{mature}} + \text{CRP},$$

where $\text{ERP}_{\text{mature}}$ is the equity risk premium of a mature market and $\beta$ the stock's beta. Other treatments multiply the premium by beta or use the full spread. The method is a convention, not a law, and results vary with assumptions.

### Debt dynamics

Let $d$ be public debt as a share of GDP, $r$ the average nominal interest rate on the debt, $g$ nominal GDP growth, and $pb$ the **primary balance** (revenue minus spending excluding interest, as a share of GDP; a surplus is positive). Then

$$d_{t+1} = d_t\,\frac{1 + r}{1 + g} - pb_t.$$

The debt ratio is constant when $pb^{*} = d\,\dfrac{r - g}{1 + g}$. If $r > g$, a country must run a primary surplus to stabilize debt; if $g > r$, it can run a primary deficit and the ratio still falls. Real-world problems also include rollover risk, exchange-rate risk on foreign-currency debt, and contingent liabilities.

### Restructuring and official support

When debt becomes unsustainable, a government may **restructure** it: lengthen maturities, cut interest or reduce principal. Many sovereign bonds include **collective action clauses** that let a qualified majority of holders bind the minority. The **IMF** lends to countries with balance-of-payments problems, usually with conditions on policy, and official bilateral creditors coordinate through bodies such as the Paris Club.

All figures here are invented. For current ratings, spreads and debt data use the IMF, the World Bank and the agencies' public releases.

@example
title: A default probability from a spread
problem: A one-year zero-coupon bond issued by a sovereign in USD yields 9%. The one-year risk-free USD rate is 4% and the assumed recovery rate is 40%. What risk-neutral default probability does the market imply?
step: Spread: $9\% - 4\% = 5\%$ (the rule of thumb gives $5\%/0.60 = 8.3\%$).
step: Exact: $p = \dfrac{1 - 1.04/1.09}{1 - 0.40} = \dfrac{1 - 0.954128}{0.60} = \dfrac{0.045872}{0.60} = 7.65\%$.
step: Check: the expected payoff is $1 - 0.0765 \times 0.60 = 0.954128$, which discounted at 4% gives $0.917431 = 1/1.09$, the bond's price.
answer: About 7.65% (risk-neutral, one year).

@example
title: Debt dynamics
problem: A country has public debt of 80% of GDP, an average interest rate of 5% and nominal GDP growth of 3%. (a) If it runs a primary deficit of 2% of GDP, what is next year's debt ratio? (b) What primary balance would hold the ratio at 80%?
step: (a) $d_1 = 0.80 \times 1.05/1.03 - (-0.02) = 0.81553 + 0.02 = 0.83553$, so the ratio rises to about 83.55%.
step: (b) $pb^{*} = 0.80 \times (0.05 - 0.03)/1.03 = 0.01553$.
step: A primary surplus of about 1.55% of GDP stabilizes the ratio. The gap between the deficit and the needed surplus is 3.55% of GDP.
answer: (a) 83.55% of GDP; (b) a primary surplus of 1.55% of GDP.

@mistakes
- Treating the spread as the default probability: it also depends on the recovery rate, and it includes a risk premium.
- Assuming a country can always avoid default on foreign-currency debt by printing money.
- Forgetting the sign convention for the primary balance: in the debt equation a surplus lowers the debt ratio.
- Confusing the primary balance with the overall balance, which includes interest payments.

@practice num
prompt: An emerging market has a sovereign default spread of 3.0%. Its equity market volatility is 24% and its government bond volatility is 12%. A firm there has beta 1.2, the risk-free rate is 4.0% and the mature-market equity risk premium is 5.5%. Using $\text{CRP} = \text{spread} \times \sigma_{\text{equity}}/\sigma_{\text{bond}}$ and $r_e = r_f + \beta\,\text{ERP} + \text{CRP}$, what is the cost of equity? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 16.6
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 4.0 + 1.2 * 5.5 + 3.0 * 24 / 12
hint: First compute the CRP: the spread times the ratio of volatilities.
solution: $\text{CRP} = 3.0\% \times 24/12 = 6.0\%$. Then $r_e = 4.0\% + 1.2 \times 5.5\% + 6.0\% = 4.0 + 6.6 + 6.0 = 16.6\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: A country's public debt is 60% of GDP, the average interest rate on it is 6% and nominal GDP growth is 2%. What primary balance (as a percent of GDP, positive for a surplus) keeps the debt ratio constant? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 2.35
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 0.60 * (0.06 - 0.02) / 1.02 * 100
hint: Use $pb^{*} = d(r - g)/(1 + g)$.
solution: $pb^{*} = 0.60 \times 0.04/1.02 = 0.02353$, a primary surplus of 2.35% of GDP. Since $r > g$, a surplus is needed.
