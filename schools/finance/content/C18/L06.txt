@article
Firms need exchange-rate forecasts to budget, price, and decide whether to hedge. Yet exchange rates are among the hardest financial variables to predict. This lesson shows the main methods, how to judge them, and why a currency's interest-rate advantage is not a free lunch.

### Approaches to forecasting

- **Fundamental forecasts** use economic relationships: relative PPP ($E[S_1] = S_0(1 + \pi_d)/(1 + \pi_f)$), the international Fisher effect, the balance of payments, relative money supplies and growth. They work best over long horizons, when price effects dominate.
- **Technical forecasts** use past prices and volumes (charts, moving averages, support and resistance levels) with no reference to economic fundamentals. They assume past patterns repeat.
- **Market-based forecasts** use prices observed today. The spot rate is a **random walk** forecast: $E[S_1] = S_0$. The forward rate is a forecast if **uncovered interest parity** (UIP) holds: $E[S_1] = F = S_0 (1 + i_d)/(1 + i_f)$.

A classic empirical result is that, at horizons of a year or less, the random walk forecast is hard to beat with fundamentals-based models, which is why many firms use the spot or the forward rate as their base case and treat the future as uncertain.

### Judging a forecast

Let $e_t = \hat{S}_t - S_t$ be the forecast error (forecast minus actual) over $n$ forecasts. Three standard measures:

$$\text{ME} = \frac{1}{n}\sum e_t, \qquad \text{MAE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum |e_t|, \qquad \text{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum e_t^{2}}.$$

The **mean error** shows bias (errors of opposite sign cancel), while MAE and RMSE show size. RMSE is never below MAE and punishes large errors more. Judge forecasts against a benchmark such as the random walk, because a forecast with a small error may still be worse than no-change.

### The carry trade and the forward premium puzzle

A **carry trade** borrows a low-interest-rate currency and invests in a high-interest-rate one without hedging. Suppose the funding currency is $L$ (rate $i_L$) and the investment currency is $H$ (rate $i_H$), with $S$ in units of $L$ per unit of $H$. Per unit of $L$ borrowed, the investor ends with $(S_0^{-1})(1 + i_H) S_1$ units of $L$ against a debt of $(1 + i_L)$. It is profitable if

$$\frac{S_1}{S_0} > \frac{1 + i_L}{1 + i_H}.$$

The break-even rate is the CIP forward rate: the carry trade gains if the high-rate currency falls by less than the interest differential and loses if it falls by more. If UIP held exactly, the expected profit would be zero. In many samples of major currencies, high-rate currencies have tended to depreciate by much less than UIP predicts (some studies find they do not depreciate at all on average), so average returns were positive, often followed by sharp losses when funding conditions tightened. This is the **forward premium puzzle**; one view is that the average profit compensates for crash risk.

All exchange rates and interest rates in this course are invented. For data use central bank sites, the IMF or FRED.

@example
title: Measuring forecast accuracy
problem: A firm's EUR/USD forecasts for five months were 1.11, 1.10, 1.12, 1.12 and 1.14, and the actual rates were 1.10, 1.12, 1.09, 1.15 and 1.13. Compute the mean error, the MAE and the RMSE.
step: Errors (forecast minus actual): $+0.01,\ -0.02,\ +0.03,\ -0.03,\ +0.01$.
step: Mean error: the sum is 0, so $\text{ME} = 0.000$. The forecasts are unbiased on average.
step: MAE: $(0.01 + 0.02 + 0.03 + 0.03 + 0.01)/5 = 0.020$.
step: RMSE: squared errors $0.0001, 0.0004, 0.0009, 0.0009, 0.0001$ sum to $0.0024$; $\sqrt{0.0024/5} = \sqrt{0.00048} = 0.0219$.
answer: ME = 0.000, MAE = 0.020, RMSE = 0.0219.

@example
title: A carry trade and its break-even
problem: An investor borrows JPY 10,000,000 for a year at 1% and converts it to AUD at 100 JPY per AUD, invests the AUD at 5%, and converts back at the end of the year. What is the profit (in JPY) if the rate is 97 JPY per AUD, and what is the break-even rate?
step: AUD bought: $10{,}000{,}000/100 = \text{AUD } 100{,}000$. After the year: $100{,}000 \times 1.05 = \text{AUD } 105{,}000$.
step: Convert at 97: $105{,}000 \times 97 = \text{JPY } 10{,}185{,}000$. The debt is $10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.01 = 10{,}100{,}000$. Profit: $\text{JPY } 85{,}000$ (0.85% of the amount borrowed).
step: Break-even: $S_1 = 100 \times 1.01/1.05 = 96.19$ JPY per AUD. The AUD may fall about 3.8% before the trade loses money.
answer: Profit JPY 85,000; break-even rate 96.19 JPY per AUD.

@mistakes
- Judging a forecast by its mean error alone: positive and negative errors cancel, so a biased-looking zero can hide large misses.
- Believing the forward rate predicts the spot rate precisely: CIP prices the forward by arbitrage; its link with the future spot rate is the UIP hypothesis, which is tested, not assumed.
- Calling the carry trade a risk-free arbitrage: it is unhedged and can lose heavily if the high-rate currency falls.
- Using a model's fit within the sample to judge it: compare out-of-sample forecasts with a random walk.

@practice num
prompt: A forecaster predicts 101, 100, 100 and 105 for an index of the exchange value of a currency, and the actual values are 100, 102, 99 and 103. Compute the root-mean-squared error to four decimal places.
answer: 1.5811
tol: 0.0001
check: (((101 - 100) ** 2 + (100 - 102) ** 2 + (100 - 99) ** 2 + (105 - 103) ** 2) / 4) ** 0.5
hint: The errors are +1, -2, +1 and +2.
solution: Squared errors are 1, 4, 1 and 4, which sum to 10. $\text{RMSE} = \sqrt{10/4} = \sqrt{2.5} = 1.5811$.

@quiz num
prompt: An investor considers borrowing JPY at 0.5% and investing in AUD at 4% for a year, with the spot rate at 98.00 JPY per AUD. What is the break-even spot rate at the end of the year (JPY per AUD), to two decimal places?

solution: Break-even $S_1 = S_0 (1 + i_{JPY})/(1 + i_{AUD}) = 98.00 \times 1.005/1.04 = 94.70$. If the AUD ends above 94.70 JPY, the carry trade profits.
