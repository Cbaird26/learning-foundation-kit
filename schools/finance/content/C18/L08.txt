@article
Lesson 7 identified the exposure. **Hedging** reduces it by taking a position that gains when the exposed position loses. This lesson prices three standard ways to hedge a **foreign currency payable**: a US firm owes EUR 2,000,000 in 180 days. Throughout, the spot rate is EUR/USD = 1.1000 (USD per EUR), the 180-day USD borrowing rate is 5.00% and the 180-day EUR deposit rate is 3.00%, both annualized, simple interest, ACT/360. All figures are invented.

### 1. Forward hedge

The firm **buys EUR forward** at today's 180-day forward rate $F$ for delivery when the payable falls due. The cost in 180 days is fixed: $A \times F$. By CIP (lesson 4), $F = 1.1000 \times (1 + 0.05 \times 0.5)/(1 + 0.03 \times 0.5) = 1.110837$; a dealer might quote 1.1108. The forward gives certainty, but no gain if the euro falls.

### 2. Money market hedge

The firm creates the euros itself, so that it can pay the debt from a euro deposit.

1. Compute the EUR needed today: $A/(1 + i_{EUR}\,d/360)$, the present value of the payable at the euro deposit rate.
2. Buy those EUR at spot, paying USD (borrowing the USD if necessary).
3. Deposit the EUR; it grows to $A$ at maturity and pays the debt.
4. At maturity, repay the USD loan with interest.

The cost at maturity is the spot cost of the euros times $(1 + i_{USD}\,d/360)$. With no spreads between borrowing and lending rates, this equals the forward hedge cost, as CIP implies.

### 3. Option hedge

A **call option on the euro** gives the right to buy EUR at a **strike price** $K$. The firm pays a **premium** up front and then buys EUR at the lower of the spot rate and $K$. Since the premium is paid today, its cost at maturity includes interest. The all-in cost at maturity is

$$A \cdot \min(S_T, K) + A \cdot \text{premium} \cdot \left(1 + i_{USD}\,\frac{d}{360}\right).$$

The option sets a **ceiling** on the cost, $K$ plus the premium with interest per euro, and keeps the benefit if the euro falls. The price of that flexibility is the premium.

### Natural hedges and policy

**Natural hedges** reduce exposure without derivatives: matching currencies of revenues and costs (producing where you sell), borrowing in the currency of foreign assets or revenues, **netting** receivables against payables in the same currency, invoicing in the home currency, and **leading and lagging** (paying early or late to benefit from expected moves, which is a bet, not a hedge). A **currency swap** exchanges principal and interest in two currencies and hedges longer-term exposures. Hedging with derivatives has costs (spreads, fees, collateral, credit exposure) and accounting rules (hedge accounting under ASC 815 and IFRS 9).

Hedging does not raise a firm's expected profit if forward rates are unbiased predictors; it reduces the **variance** of outcomes, which can lower financial distress costs and stabilize investment. Firms differ on how much to hedge. This is education, not advice.

@example
title: Three ways to pay EUR 2,000,000 in 180 days
problem: Using the data in the article, quote the USD cost at maturity under (a) the forward at 1.1108, (b) the money market hedge, and (c) a call with strike 1.1100 and premium USD 0.0150 per EUR, if the spot rate at maturity is 1.1500.
step: (a) Forward: $2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1108 = \text{USD } 2{,}221{,}600$.
step: (b) Money market: EUR needed today $= 2{,}000{,}000/(1 + 0.03 \times 0.5) = 1{,}970{,}443.35$, costing $1{,}970{,}443.35 \times 1.1000 = 2{,}167{,}487.68$ today. Repaying with 5% (180/360) interest gives $2{,}167{,}487.68 \times 1.025 = \text{USD } 2{,}221{,}674.88$, which is the same as the forward within rounding of the quoted rate.
step: (c) Option: premium $= 2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0150 = 30{,}000$, which is $30{,}000 \times 1.025 = 30{,}750$ at maturity. At 1.1500 the firm exercises at 1.1100: $2{,}220{,}000 + 30{,}750 = \text{USD } 2{,}250{,}750$.
step: Compare: the option costs more than the forward at 1.1500, because of the premium. If the spot at maturity were 1.0500, the option cost would be $2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.0500 + 30{,}750 = 2{,}130{,}750$, far below the forward cost.
answer: (a) USD 2,221,600; (b) USD 2,221,674.88; (c) USD 2,250,750 at 1.1500.

@example
title: Break-even against the forward
problem: Using the forward at 1.1108 and the option (strike 1.1100, premium cost at maturity USD 30,750), at what spot rate at maturity do the two hedges cost the same, and what is the option's maximum cost?
step: The option's cost is lower than the forward's when the firm does not exercise, so set $2{,}000{,}000 \times S_T + 30{,}750 = 2{,}221{,}600$.
step: $S_T = (2{,}221{,}600 - 30{,}750)/2{,}000{,}000 = 1.0954$.
step: Below 1.0954 the option hedge is cheaper than the forward; above it, the forward is cheaper.
step: The maximum cost of the option hedge is $2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1100 + 30{,}750 = \text{USD } 2{,}250{,}750$, or 1.1254 per EUR.
answer: Break-even spot 1.0954; the option hedge costs at most USD 2,250,750.

model:
```python
def option_hedge_cost(spot_t, strike, premium, amount, usd_rate, days):
    """Cost at maturity of paying a foreign payable with a call option."""
    purchase = amount * min(spot_t, strike)
    premium_at_maturity = amount * premium * (1 + usd_rate * days / 360)
    return purchase + premium_at_maturity
```
hint: The firm buys at the lower of the spot rate and the strike.
hint: The premium is paid today, so grow it by `1 + usd_rate * days / 360`.
solution: Cost at maturity has two parts: the purchase of the currency at the lower of spot and strike, and the premium compounded forward at the home simple interest rate. Forgetting the interest on the premium or using the higher of spot and strike are the typical errors the tests catch.
