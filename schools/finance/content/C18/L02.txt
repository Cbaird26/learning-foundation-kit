@article
Every country must decide what its currency is worth against others, or let the market decide. That choice shapes inflation, interest rates, trade and the risk of crisis. This lesson covers the choices and the history behind them.

### The spectrum of regimes

An **exchange-rate regime** is the set of rules a country follows for its currency. From most flexible to most rigid:

- **Free (clean) float:** the market sets the rate; the central bank does not intervene as a matter of policy.
- **Managed (dirty) float:** the rate is market-determined, but the central bank intervenes now and then to smooth or steer it.
- **Crawling peg:** the rate is fixed against a currency or basket and adjusted in small, preannounced steps, often to offset an inflation gap.
- **Fixed (pegged) rate:** the central bank commits to a rate, perhaps within a band, and buys or sells foreign currency to defend it.
- **Currency board:** the domestic monetary base is fully backed by foreign reserves at a fixed rate, by law.
- **Dollarization or monetary union:** a country adopts another country's currency (dollarization) or members share one currency and one central bank (the euro area, whose currency was introduced in 1999).

### A short history

Under the **classical gold standard** (roughly 1870 to 1914), each currency was defined as a fixed weight of gold. That fixed every pair of rates: if one unit of currency A holds 0.5 gram of gold and one unit of B holds 0.25 gram, then **mint parity** is 1 A = 2 B. Rates could deviate from parity only by the cost of shipping gold.

After the Great Depression and the Second World War, the **Bretton Woods system** (agreed in 1944) was an **adjustable peg**: currencies were pegged to the US dollar within a band of 1% around par, and the dollar was convertible into gold for foreign central banks at a fixed price. The IMF was created to lend to members with temporary deficits. Strains grew, the United States suspended gold convertibility in August 1971, and major currencies began floating in 1973. Since then, the **post-Bretton Woods system** has been mixed: some major currencies float, some countries peg, and the euro area is a union.

### The impossible trinity

A country cannot have all three of these at once:

1. a fixed exchange rate,
2. free movement of capital, and
3. an independent monetary policy (setting its own interest rate).

If capital moves freely and the rate is fixed, the domestic interest rate must follow the foreign rate, or investors will move funds until it does. Pick two: a float with open capital markets keeps monetary independence; a currency board with open markets gives up monetary independence; capital controls allow a peg with some monetary independence.

### Intervention

To hold a peg, the central bank trades foreign currency. If the domestic currency is weak, it **sells foreign reserves and buys domestic currency**, which shrinks the domestic monetary base. This is **unsterilized intervention**: the base changes, so interest rates are affected. In **sterilized intervention** the central bank offsets the base change by buying domestic bonds, leaving the base unchanged. Sterilization can only last while reserves do.

All exchange-rate numbers in this course are written "price currency per base currency". A rate of 4.00 local per USD means one USD costs 4.00 units of local currency. All data are invented.

@example
title: A band around a peg
problem: A Bretton Woods style peg sets the par value at 4.00 local currency units per USD, with a permitted band of plus or minus 1%. What range of market rates is allowed, and what must the central bank do if the market rate reaches 4.04?
step: Lower limit: $4.00 \times 0.99 = 3.96$. Upper limit: $4.00 \times 1.01 = 4.04$. The permitted range is 3.96 to 4.04 local units per USD.
step: At 4.04, one USD costs the most local currency allowed, so the local currency is at its weakest permitted value.
step: To keep the rate from passing 4.04, the central bank must sell USD from its reserves and buy local currency, which pushes the local currency up.
answer: Band 3.96 to 4.04; at 4.04 the central bank must sell USD and buy local currency.

@example
title: Sterilizing an intervention
problem: To defend the peg, a central bank sells USD 5 billion at 4.00 local per USD. What happens to the monetary base if it does not sterilize, and what must it do to sterilize?
step: The local currency it receives is $5 \times 4.00 = 20$ billion local units, which leaves circulation, so the monetary base falls by 20 billion.
step: To sterilize, the bank buys 20 billion of local government bonds, injecting 20 billion back. The base is unchanged.
step: Reserves are still down by USD 5 billion, so sterilizing cannot continue forever.
answer: Unsterilized: base falls by 20 billion local units. Sterilized: buy 20 billion of bonds; reserves still fall by USD 5 billion.

@mistakes
- Believing a fixed rate and an independent interest rate are compatible when capital moves freely.
- Treating a "weak" currency as a high number in every quote: this depends on which currency is the base.
- Assuming the central bank can sterilize forever: sterilized intervention does not stop the loss of reserves.
- Confusing a currency board (a fixed rate with full reserve backing) with an ordinary peg that the central bank may abandon with discretion.
