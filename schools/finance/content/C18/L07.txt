@article
A firm is **exposed** to currency risk when a change in an exchange rate changes its value. There are three kinds, with different horizons, measures and cures.

### Transaction exposure

**Transaction exposure** is the risk that a contracted foreign-currency cash flow will be worth a different amount in the firm's home currency when it is settled. A US firm that will pay EUR 2,000,000 in 90 days has a **payable**; if the euro rises, it costs more dollars. A foreign-currency **receivable** loses dollar value when the foreign currency falls. The exposure is known in amount and date, and the gain or loss is realized in cash, so it is the easiest to hedge (lesson 8).

For an amount $A$ in foreign currency, the home-currency change is $A(S_1 - S_0)$, with $S$ in home currency per foreign currency. For a payable, a positive change is a loss; for a receivable, a positive change is a gain.

### Economic (operating) exposure

**Economic exposure**, or operating exposure, is the effect of exchange-rate changes on the present value of the firm's future operating cash flows. It includes uncontracted sales, costs and competitive position: a firm that produces at home and sells abroad loses competitiveness if the home currency rises, and a firm competing with imports can lose when the foreign currency falls. One way to estimate it is to regress cash flows (or firm value) on the exchange rate:

$$CF_t = a + b\,S_t + \varepsilon_t.$$

The slope $b$ is the **exposure coefficient**: the change in cash flow per one-unit change in the exchange rate, $b = \mathrm{Cov}(CF, S)/\mathrm{Var}(S)$. Economic exposure is long-lasting and cannot be hedged away with short-term contracts alone; it is managed by the location of production, the currency of costs and financing, and pricing.

### Translation exposure

**Translation exposure** arises when a parent consolidates the financial statements of foreign subsidiaries, and changes in rates change the reported value of foreign assets, liabilities, revenues and profits.

Under **US GAAP (ASC 830)** and **IFRS (IAS 21)**, if the subsidiary's functional currency is its local currency, the **current rate method** applies:

- assets and liabilities are translated at the **current (closing) rate**;
- revenues and expenses at the rates on the transaction dates, in practice an **average rate**;
- paid-in capital and prior retained earnings at **historical rates**;
- the balancing amount, the **translation adjustment**, goes to other comprehensive income, accumulating in equity as the **cumulative translation adjustment** (CTA). It does not affect net income until the subsidiary is sold or liquidated.

The **temporal method** (remeasurement) applies when the functional currency is the parent's currency, and under US GAAP when the subsidiary operates in a highly inflationary economy (cumulative inflation of roughly 100% or more over three years). Monetary assets and liabilities (cash, receivables, payables, debt) are remeasured at the current rate; nonmonetary items such as inventory at cost and fixed assets are kept at historical rates; and the resulting **remeasurement gain or loss goes to net income**.

Translation effects are accounting effects, not cash flows, yet they can change reported equity, leverage ratios and debt covenants. For a subsidiary with net assets $N$ (foreign currency), the CTA from rates alone is $N(S_{\text{end}} - S_{\text{begin}})$.

@example
title: A euro payable
problem: A US importer must pay EUR 2,000,000 in 90 days. The spot rate is EUR/USD = 1.1000 (USD per EUR). If the rate at settlement is 1.1400, what is the extra cost in USD? What if it is 1.0600?
step: Cost now: $2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1000 = \text{USD } 2{,}200{,}000$.
step: At 1.1400: $2{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1400 = 2{,}280{,}000$, so the loss is $2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.04 = \text{USD } 80{,}000$.
step: At 1.0600 the cost is $2{,}120{,}000$, a gain of USD 80,000. A payable gains when the foreign currency weakens and loses when it strengthens.
answer: Loss of USD 80,000 at 1.1400; gain of USD 80,000 at 1.0600.

@example
title: A translation adjustment
problem: A US parent has a euro-area subsidiary with net assets of EUR 10,000,000 at the beginning of the year (rate 1.1000 USD per EUR) and, ignoring income and dividends, the same net assets at year-end (rate 1.0600). Under the current rate method, what is the translation adjustment, and where does it appear?
step: Beginning net assets in USD: $10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.1000 = 11{,}000{,}000$.
step: Ending net assets in USD: $10{,}000{,}000 \times 1.0600 = 10{,}600{,}000$.
step: The CTA for the year is $10{,}600{,}000 - 11{,}000{,}000 = -\text{USD } 400{,}000$, equal to $10{,}000{,}000 \times (1.0600 - 1.1000)$.
step: It is a loss recorded in other comprehensive income and accumulated in equity, so reported equity falls although no cash is lost and net income is unaffected.
answer: CTA of -USD 400,000, in other comprehensive income.

@mistakes
- Confusing the sign for payables and receivables: a stronger foreign currency hurts a payer and helps a receiver.
- Saying translation gains and losses always hit net income: under the current rate method they go to OCI.
- Treating economic exposure as only the contracted cash flows: it covers uncontracted future sales, costs and competitive effects.
- Translating equity at the current rate: paid-in capital is carried at historical rates and the CTA is the balancing item.

choice*: Economic (operating) exposure, because future cash flows and competitiveness are affected

@practice num
prompt: A firm's annual cash flow (USD millions) and the average EUR/USD rate (USD per EUR) over five years were: rates 1.00, 1.05, 1.10, 1.15, 1.20 and cash flows 50, 54, 57, 63, 66. Using the least-squares regression of cash flow on the rate, by how many USD millions is the annual cash flow estimated to change if the rate rises by 0.05? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 4.1
tol: 0.01
check: statistics.linear_regression([1.00, 1.05, 1.10, 1.15, 1.20], [50, 54, 57, 63, 66]).slope * 0.05
hint: The slope of the regression is the exposure coefficient: millions of USD per 1.00 change in the rate.
solution: The slope is $b = \mathrm{Cov}(CF, S)/\mathrm{Var}(S) = 82$ USD millions per unit of the rate. A rise of 0.05 changes cash flow by $82 \times 0.05 = 4.10$ USD millions.

@practice num
prompt: A US parent's UK subsidiary (functional currency GBP) has opening net assets of GBP 18 million and earns net income of GBP 3 million for the year, with no dividends or other equity changes, so closing net assets are GBP 21 million. Rates in USD per GBP: 1.30 at the start, 1.27 average for the year, and 1.24 at the end. Under the current rate method, what is the cumulative translation adjustment for the year, in USD millions? Give it to two decimal places (negative for a loss).
answer: -1.17
tol: 0.01
check: 21 * 1.24 - 18 * 1.30 - 3 * 1.27
hint: Translate closing net assets at the closing rate, opening net assets at the opening rate and income at the average rate.
hint: The CTA is the amount that makes the opening net assets plus income plus CTA equal the closing net assets in USD.
solution: Closing net assets: $21 \times 1.24 = 26.04$. Opening net assets: $18 \times 1.30 = 23.40$. Net income: $3 \times 1.27 = 3.81$. The CTA is $26.04 - 23.40 - 3.81 = -1.17$ USD million, recorded in OCI.
