@article
If a coffee costs USD 4.00 in one city and EUR 3.00 in another, and the exchange rate is USD 1.3333 per EUR, the coffee costs the same in both places. If the rate were USD 1.50 per EUR, the coffee would cost USD 4.50 in the euro city, and you would buy coffee at home. **Purchasing power parity** (PPP) says that, in the long run, exchange rates adjust to remove such differences.

### The law of one price and absolute PPP

The **law of one price** says an identical, freely traded good has the same price in a common currency in every market, because arbitrage (buy cheap, sell dear) removes gaps. If the good costs $P_d$ in domestic currency and $P_f$ in foreign currency and $S$ is the price of one unit of foreign currency in domestic currency (for a US investor, USD per EUR), then $P_d = S P_f$. For a whole basket of goods this is **absolute PPP**:

$$S = \frac{P_d}{P_f}.$$

Here $P_d$ and $P_f$ are the costs of the same basket in each country. The PPP rate is the exchange rate that would make the basket cost the same. Comparing it with the actual rate gives a **misvaluation**: if the actual rate is $S$ and the PPP rate is $S^{*}$, the foreign currency is overvalued by $S/S^{*} - 1$ (negative: undervalued).

### Relative PPP

Price levels are hard to compare exactly, so economists use **relative PPP**: the exchange rate changes by the inflation difference. With domestic inflation $\pi_d$ and foreign inflation $\pi_f$ over one period,

$$\frac{S_1}{S_0} = \frac{1 + \pi_d}{1 + \pi_f}, \qquad \text{approximately } \frac{\Delta S}{S} \approx \pi_d - \pi_f.$$

The currency of the higher-inflation country is expected to lose value. The approximation is good for small rates; the exact form is used in this course.

### The real exchange rate

The **real exchange rate** compares the cost of the foreign basket with the domestic basket in a common currency:

$$q = \frac{S\,P_f}{P_d}.$$

With $S$ in domestic currency per foreign currency, a rise in $q$ means foreign goods have become more expensive relative to domestic goods: a **real appreciation** of the foreign currency (a real depreciation of the domestic one). Relative to a base period, $q_1/q_0 = (S_1/S_0)\,\dfrac{1 + \pi_f}{1 + \pi_d}$. If relative PPP holds exactly, $q$ is constant.

### Why PPP fails in the short run

Transport costs, tariffs and taxes, non-traded goods and services (haircuts, rent), differences in baskets and quality, and sticky prices all stop arbitrage. Richer countries tend to have higher prices for non-traded services, because productivity is higher in traded goods and raises wages everywhere (the **Balassa-Samuelson effect**), so PPP misvaluations are systematic. Empirically, deviations from PPP can be large and last for years but tend to shrink over long horizons.

### The international Fisher effect

The **Fisher equation** links a nominal rate $i$, expected inflation $E[\pi]$ and the real rate $r$: $1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + E[\pi])$. If real rates are equal across countries, then $\dfrac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f} = \dfrac{1 + E[\pi_d]}{1 + E[\pi_f]}$, so with relative PPP the expected change in the exchange rate is

$$\frac{E[S_1]}{S_0} = \frac{1 + i_d}{1 + i_f}.$$

This **international Fisher effect** (IFE) says the high-interest-rate currency is expected to depreciate. It matches the CIP forward rate of lesson 4 in form, but it is a statement about expectations that rests on assumptions (equal real rates, relative PPP), not about arbitrage.

All figures here are invented. For real data on prices and exchange rates use the OECD, the IMF or FRED.

@example
title: A PPP rate and a misvaluation
problem: A standard basket costs USD 80 in the United States and EUR 70 in the euro area. The actual rate is EUR/USD = 1.1000 (USD per EUR). What is the PPP rate, and is the euro over- or undervalued relative to PPP?
step: Absolute PPP: $S^{*} = P_d/P_f = 80/70 = 1.1429$ USD per EUR.
step: The actual rate is lower: $1.1000/1.1429 - 1 = -3.75\%$.
step: The euro is undervalued by 3.75% against the dollar: the basket costs USD 77.00 when bought in the euro area ($70 \times 1.1000$) but USD 80.00 at home.
answer: PPP rate 1.1429; the euro is undervalued by 3.75%.

@example
title: Relative PPP and a real exchange rate
problem: EUR/USD is 1.1000 today. Expected inflation is 3% a year in the United States and 1% in the euro area. (a) What does relative PPP predict for the rate in a year? (b) Suppose instead the rate rises from 1.1000 to 1.2000 while US prices rise 6% and euro-area prices 2%. By what percentage does the real exchange rate change?
step: (a) $S_1 = 1.1000 \times 1.03/1.01 = 1.1218$. The euro appreciates by 1.98% because US inflation is higher.
step: (b) $q_1/q_0 = (1.2000/1.1000) \times 1.02/1.06 = 1.0909 \times 0.9623 = 1.0497$.
step: The real exchange rate rises by 4.97%: euro-area goods have become about 5% more expensive relative to US goods, a real appreciation of the euro.
answer: (a) 1.1218; (b) +4.97% (a real appreciation of the euro).

@mistakes
- Using the wrong quote direction: absolute PPP is $S = P_d/P_f$ only when $S$ is domestic currency per unit of foreign currency.
- Assuming PPP holds every year: deviations are large and persistent.
- Mixing up nominal and real exchange rates: a nominal appreciation can still leave the real rate unchanged if inflation offsets it.
- Treating the international Fisher effect as a guaranteed return: it is a statement about expectations under assumptions.

@practice num
prompt: The exchange rate is 18.00 MXN per USD. Expected inflation is 6.0% in Mexico and 2.5% in the United States. By relative PPP, what is the expected rate in MXN per USD in one year? Give it to four decimal places.
answer: 18.6146
tol: 0.0001
check: 18.00 * 1.06 / 1.025
hint: The currency of the country with higher inflation loses value. Here MXN is the price currency.
solution: $S_1 = 18.00 \times 1.06/1.025 = 18.6146$ MXN per USD. The price of a dollar in pesos is expected to rise by about 3.4%: the peso is expected to weaken.

@quiz num
prompt: The one-year interest rate is 12% in Brazil and 4% in the United States, and the spot rate is 5.0000 BRL per USD. Assuming the international Fisher effect holds, what is the expected spot rate in a year (BRL per USD) to four decimal places?
answer: 5.3846
tol: 0.0001
check: 5.0000 * 1.12 / 1.04
hint: The expected change is the ratio of the two gross interest rates, with BRL as the price currency.
solution: $E[S_1] = 5.0000 \times 1.12/1.04 = 5.3846$ BRL per USD, a rise of about 7.7% in the price of a dollar, so the real is expected to lose about 7.1% of its value in dollars.
