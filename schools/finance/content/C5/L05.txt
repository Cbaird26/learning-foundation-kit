@article
Suppose you want the average daily return of a fund over its lifetime. You cannot observe the whole **population**, so you take a **sample** and compute a **statistic**, such as the sample mean $\bar{x}$, to estimate the **parameter**, the population mean $\mu$. A different sample would give a different $\bar{x}$. The **sampling distribution** of a statistic is its distribution over all the samples of a given size we could draw. It tells us how far a statistic typically lands from the parameter it estimates.

### The sampling distribution of the mean

Take independent observations $X_1, \dots, X_n$ from a population with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. Then the sample mean $\bar{X}$ has

$$E(\bar{X}) = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar{X}) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

The second quantity is the **standard error** of the mean, $SE = \sigma/\sqrt{n}$. The sample mean is centered at the right value (it is **unbiased**), and its spread shrinks as $n$ grows, but only like $1/\sqrt{n}$: to halve the standard error you need four times the data. In practice $\sigma$ is unknown, and we estimate the standard error by $s/\sqrt{n}$.

Be careful with two similar-sounding quantities. The **standard deviation** describes how individual observations vary; the **standard error** describes how a *statistic* varies from sample to sample.

### The central limit theorem

If the population is itself normal, $\bar{X}$ is exactly normal. The remarkable fact is that the same holds approximately for *any* population with finite variance. The **central limit theorem** (CLT) says that for large $n$,

$$\bar{X} \approx N\!\left(\mu, \frac{\sigma^2}{n}\right).$$

As a rule of thumb, $n \ge 30$ is enough for populations that are not very skewed; heavily skewed or fat-tailed populations need much larger samples. The CLT is why normal-based methods (confidence intervals and tests, next lessons) work for data that are not normal. It also needs independent observations, which is a real concern for time series with autocorrelation.

### The sampling distribution of a proportion

If each observation is a success with probability $p$, the sample proportion $\hat{p} = X/n$ (with $X$ the number of successes) has mean $p$ and standard error

$$SE(\hat{p}) = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.$$

For large $n$ (with $np$ and $n(1-p)$ at least about 10), $\hat{p}$ is approximately normal.

### In Python

```python
import random
import statistics

rng = random.Random(42)
population = [0] * 90 + [10] * 10          # skewed: mean 1
means = []
for _ in range(1000):
    sample = [rng.choice(population) for _ in range(25)]
    means.append(sum(sample) / 25)
print(statistics.mean(means))    # close to 1
print(statistics.stdev(means))   # close to 3 / 5 = 0.6
```

The population is far from normal, yet the 1000 sample means cluster symmetrically around 1 with a spread near $\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 3/5 = 0.6$.

@example
title: How far can an average of 36 days stray?
problem: Daily returns of a fund have mean 0.05% and standard deviation 1.2%. A 36-day sample is drawn. Find the standard error of the sample mean and the probability that the sample mean is below -0.30%.
step: The standard error is $SE = 1.2/\sqrt{36} = 1.2/6 = 0.20\%$.
step: By the CLT, $\bar{X} \approx N(0.05, 0.20^2)$. Standardize: $z = (-0.30 - 0.05)/0.20 = -1.75$.
step: $P(\bar{X} < -0.30) = \Phi(-1.75) = 0.0401$.
step: A single day falls below $-0.30\%$ with probability about 39%, but a 36-day average does so only 4% of the time, because averaging cancels noise.
answer: $SE = 0.20\%$; probability about 0.0401 (4.01%).

@example
title: A sample proportion
problem: A lender's long-run default rate is 8%. In a new batch of 400 loans, what is the probability that more than 10% default?
step: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.08(0.92)/400} = \sqrt{0.000184} = 0.013565$.
step: Check the conditions: $np = 32$ and $n(1-p) = 368$ are both well above 10, so the normal approximation applies.
step: $z = (0.10 - 0.08)/0.013565 = 1.474$.
step: $P(\hat{p} > 0.10) = 1 - \Phi(1.474) = 0.0702$.
answer: About 7.02%.

@mistakes
- Using $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ when finding a probability for a sample mean.
- Confusing the standard deviation of the data with the standard error of the mean.
- Believing that a bigger sample makes the *data* more normal; it makes the distribution of the *sample mean* closer to normal.
- Thinking that doubling the sample size halves the standard error; it divides it by $\sqrt{2}$.

model:
```python
import random


def simulate_means(population, n, reps, seed):
    """Sample means of n draws with replacement, repeated reps times."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    means = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        sample = [rng.choice(population) for _ in range(n)]
        means.append(sum(sample) / n)
    return means
```
hint: Create the random generator once, outside the loop over repetitions.
hint: Inside the loop, build a sample list of `n` draws and append `sum(sample) / n`.
solution: Seeding one generator makes the run repeatable. Each repetition draws `n` values with replacement and records their mean. The tests check properties, not specific random values: the number of means, their range, that their average is near the population mean 1, and that their spread is near $\sigma/\sqrt{n} = 0.6$ and much smaller than the population standard deviation of 3.

model:
```python
import math
import statistics


def standard_error(values):
    """Estimated standard error of the sample mean."""
    n = len(values)
    if n < 2:
        raise ValueError("need at least two values")
    return statistics.stdev(values) / math.sqrt(n)
```
hint: The sample standard deviation is `statistics.stdev(values)`.
hint: Divide it by `math.sqrt(len(values))`.
solution: The estimated standard error is $s/\sqrt{n}$. For `[2, 4]`, $s = \sqrt{2}$ and $n = 2$, so the standard error is exactly 1.
