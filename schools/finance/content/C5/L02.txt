@article
**Probability** measures how likely an event is, on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). It is the language of risk: the chance a borrower defaults, a trade fails, or a stock falls on a given day. We write $P(A)$ for the probability of event $A$.

### Basic rules

For events $A$ and $B$ defined on the same set of outcomes:

- **Complement:** $P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)$.
- **Addition rule:** $P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)$. We subtract the overlap so outcomes in both events are not counted twice. If $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive** (cannot both happen), $P(A \cap B) = 0$ and the rule reduces to $P(A)+P(B)$.
- **Conditional probability:** $P(A \mid B) = \dfrac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}$ for $P(B) > 0$, the probability of $A$ once we know $B$ occurred.
- **Multiplication rule:** $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A)$.

### Independence

Events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if knowing one occurred does not change the probability of the other: $P(A \mid B) = P(A)$, equivalently $P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B)$. Do not confuse independence with mutual exclusivity: two events with positive probability that cannot both happen are in fact strongly dependent, since if one occurs the other cannot. Sampling without replacement creates dependence: drawing 2 bad loans in a row from 10 loans of which 3 are bad has probability $\frac{3}{10}\cdot\frac{2}{9} = \frac{1}{15}$, not $(3/10)^2$.

### Total probability and Bayes' rule

If events $B_1, \dots, B_k$ are mutually exclusive and cover all possibilities (a **partition**), then for any event $A$,

$$P(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i).$$

**Bayes' rule** reverses a conditional probability by combining the multiplication rule with total probability:

$$P(B_j \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}{\sum_{i} P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}.$$

Here $P(B_j)$ is the **prior** probability, $P(A \mid B_j)$ the **likelihood** of the evidence, and $P(B_j \mid A)$ the **posterior**. For a test with **sensitivity** $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{condition})$ and **false-positive rate** $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{no condition})$, a rare condition means most flags are false alarms, even for an accurate test. This is the **base-rate effect**: ignoring the prior leads people to confuse $P(\text{flag} \mid \text{condition})$ with $P(\text{condition} \mid \text{flag})$.

### In Python

```python
def posterior(prior, sensitivity, false_positive_rate):
    flagged = sensitivity * prior + false_positive_rate * (1 - prior)
    return sensitivity * prior / flagged

print(round(posterior(0.04, 0.90, 0.08), 4))   # 0.3191
```

The denominator is the law of total probability; the numerator is one branch of it.

@example
title: A credit screen
problem: Bellwether Credit estimates that 4% of its applicants will default. Its screening model flags 90% of applicants who will default and, wrongly, 8% of those who will not. An applicant is flagged. What is the probability this applicant will default?
step: Define $D$ = default and $F$ = flagged. We know $P(D) = 0.04$, $P(F \mid D) = 0.90$ and $P(F \mid D^c) = 0.08$.
step: Total probability gives the chance of a flag: $P(F) = 0.90(0.04) + 0.08(0.96) = 0.0360 + 0.0768 = 0.1128$.
step: Bayes' rule: $P(D \mid F) = 0.0360/0.1128 = 0.3191$.
step: Even with a 90% sensitive model, only about 32% of flagged applicants default, because defaulters are rare and most flags come from the much larger group of good borrowers.
answer: $P(D \mid F) \approx 0.3191$, or 31.91%.

@example
title: Reading a contingency table
problem: Of 500 borrowers, 400 are employed and 100 are unemployed. Among the employed, 12 default; among the unemployed, 18 default. Find $P(\text{default})$, $P(\text{default} \mid \text{unemployed})$, $P(\text{unemployed or default})$ and decide whether employment status and default are independent.
step: Total defaults are $12 + 18 = 30$, so $P(\text{default}) = 30/500 = 0.06$.
step: Among the unemployed, $P(\text{default} \mid \text{unemployed}) = 18/100 = 0.18$; among the employed it is $12/400 = 0.03$.
step: By the addition rule, $P(U \cup D) = P(U) + P(D) - P(U \cap D) = 0.20 + 0.06 - 18/500 = 0.20 + 0.06 - 0.036 = 0.224$. Check by counting: $100 + 12 = 112$ borrowers, and $112/500 = 0.224$.
step: Independence would need $P(D \mid U) = P(D)$, but $0.18 \ne 0.06$. The events are dependent: being unemployed raises the default probability.
answer: 0.06; 0.18; 0.224; not independent.

@mistakes
- Subtracting nothing when adding probabilities of overlapping events, which counts the overlap twice.
- Treating mutually exclusive events as independent (or the reverse): exclusive events with positive probabilities are always dependent.
- Confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$, for example reading "90% of defaulters are flagged" as "90% of flagged applicants default."
- Ignoring the prior (the base rate) in Bayes' rule, and forgetting to include the false positives from the large "no condition" group in the denominator.

```python
def posterior(prior, sensitivity, false_positive_rate):
    """P(condition | flag) by Bayes' rule."""
    p_flag = sensitivity * prior + false_positive_rate * (1 - prior)
    return sensitivity * prior / p_flag
```
hint: First compute the overall probability of a flag with the law of total probability.
hint: The numerator is `sensitivity * prior`; the denominator is the total probability of a flag.
solution: The probability of a flag adds the true-positive branch `sensitivity * prior` and the false-positive branch `false_positive_rate * (1 - prior)`. Dividing the true-positive branch by that total gives the posterior. A prior of 0 gives 0 and a prior of 1 gives 1.

prompt: Supplier A provides 60% of a manufacturer's parts and 2% of its parts are defective. Supplier B provides the other 40% and 5% of its parts are defective. A part is found defective. What is the probability it came from Supplier A? Give a percent to one decimal place.

unit: %
