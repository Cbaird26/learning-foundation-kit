### Week by week

- **Week 1, describing data:** mean, median, sample standard deviation (divisor $n-1$), coefficient of variation, quartiles and IQR; skewness (right skew pulls the mean above the median); geometric versus arithmetic mean of returns.
- **Week 2, probability:** complement, addition and multiplication rules; conditional probability and independence; total probability and Bayes' rule (the base-rate effect).
- **Week 3, discrete distributions:** binomial (fixed $n$, independent trials, constant $p$) and Poisson (counts per interval); means and variances.
- **Week 4, the normal distribution:** z-scores, probabilities and percentiles, the normal approximation to the binomial with a continuity correction, fat tails.
- **Week 5, sampling distributions:** standard error, the central limit theorem, sample proportions.
- **Week 6, confidence intervals:** estimate $\pm$ critical value $\times$ standard error; z versus t; interpretation; sample size.
- **Week 7, hypothesis tests:** hypotheses, test statistics, p-values, Type I and II errors, power; failing to reject is not proof.
- **Week 8, two groups:** Welch, paired and two-proportion tests.
- **Week 9, simple regression:** covariance, correlation, least squares, $R^2$, inference on the slope, beta.
- **Week 10, multiple regression:** partial slopes, t and F tests, adjusted $R^2$, dummy variables.
- **Week 11, diagnostics:** residuals, heteroskedasticity, autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson), multicollinearity (VIF), leverage and influence.
- **Week 12, decisions:** expected value, sample size for an A/B test, multiple testing and Bonferroni, statistical versus practical significance.

### Key formulas

$$s^2 = \frac{\sum(x_i-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}, \qquad P(B\mid A) = \frac{P(A\mid B)P(B)}{P(A)}, \qquad z = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}, \qquad SE_{\bar{x}} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}$$

$$\bar{x} \pm t^*\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad t = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}, \qquad b = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}}, \qquad \bar{R}^2 = 1-(1-R^2)\frac{n-1}{n-k-1}, \qquad VIF_j = \frac{1}{1-R_j^2}$$

Binomial: $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, mean $np$, variance $np(1-p)$. Poisson: $e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k/k!$, mean and variance $\lambda$.

### Most common errors

- Dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$; using $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ for a sample mean.
- Confusing $P(A\mid B)$ with $P(B\mid A)$, and ignoring base rates.
- Saying "95% probability the parameter is in this interval" or "the p-value is the probability the null is true."
- Using an independent-samples test on paired data.
- Reading correlation as causation, and $R^2$ as proof the model is right.
- Ignoring multiple testing and outliers, and treating statistical significance as practical importance.

Use the normal approximation for the sample proportion without a continuity correction, and give a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 0.95
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1 - NormalDist().cdf((0.05 - 0.03) / sqrt(0.03 * 0.97 / 400))) * 100
hint: The standard error of the sample proportion is $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$ with $p = 0.03$.
hint: Standardize $0.05$ against the mean $0.03$ using that standard error, then take the upper-tail probability.
solution: $SE = \sqrt{0.03(0.97)/400} = 0.008530$, so $z = (0.05 - 0.03)/0.008530 = 2.345$ and $P(\hat{p} > 0.05) = 1 - \Phi(2.345) = 0.0095$, or 0.95%. (The exact binomial probability of 21 or more defaults, which is the event $\hat{p} > 0.05$ for 400 loans, is larger, about 1.05%, because the binomial is right-skewed when $p$ is small.)

model:
```python
import math


def beta_summary(stock, market):
    """Beta, its standard error, t against 1, and Durbin-Watson."""
    n = len(market)
    if n != len(stock) or n < 3:
        raise ValueError("need equal-length lists with at least three points")
    mean_x = sum(market) / n
    mean_y = sum(stock) / n
    sxx = sum((x - mean_x) ** 2 for x in market)
    if sxx == 0:
        raise ValueError("market returns are constant")
    sxy = sum((x - mean_x) * (y - mean_y) for x, y in zip(market, stock))
    beta = sxy / sxx
    alpha = mean_y - beta * mean_x
    e = [y - (alpha + beta * x) for x, y in zip(market, stock)]
    sse = sum(v * v for v in e)
    se_beta = math.sqrt(sse / (n - 2)) / math.sqrt(sxx)
    dw = sum((e[t] - e[t - 1]) ** 2 for t in range(1, n)) / sse
    return beta, se_beta, (beta - 1) / se_beta, dw
```
hint: Start with the slope and intercept, then the residuals; everything else comes from the residuals.
hint: The standard error of the slope needs $SSE/(n-2)$ and $S_{xx}$; the Durbin-Watson statistic needs the residuals in order.
solution: Fit the line, form the residuals, and use the sum of squared residuals twice: for the standard error of beta (with $n-2$ degrees of freedom) and as the denominator of the Durbin-Watson statistic. The t statistic tests beta against 1 rather than 0, because beta equal to 1 means the stock moves one for one with the market.

prompt: A student regresses a stock's 36 monthly returns on a market index and reports: beta = 1.23, standard error 0.09, $R^2 = 0.86$, 95% confidence interval for beta (1.05, 1.41), Durbin-Watson 1.9. The residual plot shows no pattern, except that one month, a market drop of nearly 15%, has leverage more than three times the average, and removing it changes beta to 1.15 with a standard error of 0.12. Write a short conclusion (five to eight sentences) that says what the regression shows about the stock's market sensitivity, tests whether beta differs from 1 (the critical value is $t^* = 2.032$), interprets the influence of the extreme month, and states two limitations.
solution: The estimated beta of 1.23 says the stock moved about 1.23% for each 1% move in the market over these 36 months, and the market explains 86% of the variation in its returns. To test $H_0: \beta = 1$, $t = (1.23 - 1)/0.09 = 2.56$, which exceeds 2.032, so we reject at the 5% level; this agrees with the interval (1.05, 1.41), which excludes 1. The Durbin-Watson value of 1.9 and the patternless residuals give no sign of autocorrelation or changing variance, so the standard errors look trustworthy. However, one extreme month has high leverage: without it beta falls to 1.15 and the standard error rises to 0.12, giving $t = 0.15/0.12 = 1.25$, which is not significant. The conclusion that the stock is more sensitive than the market therefore depends on a single crash month. Limitations: three years of monthly data is a short sample, and a beta estimated in one period need not hold in another; the result also describes association with the market in this sample and is not advice about any investment.
