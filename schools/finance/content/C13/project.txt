You will choose five to eight bonds from a supplied list, price them, compute the portfolio's duration and convexity, build an immunized portfolio and test it under three interest-rate scenarios in Python. You then write a two-page report on how the portfolio performed and what risks remain.
goal: Apply bond pricing, duration and convexity to a real design problem.
goal: Build and test an immunized portfolio against a liability under parallel and non-parallel yield shifts.
goal: Communicate assumptions, results and limits of the strategy in a short professional report.
step: Read the liability and the market data in the materials. Write down the conventions: semiannual coupons, par 100, yields are bond-equivalent yields, every bond is priced on a coupon date, and the valuation date is today.
step: In Python (or a spreadsheet), write functions to price each bond from its yield, and to compute its Macaulay duration, modified duration and convexity. Check each function on a zero-coupon bond and on a par bond, where you can predict the answers.
step: Compute the present value of the liability at the stated discount rate, and its duration and convexity. This is the amount your portfolio must be worth today.
step: Choose five to eight bonds and weights (by market value) so that the portfolio's present value equals the liability's, its Macaulay duration (market-value-weighted average of the bonds' durations) equals the liability's duration and its convexity is at least as large as the liability's. Try both a bullet (bonds near seven years) and a barbell (short and long bonds) and keep one.
step: Compute the number of par bonds you buy of each issue (rounded to whole units of par 1,000) and the cash left over.
step: Test the portfolio under the three scenarios in the materials. For each, reprice every bond and the liability and report the portfolio value, the liability value, the surplus (portfolio minus liability) and the percentage price change predicted by duration alone and by duration plus convexity.
step: Compare your barbell and bullet candidates: which has the larger surplus under each scenario, and why? Discuss which scenario hurt most and explain how the non-parallel scenario violates the assumptions of classical immunization.
step: Write the two-page report (see the deliverables), with one table of the portfolio and one table of scenario results.

### Week by week

1. **Features and markets.** Par, coupon, maturity, seniority, covenants, embedded options. Clean price $+$ accrued interest $=$ full price; Treasury quotes in 32nds; day counts (30/360, actual/actual).
2. **Pricing and yields.** $P = \sum C/(1+y/m)^t + F/(1+y/m)^N$; premium, discount, pull to par; YTM as an internal rate of return; BEY versus effective annual yield; bill discount yield versus bond-equivalent yield.
3. **Spot and forward rates.** Price with $\sum CF_t/(1+z_t)^t$; $(1+z_b)^b = (1+z_a)^a(1+f)^{b-a}$; bootstrap $Z_N = (1 - c\sum Z_t)/(1+c)$.
4. **Term structure.** Pure expectations, liquidity preference, segmentation, preferred habitat; liquidity premium $=$ forward $-$ expected spot; rolldown return.
5. **Duration.** $D_{\text{Mod}} = D_{\text{Mac}}/(1+y/m)$; $\Delta P/P \approx -D_{\text{Mod}}\Delta y$; effective duration, DV01; portfolio duration is value-weighted.
6. **Convexity and immunization.** $\Delta P/P \approx -D\Delta y + \tfrac12 C\Delta y^2$; match PV and duration, convexity at least as large; rebalance.
7. **Credit risk.** Expected loss $=$ PD $\times$ (1 $-$ recovery) $\times$ exposure; ratings; priority of claims; coverage and leverage ratios.
8. **Spreads and default probabilities.** Z-spread; credit triangle $s \approx \lambda(1-R)$; $\text{PD}(0,t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$; risk-neutral versus real-world.
9. **Embedded options.** $V_{\text{callable}} = V_{\text{straight}} - V_{\text{call}}$; YTC, YTW; negative convexity; convertible conversion value, premium and minimum value.
10. **Trees and OAS.** Backward induction with the call cap $\min(V, K)$; OAS is the spread added to every node; $\text{OAS} \approx \text{Z-spread} - \text{option cost}$.
11. **MBS and ABS.** $\text{PMT} = B\,r/(1-(1+r)^{-n})$; $\text{SMM} = 1-(1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}$; PSA ramp; contraction and extension risk; tranches; credit enhancement.
12. **Strategies.** Indexing, active positioning, cash-flow matching, immunization, contingent immunization; hedge face $=$ DV01 ratio; leverage $r_E = r_A + (B/E)(r_A - r_B)$.

### Common errors

Using the annual rate as the periodic rate; forgetting accrued interest; using Macaulay duration in the price-change formula; dropping the $\tfrac12$ in the convexity term; subtracting spreads from the wrong curve; adding the call option's value to a callable bond; applying the SMM to the balance before scheduled principal; and hedging with equal face values rather than equal DV01.

prompt: A 12-year bond pays a 4% coupon semiannually (par 100) and has a yield of 5.00% (bond-equivalent yield). Using its modified duration and convexity (both computed from its cash flows at that yield, with convexity in years squared), estimate its price if the yield rises by 80 basis points. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 84.60
tol: 0.02
check: bond_price(100, 0.04, 0.05, 12) * (1 - mod_duration(100, 0.04, 0.05, 12) * 0.008 + 0.5 * (sum(t * (t + 1) * (2 + (100 if t == 24 else 0)) / 1.025 ** (t + 2) for t in range(1, 25)) / (bond_price(100, 0.04, 0.05, 12) * 4)) * 0.008 ** 2)
hint: Compute the price (91.0575), the modified duration (9.278) and the convexity (104.13).
hint: $\Delta P/P \approx -D_{\text{Mod}}\Delta y + \tfrac12 C(\Delta y)^2$ with $\Delta y = 0.008$; multiply the price by $1 + \Delta P/P$.
solution: With $P = 91.0575$, $D_{\text{Mod}} = 9.278$ and $C = 104.13$: $\Delta P/P \approx -7.423\% + 0.333\% = -7.089\%$, so the price is about $91.0575 \times 0.92911 = 84.60$. The exact price at 5.80% is 84.59.

(b) Explain in two or three sentences why using this risk-neutral default probability would likely overstate the expected loss an investor should actually plan for, and name one other risk the spread compensates for.
hint: The credit triangle gives $\lambda = s/(1-R)$; PD $= 1 - e^{-5\lambda}$.
solution: (a) $\lambda = 0.018/0.60 = 3.0\%$ a year. The 5-year default probability is $1 - e^{-0.15} = 13.93\%$. The expected loss is $\$8{,}000{,}000 \times 0.1393 \times 0.60 = \$668{,}602$. (b) The spread contains compensation for more than expected default losses: investors demand a premium for bearing default risk (risk aversion) and for illiquidity, so the default probability backed out of the spread is a risk-neutral probability that exceeds the real-world (historical) probability. Using it overstates the loss to plan for, and the spread also pays for liquidity risk and for spread (mark-to-market) risk.
