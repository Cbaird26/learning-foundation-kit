@article
Two bonds with the same yield can react very differently to a rate change: a 2-year bond barely moves while a 30-year bond swings. **Duration** measures that sensitivity in a single number, and it is the most used risk measure in fixed income.

**Conventions.** Coupons are semiannual, yields are bond-equivalent yields (periodic yield $i = y/2$), prices are per 100 of par, and valuation is on a coupon date. Durations are stated in years.

### Macaulay duration

The **Macaulay duration** is the present-value-weighted average time to the bond's cash flows. With $N$ periods, cash flows $CF_t$, price $P$ and $m$ periods a year:

$$D_{\text{Mac}} = \frac{1}{m}\cdot\frac{\sum_{t=1}^{N} t\, \dfrac{CF_t}{(1+i)^t}}{P}.$$

Think of the bond as balancing on a seesaw: the cash flows' present values are weights placed at times $t/m$, and the duration is the balance point. A zero-coupon bond has all its weight at maturity, so its duration equals its maturity. A coupon bond's duration is shorter than its maturity.

### Modified duration and price changes

Differentiating the price with respect to the yield shows

$$D_{\text{Mod}} = \frac{D_{\text{Mac}}}{1 + y/m}, \qquad \frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{Mod}}\,\Delta y.$$

A modified duration of 5 means a 1 percentage point rise in yield lowers the price by about 5%. This is a first-order (linear) estimate that is accurate for small $\Delta y$ and understates the price after a fall in yield and overstates the loss after a rise, because the price-yield curve is convex (next lesson).

### Effective duration and DV01

Bonds with embedded options or uncertain cash flows have no fixed cash flows to weight, so duration is measured by repricing. With prices $P_-$ and $P_+$ after yield moves of $-\Delta y$ and $+\Delta y$ (a small parallel shift of the curve):

$$D_{\text{Eff}} = \frac{P_- - P_+}{2\,P_0\,\Delta y}.$$

The **DV01** (also PVBP, price value of a basis point) is the price change for a 1 basis point (0.01%) move: $\text{DV01} \approx D_{\text{Mod}} \times P \times 0.0001$. Multiplied by the position's par amount over 100, it is the dollar loss per basis point, which is how trading desks measure risk.

### What drives duration

For a given yield, duration rises with **maturity**; falls as the **coupon** rises (more cash arrives earlier); and rises as the **yield** falls (distant cash flows are discounted less). A floating-rate note reprices at each reset, so its duration is about the time until the next reset.

### Portfolio duration

A portfolio's modified (or effective) duration is the **market-value-weighted average** of its bonds' durations, $D_p = \sum w_j D_j$. This assumes every yield moves by the same amount, a parallel shift. Weighting by market value, not par, matters: use prices, including accrued interest.

@example
title: Macaulay and modified duration of a 3-year bond
problem: A 3-year bond pays a 5% coupon semiannually. Its yield to maturity is 6% (BEY). Find its price, Macaulay duration and modified duration, and estimate its price change if the yield rises by 50 basis points.
step: Each period has $i = 3\%$ and a coupon of 2.50. Discount each cash flow and multiply by its period number $t$.
step: The six rows give present values 2.4272, 2.3565, 2.2879, 2.2212, 2.1565 and 85.8421 (the last includes par), totaling the price $P = 97.2914$. The values $t \times PV$ are 2.4272, 4.7130, 6.8636, 8.8849, 10.7826 and 515.0528, totaling 548.7241.
step: $D_{\text{Mac}} = 548.7241/97.2914/2 = 2.8200$ years, because $548.7241/97.2914 = 5.640$ periods. Then $D_{\text{Mod}} = 2.8200/1.03 = 2.7379$.
step: For $\Delta y = +0.50\%$: $\Delta P/P \approx -2.7379 \times 0.005 = -1.369\%$, about $-1.33$ in price, to 95.96. The exact price at 6.5% is 95.97, a change of $-1.358\%$.
answer: $P = 97.2914$, $D_{\text{Mac}} = 2.820$ years, $D_{\text{Mod}} = 2.738$; the estimated change is $-1.369\%$ (exact $-1.358\%$).

@example
title: Duration of a portfolio and DV01
problem: A portfolio is 40% in a bond with modified duration 2.1, 35% in a bond with duration 5.4 and 25% in a bond with duration 9.8 (market-value weights). Find the portfolio's duration, and its approximate price change if all yields rise by 25 basis points.
step: $D_p = 0.40(2.1) + 0.35(5.4) + 0.25(9.8) = 0.84 + 1.89 + 2.45 = 5.18$ years.
step: $\Delta V/V \approx -5.18 \times 0.0025 = -1.295\%$. On a \$10 million portfolio that is about a \$129,500 loss.
step: The portfolio's DV01 is $5.18 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.0001 = \$5{,}180$ per basis point.
answer: Duration 5.18 years; about $-1.295\%$ (\$129,500 on \$10 million); DV01 \$5,180.

@mistakes
- Using Macaulay duration in $\Delta P/P \approx -D\,\Delta y$: the price estimate needs modified duration.
- Entering the yield change in percent (0.50) rather than decimal (0.005), or the reverse in the formula.
- Dividing $\sum t\,PV$ by the price but forgetting to convert periods to years for a semiannual bond.
- Weighting portfolio duration by par value instead of market value, or applying it to non-parallel yield changes.

model:
```python
def macaulay_duration(par, coupon_rate, ytm, years, freq=2):
    """Macaulay duration (in years) of a fixed-rate bond on a coupon date."""
    periods = round(years * freq)
    coupon = par * coupon_rate / freq
    i = ytm / freq
    price = 0.0
    weighted = 0.0
    for t in range(1, periods + 1):
        cash_flow = coupon + (par if t == periods else 0.0)
        present_value = cash_flow / (1 + i) ** t
        price += present_value
        weighted += t * present_value
    return weighted / price / freq
```
hint: Compute each cash flow's present value, and accumulate both the price and the sum of `t * present_value`.
hint: Divide the weighted sum by the price, then by `freq` to convert periods to years.
solution: The duration is the present-value-weighted average of the times of the cash flows. Counting time in periods and dividing by `freq` at the end gives years. A zero-coupon bond returns its maturity, and a higher coupon returns a shorter duration, which the last test checks.
