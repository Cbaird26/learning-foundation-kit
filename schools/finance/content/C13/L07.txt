@article
A Treasury bond promises cash flows the government can always create in its own currency. A corporate bond's promises depend on the issuer's ability and willingness to pay. **Credit risk** is the risk of loss because a borrower fails to make a payment on time and in full.

### Components of credit risk

- **Probability of default (PD)**: the chance the issuer defaults over a horizon, often a year.
- **Exposure at default (EAD)**: the amount owed when default occurs (for a bond, principal plus accrued interest).
- **Recovery rate (RR)**: the fraction of exposure recovered after default. **Loss given default** is $\text{LGD} = 1 - RR$.
- **Expected loss** $= \text{PD} \times \text{LGD} \times \text{EAD}$.

Two other forms of credit risk arise before default: **downgrade risk** (a rating cut raises the required yield and lowers the price) and **spread risk** (credit spreads widen with no change in the issuer's fundamentals). Expected loss is a statistical average; **unexpected loss** is the variability around it, and credit spreads compensate for both (and for illiquidity).

If the annual PD is a constant $p$ and defaults are independent across years (a simplification), the cumulative default probability over $T$ years is $1 - (1-p)^T$.

### Credit ratings

Rating agencies (S&P Global Ratings, Moody's and Fitch are the best known; several are registered with the SEC as nationally recognized statistical rating organizations) assign letter grades that summarize relative creditworthiness: ordinal rankings of default risk and (for some) loss severity, not guarantees or price recommendations.

| Quality | S&P and Fitch | Moody's |
|---|---|---|
| Highest | AAA | Aaa |
| High | AA+, AA, AA− | Aa1, Aa2, Aa3 |
| Upper medium | A+, A, A− | A1, A2, A3 |
| Lower medium (lowest investment grade) | BBB+, BBB, BBB− | Baa1, Baa2, Baa3 |
| Speculative (high yield) | BB+ and below | Ba1 and below |
| Very speculative or default | CCC and below, D | Caa and below, C |

The line between **investment grade** (BBB−/Baa3 and above) and **high yield** (BB+/Ba1 and below) matters because many institutional investors may hold only investment-grade bonds. A downgrade across the line can force selling.

### The four Cs and credit ratios

Analysts examine **capacity** (cash-flow ability to pay), **collateral** (assets pledged), **covenants** and **character** (management and governance). Typical ratios: **interest coverage** $= \text{EBITDA}/\text{interest expense}$; **leverage** $= \text{debt}/\text{EBITDA}$ (or net debt $=$ debt $-$ cash); and cash-flow measures such as funds from operations to debt. Coverage falling and leverage rising signal weaker credit.

### Priority of claims and recovery

In bankruptcy the **absolute priority rule** (usually respected in liquidations, less strictly in reorganizations) pays claims in order: secured creditors from their collateral, then senior unsecured, subordinated, and finally equity. Recovery therefore depends on seniority: senior secured bonds recover most and subordinated bonds least. If distributable value is $V$ and the senior claims total $S$, a junior class with claims $J$ recovers $\min(1, \max(0, V - S)/J)$.

### Structural and reduced-form models

**Structural models** (Merton) treat equity as a call option on the firm's assets: default occurs if asset value falls below the debt at maturity. **Reduced-form models** treat default as a random event arriving at a **hazard rate** and calibrate it to bond prices. Week 8 uses the reduced-form idea.

@example
title: Expected loss on a loan
problem: A bond portfolio has \$10 million exposed to an issuer with a one-year default probability of 1.8%. The expected recovery rate is 40%. Find the one-year expected loss. Then find the cumulative PD over 3 years if the annual PD is 2% each year, independently, and the expected loss on \$20 million with an LGD of 55%.
step: $\text{LGD} = 1 - 0.40 = 60\%$, so the one-year expected loss is $0.018 \times 0.60 \times \$10{,}000{,}000 = \$108{,}000$.
step: Survival for 3 years is $0.98^3 = 0.941192$, so the cumulative PD is $1 - 0.941192 = 5.8808\%$.
step: Expected loss over three years: $\$20{,}000{,}000 \times 0.058808 \times 0.55 = \$646{,}888$.
answer: \$108,000; cumulative PD 5.8808%; expected loss \$646,888.

@example
title: Credit ratios and recovery by seniority
problem: Larkspur Outfitters (a fictional issuer) has EBITDA of \$180 million, interest expense of \$30 million, debt of \$540 million and cash of \$60 million. If it is liquidated, distributable value is \$300 million against secured claims of \$150 million, senior unsecured claims of \$250 million and subordinated claims of \$100 million. Find its coverage, leverage and net leverage, and the recoveries.
step: Interest coverage is $180/30 = 6.0$ times; leverage is $540/180 = 3.0$ times; net leverage is $(540 - 60)/180 = 2.67$ times.
step: Secured creditors take the first \$150 million, so \$150 million remains for the senior unsecured creditors, who have claims of \$250 million: $150/250 = 60\%$ recovery.
step: Nothing remains for the subordinated claims (and equity): their recovery is 0%.
answer: Coverage 6.0x, leverage 3.0x, net leverage 2.67x; recoveries: secured 100%, senior unsecured 60%, subordinated 0%.

@mistakes
- Treating the recovery rate as the loss: the loss is $1 - RR$ times the exposure times PD.
- Multiplying annual PDs by the number of years instead of compounding survival, $1-(1-p)^T$.
- Believing a rating is a guarantee or a price opinion: it ranks relative default risk and changes over time.
- Giving junior creditors a share of value before senior creditors are paid in full.

model:
```python
def expected_loss(exposure, annual_pd, recovery_rate, years):
    """Expected credit loss over a horizon with a constant annual PD."""
    cumulative_pd = 1 - (1 - annual_pd) ** years
    return exposure * cumulative_pd * (1 - recovery_rate)
```
hint: First compute the probability of defaulting at some point within `years`.
hint: Multiply the exposure by that probability and by `1 - recovery_rate`.
solution: Compounding the survival probability, $(1-p)^T$, and subtracting from 1 gives the cumulative PD. The loss on default is exposure times LGD, so multiply by the cumulative PD. Full recovery and zero PD both give zero expected loss.
