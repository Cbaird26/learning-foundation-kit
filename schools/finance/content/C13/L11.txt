@article
A bank that makes a mortgage does not have to keep it. **Securitization** pools loans, transfers them to a **special purpose vehicle (SPV)** (a bankruptcy-remote legal entity) and sells securities backed by the pool's cash flows. The result is **mortgage-backed securities (MBS)** for mortgages and **asset-backed securities (ABS)** for other receivables (auto loans, credit card balances, student loans, equipment leases).

**Conventions.** Mortgages are fixed-rate, level-payment, monthly pay. Rates are annual nominal rates, divided by 12 for a month. Pool figures are in dollars. The **WAC** is the pool's weighted-average coupon (what borrowers pay), the **WAM** its weighted-average remaining maturity in months, and the **net (pass-through) rate** is the WAC less servicing and guarantee fees. All figures are hypothetical.

### Mortgage cash flows

A fixed-rate mortgage with balance $B$, monthly rate $r$ and $n$ months left has the level payment

$$\text{PMT} = B\,\frac{r}{1-(1+r)^{-n}}.$$

In each month, interest is $B r$, **scheduled principal** is $\text{PMT} - Br$, and the new balance is $B - $ scheduled principal. The borrower may also pay early (a **prepayment**, from refinancing, a home sale or default recoveries). In an **agency pass-through** (issued or guaranteed by Ginnie Mae, Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac; Ginnie Mae securities carry the full faith and credit of the US government, and Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac were placed in federal conservatorship in September 2008) investors receive their share of the pool's **net interest**, scheduled principal and prepayments each month.

### Measuring prepayments

The **single monthly mortality rate (SMM)** is the fraction of the month's remaining balance (after scheduled principal) that prepays: $\text{prepayment} = \text{SMM} \times (B - \text{scheduled principal})$. The **conditional prepayment rate (CPR)** is the same thing annualized:

$$\text{CPR} = 1 - (1 - \text{SMM})^{12}, \qquad \text{SMM} = 1 - (1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}.$$

The **PSA benchmark** (introduced by the Public Securities Association, whose role passed to what is now SIFMA) is a standard prepayment ramp. At 100 PSA, the CPR is $0.2\%$ in month 1, rising $0.2\%$ each month to $6\%$ in month 30, then constant at 6%. For $x$ PSA multiply by $x/100$:

$$\text{CPR}(m) = 0.06\cdot\frac{\min(m, 30)}{30}\cdot\frac{x}{100}.$$

So 200 PSA reaches 12% in month 30. Prepayments speed up when mortgage rates fall below pool rates (borrowers refinance) and slow when rates rise.

### Prepayment risk and tranching

Because prepayments rise when rates fall, MBS investors suffer **contraction risk** (principal returned early, to be reinvested at lower rates, and price appreciation is limited: negative convexity) and **extension risk** (when rates rise, prepayments slow and the security's life and duration lengthen). A pass-through's **weighted average life (WAL)** is the principal-weighted average time of principal payments, and changes with the PSA speed.

A **collateralized mortgage obligation (CMO)** redistributes prepayment risk across tranches. In a **sequential-pay** structure, all principal goes first to Tranche A until it is retired, then to B, and so on, so A has a short life and later tranches are more extension-prone. A **PAC (planned amortization class)** tranche is protected by a **support tranche**: it follows a schedule as long as prepayments stay inside a band, and the supports absorb the variation. **Stripped** MBS split the pool into an **interest-only (IO)** and a **principal-only (PO)** class: the PO rises in value when prepayments speed up (par returned sooner), while the IO falls (fewer interest payments).

### Asset-backed securities and credit enhancement

Non-agency MBS and ABS bear credit risk, so they use **credit enhancement**: **subordination** (senior, mezzanine and equity tranches, with losses hitting the junior tranche first), **overcollateralization** (collateral exceeds the securities), **excess spread** (interest on the collateral above what the securities pay) and **reserve accounts**. Credit card ABS have a revolving period and an early-amortization trigger. The 2007–2009 financial crisis showed that ratings of structured tranches had understated the correlation of defaults on non-agency mortgages.

@example
title: One month of pass-through cash flows
problem: A mortgage pool has a balance of \$100 million, a WAC of 6.5%, a pass-through rate of 6.0%, a WAM of 360 months and an assumed prepayment speed of 150 PSA. Find the investors' cash flow in month 1.
step: Monthly WAC $r = 0.065/12 = 0.541667\%$. $\text{PMT} = 100{,}000{,}000 \times r/(1 - (1+r)^{-360}) = \$632{,}068.02$. Borrowers' interest is $\$541{,}666.67$, so scheduled principal is $632{,}068.02 - 541{,}666.67 = \$90{,}401.36$.
step: Investors' interest: $100{,}000{,}000 \times 0.06/12 = \$500{,}000$ (the other \$41,666.67 goes to servicing and guarantee fees).
step: Prepayment: at 150 PSA month 1 has $\text{CPR} = 0.06 \times (1/30) \times 1.5 = 0.30\%$, so $\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.997^{1/12} = 0.025034\%$. Prepayment $= 0.00025034 \times (100{,}000{,}000 - 90{,}401.36) = \$25{,}011.81$.
step: Investors receive $500{,}000 + 90{,}401.36 + 25{,}011.81 = \$615{,}413.17$. The ending balance is $100{,}000{,}000 - 90{,}401.36 - 25{,}011.81 = \$99{,}884{,}586.83$.
answer: Month 1: interest \$500,000, scheduled principal \$90,401.36, prepayment \$25,011.81, total \$615,413.17.

@example
title: PSA speeds and a sequential tranche
problem: At 200 PSA, find the CPR and SMM in months 12 and 45. In a sequential CMO, Tranche A has \$900,000 of balance left and Tranche B has \$40 million. If the pool pays \$1.2 million of principal this month, how is it split?
step: Month 12: $\text{CPR} = 0.06 \times 12/30 \times 2 = 4.8\%$, $\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.952^{1/12} = 0.409\%$.
step: Month 45: the ramp has ended, so $\text{CPR} = 0.06 \times 2 = 12\%$, $\text{SMM} = 1 - 0.88^{1/12} = 1.060\%$.
step: Sequential-pay: Tranche A receives principal first, so it gets \$900,000 and is retired. Tranche B receives the remaining \$300,000.
answer: CPR 4.8% (SMM 0.409%) in month 12, CPR 12% (SMM 1.060%) in month 45; A receives \$900,000 and B \$300,000.

@mistakes
- Computing prepayment as SMM times the beginning balance: it applies to the balance after scheduled principal.
- Dividing the CPR by 12 to get the SMM: convert with $1 - (1-\text{CPR})^{1/12}$.
- Paying investors the WAC rather than the lower net pass-through rate.
- Thinking faster prepayments always help the investor: they cause contraction risk when rates are low, and the investor must reinvest at lower rates.

model:
```python
def pool_month(balance, wac, net_rate, months_left, smm):
    """One month of mortgage pool cash flows under a prepayment rate (SMM)."""
    r = wac / 12
    payment = balance * r / (1 - (1 + r) ** -months_left)
    interest = balance * net_rate / 12
    scheduled_principal = payment - balance * r
    prepayment = smm * (balance - scheduled_principal)
    ending = balance - scheduled_principal - prepayment
    return interest, scheduled_principal, prepayment, ending
```
hint: Find the level payment at the WAC, then subtract the interest the borrowers owe, `balance * r`.
hint: Investors get interest at `net_rate / 12`; the prepayment applies SMM to the balance after scheduled principal.
solution: The level payment at the WAC less borrowers' interest is the scheduled principal. Investors' interest uses the lower net rate. The SMM applies to what is left after scheduled principal. With one month left the scheduled principal is the whole balance, so the ending balance is zero whatever the SMM, which the last test checks.
