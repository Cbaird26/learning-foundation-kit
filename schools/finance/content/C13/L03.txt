@article
A 10-year Treasury's yield and a 1-year Treasury's yield are different rates, and a coupon bond mixes both: its year-1 coupon is a 1-year loan and its final payment is a 10-year loan. Discounting every cash flow at one yield to maturity hides that. The cleaner approach is to discount each cash flow at the rate for its own maturity.

**Conventions used throughout this lesson.** Bonds pay **annual** coupons, par is 100, and all rates are **annual effective rates** (annual compounding), so a $t$-year spot rate $z_t$ turns 1 into $(1+z_t)^t$. Real Treasury quotes use semiannual compounding; the algebra is identical with half-year periods. Continuously compounded rates appear only where stated.

### Spot rates and discount factors

The **spot rate** $z_t$ is the yield on a zero-coupon bond maturing in $t$ years. Its **discount factor** is $Z_t = (1+z_t)^{-t}$, the present value of 1 paid at time $t$. The **spot curve** (zero curve) plots $z_t$ against $t$. A bond's price is

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{N} \frac{CF_t}{(1+z_t)^t} = \sum_{t=1}^{N} CF_t\,Z_t.$$

If a bond's price differs from this value, an arbitrageur can strip and resell its cash flows as zeros. Its YTM is a weighted average of the spot rates, which is why two bonds with the same maturity but different coupons have different YTMs when the curve is not flat.

### Forward rates

A **forward rate** $f(a, b-a)$ is the rate agreed today for a loan that starts at time $a$ and lasts $b - a$ years. No-arbitrage requires that investing for $b$ years at $z_b$ equals investing for $a$ years at $z_a$ and then at the forward rate:

$$(1+z_b)^{b} = (1+z_a)^{a}\,\bigl(1+f(a,b-a)\bigr)^{b-a}.$$

The one-year forward rate $b$ years ahead is $f(b-1,1) = \dfrac{(1+z_b)^b}{(1+z_{b-1})^{b-1}} - 1 = \dfrac{Z_{b-1}}{Z_b} - 1$. For a multi-year forward rate, annualize: $f(a, n) = \left[\dfrac{(1+z_{a+n})^{a+n}}{(1+z_a)^a}\right]^{1/n} - 1$. With continuous compounding the forward rate is the simple weighted difference $f = \dfrac{z_b b - z_a a}{b - a}$. When the spot curve slopes upward, forward rates lie above spot rates; when it slopes downward, below.

### Par yields and bootstrapping

A **par yield** is the coupon rate that makes a bond of that maturity price at par. Market quotes on benchmark bonds are usually par-type yields, but pricing needs spot rates. **Bootstrapping** recovers spot rates one maturity at a time. For an $N$-year par bond with coupon $c_N$ (annual), the price is 100:

$$100 = c_N\sum_{t=1}^{N-1} Z_t + (100 + c_N)\,Z_N \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; Z_N = \frac{100 - c_N \sum_{t=1}^{N-1} Z_t}{100 + c_N}, \qquad z_N = Z_N^{-1/N} - 1.$$

Start with the one-year bond ($Z_1 = 100/(100 + c_1)$), use $Z_1$ to find $Z_2$, and so on. The reverse formula gives the par yield from discount factors: $c_N = \dfrac{1 - Z_N}{\sum_{t=1}^{N} Z_t}$.

@example
title: Bootstrapping four spot rates
problem: Annual-pay par bonds have par yields (coupon rates) of 3.00% (1 year), 3.50% (2 years), 4.00% (3 years) and 4.25% (4 years). Find the spot rates for years 1 to 3.
step: One year: $Z_1 = 1/1.03 = 0.970874$, so $z_1 = 3.00\%$.
step: Two years: $Z_2 = (1 - 0.035 \times 0.970874)/1.035 = 0.966019/1.035 = 0.933352$, so $z_2 = 0.933352^{-1/2} - 1 = 3.5088\%$.
step: Three years: $Z_3 = (1 - 0.04 \times (0.970874 + 0.933352))/1.04 = 0.923831/1.04 = 0.888299$, so $z_3 = 0.888299^{-1/3} - 1 = 4.0272\%$.
step: The same recursion gives $Z_4 = 0.845389$ and $z_4 = 4.2884\%$. Each spot rate exceeds the corresponding par yield on an upward-sloping curve, and the gap widens with maturity.
answer: $z_1 = 3.0000\%$, $z_2 = 3.5088\%$, $z_3 = 4.0272\%$ (and $z_4 = 4.2884\%$).

@example
title: Forward rates and a bond price from the bootstrapped curve
problem: Using $z_1 = 3.0000\%$, $z_2 = 3.5088\%$, $z_3 = 4.0272\%$, find the one-year forward rate one year ahead, the two-year forward rate starting in one year, and the price of a 3-year 5% annual-pay bond.
step: $f(1,1) = Z_1/Z_2 - 1 = 0.970874/0.933352 - 1 = 4.0201\%$.
step: $f(1,2) = \left[(1.040272)^3/1.03\right]^{1/2} - 1 = (1.125747/1.03)^{1/2} - 1 = 4.5446\%$, an annualized rate for years 2 and 3.
step: Price: $5(0.970874) + 5(0.933352) + 105(0.888299) = 4.8544 + 4.6668 + 93.2714 = 102.7925$.
answer: $f(1,1) = 4.0201\%$; $f(1,2) = 4.5446\%$ a year; price $102.7925$.

@mistakes
- Discounting every cash flow at the bond's YTM instead of at the spot rate for its own date.
- Computing a forward rate as a simple difference of spot rates when rates compound annually (use the ratio of growth factors).
- Forgetting to take the $1/n$ root when annualizing a multi-year forward rate.
- In bootstrapping, including the final coupon in the sum of earlier discount factors, or discounting a coupon with the wrong year's factor.

model:
```python
def bootstrap(par_yields):
    """Annual spot rates implied by annual-pay par yields."""
    discount_factors = []
    for n, coupon in enumerate(par_yields, start=1):
        z_n = (1 - coupon * sum(discount_factors)) / (1 + coupon)
        discount_factors.append(z_n)
    return [z ** (-1 / n) - 1 for n, z in enumerate(discount_factors, start=1)]
```
hint: Keep a list of the discount factors found so far.
hint: The next factor is `(1 - c * sum(factors)) / (1 + c)`; convert each factor to a rate with `z ** (-1 / n) - 1`.
solution: Each par bond prices at 1 per unit of par, so its last discount factor is what remains after the earlier coupons are discounted, divided by $1 + c$. A flat par curve has equal spot rates, which the test checks.
