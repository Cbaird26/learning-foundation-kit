@article
Duration treats the price-yield relationship as a straight line, but it is a curve. For a 100 basis point change in yield, a duration-only estimate can miss by several tenths of a percent of price. **Convexity** captures the curvature.

**Conventions.** Semiannual coupons, bond-equivalent yields with periodic yield $i = y/m$, prices per 100 of par, valuation on a coupon date. Convexity is in years squared and yield changes $\Delta y$ are decimals.

### Convexity

The second derivative of price with respect to yield, divided by price, is the bond's **convexity**. For a bond with $N$ periods, $m$ periods a year and cash flows $CF_t$:

$$C = \frac{1}{P\,m^2}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{t(t+1)\,CF_t}{(1+i)^{t+2}}.$$

A zero-coupon bond with $n$ periods has $C = \dfrac{n(n+1)}{m^2(1+i)^2}$. Convexity grows quickly with maturity (roughly with the square of the duration) and is higher for lower coupons and lower yields. Combining it with duration gives the second-order approximation:

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{Mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac12\,C\,(\Delta y)^2.$$

The convexity term is always positive for an option-free bond. It adds to the price after a yield fall and cushions the loss after a rise. For bonds with embedded options use **effective convexity**, found by repricing:

$$C_{\text{Eff}} = \frac{P_- + P_+ - 2P_0}{P_0\,(\Delta y)^2}.$$

### Why investors like convexity

Of two bonds with the same duration, the more convex one gains more when yields fall and loses less when they rise, so investors accept a lower yield for it. A **barbell** portfolio (short and long bonds) has more convexity than a **bullet** (bonds clustered at the middle maturity) with the same duration, because convexity depends on the dispersion of cash flows. A callable bond can have **negative convexity** when yields are low, a topic for Week 9.

### Immunization

A liability due at a known date can be funded so that a parallel yield shift does not leave a shortfall. **Classical immunization** for a single liability requires: (1) the asset portfolio's present value is at least the liability's present value; (2) the portfolio's Macaulay duration equals the liability's horizon (the duration of the liability); and (3) the portfolio has at least as much dispersion (convexity) as the liability. Rate rises lower asset values but raise reinvestment income; rate falls do the reverse; with a matched duration the two effects offset for a small parallel shift, and the convexity condition leaves the assets ahead after the shift.

With two zero-coupon bonds of durations $D_1 < H < D_2$ for a horizon $H$, the weight $w_1$ of the first, by value, solves $w_1 D_1 + (1 - w_1)D_2 = H$:

$$w_1 = \frac{D_2 - H}{D_2 - D_1}.$$

**Limits.** Immunization protects only against parallel, one-time shifts. Duration drifts as time passes and yields change, so the portfolio must be rebalanced; non-parallel shifts and default risk are not hedged. Week 12 returns to the alternative of cash-flow matching.

@example
title: Estimating a price with duration and convexity
problem: A 10-year bond pays a 5% coupon semiannually and has a yield of 6% (BEY): $P = 92.5613$, $D_{\text{Mod}} = 7.6650$ and $C = 71.7854$. Estimate the price after yield changes of $+100$ and $-100$ basis points.
step: For $+100$ bp: the duration term is $-7.6650 \times 0.01 = -7.6650\%$ and the convexity term is $\tfrac12(71.7854)(0.01)^2 = +0.3589\%$. The total is $-7.3061\%$, so $P \approx 92.5613 \times 0.926939 = 85.80$.
step: The exact price at 7% is 85.7876 ($-7.318\%$). Duration alone gives $92.5613 \times 0.92335 = 85.47$, which is off by 0.32; adding convexity cuts the error to 0.01.
step: For $-100$ bp: the terms are $+7.6650\%$ and $+0.3589\%$, so $+8.0240\%$ and $P \approx 99.99$. The exact price at 5% is 100.00 (a par bond), a change of $+8.037\%$. Duration alone gives 99.66.
answer: 85.80 for $+100$ bp (exact 85.79) and 99.99 for $-100$ bp (exact 100.00); duration alone gives 85.47 and 99.66.

@example
title: Immunizing a single liability
problem: A fund owes \$10 million in 6 years. The yield is 5% (BEY, semiannual compounding). It will hold 2-year and 10-year zero-coupon bonds. Find the amounts to invest, and check the result if yields immediately move to 4% or 6%.
step: The liability's present value is $10{,}000{,}000/1.025^{12} = \$7{,}435{,}559$ and its Macaulay duration is 6 years.
step: Weights: $w_{2} = (10 - 6)/(10 - 2) = 0.50$. Invest \$3,717,779 in each zero. Present value and duration both match.
step: At 6% (periodic 3%) the 2-year zero is worth $3{,}717{,}779 \times (1.025/1.03)^4 = \$3{,}646{,}113$ and the 10-year zero $3{,}717{,}779 \times (1.025/1.03)^{20} = \$3{,}373{,}001$. Assets total \$7,019,114 against a liability value of $10{,}000{,}000/1.03^{12} = \$7{,}013{,}799$: a surplus of \$5,315.
step: At 4% the two zeros are worth \$3,791,215 and \$4,099,751, so assets are \$7,890,966 against a liability of \$7,884,932: a surplus of \$6,034. The barbell is more convex than the single liability, so the surplus is positive for a shift in either direction.
answer: Invest \$3,717,779 in each zero; the surplus is +\$6,034 at 4% and +\$5,315 at 6%.

@mistakes
- Adding the convexity term without the factor $\tfrac12$, or using the percent form of $\Delta y$ in the squared term.
- Matching modified duration instead of Macaulay duration to the horizon for a single liability.
- Assuming an immunized portfolio stays immunized forever: it must be rebalanced as time passes and yields change.
- Thinking convexity always helps: an option-free bond's convexity is positive, but a callable bond can show negative convexity.

model:
```python
def convexity(par, coupon_rate, ytm, years, freq=2):
    """Convexity (in years squared) of a fixed-rate bond on a coupon date."""
    periods = round(years * freq)
    coupon = par * coupon_rate / freq
    i = ytm / freq
    price = 0.0
    total = 0.0
    for t in range(1, periods + 1):
        cash_flow = coupon + (par if t == periods else 0.0)
        price += cash_flow / (1 + i) ** t
        total += t * (t + 1) * cash_flow / (1 + i) ** (t + 2)
    return total / (price * freq ** 2)
```
hint: Accumulate the price and the sum of $t(t+1)CF_t/(1+i)^{t+2}$ in one loop.
hint: Divide the sum by the price times `freq ** 2`.
solution: Convexity is the discounted sum of $t(t+1)$ times each cash flow, scaled by the price and by $m^2$ to convert period units to years squared. The longer bond has the larger convexity, as the last test checks.
