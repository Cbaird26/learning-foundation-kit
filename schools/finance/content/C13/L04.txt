@article
The **term structure of interest rates** is the relationship between yield and maturity. A **normal** curve slopes upward, an **inverted** curve slopes downward, and a **flat** curve is level; curves can also be **humped**. Why does the shape vary? Theories offer different answers, and each is useful because it tells you what a forward rate means.

**Conventions.** Rates are annual effective spot rates ($z_t$ for maturity $t$ years), zero-coupon bonds have par 100, and the one-year forward rate $f(t,1)$ is computed from spot rates as in the previous lesson. All figures are hypothetical; the current Treasury curve is available from the Treasury and FRED.

### Pure expectations theory

The **pure (unbiased) expectations theory** says a forward rate equals the market's expected future spot rate: $f(1,1) = E[z_1 \text{ in one year}]$. Long-term rates are then averages of expected short rates, and the shape of the curve only reflects expected changes in short rates: an upward slope means short rates are expected to rise. A consequence is that every bond has the same expected one-year return, the one-year spot rate, whatever its maturity. Investors are assumed risk neutral, so there is no reward for bearing the price risk of long bonds.

### Liquidity preference theory

Most investors dislike price risk, and long bonds have more of it. The **liquidity preference theory** says investors demand a **liquidity (term) premium** for holding longer bonds, so

$$f(t,1) = E[\text{future one-year rate}] + L_t,$$

with $L_t \ge 0$, usually increasing with maturity. The curve then tends to slope up even when no rate rise is expected, and an inverted curve means expected declines in rates large enough to outweigh the premium. Forward rates are **biased** upward predictors of future spot rates.

### Segmentation and preferred habitat

The **market segmentation theory** holds that investors and borrowers are tied to particular maturities (for example, pension funds hold long bonds to match long liabilities), so each maturity's yield is set by supply and demand within its segment and not by expectations. The **preferred habitat theory** relaxes this: investors have favored maturities but will move to another for enough extra yield, so a premium compensates for leaving the habitat. Premiums can be positive or negative.

### Holding-period return and rolldown

Buy a zero-coupon bond with $T$ years to maturity and sell it one year later as a $(T-1)$-year zero. If the curve is unchanged (so the future $(T-1)$-year spot rate equals today's), the one-year return is

$$R = \frac{(1+z_T)^{T}}{(1+z_{T-1})^{T-1}} - 1 .$$

This exceeds $z_1$ when the curve slopes upward: the bond's yield falls as it **rolls down** the curve, which raises its price. Under pure expectations the return would be $z_1$ only if the spot rate a year later turned out to equal the forward-implied rate. If the actual curve ends up below that forward-implied curve, the return is higher.

### Yield-curve movements

Most changes in the curve are described by three factors: **level** (a parallel shift), **slope** (a steepening or flattening) and **curvature** (a change in the belly relative to the short and long ends). Parallel shifts dominate historically, which is why duration is so useful, but slope shifts need key rate durations (Week 12).

@example
title: Forward rate, liquidity premium and expected return
problem: The 1-year spot rate is 3.0%, the 2-year is 3.5% and the 3-year is 4.0% (annual effective). Survey forecasters expect the 1-year spot rate to be 3.6% in one year. (a) Find the 1-year forward rate one year ahead and the implied liquidity premium. (b) If the curve is unchanged after one year, what is the holding-period return on a 3-year zero?
step: (a) $f(1,1) = 1.035^2/1.03 - 1 = 1.071225/1.03 - 1 = 4.0024\%$. Under pure expectations that would be the expected rate; the gap to the forecast is the premium, $4.0024\% - 3.6\% = 0.4024\%$.
step: (b) Buy the 3-year zero at $100/1.04^3 = 88.8996$. A year later, with an unchanged curve, it is a 2-year zero worth $100/1.035^2 = 93.3511$.
step: The return is $93.3511/88.8996 - 1 = 5.007\%$, which is 2.0 percentage points above the 3.0% one-year spot rate. That excess is the rolldown return, earned only if the curve really does stay put.
answer: (a) forward 4.0024%, premium 0.4024%; (b) 5.007%.

@mistakes
- Reading a forward rate as a forecast: it equals the expected rate only under pure expectations; with a liquidity premium it overstates it.
- Concluding an upward-sloping curve always means rates will rise (liquidity premiums can produce a slope with no expected change).
- Computing rolldown with the wrong remaining maturity: after one year a 3-year zero has two years left.
- Treating the rolldown return as a sure gain: it is realized only if the curve is unchanged.

model:
```python
def forward_curve(spots):
    """One-year forward rates implied by annual effective spot rates."""
    forwards = []
    previous_growth = 1.0
    for t, spot in enumerate(spots, start=1):
        growth = (1 + spot) ** t
        forwards.append(growth / previous_growth - 1)
        previous_growth = growth
    return forwards
```
hint: The growth factor over $t$ years is $(1 + z_t)^t$.
hint: Divide each growth factor by the previous one (start the previous one at 1).
solution: The year-t forward rate links the $(t-1)$-year and $t$-year growth factors, so divide one by the other and subtract 1. For the first year the previous growth factor is 1, so the forward rate equals the 1-year spot rate. A flat curve produces equal forward rates.
