@article
Many bonds carry an option that changes their cash flows when rates move. Who owns the option decides whether it adds to or subtracts from the bond's value.

**Conventions.** Prices are per 100 of par, coupons are semiannual and yields are bond-equivalent yields (periodic yield $y/2$). The first call date or put date is a coupon date.

### Callable and putable bonds

A **callable bond** gives the issuer the right to buy the bond back at a **call price** on or after a **call date**. The issuer will usually call when rates have fallen enough to refinance more cheaply, which hurts the bondholder. Call features can be **European** (one date), **American** (any time after a date, with a call-protection period before) or **Bermuda** (specific dates), and **make-whole calls** let the issuer redeem at a price tied to Treasury yields, which makes calling costly. A **putable bond** lets the holder sell the bond back at the **put price**. The values are

$$V_{\text{callable}} = V_{\text{straight}} - V_{\text{call}}, \qquad V_{\text{putable}} = V_{\text{straight}} + V_{\text{put}},$$

where $V_{\text{straight}}$ is the price of an identical option-free bond. The call option is worth more when volatility is high and when rates are low.

### Yield to call, yield to put and yield to worst

The **yield to call (YTC)** is the discount rate that equates the price to the PV of coupons to the call date and the call price:

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{n}\frac{C}{(1+y/2)^t} + \frac{K}{(1+y/2)^n},$$

where $n$ is the number of periods to the call date and $K$ the call price. The **yield to put** is defined the same way with the put price. The **yield to worst (YTW)** is the lowest of the yield to maturity and the yields to every possible call date: it is the conservative return measure for callable bonds. Which of the two is lower depends on the price, the coupon and the call price, so compute both; for a bond bought well above par the yield to call is often the lower one.

### Negative convexity

When yields fall, a straight bond's price keeps rising, but a callable bond's price is held back because it is likely to be called near the call price. Its price-yield curve flattens, then bends the wrong way: it has **negative convexity** at low yields. Its **effective duration** is also lower than the straight bond's, and shortens as the call becomes likely. A putable bond has a price floor near the put price, and has *positive* convexity from the put. Measure these with effective duration and effective convexity (repricing the bond, as in the lessons on duration and convexity) rather than with the modified formulas.

### Convertible bonds

A **convertible bond** lets the holder exchange it for a fixed number of shares: the **conversion ratio**. Key quantities, for a bond with par 1,000:

- **Conversion value** $=$ share price $\times$ conversion ratio.
- **Market conversion price** $=$ bond price $/$ conversion ratio (the share price at which conversion breaks even).
- **Conversion premium** $=$ bond price $-$ conversion value, often shown as a percent of conversion value.
- **Straight value** $=$ the PV of the bond's cash flows as if it were not convertible.
- **Minimum value** $=\max(\text{straight value},\ \text{conversion value})$.

A convertible is worth straight value plus the value of a call option on the shares, so it has downside protection from the bond and upside from the stock. Its option is why its coupon is lower than that of a straight bond from the same issuer.

@example
title: Yield to call and yield to worst
problem: A 10-year bond with an 8% coupon, paid semiannually, trades at 108.00. It is callable in 5 years at 104.00. Find the YTM, YTC and YTW.
step: Yield to maturity: 20 periods, coupon 4.00, price 108.00. Solving numerically gives a periodic yield of 3.440%, so the YTM is $6.88\%$.
step: Yield to call: 10 periods, coupon 4.00, call price 104.00. Solving numerically gives a periodic yield of 3.386%, so the YTC is $6.77\%$.
step: The YTW is the lower of the two: $6.77\%$. The call date is the worst case here: the buyer pays a premium of 8 but would receive only 104 at the call date, so the premium is written off over 5 years instead of 10.
answer: YTM 6.88%, YTC 6.77%, YTW 6.77%.

@example
title: A convertible bond
problem: A convertible bond with par \$1,000 can be converted into 20 shares. The shares trade at \$42, the convertible trades at \$1,000, and a straight bond with the same cash flows is worth \$925. Find the conversion value, the premium, the market conversion price and the minimum value. Then find the value of a callable bond whose straight value is 102.50 and whose call option is worth 3.10.
step: Conversion value $= 20 \times 42 = \$840$.
step: Conversion premium $= 1{,}000 - 840 = \$160$, or $160/840 = 19.05\%$ of conversion value. Market conversion price $= 1{,}000/20 = \$50.00$, which is \$8.00 above the current share price.
step: Minimum value $= \max(925, 840) = \$925$, so the convertible trades \$75 above its floor (8.11% above straight value).
step: Callable bond: $102.50 - 3.10 = 99.40$.
answer: Conversion value \$840; premium \$160 (19.05%); conversion price \$50.00; minimum value \$925; callable bond 99.40.

@mistakes
- Quoting the YTM of a premium callable bond as its return: the yield to worst is the conservative measure.
- Adding the call option's value to the straight bond's value: the holder is short the call, so it is subtracted.
- Using modified duration for a bond with an embedded option: its cash flows change when yields change, so use effective duration.
- Treating the conversion premium as free: it is the extra paid over converting immediately, in exchange for the bond's protection.

model:
```python
def yield_to_call(price, par, coupon_rate, call_price, years_to_call, freq=2):
    """Yield to call (annual rate compounded freq times a year)."""
    periods = round(years_to_call * freq)
    coupon = par * coupon_rate / freq

    def value(i):
        pv = sum(coupon / (1 + i) ** t for t in range(1, periods + 1))
        return pv + call_price / (1 + i) ** periods

    low, high = -0.5, 1.0
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        if value(mid) > price:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return (low + high) / 2 * freq
```
hint: The value is a decreasing function of the periodic yield, so bisection works.
hint: If the value at the midpoint is above the price, the yield must be higher.
solution: Treat the call date as maturity and the call price as the final payment. Search for the periodic yield at which the present value equals the price, then multiply by `freq`. With the call price equal to par and the call date equal to maturity, it returns the YTM.
