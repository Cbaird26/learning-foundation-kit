@article
A **bond** is a loan packaged as a security. The borrower (the **issuer**) promises the lender (the **bondholder**) a stream of cash flows described in a contract called the **indenture**. Everything you will do in this course (pricing, yields, duration, credit analysis) starts from reading those promises correctly.

### The terms of a bond

- **Par value** (face value, principal): the amount repaid at maturity. Prices are quoted per 100 of par.
- **Coupon rate**: the annual interest rate applied to par. A 6% bond with par \$1,000 that pays semiannually pays \$30 every six months. The **coupon payment** is $C = \text{coupon rate} \times \text{par} / m$, where $m$ is the number of payments a year.
- **Maturity**: the date the principal is repaid. The **term** is the time remaining.
- **Currency, seniority and security**: a **senior secured** bond is backed by collateral and ranks ahead of **senior unsecured** debt, which ranks ahead of **subordinated** debt in bankruptcy.
- **Covenants**: promises that restrict the issuer (limits on additional debt, dividend limits) or require it to act (maintain insurance, deliver financial statements).
- **Embedded options**: a **callable** bond lets the issuer redeem early (it benefits the issuer); a **putable** bond lets the holder sell back at a set price (it benefits the holder); a **convertible** bond lets the holder exchange it for shares. Week 9 prices these options.
- **Sinking fund**: a requirement that the issuer retire part of the issue on a schedule.

### Types of coupon structures

A **fixed-rate** bond has a constant coupon. A **zero-coupon bond** pays only par at maturity and so sells at a discount. A **floating-rate note (FRN)** has a coupon that resets to a reference rate plus a **quoted margin**: coupon rate $=$ reference rate $+$ margin. Since the end of USD LIBOR (the panel stopped on June 30, 2023), the main US dollar reference rate is **SOFR**, the Secured Overnight Financing Rate. FRNs often use an actual/360 day count, so one coupon is $\text{par} \times (\text{reference} + \text{margin}) \times \text{days}/360$.

### Issuer sectors

**Sovereign** debt includes US Treasury bills (maturities of one year or less, sold at a discount), notes (2 to 10 years) and bonds (20 and 30 years), and Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS), whose principal adjusts with the CPI. **Agency** securities come from government-sponsored enterprises and federal agencies. **Corporate** bonds carry credit risk. **Municipal** bonds are issued by states and localities; interest on most is exempt from US federal income tax, so an investor in marginal tax rate $t$ compares a municipal yield with a taxable yield through the **taxable-equivalent yield** $y_{\text{muni}}/(1-t)$. (General education, not tax advice.)

### Quotes, accrued interest and day counts

Treasury notes and bonds are quoted in 32nds of a point: 99-16 means $99 + 16/32 = 99.50$ per 100 of par. Between coupon dates, a buyer owes the seller the interest earned since the last coupon:

$$\text{Accrued interest} = C \times \frac{\text{days since last coupon}}{\text{days in the coupon period}}.$$

The day count differs by market. **30/360** treats each month as 30 days and a year as 360 (so a semiannual period has 180 days), and is typical of corporate and municipal bonds. **Actual/actual** uses true calendar days, and is used for Treasury notes and bonds. The quoted (**clean**, or flat) price excludes accrued interest; what the buyer pays is the **full** (**dirty**, or invoice) **price** $=$ clean price $+$ accrued interest. Quoting clean prices keeps a bond's price from jumping down on each coupon date.

### Markets

New bonds are sold in the **primary market**: Treasuries by auction, corporate bonds through underwriting banks. Existing bonds trade in the **secondary market**, mostly over the counter through dealers rather than on exchanges. US corporate bond and Treasury trades typically settle one business day after the trade (T+1). Dealers finance inventories in the **repo market**: a sale of securities with an agreement to repurchase them at a higher price, which acts as a collateralized overnight loan. Current yields and prices change daily: look them up on Treasury's website or FRED; this course uses hypothetical numbers.

@example
title: Full price of a corporate bond
problem: A corporate bond with par \$1,000 pays a 6% coupon semiannually on March 15 and September 15, using 30/360. A buyer settles on June 25 at a clean price of 98.50 per 100 of par. How much does the buyer pay per bond?
step: The coupon payment is $0.06 \times 1000 / 2 = \$30$.
step: Under 30/360, March 15 to June 25 is $3 \times 30 + 10 = 100$ days, in a coupon period of 180 days.
step: Accrued interest is $30 \times 100/180 = \$16.67$.
step: The clean price in dollars is $1000 \times 0.985 = \$985.00$, so the full price is $985.00 + 16.67 = \$1{,}001.67$.
answer: \$1,001.67 per bond (accrued interest \$16.67).

@example
title: A Treasury note quoted in 32nds
problem: A 4.00% Treasury note with \$2 million par is quoted at 98-24. The settlement date is 61 days after the last coupon in a coupon period of 184 days (actual/actual). What is the invoice amount?
step: Convert the quote: $98 + 24/32 = 98.75$ per 100, so the clean price is $2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.9875 = \$1{,}975{,}000$.
step: The semiannual coupon is $2{,}000{,}000 \times 0.04/2 = \$40{,}000$.
step: Accrued interest is $40{,}000 \times 61/184 = \$13{,}260.87$.
step: The invoice amount is $1{,}975{,}000 + 13{,}260.87 = \$1{,}988{,}260.87$.
answer: \$1,988,260.87.

@mistakes
- Treating the quoted price as the amount paid: the buyer also pays accrued interest.
- Dividing the annual coupon by 2 for a bond that pays quarterly (use the number of payments a year) or forgetting to divide at all.
- Reading 99-16 as 99.16: the digits after the hyphen are 32nds, so 99-16 is 99.50.
- Using calendar days in a 30/360 calculation, or 180 days for the period of an actual/actual bond.

model:
```python
def accrued_interest(par, coupon_rate, freq, days_since, days_in_period):
    """Accrued interest between coupon dates."""
    coupon = par * coupon_rate / freq
    return coupon * days_since / days_in_period
```
hint: First compute one coupon payment, which depends on `freq`.
hint: Multiply that coupon by the fraction of the period that has elapsed.
solution: One coupon is `par * coupon_rate / freq`. Accrued interest is the elapsed fraction of the coupon period times that coupon, so it is zero right after a coupon date and a full coupon at the next one.
