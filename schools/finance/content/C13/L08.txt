@article
A corporate bond yields more than a Treasury of the same maturity. That **credit spread** pays for expected default losses, for the risk that losses will be larger than expected, and for lower liquidity. This lesson turns spreads into numbers you can use.

**Conventions.** Spreads and rates are annual; spot rates in the Z-spread example are annual effective rates; hazard rates and default probabilities use continuous compounding; recovery is a fraction of face value paid at the maturity of a zero-coupon bond. Figures are hypothetical.

### Measures of spread

- **Yield spread**: the bond's yield to maturity minus the yield of a benchmark of similar maturity.
- **G-spread**: spread over an interpolated government (Treasury) yield for the same maturity.
- **I-spread**: spread over the interpolated swap (interest-rate swap) rate of the same maturity.
- **Z-spread** (zero-volatility spread): the constant $s$ added to every spot rate on the benchmark curve that makes the discounted cash flows equal the price:

$$P = \sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{CF_t}{(1 + z_t + s)^t}.$$

The Z-spread uses the whole curve instead of one maturity, so it is better than a single-point spread when the curve is steep. For bonds with options, the **option-adjusted spread** (Week 10) removes the option's effect.

### Hazard rates and the credit triangle

A **reduced-form model** treats default as a random event arriving at a **hazard rate** (default intensity) $\lambda$: the chance of defaulting in a short interval $dt$, given survival, is $\lambda\,dt$. With constant $\lambda$:

$$Q(t) = e^{-\lambda t}\ \text{(survival probability)}, \qquad \text{PD}(0,t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}.$$

If a bond loses $\text{LGD} = 1 - R$ on default, a short-term approximation known as the **credit triangle** links three quantities:

$$s \approx \lambda\,(1 - R) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \lambda \approx \frac{s}{1-R}.$$

The spread is the hazard rate times the loss given default: with $R = 40\%$, a 1.2% spread implies $\lambda = 2\%$ a year.

### Exact relation for a zero-coupon bond

For a zero-coupon bond paying face value 1 at $T$ with recovery $R$ of face at maturity, the price relative to a risk-free zero is $P_{\text{risky}} = P_{\text{free}}\,[1 - \text{PD}\,(1 - R)]$. Hence

$$\text{PD} = \frac{1 - P_{\text{risky}}/P_{\text{free}}}{1 - R}.$$

If both yields are continuously compounded and the spread is $s$, then $P_{\text{risky}}/P_{\text{free}} = e^{-sT}$, so $\text{PD} = (1 - e^{-sT})/(1-R)$.

### Risk-neutral versus real-world probabilities

A default probability backed out of prices is **risk-neutral**: it includes the compensation investors require for default risk and illiquidity, so it is generally **higher** than the **real-world** (historical) probability. Use real-world PDs to estimate expected losses and risk-neutral PDs to price. Credit default swaps (CDS), contracts paying the loss on default in return for a periodic premium, are priced the same way, so their spreads give market-implied hazard rates.

@example
title: Default probability from a zero-coupon spread
problem: A 5-year risky zero-coupon bond yields 4.0% and a risk-free 5-year zero yields 3.0% (both continuously compounded). The recovery rate is 40% of face at maturity. Find the risk-neutral 5-year default probability and compare it with the credit-triangle approximation. Then find the expected loss on \$10 million at a 150 basis point spread (hazard rate from the triangle, $R = 40\%$) over 5 years.
step: The spread is $s = 1.0\%$. The ratio of prices is $e^{-0.01 \times 5} = 0.951229$, so $\text{PD} = (1 - 0.951229)/(1 - 0.40) = 0.048771/0.60 = 8.13\%$.
step: Triangle: $\lambda = 0.01/0.60 = 1.667\%$, so $\text{PD} \approx 1 - e^{-0.016667 \times 5} = 8.00\%$. Close to the exact value.
step: For a 150 bp spread, $\lambda = 0.015/0.60 = 2.5\%$ and $\text{PD}(0,5) = 1 - e^{-0.125} = 11.75\%$.
step: Expected loss $= \$10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.1175 \times 0.60 = \$705{,}019$ (risk-neutral).
answer: PD 8.13% (triangle 8.00%); expected loss \$705,019 at a 150 bp spread.

@example
title: A Z-spread
problem: Annual spot rates are 3.0%, 3.5% and 4.0% for 1, 2 and 3 years. A 3-year corporate bond with a 5% annual coupon (par 100) trades at 98.60. Find its Z-spread.
step: Discounting at the spot curve alone gives $5/1.03 + 5/1.035^2 + 105/1.04^3 = 102.8665$. The bond trades well below that, so the spread is positive.
step: Try $s = 1.50\%$: the price is $5/1.045 + 5/1.05^2 + 105/1.055^3 = 98.7393$. Try $s = 1.60\%$: the price is 98.4723.
step: The target 98.60 lies $(98.7393 - 98.60)/(98.7393 - 98.4723) = 52.2\%$ of the way between them, so $s \approx 1.50\% + 0.522 \times 0.10\% = 1.552\%$. A numerical solver gives 1.5521%.
answer: Z-spread about 1.552%, or 155 basis points.

@mistakes
- Using the full spread as the hazard rate: divide by $1 - R$ (the credit triangle).
- Reading a risk-neutral default probability as a forecast of the real-world frequency of default.
- Computing a spread against a Treasury of a different maturity instead of an interpolated or curve-based benchmark.
- Using recovery as the loss: LGD is $1 - R$.

spots = [0.02, 0.025, 0.03, 0.035]
flows = [4, 4, 4, 104]

model:
```python
def z_spread(price, cash_flows, spot_rates):
    """Constant spread over the spot curve that reproduces the price."""
    def pv(s):
        return sum(cf / (1 + z + s) ** (k + 1)
                   for k, (cf, z) in enumerate(zip(cash_flows, spot_rates)))
    low, high = -0.05, 0.5
    for _ in range(200):
        mid = (low + high) / 2
        if pv(mid) > price:
            low = mid
        else:
            high = mid
    return (low + high) / 2
```
hint: The present value falls as the spread rises, so bisection works.
hint: If the present value at the midpoint is above the price, the spread must be higher.
solution: Define the present value as a function of $s$, which decreases in $s$. Repeatedly halve the interval: if the value at the midpoint exceeds the market price, the spread is larger, so move the lower bound up; otherwise move the upper bound down.
