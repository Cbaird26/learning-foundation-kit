@article
Two questions occupy managers once costs are known. *What should we charge?* And *how do we judge whether a division is performing well?*

### Pricing

**Cost-plus pricing** starts from cost and adds a **markup**: $\text{Price} = \text{Cost base} \times (1 + \text{Markup})$. The markup percentage on a given cost base is chosen to cover costs not in the base and to earn a target profit:

$$\text{Markup \%} = \frac{\text{Desired profit} + \text{Costs not in the base}}{\text{Total cost base}}.$$

With an *absorption* cost base (full manufacturing cost), the markup covers selling and administrative costs plus desired profit, often a target return on invested capital: desired profit = required return × investment. With a *variable* cost base, the markup also covers fixed costs. Cost-plus is simple but ignores demand: a price that covers cost may not sell.

**Target costing** reverses the logic. The market sets the price; the firm sets the cost:

$$\text{Target cost} = \text{Market price} - \text{Desired profit per unit}.$$

If current cost exceeds the target cost, engineers and cost accountants search for design and process changes to close the gap before launch. Prices must also respect law: pricing designed to harm competition or to discriminate among buyers can violate antitrust law, so legal advice is part of pricing practice.

### Responsibility accounting

A **responsibility center** is a unit whose manager is accountable for specified results: a **cost center** (controls costs: a maintenance department), a **revenue center**, a **profit center** (revenues and costs: a product line) or an **investment center** (also controls the assets invested: a division). Managers are evaluated on what they can **control**, so performance reports separate controllable from non-controllable costs.

### Evaluating investment centers

**Return on investment (ROI)** and its DuPont-style decomposition:

$$ROI = \frac{\text{Operating income}}{\text{Average operating assets}} = \underbrace{\frac{\text{Operating income}}{\text{Sales}}}_{\text{margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Average operating assets}}}_{\text{turnover}}.$$

**Residual income (RI)** charges the division for the capital it uses:

$$RI = \text{Operating income} - \left(\text{Required rate of return} \times \text{Average operating assets}\right).$$

ROI is a percentage that is easy to compare across divisions, but a manager rewarded on ROI may reject a project whose return exceeds the cost of capital yet falls below the division's current ROI. Residual income is in dollars and avoids that conflict: any project with a return above the required rate raises RI. Other measures (economic value added, a balanced scorecard with customer, process and learning measures) broaden the view.

### Transfer pricing

When one division sells to another, the **transfer price** is revenue to the seller and cost to the buyer. The **minimum** the seller will accept is its variable cost per unit plus the **opportunity cost** (the contribution lost on outside sales it would give up; zero with idle capacity). The **maximum** the buyer will pay is the lowest price available outside. A transfer benefits the company when the buyer's maximum exceeds the seller's minimum, and any price in that range splits the gain between the divisions.

@example
title: Cost-plus and target costing
problem: Bellwether Foods plans to make 20,000 units with an absorption unit cost of \$80, selling and administrative costs of \$400,000, and invested capital of \$1,000,000 on which it requires a 15% return. (a) Find the markup percentage on absorption cost and the price. (b) Separately, a competing product sells at a market price of \$120, and Bellwether wants a profit of 25% of price; its current cost is \$96. Find the target cost and the cost reduction needed.
step: (a) Desired profit = 0.15 × 1,000,000 = \$150,000. Cost base = 20,000 × 80 = \$1,600,000. Markup = (150,000 + 400,000)/1,600,000 = 34.375%.
step: Price = 80 × 1.34375 = \$107.50. Check: revenue 2,150,000 - 1,600,000 - 400,000 = \$150,000 profit.
step: (b) Desired profit per unit = 0.25 × 120 = \$30, so target cost = 120 - 30 = \$90.
step: Required reduction = 96 - 90 = \$6 per unit, or 6.25% of the current cost.
answer: (a) Markup 34.375%, price \$107.50. (b) Target cost \$90; reduce cost by \$6.

@example
title: ROI, residual income and a project decision
problem: A division has sales of \$3,000,000, operating income of \$360,000 and average operating assets of \$2,400,000. The company's required return is 12%. The manager can invest \$500,000 in a project that adds \$67,500 of operating income. Compute ROI (with margin and turnover) and RI, then assess the project under each measure.
step: Margin = 360,000/3,000,000 = 12%; turnover = 3,000,000/2,400,000 = 1.25; ROI = 12% × 1.25 = 15%. RI = 360,000 - 0.12 × 2,400,000 = 360,000 - 288,000 = \$72,000.
step: The project earns 67,500/500,000 = 13.5%, above the 12% required return. With it, ROI = (360,000 + 67,500)/(2,400,000 + 500,000) = 427,500/2,900,000 = 14.74%.
step: An ROI-judged manager sees ROI fall from 15% to 14.74% and may reject the project. Under residual income, the project adds 67,500 - 0.12 × 500,000 = \$7,500, so RI rises to \$79,500 and the manager accepts. Accepting is consistent with the company's interest, since the project beats the required return.
answer: ROI 15%; RI \$72,000. The project lowers ROI to 14.74% but raises RI by \$7,500.

@mistakes
- Using only the desired profit in the markup and forgetting to include the costs outside the cost base (such as selling and administrative expenses).
- Computing ROI on year-end assets instead of average operating assets, or on net income instead of operating income, without saying so.
- Believing a project must be rejected whenever its return is below the division's current ROI, even when it exceeds the required return.
- Setting the minimum transfer price at full absorption cost, which ignores both variable cost and opportunity cost.

lead: \$
tol: 1
check: (40 - 28) * 5000
hint: The gain is the outside price saved minus the relevant cost of making.
solution: With idle capacity the seller's opportunity cost is zero, so the relevant cost is the \$28 variable cost. The company saves 40 - 28 = \$12 per unit, or 12 × 5,000 = \$60,000.

solution: The manager will probably reject the project, because at 15% it would pull the division's ROI down from 18% even though it earns more than the 12% required return. Residual income charges the division 12% on its assets, so the project would add income minus a 12% capital charge, a positive amount; the manager would accept it. Residual income aligns division decisions with the company's interest by accepting every project whose return exceeds the required return.
