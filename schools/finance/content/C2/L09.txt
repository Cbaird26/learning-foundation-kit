@article
Last week you built standards. This week you compare them with what happened and find out *why* total cost differed. The key idea is to split every input's cost variance into a **price** part (did we pay more or less per unit of input?) and a **quantity** part (did we use more or less input than the output should have required?).

### Symbols and sign convention

Let $AP$ and $AQ$ be the actual price and quantity of an input, $SP$ the standard price, and $SQ$ the **standard quantity allowed for the actual output** (standard quantity per unit × actual units produced). With every variance computed as actual minus standard, a **positive** number is **unfavorable (U)** because cost exceeds the standard; a **negative** number is **favorable (F)**.

### Direct materials and direct labor

| Input | Price (rate) variance | Quantity (efficiency) variance |
|---|---|---|
| Materials | $(AP - SP) \times AQ_{\text{purchased}}$ | $(AQ_{\text{used}} - SQ) \times SP$ |
| Labor | $(AR - SR) \times AH$ | $(AH - SH) \times SR$ |

Here $AR$ and $SR$ are the actual and standard hourly wage rates, $AH$ the actual hours worked and $SH$ the standard hours allowed. The two parts add to the **total variance**: actual cost minus standard cost allowed. **Convention:** in this lesson, materials purchased equal materials used. (If they differ, the price variance is computed on the quantity *purchased*, so it is recognized when the purchase is made.) Purchasing is usually responsible for price variances, production for quantity and efficiency variances, but be careful: buying cheaper, lower-grade material may give a favorable price variance and an unfavorable quantity variance.

### Variable overhead

Variable overhead is applied on a base such as labor hours at a standard rate $SVR$:

$$\text{Spending variance} = \text{Actual VOH} - AH \times SVR, \qquad \text{Efficiency variance} = (AH - SH) \times SVR.$$

The efficiency variance arises only because the *base* (hours) was used inefficiently.

### Fixed overhead

Fixed overhead is applied at a standard rate $SFR = \text{Budgeted fixed overhead}/\text{Normal capacity hours}$. Then

$$\text{Budget variance} = \text{Actual fixed OH} - \text{Budgeted fixed OH}$$
$$\text{Volume variance} = \text{Budgeted fixed OH} - SH \times SFR.$$

The **volume variance** is unfavorable when the standard hours allowed for actual output fall short of normal capacity: it is not a cost overspend but a measure of **unused capacity**, since fixed overhead is not controllable in the short run.

### Investigating variances

Managers use **management by exception**: investigate variances that are large (in dollars or as a percent of standard), recurring or controllable. Causes might be a supplier price rise, defective material, an untrained crew, machine breakdowns or an unrealistic standard. Variances can be recorded in the accounts: materials are debited at standard cost with a separate variance account, and at period-end variances are usually closed to cost of goods sold if immaterial.

@example
title: Materials and labor variances
problem: Brightwater Gear produced 9,500 units. Standards per unit: 3 lb of material at \$4.00 per lb; 0.5 direct labor hour at \$18.00. Actual: it bought and used 29,500 lb at \$4.10 per lb (\$120,950); it worked 4,900 hours at an actual rate of \$18.50 (\$90,650). Compute the variances.
step: Allowed inputs: SQ = 9,500 × 3 = 28,500 lb; SH = 9,500 × 0.5 = 4,750 hours.
step: Materials price = (4.10 - 4.00) × 29,500 = \$2,950 U. Materials quantity = (29,500 - 28,500) × 4.00 = \$4,000 U. Total = \$6,950 U, which equals 120,950 - 28,500 × 4.00 = 120,950 - 114,000.
step: Labor rate = (18.50 - 18.00) × 4,900 = \$2,450 U. Labor efficiency = (4,900 - 4,750) × 18.00 = \$2,700 U. Total = \$5,150 U, which equals 90,650 - 4,750 × 18 = 90,650 - 85,500.
step: Entry for materials: debit Raw Materials 118,000 (29,500 × 4.00) and Materials Price Variance 2,950; credit Accounts Payable 120,950. Then debit Work in Process 114,000 and Materials Quantity Variance 4,000; credit Raw Materials 118,000.
answer: Materials price \$2,950 U, quantity \$4,000 U; labor rate \$2,450 U, efficiency \$2,700 U.

@example
title: Overhead variances
problem: Continue with Brightwater (9,500 units; SH = 4,750 hours; AH = 4,900 hours). Standard variable overhead is \$8.00 per labor hour; actual variable overhead was \$40,180. Budgeted fixed overhead is \$30,000 for a normal capacity of 5,000 hours (standard rate \$6.00 per hour); actual fixed overhead was \$31,000. Compute the overhead variances.
step: Variable overhead spending = 40,180 - 4,900 × 8.00 = 40,180 - 39,200 = \$980 U.
step: Variable overhead efficiency = (4,900 - 4,750) × 8.00 = \$1,200 U. Total variable overhead variance = \$2,180 U = 40,180 - 4,750 × 8.00.
step: Fixed overhead budget variance = 31,000 - 30,000 = \$1,000 U.
step: Fixed overhead volume variance = 30,000 - 4,750 × 6.00 = 30,000 - 28,500 = \$1,500 U (the plant produced less than normal capacity). Total fixed overhead variance = \$2,500 U = 31,000 - 28,500.
answer: VOH spending \$980 U, efficiency \$1,200 U; FOH budget \$1,000 U, volume \$1,500 U.

@mistakes
- Computing the quantity variance with the *actual* price instead of the *standard* price.
- Using the quantity that was *budgeted* in the plan instead of the standard quantity allowed for the *actual output*.
- Treating the fixed overhead volume variance as a cost-control failure: it reflects the use of capacity, not spending.
- Concluding that every favorable variance is good news (cheap, poor-quality material may cause waste and rework).

model:
```python
def price_quantity_variances(actual_qty, actual_price, standard_qty_allowed,
                             standard_price):
    """Return (price variance, quantity variance); positive means unfavorable."""
    price = (actual_price - standard_price) * actual_qty
    quantity = (actual_qty - standard_qty_allowed) * standard_price
    return price, quantity
```
hint: The price variance multiplies the price difference by the actual quantity.
hint: The quantity variance multiplies the quantity difference by the standard price.
solution: Compute the price variance as (actual price - standard price) × actual quantity and the quantity variance as (actual quantity - standard quantity allowed) × standard price, and return them as a tuple. Negative values are favorable.

@quiz self
prompt: A production manager has an unfavorable labor efficiency variance of \$9,000, and the purchasing manager has a favorable materials price variance of \$6,000. In three or four sentences, explain how you would investigate whether the two are related and what you would conclude if they are.
hint: Consider how material quality could affect labor time.
solution: I would compare the materials quantity (scrap and rework) and labor hours per unit for the batches made with the cheaper material against earlier batches, and ask production whether the new material was harder to work. If the cheaper material caused more rework and slower processing, the favorable price variance partly caused the unfavorable efficiency variance, and the real saving is smaller than the \$6,000 shown or even negative against the \$9,000 extra labor cost. The standards and purchasing decisions should then be judged on the combined effect, not variance by variance.

rubric: Explains the possible causal link between lower-quality material and labor inefficiency.
rubric: Concludes that the variances should be judged together (a net effect), not separately.
