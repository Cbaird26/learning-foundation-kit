@article
A custom cabinet shop builds each order to a customer's specification; a print shop runs one customer's brochures at a time. When products are distinct batches or orders, a **job-order costing** system tracks the cost of each **job** separately. (When identical units flow continuously, as in a bottling plant, process costing applies; see week 3.)

### The job cost sheet

Each job has a **job cost sheet** that accumulates three things: **direct materials** (from materials requisitions), **direct labor** (from time tickets) and **applied manufacturing overhead**. Costs sit in **Work in Process** (WIP) until the job is finished, move to **Finished Goods**, and move to **Cost of Goods Sold** when the job is delivered.

### Applying overhead: the predetermined rate

Direct costs trace to jobs, but factory overhead (rent, utilities, supervision) does not. Waiting until year-end to learn actual overhead would delay every job's cost, and monthly overhead is lumpy (heating bills peak in winter). So firms use a **predetermined overhead rate (POHR)**, set before the year begins:

$$\text{POHR} = \frac{\text{Estimated total manufacturing overhead}}{\text{Estimated total quantity of the allocation base}}.$$

The **allocation base** (direct labor hours, machine hours, direct labor cost) should be a cost driver: something that actually causes overhead. Overhead applied to a job is

$$\text{Applied overhead} = \text{POHR} \times \text{Actual quantity of the base used by the job}.$$

### The journal entries

| Event | Debit | Credit |
|---|---|---|
| Materials issued to production | Work in Process | Raw Materials |
| Direct labor incurred | Work in Process | Wages Payable |
| Overhead applied | Work in Process | Manufacturing Overhead |
| Actual overhead costs incurred | Manufacturing Overhead | Cash, Accounts Payable, Accumulated Depreciation, etc. |
| Job completed | Finished Goods | Work in Process |
| Job sold (cost) | Cost of Goods Sold | Finished Goods |

Notice that **Manufacturing Overhead** is debited with *actual* costs and credited with *applied* overhead. It is a clearing account.

### Over- and underapplied overhead

At period-end the two sides rarely match:

$$\text{Underapplied} = \text{Actual overhead} - \text{Applied overhead} > 0, \qquad \text{Overapplied} \text{ when it is} < 0.$$

*Underapplied* means jobs were charged too little (the debit balance remains in Manufacturing Overhead). If the amount is small, close it directly to Cost of Goods Sold: debit COGS and credit Manufacturing Overhead for an underapplied balance; the reverse for overapplied. If it is material, allocate it among WIP, Finished Goods and COGS in proportion to the overhead applied in each.

### Pricing from job cost

Many job shops bill at cost plus a markup: $\text{Price} = \text{Job cost} \times (1 + \text{markup})$. Job costs are also the basis for judging profitability by job, and the unit cost of a job is its total cost divided by units produced.

@example
title: Three jobs with overhead
problem: A cabinet shop estimates annual overhead of \$360,000 and 18,000 direct labor hours (DLH). Direct labor is paid \$28 per hour. Job A used \$4,200 of materials and 120 DLH; Job B used \$7,500 and 60 DLH; Job C used \$3,000 and 200 DLH. Find each job's cost, using DLH as the allocation base.
step: POHR = 360,000 / 18,000 = \$20.00 per DLH.
step: Job A: labor 120 × 28 = \$3,360; overhead 120 × 20 = \$2,400. Total = 4,200 + 3,360 + 2,400 = \$9,960.
step: Job B: labor 60 × 28 = \$1,680; overhead 60 × 20 = \$1,200. Total = 7,500 + 1,680 + 1,200 = \$10,380.
step: Job C: labor 200 × 28 = \$5,600; overhead 200 × 20 = \$4,000. Total = 3,000 + 5,600 + 4,000 = \$12,600. Job C has the lowest materials but the highest cost, because it is labor-intensive and overhead follows labor hours.
answer: Job A \$9,960; Job B \$10,380; Job C \$12,600.

@example
title: Closing underapplied overhead
problem: The same shop used 17,600 actual DLH during the year and incurred actual overhead of \$361,000. Unadjusted cost of goods sold is \$1,850,000. Compute the over- or underapplied overhead, record the closing entry (the amount is immaterial), and give adjusted COGS.
step: Applied overhead = 20 × 17,600 = \$352,000.
step: Underapplied = 361,000 - 352,000 = \$9,000. The debit balance in Manufacturing Overhead shows costs not yet charged to jobs.
step: Entry: debit Cost of Goods Sold 9,000; credit Manufacturing Overhead 9,000. The overhead account now has a zero balance.
step: Adjusted COGS = 1,850,000 + 9,000 = \$1,859,000.
answer: Underapplied by \$9,000; adjusted COGS \$1,859,000.

@mistakes
- Applying overhead at the *actual* rate computed after the period ends, instead of a predetermined rate based on estimates.
- Applying overhead on actual overhead cost rather than on the actual quantity of the allocation base the job used.
- Reversing the entry for underapplied overhead: an underapplied balance is a debit in Manufacturing Overhead, so it is closed by debiting COGS (increasing expense).
- Crediting actual overhead costs to Manufacturing Overhead when they are incurred: actual costs are *debited* to the account and applied overhead is *credited*.

import math
assert job_cost(5000, 90, 20, 40) == 10400
assert job_cost(0, 0, 20, 40) == 0
assert math.isclose(job_cost(1200.5, 10, 25.5, 12), 1575.5, rel_tol=1e-12)

model:
```python
def job_cost(materials, labor_hours, wage_rate, oh_rate):
    """Total job cost: materials + labor + overhead applied per labor hour."""
    labor = labor_hours * wage_rate
    overhead = labor_hours * oh_rate
    return materials + labor + overhead
```
hint: Overhead is applied per labor hour, not as a percent of labor cost.
solution: Compute labor as hours times wage rate, overhead as hours times the overhead rate, and add both to materials.

@quiz self
prompt: A controller proposes charging each job its *actual* overhead computed at month-end instead of a predetermined rate. In three or four sentences, give two reasons most job shops use a predetermined rate.
hint: Think about timeliness and about uneven monthly overhead.
solution: A predetermined rate lets the firm cost and price a job as soon as it is complete, instead of waiting for month-end overhead totals. It also smooths seasonal swings: overhead such as heating or maintenance is lumpy, while production volume may not be, so an actual monthly rate would make the same job cost more in a high-overhead month. The rate is set from estimates before the year begins, and any difference from actual overhead is cleared at period-end as over- or underapplied overhead.
rubric: Gives timeliness: costs are known when the job is finished, without waiting for actual overhead.
rubric: Gives smoothing of lumpy or seasonal overhead so identical jobs get consistent costs.
rubric: Mentions that the difference between applied and actual overhead is settled at period-end.
