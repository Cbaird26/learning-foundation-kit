@article
Imagine a plant that makes a million plain widgets and a few thousand custom ones. The custom items need engineering time, frequent machine changeovers and many inspections. If overhead is spread by direct labor hours, the plain widgets (which use most of the hours) absorb most of the overhead, while the custom items look cheap. The firm may then underprice custom work and overprice its bread-and-butter product.

### Traditional costing

A **traditional** system uses one **plant-wide predetermined overhead rate** (or one per department) based on a volume measure such as direct labor hours:

$$\text{Rate} = \frac{\text{Total overhead}}{\text{Total direct labor hours}}, \qquad \text{Overhead assigned} = \text{Rate} \times \text{Hours used}.$$

It works when overhead really varies with volume and products consume resources in similar proportions.

### Activity-based costing

**Activity-based costing (ABC)** traces overhead through activities:

1. **Identify activities** and group their costs into **activity cost pools** (machine setups, machine running, inspections, purchasing).
2. **Choose a cost driver** for each pool: a measure of what causes the cost (number of setups, machine hours, inspections).
3. **Compute an activity rate**: $\text{Activity rate} = \dfrac{\text{Pool cost}}{\text{Total driver quantity}}$.
4. **Assign cost** to each product: $\text{Activity rate} \times \text{Driver quantity the product uses}$, summed over pools.
5. **Divide by units** to get overhead per unit.

ABC does not change *total* overhead; it changes how the total is divided among products.

### The cost hierarchy

- **Unit-level**: incurred for every unit (machine power).
- **Batch-level**: incurred per batch regardless of its size (setups, scheduling a production run).
- **Product-level**: sustain a product line (engineering design, product manager).
- **Facility-level**: sustain the whole plant (plant security, building depreciation); often not driven by any activity and spread arbitrarily or left unallocated.

### Costs and benefits

ABC gives more accurate costs for pricing, product-mix and cost-reduction decisions, and it exposes **cost drivers** that managers can control. But it requires data collection and maintenance, and the choice of pools and drivers involves judgment. Firms consider ABC when overhead is large relative to direct costs, product lines are diverse, and products differ in batch sizes and complexity.

@example
title: Traditional versus ABC overhead
problem: A plant has overhead of \$600,000: setups \$240,000, machine running \$200,000, inspection \$160,000. It makes Standard (40,000 units) and Custom (5,000 units). Driver quantities: direct labor hours Standard 24,000, Custom 6,000; setups Standard 20, Custom 100; machine hours Standard 8,000, Custom 2,000; inspections Standard 100, Custom 700. Find overhead per unit both ways.
step: Traditional: rate = 600,000 / 30,000 DLH = \$20 per DLH. Standard: 24,000 × 20 = \$480,000, or \$12.00 per unit. Custom: 6,000 × 20 = \$120,000, or \$24.00 per unit.
step: Activity rates: setups 240,000/120 = \$2,000 per setup; machine running 200,000/10,000 = \$20 per machine hour; inspection 160,000/800 = \$200 per inspection.
step: ABC, Standard: 20 × 2,000 + 8,000 × 20 + 100 × 200 = 40,000 + 160,000 + 20,000 = \$220,000, or \$5.50 per unit.
step: ABC, Custom: 100 × 2,000 + 2,000 × 20 + 700 × 200 = 200,000 + 40,000 + 140,000 = \$380,000, or \$76.00 per unit. Totals match: 220,000 + 380,000 = \$600,000.
answer: Traditional \$12.00 (Standard) and \$24.00 (Custom); ABC \$5.50 and \$76.00.

@example
title: What the difference does to margins
problem: Continue the plant above. Standard sells for \$26 and has direct materials and labor of \$14 per unit; Custom sells for \$110 and has direct costs of \$40 per unit. Compare each product's unit margin under traditional and ABC costing.
step: Traditional, Standard: 26 - 14 - 12.00 = \$0.00 per unit. Custom: 110 - 40 - 24.00 = \$46.00 per unit. Management would conclude Custom is a gold mine and Standard barely breaks even.
step: ABC, Standard: 26 - 14 - 5.50 = \$6.50 per unit, or \$260,000 in total. Custom: 110 - 40 - 76.00 = -\$6.00 per unit, or -\$30,000 in total.
step: Total operating income is the same under both: 0 + 5,000 × 46 = \$230,000, and 260,000 - 30,000 = \$230,000. Only the *attribution* changed: ABC shows Custom loses money at this price because it uses 83% of the setups and 88% of the inspections.
answer: Traditional shows Standard \$0.00 and Custom \$46.00 per unit; ABC shows Standard \$6.50 and Custom -\$6.00.

@mistakes
- Believing ABC changes total overhead: it only redistributes the same total among products.
- Using direct labor hours as the driver for every pool, which recreates the traditional distortion.
- Computing the activity rate with the quantity used by one product instead of the total quantity of the driver.
- Forgetting to divide the total overhead assigned to a product by its units to get overhead per unit.

lead: \$

@quiz self

solution: A plant-wide labor-hour rate loads overhead on the product with most labor hours, the high-volume one, even though specialty products consume most of the batch-level and product-level resources such as setups, inspections and engineering. ABC pools overhead by activity, assigns it with drivers such as number of setups, and so charges each product for the resources it actually uses; total overhead is unchanged.
