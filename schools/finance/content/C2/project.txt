@review
### Week by week

1. **Cost concepts.** Direct and indirect, product and period, variable, fixed, mixed. High-low method. COGM and COGS.
2. **Job-order costing.** Predetermined overhead rate; job cost sheets; entries; over- and underapplied overhead.
3. **Process costing.** Equivalent units by input; weighted average versus FIFO; the five steps.
4. **Activity-based costing.** Pools, drivers, activity rates; cost hierarchy; why traditional costing distorts.
5. **CVP.** Contribution margin; break-even; target profit; margin of safety; operating leverage; sales mix.
6. **Variable versus absorption costing.** Treatment of fixed manufacturing overhead; reconciliation by inventory change.
7. **Master budget.** Sales, production, purchases, cash budget; budgeted statements.
8. **Flexible budgets and standards.** Sales volume and flexible-budget variances; standard cost cards.
9. **Variance analysis.** Price/rate, quantity/efficiency, overhead spending, efficiency, budget and volume variances.
10. **Relevant costs.** Make or buy, special orders, constrained resources, drop a segment, process further.
11. **Pricing and responsibility accounting.** Markup, target cost, ROI, residual income, transfer prices.
12. **Capital budgeting.** Payback, NPV, IRR, PI, after-tax cash flows.

### Key formulas

- POHR = estimated overhead / estimated base; Underapplied = actual - applied.
- Activity rate = pool cost / total driver quantity.
- Break-even units = $F/(p - v)$; target units = $(F + \pi)/(p - v)$; DOL = contribution margin / operating income.
- Absorption income - variable income = (units produced - units sold) × fixed overhead per unit.
- Production (or purchases) = need + desired ending - beginning.
- Price variance = $(AP - SP) \times AQ$; quantity variance = $(AQ - SQ) \times SP$.
- Fixed overhead volume variance = budgeted fixed overhead - $SH \times$ standard rate.
- ROI = margin × turnover; RI = operating income - required return × average assets.
- $NPV = \sum CF_t/(1+r)^t$; PI = 1 + NPV/outlay; after-tax cash flow = pretax × (1 - t) + depreciation × t.

### Self-check

- Can you explain, without notes, why a plant-wide rate can overcost simple products?
- Can you build a cash budget from sales and a payment pattern?
- Can you say, for any cost, whether it is relevant to a decision?
- Can you find the NPV of a project with taxes and explain why NPV beats payback?

### Common errors

- Choosing high and low *costs* instead of high and low *activity* in the high-low method.
- Applying overhead at the actual rate, or reversing the underapplied entry.
- Using one equivalent-unit figure for materials and conversion.
- Using the wrong direction for the absorption versus variable reconciliation.
- Including depreciation in cash disbursements, or ignoring borrowing in multiples.
- Using the static budget as the benchmark for cost control.
- Including sunk or unavoidable allocated costs in a decision.
- Judging a project by ROI instead of by its return relative to the required return.

prompt: A plant has annual overhead of \$200,000: setups \$120,000 and machine running \$80,000. Product Y uses 50 of the 60 total setups and 1,000 of the 4,000 total machine hours, and 2,000 units of Y are made. Under traditional costing, overhead is applied at a plant-wide rate per direct labor hour: the plant uses 10,000 direct labor hours in total, and Y uses 2,000 of them. By how much does the overhead per unit of Y under activity-based costing exceed the overhead per unit under traditional costing? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 40.00
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (50 * (120000 / 60) + 1000 * (80000 / 4000)) / 2000 - (200000 / 10000 * 2000) / 2000
hint: Compute the traditional rate per labor hour, then the activity rates for each pool.
hint: Traditional overhead per unit of Y = rate × Y's hours / units; ABC = sum of activity rate × driver quantity, divided by units.
solution: Traditional rate = 200,000/10,000 = \$20 per DLH, so Y gets 2,000 × 20 = \$40,000, or \$20 per unit. ABC rates: setups 120,000/60 = \$2,000 per setup; machine 80,000/4,000 = \$20 per hour. Y gets 50 × 2,000 + 1,000 × 20 = \$120,000, or \$60 per unit. Difference = \$40.00 per unit: the traditional method undercosts Y.

prompt: A product sells for \$60. Standard variable cost per unit is direct materials 2 lb at \$5.00, direct labor 1 hour at \$18.00, variable overhead \$4.00 and variable selling cost \$3.00. Fixed costs are \$300,000 a year. The supplier raises the price of the material to \$5.50 per lb, with no other change in prices or quantities. What is the new break-even volume in units?
answer: 12500
unit: units
check: 300000 / (60 - (2 * 5.50 + 18 + 4 + 3))
hint: Recompute the variable cost per unit with the new material price.
solution: New variable cost = 2 × 5.50 + 18 + 4 + 3 = \$36 per unit. Contribution margin = 60 - 36 = \$24. Break-even = 300,000 / 24 = 12,500 units, up from 300,000 / 25 = 12,000 units: the \$1.00 price rise adds 500 units to break-even.

prompt: A plant has 9,000 machine hours a year. Product P sells for \$50, has a variable cost of \$30 and uses 2 machine hours; demand is up to 3,000 units. Product Q sells for \$80, has a variable cost of \$50 and uses 5 machine hours; demand is up to 1,500 units. (a) Find the plan that maximizes contribution and its total. (b) A machine costing \$50,000 would add 3,000 machine hours a year for 4 years. Ignoring taxes, find the NPV at a 10% required return and say whether to buy it. Assume end-of-year cash flows.
hint: Rank the products by contribution margin per machine hour.
hint: For part (b), the extra hours go to the product with the remaining demand.
solution: (a) Contribution per hour: P = 20/2 = \$10; Q = 30/5 = \$6. Make 3,000 P (6,000 hours, contribution \$60,000), then use the remaining 3,000 hours on Q: 600 units, contribution \$18,000. Total \$78,000. (b) The extra 3,000 hours make 600 more Q (1,200 units would still be below the 1,500 demand), adding 600 × 30 = \$18,000 a year. PV = 18,000 × 3.169865 = \$57,058. NPV = 57,058 - 50,000 = about \$7,058, positive, so buying is justified on these numbers, subject to the risk that the hours or demand are not as forecast.
rubric: Ranks by contribution per machine hour (P \$10, Q \$6) and produces 3,000 P and 600 Q for a contribution of \$78,000.
rubric: Recognizes that the extra 3,000 hours can produce 600 more Q at \$30 contribution, \$18,000 a year.
rubric: Discounts four equal annual flows at 10% to get a present value of about \$57,058 and an NPV of about \$7,058.
