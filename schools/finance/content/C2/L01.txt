@article
Financial accounting (C1) reports to outsiders under GAAP or IFRS. **Managerial accounting** serves managers inside the firm: it has no mandatory format, looks forward as well as back, and cares about the cost of a product, a job, a department or a decision. Everything in this course starts from one question: *what does it cost, and how does that cost change when we change what we do?*

### Three ways to classify a cost

**By traceability.** A **direct cost** can be traced to a specific cost object (a product, job or department) in an economically feasible way: the steel in a bicycle frame, the wages of the welder who builds it. An **indirect cost** benefits several cost objects and must be allocated: factory rent, the plant supervisor's salary.

**By function.** In a manufacturer, **product costs** are the costs of making goods and are *inventoried* until the goods are sold:

- **Direct materials (DM)**: materials that become part of the product and are traceable to it.
- **Direct labor (DL)**: wages of workers who convert the materials.
- **Manufacturing overhead (MOH)**: all other factory costs (indirect materials, indirect labor, factory depreciation, utilities, insurance).

**Period costs** (selling and administrative expenses) are expensed in the period incurred. Two useful sums: **prime cost** = DM + DL, and **conversion cost** = DL + MOH.

**By behavior.** How total cost responds to the **activity level** (units produced, machine hours, orders):

| Behavior | Total cost as activity rises | Cost per unit |
|---|---|---|
| Variable | rises in proportion | constant |
| Fixed | constant | falls |
| Mixed (semivariable) | fixed part plus variable part | falls, but not to zero |
| Step | jumps at certain capacity thresholds | irregular |

The model for a mixed cost is $$Y = F + vX,$$ where $Y$ is total cost, $F$ the fixed cost per period, $v$ the variable cost per unit of activity $X$. This linear model is valid only inside the **relevant range**, the span of activity over which the fixed amounts and the variable rate stay true. Rent is fixed until you outgrow the building.

### The high-low method

To estimate $F$ and $v$ from history, take the observation with the highest activity and the one with the lowest (by *activity*, not by cost) and fit a line through them:

$$v = \frac{Y_{\text{high}} - Y_{\text{low}}}{X_{\text{high}} - X_{\text{low}}}, \qquad F = Y_{\text{high}} - vX_{\text{high}}.$$

It is quick but uses only two points, so extreme observations can mislead. Regression (a least-squares line through all points) is the better estimator when data are available.

### From costs to cost of goods sold

Product costs flow through three inventories: raw materials, work in process (WIP) and finished goods.

$$\text{Total manufacturing cost} = DM_{\text{used}} + DL + MOH$$
$$\text{COGM} = \text{Beginning WIP} + \text{Total manufacturing cost} - \text{Ending WIP}$$
$$\text{COGS} = \text{Beginning finished goods} + \text{COGM} - \text{Ending finished goods}$$

COGM is the cost of the units *completed* in the period; COGS is the cost of the units *sold*.

@example
title: Splitting a mixed cost
problem: Larkspur Outfitters records monthly equipment maintenance cost and machine hours. The lowest-activity month had 2,000 hours and cost \$14,000. The highest-activity month had 5,000 hours and cost \$23,000. Use the high-low method, then predict the cost at 3,600 hours (inside the relevant range).
step: Variable rate: $v = (23{,}000 - 14{,}000)/(5{,}000 - 2{,}000) = 9{,}000/3{,}000 = \$3.00$ per machine hour.
step: Fixed cost, using the high point: $F = 23{,}000 - 3.00 \times 5{,}000 = \$8{,}000$ per month. The low point gives the same: $14{,}000 - 3.00 \times 2{,}000 = 8{,}000$.
step: Cost function: $Y = 8{,}000 + 3.00X$. At 3,600 hours, $Y = 8{,}000 + 3.00 \times 3{,}600 = 8{,}000 + 10{,}800 = \$18{,}800$.
answer: \$3.00 per hour variable, \$8,000 fixed; predicted cost \$18,800.

@example
title: Cost of goods manufactured and sold
problem: For a year, Bellwether Foods had these amounts: beginning WIP \$40,000; direct materials used \$120,000; direct labor \$90,000; manufacturing overhead \$60,000; ending WIP \$30,000; beginning finished goods \$50,000; ending finished goods \$65,000. Find COGM and COGS.
step: Total manufacturing cost = 120,000 + 90,000 + 60,000 = \$270,000.
step: COGM = 40,000 + 270,000 - 30,000 = \$280,000.
step: COGS = 50,000 + 280,000 - 65,000 = \$265,000.
answer: COGM \$280,000; COGS \$265,000.

@mistakes
- Picking the high and low *costs* instead of the high and low *activity levels* in the high-low method.
- Treating fixed cost per unit as if it were constant: total fixed cost is constant, but cost per unit falls as volume rises.
- Using a cost function outside the relevant range, where fixed amounts and variable rates may no longer hold.
- Expensing manufacturing overhead as a period cost: factory costs are product costs, and sales commissions are period costs.

@quiz self
prompt: A manager uses a cost function estimated from months in which a plant made between 20,000 and 40,000 units to budget a month in which it will make 70,000 units. In three or four sentences, explain why the budget may be wrong and what the relevant range means.
hint: Think about what changes when the plant must add a second shift or more equipment.
solution: The **relevant range** is the span of activity over which the estimated fixed costs and variable rate hold. Volume of 70,000 units is far outside the 20,000 to 40,000 range the data came from, so fixed costs may step up (more supervisors, a second shift, leased equipment) and the variable rate may change (overtime premiums, volume discounts), making a straight-line extrapolation unreliable. The manager should re-estimate with data near the expected volume or budget the extra capacity separately.
rubric: Defines the relevant range as the activity span over which fixed amounts and variable rates stay valid.
rubric: Notes that 70,000 units is outside the 20,000 to 40,000 range of the observed data.
rubric: Gives a concrete reason costs may change, such as step-fixed costs or overtime.
