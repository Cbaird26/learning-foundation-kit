@article
Decisions look to the future. A cost or revenue is **relevant** to a decision only if it (1) occurs in the future and (2) **differs among the alternatives**. Everything else, no matter how large, can be ignored. The method is **incremental (differential) analysis**: compare the *change* in revenues and costs between alternatives.

### What is and is not relevant

- **Sunk costs** (already incurred and unrecoverable, such as the price paid for a machine last year, or its book value) are never relevant.
- **Avoidable costs** (those that disappear if you choose an alternative) are relevant; **unavoidable** costs, including most allocated fixed costs, are not.
- **Opportunity cost**, the benefit given up by choosing one alternative over the next best, is relevant even though no cash changes hands.
- Fully allocated unit costs often mislead: the fixed overhead per unit looks like a cost of the product, but it matters only if it would actually change.

### Make or buy

Compare the **avoidable cost of making** (direct materials, direct labor, variable overhead, and any fixed cost that would be eliminated) with the **purchase price**. If the freed capacity could be used for something else, add that alternative use's contribution as an opportunity cost of making.

### Special orders

An order below the regular price is worth accepting if there is spare capacity and it does not disturb regular sales: accept when the **incremental revenue** exceeds the **incremental cost** (variable cost plus any extra fixed cost caused by the order). The minimum acceptable price per unit is variable cost plus extra per-unit cost. With no spare capacity, the lost contribution from displaced regular sales is an opportunity cost.

### A constrained resource

When a scarce resource (machine hours, skilled labor) limits output, rank products by **contribution margin per unit of the constraint**, not per unit sold, and produce in that order until the constraint or demand is exhausted.

### Drop a segment, process further

A segment should be dropped only if the **contribution it makes toward avoidable fixed costs** (its **segment margin**) is negative, after accounting for any effect on other products. For **joint products**, the costs of the joint process are sunk by the split-off point. Process a product further if the **incremental revenue** exceeds the **additional processing cost**.

### Qualitative factors

Relevant-cost analysis is the quantitative part. Quality, supplier reliability, customer relationships, employee morale and long-run strategy also matter.

@example
title: A make-or-buy decision
problem: Larkspur Outfitters makes a part used in its tents, needing 20,000 units a year. Cost to make per unit: direct materials \$6, direct labor \$4, variable overhead \$3. Fixed overhead of \$90,000 (\$4.50 per unit) is charged to the part, of which \$30,000 would be eliminated if the part were bought and \$60,000 would continue. A supplier offers the part at \$15. Compare the alternatives, first with the freed capacity idle, then if it could be rented to another firm for \$25,000 a year.
step: Absorption cost to make = 6 + 4 + 3 + 4.50 = \$17.50, which looks worse than \$15. But \$60,000 of fixed overhead continues either way and is not relevant.
step: Relevant cost to make = (6 + 4 + 3) × 20,000 + 30,000 = 260,000 + 30,000 = \$290,000 (\$14.50 per unit). Cost to buy = 15 × 20,000 = \$300,000. Making is cheaper by \$10,000.
step: With the rental opportunity, the relevant cost of making rises by \$25,000 to \$315,000 (the rent given up). Buying costs \$300,000, so buying is now cheaper by \$15,000.
step: Qualitative factors (supplier reliability, quality control) should also be weighed before deciding.
answer: Idle capacity: make (saves \$10,000). With a \$25,000 rental alternative: buy (saves \$15,000).

@example
title: A special order and a constrained resource
problem: (a) A plant has idle capacity. Regular price is \$25; variable cost per unit is \$16 (materials \$8, labor \$5, variable overhead \$3), and fixed overhead is \$7 per unit allocated. A customer offers \$19 per unit for 5,000 units, and the order needs a one-time design cost of \$4,000. Regular sales are unaffected. (b) Separately, with 1,200 machine hours available, product X contributes \$24 per unit and uses 3 hours; product Y contributes \$20 and uses 2 hours; demand for Y is at most 450 units. Find the best plan.
step: (a) Incremental revenue = 5,000 × 19 = \$95,000. Incremental cost = 5,000 × 16 + 4,000 = \$84,000. Net gain = \$11,000, so accept. The allocated fixed overhead of \$7 per unit is irrelevant. Minimum acceptable price = 16 + 4,000/5,000 = \$16.80.
step: (b) Contribution per machine hour: X = 24/3 = \$8; Y = 20/2 = \$10. Y has the higher return on the constraint.
step: Make 450 units of Y (900 hours), contributing 450 × 20 = \$9,000. The remaining 300 hours make 100 units of X, contributing 100 × 24 = \$2,400.
step: Total contribution = \$11,400. Making only X (400 units) would give just \$9,600.
answer: (a) Accept: gain \$11,000. (b) 450 Y and 100 X, contribution \$11,400.

@mistakes
- Including allocated fixed costs that continue under every alternative, which makes buying or dropping look better than it is.
- Treating a sunk cost, such as the book value of existing equipment, as relevant.
- Ranking products by contribution margin per *unit* when a scarce resource limits output.
- Ignoring opportunity cost when capacity has an alternative use.

prompt: A firm needs 10,000 units of a part per year. Making it costs \$10 per unit in variable costs, plus \$30,000 of fixed costs that would be eliminated if it stopped making the part; another \$20,000 of allocated fixed cost continues regardless. A supplier offers the part at \$12.50. What is the annual advantage of buying instead of making? Enter a negative number if making is cheaper. Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 5000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: (10 * 10000 + 30000) - 12.50 * 10000
hint: Compare the avoidable cost of making with the cost of buying; ignore the fixed cost that continues.
solution: Relevant cost to make = 100,000 + 30,000 = \$130,000. Cost to buy = 12.50 × 10,000 = \$125,000. Buying saves \$5,000.

answer: 22.50
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 21 + 9000 / 6000

solution: Minimum price = variable cost + extra cost per unit = 21 + 9,000/6,000 = \$22.50.

@practice mc
prompt: A company is deciding whether to replace a machine bought two years ago for \$200,000 (book value now \$120,000). Which amount is irrelevant to the decision?
choice: The cash price of the new machine
choice: The resale value of the old machine
choice*: The \$120,000 book value of the old machine
choice: The savings in operating costs from the new machine
hint: A cost that cannot change whichever way you decide is irrelevant.
solution: Book value is a sunk cost. It will be written off or depreciated either way, so it does not differ between keeping and replacing. (Depreciation tax effects are separate.) The new price, the resale value and the operating savings all differ between alternatives.

@practice num
prompt: A plant has 1,500 machine hours. Product X contributes \$30 per unit and uses 4 hours; product Y contributes \$22 per unit and uses 2 hours. Demand for Y is at most 500 units, and demand for X is unlimited. What is the maximum total contribution margin? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 14750
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 500 * 22 + (1500 - 500 * 2) / 4 * 30
hint: Rank the products by contribution per machine hour, make as much of the best one as demand allows, and use the rest on the other.
solution: Y earns \$11 per hour against \$7.50 for X. Make 500 Y (1,000 hours, \$11,000); the other 500 hours make 125 X (\$3,750). Total \$14,750.

@practice num
prompt: A segment has sales of \$400,000, variable costs of \$260,000, avoidable fixed costs of \$70,000 and unavoidable allocated fixed costs of \$50,000. Dropping it has no effect on other products. What is the change in company operating income if the segment is dropped? Enter a negative number if income falls.
answer: -70000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: -(400000 - 260000 - 70000)
hint: Only the contribution after avoidable fixed costs disappears; the allocated costs continue.
solution: Segment margin = 400,000 - 260,000 - 70,000 = \$70,000. Dropping the segment loses this margin (the \$50,000 of unavoidable costs remain), so operating income falls by \$70,000.

@quiz num
prompt: A joint process yields 20,000 units of an intermediate product that can be sold at split-off for \$6.00 per unit or processed further at an extra cost of \$2.75 per unit and sold for \$9.50. What is the total gain from processing further (negative if a loss)? Round to the nearest dollar.
answer: 15000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 20000 * (9.50 - 6.00 - 2.75)
hint: Compare incremental revenue with the added processing cost per unit.
solution: Incremental revenue per unit = 9.50 - 6.00 = \$3.50. Extra cost = \$2.75. Gain = \$0.75 per unit × 20,000 = \$15,000. The joint cost is sunk and ignored.

@quiz self
prompt: A manager rejects a special order priced at \$22 per unit because the full absorption cost is \$24, even though the plant has idle capacity and the variable cost is \$15. In three or four sentences, explain the error and what to compute.
hint: Which part of the \$24 would change if the order were accepted?
solution: The \$24 absorption cost includes fixed overhead per unit, which will be incurred whether or not the order is accepted when capacity is idle, so it is not relevant. The manager should compare the incremental revenue of \$22 per unit with the incremental cost of \$15 per unit (plus any extra fixed cost the order would cause). The order adds \$7 per unit to income, provided it does not displace regular sales or damage the regular price structure.
rubric: Says fixed overhead per unit is irrelevant because it continues when capacity is idle.
rubric: Compares incremental revenue (\$22) with incremental (variable) cost (\$15) and finds a gain of about \$7 per unit.
rubric: Mentions the condition that regular sales are not displaced or that capacity is truly idle.
