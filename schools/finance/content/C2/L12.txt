@article
**Capital budgeting** is the process of deciding which long-lived investments (machines, plants, product launches) to undertake. The decisions are costly to reverse and affect value for years, so they use cash flows and the time value of money (C1 and the finance courses develop this in depth; here we apply it to operating decisions).

### Cash flows, not accounting profit

Use **incremental, after-tax cash flows**: the cash the firm has *with* the project minus the cash it would have *without* it. Include the initial outlay (equipment, installation, working capital), operating cash inflows or savings, and any end-of-life salvage and working-capital recovery. Ignore sunk costs and financing costs (these are in the discount rate). **Depreciation** is not a cash flow, but it reduces taxes. With tax rate $t$:

$$\text{After-tax operating cash flow} = (\text{Pretax cash flow})(1 - t) + \text{Depreciation} \times t.$$

The second term is the **depreciation tax shield**. (Tax depreciation rules differ from book depreciation and change often; the examples use straight-line to keep the arithmetic clear, and this is general education, not tax advice.)

**Conventions in this lesson:** the initial outlay occurs at time 0, and later cash flows occur at the end of each year.

### Payback and discounted payback

The **payback period** is the time to recover the initial outlay from the cash flows, interpolating within the year the balance turns positive:

$$\text{Payback} = (\text{last year with a negative cumulative balance}) + \frac{\text{unrecovered amount at start of the next year}}{\text{cash flow of the next year}}.$$

Payback is simple and measures liquidity risk, but it ignores the time value of money and every cash flow after the cutoff. **Discounted payback** uses discounted cash flows, fixing the first flaw but not the second.

### Net present value, IRR and the profitability index

The **net present value** discounts all cash flows at the required rate of return $r$ (the cost of capital):

$$NPV = \sum_{t=0}^{n} \frac{CF_t}{(1 + r)^t}.$$

Accept projects with $NPV > 0$: they add value. The **internal rate of return (IRR)** is the rate making $NPV = 0$; accept when $IRR > r$. The **profitability index** is $PI = \dfrac{\text{PV of future cash flows}}{\text{Initial outlay}} = 1 + \dfrac{NPV}{\text{Outlay}}$; accept when $PI > 1$. NPV is in dollars and measures value added directly. IRR is a rate, which can mislead when projects differ in scale or timing, or when cash flows change sign more than once (multiple IRRs). When NPV and IRR rank *mutually exclusive* projects differently, rely on NPV. When capital is rationed, the PI helps choose the best set of independent projects. Comparing projects with unequal lives needs extra care (for instance, replacement chains or equivalent annual annuities).

### Accounting rate of return

The **accounting rate of return** is average annual operating income divided by the initial (or average) investment. It uses accounting income, not cash, and ignores timing, so it is a weaker guide than NPV.

@example
title: Ranking three projects
problem: Three projects are evaluated at a 10% required return. A: outlay \$120,000, then cash flows of \$40,000, \$45,000, \$50,000 and \$40,000 in years 1 to 4. B: outlay \$120,000, then \$70,000, \$50,000, \$30,000 and \$20,000. C: outlay \$90,000, then \$25,000 in each of years 1 to 4 and \$60,000 in year 5. Rank them by payback and by NPV.
step: Payback. A: after year 2 the balance is -120,000 + 85,000 = -35,000, so payback = 2 + 35,000/50,000 = 2.70 years. B: -120,000 + 70,000 + 50,000 = 0, so payback = 2.00 years. C: after year 3 the balance is -90,000 + 75,000 = -15,000, so payback = 3 + 15,000/25,000 = 3.60 years. Ranking by payback: B, A, C.
step: NPV at 10%. A: -120,000 + 40,000/1.1 + 45,000/1.1² + 50,000/1.1³ + 40,000/1.1⁴ = \$18,440. B: \$21,158. C: -90,000 + 25,000 × 3.169865 + 60,000/1.1⁵ = -90,000 + 79,247 + 37,255 = \$26,502. Ranking by NPV: C, B, A.
step: Other measures: PI = 1.154 (A), 1.176 (B), 1.294 (C); IRR = 16.84% (A), 20.03% (B), 19.48% (C); discounted payback = 3.33 (A), 2.67 (B), 4.29 (C) years.
step: All three have positive NPV. Payback favors B because C's large cash flow comes late. If they are mutually exclusive, NPV says choose C (but C lasts one year longer, so the comparison should consider what the firm does with the freed capital in year 5). For independent projects with ample capital, accept all three.
answer: Payback ranks B, A, C; NPV ranks C (\$26,502), B (\$21,158), A (\$18,440).

@example
title: After-tax cash flows and the tax shield
problem: A machine costs \$150,000 and saves \$55,000 a year in pretax operating costs for 5 years. It is depreciated straight-line to zero salvage over 5 years; the tax rate is 30%; the required return is 12%. Find the annual after-tax cash flow and the NPV.
step: Depreciation = 150,000/5 = \$30,000 a year.
step: After-tax cash flow = 55,000 × (1 - 0.30) + 30,000 × 0.30 = 38,500 + 9,000 = \$47,500 a year.
step: PV of the annuity = 47,500 × [1 - 1.12⁻⁵]/0.12 = 47,500 × 3.604776 = \$171,227.
step: NPV = 171,227 - 150,000 = \$21,227. Positive, so the machine adds value at a 12% required return.
answer: After-tax cash flow \$47,500; NPV about \$21,227.

@mistakes
- Using accounting income instead of cash flows, or treating depreciation itself as a cash outflow rather than as a tax shield.
- Counting a sunk cost, such as a feasibility study already paid for, in the initial outlay.
- Using Excel's NPV function on a series that includes the time-0 outlay (it treats the first flow as time 1).
- Choosing among mutually exclusive projects by IRR when NPV gives a different ranking.

model:
```python
def payback_period(flows):
    """Years until cumulative cash flow reaches zero, or None."""
    cumulative = 0.0
    for year, flow in enumerate(flows):
        previous = cumulative
        cumulative += flow
        if cumulative >= 0:
            if year == 0 or flow == 0:
                return float(year)
            return year - 1 + (-previous) / flow
    return None
```
hint: Keep a running cumulative total and remember the total before the current year.
hint: When the total first reaches zero or more, the fraction of that year used is the previous deficit divided by that year's flow.
solution: Track the cumulative cash flow. In the first year it is zero or positive, return the previous whole year plus the unrecovered deficit divided by that year's cash flow. A total that is already non-negative at time 0 returns 0.0, and a loop that ends without recovery returns `None`.
