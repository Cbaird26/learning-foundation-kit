@article
A **budget** is a quantified plan for a future period. Budgets force managers to *plan* (decide what to do), *coordinate* (production must match sales; purchasing must match production), *communicate* goals, and later *control* by comparing results with the plan (weeks 8 and 9). Good budgeting is **participative**: those who must meet the numbers help set them, which improves buy-in but risks padded targets ("budgetary slack"). Variations include **rolling budgets** (always twelve months ahead), **zero-based budgeting** (every expense justified from scratch) and **incremental budgeting** (last year plus or minus adjustments).

### The master budget

The **master budget** links the firm's budgets into one coordinated plan. Its **operating budgets** come first, and the **financial budgets** follow.

1. **Sales budget**: units × price. Everything else depends on this forecast.
2. **Production budget**: units to produce = units sold + desired ending finished-goods inventory − beginning inventory.
3. **Direct materials budget**: purchases = materials needed for production + desired ending materials inventory − beginning materials inventory.
4. **Direct labor budget**; **manufacturing overhead budget**; **selling and administrative budget**.
5. **Cost of goods sold budget** and **budgeted income statement**.
6. **Capital budget** (planned equipment purchases; see week 12).
7. **Cash budget**: receipts and payments by period, and financing.
8. **Budgeted balance sheet**.

### The inventory identities

The production and purchases budgets share one structure:

$$\text{Required} = \text{Needed for use or sale} + \text{Desired ending inventory} - \text{Beginning inventory}.$$

A common policy sets desired ending inventory at a percentage of next period's need, so the budget for the final month needs a forecast of the month that follows.

### The cash budget

The cash budget has four parts: **cash available** (beginning cash plus collections), **cash disbursements**, **excess or deficiency against a minimum cash balance**, and **financing** (borrowing to cover a shortfall, repayment when cash allows). Remember that cash and income differ: depreciation is an expense but not a cash payment; credit sales are revenue when made but cash when collected; purchases on account are paid later; equipment purchases are cash outflows but not expenses.

A typical collection pattern is 60% in the month of sale and 40% in the following month. Then

$$\text{Collections in month } t = 0.60 \times \text{Sales}_t + 0.40 \times \text{Sales}_{t-1}.$$

**Convention in this lesson:** borrowing occurs at the start of a month in multiples of \$1,000 to restore the minimum cash balance, repayment is made at the end of a month when cash allows, and interest is ignored for simplicity.

@example
title: Production and materials purchases
problem: Tamarack Candle Co. expects to sell 1,000 candles in January, 1,200 in February, 1,500 in March and 1,300 in April. Policy: ending finished goods equal 20% of the next month's sales; beginning January finished goods are 200 units. Each candle needs 2 lb of wax costing \$3 per lb. Ending wax inventory equals 10% of next month's production needs; April production is planned at 1,400 units, and beginning January wax is 208 lb. Find production by month and wax purchases in pounds and dollars.
step: Production = sales + ending FG - beginning FG. January: 1,000 + 0.20 × 1,200 - 200 = 1,040. February: 1,200 + 0.20 × 1,500 - 240 = 1,260. March: 1,500 + 0.20 × 1,300 - 300 = 1,460. Quarter: 3,760 units.
step: Wax needed = 2 × production: 2,080; 2,520; 2,920 lb. Ending wax: January 10% × 2,520 = 252; February 10% × 2,920 = 292; March 10% × (2 × 1,400) = 280 lb.
step: Purchases = need + ending - beginning. January: 2,080 + 252 - 208 = 2,124 lb. February: 2,520 + 292 - 252 = 2,560 lb. March: 2,920 + 280 - 292 = 2,908 lb.
step: At \$3 per lb: January \$6,372; February \$7,680; March \$8,724.
answer: Production 1,040; 1,260; 1,460 units. Purchases 2,124; 2,560; 2,908 lb (\$6,372; \$7,680; \$8,724).

@example
title: A quarterly cash budget
problem: Tamarack Candle Co. begins January with \$9,000 cash and requires a minimum balance of \$6,000. Budgeted collections are \$38,400 (January), \$44,800 (February) and \$55,200 (March). Budgeted cash disbursements are \$34,586, \$36,226 and \$40,302, but February also includes a \$30,000 equipment purchase not in that figure. Prepare the cash budget using the lesson's borrowing convention.
step: January: 9,000 + 38,400 - 34,586 = \$12,814, above the minimum, so no borrowing. Ending cash \$12,814.
step: February: 12,814 + 44,800 - (36,226 + 30,000) = -\$8,612. To reach \$6,000 requires 14,612, so borrow \$15,000 (the next multiple of \$1,000). Ending cash = -8,612 + 15,000 = \$6,388.
step: March: 6,388 + 55,200 - 40,302 = \$21,286 before financing. Repay \$15,000, leaving 6,286, still at least the \$6,000 minimum.
step: March ending cash is \$6,286, and the loan is fully repaid. The quarter's cash flows: collections \$138,400 in total.
answer: Borrow \$15,000 in February; repay it in March; ending cash \$12,814 (Jan), \$6,388 (Feb), \$6,286 (Mar).

@mistakes
- Building the production budget from sales alone and forgetting to adjust for the change in finished-goods inventory.
- Including depreciation (a non-cash expense) in cash disbursements, or leaving equipment purchases out of them.
- Treating the month's sales as the month's collections when customers pay on credit.
- Borrowing the exact shortfall instead of enough to reach the *minimum cash balance* (in multiples of the borrowing unit).

model:
```python
def production_budget(sales, beginning_inventory, ending_pct, next_sales):
    """Units to produce each period to meet sales and the inventory policy."""
    production = []
    inventory = beginning_inventory
    for i, units in enumerate(sales):
        following = sales[i + 1] if i + 1 < len(sales) else next_sales
        ending = ending_pct * following
        production.append(units + ending - inventory)
        inventory = ending
    return production
```
hint: Each period's ending inventory becomes the next period's beginning inventory.
solution: Loop through the periods, find the next period's sales (or `next_sales` for the last one), compute ending inventory, and apply production = sales + ending - beginning. Carry the ending inventory forward.

@quiz self
prompt: In three or four sentences, explain why a company's sales budget must be prepared first and describe one weakness of letting managers set their own budget targets.
hint: Think about which other budgets use the sales forecast as an input, and about incentives.
solution: The sales budget is the starting point because production, purchasing, labor, overhead, selling expenses and cash collections all depend on expected sales. An error there flows through every other schedule. Participative budgeting gets managers' knowledge and commitment, but a manager whose pay depends on meeting the budget may understate sales or overstate costs to create slack, so the budget is easier to achieve and less useful for planning.
