@article
A plant budgets 10,000 units but sells 11,000. Costs are higher than budgeted: is that bad? Not necessarily, since more units mean more variable cost. Comparing actual results with a budget built for a different volume mixes the effect of *volume* with the effect of *efficiency*. The fix is a **flexible budget**.

### Static versus flexible budgets

A **static budget** is prepared for one planned level of activity and is not changed when volume changes. A **flexible budget** restates revenue and costs at the **actual** level of activity using the same prices, variable cost rates and fixed amounts as the plan:

$$\text{Flexible budget} = (p - v)\,q_{\text{actual}} - F_{\text{budget}},$$

where variable costs flex in proportion to activity and fixed costs stay at their budgeted totals within the relevant range.

### Two variances for operating income

Three columns give two variances. A **favorable (F)** variance raises operating income relative to the benchmark; an **unfavorable (U)** variance lowers it.

$$\text{Sales volume variance} = \text{Flexible budget} - \text{Static budget}$$
$$\text{Flexible-budget variance} = \text{Actual} - \text{Flexible budget}$$
$$\text{Total static-budget variance} = \text{Actual} - \text{Static budget}.$$

The **sales volume variance** is the effect of selling more or fewer units than planned. The **flexible-budget variance** is the effect of price and cost performance at the *actual* volume, and it is what managers can be held accountable for in cost control. Looking at each line (revenue, variable cost, fixed cost) shows where the flexible-budget variance arises.

### Standard costs

A **standard cost** is a carefully predetermined cost per unit of output, set from engineering studies, history and expected prices. **Ideal standards** assume perfect efficiency; **practical standards** allow for normal spoilage and downtime and are more motivating. A **standard cost card** lists, for each input, a **standard quantity** and a **standard price**:

$$\text{Standard cost per unit} = \sum \left(\text{Standard quantity per unit} \times \text{Standard price}\right).$$

The standard cost of the *actual output* (the "flexed" standard, or standard cost allowed) is the standard cost per unit multiplied by the actual units produced, regardless of how much input was actually used. Variances (week 9) compare it with actual cost. Standards also simplify bookkeeping: inventory can be carried at standard cost. For fixed overhead, the standard rate is the budgeted fixed overhead divided by the normal-capacity quantity of the allocation base (for example, direct labor hours).

@example
title: Static budget, flexible budget and actual
problem: Brightwater Gear budgeted 10,000 units at a price of \$20, variable cost of \$12 per unit and fixed costs of \$50,000. It actually sold 11,000 units with revenue of \$224,400, variable costs of \$135,300 and fixed costs of \$52,000. Compute the operating income in each column and the variances.
step: Static budget: revenue 200,000; variable cost 120,000; contribution margin 80,000; fixed cost 50,000; operating income \$30,000.
step: Flexible budget at 11,000 units: revenue 11,000 × 20 = 220,000; variable cost 11,000 × 12 = 132,000; CM 88,000; fixed cost 50,000; operating income \$38,000.
step: Actual: 224,400 - 135,300 - 52,000 = \$37,100.
step: Sales volume variance = 38,000 - 30,000 = \$8,000 F. Flexible-budget variance = 37,100 - 38,000 = \$900 U, made up of revenue +4,400 F, variable cost -3,300 U and fixed cost -2,000 U (4,400 - 3,300 - 2,000 = -900). Total static-budget variance = \$7,100 F.
answer: Static \$30,000; flexible \$38,000; actual \$37,100. Volume variance \$8,000 F; flexible-budget variance \$900 U.

@example
title: A standard cost card
problem: Brightwater's standards per unit are: direct materials 3 lb at \$4.00; direct labor 0.5 hour at \$18.00; variable overhead 0.5 labor hour at \$8.00; fixed overhead at \$6.00 per direct labor hour (budgeted \$30,000 over a normal 5,000 hours). Find the standard cost per unit and the standard cost allowed for 9,500 units produced.
step: Direct materials: 3 × 4.00 = \$12.00. Direct labor: 0.5 × 18.00 = \$9.00.
step: Variable overhead: 0.5 × 8.00 = \$4.00. Fixed overhead: 0.5 × 6.00 = \$3.00.
step: Standard cost per unit = 12 + 9 + 4 + 3 = \$28.00.
step: Standard cost allowed for 9,500 units = 9,500 × 28.00 = \$266,000. Standard hours allowed = 9,500 × 0.5 = 4,750 hours and standard materials allowed = 9,500 × 3 = 28,500 lb.
answer: \$28.00 per unit; \$266,000 for 9,500 units.

@mistakes
- Comparing actual results with the *static* budget and calling the entire difference a cost-control failure.
- Flexing fixed costs with volume: within the relevant range they stay at their budgeted totals.
- Mixing up the two variances: the sales volume variance is flexible minus static, and the flexible-budget variance is actual minus flexible.
- Computing the standard cost allowed from the inputs actually used rather than from the actual *output*.

@quiz self
prompt: A sales manager says, "We sold 15% more than planned, but the static-budget report shows variable costs 15% over budget, so the plant manager failed." In three or four sentences, explain what is wrong and what report you would produce.
hint: Ask whether variable costs should be higher when volume is higher.
solution: Variable costs are supposed to rise with volume, so a 15% increase in variable cost on 15% more units is exactly on plan; the static budget was set for a different volume and makes the comparison unfair. A flexible budget restates variable costs at the actual volume. The report should show the static budget, the sales volume variance, the flexible budget, the flexible-budget variance and actual results, so cost control and volume effects are judged separately.
rubric: Explains that variable costs should rise with volume, so the static budget comparison is misleading.
rubric: Proposes a flexible budget at actual volume as the benchmark.
rubric: Separates the sales volume variance from the flexible-budget variance.
