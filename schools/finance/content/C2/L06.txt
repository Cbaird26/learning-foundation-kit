@article
Two accountants cost the same factory's output and report different profits, yet both are right. The difference lies in one question: is fixed manufacturing overhead a cost of the *product* or a cost of the *period*?

### Two product-cost definitions

**Absorption (full) costing** includes all manufacturing costs in the product: direct materials, direct labor, variable manufacturing overhead **and fixed manufacturing overhead**. It is required for external reporting under US GAAP and IFRS (IAS 2).

**Variable (direct) costing** includes only variable manufacturing costs in the product (direct materials, direct labor, variable overhead). Fixed manufacturing overhead is expensed in full in the period incurred. It is used internally, because its format matches CVP analysis, and it is not acceptable for external financial statements.

| Cost | Absorption | Variable |
|---|---|---|
| Direct materials | product | product |
| Direct labor | product | product |
| Variable manufacturing overhead | product | product |
| Fixed manufacturing overhead | product | period |
| Selling and administrative (variable and fixed) | period | period |

Under absorption costing, fixed overhead per unit is the fixed overhead divided by a volume denominator (here, units produced).

### Two income statement formats

**Variable costing (contribution format):**
$$\text{Sales} - \text{Variable COGS} - \text{Variable selling and admin.} = \text{Contribution margin}; \quad CM - \text{All fixed costs} = \text{Operating income}.$$

**Absorption format:**
$$\text{Sales} - \text{COGS (at full cost)} = \text{Gross margin}; \quad \text{Gross margin} - \text{Selling and admin. expenses} = \text{Operating income}.$$

### Why the incomes differ

Under absorption costing, fixed overhead sits inside inventory. When production exceeds sales, some fixed overhead is *deferred* in ending inventory instead of expensed:

$$\text{Absorption income} - \text{Variable income} = (\text{Units produced} - \text{Units sold}) \times \text{Fixed overhead per unit},$$

which assumes the fixed overhead per unit is the same for opening and closing inventory. Three cases:

- Production > sales: inventory grows; absorption income is **higher**.
- Production < sales: inventory shrinks; absorption income is **lower** (fixed overhead from earlier periods is released).
- Production = sales: incomes are **equal**.

Over the life of the business total income is the same; only the timing differs. Because absorption income can be raised by building inventory, a manager measured on it may be tempted to overproduce. Variable costing income moves with sales, not production, which is why many managers prefer it internally.

### Contribution format and CVP

Because contribution-format data separate variable from fixed costs, they feed straight into CVP: break-even units = total fixed costs (manufacturing and selling) divided by the unit contribution margin, where the unit margin uses *all* variable costs.

@example
title: Two income statements, two answers
problem: In its first year, Harbor Lantern Co. produced 10,000 lamps and sold 8,000 at \$30. Per-unit variable costs: direct materials \$6, direct labor \$4, variable overhead \$2, variable selling \$3. Fixed manufacturing overhead is \$60,000 and fixed selling and administrative cost is \$40,000. Prepare income under each method and reconcile.
step: Variable costing: unit product cost = 6 + 4 + 2 = \$12. Sales = 8,000 × 30 = \$240,000. Variable COGS = 8,000 × 12 = 96,000; variable selling = 8,000 × 3 = 24,000. CM = 240,000 - 96,000 - 24,000 = \$120,000. Fixed costs = 60,000 + 40,000 = 100,000. Operating income = **\$20,000**.
step: Absorption costing: fixed overhead per unit = 60,000 / 10,000 = \$6, so unit product cost = 12 + 6 = \$18. COGS = 8,000 × 18 = \$144,000. Gross margin = 240,000 - 144,000 = \$96,000. Selling and administrative = 24,000 + 40,000 = 64,000. Operating income = **\$32,000**.
step: Reconcile: inventory grew by 2,000 units, each carrying \$6 of fixed overhead, so \$12,000 of fixed overhead was deferred. 20,000 + 12,000 = \$32,000. Ending inventory is \$24,000 under variable costing and \$36,000 under absorption costing.
answer: Variable costing \$20,000; absorption costing \$32,000; difference \$12,000 = 2,000 units × \$6.

@example
title: Break-even from contribution data
problem: A different firm sells a product for \$40. Variable manufacturing cost is \$22 per unit and variable selling cost is \$3 per unit. Fixed manufacturing overhead is \$50,000 and fixed selling and administrative cost is \$30,000. How many units give a pretax profit of \$25,000?
step: Unit contribution margin = 40 - 22 - 3 = \$15.
step: Total fixed costs = 50,000 + 30,000 = \$80,000.
step: Units for target profit = (80,000 + 25,000) / 15 = 7,000 units. Break-even alone would be 80,000 / 15 = 5,333.3, or 5,334 whole units.
answer: 7,000 units.

@mistakes
- Treating fixed manufacturing overhead as a period cost in the absorption income statement: it is in the product cost there.
- Applying the reconciliation formula with units *sold* minus produced, which reverses the sign.
- Using only variable manufacturing cost in the unit margin for CVP: variable selling costs also reduce the contribution.
- Presenting variable costing statements to external users: GAAP and IFRS require absorption costing for external reporting.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in three or four sentences why a manager paid a bonus on absorption-costing operating income might be tempted to build up inventory, and why variable costing removes that incentive.
hint: Where does fixed overhead go when production exceeds sales?
solution: Under absorption costing, fixed manufacturing overhead is part of product cost, so units that are produced but not sold carry fixed overhead into inventory as an asset instead of expensing it. Producing more units spreads the same fixed overhead over more units and defers more of it, raising reported operating income even though sales are unchanged. Variable costing expenses all fixed manufacturing overhead in the period, so income depends on sales volume and not on production, removing the incentive. Extra inventory also carries real storage and obsolescence costs.
rubric: Explains that absorption costing defers fixed overhead in ending inventory when production exceeds sales.
rubric: Concludes that building inventory raises absorption income without more sales.
rubric: Explains that variable costing expenses fixed overhead each period so income follows sales.
