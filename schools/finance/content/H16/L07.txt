@article
If a country's output is 5% larger than last year, is everyone better off? Only if the 5% is real: more goods and services, not just higher prices. Macroeconomics starts with measuring output correctly.

### What GDP is

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of all **final** goods and services produced **within a country** in a **given period** (usually a quarter or a year). Each word matters:

- **Final** goods are sold to their end users. Intermediate goods (flour sold to a bakery) are not counted separately, because their value is already inside the price of the bread. This avoids double counting.
- **Produced** in this period: the sale of a used car or an old house is not counted (only the dealer's fee is a new service), and neither are purely financial transactions (buying a share of stock) or **transfer payments** (Social Security benefits, which are not payment for production).
- **Within the country**: a car made in a US plant owned by a foreign firm counts in US GDP.
- Unpaid work (housework) and the underground economy are mostly missing, which is one limit of GDP as a measure of well-being.

### The expenditure approach

$$GDP = C + I + G + NX, \qquad NX = X - M.$$

- **C**, consumption: household purchases of goods and services (the largest component).
- **I**, investment: business spending on equipment, structures and software, new housing and changes in inventories. In economics "investment" does **not** mean buying stocks or bonds.
- **G**, government purchases of goods and services (not transfers).
- **NX**, net exports: exports $X$ minus imports $M$. It is negative when a country imports more than it exports.

GDP can equally be computed by adding all incomes earned from production (wages, rent, interest and profit, plus some adjustments): the **income approach** gives the same total.

### Nominal and real GDP

**Nominal GDP** uses current prices, so it rises when prices rise. **Real GDP** values output at the prices of a fixed **base year**, so it changes only when quantities change. (Official US figures use a refinement called chained dollars, but the idea is the same.) The **GDP deflator** links them:

$$\text{GDP deflator} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{real GDP}} \times 100, \qquad \text{real GDP} = \frac{\text{nominal GDP}}{\text{deflator}} \times 100.$$

In the base year the deflator is 100. The **growth rate** of real GDP between two years is $\dfrac{\text{real GDP}_2 - \text{real GDP}_1}{\text{real GDP}_1} \times 100\%$. **Real GDP per capita** divides by population and is a better guide to average living standards: its growth is about real GDP growth minus population growth.

### The business cycle and long-run growth

The **business cycle** has an expansion, a peak, a contraction (a **recession** when it is severe and broad, commonly described as at least two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, though in the United States the National Bureau of Economic Research uses a wider set of data to date recessions) and a trough. Long-run growth comes from more labor, more capital and better technology, and the **rule of 70** approximates the **doubling time** as $70/g$ years when output grows at $g$ percent a year. Current GDP data are published by the Bureau of Economic Analysis and are available on FRED.

@example
title: Adding up GDP
problem: An economy has (in billions of dollars) consumption 14,000, investment 3,500, government purchases 3,800, exports 2,500 and imports 3,000. Find net exports and GDP.
step: Net exports are $X - M = 2{,}500 - 3{,}000 = -500$. The country imports more than it exports.
step: GDP $= C + I + G + NX = 14{,}000 + 3{,}500 + 3{,}800 + (-500)$.
step: GDP $= 20{,}800$ billion dollars. Imports are subtracted because they are part of C, I and G but were produced abroad.
answer: NX $= -500$; GDP $= \$20{,}800$ billion.

@example
title: Real growth versus nominal growth
problem: In year 1 (the base year, deflator 100) nominal GDP is \$20,000 billion. In year 2 nominal GDP is \$21,424 billion and the GDP deflator is 103.2. Find real GDP in year 2, real growth and nominal growth.
step: Year 1 real GDP equals nominal GDP in the base year: \$20,000 billion.
step: Year 2 real GDP $= 21{,}424 / 103.2 \times 100 = \$20{,}759.7$ billion.
step: Real growth $= (20{,}759.7 - 20{,}000)/20{,}000 = 3.80\%$.
step: Nominal growth was $(21{,}424 - 20{,}000)/20{,}000 = 7.12\%$, so most of the nominal increase was real; the rest, about 3.2%, was a rise in the price level (the deflator).
answer: Real GDP \$20,759.7 billion; real growth 3.80%; nominal growth 7.12%.

@mistakes
- Counting intermediate goods, used goods, stock purchases or transfer payments in GDP.
- Forgetting to subtract imports, or counting imports in $G$, $C$ or $I$ and also in $NX$ with the wrong sign.
- Comparing nominal GDP in two years as if it measured output: use real GDP.
- Calling a purchase of stocks or bonds "investment" in the GDP sense; $I$ is spending on new capital goods, housing and inventories.

@practice num
prompt: An economy has consumption of 900, investment of 250, government purchases of 300, exports of 120 and imports of 150 (all in billions of dollars). What is GDP, in billions?
answer: 1420
unit: billion dollars
check: 900 + 250 + 300 + (120 - 150)
hint: Net exports are exports minus imports.
solution: $NX = 120 - 150 = -30$, so $GDP = 900 + 250 + 300 - 30 = 1{,}420$ billion dollars.

@practice num
prompt: GDP is 5,000, consumption is 3,400, investment is 900, exports are 600 and imports are 700 (billions of dollars). How large are government purchases, in billions?
answer: 800
unit: billion dollars
check: 5000 - 3400 - 900 - (600 - 700)
hint: Rearrange $GDP = C + I + G + (X - M)$ to solve for $G$.
solution: $G = GDP - C - I - (X - M) = 5{,}000 - 3{,}400 - 900 - (-100) = 800$ billion dollars.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following is counted in this year's US GDP?
choice: A household sells its used car to a neighbor for \$8,000.
choice: A person buys 10 shares of a company's stock.
choice: The government pays Social Security benefits to retirees.
choice*: A bakery buys a new oven made this year by a US manufacturer.
hint: GDP counts new final goods and services produced in the period.
solution: The new oven is newly produced capital, so it counts as investment. The used car was produced in an earlier year, the stock purchase is a financial transaction, and Social Security benefits are a transfer payment, not payment for current production.

@practice num
prompt: Nominal GDP is 3,150 billion dollars and the GDP deflator is 105 (base year = 100). What is real GDP, in billions of dollars?
answer: 3000
unit: billion dollars
check: 3150 / 105 * 100
hint: Divide nominal GDP by the deflator expressed as a ratio to 100.
solution: Real GDP $= 3{,}150/105 \times 100 = 3{,}000$ billion dollars.

answer: 3.68
unit: %
tol: 0.01

hint: Year 2 real GDP is $8{,}626/104 \times 100 = 8{,}294.2$.
solution: Real GDP in year 2 is $8{,}626/1.04 = 8{,}294.2$, so real growth is $(8{,}294.2 - 8{,}000)/8{,}000 = 3.68\%$. (Nominal GDP grew 7.83%, but 4% of that is price change.)

@quiz num
prompt: Real GDP grows by 3.0% in a year while the population grows by 1.2%. What is the growth rate of real GDP per capita, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 1.78
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.03 / 1.012 - 1) * 100
hint: Per capita GDP is real GDP divided by population, so divide the growth factors.
solution: $1.030/1.012 - 1 = 1.78\%$. (The approximation $3.0 - 1.2 = 1.8\%$ is close.)

@quiz num
prompt: Using the rule of 70, about how many years will it take real GDP to double if it grows steadily at 2.5% a year?
answer: 28
unit: years
check: 70 / 2.5
hint: Divide 70 by the growth rate in percent.
solution: $70/2.5 = 28$ years. (The exact value is $\ln 2/\ln 1.025 = 28.07$ years.)
