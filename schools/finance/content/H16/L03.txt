@article
A bakery raises the price of bread by 10%. Will it sell 1% less, or 30% less? The law of demand says only that quantity falls. **Elasticity** tells you by how much.

### Price elasticity of demand

The **price elasticity of demand** measures how strongly quantity demanded responds to a change in price:

$$E_d = \left|\frac{\%\Delta Q_d}{\%\Delta P}\right|.$$

We report the absolute value, so $E_d$ is a positive number. To avoid getting a different answer depending on which point you start from, economists use the **midpoint (arc) formula**, which measures each change relative to the average of the two values:

$$E_d = \left|\frac{(Q_2 - Q_1)/\left[(Q_1 + Q_2)/2\right]}{(P_2 - P_1)/\left[(P_1 + P_2)/2\right]}\right|.$$

Demand is:

- **elastic** if $E_d > 1$ (quantity changes by a larger percent than price),
- **inelastic** if $E_d < 1$,
- **unit elastic** if $E_d = 1$,
- **perfectly inelastic** ($E_d = 0$, a vertical curve) or **perfectly elastic** ($E_d$ infinite, a horizontal curve) at the extremes.

### What makes demand more elastic

Demand is more elastic when there are **close substitutes**, when the good is a **luxury** rather than a necessity, when it takes a **large share of the budget**, and when buyers have **more time** to adjust. Demand for a broad category (food) is less elastic than for a narrow one (one brand of cereal).

### Total revenue and elasticity

**Total revenue** is $P \times Q$. A price change moves revenue in a direction that depends on elasticity:

| Demand | Price rises | Price falls |
|---|---|---|
| Elastic ($E_d > 1$) | Revenue falls | Revenue rises |
| Inelastic ($E_d < 1$) | Revenue rises | Revenue falls |
| Unit elastic | No change | No change |

Along a straight-line demand curve elasticity changes: it is high at high prices and low at low prices. For $Q = a - bP$, the **point elasticity** is $E_d = b \cdot P/Q$, and revenue is highest where $E_d = 1$.

### Other elasticities

- **Income elasticity of demand** $= \%\Delta Q / \%\Delta(\text{income})$. It is positive for normal goods (above 1 for luxuries) and negative for inferior goods.
- **Cross-price elasticity of demand** $= \%\Delta Q_A / \%\Delta P_B$. It is positive for substitutes and negative for complements.
- **Price elasticity of supply** $= \%\Delta Q_s/\%\Delta P$. It is larger when producers can expand output easily, for example when they hold inventories or have spare capacity.

Elasticity is a tool, and it depends on assumptions: it describes a particular range of prices, other things equal. Unless a problem says otherwise, use the midpoint formula for price elasticity and simple percentage changes from the starting values for income and cross-price elasticity.

@example
title: Raising the price of a sandwich
problem: A shop sells 100 sandwiches a day at \$10. After raising the price to \$12 it sells 80. Find the price elasticity of demand (midpoint formula) and say what happened to total revenue.
step: Percent change in quantity, using the midpoint: $(80 - 100)/90 = -22.22\%$.
step: Percent change in price: $(12 - 10)/11 = +18.18\%$.
step: $E_d = 22.22/18.18 = 1.22$. Demand is elastic over this range, since $E_d > 1$.
step: Revenue was $10 \times 100 = \$1{,}000$ and is now $12 \times 80 = \$960$. A price rise with elastic demand lowers revenue, as the table predicts.
answer: $E_d \approx 1.22$ (elastic); revenue falls from \$1,000 to \$960.

@example
title: Elasticity along a straight demand curve
problem: Demand is $Q = 100 - 2P$. Find the point elasticity and total revenue at $P = 20$, $P = 25$ and $P = 30$.
step: Point elasticity is $E_d = 2P/Q$. At $P = 20$, $Q = 60$ and $E_d = 40/60 = 0.67$ (inelastic); revenue is $20 \times 60 = 1{,}200$.
step: At $P = 25$, $Q = 50$ and $E_d = 50/50 = 1$ (unit elastic); revenue is $25 \times 50 = 1{,}250$.
step: At $P = 30$, $Q = 40$ and $E_d = 60/40 = 1.5$ (elastic); revenue is $30 \times 40 = 1{,}200$.
step: Revenue peaks where demand is unit elastic. Below that price demand is inelastic, so raising the price raises revenue; above it demand is elastic, so raising the price lowers revenue.
answer: $E_d = 0.67$, $1$ and $1.5$; revenue \$1,200, \$1,250 and \$1,200.

@mistakes
- Reporting elasticity with a negative sign, or using the slope of the demand curve as the elasticity (slope depends on units; elasticity uses percent changes).
- Using the starting point as the base instead of the midpoint when the problem calls for the midpoint formula.
- Believing that revenue always rises when the price rises: it falls if demand is elastic.
- Mixing up the signs of cross-price elasticity: positive means substitutes and negative means complements.

@practice num
prompt: A theater sells 200 tickets at \$5 each. After a price cut to \$4 it sells 230 tickets. Using the midpoint formula, what is the price elasticity of demand? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.63
unit:
tol: 0.01
check: abs((230 - 200) / ((200 + 230) / 2)) / abs((4 - 5) / ((4 + 5) / 2))
hint: Percent change in quantity is the change divided by the average of 200 and 230.
hint: Do the same for price (average of 4 and 5), then divide the two percentages.
solution: $\%\Delta Q = 30/215 = 13.95\%$ and $\%\Delta P = -1/4.5 = -22.22\%$, so $E_d = 13.95/22.22 = 0.63$. Demand is inelastic, so the price cut lowers revenue (from \$1,000 to \$920).

@practice num
prompt: When a household's income rises from \$40,000 to \$44,000, its yearly purchases of restaurant meals rise from 50 to 53. Measuring each change relative to the starting value, what is the income elasticity of demand? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.6
tol: 0.01
check: ((53 - 50) / 50) / ((44000 - 40000) / 40000)
hint: Income rises by 10%.
solution: Quantity rises by $3/50 = 6\%$ and income by $4{,}000/40{,}000 = 10\%$, so the income elasticity is $6/10 = 0.6$. It is positive, so restaurant meals are a normal good, and below 1, so not a luxury.

@practice num
prompt: When the price of tea rises from \$2.00 to \$2.20 a box, a store's sales of coffee rise from 500 to 525 bags. Measuring each change relative to the starting value, what is the cross-price elasticity of demand for coffee with respect to the price of tea?
answer: 0.5
tol: 0.01
check: ((525 - 500) / 500) / ((2.20 - 2.00) / 2.00)
hint: Tea's price rose by 10%.
solution: Coffee quantity rose $25/500 = 5\%$ and tea's price rose $0.20/2.00 = 10\%$, so the cross-price elasticity is $5/10 = 0.5$. It is positive, so tea and coffee are substitutes.

@practice num
prompt: Demand for a snack is $Q = 90 - 3P$, with $P$ in dollars. Find the point price elasticity of demand at $P = 10$, using $E_d = 3P/Q$.
answer: 0.5
tol: 0.01
check: 3 * 10 / (90 - 3 * 10)
hint: First find $Q$ at $P = 10$.
solution: $Q = 90 - 30 = 60$, so $E_d = 3 \times 10/60 = 0.5$. Demand is inelastic at this price, so a small price rise would raise revenue.

@quiz num
prompt: A gym has 120 members at a monthly fee of \$50. When it cuts the fee to \$40 it gains members and has 168. Using the midpoint formula, what is the price elasticity of demand? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 1.5
tol: 0.01
check: abs((168 - 120) / ((120 + 168) / 2)) / abs((40 - 50) / ((50 + 40) / 2))
hint: Use the average of 120 and 168 and the average of 50 and 40 as the bases.
solution: $\%\Delta Q = 48/144 = 33.33\%$ and $\%\Delta P = -10/45 = -22.22\%$, so $E_d = 33.33/22.22 = 1.5$. Demand is elastic, so the price cut raises revenue (from \$6,000 to \$6,720).

@quiz num
prompt: Over a given range of prices the price elasticity of demand for a streaming service is 0.5. Assume this elasticity applies exactly to a 10% price increase, so that quantity falls by 5%. By what percent does total revenue change? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 4.5
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (1.10 * 0.95 - 1) * 100
hint: Revenue is $P \times Q$, so multiply the two growth factors.
solution: Price is multiplied by 1.10 and quantity by $0.95$, so revenue is multiplied by $1.10 \times 0.95 = 1.045$: a rise of 4.5%.
