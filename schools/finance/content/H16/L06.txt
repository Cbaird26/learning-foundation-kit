@article
A factory sells steel at the market price but its smoke harms nearby residents, who are not paid for the damage. A market can fail even when it is competitive, because the price does not reflect every cost and benefit.

### Efficiency and market failure

A competitive market is **efficient** when it maximizes total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus): the last unit's marginal benefit equals its marginal cost. A **market failure** is a case where the market outcome is not efficient. The main causes are externalities, public goods, market power (last lesson) and imperfect information.

### Externalities

An **externality** is a cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on a third party. With a **negative externality** (pollution), the **marginal social cost (MSC)** is the private marginal cost plus the marginal external cost, so the market produces too much. With a **positive externality** (vaccination, education), the **marginal social benefit (MSB)** is private marginal benefit plus the marginal external benefit, so the market produces too little. The efficient quantity is where $MSB = MSC$.

The loss from producing the wrong quantity is a **deadweight loss**: the triangle between MSB and MSC over the units that should not (or should) be produced.

### Policies for externalities

- **Pigouvian tax** (per-unit tax equal to the marginal external cost) makes the producer face the full social cost. A **subsidy** equal to the marginal external benefit does the same for a positive externality.
- **Regulation** sets a standard, such as a limit on emissions.
- **Tradable permits** cap total pollution and let firms trade the right to emit, so the cuts happen where they cost least.
- **Coase theorem:** if property rights are clear and bargaining costs are low, the affected parties can negotiate an efficient outcome without government. With many people involved, bargaining is costly, which is why governments often step in.

Economists debate which tool is best. Taxes and permits use prices and are usually cheaper for a given reduction, standards give more certainty about the outcome, and critics of any intervention point to the difficulty of measuring the external cost correctly. Assumptions matter: the tax equals the external cost only if the external cost is measured correctly.

### Tax incidence

Who really pays a tax is not decided by who sends the money to the government. The **incidence** depends on elasticities: the more inelastic side of the market bears more of the tax. For example, a tax collected from sellers raises the price paid by buyers and lowers the price kept by sellers.

### Public goods and common resources

Goods are classified by two features. A good is **rival** if one person's use reduces another's, and **excludable** if non-payers can be kept out.

| | Excludable | Not excludable |
|---|---|---|
| **Rival** | Private good (a sandwich) | Common resource (ocean fish) |
| **Not rival** | Club good (a streaming service) | Public good (national defense) |

Public goods suffer from the **free-rider problem**: people can enjoy them without paying, so private markets underprovide them, and governments often pay for them from taxes. Common resources suffer from overuse (the **tragedy of the commons**) because each user ignores the cost imposed on others.

@example
title: A Pigouvian tax on pollution
problem: Demand for steel is $P = 100 - Q$ and the private marginal cost is $20 + Q$. Each unit also imposes an external cost of \$20 on neighbors. Find the market and efficient outputs, the deadweight loss and the tax needed.
step: Market: $100 - Q = 20 + Q$ gives $Q = 40$ and $P = \$60$.
step: Marginal social cost is $20 + Q + 20 = 40 + Q$. Efficient output: $100 - Q = 40 + Q$ gives $Q = 30$, with $P = \$70$.
step: Deadweight loss: the triangle with a base of $40 - 30 = 10$ units and a height of \$20 (the external cost, which is the gap between MSC and MB at $Q = 40$): $\frac{1}{2} \times 10 \times 20 = \$100$.
step: A tax of \$20 a unit, equal to the marginal external cost, shifts the private cost curve up to $40 + Q$, so the market produces 30 units. Revenue is $20 \times 30 = \$600$.
answer: Market $Q = 40$, efficient $Q = 30$, deadweight loss \$100, tax \$20 per unit.

@example
title: A subsidy for a positive externality
problem: Demand for flu shots is $P = 60 - Q$ and the marginal cost is $10 + Q$. Each shot gives others a benefit of \$10. Find the efficient quantity and the subsidy.
step: Market: $60 - Q = 10 + Q$ gives $Q = 25$.
step: Marginal social benefit is $60 - Q + 10 = 70 - Q$. Efficient: $70 - Q = 10 + Q$ gives $Q = 30$.
step: A subsidy of \$10 per shot, equal to the external benefit, makes buyers' effective demand $70 - Q$, so the market delivers 30 shots.
step: The deadweight loss without the subsidy is $\frac{1}{2} \times (30 - 25) \times 10 = \$25$.
answer: Efficient $Q = 30$ (market gives 25); subsidy \$10; deadweight loss \$25.

@mistakes
- Setting the Pigouvian tax equal to the price, or to the deadweight loss, instead of the marginal external cost at the efficient quantity.
- Assuming that the person who writes the check bears the tax: the incidence depends on elasticities, not on who pays the government.
- Calling any good that the government provides a public good: the definition is non-rival and non-excludable.
- Thinking that all pollution should be eliminated: the efficient level is where marginal social benefit equals marginal social cost, which is usually above zero.

answer: 36
lead: \$
check: 0.5 * (35 - 29) * 12
hint: The loss is a triangle: its base is the excess quantity and its height is the external cost per unit.
solution: At $Q = 35$ the gap between marginal social cost ($22 + 35 = 57$) and marginal benefit ($80 - 35 = 45$) is \$12. The triangle's base is $35 - 29 = 6$ units, so the deadweight loss is $\frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 12 = \$36$.
