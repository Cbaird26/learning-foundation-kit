@article
Your phone, your shirt and your breakfast cereal probably involve inputs from several countries. Trade lets each country specialize, and exchange rates translate the prices of different countries into a common unit.

### Gains from trade

As in week 1, countries gain from trade when each specializes in goods where its opportunity cost is lower (**comparative advantage**) and then trades. Trade lets a country consume outside its production possibilities frontier. When a country opens to trade, the domestic price moves to the **world price**. If the world price is below the domestic price, the country **imports** the good; consumers gain and domestic producers lose, but the consumers' gain is larger than the producers' loss, so total surplus rises.

### Tariffs and quotas

A **tariff** is a tax on imports; a **quota** is a limit on the quantity imported. A tariff raises the domestic price above the world price: domestic quantity demanded falls, domestic quantity supplied rises, imports fall and the government collects revenue. Consumers lose surplus, domestic producers gain and the government gains tariff revenue; the consumer loss exceeds the other two together, leaving a **deadweight loss**. People disagree about trade policy. Supporters of tariffs cite protecting specific jobs, national security, infant industries and bargaining power; opponents point to higher consumer prices, costs to industries that use imported inputs and the risk of retaliation. Economists also stress that the gains and losses fall on different groups, so the question is also about who bears the adjustment costs.

### The balance of payments

The **current account** records trade in goods and services, income and transfers; the **capital and financial account** records purchases of assets across borders. In principle they sum to zero: a country that imports more than it exports (a trade deficit) must finance the gap by selling assets or borrowing from abroad. Whether a trade deficit is a problem depends on why it arises and what the borrowing is used for.

### Exchange rates

The **exchange rate** is the price of one currency in terms of another. If one euro costs \$1.10, then \$1 buys $1/1.10 = 0.909$ euros. When a currency buys more foreign currency, it **appreciates** (a stronger dollar); when it buys less, it **depreciates**. In a **floating** exchange-rate system, rates are set by the supply of and demand for currencies. Currency demand rises with: foreign demand for a country's goods, **higher interest rates** at home (which attract foreign funds), lower domestic inflation (purchasing power parity: the rate adjusts toward equal purchasing power), and expectations. A **fixed** exchange rate is held by a government commitment to buy or sell its currency.

### What a stronger dollar does

If the dollar appreciates, US goods cost more in foreign currency, so **exports fall**; foreign goods cost less in dollars, so **imports rise**; net exports $X - M$ fall, which shifts aggregate demand left. A weaker dollar does the opposite. Importers and travelers abroad gain from a strong dollar, and exporters and domestic producers who compete with imports lose.

Spot rates change by the minute, so never rely on a rate in a textbook: current exchange rates are on FRED and the Federal Reserve's website.

@example
title: A tariff on imported steel
problem: Domestic demand for steel is $Q_d = 120 - 4P$ and domestic supply is $Q_s = 2P$. The world price is \$10 and the country imports freely. Then it imposes a \$4 tariff. Find imports before and after, the tariff revenue and the deadweight loss.
step: Free trade at $P = 10$: $Q_d = 120 - 40 = 80$, $Q_s = 20$, so imports are 60.
step: With the tariff the domestic price is $10 + 4 = \$14$: $Q_d = 120 - 56 = 64$, $Q_s = 28$, imports $= 36$.
step: Tariff revenue is $4 \times 36 = \$144$.
step: Consumer surplus falls by $4 \times (64 + 80)/2 = \$288$. Producers gain $4 \times (20 + 28)/2 = \$96$. Net loss $= 288 - 96 - 144 = \$48$.
step: Check with the two triangles: $\frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (28 - 20) + \frac{1}{2} \times 4 \times (80 - 64) = 16 + 32 = \$48$.
answer: Imports fall from 60 to 36; revenue \$144; deadweight loss \$48.

@example
title: A stronger dollar
problem: A US firm sells a machine for \$220. The exchange rate falls from \$1.10 per euro to \$0.90 per euro, so the dollar strengthens. How do the machine's euro price and the value of the dollar change?
step: At \$1.10 per euro, the euro price is $220/1.10 = 200$ euros.
step: At \$0.90 per euro, the euro price is $220/0.90 = 244.44$ euros, a 22.2% rise, though the dollar price has not changed.
step: The dollar now buys $1/0.90 = 1.1111$ euros instead of $1/1.10 = 0.9091$: it appreciated by $1.1111/0.9091 - 1 = 22.2\%$.
step: European buyers face a higher price, so US export sales to Europe tend to fall, other things equal. How much depends on the elasticity of their demand.
answer: The euro price rises from 200 to 244.44 euros; the dollar appreciated 22.2%.

@mistakes
- Treating a tariff as a gain for the economy overall: producers and the government gain, but consumers lose more.
- Reversing the direction of an exchange rate: if it takes fewer dollars to buy one euro, the dollar has appreciated.
- Saying that a stronger dollar raises exports: it lowers them (and raises imports).
- Assuming that a trade deficit is by itself a loss of output: it reflects capital flowing the other way, and effects depend on why it arises.

@practice self

hint: Think of what happens to the euro price of a US good priced in dollars.
solution: A stronger dollar makes US goods more expensive for European buyers: a machine priced at \$100,000 that cost 90,909 euros at \$1.10 per euro costs 100,000 euros at \$1.00 per euro, about 10% more, even though the dollar price has not changed. Net exports ($X - M$) fall, which shifts US aggregate demand left, tending to reduce real GDP and the price level. US exporters and import-competing firms lose, while importers and consumers gain.

rubric: States that European goods cost less in dollars, so imports rise, and net exports fall.
rubric: Connects the fall in net exports to a leftward shift in aggregate demand.

@quiz num
prompt: Domestic demand is $Q_d = 90 - P$ and domestic supply is $Q_s = 2P$. The world price is \$15 and the country imports freely. The government imposes a tariff of \$3 per unit. What is the deadweight loss of the tariff, in dollars?
answer: 13.5
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * 3 * ((2 * 18) - (2 * 15)) + 0.5 * 3 * ((90 - 15) - (90 - 18))
hint: Find quantities demanded and supplied at \$15 and at \$18.
hint: The loss is two triangles, each with a base of \$3 (the tariff).
solution: At \$15: $Q_d = 75$, $Q_s = 30$. At \$18: $Q_d = 72$, $Q_s = 36$. The production triangle is $\frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times (36 - 30) = 9$ and the consumption triangle is $\frac{1}{2} \times 3 \times (75 - 72) = 4.5$, so the deadweight loss is \$13.50.
