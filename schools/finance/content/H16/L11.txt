@article
When the Federal Reserve announces that it is lowering its interest-rate target, mortgage rates, car loans and business borrowing costs tend to fall. Monetary policy works mostly through the price of borrowing.

### The goals of the Fed

By law the Fed has a **dual mandate**: maximum employment and stable prices. The FOMC has stated a longer-run goal of 2% inflation, measured by the PCE price index. Its main tool is a **target range for the federal funds rate**, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves, which it steers with administered rates (IORB and the overnight reverse repurchase facility) and, when it chooses, with purchases or sales of securities. (Do not memorize a current target: look it up on the Federal Reserve's website or on FRED.)

### The transmission of policy

In the **money market**, the **demand for money** slopes down with the nominal interest rate, because the interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding money rather than bonds. In the classic money-market graph, the Fed's actions set the short-term interest rate where money demand meets the supply of money. In today's ample-reserves system the Fed gets the same result by moving its administered rates, and market rates follow.

**Expansionary (easy) policy:** the Fed lowers its target, so interest rates fall; borrowing and investment ($I$) and interest-sensitive consumption rise; a lower US interest rate also tends to weaken the dollar, raising net exports; aggregate demand shifts right, raising real GDP and, in the short run, the price level. **Contractionary (tight) policy** reverses each link: higher rates, less investment, lower AD, lower inflation but also higher unemployment. These effects operate with **long and variable lags**, and expectations matter.

Distinguish the **nominal** interest rate from the **real** interest rate ($r \approx i - \pi$): firms invest based on the real rate.

### Bond prices and interest rates

A bond that promises a single payment of \$1,000 in one year (a **zero-coupon bond**) is worth the present value of that payment: $P = 1000/(1 + y)$ where $y$ is the market interest rate. If $y$ rises, the price falls, and vice versa, because a higher rate discounts the future payment more. The longer the time to payment, the more a rate change moves the price.

### Rules, discretion and the quantity theory

The **Taylor rule** is a simple benchmark for the policy rate:

$$i = \pi + r^* + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5(\text{output gap}),$$

where $\pi$ is inflation, $r^*$ the neutral real rate, $\pi^*$ the inflation target and the output gap is the percent by which real GDP is above potential. It tells the central bank to raise rates when inflation is above target or the economy is overheating. Supporters of rules argue that they bring predictability and discipline; supporters of discretion argue that no rule can capture shocks and that judgment is needed. The Taylor rule is also a teaching model: its inputs (especially $r^*$ and the output gap) must be estimated and are uncertain.

The **equation of exchange** is $M V = P Y$: the money supply times the velocity of money equals the price level times real output. If velocity $V$ is constant, then the growth of $M$ approximates the growth of $P$ plus the growth of $Y$. This **quantity theory of money** says that money growth beyond real growth leads to inflation in the long run; economists dispute how stable velocity is in the short run. Always check the assumptions before using the formula.

@example
title: How a rate change moves a bond price
problem: A one-year zero-coupon bond pays \$1,000 at maturity. Find its price when the market rate is 4% and when it rises to 5%.
step: At 4%: $P = 1000/1.04 = \$961.54$.
step: At 5%: $P = 1000/1.05 = \$952.38$.
step: The price falls by $961.54 - 952.38 = \$9.16$ when the rate rises by one percentage point.
step: The higher rate discounts the \$1,000 more, so the bond is worth less today. A saver who holds the bond sees a loss in its market price if rates rise.
answer: \$961.54 at 4% and \$952.38 at 5%, a fall of about \$9.16.

@example
title: A Taylor-rule benchmark
problem: Inflation is 3%, the neutral real rate is 2%, the target is 2% and real GDP is 1% below potential. What policy rate does the Taylor rule suggest?
step: Inflation gap: $\pi - \pi^* = 3 - 2 = 1$. Output gap: $-1$.
step: $i = 3 + 2 + 0.5(1) + 0.5(-1)$.
step: $i = 5 + 0.5 - 0.5 = 5.0\%$.
step: Inflation above target pushes the rate up; the weak economy pushes it down. Here they cancel, so the rate is the inflation plus the neutral real rate. These numbers are hypothetical.
answer: 5.0%.

@mistakes
- Believing that the Fed sets all interest rates directly: it targets a short-term rate, and longer-term rates also depend on expectations and markets.
- Thinking that higher interest rates raise bond prices: prices and yields move in opposite directions.
- Confusing the nominal and real interest rate when judging the effect on investment.
- Treating the quantity theory as an exact law without stating that velocity and real output are assumed given.

@practice num
prompt: In the equation of exchange $MV = PY$, velocity $V$ is constant. The money supply grows 5% and real output grows 2%. By what percent does the price level rise? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 2.94
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.05 / 1.02 - 1) * 100
hint: With $V$ constant, $P = MV/Y$, so $P$ grows by the ratio of the growth factors of $M$ and $Y$.
solution: $P$ is multiplied by $1.05/1.02 = 1.0294$, a rise of 2.94% (the approximation $5 - 2 = 3\%$ is close).

@practice num
prompt: A two-year zero-coupon bond pays \$1,000 at maturity. By how many dollars does its price fall when the market interest rate rises from 4% to 5%? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 17.53
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 1000 / 1.04 ** 2 - 1000 / 1.05 ** 2
hint: Price the bond at each rate with two years of discounting.
solution: At 4%, $P = 1000/1.04^2 = \$924.56$. At 5%, $P = 1000/1.05^2 = \$907.03$. The fall is \$17.53, about twice the one-year bond's fall (\$9.16 for the same rate rise) because the longer bond is more sensitive.

@quiz num
prompt: Use the Taylor rule $i = \pi + r^* + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5(\text{gap})$. Inflation is 4%, the neutral real rate is 2%, the inflation target is 2% and the output gap is +2%. What policy rate does the rule suggest, in percent?
answer: 8
unit: %
check: 4 + 2 + 0.5 * (4 - 2) + 0.5 * 2
hint: Compute each part: inflation, neutral rate, inflation gap term and output gap term.
solution: $i = 4 + 2 + 0.5(2) + 0.5(2) = 6 + 1 + 1 = 8\%$.
