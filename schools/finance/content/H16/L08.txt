@article
A friend says, "Unemployment fell, so the economy must be healthy." But that figure counts only people actively looking for work, and inflation can make a rising paycheck buy less. Two numbers, the unemployment rate and the inflation rate, are the headline gauges of the economy.

### Measuring unemployment

In the United States the Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys households each month. Adults (16 and over, not in the military or in institutions) are classified as:

- **Employed**: worked for pay in the survey week.
- **Unemployed**: without a job, available and actively looking for work.
- **Not in the labor force**: everyone else (students, retirees, caregivers, and **discouraged workers** who have stopped looking).

The **labor force** is employed plus unemployed. Then:

$$\text{unemployment rate} = \frac{\text{unemployed}}{\text{labor force}} \times 100\%, \qquad \text{participation rate} = \frac{\text{labor force}}{\text{adult population}} \times 100\%.$$

If a discouraged worker stops looking, the unemployment rate **falls** even though nobody found a job, which is one reason to read it alongside the participation rate.

### Types of unemployment

- **Frictional**: short-term, from the normal time it takes to search and match (a graduate looking for a first job).
- **Structural**: a mismatch between workers' skills or location and available jobs, caused by technology or shifts in industries, or by wage floors.
- **Cyclical**: caused by a downturn in the business cycle, when total spending falls.

The **natural rate of unemployment** is the sum of frictional and structural unemployment: what remains at **full employment**, when cyclical unemployment is zero. It is not fixed, and it is not zero.

### Measuring inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level. The **consumer price index (CPI)** prices a fixed basket of goods and services that a typical urban household buys:

$$\text{CPI} = \frac{\text{cost of the basket in the current year}}{\text{cost of the basket in the base year}} \times 100, \qquad \text{inflation rate} = \frac{\text{CPI}_2 - \text{CPI}_1}{\text{CPI}_1} \times 100\%.$$

A fixed basket overstates the rise in the cost of living because of **substitution bias** (people buy less of what became expensive) and **quality changes**, though statisticians adjust for some of this. The Federal Reserve's preferred measure is the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE), which uses a different basket and weights.

### Costs of inflation and real values

Inflation that is **unexpected** redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers and from those on fixed incomes to others; even expected inflation causes "menu costs" and distorts taxes. To compare money across time, convert **nominal** to **real** values by dividing by a price index. The **Fisher equation** relates the nominal interest rate $i$, the real interest rate $r$ and inflation $\pi$:

$$1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi), \quad\text{so}\quad r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi} - 1 \approx i - \pi.$$

Use any real-time numbers with care: the unemployment rate and inflation rate change monthly, so look up current figures on FRED or the BLS website instead of trusting a textbook.

@example
title: Unemployment and participation
problem: A country has an adult population of 250 million. 152 million are employed and 8 million are unemployed. Find the labor force, the unemployment rate and the participation rate. Then suppose 2 million of the unemployed stop looking. What happens to the unemployment rate?
step: Labor force $= 152 + 8 = 160$ million.
step: Unemployment rate $= 8/160 = 5.0\%$. Participation rate $= 160/250 = 64.0\%$.
step: If 2 million unemployed give up, they leave the labor force: unemployed $= 6$, labor force $= 158$.
step: The new unemployment rate is $6/158 = 3.80\%$, although nobody found a job and the participation rate fell to $158/250 = 63.2\%$.
answer: Labor force 160 million; unemployment 5.0%; participation 64.0%. After discouragement, unemployment is 3.80%.

@example
title: From a price index to inflation
problem: A basket of goods cost \$200 in the base year. It costs \$212 in year 1 and \$218.36 in year 2. Find the CPI each year and the inflation rate from year 1 to year 2.
step: $\text{CPI}_1 = 212/200 \times 100 = 106.0$.
step: $\text{CPI}_2 = 218.36/200 \times 100 = 109.18$.
step: Inflation $= (109.18 - 106.0)/106.0 = 3.0\%$.
step: A worker whose nominal wage rose 3.0% has an unchanged real wage, since prices rose by the same percent.
answer: CPI 106.0 and 109.18; inflation 3.0%.

@mistakes
- Dividing unemployed by the whole population instead of the labor force, or counting students and retirees as unemployed.
- Saying that a falling unemployment rate always means more people have jobs: people leaving the labor force lower the rate too.
- Calculating inflation as a difference in CPI points rather than a percent change.
- Treating the natural rate as zero unemployment, or the real interest rate as nominal plus inflation.

unit: %
tol: 0.01
