@article
You have 3 free hours on Saturday. You could work a shift at \$14 an hour, study for a test, or go to a movie. You cannot do all three. That simple fact is the root of economics.

### Scarcity and choice

**Scarcity** means that people's wants are unlimited but the resources available to satisfy them (land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship, the **factors of production**) are limited. Because of scarcity, every choice gives something up. **Economics** is the study of how individuals, firms and societies choose to use scarce resources.

### Opportunity cost

The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the value of the best alternative you give up. It is not the sum of all alternatives, only the single next-best one. Opportunity cost includes:

- **explicit costs**, which are payments of money (a \$12 movie ticket), and
- **implicit costs**, which are the value of what you give up without writing a check (3 hours of pay you did not earn).

The **economic cost** of going to the movie is the explicit cost plus the implicit cost. A **sunk cost**, money already spent and impossible to recover, is not an opportunity cost of a future choice and should not change it.

Economists also think **at the margin**: the right question is usually "what is the extra benefit and the extra cost of one more unit?" and not "should I do this at all?"

### The production possibilities frontier

An economy that makes only two goods can be described by a **production possibilities frontier (PPF)**, the curve showing the maximum combinations of the two goods it can produce with its resources and technology. Suppose the economy makes only tractors and laptops.

| Point | Tractors | Laptops |
|---|---|---|
| A | 0 | 100 |
| B | 1 | 90 |
| C | 2 | 70 |
| D | 3 | 40 |
| E | 4 | 0 |

- Points on the PPF are **attainable and efficient**: you cannot get more of one good without giving up some of the other.
- Points inside the PPF are attainable but **inefficient** (idle or misused resources, as in a deep recession).
- Points outside the PPF are **unattainable** with current resources and technology. Economic growth, from more resources or better technology, shifts the PPF outward.

Moving from B to C costs 20 laptops for 1 more tractor; moving from C to D costs 30; from D to E, 40. The opportunity cost of a tractor **rises** as more tractors are made, because resources are not equally good at both jobs. This gives the PPF its bowed-out shape. A straight-line PPF means a constant opportunity cost.

### Comparative advantage and trade

A person has an **absolute advantage** if they can produce more of a good with the same resources. They have a **comparative advantage** if their opportunity cost of producing it is lower. Gains from trade depend on comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, because it is impossible for one party to have the lower opportunity cost of both goods.

Assume Ana makes 12 loaves of bread or 4 cakes per hour, and Ben makes 6 loaves or 3 cakes per hour. Ana has the absolute advantage in both goods. Her opportunity cost of one cake is $12/4 = 3$ loaves; Ben's is $6/3 = 2$ loaves. So Ben has the comparative advantage in cakes and Ana in bread. If each specializes in the good where their opportunity cost is lower and they trade at a price between the two opportunity costs, both can end up with a combination that neither could reach alone.

@example
title: The true cost of a concert
problem: Maya can work 4 hours at \$15 an hour or attend a concert. The ticket costs \$35 and she has already bought a nonrefundable \$10 parking pass that she can use only if she attends. What is the economic cost of attending? Assume that working is her best alternative.
step: The explicit cost of going is the ticket: \$35. The parking pass is already paid and cannot be recovered, so it is a sunk cost and does not count.
step: The implicit cost is the pay she gives up: $4 \times \$15 = \$60$.
step: The economic cost is explicit plus implicit: $\$35 + \$60 = \$95$. If the benefit she expects from the concert is worth less than \$95 to her, she should work instead.
answer: \$95 (the \$10 pass is a sunk cost).

@example
title: Who should make what?
problem: In one hour Ana makes 12 loaves of bread or 4 cakes. Ben makes 6 loaves or 3 cakes. Each works 8 hours. Without trade each splits time evenly. Show a trade that makes both better off.
step: Without trade, Ana spends 4 hours on each good: 48 loaves and 16 cakes. Ben does the same: 24 loaves and 12 cakes. Together: 72 loaves and 28 cakes.
step: Opportunity costs: Ana gives up 3 loaves per cake and Ben gives up 2. Ben has the comparative advantage in cakes, so Ben makes only cakes: $8 \times 3 = 24$ cakes.
step: To keep the total at 28 cakes, Ana makes the other 4 cakes, which takes her 1 hour. She spends the other 7 hours on bread: $7 \times 12 = 84$ loaves.
step: The total is 84 loaves and 28 cakes against 72 loaves and 28 cakes before: 12 more loaves with no loss of cakes. Trading at a price between 2 and 3 loaves per cake lets both of them share this gain.
answer: 84 loaves and 28 cakes in total (12 more loaves than without specialization).

@mistakes
- Counting every alternative, or the average of them, as the opportunity cost instead of only the best one given up.
- Treating a sunk cost (a nonrefundable payment already made) as part of the cost of a future decision, or forgetting implicit costs such as forgone wages.
- Confusing absolute and comparative advantage: the party that is better at everything still gains from trade, because the comparison that matters is opportunity cost.
- Reading a point inside the PPF as unattainable (it is attainable but inefficient) or a point outside it as attainable.
