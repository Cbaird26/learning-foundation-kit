@article
You deposit \$1,000 in a bank. The bank does not keep it in a vault: it lends most of it to someone who spends it, and that money ends up in another account, from which more is lent. How does one deposit support many dollars of money?

### Money and its functions

**Money** is anything generally accepted as payment. It has three functions: a **medium of exchange** (it avoids the double coincidence of wants in barter), a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it) and a **store of value** (it keeps purchasing power over time, though inflation erodes it). Today's money is **fiat money**: it has value by law and trust, not because it is backed by gold. Narrow measures such as **M1** count the most liquid forms (currency, checkable deposits and, since a 2020 redefinition, savings deposits), and broader measures such as **M2** add less liquid assets such as small time deposits and retail money market fund shares; the exact definitions change, and the Federal Reserve publishes them.

### How banks create deposits

A bank's **balance sheet** has assets (reserves, loans, securities) and liabilities (deposits). **Reserves** are the deposits a bank holds as vault cash or at the Federal Reserve. In the textbook model, the **required reserve ratio** $rr$ is the fraction of deposits a bank must hold in reserve; it lends the rest, called **excess reserves**.

Suppose $rr = 10\%$ and you deposit \$1,000. The bank keeps \$100 and lends \$900. The borrower spends it, and the seller deposits \$900 in another bank, which keeps \$90 and lends \$810, and so on. Each round, new deposits are 90% of the last. The total is a geometric series:

$$\text{total new deposits} = \frac{\text{initial deposit}}{rr}, \qquad \text{money multiplier} = \frac{1}{rr}.$$

With $rr = 0.10$, the multiplier is 10 and the \$1,000 deposit can support up to \$10,000 of deposits, of which \$9,000 is new money created by loans (the original \$1,000 was already in the system). This is a **maximum**: if banks hold extra reserves or if people keep some of their loans as cash (a **cash drain**), the actual multiplier is smaller. Since banks' lending is constrained mainly by loan demand and capital, a high multiplier is not an iron law.

### The Federal Reserve

The **Federal Reserve** (the Fed) is the US central bank. It has a Board of Governors (seven members) in Washington, twelve regional Reserve Banks, and the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**, which sets monetary policy: its voting members are the seven governors, the president of the New York Fed and four other Reserve Bank presidents who rotate. The Fed also acts as lender of last resort and supervises many banks; the FDIC insures deposits up to \$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, which reduces the risk of bank runs.

Fed tools:

- **Open market operations**: buying government securities adds reserves to the banking system, and selling removes them.
- **Administered rates**: the interest rate on reserve balances (IORB) paid to banks and the overnight reverse repurchase (ON RRP) facility help steer short-term market rates, including the federal funds rate, in an **ample-reserves** system.
- **The discount rate**, the rate charged on loans directly to banks.
- **Reserve requirements**: in the United States these have been set at zero since March 2020, so the textbook multiplier is a teaching model, not a description of how US banks are constrained today.

@example
title: Following a \$1,000 deposit
problem: With a required reserve ratio of 10%, trace a new \$1,000 deposit through the first three banks and find the maximum total increase in deposits.
| Bank | New deposit | Required reserves | Loan made |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,000.00 | 100.00 | 900.00 |
| 2 | 900.00 | 90.00 | 810.00 |
| 3 | 810.00 | 81.00 | 729.00 |
step: Bank 1 holds 10% of \$1,000 as reserves (\$100) and lends the remaining \$900, which becomes Bank 2's deposit.
step: Bank 2 holds \$90 and lends \$810; Bank 3 holds \$81 and lends \$729. After three banks, deposits have risen by $1{,}000 + 900 + 810 = \$2{,}710$.
step: The series continues forever. Total new deposits are $1{,}000/0.10 = \$10{,}000$, total required reserves are \$1,000 (the original deposit) and total loans are \$9,000.
answer: Deposits can rise by up to \$10,000 (loans \$9,000), with a multiplier of 10.

@example
title: An open market purchase
problem: The required reserve ratio is 10%. The Fed buys \$20 million of securities from banks. What is the maximum change in deposits, assuming banks lend all excess reserves?
step: The purchase raises banks' reserves by \$20 million (and lowers their securities by the same amount), so excess reserves rise by \$20 million.
step: The multiplier is $1/0.10 = 10$.
step: Maximum increase in deposits $= 10 \times 20 = \$200$ million.
step: The result is a ceiling: leakage into cash and extra reserves would make it smaller.
answer: At most \$200 million.

@mistakes
- Saying a bank lends out the full deposit: it keeps required reserves (and often extra) and lends only the rest.
- Mixing up the total increase in deposits ($\text{deposit}/rr$) with the new money created by loans ($\text{deposit}/rr$ minus the original deposit).
- Counting a deposit as an asset of the bank: it is a liability (the bank owes it to the depositor); loans and reserves are assets.
- Treating the multiplier as exact: cash held by the public and extra reserves held by banks reduce it.
