@article
When shoppers and businesses cut their spending, factories slow and people lose jobs. When spending surges, prices climb. The **aggregate demand and aggregate supply (AD-AS)** model organizes both stories with two curves.

### Aggregate demand

**Aggregate demand (AD)** is the total real spending on domestically produced goods and services at each price level: $AD = C + I + G + NX$. The AD curve slopes down because of the **wealth effect** (a higher price level cuts the real value of savings), the **interest-rate effect** (it raises interest rates and cuts investment) and the **exchange-rate effect** (it makes exports dearer and imports cheaper, lowering net exports).

AD **shifts** when anything in $C$, $I$, $G$ or $NX$ changes at a given price level: consumer confidence, expected profits, interest rates, government spending or taxes, and foreign incomes or exchange rates.

### Aggregate supply

**Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS)** slopes up, because some prices and wages are slow to adjust ("sticky"): when the price level rises, firms' profits rise and they produce more. SRAS shifts with **input costs** (wages, oil), productivity, taxes and regulations, and expectations of inflation. **Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS)** is vertical at **potential output** (full-employment output), because in the long run output depends on resources and technology, not on the price level.

### Equilibrium and gaps

Short-run equilibrium is where AD meets SRAS. If real GDP is **below** potential, there is a **recessionary gap** with cyclical unemployment; if it is **above** potential, an **inflationary gap** with upward pressure on wages and prices. A fall in AD lowers both output and the price level; a **supply shock** that shifts SRAS left (an oil price spike) lowers output and raises the price level at the same time, called **stagflation**. Economists differ on how fast the economy returns to potential by itself: some believe wages and prices adjust fairly quickly, and others argue that the adjustment can take long enough to justify active policy.

### Fiscal policy

**Fiscal policy** is the government's use of spending and taxes. **Expansionary** fiscal policy (more spending or lower taxes) shifts AD right; **contractionary** policy shifts it left. **Automatic stabilizers** (income taxes and unemployment benefits) change without a new law: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefits rise, cushioning demand.

The **marginal propensity to consume (MPC)** is the fraction of an extra dollar of income that is spent; the rest is saved ($MPS = 1 - MPC$). A rise in spending raises incomes, which raises further spending, so the effect is multiplied:

$$\text{spending multiplier} = \frac{1}{1 - MPC}, \qquad \text{tax multiplier} = -\frac{MPC}{1 - MPC}.$$

A tax multiplier is smaller in size than the spending multiplier because part of a tax cut is saved, so it does not all become spending. If both $G$ and taxes rise by the same amount, GDP rises by that amount: the **balanced-budget multiplier** is 1.

These formulas make assumptions: the price level is fixed (so the whole shift in demand changes real output), the economy has spare capacity, and spending does not push up interest rates. If the economy is near potential, part of the rise is price increases, and borrowing may **crowd out** private investment by raising interest rates. Fiscal policy also has **lags** (recognition, legislative and impact lags).

### Deficits and debt

A **budget deficit** is the amount by which spending exceeds revenue in a year (a flow); the **national debt** is the sum of past deficits less surpluses (a stock). Economists debate how large deficits should be: supporters of active fiscal policy stress using deficits to fight recessions and to finance productive investment, while critics worry about higher interest payments, crowding out and burdens on future taxpayers. For current figures see the Congressional Budget Office and the Treasury.

@example
title: The multiplier in action
problem: The MPC is 0.8. Compare the effect on equilibrium GDP of (a) a \$50 billion increase in government purchases and (b) a \$50 billion tax cut. Assume the price level is fixed.
step: The spending multiplier is $1/(1 - 0.8) = 5$. Option (a): $\Delta GDP = 5 \times 50 = \$250$ billion.
step: The tax multiplier is $-0.8/0.2 = -4$. A tax cut of 50 (that is, $\Delta T = -50$) gives $\Delta GDP = -4 \times (-50) = +\$200$ billion.
step: The spending increase has the bigger effect because all of it is spent at the first round, whereas only 80% of the tax cut is spent at first: the other 20% is saved.
step: The difference of \$50 billion equals the original change, consistent with the balanced-budget multiplier of 1.
answer: (a) +\$250 billion; (b) +\$200 billion.

@example
title: Closing a recessionary gap
problem: Potential GDP is \$10,000 billion and actual real GDP is \$9,600 billion. The MPC is 0.75. By how much should government purchases rise to close the gap, assuming a fixed price level?
step: The gap is $10{,}000 - 9{,}600 = \$400$ billion.
step: The multiplier is $1/(1 - 0.75) = 4$.
step: Required change in $G$ $= 400/4 = \$100$ billion.
step: In practice the price level may rise and some private spending may be crowded out, so the real increase needed could be larger; this is a first approximation.
answer: \$100 billion.

@mistakes
- Treating a movement along the AD curve (caused by a price-level change) as a shift of AD.
- Using $1/MPC$ or $1/MPS$ confusion: the multiplier is $1/(1 - MPC) = 1/MPS$, and the tax multiplier is $-MPC/(1 - MPC)$, smaller in absolute value.
- Confusing the deficit (a yearly flow) with the debt (an accumulated stock).
- Forgetting that the multipliers assume spare capacity and a fixed price level, so they overstate the effect near full employment.
