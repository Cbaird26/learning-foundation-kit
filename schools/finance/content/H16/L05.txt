@article
Why is a bushel of wheat priced the same everywhere, while the only pharmacy in a small town can charge what it likes? Because the number of competitors, and the ease of entering a market, change a firm's power over price.

### Four market structures

| Structure | Firms | Product | Entry | Control over price |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Perfect competition | Many | Identical | Free | None (price taker) |
| Monopolistic competition | Many | Differentiated | Free | Some |
| Oligopoly | Few | Identical or differentiated | Barriers | Some, with strategic interaction |
| Monopoly | One | No close substitutes | Blocked | Considerable |

Concentration is often measured by the **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI)**, the sum of the squared percentage market shares of all firms. A market with four firms of 40, 30, 20 and 10 percent has an HHI of $1600 + 900 + 400 + 100 = 3000$, and a monopoly has an HHI of 10,000.

### The profit-maximizing rule

A firm maximizes profit by producing the quantity where **marginal revenue equals marginal cost** ($MR = MC$): while the extra revenue from one more unit exceeds its extra cost, producing it adds profit. Profit is $(P - ATC) \times Q$. In the short run a firm should **shut down** if $P < AVC$, since it would lose more by producing than the fixed cost alone.

### Perfect competition

A competitive firm is a **price taker**: it can sell any quantity at the market price, so $MR = P$ and the rule becomes $P = MC$. Economic profit attracts entry in the long run, which pushes the price down; losses cause exit, which pushes it up. In long-run equilibrium, price equals the minimum of ATC and **economic profit is zero** (normal profit, covering all opportunity costs, is still earned).

### Monopoly

A monopolist faces the whole downward-sloping market demand, so to sell more it must cut the price on all units. Its marginal revenue is therefore **below** price. For linear demand $P = a - bQ$, $MR = a - 2bQ$ (twice as steep). The monopolist produces where $MR = MC$ and then reads the price off the **demand curve**, not off the MC curve. A monopoly can earn economic profit in the long run only if barriers to entry (a patent, control of a key input, economies of scale that make one firm the cheapest, or government licenses) block rivals.

Compared with competition (assuming the same costs), a monopoly produces **less** and charges a **higher price**. The units between the monopoly and competitive quantities would be worth more to buyers than they cost to make, but are not produced: this lost surplus is the **deadweight loss**. Economists disagree about how often monopoly power is large and lasting, and about whether the remedy is antitrust enforcement, regulation or leaving markets to compete, partly because large firms can also bring lower costs and innovation.

### Monopolistic competition and oligopoly

In **monopolistic competition** (restaurants, clothing brands), firms differentiate products, so each has a downward-sloping demand, but free entry drives long-run economic profit to zero, with price above marginal cost. In **oligopoly**, a few firms' choices depend on each other. They may **collude** (which is illegal in the United States when it fixes prices) or compete, and **game theory** describes why cartels are unstable: each member gains by secretly cutting its price.

@example
title: A competitive firm chooses output
problem: A competitive firm faces a market price of \$13. Its fixed cost is \$10 and its marginal costs of units 1 to 6 are \$6, \$8, \$10, \$12, \$14 and \$18. How many units does it produce and what is its profit?
| Units | MC | TC | Revenue at \$13 | Profit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 6 | 16 | 13 | -3 |
| 2 | 8 | 24 | 26 | 2 |
| 3 | 10 | 34 | 39 | 5 |
| 4 | 12 | 46 | 52 | 6 |
| 5 | 14 | 60 | 65 | 5 |
| 6 | 18 | 78 | 78 | 0 |
step: A price taker has $MR = P = 13$. Produce each unit while its MC is at or below \$13: units 1 to 4 (MC \$6, \$8, \$10, \$12). The fifth unit costs \$14, more than the \$13 it brings in.
step: Total cost is fixed cost plus the sum of marginal costs: $10 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 = 46$ for 4 units.
step: Profit at 4 units is $13 \times 4 - 46 = \$6$, the highest in the table. The firm does not shut down, since the price exceeds average variable cost ($36/4 = 9$).
answer: 4 units, profit \$6.

@example
title: Monopoly versus competition
problem: Market demand is $P = 100 - Q$. The marginal cost is a constant \$20 and there are no fixed costs. Compare the outcome under perfect competition with that under a single-firm monopoly.
step: Competition: price equals marginal cost, $100 - Q = 20$, so $Q = 80$ and $P = \$20$. Profit is zero because $P = ATC = MC$. Consumer surplus is the triangle $\frac{1}{2} \times 80 \times 80 = \$3{,}200$.
step: Monopoly: $MR = 100 - 2Q$. Set $MR = MC$: $100 - 2Q = 20$, so $Q = 40$. The price from demand is $P = 100 - 40 = \$60$.
step: Monopoly profit is $(60 - 20) \times 40 = \$1{,}600$. Consumer surplus falls to $\frac{1}{2} \times 40 \times 40 = \$800$.
step: Total surplus is $3{,}200$ under competition and $800 + 1{,}600 = 2{,}400$ under monopoly. The deadweight loss is $\frac{1}{2} \times (60 - 20) \times (80 - 40) = \$800$.
answer: Competition: $Q = 80$, $P = \$20$, profit \$0. Monopoly: $Q = 40$, $P = \$60$, profit \$1,600, deadweight loss \$800.

@mistakes
- Setting the monopoly price equal to marginal cost, or reading the price off the MR curve instead of the demand curve.
- Using $MR = P$ for a monopolist: that holds only for a price taker.
- Saying a competitive firm "earns no profit" in the long run: it earns zero economic profit but covers all costs, including a normal return to the owners.
- Forgetting the shutdown rule: in the short run a firm keeps producing at a loss as long as $P \ge AVC$.

@practice num
prompt: A monopolist faces demand $P = 80 - 2Q$ and has a constant marginal cost of \$20 per unit. Its marginal revenue is $MR = 80 - 4Q$. What price does it charge, in dollars?
answer: 50
lead: \$
check: 80 - 2 * ((80 - 20) / 4)
hint: First find the quantity where $MR = MC$.
hint: $80 - 4Q = 20$ gives $Q = 15$; then substitute into the demand equation.
solution: $80 - 4Q = 20$ gives $Q = 15$. The price on the demand curve is $80 - 2(15) = \$50$.

@practice num
prompt: A monopolist sells 15 units at a price of \$50 each, and its average total cost at that output is \$28 per unit. What is its economic profit, in dollars?
answer: 330
lead: \$
check: (50 - 28) * 15
hint: Profit per unit is price minus average total cost.
solution: Profit is $(P - ATC) \times Q = (50 - 28) \times 15 = \$330$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which rule describes the profit-maximizing output of a firm that is a price taker?
choice: Produce where marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost by as much as possible.
choice*: Produce where price equals marginal cost, because marginal revenue equals price.
choice: Produce where price equals average total cost.
choice: Produce where marginal cost is at its minimum.
hint: For a price taker, an extra unit adds exactly the market price to revenue.
solution: All firms maximize profit where $MR = MC$. For a price taker $MR = P$, so the rule becomes $P = MC$. Setting $P = ATC$ gives zero profit, and minimum MC is not the profit-maximizing point.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements describe monopolistic competition? Select all that apply.
choice*: There are many sellers of differentiated products.
choice*: Entry and exit are free, so economic profit is zero in the long run.
choice*: In long-run equilibrium, price is above marginal cost.
choice: There is a single seller protected by barriers to entry.
choice: Firms are price takers facing a horizontal demand curve.
hint: Differentiated products give each firm some control over its price.
solution: Many sellers, differentiated products and free entry define monopolistic competition. Because each firm's demand slopes down, price exceeds marginal cost, but entry drives economic profit to zero. A single seller is monopoly, and horizontal demand is perfect competition.

@practice num
prompt: Market demand is $P = 120 - Q$ and marginal cost is a constant \$30 with no fixed cost. A single monopolist ($MR = 120 - 2Q$) replaces what would be a perfectly competitive industry (price equal to marginal cost). Compute the deadweight loss of monopoly, in dollars.
answer: 1012.5
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.5 * ((120 - (120 - 30) / 2) - 30) * ((120 - 30) - (120 - 30) / 2)
hint: Find the monopoly quantity ($MR = MC$) and the competitive quantity ($P = MC$).
hint: Monopoly: $Q = 45$ and $P = 75$. Competition: $Q = 90$. The deadweight loss is a triangle.
solution: Monopoly: $120 - 2Q = 30$ gives $Q = 45$, $P = 120 - 45 = \$75$. Competition: $120 - Q = 30$ gives $Q = 90$. The triangle has height $75 - 30 = 45$ and base $90 - 45 = 45$, so the deadweight loss is $\frac{1}{2} \times 45 \times 45 = \$1{,}012.50$.

@quiz num
prompt: A monopolist faces demand $P = 200 - 2Q$, so $MR = 200 - 4Q$. Its marginal cost is a constant \$40 per unit and its fixed cost is \$500. What is its profit, in dollars?
answer: 2700
lead: \$
check: (200 - 2 * ((200 - 40) / 4) - 40) * ((200 - 40) / 4) - 500
hint: Find $Q$ from $MR = MC$, then the price from demand.
solution: $200 - 4Q = 40$ gives $Q = 40$, and $P = 200 - 80 = \$120$. Revenue is $120 \times 40 = 4{,}800$ and total cost is $500 + 40 \times 40 = 2{,}100$, so profit is \$2,700.

@quiz num
prompt: An industry has four firms with market shares of 50, 25, 15 and 10 (shares measured on a 0 to 100 scale, so a monopoly has a share of 100). Compute the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI), the sum of the squared shares.
