@article
Atoms bond to reach a lower-energy arrangement of their valence electrons, often one resembling a noble gas (an octet, or a duet for hydrogen). There are two extremes: electrons transferred from one atom to another, or electrons shared.

### Ionic and covalent bonds

An **ionic bond** is the electrostatic attraction between a cation and an anion, typically a metal and a nonmetal: sodium gives up an electron to chlorine, giving $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ in a crystal lattice. A **covalent bond** is a shared pair of electrons, typically between nonmetals. Sharing more pairs gives double and triple bonds, which are shorter and stronger.

### Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** (EN) measures how strongly an atom attracts the electrons in a bond. On the Pauling scale: H $2.20$, C $2.55$, N $3.04$, O $3.44$, F $3.98$, Na $0.93$, Mg $1.31$, S $2.58$, Cl $3.16$, K $0.82$. The difference $\Delta\text{EN}$ between two bonded atoms tells you what to expect. Common textbook cutoffs, which are guidelines rather than sharp boundaries:

| $\Delta\text{EN}$ | Bond type |
|---|---|
| below about $0.4$ | nonpolar covalent (electrons shared nearly equally) |
| about $0.4$ to $1.8$ | polar covalent (shared unequally; partial charges $\delta+$ and $\delta-$) |
| above about $1.8$ | ionic (electrons effectively transferred) |

Sodium chloride: $3.16-0.93=2.23$, ionic. Hydrogen chloride: $3.16-2.20=0.96$, polar covalent. $\text{Cl}_2$: $0$, nonpolar.

### Lewis structures

1. Add up the valence electrons of all atoms; add one for each negative charge and subtract one for each positive charge.
2. Place the least electronegative atom (not hydrogen) in the center and join atoms with single bonds.
3. Complete octets on the outer atoms with lone pairs, then put leftover electrons on the central atom.
4. If the central atom lacks an octet, convert lone pairs into double or triple bonds.

Carbon dioxide has $4+2(6)=16$ valence electrons, giving $\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}$ with two lone pairs on each oxygen.

### Shape: VSEPR

**Valence shell electron-pair repulsion** theory says that electron groups (bonds and lone pairs) around a central atom stay as far apart as possible. Count the groups, then name the shape by the atoms only:

| Electron groups | Lone pairs | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 0 | linear | $\text{CO}_2$ |
| 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | $\text{BF}_3$ |
| 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | $\text{CH}_4$ |
| 4 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | $\text{NH}_3$ |
| 4 | 2 | bent | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |

A molecule is **polar** if it has polar bonds that do not cancel by symmetry. $\text{CO}_2$ has polar bonds but is nonpolar overall; $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is polar.

@example
title: Classify bonds from electronegativity
problem: Using Pauling electronegativities (Mg $1.31$, O $3.44$, C $2.55$, H $2.20$), classify the Mg–O bond and the C–H bond.
step: Subtract for Mg–O: $3.44-1.31=2.13$.
step: $2.13$ exceeds about $1.8$, and the pair is a metal with a nonmetal, so the bond is ionic.
step: Subtract for C–H: $2.55-2.20=0.35$.
step: $0.35$ is below about $0.4$, so the C–H bond is nonpolar (or very weakly polar) covalent.

@mistakes
- Treating the cutoffs as exact boundaries; use them with the metal/nonmetal rule of thumb and say "mostly ionic" or "mostly covalent" when close.
- Forgetting to add or subtract electrons for the charge of a polyatomic ion.
- Naming the shape by electron groups instead of atoms; ammonia has four electron groups but its shape is trigonal pyramidal.
- Assuming polar bonds always give a polar molecule; symmetry can cancel the bond dipoles.
