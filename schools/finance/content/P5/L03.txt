@article
The first third of an initiation report answers two questions: **how does this company make money**, and **what kind of industry is it in**? A reader who understands both can judge your forecast; a reader who does not will distrust everything after page three.

### Business model: who pays, for what, and why again

Start with the 10-K's segment note, not the marketing copy. For each segment, write down the **customer** (who pays), the **product** (what they get), the **pricing** (per unit, per contract, per use), the **cost drivers** and the **repeat pattern** (one-off, recurring or contractual). Then express revenue as a product of drivers:

$$\text{Revenue} = \text{volume} \times \text{price per unit}.$$

Volume might be units shipped, stores times sales per store, subscribers, or contracts. The model you build in week 6 will forecast the drivers, not the total, so find them now. Many companies disclose a few (units, average selling price, store counts, customers, retention); the rest you infer from MD&A, which explains changes in revenue as "price," "volume" and "mix."

### Market size and share

The **total addressable market (TAM)** is the revenue available if a firm sold to every customer in its category; the **serviceable market** is the part it can reach with its current products and geography. Analysts quote TAM loosely, so state your source and your definition. **Market share** is company revenue divided by market revenue. Share is useful in reverse: ask what share a forecast implies. If a market grows 6% a year and your forecast has the company growing 10%, you are forecasting share gains; say why.

### Industry structure: five forces

**Porter's five forces** organizes why some industries earn high returns:

- **Rivalry** among existing competitors (concentration, growth, exit barriers, product differences).
- **Threat of new entrants** (capital needs, scale, regulation, brand, distribution).
- **Threat of substitutes** (other ways to solve the customer's problem).
- **Bargaining power of suppliers** (few suppliers, no alternative inputs, switching costs).
- **Bargaining power of buyers** (few large buyers, low switching costs, price transparency).

Concentration has a standard measure, the **Herfindahl-Hirschman Index**: $HHI = \sum s_i^2$, where $s_i$ is each firm's market share in percentage points. Four equal firms (25% each) give $4 \times 625 = 2{,}500$; a monopoly gives 10,000. Antitrust agencies use it as one input, and you can use it to describe whether rivalry is likely to be fragmented or disciplined. Name the evidence for each force; do not simply label a force "high."

### Industry life cycle

Industries move through **embryonic, growth, shakeout, mature and decline** stages. Growth industries reinvest heavily and earn uneven profit; mature industries grow near the economy, earn steady returns and often return cash; declining industries need cost discipline. The stage tells you which forecasts are plausible: a mature industry rarely supports a decade of 12% growth without share gains or price increases.

### Tie it to the thesis

For every paragraph, ask "so what for value?" High buyer power implies pressure on price and so on margin; a growth stage implies a longer high-growth period in the DCF; a fragmented industry may offer acquisition-driven growth. Write the conclusions as claims a reader can test.

@example
title: Building Corvane's revenue from drivers
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) reports three segments. Industrial sensors: 9.5 million units at an average selling price of \$96. Automation controllers: 1.15 million units at \$410. Services: 14,200 recurring contracts at \$18,500 a year. Build total revenue and each segment's share, in millions of dollars.
step: Industrial sensors: $9.5 \times 96 = \$912.0$ million.
step: Controllers: $1.15 \times 410 = \$471.5$ million. Services: $14{,}200 \times 18{,}500 = \$262.7$ million.
step: Total revenue is $912.0 + 471.5 + 262.7 = \$1{,}646.2$ million.
step: Shares: sensors $912.0/1{,}646.2 = 55.4\%$, controllers $28.6\%$, services $16.0\%$. The services share is small but recurring, which later affects margin and the multiple you can defend.
answer: Total revenue \$1,646.2 million: sensors 55.4%, controllers 28.6%, services 16.0%.

@example
title: What share does a forecast imply?
problem: Corvane's serviceable market is \$14.0 billion and grows 6% a year. Revenue is \$1,646.2 million. If you forecast 14% market share in five years, what is Corvane's implied revenue and its implied annual revenue growth?
step: Current share: $1{,}646.2 / 14{,}000 = 11.76\%$.
step: Market in five years: $14{,}000 \times 1.06^5 = \$18{,}735.2$ million.
step: Revenue at 14% share: $0.14 \times 18{,}735.2 = \$2{,}622.9$ million.
step: Implied growth: $(2{,}622.9 / 1{,}646.2)^{1/5} - 1 = 9.76\%$. The market alone supplies 6%; the other 3.76 points are share gains you must justify with the competitive analysis in week 4.
answer: Revenue \$2,622.9 million, growing 9.76% a year (6% from the market, the rest from share gains).

@mistakes
- Forecasting total revenue as a growth percentage without identifying the volume and price drivers behind it.
- Quoting a huge TAM (every possible customer worldwide) and then applying a "1% share" argument without showing how the company would win those customers.
- Labeling the five forces "high" or "low" without evidence such as concentration data, switching costs or customer concentration.
- Describing the industry's history at length without drawing a conclusion about margins, growth or the forecast.

@practice self
prompt: Write a 120 to 150 word industry paragraph for a thesis. Facts for a fictional industry, commercial refrigeration: the top four firms hold 62% of sales; installation requires certified technicians; most buyers (grocery chains) sign three-year contracts and their purchasing departments compare quotes; refrigerants are regulated, and sales grow about 3% a year with replacement purchases about 70% of demand. Apply at least three of the five forces, name the life-cycle stage, and end with a "so what for value" sentence.
hint: Match each fact to a force, then say what it implies for margins and growth.
solution: Commercial refrigeration looks like a mature industry: sales grow about 3% a year and replacement purchases are about 70% of demand, so a forecast near that rate is more defensible than one that assumes share gains. Rivalry is moderate, since the top four hold 62% of sales, which supports price discipline. Entry is hindered by the need for certified technicians and by regulation of refrigerants, so new entrants face real costs. Buyer power is meaningful: grocery chains compare quotes and sign three-year contracts, which limits price increases between renewals. So what for value: the combination supports steady, mid-single-digit growth and stable but not expanding margins, so the thesis should rest on company-specific execution, not on industry growth or pricing power.
rubric: Applies at least three forces and ties each to a fact given (concentration, entry barriers, buyer behavior, regulation).
rubric: Identifies the industry as mature (or equivalent) using the growth and replacement-demand facts.
rubric: Ends with a specific implication for forecast growth or margin, not a restatement of the facts.
