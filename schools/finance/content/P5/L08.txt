@article
Last week produced a number. This week you ask how wrong it could be. A report that shows only the base case is advertising; a report that shows what could break the thesis, and when you would know, is analysis. This is also the deliverable week: present a draft valuation for feedback.

### Risks that matter

The 10-K lists dozens of risk factors; most are boilerplate. Choose the three to five that move value, and for each state: **what** could happen, **how it would hit the model** (which driver, by how much), **how likely** you think it is, and **what you would watch** to see it coming. Group them: business and demand risks, competitive risks, financial risks (leverage, refinancing, interest rates), operational risks, regulatory and legal risks, and valuation risk (the multiple falls even if the business performs). Rank them by effect on value, not by how dramatic they sound.

### Catalysts

A **catalyst** is a dated, observable event that would make the market recognize your thesis: quarterly earnings releases, a product launch, a regulatory decision, a debt maturity, an investor day, an expiring contract. "The market will realize the value" is not a catalyst. Build a calendar with the date, the event, what result would support your thesis and what would contradict it. If you cannot list any catalyst within your horizon, say so: the stock may be right but it may stay wrong for longer than twelve months.

### Scenarios

A scenario changes the drivers together and tells a coherent story. Typical set: **bull**, **base** and **bear**. For each, write the narrative, the key assumptions (growth, margin, multiple), and the value per share. Then assign probabilities that sum to 100% and compute the **probability-weighted value**:

$$E[V] = \sum_i p_i V_i.$$

The probabilities are judgments, so say so and show how the result changes if you move them. The **upside/downside ratio** compares the gain in the bull case with the loss in the bear case relative to the current price $P_0$:

$$\frac{V_{bull} - P_0}{P_0 - V_{bear}}.$$

A ratio above 1 means the bull gain exceeds the bear loss, but it ignores probabilities, so read it alongside the expected value.

### Sensitivity tables

A one-way or two-way table shows value per share as one or two inputs change. WACC and terminal growth are the standard pair for a DCF; for the business, use growth and margin. Read the table for the size of the swing, not just the center: if a one-point move in WACC changes value by 20%, a price target stated to the cent is false precision. Also build a **reverse DCF**: what growth or margin does the current price imply, and is that plausible? This asks what the market believes, and it is often the most persuasive page in a report.

### A pre-mortem

Imagine it is twelve months from now and the thesis has failed. Write the three most likely reasons. Anything you list should be either a risk in your report or a reason you consider it unlikely. The exercise protects you from overconfidence, which is the most common error in a published view.

@example
title: Scenarios for Corvane
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) trades at \$48.20. You value it at \$62 in the bull case (25% probability), \$43 in the base case (50%) and \$28 in the bear case (25%). Compute the probability-weighted value, its difference from the price and the upside/downside ratio.
step: Weighted value: $0.25(62) + 0.50(43) + 0.25(28) = 15.50 + 21.50 + 7.00 = \$44.00$.
step: Difference from the price: $44.00/48.20 - 1 = -8.7\%$.
step: Upside/downside: $(62 - 48.20)/(48.20 - 28) = 13.80/20.20 = 0.68$.
step: Both numbers point the same way: the bear loss is larger than the bull gain, and the expected value is below the price. Say which assumption (for example the probability of the bull case) would have to change for the conclusion to flip.
answer: Expected value \$44.00 (8.7% below the price); upside/downside 0.68.

@example
title: A DCF sensitivity table
problem: Using Corvane's forecast FCFF (\$118, \$131, \$144, \$156, \$166 million), net debt of \$275 million and 62.5 million shares, value per share changes with WACC and terminal growth as shown. How large is the swing from a one-point change in WACC at 2.5% growth?
step: The table (dollars per share):

| WACC \ g | 1.5% | 2.5% | 3.5% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 7.4% | 36.75 | 43.65 | 54.10 |
| 8.4% | 30.62 | 35.35 | 42.00 |
| 9.4% | 26.05 | 29.45 | 34.01 |

step: At 2.5% growth, a one-point lower WACC raises value from \$35.35 to \$43.65: $+23.5\%$. A one-point higher WACC lowers it to \$29.45: $-16.7\%$.
step: The effect is not symmetric because the terminal value is a convex function of WACC.
step: Across the whole table the value ranges from \$26 to \$54. A price target should be presented as a range around the center, and the report should say which cells would justify the current price of \$48.20 (low WACC and high growth).
answer: A one-point change in WACC moves value +23.5% or -16.7%; the table spans about \$26 to \$54.

@mistakes
- Listing every risk factor in the 10-K instead of the few that move value, with no estimate of the effect on the model.
- Setting scenario probabilities that conveniently produce the recommended rating, without showing how the result changes if they move.
- Naming "market recognition" as a catalyst instead of a dated, observable event.
- Presenting a price target to the cent from a sensitivity table that spans a 100% range.

prompt: A stock trades at \$30. You value it at \$45 if a catalyst succeeds and \$18 if it fails, and you see no other outcome. What probability of success makes the expected value equal to the price? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 44.4
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (30 - 18) / (45 - 18) * 100
hint: Solve $p \times 45 + (1 - p) \times 18 = 30$.
solution: $45p + 18 - 18p = 30$ gives $27p = 12$, so $p = 0.4444$, or 44.4%. If you think success is more likely than that, the expected value is above the price.

solution: Earnings releases, regulatory decisions and refinancing deadlines are dated and observable, and each can be tied to a result that supports or contradicts the thesis. Market recognition and a feeling of cheapness are neither dated nor observable.

@practice self
prompt: Fictional Harborline Freight leases 60% of its trucks under contracts that expire in 18 months, has \$400 million of debt (20% floating rate) with a \$150 million maturity in 24 months, earns 70% of revenue from its five largest customers, and trades at 14 times earnings against a peer average of 12. Write a 150 to 180 word risks and catalysts section: rank three risks (what, how it hits the model, what to watch) and give two catalysts with dates relative to today.
hint: Choose the risks that move cash flow or the multiple most, and make each catalyst observable.
solution: Risk 1, customer concentration: five customers are 70% of revenue, so losing one could remove roughly 14% of revenue. It would hit the volume driver and operating leverage; watch contract renewal disclosures and customer commentary. Risk 2, lease renewal: 60% of trucks are leased under contracts expiring in 18 months, so higher lease rates would raise the cost per mile; watch fleet cost per mile in MD&A and market lease rates. Risk 3, valuation: the shares trade at 14 times earnings against 12 for peers, so a de-rating to 12 would lower value about 14% even if earnings are met. Catalysts: the lease renewals (in about 18 months) show whether costs rise; the \$150 million debt maturity (in about 24 months) shows the refinancing terms and rate exposure; in addition, quarterly earnings reveal customer volumes. A thesis that depends on margin expansion is contradicted if cost per mile rises.
rubric: Identifies and ranks at least three specific risks from the facts, each with an effect on a model driver and something observable to watch.
rubric: Quantifies at least one effect correctly (for example 14% of revenue from one customer of five at 70%, or the 14 against 12 multiple).
rubric: Gives at least two dated, observable catalysts and says what result would support or contradict the thesis.
