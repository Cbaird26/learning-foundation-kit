@article
The model is where your thesis becomes numbers. A good model is not the most detailed one; it is the one in which a reviewer can change an assumption, see the effect flow to the price target, and trust that nothing is broken. Build it so that someone else can audit it in an hour.

### Architecture

Use a few tabs with a clear flow: **Assumptions** (all inputs), **Historical** (five years from the 10-Ks), **Schedules** (PP&E, working capital, debt, equity, taxes), **Statements** (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow), **Valuation** and **Checks**. Rules that save careers:

- Every input lives in one place, formatted differently from formulas (blue font is the convention). No numbers typed inside formulas.
- One formula per row, copied across the forecast years.
- Forecast five to ten years explicitly, then a terminal value. For a company still growing quickly, ten years lets growth fade before the terminal value.
- Units, sign conventions and the fiscal year-end are stated at the top.

### Forecast the drivers, not the totals

Last week you found the drivers. Forecast them with a reason for each: volume growth (market growth plus share change), price or mix, and cost ratios. Anchor to history and the industry: write the five-year average and the range next to each assumption and explain departures. Typical drivers are:

| Line | Driver |
|---|---|
| Revenue | volume x price per segment |
| Cost of goods sold | gross margin, or cost per unit |
| SG&A and R&D | percent of revenue, with a fixed part |
| Depreciation | percent of beginning net PP&E (or by asset class) |
| Capital expenditure | percent of revenue, tied to growth |
| Working capital | days (DSO, DIO, DPO) or a percent of revenue |
| Interest | rate times average debt |
| Taxes | effective rate, which you justify against the statutory 21% federal rate plus state taxes |

### Schedules

A **PP&E schedule** rolls forward: $\text{ending net PP\&E} = \text{beginning} + \text{capex} - \text{depreciation}$. A **working capital schedule** computes the balance of each account from its driver and takes the year-to-year change: an increase in net working capital is a use of cash. A **debt schedule** tracks beginning balance, borrowings and repayments, ending balance and interest. An **equity schedule** adds net income and subtracts dividends and net share repurchases (remember the 1% excise tax on net buybacks by public companies).

### Interest and the circularity

Interest computed on the **average** balance is more accurate but creates a **circular reference** when cash or revolver borrowing depends on net income, which depends on interest. Two professional solutions: enable iterative calculation with a circuit breaker switch (a cell that sets interest to zero to flush errors), or compute interest on the **beginning** balance. State your choice, because it matters little when balances move slowly.

### Checks

Build a Checks tab that shows zero when everything is right: the balance sheet balances every year, the cash flow statement's ending cash equals balance sheet cash, retained earnings roll forward, and debt does not go negative. Then run **reasonableness tests**: ROIC against history, revenue growth against market growth, margins against peers, and the implied change in share count. A model that balances can still be wrong.

### Scenarios and documentation

Add a scenario switch (base, bull, bear) that changes the key drivers together; week 8 uses it. Document each assumption in a sentence with its source, and keep a version log. If you prefer Python on your own computer, a few functions (revenue growth, a roll-forward, a DCF) give you a second implementation to cross-check the spreadsheet.

@example
title: Revenue and PP&E for two forecast years
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) had revenue of \$1,646.2 million and net PP&E of \$640 million. Forecast revenue growth is 8% in 2026 and 7% in 2027. Capex is 4.5% of revenue; depreciation is 8% of beginning net PP&E. Forecast revenue, capex, depreciation and ending PP&E for both years.
step: 2026 revenue: $1{,}646.2 \times 1.08 = \$1{,}777.9$ million. Capex: $0.045 \times 1{,}777.9 = \$80.0$ million. Depreciation: $0.08 \times 640 = \$51.2$ million.
step: 2026 ending PP&E: $640 + 80.0 - 51.2 = \$668.8$ million.
step: 2027 revenue: $1{,}777.9 \times 1.07 = \$1{,}902.3$ million. Capex: $0.045 \times 1{,}902.3 = \$85.6$ million. Depreciation: $0.08 \times 668.8 = \$53.5$ million.
step: 2027 ending PP&E: $668.8 + 85.6 - 53.5 = \$700.9$ million. Capex exceeds depreciation each year, as expected for a growing company; a reviewer will check that PP&E growth is consistent with revenue growth.
answer: PP&E \$668.8 million (2026) and \$700.9 million (2027).

@example
title: Working capital and interest
problem: Net working capital is 12.8% of revenue. Revenue rises from \$1,646.2 million to \$1,777.9 million. Debt falls from \$410 million to \$370 million, with an interest rate of 6%. Compute the change in net working capital and the interest expense on average and on beginning debt.
step: The increase in revenue is $1{,}777.9 - 1{,}646.2 = \$131.7$ million, so net working capital rises by $0.128 \times 131.7 = \$16.9$ million. That is a use of cash in the cash flow statement.
step: Interest on average debt: $0.06 \times (410 + 370)/2 = \$23.4$ million.
step: Interest on beginning debt: $0.06 \times 410 = \$24.6$ million.
step: The \$1.2 million gap is small relative to net income of about \$110 million. Using beginning debt avoids the circularity, so choose it unless precision matters, and say which you chose.
answer: Working capital rises \$16.9 million; interest is \$23.4 million (average) or \$24.6 million (beginning).

@mistakes
- Typing numbers inside formulas or forecasting totals (revenue growth percentages) with no link to drivers.
- Forgetting that an increase in working capital reduces cash flow, or that depreciation is added back in the cash flow statement but capex is subtracted.
- A balance sheet that balances only because cash is a plug that no one checks against the cash flow statement.
- Trusting a model because it balances, without comparing ROIC, margins and growth with history and peers.

@practice code
prompt: Write `forecast_revenue(base, growth_rates)` that returns a list with the revenue for each forecast year, given the base-year revenue `base` and a list of annual growth rates as decimals (0.05 means 5%). Each year's revenue is the previous year's times one plus that year's growth rate. For example, `forecast_revenue(100, [0.10, 0.10])` returns about `[110, 121]`. An empty list of growth rates returns an empty list.
starter:
```python
def forecast_revenue(base, growth_rates):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
import math
result = forecast_revenue(820, [0.05, 0.06, 0.04])
expected = [861.0, 912.66, 949.1664]
assert len(result) == 3
assert all(math.isclose(a, b, rel_tol=1e-9) for a, b in zip(result, expected))
assert forecast_revenue(100, []) == []
assert all(math.isclose(a, 200) for a in forecast_revenue(200, [0.0, 0.0]))
assert math.isclose(forecast_revenue(500, [-0.10])[0], 450.0)
```
model:
```python
def forecast_revenue(base, growth_rates):
    """Revenue for each forecast year from a base and a list of growth rates."""
    revenues = []
    current = base
    for rate in growth_rates:
        current *= 1 + rate
        revenues.append(current)
    return revenues
```
hint: Keep a running revenue figure and update it in a loop.
hint: In each pass multiply the running figure by `1 + rate` and append it to the list.
solution: Start from the base, and for each growth rate compound the running revenue and store it. The list excludes the base year, and an empty list of rates gives an empty result.
