@article
ESG analysis in an initiation report has a narrow, practical job: **identify the environmental, social and governance factors that could change the company's cash flows, risk or cost of capital, and say how.** It is not a moral grade for the company. Investors disagree about how ESG should be used: some treat it as part of risk analysis, some add their own values, some argue that companies should focus on shareholder returns and that ESG ratings are inconsistent. Your report can stay out of that debate by being factual, specific and sourced.

### Materiality first

Not every issue matters for every company. A semiconductor maker's key issues might be energy use, water and supply-chain labor practices; a bank's might be credit underwriting practices and data security; a retailer's, product safety and workforce turnover. Industry frameworks help: the SASB Standards (now maintained by the ISSB) list financially material topics by industry, and the ISSB's IFRS S1 and S2 standards (issued in 2023) set sustainability and climate disclosure requirements that some jurisdictions have adopted. Start from the company's own 10-K: Risk Factors (Item 1A), the human capital description in Business, legal proceedings, cybersecurity (Item 1C, required since fiscal 2023 reports) and environmental matters. Then ask for each issue: through which line of the model could it reach value (revenue, cost, capex, litigation, cost of capital)?

### The rules are changing: check current

In March 2025 the SEC voted to stop defending its 2024 climate-disclosure rule, so that rule is not in effect, and US public companies' climate disclosure now depends on general materiality rules, voluntary reporting and some state laws, whose scope and timing are changing. The EU's sustainability rules (CSRD and SFDR) were being simplified in 2025 and 2026. For your company, state which regimes apply, say that the rules change, and do not assume that disclosures exist because a rule once proposed them.

### Governance: where to look

The **proxy statement (DEF 14A)** is the primary source. Check:

- **Control structure.** Do some shares carry more votes? In a dual-class structure, voting power can differ greatly from economic ownership.
- **Board.** Size, independence (the share of independent directors), whether the chair and CEO roles are separate, committee composition, tenure, and board skills.
- **Pay.** The compensation discussion: the mix of salary, bonus and equity, the metrics that determine pay and whether they match the strategy and the thesis. Shareholders cast a non-binding **say-on-pay** vote at least every three years (an advisory vote required since the Dodd-Frank Act); record the support level.
- **Audit.** The auditor, its tenure, the split between audit and non-audit fees, and any restatements or material weaknesses reported in the 10-K.
- **Related-party transactions and shareholder rights.** Dealings between the company and insiders, anti-takeover provisions and proposals shareholders have submitted.

Report what you find in neutral language: "Class B shares carry ten votes each and are held by the founders" is a fact; "the structure is entrenching" is a judgment you should attribute to a view, not state as fact.

### Quantify what you can

Some exposures can be given a size using a stated assumption. For emissions, multiply tonnes of CO2-equivalent in Scope 1 and 2 (from the company's own disclosure, if any) by a **hypothetical carbon price** to see the potential cost, and compare it with operating profit. This is a scenario, not a forecast of a regulation. For social factors, quantify turnover costs, safety incidents or litigation reserves where the filings give figures. For governance, reflect a persistent concern in a higher discount rate or a lower multiple only if you can explain why.

### Balance

Present material strengths and weaknesses, name the evidence, and flag what you could not find. Where reasonable people disagree (for example, whether a dual-class structure helps long-term decisions or reduces accountability), give both views in a sentence each and say what the data show for this company.

@example
title: Voting control and economic ownership
problem: Fictional Brightmoor Software has 52 million Class A shares (one vote each) and 14 million Class B shares (ten votes each). The founders hold all the Class B shares and 3 million Class A shares. What share of the votes and of the equity do they hold?
step: Total votes: $52 \times 1 + 14 \times 10 = 52 + 140 = 192$ million.
step: Founders' votes: $3 \times 1 + 14 \times 10 = 143$ million, which is $143/192 = 74.5\%$ of the votes.
step: Economic ownership: the founders hold $3 + 14 = 17$ million of $66$ million shares, or $25.8\%$.
step: Report the gap: the founders control about three-quarters of the votes with a quarter of the equity. Say neutrally what that implies for takeovers and shareholder proposals, note the arguments on both sides of dual-class structures, and check whether the charter has a sunset provision.
answer: 74.5% of votes against 25.8% of the equity.

@example
title: A hypothetical carbon price scenario
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) reports Scope 1 and 2 emissions of 380,000 tonnes of CO2-equivalent. Suppose a carbon cost of \$40 per tonne applied to all of it, a 22% tax rate, NOPAT of \$131.04 million and 62.5 million shares. What is the after-tax cost and its effect?
step: Pretax cost: $380{,}000 \times 40 = \$15.2$ million.
step: After tax: $15.2 \times (1 - 0.22) = \$11.86$ million.
step: As a share of NOPAT: $11.856/131.04 = 9.0\%$. Per share: $11.856/62.5 = \$0.19$ a year.
step: State the status of the number: it is a stress test with a hypothetical price, not a prediction of a tax. Show how it changes with \$20 and \$80 and what the company could do (energy efficiency, passing cost to customers), so the reader can judge exposure.
answer: After-tax cost about \$11.86 million a year, 9.0% of NOPAT (\$0.19 per share).

@mistakes
- Treating an ESG rating from one provider as a fact about the company, when providers' methods differ and their scores often disagree.
- Listing every possible issue instead of the few that are material to this company and reach the model through a named line.
- Stating a judgment ("poor governance") as a fact, or writing with advocacy for or against ESG investing instead of evidence.
- Assuming disclosure requirements still apply as proposed, without checking the current status of the rule.

@practice self
prompt: A fictional manufacturer, Eastgate Components, discloses in its proxy and 10-K: the CEO is also the board chair and there is a lead independent director; 8 of 10 directors are independent; the audit committee is entirely independent; the last say-on-pay vote received 71% support; 85% of CEO pay is tied to revenue growth targets, with no cash-flow or return metric; the auditor has served for 22 years; Scope 1 and 2 emissions have fallen 12% in three years; a plant safety incident led to a \$4 million fine last year. Write a 130 to 170 word factual, balanced ESG and governance paragraph for a report, ending with how one item could reach the model.
hint: State facts neutrally, note where views differ, and connect one item to a line of the model.
solution: Eastgate's board is 80% independent and the audit committee is fully independent, with a lead independent director offsetting the combined CEO and chair role (views differ on whether combining the roles reduces accountability). Say-on-pay support was 71%, lower than many companies report, and 85% of CEO pay depends on revenue growth targets with no cash-flow or return metric, so pay may reward growth that does not earn its cost of capital; we flag this as a question for the board's disclosure rather than a conclusion. The auditor has served for 22 years; some investors prefer rotation, others value experience. Scope 1 and 2 emissions fell 12% over three years, and a plant safety incident brought a \$4 million fine. For the model, we treat safety compliance as a recurring cost risk and include a \$4 million annual allowance in the bear case; the revenue-only pay metrics are a reason to watch capital spending and margins.
rubric: Reports facts neutrally (percent independent, vote support, pay metrics, auditor tenure, emissions, fine) and does not state judgments as facts.
rubric: Gives balance: mentions that views differ on at least one item (combined CEO and chair, auditor tenure, pay design) and includes a positive as well as a concern.
rubric: Connects at least one item to a specific line or scenario in the model (cost, capex, margin or discount rate).
