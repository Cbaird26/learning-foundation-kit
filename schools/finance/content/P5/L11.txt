@article
Every professional report goes through review: a senior analyst, a peer or a compliance reader reads the draft and objects. The reviewer is not your opponent; they are the reader who will find the mistake before your readers do. This week you give and receive review, which is also how most analysts learn.

### Reviewing a report: the order of attack

Review from the top down, because deep errors matter more than typos:

1. **The thesis test.** Can you state the thesis in two sentences after reading page one? Does every section support it, and does the valuation depend on it?
2. **The evidence test.** For each major claim ask "how do you know?" and look for a source. Look for what is missing: the strongest counter-argument, a peer comparison, the base rate.
3. **The model test.** Does the balance sheet balance? Does cash tie? Do the forecast drivers fall within historical ranges, and are departures explained? Does ROIC fade sensibly? Is the terminal value share reported? Change a key input and see whether the output moves in the expected direction.
4. **The number audit.** Do the front page, text, tables and model agree?
5. **The disclosure and standards check.** Are sources cited, conflicts disclosed, the certification included and the learning-exercise statement present?
6. **Language.** Clarity, jargon, length.

### Diagnosing model errors

When the balance sheet is out by a number, the size of the difference is a clue. If it equals a known flow (dividends, depreciation, capex, a change in working capital), the model omits or double-counts that flow somewhere. If it equals twice a known flow, a sign is probably flipped on one side. If it grows each year, check a roll-forward formula that was not copied across.

### Writing a good comment

A useful comment has four parts: **where** (page, cell, line), **what** the problem is, **why** it matters (the effect on the conclusion), and, where you can, **a suggestion**. Rank comments: *must fix* (changes a conclusion or is an error), *should fix* (weakens the argument) and *nice to have* (style). "The model is confusing" is not a comment; "Tab Schedules, row 14: capex is hard-coded for 2028 (\$92 million) while 2026 and 2027 use 4.5% of revenue; this breaks the scenario switch" is. Also say what works: reviewers who note strengths are heard more clearly.

### Receiving review

Read all comments before responding to any. Sort them by importance. For each, choose one of three responses and write it down:

- **Fix.** The reviewer is right; say what you changed.
- **Counter with evidence.** The reviewer's point is a misunderstanding or you have evidence they lack; explain politely with the source.
- **Decide not to change.** The point is reasonable but the cost or effect is small, or you disagree on judgment; say why, and whether you have disclosed the limitation.

Never change a number to please a reviewer without understanding why, and never ignore a comment. If a review changes your conclusion, change the conclusion; do not defend a rating because you published it. Under the CFA Institute Standards, Standard V(A) means your reasonable basis should survive the review, and Standard III(B), Fair Dealing, points the same way when a review changes a rating or target you have already shared: communicate the change to those who received the original.

### The revision log

Keep a table: comment, importance, response (fix, counter, decline), what changed and where. It shows the reviewer that you took the work seriously and lets you re-check that every fix reached every place the number appears (the number audit again).

@example
title: Finding a balance sheet error
problem: A fictional 2027 forecast balance sheet shows assets of cash \$190 million, receivables \$310 million, inventory \$340 million, net PP&E \$700 million and goodwill \$330 million, and liabilities and equity of payables \$160 million, debt \$330 million, other liabilities \$90 million and equity \$1,270 million. The company paid dividends of \$20 million. Find the imbalance and a likely cause.
step: Total assets: $190 + 310 + 340 + 700 + 330 = \$1{,}870$ million.
step: Total liabilities and equity: $160 + 330 + 90 + 1{,}270 = \$1{,}850$ million.
step: The balance sheet is out by $1{,}870 - 1{,}850 = \$20$ million, with assets too high (or equity too low).
step: The gap equals the dividends paid. Equity already reflects the dividend (it reduces retained earnings), so the likely cause is that the cash flow statement leaves out the \$20 million dividend payment, which leaves cash, and so assets, \$20 million too high.
step: Fix the cash flow statement, then confirm that ending cash on the cash flow statement ties to balance sheet cash and that the check row shows zero in every year.
answer: The model is out by \$20 million (assets exceed liabilities plus equity), the size of the dividend; the cash flow statement probably omits the dividend payment.

@example
title: Responding to a reviewer on terminal value
problem: A reviewer writes: "Terminal value is 78% of your enterprise value. Why should I trust a price target that depends so heavily on 2030 and beyond?" Corvane's terminal value is \$2,883.9 million at 2030, its present value is \$1,926.8 million of a \$2,484.1 million enterprise value, and 2030 EBITDA is forecast at \$330 million. Write the response.
step: Classify the comment: it is valid and important (a should-fix), so the response is a fix plus evidence.
step: Verify the number: $1{,}926.8/2{,}484.1 = 77.6\%$, so the reviewer is right.
step: Add the exit-multiple cross-check: $2{,}883.9/330 = 8.7$ times 2030 EBITDA, well below the current 15.3 times, so the terminal value does not assume that today's multiple persists.
step: Respond: "Agreed. Terminal value is 77.6% of EV. I added a cross-check: the terminal value implies 8.7 times 2030 EBITDA, below today's 15.3 times and in line with mature peers, and a two-way sensitivity (page 11) shows value from \$26 to \$54 per share across WACC of 7.4% to 9.4% and growth of 1.5% to 3.5%. The report now states the share, and I describe the price target as a range." That says what changed and where.
answer: The reviewer is right (77.6%); the response adds an implied-exit-multiple check (8.7 times) and a sensitivity table, and records the changes in the log.

@mistakes
- Writing vague comments ("this section is weak") with no location, effect or suggestion, so the author cannot act.
- Changing a number to satisfy a reviewer without understanding the cause, or fixing it in one place and not in every place it appears.
- Treating every comment as equally important, or defending a published rating instead of testing it.
- Skipping the revision log, so that neither you nor the reviewer can verify what changed.

prompt: A fictional forecast balance sheet has total assets of \$2,140 million, and liabilities and equity are payables \$190 million, debt \$700 million, other liabilities \$240 million and equity \$988 million. By how many millions of dollars is it out of balance (assets minus liabilities and equity)?
answer: 22
lead: \$
tol: 0.5
check: 2140 - (190 + 700 + 240 + 988)
hint: Add the liabilities and equity first.
solution: Liabilities and equity total $190 + 700 + 240 + 988 = \$2{,}118$ million, so the difference is $2{,}140 - 2{,}118 = \$22$ million.

@practice num
prompt: In a DCF, the present value of the explicit forecast cash flows is \$420 million and the present value of the terminal value is \$1,380 million. What percent of enterprise value is the terminal value? Give it to one decimal place.
answer: 76.7
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 1380 / (420 + 1380) * 100
hint: Enterprise value is the sum of the two present values.
solution: $EV = 420 + 1{,}380 = \$1{,}800$ million, and $1{,}380/1{,}800 = 76.7\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A reviewer says your model's 2028 capex looks too low. You examined it and believe it is right, because a plant expansion finishes in 2027 and the filing's capex guidance supports your number. What is the best response?
choice: Raise capex to what the reviewer wants without further checking.
choice: Ignore the comment, since you disagree.
choice*: Reply with the evidence (the plant timing and the guidance, with their sources), and say you have left the forecast unchanged, showing the sensitivity if capex were higher.
choice: Delete the 2028 forecast so the comment does not apply.
hint: Disagreeing is acceptable when it is documented with evidence.
solution: A good response explains the evidence, states the decision and shows what happens if the reviewer is right, which respects the comment and keeps your reasonable basis. Changing numbers without understanding, ignoring comments or deleting forecasts all avoid the issue.

@practice self
prompt: You are the author of a report on a fictional company, Pinecrest Logistics. Write short responses (one to three sentences each) to three reviewer comments, stating whether you fix, counter with evidence or decline. (1) "The front page upside is 22% but your valuation table gives a target of \$51 on a price of \$42." (2) "You rate the moat wide, but the 10-K says the top customer is 35% of revenue and its contract expires next year." (3) "Please add a section on the history of the founder's family." Include what you would change and where.
hint: Compute the upside in comment 1; for comment 2 consider whether the reviewer is right; for 3 decide whether it supports the thesis.
solution: (1) Fix: $51/42 - 1 = 21.4\%$, so the front page is wrong; I corrected it to 21.4% on the front page and in the summary paragraph, and re-audited every other figure. (2) Fix with evidence: the reviewer is right that customer concentration and the contract expiry weaken a wide-moat claim; I have lowered the rating to narrow, added the 35% concentration and contract date to the moat and risk sections, and added a scenario in which the contract is lost. (3) Decline: the family history does not support the thesis or change any forecast, and the page budget is tight; governance facts relevant to value (control, board, related-party dealings) are already covered in the ESG and governance section.
rubric: Computes the upside correctly (21.4%) and says where it was corrected, applying the number audit to the rest of the report.
rubric: Accepts the valid moat comment with a specific change (rating, risk section, scenario), not just agreement.
rubric: Declines the off-thesis request with a reason tied to value and the page budget, while pointing to where relevant content already is.

@quiz num
prompt: A reviewer finds that a model forecasts EBIT of \$200 million and taxes it at 15%, but the correct rate is 22%. By what percent is NOPAT overstated relative to the correct NOPAT? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 8.97
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (200 * 0.85 / (200 * 0.78) - 1) * 100
hint: Compute NOPAT at both tax rates.
solution: NOPAT at 15% is \$170 million and at 22% is \$156 million, so the overstatement is $170/156 - 1 = 8.97\%$.

@quiz mc
prompt: After a review you discover an error that lowers your price target from \$42 to \$38 and moves the rating one notch lower. You have already circulated the draft to readers. What should you do?
choice: Say nothing, since the report was a draft.
choice: Quietly replace the file and tell no one.
choice*: Correct the model and report, record the change in the revision log and tell the readers of the draft that the target and rating changed and why.
choice: Keep the old target and rating to avoid looking uncertain.
hint: Readers who relied on the old numbers need to know.
solution: Correcting the work and communicating the change are consistent with Standards V(A) and III(B): a reasonable basis must support the conclusion, and a changed rating should be communicated fairly to those who received the original. Silence, a quiet swap or keeping a known error all mislead readers.
