@article
Last week you described the industry; this week you answer a sharper question: **does this company earn more than its cost of capital, and why will (or will not) that last?** A company that earns exactly its cost of capital creates no value from growth, however fast it grows. An **economic moat** is a durable advantage that protects returns from competition. Your report must show a moat with evidence, not assert one.

### Measure first: ROIC and economic profit

**Return on invested capital** compares operating profit after tax with the capital used to produce it:

$$ROIC = \frac{NOPAT}{\text{invested capital}}, \qquad NOPAT = EBIT \times (1 - t).$$

Invested capital is operating working capital plus net property, plant and equipment plus goodwill and intangibles (equivalently, debt plus equity minus excess cash). State whether you use year-end or average capital, and which tax rate (statutory 21% federal plus state, or the effective rate). **Economic profit** is what remains after charging for all capital:

$$\text{Economic profit} = NOPAT - WACC \times \text{invested capital} = (ROIC - WACC) \times \text{invested capital}.$$

A positive spread (ROIC above WACC) is the numerical footprint of a moat. But numbers show *that*, not *why*: you need the source.

### Sources of a moat

1. **Intangible assets**: brands that let a company charge more, patents, licenses and regulatory approvals that block rivals.
2. **Switching costs**: the customer would lose time, money or data by changing supplier (embedded software, certified components, training).
3. **Network effects**: the product is more valuable to each user as more users join.
4. **Cost advantages**: lower unit costs from process, location, or access to a scarce resource, which allow lower prices or higher margins.
5. **Efficient scale**: a market that supports only a few firms profitably, so a new entrant would lower returns for everyone.

For each source claim, find **evidence in the filings**: customer retention or renewal rates, price increases that did not cause churn, margins above peers over several years, long contracts, patent expiries, regulatory exclusivity, or the share of revenue from customers who have used the product for more than five years. Then look for **contradicting evidence**: falling share, discounting, customers building their own solution, an expiring patent.

### Fade and durability

Competition erodes excess returns, so a good model lets ROIC **fade** toward WACC unless the moat is strong. Ask four things: Is the advantage structural or temporary (a good product cycle)? Is it widening or narrowing? What would a well-funded rival need to do? What does management do with excess returns: reinvest at similar returns, or waste them on weak acquisitions?

### Lifetime value of a customer

When customers renew, an advantage can be measured in unit economics. With annual gross profit per customer $g$, annual retention $\rho$ and discount rate $d$, if the first gross profit arrives one year from now and the customer is retained with probability $\rho$ each year, the present value of a customer is

$$LTV = \frac{g}{1 + d - \rho}.$$

(This uses expected cash flow $g\rho^{t-1}$ at time $t$, discounted at $d$.) Compare $LTV$ with the cost of acquiring the customer (CAC): an $LTV/CAC$ ratio well above 1 supports a growth investment; near 1 means growth creates little value.

### Balanced judgment

A moat is a hypothesis. Rate it (none, narrow, wide) and say what would change your mind. Honest uncertainty is more credible than a confident label.

@example
title: ROIC and economic profit for Corvane
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) has EBIT of \$168 million and a 22% tax rate. Its invested capital is operating working capital \$210 million, net PP&E \$640 million and goodwill and intangibles \$330 million. Its WACC is 8.4%. Compute ROIC and economic profit.
step: NOPAT: $168 \times (1 - 0.22) = \$131.04$ million.
step: Invested capital: $210 + 640 + 330 = \$1{,}180$ million.
step: ROIC: $131.04 / 1{,}180 = 11.11\%$.
step: Economic profit: $131.04 - 0.084 \times 1{,}180 = 131.04 - 99.12 = \$31.92$ million, equal to $(11.11\% - 8.4\%) \times 1{,}180$. The spread of 2.7 points is positive but modest, so the case for a wide moat needs strong evidence.
answer: ROIC 11.11%; economic profit \$31.92 million.

@example
title: Lifetime value of a services contract
problem: A Corvane services customer pays \$18,500 a year at a 62% gross margin. Annual retention is 94%, the discount rate is 9% and the cost to acquire a customer is \$22,000. Compute the customer's lifetime value and the LTV/CAC ratio.
step: Annual gross profit: $18{,}500 \times 0.62 = \$11{,}470$.
step: Lifetime value: $11{,}470 / (1 + 0.09 - 0.94) = 11{,}470 / 0.15 = \$76{,}467$.
step: Ratio: $76{,}467 / 22{,}000 = 3.48$.
step: An LTV/CAC of 3.5 indicates that each new customer creates value, and the high retention is evidence for switching costs. The check is the retention figure: at 90% instead of 94% the value falls to \$60,368, so the estimate is sensitive.
answer: LTV \$76,467; LTV/CAC 3.48.

@mistakes
- Treating high margins in a single year as proof of a moat, without checking that returns persist over a cycle and against peers.
- Computing ROIC with the wrong numerator or denominator (pretax EBIT, or including excess cash in invested capital) so that it cannot be compared with WACC.
- Listing moat sources from a textbook without filing evidence, and ignoring contradicting evidence such as falling share or discounting.
- Assuming returns stay above WACC forever in the model, with no fade, however weak the evidence.

@quiz mc
prompt: A company's ROIC has been 14% for years and its WACC is 8%. A new rival with similar technology and a lower price enters. Which forecast treatment is most defensible?
choice: Hold ROIC at 14% forever, because the past was good.
choice*: Let ROIC fade gradually toward WACC, with the pace set by the strength of the moat.
choice: Set ROIC to below WACC immediately, because competition eliminates all profit.
choice: Ignore ROIC and forecast only revenue.
hint: Competition erodes excess returns, but usually not instantly.
solution: A fade reflects that excess returns attract competition, and the speed depends on the evidence for durability. Holding 14% ignores the entrant, dropping below WACC immediately overstates the damage, and ignoring ROIC discards a key check on the forecast.
