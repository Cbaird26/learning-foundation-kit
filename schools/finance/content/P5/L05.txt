@article
Financial analysis in a report is not a table of ratios. It is an argument: *these are the trends, this is what drives them, and this is how much I trust the numbers.* A forecast built on earnings you do not trust is a guess with decimals.

### Start from five years of statements

Download the income statement, balance sheet and cash flow statement for at least five fiscal years from the 10-Ks (the financial statements are also available in structured form on EDGAR). Put them in one spreadsheet, one column per year, and compute **growth rates** and **common-size** statements (every line as a percent of revenue, or of total assets). Then read the notes: segments, revenue recognition (ASC 606), leases (ASC 842), debt, income taxes, and the commitments and contingencies note. Recent US GAAP updates add detail: ASU 2023-07 expands segment expense disclosure, and ASU 2024-03, effective for annual periods beginning after December 15, 2026, requires further breakdown of income statement expenses. Under IFRS, IFRS 18 replaces IAS 1 for annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2027, adding new operating, investing and financing categories. Say which framework your company uses.

### The three-step DuPont identity

Return on equity breaks into profitability, efficiency and leverage:

$$ROE = \underbrace{\frac{\text{net income}}{\text{revenue}}}_{\text{net margin}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{revenue}}{\text{total assets}}}_{\text{asset turnover}} \times \underbrace{\frac{\text{total assets}}{\text{equity}}}_{\text{leverage}}.$$

State whether you use year-end or average balances. DuPont tells you *where* ROE changed: a rising ROE from rising leverage is a different story from one driven by margin.

### Working capital and the cash conversion cycle

With revenue and cost of goods sold (COGS) and year-end balances (365 days):

$$DSO = \frac{\text{receivables}}{\text{revenue}} \times 365, \quad DIO = \frac{\text{inventory}}{COGS} \times 365, \quad DPO = \frac{\text{payables}}{COGS} \times 365.$$

The **cash conversion cycle** is $CCC = DSO + DIO - DPO$: the days a dollar is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. A lengthening cycle consumes cash even when profits look fine.

### Quality of earnings

Earnings are an opinion; cash is a fact. Check these in order:

1. **Cash flow from operations (CFO) against net income.** Persistently CFO below net income is a warning. A ratio near or above 1 is reassuring.
2. **The accruals ratio**: $(\text{net income} - CFO)/\text{average total assets}$. High positive values mean earnings rest on non-cash items.
3. **Receivables and inventory growth against revenue growth.** Faster growth in receivables can mean looser credit or aggressive revenue recognition; faster inventory growth can mean weak demand.
4. **Free cash flow**, $FCF = CFO - \text{capital expenditures}$, and **FCF conversion**, $FCF/\text{net income}$.
5. **Non-GAAP measures.** Companies may report "adjusted" figures, but SEC rules (Regulation G and Item 10(e) of Regulation S-K) require a reconciliation to the nearest GAAP measure and equal prominence for the GAAP figure. If "one-time" charges appear every year, treat them as recurring costs.

### Capital structure and liquidity

Compute net debt to EBITDA, interest coverage ($EBIT$ divided by interest expense) and the current ratio, and read the debt note for maturities, covenants and floating-rate exposure. Operating lease obligations belong in the discussion of leverage.

### From ratios to prose

For each finding write: **what** (the number and trend), **why** (the driver, with the filing reference) and **so what** (the effect on the forecast or the valuation). "DSO rose from 51 to 67 days because of extended terms to distributors, which will absorb about \$20 million of cash if terms persist" is analysis. "DSO rose" is not.

@example
title: DuPont analysis for Corvane
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) reports net income \$112.4 million, revenue \$1,646.2 million, year-end total assets \$1,980 million and equity \$1,020 million. Decompose ROE.
step: Net margin: $112.4 / 1{,}646.2 = 6.83\%$.
step: Asset turnover: $1{,}646.2 / 1{,}980 = 0.831$ times.
step: Leverage: $1{,}980 / 1{,}020 = 1.941$.
step: Multiply: $0.0683 \times 0.831 \times 1.941 = 11.02\%$, which equals $112.4 / 1{,}020 = 11.02\%$. Moderate margin and moderate leverage drive the return; a rise in ROE next year should be traced to one of these three levers.
answer: ROE 11.02% = 6.83% margin x 0.831 turnover x 1.941 leverage.

@example
title: Corvane's cash conversion cycle
problem: Corvane has receivables of \$268.4 million, inventory of \$301.6 million and payables of \$148.5 million. Revenue is \$1,646.2 million and COGS is \$1,020 million. Compute DSO, DIO, DPO and the cash conversion cycle.
step: DSO: $268.4 / 1{,}646.2 \times 365 = 59.5$ days.
step: DIO: $301.6 / 1{,}020 \times 365 = 107.9$ days. DPO: $148.5 / 1{,}020 \times 365 = 53.1$ days.
step: CCC: $59.5 + 107.9 - 53.1 = 114.3$ days.
step: Interpretation: Corvane waits nearly four months between paying for inputs and collecting cash, mostly because of inventory. If the CCC fell by 10 days, about $10/365 \times 1{,}020$, or \$28 million, of cash would be released if the change came from inventory.
answer: DSO 59.5, DIO 107.9, DPO 53.1; CCC 114.3 days.

@mistakes
- Reporting a ratio without its trend, its driver or its effect on the forecast.
- Mixing year-end and average balances between years or between company and peers, so that comparisons are not like for like.
- Accepting "adjusted" earnings that exclude recurring costs, or failing to reconcile them to GAAP.
- Computing DIO and DPO with revenue instead of COGS.

unit: days
tol: 0.01

choice: Use adjusted EBITDA, because the company's management knows its business best.
choice*: Reconcile to the GAAP figures, treat the restructuring costs as a recurring cost of doing business, and forecast them.

hint: A cost that recurs every year is not one-time.
