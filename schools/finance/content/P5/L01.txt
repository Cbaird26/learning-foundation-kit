@article
A report that fails usually fails in week one. The analyst picked a company because it was famous, or because its stock had just doubled, and then discovered that nothing about it could be explained in a paragraph. This course is a capstone: over fourteen weeks you will build an **initiation report**, the document a research team publishes when it begins covering a company, with a thesis, a model, a valuation, a price target, risks and an ESG and governance review. Choosing well is the first professional judgment you make.

### What an initiation report is for

An initiation report gives a reader a reason to hold a view: what the company does, why it earns what it earns, what the forecast is, what the shares are worth, and what could prove you wrong. It is not a summary of the company's website. Every section serves a **thesis**, a short statement of what you believe and why the market may be mispricing it. Your work here is a learning exercise: it is general education, not a recommendation to buy or sell any security.

### Four selection criteria

1. **You can explain the business in one paragraph.** If you cannot say who pays the company, for what, and why they keep paying, you are outside your circle of competence. Start with simpler models: a maker of industrial components, a regional retailer, a food producer.
2. **The data exist.** A US-listed company files an annual report (**Form 10-K**), quarterly reports (**Form 10-Q**), current reports on events (**Form 8-K**) and a proxy statement (**DEF 14A**) on **SEC EDGAR**, which is free. Foreign private issuers file Form 20-F. You want at least five years of audited statements and a management discussion (MD&A) that explains the numbers.
3. **There is a real question.** "Is this a good company?" is not a question. "Can the company keep its operating margin above 12% while it moves production to a new plant?" is. A question tells you which numbers to chase.
4. **You can add something.** Very large companies are covered by dozens of analysts; a mid-sized company with little coverage lets a careful report show its work. Coverage is not a verdict on quality, it only changes how much of your view is likely to be new.

Also check practicalities: a fiscal year you can follow, segment disclosure, no pending event (a merger, a bankruptcy) that makes ordinary valuation meaningless, and no personal conflict. If you or your family own shares, say so in the report's disclosures, as a professional would.

### A first screen

A screen is a few numbers that tell you whether to keep going. **Market capitalization** is the share price times diluted shares outstanding. **Enterprise value** is the price of the whole operating business:

$$EV = \text{market cap} + \text{debt} + \text{preferred stock} + \text{noncontrolling interests} - \text{cash and equivalents}.$$

Then compare EV with operating earnings: $EV/EBITDA$ is a common multiple. The **earnings yield**, $EPS / \text{price}$, is the inverse of the P/E. These numbers do not say "cheap" or "expensive"; they place the company against its peers and its own history, which becomes your valuation in week 7. Look up the current price and share count on the cover of the latest 10-K or 10-Q and from a market data source; they change daily, so always record the date.

### From interest to a question

Read the latest 10-K's Business section (Item 1), Risk Factors (Item 1A) and MD&A (Item 7). Then write down what management says is changing, what the numbers say is changing, and where the two disagree. The gap is your question. Good questions are specific, answerable with filings, and matter to value: *Will the services segment, now 16% of revenue, lift the margin enough to justify a premium multiple?*

### The one-sentence test

Before leaving this week, write: "I will research [company] to answer whether [specific claim], because [why it matters to value]." If you cannot, keep reading filings until you can.

@example
title: A first screen of Corvane Instruments
problem: Corvane Instruments (a fictional maker of industrial sensors) trades at \$48.20. It has 62.5 million diluted shares, \$410 million of debt, \$135 million of cash and no preferred stock or noncontrolling interests. Its EBITDA is \$215 million. Compute market cap, enterprise value and EV/EBITDA.
step: Market cap is price times diluted shares: $48.20 \times 62.5 = \$3{,}012.5$ million.
step: Add debt and subtract cash: $EV = 3{,}012.5 + 410 - 135 = \$3{,}287.5$ million.
step: Divide by EBITDA: $3{,}287.5 / 215 = 15.29$ times.
step: Note what the number does and does not say. 15.3 times is a fact about price, not a verdict: you decide whether it is high only after comparing peers, growth and returns in later weeks.
answer: Market cap \$3,012.5 million; EV \$3,287.5 million; EV/EBITDA 15.29.

@example
title: Scoring three candidates
problem: You weight four criteria: data availability 30%, understandability 25%, question richness 25% and coverage gap 20%. You score each candidate from 1 to 5. Candidate A scores 4, 5, 3, 2; B scores 5, 3, 4, 3; C scores 3, 4, 5, 4 in the same order. Which candidate scores highest?
step: Multiply each score by its weight and add. A: $0.30(4) + 0.25(5) + 0.25(3) + 0.20(2) = 1.20 + 1.25 + 0.75 + 0.40 = 3.60$.
step: B: $0.30(5) + 0.25(3) + 0.25(4) + 0.20(3) = 1.50 + 0.75 + 1.00 + 0.60 = 3.85$.
step: C: $0.30(3) + 0.25(4) + 0.25(5) + 0.20(4) = 0.90 + 1.00 + 1.25 + 0.80 = 3.95$.
step: C scores highest. The score organizes your thinking; if the result surprises you, the weights or your gut need another look.
answer: Candidate C (3.95, against 3.85 and 3.60).

@mistakes
- Picking a company because its stock has just risen or fallen sharply, which makes the price the story instead of the business.
- Writing a topic ("the semiconductor industry") instead of a question that filings can answer.
- Using a stale share count or price in the screen, or forgetting to subtract cash and add debt when computing enterprise value.
- Choosing a company in the middle of a merger or restructuring without saying that the ordinary valuation methods may not apply.

@practice self
prompt: You are reading the 10-K of a fictional bakery chain, Wheatfield Bakehouse. Revenue grew 6% a year for five years, but operating margin fell from 14% to 10% as ingredient costs and wages rose, and management says new "express" stores, now 20% of revenue, will restore margins. Write one sentence in the form "I will research ... to answer whether ..., because ..." and name two filing sections or data you would read first.
hint: The question should concern whether margins can be restored and why that matters for value.
solution: I will research Wheatfield Bakehouse to answer whether its express stores can lift the company's operating margin back toward 14% while ingredient and wage costs stay high, because the margin recovery drives most of the difference between the current share price and a higher valuation. I would read MD&A (Item 7) for management's explanation of margin changes and the segment or store-format disclosure in the notes for the express stores' sales and profit, plus Risk Factors (Item 1A) for cost exposure.
rubric: States a single, specific question about margin recovery (not a general topic like "the bakery industry").
rubric: Links the question to value: says why the answer matters to the share price or forecast.
rubric: Names at least two concrete places to look (for example MD&A, segment note, risk factors, store data) that exist in a 10-K.
