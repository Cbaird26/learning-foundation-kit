@article
A pitch is the report made audible. Colleagues, portfolio managers and clients hear it once, cannot flip back, and decide in minutes whether you understand the company. The Q&A afterwards is where they find out. Preparation for both is the same: know the three things you want them to remember, and know where your analysis is weakest.

### The ten-minute structure

Lead with the answer. A pitch that builds suspense about the rating wastes the audience's best attention. A workable allocation:

| Segment | Minutes | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Thesis and recommendation | 1.0 | Rating, price target, three reasons, what the market misses |
| Business and moat | 2.0 | Who pays, why they stay, evidence |
| Forecast and valuation | 3.0 | Key drivers, DCF, multiples, target, sensitivity |
| Risks and catalysts | 2.0 | Top three risks, dated catalysts, what would change your mind |
| ESG and governance | 0.5 | The one or two material facts |
| Close | 1.5 | Restate the thesis, the target, and the rating framework |

Speakers talk at roughly 130 to 150 words a minute, so ten minutes is about 1,400 words, less if you pause on a chart. Use at most eight slides, one message per slide, a title that states the message, and a number that carries it. Describe what a chart shows in words as you present, because a recording may be watched without the slides in view.

### Delivery

Rehearse aloud with a timer and record yourself, which is the assignment's recorded pitch. Cut anything you cannot say in one breath. Use plain words: "we think the market is too pessimistic about services growth" beats "the opportunity set remains compelling." Own the numbers: if you read from slides, the audience stops listening. Keep the learning-exercise statement visible, and present estimates as estimates and facts as facts, which is part of fair communication under Standard V(B).

### The reverse DCF as your best slide

The market price is a forecast. Ask what it implies. With a single-stage approximation, enterprise value equals next year's free cash flow divided by $WACC - g$, so the **implied perpetual growth** is

$$g = WACC - \frac{FCFF_1}{EV}.$$

If the implied $g$ is far above what the industry can sustain, the price assumes more than you do; if it is below, the market is pessimistic. Use it with care: a single stage ignores years of faster growth, so the implied $g$ is a rough guide to compare against your own forecast, not an estimate of the long-run rate.

### The five hard questions

Before the Q&A, write and rehearse answers to these, adapted to your company:

1. **"What do you know that the market does not?"** (Your variant perception, with evidence.)
2. **"What is priced in?"** (Your reverse DCF.)
3. **"What would make you change your rating?"** (Specific, observable triggers.)
4. **"Why this multiple or this WACC?"** (Sources, ranges, sensitivity.)
5. **"What is the biggest risk you are not modeling?"** (Honest humility, plus how you would monitor it.)

### Answering well

Listen to the whole question; restate it if it is long. Answer in the first sentence, then support it. If you do not know, say so: "I don't know; here is how I would find out, and I will send the answer." A confident wrong answer costs far more than an honest gap. If the question reveals an error, thank the questioner, say what changes and correct the report afterwards. If the question is hostile, answer the substance and ignore the tone. Never invent a statistic, and never answer with information you could not disclose.

@example
title: A time budget for the pitch
problem: Your pitch is 10 minutes at 140 words a minute. You allocate 1.0 minute to the thesis, 2.0 to business and moat, 3.0 to forecast and valuation, 2.0 to risks and catalysts, 0.5 to ESG and 1.5 to the close. How many words does each segment get, and does the plan fit?
step: Total words: $10 \times 140 = 1{,}400$.
step: Minutes add to $1.0 + 2.0 + 3.0 + 2.0 + 0.5 + 1.5 = 10.0$, so the plan fits.
step: Words per segment: thesis 140, business and moat 280, forecast and valuation 420, risks and catalysts 280, ESG 70, close 210. They sum to 1,400.
step: Test the plan against your report: valuation is the largest segment at 30% of the words, matching its weight in the thesis. If your thesis depends on the moat, the moat deserves more than 280 words and something else must give.
answer: 1,400 words in total; 140, 280, 420, 280, 70 and 210 words by segment.

@example
title: What is priced in?
problem: Corvane Instruments (fictional) has an enterprise value of \$3,287.5 million at the current price, next year's FCFF is forecast at \$118 million and WACC is 8.4%. What perpetual growth does the price imply, and how do you use it?
step: Compute the cash flow yield: $118/3{,}287.5 = 3.59\%$.
step: Implied growth: $g = 8.4\% - 3.59\% = 4.81\%$.
step: Interpret: for the price to be justified by a single stage of growth, Corvane's free cash flow would need to grow at about 4.8% forever, well above a long-run nominal growth rate near 2.5% to 3%. Since our forecast has faster growth for five years and then 2.5%, the stock is priced for more than our case.
step: Present it as a rough guide: a single stage ignores the higher growth in early years, so say that the market may be assuming a longer period of high growth, and show the DCF sensitivity for WACC and growth that would be needed to reach \$48.20.
answer: Implied perpetual growth about 4.8%, above the 2.5% in our terminal value, so the price assumes more than we do.

@mistakes
- Building suspense instead of stating the rating and thesis in the first minute.
- Putting dense tables on slides and reading them aloud, or timing no rehearsal and running over.
- Bluffing an answer, inventing a figure, or defending an error instead of acknowledging it.
- Treating the implied growth from a reverse DCF as precise, when it comes from a simplified single-stage formula.

@practice self
prompt: Fictional Northgate Tools trades at \$30. Your report values it at \$26 (DCF \$24, multiples \$28), rates it Equal-weight (your framework: Overweight above +15% expected total return, Underweight below -15%, otherwise Equal-weight; ignore dividends), and your reverse DCF shows the price implies about 4.6% perpetual growth against your 2.5%. Write short model answers (two to three sentences each) to three hard questions: (1) "Your DCF is below the price. Why shouldn't I just call it a Sell?" (2) "What would change your rating?" (3) "What is the biggest risk you are not modeling?"
hint: Use the facts given and be specific about numbers, triggers and limits.
solution: (1) Because the gap between \$26 and \$30 is 13% and my two methods disagree by \$4, so the difference is inside the range of my WACC and growth assumptions; on my framework an expected return of about -13% is inside the Equal-weight band, and a Sell-type rating would need a return worse than -15%; and the reverse DCF shows the market assumes about 4.6% growth against my 2.5%, which makes the stock expensive but not clearly mispriced. (2) A fall in the price toward \$22 would raise the expected return to Overweight levels; upside comes from two quarters of margin gains that lift my 2027 EPS estimate, and downside from a loss of its largest customer. (3) The risk I do not model is a sharp change in tariff or input-cost policy affecting its imported components. I treat it qualitatively because I have no way to estimate its probability; I would watch supplier cost disclosures and the cost-of-sales line each quarter.
rubric: Answer 1 uses the numbers (a 13% gap, a \$4 difference between methods, 4.6% against 2.5%) and explains why the evidence supports Equal-weight and not Sell.
rubric: Answer 2 gives specific, observable triggers (price level, margin or EPS result, a customer event) and does not just say "new information".
rubric: Answer 3 names a real unmodeled risk honestly, explains why it is not modeled and says how it would be monitored, without inventing data.

prompt: During Q&A, a listener shows you that you used the wrong share count, which lowers your price target by 4%. What should you do?
choice: Argue that the difference is too small to matter.
choice*: Thank the listener, state the corrected target, and update the report and model afterwards.
choice: Say the number came from the company, so it is not your responsibility.
