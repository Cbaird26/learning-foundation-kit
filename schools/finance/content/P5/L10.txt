@article
Most readers give a report one page. If the front page does not state the rating, the price target, the thesis and the evidence, many will never reach page two. Writing is not decoration applied to analysis: it is where you find out whether you understand your own argument. This week produces the full draft.

### The front page

The front page should work alone. Include: company, ticker and date; **rating and price target** with the price on that date; a **title that states the thesis** ("Margins have more room than the market believes," not "Initiating coverage"); three to four bullets giving the reasons; a key data table (market cap, EV, multiples, estimates, dividend yield); and a small valuation summary. Everything else in the report proves what the front page claims.

### Writing the argument

Use the pyramid structure: **answer first, then support.** Each section opens with a sentence that states its conclusion ("Corvane's returns exceed its cost of capital because customers rarely switch"), then gives the evidence, then the implication for value. Rules that make writing better:

- One idea per paragraph; the first sentence carries it.
- Use numbers with units, dates and sources: "operating margin rose from 9.1% to 11.4% between fiscal 2022 and fiscal 2025 (10-K)."
- Prefer verbs and short sentences. Cut adverbs ("significantly", "very") and replace them with the number.
- Every exhibit has a title that states its message and a source line. Because your readers may not see images, describe each chart's message in the text as well.
- Separate fact, estimate and opinion. "We estimate" and "management states" are different claims.

### Editing in passes

Edit in order, because there is no point polishing a sentence in a section you will cut:

1. **Structure**: does each section serve the thesis? Delete what does not.
2. **Argument**: is every claim supported, and every risk to the claim faced?
3. **Numbers**: check each figure against its source and against every other place it appears (see below).
4. **Sentences**: clarity, length, jargon, consistency of terms.
5. **Proofread**: spelling, units, formatting.

### The number audit

Inconsistent numbers destroy trust faster than a bad rating. Make a list of every number on the front page and check: the price target equals the valuation table; the upside equals $PT/P_0 - 1$; the multiples equal price (or target) divided by the stated estimates; text, tables and the model agree; units and fiscal years are labeled. Tie every historical number to a filing and every forecast to the model's cell.

### Standards, sources and disclosures

Under the CFA Institute Standards, Standard V(B) requires clear communication of the basis for your conclusions and of the limits of your analysis, Standard V(C) requires you to maintain records that support your research (the Handbook recommends keeping them at least seven years, absent other requirements), and Standard I(C) bars misrepresentation, which includes plagiarism: using others' words, charts or data without attribution. Cite every source by name and date. Professional reports also carry disclosures. For broker-dealer research, FINRA Rule 2241 requires policies on conflicts and disclosures such as the firm's rating definitions, how ratings are distributed and material conflicts of interest, and the SEC's Regulation AC requires the analyst certification. Your learning-exercise report should include: an **analyst certification** that the views are your own and not influenced by compensation; **conflicts** (any holding in the company or its peers); **methodology** and the rating framework; the **date and price** used; the **sources**; and a plain statement that the report is a learning exercise and not investment advice. Rules change; check current requirements.

### Final read

Read the report aloud, then as a skeptic who owns the stock and as one who is short it. If either finds an unanswered objection, add the answer.

@example
title: Editing a weak paragraph
problem: A draft says: "The company has a very strong position in the market and is well positioned for significant growth going forward, which management believes will drive meaningful margin expansion." Rewrite it for a front-page bullet using these facts: Corvane has 11.8% of a \$14.0 billion market and earns an 11.1% ROIC against an 8.4% WACC; services (16% of revenue) carry a 62% gross margin against 38% for hardware; management targets 20% services by 2030.
step: Find the claim hiding in the vague sentence: that growth in services lifts margin.
step: Replace adjectives with numbers and sources: 11.8% share, 11.1% ROIC versus 8.4% WACC, services mix 16% and 62% gross margin versus 38%.
step: Put the answer first and the implication last. Rewrite: "A shift toward services should lift margins: services carry a 62% gross margin against 38% for hardware, and moving from 16% to management's 20% target of revenue adds about 1 point to the company's gross margin. At an 11.1% ROIC against an 8.4% WACC, the extra margin creates value."
step: Check the arithmetic in the rewrite: a mix change of 4 points times a 24-point margin gap is $0.04 \times 0.24 = 0.96$ points, so "about 1 point" is right.
answer: A bullet that states the claim (services lift margin), the numbers and the value implication, with the 0.96-point mix effect checked.

@example
title: A number-consistency audit
problem: A draft front page says: price \$44, price target \$55, "30% upside"; 2027 EPS estimate \$2.75 and "target of 18 times earnings"; the text says net margin is 9%, while the model shows net income of \$48 million on revenue of \$585 million. Which statements are wrong?
step: Upside: $55/44 - 1 = 25.0\%$, not 30%. Correct the front page to 25%.
step: Implied multiple: $55/2.75 = 20.0$ times, not 18. Either the target multiple or the EPS is misstated: check the valuation tab.
step: Net margin: $48/585 = 8.2\%$, not 9%.
step: All three checks fail, so audit every other figure. Fix the source, then fix every place the number appears.
answer: Upside should be 25%, the implied multiple is 20.0 times and net margin is 8.2%.

@mistakes
- Titles that describe the report ("Initiating coverage") instead of stating the thesis, and front pages that make the reader hunt for the rating and target.
- Vague adjectives ("strong", "significant") where a number and a source belong.
- Numbers that disagree between the front page, text, tables and model, because they were edited in one place only.
- Using another author's chart, data or wording without attribution, which is misrepresentation as well as poor practice.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite this front-page bullet so that it states a claim with numbers, using these facts: Redwater Beverages' operating margin was 12.4% in fiscal 2022 and 14.1% in fiscal 2025; it raised prices 3% a year while input costs rose 2% a year; a new bottling line cuts unit cost by about 4%. Draft: "Redwater is well run and should see significant margin improvement going forward given its favorable trends." Your bullet should be one or two sentences.
hint: Say what you expect, why, and give the evidence in numbers with dates.
solution: Redwater's operating margin should keep widening from 14.1% (fiscal 2025, up from 12.4% in fiscal 2022): price increases of 3% a year have outpaced input-cost inflation of 2%, and a new bottling line cuts unit cost by about 4%. We forecast the margin continuing to rise, because both drivers persist, but the gain slows as price increases meet resistance.
rubric: States a clear claim about the margin with the supporting drivers (price versus cost, the bottling line) rather than adjectives.
rubric: Uses at least two numbers from the facts with dates or units (14.1%, 12.4%, 3% against 2%, 4%).
rubric: Stays within one or two sentences and avoids vague words such as "well run", "significant" or "favorable".
