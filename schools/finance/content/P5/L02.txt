@article
Professional analysts plan before they research, because a report is a chain of dependent tasks: you cannot value what you have not forecast, and you cannot forecast what you have not understood. A plan also protects you from the most common failure of capstone work, which is spending two months on the business description and two days on the valuation.

### The standard anatomy of an initiation report

Reports differ by firm, but a 10 to 15 page initiation follows a recognizable order:

| Section | Job it does | Typical share of pages |
|---|---|---|
| Front page | Rating, price target, thesis in 3 bullets, key data table | 1 |
| Business and industry | Who pays, for what, in what market | 2 |
| Competitive position | Why the company earns above (or below) its cost of capital | 1 |
| Financial analysis | Quality and trend of results | 2 |
| Forecast and valuation | Key assumptions, DCF, multiples, price target | 3 |
| Risks and catalysts | What could prove you wrong, and when you will know | 1 |
| ESG and governance | Material issues, factually | 1 |
| Appendix and disclosures | Statements, sources, conflicts, analyst certification | 1 to 2 |

The page budget tells you where the argument lives. If valuation is three pages and the model is one tab you never reviewed, the plan is unbalanced.

### Research standards that apply from day one

Even a learning exercise should follow the habits of the profession. Under the CFA Institute Code and Standards, Standard V(A) requires **diligence and a reasonable basis** for any recommendation, Standard V(B) requires clear **communication** of the process, and Standard V(C) requires you to maintain **records** of what supported your conclusions (the Standards of Practice Handbook recommends keeping them at least seven years, absent other requirements). Standard I(C) forbids misrepresentation, which includes passing off others' work as your own, so every fact needs a source you can name. In the United States, broker-dealer research is governed by FINRA Rule 2241 (research analysts and research reports: policies on conflicts, required disclosures) and by the SEC's Regulation Analyst Certification (Regulation AC), under which the analyst certifies that the views expressed accurately reflect his or her personal views and that no part of the analyst's pay was, is or will be directly or indirectly related to specific recommendations or views. You are not publishing as a firm, but your report should carry a similar certification and a plain statement of any conflicts. Rules change, so check current requirements before relying on any summary.

### The one-page proposal

A proposal lets a reviewer say "yes" or "narrow it" before you spend 80 hours. It contains:

1. **Company and ticker**, with the filing date of the 10-K you will start from.
2. **Research question** (the sentence from week 1) and a preliminary thesis you are willing to abandon.
3. **Why it matters to value**: which forecast or multiple the answer drives.
4. **Sources**: the filings, industry data and peers you will use.
5. **Plan**: major tasks with dates and the biggest risk to the plan.

Notice what it does not contain: a price target. You do not have one yet, and announcing one on day one creates a conclusion to defend.

### Scheduling backward

Start from the deadline and subtract. List tasks, estimate days for each, add a **buffer** for the work you will redo, and see when you must begin. Two rules: put the model before the valuation and the valuation before the writing, but start drafting sections that do not depend on the numbers (business, industry) early; and reserve time at the end for a peer review, because week 11 is for revision.

@example
title: Working backward from a deadline
problem: Your tasks take: data collection 6 days, business and industry 8, financial analysis 7, model 12, valuation 6, risks and ESG 6, writing 10 and review and revision 7. They run one after another. You add a 15% buffer. How many calendar days before the deadline must you start, rounding up?
step: Add the task days: $6 + 8 + 7 + 12 + 6 + 6 + 10 + 7 = 62$ days.
step: Add the buffer: $62 \times 1.15 = 71.3$ days.
step: Round up, because you cannot start in the middle of a day: 72 days.
step: Place milestones backward from the deadline: revision starts 7 days (plus buffer share) before it, writing before that, and so on.
answer: 72 days.

@example
title: Budgeting hours
problem: You can spend 90 hours. You allot 15% to business and industry, 8% to competitive position, 12% to financial analysis, 25% to the model, 12% to valuation, 8% to risks and ESG, 14% to writing and 6% to review. How many hours do the model and valuation get together, and do the shares add to 100%?
step: Check the shares: $15 + 8 + 12 + 25 + 12 + 8 + 14 + 6 = 100\%$.
step: Model and valuation together are $25\% + 12\% = 37\%$ of the time.
step: In hours: $0.37 \times 90 = 33.3$ hours.
step: Compare with the page plan: those two sections are about 3 of 12 pages (25%), but they need 37% of the hours because building and testing a model is slower than reporting it. A plan can allow different shares of pages and hours, but you should be able to explain the difference.
answer: 33.3 hours; the shares add to 100%.

@mistakes
- Planning only the writing, so that the model and valuation have no time and the report lacks a defensible price target.
- Stating a price target or rating in the proposal, before any analysis, and then bending the work toward it.
- Leaving no buffer or review time: revision is where reports improve, and it always takes longer than planned.
- Treating regulatory and ethics rules as paperwork for the end rather than habits (sources, records, disclosures) for the whole project.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page-style proposal (about 150 words) for Brightwater Marine Supply, a fictional distributor of boat parts. Facts from its 10-K: revenue \$720 million, up 4% a year for five years; gross margin 31%; 40% of sales come from online orders, up from 22% five years ago; inventory is 48% of total assets; the fiscal year ends in September. Include the company, your research question and why it matters to value, your sources, and a three-line schedule.
hint: Pick one question, such as whether online growth can lift margins or whether inventory will tie up cash.
solution: Company: Brightwater Marine Supply (fictional), starting from the latest September 10-K. Question: Can the shift of sales to online orders (40% of revenue, from 22%) lift the gross margin above 31% without raising the inventory the company must hold? This matters to value because margin sets operating earnings and inventory sets how much cash the business consumes as it grows, which together drive free cash flow. Sources: the 10-K and 10-Qs (MD&A, segment and inventory notes), proxy statement, peer 10-Ks and industry sales data. Plan: weeks 3 to 4, business and industry sections; weeks 5 to 6, financial analysis and model; weeks 7 to 8, valuation and risks; weeks 9 to 12, ESG, draft, review and pitch. Biggest risk to the plan: peer data for margin comparisons may be thin, so I will start collecting it in week 3.
rubric: Names the company and the filing it starts from and states one specific, answerable question tied to online mix, margin or inventory.
rubric: Explains why the answer matters to value (links to margin, cash flow or forecast), without stating a price target or rating.
rubric: Lists concrete sources (10-K sections, peers, industry data) and a dated or week-based schedule with at least one named risk.
