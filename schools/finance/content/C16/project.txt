@review
## Week-by-week review

1. **Process.** Planning (IPS, capital market expectations, SAA), execution, feedback. Holding period return; arithmetic versus geometric mean (geometric is lower; $\bar R_G \approx \bar R - \sigma^2/2$); two-asset risk includes the covariance term.
2. **IPS.** Return and risk objectives; constraints (time horizon, taxes, liquidity, legal and regulatory, unique). Ability versus willingness: the lower governs. Required return $(FV/PV)^{1/n}-1$; real return $(1+r)/(1+\pi)-1$; after tax $r(1-t)$.
3. **SAA.** Capital market assumptions; $\sigma_p^2 = \sum\sum w_iw_j\sigma_{ij}$; the global minimum-variance weight; Sharpe ratio; the capital allocation line $E(R) = R_f + S\sigma$; sensitivity of optimizers to inputs.
4. **Tactical and rebalancing.** Active weights times returns; drift; trades $= w^*V - V_i$; bands, calendar, constant-mix (concave), buy-and-hold (linear), CPPI (convex, $m(V-F)$).
5. **Equity.** Free-float cap weights; replication; active share $\tfrac12\sum|w_p-w_b|$; tracking error (sample standard deviation, $\times\sqrt{k}$); information ratio; net-of-fee comparison; core-satellite.
6. **Fixed income.** $\Delta P/P \approx -D\Delta y + \tfrac12C\Delta y^2$; DV01; portfolio duration; immunization (PV, Macaulay duration, convexity); barbell, bullet, ladder; rolldown.
7. **Alternatives.** DPI, RVPI, TVPI, IRR; hedge fund fees (2 and 20, hurdle, high-water mark); unsmoothing $r=(r^{obs}-\varphi r^{obs}_{-1})/(1-\varphi)$; survivorship and backfill bias.
8. **Risk.** VaR $=V\sigma z_c$ (state confidence and horizon; $\sqrt T$ scaling); historical simulation $k$-th worst loss; Monte Carlo; expected shortfall; stress tests; backtesting.
9. **Performance.** Time-weighted versus money-weighted return; Sharpe, Treynor, Jensen's alpha, $M^2$; GIPS 2020 (firm-wide, composites, voluntary verification).
10. **Attribution.** Brinson-Fachler: allocation $(w_p-w_b)(R_{b,i}-R_b)$, selection $w_b(R_p-R_b)$, interaction $(w_p-w_b)(R_p-R_b)$; they sum to the active return.
11. **Behavioral and institutional.** Loss aversion, overconfidence, anchoring, herding, disposition effect; funded ratio; spending rules; legal constraints.
12. **Ethics.** Loyalty, prudence and care; Standards III(A) to III(D), VI(A) to VI(C); conflicts and remedies.

## Common errors

Averaging standard deviations; using a periodic rate as annual; using the arithmetic mean for multi-year growth; scaling VaR by $T$ instead of $\sqrt T$; reading VaR as a maximum; mixing BHB and Brinson-Fachler allocation; using end-of-period weights; calling disclosure a cure for every conflict.

@exam num
prompt: A fictional client's portfolio holds 55% US equity, 15% international equity and 30% bonds. Use these hypothetical annual capital market assumptions: expected returns 8.0%, 8.5% and 4.0%; standard deviations 16%, 19% and 6%; correlations US-international 0.80, US-bonds 0.15, international-bonds 0.10; risk-free rate 2.5%. What is the portfolio's Sharpe ratio? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.38
unit: times
tol: 0.01
check: (0.55 * 0.08 + 0.15 * 0.085 + 0.30 * 0.04 - 0.025) / sqrt(0.55**2 * 0.16**2 + 0.15**2 * 0.19**2 + 0.30**2 * 0.06**2 + 2 * 0.55 * 0.15 * 0.8 * 0.16 * 0.19 + 2 * 0.55 * 0.30 * 0.15 * 0.16 * 0.06 + 2 * 0.15 * 0.30 * 0.10 * 0.19 * 0.06)
hint: Compute the expected return first (6.875%), then the variance with three variance terms and three covariance terms.
hint: The portfolio variance is about $0.013471$, so the standard deviation is about 11.61%.
solution: $E(R_p) = 0.55(8) + 0.15(8.5) + 0.30(4) = 6.875\%$. The variance, with all covariance terms, is 0.013471, so $\sigma_p = 11.61\%$. Sharpe ratio $= (6.875 - 2.5)/11.61 = 0.38$.

@exam num
prompt: A fictional portfolio and its benchmark have these beginning weights and one-year returns. US equity: portfolio 45%, benchmark 40%, portfolio return 10.35%, benchmark return 9.75%. International equity: portfolio 15%, benchmark 20%, portfolio return 5.32%, benchmark return 5.72%. Bonds: portfolio 40%, benchmark 40%, portfolio return 4.06%, benchmark return 3.96%. Using the Brinson-Fachler form, what is the total allocation effect in percentage points? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.20
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: sum((p - b) * (r - sum(x * y for x, y in zip([0.40, 0.20, 0.40], [9.75, 5.72, 3.96]))) for p, b, r in zip([0.45, 0.15, 0.40], [0.40, 0.20, 0.40], [9.75, 5.72, 3.96]))
hint: First find the benchmark's total return as the weighted sum of its segment returns (6.628%).
hint: Allocation for each segment is $(w_p - w_b)(R_{b,i} - 6.628)$; add the three segments.
solution: The benchmark return is $0.4(9.75) + 0.2(5.72) + 0.4(3.96) = 6.628\%$. US equity: $0.05 \times (9.75 - 6.628) = +0.1561$; international: $-0.05 \times (5.72 - 6.628) = +0.0454$; bonds: 0. Total allocation $= +0.2015$, or 0.20 points. Selection is +0.20 and interaction +0.05, so together they make the 0.45-point active return.

@exam self
prompt: A fictional manager's client, Naomi, has \$900,000, a 15% maximum tolerable one-year loss and a plan to withdraw \$60,000 in three years. The portfolio's annual standard deviation is 10%. In a short section of a client report: (1) compute the one-month 95% parametric VaR of the \$900,000 portfolio, assuming a normal return with zero mean and the square-root-of-time rule to scale the annual standard deviation, (2) explain in plain language what the number does and does not tell the client, and (3) name one conflict of interest the manager should disclose when presenting the report and the Standard that applies.
hint: The monthly standard deviation is $10\%/\sqrt{12}$, and $z_{0.95} = 1.6449$.
hint: A VaR is not a maximum; consider what happens in the worst 5% of months and how the model can be wrong.
solution: (1) Monthly standard deviation $= 10\%/\sqrt{12} = 2.887\%$, so the one-month 95% VaR is $900{,}000 \times 0.02887 \times 1.6449 = \$42{,}735$. (2) Under the model, in about 1 month in 20 the portfolio should lose more than about \$42,700, a bit under 5% of its value; the loss in those months averages more than the VaR, and the VaR is not a worst case. It assumes normal returns and a stable volatility, so fat tails can make real losses larger, and it says nothing about the 15% annual loss limit, which a stress test and the horizon of a year address separately. (3) Example: if the manager recommends funds from its own firm that pay the firm more, VI(A) Disclosure of Conflicts and III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care require disclosure and a documented reasonable basis; other acceptable answers are trade allocation (III(B)), personal trading (VI(B)) or referral fees (VI(C)).
rubric: Computes the monthly standard deviation (2.887%) and a VaR of about \$42,700, stating the 95% confidence, one-month horizon and zero mean.
rubric: Explains that VaR is a threshold exceeded about 1 month in 20, not a maximum, and notes the normality and stability assumptions.
rubric: Names a relevant conflict of interest and the correct Standard, and states the disclosure or remedy.
