@article
An **investment policy statement** (IPS) is the written agreement between an investor and the manager (or the investor's own rulebook) that says what the portfolio is for and how it may be run. Writing it before investing prevents the most expensive mistake in portfolio management: reacting to markets with no plan. This lesson is general education; the cases are fictional.

### Parts of an IPS

An IPS usually contains: a description of the client and the purpose of the portfolio; **governance** (who decides, who is responsible for what); **objectives** (return and risk); **constraints**; the strategic asset allocation with permitted ranges; rebalancing and review procedures; and how performance will be measured against a **benchmark**. Objectives say what the client wants; constraints say what limits how it can be pursued.

### Return objective

A **required return** is the return needed to meet a goal. A **desired return** is what the client would like beyond that. Three conversions come up constantly.

- **Goal-based:** to grow $PV$ to $FV$ in $n$ years with no further contributions, $r = (FV/PV)^{1/n} - 1$. With regular end-of-year contributions, solve $FV = PV(1+r)^n + C\,\frac{(1+r)^n - 1}{r}$ for $r$ numerically.
- **After tax:** if a constant fraction $t$ of each year's return is taxed, $r_{\text{after-tax}} = r(1-t)$.
- **Real:** $r_{\text{real}} = \dfrac{1+r}{1+\pi} - 1$, where $\pi$ is inflation. The approximation $r - \pi$ is close for small numbers but not exact.

### Risk objective

Risk has two sides. **Ability to take risk** is objective: wealth relative to needs, time horizon, income stability, liquidity needs. **Willingness to take risk** is psychological: tolerance for losses, measured by questionnaires and conversation. When the two disagree, the lower one governs the IPS, because a plan the client abandons in a downturn fails. Risk objectives are stated in absolute terms (a maximum tolerable loss in a bad year) or relative terms (tracking error against a benchmark).

### Constraints

A memorable list is **RRTTLLU**: **R**eturn and **R**isk (the objectives) and then the constraints, **T**ime horizon, **T**axes, **L**iquidity, **L**egal and regulatory, and **U**nique circumstances (for example, a concentrated employer-stock holding or values-based exclusions). A client who will need the money in 3 years has a short horizon; a 35-year-old saving for retirement has a multistage one. Liquidity needs are cash withdrawals expected from the portfolio, planned or emergency. Taxes affect both returns and asset location, and the rules change, so check current tax law.

### From the IPS to the portfolio

The IPS does not choose securities. It sets the return that must be earned, the risk that may be taken and the limits, which together point to an asset allocation that the next lessons build.

@example
title: Required return for a retirement goal
problem: Fictional client Dana Whitfield has \$800,000 invested and wants \$1,500,000 in 15 years, with no further contributions. Her portfolio is taxed at a constant 20% of each year's return. Inflation is assumed to be 2.5% a year. Find the required pre-tax return, and show the equivalent after-tax and real returns of a pre-tax 7% portfolio.
step: Required growth rate: $(1{,}500{,}000/800{,}000)^{1/15} - 1 = 1.875^{1/15} - 1 = 4.28\%$ a year, the return needed after tax if the \$1,500,000 is an after-tax target.
step: If the tax is paid on each year's return, the pre-tax return needed is $4.28\%/(1 - 0.20) = 5.35\%$.
step: A pre-tax 7% portfolio earns $7\% \times 0.80 = 5.6\%$ after tax.
step: In real terms, $1.056/1.025 - 1 = 3.02\%$. The shortcut $5.6 - 2.5 = 3.1\%$ is slightly off.
answer: Required pre-tax return 5.35%; a 7% pre-tax portfolio earns 5.6% after tax and 3.02% real.

@example
title: Willingness versus ability
problem: A fictional client, Marcus Adeyemi, has a large portfolio relative to his spending and a long horizon (high ability), but a questionnaire and a conversation show that a 15% drop would make him sell. What should the IPS risk objective reflect?
step: Ability to take risk is high: ample wealth, long horizon, stable income.
step: Willingness is low to moderate: a loss of 15% would trigger a sale, which would lock in the loss and leave him out of the recovery.
step: The lower of the two governs, so the IPS should set a below-average risk objective, for example a maximum tolerable one-year loss at about 10%, and plan to educate the client on the long-run record of risk and return.
answer: The risk objective should follow his lower willingness, not his higher ability.

@mistakes
- Setting the risk objective from ability alone when the client's willingness is lower.
- Using $r - \pi$ as an exact real return, or taxing the real return when taxes apply to the nominal return.
- Writing an IPS that names specific securities; it should set objectives, constraints and allocation ranges instead.
- Forgetting that a required return is a goal, not a forecast: it can exceed what the markets are likely to deliver.

@practice num
prompt: A retiree with a \$1,800,000 portfolio plans to withdraw \$72,000 at the end of the first year. The portfolio's value after the withdrawal should equal its starting value grown by inflation of 2.5%, so that its real value is preserved. Taking the withdrawal as a fraction of the starting value, what nominal return is needed in year 1? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 6.5
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (72000 / 1800000 + 0.025) * 100
hint: The spending rate is the withdrawal divided by the starting value.
hint: Ending value after withdrawal $= V_0(1+r) - W$ must equal $V_0(1+\pi)$, so $r = W/V_0 + \pi$.
solution: The spending rate is $72{,}000/1{,}800{,}000 = 4.0\%$. Requiring $V_0(1+r) - W = V_0(1.025)$ gives $r = 4.0\% + 2.5\% = 6.5\%$. Taxes and fees would raise the required return further.

@practice self
prompt: Fictional client Priya Raman, 38, earns a stable salary, has \$250,000 invested for retirement at 65, saves \$15,000 a year, owns her home, has an emergency fund of 6 months' expenses, and says she would be uncomfortable with a loss of more than about 15% in any year. Write the return objective, risk objective, and the time horizon, liquidity, tax and unique constraints of her IPS in a short structured statement. Do not name any securities. State the convention you use for any numbers.
hint: Separate objectives from constraints, and compare her ability to take risk with her stated willingness.
hint: A required return can come from the goal: with \$250,000, annual end-of-year savings of \$15,000 and 27 years, a target number lets you solve for $r$.
solution: Return objective: grow the portfolio to fund retirement at 65; for an illustrative target of \$2,000,000 in nominal terms in 27 years with end-of-year contributions of \$15,000, the required annual return is about 5.6% before taxes and fees (solve $FV = PV(1+r)^n + C[(1+r)^n - 1]/r$); the desired return is higher if she wants a larger lifestyle. Risk objective: ability is above average (long horizon, stable income, emergency fund), willingness is moderate (maximum tolerable one-year loss about 15%); the IPS adopts the lower, moderate risk. Time horizon: multistage, 27 years to retirement and then a long drawdown phase. Liquidity: low, since the emergency fund covers shocks; no planned withdrawals. Taxes: contributions in tax-advantaged retirement accounts where possible, with asset location to be reviewed against current tax law. Legal and regulatory: retirement account rules on contributions and distributions. Unique: none beyond the 15% loss limit; any values-based exclusions to be added if she requests them. Convention: nominal annual returns, annual compounding, end-of-year contributions.
rubric: Separates objectives (return, risk) from constraints (time horizon, liquidity, taxes, legal and regulatory, unique).
rubric: States a required return with the calculation or the assumptions behind it, and the convention (nominal, annual, contributions timing).
rubric: Sets the risk objective from the lower of ability and willingness, citing the 15% loss limit.
rubric: Names no specific securities and keeps the statement client-specific.

@quiz num
prompt: A fictional portfolio earns a pre-tax return of 7.2% a year. A constant 30% of each year's nominal return is paid in tax, and inflation is 2.5% a year. What is the exact after-tax real return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 2.48
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1 + 0.072 * (1 - 0.30)) / 1.025 - 1) * 100
hint: Tax first: multiply the pre-tax return by $1 - t$.
hint: Then convert the after-tax nominal return to real with $(1+r)/(1+\pi) - 1$.
solution: After tax: $7.2\% \times 0.70 = 5.04\%$. Real: $1.0504/1.025 - 1 = 2.48\%$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following are constraints (rather than objectives) in an investment policy statement?
choice*: A planned withdrawal of \$40,000 next year for a home purchase
choice*: A large holding of the client's employer stock that the client does not wish to sell
choice: A required return of 5% a year after tax
choice: A maximum tolerable loss of 12% in any one year
hint: Objectives are return and risk; constraints limit how they may be pursued.
solution: A planned withdrawal is a liquidity constraint, and the employer-stock holding is a unique circumstance. The required return and the maximum tolerable loss are the return and risk objectives.
