@article
**Alternative investments** are assets outside traditional long-only stocks, bonds and cash: hedge funds, private equity and private debt, real estate, infrastructure, commodities and others. Investors hold them for diversification, higher expected returns, inflation protection or access to different risks. They also bring illiquidity, higher fees, complex valuation and limited data, so an IPS should set limits and the due diligence a manager applies.

### Main types

- **Hedge funds** use flexible mandates: short selling, leverage, derivatives. Strategies include equity long/short, event-driven, relative value and global macro. Fees usually combine a **management fee** (a percentage of assets) and an **incentive fee** (a share of profits), often with a **hurdle rate** (a minimum return before the incentive is earned, hard if the fee applies only above the hurdle, soft if it applies to all profit once the hurdle is cleared) and a **high-water mark** (an incentive fee is earned only on profits above the previous peak NAV). Lock-ups and redemption notice periods limit withdrawals.
- **Private capital** (buyout, venture capital, private debt): investors commit capital, and the manager calls it over several years and returns it at exits. Net returns often follow a **J-curve**: fees and early write-downs make early returns negative, and gains come later. Reported measures: $\text{DPI} = \dfrac{\text{distributions}}{\text{paid-in capital}}$, $\text{RVPI} = \dfrac{\text{remaining NAV}}{\text{paid-in capital}}$ and $\text{TVPI} = \text{DPI} + \text{RVPI}$, plus the since-inception **IRR**, a money-weighted return that depends on the timing of calls and distributions. Under the GIPS 2020 standards, closed-end funds and private capital are typically presented with since-inception IRR rather than time-weighted returns.
- **Real assets**: real estate (direct, appraisal-based, or listed through REITs), infrastructure, farmland and timberland. **Commodities** are usually held through futures. A fully collateralized futures position earns spot price change plus **roll yield** (from rolling contracts, positive in backwardation and negative in contango) plus the interest on the collateral.

### Smoothing and biases

Appraisal-based returns lag the market because appraisers use past sales and smooth changes. A simple model: observed return $r_t^{obs} = (1-\varphi)\,r_t + \varphi\,r_{t-1}^{obs}$, where $r_t$ is the true return and $\varphi$ the smoothing parameter ($0 \le \varphi < 1$). Solve for the true return (**unsmoothing**):
$$r_t = \frac{r_t^{obs} - \varphi\,r_{t-1}^{obs}}{1-\varphi}.$$
If true returns are independent over time, the observed standard deviation understates the true one by the factor $\sqrt{(1-\varphi)/(1+\varphi)}$, so $\sigma_{\text{true}} = \sigma_{\text{obs}}\sqrt{(1+\varphi)/(1-\varphi)}$. Smoothing also lowers measured correlation with public markets. An optimizer fed with smoothed data overweights these assets.

Hedge fund indexes have biases. **Survivorship bias** (funds that closed are dropped, so average returns are overstated), **backfill bias** (a fund added to a database brings its good history with it) and **selection bias** (only funds that choose to report are included) all overstate returns.

### Role in the portfolio

Alternatives can improve diversification, but correlations rise in market stress, leverage can magnify losses, and fees are high, so what matters is **net-of-fee** return and the true risk. Rebalancing is harder because illiquid positions cannot be sized quickly.

@example
title: Unsmoothing appraisal returns
problem: A fictional real estate index reports quarterly returns of 2.0% in one quarter and 3.5% in the next. Assume appraisal smoothing with $\varphi = 0.5$. What is the unsmoothed return for the second quarter, and how much does the observed annual standard deviation of 4.0% change?
step: Unsmoothed return $= (3.5 - 0.5 \times 2.0)/(1 - 0.5) = 2.5/0.5 = 5.0\%$.
step: The volatility factor is $\sqrt{(1 + 0.5)/(1 - 0.5)} = \sqrt{3} = 1.732$.
step: True standard deviation $\approx 4.0\% \times 1.732 = 6.93\%$ (assuming independent true returns).
step: Treated as volatility 4.0%, the asset looks far less risky than it is, and its correlation with equities looks lower too.
answer: Unsmoothed return 5.0%; the true annual standard deviation is about 6.93%.

@example
title: Net return under a 2-and-20 fee
problem: A fictional hedge fund earns a gross return of 15% on its beginning net asset value (NAV) of \$100. It charges a 2% management fee on the beginning NAV and a 20% incentive fee on profit after the management fee, with no hurdle and no high-water mark effect. What is the net return to the investor?
step: Gross profit $= \$15$; management fee $= 2\% \times 100 = \$2$.
step: Profit after the management fee $= 15 - 2 = \$13$; incentive fee $= 20\% \times 13 = \$2.60$.
step: Net profit $= 15 - 2 - 2.60 = \$10.40$, a net return of 10.40% on the \$100.
step: Fees took 4.60 of the 15 points of gross return, about 31%.
answer: Net return 10.40%.

@mistakes
- Computing the incentive fee on gross profit when the fund charges it after the management fee.
- Taking appraisal-based volatility and correlation at face value and concluding the asset is a strong diversifier.
- Treating private-equity IRR as a time-weighted return and comparing it directly with a public index's return.
- Ignoring survivorship, backfill and selection bias when judging a hedge fund index's history.

@practice num
prompt: A fictional real estate fund reports an annual standard deviation of 6.0% from appraisals. Assume smoothing with $\varphi = 0.60$ and independent true returns. What is the estimate of the true annual standard deviation? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 12.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 6.0 * sqrt((1 + 0.6) / (1 - 0.6))
hint: Multiply by $\sqrt{(1+\varphi)/(1-\varphi)}$.
hint: $(1.6)/(0.4) = 4$.
solution: The factor is $\sqrt{4} = 2$, so the true standard deviation is about $6.0\% \times 2 = 12.0\%$. Smoothing hid half of the volatility.

@practice num
prompt: A fictional hedge fund earns a gross return of 14% on its beginning NAV. It charges a 1.5% management fee on beginning NAV and a 20% incentive fee applied only to the return above a 6% hard hurdle, measured after the management fee (so the incentive base is $14 - 1.5 - 6$ points). What is the investor's net return? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 11.2
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 14 - 1.5 - 0.2 * (14 - 1.5 - 6)
hint: The return after the management fee is 12.5%; only the part above 6% earns an incentive fee.
hint: Incentive fee $= 0.20 \times (12.5 - 6.0)$.
solution: After the management fee the return is 12.5%, the excess over the hurdle is 6.5 points, and the incentive fee is $0.20 \times 6.5 = 1.3$ points. Net return $= 12.5 - 1.3 = 11.2\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: An investor puts \$10 million into a fictional private equity fund. There are no other cash flows until the end of year 3, when she receives \$25 million. What is her IRR (annual, since inception)? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 35.72
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((25 / 10) ** (1 / 3) - 1) * 100
hint: With one outflow and one inflow, the IRR solves $10(1+r)^3 = 25$.
hint: $r = 2.5^{1/3} - 1$.
solution: The multiple is $25/10 = 2.5$ and $2.5^{1/3} = 1.3572$, so the IRR is 35.72%. The MOIC is 2.5, which does not reflect how long the money was invested.
