@article
A portfolio earned 6.5% and its benchmark earned 5.8%. Good, but why? Did the manager hold more of the sectors that did well (**allocation**), choose better securities within sectors (**selection**), or both? **Performance attribution** splits the active return into the decisions that caused it. The most widely taught method is the **Brinson model** (Brinson, Hood and Beebower; Brinson and Fachler).

### Setup and the form we use

Divide the portfolio and the benchmark into the same segments $i$ (sectors, asset classes). In one period, let $w_{p,i}$ and $w_{b,i}$ be the beginning-of-period weights, $R_{p,i}$ and $R_{b,i}$ the segment returns in the portfolio and benchmark, and $R_p = \sum w_{p,i}R_{p,i}$, $R_b = \sum w_{b,i}R_{b,i}$. The active return is $R_p - R_b$. The **Brinson-Fachler** effects for segment $i$ are:

- **Allocation**: $A_i = (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})\,(R_{b,i} - R_b)$. Overweighting a segment pays only if the segment beat the benchmark's overall return.
- **Selection**: $S_i = w_{b,i}\,(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})$: the manager's segment return versus the benchmark's, at benchmark weight.
- **Interaction**: $I_i = (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})\,(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})$: the combined effect of holding a different weight and earning a different return in the same segment.

Summed over segments, $\sum_i (A_i + S_i + I_i) = R_p - R_b$ exactly, for a single period with arithmetic returns. The original **Brinson-Hood-Beebower** (BHB) form uses $(w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})\,R_{b,i}$ for allocation. The two forms give the same selection and interaction effects, and the same total allocation, because $\sum_i (w_{p,i} - w_{b,i}) R_b = 0$ when both weights sum to 1; they differ only in the allocation effect of individual segments (BHB credits any overweight of a segment with a positive return, Brinson-Fachler credits it only against the overall benchmark return). Some reports combine selection and interaction into one effect.

### Reading the results

Each effect needs context. A positive allocation effect says the manager overweighted outperforming segments, not that the manager had a good forecast. A negative selection effect in a segment means the manager's holdings there lagged the segment's benchmark. A large interaction term warns that the other two effects are not separable.

### Limits and extensions

Attribution is only as good as its benchmark and segments. Arithmetic effects from different periods do not add up to the geometric active return, so multi-period reports use **linking** or smoothing algorithms. Weights must be beginning-of-period, with buy-and-hold segments; flows and trading within a period blur the results. Bond portfolios are often attributed by duration, curve, sector and spread effects, and equity portfolios by factor exposures (value, size, momentum), rather than by sectors alone.

### Convention

One period, beginning weights, arithmetic returns, Brinson-Fachler allocation, effects reported in percentage points of return.

@example
title: A three-segment Brinson-Fachler attribution
problem: A fictional balanced portfolio and its benchmark have these beginning weights and one-year returns:

| Segment | Portfolio weight | Benchmark weight | Portfolio return | Benchmark return |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equities | 60% | 50% | 9% | 8% |
| Bonds | 30% | 40% | 3% | 4% |
| Cash | 10% | 10% | 2% | 2% |

Find the active return and the allocation, selection and interaction effects (Brinson-Fachler).
step: Returns: $R_p = 0.6(9) + 0.3(3) + 0.1(2) = 6.5\%$; $R_b = 0.5(8) + 0.4(4) + 0.1(2) = 5.8\%$. Active return $= 0.7$ points.
step: Allocation: equities $(0.10)(8 - 5.8) = +0.22$; bonds $(-0.10)(4 - 5.8) = +0.18$; cash $0$. Total $+0.40$. Underweighting bonds helped, because bonds returned less than the benchmark overall.
step: Selection: equities $0.5(9 - 8) = +0.50$; bonds $0.4(3 - 4) = -0.40$; cash $0$. Total $+0.10$.
step: Interaction: equities $(0.10)(1) = +0.10$; bonds $(-0.10)(-1) = +0.10$; cash 0. Total $+0.20$. Check: $0.40 + 0.10 + 0.20 = 0.70$, the active return.
answer: Active return 0.70 points: allocation +0.40, selection +0.10, interaction +0.20.

@example
title: Brinson-Fachler versus BHB allocation
problem: A fictional portfolio holds 60% in equities against a benchmark weight of 50%. The benchmark's equity segment returned 8% and the benchmark as a whole returned 5.8%. Compare the BHB and Brinson-Fachler allocation effects of the equity segment.
step: BHB: $(w_p - w_b)R_{b,i} = 0.10 \times 8 = +0.80$.
step: Brinson-Fachler: $0.10 \times (8 - 5.8) = +0.22$.
step: The difference, $0.10 \times 5.8 = 0.58$, is the overweight times the benchmark's overall return. Summed over all segments these differences cancel (the weight differences sum to zero), so total allocation is the same in both forms.
answer: BHB +0.80 and Brinson-Fachler +0.22 for equities; the totals across all segments match.

@mistakes
- Mixing the BHB and Brinson-Fachler allocation formulas when comparing segment results.
- Using end-of-period instead of beginning-of-period weights.
- Reading allocation as skill without asking what the benchmark return was, or ignoring the interaction term.
- Adding single-period arithmetic effects across several periods and expecting the sum to equal the compound active return.

model:
```python
def brinson_fachler(wp, wb, rp, rb):
    """Total Brinson-Fachler allocation, selection and interaction effects."""
    bench_total = sum(w * r for w, r in zip(wb, rb))
    allocation = sum((p - b) * (r - bench_total)
                     for p, b, r in zip(wp, wb, rb))
    selection = sum(b * (x - r) for b, x, r in zip(wb, rp, rb))
    interaction = sum((p - b) * (x - r)
                      for p, b, x, r in zip(wp, wb, rp, rb))
    return allocation, selection, interaction
```
hint: First compute the benchmark's total return as the weighted sum of its segment returns.
hint: Then add up each effect over the segments with `zip`. The three totals must sum to the portfolio return minus the benchmark return.
solution: The allocation effect compares each segment's benchmark return with the benchmark's total return, so compute that total first. Selection uses the benchmark weights and interaction uses the weight difference times the return difference. Because the three effects sum to the active return, the last test checks the whole function against an independent calculation of $R_p - R_b$.
