@article
The mean-variance investor of Lesson 3 is rational, consistent and unemotional. Real investors are not. **Behavioral finance** studies systematic departures from that model, and a good IPS anticipates them. Institutions add another layer: legal, regulatory and governance constraints that shape what the portfolio may do.

### Behavioral biases

**Cognitive errors** come from faulty reasoning; **emotional biases** come from feelings and are harder to correct. Common ones:

- **Loss aversion** (emotional): losses hurt more than equal gains please. Investors sell after drops, or hold losers hoping to break even.
- **Disposition effect**: selling winners too early and holding losers too long, a form of loss aversion and regret avoidance.
- **Overconfidence**: overestimating skill and the precision of forecasts; leads to excessive trading and under-diversification.
- **Anchoring**: relying on an arbitrary reference such as the purchase price or a past high.
- **Mental accounting**: treating money in separate "buckets" instead of looking at the whole portfolio.
- **Herding**: following the crowd, which can amplify bubbles and crashes.
- **Home bias** and **status quo bias**: favoring familiar domestic assets, and leaving the allocation untouched out of inertia.

**Prospect theory** models choices over gains and losses relative to a reference point. A commonly cited form of the value function is
$$v(x) = \begin{cases} x^{\alpha}, & x \ge 0 \\ -\lambda(-x)^{\beta}, & x < 0 \end{cases}$$
with estimates $\alpha = \beta = 0.88$ and loss-aversion coefficient $\lambda = 2.25$ from one well-known experimental study: a loss counts a little over twice as much as a gain of equal size, and the value function is concave for gains and convex for losses.

How a manager responds: use a written IPS and rebalancing rules (commitment devices), frame risk with the whole portfolio and several horizons, document decisions, and educate clients, working with the bias where it cannot be removed.

### Institutional investors

- **Defined benefit pension plans** owe retirees fixed benefits. The **funded ratio** is $\dfrac{\text{assets}}{\text{PV of liabilities}}$, and the **surplus** is assets minus liabilities. Liabilities behave like long-duration bonds, so falling discount rates raise liabilities. Plans use liability-driven investing (Lesson 6). In the United States, private plans are governed by ERISA, which requires fiduciaries to act with the care of a prudent expert and for the exclusive benefit of participants.
- **Endowments** support an institution in perpetuity, so they weigh **intergenerational equity** (today's spending against tomorrow's). A **smoothing spending rule** blends past spending with a percentage of current market value: $S_t = w\,S_{t-1}(1+\pi) + (1-w)\,s\,M_{t-1}$, where $w$ is the weight on the inflation-adjusted prior spending, $\pi$ inflation, $s$ the target spending rate and $M_{t-1}$ the prior year-end market value. Most US states follow the Uniform Prudent Management of Institutional Funds Act (UPMIFA) for charities.
- **Private foundations** in the United States generally must distribute about 5% of their investment assets each year for charitable purposes (check current tax rules).
- **Insurers** match assets to policy liabilities and face regulatory capital rules; life insurers favor long-dated bonds, property and casualty insurers need liquidity for claims.
- **Sovereign wealth funds** vary: stabilization, savings or development, and their horizons and liquidity needs differ accordingly.

Each is described by the same RRTTLLU outline: a liability-driven pension has a low risk tolerance for surplus, a long horizon with predictable cash flows, and heavy legal constraints; an endowment has high ability to take risk and low liquidity needs but spending commitments.

@example
title: A pension plan after a fall in rates
problem: A fictional defined benefit plan has assets of \$480 million and liabilities with a present value of \$500 million. Assets have a modified duration of 6 and liabilities have a modified duration of 12. Discount rates and asset yields fall by 50 basis points (parallel). Using first-order duration approximations, find the funded ratio before and after.
step: Before: $480/500 = 96.0\%$, a surplus of $-\$20$ million.
step: Assets rise by about $6 \times 0.5\% = 3.0\%$, to $480 \times 1.03 = \$494.4$ million.
step: Liabilities rise by about $12 \times 0.5\% = 6.0\%$, to $500 \times 1.06 = \$530.0$ million.
step: After: $494.4/530.0 = 93.28\%$. A fall in rates made the plan's funding worse because its assets were less interest-rate sensitive than its liabilities, which is why liability-driven investing increases asset duration.
answer: Funded ratio 96.0% before and about 93.3% after.

@example
title: An endowment spending rule
problem: A fictional endowment used a smoothing rule with weight $w = 0.60$ on prior spending grown by inflation, and a target spending rate of 4.5% of the prior year-end market value. Prior-year spending was \$3.0 million, inflation is 2%, and the prior year-end market value was \$70 million. What is this year's spending?
step: Inflation-adjusted prior spending: $3.0 \times 1.02 = \$3.06$ million; weighted: $0.60 \times 3.06 = \$1.836$ million.
step: Market-value component: $0.045 \times 70 = \$3.15$ million; weighted: $0.40 \times 3.15 = \$1.26$ million.
step: Spending $= 1.836 + 1.26 = \$3.096$ million.
step: The rule moves spending part of the way toward the market-based amount: it smooths the budget when markets move and keeps spending tied to the endowment's value.
answer: \$3.096 million.

@mistakes
- Setting a pension plan's risk objective from the asset side alone; the liabilities set the risk, and the funded ratio is what matters.
- Treating behavioral biases as errors to scold; build the IPS, rules and communication so the bias does less damage.
- Forgetting that the endowment's spending rule is part of the return objective: the required return is spending plus inflation plus costs.
- Mixing up the legal standards: ERISA applies to private pensions, UPMIFA to charitable institutions' funds.

@quiz num
prompt: A fictional private foundation must pay out 5% of its beginning-of-year assets to charity each year, expects investment costs of 0.5% of beginning assets, and wants to keep the real value of its assets constant, with inflation of 2.5%. Treating payout and costs as year-end fractions of beginning assets, so that the required return is the sum of the payout, costs and inflation, what nominal return does it need? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 8.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 5 + 0.5 + 2.5
hint: Ending assets after payout and costs must equal beginning assets times $(1 + \pi)$.
hint: $r = 5.0\% + 0.5\% + 2.5\%$.
solution: Requiring $A_0(1+r) - 0.05A_0 - 0.005A_0 = A_0(1.025)$ gives $r = 0.05 + 0.005 + 0.025 = 8.0\%$. This is a goal: whether markets will deliver it is a separate question.
