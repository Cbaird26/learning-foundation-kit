@article
A bond portfolio's returns come from coupon income, price changes when yields move, and the reinvestment of coupons. Fixed income management is largely the management of **interest rate risk** against a benchmark or a liability. Prerequisite reminders: the price of a bond is the present value of its cash flows at the yield; **modified duration** $D_{\text{mod}}$ is the approximate percentage price change for a 1-unit (100%) change in yield, and **convexity** $C$ corrects for the curvature of the price-yield curve.

### Measuring interest rate risk

For a small change in yield $\Delta y$ (a decimal, so 50 basis points is $0.005$), the approximate percentage price change is
$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac12\,C\,(\Delta y)^2,$$
with convexity measured in years squared for annual compounding. The **dollar value of a basis point** (DV01, also called PVBP) is $\text{DV01} = D_{\text{mod}} \times P \times 0.0001$: the dollar loss for a one basis point rise in yield, using duration alone. The duration of a portfolio is approximately the market-value-weighted average of the bonds' durations, which assumes a parallel yield shift (all yields move the same amount). Real yield curves twist as well as shift, so practitioners also track **key rate durations**.

### Liability-driven investing

A defined benefit pension plan or an insurer owes fixed future payments. Two ways to fund them:

- **Cash-flow matching**: buy bonds whose coupons and principal match each liability date. It removes interest rate risk, but it is costly and needs bonds that fit.
- **Immunization** (classical): hold a portfolio such that (1) the present value of the assets at least equals the present value of the liabilities, (2) the Macaulay duration of the assets equals the Macaulay duration of the liabilities, and (3) the assets' convexity (the dispersion of their cash flows) is at least the liabilities'. For a single liability, a parallel shift in yields then moves reinvestment income and bond prices in offsetting directions: after a rise, bond prices fall, but coupons are reinvested at higher rates. Matching duration means the horizon equals the point at which the two effects balance. Immunization needs periodic **rebalancing**, because duration changes with time and yields, and it works for parallel shifts only; it is not a guarantee against other changes in the curve or against default.

### Active strategies

Active bond managers use some of: **duration positioning** (longer than the benchmark if they expect yields to fall), **yield curve positioning** (a **bullet** concentrates on one maturity, a **barbell** holds short and long bonds, a **ladder** spreads holdings evenly across maturities), **credit and sector selection** (taking spread risk for extra yield), and **rolldown** (buying bonds that will age into a lower yield on an upward-sloping curve). Passive managers typically use stratified sampling of the bond index by duration, sector and credit quality, because a bond index holds thousands of bonds that trade infrequently.

### Convention

Yields and returns are annual; coupons are paid annually unless stated; bond prices are per 100 of par; fees are subtracted from annual returns.

@example
title: Immunizing a single liability with a barbell
problem: A fictional pension plan owes \$2,000,000 in 6 years. The discount rate is 5% effective annual. It will fund the liability with two zero-coupon bonds (2 years and 10 years to maturity). What present value of assets is needed, and how much goes into each bond to match duration?
step: Present value of the liability: $2{,}000{,}000/1.05^6 = \$1{,}492{,}431$. The liability's Macaulay duration is 6 years (a single payment).
step: A zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity. With weight $w$ in the 2-year bond, $2w + 10(1-w) = 6$, so $w = 0.5$: \$746,215 in each bond.
step: Convexity (for zeros at yield $y$, $C = T(T+1)/(1+y)^2$): 2-year 5.44, 10-year 99.77, so the barbell has $0.5(5.44) + 0.5(99.77) = 52.6$, above the liability's $6 \times 7/1.05^2 = 38.1$. The convexity condition holds.
step: All three conditions hold, so the plan is immunized against a small parallel shift. It must still be rebalanced as time passes.
answer: Assets of \$1,492,431 split \$746,215 in the 2-year zero and \$746,215 in the 10-year zero.

@example
title: Duration and convexity estimate
problem: A fictional bond portfolio has a modified duration of 7.5 and a convexity of 70 (annual compounding). Estimate the percentage price change if yields rise 50 basis points in a parallel shift.
step: Duration term: $-7.5 \times 0.005 = -3.75\%$.
step: Convexity term: $\tfrac12 \times 70 \times 0.005^2 = +0.0875\%$.
step: Total: $-3.75\% + 0.0875\% = -3.6625\%$, about $-3.66\%$.
step: Using duration alone overstates the loss by about 0.09 points; the gap grows with the square of the yield change.
answer: About -3.66%.

@mistakes
- Using duration alone for large yield changes and ignoring convexity.
- Entering basis points as a percent: 50 basis points is 0.005, not 0.5.
- Treating immunization as one-off: the durations drift and the portfolio must be rebalanced.
- Comparing an active fund's return before fees with an index fund's return after fees.

@practice num
prompt: A fictional bond portfolio has a modified duration of 6.2 and a convexity of 52 (annual compounding). Yields fall 100 basis points in a parallel shift. Using duration and convexity, what is the estimated percentage price change? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 6.46
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (-6.2 * -0.01 + 0.5 * 52 * 0.01**2) * 100
hint: Use $-D\Delta y + \tfrac12 C(\Delta y)^2$ with $\Delta y = -0.01$.
hint: The duration term is $+6.20\%$; the convexity term is $0.5 \times 52 \times 0.0001$.
solution: $6.2\% + 0.26\% = 6.46\%$. The convexity term adds to the gain when yields fall and also reduces the loss when yields rise.

@practice mc
prompt: A plan owes a single payment in 6 years. It holds a bond portfolio whose present value equals the liability's present value and whose Macaulay duration is 6 years. Right after purchase, all yields rise by a small amount. Which statement best describes classical immunization?
choice: The portfolio loses value and cannot meet the liability, because duration measures only losses
choice*: The fall in bond prices is offset by higher reinvestment income, so the portfolio's value at the horizon stays close to the liability
choice: The coupons are fixed, so a change in yields has no effect on the portfolio
choice: The portfolio gains, because reinvestment income always exceeds the price loss
hint: Duration equals the horizon at which price risk and reinvestment risk cancel.
solution: When the asset duration equals the horizon, the capital loss from a small parallel rise in yields is offset, to first order, by the extra interest earned on reinvested cash flows. The offset is approximate: it depends on a small, parallel shift, rebalancing and the convexity condition.

@quiz num
prompt: A fictional bond pays an annual coupon of 4% on a par value of 100 and has 5 years to maturity, priced at par (a yield of 4.0%). Assume that one year later it has 4 years to maturity and a yield of 3.7%, and that the coupon is received at that time. What is the one-year total return from carry (coupon) and rolldown (price change from the lower yield)? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 5.10
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (sum(4 / 1.037 ** t for t in range(1, 5)) + 100 / 1.037 ** 4 + 4 - 100) / 100 * 100
hint: Price the 4-year bond at a yield of 3.7%, then add the coupon received.
hint: Price $= \sum_{t=1}^{4} 4/1.037^t + 100/1.037^4$.
solution: The bond's price at 3.7% is 101.0967, a gain of 1.0967. With the coupon of 4, the return is $(1.0967 + 4)/100 = 5.10\%$ (1.10% from rolldown and 4.00% from the coupon).

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following are required for the classical immunization of a single liability against a parallel yield shift?
choice*: The present value of the assets is at least the present value of the liability
choice*: The Macaulay duration of the assets equals that of the liability
choice: The assets must all be zero-coupon bonds that mature on the liability date
choice: The portfolio never needs to be rebalanced once it is set up
hint: Think of the conditions on value, duration and convexity.
solution: Classical immunization needs enough assets, a matching duration, and asset convexity at least as high as the liability's. It does not require zero-coupon bonds on the liability date, which would be cash-flow matching, and it does need rebalancing because durations drift as time passes and yields change.
