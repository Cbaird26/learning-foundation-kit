@article
An equity portfolio manager answers two questions: what should the portfolio look like compared with a benchmark, and how much should it deviate from it? A **passive** strategy aims to match the benchmark's return at low cost. An **active** strategy deviates from it, hoping to earn more than the benchmark after fees and risk.

### Indexes and how they are built

A **market-capitalization-weighted** index holds each stock in proportion to its market value, usually **free-float adjusted** (only shares available to public investors count):
$$w_i = \frac{\text{price}_i \times \text{shares}_i \times \text{float factor}_i}{\sum_j \text{price}_j \times \text{shares}_j \times \text{float factor}_j}.$$
It is a buy-and-hold portfolio of the whole market: it needs little trading, because weights move with prices. Alternatives include **equal-weighted** indexes (higher turnover, more small-company exposure), **fundamental-weighted** indexes (weights from sales, earnings or dividends) and **factor** or "smart beta" indexes that tilt toward characteristics such as value, size, momentum, quality or low volatility. These tilts are rules-based, cheaper than discretionary management, but still active choices relative to the market.

### Replicating an index

- **Full replication**: hold every stock at its index weight. Best tracking, highest cost for large indexes with illiquid names.
- **Stratified sampling**: divide the index into cells (sector, size, style) and hold a sample in each cell to match the index's characteristics.
- **Optimization**: choose a smaller set of stocks to minimize expected tracking error against the index.

### Measuring active management

**Active share** measures how different the holdings are from the benchmark:
$$\text{Active share} = \tfrac12\sum_i |w_{p,i} - w_{b,i}|,$$
from 0% (the benchmark) to 100% (no overlap). **Tracking error** (active risk) is the standard deviation of active returns $R_p - R_b$. The **information ratio** is
$$\text{IR} = \frac{\bar R_p - \bar R_b}{\text{TE}},$$
mean active return per unit of active risk. Convention used here: active returns are arithmetic differences per period, the standard deviation is the sample standard deviation (divisor $n-1$), and annualizing multiplies the mean by the number of periods per year $k$ and the standard deviation by $\sqrt{k}$. A rule of thumb, the **fundamental law of active management**, says $\text{IR} \approx \text{IC}\sqrt{\text{BR}}$ (skill, measured by the information coefficient, times the square root of the number of independent decisions), before any constraints.

### Can active management pay?

Before costs, the average active dollar must match the market return, because every active holding is matched by the rest of the market holding the other side. After costs the average active manager trails the average passive one by the extra fees and trading costs. That does not mean every manager must lose: skill, differences between managers, inefficient or less-covered markets, and constraints on other investors can create opportunities, but they are hard to identify beforehand, and past performance is a weak guide. The comparison to make is **net-of-fee** alpha against the cost of the passive alternative.

### Core-satellite

Many investors hold a low-cost passive **core** for broad market exposure and add smaller **satellite** active or factor positions where they expect to add value, which controls total active risk and cost. This is education, not advice about any strategy.

@example
title: Weights and active share
problem: A fictional benchmark holds six stocks at weights A 30%, B 25%, C 20%, D 15%, E 5% and F 5%. A fund holds A 10%, B 25%, C 35%, D 15%, E 0% and F 15%. What is the fund's active share?
step: Differences in weight (fund minus benchmark): A $-20$, B $0$, C $+15$, D $0$, E $-5$, F $+10$ percentage points.
step: Sum of absolute differences: $20 + 0 + 15 + 0 + 5 + 10 = 50$.
step: Active share $= \tfrac12 \times 50 = 25\%$: a quarter of the portfolio differs from the benchmark.
answer: Active share 25%.

@example
title: Tracking error and information ratio
problem: A fictional fund's quarterly active returns (fund minus benchmark) over eight quarters were -0.4%, +0.9%, +0.3%, -0.2%, +0.6%, -0.5%, +0.2% and +0.5%. Find the annualized tracking error and information ratio, using the sample standard deviation and $k = 4$.
step: Mean active return per quarter: $1.4/8 = 0.175\%$; annualized, $0.175 \times 4 = 0.70\%$.
step: Sample standard deviation of the eight values: $0.5007\%$ per quarter.
step: Annualized tracking error $= 0.5007 \times \sqrt{4} = 1.00\%$.
step: $\text{IR} = 0.70/1.00 = 0.70$. With only eight observations the estimate is very noisy, so treat it with caution.
answer: Tracking error 1.00%; information ratio 0.70.

@mistakes
- Using the full market capitalization instead of the free-float-adjusted capitalization when computing index weights.
- Forgetting the one-half in active share, which would double it.
- Annualizing tracking error by multiplying by $k$ instead of $\sqrt{k}$ (the mean is multiplied by $k$).
- Comparing an active fund's gross return with an index fund's net return, or ignoring the active fee.

Stock A has 300 million shares at \$20 with a float factor of 1.00; stock B has 100 million shares at \$80 with a float factor of 0.50; stock C has 250 million shares at \$32 with a float factor of 0.75. What is stock B's index weight? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 25.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * 80 * 0.5 / (300 * 20 * 1.0 + 100 * 80 * 0.5 + 250 * 32 * 0.75) * 100

hint: Find the mean, the squared deviations and divide their sum by 7.

@practice num
prompt: A fund's annual active returns (fund minus benchmark) over five years were +1.5%, -0.5%, +2.0%, +0.5% and +1.0%. Using the arithmetic mean and the sample standard deviation (divisor $n-1$) of these annual figures, what is the information ratio? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.94

hint: The mean is 0.90%; the sample standard deviation is about 0.9618%.
solution: The mean active return is $4.5/5 = 0.90\%$, and the sample standard deviation is 0.9618%, so $\text{IR} = 0.90/0.9618 = 0.94$.

Treating fees as direct deductions from annual return, by how many percentage points does the active fund's net return exceed the index fund's net return? unit: %
tol: 0.01

hint: Write each fund's net return relative to the benchmark's gross return.

@quiz mc
prompt: An index provider holds 2,500 stocks, many of which are small and illiquid. A passive manager wants low tracking error with low trading costs. Which approach is typically used?
choice: Full replication of every stock at its index weight, since any alternative creates large tracking error
choice*: Stratified sampling or optimization, holding a smaller set of stocks chosen to match the index's characteristics

solution: For broad indexes with illiquid names, managers hold a sample or an optimized subset that matches sector, size and style exposures, accepting a small tracking error to save trading costs. Equal weighting and the largest-ten approach would deviate far from a cap-weighted index.
