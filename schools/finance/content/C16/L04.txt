@article
The strategic asset allocation is a long-run anchor. Two decisions follow from it. **Tactical asset allocation** (TAA) deliberately departs from the SAA, within IPS ranges, to exploit short-run views about asset-class returns. **Rebalancing** returns the portfolio to its targets after market moves have changed the weights. Tactical decisions are active bets; rebalancing is risk control.

### Tactical allocation

Suppose the SAA is 60% equities and 40% bonds, and the IPS allows equities between 50% and 70%. A manager who expects equities to do better could hold 65/35. The **active weight** in each asset class is the portfolio weight minus the SAA weight (+5% and -5%). If $R_i$ is asset class $i$'s return, the arithmetic contribution of the tactical decisions over a period is
$$\sum_i (w_{p,i} - w_{\text{SAA},i})\,R_i.$$
Tactical allocation adds value only if the views are right more often than their costs, and the evidence on timing skill is mixed, so many IPSs keep the ranges narrow. The tactical contribution is measured against the SAA benchmark, which is also how attribution (Lesson 10) works.

### Why rebalance

Weights drift. If equities rise 20% and bonds 2%, a 60/40 portfolio becomes about 64/36: more risk than the IPS intended. Rebalancing sells the asset class that did well and buys the one that did not. It controls risk and imposes discipline, at the price of trading costs, taxes in taxable accounts, and, in strongly trending markets, possibly lower returns than letting winners run.

### Rebalancing policies

- **Calendar rebalancing**: at fixed dates (quarterly, annually). Simple and predictable, but it may trade when nothing has moved, or wait while risk drifts.
- **Percentage-range (band) rebalancing**: trade when an asset class leaves a band around its target, say 60% $\pm$ 5%. It reacts to the size of the drift. Either rebalance fully to the target, or only back to the band edge, which trades less.
- **Band width** depends on the investor and market. Wider bands suit higher transaction costs, higher risk tolerance, lower volatility of the asset class and higher correlation of the asset class with the rest of the portfolio. Narrower bands suit the opposite.
- **Constant-mix** rebalances to fixed weights, so it sells winners and buys losers (a concave payoff: it does well in choppy, range-bound markets). **Buy-and-hold** never trades (a linear payoff). **Constant-proportion portfolio insurance** (CPPI) buys more of the risky asset as it rises and sells as it falls, giving a convex payoff that protects a floor but does poorly in whipsaw markets.

### CPPI in one rule

CPPI sets a **floor** (the minimum acceptable value, for example 80% of capital). The **cushion** is portfolio value minus the floor. The investor holds a risky exposure of $m \times \text{cushion}$, where $m$ is the **multiplier**, and puts the rest in a safe asset:
$$\text{Risky exposure} = m\,(V - F),\qquad \text{capped at } V \text{ if borrowing is not allowed.}$$
In a sudden gap down, the strategy can fall through the floor, because it cannot trade fast enough.

### Convention

In this lesson returns are for one year, rebalancing occurs at year end, and trading costs and taxes are ignored unless stated.

@example
title: Drift and the rebalancing trade
problem: A fictional portfolio of \$1,000,000 starts at 60% equities and 40% bonds. Over a year equities return +20% and bonds +2%. What are the new weights, and what trades restore 60/40 at year end (no costs)?
step: Values: equities $600{,}000 \times 1.20 = \$720{,}000$; bonds $400{,}000 \times 1.02 = \$408{,}000$; total \$1,128,000.
step: Weights: equities $720{,}000/1{,}128{,}000 = 63.83\%$; bonds 36.17%.
step: Targets: equities $0.60 \times 1{,}128{,}000 = \$676{,}800$; bonds $0.40 \times 1{,}128{,}000 = \$451{,}200$.
step: Trades: sell $720{,}000 - 676{,}800 = \$43{,}200$ of equities and buy \$43{,}200 of bonds.
answer: Weights 63.83/36.17; sell \$43,200 of equities and buy \$43,200 of bonds.

@example
title: A CPPI rule after a fall
problem: A fictional CPPI portfolio starts with \$1,000,000, a floor of \$800,000 and a multiplier of 4. After the initial allocation, the risky asset falls 10% and the safe asset earns nothing. What is the risky holding before and after the fall, and what trade does the rule call for?
step: Initial cushion $= 1{,}000{,}000 - 800{,}000 = \$200{,}000$; risky exposure $= 4 \times 200{,}000 = \$800{,}000$; safe asset \$200,000.
step: After a 10% fall the risky holding is \$720,000 and the portfolio is worth \$920,000.
step: New cushion $= 920{,}000 - 800{,}000 = \$120{,}000$, so the target risky exposure is $4 \times 120{,}000 = \$480{,}000$.
step: Sell $720{,}000 - 480{,}000 = \$240{,}000$ of the risky asset, buying the safe asset. CPPI sells after a fall, unlike constant-mix, which would buy.
answer: Risky holding \$800,000 before and \$720,000 after the fall; the rule sells \$240,000 of the risky asset.

@mistakes
- Rebalancing to target weights computed on the old portfolio value instead of the current total.
- Calling constant-mix a convex strategy; constant-mix is concave, CPPI is convex and buy-and-hold is linear.
- Setting bands without regard to costs and risk tolerance: wide bands save on trading but allow more drift.
- Counting the SAA benchmark's return as tactical value; only the active weights times returns are tactical.

model:
```python
def rebalance_trades(values, targets):
    """Dollar trades (buy positive) that restore the target weights."""
    total = sum(values)
    return [weight * total - value for value, weight in zip(values, targets)]
```
hint: First find the total value of the portfolio.
hint: For each asset the target dollar value is its target weight times the total; the trade is the target minus the current value.
solution: Sum the current values, multiply each target weight by that total to get the target value, and subtract the current value so that buys are positive. Because the targets sum to 1, the trades sum to zero: the portfolio only moves money between asset classes.
