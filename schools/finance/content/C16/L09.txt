@article
Performance measurement answers three questions: what was the return, how much risk was taken to earn it, and was the result due to skill? This lesson covers the first two; Lesson 10 covers the third.

### Time-weighted and money-weighted returns

When the client adds or withdraws money, the portfolio's ending value is no longer a measure of the manager's skill. The **time-weighted return** (TWR) removes the effect of external cash flows by valuing the portfolio just before each flow and linking the sub-period returns geometrically:
$$R_{TW} = \prod_{j=1}^{m}(1 + R_j) - 1, \qquad R_j = \frac{V_j^{\text{before flow}}}{V_{j-1}^{\text{after flow}}} - 1.$$
The **money-weighted return** (MWR) is the internal rate of return of the client's cash flows: the rate that sets the present value of the contributions, withdrawals and ending value to zero. It reflects the timing and size of the flows, which the manager usually does not control, so the TWR is the standard for judging managers, while the MWR measures what the investor earned. Under the **GIPS 2020 standards** (the Global Investment Performance Standards, 2020 edition, from CFA Institute), composites of segregated accounts are presented with time-weighted returns that adjust for external cash flows, while closed-end funds and private capital are presented with a since-inception IRR.

To annualize a multi-year return, use geometric compounding: $R_{\text{ann}} = (1+R_{\text{cum}})^{1/n} - 1$ for $n$ years.

### Risk-adjusted measures

Let $R_p$ be the portfolio's mean return, $R_f$ the risk-free rate, $\sigma_p$ its standard deviation, $\beta_p$ its beta and $R_m$ and $\sigma_m$ the market (benchmark) return and standard deviation. The conventions here are annual figures, with arithmetic mean excess returns.

- **Sharpe ratio**: $\dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}$, excess return per unit of total risk; suited to a whole portfolio.
- **Treynor ratio**: $\dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\beta_p}$, excess return per unit of systematic risk; suited to one component of a diversified portfolio.
- **Jensen's alpha**: $\alpha_p = R_p - [R_f + \beta_p(R_m - R_f)]$, the return above what the CAPM predicts for the portfolio's beta.
- **M-squared** ($M^2$): the return of the portfolio levered or de-levered with the risk-free asset to the market's risk, minus the market return: $M^2 = R_f + \dfrac{R_p - R_f}{\sigma_p}\,\sigma_m - R_m$.
- **Information ratio**: active return divided by tracking error (Lesson 5).

Rankings can differ, because the measures use different risk definitions. Estimates are noisy, so a few years of data cannot prove skill.

### GIPS in brief

The GIPS standards are voluntary, ethical standards for presenting investment performance fairly and consistently. A **firm** claims compliance for the entire firm (not for a single product), and must follow all the requirements. Key ideas: every fee-paying, discretionary portfolio belongs to at least one **composite** that groups portfolios with a similar strategy, so a firm cannot show only its best accounts; returns must be calculated consistently; and the presentation must include required disclosures. Independent **verification** of a firm's compliance is voluntary. The standards are not a law and cannot be claimed by a person, only by a firm that complies.

@example
title: Time-weighted versus money-weighted return
problem: A fictional portfolio starts the year at \$1,000,000. Midway through the year, its value is \$1,060,000 immediately before the client deposits \$400,000. It ends the year at \$1,533,000. Find the time-weighted return, and the money-weighted return (assume the deposit is exactly at mid-year).
step: Sub-period 1: $1{,}060{,}000/1{,}000{,}000 - 1 = 6.00\%$. After the deposit the value is \$1,460,000.
step: Sub-period 2: $1{,}533{,}000/1{,}460{,}000 - 1 = 5.00\%$.
step: $R_{TW} = 1.06 \times 1.05 - 1 = 11.30\%$.
step: MWR: solve $-1{,}000{,}000 - 400{,}000/(1+r)^{0.5} + 1{,}533{,}000/(1+r) = 0$, which gives $r = 11.13\%$. The deposit went in before the weaker second sub-period, so the investor earned slightly less than the manager's TWR.
answer: TWR 11.30%; MWR about 11.13%.

@example
title: Four risk-adjusted measures
problem: Over a year, a fictional portfolio earned 9.0% with a standard deviation of 14% and a beta of 1.1. The risk-free rate was 2.0%, and the market returned 7.5% with a standard deviation of 12%. Find the Sharpe ratio, Treynor ratio, Jensen's alpha and $M^2$ (annual, arithmetic).
step: Sharpe $= (9.0 - 2.0)/14 = 0.50$.
step: Treynor $= (9.0 - 2.0)/1.1 = 6.36$ (percent excess return per unit of beta).
step: Required return by CAPM $= 2.0 + 1.1 \times (7.5 - 2.0) = 8.05\%$, so $\alpha = 9.0 - 8.05 = 0.95\%$.
step: $M^2 = 2.0 + 0.50 \times 12 - 7.5 = 0.50\%$: at the market's risk the portfolio would have earned 0.5 points more than the market.
answer: Sharpe 0.50; Treynor 6.36; Jensen's alpha 0.95%; $M^2$ 0.50%.

@mistakes
- Using the ending value of a portfolio with deposits to compute return, without removing the effect of the flows.
- Averaging sub-period returns instead of linking them geometrically.
- Using the Treynor ratio or Jensen's alpha for a portfolio that is not well diversified, or comparing a Sharpe ratio with a Treynor ratio.
- Treating GIPS as a regulation or claiming compliance for one product or one composite.

tests:

```python
import math
assert math.isclose(twr(1000, [(1100, 200), (1560, 0)]), 0.32, rel_tol=1e-9)
assert math.isclose(twr(500000, [(540000, -100000), (484000, 0)]), 0.188, rel_tol=1e-9)

```
model:
```python
def twr(begin_value, subperiods):
    """Time-weighted return from values just before each external flow."""
    growth = 1.0
    start = begin_value
    for value_before, flow in subperiods:
        growth *= value_before / start
        start = value_before + flow
    return growth - 1.0
```
hint: Keep the value at the start of the current sub-period and a running product of growth factors.
hint: In each pass multiply the growth by `value_before / start`, then reset `start` to `value_before + flow`. Return the product minus 1.
solution: Each sub-period's growth factor is its ending value before the flow divided by its starting value; the flow then changes the starting value of the next sub-period. Multiplying the growth factors links the returns geometrically. A simple (end minus begin minus flows) divided by begin would fail the test where a deposit sits in the account for only part of the year.
