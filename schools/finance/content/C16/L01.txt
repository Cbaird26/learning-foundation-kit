@article
Suppose a client owns one stock that has done wonderfully. Is the client well invested? You cannot say from the stock's return alone. **Portfolio management** judges every holding by what it does to the whole portfolio's return and risk, relative to what the client needs. That judgment is organized as a repeating process.

### The three steps

1. **Planning.** Learn the client's objectives and constraints and write them down in an **investment policy statement** (IPS, Lesson 2). Form **capital market expectations** (forecasts of asset-class returns, risks and correlations). Combine the two into a **strategic asset allocation** (SAA, Lesson 3), the long-run target mix of asset classes.
2. **Execution.** Build the portfolio: choose securities or funds within each asset class, decide how much to deviate from the targets (tactical asset allocation, Lesson 4), and trade at low cost.
3. **Feedback.** Monitor the client's circumstances and the markets, **rebalance** when the portfolio drifts, and measure and attribute performance (Lessons 9 and 10). What you learn updates the plan, so the process is a loop, not a line.

Clients differ. An individual has a lifetime to fund and a tax bill. A defined benefit pension plan has liabilities. An endowment has a spending rule. A bank or insurer has regulators. The same process serves all of them; the IPS is where they differ.

### Returns: the conventions we use

The **holding period return** over one period is
$$R = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + D}{P_0},$$
where $P_0$ and $P_1$ are the beginning and ending prices and $D$ is income received (a dividend or coupon). Unless stated, returns in this course are total returns over a calendar year, in decimals or percents.

For a series of annual returns $R_1,\dots,R_n$, the **arithmetic mean** is $\bar R = \frac1n\sum R_t$, and the **geometric mean** is
$$\bar R_G = \left[\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1+R_t)\right]^{1/n} - 1.$$
The geometric mean is the constant annual return that gives the same ending wealth, so it describes what an investor actually earned. The arithmetic mean is the better estimate of the expected return for one coming period. The arithmetic mean is always at least the geometric mean, with the gap growing with volatility: roughly $\bar R_G \approx \bar R - \sigma^2/2$.

### The portfolio perspective

For weights $w_i$ that sum to 1, the portfolio's expected return is the weighted average $E(R_p) = \sum w_i E(R_i)$. Risk is different. For two assets,
$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2,$$
where $\rho_{12}$ is the correlation. Unless $\rho_{12}=1$, $\sigma_p$ is below the weighted average of the two standard deviations. That gap is the **benefit of diversification**, and it is the reason a portfolio is more than a list of holdings. An asset's contribution to portfolio risk depends on how it co-moves with the rest, not on its stand-alone volatility.

All expected returns and risks below are hypothetical annual figures, not forecasts. For current market data look at sources such as FRED.

@example
title: Arithmetic versus geometric mean
problem: A fictional balanced fund earned +18%, -12%, +9% and +6% in four consecutive calendar years (total returns). Find the arithmetic and geometric mean annual returns, and the ending value of \$100.
step: Arithmetic mean: $(18 - 12 + 9 + 6)/4 = 21/4 = 5.25\%$.
step: Growth factors: $1.18 \times 0.88 = 1.0384$; $\times 1.09 = 1.131856$; $\times 1.06 = 1.199767$. So \$100 becomes \$119.98.
step: Geometric mean: $1.199767^{1/4} - 1 = 1.046584 - 1 = 4.66\%$.
step: The geometric mean is lower, as it must be when returns vary. Compounding 5.25% for four years would give \$122.7, more than the investor really had.
answer: Arithmetic 5.25%, geometric 4.66%, ending value \$119.98.

@example
title: Risk of a two-asset portfolio
problem: A portfolio is 60% in a stock fund (expected return 9%, standard deviation 18%) and 40% in a bond fund (expected return 4%, standard deviation 7%). The correlation is 0.25. Find the portfolio's expected return and standard deviation (annual, hypothetical).
step: Expected return: $0.6 \times 9 + 0.4 \times 4 = 5.4 + 1.6 = 7.0\%$.
step: Variance terms (decimals): $0.6^2 \times 0.18^2 = 0.011664$; $0.4^2 \times 0.07^2 = 0.000784$; covariance term $2 \times 0.6 \times 0.4 \times 0.25 \times 0.18 \times 0.07 = 0.001512$.
step: $\sigma_p^2 = 0.011664 + 0.000784 + 0.001512 = 0.013960$, so $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.013960} = 11.82\%$.
step: The weighted average of the two standard deviations is $0.6 \times 18 + 0.4 \times 7 = 13.6\%$. Diversification removed about 1.8 percentage points of risk.
answer: Expected return 7.0%, standard deviation 11.82%.

@mistakes
- Averaging standard deviations with the weights instead of using the variance formula with the correlation term.
- Reporting the arithmetic mean as the return an investor actually earned over several years; use the geometric mean for that.
- Adding returns instead of compounding growth factors ($1+R$) when linking periods.
- Treating the process as finished once the portfolio is built; monitoring, rebalancing and performance review are part of it.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio is split 50/50 between two funds with standard deviations of 20% and 10% (annual) and a correlation of 0.30. What is the portfolio's standard deviation? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 12.45
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: sqrt(0.5**2 * 0.20**2 + 0.5**2 * 0.10**2 + 2 * 0.5 * 0.5 * 0.30 * 0.20 * 0.10) * 100

hint: The variance is $0.0100 + 0.0025 + 0.0030 = 0.0155$; take the square root.
solution: $\sigma_p^2 = 0.25(0.04) + 0.25(0.01) + 2(0.25)(0.30)(0.20)(0.10) = 0.0155$, so $\sigma_p = 12.45\%$. The weighted average of the standard deviations is 15%, so diversification lowers risk by about 2.5 points.

@quiz num
prompt: A fictional fund returned -20%, +30% and +10% in three consecutive calendar years. What is its geometric mean annual return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.59
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((0.8 * 1.3 * 1.1) ** (1 / 3) - 1) * 100
hint: Convert each return to a growth factor, $1 + R$.
hint: Multiply the three factors, take the cube root, subtract 1.
solution: $0.80 \times 1.30 \times 1.10 = 1.144$, and $1.144^{1/3} - 1 = 4.59\%$. The arithmetic mean, 6.67%, overstates what the investor earned.

@quiz ms
prompt: A portfolio holds two risky assets with positive weights that sum to 1 and a correlation below 1. Which statements are true?
choice*: The portfolio's expected return is the weighted average of the assets' expected returns, whatever the correlation.
choice*: The portfolio's standard deviation is below the weighted average of the two standard deviations.
choice: The portfolio's variance is the weighted average of the two variances.
choice: The benefit of diversification grows as the correlation rises toward 1.
hint: Expected return is linear in the weights; variance includes a covariance term.
solution: Expected return is always a weighted average. Because the correlation is below 1, the covariance term is smaller than it would be at 1, so the standard deviation falls below the weighted average. Variance is not a weighted average of variances, because of the covariance term, and the diversification benefit shrinks to zero as the correlation approaches 1.
