@article
A portfolio manager holds other people's money and makes decisions that others cannot easily check. That is why portfolio management is built on **trust**, and why the law and professional codes require managers to put clients first. This lesson is general education, not legal advice; rules change, so check current law.

### Fiduciary duty

A **fiduciary** must act in the client's interest. Two core duties are:

- **Loyalty**: put the client's interest ahead of your own and your firm's, avoid or fully disclose and manage conflicts, and obtain informed consent where conflicts remain.
- **Prudence and care**: act with the skill, care and diligence of a knowledgeable professional, with a reasonable basis for recommendations, and consider costs, the client's circumstances and the portfolio as a whole.

In the United States, **registered investment advisers** owe their clients a federal fiduciary duty under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940, as the SEC described in a 2019 interpretation (a duty of care, including advice in the client's best interest and best execution, and a duty of loyalty). **Broker-dealers** follow Regulation Best Interest (since June 30, 2020) and deliver Form CRS, a best-interest standard for recommendations to retail customers. Fiduciaries of private retirement plans are held to the prudent-expert standard of ERISA. Standards and agencies' rules evolve, so check the current rules.

### The CFA Institute Code and Standards

The Code of Ethics and Standards of Professional Conduct (updated effective January 1, 2024; Standards of Practice Handbook, 12th edition) bind members and candidates. Those most often tested in portfolio management, in plain words:

- **III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care**: act for clients' benefit, putting their interests before the employer's and your own; this includes using client brokerage for client benefit.
- **III(B) Fair Dealing**: treat clients fairly and objectively when making recommendations or taking action, for example by allocating trades by a documented, pre-set policy.
- **III(C) Suitability**: make a reasonable inquiry into the client's objectives and constraints, write them down (the IPS), judge suitability in the context of the total portfolio, and update at least annually.
- **III(D) Performance Presentation**: present performance fairly, accurately and completely; GIPS compliance is a way to do this.
- **V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis**, **V(B) Communication with Clients**.
- **VI(A) Disclosure of Conflicts**, **VI(B) Priority of Transactions** (clients first, then the employer, then personal trades) and **VI(C) Referral Fees**.
- **I(B) Independence and Objectivity**, **IV(B) Additional Compensation Arrangements**.

### Common conflicts and remedies

| Conflict | Why it harms clients | Typical remedy |
|---|---|---|
| Trade allocation between accounts (including accounts with performance fees) | Hot IPOs or limited trades go to the favored accounts | A written allocation policy, allocation before the order is placed, pro rata fills, monitoring |
| Personal trading | Front-running or competing with clients | Pre-clearance, blackout periods, holdings reports, clients' orders first (VI(B)) |
| Proprietary products or revenue sharing | The manager picks funds that pay the firm | Disclosure (VI(A)), documented reasonable basis, comparison with alternatives, consent |
| Soft dollars | Client commissions pay for research that benefits the manager | Use only for research and brokerage that benefits clients; disclose |
| Referral fees | The recommendation reflects the fee | Disclose to clients and prospects before they commit (VI(C)) |
| Performance fees | Incentive to take excess risk | Limits in the IPS, risk monitoring, high-water marks |

"Soft dollars" refers to client commissions that pay for research or services: US law, under Section 28(e) of the Securities Exchange Act of 1934, provides a safe harbor for paying more than the lowest commission for eligible research and brokerage if the manager determines in good faith the amount is reasonable. A remedy is only as strong as the controls behind it: a policy that is not tested and enforced is not a remedy.

### Quantifying a conflict

A fee difference compounds. If two funds earn the same gross return and differ only in expenses, the client who is steered to the costlier one loses the difference in terminal value; the example below shows how large it is.

@example
title: A trade allocation conflict
problem: A fictional portfolio manager receives a limited allocation of a hot new share issue that will probably rise on the first day. Her firm runs both a performance-fee fund and a group of ordinary client accounts. She puts the whole allocation into the performance-fee fund. Which Standards are involved, and what remedy should the firm use?
step: The action favors the account that pays the firm more, at the expense of the other clients: a breach of III(B) Fair Dealing and of III(A) Loyalty, Prudence, and Care.
step: The firm should have a written allocation policy, with the allocation decided before the order is placed (pro rata by account size, for example), not after seeing which shares rose.
step: Remedies: disclose the policy (VI(A)), review allocations regularly, and compensate affected clients if the policy was breached.
answer: III(B) and III(A) are breached; the remedy is a pre-trade, pro rata allocation policy with monitoring and disclosure.

@example
title: The cost of a conflict
problem: A fictional adviser steers a client's \$500,000 into a fund with an annual expense ratio of 0.90% because it pays the adviser a fee, instead of an otherwise identical fund with an expense ratio of 0.40%. Both funds earn a gross 6.0% a year. Fees reduce the annual return (compounded annually). How much less does the client have after 10 years?
step: Net returns: $6.0 - 0.9 = 5.1\%$ and $6.0 - 0.4 = 5.6\%$.
step: After 10 years, $500{,}000 \times 1.056^{10} = \$862{,}202$ and $500{,}000 \times 1.051^{10} = \$822{,}237$, so the client has \$39,965 less.
step: Remedy: disclose the compensation (VI(A)), document why the costlier fund is better for this client, or choose the cheaper one.
answer: About \$39,965 less after 10 years.

@mistakes
- Treating disclosure as a cure for every conflict: some conflicts must be avoided or removed, not only disclosed.
- Confusing fair dealing with equal treatment: clients may be treated differently when the differences are disclosed and based on their circumstances, but not at one client's expense.
- Forgetting that a firm's policy on personal trading must put client orders first and has to be monitored.
- Treating a fiduciary and a broker-dealer standard as the same: the current rules differ, so check them.

@practice mc
prompt: A portfolio manager receives a limited allocation of a profitable new share issue and, after seeing the first-day price jump, assigns most of it to accounts owned by the firm's owner and his relatives, leaving other clients with little. Which Standard is most directly violated?
choice*: III(B) Fair Dealing
choice: II(A) Material Nonpublic Information
choice: V(C) Record Retention
choice: I(E) Competence
hint: The issue is how limited investment opportunities are divided among clients.
solution: Allocating a limited, profitable opportunity after the fact in favor of insiders is unfair to other clients: III(B) Fair Dealing, together with III(A) and VI(A). The correct approach is a documented, pre-trade allocation policy.

@practice mc
prompt: A portfolio manager buys 20,000 shares of a stock for his own account the day before he places a large buy order for the same stock for his clients, and sells his shares the next day at a higher price. Which Standard is most directly violated?
choice: V(A) Diligence and Reasonable Basis
choice: III(D) Performance Presentation
choice*: VI(B) Priority of Transactions
choice: IV(A) Loyalty to employer only
hint: Whose orders should be executed first?
solution: Client transactions have priority over the manager's personal ones. Trading ahead of clients' orders (front-running) breaches VI(B) Priority of Transactions and also III(A), because the manager used knowledge of the clients' orders for his own benefit.

@practice ms
prompt: Which actions are consistent with the Code and Standards?
choice*: Disclosing a referral fee arrangement to clients before they commit to the adviser's services
choice*: Using client brokerage commissions only to buy research and execution that benefits the clients
choice: Allocating oversubscribed trades first to the accounts that pay performance fees
choice: Recommending the firm's own fund because it pays the firm more, without telling the client
hint: Look for actions that put clients first and disclose conflicts.
solution: Referral fees must be disclosed (VI(C)), and client commissions must be used for the clients' benefit (III(A)). Favoring performance-fee accounts breaches fair dealing, and recommending a fund for the firm's gain without disclosure breaches VI(A) and the duty of loyalty.

@practice self
prompt: A fictional investment firm runs a performance-fee fund and ordinary client accounts, pays its portfolio managers a bonus on firm revenue, sells its own mutual funds, and allows managers to trade for their own accounts. Explain three conflicts of interest that arise, the Standard each implicates, and a remedy for each.
hint: Choose three of: trade allocation, proprietary products, personal trading, performance-based incentives, soft dollars, referral fees.
hint: For each conflict, say who could be harmed, which Standard applies and what policy or control prevents the harm.
solution: (1) Trade allocation between the performance-fee fund and other accounts: the manager might give the best trades to the account that pays more. Standards: III(B) Fair Dealing and III(A). Remedy: a written policy, allocation decided before the trade is placed (pro rata), regular review of allocations by compliance, and disclosure. (2) Proprietary mutual funds: managers may recommend the firm's own funds even when better or cheaper alternatives exist. Standards: VI(A) Disclosure of Conflicts, III(A) and V(A). Remedy: disclose the relationship and compensation, document the reasonable basis and comparison with alternatives, and make compensation neutral across products. (3) Personal trading: managers could trade ahead of clients or use client information. Standard: VI(B) Priority of Transactions. Remedy: pre-clearance, blackout periods around client trades, holdings and transactions reports, and monitoring. Disclosure alone is not enough for the most serious conflicts, which must be avoided.
rubric: Identifies three distinct conflicts from the case and states who could be harmed in each.
rubric: Names the relevant Standard for each (for example III(B), VI(A), VI(B), III(A)).
rubric: Proposes a specific, enforceable remedy for each (policy, control, monitoring, disclosure), not only a statement to be careful.
rubric: Recognizes that some conflicts must be avoided or managed, not just disclosed.
