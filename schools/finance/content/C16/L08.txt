@article
How bad could a bad day be? **Value at risk** (VaR) answers with a number and a probability: a one-day 95% VaR of \$200,000 means that, under the assumptions of the model, the portfolio should lose more than \$200,000 on about 5% of days (1 day in 20), and less on the other 95%. A VaR is always quoted with a **confidence level**, a **horizon** and a method. It is a minimum loss in the worst $(1-c)$ of outcomes, not the maximum loss.

### Conventions in this lesson

Unless stated: horizon one day; returns are arithmetic percentage changes in value; the portfolio value is held fixed; the mean return is zero (a common simplification for short horizons); losses are positive numbers; the confidence level is $c$ (for example 95% or 99%).

### Method 1: parametric (variance-covariance)

If the portfolio's return is normal with standard deviation $\sigma$ per day, then
$$\text{VaR}_c = V\,\sigma\,z_c,$$
where $V$ is the value and $z_c$ is the standard normal quantile: $z_{0.95} = 1.6449$, $z_{0.99} = 2.3263$. With a mean $\mu$ per day, $\text{VaR} = V(z_c\sigma - \mu)$. For a horizon of $T$ days, assuming independent daily returns, the **square-root-of-time rule** gives $\text{VaR}_T = \text{VaR}_1\sqrt{T}$ (with a zero mean). For a multi-asset portfolio, $\sigma$ comes from the covariance matrix (Lesson 3). The method is fast but assumes normality: real returns have fat tails, so it can understate extreme losses, and it works poorly for options and nonlinear portfolios.

### Method 2: historical simulation

Take the portfolio's actual (or reconstructed) returns over the past $n$ days, sort the losses, and read the tail. Convention here: with $n$ observations and confidence $c$, let $k = (1-c)n$; the VaR is the $k$-th worst loss. With 20 daily returns and $c = 90\%$, $k = 2$, the VaR is the second-worst loss. (Other conventions interpolate between observations.) No distribution is assumed, but the answer depends on the sample window: it only knows crises the window contains.

### Method 3: Monte Carlo simulation

Specify a model for the risk factors (distributions, correlations), simulate thousands of scenarios, revalue the portfolio in each and read the loss percentile. It handles options, fat tails and nonlinearity, at the price of computation and model risk. Because the draws are random, two runs give slightly different results; more scenarios reduce the sampling error.

### Expected shortfall

**Expected shortfall** (ES, also conditional VaR) is the average loss in the worst $(1-c)$ of cases. For normal returns with zero mean,
$$\text{ES}_c = V\,\sigma\,\frac{\varphi(z_c)}{1-c},$$
where $\varphi$ is the standard normal density. At 95%, $\varphi(1.6449)/0.05 = 2.0627$, so ES is $2.0627\,V\sigma$ against VaR's $1.6449\,V\sigma$. ES says what happens inside the tail, which VaR ignores, and unlike VaR it is always subadditive (diversification never raises it).

### Stress tests and scenario analysis

A **stress test** revalues the portfolio under extreme but plausible changes in risk factors. A **historical scenario** replays a past episode (for example, the 1987 crash or the 2007 to 2009 financial crisis); a **hypothetical scenario** invents a shock (equities fall 30% and yields rise 150 basis points); a **reverse stress test** asks what combination of shocks would cause a given loss. For bonds, a first-order loss is $-D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y\,V$. Stress tests need no probability, which is their strength and their limit.

### Limits and backtesting

VaR ignores losses beyond its threshold, depends on its inputs, and can be gamed. **Backtesting** counts the days on which the loss exceeded the VaR: at 99% over 250 days, about 2.5 exceptions are expected, and many more means the model understates risk. Use VaR together with ES, stress tests and limits.

@example
title: Parametric VaR and expected shortfall
problem: A fictional portfolio is worth \$10,000,000, and its daily return has a standard deviation of 1.2%. Assuming normal returns with zero mean, find the one-day 95% VaR, the ten-day 95% VaR and the one-day 95% expected shortfall.
step: One-day VaR $= 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.012 \times 1.6449 = \$197{,}382$.
step: Ten-day VaR, with the square-root-of-time rule: $197{,}382 \times \sqrt{10} = \$624{,}178$.
step: One-day ES $= 10{,}000{,}000 \times 0.012 \times 2.0627 = \$247{,}526$ (using the exact normal density; rounding $2.0627$ gives about the same).
step: On the worst 1 day in 20, the loss would average about \$247,500, about 25% more than the VaR threshold.
answer: One-day VaR \$197,382; ten-day VaR \$624,178; one-day ES about \$247,526 (all 95%, normal, zero mean).

problem: A fictional \$5,000,000 portfolio had these 20 daily returns (%): 0.8, -1.2, 0.3, 1.5, -0.4, -2.1, 0.9, 0.2, -0.7, 1.1, -1.6, 0.5, 0.0, 1.8, -0.3, -2.6, 0.6, 1.0, -0.9, 0.4. Find the one-day 90% VaR using the convention $k = (1-c)n$ with the $k$-th worst loss.

@mistakes
- Calling VaR the maximum loss, or reading a 95% VaR as a loss that will happen only once in a lifetime.
- Quoting a VaR without saying the confidence level and horizon.
- Scaling a one-day VaR by $T$ instead of $\sqrt{T}$ (the square-root rule assumes independent, zero-mean returns).
- Using the 5% quantile for a 99% VaR, or mixing up the confidence level with the tail probability.

model:
```python
import math
from statistics import NormalDist


def parametric_var(value, daily_sigma, confidence, days=1, daily_mean=0.0):
    """Parametric (normal) value at risk, as a positive dollar loss."""
    z = NormalDist().inv_cdf(confidence)
    return value * (z * daily_sigma * math.sqrt(days) - daily_mean * days)
```
hint: Get the normal quantile $z$ for the confidence level with `NormalDist().inv_cdf(confidence)`.
hint: Scale the standard deviation by the square root of `days` and the mean by `days`, then subtract the scaled mean inside the parentheses.
solution: The quantile $z$ turns the confidence level into a number of standard deviations. Volatility scales with the square root of the horizon but the mean scales with the horizon itself, so the mean enters as a separate term. A positive mean reduces the VaR, and a zero volatility with a zero mean gives zero.
