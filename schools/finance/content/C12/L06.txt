@article
Cross-sectional data have no natural order: shuffling the firms changes nothing. **Time series** data are ordered, and order matters: today's sales are related to yesterday's, and a growing series looks different from a flat one. Three features dominate the first look at a series: a **trend** (a long-run drift), **seasonality** (a pattern that repeats every year), and **autocorrelation** (dependence between neighboring observations).

### Trends

A **linear trend** model regresses the series on a time index $t = 1, 2, \dots, n$:

$$y_t = a + b\,t + \varepsilon_t.$$

The slope $b$ is the average change per period. For a series that grows by a roughly constant *percentage*, use the **log-linear trend**:

$$\ln y_t = a + g\,t + \varepsilon_t.$$

Then $g$ is the continuously compounded growth rate per period, and the exact growth rate per period is $e^{g} - 1$ (for example $g = 0.012$ per quarter is $1.207\%$ a quarter, or $e^{4 \times 0.012} - 1 = 4.92\%$ a year). Revenue, prices and index levels usually fit a log trend better than a straight line; a straight line fitted to exponential growth leaves residuals that are positive at both ends and negative in the middle.

To **forecast** $h$ periods beyond a sample of $n$ observations, evaluate the fitted trend at $t = n + h$. A trend forecast assumes the past drift continues, which becomes less credible as $h$ grows.

### Seasonality

For quarterly data, add three quarterly dummies $Q2, Q3, Q4$ (the first quarter is the base, avoiding the dummy-variable trap of week 5):

$$y_t = a + b\,t + s_2 Q2_t + s_3 Q3_t + s_4 Q4_t + \varepsilon_t.$$

Each $s_j$ is the average difference between quarter $j$ and the first quarter at the same point on the trend. An alternative is **seasonal differencing**, $y_t - y_{t-4}$ for quarterly data, which removes a stable seasonal pattern and, to a large extent, a trend. A **moving average** over one full seasonal cycle smooths a series without needing a model.

### Autocorrelation of the errors

Regression errors in time series are often correlated: $\text{Cov}(\varepsilon_t, \varepsilon_{t-1}) \ne 0$. If the regressors are strictly exogenous, OLS is still unbiased, but the usual standard errors are wrong (usually too small when errors are positively correlated), so the $t$ statistics overstate significance. The classic diagnostic is the **Durbin–Watson statistic** of the residuals $e_t$:

$$DW = \frac{\sum_{t=2}^{n}(e_t - e_{t-1})^2}{\sum_{t=1}^{n} e_t^2} \approx 2(1 - \hat\rho),$$

where $\hat\rho$ is the first-order autocorrelation of the residuals. $DW$ lies between 0 and 4: values near 2 mean no first-order autocorrelation, values toward 0 mean positive autocorrelation, and values toward 4 negative. The test compares $DW$ with a lower and an upper bound $d_L, d_U$ from tables: below $d_L$ reject $H_0$ (no autocorrelation) in favor of positive autocorrelation, above $d_U$ do not reject, in between the test is inconclusive. For a model with an intercept and one regressor at the 5% level:

| $n$ | 15 | 20 | 40 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $d_L$ | 1.08 | 1.20 | 1.44 | 1.65 |
| $d_U$ | 1.36 | 1.41 | 1.54 | 1.69 |

The test assumes an intercept, strictly exogenous regressors (it is invalid when a lagged dependent variable is a regressor), and AR(1) errors; its exact version needs normal errors. When it signals a problem, keep OLS and use **Newey–West** (HAC) standard errors, which are robust to both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation up to a chosen lag, or model the dynamics directly (week 7).

### A warning

A series with a trend regressed on another series with a trend usually shows a high $R^2$ and a "significant" slope even when the two are unrelated. Week 8 explains this **spurious regression** and what to do about it.

@example
title: Fitting and checking a linear trend
problem: Quarterly sales of Bellwether Foods (in \$ millions) over eight quarters are $100, 104, 109, 115, 122, 131, 140, 151$. Fit a linear trend, compute $DW$ and a log-linear trend growth rate.
step: With $t = 1, \dots, 8$: $\bar t = 4.5$, $\bar y = 121.5$, $S_{tt} = 42$ and $S_{ty} = \sum t y - n \bar t \bar y = 4679 - 4374 = 305$. So $\hat b = 305/42 = 7.262$ and $\hat a = 121.5 - 7.262 \times 4.5 = 88.82$: sales rise about \$7.26 million per quarter on average.
step: The residuals are $(3.92, 0.65, -1.61, -2.87, -3.13, -1.39, 0.35, 4.08)$, positive at both ends and negative in the middle: the line misses the curvature of the series.
step: $\sum e_t^2 = 55.12$ and $\sum (e_t - e_{t-1})^2 = 37.43$, so $DW = 37.43/55.12 = 0.68$, far below 2. The neighboring residuals are strongly positively related, so the usual standard errors of the trend regression would be misleading.
step: Regressing $\ln y_t$ on $t$ gives a slope of $\hat g = 0.0593$, an exact growth rate of $e^{0.0593} - 1 = 6.11\%$ per quarter. This matches the pattern of accelerating dollar increases much better than one straight line, though the residuals are still not perfectly clean in so short a sample.
answer: $\hat y = 88.82 + 7.262\,t$ with $DW = 0.68$; log-trend growth of $6.11\%$ per quarter.

@example
title: Forecasting with trend and seasonal dummies
problem: A model for quarterly sales (in \$ millions) estimated on twelve quarters, $t = 1, \dots, 12$ (the first quarter of year 1 is $t = 1$), is $\hat y_t = 100 + 2.0\,t + 8\,Q2_t - 5\,Q3_t + 3\,Q4_t$. Forecast sales for $t = 14$ and $t = 15$ and interpret the dummies.
step: Quarter 14 is the second quarter of year 4, so $Q2 = 1$: $\hat y_{14} = 100 + 2.0(14) + 8 = 136$.
step: Quarter 15 is the third quarter of year 4, so $Q3 = 1$: $\hat y_{15} = 100 + 30 - 5 = 125$.
step: Interpretation: relative to the first quarter, the second quarter averages \$8 million more, the third \$5 million less and the fourth \$3 million more, at the same value of the trend. The trend adds \$2 million per quarter.
step: Forecasts with a fitted trend and stable seasonal pattern assume both continue; seasonal dummies cannot capture a pattern that changes over time.
answer: $\hat y_{14} = 136$ and $\hat y_{15} = 125$ (millions of dollars).

@mistakes
- Fitting a straight-line trend to a series that grows at a constant percentage rate, then reading the residual pattern as noise.
- Using the Durbin–Watson test when a lagged dependent variable is among the regressors, where it is biased toward 2.
- Reading a high $R^2$ in a regression of one trending series on another as evidence of a relationship.
- Treating the log-trend slope $g$ as the growth rate without converting it with $e^{g} - 1$ when $g$ is not small.

model:
```python
def trend_forecast(y, h):
    """Forecast h periods ahead from a linear time trend fitted by OLS."""
    n = len(y)
    t = list(range(1, n + 1))
    t_bar = sum(t) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    s_tt = sum((ti - t_bar) ** 2 for ti in t)
    b = sum((ti - t_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for ti, yi in zip(t, y)) / s_tt
    a = y_bar - b * t_bar
    return a + b * (n + h)
```
hint: Build the time index with `range(1, n + 1)` and run a simple regression of `y` on it.
hint: The forecast evaluates the fitted line at `n + h`, not at `h`.
solution: Fit $\hat b = S_{ty}/S_{tt}$ and $\hat a = \bar y - \hat b \bar t$ with $t = 1, \dots, n$, then return $\hat a + \hat b(n + h)$. A common error is to start the index at 0 or to evaluate at `h`; in the first test $\hat a = 7.9$ and $\hat b = 1.9$, so the forecast for $t = 6$ is $7.9 + 1.9 \times 6 = 19.3$.

model:
```python
def durbin_watson(resid):
    """Durbin-Watson statistic of a residual series."""
    num = sum((resid[i] - resid[i - 1]) ** 2 for i in range(1, len(resid)))
    den = sum(e ** 2 for e in resid)
    return num / den
```
hint: The numerator uses consecutive pairs: start the loop at index 1.
hint: The denominator sums all the squared residuals, including the first.
solution: Sum $(e_t - e_{t-1})^2$ for $t = 2, \dots, n$ and divide by $\sum e_t^2$. Alternating residuals give 3 (strong negative autocorrelation), constant residuals give 0 (the extreme of positive autocorrelation).
