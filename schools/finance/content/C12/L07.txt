@article
Yesterday's value of a stock's return tells you almost nothing about today's, but yesterday's value of an interest-rate spread, a volatility index or a firm's profit margin tells you a good deal. **Autoregressive** and **moving-average** models describe this memory in a stationary series with a few parameters, and they are the standard tools for forecasting.

### Stationarity and white noise

A series $y_t$ is **weakly (covariance) stationary** if its mean $E[y_t] = \mu$ and variance are constant over time, and the covariance $\gamma_k = \text{Cov}(y_t, y_{t-k})$ depends only on the lag $k$, not on $t$. **White noise** $\varepsilon_t$ is the simplest case: mean zero, constant variance $\sigma^2$, and no correlation across time. The **autocorrelation function** (ACF) is $\rho_k = \gamma_k/\gamma_0$. Its sample version for a series of length $T$ is

$$\hat\rho_k = \frac{\sum_{t=k+1}^{T}(y_t - \bar y)(y_{t-k} - \bar y)}{\sum_{t=1}^{T}(y_t - \bar y)^2}.$$

If the series is white noise, $\hat\rho_k$ is approximately normal with standard deviation $1/\sqrt T$, so values outside $\pm 1.96/\sqrt T$ are significant at about 5%. The **partial autocorrelation function** (PACF) at lag $k$ is the correlation between $y_t$ and $y_{t-k}$ after removing the effect of the lags in between; it is the coefficient on $y_{t-k}$ in a regression on $k$ lags.

### The AR(1) model

$$y_t = c + \phi\, y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t, \qquad \varepsilon_t \sim \text{white noise}(0, \sigma^2).$$

It is stationary when $|\phi| < 1$, and then

$$\mu = \frac{c}{1 - \phi}, \qquad \gamma_0 = \frac{\sigma^2}{1 - \phi^2}, \qquad \rho_k = \phi^k.$$

The ACF decays geometrically (and alternates in sign if $\phi < 0$), while the PACF is $\phi$ at lag 1 and zero afterward. The **forecast** $h$ steps ahead from the last observation $y_T$ is

$$\hat y_{T+h} = \mu + \phi^{h}(y_T - \mu),$$

equivalently the one-step forecast $c + \phi y_T$ applied repeatedly. It moves from the last value toward the unconditional mean $\mu$ at the rate $\phi$ per step: **mean reversion**. A larger $|\phi|$ means slower reversion. For $\phi = 1$ the series is a random walk and is not stationary (week 8). An AR($p$) model uses $p$ lags, $y_t = c + \phi_1 y_{t-1} + \dots + \phi_p y_{t-p} + \varepsilon_t$; its PACF cuts off after lag $p$. OLS applied to the regression of $y_t$ on its lags is consistent, though biased in small samples, and is what week 8 uses.

### The MA(1) model

$$y_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t + \theta\,\varepsilon_{t-1}.$$

It is always stationary, with $\gamma_0 = \sigma^2(1 + \theta^2)$ and

$$\rho_1 = \frac{\theta}{1 + \theta^2}, \qquad \rho_k = 0 \text{ for } k \ge 2.$$

The ACF *cuts off* after one lag, and $|\rho_1| \le 0.5$ whatever $\theta$ is. The usual convention is **invertibility**, $|\theta| < 1$, which makes the representation unique. An MA($q$) has an ACF that cuts off after lag $q$.

### ARMA models and the Box–Jenkins approach

An **ARMA($p, q$)** model combines both: $y_t = c + \sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_i y_{t-i} + \varepsilon_t + \sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_j \varepsilon_{t-j}$. The Box–Jenkins procedure is: (1) make the series stationary; (2) inspect the ACF and PACF to suggest orders (a PACF that cuts off at $p$ with a decaying ACF suggests AR($p$); an ACF that cuts off at $q$ with a decaying PACF suggests MA($q$); both decaying suggests ARMA); (3) estimate; (4) compare candidates by AIC or BIC; and (5) check that the residuals look like white noise, for instance with the **Ljung–Box** statistic

$$Q = T(T+2)\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\hat\rho_k^{\,2}}{T-k},$$

compared with $\chi^2$ with $m - p - q$ degrees of freedom (5% critical values: 3.841 for 1, 5.991 for 2, 7.815 for 3, 9.488 for 4). The test assumes the residuals are the errors of the fitted model with independent shocks.

### Returns versus other series

Returns on liquid assets are close to white noise, which is consistent with weak-form market efficiency, so AR and MA models rarely add much for returns themselves. They matter for series with genuine persistence: yields, spreads, valuation ratios, and the variance of returns (week 9). A statistically significant autocorrelation is not necessarily a profitable strategy once costs are counted.

@example
title: A sample autocorrelation by hand
problem: A yield-spread series (in percentage points) over eight months is $2.1, 2.8, 3.4, 2.9, 3.6, 4.2, 3.5, 3.9$. Compute $\hat\rho_1$ and decide whether it is significant at about 5%.
step: The mean is $\bar y = 3.3$. The deviations are $(-1.2, -0.5, 0.1, -0.4, 0.3, 0.9, 0.2, 0.6)$ and the sum of squared deviations is $3.16$.
step: The lag-1 cross-products $(y_t - \bar y)(y_{t-1} - \bar y)$ for $t = 2, \dots, 8$ are $0.60, -0.05, -0.04, -0.12, 0.27, 0.18, 0.12$, which sum to $0.96$.
step: $\hat\rho_1 = 0.96/3.16 = 0.304$.
step: The approximate 5% band is $\pm 1.96/\sqrt 8 = \pm 0.693$. Since $0.304$ lies inside it, we cannot reject zero autocorrelation: with eight observations a correlation must be very large to be detected.
answer: $\hat\rho_1 = 0.304$, not significant against the band $\pm 0.693$.

@example
title: Forecasting with an AR(1) model
problem: A spread follows $y_t = 0.6 + 0.7\,y_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, and the last observation is $y_T = 3.0$. Find the unconditional mean and the forecasts for $h = 1$, $2$ and $5$.
step: The mean is $\mu = 0.6/(1 - 0.7) = 2.0$, and $y_T - \mu = 1.0$.
step: $h = 1$: $\hat y_{T+1} = 0.6 + 0.7(3.0) = 2.70$. $h = 2$: $\hat y_{T+2} = 0.6 + 0.7(2.70) = 2.49$.
step: $h = 5$: $\hat y_{T+5} = 2.0 + 0.7^5(1.0) = 2.0 + 0.16807 = 2.168$.
step: The forecasts decay toward the mean 2.0: the gap shrinks by 30% each step, which is what $\phi = 0.7$ means.
answer: $\mu = 2.0$; forecasts $2.70$, $2.49$ and $2.168$.

@mistakes
- Believing an AR(1) forecast stays near the last observation: for $|\phi| < 1$ it converges to the mean $c/(1-\phi)$.
- Expecting an MA(1) to have an autocorrelation above 0.5, or nonzero autocorrelations beyond lag 1.
- Fitting an AR or ARMA model to a series with a trend or unit root without first making it stationary.
- Using the Ljung–Box degrees of freedom $m$ instead of $m - p - q$ on residuals from a fitted model.

model:
```python
def sample_acf(y, k):
    """Sample autocorrelation of the series y at lag k."""
    n = len(y)
    mean = sum(y) / n
    num = sum((y[t] - mean) * (y[t - k] - mean) for t in range(k, n))
    den = sum((v - mean) ** 2 for v in y)
    return num / den
```
hint: The denominator always uses all observations; the numerator pairs each value with the one `k` steps earlier.
hint: Loop `t` from `k` to `n - 1` and multiply the deviation at `t` by the deviation at `t - k`.
solution: Compute the mean, then the lagged cross-products of deviations for $t = k, \dots, n-1$ (zero-based), and divide by the total sum of squared deviations. Dividing the numerator by fewer terms or the denominator by $n - k$ would give a different (and incorrect) estimator; the lag-0 value is exactly 1.

model:
```python
def ar1_forecast(c, phi, y_last, h):
    """Iterated AR(1) forecasts for horizons 1..h."""
    forecasts = []
    current = y_last
    for _ in range(h):
        current = c + phi * current
        forecasts.append(current)
    return forecasts
```
hint: Start from `y_last` and apply $c + \phi \times (\text{previous value})$ once per step.
hint: Append each new forecast to a list and feed it into the next step.
solution: The $h$-step forecast of an AR(1) is the one-step rule applied $h$ times, because the future shocks have mean zero. With $c = 0$ and $\phi = 0.5$ the forecasts halve each step. With $\phi = 1$ (a random walk with drift $c$) the forecasts grow by $c$ each step instead of reverting.
