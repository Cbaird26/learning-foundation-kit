@article
Factors in finance overlap. A value factor and a profitability factor, or two measures of firm size, move together. When regressors are strongly correlated, OLS struggles to separate their effects. And when an important regressor is *left out*, the remaining coefficients absorb its effect. These two problems, **multicollinearity** and **misspecification**, are the subject of this week.

### Multicollinearity

**Perfect collinearity**, where one regressor is an exact linear combination of the others (the classic case is the dummy-variable trap of week 5), makes $X'X$ singular and OLS impossible. **Imperfect** (high) multicollinearity leaves OLS defined, **unbiased and BLUE**, but makes the estimates imprecise. For regressor $j$, with $SST_j = \sum_i (x_{ij} - \bar x_j)^2$,

$$\text{Var}(\hat\beta_j) = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j\,(1 - R_j^2)} = \frac{\sigma^2}{SST_j}\,\text{VIF}_j, \qquad \text{VIF}_j = \frac{1}{1 - R_j^2},$$

where $R_j^2$ is the $R^2$ from regressing $x_j$ on all the *other* regressors. The **variance inflation factor** says how many times larger the variance is than it would be if $x_j$ were uncorrelated with the others; the standard error is inflated by $\sqrt{\text{VIF}_j}$. With two regressors, $R_j^2$ is the squared correlation $r^2$ between them. A common rule of thumb flags $\text{VIF} > 10$ (that is, $R_j^2 > 0.90$), but it is a guide, not a test.

Typical symptoms: a high $R^2$ and a significant overall $F$ test, yet insignificant individual $t$ statistics; coefficients that change sign or size when one observation or regressor is dropped. Multicollinearity does *not* bias coefficients, does not invalidate predictions within the range of the data, and does not affect the $F$ test of the collinear variables *together*. Remedies: more data, combining the variables (an index), dropping one only if theory says it is redundant, or a shrinkage method such as ridge regression (week 12).

### Omitted-variable bias

Suppose the true model is $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \beta_2 x_2 + \varepsilon$ but you regress $y$ on $x_1$ alone and get the slope $\tilde\beta_1$. Let $\tilde\delta_1$ be the slope from regressing $x_2$ on $x_1$. In any sample,

$$\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\,\tilde\delta_1,$$

where $\hat\beta_1, \hat\beta_2$ come from the full regression. In expectation, the bias is $\beta_2\,\text{Cov}(x_1, x_2)/\text{Var}(x_1)$. It vanishes only if $\beta_2 = 0$ (the omitted variable does not matter) or $x_1$ and $x_2$ are uncorrelated. If the omitted variable is positively related to $y$ and positively correlated with $x_1$, the short regression overstates $\beta_1$. Including an **irrelevant** variable ($\beta_2 = 0$) does not bias the others but inflates their variances. The trade-off is why "kitchen sink" regressions are not the answer either.

### Choosing and checking a specification

- **Adjusted $R^2$** penalizes extra regressors: $\bar R^2 = 1 - (1 - R^2)\dfrac{n-1}{n-k-1}$. Unlike $R^2$, it can fall when a regressor is added.
- **Information criteria** (lower is better), for $k+1$ estimated coefficients and the same data and dependent variable: $AIC = n\ln(SSR/n) + 2(k+1)$ and $BIC = n\ln(SSR/n) + (k+1)\ln n$. BIC penalizes complexity more heavily when $n \ge 8$ and tends to choose smaller models.
- **RESET test** (Ramsey): add the squared and cubed fitted values $\hat y^2$ and $\hat y^3$ to the regression and use an $F$ test ($q = 2$) of whether their coefficients are zero. Rejection signals that a nonlinear function or an omitted variable is present, but not which. It assumes the classical conditions otherwise.
- **Robust inference**: report both ordinary and heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors and look at whether conclusions change. If they differ greatly, investigate before trusting either.

The deeper lesson is about discipline: choose a specification from economic theory first, then test it, and do not pick the specification that gives the best-looking $t$ statistic (data snooping, week 12).

@example
title: How much does collinearity inflate a standard error?
problem: In a regression of fund returns on a value factor and a profitability factor, the two regressors have a sample correlation of 0.95. By what factor is the variance of each coefficient inflated, relative to uncorrelated regressors, and by what factor the standard error?
step: With two regressors, $R_j^2 = r^2 = 0.95^2 = 0.9025$ for each.
step: $\text{VIF} = 1/(1 - 0.9025) = 1/0.0975 = 10.26$.
step: The standard error is inflated by $\sqrt{10.26} = 3.20$, so a coefficient that would have a $t$ statistic of 3.2 with uncorrelated factors would have $t \approx 1.0$.
step: The VIF exceeds the rule-of-thumb threshold of 10. The estimates are still unbiased; they are just imprecise.
answer: $\text{VIF} = 10.26$, so the variance is 10.26 times larger and the standard error 3.20 times larger.

@example
title: Omitted-variable bias in a beta
problem: A full regression of a fund's excess return on the market ($x_1$) and a size factor ($x_2$) gives $\hat\beta_1 = 1.20$ and $\hat\beta_2 = 0.50$. Regressing the size factor on the market gives a slope of $\tilde\delta_1 = 0.60$. What slope would you get from regressing the fund on the market alone?
step: Use the identity $\tilde\beta_1 = \hat\beta_1 + \hat\beta_2\tilde\delta_1$.
step: $\tilde\beta_1 = 1.20 + 0.50 \times 0.60 = 1.50$.
step: The short regression overstates the market loading by $0.30$: the size factor is positively related to the fund and positively correlated with the market, so the market's coefficient picks up part of the size effect.
answer: $\tilde\beta_1 = 1.50$, a bias of $+0.30$ compared with $1.20$.

@mistakes
- Dropping a variable because it is collinear and then ignoring the omitted-variable bias this creates in the others.
- Believing multicollinearity biases OLS or breaks the joint $F$ test; it only inflates the variances of individual coefficients.
- Using $R^2$, which never falls, to choose between models with different numbers of regressors, instead of adjusted $R^2$, AIC or BIC.
- Comparing AIC or BIC across models with different dependent variables (for example $y$ and $\ln y$) or different samples.

model:
```python
import math


def slope_se_comparison(x, y):
    """Return (ordinary, HC1) standard errors of the slope."""
    n = len(x)
    x_bar = sum(x) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    s_xx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)
    slope = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y)) / s_xx
    intercept = y_bar - slope * x_bar
    resid = [yi - intercept - slope * xi for xi, yi in zip(x, y)]
    s2 = sum(e ** 2 for e in resid) / (n - 2)
    ordinary = math.sqrt(s2 / s_xx)
    meat = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 * e ** 2 for xi, e in zip(x, resid))
    hc1 = math.sqrt(n / (n - 2) * meat) / s_xx
    return ordinary, hc1
```
hint: Both standard errors use the same residuals; they differ in how the squared residuals are combined.
hint: Ordinary: $\sqrt{(SSR/(n-2))/S_{xx}}$. HC1: multiply the weighted sum $\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2 e_i^2$ by $n/(n-2)$, take the square root and divide by $S_{xx}$.
solution: Fit the line, compute residuals, and build both estimates from them. The ordinary version assumes every residual has the same variance and averages them (divided by $n-2$); the robust version weights each squared residual by the squared distance of its $x$ from the mean. In the first data set the residuals are larger at the extreme $x$ values, so the robust standard error is bigger; in the second, the residuals are spread more evenly and the robust standard error is smaller.

model:
```python
import math


def fit_stats(ssr, tss, n, k):
    """Adjusted R-squared, AIC and BIC of a regression with an intercept."""
    adj_r2 = 1 - (ssr / (n - k - 1)) / (tss / (n - 1))
    base = n * math.log(ssr / n)
    aic = base + 2 * (k + 1)
    bic = base + (k + 1) * math.log(n)
    return adj_r2, aic, bic
```
hint: All three statistics are formulas in `ssr`, `tss`, `n` and `k`; start with the adjusted $R^2$.
hint: AIC and BIC share the term $n\ln(SSR/n)$; they differ only in the penalty, $2(k+1)$ versus $(k+1)\ln n$.
solution: Compute $\bar R^2$ from the two mean squares, then add the penalty for the $k+1$ coefficients to the common fit term $n\ln(SSR/n)$. In the second test the model has no regressors, so $SSR = TSS$ and the adjusted $R^2$ is exactly 0.

solution: $F = \dfrac{(48 - 42)/2}{42/45} = \dfrac{3}{0.9333} = 3.21$. The 5% critical value of $F_{2,45}$ is about 3.204, so the test (just) rejects correct functional form.
