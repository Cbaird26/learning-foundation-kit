@article
Suppose you want to know how strongly a stock moves when the market moves. You have ten monthly observations of the stock's return and the market's return. A scatter of the points slopes upward but does not lie on a line. **Regression** is the tool that fits the best line through such a cloud and tells you how much to trust it. In finance it is the workhorse behind betas, factor models, event studies and forecasting.

### The model

The **linear regression model** with $k$ explanatory variables (regressors) says that for observation $i = 1, \dots, n$

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_{i1} + \dots + \beta_k x_{ik} + \varepsilon_i .$$

Here $y_i$ is the **dependent variable**, the $x_{ij}$ are the regressors, the $\beta_j$ are unknown **parameters** and $\varepsilon_i$ is the **error term**, everything that affects $y_i$ but is not in the model. Stacking all observations gives the matrix form

$$y = X\beta + \varepsilon,$$

where $y$ is $n \times 1$, $X$ is $n \times (k+1)$ with a first column of ones for the intercept, and $\beta$ is $(k+1) \times 1$. "Linear" means linear in the *parameters*: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \ln x + \varepsilon$ is a linear model, but $y = \beta_0 + x^{\beta_1}$ is not.

### Ordinary least squares

OLS chooses the coefficients that minimize the **sum of squared residuals** $SSR = \sum_i (y_i - \hat y_i)^2$, where $\hat y_i$ is the fitted value and $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$ is the **residual**. Setting the derivative to zero gives the **normal equations** $X'X\hat\beta = X'y$, so

$$\hat\beta = (X'X)^{-1}X'y,$$

which exists when the columns of $X$ are not perfectly collinear. For one regressor this reduces to

$$\hat\beta_1 = \frac{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2} = \frac{\widehat{\text{Cov}}(x, y)}{\widehat{\text{Var}}(x)}, \qquad \hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1 \bar x .$$

The fitted line always passes through the point of means $(\bar x, \bar y)$. When the model has an intercept, the residuals sum to zero and are uncorrelated with every regressor in the sample.

### Assumptions

OLS is a good estimator only under conditions on the data-generating process:

1. **Linearity**: the model is linear in the parameters.
2. **No perfect collinearity**: no regressor is an exact linear combination of the others.
3. **Zero conditional mean** (exogeneity): $E[\varepsilon_i \mid X] = 0$. The errors are not related to the regressors.
4. **Spherical errors**: $\text{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I$, meaning constant variance (homoskedasticity) and no correlation between errors of different observations.
5. For exact small-sample $t$ and $F$ tests, errors that are **normally distributed**.

Under assumptions 1 to 3 OLS is **unbiased**: $E[\hat\beta] = \beta$. Adding assumption 4, the **Gauss–Markov theorem** says OLS has the smallest variance among all linear unbiased estimators (it is **BLUE**). Weeks 3 and 6 show what to do when assumption 4 fails.

### Goodness of fit

The total variation in $y$ splits into explained and unexplained parts, $TSS = ESS + SSR$, where $TSS = \sum_i (y_i - \bar y)^2$. The **R-squared** is

$$R^2 = 1 - \frac{SSR}{TSS},$$

the fraction of the sample variation in $y$ that the regressors explain. It lies between 0 and 1 when the model has an intercept, and it never falls when you add a regressor. A high $R^2$ does not make a model correct, and a low one does not make it useless: monthly stock returns are mostly noise, so an $R^2$ of 0.05 can still be informative.

### The market model

For stock $i$, the **market model** regresses its excess return on the market's excess return:

$$R_{it} - R_{ft} = \alpha_i + \beta_i (R_{mt} - R_{ft}) + \varepsilon_{it}.$$

The slope $\beta_i = \text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)/\text{Var}(R_m)$ is the stock's **beta**, its sensitivity to market moves; the intercept $\alpha_i$ is the average return not explained by the market (Jensen's alpha). Always regress *returns*, not prices: prices trend and are not stationary, a problem week 8 returns to.

@example
title: A market-model regression by hand
problem: Over five months, the market's excess returns (in percent) were $x = (-2, -1, 1, 2, 5)$ and the excess returns of Bellwether Foods were $y = (-3, 0, 2, 2, 4)$. Estimate the intercept and slope and find the fitted values.
step: Compute the means: $\bar x = 5/5 = 1$ and $\bar y = 5/5 = 1$.
step: Compute the sums of squares and cross-products. $S_{xx} = \sum x_i^2 - n\bar x^2 = 35 - 5(1)^2 = 30$ and $S_{xy} = \sum x_i y_i - n\bar x\bar y = 32 - 5(1)(1) = 27$.
step: The slope is $\hat\beta_1 = S_{xy}/S_{xx} = 27/30 = 0.9$. The intercept is $\hat\beta_0 = \bar y - \hat\beta_1\bar x = 1 - 0.9(1) = 0.1$.
step: The fitted values $\hat y = 0.1 + 0.9x$ are given below, with the residuals $e = y - \hat y$ (they sum to zero).

| $x$ | $y$ | $\hat y$ | $e$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| -2 | -3 | -1.7 | -1.3 |
| -1 | 0 | -0.8 | 0.8 |
| 1 | 2 | 1.0 | 1.0 |
| 2 | 2 | 1.9 | 0.1 |
| 5 | 4 | 4.6 | -0.6 |
answer: $\hat y = 0.1 + 0.9x$: a beta of 0.9 and a monthly alpha of 0.1%.

@example
title: R-squared and a prediction
problem: A regression $\hat y = 0.1 + 0.9x$ fits the five observations $y = (-3, 0, 2, 2, 4)$ with residuals $(-1.3, 0.8, 1.0, 0.1, -0.6)$, compute $R^2$ and predict the stock's excess return in a month when the market's excess return is 3%.
step: The residual sum of squares is $SSR = 1.69 + 0.64 + 1.00 + 0.01 + 0.36 = 3.70$.
step: With $\bar y = 1$, the total sum of squares is $TSS = (-4)^2 + (-1)^2 + 1^2 + 1^2 + 3^2 = 28$.
step: $R^2 = 1 - 3.70/28 = 0.8679$. The market explains about 87% of the sample variation in this stock's excess return (an unusually high figure, because there are only five invented observations).
step: The prediction is $\hat y = 0.1 + 0.9(3) = 2.8\%$. A prediction is only a conditional average, and with five observations it is imprecise.
answer: $R^2 = 0.8679$; predicted excess return 2.8%.

@mistakes
- Reading a regression coefficient as causal. $\hat\beta_1$ measures how $y$ differs on average with $x$, holding the other regressors fixed; it is causal only if the exogeneity assumption holds.
- Regressing returns on returns in different units (percent against decimals), or regressing raw returns when the model calls for excess returns.
- Treating a high $R^2$ as proof the model is right, or comparing the $R^2$ of models with different dependent variables.
- Forgetting the column of ones in $X$, which forces the line through the origin and changes every coefficient.

model:
```python
def simple_ols(x, y):
    """Return (intercept, slope) of the OLS regression of y on x."""
    n = len(x)
    x_bar = sum(x) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    s_xx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)
    s_xy = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y))
    slope = s_xy / s_xx
    return y_bar - slope * x_bar, slope
```
hint: The slope is the sum of cross-products of deviations from the means divided by the sum of squared deviations of `x`.
hint: Compute `x_bar` and `y_bar` first; the intercept is `y_bar - slope * x_bar`. Return the intercept first.
solution: Compute both means, then $S_{xy}$ and $S_{xx}$ around them. The slope is $S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ and the intercept makes the line pass through the means. The flat data in the last test give a slope of zero and an intercept equal to the mean of `y`.

model:
```python
def r_squared(y, y_hat):
    """R-squared of fitted values y_hat against observations y."""
    y_bar = sum(y) / len(y)
    ssr = sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip(y, y_hat))
    tss = sum((a - y_bar) ** 2 for a in y)
    return 1 - ssr / tss
```
hint: You need two sums: squared residuals, and squared deviations of `y` from its mean.
hint: Return `1 - ssr / tss`.
solution: $SSR$ adds the squared differences between `y` and `y_hat`; $TSS$ adds the squared deviations of `y` from its mean. A prediction that is worse than simply using the mean has $SSR > TSS$, so $R^2$ is negative, as the last test shows (SSR 8, TSS 2).

model:
```python
def ols(X, y):
    """OLS coefficients by Gauss-Jordan elimination on X'X b = X'y."""
    n, k = len(X), len(X[0])
    a = []
    for i in range(k):
        row = [sum(X[r][i] * X[r][j] for r in range(n)) for j in range(k)]
        row.append(sum(X[r][i] * y[r] for r in range(n)))
        a.append(row)
    for i in range(k):
        p = max(range(i, k), key=lambda r: abs(a[r][i]))
        a[i], a[p] = a[p], a[i]
        pivot = a[i][i]
        a[i] = [v / pivot for v in a[i]]
        for r in range(k):
            if r != i:
                f = a[r][i]
                a[r] = [u - f * v for u, v in zip(a[r], a[i])]
    return [a[i][k] for i in range(k)]
```
hint: Build the augmented matrix $[X'X \mid X'y]$ with $(X'X)_{ij} = \sum_r X_{ri}X_{rj}$.
hint: Reduce it to the identity on the left; the right-hand column is then the solution. Swap rows to put the largest pivot on the diagonal.
solution: Form $X'X$ and $X'y$ with sums over the observations, append $X'y$ as an extra column, and apply Gauss–Jordan elimination (with partial pivoting for stability). The last column then holds $\hat\beta$. The second test recovers the exact coefficients of $y = 1 + 2x_1 - x_2$.
