@article
Returns on small stocks are more variable than returns on large ones, and returns in turbulent months scatter more than returns in calm months. In regression language, the error variance is not the same for every observation. This is **heteroskedasticity**: $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X) = \sigma_i^2$ depends on $i$, usually on the regressors. Its opposite, a common variance $\sigma^2$, is **homoskedasticity**.

### What it breaks

With heteroskedastic errors, OLS is still **unbiased and consistent** (that needs only exogeneity), and $R^2$ is still a fine descriptive statistic. What fails is inference. The usual formula $s^2(X'X)^{-1}$ for the variance of $\hat\beta$ assumes a constant variance, so the usual standard errors, $t$ statistics and $F$ statistics are wrong, in either direction, even in large samples. OLS is also no longer the minimum-variance linear unbiased estimator.

### Detecting it

First look: plot the residuals $e_i$ against the fitted values or a regressor. A funnel shape (the spread of the residuals widens as the fitted value grows) suggests heteroskedasticity. Two formal tests regress the squared residuals on the regressors:

- **Breusch–Pagan** (the studentized, or Koenker, version): run the regression, save the residuals, regress $e_i^2$ on the original regressors, and compute $LM = nR^2_{\text{aux}}$, where $R^2_{\text{aux}}$ is the $R^2$ of the auxiliary regression. Under $H_0$: constant variance, $LM$ is approximately $\chi^2$ with $k$ degrees of freedom ($k$ = number of regressors in the auxiliary regression, excluding the intercept). It needs a large sample, but not normal errors.
- **White test**: the auxiliary regression also contains the squares and cross-products of the regressors, which detects more general forms. With two regressors it has five terms, so $\chi^2_5$.

5% critical values of $\chi^2$: 3.841 (1 df), 5.991 (2 df), 7.815 (3 df), 11.070 (5 df). Both tests assume the mean equation is correctly specified; a misspecified mean (a missing squared term, say) can look like heteroskedasticity.

### Robust standard errors

The idea of White's estimator is to keep the OLS coefficients and fix only the standard errors. Replace the unknown $\sigma_i^2$ with the squared residual $e_i^2$:

$$\widehat{\text{Var}}_{HC0}(\hat\beta) = (X'X)^{-1}\Big(\sum_i e_i^2\, x_i x_i'\Big)(X'X)^{-1},$$

a "sandwich" formula, where $x_i$ is the $i$-th row of $X$ written as a column. For a simple regression this is

$$\text{se}_{HC0}(\hat\beta_1) = \frac{\sqrt{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 e_i^2}}{\sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2}.$$

**HC1** multiplies the variance by $n/(n-k-1)$, a degrees-of-freedom correction used by many software packages by default; **HC3** divides each $e_i^2$ by $(1-h_i)^2$, where $h_i$ is the observation's leverage, and behaves better in small samples. Robust standard errors are valid in large samples whatever the form of heteroskedasticity, and they can be larger or smaller than the ordinary ones. Use them with the ordinary $t$ statistic, $\hat\beta_j/\text{se}_{robust}$, compared with the normal or $t$ critical value.

On your own computer, with a package, this is `sm.OLS(y, X).fit(cov_type="HC1")`; the standard library code in this course shows the arithmetic.

### Weighted least squares

If you know the form, say $\text{Var}(\varepsilon_i) = \sigma^2 h_i$ with $h_i$ known, divide each observation by $\sqrt{h_i}$ (weighted least squares, WLS) to get a homoskedastic model and a more efficient estimator. If $h_i$ must be estimated, the method is feasible GLS. In practice analysts often keep OLS and report robust standard errors, because a wrong guess about $h_i$ can do more harm than good. Heteroskedasticity over *time*, volatility that clusters, gets its own models in week 9.

@example
title: Ordinary and robust standard errors
problem: Six months of data for the regression of a fund's return $y$ on a signal $x$ are $x = (1,2,3,4,5,6)$ and $y = (2.0, 3.1, 6.3, 5.2, 10.1, 8.4)$. OLS gives $\hat y = 0.660 + 1.4829x$. Compute the ordinary and the HC0 standard error of the slope.
step: The residuals are $e = (-0.1429, -0.5257, 1.1914, -1.3914, 2.0257, -1.1571)$; they grow in size with $x$. With $\bar x = 3.5$, $S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2 = 17.5$.
step: Ordinary: $SSR = \sum e_i^2 = 9.0949$ and $s^2 = SSR/(n-2) = 9.0949/4 = 2.2737$, so $\text{se} = \sqrt{s^2/S_{xx}} = \sqrt{2.2737/17.5} = 0.3605$.
step: Robust: weight each squared residual by the squared deviation of its $x$. The terms $(x_i - \bar x)^2 e_i^2$ are $0.1276,\ 0.6218,\ 0.3549,\ 0.4840,\ 9.2329,\ 8.3686$, which sum to $19.1898$.
step: Then $\text{se}_{HC0} = \sqrt{19.1898}/17.5 = 4.3806/17.5 = 0.2503$. HC1 multiplies the variance by $6/4$, giving $0.2503\sqrt{1.5} = 0.3066$.
step: The slope's $t$ statistic is $1.4829/0.3605 = 4.11$ with ordinary errors and $1.4829/0.2503 = 5.92$ with HC0 errors. Here robust errors happen to be smaller; with a different pattern of residuals they would be larger. With only six points none of these is reliable.
answer: Ordinary se $= 0.3605$, HC0 se $= 0.2503$ (HC1: $0.3066$).

@example
title: A Breusch–Pagan test
problem: A regression of monthly stock returns on the log of firm size uses $n = 40$ firms. The auxiliary regression of the squared residuals on the log of firm size has $R^2 = 0.18$. Test for heteroskedasticity at the 5% level.
step: The null hypothesis is constant error variance. The auxiliary regression has $k = 1$ regressor, so the statistic has $1$ degree of freedom under $H_0$.
step: $LM = nR^2_{\text{aux}} = 40 \times 0.18 = 7.2$.
step: The 5% critical value of $\chi^2_1$ is 3.841. Since $7.2 > 3.841$, we reject constant variance.
step: Conclusion: the squared residuals are related to firm size, so report heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors. The test assumes a large sample and a correctly specified mean equation.
answer: $LM = 7.2 > 3.841$: reject homoskedasticity at 5%.

@mistakes
- Believing heteroskedasticity biases the OLS coefficients. It does not; it invalidates the usual standard errors and tests.
- Using the Breusch–Pagan statistic with the wrong degrees of freedom: they equal the number of regressors in the auxiliary regression, not $n$ and not $n-k-1$.
- Replacing the coefficients with "robust" ones: robust standard errors change only the standard errors, never $\hat\beta$.
- Assuming robust standard errors are always larger than the ordinary ones; their direction depends on how the residual spread relates to the regressor.

model:
```python
import math


def hc0_slope_se(x, y):
    """White (HC0) standard error of the slope in a simple regression."""
    n = len(x)
    x_bar = sum(x) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    s_xx = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 for xi in x)
    slope = sum((xi - x_bar) * (yi - y_bar) for xi, yi in zip(x, y)) / s_xx
    intercept = y_bar - slope * x_bar
    resid = [yi - intercept - slope * xi for xi, yi in zip(x, y)]
    meat = sum((xi - x_bar) ** 2 * e ** 2 for xi, e in zip(x, resid))
    return math.sqrt(meat) / s_xx
```
hint: Fit the line first, then compute the residual for every observation.
hint: Add up $(x_i - \bar x)^2 e_i^2$ over the observations, take the square root and divide by $S_{xx}$.
solution: Estimate the slope and intercept, compute the residuals, and form the sum of squared-deviation-weighted squared residuals. Its square root over $S_{xx}$ is the HC0 standard error. A perfect fit has zero residuals, so the standard error is zero (second-to-last test).

model:
```python
def _fit(x, y):
    n = len(x)
    x_bar = sum(x) / n
    y_bar = sum(y) / n
    s_xx = sum((a - x_bar) ** 2 for a in x)
    slope = sum((a - x_bar) * (b - y_bar) for a, b in zip(x, y)) / s_xx
    intercept = y_bar - slope * x_bar
    fitted = [intercept + slope * a for a in x]
    return [b - f for b, f in zip(y, fitted)], fitted


def breusch_pagan_lm(x, y):
    """Studentized Breusch-Pagan LM statistic n * R^2 for one regressor."""
    resid, _ = _fit(x, y)
    sq = [e ** 2 for e in resid]
    aux_resid, _ = _fit(x, sq)
    mean_sq = sum(sq) / len(sq)
    tss = sum((v - mean_sq) ** 2 for v in sq)
    ssr = sum(u ** 2 for u in aux_resid)
    return len(x) * (1 - ssr / tss)
```
hint: You need the same simple regression twice: once for `y` on `x`, once for the squared residuals on `x`.
hint: $R^2_{\text{aux}} = 1 - SSR_{\text{aux}}/TSS_{\text{aux}}$, where the total sum of squares is around the mean of the squared residuals. Multiply by $n$.
solution: Write a helper that fits a simple regression and returns its residuals. Apply it to `y`, square the residuals, apply it again to the squares, compute the auxiliary $R^2$ from its SSR and TSS, and multiply by $n$. The statistic is compared with a $\chi^2_1$ critical value (3.841 at 5%).
