@article
Look at a daily return series from any equity market: calm stretches, where returns are small, alternate with turbulent ones, where large moves of either sign follow each other. Returns themselves are nearly unpredictable, but their *size* is predictable. This **volatility clustering** means the variance of the error is not constant over time, which is conditional heteroskedasticity, the time-series cousin of week 3.

### The setup

Write the return as $r_t = \mu + \varepsilon_t$ with $\varepsilon_t = \sigma_t z_t$, where $z_t$ is an independent shock with mean 0 and variance 1. The **conditional variance** $\sigma_t^2 = \text{Var}(\varepsilon_t \mid \text{information at } t-1)$ is what the model describes. Returns are (nearly) uncorrelated, but squared returns are correlated.

### ARCH(1)

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2, \qquad \omega > 0,\ \alpha \ge 0.$$

A large shock yesterday raises today's variance. The unconditional variance is $\omega/(1-\alpha)$ if $\alpha < 1$. An ARCH($q$) model uses $q$ lagged squared shocks.

### GARCH(1,1)

The generalized ARCH (GARCH) model adds last period's variance:

$$\sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha\,\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\,\sigma_{t-1}^2,\qquad \omega > 0,\ \alpha \ge 0,\ \beta \ge 0.$$

The parameter $\alpha$ is how strongly volatility reacts to news, $\beta$ is how persistent it is, and $\alpha + \beta$ is the **persistence**. If $\alpha + \beta < 1$ the process is covariance stationary, and

$$V = \frac{\omega}{1 - \alpha - \beta}$$

is the **long-run (unconditional) variance**. Daily financial data typically give $\alpha$ small (0.05 to 0.15) and $\beta$ large (0.8 to 0.95), so $\alpha + \beta$ is close to 1. Even with normally distributed $z_t$, GARCH implies fat-tailed returns, as observed in practice.

**Variance forecasts.** Given the one-step-ahead variance $\sigma_{T+1}^2$ (computed from the last observation), the forecast $h$ steps ahead mean-reverts to $V$:

$$E[\sigma_{T+h}^2] = V + (\alpha+\beta)^{h-1}\,(\sigma_{T+1}^2 - V),$$

and the total variance over the next $n$ days is the sum of the $n$ daily forecasts. The **half-life** of a volatility shock is $\ln 0.5/\ln(\alpha+\beta)$ periods. With daily data and independent days, annual volatility is roughly the daily volatility times $\sqrt{252}$ (the number of trading days in a year is a convention; 252 is common).

### Testing for ARCH effects

First estimate the mean equation (often just a constant or an AR(1)) and save the residuals $e_t$. The **ARCH LM test** regresses $e_t^2$ on a constant and $q$ of its own lags and computes $LM = T R^2$, where $T$ is the number of observations in the auxiliary regression. Under $H_0$ (no ARCH effects), $LM \sim \chi^2_q$ approximately: 5% critical values are 3.841 ($q = 1$), 5.991 (2), 7.815 (3), 9.488 (4), 11.070 (5). It assumes the mean equation is adequate and the sample is large.

### Estimation

The parameters are estimated by **maximum likelihood**. With normal $z_t$, the log-likelihood of $T$ observations is

$$\ell(\omega, \alpha, \beta) = -\frac12\sum_{t=1}^{T}\left[\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2 + \frac{\varepsilon_t^2}{\sigma_t^2}\right],$$

where $\sigma_t^2$ comes from the recursion started at a chosen $\sigma_1^2$ (often the sample variance). There is no closed form, so software searches numerically for the parameters that maximize $\ell$, respecting the constraints. If the shocks are not truly normal, the same estimator (quasi-maximum likelihood) is still consistent when the variance equation is right, but standard errors should be the robust ones. On your own computer, the `arch` package does this in one line, `arch_model(returns, vol="GARCH", p=1, q=1).fit()`; scaling the returns to percent helps the optimizer. To check the fit, the standardized residuals $e_t/\hat\sigma_t$ should show no remaining ARCH effects.

Extensions allow asymmetry (bad news raises volatility more than good news of the same size, the *leverage effect*) as in the GJR-GARCH and EGARCH models, and fat-tailed $z_t$ such as a Student $t$.

@example
title: Running the GARCH(1,1) recursion
problem: A GARCH(1,1) model for daily returns in percent has $\omega = 0.05$, $\alpha = 0.10$, $\beta = 0.85$. Start at $\sigma_1^2 = 1.0$ and suppose the first three shocks are $\varepsilon_1 = 2.0$, $\varepsilon_2 = -1.0$, $\varepsilon_3 = 0.5$. Find $\sigma_2^2$, $\sigma_3^2$, $\sigma_4^2$, the long-run variance and the long-run annual volatility.
step: $\sigma_2^2 = 0.05 + 0.10(2.0)^2 + 0.85(1.0) = 0.05 + 0.40 + 0.85 = 1.30$.
step: $\sigma_3^2 = 0.05 + 0.10(-1.0)^2 + 0.85(1.30) = 0.05 + 0.10 + 1.105 = 1.255$.
step: $\sigma_4^2 = 0.05 + 0.10(0.5)^2 + 0.85(1.255) = 0.05 + 0.025 + 1.06675 = 1.14175$. The large first shock lifted variance to 1.30, and the variance has been decaying toward the long-run level since.
step: The persistence is $0.95 < 1$, so $V = 0.05/(1 - 0.95) = 1.0$ (percent squared per day), a daily volatility of 1%, or $\sqrt{252} \times 1\% = 15.87\%$ a year.
answer: $\sigma_2^2 = 1.30$, $\sigma_3^2 = 1.255$, $\sigma_4^2 = 1.14175$; $V = 1.0$, about 15.87% a year.

@example
title: Forecasting volatility
problem: For the model above ($V = 1.0$, $\alpha + \beta = 0.95$), suppose after a turbulent week the one-step-ahead variance is $\sigma_{T+1}^2 = 1.60$. Forecast the variance 5 days ahead, the total variance and volatility over the next 5 days, and the half-life of a shock.
step: The gap from the long-run level is $1.60 - 1.0 = 0.60$.
step: $E[\sigma_{T+5}^2] = 1.0 + 0.95^{4}(0.60) = 1.0 + 0.8145 \times 0.60 = 1.4887$.
step: The total variance over days 1 to 5 is $\sum_{h=1}^{5}[1 + 0.95^{h-1}(0.60)] = 5 + 0.60\,\dfrac{1 - 0.95^5}{0.05} = 5 + 0.60 \times 4.5244 = 7.7146$, so the 5-day volatility is $\sqrt{7.7146} = 2.78\%$ (independent days are assumed).
step: The half-life is $\ln 0.5/\ln 0.95 = 13.5$ days: after about two and a half weeks, half of the excess variance has gone.
answer: $1.4887$ for day 5; $7.7146$ total variance, a 5-day volatility of $2.78\%$; half-life $13.5$ days.

@mistakes
- Using $\sigma_t^2$ in the recursion where $\varepsilon_{t-1}^2$ belongs (or the reverse): the news term is the *previous squared shock*, and the persistence term is the *previous variance*.
- Computing the long-run variance as $\omega/(1-\alpha)$ for a GARCH(1,1), forgetting $\beta$, or computing it when $\alpha + \beta \ge 1$ (it does not exist).
- Scaling daily volatility to annual volatility by multiplying by 252 instead of $\sqrt{252}$.
- Treating GARCH as a model of the *level* of returns: it models their conditional variance, and the mean equation is separate.

prompt: A GARCH(1,1) model is estimated with $\omega = 0.02$, $\alpha = 0.12$ and $\beta = 0.90$. What does this imply?

solution: Positivity of the parameters is necessary but not sufficient: covariance stationarity needs $\alpha + \beta < 1$. model:
```python
def garch_variances(eps, omega, alpha, beta, sigma2_start):
    """Conditional variances of a GARCH(1,1), including the one-step forecast."""
    variances = [sigma2_start]
    for e in eps:
        variances.append(omega + alpha * e ** 2 + beta * variances[-1])
    return variances
```
hint: Start the list with `sigma2_start`; each new variance uses the previous shock and the previous variance.
hint: Looping over every shock in `eps` produces exactly one new variance per shock, so the list ends up one longer than `eps`.
solution: For each shock $\varepsilon_{t-1}$ append $\omega + \alpha\varepsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta\sigma_{t-1}^2$, where $\sigma_{t-1}^2$ is the last value in the list. With zero shocks the variance decays geometrically toward $\omega/(1-\beta)$: $0.2 + 0.8 \times 3.0 = 2.6$, and so on. The extra last value is the forecast for the day after the data end.

model:
```python
import math


def gaussian_loglik(eps, sigma2):
    """Gaussian log-likelihood of shocks given conditional variances."""
    total = 0.0
    for e, s in zip(eps, sigma2):
        total += math.log(2 * math.pi) + math.log(s) + e ** 2 / s
    return -0.5 * total
```
hint: Each observation contributes $\ln(2\pi) + \ln\sigma_t^2 + \varepsilon_t^2/\sigma_t^2$; add them up and multiply by $-1/2$.
hint: Use `zip(eps, sigma2)` to walk through both lists together.
solution: Accumulate the three terms for each observation and multiply the total by $-1/2$ at the end. A single standard-normal observation at zero has log-likelihood $-\tfrac12\ln(2\pi) = -0.919$. Higher log-likelihood means a better fit, which is what maximum likelihood estimation maximizes.
