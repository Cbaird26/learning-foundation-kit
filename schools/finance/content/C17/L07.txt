@article
A forecast is a function of **assumptions**. Keep the assumptions (the *drivers*) in one place, keep the calculations in functions, and every number in the forecast can be traced to a driver. That is the difference between a model and a pile of arithmetic.

### The structure
An income statement forecast runs from the top line down:

1. **Revenue** grows at a rate each year: $\text{Rev}_t = \text{Rev}_{t-1}(1 + g_t)$.
2. **Cost of goods sold (COGS)** and **selling, general and administrative (SG&A)** expenses are modeled as percents of revenue, so $\text{COGS}_t = c \cdot \text{Rev}_t$.
3. **Depreciation and amortization (D&A)** is usually driven by the asset base (next week) and entered as a separate line.
4. **EBIT** (earnings before interest and taxes) $= \text{Rev} - \text{COGS} - \text{SG\&A} - \text{D\&A}$.
5. **Interest** comes from the debt schedule; **EBT** (earnings before tax) $= \text{EBIT} - \text{interest}$.
6. **Tax** is the tax rate times EBT, and **net income** is EBT minus tax.

Taxes cannot be negative in this simple model: if EBT is below zero, tax is `0` and net income equals EBT. (Real rules for loss carryforwards are more detailed; ignore them here.)

### Percent of sales, and when it fails
Modeling a cost as a fixed percent of revenue assumes it moves in proportion to sales. That suits COGS for a retailer. It is wrong for a cost that is fixed in the short run, such as rent or salaries, and for depreciation, which depends on the assets already built, not on this year's sales. A good analyst checks each driver against history: compute **common-size** statements, where every line is divided by revenue, and see whether the percentages are stable.

### A worked forecast
Base revenue \$1,000 (thousands of dollars throughout); growth 10%, 8% and 5%; COGS 60% and SG&A 20% of revenue; D&A of 30, 32 and 34; interest 10; tax rate 25%.

| COGS | 660.00 | 712.80 | 748.44 |

| SG&A | 220.00 | 237.60 | 249.48 |

### In code
```python
def income_statement(revenue, cogs_pct, sga_pct, depreciation,
                     interest, tax_rate):
    gross_profit = revenue * (1 - cogs_pct)
    ebit = gross_profit - revenue * sga_pct - depreciation
    ebt = ebit - interest
    tax = max(0.0, ebt) * tax_rate
    return {"gross_profit": gross_profit, "ebit": ebit, "ebt": ebt,
            "tax": tax, "net_income": ebt - tax}
```

Note `max(0.0, ebt)`: a loss pays no tax. Percents are decimals (`0.60`), amounts are in one unit throughout (thousands here), and the function returns a dictionary so later steps can pick the line they need. Test it with a profit case and a loss case.

### Judging the forecast
A forecast is a set of beliefs. Ask whether growth is plausible against the industry and the company's history, whether margins are consistent with competitors, and whether a single assumption (growth held at 10% for ten years) quietly drives the answer. Next week's sensitivity tables put numbers on this.

@example
title: Building one year of the forecast
problem: Revenue was \$2,000 million last year and grows 8%. COGS is 62% and SG&A 18% of revenue, D&A is \$90 million, interest is \$25 million and the tax rate is 24%. Find net income.
step: Revenue: $2000 \times 1.08 = 2160$.
step: COGS $= 0.62 \times 2160 = 1339.2$ and SG&A $= 0.18 \times 2160 = 388.8$, so gross profit is $820.8$ and $EBIT = 820.8 - 388.8 - 90 = 342.0$.
step: EBT $= 342.0 - 25 = 317.0$ and tax $= 0.24 \times 317.0 = 76.08$.
step: Net income $= 317.0 - 76.08 = 240.92$ million.
answer: Net income is \$240.92 million.

@example
title: A loss year pays no tax
problem: Revenue is 100, COGS 70% and SG&A 30% of revenue, D&A 10, interest 5, tax rate 25%. Find net income.
step: Gross profit $= 100 \times 0.30 = 30$; EBIT $= 30 - 30 - 10 = -10$.
step: EBT $= -10 - 5 = -15$.
step: EBT is negative, so tax is `max(0.0, -15) * 0.25 = 0`.
step: Net income equals EBT: $-15$, not $-15 \times 0.75 = -11.25$, which would wrongly create a tax refund.
answer: Net income is -15.

@mistakes
- Entering growth as `8` instead of `0.08`, or mixing millions and thousands between lines.
- Applying the tax rate to a negative EBT, which creates a tax refund the model has no basis for.
- Linking depreciation to revenue when it depends on the assets already built.
- Overwriting an assumption inside a formula (a "hard-coded" `0.25`) so the driver table no longer controls the model.

```python
def forecast_revenue(base, growth_rates):
    """Revenue for each year, compounding the growth rates."""
    revenue = base
    path = []
    for g in growth_rates:

        path.append(revenue)
    return path
```

```python
def forecast_net_income(base_revenue, growth_rates, ebit_margin,
                        interest, tax_rate):
    """Net income for each year of a margin-based forecast."""
    revenue = base_revenue
    results = []
    for g in growth_rates:
        revenue *= 1 + g
        ebt = revenue * ebit_margin - interest
        tax = max(0.0, ebt) * tax_rate
        results.append(ebt - tax)
    return results
```
hint: Inside one loop, update revenue, compute EBT, then tax, then net income.
hint: EBT is `revenue * ebit_margin - interest`; use `max(0.0, ebt)` for the tax base.
solution: For each year compound revenue, compute EBIT from the margin, subtract the fixed interest, tax only a positive EBT, and append net income. A year with EBIT below interest is a loss with no tax.

```python
import math
stmt = {"revenue": 1600, "cogs": 960, "sga": 240, "net_income": 128}
got = common_size(stmt)
want = {"revenue": 100.0, "cogs": 60.0, "sga": 15.0, "net_income": 8.0}
```

model:
```python
def common_size(statement):
    """Each line as a percent of revenue."""
    revenue = statement["revenue"]
    if revenue == 0:
        raise ValueError("revenue is zero")
    return {name: 100 * amount / revenue for name, amount in statement.items()}
```
hint: Divide each amount by the revenue line and multiply by 100.
hint: A dictionary comprehension over `statement.items()` builds the new dictionary without changing the old one.
solution: Look up revenue, refuse to divide by zero, and build a new dictionary of `100 * amount / revenue`. Revenue maps to exactly 100, and a negative line (a loss) becomes a negative percent.
