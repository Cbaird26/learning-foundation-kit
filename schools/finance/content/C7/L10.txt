@article
Last week we saw that diversification removes company-specific risk and leaves **market risk**. Investors who hold diversified portfolios care how much a new asset adds to the portfolio's market risk, not how risky the asset is by itself. The **capital asset pricing model** (CAPM) turns that idea into a formula for the required return, which is also the **cost of equity** for a firm.

### Beta

The **beta** of asset $i$ measures its sensitivity to the market portfolio's return $R_m$:

$$\beta_i = \frac{\text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)}{\text{Var}(R_m)} = \rho_{im}\,\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_m}.$$

By definition the market portfolio has $\beta = 1$ and a risk-free asset has $\beta = 0$. A stock with $\beta = 1.5$ tends to move 1.5% when the market moves 1%, so it has more market risk than the average stock; a $\beta$ of 0.6 has less. Beta can be negative in principle. It is the **slope of the regression** of the asset's returns on the market's returns, so you can estimate it from a time series of paired returns, commonly five years of monthly or two years of weekly data and a broad stock index as the market proxy. Practitioners often adjust the raw estimate toward 1, for example $0.67 \times \text{raw} + 0.33 \times 1$, because estimates contain noise. Beta depends on both a firm's business and its leverage: more debt raises equity beta.

### The security market line

The CAPM says an asset's required (expected) return rises linearly with its beta:

$$E(R_i) = r_f + \beta_i\,[E(R_m) - r_f].$$

The line is the **security market line** (SML). $E(R_m) - r_f$ is the **market risk premium** (or equity risk premium). The model rests on assumptions: investors hold diversified portfolios, care only about expected return and standard deviation, share the same expectations, and can borrow and lend at the risk-free rate. A portfolio's beta is the weighted average of the betas of its holdings, with the risk-free asset counted at zero, and its expected return is $r_f + \beta_p \times$ the premium.

If an asset's expected return exceeds its CAPM return, it plots **above the SML** and has a positive **alpha**: it seems underpriced, since it offers more than its risk requires. If it falls below the SML, it seems overpriced.

### Estimating the inputs for a cost of equity

1. **Risk-free rate**: the yield on a government security whose maturity matches the cash flows being valued, often the 10-year Treasury yield (current values on the Treasury's website or FRED).
2. **Market risk premium**: estimated from long historical averages, from surveys or implied by market prices. Estimates differ across sources and over time, and there is no single right number.
3. **Beta**: from a regression, from a data vendor or from comparable firms.

In this lesson and in the problems that follow we use hypothetical inputs, such as a risk-free rate of 4% and a market premium of 5.5%. Always say which values you used, and remember that real values change.

### Limits

Empirical work finds that realized returns also relate to size, value and momentum, that betas move over time and depend on the data used, and that the market portfolio cannot be observed. The CAPM remains the standard way to estimate the cost of equity because it is simple and clearly connects required return to systematic risk.

@example
title: A cost of equity from the CAPM
problem: Marlowe Tools has an equity beta of 1.3. Suppose the risk-free rate is 4% and the market risk premium is 5.5% (hypothetical). What is the required return on its equity? If the stock's expected return from an analyst's model is 12%, is it above or below the SML?
step: $E(R) = 0.04 + 1.3 \times 0.055 = 0.04 + 0.0715 = 11.15\%$.
step: The expected return of 12% is above the required 11.15%, so the stock plots above the SML, with an alpha of $+0.85$ percentage points.
step: This says the model's forecast is more than the stock's risk requires, not that the stock will outperform: the forecast might be wrong.
answer: 11.15% required return; the stock is above the SML (alpha +0.85 points).

@example
title: Estimating beta from returns
problem: Over five periods a stock and the market returned as in the table. Estimate the stock's beta and its required return if $r_f = 4\%$ and the market premium is 5.5%.

| Period | Market | Stock |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4% | 8% |
| 2 | $-2\%$ | $-5\%$ |
| 3 | 8% | 14% |
| 4 | 1% | $-1\%$ |
| 5 | 6% | 7% |

step: The market's mean is 3.4% and the stock's mean is 4.6%. Market deviations: $+0.6, -5.4, +4.6, -2.4, +2.6$ points; stock deviations: $+3.4, -9.6, +9.4, -5.6, +2.4$.
step: Sample variance of the market: $\sum d^2/(n-1) = 0.00632/4 = 0.00158$. Sample covariance: the sum of products is $0.01168$, divided by 4 is $0.00292$.
step: $\beta = 0.00292/0.00158 = 1.848$ (the $n-1$ divisor cancels, so dividing sums of squares and products gives the same answer).
step: Required return: $0.04 + 1.848 \times 0.055 = 14.16\%$. Five observations are far too few for a reliable beta; real estimates use many more.
answer: Beta about 1.848; required return about 14.16%.

@mistakes
- Using total risk (standard deviation) rather than beta in the CAPM: unsystematic risk earns no premium.
- Inserting the market return instead of the market risk premium (return minus the risk-free rate) in the formula.
- Forgetting that the risk-free asset has beta zero when computing a portfolio's beta.
- Treating an estimated beta as exact: it depends on the sample period, the index and the frequency.

model:
```python
def beta(stock_returns, market_returns):
    """Slope of stock returns on market returns (sample cov / sample var)."""
    n = len(market_returns)
    mean_s = sum(stock_returns) / n
    mean_m = sum(market_returns) / n
    cov = sum((s - mean_s) * (m - mean_m)
              for s, m in zip(stock_returns, market_returns)) / (n - 1)
    var = sum((m - mean_m) ** 2 for m in market_returns) / (n - 1)
    return cov / var
```
hint: Compute both means first, then sum the products of deviations and the squared market deviations.
hint: The divisors of $n - 1$ cancel in the ratio, but write them out to match the definitions.
solution: Compute the mean of each series. The covariance is the sum of products of paired deviations divided by $n - 1$, the market variance is the sum of squared market deviations divided by $n - 1$, and beta is their ratio. A stock with returns exactly twice the market's has beta 2, the market against itself has beta 1, and a stock that moves opposite the market has a negative beta.
