@article
A **bond** is a loan packaged as a security. The issuer promises to pay the holder a stream of **coupon** payments and to repay the **face value** (also called **par value** or principal) at **maturity**. Because the promised cash flows are fixed, a bond's value is a direct application of the annuity and single-sum formulas from weeks 2 and 3.

### Terms and conventions

The **coupon rate** is the annual coupon divided by face value. A bond with a 5% coupon and \$1,000 face value pays \$50 a year, which is \$25 every six months if it pays **semiannual** coupons, as most US bonds do. The **yield to maturity** (YTM) is the single discount rate that equates the present value of the promised cash flows to the bond's price: it is the bond's internal rate of return if held to maturity and all payments are made. In this lesson: face value is \$1,000, prices are in dollars per bond, valuation is on a coupon date (so there is no accrued interest), and the YTM is a stated annual rate compounded at the coupon frequency, so a semiannual bond uses $y/2$ per half-year. The bond's issuer is assumed not to default.

### The pricing formula

With $m$ payments per year, $n$ total payments, coupon payment $C = (\text{coupon rate} \times F)/m$ and periodic yield $i = y/m$,

$$P = C\,\frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i} + \frac{F}{(1+i)^{n}}.$$

The first term is the present value of the coupons (an ordinary annuity) and the second is the present value of the principal. A **zero-coupon bond** has $C = 0$ and sells at a discount, $P = F/(1+i)^n$.

### Price and yield move in opposite directions

When market yields rise above a bond's coupon rate, its fixed payments are less attractive, so its price falls below par: it sells at a **discount**. When yields fall below the coupon rate, it sells at a **premium**. When $y$ equals the coupon rate, the bond sells at **par**. As a bond approaches maturity its price converges to par (assuming no default). The **current yield** is annual coupon divided by price, and it ignores the gain or loss at maturity.

### Interest rate risk and duration

Prices respond more to yield changes for bonds with longer maturities and lower coupons. **Macaulay duration** is the weighted average time to receive the cash flows, using present values as weights; a zero-coupon bond's Macaulay duration equals its maturity. **Modified duration** is Macaulay duration divided by $1 + y/m$, and it gives a first-order estimate:

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -\,D_{mod}\,\Delta y.$$

The estimate is good for small changes. The true price curve is convex, so the estimate understates the gain when yields fall and overstates the loss when yields rise.

### Pricing in code

Writing the pricing formula as a sum over every payment date avoids dividing by zero when the yield is zero and shows the cash flows clearly. A bond is a list of dated cash flows, and its price is their discounted sum:

```python
def price_from_flows(flows, rate):
    """flows[t-1] is the cash flow at period t = 1, 2, ..."""
    return sum(cf / (1 + rate) ** t for t, cf in enumerate(flows, start=1))


print(round(price_from_flows([50, 50, 1050], 0.05), 2))  # 3 payments
```

The loop discounts the first payment once, the second twice and the last three times, then adds the three present values: the call prints `1000.0` because a 5% coupon bond at a 5% periodic yield sells at par.

(Yields and prices of actual bonds change daily; to see current Treasury yields, use the Treasury's website or FRED.)

@example
title: Pricing a coupon bond and testing the yield sensitivity
problem: A 6-year bond has a 5% coupon paid semiannually and a face value of \$1,000. The YTM is 6.4% (stated annual, semiannual compounding). Find the price, then the price if the yield rises or falls by one percentage point.
step: Per half-year: coupon $C = 25$, $i = 0.064/2 = 0.032$, $n = 12$.
step: PV of coupons: $25 \times (1 - 1.032^{-12})/0.032 = 25 \times 9.8362 = \$245.91$. PV of principal: $1{,}000/1.032^{12} = \$685.24$. Price $= \$931.15$, a discount, because the yield exceeds the coupon rate.
step: At 7.4% the price is \$885.39, a fall of 4.91%. At 5.4% the price is \$979.73, a rise of 5.22%.
step: The bond's modified duration is about 5.06, so the duration estimate is $\pm 5.06\%$. The actual gain (5.22%) exceeds the actual loss (4.91%) because of convexity.
answer: \$931.15; \$885.39 at 7.4% (down 4.91%); \$979.73 at 5.4% (up 5.22%).

@example
title: Finding a yield to maturity
problem: A 4-year bond with a 4% semiannual coupon and \$1,000 face value sells for \$962.50. What are its YTM and current yield?
step: Per half-year: coupon \$20, $n = 8$, price \$962.50. We need $i$ in $962.50 = 20\,\dfrac{1 - (1+i)^{-8}}{i} + \dfrac{1{,}000}{(1+i)^8}$. There is no closed-form solution, so use a financial calculator, a spreadsheet's rate function or trial and error.
step: Trying $i = 2.5\%$ gives \$964.15 (a little too high, so $i$ must be a bit higher). Trying $i = 2.55\%$ gives about \$960.65. Interpolating, the price \$962.50 is reached at $i \approx 2.5235\%$.
step: The YTM is the stated annual rate $2 \times 2.5235\% = 5.05\%$. The current yield is $40/962.50 = 4.16\%$, below the YTM because the bond also gains \$37.50 as it moves to par.
answer: YTM about 5.05%; current yield 4.16%.

@mistakes
- Using the annual yield and annual number of years with semiannual coupons: use $y/2$ and $2 \times$ years, and a coupon of half the annual coupon.
- Believing a high coupon rate means a high return: the return from buying at the market price is the YTM.
- Reversing the price-yield relationship, or forgetting that a bond sells at a discount when its coupon rate is below the YTM.
- Treating a bond's coupon rate as its YTM when the bond is not trading at par.

model:
```python
def bond_price(face, coupon_rate, ytm, years, freq=2):
    """Price of a bond on a coupon date (sum of discounted cash flows)."""
    periods = round(years * freq)
    coupon = face * coupon_rate / freq
    rate = ytm / freq
    price = 0.0
    for t in range(1, periods + 1):
        cash_flow = coupon + (face if t == periods else 0)
        price += cash_flow / (1 + rate) ** t
    return price
```
hint: Loop over the payment dates $t = 1, \ldots, n$ and discount each cash flow by $(1+i)^t$.
hint: The last cash flow is the coupon plus the face value.
solution: Compute the number of periods, the coupon per period and the periodic yield. Each cash flow is the coupon, plus the face value on the last date, divided by $(1+i)^t$. Summing the discounted flows avoids the annuity formula's division by zero when the yield is zero (the price is then just the total of all payments).
