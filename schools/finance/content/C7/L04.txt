@article
A lender may say "9% a year," a bank "8.6% APY," and a bond dealer "a 4% semiannual yield." These numbers describe different things, and the TVM formulas work only with the **periodic rate** that matches the length of each period. This week is about getting that rate right.

### Three kinds of annual rate

A **stated annual rate**, also called a **nominal rate** or **APR** (annual percentage rate), is a periodic rate times the number of periods per year $m$. It does not include the effect of compounding. The **periodic rate** is $r_s/m$. The **effective annual rate** (EAR, called APY on deposit accounts in the United States) is the actual one-year growth:

$$\text{EAR} = \left(1 + \frac{r_s}{m}\right)^{m} - 1.$$

For a stated rate of 12% compounded monthly the periodic rate is 1% and $\text{EAR} = 1.01^{12} - 1 = 12.68\%$. Lenders of consumer credit in the United States must disclose an APR, which is a stated rate (with certain fees included), so a borrower should convert it to an EAR to compare it with a rate quoted another way. Conventions: we take 365 days in a year when compounding daily; some loans use a 360-day year.

### Continuous compounding

As $m$ grows without bound, $(1 + r_s/m)^m \to e^{r_s}$. With **continuous compounding** at stated rate $r_s$,

$$FV = PV\,e^{r_s t}, \qquad \text{EAR} = e^{r_s} - 1.$$

### From an EAR to a periodic rate

To change the period, convert through the EAR. The rate per period that is $1/k$ of a year long is

$$r_{period} = (1 + \text{EAR})^{1/k} - 1.$$

**Matching payments and compounding.** If cash flows come every quarter but interest compounds monthly, find the **effective quarterly rate**, $(1 + r_s/12)^3 - 1$, and use it as $r$ in the annuity formulas with $n$ counting quarters. Never use the monthly rate with quarterly payments.

### Nominal and real rates

A **nominal rate** is measured in money. A **real rate** is measured in purchasing power: it is the growth in what the money can buy. With inflation rate $i$, the exact relation (the **Fisher equation**) is

$$1 + r_{nominal} = (1 + r_{real})(1 + i), \quad\text{so}\quad r_{real} = \frac{1 + r_{nominal}}{1 + i} - 1.$$

The common approximation $r_{real} \approx r_{nominal} - i$ is close when rates are low. Do not mix: discount nominal cash flows at a nominal rate and real cash flows at a real rate. (Current interest and inflation figures change: look them up on FRED, the St. Louis Fed's data site.)

### Amortization in code

The week 3 payment formula and the interest-on-balance rule combine into a schedule. We trace the loop: each month the interest is the rate times the opening balance, the principal paid is the payment minus interest, and the balance falls by the principal.

@example
title: Quarterly deposits and monthly compounding
problem: You deposit \$600 at the end of every quarter for 10 years into an account with a stated rate of 6% a year compounded monthly. What is the balance after 10 years?
step: Deposits are quarterly but compounding is monthly, so find the effective quarterly rate: $(1 + 0.06/12)^3 - 1 = 1.005^3 - 1 = 0.0150751$, or 1.5075% a quarter.
step: There are $10 \times 4 = 40$ quarterly deposits at the end of each quarter.
step: $FV = 600 \times \dfrac{1.015075^{40} - 1}{0.015075} = 600 \times 54.3542 = \$32{,}612.53$.
answer: \$32,612.53.

@example
title: A real rate of return
problem: A bond pays a nominal effective annual rate of 7.5%, and inflation is expected to be 3.1% a year (hypothetical figures). What real rate does an investor earn, and how far off is the simple subtraction?
step: Apply the Fisher equation: $r_{real} = 1.075/1.031 - 1$.
step: $1.075/1.031 = 1.042677$, so the real rate is 4.27%.
step: Simple subtraction gives $7.5\% - 3.1\% = 4.40\%$, about 0.13 percentage points too high.
answer: 4.27% (the approximation gives 4.40%).

@mistakes
- Treating the APR as an effective rate, and so understating the true cost of a loan with frequent compounding.
- Dividing the EAR by $m$ to get a periodic rate; the correct step is $(1 + \text{EAR})^{1/m} - 1$.
- Using the monthly rate in an annuity whose payments are quarterly or annual.
- Discounting nominal cash flows with a real rate, or real cash flows with a nominal rate.

model:
```python
def amortization_schedule(principal, annual_rate, months):
    """Level-payment schedule: (month, payment, interest, principal, balance)."""
    rate = annual_rate / 12
    if rate == 0:
        payment = principal / months
    else:
        payment = principal * rate / (1 - (1 + rate) ** -months)
    balance = principal
    rows = []
    for month in range(1, months + 1):
        interest = balance * rate
        principal_paid = payment - interest
        balance -= principal_paid
        rows.append((month, payment, interest, principal_paid, balance))
    return rows
```
hint: Compute the payment first, then loop month by month, updating the balance.
hint: Each row needs the interest on the opening balance, the principal paid (payment minus interest) and the new balance.
solution: The payment comes from the level-payment formula (or `principal / months` at a zero rate). In each pass of the loop, interest is the opening balance times the monthly rate, the principal paid is the payment minus interest, and the balance falls by the principal paid. The last balance is zero up to floating-point error, which is why the test uses a small tolerance.

choice*: The EAR is higher with monthly compounding than with annual compounding, but it can never exceed $e^{0.08} - 1$, which is about 8.33%.
choice: The EAR is always 8%, because the stated rate is the annual rate.
choice: The EAR is 8% divided by the number of compounding periods per year.
choice: The EAR falls as compounding becomes more frequent, because each period's rate is smaller.
hint: Compare 8% compounded annually, monthly, and continuously.
solution: The EAR is $1.08 - 1 = 8\%$ with annual compounding, $1.00667^{12} - 1 = 8.30\%$ with monthly, and $e^{0.08} - 1 = 8.33\%$ with continuous compounding, so more frequent compounding raises the EAR toward a ceiling.
