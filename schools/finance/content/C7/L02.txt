@article
Would you rather have \$1,000 today or \$1,000 in five years? Nearly everyone picks today, even with no inflation and no risk, because \$1,000 today can be invested and will grow. Interest is the price of time, and the **time value of money** is the idea that a sum of money has a value that depends on when it is received. Every valuation in this course, from bonds to projects to whole companies, rests on it.

### Conventions used in this lesson

Unless stated otherwise: the interest rate $r$ is an **effective annual rate**, interest is compounded once a year, cash flows occur at the **end** of a year, and $n$ counts whole years. Time 0 is today.

### Future value and compounding

If you invest $PV$ today at rate $r$ per year, interest earns interest. After one year you have $PV(1+r)$, after two $PV(1+r)^2$, and after $n$ years

$$FV = PV\,(1+r)^{n}.$$

$(1+r)^n$ is the **future value factor**. Growth with interest on interest is **compound growth**; interest only on the original amount is **simple interest**, which gives $PV(1 + rn)$. Over long horizons compounding makes a large difference: at 6% a year, \$1 grows to \$1.79 in 10 years and to \$5.74 in 30.

### Present value and discounting

Reverse the process to find what a future sum is worth today:

$$PV = \frac{FV}{(1+r)^{n}} = FV\,(1+r)^{-n}.$$

This is **discounting**, $r$ is then called the **discount rate**, and $(1+r)^{-n}$ is the **discount factor**. A higher discount rate or a longer wait lowers the present value. Present value is the single most important tool in finance because it turns cash flows at different dates into one number at time 0 that can be compared and added.

### The rule: compare only at one date

Never add or compare amounts at different dates. Move them to a common date, usually time 0, with a rate that reflects the opportunity cost of the money. A timeline (a line marked 0, 1, 2, ... with each cash flow above its date) is the best way to avoid errors.

### Solving for the rate and for time

With three of the four values $PV$, $FV$, $r$, $n$, solve for the fourth:

$$r = \left(\frac{FV}{PV}\right)^{1/n} - 1, \qquad n = \frac{\ln(FV/PV)}{\ln(1+r)}.$$

The first gives a **compound annual growth rate** (CAGR). The **rule of 72** says money doubles in about $72/r$ years when $r$ is written as a percent: a handy mental estimate, accurate for rates around 6% to 10%. The exact doubling time is $\ln 2 / \ln(1+r)$.

### Changing rates

If the rate differs by year, multiply the factors: $FV = PV(1+r_1)(1+r_2)\cdots(1+r_n)$. Do not average the rates and then use $n$ periods unless the question asks for a geometric average.

@example
title: Growing a lump sum
problem: You deposit \$8,000 in an account that pays 5.5% a year, compounded annually. How much is in the account after 7 years?
step: The factor is $(1.055)^7 = 1.454679$.
step: $FV = 8{,}000 \times 1.454679 = \$11{,}637.43$.
step: Interest earned is $11{,}637.43 - 8{,}000 = \$3{,}637.43$. Simple interest would give only $8{,}000 \times 0.055 \times 7 = \$3{,}080$, so compounding adds about \$557.
answer: \$11,637.43.

@example
title: What rate did the investment earn?
problem: An investment of \$12,000 grew to \$19,500 over 9 years with no other cash flows. What was its compound annual growth rate? How long would the same rate take to double the money, and how close is the rule of 72?
step: Divide: $19{,}500 / 12{,}000 = 1.625$.
step: Take the 9th root and subtract 1: $r = 1.625^{1/9} - 1 = 0.055427$, or 5.54% a year.
step: Doubling time: $n = \ln 2 / \ln 1.055427 = 0.693147 / 0.053945 = 12.85$ years.
step: The rule of 72 gives $72 / 5.54 = 13.0$ years, close to the exact value.
answer: 5.54% a year; about 12.8 years to double (the rule of 72 gives 13.0).

@mistakes
- Using the rate as a percent in the formula (5.5 instead of 0.055).
- Discounting by multiplying: finding $PV$ with $FV(1+r)^n$ instead of dividing.
- Forgetting the final subtraction of 1 when solving for the rate, and reporting a growth factor such as 1.0544.
- Adding cash flows from different dates before moving them to a common date.

@quiz self
prompt: Use the rule of 72 to estimate how long money takes to double at 9% a year, then compute the exact time with logarithms. State both answers and say why the rule is only an approximation.
hint: The rule gives $72/9$. The exact time solves $1.09^n = 2$.
solution: The rule of 72 gives $72/9 = 8$ years. The exact time is $n = \ln 2 / \ln 1.09 = 0.693147 / 0.086178 = 8.04$ years, so the rule is accurate here. It is an approximation because $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$ and $\ln(1+r) \approx r$ only for small $r$: the rule works best for rates near 8% and drifts for very low or very high rates.
rubric: Gives 8 years from the rule of 72 and about 8.04 years from the exact formula.
rubric: States the exact formula $n = \ln 2 / \ln(1+r)$.
rubric: Explains that the rule rests on an approximation of the logarithm that holds best at moderate rates.
