@article
Investors do not know what an asset will earn. They know a range of outcomes with different chances, and they demand higher expected returns for bearing more uncertainty. Finance turns this into numbers: expected return as the measure of reward, and standard deviation or beta as measures of risk.

### Returns

The **holding period return** on an asset over one period is
$$R = \frac{P_1 - P_0 + D_1}{P_0},$$
the price change plus any income, divided by the starting price. Over several periods, the **arithmetic average** is the simple mean of the returns, the best estimate of a typical single period's expected return. The **geometric average** is the constant per-period return that gives the same final wealth:
$$R_G = \left[\prod_{t=1}^{n}(1+R_t)\right]^{1/n} - 1.$$
It is never above the arithmetic average and falls further below it when returns are more volatile. Use it to describe what an investor actually earned over a period.

### Expected return and risk from scenarios

If outcome $s$ has probability $p_s$ and return $R_s$, the **expected return** is $E(R) = \sum p_s R_s$, the **variance** is $\sigma^2 = \sum p_s [R_s - E(R)]^2$, and the **standard deviation** is $\sigma = \sqrt{\sigma^2}$. From a sample of $n$ historical returns, the **sample variance** divides by $n - 1$:
$$s^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(R_t - \bar R)^2.$$
Standard deviation is in the same units as returns (percent), which is why we usually quote it. The returns here are hypothetical; actual historical data are available from public sources such as FRED, and the long historical record shows that assets with higher average returns, such as stocks over bonds, have also had higher volatility.

### Portfolios

For a portfolio with weights $w_i$ that sum to 1, the expected return is the weighted average, $E(R_p) = \sum w_i E(R_i)$. **Risk is not a weighted average.** For two assets,
$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\,\rho_{12}\,\sigma_1\sigma_2,$$
where the **correlation** $\rho_{12} = \text{Cov}(R_1,R_2)/(\sigma_1\sigma_2)$ lies between $-1$ and $+1$. Only when $\rho = +1$ is the portfolio standard deviation the weighted average. For any lower correlation it is less: this is the benefit of **diversification**.

### Systematic and unsystematic risk

Adding many assets removes risk specific to individual firms (a lawsuit, a product failure, a strike): **unsystematic**, **idiosyncratic** or **diversifiable** risk. What remains is **systematic** or **market** risk that affects all assets (recessions, interest rate changes, wars). Because investors can diversify away the first kind at almost no cost, markets do not pay a premium for it. Only systematic risk is rewarded, and next week's measure of systematic risk is **beta**.

@example
title: Expected return and risk of one stock
problem: Alder Co.'s return next year depends on the economy: in a recession (probability 25%) it returns $-12\%$, in a normal year (50%) 8%, and in a boom (25%) 26%. Find the expected return, variance and standard deviation.
step: $E(R) = 0.25(-0.12) + 0.50(0.08) + 0.25(0.26) = -0.03 + 0.04 + 0.065 = 7.5\%$.
step: Deviations from 7.5%: $-0.195$, $+0.005$ and $+0.185$.
step: $\sigma^2 = 0.25(0.195)^2 + 0.50(0.005)^2 + 0.25(0.185)^2 = 0.009506 + 0.0000125 + 0.008556 = 0.018075$.
step: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.018075} = 13.44\%$.
answer: Expected return 7.5%; variance 0.018075; standard deviation 13.44%.

@example
title: A two-asset portfolio
problem: A portfolio holds 60% in stock fund X (expected return 9%, standard deviation 20%) and 40% in bond fund Y (expected return 5%, standard deviation 8%). The correlation of their returns is 0.3. Find the portfolio's expected return and standard deviation.
step: $E(R_p) = 0.6(9\%) + 0.4(5\%) = 5.4\% + 2.0\% = 7.4\%$.
step: $\sigma_p^2 = (0.6 \times 0.20)^2 + (0.4 \times 0.08)^2 + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.3)(0.20)(0.08)$ $= 0.0144 + 0.001024 + 0.002304 = 0.017728$.
step: $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.017728} = 13.31\%$, below the weighted average of the two standard deviations, $0.6(20\%) + 0.4(8\%) = 15.2\%$. Diversification removes about 1.9 percentage points of risk.
answer: Expected return 7.4%; standard deviation 13.31%.

@mistakes
- Averaging standard deviations by weight: portfolio risk depends on correlations and is below the weighted average unless $\rho = 1$.
- Dividing by $n$ instead of $n - 1$ when estimating variance from a sample of historical returns (but using the probability weights, not $n - 1$, for scenarios).
- Treating the arithmetic average of past returns as the growth rate of wealth: the geometric average is.
- Believing that total risk is rewarded: only systematic risk earns a premium.

prompt: A stock's return depends on three states of the economy: weak (probability 20%) with a return of $-15\%$, average (50%) with 6%, and strong (30%) with 20%. What is its expected return, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 6.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (0.2 * -0.15 + 0.5 * 0.06 + 0.3 * 0.20) * 100
hint: Multiply each return by its probability and add.

@practice num
prompt: A fund returned $+20\%$, $-10\%$ and $+15\%$ in three consecutive years. What was its geometric average annual return, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 7.49
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1.20 * 0.90 * 1.15) ** (1 / 3) - 1) * 100
hint: Multiply the growth factors 1.20, 0.90 and 1.15 and take the cube root.
solution: The product is $1.20 \times 0.90 \times 1.15 = 1.242$, and $1.242^{1/3} - 1 = 7.49\%$. The arithmetic average is $(20 - 10 + 15)/3 = 8.33\%$, higher.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio holds 50% in asset A (standard deviation 25%) and 50% in asset B (standard deviation 15%). The correlation between their returns is 0.2. What is the portfolio's standard deviation, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 15.81
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: sqrt((0.5 * 0.25) ** 2 + (0.5 * 0.15) ** 2 + 2 * 0.5 * 0.5 * 0.2 * 0.25 * 0.15) * 100
hint: Use the two-asset variance formula with $w_1 = w_2 = 0.5$ and $\rho = 0.2$.
solution: $\sigma_p^2 = (0.125)^2 + (0.075)^2 + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.2)(0.25)(0.15) = 0.015625 + 0.005625 + 0.00375 = 0.025$. So $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.025} = 15.81\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A portfolio holds two stocks in equal amounts. Each stock has a standard deviation of 30%. For which correlation between the two stocks is the portfolio's standard deviation lowest?
choice: $+1.0$
choice: $+0.5$
choice: $0$
choice*: $-0.4$
hint: The covariance term in the portfolio variance gets smaller as the correlation falls.
solution: With equal weights and equal standard deviations, $\sigma_p^2 = 0.045(1 + \rho)$, so $\sigma_p = 30\%\sqrt{(1+\rho)/2}$, which is 30% at $\rho = 1$, 26% at 0.5, 21.2% at 0 and 16.4% at $-0.4$. Lower correlation means more diversification.

@quiz num
prompt: A stock had annual returns of 10%, $-4\%$, 18%, 7% and 14% over five years. Estimate the standard deviation of its returns from this sample (dividing by $n - 1$), as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 8.37
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: statistics.stdev([0.10, -0.04, 0.18, 0.07, 0.14]) * 100
hint: The mean is 9%; square each deviation from the mean and add them up.
solution: Deviations from the mean 9%: $+1, -13, +9, -2, +5$ percentage points. Squares: $1, 169, 81, 4, 25$, sum 280. Divide by $n - 1 = 4$ to get 70 and take the square root: $\sqrt{70} = 8.37\%$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements about risk are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Holding many stocks eliminates most firm-specific (unsystematic) risk.
choice*: A stock's geometric average return over several years is no larger than its arithmetic average.
choice*: A portfolio's standard deviation is less than the weighted average of its assets' standard deviations when their correlation is below 1.
choice: A well-diversified investor is rewarded for bearing the unsystematic risk of each stock held.
choice: A portfolio's expected return is below the weighted average of its assets' expected returns when correlations are low.
hint: Separate what diversification does to expected return from what it does to risk.
solution: Diversification removes firm-specific risk, so it earns no premium. The geometric average never exceeds the arithmetic average, and portfolio risk is below the weighted average when $\rho < 1$. A portfolio's expected return is always exactly the weighted average of the expected returns, whatever the correlation.
