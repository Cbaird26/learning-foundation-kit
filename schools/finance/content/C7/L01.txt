@article
Suppose a bakery owner can choose between two oven models. One is cheap now but costs more to run; the other costs more now but saves money for years. Every such choice trades cash today for cash later, and corporate finance gives the tools to decide. Before using those tools, we need to say what the decision-maker is trying to achieve.

### The goal of the firm

The standard goal of corporate finance is to **maximize the current market value of the owners' equity**: for a corporation with publicly traded shares, to maximize the share price. This goal is better than "maximize profit" for three reasons. A share price reflects the **timing** of cash flows, not just their size. It reflects **risk**, because investors pay less for uncertain cash flows. And it reflects **cash flows to owners**, not accounting profit, which can be changed by accounting choices.

The goal is a benchmark, not a licence to ignore the law or ethics. Firms also have obligations to customers, employees, lenders and society, and some managers pursue other objectives. Finance asks what value-maximizing action would be and what a different action would cost shareholders.

### Agency problems

In a large corporation the owners (shareholders, the **principals**) hire managers (the **agents**) to run the firm. Managers may prefer a quiet life, large perks or empire-building to maximizing value. This is the **agency problem**, and the cost of managing it is an **agency cost**. Common remedies are:

- **Compensation** tied to long-run share performance, such as stock grants that vest over several years.
- **Board oversight** by directors independent of management.
- **The market for corporate control**: poorly run firms can be acquired and their managers replaced.
- **Debt covenants and monitoring** by lenders.

### The financial statements

Three statements describe a firm. The **income statement** covers a period:

$$\text{Net income} = \text{Revenue} - \text{Cost of goods sold} - \text{Operating expenses} - \text{Depreciation} - \text{Interest} - \text{Taxes}.$$

Revenue minus cost of goods sold, operating expenses and depreciation is **EBIT** (earnings before interest and taxes). The **balance sheet** is a snapshot at one date and always satisfies

$$\text{Assets} = \text{Liabilities} + \text{Owners' equity}.$$

**Net working capital** is current assets minus current liabilities, and the **current ratio** is current assets divided by current liabilities. The **statement of cash flows** reconciles profit with cash. Under the **indirect method**, cash flow from operations is

$$\text{CFO} = \text{Net income} + \text{Depreciation} - \Delta\text{Receivables} - \Delta\text{Inventory} + \Delta\text{Payables},$$

where each $\Delta$ is this year's change in the balance. Depreciation is added back because it reduces income but spends no cash. A rise in receivables or inventory uses cash, and a rise in payables provides it. This is a simplified form: real statements also adjust for other non-cash items.

### Profit is not cash

A firm that sells on credit books revenue when it delivers, but receives cash later. A firm can show a profit and still run short of cash if receivables and inventory grow quickly. Finance values **cash flows**, so most of this course works with cash.

### Return on equity and the DuPont decomposition

**Return on equity** is $\text{ROE} = \text{Net income} / \text{Equity}$. The **DuPont decomposition** splits it into three drivers:

$$\text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Sales}} \times \frac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Assets}} \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}.$$

These are the **net profit margin**, the **total asset turnover** and the **equity multiplier** (a measure of leverage). Conventions in this lesson: statements are in thousands of dollars, and ratios use year-end balances unless stated.

@example
title: Reading Larkspur Outfitters' statements
problem: Larkspur Outfitters (amounts in thousands of dollars) reports revenue \$8,400, cost of goods sold \$5,040, operating expenses \$1,640, depreciation \$320, interest \$200 and a tax rate of 25% of pretax income. Its year-end balance sheet is below. Compute net income, net working capital, the current ratio and ROE.

| Assets | | Liabilities and equity | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 600 | Accounts payable | 700 |
| Receivables | 900 | Accrued expenses | 300 |
| Inventory | 1,300 | Current portion of debt | 400 |
| **Current assets** | **2,800** | **Current liabilities** | **1,400** |
| Net plant and equipment | 4,200 | Long-term debt | 2,500 |
| | | Owners' equity | 3,100 |
| **Total assets** | **7,000** | **Total liabilities and equity** | **7,000** |

step: Build the income statement from the top. EBIT is $8{,}400 - 5{,}040 - 1{,}640 - 320 = 1{,}400$. Subtract interest to get pretax income $1{,}400 - 200 = 1{,}200$. Taxes are $0.25 \times 1{,}200 = 300$, so net income is $1{,}200 - 300 = \$900$ thousand.
step: Check the balance sheet: liabilities are $1{,}400 + 2{,}500 = 3{,}900$, and $3{,}900 + 3{,}100 = 7{,}000$, which equals total assets.
step: Net working capital is $2{,}800 - 1{,}400 = \$1{,}400$ thousand. The current ratio is $2{,}800 / 1{,}400 = 2.0$ times.
step: ROE uses year-end equity: $900 / 3{,}100 = 29.03\%$.
answer: Net income \$900 thousand; net working capital \$1,400 thousand; current ratio 2.0; ROE 29.03%.

@example
title: Why profit and cash differ
problem: In the same year Larkspur's receivables rose by \$150 thousand, inventory rose by \$100 thousand and accounts payable rose by \$50 thousand. Net income was \$900 thousand and depreciation \$320 thousand. Find cash flow from operations with the simplified indirect method.
step: Start with net income and add back the non-cash charge: $900 + 320 = 1{,}220$.
step: Subtract the cash tied up in customers and stock: $1{,}220 - 150 - 100 = 970$.
step: Add the cash retained by paying suppliers later: $970 + 50 = 1{,}020$.
step: CFO is \$1,020 thousand, \$120 thousand more than net income, because depreciation is larger than the net build-up of working capital.
answer: \$1,020 thousand.

@mistakes
- Equating "maximize profit" with "maximize shareholder value": profit ignores timing, risk and the cash actually paid to owners.
- Forgetting to add back depreciation when moving from net income to operating cash flow, or adding it back twice.
- Treating a rise in receivables or inventory as a source of cash: it is a use of cash.
- Mixing year-end and average balances without saying which, so that ROE and turnover ratios cannot be compared.

@quiz self

solution: Net income is an accounting measure that records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, not when cash moves. I would check cash flow from operations on the statement of cash flows, the changes in receivables, inventory and payables, and the current ratio and cash balance. A large gap between net income and operating cash flow is a warning sign.
