@article
Most real cash flows come in streams: rent, a car loan, a pension, a bond's coupons. Valuing each payment separately would be tedious, so for streams of equal payments we use short formulas, all built from the single-sum formula of last week.

### Conventions

An **ordinary annuity** pays a level amount $C$ at the **end** of each period for $n$ periods. An **annuity due** pays at the **start** of each period. The rate $r$ in every formula is the rate **per period**: for monthly payments on a loan with a stated annual rate (APR) compounded monthly, $r = \text{APR}/12$ and $n$ is the number of months. Unless stated, payments are at period ends.

### Ordinary annuity

The present value is the sum of discounted payments, which simplifies to

$$PV = C\,\frac{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}{r}, \qquad FV = C\,\frac{(1+r)^{n} - 1}{r}.$$

The fraction in the first formula is the **annuity factor**. For \$1,000 a year for 5 years at 8%, the factor is $(1 - 1.08^{-5})/0.08 = 3.9927$, so $PV = \$3{,}992.71$. For an **annuity due**, every payment comes one period earlier, so each value is multiplied by $(1+r)$: $PV_{due} = PV_{ordinary}\,(1+r)$ and likewise for $FV$.

### Perpetuities

A **perpetuity** pays $C$ at the end of every period forever. Letting $n \to \infty$ in the annuity formula gives

$$PV = \frac{C}{r}.$$

A **growing perpetuity** has its first payment $C_1$ one period from now, and each later payment is $g$ larger than the one before, with $g < r$:

$$PV = \frac{C_1}{r - g}.$$

Note that $C_1$ is the first payment, not the payment today. If $g \ge r$ the formula fails because the value would be infinite. We use it for stocks in week 6.

### Loan amortization

A level loan is the present value of its payments, so for loan amount $L$, rate $r$ per period and $n$ payments the payment is

$$C = \frac{L\,r}{1 - (1+r)^{-n}}.$$

Each payment covers **interest** on the opening balance, $\text{interest}_t = r \times \text{balance}_{t-1}$, and the rest, $C - \text{interest}_t$, is **principal** that reduces the balance. Because the balance falls, interest falls and principal rises each period. An **amortization schedule** lists these for each payment.

The **remaining balance** just after payment $k$ is the present value of the remaining payments:

$$B_k = C\,\frac{1 - (1+r)^{-(n-k)}}{r}.$$

In practice lenders round the payment to cents and adjust the last payment. A function in Python is a good way to avoid slips; here is the payment formula as code, with a guard for a zero rate:

```python
def loan_payment(principal, annual_rate, months):
    """Level monthly payment, rate compounded monthly, paid at month end."""
    r = annual_rate / 12
    if r == 0:
        return principal / months
    return principal * r / (1 - (1 + r) ** -months)
```

@example
title: A five-year car loan
problem: You borrow \$18,000 for 60 months at 6% APR compounded monthly, with payments at the end of each month. Find the payment, the interest and principal in the first three payments, and the total interest paid.
step: The monthly rate is $0.06/12 = 0.005$. $C = 18{,}000 \times 0.005 / (1 - 1.005^{-60}) = 90 / 0.258628 = \$347.99$.
step: Payment 1: interest $0.005 \times 18{,}000 = \$90.00$, principal $347.99 - 90.00 = \$257.99$, balance \$17,742.01. Payment 2: interest $0.005 \times 17{,}742.01 = \$88.71$, principal \$259.28, balance \$17,482.73. Payment 3: interest \$87.41, principal \$260.58, balance \$17,222.15.
step: Total paid over 60 months is about $347.99 \times 60 = \$20{,}879.40$ (the unrounded payment gives \$20,879.43), so interest is about \$2,879.43.
answer: Payment \$347.99; interest \$90.00, \$88.71, \$87.41 in payments 1 to 3; total interest about \$2,879.43.

@example
title: Saving at the start of each year
problem: Starting today, you deposit \$5,000 at the beginning of each year for 20 years (20 deposits) in an account paying 7% a year. What is the balance at the end of year 20, one year after the last deposit?
step: Treat it as an ordinary annuity first: $FV = 5{,}000 \times (1.07^{20} - 1)/0.07 = 5{,}000 \times 40.9955 = \$204{,}977.46$.
step: Deposits are at the start of each year, so each earns one more year of interest: multiply by $1.07$.
step: $FV_{due} = 204{,}977.46 \times 1.07 = \$219{,}325.88$.
answer: \$219,325.88.

@mistakes
- Using the annuity formulas for an annuity due without multiplying by $(1+r)$, or doing so for an ordinary annuity.
- Using the annual rate with monthly payments: the rate must match the payment period ($\text{APR}/12$, with $n$ in months).
- In a growing perpetuity, plugging in this year's payment instead of the first future payment $C_1$.
- Computing interest on the original loan amount each month instead of on the declining balance.
