@article
A share of stock is a claim on a firm's future cash flows, but unlike a bond it has no promised payments and no maturity. The cash a shareholder receives while holding the stock is dividends, and when she sells, the buyer's price in turn is the value of the dividends after that. So we can value stock by following dividends forward.

### The dividend discount model

If $D_t$ is the dividend at the end of year $t$ and $r$ is the shareholders' **required return** (the cost of equity), then

$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{\infty} \frac{D_t}{(1+r)^t}.$$

This is the **dividend discount model** (DDM). The sale price at any horizon is itself a present value of later dividends, so it does not matter that investors expect to sell. To use the model, we must specify how the dividends grow. Conventions in this lesson: dividends are paid once a year at year end; $D_0$ is the dividend just paid and $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$ is the next one; $r$ is an annual effective rate; and $P_0$ is the price just after $D_0$ is paid.

### Zero growth

If dividends are constant forever, as for a fixed-dividend preferred stock, the stock is a perpetuity:

$$P_0 = \frac{D}{r}.$$

### Constant growth (the Gordon growth model)

If dividends grow at a constant rate $g$ forever and $g < r$, the stream is a growing perpetuity:

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r - g} = \frac{D_0(1+g)}{r - g}.$$

The model also implies that the price grows at $g$ and that the required return has two parts,

$$r = \frac{D_1}{P_0} + g,$$

the **dividend yield** plus the **capital gains yield**. The price is very sensitive to the gap $r - g$: when it is small, a modest change in either number moves the price a lot.

### Where does growth come from?

If a firm keeps a fraction $b$ of earnings (the **retention ratio**) and earns a return on equity $\text{ROE}$ on new investment, the **sustainable growth rate** is

$$g = b \times \text{ROE}.$$

The **payout ratio** is $1 - b$, the fraction of earnings paid as dividends.

### Multi-stage growth

Young firms grow quickly and then slow down. For a firm growing at $g_1$ for $N$ years and at $g_2$ afterward, discount the dividends of the high-growth years one by one, and value the dividends from year $N+1$ on as a growing perpetuity at time $N$:

$$P_0 = \sum_{t=1}^{N}\frac{D_t}{(1+r)^t} + \frac{1}{(1+r)^N}\,\frac{D_{N+1}}{r - g_2}.$$

### Limitations

A DDM requires estimates of $r$ and $g$ that are uncertain, and it breaks down when $g \ge r$ or for a firm that pays no dividends (one then discounts free cash flow, a topic for later courses). The price estimate is an opinion about value, not a forecast of the price at which the stock will trade. Real prices and dividend figures change daily; look up company data on SEC EDGAR, and treat every problem here as a hypothetical.

@example
title: A growing dividend
problem: Hollis Tool Co. just paid an annual dividend of \$2.40 per share. Dividends are expected to grow at 4% a year forever, and the required return is 9.5%. Find the price and the dividend yield.
step: The next dividend is $D_1 = 2.40 \times 1.04 = \$2.496$.
step: $P_0 = 2.496 / (0.095 - 0.04) = 2.496 / 0.055 = \$45.38$.
step: The dividend yield is $2.496 / 45.38 = 5.5\%$, and the capital gains yield is 4%. Together they make the required return of 9.5%.
answer: \$45.38 per share; dividend yield 5.5%.

@example
title: Fast growth, then maturity
problem: A firm just paid a dividend of \$1.50. Dividends will grow 12% a year for 3 years and then 4% a year forever. The required return is 10%. Find the price today.
step: Dividends: $D_1 = 1.50 \times 1.12 = 1.680$, $D_2 = 1.8816$, $D_3 = 2.1074$. Present values at 10%: $1.680/1.1 = 1.5273$, $1.8816/1.21 = 1.5550$, $2.1074/1.331 = 1.5834$, a total of \$4.6656.
step: At the end of year 3, dividends settle at 4% growth: $D_4 = 2.1074 \times 1.04 = 2.1917$ and $P_3 = 2.1917/(0.10 - 0.04) = \$36.53$.
step: Discount that value three years: $36.528 / 1.331 = \$27.44$.
step: $P_0 = 4.67 + 27.44 = \$32.11$.
answer: About \$32.11 per share.

@mistakes
- Using $D_0$ instead of $D_1$ in the Gordon growth formula.
- Applying the constant-growth formula when $g \ge r$, or when growth will not last forever.
- Forgetting to discount the terminal value $P_N$ back from year $N$ to today.
- Treating the model price as a market price: it is a value implied by assumptions that can be wrong.

@practice num
prompt: A preferred stock pays a fixed dividend of \$3.20 per share at the end of each year forever. Investors require 8% a year. What is its price per share? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 40.00
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 3.20 / 0.08
hint: A fixed dividend forever is a perpetuity.
solution: $P_0 = D/r = 3.20 / 0.08 = \$40.00$.

@practice num
prompt: A company just paid an annual dividend of \$1.80 per share. Dividends are expected to grow at 3% a year forever, and the required return is 8%. What is the price per share? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 37.08
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 1.80 * 1.03 / (0.08 - 0.03)
hint: First compute the dividend expected next year, $D_1 = D_0(1+g)$.
solution: $D_1 = 1.80 \times 1.03 = 1.854$ and $P_0 = 1.854 / (0.08 - 0.03) = \$37.08$.

@practice mc
prompt: Using the constant-growth dividend discount model, which change by itself would raise the estimated price of a stock?
choice*: A lower required return, with the next dividend and the growth rate unchanged
choice: A higher required return, with the next dividend and the growth rate unchanged
choice: A lower expected growth rate, with the next dividend and the required return unchanged
choice: A lower next dividend, with the required return and growth rate unchanged
hint: Look at $P_0 = D_1/(r - g)$ and ask which change shrinks the denominator or raises the numerator.
solution: A lower $r$ shrinks the denominator $r - g$ and so raises the price. A higher $r$, a lower $g$ or a lower $D_1$ each lower the price.

@practice num
prompt: A stock's price is \$52.00, its next annual dividend is expected to be \$2.60, and dividends are expected to grow at 4.5% a year forever. What required return does the constant-growth model imply? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 9.50
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (2.60 / 52.00 + 0.045) * 100
hint: Rearrange $P_0 = D_1/(r-g)$ for $r$.
solution: $r = D_1/P_0 + g = 2.60/52.00 + 0.045 = 0.05 + 0.045 = 9.50\%$.

@practice num
prompt: A firm just paid a dividend of \$0.80. Dividends will grow 15% a year for the next 2 years and then 5% a year forever. The required return is 11%. What is the price per share today? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 16.71
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.80 * 1.15 / 1.11 + 0.80 * 1.15 ** 2 / 1.11 ** 2 + 0.80 * 1.15 ** 2 * 1.05 / (0.11 - 0.05) / 1.11 ** 2
hint: Find $D_1$ and $D_2$, then value the dividends from year 3 on as a growing perpetuity at the end of year 2.
hint: $P_2 = D_3/(r - g_2)$ with $D_3 = D_2 \times 1.05$; discount $P_2$ by $1.11^2$.
solution: $D_1 = 0.92$, $D_2 = 1.058$, $D_3 = 1.1109$. $P_2 = 1.1109/0.06 = 18.515$. $P_0 = 0.92/1.11 + 1.058/1.11^2 + 18.515/1.11^2 = 0.8288 + 0.8587 + 15.0271 = \$16.71$.

@quiz num
prompt: A firm expects next year's earnings of \$5.00 per share, and it pays out 40% of earnings as dividends each year, retaining 60%. It earns a 15% return on equity on retained earnings, which makes its growth sustainable. Using the constant-growth model with a required return of 11%, what is the price per share? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 100.00
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.40 * 5.00 / (0.11 - 0.60 * 0.15)
hint: Find the growth rate from the retention ratio and ROE, and the dividend from the payout ratio.
solution: $g = b \times \text{ROE} = 0.60 \times 0.15 = 9\%$. $D_1 = 0.40 \times 5.00 = 2.00$. $P_0 = 2.00/(0.11 - 0.09) = \$100.00$. Note how a 2-point gap between $r$ and $g$ makes the price very sensitive to the inputs.
