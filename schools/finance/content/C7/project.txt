## Week 13 review: corporate finance I

**Week by week.**
1. **The firm and statements.** The goal is to maximize the market value of owners' equity; agency problems arise when managers' interests differ. Assets = liabilities + equity; net income differs from cash flow; $\text{ROE} = \text{margin} \times \text{turnover} \times \text{equity multiplier}$.
2. **Single sums.** $FV = PV(1+r)^n$, $PV = FV/(1+r)^n$, $r = (FV/PV)^{1/n} - 1$, $n = \ln(FV/PV)/\ln(1+r)$.
3. **Annuities.** $PV = C\,[1 - (1+r)^{-n}]/r$; annuity due times $(1+r)$; perpetuity $C/r$; growing perpetuity $C_1/(r-g)$; loan payment $= Lr/[1 - (1+r)^{-n}]$; balance = present value of remaining payments.
4. **Rates.** $\text{EAR} = (1 + r_s/m)^m - 1$; continuous: $e^{r_s} - 1$; match the rate to the payment period; Fisher: $1 + r_{nom} = (1 + r_{real})(1+i)$.
5. **Bonds.** Price = PV of coupons plus PV of face value at the periodic yield; price falls when yield rises; premium when coupon exceeds YTM; $\Delta P/P \approx -D_{mod}\Delta y$.
6. **Stocks.** $P_0 = D_1/(r-g)$; $r = D_1/P_0 + g$; $g = b \times \text{ROE}$; multi-stage models discount the terminal value.
7. **Capital budgeting.** Accept if $NPV > 0$; IRR is the rate with $NPV = 0$ and can mislead for mutually exclusive or nonconventional projects; payback ignores value.
8. **Cash flows.** Incremental, after-tax; $\text{OCF} = (R - C)(1 - T) + T D$; include working capital, opportunity costs and after-tax salvage $S - T(S - BV)$; exclude sunk and financing costs.
9. **Risk.** $E(R) = \sum p R$; portfolio variance depends on correlation; diversification removes unsystematic risk only.
10. **CAPM.** $\beta = \text{Cov}(R_i, R_m)/\text{Var}(R_m)$; $E(R) = r_f + \beta(E(R_m) - r_f)$.
11. **WACC.** $\text{WACC} = (E/V)r_E + (P/V)r_P + (D/V)r_D(1-T)$ with market-value weights and the YTM as the cost of debt.
12. **Sensitivity.** One variable at a time; scenarios change several together; accounting break-even $(FC + D)/(P - v)$; financial break-even is where NPV is zero.

**Self-check.** Can you (a) move any cash flow to any date and say which rate you used; (b) convert a stated rate into the periodic rate that matches a payment schedule; (c) price a bond and find its yield; (d) turn a project description into a cash flow table; (e) estimate a WACC and explain each input; (f) say what a sensitivity table does not tell you?

**Common errors.** Using the annual rate with monthly periods; forgetting to add back depreciation; counting sunk costs or interest; using book value weights; using the coupon rate as the cost of debt; ranking mutually exclusive projects by IRR; averaging standard deviations; treating a model value as a market price.

prompt: Marlin Instruments has equity with a market value of \$120 million and debt with a market value of \$80 million. The debt has a yield to maturity of 5.8% (stated annual rate), the equity beta is 1.2, the risk-free rate is 4.0%, the market risk premium is 5.5% and the tax rate is 25% (all hypothetical). The company considers a project with the same risk as its business: equipment costing \$3,000,000, depreciated straight-line to zero over 5 years with no salvage value; revenue minus cash operating costs of \$1,100,000 each year for 5 years; and net working capital of \$200,000 invested at time 0 and recovered at the end of year 5. Cash flows are at year ends. Discount at the company's WACC. What is the project's NPV, to the nearest dollar?
answer: 818142
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: npv((120 * (0.04 + 1.2 * 0.055) + 80 * 0.058 * 0.75) / 200, [-3200000] + [1100000 * 0.75 + 0.25 * 600000] * 4 + [1100000 * 0.75 + 0.25 * 600000 + 200000])
hint: First find the WACC: the cost of equity from the CAPM, the after-tax cost of debt and market-value weights of 60% and 40%.
hint: The annual OCF is $1{,}100{,}000(0.75) + 0.25(600{,}000)$; year 5 adds the working capital recovery; year 0 is the equipment plus working capital.
solution: $r_E = 4.0\% + 1.2 \times 5.5\% = 10.6\%$ and $r_D(1 - T) = 5.8\% \times 0.75 = 4.35\%$. $\text{WACC} = 0.6(10.6\%) + 0.4(4.35\%) = 8.10\%$. $\text{OCF} = 825{,}000 + 150{,}000 = 975{,}000$. The cash flows are $-3{,}200{,}000$, then 975,000 in years 1 to 4 and 1,175,000 in year 5. $NPV = -3{,}200{,}000 + 975{,}000 \times 3.3048 + 1{,}175{,}000/1.081^5 = -3{,}200{,}000 + 3{,}222{,}149 + 795{,}993 = \$818{,}142$, positive, so the project adds value.

prompt: An analyst presents these results for a proposed plant expansion, with a WACC of 8.7%: base-case NPV of +\$82,000 on an investment of \$5.85 million; IRR 9.1%; payback 4.8 years; discounted payback 5.9 years. Selling price is the key uncertainty: each \$1 change in the price per case changes the NPV by about \$342,000, and the NPV is zero at a price of about \$50.76 against the base price of \$51. In a worst case (probability 25%) the NPV is about $-\$2.0$ million, and in a best case (25%) it is about +\$1.65 million. Write a recommendation to the board of five to eight sentences. State a decision, justify it with the numbers, and say what you would do to reduce the uncertainty.
hint: The NPV is barely positive and the price has almost no cushion. Use the break-even price and the spread of scenarios.
solution: I recommend that the board not approve the expansion yet, and instead spend a small amount to reduce the price uncertainty. The base case NPV of \$82,000 is only 1.4% of the \$5.85 million investment and the IRR of 9.1% is barely above the 8.7% WACC, so the project only just covers its cost of capital. The break-even price of \$50.76 is within 50 cents of the \$51 base price, so a price shortfall of less than 1% would erase the value, and each dollar of price moves the NPV by about \$342,000. The worst case loses about \$2.0 million while the best case gains about \$1.65 million; the expected gain is small relative to that spread. Payback of 4.8 years and discounted payback of 5.9 years in a 6-year project also leave little margin. Before committing, the company should test pricing with customers and distributors, negotiate contracts for volume or price, and check whether the cannibalization and cost forecasts hold. If those efforts show prices of at least a dollar or two higher, the project becomes clearly attractive. A reasonable alternative is to approve it conditionally on securing that pricing.
