@article
A firm raises money from lenders, preferred shareholders and common shareholders, and each provider expects a return. The firm's **cost of capital** is the return it must earn on its investments to satisfy all of them. We weight each source's cost by its share of the firm's financing to get one discount rate for projects like the firm's existing business: the **weighted average cost of capital** (WACC).

### Cost of debt

The cost of debt is the return lenders require **today**, not the interest rate on old loans. The best estimate is the **yield to maturity** (YTM) on the firm's long-term bonds (week 5). If the firm has no traded bonds, use the yield on bonds with the same credit rating and maturity. The coupon rate of an old bond is a historical number and is not the cost of debt.

Interest is tax deductible, so for a firm with marginal tax rate $T$ every dollar of interest saves $T$ in taxes. This **interest tax shield** makes the **after-tax cost of debt**

$$r_D(1 - T).$$

Conventions: $r_D$ is the YTM as a stated annual rate; $T$ is the marginal tax rate (the US federal statutory corporate rate is 21%, plus state taxes; deductibility of interest is subject to limits). The tax shield applies only if the firm has taxable income to shelter.

### Cost of preferred stock and of equity

A preferred stock with a fixed dividend $D_p$ and price $P_p$ costs $r_P = D_p/P_p$ (a perpetuity, week 6). Preferred dividends are not tax deductible. The cost of common equity $r_E$ is estimated with the CAPM, $r_E = r_f + \beta_E(E(R_m) - r_f)$ (week 10), or with the dividend discount model $r_E = D_1/P_0 + g$ (week 6). Both need judgment, and the two methods may disagree.

### Weights

Use **market values**, not book values, because the weights should reflect the amounts investors would now provide. Market value of equity is shares outstanding times price. For debt, use the market value of the bonds (or an approximation such as book value when the debt is not traded and its rate is near the market rate). With $E$, $P$ and $D$ the market values and $V = E + P + D$,

$$\text{WACC} = \frac{E}{V}\,r_E + \frac{P}{V}\,r_P + \frac{D}{V}\,r_D(1 - T).$$

If the firm has no preferred stock, the middle term drops out. A firm with obligations that are really debt, such as lease liabilities under ASC 842 or IFRS 16, should consider whether to count them in $D$ and to keep the treatment consistent with its cash flows.

### Using the WACC

WACC is the right discount rate for a project that has **the same risk as the firm's existing business** and is financed in the firm's usual proportions. For a project with different risk (a bank's new venture into software), use a rate that reflects the project's risk, for example the WACC of firms in that industry. Using one company-wide WACC for every project makes high-risk projects look too good and low-risk projects too bad.

### Getting data

For a public company, shares outstanding, debt details and the tax rate are in its annual report (Form 10-K) on SEC EDGAR. Prices, bond yields and Treasury rates change daily, so look up current values and record the date. The calculations here use hypothetical figures.

@example
title: The after-tax cost of debt
problem: A firm's 10-year bonds have a 6% semiannual coupon and \$1,000 face value, and they trade at \$1,045. The firm's marginal tax rate is 25%. What is the firm's after-tax cost of debt?
step: Find the YTM: solve $1{,}045 = 30 \times \dfrac{1 - (1+i)^{-20}}{i} + \dfrac{1{,}000}{(1+i)^{20}}$. A trial at $i = 2.7\%$ gives about \$1,045.90 and at $i = 2.75\%$ about \$1,038.07, so $i \approx 2.7057\%$ per half-year.
step: The stated annual YTM is $2 \times 2.7057\% = 5.41\%$. (The effective annual yield is 5.48%; the convention here uses the stated yield.)
step: After-tax cost: $5.41\% \times (1 - 0.25) = 4.06\%$. The coupon rate of 6% is not the cost of debt: investors will accept only 5.41% today.
answer: YTM about 5.41%; after-tax cost of debt about 4.06%.

@example
title: Computing a WACC
problem: Calloway Metals has 4 million shares trading at \$30 and \$60 million (market value) of debt with a YTM of 7%. It has no preferred stock. Its equity beta is 1.2, the risk-free rate is 4%, the market risk premium is 5.5% and the tax rate is 25% (hypothetical). Find the WACC.
step: Equity market value $= 4 \times 30 = \$120$ million, debt $= \$60$ million, so $V = \$180$ million, with weights $E/V = 2/3$ and $D/V = 1/3$.
step: Cost of equity: $r_E = 0.04 + 1.2 \times 0.055 = 10.6\%$. After-tax cost of debt: $0.07 \times 0.75 = 5.25\%$.
step: $\text{WACC} = (2/3)(10.6\%) + (1/3)(5.25\%) = 7.067\% + 1.750\% = 8.82\%$.
answer: WACC about 8.82%.

@mistakes
- Using the coupon rate on existing bonds as the cost of debt instead of the current YTM.
- Forgetting the tax shield and using the pretax cost of debt (or applying $1 - T$ to the cost of equity).
- Weighting with book values rather than market values.
- Using the firm's WACC for a project whose risk differs substantially from the firm's average risk.

@practice num
prompt: A firm's capital consists of \$600 million of equity (market value) and \$400 million of debt (market value). The cost of equity is 11%, the pretax cost of debt is 6% and the tax rate is 25%. What is the WACC, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 8.40
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (600 * 0.11 + 400 * 0.06 * (1 - 0.25)) / 1000 * 100
hint: The weights are 60% and 40%, and the debt cost is after tax.
solution: $\text{WACC} = 0.6(11\%) + 0.4(6\%)(0.75) = 6.6\% + 1.8\% = 8.40\%$.

@practice num
prompt: A firm's capital is \$500 million of common equity, \$100 million of preferred stock and \$400 million of debt (all market values). The cost of equity is 12%. The preferred stock pays a \$6 dividend per share and sells for \$80. The pretax cost of debt is 6.5% and the tax rate is 24%. What is the WACC, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 8.73
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (500 * 0.12 + 100 * (6 / 80) + 400 * 0.065 * (1 - 0.24)) / 1000 * 100
hint: Find the cost of preferred stock as dividend over price, and apply the tax shield only to debt.
solution: $r_P = 6/80 = 7.5\%$. $\text{WACC} = 0.5(12\%) + 0.1(7.5\%) + 0.4(6.5\%)(0.76) = 6.0\% + 0.75\% + 1.976\% = 8.726\%$, or 8.73%.

@quiz num
prompt: A firm has \$900 million of equity (market value) and \$300 million of debt. Its equity beta is 1.1, the risk-free rate is 4.2%, the market risk premium is 5.0%, the debt's YTM is 5.8% and the tax rate is 25% (hypothetical). What is the WACC, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 8.36
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (900 * (0.042 + 1.1 * 0.05) + 300 * 0.058 * (1 - 0.25)) / 1200 * 100
hint: Use the CAPM for the cost of equity, then weights of 75% and 25%.
solution: $r_E = 4.2\% + 1.1 \times 5.0\% = 9.7\%$. After-tax debt cost $= 5.8\% \times 0.75 = 4.35\%$. $\text{WACC} = 0.75(9.7\%) + 0.25(4.35\%) = 7.275\% + 1.0875\% = 8.36\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: A consumer-goods company with a WACC of 8% wants to use that rate to evaluate an investment in a speculative biotechnology venture. In four or five sentences, explain whether that is appropriate and what the company should do.
hint: The right discount rate depends on the risk of the project's cash flows, not on who is investing.
solution: The WACC reflects the risk of the company's existing business and its usual financing. A speculative biotechnology venture has much riskier cash flows, so its required return is higher. Discounting at 8% would overstate its NPV and could lead the company to accept projects that destroy value. The company should estimate a rate based on the project's risk, for example by finding the equity betas of comparable biotechnology firms, adjusting for their leverage, and computing a WACC for that industry. It could also add a justified risk premium to its own WACC, noting that this is a judgment.
rubric: States that the discount rate must reflect the risk of the project, not the firm's average risk.
rubric: Explains that using 8% would overstate the NPV of the riskier project.
rubric: Proposes a remedy such as a rate based on comparable firms' betas or a risk-adjusted rate.
