@article
Corporate issuers is a smaller topic on the Level II exam (weights change each year; check cfainstitute.org), but it feeds equity valuation: payout, leverage and deal assumptions change the cash flows you value. Vignettes here usually describe a company's decision and ask which action is value-increasing, what the EPS effect is, or what a governance feature signals.

### Capital structure

**Modigliani-Miller** without taxes says capital structure is irrelevant; with taxes, debt adds value through the interest tax shield, offset by expected costs of financial distress. The **static trade-off theory** balances these, giving an optimal debt level; the **pecking order theory** says firms prefer internal funds, then debt, then new equity, because of information asymmetry. In the United States, the federal corporate rate is a flat 21%, and the deduction for business interest is limited to 30% of adjusted taxable income (measured again before depreciation and amortization for tax years beginning after 2024); these rules change, so check current law. The weighted average cost of capital is

$$\text{WACC} = w_E r_E + w_D r_D (1 - t),$$

with market-value weights.

### Dividends and repurchases

Under a **residual dividend** policy, the firm funds all positive-NPV projects first, using its target mix of debt and equity, and pays out the remaining earnings:

$$\text{Dividend} = \text{Earnings} - (1 - w_D)\times\text{Capital spending}.$$

A repurchase reduces shares outstanding, so EPS rises when the earnings yield on the buyback price exceeds the after-tax cost of the funds used; it does not by itself create value. Dividends are taxed when received, while repurchases let the shareholder choose when to realize gains, a tax-timing difference; public companies also pay a 1% federal excise tax on net buybacks (since 2023). Dividends can signal management's confidence, and a cut can signal the opposite. Share repurchases affect EPS but also the book value per share and leverage ratios.

### Mergers and acquisitions

A **horizontal** merger combines competitors, a **vertical** one a supplier or customer, a **conglomerate** merger unrelated businesses. The value created is the **synergy**; the acquirer pays a **premium** over the target's market price. Gain to the acquirer's shareholders $=$ synergies $-$ premium; gain to the target's shareholders is the premium. In a stock deal, new shares equal the offer value divided by the acquirer's share price, and post-merger EPS is combined earnings over combined shares:

$$\text{EPS}_{post} = \frac{NI_A + NI_T + \text{synergies}}{N_A + N_{new}}.$$

A stock deal is accretive without synergies when the target's P/E at the offer price is **lower** than the acquirer's P/E. Do not confuse accretion with value creation: a deal can raise EPS and still destroy value if the price is too high.

**Takeover defenses** include the **poison pill** (shareholders other than the bidder can buy shares at a discount if the bidder passes a threshold), the **white knight** (a friendlier bidder), **greenmail** (buying back the bidder's stake at a premium), the **Pac-Man defense** (the target bids for the acquirer), **staggered boards**, and **golden parachutes**. Many are controversial because they may entrench management.

### Governance and ESG

Good governance means a board that is independent of management, aligned compensation, transparent disclosure, and respect for shareholders' rights. For ESG, three terms matter: **ESG integration** (adjusting cash flows or discount rates for material ESG risks), **negative screening** (excluding sectors), and **thematic or impact investing** (targeting specific outcomes). Regulation of sustainability disclosure is changing: the SEC voted in March 2025 to stop defending its 2024 climate rule, so that rule is not in effect, and European rules are being simplified, so check current requirements. Arguments differ on how far companies should pursue goals beyond shareholder value; present both sides.

@example
title: Stock deal and EPS
problem: Acquirer Westmark has net income of 200 million and 100 million shares trading at \$40 (EPS \$2.00, P/E 20). It offers \$22.50 a share for all 40 million shares of Target Reed, whose net income is 60 million, paying in Westmark stock. There are no synergies. What is post-merger EPS?
step: Offer value: $22.50 \times 40 = \$900$ million; new shares: $900/40 = 22.5$ million.
step: Combined net income: $200 + 60 = 260$ million; combined shares: $100 + 22.5 = 122.5$ million.
step: EPS: $260/122.5 = \$2.12$, up from \$2.00.
step: The deal is accretive because the target's P/E at the offer, $900/60 = 15$, is below the acquirer's 20.
answer: \$2.12; accretive.

@example
title: Buyback financed with debt
problem: A firm earns \$300 million with 100 million shares at \$50 (EPS \$3.00). It repurchases 10 million shares with borrowed money at an after-tax cost of 3.6%. What is the new EPS?
step: Cost of the repurchase: $10 \times 50 = \$500$ million.
step: After-tax interest: $500 \times 0.036 = \$18$ million, so net income is $300 - 18 = \$282$ million.
step: New shares: $90$ million; EPS $= 282/90 = \$3.13$.
step: The earnings yield $3/50 = 6\%$ exceeds the after-tax cost of 3.6%, so EPS rises.
answer: \$3.13.

@mistakes
- Treating an EPS increase as proof of value creation; accretion depends on P/E and financing, not on whether the price is fair.
- Using book weights in WACC instead of market-value weights, or forgetting $(1 - t)$ on debt.
- Subtracting the full capital budget in the residual dividend formula rather than only the equity-funded share.
- Assuming a repurchase always creates value; it changes EPS but also the capital structure.

answer: 3.17

solution: The repurchase costs \$600 million, so after-tax interest is \$22.5 million. Net income is \$427.5 million and shares are 135 million, so EPS is \$3.17, up from \$3.00.

@quiz num
prompt: A firm's capital structure by market value is \$600 million of equity and \$400 million of debt. Its cost of equity is 10%, its pre-tax cost of debt is 6%, and its tax rate is 25%. Compute the WACC as a percent, to one decimal place.
answer: 7.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (0.6 * 0.10 + 0.4 * 0.06 * (1 - 0.25)) * 100
hint: Weights are 60% equity and 40% debt.
hint: $0.6(10\%) + 0.4(6\%)(0.75)$.
solution: $\text{WACC} = 0.6 \times 10\% + 0.4 \times 6\% \times 0.75 = 6.0\% + 1.8\% = 7.8\%$.
