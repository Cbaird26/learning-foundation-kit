@article
Derivatives at Level II is about **no-arbitrage pricing**: the price of a derivative is the cost of replicating it. Weights change each year, so check cfainstitute.org. Always ask two different questions: what price makes a new contract worth zero at initiation, and what is an existing contract worth now.

### Forwards

For an asset with no income, $F_0 = S_0(1 + r)^T$. With income (dividends) or storage benefits and costs, subtract the future value of income and add costs:

$$F_0 = (S_0 - PV(\text{income}))(1 + r)^T.$$

Currency forwards use covered interest rate parity (see the economics week). At initiation, a forward has zero value. Later, the value to the long is the spot price (net of the present value of remaining income) less the present value of the forward price:

$$V_t(\text{long}) = S_t - PV_t(\text{income}) - \frac{F_0}{(1 + r)^{T-t}}.$$

**Futures** differ from forwards through daily mark-to-market settlement and exchange guarantees; with constant rates, futures and forward prices are almost identical. A **forward rate agreement** (FRA) locks in a borrowing rate; its value uses the forward rate implied by the curve.

### Swaps

A plain-vanilla interest rate swap exchanges fixed payments for floating payments on a notional principal, with no principal exchange. At initiation, the **swap fixed rate** makes the swap worth zero, so the present value of fixed payments equals the present value of floating payments. With discount factors $Z_i$ (the present value of 1 received at time $i$):

$$\text{SFR} = \frac{1 - Z_n}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} Z_i}.$$

After rates move, the swap's value to the **fixed-rate payer** is the present value of the remaining fixed payments avoided or owed relative to the floating side:

$$V = N\left[(1 - Z_n) - \text{SFR}_{old}\sum_i Z_i\right],$$

or, equivalently, $N(\text{SFR}_{new} - \text{SFR}_{old})\sum Z_i$. A fixed-rate payer gains when rates rise, because the floating payments received exceed the fixed payments made. Currency swaps and equity swaps follow the same replication logic.

### Options

**Put-call parity** for European options on a no-income stock:

$$C + \frac{K}{(1+r)^T} = P + S_0.$$

In the **one-period binomial model**, the stock goes up to $S_0u$ or down to $S_0d$. The risk-neutral probability of an up move is $\pi = (1 + r - d)/(u - d)$ and the option value is $[\pi c_u + (1 - \pi)c_d]/(1 + r)$. The **Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM)** model values a European option with five inputs, the stock price, strike, time, interest rate and volatility, and an adjustment for dividends; its assumptions include lognormal prices and constant volatility, which is why implied volatility differs across strikes (the volatility smile). **Delta** is the change in option value for a small change in the underlying: about 0 to 1 for a call and $-1$ to 0 for a put. A dealer who sold calls hedges by holding $\Delta \times$ the number of options in shares; **gamma** measures how fast delta changes and **vega** the sensitivity to volatility. Delta hedges need to be rebalanced as the underlying moves. Early exercise matters for American options on dividend-paying stocks, and American calls on non-dividend stocks are never exercised early.

### Working the vignettes

Write the timeline and say whether the contract is at initiation or later. For swaps, decide the position (fixed payer or receiver) before computing. For options, check that put-call parity is satisfied; a violation is an arbitrage you can state.

@example
title: Swap fixed rate
problem: Annual discount factors for one, two and three years are 0.980, 0.955 and 0.925. What is the three-year swap fixed rate?
step: Sum of discount factors: $0.980 + 0.955 + 0.925 = 2.860$.
step: Numerator: $1 - 0.925 = 0.075$.
step: Rate: $0.075/2.860 = 2.62\%$.
answer: 2.62%.

@example
title: One-period option
problem: A stock is at \$50. In one period it will be \$60 or \$45. The risk-free rate is 3% per period. What is a call with strike \$52 worth?
step: $u = 1.20$ and $d = 0.90$, so $\pi = (1.03 - 0.90)/(1.20 - 0.90) = 0.4333$.
step: Payoffs: the call is worth $60 - 52 = 8$ when the stock goes up and 0 when it goes down.
step: Value: $0.4333 \times 8/1.03 = \$3.37$.
answer: \$3.37.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to subtract the future value of dividends when pricing a forward on a dividend-paying stock.
- Confusing the forward price at initiation (value zero) with the value of an existing forward.
- Getting the direction of a swap wrong: the fixed payer gains when rates rise.
- Using the real-world probability of an up move in the binomial formula instead of the risk-neutral one.

@practice num
prompt: A stock trades at \$60 and the risk-free rate is 4% a year (annual compounding). The stock will pay a \$2.00 dividend in six months. What is the one-year forward price? Round to the nearest cent.
answer: 60.36
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 60 * 1.04 - 2.00 * 1.04 ** 0.5
hint: Subtract the dividend's value at the forward's maturity.
hint: $F = 60(1.04) - 2.00(1.04)^{0.5}$.
solution: $60 \times 1.04 = 62.40$ and the dividend grows to $2.00 \times 1.04^{0.5} = 2.0396$ at year-end, so $F = \$60.36$.

@practice num
prompt: A European call with a strike of \$100 and one year to expiration costs \$6.20. The stock is at \$100 and the risk-free rate is 4% (annual compounding). No dividends are paid. What is the value of the put with the same strike and expiration, to the nearest cent?
answer: 2.35
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 6.20 - 100 + 100 / 1.04
hint: Rearrange put-call parity for the put.
hint: $P = C - S_0 + K/(1 + r)^T$.
solution: $P = 6.20 - 100 + 96.15 = \$2.35$.

@practice mc
prompt: A company enters a three-year pay-fixed, receive-floating interest rate swap. One year later, market interest rates have risen across the curve. What happens to the value of the swap to the company?
choice*: It rises, because the company pays a below-market fixed rate and receives floating payments that are now higher
choice: It falls, because the fixed payments are now above market
choice: It stays at zero, because swaps are zero-value contracts
hint: The company pays fixed.
solution: The swap was zero at initiation, but after rates rise the fixed rate the company pays is below the new swap rate. Receiving higher floating payments for a lower fixed payment gives the fixed-rate payer a positive value.

@practice num
prompt: A company pays a fixed rate of 3.0% on a three-year swap with a notional of \$10 million, with annual payments. The current discount factors for years 1, 2 and 3 are 0.970, 0.940 and 0.910. What is the value of the swap to the fixed-rate payer, in dollars?
answer: 54000
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 1e7 * ((1 - 0.910) - 0.03 * (0.970 + 0.940 + 0.910))
hint: The floating side is worth $1 - Z_n$ per unit of notional.
hint: Compare $0.09$ with $0.03 \times 2.82$.
solution: The floating side is worth $0.09 \times 10{,}000{,}000 = \$900{,}000$ and the fixed side $0.03 \times 2.82 \times 10{,}000{,}000 = \$846{,}000$, so the swap is worth \$54,000 to the fixed payer.

@practice num
prompt: A stock is at \$40. In one period it will be \$50 or \$32. The risk-free rate is 2% per period. What is a European put with a strike of \$42 worth, to the nearest cent?
answer: 5.01
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (1 - (1.02 - 32 / 40) / (50 / 40 - 32 / 40)) * (42 - 32) / 1.02
hint: Find the risk-neutral probability of an up move first.
hint: $\pi = (1.02 - 0.80)/(1.25 - 0.80)$; the put pays 10 only in the down state.
solution: $\pi = 0.4889$, so the down probability is 0.5111. The put pays 0 if the stock rises and \$10 if it falls, so it is worth $0.5111 \times 10/1.02 = \$5.01$.

@quiz num
prompt: An investor entered a one-year forward on a stock with no dividends at a forward price of \$52.00. Three months later the stock is at \$55.00 and the risk-free rate is 4% a year (annual compounding). What is the value of the long position, to the nearest cent?
answer: 4.51
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 55 - 52 / 1.04 ** 0.75
hint: Discount the forward price over the remaining nine months.
hint: $V = S_t - F_0/(1.04)^{0.75}$.
solution: The remaining time is 0.75 years, so $V = 55 - 52/1.04^{0.75} = 55 - 50.49 = \$4.51$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A dealer sold 1,000 call options on a stock, each on one share. The calls have a delta of 0.55. To delta-hedge the position, what should the dealer do?
choice: Sell 550 shares of the stock
choice*: Buy 550 shares of the stock
choice: Buy 1,000 shares of the stock
hint: The dealer is short the calls, so the position loses when the stock rises.
solution: The short calls lose about 0.55 per share per option when the stock rises, so the dealer buys $1{,}000 \times 0.55 = 550$ shares. The hedge changes as delta changes and must be rebalanced.
