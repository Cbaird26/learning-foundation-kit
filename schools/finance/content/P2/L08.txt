@article
Multiples are how most practitioners value stocks; the exam tests whether you understand where they come from and when they mislead. Residual income is the model that handles companies where dividends and free cash flow are poor guides. Weights change each year, so check cfainstitute.org.

### Two ways to use multiples

The **comparables approach** applies a peer group's multiple to the target's own fundamental (for example, peer average EV/EBITDA times the target's EBITDA). The **fundamentals approach** derives a **justified multiple** from the Gordon growth model. With a payout ratio $p$, growth $g$ and required return $r$:

$$\left(\frac{P}{E}\right)_{leading} = \frac{p}{r - g}, \qquad \left(\frac{P}{E}\right)_{trailing} = \frac{p(1 + g)}{r - g},$$

$$\left(\frac{P}{B}\right) = \frac{\text{ROE} - g}{r - g}.$$

The P/B formula shows that a stock should trade above book value only if $\text{ROE} > r$ (value creation). A higher required return lowers justified multiples, and a higher growth rate raises them (when ROE exceeds $r$ for P/B). Know the **leading** (forward earnings) and **trailing** (reported earnings) versions, and the **PEG ratio** $\text{P/E} \div \text{growth}$, which is a shortcut that assumes similar risk and a linear relation.

### Enterprise value multiples

**Enterprise value (EV)** equals market value of equity plus debt (and preferred stock, minority interest) less cash. Multiples such as EV/EBITDA, EV/EBIT and EV/Sales are neutral to capital structure, so they suit comparisons across firms with different leverage and are used for firms with negative earnings. To reach equity value from an EV multiple, subtract debt, add cash and divide by shares:

$$\text{Equity} = EV - \text{Debt} + \text{Cash}.$$

Match numerator and denominator: price multiples (P/E, P/B, P/S) use per-share equity items; EV multiples use items attributable to all capital providers. Watch for differences in accounting (LIFO versus FIFO inventory in the US, leases under IFRS 16 versus ASC 842 operating leases), which move EBITDA and debt, and normalize earnings for one-time items.

### Residual income

**Residual income** is the earnings left after charging shareholders for the equity capital used:

$$RI_t = NI_t - r\,B_{t-1} = (\text{ROE}_t - r)B_{t-1}.$$

The model values equity as book value plus the present value of expected residual income:

$$V_0 = B_0 + \sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\frac{RI_t}{(1 + r)^t}.$$

With residual income growing at a constant rate $g$ from year 1,

$$V_0 = B_0 + \frac{RI_1}{r - g}.$$

A **persistence factor** $\omega$ between 0 and 1 models residual income that fades toward zero as competition erodes excess returns. Residual income is suited to firms that pay no dividends, have negative expected free cash flow, or are valued at levels where terminal values would dominate. Its weaknesses: it relies on accounting book value, so check for clean surplus violations (items bypassing income, such as the translation adjustment) and adjust for aggressive accounting. Because book value is a large share of the value, the model is less sensitive to the terminal value than the DDM.

### Choosing the approach

A short checklist for a vignette: does the firm pay dividends in line with cash flow (DDM)? Is FCF positive and forecastable (FCFE or FCFF)? Is the company young with negative FCF but sound accounting (residual income)? Are you comparing peers with different capital structures (EV multiples)? Is the firm in a cyclical industry (normalize earnings or use P/B or EV/Sales)? Say which multiple, why it fits, and what the main risk is.

@example
title: Justified P/E
problem: A mature company has a payout ratio of 40%, expected dividend growth of 5% and a required return of 9%. What are its justified leading and trailing P/E ratios?
step: Leading: $0.40/(0.09 - 0.05) = 10.0$.
step: Trailing: multiply by $(1 + g)$: $10.0 \times 1.05 = 10.5$.
step: Interpretation: the stock is fairly priced at about 10 times next year's earnings; a market P/E above that implies higher growth or a lower required return than assumed.
answer: Leading 10.0; trailing 10.5.

@example
title: Residual income value
problem: A company has a book value of \$50 per share. It is expected to earn \$6.00 a share next year, the required return is 10%, and residual income is expected to grow 3% a year indefinitely. What is the value per share?
step: Equity charge: $0.10 \times 50 = \$5.00$.
step: Residual income next year: $6.00 - 5.00 = \$1.00$.
step: Value: $50 + 1.00/(0.10 - 0.03) = 50 + 14.29 = \$64.29$.
answer: About \$64.29 a share.

@mistakes
- Using the trailing formula for a forward multiple (or the reverse): the difference is the factor $1 + g$.
- Applying a P/B above one to a firm whose ROE is below its cost of equity.
- Mixing an enterprise value numerator with an equity denominator (such as EV/net income).
- Forgetting to subtract debt and add cash when turning EV into equity value.

@quiz num
prompt: A peer group trades at an average EV/EBITDA of 9.0. A target company has EBITDA of \$140 million, debt of \$500 million, cash of \$60 million and 50 million shares outstanding. What is the implied value per share, to the nearest cent?
answer: 16.40
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: (9.0 * 140 - 500 + 60) / 50
hint: Multiply EBITDA by the multiple to get enterprise value.
hint: Equity equals EV less debt plus cash.
solution: $EV = 9.0 \times 140 = \$1{,}260$ million. Equity is $1{,}260 - 500 + 60 = \$820$ million, or $820/50 = \$16.40$ per share.
