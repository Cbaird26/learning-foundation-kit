@article
Level II economics is the macro and international half of the curriculum, and it is tested through currency arithmetic and short policy scenarios. You will see forward-rate calculations, a parity condition applied to a hypothetical country pair, and a question on why an economy grows. Weights change from year to year, so check cfainstitute.org; the topics below are the stable core.

### Quoting currencies

A quote is written **price currency per base currency**. A quote of 1.2500 USD/EUR means one euro costs 1.2500 dollars: EUR is the base, USD the price currency. Always identify base and price before applying a formula. A **cross rate** is derived from two quotes that share a currency; if the bid and offer are quoted, buy at the offer and sell at the bid, and a triangular-arbitrage opportunity exists when the quoted cross rate differs from the implied one.

### Covered interest rate parity

If investors can lock in a forward rate, no-arbitrage requires

$$F = S\,\frac{1 + i_P\,t}{1 + i_B\,t},$$

where $S$ and $F$ are in price per base, $i_P$ and $i_B$ are the interest rates of the price and base currencies, and $t$ is the fraction of a year (for money-market rates, days/360 or days/365 by convention; state the convention you use). The currency with the **higher interest rate trades at a forward discount**, and the lower-rate currency at a forward premium. **Forward points** are $(F - S)$ quoted in the last decimal place, usually scaled by 10,000. Covered parity is a no-arbitrage relation and holds closely in liquid markets.

### Uncovered parity, PPP and the carry trade

**Uncovered interest rate parity** says the expected change in the spot rate equals the interest differential, so expected returns are the same in either currency. It is a statement about expectations with no hedge, and it often fails: high-yield currencies have, on average, not depreciated by the full differential. That failure is what a **carry trade** exploits (borrow in a low-rate currency, lend in a high-rate one), earning the differential, at the risk of a sudden reversal. **Absolute PPP** says price levels are equal after conversion; **relative PPP** says currencies move by the inflation differential. PPP holds poorly over short horizons and is more useful over many years. The **real exchange rate** (domestic per foreign) is $Q = S\,P_{foreign}/P_{domestic}$, so a rise in $Q$ makes foreign goods relatively more expensive.

Other determinants to know: the **balance of payments** (current account plus capital account plus financial account sum to zero in principle), the **Marshall-Lerner condition** (depreciation improves the trade balance when the sum of the import and export demand elasticities exceeds 1), and the **Mundell-Fleming** result that, with high capital mobility, expansionary fiscal policy combined with tight monetary policy attracts capital and appreciates the currency, while the reverse mix depreciates it. Capital controls, intervention and a central bank's inflation target all feed exam scenarios.

### Economic growth

Output growth comes from more inputs and from better use of them. With Cobb-Douglas production, **growth accounting** gives

$$\Delta Y/Y = \Delta A/A + \alpha\,\Delta K/K + (1-\alpha)\,\Delta L/L,$$

where $\alpha$ is capital's share of income and $\Delta A/A$ is **total factor productivity** growth (the Solow residual). In the **neoclassical (Solow) model**, capital has diminishing returns, so a higher saving rate or more capital raises the *level* of output per worker but only temporarily the growth rate; sustained growth in output per worker comes from technological progress. Convergence follows if countries have similar parameters. **Endogenous growth** models make productivity gains depend on investment in human capital and research, so growth can be self-sustaining. Most items ask which kind of change affects growth versus level.

### Regulation in one line

Exam items also test why regulation exists (to correct market failures and protect investors) and the costs (compliance burden, distortions); expect a short scenario on cost-benefit analysis, not a calculation.

@example
title: A forward rate
problem: The spot rate is 1.2500 USD/EUR. The six-month (180-day, 360-day basis) interest rates are 4.0% a year in USD and 2.5% in EUR. What is the six-month forward rate, and how many forward points is it?
step: Identify the currencies: USD is the price currency ($i_P = 4.0\%$) and EUR is the base ($i_B = 2.5\%$); $t = 180/360 = 0.5$.
step: Apply parity: $F = 1.2500 \times (1 + 0.04 \times 0.5)/(1 + 0.025 \times 0.5) = 1.2500 \times 1.0200/1.0125 = 1.25926$.
step: Forward points: $(1.25926 - 1.2500) \times 10{,}000 = 92.6$. The euro, with the lower rate, trades at a forward premium.
answer: 1.25926 USD/EUR; about +92.6 points.

@example
title: Growth accounting
problem: An economy's output grows 3.5% a year while capital grows 4.0% and labor grows 1.0%. Capital's share of income is 0.35. What is total factor productivity growth?
step: Capital's contribution is $0.35 \times 4.0 = 1.40$ points.
step: Labor's contribution is $0.65 \times 1.0 = 0.65$ points.
step: TFP growth is the remainder: $3.5 - 1.40 - 0.65 = 1.45\%$.
answer: 1.45% a year.

@mistakes
- Inverting the forward formula or swapping the base and price currency interest rates; label each rate with its currency first.
- Concluding that the higher-rate currency trades at a forward premium: it trades at a discount.
- Treating uncovered parity as a no-arbitrage law like covered parity; it is an expectations condition that often fails.
- Believing a higher saving rate permanently raises the growth rate in the neoclassical model; it raises the level.

prompt: The spot rate is 150.00 JPY/USD. The 90-day interest rate is 0.5% a year in JPY and 4.5% a year in USD (360-day basis). Using covered interest rate parity, what is the 90-day forward rate in JPY/USD? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 148.52
tol: 0.01
check: 150.00 * (1 + 0.005 * 90 / 360) / (1 + 0.045 * 90 / 360)
hint: JPY is the price currency and USD the base currency.
hint: $F = S(1 + i_{JPY}t)/(1 + i_{USD}t)$ with $t = 0.25$.
solution: $F = 150.00 \times 1.00125/1.01125 = 148.52$. The dollar, with the higher interest rate, trades at a forward discount.

@practice mc
prompt: Interest rates are 3.0% in the euro area and 5.0% in the United Kingdom, and covered interest rate parity holds. Compared with the spot rate, what should the one-year forward rate between the pound and the euro show?
choice: The pound trades at a forward premium against the euro, because its interest rate is higher
choice*: The pound trades at a forward discount against the euro, because its interest rate is higher
choice: The forward and spot rates are equal, because parity cancels the interest rates
hint: The higher-rate currency must be expected to lose value in the forward market to prevent arbitrage.
solution: With covered parity, the higher interest rate in the pound is offset by a lower forward value of the pound, so it trades at a forward discount and the lower-rate euro at a premium.

prompt: The spot rate is 1.40 USD/GBP. The price level in the United Kingdom is 108 and in the United States is 112 (same base year). Compute the real exchange rate as USD per GBP, $Q = S \times P_{UK}/P_{US}$, to two decimal places.
answer: 1.35
tol: 0.01
check: 1.40 * 108 / 112
hint: The real rate multiplies the nominal rate by the foreign price level over the domestic one, with the dollar as the domestic currency.
hint: $Q = 1.40 \times 108/112$.
solution: $Q = 1.40 \times 108/112 = 1.35$. The real rate is lower than the nominal rate because the UK price level is lower than the US one in this data.

@practice mc
prompt: According to the neoclassical (Solow) growth model, a country raises its national saving rate from 15% to 25% and keeps its population growth and technology unchanged. What happens to long-run output per worker?
choice: Its growth rate rises permanently, because investment drives growth
choice*: Its level rises to a new higher steady state, but its long-run growth rate returns to the rate of technological progress
choice: It is unchanged, because capital has constant returns
hint: Capital has diminishing returns in the model.
solution: A higher saving rate deepens capital and raises output per worker temporarily faster, but diminishing returns pull growth back to the rate of technological progress. The change is a level effect, not a permanent growth effect.

@practice mc
prompt: An economy has a floating exchange rate and highly mobile capital. Its government increases spending sharply while its central bank raises its policy rate. According to the Mundell-Fleming model, what is the most likely effect on the currency?
choice*: It appreciates, because both the fiscal and the monetary policy attract capital inflows
choice: It depreciates, because the fiscal deficit widens
choice: It is unchanged, because the effects offset each other
hint: Higher domestic interest rates attract capital.
solution: With high capital mobility, expansionary fiscal policy raises domestic interest rates and tight monetary policy raises them further, so capital flows in and the currency appreciates.

@quiz num
prompt: Real output grows 4.2% a year in an economy where capital grows 6.0% and labor grows 1.5%. Capital's share of income is 0.40. Compute total factor productivity growth as a percent, to one decimal place.
answer: 0.9
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 4.2 - 0.40 * 6.0 - 0.60 * 1.5
hint: Subtract the contribution of each input from output growth.
hint: Capital contributes $0.40 \times 6.0$ and labor contributes $0.60 \times 1.5$.
solution: Capital contributes 2.4 points and labor 0.9, so TFP growth is $4.2 - 2.4 - 0.9 = 0.9\%$.

@quiz mc
prompt: An investor borrows Japanese yen at a low interest rate and invests the proceeds in Australian dollar deposits that pay a higher rate. The position is unhedged and held for one year. Under which outcome does the investor lose money?
choice: The Australian dollar appreciates against the yen
choice: The exchange rate does not change
choice*: The Australian dollar depreciates against the yen by more than the interest rate differential
hint: The trade earns the interest differential unless the currency move takes it away.
solution: The carry trade earns the interest differential, and loses only if the high-rate currency falls by more than that differential. If the exchange rate is unchanged or the high-rate currency rises, the trade gains.
