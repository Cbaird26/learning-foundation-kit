@article
Financial statement analysis is a heavy topic at Level II, and these two areas are a favorite: they combine accounting mechanics with a judgment question ("how should an analyst adjust?"). Weights change each year, so check cfainstitute.org. The approach is to identify the method first, then the mechanics, then the effect on ratios.

### Intercorporate investments: pick the method by influence

| Ownership and influence | IFRS | US GAAP | Effect on the investor |
|---|---|---|---|
| Little influence (usually under 20%) | Financial asset at fair value (FVPL, or FVOCI by election for equities) | Fair value through net income (with a measurement alternative for equity without readily determinable fair value) | Dividends and fair-value changes reported; no share of income |
| Significant influence (about 20–50%) | Associate: equity method | Equity method | Share of investee net income on one line; dividends reduce the investment |
| Joint control | Joint venture: equity method; joint operation: share of assets and liabilities | Equity method (joint venture), proportionate share in limited cases | Same |
| Control (usually over 50%) | Consolidate | Consolidate | Add 100% of assets, liabilities, revenues and expenses; show non-controlling interest |

Percentages are guidelines: board seats, voting rights and policy-making role decide significant influence and control.

**Equity method.** The investment starts at cost and then rises with the investor's share of investee net income and falls with dividends received:

$$\text{Carrying amount}_{end} = \text{Cost} + s\,\text{NI} - s\,\text{Dividends},$$

where $s$ is the ownership share. Dividends are a return of capital, not income. Any excess of cost over the share of identifiable net assets is goodwill, which is not amortized or separately tested under the equity method (the investment as a whole is tested for impairment). Profit from sales between investor and investee is eliminated in proportion to ownership.

**Acquisition method (consolidation).** The acquirer measures identifiable assets and liabilities at fair value. **Goodwill** is the excess of the consideration plus non-controlling interest over the fair value of identifiable net assets. Under IFRS the NCI may be measured at fair value (full goodwill) or at its share of net assets (partial goodwill); US GAAP requires fair value (full goodwill). Goodwill is tested for impairment, not amortized. Analysts see higher revenues, assets and liabilities after consolidation than under the equity method, with the same net income attributable to the parent, so margins and return on assets are lower, and leverage ratios usually higher.

### Defined benefit pensions

In a **defined benefit (DB)** plan the employer promises benefits, so it bears the investment and longevity risk. The **projected benefit obligation (PBO)** is the present value of earned benefits using projected pay. **Funded status** is plan assets minus the PBO; a deficit is a liability on the balance sheet.

- **PBO rollforward**: beginning PBO plus current service cost plus interest cost (discount rate times beginning PBO) plus past service cost plus actuarial losses (or minus gains) minus benefits paid.
- **Plan assets rollforward**: beginning assets plus actual return plus employer contributions minus benefits paid.

The **P&L expense** differs by standard. Under **IFRS**, profit or loss includes current service cost, past service cost, and **net interest** (discount rate times the net defined benefit liability or asset). Remeasurements (actuarial gains and losses and the return on assets above the interest income) go to other comprehensive income and are not reclassified. Under **US GAAP**, net periodic pension cost includes service cost, interest cost, the **expected** return on assets (a deduction) and amortization of prior service cost and of actuarial gains or losses beyond a corridor. Only the service cost component is reported with operating compensation; other components appear outside operating income.

### Analyst adjustments

Analysts often (1) treat the funded status deficit as debt, (2) move non-service components out of operating income, and (3) replace an aggressive assumed return on assets with a realistic one. A higher discount rate lowers the PBO; a higher assumed return raises US GAAP income without any change in cash flows. DB pensions are the main reason two firms with the same operations can show different profits.

@example
title: Equity method and consolidation
problem: Harlow Corp buys 30% of Brightwater Ltd and exercises significant influence. During the year Brightwater reports net income of \$40 million and pays dividends of \$10 million. How much does Harlow's net income increase, and by how much does the carrying amount of the investment change?
step: Identify the method: significant influence, so the equity method applies.
step: Share of income: $0.30 \times 40 = \$12$ million is reported in Harlow's profit.
step: Share of dividends: $0.30 \times 10 = \$3$ million reduces the investment, and is not income.
step: Change in carrying amount: $12 - 3 = \$9$ million.
answer: Net income rises by \$12 million; the investment rises by \$9 million.

@example
title: IFRS pension cost
problem: At the start of the year a plan has a PBO of 500 and plan assets of 430 (millions). The discount rate is 5%, and current service cost is 20. What is the IFRS pension cost in profit or loss, ignoring past service cost?
step: Net defined benefit liability: $500 - 430 = 70$.
step: Net interest: $0.05 \times 70 = 3.5$.
step: Profit or loss: service cost plus net interest, $20 + 3.5 = 23.5$ million.
answer: 23.5 million (remeasurements go to OCI).

@mistakes
- Recording dividends from an equity-method investee as income; they reduce the investment.
- Using the PBO's interest rate on the PBO alone instead of net interest on the net liability under IFRS.
- Treating goodwill from a consolidation as amortized (it is tested for impairment).
- Forgetting that consolidation adds 100% of the subsidiary even when the parent owns only a majority.

prompt: Orchard Group buys 25% of Tamarack Inc. for \$120 million, giving it significant influence. In the following year Tamarack reports net income of \$60 million and pays dividends of \$16 million. What is the carrying amount of Orchard's investment at year end under the equity method, in millions of dollars to one decimal place?
answer: 131.0
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 120 + 0.25 * 60 - 0.25 * 16
hint: The investment rises with the share of net income and falls with the share of dividends.
hint: $120 + 0.25(60) - 0.25(16)$.
solution: Share of income is 15 and share of dividends is 4, so the carrying amount is $120 + 15 - 4 = \$131.0$ million.

@practice num
prompt: Sedge Holdings acquires 80% of Marlin Ltd for \$560 million. The fair value of the non-controlling interest is \$140 million and the fair value of Marlin's identifiable net assets is \$600 million. Using the full goodwill method, what is the goodwill, in millions of dollars?
answer: 100
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 560 + 140 - 600
hint: Add the consideration and the fair value of the non-controlling interest, then subtract the identifiable net assets.
hint: $560 + 140 - 600$.
solution: Full goodwill is $560 + 140 - 600 = \$100$ million. (Partial goodwill would be $560 - 0.8 \times 600 = \$80$ million.)

@practice num
prompt: At the start of the year a pension plan reports a PBO of 900 and plan assets of 780 (millions). The discount rate is 4.5% and current service cost is 35. Under IFRS, what is the pension cost recognized in profit or loss, in millions to one decimal place (assume no past service cost)?
answer: 40.4
tol: 0.1
check: 35 + 0.045 * (900 - 780)
hint: Net interest uses the net defined benefit liability.
hint: $35 + 0.045 \times 120$.
solution: The net liability is 120, so net interest is 5.4, and the cost is $35 + 5.4 = 40.4$ million.

@quiz num
prompt: A plan starts the year with a PBO of 600 and plan assets of 540 (millions). During the year the discount rate of 6% creates interest cost, current service cost is 30, actuarial losses are 14, benefits paid are 25, the actual return on assets is 48, and employer contributions are 40. What is the plan's net pension liability at year end (the PBO less plan assets), in millions?
answer: 52
tol: 0.1
check: (600 + 30 + 0.06 * 600 + 14 - 25) - (540 + 48 + 40 - 25)
hint: Roll forward each side separately.
hint: PBO end $= 600 + 30 + 36 + 14 - 25$; assets end $= 540 + 48 + 40 - 25$.
solution: The ending PBO is $600 + 30 + 36 + 14 - 25 = 655$, and ending assets are $540 + 48 + 40 - 25 = 603$. The funded status is $603 - 655 = -52$, a net liability of 52 million.

@quiz mc
prompt: A parent owns 30% of an affiliate that it accounts for under the equity method. An analyst wants to compare it with a competitor that consolidates a similar 60% subsidiary. If the parent had consolidated its affiliate instead (suppose it held control through other rights), which effect would the analyst expect on the parent's reported financials?
choice: Net income attributable to the parent would rise, because 100% of income is included
choice*: Revenue and total liabilities would rise, while net income attributable to the parent would be unchanged
choice: Return on assets would rise, because total assets increase
hint: Consolidation changes presentation, not the parent's share of earnings.
solution: Consolidation adds 100% of the affiliate's revenue, assets and liabilities, so revenue and liabilities rise. Net income attributable to the parent stays the same because the non-controlling interest absorbs the rest. Return on assets falls because the denominator grows with little change in the numerator.
