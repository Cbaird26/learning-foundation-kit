Fixed income at Level II moves from "price a bond at its yield" to "price it off the whole curve and account for embedded options and default." Weights change each year, so check cfainstitute.org. The questions are numerical but short; the traps are conceptual.

### Spot and forward rates

A **spot rate** $S_t$ is the yield on a zero-coupon bond maturing in $t$ years. A bond is valued by discounting each cash flow at the spot rate for its own date:

$$P = \sum_{t}\frac{CF_t}{(1 + S_t)^t}.$$

A **forward rate** $f(j,k)$ is the rate agreed today for a $k$-year loan starting in $j$ years. No-arbitrage links them:

$$(1 + S_{j+k})^{j+k} = (1 + S_j)^{j}\,(1 + f(j,k))^{k}.$$

For example $f(1,1) = (1 + S_2)^2/(1 + S_1) - 1$. An upward-sloping spot curve implies forward rates above spot rates. Expectations theory says forward rates are unbiased predictors of future spot rates; liquidity-preference and preferred-habitat theories add term premiums. If the curve is steep, the **rolldown return** matters: a bond held while it ages along an upward-sloping curve gains if the curve is unchanged. Know the **par, spot and forward curve** relationship and the idea of **swap-curve and Treasury-curve spreads** (I-spread, Z-spread). The **Z-spread** is the constant spread added to each spot rate on the benchmark curve that makes the present value of cash flows equal the price.

### Bonds with embedded options

An option embedded in a bond changes its value and its risk:

$$V_{callable} = V_{straight} - V_{call}, \qquad V_{putable} = V_{straight} + V_{put}.$$

The issuer owns a call option and the investor owns a put option. Valuation uses a **binomial interest rate tree** calibrated to the benchmark curve: work backwards from maturity, and at each node the issuer calls (replaces the value by the call price if it is lower) or the investor puts (value floor at put price). The **option-adjusted spread (OAS)** is the constant spread added to every tree rate that equates the tree value to the market price; for a callable bond, $\text{OAS} = \text{Z-spread} - \text{option cost}$. Callable bonds exhibit **negative convexity** at low yields: price gains are capped near the call price. **Effective duration** uses prices from shifting the whole curve by $\pm\Delta y$:

$$\text{EffDur} = \frac{V_- - V_+}{2\,V_0\,\Delta y}, \qquad \text{EffConv} = \frac{V_- + V_+ - 2V_0}{V_0(\Delta y)^2}.$$

Convertible bonds are valued as a straight bond plus a call option on the stock; the investor holds both.

### Credit analysis

**Expected loss** is the probability of default times the loss given default:

$$EL = PD \times LGD, \qquad LGD = 1 - \text{recovery rate}.$$

The bond's value reflects expected loss and a **risk premium** for bearing uncertainty about that loss. **Structural models** (options-theory style) treat equity as a call option on firm assets, so default happens when assets fall below debt; **reduced-form models** model default as a random event with an intensity inferred from market prices. Credit analysis of an issuer covers capacity (cash flow and leverage), collateral, covenants and character. For **securitized** debt, analysts look at credit enhancement and tranche subordination. A bond's **credit spread** tends to widen in a recession, with a **flight to quality** toward safer assets. Do not confuse **credit spread duration** with interest rate duration.

### How to work these vignettes

Draw the cash flow timeline first. Decide which curve to discount on. For option items, ask whose option it is (call: issuer; put: investor) and what happens to price when rates fall. State direction before arithmetic: a quick check on direction often eliminates two of three choices.

@example
title: Pricing off a spot curve
problem: One-, two- and three-year spot rates are 2.0%, 2.6% and 3.1%. Price a 3-year annual-pay 4% coupon bond (par 100), and find the one-year forward rate one year from now.
step: Discount each flow at its own spot rate: $4/1.020 + 4/1.026^2 + 104/1.031^3 = 3.922 + 3.800 + 94.897 = 102.62$.
step: Forward rate: $f(1,1) = 1.026^2/1.020 - 1 = 1.052676/1.020 - 1 = 3.20\%$.
step: Check: an upward-sloping curve gives a forward rate above the two-year spot rate.
answer: Price 102.62; $f(1,1) = 3.20\%$.

@example
title: Callable bond value and effective duration
problem: A straight bond is worth 103.40 and the embedded call is worth 2.90. If rates fall 50 bp the callable is worth 101.90 and if they rise 50 bp it is worth 98.70. Find its value and its effective duration.
step: Callable value: $103.40 - 2.90 = 100.50$.
step: Duration: $(101.90 - 98.70)/(2 \times 100.50 \times 0.005) = 3.20/1.005 = 3.18$.
step: A comparable straight bond would have a higher effective duration because its price keeps rising when yields fall; the call limits the callable's gain.
answer: 100.50; effective duration 3.18.

@mistakes
- Using a flat yield for every cash flow instead of the spot rate for each date.
- Adding the call option to the straight bond's value for a callable bond; the investor sold the option, so subtract it.
- Computing effective duration with yield changes of the wrong size (the denominator uses the full shift $\Delta y$ times two).
- Believing the OAS of a callable bond exceeds its Z-spread: the option cost makes OAS smaller.

@practice num
prompt: Two-year and three-year spot rates are 3.0% and 3.6% (annual compounding). What is the one-year forward rate two years from now, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 4.81
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.036 ** 3 / 1.03 ** 2 - 1) * 100
hint: Use $(1 + S_3)^3 = (1 + S_2)^2(1 + f(2,1))$.
hint: Compute $1.036^3/1.03^2 - 1$.
solution: $f(2,1) = 1.036^3/1.03^2 - 1 = 1.111933/1.0609 - 1 = 4.81\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: Yields fall sharply, and a bond that is callable at 102 moves to trade near 102. Compared with an otherwise identical straight bond, how will the callable bond behave as yields fall further?
choice: Its price will keep rising faster than the straight bond's because its coupon is higher
choice*: Its price will rise much less than the straight bond's because the call caps its value, giving negative convexity
choice: Its price will rise by the same amount, because the call option is out of the money
hint: The issuer is likely to call the bond when yields are low.
solution: When yields fall, the issuer's call option becomes valuable, so the callable bond's price is capped near the call price and its price-yield curve bends the wrong way (negative convexity). The straight bond continues to rise.

prompt: A bond is priced at 98.00. If the benchmark curve shifts down by 25 bp its price is 98.90, and if it shifts up by 25 bp its price is 97.10. What is its effective duration, to two decimal places?
answer: 3.67
unit: years

@practice mc
prompt: A callable bond has a Z-spread of 180 bp over the benchmark curve and an option-adjusted spread of 130 bp. Which statement is most accurate?
choice: The call option is worth 310 bp to the issuer
choice: The investor is compensated 130 bp more because of the call
choice*: The option cost is 50 bp: part of the 180 bp Z-spread pays the investor for having sold the call
hint: OAS equals the Z-spread less the option cost for a callable bond.
solution: For a callable bond, OAS equals Z-spread minus option cost, so the option cost is $180 - 130 = 50$ bp. The investor receives the Z-spread, but 50 bp of it compensates for the call sold to the issuer.

@quiz num
prompt: A bond's probability of default over the next year is 1.8% and its recovery rate is 45%. What is the expected loss over the year, as a percent to two decimal places?
answer: 0.99
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 1.8 * (1 - 0.45)
hint: Loss given default is one minus the recovery rate.
hint: $1.8\% \times 0.55$.
solution: $\text{LGD} = 55\%$, so $EL = 1.8\% \times 55\% = 0.99\%$.

@quiz mc
prompt: An investor compares a putable bond with an otherwise identical straight bond. Yields on bonds of similar risk rise sharply to well above the coupon rate. What is the most likely outcome?
choice: The putable bond falls more, because the put adds interest rate risk
choice*: The putable bond falls less, because the investor can sell it back at the put price
choice: Both fall by the same amount, because the option expires at maturity
hint: The put is the investor's option.
solution: A putable bond equals a straight bond plus a put, so when yields rise the put becomes valuable and limits the price decline, giving it a shorter effective duration than the straight bond.
