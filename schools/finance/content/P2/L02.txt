### Multiple regression: what the output says

A multiple regression explains $Y_i = b_0 + b_1 X_{1i} + \dots + b_k X_{ki} + \varepsilon_i$. The standard tests are:

- **Coefficient t-test.** $t = \dfrac{\hat b_j - H_0\text{ value}}{s_{\hat b_j}}$ with $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom. Reject at the 5% level if $|t|$ exceeds the critical value (about 2 for large samples).
- **F-test for the whole model.** $F = \dfrac{\text{MSR}}{\text{MSE}} = \dfrac{\text{RSS}/k}{\text{SSE}/(n-k-1)}$, where RSS is the explained (regression) sum of squares. It tests that all slope coefficients are zero.
- **Adjusted R-squared.** $\bar R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{n-1}{n-k-1}(1 - R^2)$. It penalizes extra variables and can fall when a variable adds nothing.

Read output tables in order: the sign and size of each coefficient, its t-statistic, then the F and R-squared. A vignette that gives a coefficient and its standard error is asking for a t-statistic.

### Violations and their consequences

| Problem | Symptom | Consequence | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Heteroskedasticity** (error variance varies with $X$) | Breusch-Pagan test rejects | Coefficients unbiased, but standard errors understated if conditional; t-statistics too large | Robust (White) standard errors |
| **Serial correlation** (errors correlated over time) | Durbin-Watson well below 2 (positive) | With positive correlation, standard errors understated, t too large, F too large | Robust standard errors (Newey-West), or fix the specification |
| **Multicollinearity** (regressors highly correlated) | High $R^2$ and significant F, but insignificant t's; VIF above about 5 to 10 | Standard errors inflated; coefficient estimates unreliable | Drop or combine variables |

Durbin-Watson is approximately $2(1 - r)$, where $r$ is the first-order correlation of residuals, so a value near 2 means no first-order serial correlation. Also know **model misspecification**: omitted variables, wrong functional form, pooling data from different regimes, and using a lagged dependent variable with serially correlated errors. **Dummy variables** shift the intercept for a category; a **logit** model gives a probability for a yes/no outcome.

### Time series

A **trend model** regresses a series on time, linearly or on the log of the series for exponential growth. An **autoregressive** model, AR(1), is $x_t = b_0 + b_1 x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$. It needs a **covariance-stationary** series: constant mean, variance and covariances. For AR(1), that holds when $|b_1| < 1$, and then the series reverts to its mean-reverting level

$$x^* = \frac{b_0}{1 - b_1}.$$

Forecast one step ahead by plugging in; a two-step forecast uses the one-step forecast in place of the unknown value (the chain rule). If $b_1 = 1$, the series is a **random walk** with a **unit root**: no finite mean-reverting level, nonstationary, so first-difference it before estimating. The Dickey-Fuller test examines the unit root. Also check the residuals of an AR model for serial correlation (a significant autocorrelation means the model is incomplete); watch for **seasonality** (a significant residual autocorrelation at lag 4 in quarterly data); and **ARCH** when residual variance depends on past variance. Compare forecasting models by out-of-sample root mean squared error.

### Machine learning in one paragraph

Supervised learning maps inputs to labeled outputs (regression, classification); unsupervised learning finds structure without labels (clustering, dimension reduction). The core risk is **overfitting**: a model that fits noise in the training sample and fails out of sample. Remedies are cross-validation, penalized regression, and simpler models.

@example
title: Read a regression
problem: A regression of the excess return of Larkspur Growth Fund on the market excess return (MKT) and a size factor (SMB) uses 60 monthly observations. The estimates are: intercept 0.40, MKT coefficient 0.90, SMB coefficient 0.35 with a standard error of 0.14. R-squared is 0.64. (a) Predict the fund's excess return when MKT is 1.0 and SMB is 0.5. (b) Test whether the SMB coefficient is zero at the 5% level (critical value about 2.0). (c) Compute adjusted R-squared.
step: (a) Plug in: $0.40 + 0.90(1.0) + 0.35(0.5) = 1.475$.
step: (b) $t = 0.35/0.14 = 2.50$, which exceeds 2.0 in absolute value, so reject the null: size exposure is statistically significant.
step: (c) $n = 60$, $k = 2$: $\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{59}{57}(1 - 0.64) = 1 - 1.0351 \times 0.36 = 0.6274$.
answer: 1.475; reject (t = 2.50); adjusted R-squared 0.627.

@example
title: Forecast and mean reversion
problem: A quarterly spread series follows $x_t = 0.8 + 0.6\,x_{t-1}$. The latest value is 3.0. Forecast one and two periods ahead, and find the mean-reverting level.
step: One step: $0.8 + 0.6(3.0) = 2.6$.
step: Two steps use the one-step forecast: $0.8 + 0.6(2.6) = 2.36$.
step: Mean-reverting level: $0.8/(1 - 0.6) = 2.0$. The forecasts move from 3.0 toward 2.0.
answer: 2.6, 2.36; mean-reverting level 2.0.

@mistakes
- Using $n - k$ instead of $n - k - 1$ degrees of freedom, or forgetting that R-squared never falls when you add a variable (adjusted R-squared can).
- Calling a Durbin-Watson well below 2 "negative serial correlation": below 2 signals positive correlation.
- Saying multicollinearity biases the coefficients; it inflates their standard errors.
- Treating a unit root as a stationary series with a high coefficient: at $b_1 = 1$ the mean-reverting level is undefined, and the cure is first differencing.

@practice num
prompt: An ANOVA table for a regression with 3 independent variables and 34 observations shows a regression (explained) sum of squares of 180 and a sum of squared errors of 120. Compute the F-statistic. Round to one decimal place.
answer: 15.0
tol: 0.1
check: (180 / 3) / (120 / (34 - 3 - 1))
hint: Divide each sum of squares by its degrees of freedom: $k$ for the regression and $n - k - 1$ for the error.
hint: $F = (180/3)/(120/30)$.
solution: MSR is $180/3 = 60$ and MSE is $120/30 = 4$, so $F = 15.0$.

@practice mc
prompt: An analyst regresses a bond fund's quarterly returns on a market index using 80 observations. The residuals show a Durbin-Watson statistic of 0.95, far below 2. Which conclusion is most appropriate?
choice*: Residuals are positively serially correlated, so the standard errors are likely understated and t-statistics overstated
choice: Residuals are negatively serially correlated, so the coefficient estimates are biased
choice: Residuals show no first-order serial correlation, so no correction is needed
hint: Durbin-Watson is about $2(1 - r)$.
solution: A value of 0.95 implies $r$ of roughly 0.5, so residuals are positively correlated. In the usual case this understates standard errors and overstates t-statistics, while coefficient estimates remain unbiased when the independent variables are not lagged dependent variables.

@practice mc
prompt: A model of a company's monthly revenue growth is estimated as $x_t = 0.3 + 1.0\,x_{t-1} + \varepsilon_t$, and a Dickey-Fuller test fails to reject a unit root. What should the analyst do?
choice: Use the model as it is, because the mean-reverting level is 0.3
choice*: Treat the series as a random walk with drift and estimate the model on the first differences
choice: Add more lagged values of the dependent variable to remove the unit root
hint: With a coefficient of 1 on the lagged value, the mean-reverting level formula has a zero denominator.
solution: With $b_1 = 1$ the series has a unit root, so it is not covariance stationary and has no finite mean-reverting level ($b_0/(1 - b_1)$ is undefined). The standard remedy is to first-difference the series and model the changes.

prompt: A series follows $x_t = 1.2 + 0.7\,x_{t-1}$. The current value is 5.0. What is the two-period-ahead forecast? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 4.49
tol: 0.01
check: 1.2 + 0.7 * (1.2 + 0.7 * 5.0)
hint: First forecast one period ahead.
hint: Use the one-step forecast as the input for the second step.
solution: One step: $1.2 + 0.7(5.0) = 4.70$. Two steps: $1.2 + 0.7(4.70) = 4.49$. The mean-reverting level is $1.2/0.3 = 4.0$, so the forecasts decline toward it.

@practice mc
prompt: A regression of a stock's return on five valuation variables has an R-squared of 0.78 and a significant F-statistic, but none of the five t-statistics is significant at the 5% level. Variance inflation factors range from 9 to 14. What is the most likely problem and the most appropriate response?
choice: Heteroskedasticity; use White-corrected standard errors
choice*: Multicollinearity; remove or combine highly correlated variables
choice: Serial correlation; use first differences of the dependent variable only
hint: High R-squared and a significant F-test with insignificant t's is a classic pattern.
solution: Strong overall fit with weak individual significance and large variance inflation factors is the signature of multicollinearity. It inflates standard errors, so the response is to drop or combine variables that overlap in what they measure.

@quiz num
prompt: A regression with 4 independent variables and 45 observations has an R-squared of 0.52. Compute the adjusted R-squared as a percent, to one decimal place.
answer: 47.2
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (1 - (45 - 1) / (45 - 4 - 1) * (1 - 0.52)) * 100
hint: The formula uses $n - 1$ over $n - k - 1$.
hint: $1 - (44/40)(0.48)$.
solution: $\bar R^2 = 1 - \frac{44}{40}(0.48) = 1 - 0.528 = 0.472$, or 47.2%.

@quiz mc
prompt: A Breusch-Pagan test on the residuals of a cross-sectional regression of price-to-earnings ratios on growth and risk rejects the null of no conditional heteroskedasticity. The coefficient estimates are unchanged. Which statement is most accurate?
choice: The coefficient estimates are biased, so the regression must be re-estimated with additional variables
choice*: The coefficient estimates remain unbiased, but the usual standard errors are unreliable, so the analyst should use heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors
choice: Only the F-statistic is affected, and the t-statistics may be used as reported
hint: Think about which quantities heteroskedasticity damages.
solution: Conditional heteroskedasticity leaves coefficient estimates unbiased and consistent, but the usual standard errors are wrong, typically too small, so t-statistics and the F-statistic are unreliable. White (robust) standard errors fix the inference without changing the estimates.
