### Real estate

Three valuation approaches appear: **sales comparison**, **income** and **cost**. The income approach uses **direct capitalization** or discounted cash flow. Net operating income (NOI) is rental income less operating expenses, before debt service and taxes, depreciation excluded:

$$\text{Value} = \frac{NOI_1}{\text{cap rate}}.$$

The cap rate approximates the required return less the expected growth in NOI, so it behaves like $r - g$: lower cap rates mean higher values. Compare properties using the same year's NOI. **REITs** trade like stocks and are valued by net asset value and by price to funds from operations. Appraisal-based indexes smooth returns, because appraisals lag the market: they understate volatility and correlations with other assets.

### Private equity and venture capital

Buyout funds use leverage; venture funds invest in early-stage companies. Fund terms include a **management fee** on committed or invested capital, **carried interest** (a share of profits, often 20%) subject to a **hurdle (preferred return)**, and a **waterfall** that decides the order of distributions (a European waterfall returns all capital and the hurdle before carry; an American one distributes deal by deal). Net returns are far below gross because of fees. Performance measures: **IRR** (sensitive to timing), **MOIC** or **TVPI** (total value to paid-in), $\text{TVPI} = (\text{distributions} + \text{residual value})/\text{paid-in capital}$, and **DPI** (distributions to paid-in). A **J-curve** shows negative early returns because fees and write-downs come before gains. Valuation uses discounted cash flow, comparables and exit-multiple methods; illiquidity makes marks unreliable.

### Hedge funds and other alternatives

Strategies include equity long/short, event-driven, relative value and macro. Fees are typically a management fee plus an **incentive fee**, often with a **high-water mark** (no incentive until prior losses are recovered) and a **hurdle**. Biases in reported returns include **survivorship bias** (failed funds drop out), **backfill bias** and **smoothing** of illiquid holdings; survivorship and backfill bias overstate return, and smoothing understates risk. Commodities earn returns from spot changes, **roll yield** (positive in backwardation) and collateral yield. Digital assets lack cash flows, so valuation relies on adoption, network measures and scarcity arguments; regulation is evolving (the GENIUS Act of July 2025 created a federal framework for payment stablecoins), so check current rules.

### Multifactor models and active management

A multifactor model gives expected return as

$$E(R_i) = R_f + \sum_{k}\beta_{ik}\lambda_k,$$

where $\lambda_k$ is the risk premium for factor $k$. **Active return** is portfolio return less benchmark return; **active risk** (tracking error) is the standard deviation of active return. The **information ratio** is active return over active risk. The **fundamental law of active management** says

$$IR \approx IC \times \sqrt{BR} \times TC,$$

where $IC$ is the information coefficient (skill: correlation between forecasts and outcomes), $BR$ is breadth (the number of independent bets per year) and $TC$ is the transfer coefficient (how well the portfolio expresses the views, reduced by constraints). Expected active return equals $IR \times$ active risk. Doubling breadth multiplies the information ratio by $\sqrt{2}$, not 2. Other exam topics: **active share**, **risk budgeting**, and how exchange-traded funds differ from mutual funds (creation and redemption, tax efficiency).

@example
title: Fundamental law
problem: A manager has an information coefficient of 0.05 and makes 100 independent bets a year, with a transfer coefficient of 1.0. The portfolio's active risk is 4%. What is the expected information ratio and active return?
step: $IR = 0.05 \times \sqrt{100} \times 1.0 = 0.50$.
step: Expected active return: $0.50 \times 4\% = 2.0\%$.
step: Doubling breadth to 200 raises the IR to $0.05\sqrt{200} = 0.71$, less than twice.
answer: IR 0.50; expected active return 2.0%.

@example
title: Net return after hedge fund fees
problem: A fund starts the year with \$100 million. Its gross return is 15%. It charges a 2% management fee on beginning assets and a 20% incentive fee on profits after the management fee. What is the net return?
step: Gross profit \$15.0 million; management fee \$2.0 million.
step: Incentive fee: $0.20 \times (15.0 - 2.0) = \$2.6$ million.
step: Net profit: $15.0 - 2.0 - 2.6 = \$10.4$ million, a net return of 10.4%.
answer: 10.4%.

@mistakes
- Dividing next year's NOI by the cap rate for a property valued on trailing income; use the NOI basis stated.
- Reading an appraisal-based return series as if it were market volatility; it is smoothed and understates risk.
- Treating a rise in breadth as scaling the information ratio linearly; it scales with the square root.
- Calling TVPI the same as DPI; DPI counts only cash already returned.

prompt: A manager has an information coefficient of 0.04 and a transfer coefficient of 1. She expands her coverage so that the number of independent bets per year rises from 50 to 200, with the same skill. What happens to her expected information ratio?
choice: It rises fourfold, because breadth is four times higher
choice*: It doubles, because the information ratio rises with the square root of breadth
choice: It is unchanged, because skill did not change
hint: The fundamental law uses $\sqrt{BR}$.
solution: $IR = IC \times \sqrt{BR}$. Going from 50 to 200 bets multiplies $\sqrt{BR}$ by $\sqrt{4} = 2$, so the information ratio doubles.

@quiz mc
prompt: An analyst reports that a private real estate index based on quarterly appraisals has an annual standard deviation of 5%, while a listed REIT index has an annual standard deviation of 18%. Which interpretation is most appropriate?
choice: Private real estate is truly much less risky, so it deserves a larger portfolio weight than its return suggests
choice*: Appraisals lag market values and smooth returns, so the 5% understates the true volatility and correlation with other assets
choice: The REIT index is overstated, because listed prices do not reflect value
hint: How often are appraisals updated, and what do they follow?
solution: Appraisal-based indexes smooth returns because appraisals lag transactions, so measured volatility and correlations are biased downward. The difference between the 5% and the 18% overstates the diversification benefit.
