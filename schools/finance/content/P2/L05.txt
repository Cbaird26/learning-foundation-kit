### Foreign currency: which method?

First identify the subsidiary's **functional currency**, the currency of its primary economic environment. The **presentation currency** is the currency in which the parent reports.

| Situation | Method | Where the gain or loss goes |
|---|---|---|
| Functional currency is the foreign (local) currency | **Current rate method**: translate assets and liabilities at the current rate, equity at historical rates, income at the average rate | **Cumulative translation adjustment (CTA)** in equity (other comprehensive income); no effect on net income until the subsidiary is disposed of |
| Functional currency is the parent's currency, or the economy is hyperinflationary (US GAAP) | **Temporal method (remeasurement)**: monetary items at the current rate, nonmonetary items at historical rates | **Remeasurement gain or loss in net income**, so earnings are more volatile |

Under the current rate method, a stronger foreign currency makes a subsidiary with positive net assets increase the parent's equity (a positive CTA), while a weaker foreign currency reduces it. Because all assets and liabilities are translated at the same rate, ratios that use only balance-sheet items (such as the debt-to-equity ratio) are unchanged, while ratios mixing current-rate balance-sheet items and average-rate income items change slightly. Under the temporal method, nonmonetary items such as inventory, fixed assets and equity stay at historical rates, so margins, turnover and returns shift.

The CTA when the subsidiary has no dividends in the year is the translated ending net assets less the translated beginning net assets less the translated net income:

$$\text{CTA} = NA_{end} \times S_{end} - NA_{begin} \times S_{begin} - NI \times S_{avg}.$$

**Hyperinflation** needs separate treatment: under IFRS (IAS 29) the foreign statements are restated for inflation first and then translated at the current rate; under US GAAP, when cumulative inflation over three years reaches 100%, the temporal method is used. Parent and subsidiary effective tax rates, transfer prices and the geographic segment note matter too: the headline tax rate can differ from the statutory rate because of income earned in lower-tax jurisdictions.

### Financial reporting quality

Think of quality in two dimensions: the quality of the **reporting** (is it complete and compliant?) and the quality of the **earnings** (are they sustainable, driven by operations and backed by cash?). High-quality earnings are repeatable and cash-backed; low-quality earnings come from aggressive recognition, one-time items or estimates pushed to the favorable edge.

Warning signs to know:

- **Revenue recognition**: receivables or days sales outstanding rising much faster than sales; bill-and-hold; channel stuffing; revenue ahead of performance under ASC 606 and IFRS 15 (the five-step model); changes in estimates of variable consideration.
- **Expenses**: capitalizing costs that peers expense; longer useful lives; lower reserves or bad-debt provisions; deferring maintenance.
- **Classification**: shifting items from operating to non-operating, or moving cash flows from financing to operating; reliance on non-GAAP measures with growing adjustments (IFRS 18, effective for annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2027, will require disclosure of management-defined performance measures).
- **Cash flow**: net income persistently above operating cash flow.

**Accruals ratios** quantify this. With net operating assets (NOA, operating assets less operating liabilities):

$$\text{Balance sheet accruals} = \frac{NOA_{end} - NOA_{begin}}{\text{average } NOA},$$

$$\text{Cash flow accruals} = \frac{NI - (CFO + CFI)}{\text{average } NOA}.$$

A high or rising ratio signals that earnings are growing faster than cash, so reversals are more likely. Compare with the company's history and peers. Quantitative scores that combine ratios (such as a manipulation score) are only screens, not proof.

A vignette usually gives you a small table of ratios or a description and asks "which of these is the best indicator of low quality?" or "what is the cumulative translation adjustment?"

@example
title: Cumulative translation adjustment
problem: A parent that reports in dollars owns a euro-functional subsidiary. At the start of the year the subsidiary had net assets of EUR 100 million; the rate was 1.10 USD/EUR. Net income was EUR 10 million, earned evenly through the year at an average rate of 1.15 USD/EUR. There were no dividends. The year-end rate is 1.20 USD/EUR. What is the CTA for the year under the current rate method?
step: Beginning net assets in dollars: $100 \times 1.10 = \$110.0$ million.
step: Ending net assets in euros: $100 + 10 = 110$; in dollars at the year-end rate: $110 \times 1.20 = \$132.0$ million.
step: Net income in dollars: $10 \times 1.15 = \$11.5$ million.
step: CTA: $132.0 - 110.0 - 11.5 = \$10.5$ million, a gain reported in other comprehensive income because the euro strengthened.
answer: \$10.5 million gain in equity (OCI).

@example
title: Accruals ratios
problem: A company reports net income of 80, operating cash flow of 50 and investing cash flow of -30. Net operating assets were 380 at the start and 420 at the end. Compute the two accruals ratios.
step: Average NOA: $(380 + 420)/2 = 400$.
step: Balance sheet accruals: $(420 - 380)/400 = 10.0\%$.
step: Cash flow accruals: $(80 - (50 - 30))/400 = 60/400 = 15.0\%$.
step: Both are positive and the cash flow ratio is large, so a good share of earnings did not arrive as cash, a warning sign to compare with prior years and peers.
answer: Balance sheet accruals 10.0%; cash flow accruals 15.0%.

@mistakes
- Putting a current-rate-method translation gain in net income; it goes to OCI. A temporal remeasurement gain goes to net income.
- Translating equity or inventory at the current rate under the temporal method; nonmonetary items stay at historical rates.
- Using ending instead of average net operating assets in an accruals ratio.
- Treating high operating cash flow relative to net income as a warning sign: the warning sign is net income persistently above cash flow.

prompt: A subsidiary's functional currency is the local currency. It starts the year with net assets of 200 (millions of local currency) at an exchange rate of 0.50 USD per local unit. Net income is 20, earned at an average rate of 0.52, with no dividends. The year-end rate is 0.55. Compute the cumulative translation adjustment under the current rate method, in millions of dollars to one decimal place.
answer: 10.6
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 220 * 0.55 - 200 * 0.50 - 20 * 0.52
hint: Translate ending net assets at the year-end rate, beginning net assets at the beginning rate, and net income at the average rate.
hint: $220 \times 0.55 - 200 \times 0.50 - 20 \times 0.52$.
solution: Ending net assets are $220 \times 0.55 = 121.0$, beginning net assets $100.0$ and net income $10.4$, so the CTA is $121.0 - 100.0 - 10.4 = \$10.6$ million.

@practice mc
prompt: A Brazilian subsidiary of a US parent has the parent's currency as its functional currency, so the parent applies the temporal method. During the year the Brazilian real weakens against the dollar, and the subsidiary has net monetary liabilities denominated in reais. How is the effect reported?
choice: As a translation gain in other comprehensive income, because the current rate method applies
choice*: As a remeasurement gain in net income, because net monetary liabilities fall in dollar terms when the real weakens
choice: As a remeasurement loss in net income, because a weaker real always reduces profits
hint: Monetary items are remeasured at the current rate, and the gain or loss goes to profit.
solution: Under the temporal method, monetary items are remeasured at the current rate and the result goes to net income. If the real weakens, a net monetary liability in reais is worth fewer dollars, giving a remeasurement gain.

@practice mc
prompt: A US parent reports in dollars and owns a foreign subsidiary with positive net assets whose functional currency is its local currency. The local currency depreciates against the dollar during the year, and the subsidiary has no dividends. What is the most likely effect on the parent's financial statements under the current rate method?
choice: A translation gain in net income
choice*: A negative cumulative translation adjustment in equity, reported through other comprehensive income
choice: No effect, because the subsidiary's local-currency results are unchanged
hint: Positive net assets lose dollar value when the local currency weakens.
solution: Positive net assets translated at a weaker current rate are worth fewer dollars, so the translation adjustment is a loss reported in OCI and accumulated in equity. Net income is not affected until the subsidiary is sold.

@quiz num
prompt: A company reports net income of 150, operating cash flow of 90 and investing cash flow of -40. Its average net operating assets are 500. What is the cash flow based accruals ratio, as a percent?
answer: 20
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (150 - (90 - 40)) / 500 * 100
hint: Cash flow accruals equal net income less the sum of operating and investing cash flows, divided by average net operating assets.
hint: $(150 - 50)/500$.
solution: $CFO + CFI = 50$, so accruals are $150 - 50 = 100$, and the ratio is $100/500 = 20\%$.

prompt: A subsidiary operates in an economy whose cumulative inflation over the last three years has reached 120%. The parent reports under US GAAP in US dollars. Which treatment applies to the subsidiary's statements?
choice: Current rate method, with the translation adjustment reported in other comprehensive income
choice*: Temporal method, with the remeasurement gain or loss reported in net income
