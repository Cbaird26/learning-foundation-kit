@article
Real functions are rarely bare powers or sines; they are *built* from simpler pieces: $\sin(3x)$, $\sqrt{1 + x^2}$, $e^{-x^2}$. The **chain rule** differentiates such compositions.

### The chain rule

If $y = f(u)$ and $u = g(x)$, then

$$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du}\cdot\frac{du}{dx}, \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \big[f(g(x))\big]' = f'(g(x))\,g'(x).$$

Think of gears: if $y$ turns 4 times as fast as $u$, and $u$ turns 3 times as fast as $x$, then $y$ turns $4 \times 3 = 12$ times as fast as $x$. **Rates multiply.**

In practice: differentiate the outer function, leaving the inside alone, then multiply by the derivative of the inside. For $(3x + 1)^5$ the outer function is "fifth power" and the inside is $3x + 1$:

$$\frac{d}{dx}(3x + 1)^5 = 5(3x + 1)^4 \cdot 3.$$

Patterns worth memorising, where $u = g(x)$:

- $\dfrac{d}{dx}\, u^n = n u^{n-1}\, u'$
- $\dfrac{d}{dx}\, e^u = e^u\, u'$
- $\dfrac{d}{dx}\, \sin u = \cos u \cdot u'$
- $\dfrac{d}{dx}\, \ln u = \dfrac{u'}{u}$

With several layers, peel them from the outside in, picking up one factor per layer.

### Implicit differentiation

Some curves are not graphs of the form $y = f(x)$, for example the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. Yet near most points $y$ still behaves as a function of $x$, so we may differentiate **both sides of the equation with respect to $x$**, remembering that $y$ depends on $x$. The chain rule then gives, for instance,

$$\frac{d}{dx}\big(y^2\big) = 2y\,\frac{dy}{dx}.$$

For the circle: $2x + 2y\,y' = 0$, so $y' = -\dfrac{x}{y}$. At $(3, 4)$ the slope is $-\frac{3}{4}$, perpendicular to the radius (slope $\frac{4}{3}$), as geometry predicts.

The method:

1. Differentiate every term with respect to $x$. Use the product rule on terms such as $xy$.
2. Collect all terms containing $y'$ on one side.
3. Factor out $y'$ and solve.

Substitute the coordinates of a point only **after** solving. The formula for $y'$ may contain both $x$ and $y$; that is normal. Next week the same idea works with time $t$ as the independent variable, which is exactly what related-rates problems need.

@example
title: A three-layer composition
problem: Differentiate $y = \sqrt{1 + e^{2x}}$.
step: Peel the layers from the outside in. The outer layer is $\sqrt{u}$ with $u = 1 + e^{2x}$. Its derivative with respect to $u$ is $\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}}$.
step: Differentiate the inside $u = 1 + e^{2x}$. This is another chain: $\dfrac{d}{dx} e^{2x} = e^{2x} \cdot 2$, so $u' = 2e^{2x}$.
step: Multiply the factors: $y' = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{1 + e^{2x}}} \cdot 2e^{2x}$.
step: Cancel the $2$: $y' = \dfrac{e^{2x}}{\sqrt{1 + e^{2x}}}$.

@example
title: Slope on an implicit curve
problem: Find the slope of the curve $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$ at the point $(3, 3)$.
step: First confirm the point lies on the curve: $27 + 27 = 54$ and $6 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 = 54$.
step: Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a function of $x$. The product rule applies to $6xy$: $3x^2 + 3y^2 y' = 6y + 6x\,y'$.
step: Gather the $y'$ terms on one side: $3y^2 y' - 6x\,y' = 6y - 3x^2$.
step: Factor and solve: $y' = \dfrac{6y - 3x^2}{3y^2 - 6x} = \dfrac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x}$.
step: Only now substitute $(3, 3)$: $y' = \dfrac{6 - 9}{9 - 6} = -1$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the inner derivative, for example writing $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x) = \cos(3x)$ and omitting the factor $3$.
- Differentiating $y^2$ as $2y$ and dropping the factor $y'$. Every $y$ is a function of $x$.
- Differentiating $xy$ as $x$ or as $y'$, forgetting that it is a product.
- Substituting the point's coordinates into the equation before differentiating. That turns the equation into a statement about numbers and destroys the rates you need.

@practice num
prompt: The curve $x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$ passes through $(2, 1)$. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ at that point.
answer: -5/4
check: nderiv(lambda x: root(lambda y: x**2 + x*y + y**2 - 7, 0, 2), 2)
hint: Differentiate every term with respect to $x$. The term $xy$ needs the product rule and $y^2$ needs the chain rule.
hint: You should get $2x + y + x\,y' + 2y\,y' = 0$. Solve for $y'$, then substitute $x = 2$, $y = 1$.
solution: Differentiating gives $2x + y + x\,y' + 2y\,y' = 0$, so $y' = -\dfrac{2x + y}{x + 2y}$. At $(2, 1)$: $y' = -\dfrac{5}{4}$.

@practice self
prompt: A classmate writes $\dfrac{d}{dx}\big(y^2\big) = 2y$. Explain what is missing and why. Then test your explanation on the case $y = x^3$.
hint: Is $y$ a constant, or does it change when $x$ changes?
hint: Treat $y^2$ as a composition with outer function $u^2$ and inner function $y(x)$.
solution: Since $y$ depends on $x$, $y^2$ is a composition: outer function $u^2$, inner function $y(x)$. The chain rule gives $\frac{d}{dx}(y^2) = 2y \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}$; the factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is missing. Test: if $y = x^3$ then $y^2 = x^6$, whose derivative is $6x^5$. The formula gives $2y\,y' = 2x^3 \cdot 3x^2 = 6x^5$, which matches, whereas $2y = 2x^3$ does not.

rubric: Names the chain rule with outer function $u^2$ and inner function $y(x)$.
rubric: Computes both sides for $y = x^3$ and shows $2y\,y' = 6x^5$ agrees with $\frac{d}{dx}x^6$.

@quiz expr
prompt: Differentiate $f(x) = \cos^3(2x)$.
vars: x
lead: $f'(x) =$
answer: -6 cos(2x)^2 sin(2x)
check: lambda x: nderiv(lambda t: cos(2*t)**3, x)
hint: There are three layers: a cube, a cosine and the linear function $2x$.
hint: Cube layer gives $3\cos^2(2x)$, cosine layer gives $-\sin(2x)$, inside layer gives $2$. Multiply the three factors.
solution: $f'(x) = 3\cos^2(2x) \cdot \big(-\sin(2x)\big) \cdot 2 = -6\cos^2(2x)\sin(2x)$.

@quiz num
prompt: The curve $e^y + xy = e$ passes through the point $(0, 1)$. Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ there. Give an exact answer.
answer: -1/e
check: nderiv(lambda x: root(lambda y: exp(y) + x*y - e, 0, 2), 0)
hint: Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$. The derivative of $e^y$ is $e^y\,y'$ by the chain rule.
hint: You should get $e^y\,y' + y + x\,y' = 0$. Factor out $y'$ and solve before substituting.
solution: $y'(e^y + x) = -y$, so $y' = -\dfrac{y}{e^y + x}$. At $(0, 1)$: $y' = -\dfrac{1}{e}$.
