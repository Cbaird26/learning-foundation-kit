@article
The accounting equation tells you what happened to a business in total. A real company has thousands of transactions a day and needs a record of each one and a balance for each kind of asset, liability and equity item. **Double-entry bookkeeping** solves this: every transaction is recorded with at least two parts, and the total of the amounts on the **debit** side equals the total on the **credit** side. These words only mean left and right: a **debit** is an entry on the left side of an account and a **credit** is an entry on the right side. They do not mean "good" or "bad".

### Accounts and their normal balances

An **account** is a record for one type of item (Cash, Accounts Receivable, Rent Expense). A **T-account** shows the account as a letter T, debits on the left and credits on the right. The accounting equation, extended with revenues, expenses and dividends, decides which side increases each account:

| Account type | Increases with | Decreases with | Normal balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Assets | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Expenses | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Dividends | Debit | Credit | Debit |
| Liabilities | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Contributed capital and retained earnings | Credit | Debit | Credit |
| Revenues | Credit | Debit | Credit |

Assets sit on the left of the equation, so debits increase them. Liabilities and equity sit on the right, so credits increase them. Revenues raise equity (credit) while expenses and dividends reduce it (debit). The **normal balance** is the side on which an account normally increases. For a T-account, the balance is the sum of the increases minus the sum of the decreases.

### The journal and the ledger

A **journal** is the chronological record of transactions. Each **journal entry** shows the date, the accounts debited (listed first) and credited (indented) and the amounts. A **compound entry** involves more than two accounts. The **ledger** is the collection of all accounts. **Posting** is copying each journal amount to the matching ledger account. Software does the posting automatically, but you must understand what it does.

A **trial balance** lists every account's balance with debits in one column and credits in the other. If the two totals differ, an error was made. If they agree, the ledger is in balance, but errors can still hide: a wrong account chosen with the right amount, an omitted transaction, or two offsetting mistakes.

### Steps in analysis

For each transaction: (1) identify the accounts affected, (2) classify each as asset, liability, equity, revenue, expense or dividend, (3) decide whether each increases or decreases, (4) apply the debit-credit rules and check that debits equal credits.

@example
title: Journalizing five transactions
problem: Pine Hollow Cycles has these transactions in January. Jan 2: owners invest \$25,000 cash for stock. Jan 5: buys equipment for \$9,000, paying \$3,000 cash and signing a note for the rest. Jan 9: performs repairs for \$4,200 on account. Jan 12: pays \$1,100 rent. Jan 20: collects \$2,500 from a customer. Write the journal entries.
step: Jan 2. Cash (asset) rises, so debit it. Common stock (equity) rises, so credit it.
step: Jan 5 is a compound entry. Equipment rises by \$9,000 (debit). Cash falls by \$3,000 (credit) and Notes Payable rises by \$6,000 (credit). Debits \$9,000 equal credits \$3,000 + \$6,000.
step: Jan 9 earns revenue now, even though no cash has arrived: debit Accounts Receivable, credit Service Revenue. Jan 12 is a rent expense (debit) paid in cash (credit). Jan 20 swaps one asset for another: debit Cash, credit Accounts Receivable. Revenue is not recorded again.
step: The journal:

```text
Jan 2   Cash                         25,000
          Common Stock                         25,000
Jan 5   Equipment                     9,000
          Cash                                  3,000
          Notes Payable                         6,000
Jan 9   Accounts Receivable           4,200
          Service Revenue                       4,200
Jan 12  Rent Expense                  1,100
          Cash                                  1,100
Jan 20  Cash                          2,500
          Accounts Receivable                   2,500
```
answer: Five entries, each with equal debits and credits (the Jan 5 entry has three lines).

@mistakes
- Thinking debit means increase and credit means decrease for every account. It depends on the account type.
- Recording collection of a receivable as revenue. The revenue was recorded when the service was performed; collection swaps one asset for another.
- Crediting Revenue when a customer pays in advance. The company owes service, so the credit goes to Unearned Revenue, a liability.
- Assuming that a balanced trial balance proves the books are correct. It cannot detect wrong accounts, omitted entries or offsetting errors.

tests:
```python
entry1 = [("Cash", 2500000, 0), ("Stock", 0, 2500000)]
entry2 = [("Equipment", 900000, 0), ("Cash", 0, 300000),
          ("Notes", 0, 600000)]
assert post([entry1, entry2]) == {
    "Cash": 2200000, "Stock": -2500000,
    "Equipment": 900000, "Notes": -600000}
assert post([]) == {}
swap = [[("A", 100, 0), ("B", 0, 100)], [("A", 0, 100), ("B", 100, 0)]]
assert post(swap) == {"A": 0, "B": 0}
assert raises(ValueError, post, [[("Cash", 100, 0), ("Rev", 0, 90)]])
assert raises(ValueError, post, [entry1, [("Cash", 50, 0)]])
```
model:
```python
def post(journal):
    """Post balanced journal entries; return account balances in cents."""
    ledger = {}
    for entry in journal:
        debits = sum(debit for _, debit, _ in entry)
        credits = sum(credit for _, _, credit in entry)
        if debits != credits:
            raise ValueError("entry does not balance")
        for account, debit, credit in entry:
            ledger[account] = ledger.get(account, 0) + debit - credit
    return ledger
```
hint: Check each entry's debit total against its credit total before posting any of its lines.
hint: Keep a dictionary and add `debit - credit` to each account's running balance.
solution: Loop over the entries, sum each entry's debits and credits, and raise `ValueError` if they differ. Otherwise add each line's debit minus credit to its account in a dictionary. Accounts that net to zero stay in the dictionary with a balance of 0.
