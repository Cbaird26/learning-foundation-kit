@article
A company can report profit and still run out of cash, because accrual accounting records revenue when it is earned and expenses when they are incurred, and not when cash moves. The **statement of cash flows** reports the cash a company actually received and paid during the period and explains the change in cash and cash equivalents (cash plus short-term, highly liquid investments with original maturities of three months or less; under US GAAP restricted cash is included as well).

### Three activities

- **Operating activities:** cash effects of the transactions that make up net income: collections from customers, payments to suppliers and employees, interest and taxes paid.
- **Investing activities:** buying and selling long-lived assets and investments, and making and collecting loans.
- **Financing activities:** raising and repaying debt and equity: borrowing, repaying principal, issuing and repurchasing stock, paying dividends.

Under US GAAP, interest paid, interest received and dividends received are operating, and dividends paid are financing. IFRS allows interest and dividends paid to be classed as operating or financing, and interest and dividends received as operating or investing, if the choice is applied consistently. **Noncash investing and financing activities** (buying equipment by signing a note) are disclosed separately.

### The indirect method

The **direct method** lists cash collected and paid. The **indirect method**, used by nearly all companies, starts with net income and adjusts it:

$$\text{CFO} = \text{Net income} + \text{Noncash expenses} - \text{Gains} + \text{Losses} \pm \Delta\,\text{Working capital}.$$

| Item | Adjustment to net income |
|---|---|
| Depreciation and amortization | Add back (no cash paid in the period) |
| Gain on sale of an asset | Subtract (the cash proceeds are in investing) |
| Loss on sale of an asset | Add back |
| Increase in a current operating asset (receivables, inventory, prepaids) | Subtract |
| Decrease in a current operating asset | Add |
| Increase in a current operating liability (payables, accruals, unearned revenue) | Add |
| Decrease in a current operating liability | Subtract |

An increase in receivables means that some of the revenue in net income has not yet been collected, so it is subtracted; an increase in payables means that some expense has not yet been paid, so it is added. For the direct method, for example, cash collected from customers = revenue − increase in receivables.

### Investing and financing

Find investing flows from changes in long-term assets: equipment purchased = increase in equipment cost (plus the cost of any equipment sold). Find financing flows from changes in debt and equity: new notes and stock issued are inflows, principal repaid is an outflow, and cash dividends paid are an outflow. Then

$$\text{Net change in cash} = \text{CFO} + \text{CFI} + \text{CFF},$$

and the beginning cash plus this change equals ending cash on the balance sheet. A common measure of the cash available after investment is **free cash flow**, which analysts often define as CFO minus capital expenditures. It is not a GAAP measure, so definitions vary.

@example
title: Preparing the statement from comparative balance sheets
problem: Tamarack Supply reports net income of \$24,000 and paid dividends of \$8,000. It sold no equipment. Its balance sheets show:

| Account | Start | End |
|---|---|---|
| Cash | 20,000 | 40,000 |
| Accounts receivable | 30,000 | 38,000 |
| Inventory | 40,000 | 35,000 |
| Prepaid expenses | 5,000 | 6,000 |
| Equipment | 100,000 | 130,000 |
| Accumulated depreciation | (30,000) | (40,000) |
| Accounts payable | 25,000 | 29,000 |
| Wages payable | 4,000 | 3,000 |
| Notes payable | 20,000 | 35,000 |
| Common stock | 50,000 | 60,000 |
| Retained earnings | 66,000 | 82,000 |

Prepare the statement of cash flows.
step: Operating. Depreciation = 40,000 − 30,000 = 10,000 (add back). Receivables rose 8,000 (subtract), inventory fell 5,000 (add), prepaids rose 1,000 (subtract), payables rose 4,000 (add), wages payable fell 1,000 (subtract). CFO = 24,000 + 10,000 − 8,000 + 5,000 − 1,000 + 4,000 − 1,000 = 33,000.
step: Investing. With no sales, equipment purchased = 130,000 − 100,000 = 30,000. CFI = −30,000.
step: Financing. Notes rose 15,000 (inflow), stock rose 10,000 (inflow), dividends paid 8,000 (outflow). CFF = 15,000 + 10,000 − 8,000 = 17,000.
step: Net change = 33,000 − 30,000 + 17,000 = 20,000. Beginning cash 20,000 + 20,000 = 40,000, which matches the balance sheet.
answer: CFO 33,000; CFI −30,000; CFF 17,000; cash rises by 20,000 to 40,000.

@example
title: A sale of equipment
problem: Wrenfield Co. sells equipment that cost \$20,000 and has accumulated depreciation of \$14,000 for \$9,000 cash. Net income is \$35,000, depreciation expense is \$8,000 and receivables rose by \$2,000. How are these shown in the indirect method?
step: Book value = 20,000 − 14,000 = 6,000. Gain = 9,000 − 6,000 = 3,000.
step: Operating: 35,000 + 8,000 − 3,000 (gain) − 2,000 (receivables) = 38,000. The gain is subtracted because the whole 9,000 of proceeds belongs in investing.
step: Investing: proceeds from sale of equipment of +9,000.
answer: CFO \$38,000; investing inflow of \$9,000.

@mistakes
- Adding depreciation to net income and calling it a source of cash. It is added back only because it was deducted without any cash being paid.
- Getting the signs of working-capital changes backwards. An increase in an operating asset reduces cash; an increase in an operating liability raises it.
- Leaving a gain on sale in operating cash flow and also showing the proceeds in investing, which counts the gain twice.
- Putting dividends paid in operating cash flows under US GAAP. They are financing.

@quiz self
prompt: A fast-growing retailer reports positive net income, but its cash flow from operations is negative. In four or five sentences, give two reasons this can happen and say which statement shows it.
hint: Think about receivables and inventory when a company grows quickly.
solution: Accrual net income includes revenue that has not yet been collected, so growing receivables reduce operating cash relative to income. A growing retailer must also build inventory and pay suppliers before it sells the goods, so rising inventory consumes cash that is not an expense yet. Other possible reasons are paying down payables faster than they grow. The statement of cash flows shows this: its indirect-method reconciliation starts from net income and subtracts the increases in receivables and inventory to reach operating cash flow.
