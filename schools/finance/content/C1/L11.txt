@article
Users trust financial statements only if the numbers are reliable. **Internal control** is a process, carried out by a company's board, management and other personnel, that provides *reasonable assurance* about three objectives: effective and efficient operations, reliable financial reporting, and compliance with laws and regulations. The most widely used framework is the COSO *Internal Control: Integrated Framework* (2013 edition), which has five components: the **control environment** (integrity, ethical values, board oversight), **risk assessment**, **control activities**, **information and communication**, and **monitoring**.

### Control activities

- **Segregation of duties:** no one person should authorize a transaction, record it and have custody of the related asset. The person who opens the mail and deposits customer checks should not also post the payments to customer accounts or reconcile the bank account.
- **Authorization and approval:** purchases above a limit need a second signature.
- **Documentation:** prenumbered documents, and receipts kept for every disbursement.
- **Physical controls:** safes, locked inventory rooms, restricted system access.
- **Independent verification:** comparing recorded amounts to outside evidence, such as the **bank reconciliation**.

Controls have limits: collusion between employees can defeat segregation of duties, management can override controls, and controls must be worth their cost, so assurance is *reasonable*, not absolute.

### Fraud and ethics

The **fraud triangle** names three conditions that tend to be present when fraud occurs: **pressure** (financial need or an earnings target), **opportunity** (weak controls) and **rationalization** ("I will pay it back"). **Misappropriation of assets** (theft) and **fraudulent financial reporting** (misstating the statements, for example by recording revenue early or understating expenses) are the two main categories. Accountants are bound by professional codes that demand integrity, objectivity and competence, and public confidence rests on being able to raise concerns without retaliation.

### Sarbanes-Oxley and auditors

The **Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 (SOX)**, passed after a wave of reporting failures in 2001–2002, created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to oversee auditors of public companies. Section 302 requires the CEO and CFO to certify each periodic report, and Section 404 requires management to assess and report on the effectiveness of internal control over financial reporting (ICFR). Larger public companies (large accelerated filers, and accelerated filers that are not smaller reporting companies with under \$100 million of annual revenue) also obtain an auditor's opinion on ICFR; non-accelerated filers are exempt from that auditor attestation (Section 404(b)). An auditor's opinion on the statements can be **unqualified** (clean), **qualified**, **adverse** or a **disclaimer**; auditor reports also discuss **critical audit matters**.

### Bank reconciliation

The balance on the bank statement and the balance in the company's Cash account differ for predictable reasons. Reconcile both to the true cash balance:

- **Bank side:** add **deposits in transit**, subtract **outstanding checks**, and correct bank errors.
- **Book side:** add collections and interest the bank has credited, subtract service charges and **NSF checks** (customer checks returned for insufficient funds), and correct book errors. Each book-side item needs a journal entry; bank-side items do not.

A **petty cash** fund is a small imprest fund (a fixed amount) for minor expenses. Spending is supported by receipts. To replenish, cash is added equal to the fund's fixed amount minus the cash remaining, and expenses are debited from the receipts.

@example
title: A bank reconciliation
problem: At month end, Lindenwood Co. has a bank statement balance of \$18,450 and a Cash account balance of \$17,000. Deposits in transit are \$2,300 and outstanding checks are \$3,150. The bank collected a \$1,200 note receivable for the company, credited \$20 of interest, charged a \$45 service fee and returned a customer's \$575 check marked NSF. Reconcile and record entries.
step: Bank side: \$18,450 + \$2,300 − \$3,150 = \$17,600.
step: Book side: \$17,000 + \$1,200 + \$20 − \$45 − \$575 = \$17,600. The two sides agree, so the true cash balance is \$17,600.
step: Entries on the books:

```text
Cash                             1,200
  Notes Receivable                         1,200
Cash                                20
  Interest Revenue                            20
Bank Service Charges                45
  Cash                                        45
Accounts Receivable                575
  Cash                                       575
```
step: The NSF check is debited back to Accounts Receivable because the customer still owes the money. Deposits in transit and outstanding checks need no entry, since the company has already recorded them.
answer: Adjusted cash balance \$17,600.

@example
title: Replenishing petty cash
problem: A petty cash fund of \$200 has receipts for postage (\$62) and supplies (\$106) and \$27 of cash. Record the replenishment.
step: Receipts total \$168 and cash on hand is \$27, so the total accounted for is \$195. The fund is short by \$200 − \$195 = \$5.
step: The replenishment check is the fund amount minus the cash on hand: \$200 − \$27 = \$173.
step: Entry: debit Postage Expense 62, Supplies 106 and Cash Short and Over 5; credit Cash 173. Debits 173 equal credits 173.
answer: Replenish with \$173; the \$5 shortage is recorded in Cash Short and Over.

@mistakes
- Adjusting the book balance for outstanding checks or deposits in transit. They are bank-side items and require no journal entry.
- Subtracting an NSF check from the bank balance instead of from the book balance. The bank has already removed it.
- Believing that strong controls guarantee that no fraud occurs. They give only reasonable assurance.
- Giving one person the roles of authorizing, recording and holding the asset because the company is small.

@quiz self
prompt: A small company's single bookkeeper opens the mail, deposits customer checks, records the receipts, writes the checks to suppliers and prepares the bank reconciliation. Using the fraud triangle, explain in four or five sentences why this is risky and recommend two specific control changes.
hint: Which element of the fraud triangle does the lack of controls create?
solution: The bookkeeper has complete opportunity to take cash and conceal it, since no one else sees the records. If personal financial pressure appears, a rationalization such as intending to repay the money is easy to find, so all three conditions of the fraud triangle can be met. The owner should separate duties: for example, a second person opens the mail and lists receipts and the bookkeeper records them, and the owner (who does not record transactions) reviews the bank reconciliation and signs checks. Required vacations and unannounced reviews of reconciliations would add detection.
rubric: Identifies that the lack of segregation of duties gives opportunity, linking it to pressure and rationalization.
rubric: Recommends at least two concrete controls, such as separating receipt, recording and reconciliation duties, or owner review of the reconciliation.
rubric: Explains that controls give reasonable assurance and detect or deter fraud.
