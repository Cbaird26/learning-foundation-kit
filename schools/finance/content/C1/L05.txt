@article
When does a company earn revenue: when it signs a contract, ships the product or collects the cash? Under **ASC 606** (US GAAP) and **IFRS 15** (which are largely converged), a company recognizes revenue when it transfers promised goods or services to a customer, in the amount it expects to be entitled to. A **five-step model** applies the principle:

1. Identify the **contract** with the customer.
2. Identify the **performance obligations**, the distinct goods or services promised.
3. Determine the **transaction price**, the amount the company expects to receive (adjusted for discounts, returns and other variable consideration).
4. **Allocate** the transaction price to each performance obligation in proportion to **standalone selling prices**.
5. Recognize revenue when (or as) each performance obligation is **satisfied**, that is, when control passes to the customer, at a point in time or over time.

If a customer pays before the company performs, the company records a **contract liability** (often called Unearned Revenue). If the company performs before it can bill, it records a **contract asset**; once only time must pass, it is a receivable.

$$\text{Allocated price}_i = \text{Transaction price} \times \frac{\text{Standalone price}_i}{\sum \text{Standalone prices}}.$$

### Accounts receivable and credit losses

Selling on credit creates **accounts receivable**: debit Accounts Receivable and credit Revenue when the performance obligation is satisfied. Some customers will not pay. The matching idea says the expected loss belongs to the period of the sale, before the company knows which customer will default. So the **allowance method** estimates losses and records them in an **Allowance for Credit Losses** (formerly "allowance for doubtful accounts"), a contra-asset:

- **Estimate:** debit Bad Debt Expense (credit losses), credit the Allowance.
- **Write off** a specific account: debit Allowance, credit Accounts Receivable. This does not change net receivables or net income.
- **Recovery** of a written-off account: reinstate (debit Receivable, credit Allowance), then record the cash collection.

The **direct write-off** method (expense recorded only when an account proves uncollectible) violates matching and is acceptable only when amounts are immaterial. US GAAP's CECL model (ASC 326) and IFRS 9 require allowances based on **expected credit losses**, drawing on history, current conditions and forecasts. The two methods below are practical estimation tools.

**Percentage of credit sales** (income statement approach): expense = credit sales × estimated loss rate. The allowance's existing balance is ignored. **Aging of receivables** (balance sheet approach): classify receivables by days past due, apply a higher loss rate to older ones and sum to the required ending allowance. Then expense = required allowance − existing credit balance (or + existing debit balance).

### Receivables analysis

$$\text{Receivables turnover} = \frac{\text{Net credit sales}}{\text{Average net receivables}}, \qquad \text{DSO} = \frac{365}{\text{turnover}}.$$

**Days sales outstanding** (DSO) estimates the average days to collect. Here a 365-day year is used.

@example
title: Allocating a bundled price
problem: Quillfeather Software sells a customer a software license (standalone price \$800) and 12 months of support (standalone price \$400) for a bundle price of \$1,080 cash on January 1. The license is delivered on January 1 and support is provided evenly over the year. How much revenue is recognized on January 1 and in each month afterward?
step: There are two performance obligations. The standalone prices total \$1,200.
step: License: \$1,080 × 800/1,200 = \$720. Support: \$1,080 × 400/1,200 = \$360.
step: On January 1, debit Cash 1,080; credit License Revenue 720 and Unearned Support Revenue (contract liability) 360.
step: Each month, debit Unearned Support Revenue 30 and credit Support Revenue 30, since \$360 / 12 = \$30.
answer: \$720 on January 1, then \$30 each month.

@example
title: Allowance by aging
problem: Bellwether Foods has \$200,000 of receivables. Aging shows: not yet due \$140,000 (2% estimated uncollectible), 1–30 days past due \$40,000 (5%), 31–90 days \$15,000 (15%) and over 90 days \$5,000 (40%). The Allowance has a \$3,000 credit balance before adjustment. Find the adjusting entry, then record the write-off of a \$1,200 account.
step: Required allowance: 2,800 + 2,000 + 2,250 + 2,000 = \$9,050.
step: Bad debt expense = required allowance − existing credit balance = 9,050 − 3,000 = \$6,050. Entry: debit Bad Debt Expense 6,050; credit Allowance 6,050.
step: Net receivables are 200,000 − 9,050 = \$190,950.
step: Write-off of \$1,200: debit Allowance 1,200; credit Accounts Receivable 1,200. Receivables are \$198,800 and the allowance is \$7,850, so net receivables stay at \$190,950 and no expense is recorded.
answer: Bad debt expense \$6,050; the write-off does not change net receivables.

@mistakes
- Debiting Bad Debt Expense at the time of a write-off under the allowance method. The expense was recorded when the allowance was estimated.
- Under the aging method, setting expense equal to the required allowance without adjusting for the allowance's existing balance.
- Recognizing revenue when cash is received or the contract is signed instead of when control transfers.
- Allocating a bundle's price equally to the items, rather than in proportion to their standalone selling prices.

@quiz self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain how a write-off of a customer's \$1,500 account affects net income and net receivables under (a) the allowance method and (b) the direct write-off method, and say which method GAAP generally requires.
hint: Under the allowance method, when was the expense recorded?
solution: Under the allowance method, the write-off debits the Allowance and credits Accounts Receivable, so net receivables and net income are unchanged: the loss was recognized earlier when the allowance was estimated. Under the direct write-off method, the write-off debits Bad Debt Expense, so net income falls by \$1,500 in the period of write-off, which may be long after the sale, and receivables fall by \$1,500. GAAP generally requires the allowance method (expected credit losses under CECL), because it matches the loss to the period of the related revenue.
rubric: States that under the allowance method the write-off leaves net income and net receivables unchanged.
rubric: States that under direct write-off, expense and the reduction of receivables are recorded at write-off, lowering net income by \$1,500 that period.
rubric: Names the allowance method (expected credit losses) as the GAAP requirement and links it to matching.
