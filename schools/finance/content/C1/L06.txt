@article
A merchandiser buys goods at different prices during the year. When it sells some, which cost belongs in cost of goods sold, and which stays in inventory? If the physical goods are identical, no law of nature decides, so accounting standards allow **cost flow assumptions**. The choice moves cost between the income statement and the balance sheet, and so it changes reported profit.

### Cost of goods sold

Inventory is recorded at cost: the purchase price plus freight-in and other costs of bringing it to its location and condition, less discounts and returns. Then

$$\text{Goods available for sale} = \text{Beginning inventory} + \text{Purchases},$$

$$\text{COGS} = \text{Goods available for sale} - \text{Ending inventory}.$$

Under a **periodic** system the company counts ending inventory and computes COGS at period end. Under a **perpetual** system it updates inventory and COGS at every sale. The examples here are periodic, with all purchases made before the sales are costed. Under **specific identification**, each unit sold is matched to its actual cost, which suits unique items such as cars or art.

### Three cost flow methods

- **FIFO** (first in, first out): the oldest costs go to COGS, so ending inventory holds the latest costs.
- **LIFO** (last in, first out): the newest costs go to COGS, so ending inventory holds the oldest costs.
- **Weighted average:** every unit costs the average, $\text{total cost available}/\text{units available}$.

When prices rise, FIFO has the lowest COGS, the highest gross profit and net income, and an ending inventory close to current cost. LIFO has the highest COGS and the lowest income and taxable income, but its inventory is stated at old costs and may be far below replacement cost. Weighted average falls in between. If prices fall, the ranking reverses. Total COGS over the life of the business is the same under every method, since all costs eventually leave inventory. Only the timing differs.

**LIFO** is permitted under US GAAP but not under IFRS. In the United States, a company that uses LIFO for its tax return must also use it in its financial statements (the LIFO conformity rule). LIFO users disclose a **LIFO reserve**, the difference between FIFO and LIFO inventory, so analysts can compare them. A **LIFO liquidation**, selling more units than were bought, charges old low costs to COGS and inflates profit.

### Lower of cost and net realizable value

Inventory must not be carried above what it can earn. For FIFO and weighted average (and under IFRS for every method), inventory is reported at the **lower of cost and net realizable value** (NRV), where

$$\text{NRV} = \text{Estimated selling price} - \text{Costs of completion and disposal}.$$

If NRV is below cost, write the inventory down by the difference. (US GAAP uses lower of cost or market for LIFO and the retail inventory method.) Under US GAAP, the write-down is not reversed later, while IFRS allows a reversal up to the original cost.

@example
title: Three methods, one data set
problem: Fernhill Supply starts the year with 100 units at \$10. It buys 200 units at \$12 on March 5 and 100 units at \$15 on August 10. It sells 300 units during the year for \$20 each. Using a periodic system, compute COGS, ending inventory and gross profit under FIFO, LIFO and weighted average.
step: Goods available: 400 units costing \$1,000 + \$2,400 + \$1,500 = \$4,900. Ending inventory is 100 units. Revenue is 300 × \$20 = \$6,000.
step: FIFO. COGS = 100 × \$10 + 200 × \$12 = \$3,400. Ending inventory is the 100 newest units: 100 × \$15 = \$1,500.
step: LIFO. COGS = 100 × \$15 + 200 × \$12 = \$3,900. Ending inventory is 100 × \$10 = \$1,000.
step: Weighted average. Average cost = \$4,900 / 400 = \$12.25. COGS = 300 × \$12.25 = \$3,675; ending inventory = 100 × \$12.25 = \$1,225.
step: Summary. In each case COGS plus ending inventory equals \$4,900.

| Method | COGS | Ending inventory | Gross profit |
|---|---|---|---|
| FIFO | \$3,400 | \$1,500 | \$2,600 |
| Weighted average | \$3,675 | \$1,225 | \$2,325 |
| LIFO | \$3,900 | \$1,000 | \$2,100 |
answer: FIFO COGS \$3,400; LIFO \$3,900; weighted average \$3,675.

@mistakes
- Confusing the cost flow assumption with the physical flow of goods. A company can use LIFO while shipping its oldest units first.
- Taking LIFO's ending inventory from the newest layers. Under LIFO, ending inventory consists of the oldest costs.
- Computing the average cost as a simple average of the unit prices (such as (10 + 12 + 15)/3) instead of dividing total cost by total units.
- Writing inventory down to selling price instead of net realizable value, forgetting to subtract the costs of completion and disposal.

model:
```python
def periodic_cogs(layers, units_sold, method):
    """Cost of goods sold under FIFO, LIFO or weighted average."""
    available = sum(units for units, _ in layers)
    if units_sold > available or method not in ("fifo", "lifo", "average"):
        raise ValueError("invalid sale or method")
    if units_sold == 0:
        return 0
    if method == "average":
        total_cost = sum(units * cost for units, cost in layers)
        return units_sold * total_cost / available
    ordered = layers if method == "fifo" else list(reversed(layers))
    remaining = units_sold
    cogs = 0
    for units, cost in ordered:
        taken = min(units, remaining)
        cogs += taken * cost
        remaining -= taken
    return cogs
```
hint: Handle the average method separately: total cost divided by total units, times units sold.
hint: For FIFO and LIFO, walk through the layers (reversed for LIFO), taking `min(units, remaining)` from each.
solution: Validate the inputs first. For the average method, multiply units sold by total cost over total units. For FIFO and LIFO, order the layers (newest first for LIFO) and take units from each layer until the units sold are used up, adding units taken times cost to COGS.

solution: FIFO charges the older, lower costs to COGS, so COGS falls and reported gross profit and net income rise relative to LIFO.
