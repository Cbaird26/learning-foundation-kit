@article
A delivery van will help earn revenue for years, so expensing its whole price in the year of purchase would distort every year's profit. Instead, a company **capitalizes** the cost, recording it as an asset, and then spreads it over the periods that benefit through **depreciation**. Long-lived assets include **property, plant and equipment** (PP&E), natural resources and intangible assets.

### Cost of an asset

The **capitalized cost** includes everything needed to acquire the asset and make it ready for use: the purchase price net of discounts, sales taxes, freight, installation and testing. Ordinary repairs and maintenance that merely keep the asset working are **expensed**. Expenditures that extend its life or increase its capacity are capitalized. Land is not depreciated, since its life is not limited.

### Depreciation methods

Depreciation is a systematic **allocation** of cost, not a valuation of the asset. Three inputs are estimated: **cost**, **salvage value** (the expected residual value) and **useful life**. The depreciable base is cost minus salvage.

- **Straight-line:** $\text{Expense} = \dfrac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage}}{\text{Useful life}}$, the same each year.
- **Double-declining-balance (DDB):** an accelerated method with $\text{Expense}_t = \dfrac{2}{\text{Life}} \times \text{Book value at start of year}_t$. Salvage is ignored in the rate, but book value is never taken below salvage, so the last year's expense is the amount that brings book value down to salvage.
- **Units of production:** $\text{Rate per unit} = \dfrac{\text{Cost} - \text{Salvage}}{\text{Total expected units}}$, and expense is the rate times the units used in the period (miles, machine hours, units made).

All three methods total the same depreciable base over the asset's life. They differ in timing: DDB gives the largest expense in early years. If an asset is placed in service partway through the year, the first year's straight-line expense is prorated by months. Estimates change over time. A change in useful life or salvage value is applied **prospectively**: remaining book value less new salvage is spread over the remaining life. (Tax depreciation, such as the US MACRS system, follows its own rules and differs from book depreciation.)

### Disposal

When an asset is sold, first record depreciation up to the date of sale. Then remove the cost and accumulated depreciation and compare the cash received with the **book value** (cost minus accumulated depreciation):

$$\text{Gain or loss} = \text{Proceeds} - \text{Book value}.$$

### Impairment and intangibles

If events suggest an asset's value has dropped, the asset is tested for **impairment**. Under US GAAP, an asset held for use is impaired when its carrying amount exceeds the undiscounted future cash flows it will generate. The loss is the carrying amount minus fair value, and it is not reversed later. IFRS (IAS 36) compares the carrying amount with the **recoverable amount**, the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use; reversals are allowed except for goodwill, and IFRS also permits a revaluation model that US GAAP does not.

**Intangible assets** lack physical substance. Finite-life intangibles (patents, licenses) are **amortized**, which is depreciation by another name. Goodwill and other indefinite-life intangibles are not amortized for public companies but are tested for impairment at least annually. Under US GAAP, research costs and most internal development costs are expensed as incurred; IFRS capitalizes development costs once specific criteria are met.

@example
title: Straight-line and double-declining-balance
problem: Redwood Cabinets buys a machine for \$45,000 with a salvage value of \$5,000 and a four-year life. Compute each year's depreciation under straight-line and DDB.
step: Straight-line: (\$45,000 − \$5,000) / 4 = \$10,000 a year.
step: DDB: the rate is 2/4 = 50%. Year 1: 50% × \$45,000 = \$22,500, so book value falls to \$22,500. Year 2: 50% × \$22,500 = \$11,250, book value \$11,250. Year 3: 50% × \$11,250 = \$5,625, book value \$5,625.
step: Year 4: 50% × \$5,625 = \$2,812.50 would take book value below salvage of \$5,000, so the expense is limited to \$5,625 − \$5,000 = \$625.
step: Both methods total \$40,000. DDB is larger in early years: \$22,500 against \$10,000 in year 1.
answer: Straight-line \$10,000 a year; DDB \$22,500, \$11,250, \$5,625 and \$625.

@example
title: Units of production and a disposal
problem: Part A. A press costing \$90,000 has salvage value \$6,000 and is expected to make 280,000 units over its life. It makes 41,500 units this year. What is depreciation? Part B. Another asset cost \$40,000 and has accumulated depreciation of \$31,500 at the date of sale (depreciation through that date is already recorded). It is sold for \$6,000 cash. Record the sale.
step: Part A. Rate = (\$90,000 − \$6,000) / 280,000 = \$0.30 per unit. Expense = 41,500 × \$0.30 = \$12,450.
step: Part B. Book value = \$40,000 − \$31,500 = \$8,500. Proceeds are \$6,000, so there is a loss of \$8,500 − \$6,000 = \$2,500.
step: Entry:

```text
Cash                           6,000
Accumulated Depreciation      31,500
Loss on Sale of Equipment      2,500
  Equipment                                 40,000
```
step: Debits are 6,000 + 31,500 + 2,500 = 40,000, which equals the credit to Equipment.
answer: Depreciation \$12,450; loss on sale \$2,500.

@mistakes
- Subtracting salvage value from the book value in the DDB rate calculation. DDB applies the rate to book value (cost less accumulated depreciation) and ignores salvage until the last year's limit.
- Depreciating land, or treating depreciation as the decline in the asset's market value.
- Expensing the freight and installation cost of a new machine instead of capitalizing it.
- Recording a gain or loss on sale by comparing proceeds with original cost instead of with book value.

model:
```python
def ddb_schedule(cost, salvage, life):
    """Annual double-declining-balance depreciation."""
    book_value = cost
    schedule = []
    for year in range(1, life + 1):
        if year == life:
            expense = book_value - salvage
        else:
            expense = min(book_value * 2 / life, book_value - salvage)
        schedule.append(expense)
        book_value -= expense
    return schedule
```
hint: Keep the beginning-of-year book value in a variable and update it each year.
hint: In the final year, use `book_value - salvage`; in other years use the smaller of the DDB amount and `book_value - salvage`.
solution: Loop over the years while tracking book value. The DDB amount is `book_value * 2 / life`, capped so that book value does not go below salvage, and the last year takes whatever remains above salvage. The total always equals cost minus salvage.
