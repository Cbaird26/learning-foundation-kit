@article
A dollar buys less every year in most economies. **Inflation** is the percentage rate of increase in the general price level; **deflation** is a sustained fall, and **disinflation** is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise. To measure it we need a price index.

### The consumer price index

The **consumer price index (CPI)**, published by the US Bureau of Labor Statistics, prices a fixed basket of goods and services bought by typical urban consumers:

$$\text{CPI}_t = \frac{\text{cost of the basket at year-}t\text{ prices}}{\text{cost of the same basket at base-year prices}} \times 100,
\qquad \pi_t = \frac{\text{CPI}_t}{\text{CPI}_{t-1}} - 1.$$

Because the weights are fixed, the CPI is a Laspeyres price index. It tends to overstate the rise in the cost of living for four reasons: **substitution bias** (consumers switch away from goods that became relatively expensive, but the basket does not), **new goods** (they enter the basket late), **quality change** (a price rise may reflect a better product) and **outlet substitution** (shifting to discount stores). Statistical agencies adjust for some of these.

### CPI, the deflator and the Fed's preferred index

The **GDP deflator** (Lesson 1) covers all domestically produced goods and uses current-year weights, so it includes investment and government goods but excludes imports. The CPI covers what consumers buy, including imports. The **PCE price index** (personal consumption expenditures, from the BEA) has broader coverage and updating weights; the Federal Reserve states its longer-run inflation goal of 2 percent in terms of PCE inflation. **Core** inflation excludes food and energy, whose prices are volatile.

### Nominal and real rates

Lenders care about purchasing power. The **Fisher equation** links the nominal interest rate $i$, the real interest rate $r$ and expected inflation $\pi^e$:

$$1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi^e} - 1 \approx i - \pi^e.$$

The *ex ante* real rate uses expected inflation; the *ex post* real rate uses actual inflation. Real wages work the same way: divide a nominal wage by a price index.

### Why inflation is costly

Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth from lenders to borrowers on fixed-rate nominal loans. Even expected inflation causes **menu costs** (repricing), **shoe-leather costs** (effort to economize on cash) and distortions where tax systems are not indexed. Very high inflation also makes relative prices hard to read.

### Unemployment

The **labor force** is the employed ($E$) plus the unemployed ($U$): a person is unemployed if they have no job, are available for work and have actively looked in the past four weeks. Then

$$u = \frac{U}{E + U}, \qquad \text{participation rate} = \frac{E + U}{\text{adult civilian non-institutional population}}.$$

A **discouraged worker** stops looking and leaves the labor force, which *lowers* the measured unemployment rate. **Frictional** unemployment comes from the time it takes to match workers and jobs, **structural** unemployment from a mismatch of skills or location, and **cyclical** unemployment from recessions. The **natural rate of unemployment** is the rate around which the actual rate fluctuates, when cyclical unemployment is zero.

To see what sets the natural rate, let $s$ be the monthly fraction of employed people who lose their job (separation rate) and $f$ the monthly fraction of unemployed people who find one. In a steady state the flows balance, $sE = fU$. With $E = L - U$ and $u = U/L$ this gives

$$u^* = \frac{s}{s + f}.$$

Higher separation or slower job finding raises the natural rate. All figures in this course are hypothetical; current inflation and unemployment data are on FRED.

@example
title: Building a CPI and inflation in a small basket
problem: A basket contains 50 meals, 10 units of housing and 20 transit rides. Prices are in the table. Find the CPI in each year (base year 0) and inflation in year 2.

| Item | Quantity | Price, year 0 | Price, year 1 | Price, year 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Meal | 50 | \$3.00 | \$3.30 | \$3.45 |
| Housing unit | 10 | \$200 | \$210 | \$216 |
| Transit ride | 20 | \$5.00 | \$5.50 | \$5.40 |
step: Cost of the basket in year 0: $50(3.00) + 10(200) + 20(5.00) = 150 + 2000 + 100 = \$2{,}250$.
step: Year 1: $50(3.30) + 10(210) + 20(5.50) = 165 + 2100 + 110 = \$2{,}375$. Year 2: $172.50 + 2160 + 108 = \$2{,}440.50$.
step: CPI year 1 is $2375/2250 \times 100 = 105.56$; CPI year 2 is $2440.50/2250 \times 100 = 108.47$.
step: Inflation in year 2 is $108.47/105.56 - 1 = 2440.50/2375 - 1 = 2.76\%$.
answer: CPI 100, 105.56, 108.47; year-2 inflation 2.76%.

@example
title: Unemployment and a discouraged worker effect
problem: An economy has 8,000 thousand adults in the civilian non-institutional population, of whom 4,900 thousand are employed and 300 thousand are unemployed. Find the unemployment rate and participation rate. Then 100 thousand unemployed people give up looking and leave the labor force; recompute.
step: Labor force $= 4900 + 300 = 5{,}200$ thousand. Unemployment rate $= 300/5200 = 5.77\%$. Participation rate $= 5200/8000 = 65.0\%$.
step: After 100 thousand leave, unemployed $= 200$ thousand and the labor force is $5{,}100$ thousand. The unemployment rate is $200/5100 = 3.92\%$.
step: The participation rate falls to $5100/8000 = 63.75\%$. No one found a job, yet the measured rate fell almost two points: this is why the participation rate and the employment-population ratio ($4900/8000 = 61.25\%$) must be read alongside the unemployment rate.
answer: 5.77% and 65.0% at first; 3.92% and 63.75% afterward.

@mistakes
- Computing inflation as the difference in index levels (108.47 - 105.56) instead of the percentage change in the index.
- Using the approximation $r \approx i - \pi^e$ when the problem asks for the exact real rate, or using actual inflation when a problem asks for the ex ante rate.
- Treating a fall in the unemployment rate as unambiguously good news without checking whether people left the labor force.
- Mixing up the CPI (fixed basket, includes imports) and the GDP deflator (current output, excludes imports).

@practice num
prompt: Each month 2% of employed workers lose their jobs and 30% of unemployed workers find jobs. Assuming the labor force is constant and flows are balanced, what is the steady-state unemployment rate? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 6.25
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 0.02 / (0.02 + 0.30) * 100

solution: $u^* = s/(s + f) = 0.02/0.32 = 6.25\%$.

```python
got = cpi_index([30, 4, 12], [[2, 150, 4], [2.2, 156, 4.4], [2.1, 165, 4.5]])

```
model:
```python
def cpi_index(quantities, price_table):
    """CPI for each period, using the first period as the base (= 100)."""
    costs = [sum(q * p for q, p in zip(quantities, prices))
             for prices in price_table]
    return [100 * cost / costs[0] for cost in costs]
```
hint: Compute the cost of the basket in each period first.
hint: Cost is the sum of quantity times price over items; divide each period's cost by the first period's cost.
solution: A fixed-weight index multiplies each price by its fixed quantity, sums to get the basket cost, and scales by the base cost. The last test would fail for an unweighted average of price changes (which gives 150), because the quantities weight the items.
