@article
Macroeconomics asks questions about whole economies: why output grows, why prices rise, why people lose jobs, what a central bank can do about it. Every one of those questions begins with measurement. If you cannot say how much an economy produced last year, you cannot say whether it is growing.

### What GDP measures

**Gross domestic product (GDP)** is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period. Each word matters:

- **Final**: only goods sold to their end user count. Flour bought by a baker is an intermediate good; the bread is final. Counting both would count the flour twice.
- **Produced**: only current production counts. Selling a ten-year-old house is not GDP, but the real estate agent's commission is, because the agent's service was produced this year. Purchases of existing shares, transfer payments (Social Security, unemployment benefits) and unpaid housework are not counted.
- **Within borders**: a factory owned by foreigners but located at home counts in home GDP. **Gross national product (GNP)** instead counts output produced by a country's residents wherever they are: $\text{GNP} = \text{GDP} + \text{net factor income from abroad}$. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) publishes GNP, and also gross national income, which is GNP adjusted for the statistical discrepancy.

### Three ways to add it up

**Expenditure approach.** Final output must be bought by someone:

$$Y = C + I + G + NX, \qquad NX = X - M,$$

where $C$ is household consumption, $I$ is investment (business equipment, structures, residential construction and changes in inventories; buying shares is *not* investment in this sense), $G$ is government purchases of goods and services (not transfers), $X$ is exports and $M$ is imports. Imports are subtracted because they are included in $C$, $I$ and $G$ but were not produced at home.

**Value-added approach.** A firm's **value added** is its sales minus the intermediate goods it buys. Summing value added over every firm and every stage gives GDP. If a farmer sells wheat for \$40 to a miller, the miller sells flour for \$100 to a baker, and the baker sells bread for \$250, then value added is \$40, \$60 and \$150, which sum to the \$250 price of the final bread.

**Income approach.** Every dollar of value added becomes someone's income: wages, profits, rent and interest, plus indirect taxes and depreciation. Total income equals total output, which is why the three approaches agree in principle (in practice, a *statistical discrepancy* remains).

### Nominal, real and the deflator

**Nominal GDP** values current output at current prices, so it rises when either quantities or prices rise. **Real GDP** values output at the prices of a fixed *base year*, so it changes only when quantities change. The **GDP deflator** is the price index that links them:

$$\text{Deflator}_t = \frac{\text{Nominal GDP}_t}{\text{Real GDP}_t} \times 100.$$

Real growth is $g = \dfrac{\text{Real GDP}_t}{\text{Real GDP}_{t-1}} - 1$. Because $\dfrac{\text{Nominal}_t}{\text{Nominal}_{t-1}} = \dfrac{\text{Real}_t}{\text{Real}_{t-1}} \times \dfrac{\text{Deflator}_t}{\text{Deflator}_{t-1}}$, nominal growth is approximately real growth plus deflator inflation.

A fixed base year becomes misleading as relative prices change, so the BEA uses **chain-weighted** real GDP: each year-to-year growth rate is a **Fisher index**, the geometric mean of a Laspeyres index (base-year weights) and a Paasche index (current-year weights), and the yearly growth rates are chained together.

### The saving identity

Start from $Y = C + I + G + NX$. Define private saving $S_p = Y - T - C$ and public saving $T - G$ (negative when the government runs a deficit), where $T$ is taxes net of transfers. Then national saving satisfies

$$S = S_p + (T - G) = I + NX.$$

An economy that saves more than it invests must lend the difference abroad by running positive net exports.

@example
title: Real GDP, the deflator and growth in Larkspur
problem: Larkspur produces wheat (bushels) and tablets. In 2025, the base year, wheat sells at \$2.00 with 100 bushels produced and tablets at \$300 with 10 produced. In 2026, wheat sells at \$2.50 with 120 bushels and tablets at \$280 with 11 produced. Find nominal and real GDP for each year, the 2026 deflator, real growth and deflator inflation.
step: Nominal GDP, 2025: $2.00 \times 100 + 300 \times 10 = 200 + 3000 = \$3{,}200$. Nominal GDP, 2026: $2.50 \times 120 + 280 \times 11 = 300 + 3080 = \$3{,}380$.
step: Real GDP uses base-year prices. In 2025 it equals nominal GDP, \$3,200. In 2026, $2.00 \times 120 + 300 \times 11 = 240 + 3300 = \$3{,}540$ at 2025 prices.
step: The 2026 deflator is $3380/3540 \times 100 = 95.48$. The 2025 deflator is 100, so deflator inflation is $-4.52\%$: the average price fell because tablets became cheaper.
step: Real growth is $3540/3200 - 1 = 10.625\%$. Nominal growth was only $3380/3200 - 1 = 5.625\%$, because falling prices offset part of the quantity growth.
step: The Paasche quantity index (2026 prices) is $3380/3050 = 1.1082$ and the Laspeyres index is $3540/3200 = 1.10625$, so the Fisher index is $\sqrt{1.10625 \times 1.1082} = 1.1072$ and chained growth is about 10.72%.
answer: Real GDP \$3,200 and \$3,540 (2025 prices); deflator 95.48; real growth 10.625%; deflator inflation -4.52%.

@example
title: Expenditure, income and saving in Bellwether
problem: In Bellwether, consumption is \$620 billion, investment \$170 billion, government purchases \$200 billion, exports \$80 billion and imports \$70 billion. Taxes net of transfers are \$180 billion. Find GDP, private saving, public saving, national saving and check the identity.
step: GDP by expenditure: $Y = 620 + 170 + 200 + (80 - 70) = \$1{,}000$ billion.
step: Private saving: $Y - T - C = 1000 - 180 - 620 = \$200$ billion. Public saving: $T - G = 180 - 200 = -\$20$ billion, a budget deficit.
step: National saving is $200 + (-20) = \$180$ billion.
step: Check: $I + NX = 170 + 10 = \$180$ billion. The identity holds, so Bellwether lends \$10 billion to the rest of the world.
answer: GDP \$1,000 billion; national saving \$180 billion, equal to $I + NX$.

@mistakes
- Counting intermediate goods (or the sale of used goods and financial assets) in GDP, which double counts or counts output from a past period.
- Treating government transfer payments as part of $G$; only purchases of goods and services are in GDP.
- Adding imports instead of subtracting them, or forgetting that imports are already inside $C$, $I$ and $G$.
- Computing real growth from nominal GDP without removing the price change, or using the deflator as if it measured only consumer prices.

@quiz num
prompt: A miller buys wheat for \$70 and sells flour to a baker for \$180. The baker sells bread to consumers for \$400, using only that flour as an intermediate input. What is the baker's value added in dollars?
answer: 220
lead: \$
check: 400 - 180
hint: Value added is sales minus the intermediate goods purchased.
solution: The baker's value added is $400 - 180 = \$220$. Across the chain, value added is $70 + 110 + 220 = \$400$, which is the price of the final bread.
