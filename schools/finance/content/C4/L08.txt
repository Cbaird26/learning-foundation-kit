@article
IS-LM held the price level fixed. To study inflation we let it move. **Aggregate demand (AD)** relates the price level to the quantity of output demanded; **aggregate supply (AS)** relates the price level to the quantity supplied. Their intersection gives output and the price level.

### Aggregate demand from IS-LM

A higher price level lowers real money balances $M/P$, shifting LM left: the interest rate rises and output demanded falls. That gives AD a negative slope. For the model of Lesson 7 with IS: $Y = 3200 - 80r$ and LM: $M/P = 0.5Y - 40r$ and $M = 1000$, substitute $r = (3200 - Y)/80$ into the LM equation:

$$\frac{1000}{P} = 0.5Y - 0.5(3200 - Y) = Y - 1600 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad Y^{AD} = 1600 + \frac{1000}{P}.$$

At $P = 1$, $Y = 2600$ (the Lesson 7 equilibrium); at $P = 1.25$, $Y = 2400$. AD shifts right with an increase in $G$, a cut in $T$, more optimistic investment, a higher money supply $M$, or higher foreign demand; it shifts left with the opposites.

### Aggregate supply

In the **long run** prices and wages adjust and output returns to its natural level $Y_n$ (determined by capital, labor and technology, Lesson 3), so long-run AS is vertical. In the **short run**, prices and wages are sticky: if the actual price level exceeds what workers and firms expected, output rises above $Y_n$. Short-run AS slopes up: $Y = Y_n + a(P - P^e)$.

The **expectations-augmented Phillips curve** expresses the same idea in terms of inflation and unemployment:

$$\pi = \pi^e - \beta(u - u_n) + \nu,$$

where $\pi^e$ is expected inflation, $u_n$ the natural rate of unemployment, $\beta > 0$ the sensitivity of inflation to labor-market slack and $\nu$ a **supply shock** (an oil price jump, say). Low unemployment ($u < u_n$) pushes inflation above expectations, and a supply shock raises inflation while lowering output: **stagflation**.

**Okun's law** links output and unemployment. As a rule of thumb used here, each percentage point of unemployment above the natural rate goes with about $\theta = 2$ percent of output below potential: $\tilde y = -\theta(u - u_n)$. The coefficient is an empirical regularity that varies over time and across countries.

### Adjustment and the policy trade-off

Suppose AD rises. In the short run output rises above $Y_n$ and the price level rises. With output above $Y_n$, unemployment is below $u_n$, wages and prices rise, expectations adjust, and SRAS shifts up until output returns to $Y_n$ at a higher price level. In the long run, demand policy changes prices, not output: **monetary neutrality**.

If expectations are **adaptive** ($\pi^e$ equals last year's inflation), holding $u$ below $u_n$ requires ever-rising inflation: there is no permanent trade-off. Bringing inflation down requires a period of slack. The **sacrifice ratio** is the cumulative output loss per point of inflation reduction. With $\beta = 0.5$, cutting inflation by 1 point needs $1/\beta = 2$ point-years of cyclical unemployment, an output loss of $\theta \times 2 = 4\%$ of a year's output. If expectations adjust quickly because the central bank is credible, the cost is lower (Lesson 12).

@example
title: Phillips curve, acceleration and disinflation
problem: Suppose $\pi^e$ equals last year's inflation, $\beta = 0.5$, $u_n = 5\%$, $\theta = 2$ and there are no supply shocks. Inflation was 2% last year (so $\pi^e = 2\%$) and policy holds unemployment at 4%. Find inflation for the next three years, and then the output cost of bringing inflation from the third-year level back to 2%.
step: Year 1: $\pi_1 = 2 - 0.5(4 - 5) = 2.5\%$. Year 2: $\pi^e = 2.5$ so $\pi_2 = 3.0\%$. Year 3: $\pi_3 = 3.5\%$. Inflation accelerates by 0.5 points a year while $u$ stays below $u_n$.
step: Output is above potential by $\tilde y = -2(4 - 5) = +2\%$ in each of these years.
step: To bring inflation down by 1.5 points, we need a cumulative $\sum (u - u_n) = 1.5/0.5 = 3$ point-years of cyclical unemployment.
step: The output cost is $2 \times 3 = 6\%$ of one year's potential output. This is the price of undoing the expansion.
answer: Inflation 2.5%, 3.0%, 3.5%; disinflation back to 2% costs about 6% of a year's potential output.

@example
title: Two policy shocks in IS-LM
problem: $C = 150 + 0.8(Y - T)$, $I = 350 - 30r$, $G = 300$, $T = 250$, money demand $L = 0.4Y - 20r$ and $M/P = 600$. Find the equilibrium, and then analyze (a) $G$ rises by 40, (b) $M/P$ rises by 100, and (c) both.
step: Baseline IS: $Y = 150 + 0.8(Y - 250) + 350 - 30r + 300$, so $0.2Y = 600 - 30r$ and $Y = 3000 - 150r$. LM: $Y = 1500 + 50r$. Equilibrium: $r = 7.5$ and $Y = 1875$.
step: (a) The multiplier is $1/0.2 = 5$, so IS becomes $Y = 3200 - 150r$. Equilibrium: $3200 - 150r = 1500 + 50r$ gives $r = 8.5$ and $Y = 1925$. Output is up 50, and the rate is up 1 point.
step: (b) LM: $700 = 0.4Y - 20r$ gives $Y = 1750 + 50r$. With the original IS: $3000 - 150r = 1750 + 50r$, so $r = 6.25$ and $Y = 2062.5$. Output is up 187.5 and the interest rate is down 1.25 points.
step: (c) With both: $3200 - 150r = 1750 + 50r$ gives $r = 7.25$ and $Y = 2112.5$. Monetary accommodation keeps the interest rate nearly unchanged, so crowding out is small.
step: In AD-AS terms, each shock shifts AD right. If the economy was at $Y_n = 1875$, output above potential will put upward pressure on prices and the economy will return to $Y_n$ at a higher price level.
answer: (a) $Y = 1925$, $r = 8.5\%$; (b) $Y = 2062.5$, $r = 6.25\%$; (c) $Y = 2112.5$, $r = 7.25\%$.

@mistakes
- Treating the AD curve as a market demand curve; its slope comes from real balances and interest rates, not substitution between goods.
- Thinking that demand policy raises output permanently; in the long run, it only changes prices (monetary neutrality).
- Reading the Phillips curve as a stable menu of inflation and unemployment: if expectations adjust, the trade-off is temporary.
- Forgetting that the Phillips curve includes expected inflation and supply shocks, so inflation can rise when unemployment is also rising.

Analyze two policy shocks: (a) taxes rise from 300 to 360; (b) taxes rise to 360 and the central bank raises $M/P$ to 945. prompt: In the Phillips curve $\pi = \pi^e - \beta(u - u_n) + \nu$, expected inflation is 3.0%, $\beta = 0.4$, unemployment is 6.5% against a natural rate of 5.5%, and a supply shock adds $\nu = 1.0$ point. What is inflation? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 3.6
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 3.0 - 0.4 * (6.5 - 5.5) + 1.0
hint: Slack of 1 point lowers inflation by $\beta$; the supply shock raises it.
solution: $\pi = 3.0 - 0.4(1.0) + 1.0 = 3.6\%$: despite slack in the labor market, inflation is above expectations because of the supply shock (stagflation).
