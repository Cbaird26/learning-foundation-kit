@article
Over decades, differences in the growth rate of output per person decide living standards far more than the business cycle does. The **Solow model** is the basic tool for thinking about it: output comes from capital and labor, people save part of income, capital wears out, and the population grows.

### Assumptions

The economy is closed. Time is discrete (years). Output is $Y = K^{\alpha}L^{1-\alpha}$ (Cobb-Douglas, constant returns to scale), where $K$ is capital, $L$ is labor and $0 < \alpha < 1$ is capital's share of income. A constant fraction $s$ of output is saved and invested; capital depreciates at rate $\delta$; labor grows at the constant rate $n$. In per-worker terms, with $k = K/L$ and $y = Y/L$,

$$y = k^{\alpha}.$$

### The law of motion

Next year's capital per worker equals this year's, plus investment per worker $sy$, minus **break-even investment** $(n + \delta)k$: depreciation replaces worn-out capital and $nk$ equips new workers.

$$\Delta k = s\,k^{\alpha} - (n + \delta)k.$$

When $sk^{\alpha}$ exceeds $(n+\delta)k$, capital per worker rises; when it is smaller, capital per worker falls. Because of diminishing returns, the first term flattens as $k$ rises, so the two meet at a unique positive **steady state** $k^*$ where $\Delta k = 0$:

$$k^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + \delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}, \qquad y^* = (k^*)^{\alpha}, \qquad c^* = (1 - s)\,y^*.$$

### What the model says

- A higher **saving rate** raises $k^*$, $y^*$ and the *level* of income, but without technological progress it does not raise the long-run *growth rate* of output per worker, which is zero. Growth is only faster during the transition.
- Higher **population growth** $n$ lowers $k^*$ and $y^*$ per worker.
- **Convergence**: economies with the same parameters but less capital grow faster toward the same steady state (*conditional convergence*).
- **Golden rule**: consumption per worker $c^* = (1-s)(k^*)^\alpha$ is maximized at $s = \alpha$. Saving above that rate is dynamically inefficient.

### Technological progress

Let labor-augmenting technology $A$ grow at rate $g$, with $Y = K^{\alpha}(AL)^{1-\alpha}$. Define $\tilde k = K/(AL)$. Then break-even investment becomes $(n + g + \delta)\tilde k$ and

$$\tilde k^* = \left(\frac{s}{n + g + \delta}\right)^{\frac{1}{1-\alpha}}.$$

In the steady state, output per worker $Y/L$ grows at $g$, total output at about $n + g$ and capital per worker at $g$: technology is the source of sustained growth in living standards.

### Growth accounting

Taking logs and differentiating the production function gives

$$g_Y = \alpha\, g_K + (1 - \alpha)\, g_L + g_A.$$

The last term, the **Solow residual** (total factor productivity growth), is the part of output growth not explained by measured inputs. It is computed as $g_A = g_Y - \alpha g_K - (1-\alpha)g_L$ and is a catch-all that captures technology, but also measurement error and efficiency changes.

@example
title: Steady state and the golden rule
problem: Take $\alpha = 1/3$, $n = 0.01$, $\delta = 0.05$ and $s = 0.24$. Find $k^*$, $y^*$ and $c^*$. Then compare $c^*$ at $s = 0.30$ and at the golden-rule rate.
step: Here $n + \delta = 0.06$ and $1/(1-\alpha) = 1.5$. So $k^* = (0.24/0.06)^{1.5} = 4^{1.5} = 8$.
step: $y^* = 8^{1/3} = 2$. Check the steady state: saving per worker is $0.24 \times 2 = 0.48$ and break-even investment is $0.06 \times 8 = 0.48$. Consumption is $c^* = 0.76 \times 2 = 1.52$.
step: At $s = 0.30$: $k^* = 5^{1.5} = 11.180$, $y^* = 5^{0.5} = 2.236$, $c^* = 0.70 \times 2.236 = 1.565$.
step: The golden-rule rate is $s = \alpha = 1/3$: $k^* = (1/3/0.06)^{1.5} = 13.095$, $y^* = 2.357$, $c^* = (2/3) \times 2.357 = 1.571$. Consumption rises as $s$ goes from 0.24 to 1/3 and would fall beyond it.
answer: $k^* = 8$, $y^* = 2$, $c^* = 1.52$; $c^* = 1.565$ at $s = 0.30$ and $1.571$ at the golden-rule $s = 1/3$.

@example
title: Transition to the steady state
problem: Using the same parameters ($\alpha = 1/3$, $s = 0.24$, $n + \delta = 0.06$), an economy starts with $k_0 = 2$. Find $k_1$ and $k_2$ and explain the direction of change.
step: $y_0 = 2^{1/3} = 1.2599$. Investment is $0.24 \times 1.2599 = 0.3024$ and break-even investment is $0.06 \times 2 = 0.12$, so $\Delta k_0 = 0.1824$ and $k_1 = 2.1824$.
step: $y_1 = 2.1824^{1/3} = 1.2971$. Investment is $0.3113$, break-even is $0.1309$, so $\Delta k_1 = 0.1804$ and $k_2 = 2.3627$.
step: Capital rises each year because investment exceeds break-even investment, but the increments shrink as $k$ approaches $k^* = 8$, so growth slows.
answer: $k_1 = 2.1824$ and $k_2 = 2.3627$; capital per worker rises toward 8 at a slowing pace.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that population growth enters break-even investment alongside depreciation: use $n + \delta$, not $\delta$ alone.
- Saying a higher saving rate raises the long-run growth rate of output per worker; it raises the level, and growth is temporarily higher.
- Confusing $\tilde k^*$ (capital per effective worker) with capital per worker; with technology, capital per worker grows at $g$ in the steady state.
- Treating the Solow residual as pure technology: it also absorbs anything else that is unmeasured.

model:
```python
def solow_path(k0, s, alpha, n, delta, periods):
    """Capital per worker over time in the Solow model."""
    path = [k0]
    k = k0
    for _ in range(periods):
        k = k + s * k ** alpha - (n + delta) * k
        path.append(k)
    return path
```
hint: Start the list with `k0` and apply the law of motion once per period.
solution: Each loop pass computes investment per worker and subtracts break-even investment. Starting below the steady state, capital rises toward the steady state; starting above it (the last test), capital falls, showing that the steady state is stable.

@quiz self
prompt: Two economies have identical saving rates, population growth, depreciation and technology, but economy A has half the capital per worker of economy B, and both are below their common steady state. Explain in three or four sentences, using the Solow model, which grows faster in the short run and why.
hint: Compare the gap between saving per worker and break-even investment in each economy.
solution: Economy A grows faster. Because of diminishing returns, the marginal product of capital is higher where capital per worker is lower, and the gap between saving per worker $sk^{\alpha}$ and break-even investment $(n+\delta)k$ is larger in proportional terms for A. Both converge to the same steady state, so A catches up. This is conditional convergence: it holds only when the economies share the same parameters.
rubric: States that diminishing returns to capital imply a higher growth rate of capital per worker for the economy with less capital.
rubric: Refers to saving (investment) per worker versus break-even investment.
rubric: Notes that convergence is to the same steady state and is conditional on identical parameters.
