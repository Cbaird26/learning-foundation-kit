@article
You probably used money today without thinking about what it is. **Money** is any asset widely accepted as payment. It does three jobs: a **medium of exchange** (it avoids the double coincidence of wants in barter), a **unit of account** (prices are quoted in it) and a **store of value**. Modern money is **fiat money**: it has value because the government declares it legal tender and people accept it, not because it is convertible into a commodity. (The US dollar stopped being convertible into gold in 1971.)

### Measuring the money supply

The Federal Reserve publishes **monetary aggregates**. **M1** is currency in circulation plus balances in checkable and savings deposits (since May 2020 savings deposits are included). **M2** adds small-denomination time deposits and retail money market fund balances. The **monetary base** $B$ is currency plus bank reserves, the liabilities of the central bank. Definitions change, so check the Federal Reserve for the current ones.

### What banks do

A bank takes deposits and makes loans. Its balance sheet (a T-account) lists:

| Assets | Liabilities and equity |
|---|---|
| Reserves (cash and balances at the central bank) | Deposits |
| Loans | Borrowings |
| Securities | Equity capital |

Reserves are only a small share of assets: banks perform **maturity transformation**, funding long-term loans with short-term deposits that depositors can withdraw on demand. That is profitable, but it is what makes banks vulnerable to runs (Lesson 10).

### Deposit creation and the money multiplier

Suppose people deposit \$1,000 in cash, banks must hold a fraction $rr$ of deposits as reserves, and every bank lends out all it can. A bank lends $(1 - rr)\times$ the deposit; the borrower spends it; the seller deposits it; and the next bank lends $(1-rr)$ of that. Total deposits form a geometric series:

$$D = 1000\left[1 + (1-rr) + (1-rr)^2 + \dots\right] = \frac{1000}{rr}.$$

The **simple deposit multiplier** is $1/rr$. Two leakages reduce it. If the public holds currency equal to a fraction $cu = C/D$ of deposits, and banks hold reserves $re = R/D$ (required plus excess) of deposits, then the money supply $M = C + D$ and the base $B = C + R$ give

$$\frac{M}{B} = \frac{cu + 1}{cu + re}.$$

With $cu = 0$ and $re = rr$ this collapses to $1/rr$.

*How realistic is the textbook story?* In a modern system, a bank creates a deposit at the moment it makes a loan, crediting the borrower's account; the central bank supplies reserves to settle payments between banks. In the United States reserve requirements have been zero since March 2020, so what limits lending is capital, liquidity needs, risk and loan demand, not a required ratio. The multiplier is a useful accounting framework but not a mechanical supply rule.

### The quantity theory of money

The **quantity equation** is an identity defining **velocity** $V$:

$$M V = P Y,$$

where $Y$ is real output and $P$ the price level. In growth rates, $\%\Delta M + \%\Delta V \approx \%\Delta P + \%\Delta Y$. The **quantity theory** adds the assumptions that velocity is stable and output is determined by real factors (the Solow model), so that in the long run inflation equals money growth minus real output growth. Velocity does in fact vary with interest rates and financial innovation, so the theory is a long-run guide.

@example
title: Deposit expansion round by round
problem: People deposit \$1,000 in cash in the banking system. The reserve ratio is 10% and banks lend out every dollar they can. Show the first rounds and the total change in deposits.
step: Round 1: deposit \$1,000, reserves \$100, new loans \$900.
step: Round 2: the \$900 is redeposited: deposit \$900, reserves \$90, new loans \$810. Round 3: deposit \$810, reserves \$81, loans \$729.
step: After 10 rounds deposits total $1000(1 - 0.9^{10})/0.1 = \$6{,}513.22$; the limit is $1000/0.10 = \$10{,}000$.
step: Of the \$10,000, the original \$1,000 was cash and \$9,000 is new deposit money created by lending. Reserves end at $0.10 \times 10{,}000 = \$1{,}000$, equal to the cash deposited.
answer: Total deposits rise by \$10,000 (a multiplier of 10); \$9,000 is created by lending.

@example
title: Money multiplier with currency drain and excess reserves
problem: The monetary base is \$500 billion. The public holds currency equal to 20% of deposits, and banks hold total reserves of 12% of deposits. Find the money multiplier and the money supply.
step: With $cu = 0.20$ and $re = 0.12$: $M/B = (0.20 + 1)/(0.20 + 0.12) = 1.20/0.32 = 3.75$.
step: $M = 3.75 \times 500 = \$1{,}875$ billion.
step: Check: $D = M/(1 + cu) = 1875/1.2 = 1{,}562.5$, $C = 0.2 \times 1562.5 = 312.5$, $R = 0.12 \times 1562.5 = 187.5$, and $C + R = 500 = B$.
answer: Multiplier 3.75; money supply \$1,875 billion.

@mistakes
- Using $1/rr$ when the public holds currency or banks hold excess reserves; the true multiplier is smaller.
- Treating the money multiplier as a constant of nature, or saying banks can lend only what customers have previously deposited at that very bank.
- Confusing the monetary base (central bank liabilities) with the money supply (M1, M2).
- Applying the quantity equation to inflation without allowing for changes in velocity and real output.

model:
```python
def total_deposits(initial, rr, rounds):
    """Total deposits after a given number of rounds of expansion."""
    total = 0.0
    deposit = initial
    for _ in range(rounds):
        total += deposit
        deposit *= 1 - rr
    return total
```
hint: Keep the current round's deposit in a variable and shrink it by $(1 - rr)$ after adding it.
solution: Each pass adds the current deposit to the total, then multiplies it by $1 - rr$ to get the next round's deposit. With 500 rounds the sum is within $10^{-6}$ of the limit $\text{initial}/rr$, and with $rr = 1$ there is no expansion beyond the first deposit.
