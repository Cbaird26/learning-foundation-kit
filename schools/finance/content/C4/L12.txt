@article
Households and firms do not merely react to policy: they anticipate it. Whether wage setters believe the central bank will keep inflation at 2% changes how much unemployment it takes to achieve that.

### How expectations are formed

Under **adaptive expectations**, people extrapolate from the past: $\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}$. Mistakes are systematic when inflation trends. Under **rational expectations**, people use all available information, including their understanding of how policy works, so forecast errors are unpredictable. The truth is probably in between: survey and market-based measures show expectations moving gradually and responding to policy communication.

### The Lucas critique and policy effectiveness

The **Lucas critique** says that the statistical relationships estimated under one policy regime (such as the Phillips curve) can break down when the regime changes, because people's behavior depends on the policy they expect. The **policy ineffectiveness proposition** goes further: if prices are fully flexible and expectations are rational, only *unanticipated* changes in policy move output; systematic, anticipated policy affects prices only. Most economists accept the logic but not the strong conclusion, since wages and prices are sticky, so even anticipated policy has real effects in the short run.

### Credibility, commitment and time inconsistency

A policymaker may promise low inflation but be tempted, once expectations are set, to create a surprise expansion to cut unemployment. People who foresee this temptation expect high inflation, so the equilibrium has high inflation and no gain in output: the policy is **time inconsistent**. Solutions are commitment devices: **rules**, an **independent central bank** with an inflation mandate (insulated from short-term political pressure, which is the sense in which the Federal Reserve is independent), and **inflation targeting** with a public numerical goal. Credibility lowers the **sacrifice ratio**: if the public believes the announced target, expected inflation falls immediately and little slack is needed. Independence is debated: critics point to accountability and democratic legitimacy, and defenders to the evidence that insulated central banks achieve lower inflation. In *Trump v. Slaughter* (June 29, 2026) the Supreme Court overruled *Humphrey's Executor* (1935), so the President may remove FTC commissioners at will; the Court treated the Federal Reserve as distinct and left its independence in place, and how the ruling applies to other agencies is still being worked out.

### Currency depreciation: a worked framework

A depreciation of $d$ (a rise in $S$) works through several channels. (1) **Trade**: the trade balance changes by about $X(\eta_X + \eta_M - 1)d$ when initially balanced, so it improves only if the Marshall-Lerner condition holds, perhaps after a J-curve delay. (2) **Prices**: import prices rise by the pass-through rate times $d$, and consumer price inflation rises by that times the import share of the consumption basket in the first round, with further effects if wages respond. (3) **Demand**: higher net exports raise aggregate demand by the multiplier, if there is slack. (4) **Balance sheets**: firms and governments with debts denominated in foreign currency see their debts rise in domestic-currency terms. (5) **Policy**: the central bank balances stronger demand and higher inflation against weaker balance sheets.

### Current debates

Economists disagree on several questions, and this course describes the main positions without taking sides.

- **How much slack is there, and how flat is the Phillips curve?** Estimates of the natural rate and the neutral real rate $r^*$ are uncertain and revised, so policy rules are guides, not mechanical settings.
- **Supply or demand?** Inflation episodes can result from demand, supply shocks or both, which changes how much unemployment a central bank must accept to reduce inflation.
- **Balance sheet policy.** QE and QT, their effects on long-term yields, financial stability and fiscal costs.
- **Fiscal-monetary interaction.** Whether high public debt could constrain central banks, and views such as **modern monetary theory**, which argues a sovereign issuing its own currency is limited by inflation rather than by finance; most economists emphasize that inflation, interest costs and debt dynamics (Lesson 9) are constraints.
- **Money and payments.** The GENIUS Act (July 18, 2025) created a federal framework for payment stablecoins, and central bank digital currencies remain debated.

@example
title: The cost of disinflation under different expectations
problem: Inflation is 5% and the target is 2%. The Phillips curve is $\pi = \pi^e - 0.4(u - u_n)$, with Okun's coefficient $\theta = 2$. Find the cost under adaptive expectations in cumulative cyclical unemployment and output loss. What if the public fully believes an announced 2% target?
step: Under adaptive expectations, $\pi^e = \pi_{-1}$, so inflation falls by $0.4(u - u_n)$ each year. A fall of 3 points needs $\sum (u - u_n) = 3/0.4 = 7.5$ point-years of cyclical unemployment.
step: The output loss is $2 \times 7.5 = 15\%$ of one year's potential output (for instance, 2.5 percentage points of unemployment above the natural rate for 3 years).
step: If the public fully believes the target, $\pi^e$ falls to 2% at once and $\pi = 2\%$ with $u = u_n$: no cyclical unemployment is needed, and the cost is zero.
step: In practice credibility is partial, so the cost lies between zero and 15%. Central banks therefore invest in communication and consistent behavior.
answer: Adaptive: 7.5 point-years of unemployment, 15% of a year's output; fully credible: zero.

@example
title: Effects of a currency depreciation
problem: A country's exports and imports are each 300 (billions of domestic currency). Export demand has elasticity 0.8 and import demand 0.7. The currency depreciates 10%. Import price pass-through is 60% and imports are 20% of the consumption basket. The spending multiplier is 2.0, and there is slack in the economy. Find the change in net exports, the first-round effect on consumer prices and the demand effect.
step: Marshall-Lerner: $\eta_X + \eta_M = 1.5 > 1$, so the balance improves: $\Delta NX \approx 300 \times (1.5 - 1) \times 0.10 = 15$ billion.
step: Import prices rise by $0.60 \times 10\% = 6\%$. With a 20% basket share, consumer prices rise by $0.20 \times 6\% = 1.2$ percentage points in the first round.
step: With a multiplier of 2.0, output rises by about $2.0 \times 15 = 30$ billion if there is slack.
step: The trade-offs: output and the trade balance improve, but inflation rises and firms with foreign-currency debt face higher debt burdens. The central bank must weigh these effects.
answer: $\Delta NX \approx 15$ billion; consumer prices up 1.2 points in the first round; output up about 30 billion.

@mistakes
- Assuming that expectations are always adaptive (or always rational); the effects of policy depend on credibility.
- Concluding from the Lucas critique that policy cannot ever affect output; with sticky prices, anticipated policy has real effects in the short run.
- Treating a depreciation as unambiguously expansionary; it raises import prices, can worsen foreign-currency debt burdens and depends on elasticities.
- Presenting a contested policy issue as settled; state the positions and the evidence.

prompt: Expectations are adaptive ($\pi^e_t = \pi_{t-1}$), the Phillips curve is $\pi = \pi^e - 0.5(u - u_n)$, the natural rate is 5% and policy holds unemployment at 4.5%. Inflation is 3% in year 0. What is inflation in year 4? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 4.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 3 + 4 * 0.5 * (5 - 4.5)
hint: Unemployment is 0.5 below the natural rate, so inflation rises by 0.25 each year.
solution: Each year inflation rises by $0.5 \times 0.5 = 0.25$ points, so after four years it is $3 + 4(0.25) = 4.0\%$. Unemployment below the natural rate requires accelerating inflation.

@practice self
prompt: A country's currency depreciates 10% against its trading partners. Exports and imports are each 400 (billions of domestic currency); the price elasticity of export demand is 0.7 and of import demand 0.6; pass-through to import prices is 70% and imports are 25% of the consumption basket; the spending multiplier is 1.8; the economy has slack. Domestic firms owe 100 units of foreign currency at a starting exchange rate of 1.0 domestic unit per foreign unit. Analyze the effects of the depreciation on the trade balance, consumer prices, output and firm balance sheets, and say what dilemma it poses for the central bank.
hint: Check the Marshall-Lerner condition first.
solution: Marshall-Lerner: $0.7 + 0.6 = 1.3 > 1$, so the trade balance improves by about $400 \times 0.3 \times 0.10 = 12$ billion (possibly after a J-curve delay). Firms with foreign-currency debt see their debt rise from 100 to 110 domestic units, a balance sheet loss of 10, which can restrain investment and raise default risk if their revenue is in domestic currency. The central bank faces a dilemma: the demand boost is welcome with slack, but higher inflation and weaker balance sheets argue for caution, and raising interest rates to curb inflation would support the currency but weaken demand.

@quiz num
prompt: A central bank follows $i = r^* + 1.5\pi$ (the nominal policy rate responds 1.5 points per point of inflation). If inflation, and expected inflation, rise by 2 percentage points, by how many percentage points does the approximate real policy rate $i - \pi^e$ rise?
answer: 1
unit: percentage points
check: 1.5 * 2 - 2
hint: The nominal rate rises by $1.5 \times 2$ points.
solution: The nominal rate rises by 3 points and expected inflation by 2, so the real rate rises by 1 point. Because the response exceeds one-for-one (the Taylor principle), the real rate rises and cools demand.
