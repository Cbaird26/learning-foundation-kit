@article
A central bank is a government institution that issues the economy's base money, supervises or backs the banking system and conducts **monetary policy**: the control of short-term interest rates and money conditions to stabilize prices and employment. In the United States the central bank is the Federal Reserve (the Fed).

### Mandate and structure

By statute the Fed has a **dual mandate**: maximum employment and stable prices (with moderate long-term interest rates as a third element). It states its longer-run inflation goal as 2 percent, measured by the PCE price index. Other central banks differ: the European Central Bank's primary objective is price stability. Policy rate decisions in the US are made by the **Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)**, with twelve voting members: the seven Board governors and five Reserve Bank presidents (the New York Fed president always votes). The Federal Reserve is designed to be independent of day-to-day political control; Lesson 12 returns to the debate.

### Steering the policy rate

The FOMC sets a **target range** for the **federal funds rate**, the rate on overnight unsecured lending between banks and other eligible institutions. It no longer controls the rate by scarcity of reserves. In an **ample-reserves system**, banks hold large reserve balances and the Fed steers the rate with administered rates:

- The **interest rate on reserve balances (IORB)**: the Fed pays it on reserves banks hold at the Fed. A bank will not lend reserves for less than it can earn risk-free at the Fed, so IORB is a main reference for short-term rates.
- The **overnight reverse repurchase facility (ON RRP)** rate: eligible institutions can lend cash to the Fed overnight against securities, which sets a floor for money market rates.
- The **discount rate** (primary credit rate): the rate at which banks borrow from the Fed's **discount window**, a ceiling for the rate, and the channel for emergency liquidity.

**Reserve requirements** have been zero since March 2020, so they are not currently a policy tool. **Open market operations** (buying and selling securities) are now used mainly to manage the size and composition of the balance sheet. Do not rely on this course for the current target range; look it up at the Federal Reserve.

### Balance sheet policy and communication

When short-term rates cannot be lowered further (the **effective lower bound**), the Fed can buy longer-term securities (**quantitative easing, QE**), creating reserves and pushing down long-term yields; **quantitative tightening (QT)** lets the portfolio shrink as securities mature. **Forward guidance** communicates the likely path of the policy rate, which matters because long-term rates depend on expected future short rates (Lesson 6).

### A policy rule: the Taylor rule

A **Taylor rule** describes a policy rate that responds to inflation and the output gap:

$$i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5\,\tilde y,$$

where $i$ is the nominal policy rate, $r^*$ the neutral real rate (unobservable and uncertain), $\pi$ inflation, $\pi^*$ the target and $\tilde y = 100(Y - Y^p)/Y^p$ the output gap in percent. The total response to inflation is 1.5: the **Taylor principle** says the nominal rate should rise more than one-for-one with inflation, so the real rate rises and demand cools. Rules are benchmarks, not mechanical prescriptions: central banks weigh other information.

### Transmission

A policy rate change works through several channels: the **interest rate channel** (higher borrowing costs reduce investment and durable spending), the **credit channel** (tighter bank lending), the **asset price and wealth channel**, the **exchange rate channel** (Lesson 11) and **expectations**. The effects operate with long and variable lags.

@example
title: Taylor rule prescriptions
problem: Suppose $r^* = 2\%$ and the target is $\pi^* = 2\%$. What nominal policy rate does the rule prescribe when (a) $\pi = 2\%$ and the output gap is zero, and (b) $\pi = 3\%$ and the output gap is $-1\%$? All figures are hypothetical.
step: With inflation at target and no gap, $i = 2 + 2 + 0.5(0) + 0.5(0) = 4\%$: the neutral nominal rate, equal to $r^*$ plus the inflation target.
step: In (b): $i = 2 + 3 + 0.5(3 - 2) + 0.5(-1) = 2 + 3 + 0.5 - 0.5 = 5\%$.
step: The ex ante real rate in (b) with expected inflation of 3% is $1.05/1.03 - 1 = 1.94\%$, close to $r^*$ because the inflation and gap responses offset.
answer: (a) 4%; (b) 5%.

@example
title: A \$10 billion open market purchase
problem: The Fed buys \$10 billion of Treasury securities from a securities dealer. The dealer deposits the payment at Bank A. Show the balance sheet changes of the Fed and Bank A.
step: The Fed credits \$10 billion to Bank A's reserve account at the Fed. Fed: securities up \$10 billion (asset); reserves up \$10 billion (liability).
step: Bank A: reserves up \$10 billion (asset) and the dealer's deposit up \$10 billion (liability).
step: The monetary base rises by \$10 billion because reserves are part of it. Bank A now holds more reserves and can lend or buy assets more cheaply; the larger purchase of securities lowers their yields (higher prices).
answer: Fed: assets +\$10 billion securities, liabilities +\$10 billion reserves; Bank A: reserves and deposits each +\$10 billion.

@mistakes
- Saying the Fed sets the federal funds rate directly, or that reserve requirements are the main tool today. It sets a target range and steers it with administered rates.
- Confusing quantitative easing (buying securities with newly created reserves) with printing cash for the government.
- Applying the Taylor rule with the real rate instead of the nominal rate, or forgetting to add inflation to $r^*$.
- Treating $r^*$ as observable; estimates of the neutral rate are uncertain and revised.

@practice self
prompt: The Fed buys \$10 billion of long-term Treasury securities from dealers (quantitative easing). In four or five sentences, show the changes on the Fed's balance sheet and explain two ways this lowers long-term interest rates.
hint: Which side of the Fed's balance sheet records reserves?
solution: The Fed's assets (securities) rise by \$10 billion and its liabilities (bank reserves) rise by \$10 billion, so the monetary base grows. Buying long-term securities raises their prices and lowers yields directly, because the Fed removes duration from private portfolios, and investors rebalance toward other assets, pushing their yields down as well (the portfolio balance channel). QE also signals that short-term rates will stay low for longer, and long-term yields depend on expected future short rates.
rubric: Shows assets (securities) and liabilities (reserves) each rising by \$10 billion.
rubric: Explains the portfolio balance or direct price effect on long-term securities.
rubric: Mentions expectations or signaling about the future path of short rates.

@quiz num
prompt: A variant of the Taylor rule is $i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 1.0\,\tilde y$. With $r^* = 1.5\%$, $\pi = 3\%$, $\pi^* = 2\%$ and an output gap of $-3\%$, what policy rate does it prescribe? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 2.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 1.5 + 3 + 0.5 * (3 - 2) + 1.0 * (-3)
hint: The weight on the output gap is 1.0 in this variant.
solution: $i = 1.5 + 3 + 0.5(1) + 1.0(-3) = 2.0\%$. A larger weight on the gap lowers the prescribed rate in a slump.
