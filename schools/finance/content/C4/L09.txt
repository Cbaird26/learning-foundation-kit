@article
**Fiscal policy** is the government's choice of spending and taxes. It affects aggregate demand in the short run (Lesson 7) and the public debt over time.

### Deficits and debt

The **budget deficit** is the flow: government spending (including interest) minus tax revenue. The **public debt** is the stock: the accumulated past deficits (net of surpluses). The **primary balance** excludes interest payments, so the primary deficit is $G - T$ where $G$ is non-interest spending. The **total deficit** is $G + iB - T$, where $B$ is the debt and $i$ the average interest rate it pays. Debt is usually compared with output as the **debt-to-GDP ratio** $b = B/Y$.

Because taxes rise and transfers fall in booms, the deficit shrinks in booms and widens in recessions: these **automatic stabilizers** need no new legislation. The **cyclically adjusted (structural) balance** removes this cyclical effect to show the underlying stance. **Discretionary** policy is a deliberate change in law. Discretionary changes face lags: recognizing the problem, legislating and implementing.

### Fiscal multipliers with income taxes

If taxes are a proportional fraction $t$ of income, an extra dollar of income raises consumption by $c(1-t)$, so the simple spending multiplier is

$$\frac{1}{1 - c(1 - t)}.$$

Taxes lower the multiplier, since they act as an automatic stabilizer. Empirical multipliers vary with the state of the economy, the monetary policy response (Lesson 7: a central bank that raises rates offsets the stimulus; one at the lower bound does not) and openness. Treat any single number with caution.

### Debt dynamics

Work in real terms with a constant real interest rate $r$ on government debt and real GDP growth $g$. Let $s_t$ be the primary surplus (a negative number is a primary deficit) as a share of GDP, received at the end of year $t$. Then $B_{t+1} = (1 + r)B_t - sY_t$, and dividing by $Y_{t+1} = (1 + g)Y_t$:

$$b_{t+1} = \frac{(1 + r)\,b_t - s_t}{1 + g}.$$

To a close approximation, $\Delta b \approx (r - g)\,b - s$. The debt ratio is **stable** when $b_{t+1} = b_t$, which requires the primary surplus

$$s^* = (r - g)\,b.$$

If $r > g$, the government must run a primary surplus just to keep the ratio constant, and the larger $b$ or the gap $r - g$, the larger the surplus. If $r < g$, a government can run a primary deficit and still see the ratio constant or fall. Note that the *flow* of the deficit and the *ratio* of the debt are different things: an economy that grows faster than its debt can sustain a deficit.

### Debts, deficits and the economy

**Crowding out**: if deficits raise interest rates, private investment falls. **Ricardian equivalence**: if households are forward-looking, can borrow freely and know that debt will be repaid out of future taxes, they save the tax cut and a debt-financed tax cut has no effect on consumption. Most economists regard this as a benchmark that fails when households are liquidity-constrained or short-sighted, so the truth lies in between. Debt also redistributes between generations and can constrain future policy. These topics involve value judgments, such as the proper size of government, and economists disagree. Current deficit and debt data are on FRED and from the Congressional Budget Office; this lesson uses only hypothetical numbers.

@example
title: Primary and total deficit
problem: In a hypothetical economy with GDP of \$10,000 billion, non-interest government spending is \$2,300 billion, interest payments are \$300 billion and tax revenue is \$2,100 billion. The public debt is \$6,000 billion. Find the total and primary deficits (as a share of GDP), the debt ratio and the average interest rate.
step: The primary deficit is $2300 - 2100 = \$200$ billion, or 2.0% of GDP.
step: The total deficit is $200 + 300 = \$500$ billion, or 5.0% of GDP.
step: The debt ratio is $6000/10000 = 60\%$, and the average nominal interest rate is $300/6000 = 5.0\%$.
answer: Primary deficit 2.0% of GDP, total deficit 5.0%, debt ratio 60%, average rate 5.0%.

@example
title: Projecting the debt ratio
problem: The debt ratio is 60%, the real interest rate is 3%, real growth is 2%, and the government runs a primary deficit of 1% of GDP each year ($s = -0.01$). Find the debt ratio for the next three years and the primary surplus that would stabilize the debt at 60%.
step: Year 1: $b_1 = (1.03 \times 0.60 + 0.01)/1.02 = 0.628/1.02 = 61.57\%$.
step: Year 2: $b_2 = (1.03 \times 0.6157 + 0.01)/1.02 = 63.15\%$. Year 3: $b_3 = (1.03 \times 0.6315 + 0.01)/1.02 = 64.75\%$. The ratio rises by about 1.6 points a year, and the increase slowly accelerates.
step: To stabilize the ratio at 60%, the primary surplus needed is $s^* = (0.03 - 0.02) \times 0.60 = 0.6\%$ of GDP: a swing of 1.6 percentage points of GDP from the primary deficit to a surplus.
answer: $b = 61.57\%$, $63.15\%$, $64.75\%$; stabilizing primary surplus 0.6% of GDP.

@mistakes
- Confusing the deficit (a flow) with the debt (a stock), or reading a falling deficit as a falling debt.
- Using nominal interest with real growth (or the reverse) in the debt dynamics; use consistent real or nominal rates.
- Omitting interest from the total deficit, or including it in the primary balance.
- Treating Ricardian equivalence or the fiscal multiplier as settled numbers; both depend on assumptions.

model:
```python
def debt_path(b0, r, g, surpluses):
    """Debt-to-GDP ratios under a given sequence of primary surpluses."""
    path = [b0]
    b = b0
    for s in surpluses:
        b = ((1 + r) * b - s) / (1 + g)
        path.append(b)
    return path
```
hint: Start the list with `b0` and update the ratio once per surplus.
solution: Each year the old debt grows with the interest rate, the primary surplus reduces it, and dividing by $1 + g$ expresses it relative to the larger GDP. The last test shows that the surplus $s^* = (r - g)b = 0.01$ holds the ratio at 0.5.
