@article
A central bank controls an overnight rate, yet households borrow for 30 years and firms issue 10-year bonds. How does one overnight rate influence all of them? The answer is the **term structure of interest rates**, and the picture of it is the **yield curve**: yields on securities of similar credit risk (usually Treasury securities) plotted against maturity.

### Bond prices and yields

A **zero-coupon bond** with face value $F$ maturing in $n$ years, at an annual yield $y$ (compounded annually), has price

$$P = \frac{F}{(1 + y)^n}.$$

Price and yield move in opposite directions. A **spot rate** $y_n$ is the yield on an $n$-year zero-coupon bond. The nominal yield includes compensation for expected inflation (Fisher, Lesson 2): $1 + y = (1 + r)(1 + \pi^e)$. Inflation-protected Treasury securities pay a *real* yield, and the difference between the nominal and real yield of the same maturity is the **breakeven inflation rate**, which reflects expected inflation plus an inflation risk premium.

### Forward rates

Investing for $n$ years at the spot rate must be equivalent to investing for $m < n$ years and then reinvesting at the rate agreed today. The **implied forward rate** from year $m$ to year $n$ (annualized) satisfies

$$(1 + y_n)^n = (1 + y_m)^m\,(1 + f_{m,n})^{\,n - m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_{m,n} = \left[\frac{(1 + y_n)^n}{(1 + y_m)^m}\right]^{\frac{1}{n-m}} - 1.$$

### Theories of the term structure

- **Expectations hypothesis (EH).** Long-term yields are the average (geometric) of expected future short-term rates, so a long bond and a roll-over of short bonds earn the same expected return. Then $(1 + y_n)^n = (1 + i_1)(1 + E[i_2])\cdots(1 + E[i_n])$, and implied forward rates equal expected future short rates.
- **Liquidity (term) premium theory.** Investors dislike the price risk of long bonds, so a **term premium** $\theta_n$ is added: $y_n = $ (EH yield) $+ \theta_n$. Term premiums vary over time and can be negative.
- **Segmented markets / preferred habitat.** Investors prefer particular maturities (pension funds hold long bonds, banks hold short ones), so supply and demand at each maturity also move yields.

### Shapes of the curve

A **normal** (upward-sloping) curve arises when short rates are expected to rise or term premiums are positive. A **flat** curve signals that expected short rates and premiums roughly offset. An **inverted** curve slopes down: markets expect the policy rate to fall (for instance, because a slowdown is anticipated and the central bank will cut) by more than any term premium. An inverted curve has often preceded US recessions, with lags of varying length and some false alarms; it is a signal, not a forecast. FRED publishes constant-maturity Treasury yields (for example the series DGS10 and DGS2) and the 10-year minus 2-year spread (T10Y2Y).

### From the policy rate to the whole curve

The central bank sets the shortest rate directly and influences all others by shaping expectations of the path of policy. A credible announcement that the policy rate will stay low lowers medium-term yields today. The yield curve is therefore the market's summary of the expected policy path, the term premium and the credit and liquidity conditions of each maturity. All numbers in this course are hypothetical; look up current yields on FRED or the US Treasury.

@example
title: Forward rates and the expectations hypothesis
problem: The one-year spot rate is 4.0% and the two-year spot rate is 4.5% (annual compounding). (a) What is the implied one-year forward rate one year from now? (b) Suppose the expected one-year rates are 3%, 4% and 5% over the next three years. What is the three-year yield under the EH, and what does a term premium of 0.30 percentage points do?
step: (a) $(1.045)^2 = (1.04)(1 + f_{1,2})$, so $1 + f_{1,2} = 1.092025/1.04 = 1.050024$ and $f_{1,2} = 5.002\%$. The upward-sloping curve implies that rates rise.
step: (b) The three-year EH yield is $y_3 = (1.03 \times 1.04 \times 1.05)^{1/3} - 1 = 1.1248^{1/3} - 1 = 3.997\%$, slightly below the arithmetic average of 4%.
step: With a term premium of 0.30 points, $y_3 = 3.997\% + 0.30\% = 4.297\%$; the premium raises the long yield above the expected average of short rates and steepens the curve.
answer: (a) 5.002%; (b) 3.997% under the EH and about 4.30% with the term premium.

@example
title: Pricing and the effect of a change in yield
problem: A two-year zero-coupon bond with face value \$100 has a yield of 4.5%. Find its price. If the yield rises to 5.5%, how does the price change?
step: At 4.5%: $P = 100/1.045^2 = 100/1.092025 = \$91.57$.
step: At 5.5%: $P = 100/1.055^2 = 100/1.113025 = \$89.85$.
step: The price falls by \$1.73 or 1.89% when the yield rises by one percentage point: the inverse relation between bond prices and yields.
answer: \$91.57 at 4.5% and \$89.85 at 5.5% (a fall of about 1.89%).

@mistakes
- Averaging spot rates arithmetically, or using $2y_2 - y_1$, to get a forward rate; use the exact compounding relation.
- Believing an inverted yield curve means the central bank has just cut rates, or that it guarantees a recession.
- Confusing a spot rate (yield to maturity of a zero) with a forward rate (a rate for a future period implied today).
- Treating the term premium as a constant that can be directly observed; estimates vary.

model:
```python
def forward_rate(y_short, n_short, y_long, n_long):
    """Implied annualized forward rate between two maturities."""
    growth = (1 + y_long) ** n_long / (1 + y_short) ** n_short
    return growth ** (1 / (n_long - n_short)) - 1
```
hint: Divide the long growth factor by the short growth factor.
hint: Take the root over the length of the forward period, $n_{long} - n_{short}$, then subtract 1.
solution: The forward growth factor is $(1+y_{long})^{n_{long}}/(1+y_{short})^{n_{short}}$; annualize it with the root $1/(n_{long} - n_{short})$. The approximation $2y_2 - y_1$ would give exactly 0.04 in the first test, but the correct rate is 0.040098.
