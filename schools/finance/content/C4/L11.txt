@article
Most economies trade goods and borrow and lend across borders. Open-economy macroeconomics adds three things to the closed-economy model: the **exchange rate**, **capital flows** and **net exports**.

### Exchange rates

The **nominal exchange rate** $S$ is the price of one unit of foreign currency in units of domestic currency (for a US reader, dollars per euro). Quotation conventions vary, so always check which currency is being priced. A rise in $S$ is a **depreciation** of the domestic currency; a fall is an **appreciation**. The **real exchange rate** compares the price of foreign goods with domestic goods:

$$q = \frac{S\,P^*}{P},$$

where $P$ and $P^*$ are domestic and foreign price levels. A higher $q$ means foreign goods are relatively expensive. **Purchasing power parity (PPP)** says that the same basket should cost the same everywhere when converted, so $S^{PPP} = P/P^*$ and $q = 1$. PPP is a useful long-run anchor, but real exchange rates deviate from it for long periods because of non-traded goods, trade barriers and sticky prices.

### Interest parity

An investor can hold a domestic asset yielding $i$ or convert to foreign currency, earn $i^*$ and convert back. **Uncovered interest parity (UIP)** says that expected returns are equal in the same currency:

$$1 + i = (1 + i^*)\,\frac{S^e_{t+1}}{S_t} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{S^e_{t+1}}{S_t} - 1 = \frac{1 + i}{1 + i^*} - 1 \approx i - i^*.$$

The currency with the higher interest rate is expected to depreciate by about the interest differential. UIP often fails in the data, and a **risk premium** may separate the two sides. **Covered interest parity (CIP)** replaces the expected future rate with the forward rate $F$ that can be locked in today, $F/S = (1 + i)/(1 + i^*)$, and holds closely because arbitrage enforces it.

### Balance of payments and net exports

The **current account** (CA) records net exports of goods and services, net income from abroad and net transfers. It is offset by the **financial account** (net capital flows), with the **capital account** small, so that the total is zero apart from measurement error. The national accounts of Lesson 1 imply

$$CA \approx NX = S_{national} - I,$$

where $S_{national}$ is national saving. A country that invests more than it saves has a current account deficit financed by borrowing from abroad (net capital inflow).

### The Mundell-Fleming model

The **Mundell-Fleming model** is the IS-LM model for a small open economy with perfect capital mobility, so the domestic interest rate equals the world rate, $r = r^*$. Net exports depend on the real exchange rate: depreciation raises $NX$. Output is demand-determined at a fixed price level.

- **Floating exchange rate.** *Fiscal expansion* shifts IS right, which would raise $r$; capital inflows appreciate the currency, net exports fall and offset the stimulus, so output is unchanged. *Monetary expansion* lowers $r$, capital flows out, the currency depreciates, net exports rise and output rises.
- **Fixed exchange rate.** The central bank must buy or sell its currency to hold the peg, so the money supply is endogenous: *monetary policy is ineffective*, while *fiscal policy* is effective.

The **policy trilemma** (impossible trinity): a country cannot simultaneously have a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement and an independent monetary policy; it can choose two.

### Does depreciation improve the trade balance?

A depreciation raises the domestic price of imports and lowers the foreign price of exports. The trade balance improves if the quantity responses are large enough: the **Marshall-Lerner condition** says that depreciation improves the trade balance (starting from balance) when $\eta_X + \eta_M > 1$, where $\eta_X$ and $\eta_M$ are the absolute price elasticities of export and import demand. With balanced trade ($X = M$), and holding foreign-currency prices of imports and domestic-currency prices of exports fixed, a depreciation of $d$ changes the trade balance by approximately $X(\eta_X + \eta_M - 1)d$. In the short run quantities respond slowly, so the balance can worsen before it improves, the **J-curve**.

All data in this course are hypothetical; exchange rates and balance-of-payments data are on FRED and from national statistical agencies.

@example
title: Interest parity and PPP
problem: The domestic interest rate is 5%, the foreign rate is 3%, and the spot rate is $S = 1.25$ domestic units per unit of foreign currency. (a) What spot rate does UIP imply one year ahead? (b) A domestic basket costs 200 domestic units and the same foreign basket costs 160 foreign units; with $S = 1.40$, find the PPP rate and the real exchange rate.
step: (a) $S^e/S = 1.05/1.03 = 1.019417$, so $S^e = 1.25 \times 1.019417 = 1.2743$: the domestic currency is expected to depreciate by 1.94%, offsetting its 2-point interest advantage.
step: (b) The PPP rate is $P/P^* = 200/160 = 1.25$.
step: The real exchange rate is $q = 1.40 \times 160/200 = 1.12$.
step: Because $S = 1.40$ exceeds the PPP rate of 1.25, the domestic currency is weak relative to PPP: foreign goods are 12% more expensive than domestic goods, which favors domestic exports.
answer: (a) $S^e = 1.2743$; (b) PPP rate 1.25, real exchange rate 1.12.

@example
title: The current account identity and an inflow of foreign savings
problem: A country has national saving of \$480 billion and domestic investment of \$520 billion. Find the current account balance and describe the financial account. If investment rises by \$30 billion with saving unchanged, what happens?
step: $CA = S - I = 480 - 520 = -\$40$ billion, a current account deficit.
step: The financial account must show a net capital inflow of \$40 billion: foreigners buy the country's assets or lend to it by that amount.
step: If investment rises by \$30 billion, $CA = 480 - 550 = -\$70$ billion. A larger inflow of foreign capital finances the investment, so net exports must fall (through a stronger real exchange rate or lower demand).
answer: $CA = -\$40$ billion, financed by a \$40 billion capital inflow; $-\$70$ billion after the rise in investment.

@mistakes
- Mixing up the quotation convention: a rise in the exchange rate is a depreciation only if the rate is quoted as domestic units per foreign unit.
- Expecting depreciation to improve the trade balance immediately; the J-curve and the Marshall-Lerner condition both matter.
- Assuming that a country with a current account deficit is necessarily in trouble; it is borrowing from abroad, which can finance productive investment or consumption.
- Forgetting that under a fixed exchange rate with free capital flows, the central bank loses control over the domestic interest rate.

@practice num
prompt: Exports and imports are each 200 (in domestic currency units). The price elasticity of export demand is 0.9 and of import demand is 0.8. Using $\Delta NX \approx X(\eta_X + \eta_M - 1)d$, by how much does the trade balance change after a 10% depreciation?
answer: 14
check: 200 * (0.9 + 0.8 - 1) * 0.10
hint: The sum of the elasticities exceeds 1, so the balance improves.
solution: $\Delta NX \approx 200 \times (1.7 - 1) \times 0.10 = 14$. The effect is the long-run quantity response; in the short run, the J-curve may delay it.

@quiz num
prompt: The spot exchange rate is 1.10 domestic units per unit of foreign currency. The one-year domestic interest rate is 4% and the foreign rate is 2%. What one-year forward rate $F$ satisfies covered interest parity? Give it to four decimal places.
answer: 1.1216
tol: 0.0001
check: 1.10 * 1.04 / 1.02
hint: CIP: $F/S = (1 + i)/(1 + i^*)$.
solution: $F = 1.10 \times 1.04/1.02 = 1.1216$. The forward rate is higher than the spot rate because the domestic currency has the higher interest rate.
