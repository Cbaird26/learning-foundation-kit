@article
Banks accept deposits that can be withdrawn on demand and lend long. That **maturity transformation** is useful (it finances houses and factories) and fragile. A bank that is solvent on paper can fail if too many depositors ask for their money at once.

### Bank runs

In the **Diamond-Dybvig model**, a depositor who expects others to withdraw should withdraw too, because the bank cannot pay everyone: it has only a little cash, and selling its illiquid loans quickly yields less than their book value (a **fire sale**). The model has two equilibria: if depositors stay, the bank is fine; if they run, the bank fails. A run is a coordination failure that can be triggered by a rumor, even though it is rational for each depositor given what the others do. Runs can start at a bank that is truly insolvent and spread to sound banks through **contagion** when depositors cannot tell them apart.

### Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort

Two institutions reduce run risk.

- **Deposit insurance.** In the United States the **FDIC** insures deposits up to \$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category (single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts and others). If your deposits are insured, you have no reason to run. The FDIC was created in 1933 in the wake of the bank failures of the Great Depression.
- **Lender of last resort.** A central bank can lend to solvent but illiquid banks against good collateral, through the discount window and other facilities, so they need not fire-sell assets.

Both create **moral hazard**: insured depositors have little reason to monitor the bank, and banks may take more risk because losses fall partly on others. Regulation (capital, liquidity, supervision) and risk-based insurance premiums are the counterweights.

### Two episodes

**2007 to 2009.** Rapid growth in house prices and subprime mortgages, securitized into mortgage-backed securities and funded by short-term borrowing (repurchase agreements, commercial paper) at high leverage, left institutions exposed. When house prices fell, losses mounted and short-term funders ran, a run on *shadow banking* rather than on insured deposits. Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008, funding markets froze, and governments and central banks responded with liquidity facilities, capital injections and, in the United States, the Dodd-Frank Act of 2010. Falling asset prices and forced selling weakened balance sheets further, a loop sometimes called **debt deflation**.

**March 2023.** Silicon Valley Bank failed on March 10, 2023. It held large long-term securities that lost value as interest rates rose, and most of its deposits were uninsured and concentrated among depositors who communicated quickly. Withdrawals came within hours. Authorities invoked a systemic-risk exception to protect all of its depositors. The episode is a modern example of maturity transformation, interest rate risk and uninsured funding.

### Capital and liquidity regulation (Basel III)

A bank's **capital** (equity) absorbs losses. The **Basel III** framework (set by the Basel Committee; national authorities, including the Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC, implement it, so details differ) sets minimums as a share of **risk-weighted assets (RWA)**, where riskier assets receive higher weights:

| Requirement | Minimum |
|---|---|
| Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) / RWA | 4.5% |
| Tier 1 capital / RWA | 6% |
| Total capital / RWA | 8% |
| Capital conservation buffer (CET1) | 2.5% on top |
| Leverage ratio: Tier 1 / total (unweighted) exposure | 3% |

A bank whose CET1 ratio falls below minimum plus buffer (7.0%) faces limits on dividends and bonuses. The **liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)** requires enough high-quality liquid assets to cover net outflows over 30 days of stress, and the **net stable funding ratio (NSFR)** requires stable funding for long-term assets. Large banks also face stress tests and, if systemically important, extra surcharges. Rules change, so check the current rules.

@example
title: A run on a solvent bank
problem: A bank has reserves of 10 and loans with a book value of 90. Its liabilities are deposits of 92 and equity of 8. If it must sell loans early, it receives only 80 cents per dollar of book value. A fraction $f$ of deposits is withdrawn. What is the largest fraction it can pay in full, and at what $f$ is equity wiped out?
step: With no run, assets of 100 exceed deposits of 92; equity is 8, so the bank is solvent.
step: If everything were liquidated, the bank would raise $10 + 0.8 \times 90 = 82$, which would pay only $82/92 = 89.1\%$ of deposits.
step: For $f = 0.4$: withdrawals are $0.4 \times 92 = 36.8$; the reserves cover 10 and 26.8 must come from loan sales, which requires selling $26.8/0.8 = 33.5$ of loans (book value), a loss of $33.5 - 26.8 = 6.7$. Equity falls to $8 - 6.7 = 1.3$.
step: The loss is $0.25 \times (92f - 10)$. It equals equity of 8 when $92f - 10 = 32$, so $f = 42/92 = 45.65\%$. At $f = 0.5$, equity would be $-1$: a run of that size makes a solvent bank insolvent, which is why running is rational.
answer: At most 89.1% of deposits can be paid in full; equity is wiped out when more than 45.65% of deposits are withdrawn.

@example
title: Capital ratios after a loss
problem: A bank has CET1 capital of 60 and risk-weighted assets of 800. It then loses 8 on a portfolio of loans (assume RWA is unchanged). Compare the CET1 ratio with the 4.5% minimum and the 7.0% minimum including the conservation buffer.
step: Before: $60/800 = 7.5\%$, above 7.0%.
step: After the loss: $52/800 = 6.5\%$, above the 4.5% minimum but below the 7.0% minimum plus buffer.
step: The bank must conserve capital: it faces limits on dividends, share repurchases and bonuses until it rebuilds CET1 to at least $0.07 \times 800 = 56$, a shortfall of 4.
answer: CET1 ratio falls from 7.5% to 6.5%; below the buffered minimum, with a shortfall of 4.

@mistakes
- Assuming runs happen only at insolvent banks; a solvent but illiquid bank can fail if depositors coordinate on withdrawing.
- Thinking FDIC coverage is \$250,000 per account; it is per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.
- Computing capital ratios on unweighted assets (a leverage ratio) when the requirement is on risk-weighted assets.
- Forgetting moral hazard: insurance and bailouts reduce the incentive to monitor and to limit risk.

@practice self
prompt: A rumor spreads that a bank holding long-term securities may fail. In a short paragraph (five or six sentences), explain why depositors rationally run even if the bank is solvent, how deposit insurance and a lender of last resort reduce the risk, and what moral hazard cost they create.
hint: Think about what each depositor expects the others to do.
solution: A bank's liquid reserves are small relative to its deposits, so if many depositors withdraw, it must fire-sell long-term assets at a discount and may become insolvent; each depositor knows that those at the front of the line are paid in full and those at the back may lose, so withdrawing is rational if others are expected to do the same. This is a coordination failure with two equilibria, one with a run and one without. Deposit insurance removes the reason to run for insured depositors, because they will be paid anyway, and a lender of last resort can lend against good collateral so the bank does not need to fire-sell. The cost is moral hazard: insured depositors have little incentive to monitor, and banks may take on more risk, which is why capital, liquidity rules and supervision accompany the safety net. Note that deposits above the insurance limit, which are not covered, remain run-prone.
