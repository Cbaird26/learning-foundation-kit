step: Choose an economy (the United States is the easiest, since FRED covers it best). Download the series listed in the first material below from FRED (fred.stlouisfed.org) for the past 10 to 15 years, and record the download date and each series' units and frequency.
step: Transform the data: compute real GDP growth (annualized quarterly growth is $(\text{GDP}_t/\text{GDP}_{t-1})^4 - 1$), year-over-year inflation from price indexes, the output gap using a published estimate of potential output, the unemployment gap relative to an estimate of the natural rate, the real policy rate (policy rate minus a measure of expected or recent inflation) and the 10-year minus 2-year Treasury spread.
step: Make three charts: (1) real GDP growth and the output gap; (2) inflation (headline and core) against the central bank's target; (3) the policy rate with the yield curve spread. Label axes, units and sources, and shade recessions if the data source supplies them.
step: Compute a Taylor rule prescription $i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - \pi^*) + 0.5\tilde y$ for the latest data, choosing and defending your $r^*$ (try at least two values), and compare it with the actual policy rate. Explain the gap between them.
step: Explain the central bank's recent decisions with the course models: use IS-LM and AD-AS to describe the shocks and the policy response, the Phillips curve to discuss the inflation-unemployment trade-off and the role of expectations, and the expectations hypothesis to explain what the yield curve implies about the expected policy path.
step: Write a three-page brief (about 1,200 words plus charts and a short data appendix) with a summary box, the analysis, an outlook with at least two scenarios (for example a soft landing and a downside case) and a list of risks and data caveats. Include what would change your view.
step: Review for balance and accuracy: separate facts from judgments, state each assumption, avoid predicting exact numbers, and cite the series IDs and download date.

## Week 13 review: Macroeconomics, money and banking

### Key ideas by week

1. **National income accounting.** $Y = C + I + G + NX$; value added; nominal versus real GDP; the deflator; $S = I + NX$.
2. **Inflation and unemployment.** CPI as a fixed basket and its biases; the Fisher equation; unemployment rate, participation, natural rate $u^* = s/(s+f)$.
3. **Solow model.** $\Delta k = sk^{\alpha} - (n+\delta)k$; steady state; golden rule $s = \alpha$; growth accounting.
4. **Money and banks.** Deposit creation; $M/B = (cu+1)/(cu+re)$; the quantity equation $MV = PY$.
5. **Central bank tools.** Dual mandate; IORB, ON RRP, discount rate; balance sheet policy; Taylor rule.
6. **Yield curve.** Zero-coupon prices; forward rates; expectations hypothesis and term premium; curve shapes.
7. **IS-LM.** IS and LM curves; multipliers; crowding out; liquidity trap.
8. **AD-AS and the Phillips curve.** AD from IS-LM; SRAS and LRAS; $\pi = \pi^e - \beta(u-u_n) + \nu$; Okun's law; sacrifice ratio.
9. **Fiscal policy.** Primary versus total deficit; $b' = ((1+r)b - s)/(1+g)$; $s^* = (r-g)b$; Ricardian equivalence.
10. **Crises and regulation.** Runs, fire sales, deposit insurance, lender of last resort, Basel III ratios.
11. **Open economy.** $q = SP^*/P$; UIP and CIP; $CA = S - I$; Mundell-Fleming; trilemma; Marshall-Lerner.
12. **Expectations and debates.** Adaptive versus rational expectations; Lucas critique; time inconsistency; credibility.

### Key formulas

- Deflator $= \text{nominal}/\text{real} \times 100$; Fisher: $1 + i = (1+r)(1+\pi^e)$.
- Solow: $k^* = (s/(n+\delta))^{1/(1-\alpha)}$; with technology, replace $n + \delta$ by $n + g + \delta$.
- Multipliers: $1/(1-c_1)$ for $G$ and $-c_1/(1-c_1)$ for $T$.
- Forward rate: $f_{m,n} = [(1+y_n)^n/(1+y_m)^m]^{1/(n-m)} - 1$.
- UIP: $S^e/S = (1+i)/(1+i^*)$; Marshall-Lerner: $\eta_X + \eta_M > 1$.

### Self-check

- Can you compute real GDP, the deflator and CPI inflation from price and quantity tables?
- Can you solve a Solow steady state and explain the effect of a rise in saving?
- Can you solve IS-LM numerically for two policy shocks and describe crowding out?
- Can you explain a bank run, the role of deposit insurance, and the Basel ratios?
- Can you trace a currency depreciation through trade, prices, output and balance sheets?

### Common errors

- Counting intermediate goods in GDP; computing real growth without removing prices.
- Using $1/rr$ when currency drain or excess reserves exist; forgetting $n$ in break-even investment.
- Using the simple multiplier and ignoring crowding out; shifting the wrong curve.
- Using the arithmetic average of spot rates for a forward rate; stating current rates or data as fact.

In 2026, nominal GDP is 5,300 and the GDP deflator (2025 = 100) is 104. Potential output grows 2.5% in 2026. Using Okun's law $\tilde y = -2(u - u_n)$, what is the unemployment rate in 2026? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 5.28
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 5.0 - (100 * (5300 / 1.04 - 5000 * 1.025) / (5000 * 1.025)) / 2
hint: First compute 2026 real GDP, then the output gap relative to 2026 potential output.
hint: Real GDP is $5300/1.04 = 5096.15$ and potential is $5000 \times 1.025 = 5125$.
solution: Real GDP is $5300/1.04 = 5096.15$; potential is 5,125, so the gap is $(5096.15 - 5125)/5125 = -0.563\%$. Okun's law gives $u - u_n = 0.563/2 = 0.281$, so $u = 5.28\%$.

prompt: In a hypothetical economy the monetary base is 250, the public holds currency equal to 20% of deposits, and banks hold reserves of 10% of deposits. Consumption is $C = 200 + 0.75(Y - T)$, investment is $I = 400 - 20r$, $G = 500$ and $T = 400$. Money demand is $L = 0.5Y - 40r$ and the price level is 1. The central bank then buys securities, raising the monetary base by 25 (the ratios $cu$ and $re$ are unchanged). What is the new equilibrium output?
answer: 2700
check: 3200 - 80 * (0.5 * 3200 - 275 * (1 + 0.2) / (0.2 + 0.10)) / (0.5 * 80 + 40)
hint: The money multiplier is $(cu + 1)/(cu + re)$. Find $M$, then the LM curve, and intersect it with $Y = 3200 - 80r$.
hint: $M = 275 \times 1.2/0.3 = 1100$, so LM is $Y = 2200 + 80r$.
solution: The multiplier is $1.2/0.3 = 4$, so the money supply rises from 1,000 to $4 \times 275 = 1{,}100$. IS: $Y = 3200 - 80r$; LM: $1100 = 0.5Y - 40r$, so $Y = 2200 + 80r$. Then $r = 6.25$ and $Y = 2700$. Before the purchase equilibrium was $r = 7.5$, $Y = 2600$.

@exam self
prompt: A country is hit by an oil price shock. Expected inflation is 2%, $\beta = 0.5$, the natural rate is 5% and unemployment rises to 5.5%. The supply shock adds $\nu = 1.5$ points to inflation. The central bank uses $i = r^* + \pi + 0.5(\pi - 2) + 0.5\tilde y$ with $r^* = 1\%$, and Okun's coefficient is 2. (a) Find inflation, the output gap and the rule's prescribed policy rate. (b) Using the expectations hypothesis, find the two-year yield if markets expect the policy rate to be the rule's value now and 1 point lower next year. (c) Explain, in a short paragraph, the dilemma the shock poses, how credibility affects the choice, and how the shock would show up in AD-AS.
hint: Inflation is $2 - 0.5(0.5) + 1.5$, and the output gap is $-2 \times 0.5$.
solution: (a) $\pi = 2 - 0.5(5.5 - 5) + 1.5 = 3.25\%$; the output gap is $-2(0.5) = -1.0\%$; the rule gives $i = 1 + 3.25 + 0.5(1.25) + 0.5(-1.0) = 4.375\%$. (b) $y_2 = \sqrt{1.04375 \times 1.03375} - 1 = 3.87\%$, which is below the current policy rate: the curve is inverted if the policy rate is expected to fall. (c) A negative supply shift moves SRAS up and left, raising prices and reducing output (stagflation), so the central bank faces a dilemma: tightening to fight inflation deepens the output loss, while easing risks entrenching higher inflation expectations. If the central bank is credible and expectations stay anchored near 2%, the inflation effect is temporary and it can respond less aggressively, so a smaller sacrifice is needed; if expectations rise, $\pi^e$ shifts the Phillips curve up and the cost of disinflation rises.
rubric: Computes inflation (3.25%), the output gap (-1.0%) and the Taylor rule rate (4.375%).
rubric: Computes the two-year EH yield (about 3.87%) and notes that it is below the policy rate.
rubric: Describes the shock as a leftward shift of SRAS (stagflation) in AD-AS and states the central bank's dilemma.
rubric: Explains the role of credibility and anchored expectations in the size of the sacrifice.
