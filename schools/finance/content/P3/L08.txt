@article
Institutions are defined by their **liabilities**. Once you know what the money must do, the policy follows. A Level III case gives you the facts of an institution and asks for a return objective, a risk assessment (ability and willingness) and constraints (liquidity, horizon, tax, legal and regulatory, unique circumstances).

### Defined benefit pension plans

The liability is the stream of future benefits, so risk is measured on the **funded ratio** $A/L$ and surplus. The return objective is usually to earn at least the discount rate on liabilities plus costs, so contributions stay low. Ability to take risk depends on the funded ratio (higher is better), the sponsor's financial strength and its correlation with the plan, the plan's maturity (retirees versus active workers), and the sponsor's liquidity. A frozen plan with many retirees and a weak sponsor has low risk tolerance and a short effective horizon; liability-driven investing hedges the interest rate and inflation risk of liabilities. **Defined contribution** plans put the investment risk on participants, so the sponsor's focus is a well-designed menu and defaults.

### Endowments and foundations

The aim is typically **intergenerational equity**: support today's spending without eroding purchasing power. The return objective is spending plus inflation plus costs, compounded:

$$R_{\text{req}} = (1 + s)(1 + \pi)(1 + c) - 1.$$

A **spending rule** smooths what is paid out. A common hybrid (a moving-average or smoothing rule) is

$$\text{Spend}_t = w\times\text{Spend}_{t-1}(1+\pi) + (1-w)\times s\times\text{MV}_{t-1},$$

where $w$ is the weight on the inflation-adjusted prior spending and $s$ the target spending rate. Endowments usually have long horizons and high risk capacity, with liquidity needs set by spending and capital calls from private funds. Foundations may have a minimum payout set by law and may face tax and legal constraints; unique circumstances include donor restrictions and mission-related or ESG preferences.

### Banks and insurers

A **bank** faces deposit and loan maturity mismatch: priorities are liquidity, interest rate risk and credit quality, with a high-quality, short to medium duration portfolio and tight regulatory limits. **Life insurers** have long, relatively predictable liabilities, so can hold longer, less liquid bonds; **property and casualty insurers** have shorter, less predictable claims (especially in catastrophe years) and need more liquidity. Insurers manage surplus relative to regulatory capital and ratings, so regulatory constraints are central.

### Sovereign wealth funds

These vary: **stabilization** funds need liquidity and low risk, **savings** funds for future generations can take long-horizon risk and hold illiquid assets, and **development** funds invest domestically. Governance, transparency and political constraints are unique circumstances.

### Exam technique

Make the answer a table-like structure in words: return (required and desired), risk (ability then willingness, each with reasons from the case, ending with an overall rating), and each constraint with one fact. Do the arithmetic where you can: required return, funded ratio, expected spending. State whether overall risk tolerance is below average, average or above average and why.

@example
title: A smoothed spending rule and a required return
problem: An endowment spent \$24 million last year, inflation is 3.0%, last year's ending market value was \$520 million, and the rule uses a weight of 0.70 on inflation-adjusted prior spending and a 5.0% target spending rate. What is this year's spending? Separately, a foundation spends 5.0%, expects inflation of 2.8% and incurs 0.4% costs: what return does it need?
step: The inflation-adjusted prior spending is $24 \times 1.03 = 24.72$, and 70% of it is $17.304$ million.
step: The market-value part is $0.30 \times 0.05 \times 520 = 7.8$ million.
step: Spending $= 17.304 + 7.8 = \$25.104$ million, between last year's spending grown with inflation (\$24.72 million) and the pure 5% rule (\$26.0 million).
step: Required return for the foundation: $(1.05)(1.028)(1.004) - 1 = 8.37\%$.
answer: Spending \$25.104 million; required return 8.37%.

@mistakes
- Setting an endowment's risk tolerance only from the board's attitude, ignoring its spending needs and liquidity from capital calls.
- Adding spending, inflation and costs arithmetically when the case asks for the compounded (geometric) required return.
- Treating every pension plan alike: funded ratio, sponsor strength and retiree mix change the risk capacity.
- Forgetting the regulatory constraints that dominate insurers and banks.

@practice mc
prompt: A property and casualty insurer and a life insurer each ask for a portfolio recommendation. The life insurer's liabilities are long and predictable; the casualty insurer's claims are shorter and may spike after catastrophes. Which statement best describes the difference?
choice: The casualty insurer should hold longer, less liquid assets than the life insurer, because its premiums are collected in advance.
choice*: The casualty insurer needs more liquidity and shorter duration because its claim timing is less predictable.
choice: Both should hold the same portfolio, because both are regulated insurers.
hint: Which liabilities are harder to time?
solution: Casualty claims are uncertain in timing and size (catastrophes), so the insurer needs liquid, shorter-duration assets. The life insurer can hold longer, less liquid assets because benefit payments are more predictable. Regulation shapes both but does not make their portfolios identical.

Inflation is 2.5%, last year's ending market value was \$400 million, the spending rule gives 75% weight to inflation-adjusted prior spending and 25% to a 4.5% target rate applied to market value. What is this year's spending, in millions of dollars, to two decimal places?

lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: 0.75 * 18 * 1.025 + 0.25 * 0.045 * 400
hint: Compute the two pieces of the rule separately and add them.

@practice mc
prompt: A defined benefit plan has a funded ratio of 95%, most of its participants are retirees, the plan is frozen to new entrants, and the sponsor has weak credit and little ability to make extra contributions. What is the best overall assessment of its risk tolerance?
choice: Above average, because a funded ratio near 100% means little risk of shortfall.
choice: Average, because the strong and weak factors offset each other.
choice*: Below average, because the plan is mature, the sponsor is weak, and a shortfall would be hard to repair.
hint: Weigh the funded ratio against the maturity of the plan and the sponsor's strength.
solution: A 95% funded ratio is close to fully funded, but the plan's high share of retirees (short effective horizon), the freeze (no new contributions or participants to share risk) and the sponsor's weak credit all reduce its ability to recover from losses. The overall risk tolerance is below average.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Alder Hospital Foundation (fictional) has \$400 million in assets and is the main source of funding for a hospital's research grants. The board spends 4.0% of assets a year, expects inflation of 3.1% and estimates investment costs of 0.6%. Grants may be cut by up to 10% in a bad year but the board does not want to reduce them permanently. The foundation has \$25 million in outstanding capital calls from private funds, and the donor's gift agreement forbids investment in tobacco. (a) Calculate the required return. (b) Assess risk tolerance (ability and willingness) and give an overall rating. (c) State the liquidity, legal and unique constraints. (d) State whether a below-average, average or above-average equity share suits the foundation and why.
hint: Use the three-factor required return, then walk through the IPS headings in order.
solution: (a) Required return $= (1.04)(1.031)(1.006) - 1 = 7.87\%$. (b) Ability is above average because the horizon is perpetual and spending is only 4.0% of assets; but the grants fund a core mission and permanent cuts are unwelcome, and the capital calls take liquidity, which tempers ability to about average. Willingness is average: the board tolerates a temporary cut of 10% but not a permanent one. Overall risk tolerance is average. (c) Liquidity: annual spending of about \$16 million plus the \$25 million of capital calls must be met without forced sales. Legal and regulatory: charitable-foundation rules and any minimum payout. Unique circumstance: the donor's tobacco exclusion must be applied to every holding. (d) An average equity share and diversified growth assets suit it: the 7.87% required return needs growth assets, but the average risk tolerance and capital calls argue against an aggressive allocation.
rubric: Computes the required return of about 7.87% by compounding spending, inflation and costs. 1 point.
rubric: Assesses both ability and willingness with reasons from the case and states an overall rating. 1 point.
rubric: States liquidity (spending plus capital calls), legal and the tobacco unique constraint. 1 point.
rubric: Concludes on an average equity share and ties it to the required return and the risk assessment. 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A pension plan has assets of \$900 million and liabilities of \$1,000 million. Over the next year assets return 8% and liabilities grow 3%. What is the funded ratio at the end of the year? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 94.37
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 900 * 1.08 / (1000 * 1.03) * 100
hint: Grow each side separately, then divide.
solution: Assets $= 900 \times 1.08 = 972$; liabilities $= 1{,}000 \times 1.03 = 1{,}030$; funded ratio $= 972/1{,}030 = 94.37\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A university endowment and a regulated bank each ask about holding a 20% allocation to illiquid private funds. In four or five sentences, explain why the answers differ.
hint: Compare liquidity needs, horizon and regulation for the two institutions.
solution: The endowment has a perpetual horizon, predictable spending and little risk of sudden withdrawals, so it can hold illiquid assets and earn an illiquidity premium, subject to meeting spending and capital calls. The bank's deposits can be withdrawn on demand, and it faces regulatory liquidity and capital rules, so a 20% allocation to assets that cannot be sold quickly would be inappropriate. The bank's priority is liquidity and credit quality, while the endowment's is long-term real return with enough liquidity for spending.
rubric: Explains the endowment's long horizon and ability to hold illiquid assets. 1 point.
rubric: Explains the bank's liquidity needs and regulatory limits. 1 point.
rubric: Contrasts the priorities (real return versus liquidity and credit quality). 1 point.
