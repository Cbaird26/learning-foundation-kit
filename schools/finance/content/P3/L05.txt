@article
At Level III derivatives are tools of portfolio management, not objects of valuation. You use them to change exposures quickly and cheaply: to move to a target beta or duration, to equitize cash, to hedge a currency or to shape the payoff of a position. The questions ask how many contracts, what the position does, and what can go wrong.

### Futures to change equity beta

To move a stock portfolio of value $V$ and beta $\beta_P$ to a target beta $\beta_T$ with futures of price $f$, multiplier $m$ and beta $\beta_f$:

$$N_f = \frac{\beta_T - \beta_P}{\beta_f}\times\frac{V}{f\,m}.$$

A positive $N_f$ means buy, a negative one means sell. To **equitize cash** (hold futures that give equity exposure on money waiting to be invested), buy $V/(f\,m)$ contracts for a beta-1 futures contract. Futures for asset allocation change the exposure without trading the underlying holdings, avoiding transaction costs and manager disruption.

### Futures to change duration

To move a bond portfolio to a target duration, match basis-point values:

$$N_f = \frac{\text{BPV}_T - \text{BPV}_P}{\text{BPV}_f}.$$

The portfolio BPV is $D_{\text{mod}}\times V\times 0.0001$, and the futures BPV is given per contract. Shortening duration means selling contracts.

### Currency management

The **covered interest rate parity** forward rate for a quote in price-currency units per base-currency unit is

$$F = S\times\frac{1 + r_{\text{price}}}{1 + r_{\text{base}}},$$

where $r_{\text{price}}$ and $r_{\text{base}}$ are the interest rates of the price and base currencies over the period (annual compounding here for simplicity). The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a **forward discount**. An investor who hedges a foreign asset by selling the foreign currency forward earns approximately the foreign local return plus the interest rate differential (domestic rate minus foreign rate): hedging a currency with lower interest rates than the home currency **adds** carry, and hedging a higher-yielding currency **costs** carry.

Policy choices run from fully hedged to unhedged. A **minimum-variance hedge ratio** comes from the regression of the foreign asset's return in domestic terms on the currency return. **Currency overlays** may be passive (a fixed hedge ratio), discretionary or systematic active, or a mix. Tools include forwards, futures, options and **option strategies** such as the **risk reversal** and **seagull**, which cut the cost of protection by giving up some currency upside. Hedging costs, cash needed for forward settlement (liquidity), and counterparty risk are the standard constraints.

### Option strategies on a portfolio

A **protective put** floors the value at the strike (less the premium) while keeping upside. A **covered call** earns the premium but caps the upside. A **collar** combines a long put with a short call: the call premium offsets the put cost, so protection is cheaper, but gains are capped. For a collar on one share bought at $S$, long put at $K_p$, short call at $K_c$ and net premium paid $c$, the worst outcome is $S - K_p + c$ lost, and the best is $K_c - S - c$ gained.

### Risks of overlays

Typical risks: **basis risk** between the futures and the portfolio, **margin and liquidity** needs when markets move against the position, **rolling** and tracking differences, **counterparty** risk on forwards and swaps, and the gap between the exposure the overlay creates and the client's policy limits. A well-designed overlay is documented with a purpose, size, risk limits and a monitoring plan.

### Exam technique

State the formula, substitute, round contracts to a whole number, and give the direction (buy or sell). When asked for risks, give three distinct ones and tie each to the facts in the case.

@example
title: Futures to reduce beta and duration
problem: A \$250 million equity portfolio has a beta of 1.10. A manager wants a beta of 0.80 using futures priced at 4,500 with a multiplier of 50 and a beta of 1. Separately, a \$150 million bond portfolio with modified duration 5.5 is to be cut to 4.0 using futures with a BPV of \$85 per contract. How many contracts in each case?
step: Equity: each contract has notional value $4{,}500 \times 50 = \$225{,}000$.
step: $N_f = \dfrac{0.80 - 1.10}{1}\times\dfrac{250{,}000{,}000}{225{,}000} = -0.30 \times 1{,}111.1 = -333.3$, so sell about 333 contracts.
step: Bonds: $\text{BPV}_P = 150{,}000{,}000 \times 5.5 \times 0.0001 = \$82{,}500$ and $\text{BPV}_T = 150{,}000{,}000 \times 4.0 \times 0.0001 = \$60{,}000$.
step: $N_f = (60{,}000 - 82{,}500)/85 = -264.7$, so sell about 265 contracts.
answer: Sell about 333 equity futures; sell about 265 bond futures.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong sign: raising the beta or duration needs buying contracts, and lowering it needs selling.
- Forgetting the futures multiplier when computing notional value.
- Inverting the covered interest parity formula, so the forward moves the wrong way.
- Calling a collar "free protection": the cost is the upside given up above the call strike.

answer: 193
unit: contracts

check: abs((90e6 * 4.5 * 0.0001 - 90e6 * 6.0 * 0.0001) / 70)

solution: $\text{BPV}_P = 90{,}000{,}000 \times 6.0 \times 0.0001 = \$54{,}000$ and $\text{BPV}_T = \$40{,}500$. The change is $-13{,}500$, and $13{,}500/70 = 192.9$, so sell about 193 contracts.

@practice mc
prompt: An investor owns a share worth 100. She buys a put with a strike of 95 for 2.40 and sells a call with a strike of 110 for 1.80. Ignoring dividends and financing, what is the maximum gain per share at expiration?
choice: 10.60 per share.
choice*: 9.40 per share.
choice: Unlimited, because she still owns the share.
hint: The net premium paid is 0.60, and the call caps the share price benefit at 110.
solution: Net premium paid is $2.40 - 1.80 = 0.60$. Above the call strike the share gain is capped at $110 - 100 = 10$, less the 0.60 net cost, so the maximum gain is 9.40. The 10.60 figure adds the net premium instead of subtracting it, and the call removes the unlimited upside.

prompt: The spot rate is 1.3500 Canadian dollars per US dollar (price currency CAD, base currency USD). The one-year CAD interest rate is 3.0% and the one-year USD rate is 4.2%. Using covered interest rate parity with annual compounding, what is the one-year forward rate in CAD per USD? Give it to four decimal places.
answer: 1.3345
tol: 0.0001
check: 1.35 * 1.03 / 1.042
hint: The price currency is CAD, so its interest rate goes in the numerator.
solution: $F = 1.3500 \times 1.030/1.042 = 1.3345$ CAD per USD. The USD has the higher interest rate, so it trades at a forward discount.

@practice mc
prompt: A US investor holds Japanese equities and sells the yen forward each year to hedge. The one-year US dollar interest rate is 4.0% and the one-year yen rate is 0.5%. Ignoring other costs, what is the approximate effect of the hedge on the investor's annual return?
choice: It costs about 3.5%, because the investor gives up the yen's higher yield.
choice*: It adds about 3.5%, because the yen trades at a forward premium that the seller of yen forward collects.
choice: It has no effect on expected return, because a hedge only removes risk.
hint: The currency with the lower interest rate trades at a forward premium.
solution: By covered interest parity the yen (the lower-rate currency) trades at a forward premium of about $4.0\% - 0.5\% = 3.5\%$. Selling yen forward at that premium earns about 3.5% a year relative to the spot rate. Hedging a higher-yielding currency would cost the differential, but the yen is the lower-yielding one here.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Ridgeway Pension (fictional) has \$400 million in a 50/50 split of equities (beta 1.0) and bonds. The board wants a tactical overlay that lifts equity exposure by 10 percentage points of total assets without selling bonds, and the committee also wants downside protection on equities. Equity futures trade at 5,000 with a multiplier of 50 and a beta of 1. (a) Compute the futures position that adds the exposure. (b) Propose an option structure that protects the equity sleeve and explain its cost trade-off. (c) Give three risks of the overlay.
hint: Ten percent of \$400 million is the exposure to add; divide by the notional value of one contract.
solution: (a) The exposure to add is $0.10 \times \$400$ million $= \$40$ million. One contract has notional $5{,}000 \times 50 = \$250{,}000$, so $N_f = 40{,}000{,}000/250{,}000 = 160$: buy 160 contracts. (b) A collar on the equity sleeve (buy a put, sell a call) limits losses below the put strike; the call premium reduces the put's cost, but gains above the call strike are given up. A plain protective put keeps full upside at a higher premium. (c) Basis risk (the futures differ from the actual equity holdings); margin and liquidity risk (variation margin must be paid in cash if markets fall, and the portfolio holds bonds, not cash); and rolling and counterparty or operational risk, together with the possibility that the leverage breaches policy limits.
rubric: Computes the number of contracts as 160 and states that they are bought. 1 point.
rubric: Proposes a collar or protective put and explains the cost versus upside trade-off. 1 point.
rubric: Names three distinct risks (basis, margin or liquidity, rolling, counterparty or policy limits) and ties them to the case. 1 point.

answer: 30

@quiz self
prompt: Case: Halden Fund (fictional), a euro-based investor, holds US equities worth USD 100 million. The manager is considering three policies: unhedged, fully hedged, and a 50% hedge. In four or five sentences, explain the case for a partial hedge and what the manager should consider in setting it.
hint: Think about risk reduction, hedging cost and the possibility that the currency exposure diversifies the equities.
solution: A partial hedge balances risk reduction against cost and regret. Foreign currency exposure adds volatility, so hedging reduces risk, but it also costs or earns the interest rate differential, needs cash for forward settlement and removes a source of potential diversification if the currency tends to move opposite the equities. The manager should consider the minimum-variance hedge ratio from a regression of the equities' returns in the investor's own currency on currency returns, the carry (forward points), the client's liquidity and the risk tolerance for currency swings. A 50% hedge keeps half the exposure, limiting both losses and the regret from either outcome.
rubric: Explains that hedging reduces currency volatility but has costs or carry effects. 1 point.
rubric: Notes that unhedged currency exposure can diversify equities or give upside. 1 point.
rubric: Names considerations for setting the ratio (minimum-variance hedge ratio, carry, liquidity, risk tolerance). 1 point.
