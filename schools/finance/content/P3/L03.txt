@article
An equity mandate answers three questions: how much of the portfolio to manage actively, how to hold the rest, and how much risk relative to the benchmark the client can tolerate. Level III item sets give you a client, a benchmark and a candidate manager and ask you to judge fit.

### Passive structures and index weighting

A **cap-weighted** index holds each stock in proportion to market value, so it has low turnover and automatically tracks the market, but it concentrates in the largest and most richly valued stocks. **Equal weighting** has a small-cap and value tilt, more turnover (because weights must be reset) and less concentration. **Fundamental weighting** (by sales, earnings, dividends or book value) leans toward value. Passive investors replicate by **full replication** (hold every stock; lowest tracking error, higher cost for large indexes), **stratified sampling** (hold a representative subset by sector, size and style) or **synthetic replication** with derivatives, which adds counterparty and collateral issues.

### Active share and tracking risk

**Active share** measures how different the holdings are from the benchmark:

$$\text{Active share} = \tfrac{1}{2}\sum_i |w_{p,i} - w_{b,i}|.$$

It ranges from 0% (the index) to 100% (no overlap). **Tracking risk** (tracking error) is the standard deviation of active returns, $\sigma_{A}$. A fund with low active share and low tracking risk and a high fee is a **closet indexer**. A fund with high active share but low tracking risk may hold diversified positions that offset one another's risk. A fund with high both is a concentrated stock picker or factor bettor.

### The fundamental law of active management

The expected **information ratio** (active return per unit of tracking risk) is

$$IR \approx TC \times IC \times \sqrt{BR},$$

where $IC$ is the information coefficient (the correlation between forecasts and outcomes), $BR$ is breadth (the number of independent decisions per year) and $TC$ is the transfer coefficient (how fully the forecasts are reflected in actual weights, reduced by constraints such as no short sales). The expected active return is $E(R_A) = IR \times \sigma_A$. Skill is small but breadth is large: an $IC$ of 0.05 with 100 independent bets gives an $IR$ of 0.5 before constraints. The lesson for the exam is that breadth (independent bets, not the number of stocks), not just skill, drives results, and constraints cut $TC$.

### Core-satellite

In a **core-satellite** structure, a low-cost passive or enhanced-index core delivers market exposure and satellites (active or factor strategies) take active risk. The core controls cost and total tracking risk; the satellites are sized by the **tracking risk budget**. With a passive core, the portfolio's tracking risk is roughly the satellite weight times the satellite's tracking risk, ignoring correlation among satellites.

### Active styles and factors

Value, growth, quality, momentum, low volatility and size are common styles or factors; rules-based **smart beta** or factor products capture them cheaply. Fundamental active managers add unique return from security selection but charge higher fees and show style drift if unmonitored.

### Exam technique

When asked "which manager" or "which structure," compute the information ratio for each candidate before opining, then say what the number does not capture (capacity, fees, consistency, style drift). When a budget is stated, convert it to a maximum weight: budget divided by the manager's tracking risk.

@example
title: The fundamental law and active share
problem: A manager has an information coefficient of 0.06, makes 64 independent bets a year, has a transfer coefficient of 0.75 and a tracking risk of 5%. What are the expected information ratio and active return? Separately, a portfolio holds A 35%, B 25%, C 20%, D 20%, E 0% against a benchmark of A 20%, B 20%, C 20%, D 10%, E 30%: what is its active share?
step: $\sqrt{64} = 8$, so $IR = 0.75 \times 0.06 \times 8 = 0.36$.
step: Expected active return $= 0.36 \times 5\% = 1.8\%$ a year.
step: Absolute weight differences: $15 + 5 + 0 + 10 + 30 = 60$.
step: Active share is half of that sum: $60/2 = 30\%$.
answer: IR 0.36 and expected active return 1.8%; active share 30%.

@mistakes
- Using the number of stocks as breadth instead of the number of independent decisions per year.
- Forgetting the transfer coefficient and so overstating the information ratio of a constrained (for example long-only) manager.
- Computing active share from the sum of weight differences without halving it.
- Sizing a satellite by its expected alpha instead of by the tracking risk budget.

prompt: A manager has an information coefficient of 0.04, makes 100 independent bets a year and has a transfer coefficient of 0.90. If the portfolio's tracking risk is 3.5%, what active return does the fundamental law predict? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 1.26
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 0.90 * 0.04 * sqrt(100) * 3.5
hint: First find the information ratio, $TC \times IC \times \sqrt{BR}$.
solution: $IR = 0.90 \times 0.04 \times 10 = 0.36$; expected active return $= 0.36 \times 3.5\% = 1.26\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A US equity fund charges 0.85% a year. Its active share is 18% and its tracking risk is 1.1%. Which description fits best?
choice: A concentrated stock picker, because any fee above 0.5% implies high conviction.
choice*: A closet indexer, because it takes little active risk but charges an active fee.
choice: A factor tilt, because tracking risk above 1% implies a deliberate factor bet.
hint: Look at both active share and tracking risk, then at the fee.
solution: Active share of 18% means 82% of the portfolio overlaps with the benchmark, and tracking risk of 1.1% is low. With an active-management fee, the fund is a closet indexer: the investor pays for active management but gets near-index exposure.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio holds Stock P 40%, Q 30%, R 20%, S 10% and T 0%. Its benchmark holds P 25%, Q 25%, R 20%, S 10% and T 20%. What is the portfolio's active share? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 20.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 0.5 * (abs(40-25) + abs(30-25) + abs(20-20) + abs(10-10) + abs(0-20))
hint: Add the absolute differences of weights stock by stock.
solution: Differences: $15 + 5 + 0 + 0 + 20 = 40$. Active share is $40/2 = 20.0\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A client's committee dislikes the concentration of a cap-weighted index in its largest constituents and asks how an equal-weighted version of the same index would differ. Which statement is correct?
choice: It would have lower turnover and a stronger large-cap tilt, because equal weights do not need resetting.
choice: It would have the same style exposures, because both hold the same stocks.
choice*: It would tilt toward smaller and cheaper stocks and have higher turnover, because weights must be reset to equal.
hint: Equal weighting gives every stock the same weight regardless of size.
solution: Equal weighting overweights smaller companies relative to cap weighting, which brings a small-cap and typically value tilt, and weights drift, so rebalancing creates more turnover. Holding the same stocks does not give the same exposure when the weights differ.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Quill County Pension (fictional) holds a passive equity core and plans a 30% satellite with one active manager. Its tracking risk budget for the equity program is 1.0%. Candidate A has an expected active return of 1.2% and tracking risk of 2.0%. Candidate B has 2.5% and 5.5%. Treat the core as having zero tracking risk. (a) Compute each candidate's information ratio. (b) Compute the tracking risk each would add at a 30% weight. (c) Recommend a candidate and explain.
hint: Information ratio is active return divided by tracking risk; satellite risk is weight times tracking risk.
solution: (a) $IR_A = 1.2/2.0 = 0.60$ and $IR_B = 2.5/5.5 = 0.45$. (b) At a 30% weight, tracking risk is $0.30 \times 2.0 = 0.60\%$ for A and $0.30 \times 5.5 = 1.65\%$ for B. (c) Recommend A: it has the higher information ratio, and its 0.60% contribution fits inside the 1.0% budget. Candidate B at 30% breaches the budget by 0.65 percentage points; to fit, B would be limited to about $1.0/5.5 = 18\%$ of the program. Spending the whole 1.0% budget, A would add $0.60 \times 1.0 = 0.60\%$ of expected active return and B only $0.45 \times 1.0 = 0.45\%$, so A is the more efficient use of the budget despite B's higher headline alpha.
rubric: Computes the information ratios 0.60 and about 0.45. 1 point.
rubric: Computes the satellite tracking risk as weight times manager tracking risk (0.60% and 1.65%). 1 point.
rubric: Compares each with the 1.0% budget and notes B's maximum weight of about 18%. 1 point.
rubric: Recommends A with a justification tied to both the information ratio and the budget. 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A client wants an information ratio of 0.50. A manager's information coefficient is 0.05 and its transfer coefficient is 0.80. How many independent bets a year does the fundamental law require? Give the number to one decimal place.
answer: 156.3
unit: bets
tol: 0.1
check: (0.5 / (0.05 * 0.80)) ** 2
hint: Solve $IR = TC \times IC \times \sqrt{BR}$ for $BR$.
solution: $\sqrt{BR} = 0.50/(0.05 \times 0.80) = 12.5$, so $BR = 12.5^2 = 156.25$, or about 156.3 independent bets.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A trustee notices that Fund X holds 150 stocks and Fund Y holds 40. Fund X has an active share of 25% and Fund Y of 85%. Both charge the same active fee. In three or four sentences, explain which fund gives more for the fee and what else you would check.
hint: Active share, not the number of holdings, tells you how different a portfolio is.
solution: Fund Y gives more active management for the fee: an active share of 85% means its holdings differ greatly from the benchmark, while Fund X overlaps with the benchmark in about 75% of its weights and looks close to a closet index despite holding more stocks. The number of holdings is not a measure of activity. Before concluding, check each fund's tracking risk, its information ratio over a full cycle, turnover and costs, and whether Fund Y's bets are concentrated in one factor or sector that would add risk the trustee did not intend.
rubric: Concludes that Fund Y is more active and Fund X is closer to a closet indexer, using active share. 1 point.
rubric: Notes that the number of holdings does not measure activity. 1 point.
rubric: Names one or two further checks (tracking risk, information ratio, concentration, fees or turnover). 1 point.
