@article
A mock exam is the best predictor you have of exam day, but only if you take it under exam conditions and learn from it. This lesson sets out how to run a full-length practice and how to turn the results into a plan. The facts you plan around (2026 exams): Level III has item sets and constructed-response questions; the core is 65-70% of the exam and your chosen pathway is 30-35%; CFA Institute recommends about 300 hours of study per level. All of these can change, so confirm them in the current exam guide.

### Setting up the mock

- **Use the real format.** Match the current exam's mix of item sets and constructed-response questions, its time limit and its calculator rules. Use original practice material or official mock exams; never study "recalled" exam questions, which is a breach of the exam rules and of Standard VII(A), Conduct as Participants in CFA Institute Programs.
- **Simulate conditions.** One sitting, no notes, phone away, same hour as your exam, only the allowed calculator. Write constructed responses by hand or at the keyboard, whichever matches the exam.
- **Pace by points.** Convert time to minutes per point for each section (Lesson 11) and mark checkpoints on your scratch paper, for example "finished item sets by minute 60".

### Marking the mock

Mark constructed responses against a model answer and rubric, awarding points only for what is on the page. Be strict: write "0.5" points when part of an element is missing. Then compute the score for each section and each topic. A raw percentage is only a rough guide, since the passing standard is set by the exam and not published as a fixed score, so look for **topic gaps** rather than a single pass mark.

### The error log

For every missed or guessed question, write one line: topic, question type, **cause**, the correct idea in one sentence, and the **fix** with a date. Causes worth separating:

1. **Knowledge gap:** you did not know the concept or formula.
2. **Application error:** you knew it but applied it to the wrong case fact.
3. **Calculation error:** wrong arithmetic, rounding or sign.
4. **Reading error:** you answered a different question (for example "calculate" versus "justify", or missed a condition in the vignette).
5. **Time error:** you ran out of time or rushed.

A log of 40 entries tells you far more than a score: if 18 are reading errors, more content review will not help; slowing down on the stem and the command word will.

### Turning the log into a plan

Weight your review hours by two things: the **exam weight** of the topic and your **error rate** in it. If you have 300 study hours, about 32.5% (the midpoint of 30-35%) or 97.5 hours go to the pathway and the rest to the core, before adjusting for strengths. Then add to your weakest topics: a topic where you missed half the questions deserves more than one where you missed one in ten, even if both have equal weights. Reserve the last weeks for another full mock, and keep a short list of formulas you reliably forget to rewrite from memory each day.

### Mixed practice: what the exam blends

A single case may combine, for example, a client's IPS (asset allocation), a duration overlay (derivatives), a performance question (attribution) and an ethics issue (referral fees). Practice moving between topics: identify the topic from the question stem first, recall the method, then compute.

@example
title: Building a review plan from a mock
problem: You score 70% on the item-set section and 50% on constructed response of a mock. Your error log has 20 entries: 8 knowledge gaps, 6 reading errors, 4 calculation errors and 2 time errors. You have 60 study hours left. How would you allocate them?
step: Group the log: knowledge gaps are 40% of entries, reading errors 30%, calculation errors 20% and time errors 10%.
step: Knowledge gaps: about 40% of the time, or 24 hours, spent on the topics behind those entries, using short practice sets by topic.
step: Reading and time errors: these are habits, not content, so use about 18 hours (30%) of timed practice with a command-word checklist, plus a pacing plan.
step: Calculation errors: about 12 hours (20%) of formula drills (BPV, futures, attribution) and a full recalculation check; the rest (6 hours, 10%) goes to a final full mock and its review.
answer: About 24 hours on knowledge gaps, 18 on reading and timing habits, 12 on calculation drills and 6 on a final mock.

@mistakes
- Taking a mock untimed or with notes, which gives a score that cannot be compared with exam day.
- Logging only the topic of a miss and not its cause, so the fix is always "study more".
- Reviewing equally across all topics instead of weighting by exam weight and error rate.
- Studying recalled exam questions, which breaches the exam rules and Standard VII(A).

answer: 5.5
tol: 0.1

@practice mc
prompt: A defined benefit plan is funded at 120%, its sponsor is financially strong, the plan is closed to new participants, and the trustees want to protect the surplus for the next five years without giving up all growth. Which action is most consistent with these goals?
choice: Raise the allocation to equities to maximize surplus growth, because the plan can afford losses.
choice*: Increase the liability-hedging portfolio (long-duration bonds or an LDI overlay) while keeping a smaller growth allocation.
choice: Hold all assets in cash, so that there is no risk of any loss.
hint: What protects the surplus against changes in the liabilities?
solution: A high funded ratio in a closed plan means a surplus worth protecting; increasing the share of liability-matching assets locks in the funded status while a smaller growth portfolio keeps some upside. Raising equities would add surplus risk, and cash would leave the plan exposed to falling rates, which raise liabilities.

@practice self
prompt: Case (mini mock, allow 20 minutes): Priya Nandakumar (fictional), age 45, plans to invest \$200,000 for 10 years at an expected 6% a year. She is comparing two options: a taxable portfolio in which all return is taxed each year at 25%, and a portfolio that defers all gains until the end and then pays 15% on the gain, with cost basis equal to the amount invested. Her adviser, Joel, plans to recommend the option from a partner firm that pays him a referral fee, and has not told her. (a) Calculate the ending after-tax value of each option. (b) State which option is better and by how much. (c) Identify the Standard that Joel's plan raises and what he should do.
hint: Annual taxation compounds at 6% times 0.75; deferral compounds at 6% and is taxed once at the end.
solution: (a) Annual taxation: $200{,}000 \times (1 + 0.06 \times 0.75)^{10} = 200{,}000 \times 1.045^{10} = \$310{,}594$. Deferral: pretax value $200{,}000 \times 1.06^{10} = \$358{,}170$; the gain is \$158,170 and the tax is $0.15 \times 158{,}170 = \$23{,}725$, so the ending value is \$334,444. (b) The deferral option is better by $334{,}444 - 310{,}594 = \$23{,}850$ because tax is deferred and charged at a lower rate. (c) Standard VI(C), Referral Fees, requires disclosure to the client of any compensation for recommending a service, and Standard VI(A), Disclosure of Conflicts, applies too. Joel must disclose the referral fee before she decides and base the recommendation on her interests, not on the fee.
rubric: Computes \$310,594 for the annual tax option. 1 point.
rubric: Computes \$334,444 for the deferral option and the \$23,850 difference. 1 point.
rubric: Identifies Standard VI(C) Referral Fees (and may cite VI(A)). 1 point.
rubric: States the corrective action: disclose the fee and recommend in her interest. 1 point.

@quiz mc
prompt: A candidate scored 62% on a full mock. Of 30 logged errors, 17 were reading errors (missed a command word or condition in the question), 8 were knowledge gaps and 5 were calculation errors. Which review plan is most efficient?
choice: Spend most of the remaining time rereading the curriculum from the beginning.
choice*: Spend a large share on timed practice with a command-word and condition checklist, with targeted content review for the knowledge gaps.
choice: Take no further practice exams to avoid damaging confidence.
hint: What do most of the errors have in common, and is more content the cure?
solution: More than half of the errors were reading errors, which are habit and process problems that content review does not fix. Timed practice with a checklist addresses them directly, and a smaller block of targeted review covers the knowledge gaps and drills cover the calculations.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: After your first full mock you scored 58%, with weaknesses in derivatives overlays and ethics. You have eight weeks left and 80 study hours. In five or six sentences, write a review plan that uses exam weights and your error log, including at least one more timed mock.
hint: Mention how you will split the hours, the kinds of practice, and when the next mock will be.
solution: I will put about 55% of the hours (44 hours) into derivatives overlays and ethics, the two weakest topics, and the rest into maintaining the other core topics and my pathway, in proportion to their exam weights. For overlays I will drill contract-count formulas (beta, duration, currency) until they are automatic. For ethics I will do daily case items, naming the Standard by number and the corrective action, since ethics has a high weight across levels. Each week I will log every miss by cause and rewrite the formula sheet from memory. In week five I will take a second timed full mock under exam conditions, compare topic scores with the first, and spend the final two weeks on the topics that remain weakest and on pacing.
rubric: Allocates hours with a rationale tied to weakness and exam weight. 1 point.
rubric: Names specific practice for each weak topic (overlay drills, case items with Standards). 1 point.
rubric: Includes an error log by cause and at least one more timed mock with a date. 1 point.
