@article
Individual clients differ from institutions in four ways: their goals are personal and changing, their horizon is a lifetime, their taxes are significant, and their behavior matters. A Level III answer starts with the client's balance sheet and ends with a policy that the client can actually follow. This is general education, not tax or legal advice.

### Human capital and financial capital

A young professional's biggest asset is **human capital**, the present value of future earnings, which behaves like a bond if earnings are stable or like equity if they depend on the market. The **economic net worth** is financial plus human capital minus liabilities (including the present value of future consumption needs and insurance gaps). A client whose human capital is bond-like can hold more equities; one whose job depends on the same industry as the portfolio should hold less of it. The present value of a salary growing at $g$ for $n$ years, discounted at $r$, is a growing annuity:

$$PV = \frac{C_1}{r - g}\left[1 - \left(\frac{1+g}{1+r}\right)^n\right].$$

### Ability and willingness to take risk

**Ability** to take risk is objective: wealth relative to spending, time horizon, income stability and liquidity needs. **Willingness** is psychological: attitude, experience and behavior in drawdowns. The policy risk tolerance is the **lower** of the two. A client who is willing to take risk but has little cushion against loss (high spending relative to wealth, short horizon) has low ability, so the portfolio should be conservative. A client with large surplus wealth who dislikes risk should be treated as having below-average willingness: you can improve the client's outcome by education, but you should not impose risk the client will abandon in a drawdown. Behavioral biases (loss aversion, overconfidence, mental accounting, home bias) are part of the analysis, with **goal-based** buckets a practical way to match risk to need.

### Taxes and account types

The after-tax return on a taxable account, with all return taxed annually at rate $t$, is $r(1 - t)$, so $FV = PV[1 + r(1 - t)]^n$. If the return is mostly unrealized gains taxed only at the end (with cost basis equal to the initial investment), $FV = PV[(1+r)^n(1 - t_{cg}) + t_{cg}]$. In a **tax-deferred** account (such as a traditional retirement account), growth is untaxed and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, so $FV = PV(1+r)^n(1 - t)$. In a **tax-free** account (such as a Roth) qualified withdrawals are untaxed: $FV = PV(1+r)^n$. Tax rates in these formulas are illustrative; real tax parameters (such as the 2026 US limits and brackets) are labeled by year and change, so always look them up. Tax-aware practices include **asset location** (put tax-inefficient assets such as taxable bonds in deferred accounts), **tax-loss harvesting**, and holding periods that reach long-term rates. Jurisdictions differ: some tax wealth or gains differently, so a cross-border client needs advice in each country.

### Estate planning basics

Tools include **gifts** (in the US for 2026, an annual exclusion of \$19,000 per recipient and a lifetime estate and gift exemption of \$15,000,000 per person, both subject to change), **trusts** (which can control timing, protect assets and in some cases reduce estate tax), **life insurance** (to provide liquidity or replace income) and charitable vehicles. Annual-exclusion gifts reduce the estate without using the lifetime exemption. Civil-law systems may have forced heirship rules, which can override a client's wishes.

### Exam technique

Individual-client constructed-response answers follow the IPS structure: return objective (required and desired), risk (ability, willingness), constraints (liquidity, horizon, taxes, legal, unique circumstances). Give each part a number or a fact from the case, not a generic phrase.

@example
title: After-tax growth in three accounts
problem: A client invests \$100,000 for 15 years at 6% a year. Compare the ending value after tax if (i) the return is taxed annually at 30%, (ii) the return is untaxed until the end and then taxed at 20% as a capital gain (cost basis equals the investment), and (iii) the account is tax-deferred with a 30% tax on withdrawal.
step: (i) The after-tax annual return is $6\% \times 0.70 = 4.2\%$, so $FV = 100{,}000 \times 1.042^{15} = \$185{,}360$.
step: (ii) Pretax growth: $100{,}000 \times 1.06^{15} = \$239{,}656$; the gain is \$139,656 and the tax is $0.20 \times 139{,}656 = \$27{,}931$, so $FV = \$211{,}725$.
step: (iii) The deferred account grows to \$239,656 and is then taxed at 30%, leaving \$167,759.
step: Case (ii) wins because tax is deferred and charged at the lower rate on the gain only. Case (iii) ends lowest here because, as set up, the 30% rate applies to the whole balance, principal included; a real tax-deferred account usually gives a deduction on the contribution, which this comparison leaves out.
answer: (i) \$185,360; (ii) \$211,725; (iii) \$167,759.

@mistakes
- Setting risk tolerance by the client's stated willingness alone when the ability to bear loss is lower.
- Applying the tax rate to the balance in a taxable account when only the return (or gain) is taxed.
- Treating the exemption and annual exclusion as the same thing.
- Ignoring human capital: a client with a stable salary can hold more risky assets than one whose income depends on the portfolio's industry.

choice: Her risk tolerance is high, because willingness is what the client reports and ability is only a constraint of the adviser.
choice*: Her risk tolerance should be set by her low ability to take risk, so the portfolio should be conservative despite her willingness.
choice: Her risk tolerance cannot be set until her willingness is formally tested in a simulation.
hint: Ability and willingness can disagree; which one governs?
solution: The policy risk tolerance is the lower of ability and willingness. With wealth covering only 70% of needed spending and no earnings once retired, her ability is low, so the portfolio should be conservative even though she is willing to take more risk.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Marcus and Elena Varga (fictional), both 52, have investable assets of \$3.2 million and want to retire at 62 with spending of \$140,000 a year in today's money. Expected inflation is 2.5%, and they assume all investment return is taxed at 20%. Marcus's income depends on a technology employer whose stock is 25% of their portfolio. (a) Compute the current spending rate and the required after-tax nominal return to preserve real capital (geometric), and the pretax return implied. (b) Explain two risk factors from their balance sheet that affect the IPS.
hint: The spending rate is spending divided by assets; compound it with inflation.
solution: (a) The spending rate is $140{,}000/3{,}200{,}000 = 4.375\%$. The after-tax nominal return needed is $(1.04375)(1.025) - 1 = 6.98\%$; with all return taxed at 20%, the pretax return needed is $6.98/(1 - 0.20) = 8.73\%$. (b) First, correlation between human and financial capital: Marcus's income and 25% of the portfolio depend on the same employer, so a downturn would hit both; the portfolio should diversify away from that stock. Second, the required return of 8.73% is demanding for a diversified portfolio, so the plan may need lower spending, later retirement or more saving to avoid forcing risk.
rubric: Computes the spending rate 4.375% and the required after-tax return of about 6.98%. 1 point.
rubric: Converts to a pretax return of about 8.73% using the 20% tax assumption. 1 point.
rubric: Identifies concentration or human-capital correlation with the employer's stock and the remedy. 1 point.
rubric: Notes that the required return is high relative to risk capacity and gives an adjustment. 1 point.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A client has bonds paying interest, equities with large unrealized gains and an inheritance to invest. She has a taxable account, a tax-deferred retirement account and a tax-free (Roth-type) account. In four or five sentences, explain how asset location should guide where each investment is held.
hint: Which assets create the most taxable income each year, and which account shelters it best?
solution: Asset location puts the most tax-inefficient assets where tax is deferred or avoided. Taxable bonds, whose interest is taxed at ordinary rates every year, belong in the tax-deferred account. The assets expected to grow the most go in the tax-free account, since all growth escapes tax. Equities with large unrealized gains can sit in the taxable account, where the gains are deferred until sale and taxed at capital-gains rates, with tax-loss harvesting available. The overall asset allocation still comes first, and liquidity needs and withdrawal rules limit what can be placed in the retirement accounts.
rubric: Puts taxable bonds in the tax-deferred account because of annual ordinary-rate tax. 1 point.
rubric: Places high-growth assets in the tax-free account and equities with unrealized gains in the taxable account, with reasons. 1 point.
rubric: Notes that asset location is subordinate to the asset allocation and liquidity constraints. 1 point.
