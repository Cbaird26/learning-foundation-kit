@article
The **strategic asset allocation** (SAA) is the long-run policy mix that comes out of the client's investment policy statement (IPS): the return needed, the risk the client is willing and able to take, and the constraints. Level III asks you to choose the right approach for the client first, and only then do the arithmetic.

### Three approaches

- **Asset-only.** Choose the mix by expected return, risk and correlation of the assets, ignoring liabilities. This suits a client with no specific liabilities, such as many endowments and wealthy individuals.
- **Liability-relative** (asset-liability management, ALM, and liability-driven investing, LDI). Risk is measured as the volatility of the **surplus** (assets minus liabilities), so assets are chosen to hedge liabilities, for example with long-duration bonds for a pension plan. This suits defined benefit plans and insurers.
- **Goal-based.** Divide the client's wealth into goal buckets (a safe bucket for essential spending, a growth bucket for aspirational goals), each with its own horizon and required probability of success. This suits individuals.

### Portfolio mathematics you must do quickly

For two assets with weights $w_1, w_2$, expected returns $E_1, E_2$, volatilities $\sigma_1, \sigma_2$ and correlation $\rho$:

$$E_p = w_1E_1 + w_2E_2, \qquad \sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2.$$

The minimum-variance weight in asset 1 is $w_1^* = \dfrac{\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2}$.

For a liability-relative client, the **funded ratio** is $A/L$, the surplus is $A - L$, and the return on surplus over a period is $\dfrac{A(1+R_A) - L(1+R_L)}{A - L} - 1$. A plan with a funded ratio below 1 has a negative cushion, so a given asset loss has an outsized effect on the ratio.

### Beyond simple mean-variance optimization

Mean-variance optimization (MVO) is very sensitive to its inputs: small changes in expected returns produce large changes in weights, and it often gives concentrated corner solutions. Remedies include **resampled optimization** (average the weights from many simulated input sets), **constraints** on weights, the **Black-Litterman** model (start from the equilibrium returns implied by market weights, then tilt toward the analyst's views in proportion to confidence), and **Monte Carlo simulation** for multi-period goals, spending and taxes. **Risk budgeting** allocates risk rather than capital, sizing each position or asset class by its contribution to total risk. **Factor-based allocation** replaces asset classes with factors such as equity beta, duration, credit, inflation and liquidity so that hidden overlaps between assets are visible.

### Implementing and rebalancing

The SAA is supplemented by **tactical asset allocation** (TAA), short-term tilts that exploit expected mispricing within risk limits. Drift from the policy weights is controlled by a **rebalancing discipline**: calendar rebalancing (for example quarterly), percentage-range (corridor) rebalancing (trade when a weight leaves its band) or a mix. Wider bands mean lower trading cost but more drift; assets that are illiquid or costly to trade call for wider bands.

### Exam technique

Open every asset allocation answer by naming the client type and the approach it implies (asset-only, liability-relative or goal-based). Then check each candidate portfolio against **return requirement first, risk tolerance second, constraints third**: discard any portfolio that fails the required return, and any whose downside breaks the stated loss limit. State a downside in the same units as the limit; a common shortcut is expected return minus 1.645 standard deviations for a 5% one-year tail under normality.

@example
title: Two-asset portfolio and surplus return
problem: A 60/40 portfolio holds equities (expected return 7.0%, volatility 15%) and bonds (3.5%, 5%) with correlation 0.10. Find its expected return and volatility. Then a pension plan with assets of \$520 million and liabilities of \$450 million earns 6.0% on assets while liabilities grow 4.5%: what is the return on surplus?
step: Expected return: $0.6(7.0) + 0.4(3.5) = 5.6\%$.
step: Variance: $0.36(225) + 0.16(25) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.10)(15)(5) = 81 + 4 + 3.6 = 88.6$, so $\sigma_p = 9.41\%$.
step: Surplus now: $520 - 450 = 70$. After a year: $520(1.06) - 450(1.045) = 551.2 - 470.25 = 80.95$.
step: Return on surplus: $80.95/70 - 1 = 15.64\%$. Because surplus is a thin slice of assets, its return is far more volatile than either side alone.
answer: 5.6% and 9.41%; surplus return 15.64%.

@mistakes
- Using an asset-only mean-variance approach for a plan with firm liabilities, ignoring surplus risk.
- Dropping the correlation term, or using $\rho$ in place of the covariance, when computing portfolio variance.
- Choosing the portfolio with the highest Sharpe ratio even though it fails the required return or the stated loss limit.
- Treating rebalancing bands as symmetric reasons to trade every time, ignoring transaction costs and liquidity.

@practice mc
prompt: An analyst's mean-variance optimizer assigns 70% to emerging market equity after the analyst raised its expected return by 0.5 percentage points. The investment committee dislikes such sensitivity and wants an approach that starts from market-implied returns and tilts toward the analyst's views according to how confident the analyst is. Which approach fits?
choice: Resampled optimization with the analyst's original expected returns held fixed.
choice*: The Black-Litterman model, which starts from equilibrium returns implied by market weights and adjusts them for views weighted by confidence.
choice: Equal-weighting all asset classes in the optimizer.
hint: Which model starts from equilibrium returns rather than historical averages?
solution: Black-Litterman anchors the inputs on market-implied equilibrium returns and shifts them only as far as the confidence in each view justifies, which dampens the extreme weights. Resampling averages over input uncertainty but does not use equilibrium views, and equal weighting abandons optimization entirely.

@practice mc
prompt: A foundation's policy is 60% equities with a rebalancing range of 55% to 65%. After a strong market the equity weight is 66.5%. The next calendar rebalancing date is two months away. Under a percentage-range (corridor) policy, what should the foundation do?
choice: Wait until the calendar date because the range is only a guideline.
choice: Buy more equities because the recent trend is positive.
choice*: Rebalance now by selling equities, because the weight has left its range.
hint: What triggers a trade under a corridor policy?
solution: A corridor policy trades whenever a weight leaves its band, regardless of the calendar. At 66.5% the equity weight exceeds 65%, so the foundation sells equities, back to the 60% target or at least back inside the band depending on its cost policy. Buying more equities would increase the drift.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Alder Hospital Foundation (fictional) has \$400 million and spends 4.5% of assets a year. Expected inflation is 2.5% and investment costs are 0.5%. The board will not accept more than a 12% loss in a one-year, 5% worst case (use expected return minus 1.645 standard deviations). Three candidate portfolios have expected returns and volatilities of: A, 6.4% and 7.0%; B, 7.8% and 10.5%; C, 9.1% and 14.5%. (a) Calculate the required return (geometric, to preserve real capital). (b) Recommend a portfolio and justify it with numbers.
hint: Compute the required return first, then test each portfolio against it and the loss limit.
solution: (a) Required return $= (1.045)(1.025)(1.005) - 1 = 7.65\%$. (b) Portfolio A returns only 6.4%, below 7.65%, so it fails the return requirement. Portfolio C meets the return (9.1%) but its 5% worst case is $9.1 - 1.645 \times 14.5 = -14.8\%$, which breaks the 12% loss limit. Portfolio B returns 7.8%, above the requirement, and its worst case is $7.8 - 1.645 \times 10.5 = -9.5\%$, inside the limit. Recommend B: it is the only portfolio meeting both the return objective and the risk limit.

rubric: Tests every portfolio against the return requirement, rejecting A with its figure. 1 point.
rubric: Computes the 5% worst cases (about -9.5% for B, -14.8% for C) and rejects C against the 12% limit. 1 point.
rubric: Recommends B and states that it is the only portfolio meeting both constraints. 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A portfolio can hold asset 1 (volatility 20%) and asset 2 (volatility 8%) with a correlation of 0.25. What weight in asset 1 gives the minimum-variance portfolio? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 6.25
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (8**2 - 0.25 * 20 * 8) / (20**2 + 8**2 - 2 * 0.25 * 20 * 8) * 100
hint: Use $w_1^* = (\sigma_2^2 - \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)/(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2)$.
solution: The numerator is $64 - 40 = 24$; the denominator is $400 + 64 - 80 = 384$; $w_1^* = 24/384 = 6.25\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: Tern Industries (fictional) sponsors a defined benefit pension plan funded at 85%, with liabilities that are sensitive to interest rates. The trustees ask why the plan's allocation should not simply be the portfolio with the best Sharpe ratio. In four or five sentences, explain the liability-relative approach and how it changes the allocation.
hint: Ask what the plan's real risk is, and what that implies for the assets it should hold.
solution: The plan's real risk is a shortfall of assets against liabilities, not the volatility of assets alone. A liability-relative approach measures risk as the volatility of the surplus and chooses assets that hedge the liabilities, for example long-duration bonds that move with the liability discount rate, while a growth portfolio supplies the extra return needed to close the 15% funding gap. A Sharpe-optimal asset-only portfolio might hold little duration and so leave the plan exposed to falling rates, which raise liabilities more than assets. Because the plan is underfunded, the sponsor's ability to take risk is also limited, so the allocation balances hedging against return seeking.
rubric: States that risk is measured on the surplus (assets minus liabilities) rather than assets alone. 1 point.
rubric: Explains that assets are chosen to hedge liability sensitivity, such as interest rate duration. 1 point.
rubric: Notes the funding gap or sponsor's reduced risk capacity and the trade-off with return seeking. 1 point.
