@article
Level III is the first CFA exam where you write. Constructed-response questions reward a candidate who answers exactly what is asked, with the numbers, in the client's context, and nothing wasted. The curriculum is the same as for item sets; the difference is technique. (This lesson describes general technique. Exam length, point values, calculator rules and the mix of item sets and written questions change each year, so read the current exam guide.)

### Decode the command word

- **Calculate / determine:** give a number with units, the formula and the substituted values. A bare number may earn little.
- **Identify / state / list:** short, precise items; no explanation needed unless asked. Give exactly the number requested (if "two", give two).
- **Explain / discuss:** a claim plus a reason that connects to the case.
- **Justify:** a decision plus the evidence from the case (a number or fact) and the reason it supports the decision.
- **Recommend:** the choice, the rationale and, usually, one risk or condition.
- **Compare / contrast:** both sides on the same dimension, with the verdict.

### Plan your time by points

Most written questions state their points. Divide the time available for the section by its total points to get **minutes per point**, then multiply by a question's points. If a section has 66 points and 90 minutes, a 9-point question deserves $90/66 \times 9 = 12.3$ minutes, including reading. Spend the first minute reading the whole case and the question parts, then the case again with a pen on the facts you will need. Do easy points first, but never leave a question blank: a partial answer earns partial points.

### A structure that earns points

1. **Answer first.** State the conclusion in the first line ("Recommend portfolio B").
2. **Show the numbers.** Formula, substitution, result with units. Grade-able lines are better than prose.
3. **Give the reason.** One sentence tying the number to the case fact and to the concept.
4. **Add the limit.** If the question asks for a risk or limitation, give it; otherwise skip it.

Match the answer length to the points: about one distinct, case-specific idea per point. Repeating the same idea in different words earns nothing extra, and a long list of possible factors does not help when the question asked for two.

### Typical traps

- Answering with a generic list that never mentions the case facts.
- Giving more items than asked (a grader may count only the first ones).
- Rounding too early, so the final number is off.
- Contradicting yourself in the justification.
- Spending the time on a calculation that carries one point.

### Item sets

An item set gives a vignette (a case with data) and several multiple-choice questions, each with three choices. Read the questions first, then read the vignette looking for what they need. Choose the answer that applies the concept to the case facts; three plausible-looking choices are usually built on common errors (wrong sign, wrong formula, wrong measure). If a question needs a calculation, do it, then compare the result with the choices before looking at the reasoning options.

### When you do not know

Write what you do know: the formula, the definition, the general principle, and apply it to the case facts. Graders award points for correct elements even when the final answer is wrong. Keep going: a miss on one part rarely stops you from earning points on the next.

@example
title: Structuring a short answer
problem: Question (6 points, about 9 minutes): "A pension plan has assets of \$540 million and liabilities of \$600 million, and its sponsor has weak credit. (a) Calculate the funded ratio. (b) Recommend whether the plan should raise or lower its allocation to risk assets, and justify your answer."
step: Answer first: "Funded ratio $= 540/600 = 90\%$. Recommend lowering (or at least not raising) risk assets."
step: Reason with facts: "The plan is 10% underfunded, so a loss would deepen the gap, and a weak sponsor has limited ability to contribute more, so the plan's ability to take risk is low."
step: Add the concept: "Risk tolerance is the lower of ability and willingness; ability is low, so a liability-hedging bias (long-duration bonds) is appropriate."
step: Check the points: one for the ratio, one for the direction, two for the case-based reasons. Time used: about 4 minutes, within budget.
answer: Funded ratio 90%; reduce risk assets because ability to take risk is low.

@mistakes
- Writing only the final number on a calculation, so no points can be awarded for method.
- Giving a long list of factors instead of the specific number of items the question asked for.
- Using a generic textbook answer that never mentions the case facts.
- Running out of time because an early question was answered at too much length.

choice: "Fund X is probably not good enough; it is common for funds to have lower ratios."
choice*: "No. The information ratio of 0.45 is below the committee's 0.60 requirement, so Fund X delivers less active return per unit of tracking risk than required."
choice: "The information ratio is active return divided by tracking risk, and it is a standard measure used by many investors."
hint: A justification needs a conclusion, a case fact and a reason.
solution: Justify means a decision plus evidence and reasoning. The correct choice states the conclusion, uses the case numbers (0.45 against 0.60) and gives the reason. The others are vague or only define the term without applying it.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio returned +8%, -5% and +12% in three consecutive years. What is its geometric (annualized) return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.74
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1.08 * 0.95 * 1.12) ** (1 / 3) - 1) * 100
hint: Link the growth factors, then take the cube root.
solution: The growth factors multiply to $1.08 \times 0.95 \times 1.12 = 1.14912$. The cube root is $1.0474$, so the annualized return is 4.74%. The arithmetic mean (5.0%) would overstate the result.

@practice mc
prompt: Halfway through a constructed-response section you have used 60% of your time and have completed 40% of the points available. Two questions remain: one worth 12 points on a topic you know well and one worth 12 points on a topic you find hard. Which approach is best?
choice: Spend all remaining time on the hard question, because it will be the most valuable to master.
choice: Skip the hard question entirely, because it is unlikely to earn points.
choice*: Complete the familiar question first, then write what you know of the hard question (formulas and applicable facts) for partial points.
hint: What earns points fastest, and can a partial answer earn any?
solution: Securing the points you can earn quickly is efficient, and partial answers earn partial credit, so after the familiar question you write whatever correct elements you can on the harder one. Spending all the time on the hard question risks losing both, and a blank answer cannot earn points.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Hartwell Trust (fictional) is a pension plan with liabilities of \$300 million with a modified duration of 11.0. Its bond portfolio is \$150 million with a modified duration of 6.0. (a) Calculate the basis-point value of each. (b) If interest rates fall by 100 basis points, estimate the change in each and the effect on the surplus, ignoring convexity and all other assets. (c) Recommend an action and justify it in two sentences. Write your answer as you would in the exam: conclusion first, numbers shown.
hint: BPV is duration times value times 0.0001; a 100 basis point move is 100 times the BPV.
solution: Conclusion: the plan has a large negative duration gap, so falling rates hurt it; extend asset duration. (a) Liability BPV $= 300{,}000{,}000 \times 11.0 \times 0.0001 = \$330{,}000$; bond portfolio BPV $= 150{,}000{,}000 \times 6.0 \times 0.0001 = \$90{,}000$. (b) A 100 basis point fall raises liabilities by about \$33.0 million and the bonds by about \$9.0 million, so the surplus falls by about \$24.0 million. (c) Recommend raising the portfolio's duration toward the liabilities (for example, buying long bond futures or extending bond maturities), because the mismatch exposes the surplus to falling rates and the plan can hedge it without raising equity risk.
rubric: States the conclusion first (duration gap, extend asset duration). 1 point.
rubric: Computes both BPVs correctly (\$330,000 and \$90,000). 1 point.
rubric: Computes the 100 basis point effects (+\$33.0 million, +\$9.0 million, -\$24.0 million net). 1 point.
rubric: Gives a recommendation with a justification tied to the surplus and the duration gap. 1 point.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A candidate's answer to "Justify why a corridor rebalancing policy suits an endowment holding illiquid assets" reads: "Rebalancing is important for risk control. Many investors rebalance regularly. Corridors are useful." The question is worth 4 points. Critique the answer in three or four sentences and write a better answer of about three sentences.
hint: Look for case facts, a clear decision and one reason per point.
solution: The answer defines nothing, states no decision and gives no reasons tied to the endowment's illiquid assets, so it would earn almost no points. A better answer: "A corridor policy suits the endowment because it trades only when an asset class leaves its band, so trading costs are incurred only when drift is large. For illiquid assets such as private funds, which are costly and slow to trade, wide bands avoid forced trading while still limiting drift. It also keeps the policy risk profile within limits without the cost of rebalancing on a fixed calendar date."
rubric: Notes that the original is generic and gives no decision, case link or reasons. 1 point.
rubric: The improved answer states the conclusion that a corridor suits the endowment. 1 point.
rubric: The improved answer gives a reason tied to trading costs. 1 point.
rubric: The improved answer ties the reason to illiquid assets and wide bands. 1 point.
