@article
Level III is the exam where you stop valuing securities and start advising clients. Every recommendation rests on **capital market expectations** (CME): forward-looking views of returns, risks and correlations for the asset classes a client might hold. Candidates who treat CME as a formula list lose points on the questions that ask what could go wrong with a forecast, so this lesson covers both the formulas and the traps.

*How the exam works, in brief.* Level III uses item sets (a vignette followed by multiple-choice questions) and constructed-response (essay) questions. The core curriculum is about 65-70% of the exam and one specialized pathway (Portfolio Management, Private Markets or Private Wealth) the other 30-35%. These facts are for the 2026 exams and change each year; confirm them at cfainstitute.org.

### Equity expected return: Grinold-Kroner

The Grinold-Kroner model splits the expected equity return into income, growth and repricing:

$$E(R_e) \approx \frac{D}{P} - \Delta S + i + g + \Delta(P/E),$$

where $D/P$ is the dividend yield, $\Delta S$ the expected percentage change in shares outstanding (negative when firms are net buyers, so $-\Delta S$ adds to the return), $i$ expected inflation, $g$ real earnings growth and $\Delta(P/E)$ the expected annual percentage change in the valuation multiple. Notice that $i + g$ is nominal earnings growth. The repricing term is where an analyst's view of valuation enters: a market trading at a rich multiple has a negative $\Delta(P/E)$ term.

### Bond expected return: the building blocks

For a bond portfolio, expected return over a horizon is approximately

$$E(R_b) \approx \text{YTM} + \text{rolldown} + E(\Delta P \text{ from yield change}) - E(\text{credit losses}).$$

The yield is a starting point, not a forecast: it is earned only if yields stay put and no defaults occur. The price effect of a yield forecast is $-D_{\text{mod}} \times \Delta y$ (plus a convexity term for large moves).

### Risk premiums: Singer-Terhaar

An asset's risk premium depends on whether its market is integrated with global capital markets. In a fully integrated market, the premium is the global market Sharpe ratio times the asset's volatility times its correlation with the global market portfolio, $RP_{int} = SR_M \sigma_i \rho_{i,M}$. In a fully segmented market, investors price the asset's total risk (the correlation is taken as 1), so $RP_{seg} = SR_i \sigma_i$, where $SR_i$ is the Sharpe ratio of the asset's own market; in practice it is often set equal to the global Sharpe ratio, but the items here give it separately. Real markets sit between, so the estimate is a weighted average, $RP = \varphi\, RP_{seg} + (1-\varphi)\, RP_{int}$, where $\varphi$ is the degree of segmentation. Add the risk-free rate to get an expected return.

### Policy rates: the Taylor rule

$$i^{*} = r_{n} + E(\pi) + 0.5\,(g_{e} - g_{t}) + 0.5\,(E(\pi) - \pi_{t}),$$

where $r_n$ is the neutral real rate, $g_e$ expected real GDP growth, $g_t$ trend growth and $\pi_t$ the inflation target. If the central bank's actual rate is below $i^*$, policy is easy and short rates are likely to rise, other things equal.

### Forecasting traps

The exam likes these: **data mismeasurement** (appraisal-smoothed private returns understate volatility and correlations), **survivorship and backfill bias**, **transcription and data-mining errors**, **using historical estimates** that include one-off repricing gains, **ignoring regime change** (correlations rise in crises), **model and input uncertainty**, and **psychological traps** such as anchoring, overconfidence and confirming evidence. The cure is to state assumptions, test sensitivity, use several methods and compare them with a simple cross-check.

### Exam technique

In an item set, read the question stems first, then the vignette for the numbers they need. In a constructed-response answer, show the formula, the substituted numbers and the units, then one sentence of interpretation. A number with no reasoning earns only part of the points.

@example
title: Equity and policy-rate expectations
problem: A market has a dividend yield of 2.0%, expected inflation of 2.5%, expected real earnings growth of 1.5%, net buybacks that shrink the share count by 0.6% a year and a multiple expected to fall 0.4% a year. Estimate the expected equity return. Separately, with a neutral real rate of 1.0%, expected inflation of 3.0%, a 2.0% target, expected growth of 1.0% and trend growth of 2.0%, estimate the Taylor-rule policy rate.
step: Equity income and growth: $2.0\% + 2.5\% + 1.5\% = 6.0\%$.
step: Buybacks add $0.6\%$ (because $\Delta S = -0.6\%$) and repricing subtracts $0.4\%$, so $E(R_e) = 6.0 + 0.6 - 0.4 = 6.2\%$.
step: Taylor rule: the growth gap is $1.0 - 2.0 = -1.0$ and the inflation gap is $3.0 - 2.0 = +1.0$.
step: $i^* = 1.0 + 3.0 + 0.5(-1.0) + 0.5(1.0) = 4.0\%$. The two gaps offset each other here.
answer: Expected equity return 6.2%; Taylor-rule rate 4.0%.

@example
title: Blending integrated and segmented premiums
problem: A frontier equity market has volatility of 20% and correlation 0.70 with the global market. The global market Sharpe ratio is 0.30, the market's own segmented Sharpe ratio is 0.35, and the degree of segmentation is 0.40. Estimate its risk premium.
step: Integrated premium: $0.30 \times 20\% \times 0.70 = 4.2\%$.
step: Segmented premium: $0.35 \times 20\% = 7.0\%$.
step: Blend: $0.40 \times 7.0\% + 0.60 \times 4.2\% = 2.80\% + 2.52\% = 5.32\%$.
answer: 5.32%.

@mistakes
- Adding the buyback yield with the wrong sign: net buybacks (a falling share count) increase the expected return, and net issuance reduces it.
- Treating a bond's yield to maturity as its expected return and forgetting rolldown, expected yield change and credit losses.
- Using the segmented market's Sharpe ratio in the integrated premium, or a correlation below 1 in the segmented premium.
- Reading a Taylor-rule rate above the actual policy rate as "policy is tight" (it means policy is easy).

@practice num
prompt: A market has a dividend yield of 1.6%, expected inflation of 2.2%, expected real earnings growth of 1.8%, a net buyback yield that reduces the share count by 0.3% a year, and an expected annual repricing of -0.7%. Using the Grinold-Kroner approximation, what is the expected equity return? Give it as a percent to one decimal place.
answer: 5.2
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 1.6 + 2.2 + 1.8 + 0.3 - 0.7
hint: Nominal earnings growth is inflation plus real earnings growth.
hint: A falling share count adds to the return; a falling multiple subtracts from it.
solution: $E(R_e) \approx 1.6 + (2.2 + 1.8) + 0.3 - 0.7 = 5.2\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A strategist projects the next decade's return on a government bond index using its average return over the past 30 years. Over that span yields fell from 12% to 2%, and the index now yields 4%. Which statement best describes the problem with the strategist's approach?
choice*: The historical average includes large price gains from falling yields that cannot repeat, so a forward-looking estimate built from the current yield, rolldown and expected yield change is more appropriate.
choice: The historical average suffers from survivorship bias because bonds that defaulted dropped out of a government index.
choice: The historical average is biased downward because falling yields reduce coupon income.
hint: Ask what drove the past return, and whether that driver is available now.
solution: Much of the past return came from capital gains as yields fell from 12% to 2%. With yields at 4%, those gains cannot be repeated on the same scale, so the building-block approach (yield, rolldown, expected price change, credit losses) is a better guide. Government bonds in a broad index do not default, so survivorship is not the issue, and falling yields raise, not lower, historical total returns.

@practice num
prompt: The global market Sharpe ratio is 0.28. An emerging equity market has volatility of 18%, correlation of 0.75 with the global market, and, if it were fully segmented, a Sharpe ratio of 0.40. The degree of segmentation is 0.50 and the risk-free rate is 3.0%. Using the Singer-Terhaar approach, what is the market's expected return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 8.49
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 3 + 0.5 * 0.40 * 18 + 0.5 * 0.28 * 18 * 0.75
hint: Compute the integrated premium $SR_M \sigma \rho$ and the segmented premium $SR_i \sigma$ separately.
hint: Weight them 50/50, then add the risk-free rate.
solution: Integrated premium $0.28 \times 18 \times 0.75 = 3.78\%$; segmented premium $0.40 \times 18 = 7.20\%$; blended premium $0.5(7.20) + 0.5(3.78) = 5.49\%$; expected return $3.0 + 5.49 = 8.49\%$.

@practice mc
prompt: A central bank has a neutral real rate of 1.5% and an inflation target of 2.0%. Expected inflation is 4.0%, expected real growth is 3.0% and trend growth is 2.0%. The policy rate is 5.0%. Using the Taylor rule with 0.5 weights on both gaps, which conclusion is correct?
choice*: The rule implies 7.0%, so policy is easy relative to the rule and short rates are more likely to rise than fall.
choice: The rule implies 7.0%, so policy is tight relative to the rule and short rates are more likely to fall.
choice: The rule implies 3.0%, so policy is easy relative to the rule and short rates are more likely to rise.
hint: Add the neutral rate, expected inflation and half of each gap.
solution: $i^* = 1.5 + 4.0 + 0.5(3.0 - 2.0) + 0.5(4.0 - 2.0) = 7.0\%$. The actual rate of 5.0% is below that, so policy is easy and the rule points to higher rates. The 3.0% figure comes from leaving out expected inflation.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Fenwick Pension Trust (fictional) asks you to estimate the one-year expected return on a six-year corporate bond index. The index yield to maturity is 4.40%, rolldown return is 0.35%, expected annual credit losses are 0.60%, and modified duration is 6.0. The trust's economist expects yields to rise by 25 basis points. (a) Estimate the expected return. (b) Explain, in two or three sentences, why using the 4.40% yield alone would mislead the trust.
hint: Start with the structure: yield plus rolldown, minus the duration effect and credit losses.
solution: (a) Price effect of the yield view: $-6.0 \times 0.25\% = -1.50\%$. Expected return $= 4.40 + 0.35 - 1.50 - 0.60 = 2.65\%$. (b) The yield is earned only if yields stay unchanged and no defaults occur. Here the expected rise in yields costs 1.50%, credit losses cost 0.60%, and rolldown adds back 0.35%, so the expected return is about 1.75 percentage points below the yield. Using the yield alone would overstate the return the trust can plan on.
rubric: Uses the building-block structure and signs correctly (adds rolldown, subtracts credit losses and the duration effect). 1 point.
rubric: Computes the duration effect as -1.50% and the final answer as 2.65%. 1 point.
rubric: Explains that yield equals expected return only if yields are unchanged and no losses occur. 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A central bank has a neutral real rate of 1.25%, expected inflation of 2.6%, a 2.0% target, expected real growth of 2.4% and trend growth of 1.8%. Using the Taylor rule with 0.5 weights on both gaps, what policy rate does the rule give? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.45
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 1.25 + 2.6 + 0.5 * (2.4 - 1.8) + 0.5 * (2.6 - 2.0)
hint: There are two gaps: growth versus trend and inflation versus target.
solution: $i^* = 1.25 + 2.6 + 0.5(0.6) + 0.5(0.6) = 1.25 + 2.6 + 0.3 + 0.3 = 4.45\%$.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: An analyst at Marlow Capital (fictional) builds expected returns for a client by averaging 15 years of reported returns of a private real estate index (appraisal-based) and a listed equity index. The report shows private real estate with low volatility and near-zero correlation with equities, and the analyst concludes it deserves a large allocation. Identify two flaws in the inputs and explain how each distorts the conclusion and how to correct it.
hint: Look for what the reported numbers hide about true risk, and how you would fix each input.
solution: First, appraisal smoothing: appraised values lag market values, so reported returns understate volatility and correlation. Real estate then looks like a low-risk diversifier when its true risk is higher. Correct by unsmoothing the series or using transaction-based or listed proxies. Second, the historical sample choice and extrapolation: 15 years of reported returns embeds one economic regime and may include one-off repricing gains, and correlations tend to rise in crises. Replace the average with forward-looking building blocks (income, growth, repricing) and run sensitivity tests on risk and correlation inputs. Together, the flaws make the allocation look more efficient than it is.
rubric: Identifies appraisal smoothing and explains that it understates volatility and correlation. 1 point.
rubric: Identifies a second flaw (a single historical regime, extrapolating past returns, or unstable correlations) and explains its effect. 1 point.
rubric: Gives a correction for each flaw (unsmoothing, forward-looking estimates, sensitivity analysis). 1 point.
