At Level III each candidate chooses one **pathway**: Portfolio Management, Private Markets or Private Wealth. Together a pathway is about 30-35% of the exam, and the core is the other 65-70% (2026 figures; confirm each year). Pathway questions are usually set as item sets or constructed-response cases, and they reward candidates who can do the arithmetic quickly and discuss trade-offs in the client's context. This lesson covers private-markets mechanics and a sample of portfolio-management themes; private wealth was Lesson 7.

### Private fund economics

A private equity fund is a limited partnership: **limited partners** (LPs) commit capital that the **general partner** (GP) calls over time, and the GP earns a **management fee** (on committed or invested capital) plus **carried interest** (carry, a share of profits, often 20%). A **distribution waterfall** sets the order of payments. In a whole-fund (European-style) waterfall:

1. Return of the LPs' paid-in capital.
2. A **preferred return** (hurdle), for example 8% a year compounded on paid-in capital, to the LPs.
3. A **catch-up** to the GP, until the GP has received its carry share (say 20%) of total profits so far; with a 100% catch-up, the GP receives $\text{pref} \times c/(1-c)$ where $c$ is the carry rate.
4. The remainder split 80/20 (LP/GP).

A deal-by-deal (American-style) waterfall pays carry on each investment as it is realized, which favors the GP and requires **clawback** provisions to protect LPs.

Fund performance is reported with the **IRR** (since inception, sensitive to timing) and multiples: **DPI** (distributions to paid-in capital, the realized part), **RVPI** (residual net asset value to paid-in capital) and **TVPI** (the sum, DPI + RVPI). The early **J-curve** shows negative returns in the first years because fees are charged on committed capital and investments are held at cost or marked down before value is created. Valuations are appraisal-based, so reported volatility is understated.

### Real estate and other private assets

Income property is valued by **direct capitalization**: $V = \text{NOI}/\text{cap rate}$, where net operating income is effective gross income (potential rent less vacancy) minus operating expenses. Lower cap rates mean higher values. Private real estate, infrastructure, private credit and natural resources have long horizons, illiquidity premiums, valuation lags and capital-call liquidity needs. Pacing models plan **commitments** over several years so that the program reaches a target NAV, using the fund's expected call and distribution patterns. The **denominator effect** occurs when public assets fall: the private allocation, valued with a lag, becomes too large a share of the total and can breach policy limits even without new commitments.

### Portfolio Management pathway themes

The pathway builds on the core with active-management topics: **factor-based investing** (exposure to value, momentum, quality, low volatility and size, with the risk of crowding and factor cycles), **risk budgeting** and total-portfolio approaches, **liquidity management**, and **responsible investing**. ESG approaches include **negative (exclusionary) screening** (remove sectors or issuers), **positive or best-in-class screening**, **ESG integration** (include ESG risks in valuation and weighting), **thematic investing** and **impact investing** (intentional measurable outcomes alongside return). Regulation of sustainability disclosure is changing, so check the current rules in each jurisdiction.

### Exam technique

Pathway answers need numbers first and reasons second. In a waterfall, list each tier in order and check that the GP carry equals the stated share of total profit when a full catch-up is reached. For allocation questions, compute the weights before and after, then discuss liquidity, commitment pacing and governance.

@example
title: A whole-fund waterfall and fund multiples
problem: A fund calls \$100 million over five years (treat all as invested at the start for the hurdle) and distributes \$220 million at the end. The terms are an 8% compounded preferred return, a 100% GP catch-up and 20% carry. How much does the GP receive? Separately, a fund with \$80 million paid in has distributed \$30 million and holds NAV of \$70 million: find DPI, RVPI and TVPI.
step: Return of capital: LPs receive \$100 million, leaving \$120 million.
step: Preferred return: $100(1.08^5 - 1) = \$46.93$ million to the LPs, leaving \$73.07 million.
step: Catch-up: the GP receives $46.93 \times 0.20/0.80 = \$11.73$ million, leaving \$61.34 million.
step: Split the remainder 80/20: the GP gets \$12.27 million and the LPs \$49.07 million. GP carry is $11.73 + 12.27 = \$24.00$ million, which is 20% of the \$120 million profit.
step: DPI $= 30/80 = 0.375$, RVPI $= 70/80 = 0.875$ and TVPI $= 1.25$.
answer: GP carry \$24.00 million; DPI 0.375, RVPI 0.875, TVPI 1.25.

@mistakes
- Applying the carry to the whole profit and forgetting the preferred return and catch-up order.
- Reporting TVPI as if it were a realized return: only DPI has been paid out.
- Reading a negative early IRR as proof of a poor manager when the J-curve and fee drag explain it.
- Missing the denominator effect: a private allocation can exceed its limit when public assets fall, without any new commitment.

@practice mc
prompt: In its second year, a venture fund reports a negative net IRR although its portfolio companies are developing as planned. The fund charges management fees on committed capital and holds investments at cost until a new financing round. Which explanation is most likely?
choice: The manager is destroying value, because a negative IRR in the second year is always a sign of poor selection.
choice*: The J-curve: fees on committed capital and cost-based valuations depress early returns before value creation shows up.
choice: The IRR is wrong, because private funds cannot report an IRR in early years.
hint: Think about fees against invested capital and when valuations are marked up.
solution: Early private fund returns typically dip because fees are charged from the start on committed capital while investments are held at cost. As portfolio companies grow and are marked up or sold, returns rise, which gives the J shape. Poor selection is one possible cause, but it is not implied by an early negative IRR.

lead: \$

@practice self
prompt: Case: Maple Ridge Pension (fictional) has total assets of \$2,000 million, of which \$120 million is private equity NAV (6%) and \$1,880 million is public assets. Its policy target for private equity is 10% with a maximum of 12%. Outstanding unfunded commitments are \$90 million. A public market fall of 20% is possible. (a) Compute the private equity weight after a 20% fall in public assets, with private NAV unchanged. (b) Explain the denominator effect and what Maple Ridge should consider before making new commitments. (c) Name two liquidity risks from unfunded commitments.
hint: After the fall, public assets are 80% of their old value; recompute the weight on the new total.
solution: (a) Public assets become $1{,}880 \times 0.80 = 1{,}504$; the total is $1{,}504 + 120 = 1{,}624$; the private equity weight is $120/1{,}624 = 7.39\%$. (b) The denominator effect is a rise in the private allocation as the total portfolio shrinks while private values, being appraisal-based, adjust slowly. Here the weight moves toward target from below, so the plan is still under its 10% target and could keep committing, but it should pace commitments over several vintages, stress-test calls against a market fall, and recognize that the weight could exceed 12% if private values hold up while public assets fall further. (c) The unfunded \$90 million can be called when public assets are down, forcing sales at low prices; and calls can arrive together with a fall in distributions from existing funds, tightening liquidity.
rubric: Computes the post-fall weight of about 7.39%. 1 point.
rubric: Defines the denominator effect and ties it to the lag in private valuations. 1 point.
rubric: Recommends pacing and stress testing new commitments before committing. 1 point.
rubric: Gives two liquidity risks of unfunded commitments (calls during market stress, falling distributions). 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A fund returned \$90 million to its LPs on \$50 million of paid-in capital (all paid in at the start) after four years. The LPs first receive their capital and an 8% compounded preferred return; the remaining profit is split 80% to LPs and 20% to the GP, with no catch-up. How much carry does the GP receive, in millions of dollars? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 4.40
lead: \$
tol: 0.01
check: ((90 - 50) - 50 * (1.08 ** 4 - 1)) * 0.20
hint: Profit is distributions minus capital; remove the preferred return before applying the 20%.
solution: Profit $= 90 - 50 = 40$. The preferred return is $50(1.08^4 - 1) = 18.02$. Profit above the hurdle is $40 - 18.02 = 21.98$, and the GP receives $0.20 \times 21.98 = \$4.40$ million.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A trustee of a pension plan asks why the plan's private equity weight rose above its limit in a year when public equities fell sharply. In four or five sentences, explain the cause and two policy responses.
hint: Consider what happens to the denominator and to the reported value of private holdings.
solution: The cause is the denominator effect: public assets fell, shrinking the total portfolio, while private equity NAV, based on appraisals that adjust with a lag, fell much less, so its share of the total rose. Policy responses include allowing a temporary band around policy for illiquid assets and pausing or slowing new commitments until the weight returns to range. Rebalancing by selling private fund interests is possible in the secondary market but is expensive, so it is usually a last resort. The plan should also stress-test its unfunded commitments against falls in public assets.
rubric: Explains the denominator effect with the lag in private valuations. 1 point.
rubric: Gives a policy response such as temporary bands or slowing new commitments. 1 point.
rubric: Notes that selling in the secondary market is costly or mentions stress tests of commitments. 1 point.
