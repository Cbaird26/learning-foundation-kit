@article
Bond portfolios are built for one of two purposes. A **liability-driven** portfolio must fund known payments, so the goal is to match the liabilities. A **total-return** (benchmark-relative) portfolio tries to beat an index within limits on duration, curve and credit exposure. Level III expects you to name the purpose, pick the tool and compute what it does.

### Duration, convexity and basis-point value

The approximate percentage price change for a yield change $\Delta y$ is

$$\frac{\Delta P}{P} \approx -D_{\text{mod}}\,\Delta y + \tfrac{1}{2}\,C\,(\Delta y)^2.$$

The **basis-point value** (BPV, also called DV01 or PVBP) is the money change for one basis point: $\text{BPV} \approx D_{\text{mod}} \times P \times 0.0001$. A portfolio's duration is the market-value-weighted average of its bonds' durations. **Key rate durations** show sensitivity to each point on the curve, which matters when the curve does not move in parallel. **Effective duration** applies to bonds with options.

### Immunization

**Classical immunization** protects a single future liability against parallel yield shifts. Reinvestment risk and price risk offset when the portfolio's **Macaulay duration** equals the liability's horizon. The conditions are: (1) the initial portfolio present value is at least the present value of the liabilities, (2) the portfolio's Macaulay duration equals the liability's duration, and (3) the dispersion of the portfolio's cash flows is not much larger than that of the liability (equivalently, asset convexity is not far above liability convexity; a barbell has more dispersion than a bullet). Immunization fails with non-parallel shifts and needs rebalancing as time passes and yields change.

With two bonds of durations $D_1 < D_L < D_2$, the weight in the longer bond is $w_2 = (D_L - D_1)/(D_2 - D_1)$.

### Other liability tools

**Cash flow matching** buys bonds whose coupons and principal pay each liability on the date due. It removes interest rate risk and rebalancing but is costly and constrained by what is available. **Contingent immunization** pursues active management while the portfolio value stays above a floor set by the **safety net** (the minimum acceptable return); when the value falls to that floor, the **trigger point**, the manager must lock in an immunized portfolio. **Multiple-liability immunization** matches the present value and duration of the assets to the liabilities as a group, with asset dispersion at least that of the liabilities.

### Total-return strategies

Active bond managers use: **duration management** (extend when yields are expected to fall), **yield curve positioning** (bullet, barbell or ladder), **rolldown**, **credit and sector selection** and **spread-duration** control. **Rolldown return** is the price gain as a bond ages along an upward-sloping curve with an unchanged curve; **riding the yield curve** buys longer bonds than the horizon to earn it. A **laddered** portfolio spreads maturities evenly, giving liquidity and reinvestment diversification.

Floating-rate notes have an effective duration near the time to the next reset but a **spread duration** close to their remaining maturity, because their fixed quoted margin still moves in price when credit spreads change. Credit spread widening hurts in proportion to spread duration, not Treasury duration.

### Exam technique

A constructed-response bond question usually has four parts: compute (duration, BPV, weights), identify the approach, justify it, and state its limits. Show each number with its formula, then write one clear sentence of justification. State the limit of any immunization answer: parallel shifts only, rebalancing needed, and credit and call risk are ignored.

@example
title: Immunizing a liability with two bonds
problem: A trust must pay one liability whose duration is 8.0 years. It can hold a short bond with Macaulay duration 5.0 and a long bond with 14.0. What weights immunize the duration? Then estimate the price change of a portfolio with modified duration 7.2 and convexity 68 if yields rise 60 basis points.
step: The long-bond weight is $w_L = (8.0 - 5.0)/(14.0 - 5.0) = 3/9 = 33.3\%$, so the short bond is 66.7%.
step: Check the duration: $0.667(5.0) + 0.333(14.0) = 3.33 + 4.67 = 8.0$.
step: Duration effect for $\Delta y = +0.006$: $-7.2 \times 0.006 = -4.32\%$.
step: Convexity effect: $0.5 \times 68 \times 0.006^2 = +0.1224\%$. The total is $-4.32 + 0.12 = -4.20\%$.
answer: 33.3% in the long bond and 66.7% in the short bond; price change about -4.20%.

@mistakes
- Matching modified duration to the liability horizon instead of matching Macaulay duration to the horizon.
- Forgetting the present-value condition: an immunized portfolio must start with at least the present value of the liabilities.
- Applying the convexity term with the wrong sign: it adds to the price in both directions.
- Treating a floating-rate note as risk-free because its effective duration is low; it still carries spread duration.

answer: 4.61
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (-5.6 * -0.008 + 0.5 * 42 * 0.008**2) * 100
hint: The duration effect is $-D_{\text{mod}}\Delta y$ with $\Delta y = -0.008$.

solution: Duration effect: $-5.6 \times (-0.008) = +4.48\%$. Convexity effect: $0.5 \times 42 \times 0.008^2 = 0.1344\%$. Total $= 4.6144\%$, about 4.61%.

@practice mc
prompt: A trust must pay a single liability of \$18 million in six years. The trustees hold bonds whose present value equals the present value of the liability. To immunize against parallel yield shifts, which conditions should the portfolio meet?
choice: Modified duration equal to six years and convexity below the liability's.
choice*: Macaulay duration equal to the liability's duration, with present value at least that of the liability and cash-flow dispersion not far above the liability's.
choice: Effective duration of zero, so that price changes do not matter.
hint: Which duration is set equal to the horizon, and which two other conditions apply?
solution: Classical immunization needs the assets' Macaulay duration to match the liability's duration (six years here), initial present value at least equal to the liability's present value, and asset dispersion (convexity) not much above the liability's. Matching modified duration to the horizon is the wrong measure, and a zero-duration portfolio would not fund a payment in six years.

@practice mc
prompt: A manager holds a five-year floating-rate note that resets quarterly at a reference rate plus a fixed quoted margin. Credit spreads in the issuer's sector are expected to widen. Which statement is correct?
choice: The note is protected, because floating coupons make both effective duration and spread duration near zero.
choice: The note is exposed only if the reference rate changes, because its quoted margin does not affect price.
choice*: The note has low effective duration but a spread duration close to its remaining maturity, so wider spreads will cut its price.
hint: Separate the sensitivity to the reference rate from the sensitivity to the credit spread.
solution: The coupon resets with the reference rate, so effective duration is small. But the fixed quoted margin is discounted at the market spread, so if spreads widen the price falls roughly in proportion to spread duration, which is close to the remaining maturity.

(a) Calculate the amount to invest and the amounts in S and L that match duration. (b) State the three conditions for classical immunization. (c) Give two limitations of the strategy.

(c) It protects only against parallel yield shifts, so a twist in the curve can leave a shortfall; and it needs periodic rebalancing because duration drifts with time and yield changes. Credit and call risk of the bonds are also ignored.

@quiz num
prompt: The yield curve for annual-pay zero-coupon bonds is upward sloping, with a 2-year yield of 3.4% and a 3-year yield of 3.9%. A manager buys a 3-year zero and expects the curve to be unchanged when the bond has two years left, one year from now. What is the bond's total holding-period return over the year, which includes the rolldown? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.91
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: ((1.034 ** -2) / (1.039 ** -3) - 1) * 100
hint: Price the bond now at the 3-year yield, and in one year at the 2-year yield.
solution: Price now $= 1.039^{-3} = 0.89157$ per 1 of par. In one year, with two years left at 3.4%, price $= 1.034^{-2} = 0.93532$. The holding-period return is $0.93532/0.89157 - 1 = 4.91\%$: the 3.9% accretion at the starting yield plus about 1.0% of rolldown from the curve's slope.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A pension committee asks whether to match its next five years of benefit payments with cash flow matching or with duration matching. In four or five sentences, compare the two approaches and say when each is preferable.
hint: Think about what each approach removes, what it costs and how long the liabilities are.
solution: Cash flow matching buys bonds whose coupons and principal arrive on the liability dates, so it removes interest rate risk and the need for rebalancing, but it is expensive, constrains the choice of bonds and leaves no room for active return. Duration matching holds a portfolio whose present value and duration equal the liabilities', which is cheaper and flexible but exposed to non-parallel curve shifts and to rebalancing needs. Cash flow matching suits short, certain liabilities such as the next five years of benefits and sponsors with low risk tolerance, while duration matching suits long liability streams that cannot be matched bond by bond.
rubric: Describes cash flow matching as matching payments date by date with no rebalancing but higher cost. 1 point.
rubric: Describes duration matching as matching present value and duration, with curve-shift and rebalancing risk. 1 point.
rubric: Says when each is preferable, short certain liabilities versus long ones. 1 point.
