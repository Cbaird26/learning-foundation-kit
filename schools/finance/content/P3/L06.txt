@article
A portfolio decision becomes a result only after it is traded, and the result must then be measured and judged. This lesson links three Level III tasks: trading well, attributing what happened, and hiring or firing managers.

### Trading and implementation shortfall

**Implementation shortfall** (IS) measures the cost of turning a decision into a trade against a "paper portfolio" in which the whole order executes at the decision price. For a buy order of $Q$ shares at decision price $P_d$, filled in part at an average price $P_x$ with commissions and an unfilled remainder valued at the end price $P_e$:

$$\text{IS} = \underbrace{Q(P_e - P_d)}_{\text{paper gain}} - \underbrace{\left[Q_x(P_e - P_x) - \text{costs}\right]}_{\text{actual gain}}.$$

It splits into the **realized cost** on executed shares ($Q_x(P_x - P_d)$ plus commissions), and the **missed trade opportunity cost** on unexecuted shares ($(Q - Q_x)(P_e - P_d)$). Delay costs (price movement between decision and order) and market-impact costs sit inside these. Other benchmarks include **VWAP** (volume-weighted average price), appropriate for a trade that tracks volume but easy to game, and the arrival price. Algorithms, dark pools and block trading trade off urgency, impact and information leakage.

### Attribution: the Brinson model

For each sector $i$, with portfolio weight $w_{p,i}$, benchmark weight $w_{b,i}$, portfolio sector return $R_{p,i}$, benchmark sector return $R_{b,i}$ and total benchmark return $R_b$:

- Allocation: $(w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{b,i} - R_b)$
- Selection: $w_{b,i}(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})$
- Interaction: $(w_{p,i} - w_{b,i})(R_{p,i} - R_{b,i})$

The three effects sum across sectors to the total active return, $R_p - R_b$. Overweighting a sector that beat the *total* benchmark earns a positive allocation effect, even if the sector's return is negative in absolute terms. Factor-based attribution extends the same logic to style factors.

### Risk-adjusted measures

- Sharpe ratio: $(R_p - R_f)/\sigma_p$ for total risk.
- Treynor ratio: $(R_p - R_f)/\beta_p$ for systematic risk.
- $M^2$: $R_f + SR_p\times\sigma_b$, the return the portfolio would have earned at the benchmark's volatility.
- Information ratio: active return divided by tracking risk.
- Appraisal ratio: alpha divided by residual risk.
- Sortino ratio: excess return over a minimum acceptable return divided by downside deviation.

### Manager selection and monitoring

A sound process looks at **philosophy, people, process, portfolio and performance, and price** (fees and terms). Past returns alone are weak evidence: strong records can come from luck, a hot style, survivorship in databases or extra risk, and **style analysis** or **returns-based** regressions show what the manager actually holds. Decisions to hire or fire can fail through **Type I errors** (retaining a manager who lacks skill) and **Type II errors** (firing a manager who has skill). Many sponsors diversify across managers, set tracking risk budgets and review qualitative red flags (turnover of key people, style drift, asset growth). Due diligence covers operations, compliance, valuation and custody.

### Exam technique

For attribution, show a three-column table (allocation, selection, interaction) and check that the total equals the active return. For cost problems, give the dollar components, then the total; a final line explaining which component dominated earns the interpretation point.

@example
title: Implementation shortfall and attribution
problem: A manager decides to buy 10,000 shares at \$40.00. Only 6,000 shares fill, at an average of \$40.15, with commissions of \$0.02 a share on shares bought; the rest is cancelled, and the stock closes at \$40.90. Find the implementation shortfall in dollars. Then a portfolio weights sector X at 60% (return 12%) and sector Y at 40% (return 4%), against a benchmark with 50% in each (returns 10% and 3%): find the total active return.
step: Paper gain: $10{,}000 \times (40.90 - 40.00) = \$9{,}000$.
step: Actual gain: $6{,}000 \times (40.90 - 40.15) = \$4{,}500$, less commissions $6{,}000 \times 0.02 = \$120$, giving \$4,380.
step: $\text{IS} = 9{,}000 - 4{,}380 = \$4{,}620$, made of realized cost \$1,020 and a missed opportunity of $4{,}000 \times 0.90 = \$3{,}600$.
step: Portfolio return $0.6(12) + 0.4(4) = 8.8\%$; benchmark return $0.5(10) + 0.5(3) = 6.5\%$; active return 2.3 percentage points.
answer: IS \$4,620; active return 2.3 percentage points (allocation 0.70, selection 1.50, interaction 0.10).

@mistakes
- Treating the unfilled part of an order as free: missed opportunity cost is a real part of implementation shortfall.
- Computing allocation against the sector's own benchmark return rather than against the total benchmark return.
- Leaving out the interaction term, so the effects do not sum to the active return.
- Reading a high Sharpe ratio as proof of skill without asking about the sample period, style and survivorship.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio returned 8.4% with a standard deviation of 12%. The benchmark's standard deviation is 10% and the risk-free rate is 2.0%. What is the portfolio's M-squared return? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 7.33
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 2.0 + (8.4 - 2.0) / 12 * 10
hint: First compute the Sharpe ratio, $(8.4 - 2.0)/12$.
solution: $SR = 6.4/12 = 0.5333$. $M^2 = 2.0 + 0.5333 \times 10 = 7.33\%$, the return the portfolio would earn at the benchmark's volatility.

@practice mc
prompt: A committee hired a manager after three years of returns above the benchmark. Over the three years the manager's style (small-cap value) outperformed broadly, and the manager's style drift has since been noted. Which is the most appropriate next step in the selection and monitoring process?
choice*: Use returns-based style analysis and review philosophy, people and process to see whether the outperformance reflects skill or style exposure.
choice: Keep the manager, because three years of outperformance is sufficient evidence of skill.
choice: Fire the manager immediately, because style drift proves the track record was luck.
hint: Separate skill from the return that a style delivers anyway.
solution: A track record is weak evidence on its own: a style that is in favor lifts returns without skill. Returns-based style analysis and a qualitative review of philosophy, people and process show whether the results are due to skill and whether drift is a concern. Neither retaining nor firing on the three-year record alone is justified.

@practice self
prompt: Case: Pinewood Endowment (fictional) will receive \$60 million of gifts in three months and wants to be invested in equities from today. It also holds euro equities worth EUR 70 million; policy is to hedge 50% of the euro exposure. Equity futures trade at 4,800 with a multiplier of 50 and a beta of 1. (a) Design the overlay: how many futures contracts and what currency hedge? (b) Give two risks of the design and a way to mitigate each.
hint: Equitizing cash needs the amount of money divided by the notional value of one contract.
solution: (a) To equitize the \$60 million, buy $60{,}000{,}000/(4{,}800 \times 50) = 250$ equity futures contracts now and sell them as the cash is invested in the physical portfolio. For the currency policy, sell EUR 35 million (50% of EUR 70 million) forward against the endowment's base currency, with maturities set to the review date and rolled as needed. (b) Basis risk: the futures may not track the endowment's equity mix, which can be mitigated by using the contract closest to the benchmark and monitoring tracking. Liquidity risk: variation margin on futures and forward settlement need cash, which can be mitigated by holding a cash buffer and setting margin limits.
rubric: Computes 250 contracts and explains that they are sold as cash is invested. 1 point.
rubric: Hedges 50% of EUR 70 million (EUR 35 million) by selling the euro forward. 1 point.
rubric: Names two risks (basis, margin or liquidity, counterparty, rolling) with a mitigation for each. 1 point.

@quiz num
prompt: A portfolio is 35% weighted in a sector against a benchmark weight of 25%. The portfolio's return in that sector was 7.0% and the benchmark's return in that sector was 5.0%. What is the Brinson interaction effect, in percentage points? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 0.20
unit: percentage points
tol: 0.01
check: (0.35 - 0.25) * (7.0 - 5.0)
hint: Interaction multiplies the weight difference by the return difference in the sector.
solution: Interaction $= (0.35 - 0.25)(7.0 - 5.0) = 0.10 \times 2.0 = 0.20$ percentage points.

@quiz self
prompt: Case: A foundation's portfolio beat its benchmark by 1.8 percentage points. Attribution shows allocation of +0.2, selection of +2.1 and interaction of -0.5. The committee asks you what this says about the manager and what you would ask next. Answer in four or five sentences.
hint: Check that the effects sum to the active return, then decide which decision added value.
solution: The three effects sum to $0.2 + 2.1 - 0.5 = 1.8$, matching the active return. Most of the value came from security selection within sectors, while sector allocation added little and a negative interaction suggests the manager's overweights were in sectors where its own selection was weaker (or its underweights where selection was stronger). I would ask whether selection was concentrated in a few stocks, whether the result is repeatable across periods, how much active risk was taken (the information ratio), and whether the negative interaction reflects a systematic pattern in which sectors were overweighted.
rubric: Verifies that the effects sum to 1.8 and identifies selection as the main source. 1 point.
rubric: Interprets the negative interaction correctly (overweights where selection was weaker, or underweights where it was stronger). 1 point.
rubric: Asks a sensible follow-up (repeatability, information ratio, concentration). 1 point.
