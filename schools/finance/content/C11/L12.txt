@article
Everything so far describes the past. Analysts need a view of the future: what will earnings be next year, how much will the company need to borrow, will covenants hold? A **forecast of the financial statements** turns a small set of explicit assumptions into a complete, balanced set of statements, so that each assumption's consequences are visible.

### The logic

Forecast from the top down, one driver at a time:

1. **Revenue**: prior revenue times $(1 + g)$, with $g$ based on history, industry growth and company guidance.
2. **Operating costs**: cost of goods sold and SG&A as a percent of revenue (or margins), with depreciation tied to the asset base.
3. **Interest and tax**: interest on debt, tax at the effective rate.
4. **Balance sheet**: working capital from days or percent of sales (receivables from days sales, inventory and payables from days of cost of goods sold), PP&E from capital expenditures and depreciation, debt from financing decisions, equity from earnings and dividends.
5. **Balance**: cash, or a **revolving credit facility**, is the balancing item: cash $=$ total liabilities and equity minus all other assets.

Check the result with the cash flow statement: the change in cash from CFO, CFI and CFF must equal the change in the balance sheet cash.

### Roll-forwards

$$\text{Net PPE}_{end} = \text{Net PPE}_{begin} + \text{Capex} - \text{Depreciation} - \text{Book value of assets sold}$$

$$\text{Equity}_{end} = \text{Equity}_{begin} + \text{Net income} - \text{Dividends} \pm \text{Other}$$

$$\text{Debt}_{end} = \text{Debt}_{begin} + \text{Borrowing} - \text{Repayments}.$$

### Circularity

If interest expense is computed on **average** debt and borrowing depends on cash flow, which depends on net income, which depends on interest, the model refers to itself (a circular reference). Two standard fixes: compute interest on **beginning** balances (used here), or iterate to a solution.

### External financing needed

When a company grows, it must fund more assets. A quick estimate when all assets and some liabilities vary with sales:

$$\text{AFN} = \frac{A^*}{S_0}\,\Delta S - \frac{L^*}{S_0}\,\Delta S - M\,S_1\,(1 - b),$$

where $A^*$ is assets that grow with sales, $L^*$ is spontaneous liabilities (payables and accruals) that grow with sales, $\Delta S = S_1 - S_0$ is the increase in sales, $M$ is the net profit margin, $S_1$ is next year's sales and $b$ is the dividend payout ratio. A positive AFN is external capital that must be raised.

### Judgment and sensitivity

A forecast is only as good as its assumptions, so tie them to evidence (history, competitors, capacity), make the most uncertain drivers explicit, and run **sensitivity** and **scenario** analyses: vary growth, gross margin or days receivable and see how earnings, cash and covenant ratios respond. Keep it simple enough to explain. A forecast with fifty drivers is less credible than one with eight.

@example
title: One-year forecast for Juniper Packaging
problem: Forecast Year 1 for Juniper Packaging from the Year 0 statements below (millions of dollars). Assumptions: revenue grows 10%; COGS is 60% of revenue; SG&A is 20% of revenue; depreciation is 50; interest is 7.5% of beginning debt; tax is 25%; dividends are 40% of net income; capital expenditures are 80; receivables are 12.5% of revenue; inventory and payables are 16.67% and 12.5% of cost of goods sold; accrued liabilities are 2.5% of revenue; debt is unchanged; cash is the balancing item.

| Year 0 income statement | | Year 0 balance sheet | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 1,000 | Cash | 80 |
| Cost of goods sold | 600 | Receivables | 125 |
| SG&A | 200 | Inventory | 100 |
| Depreciation | 50 | Net PPE | 500 |
| EBIT | 150 | Accounts payable | 75 |
| Interest | 30 | Accrued liabilities | 25 |
| Pretax income | 120 | Debt | 400 |
| Tax | 30 | Equity | 305 |
| Net income | 90 | | |
step: Income statement. Revenue $1000 \times 1.10 = 1{,}100$; COGS $= 660$; SG&A $= 220$; EBIT $= 1100 - 660 - 220 - 50 = 170$; interest $= 0.075 \times 400 = 30$; pretax income $= 140$; tax $= 35$; net income $= 105$. Dividends $= 42$.
step: Working capital. Receivables $= 0.125 \times 1100 = 137.5$; inventory $= 660/6 = 110$; payables $= 0.125 \times 660 = 82.5$; accrued liabilities $= 0.025 \times 1100 = 27.5$.
step: Roll-forwards. Net PPE $= 500 + 80 - 50 = 530$; debt stays 400; equity $= 305 + 105 - 42 = 368$.
step: Balance. Liabilities and equity $= 82.5 + 27.5 + 400 + 368 = 878$. Non-cash assets $= 137.5 + 110 + 530 = 777.5$. Cash $= 878 - 777.5 = 100.5$, so total assets are 878.
step: Check with cash flow: CFO $= 105 + 50 - 12.5 - 10 + 7.5 + 2.5 = 142.5$; capex $-80$; dividends $-42$; net change $= 20.5$, and $80 + 20.5 = 100.5$. The forecast is consistent.
answer: Net income 105; ending cash 100.5; total assets 878, equal to liabilities and equity.

@example
title: A minimum cash balance and a revolver
problem: Juniper's treasury policy requires at least 120 of cash at year-end. Using the Year 1 forecast (balancing cash 100.5 before any revolver), how much must it draw on a revolving credit facility, and what happens to leverage?
step: The shortfall is $120 - 100.5 = 19.5$, so the revolver draw is 19.5.
step: Cash rises to 120 and debt to $400 + 19.5 = 419.5$; total assets become $878 + 19.5 = 897.5$, equal to total liabilities and equity.
step: Debt to equity rises from $400/368 = 1.09$ to $419.5/368 = 1.14$. Interest on the draw is ignored in this year because interest is computed on beginning balances; with average balances the next year's interest would rise by about 19.5 times the rate.
answer: Draw 19.5 on the revolver, taking debt to 419.5 and debt to equity to 1.14.

@mistakes
- Forecasting the balance sheet without checking that it balances, or letting cash be a forecast driver and the plug at the same time.
- Computing interest on average debt without recognizing the circular reference, or using it with a hard-coded number that no longer matches.
- Letting depreciation or working capital grow without tying them to capital expenditures or sales.
- Presenting a single point forecast with no sensitivity analysis.

model:
```python
def forecast_income(revenue, growth, cogs_pct, sga_pct, depreciation,
                    beginning_debt, interest_rate, tax_rate):
    """Forecast next year's income statement from simple drivers."""
    new_revenue = revenue * (1 + growth)
    cogs = new_revenue * cogs_pct
    sga = new_revenue * sga_pct
    ebit = new_revenue - cogs - sga - depreciation
    interest = interest_rate * beginning_debt
    pretax = ebit - interest
    tax = max(0.0, pretax * tax_rate)
    return {
        "revenue": new_revenue,
        "ebit": ebit,
        "interest": interest,
        "net_income": pretax - tax,
    }
```
hint: Compute forecast revenue first; every other cost line depends on it.
hint: Taxes are zero when pretax income is negative: use `max(0, ...)`.
solution: Grow revenue, apply the cost percentages, subtract depreciation to get EBIT, then deduct interest on the beginning debt. Tax applies only to positive pretax income, so a loss passes through to net income untaxed, as the last test checks.

prompt: A company has sales of \$2,000 million, all of its \$1,200 million of assets vary with sales, and \$300 million of its liabilities (payables and accruals) rise with sales. Sales are forecast to grow 15%, its net profit margin is 6% and it pays out 40% of earnings as dividends. What is the additional external financing needed (AFN), in millions of dollars to one decimal place?
answer: 52.2
lead: \$
tol: 0.1
check: 1200 / 2000 * (2000 * 0.15) - 300 / 2000 * (2000 * 0.15) - 0.06 * 2000 * 1.15 * (1 - 0.40)
hint: The increase in sales is $0.15 \times 2000 = 300$ and next year's sales are 2,300.
solution: $\Delta S = 300$. Required asset growth is $0.6 \times 300 = 180$; spontaneous liabilities add $0.15 \times 300 = 45$; retained earnings add $0.06 \times 2300 \times 0.6 = 82.8$. $\text{AFN} = 180 - 45 - 82.8 = 52.2$ million dollars.
