@article
Two hardware wholesalers sell identical goods at identical prices. One reports a gross margin two points higher, a higher net income and a larger inventory. Nothing about the businesses differs: one values inventory with FIFO and the other with LIFO. Before comparing ratios you must put the statements on the same basis. That is what **adjusting for accounting differences** means.

### Why adjust

Accounting standards allow choices (inventory cost-flow method, capitalizing or expensing certain costs, useful lives, impairment testing) and differ across frameworks. The goal of an adjustment is not to find the "right" accounting but to make companies **comparable**, or to make one company's years comparable after a policy change. Adjust only where the effect is material, and say what you changed and why.

### LIFO to FIFO

During a period of rising costs, a **LIFO** (last-in, first-out) company charges the newest, higher costs to cost of goods sold, so its income is lower and its inventory is carried at old, lower costs. LIFO is permitted under US GAAP but not under IFRS. Companies using LIFO disclose the **LIFO reserve**: the amount by which FIFO (or current-cost) inventory exceeds LIFO inventory. With reserve $R$ and tax rate $t$:

$$\text{Inventory}_{FIFO} = \text{Inventory}_{LIFO} + R_{end}$$

$$\text{COGS}_{FIFO} = \text{COGS}_{LIFO} - (R_{end} - R_{begin})$$

$$\text{Pretax income}_{FIFO} = \text{Pretax income}_{LIFO} + (R_{end} - R_{begin}), \qquad \text{NI}_{FIFO} = \text{NI}_{LIFO} + (R_{end} - R_{begin})(1 - t)$$

$$\text{Equity}_{FIFO} = \text{Equity}_{LIFO} + R_{end}(1 - t).$$

The equity adjustment is after tax because the higher FIFO profits would have been taxed: the offset is a larger deferred tax liability of $R_{end} \times t$ (assuming the company would have paid tax on the higher income). When costs fall, the reserve shrinks and the adjustments reverse. LIFO also overstates inventory turnover and understates the current ratio during rising prices, because cost of goods sold is current while inventory is old.

### Capitalizing versus expensing

Some costs can be capitalized (recorded as an asset and amortized) or expensed immediately. Under IFRS (IAS 38), **development costs** that meet specific criteria are capitalized; under US GAAP most research and development is expensed (software development after technological feasibility has its own rules). Capitalizing raises current income and assets, and moves the cash outflow from operating to investing activity, which raises CFO. To compare with an expensing company, restate: remove the asset and the amortization, and charge the full cost.

### Other differences to know

| Topic | US GAAP | IFRS |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory method | FIFO, weighted average or LIFO | FIFO or weighted average; LIFO prohibited |
| Inventory write-down reversal | Not permitted | Permitted, up to original cost |
| Property, plant and equipment | Cost model only | Cost model or revaluation model |
| Development costs | Generally expensed | Capitalized once criteria are met |
| Impairment of long-lived assets | Recoverability test on undiscounted cash flows, then write-down to fair value; no reversal | Compare carrying amount with recoverable amount; reversal allowed (except goodwill) |

IFRS and US GAAP have converged on revenue (IFRS 15 and ASC 606) and, in structure, on leases (IFRS 16 and ASC 842, Week 9), but differences remain, and standards change, so check the current text.

### A practical approach

1. Read the accounting policies note to list the choices each company made.
2. Estimate the effect with disclosed data (the LIFO reserve, capitalized amounts and amortization, revaluation surplus).
3. Restate the statements and recompute the ratios on the common basis.
4. Document your assumptions, and do not pretend that the adjusted numbers are precise.

@example
title: Restating Harlan Metals from LIFO to FIFO
problem: Harlan Metals uses LIFO. It reports inventory of 600, COGS of 2,400, net income of 210, equity of 1,500, current assets of 1,400 and current liabilities of 700 (millions of dollars). The LIFO reserve was 80 at the start of the year and 110 at the end. The tax rate is 25%. Restate to FIFO.
step: Inventory: $600 + 110 = 710$. Current assets become $1400 + 110 = 1{,}510$, so the current ratio rises from $1400/700 = 2.00$ to $1510/700 = 2.16$.
step: The reserve rose by $110 - 80 = 30$. COGS: $2400 - 30 = 2{,}370$. Pretax income rises by 30.
step: Net income: $210 + 30 \times 0.75 = 232.5$.
step: Equity: $1500 + 110 \times 0.75 = 1{,}582.5$ (the reserve net of 27.5 of tax).
answer: FIFO inventory 710, COGS 2,370, net income 232.5, equity 1,582.5, current ratio 2.16.

@example
title: Restating capitalized development costs
problem: Orchard Analytics reports under IFRS and capitalized 60 of development costs at the start of the year, amortizing them over five years on a straight-line basis. Its reported EBIT is 300, its CFO is 400 and its tax rate is 25%. To compare it with a US GAAP competitor that expenses such costs, restate Orchard to expensing.
step: Amortization recorded this year: $60/5 = 12$. Under expensing, the full 60 is charged and there is no amortization, so pretax income falls by $60 - 12 = 48$.
step: Restated EBIT $= 300 - 48 = 252$. Net income falls by $48 \times 0.75 = 36$.
step: The asset is removed: capitalized costs net of amortization are $60 - 12 = 48$ lower. Equity is lower by 36 and the deferred tax liability by 12 (assuming tax deducted the cost immediately).
step: Cash flow: the 60 is reclassified from investing to operating, so restated CFO is $400 - 60 = 340$ and investing outflows are 60 smaller; total cash is unchanged.
answer: Restated EBIT 252, net income lower by 36, CFO 340.

@mistakes
- Adjusting equity by the full LIFO reserve and forgetting the tax effect (the adjustment is $R \times (1 - t)$).
- Using the ending LIFO reserve for the cost of goods sold adjustment; use the change in the reserve.
- Believing that the restated numbers are exact, or restating every difference, however small.
- Forgetting that capitalizing a cost raises CFO as well as net income.
