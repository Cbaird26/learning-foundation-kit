@article
A profitable company can still fail if it cannot pay bills when they fall due, and an unprofitable one can survive for years if its debt is modest and its cash is large. Analysts therefore ask two separate questions. **Liquidity**: can the company meet obligations due in the next year? **Solvency**: can it meet all its obligations over the long run, given how much it has borrowed?

### Liquidity ratios

All use balance sheet amounts at the end of the period (the question is about the present position, so averages are not used):

$$\text{Current ratio} = \frac{\text{Current assets}}{\text{Current liabilities}}, \qquad \text{Quick ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Current liabilities}},$$

$$\text{Cash ratio} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities}}{\text{Current liabilities}}.$$

The quick ratio drops inventory and prepaid items, which are slower to turn into cash. (Some definitions subtract inventory and prepaids from current assets instead; the results are usually close.) The **defensive interval ratio** asks how many days the company could pay its cash operating costs from its liquid assets alone:

$$\text{DIR} = \frac{\text{Cash} + \text{Marketable securities} + \text{Receivables}}{\text{Daily cash expenditures}}, \qquad \text{Daily cash expenditures} = \frac{\text{Cash operating costs}}{365}.$$

Here cash operating costs are COGS plus operating expenses less non-cash charges such as depreciation; the definition varies, so state yours.

### Solvency ratios

**Leverage** ratios measure the mix of financing. Definitions of "debt" differ, so always state which. This lesson uses **interest-bearing debt** (short-term borrowings, current portion of long-term debt and long-term debt) unless stated, and mentions lease liabilities when they matter (Week 9). Debt and equity are year-end balances here, like the liquidity ratios.

$$\text{Debt-to-equity} = \frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{Total equity}}, \quad \text{Debt-to-assets} = \frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{Total assets}}, \quad \text{Financial leverage} = \frac{\text{Total assets}}{\text{Total equity}}.$$

An analyst may instead use total liabilities in the numerator (often called total liabilities to equity); both are legitimate, but the numbers differ. Equity may be book or market value.

**Coverage** ratios measure ability to pay the cost of debt from earnings:

$$\text{Interest coverage} = \frac{\text{EBIT}}{\text{Interest expense}}, \qquad \text{Fixed charge coverage} = \frac{\text{EBIT} + \text{Lease payments}}{\text{Interest} + \text{Lease payments}}.$$

Coverage ratios use EBIT (before interest and tax) because interest is paid before taxes. Variants use EBITDA in the numerator, which is more generous because depreciation is not a cash charge, but a capital-intensive company must still replace its equipment. The **net debt to EBITDA** ratio, $(\text{Debt} - \text{Cash and marketable securities})/\text{EBITDA}$, shows roughly how many years of earnings it would take to repay the debt.

### One transaction, opposite effects

Ratios respond to transactions differently. Suppose current assets are 1,450 and current liabilities 900, so the current ratio is 1.61. Paying 100 of payables with cash lowers both by 100: the current ratio becomes $1{,}350/800 = 1.69$ (a ratio above 1 rises when equal amounts leave top and bottom). But if the quick ratio is below 1, the same payment lowers it. Do not read one ratio alone, and remember that window dressing near the period end (paying off payables, delaying purchases) can improve ratios temporarily.

### Interpreting levels

A "good" level depends on the industry: a grocer with fast-turning inventory and supplier credit can run a current ratio near 1, while a shipbuilder cannot. Compare with competitors, with history, and with **covenants** in the debt agreements (for example, a maximum debt-to-EBITDA and a minimum interest coverage), which lenders use to trigger protections.

title: Liquidity and solvency of Alder Home Goods
problem: Alder Home Goods has the year-end balance sheet and income data below (millions of dollars). Compute the current, quick and cash ratios, the defensive interval ratio (cash operating costs are COGS plus SG&A), debt-to-equity and debt-to-assets using interest-bearing debt, interest coverage, fixed charge coverage and net debt to EBITDA.

| Balance sheet | | Income data | |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cash | 180 | Revenue | 4,800 |
| Marketable securities | 60 | Cost of goods sold | 3,120 |
| Receivables | 620 | SG&A (includes rent of 120) | 1,100 |
| Inventory | 780 | Depreciation and amortization | 180 |
| Prepaid and other | 60 | EBIT | 400 |
| Net property | 1,150 | Interest expense | 80 |
| Goodwill and other | 150 | | |
| Accounts payable | 420 | | |
| Accrued liabilities | 180 | | |
| Short-term debt | 200 | | |
| Long-term debt | 900 | | |
| Other long-term liabilities | 100 | | |
| Total equity | 1,200 | | |
step: Current assets are $180 + 60 + 620 + 780 + 60 = 1{,}700$ and current liabilities are $420 + 180 + 200 = 800$. Current ratio $= 1700/800 = 2.13$. Quick ratio $= (180 + 60 + 620)/800 = 1.08$. Cash ratio $= 240/800 = 0.30$.
step: Daily cash costs $= (3120 + 1100)/365 = 11.56$, so $\text{DIR} = 860/11.56 = 74.4$ days.
step: Total debt $= 200 + 900 = 1{,}100$ and total assets are $1700 + 1150 + 150 = 3{,}000$. Debt-to-equity $= 1100/1200 = 0.92$; debt-to-assets $= 1100/3000 = 36.7\%$.
step: Interest coverage $= 400/80 = 5.0$ times. Fixed charge coverage (rent of 120 as the lease payment) $= (400 + 120)/(80 + 120) = 2.60$.
step: EBITDA $= 400 + 180 = 580$. Net debt $= 1100 - 240 = 860$, so net debt to EBITDA $= 860/580 = 1.48$ years.
answer: Current 2.13, quick 1.08, cash 0.30, DIR 74.4 days, D/E 0.92, debt-to-assets 36.7%, interest coverage 5.0, fixed charge coverage 2.60, net debt/EBITDA 1.48.

@example
title: A payment that moves ratios in opposite directions
problem: A company has cash and receivables of 710 in total (cash and securities 190, receivables 520), inventory 660, prepaid 80, and current liabilities 900. It uses 100 of cash to pay accounts payable. Compute the current and quick ratios before and after.
step: Before: current assets $= 710 + 660 + 80 = 1{,}450$. Current ratio $= 1450/900 = 1.61$ and quick ratio $= 710/900 = 0.79$.
step: After: cash falls to 90 and payables fall by 100, so current assets are 1,350 and current liabilities 800. Current ratio $= 1350/800 = 1.69$ and quick ratio $= 610/800 = 0.76$.
step: The current ratio rose and the quick ratio fell, because the current ratio started above 1 and the quick ratio started below 1.
answer: Current ratio rises from 1.61 to 1.69; quick ratio falls from 0.79 to 0.76.

@mistakes
- Using averages for liquidity ratios, which describe the position at one date, or using year-end balances for activity ratios.
- Computing debt-to-equity with total liabilities in one company and interest-bearing debt in another without saying so.
- Using net income instead of EBIT in interest coverage; interest is paid out of earnings before interest and tax.
- Reading a high current ratio as always good: it can mean idle cash or bloated inventory.
