@article
A retailer's inventory, its stores and its leased warehouses are all assets that the balance sheet records at accounting values that can drift far from economic reality. This lesson turns the footnotes on those assets into analytical signals.

### Inventory

Inventory is carried at the **lower of cost and net realizable value** (NRV) under IFRS, and under US GAAP for inventory valued by FIFO or average cost (inventory valued by LIFO or the retail method uses lower of cost or market). NRV is the estimated selling price less the estimated costs to complete and sell:

$$\text{NRV} = \text{Estimated selling price} - \text{Costs to complete and sell}.$$

If NRV is below cost, the inventory is written down and the loss goes to cost of goods sold. IFRS allows a later reversal up to the original cost, US GAAP does not. Analysts watch for rising inventory days, shrinking margins and growing write-downs as signs that goods are not selling; a sudden large write-down also shifts profit between years.

### Long-lived assets: how old is the asset base?

The fixed asset note gives gross property, plant and equipment (PPE), accumulated depreciation and depreciation expense. Three approximations assume straight-line depreciation and no major disposals:

$$\text{Average age} = \frac{\text{Accumulated depreciation}}{\text{Depreciation expense}}, \qquad \text{Total useful life} = \frac{\text{Gross PPE}}{\text{Depreciation expense}}, \qquad \text{Remaining life} = \frac{\text{Net PPE}}{\text{Depreciation expense}}.$$

Remaining life equals total life minus average age. The **percent depreciated**, accumulated depreciation divided by gross PPE, tells the same story as a proportion. An old, heavily depreciated base signals large future replacement spending, and a company with long assumed lives relative to peers may be understating depreciation. A long-lived asset whose carrying amount is not recoverable is written down (an **impairment**); large impairments are a signal that past investments earned less than expected, and they move profit between periods.

### Leases

A lessee obtains the right to use an asset for a term in return for payments. Under **ASC 842** (US GAAP), a lessee records almost every lease longer than 12 months on the balance sheet as a **right-of-use (ROU) asset** and a **lease liability**, both initially the present value of the lease payments at the discount rate. The difference between the two types lies in the income statement:

- **Operating lease (ASC 842)**: one straight-line lease cost, in operating expenses. EBIT, EBITDA and CFO are all after the full lease cost, and the payments sit in CFO.
- **Finance lease (ASC 842)** and **all leases under IFRS 16**: the lessee records depreciation of the ROU asset (usually straight-line) and interest on the liability. EBITDA excludes both, so it is higher. The total expense is front-loaded: interest is highest early. The principal repayment is a financing cash flow.

Short-term (12 months or less) and, under IFRS 16, low-value leases may be expensed. Because IFRS 16 uses one model, an IFRS company's EBITDA and debt are higher than the US GAAP operating-lease treatment of an identical lease.

### Adjusting for comparability

To compare a US GAAP company with operating leases to an IFRS company, treat the operating lease like a finance lease: add the lease liability to debt, add the lease cost back to EBITDA, replace the lease cost in EBIT by the ROU depreciation, and add the interest on the liability to interest expense. For a more careful analysis, use the present value of future lease payments disclosed in the footnote. Payments of 100 at the end of each of 5 years, discounted at 6%, have a present value of 421.24, not 500, because later payments are worth less today.

@example
title: Age of the asset base
problem: Gimlet Freight reports gross PPE of 2,400, accumulated depreciation of 1,080 and depreciation expense of 160 (millions of dollars). Estimate the average age, total useful life, remaining life and percent depreciated, and interpret.
step: Net PPE is $2400 - 1080 = 1{,}320$.
step: Average age $= 1080/160 = 6.75$ years. Total useful life $= 2400/160 = 15.0$ years. Remaining life $= 1320/160 = 8.25$ years (check: $15.0 - 6.75 = 8.25$).
step: Percent depreciated $= 1080/2400 = 45\%$.
step: On average the assets are about halfway through their lives. If a competitor's percent depreciated is 70%, Gimlet is better placed; if its capital expenditures are well below depreciation, the base is aging.
answer: Average age 6.75 years, total life 15.0 years, remaining life 8.25 years, 45% depreciated.

@example
title: Adjusting Tern Retail for a lease
problem: Tern Retail (US GAAP) signed a 5-year operating lease on the first day of the year with end-of-year payments of 100, discounted at 6%. Its reported EBIT is 400, EBITDA 580 (after lease cost), interest expense 80, debt 1,100, cash and securities 240 and equity 1,200 (millions of dollars; at year-end). Restate for IFRS 16-style comparability.
step: Present value of the payments: $100 \times \left[1 - 1.06^{-5}\right]/0.06 = 421.24$. Year 1 interest is $421.24 \times 0.06 = 25.27$, so the year-end liability is $421.24 + 25.27 - 100 = 346.51$. ROU depreciation is $421.24/5 = 84.25$.
step: EBITDA: add back the lease cost, $580 + 100 = 680$. EBIT: replace the lease cost of 100 by depreciation of 84.25, so $400 + 100 - 84.25 = 415.75$. Interest becomes $80 + 25.27 = 105.27$.
step: Debt-to-equity rises from $1100/1200 = 0.92$ to $(1100 + 346.51)/1200 = 1.21$. Net debt to EBITDA moves from $860/580 = 1.48$ to $(1100 + 346.51 - 240)/680 = 1.77$.
step: Interest coverage falls from $400/80 = 5.00$ to $415.75/105.27 = 3.95$. The firm looks more leveraged once leases are treated as debt, and comparable with an IFRS peer.
answer: Adjusted EBITDA 680, EBIT 415.75, debt-to-equity 1.21, net debt to EBITDA 1.77, interest coverage 3.95.

@mistakes
- Treating the formulas for average age and remaining life as exact: they assume straight-line depreciation and few disposals.
- Comparing the leverage of an IFRS company with a US GAAP company's debt without including operating lease liabilities.
- Adding the lease liability to debt but leaving EBITDA unchanged, which double-counts the lease cost.
- Assuming a write-down is a cash loss: it is a non-cash charge that reflects past spending.
