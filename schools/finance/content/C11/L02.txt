@article
Larkspur Outfitters earns \$99 million and Bellwether Foods earns \$192 million. Bellwether is nearly twice as profitable, but it is also much larger, so the raw dollars say little about which business is better run. **Common-size analysis** removes size by expressing every line as a percentage of one base line; **trend analysis** removes the passage of time by comparing each year with a base year.

### Vertical common-size statements

A **vertical common-size income statement** divides every line by revenue:

$$\text{Line (\%)} = \frac{\text{Line item}}{\text{Revenue}} \times 100.$$

A **vertical common-size balance sheet** divides every line by total assets. The base lines are 100%. Because every column now adds to the same total, you can compare structure directly: how much of each sales dollar goes to cost of goods sold, selling expense and tax; how much of the assets are inventory or goodwill; how much is financed with debt.

| Income statement (millions of dollars) | Larkspur | % of revenue | Bellwether | % of revenue |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue | 2,000 | 100.0 | 3,200 | 100.0 |
| Cost of goods sold | 1,240 | 62.0 | 2,176 | 68.0 |
| Gross profit | 760 | 38.0 | 1,024 | 32.0 |
| SG&A | 520 | 26.0 | 560 | 17.5 |
| Depreciation and amortization | 80 | 4.0 | 144 | 4.5 |
| Operating income | 160 | 8.0 | 320 | 10.0 |
| Interest expense | 28 | 1.4 | 64 | 2.0 |
| Pretax income | 132 | 6.6 | 256 | 8.0 |
| Income tax | 33 | 1.65 | 64 | 2.0 |
| Net income | 99 | 4.95 | 192 | 6.0 |

Larkspur keeps a higher gross margin (38.0% against 32.0%) but spends much more on selling (26.0% against 17.5%), which is typical of a retailer against a packaged-goods maker. The tables say where to dig; they do not say why.

### Horizontal analysis and growth

**Horizontal analysis** compares the same line over time. Two forms are common:

- **Percentage change:** $g_t = \dfrac{X_t}{X_{t-1}} - 1$.
- **Base-year index:** $\text{Index}_t = \dfrac{X_t}{X_0} \times 100$, so the base year equals 100.

For growth over several years, use the **compound annual growth rate**:

$$\text{CAGR} = \left(\frac{X_n}{X_0}\right)^{1/n} - 1,$$

where $n$ is the number of years between the first and last values (the number of growth periods, not the number of data points).

### Comparing the two together

Always compare the growth of related lines. If revenue grows 12% a year while receivables grow 25%, collections are slowing. If revenue is flat while SG&A rises, margins will fall. Trend analysis is also the first place to spot a change in accounting policy or presentation, such as a new revenue standard or the move to IFRS 18's operating, investing and financing categories (effective for annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2027), which can reclassify lines and break comparisons with earlier years; US GAAP's ASU 2024-03 (annual periods beginning after December 15, 2026) will also add detail on expense types.

### Limits

Common-size percentages depend on the base: a one-time revenue spike lowers every other percentage. An index depends on the base year: if the base year was a recession trough, growth looks spectacular. Percentages hide absolute size and mix different business models. Compare like with like, and look at several years.

@example
title: Common-size balance sheet
problem: Larkspur Outfitters (millions of dollars) has: cash 120, receivables 210, inventory 330, other current assets 40, net property 900, goodwill 150, other long-term assets 50. Prepare the common-size asset side and say which line is the largest and what it suggests.
step: Total assets $= 120 + 210 + 330 + 40 + 900 + 150 + 50 = 1{,}800$.
step: Divide each line by 1,800: cash $6.67\%$, receivables $11.67\%$, inventory $18.33\%$, other current $2.22\%$, net property $50.00\%$, goodwill $8.33\%$, other long-term $2.78\%$. The percentages add to 100.
step: Net property is half of all assets, so the company is capital-intensive (stores and equipment), and inventory, at 18.3%, is the largest working capital asset.
answer: Net property is 50.0% of assets; inventory is 18.3%.

@example
title: Trend and CAGR for revenue and earnings
problem: Larkspur's revenue was 1,600, 1,800 and 2,000 (millions of dollars) in Years 1, 2 and 3, and net income was 72, 85 and 99. Compute indexes with Year 1 as the base and the Year 1 to Year 3 CAGR of each.
step: Revenue index: Year 2 is $1800/1600 \times 100 = 112.5$ and Year 3 is $2000/1600 \times 100 = 125.0$.
step: Net income index: Year 2 is $85/72 \times 100 = 118.1$ and Year 3 is $99/72 \times 100 = 137.5$.
step: There are $n = 2$ growth periods. Revenue CAGR $= (2000/1600)^{1/2} - 1 = 1.25^{0.5} - 1 = 11.80\%$. Net income CAGR $= (99/72)^{1/2} - 1 = 1.375^{0.5} - 1 = 17.26\%$.
step: Net income grew faster than revenue, so the net margin rose from $72/1600 = 4.50\%$ to $99/2000 = 4.95\%$.
answer: Revenue CAGR 11.80%, net income CAGR 17.26%; margin up from 4.50% to 4.95%.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong base: income statement items are divided by revenue, balance sheet items by total assets.
- Counting data points instead of growth periods in the CAGR: three years of data is $n = 2$.
- Reading a change in percent of revenue as a change in dollars: a line can fall as a percentage while rising in dollars.
- Subtracting percentages as if they were growth rates: margin changes are in percentage points, not percent.

import math
result = common_size({"revenue": 200, "cogs": 120, "sga": 50}, "revenue")

model:
```python
def common_size(items, base):
    """Return each item as a percentage of the base item."""
    base_amount = items[base]
    if base_amount == 0:
        raise ValueError("base amount must not be zero")
    return {name: amount / base_amount * 100
            for name, amount in items.items()}
```
hint: Look up the base amount first, and check it is not zero.
hint: Use a dictionary comprehension that divides each amount by the base amount and multiplies by 100.
solution: Divide every amount by the base amount and scale by 100. The zero check prevents a division error and is a sensible guard when a company reports no revenue. Because the function takes the base line as a parameter, the same code works for income statements (base revenue) and balance sheets (base total assets).
