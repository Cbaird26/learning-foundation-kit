@article
Two companies both report net income of \$100 million. One collected \$110 million in cash from operations and earned its profit from selling products it will sell again next year. The other collected \$20 million, booked a large sale to a distributor that has not paid, and recorded a one-time gain from selling a building. Same earnings, very different quality.

### What earnings quality means

**Earnings quality** has two parts. **Quality of reported results**: are the earnings *sustainable* (likely to recur), *cash-backed*, and *representative* of the true economics, free from distortion by aggressive estimates or choices? **Quality of financial reporting**: is the disclosure complete and transparent? High-quality earnings come from core operations, convert to cash, and rest on conservative, consistently applied accounting. Standards such as ASC 606 and IFRS 15 leave room for judgment (when a performance obligation is satisfied, how much variable consideration to estimate), and judgment is where bias creeps in.

### Accruals

Accounting profit equals cash flow plus **accruals**. Accruals are the part of earnings not yet received in cash: receivables not collected, inventory not sold, expenses deferred. Because accruals tend to reverse in later periods (receivables go uncollected, inventory is marked down), large positive accruals relative to the size of the business predict weaker future earnings. Two common measures:

- **Cash flow accruals ratio** $= \dfrac{\text{Net income} - \text{CFO}}{\text{Average total assets}}$. This course uses the simple form above; some analysts use average net operating assets in the denominator and include investing cash flow in the numerator (a balance-sheet version computes the change in net operating assets instead).
- **Cash flow to net income** $= \text{CFO}/\text{NI}$, a related check from Week 6.

A ratio far above zero (a rule of thumb: persistently above about 10%) or CFO persistently below net income deserves scrutiny, especially when the business is not growing quickly.

### Diagnostic indices

Researchers have built screens that compare this year with last year. Three of the simplest, with Year $t$ against Year $t-1$:

$$\text{DSRI} = \frac{\text{Receivables}_t/\text{Sales}_t}{\text{Receivables}_{t-1}/\text{Sales}_{t-1}}, \quad \text{GMI} = \frac{\text{Gross margin}_{t-1}}{\text{Gross margin}_t}, \quad \text{SGI} = \frac{\text{Sales}_t}{\text{Sales}_{t-1}}.$$

A DSRI above 1 means receivables grew faster than sales; a GMI above 1 means gross margin fell; a high SGI means rapid growth, which creates pressure to keep up. These are three of the eight indices in the Beneish model of earnings manipulation, which combines them into one score; the indices are warnings, not proof.

### Common red flags

- **Revenue**: sales growing faster than receivables is normal; receivables growing faster, or sales concentrated in the last days of a quarter, is not. Watch for bill-and-hold sales, extended customer payment terms and changes in revenue-recognition policy.
- **Expenses**: capitalizing costs that peers expense, lengthening useful lives to cut depreciation, understating reserves (then releasing them in weak years, "cookie jar"), and taking large write-offs in one year to flatter later years (a "big bath").
- **Recurring "non-recurring" items**: restructuring charges or impairments that appear every year are really operating costs.
- **Balance sheet**: inventory growing faster than sales, rising goodwill from many small acquisitions, off-balance-sheet entities and related-party dealings.
- **Cash flow**: CFO below net income year after year; operating cash flow lifted by stretching payables or selling receivables.
- **Governance**: frequent auditor changes, qualified opinions, management compensation tied tightly to a single earnings target, and restatements. The Sarbanes-Oxley Act of 2002 followed a wave of accounting failures and requires management and auditors to assess internal control over financial reporting.

### Non-GAAP measures

Companies report adjusted measures such as "adjusted EBITDA." In the United States, the SEC requires a reconciliation to the nearest GAAP measure and prohibits giving the adjusted figure undue prominence. Ask what was excluded, whether it recurs, and whether adjustments are symmetric (are gains removed as readily as losses?). IFRS 18 will add disclosure of management-defined performance measures from 2027.

@example
title: Screening Quillon Devices
problem: Quillon Devices reports the figures below (millions of dollars). Compute the DSRI, GMI, SGI, the Year 2 cash flow accruals ratio, and say what they suggest.

| | Year 1 | Year 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Sales | 1,000 | 1,250 |
| Cost of goods sold | 600 | 800 |
| Receivables (year-end) | 100 | 190 |
| Net income | 120 | 150 |
| Cash flow from operations | 130 | 60 |
| Total assets (year-end; Year 0 was 900) | 1,000 | 1,200 |
step: Receivables to sales: Year 1 $100/1000 = 0.100$; Year 2 $190/1250 = 0.152$. $\text{DSRI} = 0.152/0.100 = 1.52$.
step: Gross margin: Year 1 $400/1000 = 40.0\%$; Year 2 $450/1250 = 36.0\%$. $\text{GMI} = 40/36 = 1.11$. $\text{SGI} = 1250/1000 = 1.25$.
step: Average total assets in Year 2 $= (1000 + 1200)/2 = 1{,}100$. Accruals ratio $= (150 - 60)/1100 = 8.2\%$ (Year 1: $(120 - 130)/950 = -1.1\%$). CFO to net income fell from 1.08 to 0.40.
step: Sales are growing 25%, but receivables grew 90%, margin fell and earnings are no longer backed by cash. These are warning signs, not proof of manipulation: the next step is the notes on revenue recognition and customer terms.
answer: DSRI 1.52, GMI 1.11, SGI 1.25, accruals ratio 8.2%; receivables, margin and cash flow all point to weaker earnings quality.

@example
title: A recurring "non-recurring" charge
problem: A company reports GAAP net income of 100 and adjusted net income that adds back a pretax restructuring charge of 30, taxed at 25%. The company has recorded restructuring charges of 30, 25 and 35 in the last three years. What is adjusted net income, and how should an analyst treat the charge?
step: After-tax charge $= 30 \times (1 - 0.25) = 22.5$. Adjusted net income $= 100 + 22.5 = 122.5$.
step: A charge that recurs every year at about the same size is a normal cost of running the business.
step: A sustainable earnings figure should keep an average charge (about 30 pretax, 22.5 after tax) in costs, so about 100 is the better estimate of ongoing earnings, not 122.5.
answer: Adjusted net income is 122.5, but because the charge recurs, the analyst treats about 100 as sustainable earnings.

@mistakes
- Treating a red-flag index as proof of fraud; it only tells you where to look.
- Computing the accruals ratio with total assets at one date while the other inputs are for the year (use averages, as the course does).
- Accepting adjusted or "pro forma" earnings without checking whether excluded items recur.
- Looking at one year: earnings quality is a pattern over several years and against peers.
