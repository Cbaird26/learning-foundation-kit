@article
A shareholder wants the company to grow and is paid from what is left. A lender is paid a fixed amount, gains nothing from success beyond that amount, and loses if the borrower fails. **Credit analysis** therefore looks downward: how bad can things get, and will the borrower still be able to pay? It emphasizes cash flow, leverage in a downturn and the protections written into the contract.

### The four Cs

- **Capacity**: the ability to generate cash to service and repay debt (the focus of the ratios below).
- **Collateral**: the assets pledged or available if the borrower fails, and the seniority of the claim.
- **Covenants**: promises in the debt agreement that limit risk.
- **Character**: management's record, governance and willingness to repay, drawn partly from the earnings-quality work of Week 7.

### Core credit ratios

Definitions vary by lender and agency; state yours.

$$\frac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{EBITDA}}, \qquad \frac{\text{EBITDA}}{\text{Interest expense}}, \qquad \frac{\text{FFO}}{\text{Total debt}}, \qquad \frac{\text{Retained cash flow}}{\text{Total debt}} = \frac{\text{FFO} - \text{Dividends}}{\text{Total debt}}.$$

**Funds from operations** (FFO) is net income plus depreciation, amortization and other non-cash charges (and deferred taxes), that is, operating cash flow before changes in working capital. Many analysts include leases in debt (Week 9), use net debt, and measure ratios over a full cycle rather than a good year. Rating agencies publish ranges by category: broadly, a borrower rated BBB− (Baa3 on the other major scale) or above is **investment grade**; lower is speculative grade (high yield). Lower leverage and higher coverage map to higher ratings, but the thresholds differ by industry because stable businesses carry more debt.

### Covenants

**Maintenance covenants** must be satisfied at every test date (for example, debt to EBITDA no greater than 3.5 times, tested quarterly); a breach is a default unless waived. **Incurrence covenants** are tested only when the company takes an action, such as borrowing more or paying a large dividend, and block that action if the ratio would breach. Covenant **headroom** is how far the ratio is from the limit: for a maximum leverage covenant $L$, EBITDA can fall to $\text{Debt}/L$ before breach, a decline of $1 - \text{Debt}/(L \times \text{EBITDA})$.

### The Altman Z-score

The original **Altman Z-score** (1968), built for public manufacturing companies, is

$$Z = 1.2\,X_1 + 1.4\,X_2 + 3.3\,X_3 + 0.6\,X_4 + 1.0\,X_5,$$

where $X_1 = \text{Working capital}/\text{Total assets}$, $X_2 = \text{Retained earnings}/\text{Total assets}$, $X_3 = \text{EBIT}/\text{Total assets}$, $X_4 = \text{Market value of equity}/\text{Book value of total liabilities}$ and $X_5 = \text{Sales}/\text{Total assets}$. The conventional zones are: $Z > 2.99$ "safe", $1.81 \le Z \le 2.99$ "grey", and $Z < 1.81$ "distress". The model was fitted to a particular sample in a particular era; use it as a screen, with other variants for private, non-manufacturing or emerging-market firms.

### Seniority and structure

Claims are paid in order of priority: secured creditors from their collateral, then senior unsecured, subordinated, and finally equity. **Structural subordination** arises when debt is issued by a holding company that owns operating subsidiaries: creditors of a subsidiary are paid from the subsidiary's assets before anything flows up to the holding company's creditors. Always identify which entity borrowed and which entities guarantee the debt.

@example
title: Altman Z-score for Marlow Components
problem: Marlow Components has total assets of 2,000, working capital of 300, retained earnings of 500, EBIT of 240, market value of equity of 1,800, total liabilities of 1,100 and sales of 2,600 (millions of dollars). Compute the Z-score and classify it.
step: $X_1 = 300/2000 = 0.150$, $X_2 = 500/2000 = 0.250$, $X_3 = 240/2000 = 0.120$.
step: $X_4 = 1800/1100 = 1.636$ and $X_5 = 2600/2000 = 1.300$.
step: $Z = 1.2(0.150) + 1.4(0.250) + 3.3(0.120) + 0.6(1.636) + 1.0(1.300) = 0.180 + 0.350 + 0.396 + 0.982 + 1.300 = 3.21$.
step: $Z = 3.21 > 2.99$, the "safe" zone. The large market capitalization contributes $0.982$, so a fall in the share price would reduce the score even with unchanged fundamentals.
answer: Z is about 3.21, in the safe zone.

@example
title: Covenant headroom
problem: A borrower has total debt of 1,800, EBITDA of 600 and interest expense of 150 (millions of dollars). Its loan has a maintenance covenant of debt to EBITDA of at most 3.5 and EBITDA to interest of at least 3.0. How far can EBITDA fall before each covenant is breached?
step: Leverage today: $1800/600 = 3.00$. EBITDA at which leverage reaches 3.5: $1800/3.5 = 514.3$, a decline of $1 - 514.3/600 = 14.3\%$.
step: Coverage today: $600/150 = 4.00$. EBITDA at which coverage reaches 3.0: $3.0 \times 150 = 450$, a decline of $25.0\%$.
step: The leverage covenant is tighter: it breaks first, after a 14.3% fall in EBITDA, so that is the binding constraint. A lender would ask whether a downturn could cut EBITDA by that much.
answer: Leverage breaches after a 14.3% EBITDA decline; coverage after 25.0%; leverage is binding.

@mistakes
- Using the ratios of a single good year; credit analysis stresses a downturn.
- Forgetting that leverage covenants are defined in the agreement (EBITDA may be adjusted, debt may be net of cash), so check the definitions.
- Treating the Z-score as a probability of default or applying it unchanged to banks, private firms or non-manufacturers.
- Ignoring structural subordination and treating all debt of a corporate group as equal.

model:
```python
def altman_z(working_capital, retained_earnings, ebit,
             market_value_equity, sales, total_assets, total_liabilities):
    """Original Altman Z-score for public manufacturing companies."""
    x1 = working_capital / total_assets
    x2 = retained_earnings / total_assets
    x3 = ebit / total_assets
    x4 = market_value_equity / total_liabilities
    x5 = sales / total_assets
    return 1.2 * x1 + 1.4 * x2 + 3.3 * x3 + 0.6 * x4 + 1.0 * x5


def z_zone(z):
    """Classify a Z-score as safe, grey or distress."""
    if z > 2.99:
        return "safe"
    if z < 1.81:
        return "distress"
    return "grey"
```
hint: Compute the five ratios first; only X4 divides by total liabilities.
hint: In `z_zone`, check the two outer zones and return "grey" for everything else.
solution: Compute the five ratios, weight them with 1.2, 1.4, 3.3, 0.6 and 1.0, and add. The zone function tests the two outer zones with strict inequalities, so the boundary values 2.99 and 1.81 fall in the grey zone.

prompt: A holding company issues bonds and owns operating subsidiaries that have their own bank loans. If the group fails, which statement best describes structural subordination?
choice: Holding company bondholders are paid first because they lent to the parent.
choice*: Creditors of the operating subsidiaries are paid from the subsidiaries' assets before value flows up to the holding company's creditors.
