@article
Net income is an opinion shaped by accrual accounting; cash is a fact. A company that reports a profit but cannot generate cash from its operations will eventually have to borrow or sell shares to survive, so analysts read the **statement of cash flows** as closely as the income statement.

### The three sections

Cash flow from **operating activities** (CFO) comes from the main business, **investing activities** (CFI) from buying and selling long-term assets and investments, and **financing activities** (CFF) from raising and returning capital:

$$\Delta\text{Cash} = \text{CFO} + \text{CFI} + \text{CFF}.$$

Under **US GAAP**, interest paid, interest received, dividends received and taxes paid are operating, while dividends paid are financing. Under **IFRS**, interest paid and dividends paid may be classified as operating or financing, and interest and dividends received as operating or investing, as long as the choice is applied consistently. IFRS 18 (effective for annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2027) narrows these choices for most companies, so check the current text. Because of this, a company's CFO is not directly comparable across frameworks until you adjust.

### The indirect method

Most companies start from net income and convert it to cash:

$$\text{CFO} = \text{NI} + \text{Non-cash charges} \pm \text{Gains or losses on investing and financing items} - \Delta\text{Net working capital}.$$

Add back depreciation and amortization, stock-based compensation and impairments (they reduced income but not cash). Remove gains on asset sales (the whole proceeds appear in investing) and add back losses. Subtract increases in operating working capital assets (receivables, inventory) and add increases in operating liabilities (payables, accruals), because cash was tied up or provided.

### Free cash flow

**Free cash flow to the firm** is the cash available to all capital providers after investment in fixed capital. Starting from CFO:

$$\text{FCFF} = \text{CFO} + \text{Interest paid} \times (1 - t) - \text{FCInv},$$

where $t$ is the tax rate and $\text{FCInv}$ is **fixed capital investment**: capital expenditures less proceeds from sales of fixed assets. The interest term is added back (net of the tax shield) only because interest was deducted in CFO, as it is under US GAAP. If interest paid is classified as financing, CFO already excludes the interest payment but still includes the tax saving it created, so $\text{FCFF} = \text{CFO} - \text{Interest paid}\times t - \text{FCInv}$.

**Free cash flow to equity** is the cash available to shareholders after debt holders are paid:

$$\text{FCFE} = \text{CFO} - \text{FCInv} + \text{Net borrowing}.$$

Both formulas assume the US GAAP classification, in which interest paid is deducted in CFO and dividends paid are not. Under IFRS, if interest paid is classified as financing, subtract it from CFO before computing FCFE; if dividends paid are in CFO, add them back before computing either measure. For annual periods beginning on or after January 1, 2027, IFRS 18's amendments to IAS 7 require most companies (those whose main business is not lending or investing) to classify interest paid and dividends paid as financing cash flows, and interest and dividends received as investing cash flows, so these IFRS adjustments become the normal case.

Several definitions of "free cash flow" exist in practice (some subtract only capital expenditures; some deduct acquisitions); always state yours.

### Cash flow ratios

- **Cash flow to net income** $= \text{CFO}/\text{NI}$: persistent values below 1 mean earnings are not converting to cash.
- **CFO to total debt**: a rough measure of how many years of operating cash would repay the debt.
- **CFO to capital expenditures**: whether operations fund investment internally.
- **Cash flow patterns**: positive CFO, negative CFI and negative CFF is the profile of a healthy, growing company that invests and repays capital. Negative CFO financed by borrowing is a red flag unless the company is early-stage and growing quickly.

@example
title: Cash flow from operations and free cash flow
problem: Cedar Ridge Hardware had net income of 240, depreciation and amortization of 150 and a loss of 10 on the sale of equipment. Receivables rose 60, inventory rose 45, accounts payable rose 30, accrued liabilities fell 10 and deferred tax liabilities rose 15. It spent 250 on equipment, received 40 from selling old equipment, borrowed 100, repaid 60 of debt, paid dividends of 80 and repurchased shares for 50. Interest paid (included in CFO) was 50, the tax rate is 25%, and beginning cash was 120 (all in millions of dollars). Build the cash flow statement and compute FCFF and FCFE.
step: CFO: $240 + 150 + 10 - 60 - 45 + 30 - 10 + 15 = 330$.
step: CFI: $-250 + 40 = -210$. CFF: $100 - 60 - 80 - 50 = -90$.
step: Net change in cash $= 330 - 210 - 90 = 30$, so ending cash $= 120 + 30 = 150$.
step: $\text{FCInv} = 250 - 40 = 210$. $\text{FCFF} = 330 + 50 \times 0.75 - 210 = 157.5$.
step: $\text{FCFE} = 330 - 210 + (100 - 60) = 160$. Cash flow to net income is $330/240 = 1.38$, comfortably above 1.
answer: CFO 330, CFI -210, CFF -90, ending cash 150; FCFF 157.5; FCFE 160.

@example
title: Same company, different classification
problem: Cedar Ridge pays interest of 50 in cash. Show how CFO and FCFF change if the company reported under IFRS and classified interest paid as financing instead of operating.
step: Operating cash flow would exclude the interest: $330 + 50 = 380$ (the 50 moves from operating to financing; the tax effect stays in operating).
step: Financing cash flow becomes $-90 - 50 = -140$. Total change in cash is still $380 - 210 - 140 = 30$.
step: FCFF must not add interest back again, because CFO already excludes it; only the interest tax shield is removed: $\text{FCFF} = 380 - 50 \times 0.25 - 210 = 157.5$, the same as before.
answer: CFO would be 380 instead of 330, but FCFF is 157.5 either way; compare CFO only after putting both companies on the same classification.

@mistakes
- Adding back interest to CFO that does not contain it (interest classified as financing).
- Forgetting to remove gains (or add back losses) on asset sales in CFO, since proceeds belong in investing.
- Reading an increase in receivables or inventory as a source of cash; it is a use.
- Comparing CFO between companies without checking where interest and dividends are classified.

model:
```python
def fcff(cfo, interest_paid, tax_rate, capex, ppe_sale_proceeds=0):
    """Free cash flow to the firm from cash flow from operations."""
    fixed_capital_investment = capex - ppe_sale_proceeds
    after_tax_interest = interest_paid * (1 - tax_rate)
    return cfo + after_tax_interest - fixed_capital_investment
```
hint: Fixed capital investment is capex minus sale proceeds.
hint: Add interest after the tax shield, then subtract fixed capital investment.
solution: Compute fixed capital investment and after-tax interest, then add the interest to CFO and subtract the investment. The default of zero for sale proceeds makes the parameter optional, and the last test shows a case in which asset sales exceed capex so that investment is negative.

@quiz num
prompt: A company generated cash flow from operations of \$410 million, spent \$300 million on capital expenditures and received \$25 million from selling equipment. It borrowed \$150 million, repaid \$90 million of debt, paid dividends of \$110 million and repurchased \$70 million of shares. Beginning cash was \$200 million. What was ending cash, in millions of dollars?
answer: 215
lead: \$
tol: 0.5
check: 200 + 410 + (-300 + 25) + (150 - 90 - 110 - 70)
hint: Add the three section totals to beginning cash.
solution: CFI $= -300 + 25 = -275$ and CFF $= 150 - 90 - 110 - 70 = -120$. Net change $= 410 - 275 - 120 = 15$, so ending cash is $200 + 15 = 215$ million dollars.
