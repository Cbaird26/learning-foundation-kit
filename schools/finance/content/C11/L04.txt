@article
A grocery chain collects cash at the register and pays its suppliers weeks later; a heavy-equipment maker builds for months and then waits 90 days for payment. Both may be sound businesses, but they need very different amounts of cash to fund working capital. **Activity (efficiency) ratios** measure how quickly a company converts its assets and obligations into sales and cash.

### Turnover and days

A **turnover** ratio divides a flow (sales or cost of goods sold) by the average balance of the related asset or liability. A **days** measure divides the number of days in the period, here **365**, by the turnover. Many analysts use 360 or quarterly figures; choose one and keep it consistent.

| Measure | Formula (average balances) |
|---|---|
| Receivables turnover | $\dfrac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average accounts receivable}}$ |
| Days sales outstanding (DSO) | $\dfrac{365}{\text{Receivables turnover}}$ |
| Inventory turnover | $\dfrac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average inventory}}$ |
| Days inventory on hand (DIO) | $\dfrac{365}{\text{Inventory turnover}}$ |
| Payables turnover | $\dfrac{\text{COGS}}{\text{Average accounts payable}}$ |
| Days payables outstanding (DPO) | $\dfrac{365}{\text{Payables turnover}}$ |

The receivables formula properly uses **credit sales**; outside analysts usually cannot see them and use total revenue, which understates DSO for firms with large cash sales. Payables are owed for **purchases**, not cost of goods sold, so the more careful payables turnover uses

$$\text{Purchases} = \text{COGS} + \text{Ending inventory} - \text{Beginning inventory}.$$

Many analysts use COGS for simplicity; both are valid if you say which and apply it consistently.

### The cash conversion cycle

The **cash conversion cycle** (CCC, or net operating cycle) is the time between paying suppliers and collecting from customers:

$$\text{CCC} = \text{DIO} + \text{DSO} - \text{DPO}.$$

A shorter CCC means less cash is tied up. A falling CCC is usually good, but beware of a cause that is not efficiency: stretching payables beyond normal terms damages supplier relations, and slashing inventory below what is needed loses sales. A negative CCC is possible when a company sells quickly for cash and pays suppliers slowly, as some retailers do.

### Long-term asset turnover

$$\text{Fixed asset turnover} = \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average net property, plant and equipment}}, \qquad \text{Total asset turnover} = \frac{\text{Revenue}}{\text{Average total assets}}.$$

Total asset turnover is the middle term of the DuPont analysis. Low fixed asset turnover may mean underused capacity or heavy recent investment that has not yet produced sales; high turnover can mean old, mostly depreciated assets, or capacity that is stretched thin.

### Interpreting ratios

Compare against the company's history, its competitors and its industry. Look for **trends**: receivable days rising faster than sales growth may mean looser credit terms, collection problems or revenue recognized too early (a topic of Week 7); inventory days rising may mean obsolescence risk. Seasonal businesses distort year-end balances, so averages of quarterly balances are better than a simple two-point average.

@example
title: Working capital ratios for Alder Home Goods
problem: Alder Home Goods reports revenue of 4,800 and cost of goods sold of 3,120 (millions of dollars). Balances at the start and end of the year: receivables 540 and 620, inventory 700 and 780, payables 380 and 420, net property 1,050 and 1,150. Assume all revenue is on credit, use average balances, 365 days, and COGS for payables. Compute the activity ratios and the cash conversion cycle.
step: Averages: receivables $(540 + 620)/2 = 580$; inventory $(700 + 780)/2 = 740$; payables $(380 + 420)/2 = 400$; net property $(1050 + 1150)/2 = 1{,}100$.
step: Receivables turnover $= 4800/580 = 8.276$, so DSO $= 365/8.276 = 44.1$ days.
step: Inventory turnover $= 3120/740 = 4.216$, so DIO $= 365/4.216 = 86.6$ days. Payables turnover $= 3120/400 = 7.800$, so DPO $= 365/7.8 = 46.8$ days.
step: $\text{CCC} = 86.6 + 44.1 - 46.8 = 83.9$ days. Fixed asset turnover $= 4800/1100 = 4.36$.
answer: DSO 44.1 days, DIO 86.6 days, DPO 46.8 days, CCC 83.9 days; fixed asset turnover 4.36.

@example
title: The purchases variant of payables days
problem: For Alder (cost of goods sold 3,120; inventory 700 at the start and 780 at the end; average payables 400, in millions of dollars), recompute days payables using purchases, and the new cash conversion cycle.
step: Purchases $= 3120 + 780 - 700 = 3{,}200$.
step: Payables turnover $= 3200/400 = 8.000$, so $\text{DPO} = 365/8 = 45.6$ days.
step: With DIO 86.6 and DSO 44.1 from before, $\text{CCC} = 86.57 + 44.10 - 45.63 = 85.05$ days, or about 85 days (summing the one-decimal values gives 85.1 because of rounding).
step: The definition changes the cycle by about one day here, because inventory grew by 80. When a company's inventory is growing or shrinking quickly, the gap widens, so always state the definition.
answer: DPO is 45.6 days on purchases and the cash conversion cycle is about 85 days.

@mistakes
- Using year-end balances for some ratios and averages for others, then comparing across companies or years.
- Dividing payables by sales instead of by cost of goods sold or purchases; payables arise from costs, not from revenue.
- Forgetting to subtract days payables in the cash conversion cycle (adding all three days measures).
- Reading a shorter cycle as automatically good without checking whether payables were stretched or inventory starved.

prompt: A company's credit sales were \$2,400 million. Accounts receivable were \$280 million at the start of the year and \$320 million at the end. Using average receivables and 365 days, compute days sales outstanding to one decimal place.
answer: 45.6
unit: days
tol: 0.1
check: 365 / (2400 / ((280 + 320) / 2))
hint: Average receivables are $(280 + 320)/2 = 300$.
solution: Receivables turnover $= 2400/300 = 8.0$, so $\text{DSO} = 365/8 = 45.6$ days.

@practice num
prompt: A company has receivables turnover of 9.0, inventory turnover of 6.0 and payables turnover of 8.0 (all based on average balances). Using 365 days, compute its cash conversion cycle in days, to one decimal place.
answer: 55.8
unit: days
tol: 0.1
check: 365 / 9.0 + 365 / 6.0 - 365 / 8.0
hint: Convert each turnover to days by dividing 365 by it.
solution: $\text{DSO} = 365/9 = 40.6$, $\text{DIO} = 365/6 = 60.8$, $\text{DPO} = 365/8 = 45.6$, so $\text{CCC} = 40.6 + 60.8 - 45.6 = 55.8$ days.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how a company can have a negative cash conversion cycle, give one kind of business where it is common, and name one risk an analyst should check before treating it as a sign of strength.
hint: Write the cash conversion cycle formula and ask when it can fall below zero.
solution: The CCC is DIO plus DSO minus DPO, so it is negative when days payable exceed the days needed to sell inventory and collect cash. A supermarket or online retailer sells inventory quickly, is paid at the sale, and pays suppliers on 45 to 60 day terms, so suppliers effectively finance its operations. The risk is dependence on supplier credit: if suppliers tighten terms in a downturn, or if payables were stretched beyond normal terms, the company must find cash, and a shrinking business can see the favorable cycle reverse.
