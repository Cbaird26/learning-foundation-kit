@article
A **system of linear equations** such as
$$2x + y = 7, \qquad x - y = 2$$
can be written compactly as $Ax = b$, where $A$ holds the coefficients, $x$ the unknowns and $b$ the right-hand sides:
$$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 7 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Gaussian elimination

The systematic method uses three **row operations** that never change the solution: swap two equations, multiply an equation by a nonzero number, add a multiple of one equation to another. Use them to create zeros below the diagonal (**forward elimination**), then solve from the bottom up (**back substitution**). Adding the two equations above eliminates $y$: $3x = 9$, so $x = 3$ and $y = 1$. Elimination is the method computers use, because it is fast and numerically stable when you swap in the largest available pivot.

### The determinant and the inverse

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$, the **determinant** is $\det A = ad - bc$. If $\det A \neq 0$, the matrix is **invertible** and its inverse is
$$A^{-1} = \frac{1}{ad - bc}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix},$$
which satisfies $AA^{-1} = A^{-1}A = I$. Then the system $Ax = b$ has the unique solution $x = A^{-1}b$.

If $\det A = 0$ the matrix is **singular**: the equations are redundant or contradictory, and the system has either no solution or infinitely many. For larger matrices the determinant and inverse are computed by software, but the rule is the same: a unique solution exists for every $b$ exactly when $\det A \neq 0$.

### Pricing systems and state prices

Suppose tomorrow the economy ends in one of two states, *up* or *down*, and assets pay known amounts in each. The price of each asset is the sum over states of (payoff in that state) $\times$ (the price today of a dollar delivered in that state). These state prices $q = (q_1, q_2)$ solve a linear system:
$$\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \text{payoff}_{1,\text{up}} & \text{payoff}_{1,\text{down}} \\ \text{payoff}_{2,\text{up}} & \text{payoff}_{2,\text{down}} \end{pmatrix}}_{A}\begin{pmatrix} q_1 \\ q_2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} P_1 \\ P_2 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Once you know $q$, the price of any other asset with payoffs $(x_1, x_2)$ is $x_1q_1 + x_2q_2$. This is the idea behind the no-arbitrage pricing of derivatives.

Practical advice: check a solution by substituting it back into **every** original equation, and do not compute an inverse when elimination suffices.

@example
title: State prices from a bond and a stock
problem: A risk-free bond pays \$105 in both states and costs \$100. A stock of Larkspur Outfitters pays \$130 in the up state and \$90 in the down state and costs \$100. Find the state prices.
step: Write the system: $105q_1 + 105q_2 = 100$ and $130q_1 + 90q_2 = 100$, so $A = \begin{pmatrix} 105 & 105 \\ 130 & 90 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: $\det A = 105(90) - 105(130) = 9450 - 13650 = -4200 \neq 0$, so a unique solution exists. The inverse is $A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-4200}\begin{pmatrix} 90 & -105 \\ -130 & 105 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: $q_1 = \frac{90(100) - 105(100)}{-4200} = \frac{-1500}{-4200} = \frac{5}{14} = 0.3571$ and $q_2 = \frac{-130(100) + 105(100)}{-4200} = \frac{-2500}{-4200} = \frac{25}{42} = 0.5952$.
step: Check: $q_1 + q_2 = \frac{15 + 25}{42} = \frac{20}{21} = 0.9524$, which is the price of \$1 for sure ($\frac{100}{105}$). Both prices are positive, as no-arbitrage requires.
answer: $q_1 = 5/14 \approx 0.3571$ (up), $q_2 = 25/42 \approx 0.5952$ (down).

@example
title: Gaussian elimination on a three-variable system
problem: Solve $x + y + z = 6$, $2y + 5z = -4$ and $2x + 5y - z = 27$.
step: Eliminate $x$ from the third equation by subtracting $2\times$ the first: $3y - 3z = 15$, which simplifies to $y - z = 5$.
step: Now use the second equation $2y + 5z = -4$ with $y = z + 5$: $2z + 10 + 5z = -4$, so $7z = -14$ and $z = -2$.
step: Back-substitute: $y = z + 5 = 3$, then $x = 6 - y - z = 6 - 3 + 2 = 5$.
step: Check in the third equation: $2(5) + 5(3) - (-2) = 10 + 15 + 2 = 27$. The solution is $(5, 3, -2)$.
answer: $(x, y, z) = (5, 3, -2)$

@mistakes
- Applying a row operation to only some entries of an equation instead of every term including the right-hand side.
- Using $A^{-1}b$ when $\det A = 0$; a singular matrix has no inverse.
- Multiplying $b$ by $A^{-1}$ on the wrong side: the solution is $x = A^{-1}b$, never $bA^{-1}$.
- Forgetting to check the solution in all of the original equations.

solution: A unique solution $x = A^{-1}b$ exists for every $b$ precisely when $A$ is invertible, that is, when $\det A \neq 0$. A zero determinant means the equations are dependent or inconsistent, and positivity or symmetry of the entries does not decide invertibility.

model:
```python
def solve_linear(A, b):
    """Solve Ax = b by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(A)
    M = [list(map(float, row)) + [float(rhs)] for row, rhs in zip(A, b)]
    for col in range(n):
        pivot = max(range(col, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][col]))
        M[col], M[pivot] = M[pivot], M[col]
        for r in range(col + 1, n):
            factor = M[r][col] / M[col][col]
            for c in range(col, n + 1):
                M[r][c] -= factor * M[col][c]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for r in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        total = sum(M[r][c] * x[c] for c in range(r + 1, n))
        x[r] = (M[r][n] - total) / M[r][r]
    return x
```
hint: Build an augmented matrix with a copy of each row plus its right-hand side, so the inputs are not modified.
hint: For each column, swap the best pivot row up, subtract multiples of it from the rows below, then back-substitute from the last row up.
solution: Forward elimination turns the augmented matrix into upper-triangular form, and back substitution then solves for the last unknown first. The row swap handles a zero on the diagonal, as in the second test.
