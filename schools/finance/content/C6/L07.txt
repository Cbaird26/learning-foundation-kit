@article
A **vector** is an ordered list of numbers. In finance a vector can hold the weights of a portfolio, the expected returns of assets, or a day's prices. We write column vectors, $w = (w_1, \dots, w_n)^T$, and the superscript $T$ means **transpose** (turn a column into a row, or the reverse). A **matrix** is a rectangular array: an $m \times n$ matrix has $m$ rows and $n$ columns, and its entry $a_{ij}$ is in row $i$, column $j$.

### Dot product and norm

The **dot product** of two vectors of equal length is the sum of products of matching entries:
$$u \cdot v = u^Tv = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 + \dots + u_nv_n.$$
The **norm** (length) of a vector is $\lVert v\rVert = \sqrt{v \cdot v}$. For example, $(3, 4, 12)$ has norm $\sqrt{9 + 16 + 144} = 13$.

The most important finance use: if $w$ is the vector of portfolio weights and $\mu$ is the vector of expected returns, the **expected return of the portfolio** is
$$\mu_p = w^T\mu = \sum_i w_i\mu_i.$$
The weights fully invested sum to 1, which is written $\mathbf 1^Tw = 1$, where $\mathbf 1$ is a vector of ones. The sum of squared weights, $w^Tw$, is a simple concentration measure: it equals 1 for a single asset and $1/n$ for $n$ equal weights.

### Matrix-vector product

Multiplying an $m \times n$ matrix $A$ by a vector $x$ with $n$ entries gives a vector with $m$ entries: each entry is the dot product of a row of $A$ with $x$. If the rows of $R$ are scenarios and the columns are assets, then $Rw$ gives the portfolio return in every scenario at once. With
$$R = \begin{pmatrix} 0.10 & 0.04 \\ 0.02 & 0.03 \\ -0.06 & 0.02 \end{pmatrix}, \quad w = \begin{pmatrix} 0.6 \\ 0.4 \end{pmatrix}, \quad Rw = \begin{pmatrix} 0.076 \\ 0.024 \\ -0.028 \end{pmatrix}.$$

### Matrix multiplication

The product $AB$ is defined only when the number of columns of $A$ equals the number of rows of $B$. If $A$ is $m \times n$ and $B$ is $n \times p$, then $AB$ is $m \times p$, and
$$(AB)_{ij} = \sum_{k=1}^n a_{ik}b_{kj},$$
the dot product of row $i$ of $A$ with column $j$ of $B$. Key facts:

- **Not commutative**: even when both $AB$ and $BA$ exist, they usually differ.
- **Associative and distributive**: $(AB)C = A(BC)$ and $A(B + C) = AB + AC$.
- **Transpose of a product reverses order**: $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$.
- The **identity matrix** $I$ has ones on the diagonal and zeros elsewhere, and $AI = IA = A$.
- A matrix is **symmetric** if $A^T = A$. Covariance matrices are symmetric, because the covariance of $X$ with $Y$ equals the covariance of $Y$ with $X$.

Every product here has a dimension check: write the shapes side by side, $(m \times n)(n \times p)$, and confirm the inner numbers match before you compute.

@example
title: A portfolio's expected return as a dot product
problem: A portfolio holds three funds with weights $w = (0.50, 0.30, 0.20)$ and expected returns $\mu = (8\%, 5\%, 12\%)$. Find the expected return and verify that the weights are fully invested.
step: Check the budget constraint: $\mathbf 1^Tw = 0.50 + 0.30 + 0.20 = 1$.
step: Multiply matching entries: $0.50 \times 8 = 4.0$, $0.30 \times 5 = 1.5$, $0.20 \times 12 = 2.4$.
step: Add: $\mu_p = w^T\mu = 4.0 + 1.5 + 2.4 = 7.9\%$.
answer: 7.9% expected return; the weights sum to 1.

@example
title: Matrix multiplication is not commutative
problem: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 5 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$. Compute $AB$ and $BA$.
step: Both are $2 \times 2$, so both products exist and are $2 \times 2$.
step: $AB$: row 1 of $A$ with each column of $B$ gives $1(0) + 2(5) = 10$ and $1(1) + 2(2) = 5$. Row 2 gives $3(0) + 4(5) = 20$ and $3(1) + 4(2) = 11$. So $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 20 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: $BA$: row 1 of $B$ gives $0(1) + 1(3) = 3$ and $0(2) + 1(4) = 4$. Row 2 gives $5(1) + 2(3) = 11$ and $5(2) + 2(4) = 18$. So $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 11 & 18 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: $AB \neq BA$. Order matters, so always say "multiply $A$ on the left of $B$".
answer: $AB = \begin{pmatrix} 10 & 5 \\ 20 & 11 \end{pmatrix}$ and $BA = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 11 & 18 \end{pmatrix}$.

@mistakes
- Multiplying matrices entry by entry instead of row-by-column.
- Multiplying matrices whose inner dimensions do not match, or assuming $AB = BA$.
- Writing $(AB)^T = A^TB^T$; the correct result reverses the order, $B^TA^T$.
- Mixing percents and decimals: weights as decimals with returns in percent gives a percent; keep track of units.

@practice num
prompt: Find the dot product $u \cdot v$ for $u = (2, -1, 4)$ and $v = (3, 5, 1)$.
answer: 5
tol: 0.001
check: sum(a * b for a, b in zip([2, -1, 4], [3, 5, 1]))
hint: Multiply matching entries, then add.
hint: $2 \cdot 3 + (-1)\cdot 5 + 4\cdot 1$.
solution: $6 - 5 + 4 = 5$.

@practice num
prompt: Find the norm $\lVert v\rVert$ of the vector $v = (3, 4, 12)$.
answer: 13
tol: 0.001
check: sqrt(sum(a * a for a in [3, 4, 12]))
hint: The norm is the square root of the dot product of the vector with itself.
hint: $9 + 16 + 144$.
solution: $\sqrt{9 + 16 + 144} = \sqrt{169} = 13$.

@practice num
prompt: A portfolio has weights $w = (0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$ in three assets with expected returns $\mu = (6\%, 9\%, 14\%)$. Find the portfolio's expected return as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 9.05
tol: 0.01
unit: %
check: sum(w * m for w, m in zip([0.40, 0.35, 0.25], [6, 9, 14]))
hint: The expected return is the dot product $w^T\mu$.
hint: $0.40 \times 6 + 0.35 \times 9 + 0.25 \times 14$.
solution: $2.40 + 3.15 + 3.50 = 9.05\%$.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all true statements about matrices.
choice*: If $A$ is $2 \times 3$ and $B$ is $3 \times 4$, then $AB$ is a $2 \times 4$ matrix.
choice*: $(AB)^T = B^TA^T$ whenever the product $AB$ is defined.
choice*: For any square matrix $A$ and the identity matrix $I$ of the same size, $AI = A$.
choice: $AB = BA$ whenever both products are defined.
choice: A $2 \times 3$ matrix times another $2 \times 3$ matrix is always defined.
hint: Check the dimension rule: the number of columns of the left matrix must equal the number of rows of the right matrix.
solution: The $2 \times 3$ by $3 \times 4$ product is defined and is $2 \times 4$; transposing a product reverses the order; and the identity leaves a matrix unchanged. Matrix multiplication is not commutative, and a $2 \times 3$ matrix cannot multiply another $2 \times 3$ matrix because $3 \neq 2$.

@practice tuple
prompt: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 2 & 0 \\ 5 & -2 \end{pmatrix}$. Find the second row of $AB$, written as $(a, b)$.
answer: (2, 6)
check: tuple(sum([1, 3, -1][k] * [[1, 4], [2, 0], [5, -2]][k][j] for k in range(3)) for j in range(2))
hint: $A$ is $2 \times 3$ and $B$ is $3 \times 2$, so $AB$ is $2 \times 2$. The second row of $AB$ uses only the second row of $A$.
hint: Dot the row $(1, 3, -1)$ with each column of $B$.
solution: Column 1 of $B$ is $(1, 2, 5)$, giving $1 + 6 - 5 = 2$. Column 2 is $(4, 0, -2)$, giving $4 + 0 + 2 = 6$. So the row is $(2, 6)$.

@quiz num
prompt: A portfolio has weights $w = (0.50, 0.25, 0.15, 0.10)$. Compute the sum of squared weights $w^Tw$ (a concentration measure).
answer: 0.345
tol: 0.001
check: sum(w * w for w in [0.50, 0.25, 0.15, 0.10])
hint: Square each weight, then add.
hint: $0.25 + 0.0625 + 0.0225 + 0.01$.
solution: $w^Tw = 0.25 + 0.0625 + 0.0225 + 0.01 = 0.345$. Equal weights would give $0.25$.

@quiz tuple
prompt: Let $A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \\ 4 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ and $x = (2, 5)^T$. Compute $Ax$, written as a triple $(a, b, c)$.
answer: (12, 15, 3)
check: tuple(sum(row[k] * [2, 5][k] for k in range(2)) for row in [[1, 2], [0, 3], [4, -1]])
hint: $A$ is $3 \times 2$ and $x$ has 2 entries, so $Ax$ has 3 entries, one for each row of $A$.
hint: Dot each row of $A$ with $(2, 5)$.
solution: Row 1: $2 + 10 = 12$. Row 2: $0 + 15 = 15$. Row 3: $8 - 5 = 3$. So $Ax = (12, 15, 3)$.
