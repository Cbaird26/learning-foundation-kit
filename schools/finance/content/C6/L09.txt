@article
Return on an asset is uncertain, and risk is usually measured by the **variance** $\sigma^2$ of its return, or the **standard deviation** $\sigma$ (the same units as the return). For two assets, the **covariance** $\sigma_{ij} = \text{Cov}(R_i, R_j)$ measures how they move together, and the **correlation** is
$$\rho_{ij} = \frac{\sigma_{ij}}{\sigma_i\sigma_j}, \qquad -1 \le \rho_{ij} \le 1.$$
Equivalently, $\sigma_{ij} = \rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$, and $\sigma_{ii} = \sigma_i^2$.

### The covariance matrix

For $n$ assets, collect all variances and covariances into the $n \times n$ **covariance matrix** $\Sigma$ with entry $\Sigma_{ij} = \sigma_{ij}$. It is symmetric, with variances on the diagonal. For three assets with standard deviations $20\%$, $30\%$, $25\%$ and correlations $\rho_{12} = 0.3$, $\rho_{13} = 0.5$, $\rho_{23} = 0.2$,
$$\Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} 0.0400 & 0.0180 & 0.0250 \\ 0.0180 & 0.0900 & 0.0150 \\ 0.0250 & 0.0150 & 0.0625 \end{pmatrix}.$$
For example, $\sigma_{12} = 0.3 \times 0.20 \times 0.30 = 0.018$.

### Portfolio variance

A portfolio with weights $w$ has return $R_p = \sum_i w_iR_i$. Its variance is the **quadratic form**
$$\sigma_p^2 = w^T\Sigma w = \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n w_iw_j\sigma_{ij}.$$
The standard deviation is $\sigma_p = \sqrt{w^T\Sigma w}$. This formula holds for any weights, including negative ones (short positions). For two assets it expands to the familiar
$$\sigma_p^2 = w_1^2\sigma_1^2 + w_2^2\sigma_2^2 + 2w_1w_2\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2.$$
Compute it in two steps: first the vector $\Sigma w$, then the dot product $w^T(\Sigma w)$.

### Diversification

Because $-1 \le \rho \le 1$, a portfolio with no short positions (all $w_i \ge 0$) has a standard deviation of at most the weighted average of the asset standard deviations, $\sum w_i\sigma_i$, with equality only when all correlations are $+1$. Whenever $\rho < 1$ there is a **diversification benefit**. For $n$ assets with equal weights $1/n$, equal variances $\sigma^2$ and zero covariances, the portfolio variance is $\sigma^2/n$, which falls to zero as $n$ grows. Real assets share market risk, so correlations are positive and the benefit has a floor: with equal correlation $\rho$, the variance tends to $\rho\sigma^2$.

### Risk contributions

Since $\sigma_p^2 = \sum_i w_i(\Sigma w)_i$, the term $w_i(\Sigma w)_i$ is asset $i$'s **contribution to portfolio variance**, and the contributions add up to $\sigma_p^2$. The fraction $\frac{w_i(\Sigma w)_i}{\sigma_p^2}$ is its percentage contribution. An asset with a small weight can contribute a large share if it is volatile and correlated with the rest.

In Python, with plain lists, the double sum is two nested loops:

```python
def portfolio_variance(w, cov):
    n = len(w)
    return sum(w[i] * w[j] * cov[i][j] for i in range(n) for j in range(n))
```

Always check that your covariance matrix uses the same time unit (annual, monthly) as your intended answer.

@example
title: Variance of a three-asset portfolio
problem: Use the covariance matrix above with weights $w = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$. Find the portfolio variance and standard deviation, and compare with the weighted average of the standard deviations.
step: Compute $\Sigma w$ row by row. Row 1: $0.04(0.5) + 0.018(0.3) + 0.025(0.2) = 0.0304$. Row 2: $0.018(0.5) + 0.09(0.3) + 0.015(0.2) = 0.0390$. Row 3: $0.025(0.5) + 0.015(0.3) + 0.0625(0.2) = 0.0295$.
step: Take the dot product with $w$: $0.5(0.0304) + 0.3(0.0390) + 0.2(0.0295) = 0.0152 + 0.0117 + 0.0059 = 0.0328$.
step: The standard deviation is $\sqrt{0.0328} = 18.11\%$.
step: The weighted average of the individual standard deviations is $0.5(20) + 0.3(30) + 0.2(25) = 24\%$. The portfolio is much less risky than the average, because correlations are below 1.
answer: Variance $0.0328$; standard deviation $18.11\%$ versus a weighted average of $24\%$.

@example
title: Two-asset variance and the role of correlation
problem: Two assets have $\sigma_1 = 20\%$ and $\sigma_2 = 30\%$ and are held with weights $0.6$ and $0.4$. Find the portfolio standard deviation when $\rho = 0.25$ and when $\rho = 1$.
step: With $\rho = 0.25$: $\sigma_{12} = 0.25 \times 0.2 \times 0.3 = 0.015$. Then $\sigma_p^2 = 0.36(0.04) + 0.16(0.09) + 2(0.6)(0.4)(0.015) = 0.0144 + 0.0144 + 0.0072 = 0.036$.
step: $\sigma_p = \sqrt{0.036} = 18.97\%$.
step: With $\rho = 1$: $\sigma_{12} = 0.06$ and $\sigma_p^2 = 0.0144 + 0.0144 + 2(0.24)(0.06) = 0.0576$, so $\sigma_p = 24\%$, exactly the weighted average $0.6(20) + 0.4(30)$. Correlation below 1 buys the difference.
answer: $\sigma_p = 18.97\%$ for $\rho = 0.25$ and $24\%$ for $\rho = 1$.

@mistakes
- Omitting the covariance terms, or counting each off-diagonal pair only once instead of twice (the cross term has a factor 2).
- Mixing up variance and standard deviation: weights multiply covariances, not standard deviations, in the quadratic form.
- Using a percent (20) where a decimal (0.20) is needed, so variances come out 10,000 times too large.
- Adding standard deviations to get portfolio risk; only variances combine through $w^T\Sigma w$.

solution: $\sigma_p^2 = n \times \frac{1}{n^2}\sigma^2 = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$. It falls toward zero as $n$ grows, which is the textbook illustration of diversifying away independent risk.

model:
```python
def portfolio_variance(weights, cov):
    """Return w'Sigma w for a covariance matrix given as a list of lists."""
    n = len(weights)
    return sum(weights[i] * weights[j] * cov[i][j]
               for i in range(n) for j in range(n))
```
hint: Every pair of assets $(i, j)$, including $i = j$, contributes $w_iw_j\sigma_{ij}$.
hint: Use two nested loops (or a double comprehension) over the indices and add the terms.
solution: The quadratic form is a double sum over all index pairs. Looping over every ordered pair counts each cross term twice automatically, which supplies the factor $2$ in the two-asset formula. The short-position test checks that negative weights work.
