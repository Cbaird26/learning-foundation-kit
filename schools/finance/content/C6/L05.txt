@article
A **sequence** is an ordered list of numbers $a_1, a_2, a_3, \dots$. A **series** is the sum of the terms of a sequence. Annuities, loans and bonds are all series of cash flows, so the formulas in finance calculators are series sums in disguise.

### Arithmetic and geometric sequences

In an **arithmetic** sequence each term adds a constant difference $d$: $a_n = a_1 + (n-1)d$, as in a savings plan that adds \$100 more each month. In a **geometric** sequence each term multiplies by a constant ratio $r$: $a_n = a_1r^{n-1}$, as in a balance growing at a fixed rate.

### The geometric series

Let $S_n = a + ar + ar^2 + \dots + ar^{n-1}$. Multiply by $r$ to get $rS_n = ar + ar^2 + \dots + ar^n$. Subtracting the two lines cancels all but the first and last terms: $S_n - rS_n = a - ar^n$. Hence, for $r \neq 1$,
$$S_n = a\,\frac{1 - r^n}{1 - r}.$$
If $\lvert r\rvert < 1$, then $r^n \to 0$ as $n \to \infty$ and the series converges:
$$S_\infty = \frac{a}{1 - r}, \qquad \lvert r\rvert < 1.$$
For example, $50 + 40 + 32 + \dots$ has $a = 50$ and $r = 0.8$, so it sums to $\frac{50}{0.2} = 250$. If $\lvert r\rvert \ge 1$ the infinite sum does not exist.

### Present value of an ordinary annuity

An **ordinary annuity** pays a level amount $C$ at the end of each of $n$ periods. With a periodic interest rate $i$, the discount factor for one period is $v = \frac{1}{1+i}$, and the present value is
$$PV = Cv + Cv^2 + \dots + Cv^n.$$
This is a geometric series with first term $Cv$ and ratio $v$. Applying the formula,
$$PV = Cv\,\frac{1 - v^n}{1 - v}.$$
Since $1 - v = \frac{i}{1+i} = iv$, the factor $\frac{v}{1-v}$ simplifies to $\frac1i$, giving the standard result
$$PV = C\,\frac{1 - (1 + i)^{-n}}{i}.$$
The future value is $FV = PV(1+i)^n = C\,\frac{(1+i)^n - 1}{i}$. An **annuity due** pays at the *beginning* of each period, so every payment is one period closer to today: $PV_{\text{due}} = PV_{\text{ordinary}} \times (1 + i)$.

### Perpetuities

Let the payments go on forever. Since $0 < v < 1$ for $i > 0$, the series converges: $PV = \frac{Cv}{1 - v} = \frac{C}{i}$. This is the **perpetuity** formula. If the first payment is $C_1$ one period from now and payments **grow** at rate $g$ per period, the series has first term $\frac{C_1}{1+i}$ and ratio $\frac{1+g}{1+i}$, which is below 1 when $g < i$, so
$$PV = \frac{C_1}{i - g}.$$
This is the growing perpetuity, used in the Gordon growth model for stock valuation. It requires $g < i$; if $g \ge i$ the series diverges and the formula is meaningless.

Always match the rate to the payment frequency: monthly payments need the monthly rate $i$ and the number of months $n$. State whether payments are at the end (ordinary) or start (due) of each period.

@example
title: Present value of a monthly annuity
problem: A supplier agrees to receive \$500 at the end of each month for 36 months. The nominal annual rate is 6%, compounded monthly. Find the present value from the geometric series, then with the closed form.
step: The monthly rate is $i = 0.06/12 = 0.005$, so $v = \frac{1}{1.005} = 0.995025$. The payments form a geometric series with first term $Cv = 497.51$ and ratio $v$, with $n = 36$ terms.
step: $v^{36} = 1.005^{-36} = 0.835645$, so $1 - v^{36} = 0.164355$.
step: $PV = 500\,\frac{1 - 0.835645}{0.005} = 500 \times 32.8710 = \$16{,}435.51$.
step: Check against the series formula: $497.51 \times \frac{1 - 0.835645}{1 - 0.995025} = 497.51 \times 33.0354 =\$16{,}435.5$. The same answer, because the closed form is the series sum.
answer: \$16,435.51

@example
title: A growing perpetuity as a convergent series
problem: Next year's dividend on a share of Bellwether Foods is expected to be \$2.40, growing 4% per year forever. The required return is 9% per year. Find the present value of the dividends.
step: The series is $\frac{2.40}{1.09} + \frac{2.40(1.04)}{1.09^2} + \dots$, with first term $\frac{2.40}{1.09}$ and ratio $\frac{1.04}{1.09} = 0.954128$.
step: The ratio is below 1, so the series converges: $PV = \frac{a}{1 - r} = \frac{2.40/1.09}{1 - 1.04/1.09} = \frac{2.40/1.09}{0.05/1.09} = \frac{2.40}{0.05}$.
step: $PV = \$48.00$, which is the formula $\frac{C_1}{i - g}$.
answer: \$48.00 per share

@mistakes
- Using the annual rate with monthly payments: the rate and the number of periods must both be monthly.
- Miscounting the terms of $S_n$ (there are $n$ of them, ending at $ar^{n-1}$), or mixing up the first term ($a$ versus $Cv$) when discounting.
- Applying $\frac{a}{1-r}$ when $\lvert r\rvert \ge 1$, or $\frac{C_1}{i-g}$ when $g \ge i$.
- Treating an annuity due as an ordinary annuity, which understates its present value by a factor of $(1+i)$.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from the sum of a finite geometric series, derive the formula $PV = C\,\frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i}$ for the present value of an ordinary annuity paying $C$ at the end of each of $n$ periods at periodic rate $i$. State what happens as $n \to \infty$.
hint: Write the present value as a sum of discounted payments, find its first term and ratio, and use the geometric-series formula.
solution: Let $v = 1/(1+i)$. Discounting each payment gives $PV = Cv + Cv^2 + \dots + Cv^n$, a geometric series with first term $Cv$, ratio $v$ and $n$ terms. Its sum is $Cv\frac{1 - v^n}{1 - v}$. Since $1 - v = \frac{i}{1+i} = iv$, we get $\frac{v}{1-v} = \frac1i$, so $PV = C\frac{1 - v^n}{i} = C\frac{1 - (1+i)^{-n}}{i}$. As $n \to \infty$ with $i > 0$, $v^n \to 0$ and $PV \to \frac{C}{i}$, the perpetuity formula.
