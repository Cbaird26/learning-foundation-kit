@article
A **story** is more than a list of events. Readers care about **characters**, the people (or animals or creatures) the story follows, and about what the story *means*. That meaning is called the **theme**.

### Topic versus theme

A **topic** is a word or short phrase: friendship, courage, honesty. A **theme** is a full-sentence message about life that the story suggests: "True friends tell you hard truths." A theme is not the plot ("Maya loses a race") and it is not a moral command ("Always be honest"). It is a statement that could be true beyond this one story.

A quick test: if your answer is one or two words, it is a topic. Turn it into a sentence with a subject and a verb that says something about people or life.

### How authors show character

Authors build characters in four main ways:

- **What the narrator says** about the character (direct characterization).
- **Thoughts and feelings** the character has.
- **Dialogue**: what the character says and how.
- **Actions**: what the character does, especially under pressure.

A **trait** is a quality such as stubborn, generous or anxious. Good readers support a trait with evidence, not a guess.

### Character change

A **dynamic character** changes in an important way, usually because of a **conflict** (a struggle against another person, nature, society or the character's own fears). A **static character** stays basically the same. To trace change, compare the character near the beginning with the character near the end, and find the turning point between them.

### From change to theme

The lesson a character learns, or fails to learn, often points to the theme. Ask: What did the character want? What got in the way? What did the character understand at the end that they did not understand at the start? Then state that understanding as a general sentence.

### Evidence

A theme needs proof. Quote or closely paraphrase a moment from the text, then explain how it supports your statement. Use at least two different moments, one early and one late, when you are showing change.

@example
title: Tracing change and stating a theme
problem: Read: "Dev refused to let anyone touch his telescope. When his sister begged to look, he said, 'You'll only break it.' Weeks later the lens cracked when Dev fell on the hill. His sister found him sitting in the dark. 'Take mine,' she said, holding out her small one. Dev took it and whispered, 'Show me how you set it up.'" Trace how Dev changes and state a theme.
step: Early Dev is possessive and distrustful. Evidence: he "refused to let anyone touch" the telescope and tells his sister, "You'll only break it."
step: The turning point is the cracked lens, which removes the thing he was guarding. He is vulnerable.
step: Late Dev is humble and willing to learn. Evidence: he accepts his sister's lens and asks, "Show me how you set it up."
step: What he learned: sharing and accepting help matters more than protecting what you own. Theme: People grow closer when they stop guarding their pride and accept help from others.
@mistakes
- Writing a topic ("friendship") or a command ("Be kind") instead of a full-sentence theme.
- Retelling the plot as the theme, or choosing a theme with no quotation or event to back it up.
- Naming a trait from a single moment without checking whether the character acts that way elsewhere.

choice: Ana's tomatoes survive the drought.
choice*: Patient, steady effort can succeed where scorn and haste do not.

hint: A theme is a general message about life, not the topic or the plot.
solution: Ana's steady care is rewarded while those who laughed have nothing. The patient-effort sentence is a general message; "Gardening" is a topic, the tomato sentence is plot, and the drought claim is not supported as a general truth.
