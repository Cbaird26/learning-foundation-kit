A **phrase** is a group of words that works together but lacks a subject-verb pair. Examples: *in the park*, *running quickly*, *the tall girl*. A **clause** has both a **subject** and a **predicate** (a verb).

### Independent and dependent clauses

An **independent clause** can stand alone as a sentence: *The bell rang.* A **dependent (subordinate) clause** has a subject and a verb but cannot stand alone because it starts with a subordinating word: *because the bell rang*, *when I arrived*, *who sings well*. Common subordinators: *although, because, if, since, when, while, after, before, unless, who, which, that*.

### Phrases you should know

- **Prepositional phrase**: preposition + noun (*under the table*).
- **Participial phrase**: begins with a verb form acting as an adjective (*Running down the hall, Eli dropped his book*).
- **Appositive phrase**: renames a noun (*My dog, a brown terrier, barks loudly*).

### Four sentence types

| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Simple | one independent clause | The team won. |
| Compound | two or more independent clauses joined by a comma + FANBOYS or a semicolon | The team won, and the crowd cheered. |
| Complex | one independent + at least one dependent clause | Because the team won, the crowd cheered. |
| Compound-complex | two or more independent + at least one dependent | The team won, and the crowd cheered because they were proud. |

FANBOYS are the coordinating conjunctions *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*. A simple sentence can still have a compound subject or predicate (*Mia and Leo sang and danced*) because it has only one clause.

### Common errors

- **Fragment**: missing a subject, a verb, or an independent clause. "Because it rained." Fix: "Because it rained, we stayed in."
- **Run-on (fused sentence)**: two independent clauses with no punctuation. "I like tea she likes coffee."
- **Comma splice**: two independent clauses joined by only a comma. "I like tea, she likes coffee." Fix with a period, a semicolon, or a comma plus a conjunction.

### Sentence variety

Writing with only one type sounds choppy or monotonous. Mix short and long sentences, begin some with a phrase or dependent clause, and combine short sentences with conjunctions or subordinators to show relationships.

@example
title: Classifying and fixing sentences
problem: Classify: "Although the road was icy, Dara walked to school, and she arrived on time." Then fix this comma splice: "The road was icy, Dara walked carefully."
step: Find clauses. "Although the road was icy" is dependent (subordinator although). "Dara walked to school" and "she arrived on time" are independent.
step: Two independent clauses plus one dependent clause make the sentence compound-complex.
step: In the second sentence, two independent clauses are joined by only a comma, so it is a comma splice.
step: Fix it: "The road was icy, so Dara walked carefully" or "The road was icy; Dara walked carefully."
@mistakes
- Treating any long sentence as compound or complex; count clauses, not words.
- Thinking a dependent clause with a period after it is a sentence; it is a fragment.
- Fixing a comma splice by adding another comma instead of a period, a semicolon or a conjunction.
