@article
Epidemics have shaped history by killing, by changing economies and politics, and by forcing new ideas and institutions. Studying them also shows how medical understanding changed.

### Plague

The **Plague of Justinian** (from 541 CE) struck the Mediterranean world, and the Black Death (1347 to 1351) killed an estimated third or more of Europe's population. Contemporaries offered religious, astrological and miasma explanations; the humoral theory of health, inherited from Greek medicine, shaped treatments such as bloodletting. Practical responses developed: the port of Ragusa (now Dubrovnik) in 1377 required arriving ships to wait 30 days (a *trentino*), later extended to 40 days (a *quarantena*, from which **quarantine** comes). Italian cities created health boards and records of deaths.

### Smallpox

Smallpox is caused by the variola virus, spread person to person, with a high case fatality rate. **Variolation** (deliberate inoculation with material from smallpox sores) was practiced in parts of Asia, Africa and the Middle East and reached Europe in the 1700s. In 1796 Jenner demonstrated protection by cowpox inoculation (**vaccination**). A WHO campaign led to eradication, declared in 1980, the only human disease eradicated so far.

### Cholera

Caused by the bacterium *Vibrio cholerae* and spread by contaminated water, cholera caused several pandemics from 1817 on. It produces severe watery diarrhea and dehydration; the key treatment, then and now, is rapid rehydration. The 1854 Broad Street investigation and sanitary engineering showed the importance of clean water. Koch identified the cholera bacterium in 1883 to 1884.

### Germ theory and bacteriology

Pasteur's work in the 1860s on microbes and Koch's methods in the 1870s and 1880s led to **germ theory**: specific microbes cause specific diseases. **Koch's postulates** gave criteria for linking a microbe with a disease. Antisepsis and asepsis reduced infection after surgery. Vaccines and, from the 1940s, antibiotics (penicillin was discovered in 1928) changed treatment.

### Influenza and later epidemics

The 1918 influenza pandemic killed an estimated 17 million to 50 million or more, depending on the study. Public-health tools included isolation, mask wearing and closing public gatherings, applied unevenly. HIV/AIDS, recognized in 1981, led to antiretroviral therapy and showed the importance of community activism and stigma reduction. COVID-19 (declared a pandemic in March 2020) led to rapid vaccine development, and the debates about public-health measures continued.

### Epidemiology basics

- **Case fatality rate (CFR)** = deaths among cases / cases.
- **Basic reproduction number $R_0$**: the average number of people infected by one case in a fully susceptible population.
- **Herd immunity threshold** $= 1 - 1/R_0$: the fraction of the population that must be immune to stop sustained spread.

### How an epidemic changes medicine

A good account of an epidemic names the disease and cause, how people explained it then, how society responded, and what changed afterward in knowledge, institutions or practice. Use both primary sources (reports, letters, registers) and secondary sources (later histories).

@example
title: Herd immunity threshold
problem: A disease has $R_0 = 4$. What fraction of the population must be immune to prevent sustained spread?
step: Use the formula: threshold $= 1 - 1/R_0$.
step: Substitute: $1 - 1/4 = 3/4$.
step: So 75 percent must be immune; with imperfect vaccines the coverage needed is higher.

@mistakes
- Saying that Koch or Pasteur invented medicine; many earlier traditions contributed, and discoveries built on many people's work.
- Treating older explanations (miasma, humors) as foolish; they produced real reforms such as drainage and cleaner streets.
- Treating epidemics as caused only by microbes; crowding, trade, nutrition and policy shape outcomes.
- Using the term case fatality rate for the share of the whole population that died.
