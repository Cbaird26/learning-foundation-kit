@project
title: History of a disease
overview: Trace one epidemic, such as cholera or smallpox, through primary and secondary sources and explain how it changed medicine and society. You will write a short research essay and a one-page source table.
goal: Describe the disease, its cause and how people at the time explained it.
goal: Use at least two primary and two secondary sources and say what each can and cannot tell you.
goal: Explain what changed afterward in medicine, public health, politics or daily life, from more than one perspective.
step: Choose one disease and one place and period (for example cholera in London, 1848 to 1854, or smallpox and the 1796 to 1980 vaccination story). Write one sentence stating your question.
step: Build a source table with four or more rows. For each source write: type (primary or secondary), author, date, audience, and what it can and cannot tell you. Primary examples: a health report, a newspaper notice, a letter, a death register. Secondary examples: a textbook chapter or a history article.
step: Write a timeline of at least six dated events, including the first outbreak, the scientific explanation in use, the key response, and the later change.
step: Describe the explanations of the time (for example miasma or humoral theory) fairly, and then the modern explanation (for example the bacterium or virus and its route of spread).
step: Write a 500 to 800 word essay with a thesis in the first paragraph, evidence in the body, and a paragraph that presents at least two perspectives (for example officials, physicians, patients, colonized or colonizing peoples, poor and wealthy residents).
step: Add a short quantitative paragraph: calculate one number from data in your sources, such as a case fatality rate (deaths divided by cases), a percentage change, or a herd immunity threshold $1 - 1/R_0$.
step: Revise using the rubric, check that every claim has a source and that dates are accurate, and write a two-sentence conclusion that answers your question.
deliverable: A source table with at least four sources (two primary, two secondary).
deliverable: A dated timeline of at least six events.
deliverable: A 500 to 800 word essay with a thesis, evidence, multiple perspectives and a conclusion.
rubric: Question and thesis are clear and the essay answers the question asked.
rubric: Facts, dates and causes are accurate, and uncertain claims are labeled as estimates or debated.
rubric: Primary and secondary sources are used correctly, with their limits and point of view identified.
rubric: Several perspectives are presented fairly, without taking sides on contested political questions.
rubric: The quantitative paragraph uses a correct calculation with units or percentages stated.
safety: This project uses history and published data only; do not include real patients' private information, and do not use the project as medical advice.
tutor: What are the strengths and limits of a death register as a source for an epidemic?
tutor: How would an official, a physician and a poor resident each have described this outbreak?
tutor: How can I tell whether a change in medicine was caused by this epidemic or by other developments?

### Key ideas by week
- **Week 1, early civilizations:** surplus food leads to specialization, cities, writing and government; Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley and Shang China.
- **Week 2, classical empires:** Persia (satraps, Royal Road), Mauryan India, Han China (examination-selected officials) and Rome (provinces, law, army). No year 0 between BCE and CE.
- **Week 3, world religions:** describe beliefs as believers hold them; map origin, route, carriers and date.
- **Week 4, medieval societies:** feudalism and manors, the Islamic world, Song China, Mali, the Mongols and the Black Death.
- **Week 5, exploration and exchange:** the Columbian Exchange of crops, animals, people and diseases; the slave trade; estimates are uncertain.
- **Week 6, revolutions:** the Scientific Revolution, the Enlightenment and the American, French and Haitian revolutions.
- **Week 7, industrialization:** factories, cities, cholera, miasma and sanitary reform.
- **Week 8, imperialism and nationalism:** motives, the Scramble for Africa, responses by colonized peoples.
- **Week 9, World Wars:** MAIN causes, Versailles, the 1918 flu, the Holocaust, Nuremberg Code.
- **Week 10, decolonization and Cold War:** routes to independence, proxy wars, smallpox eradication.
- **Week 11, globalization:** institutions, comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost), rule of 70.
- **Week 12, epidemics:** quarantine, variolation and vaccination, germ theory, CFR and herd immunity $1 - 1/R_0$.

### Self-check
- Can I define a civilization by its features and not rank peoples?
- Can I compare two governments using ruler, officials, law and territory?
- Can I describe a belief without endorsing it?
- Can I separate long-term causes, triggers and consequences?
- Can I name two perspectives on an event?

### Common errors
- Using one cause to explain a complex event.
- Subtracting BCE and CE years without the missing year 0.
- Presenting estimates as exact figures.
- Confusing absolute advantage with comparative advantage.
- Treating old theories as foolish rather than explaining why people held them.

@exam self
prompt: Write an 8 to 10 sentence explanation that links two units of this course to the history of an epidemic: choose one unit from weeks 1 to 6 and one from weeks 7 to 12, and explain how the first helped set the conditions for the second.
solution: The Columbian Exchange (week 5) carried smallpox to the Americas, where populations without immunity suffered severe losses, and it shifted labor and trade in ways that later fed industrial economies. Industrial cities (week 7) crowded workers together with poor water supplies, and cholera outbreaks led to sanitary reform and then to germ theory (week 12). Both cases show that trade and population movement carry disease, and that responses combined local reforms and new scientific knowledge. Different groups experienced the events differently, and sources differ in what they report.
