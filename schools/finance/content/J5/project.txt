@project
title: Heart-rate investigation
overview: You will measure resting pulse and pulse after one minute of jumping jacks for several willing, healthy family members, graph the results and explain the change using what you know about the circulatory, respiratory and muscular systems. You will use data to support a claim, as scientists do.
goal: Collect and organize pulse data carefully, using a repeatable method.
goal: Display the data in a clear graph and calculate the mean and the change for each person.
goal: Explain the change in heart rate by tracing oxygen and food from the air and the digestive system to working muscle cells.
step: Choose 3 to 5 willing, healthy family members. Ask each one for permission, and explain what you will do. If anyone has a health condition or does not want to take part, do not include them. If you have no helpers, use this sample data instead and say so in your report: Ana rests at 72 beats per minute and reaches 120 after exercise, Ben rests at 66 and reaches 108, and Chloe rests at 78 and reaches 126.
step: Learn to find a pulse: place two fingers (not your thumb) on the inside of the wrist below the thumb, press lightly and count beats. Count for 30 seconds and multiply by 2 to get beats per minute. Practice on yourself first.
step: Have each person sit quietly for 3 minutes. Measure their resting pulse twice and write both values; use the average.
step: Have each person do jumping jacks at an easy, comfortable pace for exactly one minute. Measure the pulse immediately afterward (count for 30 seconds, multiply by 2). Anyone who feels dizzy, short of breath or unwell stops right away and rests.
step: Record the data in a table with columns for name (or initials), resting pulse, pulse after exercise and change. Compute the mean resting pulse, the mean pulse after exercise and the mean change.
step: Draw a bar graph with one pair of bars (rest and after exercise) for each person. Label the axes with units (beats per minute), add a title and use a consistent scale.
step: Optionally, measure how long the pulse takes to return to the resting value by counting every minute until it matches the rest value; stop if anyone is tired.
step: Write an explanation of 150 to 250 words that uses your data to describe the change and explains it using muscles, oxygen, the lungs and the heart.
deliverable: A data table with resting and after-exercise pulses, the changes and the means.
deliverable: A neatly labeled graph with a title, axis labels with units and a scale.
deliverable: A written explanation (150 to 250 words) linking your results to the circulatory, respiratory and muscular systems, with one limitation of your investigation.
rubric: Data are complete, with units, repeated resting measurements and correct calculations of means and changes.
rubric: The graph is accurate and clear, with a title, labeled axes with units and an even scale.
rubric: The explanation traces oxygen and sugar to muscle cells, mentions mitochondria and that the heart and breathing rates both rise.
rubric: The report identifies at least one limitation, such as counting errors, different effort or small sample size, and suggests an improvement.
rubric: Claims are supported by the numbers in the data table rather than only by general statements.
safety: Use only light exercise (easy jumping jacks for one minute) for willing, healthy family members, with a grown-up present. Do not include anyone with a heart, lung or joint condition, or anyone who has been told not to exercise. Exercise on a flat, clear floor with enough room. Anyone who feels dizzy, has chest pain, is short of breath or feels unwell stops at once and rests; if symptoms do not pass quickly, tell an adult and get medical help. This project is a science activity and is not medical advice.

@review
### Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Cells and microscopes**: the cell theory; total magnification = eyepiece × objective; actual size = image size ÷ magnification; 1 mm = 1000 µm.
2. **Cell parts**: nucleus, membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, mitochondria, vacuoles; plant cells also have a cell wall and chloroplasts.
3. **Cell division**: surface area to volume limits cell size; interphase, then prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase; mitosis makes identical cells.
4. **Cells to systems**: cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism; four tissue types; homeostasis through negative feedback.
5. **Digestion, breathing, circulation**: absorption in the small intestine; gas exchange in alveoli; cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.
6. **Nervous, skeletal, muscular**: reflex arc; bones protect and support; muscles pull in pairs; oxygen reaches mitochondria through the airway, blood and capillaries.
7. **Reproduction and heredity**: asexual versus sexual; meiosis halves chromosomes; A pairs with T and C with G.
8. **Genes and Punnett squares**: dominant and recessive alleles, genotype versus phenotype, ratios and expected counts.
9. **Evolution**: variation, inheritance, overproduction and differential survival lead to natural selection.
10. **Classification**: eight levels; binomial names; three domains; dichotomous keys with $n - 1$ couplets.
11. **Ecosystems**: producers, consumers, decomposers; arrows show energy flow; about 10% moves up each level.
12. **Human impact**: habitat loss, pollution, invasive species, climate change; percent change; biomagnification; solutions have trade-offs.

### Self-check

- I can calculate total magnification and actual size from a drawing.
- I can compare plant and animal cells and name the job of each organelle.
- I can trace oxygen from the air to a muscle cell in order.
- I can fill in a Punnett square and give the ratio and the probability.
- I can explain natural selection using variation, inheritance and survival.
- I can build an energy pyramid and say what happens to the energy.

### Common errors

- Adding magnifications instead of multiplying them.
- Saying that mitosis halves the chromosome number.
- Mixing up genotype and phenotype, or arteries and veins.
- Saying individuals evolve, or that organisms change because they need to.
- Drawing food-web arrows from predator to prey.
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original in a percent change.

answer: 40

unit: beats per minute

@exam num
prompt: At rest, a person's heart pumps 70 mL of blood per beat at 72 beats per minute. After light exercise the heart pumps 90 mL per beat at 120 beats per minute. By how many liters per minute does the cardiac output increase?
answer: 5.76
check: 90 * 120 / 1000 - 70 * 72 / 1000
unit: L/min
tol: 1%
hint: Cardiac output = stroke volume times heart rate. Compute it for each situation.
hint: Rest is $5.04$ L per minute.
solution: At rest, $70 \times 72 = 5040$ mL, or $5.04$ L per minute. After exercise, $90 \times 120 = 10{,}800$ mL, or $10.8$ L per minute. The increase is $10.8 - 5.04 = 5.76$ L per minute.

@exam self
prompt: Using what you know about the body, explain why your breathing rate and heart rate both rise during exercise. Trace oxygen from the air to a muscle cell, say what the mitochondria do with it, and explain how the nervous system helps control the change. Write at least 6 sentences.
solution: During exercise, muscle cells use more energy, so their mitochondria need more oxygen and sugar to release energy. Carbon dioxide also builds up, and the nervous system detects the change and signals the heart and the breathing muscles to speed up. Faster breathing brings more air through the trachea and bronchi into the alveoli, where oxygen diffuses into capillaries and binds to hemoglobin in red blood cells. The faster heart pumps this oxygen-rich blood through the arteries to the muscles. In muscle capillaries, oxygen diffuses into the muscle cells, and mitochondria use it with sugar to release energy. The blood carries carbon dioxide back to the lungs to be breathed out. This negative feedback keeps the supply matching the demand.
