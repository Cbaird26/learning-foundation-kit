@article
With simple interest, you earn interest only on your original deposit. With **compound interest**, the interest you have already earned is added to the balance, and next year it earns interest too. This is "interest on interest," and over many years it makes a large difference.

### The compound interest formula

In this lesson interest is added **once a year** (annual compounding), and no money is added or removed. With principal $P$, yearly rate $r$ (a decimal) and $t$ years:

$$A = P(1 + r)^t.$$

Each year the balance is multiplied by $1 + r$. At 5%, the multiplier is 1.05. Banks may compound monthly or daily, which gives slightly more; we use yearly compounding to keep the arithmetic simple.

### Compound versus simple

Take \$1,000 at 5%:

| Year | Compound balance | Simple balance |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | \$1,000.00 | \$1,000 |
| 1 | \$1,050.00 | \$1,050 |
| 2 | \$1,102.50 | \$1,100 |
| 3 | \$1,157.63 | \$1,150 |

The two start the same, but compound pulls ahead each year because the interest earned in year 2 also earns interest in year 3. Compound balances are rounded to the cent. The gap grows faster the longer the money stays.

### The rule of 72

How long until money doubles? A quick estimate is the **rule of 72**:

$$\text{years to double} \approx \frac{72}{\text{rate in percent}}.$$

At 6%, money doubles in about $72 \div 6 = 12$ years. At 9%, in about 8 years. It is an estimate, not an exact answer: at 6% the true time is about 11.9 years. It works well for everyday rates such as 4% to 12%, and assumes compounding and no withdrawals.

The rule also works in reverse for a goal: to double in 6 years, you need about $72 \div 6 = 12\%$ a year.

### Why this matters

The same math works against you when you borrow: unpaid interest is added to the debt and then grows. Compounding helps savers and hurts borrowers who do not pay.

@example
title: Three years of compounding
problem: You deposit \$1,000 at 5% interest compounded once a year. What is the balance after 3 years, and how much more is that than with simple interest?
step: Use $A = P(1 + r)^t$ with $P = 1000$, $r = 0.05$, $t = 3$: $A = 1000 \times 1.05^3$.
step: $1.05^3 = 1.157625$, so $A = \$1{,}157.625$, which rounds to \$1,157.63.
step: Simple interest would give $1000 + 1000 \times 0.05 \times 3 = \$1{,}150$.
step: The difference is $\$1{,}157.63 - \$1{,}150 = \$7.63$.
answer: \$1,157.63, which is \$7.63 more than simple interest.

@example
title: Doubling time
problem: About how long does it take money to double at 8% a year, compounded annually?
step: Apply the rule of 72: $72 \div 8 = 9$.
step: Check: $1.08^9 = 1.999$, so after 9 years \$1 has grown to almost \$2.
answer: About 9 years.

@mistakes
- Multiplying the rate by the years (a simple interest idea) when the question says compound.
- Using $r = 5$ instead of $r = 0.05$ in the formula; the multiplier should be 1.05, not 6.
- Using the rule of 72 with the rate written as a decimal (72 divided by 0.06) instead of 6.
- Treating the rule of 72 as exact; it is a good estimate, not an answer to the cent.
