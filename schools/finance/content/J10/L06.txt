@article
**Saving** means setting money aside in a place where it is safe and easy to get back, such as a savings account. The balance does not drop, and deposits at insured institutions are protected. The reward is modest interest.

**Investing** means buying something that you hope will grow in value or pay you income. Examples are **stocks** (small ownership shares in companies), **bonds** (loans to companies or governments that pay interest) and **funds** (baskets that hold many stocks or bonds). Investments have historically grown faster than savings accounts over long periods, but they are **not guaranteed**. Their value goes up and down, and you can lose money. Investments are not insured by the FDIC.

### Risk and return

**Risk** is the chance that the result differs from what you expected, including losing money. **Return** is the gain on your money. In general, the higher the possible return, the higher the risk. **Diversification**, spreading money across many investments, reduces the harm from any one of them doing badly. Money you will need soon (within a few years) usually belongs in savings. Money for the far future can be invested, because it has time to recover from bad years.

This is general education, not investment advice.

### Time does the heavy lifting

With compounding, $A = P(1 + r)^t$. Because $t$ is an exponent, each extra year multiplies the balance again. The calculations below assume a **constant** yearly rate with annual compounding. Real investments do not grow in a straight line, so treat these numbers as illustrations.

Here is what \$400 becomes at 5% a year:

| Years | Balance |
|---|---|
| 1 | \$420.00 |
| 5 | \$510.51 |
| 10 | \$651.56 |

Now compare starting early and late with \$1,000 and an assumed 7% yearly rate:

| Years invested | Balance |
|---|---|
| 10 | \$1,967.15 |
| 30 | \$7,612.26 |
| 40 | \$14,974.46 |

Ten extra years, 30 to 40, nearly doubled the result, while the deposit stayed the same. That is why financial teachers say: start early, even with small amounts.

@example
title: Balance after 1, 5 and 10 years
problem: You deposit \$400 in an account paying 5% a year, compounded once a year, and add nothing more. Find the balance after 1, 5 and 10 years.
step: After 1 year: $400 \times 1.05 = \$420.00$.
step: After 5 years: $400 \times 1.05^5 = 400 \times 1.276282 = \$510.51$.
step: After 10 years: $400 \times 1.05^{10} = 400 \times 1.628895 = \$651.56$.
step: The money grew \$20 in the first year but \$141.05 between years 5 and 10, because later years earn interest on a bigger balance.
answer: \$420.00, \$510.51 and \$651.56.

@example
title: Starting early
problem: Two cousins each put \$1,000 into an investment that is assumed to grow 7% a year, compounded annually. Ana invests for 40 years and Ben for 30 years. How much more does Ana have?
step: Ana: $1000 \times 1.07^{40} = 1000 \times 14.974458 = \$14{,}974.46$.
step: Ben: $1000 \times 1.07^{30} = 1000 \times 7.612255 = \$7{,}612.26$.
step: Difference: $\$14{,}974.46 - \$7{,}612.26 = \$7{,}362.20$.
answer: Ana has \$7,362.20 more, under the assumed constant 7% rate.

@mistakes
- Believing that investments grow every year at a steady rate; the 7% here is an assumption, and real returns vary.
- Treating investments as insured against losses the way an insured savings deposit is protected.
- Thinking a small deposit is not worth making; with enough years it can grow a lot.
- Investing money you will need soon, where a bad year could leave you short.
