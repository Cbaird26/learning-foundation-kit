@article
A **debit card** takes money straight out of your checking account. You can only spend what you have. A **credit card** lets you **borrow** money from the card company to pay a store. Later you get a bill, the **statement**, and you must pay it back.

### The grace period and interest

If you pay the **full statement balance** by the due date, you usually pay no interest on your purchases. Otherwise the unpaid balance is charged interest at the card's **APR**, the annual percentage rate. Credit card APRs are often much higher than savings rates, so interest can pile up quickly. To keep the math simple, we find the monthly interest by dividing the APR by 12 (many cards actually compute it daily, which gives a similar result):

$$\text{monthly interest} = \text{balance} \times \frac{\text{APR}}{12}.$$

A card also has a **credit limit**, the most you can borrow, and a **minimum payment**, the smallest amount you must pay to stay in good standing. Paying only the minimum is costly, as the next lesson shows. In the United States, people under 21 generally need an adult co-signer or their own income to open a credit card.

### Credit utilization

**Credit utilization** is the share of your credit limit that you are using:

$$\text{utilization} = \frac{\text{balance}}{\text{credit limit}} \times 100\%.$$

Lower utilization is better for your score.

### Credit scores

A **credit score** is a number that estimates how likely you are to repay debts. A common one is the **FICO score**, which ranges from 300 to 850. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend to you and at what interest rate; landlords and some employers may also check your credit report. FICO says its score is built mainly from these parts, which are approximate:

| Factor | Approximate weight |
|---|---|
| Payment history (paying on time) | 35% |
| Amounts owed (including utilization) | 30% |
| Length of credit history | 15% |
| New credit (recent applications) | 10% |
| Credit mix | 10% |

Your income is not part of your credit score. A **credit report** lists your accounts and payment record. You can get free reports from all three credit bureaus every week at AnnualCreditReport.com; check them for mistakes.

This is general education, not financial advice.

@example
title: One month of card interest
problem: A card has an APR of 24%. You charge \$300, pay nothing by the due date and carry the balance. About how much interest is charged for the month?
step: Monthly rate: $24\% \div 12 = 2\%$ per month.
step: Interest: $\$300 \times 0.02 = \$6.00$.
step: Your new balance is about \$306 before any new purchases or payments.
answer: About \$6.00.

@example
title: Credit utilization
problem: Your card has a limit of \$1,500 and you owe \$450. What is your credit utilization?
step: Divide the balance by the limit: $450 \div 1500 = 0.30$.
step: As a percent: 30%.
answer: 30%.

@mistakes
- Thinking a credit card is "free money"; every purchase is borrowed and must be repaid.
- Using the APR as the monthly rate (24% a month instead of 2%).
- Paying only part of the statement balance and assuming no interest is charged.
- Believing that income, or the balance in your savings account, is part of the credit score.
