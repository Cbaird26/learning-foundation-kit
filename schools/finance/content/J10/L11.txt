@article
Scammers want your money or your personal information, and they target all ages. Teens are targeted with fake giveaways, game-currency offers, and messages that look like they are from friends. Knowing the patterns protects you.

### Common scams

- **Phishing** uses email, text or social media messages that pretend to be from a bank, store or friend, to trick you into clicking a link or sharing a password.
- **Imposter scams**: someone claims to be from a bank, a government agency or tech support and says you are in trouble.
- **Prize and "too good to be true" offers**: you "won" something but must pay a fee first, or you can "double your money" quickly.
- **Fake check scams**: someone sends a check, asks you to send back part of it, and the check turns out to be fake.

### Red flags

1. **Urgency**: "Act now or lose your account."
2. **Secrecy**: "Do not tell your parents."
3. **Unusual payment**: demands for gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency, which are hard to undo.
4. **A request for passwords, PINs or one-time codes.** A real bank will not ask for them.
5. **A link or phone number you did not look up yourself.**

### Protecting yourself

- Use a **different strong password** for each account, and turn on **multi-factor authentication** (a second proof, such as a code) where it is offered.
- Never share a PIN or one-time code.
- When a message worries you, go to the real website or call the number on your card, not the one in the message.
- Check statements often for charges you do not recognize.
- Talk to a trusted adult before you pay or send money to anyone online.

Passwords are stronger when there are more possible combinations. A 4-digit PIN has $10^4 = 10{,}000$ possibilities; a 6-digit PIN has $10^6 = 1{,}000{,}000$, which is 100 times more.

### Identity theft

**Identity theft** is when someone uses your personal information (name, Social Security number, account numbers) to open accounts or buy things. If it happens, contact the affected bank or card company, report it to the Federal Trade Commission, and consider placing a **credit freeze** with the credit bureaus, which blocks new accounts being opened and is free by law. Review your credit reports for accounts you do not recognize.

### Who pays for fraud?

Federal law limits your loss from an unauthorized **credit card** charge to \$50, if you report it promptly. For **debit cards**, the limit is \$50 only if you report within 2 business days of learning of the loss or theft; it can be higher if you wait. Many card companies offer extra "zero liability" policies. Reporting quickly always matters.

This is general education, not legal advice.

@example
title: Spotting unauthorized charges
problem: Your statement shows three charges you do not recognize: \$12.99, \$24.50 and \$8.75. What is the total, and what should you do?
step: Add them: $\$12.99 + \$24.50 + \$8.75 = \$46.24$.
step: Contact the card company right away to report the charges as unauthorized and ask for the card to be replaced.
step: Keep a record of the date and what was said, and watch later statements for more charges.
answer: \$46.24 in unauthorized charges; report them immediately.

@example
title: Counting password possibilities
problem: How many different passwords can be made from 4 lowercase letters, and how many from 6 lowercase letters?
step: Each position can be any of 26 letters, so 4 letters give $26^4 = 456{,}976$ passwords.
step: Six letters give $26^6 = 308{,}915{,}776$ passwords.
step: The 6-letter space is $26^2 = 676$ times as large, so adding length helps a lot.
answer: 456,976 for 4 letters; 308,915,776 for 6 letters.

@mistakes
- Clicking a link in an urgent message instead of going to the official website yourself.
- Using one password everywhere, so one leak opens every account.
- Paying a fee or "tax" up front to receive a prize; real prizes do not require it.
- Waiting to report a suspicious charge, which can raise how much you are responsible for.
