@article
Keeping all your money as cash in a drawer is risky: it can be lost, stolen or burned, and it earns nothing. A **financial institution** such as a bank or a credit union keeps your money safe and lets you spend and save it.

### Checking and savings

A **checking account** is for spending. You can deposit money, pay with a debit card, write checks, pay bills and withdraw cash. It usually pays little or no interest. A **savings account** is for money you do not plan to spend soon. It pays **interest**, a payment from the bank for letting it hold your money, and it often limits how freely you can move money out.

Banks quote savings interest as an **APY** (annual percentage yield): the percent your balance grows in a year, including interest on interest. Rates change over time, so look up current offers instead of trusting old numbers.

In the United States, people under 18 usually open an account together with an adult (a joint or custodial account).

### Banks and credit unions

A **bank** is a business owned by investors. A **credit union** is a not-for-profit owned by its members, who usually share a common bond such as an employer, a school or a neighborhood. Credit unions often charge lower fees, but both kinds offer checking and savings.

### Is my money safe?

Deposits at a bank are insured by the **FDIC** (Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation), and deposits at a federal credit union by the **NCUA** (National Credit Union Administration). Coverage is \$250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category. If the institution fails, you are paid back up to that limit. Always check that an institution is insured.

### Fees

Fees can eat your balance. Common ones are a **monthly maintenance fee** (sometimes waived if you keep a minimum balance), an **overdraft fee** when you spend more than you have, and an **ATM fee** for using another institution's machine. Compare fees as well as interest.

### Keeping track

A **check register** is a running record of every deposit and withdrawal. Each line changes the balance: deposits add, payments and withdrawals subtract. Checking the register against your bank's statement catches mistakes and fraud early.

@example
title: A check register
problem: Your balance is \$120. You deposit \$45 from babysitting, pay \$18.50 with your debit card and withdraw \$20 in cash. What is your balance?
step: After the deposit: $\$120 + \$45 = \$165$.
step: After the debit card payment: $\$165 - \$18.50 = \$146.50$.
step: After the cash withdrawal: $\$146.50 - \$20 = \$126.50$.
answer: \$126.50.

@example
title: What a fee costs
problem: Account A has no monthly fee. Account B charges \$4 a month but waives the fee if the balance is at least \$300. You keep about \$150 in the account all year. How much does Account B's fee cost in a year?
step: Your balance is below \$300, so the fee is not waived.
step: Twelve months of fees: $12 \times \$4 = \$48$.
step: Account A costs nothing, so unless Account B pays more than \$48 in interest, Account A is the better deal for this balance.
answer: \$48 a year.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to subtract a debit card purchase in the register, so the balance looks bigger than it is.
- Thinking FDIC or NCUA insurance protects you from spending too much; it only protects deposits if the institution fails.
- Choosing an account by its interest rate alone and ignoring monthly fees that cost more than the interest earned.
- Assuming a credit union is not safe because it is not a bank; federally insured credit unions have NCUA coverage.

solution: FDIC insurance protects deposits up to the limit if an insured bank fails. It does not guarantee any interest rate and does not cover your own spending decisions or depend on having a loan.
