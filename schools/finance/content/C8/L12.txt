@article
A financial crisis is a sudden, widespread breakdown in the system's ability to move money from savers to borrowers. **Systemic risk** is the risk that distress at one institution or market spreads and disrupts the whole system. Understanding the mechanisms explains much of the regulation in weeks 7 to 11.

### Mechanisms

- **Runs and liquidity.** If depositors or lenders expect others to withdraw, each has a reason to withdraw first, even if the institution is solvent. The Diamond-Dybvig model shows two equilibria: all stay and the bank survives, or all run and it fails. A **solvent** institution (assets exceed liabilities) can fail from **illiquidity** if it cannot turn assets into cash quickly without large losses.
- **Leverage.** With assets $A$ and equity $E$, a fall of $x$ in asset values reduces equity by $xA$; equity is exhausted when $x = E/A$. Leverage of $A/E = 25$ means a 4% asset fall wipes out equity.
- **Fire sales.** Distressed sellers dump assets, pushing prices down, which hurts other holders of the same assets.
- **Margin and haircut spirals.** When asset values fall or risk rises, lenders raise **haircuts** on repo, forcing borrowers to raise cash by selling assets, which lowers prices further.
- **Interconnection.** Counterparty exposures (loans, derivatives) and common exposures spread losses; **information contagion** makes lenders doubt similar institutions.
- **Securitization.** In an **originate-to-distribute** model, lenders sell loans packaged into tranches. If a pool suffers losses, the **equity tranche** is hit first, then **mezzanine**, then **senior**. Complexity and weak underwriting hid the risk.

### The Great Depression and the 1930s reforms

After the 1929 stock market crash, waves of bank failures between 1930 and 1933 destroyed deposits and credit. The response: the Banking Act of 1933 (the Glass-Steagall provisions and creation of the FDIC), the Securities Act of 1933 and the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 (which created the SEC).

### 2007–2009

Rising house prices, loose mortgage underwriting and securitization of subprime loans, financed with short-term repo and commercial paper, left institutions highly leveraged. When house prices fell, mortgage securities lost value and funding dried up: Bear Stearns was sold in March 2008, and Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy on September 15, 2008. A money market fund that held Lehman paper fell below a \$1 NAV the next day, and runs on money funds followed; governments and central banks responded with guarantees, emergency lending and capital injections.

Reforms: the **Dodd-Frank Act** (2010) created the Financial Stability Oversight Council to monitor systemic risk, an orderly liquidation authority for failing large firms, the Volcker Rule, central clearing of standardized swaps, stress testing and the CFPB; **Basel III** raised capital requirements and added the LCR and NSFR.

### 2023

Silicon Valley Bank failed on March 10, 2023, followed by Signature Bank. SVB had a large share of uninsured deposits from a concentrated client base and had invested heavily in long-term securities that lost value as rates rose. News of losses triggered rapid withdrawals, helped by digital banking. Authorities protected all depositors using a systemic risk exception and the Federal Reserve set up a temporary lending facility for banks. Debate continues about capital and liquidity rules for mid-sized banks, so check the current state.

@example
title: Leverage and the haircut spiral
problem: (a) A bank has assets of \$100 and equity of \$4. By how much does equity fall if assets lose 3%, and when is equity exhausted? (b) A dealer funds \$500 million of bonds with repo at a 3% haircut. If the haircut rises to 12%, how much new funding must it find?
step: (a) Equity falls by $0.03 \times 100 = \$3$, from \$4 to \$1, a 75% fall. Equity is exhausted at a 4% asset loss ($E/A = 4/100$).
step: (b) Initial cash borrowed $= 500 \times 0.97 = \$485$ million; after $= 500 \times 0.88 = \$440$ million.
step: The dealer must replace $485 - 440 = \$45$ million of funding (equal to $500 \times 0.09$), usually by selling assets, which pushes prices down for everyone.
answer: (a) Equity falls 75%; a 4% loss wipes it out. (b) \$45 million.

@example
title: A run on a solvent bank
problem: A bank has cash \$10, Treasuries \$20 (sold in a hurry at 98), loans \$70 (sold in a hurry at 85) and deposits \$90, so equity is \$10. A run withdraws \$35. What does the bank sell and what happens to equity?
step: Cash \$10 plus Treasuries sold for $20 \times 0.98 = \$19.60$ gives \$29.60, leaving $35 - 29.60 = \$5.40$ to raise.
step: Loans must be sold at 85 cents: face value sold $= 5.40/0.85 = \$6.35$.
step: Losses: \$0.40 on the Treasuries and $6.35 \times 0.15 = \$0.95$ on the loans, a total of \$1.35, so equity falls from \$10 to about \$8.65.
step: The bank survives this run but lost 13.5% of its equity to fire-sale discounts, and a larger run would exhaust it.
answer: It sells all liquid assets and some loans; equity falls to about \$8.65.

@mistakes
- Equating illiquidity with insolvency: a bank with assets above liabilities can still fail if it cannot raise cash in time.
- Judging leverage by the asset losses it takes to wipe out equity in percentage of equity rather than of assets (the threshold is $E/A$).
- Attributing the FDIC to the 2008 crisis; it dates from 1933, and the 2010 reforms are those of Dodd-Frank.
- Treating tranches as equally risky: losses hit the equity tranche first.

What percent of the mezzanine tranche's principal is lost? Give it to two decimal places.
answer: 33.33
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (12 - 8) / (20 - 8) * 100
hint: The equity tranche is wiped out first.
hint: Losses to the mezzanine tranche are $12\% - 8\% = 4\%$ of the pool.
solution: The mezzanine tranche loses $4\%$ of the pool out of its width of $12\%$, or $4/12 = 33.33\%$ of its principal. Senior tranches are untouched.
