@article
Lend for one year or for ten? The price you require differs, and the **term structure of interest rates** describes how it differs with maturity. The graph of yield against maturity, for securities of similar credit quality such as US Treasuries, is the **yield curve**. Its shape is one of the most-watched summaries of what markets expect and fear. Conventions in this lesson: rates are annual effective rates (annual compounding), and cash flows are at the end of each year.

### Spot rates and forward rates

The **spot rate** $s_n$ is the yield on a zero-coupon bond maturing in $n$ years: \$1 paid at year $n$ is worth $1/(1+s_n)^n$ today. A **forward rate** $f_{a,b}$ is the annual rate, agreed today, for lending between year $a$ and year $b$. Investing to year $b$ at the spot rate must equal investing to year $a$ and then at the forward rate (no-arbitrage):

$$(1 + s_b)^b = (1 + s_a)^a\,(1 + f_{a,b})^{\,b-a} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f_{a,b} = \left[\frac{(1+s_b)^b}{(1+s_a)^a}\right]^{1/(b-a)} - 1.$$

When the spot curve slopes up, forward rates lie above spot rates; when it slopes down, below.

### Bootstrapping

A **par yield** is the coupon rate that makes a bond price equal 100. Given par yields, you solve for spot rates one maturity at a time. For a two-year annual-pay bond with coupon $c$, $100 = \dfrac{c}{1+s_1} + \dfrac{100 + c}{(1+s_2)^2}$, so once $s_1$ is known, $s_2$ follows.

### Theories of the term structure

- **Pure expectations theory.** Forward rates equal expected future spot rates, so a long yield is an average of expected short rates. An upward curve means short rates are expected to rise; a downward curve, to fall.
- **Liquidity preference (term premium) theory.** Longer bonds have more price risk, so investors demand a premium that grows with maturity: forward rate = expected future spot rate + premium. The curve tends to slope upward even if short rates are expected to stay flat.
- **Market segmentation and preferred habitat.** Investors and borrowers have maturity preferences (a pension fund matching long liabilities), so supply and demand within each segment shape yields; investors move across maturities only if compensated.

Modern models also include an **inflation risk premium** and the effect of central bank policy, which anchors the short end.

### Three shapes

| Shape | Description | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| Normal (upward) | Long yields above short yields | Expected growth and rising rates; a positive term premium; inflation uncertainty |
| Inverted | Short yields above long yields | A restrictive policy rate with expected future easing; weak growth or falling inflation expected; strong demand for long bonds |
| Flat | Similar yields at all maturities | A transition between the two; expected short rates roughly constant and a small premium |

Historically inversions have often preceded recessions, but this is an empirical pattern, not a law, and the curve's shape today is data you can look up (Treasury or FRED), not something to memorize.

@example
title: From spot rates to forward rates
problem: One-year and two-year spot rates are 3.0% and 3.5%; the three-year spot rate is 3.9%. Find the one-year forward rates starting in one year and starting in two years.
step: First forward rate: $(1.035)^2 = (1.03)(1 + f_{1,2})$, so $1 + f_{1,2} = 1.071225/1.03 = 1.040024$ and $f_{1,2} = 4.0024\%$.
step: Second forward rate: $(1.039)^3 = (1.035)^2(1 + f_{2,3})$, so $1 + f_{2,3} = 1.121622/1.071225 = 1.047046$ and $f_{2,3} = 4.7046\%$.
step: The curve slopes up, so the forward rates (4.00% and 4.70%) exceed the spot rates. Under pure expectations, the market expects the one-year rate to be about 4.0% next year and 4.7% the year after.
answer: $f_{1,2} \approx 4.00\%$ and $f_{2,3} \approx 4.70\%$.

@example
title: Bootstrapping a spot rate
problem: Annual-pay par bonds yield 3.0% (one year) and 3.5% (two years). Find the two-year spot rate.
step: The one-year par bond pays 103 at year 1 at price 100, so $s_1 = 3.0\%$.
step: Two-year bond: $100 = 3.5/1.03 + 103.5/(1 + s_2)^2$. Since $3.5/1.03 = 3.3981$, the final payment has present value $96.6019$.
step: $(1 + s_2)^2 = 103.5/96.6019 = 1.071441$, so $s_2 = 3.5088\%$, slightly above the 3.5% par yield because the first coupon is discounted at the lower one-year rate.
answer: $s_2 \approx 3.51\%$.

@mistakes
- Averaging spot rates to get a forward rate; forward rates come from ratios of growth factors.
- Raising to the wrong power: the root is $1/(b-a)$, not $1/b$.
- Calling the par yield and the spot rate the same thing; they differ when the curve is not flat.
- Treating the yield curve as a forecast: under the liquidity preference theory, forward rates include a premium and overstate expected rates.

model:
```python
def forward_rate(spot_a, a, spot_b, b):
    """Annual forward rate between years a and b from annual spot rates."""
    growth = (1 + spot_b) ** b / (1 + spot_a) ** a
    return growth ** (1 / (b - a)) - 1
```
hint: Compute the growth factor from year `a` to year `b` as a ratio of the two spot growth factors.
hint: Take the root $1/(b - a)$ of that ratio, then subtract 1.
solution: The ratio $(1+s_b)^b/(1+s_a)^a$ is the total growth over the forward period; the `(b - a)`-th root converts it into an annual factor. The last test, a three-year forward period, catches solutions that forget the root.

solution: A normal curve slopes upward: long yields exceed short yields because the market expects short rates to rise as growth and inflation pick up, and because investors demand a term premium for the greater price risk of long bonds. A flat curve has similar yields at all maturities, often during a transition between the other two shapes, when expected short rates are about constant and the premium is small. Liquidity preference (the term premium theory) best explains why the curve usually slopes upward, since long bonds carry more price risk and investors want compensation even when short rates are expected to stay flat.
