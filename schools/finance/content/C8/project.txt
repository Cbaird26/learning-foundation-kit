### Week by week

1. **The financial system.** Functions: moving resources over time, pooling, risk sharing, price discovery, payments, incentive problems. Direct and indirect finance; primary and secondary markets. Fisher: $1+i = (1+r)(1+\pi)$.
2. **Interest rates and the term structure.** Spot and forward rates, $f_{a,b} = [(1+s_b)^b/(1+s_a)^a]^{1/(b-a)} - 1$; expectations, liquidity preference, segmentation; normal, inverted and flat curves.
3. **Money markets.** Bills and CP: $P = F(1 - dt/360)$; BEY $= (F-P)/P \times 365/t$; repos: cash $= V(1-h)$, interest on actual/360; SOFR.
4. **Bond markets.** Segments; 32nds quotes; accrued interest (30/360 corporates, actual/actual Treasuries); invoice price $=$ clean $+$ accrued; muni tax-equivalent yield $y/(1-t)$.
5. **Stock markets.** Orders, price-time priority, the book, NBBO; margin: call price $L/(N(1-m))$.
6. **Clearing and settlement.** CCP, novation, netting, DVP, T+1; replacement cost.
7. **Banks.** Balance sheet; NIM, ROA, ROE (DuPont); repricing and duration gaps.
8. **Regulation.** RWA, CET1 4.5%, Tier 1 6%, total 8%, buffer 2.5%, leverage 3%; LCR and NSFR; FDIC \$250,000 per depositor, bank and category.
9. **Investment banks.** Underwriting, spread, greenshoe, Glass-Steagall, Volcker Rule.
10. **Funds.** NAV; ETF creation and redemption; fee drag; two-and-twenty; private equity carry.
11. **Insurance and pensions.** Pure and gross premium, combined ratio; DB versus DC; funded ratio.
12. **Crises.** Runs, leverage, fire sales, haircut spirals; 1933, 2008, 2023 and reforms.

### Most common errors

- Using a discount yield as a rate of return; mixing 360 and 365 days.
- Confusing clean and dirty prices.
- Treating execution as settlement; counting calendar days for T+1.
- Forgetting leverage when comparing bank ROE; using total assets instead of RWA.
- Applying the FDIC limit per account rather than per depositor, bank and category.
- Confusing adverse selection (before the contract) with moral hazard (after it).
- Calling illiquid banks insolvent, or the reverse.

prompt: A bank has assets of \$2,000 million (duration 5.0 years), liabilities of \$1,840 million (duration 1.0 year) and equity of \$160 million. Yields are 4%, and all yields rise by 1 percentage point. Using duration approximations separately for assets and liabilities, assets fall by $D_A A \Delta y/(1+y)$ and liabilities by $D_L L \Delta y/(1+y)$. What is the bank's equity-to-assets ratio after the move? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 4.28
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (160 - (5.0 * 2000 * 0.01 / 1.04 - 1.0 * 1840 * 0.01 / 1.04)) / (2000 - 5.0 * 2000 * 0.01 / 1.04) * 100
hint: Compute the fall in the value of assets and in the value of liabilities separately.
hint: Assets fall by \$96.15 million and liabilities by \$17.69 million.
solution: Equity falls by $96.15 - 17.69 = \$78.46$ million, from \$160 to \$81.54 million. Assets are $2{,}000 - 96.15 = \$1{,}903.85$ million, so the ratio is $81.54/1{,}903.85 = 4.28\%$, down from 8.0%. A 1-point rate rise nearly halved the bank's equity cushion.

prompt: A dealer buys \$10,000,000 face value of 91-day Treasury bills at a discount yield of 4.00% (360-day basis). It finances the purchase in a 91-day term repo with the bills as collateral, borrowing 98% of the purchase price at a repo rate of 3.90% on an actual/360 basis. At maturity it receives the face value and repays the repo. What is its profit on the position, in dollars to the nearest dollar? Ignore all other costs.
answer: 5476
lead: \$
tol: 1
check: 10000000 - 10000000 * (1 - 0.04 * 91 / 360) - 0.98 * 10000000 * (1 - 0.04 * 91 / 360) * 0.039 * 91 / 360
hint: Find the purchase price from the discount yield and the cash borrowed in the repo.
hint: Profit equals the bill's discount income minus the repo interest.
solution: Purchase price $= 10{,}000{,}000 \times (1 - 0.04 \times 91/360) = \$9{,}898{,}888.89$, so the discount income is \$101,111.11. The repo borrows $0.98 \times 9{,}898{,}888.89 = \$9{,}700{,}911.11$ and costs $9{,}700{,}911.11 \times 0.039 \times 91/360 = \$95{,}634.82$ of interest. Profit $= 101{,}111.11 - 95{,}634.82 = \$5{,}476$ on the dealer's own investment of about \$197,978.

@exam self
prompt: Redwood Savings Bank (hypothetical) has total assets of \$12 billion, equity of \$720 million, \$5 billion of 10-year fixed-rate government securities with a duration of 7 years, and deposits of which 60% are uninsured. Market yields are 4%, and then rise by 2 percentage points. In a memo of 6 to 8 sentences, estimate the loss in market value of the securities, say what it means for the bank's capital, explain how a run could start and spread, and name two policies or rules (from the course) that address these risks.
hint: Use $\Delta V \approx -D\,V\,\Delta y/(1+y)$, then compare the loss with equity.
solution: The securities lose about $7 \times 5{,}000 \times 0.02/1.04 = \$673$ million in market value, nearly all of the \$720 million of equity (93%), so economically the bank is close to insolvent even if the losses are unrealized and its reported regulatory capital looks healthy. Because 60% of deposits are uninsured, those depositors have no protection beyond the \$250,000 limit and a reason to leave at the first sign of trouble; once some withdraw, the bank must sell securities at a loss, which confirms the fear and spreads it to similar banks by information contagion. Relevant tools are the liquidity coverage ratio and capital requirements (so the bank holds liquid assets and loss-absorbing equity), supervision and stress tests that include interest rate scenarios, FDIC insurance (which stops runs by insured depositors) and the central bank's role as lender of last resort against collateral.
rubric: Computes the securities loss correctly at about \$673 million and compares it with equity of \$720 million.
rubric: Explains the run mechanism: uninsured depositors, forced sales at a loss and contagion to similar banks.
rubric: Names two valid policies or rules (for example LCR, capital rules, stress tests, deposit insurance, lender of last resort) and says what each does.
