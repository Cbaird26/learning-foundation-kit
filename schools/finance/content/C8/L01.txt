@article
A household with spare savings and a firm with a profitable project it cannot fund out of its own cash are made for each other, yet they rarely meet. The **financial system** is the set of markets, institutions, instruments and rules that connects them, and it is worth studying because it decides which projects get funded, at what price, and who bears the risk.

### What the system does

- **Moves resources across time and place.** Savers lend today; borrowers invest today and repay from future income.
- **Pools funds and divides them.** A large factory needs more money than any one saver has; a saver wants to hold small pieces of many projects. Bonds, shares and fund units solve both problems.
- **Manages risk.** Diversification, insurance and derivatives let risks move to those best able to bear them.
- **Produces information and prices.** Market prices summarize what many participants know, and they guide the allocation of capital.
- **Processes payments** and settles trades safely.
- **Addresses incentive problems.** Lenders know less about borrowers than borrowers do.

### Direct and indirect finance

In **direct finance** borrowers sell securities to savers in financial markets: a firm issues a bond to investors. In **indirect finance** a **financial intermediary** stands between the two: a bank takes deposits and makes loans, an insurer collects premiums and holds a portfolio. Intermediaries exist because they cut **transaction costs** (search, contracting, monitoring), pool risks and specialize in information.

### Classifying markets

| Distinction | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Primary vs secondary | Primary: the issuer sells new securities and receives the proceeds. Secondary: investors trade existing securities among themselves; the issuer receives nothing. |
| Money vs capital | Money markets trade debt maturing in a year or less. Capital markets trade longer-term debt and equity. |
| Exchange vs over-the-counter (OTC) | On an exchange, orders meet in a central, regulated venue. In an OTC market, dealers quote prices and trade bilaterally. |
| Broker vs dealer | A broker arranges trades as an agent for a fee. A dealer trades from its own inventory and earns the bid-ask spread. |

### Real and nominal rates

A nominal interest rate $i$ is measured in money; the real rate $r$ is measured in purchasing power. With expected inflation $\pi$, the **Fisher relation** is

$$1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad r = \frac{1 + i}{1 + \pi} - 1 \approx i - \pi.$$

The approximation is fine for small rates; this course uses the exact form unless it says otherwise.

### Why intermediaries help: pooling and information

If a lender makes one loan that defaults with probability $p$ and loses everything on default, the fraction lost has standard deviation $\sqrt{p(1-p)}$. A pool of $n$ independent, identical loans has a fraction of defaults with standard deviation

$$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}},$$

which shrinks with $n$. Real loans are not fully independent (a recession hits many at once), so diversification reduces but never removes risk.

Two information problems explain much of the system's design. **Adverse selection** arises before a deal: the borrowers most eager to borrow at a given rate are the riskiest, so lenders screen applicants and may ration credit. **Moral hazard** arises after a deal: a borrower with little to lose may take more risk with the lender's money, so lenders add covenants, collateral and monitoring. Banks, credit-rating agencies, auditors and disclosure rules all respond to these problems.

@example
title: A real rate from a nominal rate
problem: A one-year bond pays a nominal 6% and investors expect inflation of 2.5% over the year. What real rate do they expect to earn? Use the exact Fisher relation.
step: Write the relation: $1 + r = (1 + i)/(1 + \pi)$.
step: Substitute: $1 + r = 1.06/1.025 = 1.034146$.
step: Subtract 1: $r = 3.4146\%$. The approximation $i - \pi = 3.5\%$ is close but slightly high.
answer: About 3.41% real.

@example
title: How pooling shrinks risk
problem: Each loan in a book defaults with probability 2%, and defaults are independent. Compare the standard deviation of the default fraction for one loan and for a pool of 100 loans, and find the probability that none of the 100 defaults.
step: One loan: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.02 \times 0.98} = 0.14$, or 14 percentage points, which is large compared with the 2% mean.
step: One hundred loans: $\sigma = \sqrt{0.02 \times 0.98/100} = 0.014$, or 1.4 percentage points. The pool is ten times less volatile, since $\sqrt{100} = 10$.
step: Probability of no defaults: $0.98^{100} = 0.1326$, so in 87% of cases at least one loan defaults, but the number is usually small (the expected count is 2).
answer: 14% versus 1.4%; the probability of zero defaults is about 13.3%.

@mistakes
- Treating the secondary market as a source of funds for the issuer: when you buy a share from another investor, the company receives nothing.
- Subtracting inflation from the nominal rate and calling it exact; the exact real rate divides $1+i$ by $1+\pi$.
- Assuming diversification removes all risk; it removes only the part that is independent across loans.
- Mixing up adverse selection (a problem before the contract) and moral hazard (a problem after it).

choice: A manufacturer sells bonds directly to a group of institutional investors in a new issue
choice*: A household deposits savings in a bank, and the bank lends the money to a small business
choice: An investor buys shares of a listed company from another investor through a broker
choice: Two companies agree to a loan without any intermediary or security

solution: In indirect finance the saver holds a claim on an intermediary (a deposit), and the intermediary holds a claim on the final borrower (a loan). Selling bonds to investors is direct finance, and buying existing shares is a secondary-market trade, which does not fund the company at all.

@quiz num
prompt: An investor wants a real return of 1.5% over the next year, and expects inflation of 2.0%. Using the exact Fisher relation, what nominal rate does the investor need? Give it as a percent to two decimal places.
answer: 3.53
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: (1.015 * 1.02 - 1) * 100
hint: Rearrange the Fisher relation to $1 + i = (1 + r)(1 + \pi)$.
hint: Multiply $1.015 \times 1.02$ and subtract 1.
solution: $i = 1.015 \times 1.02 - 1 = 3.53\%$. The shortcut $r + \pi$ gives 3.50%; the extra 0.03% is the return on the inflation compensation.

@quiz self
prompt: A bank manager says: "Our loan officers visit borrowers' sites and write restrictive covenants into every business loan." In four to six sentences, explain which two information problems this practice addresses, and why a saver is better off lending through the bank than lending directly to the business.
hint: One problem occurs before the loan is made and one after.
solution: Visiting sites and screening applicants addresses adverse selection: before lending, the bank wants to avoid the borrowers most likely to default, who are also the most eager to borrow. Covenants and monitoring address moral hazard: after the loan is made, a borrower might take more risk with the bank's money than the bank agreed to. A saver acting alone would find it expensive to screen and monitor a business, and would hold one risky claim. The bank spreads this cost over many depositors, diversifies across many loans and offers the saver a simple deposit, so the saver gets lower transaction costs and lower risk.
rubric: Names adverse selection (before the loan, screening) and moral hazard (after the loan, monitoring and covenants).
rubric: Explains that screening and monitoring are costly for an individual saver and cheaper when spread over many depositors.
rubric: Mentions diversification or pooling across many loans as a benefit to the saver.
