@article
Firms and governments constantly need cash for days or months, and some investors have cash to park for just as long. The **money market** is the market for short-term, highly liquid, low-risk debt, with original maturities of one year or less. Its instruments are usually large, traded over the counter, and priced as a **discount** from face value instead of with a coupon.

### Main instruments

- **Treasury bills** are zero-coupon obligations of the US government, issued at auction with original maturities of four weeks up to one year. They carry no credit risk in the usual sense and are the benchmark short-term asset.
- **Commercial paper (CP)** is unsecured short-term debt issued by large corporations and financial firms, with maturities up to 270 days when sold without SEC registration. It is rated, and its yield exceeds the bill yield by a credit spread.
- **Negotiable certificates of deposit** are large time deposits that can be sold before maturity.
- **Federal funds** are overnight unsecured loans of reserve balances between banks; the Fed sets a target range and steers the rate on these loans with administered rates.
- **Repurchase agreements (repos)** are sales of securities with a promise to buy them back at a higher price later. Economically, a repo is a **collateralized loan**.
- **Money market funds** pool investor cash into these instruments.

### Discount yields and prices

For bills and commercial paper, the quoted **discount yield** $d$ uses face value $F$ in the denominator and a 360-day year:

$$P = F\left(1 - d\,\frac{t}{360}\right), \qquad d = \frac{F - P}{F}\cdot\frac{360}{t},$$

where $t$ is days to maturity. The discount yield understates the return because it divides by $F$ instead of the amount invested. Two better measures:

$$\text{Bond-equivalent yield (BEY)} = \frac{F - P}{P}\cdot\frac{365}{t}, \qquad \text{Money market yield} = \frac{F - P}{P}\cdot\frac{360}{t}.$$

The BEY puts a bill on a 365-day basis, comparable with bond yields (this simple form is for bills of up to about six months); the money market yield keeps the 360-day basis used for CP, CDs and repos. For the same security, $d <$ money market yield $<$ BEY.

### Repurchase agreements

In a repo, the borrower of cash sells securities (the collateral) and agrees to repurchase them. The lender of cash is said to do a **reverse repo**. Terms: the **repo rate**, quoted on an actual/360 basis; the **term** (overnight or term); and the **haircut** $h$, the excess of collateral value over cash lent:

$$\text{Cash lent} = V(1 - h), \qquad \text{Interest} = \text{Cash} \times r \times \frac{\text{days}}{360}.$$

(Here $V$ is the collateral's market value, and the haircut is a fraction of that value. Other quoting conventions exist.) The haircut protects the lender if collateral falls in value. The **Secured Overnight Financing Rate (SOFR)** is a benchmark based on overnight repo transactions secured by Treasuries; it replaced USD LIBOR as the main US dollar reference rate. Repo markets are a key source of dealer funding, which is why stress there can spread widely (see week 12). For current rates, use the New York Fed or FRED, not a textbook.

### Comparing three instruments

| | Treasury bill | Commercial paper | Repo |
|---|---|---|---|
| Borrower | US Treasury | Corporation or financial firm | Dealer, fund or bank |
| Security | Unsecured, government | Unsecured | Secured by collateral |
| Quote | Discount yield (360) | Discount yield (360) | Rate on actual/360, with haircut |
| Credit risk | Minimal | Issuer credit risk | Counterparty plus collateral risk |

@example
title: A 91-day Treasury bill
problem: A 91-day bill with face value 100 is quoted at a discount yield of 4.00%. Find its price and its bond-equivalent and money market yields.
step: Price: $P = 100(1 - 0.04 \times 91/360) = 100(1 - 0.010111) = 98.98889$.
step: Dollar return per \$98.98889 invested: $100 - 98.98889 = 1.01111$.
step: BEY: $(1.01111/98.98889)(365/91) = 0.010214 \times 4.010989 = 4.0970\%$.
step: Money market yield: $0.010214 \times 360/91 = 4.0409\%$. Ordering: $4.00\% < 4.04\% < 4.10\%$.
answer: Price 98.9889; BEY 4.10%; money market yield 4.04%.

@example
title: Financing a position with an overnight-to-term repo
problem: A dealer repos Treasury securities worth \$5,000,000 for 7 days at a repo rate of 4.20% (actual/360, hypothetical) with a 2% haircut. How much cash does it get, and what does it repay?
step: Cash lent $= 5{,}000{,}000 \times (1 - 0.02) = \$4{,}900{,}000$.
step: Interest $= 4{,}900{,}000 \times 0.042 \times 7/360 = \$4{,}001.67$.
step: Repurchase price $= \$4{,}904{,}001.67$. The dealer funds only 98% of the position and must finance the 2% (\$100,000) with its own capital.
answer: Cash \$4,900,000; repayment \$4,904,001.67.

@mistakes
- Using the discount yield as the rate of return; it divides by face value, not by the price paid.
- Mixing day counts: discount yields and repo rates use 360 days; the bond-equivalent yield uses 365.
- Thinking the repo lender receives the collateral's full value in cash; the haircut keeps the cash below the collateral value.
- Confusing repo and reverse repo: they are the same trade seen from the two sides.

@quiz num

answer: 1.58
unit: %
tol: 0.01

solution: $d = (0.80/100)(360/182) = 0.008 \times 1.978022 = 1.58\%$. The bond-equivalent yield on the same bill is higher, about 1.62%.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about money market instruments are correct?
choice*: A repo is economically a collateralized loan
choice: Commercial paper is secured by a pledge of the issuer's receivables in every case
choice*: A Treasury bill's discount yield is lower than its bond-equivalent yield
choice*: Federal funds trades are overnight unsecured loans of reserve balances between banks
choice: Money market instruments have original maturities of more than ten years
hint: Think about collateral, day counts and maturity.
solution: A repo is a loan secured by securities. Standard commercial paper is unsecured (asset-backed CP is a separate type). The discount yield divides by face value, which exceeds the price, so it understates the bond-equivalent yield. Federal funds are overnight unsecured interbank loans. Money market maturities are one year or less.
