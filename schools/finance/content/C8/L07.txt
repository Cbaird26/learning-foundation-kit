@article
A bank is a highly leveraged intermediary: it funds long-lived, illiquid assets (loans) with short-term, liquid liabilities (deposits). This **maturity transformation** is its economic function, and the source of its fragility.

### The balance sheet

| Assets | Liabilities and equity |
|---|---|
| Cash and reserve balances at the central bank | Deposits (checking, savings, time) |
| Securities (Treasuries, agencies, mortgage-backed securities) | Borrowings (repo, federal funds, bonds) |
| Loans (commercial, real estate, consumer), net of loan loss allowance | Equity (common stock, retained earnings) |
| Other assets | |

Assets equal liabilities plus equity. Because equity is a thin slice, a small percentage loss on assets is a large percentage loss on equity. The **equity multiplier** is assets divided by equity.

### Income and profitability

**Net interest income (NII)** is interest income minus interest expense. The **net interest margin (NIM)** scales it by earning assets:

$$\text{NIM} = \frac{\text{Interest income} - \text{Interest expense}}{\text{Average earning assets}}.$$

Banks also earn **noninterest income** (fees) and pay **noninterest expense** (salaries, premises), and they set aside a **provision for credit losses** (under CECL, expected lifetime losses). Profitability is summarized by

$$\text{ROA} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average assets}}, \quad \text{ROE} = \frac{\text{Net income}}{\text{Average equity}} = \text{ROA} \times \frac{\text{Assets}}{\text{Equity}}.$$

The last identity is the **DuPont decomposition**: a bank earns a given ROE either with a high ROA or with high leverage, and leverage raises risk.

### Risks

- **Credit risk**: borrowers fail to repay; managed by underwriting, diversification, collateral and loss provisions.
- **Interest rate risk**: asset and liability values and incomes change when rates move.
- **Liquidity risk**: depositors withdraw faster than assets can be sold or borrowing arranged.
- **Market risk** (trading positions) and **operational risk** (fraud, systems, legal).

### Measuring interest rate risk

The **repricing (funding) gap** is rate-sensitive assets minus rate-sensitive liabilities over a horizon. A negative gap means that if rates rise, interest expense rises faster than interest income and NII falls. It looks at income only.

The **duration gap** looks at value. With assets $A$, liabilities $L$, equity $E = A - L$, asset and liability durations $D_A$ and $D_L$ and a parallel shift $\Delta y$ in yields around level $y$:

$$\Delta E \approx -\left(D_A - \frac{L}{A}\,D_L\right) A\,\frac{\Delta y}{1 + y}.$$

The term in parentheses is the duration gap. A bank with long-duration assets (fixed-rate mortgages, long bonds) and short-duration liabilities (deposits) has a positive gap and loses equity value when rates rise. The estimate is a first-order approximation; large rate moves need convexity. This is how long-term securities held with fixed rates produced large unrealized losses at some banks when rates rose (week 12).

@example
title: Profitability of a bank
problem: A bank has average assets of \$1,000 million, average earning assets of \$900 million and average equity of \$80 million. Interest income is \$54.0 million, interest expense \$20.0 million and net income \$8.0 million. Find NIM, ROA, ROE and the equity multiplier, and check the DuPont identity.
step: $\text{NII} = 54.0 - 20.0 = \$34.0$ million, so $\text{NIM} = 34.0/900 = 3.78\%$.
step: $\text{ROA} = 8.0/1{,}000 = 0.80\%$ and $\text{ROE} = 8.0/80 = 10.0\%$.
step: Equity multiplier $= 1{,}000/80 = 12.5$.
step: DuPont: $0.80\% \times 12.5 = 10.0\%$, matching ROE.
answer: NIM 3.78%, ROA 0.80%, ROE 10.0%, multiplier 12.5.

@example
title: A positive duration gap
problem: A bank has assets of \$1,000 million with duration 4.0 years, liabilities of \$920 million with duration 1.5 years and equity of \$80 million. Yields are 5% and rise by 1 percentage point. Estimate the change in equity.
step: $L/A = 0.92$, so the duration gap is $4.0 - 0.92 \times 1.5 = 2.62$ years.
step: $\Delta E \approx -2.62 \times 1{,}000 \times 0.01/1.05 = -\$24.95$ million.
step: That is $24.95/80 = 31\%$ of equity from a 1-point rate rise: thin capital makes interest rate risk serious.
answer: About -\$24.95 million (a 31% fall in equity).

@mistakes
- Dividing net interest income by total assets instead of earning assets when asked for the NIM.
- Ignoring leverage: comparing ROE across banks without looking at the equity multiplier.
- Forgetting to scale liability duration by $L/A$ in the duration gap.
- Assuming a bank is safe because its income is rate-neutral over a year; a long-duration asset portfolio can still lose value.

model:
```python
def equity_change(assets, liabilities, d_assets, d_liabilities, y, dy):
    """Approximate change in bank equity value from a parallel yield shift."""
    duration_gap = d_assets - (liabilities / assets) * d_liabilities
    return -duration_gap * assets * dy / (1 + y)
```
hint: First compute the duration gap, scaling the liability duration by liabilities over assets.
hint: Multiply the negative of the gap by assets and by `dy / (1 + y)`.
solution: The duration gap measures how much more sensitive assets are than liabilities to yields once leverage is accounted for. Multiplying by assets and the relative yield change gives the equity change; a positive gap means falling equity when yields rise, which the last test checks by reversing the sign of `dy`.
