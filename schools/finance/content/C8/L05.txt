@article
When you buy a share of a listed company, your order does not travel to the company. It goes to your broker, which routes it to a venue where it meets a seller. This lesson follows what happens at that venue; week 6 follows what happens afterward.

### Venues

A **national securities exchange** is a regulated marketplace with rules for listing and trading. Trades in US stocks also occur on **alternative trading systems (ATSs)**, including **dark pools**, which match orders without displaying quotes before the trade, and through **wholesale market makers**, which execute many retail orders from their own inventory. A **market maker** posts bid and ask quotes continuously, earns the spread and bears inventory risk. Regular trading hours are 9:30 a.m. to 4:00 p.m. Eastern time, with opening and closing auctions on exchanges.

Regulation NMS ties the venues together. The **national best bid and offer (NBBO)** is the highest bid and lowest ask across protected quotes; the **Order Protection Rule** generally prevents a trade at a price worse than a protected quote displayed elsewhere. Broker-dealers also owe customers **best execution**. Quoting increments (tick sizes) and fee rules have been amended in recent years, so check current SEC rules.

### The limit order book

A **limit order book** lists resting buy orders (**bids**) and sell orders (**asks**) by price. The **bid-ask spread** is ask minus bid; the **midpoint** is their average. Orders have four main types:

- **Market order**: execute now at the best available prices. Certain execution, uncertain price.
- **Limit order**: execute only at the limit price or better. Certain price, uncertain execution. A limit buy below the best ask rests on the book and **makes** liquidity; a market order **takes** liquidity.
- **Stop order**: becomes a market order when the stop price is reached. A sell stop sits below the current price. It does not guarantee the price, which can be worse in a fast market.
- **Stop-limit order**: becomes a limit order when the stop price is reached.

Matching follows **price-time priority**: the best price first, and among orders at the same price, the earliest first. A large market order "walks the book" through successive price levels.

During extreme declines, market-wide **circuit breakers** halt trading when the S&P 500 falls 7%, 13% or 20% below the previous close, and single-stock limit up-limit down bands pause trading in individual stocks.

### Buying on margin and selling short

Under the Federal Reserve's Regulation T, an investor can borrow up to 50% of the purchase price of stock (**initial margin 50%**). FINRA requires a **maintenance margin** of at least 25% of the market value of long positions, and brokers often require more. With $V$ the market value and $L$ the loan, the equity ratio is $(V - L)/V$. A **margin call** occurs when it falls below the maintenance level. For $N$ shares and a maintenance requirement $m$, the call price is

$$P^{*} = \frac{L}{N(1 - m)}.$$

In a **short sale**, the investor borrows shares, sells them and must later buy them back. If proceeds plus the margin deposit are held as cash $C$ and the price rises to $P$, the equity ratio relative to the value owed is $(C - NP)/(NP)$, so the call price is $P^{*} = C/(N(1 + m))$. Losses on a short are unlimited in principle. This lesson is education, not advice.

@example
title: Walking the book
problem: The book for a stock shows asks of 200 shares at \$50.02, 300 at \$50.03 and 400 at \$50.05, and bids of 300 at \$50.00 and 500 at \$49.99. Find the spread and midpoint, and the cost and average price of a market order to buy 450 shares.
step: The best bid is \$50.00 and the best ask \$50.02, so the spread is \$0.02 and the midpoint \$50.01.
step: The order takes 200 shares at \$50.02 (\$10,004.00), then 250 of the 300 at \$50.03 (\$12,507.50).
step: Total cost $= \$22{,}511.50$; average price $= 22{,}511.50/450 = \$50.0256$, which is 1.56 cents above the midpoint.
answer: Spread \$0.02, midpoint \$50.01; the order costs \$22,511.50 (average \$50.0256).

@example
title: A margin purchase
problem: An investor buys 1,000 shares at \$40 with 50% initial margin and a 25% maintenance margin. Find the loan, the return if the price rises to \$48, and the price that triggers a margin call. Ignore interest and commissions.
step: Purchase \$40,000; the investor puts up \$20,000 and borrows $L = \$20{,}000$.
step: At \$48 the position is worth \$48,000; equity is \$28,000, a gain of \$8,000 on \$20,000, a 40% return against 20% for the share itself.
step: Margin call price: $P^{*} = 20{,}000/(1{,}000 \times 0.75) = \$26.67$. Leverage also magnifies losses: a fall of about 33% in the price triggers the call.
answer: Loan \$20,000; return 40%; margin call at about \$26.67.

@mistakes
- Assuming a market order executes at the quoted price; a large order can walk the book, and prices can move before it arrives.
- Believing a stop order guarantees its price; once triggered, it is a market order.
- Computing the margin call from the purchase price rather than from the loan: the loan stays fixed while the value moves.
- Thinking that buying shares in the secondary market raises money for the company.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements about orders are correct?
choice*: A sell stop order becomes a market order once the stop price trades or is passed
choice: A limit order guarantees both the price and that it will execute
choice*: Under price-time priority, among bids at the same price the earliest order executes first
choice*: A market order takes liquidity, and a resting limit order provides it

hint: Separate certainty of price from certainty of execution.
solution: Stop orders turn into market orders, so they can fill at worse prices in a fast market. A limit order guarantees its price (or better) but not execution. Time priority applies within a price level, and market orders take liquidity from resting limit orders.
