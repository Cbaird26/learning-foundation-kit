@article
The bond market is larger than the stock market in most countries, and its structure is different. Most bonds are not traded on exchanges at all: dealers quote prices and trade with institutions over the counter. This lesson covers the segments, the conventions for quoting and settling a bond trade, and the link between price and yield.

### Segments

- **US Treasury securities**: bills (up to one year), **notes** (2 to 10 years), **bonds** (20 and 30 years), inflation-protected securities (TIPS) and floating-rate notes. They are sold by the Treasury in regular **auctions** (competitive bidders state a yield; noncompetitive bidders accept the result; Treasury auctions use a single-price format), then trade in the **secondary market** among primary dealers and others. The Treasury market is the deepest and most liquid in the world, and its yields are the benchmark for other rates.
- **Corporate bonds**: issued by firms, rated by credit-rating agencies. **Investment grade** means rated BBB-/Baa3 or higher; below that is **high yield**. Yields exceed Treasury yields by a **credit spread**. Trades are reported to FINRA's TRACE system.
- **Municipal bonds**: issued by states and local governments. **General obligation** bonds are backed by taxing power, **revenue bonds** by the income of a project. Interest is generally exempt from federal income tax, which lowers the yield investors require. Trades are reported through the MSRB's EMMA system.
- **Agency and mortgage-backed securities**: debt of government agencies and government-sponsored enterprises, and securities backed by pools of mortgages (week 12 explains how mortgage securitization went wrong).

### Quotes and dollar prices

Bond prices are quoted per 100 of face value. Treasury notes and bonds are quoted in **32nds**: $101\text{-}16$ means $101 + 16/32 = 101.50$, and a trailing "+" adds $1/64$ (so $99\text{-}24+ = 99.765625$). A quote is a **clean price** that excludes accrued interest.

### Accrued interest and the invoice price

Between coupon dates, the buyer compensates the seller for the part of the coupon the seller has earned:

$$\text{Accrued interest} = \frac{C}{m} \times \frac{\text{days since last coupon}}{\text{days in the coupon period}},$$

where $C$ is the annual coupon per 100 and $m$ the number of coupons per year. The **invoice (dirty) price** equals the clean price plus accrued interest. The day count depends on the market:

| Market | Convention |
|---|---|
| US Treasury notes and bonds | Actual/actual (actual days in the period) |
| US corporate and municipal bonds | 30/360 (each month 30 days, year 360) |

Under 30/360 (US), days between $(y_1, m_1, d_1)$ and $(y_2, m_2, d_2)$ are $360(y_2 - y_1) + 30(m_2 - m_1) + (d_2 - d_1)$, after setting $d_1 = 30$ if $d_1 = 31$ and then $d_2 = 30$ if $d_2 = 31$ and $d_1 = 30$.

### Price and yield

Price is the present value of the coupons and principal at the yield to maturity. Price falls when yield rises. A bond with a coupon above the market yield trades at a **premium**; below, at a **discount**; at par when they are equal. Bond price conventions in this course assume a coupon date as the settlement date unless dates are given.

### Tax-equivalent yield

A taxable bond must yield more than a tax-exempt one to give the same after-tax return:

$$y_{\text{taxable equivalent}} = \frac{y_{\text{muni}}}{1 - t},$$

where $t$ is the investor's marginal tax rate. This is general education, not tax advice.

@example
title: A Treasury quote and a corporate invoice price
problem: (a) A Treasury note is quoted at 99-24+. What is the clean dollar price of \$1,000,000 face? (b) A corporate bond with a 5% semiannual coupon (coupon dates March 15 and September 15) is quoted at a clean price of 97.20, and a trade settles June 10. Find the accrued interest and invoice price per 100 and for \$100,000 face, using 30/360.
step: (a) $99 + 24/32 + 1/64 = 99.765625$ per 100, so the dollar price is $0.99765625 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = \$997{,}656.25$.
step: (b) Days from March 15 to June 10 under 30/360: $30 \times 3 + (10 - 15) = 85$. A semiannual period has 180 days.
step: Accrued interest $= 2.50 \times 85/180 = 1.1806$ per 100.
step: Invoice price $= 97.20 + 1.1806 = 98.3806$ per 100, so \$100,000 face costs \$98,380.56.
answer: (a) \$997,656.25. (b) Accrued 1.1806; invoice price 98.3806, or \$98,380.56.

@example
title: Price at a given yield
problem: A 10-year bond has a 4% coupon paid semiannually and yields 5% (annual rate compounded semiannually). Find its price per 100 on a coupon date.
step: Per half-year: coupon 2.00, yield $2.5\%$, and $n = 20$ periods.
step: $P = 2\,\dfrac{1 - 1.025^{-20}}{0.025} + \dfrac{100}{1.025^{20}} = 31.1783 + 61.0271 = 92.2054$.
step: The bond trades at a discount because its 4% coupon is below the 5% market yield.
answer: 92.21 per 100.

@mistakes
- Reading a 32nds quote as a decimal: 102-08 is 102.25, not 102.08.
- Mixing up clean and dirty prices: quotes are clean; the buyer pays clean plus accrued interest.
- Using actual days for a corporate bond (30/360) or 30/360 for a Treasury (actual/actual).
- Applying the tax rate the wrong way: the tax-equivalent yield divides by $1 - t$; it does not multiply by $1 + t$.

model:
```python
def accrued_interest(coupon_rate, last_coupon, settlement, freq=2):
    """Accrued interest per 100 face, 30/360 US convention."""
    d1 = min(last_coupon.day, 30)
    d2 = settlement.day
    if d2 == 31 and d1 == 30:
        d2 = 30
    days = (360 * (settlement.year - last_coupon.year)
            + 30 * (settlement.month - last_coupon.month)
            + (d2 - d1))
    coupon = 100 * coupon_rate / freq
    return coupon * days / (360 / freq)
```
hint: Count the 30/360 days first, then take that fraction of one coupon.
hint: The coupon per period is `100 * coupon_rate / freq`, and a period is `360 / freq` days.
solution: The 30/360 day count adjusts month-end days so that every month has 30 days; accrued interest is the fraction of the period's coupon earned. The tests cover month-end adjustments, a settlement on a coupon date (zero accrual) and a quarterly coupon; a solution that counts actual days fails the month-end cases.

prompt: Compare Treasury bills, commercial paper and repurchase agreements. For each, say who borrows, what secures the claim (if anything), how the quote is expressed and the main risk the lender bears. Then say which has the lowest yield for the same maturity and why.
hint: Consider issuer, collateral, quote convention and credit risk.
solution: A Treasury bill is borrowed by the US government, is unsecured but backed by the government's taxing power, and is quoted as a discount yield on 360 days; its credit risk is minimal, so it usually has the lowest yield for a given maturity. Commercial paper is borrowed by corporations and financial firms, is generally unsecured, is also quoted as a discount yield, and the lender bears the issuer's credit risk, so its yield includes a credit spread over bills. A repo is borrowed by a dealer, fund or bank, is secured by securities with a haircut, is quoted as a rate on an actual/360 basis, and the lender bears counterparty and collateral-value risk, limited by the haircut. Bills have the lowest yield because of the lowest credit risk and the highest liquidity; repos secured by Treasuries have similar low yields because of the collateral.
