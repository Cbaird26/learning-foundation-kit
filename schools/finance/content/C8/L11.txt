@article
Insurers and pension funds are the largest long-term institutional investors, and both are built on one idea: with many independent exposures, the total is much more predictable than any one of them. They differ in what they promise. An insurer promises to pay if a specific event occurs; a pension fund promises to pay income in retirement.

### Insurance

An insurer collects **premiums** from policyholders and pays **claims**. Pooling works through the **law of large numbers**: if a policy has claim probability $p$ and the loss is $S$ (the severity), the expected loss per policy is the **pure premium**

$$\text{Pure premium} = \text{frequency} \times \text{severity} = p \times S.$$

The insurer charges a **gross premium** that also covers expenses and profit. If these are a fraction $\ell$ of the gross premium, then

$$\text{Gross premium} = \frac{\text{Pure premium}}{1 - \ell}.$$

For $n$ independent policies, the number of claims has standard deviation $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$, which grows more slowly than the expected number $np$, so the relative uncertainty falls with $n$. It does not vanish: catastrophes (hurricanes, pandemics) hit many policies at once, and the insurer buys **reinsurance** to share such risks.

Insurers also fight two information problems. **Adverse selection**: high-risk people buy more insurance, so insurers underwrite and price by risk class. **Moral hazard**: insured people take less care, so policies use **deductibles**, coinsurance and limits.

Property and casualty insurers are judged by

$$\text{Loss ratio} = \frac{\text{Incurred losses}}{\text{Earned premiums}}, \quad \text{Expense ratio} = \frac{\text{Underwriting expenses}}{\text{Earned premiums}}, \quad \text{Combined ratio} = \text{Loss ratio} + \text{Expense ratio}.$$

A combined ratio below 100% means an underwriting profit. Even above 100% an insurer can profit from investment income on the **float**, the premiums held until claims are paid. Life insurers promise long-term payments and hold long-duration assets to match. In the United States, insurance is regulated mainly by the states (the McCarran-Ferguson Act of 1945 left regulation to them), coordinated by the National Association of Insurance Commissioners, with state guaranty associations protecting policyholders if an insurer fails.

### Pension funds

In a **defined benefit (DB) plan**, the sponsor promises a benefit set by a formula (for example, a fraction of final salary per year of service). The sponsor bears investment and longevity risk. In a **defined contribution (DC) plan**, such as a 401(k), the worker and employer contribute and the worker bears the investment risk (the 2026 employee deferral limit for 401(k) plans is \$24,500; limits change each year). Private DB plans are governed by ERISA (1974), which sets funding and fiduciary standards, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) insures benefits up to limits.

The plan's liability is the present value of promised benefits, the **projected benefit obligation (PBO)**, discounted at a rate based on high-quality bond yields. The **funded ratio** is

$$\text{Funded ratio} = \frac{\text{Plan assets}}{\text{PBO}}.$$

A fall in the discount rate raises the PBO, like a bond's price rising when yields fall; because liabilities are long-duration, this can cut the funded ratio even when assets are stable. **Liability-driven investing** tries to hold assets whose value moves with the liabilities.

@example
title: Pricing a policy
problem: An insurer writes 10,000 policies. The annual claim probability is 3% and the average claim is \$12,000. Expenses and profit are 25% of gross premium, of which expenses are 20%. Find the pure premium, the gross premium, the combined ratio if claims are exactly as expected, and the standard deviation of the number of claims.
step: Pure premium $= 0.03 \times 12{,}000 = \$360$.
step: Gross premium $= 360/(1 - 0.25) = \$480$.
step: Loss ratio $= 360/480 = 75\%$; expense ratio $= 20\%$; combined ratio $= 95\%$, leaving a 5% underwriting margin.
step: Expected claims $= 300$; standard deviation $= \sqrt{10{,}000 \times 0.03 \times 0.97} = 17.06$, about 5.7% of the expected count.
answer: Pure premium \$360; gross premium \$480; combined ratio 95%; standard deviation of claims about 17.

@example
title: A pension liability
problem: A plan owes a worker \$40,000 a year for 20 years, with the first payment one year after retirement, which is 10 years away. Find the present value of this liability at a 5% discount rate and at 4%, and the funded ratio at each rate if assets are \$300,000.
step: At 5%, value at retirement $= 40{,}000 \times \dfrac{1 - 1.05^{-20}}{0.05} = \$498{,}488$. Today: $498{,}488/1.05^{10} = \$306{,}029$.
step: At 4%, value at retirement $= \$543{,}613$; today $543{,}613/1.04^{10} = \$367{,}246$.
step: The liability rises by 20.0% when the rate falls one point.
step: Funded ratio $= 300{,}000/306{,}029 = 98.0\%$ at 5% and $300{,}000/367{,}246 = 81.7\%$ at 4%.
answer: \$306,029 and \$367,246; funded 98.0% and 81.7%.

@mistakes
- Setting the premium equal to the pure premium: that leaves nothing for expenses, profit or the risk of adverse results.
- Applying the loading to the pure premium instead of to the gross premium; divide by $1 - \ell$, do not multiply by $1 + \ell$ when $\ell$ is a share of the gross premium.
- Thinking a funded ratio is only about asset returns: the discount rate moves the liability.
- Assuming the sponsor bears the risk in a defined contribution plan; the participant does.

@quiz self
prompt: In a short paragraph, compare a defined benefit plan and a defined contribution plan. Say who bears investment risk and longevity risk in each, and explain why a fall in interest rates is a problem for the sponsor of a defined benefit plan.
hint: Think about who owns the promise, and about the discount rate.
solution: In a defined benefit plan, the sponsor promises a benefit set by a formula, so the sponsor bears the investment risk (assets may fall short) and the longevity risk (retirees may live longer than assumed). In a defined contribution plan, the participant owns an account whose value depends on contributions and investment returns, so the participant bears the investment risk and the risk of outliving savings, unless an annuity is bought. When interest rates fall, the present value of a DB plan's long-term promised payments rises, because it is discounted at a lower rate. If assets do not rise as much, the funded ratio falls and the sponsor may have to contribute more cash.
rubric: States that the DB sponsor bears investment and longevity risk, while the DC participant bears investment risk (and longevity risk without an annuity).
rubric: Explains that lower discount rates raise the present value of DB liabilities.
rubric: Connects the larger liability to a lower funded ratio and possible higher sponsor contributions.
