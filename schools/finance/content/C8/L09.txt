@article
Banks that take deposits make loans and hold them. **Investment banks** do something different: they help clients raise capital and trade securities, and they mostly earn fees and trading margins rather than interest spreads. Many are now units of large bank holding companies, but the businesses remain distinct.

### What they do

- **Underwriting.** The bank helps an issuer sell new stocks or bonds to investors, handling pricing, marketing and distribution.
- **Advisory.** Advice on mergers, acquisitions, divestitures and restructurings, for a fee.
- **Sales and trading.** Selling securities to institutional clients and **making markets**: quoting two-sided prices and holding inventory. Revenue comes from spreads and commissions.
- **Research.** Analysts write reports; conflicts of interest between research and banking are regulated.
- **Prime brokerage.** Financing, custody and trade execution for hedge funds.

A **broker-dealer** is the regulated legal entity that does the securities business. It acts as a **broker** (agent) when it executes a trade for a customer for a commission, and as a **dealer** (principal) when it trades from its own account.

### How an IPO works

In an **initial public offering (IPO)** a private firm sells shares to the public for the first time. The firm hires lead **underwriters**, who perform due diligence; the firm files a registration statement with the SEC under the Securities Act of 1933, which requires a prospectus. Underwriters then run a **roadshow** and **bookbuilding** (collecting investors' orders at different prices), set the **offer price** and allocate shares.

Two underwriting forms exist:

| Form | Who bears the risk of unsold shares |
|---|---|
| **Firm commitment** | The underwriter buys the whole issue from the issuer at the offer price less the spread and resells it; the underwriter bears the risk |
| **Best efforts** | The underwriter only tries to sell and returns unsold shares; the issuer bears the risk |

The underwriter's compensation is the **gross spread**: the offer price minus the price paid to the issuer, as a percent of the offer price. The issuer's net proceeds per share are

$$\text{Net proceeds per share} = P_{\text{offer}}\,(1 - s),$$

where $s$ is the spread (before legal, accounting and listing costs). Spreads depend on the size of the deal. IPO shares have, on average, risen on the first day of trading; the **first-day return** $(P_{\text{close}} - P_{\text{offer}})/P_{\text{offer}}$ measures this **underpricing**, which is money "left on the table" by the issuer.

### The greenshoe

A **greenshoe** (over-allotment option) lets underwriters buy up to 15% more shares from the issuer at the offer price, usually within 30 days. Underwriters sell the extra shares at the offer, creating a short position. If the price rises, they exercise the option and deliver. If it falls, they buy shares in the market at the lower price to cover the short, which supports the price (**stabilization**), and earn the difference.

### Regulation

The **Glass-Steagall provisions** of the Banking Act of 1933 separated commercial and investment banking; the **Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999** repealed the core separation. After the 2007–2009 crisis, the **Volcker Rule** (Dodd-Frank) restricts banking entities from short-term proprietary trading and from sponsoring certain covered funds, with exemptions for market making and hedging. Broker-dealers are regulated by the SEC and the self-regulatory organization FINRA; they must meet the SEC net capital rule (Rule 15c3-1), segregate customer assets, follow Regulation Best Interest when recommending to retail customers and deliver Form CRS. SIPC protects customers if a member firm fails (not against market losses).

@example
title: Pricing an IPO
problem: A firm sells 20 million shares at an offer price of \$25 under a firm commitment, with a 6% gross spread and a greenshoe of 15%. Find the issuer's net proceeds before other costs, the underwriters' total spread if the greenshoe is fully exercised, and the first-day return if the shares close at \$31.
step: Base net proceeds $= 20{,}000{,}000 \times 25 \times (1 - 0.06) = \$470.0$ million.
step: Greenshoe shares $= 0.15 \times 20 = 3$ million, so total shares are 23 million. Total net proceeds $= 23 \times 25 \times 0.94 = \$540.5$ million, and the spread is $23 \times 25 \times 0.06 = \$34.5$ million.
step: First-day return $= (31 - 25)/25 = 24\%$. On the 20 million base shares, \$120 million was left on the table relative to a \$31 offer.
answer: Net proceeds \$470.0 million (\$540.5 million with the greenshoe); spread \$34.5 million; first-day return 24%.

@example
title: Stabilization
problem: In an offering of 10 million shares at \$30, underwriters over-allot 1.5 million shares under the greenshoe. After the offering the price falls to \$28.50. What do underwriters do, and what do they earn, ignoring costs and the spread?
step: They have sold 1.5 million shares they do not own at \$30.
step: At \$28.50 it is cheaper to buy 1.5 million shares in the market than to exercise the option at \$30; buying also supports the price.
step: Gain $= 1.5\text{ million} \times (30 - 28.50) = \$2.25$ million.
answer: They buy the shares in the market and earn about \$2.25 million; the option goes unexercised.

@mistakes
- Treating the gross spread as the issuer's total cost; legal, audit and listing costs are extra.
- Calling a first-day pop an underwriter profit: the issuer gets the offer price; the pop goes to investors who were allocated shares.
- Mixing up the two underwriting forms: in a firm commitment the underwriter holds the risk.
- Believing the Volcker Rule bans all trading by banks; it targets proprietary trading, with market-making and hedging exemptions.
