@article
Because a bank's liabilities are payable on demand and its assets are not, the failure of one bank can start a run on others. Failure also harms borrowers who lose their lender. These **externalities** are why banks are among the most regulated firms. The main tools are capital requirements, liquidity requirements, supervision, deposit insurance and a resolution regime, supplemented by the central bank acting as **lender of last resort**. In the US, the Federal Reserve, the OCC and the FDIC supervise banks, and the CFPB oversees consumer finance (check its current scope).

### Capital: Basel III

The Basel Committee's standards, implemented in the US by the banking agencies, measure capital against **risk-weighted assets (RWA)**: each asset's amount times a risk weight (for example 0% for cash and government securities, 50% for many residential mortgages, 100% for most corporate loans; the weights here are illustrative).

| Requirement (Basel III minimums) | Ratio of RWA |
|---|---|
| Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1) | 4.5% |
| Tier 1 (CET1 plus additional Tier 1) | 6.0% |
| Total capital (Tier 1 plus Tier 2) | 8.0% |
| Capital conservation buffer (CET1) | 2.5% on top |

With the conservation buffer, the effective requirements are 7.0% CET1, 8.5% Tier 1 and 10.5% total capital; a bank below them faces limits on dividends and bonuses. There are also a countercyclical buffer (set by national authorities) and surcharges for globally systemic banks. The **leverage ratio** ignores risk weights: Tier 1 capital divided by total exposure, with a Basel minimum of 3%, a backstop against models that understate risk. US regulators also run annual **stress tests** of large banks and use **prompt corrective action** categories (well capitalized, adequately capitalized and so on) that trigger supervisory steps as capital falls.

### Liquidity

The **liquidity coverage ratio (LCR)** requires enough **high-quality liquid assets (HQLA)** to cover net cash outflows over a 30-day stress:

$$\text{LCR} = \frac{\text{HQLA}}{\text{Net cash outflows over 30 days}} \ge 100\%,$$

where net outflows equal expected outflows minus inflows, with inflows capped at 75% of outflows. The **net stable funding ratio (NSFR)** requires available stable funding to be at least required stable funding over one year, limiting the use of short-term funding for long-term assets.

### Deposit insurance

The FDIC, created by the Banking Act of 1933, insures deposits up to **\$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category**. Categories include single accounts, joint accounts (each co-owner is insured up to \$250,000 of their share), certain retirement accounts and trusts. The insurance covers deposits (checking, savings, certificates of deposit) but not stocks, bonds, mutual funds or crypto assets. It is funded by premiums paid by banks into the Deposit Insurance Fund, not by taxes. Credit unions have a parallel federal insurer, the NCUA.

Insurance prevents runs by insured depositors, but creates **moral hazard**: insured depositors have less reason to monitor the bank's risk. Regulators respond with risk-based premiums, capital rules and supervision. Limits and rules change; check fdic.gov for current ones.

@example
title: Capital ratios and the buffer
problem: A bank has \$50 million cash and \$150 million Treasuries (0% risk weight), \$300 million residential mortgages (50%), \$400 million commercial loans (100%) and \$100 million other assets (100%). CET1 capital is \$45 million, additional Tier 1 is \$7 million and Tier 2 is \$13 million. Find the ratios and compare them with the minimums plus the conservation buffer.
step: RWA $= 0 + 0 + 0.50 \times 300 + 400 + 100 = \$650$ million.
step: CET1 ratio $= 45/650 = 6.92\%$; Tier 1 $= 52/650 = 8.00\%$; total capital $= 65/650 = 10.00\%$.
step: Against the minimums (4.5%, 6.0%, 8.0%) it passes, but with the 2.5% buffer the targets are 7.0%, 8.5% and 10.5%, and it falls short on all three, so distributions are restricted.
step: Leverage ratio $= 52/1{,}000 = 5.2\%$ (taking exposure as total assets), above the 3% minimum.
answer: CET1 6.92%, Tier 1 8.00%, total 10.00%; below the buffered targets.

@example
title: FDIC coverage
problem: A depositor has \$180,000 in a single-ownership checking account and \$120,000 in a single-ownership certificate of deposit at one bank, plus \$400,000 in a joint account with a spouse at the same bank. How much is insured?
step: Both single-ownership accounts fall in one category: $180{,}000 + 120{,}000 = \$300{,}000$, insured up to \$250,000.
step: The joint account is a separate category. Each of two co-owners is insured up to \$250,000, so \$500,000 in total, which covers \$400,000.
step: Insured total $= 250{,}000 + 400{,}000 = \$650{,}000$; uninsured $= \$50{,}000$.
answer: \$650,000 insured; \$50,000 uninsured.

@mistakes
- Treating the \$250,000 limit as per account; it is per depositor, per bank, per ownership category.
- Using total assets instead of risk-weighted assets in the denominator of the CET1 ratio.
- Forgetting the capital conservation buffer when comparing a ratio with the requirement.
- Counting the 75% cap on LCR inflows incorrectly: inflows count only up to 75% of outflows.
