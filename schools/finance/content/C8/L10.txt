@article
A fund pools money from many investors and invests it under one strategy. Investors get diversification and professional management, and pay fees. Funds differ most in **who can invest**, **how investors get in and out**, and **how managers are paid**.

### Mutual funds

An open-end **mutual fund** is a pooled vehicle registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940. It issues and redeems shares every day at the **net asset value (NAV)**, calculated once after the close:

$$\text{NAV per share} = \frac{\text{Total assets} - \text{Liabilities}}{\text{Shares outstanding}}.$$

Orders placed during the day get that day's closing NAV (forward pricing). Costs include the **expense ratio** (annual fees as a percent of assets, charged continuously inside the NAV) and sometimes sales **loads**. A **closed-end fund** instead issues a fixed number of shares that trade on an exchange, often at a premium or discount to NAV.

### Exchange-traded funds

An **ETF** is a fund whose shares trade on an exchange all day at a market price. Large financial firms called **authorized participants (APs)** create and redeem ETF shares in blocks (creation units), usually in kind: to create, an AP delivers the basket of underlying securities to the fund and receives ETF shares; to redeem, it returns ETF shares and receives the basket. This arbitrage keeps the price near NAV:

- If the ETF trades at a **premium** to NAV, APs buy the basket, create shares and sell them, pushing the price down.
- At a **discount**, APs buy ETF shares, redeem them for the basket and sell the securities, pushing the price up.

Premium or discount $= (\text{Price} - \text{NAV})/\text{NAV}$. In-kind redemption also generally lets ETFs avoid distributing capital gains, a tax feature that varies by case and jurisdiction.

### Hedge funds

Hedge funds are private pools sold to wealthy or institutional investors and generally exempt from registration as investment companies, with less disclosure and more freedom: leverage, short selling, derivatives. Redemptions are limited (lockups, notice periods, gates). The classic fee is **"2 and 20"**: a management fee of about 2% of assets and an incentive fee of about 20% of profits, often subject to a **hurdle** (a minimum return before incentive fees) and a **high-water mark** (fees only on gains above the previous peak value). Terms vary widely.

A common convention, used in this course: the management fee is charged on beginning assets; the incentive fee applies to the return after the management fee, in excess of the hurdle (a hard hurdle).

### Private equity

A private equity fund is a limited partnership: **limited partners (LPs)** commit capital, and the **general partner (GP)** invests it, drawing the commitments over several years (capital calls) and distributing proceeds when investments are sold. The GP earns a management fee and **carried interest**, typically about 20% of profits, after the LPs have received their capital back and often a preferred return (about 8%). Returns are shown by the **IRR** and by multiples: **DPI** (distributions divided by paid-in capital) and **TVPI** (distributions plus remaining value, divided by paid-in capital). Early fees and writedowns make the interim return curve dip before rising, the **J-curve**. Holdings are illiquid and valued infrequently.

### Comparing structures

| | Mutual fund | ETF | Hedge fund | Private equity |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Investors | Public | Public | Qualified investors | Qualified investors |
| Liquidity | Daily at NAV | Intraday on exchange | Periodic, with limits | Locked for years |
| Fee style | Expense ratio | Expense ratio | Management plus incentive | Management plus carry |

@example
title: NAV and fees
problem: A fund holds securities worth \$480 million and cash of \$20 million, owes \$5 million in liabilities and has 25 million shares. A hedge fund starts the year at 100, earns a gross return of 14%, charges a 2% management fee on beginning assets and a 20% incentive fee on the return above a 4% hard hurdle (after the management fee). Find the mutual fund's NAV and the hedge fund's net return.
step: NAV per share $= (480 + 20 - 5)/25 = 495/25 = \$19.80$.
step: Hedge fund: gross gain 14, less the management fee 2, leaves 12 (12% after management fee).
step: Excess over the hurdle: $12 - 4 = 8$; incentive fee $= 0.20 \times 8 = 1.6$.
step: Net gain $= 12 - 1.6 = 10.4$, so the net return is 10.4%.
answer: NAV \$19.80; hedge fund net return 10.4%.

@example
title: The fee drag
problem: An investor puts \$10,000 in a fund earning a gross return of 7% a year for 25 years, with annual compounding and with the expense ratio subtracted from the annual return. Compare an expense ratio of 0.75% with 0.10%.
step: Net returns: $7\% - 0.75\% = 6.25\%$ and $7\% - 0.10\% = 6.90\%$.
step: End values: $10{,}000 \times 1.0625^{25} = \$45{,}522$ and $10{,}000 \times 1.069^{25} = \$53{,}020$.
step: The 0.65-point difference in fees costs about \$7,498 after 25 years, 14% of the cheaper fund's final value.
answer: \$45,522 versus \$53,020, a difference of about \$7,498.

@mistakes
- Computing NAV without subtracting liabilities, or using the number of shares at the wrong date.
- Assuming an ETF's price always equals NAV; it can deviate, especially in stressed or illiquid markets.
- Charging the incentive fee before the management fee, or on the whole return when a hurdle applies.
- Treating private equity IRR and a public fund's time-weighted return as directly comparable.

model:
```python
def net_return(gross, mgmt, perf, hurdle=0.0):
    """Net return after a management fee and an incentive fee over a hurdle."""
    after_mgmt = gross - mgmt
    incentive = perf * max(0.0, after_mgmt - hurdle)
    return after_mgmt - incentive
```
hint: Subtract the management fee first.
hint: The incentive fee is `perf` times the excess over the hurdle, floored at zero.
solution: After the management fee, the incentive fee applies only to the part of the return above the hurdle, and never when the return is below the hurdle or negative. The fourth test checks that losses are not given back as negative fees.
