@article
Executing a trade only agrees a price. Before the buyer owns the shares and the seller has the cash, two further steps must happen, and they are where much of the system's safety is built.

### The life of a trade

1. **Order and routing.** The investor instructs a broker, which routes the order to a venue (week 5).
2. **Execution.** The venue matches the order and reports the trade.
3. **Confirmation and affirmation.** The parties agree the details: security, quantity, price, settlement date.
4. **Clearing.** The trade is matched, checked and, in a cleared market, passed to a **central counterparty (CCP)**, which calculates what each member owes.
5. **Settlement.** Securities and cash are exchanged and ownership is recorded in the books of a depository.

In US equities, the National Securities Clearing Corporation (NSCC) is the CCP and the Depository Trust Company (DTC) is the central securities depository; both are subsidiaries of DTCC. Since May 28, 2024, US stock trades settle on **T+1**: one business day after the trade date $T$. Settlement uses **delivery versus payment (DVP)**: securities are delivered only if payment is made, which removes the risk that one side gives up its asset and gets nothing.

### Novation and netting

Through **novation**, the CCP becomes the buyer to every seller and the seller to every buyer. A party's exposure is then to the CCP, not to a stranger. With **multilateral netting**, each member's trades in a security are summed into one net position, so far fewer shares and less cash move. If the gross value of trades is $G$ and the net amount settled is $N$, netting eliminates the fraction $1 - N/G$ of obligations.

### Managing counterparty risk

If a member fails before settlement, the CCP must still deliver to the other side and replace the position at the current market price. The **replacement cost** of a failed purchase is $\max(0,\ \text{quantity} \times (P_{\text{now}} - P_{\text{contract}}))$ for the buyer who must re-buy. CCPs protect themselves with:

- **Margin** collected from members (initial margin for potential future losses, variation margin for daily moves),
- a **default fund** contributed by members, and
- strict membership and risk rules, under SEC oversight.

Derivatives work similarly: since the 2007–2009 crisis, Dodd-Frank has required many standardized swaps to be cleared through CCPs, supervised by the CFTC and the SEC. A **settlement fail** is a trade that does not settle on time; fails are costly, and rules require them to be closed out.

### Market structure

Equity trading is fragmented across **lit exchanges** (displayed quotes), **ATSs and dark pools** (undisplayed liquidity) and **wholesale market makers**, who often execute retail orders by internalizing them. Some brokers receive **payment for order flow (PFOF)** from wholesalers; this creates a conflict of interest that the SEC addresses with disclosure rules and best-execution duties, and policymakers debate whether it benefits or harms investors. Regulation NMS and the NBBO link the venues.

### Calendar convention

Settlement dates count **business days**, which exclude weekends and days when the markets are closed. A trade on Friday settles on Monday.

@example
title: Netting among three firms
problem: In one stock at \$20.00, Firm A buys 1,000 shares from Firm B, Firm B buys 600 shares from Firm C, and Firm C buys 700 shares from Firm A. Find each firm's net position and the share of gross cash eliminated by netting.
step: Net shares: A $= +1{,}000 - 700 = +300$; B $= -1{,}000 + 600 = -400$; C $= -600 + 700 = +100$. They sum to zero.
step: Net cash: A pays \$20,000 and receives \$14,000, so $-\$6{,}000$; B receives \$20,000 and pays \$12,000, so $+\$8{,}000$; C receives \$12,000 and pays \$14,000, so $-\$2{,}000$.
step: Without netting, 2,300 shares and \$46,000 of gross payments move. With netting, B delivers 400 shares to the CCP, which delivers 300 to A and 100 to C, and net cash of \$8,000 moves to B.
step: Gross payments are \$46,000 against \$8,000 net, so netting eliminates $1 - 8/46 = 82.6\%$.
answer: A +300 shares, B -400, C +100; netting eliminates about 82.6% of gross cash movement.

@example
title: Settlement dates around a holiday
problem: Suppose US markets and settlement systems are closed on Thursday, November 26, 2026. When do trades made on Wednesday, November 25 and on Friday, November 6, 2026 settle under T+1?
step: November 25, 2026 is a Wednesday. The next business day is not Thursday (closed), so it is Friday, November 27.
step: November 6, 2026 is a Friday. The next business day is Monday, November 9.
step: Settlement is T+1 in business days, not calendar days.
answer: November 27, 2026 and November 9, 2026.

@mistakes
- Treating execution as settlement: the trade date and the settlement date are different, and ownership is recorded at settlement.
- Counting calendar days for T+1 instead of business days.
- Assuming the CCP removes all risk; it concentrates and manages counterparty risk using margin and a default fund, and the CCP itself must be well supervised.
- Thinking netting changes what firms owe in total: it reduces the number of transfers, not the net economic position.

@quiz self
prompt: A retail investor places a limit order to buy 100 shares through an online broker, and the order executes. Trace the order from placement to settlement, naming each step, who performs it and when ownership and cash change hands. Assume US equities and T+1.
hint: Order, routing, execution, confirmation, clearing, settlement.
solution: The investor sends the order to the broker, which checks the account and routes it to a venue (an exchange, an ATS or a wholesale market maker) under its best-execution duty. The venue matches the order against a seller at the limit price or better and reports the trade. The broker confirms the trade, and the details are affirmed and submitted for clearing. The CCP (NSCC) novates the trade, becomes counterparty to both sides and nets it with other trades of the same member. On the next business day (T+1) settlement occurs at the depository (DTC) on a delivery-versus-payment basis: shares move to the investor's broker, cash moves to the seller's broker, and the investor becomes the beneficial owner (the broker holds the shares in street name). The investor's economic risk starts at execution, but legal settlement happens at T+1.
