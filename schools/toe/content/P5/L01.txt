@article
A bead on a wire has one real freedom: it can only slide along the wire. Describing it by three Cartesian coordinates $(x,y,z)$ hides that fact. Analytical mechanics begins by choosing coordinates that match what the system can actually do.

### Degrees of freedom

The number of **degrees of freedom** is the number of independent real numbers needed to fix the configuration of the system at one instant. $N$ free particles in space have $3N$. Every independent constraint (a rule the particles must obey, such as a rigid rod of fixed length) removes one. Two particles joined by a rigid rod therefore have $3\cdot 2 - 1 = 5$ degrees of freedom.

### Generalized coordinates

Pick $n$ independent variables $q_1,\dots,q_n$, one per degree of freedom, that fix the configuration. These are **generalized coordinates**. They may be lengths, angles, or anything convenient. The Cartesian positions become functions of them:

$$\mathbf r_a = \mathbf r_a(q_1,\dots,q_n,t).$$

For a plane pendulum of length $\ell$: $x=\ell\sin\theta$, $y=-\ell\cos\theta$, and the single coordinate is the angle $\theta$.

The set of all allowed values of $(q_1,\dots,q_n)$ is the **configuration space**. For the pendulum it is a circle; for a free particle in a plane it is the plane itself; for a double pendulum (two angles) it is a torus.

### Generalized velocities and kinetic energy

Differentiate $\mathbf r_a(q,t)$ with the chain rule:

$$\dot{\mathbf r}_a=\sum_i \frac{\partial \mathbf r_a}{\partial q_i}\,\dot q_i+\frac{\partial \mathbf r_a}{\partial t}.$$

When the positions have no explicit time dependence, the kinetic energy $T=\tfrac12\sum_a m_a|\dot{\mathbf r}_a|^2$ becomes a quadratic form in the generalized velocities:

$$T=\tfrac12\sum_{i,j}M_{ij}(q)\,\dot q_i\dot q_j,\qquad M_{ij}=\sum_a m_a\frac{\partial \mathbf r_a}{\partial q_i}\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf r_a}{\partial q_j}.$$

In plane polar coordinates, $x=r\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\phi$ give

$$T=\tfrac12 m\left(\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\phi^{2}\right).$$

The speed in the $\phi$ direction is $r\dot\phi$, not $\dot\phi$, because $\dot\phi$ is an angular rate. The cross term vanishes because the radial and angular directions are perpendicular.

### Choosing coordinates

Let the symmetry of the problem choose: an angle for motion on a circle, polar coordinates when the force points to a centre, and so on. A good choice makes $T$ and the potential energy $V$ short, and it builds the constraints in so that no further bookkeeping is needed. The coordinates $q_i$ and the velocities $\dot q_i$ will later be treated as independent variables, which is the key to the Lagrangian method.

@example
title: Kinetic energy of a double pendulum
problem: Masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ hang from massless rigid rods of lengths $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$. The first rod is pivoted at a fixed point and the second hangs from $m_1$. The motion is in a vertical plane, and $\theta_1,\theta_2$ are the rod angles from the downward vertical. Count the degrees of freedom and write $T$ in terms of $\theta_1,\theta_2,\dot\theta_1,\dot\theta_2$.
step: Two particles in a plane have $2\cdot 2=4$ Cartesian coordinates. The two rod lengths give two constraints, so there are $4-2=2$ degrees of freedom, matching the two angles.
step: Put the origin at the pivot with $y$ upward: $x_1=\ell_1\sin\theta_1$, $y_1=-\ell_1\cos\theta_1$, $x_2=x_1+\ell_2\sin\theta_2$, $y_2=y_1-\ell_2\cos\theta_2$.
step: Differentiate: $\dot x_1=\ell_1\cos\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1$, $\dot y_1=\ell_1\sin\theta_1\,\dot\theta_1$, $\dot x_2=\dot x_1+\ell_2\cos\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2$, $\dot y_2=\dot y_1+\ell_2\sin\theta_2\,\dot\theta_2$.
step: Mass 1: $\dot x_1^2+\dot y_1^2=\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2$. Mass 2: $\dot x_2^2+\dot y_2^2=\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+\ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2+2\ell_1\ell_2\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\left(\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2+\sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2\right)$, and the bracket equals $\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)$.
step: Assemble $T=\tfrac12 m_1(\dot x_1^2+\dot y_1^2)+\tfrac12 m_2(\dot x_2^2+\dot y_2^2)$:
$$T=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\ell_1^2\dot\theta_1^2+\tfrac12 m_2\ell_2^2\dot\theta_2^2+m_2\ell_1\ell_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\,\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2.$$ The cross term couples the two angles.

@example
title: Degrees of freedom of a rigid triangle
problem: Three point masses sit at the corners of a rigid triangle that moves freely in three-dimensional space. How many degrees of freedom does it have?
step: Three free particles have $3\cdot 3=9$ Cartesian coordinates.
step: Rigidity fixes the three pairwise distances, which are three independent constraints, so $9-3=6$.
step: Interpretation: three numbers locate a reference point and three angles fix the orientation. Adding more particles to a rigid body adds no new freedom, because each new particle is fixed relative to the triangle. Every rigid body whose particles do not all lie on one line has 6 degrees of freedom (week 9).

@mistakes
- Counting all the Cartesian coordinates of the particles and forgetting to subtract the constraints.
- Writing the polar kinetic energy as $\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+\dot\phi^2)$, forgetting that the speed in the $\phi$ direction is $r\dot\phi$.
- Keeping more coordinates than degrees of freedom (say both $x$ and $y$ of a pendulum bob) and treating them as independent.

lead: $T =$
answer: m*(v_r^2 + r^2*omega^2)/2
check: lambda m, r, v_r, omega: 0.5*m*(nderiv(lambda t: (r + v_r*t)*cos(0.7 + omega*t), 0)**2 + nderiv(lambda t: (r + v_r*t)*sin(0.7 + omega*t), 0)**2)
hint: Start from $x=r\cos\phi$, $y=r\sin\phi$ and differentiate with the product rule.
hint: The velocity has a radial part $\dot r$ and a perpendicular part of size $r\dot\phi$.
solution: $\dot x=\dot r\cos\phi-r\dot\phi\sin\phi$ and $\dot y=\dot r\sin\phi+r\dot\phi\cos\phi$. Then $\dot x^2+\dot y^2=\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2$, so $T=\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)$.

@quiz num
prompt: Three particles move in three-dimensional space. Particle 1 is fixed at the origin, particle 2 must stay a distance $d$ from particle 1, and particle 3 must stay on the plane $z=0$. How many degrees of freedom does the system have?
answer: 4
check: 3*3 - (3 + 1 + 1)
hint: Start from $9$ Cartesian coordinates and count how many each condition removes.
hint: Fixing a particle at a point removes 3, a fixed distance removes 1, and staying on a plane removes 1.
solution: $9-3-1-1=4$. Particle 2 has 2 angles on its sphere and particle 3 has 2 coordinates in its plane.

@quiz expr
prompt: A bead of mass $m$ slides on the helical wire $x=R\cos\phi$, $y=R\sin\phi$, $z=b\phi$, with $R$ and $b$ constants. Using $\phi$ as the generalized coordinate and $\omega=\dot\phi$, write the kinetic energy.
vars: m, R, b, omega
lead: $T =$
answer: m*(R^2 + b^2)*omega^2/2
check: lambda m, R, b, omega: 0.5*m*sum(nderiv(f, 0)**2 for f in [lambda t: R*cos(0.3 + omega*t), lambda t: R*sin(0.3 + omega*t), lambda t: b*(0.3 + omega*t)])
hint: Differentiate $x$, $y$ and $z$ with respect to time using $\dot\phi=\omega$.
hint: $\dot x^2+\dot y^2=R^2\omega^2$ and $\dot z=b\omega$.
solution: $\dot x=-R\omega\sin\phi$, $\dot y=R\omega\cos\phi$, $\dot z=b\omega$, so $v^2=(R^2+b^2)\omega^2$ and $T=\tfrac12 m(R^2+b^2)\omega^2$.
