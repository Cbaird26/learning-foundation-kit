@lesson
title: Euler–Lagrange equations
summary: Form the Lagrangian of a system, apply the Euler–Lagrange equations to its action, and recover the equations of motion.
objective: State Hamilton's principle and write the action as the time integral of $L=T-V$.
objective: Derive equations of motion from $L$ by computing $\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ and $\partial L/\partial q_i$ correctly.
objective: Recognise that different Lagrangians can give the same motion.
reading: David Tong, Classical Dynamics notes: look up the principle of least action and the Euler–Lagrange equations in the Lagrangian formulation material.

@article
Newton's laws need forces. For a bead on a wire or a pendulum the forces include unknown constraint forces, and the vector bookkeeping is a nuisance. The Lagrangian method replaces all of it with one scalar function and one recipe.

### Hamilton's principle

For generalized coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_n$ define the **Lagrangian**

$$L(q,\dot q,t)=T-V,$$

the kinetic energy minus the potential energy, and the **action**

$$S[q]=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}L\big(q(t),\dot q(t),t\big)\,dt.$$

**Hamilton's principle:** between fixed configurations $q(t_1)$ and $q(t_2)$, the motion that actually occurs makes $S$ stationary. (Calling it "least action" is common, but the correct word is stationary.)

### The Euler–Lagrange equations

Last week's derivation applies to each coordinate in turn, with $x\to t$, $y\to q_i$, $F\to L$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}=0,\qquad i=1,\dots,n.$$

You get one second-order equation per degree of freedom, and no constraint forces appear. The quantity $p_i=\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$ is the **generalized momentum** conjugate to $q_i$. When $L$ is built from a potential, $\partial L/\partial q_i$ plays the role of a generalized force.

### Check against Newton

For one particle in one dimension, $L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2-V(x)$ gives $p=m\dot x$ and $\partial L/\partial x=-V'(x)$, so

$$m\ddot x=-V'(x),$$

which is Newton's second law. For a pendulum of length $\ell$, with $\theta$ from the downward vertical, $T=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2$ and $V=-mg\ell\cos\theta$, so $L=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\cos\theta$ and the equation is $m\ell^2\ddot\theta=-mg\ell\sin\theta$.

### The recipe

1. Choose independent coordinates $q_i$, one per degree of freedom.
2. Write $T$ and $V$ in terms of $q_i$ and $\dot q_i$.
3. Form $L=T-V$.
4. For each $i$ compute $\partial L/\partial\dot q_i$, then take its **total** time derivative (through $q_j(t)$, $\dot q_j(t)$ and any explicit $t$), and compute $\partial L/\partial q_i$.
5. Set the difference to zero and tidy.

### The Lagrangian is not unique

Multiplying $L$ by a nonzero constant, adding a constant, or adding a total time derivative $\frac{d}{dt}f(q,t)$ leaves the equations of motion unchanged. The last one changes $S$ only by a boundary term, which does not affect which path is stationary. So different Lagrangians can describe identical motion.

@example
title: The Atwood machine
problem: Masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ hang on either side of a light frictionless pulley, joined by an inextensible string of length $\ell$. Use the Euler–Lagrange equation to find the acceleration.
step: The string length is a holonomic constraint, so there is one degree of freedom. Let $x$ be the depth of $m_1$ below the pulley, measured downward; then $m_2$ is at depth $\ell-x$ and moves with speed $|\dot x|$.
step: $T=\tfrac12 m_1\dot x^2+\tfrac12 m_2\dot x^2$. With zero potential energy at the pulley, $V=-m_1gx-m_2g(\ell-x)$.
step: $L=T-V=\tfrac12(m_1+m_2)\dot x^2+(m_1-m_2)gx+m_2g\ell$.
step: $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}=(m_1+m_2)\dot x$, so $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}=(m_1+m_2)\ddot x$. Also $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial x}=(m_1-m_2)g$.
step: The equation $(m_1+m_2)\ddot x=(m_1-m_2)g$ gives $\ddot x=\dfrac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}\,g$. The string tension never appeared.

@example
title: Equation of motion for the rotating hoop
problem: A bead of mass $m$ slides on a hoop of radius $R$ that rotates at constant angular speed $\omega$ about a vertical diameter, in gravity $g$. With $\theta$ measured from the downward vertical, $T=\tfrac12 mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta)$. Derive the equation of motion from the action.
step: The bead's height is $z=-R\cos\theta$, so $V=-mgR\cos\theta$.
step: $L=\tfrac12 mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta)+mgR\cos\theta$, and the action is $S=\int L\,dt$.
step: $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}=mR^2\dot\theta$, so $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\dot\theta}=mR^2\ddot\theta$.
step: $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\theta}=mR^2\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta-mgR\sin\theta$.
step: Equate: $mR^2\ddot\theta=mR^2\omega^2\sin\theta\cos\theta-mgR\sin\theta$, so
$$\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\left(\omega^2\cos\theta-\frac gR\right).$$
step: Check: for $\omega=0$ this is the pendulum equation $\ddot\theta=-(g/R)\sin\theta$.

@mistakes
- Writing $L=T+V$, or getting the sign of $V$ wrong (here $V=-mg\ell\cos\theta$ for $\theta$ measured from the downward vertical).
- Taking $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q}$ as if $q$ and $\dot q$ were constants, so that terms like $2r\dot r\dot\theta$ are lost.
- Treating $q$ and $\dot q$ as dependent when taking the partial derivatives: $\partial L/\partial q$ holds $\dot q$ fixed, and $\partial L/\partial\dot q$ holds $q$ fixed.

@practice num
prompt: A particle has $L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2-V(x)$ with $m=2\ \text{kg}$. Find its generalized momentum $p=\partial L/\partial\dot x$ when $\dot x=3\ \text{m/s}$.
answer: 6
unit: kg m/s
check: nderiv(lambda w: 0.5*2*w**2, 3)
hint: Differentiate $L$ with respect to $\dot x$, holding $x$ fixed. The potential $V(x)$ does not depend on $\dot x$.
solution: $p=\partial L/\partial\dot x=m\dot x=2\cdot3=6\ \text{kg m/s}$.

choice*: $L=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\cos\theta$
choice: $L=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2-mg\ell\cos\theta$
choice: $L=\tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\sin\theta$
choice: $L=\tfrac12 m\ell\dot\theta^2+mg\ell\cos\theta$

With $\theta$ measured from the downward vertical, the Lagrangian is $L=\tfrac12 mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta)+mgR\cos\theta$. Find $\ddot\theta$ at the instant when $\theta=\pi/3$.
answer: -1.386
unit: rad/s^2
tol: 0.01
check: nderiv(lambda th: 0.5*0.2*0.5**2*(6**2*sin(th)**2) + 0.2*9.8*0.5*cos(th), pi/3)/(0.2*0.5**2)

@practice self
prompt: A mass $m$ hangs from a spring of natural length $\ell_0$ and stiffness $k$, and the spring is free to swing in a vertical plane about its fixed upper end. Use the length $r$ of the spring and the angle $\theta$ from the downward vertical as coordinates. Starting from the action, derive the two equations of motion.
hint: Write $T$ in plane polar coordinates with $r$ the spring length, and $V$ as gravitational plus elastic energy.
hint: Form $L=T-V$, then apply the Euler–Lagrange equation once for $r$ and once for $\theta$. For $\theta$, differentiate $mr^2\dot\theta$ with the product rule.
solution: The speed squared in polar coordinates is $\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2$, so $T=\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)$. The potential energy is gravitational plus elastic, $V=-mgr\cos\theta+\tfrac12k(r-\ell_0)^2$. So $L=\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+mgr\cos\theta-\tfrac12k(r-\ell_0)^2$.
For $r$: $\frac{d}{dt}(m\dot r)=m\ddot r$ and $\partial L/\partial r=mr\dot\theta^2+mg\cos\theta-k(r-\ell_0)$, so $m\ddot r=mr\dot\theta^2+mg\cos\theta-k(r-\ell_0)$.
For $\theta$: $\frac{d}{dt}(mr^2\dot\theta)=mr^2\ddot\theta+2mr\dot r\dot\theta$ and $\partial L/\partial\theta=-mgr\sin\theta$, so $r\ddot\theta+2\dot r\dot\theta=-g\sin\theta$.
rubric: Writes $T$ in polar form and $V$ with both the gravitational and the elastic parts, with correct signs.
rubric: Forms $L=T-V$ explicitly before differentiating.
rubric: Obtains the radial equation including the centrifugal-type term $mr\dot\theta^2$ and the spring force.
rubric: Differentiates $mr^2\dot\theta$ with respect to time using the product rule, giving the $2mr\dot r\dot\theta$ term.

@quiz expr
prompt: A bead of mass $m$ slides without friction on the wire $y=ax^2$ in a vertical plane, with gravity $g$ acting in the $-y$ direction. Using $x$ as the coordinate and $v=\dot x$, find the acceleration $\ddot x$ from the Euler–Lagrange equation.
vars: a, g, x, v
lead: $\ddot x =$
answer: -(4*a^2*x*v^2 + 2*a*g*x)/(1 + 4*a^2*x^2)
check: lambda a, g, x, v: -(0.5*nderiv(lambda s: 1 + 4*a**2*s**2, x)*v**2 + nderiv(lambda s: g*a*s**2, x))/(1 + 4*a**2*x**2)
hint: Write $T=\tfrac12 m(1+4a^2x^2)\dot x^2$ and $V=mgax^2$, then form $L$.
hint: The total time derivative of $\partial L/\partial\dot x=m(1+4a^2x^2)\dot x$ contains both $\ddot x$ and $\dot x^2$ terms.
solution: $L=\tfrac12 m(1+4a^2x^2)\dot x^2-mgax^2$. Then $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x}=m(1+4a^2x^2)\ddot x+8ma^2x\dot x^2$ and $\frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=4ma^2x\dot x^2-2mgax$. Equating and cancelling $m$: $(1+4a^2x^2)\ddot x+4a^2x\dot x^2+2gax=0$, so $\ddot x=-\dfrac{4a^2x\dot x^2+2agx}{1+4a^2x^2}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Let $L=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2-V(x)$. Select **all** the Lagrangians that give the same equation of motion as $L$.
choice*: $L'=L+c$ for a constant $c$
choice*: $L'=3L$
choice*: $L'=L+\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(x^2\right)$
choice: $L'=T+V$
choice: $L'=L+x\dot x^2$
hint: A constant factor multiplies the whole Euler–Lagrange equation, and a total time derivative of a function of $x$ changes the action only by boundary terms.
solution: Adding a constant, multiplying by a nonzero constant, and adding the total derivative $\frac{d}{dt}f(x,t)$ all leave the equation of motion unchanged. $T+V$ gives $m\ddot x=+V'$, the wrong sign. The term $x\dot x^2$ is not a total time derivative; it changes the equation to $(m+2x)\ddot x+\dot x^2+V'=0$.
