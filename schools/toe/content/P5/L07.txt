@article
A planet circling the Sun moves in a plane, yet its distance from the Sun behaves like a bead rolling along a one-dimensional landscape. That landscape is the **effective potential**, and reading it is the fastest way to understand every central-force orbit.

### From two variables to one

For a particle of mass $m$ in a central potential $V(r)$, the polar-coordinate Lagrangian is

$$L=\tfrac12 m\left(\dot r^{2}+r^{2}\dot\theta^{2}\right)-V(r).$$

The coordinate $\theta$ is cyclic, so $\ell=mr^{2}\dot\theta$ is conserved (the angular momentum of weeks 5 and 6). Energy is conserved too. Replace $\dot\theta=\ell/(mr^{2})$ in the energy:

$$E=\tfrac12 m\dot r^{2}+U_{\text{eff}}(r),\qquad U_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2mr^{2}}.$$

The second term is just the angular kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mr^{2}\dot\theta^{2}$ rewritten with $\ell$. It grows without bound as $r\to0$, so for a potential less singular than $1/r^{2}$ (such as $-k/r$) a particle with $\ell\neq0$ meets a **centrifugal barrier** and never reaches the centre. Differentiating the energy equation in time and dividing by $\dot r$ gives the radial equation of motion

$$m\ddot r=-\frac{dU_{\text{eff}}}{dr}.$$

### Reading the graph

Since $\tfrac12 m\dot r^{2}\ge0$, motion is allowed only where $E\ge U_{\text{eff}}(r)$.

- **Turning points** solve $E=U_{\text{eff}}(r)$; there $\dot r=0$.
- If $E$ cuts the graph at two turning points around a well, $r$ oscillates between them: a **bound orbit**.
- If the allowed region reaches $r\to\infty$, the orbit is **unbounded**.
- If $E$ equals the bottom of the well, $r$ stays fixed: a **circular orbit**.

### Circular orbits and stability

A circular orbit at radius $r_0$ needs $\ddot r=0$, so

$$U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0)=0.$$

It is stable when this equilibrium is a minimum, $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)>0$. A small radial nudge then oscillates at angular frequency

$$\omega_r=\sqrt{\frac{U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)}{m}},$$

which is the one-dimensional small-oscillation result that week 8 generalises. Meanwhile the particle circulates at $\omega_\theta=\ell/(mr_0^{2})$. A near-circular orbit closes when $\omega_r/\omega_\theta$ is a rational number.

### The Kepler potential

For $V=-k/r$ with $k>0$,

$$U_{\text{eff}}=-\frac{k}{r}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{2mr^{2}},\qquad r_0=\frac{\ell^{2}}{mk},\qquad U_{\text{eff}}(r_0)=-\frac{mk^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}.$$

Bound orbits therefore have $-mk^{2}/(2\ell^{2})\le E<0$; $E=0$ is a parabola and $E>0$ a hyperbola. At $r_0$ one finds $\omega_r=\omega_\theta$, which is why Kepler orbits close. For the attractive power law $V=-k/r^{n}$ (with $k,n>0$) the stability test gives $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)=nk(2-n)\,r_0^{-n-2}$, so circular orbits are stable only for $n<2$.

**Warning:** apply the Euler–Lagrange equation to $L$ first. Substituting $\dot\theta=\ell/(mr^{2})$ into $L$ before varying $r$ flips the sign of the centrifugal term.

@example
title: A circular orbit in the Kepler potential
problem: A particle of mass $m=1\ \text{kg}$ moves in $V(r)=-k/r$ with $k=4\ \text{J m}$ and angular momentum $\ell=4\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$. Find the circular-orbit radius, its energy, and the angular frequency of small radial oscillations about it.
step: Build the effective potential. Since $\ell^{2}/(2m)=8$ in SI units, $U_{\text{eff}}(r)=-\dfrac{4}{r}+\dfrac{8}{r^{2}}$.
step: Set the first derivative to zero. $U_{\text{eff}}'=\dfrac{4}{r^{2}}-\dfrac{16}{r^{3}}=0$ gives $4r=16$, so $r_0=4\ \text{m}$.
step: The energy of the circular orbit is the value of $U_{\text{eff}}$ there: $U_{\text{eff}}(4)=-1+\dfrac12=-\dfrac12\ \text{J}$. This matches $-mk^{2}/(2\ell^{2})=-16/32$.
step: Test stability. $U_{\text{eff}}''=-\dfrac{8}{r^{3}}+\dfrac{48}{r^{4}}$, and at $r=4$ this is $-\dfrac18+\dfrac3{16}=\dfrac1{16}>0$, so the orbit is stable.
step: The radial frequency is $\omega_r=\sqrt{U_{\text{eff}}''/m}=\sqrt{1/16}=\dfrac14\ \text{rad/s}$. The circulation rate is $\omega_\theta=\ell/(mr_0^{2})=4/16=\dfrac14\ \text{rad/s}$, the same value, so nearby orbits close.

@example
title: When is a circular orbit stable?
problem: A particle moves under the attractive potential $V(r)=-k/r^{n}$ with $k>0$ and $n>0$. Show that a circular orbit is stable only if $n<2$.
step: Write the effective potential: $U_{\text{eff}}(r)=-k\,r^{-n}+\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{2m}\,r^{-2}$.
step: Differentiate once: $U_{\text{eff}}'=nk\,r^{-n-1}-\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{m}\,r^{-3}$. Setting this to zero at $r_0$ gives the circular condition $\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{m}=nk\,r_0^{\,2-n}$.
step: Differentiate again: $U_{\text{eff}}''=-n(n+1)k\,r^{-n-2}+\dfrac{3\ell^{2}}{m}\,r^{-4}$.
step: At $r_0$, use the circular condition in the second term: $\dfrac{3\ell^{2}}{m\,r_0^{4}}=3nk\,r_0^{-n-2}$.
step: Combine: $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)=nk\,r_0^{-n-2}\,[\,3-(n+1)\,]=nk(2-n)\,r_0^{-n-2}$. The prefactor is positive, so the sign is the sign of $2-n$: stable exactly when $n<2$. Newtonian gravity ($n=1$) is stable; $n=3$ is not.

@mistakes
- Reading turning points off the graph of $V(r)$ instead of $U_{\text{eff}}(r)$. The centrifugal term depends on $\ell$, so every value of $\ell$ has its own graph.
- Testing stability with $V''(r_0)$. A circular orbit is an equilibrium of $U_{\text{eff}}$, not of $V$.
- Writing the barrier as $\ell/(2mr^{2})$ or $\ell^{2}/(mr^{2})$; it is $\ell^{2}/(2mr^{2})$, the angular kinetic energy.
- Substituting $\dot\theta=\ell/(mr^{2})$ into the Lagrangian before applying the Euler–Lagrange equation for $r$, which gives the wrong sign for the centrifugal force.

answer: 6
check: root(lambda r: nderiv(lambda s: -3/s + 6**2/(2*2*s**2), r), 0.5, 100)
unit: m
hint: A circular orbit sits at a stationary point of $U_{\text{eff}}(r)=V(r)+\ell^{2}/(2mr^{2})$, so $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0)=0$.
hint: Here $U_{\text{eff}}=-3/r+9/r^{2}$. Differentiate and solve for $r$.
solution: $U_{\text{eff}}=-\dfrac3r+\dfrac{36}{4r^{2}}=-\dfrac3r+\dfrac9{r^{2}}$, so $U_{\text{eff}}'=\dfrac3{r^{2}}-\dfrac{18}{r^{3}}=0$ and $r_0=6\ \text{m}$. This agrees with $r_0=\ell^{2}/(mk)=36/6$.

@practice num
prompt: A particle of mass $m=1\ \text{kg}$ moves in $V(r)=-k/r$ with $k=8\ \text{J m}$ and angular momentum $\ell=2\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$. It is on a circular orbit and receives a tiny radial kick. Find the angular frequency of the resulting radial oscillations.
answer: 8
check: (lambda r0: sqrt(nderiv(lambda s: -8/s + 2**2/(2*1*s**2), r0, 2)/1))(root(lambda r: nderiv(lambda s: -8/s + 2**2/(2*1*s**2), r), 0.05, 50))
unit: rad/s
tol: 1%
hint: First find $r_0$ from $U_{\text{eff}}'(r_0)=0$, then use $\omega_r=\sqrt{U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)/m}$.
hint: $U_{\text{eff}}=-8/r+2/r^{2}$, so $r_0=0.5\ \text{m}$. Now compute the second derivative there.
solution: $U_{\text{eff}}'=\dfrac8{r^{2}}-\dfrac4{r^{3}}=0$ gives $r_0=\tfrac12\ \text{m}$. Then $U_{\text{eff}}''=-\dfrac{16}{r^{3}}+\dfrac{12}{r^{4}}$, which at $r_0=\tfrac12$ equals $-128+192=64$. So $\omega_r=\sqrt{64/1}=8\ \text{rad/s}$. As a check, $\omega_\theta=\ell/(mr_0^{2})=2/0.25=8\ \text{rad/s}$ as well.

@practice expr
prompt: A particle of mass $m$ moves in the isotropic harmonic potential $V(r)=\tfrac12 kr^{2}$ with angular momentum $\ell\neq0$. Find the radius $r_0$ of its circular orbit in terms of $\ell$, $m$ and $k$.
vars: ell, m, k
lead: $r_0 =$
answer: (ell^2/(m*k))^(1/4)
check: lambda ell, m, k: root(lambda r: nderiv(lambda s: 0.5*k*s**2 + ell**2/(2*m*s**2), r), 0.05, 20)
domain: ell: 0.5..2; m: 0.5..2; k: 0.5..2
hint: Write $U_{\text{eff}}(r)=\tfrac12 kr^{2}+\ell^{2}/(2mr^{2})$ and set its derivative to zero.
hint: You get $kr=\ell^{2}/(mr^{3})$. Solve for $r^{4}$, then take a fourth root.
solution: $U_{\text{eff}}'=kr-\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{mr^{3}}=0$ gives $r_0^{4}=\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{mk}$, so $r_0=\left(\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{mk}\right)^{1/4}$. Here $U_{\text{eff}}''=k+3\ell^{2}/(mr^{4})=4k>0$, so the orbit is stable.

@practice self
prompt: A particle of mass $m$ moves in $V(r)=-k/r$ with $k>0$, angular momentum $\ell\neq0$ and total energy $E=0$. (a) Find the turning point(s). (b) Describe the radial motion and explain why the particle never reaches $r=0$. (c) Compare the turning point with the circular-orbit radius $r_0$ for the same $\ell$.
hint: Set $E=U_{\text{eff}}(r)$ and solve for $r$.
hint: Compare the sign of $E-U_{\text{eff}}$ for large $r$, just above the root, and as $r\to0$.
solution: With $E=0$ the turning-point condition is $-\dfrac kr+\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{2mr^{2}}=0$, so $r_{\min}=\dfrac{\ell^{2}}{2mk}$, the only root. For $r>r_{\min}$ we have $E-U_{\text{eff}}>0$, so the particle comes in from infinity, stops radially at $r_{\min}$ and moves out again: an unbounded (parabolic) orbit. For $r<r_{\min}$ the barrier term wins, $U_{\text{eff}}>0=E$, so that region is forbidden and $r=0$ is never reached. Since $r_0=\ell^{2}/(mk)$, the turning point is $r_{\min}=r_0/2$.
rubric: Solves $E=U_{\text{eff}}$ to get $r_{\min}=\ell^{2}/(2mk)$ and notes that it is the only turning point.
rubric: Explains that $E\ge U_{\text{eff}}$ for all $r\ge r_{\min}$, so the motion is unbounded: one pass in, one pass out.
rubric: Uses the dominance of the $\ell^{2}/(2mr^{2})$ barrier at small $r$ to explain why $r=0$ is forbidden.
rubric: States that $r_{\min}=r_0/2$.

@quiz ms
prompt: A particle of mass $m$ moves under the attractive potential $V(r)=-k/r^{n}$ with $k>0$. For which exponents can a circular orbit be stable, for a suitable value of $\ell$? Select all that apply.
choice*: $n=1$
choice*: $n=\tfrac32$
choice: $n=3$
choice: $n=4$
hint: At a circular orbit, $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)=nk(2-n)\,r_0^{-n-2}$ for this family of potentials.
hint: A steeper potential is not automatically more stable; the stability test is the sign of $U_{\text{eff}}''$.
solution: Stability needs $U_{\text{eff}}''(r_0)>0$, which requires $2-n>0$, that is $n<2$. So $n=1$ and $n=\tfrac32$ are stable, while $n=3$ and $n=4$ give $U_{\text{eff}}''<0$ and any circular orbit is unstable.
