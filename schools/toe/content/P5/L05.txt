@article
Conservation laws are the most valuable output of the Lagrangian method. Each is a first integral of the motion: an equation with one fewer derivative than Newton's second law, obtained almost for free.

### Cyclic coordinates

A coordinate $q_k$ is **cyclic** (or ignorable) if it does not appear in $L$, although $\dot q_k$ may. Then $\partial L/\partial q_k=0$, and the Euler–Lagrange equation reads

$$\frac{dp_k}{dt}=0,\qquad p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_k}.$$

For a free particle $x$ is cyclic and $p_x=m\dot x$ is conserved. For a particle in a plane with a central potential, $L=\tfrac12 m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\phi^2)-V(r)$ contains no $\phi$, so $p_\phi=mr^2\dot\phi$, the angular momentum, is conserved. In Cartesian coordinates neither $x$ nor $y$ is cyclic there: the symmetry is hidden. Choosing coordinates in which the symmetry is a shift of one coordinate exposes the conservation law.

### The energy function

Define

$$h=\sum_i\dot q_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_i}-L.$$

Along solutions, $\dfrac{dh}{dt}=-\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial t}$. So if $L$ has no explicit time dependence, $h$ is conserved. If in addition the constraints do not depend on time (so $T$ is quadratic in the velocities) and $V$ depends only on positions, then $h=T+V$, the familiar energy. With time-dependent constraints, $h$ can be conserved even though $T+V$ is not, as the second example shows.

### Noether's theorem, simple form

Suppose the shift $q_i\to q_i+\epsilon\,\delta q_i$ leaves $L$ unchanged. To first order in $\epsilon$, and using $\partial L/\partial q_i=\dot p_i$ from the Euler–Lagrange equations,

$$0=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_i}\,\delta q_i+p_i\,\frac{d(\delta q_i)}{dt}\right)=\frac{d}{dt}\sum_i p_i\,\delta q_i.$$

So $Q=\sum_i p_i\,\delta q_i$ is conserved. Translating the whole system along a direction conserves the momentum component along it ($\delta\mathbf r_a=\hat{\mathbf n}$ gives $Q=\hat{\mathbf n}\cdot\sum_a\mathbf p_a$). Rotating about an axis conserves the angular momentum about it. Shifting time conserves $h$. Each continuous symmetry of $L$ gives a conserved quantity.

@example
title: Uniform gravity and cylindrical coordinates
problem: A particle of mass $m$ moves in uniform gravity, $V=mgz$. Find its conserved quantities by looking for cyclic coordinates.
step: In Cartesian coordinates $L=\tfrac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2)-mgz$. Neither $x$ nor $y$ appears, so $p_x=m\dot x$ and $p_y=m\dot y$ are conserved. The coordinate $z$ appears in $V$, so $p_z$ is not conserved: $\dot p_z=-mg$.
step: Rotation about the vertical axis is also a symmetry, but it mixes $x$ and $y$ and so is not a simple shift. Switch to cylindrical coordinates: $L=\tfrac12 m(\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2)-mgz$.
step: Now $\phi$ is cyclic, so $p_\phi=m\rho^2\dot\phi$ is conserved. This is the vertical component of the angular momentum.
step: $L$ has no explicit time dependence and the constraints are trivial, so $E=\tfrac12 m(\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2)+mgz$ is conserved too.

@example
title: Energy function of the rotating hoop
problem: For the bead on the hoop that rotates at constant $\omega$, $L=\tfrac12 mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta)+mgR\cos\theta$. Find the conserved energy function $h$ and compare it with $T+V$.
step: $L$ has no explicit time dependence ($\omega$ is constant), so $h$ is conserved.
step: The momentum is $p_\theta=\partial L/\partial\dot\theta=mR^2\dot\theta$.
step: $h=\dot\theta\,p_\theta-L=mR^2\dot\theta^2-L=\tfrac12 mR^2\dot\theta^2-\tfrac12 mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta-mgR\cos\theta$.
step: By contrast $T+V=\tfrac12 mR^2(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta)-mgR\cos\theta$, so $h=T+V-mR^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta$.
step: They differ because the constraint depends on time. $T+V$ is not conserved, since the motor that holds $\omega$ fixed does work on the bead, but $h$ is.

@mistakes
- Calling a coordinate cyclic because its velocity is missing or because the potential does not depend on it; cyclic means the coordinate $q$ itself is absent from $L$ (including from $T$).
- Assuming $h=T+V$ always; this needs time-independent constraints and a velocity-independent potential.
- Treating a conserved $p_k$ as having units of momentum; for an angle, $p_\phi$ is an angular momentum.

@practice self
prompt: Two particles interact through a potential that depends only on their separation, so $L=\tfrac12 m_1|\dot{\mathbf r}_1|^2+\tfrac12 m_2|\dot{\mathbf r}_2|^2-V(|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|)$. Use a symmetry argument and Noether's theorem to show that the total momentum is conserved.
hint: Shift both particles by the same small vector $\epsilon\,\mathbf a$ and ask what happens to the separation.
hint: Apply $Q=\sum_i p_i\,\delta q_i$ with $\delta\mathbf r_1=\delta\mathbf r_2=\mathbf a$, and remember that $\mathbf a$ is arbitrary.
solution: Under $\mathbf r_1\to\mathbf r_1+\epsilon\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf r_2\to\mathbf r_2+\epsilon\mathbf a$ the separation $\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2$ and both velocities are unchanged, so $L$ is invariant. Noether's theorem gives the conserved quantity $Q=\mathbf p_1\cdot\mathbf a+\mathbf p_2\cdot\mathbf a=\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2)$. Since the direction $\mathbf a$ is arbitrary, each component of $\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2$ is conserved.
rubric: Identifies the transformation (a common translation of both particles) and shows that the separation is unchanged, so $L$ is invariant.
rubric: Forms the conserved quantity as $\sum p_i\,\delta q_i$, here $\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2)$.
rubric: Uses the arbitrariness of $\mathbf a$ to conclude that all three components of total momentum are conserved.
