@article
Near a stable equilibrium almost every potential looks like a parabola, and a system with many coordinates behaves like a set of independent oscillators. Small-oscillation theory is the method for finding those oscillators.

### One coordinate first

Let $q_0$ be an equilibrium, so $V'(q_0)=0$, and let $\eta=q-q_0$ be a small displacement. Then $V\approx V(q_0)+\tfrac12V''(q_0)\,\eta^{2}$, and with $T=\tfrac12m\dot\eta^{2}$ the equation of motion is $m\ddot\eta=-V''(q_0)\,\eta$. The motion is harmonic with $\omega=\sqrt{V''(q_0)/m}$ when $V''(q_0)>0$. If $V''(q_0)<0$, the same equation gives exponential growth: the equilibrium is unstable.

### Several coordinates

For coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_n$ an equilibrium has $\partial V/\partial q_i=0$ for every $i$. Write $\eta_i=q_i-q_i^{(0)}$ and expand both energies to second order:

$$L\approx\tfrac12\sum_{ij}M_{ij}\dot\eta_i\dot\eta_j-\tfrac12\sum_{ij}K_{ij}\eta_i\eta_j,\qquad M_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}T}{\partial\dot q_i\,\partial\dot q_j},\quad K_{ij}=\frac{\partial^{2}V}{\partial q_i\,\partial q_j},$$

with both matrices evaluated at the equilibrium. They are symmetric, and $M$ is positive definite. The Euler–Lagrange equations become

$$M\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta}=-K\boldsymbol\eta.$$

### Normal modes

Look for a solution in which every coordinate oscillates at one frequency, $\boldsymbol\eta=\mathbf a\cos(\omega t-\phi)$. Substituting gives an eigenvalue problem:

$$(K-\omega^{2}M)\,\mathbf a=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\det(K-\omega^{2}M)=0.$$

Each root $\omega_s^{2}$ with its vector $\mathbf a^{(s)}$ is a **normal mode**. Modes with different frequencies satisfy $\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\,\mathbf a^{(r)}=0$, and the general small motion is a superposition of them with amplitudes and phases set by the initial conditions.

### Classifying the equilibrium

The signs of the $\omega^{2}$ values decide the type:

- all $\omega_s^{2}>0$ ($K$ positive definite): **stable**;
- some $\omega_s^{2}<0$: **unstable**, since that mode grows like $e^{|\omega_s|t}$;
- some $\omega_s^{2}=0$: a **zero mode**, where the quadratic terms are silent. For a free translation or rotation this is harmless; otherwise higher-order terms decide.

Positive diagonal entries of $K$ are not enough; the eigenvalues are what count. In Python, `scipy.linalg.eigh(K, M)` solves the generalised problem directly:

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eigh

M = np.diag([1.0, 1.0])
K = np.array([[3.0, -1.0], [-1.0, 3.0]])
w2, modes = eigh(K, M)   # columns of modes are the vectors a
print(np.sqrt(w2))       # about [1.414 2.0]
```

@example
title: Two masses between two walls
problem: Two identical masses $m$ slide on a frictionless line. Mass 1 is joined to the left wall by a spring of constant $k$, mass 2 is joined to the right wall by a spring of constant $k$, and the masses are joined to each other by a spring of constant $\kappa$. All springs are unstretched at $x_1=x_2=0$. Find the normal modes and classify the equilibrium.
step: With displacements $x_1,x_2$, the Lagrangian is $L=\tfrac12m(\dot x_1^{2}+\dot x_2^{2})-\tfrac12kx_1^{2}-\tfrac12kx_2^{2}-\tfrac12\kappa(x_2-x_1)^{2}$.
step: Read off the matrices: $M=\begin{pmatrix}m&0\\0&m\end{pmatrix}$ and $K=\begin{pmatrix}k+\kappa&-\kappa\\-\kappa&k+\kappa\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The secular equation is $\det(K-\omega^{2}M)=(k+\kappa-m\omega^{2})^{2}-\kappa^{2}=0$. So $k+\kappa-m\omega^{2}=\pm\kappa$, which gives $\omega_1^{2}=k/m$ and $\omega_2^{2}=(k+2\kappa)/m$.
step: Mode shapes. For $\omega_1^{2}$, $(K-kI)\mathbf a=0$ gives $a_1=a_2$: the masses move together and the middle spring is never stretched. For $\omega_2^{2}$ it gives $a_1=-a_2$: the masses move oppositely and stretch the middle spring, which raises the frequency.
step: Both $\omega^{2}$ values are positive for $k,\kappa>0$, so the equilibrium is stable and every motion is a sum of these two modes.

@example
title: The double pendulum
problem: Two particles of mass $m$ hang from a pivot on two light rods, each of length $\ell$, as a double pendulum. Let $\theta_1,\theta_2$ be the rod angles from the downward vertical. Find the normal-mode frequencies and shapes about $\theta_1=\theta_2=0$.
step: The energies are $T=m\ell^{2}\dot\theta_1^{2}+\tfrac12m\ell^{2}\dot\theta_2^{2}+m\ell^{2}\dot\theta_1\dot\theta_2\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)$ and $V=-mg\ell\,(2\cos\theta_1+\cos\theta_2)$. The hanging state is an equilibrium because $\partial V/\partial\theta_i=0$ there.
step: Expand to second order. Set $\cos(\theta_1-\theta_2)\to1$ in $T$ and $\cos\theta\to1-\theta^{2}/2$ in $V$. This gives $M=m\ell^{2}\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $K=mg\ell\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Let $\lambda=\omega^{2}\ell/g$. Then $\det(K-\omega^{2}M)=0$ becomes $(2-2\lambda)(1-\lambda)-\lambda^{2}=0$, that is $\lambda^{2}-4\lambda+2=0$.
step: The roots are $\lambda=2\pm\sqrt2$, so $\omega^{2}=(2\pm\sqrt2)\,g/\ell$. Both are positive, so the equilibrium is stable.
step: The first row of $(K-\omega^{2}M)\mathbf a=0$ reads $(2-2\lambda)a_1=\lambda a_2$. For $\lambda=2-\sqrt2$ it gives $a_2=\sqrt2\,a_1$ (rods swing together, lower frequency). For $\lambda=2+\sqrt2$ it gives $a_2=-\sqrt2\,a_1$ (rods swing in opposition, higher frequency).

@mistakes
- Evaluating $M_{ij}$ or $K_{ij}$ away from the equilibrium, or skipping the check that $\partial V/\partial q_i=0$. Away from an equilibrium the expansion has linear terms.
- Calling an equilibrium stable because the diagonal entries of $K$ are positive. A large cross term can make one eigenvalue negative.
- Solving $\det(K-\omega^{2}M)=0$ as if $M$ were the identity when the kinetic matrix has off-diagonal entries, or reporting $\omega^{2}$ without taking the square root.
- Expecting a single displaced mass to excite a single mode. A generic starting state is a superposition of all modes.

@practice self
prompt: Let $K$ and $M$ be real symmetric matrices, and let $\mathbf a^{(r)}$ and $\mathbf a^{(s)}$ satisfy $K\mathbf a^{(r)}=\omega_r^{2}M\mathbf a^{(r)}$ and $K\mathbf a^{(s)}=\omega_s^{2}M\mathbf a^{(s)}$ with $\omega_r^{2}\neq\omega_s^{2}$. Prove that $\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\,\mathbf a^{(r)}=0$, and say what this means for the general solution of $M\ddot{\boldsymbol\eta}=-K\boldsymbol\eta$.
hint: Multiply the first equation on the left by $\mathbf a^{(s)T}$ and the second by $\mathbf a^{(r)T}$.
hint: Both results are scalars. Use the symmetry of $K$ and $M$ to compare their left-hand sides, then subtract.
solution: Left-multiplying by $\mathbf a^{(s)T}$ gives $\mathbf a^{(s)T}K\mathbf a^{(r)}=\omega_r^{2}\,\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\mathbf a^{(r)}$. Likewise $\mathbf a^{(r)T}K\mathbf a^{(s)}=\omega_s^{2}\,\mathbf a^{(r)T}M\mathbf a^{(s)}$. Because $K$ and $M$ are symmetric, $\mathbf a^{(r)T}K\mathbf a^{(s)}=\mathbf a^{(s)T}K\mathbf a^{(r)}$ and $\mathbf a^{(r)T}M\mathbf a^{(s)}=\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\mathbf a^{(r)}$. Subtracting the two equations gives $(\omega_r^{2}-\omega_s^{2})\,\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\mathbf a^{(r)}=0$, and since $\omega_r^{2}\neq\omega_s^{2}$ the product vanishes. The modes are therefore orthogonal with respect to the kinetic-energy metric $M$. The energy splits into independent mode energies, and the general solution $\boldsymbol\eta=\sum_sC_s\mathbf a^{(s)}\cos(\omega_st-\phi_s)$ has amplitudes and phases that can be fixed mode by mode from the initial conditions.

rubric: Uses the symmetry of $K$ and $M$ to show that the two scalar left-hand sides are equal.
rubric: Subtracts to reach $(\omega_r^{2}-\omega_s^{2})\,\mathbf a^{(s)T}M\mathbf a^{(r)}=0$ and uses $\omega_r^{2}\neq\omega_s^{2}$.
rubric: States that the general solution is a superposition of normal modes with independent amplitudes and phases.

@quiz ms
prompt: A stable equilibrium has two degrees of freedom and two distinct normal-mode frequencies. Which statements are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: In a single normal mode every coordinate oscillates at the same angular frequency.
choice*: The general small motion is a superposition of the two normal modes, with amplitudes and phases set by the initial conditions.
choice: Each normal-mode frequency equals the frequency its coordinate would have if the coupling terms were switched off.
choice: Displacing one coordinate and releasing the system from rest excites only one normal mode.
hint: Think of the two-mass example: the frequency $\omega_2^{2}=(k+2\kappa)/m$ contains the coupling $\kappa$.
hint: A single displaced mass is a sum of the in-phase and out-of-phase shapes.
solution: In a normal mode all coordinates share one frequency, and the general motion is a sum of the modes. Coupling shifts the frequencies away from those of the uncoupled oscillators: in the two-mass example, switching the coupling off gives $\omega^{2}=k/m$ for both masses, while the actual modes have $k/m$ and $(k+2\kappa)/m$. A displacement of only one coordinate is a combination of both mode shapes, so it excites both modes.
