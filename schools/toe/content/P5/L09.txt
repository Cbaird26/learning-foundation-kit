@article
A rigid body is a collection of particles whose mutual distances never change. Those constraints remove all internal freedom, leaving **six degrees of freedom**: three for the position of the centre of mass and three for the orientation. (A body that is a single line, such as a dumbbell, has five, because spinning about its own axis changes nothing.)

### Velocity and kinetic energy

Every point of the body has velocity $\mathbf v=\mathbf V+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r$, where $\mathbf V$ is the centre-of-mass velocity, $\mathbf r$ is measured from the centre of mass and $\boldsymbol\omega$ is the angular velocity. Summing $\tfrac12mv^{2}$ over the body splits the kinetic energy cleanly:

$$T=\tfrac12MV^{2}+\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\omega^{T}I\,\boldsymbol\omega,\qquad I_{ab}=\sum_\alpha m_\alpha\left(r_\alpha^{2}\delta_{ab}-r_{\alpha a}r_{\alpha b}\right).$$

$I$ is the **inertia tensor** (replace the sum by an integral over the density for a continuous body). The angular momentum about the centre of mass is $\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega$. Since $I$ is a real symmetric matrix, it has three orthogonal eigenvectors, the **principal axes**, with eigenvalues $I_1,I_2,I_3$, the **principal moments**. If $\boldsymbol\omega$ points along a principal axis then $\mathbf L\parallel\boldsymbol\omega$; otherwise it does not. For an axis parallel to one through the centre of mass at distance $d$, the **parallel-axis theorem** gives $I=I_{\text{cm}}+Md^{2}$.

### Euler's equations

In the body frame, along the principal axes and with no external torque, $\dot{\mathbf L}+\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf L=0$ becomes

$$I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\omega_2\omega_3,\quad I_2\dot\omega_2=(I_3-I_1)\omega_3\omega_1,\quad I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\omega_1\omega_2.$$

Two quantities are conserved: the rotational energy $\tfrac12\sum I_i\omega_i^{2}$ and the squared magnitude $\sum I_i^{2}\omega_i^{2}$ of $\mathbf L$.

### Stability of a spin

Spin mostly about axis 1, with $\omega_2,\omega_3$ small. Linearising gives

$$\ddot\omega_2=\frac{(I_3-I_1)(I_1-I_2)}{I_2I_3}\,\omega_1^{2}\,\omega_2 .$$

If $I_1$ is the largest or the smallest moment, the coefficient is negative and the perturbation oscillates: the spin is stable. If $I_1$ is the intermediate moment, the coefficient is positive and the perturbation grows exponentially: the spin is unstable (the tennis-racket effect).

### Axisymmetric bodies

If $I_1=I_2$, then $\omega_3$ is constant and $(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ rotates in the body frame at angular frequency

$$\Omega_b=\frac{I_3-I_1}{I_1}\,\omega_3 .$$

The Euler angles $(\phi,\theta,\psi)$, for precession, nutation and spin, are generalised coordinates for the orientation. In the z–x–z convention a symmetric body has

$$T_{\text{rot}}=\tfrac12I_1\left(\dot\theta^{2}+\dot\phi^{2}\sin^{2}\theta\right)+\tfrac12I_3\left(\dot\psi+\dot\phi\cos\theta\right)^{2},$$

the form that week 10 turns into a Hamiltonian.

@example
title: Inertia tensor of a dumbbell
problem: Two point masses $m$ at $(b,b,0)$ and $(-b,-b,0)$ are joined by a light rigid rod. Find the inertia tensor about the origin, which is the centre of mass, and its principal moments and axes.
step: Diagonal entries: $I_{xx}=\sum m(y^{2}+z^{2})=2mb^{2}$, $I_{yy}=\sum m(x^{2}+z^{2})=2mb^{2}$ and $I_{zz}=\sum m(x^{2}+y^{2})=4mb^{2}$.
step: Off-diagonal entries: $I_{xy}=-\sum mxy=-2mb^{2}$, while $I_{xz}=I_{yz}=0$. Hence $I=mb^{2}\begin{pmatrix}2&-2&0\\-2&2&0\\0&0&4\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The matrix splits into a $2\times2$ block and a lone entry. The block $\begin{pmatrix}2&-2\\-2&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $0$ and $4$, so the principal moments are $0$, $4mb^{2}$ and $4mb^{2}$.
step: Principal axes: $(1,1,0)/\sqrt2$ for the eigenvalue $0$ (along the rod), $(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$ for $4mb^{2}$, and $(0,0,1)$ for $4mb^{2}$.
step: Check: a pair of point masses has no moment about the line joining them, and about any perpendicular axis through the centre it has $2m(\sqrt2\,b)^{2}=4mb^{2}$.

@example
title: Spin about the intermediate axis
problem: A torque-free body has principal moments $I_1=1$, $I_2=2$ and $I_3=3\ \text{kg m}^2$. It spins at $\omega_2\approx\Omega$ about its intermediate axis, with small perturbations $\omega_1,\omega_3$. How do the perturbations behave, and how does this compare with a spin about axis 3?
step: Keep first-order terms in Euler's equations. The equation for $\omega_2$ is second order in the small quantities, so $\omega_2$ stays near $\Omega$, and the other two give $I_1\dot\omega_1=(I_2-I_3)\Omega\,\omega_3$ and $I_3\dot\omega_3=(I_1-I_2)\Omega\,\omega_1$.
step: Insert the numbers: $\dot\omega_1=-\Omega\,\omega_3$ and $\dot\omega_3=-\dfrac{\Omega}{3}\,\omega_1$.
step: Differentiate the first equation and substitute the second: $\ddot\omega_1=-\Omega\,\dot\omega_3=+\dfrac{\Omega^{2}}{3}\,\omega_1$.
step: The coefficient is positive, so $\omega_1\propto e^{\pm\Omega t/\sqrt3}$. A tiny perturbation grows exponentially at rate $\Omega/\sqrt3$: the spin is unstable.
step: Now spin about axis 3, the largest moment, at rate $\Omega$. The same steps give $\ddot\omega_1=\dfrac{(I_2-I_3)(I_3-I_1)}{I_1I_2}\,\Omega^{2}\omega_1=-\Omega^{2}\omega_1$, an oscillation at frequency $\Omega$. This spin is stable, as is a spin about the smallest-moment axis.

@mistakes
- Treating $I$ as a single number, so that $\mathbf L=I\boldsymbol\omega$ is always parallel to $\boldsymbol\omega$. It is a matrix, and the two vectors are parallel only along a principal axis.
- Using the parallel-axis theorem between two axes when neither passes through the centre of mass. Always go through the centre of mass: $I=I_{\text{cm}}+Md^{2}$.
- Scrambling the cyclic pattern of Euler's equations, or applying them in the lab frame. They hold in the body frame, along the principal axes.
- Assuming the longest axis of a body is the stable one. Stability depends on the ordering of the principal moments, not on length.

@practice num
prompt: A uniform thin rod has mass $3\ \text{kg}$ and length $2\ \text{m}$. Find its moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the rod through one end.
answer: 4
check: quad(lambda x: (3/2)*x**2, 0, 2)
unit: kg m^2
hint: Cut the rod into slices of length $dx$. Each has mass $(M/L)\,dx$ and sits at distance $x$ from the axis.
hint: $I=\displaystyle\int_0^{L}\frac{M}{L}\,x^{2}\,dx$.
solution: $I=\displaystyle\int_0^{L}\frac ML x^{2}\,dx=\frac13ML^{2}=\frac13(3)(4)=4\ \text{kg m}^2$. Check with the parallel-axis theorem: $\frac1{12}ML^{2}+M(L/2)^{2}=1+3=4$.

@practice list
prompt: The inertia tensor of a body about its centre of mass, in convenient axes, is $I=\begin{pmatrix}3&-1&0\\-1&3&0\\0&0&3\end{pmatrix}\ \text{kg m}^2$. Enter its three principal moments of inertia, in $\text{kg m}^2$ and in any order.
answer: 2, 3, 4

hint: The matrix splits into a $2\times2$ block and the lone entry $3$. Solve $\det\begin{pmatrix}3-\lambda&-1\\-1&3-\lambda\end{pmatrix}=0$.
solution: The lone entry gives $3$. The block has $(3-\lambda)^{2}-1=0$, so $\lambda=3\pm1=2$ or $4$. The principal moments are $2$, $3$ and $4\ \text{kg m}^2$.

@practice num
prompt: A torque-free axisymmetric body has $I_1=I_2=4\ \text{kg m}^2$ and $I_3=5\ \text{kg m}^2$, and spins about its symmetry axis with $\omega_3=20\ \text{rad/s}$. Find the angular frequency at which the components $(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ rotate in the body frame.
answer: 5
check: max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(np.array([[0.0, (4-5)/4*20], [(5-4)/4*20, 0.0]])).imag))
unit: rad/s
hint: With $I_1=I_2$ the third Euler equation gives $\dot\omega_3=0$, so $\omega_3$ stays at $20\ \text{rad/s}$.
hint: Then $\dot\omega_1=-\Omega_b\,\omega_2$ and $\dot\omega_2=\Omega_b\,\omega_1$. Read $\Omega_b$ from the coefficients in the first two Euler equations.
solution: Euler's first two equations give $\dot\omega_1=\dfrac{I_2-I_3}{I_1}\omega_3\,\omega_2=-5\,\omega_2$ and $\dot\omega_2=\dfrac{I_3-I_1}{I_2}\omega_3\,\omega_1=5\,\omega_1$. This is a rotation of the vector $(\omega_1,\omega_2)$ at angular frequency $\Omega_b=\dfrac{I_3-I_1}{I_1}\omega_3=\dfrac14(20)=5\ \text{rad/s}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A torque-free body with principal moments $I_1=2$, $I_2=3$ and $I_3=6\ \text{kg m}^2$ spins almost exactly about its intermediate axis at $\omega_2=8\ \text{rad/s}$. A tiny perturbation of the other two components grows as $e^{\gamma t}$. Find $\gamma$.
answer: 4
check: max(np.linalg.eigvals(np.array([[0.0, (3-6)*8/2], [(2-3)*8/6, 0.0]])).real)
unit: 1/s
tol: 1%
hint: Linearise Euler's first and third equations with $\omega_2=8$ fixed.
hint: You should get $\dot\omega_1=-12\,\omega_3$ and $\dot\omega_3=-\tfrac43\,\omega_1$. Combine them into an equation for $\ddot\omega_1$.
solution: $\dot\omega_1=\dfrac{I_2-I_3}{I_1}\omega_2\,\omega_3=-12\,\omega_3$ and $\dot\omega_3=\dfrac{I_1-I_2}{I_3}\omega_2\,\omega_1=-\dfrac43\,\omega_1$. Then $\ddot\omega_1=-12\,\dot\omega_3=16\,\omega_1$, so $\gamma=\sqrt{16}=4\ \text{s}^{-1}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: A uniform rectangular block has edge lengths $1$, $2$ and $3$ in arbitrary units. It spins freely about an axis through its centre with no torque acting. For which axes is the spin steady and stable against small perturbations? Select all that apply.
choice*: The axis parallel to the edges of length $1$.
choice*: The axis parallel to the edges of length $3$.
choice: The axis parallel to the edges of length $2$.
choice: The axis along a body diagonal.
hint: For an axis parallel to one edge, the moment is $\frac{M}{12}$ times the sum of the squares of the other two edge lengths.
hint: Steady spin needs a principal axis; stability needs the largest or the smallest principal moment.
solution: The three edge directions are principal axes, with moments $\frac{M}{12}(2^{2}+3^{2})=\frac{13M}{12}$ for the edges of length $1$, $\frac{M}{12}(1^{2}+3^{2})=\frac{10M}{12}$ for length $2$, and $\frac{M}{12}(1^{2}+2^{2})=\frac{5M}{12}$ for length $3$. The largest and smallest give stable spins, while the intermediate one (edges of length $2$) is unstable. A body diagonal is not a principal axis, so a spin about it is not steady.
