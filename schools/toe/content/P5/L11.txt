@article
Hamilton's equations make the Hamiltonian special. The **Poisson bracket** puts every function on phase space on an equal footing and turns "how does $f$ change in time?" into algebra.

### Definition

For functions $f(q,p,t)$ and $g(q,p,t)$,

$$\{f,g\}=\sum_i\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right).$$

This is the sign convention used here; some books reverse the order, so always check. The coordinates and momenta satisfy the **fundamental brackets**

$$\{q_i,q_j\}=0,\qquad\{p_i,p_j\}=0,\qquad\{q_i,p_j\}=\delta_{ij}.$$

### Algebra

For all functions $f,g,h$ and constants $a,b$:

- antisymmetry: $\{f,g\}=-\{g,f\}$, so $\{f,f\}=0$;
- linearity: $\{af+bg,h\}=a\{f,h\}+b\{g,h\}$;
- Leibniz rule: $\{f,gh\}=\{f,g\}\,h+g\,\{f,h\}$;
- Jacobi identity: $\{f,\{g,h\}\}+\{g,\{h,f\}\}+\{h,\{f,g\}\}=0$.

The Leibniz rule builds the brackets of complicated functions from the fundamental ones. A quick check in Python:

```python
import sympy as sp
q, p = sp.symbols('q p')
def pb(f, g):
    return sp.diff(f, q)*sp.diff(g, p) - sp.diff(f, p)*sp.diff(g, q)
print(pb(q**3, p**2))    # 6*p*q**2
```

### Time evolution

Along a trajectory, the chain rule together with Hamilton's equations gives

$$\frac{df}{dt}=\{f,H\}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}.$$

Hamilton's equations are the special cases $\dot q_i=\{q_i,H\}$ and $\dot p_i=\{p_i,H\}$. A function with no explicit time dependence is conserved exactly when $\{f,H\}=0$.

### Brackets of conserved quantities

If $f$ and $g$ are conserved, then so is $\{f,g\}$ (Poisson's theorem, which follows from the Jacobi identity). The new quantity may be genuinely new. For $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$,

$$\{L_i,L_j\}=\sum_k\epsilon_{ijk}L_k,\qquad\{L^{2},L_i\}=0.$$

So in a central field, where all three components are conserved, the brackets of two of them reproduce the third, and they do not commute with each other. Only $L^{2}$ and one component can be used together as simultaneous labels.

### Flows

A function $G$ generates a flow on phase space through $dq/d\epsilon=\{q,G\}$ and $dp/d\epsilon=\{p,G\}$. Momentum generates translations, $L_z$ generates rotations and $H$ generates motion in time. If $\{f,G\}=0$, then $f$ is unchanged by the flow of $G$: a conserved quantity generates a symmetry, which is Noether's theorem in Hamiltonian language.

@example
title: The oscillator and the Leibniz rule
problem: For $H=\dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}+\tfrac12m\omega^{2}q^{2}$, use Poisson brackets to find $d(qp)/dt$, and deduce a relation between the average kinetic and potential energies.
step: Hamilton's equations as brackets: $\{q,H\}=\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial p}=\dfrac pm$ and $\{p,H\}=-\dfrac{\partial H}{\partial q}=-m\omega^{2}q$.
step: Apply the Leibniz rule to the product: $\{qp,H\}=q\{p,H\}+\{q,H\}\,p=-m\omega^{2}q^{2}+\dfrac{p^{2}}{m}$.
step: Since $qp$ has no explicit time dependence, $\dfrac{d(qp)}{dt}=\dfrac{p^{2}}{m}-m\omega^{2}q^{2}=2T-2V$.
step: The motion is periodic, so $qp$ returns to its starting value after one period and the average of its derivative over a period is zero. Hence $\langle T\rangle=\langle V\rangle$: the oscillator spends equal average energy in kinetic and potential form.

@example
title: The angular-momentum bracket
problem: Show that $\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z$ for a single particle, where $L_x=yp_z-zp_y$ and $L_y=zp_x-xp_z$.
step: List the nonzero partial derivatives. For $L_x$: $\partial_yL_x=p_z$, $\partial_zL_x=-p_y$, $\partial_{p_y}L_x=-z$, $\partial_{p_z}L_x=y$. For $L_y$: $\partial_xL_y=-p_z$, $\partial_zL_y=p_x$, $\partial_{p_x}L_y=z$, $\partial_{p_z}L_y=-x$.
step: The terms $\partial_{q_i}L_x\,\partial_{p_i}L_y$: only $i=z$ survives, giving $(-p_y)(-x)=xp_y$.
step: The terms $\partial_{p_i}L_x\,\partial_{q_i}L_y$: only $i=z$ survives, giving $(y)(p_x)=yp_x$.
step: Subtract: $\{L_x,L_y\}=xp_y-yp_x=L_z$. The cyclic permutations give $\{L_y,L_z\}=L_x$ and $\{L_z,L_x\}=L_y$.
step: Consequence: if $L_x$ and $L_y$ are conserved then so is $\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z$. Also, by the Leibniz rule, $\{L^{2},L_z\}=2L_x\{L_x,L_z\}+2L_y\{L_y,L_z\}=-2L_xL_y+2L_yL_x=0$.

@mistakes
- Reversing the order: $\{f,H\}$ is $\dot f$, while $\{H,f\}$ is $-\dot f$.
- Dropping terms. The bracket sums over every degree of freedom, and each pair has two products joined by a minus sign.
- Believing that two conserved quantities must have zero bracket. In a central field all components of $\mathbf L$ are conserved, yet $\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z\neq0$.
- Treating the bracket as multiplicative. The Leibniz rule is $\{f,gh\}=\{f,g\}h+g\{f,h\}$, with two terms.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements hold for all functions $f,g,h$ on phase space? Select all that apply.
choice*: $\{f,g\}=-\{g,f\}$
choice*: $\{f,gh\}=\{f,g\}\,h+g\,\{f,h\}$
choice: $\{f,g\}=\{g,f\}$
choice: If $f$ and $g$ are both conserved, then $\{f,g\}=0$.
hint: Two of these are structural properties of the bracket. Test the others on an example.
hint: Think of the angular-momentum components in a central field: all three are conserved.
solution: Antisymmetry and the Leibniz rule are properties of the bracket. Symmetry would force $\{f,g\}=0$ always, which is false: $\{q,p\}=1$. Two conserved quantities need not commute: in a central field $L_x$ and $L_y$ are conserved, yet $\{L_x,L_y\}=L_z\neq0$. What is true is Poisson's theorem: the bracket $\{f,g\}$ is itself conserved.
