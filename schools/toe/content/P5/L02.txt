@article
A **constraint** restricts where a system may be or how it may move: a bead on a wire, a bob on a rod, a wheel that does not slip. Constraints are enforced by forces (a normal force, a tension) that are not known in advance. They adjust themselves to whatever value keeps the constraint satisfied. The great convenience of generalized coordinates is that if you choose coordinates that already obey the constraints, those unknown forces never appear.

### Holonomic constraints

A constraint is **holonomic** if it can be written as an equation relating the coordinates (and possibly time) alone:

$$f(\mathbf r_1,\dots,\mathbf r_N,t)=0.$$

The rod of a pendulum, $x^2+y^2=\ell^2$, is an example. Each independent holonomic constraint removes one degree of freedom, so with $k$ of them

$$n = 3N - k.$$

A velocity condition such as $a(\mathbf r)\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}=0$ is still holonomic if it can be integrated to an equation of the form above. For instance $x\dot y-y\dot x=0$ has the integrating factor $1/x^2$ and becomes $\frac{d}{dt}(y/x)=0$, so $y=cx$.

### Nonholonomic constraints

A constraint is **nonholonomic** if it cannot be reduced to such an equation. Two common cases:

- **Inequalities.** A particle inside a box obeys $x\le L$; a mass on a string that may go slack obeys $r\le\ell$.
- **Non-integrable velocity conditions.** A vertical coin of radius $R$ rolling without slipping on a table obeys $\dot x=R\dot\theta\cos\phi$ and $\dot y=R\dot\theta\sin\phi$ ($\phi$ is the heading and $\theta$ the rolling angle). These cannot be integrated, so all four coordinates $(x,y,\phi,\theta)$ stay independent even though only two velocity directions are allowed at a given instant.

Nonholonomic constraints do not remove coordinates, and the elegant elimination method of this course does not apply to them directly.

### Time dependence

If $t$ appears explicitly in the constraint it is **rheonomic**; otherwise it is **scleronomic**. A bead on a hoop forced to rotate at a given rate is rheonomic. In that case the positions depend explicitly on $t$, so the $\partial\mathbf r/\partial t$ term in the velocity survives and $T$ contains terms that do not involve $\dot q$.

### Virtual displacements

A **virtual displacement** $\delta\mathbf r$ is an infinitesimal displacement allowed by the constraints at a frozen instant. For smooth ("ideal") constraints, the constraint forces do no work in virtual displacements. This is why eliminating the constraints costs nothing: the next weeks show that the equations of motion in the coordinates $q_i$ follow from $T$ and $V$ alone.

@example
title: A bead on a rotating hoop
problem: A bead of mass $m$ slides on a circular hoop of radius $R$. The hoop rotates about a fixed vertical diameter at constant angular speed $\omega$. Classify the constraints, count the degrees of freedom and find $T$ using $\theta$, the angle from the downward vertical.
step: In Cartesian coordinates the bead obeys $x^2+y^2+z^2=R^2$ and $-x\sin\omega t+y\cos\omega t=0$ (it lies in the rotating plane). Both are holonomic, and the second depends explicitly on $t$, so it is rheonomic. Then $n=3-2=1$.
step: Choose $\theta$ as the coordinate: $x=R\sin\theta\cos\omega t$, $y=R\sin\theta\sin\omega t$, $z=-R\cos\theta$.
step: Differentiate: $\dot x=R\cos\theta\cos\omega t\,\dot\theta-R\omega\sin\theta\sin\omega t$, $\dot y=R\cos\theta\sin\omega t\,\dot\theta+R\omega\sin\theta\cos\omega t$, $\dot z=R\sin\theta\,\dot\theta$.
step: Square and add. In $\dot x^2+\dot y^2$ the cross terms cancel and $\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t=1$, leaving $R^2\cos^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2+R^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta$. Adding $\dot z^2=R^2\sin^2\theta\,\dot\theta^2$ gives $R^2\dot\theta^2+R^2\omega^2\sin^2\theta$.
step: Therefore $T=\tfrac12 mR^2\left(\dot\theta^2+\omega^2\sin^2\theta\right)$. The term $\omega^2\sin^2\theta$ survives even at $\dot\theta=0$: that is the signature of a rheonomic constraint.

@example
title: Classifying four constraints
problem: Classify each as holonomic or nonholonomic, and as scleronomic or rheonomic: (a) $x^2+y^2=\ell^2$; (b) $x^2+y^2=(\ell_0+ct)^2$; (c) $r\le\ell$; (d) a vertical disc rolling without slipping.
step: (a) A relation among coordinates only, with no $t$: holonomic and scleronomic (a rigid rod of fixed length).
step: (b) Still a relation among coordinates, but now with explicit $t$: holonomic and rheonomic (a string reeled in at constant rate).
step: (c) An inequality cannot be written as an equation: nonholonomic. No time appears, so it is scleronomic. While the string is taut it acts like (a), but the particle may leave the taut regime.
step: (d) The velocity conditions $\dot x=R\dot\theta\cos\phi$, $\dot y=R\dot\theta\sin\phi$ are not integrable: nonholonomic. No time appears, so they are scleronomic.

@mistakes
- Calling a constraint nonholonomic just because it is written with velocities; first check whether it integrates to a relation among coordinates, as $x\dot y-y\dot x=0$ does.
- Dropping the $\partial\mathbf r/\partial t$ term in the velocity when the constraint depends explicitly on time.
- Subtracting a degree of freedom for an inequality constraint or a non-integrable rolling condition.

@practice num
prompt: Two particles in three-dimensional space are joined by a rigid massless rod, and the first particle is also constrained to stay in the plane $z=0$. How many degrees of freedom does the system have?
answer: 4
check: 2*3 - 1 - 1
hint: Start from the coordinates of two free particles in space.
hint: The rod removes one degree of freedom and the plane condition removes another.
solution: $6-1-1=4$. For example, the two plane coordinates of the first particle plus two angles for the direction of the rod.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following constraints is holonomic?
choice*: A bead on a fixed circular wire: $x^2+z^2=R^2$ and $y=0$.
choice: A particle that must stay inside a spherical container: $x^2+y^2+z^2\le R^2$.
choice: A vertical coin rolling without slipping: $\dot x=R\dot\theta\cos\phi$ and $\dot y=R\dot\theta\sin\phi$.
choice: A skate blade that can only slide along its own heading: $\dot x\sin\phi=\dot y\cos\phi$.
hint: A holonomic constraint is, or integrates to, an equation among coordinates and time.
solution: The wire gives two equations among coordinates, so it is holonomic. An inequality is nonholonomic, and the rolling coin and skate-blade conditions are velocity conditions that cannot be integrated to coordinate equations.

@practice expr
prompt: A bead of mass $m$ slides on the parabolic wire $y=ax^2$ in the $xy$ plane. Using $x$ as the generalized coordinate and $v=\dot x$, write the kinetic energy.
vars: m, a, x, v
lead: $T =$
answer: m*v^2*(1 + 4*a^2*x^2)/2
check: lambda m, a, x, v: 0.5*m*(nderiv(lambda t: x + v*t, 0)**2 + nderiv(lambda t: a*(x + v*t)**2, 0)**2)
hint: The constraint gives $\dot y$ in terms of $x$ and $\dot x$ by the chain rule.
hint: $\dot y=2ax\dot x$.
solution: $\dot y=2ax\dot x$, so $v^2=\dot x^2+\dot y^2=\dot x^2(1+4a^2x^2)$ and $T=\tfrac12 m\dot x^2(1+4a^2x^2)$.

@practice self
prompt: A particle of mass $m$ slides on the inside surface of a cone with its apex at the origin, a vertical axis and half-angle $\alpha$, so that $\rho=z\tan\alpha$ with $\rho=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. (a) How many degrees of freedom does it have? (b) Choose generalized coordinates and say why they are suitable. (c) Write the kinetic energy.
hint: Count coordinates minus constraints for (a). For (b), pick coordinates that satisfy the cone condition automatically.
hint: Use the distance $s$ from the apex along the surface and the azimuth $\phi$, then write the speed in cylindrical form $\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2$.
solution: (a) One holonomic, scleronomic constraint on three coordinates leaves $3-1=2$ degrees of freedom. (b) Use the distance $s$ from the apex measured along the cone surface and the azimuth $\phi$. They are independent, the cone constraint is built in through $\rho=s\sin\alpha$, $z=s\cos\alpha$, and $\phi$ matches the axial symmetry. (c) $\dot\rho=\dot s\sin\alpha$, $\dot z=\dot s\cos\alpha$, so $v^2=\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2=\dot s^2+s^2\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\phi^2$ and $T=\tfrac12 m\left(\dot s^2+s^2\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\phi^2\right)$.
rubric: States 2 degrees of freedom from $3-1$.
rubric: Picks two independent coordinates that satisfy the cone constraint automatically, such as $(s,\phi)$ or $(\rho,\phi)$.
rubric: Writes the speed in cylindrical form $\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2$ and substitutes the constraint.
rubric: Obtains $T=\tfrac12 m(\dot s^2+s^2\sin^2\alpha\,\dot\phi^2)$ or an equivalent form in other coordinates.

@quiz num
prompt: A block of mass $M$ slides on a fixed horizontal rail. A bob of mass $m$ hangs from the block by a rigid massless rod and swings in the vertical plane containing the rail. How many degrees of freedom does the system have?
answer: 2
check: 2*2 - 1 - 1
hint: Count the plane coordinates of the two masses first.
hint: The rail removes one coordinate and the rod length removes one.
solution: Two particles in a plane have $4$ coordinates; the rail ($y_M=\text{const}$) and the rod length are two holonomic constraints, leaving $2$. Natural coordinates are the block position $x$ and the rod angle $\theta$.

@quiz expr
prompt: A bead of mass $m$ slides on a straight rod that makes a fixed angle $\alpha$ with a vertical axis and passes through a point on that axis. The rod is turned about the axis at constant angular speed $\omega$. Let $s$ be the bead's distance from that point along the rod and $v=\dot s$. Write the kinetic energy.
vars: m, s, v, omega, alpha
lead: $T =$
answer: m*(v^2 + s^2*omega^2*sin(alpha)^2)/2
check: lambda m, s, v, omega, alpha: 0.5*m*sum(nderiv(f, 0)**2 for f in [lambda t: (s + v*t)*sin(alpha)*cos(omega*t), lambda t: (s + v*t)*sin(alpha)*sin(omega*t), lambda t: (s + v*t)*cos(alpha)])
hint: Write the bead's position using $s$, $\alpha$ and the azimuth $\phi=\omega t$.
hint: The distance from the axis is $s\sin\alpha$, and the height is $s\cos\alpha$.
solution: $\rho=s\sin\alpha$, $z=s\cos\alpha$, $\phi=\omega t$. Then $v^2=\dot\rho^2+\rho^2\dot\phi^2+\dot z^2=\dot s^2+s^2\omega^2\sin^2\alpha$, so $T=\tfrac12 m\left(\dot s^2+\omega^2s^2\sin^2\alpha\right)$.
