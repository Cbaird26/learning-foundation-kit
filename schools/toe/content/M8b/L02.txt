@article
In the plane, if $\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2$ are perpendicular unit vectors then $\mathbf v=(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf e_1)\mathbf e_1+(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf e_2)\mathbf e_2$. To find one component you take a dot product, and the other components do not interfere. Fourier series is this idea with functions as vectors and an integral as the dot product.

### Inner product and orthogonality

For real functions on $[-L,L]$ define
$$\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)\,g(x)\,dx,\qquad \|f\|^2=\langle f,f\rangle.$$
Two functions are **orthogonal** if $\langle f,g\rangle=0$. For integers $m,n\ge1$:
$$\Big\langle\cos\tfrac{m\pi x}{L},\cos\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big\rangle=\Big\langle\sin\tfrac{m\pi x}{L},\sin\tfrac{n\pi x}{L}\Big\rangle=\begin{cases}0,&m\ne n,\\ L,&m=n,\end{cases}$$
while every sine is orthogonal to every cosine, and the constant $1$ (with $\|1\|^2=2L$) is orthogonal to all of them. These follow from product-to-sum identities such as $\cos A\cos B=\tfrac12[\cos(A-B)+\cos(A+B)]$: a sine or cosine with a nonzero whole number of periods integrates to zero.

### Why the coefficient formulas work

Suppose $f=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_n\big(a_n\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}\big)$. Take the inner product with $\cos\frac{m\pi x}{L}$. Every term vanishes except $n=m$, leaving $\langle f,\cos\frac{m\pi x}{L}\rangle=La_m$, which is the week 1 formula. (This argument integrates term by term; the convergence theorems show the formulas are the right definition even without that assumption.)

### Parseval's identity

The squared length of a vector is the sum of its squared components. In the same way, for square-integrable $f$,
$$\frac1L\int_{-L}^{L}f(x)^2\,dx=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(a_n^2+b_n^2\right).$$
For $f(x)=x$ on $(-\pi,\pi)$ the left side is $\frac1\pi\cdot\frac{2\pi^3}{3}$ and the right side is $\sum\frac{4}{n^2}$, so $\sum_{n\ge1}\frac{1}{n^2}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}$.

### Jumps, endpoints and Gibbs

For piecewise smooth $f$ the series converges at every $x$ to $\frac12[f(x^-)+f(x^+)]$. Because the series is periodic, it joins the right end of the interval to the left end. So at $x=\pm L$ it converges to
$$\tfrac12\big[f(L^-)+f(-L^+)\big],$$
which differs from $f(L^-)$ unless the two ends match. If they do match, the periodic extension is continuous there and no jump is felt.

Near a jump the partial sums $S_N$ **overshoot** by about $9\%$ of the jump size, and this does not shrink as $N$ grows: the bump only gets narrower and moves toward the jump. Convergence holds at each fixed point but is not uniform near the jump.

Smoothness shows up in the coefficients. A jump gives coefficients that decay like $\frac1n$; a continuous periodic function with a piecewise continuous derivative has coefficients decaying like $\frac1{n^2}$.

@example
title: Expanding a piecewise function and checking its endpoints
problem: Expand $f(x)=0$ for $-\pi<x<0$ and $f(x)=x$ for $0<x<\pi$ in a Fourier series, and find the value of the series at $x=0$ and at $x=\pi$.
step: Only $(0,\pi)$ contributes. $a_0=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi x\,dx=\frac\pi2$.
step: $a_n=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi x\cos nx\,dx=\frac1\pi\left[\frac{x\sin nx}{n}+\frac{\cos nx}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi=\frac{(-1)^n-1}{\pi n^2}$, which is $-\frac{2}{\pi n^2}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.
step: $b_n=\frac1\pi\int_0^\pi x\sin nx\,dx=\frac1\pi\left[-\frac{x\cos nx}{n}+\frac{\sin nx}{n^2}\right]_0^\pi=\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$.
step: The series is $f(x)\sim\frac\pi4-\frac2\pi\left(\cos x+\frac{\cos 3x}{9}+\cdots\right)+\left(\sin x-\frac{\sin 2x}{2}+\cdots\right)$.
step: At $x=0$ the function is continuous ($f(0^-)=f(0^+)=0$), so the series converges to $0$. Indeed $\frac\pi4-\frac2\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac1{n^2}=\frac\pi4-\frac2\pi\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8}=0$.
step: At $x=\pi$ every sine vanishes and $\cos n\pi=(-1)^n$, so the series gives $\frac\pi4+\frac2\pi\cdot\frac{\pi^2}{8}=\frac\pi2$. This is the midpoint of $f(\pi^-)=\pi$ and $f(-\pi^+)=0$: the periodic extension jumps at the ends of the interval.

@example
title: Parseval for a trigonometric polynomial
problem: Let $f(x)=3+2\cos x-5\sin 2x+\cos 3x$. Use Parseval's identity to compute $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f(x)^2\,dx$.
step: Read off the coefficients. The constant $3$ equals $\frac{a_0}{2}$, so $a_0=6$. Also $a_1=2$, $b_2=-5$, $a_3=1$, and all other coefficients are $0$.
step: Parseval with $L=\pi$: $\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dx=\frac{a_0^2}{2}+a_1^2+b_2^2+a_3^2=18+4+25+1=48$.
step: Multiply by $\pi$: $\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dx=48\pi$. Cross-check: the squares of the four pieces give $18\pi+4\pi+25\pi+\pi$ and every cross term integrates to zero by orthogonality.

@mistakes
- Treating $\sin mx$ and $\sin nx$ as orthogonal on an interval that is not a whole number of periods; orthogonality is a statement about $[-L,L]$ with the matching frequencies $\frac{n\pi}{L}$.
- Forgetting that $\|1\|^2=2L$ while $\|\cos\frac{n\pi x}{L}\|^2=L$; this is why $a_0$ carries the extra factor $\frac12$ in the series.
- Believing the series equals $f(L)$ at the end of the interval; it equals $\frac12[f(L^-)+f(-L^+)]$.
- Expecting the Gibbs overshoot to disappear as more terms are added.

answer: 1

@practice self
prompt: Let $f(x)=0$ for $-1<x<0$ and $f(x)=1-x$ for $0<x<1$, extended with period $2$ (so $L=1$). (a) Find $a_0$, $a_n$ and $b_n$. (b) State the value of the series at $x=0$ and at $x=\pm1$, and explain why the ends of the interval behave differently from $x=0$.
hint: Only the piece on $(0,1)$ contributes to the integrals; use $\cos n\pi x$, $\sin n\pi x$ and the prefactor $\frac1L=1$.
hint: For the endpoints, write down $f(0^-)$, $f(0^+)$, $f(1^-)$ and $f(-1^+)$ and average the pair that meets at each point of the periodic extension.
solution: (a) Only $(0,1)$ contributes. $a_0=\int_0^1(1-x)\,dx=\frac12$. By parts, $a_n=\int_0^1(1-x)\cos n\pi x\,dx=\frac{1-(-1)^n}{n^2\pi^2}$ and $b_n=\int_0^1(1-x)\sin n\pi x\,dx=\frac{1}{n\pi}$. (b) At $x=0$ the extension jumps from $f(0^-)=0$ to $f(0^+)=1$, so the series gives the midpoint $\frac12$. At $x=\pm1$ we have $f(1^-)=0$ and $f(-1^+)=0$: the ends match, so the periodic extension is continuous there and the series converges to $0$. The behavior at the ends is decided by the one-sided limits of the periodic extension, not by how the interval was cut.

hint: Convergence at a fixed point is different from convergence uniformly near the jump.
solution: The bump next to the jump keeps a height of about $9\%$ of the jump but becomes narrower and moves toward the jump, so every fixed point away from the jump is eventually left behind and $S_N(x)\to f(x)$ there. The overshoot is caused by a jump, not by a corner, and it never disappears.

prompt: The square wave ($f=1$ on $(0,\pi)$, $f=-1$ on $(-\pi,0)$) has $a_n=0$ and $b_n=\frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$, $b_n=0$ for even $n$. Apply Parseval's identity to find $\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k+1)^2}$.

hint: The left side is $\frac1\pi\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}f^2\,dx$ and $f^2=1$.
