@article
An **energy estimate** extracts information from a PDE without solving it. Build a positive quantity from $u$ and its derivatives, differentiate it in time, use the equation and an integration by parts, and read off a conclusion. The method gives uniqueness, stability and decay rates even where no formula is available.

### Wave energy

For $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$, let
$$E(t)=\frac12\int_0^L\left(u_t^2+c^2u_x^2\right)dx.$$
The first term is kinetic energy and the second is elastic potential energy (both per unit density). Differentiating and integrating the second term by parts,
$$E'(t)=\int_0^L\!\left(u_tu_{tt}+c^2u_xu_{xt}\right)dx=\int_0^L u_t\left(u_{tt}-c^2u_{xx}\right)dx+c^2\big[u_xu_t\big]_0^L=0.$$
The integral vanishes by the PDE and the boundary term vanishes because $u_t=0$ at the fixed ends. Energy is **conserved**.

**Uniqueness.** If $u_1,u_2$ solve the same problem, then $w=u_1-u_2$ has $f=g=0$, so $E_w(0)=0$. Conservation gives $E_w(t)=0$ for all $t$, hence $w_t=w_x=0$, $w$ is constant, and the boundary condition makes it $0$.

### Heat energy

For $u_t=ku_{xx}$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$ use $E(t)=\frac12\int_0^Lu^2\,dx$:
$$E'(t)=\int_0^Luu_t\,dx=k\int_0^Luu_{xx}\,dx=-k\int_0^Lu_x^2\,dx\le0.$$
Energy is dissipated, not conserved. The same uniqueness argument works, and the estimate also shows stability: a small change in $f$ cannot grow.

### A sharp decay rate

If $w$ vanishes at $0$ and $L$ and $w=\sum b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$, then Parseval gives $\int w^2=\frac L2\sum b_n^2$ and $\int w_x^2=\frac L2\sum\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^2b_n^2\ge\frac{\pi^2}{L^2}\int w^2$. Therefore $E'\le-\frac{2k\pi^2}{L^2}E$, and
$$E(t)\le E(0)\,e^{-2k\pi^2t/L^2}.$$
This is exactly the decay of the energy in the slowest mode, so the estimate cannot be improved.

### Energy in modes: displacement versus velocity

For the string write $u=\sum\left(A_n\cos\omega_nt+B_n\sin\omega_nt\right)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}$ with $\omega_n=\frac{n\pi c}{L}$. Orthogonality turns the energy integral into a sum:
$$E=\frac L4\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\omega_n^2\left(A_n^2+B_n^2\right).$$
At $t=0$ we also have $E=\frac12\int_0^L\left(g^2+c^2f'^2\right)dx$. Initial velocity brings in kinetic energy directly; initial displacement brings in potential energy through its slope. The two enter symmetrically: $A_n$ is the displacement amplitude of mode $n$ and $\omega_nB_n$ is its velocity amplitude. A mode started from displacement alone is a cosine in time; started from velocity alone, a sine. It is the same oscillation shifted by a quarter period, and energy sloshes between the kinetic and potential forms.

@example
title: Conservation of wave energy
problem: Show that $E(t)=\frac12\int_0^L(u_t^2+c^2u_x^2)\,dx$ is constant for solutions of $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$.
step: Differentiate under the integral sign: $E'=\int_0^L(u_tu_{tt}+c^2u_xu_{xt})\,dx$.
step: Integrate the second term by parts in $x$: $\int_0^Lc^2u_xu_{xt}\,dx=\big[c^2u_xu_t\big]_0^L-\int_0^Lc^2u_{xx}u_t\,dx$.
step: The boundary term is zero because $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$ for all $t$ implies $u_t(0,t)=u_t(L,t)=0$.
step: Combine: $E'=\int_0^Lu_t\left(u_{tt}-c^2u_{xx}\right)dx=0$ by the PDE, so $E(t)=E(0)$ for all $t$.

@example
title: Uniqueness for the heat equation
problem: Show that $u_t=ku_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$ and $u(x,0)=f(x)$ has at most one smooth solution.
step: Suppose $u_1,u_2$ are two solutions and let $w=u_1-u_2$. Then $w$ solves the same equation and boundary conditions, with $w(x,0)=0$.
step: Let $E(t)=\frac12\int_0^Lw^2\,dx\ge0$. Then $E'=\int_0^Lww_t\,dx=k\int_0^Lww_{xx}\,dx=-k\int_0^Lw_x^2\,dx$, since $\big[ww_x\big]_0^L=0$.
step: So $E'\le0$ and $E(0)=0$. Together with $E\ge0$ this forces $E(t)=0$ for all $t\ge0$.
step: A continuous function with $\int w^2=0$ is identically zero, so $w\equiv0$ and $u_1=u_2$.

@mistakes
- Dropping the boundary term after integration by parts without checking that the boundary conditions really make it vanish.
- Using $\frac12\int u^2\,dx$ as the energy of the wave equation; it is not conserved, because $\frac{d}{dt}\frac12\int u^2\,dx=\int uu_t\,dx$ has no definite sign.
- Forgetting the factor $c^2$ in front of $u_x^2$, which breaks the cancellation with $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$.
- Treating initial displacement and velocity as playing unrelated roles: both feed the same energy, with $A_n$ and $\omega_nB_n$ on an equal footing.

@practice num
prompt: The standing wave $u(x,t)=\sin x\cos t$ solves $u_{tt}=u_{xx}$ on $0<x<\pi$ with fixed ends. Compute its energy $E=\frac12\int_0^\pi\left(u_t^2+u_x^2\right)dx$ at $t=0.7$.
answer: pi/4
check: 0.5*quad(lambda x: nderiv(lambda s: sin(x)*cos(s), 0.7)**2 + nderiv(lambda s: sin(s)*cos(0.7), x)**2, 0, pi)
hint: Compute $u_t=-\sin x\sin t$ and $u_x=\cos x\cos t$.
hint: $\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx=\int_0^\pi\cos^2x\,dx=\frac\pi2$, and $\sin^2t+\cos^2t=1$.
solution: $E=\frac12\left[\sin^2t\cdot\frac\pi2+\cos^2t\cdot\frac\pi2\right]=\frac\pi4$, independent of $t$, as conservation of energy requires.

@practice mc
prompt: For $u_t=ku_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$ with $u(0,t)=u(L,t)=0$, let $E(t)=\frac12\int_0^Lu^2\,dx$. Which formula is correct?
choice*: $E'(t)=-k\displaystyle\int_0^Lu_x^2\,dx$
choice: $E'(t)=+k\displaystyle\int_0^Lu_x^2\,dx$
choice: $E'(t)=-k\displaystyle\int_0^Lu_{xx}^2\,dx$
choice: $E'(t)=0$
hint: Start with $E'=\int uu_t\,dx$ and replace $u_t$ using the equation.
hint: Integrate $\int uu_{xx}\,dx$ by parts; the boundary term $\big[uu_x\big]_0^L$ vanishes.
solution: $E'=k\int uu_{xx}\,dx=k\left[uu_x\right]_0^L-k\int u_x^2\,dx=-k\int u_x^2\,dx$. The sign is negative, so energy decreases; the other choices flip the sign, differentiate twice, or claim conservation.

answer: 5pi/4
check: 0.5*quad(lambda x: (2*sin(x))**2 + nderiv(lambda s: sin(s), x)**2, 0, pi)
hint: $g^2=4\sin^2x$ and $f'^2=\cos^2x$.
hint: Both $\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx$ and $\int_0^\pi\cos^2x\,dx$ equal $\frac\pi2$.
solution: $E=\frac12\left(4\cdot\frac\pi2+\frac\pi2\right)=\frac{5\pi}{4}$. By the mode formula, $A_1=1$, $\omega_1B_1=2$, so $B_1=2$ and $E=\frac\pi4(1+4)=\frac{5\pi}4$.

prompt: A string on $0<x<\pi$ with fixed ends has $c=1$. Compare (i) $f=\sin x$, $g=0$ with (ii) $f=0$, $g=\sin x$. (a) Solve both. (b) At what times is the string flat, and at what times is it moving fastest, in each case? (c) Compute the energy in each case and explain, in terms of the roles of displacement and velocity, why the two values agree.
hint: With $c=1$, $L=\pi$ the first mode has $\omega_1=1$, so (i) has $A_1=1$ and (ii) has $\omega_1B_1=1$.
hint: For the energy use $E=\frac12\int(g^2+f'^2)\,dx$ at $t=0$.
solution: (a) (i) $u=\sin x\cos t$; (ii) $u=\sin x\sin t$. (b) In (i) the string is flat at $t=\frac\pi2+m\pi$, exactly when it moves fastest; it is at maximum displacement and at rest at $t=m\pi$. In (ii) the roles swap: flat and fastest at $t=m\pi$, at maximum displacement and instantaneously at rest at $t=\frac\pi2+m\pi$. (c) $E_{(i)}=\frac12\int_0^\pi\cos^2x\,dx=\frac\pi4$ (all potential at $t=0$) and $E_{(ii)}=\frac12\int_0^\pi\sin^2x\,dx=\frac\pi4$ (all kinetic at $t=0$). They agree because the two motions are the same oscillation shifted by a quarter period, $\sin t=\cos(t-\frac\pi2)$; energy only changes form between potential and kinetic, and both amplitudes $A_1$ and $\omega_1B_1$ enter $E=\frac\pi4\omega_1^2(A_1^2+B_1^2)$ in the same way.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all true statements about energy methods for $u_{tt}=c^2u_{xx}$ and $u_t=ku_{xx}$ on $0<x<L$ with both ends fixed at $0$.
choice*: For the wave equation, $E=\frac12\int(u_t^2+c^2u_x^2)\,dx$ is constant in time.
choice*: Two solutions with the same initial data and boundary conditions must coincide, because their difference has zero energy.
choice: For the heat equation, $\frac12\int u^2\,dx$ is constant in time.
choice: Conservation of the wave energy shows that the wave equation smooths rough initial data.
hint: Recall the signs of $E'$ in the two computations.
hint: Conserved energy means nothing is lost; smoothing needs the high modes to decay.
solution: The statement that the wave energy is constant and the statement that solutions with the same data coincide follow from the computations in this lesson. The heat energy satisfies $E'=-k\int u_x^2dx\le0$, so it decreases. A conserved energy with $E=\frac L4\sum\omega_n^2(A_n^2+B_n^2)$ keeps all modes at constant amplitude, so no smoothing occurs.
