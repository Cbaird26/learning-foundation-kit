@article
**Laplace's equation**
$$\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$$
describes steady states: the temperature in a plate after the transients have died ($u_t=0$ in $u_t=k\Delta u$), the electrostatic potential in a region without charge, the height of a stretched membrane. There is no time variable; instead $u$ is prescribed on the whole boundary. Solutions are called **harmonic functions**. Examples are $x^2-y^2$, $xy$, $e^x\sin y$ and $\ln\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ (away from the origin); they come from the real and imaginary parts of the complex functions $z^2$, $e^z$ and $\log z$.

### Two key properties

- **Mean value property.** If $u$ is harmonic in a disc, its value at the center is the average over the boundary circle: $u(x_0,y_0)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}u(x_0+r\cos\theta,\,y_0+r\sin\theta)\,d\theta$.
- **Maximum principle.** A nonconstant harmonic function on a bounded region has no interior maximum or minimum. Its extreme values occur on the boundary, so an equilibrium temperature lies between the extreme boundary temperatures and has no interior hot spots.

### A rectangle

Solve $\Delta u=0$ on $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$ with $u=0$ on $x=0$, $x=a$ and $y=0$, and $u(x,b)=f(x)$ on top. Put $u=X(x)Y(y)$. Then $\frac{X''}{X}=-\frac{Y''}{Y}=-\lambda$, so
$$X''+\lambda X=0,\qquad Y''-\lambda Y=0.$$
The zero sides give $\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{a}\right)^2$ and $X_n=\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}$. In $y$ the equation $Y''=\lambda_nY$ has exponential, not oscillating, solutions; $Y(0)=0$ selects $Y_n=\sinh\frac{n\pi y}{a}$. Superposing and matching the top gives
$$u(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}b_n\,\frac{\sinh(n\pi y/a)}{\sinh(n\pi b/a)}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a},\qquad b_n=\frac2a\int_0^af(x)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx.$$
The denominator makes the factor equal to $1$ at $y=b$. For large $n$ the factor is about $e^{-n\pi(b-y)/a}$, so rough data on the top edge are smoothed out away from that edge. The sign flip between the $X$ and $Y$ equations is the whole difference from the heat and wave equations: Laplace's equation is not an evolution in $y$.

### A disk

In polar coordinates $\Delta u=u_{rr}+\frac1ru_r+\frac1{r^2}u_{\theta\theta}$. For $u(1,\theta)=h(\theta)$ on the unit disk, separation gives $\Theta''+\lambda\Theta=0$ with $2\pi$-periodic $\Theta$, so $\lambda=n^2$ and $\Theta=\cos n\theta,\sin n\theta$. The radial equation $r^2R''+rR'-n^2R=0$ has solutions $r^{n}$ and $r^{-n}$, and boundedness at the origin keeps $r^n$:
$$u(r,\theta)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}r^n\left(a_n\cos n\theta+b_n\sin n\theta\right),$$
where $a_n,b_n$ are the Fourier coefficients of $h$. At $r=0$ this gives $u=\frac{a_0}{2}$, the average of $h$: the mean value property again.

@example
title: A square with one hot side
problem: Solve $\Delta u=0$ on $0<x<\pi$, $0<y<\pi$ with $u=0$ on the left, right and bottom sides and $u(x,\pi)=1$ on top. Estimate $u\!\left(\frac\pi2,\frac\pi2\right)$.
step: Here $a=b=\pi$ and $b_n=\frac2\pi\int_0^\pi\sin nx\,dx=\frac{2(1-(-1)^n)}{n\pi}$, which is $\frac{4}{n\pi}$ for odd $n$ and $0$ for even $n$.
step: Therefore $u(x,y)=\frac4\pi\sum_{n\text{ odd}}\frac{\sinh ny}{n\sinh n\pi}\sin nx$.
step: At the center the first term is $\frac4\pi\cdot\frac{\sinh(\pi/2)}{\sinh\pi}=\frac{4}{\pi}\cdot\frac{1}{2\cosh(\pi/2)}\approx0.2537$; the $n=3$ term is $\approx-0.0038$, so the sum so far is $\approx0.2499$.
step: Symmetry explains the limit $\frac14$: adding the four copies of this solution, each with one side at $1$ and the rest at $0$, gives a harmonic function equal to $1$ on the whole boundary. By uniqueness it is the constant $1$, so each copy contributes $\frac14$ at the center.

@example
title: Dirichlet problem on the unit disk
problem: Find the harmonic function in the unit disk with boundary values $u(1,\theta)=\cos^2\theta$, and evaluate it at $r=\frac12$, $\theta=0$.
step: Rewrite the data as a Fourier series: $\cos^2\theta=\frac12+\frac12\cos2\theta$, so $\frac{a_0}{2}=\frac12$, $a_2=\frac12$, and all other coefficients are $0$.
step: Insert the factor $r^n$ in front of each term: $u(r,\theta)=\frac12+\frac12r^2\cos2\theta$.
step: Check in Cartesian form: $r^2\cos2\theta=x^2-y^2$, so $u=\frac12+\frac12(x^2-y^2)$, and $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=1-1=0$. On $r=1$ it equals $\frac12+\frac12\cos2\theta=\cos^2\theta$.
step: At $r=\frac12$, $\theta=0$: $u=\frac12+\frac18=\frac58$. The center value $u(0)=\frac12$ equals the average of $\cos^2\theta$, as the mean value property says.

@mistakes
- Using $\sin$ and $\cos$ in the $y$ variable: with data that vanish at both ends of the $x$ direction, the $y$ equation is $Y''-\lambda Y=0$, with $\sinh$ and $\cosh$ solutions.
- Putting the sine eigenfunctions in the direction where the data do not vanish at both ends; the eigenfunctions belong to the variable that carries the homogeneous conditions.
- Omitting the denominator $\sinh\frac{n\pi b}{a}$, so that the top boundary condition fails.
- Treating Laplace's equation as an initial-value problem. It needs conditions on the entire boundary.

@practice self
prompt: Solve $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0$ on the rectangle $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$ with $u=0$ on the sides $x=0$, $y=0$ and $y=b$, and $u(a,y)=g(y)$ on the right side. (a) Which variable gets the sine eigenfunctions, and why? (b) Write $u$ as a series with the coefficient formula. (c) Check that the series meets the boundary condition on the side $x=a$.
hint: The eigenvalue problem lives in the variable whose boundary conditions are homogeneous at both ends.
hint: By symmetry with the lesson's solution, swap the roles of $x$ and $y$ and of $a$ and $b$.
solution: (a) The homogeneous conditions $u=0$ at $y=0$ and $y=b$ hold on a whole interval of $x$, so the eigenproblem is in $y$: $Y''+\lambda Y=0$, $Y(0)=Y(b)=0$, giving $Y_n=\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}$ and $\lambda_n=\left(\frac{n\pi}{b}\right)^2$. Then $X''-\lambda_nX=0$ and $X(0)=0$ give $X_n=\sinh\frac{n\pi x}{b}$. (b) $u(x,y)=\sum_{n\ge1}c_n\frac{\sinh(n\pi x/b)}{\sinh(n\pi a/b)}\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}$ with $c_n=\frac2b\int_0^bg(y)\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}\,dy$. (c) At $x=a$ the quotient of sinh factors equals $1$, so $u(a,y)=\sum c_n\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}=g(y)$ (its sine series). At $x=0$ the factor $\sinh0$ vanishes, and at $y=0$ and $y=b$ the sines vanish.
rubric: Identifies $y$ as the eigenvalue variable and obtains $Y_n=\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}$ with $\lambda_n=(n\pi/b)^2$.
rubric: Obtains $\sinh\frac{n\pi x}{b}$ in $x$ (not an oscillation) and the normalizing denominator $\sinh\frac{n\pi a}{b}$.
rubric: Gives $c_n=\frac2b\int_0^bg(y)\sin\frac{n\pi y}{b}dy$ and verifies the condition at $x=a$ and the zero sides.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all true statements about harmonic functions on a bounded region.
choice*: The value of $u$ at the center of a disc inside the region equals the average of $u$ over the boundary circle of the disc.
choice*: A nonconstant harmonic function cannot attain its maximum at an interior point.
choice: Laplace's equation needs an initial condition in time, just as the heat equation does.
choice: Any function symmetric under rotation, such as $x^2+y^2$, is harmonic.
hint: Recall what the two key properties say, and test $x^2+y^2$ directly.
solution: The statement about the center of a disc is the mean value property, and the statement about interior maxima is the maximum principle. Laplace's equation has no time variable and requires conditions on the whole boundary. $x^2+y^2$ has $\Delta u=4\ne0$, so rotational symmetry does not imply harmonicity.
