@article
A Fourier series needs a periodic function, but many signals are not periodic: a single pulse, a decaying echo. The idea is to let the period $2L$ grow without bound. The frequencies $\frac{n\pi}{L}$ crowd together, and the sum over $n$ becomes an integral over a continuous frequency $\xi$.

### Definition and convention

For an absolutely integrable $f$ on $\mathbb R$,
$$\hat f(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-i\xi x}\,dx,\qquad f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(\xi)\,e^{i\xi x}\,d\xi.$$
This course uses $e^{-i\xi x}$ going forward and the factor $\frac1{2\pi}$ on the inverse. Other books put $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ in both directions or use $e^{-2\pi i\xi x}$, so always check the convention before quoting a formula. By Euler's formula $e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta$, the number $\hat f(\xi)$ measures how much of the oscillation of angular frequency $\xi$ is present in $f$; the inversion formula writes $f$ as a continuous superposition of those oscillations. If $f$ is even, $\hat f(\xi)=2\int_0^\infty f(x)\cos\xi x\,dx$ is real.

### Three transforms to know

| $f(x)$ | $\hat f(\xi)$ |
|---|---|
| $1$ for $\lvert x\rvert<a$, else $0$ | $\dfrac{2\sin a\xi}{\xi}$ |
| $e^{-a\lvert x\rvert}$, $a>0$ | $\dfrac{2a}{a^2+\xi^2}$ |
| $e^{-x^2/2}$ | $\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$ |

### Rules

| operation on $f$ | effect on $\hat f$ |
|---|---|
| shift: $f(x-x_0)$ | $e^{-i\xi x_0}\hat f(\xi)$ |
| scaling: $f(ax)$, $a\ne0$ | $\dfrac{1}{\lvert a\rvert}\hat f\!\left(\dfrac{\xi}{a}\right)$ |
| derivative: $f'(x)$ | $i\xi\,\hat f(\xi)$ |
| multiplication: $x f(x)$ | $i\,\hat f'(\xi)$ |

The derivative rule is why transforms solve PDEs: differentiation in $x$ becomes multiplication by $i\xi$, so a constant-coefficient equation turns into an algebraic or ordinary differential equation in $\xi$. The derivative rule follows by integrating by parts once, since $f\to0$ at $\pm\infty$.

### Reading a transform

The graph of $|\hat f(\xi)|$ is the **spectrum** of $f$. The scaling rule says that squeezing $f$ stretches $\hat f$: a narrow pulse needs a wide range of frequencies, a long gentle wave needs a narrow range. This trade-off is the mathematical core of the uncertainty principle. Energy is conserved by **Plancherel's identity**:
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2\,dx=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\hat f(\xi)|^2\,d\xi.$$
Smoothness shows up as decay: the box (with jumps) has $\hat f\sim\frac1\xi$, the exponential (with a corner) has $\hat f\sim\frac1{\xi^2}$, and the Gaussian (infinitely smooth) has a Gaussian transform.

@example
title: The transform of a two-sided exponential
problem: Compute $\hat f(\xi)$ for $f(x)=e^{-|x|}$.
step: $f$ is even, so $\hat f(\xi)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\cos\xi x\,dx$; the sine part of $e^{-i\xi x}$ integrates to zero.
step: Write $I=\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\cos\xi x\,dx$. Integrating by parts twice gives $I=1-\xi^2 I$.
step: Solve: $I=\frac{1}{1+\xi^2}$.
step: Therefore $\hat f(\xi)=\frac{2}{1+\xi^2}$. Check: $\hat f(0)=2=\int e^{-|x|}dx$, the total area of $f$.

@example
title: The derivative and multiplication rules agree
problem: Given $\widehat{e^{-x^2/2}}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, find the transform of $h(x)=-x\,e^{-x^2/2}$ in two ways.
step: Since $h=\frac{d}{dx}e^{-x^2/2}$, the derivative rule gives $\hat h(\xi)=i\xi\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$.
step: Second way: with $f=e^{-x^2/2}$ the multiplication rule gives $\widehat{xf}=i\hat f'(\xi)=i\left(-\xi\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}\right)$.
step: Since $h=-xf$, we get $\hat h(\xi)=+i\xi\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, the same answer.
step: Interpretation: $h$ is odd with zero total area, so $\hat h$ is purely imaginary, odd in $\xi$, and vanishes at $\xi=0$.

@mistakes
- Mixing conventions: forgetting the factor $\frac1{2\pi}$ in the inverse transform, or quoting a table that uses $e^{-2\pi i\xi x}$.
- Using the wrong sign in the shift rule; with $e^{-i\xi x}$ the factor is $e^{-i\xi x_0}$.
- Forgetting the $\frac{1}{|a|}$ in the scaling rule, which makes the area of $f(ax)$ come out wrong.
- Using the cosine shortcut $2\int_0^\infty f\cos\xi x\,dx$ for a function that is not even.

prompt: Let $f(x)=e^{-x^2}$, with the convention $\hat f(\xi)=\int f(x)e^{-i\xi x}\,dx$. Compute $\hat f(0)$.
answer: sqrt(pi)
check: quad(lambda x: exp(-x**2), -10, 10)
hint: Set $\xi=0$ in the definition: $e^{-i\cdot0\cdot x}=1$.
hint: So $\hat f(0)$ is the total area $\int_{-\infty}^\infty e^{-x^2}\,dx$, the Gaussian integral.
solution: $\hat f(0)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2}dx=\sqrt\pi$. In general $\hat f(0)$ is the total area under $f$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x)=1$ for $|x|<2$ and $f(x)=0$ otherwise. Using $\hat f(\xi)=\int_{-2}^{2}\cos\xi x\,dx$, compute $\hat f\!\left(\frac\pi4\right)$.
answer: 8/pi
check: quad(lambda x: cos(pi/4*x), -2, 2)
hint: Integrate $\cos\xi x$ to get $\frac{\sin\xi x}{\xi}$ and evaluate between $-2$ and $2$.
hint: $\hat f(\xi)=\frac{2\sin 2\xi}{\xi}$; now put $\xi=\frac\pi4$.
solution: $\hat f(\xi)=\frac{\sin2\xi-\sin(-2\xi)}{\xi}=\frac{2\sin2\xi}{\xi}$. At $\xi=\frac\pi4$: $\frac{2\sin(\pi/2)}{\pi/4}=\frac8\pi$.

@quiz num
prompt: Given $\widehat{e^{-x^2/2}}=\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, use the scaling rule to find the transform of $g(x)=e^{-2x^2}$ at $\xi=1$. Give an exact value.
answer: sqrt(pi/2)*e^(-1/8)
check: quad(lambda x: exp(-2*x**2)*cos(x), -10, 10)
hint: Write $g(x)=f(2x)$ where $f(x)=e^{-x^2/2}$ (since $\frac{(2x)^2}{2}=2x^2$).
hint: The scaling rule gives $\hat g(\xi)=\frac12\hat f\!\left(\frac\xi2\right)$.
solution: $\hat g(\xi)=\frac12\sqrt{2\pi}\,e^{-(\xi/2)^2/2}=\sqrt{\tfrac\pi2}\,e^{-\xi^2/8}$. At $\xi=1$ this is $\sqrt{\pi/2}\,e^{-1/8}\approx1.106$.
