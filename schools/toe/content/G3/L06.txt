@article
**Conventions.** SI units. The symbol $\mathbf S$ is energy flux, $u$ energy density and $\mathbf g$ momentum density, and $\hat n$ points out of the volume $V$.

### Energy balance in vacuum

Dot Faraday's law with $\mathbf B/\mu_0$ and the Ampère–Maxwell law with $\mathbf E$, subtract, and use $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B)=\mathbf B\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf E-\mathbf E\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf B$. The result is **Poynting's theorem**

$$\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E,\qquad u=\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}E^2+\frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2,\quad \mathbf S=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\mathbf E\times\mathbf B.$$

Here $\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E$ is the power per volume delivered to charges. In integral form

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_Vu\,d^3x+\oint_S\mathbf S\cdot\hat n\,da=-\int_V\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\,d^3x.$$

The theorem fixes only $\nabla\cdot\mathbf S$: adding the curl of any vector field to $\mathbf S$ changes no flux through a closed surface.

### Energy balance in matter: assumptions matter

With the macroscopic equations the same steps give

$$\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)+\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D+\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E.$$

The flux is $\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$, and the two middle terms are the rate of work on polarization and magnetization. You may write them as $\partial_tu$ only if they form an exact time derivative. That holds for **linear, lossless, non-dispersive** media, where $\epsilon$ and $\mu$ are real, constant and (if tensors) symmetric:

$$u=\tfrac12\left(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H\right).$$

Each assumption can fail. For ohmic conduction $\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E$ adds Joule heating $\sigma E^2$ on the right side. For a dispersive, absorbing medium no local $u$ exists in general. For a nearly monochromatic, weakly absorbing wave with complex amplitudes $\mathbf E_0,\mathbf H_0$ and time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, only the time average is defined: $\langle u\rangle=\frac14\left[\frac{d(\omega\epsilon)}{d\omega}|\mathbf E_0|^2+\frac{d(\omega\mu)}{d\omega}|\mathbf H_0|^2\right]$, while the imaginary parts of $\epsilon,\mu$ measure dissipation. Always state the material assumptions together with the energy balance.

### Momentum and the stress tensor

The vacuum field carries momentum density $\mathbf g=\epsilon_0\mathbf E\times\mathbf B=\mathbf S/c^2$. With the Maxwell stress tensor

$$T_{ij}=\epsilon_0\left(E_iE_j-\tfrac12\delta_{ij}E^2\right)+\frac1{\mu_0}\left(B_iB_j-\tfrac12\delta_{ij}B^2\right),$$

the Lorentz force on all charges inside $V$ obeys

$$\mathbf F=\oint_ST_{ij}n_j\,da-\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_V\mathbf S\,d^3x.$$

For static fields only the surface integral remains, and you may evaluate it on **any** surface in vacuum that encloses the body: $T_{ij}n_j$ is the force per area exerted on the interior by the fields outside. At a conductor surface with $\mathbf E=(\sigma/\epsilon_0)\hat n$ this gives an outward pull $\sigma^2/(2\epsilon_0)$ per area.

### Radiation pressure

A plane wave of time-averaged intensity $I=\langle|\mathbf S|\rangle$ carries momentum flux $I/c$. At normal incidence a perfect absorber receives pressure $I/c$ and a perfect reflector $2I/c$. At angle of incidence $\theta$ both are multiplied by $\cos^2\theta$: one factor for the reduced intensity per unit area of surface, one for the normal component of the momentum. For a wave of amplitude $E_0$ in vacuum, $I=\tfrac12\epsilon_0cE_0^2$.

@example
title: Where does a resistor's energy come from?
problem: A straight wire of radius $a$ and length $L$ carries a steady uniform current $I$ with voltage $V$ along its length. Show that the Poynting flux through its side surface equals the Joule heating.
step: Tangential $\mathbf E$ is continuous, so just outside the wire $\mathbf E=(V/L)\hat z$. Ampère's law gives $\mathbf H=\dfrac{I}{2\pi a}\hat\phi$ at the surface.
step: The flux is $\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H=\dfrac{V}{L}\dfrac{I}{2\pi a}\,(\hat z\times\hat\phi)=-\dfrac{VI}{2\pi aL}\hat\rho$, pointing radially inward.
step: The inward power through the side surface of area $2\pi aL$ is $\dfrac{VI}{2\pi aL}\cdot2\pi aL=VI$.
step: The Joule heating is $\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf E\,d^3x=\dfrac{I}{\pi a^2}\dfrac{V}{L}\,\pi a^2L=VI$. With $\partial_tu=0$ in steady state, Poynting's theorem balances exactly.
step: The energy enters from the surrounding field through the sides, not along the wire, and the balance holds no matter what the wire is made of, provided the current is steady.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbf S$ as uniquely defined locally. Only its flux through closed surfaces is physical.
- Using $u=\tfrac12(\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf D+\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf H)$ in a dispersive or absorbing medium, where it is not the stored energy.
- Dropping $-\dfrac{d}{dt}\int\mathbf g\,d^3x$ when the stress tensor is used for time-dependent fields.
- Taking the outward normal the wrong way, so that the sign of the flux, and hence of the force, flips.

choice*: Linear constitutive relations with real, frequency-independent (and symmetric) $\epsilon$ and $\mu$.
choice: The absence of free current.
choice: The fields are static.
choice: The medium is vacuum, $\epsilon=\epsilon_0$ and $\mu=\mu_0$.
hint: The question is when $\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D+\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B$ is an exact time derivative.
solution: For $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$ with constant real $\epsilon,\mu$, $\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D=\partial_t(\tfrac12\epsilon E^2)$, and similarly for the magnetic term. @practice self
prompt: Starting from the macroscopic Maxwell equations, derive the energy balance for a medium with ohmic conduction $\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E+\mathbf J_s$ (where $\mathbf J_s$ is an externally driven source current) and linear relations $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$, $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$ with constant real $\epsilon,\mu,\sigma$. State which assumptions identify the stored energy density and the Joule term, and say what changes if $\epsilon$ depends on frequency.
hint: Dot $\nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B$ with $\mathbf H$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D$ with $\mathbf E$, then subtract.
hint: Use $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)=\mathbf H\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf E-\mathbf E\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf H$ and write $\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D$ as a time derivative.
solution: Subtracting gives $\mathbf H\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf E-\mathbf E\cdot\nabla\times\mathbf H=-\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B-\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf J_f-\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D$. The left side is $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf S+\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D+\mathbf H\cdot\partial_t\mathbf B=-\mathbf J_f\cdot\mathbf E$ with $\mathbf S=\mathbf E\times\mathbf H$. For constant real $\epsilon,\mu$ the second and third terms equal $\partial_tu$ with $u=\tfrac12(\epsilon E^2+\mu H^2)$. With $\mathbf J_f=\sigma\mathbf E+\mathbf J_s$ the balance is $\partial_tu+\nabla\cdot\mathbf S=-\sigma E^2-\mathbf J_s\cdot\mathbf E$, where $\sigma E^2\ge0$ is irreversible Joule heating (local, instantaneous Ohm law) and $-\mathbf J_s\cdot\mathbf E$ is power supplied by the source. If $\epsilon$ depends on frequency, $\mathbf E\cdot\partial_t\mathbf D$ is not an exact derivative: no local $u$ exists in general, only a time-averaged one for narrow-band waves, and $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon$ represents dissipation.
rubric: Combines the two curl equations with the vector identity to obtain $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf E\times\mathbf H)$ and the work terms.
rubric: Identifies $u=\tfrac12(\epsilon E^2+\mu H^2)$ and states the assumption (linear, real, constant) that makes the work terms exact derivatives.
rubric: Obtains the Joule term $\sigma E^2$ and the source term with correct signs in the final balance.
rubric: States that with frequency-dependent $\epsilon$ there is no local stored-energy density, and that $\mathrm{Im}\,\epsilon$ gives dissipation.

@quiz num
prompt: A conductor in electrostatic equilibrium has surface charge density $\sigma=2.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C/m}^2$. Take $\epsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$. Use the Maxwell stress tensor on the field just outside the surface to find the outward force per unit area on the conductor.
answer: 0.2259
check: 0.5*8.854e-12*(2.0e-6/8.854e-12)**2
tol: 1%
unit: Pa
hint: Just outside, $\mathbf E=(\sigma/\epsilon_0)\hat n$ and the field inside the conductor is zero.
hint: For $\mathbf B=0$ and $\mathbf E$ along $\hat n$, $T_{nn}=\epsilon_0E^2/2$.
solution: With $\hat n$ out of the conductor, the force per area is $T_{ij}n_j$ evaluated on the outside, $\tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2$ along $\hat n$, so $\dfrac{\sigma^2}{2\epsilon_0}=\dfrac{(2.0\times10^{-6})^2}{2(8.854\times10^{-12})}\approx0.226$ Pa.

@quiz num
prompt: A perfectly reflecting mirror receives light of intensity $2.0\ \text{kW/m}^2$ at an angle of incidence of $60^\circ$ from the normal. Take $c=2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$. Find the radiation pressure (force per unit area of mirror, along the normal).
answer: 3.336e-6
check: 2*2.0e3/2.998e8*math.cos(math.radians(60))**2
tol: 1%
unit: Pa
hint: Per unit area of the mirror, the incident intensity is reduced by $\cos\theta$.
hint: Reflection reverses only the normal component of the momentum, which contributes another factor $\cos\theta$ and a factor 2.
solution: The momentum flux through a unit area of mirror is $(I/c)\cos\theta$ along the beam. Its normal component is $(I/c)\cos^2\theta$, and reflection doubles the normal momentum change, so $P=2(I/c)\cos^2\theta=2(2000)(0.25)/(2.998\times10^8)\approx3.34\times10^{-6}$ Pa.
