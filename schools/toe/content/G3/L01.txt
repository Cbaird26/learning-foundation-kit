@article
**Conventions.** SI units throughout, with $\epsilon_0$ and $\mu_0$ explicit and $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}$. In statics $\mathbf E = -\nabla\Phi$, so Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\epsilon_0$ becomes the **Poisson equation**

$$\nabla^2\Phi = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},$$

which is the **Laplace equation** wherever $\rho = 0$. Later weeks add the time convention $e^{-i\omega t}$ (from week 7) and the metric signature $(+,-,-,-)$ (week 11).

### What a boundary-value problem is

You are given a region $V$ with boundary $S$, the charge density $\rho$ inside $V$, and conditions on $S$. Let $\hat n$ be the unit normal pointing out of $V$ and write $\partial_n = \hat n\cdot\nabla$. The standard data are:

- **Dirichlet:** $\Phi$ is given on $S$ (conductors held at fixed potentials).
- **Neumann:** $\partial_n\Phi$ is given on $S$ (the normal field, equivalently a surface charge).
- **Mixed:** Dirichlet on one part of $S$, Neumann on the rest.

Two conditions hold across any charged surface, with $\hat n$ pointing from side 1 to side 2: the potential is continuous and the normal field jumps,

$$\Phi_2 = \Phi_1, \qquad \hat n\cdot(\mathbf E_2 - \mathbf E_1) = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}.$$

On an electrostatic conductor $\mathbf E = 0$ inside, $\Phi$ is constant over the whole conductor, the field outside is normal to the surface, and $\sigma = \epsilon_0 E_n$ with $\hat n$ pointing **out of the conductor**.

### Uniqueness

Suppose $\Phi_1$ and $\Phi_2$ solve the same Poisson problem with the same data. Then $\psi = \Phi_1 - \Phi_2$ satisfies $\nabla^2\psi = 0$ in $V$. Integrating $\nabla\cdot(\psi\nabla\psi) = |\nabla\psi|^2 + \psi\nabla^2\psi$ over $V$ gives

$$\int_V |\nabla\psi|^2\, d^3x = \oint_S \psi\,\partial_n\psi\, da.$$

The right side vanishes if, on each piece of $S$, either $\psi = 0$ or $\partial_n\psi = 0$. Then $\nabla\psi = 0$ and $\psi$ is constant. With Dirichlet data on at least part of $S$ the constant is zero, so the solution is **unique**. With pure Neumann data, $\Phi$ is fixed only up to an additive constant, and the data must obey $\oint_S \partial_n\Phi\, da = -Q_V/\epsilon_0$ (Gauss's law, $Q_V$ the charge in $V$) or no solution exists. In an unbounded region you also need $\Phi \to 0$ at infinity fast enough ($\Phi = O(1/r)$ suffices) for the surface term at infinity to vanish. Giving the total charge on each conductor instead of its potential also yields a unique answer.

### The method of images

Uniqueness licenses a strategy: *guess* fictitious charges **outside** $V$ so that the real-plus-fictitious potential meets the data on $S$. Inside $V$ that potential is the solution, because no other exists.

- **Grounded plane.** A charge $q$ at distance $d$ from the plane has an image $-q$ at the mirror point.
- **Grounded sphere of radius $a$.** A charge $q$ at distance $d$ from the center has an image $q' = -qa/d$ on the same ray at distance $b = a^2/d$ from the center. This works for $d>a$ (field outside) and $d<a$ (field inside, image outside).
- **Isolated neutral sphere.** Add $+qa/d$ at the center of the grounded-sphere system. For a sphere held at potential $V_0$, add $4\pi\epsilon_0 aV_0$ at the center.

The images are bookkeeping. The force on $q$ is the Coulomb force from the images, but the energy is not that of the real-plus-image pair: the field exists only in $V$, so the work needed to bring $q$ in from infinity to a distance $d$ from the plane is $-q^2/(16\pi\epsilon_0 d)$, half the two-charge value.

@example
title: Induced charge and force for a charge above a grounded plane
problem: A point charge $q$ sits at height $d$ above the grounded plane $z=0$. Find the induced surface charge density, the total induced charge, and the force on $q$.
step: Place an image charge $-q$ at $(0,0,-d)$. The sum of the two potentials vanishes on $z=0$, has the right source in $z>0$, and decays at infinity, so by uniqueness it is the potential for $z>0$.
step: At a point $(\rho,0,0)$ of the plane both charges give the same $z$-component, and the parallel components cancel. With $R=\sqrt{\rho^2+d^2}$, $E_z = -\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2d}{R^3}$.
step: Take $\hat n=+\hat z$ (out of the conductor): $\sigma = \epsilon_0 E_z = -\dfrac{qd}{2\pi(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}$, negative and concentrated within a few $d$ of the point below $q$.
step: Integrate over the plane: $\int_0^\infty \sigma\,2\pi\rho\,d\rho = -qd\int_0^\infty \dfrac{\rho\,d\rho}{(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}} = -qd\cdot\dfrac1d = -q$. The plane carries exactly $-q$.
step: The force on $q$ is the Coulomb force from the image at distance $2d$: $F = \dfrac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0(2d)^2} = \dfrac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0 d^2}$, directed toward the plane.

@mistakes
- Placing an image charge inside the region where the potential is wanted. Images must lie outside $V$, or they change the Poisson equation there.
- Using the full two-charge energy $-q^2/(8\pi\epsilon_0 d)$ for a charge near a conducting plane. The physical energy is half of it, because the field exists only in $V$.
- Treating Neumann data as enough for a unique $\Phi$ (it fixes $\Phi$ only up to a constant), or choosing Neumann data that violate Gauss's law.
- Using $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_n$ with $\hat n$ pointing into the conductor, which flips the sign of the induced charge.

@practice expr
prompt: A point charge $q$ is at height $d$ above the grounded conducting plane $z=0$, and the region of interest is $z>0$. Find the induced surface charge density $\sigma(\rho)$ on the plane at distance $\rho$ from the point directly below the charge. Use $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_n$ with $\hat n$ pointing out of the conductor.
vars: q, d, rho
lead: $\sigma(\rho) =$
answer: -q*d/(2*pi*(rho^2 + d^2)^(3/2))
check: lambda q, d, rho: -nderiv(lambda z: q/(4*pi)*(1/sqrt(rho**2 + (z - d)**2) - 1/sqrt(rho**2 + (z + d)**2)), 0)
domain: q: 0.5..2; d: 0.5..1.5; rho: 0..2
hint: Write the potential for $z>0$ from $q$ and its image, then use $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\,\partial_z\Phi$ at $z=0$.
hint: Differentiate $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z-d)^2}}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z+d)^2}}$ with respect to $z$ and set $z=0$.
solution: With the image $-q$ at $-d$, $\Phi = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z-d)^2}}-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\rho^2+(z+d)^2}}\right]$. Then $\partial_z\Phi|_{z=0} = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\cdot\dfrac{2d}{(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}$ and $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\partial_z\Phi|_{0} = -\dfrac{qd}{2\pi(\rho^2+d^2)^{3/2}}$.

@practice self
prompt: A grounded conducting sphere of radius $a$ has a point charge $q$ at distance $d>a$ from its center. Show that the image charge $q' = -qa/d$ at distance $b = a^2/d$ from the center makes the potential vanish at every point of the sphere, not only on the axis. Then state what must be added to describe an isolated sphere carrying zero net charge, and why uniqueness justifies the result.
hint: For a surface point at angle $\gamma$ from the axis through $q$, write the distances to $q$ and to $q'$ with the law of cosines.
hint: Substitute $b=a^2/d$ and compare the two distances.
solution: For a surface point at angle $\gamma$ from the axis, $R_1^2 = a^2+d^2-2ad\cos\gamma$ and $R_2^2 = a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma$. With $b=a^2/d$, $R_2^2 = \dfrac{a^2}{d^2}\left(d^2+a^2-2ad\cos\gamma\right) = \dfrac{a^2}{d^2}R_1^2$, so $R_2 = (a/d)R_1$. Then $\dfrac{q}{R_1}+\dfrac{q'}{R_2} = \dfrac{q}{R_1}-\dfrac{qa}{d}\cdot\dfrac{d}{aR_1} = 0$ for every $\gamma$. The induced charge on the grounded sphere is $q'$, so for a neutral isolated sphere add $+qa/d$ at the center. That keeps the sphere an equipotential and its net charge zero; since the potential outside then satisfies Poisson's equation and the conductor conditions, uniqueness says it is the solution.
rubric: Uses the law of cosines for both distances with the same angle $\gamma$.
rubric: Shows $R_2=(a/d)R_1$ from $b=a^2/d$ and concludes the potential vanishes for all $\gamma$.
rubric: Adds $+qa/d$ at the center for the neutral isolated sphere.
rubric: Invokes uniqueness for the region outside the sphere.
