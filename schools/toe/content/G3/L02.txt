@article
Last week's uniqueness proof used one trick: integrate a divergence. Two identities generalize it, and with a **Green function** they turn boundary data directly into a formula for $\Phi$. Conventions are as in week 1: SI units, $\hat n$ points out of $V$, $\partial_n=\hat n\cdot\nabla$.

### Green's identities

The divergence theorem applied to $\phi\nabla\psi$ gives the **first identity**:

$$\int_V\left(\phi\nabla^2\psi + \nabla\phi\cdot\nabla\psi\right)d^3x = \oint_S \phi\,\partial_n\psi\, da.$$

Swap $\phi\leftrightarrow\psi$ and subtract to cancel the gradient term. The result is the **second identity**:

$$\int_V\left(\phi\nabla^2\psi - \psi\nabla^2\phi\right)d^3x = \oint_S\left(\phi\,\partial_n\psi - \psi\,\partial_n\phi\right)da.$$

Setting $\phi=\psi=\Phi$ in the first identity is the uniqueness argument. With $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi$ it also gives the field energy in $V$:

$$\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_V|\mathbf E|^2\,d^3x = \frac12\int_V\rho\,\Phi\,d^3x + \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\oint_S\Phi\,\partial_n\Phi\,da.$$

### Green functions

A **Green function** obeys, in the source variable $\mathbf x'$,

$$\nabla'^2 G(\mathbf x,\mathbf x') = -\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf x').$$

This course's normalization has no factor $4\pi$ and carries a minus sign, so $G/\epsilon_0$ is the potential of a unit point charge. In free space $G_0 = 1/(4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|)$. Any other choice is $G=G_0+F$ with $\nabla'^2F=0$ in $V$: the harmonic piece $F$ is yours to tune.

### The representation formula

Put $\phi=\Phi$ (with $\nabla'^2\Phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$) and $\psi=G$ in the second identity and integrate over $\mathbf x'$, with $\mathbf x$ inside $V$. The delta function picks out $-\Phi(\mathbf x)$, and

$$\Phi(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V \rho\,G\,d^3x' - \oint_S\left[\Phi\,\partial_{n'}G - G\,\partial_{n'}\Phi\right]da'.$$

This holds for every admissible $G$, but it is a *representation*, not a solution: it contains both $\Phi$ and $\partial_n\Phi$ on $S$, and uniqueness says only one may be prescribed. Choose $G$ so that the unknown term disappears.

**Dirichlet.** Require $G_D=0$ for $\mathbf x'$ on $S$. Then

$$\Phi(\mathbf x) = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,G_D\,d^3x' - \oint_S\Phi\,\partial_{n'}G_D\,da'.$$

**Neumann.** Integrating $\nabla'^2G=-\delta$ over $V$ gives $\oint_S\partial_{n'}G\,da'=-1$, so $\partial_{n'}G$ cannot vanish on $S$. Take $\partial_{n'}G_N=-1/A$, with $A$ the area of $S$:

$$\Phi(\mathbf x) = \langle\Phi\rangle_S + \frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,G_N\,d^3x' + \oint_S G_N\,\partial_{n'}\Phi\,da'.$$

Here $\langle\Phi\rangle_S$ is the average of $\Phi$ over $S$: the constant that Neumann data cannot fix.

### What $\partial_{n'}G_D$ means

Let $S$ be a grounded conductor. A point charge $q$ at $\mathbf x$ has potential $qG_D/\epsilon_0$ and induces the surface density $\sigma(\mathbf x')=q\,\partial_{n'}G_D$ (from $\sigma=\epsilon_0\partial_n\Phi$ with $\hat n$ pointing out of $V$, into the conductor). Since $\oint\partial_{n'}G_D\,da'=-1$, the induced charge is $-q$ in total. So $-\partial_{n'}G_D$ is a positive weight that integrates to $1$, and the Dirichlet surface term says: *the potential is a weighted average of the boundary potential.* A second use of the identity shows $G_D(\mathbf x,\mathbf x')=G_D(\mathbf x',\mathbf x)$.

### Two kernels worth knowing

For the half-space $z>0$ with $\rho=0$, the image Green function $G_D=\frac{1}{4\pi}\left[1/R-1/R_*\right]$ gives

$$\Phi(\mathbf x)=\frac{z}{2\pi}\int\frac{\Phi(x',y',0)\,dx'\,dy'}{\left[(x-x')^2+(y-y')^2+z^2\right]^{3/2}}.$$

For the interior of a ball of radius $a$ with $\rho=0$,

$$\Phi(\mathbf x)=\oint\frac{a(a^2-r^2)}{4\pi\left(a^2+r^2-2ar\cos\gamma\right)^{3/2}}\,\Phi(a,\Omega')\,d\Omega',$$

with $\gamma$ the angle between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$. At $r=0$ it reduces to the mean-value property $\Phi(0)=\langle\Phi\rangle_S$. For the exterior region (with $\Phi\to0$ at infinity), replace $a^2-r^2$ by $r^2-a^2$.

@example
title: Potential above a disc held at fixed potential
problem: The plane $z=0$ is grounded except for a disc of radius $a$ centered on the $z$-axis, held at potential $V_0$. There is no charge in $z>0$ and $\Phi\to0$ at infinity. Find $\Phi$ on the $z$-axis.
step: The region is the half-space $z>0$ with Dirichlet data on its boundary, so use the image Green function $G_D=\dfrac{1}{4\pi}\left[\dfrac1R-\dfrac1{R_*}\right]$, which vanishes on $z'=0$.
step: The outward normal on the plane is $-\hat z$, so $-\partial_{n'}G_D=\partial_{z'}G_D\big|_{z'=0}$. The two terms give $(z-z')/R^3$ and $(z+z')/R_*^3$, which add to $2z/R^3$ at $z'=0$. The kernel is $\dfrac{z}{2\pi R^3}$, with $R^2=\rho'^2+z^2$ for a point on the axis.
step: With $\rho=0$ only the Dirichlet surface term survives, and the data is $V_0$ on the disc and zero elsewhere: $\Phi(0,0,z)=\displaystyle\int_0^a V_0\,\frac{z}{2\pi(\rho'^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\,2\pi\rho'\,d\rho'$.
step: The integrand is a perfect derivative: $\Phi(0,0,z)=V_0z\left[-\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\rho'^2+z^2}}\right]_0^a=V_0\left(1-\dfrac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+a^2}}\right)$.
step: Check the limits. As $z\to0^+$, $\Phi\to V_0$, the disc potential. For $z\gg a$, $\Phi\approx V_0a^2/(2z^2)$: the falloff is faster than $1/z$ because every charge on the plane has an opposite image.

@mistakes
- Using $+\partial_{z'}$ instead of $\partial_{n'}=-\partial_{z'}$ at the plane. The outward normal points away from the region, and the wrong sign gives a potential of the opposite sign.
- Mixing normalizations: here $\nabla'^2G=-\delta$ (no $4\pi$), so $G=1/(4\pi R)$. A formula quoted for $G=1/R$ has different prefactors.
- Trying to impose both $\Phi$ and $\partial_n\Phi$ on $S$. For Laplace's equation that over-determines the problem.
- Setting $\partial_{n'}G_N=0$ on all of $S$ for a Neumann problem, which contradicts $\oint\partial_{n'}G\,da'=-1$.

prompt: Starting from Green's second identity, derive the representation of $\Phi(\mathbf x)$ inside $V$ in terms of $\rho$ and the boundary values of $\Phi$ for a Dirichlet Green function $G_D$ obeying $\nabla'^2G_D=-\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf x')$. Explain which boundary term you eliminate and why, and state the sign convention for the normal derivative.
hint: Substitute $\phi=\Phi$, $\psi=G$ and use both differential equations inside $V$.
hint: Let the delta function act, then decide which surface term involves data you are not allowed to prescribe.
solution: With $\phi=\Phi$ and $\psi=G$, $\nabla'^2\Phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$ and $\nabla'^2G=-\delta^3(\mathbf x'-\mathbf x)$, the left side of the second identity is $-\Phi(\mathbf x)+\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,G\,d^3x'$. The right side is $\oint_S\left(\Phi\,\partial_{n'}G-G\,\partial_{n'}\Phi\right)da'$. Hence $\Phi(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\int_V\rho\,G\,d^3x'-\oint_S\left(\Phi\,\partial_{n'}G-G\,\partial_{n'}\Phi\right)da'$.
