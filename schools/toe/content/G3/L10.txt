@article
**Definitions.** A plane wave of time-averaged intensity $I_{\rm inc}$ falls on a scatterer. The scattered power per solid angle defines the **differential cross-section**, and its integral the **scattering cross-section**:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\frac{1}{I_{\rm inc}}\frac{dP_{\rm sc}}{d\Omega},\qquad \sigma_{\rm sc}=\int\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\,d\Omega.$$

Absorbed power gives $\sigma_{\rm abs}=P_{\rm abs}/I_{\rm inc}$, and the **extinction** cross-section is $\sigma_{\rm ext}=\sigma_{\rm sc}+\sigma_{\rm abs}$. Conventions are those of weeks 7 and 9: SI units, $e^{-i\omega t}$, $k=\omega/c$.

### Small scatterers: the induced dipole

For an object of size $a$ with $ka\ll1$, the incident field $E_0\hat\epsilon_0\,e^{i(\mathbf k_0\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}$ induces a dipole $\mathbf p=\alpha\,\mathbf E_{\rm inc}$, where $\alpha$ is the polarizability (in units of $\epsilon_0\times$ volume). The dipole radiates as in week 9. For scattered polarization $\hat\epsilon$,

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=k^4\left|\frac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right|^2\left|\hat\epsilon^*\cdot\hat\epsilon_0\right|^2.$$

Summing over the two scattered polarizations and averaging over the incident ones (an unpolarized beam), with $\theta$ the scattering angle,

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=k^4\left|\frac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right|^2\frac{1+\cos^2\theta}{2},\qquad \sigma_{\rm sc}=\frac{8\pi}{3}\,k^4\left|\frac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right|^2.$$

Light scattered at $\theta=90^\circ$ is completely linearly polarized, perpendicular to the scattering plane. Since $\sigma\propto k^4\propto\lambda^{-4}$, short wavelengths scatter more strongly.

### Three polarizabilities

- **Free electron:** $m\ddot{\mathbf x}=-e\mathbf E$ gives $\alpha=-e^2/(m\omega^2)$, hence the frequency-independent **Thomson** cross-section $\sigma_T=\tfrac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2$, with $r_e=e^2/(4\pi\epsilon_0mc^2)$.
- **Dielectric sphere** (quasi-static, week 5): $\alpha=4\pi\epsilon_0a^3\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}$. With complex $\epsilon_r$ the particle absorbs. The time-averaged work on the dipole is $\tfrac12\omega\,\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha\,|E_0|^2$, so $\sigma_{\rm abs}=(k/\epsilon_0)\,\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha\propto a^3$, while $\sigma_{\rm sc}\propto a^6$. Small absorbing particles mostly absorb.
- **Perfectly conducting sphere:** the induced electric and magnetic dipoles both contribute, giving $\dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=k^4a^6\left[\tfrac58(1+\cos^2\theta)-\cos\theta\right]$ and $\sigma=\tfrac{10\pi}{3}k^4a^6$, with a preference for backscattering.

### Validity and error

The Rayleigh formulas need $ka\ll1$ and, for a dielectric, also $\sqrt{|\epsilon_r|}\,ka\ll1$, so that the field inside is nearly uniform. The leading corrections are of relative order $(ka)^2$, multiplied by a coefficient that depends on $\epsilon_r$. They come from retardation across the particle and from magnetic-dipole and quadrupole terms. Near a resonance of $\alpha$ (for example $\epsilon_r\to-2$) the quasi-static polarizability diverges and the estimate fails.

### Consistency: the optical theorem

For a lossless particle, $\sigma_{\rm ext}=\sigma_{\rm sc}$, yet $\sigma_{\rm ext}=(k/\epsilon_0)\,\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_{\rm eff}$ vanishes for a real quasi-static $\alpha$. The resolution is radiation reaction: the dipole radiates, so $\alpha_{\rm eff}=\alpha\big/\left[1-ik^3\alpha/(6\pi\epsilon_0)\right]$ and $\mathrm{Im}\,\alpha_{\rm eff}\approx k^3\alpha^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0)$. Then $\sigma_{\rm ext}=k^4\alpha^2/(6\pi\epsilon_0^2)$, which equals $\sigma_{\rm sc}$ computed above.

@example
title: Thomson scattering
problem: A free electron is driven by a non-relativistic plane wave. Find its differential and total cross sections for an unpolarized beam, and state when the result fails.
step: Newton's law with $e^{-i\omega t}$ gives $\mathbf x=\dfrac{e}{m\omega^2}\mathbf E$, so $\mathbf p=-e\mathbf x=-\dfrac{e^2}{m\omega^2}\mathbf E$ and $\alpha=-\dfrac{e^2}{m\omega^2}$.
step: Then $k^2\left|\dfrac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right|=\dfrac{\omega^2}{c^2}\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0m\omega^2}=r_e$, with $r_e=\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0mc^2}=2.818\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}$.
step: Hence $\dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=r_e^2\,\dfrac{1+\cos^2\theta}{2}$, and $\sigma_T=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}r_e^2=6.65\times10^{-29}\ \text{m}^2$, independent of frequency.
step: The result needs $v\ll c$ (field amplitude $eE_0/(m\omega c)\ll1$) and photon energy $\hbar\omega\ll mc^2$ so that recoil is negligible. Beyond these limits, relativistic motion or Compton scattering takes over.

@example
title: Rayleigh scattering by a small dielectric sphere
problem: A sphere of radius $a$ and relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$ is illuminated by unpolarized light of wavenumber $k$, with $ka\ll1$. Find $d\sigma/d\Omega$, $\sigma$, and the conditions for validity.
step: From week 5, a dielectric sphere in a uniform field acquires $\mathbf p=4\pi\epsilon_0a^3\,g\,\mathbf E$ with $g=\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}$. So $\dfrac{\alpha}{4\pi\epsilon_0}=a^3g$.
step: Substituting into the dipole result, $\dfrac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=k^4a^6g^2\,\dfrac{1+\cos^2\theta}{2}$ for unpolarized light.
step: Integrating, $\sigma=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\,k^4a^6\left(\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\right)^2$. It grows as $a^6$ and falls as $\lambda^{-4}$.
step: At $\theta=90^\circ$ the scattered light is linearly polarized perpendicular to the scattering plane. Forward and backward intensities are equal.
step: The quasi-static polarizability needs the field inside to be uniform: $ka\ll1$ and $\sqrt{|\epsilon_r|}\,ka\ll1$. The leading correction is of relative order $(ka)^2$, so quote it with the result.

@mistakes
- Using a Rayleigh formula without checking both $ka\ll1$ and $\sqrt{|\epsilon_r|}\,ka\ll1$.
- Treating scattering and absorption alike. For small particles absorption scales with the volume and scattering with the volume squared.
- Using the factor $(1+\cos^2\theta)/2$ for a polarized beam. It holds for an unpolarized beam; a linearly polarized beam has an azimuthal dependence.
- Using the Thomson cross-section for relativistic electron motion or photon energies comparable to $mc^2$.

@practice num
prompt: A glass bead of radius $a=20\ \text{nm}$ and $\epsilon_r=2.25$ is illuminated by unpolarized light of vacuum wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$. Use the Rayleigh formula $\sigma=\frac{8\pi}{3}k^4a^6\left(\frac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\right)^2$ to find its scattering cross-section.
answer: 1.157
check: 8*pi/3*(2*pi/500)**4*20**6*((2.25-1)/(2.25+2))**2
tol: 1%
unit: nm^2
hint: Work in nanometres throughout: $k=2\pi/500\ \text{nm}^{-1}$.
hint: Evaluate $k^4a^6$ in $\text{nm}^2$, then multiply by $8\pi/3$ and by $\left(1.25/4.25\right)^2$.

@practice ms
prompt: Unpolarized light scatters from a small dielectric sphere with $ka\ll1$ and $\sqrt{|\epsilon_r|}\,ka\ll1$. Which statements are true?
choice*: The scattering cross-section is proportional to $\lambda^{-4}$.
choice*: Light scattered at $90^\circ$ to the incident direction is completely linearly polarized.
choice*: The scattered intensities in the forward and backward directions are equal.
choice: The scattering cross-section is independent of frequency.
hint: Start from $d\sigma/d\Omega\propto k^4(1+\cos^2\theta)$.
hint: A dipole radiates no component of $\mathbf E$ along its own axis, and the incident $\mathbf E$ is perpendicular to the beam.
solution: $\sigma\propto k^4\propto\lambda^{-4}$. The pattern $1+\cos^2\theta$ is symmetric under $\theta\to\pi-\theta$. At $90^\circ$ only the incident polarization component perpendicular to the scattering plane radiates toward the observer, so the light is fully polarized. Frequency independence is the Thomson result for a free electron, not the Rayleigh result.
