@article
**Idea.** Laplace's equation is linear. When the boundaries lie on coordinate surfaces, look for product solutions, let the homogeneous boundary conditions select a discrete set of separation constants, superpose the products, and match the remaining boundary data with orthogonality. Units are SI.

### Cartesian coordinates

Write $\Phi=X(x)Y(y)$ in two dimensions: $X''/X+Y''/Y=0$, so $X''=-k^2X$ and $Y''=k^2Y$. For a channel $0<x<a$, $y>0$ with grounded side walls, $X=\sin(n\pi x/a)$ with $k=n\pi/a$, and decay as $y\to\infty$ selects $e^{-n\pi y/a}$:

$$\Phi(x,y)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,e^{-n\pi y/a},\qquad b_n=\frac2a\int_0^a\Phi(x,0)\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx.$$

The factor $2/a$ comes from $\int_0^a\sin^2(n\pi x/a)\,dx=a/2$. In a region of finite height in $y$, use $\sinh$ or $\cosh$ in place of the decaying exponential.

### Spherical coordinates, axisymmetric

Write $\Phi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$ and $x=\cos\theta$. The angular equation is Legendre's equation, and regularity at both poles ($x=\pm1$) forces the separation constant to be $l(l+1)$ with $l=0,1,2,\dots$, giving $\Theta=P_l(\cos\theta)$. The radial equation $r^2R''+2rR'-l(l+1)R=0$ has solutions $r^l$ and $r^{-l-1}$:

$$\Phi(r,\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^\infty\left(A_lr^l+B_lr^{-l-1}\right)P_l(\cos\theta).$$

Regularity picks the terms. **Inside** a ball (origin included) $B_l=0$. **Outside** a ball with $\Phi\to0$ at infinity $A_l=0$. **Between** two spheres keep both. To match $\Phi(a,\theta)=\sum_l c_lP_l(\cos\theta)$ use

$$\int_{-1}^{1}P_l(x)P_{l'}(x)\,dx=\frac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{ll'},\qquad c_l=\frac{2l+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1\Phi(a,\arccos x)\,P_l(x)\,dx.$$

Then $A_l=c_la^{-l}$ inside and $B_l=c_la^{l+1}$ outside. Without azimuthal symmetry the angular functions become the spherical harmonics $Y_{lm}$ and the sums also run over $m$.

### Cylindrical coordinates

For $\Phi=R(\rho)\,e^{\pm kz}$ with no $\phi$-dependence, $\rho^2R''+\rho R'+k^2\rho^2R=0$: Bessel's equation of order $0$. Regularity on the axis keeps $J_0(k\rho)$ and discards $Y_0$, which diverges as $\rho\to0$. A grounded wall at $\rho=a$ requires $J_0(ka)=0$, so $k_n=j_{0n}/a$, where $j_{0n}$ is the $n$th zero of $J_0$ and $j_{01}\approx2.405$. Orthogonality carries the weight $\rho$:

$$\int_0^aJ_0\!\left(\frac{j_{0n}\rho}{a}\right)J_0\!\left(\frac{j_{0n'}\rho}{a}\right)\rho\,d\rho=\frac{a^2}{2}J_1(j_{0n})^2\,\delta_{nn'},\qquad \int_0^aJ_0\!\left(\frac{j_{0n}\rho}{a}\right)\rho\,d\rho=\frac{a^2J_1(j_{0n})}{j_{0n}}.$$

Along the tube the slowest mode dominates at long range: $\Phi\sim e^{-j_{01}z/a}$.

### Matching series to geometry

Sines go with grounded parallel walls, Legendre polynomials with spherical boundaries, Bessel functions with cylindrical walls. Always test the result against limits: the value on the boundary, the behaviour at infinity, and the symmetry of the data. The series converge quickly away from the data surface and slowly (with Gibbs oscillations) at a discontinuity of the boundary data.

@example
title: A grounded channel with a raised floor
problem: A channel $0<x<a$, $y>0$ has grounded side walls and $\Phi\to0$ as $y\to\infty$. The floor $y=0$ is held at the constant potential $V_0$. Find $\Phi$ and evaluate it at the center point $(a/2,\,a/2)$.
step: The side walls need $\sin(n\pi x/a)$, and decay at large $y$ needs $e^{-n\pi y/a}$, so $\Phi=\sum_{n\ge1}b_n\sin(n\pi x/a)\,e^{-n\pi y/a}$.
step: At $y=0$ this must equal $V_0$, so $b_n=\dfrac2a\displaystyle\int_0^aV_0\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,dx=\frac{2V_0}{n\pi}\left(1-\cos n\pi\right)$.
step: Even $n$ give $0$ and odd $n$ give $4V_0/(n\pi)$, so $\Phi=\dfrac{4V_0}{\pi}\displaystyle\sum_{n\ \mathrm{odd}}\frac1n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\,e^{-n\pi y/a}$.
step: At the center $\sin(n\pi/2)=\pm1$ and $e^{-n\pi/2}$ shrinks fast: $\Phi\approx\dfrac{4V_0}{\pi}\left(e^{-\pi/2}-\tfrac13e^{-3\pi/2}+\tfrac15e^{-5\pi/2}\right)\approx0.261\,V_0$.
step: Check with the closed form $\Phi=\dfrac{2V_0}{\pi}\arctan\dfrac{\sin(\pi x/a)}{\sinh(\pi y/a)}$, which gives $0.2610\,V_0$ at the center.

@example
title: A grounded conducting sphere in a uniform field
problem: A grounded conducting sphere of radius $a$ sits in a uniform field $\mathbf E_0=E_0\hat z$ that is present far away. Find $\Phi$ outside and the induced surface charge density.
step: Far away $\Phi\to-E_0z=-E_0r\cos\theta$. In the exterior series only $l=1$ can grow like $r$, so $A_1=-E_0$ and the other $A_l=0$: $\Phi=-E_0r\cos\theta+\sum_lB_lr^{-l-1}P_l(\cos\theta)$.
step: The conductor is grounded, so $\Phi(a,\theta)=0$ for all $\theta$. Orthogonality of the $P_l$ gives $B_1=E_0a^3$ and every other $B_l=0$.
step: Therefore $\Phi=-E_0\left(r-\dfrac{a^3}{r^2}\right)\cos\theta$ for $r\ge a$.
step: The outward normal from the conductor is $\hat r$, so $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_r\big|_a=-\epsilon_0\partial_r\Phi\big|_a=3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$. Its integral over the sphere vanishes, consistent with $B_0=0$ (no net charge).

@mistakes
- Keeping both $r^l$ and $r^{-l-1}$ in a region that contains the origin or extends to infinity, instead of letting regularity discard one of them.
- Dropping the factor $2/a$ in the sine coefficients, or the factor $(2l+1)/2$ in the Legendre coefficients, because the basis functions are not normalized to $1$.
- Forgetting the weight $\rho$ in the Bessel orthogonality integral.
- Expecting a Fourier series to converge uniformly at a point where the boundary data jump (for example the corners of the channel floor).

answer: -15.92
check: 2*quad(lambda x: 50*x*sin(2*pi*x), 0, 1)

unit: V
hint: $b_n=\frac{2}{a}\int_0^a\Phi(x,0)\sin(n\pi x/a)\,dx$. Set $a=1$, since only $x/a$ matters.
hint: Integrate by parts: $\int_0^1x\sin(2\pi x)\,dx$.
solution: $b_2=2\int_0^1 50x\sin(2\pi x)\,dx=100\left[-\dfrac{x\cos2\pi x}{2\pi}+\dfrac{\sin2\pi x}{4\pi^2}\right]_0^1=-\dfrac{50}{\pi}\approx-15.92$ V.

@practice self
prompt: Separate the axisymmetric Laplace equation $\dfrac{1}{r^2}\partial_r\!\left(r^2\partial_r\Phi\right)+\dfrac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\partial_\theta\!\left(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta\Phi\right)=0$ with $\Phi=R(r)\Theta(\theta)$. Show that with separation constant $l(l+1)$ the radial solutions are $r^l$ and $r^{-l-1}$, and explain why regularity on the polar axis forces $l$ to be a non-negative integer.
hint: Multiply by $r^2/(R\Theta)$ so that one side depends only on $r$ and the other only on $\theta$.
hint: Try $R=r^s$ in the radial equation and solve for $s$. For the angular part set $x=\cos\theta$.
solution: Multiplying by $r^2/(R\Theta)$ gives $\dfrac1R\dfrac{d}{dr}\!\left(r^2R'\right)=-\dfrac{1}{\Theta\sin\theta}\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\!\left(\sin\theta\,\Theta'\right)=\lambda$, a constant. The radial equation is $r^2R''+2rR'-\lambda R=0$; with $R=r^s$ it gives $s(s+1)=\lambda$, and writing $\lambda=l(l+1)$ gives $s=l$ or $s=-l-1$. With $x=\cos\theta$ the angular equation is Legendre's equation, $\dfrac{d}{dx}\!\left[(1-x^2)\Theta'\right]+l(l+1)\Theta=0$. Its power-series solutions are finite at both $x=\pm1$ only if the series terminates, which happens for $l=0,1,2,\dots$; otherwise the solution diverges logarithmically at a pole. Then $\Theta=P_l(\cos\theta)$.
rubric: Separates the equation into a radial and an angular ordinary differential equation with a common constant.
rubric: Obtains $s(s+1)=l(l+1)$ and the two radial solutions $r^l$, $r^{-l-1}$.
rubric: Identifies the angular equation as Legendre's equation in $x=\cos\theta$.
rubric: Explains that finiteness at both poles requires a terminating series, hence integer $l\ge0$.

@quiz num
prompt: A sphere of radius $a$ has its upper hemisphere held at $+10\ \text{V}$ and its lower hemisphere at $-10\ \text{V}$, with an insulating gap at the equator. Find the potential on the $+z$ axis at $z=2a$ outside the sphere, with $\Phi\to0$ at infinity.
answer: 3.292
check: sum((2*l+1)*10*quad(lambda x: special.eval_legendre(l, x), 0, 1)*0.5**(l+1) for l in range(1, 80, 2))
tol: 1%
unit: V
hint: Even-$l$ coefficients vanish by antisymmetry. For odd $l$, $c_l=(2l+1)V_0\int_0^1P_l(x)\,dx$, and outside $\Phi=\sum_lc_l(a/r)^{l+1}P_l(\cos\theta)$.
hint: The first coefficients are $c_1=\frac32V_0$, $c_3=-\frac78V_0$, $c_5=\frac{11}{16}V_0$. Sum terms at $r=2a$ until they are negligible.
solution: With $V_0=10$ V, $c_1=15$, $c_3=-8.75$, $c_5=6.875$, and so on. At $r=2a$ the sum $\sum_l c_l2^{-(l+1)}$ converges to about $3.292$ V. The closed form $V_0\left[1-\dfrac{z^2-a^2}{z\sqrt{z^2+a^2}}\right]$ gives the same value.

@quiz num
prompt: A grounded metal tube of radius $a$ occupies $z>0$ and is closed at $z=0$ by an insulated end disc held at $100\ \text{V}$. The tube is charge-free inside and $\Phi\to0$ as $z\to\infty$. Expand in the modes $J_0(j_{0n}\rho/a)\,e^{-j_{0n}z/a}$ and find the potential on the axis at $z=a$.
answer: 14.05
check: sum(2*100/(j*special.jv(1, j))*exp(-j) for j in special.jn_zeros(0, 60))
tol: 1%
unit: V
hint: Match $\Phi(\rho,0)=V_0$ using orthogonality with weight $\rho$: $A_n=V_0\int_0^aJ_0(j_{0n}\rho/a)\rho\,d\rho\Big/\left[\tfrac{a^2}{2}J_1(j_{0n})^2\right]$.
hint: This gives $A_n=\dfrac{2V_0}{j_{0n}J_1(j_{0n})}$. On the axis $J_0(0)=1$, so $\Phi(0,a)=\sum_nA_ne^{-j_{0n}}$. A few terms suffice.
