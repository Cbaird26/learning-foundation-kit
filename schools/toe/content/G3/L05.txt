@article
**Conventions.** SI units. Macroscopic fields are averages of the microscopic fields over regions large compared with atomic spacings but small compared with the scale on which the averaged fields vary. The polarization $\mathbf P$ is dipole moment per volume and the magnetization $\mathbf M$ is magnetic moment per volume. Define

$$\mathbf D=\epsilon_0\mathbf E+\mathbf P,\qquad \mathbf H=\frac{\mathbf B}{\mu_0}-\mathbf M.$$

### Bound sources

Averaging the multipole expansion of the molecules shows that matter acts like extra sources:

$$\rho_b=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf P,\quad \sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat n,\qquad \mathbf J_b=\nabla\times\mathbf M+\partial_t\mathbf P,\quad \mathbf K_b=\mathbf M\times\hat n,$$

with $\hat n$ the outward normal of the material. Splitting charge and current into free (subscript $f$) and bound parts turns Maxwell's equations into

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=\rho_f,\quad \nabla\times\mathbf H=\mathbf J_f+\partial_t\mathbf D,\quad \nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0,\quad \nabla\times\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf B.$$

These are exact consequences of the averaging, but they are not closed until constitutive relations express $\mathbf P$ and $\mathbf M$ through the fields.

### Interfaces

With $\hat n$ pointing from medium 1 to medium 2, free surface charge $\sigma_f$ and free surface current $\mathbf K_f$ on the interface,

$$\hat n\cdot(\mathbf D_2-\mathbf D_1)=\sigma_f,\quad \hat n\times(\mathbf E_2-\mathbf E_1)=0,\quad \hat n\cdot(\mathbf B_2-\mathbf B_1)=0,\quad \hat n\times(\mathbf H_2-\mathbf H_1)=\mathbf K_f.$$

### Constitutive assumptions

The simplest closure is **linear, isotropic, local and instantaneous**: $\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E$, so $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ with $\epsilon=\epsilon_0(1+\chi_e)=\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$, and $\mathbf B=\mu\mathbf H$. Each word is an assumption. Nonlinear media break linearity, crystals need a tensor $\epsilon_{ij}$, strong gradients need spatial nonlocality, and every real material responds with a delay, so $\epsilon$ depends on frequency (week 7). Results below hold only under the stated assumptions.

In a uniform linear dielectric with no free charge, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D=0$ gives $\nabla^2\Phi=0$, and the interface conditions become

$$\Phi_1=\Phi_2,\qquad \epsilon_1\partial_n\Phi_1=\epsilon_2\partial_n\Phi_2.$$

For field lines making angles $\theta_{1,2}$ with the normal, continuity of tangential $\mathbf E$ and normal $\mathbf D$ combine to $\epsilon_2\tan\theta_1=\epsilon_1\tan\theta_2$: lines bend away from the normal in the medium of larger $\epsilon$.

### Depolarization

A dielectric body does not feel only the applied field: its own bound surface charge changes the interior field. For a sphere of relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$ in a uniform external field $\mathbf E_0$ in vacuum, the interior field is uniform,

$$\mathbf E_{\rm in}=\frac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}\,\mathbf E_0,$$

and outside the sphere the field is $\mathbf E_0$ plus that of a dipole. For a spherical cavity in a medium of relative permittivity $\epsilon_r$, with $\mathbf E_0$ the far field in the medium, $\mathbf E_{\rm cav}=\dfrac{3\epsilon_r}{2\epsilon_r+1}\mathbf E_0$.

### Magnetic media

A uniformly magnetized sphere has no bulk bound current, only the surface current $\mathbf K_b=\mathbf M\times\hat n=M\sin\theta\,\hat\phi$. Summing the fields of the rings gives a uniform interior $\mathbf B=\tfrac23\mu_0\mathbf M$, so $\mathbf H=\mathbf B/\mu_0-\mathbf M=-\mathbf M/3$ inside: $\mathbf H$ opposes $\mathbf M$ there. Here $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$ differ in direction, which is why the boundary conditions for $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$ must not be mixed.

@example
title: Dielectric sphere in a uniform field
problem: A sphere of radius $a$ and permittivity $\epsilon=\epsilon_r\epsilon_0$ is placed in vacuum with a uniform field $E_0\hat z$ far away, and there is no free charge. Find the potential, the field inside, and the sphere's dipole moment.
step: Use axisymmetric separation of variables. Regularity at the origin and the far-field condition leave $\Phi_{\rm in}=A\,r\cos\theta$ and $\Phi_{\rm out}=-E_0\,r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2$.
step: Continuity of $\Phi$ at $r=a$ gives $A=-E_0+B/a^3$.
step: With $D_r=-\epsilon\,\partial_r\Phi$ and $\sigma_f=0$, continuity of $D_r$ gives $-\epsilon A=\epsilon_0\left(E_0+2B/a^3\right)$.
step: Solving, $B=\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}E_0a^3$ and $A=-\dfrac{3E_0}{\epsilon_r+2}$. The interior field $-A\hat z=\dfrac{3}{\epsilon_r+2}E_0\hat z$ is uniform and smaller than $E_0$.
step: The dipole term outside is $p\cos\theta/(4\pi\epsilon_0r^2)$, so $p=4\pi\epsilon_0B=4\pi\epsilon_0\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}a^3E_0$. Check: $\mathbf P=(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)\mathbf E_{\rm in}$ is uniform, and $P\cdot\frac43\pi a^3$ gives the same $p$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbf D$ as determined by free charge alone. Its divergence is $\rho_f$, but $\nabla\times\mathbf D=\nabla\times\mathbf P$ need not vanish, so the boundary conditions are still required.
- Using continuity of normal $\mathbf E$ at a dielectric interface. It is normal $\mathbf D$ that is continuous when $\sigma_f=0$; normal $\mathbf E$ jumps by the bound surface charge over $\epsilon_0$.
- Taking $\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat n$ with $\hat n$ pointing into the material, which flips the sign of the bound charge.
- Writing $\mathbf P=\epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E_0$ with the applied field instead of the total macroscopic field inside the body.

answer: -1.5e-4

@practice num
prompt: A spherical vacuum cavity is cut in a large block of dielectric with $\epsilon_r=4$. Far from the cavity the field inside the dielectric is uniform with magnitude $6.0\ \text{kV/m}$. Find the (uniform) field inside the cavity.
answer: 8.0
check: -lin([[1, -1], [1, 8]], [-6.0, -24.0])[0]
unit: kV/m
tol: 1%
hint: Take $\Phi_{\rm in}=Ar\cos\theta$ and $\Phi_{\rm out}=-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2$, and match $\Phi$ and $D_r$ at the wall.
hint: With $a=1$ the matching conditions are $A-B=-E_0$ and $-\epsilon_{\rm in}A=\epsilon_{\rm out}(E_0+2B)$, with $\epsilon_{\rm in}=1$ and $\epsilon_{\rm out}=4$ in units of $\epsilon_0$.
solution: The equations $A-B=-E_0$ and $A+8B=-4E_0$ give $B=-E_0/3$ and $A=-4E_0/3$. The cavity field is $4E_0/3=8.0$ kV/m, in agreement with $3\epsilon_r/(2\epsilon_r+1)=12/9$.

@practice ms
prompt: A planar interface carries free surface charge $\sigma_f\neq0$ but no free surface current. Which quantities are continuous across it?
choice*: The tangential component of $\mathbf E$.
choice*: The normal component of $\mathbf B$.
choice*: The tangential component of $\mathbf H$.
choice: The normal component of $\mathbf D$.
hint: Compare each condition with the general interface conditions, replacing $\sigma_f$ and $\mathbf K_f$ by their values.
hint: A nonzero $\sigma_f$ affects exactly one of the four conditions.
solution: $\hat n\times\Delta\mathbf E=0$, $\hat n\cdot\Delta\mathbf B=0$ and $\hat n\times\Delta\mathbf H=\mathbf K_f=0$ hold. The normal component of $\mathbf D$ jumps by $\sigma_f\neq0$.

@practice num
prompt: A sphere of radius $5.0\ \text{cm}$ is uniformly magnetized with $\mathbf M=1.0\times10^5\ \text{A/m}\ \hat z$ and carries no free current. Take $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$. Find $B$ at its center by summing the fields of the bound surface-current rings.
answer: 0.0838
check: quad(lambda t: 4e-7*pi*1.0e5/2*sin(t)**3, 0, pi)
tol: 1%
unit: T
hint: The bound surface current is $K_b=M\sin\theta$ along $\hat\phi$. A ring between $\theta$ and $\theta+d\theta$ carries $dI=K_b\,a\,d\theta$ at radius $a\sin\theta$.
hint: A ring of radius $s$ and current $dI$ produces $\mu_0\,dI\,s^2/(2a^3)$ at the center, where $a$ is the distance from every ring point to the center. Integrate over $\theta$.
solution: $dB=\dfrac{\mu_0}{2}M\sin^3\theta\,d\theta$, and $\int_0^\pi\sin^3\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac43$, so $B=\tfrac23\mu_0M=\tfrac23(4\pi\times10^{-7})(10^5)\approx0.0838$ T. Nothing depends on the sphere's radius.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the interior field of a sphere of permittivity $\epsilon_1$ embedded in an infinite medium of permittivity $\epsilon_2$, with uniform far field $\mathbf E_0$ in the medium and no free charge. Specialize to a vacuum cavity ($\epsilon_1=\epsilon_0$) and to a sphere in vacuum ($\epsilon_2=\epsilon_0$), and find the bound surface charge on a vacuum cavity wall.
hint: Match $\Phi$ and $\epsilon\,\partial_r\Phi$ at $r=a$ using the interior $A\,r\cos\theta$ and exterior $-E_0r\cos\theta+B\cos\theta/r^2$.
hint: The cavity wall is the boundary of the dielectric. Take $\hat n$ out of the dielectric, that is, into the cavity.
solution: Continuity of $\Phi$ gives $A=-E_0+B/a^3$. Continuity of $D_r$ gives $-\epsilon_1A=\epsilon_2(E_0+2B/a^3)$. Eliminating $B$ gives $A=-\dfrac{3\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_1+2\epsilon_2}E_0$, so $E_{\rm in}=\dfrac{3\epsilon_2}{\epsilon_1+2\epsilon_2}E_0$. For a sphere in vacuum, $\epsilon_2=\epsilon_0$ gives $3/(\epsilon_r+2)$. For a vacuum cavity, $\epsilon_1=\epsilon_0$ gives $3\epsilon_r/(2\epsilon_r+1)$. On the cavity wall the dielectric polarization is $P=(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)E_r^{\rm out}$ and $\hat n=-\hat r$, so $\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat n=-(\epsilon-\epsilon_0)E_r^{\rm out}(a)=-\dfrac{3\epsilon_0(\epsilon_r-1)}{2\epsilon_r+1}E_0\cos\theta$ (from continuity of $D_r$, $\epsilon E_r^{\rm out}(a)=\epsilon_0E_{\rm in}\cos\theta$ with $E_{\rm in}=3\epsilon_rE_0/(2\epsilon_r+1)$), which is why the cavity field exceeds $E_0$.
rubric: Writes the correct interior and exterior $l=1$ potentials and the two matching conditions ($\Phi$ and $D_r$).
rubric: Solves for $A$ and states $E_{\rm in}=3\epsilon_2E_0/(\epsilon_1+2\epsilon_2)$.
rubric: Recovers both special cases, $3/(\epsilon_r+2)$ and $3\epsilon_r/(2\epsilon_r+1)$.
rubric: Uses $\sigma_b=\mathbf P\cdot\hat n$ with $\hat n$ pointing out of the dielectric (into the cavity).

@quiz mc
prompt: Replacing $\mathbf D=\epsilon\mathbf E$ by $\mathbf D(\omega)=\epsilon(\omega)\mathbf E(\omega)$ gives up which of the four assumptions linear, isotropic, local in space, and instantaneous in time?
choice*: Instantaneous in time: the response at time $t$ depends on the field at earlier times.
choice: Linearity: the response is no longer proportional to the field.
choice: Isotropy: $\mathbf D$ is no longer parallel to $\mathbf E$.
choice: Locality in space: $\mathbf D$ at a point depends on $\mathbf E$ at neighbouring points.
hint: A product $\epsilon(\omega)\mathbf E(\omega)$ in frequency space corresponds to a convolution in the other variable.
solution: A product of transforms is a convolution in time, so $\mathbf D(t)$ depends on $\mathbf E$ at earlier times. The relation is still linear, isotropic and local in space. Spatial nonlocality would require a dependence on the wavevector, $\epsilon(\mathbf k,\omega)$.
