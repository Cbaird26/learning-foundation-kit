@article
**Conventions.** SI units. Coordinates $x^\mu=(ct,\mathbf x)$; metric $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(+1,-1,-1,-1)$, so $x_\mu=(ct,-\mathbf x)$. Derivatives are $\partial_\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,\nabla)$ and $\partial^\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,-\nabla)$, with $\Box=\partial_\mu\partial^\mu=c^{-2}\partial_t^2-\nabla^2$. Greek indices run over $0,1,2,3$, Latin over $1,2,3$, and repeated indices are summed. The four-current and four-potential are

$$J^\mu=(c\rho,\mathbf J),\qquad A^\mu=(\Phi/c,\mathbf A),$$

and charge conservation is $\partial_\mu J^\mu=0$.

### The field-strength tensor

Define $F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu A_\nu-\partial_\nu A_\mu$. With $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, the contravariant components are $F^{0i}=-E^i/c$ and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B^k$:

$$F^{\mu\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-E_x/c&-E_y/c&-E_z/c\\E_x/c&0&-B_z&B_y\\E_y/c&B_z&0&-B_x\\E_z/c&-B_y&B_x&0\end{pmatrix}.$$

### Maxwell's equations and the force law

$$\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu,\qquad \partial_\lambda F_{\mu\nu}+\partial_\mu F_{\nu\lambda}+\partial_\nu F_{\lambda\mu}=0.$$

The $\nu=0$ component of the first equation is Gauss's law and $\nu=j$ is the Ampère–Maxwell law. The second equation contains $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and Faraday's law, and it holds identically once $F=\partial A-\partial A$. Applying $\partial_\nu$ to the first and using antisymmetry gives $\partial_\nu J^\nu=0$. The Lorentz force on a charge $q$ with $u^\mu=\gamma(c,\mathbf v)$ is

$$\frac{dp^\mu}{d\tau}=q\,F^{\mu\nu}u_\nu.$$

### Transformations and invariants

Tensors transform with the Lorentz matrix, $F'^{\mu\nu}=\Lambda^\mu{}_\alpha\Lambda^\nu{}_\beta F^{\alpha\beta}$. For a frame $S'$ moving with velocity $\mathbf v$ relative to $S$, with $\beta=v/c$ and $\gamma=(1-\beta^2)^{-1/2}$,

$$\mathbf E'_\parallel=\mathbf E_\parallel,\quad \mathbf E'_\perp=\gamma(\mathbf E+\mathbf v\times\mathbf B)_\perp,\qquad \mathbf B'_\parallel=\mathbf B_\parallel,\quad \mathbf B'_\perp=\gamma\left(\mathbf B-\frac{\mathbf v\times\mathbf E}{c^2}\right)_\perp,$$

where $\parallel$ and $\perp$ refer to $\mathbf v$. The fields are not components of a four-vector: they mix as the components of $F^{\mu\nu}$. The two Lorentz invariants are

$$F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}=2\left(B^2-\frac{E^2}{c^2}\right),\qquad \mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B.$$

If $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $E<cB$, a frame with $\mathbf E'=0$ moves at $\mathbf v=\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$. If $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $E>cB$, a frame with $\mathbf B'=0$ moves at $\mathbf v=c^2\,\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/E^2$.

### Energy–momentum tensor

$$T^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{\mu_0}\left[F^{\mu\lambda}F_\lambda{}^{\nu}+\frac14\,\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}\right],$$

with $T^{00}=u$ (the energy density), $T^{0i}=S^i/c$, and $T^{ij}=-\sigma_{ij}$, where $\sigma_{ij}$ is the Maxwell stress tensor of week 6 (written $T_{ij}$ there). Its divergence is the force density on the sources,

$$\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}=-F^{\nu\lambda}J_\lambda.$$

Its $\nu=0$ component is Poynting's theorem and its $\nu=i$ components are the momentum balance of week 6.

@example
title: Field of a uniformly moving point charge by a boost
problem: A point charge $q$ moves with constant velocity $v\hat x$ in the lab frame $S$. Use the field transformation to find $\mathbf E$ at the instant the charge passes the origin, and check the invariant $E^2-c^2B^2$.
step: In the rest frame $S'$ of the charge, $\mathbf E'=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\mathbf r'}{r'^3}$ and $\mathbf B'=0$. The frame $S'$ moves with velocity $v\hat x$ relative to $S$, so $S$ moves with $-v\hat x$ relative to $S'$.
step: At $t=0$ in the lab, a field point $(x,y,z)$ has $x'=\gamma x$, $y'=y$, $z'=z$ in $S'$, so $r'^2=\gamma^2x^2+\rho^2$ with $\rho^2=y^2+z^2$.
step: Transform back with $-\mathbf v$. Because $\mathbf B'=0$, $E_x=E'_x$ and $\mathbf E_\perp=\gamma\mathbf E'_\perp$. Therefore $E_x=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\gamma x}{(\gamma^2x^2+\rho^2)^{3/2}}$ and $E_\perp=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{\gamma\rho}{(\gamma^2x^2+\rho^2)^{3/2}}$.
step: In the transverse plane $x=0$, $E_\perp=\gamma\,q/(4\pi\epsilon_0\rho^2)$ is enhanced by $\gamma$. On the line of motion, $E_x=q/(4\pi\epsilon_0\gamma^2x^2)$ is reduced by $\gamma^{-2}$.
step: The magnetic field in $S$ is $\mathbf B=\mathbf v\times\mathbf E/c^2$, so $c^2B^2=\beta^2E_\perp^2$. Then $E^2-c^2B^2=E_x^2+E_\perp^2/\gamma^2=E_x'^2+E_\perp'^2=E'^2$, as the invariant requires.

@example
title: Crossed fields and the drift frame
problem: In a frame $S$, $\mathbf E=E_0\hat y$ and $\mathbf B=B_0\hat z$ with $E_0<cB_0$. Find a frame $S'$ in which the electric field vanishes and compute the magnetic field there.
step: Since $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B=0$ and $E_0<cB_0$, the invariants allow a frame with $\mathbf E'=0$. Boost along $x$ with speed $v$. Then $E'_y=\gamma(E_y-vB_z)=\gamma(E_0-vB_0)$.
step: Setting $E'_y=0$ gives $v=E_0/B_0=c\,(E_0/cB_0)<c$. This equals $\mathbf E\times\mathbf B/B^2$, directed along $+\hat x$.
step: The magnetic field is $B'_z=\gamma(B_z-vE_y/c^2)=\gamma B_0\left(1-\dfrac{E_0^2}{c^2B_0^2}\right)=B_0\sqrt{1-\dfrac{E_0^2}{c^2B_0^2}}$.
step: Check the invariant: $B'^2=B_0^2-E_0^2/c^2=B^2-E^2/c^2$.
step: A charge in the crossed fields drifts at $v=E_0/B_0$ in $S$; in $S'$ it moves in a circle, as in a purely magnetic field.

@mistakes
- Mixing signature conventions. With $(+,-,-,-)$, $x_\mu=(ct,-\mathbf x)$ and $\partial_\mu=(c^{-1}\partial_t,\nabla)$. Formulas from a $(-,+,+,+)$ source change signs.
- Misplacing signs or factors of $c$ in $F^{\mu\nu}$, so that Gauss's law or the Lorentz force comes out wrong. Always test your matrix on $\nu=0$.
- Treating $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$ as components of four-vectors. They transform as the components of an antisymmetric tensor and mix with each other.
- Contracting $F^{\mu\nu}J_\nu$ with the upper-index current. The lower-index current is $J_\nu=(c\rho,-\mathbf J)$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following quantities is NOT invariant under a Lorentz boost?
choice*: $E^2+c^2B^2$
choice: $E^2-c^2B^2$
choice: $\mathbf E\cdot\mathbf B$
choice: $c^2\rho^2-J^2$
hint: One of these is a component of the energy-momentum tensor rather than a scalar built from a full contraction.
solution: $E^2+c^2B^2$ is proportional to the energy density $T^{00}$, which is a tensor component and changes under boosts. @practice self
prompt: Using the component table of $F^{\mu\nu}$, show that the equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu$ reproduces Gauss's law for $\nu=0$ and the Ampère–Maxwell law for $\nu=j$, and show that it implies charge conservation.
hint: For $\nu=0$ the sum over $\mu$ contains only spatial derivatives of $F^{i0}$.
hint: For $\nu=j$ use $F^{0j}=-E^j/c$ and $F^{ij}=-\epsilon^{ijk}B^k$, and note $\epsilon^{jik}\partial_iB^k=(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^j$.
solution: For $\nu=0$: $\partial_iF^{i0}=\partial_iE^i/c=\mu_0c\rho$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\mu_0c^2\rho=\rho/\epsilon_0$. For $\nu=j$: $\partial_0F^{0j}+\partial_iF^{ij}=-\dfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_tE^j-\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_iB^k=-\dfrac{1}{c^2}\partial_tE^j+(\nabla\times\mathbf B)^j=\mu_0J^j$, which is the Ampère–Maxwell law. Applying $\partial_\nu$ to the equation gives $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0\partial_\nu J^\nu$. The left side vanishes because $F^{\mu\nu}$ is antisymmetric and the derivatives commute, so $\partial_\nu J^\nu=0$, that is $\partial_t\rho+\nabla\cdot\mathbf J=0$.
rubric: Evaluates the $\nu=0$ component with the correct signs and obtains $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ (using $\mu_0c^2=1/\epsilon_0$).
rubric: Evaluates the $\nu=j$ components and identifies $\nabla\times\mathbf B-c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E=\mu_0\mathbf J$.
rubric: Derives $\partial_\nu J^\nu=0$ from the antisymmetry of $F^{\mu\nu}$ and the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives.

@quiz num
prompt: In a region, $\mathbf E=3.0\times10^7\ \text{V/m}\ \hat x$ and $\mathbf B=0.20\ \text{T}\ \hat y$. Take $c=3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$ and $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$. Evaluate $T^{00}=\dfrac{1}{\mu_0}\left[F^{0\lambda}F_\lambda{}^{0}+\tfrac14\eta^{00}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}\right]$ and give the energy density.
answer: 1.989e4
check: (lambda F, eta, mu0: ((F @ (eta @ F @ eta) @ eta + 0.25*eta*np.sum((eta @ F @ eta)*F))/mu0)[0, 0])(np.array([[0, -0.1, 0, 0], [0.1, 0, 0, 0.2], [0, 0, 0, 0], [0, -0.2, 0, 0]]), np.diag([1, -1, -1, -1]), 4e-7*pi)
tol: 1%
unit: J/m^3
hint: $F^{0\lambda}F_\lambda{}^0=E^2/c^2$ and $F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}=2(B^2-E^2/c^2)$.
hint: The result should equal $\dfrac{1}{2\mu_0}\left(B^2+E^2/c^2\right)$.
solution: $T^{00}=\dfrac{1}{\mu_0}\left[\dfrac{E^2}{c^2}+\dfrac12\left(B^2-\dfrac{E^2}{c^2}\right)\right]=\dfrac{1}{2\mu_0}\left(B^2+\dfrac{E^2}{c^2}\right)=\dfrac{0.04+0.01}{2(4\pi\times10^{-7})}\approx1.989\times10^4\ \text{J/m}^3$.
