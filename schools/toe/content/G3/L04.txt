@article
**Setting.** SI units. A charge density $\rho$ lies inside the sphere $r'<R$ about the origin. You want its potential far away, at $r>R$, without redoing the integral for every field point.

### The expansion

Start from the exact potential and the generating function of the Legendre polynomials,

$$\Phi(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf x')}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}\,d^3x',\qquad \frac{1}{|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}=\sum_{l=0}^\infty\frac{r'^{\,l}}{r^{l+1}}P_l(\cos\gamma)\quad(r'<r),$$

where $\gamma$ is the angle between $\mathbf x$ and $\mathbf x'$. Integrating term by term gives, through $l=2$ (repeated indices summed),

$$\Phi(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{q}{r}+\frac{\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}+\frac{Q_{ij}\hat r_i\hat r_j}{2r^3}+\cdots\right],$$

with the moments

$$q=\int\rho\,d^3x',\qquad \mathbf p=\int\rho\,\mathbf x'\,d^3x',\qquad Q_{ij}=\int\rho\,(3x'_ix'_j-r'^2\delta_{ij})\,d^3x'.$$

This $Q_{ij}$ is symmetric and traceless. For an axisymmetric $\rho$ the expansion collapses to one sum, $\Phi=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_lM_l\,r^{-l-1}P_l(\cos\theta)$ with $M_l=\int\rho\,r'^lP_l(\cos\theta')\,d^3x'$, and $M_2=\tfrac12Q_{zz}$.

### Convergence domain and error

The generating function converges only for $r'<r$. The multipole series therefore converges for $r>r'_{\max}$, the largest distance of any charge from the expansion origin, and it is guaranteed outside every sphere centered at the origin that contains all the charge. Inside that sphere the series does not represent the potential. Using $|P_l|\le1$ to bound the tail after truncating at $l=L$, with $Q_{\rm abs}=\int|\rho|\,d^3x'$, for $r>R$ you get

$$\left|\Phi-\Phi_{\le L}\right|\le\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{Q_{\rm abs}}{r-R}\left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^{L+1}.$$

Each extra order gains a factor $R/r$. The origin matters: a displaced origin enlarges $R$ and shrinks the domain, so expand about the center of the charge.

### Facts about the moments

- The lowest nonvanishing moment does not depend on the origin. Under $\mathbf x'\to\mathbf x'-\mathbf a$ the dipole moment changes by $-q\mathbf a$, so it is origin-independent only if $q=0$.
- A point dipole has, away from the origin, $\mathbf E=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{3(\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r}-\mathbf p}{r^3}$.
- A localized charge in a static external field with no external sources at the charge has energy, evaluated at the origin,

$$U=q\Phi_{\rm ext}-\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf E_{\rm ext}-\tfrac16Q_{ij}\,\partial_iE_{{\rm ext},j}+\cdots,$$

with torque $\mathbf p\times\mathbf E$ and force $(\mathbf p\cdot\nabla)\mathbf E$ on a dipole.

@example
title: A linear quadrupole, its error and its convergence domain
problem: Charges $+q$ sit at $z=\pm d$ and $-2q$ at the origin. Find the leading multipole term of the potential on the $z$-axis, compare it with the exact potential, and state where the expansion converges.
step: The net charge is $q-2q+q=0$ and $p_z=qd-qd+0=0$. The first nonzero moment is $Q_{zz}=\sum q_i(3z_i^2-z_i^2)=2\cdot q\cdot2d^2=4qd^2$ (the central charge contributes zero).
step: On the axis ($\hat r=\hat z$) the quadrupole term is $\Phi^{(2)}=\dfrac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{Q_{zz}}{2z^3}=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2d^2}{z^3}$.
step: The exact potential for $z>d$ is $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{1}{z-d}+\dfrac{1}{z+d}-\dfrac2z\right]=\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2d^2}{z(z^2-d^2)}$.
step: Expand with $t=d/z$: $\dfrac{2d^2}{z(z^2-d^2)}=\dfrac{2}{z}\left(t^2+t^4+t^6+\cdots\right)$. The first term reproduces $\Phi^{(2)}$, and the series converges only for $|t|<1$, that is $z>d$: outside the sphere through the outermost charges.
step: The next term is smaller by a factor $t^2$, so at $z=3d$ the quadrupole-only potential is low by $1/9\approx11\%$ of the exact value, which is the size of the error you must state with the result.

@mistakes
- Using the multipole series at points inside the smallest sphere that contains the charges, where it need not converge to the potential.
- Quoting a dipole moment for a system with net charge without naming the origin, since $\mathbf p$ then depends on it.
- Mixing quadrupole conventions. Here $Q_{ij}=\int\rho(3x_ix_j-r^2\delta_{ij})$ is traceless and the potential term carries a factor $\tfrac12$; with $\int\rho x_ix_j$ the formulas change.
- Evaluating a higher-order term as a function of $r$ alone. The $l$th term depends on the direction of $\mathbf x$ through $P_l(\cos\theta)$ or $\hat r_i\hat r_j$.

@practice tuple
prompt: Three point charges are fixed: $+3\ \mu\text{C}$ at $(1,0,0)\ \text{m}$, $-1\ \mu\text{C}$ at $(0,2,0)\ \text{m}$ and $-2\ \mu\text{C}$ at $(0,0,-1)\ \text{m}$. Find the dipole moment $\mathbf p$ about the origin as components in $\mu\text{C}\cdot\text{m}$.
answer: (3, -2, 2)
check: tuple(sum(q*r[i] for q, r in [(3, (1, 0, 0)), (-1, (0, 2, 0)), (-2, (0, 0, -1))]) for i in range(3))
hint: $\mathbf p=\sum_iq_i\mathbf r_i$. Compute each Cartesian component separately.
hint: The net charge is zero, so the result is the same about any origin.

@practice num
prompt: Charges $+q$ sit at $(\pm a,0,0)$ and $-q$ at $(0,\pm a,0)$, with $q=2.0\ \text{nC}$ and $a=3.0\ \text{cm}$. Find the quadrupole moment component $Q_{xx}=\sum q_i(3x_i^2-r_i^2)$.
answer: 1.08e-11
check: sum(q*(3*x*x - (x*x + y*y + z*z)) for q, (x, y, z) in [(2.0e-9, (0.03, 0, 0)), (2.0e-9, (-0.03, 0, 0)), (-2.0e-9, (0, 0.03, 0)), (-2.0e-9, (0, -0.03, 0))])
tol: 1%
unit: C m^2
hint: Evaluate $3x_i^2-r_i^2$ separately for the charges on the $x$-axis and for those on the $y$-axis.
hint: For a charge on the $x$-axis the bracket is $2a^2$; for a charge on the $y$-axis it is $-a^2$.
solution: The two $+q$ charges give $2\,q\,(2a^2)=4qa^2$. The two $-q$ charges give $2(-q)(-a^2)=2qa^2$. So $Q_{xx}=6qa^2=6(2.0\times10^{-9})(9.0\times10^{-4})=1.08\times10^{-11}\ \text{C m}^2$.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements about multipole moments of a bounded charge distribution are correct?
choice*: Under a shift of origin $\mathbf x'\to\mathbf x'-\mathbf a$, the dipole moment changes by $-q\mathbf a$.
choice*: A distribution with zero net charge has an origin-independent dipole moment.
choice: The multipole series about the origin converges at every point $r>0$ for any bounded charge distribution.
choice: The electric field of a point dipole falls off as $1/r^2$.
hint: Write $\mathbf p'=\int\rho\,(\mathbf x'-\mathbf a)\,d^3x'$ and compare with $\mathbf p$.
hint: Recall where the generating function of the Legendre polynomials converges, and the power of $r$ in $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi$ for $\Phi\propto r^{-2}$.
solution: $\mathbf p'=\mathbf p-q\mathbf a$, so the dipole moment is origin-independent exactly when $q=0$. The series converges only for $r>r'_{\max}$, not inside the charge distribution. A dipole potential falls as $r^{-2}$, so its field falls as $r^{-3}$.

@practice num
prompt: A dipole consists of $+q$ at $z=+d$ and $-q$ at $z=-d$. On the $z$-axis, for $z>d$, compare its exact potential with the point-dipole potential $p/(4\pi\epsilon_0z^2)$, $p=2qd$. Define the relative error as (exact $-$ dipole)/exact. At what distance $z$, in units of $d$, does the relative error fall to $1\%$?
answer: 10
check: root(lambda z: (1/(z-1) - 1/(z+1) - 2/z**2)/(1/(z-1) - 1/(z+1)) - 0.01, 1.5, 100)
hint: The exact on-axis potential is $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{1}{z-d}-\dfrac{1}{z+d}\right]$.
hint: Write the exact bracket as $2d/(z^2-d^2)$ and divide the dipole term by it.
solution: Exact: $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2d}{z^2-d^2}$. Dipole: $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{2d}{z^2}$. The ratio is $1-d^2/z^2$, so the relative error is $d^2/z^2$. Setting it to $0.01$ gives $z=10d$.

@practice self
prompt: A thin ring of radius $a$ carries total charge $Q$ uniformly and lies in the plane $z=0$, centered on the origin. (a) Find the axisymmetric multipole moments $M_l$. (b) Write the on-axis potential as a series in $a/z$ and sum it exactly. (c) State the domain of convergence, explain why it cannot be larger, and give a bound for the error of truncating at order $L$.
hint: Every point of the ring has $r'=a$ and $\theta'=\pi/2$, so $M_l=Qa^lP_l(0)$.
hint: On the axis $P_l(1)=1$. Compare with the generating function $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}=\sum_lP_l(0)t^l$ and ask where the exact potential is singular as a function of complex $z$.
solution: (a) $M_l=\int\rho\,r'^lP_l(\cos\theta')\,d^3x'=Qa^lP_l(0)$. Odd $l$ vanish because $P_l(0)=0$; $M_0=Q$, $M_2=-Qa^2/2$, $M_4=\tfrac38Qa^4$. (b) On the axis, $\Phi=\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0z}\left[1-\dfrac{a^2}{2z^2}+\dfrac{3a^4}{8z^4}-\cdots\right]$. This is the binomial series of $(1+a^2/z^2)^{-1/2}$, so it sums to $\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{z^2+a^2}}$, which is also the direct result because every point of the ring is at distance $\sqrt{z^2+a^2}$. (c) The series converges for $|z|>a$, outside the smallest sphere containing the ring. The exact function has branch points at $z=\pm ia$, at distance $a$ from the origin, so no larger domain is possible. With $Q_{\rm abs}=|Q|$ and $R=a$ the truncation error after order $L$ is at most $\dfrac{|Q|}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{(a/r)^{L+1}}{r-a}$.
rubric: Computes $M_l=Qa^lP_l(0)$ and notes that odd-$l$ moments vanish.
rubric: Sums the on-axis series to $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{z^2+a^2})$ and agrees with the direct integral.
rubric: States convergence for $r>a$ and justifies it (generating function needs $r'<r$, or singularity at $z=\pm ia$).
rubric: Gives a truncation-error bound or estimate that shrinks by a factor $a/r$ per order.

@quiz num
prompt: A uniformly charged prolate spheroid, $x^2/a^2+y^2/a^2+z^2/c^2\le1$, carries total charge $Q=5.0\ \text{nC}$, with $a=1.0\ \text{cm}$ and $c=3.0\ \text{cm}$. Find the quadrupole moment $Q_{zz}=\int\rho\,(3z^2-r^2)\,d^3x$ about its center.
answer: 1.6e-12
check: (5.0e-9/(4*pi*0.01**2*0.03/3))*quad(lambda z: 2*z*z*pi*0.01**2*(1 - z*z/0.03**2) - pi*(0.01**2*(1 - z*z/0.03**2))**2/2, -0.03, 0.03)
tol: 1%
unit: C m^2
hint: Slice the spheroid into discs of radius $s(z)=a\sqrt{1-z^2/c^2}$. In each disc $3z^2-r^2=2z^2-(x^2+y^2)$.
hint: For a uniform ellipsoid $\langle x^2\rangle=a^2/5$ and $\langle z^2\rangle=c^2/5$, so $Q_{zz}=Q\,(2\langle z^2\rangle-\langle x^2\rangle-\langle y^2\rangle)$.
solution: $Q_{zz}=Q\left(\dfrac{2c^2}{5}-\dfrac{2a^2}{5}\right)=\dfrac{2Q}{5}(c^2-a^2)=2.0\times10^{-9}\,(9.0\times10^{-4}-1.0\times10^{-4})=1.6\times10^{-12}\ \text{C m}^2$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Every charge of a distribution lies inside a sphere of radius $R$ centered at a point $C$, which is at distance $s$ from the origin $O$ (with $s$ arbitrary). For which field points is the multipole expansion about $O$ guaranteed to converge for every such distribution?
choice*: Points with $r>R+s$, farther from $O$ than any charge can be.
choice: Points with $r>R$, since the charges lie within distance $R$ of $C$.
choice: Points with $r>s$, since only the displacement of the center matters.
choice: Points with $r>|R-s|$, since the nearest charge sets the limit.
hint: The expansion needs $r'<r$ for every charge, where $r'$ is measured from $O$.
solution: The farthest possible charge from $O$ is at distance $s+R$. The generating function converges for each charge only when $r>r'$, so convergence for every such distribution is guaranteed exactly for $r>R+s$. The nearest charge is irrelevant, and the origin shift enlarges the domain's radius.
