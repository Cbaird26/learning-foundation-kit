@article
**Conventions.** SI units. Lorenz gauge: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A+c^{-2}\partial_t\Phi=0$ (week 12 compares gauges). With $\Box\equiv c^{-2}\partial_t^2-\nabla^2$ the potentials obey

$$\Box\Phi=\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad \Box\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf J.$$

The retarded Green function, $\Box G=\delta^3(\mathbf x-\mathbf x')\,\delta(t-t')$, is

$$G(\mathbf x,t;\mathbf x',t')=\frac{\delta\!\left(t-t'-|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|/c\right)}{4\pi|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|}.$$

With $R=|\mathbf x-\mathbf x'|$ and retarded time $t_r=t-R/c$ this gives

$$\mathbf A(\mathbf x,t)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf x',t_r)}{R}\,d^3x',$$

and the same form for $\Phi$ with $\rho/(4\pi\epsilon_0)$. The field at time $t$ is set by the source at the earlier time $t_r$; the advanced solution ($t+R/c$) is excluded by causality.

### Harmonic sources and the far zone

For $\mathbf J(\mathbf x,t)=\mathrm{Re}[\mathbf J(\mathbf x)e^{-i\omega t}]$ and $k=\omega/c$,

$$\mathbf A(\mathbf x)=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\mathbf J(\mathbf x')\,\frac{e^{ikR}}{R}\,d^3x'.$$

Let the source have size $d$ and let $\hat n=\mathbf x/r$. In the radiation zone, $r\gg d$, $r\gg\lambda$ and $r\gg kd^2$, use $R\approx r-\hat n\cdot\mathbf x'$ in the phase and $R\approx r$ elsewhere:

$$\mathbf A\approx\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\int\mathbf J(\mathbf x')\,e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\mathbf x'}\,d^3x'.$$

The radiated fields and the time-averaged power per solid angle are

$$\mathbf B=ik\,\hat n\times\mathbf A,\qquad \mathbf E=c\,\mathbf B\times\hat n,\qquad \frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{r^2c}{2\mu_0}|\mathbf B|^2.$$

### Multipole expansion of the radiation

For $kd\ll1$ expand $e^{-ik\hat n\cdot\mathbf x'}=1-ik\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf x'+\cdots$. Each order is smaller by a factor $kd$ in amplitude.

- **Electric dipole.** Continuity gives $\int\mathbf J\,d^3x'=-i\omega\mathbf p$, so $\mathbf A=-\dfrac{i\mu_0\omega}{4\pi}\,\mathbf p\,\dfrac{e^{ikr}}{r}$. For real $\mathbf p$, with $\theta$ measured from $\mathbf p$,

$$\frac{dP}{d\Omega}=\frac{\mu_0\omega^4p^2}{32\pi^2c}\sin^2\theta,\qquad P=\frac{\mu_0\omega^4p^2}{12\pi c}.$$

- **Magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole** come from the next term. A magnetic dipole $\mathbf m=\tfrac12\int\mathbf x'\times\mathbf J\,d^3x'$ radiates like an electric dipole with $\mathbf p\to\mathbf m/c$, so $P_m=\mu_0\omega^4m^2/(12\pi c^3)$. The electric quadrupole, with $Q_{ij}$ as defined in week 4, radiates $P_Q=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^6}{1440\pi c^3}\sum_{ij}|Q_{ij}|^2$.

Here $p$, $m$ and $Q_{ij}$ are the amplitudes in $\mathrm{Re}[\,\cdot\,e^{-i\omega t}]$, so the powers are time averages.

### Larmor's formula

For a slowly moving charge ($v\ll c$), $\mathbf p=q\mathbf x_q(t)$ and $\ddot{\mathbf p}=q\mathbf a$. The time-domain version of the dipole result is

$$P(t)=\frac{\mu_0q^2a^2}{6\pi c}.$$

It agrees with the harmonic formula: for $p(t)=p_0\cos\omega t$ the average of $\ddot p^2$ is $\omega^4p_0^2/2$, which turns $1/(6\pi)$ into $1/(12\pi)$.

### Error estimate

The total power from the dipole term is correct up to relative corrections of order $(kd)^2$; the angular pattern may change at relative order $kd$ because the multipole terms interfere with it. If symmetry makes $\mathbf p=0$, the leading radiation is $P_m+P_Q$, suppressed by $(kd)^2$ relative to a dipole of the same size. State $kd$ whenever you quote a multipole estimate.

@example
title: A short center-fed antenna
problem: A wire of length $\ell\ll\lambda$ along $z$ carries a uniform current $I(t)=\mathrm{Re}[I_0e^{-i\omega t}]$, with charge accumulating at its ends. Find the radiated power and the radiation resistance, and state the accuracy of the result.
step: Charge $\pm q(t)$ sits at the ends and $I=dq/dt$, so $\dot p=I\ell$. With $e^{-i\omega t}$, $-i\omega p_0=I_0\ell$, so $|p_0|=I_0\ell/\omega$.
step: The pattern is $\dfrac{dP}{d\Omega}=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^4p_0^2}{32\pi^2c}\sin^2\theta$, zero along the wire and maximal broadside.
step: The total power is $P=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^4p_0^2}{12\pi c}=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^2\ell^2I_0^2}{12\pi c}=\dfrac{Z_0(k\ell)^2}{12\pi}I_0^2$, with $Z_0=\mu_0c$.
step: Writing $P=\tfrac12R_{\rm rad}I_0^2$ gives $R_{\rm rad}=\dfrac{Z_0}{6\pi}(k\ell)^2=\dfrac{2\pi}{3}Z_0\left(\dfrac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2\approx790\left(\dfrac{\ell}{\lambda}\right)^2\ \Omega$.
step: The current is symmetric about the center, so the magnetic dipole and quadrupole terms vanish. The next correction is of relative order $(k\ell)^2$. For this uniform current it is $-(k\ell)^2/60$, about $0.2\%$ at $\ell=\lambda/20$, so the estimate is accurate to well under $1\%$ there.

@example
title: A charge moving in a circle
problem: A charge $q$ moves non-relativistically in a circle of radius $a$ with angular speed $\Omega$. Find the radiated power two ways.
step: The acceleration is centripetal, $a_{\rm acc}=a\Omega^2$. Larmor's formula gives $P=\dfrac{\mu_0q^2a^2\Omega^4}{6\pi c}$, constant in time.
step: The dipole moment is $\mathbf p=qa(\cos\Omega t,\sin\Omega t,0)=\mathrm{Re}\left[qa(\hat x+i\hat y)e^{-i\Omega t}\right]$, so the complex amplitude has $|\mathbf p_0|^2=2q^2a^2$.
step: The harmonic formula for a complex amplitude gives $P=\dfrac{\mu_0\Omega^4|\mathbf p_0|^2}{12\pi c}=\dfrac{\mu_0\Omega^4\,2q^2a^2}{12\pi c}$, the same as Larmor's result.
step: Validity needs $v=a\Omega\ll c$, which is the same as $ka\ll1$ for $k=\Omega/c$: the dipole approximation and the non-relativistic Larmor formula fail together.

@mistakes
- Evaluating the source at time $t$ instead of the retarded time $t-R/c$, which removes the finite propagation speed.
- Quoting a dipole estimate without checking $kd\ll1$, or using the $1/r$ radiation fields at $r\lesssim\lambda$ where the near-zone terms dominate.
- Mixing the time-averaged harmonic power $\mu_0\omega^4p_0^2/(12\pi c)$ with the instantaneous Larmor form $\mu_0\ddot p^2/(6\pi c)$, which differ by the factor $1/2$ from the average of $\cos^2$.
- Applying the electric dipole formula to a system with $\mathbf p=0$ without checking the magnetic dipole and quadrupole terms.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from the far-zone vector potential $\mathbf A=-\dfrac{i\mu_0\omega}{4\pi}\,\mathbf p\,\dfrac{e^{ikr}}{r}$ of an oscillating electric dipole with real amplitude $\mathbf p$, derive $\mathbf B$, $\mathbf E$ and $dP/d\Omega$, and integrate to get the total power. State the conditions under which the result holds.
hint: In the radiation zone $\nabla\to ik\hat n$ acting on $e^{ikr}$, so $\mathbf B=ik\,\hat n\times\mathbf A$.
hint: The time-averaged Poynting vector for complex amplitudes is $\tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf E\times\mathbf B^*)$, and $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$.
solution: $\mathbf B=ik\,\hat n\times\mathbf A=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega k}{4\pi}\,(\hat n\times\mathbf p)\,\dfrac{e^{ikr}}{r}$, so $|\mathbf B|=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega kp\sin\theta}{4\pi r}$, and $\mathbf E=c\,\mathbf B\times\hat n$ has magnitude $c|\mathbf B|$ and is perpendicular to both $\hat n$ and $\mathbf B$. The time-averaged flux is radial with $\dfrac{dP}{d\Omega}=\dfrac{r^2c}{2\mu_0}|\mathbf B|^2=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^4p^2}{32\pi^2c}\sin^2\theta$ (using $ck^2=\omega^2/c$). Integrating with $\int\sin^2\theta\,d\Omega=8\pi/3$ gives $P=\dfrac{\mu_0\omega^4p^2}{12\pi c}$. The result needs the radiation zone ($r\gg\lambda$, $r\gg d$, $r\gg kd^2$) and $kd\ll1$ so that higher multipoles are negligible, with relative power corrections of order $(kd)^2$.
