@article
**Conventions.** As in week 11: SI units, signature $(+,-,-,-)$, $A^\mu=(\Phi/c,\mathbf A)$, $F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu$.

### Gauge freedom

The potentials are not unique. For any smooth function $\chi(\mathbf x,t)$,

$$A^\mu\to A^\mu+\partial^\mu\chi\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Phi\to\Phi+\partial_t\chi,\qquad \mathbf A\to\mathbf A-\nabla\chi.$$

Then $F^{\mu\nu}\to F^{\mu\nu}+\partial^\mu\partial^\nu\chi-\partial^\nu\partial^\mu\chi=F^{\mu\nu}$, so $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$ are unchanged. Only gauge-invariant quantities are observable: $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf B$ and, for a closed loop $C$, the integral $\oint_C\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l=\int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a$, the magnetic flux through $C$.

A **gauge condition** removes most of this freedom. The **residual gauge freedom** is the set of $\chi$ that preserves the condition. Choosing a gauge is a convenience for calculation, never a physical assumption.

### Lorenz gauge

The condition $\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$, that is $c^{-2}\partial_t\Phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, simplifies the inhomogeneous Maxwell equations because $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\Box A^\nu-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)$:

$$\Box A^\mu=\mu_0J^\mu.$$

This is manifestly covariant and underlies the retarded solutions of week 9. From any potentials, the Lorenz condition is reached with a $\chi$ satisfying $\Box\chi=-\partial_\mu A^\mu$, which always has a solution. The residual freedom is every $\chi$ with $\Box\chi=0$.

### Coulomb (radiation) gauge

The condition $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ gives

$$\nabla^2\Phi=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0},\qquad \Box\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf J_t,$$

where $\mathbf J=\mathbf J_t+\mathbf J_\ell$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf J_t=0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf J_\ell=0$. The scalar potential is the instantaneous Coulomb potential. The instantaneous pieces of $-\nabla\Phi$ and $-\partial_t\mathbf A$ cancel in $\mathbf E$, which propagates causally. The condition is not Lorentz covariant, but it isolates the physical degrees of freedom: in empty space $\Phi=0$ and $\mathbf A$ is a transverse wave with two polarizations. The residual freedom needs $\nabla^2\chi=0$.

### Temporal gauge

The condition $\Phi=0$ gives $\mathbf E=-\partial_t\mathbf A$. Gauss's law becomes a constraint, $\nabla\cdot\partial_t\mathbf A=-\rho/\epsilon_0$. The residual freedom is any time-independent $\chi(\mathbf x)$. A uniform static field $E_0\hat x$ has $\mathbf A=-E_0t\,\hat x$ in this gauge.

### Choosing and checking

- Use Lorenz gauge for radiation from sources and covariant work.
- Use Coulomb gauge for non-relativistic charges and to count radiation polarizations.
- Use temporal gauge in Hamiltonian formulations.
- Whatever gauge you use, check the answer by computing $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf B$, or a loop integral of $\mathbf A$. These must not depend on $\chi$.

Counting: $A^\mu$ has four components; one is removed by the gauge condition and one more by the residual freedom (or by Gauss's constraint), leaving two physical polarizations of a free wave.

@example
title: One magnetic field, two vector potentials
problem: A uniform field $B_0\hat z$ is described in the symmetric gauge by $\mathbf A_s=\tfrac12B_0(-y,\,x,\,0)$. Show that $\mathbf A_L=(-B_0y,0,0)$ describes the same field, find the gauge function connecting them, and compare $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ around a circle of radius $R$ in the $xy$-plane.
step: For $\mathbf A_s$, $(\nabla\times\mathbf A_s)_z=\partial_xA_y-\partial_yA_x=\tfrac12B_0+\tfrac12B_0=B_0$, and for $\mathbf A_L$, $(\nabla\times\mathbf A_L)_z=0-\partial_y(-B_0y)=B_0$. The other components vanish, so both give $\mathbf B=B_0\hat z$.
step: Their difference is $\mathbf A_s-\mathbf A_L=\left(\tfrac12B_0y,\ \tfrac12B_0x,\ 0\right)=\nabla\left(\tfrac12B_0xy\right)$. With $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A-\nabla\chi$ this means $\mathbf A_L=\mathbf A_s-\nabla\chi$ with $\chi=\tfrac12B_0\,xy$.
step: Both potentials satisfy the Coulomb condition $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, and $\chi=\tfrac12B_0xy$ is harmonic, $\nabla^2\chi=0$, so the transformation stays inside the Coulomb gauge: it is residual gauge freedom.
step: For $\mathbf A_s$ the field is tangential with magnitude $B_0R/2$ on the circle, so $\oint\mathbf A_s\cdot d\mathbf l=\tfrac12B_0R\cdot2\pi R=\pi R^2B_0$.
step: For $\mathbf A_L$, $\oint\mathbf A_L\cdot d\mathbf l=-B_0\oint y\,dx=B_0\pi R^2$ (the signed area of the circle). The two loop integrals agree, equal to the flux $\pi R^2B_0$.

@example
title: Equations of the Coulomb gauge
problem: Starting from Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, derive the equations obeyed by $\Phi$ and $\mathbf A$ when $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, and show what the longitudinal current does.
step: Gauss's law $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ with $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ gives $-\nabla^2\Phi-\partial_t\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=\rho/\epsilon_0$. With $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$, $\nabla^2\Phi=-\rho/\epsilon_0$, whose solution uses $\rho$ at the same time $t$.
step: The Ampère–Maxwell law $\nabla\times\mathbf B=\mu_0\mathbf J+c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E$ with $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A)=-\nabla^2\mathbf A$ gives $\Box\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf J-c^{-2}\nabla\partial_t\Phi$.
step: Take $\partial_t$ of Poisson's equation and use continuity: $\nabla^2\partial_t\Phi=\nabla\cdot\mathbf J/\epsilon_0$. Hence $\nabla\partial_t\Phi=\mathbf J_\ell/\epsilon_0$, because both sides are curl-free with the same divergence.
step: Substituting, $c^{-2}\nabla\partial_t\Phi=\mu_0\mathbf J_\ell$, so $\Box\mathbf A=\mu_0(\mathbf J-\mathbf J_\ell)=\mu_0\mathbf J_t$.
step: In empty space $\rho=0$, $\mathbf J=0$, so $\Phi=0$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$ and $\Box\mathbf A=0$. Plane waves $\mathbf A_0e^{i(\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf x-\omega t)}$ must have $\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf A_0=0$: two transverse polarizations.

@mistakes
- Treating the Lorenz condition as fixing the potentials completely. Any $\chi$ with $\Box\chi=0$ is still allowed, so unphysical pure-gauge solutions can appear.
- Treating $\Phi$ or $\mathbf A$ as measurable. The Coulomb-gauge $\Phi$ is instantaneous although $\mathbf E$ is causal.
- Sign slips in the transformation. In this course $\Phi\to\Phi+\partial_t\chi$ and $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A-\nabla\chi$ go together.
- Using $\Box A^\mu=\mu_0J^\mu$ in a gauge where $\partial_\mu A^\mu\neq0$. It holds only in Lorenz gauge.

prompt: Translate the following three-vector derivation into tensor notation. Starting from $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf B-c^{-2}\partial_t\mathbf E=\mu_0\mathbf J$ with $\mathbf E=-\nabla\Phi-\partial_t\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B=\nabla\times\mathbf A$, one obtains wave equations for $\Phi$ and $\mathbf A$ when $c^{-2}\partial_t\Phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Redo it with $F^{\mu\nu}$ and $A^\mu$, show that the result is a single equation $\Box A^\mu=\mu_0J^\mu$, extract its $\mu=0$ and $\mu=i$ components, and state the residual gauge freedom.
hint: Start from $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu$ and insert $F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu$.
hint: Use $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu=\Box$ and the identity $\partial_\mu\partial^\nu=\partial^\nu\partial_\mu$.
solution: Inserting $F^{\mu\nu}=\partial^\mu A^\nu-\partial^\nu A^\mu$ into $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=\mu_0J^\nu$ gives $\Box A^\nu-\partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu)=\mu_0J^\nu$. In the Lorenz gauge $\partial_\mu A^\mu=0$, so $\Box A^\nu=\mu_0J^\nu$. For $\nu=0$, with $A^0=\Phi/c$ and $J^0=c\rho$: $\Box\Phi=\mu_0c^2\rho=\rho/\epsilon_0$. For $\nu=i$: $\Box A^i=\mu_0J^i$, i.e. $\Box\mathbf A=\mu_0\mathbf J$. The gauge condition itself is $c^{-2}\partial_t\Phi+\nabla\cdot\mathbf A=0$. Under $A^\mu\to A^\mu+\partial^\mu\chi$ the condition changes by $\Box\chi$, so the residual freedom is every $\chi$ with $\Box\chi=0$; and any potentials can be brought to the Lorenz gauge by solving $\Box\chi=-\partial_\mu A^\mu$.
