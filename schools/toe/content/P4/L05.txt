@article
Every object glows with thermal radiation. A **blackbody** is an idealized object that absorbs all radiation falling on it. A small hole in the wall of a closed, hot cavity emits blackbody radiation at the cavity's temperature $T$, and the spectrum depends on $T$ alone, not on the material or shape.

### What is measured

- The spectrum has one peak, and the peak moves to shorter wavelengths as $T$ rises. This is **Wien's displacement law**:

$$\lambda_{\max}\,T = b = 2.898\times10^{-3}\ \text{m K}.$$

- The total power per unit area follows the **Stefan–Boltzmann law** (a $T^4$ law): $P/A = \sigma T^4$ with the Stefan–Boltzmann constant $\sigma = 5.670\times10^{-8}$ W m$^{-2}$ K$^{-4}$. A real surface emits a fraction $\varepsilon \le 1$ of this, so $P = \varepsilon\sigma A T^4$.
- The spectrum rises at long wavelength, peaks, and then falls to zero at short wavelength. Temperatures are always absolute (kelvin).

### The classical prediction fails

Inside a cavity the radiation forms standing electromagnetic waves (modes). The number of modes per unit volume in a frequency range $df$ is $8\pi f^2\,df/c^3$, and classical **equipartition** gives each mode an average energy $k_BT$, where $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K is Boltzmann's constant. The energy density per unit frequency would then follow the **Rayleigh–Jeans law**:

$$u(f) = \frac{8\pi f^2}{c^3}\,k_BT .$$

This matches the data at low frequency, but it grows as $f^2$ without limit, so the total energy is infinite. That is the **ultraviolet catastrophe**: no classical argument avoids it, yet real cavities glow with finite energy.

### The quantum repair

Suppose a mode of frequency $f$ can only hold energy $0, hf, 2hf, \dots$. Weighting each level by $e^{-E/k_BT}$ gives the mean energy

$$\langle E\rangle = \frac{hf}{e^{hf/k_BT} - 1}.$$

- If $hf \ll k_BT$, then $\langle E\rangle \to k_BT$, the classical value, so low-frequency data are reproduced.
- If $hf \gg k_BT$, then $\langle E\rangle \approx hf\,e^{-hf/k_BT}$, which is tiny. A single quantum costs more than the thermal energy available, so high-frequency modes are almost never excited.

The resulting spectrum, **Planck's law**, is $u(f) = \dfrac{8\pi f^2}{c^3}\,\dfrac{hf}{e^{hf/k_BT} - 1}$. It fits the measurements at every $f$ and $T$ with one new constant, $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s (Planck's constant, the quantum of action). Wien's displacement law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law both follow from it.

### Photon energy and estimates

One quantum has energy $E = hf = hc/\lambda$. A handy value is $hc = 1240$ eV nm, so a 500 nm photon carries $2.48$ eV (with $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J).

- **Temperature from colour (Wien's displacement law):** $T = b/\lambda_{\max}$. A peak near 500 nm means about $5800$ K.
- **Power from temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law):** $P = \sigma A T^4$ for an ideal surface. Doubling $T$ multiplies the power by $16$.

@example
title: A star's temperature and surface flux
problem: A star's spectrum peaks at $\lambda_{\max} = 500$ nm. Treating it as a blackbody, find its surface temperature and the power radiated per square metre.
step: Use Wien's displacement law: $T = b/\lambda_{\max} = (2.898\times10^{-3})/(500\times10^{-9}) \approx 5.80\times10^{3}$ K.
step: Use the Stefan–Boltzmann law: $P/A = \sigma T^4 = (5.670\times10^{-8})(5796)^4$.
step: Evaluate $5796^4 \approx 1.129\times10^{15}$, so $P/A \approx 6.4\times10^{7}$ W/m$^2$.
step: Sanity check: a body at $300$ K radiates about $460$ W/m$^2$; the star is $\approx 19.3$ times hotter, so its flux is $19.3^4 \approx 1.4\times10^{5}$ times larger. That agrees with $6.4\times10^{7}/460$.

@example
title: Mean mode energy at low and high frequency
problem: Find the quantized mean energy $\langle E\rangle$ of a cavity mode, as a multiple of $k_BT$, when $hf/k_BT = 0.1$ and when $hf/k_BT = 5$. Compare with the classical value $k_BT$.
step: Write $x = hf/k_BT$, so $\langle E\rangle/k_BT = x/(e^{x} - 1)$.
step: For $x = 0.1$: $e^{0.1} - 1 = 0.10517$, so the ratio is $0.1/0.10517 \approx 0.951$.
step: For $x = 5$: $e^{5} - 1 = 147.4$, so the ratio is $5/147.4 \approx 0.034$.
step: Interpretation: at low $f$ the classical value is nearly right (within $5\%$); at high $f$ it overestimates by a factor of about $30$, which is what causes the ultraviolet catastrophe.

@mistakes
- Using Celsius temperatures in the Stefan–Boltzmann law or Wien's displacement law. Convert to kelvin first.
- Converting the peak of the frequency spectrum with $\lambda = c/f$ and calling the result $\lambda_{\max}$. The spectra per unit frequency and per unit wavelength peak at different places, so use Wien's displacement law.
- Thinking classical theory is wrong at every frequency; it fails at high frequency but agrees with the data at low frequency.
- Doubling the power when the temperature doubles. The power scales as $T^4$.

prompt: Which statement correctly describes the ultraviolet catastrophe?
choice*: Equipartition gives every cavity mode energy $k_BT$ and the mode count grows as $f^2$, so the predicted energy density rises without limit at high frequency.
choice: Classical theory predicts that a hot body emits no ultraviolet light at all.
choice: Measured spectra rise without limit at short wavelength, while classical theory predicts a peak.
choice: Classical theory fails at low frequency, where it predicts zero emission.
hint: Think about what equipartition says about the energy in each mode, and how many modes there are at high frequency.
solution: The classical spectrum $u(f) = 8\pi f^2 k_BT/c^3$ grows as $f^2$, giving infinite total energy, whereas measured spectra peak and fall. The classical result agrees with data at low frequency.

@practice num
prompt: A cavity mode may hold only the energies $0, hf, 2hf, \dots$, each weighted by $e^{-E/k_BT}$. For a mode with $hf = k_BT$, find the mean energy in units of $k_BT$.
answer: 0.582
tol: 1%
check: nsum(lambda n: n*math.exp(-n), 0, 200)/nsum(lambda n: math.exp(-n), 0, 200)
hint: The mean is a weighted average: $\langle E\rangle = \sum_n (n\,hf)\,e^{-n\,hf/k_BT}\big/\sum_n e^{-n\,hf/k_BT}$. With $hf = k_BT$, in units of $k_BT$ this is $\sum n\,e^{-n}\big/\sum e^{-n}$.
hint: Both sums are geometric-type series, and the ratio simplifies to $x/(e^{x} - 1)$ with $x = 1$.
solution: $\langle E\rangle/k_BT = x/(e^{x} - 1) = 1/(e - 1) \approx 0.582$. The classical value would be $1$.

@quiz num
prompt: The peak wavelength of a blackbody's spectrum is halved. By what factor does its total radiated power per unit area change?
answer: 16
check: (1000/500)**4
hint: Halving $\lambda_{\max}$ changes $T$ by what factor?
