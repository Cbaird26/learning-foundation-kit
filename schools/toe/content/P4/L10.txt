@article
Hydrogen's levels came from the Bohr model's quantization rule. A more general idea underlies them: a wave confined by a potential can form standing waves only at certain energies. Three models give three characteristic spectra.

### Particle in a box

A particle of mass $m$ moves in one dimension between impenetrable walls at $x = 0$ and $x = L$. The matter wave must vanish at the walls, so a whole number of half-wavelengths fits: $\lambda_n = 2L/n$. With $p = h/\lambda$ and $K = p^2/2m$,

$$E_n = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2},\qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

The wavefunctions, which solve the **Schrödinger equation** for this potential, are $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(n\pi x/L)$, with $|\psi_n|^2$ the probability density. Note three features:

- $n = 0$ is not allowed, since it would mean no wave at all. The lowest energy $E_1$ is positive: a confinement (zero-point) energy, consistent with week 8's estimate.
- The spacing grows with $n$: $E_{n+1} - E_n = (2n + 1)E_1$.
- Narrowing the box raises every level as $1/L^2$.

### Harmonic oscillator

A potential $V = \tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$ describes small vibrations, such as a bond between two atoms in a molecule. The allowed energies are

$$E_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega,\qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

The levels are equally spaced by $\hbar\omega$, and even the ground state has the zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$. A jump between neighbours emits or absorbs light of frequency $\omega/2\pi$, the same as the classical oscillation frequency.

### Hydrogen

The Coulomb potential $-1/r$ gives $E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2$, the same levels as the Bohr model. The spacing shrinks as $n$ increases, and the levels pile up towards $E = 0$, the ionization threshold. Above it the electron is free and the spectrum is continuous.

### Comparing the spectra

| model | potential assumed | energy | spacing as $n$ grows |
|---|---|---|---|
| box | flat inside, infinite walls | $\propto n^2$ | grows |
| oscillator | parabola | $\propto n + \tfrac12$ | constant |
| hydrogen | Coulomb $-1/r$ | $\propto -1/n^2$ | shrinks |

Each spectrum is only as good as its assumptions. A real box has finite walls, so the levels are lower and only finitely many are bound. A real molecular bond is anharmonic, so the spacing shrinks at high $n$. Hydrogen's formula assumes one electron and ignores fine structure.

### Photons from transitions

A jump between levels emits a photon of energy $\Delta E$, with wavelength $\lambda = hc/\Delta E = 1240\ \text{eV nm}/\Delta E$.

@example
title: Levels of an electron in a 2.0 nm box
problem: An electron moves in a one-dimensional box of width $L = 2.0$ nm. Take $E_1 = 0.0940$ eV for this width. Find $E_2$, $E_3$ and the wavelength of the $3 \to 1$ photon. Use $hc = 1240$ eV nm.
step: The energy scales as $n^2$: $E_2 = 4E_1 = 0.376$ eV and $E_3 = 9E_1 = 0.846$ eV.
step: The transition energy is $\Delta E = E_3 - E_1 = 8E_1 = 0.752$ eV.
step: Wavelength: $\lambda = 1240/0.752 \approx 1650$ nm, in the infrared.
step: Check the spacing rule: $E_3 - E_2 = 5E_1 = 0.470$ eV is larger than $E_2 - E_1 = 3E_1 = 0.282$ eV, as expected for a box.

@example
title: A vibrating molecule
problem: A molecular bond behaves as an oscillator with $\omega = 4.0\times10^{14}$ rad/s. Find the level spacing, the zero-point energy and the wavelength of the $1 \to 0$ photon. Take $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J and $hc = 1240$ eV nm.
step: Level spacing: $\hbar\omega = (1.055\times10^{-34})(4.0\times10^{14}) = 4.22\times10^{-20}$ J $= 0.263$ eV.
step: Zero-point energy: $E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega = 0.132$ eV, and $E_1 = \tfrac32\hbar\omega = 0.395$ eV.
step: Photon: $\Delta E = \hbar\omega = 0.263$ eV, so $\lambda = 1240/0.263 \approx 4.7\times10^{3}$ nm $= 4.7\ \mu$m, in the infrared.
step: Check: $\lambda = 2\pi c/\omega = 2\pi(3.00\times10^{8})/(4.0\times10^{14}) = 4.7\ \mu$m, the classical oscillation wavelength.

@mistakes
- Starting the box levels at $n = 0$. There is no $n = 0$ state, and $E_1 > 0$.
- Using $n$ instead of $n + \tfrac12$ for the oscillator, which loses the zero-point energy.
- Mixing up the patterns: $E \propto n^2$ for the box, $E \propto n + \tfrac12$ for the oscillator and $E \propto -1/n^2$ for hydrogen.
- Treating the photon energy as a level energy rather than the difference between two levels.

prompt: An electron is confined to a one-dimensional box of width $L = 0.50$ nm. Take $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J. What is its ground-state energy?
unit: eV
answer: 1.50
tol: 1%
check: (6.626e-34/(2*0.5e-9))**2/(2*9.109e-31)/1.602e-19
hint: In the ground state $L = \lambda/2$, so $\lambda = 2L$.
hint: Then $p = h/(2L)$ and $E_1 = p^2/2m$; convert joules to eV.
solution: $p = h/(2L) = 6.626\times10^{-34}/(1.0\times10^{-9}) = 6.626\times10^{-25}$ kg m/s and $E_1 = p^2/2m = 2.41\times10^{-19}$ J $= 1.50$ eV.

solution: $E_n \propto n^2/L^2$, so levels scale as $n^2$, the spacing $(2n + 1)E_1$ grows, and halving $L$ multiplies all energies by $4$. The ground state has $E_1 > 0$ because $n = 0$ is not allowed.

@quiz num
prompt: An electron in a one-dimensional box emits a photon of energy $1.88$ eV when it falls from $n = 3$ to $n = 2$. Find the width of the box. Take $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J.
unit: nm
answer: 1.00
tol: 1%
check: root(lambda L: 5*(6.626e-34)**2/(8*9.109e-31*(L*1e-9)**2)/1.602e-19 - 1.88, 0.2, 5)
hint: The transition energy is $E_3 - E_2$ in terms of $E_1$.
hint: $E_3 - E_2 = 5E_1 = 5h^2/(8mL^2)$; solve for $L$.
solution: $5h^2/(8mL^2) = 1.88\ \text{eV} = 3.01\times10^{-19}$ J gives $L^2 = 5h^2/(8m \times 3.01\times10^{-19}) = 1.0\times10^{-18}$ m$^2$, so $L \approx 1.00$ nm.

@quiz num
prompt: A molecular vibration with $\omega = 5.0\times10^{14}$ rad/s makes the transition $n = 1 \to n = 0$. What is the wavelength of the emitted photon? Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}$ m/s.
unit: μm
answer: 3.77
tol: 1%
check: 2*math.pi*3.00e8/5.0e14*1e6
hint: The photon frequency is $f = \Delta E/h$ with $\Delta E = \hbar\omega$.
hint: That makes $f = \omega/2\pi$, so $\lambda = c/f = 2\pi c/\omega$.
solution: $\lambda = 2\pi(3.00\times10^{8})/(5.0\times10^{14}) = 3.77\times10^{-6}$ m $= 3.77\ \mu$m, in the infrared.
