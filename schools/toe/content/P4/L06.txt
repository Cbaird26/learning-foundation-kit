@article
Shine light on a clean metal surface in vacuum and electrons can come out. These are **photoelectrons**. To measure their energy, apply a retarding voltage between the metal and a collector. The **stopping potential** $V_0$ is the smallest retarding voltage that brings the current to zero, so the fastest electrons have

$$K_{\max} = eV_0 .$$

### What the wave picture predicts

Classically, light is a continuous wave carrying energy at a rate proportional to its intensity. That implies:

1. Brighter light gives electrons more energy, so $K_{\max}$ should grow with intensity.
2. Any frequency should work if the light is bright enough or shines long enough, so there should be no threshold.
3. In dim light an electron would need time to soak up enough energy, so there should be a delay.
4. $K_{\max}$ should not depend on the colour (frequency).

### What is observed

1. $K_{\max}$ does **not** depend on intensity. Intensity changes only the photocurrent (the number of electrons per second).
2. Below a **threshold frequency** $f_0$, no electrons are emitted however bright the light.
3. There is no measurable delay (less than about a nanosecond), even for very dim light.
4. $K_{\max}$ rises linearly with $f$ above threshold, with a slope that is the same for every metal.

Each of the four observations contradicts the matching expectation of the wave picture.

### The photon model

In Einstein's photon model, light of frequency $f$ arrives in quanta (photons) with energy $hf$, where $h$ is Planck's constant. An electron absorbs one photon at a time. It needs at least the **work function** $\phi$ to escape the metal, and the rest becomes kinetic energy (Einstein's photoelectric equation):

$$K_{\max} = hf - \phi,\qquad eV_0 = hf - \phi,\qquad f_0 = \frac{\phi}{h}.$$

A graph of $V_0$ against $f$ is a straight line with slope $h/e$ and intercept $-\phi/e$ on the vertical axis. The slope is the same for all metals; the work function only slides the line sideways. The value of $h$ found this way agrees with Planck's value from blackbody radiation, which is strong evidence that one constant governs both phenomena.

More intense light of the same frequency means more photons per second and so more electrons per second (a larger current), but every photon still carries $hf$, so $K_{\max}$ does not change.

### Numbers

With $hc = 1240$ eV nm, a photon of wavelength $\lambda$ (in nm) has energy $1240/\lambda$ eV. For $\lambda = 400$ nm that is $3.10$ eV. The threshold wavelength is $\lambda_0 = 1240/\phi$.

### Writing the argument

When asked to explain a failure of a classical model, do three things: state the classical prediction, state the observation that contradicts it, and state the new assumption (one photon, one electron, energy $hf$) with a number computed from it.

@example
title: Stopping potential and threshold
problem: A metal has work function $\phi = 4.1$ eV. Light of wavelength $250$ nm falls on it. Take $hc = 1240$ eV nm. Find the photon energy, $K_{\max}$, the stopping potential and the threshold wavelength.
step: Photon energy: $E = 1240/250 = 4.96$ eV.
step: Since $4.96 > 4.1$, electrons are emitted with $K_{\max} = 4.96 - 4.1 = 0.86$ eV.
step: The stopping potential satisfies $eV_0 = K_{\max}$, so $V_0 = 0.86$ V.
step: The threshold wavelength is $\lambda_0 = 1240/4.1 \approx 302$ nm. Any light with a longer wavelength ejects no electrons from this metal.

@example
title: From power to photocurrent
problem: A $2.0$ mW beam of $400$ nm light falls on a metal, and one photon in $1000$ ejects an electron. Estimate the photocurrent. Use $hc = 1240$ eV nm, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C.
step: Photon energy: $1240/400 = 3.10$ eV $= 4.97\times10^{-19}$ J.
step: Photon rate: $(2.0\times10^{-3})/(4.97\times10^{-19}) \approx 4.0\times10^{15}$ photons per second.
step: Electron rate: $4.0\times10^{15}/1000 = 4.0\times10^{12}$ per second.
step: Current: $I = (4.0\times10^{12})(1.602\times10^{-19}) \approx 6.4\times10^{-7}$ A. Doubling the power doubles the current but leaves $K_{\max}$ unchanged.

@mistakes
- Believing that a very bright beam below the threshold frequency can eject electrons by "adding up" energy. One photon gives its energy to one electron.
- Mixing up the roles: frequency sets $K_{\max}$, while intensity sets the photocurrent.
- Combining energies in eV with $h$ in J s without converting. Use $hc = 1240$ eV nm, or convert eV to joules first.
- Using the same work function for every metal. The slope $h/e$ of the $V_0$ versus $f$ line is universal; $\phi$ is material-specific.

@practice self
prompt: A student finds that bright red light ejects no electrons from a metal, while very dim ultraviolet light of wavelength $270$ nm ejects some at once. The metal has work function $4.3$ eV (take $hc = 1240$ eV nm). (a) Explain why the wave model cannot account for this. (b) Explain it with photons. (c) Compute the stopping potential for the $270$ nm light. (d) Say what happens to the stopping potential and the photocurrent if the ultraviolet intensity is doubled.
solution: (a) A continuous wave delivers energy at a rate proportional to its intensity, so bright red light should eventually eject electrons and dim light should show a delay; neither is observed. (b) Each photon carries $hf$ and gives all of it to one electron. Red photons each carry less than $\phi = 4.3$ eV, so none can eject an electron however many arrive; each ultraviolet photon has $1240/270 \approx 4.59$ eV $> \phi$. (c) $K_{\max} = 4.59 - 4.3 \approx 0.29$ eV, so $V_0 \approx 0.29$ V. (d) $V_0$ is unchanged because each photon's energy is unchanged; the photocurrent doubles because twice as many photons arrive per second.
rubric: Names the classical prediction that fails (no threshold or a delay at low intensity) and says that the observations contradict it.
rubric: Uses one photon, one electron, with photon energy $hf$ compared with $\phi$, to explain the lack of emission for red light.
rubric: Computes $V_0 \approx 0.29$ V with correct use of $hc = 1240$ eV nm and units.
rubric: States that $V_0$ stays the same and the photocurrent doubles when the intensity doubles.
hint: Compare the energy of one photon of each colour with the work function, not the total energy delivered.
hint: Photon energy at $270$ nm is $1240/270$ eV.

@quiz num
prompt: Photoelectrons have a maximum kinetic energy of $0.80$ eV. Taking the electron mass $9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J, find the speed of the fastest electrons (nonrelativistic treatment is fine).
unit: m/s
answer: 5.30e5
tol: 1%
check: math.sqrt(2*0.80*1.602e-19/9.109e-31)
hint: Convert the energy to joules first.
hint: Use $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$, so $v = \sqrt{2K/m}$.
solution: $K = 1.28\times10^{-19}$ J, so $v = \sqrt{2(1.28\times10^{-19})/(9.109\times10^{-31})} \approx 5.30\times10^{5}$ m/s, about $0.18\%$ of $c$, so ignoring relativity is justified.
