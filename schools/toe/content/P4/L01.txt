@article
An **event** is something that happens at one place and one instant: a flash, a collision, a clock reading. To describe events you need a **frame of reference**: a set of rulers and synchronized clocks that assigns every event coordinates $(t, x, y, z)$. Different observers use different frames, and the whole subject is about how their descriptions fit together.

### Inertial frames

A frame is **inertial** if a free object (no net force on it) moves in a straight line at constant speed. A spacecraft coasting far from any star is a good inertial frame. A car taking a sharp bend is not: loose objects inside it appear to be pushed sideways. Any frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial.

That leads to the **principle of relativity**: the laws of physics take the same form in every inertial frame. No experiment done entirely inside a smoothly moving cabin can tell you that you are moving.

### The Galilean picture

Let frame $S'$ move with constant speed $v$ along $+x$ relative to frame $S$, with the origins coinciding at $t = 0$. Everyday intuition says

$$x' = x - vt,\qquad y' = y,\qquad z' = z,\qquad t' = t.$$

This is the **Galilean transformation**. Differentiating gives the velocity rule $u' = u - v$. A passenger walking at $1.5$ m/s along a train moving at $30$ m/s has speed $31.5$ m/s relative to the ground.

### Light breaks the rule

Maxwell's equations of electromagnetism predict one speed $c = 3.00\times10^{8}$ m/s for light in vacuum, and experiments find that the measured value does not depend on the motion of the source or the observer. The Michelson–Morley experiment, which looked for an effect of Earth's motion on the speed of light, is the classic null result. Galilean addition would give $1.5c$ for a pulse fired forward from a source moving at $0.5c$; nature gives $c$. Special relativity rests on two postulates:

1. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames.
2. The speed of light in vacuum is $c$ in all inertial frames.

If speeds do not add the Galilean way, the assumption $t' = t$ must go.

### A light clock

A **light clock** is two mirrors a distance $L$ apart with a light pulse bouncing between them. At rest, one round trip (one tick) takes the **proper time** $\Delta t_0 = 2L/c$. Now let the clock move at speed $v$ perpendicular to the line joining the mirrors. In the lab frame the pulse follows a zigzag. During one tick $\Delta t$ each half-trip has length $c\,\Delta t/2$, vertical part $L$ and horizontal part $v\,\Delta t/2$. Pythagoras gives

$$\left(\tfrac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = L^2 + \left(\tfrac{v\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \Delta t = \frac{2L/c}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\,\Delta t_0 .$$

$$\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} \ge 1 .$$

**Moving clocks run slow** by the factor $\gamma$ (the **Lorentz factor**). At $v = 0.6c$ you get $\gamma = 1.25$. For $v \ll c$, $\gamma \approx 1 + v^2/2c^2$, which is why you never notice the effect at everyday speeds. The proper time is always the shortest time: it is the time between two events as read on a single clock present at both.

### Simultaneity is relative

Because every frame measures the same light speed, two events that are simultaneous in one frame are generally not simultaneous in another. Next week's Lorentz transformation makes this exact.

@example
title: Transforming an event and a velocity (Galilean)
problem: A train (frame $S'$) moves at $30$ m/s along $+x$ relative to the ground ($S$). At $t = t' = 0$ a passenger leaves the rear door, which is at $x' = 0$, and walks forward at $1.5$ m/s relative to the train. Find the passenger's position and velocity in the ground frame at $t = 4.0$ s.
step: In the train frame, $x' = (1.5)(4.0) = 6.0$ m at $t' = t = 4.0$ s.
step: Invert the Galilean transformation $x' = x - vt$: $x = x' + vt = 6.0 + (30)(4.0) = 126$ m.
step: Velocity: $u = u' + v = 1.5 + 30 = 31.5$ m/s.
step: Check: a constant $31.5$ m/s for $4.0$ s covers $126$ m, matching the position found in the previous steps.

@example
title: How long does a moving clock take to tick?
problem: A clock ticks once every $2.0$ s in its own rest frame. It passes you at $v = 0.8c$. How long between ticks do you measure?
step: The $2.0$ s is the proper time $\Delta t_0$, because both ticks happen at the clock's own location.
step: Compute $v^2/c^2 = 0.64$, so $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 1/0.6 = 5/3$.
step: Your measured time is longer: $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_0 = (5/3)(2.0\ \text{s}) \approx 3.3$ s.
step: Sanity check: $\gamma > 1$ and the moving clock's proper time is the smaller of the two intervals, as it must be.

@mistakes
- Swapping the roles of $\Delta t$ and $\Delta t_0$, which gives a moving clock that runs fast. Proper time is the shortest interval.
- Keeping $t' = t$ while also insisting that light has speed $c$ in every frame; the two cannot both hold.
- Calling a frame inertial because its speed is constant. Going round a bend at constant speed still means accelerating.
- Forgetting that $\gamma$ uses $v^2/c^2$: entering $v$ in m/s and $c$ as $3$ gives nonsense.

prompt: Frame $S'$ moves at $v = 20$ m/s along $+x$ relative to $S$. An event occurs at $x = 100$ m and $t = 3.0$ s in $S$. Using the Galilean transformation, find its coordinate $x'$ in $S'$.
unit: m
answer: 40
check: (lambda v, x, t: x - v*t)(20, 100, 3.0)
hint: The Galilean rule is $x' = x - vt$ with $t' = t$.

prompt: Compute the Lorentz factor $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$ for $v = 0.8c$. Give an exact fraction.
answer: 5/3
check: 1/math.sqrt(1 - 0.8**2)
hint: First find $v^2/c^2 = 0.8^2$.

solution: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 1/0.6 = 5/3 \approx 1.667$.

@practice num
prompt: A light clock has mirrors $1.5$ m apart, with the line joining them perpendicular to the clock's motion. In the lab the clock moves at $0.6c$. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}$ m/s. How long does one round-trip tick last in the lab frame?
unit: ns
answer: 12.5
tol: 1%
check: 2*1.5/math.sqrt((3.00e8)**2 - (0.6*3.00e8)**2)*1e9
hint: In the lab frame the pulse travels a slanted path. Each half-trip has vertical part $L$ and horizontal part $v\,\Delta t/2$.
hint: Pythagoras: $(c\,\Delta t/2)^2 = L^2 + (v\,\Delta t/2)^2$, so $\Delta t = 2L/\sqrt{c^2 - v^2}$.
solution: $\Delta t = 2(1.5)/\sqrt{c^2 - 0.36c^2} = 3.0/(0.8 \times 3.00\times10^{8}) = 1.25\times10^{-8}$ s $= 12.5$ ns. At rest the tick would be $10$ ns, and $\gamma = 1.25$.

@quiz num
prompt: An unstable particle has a proper mean lifetime of $2.2\ \mu$s. In the lab it moves at $0.96c$. What mean lifetime does the lab measure?
unit: μs
answer: 7.86
tol: 1%
check: 2.2/math.sqrt(1 - 0.96**2)
hint: The $2.2\ \mu$s is the proper time, measured in the particle's rest frame.
hint: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.96^2}$, and $1 - 0.9216 = 0.0784 = 0.28^2$.
solution: $\gamma = 1/0.28 = 25/7 \approx 3.571$, so the lab lifetime is $(25/7)(2.2) \approx 7.86\ \mu$s.

@quiz ms
