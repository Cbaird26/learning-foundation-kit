@article
Light, which looks like a wave, also behaves like particles (photons) with energy $E = hf$ and momentum $p = h/\lambda$ (confirmed by Compton scattering of X-rays from electrons). The **matter-wave hypothesis** (de Broglie's hypothesis) turns the idea around: a particle with momentum $p$ and energy $E$ also has wave properties,

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p},\qquad f = \frac{E}{h}.$$

This $\lambda$ is the particle's **de Broglie wavelength** (its matter-wave wavelength). For a photon, $p = E/c = hf/c = h/\lambda$, so the same relation covers light.

### Evidence: electron diffraction

A beam of electrons scattered from a crystal produces bright spots at particular angles, just as X-rays do (the Davisson–Germer experiment). Treating the crystal planes (spacing $d$) as partial mirrors, waves reflected from adjacent planes reinforce when they satisfy the **Bragg condition**:

$$2d\sin\theta = n\lambda,\qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$

where $\theta$ is the glancing angle measured from the planes, not from the normal. The angles depend on $\lambda = h/p$ and on nothing else about the electrons. Electrons sent one at a time through a double slit also build up interference fringes, and so do neutrons and whole atoms.

### Computing wavelengths

Nonrelativistically $p = \sqrt{2mK}$. For an electron accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$, $K = eV$ and

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e e V}} \approx \frac{1.226\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{V/\text{volts}}}.$$

At $150$ V this gives about $0.100$ nm, the spacing of atoms in a crystal. That is why crystals diffract electrons of such energies.

At high energy use the relativistic momentum from week 4, $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2}$, then $\lambda = hc/(pc)$ with $hc = 1240$ eV nm.

### When do waves matter?

A baseball ($0.145$ kg) at $40$ m/s has $\lambda = h/p \approx 1.1\times10^{-34}$ m. This is far smaller than anything it can interact with, so no wave effect is ever seen. Wave behaviour shows up only when $\lambda$ is comparable to the size or spacing of the structures a particle meets: slits, crystal planes, atoms.

### Packets and standing waves

A particle is localized but a pure sine wave fills all space, so a particle must be described by a **wave packet**, a sum of waves with a spread of wavelengths. The packet's envelope moves with the group velocity, which equals the particle's speed $v$. (The phase velocity is $E/p = c^2/v$, which can exceed $c$, but it carries no signal.) Next week this gives the Heisenberg uncertainty principle.

A wave confined between two walls a distance $L$ apart must fit a whole number of half-wavelengths: $L = n\lambda/2$. Then $p_n = nh/(2L)$ and $K_n = n^2h^2/(8mL^2)$. This is the first hint of quantized energies.

### Same wavelength, different energy

At equal wavelength a photon carries far more energy than an electron: $E_\gamma = hc/\lambda$ but $K_e = h^2/(2m\lambda^2)$. At $0.5$ nm the ratio is about $400$.

@example
title: De Broglie wavelength and Bragg angle for 100 V electrons
problem: Electrons are accelerated from rest through $100$ V and strike a crystal whose planes are $0.215$ nm apart. Find the de Broglie wavelength $\lambda$ and the first-order Bragg angle. Use $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ C.
step: Kinetic energy: $K = eV = 1.602\times10^{-17}$ J, which is only $100$ eV, so the nonrelativistic formula is safe.
step: Momentum: $p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.602\times10^{-17})} = 5.40\times10^{-24}$ kg m/s.
step: Wavelength: $\lambda = h/p = 6.626\times10^{-34}/5.40\times10^{-24} = 1.23\times10^{-10}$ m $= 0.123$ nm.
step: Bragg condition: $\sin\theta = \lambda/(2d) = 0.123/0.430 = 0.286$, so $\theta \approx 16.6^\circ$.

@example
title: A relativistic electron
problem: An electron has kinetic energy $5.0$ MeV ($mc^2 = 0.511$ MeV). Find its de Broglie wavelength and compare with the nonrelativistic formula. Use $hc = 1240$ eV nm.
step: Relativistic momentum: $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2} = \sqrt{25 + 5.11} = 5.49$ MeV.
step: Wavelength: $\lambda = hc/(pc) = 1240\ \text{eV nm}/(5.49\times10^{6}\ \text{eV}) = 2.26\times10^{-4}$ nm $= 226$ fm.
step: The nonrelativistic momentum would be $pc = \sqrt{2mc^2K} = \sqrt{5.11} = 2.26$ MeV, giving $549$ fm.
step: The nonrelativistic formula is wrong by more than a factor of $2$ here, because $K \gg mc^2$.

@mistakes
- Using $\lambda = h/(mv)$ at speeds near $c$; the momentum is $\gamma m v$, or use $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2}$.
- Measuring the Bragg angle from the normal to the surface. In $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ the angle is measured from the planes.
- Expecting visible wave effects for everyday objects. Their wavelengths are many orders of magnitude below any structure they meet.
- Forgetting to convert electron-volts to joules before using $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ with SI constants.

@practice mc
prompt: An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy. Which has the longer de Broglie wavelength, and why?
choice*: The electron, because its smaller mass gives it a smaller momentum $p = \sqrt{2mK}$.
choice: The proton, because it is heavier and so carries more momentum at equal kinetic energy.
choice: They have equal wavelengths, because wavelength depends only on the energy.
choice: The electron, because it has the larger charge-to-mass ratio, independent of momentum.
hint: At fixed $K$, how does the momentum $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ depend on the mass?
solution: At equal $K$, $p \propto \sqrt{m}$ and $\lambda = h/p \propto 1/\sqrt{m}$. The electron is lighter, so its wavelength is longer, by $\sqrt{m_p/m_e} \approx 43$.

@practice num
prompt: An electron and a photon both have wavelength $0.50$ nm. Take $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 3.00\times10^{8}$ m/s and $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg. Find the ratio of the photon's energy to the electron's kinetic energy.
answer: 412
tol: 1%
check: (6.626e-34*3.00e8/0.5e-9)/((6.626e-34/0.5e-9)**2/(2*9.109e-31))
hint: Photon: $E = hc/\lambda$. Electron: $p = h/\lambda$ and $K = p^2/2m$.
hint: The photon energy is about $2.5$ keV, while the electron's kinetic energy is only a few eV.
solution: $E_\gamma = hc/\lambda = 3.98\times10^{-16}$ J. Electron: $p = h/\lambda = 1.325\times10^{-24}$ kg m/s, so $K = p^2/2m = 9.64\times10^{-19}$ J ($6.0$ eV). The ratio is $412$.
