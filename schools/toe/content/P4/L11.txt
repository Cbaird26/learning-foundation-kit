@article
A nucleus contains $Z$ protons and $N$ neutrons, together called **nucleons**, with mass number $A = Z + N$. Isotopes of an element share $Z$ but differ in $N$. Nuclear masses are quoted in atomic mass units, where $1\ \text{u} = 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg $= 931.494\ \text{MeV}/c^2$.

### Size and density

Scattering experiments show that nuclei are nearly spherical with radius

$$R \approx r_0A^{1/3},\qquad r_0 \approx 1.2\ \text{fm}\quad (1\ \text{fm} = 10^{-15}\ \text{m}).$$

(The femtometre, fm, is also called the fermi.) The volume is then proportional to $A$, so the density is nearly the same for all nuclei, about $2.3\times10^{17}$ kg/m$^3$. A teaspoon of such matter would weigh about a billion tonnes. Constant density tells you that each nucleon interacts only with its nearest neighbours.

### Forces and stability

The **strong force** attracts any two nucleons (proton-proton, neutron-neutron or proton-neutron) at distances of 1 to 2 fm, and it is far stronger than the electric force there. Protons also repel each other electrically (the Coulomb repulsion), and that repulsion has a long range. In heavy nuclei the repulsion adds up, so extra neutrons are needed to dilute it. Stable light nuclei have $N \approx Z$, while stable heavy nuclei have $N > Z$. Nuclei with $2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82$ or $126$ protons or neutrons (the **magic numbers**) are unusually stable, hinting at shell structure like that of atomic electrons.

### Mass defect and binding energy

A bound nucleus has less mass than its separated nucleons; the difference is the **mass defect**. The **binding energy** $B$ is the energy needed to pull the nucleus completely apart, and by Einstein's mass–energy relation $E = mc^2$ it equals the mass defect times $c^2$:

$$B = \big[\,Z\,m_{\text{H}} + N\,m_n - M(^{A}\text{X})\,\big]c^2 .$$

Here $m_{\text{H}}$ and $M$ are **atomic** masses, so the electron masses cancel, and $m_n = 1.008665$ u. Multiply the mass defect in u by $931.494$ MeV to get $B$ in MeV. The deuteron has $B = 2.22$ MeV, and each nucleon in $^{4}$He is bound by $7.07$ MeV.

### Binding energy per nucleon

The ratio $B/A$ rises steeply for light nuclei, peaks near $A \approx 56$ (iron and nickel) at about $8.8$ MeV, and then falls slowly to about $7.6$ MeV for uranium. A reaction that moves nuclei toward the peak releases energy:

- **Fusion** of light nuclei (moving up the steep side) releases energy.
- **Fission** of a very heavy nucleus into medium-mass fragments (moving up the gentle side) releases energy.

The energy released in a reaction equals the mass difference times $c^2$, which is also the increase in total binding energy: $Q = (\sum m_{\text{in}} - \sum m_{\text{out}})c^2$.

### Scales

Nuclear energies are of the order of MeV per nucleon, about a million times atomic energies. Week 8's confinement estimate explains this: the nucleus is about $10^5$ times smaller than an atom, which raises $K \sim \hbar^2/(8m\,\Delta x^2)$ by about $10^{10}$, and the nucleon's roughly $2000$ times larger mass lowers it by about $2000$, leaving a factor of a few million.

@example
title: Binding energy of helium-4
problem: Find the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon of $^{4}$He. Atomic masses: $m_{\text{H}} = 1.007825$ u, $m_n = 1.008665$ u and $M(^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603$ u.
step: Mass of the separated parts: $2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665) = 2.015650 + 2.017330 = 4.032980$ u.
step: Mass defect: $4.032980 - 4.002603 = 0.030377$ u.
step: Binding energy: $B = 0.030377 \times 931.494 \approx 28.30$ MeV.
step: Per nucleon: $28.30/4 \approx 7.07$ MeV, high for such a light nucleus, which is why $^{4}$He is so stable.

@example
title: Radius and density of a nucleus
problem: A nucleus has mass number $A = 64$. Estimate its radius and its mass density. Use $r_0 = 1.2$ fm and a nuclear mass of $A \times 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg.
step: Radius: $A^{1/3} = 4$, so $R = 1.2 \times 4 = 4.8$ fm.
step: Volume: $\tfrac43\pi R^3 = \tfrac43\pi(4.8\times10^{-15})^3 = 4.63\times10^{-43}$ m$^3$.
step: Mass: $64 \times 1.6605\times10^{-27} = 1.063\times10^{-25}$ kg.
step: Density: $1.063\times10^{-25}/4.63\times10^{-43} \approx 2.3\times10^{17}$ kg/m$^3$. The factor $A$ cancels, so every nucleus has about this density.

@mistakes
- Using the sign backwards: the nucleus is lighter than its parts, so $B = (\text{parts} - \text{nucleus})c^2$ is positive.
- Mixing nuclear and atomic masses, so electron masses fail to cancel. Use $m_{\text{H}}$ with atomic masses, not $m_p$.
- Judging a reaction by $B$ instead of $B/A$, or forgetting that the sum of binding energies must increase for energy to be released.
- Expecting nuclear density to change much with $A$; it is nearly constant.

@practice num
prompt: Estimate the radius of an iron-56 nucleus using $R = r_0A^{1/3}$ with $r_0 = 1.2$ fm.
unit: fm
answer: 4.59
tol: 1%
check: 1.2*56**(1/3)
hint: Take the cube root of $56$.
hint: $56^{1/3} \approx 3.83$.
solution: $R = 1.2 \times 56^{1/3} = 1.2 \times 3.826 \approx 4.59$ fm.

answer: 2.29e17
tol: 1%

@practice num
prompt: Find the binding energy of the deuteron. Atomic masses: $m_{\text{H}} = 1.007825$ u, $m_n = 1.008665$ u, $M(^{2}\text{H}) = 2.014102$ u. Use $1\ \text{u} = 931.494$ MeV$/c^2$.
unit: MeV
answer: 2.22
tol: 1%
check: (1.007825 + 1.008665 - 2.014102)*931.494
hint: Compare the mass of one hydrogen atom plus one neutron with the mass of the deuterium atom.
hint: The mass defect is $2.016490 - 2.014102$ u.
solution: The defect is $0.002388$ u, so $B = 0.002388 \times 931.494 \approx 2.22$ MeV.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements about the curve of binding energy per nucleon are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Fusing two very light nuclei releases energy.
choice*: Splitting a very heavy nucleus into two medium-mass nuclei releases energy.
choice: The heaviest nuclei have the largest binding energy per nucleon.
choice: Fusing two iron nuclei releases a large amount of energy.
hint: Energy is released when the products lie closer to the peak near $A = 56$ than the reactants.
solution: Light nuclei are on the rising side of the curve and heavy nuclei on the falling side, so both fusion of light and fission of heavy nuclei move toward the peak and release energy. The peak is near iron, so iron nuclei fusing would move away from it and require energy.

@practice num
prompt: Find the binding energy per nucleon of $^{16}$O. Atomic masses: $m_{\text{H}} = 1.007825$ u, $m_n = 1.008665$ u, $M(^{16}\text{O}) = 15.994915$ u. Use $1\ \text{u} = 931.494$ MeV$/c^2$.
unit: MeV
answer: 7.98
tol: 1%
check: (8*1.007825 + 8*1.008665 - 15.994915)*931.494/16
hint: $^{16}$O has $Z = 8$ and $N = 8$.
hint: Mass of the parts is $8(1.007825) + 8(1.008665)$ u; find the defect, convert, then divide by $16$.
solution: Parts: $8.062600 + 8.069320 = 16.131920$ u. Defect: $0.137005$ u, so $B = 127.6$ MeV and $B/A = 127.6/16 \approx 7.98$ MeV.

@quiz num
prompt: Two deuterium nuclei fuse into helium-4 in the reaction $^{2}\text{H} + {}^{2}\text{H} \to {}^{4}\text{He}$. Atomic masses: $M(^{2}\text{H}) = 2.014102$ u and $M(^{4}\text{He}) = 4.002603$ u. How much energy is released? Use $1\ \text{u} = 931.494$ MeV$/c^2$.
unit: MeV
answer: 23.8
tol: 1%
check: (2*2.014102 - 4.002603)*931.494
hint: The energy released is the decrease in mass times $c^2$.
hint: Initial mass $2 \times 2.014102 = 4.028204$ u.
solution: $\Delta m = 4.028204 - 4.002603 = 0.025601$ u, so $Q = 0.025601 \times 931.494 \approx 23.8$ MeV.

@quiz num
prompt: By what factor is the radius of a uranium-238 nucleus larger than the radius of a helium-4 nucleus, using $R \propto A^{1/3}$?
answer: 3.90
tol: 1%
check: (238/4)**(1/3)
hint: The ratio of radii is the cube root of the ratio of mass numbers.
hint: $238/4 = 59.5$.
solution: $R_U/R_{He} = (238/4)^{1/3} = 59.5^{1/3} \approx 3.90$.
