### Constituents and forces

The Standard Model of particle physics lists the fundamental matter particles as **quarks** (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom, with charges $+\tfrac23$ or $-\tfrac13$) and **leptons** (electron, muon, tau and their three neutrinos, with charges $-1$ or $0$), each with an antiparticle. Quarks are never found alone. They combine into **hadrons**: **baryons** (three quarks, such as the proton $uud$ and neutron $udd$) and **mesons** (a quark and an antiquark).

Four interactions act: the **strong** interaction (carried by gluons) binds quarks and, as a residual effect, nucleons; the **electromagnetic** interaction (photon); the **weak** interaction (W and Z bosons), which can change flavour and causes beta decay; and gravity, negligible for single particles. The Higgs boson is tied to how the other fundamental particles acquire mass.

### Conservation laws

In every observed interaction these quantities are conserved: energy, momentum, angular momentum, electric charge, baryon number (baryon $+1$, antibaryon $-1$) and lepton number (lepton $+1$, antilepton $-1$). Strangeness is conserved by the strong and electromagnetic interactions but not by the weak one. A reaction proceeds only if all conservation laws allow it and enough energy is available. For beta decay,

$$n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e :\quad \text{charge } 0 \to +1 - 1 + 0,\quad \text{baryon } 1 \to 1,\quad \text{lepton } 0 \to 0 + 1 - 1 .$$

The antineutrino is required: without it, lepton number would change from $0$ to $+1$.

### Decay energy

Radioactive decays can be alpha ($^4$He emitted), beta ($e^\pm$ and a neutrino) or gamma (a photon from an excited nucleus). The energy released is

$$Q = \Big(\sum m_{\text{initial}} - \sum m_{\text{final}}\Big)c^2 > 0 ,$$

shared among the products as kinetic energy. For the neutron, $Q = (939.565 - 938.272 - 0.511)\ \text{MeV} = 0.782$ MeV.

### The decay law

Decay is random for each nucleus but predictable for large samples:

$$N(t) = N_0e^{-\lambda t},\qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda},\qquad \tau = \frac1\lambda = 1.443\,t_{1/2}.$$

The fraction left after time $t$ is $2^{-t/t_{1/2}}$. The activity is $A = \lambda N$, measured in becquerels (decays per second).

### Relativity in decays

A moving unstable particle lives longer in the lab by the factor $\gamma$. Cosmic-ray muons ($\tau = 2.2\ \mu$s) made about $10$ km above the ground can reach it only because of this time dilation.

### Thresholds from invariants

To make new particles in a collision, the invariant $s = E_{\text{tot}}^2 - (p_{\text{tot}}c)^2$ must be at least the square of the sum of the product rest energies. In the lab with a beam particle of energy $E$ on a stationary target of mass $m$, $s = 2Emc^2 + 2(mc^2)^2$. For $p + p \to p + p + p + \bar p$, require $s = (4mc^2)^2$, so $E = 7mc^2$ and the beam kinetic energy is $K = 6mc^2 \approx 5.63$ GeV. The invariant makes the calculation frame-independent.

@example
title: Testing reactions against conservation laws
problem: Decide whether (a) $\pi^- \to \mu^- + \bar\nu_\mu$ and (b) $\mu^- \to e^- + \gamma$ are allowed by charge, baryon number and lepton number (with separate electron and muon lepton numbers).
step: (a) Charge: $-1 \to -1 + 0$. Baryon number: $0 \to 0$. Muon number: $0 \to +1 - 1 = 0$. Electron number: $0 \to 0$. All conserved, so it is allowed (it is the main pion decay).
step: (b) Charge: $-1 \to -1 + 0$. Baryon number: $0 \to 0$.
step: Muon number: $+1 \to 0$ (neither the electron nor the photon carries it), and electron number: $0 \to +1$. Lepton flavour is not conserved.
step: So (b) is forbidden under these rules. Searches for it have never found it, limiting it to fewer than about one in $10^{12}$ muon decays.

@example
title: How much of a sample remains?
problem: A radioactive sample has a half-life of $8.0$ days. Find its decay constant and the fraction remaining after $20$ days.
step: Decay constant: $\lambda = \ln 2/t_{1/2} = 0.693/8.0 = 0.0866$ day$^{-1}$.
step: Number of half-lives: $20/8.0 = 2.5$.
step: Fraction remaining: $2^{-2.5} = 0.177$.
step: Check with the exponential form: $e^{-\lambda t} = e^{-0.0866 \times 20} = e^{-1.73} = 0.177$.

@mistakes
- Checking only electric charge. A reaction also needs baryon number and lepton number to balance, as in $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$.
- Writing a neutrino where an antineutrino belongs. Electrons in beta decay are accompanied by antineutrinos.
- Using the half-life as the mean lifetime. They differ by the factor $\ln 2$: $\tau = 1.443\,t_{1/2}$.
- Forgetting time dilation for fast-moving unstable particles in the lab frame.

@practice num
prompt: Muons are created $10$ km above the ground and travel straight down at $0.99c$. Their proper mean lifetime is $2.2\ \mu$s. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}$ m/s. What fraction of them survive to reach the ground?
answer: 0.1154
tol: 1%
check: math.exp(-(10000/(0.99*3.00e8))/((1/math.sqrt(1 - 0.99**2))*2.2e-6))
hint: Find the travel time in the ground frame, then use the lifetime as measured in that frame.
hint: In the ground frame the mean lifetime is $\gamma\tau$ with $\gamma = 7.09$, and the fraction left is $e^{-t/(\gamma\tau)}$.
solution: The travel time is $t = 10^4/(0.99 \times 3.00\times10^{8}) = 33.7\ \mu$s. The dilated lifetime is $\gamma\tau = 7.09 \times 2.2 = 15.6\ \mu$s, so the fraction is $e^{-33.7/15.6} = e^{-2.16} \approx 0.115$. Without time dilation it would be $e^{-33.7/2.2} \approx 2\times10^{-7}$.

@practice num
prompt: A proton beam strikes protons at rest. Find the minimum beam kinetic energy for $p + p \to p + p + p + \bar p$. Use $m_pc^2 = 0.938$ GeV and the invariant $s = E_{\text{tot}}^2 - (p_{\text{tot}}c)^2$, which must equal $(4m_pc^2)^2$ at threshold.
unit: GeV
answer: 5.63
tol: 1%
check: root(lambda E: (E + 0.938)**2 - (E**2 - 0.938**2) - (4*0.938)**2, 0.938, 100) - 0.938
hint: In the lab, the total energy is $E + m_pc^2$ and the total momentum is that of the beam, with $(pc)^2 = E^2 - (m_pc^2)^2$.
hint: $s = 2Em_pc^2 + 2(m_pc^2)^2$; set it equal to $16(m_pc^2)^2$, solve for $E$, then subtract $m_pc^2$.
solution: $2E m + 2m^2 = 16m^2$ gives $E = 7m$, so $K = E - m = 6m_pc^2 = 6(0.938) \approx 5.63$ GeV.

@quiz num
prompt: Uranium-238 decays by alpha emission: $^{238}\text{U} \to {}^{234}\text{Th} + {}^{4}\text{He}$. Atomic masses: $238.050788$ u, $234.043601$ u and $4.002603$ u. How much energy is released? Use $1\ \text{u} = 931.494$ MeV$/c^2$.
unit: MeV
answer: 4.27
tol: 1%
check: (238.050788 - 234.043601 - 4.002603)*931.494
hint: Subtract the final masses from the initial mass.
hint: The mass difference is a few thousandths of a u.
solution: $\Delta m = 238.050788 - 234.043601 - 4.002603 = 0.004584$ u, so $Q = 0.004584 \times 931.494 \approx 4.27$ MeV.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which quantities are conserved in the muon decay $\mu^- \to e^- + \bar\nu_e + \nu_\mu$? Select all that apply.
choice*: Electric charge.
choice*: Total lepton number.
choice: The sum of the rest masses of the particles.
choice*: Electron lepton number.
hint: Count lepton number with $+1$ for leptons and $-1$ for antileptons, and electron number for $e^-$ and $\nu_e$ only.
solution: Charge goes from $-1$ to $-1$. Total lepton number goes from $+1$ to $+1 - 1 + 1$. Electron lepton number goes from $0$ to $+1 - 1$. The rest masses are not conserved: the muon ($105.7$ MeV$/c^2$) is far heavier than the products, and the difference becomes kinetic energy.
