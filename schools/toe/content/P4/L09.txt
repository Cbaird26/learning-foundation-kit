@article
A hot, thin gas emits light only at particular wavelengths, an **emission line spectrum**. White light passed through a cool gas loses exactly the same wavelengths, an **absorption spectrum**. Each element has its own set of lines, a fingerprint used to identify atoms in laboratories and in stars.

### What classical physics predicts

A classical electron orbiting a nucleus is accelerating, so it should radiate at its orbital frequency, lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in roughly $10^{-11}$ s. The emitted light would be a continuous smear as the frequency drifts, and atoms would not be stable. Neither prediction is seen.

### The hydrogen pattern

Hydrogen wavelengths follow the **Rydberg formula**, where $R$ is the **Rydberg constant**:

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right),\qquad n_i > n_f,\qquad R = 1.097\times10^{7}\ \text{m}^{-1}.$$

The final level $n_f$ labels a series: $n_f = 1$ is the **Lyman series** (ultraviolet), $n_f = 2$ the **Balmer series** (mostly visible) and $n_f = 3$ the **Paschen series** (infrared). The red line of the Balmer series, from $n_i = 3$, has $\lambda = 656$ nm.

### The Bohr model: quantized orbits

The Bohr model keeps the classical picture of a circular orbit but adds three rules:

1. Only orbits with angular momentum $L = n\hbar$ ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$) are allowed.
2. An electron in an allowed orbit does not radiate.
3. Light is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps between allowed orbits, with photon energy $hf = |E_i - E_f|$.

Combining the first rule with the Coulomb force gives, for a single electron around a nucleus of charge $Ze$,

$$r_n = \frac{n^2 a_0}{Z},\quad a_0 = 0.0529\ \text{nm},\qquad E_n = -\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2}Z^2 .$$

Here $a_0$ is the **Bohr radius**, the radius of the $n = 1$ orbit in hydrogen. The negative sign means the electron is bound. The ground state is $n = 1$; the energy needed to ionize from level $n$ is $13.6Z^2/n^2$ eV. The photon energy for a jump is

$$E_\gamma = 13.6\,Z^2\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)\text{eV},\qquad \lambda = \frac{1240\ \text{eV nm}}{E_\gamma}.$$

This reproduces the Rydberg formula, with the Rydberg constant $R = 13.6\ \text{eV}/(hc) = 1.097\times10^{7}$ m$^{-1}$.

### What the model gets right and wrong

The Bohr model gets hydrogen's energy levels and the series right, and it works for one-electron ions such as He$^+$ and Li$^{2+}$. It fails for atoms with several electrons, it predicts the wrong angular momentum for the ground state (the full quantum theory gives $L = 0$), and it cannot explain line intensities or fine structure. Treat it as a bridge to the quantum-mechanical models of next week.

### Calculation pattern

1. Choose $n_i$, $n_f$ and $Z$.
2. Find $E_\gamma = 13.6\,Z^2(1/n_f^2 - 1/n_i^2)$ eV.
3. Convert with $\lambda = 1240/E_\gamma$ nm, or use the Rydberg formula directly.

@example
title: The first ultraviolet (Lyman) line of hydrogen
problem: Find the wavelength and photon energy of the line from $n_i = 2$ to $n_f = 1$ in hydrogen.
step: Rydberg formula: $1/\lambda = R(1 - 1/4) = (1.097\times10^{7})(0.75) = 8.23\times10^{6}$ m$^{-1}$.
step: Invert: $\lambda = 1.215\times10^{-7}$ m $= 121.5$ nm, which is in the ultraviolet.
step: Photon energy: $E_\gamma = 13.6(1 - 1/4) = 10.2$ eV.
step: Check: $1240/10.2 \approx 121.6$ nm, in agreement.

@example
title: An absorption transition
problem: What photon energy and wavelength lift a hydrogen atom from $n = 2$ to $n = 5$?
step: Absorption needs the energy difference: $E_\gamma = 13.6(1/2^2 - 1/5^2)$ eV.
step: $1/4 - 1/25 = 0.25 - 0.04 = 0.21$, so $E_\gamma = 13.6 \times 0.21 = 2.86$ eV.
step: Wavelength: $\lambda = 1240/2.86 \approx 434$ nm, a violet line of the Balmer series.
step: The atom absorbs only this wavelength (and the other lines of the series), which is why absorption lines appear at the same wavelengths as emission lines.

@mistakes
- Putting $n_i$ and $n_f$ the wrong way round, so that $1/\lambda$ comes out negative. Always take $n_i > n_f$ for emission.
- Forgetting the factor $Z^2$ for hydrogen-like ions such as He$^+$ and Li$^{2+}$.
- Saying the visible (Balmer) series ends on $n_f = 1$. The visible lines end on $n_f = 2$.
- Taking the photon energy to be the energy of one level instead of the difference between two levels.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these are assumptions of the Bohr (quantized-orbit) model of hydrogen? Select all that apply.
choice*: Only orbits with angular momentum $L = n\hbar$ are allowed.
choice*: An electron in an allowed orbit does not radiate.
choice*: A photon is emitted or absorbed when the electron jumps between allowed orbits, with $hf$ equal to the energy difference.
choice: The electron's energy varies continuously with the orbit radius.
hint: The model adds rules to the classical orbit picture; one of the choices is exactly what the added rules forbid.
solution: The statements about $L = n\hbar$, about no radiation in an allowed orbit, and about the photon at a jump are the model's rules. The statement that the energy varies continuously with the orbit radius is the classical expectation; the model replaces it with discrete allowed energies $E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV.

@quiz num
prompt: The Balmer series ($n_f = 2$) converges to a short-wavelength limit as $n_i \to \infty$. Use $R = 1.097\times10^{7}$ m$^{-1}$ to find that limit wavelength.
unit: nm
answer: 365
tol: 1%
check: 1/(1.097e7*(1/2**2 - 1/1e6**2))*1e9
hint: As $n_i \to \infty$, $1/n_i^2 \to 0$.
hint: $1/\lambda = R/4$.
solution: $\lambda = 4/R = 4/(1.097\times10^{7}) = 3.65\times10^{-7}$ m $= 365$ nm.

@quiz num
prompt: A lithium ion Li$^{2+}$ (one electron, $Z = 3$) makes the transition $n = 2 \to 1$. What is the photon energy? Use $E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ eV.
unit: eV
answer: 91.8
tol: 1%
check: 13.6*3**2*(1 - 1/2**2)
hint: The energy levels scale with $Z^2$.
hint: $E_\gamma = 13.6 \times 9 \times (1/1^2 - 1/2^2)$ eV.
solution: $E_\gamma = 13.6(9)(1 - 1/4) = 91.8$ eV, which is $9$ times the corresponding hydrogen line ($10.2$ eV).
