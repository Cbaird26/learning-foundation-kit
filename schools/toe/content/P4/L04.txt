@article
Momentum conservation should hold in every inertial frame. If you use $p = mv$ together with the relativistic velocity rule, a collision that conserves momentum in one frame fails to conserve it in another. The cure is to redefine momentum so the conservation law survives the Lorentz transformation.

### Definitions

$$\vec p = \gamma\, m\vec v,\qquad E = \gamma\, m c^2,\qquad K = E - mc^2 = (\gamma - 1)mc^2 .$$

Here $m$ is the **invariant (rest) mass**; the quantity $mc^2$ is the **rest energy**, so at rest $E = mc^2$, Einstein's mass–energy relation. For $v \ll c$, $\gamma \approx 1 + v^2/2c^2$, so $p \approx mv$ and $K \approx \tfrac12 mv^2$. The nonrelativistic kinetic energy is low by under $1\%$ at $v = 0.1c$ but by about $19\%$ at $v = 0.5c$.

### Energy and momentum together

Eliminating the speed gives the key relation

$$E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2,\qquad \beta = \frac{pc}{E}.$$

A massless particle has $E = pc$ and travels at $c$. It is convenient to quote energies in eV and momenta in eV$/c$, with masses in eV$/c^2$. The electron has $mc^2 = 0.511$ MeV and the proton $mc^2 \approx 938$ MeV. Given kinetic energy, $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2}$.

### An invariant from energy and momentum

The pair $(E/c,\ p_x)$ transforms between frames exactly like $(ct,\ x)$:

$$E' = \gamma\,(E - \beta\,p_x c),\qquad p_x' c = \gamma\,(p_x c - \beta E).$$

So $E^2 - (pc)^2$ is unchanged by a boost, by the same algebra that made $\Delta s^2$ invariant, and it equals $(mc^2)^2$. Energy and momentum are frame dependent individually; this combination is not. You can test that numerically:

```python
import numpy as np
mc2, pc, beta = 0.511, 1.000, 0.6      # MeV, MeV, dimensionless
E = np.sqrt(pc**2 + mc2**2)
g = 1/np.sqrt(1 - beta**2)
E2  = g*(E - beta*pc)
pc2 = g*(pc - beta*E)
print(E**2 - pc**2, E2**2 - pc2**2, mc2**2)
```

All three numbers agree at $0.2611\ \text{MeV}^2$, up to rounding.

### Conservation and mass

In an isolated system, total energy and total momentum (a vector) are conserved in every frame. Rest mass is **not** conserved: rest energy converts into kinetic energy in decays and back in collisions. For a system, the invariant mass is defined by $M^2c^4 = E_{\text{tot}}^2 - (p_{\text{tot}}c)^2$.

### Speed is a poor guide near $c$

Raising an electron's kinetic energy from 1 MeV to 2 MeV raises its speed only from about $0.941c$ to $0.979c$. Most of the added energy goes into $\gamma$, not into $v$. This is why energy and momentum, not speed, are the natural variables.

@example
title: Energy and momentum of a fast proton
problem: A proton ($mc^2 = 938$ MeV) moves at $v = 0.8c$. Find $E$, $K$, $pc$, and verify $E^2 - (pc)^2 = (mc^2)^2$.
step: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 5/3$.
step: $E = \gamma mc^2 = (5/3)(938) \approx 1563$ MeV, so $K = E - mc^2 = (2/3)(938) \approx 625$ MeV.
step: $pc = \gamma\beta\, mc^2 = (5/3)(0.8)(938) = (4/3)(938) \approx 1251$ MeV.
step: Check: $E^2 - (pc)^2 = (938)^2\,[(5/3)^2 - (4/3)^2] = (938)^2\,(25/9 - 16/9) = (938)^2$. The invariant gives back the rest energy.

@example
title: Speed and momentum from kinetic energy
problem: An electron ($mc^2 = 0.511$ MeV) has kinetic energy $K = 1.022$ MeV. Find its $\gamma$, its speed and its $pc$.
step: $K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$ gives $\gamma = 1 + K/mc^2 = 1 + 2 = 3$.
step: $\beta = \sqrt{1 - 1/\gamma^2} = \sqrt{1 - 1/9} = \sqrt{8}/3 \approx 0.943$.
step: $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2} = \sqrt{1.0445 + 1.0445} \approx 1.445$ MeV.
step: Check with $pc = \gamma\beta\,mc^2 = 3(0.9428)(0.511) \approx 1.445$ MeV.

@mistakes
- Using $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ or $p = mv$ at speeds above a few tenths of $c$.
- Equating total energy $E$ with kinetic energy $K$; the rest energy $mc^2$ is part of $E$.
- Adding $pc$ and $mc^2$ directly instead of adding their squares: $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$.
- Taking the invariant mass of a system as the sum of the parts' masses. It is computed from the total $E$ and total $p$.

@practice num

unit: MeV$^2$
answer: 0.2611
tol: 1%
check: (lambda g, b, E, pc: (g*(E - b*pc))**2 - (g*(pc - b*E))**2)(1.25, 0.6, math.sqrt(1.0**2 + 0.511**2), 1.0)
hint: First find $E$ in $S$ from $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$.
hint: $E \approx 1.1230$ MeV. Transform both $E$ and $pc$, then square and subtract.
solution: $E = 1.1230$ MeV. Then $E' = 1.25(1.1230 - 0.6) = 0.6538$ MeV and $p'c = 1.25(1 - 0.6738) = 0.4078$ MeV. So $E'^2 - (p'c)^2 \approx 0.4274 - 0.1663 = 0.2611$ MeV$^2$, which equals $(0.511)^2$. The quantity is invariant.
