@article
Week 1 showed that $t' = t$ cannot survive a constant speed of light. The **Lorentz transformation** is the replacement, and it reduces to the Galilean one at low speed.

### Standard configuration and conventions

Frame $S'$ moves with constant velocity $v$ along $+x$ relative to frame $S$. The axes are parallel and the origins coincide at $t = t' = 0$. Define the speed ratio $\beta$ and the **Lorentz factor** $\gamma$:

$$\beta = \frac{v}{c},\qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} .$$

It is convenient to use $ct$ (in metres) as the time coordinate, so all four coordinates have the same unit. You can also work in units with $c = 1$, where time is in years and distance in light-years.

### The transformation

$$ct' = \gamma\,(ct - \beta x),\qquad x' = \gamma\,(x - \beta\, ct),\qquad y' = y,\qquad z' = z .$$

The inverse transformation follows from a symmetry argument: $S$ moves at velocity $-v$ relative to $S'$, so swap primes and flip the sign of $\beta$:

$$ct = \gamma\,(ct' + \beta x'),\qquad x = \gamma\,(x' + \beta\, ct') .$$

Check the low-speed limit: for $\beta \ll 1$, $\gamma \to 1$ and you recover $x' = x - vt$, $t' = t$.

### Three consequences

Each follows by choosing which differences vanish.

- **Relativity of simultaneity.** Two events with $\Delta t = 0$ but separated by $\Delta x$ have $c\,\Delta t' = -\gamma\beta\,\Delta x \neq 0$. Events simultaneous in $S$ are not simultaneous in $S'$.
- **Time dilation.** A clock at rest in $S'$ has $\Delta x' = 0$ between ticks, so $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t'$.
- **Length contraction.** A rod of rest length $L_0$ lies along $x'$ in $S'$. Mark its two ends at the same time in $S$ ($\Delta t = 0$): then $\Delta x' = \gamma\,\Delta x$, so $L = L_0/\gamma$. Only lengths along the motion shrink; $y$ and $z$ are unchanged.

### Velocity addition

An object moves at speed $u'$ along $x'$ in $S'$. Its speed in $S$ is

$$u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + u'v/c^2}.$$

For $u', v \ll c$ this reduces to $u \approx u' + v$. If $u' = c$ then $u = c$ for every $v$, and if $u'$ and $v$ are both below $c$ then $u$ is below $c$. A neat way to see this: velocities do not add, but a quantity called rapidity ($\tanh\theta = \beta$) does.

### Convention checklist

Whenever you transform an event, write down which frame is $S$ and which is $S'$, the direction of $+x$, the sign of $v$, and the unit of time (seconds, or $ct$ in metres, or years with $c = 1$). Most errors come from one of these being left implicit.

@example
title: Transforming one event
problem: Frame $S'$ moves at $v = 0.6c$ along $+x$ relative to $S$. An event has $(ct, x) = (5, 4)$ m in $S$. Find $(ct', x')$ and verify with the inverse transformation.
step: $\beta = 0.6$ and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 1.25$.
step: $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x) = 1.25\,(5 - 0.6 \cdot 4) = 1.25 \cdot 2.6 = 3.25$ m.
step: $x' = \gamma(x - \beta\, ct) = 1.25\,(4 - 0.6 \cdot 5) = 1.25 \cdot 1 = 1.25$ m.
step: Inverse check: $ct = 1.25\,(3.25 + 0.6 \cdot 1.25) = 1.25 \cdot 4.0 = 5$ m and $x = 1.25\,(1.25 + 0.6 \cdot 3.25) = 1.25 \cdot 3.2 = 4$ m. Both match the original event.

@example
title: Adding two relativistic velocities
problem: A rocket moves at $0.8c$ relative to Earth along $+x$. It fires a missile forward at $0.6c$ relative to the rocket. What is the missile's speed relative to Earth?
step: Take Earth as $S$ and the rocket as $S'$, so $v = 0.8c$ and $u' = 0.6c$.
step: Apply the rule: $u/c = (0.6 + 0.8)/(1 + 0.6 \cdot 0.8) = 1.4/1.48$.
step: Simplify: $1.4/1.48 = 35/37 \approx 0.946$, so $u \approx 0.946c$.
step: Sanity check: the Galilean sum $1.4c$ is impossible; the relativistic result stays below $c$.

@mistakes
- Putting $\gamma$ on only one of the two terms, or dropping it entirely. Either way $c^2t^2-x^2$ is no longer invariant.
- Inverting the transformation by swapping primes but not flipping the sign of $\beta$.
- Mixing time units: subtracting $\beta x$ from $t$ in seconds instead of from $ct$ in metres.
- Contracting a transverse length. Only the component along the relative motion changes.

answer: (-2.5, 17.5, 3, -2)
check: (lambda g, b: (g*(10 - b*20), g*(20 - b*10), 3, -2))(1/math.sqrt(1 - 0.6**2), 0.6)
hint: Use $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$ and $x' = \gamma(x - \beta\,ct)$.
hint: Note that $y$ and $z$ do not change. Be careful with the sign of $ct'$: $ct - \beta x = 10 - 12$.
solution: $ct' = 1.25\,(10 - 12) = -2.5$ m and $x' = 1.25\,(20 - 6) = 17.5$ m, with $y' = 3$ m and $z' = -2$ m. The negative $ct'$ means the event occurs before the common origin time in $S'$.

@practice num
prompt: Rocket A moves at $0.5c$ relative to Earth along $+x$ and fires a probe forward at $0.5c$ relative to the rocket. What is the probe's speed relative to Earth, as a fraction of $c$?
answer: 4/5
check: (lambda g, b: (lambda ct, x: x/ct)(g*(1 + b*0.5), g*(0.5 + b*1)))(1/math.sqrt(1 - 0.5**2), 0.5)
hint: Use $u = (u' + v)/(1 + u'v/c^2)$ with $u'/c = v/c = 0.5$.
hint: The numerator is $1.0$ and the denominator is $1 + 0.25$.
solution: $u/c = (0.5 + 0.5)/(1 + 0.25) = 1/1.25 = 4/5$. The Galilean answer $c$ would be wrong.

vars: xp, tp, v
lead: $x =$

@quiz num
prompt: Frame $S'$ moves at $v = 5c/13$ along $+x$ relative to $S$. An event has $ct = 13$ m and $x = 26$ m in $S$. Find $ct'$ in $S'$.
unit: m
answer: 3.25
check: (lambda b: (1/math.sqrt(1 - b*b))*(13 - b*26))(5/13)
hint: First find $\gamma$: $1 - (5/13)^2 = 144/169$.
hint: $\gamma = 13/12$, and $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$ with $\beta x = 10$ m.
solution: $\gamma = 13/12$ and $\beta x = (5/13)(26) = 10$, so $ct' = (13/12)(13 - 10) = 13/4 = 3.25$ m.

@quiz num
prompt: Two rockets approach each other head-on, each moving at $0.6c$ relative to Earth. What is the speed of one rocket as measured from the other, as a fraction of $c$?
answer: 15/17
check: math.tanh(2*math.atanh(0.6))
hint: Take the first rocket as $S'$ moving at $v = 0.6c$ relative to Earth ($S$); the second moves at $-0.6c$ in $S$.
hint: Transform the speed $0.6c$ into the first rocket's frame using $u' = (u - v)/(1 - uv/c^2)$ with $u = -0.6c$; take the magnitude.
solution: $u'/c = (-0.6 - 0.6)/(1 - (-0.6)(0.6)) = -1.2/1.36$, so the magnitude is $1.2/1.36 = 15/17 \approx 0.882$. This is below $c$, unlike the Galilean $1.2c$.
