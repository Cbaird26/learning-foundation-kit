@article
A pure sine wave has one definite wavelength but extends over all space. A short pulse is localized but is built from many wavelengths. Fourier analysis makes this precise: the spread in position, $\Delta x$, and the spread in wavenumber, $\Delta k$, obey $\Delta x\,\Delta k \ge \tfrac12$. For a matter wave $p = \hbar k$, where $\hbar = h/2\pi$ is the reduced Planck constant, so

$$\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

This is the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle**. Here $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p$ are the spreads (standard deviations) of the state's position and momentum. This is a statement about the **state itself**, not only about clumsy instruments: no state, however carefully prepared, has both a sharp position and a sharp momentum.

Useful values: $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s $= 6.582\times10^{-16}$ eV s, and $\hbar c = 197.3$ MeV fm $= 197.3$ eV nm.

### Energy and time

An excited atomic state that lasts a time $\Delta t$ (its lifetime) cannot have a perfectly sharp energy. The same wave argument, applied to a pulse of duration $\Delta t$, gives

$$\Delta E \gtrsim \frac{\hbar}{2\,\Delta t}.$$

This is the origin of the **natural linewidth** of spectral lines. Here $\Delta t$ is a duration, not the spread of a measured quantity, so treat this as an order-of-magnitude statement about the frequency spread of a finite pulse.

### The localization energy scale

Confine a particle to a region of size $\Delta x$. Then $\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar/(2\Delta x)$, and the kinetic energy is at least of order

$$K \sim \frac{(\Delta p)^2}{2m} = \frac{\hbar^2}{8m\,(\Delta x)^2}.$$

Squeezing the region by a factor of $3$ raises the minimum kinetic energy by a factor of $9$. Two cases set the scale of modern physics:

- **Electron in an atom**, $\Delta x \approx 0.1$ nm: $K \sim 1$ eV, which is the right order of magnitude for atomic binding energies.
- **Electron in a nucleus**, $\Delta x \approx 5$ fm: $\Delta p\,c \gtrsim \hbar c/(2\Delta x) \approx 20$ MeV, much larger than $mc^2 = 0.511$ MeV, so the electron would be ultra-relativistic with $E \approx pc$. Electrons emitted in nuclear decays have energies of at most a few MeV, so electrons cannot be confined inside nuclei.

For a nucleon ($mc^2 \approx 938$ MeV) in the same region, $pc \gtrsim 20$ MeV is far below $mc^2$, so $K \approx (pc)^2/2mc^2 \sim 0.2$ MeV or more. Nuclear energy scales are of the order of an MeV, about a million times atomic ones: the sizes are about $10^5$ times smaller, which raises $1/L^2$ by $10^{10}$, and the nucleon is about 2000 times heavier than the electron.

### What it does not say

It does not say that position cannot be measured precisely; it says that a state with a small $\Delta x$ has a large $\Delta p$. Factors of $2$ depend on how $\Delta x$ is defined, so quote the convention you use and treat results as estimates.

### Estimation recipe

1. Choose $\Delta x$ from the size of the region.
2. Compute $\Delta p \ge \hbar/(2\Delta x)$.
3. Convert to energy: $K = (\Delta p)^2/2m$ if $pc \ll mc^2$, otherwise $E \approx pc$.
4. Compare with the known energy scale of the system.

@example
title: Localization energy of an electron in an atom
problem: Estimate the minimum kinetic energy of an electron confined to $\Delta x = 0.050$ nm. Use $\hbar = 1.055\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J.
step: Minimum momentum spread: $\Delta p = \hbar/(2\Delta x) = 1.055\times10^{-34}/(2 \times 5.0\times10^{-11}) = 1.055\times10^{-24}$ kg m/s.
step: Kinetic energy: $K \approx (\Delta p)^2/2m = (1.113\times10^{-48})/(1.822\times10^{-30}) = 6.11\times10^{-19}$ J.
step: Convert: $K \approx 6.11\times10^{-19}/1.602\times10^{-19} \approx 3.8$ eV.
step: This is an eV-scale energy, the right scale for atoms. Halving $\Delta x$ would quadruple it.

@example
title: A nucleon in a nucleus
problem: A proton ($mc^2 = 938$ MeV) is confined to a nucleus of radius $\Delta x = 5.0$ fm. Estimate its minimum kinetic energy using $\hbar c = 197.3$ MeV fm.
step: $\Delta p\,c = \hbar c/(2\Delta x) = 197.3/(2 \times 5.0) \approx 19.7$ MeV.
step: Since $19.7 \ll 938$, the nonrelativistic formula is adequate: $K \approx (\Delta p\,c)^2/(2mc^2)$.
step: $K \approx (19.7)^2/(2 \times 938) = 388.1/1876 \approx 0.21$ MeV.
step: This is a lower bound; it shows that nuclear energies are of the order of MeV, a million times atomic energies, as the size is about $10^5$ times smaller and the mass about 2000 times larger.

@mistakes
- Treating the relation as only a limit on measurement apparatus, rather than a property of the state.
- Using $\Delta p = \hbar/\Delta x$ in one step and $\hbar/(2\Delta x)$ in another; state which convention you use and note that the answer is an estimate.
- Applying $K = p^2/2m$ when $pc$ is comparable to or larger than $mc^2$, as for an electron in a nucleus.
- Mixing units: $\hbar$ in J s with lengths in fm. For nuclear problems use $\hbar c = 197.3$ MeV fm.

@practice self
prompt: A classmate says: "The uncertainty principle just means that when you measure the position of an electron you disturb it, so you can never know its momentum afterwards." Explain why this is incomplete, and use a localized wave packet to explain why a small $\Delta x$ forces a large $\Delta p$.
solution: The disturbance picture suggests the electron had a sharp position and momentum beforehand and the measurement spoiled it. In fact the relation holds for the state itself, even before any measurement. A localized wave packet is a sum of waves with a range of wavelengths. If the sum has to cancel everywhere outside a small region of width $\Delta x$, it needs a wide spread of wavenumbers $\Delta k \gtrsim 1/(2\Delta x)$. With $p = \hbar k$ this means $\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar/(2\Delta x)$. Narrowing the packet in space necessarily broadens it in momentum, just as a short sound pulse needs a wide band of frequencies.
rubric: States that the relation applies to the state itself, not only to measurement disturbance.
rubric: Describes a wave packet as a superposition of different wavelengths and says that a narrower packet needs a wider range.
rubric: Connects wavelength to momentum with $p = \hbar k$ (or $p = h/\lambda$) to reach $\Delta p \gtrsim \hbar/(2\Delta x)$.
hint: Think about how a short pulse is built from sine waves.
hint: Use $p = h/\lambda$ to translate a spread of wavelengths into a spread of momenta.

@quiz num
prompt: An excited atomic state has a mean lifetime of $\Delta t = 1.6$ ns. Estimate its natural energy width using $\Delta E \approx \hbar/(2\Delta t)$ with $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-16}$ eV s.
unit: eV
answer: 2.06e-7
tol: 1%
check: 6.582e-16/(2*1.6e-9)
hint: Use the energy-time relation with the lifetime as $\Delta t$.
hint: $\Delta E = 6.582\times10^{-16}/(3.2\times10^{-9})$ eV.
solution: $\Delta E \approx 6.582\times10^{-16}/(2 \times 1.6\times10^{-9}) = 2.06\times10^{-7}$ eV. This is tiny compared with the eV-scale transition energy, which is why spectral lines are so sharp.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle confined to a region of width $2.0$ nm has a minimum kinetic energy of about $0.10$ eV from the uncertainty estimate. Using the same estimate, what is the minimum kinetic energy of the same particle when it is confined to $0.50$ nm?
unit: eV
answer: 1.6
check: 0.10*(2.0/0.50)**2
hint: How does $K \sim \hbar^2/(8m\,\Delta x^2)$ depend on $\Delta x$?

solution: $K \propto 1/\Delta x^2$, so shrinking the width by $4$ multiplies the energy by $16$: $0.10 \times 16 = 1.6$ eV.
