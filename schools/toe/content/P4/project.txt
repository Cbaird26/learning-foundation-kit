@review
### Weeks 1-4: spacetime and dynamics

- **Week 1, inertial frames.** Events, inertial frames and the two postulates. The Galilean rule $x' = x - vt$ fails for light. A light clock gives $\Delta t = \gamma\,\Delta t_0$ with the Lorentz factor $\gamma = (1 - v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}$.
- **Week 2, Lorentz transformations.** $ct' = \gamma(ct - \beta x)$ and $x' = \gamma(x - \beta\,ct)$; the inverse flips the sign of $\beta$. Consequences: simultaneity, time dilation, length contraction, and $u = (u' + v)/(1 + u'v/c^2)$.
- **Week 3, spacetime intervals.** $\Delta s^2 = (c\,\Delta t)^2 - \Delta x^2$ is invariant. Timelike, lightlike and spacelike separations; proper time $\sqrt{\Delta s^2}/c$; light cones and causality.
- **Week 4, momentum and energy.** $p = \gamma mv$, $E = \gamma mc^2$, $K = (\gamma - 1)mc^2$, $E^2 = (pc)^2 + (mc^2)^2$ and $pc = \sqrt{K^2 + 2K\,mc^2}$. The combination $E^2 - (pc)^2$ is invariant.

### Weeks 5-8: the quantum evidence

- **Week 5, blackbody radiation.** Wien's displacement law $\lambda_{\max}T = 2.898\times10^{-3}$ m K and the Stefan–Boltzmann law $P = \sigma AT^4$. Equipartition (the Rayleigh–Jeans law) causes the ultraviolet catastrophe; Planck's quanta $hf$ give $\langle E\rangle = hf/(e^{hf/k_BT} - 1)$.
- **Week 6, photoelectric effect.** Einstein's equation $K_{\max} = hf - \phi$. Threshold frequency, intensity-independent $K_{\max}$ and no delay contradict the wave model; the $V_0$-$f$ slope is $h/e$.
- **Week 7, matter waves.** The de Broglie wavelength $\lambda = h/p$, the Bragg condition $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$, and $\lambda \approx 1.226/\sqrt{V}$ nm for electrons. Use relativistic momentum at high energy.
- **Week 8, uncertainty.** The Heisenberg relation $\Delta x\,\Delta p \ge \hbar/2$, $K \sim \hbar^2/(8m\,\Delta x^2)$ and $\Delta E \gtrsim \hbar/(2\Delta t)$.

### Weeks 9-12: atoms, nuclei and particles

- **Week 9, atomic spectra.** The Rydberg formula, $E_n = -13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ eV and the three rules of the Bohr model.
- **Week 10, elementary quantum models.** Box $E_n \propto n^2$, oscillator $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$, hydrogen $E_n \propto -1/n^2$; each spectrum reflects its potential.
- **Week 11, nuclear structure.** $R = r_0A^{1/3}$, nearly constant density, binding energy from the mass defect and the shape of the $B/A$ curve.
- **Week 12, particle overview.** Quarks, leptons and force carriers; conservation laws; $Q$-values, the decay law and thresholds from invariants.

### Most common errors

- Mixed units: $t$ in seconds with $x$ in metres, eV with joules, $\hbar$ with $\hbar c$.
- A missing $\gamma$ or a wrong sign in the inverse Lorentz transformation.
- Using $p = mv$ or $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$ near $c$, or $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ for MeV electrons.
- Reversing proper time and dilated time. Proper time is the shortest.
- Letting intensity decide the photoelectron energy; frequency does.
- Treating a photon energy as a level energy (it is a difference), or starting box levels at $n = 0$.
- Checking only the charge in a reaction.

prompt: A neutral pion (rest energy $135$ MeV) moves with total energy $700$ MeV and decays into two photons, one emitted exactly forward and one exactly backward along its direction of motion. Find the energy of the backward photon in the lab frame.
unit: MeV
answer: 6.57
tol: 1%
check: (lambda g, b: g*(1 - b)*135/2)(700/135, math.sqrt(1 - (135/700)**2))
hint: In the pion's rest frame the two photons have equal and opposite momenta, so each carries half of the rest energy.
hint: Boost the backward photon into the lab frame: its energy becomes $\gamma(1 - \beta)\,(mc^2/2)$ with $\gamma = E/mc^2$ and $\beta = pc/E$.
solution: In the rest frame each photon has $67.5$ MeV. In the lab, $\gamma = 700/135 = 5.185$ and $pc = \sqrt{700^2 - 135^2} = 686.9$ MeV, so $\beta = 0.9812$. The backward photon has $E = \gamma(1 - \beta)(67.5) = (E_\pi - pc)/2 = 6.57$ MeV, and the forward photon has $693.4$ MeV. Check: the two energies sum to $700$ MeV, and their product is $(135/2)^2$, as the invariant requires.

@exam self
prompt: An electron is confined (i) in a one-dimensional box of width $0.20$ nm, about the size of an atom, and (ii) in a region of width $10$ fm, about the size of a nucleus. Take $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J, $hc = 1240$ eV nm, $\hbar c = 197.3$ MeV fm and $m_ec^2 = 0.511$ MeV. For (i), find the ground-state energy and the wavelength of the photon emitted in the $n = 2 \to 1$ transition. For (ii), use the Heisenberg uncertainty relation to estimate the minimum $pc$. In each case decide whether a nonrelativistic treatment is justified, and say what the two results tell you about whether electrons can be constituents of nuclei.
solution: (i) $E_1 = h^2/(8mL^2) = (6.626\times10^{-34})^2/[8(9.109\times10^{-31})(0.20\times10^{-9})^2] = 1.51\times10^{-18}$ J $= 9.4$ eV. The $2 \to 1$ photon has $\Delta E = 3E_1 = 28.2$ eV, so $\lambda = 1240/28.2 \approx 44$ nm, in the extreme ultraviolet. Since $E_1/mc^2 \sim 2\times10^{-5}$, the nonrelativistic treatment is justified. (ii) $\Delta p\,c \gtrsim \hbar c/(2\Delta x) = 197.3/(2 \times 10) \approx 9.9$ MeV, which is about $19$ times $mc^2$, so the electron would be ultra-relativistic with $E \approx pc \approx 10$ MeV and the nonrelativistic formula would fail badly. Electrons emitted in nuclear decays typically carry a few MeV or less, well below this confinement energy, and electrons do not feel the strong force that holds nucleons together, so electrons are not nuclear constituents. They are created in weak decays such as $n \to p + e^- + \bar\nu_e$.
