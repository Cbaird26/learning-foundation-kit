@lesson
title: Proposal review
summary: Write a one-page proposal for a single model change and review it against fixed criteria, so that a go or no-go decision is made before any change is added.
objective: Assemble a proposal with its aim, baseline status, one proposed change, predictions, falsification criteria, plan and risks.
objective: Judge a proposal by effect size against resolution, falsifiability, feasibility and scope.
objective: State go and no-go rules in advance for a midcourse review.
reading: Look up "structure of a research proposal" (aims, background, methods, timeline, risks) and typical reviewer criteria (significance, approach, feasibility) in a graduate-school or funding-agency applicant guide.

@article
You now have a reproduced and cross-checked baseline. Before you add anything to it, write a **proposal** for the one change you will make and have it reviewed. This week is about the proposal and its review. Next week you carry out the change.

### One page, seven parts

1. **Aim.** The question from week 1, in one sentence.
2. **Baseline status.** What you reproduced, with numbers: the value, the discrepancy, the convergence order and the independent check.
3. **The change.** Exactly one: a new term, a wider regime or a different measurement.
4. **Prediction.** The size and sign of the expected effect on the observable, estimated before computing.
5. **Falsification.** The result that would show the prediction wrong.
6. **Plan.** Runs, hours, and the weeks reserved for sensitivity analysis and writing.
7. **Risks.** What could go wrong and what you would do.

### Four review questions

- **Scope.** Is it one change, and does the answer bear on the question?
- **Resolvability.** Is the predicted effect larger than the combined numerical and measurement uncertainty? Define the **effect-to-resolution ratio** as predicted effect divided by uncertainty. Below about 3, the test cannot give a decision.
- **Falsifiability.** Is there a stated result that would count against the prediction?
- **Feasibility.** Can it be done in the time you have, from a baseline you trust?

### Effect size against resolution

A damped oscillator with quality factor $Q$ has $\omega_d = \omega_0\sqrt{1 - 1/(4Q^2)}$. Its period is longer than the undamped period by a fraction

$$\frac{T_d - T_0}{T_0} \approx \frac{1}{8Q^2}.$$

For $Q = 100$ this is $1.25\times10^{-5}$. If your timing uncertainty is 0.25%, the ratio is $0.005$, and a test of "does damping change the period?" cannot succeed. The amplitude is a much better observable: $A_n/A_0 \approx e^{-\pi n/Q}$ after $n$ periods, which for $Q = 100$ and $n = 10$ is $0.73$, a 27% drop that is easy to see. When the effect is too small, change the observable or the design, not your hopes.

### Go and no-go rules

At the midcourse review, write the rules beforehand: **go** if the baseline meets its acceptance criteria, the proposal passes all four questions, and the prediction and falsification statement are written. Otherwise **revise**. Score each question 0, 1 or 2; any 0 means revise.

@example
title: Reviewing a draft proposal
problem: A draft reads: "Aim: test whether adding linear drag changes the pendulum period. Baseline: the undamped period, reproduced to a relative difference of $10^{-7}$. Change: add linear drag with $Q = 100$. Observable: the period, timed over 10 swings to 0.25%. Success: a period shift detected at three standard deviations." Review it.
step: Check resolvability. The predicted fractional shift is $1/(8Q^2) = 1.25\times10^{-5}$ against an uncertainty of $2.5\times10^{-3}$, a ratio of $0.005$. The test cannot detect the effect.
step: Check falsifiability. The "success" criterion will almost certainly fail, whatever the physics, so the proposal can only give a null result that tells you nothing about drag.
step: Revise the observable to the amplitude after $n = 10$ periods: $A_{10}/A_0 \approx e^{-\pi \cdot 10/100} = 0.73$. With the amplitude read to 2%, the predicted drop of 27% is more than ten times the uncertainty.
step: Add a falsification statement: "if $A_{10}/A_0$ lies outside 0.69 to 0.77 (three standard errors), linear drag with $Q = 100$ is not the dominant loss."
step: Verdict: revise and resubmit. The revised version passes scope, resolvability, falsifiability and feasibility.

@mistakes
- Predicting an effect far below the resolution and calling a null result a finding.
- Writing success criteria that cannot fail.
- Proposing several changes at once, so that no single result can be attributed.
- Spending all the remaining weeks on the extension, leaving none for sensitivity analysis and writing.

@practice mc
prompt: Which sentence from a proposal is falsifiable?
choice: "We will explore the effect of damping on the oscillator."
choice*: "We expect the amplitude after 10 periods to be $0.73 \pm 0.04$ of its initial value for $Q = 100$; a value outside that range would contradict linear drag as the dominant loss."
choice: "We expect the results to be interesting and to improve our understanding."
choice: "The damping will be studied with the best available methods."
hint: A falsifiable statement names an observable and a result that would count against it.
solution: Only the sentence that predicts the amplitude after 10 periods names an observable, a predicted value with a range, and an outcome that would contradict the expectation. The others cannot be shown wrong.

@practice num
prompt: A damped oscillator has $Q = 50$. Its period exceeds the undamped period by the fraction $1/\sqrt{1 - 1/(4Q^2)} - 1$. Your period measurement has a relative uncertainty of $0.0025$. What is the effect-to-resolution ratio (fractional period shift divided by the uncertainty)? Give a value to two decimal places.
answer: 0.02
check: (1/math.sqrt(1 - 1/(4*50**2)) - 1)/0.0025
tol: 5%
hint: First compute the fractional period shift: $1/(4Q^2) = 1/10000$.
hint: The shift is about $1/(8Q^2) = 5\times10^{-5}$. Divide by $0.0025$.
solution: The fractional shift is $1/\sqrt{1 - 10^{-4}} - 1 = 5.0\times10^{-5}$. Dividing by $0.0025$ gives $0.020$. The effect is fifty times smaller than the uncertainty, so this measurement cannot detect it.

choice*: The baseline has not met its acceptance criteria.
choice*: The predicted effect is smaller than the combined uncertainty of the test.
choice*: No result is stated that would contradict the prediction.
choice: The idea is exciting and the team would like to try it.

solution: A baseline that fails its criteria, an unresolvable effect and an unfalsifiable prediction are all defects in the evidence or design. Excitement and the opinion of a colleague are not evidence.

@practice mc
prompt: A proposal allots all eight remaining weeks to the model extension and none to sensitivity analysis, null results or writing. What is the main problem?
choice: The extension should take less than eight weeks.
choice*: There is no time reserve for the later steps that make the result trustworthy and reportable, and no slack for things that go wrong.
choice: Writing can be done in a single afternoon, so no time is needed.
choice: Sensitivity analysis is only needed for experiments, not for computations.
hint: Think about what the report must contain besides the extension itself.
solution: Sensitivity analysis, tests of limits and the report all take time, and extensions rarely go exactly to plan. A plan with no reserve is infeasible even if each step looks small.

@practice self
prompt: Review this draft proposal using the four review questions, then give a verdict and the two most important changes. Draft: "Aim: explore whether the new model improves on the old one. Baseline: the old model works well. Change: modify the potential, switch to a faster integrator, and extend the amplitude range to 170 degrees. Observable: how the results look. Plan: eight weeks of runs and a quick write-up in the last week. Risks: none."
hint: Go through scope, resolvability, falsifiability and feasibility one at a time and quote the part of the draft that fails each.
solution: Model answer. Scope: three changes (potential, integrator, range) are made at once, so no result can be attributed; fail. Resolvability: no observable, effect size or uncertainty is given, so the effect-to-resolution ratio cannot be computed; fail. Falsifiability: "how the results look" cannot contradict anything; fail. Feasibility: all remaining time is spent on runs, the baseline status has no numbers (no discrepancy, convergence or independent check), and "risks: none" is not credible; fail. Verdict: revise and resubmit. Two key changes: (1) reduce to one change, for example the potential only, keeping the integrator and range fixed, and name an observable with a predicted size, sign and uncertainty, for example the period ratio at $60^\circ$ with a predicted shift and a 0.1% uncertainty; (2) state a result that would falsify the prediction, give baseline numbers, and reserve at least two weeks for sensitivity analysis and writing.
rubric: Applies all four questions and quotes the weak part of the draft for each.
rubric: Identifies that three changes at once prevent attribution, and that "how the results look" is not an observable.
rubric: Gives a clear verdict (revise) consistent with the findings.
rubric: The suggested changes are specific enough to act on, including a falsification statement and a time reserve.

@quiz num
prompt: For a damped oscillator with $Q = 40$, estimate the ratio $A_n/A_0$ of the amplitude after $n = 5$ periods to the initial amplitude, using $A_n/A_0 \approx e^{-\pi n/Q}$. Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.675
check: math.exp(-5*math.pi/(40*math.sqrt(1 - 1/(4*40**2))))
tol: 1%
hint: Substitute $n = 5$ and $Q = 40$ into the exponent.
hint: Evaluate the exponent $-\pi n/Q$ first, then take the exponential.
solution: $A_5/A_0 = e^{-0.3927} = 0.675$. After five periods the amplitude has fallen by about a third.

@quiz self
prompt: You plan to test a single model change (for example, adding a finite-size correction to a pendulum baseline). Write the go and no-go criteria that you would fix before testing it, covering the observable, the predicted effect, the resolution needed, and what each outcome would mean.
hint: Write each criterion as a sentence with a number in it, and make at least one of them able to contradict your prediction.
solution: Model answer. Observable: the small-angle period of a solid-sphere bob with $r/L = 0.1$, relative to the point-mass baseline. Predicted effect: the period is longer by a factor $\sqrt{1 + 2r^2/(5L^2)} = 1.0020$, i.e. 0.20%. Resolution needed: combined numerical and measurement uncertainty of at most 0.05%, so that the effect-to-resolution ratio is at least 4. Go: the baseline meets its criteria (relative difference below $10^{-6}$ from the exact period, observed order 3.5 to 4.5), and the uncertainty budget shows 0.05% or better. Supports the prediction: the measured shift lies within 0.20% plus or minus two combined standard uncertainties. Falsifies it: a shift that differs from 0.20% by more than three combined standard uncertainties, in which case the model change is incomplete or another effect dominates. No-go: if the achievable uncertainty is above 0.07%, redesign the measurement before running.
rubric: The observable and the predicted effect are stated with numbers.
rubric: A resolution requirement is given and linked to an effect-to-resolution ratio.
rubric: Outcomes are mapped to conclusions before any data are taken, including a result that would falsify the prediction.
rubric: A no-go condition is stated.
