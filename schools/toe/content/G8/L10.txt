@lesson
title: Null and failure results
summary: Interpret a null result by what it rules out, record in advance what would falsify your claim, test the relevant limits, and report failed attempts honestly.
objective: Interpret a null result using the sensitivity of the measurement (an uncertainty, an upper limit, a comparison with both hypotheses) instead of "no effect".
objective: Write falsification criteria before testing, and design limit tests that could break the claim.
objective: Report a failed reproduction or negative outcome with enough detail to be useful, and avoid the multiple-comparison trap.
reading: Look up "null results and publication bias", "confidence intervals and upper limits", "preregistration" and "multiple comparisons (the look-elsewhere effect)" in a statistics-for-scientists text or a research-integrity guide.

@article
A **null result** says that the data are consistent with no effect. How much that is worth depends on the **sensitivity** of the measurement: "no effect found" with an uncertainty of 5% rules out only effects above roughly 10%.

### Report what a null result rules out

Give:

- the measured value with its uncertainty, not just "not significant";
- an **upper limit**. For Gaussian uncertainty $\sigma$, a one-sided 95% limit is the measured value plus $1.645\,\sigma$;
- the value your hypothesis predicted, and how many standard deviations the data lie from it.

Compare the data with *both* hypotheses. Suppose you measure the fractional period excess of a pendulum at $30^\circ$ as $0.9\% \pm 0.5\%$. "No amplitude dependence" predicts 0 and lies $1.8\sigma$ away. The two-term series predicts $1.71\%$ and lies $1.6\sigma$ away. Both are acceptable, so the test is **inconclusive**, not a confirmation of either. To separate the two hypotheses by four standard deviations (each $2\sigma$ from a result halfway between) you would need $\sigma \le 1.71/4 = 0.43\%$.

### Falsification criteria

Write them before testing: "If the observable lies outside the interval $[a, b]$, I will conclude that the claim fails." A vague claim ("the series works well") cannot fail. A precise one can: "the series $T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16)$ is within 0.1% of the exact period for every $\theta_0 \le 45^\circ$."

### Test the limits

Probe where a claim is most likely to break:

- the **small-parameter limit** ($\theta_0 \to 0$ must give $T_0$);
- the **large-parameter limit** (as $\theta_0 \to \pi$, $T/T_0 \to \tfrac{2}{\pi}\ln\!\big(4/\cos(\theta_0/2)\big)$, which diverges);
- **symmetries** (the period must not change under $\theta_0 \to -\theta_0$);
- the **edge of the claimed range**, where a monotonically growing error is largest.

### The multiple-comparison trap

If you test many hypotheses or cut the data many ways, some tests will look significant by chance. With 20 independent tests at the 5% level, the chance of at least one false alarm is $1 - 0.95^{20} = 64\%$. Decide the number of tests in advance, or tighten the threshold (the Bonferroni correction divides the level by the number of tests).

### Reporting a failure

A report of a failed attempt gives what you tried (inputs, code version), what you obtained, what you checked (units, conventions, numerics), what remains unresolved, and its scope: "I could not reproduce X under conditions Y", not "X is wrong". Keep the failed runs in the record. A clearly documented failure helps the next person, and sometimes it is the result.

@example
title: Trying to break a claim at its boundary
problem: Claim: "the two-term series $T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16)$ is within 0.1% of the exact period for every $\theta_0 \le 45^\circ$." Design the test and judge the claim.
step: State the falsifying outcome in advance: any amplitude up to $45^\circ$ at which $|T_{\rm series} - T_{\rm exact}|/T_{\rm exact} > 0.1\%$ falsifies the claim.
step: Test the edge first, since the neglected terms grow with amplitude. At $45^\circ$, $\theta_0 = 0.7854$ rad and the series gives $T/T_0 = 1 + 0.61685/16 = 1.03855$.
step: The exact value, from $2K/\pi$ with $k = \sin 22.5^\circ$, is $T/T_0 = 1.03997$.
step: The relative error is $(1.03997 - 1.03855)/1.03997 = 0.137\%$, which exceeds $0.1\%$. The claim is falsified.
step: Report scope, not just failure: the next term $11\theta_0^4/3072$ reaches 0.1% at about $\theta_0 = 41.7^\circ$, so the series meets 0.1% up to roughly $41^\circ$.

@mistakes
- Reading "within 2 standard deviations of zero" as "there is no effect".
- Stating a claim so loosely that no result could contradict it.
- Testing only the middle of the claimed range, where the claim is most likely to hold.
- Dropping failed runs or non-converged results from the record.

@practice mc
prompt: A measurement of the fractional period excess of a pendulum at $30^\circ$ gives $0.9\% \pm 0.5\%$ (one standard deviation). "No amplitude dependence" predicts 0 and the two-term series predicts $1.71\%$. Which conclusion is best supported?
choice: There is no amplitude dependence, because the result is within $2\sigma$ of zero.
choice: The series is confirmed, because the result is within $2\sigma$ of $1.71\%$.
choice*: The data are consistent with both hypotheses, so the measurement is not sensitive enough to decide between them.
choice: The series is refuted, because $0.9 < 1.71$.
hint: Compute how many standard deviations the data lie from each prediction.
solution: The data are $1.8\sigma$ from zero and $1.6\sigma$ from $1.71\%$, so neither hypothesis is excluded. The honest statement is that the test is inconclusive at this precision.

@practice num
prompt: The two-term series predicts a fractional period excess of $\theta_0^2/16$ at $\theta_0 = 30^\circ$, and "no amplitude dependence" predicts zero. What standard uncertainty $\sigma$ (in percent) would separate these two predictions by four standard deviations? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.43
check: (math.radians(30)**2/16*100)/4
tol: 0.01
hint: The separation of the two predictions is $\theta_0^2/16$ expressed in percent.
hint: Separation divided by $\sigma$ must equal $4$.
solution: The separation is $(\pi/6)^2/16 \times 100 = 1.71\%$, so $\sigma = 1.71/4 = 0.43\%$.

@practice num
prompt: You test 20 independent hypotheses at the 5% significance level, and in every case there is no real effect (every null hypothesis is true). What is the probability of at least one false positive? Give a value to two decimal places.
answer: 0.64
check: 1 - 0.95**20
tol: 0.01
hint: First find the probability that none of the 20 tests gives a false positive.
hint: That probability is $0.95^{20}$.
solution: $0.95^{20} = 0.358$, so the chance of at least one false positive is $1 - 0.358 = 0.64$.

prompt: Select all the items that belong in an honest report of a failed reproduction.
choice*: The inputs and code version that were used.
choice*: The checks that were tried (units, conventions, numerical convergence) and their outcomes.
choice*: What remains unresolved, and the conditions under which the failure was observed.
choice: Only the runs that agree with the source, to keep the report short.
choice: A statement that the original authors made an error, even without evidence.
hint: The report should let another person repeat your attempt and understand what is and is not known.
solution: A useful failure report records inputs, versions, checks and open issues. Leaving out disagreeing runs hides information, and accusing the source without evidence goes beyond what the work shows.

@practice self
prompt: Block deliverable: record what would falsify the claim and test relevant limits. Claim: "the two-term series $T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16)$ is within 0.1% of the exact pendulum period for every amplitude up to $20^\circ$." Write the falsification criterion, then list the limit and edge tests you would run, with the expected result of each.
hint: Write the criterion as an inequality with a number in it.
hint: Include a test that is designed to fail outside the claimed range, to show that your test has the power to fail.
solution: Model answer. Falsification criterion: the claim is false if there is any amplitude $\theta_0 \le 20^\circ$ at which $|T_{\rm series} - T_{\rm exact}|/T_{\rm exact} > 10^{-3}$, where $T_{\rm exact} = 4\sqrt{L/g}\,K(\sin(\theta_0/2))$ is evaluated by two independent methods that agree to $10^{-8}$. Tests: (1) edge of the range, $20^\circ$: exact ratio $1.007669$ against series $1.007615$, relative error $5\times10^{-5}$, so the claim passes with a margin of about 20; (2) small-amplitude limit, $\theta_0 = 1^\circ$: both ratios are $1.000019$ and agree to $10^{-8}$, with a difference of order $\theta_0^4$, so it passes; (3) symmetry, $\theta_0 \to -\theta_0$: identical results; (4) a deliberate failure test beyond the range, $\theta_0 = 45^\circ$, where the error should be $0.14\%$ and the criterion should flag it, demonstrating that the test can fail; (5) a scan of the error over $0$ to $20^\circ$, which should grow like $\theta_0^4$. The result is recorded whichever way it falls.
rubric: The falsification criterion is a numerical inequality fixed before testing.
rubric: Edge, small-parameter limit and a symmetry or scaling test are included, each with an expected result.
rubric: At least one test outside the claimed range shows that the procedure can fail.
rubric: The reference used for the exact period is itself independently checked.

@quiz num
prompt: A measurement of a possible small effect gives $0.2\% \pm 0.3\%$ (one standard deviation, Gaussian). What is the one-sided 95% upper limit on the effect, in percent? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.69
check: 0.2 + special.ndtri(0.95)*0.3
tol: 0.01
hint: The one-sided 95% limit is the measured value plus $1.645$ standard deviations.
hint: Here the measured value is $0.2$ and $\sigma = 0.3$, both in percent.
solution: $0.2 + 1.645(0.3) = 0.69\%$. The data exclude effects above about $0.69\%$ at the 95% confidence level, which is a more useful statement than "no significant effect".

@quiz self
prompt: You try to reproduce a published table of pendulum periods and find that every one of your values is 3% higher than the table's, whatever the amplitude. Write the entry you would put in the failure log: what you did, what you found, the three checks you would make next (in order), and the wording of the scope statement.
hint: A constant offset at all amplitudes points away from the nonlinear physics and towards inputs or conventions.
solution: Model answer. Entry: "Reproduction of the period table (source, version and date recorded). Inputs: $L = 1.000\ \text{m}$, $g = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2$, amplitudes 10 to 90 degrees. Code version: commit identifier. Result: periods are 3.0% higher than the table at all amplitudes; the small-angle limit reproduces $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ exactly and the baseline passes its own criteria. Pattern: the offset is independent of amplitude, so it is not a nonlinear or numerical effect. Next checks: (1) the value of $g$ or $L$ used by the source: a 3% period difference corresponds to about a 6% difference in $g/L$, so check whether the source used a different $g$ or a different length, for example $L$ measured to another point of the bob; (2) the definition of the period or amplitude in the table (for example half-period or peak-to-peak amplitude); (3) the unit conversion of the time in the table. Status: unresolved. Scope: I could not reproduce the table under these inputs and conventions; I have not shown that the table is wrong."
rubric: Records inputs, versions, results and the pattern (constant offset) observed.
rubric: Next checks are ordered and target inputs, conventions and definitions before suspecting the source.
rubric: States the status as unresolved and limits the claim to the conditions tested.
rubric: Does not claim that the published table is wrong without evidence.
