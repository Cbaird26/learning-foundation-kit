@article
Your result $y = f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ depends on inputs, each known only to within an uncertainty $u_i$. **Sensitivity analysis** asks which inputs and assumptions dominate the uncertainty of $y$, and so where effort should go.

### Linear propagation

For independent inputs and small uncertainties,

$$u_y^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\right)^2 u_i^2 .$$

Each term is that input's **contribution to the variance**. Multiply the derivative by $u_i$ before comparing inputs: a large derivative on a precisely known input can matter less than a small derivative on a poorly known one.

For a power law $y = C\prod_i x_i^{p_i}$, the relative sensitivity is $\dfrac{x_i}{y}\dfrac{\partial y}{\partial x_i} = p_i$, and

$$\left(\frac{u_y}{y}\right)^2 = \sum_i p_i^2\left(\frac{u_i}{x_i}\right)^2 .$$

For $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$ the exponents are $p_L = 1$ and $p_T = -2$. A repeated variable is one input: for $y = x^3$, $u_y/y = 3\,u_x/x$, not $\sqrt{3}\,u_x/x$.

### Scans and Monte Carlo

- A **one-at-a-time scan** moves each input over its plus and minus uncertainty with the others fixed, and records the change in $y$. It is cheap and shows nonlinearity.
- A **Monte Carlo** run draws all inputs from their distributions, computes $y$ for each draw, and reports the mean and standard deviation. It handles nonlinear $f$ and checks the linear formula. Record the seed.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
L = rng.normal(1.000, 0.002, 100_000)
T = rng.normal(2.000, 0.004, 100_000)
g = 4*np.pi**2*L/T**2
print(g.mean(), g.std(ddof=1))
```

The standard deviation estimated from $N$ draws has a relative error of about $1/\sqrt{2(N-1)}$, so $10^5$ draws give a value good to about 0.2%.

### Model-form sensitivity

Assumptions have uncertainty too. If you compute $g$ from a measured pendulum period using $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, but the true period is $T = T_0(1 + \theta_0^2/16 + \dots)$, then $g$ comes out low by a fraction $1 - (T_0/T)^2$. For an amplitude of $12^\circ$ that is 0.55%, larger than the statistical contribution in the example below. Apply the correction, and propagate the uncertainty of the amplitude through it.

### What to report

Give a table of contributions: input, value, uncertainty, sensitivity, and share of the variance. Say which input to improve first, and by how much it must improve for the total to meet your goal.

@example
title: Which measurement limits g?
problem: A pendulum gives $L = 1.000 \pm 0.002\ \text{m}$ and $T = 2.000 \pm 0.004\ \text{s}$. Compute $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$ and its uncertainty, and decide which measurement to improve.
step: The value is $g = 4\pi^2(1.000)/(2.000)^2 = \pi^2 = 9.870\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: The relative sensitivities are $p_L = 1$ and $p_T = -2$.
step: The relative contributions are $(u_L/L)^2 = (0.002)^2 = 4.0\times10^{-6}$ and $(2u_T/T)^2 = (0.004)^2 = 1.6\times10^{-5}$.
step: The combined relative uncertainty is $\sqrt{2.0\times10^{-5}} = 0.0045$, so $u_g = 0.044\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $g = 9.87 \pm 0.04\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Ranking: $T$ supplies 80% of the variance and $L$ 20%. Halving $u_T$ (for example by timing more swings) reduces the total to $\sqrt{8.0\times10^{-6}} = 0.28\%$, whereas halving $u_L$ alone would only reach 0.41%.

@mistakes
- Comparing derivatives instead of derivatives multiplied by the input uncertainties.
- Treating a repeated variable ($x^3$) as three independent inputs.
- Quoting only the statistical uncertainty while a model-form effect is larger.
- Running a Monte Carlo without a seed, so the quoted number cannot be reproduced.
