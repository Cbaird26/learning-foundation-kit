@article
A **topic** is an area ("the pendulum"). A **goal** is a wish ("understand damping"). A **method** is a tool ("use Runge–Kutta"). None of these is a **research question**, because none says what result would count as an answer. This course asks you to turn one topic into one question that you can answer, with evidence, in about twelve weeks.

### Four ingredients

A usable question names:

1. a **quantity** you can measure or compute,
2. a **regime**, meaning the parameter ranges where you will look,
3. a **precision** that decides when you are done,
4. a **way of being wrong**: an outcome that would contradict your expectation.

Compare the topic "the pendulum" with this question: *for a rigid planar pendulum released from rest at amplitude $\theta_0$, at what amplitude does the exact period first exceed the small-angle period $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ by 1%, to within $0.1^\circ$?* The quantity is the amplitude where $T/T_0 = 1.01$, the regime is $0 < \theta_0 < \pi/2$, the precision is $0.1^\circ$, and the answer can be wrong: a code or an experiment could disagree with the expected value.

### Screening a candidate

Ask five things before you commit:

- **Answer shape.** Can you write down what an answer looks like (a number with an uncertainty, a curve, a bound, a yes or no with a threshold)?
- **Falsifiable.** Is there a result that would count against the expected answer?
- **Feasible.** Can you do it with the time, tools and mathematics you have? Estimate the hours.
- **Resolvable.** Is the effect bigger than your uncertainty? An effect of 0.01% cannot be seen with a 1% measurement.
- **Narrow.** If the question has several parts, cut it to one. One extra week should sharpen the answer, not change the question.

### Resolution comes first

Suppose a stopwatch timing has random scatter of $0.05\ \text{s}$, and you time $n$ swings of a pendulum whose period is near $2\ \text{s}$. The uncertainty of the period is $0.05/n$ seconds. With $n = 10$ that is $0.005\ \text{s}$, or 0.25%.

The leading amplitude correction is $\theta_0^2/16$ (with $\theta_0$ in radians). At $20^\circ$ it is 0.76%, about three times the uncertainty: visible, but not overwhelming. At $5^\circ$ it is 0.05%, far below the uncertainty, so those runs would teach you nothing. Doing this arithmetic *before* the experiment tells you which amplitudes are worth running.

### Write it as one sentence

Finish by writing the question in a single sentence, followed by one line stating the expected answer and one line stating the result that would contradict it. If you cannot write all three, you still have a topic.

@example
title: From topic to question
problem: Narrow the topic "damping of oscillators" into one question that a learner with P5 to P9 preparation could answer in twelve weeks.
step: Choose a system. A mass on a spring with a viscous drag force $-b\dot{x}$ can be solved exactly and simulated easily.
step: Choose a quantity. The quality factor $Q = \omega_0/(2\gamma)$ summarizes the damping, where $\gamma = b/(2m)$ and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$.
step: Choose a regime and a precision. Take underdamped motion with $5 \le Q \le 50$, and ask for $Q$ to 5%.
step: Add a way of being wrong. Two ways of extracting $Q$ from the same ring-down, the logarithmic decrement and a fit to the whole envelope, should agree within their uncertainties if the drag is linear. Disagreement beyond that would contradict the model.
step: Write the question: for underdamped ring-down with $5 \le Q \le 50$, do the logarithmic-decrement and envelope-fit estimates of $Q$ agree within 5%, as linear drag predicts? The answer shape is a table of two $Q$ values and their difference for each run.

@mistakes
- Writing a topic or a method ("study chaos in the double pendulum") and calling it a question.
- Choosing an effect smaller than your measurement or numerical resolution.
- Packing three questions into one sentence, so that no single result answers it.
- Choosing the precision after seeing the first results.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of the following is a narrow, answerable research question?
choice: Understand why pendulums slow down.
choice: Use numerical integration to study the pendulum.
choice*: For $0 < \theta_0 < 60^\circ$, does the fourth-order Runge–Kutta period of a frictionless pendulum agree with the elliptic-integral period to a relative difference below $10^{-6}$?
choice: Is the pendulum the best system for learning about oscillators?
hint: Look for the option that names a quantity, a range, a precision and a way to fail.
solution: Only the question comparing the Runge–Kutta period with the elliptic-integral period names a quantity (the period), a regime ($0$ to $60^\circ$), a precision ($10^{-6}$ relative) and a pass or fail outcome. The others are a goal, a method and an opinion.

@practice num
prompt: A stopwatch has random scatter of $0.05\ \text{s}$ per timing. You time 10 swings of a pendulum whose period is $2.0\ \text{s}$, so the relative uncertainty of the period is $0.05/(10 \times 2.0)$. The leading amplitude correction at $\theta_0 = 20^\circ$ is $\theta_0^2/16$ with $\theta_0$ in radians. How many times larger is this correction than the relative uncertainty of the period? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 3.0
check: (math.radians(20)**2/16)/(0.05/(10*2.0))
tol: 0.1
hint: Convert $20^\circ$ to radians first: $20\pi/180 \approx 0.349$.
hint: The correction is $0.349^2/16$. Divide it by the relative uncertainty $0.0025$.
solution: $\theta_0 = 0.3491$ rad, so $\theta_0^2/16 = 0.1219/16 = 0.00762$. Dividing by $0.0025$ gives about $3.0$. The effect is visible, but only at roughly the three-sigma level.

@practice self
prompt: Pick a topic from your own preparation (a mechanics, waves, circuits or thermodynamics system). Write one research question about it that names a quantity, a regime, a precision and a way of being wrong. Then write one sentence on the expected answer and one sentence on the result that would contradict it.
hint: Start from a system you can analyze or measure, then add the range, the precision and the failure condition one at a time.
solution: Model answer (topic: a series RC circuit). Question: for a series RC circuit with $R = 10\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $C = 100\ \text{nF}$, each measured with a multimeter to 1%, does the time constant extracted from the discharge curve agree with $RC$ within 3% for starting voltages between 1 V and 5 V? Expected answer: yes, because $RC = 1.0\ \text{ms}$ is known to about 1.4% from the two meter readings and the model is simple, provided the voltmeter or oscilloscope input impedance is much larger than $R$. Contradicting result: a time constant that differs from $RC$ by more than 3%, or one that changes systematically with starting voltage.
rubric: The question names a measurable or computable quantity and the range of conditions where it will be answered.
rubric: A numerical precision or threshold is stated before any data exist.
rubric: A specific outcome is named that would contradict the expected answer.
rubric: It is one question, answerable with tools, mathematics and time you actually have.

@practice mc
prompt: A draft says: "We will test whether the new correction is better than the old one." Which revision turns this into a question?
choice: Add the word "significantly" before "better".
choice*: Name the observable, the data and the metric, for example "the new correction gives a smaller root-mean-square residual than the old one on the period data for $\theta_0 \le 60^\circ$".
choice: Replace "test" with "investigate".
choice: Add a list of references on corrections.
hint: "Better" has no meaning until you say better at predicting what, on which data, by what measure.
solution: A comparison becomes answerable when the observable, the data and the metric are named, so that the result can show the new correction is not better. Adverbs, synonyms and references leave the question as vague as before.

@quiz num
prompt: A single timing of a pendulum period has a random relative scatter of 1.0%. You want the mean of $n$ independent timings to have a relative standard error of 0.25%. How many timings do you need?
answer: 16
check: math.ceil((1.0/0.25)**2)
hint: The standard error of the mean is the single-timing scatter divided by $\sqrt{n}$.
hint: Set $1.0/\sqrt{n} = 0.25$ and solve for $n$.
solution: $1.0/\sqrt{n} = 0.25$ gives $\sqrt{n} = 4$, so $n = 16$.

@quiz self
prompt: A classmate proposes to "study chaos in the double pendulum" as a twelve-week project. Rewrite it as one narrow question with a quantity, a regime, a precision and a way of being wrong, and name one thing you would deliberately leave out.
hint: Choose one release condition, one measurable quantity and one numerical threshold, and write what you leave out.
solution: Model answer. Question: for a frictionless planar double pendulum with equal masses and equal rod lengths, released from rest with both angles equal to $\theta$, how does the time at which two runs whose initial angles differ by $10^{-6}$ rad separate by more than $0.1$ rad depend on $\theta$ for $60^\circ \le \theta \le 150^\circ$, with that time found to 10%? The answer is a curve of separation time against $\theta$, computed with an integrator whose error is checked to stay well below the initial difference. Expected answer (a hypothesis to be tested): the separation time shortens on the whole as the release energy grows, because the nonlinearity is stronger. A curve that shows no shortening at all across the range, beyond the numerical uncertainty, would contradict that expectation. Left out: friction, driving, experiments on a real device and a full Lyapunov-exponent analysis.
rubric: The rewritten question names a computable quantity, a parameter range and a precision.
rubric: A result that would contradict the expected answer is stated.
rubric: The question is one question, and at least one tempting extension is explicitly excluded.
rubric: The numerical accuracy needed to make the result trustworthy is mentioned.
