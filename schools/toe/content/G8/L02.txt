@article
A literature map is a table, not a pile of PDFs. Its job is to tell you what is already known about your question, how well, and where the gaps are, so that your project adds something checkable instead of repeating or contradicting work you did not know about.

### Kinds of source

- A **primary source** reports original work: a research article with its own measurement, derivation or calculation, a data set, or documented code.
- A **secondary source** interprets primary work: a review article, a textbook chapter, lecture notes.
- A **tertiary source** compiles from the others: an encyclopedia entry, a table of constants, a handbook.

Use tertiary sources to orient, secondary sources to find the primary ones, and primary sources for every number or claim you rely on. If your report says "the period grows by 1.7% at $30^\circ$", you should be able to point to where that came from, or to the line of your own code that computed it.

### Chaining citations

Start from a **seed**: one good paper or review. **Backward chaining** reads its reference list to find the work it builds on. **Forward chaining** uses a citation database to list the papers that cite it, which is how you find corrections, extensions and disagreements. Do both. A forward search also shows whether the seed's result was later reproduced, corrected or retracted.

### What goes in a row

| field | question it answers |
|---|---|
| citation | exactly what is it, and which version (preprint, published, erratum)? |
| claim | what specific result might you use? |
| method | derivation, measurement or simulation, and the key assumptions |
| regime | in what parameter range does it hold? |
| uncertainty | how well is the result known? |
| limits | what did the authors not do, or what did later work change? |
| role | what will you use it for? |

The **role** field prevents hoarding. A narrow project usually needs three primary sources: one that fixes the **baseline** (the model or derivation you compare against), one that supplies **inputs or reference values** (constants, data, benchmark numbers), and one that supplies a **method or an independent check**.

### Evaluate before relying

Ask: are the assumptions stated? Are uncertainties given? Has anyone reproduced it? Is there an erratum? Fame and citation counts are not evidence. A number copied into a review is only as good as the primary source behind it, so trace it. When two sources disagree, record both and say why you believe one of them: a different regime, a different convention, or an error.

### Stopping rule

You are done mapping when new searches mostly return sources you already have. Write down the date of the search so that the map can be repeated and updated.

@example
title: Triage one quoted number
problem: A review says that a pendulum's period at amplitude $30^\circ$ is "about 1.7% longer than $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$". Decide whether to rely on it and record a map row.
step: Identify the claim and its regime: the fractional excess of the period over $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ at $\theta_0 = 30^\circ$, for a rigid planar pendulum released from rest.
step: Find the primary basis. The review quotes the series $T = T_0\left(1 + \theta_0^2/16 + \dots\right)$. It comes from expanding the exact period integral, so trace it to a derivation in a paper or text, and note what the dots hide.
step: Recompute independently. With $\theta_0 = 0.5236$ rad, $\theta_0^2/16 = 0.01713$. The next term, $11\theta_0^4/3072$, adds $0.00027$, giving about $1.74\%$. The review's 1.7% is consistent.
step: Record the row: claim "excess about 1.7% at $30^\circ$"; method "series from the exact integral"; regime "rigid planar pendulum, no friction, released from rest"; limit "the two-term value is low by about 0.03 percentage points"; role "baseline value to compare my code against".

@mistakes
- Citing a review for a number without finding the primary source behind it.
- Recording titles only, with no claim, regime or limitation, so the map cannot be used.
- Chaining from a single paper and mistaking its viewpoint for the whole field.
- Forgetting the search date and the version, so the map cannot be repeated or updated.

@practice num
prompt: A review quotes the pendulum period at $\theta_0 = 30^\circ$, and you want to check it from the two-term series $T/T_0 = 1 + \theta_0^2/16$ with $\theta_0$ in radians. What percentage excess over $T_0$ does the two-term series give? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 1.71
check: math.radians(30)**2/16*100
tol: 0.01
hint: Convert $30^\circ$ to radians: $\pi/6 \approx 0.5236$.
hint: Square it, divide by 16, and multiply by 100 to get a percentage.
solution: $(\pi/6)^2 = 0.27416$ and $0.27416/16 = 0.017135$, so the excess is $1.71\%$. The exact value is about $1.74\%$, so the two-term series is a slight underestimate.

@practice self
prompt: Block deliverable: define one narrow question and identify three primary sources. Write your question in one sentence (quantity, regime, precision, way of being wrong). Then list three primary sources you would use. If you do not yet hold a source, describe precisely what you would search for. Give each source a role: baseline, inputs or reference values, and method or independent check.
hint: Give each source a different job. Ask yourself which one could disagree with your own calculation.
solution: Model answer. Question: for a rigid planar pendulum released from rest, at what amplitude does the exact period exceed $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ by 1%, to within $0.1^\circ$, and does a fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration reproduce it? Source 1 (baseline): the primary derivation of the exact period as $4\sqrt{L/g}\,K(k)$ with $k=\sin(\theta_0/2)$, in a paper or in a text that gives it in full and cites the original; take the derivation and its assumptions. Source 2 (reference values): a standard mathematical-functions reference with numerical values of the complete elliptic integral $K$, traced to the primary table or computation it cites; use its tabulated values as benchmark numbers. Source 3 (independent check): a numerical-analysis paper, or a text traced to the paper it cites, on evaluating $K$ by the arithmetic–geometric mean and on the order of accuracy of Runge–Kutta methods; use it for the independent calculation and the convergence test.
rubric: The question has a quantity, a regime, a precision and a way of being wrong.
rubric: Three sources are named or described precisely enough that another person could find them.
rubric: Each source has a distinct role, and at least one source can serve as an independent check.
rubric: Each source is primary, or the entry says which primary source it would be traced to.

@quiz self
prompt: Here is one row of a literature map. Source: "a review of pendulum motion". Claim: "period depends on amplitude". Role: "background". Explain what is missing or weak, and rewrite the row so that it could be used to compare against a numerical code.
hint: Compare the row with the seven fields in the table and see which are empty or vague.
solution: Model answer. Weaknesses: the source is secondary and unspecified (no version or date), the claim has no number, no regime and no uncertainty, no limitation is recorded, and the role is too vague to guide use. Rewrite. Citation: the primary derivation of the exact pendulum period (version and search date recorded). Claim: for a rigid planar pendulum released from rest, $T = 4\sqrt{L/g}\,K(k)$ with $k=\sin(\theta_0/2)$, so $T/T_0 = 1.0400$ at $45^\circ$. Method: energy conservation and reduction to a complete elliptic integral. Regime: no friction, uniform $g$, point bob on a massless rigid rod. Uncertainty: exact within the model, so limited only by the evaluation of $K$. Limits: finite bob size, drag and pivot flexibility are not included. Role: baseline to compare the numerical integration against, with a stated relative tolerance.
rubric: Notes that the source is secondary or unspecified and needs a version or date.
rubric: The rewritten claim includes a number, a regime and the model's assumptions.
rubric: The rewritten role says how the source will be used, for example as a baseline with a tolerance.
rubric: States at least one limitation of the source relative to a real experiment or code.
