@article
The **baseline** is the simplest model that makes a quantitative prediction for your observable. Everything you add later is compared with it, so it must be derived cleanly, and its assumptions must be written down before you start.

### Assumptions first

For a pendulum, the baseline assumptions are:

1. a point mass $m$ on a massless rigid rod of length $L$;
2. a frictionless pivot and no air drag;
3. a uniform gravitational acceleration $g$;
4. motion in one vertical plane.

Only then derive. The angle $\theta$ from the vertical obeys $mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta$, so

$$\ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta.$$

### Small-angle baseline

For $|\theta| \ll 1$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and the equation is that of a harmonic oscillator with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$:

$$T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}.$$

### Exact baseline

Energy conservation with release from rest at $\theta_0$ gives $\tfrac12 L^2\dot\theta^2 = gL(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)$. Separate variables and substitute $\sin(\theta/2) = k\sin\varphi$ with $k = \sin(\theta_0/2)$:

$$T = 4\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}\,K(k),\qquad K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\varphi}{\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\varphi}}.$$

Here $K$ is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind. For small $\theta_0$ it gives $T = T_0\left(1 + \tfrac{1}{16}\theta_0^2 + \dots\right)$.

### Check before you trust

- **Dimensions.** $L/g$ has units $\text{m}/(\text{m/s}^2) = \text{s}^2$, so $\sqrt{L/g}$ is a time.
- **Limits.** As $\theta_0 \to 0$, $k \to 0$ and $K(0) = \pi/2$, so $T \to 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. As $\theta_0 \to \pi$, $k \to 1$ and $K \to \infty$: a pendulum released almost at the top takes a very long time to leave, as it should.
- **Numbers.** Evaluate one case and compare it with an independent estimate.

### Size of neglected effects

A baseline is useful only if you know roughly how wrong it is. A solid-sphere bob of radius $r$, with its centre a distance $L$ from the pivot, has moment of inertia $I = mL^2 + \tfrac25 mr^2$, so the small-angle period is multiplied by $\sqrt{1 + 2r^2/(5L^2)}$. For $r/L = 0.05$ the factor is $\sqrt{1.001} \approx 1.0005$, a 0.05% effect. Write each such estimate beside the assumption that causes it. Together, the assumptions, equations with units, validity range and neglected-effect sizes form your **baseline card**, which you will use next week.

@example
title: Baseline with a finite bob
problem: A solid sphere of mass $m$ and radius $r$ hangs from a massless rod so that its centre is a distance $L$ from the pivot. Derive its small-angle period and the fractional change from the point-mass baseline when $r/L = 0.1$.
step: Find the moment of inertia about the pivot with the parallel-axis theorem: $I = \tfrac25 mr^2 + mL^2$.
step: The restoring torque about the pivot is $-mgL\sin\theta \approx -mgL\theta$ for small angles, so $I\ddot\theta = -mgL\,\theta$ and $\omega_0 = \sqrt{mgL/I}$.
step: The period is $T_0' = 2\pi\sqrt{I/(mgL)} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{L}{g}\left(1 + \dfrac{2r^2}{5L^2}\right)}$.
step: Check it. The ratio $r^2/L^2$ is dimensionless, and $r \to 0$ gives the point-mass result $2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.
step: Size the effect. For $r/L = 0.1$: $2(0.01)/5 = 0.004$, and $\sqrt{1.004} = 1.0020$. The period is about 0.2% longer than the point-mass baseline.

@mistakes
- Deriving first and listing assumptions afterwards, so some of them are never noticed.
- Replacing $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$ without stating the amplitude range where that is acceptable.
- Skipping the dimension and limit checks because the algebra "looked fine".
- Quoting a baseline value without any estimate of the neglected effects.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from Newton's second law for rotation about the pivot, derive the pendulum equation of motion and the small-angle period. List the assumptions you used, then check the result by units and by one limiting case.
hint: Torque about the pivot equals moment of inertia times angular acceleration. Write the sign of the restoring torque carefully.
solution: Assumptions: point mass $m$ on a massless rigid rod of length $L$, frictionless pivot, no drag, uniform $g$, one vertical plane. The torque about the pivot is $-mgL\sin\theta$ and the moment of inertia is $mL^2$, so $mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta$, which gives $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta$. For $|\theta| \ll 1$, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, a harmonic oscillator with $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$ and $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. Units: $L/g$ is in $\text{s}^2$, so $T_0$ is in seconds. Limit: increasing $L$ at fixed $g$ lengthens the period, and the mass cancels, which agrees with the equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass in the model.

rubric: The small-angle step is stated, giving $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.
rubric: A units check and at least one limiting case or sanity check are included.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a baseline card for the time for a steel ball of radius $1.0\ \text{cm}$ to fall $2.00\ \text{m}$ from rest in air. Include the assumptions, the equation and the predicted time with units, the regime where the baseline is valid, and an order-of-magnitude estimate of the neglected air drag.
hint: Compare the drag force at the final speed with the weight of the ball to see how large the neglected effect can be.
solution: Assumptions: point-like ball, uniform $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, no air resistance, release from rest, vertical motion. Equation: $h = \tfrac12 gt^2$, so $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$. Prediction: $t = \sqrt{4.00/9.8} = 0.639\ \text{s}$. Units: $\text{m}/(\text{m/s}^2)$ gives $\text{s}^2$ under the root. Validity: heights over which the speed stays well below the terminal speed and $g$ is uniform. Neglected drag: the ball's mass is about $0.033\ \text{kg}$ (steel, density $7800\ \text{kg/m}^3$), so its weight is about $0.32\ \text{N}$. At the final speed $v \approx gt = 6.3\ \text{m/s}$ the quadratic drag $\tfrac12 C_d \rho A v^2$, with $C_d \approx 0.47$, air density $1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and $A = \pi r^2 = 3.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$, is about $3\times10^{-3}\ \text{N}$, i.e. around 1% of the weight at the end and much less on average. The fall time is therefore longer by roughly 0.1%, which is below a stopwatch's resolution but above a photogate's.
rubric: Assumptions are listed and include the neglect of air resistance.
rubric: The prediction $t = \sqrt{2h/g} \approx 0.64\ \text{s}$ is derived with units.
rubric: The regime of validity is stated.
rubric: The neglected effect is estimated to order of magnitude and compared with a measurement resolution.
