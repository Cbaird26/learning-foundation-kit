@lesson
title: External critique
summary: Invite a qualified reader to find mathematical and inference errors in your draft, triage their comments, and respond point by point with evidence.
objective: Choose a reader, define the scope of the review, and prepare a package that lets them check derivations, code and claims.
objective: Recognize common inference errors, such as neglected systematics, double use of data and post hoc cuts, and test your own draft for them.
objective: Classify each comment as an error, an ambiguity, a scope issue or a matter of taste, and respond with evidence and a recorded outcome.
reading: Look up "how reviewers evaluate a physics manuscript", "responding to referee reports point by point" and "common statistical errors in the physical sciences" (post hoc cuts, double use of data) in a research-integrity guide or journal author instructions.

@article
You cannot see your own blind spots. An **external critique** by a qualified reader, someone with the mathematics and physics to check you and no stake in your being right, is the cheapest way to catch errors before they become claims.

### Choosing and briefing the reader

- **Who.** Someone competent in the relevant mathematics, willing to be critical, and independent of your result. A reader who will only skim is no help.
- **Scope.** Say what you want checked: the derivations, the numerical claims, the uncertainty budget, the inference from data. Give a page limit and a date.
- **Package.** Send the draft, the claim audit table, the derivations, the code with its environment and the command that regenerates the figures.
- **Questions.** Ask specific ones: "Is step 3 of the derivation valid? Does the uncertainty budget omit any effect above 0.1%? Is the claim in Section 5 supported by Figure 3?" Invite the reader to try to break the result.

### Inference errors to look for

- A **neglected systematic**: quoting only the statistical uncertainty when a systematic effect is larger.
- **Double use of data**: choosing a model or a cut after seeing the data, then using the same data as evidence for it.
- **Post hoc choices** and unreported multiple comparisons.
- **Confusing** a standard deviation with the standard error of the mean, or one standard deviation with a 95% interval.
- Comparing with the **wrong reference**, such as the conventional value of $g$ instead of the local one.
- Reading "not significant" as "no effect".
- A **unit or convention mismatch** between sources.

A systematic can change the inference. Suppose a pendulum at $12^\circ$ amplitude gives $g = 9.70 \pm 0.05\ \text{m/s}^2$ when analysed with the small-angle formula, and the draft says this disagrees with $9.80665$ at $2.1\sigma$. The exact period is longer than $T_0$ by a factor $1.00275$ at that amplitude, so the corrected $g$ is $9.70 \times 1.00275^2 = 9.753$, and the discrepancy shrinks to $1.1\sigma$.

### Triage and respond

Log every comment and classify it:

1. **Error**: a mistake in the mathematics, code or inference. Fix it, rerun, and say what changed.
2. **Ambiguity**: the text is unclear. Rewrite it.
3. **Scope**: the comment concerns something you did not claim. Add it to the limitations, or decline with a reason.
4. **Taste**: a matter of presentation. Consider it, and decide.
5. **Disagreement**: reply with evidence (a calculation or a test), not authority. If unresolved, say so in the report.

A response table with the columns comment, class, action, evidence and status keeps the record honest. Thank the reader, and do not argue before you have checked.

@example
title: A comment that changes an inference
problem: A draft says that $g = 9.70 \pm 0.05\ \text{m/s}^2$ (statistical) "disagrees with the standard value $9.80665$ at $2\sigma$". The pendulum amplitude was $12^\circ$ and the analysis used $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$. A reviewer writes: "Is the finite-amplitude correction included?" Respond.
step: Classify the comment. It is a possible error of the kind "neglected systematic", and it can be checked by calculation.
step: Reproduce the draft's claim: $z = (9.80665 - 9.70)/0.05 = 2.13$.
step: Compute the correction. The exact period ratio at $12^\circ$ is $T/T_0 = 2K/\pi = 1.00275$. Since $g \propto T^{-2}$ at fixed measured $T$, $g_{\rm corr} = 9.70 \times (1.00275)^2 = 9.753\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Recompute the discrepancy: $z = (9.80665 - 9.753)/0.05 = 1.07$. The claimed disagreement largely disappears.
step: Respond: accept the comment, apply the correction, propagate the amplitude uncertainty, and change the claim to "consistent with the standard value". Also note that the standard value is a conventional one and the local $g$ differs from it by up to a few tenths of a percent. Log the comment as an error, fixed, with the new numbers.

@mistakes
- Asking only friends, who agree with you, or someone who will not read the derivations.
- Defending the draft in the first reply instead of checking the comment.
- Fixing a comment in the text without rerunning the numbers it affects.
- Deleting a comment you could not resolve instead of recording it as unresolved.

@practice num
prompt: The draft's discrepancy between the measured and the standard $g$ is $z = 2.13$ standard deviations. Assuming a Gaussian uncertainty and that the true value equals the standard value, what is the two-sided probability of a discrepancy at least this large? Give a value to three decimal places.
answer: 0.033
check: 2*(1 - 0.5*(1 + special.erf(2.13/math.sqrt(2))))
tol: 0.002
hint: For a Gaussian, the two-sided probability beyond $z$ is $1 - \mathrm{erf}(z/\sqrt{2})$.
hint: A discrepancy of $2.13\sigma$ is just beyond the 5% threshold of $1.96\sigma$, so expect a value a little under 0.05.
solution: $1 - \mathrm{erf}(2.13/\sqrt{2}) = 0.033$. A discrepancy this large would occur about 3% of the time by chance alone, which is worth a closer look, but the neglected amplitude correction can account for it.

@practice mc
prompt: A reviewer notes: "You examined the residuals of the small-angle fit, saw that the data deviate above $25^\circ$, restricted the fit to amplitudes below $25^\circ$, and then reported that the fit agrees with the small-angle formula." What is the issue?
choice: A neglected systematic uncertainty.
choice*: The fit range was chosen after seeing the data, and the same data are then used as evidence for the model.
choice: A unit mismatch between the sources.
choice: Confusing the standard deviation with the standard error.
hint: Ask when the choice of the $25^\circ$ cut was made, and with what information.
solution: Choosing the cut after seeing which points disagree builds the agreement into the analysis. The range should be fixed in advance, or the choice reported as post hoc and tested on new data.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all the practices that are inference errors.
choice*: Choosing the amplitude cut after seeing which choice gives agreement with the formula.
choice*: Quoting only the statistical uncertainty when a larger systematic effect is known.
choice*: Fitting a model to data and then citing the same data as independent confirmation of the model.
choice: Reporting a one-sided 95% upper limit for a null result.
choice: Stating the number of tests performed in the analysis.
hint: Look for choices that use information from the data in a way that cannot be undone, or that omit part of the uncertainty.
solution: Choosing the cut after seeing the data, quoting only the statistical uncertainty and citing the same data as independent confirmation all build in agreement or hide uncertainty. An upper limit and the number of tests are good reporting practice.

@practice num
prompt: A reviewer points out that $0.040\ \text{s}$ in your table is the sample standard deviation of 16 independent period timings, not the uncertainty of their mean. What is the standard uncertainty of the mean, in seconds?
answer: 0.01
check: 0.040/math.sqrt(16)
tol: 1%
unit: s
hint: The standard error of the mean of $n$ independent values is the standard deviation divided by $\sqrt{n}$.
hint: $\sqrt{16} = 4$.
solution: $0.040/\sqrt{16} = 0.010\ \text{s}$. Quoting $0.040$ as the uncertainty of the mean would overstate it by a factor of 4.

@practice self
prompt: Block deliverable: invite a qualified reader to identify mathematical or inference errors. Write the invitation you would send for your own draft (or for the pendulum report): who you would ask and why, what you send, what you want checked, three specific questions, a deadline, and how you will log and answer the comments.
hint: Make the questions specific enough that a reader cannot answer them with "looks fine".
hint: Say what you will not do, such as arguing before checking.
solution: Model answer. Reader: a colleague with graduate-level mechanics and numerical analysis who did not work on the project, asked because they can check derivations and error estimates. Message: "Could you spend about two hours on a 9-page report on the amplitude dependence of the pendulum period, by Friday the 14th? I attach the draft, the claim audit table (Section 6), the derivations (Appendix A), and the code with a pinned environment and a single command that regenerates every figure. I would most like you to try to break three things: (1) Is the derivation of the exact period integral in Appendix A valid, including the substitution and the limits? (2) Does the uncertainty budget in Section 5 omit any effect larger than 0.1% of the period? (3) Is the claim in Section 6, that the two-term series is within 0.1% up to $41^\circ$, supported by Figure 3 and the tolerance used, or is there a way the comparison could fail to detect an error? Please mark anything you cannot follow, not only errors. I will log each comment as an error, ambiguity, scope issue, taste or disagreement, reply to each with evidence, rerun anything affected, and send you a response table. I will not change the claims until I have checked your comments." Log: a table with comment, class, action, evidence and status.
rubric: The reader is chosen for competence and independence, with a reason.
rubric: The package includes the draft, claim audit, derivations and runnable code.
rubric: At least three specific, answerable questions target mathematics or inference.
rubric: A plan for logging, classifying and answering the comments is given.

@quiz num
prompt: A pendulum measurement at an amplitude of $15^\circ$ gave $g = 9.65 \pm 0.06\ \text{m/s}^2$ using the small-angle formula. Correct it with the exact period ratio $T/T_0 = 2K/\pi$ (which makes the corrected $g = g_{\rm naive}(T/T_0)^2$), then find how many standard deviations the corrected value lies from $9.80665$, taking the uncertainty as unchanged. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 1.22
check: (9.80665 - 9.65*(special.ellipk(math.sin(math.radians(15)/2)**2)/(pi/2))**2)/0.06
tol: 0.03
hint: At $15^\circ$, $T/T_0 \approx 1 + \theta_0^2/16$ with $\theta_0 = 0.2618$ rad.
hint: $T/T_0 \approx 1.0043$, so the corrected $g$ is about $9.65 \times 1.0086$.
solution: $T/T_0 = 1.00430$ and $(T/T_0)^2 = 1.00862$, so $g_{\rm corr} = 9.7332\ \text{m/s}^2$. Then $z = (9.80665 - 9.7332)/0.06 = 1.22$, down from $2.6$ before the correction.

@quiz self
prompt: A referee writes: "The authors say the extension 'improves agreement with experiment', but the improvement is judged from a plot alone, with no uncertainties, and the extension's free parameter was tuned on the same data. I am not convinced." Classify the comment, say whether it is justified, and write the response, including the evidence you would add.
hint: Decide first which inference errors apply: judging by eye, and using the same data for tuning and testing.
solution: Model answer. Classification: error in inference (double use of data) combined with an ambiguity (no quantitative measure of agreement). The comment is justified. Response: "We agree. We have (1) replaced the visual comparison by the reduced chi-squared of both models, with the uncertainties shown as error bars in the new Figure 4: baseline $\chi^2_\nu = [\text{value}]$, extension $\chi^2_\nu = [\text{value}]$; (2) split the data so that the free parameter is tuned on the first half and the comparison reported on the second half, which was not used for tuning, and added the number of fitted parameters to the comparison, since an extra parameter always lowers $\chi^2$ on the data it was tuned on; (3) added a statement of what would count against the extension, namely a held-out $\chi^2_\nu$ not lower than the baseline's by more than its expected scatter. The conclusion in Section 5 has been changed from 'improves agreement' to the wording that the held-out data support." Status: error accepted; changes made; numbers rerun.
rubric: Classifies the comment correctly and says that it is justified.
rubric: Identifies the double use of data and the absence of a quantitative measure of agreement.
rubric: Proposes a quantitative comparison with uncertainties on data that were not used for tuning.
rubric: Changes the claim to match the strength of the new evidence.
