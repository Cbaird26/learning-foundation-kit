@project
title: A reproducible investigation with a claim audit
overview: Choose one narrow question within your completed preparation, reproduce a known baseline, test exactly one controlled model change, and write a 6 to 10 page report with code or derivations, a claim audit and a reproducibility package. Then present and defend it orally. The pendulum amplitude question used throughout the course shows the intended scale, but your own question, or a clearly different variant, is better.
goal: Turn a precise question into a reproducible investigation with an explicit baseline, an uncertainty analysis and falsification criteria.
goal: Show that a known result was reproduced and independently checked, and that one model change was tested in a controlled way.
goal: Communicate each claim with its evidence, its conditions and its limits, in a report and in an oral defence.
step: Weeks 1 and 2. Write the narrow question (quantity, regime, precision, way of being wrong) and a literature map with three primary sources, each with a role.
step: Weeks 3 and 4. Write the baseline card (assumptions, equations, units, estimates of neglected effects) and a reproducibility plan with numerical acceptance criteria, before any testing.
step: Weeks 5 and 6. Reproduce a known result with your own code, run a convergence study, and add an independent check. Report relative differences or z-scores against the pre-set criteria.
step: Weeks 7 and 8. Write the proposal and hold the midcourse review with go and no-go rules fixed in advance. Then add one controlled change with an off switch, a regression test and a first-order prediction made beforehand.
step: Weeks 9 and 10. Build the uncertainty budget (linear propagation plus a seeded Monte Carlo run), record what would falsify the claim, test the limits, and report any null or failed result in full.
step: Weeks 11 and 12. Draft the report with a claim audit table. Send the package to a qualified reader with specific questions, then log and classify every comment.
step: Week 13. Respond point by point, rerun everything the corrections affect, and assemble the reproducibility package with a short account of the remaining limitations.
step: Week 14. Prepare a ten-minute talk (question, baseline, key evidence, claim audit, limits), rehearse answers to the three hardest questions you expect, and give the defence.
deliverable: A 6 to 10 page report with abstract, baseline, methods, results with uncertainties, claim audit table, limitations and a reproducibility statement.
deliverable: A reproducibility package: code, inputs with units, version information, seeds, one command that regenerates every table and figure, and the expected outputs.
deliverable: A critique log with each reader comment, its class, your response, the evidence and its status, with unresolved issues clearly stated.
deliverable: Slides or notes for a ten-minute oral presentation.
rubric: Question and baseline. The question has a quantity, a regime, a precision and a way of being wrong. The baseline is derived from written assumptions with units and estimates of neglected effects.
rubric: Reproduction and verification. Acceptance criteria were written before testing and contain numbers. A convergence study and an independent check are present, and agreement is quantified.
rubric: Extension and uncertainty. Exactly one change is tested, with a regression test and a prediction made first. The uncertainty budget ranks the inputs, and limit tests and falsification criteria are recorded.
rubric: Claim audit and inference. Every claim has evidence, conditions, strength and a refutation line; the language is calibrated; null and failed results are reported honestly.
rubric: Reproducibility and critique. Another person could rerun the work from the package. The critique log shows the reader's comments, your evidence-based responses, and any unresolved issues.
rubric: Communication. The report and talk are clear, the figures carry units and defined error bars, and answers in the defence point to evidence.
safety: If you choose a physical measurement, use only safe home experiments (motion, low-voltage circuits at battery level). Computational and analytic projects have no hazards beyond ordinary care with a computer.
tutor: Here is my question and my claimed precision. Which of the four ingredients (quantity, regime, precision, way of being wrong) is weakest, and how would you tighten it?
tutor: Act as a sceptical referee on my claim audit table. Which claim has the weakest evidence, and what test would strengthen it or refute it?
tutor: Question me for ten minutes on my uncertainty budget and my limit tests as if this were the oral defence, and tell me which answers pointed to evidence and which did not.

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, question selection.** A question names a quantity, a regime, a precision and a way of being wrong. Check that the effect is larger than your resolution, for example the correction $\theta_0^2/16$ against the timing uncertainty.
- **Week 2, literature map.** Primary, secondary and tertiary sources; backward and forward chaining; a row has claim, method, regime, uncertainty, limits and role; choose three primary sources.
- **Week 3, baseline derivation.** Assumptions first. $T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ and the exact $T = 4\sqrt{L/g}\,K(k)$ with $k = \sin(\theta_0/2)$. Check by dimensions, limits and the size of neglected effects.
- **Week 4, reproducibility plan.** Parameters with units, seeds, versions and one command. Fix acceptance criteria before testing. The observed order is $p = \log_2(E_1/E_2)$, and Monte Carlo tolerances need $N \propto 1/\text{tolerance}^2$.
- **Week 5, baseline reproduction.** RK4, a Newton-refined crossing, step halving, Richardson extrapolation, and a relative difference against the criterion. Diagnose failures from the cheapest check upward.
- **Week 6, independent verification.** Verification is not validation. An independent check fails differently. Use the AGM for $K$, remember that conservation laws test numerics only, and quote $z = |x_1 - x_2|/\sqrt{u_1^2 + u_2^2}$.
- **Week 7, proposal review.** Seven parts, four review questions, an effect-to-resolution ratio of at least about 3, and go and no-go rules written in advance.
- **Week 8, controlled extension.** One change with an off switch, a regression test, a first-order prediction ($\omega \approx \omega_0(1 + 3\epsilon A^2/8\omega_0^2)$), and a midcourse review.
- **Week 9, sensitivity analysis.** $u_y^2 = \sum (\partial f/\partial x_i)^2u_i^2$, power-law shortcuts, variance shares, scans, seeded Monte Carlo, and model-form effects.
- **Week 10, null and failure results.** Report limits and sensitivity, compare with both hypotheses, write falsification criteria first, test limits and edges, and remember $1 - 0.95^n$.
- **Week 11, draft report.** Structure, rounding of uncertainties, reduced chi-squared, figure captions, the claim audit and calibrated verbs.
- **Week 12, external critique.** Choose and brief a reader, look for inference errors, and triage comments into error, ambiguity, scope, taste and disagreement.

### Self-check

- Can I state my question with a quantity, a regime, a precision and a failing outcome?
- Does every number in my report trace to a source or a script?
- Were my acceptance criteria written before the tests, and do they contain numbers?
- Does my extended code reduce to the baseline when the new parameter is zero?
- By what factor is the effect larger than my uncertainty?
- Can another person regenerate every figure with one command?
- Which claim in my audit is weakest, and what would refute it?

### Most common errors

- Choosing a tolerance after seeing the result, or writing adjectives where numbers belong.
- Calling two checks independent when they share code, constants or inputs.
- Changing several things at once, so no difference can be attributed.
- Reading a null result as "no effect", or leaving failed runs out of the record.
- Quoting only the statistical uncertainty, choosing cuts after seeing the data, or using the same data to tune and to test.
- Overclaiming ("proves", "confirms") where the evidence shows consistency.

@exam num
prompt: A student times 10 swings of a pendulum with $L = 0.800\ \text{m}$ (treat the bob as a point mass) released from rest at an amplitude of $20^\circ$, and obtains $18.09\ \text{s}$. The measured period is $T = 1.809\ \text{s}$. The small-angle period of the same pendulum is $T_0 = T/(T/T_0)$, where the exact ratio is $T/T_0 = 2K/\pi$ with $k = \sin(\theta_0/2)$ and $K(k) = \int_0^{\pi/2} d\varphi/\sqrt{1 - k^2\sin^2\varphi}$. Find $g = 4\pi^2L/T_0^2$ in $\text{m/s}^2$, to three significant figures.
answer: 9.80
check: 4*pi**2*0.800/((18.09/10)/(2*quad(lambda p: 1/math.sqrt(1 - math.sin(math.radians(10))**2*math.sin(p)**2), 0, pi/2)/pi))**2
tol: 0.5%
unit: m/s^2
hint: First find the naive value $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$ with $T = 1.809\ \text{s}$. It is about $9.65\ \text{m/s}^2$.
hint: At $20^\circ$, $k = \sin 10^\circ$ and $T/T_0 = 2K/\pi \approx 1.00767$.
hint: Since $g \propto T_0^{-2}$ and $T_0 = T/1.00767$, the corrected value is the naive one multiplied by $1.00767^2$.
solution: The naive value is $g = 4\pi^2(0.800)/1.809^2 = 9.651$. At $20^\circ$, $K = 1.5828$ and $T/T_0 = 2K/\pi = 1.00767$, so $T_0 = 1.809/1.00767 = 1.7952\ \text{s}$. Then $g = 4\pi^2(0.800)/1.7952^2 = 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2$, equal to $9.651 \times 1.00767^2$. Neglecting the amplitude correction would have given $g$ low by about 1.5%, far larger than the 0.4% statistical uncertainty typical of such a measurement.

@exam ms
prompt: Select all the statements that would survive a claim audit.
choice*: "RK4 at $h = 0.1$ reproduces $4K(0.5)$ at $60^\circ$ to a relative difference of $6\times10^{-7}$, below the pre-set criterion of $10^{-6}$, with observed order 4.0."
choice*: "The measured excess of $0.9\% \pm 0.5\%$ at $30^\circ$ is consistent with both zero and the predicted $1.71\%$, so the measurement cannot discriminate between them."
choice*: "After the amplitude correction the measured $g$ lies $1.1\sigma$ from the conventional value, so no disagreement is claimed; the local $g$ may itself differ from the conventional value by a few tenths of a percent."
choice: "Both programs agree to twelve digits, so the equation of motion they share is verified."
choice: "The result lies within $2\sigma$ of zero, so there is no dependence on amplitude."
hint: A statement survives if it gives a number, states its conditions, and claims no more than the evidence supports.
hint: Ask, for the statement about two agreeing programs and the statement about a result within $2\sigma$ of zero, what part of the analysis they share or leave out.
solution: The statements about the RK4 reproduction, the $0.9\% \pm 0.5\%$ excess and the corrected $g$ give numbers and conditions, and claim only what the evidence shows. Agreement between two programs that share the same equation of motion cannot verify that equation (a common-mode failure), and a result within $2\sigma$ of zero is also within $2\sigma$ of other values, so it does not show the absence of an effect.

@exam self
prompt: Audit this claim from a published-style abstract: "The period of a simple pendulum is independent of amplitude to within 1% for amplitudes up to $30^\circ$; our measurement of a $0.5\% \pm 0.4\%$ excess at $30^\circ$ confirms this." Using the exact baseline, say whether the claim is supported. Give the numbers that you compute, an interpretation of the measurement, what you would check next, and a rewritten claim that is justified.
hint: Compute the exact fractional excess at $30^\circ$ from $2K/\pi$ with $k = \sin 15^\circ$, and compare it with 1%.
hint: Then compute how many standard deviations the measurement lies from the exact value, and from zero.
hint: Consider both a problem with the claim and a possible problem with the measurement.
solution: Model answer. Baseline: at $30^\circ$ the exact period ratio is $2K/\pi = 1.01741$, an excess of $1.741\%$, so the claim of a deviation below 1% is false under the baseline; the 1% bound holds only up to about $22.8^\circ$. Measurement: $0.5\% \pm 0.4\%$ is $(1.741 - 0.5)/0.4 = 3.1\sigma$ below the baseline prediction, and it is $1.25\sigma$ from zero and $1.25\sigma$ below the 1% line. So the measurement is consistent with a deviation below 1% but is in tension with the baseline at about three standard deviations, which is not a confirmation: "confirms" does not follow, because the data are not compared with the prediction. Next checks: the amplitude calibration (an amplitude overestimate of a few degrees would reduce the expected excess), the timing method and its systematic offset, whether the quoted uncertainty is a standard deviation or a standard error, and the multiple-comparison count if several amplitudes were tried. Justified rewrite: "Under the frictionless rigid-pendulum model the period exceeds the small-angle value by 1% at $22.8^\circ$ and by $1.74\%$ at $30^\circ$. Our measurement at $30^\circ$ ($0.5\% \pm 0.4\%$) lies $3.1\sigma$ below the model value; we have not identified the cause, and we list the amplitude calibration and timing offset as candidates."
rubric: Computes the exact excess at $30^\circ$ (about 1.74%) and concludes that the claim of staying below 1% is false under the baseline.
rubric: Compares the measurement with both the prediction and the claimed bound in units of the uncertainty (about 3.1 and 1.25 standard deviations).
rubric: Identifies "confirms" as unsupported and proposes specific checks of systematics and of how the uncertainty is defined.
rubric: Gives a rewritten claim with calibrated language, conditions and the unresolved tension stated.
