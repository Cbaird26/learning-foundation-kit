@article
The scale $\mu$ in dimensional regularization is arbitrary, yet the renormalized coupling is defined at that scale. The **renormalization group** says how the parameters must change with $\mu$ so that physics does not.

### Where running comes from

The bare coupling is $\lambda_0 = \mu^{\epsilon}\big(\lambda + \frac{3\lambda^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon}+\dots\big)$ in $d=4-\epsilon$ ($\overline{\rm MS}$). It describes the unrenormalized theory and cannot depend on $\mu$. Setting $\mu\,\frac{d\lambda_0}{d\mu}=0$ and writing $\beta(\lambda) \equiv \mu\frac{d\lambda}{d\mu}$,

$$0 = \epsilon\Big(\lambda + \frac{3\lambda^2}{16\pi^2\epsilon}\Big) + \beta(\lambda)\Big(1 + \frac{6\lambda}{16\pi^2\epsilon}\Big) \;\Longrightarrow\; \beta(\lambda) = -\epsilon\lambda + \frac{3\lambda^2}{16\pi^2}.$$

At $\epsilon\to0$ this is the one-loop beta function of $\phi^4$ theory: $\beta(\lambda) = \frac{3\lambda^2}{16\pi^2}$. The same result follows from requiring the amplitude of week 6 to be independent of $\mu$.

Correlation functions obey the **Callan–Symanzik equation**

$$\big[\mu\partial_\mu + \beta(\lambda)\partial_\lambda + n\gamma(\lambda)\big]\,G^{(n)}(p_i;\lambda,m,\mu) = 0,$$

where $\gamma$ is the anomalous dimension of the field. In $\phi^4$ theory $\gamma=\frac{\lambda^2}{12(4\pi)^4}$ at the first nonvanishing order. The mass runs too: $\mu\frac{dm^2}{d\mu} = \frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2}\,m^2$ at one loop.

### Solving one-loop flows

For $\mu\frac{d\lambda}{d\mu}=b\lambda^2$ with $b=\frac{3}{16\pi^2}$, write $t=\ln(\mu/\mu_0)$ and note that $\frac{d(1/\lambda)}{dt}=-b$:

$$\lambda(\mu) = \frac{\lambda_0}{1 - b\,\lambda_0\ln(\mu/\mu_0)}.$$

The coupling grows with energy and diverges at $\ln(\mu_L/\mu_0) = \frac{1}{b\lambda_0} = \frac{16\pi^2}{3\lambda_0}$, the **Landau pole**. Read this correctly: it marks where the one-loop formula and perturbation theory stop being reliable, usually long before. It is a statement about the limits of an effective description, not a prediction that a physical singularity exists. Its location is also scheme dependent through $\lambda_0$.

For QED with one Dirac fermion of unit charge, $\mu\frac{d\alpha}{d\mu} = \frac{2\alpha^2}{3\pi}$, so $\frac{1}{\alpha(\mu)} = \frac{1}{\alpha(\mu_0)} - \frac{2}{3\pi}\ln\frac{\mu}{\mu_0}$. More charged fermions run it faster, with $\frac{2}{3\pi}\to\frac{2}{3\pi}\sum_fQ_f^2$ (times the colour multiplicity for quarks).

For a non-Abelian gauge coupling (week 11), $\mu\frac{dg}{d\mu} = -\frac{b_0}{16\pi^2}g^3$, so with $\alpha_s=g^2/4\pi$,

$$\alpha_s(\mu) = \frac{\alpha_s(\mu_0)}{1 + \frac{b_0}{2\pi}\alpha_s(\mu_0)\ln(\mu/\mu_0)}.$$

If $b_0>0$ the coupling shrinks at high energy: **asymptotic freedom**.

### Fixed points and the Wilsonian picture

A **fixed point** satisfies $\beta(\lambda_*)=0$. In $d=4-\epsilon$ the beta function above has the Wilson–Fisher point $\lambda_* = \frac{16\pi^2\epsilon}{3}$. Near it, $\delta\lambda\propto\mu^{\beta'(\lambda_*)}$ with $\beta'(\lambda_*)=+\epsilon$, so the deviation dies out as $\mu\to0$: the fixed point attracts flows toward the infrared.

Wilson's picture: lowering the cutoff by integrating out a shell of modes changes the couplings. An operator of scaling dimension $\Delta$ is **relevant** if $\Delta<d$, **marginal** if $\Delta=d$, **irrelevant** if $\Delta>d$. The dimensionless coefficient of an irrelevant operator fades as $(E/\Lambda)^{\Delta-d}$ toward the infrared; marginal ones run logarithmically.

### Leading logarithms

A fixed-order result contains $\lambda^n\ln^n(E/\mu)$ terms that spoil it when $E\gg\mu$. Choosing $\mu\sim E$ and using $\lambda(E)$ resums these logarithms.

@example
title: Running mass and coupling together
problem: Show that in one-loop $\phi^4$ theory $m^2(\mu)\propto\lambda(\mu)^{1/3}$.
step: Take the ratio of the two flow equations: $\frac{d\ln m^2}{d\ln\mu}=\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2}$ and $\frac{d\ln\lambda}{d\ln\mu}=\frac{3\lambda}{16\pi^2}$.
step: Dividing, $\frac{d\ln m^2}{d\ln\lambda} = \frac{1}{3}$, independent of $\lambda$.
step: Integrate: $\ln m^2 = \frac13\ln\lambda + \text{const}$, so $m^2(\mu)\propto\lambda(\mu)^{1/3}$.
step: Thus $m^2(\mu_2)/m^2(\mu_1) = (\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^{1/3}$: doubling the coupling raises the running mass squared by $2^{1/3}$.

@example
title: Location of a Landau pole
problem: Starting from $\lambda(\mu_0)=0.5$, find $\ln(\mu_L/\mu_0)$ for the one-loop $\phi^4$ flow.
step: Use $\frac{1}{\lambda(\mu)} = \frac{1}{\lambda_0} - b\,t$ with $b = \frac{3}{16\pi^2}$ and $t=\ln(\mu/\mu_0)$.
step: The coupling diverges when $\frac1\lambda\to0$, i.e. $t_L = \frac{1}{b\lambda_0}$.
step: Numerically $b = 3/157.91 = 0.019$, so $t_L = \frac{1}{0.019\times0.5}\approx105.3$.
step: The pole is $e^{105}$ times above $\mu_0$, absurdly far above any scale where the model could be trusted, which is the right way to read it: a bound on the validity of the description, not an observable.

@mistakes
- Treating the Landau pole as a prediction about nature rather than a limit on the validity of a perturbative effective description.
- Confusing $\beta=\mu\,d\lambda/d\mu$ with the flow toward the infrared: a positive $\beta$ means the coupling decreases as $\mu$ decreases.
- Applying the one-loop solution far outside the range where $b\lambda\ln(\mu/\mu_0)$ is small without checking higher orders.
- Writing $\alpha(\mu)$ for QED with the wrong sign in $\frac1\alpha$: the electromagnetic coupling grows with energy, so $1/\alpha$ decreases.

@practice self
prompt: The mass counterterm in $\overline{\rm MS}$ is $m_0^2 = m^2\big(1+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2\epsilon}\big)$. Derive the one-loop running $\mu\,\frac{dm^2}{d\mu} = \frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2}m^2$ from $\mu$-independence of $m_0^2$.
hint: Differentiate $m_0^2$ with respect to $\ln\mu$ and use $\beta(\lambda) = -\epsilon\lambda + O(\lambda^2)$.
hint: Solve for $\mu\,dm^2/d\mu$ and keep terms of order $\lambda$ as $\epsilon\to0$.
solution: $0 = \mu\frac{d}{d\mu}\big[m^2(1+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2\epsilon})\big] = \dot{m^2}\big(1+\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2\epsilon}\big) + m^2\frac{\beta(\lambda)}{16\pi^2\epsilon}$. With $\beta = -\epsilon\lambda+O(\lambda^2)$ the last term is $-m^2\frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2}+O(\lambda^2/\epsilon)$. Keeping terms of order $\lambda$ (the $\lambda^2/\epsilon$ terms are cancelled by the two-loop counterterms omitted from $m_0^2$) and solving for $\dot{m^2}$ gives $\mu\frac{dm^2}{d\mu} = \frac{\lambda}{16\pi^2}m^2$ as $\epsilon\to0$.
