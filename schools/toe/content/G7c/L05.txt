@article
A **regulator** makes a divergent integral finite by introducing a parameter: a cutoff $\Lambda$, a heavy mass $M$, or a deviation $\epsilon$ from four dimensions. Physical predictions must not depend on the regulator after renormalization, but intermediate results do. A good regulator preserves the symmetries you need.

### Cutoff and Pauli–Villars

A hard momentum cutoff is simple but breaks translation-covariant shifts of loop momenta and, in gauge theories, gauge invariance. **Pauli–Villars** subtracts a heavy copy of each propagator, $\frac{1}{k^2+m^2}\to\frac{1}{k^2+m^2}-\frac{1}{k^2+M^2}$. For the zero-momentum bubble, with both propagators regulated, this gives $\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\big[\ln\frac{M^2}{m^2}-2\big]$ for $M\gg m$: the divergence reappears as a logarithm of the regulator scale. The tadpole needs more subtractions because it is quadratically divergent.

### Dimensional regularization

Continue the spacetime dimension to $d = 4-\epsilon$. Then the field has mass dimension $[\phi] = \frac{d-2}{2} = 1-\frac\epsilon2$ and the coupling in $\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$ has $[\lambda] = 4-d = \epsilon$. To keep $\lambda$ dimensionless we write $\lambda\to\lambda\mu^{\epsilon}$, which introduces an arbitrary mass scale $\mu$. The Euclidean master formula is

$$\int\frac{d^dk_E}{(2\pi)^d}\frac{1}{(k^2+\Delta)^n} = \frac{1}{(4\pi)^{d/2}}\,\frac{\Gamma(n-\frac d2)}{\Gamma(n)}\,\Delta^{\frac d2-n}.$$

Divergences become poles in $\epsilon$:

$$\Gamma\big(\tfrac\epsilon2\big) = \frac2\epsilon - \gamma_E + O(\epsilon),\qquad \Gamma\big(-1+\tfrac\epsilon2\big) = -\frac2\epsilon - 1 + \gamma_E + O(\epsilon),$$

where $\gamma_E\approx0.5772$ is the Euler–Mascheroni constant.

### The bubble and the tadpole

Using the Feynman parameter from week 4,

$$\mu^\epsilon\!\int\!\frac{d^dk_E}{(2\pi)^d}\frac{1}{(k^2+m^2)((k+p)^2+m^2)} = \frac{1}{16\pi^2}\Big[\frac{2}{\epsilon} - \gamma_E + \ln 4\pi - \int_0^1\!dx\,\ln\frac{m^2+x(1-x)p^2}{\mu^2}\Big] + O(\epsilon).$$

The tadpole is

$$\mu^\epsilon\!\int\!\frac{d^dk_E}{(2\pi)^d}\frac{1}{k^2+m^2} = \frac{m^2}{16\pi^2}\Big[-\frac2\epsilon + \gamma_E - \ln4\pi - 1 + \ln\frac{m^2}{\mu^2}\Big] + O(\epsilon).$$

There is no $\Lambda^2$ term: **power divergences are invisible** in dimensional regularization, because the integral $\int d^dk\,k^{-2}$ with no scale is set to zero. The quadratic divergence of the cutoff calculation shows up here only as a pole at $d=2$. The pole $\frac{2}{\epsilon}$ plays the role of $\ln\Lambda^2$.

### What survives a change of regulator

| Piece of a loop result | Regulator dependent? |
|---|---|
| Coefficient of the logarithm ($\frac{1}{16\pi^2}$ for the bubble) | No |
| Finite constant accompanying the log | Yes |
| Power divergences ($\Lambda^2$) | Yes |
| Dependence on external momentum, such as $\ln(m^2+x(1-x)p^2)$ | No |

### Why gauge theories prefer dimensional regularization

A cutoff on $|k|$ breaks gauge invariance: the photon vacuum polarization acquires a term proportional to $\Lambda^2g^{\mu\nu}$, a photon mass that must be removed by hand. Dimensional regularization preserves Lorentz and gauge invariance and gives the transverse form $(p^2g^{\mu\nu}-p^\mu p^\nu)\Pi(p^2)$ directly. Its cost is conceptual. It hides power divergences, which encode sensitivity to heavy physics (week 8), and it needs care with $\gamma_5$ and with the number of gauge-field polarizations in $d$ dimensions.

### The $\overline{\rm MS}$ combination

The constants $-\gamma_E+\ln4\pi$ travel with every pole. Defining $\bar\mu^2 = 4\pi e^{-\gamma_E}\mu^2$ absorbs them: $\frac2\epsilon - \gamma_E+\ln 4\pi-\ln\frac{\Delta}{\mu^2} = \frac2\epsilon - \ln\frac{\Delta}{\bar\mu^2}$. Subtracting only $\frac2\epsilon$ in terms of $\bar\mu$ is the **modified minimal subtraction** ($\overline{\rm MS}$) scheme of this course; week 6 makes it precise.

@example
title: The finite constant that travels with the pole
problem: Show that $\mu^\epsilon\,(4\pi)^{-d/2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac\epsilon2)\,\Delta^{-\epsilon/2}$ equals $\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\big[\frac{2}{\epsilon} - \gamma_E + \ln4\pi - \ln\frac{\Delta}{\mu^2}\big] + O(\epsilon)$.
step: Use $d = 4-\epsilon$: $(4\pi)^{-d/2} = \frac{1}{16\pi^2}(4\pi)^{\epsilon/2}$.
step: Combine the factors that are raised to the power $\epsilon/2$: $\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\,\Gamma(\tfrac\epsilon2)\,\big(\tfrac{4\pi\mu^2}{\Delta}\big)^{\epsilon/2}$.
step: Expand each factor: $\Gamma(\tfrac\epsilon2) = \frac2\epsilon-\gamma_E+O(\epsilon)$ and $\big(\tfrac{4\pi\mu^2}{\Delta}\big)^{\epsilon/2} = 1 + \tfrac\epsilon2\ln\tfrac{4\pi\mu^2}{\Delta}+O(\epsilon^2)$.
step: Multiply. The pole times the $O(\epsilon)$ term gives a finite piece $\ln\frac{4\pi\mu^2}{\Delta}$, so the result is $\frac{1}{16\pi^2}\big[\frac2\epsilon - \gamma_E + \ln4\pi - \ln\frac{\Delta}{\mu^2}\big]$.

@example
title: Dimension of the coupling in $d$ dimensions
problem: In $d$ dimensions find the mass dimension of $\lambda$ in $\mathcal L\supset-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$ and explain why $\mu^\epsilon$ appears.
step: The kinetic term $\frac12(\partial\phi)^2$ has mass dimension $d$, so $2[\phi] + 2 = d$ and $[\phi] = \frac{d-2}{2}$.
step: The interaction term must also have dimension $d$: $[\lambda] + 4[\phi] = d$, so $[\lambda] = d - 2(d-2) = 4-d$.
step: In $d=4-\epsilon$ the coupling has dimension $\epsilon$. Writing $\lambda\to\lambda\mu^\epsilon$ with dimensionless $\lambda$ keeps loop expansions dimensionally consistent.
step: The arbitrary scale $\mu$ leaves a trace in every renormalized result as $\ln\mu$; this is the origin of the running coupling (week 7).

@mistakes
- Dropping the $\mu^\epsilon$ factor, then finding logarithms of a dimensionful quantity such as $\ln m^2$ instead of $\ln(m^2/\mu^2)$.
- Multiplying a pole $\frac{2}{\epsilon}$ by $O(\epsilon)$ terms and forgetting the finite piece they leave behind.
- Believing that dimensional regularization removes the physics of a quadratic divergence; it only hides it, and heavy particles still shift low-energy masses.
- Discarding $\gamma_E$ and $\ln4\pi$ inconsistently between the bubble and the tadpole.
