@article
A symmetry is **global** if the same transformation is applied everywhere and **local** if it may vary from point to point. Demanding a local symmetry forces a new field into the theory.

### Making the symmetry local

Let a field of charge $q$ transform as $\psi\to e^{iq\alpha(x)}\psi$. A constant $\alpha$ leaves $\bar\psi i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi$ invariant, but for $\alpha(x)$ we get $\partial_\mu\psi\to e^{iq\alpha}(\partial_\mu\psi + iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\psi)$, with an unwanted extra term. In this course

$$D_\mu = \partial_\mu - iqA_\mu,\qquad \psi\to e^{iq\alpha}\psi,\qquad A_\mu\to A_\mu + \partial_\mu\alpha,$$

and then $D_\mu\psi\to e^{iq\alpha}D_\mu\psi$. The commutator $[D_\mu,D_\nu] = -iq F_{\mu\nu}$ defines the gauge-invariant **field strength** $F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu$. The Lagrangians are

$$\mathcal L = -\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} + \bar\psi(i\gamma^\mu D_\mu - m)\psi,\qquad \mathcal L = -\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} + (D_\mu\phi)^*(D^\mu\phi) - m^2|\phi|^2.$$

With this sign the interaction is $+A_\mu J^\mu$, $J^\mu = q\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$, and the Euler–Lagrange equation for $A_\mu$ is $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = -J^\nu$. Relative to the convention $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=+j^\nu$ this is the replacement $q\to-q$, equivalently $A\to-A$. Physical results depend on $q^2$ and on relative signs of charges.

Antisymmetry gives $\partial_\nu\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$, hence $\partial_\nu J^\nu = 0$: **gauge invariance implies current conservation**.

### Redundant variables

$A_\mu$ has four components, but $A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha$ with arbitrary $\alpha(x)$ changes nothing observable. Only $F_{\mu\nu}$, and phases such as $\exp(iq\oint A_\mu dx^\mu)$ around closed loops, are gauge invariant. Counting the physical content of a massless vector in $d=4$:

- $A_0$ has no time derivative in $\mathcal L$, so its conjugate momentum vanishes: it is fixed by the Gauss-law constraint rather than evolving.
- One more combination is pure gauge.

That leaves $4-1-1=2$ physical polarizations. In momentum space with $k^2=0$: the conditions $\epsilon\cdot k=0$ leave $3$ vectors, and $\epsilon\sim\epsilon + ck$ removes one, so $d-2$ polarizations in $d$ dimensions.

The redundancy shows up in the kinetic operator. In momentum space $-\frac14F^2 = \frac12\tilde A_\mu\,\big(-k^2g^{\mu\nu}+k^\mu k^\nu\big)\tilde A_\nu$, and this operator annihilates $\tilde A_\nu\propto k_\nu$: it has a zero mode for every $k$. It cannot be inverted, so the path integral $\int\mathcal DA\,e^{iS}$ has no propagator and an infinite gauge-orbit volume. Week 10 fixes this by gauge fixing.

### The Ward identity

Gauge invariance of the effective action requires the photon self-energy $\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k)$ to be transverse:

$$k_\mu\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k)=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\Pi^{\mu\nu}(k)=(k^2g^{\mu\nu}-k^\mu k^\nu)\,\Pi(k^2).$$

A term $\propto g^{\mu\nu}$ alone would be a photon mass term $\frac12m_\gamma^2A_\mu A^\mu$, which is not gauge invariant. So the photon stays massless to all orders if the regulator respects gauge invariance. Dimensional regularization does; a hard cutoff on loop momentum generates a spurious $\Lambda^2g^{\mu\nu}$. The same identity gives $Z_1=Z_2$ in QED: the vertex and the electron wave-function renormalizations are equal.

The remaining divergence of $\Pi(k^2)$ renormalizes the charge. For one Dirac fermion of charge $Q$ (in units of $e$), $\mu\frac{de}{d\mu}=\frac{e^3Q^2}{12\pi^2}$. In general

$$\mu\frac{de}{d\mu}=\frac{e^3}{16\pi^2}\Big[\tfrac43\sum_{\rm Dirac}Q^2+\tfrac13\sum_{\rm complex\ scalars}Q^2\Big],$$

equivalent to $\mu\frac{d\alpha}{d\mu}=\frac{b}{2\pi}\alpha^2$ with $b$ the bracket. This reproduces the $\frac{2\alpha^2}{3\pi}$ of week 7 for $b=\frac43$.

### Observable versus redundant

The Aharonov–Bohm phase $q\oint A_\mu dx^\mu$ around a closed loop is gauge invariant (for single-valued $\alpha$ the change is $q\oint\partial_\mu\alpha\,dx^\mu=0$) and can be nonzero where $F_{\mu\nu}=0$. So $F_{\mu\nu}$ is not the whole story globally, but $A_\mu$ itself, pointwise, is never observable.

@example
title: Covariance of the covariant derivative
problem: With $D_\mu=\partial_\mu-iqA_\mu$, show that $D_\mu\psi\to e^{iq\alpha}D_\mu\psi$ when $\psi\to e^{iq\alpha}\psi$ and $A_\mu\to A_\mu+\partial_\mu\alpha$.
step: Apply the transformed derivative to the transformed field: $\big(\partial_\mu - iqA_\mu - iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\big)\,e^{iq\alpha}\psi$.
step: Use the product rule: $\partial_\mu(e^{iq\alpha}\psi) = e^{iq\alpha}(\partial_\mu\psi + iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\psi)$.
step: Collect terms: $e^{iq\alpha}\big[\partial_\mu\psi + iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\psi - iqA_\mu\psi - iq\,\partial_\mu\alpha\,\psi\big]$.
step: The $\partial_\mu\alpha$ terms cancel, leaving $e^{iq\alpha}(\partial_\mu - iqA_\mu)\psi = e^{iq\alpha}D_\mu\psi$.

@example
title: Counting the polarizations of a massless vector
problem: Show that a massless gauge field in $d=4$ has two physical polarizations.
step: A plane wave $A_\mu=\epsilon_\mu e^{-ik\cdot x}$ with $k^2=0$ has $4$ components $\epsilon_\mu$.
step: The field equation $\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}=0$ gives $k^2\epsilon^\nu - k^\nu(k\cdot\epsilon)=0$, so $k\cdot\epsilon=0$: one condition, $3$ components left.
step: A gauge transformation $\alpha=c\,e^{-ik\cdot x}$ shifts $\epsilon_\mu\to\epsilon_\mu-ick_\mu$. Since $k\cdot k=0$ this preserves $k\cdot\epsilon=0$, so one more component is redundant.
step: Result: $3-1=2$ polarizations, the two transverse ones, $d-2$ in general.

@mistakes
- Using $D_\mu=\partial_\mu-iqA_\mu$ together with $A_\mu\to A_\mu-\partial_\mu\alpha$, or with the wrong sign of the phase on $\psi$; the pieces must be chosen consistently.
- Adding a photon mass term $\frac12m^2A_\mu A^\mu$ and still calling the theory gauge invariant.
- Saying the photon has three polarizations (as for a massive vector) or four (the number of components of $A_\mu$).
- Treating $A_\mu(x)$ at a point as measurable, instead of $F_{\mu\nu}$ and gauge-invariant holonomies.
