@article
A **vector field** assigns a vector to every point. In the plane $\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle P(x,y),\,Q(x,y)\rangle$, and in space $\mathbf F=\langle P,Q,R\rangle$. Think of wind on a weather map: one arrow at each point. Typical examples are the velocity of a flowing fluid, the force of gravity or of an electric charge, and the gradient $\nabla f$ of a scalar function.

### Seeing a field

To sketch a field, evaluate it at a few points and draw arrows. For $\mathbf F=\langle -y,\,x\rangle$ the arrow at $(1,0)$ is $\langle 0,1\rangle$ and at $(0,1)$ it is $\langle -1,0\rangle$: the arrows circle the origin counterclockwise, and their length $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ grows with the distance from the origin.

A **field line** is a curve $\mathbf r(t)$ whose velocity equals the field, $\mathbf r'(t)=\mathbf F(\mathbf r(t))$. A particle dropped into the flow follows a field line, which connects this week to Week 1.

### Divergence and curl

Two derivatives of a field carry its local behavior:

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=P_x+Q_y+R_z,\qquad \nabla\times\mathbf F=\langle R_y-Q_z,\ P_z-R_x,\ Q_x-P_y\rangle .$$

- **Divergence** is a number. Positive means the flow spreads out from the point (a source); negative means it gathers in (a sink). It is the rate at which a small blob of fluid expands, per unit volume.
- **Curl** is a vector along the local axis of rotation (right-hand rule), with magnitude twice the angular speed. In the plane only the $z$-component survives, the scalar curl $Q_x-P_y$.

For $\langle -y,x\rangle$ the divergence is $0$ and the scalar curl is $1-(-1)=2$: pure rotation. For $\langle x,y\rangle$ the divergence is $2$ and the curl is $0$: pure spreading.

### The Jacobian matrix of a field

Differentiating every component with respect to every variable gives the Jacobian matrix, which is the best linear approximation to the field near a point:

$$D\mathbf F=\begin{pmatrix}P_x&P_y\\ Q_x&Q_y\end{pmatrix}.$$

Its trace is the divergence, and $Q_x-P_y$ is the curl.

Here is the meaning for a flow. In a short time $h$ each particle moves from $\mathbf x$ to $\mathbf x+h\,\mathbf F(\mathbf x)$. This map has Jacobian matrix $I+h\,D\mathbf F$, whose determinant is

$$1+h\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf F+h^2\det D\mathbf F .$$

So to first order the divergence is the rate at which a small patch of fluid grows or shrinks. This is the idea behind the divergence theorem in Week 12.

@example
title: Divergence and curl in space
problem: Let $\mathbf F=\langle xy,\ yz,\ zx\rangle$. Find $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ and $\nabla\times\mathbf F$, and evaluate both at $(1,2,3)$.
step: Here $P=xy$, $Q=yz$, $R=zx$. The divergence is $P_x+Q_y+R_z=y+z+x$.
step: At $(1,2,3)$ the divergence is $2+3+1=6$.
step: For the curl, $R_y-Q_z=0-y$, $P_z-R_x=0-z$ and $Q_x-P_y=0-x$. So $\nabla\times\mathbf F=\langle -y,\,-z,\,-x\rangle$.
step: At $(1,2,3)$ the curl is $\langle -2,\,-3,\,-1\rangle$.

@example
title: Reading the Jacobian matrix of a flow
problem: For the plane field $\mathbf F=\langle x^2,\ xy\rangle$, find the Jacobian matrix at $(1,2)$, and use it to find the area scale factor of the short-time flow map $\mathbf x\mapsto\mathbf x+h\mathbf F(\mathbf x)$ with $h=0.1$.
step: Differentiate: $D\mathbf F=\begin{pmatrix}2x&0\\ y&x\end{pmatrix}$, which at $(1,2)$ is $\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\ 2&1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The trace is $3$, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$ there. The scalar curl is $Q_x-P_y=2-0=2$.
step: The flow map has Jacobian matrix $I+0.1\,D\mathbf F=\begin{pmatrix}1.2&0\\ 0.2&1.1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Its determinant is $1.2\cdot 1.1=1.32$, so a small patch near $(1,2)$ grows in area by about $32\%$ over this step.
step: Compare with the first-order estimate $1+h\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1.3$. The difference $0.02$ is the second-order term $h^2\det D\mathbf F=0.01\cdot 2$.

@mistakes
- Mixing up divergence and curl: divergence is a scalar, a sum of $\partial P/\partial x$-type terms, while the planar scalar curl is the difference $Q_x-P_y$, not the sum.
- Believing a field needs circular field lines to have nonzero curl. The shear field $\langle y,0\rangle$ has straight field lines but scalar curl $-1$.
- Using $h\,D\mathbf F$ instead of $I+h\,D\mathbf F$ as the Jacobian matrix of the flow map.
- Confusing a field line, a curve tangent to the field, with the field's arrows themselves.

answer: 1/5
check: norm([-4/(3**2 + 4**2), 3/(3**2 + 4**2)])
hint: Evaluate both components at $(3,4)$ first, then take the length.
hint: At $(3,4)$ the denominator is $25$ and the numerator is $\langle -4,3\rangle$.
solution: $\mathbf F(3,4)=\frac{1}{25}\langle -4,3\rangle$, whose length is $\frac{5}{25}=\frac15$. In general $|\mathbf F|=1/r$.

@practice num
prompt: Find the divergence of $\mathbf F=\langle x^2y,\ y^2z,\ z^2x\rangle$ at the point $(1,2,3)$.
answer: 22
check: nderiv(lambda x: x**2*2, 1) + nderiv(lambda y: y**2*3, 2) + nderiv(lambda z: z**2*1, 3)
hint: The divergence is $P_x+Q_y+R_z$. Each term differentiates one component with respect to its own variable.
hint: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2xy+2yz+2zx$.
solution: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2xy+2yz+2zx$. At $(1,2,3)$ this is $4+12+6=22$.

check: (nderiv(lambda y: 1*3, 2) - nderiv(lambda z: 2*z**2, 3), nderiv(lambda z: 1**2*2, 3) - nderiv(lambda x: x*3, 1), nderiv(lambda x: 2*3**2, 1) - nderiv(lambda y: 1**2*y, 2))

solution: $\nabla\times\mathbf F=\langle -2yz,\ -z,\ -x^2\rangle$, which at $(1,2,3)$ is $\langle -12,\,-3,\,-1\rangle$.

@practice num
prompt: A fluid has velocity field $\mathbf F(x,y)=\langle 2x+y,\ x-3y\rangle$. In a short time $h=0.01$ each particle moves from $\mathbf x$ to $\mathbf x+h\,\mathbf F(\mathbf x)$. Compute the Jacobian determinant of this map. By what factor does the area of a small patch change?
answer: 0.9893
tol: 0.00005
check: det([[nderiv(lambda x: x + 0.01*(2*x + 3), 1), nderiv(lambda y: 1 + 0.01*(2*1 + y), 3)], [nderiv(lambda x: 3 + 0.01*(x - 3*3), 1), nderiv(lambda y: y + 0.01*(1 - 3*y), 3)]])
hint: The Jacobian matrix of the map is $I+h\,D\mathbf F$, where $D\mathbf F$ is the matrix of partial derivatives of the components of $\mathbf F$.
hint: $D\mathbf F=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&-3\end{pmatrix}$, so the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}1.02&0.01\\0.01&0.97\end{pmatrix}$.
solution: $\det=1.02\cdot 0.97-0.01\cdot 0.01=0.9894-0.0001=0.9893$. A small patch shrinks to about $98.93\%$ of its area. This agrees with the divergence: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2-3=-1$, so to first order the area changes by the factor $1+h(-1)=0.99$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $\mathbf F=\dfrac{\langle x,\,y,\,z\rangle}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}}$, a field of unit vectors pointing away from the origin. Find $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$ at the point $(1,2,2)$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 2/3
check: nderiv(lambda x: x/sqrt(x*x + 2**2 + 2**2), 1) + nderiv(lambda y: y/sqrt(1 + y*y + 2**2), 2) + nderiv(lambda z: z/sqrt(1 + 2**2 + z*z), 2)
hint: Each term has the form $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\dfrac{x}{r}\right)=\dfrac1r-\dfrac{x^2}{r^3}$, with $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.
hint: Add the three terms. The squares combine to $x^2+y^2+z^2=r^2$.
solution: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac3r-\frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{r^3}=\frac3r-\frac1r=\frac2r$. At $(1,2,2)$ we have $r=3$, so the divergence is $\frac23$.
