@article
A line integral adds along a curve. A **surface integral** adds over a surface: surface area, the mass of a thin shell, or the flow of a fluid through a membrane.

### Parametrized surfaces

A surface is described by two parameters, $\mathbf r(u,v)=\langle x(u,v),\,y(u,v),\,z(u,v)\rangle$ for $(u,v)$ in a region $D$. Three standard cases:

- a graph $z=g(x,y)$: $\mathbf r(x,y)=\langle x,\,y,\,g(x,y)\rangle$;
- a sphere of radius $a$: $\mathbf r(\phi,\theta)=a\langle\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \cos\phi\rangle$;
- a cylinder of radius $a$: $\mathbf r(\theta,z)=\langle a\cos\theta,\ a\sin\theta,\ z\rangle$.

### The area element

Holding $v$ fixed and varying $u$ traces a curve with tangent $\mathbf r_u$, and similarly for $\mathbf r_v$. A small $du\times dv$ rectangle becomes a patch that is nearly the parallelogram spanned by $\mathbf r_u\,du$ and $\mathbf r_v\,dv$. Its area is the length of a cross product:

$$dS=|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|\,du\,dv,\qquad \text{Area}(S)=\iint_D|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|\,du\,dv .$$

The cross product $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ is also a vector normal to the surface. For a graph it equals $\langle -g_x,\,-g_y,\,1\rangle$, so $dS=\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}\,dA$. For a sphere $dS=a^2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta$, which is the spherical volume element of Week 6 with $\rho=a$ and the $d\rho$ removed. For a scalar function,

$$\iint_S f\,dS=\iint_D f(\mathbf r(u,v))\,|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|\,du\,dv .$$

### Orientation and flux

An **oriented surface** has a chosen unit normal $\mathbf n$ that varies continuously. For a closed surface, the convention is the **outward** normal. The **flux** of a field $\mathbf F$ through $S$ is

$$\iint_S\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\iint_D\mathbf F(\mathbf r(u,v))\cdot(\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v)\,du\,dv ,$$

provided $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ points the way you chose. If it points the other way, put a minus sign in front, or swap the order of the parameters. The length of the cross product does the work of $dS$, because $\mathbf n\,dS=\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v\,du\,dv$.

If $\mathbf F$ is a fluid velocity in m/s, the flux is the volume of fluid crossing $S$ per second, in m$^3$/s. A positive flux means a net flow in the direction of $\mathbf n$. Reversing the orientation changes the sign of the flux.

For a graph with upward normal the flux becomes the simple double integral $\iint_D\mathbf F\cdot\langle -g_x,\,-g_y,\,1\rangle\,dA$.

@example
title: Area of a paraboloid cap
problem: Find the area of the part of the paraboloid $z=x^2+y^2$ with $z\le 1$.
step: This is a graph with $g=x^2+y^2$, so $g_x=2x$, $g_y=2y$ and $dS=\sqrt{1+4x^2+4y^2}\,dA$. The region below $z\le 1$ projects onto the disk $x^2+y^2\le 1$.
step: In polar coordinates $dS=\sqrt{1+4r^2}\;r\,dr\,d\theta$, with $0\le r\le 1$ and $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$.
step: Substitute $u=1+4r^2$, so $du=8r\,dr$: $\int_0^1 r\sqrt{1+4r^2}\,dr=\frac18\int_1^5 u^{1/2}\,du=\frac{1}{12}\left(5\sqrt5-1\right)$.
step: Multiply by $2\pi$: the area is $\dfrac{\pi}{6}\left(5\sqrt5-1\right)\approx 5.33$. That is larger than the area $\pi$ of the disk below it, as it must be for a tilted surface.

@example
title: Flux through a sphere
problem: Find the outward flux of $\mathbf F=\langle x,y,z\rangle$ through the sphere $S$ of radius $a$ centered at the origin.
step: Use $\mathbf r(\phi,\theta)=a\langle\sin\phi\cos\theta,\ \sin\phi\sin\theta,\ \cos\phi\rangle$ with $0\le\phi\le\pi$, $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$.
step: A direct computation gives $\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta=a\sin\phi\;\mathbf r$. It points outward, because $\sin\phi\ge 0$, so this order of parameters gives the right orientation.
step: On the sphere $\mathbf F=\mathbf r$, so $\mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta)=a\sin\phi\,|\mathbf r|^2=a^3\sin\phi$.
step: The flux is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\pi a^3\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta=a^3\cdot 2\cdot 2\pi=4\pi a^3$. Notice that this is $3$ times the volume $\frac43\pi a^3$ of the ball, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3$. Next week this will turn out to be no accident.

@mistakes
- Using $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ in a scalar integral or an area, instead of its length $|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|$. For flux, in contrast, the vector is dotted with $\mathbf F$ and its sign carries the orientation.
- Ignoring orientation: for a closed surface the normal points outward, and swapping $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ for $\mathbf r_v\times\mathbf r_u$ flips the sign of the flux.
- Using $dS=dA$ for a tilted surface. The factor $\sqrt{1+g_x^2+g_y^2}$ is the extra area caused by the tilt.
- Forgetting to project onto the correct parameter region $D$, for example the disk below the cap rather than the cap itself.

choice: $\langle 2u,\ 2v,\ 1\rangle$
choice: $\langle -2u,\ 2v,\ 1\rangle$
hint: Compute $\mathbf r_u=\langle 1,0,2u\rangle$ and $\mathbf r_v=\langle 0,1,2v\rangle$, then take the cross product in that order.
solution: $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=\langle 0\cdot 2v-2u\cdot 1,\ -(1\cdot 2v-2u\cdot 0),\ 1\cdot 1-0\cdot 0\rangle=\langle -2u,\,-2v,\,1\rangle$. It has a positive $z$-component, so it points upward, matching the pattern $\langle -g_x,-g_y,1\rangle$ for a graph.

check: quad2(lambda x, y: sqrt(1 + nderiv(lambda s: (6 - s - 2*y)/2, x)**2 + nderiv(lambda s: (6 - x - 2*s)/2, y)**2), 0, 6, 0, lambda x: (6 - x)/2)

prompt: Find the upward flux of $\mathbf F=\langle x,\ y,\ z\rangle$ through the part of the plane $z=1-x-y$ with $x\ge 0$, $y\ge 0$ and $x+y\le 1$.
answer: 1/2
check: quad2(lambda x, y: dot([x, y, 1 - x - y], [-nderiv(lambda s: 1 - s - y, x), -nderiv(lambda s: 1 - x - s, y), 1]), 0, 1, 0, lambda x: 1 - x)

@practice num
prompt: Find the outward flux of $\mathbf F=\langle x,\ y,\ 0\rangle$ through the lateral surface of the cylinder $x^2+y^2=4$, $0\le z\le 3$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 24pi
check: quad2(lambda t, z: dot([2*cos(t), 2*sin(t), 0], cross([nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 0*s, t)], [0, 0, 1])), 0, 2*pi, 0, 3)
hint: Parametrize with $\mathbf r(\theta,z)=\langle 2\cos\theta,\ 2\sin\theta,\ z\rangle$ and check that $\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z$ points outward.
hint: $\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z=\langle 2\cos\theta,\ 2\sin\theta,\ 0\rangle$. Dot it with $\mathbf F=\langle 2\cos\theta,\ 2\sin\theta,\ 0\rangle$.
solution: $\mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_\theta\times\mathbf r_z)=4\cos^2\theta+4\sin^2\theta=4$. The flux is $\int_0^3\int_0^{2\pi}4\,d\theta\,dz=24\pi$. Check: on the cylinder $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=2$ and the area is $2\pi\cdot 2\cdot 3=12\pi$.

answer: 32pi/3
check: quad2(lambda p, t: dot([0, 0, 2*cos(p)], cross([nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s)*cos(t), p), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s)*sin(t), p), nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(s), p)], [nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(p)*cos(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(p)*sin(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(p) + 0*s, t)])), 0, pi, 0, 2*pi)
hint: Use $\mathbf r(\phi,\theta)=2\langle\sin\phi\cos\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\phi\rangle$, for which $\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta=2\sin\phi\,\mathbf r$ points outward.
hint: On the sphere $z=2\cos\phi$, so $\mathbf F\cdot(\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta)=2\cos\phi\cdot 2\sin\phi\cdot 2\cos\phi$.
solution: The integrand is $8\cos^2\phi\sin\phi$. Then $\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^\pi 8\cos^2\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi=2\pi\cdot 8\cdot\frac23=\frac{32\pi}{3}$.

@quiz num
prompt: The helicoid is parametrized by $\mathbf r(u,v)=\langle u\cos v,\ u\sin v,\ v\rangle$ with $0\le u\le 1$ and $0\le v\le 2\pi$. Find its surface area. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi*(sqrt(2) + ln(1 + sqrt(2)))
check: quad2(lambda u, v: norm(cross([nderiv(lambda s: s*cos(v), u), nderiv(lambda s: s*sin(v), u), nderiv(lambda s: v + 0*s, u)], [nderiv(lambda s: u*cos(s), v), nderiv(lambda s: u*sin(s), v), nderiv(lambda s: s, v)])), 0, 1, 0, 2*pi)
hint: Compute $\mathbf r_u=\langle\cos v,\sin v,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf r_v=\langle -u\sin v,\ u\cos v,\ 1\rangle$, then their cross product.
hint: $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=\langle\sin v,\ -\cos v,\ u\rangle$, so $dS=\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du\,dv$. For the $u$-integral use $\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=\tfrac12\left(u\sqrt{1+u^2}+\ln(u+\sqrt{1+u^2})\right)$.
solution: Area $=2\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=2\pi\cdot\frac12\left[\sqrt2+\ln(1+\sqrt2)\right]=\pi\left(\sqrt2+\ln(1+\sqrt2)\right)\approx 7.21$.
