@article
Cut a region $R$ in the plane into many small pieces of area $\Delta A$, multiply the value of $f$ on each piece by $\Delta A$, and add. As the pieces shrink, the sums approach the **double integral**

$$\iint_R f\,dA .$$

If $f\ge 0$ it is the volume under the graph of $f$ over $R$. With $f=1$ it is the area of $R$. With $f$ a density it is the total mass. Dividing by the area gives the **average value** of $f$ over $R$.

### Iterated integrals

To compute it, slice the region. Fix $x$, integrate over the vertical slice, then add up the slices:

$$\iint_R f\,dA=\int_a^b\left(\int_{g_1(x)}^{g_2(x)}f(x,y)\,dy\right)dx .$$

The inner integral treats $x$ as a constant, and its value is a function of $x$. Two rules keep the bounds honest:

- the **outer** limits are constants;
- the **inner** limits may depend on the outer variable only.

### Bounds from a sketch

Always draw the region first. For $dy\,dx$, sweep a vertical line across the region: the outer limits are the smallest and largest $x$ reached, and for each $x$ the inner limits are where the line enters and leaves, namely the lower and upper boundary curves. For $dx\,dy$ do the same with horizontal lines, writing each boundary as $x$ in terms of $y$.

Some integrals are only possible in one order, so **reversing the order** is a real skill. Describe the same region again from the other direction; never just swap the symbols on the old limits.

### Polar coordinates

For disks, sectors and rings use $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. A small polar patch has sides $dr$ and $r\,d\theta$, so

$$dA=r\,dr\,d\theta .$$

The factor $r$ is not optional. A disk of radius $a$ becomes the rectangle $0\le r\le a$, $0\le\theta\le 2\pi$, and $x^2+y^2$ becomes $r^2$.

### Checking numerically

A numerical check is a good habit. In SciPy the function passed to `dblquad` takes the inner variable first:

```python
from scipy import integrate

# integral of x*y over 0 <= x <= 2, 0 <= y <= x
val, err = integrate.dblquad(lambda y, x: x*y, 0, 2, 0, lambda x: x)
print(round(val, 6))   # 2.0
```

@example
title: Setting up bounds from a sketch
problem: Evaluate $\iint_R (x+y)\,dA$, where $R$ is the region between $y=x^2$ and $y=x$.
step: The curves meet where $x^2=x$, at $x=0$ and $x=1$. Between those values $x^2\le x$, so the line is the upper boundary and the parabola the lower boundary.
step: Sweeping vertical lines gives $0\le x\le 1$ and $x^2\le y\le x$, so the integral is $\int_0^1\int_{x^2}^{x}(x+y)\,dy\,dx$.
step: Inner integral, with $x$ constant: $\left[xy+\tfrac{y^2}{2}\right]_{x^2}^{x}=\tfrac32x^2-x^3-\tfrac12x^4$.
step: Outer integral: $\int_0^1\left(\tfrac32x^2-x^3-\tfrac12x^4\right)dx=\tfrac12-\tfrac14-\tfrac1{10}=\tfrac{3}{20}$.

@example
title: Reversing the order of integration
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2\int_{x/2}^{1}\cos(y^2)\,dy\,dx$.
step: The function $\cos(y^2)$ has no elementary antiderivative in $y$, so we want $dx$ on the inside. First read the region: $0\le x\le 2$ and $x/2\le y\le 1$.
step: Sketch it: a triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(2,1)$. A horizontal line at height $y\in[0,1]$ enters at $x=0$ and leaves at the line $y=x/2$, which is $x=2y$.
step: The integral becomes $\int_0^1\int_0^{2y}\cos(y^2)\,dx\,dy=\int_0^1 2y\cos(y^2)\,dy$.
step: With $u=y^2$ this is $\int_0^1\cos u\,du=\sin 1\approx 0.841$.

@mistakes
- Giving the outer integral limits that depend on the inner variable, or the inner integral constant limits when the boundary is a curve.
- Forgetting the factor $r$ when converting to polar coordinates.
- Reversing the order by swapping the symbols on the old limits instead of describing the region again from a sketch.
- Evaluating the inner integral as if the outer variable were also changing, instead of holding it constant.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2\int_0^3 (x^2+y)\,dy\,dx$.
answer: 17
check: quad2(lambda x, y: x**2 + y, 0, 2, 0, 3)
hint: Do the inner integral in $y$ first, treating $x$ as a constant.
hint: The inner integral equals $3x^2+\tfrac92$. Now integrate in $x$ from $0$ to $2$.
solution: $\int_0^3(x^2+y)\,dy=3x^2+\frac92$, and $\int_0^2\left(3x^2+\frac92\right)dx=8+9=17$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\iint_R xy\,dA$, where $R$ is the triangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(2,0)$ and $(2,2)$.
answer: 2
check: quad2(lambda x, y: x*y, 0, 2, 0, lambda x: x)
hint: The top edge is the line $y=x$. Describe $R$ as $0\le x\le 2$, $0\le y\le x$.
hint: The inner integral is $\int_0^x xy\,dy=\tfrac{x^3}{2}$.
solution: $\int_0^2\int_0^x xy\,dy\,dx=\int_0^2\frac{x^3}{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{16}{4}=2$.

@practice mc
prompt: The integral $\displaystyle\int_0^4\int_{\sqrt x}^{2}f(x,y)\,dy\,dx$ is taken over a region of the plane. Which iterated integral over the same region, with $dx$ on the inside, is correct?
choice*: $\displaystyle\int_0^2\int_0^{y^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_0^2\int_0^{\sqrt y}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_0^4\int_0^{y^2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$
choice: $\displaystyle\int_0^2\int_{y^2}^{4}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$
hint: Sketch the region: for $0\le x\le 4$, $y$ runs from the curve $y=\sqrt x$ up to the line $y=2$.
hint: Slice horizontally. For a fixed $y$ between $0$ and $2$, solve $y=\sqrt x$ for $x$ to find where each slice ends.
solution: The region lies between the curve $y=\sqrt x$, which is $x=y^2$, and the line $y=2$, for $0\le x\le 4$. A horizontal slice at height $y\in[0,2]$ runs from $x=0$ to $x=y^2$. The choice with inner upper limit $\sqrt y$ uses $\sqrt y$ instead of $y^2$, the choice with outer limits $0$ to $4$ lets $y$ range up to $4$ (it stops at $2$), and the choice with inner limits $y^2$ to $4$ describes the complementary region.

@practice num
prompt: Let $D$ be the disk $x^2+y^2\le 9$. Evaluate $\iint_D (x^2+y^2)\,dA$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 81pi/2
check: quad2(lambda x, y: x**2 + y**2, -3, 3, lambda x: -sqrt(9 - x**2), lambda x: sqrt(9 - x**2))
hint: Use polar coordinates: $x^2+y^2=r^2$ and $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$.
hint: The integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 r^2\cdot r\,dr\,d\theta$.
solution: $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^3 r^3\,dr\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot\frac{3^4}{4}=\frac{81\pi}{2}$.

@practice num
prompt: Find the average value of $f(x,y)=x$ over the half-disk $x^2+y^2\le 1$, $x\ge 0$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 4/(3pi)
check: quad2(lambda x, y: x, 0, 1, lambda x: -sqrt(1 - x**2), lambda x: sqrt(1 - x**2))/quad2(lambda x, y: 1, 0, 1, lambda x: -sqrt(1 - x**2), lambda x: sqrt(1 - x**2))
hint: Average value is $\frac{1}{\text{area}}\iint_R f\,dA$. The half-disk has area $\pi/2$.
hint: In polar coordinates the half-disk is $0\le r\le 1$, $-\pi/2\le\theta\le\pi/2$, and $x=r\cos\theta$.
solution: $\iint x\,dA=\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\theta\,d\theta\int_0^1 r^2\,dr=2\cdot\frac13=\frac23$. Dividing by the area $\frac\pi2$ gives $\frac{4}{3\pi}\approx 0.424$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\int_y^1 e^{x^2}\,dx\,dy$. Give an exact answer.
answer: (e-1)/2
check: quad2(lambda x, y: exp(x**2), 0, 1, 0, lambda x: x)
hint: $e^{x^2}$ has no elementary antiderivative in $x$, so reverse the order.
hint: The region is $0\le y\le x\le 1$. Slicing vertically, $0\le x\le 1$ and $0\le y\le x$.
solution: $\int_0^1\int_0^x e^{x^2}\,dy\,dx=\int_0^1 xe^{x^2}\,dx=\frac12\left(e-1\right)$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\iint_D e^{-(x^2+y^2)}\,dA$, where $D$ is the disk $x^2+y^2\le\ln 4$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 3pi/4
check: quad2(lambda x, y: exp(-(x**2 + y**2)), -sqrt(log(4)), sqrt(log(4)), lambda x: -sqrt(log(4) - x**2), lambda x: sqrt(log(4) - x**2))
hint: This integral is only practical in polar coordinates, where $x^2+y^2=r^2$.
hint: $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\sqrt{\ln 4}}e^{-r^2}\,r\,dr\,d\theta$. Substitute $u=r^2$ in the inner integral.
solution: The inner integral is $\frac12\left(1-e^{-\ln 4}\right)=\frac12\left(1-\frac14\right)=\frac38$. Multiplying by $2\pi$ gives $\frac{3\pi}{4}$.
