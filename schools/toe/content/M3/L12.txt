@article
The fundamental theorem of calculus says that the integral of a derivative over an interval equals the values at the boundary. This week the same pattern appears for fields: **a derivative of a field integrated over a region equals the field integrated over the boundary.**

### The divergence theorem

Let $E$ be a solid whose boundary $S$ is a closed surface oriented outward, and let $\mathbf F$ have continuous first partial derivatives on an open region containing $E$. Then

$$\oiint_S\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=\iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV .$$

The idea comes from Week 8: divergence measures how fast a small blob of fluid expands. Add up all the expansion inside, and you get the net outflow across the boundary.

### Stokes' theorem

Let $S$ be an oriented surface whose boundary is a closed curve $\partial S$. Orient $\partial S$ by the right-hand rule: with your thumb along $\mathbf n$, your fingers curl in the direction of travel. Then

$$\oint_{\partial S}\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\iint_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS .$$

Circulation around the edge equals the total rotation of the field across the surface. The tiny circulations of neighboring patches cancel along their shared edges and only the outer edge survives. When $S$ is flat in the $xy$-plane, with the curve counterclockwise, this is the planar identity $\oint P\,dx+Q\,dy=\iint(Q_x-P_y)\,dA$.

### Consequences

- $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$, so the flux of a curl through any closed surface is $0$.
- $\nabla\times\nabla f=\mathbf 0$, so the circulation of a gradient around a closed curve is $0$ (Week 10).
- If $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0$ throughout a solid, the flux through its boundary surface is $0$.

### Choosing the easier side

Many surface integrals are hard while the corresponding volume integral is simple. If the surface is open, close it with a lid, apply the theorem, and subtract the flux through the lid. Stokes lets you replace a complicated surface by a simpler one with the same boundary curve.

### What can go wrong

The theorems need their hypotheses. The inverse-square field $\mathbf F=\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3$ has $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=0$ everywhere except the origin, yet its outward flux through any sphere around the origin is $4\pi$. The theorem does not apply because $\mathbf F$ is not smooth inside the ball. Always check that the surface is closed, the normal points outward, and the field is smooth throughout the region.

@example
title: Verifying the divergence theorem on a cube
problem: Verify the divergence theorem for $\mathbf F=\langle x^2,\ y^2,\ z^2\rangle$ and the unit cube $0\le x,y,z\le 1$.
step: Volume side: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2x+2y+2z$, so $\iiint_E\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV=2\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac12+\tfrac12\right)=3$.
step: Surface side, face $x=1$: here $\mathbf n=\langle 1,0,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=x^2=1$. The face has area $1$, so its flux is $1$.
step: Face $x=0$: $\mathbf n=\langle -1,0,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=-x^2=0$, so its flux is $0$.
step: The faces $y=1$, $y=0$, $z=1$ and $z=0$ behave the same way, giving $1,0,1,0$. The total flux is $3\times 1=3$.
step: Both sides equal $3$, so the theorem checks out on this example.

@example
title: Verifying Stokes' theorem on a paraboloid
problem: Let $\mathbf F=\langle -y,\ x,\ z^2\rangle$ and let $S$ be the part of $z=x^2+y^2$ with $z\le 1$, with upward normal. Verify Stokes' theorem.
step: Surface side. The curl is $\nabla\times\mathbf F=\langle 0-0,\ 0-0,\ 1-(-1)\rangle=\langle 0,0,2\rangle$.
step: For the upward normal, $\mathbf n\,dS=\langle -2x,-2y,1\rangle\,dA$, so $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot\mathbf n\,dS=2\,dA$ over the unit disk. The surface integral is $2\pi$.
step: Boundary side. The edge is the circle $\mathbf r(t)=\langle\cos t,\sin t,1\rangle$. It runs counterclockwise as seen from above, which matches an upward normal by the right-hand rule.
step: On the circle $\mathbf F=\langle-\sin t,\cos t,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf r'=\langle-\sin t,\cos t,0\rangle$, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r'=1$ and $\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi$.
step: Both sides equal $2\pi$.

@mistakes
- Applying the divergence theorem to an open surface without adding the lid, or using the inward normal.
- Ignoring a singularity of the field inside the region, such as the origin for $\mathbf r/|\mathbf r|^3$.
- Orienting the boundary curve in Stokes' theorem the wrong way, which flips the sign of one side.
- Integrating $\nabla\times\mathbf F$ over $S$ without dotting it with the normal $\mathbf n\,dS$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $\mathbf F=\langle y^2,\ 3x\rangle$ and let $C$ be the circle $x^2+y^2=4$ traversed counterclockwise. Use Stokes' theorem in its planar form to find $\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 12pi
check: quad(lambda t: dot([(2*sin(t))**2, 3*2*cos(t)], [nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s), t)]), 0, 2*pi)

@practice self
prompt: Verify the divergence theorem for $\mathbf F=\langle x,\ y,\ z^2\rangle$ and the solid cylinder $E$: $x^2+y^2\le 1$, $0\le z\le 2$. Compute the volume integral, then the flux through the lateral surface, the top and the bottom, and compare.
solution: Volume side: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1+1+2z=2+2z$. In cylindrical coordinates, $\iiint_E(2+2z)\,dV=\int_0^2(2+2z)\,\pi\,dz=\pi\left[2z+z^2\right]_0^2=8\pi$. Surface side. Lateral surface: $\mathbf n=\langle x,y,0\rangle$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=x^2+y^2=1$; the area is $2\pi\cdot 1\cdot 2=4\pi$, so the flux is $4\pi$. Top $z=2$: $\mathbf n=\langle 0,0,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=z^2=4$; the area is $\pi$, so the flux is $4\pi$. Bottom $z=0$: $\mathbf n=\langle 0,0,-1\rangle$ and $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=-z^2=0$, so the flux is $0$. The total is $4\pi+4\pi+0=8\pi$, which equals the volume integral.
rubric: Computes $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=2+2z$ and evaluates the volume integral to $8\pi$.
rubric: Finds the outward normal on each of the three pieces (lateral, top, bottom), with the bottom pointing downward.
rubric: Computes the three fluxes ($4\pi$, $4\pi$, $0$) and adds them.
rubric: States that the two sides agree, and notes that the surface was closed and the field is smooth.
hint: The cylinder has three boundary pieces. Find $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n$ on each.
hint: On the lateral surface $\mathbf n=\langle x,y,0\rangle$ for the unit radius, so $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf n=x^2+y^2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $S$ be the closed surface made of the paraboloid $z=x^2+y^2$, $0\le z\le 4$, and the disk $x^2+y^2\le 4$ at $z=4$. Find the outward flux of $\mathbf F=\langle x^3,\ y^3,\ 3z\rangle$ through $S$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 56pi
check: quad2(lambda r, t: dot([(r*cos(t))**3, (r*sin(t))**3, 3*r**2], cross([nderiv(lambda s: r*cos(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: r*sin(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: r**2 + 0*s, t)], [nderiv(lambda s: s*cos(t), r), nderiv(lambda s: s*sin(t), r), nderiv(lambda s: s**2, r)])), 0, 2, 0, 2*pi) + quad2(lambda r, t: 12*r, 0, 2, 0, 2*pi)
hint: Use the divergence theorem in cylindrical coordinates. The solid is $r^2\le z\le 4$, $0\le r\le 2$.
hint: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=3x^2+3y^2+3=3r^2+3$. The integral is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^2\int_{r^2}^4(3r^2+3)\,r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$.
solution: $\int_{r^2}^4 dz=4-r^2$, and $(3r^2+3)(4-r^2)\,r=-3r^5+9r^3+12r$. Then $\int_0^2(-3r^5+9r^3+12r)\,dr=-32+36+24=28$, so the flux is $2\pi\cdot 28=56\pi$.
