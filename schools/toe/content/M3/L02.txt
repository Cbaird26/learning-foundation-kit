@article
A function of two variables $z=f(x,y)$ gives a height above each point of the plane, so its graph is a surface. A **level curve** $f(x,y)=c$ is a contour line of that surface seen from above.

### Slicing the surface

To see how $f$ changes with $x$ alone, freeze $y=b$. Then $g(x)=f(x,b)$ is an ordinary one-variable function; its graph is the **trace** of the surface in the plane $y=b$. The slope of the trace at $x=a$ is the **partial derivative**:

$$f_x(a,b)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a,b)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a+h,b)-f(a,b)}{h}.$$

In the same way, $f_y(a,b)=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{f(a,b+h)-f(a,b)}{h}$.

The working rule: **differentiate with respect to one variable and treat every other variable as a constant.** For $f=x^2y+\sin(xy)$,

$$f_x=2xy+y\cos(xy),\qquad f_y=x^2+x\cos(xy).$$

### Linear approximation

Close to a point $(a,b)$ the surface looks like its tangent plane:

$$f(x,y)\approx f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)\,(x-a)+f_y(a,b)\,(y-b).$$

Read it as "start at the known height, then add the slope in the $x$-direction times the $x$-step, and the slope in the $y$-direction times the $y$-step." The approximation is good for small steps when the partial derivatives are continuous, as they are for the functions in this lesson.

### Second and mixed partials

Differentiating again gives $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$ and the mixed partials $f_{xy}=(f_x)_y$ and $f_{yx}=(f_y)_x$. If the second partials are continuous, then $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$. For the function above both equal $2x+\cos(xy)-xy\sin(xy)$. Computing both orders is a cheap way to catch an algebra slip.

### Curves on a surface

Freeze $y=b$ and let $x=t$. The curve

$$\mathbf r(t)=\langle t,\ b,\ f(t,b)\rangle$$

lies on the surface, and its velocity is $\mathbf r'(t)=\langle 1,\ 0,\ f_x(t,b)\rangle$. So the partial derivative is exactly the rise-over-run of a tangent vector to the surface. This links last week's curves to this week's slopes: parametrize, differentiate, read the tangent.

@example
title: Partial derivatives and a tangent-plane estimate
problem: Let $f(x,y)=x^2y+y^3$. Find $f_x$ and $f_y$ at $(1,2)$, write the linear approximation there, and use it to estimate $f(1.1,\,1.9)$.
step: Treat $y$ as constant: $f_x=2xy$, so $f_x(1,2)=4$.
step: Treat $x$ as constant: $f_y=x^2+3y^2$, so $f_y(1,2)=1+12=13$. Also $f(1,2)=2+8=10$.
step: The linear approximation is $f(x,y)\approx 10+4(x-1)+13(y-2)$.
step: With $x-1=0.1$ and $y-2=-0.1$ we get $10+0.4-1.3=9.1$.
step: Compare with the exact value $f(1.1,1.9)=1.21\cdot 1.9+6.859=9.158$. The estimate is off by about $0.06$, which is reasonable for steps of size $0.1$.

@example
title: A tangent vector from a trace
problem: On the surface $z=x^2y+y^3$, the plane $x=1$ cuts out a curve. Parametrize it by $y=t$ and find its velocity at $t=2$.
step: With $x=1$ and $y=t$ the height is $z=t+t^3$, so $\mathbf r(t)=\langle 1,\ t,\ t+t^3\rangle$.
step: Differentiate each component: $\mathbf r'(t)=\langle 0,\ 1,\ 1+3t^2\rangle$.
step: At $t=2$ the velocity is $\langle 0,\ 1,\ 13\rangle$.
step: Interpret: the last entry is $13=f_y(1,2)$, the slope of the trace, as the theory predicts.

@mistakes
- Letting the "constant" variable vary: writing $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(x^2y)=2xy+x^2$ instead of $2xy$.
- Mixing up notation. In $f_{xy}$ most books differentiate with respect to $x$ first, but some reverse it. For smooth functions both orders agree, so this is harmless here, but stay consistent.
- Leaving out the $f(a,b)$ term in the linear approximation, or using it for steps that are not small.
- Stopping at a partial derivative formula when a number at a specific point is requested; substitute the point at the end.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x,y)=3x^2y-y^3$. Find $f_y(2,1)$.
answer: 9
check: nderiv(lambda y: 3*2**2*y - y**3, 1)
hint: Treat $x$ as a constant and differentiate with respect to $y$.
hint: $f_y=3x^2-3y^2$. Now substitute $x=2$ and $y=1$.
solution: $f_y=3x^2-3y^2$, so $f_y(2,1)=12-3=9$.

@practice expr
prompt: Find $f_x$ for $f(x,y)=x\,e^{xy}$.
vars: x, y
lead: $f_x =$
answer: (1 + x*y)*e^(x*y)
check: lambda x, y: nderiv(lambda t: t*exp(t*y), x)
domain: x: 0.2..1.5; y: 0.2..1.5
hint: Use the product rule in $x$: both the factor $x$ and the factor $e^{xy}$ depend on $x$.
hint: By the chain rule with $y$ constant, $\frac{\partial}{\partial x}e^{xy}=y\,e^{xy}$.
solution: $f_x=1\cdot e^{xy}+x\cdot y\,e^{xy}=(1+xy)\,e^{xy}$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x,y)=x^3y^2-4xy$. Find the mixed partial derivative $f_{xy}$ at the point $(1,2)$.
answer: 8
check: nderiv(lambda y: nderiv(lambda x: x**3*y**2 - 4*x*y, 1), 2)
hint: First find $f_x$, treating $y$ as constant.
hint: $f_x=3x^2y^2-4y$. Now differentiate that with respect to $y$ and substitute.
solution: $f_x=3x^2y^2-4y$, so $f_{xy}=6x^2y-4$ and $f_{xy}(1,2)=12-4=8$. Check the other order: $f_y=2x^3y-4x$ and $f_{yx}=6x^2y-4$, the same.

@practice num
prompt: Let $f(x,y)=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$. Use the tangent-plane approximation at $(3,4)$ to estimate $f(3.03,\,3.96)$. Give the linear estimate, not the exact value of $f$, to three decimal places.
answer: 4.986
tol: 0.0001
check: sqrt(3**2 + 4**2) + nderiv(lambda x: sqrt(x**2 + 4**2), 3)*0.03 + nderiv(lambda y: sqrt(3**2 + y**2), 4)*(-0.04)
hint: At $(3,4)$ we have $f=5$. Find $f_x=x/\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $f_y$ there.
hint: $f_x(3,4)=3/5$ and $f_y(3,4)=4/5$. The steps are $\Delta x=0.03$ and $\Delta y=-0.04$.
solution: $f\approx 5+\tfrac35(0.03)+\tfrac45(-0.04)=5+0.018-0.032=4.986$. (The exact value is about $4.98623$.)

@practice tuple
prompt: The surface $z=x^3+xy^2$ meets the plane $y=1$ in a curve. Parametrize it as $\mathbf r(t)=\langle t,\ 1,\ f(t,1)\rangle$ and give its velocity vector at $t=2$.
answer: (1, 0, 13)
check: (nderiv(lambda s: s, 2), nderiv(lambda s: 1, 2), nderiv(lambda s: s**3 + s*1**2, 2))
hint: Write the third component as a function of $t$ alone: $f(t,1)=t^3+t$.
hint: Differentiate each component of $\langle t,\ 1,\ t^3+t\rangle$ and substitute $t=2$.
solution: $\mathbf r(t)=\langle t,1,t^3+t\rangle$ and $\mathbf r'(t)=\langle 1,0,3t^2+1\rangle$, so $\mathbf r'(2)=\langle 1,0,13\rangle$. The last entry is $f_x(2,1)=3\cdot 4+1=13$, as it should be.

@quiz expr
prompt: Find $f_y$ for $f(x,y)=\ln(x^2+y^2)$.
vars: x, y
lead: $f_y =$
answer: 2*y/(x^2 + y^2)
check: lambda x, y: nderiv(lambda t: log(x**2 + t**2), y)
hint: The derivative of $\ln u$ is $u'/u$. Here $u=x^2+y^2$ and $x$ is held constant.
hint: With $x$ constant, $u_y=2y$.
solution: $f_y=\dfrac{2y}{x^2+y^2}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these functions satisfy $f_{xx}+f_{yy}=0$ at every point of the plane?
choice*: $x^2-y^2$
choice*: $e^x\sin y$
choice: $x^2+y^2$
choice: $e^{x+y}$
hint: Compute $f_{xx}$ and $f_{yy}$ separately for each candidate, then add.
solution: For $x^2-y^2$: $2+(-2)=0$. For $e^x\sin y$: $e^x\sin y-e^x\sin y=0$. For $x^2+y^2$: $2+2=4$. For $e^{x+y}$: $e^{x+y}+e^{x+y}=2e^{x+y}$. Only $x^2-y^2$ and $e^x\sin y$ give zero.
