title: Flux through a closed surface, two ways
overview: You will compute the outward flux of a vector field through a closed surface in two independent ways: directly, as a sum of surface integrals over each smooth piece, and with the divergence theorem as a volume integral. The two answers must agree, and you will confirm them numerically with Python. The default problem uses the field $\mathbf F=\langle x,\ y,\ z^2\rangle$ and the solid cone $E$ bounded by the cone $z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and the flat lid $z=3$.
goal: Parametrize each piece of a closed surface, find its outward normal, and compute the flux through it.
goal: Choose coordinates and justify the bounds for the volume integral in the divergence theorem.
goal: Reconcile the two calculations, explaining every sign and orientation decision, and confirm both numerically.
step: Sketch $E$ and mark its two boundary pieces, the cone and the lid, with arrows for the outward normal on each. Say which coordinates suit the solid and why.
step: Divergence side. Compute $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$, write the volume integral in cylindrical coordinates with bounds you can justify from the sketch ($0\le\theta\le 2\pi$, $0\le r\le 3$, $r\le z\le 3$), and evaluate it.
step: Direct side, lid. Parametrize the disk $z=3$, $r\le 3$, give its outward normal, and compute the flux through it.
step: Direct side, cone. Parametrize $\mathbf r(r,\theta)=\langle r\cos\theta,\ r\sin\theta,\ r\rangle$ for $0\le r\le 3$, compute $\mathbf r_r\times\mathbf r_\theta$, decide whether it points outward or inward, fix the sign if needed, and compute the flux. Explain in words what the sign of this flux means.
step: Add the two direct fluxes and compare with the divergence-side value. If they differ, check each piece in turn; the orientation of the cone's normal is the usual culprit.
step: Verify numerically in Python (for example with `scipy.integrate.dblquad` for each surface piece and `tplquad` for the volume), and print the exact and numerical values side by side.
step: Extension. Add the curl field $\nabla\times\langle 0,0,xy\rangle=\langle x,\,-y,\,0\rangle$ to $\mathbf F$. Use the divergence theorem to predict the new total flux before computing anything, then check the two pieces directly.

### Week by week

| Week | Key results | Method to remember |
|---|---|---|
| 1 Space curves | $\mathbf v=\mathbf r'$, $\mathbf T=\mathbf v/\text{speed}$, $L=\int\text{speed}\,dt$ | Differentiate componentwise; integrate the length of $\mathbf r'$ |
| 2 Partial derivatives | $f_x,f_y$ are slopes of traces; $f\approx f(a,b)+f_x\Delta x+f_y\Delta y$ | Hold the other variables constant; check $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ |
| 3 Gradient, chain rule | $D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$; $\nabla f$ is perpendicular to level sets; $\frac{d}{dt}f(\mathbf r)=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf r'$ | Unit vector first; one product per path |
| 4 Constrained optimization | $\nabla f=\lambda\nabla g$ with $g=k$; $\lambda=dm/dk$ | List every candidate and compare values |
| 5 Double integrals | Iterated integrals; $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$ | Sketch first; outer limits are constants |
| 6 Triple integrals | $dV=r\,dz\,dr\,d\theta$ and $\rho^2\sin\phi\,d\rho\,d\phi\,d\theta$ | Choose coordinates by symmetry |
| 7 Coordinate changes | $dA$ is the absolute value of $\partial(x,y)/\partial(u,v)$ times $du\,dv$ | Make the region a rectangle; use the reciprocal rule |
| 8 Vector fields | $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F$, $\nabla\times\mathbf F$; trace of $D\mathbf F$ is the divergence | Divergence is a scalar, curl is a vector |
| 9 Line integrals | $\int f\,ds$ uses the speed; $\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ uses $\mathbf r'$ | Parametrize, substitute, dot, integrate |
| 10 Conservative fields | $\int\nabla f\cdot d\mathbf r=f(B)-f(A)$; curl test needs a simply connected domain | Build the potential one component at a time |
| 11 Surface integrals | $dS$ is the length of $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$; flux uses the vector itself | Check the orientation |
| 12 Theorems | Divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem | Close open surfaces; check hypotheses |

### Self-check

- I can parametrize a segment, circle, helix or intersection of surfaces, and compute velocity, unit tangent and arc length.
- I can find a directional derivative and a tangent plane, solve a Lagrange problem and say what $\lambda$ means.
- I can pick coordinates from the symmetry of a region and justify every bound from a sketch.
- I can compute a Jacobian and read its size and sign.
- I can compute divergence, curl, work, circulation, area and flux, and state the orientation I used.
- I can test path independence on a stated domain and find a potential.
- I can state both big theorems with their hypotheses and verify one on an example.

### Most common errors

- Forgetting a Jacobian factor: $r$, $\rho^2\sin\phi$, or the absolute value of a general Jacobian.
- Using a direction vector that is not a unit vector, or confusing $\nabla f$ with its length.
- Reversing the order of integration by swapping limits instead of redrawing the region.
- Concluding that zero curl means conservative on a domain with a hole.
- Choosing the wrong orientation for a flux or a Stokes boundary, or leaving the lid off an open surface.
- Taking the length of $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ in a flux integral, or forgetting the length in an area.

@exam tuple
prompt: The temperature in a room is $T(x,y,z)=xy+z$ degrees Celsius, with lengths in meters. A drone flies along the helix $\mathbf r(t)=\langle 2\cos t,\ 2\sin t,\ t\rangle$ for $0\le t\le\pi$. Find (a) the rate of change of temperature per meter traveled at $t=\pi/2$, and (b) the total change in temperature between $t=0$ and $t=\pi$. Enter (a), (b) in that order, as exact values.
answer: (-3/sqrt(5), pi)
check: (nderiv(lambda t: 4*cos(t)*sin(t) + t, pi/2)/norm([nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(s), pi/2), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s), pi/2), nderiv(lambda s: s, pi/2)]), quad(lambda t: dot([2*sin(t), 2*cos(t), 1], [nderiv(lambda s: 2*cos(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: 2*sin(s), t), nderiv(lambda s: s, t)]), 0, pi))
hint: For (a), the rate per unit distance is the directional derivative along the unit tangent, which equals $\dfrac{dT/dt}{\text{speed}}$.
hint: At $t=\pi/2$ the drone is at $(0,2,\pi/2)$, where $\nabla T=\langle y,x,1\rangle=\langle 2,0,1\rangle$ and $\mathbf v=\langle -2,0,1\rangle$.
hint: For (b), $\nabla T$ is a gradient, so the total change is the value of $T$ at the end minus its value at the start.
solution: (a) At $t=\pi/2$: $\mathbf v=\langle -2,0,1\rangle$ with speed $\sqrt5$, and $\nabla T=\langle 2,0,1\rangle$. So $dT/dt=\nabla T\cdot\mathbf v=-4+1=-3$ and the rate per meter is $-3/\sqrt5\approx -1.34$ degrees per meter. (b) Along the helix $T=4\sin t\cos t+t$, so the change from $t=0$ to $t=\pi$ is $\pi-0=\pi$ degrees. This is also $\int_C\nabla T\cdot d\mathbf r$, which depends only on the endpoints. The answer is $(-3/\sqrt5,\ \pi)$.

@exam num
prompt: A solid of uniform density occupies the region inside the sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=9$ and above the cone $z=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$, that is, $0\le\rho\le 3$ and $0\le\phi\le\pi/4$ in spherical coordinates. Find the height $\bar z=\iiint z\,dV\Big/\iiint dV$ of its center of mass. Give an exact answer.
answer: 9*(2 + sqrt(2))/16
check: quad2(lambda x, y: quad(lambda z: z, sqrt(x*x + y*y), sqrt(9 - x*x - y*y)), -sqrt(4.5), sqrt(4.5), lambda x: -sqrt(4.5 - x*x), lambda x: sqrt(4.5 - x*x))/quad2(lambda x, y: sqrt(9 - x*x - y*y) - sqrt(x*x + y*y), -sqrt(4.5), sqrt(4.5), lambda x: -sqrt(4.5 - x*x), lambda x: sqrt(4.5 - x*x))
hint: Use spherical coordinates. Each of the two integrals separates into three one-variable integrals.
hint: $\iiint dV=2\pi\cdot 9\cdot(1-\cos\tfrac\pi4)$ and $\iiint z\,dV=2\pi\cdot\int_0^{\pi/4}\cos\phi\sin\phi\,d\phi\cdot\int_0^3\rho^3\,d\rho$.
hint: Simplify with $\dfrac{1}{2-\sqrt2}=\dfrac{2+\sqrt2}{2}$.
solution: $V=\int_0^{2\pi}d\theta\int_0^{\pi/4}\sin\phi\,d\phi\int_0^3\rho^2\,d\rho=2\pi\cdot\left(1-\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)\cdot 9=9\pi(2-\sqrt2)$. For the moment, $z=\rho\cos\phi$ gives $\iiint z\,dV=2\pi\cdot\tfrac14\cdot\tfrac{81}{4}=\tfrac{81\pi}{8}$. So $\bar z=\dfrac{81/8}{9(2-\sqrt2)}=\dfrac{9}{8(2-\sqrt2)}=\dfrac{9(2+\sqrt2)}{16}\approx 1.92$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $S$ be the upper hemisphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=4$, $z\ge 0$, oriented away from the origin, and let $\mathbf F=\langle x,\ y,\ 3-z\rangle$. Find the flux of $\mathbf F$ through $S$ by closing the surface with the flat disk $D$ in the plane $z=0$ and using the divergence theorem. Then confirm your answer by computing the flux through $S$ directly in spherical coordinates. State the orientation you use on each piece.
solution: The closed surface $S\cup D$ bounds the half-ball $E$. The outward normal is $\mathbf r/2$ on $S$ and $-\mathbf k$ on $D$. Since $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1+1-1=1$, the total outward flux is $\iiint_E 1\,dV=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac43\pi\cdot 2^3=\tfrac{16\pi}{3}$. On $D$, $\mathbf F\cdot(-\mathbf k)=-(3-0)=-3$ and the area is $4\pi$, so the flux through $D$ is $-12\pi$. Hence the flux through $S$ is $\tfrac{16\pi}{3}-(-12\pi)=\tfrac{52\pi}{3}$. Direct check: with $\mathbf r=2\langle\sin\phi\cos\theta,\sin\phi\sin\theta,\cos\phi\rangle$ we have $\mathbf r_\phi\times\mathbf r_\theta=2\sin\phi\,\mathbf r$, which points outward. Also $\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf r=x^2+y^2+3z-z^2=4+3z-2z^2$ with $z=2\cos\phi$. So the flux is $\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi/2}(4+6\cos\phi-8\cos^2\phi)\,2\sin\phi\,d\phi\,d\theta=2\pi\cdot 2\left[4+3-\tfrac83\right]=\tfrac{52\pi}{3}$.
rubric: Closes the surface with the disk and gives the outward normal on each piece, with $-\mathbf k$ on the disk.
rubric: Computes $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=1$ and the half-ball volume $\frac{16\pi}{3}$.
rubric: Computes the flux through the disk as $-12\pi$, with the correct sign, and subtracts it to get $\frac{52\pi}{3}$.
rubric: Confirms the value by a direct spherical-coordinates calculation, or explains clearly how that check works.
