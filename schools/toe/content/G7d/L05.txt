@article
The early universe was hot, dense and very nearly in thermal equilibrium, so a handful of numbers (a temperature and a list of species) determine its state. The cosmological questions then become: how fast does the temperature fall, and when do interactions become too slow to keep up?

### Conventions: natural units

In this lesson and the next, $\hbar = c = k_B = 1$. Temperatures are quoted as energies ($1\ \mathrm{MeV}\leftrightarrow 1.160\times10^{10}$ K), and times are converted using $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-25}$ GeV s. The Planck mass here is $M_{\rm Pl} = G^{-1/2} = 1.221\times10^{19}$ GeV (the reduced Planck mass used for inflation in week 11 is smaller by $\sqrt{8\pi}$). Metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$ as before.

### Radiation and degrees of freedom

For relativistic species in equilibrium the energy and entropy densities are
$$\rho = \frac{\pi^2}{30}\,g_*\,T^4,\qquad s = \frac{2\pi^2}{45}\,g_{*s}\,T^3,$$
where each boson contributes its number of internal states $g$ and each fermion contributes $\tfrac78 g$. (If a species has its own temperature $T_i$, weight it by $(T_i/T)^4$ in $g_*$ and $(T_i/T)^3$ in $g_{*s}$.) Typical values:

- above about 200 GeV, every Standard Model particle counts: $g_* = 106.75$;
- at a few MeV: photons, $e^\pm$ and three neutrino species give $g_* = 2 + \tfrac78(4+6) = 10.75$;
- today: $g_* = 3.36$ and $g_{*s} = 3.91$.

### Time and temperature

Inserting $\rho$ into the Friedmann equation of week 3 for a flat universe gives
$$H = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi^3 g_*}{45}}\,\frac{T^2}{M_{\rm Pl}} \simeq 1.66\,\sqrt{g_*}\,\frac{T^2}{M_{\rm Pl}} .$$
In radiation domination $t = 1/(2H)$, which in convenient units is
$$t \approx \frac{2.42\ \mathrm{s}}{\sqrt{g_*}}\left(\frac{1\ \mathrm{MeV}}{T}\right)^{2}.$$
Conservation of entropy $s\,a^3$ means $T\propto g_{*s}^{-1/3}a^{-1}$, so $T\propto 1/a$ except when species annihilate and change $g_{*s}$. The estimate $t = 1/2H$ ignores changes in $g_*$ and is good to tens of percent across such transitions.

### Freeze-out

A reaction with rate $\Gamma$ per particle keeps a species in equilibrium while $\Gamma \gg H$. When $\Gamma$ falls below $H$, the average particle never meets another before the universe has expanded appreciably: the species **freezes out**. For weak interactions $\Gamma\sim G_F^2T^5$ falls much faster than $H\propto T^2$, so neutrinos decouple when $\Gamma\simeq H$, giving
$$T_{\rm dec}\sim\left(\frac{1.66\sqrt{g_*}}{G_F^2 M_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{1/3}\sim 1\ \mathrm{MeV}.$$

### Neutrinos are colder than photons

Shortly after neutrinos decouple, $e^\pm$ pairs annihilate (their abundance drops once $T\lesssim m_e = 0.511$ MeV) and dump their entropy into photons only. The plasma's entropy is conserved: $g_{*s}$ falls from $2 + \tfrac78\cdot 4 = 5.5$ to $2$, so $a T_\gamma$ rises by $(11/4)^{1/3}$ while $aT_\nu$ stays constant:
$$\frac{T_\nu}{T_\gamma} = \left(\frac{4}{11}\right)^{1/3} = 0.714,\qquad T_{\nu,0}\approx 1.945\ \mathrm{K}.$$

### A map of the early universe (approximate)

| event | temperature | time |
|---|---|---|
| electroweak crossover | $\sim 160$ GeV | $\sim 10^{-11}$ s |
| QCD crossover (quarks to hadrons) | $\sim 150$ MeV | $\sim 10^{-5}$ s |
| neutrino decoupling | $\sim 1$ MeV | $\sim 1$ s |
| $e^\pm$ annihilation | $\sim 0.5$ to $0.1$ MeV | $\sim 10$ s |
| nucleosynthesis | $\sim 0.1$ to $0.07$ MeV | $\sim 3$ min |
| matter–radiation equality | $\sim 0.8$ eV | $z\approx 3400$ |
| recombination | $\sim 0.26$ eV | $z\approx 1090$ |

Above the electroweak scale the particle content is a model assumption; below about 100 MeV the history is tied to laboratory physics.

@example
title: Time and temperature
problem: Using $H = 1.66\sqrt{g_*}\,T^2/M_{\rm Pl}$ with $M_{\rm Pl} = 1.221\times10^{19}$ GeV and $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-25}$ GeV s, find the cosmic time at $T = 1$ MeV ($g_*=10.75$) and at $T = 100$ GeV ($g_* = 106.75$).
step: At $T = 10^{-3}$ GeV: $H = 1.66\times 3.279\times10^{-6}/(1.221\times10^{19}) = 4.46\times10^{-25}$ GeV.
step: In radiation domination $t = 1/(2H) = 1.12\times10^{24}\ \mathrm{GeV^{-1}}$. Multiply by $\hbar$ to convert: $t = 1.12\times10^{24}\times 6.582\times10^{-25}\ \mathrm{s} = 0.74$ s.
step: At $T = 100$ GeV: $H = 1.66\times 10.33\times 10^{4}/(1.221\times10^{19}) = 1.40\times10^{-14}$ GeV, so $t = 1/(2H) = 3.6\times10^{13}\ \mathrm{GeV^{-1}} = 2.3\times10^{-11}$ s.
step: Check the scaling: $t\propto g_*^{-1/2}T^{-2}$, so from 1 MeV to 100 GeV the time drops by $10^{-10}\times\sqrt{10.75/106.75}\approx 3\times10^{-11}$, consistent with $0.74\ \mathrm{s}\times 3.2\times10^{-11}$.

@example
title: The neutrino temperature today
problem: Use entropy conservation to show that $T_\nu/T_\gamma = (4/11)^{1/3}$ after $e^\pm$ annihilation, and compute the neutrino temperature today for $T_0 = 2.7255$ K.
step: After neutrinos decouple they free-stream, so $T_\nu\propto 1/a$ exactly. The photon–$e^\pm$ plasma conserves its own entropy: $g_{*s}\,(aT)^3$ is constant.
step: Before annihilation the plasma has $g_{*s} = 2 + \tfrac78\times4 = 11/2$ and temperature $T_{\rm before}$. After, only photons remain: $g_{*s}=2$.
step: Equating, $\tfrac{11}{2}(aT_{\rm before})^3 = 2(aT_\gamma)^3$, so $T_\gamma = (11/4)^{1/3}\,T_{\rm before}$. Neutrinos kept $T_\nu = T_{\rm before}$ (in the sense of the same $a$-scaling), so $T_\nu/T_\gamma = (4/11)^{1/3} = 0.714$.
step: Today $T_{\nu,0} = 0.714\times 2.7255\ \mathrm{K} = 1.945$ K, which corresponds to $k_BT_\nu\approx 1.7\times10^{-4}$ eV.

@mistakes
- Using $T\propto 1/a$ across an annihilation epoch without accounting for the change in $g_{*s}$.
- Applying $t = 1/(2H)$ with a wrong $g_*$, or forgetting that the formula holds only during radiation domination.
- Mixing units: in natural units $H$ comes out in GeV and must be converted to s$^{-1}$ with $\hbar$.
- Counting a fermion's degrees of freedom without the factor $7/8$, or forgetting antiparticles and spin states (an $e^\pm$ pair gives $g=4$; each neutrino species gives $g=2$ for $\nu$ plus $\bar\nu$).

@practice mc

choice*: The interaction rate $\Gamma\propto T^5$ falls faster than the expansion rate $H\propto T^2$, so eventually $\Gamma<H$.
choice: The universe stops expanding when it reaches $T\sim 1$ MeV.
choice: The Hubble rate $H$ falls faster than the interaction rate, so $H$ becomes negligible.
choice: Neutrinos become too massive to interact at low temperature.
hint: Compare how the two rates scale with $T$ in radiation domination.
solution: In radiation domination $H\propto T^2$ while the weak rate scales roughly as $T^5$. Since $T^5$ falls faster, the ratio $\Gamma/H\propto T^3$ drops below 1 at $T\sim 1$ MeV.
