@article
"Dark matter" is first of all a statement about gravity: there is more mass than light accounts for. What that mass is made of is a separate question that the data have not yet answered.

### Conventions and inputs

SI units with $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$ m$^3$ kg$^{-1}$ s$^{-2}$, $M_\odot = 1.989\times10^{30}$ kg, $1\ \mathrm{kpc} = 3.0857\times10^{19}$ m. Approximate standard values, to be checked against current data: $\Omega_c h^2\approx0.120$, $\Omega_b h^2\approx 0.0224$, so non-baryonic matter is about $5/6$ of all matter.

### Independent lines of evidence

1. **Rotation curves.** For a circular orbit $v_c^2 = GM(<r)/r$. Outside the stellar disk the visible mass is nearly constant, which predicts $v_c\propto r^{-1/2}$, but measured curves stay flat. A flat curve means $M(<r)\propto r$ and $\rho\propto r^{-2}$.
2. **Clusters.** Galaxy velocity dispersions, X-ray gas temperatures (assuming hydrostatic balance) and lensing give masses several times the baryonic mass (baryon fraction about 0.15).
3. **Gravitational lensing.** For a point-like lens the Einstein angle is
$$\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\,\frac{D_{LS}}{D_L D_S}},$$
where $D$ are angular-diameter distances to the lens, the source, and between them. Weak-lensing shear maps the mass directly.
4. **Colliding clusters.** In the Bullet Cluster the lensing mass peaks sit apart from the hot X-ray gas, which contains most of the visible baryons: most mass is collisionless and does not trace the gas.
5. **CMB, BBN and growth.** Big bang nucleosynthesis fixes $\Omega_b h^2\approx0.022$; the CMB peak heights need $\Omega_m h^2\approx0.14$; and perturbations of size $10^{-5}$ could not have grown to galaxies without matter that decoupled from the photons (week 8).

What these results establish is mass that behaves like cold collisionless matter on large scales. What they do not establish is its identity. Candidates include weakly interacting massive particles, axions, sterile neutrinos and primordial black holes (heavily constrained over much of the mass range). Direct-detection experiments have so far reported no confirmed signal and have set increasingly strong limits, and indirect searches are not conclusive. Modified-gravity proposals have not yet reproduced the rotation curves, lensing, clusters, CMB and structure growth in a single framework; that comparison is an active research question, not a closed one.

### Cold, warm and hot

A relic particle that is still relativistic when structure forms free-streams out of small perturbations and erases them. Neutrinos are the known example:
$$\Omega_\nu h^2 = \frac{\sum m_\nu}{93.14\ \mathrm{eV}} .$$
Oscillation data require $\sum m_\nu\gtrsim 0.06$ eV, and cosmological data give an upper limit: CMB data alone give roughly $\sum m_\nu\lesssim0.25$ eV, and adding baryon acoustic oscillation surveys tightens this to roughly $0.12$ eV (about $0.07$ eV with the most recent surveys), close to the oscillation minimum (check current limits), so neutrinos are at most about 1% of the matter and cannot be the dark matter. They do suppress small-scale power by roughly $8f_\nu$, where $f_\nu = \Omega_\nu/\Omega_m$. **Cold** dark matter has negligible velocities, so structure forms on all scales and hierarchically: small halos first, merging into larger ones. **Warm** dark matter would erase dwarf-galaxy-scale structure, and small-scale data push thermal warm-dark-matter masses to a few keV or higher.

### Halos

Spherical collapse of a matter perturbation gives the linear threshold $\delta_c\approx1.686$ in a matter-dominated universe. Halos are characterized by the radius $r_{200}$ inside which the mean density is $200\rho_c$:
$$M_{200} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\,200\,\rho_c\,r_{200}^3,\qquad v_{200}^2 = \frac{GM_{200}}{r_{200}} .$$
Simulations are well fit by the Navarro–Frenk–White (NFW) profile (an empirical fit, not a derivation)
$$\rho(r) = \frac{\rho_s}{(r/r_s)(1 + r/r_s)^2},\qquad M(<r) = 4\pi\rho_s r_s^3\left[\ln(1+x) - \frac{x}{1+x}\right],\quad x = \frac{r}{r_s},$$
with concentration $c = r_{200}/r_s\sim5$ to $15$.

@example
title: Mass from a flat rotation curve
problem: A spiral galaxy has a flat circular speed $v_c = 200$ km/s out to $r = 20$ kpc. Estimate the enclosed mass in solar masses, and the mass enclosed at $40$ kpc if the curve stays flat.
step: For a circular orbit, gravity supplies the centripetal force: $v_c^2/r = GM/r^2$, so $M(<r) = v_c^2r/G$.
step: Convert: $v_c = 2.0\times10^{5}$ m/s and $r = 20\times3.0857\times10^{19} = 6.171\times10^{20}$ m.
step: Then $M = (4.0\times10^{10})(6.171\times10^{20})/(6.674\times10^{-11}) = 3.70\times10^{41}$ kg $= 1.86\times10^{11}M_\odot$.
step: A flat curve means $M(<r)\propto r$, so at $40$ kpc the enclosed mass doubles to about $3.7\times10^{11}M_\odot$. The stars and gas of such a galaxy total a few $\times10^{10}M_\odot$, so most of the enclosed mass is not visible.

@example
title: Neutrinos as a small component
problem: Take $\sum m_\nu = 0.06$ eV and $\Omega_m h^2 = 0.1424$. Find $\Omega_\nu h^2$ and the neutrino fraction $f_\nu$ of the matter, and the neutrino mass sum that would make neutrinos all of $\Omega_c h^2 = 0.120$.
step: Neutrino density: $\Omega_\nu h^2 = 0.06/93.14 = 6.4\times10^{-4}$.
step: Fraction: $f_\nu = 6.4\times10^{-4}/0.1424 = 0.0045$, under half a percent. The suppression of small-scale power is of order $8f_\nu\approx 4\%$.
step: For neutrinos to make up all of $\Omega_c h^2 = 0.120$ we would need $\sum m_\nu = 0.120\times93.14 = 11.2$ eV, about a hundred times above the cosmological limit.
step: Conclusion: known neutrinos are a subdominant part of the matter budget. The dark matter must be something else, and "something else" is all that cosmology alone currently says.

@mistakes
- Treating a flat rotation curve or a lensing map as the detection of a particle; they show mass, not its nature.
- Using visible light as a tracer of mass in clusters and lensing, instead of the dynamics or the shear.
- Confusing the neutrino mass sum with the mass of one species, or forgetting that cosmological limits are model dependent.
- Assuming the NFW profile is exact; it is a fit with scatter, and its inner slope is an open comparison with observations.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following are lines of evidence for dark matter that do not depend on a specific particle model?
choice*: Flat rotation curves of spiral galaxies.
choice*: Cluster masses from lensing that exceed the baryonic mass.
choice*: CMB acoustic peak heights that require $\Omega_m h^2$ well above $\Omega_b h^2$.
choice: A confirmed laboratory detection of a dark matter particle.

hint: Evidence of this kind is gravitational or cosmological; none of it measures the particle's properties.
solution: Rotation curves, lensing masses and the CMB all show the gravitational effects of non-baryonic matter. No laboratory detection has been confirmed, and nucleosynthesis constrains the baryon density but says nothing about weakly interacting massive particles.
