@article
Everything so far described the smooth universe. Real structure is a small departure from smoothness, and the method is to split every field into a background that depends only on time and a perturbation that depends on position as well.

### Background versus perturbation

Write $\rho(\mathbf x, t) = \bar\rho(t)\,[1 + \delta(\mathbf x, t)]$ and likewise for the potential, velocities and temperature. The **background** ($a(t)$, $H(t)$, $\bar\rho(t)$, $\bar T(t)$) obeys the Friedmann equations. The **perturbations** ($\delta$, $\Phi$, $\delta T$) obey equations obtained by expanding Einstein's equations to first order in small quantities; this is **linear theory**, valid while $|\delta|\ll1$. The CMB has $\delta T/T\sim10^{-5}$, safely linear; galaxies today have $\delta\gg1$ and need nonlinear methods.

### Conventions

Comoving coordinate $\mathbf x$ so that proper position is $a\mathbf x$; $\nabla$ is the comoving gradient; $H = \dot a/a$; peculiar velocity $\mathbf v$; metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$. Fiducial values (approximate, check against current data): $\Omega_m\approx0.315$, $\Omega_\Lambda\approx0.685$, $H_0\approx67.4$ km/s/Mpc, $n_s\approx0.965$, $\sigma_8\approx 0.81$.

### The growth equation

For pressureless matter on scales well inside the horizon, Newtonian physics in comoving coordinates suffices:
$$\dot\delta + \frac1a\nabla\!\cdot\mathbf v = 0,\qquad \dot{\mathbf v} + H\mathbf v = -\frac1a\nabla\Phi,\qquad \nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\bar\rho\,a^2\delta .$$
Eliminating $\mathbf v$ and $\Phi$ gives
$$\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = 4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta .$$
The right side is gravity pulling overdensities together; the term $2H\dot\delta$ is Hubble friction, which slows growth. The equation has no $k$-dependence, so every Fourier mode $\delta_k$ grows by the same factor.

### Solutions

In a matter-dominated flat universe $H = 2/(3t)$ and $4\pi G\bar\rho = \tfrac32H^2 = 2/(3t^2)$. Try $\delta\propto t^p$:
$$p(p-1) + \tfrac43 p = \tfrac23\ \Longrightarrow\ p = \tfrac23\ \text{or}\ p = -1 .$$
The **growing mode** is $\delta_+\propto t^{2/3}\propto a$ and the **decaying mode** is $\delta_-\propto t^{-1}\propto a^{-3/2}$. During radiation domination, sub-horizon dark-matter perturbations grow only logarithmically because $H$ is set by the radiation (the Mészáros effect). With a cosmological constant, growth slows at late times. The solution is $\delta\propto D(a)$ with
$$D(a) = \frac52\,\Omega_m H_0^2\,H(a)\int_0^a\frac{da'}{[a'H(a')]^3},$$
normalized so that $D\to a$ early on. For $\Omega_m=0.3$, $\Omega_\Lambda=0.7$, $D(1)\approx0.78$. The growth rate $f\equiv d\ln D/d\ln a$ is well approximated by $\Omega_m(a)^{0.55}$ in $\Lambda$CDM.

### Horizon scales and the power spectrum

The comoving Hubble radius is $c/(aH)$. A mode with comoving wavenumber $k$ is super-horizon for $k\ll aH/c$ and enters the horizon when $k = aH/c$. Modes that enter during the radiation era have their growth suppressed, so the matter power spectrum $P(k)\propto k^{n_s}T^2(k)$ turns over near the horizon scale at equality,
$$k_{\rm eq} = \frac{a_{\rm eq}H_{\rm eq}}{c}\approx 0.01\ \mathrm{Mpc^{-1}},$$
rising as $k^{n_s}$ at lower $k$ and falling roughly as $k^{n_s-4}$, with logarithmic corrections, at higher $k$. The turnover position depends on $\Omega_m h^2$, which is how large-scale structure measures matter density.

### Why baryons alone are not enough

Before recombination baryons are coupled to photons and cannot collapse. Afterwards a perturbation of size $\sim10^{-5}$ grows like $D(a)$, about a factor of $860$ in $\Lambda$CDM, to $\delta\sim0.01$ today, far short of the $\delta\gg1$ seen in galaxies. Collisionless dark matter, which does not feel the photons, starts growing earlier. This is a qualitative argument; a full numerical calculation, beyond this course, makes it quantitative.

@example
title: Growing and decaying modes
problem: Show that in a flat matter-only universe the solutions of $\ddot\delta + 2H\dot\delta = 4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta$ are $\delta\propto t^{2/3}$ and $\delta\propto t^{-1}$.
step: In the flat matter-only universe $a\propto t^{2/3}$, so $H = 2/(3t)$. The Friedmann equation gives $4\pi G\bar\rho = \tfrac32H^2 = \tfrac32\cdot\tfrac{4}{9t^2} = \tfrac{2}{3t^2}$.
step: Substitute $\delta = t^p$: $\ddot\delta = p(p-1)t^{p-2}$ and $2H\dot\delta = \tfrac43 p\,t^{p-2}$. The equation becomes $p(p-1) + \tfrac43p = \tfrac23$.
step: Rearrange to $p^2 + \tfrac13p - \tfrac23 = 0$, equivalently $3p^2 + p - 2 = 0$, which factorizes as $(3p-2)(p+1)=0$.
step: Therefore $p = 2/3$ (growing, $\propto a$) or $p = -1$ (decaying, $\propto a^{-3/2}$). Only the growing mode matters after a few expansion times.

@example
title: How much can linear growth do?
problem: A baryon density perturbation has $\delta = 10^{-5}$ at recombination ($z=1100$). Estimate its linear value today if it grows like $D(a)$ with $D(1)=0.78$ and $D\simeq a$ at $z=1100$.
step: At $z=1100$, $D = a = 1/1101$. Today $D(1) = 0.78$.
step: The growth factor is $D(1)/D(a_*) = 0.78\times1101 = 859$.
step: Therefore $\delta_0 = 10^{-5}\times 859 \approx 0.009$.
step: This is far below $\delta\sim1$, the scale at which structure becomes nonlinear. Hence the argument for collisionless dark matter, which can start growing before recombination. The argument is simplified, since baryon perturbations also oscillate acoustically before recombination.

@mistakes
- Mixing up comoving and proper wavenumbers, or applying the sub-horizon (Newtonian) equation to super-horizon modes.
- Continuing to use linear theory when $\delta\sim1$; linear extrapolation tells you where structure becomes nonlinear, not what happens after.
- Forgetting the Hubble friction term $2H\dot\delta$. Without it the growth is far too fast: exponential in a static medium, and $t^{1.46}$ instead of $t^{2/3}$ in a matter-dominated universe.
- Treating $\delta T/T\sim10^{-5}$ as a failure of homogeneity; homogeneity describes the background, and perturbations are small deviations.

choice: $\ddot\delta + 4\pi G\bar\rho\,\delta = 0$

hint: Gravity should amplify an overdensity, and expansion should act as friction.
solution: The gravitational source term has the sign that drives growth, and the $2H\dot\delta$ term is the friction from expansion. The wrong-sign version would oscillate, and removing the friction (as in a static, non-expanding medium) would give exponential rather than power-law growth.

@practice self
prompt: A colleague argues: "The CMB temperature fluctuations are about $10^{-5}$, so the universe is clearly not homogeneous and the Friedmann equations cannot apply." Respond using the distinction between background and perturbation: say what is homogeneous, what is not, which equations govern each, and when linear theory stops being valid.
hint: Ask which quantity depends only on $t$ and which depends on $\mathbf x$ as well, and compare the size of $\delta$ with 1.
solution: The background quantities $a(t)$, $H(t)$, $\bar\rho(t)$ and $\bar T(t)$ are the spatial averages and depend only on time; they obey the Friedmann equations. The inhomogeneity is carried by small perturbations $\delta(\mathbf x,t)$, $\Phi(\mathbf x,t)$ and $\delta T(\mathbf x,t)$ with $|\delta|\sim10^{-5}\ll1$, which obey equations linearized about the background. Homogeneity is a statement about the statistics of the perturbations and the background, not about every point being identical. At recombination the perturbations are tiny, so the background description is excellent. As $\delta$ grows and approaches 1 (galaxies and clusters today), linear theory fails and nonlinear methods such as N-body simulations are needed, while the average expansion is still governed by the Friedmann equations.
rubric: Identifies the background ($a$, $H$, $\bar\rho$, $\bar T$) as functions of time only and the Friedmann equations as their equations.
rubric: Identifies the perturbations ($\delta$, $\Phi$, $\delta T$) as small deviations obeying linearized equations.
rubric: States that linear theory requires $|\delta|\ll1$ and fails for galaxies and clusters today, without invalidating the background.
