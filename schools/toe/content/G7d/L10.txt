@article
Acceleration is the one cosmological fact that general relativity with only matter and radiation cannot explain. Whatever drives it must have an effective equation of state $w<-1/3$ (week 3). This lesson is about how to describe such a component, compute its consequences, and be honest about what the observations can distinguish.

### Evidence, and what it is evidence for

Type Ia supernovae are fainter at a given redshift than a decelerating universe predicts, baryon acoustic oscillations measure $D_M/r_d$ and $H r_d$ at several redshifts, and the CMB fixes the geometry and $\Omega_m h^2$. Together they favour a nearly flat universe with $\Omega_\Lambda\approx0.69$ (approximate; check current values). That is evidence for a component with $w$ near $-1$, not for any particular theory.

### Parametrizations

Take $w = p/(\rho c^2)$ for the dark-energy component.

- **Cosmological constant:** $w=-1$ and constant density.
- **wCDM:** constant $w$, so $\rho_{\rm DE}(a) = \rho_{{\rm DE},0}\,a^{-3(1+w)}$.
- **CPL (Chevallier–Polarski–Linder):** $w(a) = w_0 + w_a(1-a)$, giving
$$\rho_{\rm DE}(a) = \rho_{{\rm DE},0}\,a^{-3(1+w_0+w_a)}\,e^{-3w_a(1-a)} .$$
CPL is a two-parameter description of data, not a physical theory. At high redshift $w\to w_0 + w_a$.

For a flat universe with matter and dark energy (radiation neglected at low $z$),
$$E^2(z) = \Omega_m(1+z)^3 + (1-\Omega_m)\,(1+z)^{3(1+w)} .$$

### Scalar fields

A canonical scalar field $\phi$ with potential $V(\phi)$ (quintessence) has, in natural units,
$$\rho_\phi = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + V,\qquad p_\phi = \tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - V,\qquad w_\phi = \frac{\tfrac12\dot\phi^2 - V}{\tfrac12\dot\phi^2 + V}\ \ge\ -1 .$$
When the kinetic energy is small compared with $V$ (slow roll), $w\to-1$. A canonical field cannot have $w<-1$ ("phantom" behaviour), nor cross $w=-1$; that requires non-canonical kinetic terms, modified gravity or an interacting dark sector. The same equation of state governs inflation in week 11, with very different energy scales.

### The cosmological-constant problem

The observed dark-energy density is $\rho_\Lambda c^2\approx 5\times10^{-10}$ J/m$^3$, an energy scale $\rho^{1/4}\approx 2$ meV. Naive estimates of vacuum energy from quantum field theory with a cutoff near the Planck scale exceed this by about 120 orders of magnitude. This is unresolved, and it is one reason to test whether $w$ really equals $-1$ exactly.

### Degeneracies

Distances depend on integrals of $1/E(z)$, so very different parameter sets give almost the same distances over a range of redshifts. The CMB fixes $\theta_*$, which leaves a line of degenerate $(\Omega_m, H_0, w)$. Supernovae constrain relative distances; the absolute magnitude $M$ is degenerate with $H_0$ unless calibrated. BAO need the sound horizon $r_d$ from early-universe physics. Probes with different degeneracy directions are combined, and conclusions should say which data and which model class they rest on.

### Quoting significance

For two independent Gaussian estimates $x_1\pm\sigma_1$ and $x_2\pm\sigma_2$, the tension is $|x_1-x_2|/\sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}$ standard deviations. As illustrative round numbers, a distance-ladder value $H_0 = 73.0\pm1.0$ and a CMB-inferred value $67.4\pm0.5$ (km/s/Mpc, within $\Lambda$CDM) differ by about $5\sigma$ (the Hubble tension). It may reflect unrecognized systematics or new physics; no single dark-energy model has been shown to resolve it without creating other tensions. Some recent combinations of BAO, CMB and supernova data prefer $w_0>-1$, $w_a<0$ at roughly the 2 to 4$\sigma$ level, but the preference depends on the supernova sample and the priors. That is a hint to be tested, not an established detection, so check the current literature.

@example
title: The equation of state of a scalar field
problem: A quintessence field has kinetic energy $K=\tfrac12\dot\phi^2$ and potential energy $V$. Find $w$ when $K/V = 0.1$, and the ratio $K/V$ that gives $w=-0.9$.
step: From the definition, $w = (K-V)/(K+V)$. Divide numerator and denominator by $V$: $w = (K/V - 1)/(K/V + 1)$.
step: For $K/V = 0.1$: $w = (0.1-1)/(0.1+1) = -0.9/1.1 = -0.818$.
step: Solve for the ratio: $w(K/V + 1) = K/V - 1$ gives $K/V = (1+w)/(1-w)$.
step: For $w=-0.9$: $K/V = 0.1/1.9 = 0.053$. A field that rolls slowly enough to give $w$ within 10% of $-1$ needs kinetic energy below about 5% of its potential energy.

@example
title: How much does w change the expansion rate?
problem: For a flat universe with $\Omega_m = 0.3$ and no radiation, compare $E(z=1)$ for $w=-1$ and $w=-0.9$ with the same $\Omega_m$ and $H_0$.
step: Use $E^2 = \Omega_m(1+z)^3 + (1-\Omega_m)(1+z)^{3(1+w)}$ at $z=1$, so $(1+z)=2$.
step: For $w=-1$ the dark-energy term is constant: $E^2 = 0.3\times8 + 0.7 = 3.1$, so $E = 1.761$.
step: For $w=-0.9$: $2^{3(0.1)} = 2^{0.3} = 1.231$, so $E^2 = 2.4 + 0.7\times1.231 = 3.262$ and $E = 1.806$.
step: The difference is $+2.6\%$ in $H(z=1)$. In a real fit, $\Omega_m$ and $H_0$ would shift to compensate partly, which is the degeneracy: the data constrain combinations, not each parameter separately.

@mistakes
- Stating that a best-fit $w$ close to $-1$ proves a cosmological constant; it shows consistency with $\Lambda$ at the available precision.
- Treating the CPL parameters $w_0,w_a$ as properties of a physical model rather than coefficients of a fit.
- Forgetting that a canonical scalar field cannot have $w<-1$, so a preference for $w<-1$ points to something other than simple quintessence.
- Quoting a significance in $\sigma$ without saying which data, which model and which priors it assumes, or treating correlated estimates as independent.

prompt: A quintessence field has kinetic energy equal to $20\%$ of its potential energy, $K = 0.2V$. Find its equation-of-state parameter $w$. Give three significant figures.
answer: -0.667
check: (0.2 - 1)/(0.2 + 1)
tol: 0.5%
hint: Use $w=(K-V)/(K+V)$.
solution: $w = (0.2V - V)/(0.2V + V) = -0.8/1.2 = -0.667$.

@practice num
prompt: A dark-energy component has constant $w=-0.8$. By what factor is its density at $z=1$ larger than today? Give four significant figures.
answer: 1.516
check: exp(quad(lambda z: 3*(1 - 0.8)/(1 + z), 0, 1))
tol: 0.5%
hint: For constant $w$, $\rho\propto a^{-3(1+w)} = (1+z)^{3(1+w)}$.
solution: $(1+z)^{3(1+w)} = 2^{3(0.2)} = 2^{0.6} = 1.516$.

@practice num
prompt: Treat a distance-ladder value $H_0 = 73.0\pm1.0$ and a CMB-inferred value $H_0 = 67.4\pm0.5$ (both in km/s/Mpc, uncorrelated Gaussian errors) as illustrative round numbers. How many standard deviations apart are they? Give two significant figures.
answer: 5.0
check: (73.0 - 67.4)/sqrt(1.0**2 + 0.5**2)

hint: The error on the difference is the quadrature sum of the individual errors.
solution: The difference is $5.6$ and its error is $\sqrt{1.0^2 + 0.5^2} = 1.118$, so the tension is $5.6/1.118 = 5.0\sigma$.

@practice self
prompt: A supernova sample is used to compare flat $\Lambda$CDM with a flat wCDM model with $w=-0.9$ at the same $\Omega_m$. The two models differ in $d_L$ by under 2% at $z=0.5$, the absolute magnitude $M$ is a free nuisance parameter, and the best fit gives $w = -0.92\pm0.08$. (a) Name the degeneracies that make the models hard to separate. (b) Say what additional data would help and why. (c) Write one sentence reporting the result that does not overclaim.
hint: A 2% change in distance is about $0.04$ mag. Ask which parameters can absorb that change, and which probes depend on different combinations.
solution: (a) A constant offset in $d_L$ is absorbed by the nuisance parameter $M$ (equivalently $H_0$), and changes in $w$ at low $z$ can be partly mimicked by changes in $\Omega_m$, so only the shape of the distance curve carries information. (b) BAO distances in several redshift bins (with $r_d$ calibrated by the early universe), the CMB acoustic scale, and an independent calibration of $M$ or $H_0$ break these degeneracies because they depend on different combinations of $\Omega_m$, $H_0$ and $w$; more supernovae at high redshift extend the lever arm. (c) "Supernovae alone give $w = -0.92\pm0.08$ in flat wCDM, consistent with $w=-1$ (a cosmological constant) at about one standard deviation, so they do not detect evolving or non-$\Lambda$ dark energy."
rubric: Names the $M$–$H_0$ (nuisance offset) degeneracy and the $\Omega_m$–$w$ degeneracy.
rubric: Proposes complementary data (BAO, CMB, a calibration of $M$ or $H_0$) and explains why they depend on different parameter combinations.
rubric: The reported sentence gives the central value, the uncertainty, the model assumed and the data used, and does not claim a detection or a proof of $\Lambda$.

@quiz num
prompt: For a flat universe with $\Omega_m=0.3$ and the same $H_0$, by what percentage is the luminosity distance to $z=0.5$ smaller in a wCDM model with $w=-0.9$ than in $\Lambda$CDM ($w=-1$)? Use $E^2 = 0.3(1+z)^3 + 0.7(1+z)^{3(1+w)}$ and integrate numerically. Give three significant figures.
answer: 1.59
check: (lambda I: 100*(1 - I(-0.9)/I(-1.0)))(lambda w: quad(lambda z: 1/sqrt(0.3*(1 + z)**3 + 0.7*(1 + z)**(3*(1 + w))), 0, 0.5))
tol: 3%
unit: %
hint: Since $H_0$, flatness and the factor $(1+z)c/H_0$ are common, the ratio of luminosity distances equals the ratio of the integrals $\int_0^{0.5}dz/E(z)$.
solution: The integrals are $0.4410$ for $w=-1$ and $0.4340$ for $w=-0.9$. Their ratio is $0.9841$, so $d_L$ is smaller by $1.59\%$ (about $0.035$ mag).

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about dark-energy models.
choice*: A canonical quintessence field has $w\ge-1$.
choice*: A cosmological constant has $w=-1$ exactly and a density that does not change with time.
choice*: The CPL form $w(a)=w_0+w_a(1-a)$ is a parametrization of data, not a physical model.
choice: A best-fit $w$ within one standard deviation of $-1$ proves that dark energy is a cosmological constant.
choice: Type Ia supernova distances alone determine $H_0$ with no calibration.
hint: Distinguish what a model forbids from what a data set merely allows.
solution: A canonical scalar has $\dot\phi^2\ge0$ so $w\ge-1$. $\Lambda$ has $w=-1$ and constant density. CPL is a fit form. Consistency with $w=-1$ does not prove $\Lambda$, and supernova distances alone fix only relative distances, with $M$ degenerate with $H_0$ unless calibrated.
