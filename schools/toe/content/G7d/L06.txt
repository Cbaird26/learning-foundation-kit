@article
Within the first few minutes the universe was a nuclear reactor with a fixed supply of fuel (protons and neutrons) and a very short operating time. A back-of-the-envelope estimate predicts that about a quarter of the baryonic mass ends up as helium-4, and that estimate is the main reason the hot big bang is taken seriously.

### Conventions

Natural units $\hbar=c=k_B=1$ as in week 5. Approximate inputs, to be checked against current data: neutron–proton mass difference $Q = m_n - m_p = 1.293$ MeV; neutron lifetime $\tau_n\approx 880$ s; deuterium binding energy $B_D = 2.224$ MeV; baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta = n_b/n_\gamma\approx 6.1\times10^{-10}$, equivalent to $\Omega_b h^2\approx 0.0224$.

### Neutron–proton balance

While weak reactions such as $n + \nu_e\leftrightarrow p + e^-$ are fast ($\Gamma>H$), the ratio of neutrons to protons follows its equilibrium value
$$\frac{n}{p} = e^{-Q/T}.$$
The rate falls faster than $H$ (week 5), so the ratio **freezes out** near $T_f\approx 0.7$ MeV with $n/p\approx 1/6$. After that only free neutron decay changes it:
$$\frac{n}{p}(t) = \left(\frac{n}{p}\right)_f e^{-t/\tau_n}.$$
By the start of nucleosynthesis, a few minutes later, $n/p\approx 1/7$.

### The deuterium bottleneck

Helium is built through deuterium, $n + p\leftrightarrow {\rm D} + \gamma$, but photons outnumber baryons by $1/\eta\approx 1.6\times10^9$. Even a tiny fraction of photons in the high-energy tail above $B_D$ is enough to destroy deuterium as fast as it forms. Deuterium survives only when
$$\frac{1}{\eta}\,e^{-B_D/T}\lesssim 1\quad\Longrightarrow\quad T\lesssim \frac{B_D}{\ln(1/\eta)}\approx 0.1\ \mathrm{MeV},$$
and a careful calculation puts the onset near $T\approx 0.07$ to $0.08$ MeV, at about 3 minutes. This is far below $B_D$, so nucleosynthesis starts later and with fewer neutrons than a naive estimate based on $B_D$ would suggest.

### Helium and the trace elements

Once deuterium forms, essentially every available neutron ends up in ${}^4$He, the most tightly bound light nucleus. Because there is no stable nucleus with $A=5$ or $A=8$, the chain stops at traces of D, ${}^3$He and ${}^7$Li. Counting nucleons, with $n/p\equiv r$,
$$Y_p = \frac{2r}{1+r},$$
which gives $Y_p = 0.25$ for $r = 1/7$.

### Predictions and data

The standard calculation depends on $\eta$ and (weakly) on the number of relativistic species. Approximate observed values: $Y_p\approx 0.245$, ${\rm D/H}\approx 2.5\times10^{-5}$, ${}^7{\rm Li/H}\approx 1.6\times10^{-10}$. Deuterium and helium agree with the prediction for the same $\eta$ that the CMB infers independently; the predicted lithium is a factor of about 3 above the observed value (the **lithium problem**). Deuterium is the best "baryometer": its abundance falls steeply as $\eta$ increases. Extra relativistic species increase $H$ at a given $T$, freezing out $n/p$ earlier and at a higher value, and so raise $Y_p$; this constrains $N_{\rm eff}$.

### Estimating an epoch from temperature and expansion

To date an event, combine two scales. The temperature gives the scale factor, $a = T_0/T$ (valid while $g_{*s}$ is constant), and radiation domination gives $H = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_r}\,a^{-2}$, so
$$t = \frac{1}{2H} = \frac{a^2}{2H_0\sqrt{\Omega_r}} .$$
This reproduces the $t\propto T^{-2}$ law of week 5 with the effective $g_*$ absorbed in $\Omega_r$. It is accurate to about one percent after $e^\pm$ annihilation ($T\lesssim0.1$ MeV). Before and during annihilation $g_*$ is larger and $T$ is not simply $T_0/a$, so it is off by up to a factor of about $2$: at $T=1$ MeV it gives $1.3$ s instead of $0.74$ s.

@example
title: From freeze-out to the helium fraction
problem: Take $T_f = 0.72$ MeV, $Q = 1.293$ MeV, $\tau_n = 880$ s, and let nucleosynthesis start after $180$ s. Estimate $Y_p$.
step: Equilibrium ratio at freeze-out: $n/p = e^{-1.293/0.72} = e^{-1.796} = 0.166\approx 1/6$.
step: Neutron decay for 180 s multiplies the ratio by $e^{-180/880} = 0.815$, so $n/p = 0.166\times0.815 = 0.135$.
step: Nearly all neutrons go into ${}^4$He, so $Y_p = 2r/(1+r) = 0.270/1.135 = 0.238$.
step: The measured value is about 0.245. The estimate is within 3%, which is why $Y_p$ is such a robust test; the remaining difference comes from the gradual freeze-out and other small effects.

@example
title: Dating the nucleosynthesis epoch
problem: Estimate the cosmic time at which $T = 0.08$ MeV using $T_0 = 2.7255$ K, $k_B = 8.617\times10^{-5}$ eV/K, $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc and $\Omega_r = 9.2\times10^{-5}$.
step: Present temperature in energy units: $k_BT_0 = 8.617\times10^{-5}\times 2.7255 = 2.349\times10^{-4}$ eV. Then $1+z = T/T_0 = 8\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{eV}/2.349\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{eV} = 3.41\times10^{8}$, so $a = 2.94\times10^{-9}$.
step: Convert $H_0$ to s$^{-1}$: $67.4/(3.0857\times10^{19}) = 2.184\times10^{-18}$ s$^{-1}$. Then $H_0\sqrt{\Omega_r} = 2.184\times10^{-18}\times 9.59\times10^{-3} = 2.095\times10^{-20}$ s$^{-1}$.
step: The expansion rate is $H = 2.095\times10^{-20}\times(3.41\times10^8)^2 = 2.43\times10^{-3}$ s$^{-1}$.
step: Then $t = 1/(2H) \approx 206$ s, about 3.4 minutes, in agreement with the $g_*$ formula of week 5 ($2.42/\sqrt{3.36}\times(0.08)^{-2}\approx 206$ s) and with the "three minutes" quoted for nucleosynthesis.

@mistakes
- Using $B_D = 2.2$ MeV as the temperature of nucleosynthesis; the huge photon-to-baryon ratio delays it to about 0.07 to 0.1 MeV.
- Forgetting neutron decay between freeze-out and nucleosynthesis, or applying it for the wrong time.
- Quoting $Y_p$ as a number fraction; here it is a mass fraction, $Y_p = 2r/(1+r)$.
- Treating the lithium discrepancy as a "detection" of new physics; astrophysical and nuclear systematics are among the candidate explanations.

choice*: There are about $10^9$ photons per baryon, so photodissociation by the high-energy tail of the photon distribution destroys deuterium until $T$ is well below $B_D$.

choice: Neutrinos are still in equilibrium and keep breaking up deuterium.

solution: The photon distribution has a tail above $B_D$ even when $T\ll B_D$. Because there are so many photons per baryon, deuterium photodissociates until $\eta^{-1}e^{-B_D/T}\lesssim1$, which happens near $T\approx B_D/\ln(1/\eta)\approx 0.1$ MeV.

@quiz num
prompt: At freeze-out $n/p = 1/6$. Nucleosynthesis starts $240$ s later, and free neutrons decay with $\tau_n = 880$ s in the meantime. Estimate $Y_p = 2r/(1+r)$. Give three significant figures.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following statements about standard nucleosynthesis are correct?
choice*: Increasing the baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta$ decreases the predicted deuterium-to-hydrogen ratio.
choice*: Increasing $\eta$ makes the deuterium bottleneck break at a higher temperature, so nucleosynthesis starts earlier.
choice*: Additional relativistic species at the time of freeze-out raise the predicted $Y_p$.
choice: The predicted $Y_p$ is independent of the number of relativistic species.
choice: Stable nuclei with mass number 5 and 8 allow nucleosynthesis to produce abundant carbon and oxygen.
hint: Think of how each change moves $T_f$, the photon-to-baryon ratio, and the bottleneck.
solution: More baryons per photon convert deuterium into helium more efficiently (D/H falls) and let deuterium survive at higher $T$.
