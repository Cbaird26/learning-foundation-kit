@article
Homogeneity and isotropy fix the geometry of space up to one number (the curvature) and one function of time (the scale factor). Picture a grid drawn on a balloon that is being inflated. The grid labels, the **comoving coordinates**, never change, but the physical distance between grid points grows in proportion to the balloon's radius. That radius is the **scale factor** $a(t)$.

### The metric

With signature $(-,+,+,+)$ and the normalization $a(t_0)=1$, the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker (FLRW) line element is
$$ds^2 = -c^2dt^2 + a^2(t)\left[d\chi^2 + S_K^2(\chi)\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\varphi^2\right)\right],$$
where $t$ is **cosmic time**, $\chi$ is the radial comoving coordinate (a length), and
$$S_K(\chi)=\begin{cases}\sin(\sqrt{K}\,\chi)/\sqrt{K}, & K>0\ \text{(closed)}\\[2pt] \chi, & K=0\ \text{(flat)}\\[2pt] \sinh(\sqrt{-K}\,\chi)/\sqrt{-K}, & K<0\ \text{(open)}.\end{cases}$$
The constant $K$ has units of length$^{-2}$ and is the curvature of space today. A **comoving observer** has fixed $(\chi,\theta,\varphi)$, so $d\tau = dt$ along its worldline: cosmic time is the proper time of observers at rest in the expansion.

### Redshift from the metric

Two successive wavecrests leave a comoving source at $t_e$ and $t_e+\delta t_e$ and arrive at $t_0$ and $t_0+\delta t_0$. A radial light ray has $c\,dt = -a\,d\chi$, so the comoving path is the same for both and $\delta t_e/a_e = \delta t_0/a_0$. Wavelengths scale like time intervals, so
$$1+z = \frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}}{\lambda_{\rm emit}} = \frac{a(t_0)}{a(t_e)} = \frac{1}{a_e}.$$
Cosmological redshift is the stretching of wavelengths with the scale factor, not a Doppler shift of a photon moving through space. The two agree to first order at low $z$.

### The Hubble parameter and the distances

Define $H \equiv \dot a/a$. For a source at redshift $z$:

- **Proper distance** between comoving points at time $t$: $d_{\rm prop} = a(t)\chi$, so $\dot d_{\rm prop} = H d_{\rm prop}$.
- **Comoving distance**: $\displaystyle \chi = c\int_{t_e}^{t_0}\frac{dt}{a} = c\int_0^z \frac{dz'}{H(z')}$.
- **Transverse comoving distance**: $D_M = S_K(\chi)$, equal to $\chi$ when flat.
- **Luminosity distance**, defined by $F = L/(4\pi d_L^2)$: $d_L = (1+z)D_M$.
- **Angular-diameter distance**, defined by $\theta = \ell/d_A$ for proper size $\ell$ at emission: $d_A = D_M/(1+z)$.

Together these give the distance-duality relation $d_L = (1+z)^2 d_A$. For a flat universe $d_A = a_e\chi$ is the proper distance at the moment of emission.

### Horizons and the Hubble sphere

The **particle horizon** $\chi_p = c\int_0^t dt'/a$ is the farthest comoving distance from which a signal could have reached us. The **Hubble sphere** $c/H$ is a different thing: galaxies beyond it have $H d_{\rm prop} > c$, but this is not a relative velocity in a local inertial frame, so special relativity is not violated. Light emitted from beyond the Hubble sphere can still reach us, because the Hubble sphere itself moves. What limits the observable region is the particle horizon (in the past) and the event horizon (in the future), not $c/H$.

### Practical cautions

Always name the distance. $d_A$ is not monotonic in $z$ (for the benchmark model it peaks near $z\approx 1.6$), $d_L$ is what supernova fluxes measure, and $D_M$ is what BAO and CMB angles use. Keep a short conventions sheet: signature, $a(t_0)=1$, the definition of each distance, and the units.

@example
title: Distances in an Einstein–de Sitter universe
problem: In a flat, matter-only universe (derived in week 3) $H(z) = H_0(1+z)^{3/2}$. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $c = 299792$ km/s, find $D_M$, $d_L$ and $d_A$ for a source at $z=3$.
step: In a flat universe $D_M = \chi = (c/H_0)\int_0^z (1+z')^{-3/2}\,dz'$.
step: The integral is $\left[-2(1+z')^{-1/2}\right]_0^3 = 2(1 - 1/2) = 1$, so $\chi = c/H_0 = 299792/70 \approx 4283$ Mpc.
step: Luminosity distance: $d_L = (1+z)D_M = 4\times 4283 \approx 17{,}130$ Mpc.
step: Angular-diameter distance: $d_A = D_M/(1+z) = 4283/4 \approx 1071$ Mpc, and indeed $d_L = (1+z)^2 d_A = 16\times 1071$.

@example
title: Proper distance, angle and flux for one galaxy
problem: A galaxy at $z=2$ has comoving distance $\chi = 6000$ Mpc in a flat universe. Find its proper distance now and at emission, its $d_A$ and $d_L$, and the angle subtended by a $30$ kpc structure.
step: The scale factor at emission is $a_e = 1/(1+z) = 1/3$.
step: Proper distance now is $a_0\chi = 6000$ Mpc; at emission it was $a_e\chi = 2000$ Mpc.
step: Flat, so $D_M = 6000$ Mpc. Then $d_A = 6000/3 = 2000$ Mpc (equal to the proper distance at emission) and $d_L = 3\times 6000 = 18{,}000$ Mpc.
step: The angle is $\theta = \ell/d_A = 0.030/2000 = 1.5\times10^{-5}$ rad $\approx 3.1$ arcsec.

@mistakes
- Using $d_L$ where an angle needs $d_A$ (or $D_M$ for a comoving ruler); the two differ by factors of $(1+z)^2$.
- Treating $c/H$ as the horizon, or $z$ as a Doppler velocity at high redshift.
- Forgetting the curvature function: for $K\neq 0$ the transverse distance is $S_K(\chi)$, not $\chi$.
- Mixing proper and comoving lengths, for example comparing a comoving BAO scale with a proper size without the factor $a$.

@quiz num
prompt: A closed universe has curvature radius $R = 10{,}000$ Mpc, that is $K = 1/R^2$. A source lies at comoving distance $\chi = 3000$ Mpc. Find the transverse comoving distance $D_M = S_K(\chi)$ in Mpc.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about FLRW cosmology.
choice*: Comoving observers have fixed $(\chi,\theta,\varphi)$, so their proper time equals cosmic time $t$.
choice*: A galaxy beyond the Hubble sphere has $H d_{\rm prop} > c$, and this does not violate special relativity.
choice*: In a flat universe the luminosity distance is $d_L = (1+z)\chi$.
choice: The cosmological redshift is a relativistic Doppler shift from the relative velocity of the source at emission, valid at any $z$.
choice: The angular-diameter distance increases monotonically with redshift.
hint: Check each statement against the metric: what does $ds^2$ reduce to along a comoving worldline, and along a light ray?
solution: Along a comoving worldline $ds^2 = -c^2dt^2$, so $d\tau = dt$. Recession faster than $c$ is not a relative velocity in a local inertial frame. For flat space $D_M = \chi$ and $d_L = (1+z)D_M$. Redshift is stretching by $a$, not a Doppler shift, and $d_A$ has a maximum (near $z\approx 1.6$ in the benchmark model, $z=1.25$ in Einstein–de Sitter).
