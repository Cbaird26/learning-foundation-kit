@article
Cosmology has one sky, one vantage point and no repeatable experiments, so the first skill is bookkeeping: which numbers did a telescope record, and which came out of a model?

### Conventions for this course

Unless stated otherwise: SI units (or km/s, Mpc and Gyr where customary); metric signature $(-,+,+,+)$; scale factor normalized to $a(t_0)=1$ today; $H_0 = 100\,h\ \mathrm{km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}$; $1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 3.0857\times10^{22}$ m; $c = 2.998\times10^{5}$ km/s.

The fiducial parameter values are approximate standard values from CMB-based fits to flat $\Lambda$CDM. Check them against current data before relying on them: $H_0\approx 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_m\approx 0.315$, $\Omega_\Lambda\approx 0.685$, $\Omega_b h^2\approx 0.0224$, $\Omega_c h^2\approx 0.120$, $T_0\approx 2.7255$ K, $n_s\approx 0.965$, $A_s\approx 2.1\times10^{-9}$. Local distance-ladder measurements give $H_0\approx 73$; this disagreement, the Hubble tension, returns in week 10. Every numerical problem here states the values it uses.

### Measured versus inferred

| Measured | Inferred through a model |
|---|---|
| wavelengths of spectral lines, hence the redshift $z$ | recession velocity, distance, look-back time |
| flux $F$ or apparent magnitude $m$ | luminosity $L$, absolute magnitude $M$ |
| angular positions and sizes | physical or comoving sizes |
| the microwave temperature on the sky | densities, $H_0$, spatial curvature |

A sentence such as "the galaxy is 30 Mpc away" always hides a model. The redshift is the cleanest number you will ever have.

### The pillars of the hot big bang picture

- **Expansion.** Spectral lines of distant galaxies are shifted to longer wavelengths. Define $1+z = \lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm emit}$.
- **A relic blackbody.** The sky glows at $T_0\approx 2.7255$ K with a spectrum that is a blackbody to high precision. Its energy density and number density are
$$u_\gamma=\frac{\pi^2}{15}\frac{(k_BT)^4}{(\hbar c)^3},\qquad n_\gamma=\frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{k_BT}{\hbar c}\right)^3\approx 411\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}.$$
- **Light elements.** Helium near 25% of the baryonic mass and deuterium near $2.5\times10^{-5}$ of hydrogen by number (week 6).
- **Structure.** Galaxies, clusters and a cosmic web whose statistics match nearly scale-invariant initial fluctuations (weeks 8 and 11).

### The cosmological principle

On scales above roughly 100 Mpc the universe appears statistically **homogeneous** (the same at every position) and **isotropic** (the same in every direction). Isotropy about every point implies homogeneity, and for an expanding universe it also forces the velocity law $v\propto d$ for every observer. The principle is an assumption that observations test. The CMB temperature is the same in all directions to about $10^{-5}$ once the dipole caused by our own motion (about 3.4 mK, a speed near 370 km/s) is removed.

### The Hubble–Lemaître law

For $z\ll 1$,
$$cz \simeq H_0\, d .$$
Two practical limits. First, galaxies also move relative to the Hubble flow with peculiar velocities of a few hundred km/s, so along the line of sight $cz = H_0 d + v_{\rm pec}$, and nearby galaxies give poor distances. Second, this is a low-redshift approximation: for $z\gtrsim 0.1$ you need the distance definitions of week 2. Two scales follow: the Hubble time $1/H_0 = 9.778\,h^{-1}$ Gyr and the Hubble distance $c/H_0 = 2998\,h^{-1}$ Mpc.

### Candles, rulers and magnitudes

A source of known luminosity $L$ with measured flux $F$ defines the luminosity distance through $F = L/(4\pi d_L^2)$. In astronomical magnitudes the **distance modulus** is
$$\mu \equiv m - M = 5\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{d_L}{10\ \mathrm{pc}}\right).$$
Type Ia supernovae are standardizable candles, the baryon acoustic oscillation scale (about 150 Mpc comoving) is a standard ruler, and merging compact binaries are standard sirens. Each is only as good as its calibration.

### A dark night sky is data

In an infinite, static, eternal universe every line of sight would end on a stellar surface and the night sky would be as bright as a star. A finite age together with redshift removes the paradox, so even the darkness of the night sky is a cosmological observation.

@example
title: From wavelengths to a rough distance
problem: In a nearby galaxy the H$\alpha$ line, with rest wavelength $656.3$ nm, is observed at $660.9$ nm. Take $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc and $c = 2.998\times10^5$ km/s, and estimate the distance, assuming the shift is entirely due to the Hubble flow.
step: Redshift from the measured wavelengths: $z = \lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm rest} - 1 = 660.9/656.3 - 1 \approx 0.00701$.
step: Because $z \ll 1$ the low-redshift law applies. The apparent velocity is $cz \approx 2.998\times10^5 \times 0.00701 \approx 2.10\times10^3$ km/s.
step: Invert the Hubble–Lemaître law: $d = cz/H_0 \approx 2.10\times10^3 / 70 \approx 30$ Mpc.
step: State the caveat. A peculiar velocity of 300 km/s would change $cz$ by about 14%, so this is a rough distance, and the result is conditional on the assumed $H_0$.

@example
title: A supernova distance from its magnitudes
problem: A Type Ia supernova has absolute magnitude $M = -19.3$ and apparent magnitude $m = 15.7$. Find its luminosity distance and the redshift expected at low $z$ for $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc.
step: Distance modulus: $\mu = m - M = 15.7 - (-19.3) = 35.0$.
step: Invert $\mu = 5\log_{10}(d_L/10\ \mathrm{pc})$: $d_L = 10^{(\mu+5)/5}\ \mathrm{pc} = 10^{8}$ pc $= 100$ Mpc.
step: At low redshift $d_L\approx d$, so $z \approx H_0 d/c = 70\times 100/(2.998\times10^5) \approx 0.023$.
step: The assumed $M$ is a calibration, not a measurement of this object. Changing $M$ by $0.1$ mag changes $d_L$ by about 5%.

@mistakes
- Treating $cz$ as a literal velocity at large $z$; the formula $v = cz$ is only the low-redshift limit of the Hubble–Lemaître law.
- Using very nearby galaxies to determine $H_0$, where peculiar velocities are comparable to the Hubble flow.
- Mixing $h^{-1}$ Mpc with Mpc, or quoting a distance without saying which $H_0$ it assumes.
- Reporting an inferred quantity (a distance, an age) as if it were a direct measurement.

@practice num
prompt: The hydrogen Lyman-$\alpha$ line has rest wavelength $121.567$ nm. In a quasar spectrum it is observed at $486.268$ nm. Find the redshift $z$.
answer: 3
check: 486.268/121.567 - 1
hint: Use $1+z = \lambda_{\rm obs}/\lambda_{\rm emit}$.
hint: The ratio $486.268/121.567$ is a simple integer.
solution: $1+z = 486.268/121.567 = 4.000$, so $z = 3$.

@practice num
prompt: A galaxy has a measured redshift $cz = 4200$ km/s, entirely due to the Hubble flow. Take $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc. How far away is it?
answer: 60
check: 4200/70
unit: Mpc
hint: At low redshift the Hubble–Lemaître law reads $cz = H_0 d$.
solution: $d = cz/H_0 = 4200/70 = 60$ Mpc.

@practice num
prompt: Find the Hubble time $1/H_0$ in Gyr for $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc. Use $1\ \mathrm{Mpc} = 3.0857\times10^{19}$ km and $1\ \mathrm{Gyr} = 3.156\times10^{16}$ s. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 13.97
check: 3.0857e19/70/3.156e16
tol: 1%
unit: Gyr
hint: Convert Mpc to km first so that the units of $H_0$ become s$^{-1}$.
hint: $H_0 = 70/(3.0857\times10^{19})\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$; invert it to get seconds, then divide by the number of seconds in a Gyr.
solution: $1/H_0 = 3.0857\times10^{19}/70\ \mathrm{s} = 4.408\times10^{17}$ s. Dividing by $3.156\times10^{16}$ s/Gyr gives $13.97$ Gyr.

choice*: The wavelength of a spectral line in a galaxy's spectrum.
choice: The recession velocity of the galaxy.
choice: The comoving distance to the galaxy.
choice: The age of the universe when the light was emitted.

@practice num
prompt: Compute the present photon number density of the CMB in cm$^{-3}$ using $n_\gamma = \frac{2\zeta(3)}{\pi^2}\left(\frac{k_BT_0}{\hbar c}\right)^3$ with $T_0 = 2.7255$ K, $k_B = 1.3806\times10^{-23}$ J/K, $\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-34}$ J s, $c = 2.9979\times10^{8}$ m/s and $\zeta(3) = 1.2021$. Give the answer to three significant figures.
answer: 411
check: 2*1.2021/pi**2*(1.3806e-23*2.7255/(1.0546e-34*2.9979e8))**3/1e6
tol: 1%
unit: cm^-3
hint: First compute $k_BT_0/(\hbar c)$ in m$^{-1}$; it is of order $10^3$.
hint: Cube it, multiply by $2\zeta(3)/\pi^2 \approx 0.2436$, and convert m$^{-3}$ to cm$^{-3}$ by dividing by $10^6$.
solution: $k_BT_0/(\hbar c) \approx 1190\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$. Its cube is $1.686\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}}$, and multiplying by $0.2436$ gives $4.11\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m^{-3}} \approx 411\ \mathrm{cm^{-3}}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A Type Ia supernova has absolute magnitude $M = -19.3$ and is observed at apparent magnitude $m = 24.7$. Find its luminosity distance in Mpc, to three significant figures.
answer: 6310
check: 10**((24.7 - (-19.3) + 5)/5)/1e6
tol: 1%
unit: Mpc
hint: Compute the distance modulus $\mu = m - M$ first.
hint: Invert $\mu = 5\log_{10}(d_L/10\ \mathrm{pc})$ for $d_L$ in parsecs, then convert to Mpc.
solution: $\mu = 24.7 + 19.3 = 44.0$. Then $d_L = 10^{(44.0+5)/5}$ pc $= 10^{9.8}$ pc $= 6.31\times10^{9}$ pc $= 6310$ Mpc.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following are consequences or tests of the cosmological principle (statistical homogeneity and isotropy on large scales)?
choice*: After the dipole is removed, the CMB temperature is the same in all directions to about one part in $10^5$.
choice*: An observer in any other galaxy would measure the same Hubble–Lemaître law.
choice*: The average number of galaxies in spheres several hundred Mpc across does not depend on where the sphere is centred.
choice: Galaxies have no peculiar velocities.
choice: The universe has a centre from which all galaxies recede.
hint: Homogeneity is about position, isotropy about direction; neither says that small-scale structure is absent.
solution: The CMB isotropy, the observer-independence of the velocity law and the large-scale uniformity of galaxy counts all follow from or test the principle. Peculiar velocities and local structure exist and are compatible with it, and an expanding homogeneous universe has no centre.
