@article
The hot big bang with only radiation and matter explains the expansion, the CMB and the light elements, but it leaves three features unexplained: why distant regions have the same temperature, why space is so flat, and where the primordial perturbations of week 8 came from. Inflation, a period of accelerated expansion before the radiation era, addresses all three.

### Conventions

Natural units $\hbar=c=1$; signature $(-,+,+,+)$. In this lesson $M_{\rm Pl}\equiv(8\pi G)^{-1/2} = 2.435\times10^{18}$ GeV is the **reduced** Planck mass, smaller than the $G^{-1/2}$ used in week 5 by $\sqrt{8\pi}$. Approximate observed values, to be checked against current data: $A_s\approx2.1\times10^{-9}$ at $k_*=0.05$ Mpc$^{-1}$, $n_s\approx0.965$, and an upper limit $r\lesssim0.04$ (95% confidence, indicative).

### The problems

- **Horizon.** The comoving distance light can travel before recombination is small compared with the distance to the last-scattering surface, so CMB patches separated by more than about a degree or two were never in causal contact in the standard history. Yet their temperatures agree to $10^{-5}$.
- **Flatness.** The curvature contribution is $|\Omega-1| = |K|c^2/(aH)^2$. Since $aH$ decreases in both radiation and matter eras, $|\Omega-1|$ grows. With $|\Omega_K|\lesssim0.01$ today, it must have been below about $10^{-17}$ when nucleosynthesis began ($T\sim1$ MeV) and below about $10^{-15}$ when it ended ($T\sim0.1$ MeV), and far smaller earlier.

### What inflation is

Inflation is a phase with $\ddot a>0$, which is equivalent to a shrinking comoving Hubble radius and to a small value of
$$\epsilon\equiv-\frac{\dot H}{H^2}<1,\qquad \frac{d}{dt}\!\left(\frac1{aH}\right)<0 .$$
A region smaller than the Hubble radius is stretched far outside it, so it was once causally connected, and $|\Omega-1|\propto(aH)^{-2}$ is driven toward zero. The number of **e-folds** is $N=\ln(a_{\rm end}/a_i)$; solving the horizon problem needs roughly $50$ to $60$, depending on the energy scale and on how the universe reheats.

### Scalar-field dynamics

For a scalar field $\phi$ with potential $V(\phi)$,
$$\rho=\tfrac12\dot\phi^2+V,\quad p=\tfrac12\dot\phi^2-V,\quad H^2=\frac{\rho}{3M_{\rm Pl}^2},\quad \ddot\phi+3H\dot\phi+V'=0,$$
and $\epsilon = \dot\phi^2/(2M_{\rm Pl}^2H^2)$ exactly. In the slow-roll approximation, $H^2\simeq V/(3M_{\rm Pl}^2)$ and $3H\dot\phi\simeq-V'$, with
$$\epsilon_V=\frac{M_{\rm Pl}^2}{2}\left(\frac{V'}{V}\right)^2,\qquad \eta_V=M_{\rm Pl}^2\frac{V''}{V},\qquad N(\phi)=\frac1{M_{\rm Pl}^2}\int_{\phi_{\rm end}}^{\phi}\frac{V}{V'}\,d\phi .$$
Slow roll requires $\epsilon_V,|\eta_V|\ll1$ and ends when $\epsilon_V=1$.

### Predictions for perturbations

Quantum fluctuations of the field are stretched beyond the Hubble radius, where they freeze as classical curvature perturbations with an almost scale-invariant spectrum $\Delta^2_{\mathcal R}(k)=A_s(k/k_*)^{n_s-1}$, where
$$A_s=\frac{V}{24\pi^2M_{\rm Pl}^4\,\epsilon_V},\qquad n_s-1=-6\epsilon_V+2\eta_V,\qquad r=16\,\epsilon_V .$$
Tensor modes (primordial gravitational waves) have $\Delta_t^2 = 2H^2/(\pi^2M_{\rm Pl}^2) = rA_s$, so a measurement of $r$ fixes the energy scale:
$$V^{1/4}=\left(\tfrac32\pi^2A_s\,r\right)^{1/4}M_{\rm Pl}\approx1.0\times10^{16}\ \mathrm{GeV}\left(\frac{r}{0.01}\right)^{1/4}.$$

### Two benchmark models

For $V=\tfrac12m^2\phi^2$: $\epsilon_V=\eta_V=2M_{\rm Pl}^2/\phi^2$ and $N\simeq\phi^2/(4M_{\rm Pl}^2)$, so $\epsilon\simeq1/(2N)$, $n_s-1\simeq-2/N$ and $r\simeq8/N$. At $N=60$ this gives $n_s\approx0.967$ but $r\approx0.13$, above current limits on $r$. A plateau potential of Starobinsky type has the same $n_s-1\simeq-2/N$ but $r\simeq12/N^2\approx0.003$, comfortably allowed.

### Assumptions behind the numbers

The predictions assume a single canonical field, slow roll, a vacuum initial state for the fluctuations and a particular reheating history that fixes $N$. Changing any of these changes the mapping from potential to $(n_s,r)$. This lesson ties the model to the observable only through those assumptions, which week 12 revisits.

@example
title: Slow-roll observables for a quadratic potential
problem: For $V=\tfrac12m^2\phi^2$ evaluate $\epsilon_V$, $\eta_V$, the number of e-folds, $n_s$ and $r$ when $\phi=15M_{\rm Pl}$. Take inflation to end at $\epsilon_V=1$.
step: Compute derivatives in units $M_{\rm Pl}=1$: $V'/V=2/\phi$ and $V''/V=2/\phi^2$. Therefore $\epsilon_V=\tfrac12(2/\phi)^2=2/\phi^2$ and $\eta_V=2/\phi^2$.
step: At $\phi=15$: $\epsilon_V=\eta_V=2/225=0.00889$. Inflation ends where $\epsilon_V=1$, i.e. $\phi_{\rm end}=\sqrt2$.
step: The number of e-folds is $N=\int_{\phi_{\rm end}}^{\phi}(V/V')\,d\phi=\int(\phi/2)\,d\phi=(\phi^2-\phi_{\rm end}^2)/4=(225-2)/4=55.75$.
step: Spectral tilt: $n_s=1-6\epsilon_V+2\eta_V=1-4(0.00889)=0.9644$. Tensor ratio: $r=16\epsilon_V=0.142$.
step: Interpretation: the tilt matches the measured $n_s\approx0.965$, but $r=0.14$ is well above the observational upper limit, so this simple model is disfavoured.

@example
title: The energy scale from a tensor ratio
problem: Suppose $r=0.01$, with $A_s=2.1\times10^{-9}$ and $M_{\rm Pl}=2.435\times10^{18}$ GeV. Find $H$ during inflation and $V^{1/4}$.
step: The tensor amplitude is $\Delta_t^2=rA_s=2.1\times10^{-11}$.
step: From $\Delta_t^2=2H^2/(\pi^2M_{\rm Pl}^2)$: $H^2=\pi^2M_{\rm Pl}^2\,rA_s/2$, so $H=\pi M_{\rm Pl}\sqrt{rA_s/2}=\pi(2.435\times10^{18})(3.24\times10^{-6})=2.5\times10^{13}$ GeV.
step: Then $V=3M_{\rm Pl}^2H^2=3(5.93\times10^{36})(6.14\times10^{26})=1.09\times10^{64}$ GeV$^4$.
step: Take the fourth root: $V^{1/4}=1.02\times10^{16}$ GeV, close to the scale of grand unification, far above the electroweak scale, and about a factor of 240 below the reduced Planck mass.

@mistakes
- Using the Planck mass $G^{-1/2}$ in inflation formulas that are written with the reduced Planck mass $M_{\rm Pl}=(8\pi G)^{-1/2}$.
- Applying $n_s-1=-6\epsilon_V+2\eta_V$ without checking that slow roll holds, or evaluating $\epsilon_V$ at the end of inflation instead of $N$ e-folds before the end.
- Claiming that the measured $n_s\approx0.965$ proves a particular potential; many models give the same tilt but different $r$.
- Treating $N=60$ as universal; it depends on the energy scale and on the reheating history.

@practice mc
prompt: Which condition is equivalent to accelerated expansion, $\ddot a>0$?
choice: The Hubble parameter $H$ is increasing.
choice*: The comoving Hubble radius $1/(aH)$ is decreasing.
choice: The energy density is increasing.
choice: The scale factor is growing.
hint: Write $\ddot a=a(\dot H+H^2)$ and compare with $\epsilon=-\dot H/H^2$.
solution: $\ddot a/a=\dot H+H^2=H^2(1-\epsilon)$, so $\ddot a>0$ means $\epsilon<1$. Also $\frac{d}{dt}(1/aH)=-(\ddot a)/(\dot a^2)$, so a decreasing comoving Hubble radius is the same condition. The scale factor grows in any expanding universe, and $H$ and the density do not increase during inflation.
