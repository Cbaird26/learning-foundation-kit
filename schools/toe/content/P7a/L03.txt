@article
In classical mechanics, Newton's second law tells you how a particle's position changes. In quantum mechanics the **Schrödinger equation** tells you how the wavefunction changes. For a particle of mass $m$ in a potential $V(x)$:

$$i\hbar\,\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\,\Psi.$$

Three features matter. The equation is **linear**, so sums of solutions are solutions (the superposition principle). It is **first order in time**, so $\Psi(x,0)$ alone fixes $\Psi(x,t)$ for all later times. And it preserves normalization: a state that starts with total probability $1$ keeps it.

### Separation of variables

Try $\Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,T(t)$. Dividing through by $\psi T$ puts all $x$-dependence on one side and all $t$-dependence on the other, so both equal a constant, called $E$. The time part gives $T(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}$, and the space part is the **time-independent Schrödinger equation**:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\,\psi = E\,\psi.$$

This is an eigenvalue problem for the energy: solutions that satisfy the boundary conditions exist only for certain values $E_n$ in a bound system.

### Stationary states

A **stationary state** is $\Psi_n(x,t) = \psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$. Notice the wording: the wavefunction $\Psi_n$ is **not** constant, because its phase rotates at angular frequency $E_n/\hbar$. What is constant is the probability density,

$$|\Psi_n(x,t)|^2 = |\psi_n(x)|^2,$$

and therefore every expectation value $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle E\rangle$. Only a global phase changes, and a global phase is unobservable.

### Superpositions are not stationary

Because the equation is linear, the general solution is

$$\Psi(x,t) = \sum_n c_n\,\psi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar},$$

where the constants $c_n$ come from the initial state. The probability of measuring $E_n$ is $|c_n|^2$, and it does **not** change in time. But the density has cross terms. For two states with real $\psi_1, \psi_2$ and real coefficients,

$$|\Psi|^2 = c_1^2\psi_1^2 + c_2^2\psi_2^2 + 2c_1c_2\,\psi_1\psi_2\cos\omega t, \qquad \omega = \frac{E_2 - E_1}{\hbar}.$$

The density sloshes back and forth with period $T = 2\pi\hbar/(E_2 - E_1) = h/(E_2-E_1)$. Only energy **differences** are visible; adding a constant to $V$ changes all $E_n$ equally and multiplies $\Psi$ by a global phase.

### The free particle

For $V = 0$, the plane wave $e^{i(kx - \omega t)}$ solves the equation if $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, which is $E = p^2/2m$ with $p = \hbar k$.

@example
title: A plane wave and its speeds
problem: Show that $\Psi = e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$ solves the free Schrödinger equation only if $\omega = \hbar k^2/(2m)$, and compare the phase velocity with the particle's classical speed $p/m$.
step: Time derivative: $i\hbar\,\partial_t\Psi = i\hbar(-i\omega)\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi$.
step: Space derivative: $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\partial_x^2\Psi = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}(ik)^2\Psi = \dfrac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m}\,\Psi$.
step: Equating the two sides gives $\hbar\omega = \hbar^2k^2/2m$, so $\omega = \hbar k^2/(2m)$.
step: The phase velocity is $\omega/k = \hbar k/(2m) = p/(2m)$, half the classical speed. The speed of a wave packet is the group velocity $d\omega/dk = \hbar k/m = p/m$, which does match the classical speed.

@example
title: Density of a two-state superposition
problem: A particle starts in $\Psi(x,0) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\,[\psi_1(x) + \psi_2(x)]$ with real, orthonormal energy eigenfunctions $\psi_1,\psi_2$ of energies $E_1 \neq E_2$. Find $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$.
step: Attach the phase factors: $\Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_1 e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + \psi_2 e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}\right]$.
step: Square the modulus. The diagonal terms have modulus one; the cross terms combine into a cosine: $|\Psi|^2 = \tfrac12\psi_1^2 + \tfrac12\psi_2^2 + \psi_1\psi_2\cos\omega t$, with $\omega = (E_2-E_1)/\hbar$.
step: The density oscillates with period $2\pi\hbar/(E_2 - E_1)$. Where $\psi_1\psi_2 = 0$ it does not change; where $\psi_1\psi_2$ is large it swings the most.
step: The probabilities $|c_1|^2 = |c_2|^2 = \tfrac12$ and the mean energy $\tfrac12(E_1+E_2)$ stay constant. Only the shape of the density moves.

@mistakes
- Saying a stationary state has a constant wavefunction. The wavefunction carries a rotating phase; only $|\Psi|^2$ and expectation values are constant.
- Dropping the factors $e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ when superposing states of different energy, which wrongly gives a frozen density.
- Believing the energy probabilities $|c_n|^2$ change in time. For a time-independent potential they do not.
- Confusing angular frequency $\omega = E/\hbar$ (rad/s) with frequency $f = E/h$ (Hz).
