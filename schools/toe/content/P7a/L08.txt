@article
The **standard deviation** of an observable $A$ in a state $\psi$ is $\sigma_A = \sqrt{\langle A^2\rangle - \langle A\rangle^2}$. It measures the spread of results across many identically prepared systems. Is there a state in which position and momentum both have zero spread? No, and the commutator tells us why.

### The generalized uncertainty principle

For Hermitian operators $\hat A,\hat B$ and any normalized state,

$$\sigma_A\,\sigma_B \;\ge\; \frac12\,\bigl|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\bigr|.$$

**Sketch of the proof.** Let $f = (\hat A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$ and $g = (\hat B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$, so $\sigma_A^2 = \langle f|f\rangle$ and $\sigma_B^2 = \langle g|g\rangle$. The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality gives $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2 \ge \bigl(\mathrm{Im}\langle f|g\rangle\bigr)^2$. And $\mathrm{Im}\langle f|g\rangle = \frac{1}{2i}\bigl(\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle\bigr) = \frac{1}{2i}\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle$. Taking square roots finishes the argument.

With $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$ we get the **Heisenberg uncertainty principle**:

$$\sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

Here the bound holds for every state, because $\langle i\hbar\rangle = i\hbar$.

### Minimum-uncertainty states

The Gaussian $\psi \propto e^{-x^2/(2b^2)}$ has $\sigma_x = b/\sqrt2$ and $\sigma_p = \hbar/(\sqrt2\,b)$, so $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$ exactly. Squeezing the packet in $x$ (small $b$) broadens it in $p$. Every other shape has a larger product; the ground state of the infinite well gives about $0.568\,\hbar$.

### Compatible observables

If $[\hat A,\hat B] = 0$, the bound is zero and the observables are **compatible**: there is a complete set of simultaneous eigenfunctions, so both can be sharp together. Examples are $\hat p$ and $\hat H$ for a free particle, and $\hat H$ and the parity operator for a symmetric potential. By contrast $\hat x$ and $\hat H$ do not commute ($[\hat x,\hat H] = i\hbar\hat p/m$).

When a commutator is itself an operator, the bound depends on the state. For the matrices $\sigma_x,\sigma_y$, $[\sigma_x,\sigma_y] = 2i\sigma_z$, so the bound is $|\langle\sigma_z\rangle|$ and it can vanish in some states.

### Reading the principle correctly

- It is a statement about **spreads across an ensemble**, not about clumsy instruments.
- Confinement to a size $a$ forces $\sigma_p \gtrsim \hbar/(2a)$, hence a kinetic energy of at least $\sim\hbar^2/(8ma^2)$. This is why bound systems have **zero-point energy** and why atoms have a finite size.
- Time is a parameter, not an operator, so $\Delta E\,\Delta t \gtrsim \hbar/2$ is a different kind of statement: $\Delta t$ is the time over which the state changes appreciably, roughly $\sigma_A$ divided by $|d\langle A\rangle/dt|$.

@example
title: A lower bound on the oscillator energy
problem: A particle of mass $m$ in the potential $\frac12 m\omega^2x^2$ has $\langle x\rangle = \langle p\rangle = 0$. Use $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$ to find a lower bound on $\langle E\rangle$.
step: With zero means, $\langle E\rangle = \dfrac{\sigma_p^2}{2m} + \dfrac12 m\omega^2\sigma_x^2$.
step: Replace $\sigma_p$ by its smallest allowed value $\hbar/(2\sigma_x)$: $\langle E\rangle \ge \dfrac{\hbar^2}{8m\sigma_x^2} + \dfrac12 m\omega^2\sigma_x^2$.
step: Let $u = \sigma_x^2$. Setting the derivative of the right side to zero gives $-\dfrac{\hbar^2}{8mu^2} + \dfrac12 m\omega^2 = 0$, so $u = \dfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}$.
step: Substitute: $\langle E\rangle \ge \dfrac{\hbar\omega}{4} + \dfrac{\hbar\omega}{4} = \dfrac{\hbar\omega}{2}$. The lowest possible energy is not zero; it is the zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$.

@example
title: Commutator and uncertainty for two-level matrices
problem: For $\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$ and the state $\psi = (1, 0)^T$, find $[\sigma_x,\sigma_y]$, the measurement statistics of $\sigma_x$, and check the uncertainty relation.
step: $\sigma_x\sigma_y = \begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix}$ and $\sigma_y\sigma_x = \begin{pmatrix}-i&0\\0&i\end{pmatrix}$, so $[\sigma_x,\sigma_y] = 2i\sigma_z$ with $\sigma_z = \mathrm{diag}(1,-1)$.
step: In the state $(1,0)^T$, $\langle\sigma_z\rangle = 1$, so the bound is $\frac12|2i\cdot1| = 1$.
step: The eigenvectors of $\sigma_x$ are $(1,\pm1)/\sqrt2$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$. Each is obtained with probability $\frac12$, so $\langle\sigma_x\rangle = 0$. The matrix satisfies $\sigma_x^2 = I$, so $\langle\sigma_x^2\rangle = 1$ and the standard deviation is $\Delta\sigma_x = \sqrt{1 - 0} = 1$. The same reasoning gives $\Delta\sigma_y = 1$.
step: The product is $\Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_y = 1$, equal to the bound, so this state saturates the inequality.

@mistakes
- Reading $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$ as a limit on instrument precision only. It constrains the spread over an ensemble of identically prepared systems.
- Using $\hbar$ or $h$ on the right side instead of $\hbar/2$.
- Mixing up variance and standard deviation: the bound is on $\sigma_A\sigma_B$, not $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2$.
- Forgetting that $\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle$ depends on the state when the commutator is an operator; noncommuting does not mean the bound is always positive.

prompt: Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, prove that $\sigma_A\sigma_B \ge \frac12|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|$ for Hermitian operators $\hat A$ and $\hat B$.
hint: Define $f = (\hat A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$ and $g = (\hat B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$ and express the variances as inner products.
hint: For any complex number $z$, $|z|^2 \ge (\mathrm{Im}\,z)^2$. Compute $\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle$ using Hermiticity.
solution: Let $f = (\hat A - \langle A\rangle)\psi$, $g = (\hat B - \langle B\rangle)\psi$. Hermiticity gives $\sigma_A^2 = \langle f|f\rangle$ and $\sigma_B^2 = \langle g|g\rangle$. Cauchy-Schwarz: $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \ge |\langle f|g\rangle|^2$. Since $|z|^2 \ge (\mathrm{Im}\,z)^2$ with $z = \langle f|g\rangle$, $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \ge \left[\frac{1}{2i}(\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle)\right]^2$. Using Hermiticity, $\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle = \langle\psi|(\hat A - \langle A\rangle)(\hat B - \langle B\rangle) - (\hat B - \langle B\rangle)(\hat A - \langle A\rangle)|\psi\rangle = \langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle$. Hence $\sigma_A\sigma_B \ge \frac12|\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle|$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select every correct statement about the uncertainty principle.
choice*: If $[\hat A,\hat B] = 0$, there is a basis of simultaneous eigenstates of $\hat A$ and $\hat B$.
choice*: The Gaussian wavefunction achieves $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$.
choice: The principle says that the position of a single particle cannot be measured more precisely than $\hbar/2$ divided by its momentum.
choice*: The inequality describes the spreads of results over many identically prepared systems.
hint: Decide which statements describe an ensemble and which describe a single measurement.
solution: The statements about simultaneous eigenstates for commuting operators, the Gaussian reaching $\hbar/2$, and spreads over many identically prepared systems are correct. The statement that one particle's position cannot be measured more precisely than $\hbar/2$ divided by its momentum misreads the principle: position can be measured as precisely as you like in one run, but then the momentum spread of the state afterwards is large. The relation is about the statistics of an ensemble.
