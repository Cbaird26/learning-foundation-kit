@article
Near any smooth potential minimum, $V(x) \approx V_{\min} + \frac12 V''\,(x - x_{\min})^2$, so small vibrations are harmonic. This is why the oscillator governs molecular vibrations, lattice vibrations and the modes of light. For a mass $m$ on a spring, with $V = \frac12 m\omega^2x^2$ and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, the time-independent equation is

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\psi'' + \frac12 m\omega^2x^2\psi = E\psi.$$

### Dimensionless form

Define the **oscillator length** $x_0 = \sqrt{\hbar/(m\omega)}$ and $\xi = x/x_0$, and write $E = \epsilon\,\hbar\omega$. The equation becomes

$$-\frac{d^2\psi}{d\xi^2} + \xi^2\psi = 2\epsilon\,\psi.$$

Try $\psi = e^{-\xi^2/2}$. Then $\psi'' = (\xi^2 - 1)\psi$, so $-\psi'' + \xi^2\psi = \psi$ and $2\epsilon = 1$: the Gaussian is a solution with $E_0 = \frac12\hbar\omega$. It has no nodes, so it is the ground state.

### The full spectrum

Normalizable solutions exist only for

$$E_n = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\hbar\omega, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

with eigenfunctions $\psi_n(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2^n\,n!\,\sqrt\pi\,x_0}}\,H_n(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, where $H_0 = 1$, $H_1 = 2\xi$, $H_2 = 4\xi^2 - 2$ are **Hermite polynomials**. The first three normalized states are

$$\psi_0 = \frac{e^{-\xi^2/2}}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{x_0}}, \quad \psi_1 = \frac{\sqrt2\,\xi\,e^{-\xi^2/2}}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{x_0}}, \quad \psi_2 = \frac{(2\xi^2-1)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}}{\sqrt2\,\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{x_0}}.$$

Key features:

- The levels are **equally spaced** by $\hbar\omega$, unlike the infinite well.
- The lowest energy $\frac12\hbar\omega$ is the **zero-point energy**.
- $\psi_n$ has $n$ nodes and parity $(-1)^n$.
- When integrating, remember $dx = x_0\,d\xi$.

### Classical versus quantum

A classical particle with energy $E$ turns around where $V = E$: $x_t = \pm\sqrt{2E/(m\omega^2)} = \pm x_0\sqrt{2n+1}$. The quantum wavefunction extends **beyond** $x_t$, decaying like a Gaussian, so there is a nonzero probability of finding the particle in the classically forbidden region.

In the ground state, $\langle x\rangle = 0$, $\langle x^2\rangle = x_0^2/2$ and $\langle p^2\rangle = \hbar^2/(2x_0^2)$, so $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$, the minimum allowed. The kinetic and potential energies each average $\hbar\omega/4$. In general $\langle x^2\rangle_n = (n + \frac12)x_0^2$.

@example
title: Verifying and normalizing the ground state
problem: Show that $\psi(x) = A\,e^{-x^2/(2x_0^2)}$ solves the oscillator equation, find $E$, and find $A$.
step: In terms of $\xi = x/x_0$, $\psi = Ae^{-\xi^2/2}$ and $\psi'' = (\xi^2 - 1)\psi$ (derivatives with respect to $\xi$).
step: Substitute into $-\psi'' + \xi^2\psi = 2\epsilon\psi$: the left side is $\psi$, so $2\epsilon = 1$ and $E = \epsilon\hbar\omega = \frac12\hbar\omega$.
step: Normalize: $\int|\psi|^2\,dx = |A|^2x_0\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi = |A|^2x_0\sqrt\pi = 1$.
step: Therefore $A = (\pi x_0^2)^{-1/4}$, matching $\psi_0$ above.

@example
title: Probability beyond the turning point
problem: In the ground state, what is the probability of finding the particle where a classical oscillator of the same energy could never be?
step: The energy is $E_0 = \frac12\hbar\omega = \frac12m\omega^2x_t^2$, so the turning points are $x_t = \pm x_0$.
step: The forbidden region is $|x| > x_0$. By symmetry, $P = 2\int_{x_0}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{-x^2/x_0^2}}{\sqrt\pi\,x_0}\,dx = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_1^\infty e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi = \operatorname{erfc}(1)$.
step: Numerically, $\operatorname{erfc}(1) = 1 - \operatorname{erf}(1) \approx 0.157$. About $16\%$ of the probability lies in the forbidden region.
step: The wavefunction does not stop at the classical boundary; the fraction in the forbidden region is a genuinely quantum effect.

@mistakes
- Using $E_n = n\hbar\omega$ or starting the levels at $E = 0$; the lowest energy is $\frac12\hbar\omega$.
- Integrating over $\xi$ but forgetting the factor $x_0$ from $dx = x_0\,d\xi$ in a normalization.
- Thinking the particle cannot be found beyond the classical turning points.
- Forgetting the normalization factor $1/\sqrt{2^nn!}$ when using Hermite polynomials.

@practice mc

choice: The energy levels get closer together as $n$ increases.

choice*: The eigenfunction $\psi_n$ has $n$ nodes and parity $(-1)^n$.

prompt: For the first excited state $\psi_1 = \dfrac{\sqrt2\,\xi\,e^{-\xi^2/2}}{\pi^{1/4}\sqrt{x_0}}$, find $\langle x^2\rangle/x_0^2$. Give an exact value.
answer: 3/2
check: quad(lambda s: s**2*(2/sqrt(pi))*s**2*exp(-s**2), -inf, inf)
hint: In terms of $\xi$, $\langle\xi^2\rangle = \int \xi^2|\psi_1|^2 x_0\,d\xi = \frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int\xi^4e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi$.
hint: Use $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\xi^4e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi = \frac{3\sqrt\pi}{4}$.
solution: $\langle\xi^2\rangle = \dfrac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\dfrac{3\sqrt\pi}{4} = \dfrac32$, in agreement with $\langle x^2\rangle_n = (n+\frac12)x_0^2$.

@practice num
prompt: At $t = 0$ an oscillator is in $\Psi = (\psi_0 + \psi_1)/\sqrt2$ with $\psi_0$ and $\psi_1$ the normalized ground and first excited states. Find $\langle x\rangle/x_0$ at $t=0$. Give an exact value.
answer: sqrt(2)/2
check: quad(lambda s: s*(pi**-0.25*exp(-s**2/2) + pi**-0.25*sqrt(2)*s*exp(-s**2/2))**2/2, -inf, inf)
hint: The $\psi_0^2$ and $\psi_1^2$ terms are even functions times $\xi$, hence odd, and integrate to zero. Only the cross term survives.
hint: $\langle\xi\rangle = \int\xi\,\psi_0\psi_1\,d\xi$ in dimensionless form, and $\psi_0\psi_1 = \sqrt{2}\,\xi e^{-\xi^2}/\sqrt\pi$.
solution: $\langle\xi\rangle = \dfrac12\cdot2\int\xi\,\psi_0\psi_1\,d\xi = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt\pi}\int\xi^2e^{-\xi^2}\,d\xi = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{\sqrt\pi}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt\pi}{2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$. In time, $\langle x\rangle = (x_0/\sqrt2)\cos\omega t$, which oscillates at the classical frequency.

@practice self
prompt: Show that the n-th oscillator level has classical turning points at $x_t = \pm x_0\sqrt{2n+1}$. Then explain, using the ground state, why the oscillator's lowest energy cannot be zero.
hint: Set $V(x_t) = E_n$ with $E_n = (n+\frac12)\hbar\omega$ and use $x_0^2 = \hbar/(m\omega)$.
hint: For the second part, relate a state with $E = 0$ to $\sigma_x = 0$ and $\sigma_p = 0$ and compare with $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$.
solution: At a turning point the kinetic energy vanishes: $\frac12m\omega^2x_t^2 = (n+\frac12)\hbar\omega$, so $x_t^2 = (2n+1)\hbar/(m\omega) = (2n+1)x_0^2$ and $x_t = \pm x_0\sqrt{2n+1}$. A state with $E = 0$ would need $\langle V\rangle = 0$ and $\langle T\rangle = 0$, so both $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_p$ would be zero, violating $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$. The ground state compromises with $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \hbar/2$ and $E_0 = \frac12\hbar\omega$.
rubric: Sets the potential energy equal to the total energy and solves for $x_t$ with the correct factor $2n+1$.
rubric: Links $E = 0$ to zero spreads in both $x$ and $p$ and invokes the uncertainty principle.
rubric: States that the ground state minimizes the energy subject to the uncertainty bound, giving $\frac12\hbar\omega$.
