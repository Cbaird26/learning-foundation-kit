@article
An **oscillator** is any system that is pulled back toward a stable equilibrium and overshoots it, again and again. Almost every stable equilibrium behaves this way for small displacements, so the single equation in this lesson will return in circuits, sound, light and quantum mechanics.

### The equation of motion

Take a mass $m$ on an ideal spring of constant $k$ sliding on a frictionless surface, and let $x$ be its displacement from equilibrium. Hooke's law gives the force $F = -kx$, and Newton's second law gives

$$m\ddot x = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x = -\omega_0^2\,x, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

Dots are time derivatives. The acceleration is proportional to the displacement and points the opposite way. Any motion obeying this equation is called **simple harmonic motion**.

### The solution

Sine and cosine are the functions whose second derivative is a negative multiple of themselves, so the general solution is

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi).$$

$A \ge 0$ is the **amplitude** and $\phi$ the **phase constant**. A second-order equation needs two constants, and the initial position $x_0$ and velocity $v_0$ fix them. Since $x(0) = A\cos\phi$ and $\dot x(0) = -A\omega_0\sin\phi$,

$$A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + \left(\frac{v_0}{\omega_0}\right)^2}, \qquad \cos\phi = \frac{x_0}{A}, \qquad \sin\phi = -\frac{v_0}{A\,\omega_0}.$$

Use both the cosine and the sine to pick the quadrant of $\phi$; the tangent alone cannot.

The **period** is $T = 2\pi/\omega_0$, the **frequency** is $f = 1/T$ (hertz), and $\omega_0 = 2\pi f$ is the **angular frequency** (rad/s). For a spring the period does not depend on the amplitude.

### Energy

The total energy is constant:

$$E = \tfrac12 m\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2.$$

It trades back and forth between kinetic and potential. The speed is largest at $x = 0$, where $v_{\max} = A\omega_0$, and at any displacement $v = \omega_0\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$.

### The small-angle pendulum

For a bob of mass $m$ on a light string of length $L$, the torque equation gives $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta$. For small angles (in radians) $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so

$$\ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\,\theta, \qquad \omega_0 = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}.$$

The mass cancels, so the period depends only on $L$ and $g$. Larger swings make the true period slightly longer than $2\pi/\omega_0$.

### Complex notation

Write $x = \mathrm{Re}\left(Ce^{i\omega_0 t}\right)$ with a complex constant $C = Ae^{i\phi}$. Differentiating the exponential just multiplies by $i\omega_0$, which will make driven and coupled problems much easier.

@example
title: Amplitude and phase from initial conditions
problem: A $0.25\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 100\ \text{N/m}$ is at $x_0 = 0.030\ \text{m}$ with velocity $v_0 = 0.80\ \text{m/s}$ at $t = 0$. Find $\omega_0$, $T$, $A$ and $\phi$.
step: The angular frequency is $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{100/0.25} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$, so $T = 2\pi/20 \approx 0.314\ \text{s}$.
step: The velocity scale is $v_0/\omega_0 = 0.80/20 = 0.040\ \text{m}$.
step: The amplitude is $A = \sqrt{0.030^2 + 0.040^2} = 0.050\ \text{m}$ (a 3-4-5 triangle).
step: For the phase, $\cos\phi = x_0/A = 0.6$ and $\sin\phi = -v_0/(A\omega_0) = -0.8$. Cosine positive and sine negative put $\phi$ in the fourth quadrant: $\phi = -\arctan(4/3) \approx -0.927\ \text{rad}$.
step: Check: $x(0) = 0.050\cos(-0.927) = 0.050(0.6) = 0.030\ \text{m}$, as required.

@example
title: A small-angle pendulum
problem: A pendulum of length $L = 0.80\ \text{m}$ swings with amplitude $0.050\ \text{rad}$. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the period and the maximum speed of the bob.
step: The small-angle angular frequency is $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L} = \sqrt{9.8/0.80} = \sqrt{12.25} = 3.5\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega_0 = 4\pi/7 \approx 1.80\ \text{s}$.
step: The angle is $\theta(t) = 0.050\cos(\omega_0 t + \phi)$, so the maximum angular speed is $0.050 \times 3.5 = 0.175\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: The bob moves on a circle of radius $L$, so its maximum speed is $L \times 0.175 = 0.80 \times 0.175 = 0.14\ \text{m/s}$.

@mistakes
- Mixing up $f$ in hertz and $\omega$ in rad/s, which causes a factor-of-$2\pi$ error in periods and speeds.
- Using the small-angle pendulum formula with an angle in degrees, or for a large swing where $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ fails.
- Computing $\phi = \arctan\!\big(-v_0/(\omega_0 x_0)\big)$ on a calculator and ignoring the quadrant when $x_0 < 0$.
- Believing a heavier pendulum bob swings faster, or that a bigger spring amplitude changes the period.

check: sqrt(32/0.50)

@practice num
prompt: A $0.20\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 80\ \text{N/m}$ oscillates. Find its frequency $f$ in hertz. Give an exact answer.
answer: 10/pi
check: sqrt(80/0.20)/(2*pi)
unit: Hz
hint: First find the angular frequency $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$.
hint: $\omega_0 = 20\ \text{rad/s}$. Dividing by $2\pi$ converts radians per second to cycles per second.
solution: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{80/0.20} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$, so $f = \omega_0/2\pi = 10/\pi \approx 3.18\ \text{Hz}$.

@practice num
prompt: How long must a simple pendulum be to have a small-angle period of $2.0\ \text{s}$? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.993
check: 9.8*2.0**2/(4*pi**2)
tol: 1%

hint: Start from $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ and square both sides.
hint: Solve for the length: $L = gT^2/(4\pi^2)$.
solution: $L = gT^2/(4\pi^2) = 9.8(4.0)/39.48 \approx 0.993\ \text{m}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$ oscillates with amplitude $0.10\ \text{m}$. How fast is it moving when its displacement is $0.060\ \text{m}$?
answer: 1.6
check: sqrt(2*(0.5*200*0.10**2 - 0.5*200*0.060**2)/0.50)
unit: m/s
hint: Energy is conserved: $\tfrac12 kA^2 = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2$.
hint: Equivalently $v = \omega_0\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$, with $\omega_0 = 20\ \text{rad/s}$ here.
solution: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{200/0.50} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$ and $v = 20\sqrt{0.10^2 - 0.060^2} = 20(0.080) = 1.6\ \text{m/s}$.

choice*: The spring-mass period is unchanged; the pendulum period gets longer.
choice: Both periods get longer.
choice: Both periods are unchanged.
choice: The spring-mass period gets longer; the pendulum period is unchanged.
hint: Write each $\omega_0$ in terms of its own parameters. Which one contains $g$?
hint: For the vertical spring, gravity only shifts the equilibrium position; it does not change the restoring force per unit displacement.
solution: Pendulum: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{g/L}$, so a smaller $g$ lengthens $T$. Vertical spring: measured from the new equilibrium, $m\ddot x = -kx$, so $\omega_0 = \sqrt{k/m}$ does not involve $g$.

@quiz num
prompt: A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ block on a spring with $k = 50\ \text{N/m}$ is at $x = 0.20\ \text{m}$ and moving at $1.5\ \text{m/s}$ at $t = 0$. Find the amplitude, rounded to three decimal places.
answer: 0.361
check: sqrt(0.20**2 + (1.5/sqrt(50/2.0))**2)
tol: 1%
unit: m
hint: Find $\omega_0$ first.
hint: Use $A = \sqrt{x_0^2 + (v_0/\omega_0)^2}$.
solution: $\omega_0 = \sqrt{50/2.0} = 5\ \text{rad/s}$, so $v_0/\omega_0 = 0.30\ \text{m}$ and $A = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.09} = \sqrt{0.13} \approx 0.361\ \text{m}$.

@quiz expr
prompt: For $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \phi)$, find the velocity $v(t)$.
vars: t, A, omega, phi
lead: $v(t) =$
answer: -A omega sin(omega t + phi)
check: lambda t, A, omega, phi: nderiv(lambda s: A*cos(omega*s + phi), t)
hint: Differentiate with respect to $t$; the chain rule brings out a factor of $\omega$.
hint: The derivative of $\cos u$ is $-\sin u$.
solution: $v = dx/dt = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \phi)$.
