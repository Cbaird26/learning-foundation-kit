@article
Push a child on a swing in rhythm with the swing and small pushes build a large motion. That is **resonance**. To describe it, add a sinusoidal force $F_0\cos\omega t$ to the damped oscillator of the last lesson:

$$\ddot x + \gamma\dot x + \omega_0^2 x = \frac{F_0}{m}\cos\omega t.$$

### Transient plus steady state

The general solution is the free damped motion (the **transient**, which dies out like $e^{-\gamma t/2}$) plus one particular solution that persists. After a few decay times only the **steady state** is left, and it oscillates at the *driving* frequency $\omega$, not at $\omega_0$ or $\omega_d$.

### Complex amplitude

Replace the force by $(F_0/m)e^{i\omega t}$ and try $x = \hat x\,e^{i\omega t}$; the physical motion is the real part. Each time derivative multiplies by $i\omega$, so

$$\left(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega\right)\hat x = \frac{F_0}{m} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat x = \frac{F_0/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 + i\gamma\omega}.$$

Writing $\hat x = A e^{-i\delta}$ gives the steady state $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t - \delta)$ with

$$A(\omega) = \frac{F_0/m}{\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2}}, \qquad \tan\delta = \frac{\gamma\omega}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2}.$$

$\delta$ is the **phase lag** of the displacement behind the force. Because $\gamma\omega > 0$, $\delta$ always lies between $0$ and $\pi$: take $\delta = \operatorname{atan2}(\gamma\omega,\ \omega_0^2-\omega^2)$, so that for $\omega > \omega_0$ (negative denominator) it falls between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$.

### Three regimes

- $\omega \ll \omega_0$: the mass follows the force. $A \to F_0/k$ and $\delta \to 0$.
- $\omega = \omega_0$: $A = \dfrac{F_0}{m\gamma\omega_0} = \dfrac{F_0}{b\,\omega_0}$, limited only by damping, and $\delta = \pi/2$.
- $\omega \gg \omega_0$: inertia wins. $A \to F_0/(m\omega^2)$ and $\delta \to \pi$, so the motion opposes the force.

### The peak and its width

Setting $dA/d\omega = 0$ gives the amplitude peak at

$$\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - \frac{\gamma^2}{2}}$$

(it exists for $\gamma < \sqrt2\,\omega_0$), slightly below $\omega_0$. The time-averaged power absorbed, $P = \tfrac12 b\omega^2A^2$, peaks at exactly $\omega_0$ and drops to half its maximum at $\omega_\pm = \sqrt{\omega_0^2+\gamma^2/4} \pm \gamma/2$. The full width at half power is therefore exactly

$$\Delta\omega = \gamma, \qquad\text{so}\qquad Q = \frac{\omega_0}{\gamma} = \frac{\omega_0}{\Delta\omega}.$$

On resonance the amplitude is $Q$ times the static deflection: $A(\omega_0) = QF_0/k$. A tall, narrow peak means large $Q$, the same property that makes free oscillations ring for a long time.

@example
title: Amplitude and phase below and at resonance
problem: A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 50\ \text{N/m}$ and $b = 1.0\ \text{kg/s}$ is driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$ with $F_0 = 2.0\ \text{N}$. Find the steady-state amplitude and phase lag at $\omega = 5.0\ \text{rad/s}$ and at $\omega = 10\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: The parameters are $\omega_0 = \sqrt{50/0.50} = 10\ \text{rad/s}$, $\gamma = b/m = 2.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $F_0/m = 4.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. The static deflection is $F_0/k = 0.040\ \text{m}$.
step: At $\omega = 5.0$: $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = 75$ and $\gamma\omega = 10$, so $A = 4.0/\sqrt{75^2 + 10^2} = 4.0/75.7 \approx 0.053\ \text{m}$.
step: The phase is $\delta = \operatorname{atan2}(10, 75) \approx 0.13\ \text{rad}$, so the motion follows the force closely, with a small lag.
step: At $\omega = 10$ the first term vanishes: $A = 4.0/(\gamma\omega) = 4.0/20 = 0.20\ \text{m}$ and $\delta = \pi/2$.
step: Check with $Q = \omega_0/\gamma = 5$: $A(\omega_0) = Q F_0/k = 5(0.040) = 0.20\ \text{m}$.

@example
title: From a half-power width to ringing time
problem: A resonator has $f_0 = 440\ \text{Hz}$, and its power-absorption curve has a full width at half maximum of $\Delta f = 4.0\ \text{Hz}$. Find $Q$, $\gamma$ and the amplitude decay time $\tau$ of its free ringing.
step: The full width at half power is $\Delta\omega = \gamma$, and $\Delta\omega = 2\pi\Delta f = 2\pi(4.0) \approx 25\ \text{s}^{-1}$. So $\gamma \approx 25\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
step: The quality factor is $Q = \omega_0/\gamma = f_0/\Delta f = 440/4.0 = 110$.
step: The free amplitude decays with $\tau = 2/\gamma \approx 0.080\ \text{s}$.
step: In that time the resonator completes $f_0\tau \approx 35$ cycles. A narrow resonance in frequency means a long ring in time; this reciprocal relation returns in Fourier analysis.

@mistakes
- Saying the steady state oscillates at $\omega_0$; it oscillates at the driving frequency $\omega$, and $\omega_0$ only sets the shape of the response.
- Taking $\delta = \arctan\!\big(\gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)\big)$ without checking the quadrant. Above resonance that gives a negative angle, but the true lag is between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$.
- Placing the amplitude peak at exactly $\omega_0$. It is at $\omega_r < \omega_0$; the power peak is the one at $\omega_0$.
- Expecting an infinite amplitude at resonance. Only the idealization $\gamma = 0$ does that; with damping $A(\omega_0) = F_0/(b\,\omega_0)$.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.30\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 60\ \text{N/m}$ and $b = 0.60\ \text{kg/s}$ is driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$ with $F_0 = 3.0\ \text{N}$. As the driving frequency $\omega \to 0$, what steady-state amplitude does the motion approach?
answer: 0.05
check: (lambda w: (3.0/0.30)/sqrt((60/0.30 - w**2)**2 + (0.60/0.30)**2*w**2))(1e-6)
unit: m
hint: When the force changes very slowly, the mass sits at the instantaneous equilibrium, where the spring force balances $F_0$.
hint: Set $kx = F_0$ and solve for $x$.
solution: As $\omega \to 0$, $A \to F_0/(m\omega_0^2) = F_0/k = 3.0/60 = 0.050\ \text{m}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $1.0\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 64\ \text{N/m}$ and $b = 0.50\ \text{kg/s}$ is driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$ with $F_0 = 2.0\ \text{N}$ at exactly its natural frequency $\omega_0$. Find the steady-state amplitude.
answer: 0.5
check: (lambda w: (2.0/1.0)/sqrt((64/1.0 - w**2)**2 + (0.50/1.0)**2*w**2))(sqrt(64/1.0))
unit: m
hint: At $\omega = \omega_0$ the term $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2$ vanishes, leaving only the damping term in the denominator.
hint: Then $A = \dfrac{F_0/m}{\gamma\omega_0}$ with $\gamma = b/m$.
solution: $\omega_0 = 8\ \text{rad/s}$ and $\gamma = 0.50\ \text{s}^{-1}$, so $A = \dfrac{2.0}{(0.50)(8)} = 0.50\ \text{m}$. Equivalently $A = F_0/(b\omega_0)$.

@practice num
prompt: A driven oscillator has $\omega_0 = 10\ \text{rad/s}$ and damping rate $\gamma = 4.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$. At what driving frequency is the steady-state displacement amplitude largest? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 9.59
check: max(np.linspace(5, 10, 50001), key=lambda w: 1/sqrt((100 - w**2)**2 + 16*w**2))
tol: 0.5%
unit: rad/s
hint: Maximizing $A(\omega)$ is the same as minimizing $(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2 + \gamma^2\omega^2$.
hint: Let $u = \omega^2$ and set the derivative with respect to $u$ to zero. You should find $\omega_r^2 = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/2$.
solution: With $u = \omega^2$, the quantity $(\omega_0^2-u)^2 + \gamma^2u$ is smallest when $-2(\omega_0^2-u) + \gamma^2 = 0$, i.e. $u = \omega_0^2 - \gamma^2/2 = 100 - 8 = 92$. So $\omega_r = \sqrt{92} \approx 9.59\ \text{rad/s}$, slightly below $\omega_0$.

@practice num
prompt: For an oscillator with $\omega_0 = 10\ \text{rad/s}$ and $\gamma = 2.0\ \text{s}^{-1}$, find the phase lag $\delta$ of the displacement behind the driving force when $\omega = 12\ \text{rad/s}$. Give $\delta$ in radians between $0$ and $\pi$, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 2.64
check: cmath.phase(complex(10.0**2 - 12.0**2, 2.0*12.0))
tol: 0.01
unit: rad
hint: Use $\tan\delta = \gamma\omega/(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)$ and look at the sign of the denominator.
hint: Here $\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 = -44 < 0$, so $\delta$ lies between $\pi/2$ and $\pi$. Take the arctangent of the positive ratio $24/44$ and subtract it from $\pi$.
solution: $\tan\delta = 24/(-44)$ with $\sin\delta > 0$, so $\delta$ is in the second quadrant: $\delta = \pi - \arctan(24/44) = \pi - 0.499 \approx 2.64\ \text{rad}$. Above resonance the displacement lags the force by more than a quarter cycle.

@practice mc
prompt: Two driven oscillators have the same $\omega_0$ and the same driving force. Oscillator B has half the damping rate $\gamma$ of oscillator A. Compared with A's, B's resonance curve is
choice*: about twice as tall and half as wide.
choice: about half as tall and twice as wide.
choice: the same height but narrower.
choice: about twice as tall and twice as wide.
hint: At resonance $A(\omega_0) = F_0/(m\gamma\omega_0)$.
hint: The full width at half power is $\Delta\omega = \gamma$.
solution: The peak height is proportional to $1/\gamma$, so it doubles, and the width $\Delta\omega = \gamma$ halves. Hence $Q = \omega_0/\gamma$ doubles.

@quiz num
prompt: A $0.20\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 80\ \text{N/m}$ and $b = 0.40\ \text{kg/s}$ is driven by $F_0\cos\omega t$ with $F_0 = 1.0\ \text{N}$ and $\omega = 25\ \text{rad/s}$. Find the steady-state amplitude to three significant figures.
answer: 0.0217
check: (lambda w: (1.0/0.20)/sqrt((80/0.20 - w**2)**2 + (0.40/0.20)**2*w**2))(25.0)
tol: 1%
unit: m
hint: Compute $\omega_0^2 = k/m$, $\gamma = b/m$ and $F_0/m$ first.
hint: Insert them into $A = (F_0/m)\big/\sqrt{(\omega_0^2-\omega^2)^2+\gamma^2\omega^2}$.
solution: $\omega_0^2 = 400$, $\gamma = 2.0$ and $F_0/m = 5.0$. The denominator is $\sqrt{(400-625)^2 + (2.0)^2(625)} = \sqrt{50625 + 2500} \approx 230.5$, so $A = 5.0/230.5 \approx 0.0217\ \text{m}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the steady state of a sinusoidally driven, damped oscillator are true?
choice*: The displacement oscillates at the driving frequency $\omega$.
choice*: At $\omega = \omega_0$ the displacement lags the driving force by $\pi/2$.
choice: The steady-state amplitude depends on the initial position and velocity.
choice: For any amount of damping the amplitude peak is at exactly $\omega = \omega_0$.
hint: The transient carries the memory of the initial conditions, and it decays.
hint: Check the phase formula at $\omega = \omega_0$, and recall the formula for $\omega_r$.
solution: The transient dies out, so the steady state has the driving frequency and does not depend on the initial conditions. At $\omega_0$, $\tan\delta \to \infty$, so $\delta = \pi/2$. The amplitude peak is at $\omega_r = \sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/2}$, which is below $\omega_0$ whenever $\gamma > 0$.
