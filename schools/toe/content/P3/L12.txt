@article
Interference between two sources gave $\cos^2$ fringes. **Diffraction** is interference among a continuous row of sources: by Huygens' principle every point in an opening acts as a source of secondary waves, and the pattern on a distant screen is their sum.

### A single slit

Take a slit of width $a$ and a screen so far away that the rays from the slit are effectively parallel (**Fraunhofer**, or far-field, diffraction). Pair each point in the top half of the slit with the point $a/2$ below it. Their path difference at angle $\theta$ is $(a/2)\sin\theta$. If that equals $\lambda/2$, every pair cancels, so the first dark fringe is at $a\sin\theta = \lambda$. Dividing the slit into $2m$ strips extends the argument to every minimum:

$$a\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad m = \pm1, \pm2, \dots$$

(there is no minimum at $m = 0$). The full pattern is

$$I(\theta) = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2, \qquad \beta = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}.$$

The central maximum lies between the $m = \pm1$ minima, with angular width $2\lambda/a$, or $2\lambda D/a$ on a screen at distance $D$. The next peaks are tiny: the first is only $4.7\%$ of the central one. A narrower slit spreads the light more, because a smaller $a$ means a larger $\lambda/a$.

### Two slits of finite width

Slits of width $a$ and separation $d$ give

$$I(\theta) = I_{\max}\,\cos^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\Big)\left(\frac{\sin\beta}{\beta}\right)^2.$$

The fast fringes of Lesson 11 (spacing $\lambda D/d$) sit under the slow single-slit envelope (zeros at $\pm\lambda D/a$). If a bright fringe $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$ falls exactly on an envelope zero $a\sin\theta = m'\lambda$, that order is **missing**. For $d = 3a$ the orders $m = \pm3, \pm6, \dots$ vanish.

### Limits of the approximations

- **Far field.** The formulas assume $D \gg a^2/\lambda$. Closer to the slit the pattern is Fresnel diffraction and the minima are not at $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$.
- **Small angles.** Writing $y = D\theta$ instead of $y = D\tan\theta$ makes a relative error of about $\theta^2/3$: $0.3\%$ at $\theta = 0.1\ \text{rad}$ and $1\%$ at $10^\circ$. For widely spaced orders, use $\sin\theta_m = m\lambda/d$ and $y = D\tan\theta_m$.
- **Coherent, monochromatic light.** White light smears the fringes because each color has its own spacing.

### Polarization

Light is a transverse wave: its electric field is perpendicular to the direction of travel. In **linearly polarized** light the field oscillates along one direction. An ideal polarizer passes only the field component along its axis. If the incident field makes an angle $\theta$ with the axis, the transmitted amplitude is $E_0\cos\theta$, and since intensity goes as amplitude squared we get **Malus's law**:

$$I = I_0\cos^2\theta.$$

Unpolarized light has random polarization, and an ideal polarizer transmits half of it: $I = I_0/2$. A wave on a string has two independent transverse polarizations ($y$ and $z$). A longitudinal wave such as sound in air has none, since it oscillates along the direction of travel, so it cannot be polarized.

@example
title: One slit, one pattern
problem: Light of wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ passes through a single slit of width $0.10\ \text{mm}$ onto a screen $2.0\ \text{m}$ away. Find the width of the central maximum and the relative intensity at $y = 6.0\ \text{mm}$ from the center.
step: The first minimum is at $\sin\theta = \lambda/a = 6.0\times10^{-3}$, so $y_1 = D\tan\theta \approx (2.0)(6.0\times10^{-3}) = 12\ \text{mm}$.
step: The central maximum extends from $-12\ \text{mm}$ to $+12\ \text{mm}$, a width of $24\ \text{mm}$.
step: At $y = 6.0\ \text{mm}$, $\sin\theta \approx y/D = 3.0\times10^{-3}$, so $\beta = \pi a\sin\theta/\lambda = \pi(1.0\times10^{-4})(3.0\times10^{-3})/(6.0\times10^{-7}) = \pi/2$.
step: Then $I/I_0 = (\sin\beta/\beta)^2 = (1/(\pi/2))^2 = 4/\pi^2 \approx 0.41$. Halfway to the first minimum, the intensity has fallen to about $41\%$ of the peak.

@example
title: Predicting double-slit fringes and checking the limits
problem: Light of wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$ passes through two slits of width $a = 0.050\ \text{mm}$ and separation $d = 0.20\ \text{mm}$ onto a screen $D = 2.0\ \text{m}$ away. Predict the fringe pattern and check the approximations.
step: The fringe spacing is $\Delta y = \lambda D/d = (5.0\times10^{-7})(2.0)/(2.0\times10^{-4}) = 5.0\ \text{mm}$.
step: The envelope has its first zero at $y = \lambda D/a = (5.0\times10^{-7})(2.0)/(5.0\times10^{-5}) = 20\ \text{mm}$, which is $4\Delta y$.
step: Since $d/a = 4$, the $m = \pm4$ bright fringes fall exactly on the envelope zeros and are missing. Inside the central maximum the bright fringes are $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \pm3$: seven fringes, dimmer toward the edges.
step: Far field: $a^2/\lambda = (5.0\times10^{-5})^2/(5.0\times10^{-7}) = 5.0\ \text{mm}$, far smaller than $D = 2.0\ \text{m}$, so Fraunhofer diffraction applies.
step: Small angles: the largest angle used is at the envelope zero, $\sin\theta = \lambda/a = 0.010$, and $\tan\theta$ differs from $\theta$ by about $\theta^2/3 \approx 3\times10^{-5}$. The formulas would fail if $d$ were only a few wavelengths, which puts the fringes at large angles.

@mistakes
- Using the slit width $a$ where the slit separation $d$ belongs, or the reverse. The envelope zeros use $a$ and the fast fringes use $d$.
- Expecting a minimum at $m = 0$ in single-slit diffraction. The center is the brightest point; the central maximum is bounded by the $m = \pm1$ minima.
- Applying small-angle formulas to large angles without checking. The error in $\tan\theta \approx \theta$ is about $\theta^2/3$.
- Forgetting the factor of $\tfrac12$ for unpolarized light at the first polarizer, or applying Malus's law to an unpolarized beam.

unit: mm

solution: $y_1 = D\lambda/a = (4.0)(5.0\times10^{-7})/(2.0\times10^{-4}) = 1.0\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}$, so the full width is $2y_1 = 20\ \text{mm}$.

@practice num
prompt: A single slit of width $0.10\ \text{mm}$ is illuminated by light of wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$. At the angle $\theta = 3.75\times10^{-3}\ \text{rad}$, what is $I/I_0$? Give three significant figures.
answer: 0.0901
check: (lambda b: (sin(b)/b)**2)(pi*0.10e-3*sin(3.75e-3)/500e-9)
tol: 1%
hint: First compute $\beta = \pi a\sin\theta/\lambda$.
hint: You should find $\beta = 3\pi/4$. Then $I/I_0 = (\sin\beta/\beta)^2$.
solution: $\beta = \pi(1.0\times10^{-4})(3.75\times10^{-3})/(5.0\times10^{-7}) = 3\pi/4 = 2.356$. Then $\sin\beta = 0.7071$, so $I/I_0 = (0.7071/2.356)^2 = 0.0901$.

@practice num
prompt: A double slit has separation $d = 0.050\ \text{mm}$ and is lit by light of wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$. For the $m = 10$ bright fringe, by what percentage does the exact screen position $D\tan\theta_m$ exceed the small-angle prediction $m\lambda D/d$? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.50
check: (tan(arcsin(10*500e-9/50e-6))/(10*500e-9/50e-6) - 1)*100
tol: 0.01
unit: %
hint: The exact angle satisfies $\sin\theta_m = m\lambda/d$. The small-angle prediction is $y/D = m\lambda/d$.
hint: Here $m\lambda/d = 0.100$. Compute $\tan\theta_m = \sin\theta_m/\sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta_m}$ and compare it with $0.100$.
solution: $\sin\theta_{10} = 0.100$, so $\tan\theta_{10} = 0.100/\sqrt{0.99} = 0.10050$. The small-angle prediction is $0.100$, so the exact position is larger by $0.50\%$. This matches the estimate $\theta^2/2 \approx 0.5\%$: the $\tan\theta$ versus $\theta$ error is about $\theta^2/3$, and the $\theta$ versus $\sin\theta$ error adds about $\theta^2/6$.

@practice num
prompt: Unpolarized light of intensity $800\ \text{W/m}^2$ passes through two ideal polarizers in a row. The transmission axes differ by $30^\circ$. What intensity emerges?
answer: 300
check: 800.0/2*cos(pi/6)**2
unit: W/m^2
hint: The first polarizer transmits half of the unpolarized intensity.
hint: The second applies Malus's law with $\theta = 30^\circ$ relative to the now-polarized light.
solution: After the first polarizer, $I_1 = 800/2 = 400\ \text{W/m}^2$. After the second, $I_2 = I_1\cos^2 30^\circ = 400(0.75) = 300\ \text{W/m}^2$.

@practice self
prompt: Light of wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ illuminates two slits of width $a = 0.040\ \text{mm}$ and separation $d = 0.20\ \text{mm}$. The screen is $D = 2.5\ \text{m}$ away. (a) Predict the fringe spacing and the position of the first zero of the single-slit envelope. (b) Predict how many bright fringes appear inside the central envelope and whether any order is missing. (c) State the approximations you used and check that they hold here.
hint: The fast fringes depend on $d$, and the envelope depends on $a$. Compare the two scales on the screen.
hint: A bright fringe is missing when it falls on an envelope zero. For the checks, compare $D$ with $a^2/\lambda$ and estimate $\theta^2/3$ at the largest angle.
solution: (a) $\Delta y = \lambda D/d = (6.0\times10^{-7})(2.5)/(2.0\times10^{-4}) = 7.5\ \text{mm}$, and the envelope's first zero is at $\lambda D/a = (6.0\times10^{-7})(2.5)/(4.0\times10^{-5}) = 37.5\ \text{mm}$. (b) The ratio is $37.5/7.5 = 5 = d/a$, so the $m = \pm5$ fringes fall on the envelope zeros and are missing. Inside the envelope the bright fringes are $m = 0, \pm1, \dots, \pm4$, nine in all, getting dimmer toward the edges. (c) I used the far-field and small-angle approximations, and assumed coherent monochromatic light. Far field: $a^2/\lambda = (4.0\times10^{-5})^2/(6.0\times10^{-7}) \approx 2.7\ \text{mm} \ll 2.5\ \text{m}$. Small angle: the largest angle is at the envelope zero, $\sin\theta = \lambda/a = 0.015$, and $\theta^2/3 \approx 7.5\times10^{-5}$, so the error is under $0.01\%$.
rubric: Computes the fringe spacing $\lambda D/d = 7.5\ \text{mm}$ using the separation $d$, not the width $a$.
rubric: Computes the envelope zero $\lambda D/a = 37.5\ \text{mm}$ and recognizes that $d/a = 5$.
rubric: Concludes that nine bright fringes appear and that $m = \pm5$ is missing.
rubric: States the far-field and small-angle approximations (and coherent light) and checks them with numbers.

@quiz num
prompt: A double slit has slit width $a = 0.040\ \text{mm}$ and separation $d = 0.18\ \text{mm}$. How many bright fringes lie inside the central maximum of the single-slit envelope? (Count a fringe only if it is not on an envelope zero.)
answer: 9
check: len([m for m in range(-20, 21) if abs(m)*40 < 180])
hint: A bright fringe of order $m$ is inside the central envelope when $m\lambda/d < \lambda/a$, that is $|m| < d/a$.
hint: Here $d/a = 4.5$, which is not an integer, so no order lands exactly on a zero.
solution: $d/a = 0.18/0.040 = 4.5$, so orders with $|m| < 4.5$ lie inside the central envelope: $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \pm4$. That is $9$ fringes, and none is missing because $4.5$ is not an integer.

@quiz num
prompt: Unpolarized light of intensity $800\ \text{W/m}^2$ passes through three ideal polarizers in a row. The second axis is $45^\circ$ from the first, and the third axis is $90^\circ$ from the first (so $45^\circ$ from the second). What intensity emerges?
answer: 100
check: 800.0/2*cos(pi/4)**2*cos(pi/4)**2
unit: W/m^2
hint: The first polarizer halves the unpolarized intensity.
hint: Apply Malus's law at each later polarizer, using the angle between consecutive axes.
solution: $I_1 = 800/2 = 400$, $I_2 = 400\cos^2 45^\circ = 200$, and $I_3 = 200\cos^2 45^\circ = 100\ \text{W/m}^2$. Without the middle polarizer, crossed polarizers would transmit nothing.
