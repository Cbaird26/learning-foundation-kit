@article
Waves obey superposition, so where two waves overlap their displacements add. Whether they reinforce or cancel depends on their relative phase. This is **interference**. A steady pattern appears only for **coherent** waves, which keep a fixed phase relationship (for instance, light from one source that has passed through two slits). Two independent light bulbs are incoherent: their intensities simply add.

### Path difference and phase difference

If two waves of wavelength $\lambda$ travel different distances to the same point, their phase difference is

$$\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\,\Delta, \qquad \Delta = \text{path difference}.$$

The waves reinforce when $\Delta = m\lambda$ and cancel when $\Delta = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda$, with $m$ an integer (assuming they start in phase).

### Two slits

Two narrow slits a distance $d$ apart illuminate a distant screen ($D \gg d$). At angle $\theta$ the path difference is $\Delta = d\sin\theta$, so

$$\text{bright: } d\sin\theta = m\lambda, \qquad \text{dark: } d\sin\theta = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda.$$

On a screen a distance $D$ away, the position is $y = D\tan\theta$. For small angles $\sin\theta \approx \tan\theta \approx \theta$, so the bright fringes are at $y_m \approx m\lambda D/d$, evenly spaced by

$$\Delta y = \frac{\lambda D}{d}.$$

### Intensity

Add two equal waves with phase difference $\delta$: $E_0\cos\omega t + E_0\cos(\omega t + \delta) = 2E_0\cos\frac{\delta}{2}\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\delta}{2}\right)$. Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, so

$$I = 4I_0\cos^2\frac{\delta}{2} = 4I_0\cos^2\!\Big(\frac{\pi d\sin\theta}{\lambda}\Big),$$

where $I_0$ is the intensity from one slit alone. The peaks are $4I_0$, the minima are $0$, and the average is $2I_0$: energy is redistributed, not created.

### Many slits

For $N$ equally spaced coherent sources, adding the phasors $\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}e^{ij\delta}$ gives

$$I = I_0\,\frac{\sin^2(N\delta/2)}{\sin^2(\delta/2)}.$$

The principal maxima stay at $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, but their peak intensity is $N^2I_0$ and their angular width shrinks to about $\lambda/(Nd)$. This is why a diffraction grating gives sharp, bright lines.

### Thin films

Light reflecting from the front and back of a film of thickness $t$ and index $n$ interferes. At normal incidence the back reflection travels an extra $2t$ in the film, a phase $2\pi(2nt)/\lambda_0$, where $\lambda_0$ is the wavelength in vacuum. Reflection from a higher-index medium inverts the wave (like the fixed end in Lesson 8); reflection from a lower-index medium does not. For a soap film in air only the front reflection is inverted, so the reflected light is bright when $2nt = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda_0$ and dark when $2nt = m\lambda_0$.

@example
title: Double-slit fringes
problem: Light of wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ passes through two slits $0.20\ \text{mm}$ apart onto a screen $1.50\ \text{m}$ away. Find the fringe spacing, the position of the third bright fringe, and the intensity at $y = 1.0\ \text{mm}$ relative to a bright fringe.
step: The fringe spacing is $\Delta y = \lambda D/d = (600\times10^{-9})(1.50)/(0.20\times10^{-3}) = 4.5\ \text{mm}$.
step: The third bright fringe is at $y_3 = 3\Delta y = 13.5\ \text{mm}$. The first dark fringe is halfway to the first bright one, at $2.25\ \text{mm}$.
step: Check the small-angle assumption: $\theta_3 \approx y_3/D = 9.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{rad}$, so $\tan\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ differ from $\theta$ by less than $0.01\%$.
step: At $y = 1.0\ \text{mm}$ the phase difference is $\delta = 2\pi\,y/\Delta y = 2\pi(1.0/4.5) \approx 1.40\ \text{rad}$.
step: Then $I/I_{\max} = \cos^2(\delta/2) = \cos^2(40^\circ) \approx 0.59$: the point is $59\%$ as bright as a fringe peak.

@example
title: Colors of a soap film
problem: A soap film ($n = 1.33$) of thickness $250\ \text{nm}$ hangs in air and is lit by white light at normal incidence. Which wavelengths are strongly reflected, and which are missing from the reflection?
step: Only the front reflection (air to film) is inverted, so reflected waves are in phase when $2nt = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda_0$.
step: The optical path is $2nt = 2(1.33)(250\ \text{nm}) = 665\ \text{nm}$.
step: Bright reflection: $\lambda_0 = 665/(m + \tfrac12)$, giving $1330\ \text{nm}$ ($m = 0$, infrared), $443\ \text{nm}$ ($m = 1$) and $266\ \text{nm}$ ($m = 2$, ultraviolet). Only $443\ \text{nm}$, violet-blue, is visible.
step: Dark reflection: $2nt = m\lambda_0$ gives $\lambda_0 = 665\ \text{nm}$ for $m = 1$. Red light is missing from the reflection, so the film looks blue.

@mistakes
- Using the full path length instead of the path difference, or forgetting the extra $\pi$ phase shift when reflecting from a higher-index medium.
- Using the wavelength in the film where the vacuum wavelength $\lambda_0$ belongs. Either use $2nt$ with $\lambda_0$ or $2t$ with $\lambda_0/n$, not a mixture.
- Adding intensities for coherent sources, or adding amplitudes for incoherent ones.
- Mixing up the slit separation $d$ and the screen distance $D$ in $y_m = m\lambda D/d$.

@practice num
prompt: Two coherent waves of wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ reach a point after traveling paths that differ by $450\ \text{nm}$. What is their phase difference? Give an exact answer.
answer: 3pi/2
check: 2*pi*450.0/600.0
unit: rad
hint: The phase difference is $2\pi$ times the path difference measured in wavelengths.
hint: The path difference is $450/600 = 3/4$ of a wavelength.
solution: $\delta = 2\pi\Delta/\lambda = 2\pi(450/600) = 3\pi/2\ \text{rad}$. This is neither constructive nor destructive: the waves are a quarter cycle short of being exactly in phase.

@practice num
prompt: A double slit with separation $0.25\ \text{mm}$ is illuminated by light of wavelength $500\ \text{nm}$. What is the spacing of the bright fringes on a screen $2.0\ \text{m}$ away? Give your answer in millimetres.
answer: 4
check: 500e-9*2.0/0.25e-3*1000
unit: mm
hint: Use the small-angle fringe spacing $\Delta y = \lambda D/d$.
hint: Work in metres: $\lambda = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$ and $d = 2.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}$.
solution: $\Delta y = (5.0\times10^{-7})(2.0)/(2.5\times10^{-4}) = 4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 4.0\ \text{mm}$.

@practice num
prompt: In a double-slit experiment the slit separation is $0.50\ \text{mm}$ and the screen is $3.0\ \text{m}$ away. The fifth bright fringe from the center lies $15\ \text{mm}$ from the central maximum. Find the wavelength in nanometres.
answer: 500
check: 15e-3*0.50e-3/(5*3.0)*1e9
unit: nm
hint: The fifth bright fringe has $m = 5$, and $y_m = m\lambda D/d$.
hint: Solve for $\lambda = y\,d/(mD)$ and convert to nanometres at the end.
solution: $\lambda = \dfrac{(15\times10^{-3})(0.50\times10^{-3})}{5(3.0)} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 500\ \text{nm}$.

@practice num
prompt: In a double-slit experiment each slit alone produces intensity $I_0$ at a point on the screen. At a point where the path difference from the two slits is exactly one third of a wavelength, what is the intensity in units of $I_0$?
answer: 1
check: abs(1 + cmath.exp(1j*2*pi/3))**2
hint: The phase difference is $\delta = 2\pi/3$. Add two unit phasors separated by that angle.
hint: Use $I = 4I_0\cos^2(\delta/2)$.
solution: $I = 4I_0\cos^2(\pi/3) = 4I_0(1/2)^2 = I_0$. Two phasors $120^\circ$ apart add up to a phasor of the same length as each.

@quiz num
prompt: A soap film ($n = 1.33$) of thickness $300\ \text{nm}$ hangs in air and is illuminated at normal incidence by white light. Which visible wavelength (between $400\ \text{nm}$ and $700\ \text{nm}$) is most strongly reflected? Give your answer in nanometres.
answer: 532
check: [l for l in [2*1.33*300.0/(m + 0.5) for m in range(0, 6)] if 400 < l < 700][0]
unit: nm
hint: Only the front-surface reflection is inverted, so reflection is bright when $2nt = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda_0$.
hint: Compute $2nt$, then find which value of $m$ puts $2nt/(m + \tfrac12)$ in the visible range.
solution: $2nt = 2(1.33)(300) = 798\ \text{nm}$. Then $\lambda_0 = 798/(m + \tfrac12)$ gives $1596\ \text{nm}$ for $m = 0$, $532\ \text{nm}$ for $m = 1$ and $319\ \text{nm}$ for $m = 2$. Only $532\ \text{nm}$ (green) is visible.

@quiz num
prompt: A grating has $500$ lines per millimetre and is illuminated at normal incidence by light of wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$. How many bright principal maxima (including the central one) can be observed?
answer: 7
check: 2*floor((1e-3/500)/600e-9) + 1
hint: Maxima occur where $d\sin\theta = m\lambda$, and $\sin\theta$ cannot exceed $1$.
hint: The slit spacing is $d = 1/500\ \text{mm} = 2.0\ \mu\text{m}$, so $m \le d/\lambda$.
solution: $d/\lambda = 2.0\times10^{-6}/6.0\times10^{-7} = 3.33$, so $|m| \le 3$. The maxima are $m = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \pm3$, which is $7$ in all.
