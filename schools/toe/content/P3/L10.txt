@article
On an ideal string every sinusoid travels at the same speed $v$, so a pulse, which is a sum of sinusoids, keeps its shape. In most real media $\omega$ is not proportional to $k$, and a pulse changes shape as it moves. This is **dispersion**, and the function $\omega(k)$ is the **dispersion relation**.

### Phase velocity and group velocity

A single sinusoid $\cos(kx - \omega t)$ has crests moving at the **phase velocity**

$$v_p = \frac{\omega}{k}.$$

A pulse built from wavenumbers clustered near $k_0$ looks like ripples inside an envelope. Expanding $\omega(k) \approx \omega_0 + \omega'(k_0)(k - k_0)$ shows that the envelope moves at the **group velocity**

$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk}\bigg|_{k_0},$$

while the ripples move at $v_p$. In a non-absorbing medium, energy and signals travel at $v_g$. The two are equal at every $k$ only for $\omega = vk$.

| medium | $\omega(k)$ | $v_p$ | $v_g$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| ideal string | $vk$ | $v$ | $v$ |
| deep water waves | $\sqrt{gk}$ | $\sqrt{g/k}$ | $\tfrac12\sqrt{g/k} = v_p/2$ |
| free quantum particle | $\hbar k^2/2m$ | $\hbar k/2m$ | $\hbar k/m = 2v_p$ |

### Why a pulse spreads

A sharp pulse needs many wavenumbers whose crests line up at one place at $t = 0$. If their speeds differ, they drift out of step and the pulse smears out. The speeds differ when $\omega'' = d^2\omega/dk^2 \ne 0$: components separated by $\Delta k$ have group velocities that differ by about $|\omega''|\,\Delta k$. No energy is lost, but it is spread over a larger region.

For a Gaussian pulse whose amplitude envelope is $e^{-x^2/4\sigma_0^2}$ (so its intensity has rms width $\sigma_0$ and the wavenumber spread is $\Delta k \approx 1/2\sigma_0$), the second-order expansion gives

$$\sigma(t) = \sigma_0\sqrt{1 + \left(\frac{\omega''\,t}{2\sigma_0^2}\right)^2}.$$

The width stays near $\sigma_0$ until $t \sim t_s = 2\sigma_0^2/|\omega''|$ and then grows linearly. Narrower pulses contain more wavenumbers, so they spread sooner.

### Reconstructing a pulse from modes

```python
import numpy as np

sigma0, w2 = 1.0, 0.5               # initial width (m); omega'' (m^2/s)
k = np.linspace(-6, 6, 1201)        # wavenumbers
x = np.linspace(-40, 40, 801)
a = np.exp(-sigma0**2 * k**2)       # Gaussian spectrum <-> pulse of rms width sigma0

def rms_width(t):
    # sum the modes exp(i(kx - omega t)) with omega = w2*k**2/2
    psi = (a*np.exp(1j*(np.outer(x, k) - 0.5*w2*k**2*t))).sum(axis=1)
    I = np.abs(psi)**2
    return np.sqrt((x**2*I).sum() / I.sum())

print(rms_width(0), rms_width(20))  # about 1.0 and 5.1
```

At $t = 0$ the modes add in phase and make a narrow pulse. At $t = 20$ their phases have scrambled and the same modes make a pulse about five times wider, as the formula predicts.

@example
title: Crests and groups on deep water
problem: Deep-water waves obey $\omega = \sqrt{gk}$ with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. For a wavelength of $2.0\ \text{m}$, find $v_p$ and $v_g$. Which way do crests move through a group of such waves?
step: The wavenumber is $k = 2\pi/\lambda = \pi\ \text{rad/m}$, so $\omega = \sqrt{9.8\pi} \approx 5.55\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: The phase velocity is $v_p = \omega/k = \sqrt{g/k} \approx 1.77\ \text{m/s}$.
step: The group velocity is $v_g = d\omega/dk = \tfrac12\sqrt{g/k} \approx 0.88\ \text{m/s}$, exactly half of $v_p$.
step: Crests move faster than the group, so each crest appears at the back of the group, travels forward through it, and fades at the front.

@example
title: How fast does a pulse spread?
problem: A Gaussian pulse with $\sigma_0 = 1.0\ \text{m}$ travels in a medium with $\omega = 0.25k^2$ (SI units). Find $\omega''$, the spreading time $t_s$, and the widths at $t = 10\ \text{s}$ and $t = 20\ \text{s}$.
step: Differentiating twice, $\omega' = 0.5k$ and $\omega'' = 0.5\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$.
step: The spreading time is $t_s = 2\sigma_0^2/\omega'' = 2(1.0)/0.5 = 4.0\ \text{s}$.
step: At $t = 10\ \text{s}$, $\omega''t/2\sigma_0^2 = 0.5(10)/2 = 2.5$, so $\sigma = \sqrt{1 + 6.25} \approx 2.7\ \text{m}$.
step: At $t = 20\ \text{s}$ the ratio is $5$, so $\sigma = \sqrt{26} \approx 5.1\ \text{m}$, matching the output of the program above.

@mistakes
- Using $v_p = \omega/k$ as the speed of a pulse. The envelope moves at $v_g = d\omega/dk$.
- Computing $v_g$ as $\omega/k$ or as $d(\omega/k)/dk$. It is the derivative of $\omega$ itself with respect to $k$.
- Assuming $v_g < v_p$ always. For deep water $v_g = v_p/2$, but for a free quantum particle $v_g = 2v_p$.
- Blaming spreading on energy loss. Dispersion spreads a pulse without absorbing any energy.

@practice self
prompt: Write a short Python program that builds a Gaussian pulse of rms width $\sigma_0 = 1.0\ \text{m}$ from plane-wave modes, $\psi(x,t) = \sum_k a(k)\,e^{i(kx - \omega(k)t)}$ with $a(k) = e^{-\sigma_0^2k^2}$. For (a) a non-dispersive medium $\omega = 2k$ and (b) a dispersive medium $\omega = 0.25k^2$, print the mean position and rms width of $|\psi|^2$ at $t = 0$ and $t = 10\ \text{s}$. State what you expect to see in each case and compare (b) with the formula for $\sigma(t)$.
hint: Use np.outer(x, k) to form the phases $kx$ for every position and wavenumber, subtract $\omega(k)t$, exponentiate and sum over $k$.
hint: The mean is $\sum xI/\sum I$ and the rms width is $\sqrt{\sum (x - \text{mean})^2I/\sum I}$, with $I = |\psi|^2$.
solution: ```python
import numpy as np

sigma0 = 1.0
k = np.linspace(-6, 6, 1201)
x = np.linspace(-40, 40, 801)
a = np.exp(-sigma0**2 * k**2)

def stats(omega, t):
    psi = (a*np.exp(1j*(np.outer(x, k) - omega(k)*t))).sum(axis=1)
    I = np.abs(psi)**2
    mean = (x*I).sum() / I.sum()
    width = np.sqrt(((x - mean)**2*I).sum() / I.sum())
    return mean, width

for name, omega in [("a", lambda k: 2*k), ("b", lambda k: 0.25*k**2)]:
    for t in (0, 10):
        print(name, t, stats(omega, t))
```
In (a) the mean moves from $0$ to $20\ \text{m}$ (speed $2\ \text{m/s}$) while the width stays at $1.0\ \text{m}$: the pulse translates without changing shape. In (b) the mean stays at $0$ and the width grows from $1.0\ \text{m}$ to about $2.7\ \text{m}$ at $t = 10\ \text{s}$. The formula with $\omega'' = 0.5\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$ gives $\sigma = \sqrt{1 + (0.5 \times 10/2)^2} = \sqrt{7.25} \approx 2.69\ \text{m}$.

rubric: Computes the mean and rms width from $|\psi|^2$, subtracting the mean when finding the width.

rubric: Predicts and reports for (b) a stationary center with width growing to about $2.7\ \text{m}$, in agreement with $\sigma_0\sqrt{1 + (\omega''t/2\sigma_0^2)^2}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Short ripples on water, dominated by surface tension, obey $\omega = Ck^{3/2}$, where $C$ is a constant. Find the ratio $v_g/v_p$. Give an exact fraction.
answer: 3/2
check: nderiv(lambda k: 1.7*k**1.5, 2.0)/(1.7*2.0**1.5/2.0)
hint: Differentiate $\omega = Ck^{3/2}$ with respect to $k$.
hint: Divide $d\omega/dk$ by $\omega/k$; the constants $C$ and $k$ cancel.
solution: $v_p = \omega/k = Ck^{1/2}$ and $v_g = d\omega/dk = \tfrac32Ck^{1/2}$, so $v_g/v_p = 3/2$. (In general, $\omega \propto k^p$ gives $v_g/v_p = p$.)
