@article
Bounded operators on an infinite-dimensional space can behave very differently from matrices. **Compact operators** are the ones that still behave like matrices, which is why they come with a clean spectral theory.

### Definition

A bounded operator $T:X\to Y$ between normed spaces is **compact** if the image $T(B)$ of the closed unit ball $B$ is relatively compact, meaning its closure is compact. Equivalently: for every bounded sequence $(x_n)$, the sequence $(Tx_n)$ has a norm convergent subsequence. Compact operators are bounded by definition.

Basic facts:

- A bounded operator of finite rank (range of finite dimension) is compact.
- The compact operators form a subspace of $B(X)$, closed in the operator norm, and $ST$ is compact if either factor is.
- On a Hilbert space, $T$ is compact if and only if it is a **norm limit of finite-rank operators**.
- If $T$ is compact on a Hilbert space and $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ weakly, then $Tx_n\to Tx$ **in norm**.

### Not compact: the identity

On $\ell^2$ the vectors $e_n$ form a bounded sequence with $\|e_n-e_m\|=\sqrt2$ for $n\neq m$, so no subsequence is Cauchy. Hence $I$ is not compact, and the same argument shows that no isometry on an infinite-dimensional space is compact. The closed unit ball is compact only in finite dimension.

### Diagonal operators

Let $D e_k=d_ke_k$ on $\ell^2$. The truncation $D_N$ keeping the first $N$ entries has finite rank, and $\|D-D_N\|=\sup_{k>N}|d_k|$. This tends to $0$ if and only if $d_k\to0$. So

$$D\ \text{is compact}\iff d_k\to0.$$

Boundedness requires only $\sup_k|d_k|<\infty$; convergence of the series $\sum d_k$ is not needed.

### Integral operators

For a kernel $k\in L^2\big((0,1)^2\big)$, the operator $(Kf)(x)=\int_0^1k(x,y)f(y)\,dy$ on $L^2(0,1)$ is compact (a **Hilbert–Schmidt** operator), and

$$\|K\|\le\|k\|_{L^2}=\Big(\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1|k(x,y)|^2\,dx\,dy\Big)^{1/2}.$$

The bound is generally not sharp. For a rank-one kernel $k(x,y)=u(x)v(y)$ it is an equality. The Volterra operator $(Vf)(x)=\int_0^xf$ has kernel $\mathbf 1_{y<x}$ with $\|k\|_{L^2}=\sqrt{1/2}$, so it is compact on $L^2(0,1)$. By contrast, multiplication by $x$ on $L^2(0,1)$ is bounded and self-adjoint but **not** compact, as the next weeks will show.

### Why compact operators matter

A compact operator has the pointwise-smoothing character of a limit of matrices: it squeezes oscillation and spreading away. That is why its non-zero spectrum consists of isolated eigenvalues of finite multiplicity that can accumulate only at $0$, the structure used in the spectral theorem next week.

```python
import numpy as np
n = 400
x = (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n
K = np.minimum.outer(x, x) / n               # kernel min(x, y), discretized
print(np.linalg.svd(K, compute_uv=False)[:4])               # decays: about 0.405, 0.045, 0.016, 0.008
print(np.linalg.svd(np.eye(n), compute_uv=False)[:4])       # identity: every singular value is 1
```

@example
title: Diagonal operators: compact or not
problem: Decide whether $D=\operatorname{diag}(1/k)$ and $E=\operatorname{diag}(1-1/k)$ are compact on $\ell^2$.
step: For $D$, let $D_N$ keep the first $N$ entries. Then $D_N$ has finite rank and $\|D-D_N\|=\sup_{k>N}\frac1k=\frac1{N+1}\to0$.
step: A norm limit of finite-rank operators is compact, so $D$ is compact. Consistently, $\|De_n\|=\frac1n\to0$ although $e_n\rightharpoonup0$.
step: For $E$, the entries tend to $1$, not $0$. Then $\|Ee_n\|=1-\frac1n\to1$, so $Ee_n$ has no norm convergent subsequence: if it converged along a subsequence, the limit would have to be $0$ (as $e_n\rightharpoonup0$ and $E$ is weakly continuous), contradicting $\|Ee_n\|\to1$.
step: So $E$ is not compact. Compactness depends on the entries tending to $0$, not on their being bounded or being less than $1$.

@example
title: A rank-one integral operator
problem: Let $(Kf)(x)=x\int_0^1y\,f(y)\,dy$ on $L^2(0,1)$. Show $K$ is compact and compute $\|K\|$ and the Hilbert–Schmidt bound.
step: $Kf=\langle u,f\rangle v$ with $u(y)=y$ and $v(x)=x$, so $K$ has rank one and is compact.
step: By Cauchy–Schwarz, $\|Kf\|=|\langle u,f\rangle|\,\|v\|\le\|u\|\,\|v\|\,\|f\|$, and equality holds for $f=u$. So $\|K\|=\|u\|\|v\|$.
step: $\|u\|^2=\|v\|^2=\int_0^1t^2\,dt=\frac13$, so $\|K\|=\frac13$.
step: The kernel is $k(x,y)=xy$ and $\|k\|_{L^2}^2=\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^1x^2y^2\,dx\,dy=\frac19$, so $\|k\|_{L^2}=\frac13$. For rank-one kernels the bound is sharp.

@mistakes
- Treating "bounded" as "compact": the identity is bounded, and it is compact only in finite dimension.
- Believing that $\operatorname{diag}(d_k)$ is compact when the $d_k$ are merely bounded, or when $|d_k|<1$; the condition is $d_k\to0$.
- Using the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $\|k\|_{L^2}$ as the operator norm; it is only an upper bound.
- Applying compactness results to unbounded operators; a compact operator is bounded by definition.

prompt: Let $D$ be the diagonal operator on $\ell^2$ with entries $d_k=\dfrac{k}{k^2+1}$, and let $D_3$ keep only the first three entries (so $D_3$ has rank 3). Compute $\|D-D_3\|$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 4/17
check: max(abs(k/(k**2 + 1)) for k in range(4, 10000))
hint: $D-D_3$ is diagonal with entries $d_k$ for $k\ge4$ and $0$ before. Its norm is the supremum of those entries.
hint: The sequence $d_k$ is decreasing for $k\ge1$.
solution: $\|D-D_3\|=\sup_{k\ge4}\frac{k}{k^2+1}=d_4=\frac4{17}$, because $d_k$ decreases from $k=1$ on. Since this tends to $0$ as the cut-off grows, $D$ is a norm limit of finite-rank operators and is compact.

@practice num
prompt: Find the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $\|k\|_{L^2((0,1)^2)}$ of the kernel $k(x,y)=x+y$. Give an exact value.
answer: sqrt(7/6)
check: sqrt(quad2(lambda x, y: (x + y)**2, 0, 1, 0, 1))
hint: Expand $(x+y)^2=x^2+2xy+y^2$ and integrate each term over the unit square.
solution: $\iint x^2=\frac13$, $\iint y^2=\frac13$ and $\iint2xy=2\cdot\frac12\cdot\frac12=\frac12$. The total is $\frac13+\frac13+\frac12=\frac76$, so $\|k\|_{L^2}=\sqrt{7/6}$. This is an upper bound for the operator norm of $(Kf)(x)=\int_0^1(x+y)f(y)\,dy$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $D=\operatorname{diag}(d_k)$ on $\ell^2$ with $d_k\to0$. Prove directly from the definition (every bounded sequence has an image sequence with a convergent subsequence) that $D$ is compact.
hint: Take a bounded sequence $x^{(n)}$ and use a diagonal argument to get a subsequence whose first $N$ coordinates converge for every $N$.
hint: Control the tail with $\big\|(Dx)_{k>N}\big\|\le\sup_{k>N}|d_k|\,\|x\|$ and show the image subsequence is Cauchy.
solution: Let $\|x^{(n)}\|\le M$. Each coordinate sequence $(x^{(n)}_k)_n$ is bounded, so by a diagonal argument there is a subsequence (still written $x^{(n)}$) such that $x^{(n)}_k$ converges for every $k$. Fix $\varepsilon>0$ and choose $N$ with $\sup_{k>N}|d_k|<\varepsilon/(4M)$. Then for any $n,m$, $\|Dx^{(n)}-Dx^{(m)}\|^2=\sum_{k\le N}|d_k|^2|x^{(n)}_k-x^{(m)}_k|^2+\sum_{k>N}|d_k|^2|x^{(n)}_k-x^{(m)}_k|^2$. The second sum is at most $\sup_{k>N}|d_k|^2\,\|x^{(n)}-x^{(m)}\|^2<\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{4M}\big)^2(2M)^2=\tfrac{\varepsilon^2}{4}$. The first sum is a finite sum of terms that tend to $0$ as $n,m\to\infty$, so it is less than $\varepsilon^2/4$ for large $n,m$. Hence $(Dx^{(n)})$ is Cauchy, so it converges in $\ell^2$.
rubric: Extracts a subsequence with every coordinate convergent, using a diagonal argument.
rubric: Splits the squared distance into a finite part and a tail, and bounds the tail by $\sup_{k>N}|d_k|$ times a bound on $\|x^{(n)}-x^{(m)}\|$.
rubric: Concludes that the image subsequence is Cauchy and uses completeness of $\ell^2$.

prompt: Find the Hilbert–Schmidt norm $\|k\|_{L^2((0,1)^2)}$ of the kernel $k(x,y)=\min(x,y)$. Give an exact value.
answer: 1/sqrt(6)
check: sqrt(quad2(lambda x, y: min(x, y)**2, 0, 1, 0, 1))
hint: By symmetry $\iint\min(x,y)^2=2\int_0^1\int_0^xy^2\,dy\,dx$.
solution: $2\int_0^1\int_0^xy^2\,dy\,dx=2\int_0^1\frac{x^3}{3}\,dx=\frac16$. So $\|k\|_{L^2}=\frac1{\sqrt6}\approx0.408$, an upper bound for the operator norm of the integral operator with kernel $\min(x,y)$. Its true norm is $4/\pi^2\approx0.405$, slightly smaller.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $T$ be a compact operator on a Hilbert space $H$ and let $x_n\rightharpoonup x$ weakly. Prove that $Tx_n\to Tx$ in norm. You may use that weakly convergent sequences are bounded, and that the adjoint $T^*$ exists with $\langle y,Tx\rangle=\langle T^*y,x\rangle$.
hint: Argue by contradiction: if not, some subsequence stays at distance at least $\varepsilon$ from $Tx$.
hint: Use compactness to pass to a further subsequence of $Tx_n$ that converges in norm, then identify the limit by testing against $y$.
solution: Suppose not. Then there are $\varepsilon>0$ and a subsequence with $\|Tx_{n_k}-Tx\|\ge\varepsilon$. The sequence $(x_{n_k})$ is bounded, so by compactness a further subsequence satisfies $Tx_{n_{k_j}}\to w$ in norm. For every $y\in H$, $\langle y,Tx_{n_{k_j}}\rangle=\langle T^*y,x_{n_{k_j}}\rangle\to\langle T^*y,x\rangle=\langle y,Tx\rangle$ by weak convergence, while also $\langle y,Tx_{n_{k_j}}\rangle\to\langle y,w\rangle$. Hence $\langle y,w-Tx\rangle=0$ for all $y$, so $w=Tx$. But then $\|Tx_{n_{k_j}}-Tx\|\to0$, contradicting the bound $\ge\varepsilon$.
rubric: Sets up the contradiction with a subsequence staying at distance $\varepsilon$ from $Tx$.
rubric: Uses boundedness and compactness to extract a norm convergent subsequence of $Tx_n$.
rubric: Identifies the limit as $Tx$ using weak convergence and the adjoint, and derives the contradiction.
