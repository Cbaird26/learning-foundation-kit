@article
The sequence spaces $\ell^p$ and the function spaces of earlier weeks belong to one family. For a measure space $(X,\mu)$ and $1\le p<\infty$ put

$$\|f\|_p=\Big(\int_X|f|^p\,d\mu\Big)^{1/p},\qquad L^p(X)=\{f\ \text{measurable}:\ \|f\|_p<\infty\}\,/\!\sim,$$

where $f\sim g$ means $f=g$ almost everywhere. The identification is forced. Last week you saw that $\int|f|^p=0$ only gives $f=0$ a.e., so without it $\|\cdot\|_p$ would not be definite. Elements of $L^p$ are therefore **classes** of functions, and "the value at a point" has no meaning for them. In quantum theory this is the right level of description: a wave function is an element of $L^2(\mathbb{R}^3)$.

For $p=\infty$, $\|f\|_\infty=\operatorname{ess\,sup}|f|=\inf\{M:\ |f|\le M\ \text{a.e.}\}$. Counting measure on $\mathbb{N}$ gives the sequence spaces $\ell^p$.

### Two inequalities

Exponents $p,q\in[1,\infty]$ are **conjugate** if $\frac1p+\frac1q=1$.

**Hölder.** $\displaystyle\int|fg|\,d\mu\le\|f\|_p\,\|g\|_q$. For $p=q=2$ this is Cauchy–Schwarz. It follows from Young's inequality $ab\le\frac{a^p}{p}+\frac{b^q}{q}$.

**Minkowski.** $\|f+g\|_p\le\|f\|_p+\|g\|_p$ for $1\le p\le\infty$. This is the triangle inequality, so $\|\cdot\|_p$ is a norm. For $0<p<1$ it fails, and there is no norm.

### Completeness

**Theorem (Riesz–Fischer).** $L^p(\mu)$ is a Banach space for $1\le p\le\infty$.

For $p<\infty$ the proof uses the series criterion from Week 2: if $\sum\|f_k\|_p<\infty$, Minkowski's inequality and the monotone convergence theorem show $\sum|f_k|$ is finite a.e., so the series converges a.e., and dominated convergence upgrades this to convergence in norm. As a consequence, $L^1[0,1]$ is exactly the space that supplies the limit missing from the Cauchy ramps of Week 2: continuous functions are dense in $L^p$ for $p<\infty$, and $L^p$ is the completion of $(C[0,1],\|\cdot\|_p)$.

### Which spaces contain which?

On a **finite** measure space, a larger exponent means a smaller space: $L^q\subseteq L^p$ whenever $p<q$ (Example 2). For counting measure the order reverses: $\ell^p\subseteq\ell^q$ for $p<q$, so $(1/k)_k\in\ell^2$ but $\notin\ell^1$. On all of $\mathbb{R}$, neither inclusion holds.

Power functions show the two ways a function can fail to be in $L^p$. On $(0,1)$, $x^{-a}\in L^p$ iff $ap<1$ (the singularity at $0$ must be mild). On $(1,\infty)$, $x^{-a}\in L^p$ iff $ap>1$ (the decay at infinity must be fast).

### Duality

For $1\le p<\infty$, every bounded functional on $L^p$ has the form $f\mapsto\int fg$ with $g\in L^q$ and the same norm, where $q$ is conjugate to $p$ ($\sigma$-finite measures when $p=1$). So $(L^p)^*=L^q$, and $L^2$ is its own dual. The dual of $L^\infty$ is strictly larger than $L^1$.

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

def lp_norm(f, p, a, b):
    return quad(lambda x: abs(f(x))**p, a, b)[0] ** (1 / p)

f = lambda x: x**-0.25                    # in L^p(0,1) exactly when p < 4
for p in (1, 2, 3):
    print(p, lp_norm(f, p, 0, 1))         # (1 - p/4)**(-1/p)
```

@example
title: A power function in several $L^p$ spaces
problem: For which $p\in[1,\infty)$ is $f(x)=x^{-1/4}$ in $L^p(0,1)$, and what is $\|f\|_p$ when it is?
step: $\|f\|_p^p=\int_0^1x^{-p/4}\,dx$. The antiderivative of $x^{-s}$ with $s\neq1$ is $\frac{x^{1-s}}{1-s}$, here with $s=p/4$.
step: If $p<4$, then $s<1$ and the integral equals $\frac{1}{1-p/4}$, which is finite. If $p\ge4$, then $s\ge1$ and the integral diverges at $0$, as $\int_0^1\frac{dx}{x}$ does.
step: So $f\in L^p(0,1)$ exactly when $p<4$, and then $\|f\|_p=\big(1-\tfrac p4\big)^{-1/p}$. For $p=2$ this gives $\sqrt2$.
step: The norms increase as $p\uparrow4$ and blow up at $p=4$. Note $f\in L^1\cap L^2\cap L^3$ but $f\notin L^4$, and $f$ is unbounded, so $f\notin L^\infty$.

@example
title: Inclusion of $L^q$ in $L^p$ on a finite measure space
problem: Let $\mu(X)<\infty$ and $1\le p<q<\infty$. Show $\|f\|_p\le\mu(X)^{1/p-1/q}\|f\|_q$, so $L^q\subseteq L^p$.
step: Set $r=q/p>1$ and let $r'$ be its conjugate exponent, so $\frac1r+\frac1{r'}=1$.
step: Apply Hölder to the functions $|f|^p$ and $1$ with exponents $r$ and $r'$: $\int|f|^p\cdot1\,d\mu\le\Big(\int|f|^{pr}\,d\mu\Big)^{1/r}\Big(\int1\,d\mu\Big)^{1/r'}$.
step: Since $pr=q$, the first factor is $\big(\int|f|^q\big)^{p/q}=\|f\|_q^p$, and the second is $\mu(X)^{1-p/q}$.
step: Take $p$-th roots: $\|f\|_p\le\|f\|_q\,\mu(X)^{\frac1p-\frac1q}$. The inclusion fails if $\mu(X)=\infty$: on $[1,\infty)$ the function $1/x$ is in $L^2$ but not in $L^1$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\|f\|_p=0$ as "$f=0$ at every point"; it gives only $f=0$ a.e., which is why $L^p$ consists of classes.
- Quoting an inclusion between $L^p$ spaces without checking the measure space: finite measure, counting measure and $\mathbb{R}$ behave differently.
- Applying Hölder with exponents that are not conjugate, such as $p=q=3$.
- Using the supremum instead of the essential supremum for $L^\infty$; a null set cannot change the norm.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $(f_k)$ be measurable functions on a measure space with $\sum_{k=1}^\infty\|f_k\|_1<\infty$. Prove that $\sum_kf_k$ converges almost everywhere to some $F\in L^1$, and that $\big\|F-\sum_{k\le N}f_k\big\|_1\to0$. Explain how this shows $L^1$ is complete.
hint: Apply monotone convergence to $G=\sum_k|f_k|$ to see that $G<\infty$ a.e.
hint: Use $G$ as the dominating function in the dominated convergence theorem.
solution: Let $G=\sum_k|f_k|\ge0$. By monotone convergence $\int G=\sum_k\|f_k\|_1<\infty$, so $G<\infty$ a.e. At each such point the series converges absolutely, so $F=\sum_kf_k$ exists a.e. and $|F|\le G$; thus $F\in L^1$. The partial sums $S_N=\sum_{k\le N}f_k$ satisfy $S_N\to F$ a.e. and $|S_N-F|\le2G$. Dominated convergence applied to $|S_N-F|$ gives $\|S_N-F\|_1\to0$. So every absolutely convergent series in $L^1$ converges in $L^1$, and by the criterion from Week 2 the space is complete.
