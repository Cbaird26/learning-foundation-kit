@article
A **linear functional** is a linear map $\phi:X\to\mathbb{K}$ into the scalars. The **dual space** $X^*=B(X,\mathbb{K})$ is the set of *bounded* functionals with the norm $\|\phi\|=\sup_{\|x\|\le1}|\phi(x)|$. Because $\mathbb{K}$ is complete, **$X^*$ is a Banach space even when $X$ is not**. Functionals are measurements: in quantum theory a state and an observable are paired through maps such as $x\mapsto\langle\psi,x\rangle$.

### Duality of the sequence norms

On $\mathbb{R}^n$ every linear functional is $\phi_a(x)=\sum_k a_kx_k$ for a unique vector $a$. How large $\phi_a$ is depends on the norm used for $x$. **Hölder's inequality** says

$$\Big|\sum_k a_kx_k\Big|\le\|a\|_q\,\|x\|_p\qquad\text{when }\tfrac1p+\tfrac1q=1,$$

with $q=\infty$ paired with $p=1$. The inequality is sharp, so with $x$ measured in $\|\cdot\|_p$ the functional has norm $\|a\|_q$:

- $p=1$: $\|\phi_a\|=\max_k|a_k|=\|a\|_\infty$; test vector $e_k$ at the largest $|a_k|$.
- $p=2$: $\|\phi_a\|=\|a\|_2$; this is Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality at $x=a$.
- $p=\infty$: $\|\phi_a\|=\sum_k|a_k|=\|a\|_1$; test vector $x_k=\operatorname{sign}(a_k)$.

So $(\ell^1)^*=\ell^\infty$ and $(\ell^2)^*=\ell^2$. The same holds for the infinite sequence spaces. The dual of $\ell^\infty$ is strictly larger than $\ell^1$, which is the first sign that $\ell^1$ is not reflexive.

### Functionals on function spaces

On $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm, **point evaluation** $\delta_{t_0}(f)=f(t_0)$ has norm $1$. For a continuous weight $w$, the functional $\phi_w(f)=\int_0^1w(t)f(t)\,dt$ satisfies $|\phi_w(f)|\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1|w|$, and $\|\phi_w\|=\int_0^1|w|$, because continuous functions of sup norm at most $1$ come arbitrarily close to $\operatorname{sign}(w)$. The supremum need not be attained.

Boundedness depends on the norm. With $\|\cdot\|_1$ on $C[0,1]$ the functional $\delta_0$ is unbounded: the tents $g_n(t)=\max(0,1-nt)$ have $\|g_n\|_1=\frac1{2n}$ yet $g_n(0)=1$. This is the reason the Dirac delta is not represented by an integrable function.

### Hahn–Banach

**Theorem (Hahn–Banach, normed version).** If $Y$ is a subspace of a normed space $X$ and $\phi\in Y^*$, then there is $\Phi\in X^*$ with $\Phi|_Y=\phi$ and $\|\Phi\|=\|\phi\|$.

The proof uses Zorn's lemma and is not repeated here. Its consequences are what you use. For $x_0\neq0$, extend the functional $\lambda x_0\mapsto\lambda\|x_0\|$ from the line spanned by $x_0$ (it has norm $1$) to get $\phi\in X^*$ with $\|\phi\|=1$ and $\phi(x_0)=\|x_0\|$. Hence $X^*$ separates points of $X$ and

$$\|x\|=\sup_{\|\phi\|\le1}|\phi(x)|.$$

The map sending $x$ to "evaluation at $x$" is an isometric embedding $X\to X^{**}$. If it is onto, $X$ is **reflexive**. Hilbert spaces are reflexive; $\ell^1$ and $C[0,1]$ are not.

```python
import numpy as np
a = np.array([3., -7., 2.])
print(max(abs(a)))                      # ||a||_inf: norm of phi_a on the l1 ball (vertices +-e_k)
signs = [(s1, s2, s3) for s1 in (-1, 1) for s2 in (-1, 1) for s3 in (-1, 1)]
print(max(abs(a @ np.array(s)) for s in signs))   # ||a||_1 = 12 on the cube (l-infinity ball)
```

@example
title: A functional on Euclidean $\mathbb{R}^3$
problem: Find $\|\phi\|$ for $\phi(x)=x_1-2x_2+2x_3$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the Euclidean norm.
step: Write $\phi(x)=a\cdot x$ with $a=(1,-2,2)$. By Cauchy–Schwarz, $|\phi(x)|\le\|a\|_2\|x\|_2$ and $\|a\|_2=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$. So $\|\phi\|\le3$.
step: Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz needs $x$ parallel to $a$. Take $x=a/3=(\tfrac13,-\tfrac23,\tfrac23)$, which has $\|x\|_2=1$.
step: Then $\phi(x)=\tfrac13+\tfrac43+\tfrac43=3$. The bound is attained, so $\|\phi\|=3$.

@example
title: A weighted integral on $C[0,1]$
problem: Find the norm of $\phi(f)=\int_0^1\sin(\pi t)\,f(t)\,dt$ on $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm.
step: Upper bound: $|\phi(f)|\le\int_0^1\sin(\pi t)\,|f(t)|\,dt\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1\sin(\pi t)\,dt$, since $\sin(\pi t)\ge0$ on $[0,1]$.
step: The integral is $\int_0^1\sin(\pi t)\,dt=\frac{2}{\pi}$, so $\|\phi\|\le\frac2\pi$.
step: Lower bound: for $f\equiv1$ we have $\|f\|_\infty=1$ and $\phi(f)=\frac2\pi$.
step: Hence $\|\phi\|=\frac2\pi$. When the weight changes sign, the test function would have to be the discontinuous function $\operatorname{sign}(w)$, and the norm is only approached.

@mistakes
- Writing $\|\phi_a\|=\|a\|_p$ instead of $\|a\|_q$: the exponents swap, so the dual of $\ell^1$ is $\ell^\infty$, not $\ell^1$.
- Computing $|\phi(x)|$ at one convenient vector and calling it the norm; the norm is the supremum over the whole unit ball.
- Treating a formula such as $f\mapsto f(0)$ as a functional on every normed space of functions; it is bounded for the sup norm and unbounded for the $L^1$ norm.
- Confusing $X^*$ with the algebraic dual of all linear functionals; only the bounded ones are in $X^*$.

@practice num
prompt: On $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm let $\phi(f)=\int_0^1(1-2t)\,f(t)\,dt$. Find $\|\phi\|$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 1/2
check: quad(lambda t: abs(1 - 2*t), 0, 0.5) + quad(lambda t: abs(1 - 2*t), 0.5, 1)
hint: The norm is $\int_0^1|w(t)|\,dt$ for the weight $w(t)=1-2t$. Split at the point where $w$ changes sign.
hint: The two pieces are areas of triangles with base $\tfrac12$ and height $1$.
solution: Upper bound: $|\phi(f)|\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1|1-2t|\,dt=\tfrac12\|f\|_\infty$, since the two triangles have areas $\tfrac14$ each. Lower bound: the continuous functions $f_n(t)=\max(-1,\min(1,n(\tfrac12-t)))$ have sup norm $1$ and agree with $\operatorname{sign}(1-2t)$ outside $|t-\tfrac12|<\tfrac1n$, so $\phi(f_n)\to\tfrac12$. Hence $\|\phi\|=\tfrac12$, approached but not attained.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that every bounded linear functional $\phi$ on $\ell^1$ has the form $\phi(x)=\sum_{k\ge1}a_kx_k$ for a bounded sequence $a$, and that $\|\phi\|=\|a\|_\infty=\sup_k|a_k|$.
hint: Set $a_k=\phi(e_k)$ and show that the partial sums $\sum_{k\le n}x_ke_k$ converge to $x$ in $\ell^1$.
hint: Use continuity of $\phi$ to pass $\phi$ through the limit. For the norm, bound $|a_k|$ using $\|e_k\|_1=1$, and bound $|\phi(x)|$ using the triangle inequality.
solution: Let $a_k=\phi(e_k)$. Since $\|e_k\|_1=1$, $|a_k|\le\|\phi\|$, so $a$ is bounded and $\|a\|_\infty\le\|\phi\|$. For $x\in\ell^1$ the partial sums $s_n=\sum_{k\le n}x_ke_k$ satisfy $\|x-s_n\|_1=\sum_{k>n}|x_k|\to0$. By linearity $\phi(s_n)=\sum_{k\le n}a_kx_k$ and by continuity $\phi(s_n)\to\phi(x)$, so $\phi(x)=\sum_k a_kx_k$. Finally $|\phi(x)|\le\sum_k|a_k||x_k|\le\|a\|_\infty\|x\|_1$, so $\|\phi\|\le\|a\|_\infty$. Hence $\|\phi\|=\|a\|_\infty$. Conversely each bounded $a$ defines a bounded functional by the same estimate.
