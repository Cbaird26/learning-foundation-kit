In $\mathbb{R}^n$ you measure length with the Pythagorean formula. Functional analysis asks which properties of "length" survive when the vectors are sequences or functions, and which do not. A **norm** is the abstract notion of length.

### Definition

Let $X$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. A **norm** is a function $\|\cdot\|:X\to[0,\infty)$ such that, for all $x,y\in X$ and all scalars $\lambda$:

1. (definiteness) $\|x\|=0$ if and only if $x=0$;
2. (homogeneity) $\|\lambda x\|=|\lambda|\,\|x\|$;
3. (triangle inequality) $\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|$.

The pair $(X,\|\cdot\|)$ is a **normed space**. It is a metric space with $d(x,y)=\|x-y\|$, so convergence, open balls and continuity keep their usual meaning: $x_n\to x$ means $\|x_n-x\|\to0$. A function satisfying 2 and 3 but not the "only if" in 1 is called a **seminorm**.

### Basic examples

On $\mathbb{R}^n$ (or $\mathbb{C}^n$):

$$\|x\|_1=\sum_{k=1}^n|x_k|,\qquad \|x\|_2=\Big(\sum_{k=1}^n|x_k|^2\Big)^{1/2},\qquad \|x\|_\infty=\max_{k}|x_k|.$$

On the space $C[0,1]$ of continuous functions $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$:

$$\|f\|_\infty=\max_{t\in[0,1]}|f(t)|,\qquad \|f\|_1=\int_0^1|f(t)|\,dt.$$

The maximum exists because a continuous function on a compact interval attains it. For $\|f\|_1$, definiteness uses continuity: if $f(t_0)\neq0$, then $|f|>\tfrac12|f(t_0)|$ on a small interval around $t_0$, so the integral is positive. Without continuity this argument fails, as you will see when we meet almost everywhere statements.

### Equivalent norms: where dimension matters

Two norms on $X$ are **equivalent** if there are constants $0<c\le C$ with $c\|x\|_a\le\|x\|_b\le C\|x\|_a$ for all $x$. Equivalent norms have the same convergent sequences and the same open sets. On $\mathbb{R}^n$,

$$\|x\|_\infty\le\|x\|_2\le\|x\|_1\le n\,\|x\|_\infty,$$

and in fact **any two norms on a finite-dimensional space are equivalent**. That is why finite-dimensional linear algebra never asked you to choose a norm.

In infinite dimensions this fails. On $C[0,1]$ take $f_n(t)=t^n$. Then $\|f_n\|_\infty=1$ but $\|f_n\|_1=\frac1{n+1}$, so no constant $c>0$ gives $c\|f\|_\infty\le\|f\|_1$. The sequence $f_n$ tends to $0$ in $\|\cdot\|_1$ but not in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$. In quantum theory the same warning applies: "close" depends on the norm, and the state space must come with the right one.

```python
import numpy as np
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 100001)
for n in (1, 5, 20):
    f = t**n
    print(n, f.max(), f.mean())   # sup norm stays 1, the L1 average shrinks
```

@example
title: Comparing three norms of one vector
problem: Let $x=(3,-4,12)\in\mathbb{R}^3$. Compute $\|x\|_1$, $\|x\|_2$ and $\|x\|_\infty$ and check the chain of inequalities above.
step: $\|x\|_1=3+4+12=19$.
step: $\|x\|_2=\sqrt{9+16+144}=\sqrt{169}=13$.
step: $\|x\|_\infty=\max(3,4,12)=12$.
step: The chain reads $12\le13\le19\le3\cdot12=36$, which is true, and the last two comparisons are far from equalities.

@example
title: Proving the reverse triangle inequality
problem: In any normed space, show that $\big|\|x\|-\|y\|\big|\le\|x-y\|$.
step: Write $x=(x-y)+y$. The triangle inequality gives $\|x\|\le\|x-y\|+\|y\|$.
step: Subtract $\|y\|$: $\|x\|-\|y\|\le\|x-y\|$.
step: Swap the roles of $x$ and $y$: $\|y\|-\|x\|\le\|y-x\|$. By homogeneity with $\lambda=-1$, $\|y-x\|=\|x-y\|$.
step: The real number $\|x\|-\|y\|$ and its negative are both at most $\|x-y\|$, so its absolute value is too. In particular $x_n\to x$ implies $\|x_n\|\to\|x\|$: the norm is continuous.

@mistakes
- Dropping definiteness: $|x_1|$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ vanishes at nonzero vectors, so it is only a seminorm.
- Using $\|x\|^2$ in place of $\|x\|$: squaring keeps definiteness but breaks homogeneity, since $\|\lambda x\|^2=|\lambda|^2\|x\|^2$.
- Taking the largest value of $|f|$ at the critical points only; the supremum may sit at an endpoint of the interval.
- Assuming that equivalence of all norms, true on $\mathbb{R}^n$, also holds on $C[0,1]$.

@practice self
prompt: Prove that $\|f\|_1=\int_0^1|f(t)|\,dt$ is a norm on $C[0,1]$. Take particular care over definiteness, and say exactly where continuity of $f$ is used.
hint: Homogeneity and the triangle inequality follow from $|\lambda f|=|\lambda||f|$, the pointwise triangle inequality and monotonicity of the integral.
hint: For definiteness, assume $f(t_0)\neq0$ and use continuity to find an interval on which $|f|$ is bounded below by a positive number.
solution: Homogeneity: $\int|\lambda f|=|\lambda|\int|f|$. Triangle inequality: $|f+g|\le|f|+|g|$ pointwise, so integrating gives $\|f+g\|_1\le\|f\|_1+\|g\|_1$. Definiteness: if $f=0$ then clearly $\|f\|_1=0$. Conversely suppose $f(t_0)\neq0$. By continuity there is $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)|>\tfrac12|f(t_0)|$ for $t\in[0,1]$ with $|t-t_0|<\delta$. This set contains an interval of length at least $\min(\delta,\tfrac12)$, so $\|f\|_1\ge\tfrac12|f(t_0)|\min(\delta,\tfrac12)>0$. Hence $\|f\|_1=0$ forces $f=0$. Continuity is used only in the last step.

rubric: Proves definiteness by contradiction or contrapositive, producing an interval where $|f|$ is bounded below.
rubric: Names continuity as the property that makes the interval exist.

@quiz num
prompt: For $n\ge1$ let $g_n(t)=\max(0,\,1-nt)$ on $[0,1]$. Compute the ratio $\|g_{25}\|_\infty\,/\,\|g_{25}\|_1$.
answer: 50
check: max(abs(max(0, 1 - 25*t)) for t in np.linspace(0, 1, 100001)) / (quad(lambda t: max(0, 1 - 25*t), 0, 1/25) + quad(lambda t: max(0, 1 - 25*t), 1/25, 1))
hint: $g_n$ is a tent: it equals $1$ at $t=0$ and decreases linearly to $0$ at $t=1/n$.
hint: $\|g_n\|_1$ is the area of a triangle with base $1/n$ and height $1$.
solution: $\|g_{25}\|_\infty=1$. The triangle has base $\tfrac1{25}$ and height $1$, so $\|g_{25}\|_1=\tfrac1{50}$. The ratio is $50$. In general the ratio is $2n$, which is unbounded, so the two norms are not equivalent on $C[0,1]$.
