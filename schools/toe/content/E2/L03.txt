@article
A linear map between finite-dimensional spaces is a matrix, and it is automatically continuous. In infinite dimensions a linear map can be violently discontinuous, so boundedness becomes a hypothesis that you must check. In quantum theory this is the gap between an observable that can act on every state and one that is defined only on part of the space, a point we return to in the last weeks.

### Bounded maps and the operator norm

Let $X$ and $Y$ be normed spaces and $T:X\to Y$ linear. $T$ is **bounded** if there is $M\ge0$ with $\|Tx\|\le M\|x\|$ for all $x\in X$. The smallest such $M$ is the **operator norm**

$$\|T\|=\sup_{\|x\|\le1}\|Tx\|=\sup_{x\neq0}\frac{\|Tx\|}{\|x\|},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad \|Tx\|\le\|T\|\,\|x\|.$$

**Theorem.** For a linear map $T$ the following are equivalent: (a) $T$ is bounded; (b) $T$ is continuous at $0$; (c) $T$ is continuous everywhere, in fact Lipschitz, because $\|Tx-Ty\|\le\|T\|\,\|x-y\|$.

For (b)$\Rightarrow$(a): continuity at $0$ gives $\delta>0$ with $\|Tz\|\le1$ whenever $\|z\|\le\delta$. For $x\neq0$ put $z=\delta x/\|x\|$; then $\|Tx\|=\frac{\|x\|}{\delta}\|Tz\|\le\frac1\delta\|x\|$. Linearity is what lets you rescale.

The set $B(X,Y)$ of bounded operators is a normed space under $\|T\|$, and it is complete whenever $Y$ is. Composition satisfies $\|ST\|\le\|S\|\,\|T\|$.

### How to compute an operator norm

Always prove two inequalities. For the **upper bound**, show $\|Tx\|\le M\|x\|$ for all $x$. For the **lower bound**, exhibit one $x$ (or a sequence $x_n$) with $\|Tx\|/\|x\|=M$ (or tending to $M$). Then $\|T\|=M$.

- A matrix $A$ on $(\mathbb{R}^n,\|\cdot\|_2)$ has $\|A\|=\sqrt{\lambda_{\max}(A^{T}A)}$, its largest singular value. For a symmetric matrix this is the largest $|\text{eigenvalue}|$; for a non-symmetric matrix the eigenvalues can be much smaller than the norm.
- A diagonal operator $(Dx)_k=d_kx_k$ on $\ell^2$ has $\|D\|=\sup_k|d_k|$. The supremum need not be attained.
- An integral operator on $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm: see Example 2.

### Unbounded linear maps

Take differentiation $D=\frac{d}{dt}$ on the polynomials with the sup norm on $[0,1]$. Then $\|Dt^n\|_\infty=n$ while $\|t^n\|_\infty=1$, so no constant $M$ works: $D$ is unbounded. Position and momentum in quantum theory behave this way, which is why they come with a domain. The failure disappears in finite dimensions: if $\dim X<\infty$, every linear $T:X\to Y$ is bounded, because all norms on $X$ are equivalent and a basis expansion gives the estimate.

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1., 1.], [0., 1.]])
print(np.linalg.norm(A, 2))              # operator norm for the Euclidean norm
print(max(abs(np.linalg.eigvals(A))))    # spectral radius 1.0, smaller than the norm
```

@example
title: Operator norm of a non-symmetric matrix
problem: Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the Euclidean norm. Find $\|A\|$.
step: $\|A\|^2=\lambda_{\max}(A^{T}A)$, because $\|Ax\|^2=x^{T}A^{T}Ax$ and $A^{T}A$ is symmetric. Compute $A^{T}A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Its characteristic polynomial is $\lambda^2-3\lambda+1$, with roots $\frac{3\pm\sqrt5}{2}$. The largest is $\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}$.
step: Since $\Big(\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\Big)^2=\frac{6+2\sqrt5}{4}=\frac{3+\sqrt5}{2}$, we get $\|A\|=\frac{1+\sqrt5}{2}\approx1.618$.
step: Compare with the eigenvalues of $A$: both equal $1$. The norm exceeds the spectral radius here, which cannot happen for a symmetric matrix.

@example
title: Norm of the integration operator
problem: On $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm, define $(Vf)(t)=\int_0^tf(s)\,ds$. Show that $V$ is bounded with $\|V\|=1$.
step: Upper bound: for every $t\in[0,1]$, $|(Vf)(t)|\le\int_0^t|f(s)|\,ds\le t\,\|f\|_\infty\le\|f\|_\infty$.
step: Taking the maximum over $t$ gives $\|Vf\|_\infty\le\|f\|_\infty$ for all $f$, so $V$ is bounded and $\|V\|\le1$.
step: Lower bound: take $f\equiv1$. Then $\|f\|_\infty=1$ and $(Vf)(t)=t$, so $\|Vf\|_\infty=1$.
step: Hence $1\le\|V\|\le1$, so $\|V\|=1$, and the supremum is attained.

@mistakes
- Checking $\|Tx\|\le M\|x\|$ for a few vectors; the bound must hold for all $x$, and the norm is a supremum.
- Using the largest eigenvalue (or the largest entry) of a non-symmetric matrix as its operator norm.
- Believing that every linear map is continuous; that is true only when the domain is finite dimensional.
- Quoting a boundedness result without checking the norms on both spaces; changing a norm can change the answer.

@practice num
prompt: Let $A=\operatorname{diag}(3,-5,2)$ act on $\mathbb{R}^3$ with the Euclidean norm. Find the operator norm $\|A\|$.
answer: 5
check: np.linalg.norm(np.diag([3, -5, 2]), 2)
hint: $\|Ax\|^2=9x_1^2+25x_2^2+4x_3^2$. How large can this be when $x_1^2+x_2^2+x_3^2=1$?
solution: $\|Ax\|^2=9x_1^2+25x_2^2+4x_3^2\le25\|x\|^2$, with equality at $x=(0,1,0)$. So $\|A\|=5$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&2\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}$ act on $\mathbb{R}^2$ with the Euclidean norm. Find the operator norm $\|A\|$ as an exact value.
answer: 2sqrt(2)
check: np.linalg.norm(np.array([[2, 2], [1, -1]]), 2)
hint: $\|A\|^2$ is the largest eigenvalue of $A^{T}A$.
hint: $A^{T}A=\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\3&5\end{pmatrix}$. Its trace is $10$ and its determinant is $16$.
solution: The eigenvalues of $A^{T}A$ have sum $10$ and product $16$, so they are $8$ and $2$. Hence $\|A\|=\sqrt8=2\sqrt2$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $D$ be the diagonal operator on $\ell^2$ with $(Dx)_k=d_kx_k$ and $d_k=\dfrac{4k}{k^2+3}$. Find $\|D\|$.
answer: 8/7
check: max(abs(4*k/(k**2 + 3)) for k in range(1, 5000))
hint: For a diagonal operator $\|D\|=\sup_k|d_k|$. Compute $d_1,d_2,d_3$.
hint: Compare $d_1$, $d_2$ and $d_3$. For $k\ge3$, $4k\le k^2+3$ because $(k-1)(k-3)\ge0$, so $d_k\le1$ there.
solution: The supremum of $|d_k|$ is attained at $k=2$, where $d_2=\frac87$. For $k\ge3$, $(k-1)(k-3)\ge0$ gives $4k\le k^2+3$, so $d_k\le1$. Hence $\|D\|=\frac87$, attained at $x=e_2$.

prompt: Which statement about a linear map $T:X\to Y$ between normed spaces is FALSE?
choice: If $T$ is continuous at $0$, then $T$ is bounded.
choice: If $\dim X<\infty$, then $T$ is bounded.
choice: If $T$ is bounded, then $\|Tx\|\le\|T\|\,\|x\|$ for every $x\in X$.
choice*: $T$ is bounded as soon as $\|Tx\|<\infty$ for every $x\in X$.
hint: Every vector in a normed space has a finite norm.
solution: The statement that $T$ is bounded as soon as $\|Tx\|<\infty$ for every $x$ is false: $\|Tx\|<\infty$ holds for every $x$ and every linear map, bounded or not, so it carries no information. Boundedness requires a single constant $M$ working for all $x$. The unbounded map $D$ on polynomials shows the difference.

@practice self
prompt: Let $T:X\to Y$ be a linear map between normed spaces that is continuous at $0$. Prove that there is a constant $M$ with $\|Tx\|\le M\|x\|$ for all $x\in X$, and then that $T$ is continuous at every point.
hint: Apply the definition of continuity at $0$ with $\varepsilon=1$ to get a ball on which $\|Tz\|\le1$.
hint: Rescale an arbitrary nonzero $x$ into that ball using linearity.
solution: By continuity at $0$ with $\varepsilon=1$ there is $\delta>0$ such that $\|z\|\le\delta$ implies $\|Tz\|\le1$. For $x\neq0$ set $z=\delta x/\|x\|$, so $\|z\|=\delta$ and $\|Tz\|\le1$. By linearity $\|Tz\|=\frac{\delta}{\|x\|}\|Tx\|$, hence $\|Tx\|\le\frac1\delta\|x\|$. This also holds for $x=0$, so $M=1/\delta$ works. For continuity at any $x_0$: $\|Tx-Tx_0\|=\|T(x-x_0)\|\le M\|x-x_0\|$, which tends to $0$ as $x\to x_0$.
rubric: Uses continuity at $0$ to obtain $\delta$ with $\|z\|\le\delta\Rightarrow\|Tz\|\le1$.
rubric: Rescales an arbitrary $x$ using linearity and obtains $M=1/\delta$.
rubric: Deduces continuity (or Lipschitz continuity) at every point from linearity and the bound.

@quiz num
prompt: On $C[0,1]$ with the sup norm define $(Tf)(t)=\int_0^1(t+s)\,f(s)\,ds$. Find the operator norm $\|T\|$.
answer: 3/2
check: max(quad(lambda s: abs(t + s), 0, 1) for t in np.linspace(0, 1, 1001))
hint: Bound $|(Tf)(t)|\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1(t+s)\,ds$ and maximize over $t$.
hint: To show the bound is sharp, try the constant function $f\equiv1$.
solution: For every $t$, $|(Tf)(t)|\le\|f\|_\infty\int_0^1(t+s)\,ds=\|f\|_\infty\big(t+\tfrac12\big)\le\tfrac32\|f\|_\infty$. For $f\equiv1$, $(Tf)(t)=t+\tfrac12$ has sup norm $\tfrac32$. So $\|T\|=\tfrac32$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $D:C^1[0,1]\to C[0,1]$ be $Df=f'$. (a) With the sup norm on both spaces, show that $D$ is unbounded. (b) Now give $C^1[0,1]$ the norm $\|f\|_{C^1}=\|f\|_\infty+\|f'\|_\infty$ and show that $D$ is bounded with $\|D\|=1$.
hint: For (a) try $f_n(t)=\sin(nt)$.
hint: For (b) the upper bound is immediate from the definition of the norm. For the lower bound use $f_n(t)=\frac1n\sin(nt)$ and let $n\to\infty$.
solution: (a) For $f_n(t)=\sin(nt)$ we have $\|f_n\|_\infty\le1$ and $\|Df_n\|_\infty=\max|n\cos(nt)|=n$, so no constant $M$ satisfies $\|Df\|_\infty\le M\|f\|_\infty$. (b) $\|Df\|_\infty=\|f'\|_\infty\le\|f\|_\infty+\|f'\|_\infty=\|f\|_{C^1}$, so $\|D\|\le1$. For $f_n(t)=\frac1n\sin(nt)$ with $n\ge2$, $\|f_n\|_\infty=\frac1n$ and $\|f_n'\|_\infty=1$, so $\|f_n\|_{C^1}=1+\frac1n$ and $\|Df_n\|_\infty/\|f_n\|_{C^1}=\frac{n}{n+1}\to1$. Hence $\|D\|=1$. The same operator is unbounded or bounded depending on the norm chosen on its domain.
rubric: Gives an explicit sequence for (a) and computes both norms to show the ratio is unbounded.
rubric: Proves the upper bound $\|D\|\le1$ directly from the definition of $\|\cdot\|_{C^1}$.
rubric: Shows a sequence with ratio tending to $1$ and concludes $\|D\|=1$.
