@article
Given a vector $x$ and a subspace $M$, which vector of $M$ is closest to $x$? In $\mathbb{R}^3$ you drop a perpendicular. In infinite dimensions the same answer holds, but only under two hypotheses: the space is complete and $M$ is **closed**.

### Orthogonal complement

For a subset $M\subseteq H$ put $M^\perp=\{x\in H:\ \langle y,x\rangle=0\ \text{for all }y\in M\}$. This is always a closed subspace, since it is an intersection of kernels of continuous functionals $x\mapsto\langle y,x\rangle$. For any subspace, $(M^\perp)^\perp=\overline M$, the closure of $M$.

### The projection theorem

**Theorem.** Let $M$ be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space $H$. For each $x\in H$ there is a unique $m\in M$ with $\|x-m\|=\operatorname{dist}(x,M)$. It is characterized by $x-m\in M^\perp$. Consequently every $x$ is written uniquely as $x=Px+(x-Px)$ with $Px\in M$ and $x-Px\in M^\perp$, that is, $H=M\oplus M^\perp$, and

$$\|x\|^2=\|Px\|^2+\|x-Px\|^2,\qquad \operatorname{dist}(x,M)^2=\|x\|^2-\|Px\|^2.$$

The map $P$ is the **orthogonal projection** onto $M$. It is linear, $P^2=P$, and $P=P^*$, meaning $\langle Px,y\rangle=\langle x,Py\rangle$. Its range is $M$, its kernel is $M^\perp$, $\|P\|=1$ if $M\neq\{0\}$, and its only possible eigenvalues are $0$ and $1$. The map $I-P$ is the orthogonal projection onto $M^\perp$. Conversely, a bounded $P$ with $P^2=P=P^*$ is the orthogonal projection onto its range. Condition $P^2=P$ alone gives an **oblique** projection.

### Computing projections

- If $e_1,e_2,\dots$ is an orthonormal basis of $M$, then $Px=\sum_k\langle e_k,x\rangle\,e_k$.
- If $a_1,\dots,a_n$ span $M$ but are not orthonormal, solve the **normal equations** $Gc=b$ with $G_{jk}=\langle a_j,a_k\rangle$ and $b_j=\langle a_j,x\rangle$, and set $Px=\sum_kc_ka_k$. For columns $a_k$ of a matrix $A$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$ this is $P=A(A^{T}A)^{-1}A^{T}$, the least-squares projection.

### Why closedness matters

Let $M=c_{00}\subset\ell^2$, the finitely supported sequences. Then $M^\perp=\{0\}$, so $M\oplus M^\perp=M\ne\ell^2$. The vector $(1/k)_k$ has distance $0$ from $M$ (truncations approach it) but no closest point in $M$. In quantum theory the states compatible with a measurement outcome form a closed subspace $M_E$, and $\|P_Ex\|^2$ is the probability of that outcome for a normalized state $x$.

```python
import numpy as np
A = np.array([[1., 0.], [1., 1.], [1., 2.]])        # columns span a plane M in R^3
P = A @ np.linalg.inv(A.T @ A) @ A.T                  # normal equations as a matrix
x = np.array([1., 0., 2.])
print(np.allclose(P @ P, P), np.allclose(P, P.T))     # True True: P^2 = P = P^T
print(np.linalg.eigvalsh(P))                          # only 0 and 1 (up to rounding)
print(np.linalg.norm(x - P @ x))                      # dist(x, M) = 3/sqrt(6)
```

@example
title: Best affine approximation in $L^2(0,1)$
problem: Find the orthogonal projection of $f(t)=t^2$ onto $M=\operatorname{span}\{1,t\}\subset L^2(0,1)$ and the distance from $f$ to $M$.
step: Write $Pf=a+bt$. The projection is characterized by $f-Pf\perp1$ and $f-Pf\perp t$, which give the normal equations $\langle1,1\rangle a+\langle1,t\rangle b=\langle1,t^2\rangle$ and $\langle t,1\rangle a+\langle t,t\rangle b=\langle t,t^2\rangle$.
step: Compute the inner products: $a+\frac b2=\frac13$ and $\frac a2+\frac b3=\frac14$.
step: Substitute $a=\frac13-\frac b2$ into the second equation: $\frac16-\frac b4+\frac b3=\frac14$, so $\frac b{12}=\frac1{12}$ and $b=1$, $a=-\frac16$. Hence $Pf=t-\frac16$.
step: Distance: $\|f\|^2=\frac15$ and $\langle Pf,f\rangle=\int_0^1\big(t-\tfrac16\big)t^2\,dt=\frac14-\frac1{18}=\frac7{36}$, so $\operatorname{dist}^2=\frac15-\frac7{36}=\frac1{180}$.

@example
title: Existence of the nearest point
problem: Let $M$ be a closed subspace of a Hilbert space $H$ and $x\in H$. Show there is $m\in M$ with $\|x-m\|=d:=\inf_{y\in M}\|x-y\|$, and that $x-m\perp M$.
step: Choose $m_n\in M$ with $\|x-m_n\|\to d$. The parallelogram law applied to $x-m_n$ and $x-m_k$ gives $\|m_n-m_k\|^2=2\|x-m_n\|^2+2\|x-m_k\|^2-4\big\|x-\tfrac{m_n+m_k}{2}\big\|^2$.
step: The midpoint $\frac{m_n+m_k}{2}$ lies in $M$, so the last norm is at least $d$. Hence $\|m_n-m_k\|^2\le2\|x-m_n\|^2+2\|x-m_k\|^2-4d^2\to0$, and $(m_n)$ is Cauchy.
step: $H$ is complete, so $m_n\to m$, and $M$ is closed, so $m\in M$. By continuity of the norm, $\|x-m\|=d$. This is where completeness and closedness enter.
step: Orthogonality. For $y\in M$ and real $s$, $m+sy\in M$, so $\|x-m\|^2\le\|x-m-sy\|^2$. Expanding gives $0\le-2s\operatorname{Re}\langle x-m,y\rangle+s^2\|y\|^2$ for all real $s$, which forces $\operatorname{Re}\langle x-m,y\rangle=0$. Replacing $y$ by $iy$ gives the imaginary part, so $x-m\in M^\perp$.

@mistakes
- Projecting onto a subspace that is not closed; the decomposition $H=M\oplus M^\perp$ can fail, as for $c_{00}\subset\ell^2$.
- Writing $Px=\langle e,x\rangle e$ for a vector $e$ that is not normalized (divide by $\langle e,e\rangle$), or summing over a basis that is not orthogonal.
- Calling any operator with $P^2=P$ an orthogonal projection; you also need $P=P^*$, otherwise it is oblique and $\|P\|>1$.
- Claiming $(M^\perp)^\perp=M$ for every subspace; in general it equals the closure $\overline M$.

prompt: Let $M$ be a subspace of a Hilbert space $H$. Prove that $M^\perp$ is a closed subspace and that $M\cap M^\perp=\{0\}$. Deduce that a decomposition $x=m+n$ with $m\in M$, $n\in M^\perp$ is unique if it exists.
hint: Write $M^\perp=\bigcap_{y\in M}\ker\langle y,\cdot\rangle$ and recall that each functional $\langle y,\cdot\rangle$ is continuous.
hint: For uniqueness, subtract two decompositions and see which space the difference lies in.
solution: $M^\perp=\bigcap_{y\in M}\{x:\langle y,x\rangle=0\}$. Each set is the kernel of the continuous linear functional $x\mapsto\langle y,x\rangle$ (continuous by Cauchy–Schwarz), hence a closed subspace, and an intersection of closed subspaces is a closed subspace. If $z\in M\cap M^\perp$ then $z\perp z$, so $\|z\|^2=\langle z,z\rangle=0$ and $z=0$. For uniqueness suppose $x=m+n=m'+n'$ with $m,m'\in M$ and $n,n'\in M^\perp$. Then $m-m'=n'-n$ lies in $M\cap M^\perp=\{0\}$, so $m=m'$ and $n=n'$.
rubric: Shows $M^\perp$ is a subspace and closed, via continuity of $\langle y,\cdot\rangle$ and intersection of closed sets.
rubric: Proves $M\cap M^\perp=\{0\}$ by testing $z$ against itself.
rubric: Derives uniqueness by showing the difference of two decompositions lies in $M\cap M^\perp$.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove the Riesz representation theorem: if $\phi$ is a bounded linear functional on a Hilbert space $H$, then there is a unique $y\in H$ with $\phi(x)=\langle y,x\rangle$ for all $x$, and $\|\phi\|=\|y\|$. You may use the projection theorem.
hint: If $\phi=0$ take $y=0$. Otherwise $N=\ker\phi$ is a proper closed subspace; pick a nonzero $z\in N^\perp$.
hint: For every $x$, the vector $\phi(x)z-\phi(z)x$ lies in $N$, so it is orthogonal to $z$.
solution: If $\phi=0$ take $y=0$. Otherwise $N=\ker\phi$ is closed (as $\phi$ is continuous) and $N\neq H$. By the projection theorem $N^\perp\neq\{0\}$: for $x_0\notin N$, $x_0-Px_0$ is a nonzero element of $N^\perp$. Take $z\in N^\perp$ with $z\neq0$, so $\phi(z)\neq0$. For any $x$, $\phi\big(\phi(x)z-\phi(z)x\big)=0$, so $\phi(x)z-\phi(z)x\in N$ and is orthogonal to $z$: $\phi(x)\|z\|^2=\phi(z)\langle z,x\rangle$. Hence $\phi(x)=\langle y,x\rangle$ with $y=\frac{\overline{\phi(z)}}{\|z\|^2}z$. Uniqueness: if $\langle y,x\rangle=\langle y',x\rangle$ for all $x$, take $x=y-y'$ to get $\|y-y'\|^2=0$. Norm: $|\phi(x)|\le\|y\|\|x\|$ by Cauchy–Schwarz, and $\phi(y)=\|y\|^2$ shows equality is attained, so $\|\phi\|=\|y\|$.
rubric: Shows $\ker\phi$ is closed and uses the projection theorem to find a nonzero $z\perp\ker\phi$.
rubric: Derives the formula for $y$ from the orthogonality of $\phi(x)z-\phi(z)x$ to $z$, tracking the conjugate.
rubric: Proves uniqueness and $\|\phi\|=\|y\|$ using Cauchy–Schwarz and the test vector $x=y$.
