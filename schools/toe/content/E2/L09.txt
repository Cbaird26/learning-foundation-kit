@article
A norm gives lengths. An **inner product** adds angles, and with them orthogonality, projections and the geometry that quantum theory is built on.

### Inner products

An inner product on a complex vector space $H$ is a map $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:H\times H\to\mathbb{C}$ such that, **in the convention used here** (linear in the second slot, as in physics; many mathematics texts swap the slots):

1. $\langle x,y\rangle=\overline{\langle y,x\rangle}$;
2. $\langle x,\alpha y+\beta z\rangle=\alpha\langle x,y\rangle+\beta\langle x,z\rangle$;
3. $\langle x,x\rangle\ge0$, with equality only for $x=0$.

Put $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Then:

$$|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\,\|y\|\quad\text{(Cauchy–Schwarz)},\qquad \|x+y\|^2+\|x-y\|^2=2\|x\|^2+2\|y\|^2\quad\text{(parallelogram law)}.$$

Cauchy–Schwarz gives the triangle inequality, so $\|\cdot\|$ is a norm. Conversely, a norm comes from an inner product **if and only if** it satisfies the parallelogram law, and then $\langle x,y\rangle$ is recovered by polarization. The $\|\cdot\|_1$, $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ and sup norms fail the parallelogram law, so they have no inner product.

A **Hilbert space** is an inner product space that is complete in the norm $\|x\|=\sqrt{\langle x,x\rangle}$. Examples: $\mathbb{C}^n$ with $\langle x,y\rangle=\sum\bar x_ky_k$; $\ell^2$; and $L^2(\mu)$ with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int\bar fg\,d\mu$. Completeness of the last two is the Riesz–Fischer theorem. In quantum theory, states are unit vectors, $\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle$ is an amplitude and $|\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle|^2$ a probability.

### Orthogonality

Vectors are **orthogonal**, $x\perp y$, if $\langle x,y\rangle=0$. The **Pythagorean theorem** holds: $x\perp y$ implies $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+\|y\|^2$. An **orthonormal system** $(e_k)$ has $\langle e_j,e_k\rangle=\delta_{jk}$. For any $x$:

$$\sum_k|\langle e_k,x\rangle|^2\le\|x\|^2\qquad\text{(Bessel's inequality)}.$$

The system is an **orthonormal basis** if its span is dense. Then, for every $x$,

$$x=\sum_k\langle e_k,x\rangle\,e_k\quad\text{(converging in norm)},\qquad \|x\|^2=\sum_k|\langle e_k,x\rangle|^2\quad\text{(Parseval)}.$$

Examples: $\{e^{inx}/\sqrt{2\pi}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}$ in $L^2(0,2\pi)$, and $\{\sqrt2\sin(k\pi x)\}_{k\ge1}$ in $L^2(0,1)$. Every infinite-dimensional Hilbert space with a countable dense set has a countable orthonormal basis and is isometric to $\ell^2$.

### Duality (preview)

The **Riesz representation theorem**, proved next week, says that every bounded functional on $H$ is $x\mapsto\langle y,x\rangle$ for a unique $y$, with $\|\phi\|=\|y\|$. So a Hilbert space is its own dual.

```python
import numpy as np
th = np.pi / 6
U = np.array([[np.cos(th), np.sin(th)], [-np.sin(th), np.cos(th)]])   # rows: orthonormal basis
x = np.array([2.0, 1.0])
c = U @ x                                   # coefficients <u_k, x>
print(np.allclose(U @ U.T, np.eye(2)))      # True: orthonormal
print(np.sum(c**2), x @ x)                  # Parseval: both 5.0
print(c @ U)                                # reconstructs x = sum <u_k, x> u_k
```

@example
title: The angle between two functions
problem: In the real space $L^2(0,1)$ with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1fg$, find the angle between $f(t)=1$ and $g(t)=t$.
step: $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_0^1t\,dt=\frac12$, $\|f\|^2=\int_0^11\,dt=1$, and $\|g\|^2=\int_0^1t^2\,dt=\frac13$.
step: The angle is defined by $\cos\theta=\frac{\langle f,g\rangle}{\|f\|\|g\|}$, and Cauchy–Schwarz guarantees this lies in $[-1,1]$.
step: $\cos\theta=\frac{1/2}{1\cdot1/\sqrt3}=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$, so $\theta=\frac\pi6$.
step: The functions are far from orthogonal; subtracting the component of $g$ along $f$ would leave $t-\frac12$, which is orthogonal to $1$.

@example
title: Proving Bessel's inequality
problem: Let $e_1,\dots,e_N$ be orthonormal in $H$ and $x\in H$. Show $\sum_{k=1}^N|\langle e_k,x\rangle|^2\le\|x\|^2$, and deduce the infinite version.
step: Put $c_k=\langle e_k,x\rangle$, $s=\sum_{k\le N}c_ke_k$ and $r=x-s$.
step: For each $j\le N$, $\langle e_j,r\rangle=c_j-\sum_kc_k\langle e_j,e_k\rangle=c_j-c_j=0$. So $r\perp s$ (by linearity).
step: Pythagoras gives $\|x\|^2=\|s\|^2+\|r\|^2\ge\|s\|^2=\sum_{k\le N}|c_k|^2$, using orthonormality for the last equality.
step: The partial sums of $\sum_k|c_k|^2$ are bounded by $\|x\|^2$ for every $N$, so the series converges and its sum is at most $\|x\|^2$.

@mistakes
- Using the $\ell^1$, $\ell^\infty$ or sup norm as if it had an inner product; check the parallelogram law first.
- Forgetting the complex conjugate: $\langle x,y\rangle\neq\langle y,x\rangle$ in general, and which slot is conjugated depends on the convention.
- Writing Parseval's identity for an orthonormal system that is not a basis; for a general system only Bessel's inequality holds.
- Computing $\cos\theta=\frac{\langle f,g\rangle}{\|f\|\|g\|}$ and accepting a value outside $[-1,1]$; Cauchy–Schwarz forbids it, so an arithmetic slip has occurred.

prompt: Prove the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality $|\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\,\|y\|$ in a real inner product space, and deduce the triangle inequality $\|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|$.
hint: If $y=0$ both sides vanish. Otherwise expand $0\le\|x-\lambda y\|^2$ and choose $\lambda=\langle x,y\rangle/\|y\|^2$.
solution: If $y=0$ both sides are $0$. Let $y\neq0$ and $\lambda=\langle x,y\rangle/\|y\|^2$. Then $0\le\|x-\lambda y\|^2=\|x\|^2-2\lambda\langle x,y\rangle+\lambda^2\|y\|^2=\|x\|^2-\frac{\langle x,y\rangle^2}{\|y\|^2}$. Hence $\langle x,y\rangle^2\le\|x\|^2\|y\|^2$. For the triangle inequality, $\|x+y\|^2=\|x\|^2+2\langle x,y\rangle+\|y\|^2\le\|x\|^2+2\|x\|\|y\|+\|y\|^2=(\|x\|+\|y\|)^2$, and take square roots.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $H$ be a Hilbert space, $x_n\to x$ weakly (that is, $\langle y,x_n\rangle\to\langle y,x\rangle$ for every $y\in H$), and $\|x_n\|\to\|x\|$. Prove that $x_n\to x$ in norm.
hint: Expand $\|x_n-x\|^2$ in terms of $\|x_n\|^2$, $\|x\|^2$ and an inner product.
hint: Apply weak convergence with the particular choice $y=x$.
solution: Expand $\|x_n-x\|^2=\|x_n\|^2-\langle x,x_n\rangle-\langle x_n,x\rangle+\|x\|^2=\|x_n\|^2-2\operatorname{Re}\langle x,x_n\rangle+\|x\|^2$. By hypothesis $\|x_n\|^2\to\|x\|^2$, and weak convergence with $y=x$ gives $\langle x,x_n\rangle\to\langle x,x\rangle=\|x\|^2$. Therefore $\|x_n-x\|^2\to\|x\|^2-2\|x\|^2+\|x\|^2=0$.
