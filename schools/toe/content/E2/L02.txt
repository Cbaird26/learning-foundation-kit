@article
A sequence can look as though it converges and still have no limit in the space. In $\mathbb{Q}$, the decimals $1,\,1.4,\,1.41,\,1.414,\dots$ bunch together, yet no rational number is their limit. **Completeness** rules this out. It is what lets you build solutions by a limiting process.

### Cauchy sequences and Banach spaces

A sequence $(x_n)$ in a normed space is **Cauchy** if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is $N$ such that $\|x_n-x_m\|<\varepsilon$ for all $n,m\ge N$. Every convergent sequence is Cauchy, because $\|x_n-x_m\|\le\|x_n-x\|+\|x-x_m\|$. The space is **complete** if the converse also holds, and a complete normed space is a **Banach space**. Every finite-dimensional normed space is complete, so $\mathbb{R}^n$ with any norm is a Banach space.

It is not enough that $\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|\to0$. The harmonic partial sums $a_n=\sum_{k\le n}1/k$ have that property and diverge.

### Failures of completeness

Completeness depends on the vector space and on the norm.

- **Polynomials with the sup norm.** The Taylor polynomials $s_N(t)=\sum_{k=0}^N t^k/k!$ satisfy $\|s_N-s_M\|_\infty\le\sum_{k>M}1/k!$ on $[0,1]$, so they are Cauchy. The only possible limit is $e^t$, which is not a polynomial.
- **$C[0,1]$ with $\|\cdot\|_1$.** Continuous ramps that climb from $0$ to $1$ near $t=\tfrac12$ are Cauchy, but the limit is a step function (Example 1).
- **Finitely supported sequences with the $\ell^2$ norm.** The truncations $(1,\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac1n,0,\dots)$ are Cauchy and converge coordinatewise to $(1/k)_k$, which has infinitely many nonzero entries.

### Where completeness holds

The sequence spaces $\ell^1$, $\ell^2$, $\ell^\infty$ and the function space $C[0,1]$ with $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ are Banach spaces. Their proofs share one template:

1. Take an arbitrary Cauchy sequence.
2. Use completeness of the scalars to construct a **candidate limit** (coordinatewise or pointwise).
3. Show the candidate lies in the space.
4. Show the sequence converges to the candidate **in the norm**.

For $C[0,1]$ the candidate is $f(t)=\lim f_n(t)$. The estimate $|f_n(t)-f(t)|\le\varepsilon$ then holds for all $t$ at once, so the convergence is uniform, and a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous. Without uniformity, step 3 would fail.

### Absolutely convergent series

A series $\sum x_k$ is **absolutely convergent** if $\sum\|x_k\|<\infty$. A normed space $X$ is complete if and only if every absolutely convergent series in $X$ converges. One direction is easy: the partial sums satisfy $\big\|\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}x_k\big\|\le\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\|x_k\|$, so they are Cauchy. The converse says that you only need to test series, which often shortens a completeness proof.

```python
import numpy as np
from math import factorial
t = np.linspace(0, 1, 2001)
def s(N):
    return sum(t**k / factorial(k) for k in range(N + 1))
for N in (3, 6, 9):
    print(N, np.max(np.abs(s(N) - np.exp(t))))   # sup distance to e^t shrinks
```

@example
title: A Cauchy sequence with no limit in $C[0,1]$ under the $L^1$ norm
problem: Let $f_n(t)=\min\{1,\max\{0,\,n(t-\tfrac12)\}\}$ on $[0,1]$. Show that $(f_n)$ is Cauchy in $(C[0,1],\|\cdot\|_1)$ but has no limit in that space.
step: For $n>m$ we have $0\le f_m\le f_n\le1$, and $f_n=f_m$ outside $[\tfrac12,\tfrac12+\tfrac1m]$ (both are $0$ to the left, both are $1$ to the right). Hence $\|f_n-f_m\|_1\le\tfrac1m$, which is small once $m$ is large, so $(f_n)$ is Cauchy.
step: Suppose $\|f_n-f\|_1\to0$ for some continuous $f$. Then $\int_0^{1/2}|f|=\int_0^{1/2}|f-f_n|\le\|f-f_n\|_1\to0$, so $\int_0^{1/2}|f|=0$, and by the continuity argument for definiteness $f=0$ on $[0,\tfrac12]$.
step: Fix $c\in(\tfrac12,1)$. For $n\ge1/(c-\tfrac12)$ we have $f_n=1$ on $[c,1]$, so $\int_c^1|f-1|\le\|f-f_n\|_1\to0$. Therefore $f=1$ on $[c,1]$, and since $c$ was arbitrary, $f=1$ on $(\tfrac12,1]$.
step: Then $f(\tfrac12)=0$ but $f(t)=1$ for all $t>\tfrac12$, which contradicts continuity at $\tfrac12$. So the Cauchy sequence has no limit in $C[0,1]$, and the space is not complete in $\|\cdot\|_1$.

@example
title: $\ell^\infty$ is complete
problem: Prove that the space $\ell^\infty$ of bounded real sequences with $\|x\|_\infty=\sup_k|x_k|$ is a Banach space.
step: Let $(x^{(n)})$ be Cauchy in $\ell^\infty$. For each fixed $k$, $|x^{(n)}_k-x^{(m)}_k|\le\|x^{(n)}-x^{(m)}\|_\infty$, so $(x^{(n)}_k)_n$ is Cauchy in $\mathbb{R}$. Define the candidate $x_k=\lim_n x^{(n)}_k$.
step: Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $N$ with $\|x^{(n)}-x^{(m)}\|_\infty<\varepsilon$ for $n,m\ge N$. Fix $k$ and $n\ge N$, and let $m\to\infty$ in $|x^{(n)}_k-x^{(m)}_k|<\varepsilon$. This gives $|x^{(n)}_k-x_k|\le\varepsilon$ for every $k$.
step: The candidate is bounded: $|x_k|\le|x^{(N)}_k|+\varepsilon\le\|x^{(N)}\|_\infty+\varepsilon$ for all $k$, so $x\in\ell^\infty$.
step: The estimate in step 2 says $\|x^{(n)}-x\|_\infty\le\varepsilon$ for all $n\ge N$, so $x^{(n)}\to x$ in the norm. Every Cauchy sequence converges, so $\ell^\infty$ is complete.

@mistakes
- Checking only that consecutive terms get close; Cauchy needs $\|x_n-x_m\|$ small for all large $n$ and $m$.
- Producing a limit in a bigger space (such as pointwise limits that are not continuous) and calling the space complete; the limit must lie in the space itself.
- Treating completeness as a property of the vector space alone; $C[0,1]$ is complete in $\|\cdot\|_\infty$ and not in $\|\cdot\|_1$.
- Proving only coordinatewise or pointwise convergence and skipping the final step, convergence in the norm.

prompt: Show that the space $c_{00}$ of finitely supported real sequences, with the $\ell^2$ norm $\|x\|_2=(\sum_k x_k^2)^{1/2}$, is not complete. Do this by exhibiting a Cauchy sequence in $c_{00}$ and proving it has no limit in $c_{00}$.
hint: Truncate the sequence $(1/k)_{k\ge1}$ after $n$ terms.
hint: For the Cauchy property bound $\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}1/k^2$ using $\frac1{k^2}\le\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k$. For the missing limit, note that convergence in $\ell^2$ implies convergence of each coordinate.
solution: Let $x^{(n)}=(1,\tfrac12,\dots,\tfrac1n,0,0,\dots)\in c_{00}$. For $n>m$, $\|x^{(n)}-x^{(m)}\|_2^2=\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\frac1{k^2}\le\sum_{k=m+1}^{n}\Big(\frac1{k-1}-\frac1k\Big)=\frac1m-\frac1n<\frac1m$, so $(x^{(n)})$ is Cauchy. Suppose $x^{(n)}\to y$ in $c_{00}$. Coordinate $k$ satisfies $|x^{(n)}_k-y_k|\le\|x^{(n)}-y\|_2\to0$, and $x^{(n)}_k=\frac1k$ for $n\ge k$, so $y_k=\frac1k\neq0$ for every $k$. Then $y$ has infinitely many nonzero entries, contradicting $y\in c_{00}$. So $c_{00}$ is not complete.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $X$ be a Banach space and $Y\subseteq X$ a linear subspace. Prove that $Y$ is complete in the restricted norm if and only if $Y$ is closed in $X$.
hint: For the forward direction take a sequence in $Y$ converging in $X$ and show it is Cauchy.
hint: For the converse, a Cauchy sequence in $Y$ is Cauchy in $X$, so it converges in $X$. Where must the limit lie?
solution: Suppose $Y$ is complete and $y_n\in Y$ with $y_n\to x$ in $X$. Then $(y_n)$ is Cauchy in $X$, hence in $Y$, so it converges to some $y\in Y$. Limits in $X$ are unique, so $x=y\in Y$. Thus $Y$ is closed. Conversely suppose $Y$ is closed and $(y_n)$ is Cauchy in $Y$. It is Cauchy in $X$, which is complete, so $y_n\to x$ for some $x\in X$. Since $Y$ is closed, $x\in Y$, so $y_n$ converges in $Y$. Hence $Y$ is complete.
rubric: Proves the forward direction using Cauchy sequences and uniqueness of limits in $X$.
rubric: Proves the converse using completeness of $X$ and closedness of $Y$ to place the limit in $Y$.
rubric: Notes where the hypothesis that $X$ is complete is used.
