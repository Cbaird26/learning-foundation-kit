@article
Fields store energy. The work needed to assemble charges against their own repulsion, or to establish currents against the emf they induce, ends up in the fields, and the same fields exert forces. This lesson collects the energy expressions, the assumptions behind each, and the stress tensor that turns field energy into force.

### Electrostatic energy

For point charges and for a continuous distribution,

$$W = \frac12\sum_i q_iV_i, \qquad W = \frac12\int\rho V\,d\tau,$$

where in the sum $V_i$ is the potential at charge $i$ due to all the *other* charges (for two charges $W = q_1q_2/(4\pi\epsilon_0r_{12})$). Substituting $\rho = \epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ and using the divergence theorem gives

$$W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}}E^2\,d\tau.$$

The assumptions matter. The surface term at infinity must vanish (localized charge, $V\to0$ at infinity). The integral must cover every region where $\mathbf E\neq\mathbf 0$. And for point charges it includes each charge's infinite self-energy, which the pair sum leaves out. Energy is quadratic in the fields, so for two sources $W = W_1 + W_2 + \int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$: superposition fails for energy. For a capacitor $W = \tfrac12CV^2$, and in a linear dielectric the free-charge energy is $\tfrac12\int\mathbf D\cdot\mathbf E\,d\tau$.

### Magnetostatic energy

Establishing currents requires work against the induced emf. The magnetic force itself does no work, so this energy is supplied by whatever drives the currents. The result is

$$W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}}B^2\,d\tau = \frac12\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A\,d\tau,$$

and for a circuit $W = \tfrac12LI^2$. In linear magnetic materials the integrand becomes $\tfrac12\mathbf H\cdot\mathbf B$.

### The Maxwell stress tensor

The force on the charges and currents inside a closed surface $\mathcal S$ can be found from the fields on $\mathcal S$ alone. In statics, summing over the repeated index $j$,

$$F_i = \oint_{\mathcal S}T_{ij}\,da_j, \qquad T_{ij} = \epsilon_0\!\left(E_iE_j - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}E^2\right) + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\!\left(B_iB_j - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}B^2\right),$$

with $d\mathbf a$ pointing outward. The picture: field lines pull like tension along their direction and push sideways like pressure, each of size $\epsilon_0E^2/2$ (or $B^2/2\mu_0$). A conductor with surface charge $\sigma$ is pulled outward with pressure $\sigma^2/(2\epsilon_0) = \epsilon_0E^2/2$, where $E$ is the field just outside.

The surface must lie in the field region and enclose exactly the charges or currents whose force you want. A well-chosen surface, where the field is simple or zero, turns a hard force integral into a short one.

@example
title: Two routes to the energy of a charged sphere
problem: A sphere of radius $R = 1.0$ cm carries charge $Q = 5.0$ nC spread uniformly through its volume. Compute its electrostatic energy from the field energy and from $\tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau$, and compare. Take $k = 1/(4\pi\epsilon_0) = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
step: Gauss's law gives $E = kQr/R^3$ for $r<R$ and $E = kQ/r^2$ for $r>R$. Both regions have a field, so both count.
step: Field energy inside: $\tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_0^R\left(\dfrac{kQr}{R^3}\right)^24\pi r^2\,dr = \dfrac{kQ^2}{10R}$. Outside: $\tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_R^\infty\left(\dfrac{kQ}{r^2}\right)^24\pi r^2\,dr = \dfrac{kQ^2}{2R}$. The total is $W = \dfrac{3kQ^2}{5R}$.
step: Second route: inside the sphere $V = \dfrac{kQ}{2R}\left(3 - \dfrac{r^2}{R^2}\right)$ and $\rho = \dfrac{3Q}{4\pi R^3}$. Then $\tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau = \tfrac12\cdot\dfrac{3Q}{R^3}\cdot\dfrac{kQ}{2R}\cdot\dfrac{4R^3}{5} = \dfrac{3kQ^2}{5R}$, the same.
step: Numbers: $W = \dfrac{3}{5}\,\dfrac{(8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})^2}{0.010} \approx 1.35\times10^{-5}$ J.
step: The agreement relies on the assumptions: the charge is confined, $V\to0$ at infinity, and the field integral runs over all space, not just over the charged volume.

@example
title: Force on a charged hemisphere from the stress tensor
problem: A thin spherical shell of radius $R = 5.0$ cm carries charge $Q = 2.0\ \mu$C spread uniformly over its surface. Use the Maxwell stress tensor to find the force on the upper hemisphere. Take $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
step: Choose a closed surface that encloses only the upper hemisphere: a cap just outside the shell, plus the flat disk in the plane $z = 0$.
step: On the disk, nearly all of it lies inside the shell, where $\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$, so it contributes nothing. The thin outer ring has negligible area as the cap hugs the shell.
step: On the cap, $\mathbf E = E\hat{\mathbf r}$ with $E = \sigma/\epsilon_0$ and $d\mathbf a = \hat{\mathbf r}\,dA$. Then $T_{zj}da_j = \epsilon_0\left(E_z\,\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a - \tfrac12E^2da_z\right) = \tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2\cos\theta\,dA$.
step: The integral $\int\cos\theta\,dA$ over the cap is its projected area $\pi R^2$, so $F_z = \tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2\pi R^2 = \dfrac{\sigma^2\pi R^2}{2\epsilon_0}$. With $\sigma = Q/(4\pi R^2)$ this is $F_z = \dfrac{Q^2}{32\pi\epsilon_0R^2} = \dfrac{kQ^2}{8R^2}$.
step: Numbers: $F_z = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-6})^2}{8(0.050)^2} \approx 1.8$ N, directed along $+z$: the charges on the lower half push the upper half away.

@mistakes
- Integrating $\tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}E^2$ only over the charged region. The field outside carries energy too, and the integral must run over all space.
- Adding energies of separate sources as if superposition held. The total contains the cross term $\int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$.
- Using $\tfrac12 qV$ for a charge in an external potential. The factor $\tfrac12$ appears only when $V$ is produced by the same charges; a charge in an external field has energy $qV$.
- Taking a stress-tensor surface that cuts through the charges, or that encloses charges other than the ones whose force you want.

@practice num

answer: 2.21e-7
check: 0.5*(8.854e-12*0.010/0.002)*100**2
unit: J
tol: 1%
hint: Between the plates $E = V/d$ is uniform, and outside the plates it is negligible.
hint: Multiply the energy density by the volume $Ad$.
solution: $E = V/d = 5.0\times10^{4}$ V/m, so $u = \tfrac12\epsilon_0E^2 \approx 1.11\times10^{-2}$ J/m³. With volume $Ad = 2.0\times10^{-5}$ m³, $W = uAd \approx 2.21\times10^{-7}$ J, which equals $\tfrac12CV^2$ with $C = \epsilon_0A/d$.

@practice mc
prompt: Two charge distributions $\rho_1$ and $\rho_2$ have separate energies $W_1$ and $W_2$ when each is assembled alone, and $V_1$, $V_2$ are their potentials. What is the energy $W$ of the combined distribution?
choice: $W = W_1 + W_2$, because energy is additive.
choice*: $W = W_1 + W_2 + \int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$.
choice: $W = W_1 + W_2 + \tfrac12\int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$.
choice: $W = \left(\sqrt{W_1} + \sqrt{W_2}\right)^2$.
hint: Energy is quadratic in the field: expand $\tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int(\mathbf E_1 + \mathbf E_2)^2\,d\tau$.
solution: The expansion gives $W_1 + W_2 + \epsilon_0\int\mathbf E_1\cdot\mathbf E_2\,d\tau$, and the cross term equals $\int\rho_1V_2\,d\tau$ (also $\int\rho_2V_1\,d\tau$). It is the interaction energy, counted once with no factor $\tfrac12$.

@practice num
prompt: A solid sphere of radius $R$ carries charge uniformly through its volume. Someone estimates its electrostatic energy by integrating $\tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}E^2$ only out to the radius $r = 2R$. What fraction of the correct total energy does this capture?
answer: 7/12
check: (quad(lambda r: r**4, 0, 1) + quad(lambda r: 1/r**2, 1, 2))/(quad(lambda r: r**4, 0, 1) + sum(quad(lambda r: 1/r**2, 2**k, 2**(k + 1)) for k in range(40)))
hint: Work in units $R = 1$ and $kQ = 1$, so $E = r$ for $r<1$ and $E = 1/r^2$ for $r>1$. The energy in a shell is proportional to $\int E^2r^2\,dr$.
hint: Compute three integrals: $\int_0^1$, $\int_1^2$ and $\int_1^\infty$.
solution: Inside, $\int_0^1r^4\,dr = \tfrac15$. Outside, $\int_1^2r^{-2}\,dr = \tfrac12$ and $\int_1^\infty r^{-2}\,dr = 1$. The fraction is $\dfrac{1/5 + 1/2}{1/5 + 1} = \dfrac{7/10}{6/5} = \dfrac{7}{12}$. The missing $5/12$ lies beyond $r = 2R$, which shows why the integral must run over all space.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from $W = \tfrac12\int\rho V\,d\tau$, derive $W = \tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2\,d\tau$. State the assumptions the derivation uses, and say what goes wrong for a point charge.
hint: Replace $\rho$ by $\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ and use $\nabla\cdot(V\mathbf E) = V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E + \mathbf E\cdot\nabla V$.
hint: Use $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ and the divergence theorem on a large sphere, then see how the surface term scales with the radius.
solution: With $\rho = \epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$, $W = \tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E\,d\tau$. The product rule gives $V\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \nabla\cdot(V\mathbf E) - \mathbf E\cdot\nabla V = \nabla\cdot(V\mathbf E) + E^2$, so $W = \tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\left[\int E^2\,d\tau + \oint_{\mathcal S}V\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf a\right]$ for any volume bounded by $\mathcal S$ that contains all the charge. For localized charge, on a sphere of radius $r$ we have $V\sim1/r$, $E\sim1/r^2$ and area $\sim r^2$, so the surface term goes like $1/r\to0$. Extending the volume to all space gives $W = \tfrac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}}E^2\,d\tau$. The assumptions are localized charge, $V\to0$ at infinity, and an integral over every region with a field. For a point charge $E\sim1/r^2$ near the charge makes $\int E^2\,d\tau$ diverge: the field form includes the infinite self-energy, while the pair form $\tfrac12\sum q_iV_i$, with $V_i$ due to the other charges, leaves it out.
rubric: Substitutes $\rho = \epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E$ and applies the product rule for $\nabla\cdot(V\mathbf E)$.
rubric: Uses $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ to obtain $E^2$ and the divergence theorem to produce a surface term.
rubric: Argues that the surface term vanishes for localized charge because $V\sim1/r$, $E\sim1/r^2$ and the area grows like $r^2$.
rubric: States the assumptions and notes the divergent self-energy for a point charge.

@quiz num
prompt: A long solenoid has a uniform field $B = 2.0$ T inside and none outside. Find the outward magnetic pressure on its wall. Take $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T m/A.
answer: 1.59e6
check: (2.0/(4*pi*1e-7))*((2.0 + 0.0)/2)
unit: Pa
tol: 1%
hint: The jump in $B$ across the wall tells you the surface current density $K = B/\mu_0$.
hint: The force per unit area on a current sheet is $K$ times the average of the fields on its two sides, since the sheet's own field does not push on itself.
solution: $K = B/\mu_0$ and the average field is $B/2$, so the pressure is $K\cdot B/2 = B^2/(2\mu_0) = \dfrac{(2.0)^2}{2(4\pi\times10^{-7})} \approx 1.59\times10^{6}$ Pa, about 16 atmospheres. This is the same as the stress-tensor value $B^2/(2\mu_0)$.
