@article
An electric field assigns a vector to every point in space. Two questions about any such field organise this whole course: how much does it spread out from a point, and how much does it swirl around a point? Those are the **divergence** and the **curl**. This week collects the tools you will use in every later lesson.

### The derivatives

With $\nabla=(\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z)$, a scalar function $f$ and a vector field $\mathbf F$ give

$$\nabla f=(\partial_xf,\partial_yf,\partial_zf),\qquad \nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\partial_xF_x+\partial_yF_y+\partial_zF_z,$$

$$(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x\quad(\text{other components by cyclic permutation}).$$

The gradient points in the direction of steepest increase of $f$. The divergence is net outflow per unit volume: a source. The curl is circulation per unit area: a swirl. The **Laplacian** is $\nabla^2f=\nabla\cdot\nabla f=\partial_x^2f+\partial_y^2f+\partial_z^2f$.

### Two identities

Equality of mixed partial derivatives gives

$$\nabla\times(\nabla f)=\mathbf 0,\qquad \nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0.$$

So a curl-free field can be written as a gradient (on a simply connected region), and a divergence-free field as a curl. Weeks 2 and 10 build the electric and magnetic potentials on exactly these two facts.

### Spherical and cylindrical forms

In spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\varphi)$, with $\theta$ the polar angle,

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{1}{r^2}\partial_r(r^2F_r)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\theta(\sin\theta\,F_\theta)+\frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\partial_\varphi F_\varphi.$$

For a function of $r$ alone, $\nabla^2f=\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}\big(r^2\frac{df}{dr}\big)$, which gives $\nabla^2r^n=n(n+1)r^{n-2}$. In cylindrical coordinates $(s,\varphi,z)$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac1s\partial_s(sF_s)+\frac1s\partial_\varphi F_\varphi+\partial_zF_z$.

### The two big theorems

For a closed surface $S$ bounding a volume $V$, with outward normal,

$$\oint_S\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A=\int_V\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV\qquad\text{(divergence theorem)}.$$

For a closed loop $C$ bounding a surface $S$, oriented by the right-hand rule,

$$\oint_C\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_S(\nabla\times\mathbf F)\cdot d\mathbf A\qquad\text{(Stokes' theorem)}.$$

### One field that breaks the pattern

The field $\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ has zero divergence everywhere except the origin, yet its flux through every sphere around the origin is $4\pi$. By the divergence theorem the divergence must be concentrated at the origin:

$$\nabla\cdot\frac{\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}=4\pi\,\delta^3(\mathbf r).$$

This is Gauss' law for a point charge in disguise, and it returns in every lesson from here on.

@example
title: Checking the divergence theorem on a sphere
problem: Let $\mathbf F=r^2\,\hat{\mathbf r}$. Verify $\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A=\int\nabla\cdot\mathbf F\,dV$ for a sphere of radius $R$ centred on the origin.
step: Surface side. On $r=R$ the field is $R^2\hat{\mathbf r}$, parallel to the outward normal, so $\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf A=R^2\,dA$ and the flux is $R^2\cdot4\pi R^2=4\pi R^4$.
step: Divergence. Only $F_r=r^2$ is nonzero, so $\nabla\cdot\mathbf F=\frac{1}{r^2}\frac{d}{dr}(r^2\cdot r^2)=\frac{4r^3}{r^2}=4r$.
step: Volume side. Slice the ball into thin shells with $dV=4\pi r^2\,dr$: $\int_0^R4r\cdot4\pi r^2\,dr=16\pi\int_0^Rr^3\,dr=4\pi R^4$.
step: Both sides equal $4\pi R^4$, so the theorem checks out. Had we forgotten the $r^2$ inside the derivative we would have got $2r$ for the divergence and a wrong volume integral.

@example
title: Checking Stokes' theorem on a circle
problem: Let $\mathbf F=-y\,\hat{\mathbf x}+x\,\hat{\mathbf y}$. Compare $\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l$ around the circle $x^2+y^2=R^2$ (counterclockwise from $+z$) with the surface integral of the curl over the flat disk.
step: Curl. $(\nabla\times\mathbf F)_z=\partial_x(x)-\partial_y(-y)=2$, and the other components vanish, so $\nabla\times\mathbf F=2\hat{\mathbf z}$.
step: Surface side. The disk has area $\pi R^2$ and $d\mathbf A$ points along $+z$ by the right-hand rule, so the surface integral is $2\pi R^2$.
step: Line side. On the circle $\mathbf F=R\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$ and $d\mathbf l=R\,d\varphi\,\hat{\boldsymbol\varphi}$, so $\oint\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf l=\int_0^{2\pi}R^2\,d\varphi=2\pi R^2$.
step: The two agree, and the sign confirms the counterclockwise orientation matches the $+z$ normal.

@mistakes
- Writing the spherical divergence as $\partial_rF_r+\dots$ and dropping the factors $r^2$ and $\sin\theta$ inside the derivatives.
- Concluding that zero divergence away from the origin means zero source everywhere: the field $\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2$ hides a delta function at the origin.
- Using an inward normal in the divergence theorem, or ignoring the right-hand rule linking the loop direction and the surface normal in Stokes' theorem.

@practice self
prompt: Show that $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$ for any field $\mathbf F$ with continuous second derivatives. Then say in one sentence what this implies for a magnetic field $\mathbf B$ with $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B=0$.
hint: Write the three components of $\nabla\times\mathbf F$ explicitly.
hint: Take $\partial_x$ of the first, $\partial_y$ of the second and $\partial_z$ of the third, and look for pairs of terms.
solution: $\nabla\times\mathbf F=(\partial_yF_z-\partial_zF_y,\;\partial_zF_x-\partial_xF_z,\;\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x)$. Its divergence is $\partial_x\partial_yF_z-\partial_x\partial_zF_y+\partial_y\partial_zF_x-\partial_y\partial_xF_z+\partial_z\partial_xF_y-\partial_z\partial_yF_x$. Continuous second derivatives make mixed partials commute, so the six terms cancel in pairs and the result is $0$. A divergence-free $\mathbf B$ can therefore be written as the curl of some vector field (a vector potential) on a suitable region.

prompt: Which of these fields are conservative, meaning curl-free on all of space so that they can be written as $\nabla f$?
choice*: $(y,\;x,\;0)$
choice: $(-y,\;x,\;0)$
choice*: $(2xy,\;x^2,\;0)$
choice: $(y^2,\;x,\;0)$
hint: For fields of the form $(F_x,F_y,0)$ depending on $x$ and $y$ only, the only nonzero curl component is $\partial_xF_y-\partial_yF_x$.
