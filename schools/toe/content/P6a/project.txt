@project
title: Box potential: analytic series versus numerical grid
overview: You will solve Laplace's equation in a square grounded box with one wall held at a fixed potential, first analytically by separation of variables and then numerically by relaxation on a grid. You will report how the numerical error depends on the grid spacing and on the iteration tolerance, and explain where and why the numerical solution is least accurate.
goal: Set up a complete boundary-value problem and justify the uniqueness of its solution.
goal: Derive and verify a separation-of-variables series solution.
goal: Implement a finite-difference relaxation solver and measure its error against the analytic solution.
goal: Separate iteration error from discretization error and report the observed order of convergence.
step: Define the problem. Take a square box $0\le x,y\le a$ with $a = 1$ m and no charge inside. The walls $x = 0$, $x = a$ and $y = 0$ are grounded, and the wall $y = a$ is held at $V_0 = 1$ V. Write Laplace's equation and the four boundary conditions, and give a short uniqueness argument based on the difference of two solutions.
step: Part A, a smooth warm-up. Give the top wall the profile $V(x, a) = V_0\sin(\pi x/a)$ and solve by separation of variables. Verify by hand that $V = V_0\sin(\pi x/a)\sinh(\pi y/a)/\sinh\pi$ satisfies Laplace's equation and all four boundary conditions.
step: Part B, the constant top wall. Expand $V_0$ on $0<x<a$ in a Fourier sine series and derive $V = \sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{4V_0}{n\pi}\frac{\sin(n\pi x/a)\sinh(n\pi y/a)}{\sinh n\pi}$. Code it and test it: boundary values, a finite-difference Laplacian at interior points, and the symmetry result $V(a/2, a/2) = V_0/4$. Record how many terms give $10^{-6}$ accuracy at the center and at $(0.05a,\ 0.95a)$.
step: Write a relaxation solver for the five-point stencil $V_{i,j} = \tfrac14(V_{i+1,j} + V_{i-1,j} + V_{i,j+1} + V_{i,j-1})$ on an $N\times N$ interior grid with spacing $h = a/(N+1)$. Use Jacobi first, then Gauss–Seidel or successive over-relaxation, and stop when the largest change in a sweep falls below a tolerance. The solver must not use the analytic solution.
step: Validate the solver on Part A. Run $N = 7, 15, 31, 63$ with a tight tolerance such as $10^{-12}$, tabulate the maximum error against the exact solution, and check that halving $h$ reduces the error by about a factor of 4.
step: Run Part B on the same grids. Tabulate the maximum error, the error at the center, the error near $(0.05a,\ 0.95a)$ and the number of sweeps. Then repeat one grid with loose tolerances ($10^{-2}$, $10^{-4}$, $10^{-6}$) to find where iteration error stops dominating.
step: Compute $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ by central differences and the induced surface charge $\sigma(x) = -\epsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial y$ on the bottom wall using a one-sided difference. Compare with the series derivative at $x = a/2$ and say how the relative error compares with that of $V$.
step: Write up the results with tables, a log–log plot of error against $h$ with the fitted slope, and a discussion of the corner where the wall at $V_0$ meets a grounded wall, where the potential is discontinuous.
deliverable: Commented code containing the series evaluator and the relaxation solver, runnable from one script that regenerates every table and figure.
deliverable: A two to four page report with the derivation, the error tables, the log–log plot with its fitted slope, and the discussion.
deliverable: A one-page checklist of the verification tests you ran (boundary values, Laplacian residual, center value $V_0/4$, uniqueness argument) with their results.
rubric: Boundary-value problem and uniqueness: all four boundary conditions and Laplace's equation are stated, and the uniqueness argument uses the difference of two solutions.
rubric: Analytic solution: the series is derived rather than quoted, passes tests of the boundary conditions and Laplace's equation, and reproduces $V_0/4$ at the center.
rubric: Numerical method: a correct five-point relaxation is implemented without using the analytic answer, with a stated stopping rule and boundary treatment.
rubric: Error report: errors are measured against the analytic solution for at least four grid sizes, the observed order of convergence is estimated, and iteration error is separated from discretization error.
rubric: Interpretation: the corner singularity, the slow convergence of the series there and the accuracy of the computed field and surface charge are explained with evidence from your own data.
tutor: My error stops decreasing when I tighten the iteration tolerance. How can I test whether what remains is discretization error, and what should the plot of error against $h$ look like?
tutor: Why does the sine series for the constant top wall converge slowly near the top corners, and how should that affect the way I report the maximum error?
tutor: How can I check that my series solution satisfies Laplace's equation and the boundary conditions without trusting my own derivation?

### Week by week

- **Weeks 1–2.** Gradient, divergence and curl; the divergence and Stokes theorems. $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho/\epsilon_0$, $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$, $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$, and the integral formula for $V$.
- **Weeks 3–4.** $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$; Laplace solutions have no interior extrema. Dirichlet data on the whole boundary fix $V$ uniquely (Neumann data fix it up to an additive constant), since the difference $U$ of two solutions has $\int|\nabla U|^2d\tau = 0$.
- **Weeks 5–6.** The image of $q$ at distance $d$ is $-q$ for a grounded plane and $-qR/d$ at $R^2/d$ for a grounded sphere. Separation of variables gives $\sin(n\pi x/a)\sinh(n\pi y/a)$ in boxes and $(A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell-1})P_\ell(\cos\theta)$ in spheres; orthogonality fits the boundary data.
- **Week 7.** $V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}[Q/r + \mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}/r^2 + \cdots]$; the first nonzero multipole is independent of the origin.
- **Week 8.** $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$, $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$, $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_f$; $D_\perp$ and $\mathbf E_\parallel$ are continuous.
- **Weeks 9–10.** Biot–Savart and Ampère; $\mathbf B_2 - \mathbf B_1 = \mu_0\mathbf K\times\hat{\mathbf n}$; $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$ with $\nabla^2\mathbf A = -\mu_0\mathbf J$ in the Coulomb gauge; flux equals $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$.
- **Week 11.** $\mathbf J_b = \nabla\times\mathbf M$, $\mathbf K_b = \mathbf M\times\hat{\mathbf n}$, $\nabla\times\mathbf H = \mathbf J_f$; $B_\perp$ and $\mathbf H_\parallel$ are continuous.
- **Week 12.** $W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2d\tau$ and $W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int B^2d\tau$; pressures $\epsilon_0E^2/2$ and $B^2/2\mu_0$; the stress tensor.

### Self-check

- I can write a complete boundary-value problem and argue that its solution is unique.
- I can choose between images, separation of variables and direct integration, and justify the choice.
- I can find the leading nonvanishing far-field term of a charge or current distribution.
- I can say which components of $\mathbf E$, $\mathbf D$, $\mathbf B$ and $\mathbf H$ are continuous at an interface, and why.
- I can state the assumptions behind each energy expression and test an answer by a limit, a second method or a numerical check.

### Common errors

- Using image charges in the region where they sit, or counting the energy of the image system instead of half of it.
- Forgetting that a dipole moment depends on the origin unless $Q = 0$.
- Mixing up $E_\perp$ with $D_\perp$, and $H_\parallel$ with $B_\parallel$, at interfaces.
- Treating $\mathbf A$ as unique, or as zero wherever $\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$.
- Integrating field energy over the charged region only.
- Quoting a numerical error without separating iteration error from discretization error.

@exam num
prompt: A point charge $q = 2.0$ nC is brought slowly from far away to a distance $d = 5.0$ cm from an infinite grounded conducting plane. Find the work done by the external agent; a negative sign means the plane does net work on the charge. Take $k = 1/(4\pi\epsilon_0) = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: -1.8e-7
check: -quad(lambda x: 8.99e9*(2.0e-9)**2/(4*x**2), 0.05, 1e4)
unit: J
tol: 1%
hint: Replace the plane by an image charge $-q$ at the mirror point. The real force on $q$ at height $x$ is the Coulomb attraction to the image, at separation $2x$.
hint: The external agent works against that force, so integrate it from $x = \infty$ to $x = d$. As a check, the field energy in the real half-space is half of the energy of the two-charge image system.
solution: The force on $q$ at height $x$ has magnitude $kq^2/(2x)^2 = kq^2/(4x^2)$, toward the plane. The agent's work is $-\int_d^\infty kq^2/(4x^2)\,dx = -kq^2/(4d) = -(8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-9})^2/(4\cdot0.050) \approx -1.8\times10^{-7}$ J. The image system $\pm q$ separated by $2d$ has energy $-kq^2/(2d)$, and the real region holds half of it, which agrees.

@exam num
prompt: A long solenoid of radius $R = 1.2$ cm has $n = 800$ turns per metre and carries $I = 3.0$ A. Treat the winding as a surface current $K = nI$ at $s = R$. Using $W = \tfrac12\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A\,d\tau$ with $\mathbf A$ evaluated at the winding, find the magnetic energy per unit length. Take $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T m/A.
answer: 1.64e-3
check: quad(lambda r: (4*pi*1e-7*800*3.0)**2/(2*4*pi*1e-7)*2*pi*r, 0, 0.012)
unit: J/m
tol: 1%
hint: Find $B$ inside with Ampère's law, then use $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l = \Phi$ on a circle of radius $R$ to get $A_\phi(R)$.
hint: Per unit length, $\tfrac12\int\mathbf J\cdot\mathbf A\,d\tau$ becomes $\tfrac12KA_\phi(R)\cdot2\pi R$.
solution: $B = \mu_0nI = 3.02\times10^{-3}$ T. The flux through the circle of radius $R$ is $B\pi R^2$, so $A_\phi(R) = BR/2$. Then $W/\ell = \tfrac12(nI)(BR/2)(2\pi R) = \tfrac12nIB\,\pi R^2 \approx 1.64\times10^{-3}$ J/m. The field-energy form $\dfrac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\pi R^2$ gives the same value, which checks the assumptions of both expressions.

@exam self
prompt: A grounded conducting sphere of radius $R$ is placed in a region where the field was uniform, $\mathbf E_0 = E_0\hat{\mathbf z}$, before the sphere was inserted. (a) State the boundary data for $V$ outside the sphere and explain why the solution is unique. (b) Solve for $V$ by separation of variables in spherical coordinates. (c) Find the induced surface charge density and show that the total induced charge is zero. (d) Identify the leading far-field correction to the applied potential and give its dipole moment.
hint: Only $\ell = 1$ survives when you match $V\to-E_0r\cos\theta$ at large $r$ with $V = 0$ at $r = R$.
hint: For the surface charge use $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial r$ at $r = R$.
solution: (a) $\nabla^2V = 0$ for $r>R$, with $V = 0$ on $r = R$ and $V\to-E_0r\cos\theta$ as $r\to\infty$. If $V_1$ and $V_2$ both satisfy this, then $U = V_1 - V_2$ obeys Laplace's equation with $U = 0$ on $r = R$ and $U\to0$ at infinity, so $\int|\nabla U|^2d\tau = \oint U\nabla U\cdot d\mathbf a = 0$ and $U = 0$. (b) Write $V = \sum_\ell(A_\ell r^\ell + B_\ell r^{-\ell-1})P_\ell(\cos\theta)$. The behavior at infinity gives $A_1 = -E_0$ and $A_\ell = 0$ for $\ell\neq1$. Setting $V(R,\theta) = 0$ for all $\theta$ gives $B_1 = E_0R^3$ and all other $B_\ell = 0$, so $V = -E_0\left(r - R^3/r^2\right)\cos\theta$. (c) $\sigma = -\epsilon_0\,\partial_rV|_{r=R} = \epsilon_0E_0(1 + 2)\cos\theta = 3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$. The total charge is $\int_0^\pi3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta\,2\pi R^2\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$. (d) The term $E_0R^3\cos\theta/r^2$ is a dipole potential with $p = 4\pi\epsilon_0R^3E_0$ along $\mathbf E_0$. No monopole is induced, so this dipole is the leading correction far away.
rubric: States the boundary data (Laplace's equation outside, $V = 0$ on the sphere, the uniform-field form at infinity) and gives a uniqueness argument using the difference of two solutions.
rubric: Obtains $V = -E_0(r - R^3/r^2)\cos\theta$ by matching only the $\ell = 1$ term.
rubric: Finds $\sigma = 3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$ and shows that its integral over the sphere vanishes.
rubric: Identifies the $1/r^2$ term as a dipole with $p = 4\pi\epsilon_0R^3E_0$ and explains why no monopole term appears.
