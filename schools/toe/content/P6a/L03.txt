So far we built potentials by adding up charges. Often we do not know where all the charges are, for example when conductors are held at fixed voltages. Then it is better to treat $V$ as the unknown in a differential equation and supply boundary data.

### From Gauss's law to Poisson's equation

Gauss's law in differential form is $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$. With $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ it becomes

$$\nabla^2V=-\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\qquad\text{(Poisson's equation)}.$$

Where $\rho=0$ this reduces to **Laplace's equation** $\nabla^2V=0$, whose solutions are called **harmonic** functions.

### Solutions that depend on one coordinate

Use the Laplacian in the matching coordinates:

| Symmetry | General solution of $\nabla^2V=0$ |
|---|---|
| planar, $V(x)$ | $V=a+bx$ |
| spherical, $V(r)$ | $V=a+b/r$ |
| cylindrical, $V(s)$ | $V=a+b\ln s$ |

Two constants need two pieces of boundary data, such as the voltages on two conducting surfaces.

### Harmonic functions average

Laplace's equation says each point is the average of its surroundings: $V$ at a point equals the mean of $V$ over any sphere around it that encloses no charge. Three consequences:

1. $V$ has no local maximum or minimum in a charge-free region; extremes sit on the boundary.
2. A charge cannot be held in stable equilibrium by static electric fields alone, because that would need a minimum of its potential energy.
3. On a square grid of spacing $h$ in two dimensions, $V_{i,j}\approx\tfrac14\big(V_{i+1,j}+V_{i-1,j}+V_{i,j+1}+V_{i,j-1}\big)$. Repeating this update, called relaxation, solves a boundary-value problem numerically:

```python
import numpy as np
V = np.zeros((20, 20)); V[0, :] = 1.0   # top edge at 1 V, other edges grounded
for _ in range(2000):
    V[1:-1, 1:-1] = 0.25 * (V[2:, 1:-1] + V[:-2, 1:-1] + V[1:-1, 2:] + V[1:-1, :-2])
```

### Boundary conditions

A **Dirichlet** condition specifies $V$ on the boundary. A **Neumann** condition specifies $\partial V/\partial n$, which equals $-E_n$; at a conductor surface $\sigma=\epsilon_0E_n$. Week 4 shows which combinations give exactly one solution.

@example
title: A sphere inside a grounded shell
problem: A sphere of radius $a$ is held at potential $V_0$ inside a concentric grounded spherical shell of radius $b>a$. Find $V(r)$ for $a<r<b$ and the charge on the inner sphere.
step: The gap is charge-free and the problem depends on $r$ only, so $V=A+B/r$.
step: Impose $V(b)=0$, which gives $A=-B/b$. Impose $V(a)=V_0$, which gives $B\big(\tfrac1a-\tfrac1b\big)=V_0$, so $B=\dfrac{V_0\,ab}{b-a}$.
step: Therefore $V(r)=\dfrac{V_0\,a\,(b-r)}{r\,(b-a)}$. Check: at $r=a$ it equals $V_0$ and at $r=b$ it equals $0$.
step: The field is $E_r=-dV/dr=B/r^2$. At the inner sphere $\sigma=\epsilon_0B/a^2$, so $Q=4\pi a^2\sigma=4\pi\epsilon_0B=\dfrac{4\pi\epsilon_0V_0\,ab}{b-a}$. As $b\to\infty$ this tends to $4\pi\epsilon_0aV_0$, the charge of an isolated sphere.

@example
title: Charge between grounded plates
problem: Two grounded plates sit at $x=0$ and $x=d$, with uniform charge density $\rho_0$ filling the gap. Find $V(x)$ and $E_x(x)$.
step: The problem depends on $x$ only, so Poisson's equation is $V''=-\rho_0/\epsilon_0$.
step: Integrate twice: $V=-\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0}x^2+ax+b$.
step: $V(0)=0$ gives $b=0$. $V(d)=0$ gives $a=\dfrac{\rho_0d}{2\epsilon_0}$. Hence $V(x)=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0}\,x(d-x)$.
step: The field is $E_x=-V'=\dfrac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0}(2x-d)$. It vanishes at the midplane, as symmetry demands, and the jump $E_x(d)-E_x(0)=\rho_0d/\epsilon_0$ matches Gauss's law applied to the slab.

@mistakes
- Using Laplace's equation inside a region that holds charge; there Poisson's equation with the actual $\rho$ applies.
- Using $V=a+b/r$ for a long cylinder or $V=a+b\ln s$ for a sphere: the form depends on the symmetry.
- Expecting a harmonic function to have an interior maximum or minimum.

prompt: Show that if $V(x,y)$ satisfies Laplace's equation in two dimensions, then for a small grid spacing $h$, $V(x,y)\approx\frac14\big[V(x+h,y)+V(x-h,y)+V(x,y+h)+V(x,y-h)\big]$ with an error of order $h^4$. Then describe how repeating this update solves a boundary-value problem and how you would decide when to stop.
hint: Taylor-expand each of the four neighbouring values about $(x,y)$ up to second order.
hint: Add the four expansions. The first-order terms cancel in pairs, and the second-order terms combine into $h^2\nabla^2V$.
solution: $V(x\pm h,y)=V\pm hV_x+\tfrac12h^2V_{xx}\pm\dots$ and similarly in $y$. Adding the four neighbours gives $4V+h^2(V_{xx}+V_{yy})+O(h^4)$, since odd-order terms cancel. With $V_{xx}+V_{yy}=0$ the average equals $V$ up to $O(h^4)$. To solve a boundary-value problem, fix the grid values on the boundary to the given data, guess the interior (for example zero), and sweep: replace each interior value by the average of its four neighbours, using old values (Jacobi) or freshly updated ones (Gauss-Seidel). Repeat until the largest change in a sweep falls below a chosen tolerance.
