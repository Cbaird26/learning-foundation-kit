@article
Place a point charge $q$ at the origin and the electric field is $\mathbf E=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}$. For many charges the fields simply add (superposition), but adding vectors that point in different directions is tedious. Potentials are scalars, so they add with no bookkeeping of direction. This lesson builds a potential and then recovers the field from it.

### Why a potential exists

The Coulomb field is radial and depends only on $r$, so its curl is zero, and by superposition so is the curl of every static field:

$$\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0.$$

By Stokes' theorem $\oint\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l=0$ around every closed loop, so the work done moving a test charge between two points does not depend on the path. That lets us define

$$V(\mathbf r)=-\int_{\mathcal O}^{\mathbf r}\mathbf E\cdot d\mathbf l,\qquad \mathbf E=-\nabla V.$$

$V$ is potential energy per unit charge (volts). Only differences matter. For charges confined to a finite region we use the convention $V(\infty)=0$ to fix the constant.

### Potential from sources

A point charge gives $V=\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}$. Superposition over a continuous distribution gives

$$V(\mathbf r)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{\rho(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}\,d\tau',$$

with $\sigma\,dA'$ or $\lambda\,dl'$ in place of $\rho\,d\tau'$ for surface and line charges. The integrand is a scalar, so there are no components to track.

### Results worth remembering

On the axis of a uniformly charged ring (radius $R$, total charge $Q$):

$$V(z)=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}.$$

On the axis of a uniformly charged disk (surface density $\sigma$), for $z>0$:

$$V(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\Big(\sqrt{z^2+R^2}-z\Big).$$

Outside a uniformly charged spherical shell, $V=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0r}$, exactly as for a point charge at the centre; inside the shell $V$ is constant and equal to its surface value.

### Always check the field

After finding $V$, differentiate it and compare with an independent route (Coulomb's law, symmetry or Gauss' law). Two sanity checks catch most errors: far away the potential must look like that of a point charge, and the field must point from high to low potential.

### Infinite sources

For an infinite line or plane of charge the integral that defines $V$ with $V(\infty)=0$ diverges. Choose a finite reference point instead and quote potential differences only.

@example
title: Potential and field on the axis of a ring
problem: A ring of radius $R$ in the $xy$-plane carries uniform total charge $Q$. Find $V$ on the $z$-axis, then check that $-dV/dz$ equals the field obtained by adding up Coulomb fields directly.
step: Every element $dq$ of the ring is at the same distance $s=\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$ from the axis point $(0,0,z)$.
step: Potentials add as scalars: $V(z)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{s}=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}$.
step: Differentiate along the axis: $E_z=-\frac{dV}{dz}=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{z}{(z^2+R^2)^{3/2}}$.
step: Direct route. Each $dq$ produces a field of magnitude $\frac{dq}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}$ pointing away from it. Its $z$-component has the factor $\cos\alpha=z/s$, and the transverse components cancel in pairs on opposite sides of the ring. Summing gives $E_z=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{z}{s^3}$, the same result.
step: Limit check. For $z\gg R$, $V\to\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0z}$ and $E_z\to\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0z^2}$, the point-charge values. For $z=0$ the field vanishes, as symmetry requires at the centre.

@mistakes
- Adding the magnitudes of fields first and then trying to turn them into a potential, instead of adding the scalar potentials.
- Dropping the minus sign in $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$, so the field points uphill.
- Using $V(\infty)=0$ for an infinite line or plane of charge, where the defining integral diverges.
- Using a potential known only on the symmetry axis to claim the field everywhere: $-dV/dz$ gives $E_z$ on the axis only, not the off-axis components.

@practice num
prompt: A ring of radius $R$ carries total charge $Q$ spread uniformly. Taking $V(\infty)=0$, find the potential at the axis point a distance $z=R$ from the ring's centre, in units of $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)$.
answer: sqrt(2)/2
check: quad(lambda th: 1/sqrt(cos(th)**2 + sin(th)**2 + 1), 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: Every element of the ring is the same distance from the axis point.
hint: That distance is $\sqrt{z^2+R^2}$, so $V=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}$.
hint: Put $z=R$ and express the result as a multiple of $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)$.
solution: The distance is $\sqrt{R^2+R^2}=\sqrt2R$, so $V=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0\sqrt2R}=\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\cdot\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0R}$.

@practice num
prompt: A uniformly charged disk of radius $R$ and surface charge density $\sigma$ has potential $V(z)=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\big(\sqrt{z^2+R^2}-z\big)$ on its axis for $z>0$. Find $E_z$ at $z=R$, in units of $\sigma/\epsilon_0$.
answer: (2-sqrt(2))/4
check: -nderiv(lambda z: 0.5*(sqrt(z**2 + 1) - z), 1)
hint: $E_z=-dV/dz$ along the axis.
hint: $\frac{d}{dz}\sqrt{z^2+R^2}=\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}$.
hint: Evaluate $\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\big(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\big)$ at $z=R$.
solution: $E_z=\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\Big(1-\frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\Big)$. At $z=R$: $\frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\big(1-\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big)=\frac{2-\sqrt2}{4}\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\approx0.146\,\sigma/\epsilon_0$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which fact guarantees that a static electric field can be written as $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$?
choice*: $\nabla\times\mathbf E=\mathbf 0$, so the line integral of $\mathbf E$ between two points is independent of the path.
choice: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf E=\rho/\epsilon_0$, which makes $\mathbf E$ divergence-free.
choice: The Coulomb force is inverse-square, so any potential must fall off as $1/r$.
choice: The potential is defined to be the magnitude of $\mathbf E$.
hint: A function $V$ with $\mathbf E=-\nabla V$ exists exactly when the work done by $\mathbf E$ between two points does not depend on the path.
hint: Which of the two Maxwell statements for static fields, the divergence one or the curl one, controls path independence?

@practice self
prompt: A thin rod of length $2L$ lies along the $z$-axis from $z=-L$ to $z=L$ and carries uniform charge density $\lambda$. (a) Derive the potential at the point at perpendicular distance $s$ from the midpoint of the rod, in the plane $z=0$. (b) Differentiate to find $E_s$ there and check it against a direct integration of Coulomb's law. (c) Test your result in the limits $L\to\infty$ and $s\gg L$.
hint: In the plane $z=0$ an element $\lambda\,dz'$ at height $z'$ is at distance $\sqrt{s^2+z'^2}$ from the field point.
hint: $\int\frac{dz'}{\sqrt{s^2+z'^2}}=\ln\big(z'+\sqrt{s^2+z'^2}\big)$. For the direct check, only the component along $\hat{\mathbf s}$ survives by symmetry.
solution: (a) $V(s)=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{dz'}{\sqrt{s^2+z'^2}}=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{L+\sqrt{L^2+s^2}}{s}$. (b) Differentiating, $E_s=-\frac{dV}{ds}=\frac{\lambda L}{2\pi\epsilon_0\,s\sqrt{L^2+s^2}}$. Directly, $E_s=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{s\,dz'}{(s^2+z'^2)^{3/2}}=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2L}{s\sqrt{L^2+s^2}}$, which agrees. (c) For $L\to\infty$, $E_s\to\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0s}$, the infinite-line field. For $s\gg L$, $E_s\to\frac{\lambda L}{2\pi\epsilon_0s^2}=\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0s^2}$ with $Q=2\lambda L$, the point-charge field.

rubric: Evaluates the integral to a logarithm or inverse hyperbolic sine form.
rubric: Differentiates with respect to $s$ and obtains the field, and confirms it with the direct vector integral (transverse symmetry argument included).

@quiz num
prompt: A solid ball of radius $R$ carries total charge $Q$ spread uniformly through its volume. Taking $V(\infty)=0$, find the potential at the centre in units of $Q/(4\pi\epsilon_0R)$.
answer: 3/2
check: quad(lambda r: 3/(4*pi)*4*pi*r**2/r, 0, 1)
hint: Superposition at the centre: every thin shell of radius $r$ sits a distance $r$ from the centre.
hint: Use $\rho=\frac{3Q}{4\pi R^3}$ and shell volume $d\tau=4\pi r^2\,dr$.
hint: The integral becomes $\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3Q}{R^3}\int_0^Rr\,dr$.
solution: $V(0)=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^R\frac{\rho\,4\pi r^2\,dr}{r}=\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{3Q}{R^3}\frac{R^2}{2}=\frac32\frac{Q}{4\pi\epsilon_0R}$.

@quiz mc
prompt: An infinite straight line carries uniform charge per unit length $\lambda$, producing the field $E_s=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0s}$ at distance $s$. Which statement about its potential is correct?
choice*: $V(s)=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln(s/s_0)$ for an arbitrary reference distance $s_0$; the choice $V(\infty)=0$ is impossible.
choice: $V(s)=\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0s}$, the same function as the field.
choice: $V(s)=\frac{\lambda}{4\pi\epsilon_0s}$, as for a point charge.
choice: $V=0$ everywhere, because the field is purely radial.
hint: Integrate $-E_s\,ds$ from a reference distance $s_0$ out to $s$.
hint: The antiderivative of $1/s$ grows without bound as $s\to\infty$.
solution: $V(s)-V(s_0)=-\int_{s_0}^{s}\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0s'}ds'=-\frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0}\ln\frac{s}{s_0}$. The logarithm diverges at infinity, so only the reference distance $s_0$ can be chosen freely.
