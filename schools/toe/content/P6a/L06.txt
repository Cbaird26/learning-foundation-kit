@article
When the boundaries follow coordinate surfaces (the planes of a box, a sphere, a cylinder), Laplace's equation splits into ordinary differential equations if we try a product solution. We then match the boundary data with a series. Uniqueness guarantees that a series meeting every condition is the solution.

### Cartesian: a rectangle

In two dimensions try $V=X(x)Y(y)$. Then $\nabla^2V=0$ becomes $X''/X+Y''/Y=0$, so each term is a constant: $X''=-k^2X$ and $Y''=k^2Y$. If $V=0$ on $x=0$ and $x=a$, then $X=\sin(k_nx)$ with $k_n=n\pi/a$. The condition $V=0$ at $y=0$ picks $Y=\sinh(k_ny)$, so

$$V=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\sinh\frac{n\pi y}{a}.$$

The remaining side fixes $c_n$ through orthogonality:

$$\int_0^a\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\sin\frac{m\pi x}{a}\,dx=\frac a2\,\delta_{nm}.$$

For a decaying solution on a semi-infinite strip use $e^{-k_ny}$ instead of $\sinh$.

### Spherical, with azimuthal symmetry

For $V(r,\theta)$ the separated solutions are $r^l$ and $r^{-(l+1)}$ times Legendre polynomials in $\cos\theta$:

$$V=\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}\Big(A_lr^l+\frac{B_l}{r^{l+1}}\Big)P_l(\cos\theta),\qquad P_0=1,\ P_1=\cos\theta,\ P_2=\tfrac12(3\cos^2\theta-1).$$

Orthogonality: $\int_{-1}^{1}P_lP_{l'}\,d(\cos\theta)=\dfrac{2}{2l+1}\delta_{ll'}$. In two-dimensional polar coordinates the analogues are $s^{\pm n}\cos n\varphi$ and $s^{\pm n}\sin n\varphi$.

### Strategy

1. Choose coordinates in which each boundary is a surface of constant coordinate.
2. Write the general separated solution and discard terms that are singular in your region (for example $B_l$ if the origin is included).
3. Impose the homogeneous conditions first; they fix the separation constants.
4. Match the remaining condition by orthogonality, or by inspection when the data is a single mode.
5. Check the boundary values, the behaviour at infinity and a limiting case.

@example
title: A box with one side held at a fixed potential
problem: A rectangle $0<x<a$, $0<y<b$ has the sides $x=0$, $x=a$ and $y=0$ grounded, and the side $y=b$ held at $V_0$. Find the series for $V(x,y)$.
step: Separation with $V=0$ at $x=0,a$ and $y=0$ gives $V=\sum c_n\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\sinh\frac{n\pi y}{a}$.
step: At $y=b$ we need $V_0=\sum c_n\sinh\frac{n\pi b}{a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}$. Multiply by $\sin\frac{m\pi x}{a}$ and integrate over $0<x<a$.
step: Orthogonality gives $\frac a2c_m\sinh\frac{m\pi b}{a}=V_0\int_0^a\sin\frac{m\pi x}{a}dx=V_0\,\frac{a\,(1-\cos m\pi)}{m\pi}$.
step: So $c_m\sinh\frac{m\pi b}{a}=\frac{4V_0}{m\pi}$ for odd $m$ and $0$ for even $m$. Therefore $V=\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\frac{4V_0}{n\pi}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}\frac{\sinh(n\pi y/a)}{\sinh(n\pi b/a)}$.
step: Check: at $y=b$ the series is the square-wave expansion of $V_0$, and it vanishes on the other three sides.

@example
title: Potential on a sphere that varies with angle
problem: The surface $r=R$ is held at $V(R,\theta)=V_0\cos^2\theta$, and there is no charge inside. Find $V$ for $r<R$.
step: The region contains the origin, so every $B_l=0$ and $V=\sum A_lr^lP_l(\cos\theta)$.
step: Decompose the data into Legendre polynomials. Since $P_2=\tfrac12(3\cos^2\theta-1)$, $\cos^2\theta=\tfrac13P_0+\tfrac23P_2$.
step: Match at $r=R$: $A_0=\dfrac{V_0}{3}$ and $A_2R^2=\dfrac{2V_0}{3}$, with all other $A_l=0$ by orthogonality.
step: Result: $V=V_0\Big[\dfrac13+\dfrac23\dfrac{r^2}{R^2}P_2(\cos\theta)\Big]$. Check: at $r=R$ it gives $V_0\big[\tfrac13+\tfrac13(3\cos^2\theta-1)\big]=V_0\cos^2\theta$.

@mistakes
- Choosing the sign of the separation constant without looking at the boundary conditions: oscillating (sine) factors go with the direction that has two homogeneous boundaries.
- Keeping $B_l/r^{l+1}$ terms in a region that contains the origin, or $A_lr^l$ with $l\ge0$ (the constant $A_0$ included) when $V\to0$ at infinity.
- Imposing the inhomogeneous condition before the homogeneous ones, so the series cannot be matched.
- Forgetting that the series only gives the solution if every boundary condition holds, including the one at infinity.

@practice num
prompt: Between grounded walls at $x=0$ and $x=2\ \text{m}$ the separated equation is $X''=-k^2X$ with $X(0)=X(2)=0$ and $X\not\equiv0$. Find the smallest positive value of $k$.
answer: pi/2
unit: rad/m
check: root(lambda k: sin(2*k), 1, 2)
hint: The solution vanishing at $x=0$ is $X=\sin(kx)$.
hint: Demand $\sin(2k)=0$ and pick the smallest positive $k$.
solution: $X=\sin kx$ vanishes at $x=2$ when $2k=n\pi$, so $k=n\pi/2$. The smallest positive value is $k=\pi/2\ \text{rad/m}$.

@practice num
prompt: On the side $y=1$ of a unit square, $V=1\ \text{V}$ for $0<x<1$. Writing $V(x,1)=\sum_nd_n\sin(n\pi x)$, find $d_3$.
answer: 4/(3pi)
check: 2*quad(lambda x: sin(3*pi*x), 0, 1)
hint: Orthogonality: $d_n=2\int_0^1V(x,1)\sin(n\pi x)\,dx$.
hint: With $V=1$ this is $2\int_0^1\sin(3\pi x)\,dx$.
solution: $d_3=2\cdot\frac{1-\cos3\pi}{3\pi}=\frac{4}{3\pi}\approx0.424$.

@practice num
prompt: In the strip $0<x<1$, $y>0$ the potential vanishes on $x=0$, on $x=1$ and as $y\to\infty$, and $V(x,0)=3\sin(\pi x)$ volts. Find $V(0.5,\,0.5)$ in volts, to three decimal places.
answer: 3*e^(-pi/2)
unit: V
tol: 0.002
check: sum(2*quad(lambda s: 3*sin(pi*s)*sin(n*pi*s), 0, 1)*sin(n*pi*0.5)*exp(-n*pi*0.5) for n in range(1, 6))
hint: A decaying separated solution is $\sin(n\pi x)e^{-n\pi y}$.
hint: The data $3\sin(\pi x)$ is a single mode, so only $n=1$ appears.
solution: $V(x,y)=3\sin(\pi x)e^{-\pi y}$. At $(0.5,0.5)$: $V=3e^{-\pi/2}\approx0.624\ \text{V}$.

@practice mc
prompt: In the series $V=\sum_l\big(A_lr^l+B_l/r^{l+1}\big)P_l(\cos\theta)$ for the region $r<R$ (charge-free, including the origin), which coefficients must vanish, and why?
choice*: All $B_l$, because $r^{-(l+1)}$ diverges at the origin where $V$ must be finite.
choice: All $A_l$, because the potential must vanish at the surface.
choice: Only $B_0$, because a monopole term cannot appear in a charge-free region.
choice: None of them; the boundary data always select the terms.
hint: Is $V$ finite at $r=0$ for the term $1/r^{l+1}$?
hint: A potential in a charge-free ball must be a smooth harmonic function throughout, including the centre.
solution: The terms $B_l/r^{l+1}$ blow up at $r=0$, but a charge-free ball has a finite harmonic potential at its centre. So all $B_l=0$, and the $A_l$ are fixed by the data on $r=R$.

@practice self
prompt: A grounded conducting sphere of radius $R$ sits in an initially uniform field $E_0\hat{\mathbf z}$, with the zero of potential chosen so that the undisturbed potential is $-E_0z$. (a) State the region, the equation and the boundary data, and say why they give a unique answer. (b) Find $V(r,\theta)$ for $r>R$ by separation of variables. (c) Find the induced surface charge density and check that the total induced charge is zero.
hint: Far from the sphere $V\to-E_0r\cos\theta$, which is the $l=1$ term; the grounded surface gives $V(R,\theta)=0$.
hint: Match each Legendre component separately at $r=R$: $A_lR^l+B_lR^{-(l+1)}=0$.
solution: (a) Region $r>R$ with $\nabla^2V=0$; Dirichlet data $V(R,\theta)=0$ and $V\to-E_0r\cos\theta$ at large $r$; uniqueness holds because the data fix $V$ on the whole boundary (sphere and infinity). (b) The general solution is $\sum(A_lr^l+B_lr^{-(l+1)})P_l(\cos\theta)$. At large $r$ only $A_1=-E_0$ survives and all other $A_l=0$. Then $V(R,\theta)=0$ forces $B_l=-A_lR^{2l+1}$, so only $B_1=E_0R^3$ is nonzero (and $B_0=0$). Hence $V=-E_0\big(r-\frac{R^3}{r^2}\big)\cos\theta$. (c) $\sigma=-\epsilon_0\partial_rV|_R=\epsilon_0E_0\big(1+2\big)\cos\theta=3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$. The total is $\int\sigma\,dA=3\epsilon_0E_0R^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta=0$.
rubric: States the region, Laplace's equation and both Dirichlet conditions (surface and infinity) and cites uniqueness.
rubric: Writes the general separated solution and uses the far-field condition to keep only $A_1$ and discard the other $A_l$.
rubric: Imposes $V(R,\theta)=0$ term by term and obtains $V=-E_0(r-R^3/r^2)\cos\theta$.
rubric: Computes $\sigma=3\epsilon_0E_0\cos\theta$ and confirms zero net charge.

@quiz num
prompt: A square box $0<x<1\ \text{m}$, $0<y<1\ \text{m}$ has three sides grounded and the top side $y=1$ held at $100\ \text{V}$. Find the potential at the centre of the box, in volts.
answer: 25
unit: V
tol: 0.01
check: sum(4*100/(n*pi)*sin(n*pi*0.5)*sinh(n*pi*0.5)/sinh(n*pi) for n in range(1, 60, 2))
hint: Superpose four copies of the problem, each rotated by $90^\circ$ so that each side takes its turn at $100\ \text{V}$.
hint: The four rotated problems add to the problem with all four sides at $100\ \text{V}$, whose solution is constant.
solution: With all four sides at $100\ \text{V}$ the unique solution is $V=100\ \text{V}$ everywhere. By symmetry the four rotated problems give equal values at the centre, so each contributes $100/4=25\ \text{V}$. The series from the lesson agrees: $25.37-0.38+0.01-\dots\approx25$.

@quiz num
prompt: A long grounded conducting cylinder of radius $R$ lies along the $z$-axis in an initially uniform transverse field $E_0\hat{\mathbf x}$, so that far away $V\to-E_0s\cos\varphi$. Outside the cylinder the relevant separated terms are $(As+B/s)\cos\varphi$. Find the induced surface charge density at $\varphi=0$, in units of $\epsilon_0E_0$.
answer: 2
check: (lambda ab: -(ab[0] - ab[1]))(lin([[1, 0], [1, 1]], [-1, 0]))
hint: The far-field condition fixes $A$, and $V(R,\varphi)=0$ fixes $B$.
hint: Use $\sigma=-\epsilon_0\,\partial V/\partial s$ evaluated at $s=R$.
solution: $A=-E_0$, and $AR+B/R=0$ gives $B=E_0R^2$. So $V=-E_0\big(s-R^2/s\big)\cos\varphi$, and $\sigma=-\epsilon_0\partial_sV|_R=\epsilon_0E_0\big(1+1\big)\cos\varphi=2\epsilon_0E_0\cos\varphi$. At $\varphi=0$ this is $2\epsilon_0E_0$.
