@lesson

objective: Compute the total charge, dipole moment and leading quadrupole term of a point-charge configuration.

@article
Seen from far away, any blob of charge looks like a single point charge. If the total charge is zero, the leading effect comes from the way positive and negative charge are displaced from each other: a **dipole**. If that vanishes too, the next correction is a **quadrupole**, and so on. The **multipole expansion** organises these corrections as a series in powers of $1/r$.

### The expansion

Put the origin inside a charge distribution $\rho(\mathbf r')$ of size $a$ and look at a point $\mathbf r$ with $r \gg a$. Let $\alpha$ be the angle between $\mathbf r$ and the source point $\mathbf r'$. The generating function of the Legendre polynomials, with $P_0 = 1$, $P_1(x) = x$ and $P_2(x) = \tfrac12(3x^2 - 1)$, gives

$$\frac{1}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{r'^{\,n}}{r^{\,n+1}}\, P_n(\cos\alpha) \qquad (r' < r).$$

Put this into $V(\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|}\,d\tau'$ and pull out the powers of $r$:

$$V(\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[\frac{Q}{r} + \frac{\mathbf p\cdot\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2} + \frac{1}{r^3}\int r'^2 P_2(\cos\alpha)\,\rho\,d\tau' + \cdots\right].$$

The first term involves the total charge $Q = \int\rho\,d\tau'$ (the **monopole**). The second involves the **dipole moment**

$$\mathbf p = \int \mathbf r'\,\rho(\mathbf r')\,d\tau' = \sum_i q_i\mathbf r_i \quad\text{(for point charges)}.$$

Each higher term falls off one power of $1/r$ faster, so the first nonzero term dominates at large distance.

### The dipole field

For $\mathbf p = p\,\hat{\mathbf z}$ the dipole term is $V = \dfrac{p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2}$. Applying $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$ in spherical coordinates gives

$$E_r = \frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}, \qquad E_\theta = \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}.$$

The field falls as $1/r^3$, one power faster than the $1/r^2$ field of a point charge, and it is not purely radial.

### Does the origin matter?

Shift the origin by $\mathbf d$, so each position $\mathbf r_i$ becomes $\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d$. The dipole moment becomes

$$\mathbf p' = \mathbf p - Q\,\mathbf d.$$

So $\mathbf p$ is a property of the distribution alone **only when $Q = 0$**. When $Q \neq 0$ you can choose $\mathbf d = \mathbf p/Q$ to remove the dipole term entirely. The pattern repeats: the first nonvanishing multipole does not depend on the origin.

### A pure quadrupole

Charges $+q$ at $z = \pm a$ and $-2q$ at the origin have $Q = 0$ and $\mathbf p = \mathbf{0}$. Each outer charge contributes $q a^2 P_2(\cos\theta)/r^3$, because $P_2$ is even, so

$$V \approx \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\,\frac{2 q a^2 P_2(\cos\theta)}{r^3} = \frac{q a^2\,(3\cos^2\theta - 1)}{4\pi\epsilon_0\, r^3}.$$

The potential falls as $1/r^3$ and the field as $1/r^4$.

@example
title: Leading far-field term of a linear quadrupole
problem: Charges $+q$ sit at $z = a$ and $z = -a$, and a charge $-2q$ sits at the origin. Find the leading far-field potential on the positive $z$-axis, and evaluate it for $q = 1.0$ nC, $a = 1.0$ cm and $r = 1.0$ m. Take $k = 1/(4\pi\epsilon_0) = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
step: The total charge is $Q = q + q - 2q = 0$, so there is no monopole term.
step: The dipole moment is $\mathbf p = q(a\hat{\mathbf z}) + q(-a\hat{\mathbf z}) - 2q\,\mathbf{0} = \mathbf{0}$, so there is no dipole term either. The quadrupole term leads.
step: Each outer charge is at distance $a$ from the origin and $P_2$ is even, so both give $q a^2 P_2(\cos\theta)/r^3$. The charge at the origin has $r' = 0$ and contributes nothing at this order. Hence $V \approx k\,\dfrac{2qa^2P_2(\cos\theta)}{r^3}$.
step: On the positive $z$-axis $\theta = 0$ and $P_2(1) = 1$, so $V \approx 2kqa^2/r^3$.
step: Numbers: $V \approx 2(8.99\times10^{9})(1.0\times10^{-9})(1.0\times10^{-2})^2/(1.0)^3 \approx 1.8\times10^{-3}$ V.
step: Check against the exact sum $kq\left[\frac{1}{r-a} + \frac{1}{r+a} - \frac{2}{r}\right] = 1.7982\times10^{-3}$ V. The leading term is accurate to about $0.01\%$.

@example
title: Shifting the origin
problem: A charge $+2q$ sits at $x = a$ and a charge $-q$ at $x = -a$. Find the dipole moment about the origin and about the point $x = a$, and check the shift rule $\mathbf p' = \mathbf p - Q\mathbf d$.
step: About the origin, $Q = 2q - q = q$ and $p_x = (2q)(a) + (-q)(-a) = 3qa$.
step: About $x = a$ the positions become $0$ and $-2a$, so $p'_x = (2q)(0) + (-q)(-2a) = 2qa$.
step: The shift rule with $\mathbf d = a\hat{\mathbf x}$ gives $p_x - Qd = 3qa - qa = 2qa$, in agreement.
step: Because $Q \neq 0$ the dipole moment has no unique value. Choosing $\mathbf d = \mathbf p/Q = 3a\,\hat{\mathbf x}$ puts the charges at $-2a$ and $-4a$, and then $(2q)(-2a) + (-q)(-4a) = 0$: the dipole term vanishes.

@mistakes
- Quoting "the" dipole moment of a distribution with $Q \neq 0$ as if it were unique; it depends on the origin unless $Q = 0$.
- Using the wrong angle in $P_n(\cos\alpha)$: it is the angle between the field point direction $\mathbf r$ and each source position $\mathbf r'$, not the angle of $\mathbf r'$ from the $z$-axis.
- Mixing up potential and field falloff: a dipole's potential falls as $1/r^2$ but its field falls as $1/r^3$.

lead: $V =$

@practice self
prompt: Point charges $q_i$ sit at positions $\mathbf r_i$ measured from an origin $O$. Show that the dipole moment measured from a new origin $O'$, displaced by $\mathbf d$ from $O$, is $\mathbf p' = \mathbf p - Q\mathbf d$. Then say where to put the origin so that a distribution with $Q \neq 0$ has no dipole term, and what happens when $Q = 0$.
hint: Positions measured from $O'$ are $\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d$.
hint: Pull the constant vector $\mathbf d$ out of the sum $\sum_i q_i(\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d)$.
solution: Measured from $O'$ the positions are $\mathbf r_i' = \mathbf r_i - \mathbf d$, so $\mathbf p' = \sum_i q_i(\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d) = \sum_i q_i\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d\sum_i q_i = \mathbf p - Q\mathbf d$. Setting $\mathbf p' = \mathbf{0}$ gives $\mathbf d = \mathbf p/Q$, which exists only if $Q \neq 0$. If $Q = 0$, then $\mathbf p' = \mathbf p$ for every $\mathbf d$, so the dipole moment is the same about any origin.
rubric: Writes the new positions as $\mathbf r_i - \mathbf d$ and substitutes them into $\sum q_i\mathbf r_i'$.
rubric: Takes $\mathbf d$ outside the sum to obtain the term $Q\mathbf d$.
rubric: Solves $\mathbf p - Q\mathbf d = \mathbf{0}$ for $\mathbf d = \mathbf p/Q$ and notes it needs $Q \neq 0$.
rubric: States that for $Q = 0$ the dipole moment does not depend on the origin.

@practice num
prompt: Four point charges lie in the $xy$-plane: $+q$ at $(\pm a, 0, 0)$ and $-q$ at $(0, \pm a, 0)$, with $q = 2.0$ nC and $a = 1.0$ cm. Both $Q$ and $\mathbf p$ vanish about the origin. Using the leading multipole term, find the potential at the point $(0.50\ \text{m}, 0, 0)$. Take $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: 0.0432
check: 8.99e9*2.0e-9*(1/(0.5 - 0.01) + 1/(0.5 + 0.01) - 2/sqrt(0.5**2 + 0.01**2))
unit: V
tol: 1%
hint: Since $Q = 0$ and $\mathbf p = \mathbf{0}$, the quadrupole term leads: $V \approx \dfrac{k}{r^3}\sum_i q_i r_i^2 P_2(\cos\alpha_i)$.
hint: Here $\alpha_i$ is the angle between the $x$-axis (the direction to the field point) and each charge's position. Use $P_2(\pm1) = 1$ and $P_2(0) = -\tfrac12$.
hint: The sum comes to a pure multiple of $qa^2$. Add the two $x$-axis charges and the two $y$-axis charges separately.
solution: The two charges on the $x$-axis have $\cos\alpha = \pm1$ and $P_2 = 1$, contributing $2qa^2$. The charges $-q$ at $(0, \pm a, 0)$ have $\cos\alpha = 0$ and $P_2 = -\tfrac12$, contributing $2(-q)a^2(-\tfrac12) = qa^2$. The sum is $3qa^2$, so $V \approx 3kqa^2/r^3 = 3(8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-9})(10^{-4})/(0.50)^3 \approx 0.0432$ V.

@quiz tuple
prompt: Point charges $+2$ C at $(1, 0, 0)$ m, $-3$ C at $(0, 2, 0)$ m and $+1$ C at $(0, 0, -1)$ m make up a distribution with $Q = 0$. Find its dipole moment $\mathbf p$ in C m as an ordered triple.
answer: (2, -6, -1)
check: [sum(q*r[i] for q, r in [(2, (1, 0, 0)), (-3, (0, 2, 0)), (1, (0, 0, -1))]) for i in range(3)]
hint: Use $\mathbf p = \sum_i q_i\mathbf r_i$ one component at a time.
