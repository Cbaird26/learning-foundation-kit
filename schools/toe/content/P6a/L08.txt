@article
Put an insulator in an electric field and its atoms and molecules distort: positive and negative charge shift slightly in opposite directions, so each tiny volume acquires a dipole moment. Averaged over many molecules, this is the **polarization** $\mathbf P$, the dipole moment per unit volume (units C/m²). The multipole ideas of last week do the rest: a polarized material is a continuous sea of tiny dipoles.

### Bound charge

Adding up the dipole potentials of all the little dipoles gives exactly the potential of two **bound charge** distributions,

$$\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n} \ \ (\text{surface, } \hat{\mathbf n} \text{ outward}), \qquad \rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P \ \ (\text{volume}).$$

For a uniformly polarized object $\rho_b = 0$ and all the bound charge sits on the surface. The total bound charge of a finite object is always zero, because $\int\rho_b\,d\tau = -\oint\mathbf P\cdot d\mathbf a$ cancels $\oint\sigma_b\,da$.

### The displacement field

The total charge density is $\rho = \rho_f + \rho_b$, where $\rho_f$ is the free charge you place there. Gauss's law $\epsilon_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf E = \rho_f - \nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ rearranges to

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_f, \qquad \mathbf D = \epsilon_0\mathbf E + \mathbf P.$$

Electrostatics still has $\nabla\times\mathbf E = 0$, but $\nabla\times\mathbf D = \nabla\times\mathbf P$ need not vanish. So $\mathbf D$ follows from $\rho_f$ alone only when symmetry (planar, cylindrical, spherical) lets you apply the integral form of Gauss's law. In a **linear isotropic dielectric**, $\mathbf P = \epsilon_0\chi_e\mathbf E$ and

$$\mathbf D = \epsilon\mathbf E, \qquad \epsilon = \epsilon_0\epsilon_r, \qquad \epsilon_r = 1 + \chi_e.$$

### Interface conditions

At an interface with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing from medium 1 into medium 2 and free surface charge $\sigma_f$,

$$D_{2\perp} - D_{1\perp} = \sigma_f, \qquad \mathbf E_{2\parallel} = \mathbf E_{1\parallel}.$$

Equivalently, $V$ is continuous and $\epsilon_2\,\partial_n V_2 - \epsilon_1\,\partial_n V_1 = -\sigma_f$. With no free surface charge, $D_\perp$ and $E_\parallel$ are continuous, so field lines bend at the interface: $\tan\theta_2/\tan\theta_1 = \epsilon_2/\epsilon_1$, with angles measured from the normal.

### The far field of a dielectric sphere

A dielectric sphere of radius $R$ in an otherwise uniform field $E_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ has $V = -Ar\cos\theta$ inside and $V = -E_0r\cos\theta + B\cos\theta/r^2$ outside. Matching $V$ and $\epsilon\,\partial_rV$ at $r = R$ gives

$$A = \frac{3E_0}{\epsilon_r + 2}, \qquad B = \frac{\epsilon_r - 1}{\epsilon_r + 2}\,R^3E_0.$$

The $B$ term is a dipole potential with $p = 4\pi\epsilon_0B$. Far away the sphere therefore acts like a point dipole $\mathbf p = 4\pi\epsilon_0R^3\,\dfrac{\epsilon_r-1}{\epsilon_r+2}\,\mathbf E_0$, and that dipole is the leading far-field correction to the applied field.

@example
title: A uniformly polarized sphere
problem: A sphere of radius $R = 2.0$ cm is uniformly polarized, $\mathbf P = P\hat{\mathbf z}$ with $P = 5.0\times10^{-6}$ C/m². Find its bound charge, its dipole moment and the field at its center. Take $\epsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m.
step: Uniform $\mathbf P$ means $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P = 0$. All the bound charge is on the surface, $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf r} = P\cos\theta$: positive on the upper hemisphere, negative on the lower.
step: The total is $\oint\sigma_b\,da = PR^2\cdot2\pi\int_0^\pi\cos\theta\sin\theta\,d\theta = 0$, as it must be for a finite object.
step: The dipole moment is $\mathbf p = \int\mathbf P\,d\tau = \tfrac43\pi R^3\mathbf P$. Numerically $p = (5.0\times10^{-6})\tfrac43\pi(0.020)^3 \approx 1.7\times10^{-10}$ C m. Outside the sphere the field is exactly that of a point dipole of this moment at the center.
step: Inside, the field is uniform: $\mathbf E = -\mathbf P/(3\epsilon_0)$, so $E = -5.0\times10^{-6}/(3\times8.854\times10^{-12}) \approx -1.9\times10^{5}$ V/m along $\hat{\mathbf z}$.
step: The field inside points opposite to $\mathbf P$, because the surface charge on the upper half is positive and on the lower half negative.

@example
title: Linear dielectric between charged plates
problem: Large parallel plates carry free surface charge $\pm\sigma_f$ with $\sigma_f = 1.0\times10^{-6}$ C/m². The gap is filled with a linear dielectric of $\epsilon_r = 4.0$. Find $D$, $E$, $P$ and the bound surface charge. Take $\epsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}$ F/m.
step: A Gauss pillbox around one plate, with its other face outside the capacitor where $\mathbf D = \mathbf{0}$, gives $D = \sigma_f = 1.0\times10^{-6}$ C/m² in the gap.
step: In the linear dielectric $E = D/\epsilon = \sigma_f/(\epsilon_r\epsilon_0) = 1.0\times10^{-6}/(4.0\times8.854\times10^{-12}) \approx 2.8\times10^{4}$ V/m.
step: The polarization is $P = D - \epsilon_0E = \sigma_f(1 - 1/\epsilon_r) = 7.5\times10^{-7}$ C/m².
step: Bound surface charge is $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ outward from the dielectric. The face touching the positive plate has $\sigma_b = -P$ and the other face has $+P$.
step: Next to the positive plate the net charge density is $\sigma_f - P = \sigma_f/\epsilon_r = 2.5\times10^{-7}$ C/m². Check: $E = (\sigma_f - P)/\epsilon_0 = 2.8\times10^{4}$ V/m, as before.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbf D$ as if it were just $\epsilon_0\mathbf E$ with free charge as its only source; $\nabla\times\mathbf D = \nabla\times\mathbf P$ is generally nonzero, so symmetry is needed to find $\mathbf D$ from $\rho_f$ alone.
- Getting the sign of $\sigma_b$ wrong by using an inward normal; $\hat{\mathbf n}$ in $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ points outward from the dielectric.
- Mixing up the continuous quantities: with no free surface charge it is $D_\perp$ and $E_\parallel$ that are continuous, not $E_\perp$ and $D_\parallel$.

answer: 4.43e-7

@practice mc
prompt: A flat interface separates two linear dielectrics of different permittivity and carries no free charge. Which statement about the fields at the interface is correct?
choice: The normal component of $\mathbf E$ and the tangential component of $\mathbf D$ are continuous.
choice*: The normal component of $\mathbf D$ and the tangential component of $\mathbf E$ are continuous.
choice: Both $\mathbf E$ and $\mathbf D$ are continuous in every component.
choice: Only the potential is continuous; no component of $\mathbf E$ is.
hint: A pillbox applied to $\nabla\cdot\mathbf D = \rho_f$ and a thin loop applied to $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf{0}$ give the two conditions.
solution: The pillbox gives $D_{2\perp} - D_{1\perp} = \sigma_f = 0$, and the thin loop gives continuous $\mathbf E_\parallel$. Because $\epsilon_1 \neq \epsilon_2$, the components $E_\perp$ and $\mathbf D_\parallel$ jump. The potential is continuous, but that also forces $\mathbf E_\parallel$ to be continuous.

@practice num
prompt: A field line in a dielectric with $\epsilon_r = 2.0$ meets a flat, uncharged interface with a dielectric of $\epsilon_r = 6.0$. In the first medium the field makes an angle of $30^\circ$ with the normal. At what angle to the normal does the field point in the second medium?
answer: 60
check: root(lambda t: 2*cos(pi/6)*sin(t) - 6*sin(pi/6)*cos(t), 0.01, 1.5)*180/pi
unit: degrees
tol: 1%
hint: With no free charge, $E_\parallel$ and $D_\perp$ are continuous: $E_1\sin\theta_1 = E_2\sin\theta_2$ and $\epsilon_1E_1\cos\theta_1 = \epsilon_2E_2\cos\theta_2$.
hint: Divide one equation by the other to eliminate $E_1$ and $E_2$.

@practice self
prompt: A finite dielectric object with no free charge has polarization $\mathbf P(\mathbf r)$ inside and none outside. Show that its total bound charge is zero. What does this tell you about the leading term in its far-field potential?
hint: Write the total bound charge as a volume integral of $\rho_b$ plus a surface integral of $\sigma_b$.
hint: Apply the divergence theorem to the volume integral.
solution: The total bound charge is $Q_b = \int\rho_b\,d\tau + \oint\sigma_b\,da = -\int\nabla\cdot\mathbf P\,d\tau + \oint\mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,da$. The divergence theorem turns the first term into $-\oint\mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}\,da$, which cancels the second, so $Q_b = 0$. The monopole term of the far field therefore vanishes and the dipole term, with $\mathbf p = \int\mathbf P\,d\tau$, is the leading term unless it vanishes too.
rubric: Writes $\rho_b = -\nabla\cdot\mathbf P$ and $\sigma_b = \mathbf P\cdot\hat{\mathbf n}$ with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ outward.
rubric: Uses the divergence theorem to show the volume and surface contributions cancel.
rubric: Concludes that the monopole term is zero and that the dipole term, if nonzero, leads.

Far away, the leading correction to the applied field is the field of the induced point dipole. Find the $z$-component of this dipole's field on the $z$-axis at distance $z = 10R$ from the center.
answer: 1.14
check: 1.0e3*2*lin([[1, 1], [5, -2]], [1, 1])[1]/10**3
unit: V/m
tol: 1%
hint: Inside $V = -Ar\cos\theta$ and outside $V = -E_0r\cos\theta + B\cos\theta/r^2$. Match $V$ and $\epsilon\,\partial_rV$ at $r = R$ to find $B$.
hint: The dipole potential $B\cos\theta/r^2$ has radial field $2B\cos\theta/r^3$, which on the axis is $2B/z^3$.

@quiz expr
prompt: A parallel-plate capacitor has plate area $A$ and separation $d$. A slab of dielectric constant $\kappa = \epsilon_r$ and thickness $t < d$ is inserted parallel to the plates, and the rest of the gap is vacuum. Neglecting edge effects, find $C/\epsilon_0$ as an expression in $A$, $d$, $t$ and `kappa`.
vars: A, d, t, kappa
lead: $C/\epsilon_0 =$
answer: A/(d - t + t/kappa)
check: lambda A, d, t, kappa: A*lin([[d - t, t], [1, -kappa]], [1, 0])[0]
domain: A: 0.5..2; d: 2..3; t: 0.5..1.5; kappa: 1.5..4
hint: Let $E_a$ be the field in the vacuum part and $E_k$ the field in the slab. $D_\perp$ is continuous across the slab surface, so $E_a = \kappa E_k$.
hint: The potential difference is $V = E_a(d - t) + E_kt$, and $C = Q/V$ with $Q = \epsilon_0E_aA$.
solution: Continuity of $D_\perp$ gives $E_a = \kappa E_k$. Then $V = E_a(d - t) + E_kt = E_a(d - t + t/\kappa)$. The free charge is $Q = \epsilon_0E_aA$, so $C = Q/V = \dfrac{\epsilon_0A}{d - t + t/\kappa}$.
