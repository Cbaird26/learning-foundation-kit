@lesson
title: The magnetic vector potential
summary: Use $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$ in the Coulomb gauge to compute vector potentials, and compare a direct field integral with a potential-based calculation of flux.
objective: Explain why $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ allows $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$, and how the gauge freedom $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A + \nabla\lambda$ works.
objective: Use $\nabla^2\mathbf A = -\mu_0\mathbf J$ in the Coulomb gauge and the integral formula for $\mathbf A$.
objective: Compute flux as $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ and find the field of a magnetic dipole from its vector potential.
reading: David Tong, Electromagnetism lecture notes: in the Magnetostatics chapter, look up the vector potential, gauge transformations and the magnetic dipole.

@article
For the electric field, $\nabla\times\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$ lets you write $\mathbf E = -\nabla V$. Magnetostatics has the other identity: $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$. Every divergence-free field is a curl, so

$$\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A,$$

where $\mathbf A$ is the **vector potential** (units T m). This form satisfies $\nabla\cdot\mathbf B = 0$ automatically.

### Gauge freedom

The vector potential is not unique. Because $\nabla\times(\nabla\lambda) = \mathbf 0$, the **gauge transformation** $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A + \nabla\lambda$ leaves $\mathbf B$ unchanged for any scalar function $\lambda$. Use this freedom to demand $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$, the **Coulomb gauge**. The identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf A) = \nabla(\nabla\cdot\mathbf A) - \nabla^2\mathbf A$ then turns $\nabla\times\mathbf B = \mu_0\mathbf J$ into

$$\nabla^2\mathbf A = -\mu_0\mathbf J.$$

Each Cartesian component is a Poisson equation, exactly like $\nabla^2V = -\rho/\epsilon_0$. For currents confined to a finite region the solution is

$$\mathbf A(\mathbf r) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int\frac{\mathbf J(\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|}\,d\tau', \qquad \mathbf A = \frac{\mu_0I}{4\pi}\oint\frac{d\mathbf l'}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|}\ \ (\text{thin wire}),$$

so $\mathbf A$ points along the current.

### Flux from the potential

Stokes' theorem turns flux into a line integral:

$$\Phi = \int\mathbf B\cdot d\mathbf a = \oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l.$$

You can find a flux either by integrating $\mathbf B$ over a surface or by integrating $\mathbf A$ around its edge, and the answer cannot depend on the gauge, because $\oint\nabla\lambda\cdot d\mathbf l = 0$. For a long solenoid of radius $R$ with interior field $B$, symmetry gives $\mathbf A = A(s)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ with

$$A = \frac{Bs}{2}\ \ (s<R), \qquad A = \frac{BR^2}{2s}\ \ (s>R).$$

Notice $\mathbf A\neq\mathbf 0$ outside, where $\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$: the potential carries the flux trapped inside.

### The magnetic dipole

Far from a small current loop the monopole term of $\mathbf A$ vanishes (because $\oint d\mathbf l' = \mathbf 0$) and the dipole term leads:

$$\mathbf A = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{\mathbf m\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}, \qquad \mathbf B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r^3}\left[3(\mathbf m\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\hat{\mathbf r} - \mathbf m\right],$$

where $\mathbf m = I\mathbf a$ is the magnetic dipole moment and $\mathbf a$ is the loop's area vector (right-hand rule). The field has the same form as an electric dipole's, with $\mathbf p\to\mathbf m$ and $1/\epsilon_0\to\mu_0$.

### Boundary conditions

$\mathbf A$ is continuous across a surface current $\mathbf K$, but its normal derivative jumps: $\partial_n\mathbf A_2 - \partial_n\mathbf A_1 = -\mu_0\mathbf K$, with $\hat{\mathbf n}$ pointing from side 1 to side 2.

@example
title: Flux through a loop two ways
problem: A long solenoid of radius $R = 2.0$ cm has a uniform field $B = 0.050$ T inside and none outside. Find the flux through a coaxial circular loop of radius $s = 5.0$ cm, first by integrating $\mathbf B$ and then by integrating $\mathbf A$ around the loop.
step: Direct field integral: $\mathbf B$ is nonzero only for $s<R$, so $\Phi = B\pi R^2 = 0.050\,\pi\,(0.020)^2 = 6.28\times10^{-5}$ Wb.
step: Find $\mathbf A$ by symmetry. It has the form $A(s)\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, which is divergence-free. Stokes' theorem on a circle of radius $s<R$ gives $2\pi sA = B\pi s^2$, so $A = Bs/2$. For $s>R$ it gives $2\pi sA = B\pi R^2$, so $A = BR^2/(2s)$.
step: Evaluate at the loop: $A = \dfrac{0.050\,(0.020)^2}{2(0.050)} = 2.0\times10^{-4}$ T m.
step: Potential calculation: $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l = 2\pi sA = 2\pi(0.050)(2.0\times10^{-4}) = 6.28\times10^{-5}$ Wb, the same as the direct integral.
step: Check the curl: $B_z = \frac1s\frac{d(sA)}{ds}$ equals $B$ for $s<R$ and $0$ for $s>R$. The loop sits where $\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$, yet $\mathbf A \neq \mathbf 0$ there and the line integral still counts the enclosed flux.

@example
title: Dipole field from the vector potential
problem: A small loop has magnetic moment $\mathbf m = m\hat{\mathbf z}$ with $m = 0.50$ A m². Starting from $\mathbf A = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{\mathbf m\times\hat{\mathbf r}}{r^2}$, find $\mathbf B$ on the $z$-axis at $r = 0.20$ m.
step: Since $\hat{\mathbf z}\times\hat{\mathbf r} = \sin\theta\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$, the potential is $A_\phi = \dfrac{\mu_0m\sin\theta}{4\pi r^2}$ and the other components vanish.
step: For a field with only $A_\phi$, the spherical curl gives $B_r = \dfrac{1}{r\sin\theta}\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\theta}(\sin\theta A_\phi)$ and $B_\theta = -\dfrac1r\dfrac{\partial}{\partial r}(rA_\phi)$.
step: Differentiate: $B_r = \dfrac{\mu_0m}{4\pi r^3}\,2\cos\theta$ and $B_\theta = \dfrac{\mu_0m}{4\pi r^3}\sin\theta$.
step: On the positive $z$-axis $\theta = 0$, so $B_\theta = 0$ and $B_r = \dfrac{\mu_0m}{2\pi r^3} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(0.50)}{(0.20)^3} = 1.25\times10^{-5}$ T along $+z$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbf A$ as unique. Any $\nabla\lambda$ can be added; only $\nabla\times\mathbf A$ and closed-loop integrals $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ are gauge independent.
- Concluding that $\mathbf A = \mathbf 0$ wherever $\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$. Outside a solenoid $\mathbf B = \mathbf 0$, yet $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ equals the trapped flux.
- Using $\nabla^2\mathbf A = -\mu_0\mathbf J$ without checking $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$; the equation holds only in the Coulomb gauge.

@practice num
prompt: A vector potential is $\mathbf A = \alpha\,(-y,\ x,\ 0)$ with $\alpha = 0.15$ T. Find $B_z = \partial_xA_y - \partial_yA_x$.
answer: 0.3
check: nderiv(lambda x: 0.15*x, 0.7) - nderiv(lambda y: -0.15*y, 0.4)
unit: T
tol: 1%
hint: Here $A_x = -\alpha y$ and $A_y = \alpha x$.
hint: Differentiate each component with respect to the variable in its formula and subtract.
solution: $\partial_xA_y = \alpha$ and $\partial_yA_x = -\alpha$, so $B_z = \alpha - (-\alpha) = 2\alpha = 0.30$ T. The field is uniform, and $\nabla\cdot\mathbf A = 0$.

@practice mc
prompt: Which change to the vector potential leaves $\mathbf B = \nabla\times\mathbf A$ unchanged for every scalar function $\lambda(\mathbf r)$?
choice: $\mathbf A\to\lambda\mathbf A$
choice*: $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A + \nabla\lambda$
choice: $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A + \lambda\hat{\mathbf z}$
choice: $\mathbf A\to\mathbf A + \lambda\mathbf r$
hint: The curl of the added term must vanish for every $\lambda$.
solution: The curl of a gradient is always zero, so $\nabla\lambda$ can be added freely. The terms $\lambda\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\lambda\mathbf r$ have curls $\nabla\lambda\times\hat{\mathbf z}$ and $\nabla\lambda\times\mathbf r$, which are not zero in general, and rescaling $\mathbf A$ by $\lambda$ changes $\mathbf B$.

@practice num
prompt: A long straight wire along the $z$-axis carrying $I = 12$ A has vector potential $\mathbf A = -\dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi}\ln\!\left(\dfrac{s}{s_0}\right)\hat{\mathbf z}$, where $s_0$ is a constant. Use $B_\phi = -\partial A_z/\partial s$ to find $B$ at $s = 3.0$ cm. Take $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T m/A.
answer: 8.0e-5
check: -nderiv(lambda s: -2e-7*12*log(s/0.01), 0.03)
unit: T
tol: 1%
hint: With only $A_z(s)$ nonzero, the curl has just a $\phi$-component, $B_\phi = -dA_z/ds$.
hint: The derivative of $\ln(s/s_0)$ is $1/s$, and the constant $s_0$ drops out.
solution: $B_\phi = -\dfrac{dA_z}{ds} = \dfrac{\mu_0I}{2\pi s} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(12)}{0.030} = 8.0\times10^{-5}$ T. Changing $s_0$ adds a constant to $\mathbf A$, which is a gauge change and does not affect $\mathbf B$.

On its axis at $z = 4R$, compare the exact Biot–Savart field $B_z$ with the dipole estimate $B_z = \mu_0m/(2\pi z^3)$, where $m = I\pi R^2$, which follows from the dipole vector potential. answer: 0.9131

@practice self
prompt: A uniform field $\mathbf B = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$ fills all space. Find the flux through a flat circular loop of radius $a$ in the $xy$-plane three ways: (a) by integrating $\mathbf B$ over the disk; (b) with $\mathbf A = \tfrac12\mathbf B\times\mathbf r$, by computing $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$; (c) with $\mathbf A' = (0,\ B_0x,\ 0)$, by computing $\oint\mathbf A'\cdot d\mathbf l$. Confirm that both potentials give $\mathbf B$ and explain why the three answers agree.
hint: On the loop use $x = a\cos\phi$, $y = a\sin\phi$, $d\mathbf l = a\,d\phi\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$.
hint: Compare $\mathbf A'$ with $\mathbf A$: their difference is the gradient of a simple function of $x$ and $y$.
solution: (a) $\Phi = B_0\pi a^2$. (b) $\mathbf A = \tfrac12B_0(-y, x, 0) = \tfrac12B_0s\,\hat{\boldsymbol\phi}$ has curl $B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$. On the loop $\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l = \tfrac12B_0a\cdot a\,d\phi$, so $\oint = \pi a^2B_0$. (c) $\nabla\times\mathbf A' = \partial_x(B_0x)\,\hat{\mathbf z} = B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$. On the loop $dy = a\cos\phi\,d\phi$, so $\oint\mathbf A'\cdot d\mathbf l = \oint B_0x\,dy = B_0a^2\int_0^{2\pi}\cos^2\phi\,d\phi = \pi a^2B_0$. The results agree because $\mathbf A' - \mathbf A = \nabla(B_0xy/2)$ is a gauge change, and the closed-loop integral of a gradient is zero.
rubric: Gets the direct flux $B_0\pi a^2$ and verifies that each potential has curl $B_0\hat{\mathbf z}$.
rubric: Evaluates $\oint\mathbf A\cdot d\mathbf l$ in the symmetric gauge and obtains $\pi a^2B_0$.
rubric: Evaluates $\oint\mathbf A'\cdot d\mathbf l$ using $x = a\cos\phi$ and $dy = a\cos\phi\,d\phi$ and obtains $\pi a^2B_0$.
rubric: Explains the agreement: the potentials differ by $\nabla\lambda$ with $\lambda = B_0xy/2$, whose closed-loop integral vanishes.
