@article
On a flat sheet the straightest curves are straight lines: they do not accelerate. On a curved surface we want the analogue, a curve whose acceleration as measured by someone living on the surface is zero. These curves are **geodesics**. On the Earth they are the great-circle routes that long flights follow.

### The geodesic equation

The acceleration of a curve $x^i(t)$ is the covariant derivative of its velocity along itself:

$$\frac{D\dot x^k}{dt}=\ddot x^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot x^i\dot x^j.$$

A **geodesic** is a curve for which this vanishes:

$$\ddot x^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot x^i\dot x^j=0.$$

This is a second-order system, so a starting point and a starting velocity determine exactly one geodesic. Metric compatibility gives $\frac{d}{dt}\big(g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\big)=2g_{ij}\dot x^i\,\frac{D\dot x^j}{dt}=0$, so the speed is constant and $t$ is proportional to arc length (an **affine parameter**).

The extra terms are not forces. In the polar plane the equations are $\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=0$ and $\ddot\theta+\frac{2}{r}\dot r\dot\theta=0$. The terms $r\dot\theta^2$ and $\frac2r\dot r\dot\theta$ appear because $\ddot r$ and $\ddot\theta$ are accelerations of coordinates, not of the point. Straight lines satisfy these equations.

### From a variational principle

Geodesics are also the critical paths of length, or equivalently of the energy $\int\mathcal L\,dt$ with $\mathcal L=\tfrac12 g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$. The Euler-Lagrange equations read

$$\frac{d}{dt}\big(g_{kj}\dot x^j\big)-\tfrac12\,\partial_kg_{ij}\,\dot x^i\dot x^j=0.$$

Expand the first term, multiply by $g^{mk}$, and the geodesic equation appears with exactly the Christoffel symbols of Week 7. In practice this is the quickest derivation: write $\mathcal L$, apply Euler-Lagrange to each coordinate, and read $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ off as the coefficients of $\dot x^i\dot x^j$. Note that the mixed product $\dot x^i\dot x^j$ with $i\neq j$ occurs twice in the sum, so its coefficient is $2\Gamma^k_{ij}$.

### Conserved quantities

If the metric does not depend on a coordinate $x^a$, then $\partial\mathcal L/\partial x^a=0$ and

$$p_a=g_{aj}\,\dot x^j$$

is constant along every geodesic. On the unit sphere $\phi$ is absent, so $p_\phi=\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi$ is conserved. Solving shows that the geodesics are the **great circles**: the intersections of the sphere with planes through its centre.

The equator is a geodesic. With $\theta=\pi/2$ and $\dot\theta=0$, the equation $\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2$ gives $\ddot\theta=0$. A latitude circle with $\theta_0\neq\pi/2$ is not a geodesic: there $\ddot\theta=\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,\dot\phi^2\neq0$, so a particle that starts out tangent to the circle drifts toward the equator.

On the upper half-plane, $ds^2=(dx^2+dy^2)/y^2$, the coordinate $x$ is absent, so $\dot x/y^2$ is conserved. The geodesics are vertical lines and semicircles centred on the $x$-axis.

@example
title: Geodesic equations of the unit sphere
problem: Derive the geodesic equations of the unit sphere from the Lagrangian $\mathcal L=\tfrac12\big(\dot\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi^2\big)$, and test the equator and a circle of latitude.
step: For $\theta$: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\dot\theta}=\ddot\theta$ and $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\theta}=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2$. So $\ddot\theta-\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=0$.
step: For $\phi$: $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big(\sin^2\theta\,\dot\phi\big)=0$. Expanding gives $\sin^2\theta\,\ddot\phi+2\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi=0$, that is $\ddot\phi+2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\dot\phi=0$.
step: Compare with $\ddot x^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j=0$: this gives $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $2\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=2\cot\theta$, matching Week 7. The factor $2$ comes from the two orders $\theta\phi$ and $\phi\theta$.
step: Test the equator $\theta=\pi/2$, $\phi=\omega t$: the first equation gives $0-\sin\tfrac\pi2\cos\tfrac\pi2\,\omega^2=0$ and the second gives $0=0$. It is a geodesic.
step: Test a latitude $\theta=\theta_0\neq\pi/2$, $\phi=\omega t$: the first equation would need $\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,\omega^2=0$, which fails. It is not a geodesic.

@example
title: A vertical geodesic in the upper half-plane
problem: The geodesic equations of $ds^2=(dx^2+dy^2)/y^2$ are $\ddot x-\tfrac2y\dot x\dot y=0$ and $\ddot y+\tfrac1y\big(\dot x^2-\dot y^2\big)=0$. Show that a vertical line $x=x_0$ is a geodesic, and find its unit-speed parametrization.
step: On the line $x=x_0$ we have $\dot x=\ddot x=0$, so the first equation holds automatically.
step: The second equation becomes $\ddot y=\dot y^2/y$. Put $w=\ln y$. Then $\dot w=\dot y/y$ and $\ddot w=\ddot y/y-\dot y^2/y^2=0$.
step: So $w=at+b$, that is $y=y_0\,e^{at}$.
step: The speed is $\sqrt{g_{yy}}\,|\dot y|=|\dot y|/y=|a|$. Unit speed means $a=\pm1$, so $y=y_0e^{\pm t}$.
step: Interpretation: the distance between heights $y_1$ and $y_2$ along the line is $|\ln(y_2/y_1)|$. The boundary $y=0$ is reached only as $|t|\to\infty$ (as $t\to\mp\infty$ for $y=y_0e^{\pm t}$), so it is infinitely far away.

@mistakes
- Dropping the factor $2$ on mixed terms. The sum $\Gamma^k_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$ contains both $\Gamma^k_{\theta\phi}\dot\theta\dot\phi$ and $\Gamma^k_{\phi\theta}\dot\phi\dot\theta$.
- Believing every circle on a sphere is a geodesic. Only great circles are; a latitude circle other than the equator is not.
- Equating geodesic with shortest path. Geodesics are locally shortest, but the long way around a great circle is also a geodesic.
- Using a non-affine parameter. The geodesic equation keeps its form only under $t\to ct+d$.

prompt: A geodesic of the unit sphere passes through $\theta=\pi/3$ at $t=0$ with $\dot\theta=0$ and $\dot\phi=2$. Find $\ddot\theta(0)$.
answer: sqrt(3)
check: nderiv(lambda t: arccos(cos(pi/3)*cos(sin(pi/3)*2*t)), 0, 2)
hint: Use the $\theta$-equation $\ddot\theta+\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}\dot\phi^2=0$ (the terms with $\dot\theta$ vanish at $t=0$).
hint: $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so $\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2$.
solution: $\ddot\theta=\sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\dfrac12\cdot4=\sqrt3$. It is positive, so the geodesic leaves the latitude circle toward larger $\theta$, that is toward the equator. As a check, the geodesic is the great circle through the point with speed $\sin\theta\,\dot\phi=\sqrt3$ along $\partial_\phi$, and $\cos\theta(t)=\tfrac12\cos(\sqrt3\,t)$ gives the same $\ddot\theta$.

@practice self
prompt: For the polar plane, $ds^2=dr^2+r^2d\theta^2$, take $\mathcal L=\tfrac12\big(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2\big)$. (a) Derive the geodesic equations from the Euler-Lagrange equations. (b) Show that $\ell=r^2\dot\theta$ is constant along a geodesic. (c) For a geodesic with unit speed, show that $r\ge|\ell|$ along it and interpret this geometrically.
hint: For (b), notice that $\theta$ does not appear in $\mathcal L$.
hint: For (c), write the unit-speed condition as $\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2=1$ and eliminate $\dot\theta$ with $\ell$.
solution: (a) The $r$ equation is $\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2=0$. The $\theta$ equation is $\dfrac{d}{dt}\big(r^2\dot\theta\big)=0$, which expands to $\ddot\theta+\dfrac2r\dot r\dot\theta=0$. These match $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=1/r$. (b) Since $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\theta=0$, $\ell=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\theta=r^2\dot\theta$ is conserved. (c) With $\dot\theta=\ell/r^2$, the unit-speed condition gives $\dot r^2=1-\ell^2/r^2\ge0$, so $r\ge|\ell|$, with equality when $\dot r=0$. Geometrically the geodesic is a straight line whose closest approach to the origin is $|\ell|$, the perpendicular distance from the origin; $\ell$ is speed times that distance.
rubric: Derives both Euler-Lagrange equations correctly, including the $\theta$ equation in the form $\frac{d}{dt}(r^2\dot\theta)=0$.
rubric: Explains that $\theta$ is a cyclic coordinate, so $r^2\dot\theta$ is conserved.
rubric: Uses constant speed and $\ell$ to get $\dot r^2=1-\ell^2/r^2$, concludes $r\ge|\ell|$, and interprets $|\ell|$ as the distance of closest approach of a straight line.

@quiz num
prompt: Find the length of the shortest path on the unit sphere between the points with $(\theta,\phi)=(\pi/3,\,0)$ and $(\theta,\phi)=(\pi/3,\,\pi)$.
answer: 2pi/3
check: (lambda n: math.acos(np.dot(n(pi/3, 0), n(pi/3, pi))))(lambda th, ph: np.array([sin(th)*cos(ph), sin(th)*sin(ph), cos(th)]))
hint: The shortest path lies on a great circle. If $\mathbf n_1$ and $\mathbf n_2$ are the position vectors of the endpoints, the arc length is the angle $\alpha$ with $\cos\alpha=\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2$.
hint: $\mathbf n_1=\big(\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0,\tfrac12\big)$ and $\mathbf n_2=\big(-\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2},0,\tfrac12\big)$. Compute the dot product.
solution: $\mathbf n_1\cdot\mathbf n_2=-\tfrac34+\tfrac14=-\tfrac12$, so $\alpha=\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$. The path goes over the north pole: each point is $\tfrac\pi3$ from the pole. The route along the latitude circle would have length $\pi\sin\tfrac\pi3\approx2.72$, longer than $\tfrac{2\pi}3\approx2.09$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about geodesics are true?
choice*: Along a geodesic parametrized by an affine parameter, the speed $\sqrt{g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}$ is constant.
choice*: A starting point and a starting velocity determine a unique geodesic (for a smooth metric).
choice: Every geodesic between two points of a sphere is the shortest path between them.
choice: The geodesic equation keeps the same form under every smooth change of parameter $t\to s(t)$.
choice*: If the metric does not depend on the coordinate $x^a$, then $g_{aj}\dot x^j$ is constant along geodesics.
hint: Think of the great circle through two points, and of what happens to $\ddot x$ when $t\to t^2$.
solution: Constant speed follows from metric compatibility, uniqueness from the second-order ODE, and the conserved momentum from the Euler-Lagrange equation for a missing coordinate. The long arc of a great circle is a geodesic but not the shortest path. Under $t\to s(t)$ the equation picks up a term proportional to $\ddot s\,\dot x^k$, so only affine changes $s=ct+d$ preserve its form.
