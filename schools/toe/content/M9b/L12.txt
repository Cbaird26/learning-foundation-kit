@article
A flat sheet of paper can be rolled into a cylinder or a cone without stretching or tearing. Lengths, angles and geodesics drawn on the sheet do not change, yet the shape in space clearly does. An orange peel is different: you cannot flatten it without tearing or stretching it. This week explains the difference by separating two kinds of geometry.

**Intrinsic** geometry is what an ant living on the surface could measure using only the metric $g_{ij}$: distances, angles, geodesics and, as you saw last week, the curvature $K$. **Extrinsic** geometry depends on how the surface sits in space.

### The second fundamental form

For a surface $\mathbf r(u,v)$ with unit normal $\mathbf n=\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v/|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|$, the first fundamental form is the metric $g_{ij}=\mathbf r_i\cdot\mathbf r_j$, and the **second fundamental form** is

$$h_{ij}=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_{ij},\qquad \mathbf r_{ij}=\partial_i\partial_j\mathbf r.$$

It records how the surface leaves its tangent plane: a displacement $\delta x^i$ lifts the surface a height $\tfrac12h_{ij}\delta x^i\delta x^j$ off the tangent plane. The eigenvalues $\kappa_1,\kappa_2$ of the **shape operator** $S=g^{-1}h$ are the **principal curvatures**, the extreme curvatures of the cross-sections cut by planes containing $\mathbf n$. Then

$$K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=\frac{\det h}{\det g},\qquad H=\tfrac12(\kappa_1+\kappa_2)=\tfrac12\operatorname{tr}S.$$

Flipping $\mathbf n$ flips the signs of $h$, of the $\kappa_i$ and of $H$, but not of $K$.

| Surface | $\kappa_1$ | $\kappa_2$ | $K$ | $H$ (inward $\mathbf n$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Plane | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ | $0$ |
| Cylinder, radius $R$ | $1/R$ | $0$ | $0$ | $1/(2R)$ |
| Sphere, radius $a$ | $1/a$ | $1/a$ | $1/a^2$ | $1/a$ |

### The remarkable theorem

$K=\kappa_1\kappa_2$ is built from $h$, which is extrinsic. Yet it equals the intrinsic curvature computed from $g_{ij}$ alone, the one from last week with $R=2K$. This is the *Theorema Egregium*. Bending without stretching preserves $g$ and hence $K$, while $H$ can change: the cylinder has $H\neq0$ but is intrinsically flat.

### Flat coordinates

**Flat coordinates** are coordinates in which the metric is $dX^2+dY^2$. They exist locally exactly when $K=0$. On the cylinder, $ds^2=dz^2+R^2d\phi^2$ becomes $dz^2+dX^2$ with $X=R\phi$. On the cone $ds^2=d\rho^2+\rho^2\sin^2\alpha\,d\phi^2$, the choice $\psi=\phi\sin\alpha$ gives the polar form $d\rho^2+\rho^2d\psi^2$. On the sphere $K\neq0$ and no flat coordinates exist, so every flat map of the Earth distorts something. Near a point the best one can do is flat coordinates up to errors of order $K\,r^2$. For instance, a circle of geodesic radius $r$ on the unit sphere has circumference $2\pi\sin r=2\pi(r-r^3/6+\cdots)$, not $2\pi r$.

### Angle excess

A geodesic triangle in a region of constant curvature $K$ and area $A$ has interior angles with sum

$$\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi+KA.$$

The octant of the unit sphere has three right angles and area $\pi/2$, and $\tfrac{3\pi}{2}=\pi+\tfrac\pi2$. Conversely, measuring the angle sum of a large triangle reveals the curvature without ever leaving the surface.

@example
title: The cylinder is curved in space but flat in itself
problem: Take the cylinder $\mathbf r(\phi,z)=(R\cos\phi,\,R\sin\phi,\,z)$ with inward unit normal $\mathbf n=(-\cos\phi,-\sin\phi,0)$. Find $g$, $h$, the principal curvatures, $K$ and $H$.
step: The tangent vectors are $\mathbf r_\phi=(-R\sin\phi,R\cos\phi,0)$ and $\mathbf r_z=(0,0,1)$, so $g=\operatorname{diag}(R^2,1)$.
step: The second derivatives are $\mathbf r_{\phi\phi}=(-R\cos\phi,-R\sin\phi,0)$ and $\mathbf r_{\phi z}=\mathbf r_{zz}=\mathbf 0$.
step: Then $h_{\phi\phi}=\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf r_{\phi\phi}=R\cos^2\phi+R\sin^2\phi=R$, and the other components vanish, so $h=\operatorname{diag}(R,0)$.
step: The shape operator is $S=g^{-1}h=\operatorname{diag}(1/R,0)$. So $\kappa_1=1/R$, $\kappa_2=0$, $K=\kappa_1\kappa_2=0$ and $H=\tfrac1{2R}$.
step: Interpretation: the cylinder bends in space ($H\neq0$), yet $g=R^2d\phi^2+dz^2=dX^2+dz^2$ with $X=R\phi$ is flat. Its intrinsic geometry is that of the plane.

@example
title: Angle excess on a sphere of radius 2
problem: On a sphere of radius $a=2$, take the geodesic triangle with vertices at the north pole and at the equator points with longitudes $0$ and $\pi/2$. Check that $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\pi+KA$.
step: The three arcs meet at right angles, so $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$.
step: The triangle is one eighth of the sphere, so $A=\dfrac{4\pi a^2}{8}=\dfrac{16\pi}{8}=2\pi$.
step: The curvature is $K=1/a^2=\tfrac14$, so $KA=\tfrac{\pi}{2}$.
step: Compare: $\pi+KA=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$, which agrees. Angles and areas are measured inside the surface, which is why $K$ can be intrinsic.

@mistakes
- Calling a surface curved because it bends in space. The cylinder and cone bend, but they have $K=0$.
- Ignoring the normal. $\kappa_i$ and $H$ change sign when $\mathbf n$ flips; $K$ does not. State the normal whenever you quote $H$.
- Forgetting to divide by $\det g$: it is $K=\det h/\det g$, not $\det h$.
- Treating the mean curvature $H$ as intrinsic. It depends on the embedding.
