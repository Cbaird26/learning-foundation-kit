### Vectors and covectors

For a vector field $V^i$, the **covariant derivative** in the direction $\partial_j$ is

$$\nabla_jV^i=\partial_jV^i+\Gamma^i_{jk}\,V^k.$$

It carries one new lower index, so it is a tensor of type $(1,1)$. The first term is the change of the components; the second corrects for the turning of the basis. For a covector the correction has the opposite sign:

$$\nabla_j\omega_i=\partial_j\omega_i-\Gamma^k_{ji}\,\omega_k.$$

The sign is forced by the product rule. The pairing $\omega_iV^i$ is a scalar, so $\nabla_j(\omega_iV^i)$ must equal $\partial_j(\omega_iV^i)$, and the two $\Gamma$ terms must cancel. In general every upper index receives a $+\Gamma$ term and every lower index a $-\Gamma$ term, for example

$$\nabla_kT^i{}_j=\partial_kT^i{}_j+\Gamma^i_{kl}\,T^l{}_j-\Gamma^l_{kj}\,T^i{}_l.$$

On a scalar, $\nabla_jf=\partial_jf$.

### Properties

- It is linear, obeys the product rule, and commutes with contraction.
- **Metric compatibility:** $\nabla_kg_{ij}=0$. So raising and lowering indices commutes with $\nabla$.
- A field is **parallel** if $\nabla V=0$. On the plane the parallel fields are exactly the constant fields, in any chart.

For example, $\partial_x$ has polar components $V=(\cos\theta,\,-\sin\theta/r)$, and $\nabla_\theta V^r=-\sin\theta+(-r)(-\sin\theta/r)=0$. The components vary but the field is parallel. Constant components, as for $\partial_r$, do not guarantee that.

### Divergence and Laplacian

Contracting gives $\nabla_iV^i=\partial_iV^i+\Gamma^i_{ik}V^k$. For the metric connection $\Gamma^i_{ik}=\partial_k\ln\sqrt g$, where $g=\det g_{ij}$, so

$$\nabla_iV^i=\frac{1}{\sqrt g}\,\partial_i\big(\sqrt g\,V^i\big),\qquad \nabla^2f=\frac{1}{\sqrt g}\,\partial_i\big(\sqrt g\,g^{ij}\partial_jf\big).$$

In polar coordinates $\sqrt g=r$, which gives the familiar $\nabla^2f=\frac1r\partial_r(r\,\partial_rf)+\frac1{r^2}\partial_\theta^2f$. On the unit sphere $\sqrt g=\sin\theta$ and

$$\nabla^2f=\frac1{\sin\theta}\,\partial_\theta\big(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\big)+\frac1{\sin^2\theta}\,\partial_\phi^2f.$$

### Along a curve

For a vector field defined only along a curve $x^i(t)$, the same rule gives

$$\frac{DV^i}{dt}=\frac{dV^i}{dt}+\Gamma^i_{jk}\,\dot x^{\,j}\,V^k.$$

Setting this to zero for $V=\dot x$ gives geodesics (Week 9). Setting it to zero for a general $V$ gives parallel transport (Week 10).

@example
title: The position field in polar coordinates
problem: In polar coordinates the position vector field is $V=r\,\partial_r$, so $(V^r,V^\theta)=(r,0)$. Compute all four components of $\nabla_jV^i$ and the divergence $\nabla_iV^i$.
step: Only $V^r=r$ is nonzero, so $\nabla_jV^i=\partial_jV^i+\Gamma^i_{jr}\,r$.
step: For $j=r$: $\nabla_rV^r=\partial_r(r)+\Gamma^r_{rr}\,r=1$ and $\nabla_rV^\theta=0+\Gamma^\theta_{rr}\,r=0$.
step: For $j=\theta$: $\nabla_\theta V^r=0+\Gamma^r_{\theta r}\,r=0$ and $\nabla_\theta V^\theta=0+\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}\,r=\tfrac1r\cdot r=1$.
step: So $\nabla_jV^i=\delta^i_j$, the same answer as in Cartesian coordinates, where $V=(x,y)$ and $\partial_jx^i=\delta^i_j$. The divergence is the trace, $2$. The formula agrees: $\frac1r\partial_r(r\cdot r)=2$.

@example
title: The Laplacian on the sphere
problem: On the unit sphere find $\nabla^2f$ for $f=\cos\theta$.
step: For $ds^2=d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2$ we have $\sqrt g=\sin\theta$, $g^{\theta\theta}=1$ and $g^{\phi\phi}=1/\sin^2\theta$.
step: Since $f$ does not depend on $\phi$, only the $\theta$ term survives: $\nabla^2f=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\,\partial_\theta\big(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\big)$.
step: Compute $\partial_\theta f=-\sin\theta$, then $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f=-\sin^2\theta$, whose derivative is $-2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
step: Divide by $\sin\theta$: $\nabla^2f=-2\cos\theta=-2f$. So $\cos\theta$ is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian with eigenvalue $-2$.

@mistakes
- Using $+\Gamma$ for a lower index. A covector needs $\nabla_j\omega_i=\partial_j\omega_i-\Gamma^k_{ji}\omega_k$.
- Misplacing the indices of $\Gamma$. In $\nabla_jV^i=\partial_jV^i+\Gamma^i_{jk}V^k$ the upper index matches the output, the summed index pairs with $V^k$, and the direction $j$ is the first lower index.
- Writing the Laplacian as a plain sum of second partials in curvilinear coordinates, or putting $\sqrt g$ outside the derivative.
- Concluding that $\nabla V=0$ because the components are constant, or that $\nabla V\neq0$ because they vary.

@quiz num
prompt: In polar coordinates a vector field has components $(V^r,V^\theta)=(r^2,\ \sin\theta)$. Find the divergence $\nabla_iV^i$ at the point $(r,\theta)=(2,0)$.
answer: 7
check: (lambda Vc: nderiv(lambda x: Vc(x, 0.0)[0], 2.0) + nderiv(lambda y: Vc(2.0, y)[1], 0.0))(lambda x, y: (lambda r, t: [cos(t)*r**2 - r*sin(t)*sin(t), sin(t)*r**2 + r*cos(t)*sin(t)])(sqrt(x*x + y*y), arctan2(y, x)))
hint: Use $\nabla_iV^i=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt g}\,\partial_i\big(\sqrt g\,V^i\big)$ with $\sqrt g=r$ for the polar metric.
hint: The divergence is $\dfrac1r\partial_r\big(rV^r\big)+\partial_\theta V^\theta$.
solution: $\dfrac1r\,\partial_r\big(r\cdot r^2\big)+\partial_\theta(\sin\theta)=\dfrac1r\cdot3r^2+\cos\theta=3r+\cos\theta$. At $(2,0)$ this equals $6+1=7$.

@quiz num
prompt: On the unit sphere, $ds^2=d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2$, let $f=\cos^2\theta$. Find the Laplacian $\nabla^2f$ at $\theta=\pi/3$.
answer: 1/2
check: (lambda F, p: sum(nderiv(lambda s: F(*[s if m == k else p[m] for m in range(3)]), p[k], 2) for k in range(3)))(lambda x, y, z: z**2/(x*x + y*y + z*z), [sin(pi/3), 0.0, cos(pi/3)])
hint: $\nabla^2f=\dfrac{1}{\sin\theta}\,\partial_\theta\big(\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f\big)+\dfrac1{\sin^2\theta}\,\partial_\phi^2f$, and $f$ does not depend on $\phi$.
hint: $\partial_\theta f=-2\sin\theta\cos\theta$, so $\sin\theta\,\partial_\theta f=-2\sin^2\theta\cos\theta$. Differentiate once more and divide by $\sin\theta$.
solution: $\partial_\theta\big(-2\sin^2\theta\cos\theta\big)=-4\sin\theta\cos^2\theta+2\sin^3\theta$. Dividing by $\sin\theta$ gives $\nabla^2f=-4\cos^2\theta+2\sin^2\theta=2-6\cos^2\theta$. At $\theta=\pi/3$ this is $2-6\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac12$.
