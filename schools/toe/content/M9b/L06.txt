@article
Last week's metric lets us measure. Integration needs something different, and simpler: an object that eats a small oriented piece of a curve or surface and returns a number. For curves that object is a covector, which eats one tangent vector. For surfaces it must eat **two** tangent vectors (the sides of a small parallelogram) and return a signed area. Such objects are **differential forms**, and they need no metric at all.

### Forms and the wedge product

A **$k$-form** is an antisymmetric $(0,k)$ tensor field. A $0$-form is a function and a $1$-form is $\omega=\omega_i\,dx^i$. The **wedge product** of two $1$-forms is the $2$-form

$$(\alpha\wedge\beta)(u,v)=\alpha(u)\,\beta(v)-\alpha(v)\,\beta(u).$$

So $dx\wedge dy$ sends $u,v$ to $u^xv^y-u^yv^x$, the signed area of the parallelogram they span. Swapping the arguments flips the sign: $dx^i\wedge dx^j=-dx^j\wedge dx^i$, and in particular $dx\wedge dx=0$. In $n$ dimensions a $k$-form has $\binom{n}{k}$ independent components.

### The exterior derivative

The operator $d$ turns a $k$-form into a $(k+1)$-form. On functions $df=\partial_if\,dx^i$. For a $1$-form $\omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy$ in the plane,

$$d\omega=\Big(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\Big)\,dx\wedge dy.$$

Two facts matter. First, $d(d\alpha)=0$ always: mixed partial derivatives commute, while the wedge is antisymmetric. Second, $d$ uses only partial derivatives yet produces a tensor, because the antisymmetrization cancels the extra term that spoils $\partial_jV^i$. In $\mathbb{R}^3$, $d$ on $0$-, $1$- and $2$-forms is the gradient, curl and divergence, and $d^2=0$ says $\nabla\times\nabla f=0$ and $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf F)=0$.

### Pullback and integration

To integrate a $1$-form along a curve $\gamma(t)$, substitute the curve into the form and integrate over the parameter:

$$\int_\gamma\omega=\int_a^b\omega_i(\gamma(t))\,\dot x^{\,i}(t)\,dt.$$

Reversing the direction of travel flips the sign; reparametrizing in the same direction changes nothing. For a surface piece $(u,v)\mapsto(x,y)$, substituting $dx$ and $dy$ gives

$$dx\wedge dy=\det\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(u,v)}\;du\wedge dv,$$

so the Jacobian determinant appears automatically. In polar coordinates $dx\wedge dy=r\,dr\wedge d\theta$.

One theorem ties integration to $d$: for a region $S$ with boundary $\partial S$ (oriented consistently),

$$\int_S d\omega=\int_{\partial S}\omega.$$

In the plane it says that the circulation of $\omega$ around a loop equals the integral of $d\omega$ over the enclosed region.

@example
title: Integrating a 1-form around a circle
problem: Let $\omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy$. Compute $\oint\omega$ around the unit circle traversed counterclockwise, and compare with the integral of $d\omega$ over the unit disk.
step: Parametrize the circle by $\gamma(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$ for $0\le t\le2\pi$, so $\dot x=-\sin t$ and $\dot y=\cos t$.
step: Substitute into the form: $\omega(\dot\gamma)=-y\,\dot x+x\,\dot y=\sin^2t+\cos^2t=1$.
step: Integrate: $\oint\omega=\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=2\pi$.
step: Now $d\omega=\big(\partial_x(x)-\partial_y(-y)\big)\,dx\wedge dy=2\,dx\wedge dy$. The disk has area $\pi$, so $\int_Dd\omega=2\pi$, in agreement with $\int_S d\omega=\int_{\partial S}\omega$.

@example
title: The area form in polar coordinates
problem: Express $dx\wedge dy$ in polar coordinates and use it to compute $\int dx\wedge dy$ over the half-annulus $1\le r\le2$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$.
step: From $x=r\cos\theta$ and $y=r\sin\theta$: $dx=\cos\theta\,dr-r\sin\theta\,d\theta$ and $dy=\sin\theta\,dr+r\cos\theta\,d\theta$.
step: Wedge them, using $dr\wedge dr=d\theta\wedge d\theta=0$ and $d\theta\wedge dr=-dr\wedge d\theta$: $dx\wedge dy=r\cos^2\theta\,dr\wedge d\theta-r\sin^2\theta\,d\theta\wedge dr=r\,dr\wedge d\theta$.
step: Integrate, taking $(r,\theta)$ as positively oriented: $\int_0^\pi\!\int_1^2r\,dr\,d\theta=\pi\cdot\tfrac32=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$.
step: Check: the half-annulus has area $\tfrac12\pi\,(2^2-1^2)=\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that $dy\wedge dx=-dx\wedge dy$. Swapping the order of a wedge flips the sign of the integral.
- Replacing $dx\wedge dy$ by $dr\wedge d\theta$ and dropping the Jacobian factor $r$.
- Writing $d(P\,dx+Q\,dy)=(\partial_xP-\partial_yQ)\,dx\wedge dy$. The correct coefficient is $\partial_xQ-\partial_yP$.

answer: 1
check: quad2(lambda u, v: (u*v)*(nderiv(lambda s: s**2, u)*nderiv(lambda w: u*w, v) - nderiv(lambda w: u**2 + 0*w, v)*nderiv(lambda s: s*v, u)), 0, 1, 0, 2)
hint: Pull back: $dx=2u\,du$ and $dy=v\,du+u\,dv$. Then $dx\wedge dy=2u\cdot u\,du\wedge dv$.
hint: The pulled-back integrand is $y\cdot2u^2=2u^3v$. Integrate over $0\le u\le1$ and $0\le v\le2$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about differential forms are true?
choice*: $d(d\omega)=0$ for every form $\omega$.
choice: $dx\wedge dy=dy\wedge dx$.
choice*: Traversing a curve in the opposite direction changes the sign of $\int_\gamma\omega$ for a $1$-form $\omega$.
choice: For $\omega=P\,dx+Q\,dy$, $d\omega=(\partial_xP-\partial_yQ)\,dx\wedge dy$.
choice*: Reparametrizing a curve in the same direction does not change $\int_\gamma\omega$.
hint: Recall that the wedge product is antisymmetric, and check the formula for $d\omega$ on a simple example such as $\omega=x\,dy$.
solution: $d^2=0$ holds because mixed partials commute. The wedge is antisymmetric, so $dx\wedge dy=-dy\wedge dx$. Reversing the direction of travel flips the sign of a line integral, while reparametrizing in the same direction leaves it unchanged. The correct coefficient in $d\omega$ is $\partial_xQ-\partial_yP$; for $\omega=x\,dy$ it gives $d\omega=dx\wedge dy$, not $-dx\wedge dy$.
