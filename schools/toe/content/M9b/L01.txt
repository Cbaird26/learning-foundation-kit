@article
A **curve** is the path of a moving point; a **surface** is a sheet that a point can slide over. To do calculus on them we describe both with formulas in parameters. This week you will see that those formulas are a choice: the same geometric object can be described in many ways.

### Curves as parametrized paths

A parametrized curve in space is a map $\gamma(t)=(x(t),y(t),z(t))$ for $t$ in an interval. Think of $t$ as time. The **velocity** is the derivative

$$\gamma'(t)=\big(x'(t),\,y'(t),\,z'(t)\big),$$

a vector tangent to the path at $\gamma(t)$. Its length $|\gamma'(t)|$ is the **speed**. The curve is **regular** if $\gamma'(t)\neq 0$ for all $t$, so the path has a tangent line everywhere.

Adding up the speed gives the **arc length**:

$$L=\int_a^b |\gamma'(t)|\,dt.$$

For the circle $\gamma(t)=(R\cos t,\,R\sin t)$ we get $\gamma'=(-R\sin t,\,R\cos t)$, speed $R$, and $L=2\pi R$ for $0\le t\le 2\pi$.

A path and a parametrization are different things. The maps $(\cos t,\sin t)$ and $(\cos 2t,\sin 2t)$ trace the same circle at different speeds. Velocity depends on the parametrization; the length of the traced path does not. When the speed is always $1$, the parameter itself measures distance along the curve: this is the **arc-length parametrization**.

### Surfaces as parametrized sheets

A parametrized surface is a map $\mathbf r(u,v)=(x,y,z)$ from a region of the $(u,v)$-plane into space. Hold $v$ fixed and vary $u$ to get a **coordinate curve**; do the same with $u$ fixed. Their velocities are

$$\mathbf r_u=\frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial u},\qquad \mathbf r_v=\frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial v}.$$

If $\mathbf r_u$ and $\mathbf r_v$ are linearly independent at a point, the surface is **regular** there. Their span is the **tangent plane**, and

$$\mathbf N=\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$$

is a **normal vector**, nonzero exactly when $\mathbf r_u,\mathbf r_v$ are independent.

Now draw any curve on the surface by choosing $u(t)$ and $v(t)$. By the chain rule its velocity is

$$\gamma'(t)=u'(t)\,\mathbf r_u+v'(t)\,\mathbf r_v.$$

So every tangent vector is a combination of $\mathbf r_u$ and $\mathbf r_v$, and the coefficients $u'$ and $v'$ are its **components**. **The components of a tangent vector depend on the coordinates you chose; the arrow itself does not.** That sentence is the seed of this whole course.

### Example: the sphere

For radius $a$, polar angle $\theta$ measured from the north pole and azimuth $\phi$:

$$\mathbf r(\theta,\phi)=a(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta).$$

Then $\mathbf r_\theta=a(\cos\theta\cos\phi,\cos\theta\sin\phi,-\sin\theta)$ has length $a$, and $\mathbf r_\phi=a\sin\theta(-\sin\phi,\cos\phi,0)$ has length $a\sin\theta$. The two are perpendicular, and

$$\mathbf N=a^2\sin\theta\,(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta)$$

points straight out of the sphere. At the poles $\sin\theta=0$, so $\mathbf r_\phi=\mathbf 0$ and $\mathbf N=\mathbf 0$: the parametrization breaks down there even though the sphere itself is perfectly smooth. The fault lies in the coordinates, not in the surface. That is the topic of next week.

@example
title: Length of one turn of a helix
problem: Find the speed, and the length of one full turn ($0\le t\le 2\pi$), of the helix $\gamma(t)=(2\cos t,\ 2\sin t,\ 3t)$.
step: Differentiate each component: $\gamma'(t)=(-2\sin t,\ 2\cos t,\ 3)$.
step: The speed is $|\gamma'(t)|=\sqrt{4\sin^2 t+4\cos^2 t+9}=\sqrt{13}$, a constant.
step: Integrate the speed over one turn: $L=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{13}\,dt=2\pi\sqrt{13}\approx 22.65$.
step: Sanity check by unrolling the cylinder the helix lives on: one turn goes around a circle of length $2\pi\cdot 2=4\pi$ while climbing $3\cdot 2\pi=6\pi$. The hypotenuse is $\sqrt{(4\pi)^2+(6\pi)^2}=2\pi\sqrt{13}$.

@example
title: Tangent plane of a paraboloid
problem: The surface $\mathbf r(u,v)=(u,\ v,\ u^2+v^2)$ passes through $P=(1,2,5)$ at $(u,v)=(1,2)$. Find $\mathbf r_u$, $\mathbf r_v$, a normal vector, and the equation of the tangent plane at $P$.
step: Differentiate: $\mathbf r_u=(1,0,2u)$ and $\mathbf r_v=(0,1,2v)$. At $(1,2)$ these are $(1,0,2)$ and $(0,1,4)$.
step: Take the cross product: $\mathbf N=(1,0,2)\times(0,1,4)=(0\cdot4-2\cdot1,\ 2\cdot0-1\cdot4,\ 1\cdot1-0\cdot0)=(-2,-4,1)$.
step: The tangent plane is $\mathbf N\cdot(\mathbf x-P)=0$, that is $-2(x-1)-4(y-2)+(z-5)=0$, which simplifies to $z=2x+4y-5$.
step: Check that $\mathbf N$ is perpendicular to both tangent vectors: $(1,0,2)\cdot(-2,-4,1)=0$ and $(0,1,4)\cdot(-2,-4,1)=0$.

@mistakes
- Treating the parameter $t$ as distance travelled. It equals arc length only when the speed is $1$.
- Computing length as $|\gamma(b)-\gamma(a)|$ (the straight-line distance) or as $\int\gamma'\,dt$ instead of integrating the speed $|\gamma'|$.
- Trusting a parametrization at a point where $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=\mathbf 0$, such as the poles of the sphere in $(\theta,\phi)$ coordinates.

answer: (4, 3, 12)

@practice num
prompt: Find the length of the curve $\gamma(t)=\big(t,\ t^2,\ \tfrac23 t^3\big)$ for $0\le t\le 3$.
answer: 21
check: quad(lambda t: sqrt(sum(nderiv(c, t)**2 for c in [lambda s: s, lambda s: s**2, lambda s: 2*s**3/3])), 0, 3)
hint: The length is $\int_0^3|\gamma'(t)|\,dt$. First find $\gamma'(t)$ and its squared length.
hint: $|\gamma'|^2=1+4t^2+4t^4$. Notice that this is a perfect square.
solution: $\gamma'=(1,2t,2t^2)$ and $|\gamma'|^2=1+4t^2+4t^4=(1+2t^2)^2$, so the speed is $1+2t^2$. Then $L=\int_0^3(1+2t^2)\,dt=\left[t+\tfrac23 t^3\right]_0^3=3+18=21$.

@practice tuple
prompt: The surface $\mathbf r(u,v)=(u+v,\ u-v,\ uv)$ is regular at $(u,v)=(2,1)$. Find the normal vector $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ there.
answer: (3, -1, -2)
check: (lambda r: list(cross([nderiv(lambda u: r(u, 1)[k], 2) for k in range(3)], [nderiv(lambda v: r(2, v)[k], 1) for k in range(3)])))(lambda u, v: [u+v, u-v, u*v])
hint: First compute $\mathbf r_u=(1,1,v)$ and $\mathbf r_v=(1,-1,u)$, then evaluate at $(2,1)$.
hint: At $(2,1)$ you need $(1,1,1)\times(1,-1,2)$. Use $\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(a_2b_3-a_3b_2,\ a_3b_1-a_1b_3,\ a_1b_2-a_2b_1)$.
solution: $\mathbf r_u=(1,1,v)=(1,1,1)$ and $\mathbf r_v=(1,-1,u)=(1,-1,2)$. Then $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v=(1\cdot2-1\cdot(-1),\ 1\cdot1-1\cdot2,\ 1\cdot(-1)-1\cdot1)=(3,-1,-2)$.

@practice mc
prompt: The curves $\alpha(t)=(\cos t,\sin t)$ for $0\le t\le 2\pi$ and $\beta(t)=(\cos 2t,\sin 2t)$ for $0\le t\le \pi$ both trace the unit circle exactly once. Which statement is true?
choice*: They have the same length, $2\pi$, but $\beta$ moves with twice the speed of $\alpha$.
choice: They have the same velocity vector at every point of the circle.
choice: $\beta$ has length $\pi$, because its parameter interval is half as long.
choice: $\beta$ moves with half the speed of $\alpha$, because it finishes in half the time.
hint: Compute $|\alpha'(t)|$ and $|\beta'(t)|$ separately.
hint: Length is speed integrated over the parameter interval: multiply each speed by the length of its interval.
solution: $|\alpha'|=1$ and $|\beta'|=2$. So $L_\alpha=1\cdot 2\pi=2\pi$ and $L_\beta=2\cdot\pi=2\pi$. Velocity depends on the parametrization; the traced path and its length do not.

@practice num
prompt: On the unit sphere $\mathbf r(\theta,\phi)=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\ \sin\theta\sin\phi,\ \cos\theta)$, a curve is given by $\theta(t)=t$, $\phi(t)=2t$. Find its speed at $t=\pi/3$.
answer: 2
check: sqrt(sum(nderiv(c, pi/3)**2 for c in [lambda t: sin(t)*cos(2*t), lambda t: sin(t)*sin(2*t), lambda t: cos(t)]))
hint: The velocity is $\gamma'=\theta'\,\mathbf r_\theta+\phi'\,\mathbf r_\phi$.
hint: On the unit sphere $|\mathbf r_\theta|=1$, $|\mathbf r_\phi|=\sin\theta$, and the two vectors are perpendicular, so you can add their squared lengths.
solution: $\theta'=1$ and $\phi'=2$. Since $\mathbf r_\theta\perp\mathbf r_\phi$, $|\gamma'|^2=\theta'^2|\mathbf r_\theta|^2+\phi'^2|\mathbf r_\phi|^2=1+4\sin^2\theta$. At $\theta=\pi/3$, $\sin^2\theta=\tfrac34$, so $|\gamma'|^2=1+3=4$ and the speed is $2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Consider the torus patch $\mathbf r(u,v)=\big((3+\cos v)\cos u,\ (3+\cos v)\sin u,\ \sin v\big)$. Find the length of the normal vector $\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v$ at $(u,v)=(\pi/2,\pi/3)$.
answer: 7/2
check: (lambda r: sqrt(sum(c*c for c in cross([nderiv(lambda u: r(u, pi/3)[k], pi/2) for k in range(3)], [nderiv(lambda v: r(pi/2, v)[k], pi/3) for k in range(3)]))))(lambda u, v: [(3+cos(v))*cos(u), (3+cos(v))*sin(u), sin(v)])
hint: Compute $\mathbf r_u$ and $\mathbf r_v$ and check whether they are perpendicular.
hint: If $\mathbf r_u\perp\mathbf r_v$ then $|\mathbf r_u\times\mathbf r_v|=|\mathbf r_u|\,|\mathbf r_v|$. Find each length as a function of $v$.
solution: $\mathbf r_u=(3+\cos v)(-\sin u,\cos u,0)$ has length $3+\cos v$. $\mathbf r_v=(-\sin v\cos u,\,-\sin v\sin u,\,\cos v)$ has length $1$, and $\mathbf r_u\cdot\mathbf r_v=0$. So $|\mathbf N|=3+\cos v$, which at $v=\pi/3$ equals $3+\tfrac12=\tfrac72$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these parametrizations is regular (that is, has $\mathbf r_u$ and $\mathbf r_v$ linearly independent) at $(u,v)=(0,0)$?
choice*: $\mathbf r(u,v)=(u,\ v,\ u^2+v^2)$
choice: $\mathbf r(u,v)=(u^2,\ v,\ uv)$
