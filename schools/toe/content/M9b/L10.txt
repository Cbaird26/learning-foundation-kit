@article
In a flat plane with Cartesian coordinates, moving a vector to another point is trivial: keep its components. On a curved surface two tangent vectors at different points live in different tangent spaces, and there is no canonical way to compare them. **Parallel transport** is the rule that carries a vector along a path while turning it as little as the surface allows.

### The transport equation

A vector field $V$ along a curve $x^i(t)$ is **parallel** if its covariant derivative along the curve vanishes:

$$\frac{DV^k}{dt}=\frac{dV^k}{dt}+\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\dot x^{\,i}\,V^j=0.$$

This is a linear first-order system, so $V(0)$ determines $V(t)$, and the map $V(0)\mapsto V(1)$ is linear. Three consequences follow.

- By metric compatibility, $\frac{d}{dt}g(V,W)=0$ for two parallel fields, so parallel transport **preserves lengths and angles**.
- A curve is a geodesic exactly when its velocity is parallel along itself.
- In the flat plane the parallel fields are the constant fields. In polar coordinates the components $V^r,V^\theta$ vary along a circle, but the Cartesian vector does not.

### Path dependence is curvature

In the plane, the transported vector does not depend on the path between two points. On the sphere it does. Take the latitude circle $\theta=\theta_0$ of the unit sphere, with $\phi=t$ running from $0$ to $2\pi$. With $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\cot\theta$, the transport equations are

$$\dot V^\theta=\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,V^\phi,\qquad \dot V^\phi=-\cot\theta_0\,V^\theta.$$

Starting from $V=\partial_\theta$, you can check by substitution that

$$V^\theta=\cos(t\cos\theta_0),\qquad V^\phi=-\frac{\sin(t\cos\theta_0)}{\sin\theta_0}.$$

In the orthonormal frame $\hat e_\theta=\partial_\theta$, $\hat e_\phi=\partial_\phi/\sin\theta$ the components are $(\cos\beta,-\sin\beta)$ with $\beta=t\cos\theta_0$. The vector turns steadily relative to the frame, at rate $\cos\theta_0$. After one lap the frame has returned to itself and the vector has turned by $-2\pi\cos\theta_0$, which is the same as a rotation by

$$\Delta=2\pi(1-\cos\theta_0)\pmod{2\pi}$$

from $\hat e_\theta$ toward $\hat e_\phi$. That number is exactly the **area of the polar cap** enclosed by the loop. On the equator $\theta_0=\pi/2$ it is $0$ (mod $2\pi$): a closed geodesic returns every vector unchanged.

This is general. For any simple loop on the unit sphere, the rotation of a transported vector equals the enclosed area (mod $2\pi$). On a surface of curvature $K$ it is $K$ times the area for small loops. Next week turns this into a formula for the curvature tensor.

@example
title: Transport around a circle in the flat plane
problem: In polar coordinates, transport $V=\partial_r$ (components $(1,0)$) once around the circle $r=r_0$, $\theta=t$, $0\le t\le2\pi$. Show that the components change but the vector returns unchanged.
step: With $\dot x=(0,1)$ the transport equations are $\dot V^r+\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}V^\theta=0$ and $\dot V^\theta+\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}V^r=0$, that is $\dot V^r=r_0V^\theta$ and $\dot V^\theta=-V^r/r_0$.
step: The solution with $V(0)=(1,0)$ is $V^r=\cos t$ and $V^\theta=-\sin t/r_0$. Check: $\dot V^r=-\sin t=r_0V^\theta$ and $\dot V^\theta=-\cos t/r_0=-V^r/r_0$.
step: Convert to Cartesian components using $\partial_r=(\cos t,\sin t)$ and $\partial_\theta=r_0(-\sin t,\cos t)$: $V=\cos t\,(\cos t,\sin t)-\sin t\,(-\sin t,\cos t)=(1,0)$.
step: The Cartesian vector is constant, as it must be in the flat plane. After the full lap $V=(1,0)$ again: no rotation, so no curvature.

@example
title: Transport around an octant triangle
problem: On the unit sphere let $N$ be the north pole, $A$ the equator point with $\phi=0$ and $B$ the equator point with $\phi=\pi/2$. Transport a vector once around the triangle $N\to A\to B\to N$, starting with the vector pointing along the first leg. By what angle is it rotated?
step: All three sides are great-circle arcs, so they are geodesics. Along a geodesic the velocity is parallel, so the transported vector keeps a constant angle with it.
step: Leg 1, $N\to A$: the vector equals the velocity. At $A$ it points south, perpendicular to the equator.
step: Leg 2, $A\to B$ along the equator: the angle to the velocity stays $90^\circ$, so the vector still points south at $B$.
step: Leg 3, $B\to N$ along the meridian $\phi=\pi/2$: at $B$ the velocity points north, so the vector is opposite to it. That angle of $180^\circ$ stays fixed, so on arriving at $N$ the vector points away from $N$ along the meridian $\phi=\pi/2$.
step: Initially the vector pointed along the meridian $\phi=0$; finally it points along $\phi=\pi/2$. It has turned by $\pi/2$ in the direction of travel. The triangle has area $4\pi/8=\pi/2$: rotation equals enclosed area.

@mistakes
- Expecting the components $V^k$ to stay constant along the path. In curvilinear coordinates they change even in flat space; the vector itself is what stays constant.
- Assuming the transported vector is independent of the path. On a curved surface it is not, and the difference measures curvature.
- Contracting $\Gamma$ with the wrong vectors. The velocity $\dot x^i$ goes in one lower slot and the transported vector $V^j$ in the other.

@practice mc

choice*: Parallel transport along a curve preserves the lengths of vectors and the angles between them.
choice: Parallel transport keeps the components $V^i$ constant in every coordinate chart.
choice: On a sphere, transporting a vector between two points gives the same result along every path.

@practice tuple
prompt: In polar coordinates, a vector with components $(V^r,V^\theta)=(2,3)$ at $r=1$ is parallel-transported outward along the ray $\theta=0$, with $r=1+t$. Find its components at $r=3$.
answer: (2, 1)
check: ivp(lambda t, y: [0.0, -y[1]/(1.0 + t)], [2.0, 3.0], 0.0, 2.0)
hint: Along the ray $\dot x=(\dot r,\dot\theta)=(1,0)$, so $\dot V^k=-\Gamma^k_{rj}V^j$. Which of $\Gamma^r_{rr}$, $\Gamma^r_{r\theta}$, $\Gamma^\theta_{rr}$, $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}$ are nonzero?
hint: Only $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=1/r$ is nonzero, so $\dot V^r=0$ and $\dot V^\theta=-V^\theta/r$. Solve for $V^\theta(r)$.
solution: $\dot V^r=0$, so $V^r=2$. Also $\dfrac{dV^\theta}{dr}=-\dfrac{V^\theta}{r}$, so $V^\theta\propto 1/r$ and $V^\theta=3/r$. At $r=3$ the components are $(2,1)$. Cartesian check: at $\theta=0$ the vector is $(V^r,\,rV^\theta)=(2,3)$ at every $r$, constant, as it should be in the flat plane.

@practice num
prompt: A vector is parallel-transported once around the circle of latitude on the unit sphere where $\cos\theta_0=\tfrac34$, with $\phi$ increasing from $0$ to $2\pi$. Find the net angle by which it has rotated relative to its starting direction, measured counterclockwise as seen from outside the sphere (the sense in which the loop circles the north pole), as a number between $0$ and $2\pi$.
answer: pi/2
check: (lambda c, s: (lambda y: math.atan2(s*y[1], y[0]) % (2*pi))(ivp(lambda t, y: [s*c*y[1], -(c/s)*y[0]], [1.0, 0.0], 0.0, 2*pi)))(0.75, sqrt(1 - 0.75**2))
hint: The vector turns relative to the orthonormal frame at rate $\cos\theta_0$, so after one lap it has turned by $-2\pi\cos\theta_0$.
hint: Net rotation is $2\pi(1-\cos\theta_0)$, the area of the enclosed polar cap.
solution: $\Delta=2\pi(1-\cos\theta_0)=2\pi\cdot\tfrac14=\tfrac\pi2$. Equivalently, the vector turned by $-2\pi\cdot\tfrac34=-\tfrac{3\pi}{2}\equiv\tfrac\pi2\pmod{2\pi}$. The enclosed cap has area $2\pi(1-\tfrac34)=\tfrac\pi2$.

@practice num
prompt: A geodesic triangle on the unit sphere has one vertex at the north pole and two vertices on the equator, at longitudes $0$ and $\pi/3$. A vector is parallel-transported once around it. Find the angle of rotation, in radians.
answer: pi/3
check: quad2(lambda th, ph: sin(th), 0, pi/2, 0, pi/3)
hint: On the unit sphere the rotation equals the area enclosed by the loop.
hint: The triangle is the part of the northern hemisphere between the meridians $\phi=0$ and $\phi=\pi/3$. Integrate the area element $\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi$ over it.
solution: The area is $\int_0^{\pi/3}\!\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=\tfrac\pi3\cdot1=\tfrac\pi3$. Equivalently, the triangle is $\tfrac{1}{6}$ of the northern hemisphere, whose area is $2\pi$. The angle excess $\big(\tfrac\pi2+\tfrac\pi2+\tfrac\pi3\big)-\pi=\tfrac\pi3$ agrees.

@practice self
prompt: The cylinder of radius $R$ has metric $ds^2=dz^2+R^2d\phi^2$. (a) Derive its geodesic equations from $\mathcal L=\tfrac12(\dot z^2+R^2\dot\phi^2)$ and solve them. (b) Interpret the solutions geometrically: which geodesics are circles, which are lines, which are helices? (c) For $R=1$, a geodesic starts at $(z,\phi)=(0,0)$ with $\dot z=3$ and $\dot\phi=4$. Find its length from the start until it first returns to $\phi=2\pi$, and check it by unrolling the cylinder.
hint: Both coordinates are absent from $\mathcal L$, so both momenta are conserved. What does that say about $\ddot z$ and $\ddot\phi$?
hint: For (c), find the speed first, then the time needed for $\phi$ to advance by $2\pi$.
solution: (a) The Euler-Lagrange equations are $\ddot z=0$ and $R^2\ddot\phi=0$, so $z=z_0+at$ and $\phi=\phi_0+bt$ (all Christoffel symbols vanish). (b) With $a=0$ we get a horizontal circle, with $b=0$ a vertical line, and otherwise a helix. Unrolling the cylinder into a plane with coordinates $(R\phi,z)$ turns each of these into a straight line. Only the circles are closed geodesics. (c) The speed is $\sqrt{\dot z^2+R^2\dot\phi^2}=\sqrt{9+16}=5$. One turn takes time $t=2\pi/4=\pi/2$, so the length is $5\cdot\pi/2=5\pi/2$. Unrolled, the path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $2\pi$ (around) and $3\pi/2$ (up), of length $\sqrt{4\pi^2+9\pi^2/4}=5\pi/2$.
rubric: Derives $\ddot z=0$ and $\ddot\phi=0$ from Euler-Lagrange and solves them as linear functions of $t$, noting that all Christoffel symbols vanish.
rubric: Classifies the solutions as circles, vertical lines and helices, and explains the interpretation by unrolling the cylinder into a flat sheet.
rubric: Computes the speed $5$, the time $\pi/2$ and the length $5\pi/2$, and confirms it with the unrolled right triangle.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about parallel transport on the unit sphere are true?
choice*: Transporting a vector once around the equator returns it unchanged.
choice*: If $V$ and $W$ are both parallel along a curve, then $g(V,W)$ is constant along the curve.
choice: In the flat plane, transporting a vector around a closed loop changes its Cartesian components.
choice: When a vector and the velocity of a geodesic are transported together along the geodesic, the angle between them changes.
choice*: For a small loop, the rotation of the transported vector is approximately the area enclosed by the loop.
hint: Use metric compatibility, and recall that a geodesic transports its own velocity.
solution: The equator is a closed geodesic, so the holonomy is trivial there. Parallel transport preserves $g(V,W)$. In the flat plane the Cartesian components of a parallel field are constant. Both the vector and the geodesic velocity are parallel, so the angle between them is constant. For the unit sphere the rotation equals the enclosed area.
