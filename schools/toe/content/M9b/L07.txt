@article
In Cartesian coordinates, differentiating a vector field is easy: differentiate the components. In curvilinear coordinates this breaks down. In polar coordinates the field $V=\partial_r$ has constant components $(1,0)$, yet it points in a different direction at every point, so it is certainly not constant. Conversely, the truly constant field $\partial_x$ has polar components $(\cos\theta,\,-\sin\theta/r)$ that vary from point to point. Components alone cannot say how a field changes, because the basis vectors change from point to point too. A **connection** supplies the missing rule: it says how basis vectors change.

### Connections and Christoffel symbols

A connection $\nabla$ differentiates a vector field $Y$ in the direction of a vector $X$ and produces a new vector field $\nabla_XY$. It is linear in $X$ and obeys the product rule $\nabla_X(fY)=X(f)\,Y+f\,\nabla_XY$. It is then fixed by what it does to the basis vectors:

$$\nabla_{\partial_i}\partial_j=\Gamma^k_{ij}\,\partial_k.$$

The $n^3$ functions $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ are the **connection coefficients**; they are called **Christoffel symbols** when they come from a metric, as below. On components the rule reads

$$(\nabla_iY)^k=\partial_iY^k+\Gamma^k_{ij}\,Y^j.$$

The first term is the change of the components; the second is the change of the basis vectors. Neither term is a tensor alone, but their sum is. In the same way the $\Gamma$'s are **not** tensor components: on the plane they are all zero in Cartesian coordinates, but not in polar coordinates.

### The metric connection

A metric singles out exactly one connection that is **torsion-free**, $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\Gamma^k_{ji}$, and **metric-compatible**, meaning lengths and angles are preserved when vectors are carried along. Its coefficients are

$$\Gamma^k_{ij}=\tfrac12\,g^{kl}\big(\partial_ig_{jl}+\partial_jg_{il}-\partial_lg_{ij}\big).$$

The recipe is to invert the metric, differentiate its components, and assemble the three terms. For a **diagonal** metric it collapses to this (no sum over repeated indices; $i\neq j$):

$$\Gamma^i_{ii}=\frac{\partial_ig_{ii}}{2g_{ii}},\qquad \Gamma^i_{ij}=\Gamma^i_{ji}=\frac{\partial_jg_{ii}}{2g_{ii}},\qquad \Gamma^i_{jj}=-\frac{\partial_ig_{jj}}{2g_{ii}},$$

and every symbol with three different indices is zero.

Two results to remember. For polar coordinates, $g=\operatorname{diag}(1,r^2)$, the only nonzero symbols are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}=1/r$. For the unit sphere, $g=\operatorname{diag}(1,\sin^2\theta)$, they are $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta}=\cot\theta$.

The polar symbols have a picture. $\nabla_{\partial_\theta}\partial_\theta=-r\,\partial_r$ says that as you go around a circle the vector $\partial_\theta$ turns toward the centre. $\nabla_{\partial_r}\partial_\theta=\tfrac1r\partial_\theta$ says that $\partial_\theta$, whose length is $r$, stretches as you move outward.

@example
title: Christoffel symbols of the polar metric
problem: Use $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\tfrac12 g^{kl}(\partial_ig_{jl}+\partial_jg_{il}-\partial_lg_{ij})$ to find all Christoffel symbols for $ds^2=dr^2+r^2\,d\theta^2$.
step: The metric is $g_{rr}=1$, $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2$, with inverse $g^{rr}=1$, $g^{\theta\theta}=r^{-2}$. The only nonzero derivative of a component is $\partial_rg_{\theta\theta}=2r$.
step: Because $g^{kl}$ is diagonal, $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\tfrac12 g^{kk}(\partial_ig_{jk}+\partial_jg_{ik}-\partial_kg_{ij})$. For $k=r$ and $i=j=\theta$ only the last term survives: $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=\tfrac12\cdot1\cdot(-\partial_rg_{\theta\theta})=-r$.
step: For $k=\theta$ and $(i,j)=(r,\theta)$ only the first term survives: $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\tfrac12\cdot r^{-2}\cdot\partial_rg_{\theta\theta}=\tfrac{2r}{2r^2}=\tfrac1r$. By symmetry $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}=\tfrac1r$ too.
step: Every other combination contains only derivatives that vanish, such as $\partial_\theta g_{\theta\theta}$ or $\partial_rg_{rr}$. So the nonzero symbols are $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta}=-r$ and $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta}=\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}=1/r$.

@example
title: A diagonal metric with an exponential factor
problem: Find the nonzero Christoffel symbols of $ds^2=dx^2+e^{2x}\,dy^2$.
step: Here $g_{xx}=1$ and $g_{yy}=e^{2x}$. The only nonzero derivative of a component is $\partial_xg_{yy}=2e^{2x}$.
step: Apply the diagonal recipe with $i=x$, $j=y$: $\Gamma^x_{yy}=-\dfrac{\partial_xg_{yy}}{2g_{xx}}=-e^{2x}$.
step: With $i=y$, $j=x$: $\Gamma^y_{yx}=\Gamma^y_{xy}=\dfrac{\partial_xg_{yy}}{2g_{yy}}=\dfrac{2e^{2x}}{2e^{2x}}=1$.
step: The remaining candidates need $\partial_yg_{xx}$, $\partial_yg_{yy}$ or $\partial_xg_{xx}$, which are all zero. So only $\Gamma^x_{yy}=-e^{2x}$ and $\Gamma^y_{xy}=\Gamma^y_{yx}=1$ are nonzero.

@mistakes
- Treating $\Gamma^k_{ij}$ as tensor components. They vanish in Cartesian coordinates on the plane but not in polar coordinates, so they cannot be a tensor.
- Multiplying by $g_{kk}$ instead of dividing: the raised index needs the inverse metric, so a diagonal metric gives $\tfrac{1}{2g_{kk}}$.
- Dropping a term of the combination $\partial_ig_{jl}+\partial_jg_{il}-\partial_lg_{ij}$, or flipping the sign of the last one.

@practice mc
prompt: In polar coordinates, the vector field $V=\partial_r$ has constant components $(V^r,V^\theta)=(1,0)$. Which statement is correct?
choice*: Its components do not change, yet the field is not constant: it points in a different direction at each point. The connection term $\Gamma^i_{jk}V^k$ accounts for this.
choice: The field is constant, because the partial derivatives $\partial_jV^i$ of its components all vanish.
choice: The field is not constant, because all the Christoffel symbols vanish in polar coordinates.
choice: Whether a field is constant depends on the chart, so no chart-independent statement is possible.
hint: Picture the field in the plane. Which way does $\partial_r$ point at the point $(1,0)$, and which way at $(0,1)$?
solution: $\partial_r$ is the outward radial unit vector. It points along $+x$ at $(1,0)$ and along $+y$ at $(0,1)$, so it is not constant even though $\partial_jV^i=0$. The covariant derivative detects this: $\nabla_\theta V^\theta=\partial_\theta V^\theta+\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}V^r=\tfrac1r\neq0$. Whether a field is covariantly constant is chart-independent; only the partial derivatives of components are not.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the Christoffel symbols of a metric are true?
choice*: They are not tensor components: for the flat plane they vanish in Cartesian coordinates but not in polar coordinates.
choice*: Multiplying the metric by a positive constant, $g\to c\,g$, leaves them unchanged.
choice: For a flat metric they vanish in every coordinate chart.
choice: They involve second derivatives of the metric components.
choice*: They are symmetric in the two lower indices, $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\Gamma^k_{ji}$.
hint: Look at the formula: how many derivatives of $g$ appear, and what happens to $g^{kl}$ and $\partial g$ when $g\to c\,g$?
solution: Polar coordinates on the plane show the symbols are not tensorial and need not vanish for a flat metric. Under $g\to cg$ the inverse scales as $c^{-1}$ and each derivative as $c$, so $\Gamma$ is unchanged. The formula contains only first derivatives of $g$. Torsion-freeness is the symmetry $\Gamma^k_{ij}=\Gamma^k_{ji}$.
