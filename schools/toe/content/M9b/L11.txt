@article
Last week the rotation of a transported vector depended on the loop, not only on its endpoints. That is curvature, and this week we measure it. There is a second view: in a curved space, taking covariant derivatives in two different orders gives different answers.

### The curvature tensor

For a torsion-free connection, the failure of covariant derivatives to commute acts linearly on the vector:

$$(\nabla_\mu\nabla_\nu-\nabla_\nu\nabla_\mu)V^\rho=R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}\,V^\sigma.$$

Written with Christoffel symbols (the convention used throughout this course),

$$R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}=\partial_\mu\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma}-\partial_\nu\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma}+\Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma}-\Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}.$$

This is the **curvature tensor**, also called the Riemann tensor. Although $\Gamma$ is not a tensor, $R$ is: the non-tensorial parts cancel. It is antisymmetric in $\mu\nu$. Because it is a tensor, if it vanishes in one chart it vanishes in all, and a metric is **flat** (locally Euclidean) exactly when $R=0$. Polar coordinates on the plane have nonzero $\Gamma$ but $R=0$.

### Ricci tensor and scalar curvature

Contractions give simpler objects: the **Ricci tensor** $R_{\sigma\nu}=R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu}$ and the **scalar curvature** $R=g^{\sigma\nu}R_{\sigma\nu}$.

### Surfaces: one number

In two dimensions the curvature tensor has a single independent component. It is the curvature $K$ of the surface (its Gaussian curvature):

$$R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu}=K\,(g_{\rho\mu}g_{\sigma\nu}-g_{\rho\nu}g_{\sigma\mu}),\qquad R_{\sigma\nu}=K\,g_{\sigma\nu},\qquad R=2K.$$

So one component is enough: $K=R_{1212}/\det g$. For metrics $ds^2=dr^2+G(r)^2d\theta^2$, the symbols of Week 7 give $R^r{}_{\theta r\theta}=-GG''$ and

$$K=-\frac{G''}{G}.$$

- $G=r$ gives $K=0$, the flat plane.
- $G=a\sin(r/a)$ gives $K=1/a^2$, a sphere of radius $a$.
- $G=a\sinh(r/a)$ gives $K=-1/a^2$, a hyperbolic plane.

### What the sign means

If $K>0$ the surface is sphere-like: geodesic triangles have angle sum greater than $\pi$, and geodesics that start parallel converge. If $K<0$ it is saddle-like: angle sums are less than $\pi$ and geodesics spread apart. The quantity $G(r)\,\Delta\theta$ is the separation of two geodesics fanning out from a point, and $G''=-KG$ controls how that separation grows. On the unit sphere it is $\sin r$: the geodesics spread, then reconverge at the antipode. On the hyperbolic plane it is $\sinh r$, growing exponentially.

Last week's holonomy fits in too: transporting a vector around a small loop of area $A$ rotates it by about $KA$.

@example
title: Curvature of the unit sphere from the Christoffel symbols
problem: Use $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=\Gamma^\phi_{\phi\theta}=\cot\theta$ to compute $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}$, the Ricci tensor, the scalar curvature and $K$ of the unit sphere.
step: Take $\rho=\mu=\theta$ and $\sigma=\nu=\phi$ in the definition: $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}=\partial_\theta\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}-\partial_\phi\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\phi}+\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\phi\phi}-\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\theta\phi}$.
step: Evaluate the terms. $\partial_\theta(-\sin\theta\cos\theta)=\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\theta$. Also $\partial_\phi\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\phi}=0$, and $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta\lambda}=0$ for every $\lambda$. The last term has only $\lambda=\phi$: $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}=-\sin\theta\cos\theta\cdot\cot\theta=-\cos^2\theta$.
step: Add: $R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}=(\sin^2\theta-\cos^2\theta)-0+0-(-\cos^2\theta)=\sin^2\theta$.
step: Contract: $R_{\phi\phi}=R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}=\sin^2\theta$. A similar computation gives $R^\phi{}_{\theta\phi\theta}=\csc^2\theta-\cot^2\theta=1$, so $R_{\theta\theta}=1$.
step: Scalar curvature: $R=g^{\theta\theta}R_{\theta\theta}+g^{\phi\phi}R_{\phi\phi}=1+\dfrac{\sin^2\theta}{\sin^2\theta}=2$, so $K=R/2=1$.
step: Check with the surface formula: $K=R_{\theta\phi\theta\phi}/\det g=g_{\theta\theta}R^\theta{}_{\phi\theta\phi}/\sin^2\theta=1$. For a sphere of radius $a$ the metric is $a^2$ times larger and $K=1/a^2$.

@example
title: A cone is flat
problem: Find $K$ for the cone $ds^2=d\rho^2+\tfrac14\rho^2\,d\phi^2$ (half-angle $30^\circ$, so $\sin\alpha=\tfrac12$) and explain the result.
step: This has the form $dr^2+G(r)^2d\theta^2$ with $r=\rho$, $\theta=\phi$ and $G(\rho)=\tfrac12\rho$.
step: Then $G'=\tfrac12$ and $G''=0$, so $K=-G''/G=0$ for every $\rho>0$.
step: Interpretation: with $\psi=\tfrac12\phi$ the metric becomes $d\rho^2+\rho^2d\psi^2$, the flat plane in polar coordinates, but $\psi$ only runs over $[0,\pi)$. Cutting the cone along a line and unrolling it gives a half-disk of the flat plane.
step: The apex is a singular point where the total angle is only $\pi$ instead of $2\pi$. The formula $K=-G''/G$ only describes the smooth part of the cone.

@mistakes
- Concluding that a space is curved because some $\Gamma$ are nonzero. Polar coordinates on the plane have nonzero $\Gamma$ and zero curvature.
- Mixing up the order of terms in $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}$. The pattern is the $\mu$-derivative minus the $\nu$-derivative, then the product with $\mu$ minus the product with $\nu$.
- Contracting the wrong pair of indices. The Ricci tensor is $R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu}$, the first (upper) index with the third.
- Using the wrong sign in $K=-G''/G$, which gives a sphere negative curvature.
