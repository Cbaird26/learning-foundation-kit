@article
A tangent vector says "move this way, this fast." The natural thing to do with such a direction is to differentiate: how fast does a function $f$ change if you move that way? This week's idea is to **define** a tangent vector by what it does to functions. That definition needs no surrounding space, and it works in every chart.

### Tangent vectors as derivatives

At a point $p$, a tangent vector $v$ gives a directional derivative $v(f)$. If a curve through $p$ has velocity $v$, the chain rule gives

$$v(f)=\frac{d}{dt}f(\gamma(t))\Big|_{t=0}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\,\dot x^{\,i}.$$

Read this as an operator: $v=v^i\,\partial_i$, where $\partial_i=\partial/\partial x^i$ and $v^i=\dot x^i$ (repeated indices are summed). The $n$ operators $\partial_1,\dots,\partial_n$ form a basis of the **tangent space** $T_pM$, the set of all tangent vectors at $p$.

Change charts and the chain rule gives $\partial'_a=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\,\partial_i$. The operator $v=v^i\partial_i=v'^a\partial'_a$ is the same in every chart, so its components obey the law from last week:

$$v'^a=\frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}\,v^i.$$

### Covectors

A **covector** $\omega$ at $p$ is a linear map that takes a tangent vector and returns a number. The covectors at $p$ form the **cotangent space** $T^*_pM$, also of dimension $n$. The main example is the **differential** of a function:

$$df(v)=v(f),\qquad df=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i.$$

The covectors $dx^i$ are the basis dual to $\partial_i$, meaning $dx^i(\partial_j)=\delta^i_j$ (equal to $1$ if $i=j$, else $0$). A general covector is $\omega=\omega_i\,dx^i$, and feeding it a vector gives

$$\omega(v)=\omega_i\,v^i.$$

### How components transform

Because $\omega'_a=\omega(\partial'_a)$, the formula for $\partial'_a$ gives

$$\omega'_a=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\,\omega_i.$$

Compare with the vector law: the Jacobian factor is the **inverse** one, with old and new coordinates swapped. That is exactly what makes the pairing chart-independent:

$$\omega'_a v'^a=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^j}\,\omega_i v^j=\delta^i_j\,\omega_i v^j=\omega_i v^i.$$

| | vector | covector |
|---|---|---|
| Index | upper, $v^i$ | lower, $\omega_i$ |
| Basis | $\partial_i$ | $dx^i$ |
| Law | $v'^a=\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}\,v^i$ | $\omega'_a=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\,\omega_i$ |
| Typical example | velocity of a curve | differential $df$ of a function |

In Cartesian coordinates we often write the gradient as a column of numbers $\partial f/\partial x^i$ and treat it like a velocity. That is safe only because changes between Cartesian systems are rotations, where the matrix $A$ is orthogonal and $A^{-T}=A$, so the two laws coincide. In polar coordinates they differ, which is why we keep the two kinds of object apart.

@example
title: A vector acting on a function
problem: In the plane let $f=x^2y$ and let $v=2\partial_x-\partial_y$ at the point $(1,3)$. Compute $v(f)$, and then find the same number as $df(v)$.
step: Apply the operator: $v(f)=v^x\,\partial_xf+v^y\,\partial_yf$ with $v^x=2$ and $v^y=-1$.
step: The partial derivatives are $\partial_xf=2xy=6$ and $\partial_yf=x^2=1$ at $(1,3)$.
step: So $v(f)=2\cdot6+(-1)\cdot1=11$.
step: The differential at this point is $df=6\,dx+1\,dy$, a covector with components $(6,1)$. Pairing with $v$: $df(v)=6\cdot2+1\cdot(-1)=11$. The covector $df$ is the machine that turns a direction into a rate of change.

@example
title: A covector from Cartesian to polar components
problem: At $(x,y)=(3,4)$ let $\omega=-y\,dx+x\,dy$. Find its polar components $(\omega_r,\omega_\theta)$ and check that pairing with the vector $v=(1,2)$ gives the same number in both charts.
step: The Cartesian components at $(3,4)$ are $(\omega_x,\omega_y)=(-4,3)$.
step: Covectors use the factors $\partial x^i/\partial x'^a$, which are the entries of the polar Jacobian. At $r=5$, $\cos\theta=\tfrac35$, $\sin\theta=\tfrac45$ it is $J=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3/5&-4\\ 4/5&3\end{pmatrix}$, and $\omega'_a=J^i{}_a\,\omega_i$, that is $\omega'=J^T\omega$.
step: Compute $\omega_r=\tfrac35(-4)+\tfrac45(3)=0$ and $\omega_\theta=(-4)(-4)+3\cdot3=25$. So $\omega=25\,d\theta=r^2\,d\theta$ at this point.
step: The vector $v=(1,2)$ has polar components $(\tfrac{11}{5},\tfrac{2}{25})$. Then $\omega_rv^r+\omega_\theta v^\theta=0+25\cdot\tfrac{2}{25}=2$, and in Cartesian components $\omega_xv^x+\omega_yv^y=-4+6=2$. The pairing agrees.

@mistakes
- Transforming covector components with the vector Jacobian. Vectors use $\partial x'/\partial x$; covectors use $\partial x/\partial x'$.
- Reading $dx^i$ as a "tiny displacement" and forgetting that it is a covector: $dx^i(\partial_j)=\delta^i_j$.
- Assuming the gradient vector and $df$ have the same components in every chart. They agree in Cartesian coordinates only; elsewhere you need the metric (Week 5).

answer: (3, -5)
check: (lambda X: [sum(w*nderiv(lambda s: X(*([s, 0] if a == 0 else [0, s]))[i], 0) for i, w in enumerate([3, 1])) for a in range(2)])(lambda u, v: [u - 2*v, v])

@practice tuple
prompt: At the point $(x,y)=(0,2)$, which has polar coordinates $(r,\theta)=(2,\pi/2)$, a covector has polar components $(\omega_r,\omega_\theta)=(3,4)$. Find its Cartesian components $(\omega_x,\omega_y)$.
answer: (-2, 3)
check: [3*nderiv(lambda x: sqrt(x**2 + 4), 0) + 4*nderiv(lambda x: arctan2(2, x), 0), 3*nderiv(lambda y: sqrt(y**2), 2) + 4*nderiv(lambda y: arctan2(y, 0), 2)]
hint: Now go from polar to Cartesian, so the primed chart is the polar one, $x'^a=(r,\theta)$, and the unprimed chart is Cartesian, $x^i=(x,y)$. The Cartesian covector components are $\omega_i=\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}\,\omega'_a$, where $r,\theta$ are functions of $(x,y)$.
hint: $\partial r/\partial x=x/r$, $\partial r/\partial y=y/r$, $\partial\theta/\partial x=-y/r^2$, $\partial\theta/\partial y=x/r^2$. Evaluate at $(0,2)$.
solution: At $(0,2)$: $\partial_xr=0$, $\partial_yr=1$, $\partial_x\theta=-\tfrac12$, $\partial_y\theta=0$. So $\omega_x=0\cdot3+(-\tfrac12)\cdot4=-2$ and $\omega_y=1\cdot3+0\cdot4=3$. Check: $\partial_\theta=(-2,0)$ in Cartesian, so $\omega(\partial_\theta)=(-2)(-2)=4=\omega_\theta$, and $\partial_r=(0,1)$ gives $\omega(\partial_r)=3=\omega_r$.

@practice self
prompt: Using the laws $v'^a=\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}v^i$ and $\omega'_a=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\omega_i$, show that the number $\omega_iv^i$ is the same in every chart. Then explain in one or two sentences why the numbers $\partial f/\partial x^i$ are the components of a covector rather than of a vector.
hint: Multiply the two transformed quantities and sum over the new index $a$.
hint: The chain rule says $\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^j}=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x^j}=\delta^i_j$.
solution: $\omega'_av'^a=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\omega_i\,\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^j}v^j=\left(\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^j}\right)\omega_iv^j=\delta^i_j\,\omega_iv^j=\omega_iv^i$, since summing the chain-rule product over $a$ gives $\partial x^i/\partial x^j=\delta^i_j$. For the second part, the chain rule gives $\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x'^a}=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x^i}$, which is the covector law with the inverse Jacobian, not the vector law $v'^a=\dfrac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}v^i$. Equivalently, $df(v)=v(f)$ is a chart-independent number.
rubric: Substitutes both transformation laws into $\omega'_av'^a$ and groups the two Jacobian factors that share the summed index $a$.
rubric: Uses the chain rule to replace the product of the two Jacobians by $\delta^i_j$ and concludes $\omega'_av'^a=\omega_iv^i$.
rubric: Shows that $\partial f/\partial x'^a=(\partial x^i/\partial x'^a)\,\partial f/\partial x^i$ and identifies this as the covector law.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these lists of numbers transform like the components of a covector, $\omega'_a=\dfrac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\,\omega_i$?
choice*: The partial derivatives $\partial f/\partial x^i$ of a function $f$, the components of $df$.
choice: The velocity components $dx^i/dt$ of a moving particle.
choice*: The numbers $\omega_i=\omega(\partial_i)$ obtained by feeding the coordinate basis vectors into one fixed linear map $\omega$ from tangent vectors to numbers.
choice: The coordinates $(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ of a point, listed as a column.
hint: Ask whether each list measures a rate along a direction (a vector) or assigns a number to each direction (a covector).
solution: $\partial f/\partial x^i$ obey $\partial f/\partial x'^a=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\partial f/\partial x^i$, the covector law. The numbers $\omega(\partial_i)$ do too, because $\partial'_a=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial x'^a}\partial_i$ and $\omega$ is linear. The velocity components $dx^i/dt$ are vector components. The coordinates themselves are not components of any tangent vector: under a nonlinear change they transform by $x'^a(x)$, not by a Jacobian.

@quiz num
prompt: In the plane, the 1-form $\omega=y\,dx+x\,dy$ acts on a tangent vector $v$ at the point $(x,y)=(1,1)$ whose polar components there are $(v^r,v^\theta)=(2,1)$. Find $\omega(v)$.
answer: 2sqrt(2)
check: nderiv(lambda t: (sqrt(2) + 2*t)*cos(pi/4 + t)*(sqrt(2) + 2*t)*sin(pi/4 + t), 0)
hint: $\omega=d(xy)$, so $\omega(v)$ is the rate of change of $f=xy$ along $v$. In polar form $f=r^2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
hint: At $(1,1)$, $r=\sqrt2$ and $\theta=\pi/4$. Differentiate $f$ with respect to $r$ and $\theta$ there, and combine with $(v^r,v^\theta)=(2,1)$.
solution: $f=\tfrac12r^2\sin2\theta$, so $df=r\sin2\theta\,dr+r^2\cos2\theta\,d\theta$. At $r=\sqrt2$, $\theta=\pi/4$: $\sin2\theta=1$ and $\cos2\theta=0$, so $df=\sqrt2\,dr$. Then $\omega(v)=\sqrt2\cdot2=2\sqrt2$. Cartesian check: $v^x=\sqrt2-1$, $v^y=\sqrt2+1$, and $\omega(v)=y\,v^x+x\,v^y=2\sqrt2$.
