@article
So far we can differentiate and transform, but we cannot measure. The polar vector $(v^r,v^\theta)=(1,1)$ has no length until we say how to measure it, and last week the dot product turned into $\operatorname{diag}(1,r^2)$ in polar coordinates. A **metric** is a rule for measuring, supplied at every point.

### The metric tensor

A **Riemannian metric** is a symmetric, positive-definite $(0,2)$ tensor field $g$. It gives the inner product, length and angle:

$$\langle u,v\rangle=g_{ij}u^iv^j,\qquad |v|=\sqrt{g_{ij}v^iv^j},\qquad \cos\alpha=\frac{g(u,v)}{|u|\,|v|}.$$

We usually package $g$ as the **line element** $ds^2=g_{ij}\,dx^i\,dx^j$. Three examples:

- The plane: $ds^2=dx^2+dy^2$ in Cartesian coordinates and $ds^2=dr^2+r^2\,d\theta^2$ in polar coordinates.
- The sphere of radius $a$: $ds^2=a^2\,(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$.
- Any surface $\mathbf r(u,v)$ in space has the **induced metric** $g_{ij}=\mathbf r_i\cdot\mathbf r_j$, built from the tangent vectors of Week 1.

For the sphere, $\mathbf r_\theta\cdot\mathbf r_\theta=a^2$, $\mathbf r_\phi\cdot\mathbf r_\phi=a^2\sin^2\theta$ and $\mathbf r_\theta\cdot\mathbf r_\phi=0$, which gives the line element above.

### Lengths and areas

The length of a curve $x^i(t)$ is the integral of its speed:

$$L=\int_a^b\sqrt{g_{ij}\,\dot x^{\,i}\dot x^{\,j}}\;dt.$$

On a sphere of radius $a$, the latitude circle $\theta=\theta_0$ with $\phi=t$ has speed $a\sin\theta_0$, so $L=2\pi a\sin\theta_0$: shorter than the equator, as it should be. The area of a region is $\iint\sqrt{\det g}\;du\,dv$. For the sphere $\sqrt{\det g}=a^2\sin\theta$, and integrating over all angles gives the total area $4\pi a^2$.

### Raising and lowering indices

Because $g$ is invertible we can define the **inverse metric** $g^{ij}$ by $g^{ij}g_{jk}=\delta^i_k$. Then

$$v_i=g_{ij}\,v^j\quad(\text{lowering}),\qquad \omega^i=g^{ij}\,\omega_j\quad(\text{raising}).$$

The metric thus identifies vectors with covectors. This resolves the puzzle from Week 3: the **gradient vector** is $(\nabla f)^i=g^{ij}\partial_jf$, which in polar coordinates is $\partial_rf\,\partial_r+r^{-2}\,\partial_\theta f\,\partial_\theta$. It is not the same list of numbers as the covector $df$.

### Other signatures

In relativity the metric is **Lorentzian**, with signature $(-,+,+,+)$; for flat spacetime in units where the speed of light is $1$, $ds^2=-dt^2+dx^2+dy^2+dz^2$. Now $g(v,v)$ can be negative (timelike), zero (null) or positive (spacelike). Raising and lowering use the same formulas, but a sign can flip: lowering the index of $v^\mu=(2,1,0,0)$ gives $v_\mu=(-2,1,0,0)$. This course uses mostly Riemannian metrics, but the algebra carries over unchanged.

@example
title: The length of a circle of latitude
problem: On a sphere of radius $a=2$, find the length of the circle of latitude $\theta=\pi/3$, traversed once with $\phi$ increasing from $0$ to $2\pi$.
step: The metric is $ds^2=4\,(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$, so $g_{\theta\theta}=4$ and $g_{\phi\phi}=4\sin^2\theta$.
step: Along the curve $\theta=\pi/3$ is constant, so $\dot\theta=0$, and take $\phi=t$, so $\dot\phi=1$.
step: The squared speed is $g_{\phi\phi}\dot\phi^2=4\sin^2(\pi/3)=3$, so the speed is $\sqrt3$.
step: Integrate: $L=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt3\,dt=2\sqrt3\,\pi$.
step: Check: in space this is a circle of radius $a\sin\theta=2\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}=\sqrt3$, whose circumference is $2\pi\sqrt3$.

@example
title: A gradient vector in polar coordinates
problem: Let $f=y=r\sin\theta$. Find the gradient vector $\nabla f$ in the polar chart, and check it against the Cartesian answer.
step: The covector $df$ has polar components $(f_r,f_\theta)=(\sin\theta,\ r\cos\theta)$.
step: The inverse polar metric is $g^{ij}=\operatorname{diag}(1,\,1/r^2)$. Raising the index gives $(\nabla f)^r=\sin\theta$ and $(\nabla f)^\theta=\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}$.
step: Convert to Cartesian components with the polar Jacobian: $(\nabla f)^x=\cos\theta\sin\theta-r\sin\theta\cdot\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}=0$.
step: Similarly $(\nabla f)^y=\sin\theta\cdot\sin\theta+r\cos\theta\cdot\dfrac{\cos\theta}{r}=1$. The gradient of $y$ is $(0,1)$, as it must be.

@mistakes
- Treating $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2$ as a length. It is a squared length, so the vector $\partial_\theta$ has length $r$.
- Inverting a non-diagonal metric entry by entry. The inverse metric is the matrix inverse; only for a diagonal metric is $g^{ii}=1/g_{ii}$.
- Dropping the square root in $L=\int\sqrt{g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}\,dt$ or in the area element $\sqrt{\det g}$.

answer: 5
check: sqrt(nderiv(lambda t: (2 + 3*t)*cos(0.7 + 2*t), 0)**2 + nderiv(lambda t: (2 + 3*t)*sin(0.7 + 2*t), 0)**2)
hint: Because the metric is diagonal, $|v|^2=g_{rr}(v^r)^2+g_{\theta\theta}(v^\theta)^2$.
hint: At $r=2$, $g_{rr}=1$ and $g_{\theta\theta}=r^2=4$.

@practice num
prompt: A curve in the plane is given in polar coordinates by $r(t)=e^t$, $\theta(t)=t$ for $0\le t\le\ln 2$. Find its length.
answer: sqrt(2)
check: quad(lambda t: sqrt(nderiv(lambda s: exp(s)*cos(s), t)**2 + nderiv(lambda s: exp(s)*sin(s), t)**2), 0, ln(2))
hint: The length is $\int\sqrt{g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j}\,dt=\int\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2}\,dt$.
hint: Here $\dot r=e^t$, $r=e^t$ and $\dot\theta=1$, so the integrand is a constant multiple of $e^t$.
solution: $\sqrt{\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2}=\sqrt{e^{2t}+e^{2t}}=\sqrt2\,e^t$. Then $L=\sqrt2\int_0^{\ln2}e^t\,dt=\sqrt2\,(2-1)=\sqrt2$.

@practice tuple
prompt: In a chart $(x^1,x^2)$ a metric has components $g_{ij}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&3\end{pmatrix}$. A covector has components $(\omega_1,\omega_2)=(5,5)$. Find the components $(\omega^1,\omega^2)$ of the corresponding vector, $\omega^i=g^{ij}\omega_j$.
answer: (2, 1)
check: list(np.linalg.solve(np.array([[2, 1], [1, 3]]), np.array([5, 5])))
hint: The inverse of $\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ is $\dfrac{1}{ad-bc}\begin{pmatrix}d&-b\\-c&a\end{pmatrix}$.
hint: Here $\det g=5$ and $g^{ij}=\tfrac15\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-1&2\end{pmatrix}$. Multiply it by $(5,5)^T$.
solution: $g^{ij}\omega_j=\tfrac15\,(3\cdot5-1\cdot5,\ -1\cdot5+2\cdot5)=\tfrac15\,(10,5)=(2,1)$. Check by lowering again: $g_{ij}\omega^j=(2\cdot2+1\cdot1,\ 1\cdot2+3\cdot1)=(5,5)$.

vars: u
lead: $\det g =$
answer: u^2*(1 + 4u^2)
check: lambda u: (lambda ru, rv: np.dot(ru, ru)*np.dot(rv, rv) - np.dot(ru, rv)**2)([nderiv(lambda s: [s*cos(0.3), s*sin(0.3), s**2][k], u) for k in range(3)], [nderiv(lambda w: [u*cos(w), u*sin(w), u**2][k], 0.3) for k in range(3)])

@quiz num
prompt: Find the area of the zone of a sphere of radius $2$ between the latitudes $\theta=\pi/3$ and $\theta=2\pi/3$, using the area element $\sqrt{\det g}\,d\theta\,d\phi$.
answer: 8pi
check: quad2(lambda th, ph: sqrt(np.linalg.det(np.array([[4, 0], [0, 4*sin(th)**2]]))), pi/3, 2*pi/3, 0, 2*pi)
hint: For $ds^2=4\,(d\theta^2+\sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2)$, $\det g=16\sin^2\theta$, so $\sqrt{\det g}=4\sin\theta$ for $0<\theta<\pi$.
hint: Integrate $4\sin\theta$ over $\theta\in[\pi/3,2\pi/3]$ and $\phi\in[0,2\pi]$.
solution: $A=\int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_{\pi/3}^{2\pi/3}4\sin\theta\,d\theta\,d\phi=2\pi\cdot4\,\big[-\cos\theta\big]_{\pi/3}^{2\pi/3}=8\pi\left(\tfrac12+\tfrac12\right)=8\pi$.
