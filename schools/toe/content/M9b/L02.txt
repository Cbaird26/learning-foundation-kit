@article
You already name the same point of the plane in two ways: Cartesian $(x,y)$ and polar $(r,\theta)$. Differential geometry makes this precise and asks the key question: what stays the same when you switch?

### Charts

A **chart** on a region $U$ of a surface (or of space) assigns to each point of $U$ a list of numbers, its **coordinates**, so that the assignment is smooth and invertible with a smooth inverse. On the plane, $(x,y)$ is a chart. The relations $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ make $(r,\theta)$ a chart too, but only away from the origin: at $r=0$ every value of $\theta$ gives the same point. This kind of failure is a **coordinate singularity**. The plane is fine; the chart is what breaks.

Maps of the Earth show why charts come in families. No single flat map covers the whole globe without a cut, so an atlas uses several overlapping maps. The sphere behaves the same way: the $(\theta,\phi)$ chart fails at the poles, and in fact no single chart can cover a whole sphere. Two suffice. Stereographic projection from the north pole $(0,0,1)$ uses

$$(X,Y)=\left(\frac{x}{1-z},\ \frac{y}{1-z}\right),$$

which works everywhere except the north pole itself. Projecting from the south pole covers the rest.

### Transition maps and Jacobians

Where two charts overlap, the **transition map** turns the coordinates $x^i$ of one chart into the coordinates $x'^a$ of the other. Its derivative is the **Jacobian matrix** $\partial x'^a/\partial x^i$, with row index $a$ and column index $i$. For polar to Cartesian,

$$\frac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(r,\theta)}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det=r.$$

The determinant vanishes exactly at the singular point $r=0$. The Jacobian of the reverse transition is the matrix inverse.

### One vector, two sets of components

Write a curve in the old chart as $x^i(t)$. In the new chart its coordinates are $x'^a(x^1(t),\dots,x^n(t))$, and the chain rule gives the velocity components

$$\dot x'^{\,a}=\frac{\partial x'^a}{\partial x^i}\,\dot x^{\,i}.$$

(A repeated index is summed.) So **the Jacobian converts the components of a tangent vector from the old chart to the new chart.** The arrow has not changed; only the numbers describing it have.

Look at the columns of the polar Jacobian at $(r,\theta)=(2,\pi/2)$. The $r$-column is $(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)=(0,1)$: one unit of $r$ moves you one unit up. The $\theta$-column is $(-r\sin\theta,\ r\cos\theta)=(-2,0)$: one unit of $\theta$ moves you two units to the left. Each column is the velocity of a coordinate curve. The polar coordinate directions are not unit vectors, and their lengths change from point to point.

@example
title: From polar components to Cartesian components
problem: At the point with polar coordinates $(r,\theta)=(2,\pi/2)$, a tangent vector has polar components $(v^r,v^\theta)=(1,3)$. Find its Cartesian components $(v^x,v^y)$.
step: Differentiate the transition map $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ to get the Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Evaluate it at $r=2$, $\theta=\pi/2$, where $\cos\theta=0$ and $\sin\theta=1$: $J=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Multiply: $\begin{pmatrix}v^x\\ v^y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-2\\ 1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ 3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-6\\ 1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Picture check: the point is $(0,2)$. One unit of $r$ goes straight up, giving $(0,1)$. Three units of $\theta$ go left by $3\cdot 2=6$, giving $(-6,0)$. The sum is $(-6,1)$.

@mistakes
- Using the Jacobian in the wrong direction, for example applying $\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta)$ to Cartesian components instead of polar components.
- Evaluating the Jacobian before choosing the point. Its entries depend on $r$ and $\theta$, so substitute the point first.
- Treating $v^\theta$ as a speed. It is an angular rate, and the corresponding arrow has length $r\,|v^\theta|$ in the plane.

@practice num
prompt: Parabolic coordinates $(\sigma,\tau)$ on the plane are defined by $x=\tfrac12(\sigma^2-\tau^2)$, $y=\sigma\tau$. Find the Jacobian determinant $\det\dfrac{\partial(x,y)}{\partial(\sigma,\tau)}$ at $(\sigma,\tau)=(2,1)$.
answer: 5
check: (lambda F: np.linalg.det(np.array([[nderiv(lambda s: F(s, 1)[a], 2), nderiv(lambda t: F(2, t)[a], 1)] for a in range(2)])))(lambda s, t: [(s**2 - t**2)/2, s*t])
hint: Write the $2\times2$ matrix with rows $(x_\sigma, x_\tau)$ and $(y_\sigma, y_\tau)$.
hint: $x_\sigma=\sigma$, $x_\tau=-\tau$, $y_\sigma=\tau$, $y_\tau=\sigma$. Substitute $(2,1)$ and take the determinant.
solution: $J=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma&-\tau\\ \tau&\sigma\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2&-1\\ 1&2\end{pmatrix}$ at $(2,1)$, so $\det J=\sigma^2+\tau^2=4+1=5$.

@practice tuple
prompt: At the point $(x,y)=(3,4)$ a tangent vector has Cartesian components $(v^x,v^y)=(1,2)$. Find its polar components $(v^r,v^\theta)$, with $\theta$ measured in radians.
answer: (11/5, 2/25)
check: [nderiv(lambda t: sqrt((3+t)**2 + (4+2*t)**2), 0), nderiv(lambda t: arctan2(4+2*t, 3+t), 0)]
hint: Use the Jacobian $\partial(r,\theta)/\partial(x,y)$, built from $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ and $\theta=\arctan(y/x)$, evaluated at $(3,4)$ where $r=5$.
hint: $\partial r/\partial x=x/r$, $\partial r/\partial y=y/r$, $\partial\theta/\partial x=-y/r^2$, $\partial\theta/\partial y=x/r^2$.
hint: At $(3,4)$ the matrix is $\begin{pmatrix}3/5&4/5\\ -4/25&3/25\end{pmatrix}$. Multiply it by $(1,2)^T$.
solution: $v^r=\tfrac35\cdot1+\tfrac45\cdot2=\tfrac{11}{5}$ and $v^\theta=-\tfrac{4}{25}\cdot1+\tfrac{3}{25}\cdot2=\tfrac{2}{25}$. Check by converting back: $v^x=\cos\theta\,v^r-r\sin\theta\,v^\theta=\tfrac35\cdot\tfrac{11}{5}-4\cdot\tfrac{2}{25}=1$.

@practice tuple
prompt: A curve is given in the polar chart by $r(t)=1+t$ and $\theta(t)=\tfrac{\pi}{2}t$. Find its velocity components $(\dot x,\dot y)$ in the Cartesian chart at $t=1$.
answer: (-pi, 1)

hint: At $t=1$: $r=2$, $\theta=\pi/2$, $\dot r=1$ and $\dot\theta=\pi/2$. These are the velocity components in the polar chart.
hint: Multiply the polar Jacobian $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ by $(\dot r,\dot\theta)^T$.

prompt: A spiral is given in the polar chart by $r(t)=t$, $\theta(t)=t$ for $t>0$. (a) Write its velocity components in the polar chart. (b) Write the curve in the Cartesian chart and differentiate to get its Cartesian velocity. (c) Show that (a) and (b) are related by the polar Jacobian. (d) At $t=2$, compare the Cartesian speed with the Euclidean size of the polar components $\sqrt{\dot r^2+\dot\theta^2}$, and explain the difference.
hint: In (c), build the Jacobian $\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta)$ with $r=t$ and $\theta=t$, then multiply it by the vector from (a).
hint: For (d), the Cartesian speed is $\sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2}$. Simplify it using $\cos^2t+\sin^2t=1$.
solution: (a) $(\dot r,\dot\theta)=(1,1)$. (b) $x=t\cos t$, $y=t\sin t$, so $(\dot x,\dot y)=(\cos t-t\sin t,\ \sin t+t\cos t)$. (c) With $r=t$ the Jacobian is $\begin{pmatrix}\cos t&-t\sin t\\ \sin t&t\cos t\end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying it by $(1,1)^T$ gives exactly the vector in (b). (d) $\dot x^2+\dot y^2=1+t^2$, so the Cartesian speed at $t=2$ is $\sqrt5\approx 2.24$, while $\sqrt{\dot r^2+\dot\theta^2}=\sqrt2\approx1.41$. @quiz tuple
prompt: On the unit sphere let $(\theta,\phi)$ be the usual spherical chart and $(X,Y)=\left(\dfrac{x}{1-z},\ \dfrac{y}{1-z}\right)$ the stereographic chart from the north pole. At the point with $(\theta,\phi)=(\pi/2,\,0)$ a tangent vector has components $(v^\theta,v^\phi)=(1,2)$. Find its components $(v^X,v^Y)$ in the stereographic chart.
answer: (-1, 2)
check: [nderiv(lambda t: sin(pi/2+t)*cos(2*t)/(1-cos(pi/2+t)), 0), nderiv(lambda t: sin(pi/2+t)*sin(2*t)/(1-cos(pi/2+t)), 0)]
hint: Write $X$ and $Y$ in terms of the angles: $X=\dfrac{\sin\theta\cos\phi}{1-\cos\theta}$ and $Y=\dfrac{\sin\theta\sin\phi}{1-\cos\theta}$.
hint: The Jacobian has entries $\partial X/\partial\theta$, $\partial X/\partial\phi$, $\partial Y/\partial\theta$, $\partial Y/\partial\phi$ at $(\pi/2,0)$, where $\sin\phi=0$ and $\cos\phi=1$.
solution: Using $\dfrac{d}{d\theta}\dfrac{\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}=-\dfrac{1}{1-\cos\theta}$, at $(\pi/2,0)$ we get $\partial_\theta X=-1$, $\partial_\phi X=-\dfrac{\sin\theta\sin\phi}{1-\cos\theta}=0$, $\partial_\theta Y=0$ (it carries a factor $\sin\phi$) and $\partial_\phi Y=\dfrac{\sin\theta\cos\phi}{1-\cos\theta}=1$. So $J=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\ 0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $(v^X,v^Y)=(-1,2)$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the polar chart $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ on the plane are true?
choice*: The Jacobian determinant $\det\partial(x,y)/\partial(r,\theta)$ equals $r$.
choice: The chart is valid on the whole plane, including the origin.
choice*: Curves of constant $r$ are circles centred at the origin, and curves of constant $\theta$ are rays from the origin.
choice*: The tangent vector $\partial(x,y)/\partial\theta$ has Euclidean length $r$.
choice: Because $\theta$ and $\theta+2\pi$ describe the same point, $\theta$ can range over all real numbers and each point still has exactly one coordinate pair.
hint: Compute the two columns of the Jacobian and their lengths, and recall what happens at $r=0$.
solution: The determinant of $\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-r\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&r\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$ is $r$; the second column has length $r$; level curves of $r$ and $\theta$ are circles and rays. The chart fails at the origin. And allowing all real $\theta$ gives each point infinitely many coordinate pairs, so $\theta$ must be restricted to an interval of length $2\pi$.
