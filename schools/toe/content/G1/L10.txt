@article
**Setting.** An **integral equation** contains the unknown $\phi$ under an integral sign. Conventions: $K(x,y)$ is the **kernel**, $\lambda$ is a parameter, and $(K\phi)(x)=\int_a^bK(x,y)\phi(y)\,dy$ acts on $L^2(a,b)$ with weight $1$. The inner product is $\langle f,g\rangle=\int\bar f g\,dx$.

- **Fredholm, second kind:** $\phi-\lambda K\phi=f$.
- **Fredholm, first kind:** $K\phi=f$. This is typically ill-posed: for smooth $K$ a small change in $f$ can require a huge change in $\phi$.
- **Volterra, second kind:** $\phi(x)-\lambda\int_a^xK(x,y)\phi(y)\,dy=f(x)$, with a variable upper limit.

### Where they come from

A boundary value problem becomes a Fredholm equation through its Green function: $\mathcal Lu=\lambda u$ with homogeneous BCs is equivalent to $u=\lambda\int G(x,\xi)u(\xi)\,d\xi$. An initial value problem becomes a Volterra equation by integrating. Integral operators with square-integrable kernels are compact, so their spectrum behaves like a matrix's.

### Separable kernels

If $K(x,y)=\sum_{i=1}^na_i(x)b_i(y)$, then $\phi=f+\lambda\sum_ic_ia_i$ with $c_i=\int b_i\phi\,dy$. Multiplying by $b_j$ and integrating gives the $n\times n$ system
$$(I-\lambda M)\,c=d,\qquad M_{ji}=\int b_ja_i\,dy,\quad d_j=\int b_jf\,dy .$$

### Neumann series

Iterating $\phi=f+\lambda K\phi$ gives $\phi=\sum_{n\ge0}\lambda^nK^nf$, which converges if $|\lambda|\,\|K\|<1$. A convenient bound is $\|K\|\le\big(\iint|K|^2dx\,dy\big)^{1/2}$. For a Volterra kernel the series converges for **every** $\lambda$.

### The Fredholm alternative

For a compact operator $K$ and a given $\lambda$, exactly one of the following holds:

1. $\phi-\lambda K\phi=f$ has a unique solution for every $f$.
2. The homogeneous equation has a nontrivial solution; then the inhomogeneous equation is solvable if and only if $f$ is orthogonal to every solution of the adjoint homogeneous equation $\psi-\bar\lambda K^\dagger\psi=0$.

The values of $\lambda$ in case 2 are the **characteristic values**.

### Symmetric kernels

If $K(x,y)=\overline{K(y,x)}$ and $K$ is square integrable, the eigenvalues $\mu_n$ of $K$ (with $K\phi_n=\mu_n\phi_n$) are real, the eigenfunctions are orthogonal and may be taken orthonormal, and the characteristic values are $\lambda_n=1/\mu_n$. The solution is
$$\phi=f+\lambda\sum_n\frac{\mu_n\langle\phi_n,f\rangle}{1-\lambda\mu_n}\,\phi_n .$$
For $K=G$, the Green function of $\mathcal L$, the eigenvalues of $K$ are the reciprocals of the eigenvalues of $\mathcal L$: for $-d^2/dx^2$ with Dirichlet conditions on $[0,1]$, $\mu_n=1/(n\pi)^2$.

### Numerical solution (Nyström)

Replace the integral by a quadrature rule with nodes $x_j$ and weights $h_j$, then solve a linear system.

```python
import numpy as np
n = 201
x = (np.arange(n) + 0.5)/n            # midpoint nodes on [0,1], weight 1/n
K = np.outer(x, x)                    # kernel K(x,y) = x*y
lam = 1.5
phi = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(n) - lam*K/n, np.ones(n))
print(phi[n//2])                      # value at x = 0.5
```

@example
title: A separable kernel and the Fredholm alternative
problem: Solve $\phi(x)=1+\lambda\int_0^1xy\,\phi(y)\,dy$, and discuss $\lambda=3$.
step: The kernel is $a(x)b(y)$ with $a=x$ and $b=y$, so $\phi(x)=1+\lambda c\,x$ with $c=\int_0^1y\,\phi(y)\,dy$.
step: Substitute: $c=\int_0^1y\,dy+\lambda c\int_0^1y^2dy=\tfrac12+\tfrac\lambda3c$.
step: Solve for $c$: $c=\dfrac{1/2}{1-\lambda/3}=\dfrac{3}{2(3-\lambda)}$ for $\lambda\ne3$. Hence $\phi(x)=1+\dfrac{3\lambda}{2(3-\lambda)}\,x$.
step: Check with $\lambda=1$: $\phi=1+\tfrac34x$, and $\int_0^1y(1+\tfrac34y)dy=\tfrac12+\tfrac14=\tfrac34=c$.
step: At $\lambda=3$ the homogeneous equation has the solution $\phi=x$, since $\int_0^1y\cdot y\,dy=\tfrac13$. The kernel is symmetric, so solvability requires $\int_0^1x\,f(x)\,dx=0$; here $\int_0^1x\,dx=\tfrac12\ne0$, so there is no solution.

@example
title: A Volterra equation by iteration
problem: Solve $\phi(x)=1+\int_0^x\phi(y)\,dy$.
step: Iterate from $\phi_0=1$: $\phi_1=1+\int_0^x1\,dy=1+x$.
step: Next, $\phi_2=1+\int_0^x(1+y)\,dy=1+x+\tfrac{x^2}2$, and in general $\phi_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\dfrac{x^k}{k!}$.
step: The limit is $\phi(x)=e^x$. The series converges for every $x$, as always for a Volterra equation.
step: Check by differentiation: $\phi'=\phi$ and $\phi(0)=1$, which is the initial value problem the integral equation encodes.

@mistakes
- Treating a first-kind equation $K\phi=f$ like a second-kind one; the solution may not exist, or may depend wildly on $f$.
- Dropping the condition $\lambda\ne$ characteristic value in a separable-kernel solution, so that a division by $1-\lambda/3$ goes unnoticed.
- Assuming the Neumann series converges for large $|\lambda|$ in a Fredholm equation (it need not), or forgetting that it always converges for Volterra equations.
- Confusing the eigenvalue $\mu$ of the integral operator with the characteristic value $\lambda=1/\mu$ of the equation, and the eigenvalues of $G$ with those of $\mathcal L$.

@practice num
prompt: For the kernel $K(x,y)=xy$ on $[0,1]$, the Neumann series for $\phi-\lambda K\phi=f$ converges exactly for $|\lambda|<R$. Find $R$.
answer: 3
check: 1/quad(lambda y: y*y, 0, 1)
tol: 0.001
hint: The series converges when $|\lambda|\,\|K\|<1$, where the relevant size is the largest eigenvalue $\mu_1$ of $K$.
hint: A rank-one kernel $a(x)b(y)$ has the single nonzero eigenvalue $\int ba\,dx$.
solution: $K\phi=x\int y\phi(y)\,dy$ has the single nonzero eigenvalue $\mu=\int_0^1y\cdot y\,dy=\tfrac13$ with eigenfunction $x$. The series converges iff $|\lambda|\mu<1$, so $R=3$. (The Hilbert–Schmidt norm $\big(\iint x^2y^2\big)^{1/2}=\tfrac13$ gives the same bound here.)

choice*: For a Volterra kernel it converges for every $\lambda$; for a Fredholm kernel it converges only for $|\lambda|$ small enough, in general.

hint: Compare $\|K^n\|$ for a Volterra kernel on $[0,1]$, which is bounded by $M^n/(n-1)!$-type terms, with $\|K\|^n$ for a Fredholm kernel.
solution: A bounded Volterra kernel has $\|K^n\|\lesssim M^n/(n-1)!$, which decays factorially, so the series converges for all $\lambda$.
