@article
**Conventions (read first).** For a function on $\mathbb R$, the **Fourier transform** and its inverse are
$$\hat f(k)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,e^{-ikx}\,dx,\qquad f(x)=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat f(k)\,e^{ikx}\,dk .$$
Other books put $1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ in both directions or use $e^{-2\pi ikx}$, and every formula below changes accordingly. For time and frequency, physicists often flip the sign of the exponent: week 6 used $f(t)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\tilde f(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}$, which replaces $i$ by $-i$ in the derivative rule. The **Laplace transform** is
$$F(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)\,e^{-st}\,dt,\qquad \operatorname{Re}s>\sigma_0,$$
where $\sigma_0$ is the abscissa of convergence.

### Why transforms work

Exponentials are eigenfunctions of differentiation and translation: $\frac{d}{dx}e^{ikx}=ik\,e^{ikx}$. After transforming, a constant-coefficient linear equation becomes an algebraic equation; solve it and invert.

### Rules

| operation | Fourier | Laplace |
|---|---|---|
| first derivative | $\widehat{f'}=ik\,\hat f$ | $\mathcal L[f']=sF-f(0)$ |
| second derivative | $\widehat{f''}=-k^2\hat f$ | $\mathcal L[f'']=s^2F-sf(0)-f'(0)$ |
| translation by $a$ | $\widehat{f(x-a)}=e^{-ika}\hat f$ | $\mathcal L[f(t-a)\Theta(t-a)]=e^{-as}F$ |
| multiply by the variable | $\widehat{xf}=i\,\hat f'$ | $\mathcal L[tf]=-F'$ |
| exponential factor | $\widehat{e^{iax}f}=\hat f(k-a)$ | $\mathcal L[e^{at}f]=F(s-a)$ |
| convolution | $\widehat{f*g}=\hat f\,\hat g$ | $\mathcal L[f*g]=F\,G$ |

Convolution means $(f*g)(x)=\int f(y)\,g(x-y)\,dy$ for Fourier and $\int_0^tf(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau$ for Laplace.

### Standard pairs

For $a>0$: $e^{-a|x|}\leftrightarrow\dfrac{2a}{a^2+k^2}$ and $e^{-ax^2}\leftrightarrow\sqrt{\dfrac\pi a}\,e^{-k^2/(4a)}$. For Laplace: $t^n\to\dfrac{n!}{s^{n+1}}$, $e^{at}\to\dfrac1{s-a}$, $\sin\omega t\to\dfrac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2}$, $\cos\omega t\to\dfrac{s}{s^2+\omega^2}$.

### Plancherel and the width trade-off

For $f\in L^2$,
$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f|^2\,dx=\frac1{2\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\hat f|^2\,dk .$$
A narrow $f$ has a broad $\hat f$ and conversely: for the Gaussian above, the width of $f$ scales as $a^{-1/2}$ and that of $\hat f$ as $a^{1/2}$.

### The heat equation on the line

For $u_t=D\,u_{xx}$ with $u(x,0)=u_0(x)$, transform in $x$: $\hat u_t=-Dk^2\hat u$, so $\hat u(k,t)=\hat u_0(k)\,e^{-Dk^2t}$. By the convolution theorem $u=K_t*u_0$ with the heat kernel $K_t(x)=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}$. Every Fourier mode decays at its own rate $Dk^2$, which is why diffusion smooths.

### Inverting the Laplace transform

The Bromwich integral is $f(t)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}F(s)e^{st}\,ds$ with $c>\sigma_0$. For $t>0$, close to the left (week 6) and obtain $f(t)=\sum\operatorname{Res}\big[F(s)e^{st}\big]$ over the singularities of $F$. In practice use partial fractions and a table.

### Domains

The transform is defined directly for $f\in L^1(\mathbb R)$, extends to $L^2$ by density (Plancherel), and, in week 8, to distributions. Differentiation rules assume enough decay that boundary terms vanish.

@example
title: Fourier transform of an exponential, then Plancherel
problem: Find $\hat f$ for $f(x)=e^{-a|x|}$ with $a>0$, and use Plancherel's theorem to evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{dk}{(a^2+k^2)^2}$.
step: Since $f$ is even, $\hat f(k)=2\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\cos kx\,dx$.
step: Using $\int_0^\infty e^{-ax}\cos kx\,dx=\dfrac{a}{a^2+k^2}$, we get $\hat f(k)=\dfrac{2a}{a^2+k^2}$.
step: The left side of Plancherel is $\int|f|^2dx=2\int_0^\infty e^{-2ax}dx=\dfrac1a$.
step: The right side is $\dfrac1{2\pi}\int\dfrac{4a^2}{(a^2+k^2)^2}dk=\dfrac{2a^2}{\pi}\int\dfrac{dk}{(a^2+k^2)^2}$.
step: Equate: $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\dfrac{dk}{(a^2+k^2)^2}=\dfrac{\pi}{2a^3}$.

@example
title: A Gaussian under the heat equation
problem: Solve $u_t=D\,u_{xx}$ on $\mathbb R$ with $u(x,0)=e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$.
step: The initial transform: with $a=1/(2\sigma^2)$, $\hat u_0(k)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\,e^{-\sigma^2k^2/2}$.
step: The evolution multiplies by $e^{-Dk^2t}$: $\hat u(k,t)=\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma\,e^{-(\sigma^2+2Dt)k^2/2}$.
step: This has the same form as $\hat u_0$ with $\sigma^2$ replaced by $\sigma_t^2=\sigma^2+2Dt$, except that the prefactor still contains $\sigma$.
step: Invert using the standard pair: $u(x,t)=\dfrac{\sigma}{\sigma_t}\,e^{-x^2/(2\sigma_t^2)}$.
step: Check: $\int u\,dx=\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$ is independent of $t$, so the total heat is conserved, and the width grows as $\sigma_t^2=\sigma^2+2Dt$.

@mistakes
- Mixing conventions: using the $\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}$ or $e^{+ikx}$ forward formulas together with this lesson's rules.
- Dropping the initial-value terms in $\mathcal L[f'']=s^2F-sf(0)-f'(0)$, or putting them in the wrong order.
- Forgetting that the Laplace translation rule needs the factor $\Theta(t-a)$.
- Inverting a Laplace transform by partial fractions without checking that the numerator degree is lower than the denominator degree.

@quiz num
prompt: Use Laplace transforms to solve $y''+y=\cos t$ with $y(0)=0$ and $y'(0)=0$. Find $y(\pi/2)$ exactly.
answer: pi/4
check: ivp(lambda t, y: [y[1], cos(t) - y[0]], [0, 0], 0, pi/2)[0]
hint: Transform: $(s^2+1)Y=\dfrac{s}{s^2+1}$, so $Y=\dfrac{s}{(s^2+1)^2}$.
hint: Write $Y=\dfrac1{s^2+1}\cdot\dfrac{s}{s^2+1}$ and use the convolution of $\sin t$ and $\cos t$, or the rule $\mathcal L[tf]=-F'$.
solution: $Y=\dfrac{s}{(s^2+1)^2}=-\tfrac12\dfrac{d}{ds}\dfrac1{s^2+1}$, so $y=\tfrac12t\sin t$ using $\mathcal L[tf]=-F'$. Then $y(\pi/2)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac\pi2=\tfrac\pi4$.

choice*: $\mathcal L[f'']=s^2F(s)-s\,f(0)-f'(0)$
choice: $\mathcal L[f'']=s^2F(s)-f(0)-f'(0)$
