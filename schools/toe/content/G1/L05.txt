@article
**Conventions.** Write $z=x+iy$. A function $f=u+iv$ is **analytic** (holomorphic) on an open set if it has a complex derivative at every point there. A contour is a piecewise smooth curve $z(t)$, $\alpha\le t\le\beta$, and
$$\int_Cf(z)\,dz=\int_\alpha^\beta f(z(t))\,z'(t)\,dt .$$
$\oint_C$ always means a simple closed contour traversed **counterclockwise** (positively) unless stated otherwise.

### Analyticity

The **Cauchy–Riemann equations** $u_x=v_y$ and $u_y=-v_x$ are necessary for analyticity, and sufficient when the partial derivatives are continuous. The function $\bar z$ is nowhere analytic. An analytic function is infinitely differentiable and locally equal to its Taylor series.

### Cauchy's theorem and integral formulas

If $f$ is analytic on a simply connected domain containing the closed contour $C$, then $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$, and integrals between two points are path independent. If $a$ lies inside a positively oriented simple closed contour $C$ and $f$ is analytic on and inside $C$,
$$f^{(n)}(a)=\frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz,\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots$$
The basic building block: for a circle $|z-a|=r$, $\oint(z-a)^n\,dz=2\pi i$ if $n=-1$ and $0$ for every other integer $n$.

### Laurent series, singularities, residues

Near an isolated singularity $z_0$ we have a Laurent series valid in an annulus $0<|z-z_0|<R$:
$$f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n\,(z-z_0)^n .$$
If there are no negative powers the singularity is **removable**; if the most negative power is $-m$ it is a **pole of order $m$**; if there are infinitely many negative powers it is **essential**. The **residue** is $\operatorname{Res}_{z_0}f=c_{-1}$.

How to compute it:

- Simple pole: $\operatorname{Res}=\lim_{z\to z_0}(z-z_0)f(z)$. If $f=g/h$ with $g(z_0)\ne0$, $h(z_0)=0$, $h'(z_0)\ne0$, then $\operatorname{Res}=g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$.
- Pole of order $m$: $\operatorname{Res}=\dfrac1{(m-1)!}\displaystyle\lim_{z\to z_0}\frac{d^{m-1}}{dz^{m-1}}\big[(z-z_0)^mf(z)\big]$.
- Essential singularity: expand and read off $c_{-1}$.

### The residue theorem

If $f$ is analytic on and inside $C$ except for finitely many isolated singularities $z_k$ inside $C$, none on $C$, then
$$\oint_Cf\,dz=2\pi i\sum_k\operatorname{Res}_{z_k}f .$$
Always list the singularities first and decide which lie **inside** the contour; singularities outside contribute nothing, and one on the contour makes the theorem inapplicable.

### Branches

Multivalued functions need a convention. The **principal logarithm** is $\operatorname{Log}z=\ln|z|+i\operatorname{Arg}z$ with $-\pi<\operatorname{Arg}z\le\pi$, which puts the branch cut on the negative real axis, and $z^a=e^{a\operatorname{Log}z}$. The function is analytic on the cut plane, and a contour may not cross the cut.

### The ML inequality

If $|f|\le M$ on $C$ and $C$ has length $L$, then $\big|\int_Cf\,dz\big|\le ML$. This is the tool that justifies discarding large arcs in week 6.

@example
title: A simple pole and a double pole
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{|z|=3}\dfrac{e^z}{z\,(z-1)^2}\,dz$.
step: The singularities are $z=0$ (simple pole) and $z=1$ (double pole). Both satisfy $|z|<3$, so both lie inside the contour.
step: At the simple pole: $\operatorname{Res}_{0}=\lim_{z\to0}\dfrac{e^z}{(z-1)^2}=1$.
step: At the double pole, differentiate $(z-1)^2f(z)=e^z/z$: $\operatorname{Res}_{1}=\dfrac{d}{dz}\dfrac{e^z}{z}\Big|_{z=1}=\dfrac{e^z(z-1)}{z^2}\Big|_{z=1}=0$.
step: The residue theorem gives $\oint=2\pi i\,(1+0)=2\pi i$.

@example
title: Only one pole inside
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{|z-i|=1}\dfrac{dz}{z^2+1}$.
step: Factor $z^2+1=(z-i)(z+i)$. The poles are $\pm i$.
step: The circle is centered at $i$ with radius $1$. The pole $z=i$ is at its center, inside. The pole $z=-i$ is at distance $2$ from the center, outside.
step: Since $z^2+1=h(z)$ has $h'(i)=2i$, the residue at $i$ is $\dfrac{1}{2i}$.
step: Therefore $\oint=2\pi i\cdot\dfrac1{2i}=\pi$. Check with Cauchy's formula: $f(z)=1/(z+i)$ is analytic inside and $2\pi i\,f(i)=2\pi i/(2i)=\pi$.

@mistakes
- Including poles that lie outside the contour, or forgetting that the contour of $|z-i|=1$ is not centered at the origin.
- Computing the residue at a double pole as $\lim(z-z_0)f(z)$ (which diverges), or forgetting the factor $\frac1{(m-1)!}$ and the derivative.
- Forgetting the factor $2\pi i$, or reversing the sign for a clockwise contour.
- Applying Cauchy's theorem to a function such as $\bar z$ or $|z|^2$ that is not analytic: on $|z|=1$, $\oint\bar z\,dz=2\pi i$, not $0$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\dfrac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{z}{z^2+1}\,dz$.
answer: 1
check: quad(lambda t: (lambda z: (z/(z*z + 1))*z)(2*np.exp(1j*t)).real, 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: Both poles $\pm i$ lie inside $|z|=2$. Use $\operatorname{Res}=g(z_0)/h'(z_0)$.
hint: Here $g=z$ and $h=z^2+1$ with $h'=2z$, so each residue is $z_0/(2z_0)$.
solution: Each simple pole has residue $\tfrac12$, so the sum of residues is $1$ and $\oint=2\pi i$. Dividing by $2\pi i$ gives $1$.

@practice expr
prompt: Find the residue of $f(z)=\dfrac{\cos(az)}{(z-b)^3}$ at $z=b$, as an expression in the real parameters $a$ and $b$.
vars: a, b
lead: $\operatorname{Res}_{z=b}f=$
answer: -a^2*cos(a*b)/2
check: lambda a, b: quad(lambda t: (np.cos(a*(b + np.exp(1j*t)))*np.exp(-2j*t)).real, 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: This is a pole of order $3$ at $z=b$, so the residue is $\dfrac1{2!}\dfrac{d^2}{dz^2}\big[\cos(az)\big]$ evaluated at $z=b$.
hint: $\dfrac{d^2}{dz^2}\cos(az)=-a^2\cos(az)$.
solution: $\operatorname{Res}_{b}f=\tfrac12\big(-a^2\cos(ab)\big)=-\tfrac{a^2}{2}\cos(ab)$. The same method gives the residue $2e^2$ of $e^{2z}/(z-1)^3$ at $z=1$.

@practice num
prompt: The function $z^2e^{1/z}$ has an essential singularity at $0$. Find its residue there.
answer: 1/6
check: quad(lambda t: (np.exp(3j*t)*np.exp(np.exp(-1j*t))).real, 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: Expand $e^{1/z}=\sum_{k\ge0}\frac{1}{k!\,z^k}$ and multiply by $z^2$.
hint: You need the coefficient of $z^{-1}$, which comes from the term with $k=3$.
solution: $z^2e^{1/z}=\sum_k\frac{z^{2-k}}{k!}$. The exponent $2-k=-1$ requires $k=3$, so $c_{-1}=\frac1{3!}=\frac16$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\dfrac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=4}\tan z\,dz$. (Recall $\tan z=\sin z/\cos z$.)
answer: -2
check: quad(lambda t: (np.tan(4*np.exp(1j*t))*4*np.exp(1j*t)).real, 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: The poles of $\tan z$ are the zeros of $\cos z$, at $z=\pm\pi/2,\pm3\pi/2,\dots$. Which satisfy $|z|<4$?
hint: Use $\operatorname{Res}=\sin z_0/\big(\cos z\big)'(z_0)$.
solution: Inside $|z|<4$ are $\pm\pi/2$ only ($3\pi/2\approx4.71$ is outside). At each, $\operatorname{Res}=\sin z_0/(-\sin z_0)=-1$. The sum is $-2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\dfrac1{2\pi i}\oint_{|z|=2}\dfrac{\cos z}{z^3}\,dz$.
answer: -1/2
check: quad(lambda t: (lambda z: np.cos(z)/z**2)(2*np.exp(1j*t)).real, 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)
hint: This is Cauchy's formula for the second derivative of $\cos z$ at $0$.
hint: $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f(z)}{z^{3}}dz=\frac{f''(0)}{2!}$.
solution: With $f=\cos z$, $f''(0)=-1$, so the value is $-\tfrac12$. Equivalently, $\cos z/z^3=z^{-3}-\tfrac12z^{-1}+\cdots$ has residue $-\tfrac12$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all true statements.
choice*: If $f$ is analytic on and inside a simple closed contour $C$, then $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$.
choice*: $\oint_{|z|=1}\bar z\,dz=2\pi i$, so Cauchy's theorem cannot be applied to the non-analytic function $\bar z$.
choice: If $\oint_Cf\,dz=0$ then $f$ is analytic inside $C$.
choice: If $f$ has a pole inside $C$, then $\oint_Cf\,dz\ne0$.
hint: On $|z|=1$ we have $\bar z=1/z$.
hint: Consider $f(z)=1/z^2$ on $|z|=1$.
solution: Cauchy's theorem gives integral $0$ when $f$ is analytic on and inside $C$. On the unit circle $\bar z=1/z$, so $\oint\bar z\,dz=\oint dz/z=2\pi i$. The converse (integral $0$ implies analytic) and the claim that a pole forces a nonzero integral both fail for $f=1/z^2$, whose integral over $|z|=1$ is $0$ although $f$ has a pole at $0$.
