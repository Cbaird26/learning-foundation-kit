@article
**Goal.** Evaluate $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}$ and similar integrals by adding an arc in the complex plane to make a closed contour, applying the residue theorem, and proving that the arc contributes nothing (or a computable amount).

### The two estimates

The **ML inequality** $\big|\int_Cf\,dz\big|\le ML$ handles most arcs. On a semicircle $C_R$ of radius $R$ (length $\pi R$), if $|f|\le M_R$ and $RM_R\to0$, the arc integral vanishes. For $f=P/Q$ this holds when $\deg Q\ge\deg P+2$.

When the integrand contains $e^{ikz}$ one only knows $f\to0$ with no rate, so use **Jordan's lemma**: if $k>0$, $g$ is continuous on the upper half-plane for $|z|>R_0$ and $g(z)\to0$ uniformly as $|z|\to\infty$, then $\int_{C_R^+}g(z)e^{ikz}\,dz\to0$. The proof uses $\sin\theta\ge2\theta/\pi$ on $[0,\pi/2]$. For $k<0$, close in the lower half-plane.

### Choosing the half-plane

With $z=x+iy$ we have $|e^{ikz}|=e^{-ky}$. So $e^{ikz}$ decays in the upper half-plane for $k>0$ and in the lower for $k<0$. **Close where the exponential decays.** Closing the wrong way makes the arc blow up.

- Rational integrand, no real poles: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}z_k>0}\operatorname{Res}$.
- Fourier type, $k>0$, $R\to0$ at infinity, no real poles: $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}R(x)e^{ikx}dx=2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}z_k>0}\operatorname{Res}\big[R(z)e^{ikz}\big]$. For $k<0$ sum over the lower half-plane and use $-2\pi i$ (clockwise).
- For real $R$, $\int R\cos kx\,dx$ and $\int R\sin kx\,dx$ are the real and imaginary parts of the $e^{ikx}$ integral.

### Poles on the real axis

The **principal value** is $\mathrm{P}\!\int f\,dx=\lim_{\epsilon\to0}\big(\int_{-\infty}^{x_0-\epsilon}+\int_{x_0+\epsilon}^{\infty}\big)f\,dx$. For a simple pole at $x_0$, a small semicircle of radius $\epsilon$ passing **above** the pole from left to right is traversed clockwise and contributes $-i\pi\operatorname{Res}_{x_0}f$ as $\epsilon\to0$. Closing in the upper half-plane then gives
$$\mathrm{P}\!\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f\,dx=2\pi i\sum_{\text{upper}}\operatorname{Res}+i\pi\sum_{\text{real axis}}\operatorname{Res}.$$

### Keyhole contours

For $\int_0^\infty x^{a-1}g(x)\,dx$ use a keyhole around the positive real axis, taking $\arg z\in(0,2\pi)$, so that $z^{a-1}$ above the cut is $x^{a-1}$ and below the cut is $e^{2\pi i(a-1)}x^{a-1}$. For $0<a<1$,
$$\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{\sin\pi a}.$$

### Trigonometric integrals

Put $z=e^{i\theta}$, $\cos\theta=\tfrac12(z+z^{-1})$, $d\theta=dz/(iz)$. Then $\int_0^{2\pi}F(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta=\oint_{|z|=1}F\,\dfrac{dz}{iz}$, and $\int_0^{2\pi}\dfrac{d\theta}{a+b\cos\theta}=\dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt{a^2-b^2}}$ for $a>|b|$.

### Response integrals and causality

For $\ddot x+\gamma\dot x+\omega_0^2x=f(t)$ use the convention $f(t)=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\tilde f(\omega)e^{-i\omega t}$. The Green function is
$$G(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\,\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}.$$
For $\gamma>0$ both poles lie in the lower half-plane. Since $|e^{-i\omega t}|=e^{t\operatorname{Im}\omega}$, for $t<0$ we close above, enclose nothing, and get $G=0$: the response does not precede the cause. For $t>0$ we close below, clockwise, and pick up both poles.

@example
title: A Fourier integral closed in the upper half-plane
problem: For $a>0$ and $k>0$ evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos kx}{x^2+a^2}\,dx$ and justify the contour.
step: Since $\sin(kx)/(x^2+a^2)$ is odd, the integral equals the real part of $\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{ikx}}{x^2+a^2}dx$. Let $F(z)=\dfrac{e^{ikz}}{z^2+a^2}$.
step: Close with a semicircle $C_R$ in the upper half-plane. There $|e^{ikz}|=e^{-ky}\le1$ and $|z^2+a^2|\ge R^2-a^2$, so $\big|\int_{C_R}F\,dz\big|\le\dfrac{\pi R}{R^2-a^2}\to0$. (Jordan's lemma gives the same conclusion.)
step: The only singularity of $F$ in the upper half-plane is the simple pole at $z=ia$; the pole at $-ia$ is below the axis and is not enclosed.
step: $\operatorname{Res}_{ia}F=\dfrac{e^{ik(ia)}}{2ia}=\dfrac{e^{-ka}}{2ia}$.
step: Hence $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{e^{ikx}}{x^2+a^2}dx=2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{e^{-ka}}{2ia}=\dfrac{\pi}{a}e^{-ka}$. This is real, so the cosine integral equals $\dfrac\pi ae^{-ka}$.
step: Check $k\to0$: $\pi/a=\int dx/(x^2+a^2)$.

@example
title: The causal response of a damped oscillator
problem: For $0<\gamma<2\omega_0$ evaluate $G(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\frac{e^{-i\omega t}}{\omega_0^2-\omega^2-i\gamma\omega}$ for all real $t$, and justify each contour.
step: The denominator is $-(\omega^2+i\gamma\omega-\omega_0^2)=-(\omega-\omega_+)(\omega-\omega_-)$ with $\omega_\pm=\pm\Omega-\tfrac{i\gamma}2$ and $\Omega=\sqrt{\omega_0^2-\gamma^2/4}>0$. Both poles have $\operatorname{Im}\omega=-\gamma/2<0$.
step: For $t<0$, $|e^{-i\omega t}|=e^{t\operatorname{Im}\omega}$ decays in the upper half-plane. Close there; the factor $1/\omega^2$ plus Jordan's lemma remove the arc. No poles are enclosed, so $G(t)=0$.
step: For $t>0$ the exponential decays in the lower half-plane. Close below, which is clockwise, so $G(t)=\frac{-2\pi i}{2\pi}\sum\operatorname{Res}=-i\sum\operatorname{Res}$.
step: Residues of $\dfrac{-e^{-i\omega t}}{(\omega-\omega_+)(\omega-\omega_-)}$: at $\omega_+$ it is $-\dfrac{e^{-i\omega_+t}}{2\Omega}$ and at $\omega_-$ it is $+\dfrac{e^{-i\omega_-t}}{2\Omega}$.
step: Since $e^{-i\omega_\pm t}=e^{-\gamma t/2}e^{\mp i\Omega t}$, the sum is $\dfrac{e^{-\gamma t/2}}{2\Omega}\big(e^{i\Omega t}-e^{-i\Omega t}\big)=\dfrac{i\,e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}$.
step: Therefore $G(t)=\dfrac{e^{-\gamma t/2}\sin\Omega t}{\Omega}$ for $t>0$ and $0$ for $t<0$. Check: $G(0^+)=0$ and $G'(0^+)=1$, the velocity kick produced by the delta force.

@mistakes
- Closing the contour in the half-plane where the exponential grows, so that the arc does not vanish.
- Enclosing the wrong poles, or forgetting that a clockwise contour contributes $-2\pi i$ times the residues.
- Using the residue theorem with a pole on the real axis without indenting or taking a principal value.
- Crossing a branch cut, or mixing the argument convention ($\arg z\in(0,2\pi)$ for the keyhole) when evaluating $z^{a-1}$ on the two sides of the cut.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^2+4}$ by closing in the upper half-plane. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/2
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x*x + 4), -inf, inf)
hint: The only pole in the upper half-plane is $z=2i$. Its residue is $1/(2z)$ evaluated there.
hint: The arc vanishes because the integrand is $O(R^{-2})$, so ML gives a bound of order $\pi R/R^2$.
solution: $\operatorname{Res}_{2i}=\frac1{4i}$, so the integral is $2\pi i\cdot\frac1{4i}=\frac\pi2$.

choice*: Close in the lower half-plane, clockwise, since $|e^{-ikz}|=e^{ky}$ decays for $y<0$; the enclosed pole at $-i$ contributes with the factor $-2\pi i$.
choice: Close in the upper half-plane, since the pole at $+i$ is the one that gives a real answer.
choice: Either half-plane works with no further justification, because $1/(x^2+1)$ decays like $x^{-2}$.
choice: Residues cannot be used because $e^{-ikx}$ is oscillatory.

@practice num

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{5+4\cos\theta}$ exactly.
answer: 2pi/3
check: quad(lambda t: 1/(5 + 4*cos(t)), 0, 2*pi)
hint: Substitute $z=e^{i\theta}$, $\cos\theta=\tfrac12(z+1/z)$, $d\theta=dz/(iz)$. The integrand becomes a rational function of $z$ on $|z|=1$.
hint: You should get $\oint_{|z|=1}\frac{dz}{i(2z^2+5z+2)}$. Factor $2z^2+5z+2=(2z+1)(z+2)$ and decide which root lies inside.
solution: The integral becomes $\oint\frac{dz}{i(2z+1)(z+2)}$. Only $z=-\tfrac12$ lies inside the unit circle, with residue $\frac{1}{i\cdot2\cdot(3/2)}=\frac{1}{3i}$. So the integral is $2\pi i\cdot\frac1{3i}=\frac{2\pi}3$, matching $2\pi/\sqrt{a^2-b^2}$ with $a=5$, $b=4$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A simple pole of $f$ lies on the real axis at $x_0$. A contour passes above it along a small semicircle $z=x_0+\epsilon e^{i\theta}$ going from $\theta=\pi$ to $\theta=0$. What is the contribution of this semicircle as $\epsilon\to0$?
choice*: $-i\pi\operatorname{Res}_{x_0}f$
choice: $+i\pi\operatorname{Res}_{x_0}f$
choice: $-2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{x_0}f$
choice: $0$, because the semicircle shrinks to a point
hint: Near the pole $f\approx\dfrac{\operatorname{Res}}{z-x_0}$, and $dz=i\epsilon e^{i\theta}d\theta$.
solution: $\int f\,dz\approx\operatorname{Res}\int_{\pi}^{0}\frac{i\epsilon e^{i\theta}}{\epsilon e^{i\theta}}d\theta=\operatorname{Res}\cdot i(0-\pi)=-i\pi\operatorname{Res}$. A full circle would give $2\pi i\operatorname{Res}$; a half circle traversed clockwise gives $-i\pi\operatorname{Res}$. The size of the arc shrinks but the integrand grows, so the contribution stays finite.
