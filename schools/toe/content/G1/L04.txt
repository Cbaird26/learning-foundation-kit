@article
An **asymptotic expansion** approximates a function in a limit, usually $x\to\infty$, by simple functions whose accuracy improves as the limit is approached, not as more terms are added. Conventions: $f\sim g$ means $f/g\to1$; $f=O(g)$ means $|f|\le C|g|$ near the limit point; $f=o(g)$ means $f/g\to0$.

### Asymptotic series

The series $\sum_{n\ge0}a_nx^{-n}$ is an **asymptotic expansion** of $f$ as $x\to\infty$, written $f(x)\sim\sum a_nx^{-n}$, if for every $N$

$$f(x)-\sum_{n=0}^{N}a_nx^{-n}=O\big(x^{-N-1}\big)\qquad(x\to\infty).$$

The series may diverge for every $x$. The coefficients are unique for a given scale $\{x^{-n}\}$, but the series does not determine the function: $e^{-x}$ has the zero expansion. At fixed $x$ the error of the partial sums first decreases and then grows. Stopping at the smallest term is **optimal truncation**, and the error is then about the size of that term.

### Watson's lemma

Take $I(x)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-xt}}{1+t}\,dt$. Repeated integration by parts gives $I(x)\sim\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^nn!}{x^{n+1}}$, a divergent series. The general statement is **Watson's lemma**: if $g$ is smooth near $0$ with at most exponential growth, and $\alpha>-1$,

$$\int_0^\infty t^{\alpha}g(t)\,e^{-xt}\,dt\sim\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{g^{(n)}(0)}{n!}\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+n+1)}{x^{\alpha+n+1}}\qquad(x\to\infty).$$

The reason: $e^{-xt}$ makes only $t\lesssim1/x$ matter, so expand $g$ at $0$ and integrate termwise to infinity.

### Laplace's method

Let $I(x)=\int_a^bf(t)\,e^{x\phi(t)}\,dt$ where $\phi$ has a unique maximum at an interior point $t_0$ with $\phi''(t_0)<0$ and $f(t_0)\ne0$. Then

$$I(x)\sim f(t_0)\,e^{x\phi(t_0)}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{x\,|\phi''(t_0)|}}\qquad(x\to\infty).$$

The integrand is concentrated in a window of width $\sim x^{-1/2}$ around $t_0$. Replace $\phi$ by its quadratic Taylor polynomial and extend the Gaussian integral over $\mathbb R$. If the maximum sits at an endpoint with $\phi'\ne0$, the scaling is $e^{x\phi}/x$ instead of $e^{x\phi}/\sqrt x$.

### Stirling's formula

Write $\Gamma(x+1)=\int_0^\infty t^xe^{-t}dt$ and set $t=xs$:

$$\Gamma(x+1)=x^{x+1}\int_0^\infty e^{x(\ln s-s)}\,ds .$$

Here $\phi(s)=\ln s-s$ has its maximum at $s=1$ with $\phi(1)=-1$ and $\phi''(1)=-1$, so Laplace's method gives

$$\Gamma(x+1)\sim\sqrt{2\pi x}\Big(\frac xe\Big)^{x}\Big(1+\frac1{12x}+\cdots\Big).$$

### Large-argument Bessel functions

The same ideas (with stationary phase for oscillatory integrals) give, for fixed $\nu$ as $x\to\infty$, with phase $\chi=x-\frac{\nu\pi}{2}-\frac\pi4$,

$$J_\nu(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi x}}\Big[\cos\chi-\frac{4\nu^2-1}{8x}\sin\chi+O(x^{-2})\Big].$$

The leading term is a decaying cosine whose amplitude falls like $x^{-1/2}$.

### Compare, do not trust

A limiting form is only a claim until it is compared with an accurate evaluation. Compute the relative error $|f-f_{\text{approx}}|/|f|$ for several $x$ and check that it shrinks at the predicted rate. In Python:

```python
from math import factorial, sqrt, pi, e

for n in (5, 10, 20):
    exact = factorial(n)
    stirling = sqrt(2*pi*n) * (n/e)**n
    print(n, abs(exact - stirling)/exact)   # about 1/(12 n)
```

@example
title: Stirling's formula from Laplace's method
problem: Derive the leading behavior of $\Gamma(x+1)$ as $x\to\infty$.
step: Put $t=xs$ in $\Gamma(x+1)=\int_0^\infty t^xe^{-t}dt$. Then $t^x=x^xs^x$ and $dt=x\,ds$, so $\Gamma(x+1)=x^{x+1}\int_0^\infty e^{x(\ln s-s)}\,ds$.
step: The exponent function $\phi(s)=\ln s-s$ has $\phi'(s)=\tfrac1s-1=0$ at $s=1$, and $\phi''(1)=-1<0$, a maximum, with $\phi(1)=-1$.
step: Laplace's method with $f\equiv1$ gives $\int_0^\infty e^{x\phi}ds\sim e^{-x}\sqrt{2\pi/x}$.
step: Multiply by the prefactor: $\Gamma(x+1)\sim x^{x+1}e^{-x}\sqrt{2\pi/x}=\sqrt{2\pi x}\,(x/e)^x$.
step: Numerical comparison at $n=10$: the leading form gives about $3.5987\times10^6$ against $10!=3.6288\times10^6$, a relative error near $0.83\%$, consistent with $1/(12n)$.

@example
title: Watson's lemma with an algebraic factor
problem: Find the first three terms of the expansion of $I(x)=\int_0^\infty\frac{e^{-xt}}{\sqrt{1+t^2}}\,dt$ as $x\to\infty$.
step: Here $\alpha=0$ and $g(t)=(1+t^2)^{-1/2}$, smooth at $0$ and bounded.
step: Expand $g$ with the binomial series: $g(t)=1-\tfrac12t^2+\tfrac38t^4-\cdots$. Odd powers are absent.
step: Watson's lemma with $\alpha=0$ uses $\int_0^\infty t^ne^{-xt}dt=n!/x^{n+1}$ on each term: $1\cdot\frac1x$, then $-\tfrac12\cdot\frac{2!}{x^3}$, then $\tfrac38\cdot\frac{4!}{x^5}$.
step: Therefore $I(x)\sim\frac1x-\frac1{x^3}+\frac{9}{x^5}-\cdots$, a divergent series whose terms grow like $(2k)!/x^{2k+1}$.

@mistakes
- Believing that adding more terms of a divergent asymptotic series always improves the approximation at fixed $x$.
- Applying Laplace's formula when the maximum of $\phi$ is at an endpoint, or when $\phi''(t_0)=0$ or $f(t_0)=0$, without changing the power of $x$.
- Writing $f\sim g$ when only $f=O(g)$ is known, or dropping the prefactor $x^{x+1}$ before applying Laplace's method in Stirling's derivation.
- Confusing absolute and relative error: an expansion can have tiny absolute error and be useless where the function itself is tiny.
