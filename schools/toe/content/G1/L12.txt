@article
A **perturbation problem** contains a small parameter $\epsilon$. The strategy: solve the problem at $\epsilon=0$ exactly, then correct it order by order in $\epsilon$. The result is usually an asymptotic series (week 4), not a convergent one, so every answer should come with a stated order of error.

### Regular perturbation

Substitute $y=y_0+\epsilon y_1+\epsilon^2y_2+\cdots$ and equate powers of $\epsilon$. Each order is the unperturbed problem for the next correction, with a source built from lower orders. For $x^2-\epsilon x-1=0$ near the root $x=1$, put $x=1+a\epsilon+b\epsilon^2$: order $\epsilon$ gives $2a-1=0$, order $\epsilon^2$ gives $2b+a^2-a=0$, so $x=1+\tfrac\epsilon2+\tfrac{\epsilon^2}8+\cdots$. A regular expansion is **uniformly valid** only if each correction stays small compared with the leading term over the whole domain.

### Secular terms and Poincaré–Lindstedt

Take $\ddot x+x+\epsilon x^3=0$ with $x(0)=a$, $\dot x(0)=0$. The naive expansion has $x_0=a\cos t$ and
$$\ddot x_1+x_1=-a^3\cos^3t=-\tfrac{a^3}4\,(3\cos t+\cos3t).$$
The $\cos t$ forcing is resonant and produces $t\sin t$, a **secular term** that grows without bound, so the expansion fails for $t\sim1/\epsilon$. Rescale time to $\tau=\omega t$ with $\omega=1+\epsilon\omega_1+\cdots$. At order $\epsilon$ the forcing becomes $(2\omega_1a-\tfrac34a^3)\cos\tau-\tfrac{a^3}4\cos3\tau$, and choosing $\omega_1$ to cancel the resonant term gives
$$\omega=1+\tfrac38\,\epsilon a^2+O(\epsilon^2).$$
The frequency depends on amplitude, a genuinely nonlinear effect.

### Eigenvalue perturbation

Let $\mathcal L=\mathcal L_0+\epsilon\mathcal L_1$ be self-adjoint, with $\mathcal L_0u_n=\lambda_nu_n$ and orthonormal $u_n$ (weight $1$). For a non-degenerate $\lambda_n$,
$$\lambda_n(\epsilon)=\lambda_n+\epsilon\langle u_n,\mathcal L_1u_n\rangle+\epsilon^2\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|\langle u_m,\mathcal L_1u_n\rangle|^2}{\lambda_n-\lambda_m}+O(\epsilon^3).$$
The first-order shift is the Rayleigh quotient of the unperturbed eigenfunction (week 11). The second-order shift of the lowest eigenvalue is always negative, since $\lambda_1-\lambda_m<0$. For a degenerate eigenvalue, first diagonalize $\mathcal L_1$ within the degenerate eigenspace.

### Singular perturbation and boundary layers

When $\epsilon$ multiplies the highest derivative, setting $\epsilon=0$ lowers the order and the outer solution cannot satisfy every boundary condition. Consider
$$\epsilon y''+y'+y=0,\qquad y(0)=0,\quad y(1)=1 .$$
The **outer** solution (with $x=O(1)$) solves $y_0'+y_0=0$, so $y_{\text{out}}=Ce^{-x}$; the condition at $x=1$ gives $C=e$. It cannot meet $y(0)=0$, so a **boundary layer** of thickness $O(\epsilon)$ sits at $x=0$. With $\xi=x/\epsilon$ the equation becomes $y_{\xi\xi}+y_\xi+\epsilon y=0$, and the leading **inner** solution satisfying $y(0)=0$ is $y_{\text{in}}=A(1-e^{-\xi})$. **Matching** the limit $\xi\to\infty$ of the inner solution to the limit $x\to0$ of the outer solution gives $A=e$. The **composite** approximation is inner plus outer minus their common limit:
$$y_c(x)=e^{1-x}-e\,e^{-x/\epsilon}.$$
The layer lies at the end that the sign of the $y'$ coefficient selects: with a positive coefficient the layer is at the left end, with a negative one at the right end.

### Quantifying the approximation

State the order of the error ($O(\epsilon)$ for the composite above, $O(\epsilon^3)$ after the $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon^2$ corrections of a regular series) and verify it numerically by comparing with an exact or numerically computed solution at several values of $\epsilon$. If halving $\epsilon$ halves the error, the approximation is first order. Report both absolute and relative errors.

@example
title: Eigenvalue of a perturbed string problem
problem: Estimate the lowest eigenvalue of $-u''+\epsilon x\,u=\lambda u$ on $[0,1]$ with $u(0)=u(1)=0$ to second order in $\epsilon$, and state the size of the correction.
step: Unperturbed: $\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2$ and $u_n=\sqrt2\sin n\pi x$. The perturbation is $\mathcal L_1=x$ (multiplication by $x$).
step: First order: $\langle u_1,xu_1\rangle=2\int_0^1x\sin^2\pi x\,dx=\int_0^1x\,(1-\cos2\pi x)\,dx=\tfrac12$.
step: Second order needs $\langle u_m,xu_1\rangle=\int_0^1x\,[\cos(m-1)\pi x-\cos(m+1)\pi x]\,dx$. For odd $m\ne1$ this vanishes. For even $m$ it equals $-\dfrac{8m}{\pi^2(m^2-1)^2}$.
step: The dominant term is $m=2$: $|\langle u_2,xu_1\rangle|^2=\dfrac{256}{81\pi^4}$ and $\lambda_1-\lambda_2=-3\pi^2$, giving $-\dfrac{256}{243\pi^6}\approx-0.0011$. Terms with $m\ge4$ add less than $1\%$ of this.
step: Therefore $\lambda_1\approx\pi^2+\tfrac12\epsilon-0.0011\,\epsilon^2$. For $\epsilon=2$ this is $10.8696-0.0044=10.8652$; the second-order correction is below $0.05\%$ of $\lambda_1$.

@example
title: A boundary layer and its composite approximation
problem: Construct the composite approximation to $\epsilon y''+y'+y=0$ with $y(0)=0$, $y(1)=1$ and state where it fails.
step: Outer problem: set $\epsilon=0$ to get $y'+y=0$, so $y=Ce^{-x}$. The condition at $x=1$ fixes $C=e$, so $y_{\text{out}}=e^{1-x}$, and $y_{\text{out}}(0)=e\ne0$.
step: The coefficient of $y'$ is positive, so the layer is at $x=0$. Put $\xi=x/\epsilon$: the leading equation is $y_{\xi\xi}+y_\xi=0$ with solutions $A+Be^{-\xi}$, and $y(0)=0$ gives $y_{\text{in}}=A(1-e^{-\xi})$.
step: Match: $\lim_{\xi\to\infty}y_{\text{in}}=A$ must equal $\lim_{x\to0}y_{\text{out}}=e$, so $A=e$.
step: Composite: $y_c=y_{\text{out}}+y_{\text{in}}-e=e^{1-x}-e\,e^{-x/\epsilon}$. It satisfies both conditions exactly at the leading order: $y_c(0)=0$ and $y_c(1)=1-e^{1-1/\epsilon}\approx1$.
step: The error is $O(\epsilon)$ uniformly on $[0,1]$. For $\epsilon=0.05$ and $x=0.5$ the exact value is about $1.695$ while $y_c=1.649$, a relative error of $2.7\%$, consistent with $O(\epsilon)$.

@mistakes
- Applying the outer solution everywhere, ignoring that it violates a boundary condition, or placing the boundary layer at the wrong end.
- Keeping secular terms such as $t\sin t$ and claiming the expansion is valid for all time.
- Using first-order eigenvalue perturbation theory with a degenerate eigenvalue without diagonalizing within the degenerate subspace.
- Reporting a perturbative answer without the order of the error or a numerical comparison.
