@article
**Idea.** Solve $\mathcal Lu=f$ for every $f$ at once by solving it for a point source. Since $f(x)=\int f(\xi)\,\delta(x-\xi)\,d\xi$, linearity gives $u(x)=\int G(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi$, where $G(\cdot,\xi)$ is the response to a unit point source at $\xi$.

### Definition and conventions

Let $\mathcal Lu=-(p\,u')'+q\,u$ on $(a,b)$ with $p>0$ and homogeneous separated boundary conditions (the domain of week 2). The **Green function** is defined for each fixed $\xi\in(a,b)$ by
$$\mathcal L_xG(x,\xi)=\delta(x-\xi),\qquad G(\cdot,\xi)\ \text{satisfies the homogeneous BCs}.$$
Some books use $-\delta$ or $4\pi\delta$ on the right; always check the sign convention. Then
$$u(x)=\int_a^bG(x,\xi)\,f(\xi)\,d\xi$$
solves $\mathcal Lu=f$ with those BCs. The Green function exists and is unique when $\mathcal Lu=0$ has only the trivial solution in the domain, that is, when $\lambda=0$ is not an eigenvalue.

### Jump conditions

Away from $x=\xi$ we have $\mathcal LG=0$. Integrating $\mathcal LG=\delta$ across $x=\xi$ gives
$$G\ \text{continuous at } x=\xi,\qquad \partial_xG\Big|_{x=\xi^-}^{x=\xi^+}=-\frac1{p(\xi)}.$$
The sign comes from the minus in $\mathcal L=-(pu')'+qu$.

### Construction in one dimension

Let $u_1$ solve $\mathcal Lu=0$ and satisfy the left BC, and let $u_2$ solve $\mathcal Lu=0$ and satisfy the right BC. They are linearly independent exactly when $0$ is not an eigenvalue. With the Wronskian $W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2$ (so that $pW$ is constant),
$$G(x,\xi)=-\frac{u_1(x_<)\,u_2(x_>)}{p\,W},\qquad x_<=\min(x,\xi),\quad x_>=\max(x,\xi).$$
This satisfies the BCs by construction, is continuous, and has the correct derivative jump because of the Wronskian. For real self-adjoint problems $G(x,\xi)=G(\xi,x)$.

### Eigenfunction expansion

If $\mathcal Lu_n=\lambda_nu_n$ with orthonormal $u_n$ (weight $1$), then
$$G(x,\xi)=\sum_n\frac{u_n(x)\,\overline{u_n(\xi)}}{\lambda_n}.$$
The closed form is best for point values; the series is best for spectral identities such as $\int G(x,x)\,dx=\sum_n1/\lambda_n$.

### When zero is an eigenvalue

For $-u''=f$ with $u'(0)=u'(1)=0$, the constants solve the homogeneous problem. A solution exists only if $\int_0^1f\,dx=0$ and is then unique only up to an added constant. The remedy is a modified Green function (Fredholm alternative, week 10).

### Free-space and time-domain examples

- $-u''=\delta(x-\xi)$ on $\mathbb R$: $G=-\tfrac12|x-\xi|$ plus any linear function (no BCs at infinity).
- $-u''+\kappa^2u=\delta(x-\xi)$ on $\mathbb R$, decaying: $G=\dfrac{1}{2\kappa}e^{-\kappa|x-\xi|}$.
- $-\nabla^2G=\delta^3(\mathbf r-\mathbf r')$ in $\mathbb R^3$, decaying: $G=\dfrac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$.
- The damped oscillator of week 6: $G(t-t')=\dfrac{e^{-\gamma(t-t')/2}\sin\Omega(t-t')}{\Omega}\,\Theta(t-t')$, a retarded Green function.

Inhomogeneous boundary values are handled either by subtracting a function that has the right boundary values, or by keeping the boundary terms of Green's identity.

@example
title: A mixed Dirichlet–Neumann Green function
problem: Construct the Green function of $\mathcal L=-d^2/dx^2$ on $[0,1]$ with $u(0)=0$ and $u'(1)=0$, verify it, and solve $-u''=1$.
step: Choose $u_1=x$, which solves $u''=0$ and satisfies $u_1(0)=0$, and $u_2=1$, which solves $u''=0$ and satisfies $u_2'(1)=0$. Here $p=1$.
step: The Wronskian is $W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2=0-1=-1\ne0$. Then $G=-\dfrac{u_1(x_<)u_2(x_>)}{pW}=x_<$, that is, $G(x,\xi)=\min(x,\xi)$.
step: Verify the BCs: $G(0,\xi)=0$, and $\partial_xG(1,\xi)=0$ because for $x>\xi$ we have $G=\xi$ (constant in $x$).
step: Verify the jump: for $x<\xi$, $\partial_xG=1$; for $x>\xi$, $\partial_xG=0$. The jump is $0-1=-1=-1/p$, and $G$ is continuous at $x=\xi$.
step: Solve with $f=1$: $u(x)=\int_0^1\min(x,\xi)\,d\xi=\dfrac{x^2}2+x(1-x)=x-\dfrac{x^2}2$. Check: $-u''=1$, $u(0)=0$, $u'(1)=1-1=0$.

@example
title: The same Green function two ways
problem: For $\mathcal L=-d^2/dx^2$ on $[0,1]$ with Dirichlet conditions, compute $G(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ from the closed form and from the eigenfunction expansion.
step: The homogeneous solutions are $u_1=x$ and $u_2=1-x$. Then $W=x(-1)-1\cdot(1-x)=-1$, so $G=x_<(1-x_>)$.
step: Closed form: $G(\tfrac12,\tfrac12)=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12=\tfrac14$.
step: Expansion: the orthonormal eigenfunctions are $u_n=\sqrt2\sin n\pi x$ with $\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2$, so $G=\sum_n\dfrac{2\sin n\pi x\,\sin n\pi\xi}{n^2\pi^2}$.
step: At $x=\xi=\tfrac12$ only odd $n$ survive, with $\sin^2(n\pi/2)=1$: $G=\dfrac{2}{\pi^2}\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}\dfrac1{n^2}=\dfrac2{\pi^2}\cdot\dfrac{\pi^2}{8}=\dfrac14$.
step: The two agree, and comparing them also proves $\sum_{n\ \text{odd}}n^{-2}=\pi^2/8$.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong sign in the derivative jump: with $\mathcal L=-(pu')'+qu$ and $\mathcal LG=+\delta$ the jump of $\partial_xG$ is $-1/p(\xi)$.
- Choosing $u_1$ and $u_2$ that both satisfy the same boundary condition, or forgetting that they must solve the homogeneous equation.
- Forgetting the Wronskian factor $1/(pW)$, or evaluating $u_1$ and $u_2$ at the wrong argument ($x_<$ versus $x_>$).
- Asking for a Green function when $0$ is an eigenvalue (for example the pure Neumann problem) without the solvability condition.

@practice self
prompt: Construct the Green function of $\mathcal L=-d^2/dx^2+\kappa^2$ ($\kappa>0$) on $[0,L]$ with $u(0)=u(L)=0$, and verify it. (a) Find $u_1$, $u_2$ and the Wronskian, and write $G(x,\xi)$. (b) Verify the boundary conditions, continuity at $x=\xi$, the jump of $\partial_xG$ and the symmetry. (c) Write the solution of $\mathcal Lu=f$ and check the limit $\kappa\to0$.
hint: Take $u_1=\sinh\kappa x$ (satisfies the condition at $0$) and $u_2=\sinh\kappa(L-x)$ (satisfies the condition at $L$).
hint: Use $\sinh A\cosh B+\cosh A\sinh B=\sinh(A+B)$ in the Wronskian and in the jump.
solution: (a) $W=u_1u_2'-u_1'u_2=-\kappa\big[\sinh\kappa x\cosh\kappa(L-x)+\cosh\kappa x\sinh\kappa(L-x)\big]=-\kappa\sinh\kappa L$, which is constant and nonzero, with $p=1$. Hence $G(x,\xi)=-\dfrac{u_1(x_<)u_2(x_>)}{pW}=\dfrac{\sinh\kappa x_<\,\sinh\kappa(L-x_>)}{\kappa\sinh\kappa L}$. (b) At $x=0$ we have $x_<=0$ and at $x=L$ we have $L-x_>=0$, so both boundary conditions hold. The two branches agree at $x=\xi$, so $G$ is continuous. For $x<\xi$, $\partial_xG(\xi^-)=\dfrac{\cosh\kappa\xi\,\sinh\kappa(L-\xi)}{\sinh\kappa L}$ and for $x>\xi$, $\partial_xG(\xi^+)=-\dfrac{\sinh\kappa\xi\,\cosh\kappa(L-\xi)}{\sinh\kappa L}$. The jump is $-\dfrac{\sinh\kappa L}{\sinh\kappa L}=-1=-1/p$. The formula depends on $x,\xi$ only through $x_<$ and $x_>$, so $G(x,\xi)=G(\xi,x)$. (c) $u(x)=\int_0^LG(x,\xi)f(\xi)\,d\xi$. As $\kappa\to0$, $\sinh z\approx z$ gives $G\to x_<(L-x_>)/L$, the Green function of $-d^2/dx^2$.
