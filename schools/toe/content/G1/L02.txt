@article
A **Sturm–Liouville (SL) problem** is an eigenvalue problem for a second-order differential operator that is symmetric on its domain. It contains the separation-of-variables eigenproblems of the heat, wave and Laplace equations and, as special cases, most of the special functions of week 3.

### Setup and conventions

On a finite interval $[a,b]$ consider

$$\mathcal{L}u=-(p\,u')'+q\,u=\lambda\,w\,u,\qquad p,\ p',\ q,\ w\ \text{continuous},\quad p>0,\ w>0 .$$

The minus sign is written explicitly; many books write $(pu')'+qu+\lambda wu=0$, which is the same problem with $q$ replaced by $-q$. The problem is **regular** when the boundary conditions (BCs) are **separated** and real:

$$\alpha_1u(a)+\alpha_2u'(a)=0,\qquad \beta_1u(b)+\beta_2u'(b)=0,\qquad (\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\ne(0,0),\ (\beta_1,\beta_2)\ne(0,0).$$

Dirichlet means $u=0$ at an end, Neumann means $u'=0$, Robin means $u'=\sigma u$. The **domain** of $\mathcal L$ is the set of twice-differentiable functions that satisfy both conditions, and the inner product is $\langle f,g\rangle_w=\int_a^b\bar f\,g\,w\,dx$.

### Why the operator is symmetric

Integrating by parts twice gives the **Lagrange identity**

$$\int_a^b\Big(\bar v\,\mathcal Lu-\overline{\mathcal Lv}\;u\Big)\,dx=-\Big[p\,\big(\bar v\,u'-\bar v'\,u\big)\Big]_a^b .$$

If $u$ and $v$ both lie in the domain, then at each end the pairs $(u,u')$ and $(v,v')$ satisfy the same real linear condition, so they are proportional and the bracket $\bar vu'-\bar v'u$ is zero there. Hence $\int\bar v\,\mathcal Lu=\int\overline{\mathcal Lv}\,u$ on the domain.

Now let $\mathcal Lu=\lambda wu$ and $\mathcal Lv=\mu wv$. The identity gives $(\lambda-\bar\mu)\langle v,u\rangle_w=0$. Taking $v=u$ shows $\lambda=\bar\lambda$, so **eigenvalues are real**. Taking $\mu\ne\lambda$ shows $\langle v,u\rangle_w=0$: **eigenfunctions of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal with weight $w$**.

### The regular theorem

For a regular SL problem: the eigenvalues are simple (only one independent solution meets the condition at $a$) and satisfy $\lambda_1<\lambda_2<\cdots\to\infty$; the eigenfunction $u_n$ has exactly $n-1$ zeros in $(a,b)$; and the $u_n$ are a complete orthogonal set in $L^2_w(a,b)$. Every $f$ expands as $f=\sum c_nu_n$ with $c_n=\langle u_n,f\rangle_w/\langle u_n,u_n\rangle_w$, in mean square, and uniformly when $f$ is twice differentiable and satisfies the BCs.

### The sign of $\lambda$ comes first

For $u$ in the domain, $\int\bar u\,\mathcal Lu\,dx=\int(p|u'|^2+q|u|^2)\,dx-[p\,\bar u\,u']_a^b$. With Dirichlet or Neumann ends the boundary term is zero, so $q\ge0$ forces $\lambda\ge0$. A Robin pair $u'(a)=\sigma_au(a)$, $u'(b)=-\sigma_bu(b)$ with $\sigma_a,\sigma_b\ge0$ adds a nonnegative boundary term, so $\lambda\ge0$ when $q\ge0$; the opposite signs can produce a negative eigenvalue and a $\cosh$/$\sinh$ solution. Decide the sign, and whether $\lambda=0$ is possible, before writing the general solution.

### Constant coefficients on $[0,L]$

For $-u''=\lambda u$ with $\lambda=k^2>0$, use $u=A\cos kx+B\sin kx$ and impose the BCs; the condition for a nonzero $(A,B)$ is an equation for $k$.

| BCs | eigenfunctions | eigenvalues |
|---|---|---|
| Dirichlet–Dirichlet | $\sin(n\pi x/L)$, $n\ge1$ | $(n\pi/L)^2$ |
| Neumann–Neumann | $\cos(n\pi x/L)$, $n\ge0$ | $(n\pi/L)^2$ |
| $u(0)=0$, $u'(L)=0$ | $\sin\big((n-\tfrac12)\pi x/L\big)$, $n\ge1$ | $\big((n-\tfrac12)\pi/L\big)^2$ |

Only the Neumann–Neumann problem has the eigenvalue $\lambda=0$ (constants).

### Beyond the regular case

Periodic conditions $u(a)=u(b)$, $u'(a)=u'(b)$ with $p(a)=p(b)$ are not separated. The operator is still symmetric, but eigenvalues can be doubly degenerate, as with $\cos nx$ and $\sin nx$ on $[0,2\pi]$. If $p$ vanishes at an end or the interval is infinite, the problem is **singular** and the BCs are replaced by boundedness or integrability conditions; week 3 meets Legendre and Bessel in this setting.

@example
title: A mixed Dirichlet–Neumann problem
problem: Find the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of $-u''=\lambda u$ on $[0,L]$ with $u(0)=0$ and $u'(L)=0$, and normalize the eigenfunctions with $w=1$.
step: Settle the sign. Here $\int\bar u(-u'')=\int|u'|^2-[\bar uu']_0^L=\int|u'|^2$, so $\lambda\ge0$. If $\lambda=0$ then $u=Ax+B$; $u(0)=0$ gives $B=0$ and $u'(L)=A=0$, which is trivial. So $\lambda=k^2$ with $k>0$.
step: General solution $u=A\cos kx+B\sin kx$. The condition $u(0)=0$ gives $A=0$, so $u=B\sin kx$.
step: The condition $u'(L)=Bk\cos kL=0$ with $B\ne0$ requires $\cos kL=0$, so $kL=(n-\tfrac12)\pi$ for $n=1,2,3,\dots$.
step: The eigenvalues are $\lambda_n=\big((n-\tfrac12)\pi/L\big)^2$ with eigenfunctions $u_n=\sin\big((n-\tfrac12)\pi x/L\big)$. Check the zero count: $u_2=\sin(3\pi x/2L)$ vanishes at $x=2L/3$ only, one zero, as the oscillation theorem predicts for $n=2$.
step: Norm: $\int_0^Lu_n^2dx=\tfrac12\int_0^L\big(1-\cos((2n-1)\pi x/L)\big)dx=\tfrac L2$. The normalized functions are $\sqrt{2/L}\,u_n$.

@example
title: A weighted problem: verify the weight and the boundary conditions
problem: Show that $-(xu')'=\lambda u/x$ on $[1,e]$ with $u(1)=u(e)=0$ is a regular SL problem, that $u_n=\sin(n\pi\ln x)$ solves it, and which inner product makes the $u_n$ orthogonal.
step: Read off $p=x$, $q=0$, $w=1/x$. On $[1,e]$ we have $p>0$ and $w>0$, and the BCs are separated Dirichlet, so the problem is regular.
step: Substitute $t=\ln x$, so $x\,d/dx=d/dt$ and $(xu')'=\tfrac1x u_{tt}$. The equation becomes $-u_{tt}=\lambda u$ on $0\le t\le1$ with $u=0$ at $t=0,1$. Hence $u_n=\sin(n\pi t)=\sin(n\pi\ln x)$ and $\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2$.
step: Boundary conditions: $u_n(1)=\sin 0=0$ and $u_n(e)=\sin(n\pi)=0$.
step: Orthogonality with $w=1/x$: $\int_1^e\sin(m\pi\ln x)\sin(n\pi\ln x)\,\frac{dx}{x}=\int_0^1\sin(m\pi t)\sin(n\pi t)\,dt=\tfrac12\delta_{mn}$.
step: Without the weight, $m=1$, $n=2$ gives $\int_0^1\sin(\pi t)\sin(2\pi t)\,e^t\,dt\approx-0.150\ne0$. The weight is part of the inner product.

@mistakes
- Dropping the weight $w$ when testing orthogonality; the eigenfunctions are orthogonal only in $\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_w$.
- Mixing sign conventions: $-u''=\lambda u$ and $u''+\lambda u=0$ describe the same positive eigenvalues, while $u''=\lambda u$ flips the sign of $\lambda$.
- Including $\lambda=0$ for Dirichlet problems (the solution $Ax+B$ is trivial) or forgetting it for Neumann–Neumann (the constant function is an eigenfunction).
- Applying separated-BC theorems, such as simplicity of eigenvalues, to periodic conditions.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\mathcal Lu=-(pu')'+qu$ on $[a,b]$ with real $p$, $q$, $w$, $p>0$, $w>0$, and separated real boundary conditions. Suppose $\mathcal Lu=\lambda wu$ and $\mathcal Lv=\mu wv$ with $u,v$ nonzero functions in the domain. Prove (a) that every eigenvalue $\lambda$ is real, and (b) that if $\lambda\ne\mu$ then $\int_a^b\bar v\,u\,w\,dx=0$. State exactly where the boundary conditions are used.
hint: Start from the Lagrange identity $\int_a^b(\bar v\,\mathcal Lu-\overline{\mathcal Lv}\,u)\,dx=-\big[p(\bar vu'-\bar v'u)\big]_a^b$.
hint: Show the boundary term vanishes at each end using that $(u,u')$ and $(\bar v,\bar v')$ are both orthogonal to the same real vector $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)$.
hint: For part (a) take $v=u$ and $\mu=\lambda$ and use $\int|u|^2w\,dx>0$.
solution: The Lagrange identity follows from integrating $-\bar v(pu')'$ and $+(p\bar v')'u$ by parts twice. At each endpoint, $u$ and $v$ satisfy the same condition $\alpha_1y+\alpha_2y'=0$ with real $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2)\ne(0,0)$; taking complex conjugates, $(\bar v,\bar v')$ also satisfies it. Two vectors orthogonal (in the bilinear sense) to the same nonzero real vector in the plane are parallel, so $\bar vu'-\bar v'u=0$ at each end and the right side is $0$. Since the coefficients are real, $\overline{\mathcal Lv}=\mathcal L\bar v=\bar\mu w\bar v$, so the left side is $(\lambda-\bar\mu)\int\bar v\,u\,w\,dx=0$. (a) With $v=u$, $\mu=\lambda$: $(\lambda-\bar\lambda)\int|u|^2w\,dx=0$ and the integral is positive, so $\lambda=\bar\lambda$. (b) For real $\lambda\ne\mu$, $\bar\mu=\mu$, so $(\lambda-\mu)\int\bar v\,u\,w\,dx=0$ and the integral vanishes. The boundary conditions are used only to kill the boundary term, so any conditions with that property (periodic ones when $p(a)=p(b)$) give the same conclusions except for simplicity of eigenvalues.
