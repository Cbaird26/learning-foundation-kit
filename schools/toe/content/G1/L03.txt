@article
Many eigenproblems of mathematical physics are Sturm–Liouville problems whose coefficient $p$ vanishes at an endpoint or whose interval is infinite. Their eigenfunctions are the **special functions**. Each family is pinned down by three things: a differential equation, a normalization, and an orthogonality relation with a weight. Learn those three for every family.

### The gamma and beta functions

For $\operatorname{Re}z>0$ define

$$\Gamma(z)=\int_0^\infty t^{z-1}e^{-t}\,dt .$$

Integration by parts gives $\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)$, so $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$, and $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$. The recursion extends $\Gamma$ to every complex $z$ except $0,-1,-2,\dots$, where it has simple poles. The **beta function** is

$$B(a,b)=\int_0^1t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}\,dt=\frac{\Gamma(a)\,\Gamma(b)}{\Gamma(a+b)},\qquad \operatorname{Re}a,\ \operatorname{Re}b>0 .$$

The substitution $t=\sin^2\theta$ turns it into $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2a-1}\theta\cos^{2b-1}\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac12B(a,b)$, which evaluates many trigonometric integrals.

### Legendre polynomials

The Legendre equation $-\big((1-x^2)y'\big)'=\ell(\ell+1)\,y$ on $[-1,1]$ has $p=1-x^2$ vanishing at both ends, so it is singular. Requiring $y$ to stay bounded at $x=\pm1$ plays the role of boundary conditions and selects $\ell=0,1,2,\dots$. The normalization is $P_\ell(1)=1$, and the weight is $w=1$:

$$P_\ell(x)=\frac1{2^\ell\ell!}\frac{d^\ell}{dx^\ell}(x^2-1)^\ell,\qquad \int_{-1}^{1}P_mP_n\,dx=\frac{2}{2n+1}\,\delta_{mn}.$$

$$\frac1{\sqrt{1-2xt+t^2}}=\sum_{\ell=0}^\infty P_\ell(x)\,t^\ell\ \ (|t|<1),\qquad (\ell+1)P_{\ell+1}=(2\ell+1)\,xP_\ell-\ell P_{\ell-1}.$$

The first few are $P_0=1$, $P_1=x$, $P_2=\tfrac12(3x^2-1)$, $P_3=\tfrac12(5x^3-3x)$. A function on $[-1,1]$ expands as $f=\sum a_\ell P_\ell$ with $a_\ell=\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1fP_\ell\,dx$. The generating function is the multipole expansion of $1/|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|$.

### Bessel functions

Bessel's equation $x^2y''+xy'+(x^2-\nu^2)y=0$ has the solution that is regular at $x=0$ (for $\nu\ge0$), the **Bessel function of the first kind**

$$J_\nu(x)=\sum_{m=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^m}{m!\,\Gamma(m+\nu+1)}\Big(\frac x2\Big)^{2m+\nu}.$$

The second solution $Y_\nu$ is singular at $0$. The recurrences are $J_{\nu-1}+J_{\nu+1}=\frac{2\nu}{x}J_\nu$ and $J_{\nu-1}-J_{\nu+1}=2J_\nu'$. Let $j_{\nu,k}$ denote the $k$th positive zero of $J_\nu$. The function $y(x)=J_\nu(\sqrt\lambda\,x)$ satisfies $-(xy')'+\frac{\nu^2}{x}y=\lambda\,x\,y$, so the weight is $x$:

$$\int_0^1x\,J_\nu(j_{\nu,m}x)\,J_\nu(j_{\nu,n}x)\,dx=\tfrac12\big[J_{\nu+1}(j_{\nu,n})\big]^2\,\delta_{mn}.$$

This is the orthogonality behind Fourier–Bessel series on a disk with Dirichlet condition $y(1)=0$.

### Hermite polynomials

The **physicists' Hermite polynomials** solve $y''-2xy'+2ny=0$ on $\mathbb R$ with weight $e^{-x^2}$:

$$H_0=1,\ H_1=2x,\ H_2=4x^2-2,\qquad H_{n+1}=2xH_n-2nH_{n-1},\qquad \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}H_mH_n\,e^{-x^2}dx=2^nn!\sqrt\pi\,\delta_{mn}.$$

The probabilists' polynomials $He_n$ use weight $e^{-x^2/2}$ and differ by scaling, so always check which convention a source uses. The functions $H_n(x)e^{-x^2/2}$ are the eigenfunctions of the quantum harmonic oscillator.

### Summary

| family | interval | weight |
|---|---|---|
| Legendre $P_\ell$ | $[-1,1]$ | $1$ |
| Bessel $J_\nu(j_{\nu,k}x)$ | $[0,1]$ | $x$ |
| Hermite $H_n$ | $(-\infty,\infty)$ | $e^{-x^2}$ |
| Laguerre $L_n$ | $[0,\infty)$ | $e^{-x}$ |

@example
title: Legendre expansion of $x^2$
problem: Expand $f(x)=x^2$ on $[-1,1]$ in Legendre polynomials.
step: Only even $\ell$ can appear, because $x^2$ is even and $P_\ell$ has parity $(-1)^\ell$. Use $a_\ell=\frac{2\ell+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1x^2P_\ell\,dx$.
step: For $\ell=0$: $a_0=\tfrac12\int_{-1}^1x^2dx=\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac23=\tfrac13$.
step: For $\ell=2$ with $P_2=\tfrac12(3x^2-1)$: $\int_{-1}^1x^2P_2\,dx=\tfrac12\big(3\cdot\tfrac25-\tfrac23\big)=\tfrac4{15}$, so $a_2=\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac4{15}=\tfrac23$.
step: For $\ell\ge4$ the integral vanishes, because $x^2$ is a combination of $P_0$ and $P_2$ and $P_\ell\perp P_0,P_2$. Therefore $x^2=\tfrac13P_0+\tfrac23P_2$.
step: Check: $\tfrac13+\tfrac23\cdot\tfrac12(3x^2-1)=\tfrac13+x^2-\tfrac13=x^2$.

@example
title: A beta integral with a half-integer exponent
problem: Evaluate $\int_0^1x^3\sqrt{1-x}\,dx$.
step: Match the form $\int_0^1t^{a-1}(1-t)^{b-1}dt$: $a-1=3$ and $b-1=\tfrac12$, so $a=4$ and $b=\tfrac32$.
step: The integral equals $B(4,\tfrac32)=\dfrac{\Gamma(4)\Gamma(\tfrac32)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{11}{2})}$.
step: Compute $\Gamma(4)=6$, $\Gamma(\tfrac32)=\tfrac12\sqrt\pi$, and $\Gamma(\tfrac{11}2)=\tfrac92\cdot\tfrac72\cdot\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi=\tfrac{945}{32}\sqrt\pi$.
step: Then $B=6\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi\cdot\tfrac{32}{945\sqrt\pi}=\tfrac{96}{945}=\tfrac{32}{315}$.
step: Check by substituting $u=1-x$ and integrating $(1-u)^3u^{1/2}$ termwise: $\tfrac23-\tfrac65+\tfrac67-\tfrac29=\tfrac{32}{315}$.

@mistakes
- Using $\Gamma(n)=n!$ instead of $\Gamma(n+1)=n!$ (an off-by-one that ruins every beta integral).
- Forgetting the weight $x$ in Bessel orthogonality, or the weight $e^{-x^2}$ for Hermite polynomials.
- Mixing normalizations: the Legendre polynomials are normalized by $P_\ell(1)=1$, not to unit norm, and physicists' and probabilists' Hermite polynomials differ.
- Using $\frac{2}{2n+1}$ as if it were the norm rather than the norm squared.

@practice num
prompt: Find the exact value of $\Gamma\!\left(\tfrac72\right)$.
answer: 15sqrt(pi)/8
check: gamma(3.5)
hint: Use $\Gamma(z+1)=z\,\Gamma(z)$ repeatedly, starting from $\Gamma(\tfrac72)=\tfrac52\,\Gamma(\tfrac52)$.
hint: Continue down to $\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\sqrt\pi$.
solution: $\Gamma(\tfrac72)=\tfrac52\cdot\tfrac32\cdot\tfrac12\,\Gamma(\tfrac12)=\tfrac{15}{8}\sqrt\pi\approx3.323$.

@practice num
prompt: Use the beta function to evaluate $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4\theta\,\cos^2\theta\,d\theta$ exactly.
answer: pi/32
check: quad(lambda t: sin(t)**4*cos(t)**2, 0, pi/2)
hint: Use $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^{2a-1}\theta\cos^{2b-1}\theta\,d\theta=\tfrac12B(a,b)$. Match $2a-1=4$ and $2b-1=2$.
hint: Then $a=\tfrac52$ and $b=\tfrac32$; write $B(a,b)$ with gamma functions.
solution: $\tfrac12B(\tfrac52,\tfrac32)=\tfrac12\dfrac{\Gamma(5/2)\Gamma(3/2)}{\Gamma(4)}=\tfrac12\cdot\dfrac{\tfrac34\sqrt\pi\cdot\tfrac12\sqrt\pi}{6}=\dfrac{\pi}{32}$.

@practice num
prompt: Expand $f(x)=x^3$ on $[-1,1]$ as $\sum a_\ell P_\ell(x)$. Find the exact coefficient $a_3$ of $P_3$.
answer: 2/5
check: 3.5*quad(lambda x: x**3*special.eval_legendre(3, x), -1, 1)
hint: Use $a_3=\frac{2\cdot3+1}{2}\int_{-1}^1x^3P_3(x)\,dx$ with $P_3=\tfrac12(5x^3-3x)$.
hint: You need $\int_{-1}^1x^6dx=\tfrac27$ and $\int_{-1}^1x^4dx=\tfrac25$.
solution: $\int_{-1}^1x^3P_3\,dx=\tfrac12\big(5\cdot\tfrac27-3\cdot\tfrac25\big)=\tfrac4{35}$, so $a_3=\tfrac72\cdot\tfrac4{35}=\tfrac25$. (The full expansion is $x^3=\tfrac35P_1+\tfrac25P_3$.)

choice*: $w(x)=x$
choice: $w(x)=1$
choice: $w(x)=1/x$

@quiz num
prompt: Expand the generating function $(1-2xt+t^2)^{-1/2}$ at $x=0$ in powers of $t$ and read off $P_4(0)$. Give an exact value.
answer: 3/8
check: special.eval_legendre(4, 0)
hint: At $x=0$ the generating function is $(1+t^2)^{-1/2}$.

solution: $(1+t^2)^{-1/2}=1-\tfrac12t^2+\tfrac38t^4-\cdots$, and the coefficient of $t^4$ is $P_4(0)=\tfrac38$. Check with $P_4=\tfrac18(35x^4-30x^2+3)$.
