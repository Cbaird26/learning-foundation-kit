@article
Perturbation theory begins by solving $i\,\partial_tU(t,t_0)=H_I(t)\,U(t,t_0)$ iteratively. The result, the **Dyson series**, is the bridge between the Hamiltonian and Feynman diagrams.

### The iterative solution

Integrate the equation with $U(t_0,t_0)=1$:
$$U(t,t_0)=1-i\int_{t_0}^tdt_1\,H_I(t_1)\,U(t_1,t_0).$$
Substituting this into itself repeatedly gives
$$U(t,t_0)=1+\sum_{n=1}^\infty(-i)^n\int_{t_0}^tdt_1\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt_2\cdots\int_{t_0}^{t_{n-1}}dt_n\,H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)\cdots H_I(t_n).$$
In every term $t_1>t_2>\dots>t_n$, and the operators stand in that order with later times to the left, since the rightmost factor acts first. The operators $H_I(t)$ at different times generally do not commute, so $U$ is not simply $\exp[-i\int H_I\,dt]$.

### Time ordering

Define the **time-ordered product** of bosonic operators by $T[A(t_1)B(t_2)]=A(t_1)B(t_2)$ if $t_1>t_2$ and $B(t_2)A(t_1)$ if $t_2>t_1$. The integral of $T[H_I(t_1)\cdots H_I(t_n)]$ over the whole cube $[t_0,t]^n$ equals $n!$ times the ordered integral above, because each of the $n!$ orderings of the times gives the same value after relabeling. Hence
$$U(t,t_0)=T\exp\Big[-i\int_{t_0}^tdt'\,H_I(t')\Big]=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-i)^n}{n!}\int_{t_0}^tdt_1\cdots dt_n\;T\big[H_I(t_1)\cdots H_I(t_n)\big].$$
The $1/n!$ appears only with the unrestricted integrals. If $[H_I(t),H_I(t')]=0$ for all times, $T$ can be dropped and $U=e^{-i\int H_I}$.

### The S-matrix as a covariant series

Take $t_0\to-\infty$, $t\to+\infty$ and $H_I(t)=\int d^3x\,\mathcal H_I(x)$:
$$S=T\exp\Big[-i\!\int d^4x\,\mathcal H_I(x)\Big]=1-i\!\int d^4x\,\mathcal H_I(x)+\frac{(-i)^2}{2!}\!\int d^4x\,d^4y\;T\big[\mathcal H_I(x)\mathcal H_I(y)\big]+\cdots$$
The derivation singled out a time, but the series is Lorentz covariant: at spacelike separation $[\mathcal H_I(x),\mathcal H_I(y)]=0$ (microcausality, week 4), so the time ordering is the same in every frame. For $\phi^4$ theory $\mathcal H_I=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi_I^4$, and the $n$th term carries $\lambda^n$.

### Operator content and counting

Split $\phi_I=\phi^++\phi^-$, where $\phi^+$ contains the annihilation operators and $\phi^-$ the creation operators. Between relativistically normalized states, $\langle0|\phi_I(x)|\mathbf p\rangle=e^{-ip\cdot x}$ and $\langle\mathbf p|\phi_I(x)|0\rangle=e^{ip\cdot x}$. The part of $\phi_I^4$ with two creation and two annihilation operators turns two particles into two. To count its matrix element between $|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\rangle$ and $\langle\mathbf p_1'\mathbf p_2'|$ with all momenta distinct: choose which two of the four fields annihilate, $\binom42=6$; assign the incoming particles to them, $2!$; assign the outgoing particles to the creating fields, $2!$. So
$$\langle\mathbf p_1'\mathbf p_2'|\,{:}\phi_I^4(x){:}\,|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\rangle=4!\;e^{i(p_1'+p_2'-p_1-p_2)\cdot x}.$$
The $4!$ cancels the $\frac{1}{4!}$ in $\mathcal H_I$. The other pieces of $\phi_I^4$ (fewer fields, from self-contractions) shift the vacuum energy and masses and do not contribute to this amplitude. Using $\int d^4x\,e^{iq\cdot x}=(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(q)$,
$$\langle\mathbf p_1'\mathbf p_2'|\,S^{(1)}\,|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\rangle=-i\lambda\,(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(p_1'+p_2'-p_1-p_2).$$
The delta function is conservation of energy and momentum. Wick's theorem, next week, automates this counting at every order.

### Checks to make

- $\int d^4x\,\mathcal H_I$ is dimensionless, so every term of the series is a dimensionless operator.
- Unitarity, $S^\dagger S=1$, relates the second-order term to the square of the first: probability lost from the initial state equals probability gained by the others.
- For a solvable model (below) compare the order-$g^2$ term with an exact result.

@example
title: A forced oscillator to second order
problem: A mode with $H_0=\omega a^\dagger a$ is driven by $H_{\rm int}=g(a+a^\dagger)$. Find $H_I(t)$, the first-order amplitude $\langle1|U^{(1)}(T,0)|0\rangle$ and the real part of the second-order vacuum amplitude $\langle0|U^{(2)}(T,0)|0\rangle$, and check unitarity at order $g^2$.
step: Since $e^{iH_0t}a\,e^{-iH_0t}=a\,e^{-i\omega t}$, we get $H_I(t)=g\big(a\,e^{-i\omega t}+a^\dagger e^{i\omega t}\big)$.
step: First order: $U^{(1)}=-i\int_0^Tdt\,H_I(t)$, so $\langle1|U^{(1)}|0\rangle=-ig\int_0^Te^{i\omega t}dt=-\frac{g}{\omega}\big(e^{i\omega T}-1\big)$ and $\langle0|U^{(1)}|0\rangle=0$.
step: The probability is $P_{0\to1}=\frac{g^2}{\omega^2}|e^{i\omega T}-1|^2=\frac{4g^2}{\omega^2}\sin^2\frac{\omega T}{2}$.
step: Second order: $\langle0|H_I(t_1)H_I(t_2)|0\rangle=g^2e^{-i\omega t_1}e^{i\omega t_2}\langle0|aa^\dagger|0\rangle=g^2e^{-i\omega(t_1-t_2)}$, so $\langle0|U^{(2)}|0\rangle=-g^2\int_0^Tdt_1\int_0^{t_1}dt_2\,e^{-i\omega(t_1-t_2)}$.
step: For the real part, $\int_0^{t_1}\cos\omega(t_1-t_2)\,dt_2=\frac{\sin\omega t_1}{\omega}$, and then $\int_0^T\frac{\sin\omega t_1}{\omega}dt_1=\frac{1-\cos\omega T}{\omega^2}$. So $\mathrm{Re}\,\langle0|U^{(2)}|0\rangle=-\frac{g^2(1-\cos\omega T)}{\omega^2}=-\frac{2g^2}{\omega^2}\sin^2\frac{\omega T}{2}$.
step: Unitarity: $|\langle0|U|0\rangle|^2=1+2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\langle0|U^{(2)}|0\rangle+O(g^4)=1-\frac{4g^2}{\omega^2}\sin^2\frac{\omega T}{2}=1-P_{0\to1}$. The probability lost from the vacuum is the probability found in $|1\rangle$.

@example
title: The first-order $2\to2$ amplitude in $\phi^4$ theory
problem: Find $\langle\mathbf p_1'\mathbf p_2'|S^{(1)}|\mathbf p_1\mathbf p_2\rangle$ for $\mathcal H_I=\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi_I^4$ with all four momenta distinct.
step: $S^{(1)}=-i\frac{\lambda}{4!}\int d^4x\,\phi_I^4(x)$. Only the part of $\phi_I^4$ with two annihilation and two creation operators can change two particles into two.
step: Choose which two of the four fields annihilate: $\binom42=6$ ways.
step: Assign the two incoming particles to the two annihilating fields ($2!$ ways, each giving a factor $e^{-ip\cdot x}$), and the two outgoing particles to the two creating fields ($2!$ ways, each giving $e^{ip'\cdot x}$).
step: The total count is $6\cdot2\cdot2=24=4!$, all with the common phase $e^{i(p_1'+p_2'-p_1-p_2)\cdot x}$.
step: Integrate over $x$ with $\int d^4x\,e^{iq\cdot x}=(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(q)$ to get $-i\lambda\,(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}(p_1'+p_2'-p_1-p_2)$. In the language of week 11, $i\mathcal M=-i\lambda$.

@mistakes
- Writing $U=\exp[-i\int H_I\,dt]$ without the time-ordering symbol. That is valid only when the $H_I(t)$ at different times commute.
- Putting the earliest time on the left. The latest time stands leftmost because the rightmost operator acts first.
- Mixing up the factors of $1/n!$: they appear with unrestricted integrals and $T$, but not with the nested ordered integrals.
- Confusing $H_I(t)=\int d^3x\,\mathcal H_I$ (a spatial integral) with the spacetime integral $\int d^4x\,\mathcal H_I$ that appears in $S$.

choice*: $H_I(t)$ at different times generally does not commute, so the product of exponentials is not the exponential of the sum.
choice: $H_I(t)$ is not Hermitian.
choice: The integral $\int H_I\,dt'$ diverges for any interaction.
choice: $U$ is not unitary unless time ordering is imposed by hand.
hint: Compare $e^{A+B}$ and $e^Ae^B$ for non-commuting $A$ and $B$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the Dyson series are correct?
choice*: In the nested-integral form every term has $t_1>t_2>\dots>t_n$, with later times standing to the left.
choice*: If $[H_I(t),H_I(t')]=0$ for all $t,t'$, then $U(t,t_0)=\exp\big[-i\int_{t_0}^tH_I(t')\,dt'\big]$.
choice: The factor $1/n!$ is present both in the nested-integral form and in the unrestricted $T$-ordered form.
choice: $U=\exp[-i\int H_I\,dt]$ holds for any $H_I(t)$, because the exponential of an integral is the integral of the exponential.
hint: Count how many orderings of $n$ times exist inside the cube $[t_0,t]^n$.
hint: Think about when $e^{A+B}=e^Ae^B$.
solution: The statements about the ordering $t_1>t_2>\dots>t_n$ and about commuting $H_I(t)$ giving $U=\exp[-i\int H_I]$ are correct. The claim that $1/n!$ is present in both forms is false: the nested ordered integral already restricts to one of the $n!$ orderings, so there is no $1/n!$. The unrestricted cube integral with $T$ contains all $n!$ orderings, which is where $1/n!$ comes from. The claim that $U=\exp[-i\int H_I\,dt]$ holds for any $H_I(t)$ is false in general; it requires commuting $H_I(t)$.
