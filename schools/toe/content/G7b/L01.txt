@article
A **field** assigns a value to every point of spacetime. Quantum field theory is built by quantizing a classical field theory, so you first need the classical action, equations of motion and symmetries. This lesson also fixes the conventions used in every later lesson; check them against any text you read alongside this course, because texts differ.

### Conventions

- **Units.** $\hbar = c = 1$. Length and time then have dimension $[\text{mass}]^{-1}$, while energy, momentum and mass have dimension $[\text{mass}]^{1}$. Quote every dimension as a power of mass.
- **Metric.** Signature $(+,-,-,-)$: $\eta_{\mu\nu}=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)$, $x^\mu=(t,\mathbf x)$, $x\cdot y=x^0y^0-\mathbf x\cdot\mathbf y$. A particle of mass $m$ has $p^2=p_\mu p^\mu=E^2-|\mathbf p|^2=m^2$.
- **Derivatives.** $\partial_\mu=\partial/\partial x^\mu=(\partial_t,\nabla)$, $\partial^\mu=(\partial_t,-\nabla)$ and $\Box=\partial_\mu\partial^\mu=\partial_t^2-\nabla^2$.
- **Fourier transforms.** $\tilde f(\mathbf p)=\int d^3x\,e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)$ and $f(\mathbf x)=\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}\tilde f(\mathbf p)$, so $\int d^3x\,e^{i(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)\cdot\mathbf x}=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$. Every $d^3p$ carries a $(2\pi)^{-3}$. A positive-frequency plane wave is $e^{-ip\cdot x}=e^{-iEt+i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}$.
- **Operators (from week 3).** $[a_{\mathbf p},a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}]=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$; the field is expanded with a factor $1/\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}$; one-particle states are relativistically normalized, $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle=2E_{\mathbf p}(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$.

### Action and Euler–Lagrange equations

A relativistic field theory is specified by a **Lagrangian density** $\mathcal L(\phi,\partial_\mu\phi)$, a Lorentz scalar, and the **action**
$$S[\phi]=\int d^4x\,\mathcal L(\phi,\partial_\mu\phi).$$
The physical field makes $S$ stationary under variations $\delta\phi$ that vanish on the boundary of the integration region. Vary and integrate by parts:
$$\delta S=\int d^4x\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}-\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\right]\delta\phi+\int d^4x\,\partial_\mu\!\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\,\delta\phi\right).$$
The second term is a surface term and vanishes because $\delta\phi=0$ on the boundary. Since $\delta\phi$ is otherwise arbitrary, the **Euler–Lagrange equation** follows:
$$\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}=0.$$
With several fields you get one equation per field. For a complex field, $\phi$ and $\phi^*$ may be varied as independent fields.

### The Klein–Gordon field

The free real scalar field has
$$\mathcal L=\tfrac12\,\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi-\tfrac12m^2\phi^2=\tfrac12\dot\phi^2-\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2-\tfrac12m^2\phi^2.$$
Here $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial^\mu\phi$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi=-m^2\phi$, so the equation of motion is the **Klein–Gordon equation**
$$(\Box+m^2)\phi=0.$$
A plane wave $e^{-ik\cdot x}$ solves it exactly when $k^2=m^2$, that is $\omega=\sqrt{|\mathbf k|^2+m^2}$: the relativistic dispersion relation. The signs are not optional. The kinetic term $+\frac12\dot\phi^2$ must be positive or the energy is unbounded below, and the mass term enters $\mathcal L$ with a minus sign because it is potential energy. Flip it and you get $\omega^2=k^2-m^2$, a tachyon.

### Momentum and Hamiltonian

The momentum conjugate to $\phi(\mathbf x,t)$ is $\pi=\partial\mathcal L/\partial\dot\phi=\dot\phi$, and the Hamiltonian density is
$$\mathcal H=\pi\dot\phi-\mathcal L=\tfrac12\pi^2+\tfrac12(\nabla\phi)^2+\tfrac12m^2\phi^2\ \ge\ 0.$$
Week 3 promotes $\phi(\mathbf x)$ and $\pi(\mathbf x)$ to operators with equal-time commutation relations.

### Interactions and dimensional analysis

With $\hbar=1$ the action is dimensionless, so in $d$ spacetime dimensions $[\mathcal L]=d$. The kinetic term fixes $[\phi]=(d-2)/2$, which is $1$ in $d=4$. A term $\frac{g_n}{n!}\phi^n$ then has coupling dimension
$$[g_n]=d-n\,\frac{d-2}{2}.$$
In $d=4$: $[m^2]=2$, $[g_3]=1$, $[\lambda]=0$ for $\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$, and $[g_n]<0$ for $n\ge5$. A coupling with non-negative mass dimension is a first requirement for renormalizability, a topic for a later course.

For $\mathcal L=\frac12(\partial\phi)^2-\frac12m^2\phi^2-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$ the Euler–Lagrange equation is nonlinear, $(\Box+m^2)\phi=-\frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^3$. For a spatially uniform field it reduces to Newton's equation $\ddot\phi=-V'(\phi)$ with $V=\frac12m^2\phi^2+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$, a useful sanity check on signs.

### A complex field

For a complex scalar, $\mathcal L=\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi$ (no factors of $\frac12$) gives $(\Box+m^2)\phi=0$ from varying $\phi^*$, and $(\Box+m^2)\phi^*=0$ from varying $\phi$. It describes two real fields $\phi=(\phi_1+i\phi_2)/\sqrt2$ with equal mass.

@example
title: The field equation of $\phi^4$ theory
problem: Derive the equation of motion for $\mathcal L=\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi-\frac12m^2\phi^2-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$.
step: Differentiate with respect to the derivative of the field: the two factors of $\partial\phi$ in $\frac12\eta^{\alpha\beta}\partial_\alpha\phi\,\partial_\beta\phi$ give a factor $2$ that cancels the $\frac12$, so $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial^\mu\phi$.
step: Take the divergence of that result: $\partial_\mu(\partial^\mu\phi)=\Box\phi$.
step: Differentiate with respect to the field itself: $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi=-m^2\phi-\frac{\lambda}{4!}\cdot4\phi^3=-m^2\phi-\frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^3$.
step: Insert both pieces into $\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}-\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}=0$: $\Box\phi+m^2\phi+\frac{\lambda}{3!}\phi^3=0$.
step: Check: for $\lambda=0$ this is the Klein–Gordon equation, and for a uniform field it reads $\ddot\phi=-V'(\phi)$ with $V=\frac12m^2\phi^2+\frac{\lambda}{4!}\phi^4$, so the sign of the interaction term is right.

@example
title: Mass dimension of a $\phi^6$ coupling
problem: Find the mass dimension of $g$ in $\mathcal L\supset-\frac{g}{6!}\phi^6$ in $d=4$ and in $d=3$ spacetime dimensions.
step: The action is dimensionless when $\hbar=1$ and $S=\int d^dx\,\mathcal L$ with $[d^dx]=-d$, so $[\mathcal L]=d$.
step: The kinetic term $(\partial\phi)^2$ has dimension $2[\phi]+2$, which must equal $d$. Hence $[\phi]=(d-2)/2$.
step: Then $[\phi^6]=3(d-2)$ and $[g]=d-3(d-2)=6-2d$.
step: For $d=4$ this gives $[g]=-2$: the coupling has dimension of inverse mass squared. For $d=3$ it gives $[g]=0$, so $\phi^6$ is dimensionless there.

@mistakes
- Mixing signatures: writing $p^2=-m^2$ or $\Box=-\partial_t^2+\nabla^2$ while the rest of the calculation uses $(+,-,-,-)$. Fix the metric once and always check that a plane wave satisfies $p^2=m^2$.
- Forgetting the relative minus sign in $\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi=\dot\phi^2-(\nabla\phi)^2$, which gives gradient energy of the wrong sign.
- Skipping the surface term without saying that $\delta\phi$ vanishes on the boundary, or differentiating $\mathcal L$ with respect to $\partial_\mu\phi$ and forgetting to take $\partial_\mu$ of the result.
- Counting dimensions as if $[\phi]=1$ in every spacetime dimension; $[\phi]=(d-2)/2$.

choice*: $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi-\frac12m^2\phi^2$
choice: $\frac12\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial^\mu\phi+\frac12m^2\phi^2$

choice: $\frac12\dot\phi^2+\frac12(\nabla\phi)^2-\frac12m^2\phi^2$
hint: Apply the Euler–Lagrange equation to each candidate and look at the sign of the $m^2$ term and of the $\ddot\phi$ term.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from $\delta S=0$, derive the Euler–Lagrange equation for a Lagrangian density $\mathcal L(\phi,\partial_\mu\phi)$, saying exactly where and why a surface term is dropped. Then vary $\phi^*$ and $\phi$ independently in $\mathcal L=\partial_\mu\phi^*\partial^\mu\phi-m^2\phi^*\phi$ and write the two field equations.
hint: Write $\delta S$ using $\delta(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial_\mu\delta\phi$, then move the derivative off $\delta\phi$ with the product rule.
hint: The term you create is a total divergence $\partial_\mu(\cdots)$. Ask what the divergence theorem says about its integral and what you know about $\delta\phi$ on the boundary.
hint: For the complex field you need $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi^*)$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi^*$, treating $\phi$ as a constant.
solution: Use $\delta(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial_\mu\delta\phi$ to write $\delta S=\int d^4x\,[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}\delta\phi+\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\partial_\mu\delta\phi]$. Rewrite the second term as $\partial_\mu(\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}\delta\phi)-(\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)})\delta\phi$. The total derivative integrates to a surface integral over the boundary of the spacetime region, where $\delta\phi=0$ by assumption, so it is dropped. What remains is $\delta S=\int d^4x\,[\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial\phi}-\partial_\mu\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial(\partial_\mu\phi)}]\delta\phi=0$ for arbitrary $\delta\phi$, so the bracket vanishes. For the complex field, varying $\phi^*$ uses $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\phi^*)=\partial^\mu\phi$ and $\partial\mathcal L/\partial\phi^*=-m^2\phi$, giving $(\Box+m^2)\phi=0$. Varying $\phi$ gives $(\Box+m^2)\phi^*=0$.
rubric: The variation uses $\delta(\partial_\mu\phi)=\partial_\mu\delta\phi$ and integrates the $\partial_\mu\delta\phi$ term by parts.
rubric: The surface term is identified as a boundary integral that vanishes because $\delta\phi=0$ on the boundary.
rubric: Arbitrariness of $\delta\phi$ is used to set the bracket to zero, giving the Euler–Lagrange equation.
rubric: Both $(\Box+m^2)\phi=0$ and $(\Box+m^2)\phi^*=0$ are obtained, with correct intermediate derivatives.
