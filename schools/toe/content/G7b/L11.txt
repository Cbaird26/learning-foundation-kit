@article
The Feynman rules of week 10 produce $i\mathcal M$. This lesson explains what $\mathcal M$ is, how to evaluate it at physical kinematics, and how to check it before using it for a measurable quantity.

### The invariant amplitude

Write $S=1+iT$. Momentum conservation factors out of every matrix element between relativistically normalized states:
$$\langle\mathbf p_1'\cdots\mathbf p_k'|\,iT\,|\mathbf p_1\cdots\mathbf p_j\rangle=(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}\Big(\sum p_{\rm in}-\sum p_{\rm out}\Big)\;i\mathcal M.$$
$i\mathcal M$ is the sum of connected, amputated Feynman diagrams. For scalars, external lines carry no factors because $\langle0|\phi(0)|\mathbf p\rangle=1$ in this normalization. $\mathcal M$ is built from Lorentz-invariant pieces, so it depends only on invariants. Unitarity, $S^\dagger S=1$, gives $-i(T-T^\dagger)=T^\dagger T$: at tree level $\mathcal M$ is real in the physical region, and imaginary parts first arise from loops.

### Mandelstam variables and kinematics

For $p_1p_2\to p_1'p_2'$ define
$$s=(p_1+p_2)^2,\quad t=(p_1-p_1')^2,\quad u=(p_1-p_2')^2,\qquad s+t+u=m_1^2+m_2^2+m_{1'}^2+m_{2'}^2.$$
In the CM frame with all masses equal to $m$, each particle has $E=\sqrt s/2$ and $|\mathbf p|=\sqrt{s/4-m^2}$. With $\theta$ the angle between $\mathbf p_1$ and $\mathbf p_1'$,
$$t=-2|\mathbf p|^2(1-\cos\theta),\qquad u=-2|\mathbf p|^2(1+\cos\theta).$$
The physical region is $s\ge4m^2$ and $t,u\le0$. For unequal masses compute $t=m_1^2+m_{1'}^2-2(E_1E_{1'}-|\mathbf p||\mathbf p'|\cos\theta)$ and $u$ likewise, or evaluate $(p_1-p_{1'})^2$ directly from four-vectors, which is the safest check.

### Tree-level amplitudes

- $\phi^4$ theory: $\mathcal M=-\lambda$.
- $\phi^3$ theory: $\mathcal M=-g^2\Big[\dfrac{1}{s-m^2}+\dfrac{1}{t-m^2}+\dfrac{1}{u-m^2}\Big]$, and for the decay $\phi\to\phi\phi$ simply $\mathcal M=-g$.

For identical particles $\mathcal M$ is symmetric under permutations of the momenta. **Crossing** relates an incoming particle of momentum $p$ to an outgoing one of momentum $-p$ and permutes $s$, $t$, $u$ among themselves. In $\phi^3$ theory the $s$-channel pole at $s=m^2$ lies below the threshold $4m^2$, so it is not reached by a physical $2\to2$ process.

### Dimension of an amplitude

Relativistically normalized states have mass dimension $-1$, since $\langle\mathbf p|\mathbf q\rangle=2E(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$ has dimension $-2$. Then $\langle f|S|i\rangle$ has dimension $-n$ for $n$ external particles in all, and $(2\pi)^4\delta^{(4)}$ has dimension $-4$, so
$$[\mathcal M]=4-n.$$
For $2\to2$ ($n=4$) the amplitude is dimensionless, as $\lambda$ and $g^2/s$ (with $[g]=1$) are. For $1\to2$ ($n=3$) it has dimension $1$, as $\mathcal M=-g$ does. For $2\to3$ it has dimension $-1$. A mismatch means a lost factor of a coupling or a momentum.

### Fermions and spin sums

For the Yukawa interaction $\mathcal L_{\rm int}=-g\,\phi\,\bar\psi\psi$, the decay $\phi(P)\to\psi(p_1)\bar\psi(p_2)$ has $i\mathcal M=-ig\,\bar u^s(p_1)\,v^{s'}(p_2)$: the outgoing fermion contributes $\bar u$ and the outgoing antifermion $v$. An unpolarized rate needs $\sum_{s,s'}|\mathcal M|^2$. Use $(\bar uv)^*=\bar vu$ and the completeness relations $\sum u\bar u=\not p+m$ and $\sum v\bar v=\not p-m$ from week 6:
$$\sum_{s,s'}|\mathcal M|^2=g^2\,\mathrm{tr}\big[(\not p_1+m)(\not p_2-m)\big]=4g^2\,(p_1\cdot p_2-m^2)=2g^2\,(M^2-4m^2),$$
where $M^2=(p_1+p_2)^2=2m^2+2p_1\cdot p_2$ and the terms linear in $m$ drop out of the trace. The result has dimension $2$, consistent with $[\mathcal M]=1$.

### A checklist before using an amplitude

1. Is $[\mathcal M]=4-n$?
2. Do $s+t+u$ and the signs of $t,u$ agree with the physical region?
3. Is $\mathcal M$ symmetric under exchanges of identical particles?
4. Is each vertex normalized so that the $n!$ from identical lines cancels the $1/n!$ in the coupling?

@example
title: A tree amplitude at a physical point
problem: In $\phi^3$ theory with $m=1$ and $g=1$, find $\mathcal M(\phi\phi\to\phi\phi)$ in the CM frame at $\sqrt s=4$ and $\theta=60^\circ$.
step: $s=16$, so $|\mathbf p|^2=s/4-m^2=4-1=3$.
step: $t=-2|\mathbf p|^2(1-\cos\theta)=-6(1-\tfrac12)=-3$.
step: $u=-2|\mathbf p|^2(1+\cos\theta)=-6(1+\tfrac12)=-9$. Check: $s+t+u=16-3-9=4=4m^2$.
step: $\mathcal M=-g^2\Big[\frac{1}{16-1}+\frac{1}{-3-1}+\frac{1}{-9-1}\Big]=-\Big[\frac{1}{15}-\frac14-\frac{1}{10}\Big]=-\frac{4-15-6}{60}=\frac{17}{60}$.
step: The amplitude is dimensionless, as required for $n=4$.

@example
title: Spin-summed amplitude squared for a Yukawa decay
problem: For $\phi\to\psi\bar\psi$ with $\mathcal L_{\rm int}=-g\phi\bar\psi\psi$, compute $\sum_{s,s'}|\mathcal M|^2$ in terms of $M$ (the mass of $\phi$) and $m$ (the fermion mass).
step: The amplitude is $\mathcal M=-g\,\bar u^s(p_1)v^{s'}(p_2)$, so $|\mathcal M|^2=g^2\,\bar u(p_1)v(p_2)\,\bar v(p_2)u(p_1)$.
step: Sum over spins using $\sum_su\bar u=\not p_1+m$ and $\sum_{s'}v\bar v=\not p_2-m$: the sum is $g^2\,\mathrm{tr}\big[(\not p_1+m)(\not p_2-m)\big]$.
step: Expand the trace. The terms linear in $m$ contain one gamma matrix and vanish, leaving $\mathrm{tr}(\not p_1\not p_2)-m^2\,\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbb 1=4p_1\cdot p_2-4m^2$.
step: Use $M^2=(p_1+p_2)^2=2m^2+2p_1\cdot p_2$, so $p_1\cdot p_2=\frac{M^2}{2}-m^2$.
step: Then $\sum|\mathcal M|^2=4g^2\big(\frac{M^2}{2}-2m^2\big)=2g^2(M^2-4m^2)$. It vanishes at threshold $M=2m$.

@mistakes
- Using the wrong signs for the invariants. In the physical region $s\ge4m^2$ and $t,u\le0$, and $s+t+u=4m^2$ (not $0$) for four equal masses.
- Using non-relativistic state normalization, which gives wrong powers of $2E$. The dimension rule $[\mathcal M]=4-n$ catches this.
- Putting propagators on external lines of $\mathcal M$.
- Mixing up the spin sums: $\sum u\bar u=\not p+m$ but $\sum v\bar v=\not p-m$.
