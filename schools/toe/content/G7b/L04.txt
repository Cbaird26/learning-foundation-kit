@article
The central object of perturbation theory is the **two-point function**: the amplitude to disturb the field at one point and find the disturbance at another. For the free field it follows entirely from the mode expansion and $[a_{\mathbf p},a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}]=(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$.

### The Wightman function

Insert the expansions and use $a_{\mathbf p}|0\rangle=0$. Only the term $a_{\mathbf p}a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}$ survives:
$$D(x-y)\equiv\langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle=\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}\frac{1}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}.$$
The measure is Lorentz invariant, so $D$ depends only on $(x-y)^2$ (and on the sign of $(x-y)^0$ when the separation is timelike). For spacelike separation, $(x-y)^2=-s^2<0$,
$$D(x-y)=\frac{m}{4\pi^2 s}\,K_1(ms),$$
where $K_1$ is the modified Bessel function. It decays like $e^{-ms}$: the vacuum is correlated over a Compton wavelength $1/m$. For $m=0$, $D(x)=-\dfrac{1}{4\pi^2\big[(x^0-i\epsilon)^2-|\mathbf x|^2\big]}$.

### The commutator and causality

The commutator of two free fields involves only $[a,a^\dagger]$, so it is a c-number function:
$$[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=D(x-y)-D(y-x).$$
At equal times, $e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}=e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}$, and the substitution $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$ shows $D(x-y)=D(y-x)$. Any spacelike separation can be boosted to equal times, and $D$ is invariant, so
$$[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=0\qquad\text{for}\quad(x-y)^2<0.$$
This is **microcausality**: measurements at spacelike separation cannot influence each other. Note that the correlation $D$ itself does not vanish there; only the commutator does. The same expansion reproduces $[\phi(\mathbf x),\pi(\mathbf y)]=i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$. Inside the light cone the commutator is nonzero, and the **retarded** function $\theta(x^0-y^0)\langle0|[\phi(x),\phi(y)]|0\rangle$ describes propagation of a disturbance.

### The Feynman propagator

The time-ordered product is $T\phi(x)\phi(y)=\theta(x^0-y^0)\phi(x)\phi(y)+\theta(y^0-x^0)\phi(y)\phi(x)$, and the **Feynman propagator** is
$$D_F(x-y)\equiv\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle=\theta(x^0-y^0)D(x-y)+\theta(y^0-x^0)D(y-x).$$
It has a four-dimensional Fourier representation,
$$D_F(x-y)=\int\frac{d^4p}{(2\pi)^4}\frac{i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}\,e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)},\qquad p^2=(p^0)^2-|\mathbf p|^2,\quad\epsilon\to0^+.$$
Here is why. The denominator $(p^0)^2-E_{\mathbf p}^2+i\epsilon$ vanishes at $p^0=\pm(E_{\mathbf p}-i\epsilon')$: the positive-energy pole lies just below the real axis and the negative-energy pole just above. For $t=x^0-y^0>0$ the factor $e^{-ip^0t}$ decays in the lower half-plane, so close the contour there. It encircles the pole at $+E_{\mathbf p}$ clockwise and gives $\frac{1}{2E_{\mathbf p}}e^{-iE_{\mathbf p}t}$, the integrand of $D(x-y)$. For $t<0$ close the contour above, pick up the pole at $-E_{\mathbf p}$ counterclockwise and, after $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$, obtain $D(y-x)$.

Acting on the integrand with the Klein–Gordon operator, $(\Box_x+m^2)e^{-ip\cdot x}=(m^2-p^2)e^{-ip\cdot x}$, so
$$(\Box_x+m^2)\,D_F(x-y)=-i\,\delta^{(4)}(x-y).$$
$D_F$ is therefore a Green's function of the Klein–Gordon operator, and the $i\epsilon$ prescription selects which one. The momentum-space **propagator** $\dfrac{i}{p^2-m^2+i\epsilon}$ is defined off-shell, $p^2\neq m^2$; internal lines of Feynman diagrams carry such "virtual" momenta, while external states obey $p^2=m^2$.

### Checks worth making

- $D_F(x-y)=D_F(y-x)$, since time ordering is symmetric.
- At spacelike separation $D_F=D$, which is nonzero.
- Dimension: in $d=4$, $[\phi]=1$, so $[D_F(x)]=2$ and $[\tilde D_F(p)]=-2$.

@example
title: The $p^0$ integral for $t>0$
problem: Evaluate $I(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dp^0}{2\pi}\,\frac{i}{(p^0)^2-E^2+i\epsilon}\,e^{-ip^0t}$ for $t>0$, $E>0$, in the limit $\epsilon\to0^+$.
step: Factor the denominator as $(p^0-E+i\epsilon')(p^0+E-i\epsilon')$ with $\epsilon'=\epsilon/2E$. The poles are at $p^0=E-i\epsilon'$ (below the real axis) and $p^0=-E+i\epsilon'$ (above).
step: For $t>0$, $|e^{-ip^0t}|=e^{t\,\mathrm{Im}\,p^0}$ decays in the lower half-plane, so close the contour there. It is traversed clockwise and gives $-2\pi i$ times the residue at $p^0=E-i\epsilon'$.
step: The residue of $\frac{i\,e^{-ip^0t}}{(p^0-E)(p^0+E)}$ at $p^0=E$ is $\frac{i}{2E}e^{-iEt}$.
step: $I(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi}(-2\pi i)\frac{i}{2E}e^{-iEt}=\frac{1}{2E}e^{-iEt}$.
step: This is the integrand of $D(x-y)$ at fixed $\mathbf p$, so for $x^0>y^0$ the remaining $\mathbf p$ integral gives $D_F=D(x-y)$.

@example
title: The massless two-point function at imaginary time
problem: For $m=0$, show that $D(x)=\langle0|\phi(x)\phi(0)|0\rangle$ equals $\frac{1}{4\pi^2(\tau^2+r^2)}$ at $x^0=-i\tau$ and $|\mathbf x|=r$, with $\tau>0$.
step: Set $E_{\mathbf p}=p$ and use the angular integral $\int d\Omega\,e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}=4\pi\,\frac{\sin pr}{pr}$. Then $D=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^3}\int_0^\infty p^2dp\,\frac{1}{2p}\,\frac{4\pi\sin pr}{pr}\,e^{-ipx^0}=\frac{1}{4\pi^2r}\int_0^\infty\sin(pr)\,e^{-ipx^0}\,dp$.
step: At $x^0=-i\tau$ we have $e^{-ipx^0}=e^{-p\tau}$, and the integral converges: $\int_0^\infty\sin(pr)\,e^{-p\tau}dp=\frac{r}{r^2+\tau^2}$.
step: Hence $D=\frac{1}{4\pi^2(r^2+\tau^2)}$.
step: This agrees with $-\frac{1}{4\pi^2x^2}$ because $x^2=(x^0)^2-r^2=-\tau^2-r^2$. At equal times it becomes $\frac{1}{4\pi^2r^2}>0$.

@mistakes
- Saying the commutator $[\phi(x),\phi(y)]$ vanishes everywhere, or that $D(x-y)$ vanishes at spacelike separation. The commutator vanishes only outside the light cone; the correlation function does not.
- Dropping the $i\epsilon$ or flipping its sign. Without it the $p^0$ integral is ill defined, and the opposite sign gives the anti-time-ordered function.
- Getting the sign convention of the Green's function equation wrong. With these conventions $(\Box+m^2)D_F=-i\delta^{(4)}$.
- Closing the contour in the wrong half-plane. The sign of $t=x^0-y^0$ decides it through the decay of $e^{-ip^0t}$.

@practice self
prompt: (a) Using the mode expansions of $\phi$ and $\pi$ at equal time, show that $[\phi(\mathbf x),\pi(\mathbf y)]=i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$. (b) Show that $[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=0$ for $x^0=y^0$ and explain why this extends to every spacelike separation.
hint: For (a), write both fields at $t=0$ and note that only $[a_{\mathbf p},a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}]$ and its conjugate contribute; the square-root factors become $\frac12$ after the delta function acts.
hint: For (b), write $[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=D(x-y)-D(y-x)$ and look at what $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$ does to the exponent when $x^0=y^0$.
hint: For general spacelike separation, ask what the Lorentz invariance of $d^3p/(2E_{\mathbf p})$ lets you do with the frame.
solution: (a) At $t=0$, $\phi(\mathbf x)=\int_p\frac{1}{\sqrt{2E_{\mathbf p}}}(a_{\mathbf p}e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x}+a^\dagger_{\mathbf p}e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x})$ and $\pi(\mathbf y)=\int_q(-i)\sqrt{E_{\mathbf q}/2}\,(a_{\mathbf q}e^{i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y}-a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y})$, with $\int_p=\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}$. Only $[a,a^\dagger]$ terms survive: $[\phi(\mathbf x),\pi(\mathbf y)]=\int_p\int_q\frac{-i}{2}\sqrt{E_{\mathbf q}/E_{\mathbf p}}\big(-[a_{\mathbf p},a^\dagger_{\mathbf q}]e^{i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y}+[a^\dagger_{\mathbf p},a_{\mathbf q}]e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf x+i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf y}\big)$. The delta function sets $\mathbf q=\mathbf p$, so the square root is $1$, and $[a^\dagger_{\mathbf p},a_{\mathbf q}]=-(2\pi)^3\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf p-\mathbf q)$. The result is $\frac{i}{2}\int_p\big(e^{i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}+e^{-i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}\big)=i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$. (b) $[\phi(x),\phi(y)]=D(x-y)-D(y-x)=\int_p\frac{1}{2E_{\mathbf p}}\big(e^{-ip\cdot(x-y)}-e^{ip\cdot(x-y)}\big)$. At $x^0=y^0$ the exponentials are $e^{\pm i\mathbf p\cdot(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)}$, and $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$ in one term (with $E_{\mathbf p}$ even) shows the two integrals are equal, so the difference is zero. For any spacelike $x-y$ there is a frame where $x^0=y^0$; the measure $d^3p/(2E_{\mathbf p})$ and the exponent $p\cdot(x-y)$ are invariant, so the commutator, a Lorentz scalar function, vanishes in every frame.
rubric: The mode expansions at equal time are written with the correct prefactors and only $[a,a^\dagger]$ terms are kept.
rubric: The two surviving terms combine, via $\int\frac{d^3p}{(2\pi)^3}e^{\pm i\mathbf p\cdot\mathbf r}=\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf r)$, to $i\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf x-\mathbf y)$ with the correct sign.
rubric: The commutator is written as $D(x-y)-D(y-x)$ and shown to vanish at equal times by $\mathbf p\to-\mathbf p$.
rubric: The extension to all spacelike separations uses Lorentz invariance of the measure and the existence of a frame with equal times.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the Feynman propagator $D_F(x-y)=\langle0|T\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$ of the free real scalar field are true?
choice*: $D_F(x-y)=D_F(y-x)$.
choice*: $(\Box_x+m^2)D_F(x-y)=-i\delta^{(4)}(x-y)$.
choice*: In the $p^0$ integral the positive-energy pole sits at $p^0=E_{\mathbf p}-i\epsilon'$, just below the real axis.
choice: $D_F(x-y)=0$ when $(x-y)^2<0$.
choice: For $x^0>y^0$, $D_F(x-y)=\langle0|[\phi(x),\phi(y)]|0\rangle$.
hint: Recall the definition with step functions, and what the $+i\epsilon$ in the denominator does to the zeros of $p^2-m^2+i\epsilon$.

solution: The symmetry $D_F(x-y)=D_F(y-x)$ holds because time ordering is symmetric under $x\leftrightarrow y$. The equation $(\Box_x+m^2)D_F=-i\delta^{(4)}$ is the Green's function equation. The pole location follows from $(p^0)^2-E_{\mathbf p}^2+i\epsilon=0$. The claim that $D_F$ vanishes at spacelike separation is false: there $D_F=D\neq0$. The claim that $D_F$ is the commutator for $x^0>y^0$ is false: there $D_F=D(x-y)=\langle0|\phi(x)\phi(y)|0\rangle$, not the commutator.
