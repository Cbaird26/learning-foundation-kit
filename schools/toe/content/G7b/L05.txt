@article
Scalars are only the simplest fields. Electrons, quarks and neutrinos are described by **spinor fields**, which carry several components that mix under Lorentz transformations. This lesson builds the algebra; the next solves the Dirac equation.

### The Clifford algebra and the Weyl representation

The four gamma matrices $\gamma^\mu$ are $4\times4$ matrices with
$$\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=2\eta^{\mu\nu}\,\mathbb 1,\qquad\eta=\mathrm{diag}(1,-1,-1,-1).$$
Hence $(\gamma^0)^2=1$, $(\gamma^i)^2=-1$ and distinct gamma matrices anticommute. In the **Weyl (chiral) representation**, written in $2\times2$ blocks with Pauli matrices $\sigma^i$,
$$\gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma^i=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^i\\-\sigma^i&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\gamma^5\equiv i\gamma^0\gamma^1\gamma^2\gamma^3=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.$$
$\gamma^0$ is Hermitian, $\gamma^i$ is anti-Hermitian, and $\gamma^{\mu\dagger}=\gamma^0\gamma^\mu\gamma^0$. For any four-vector $a$ define the slash $\not a\equiv\gamma^\mu a_\mu=\gamma^0a^0-\boldsymbol\gamma\cdot\mathbf a$. Then $\not a\not b+\not b\not a=2a\cdot b$ and $\not a\not a=a^2$. The traces you need are $\mathrm{tr}\,\mathbb 1=4$, $\mathrm{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu)=4\eta^{\mu\nu}$, zero for an odd number of gamma matrices, and
$$\mathrm{tr}(\gamma^\mu\gamma^\nu\gamma^\rho\gamma^\sigma)=4\big(\eta^{\mu\nu}\eta^{\rho\sigma}-\eta^{\mu\rho}\eta^{\nu\sigma}+\eta^{\mu\sigma}\eta^{\nu\rho}\big).$$
A quick numerical check of the algebra:

```python
import numpy as np
sx = np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]); sy = np.array([[0, -1j], [1j, 0]]); sz = np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]])
g = [np.kron(sx, np.eye(2))] + [np.kron(1j * sy, s) for s in (sx, sy, sz)]  # gamma^0 ... gamma^3
eta = np.diag([1, -1, -1, -1])
print(all(np.allclose(g[m] @ g[n] + g[n] @ g[m], 2 * eta[m, n] * np.eye(4))
          for m in range(4) for n in range(4)))   # True
```

### How a spinor transforms

Under $x\to x'=\Lambda x$ with $\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu=\delta^\mu_\nu+\omega^\mu{}_\nu$ and $\omega_{\mu\nu}=-\omega_{\nu\mu}$ real, a spinor field transforms by a matrix,
$$\psi'(x')=S(\Lambda)\,\psi(x),\qquad S=1-\tfrac{i}{4}\,\omega_{\mu\nu}\sigma^{\mu\nu}+\dots,\qquad\sigma^{\mu\nu}=\tfrac{i}{2}[\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu].$$
The matrix is fixed by requiring $S^{-1}\gamma^\mu S=\Lambda^\mu{}_\nu\gamma^\nu$: the gamma matrices behave like a four-vector. In the Weyl representation $\psi=(\psi_L,\psi_R)^T$ and $S$ is block diagonal. A rotation by $\theta$ about $\hat n$ acts as $e^{-i\theta\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ on both blocks; a boost with rapidity $\eta$ along $\hat n$ acts as $e^{-\eta\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ on $\psi_L$ and $e^{+\eta\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma/2}$ on $\psi_R$. A rotation by $2\pi$ gives $-1$, so a spinor changes sign under a full turn. Boosts are not unitary, so $\psi^\dagger\psi$ is not invariant.

### The Dirac adjoint and bilinears

Because $\gamma^0S^\dagger\gamma^0=S^{-1}$, the **Dirac adjoint** $\bar\psi\equiv\psi^\dagger\gamma^0$ transforms as $\bar\psi'=\bar\psi S^{-1}$. The bilinears are then
$$\bar\psi\psi\ \text{(scalar)},\quad\bar\psi\gamma^5\psi\ \text{(pseudoscalar)},\quad\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi\ \text{(vector)},\quad\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\gamma^5\psi\ \text{(axial vector)},$$
the building blocks of Lorentz-invariant Lagrangians. Note that $\psi^\dagger\psi=\bar\psi\gamma^0\psi$ is the time component of a vector, not a scalar.

### The Dirac Lagrangian and its current

$$\mathcal L=\bar\psi\,(i\not\partial-m)\,\psi.$$
Treat $\psi$ and $\bar\psi$ as independent. Varying $\bar\psi$ gives the **Dirac equation** $(i\not\partial-m)\psi=0$, and varying $\psi$ gives the conjugate equation $\partial_\mu\bar\psi\,\gamma^\mu=im\bar\psi$. Applying $(i\not\partial+m)$ to the Dirac equation and using $\not\partial\not\partial=\Box$ gives $-(\Box+m^2)\psi=0$: each component obeys the Klein–Gordon equation, so $m$ is the mass.

The symmetry $\psi\to e^{-i\alpha}\psi$ has $\Delta\psi=-i\psi$. Since $\partial\mathcal L/\partial(\partial_\mu\psi)=i\bar\psi\gamma^\mu$, the Noether current of week 2 is
$$j^\mu=\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi,\qquad\partial_\mu j^\mu=0,\qquad j^0=\psi^\dagger\psi\ge0.$$
The charge density is positive for every field configuration.

@example
title: Checking the Clifford algebra in the Weyl representation
problem: Verify $\{\gamma^0,\gamma^1\}=0$ and $(\gamma^1)^2=-1$ from the explicit $2\times2$ block matrices.
step: Multiply in blocks: $\gamma^0\gamma^1=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^1\\-\sigma^1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma^1&0\\0&\sigma^1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: In the other order, $\gamma^1\gamma^0=\begin{pmatrix}0&\sigma^1\\-\sigma^1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma^1&0\\0&-\sigma^1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The sum is zero, so $\{\gamma^0,\gamma^1\}=0=2\eta^{01}$.
step: Square: $(\gamma^1)^2=\begin{pmatrix}-\sigma^1\sigma^1&0\\0&-\sigma^1\sigma^1\end{pmatrix}=-\mathbb 1$, consistent with $2\eta^{11}=-2$ because $(\sigma^1)^2=1$.

@example
title: Conservation of the Dirac current
problem: Show that $j^\mu=\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$ satisfies $\partial_\mu j^\mu=0$ when $\psi$ obeys the Dirac equation.
step: Apply the product rule: $\partial_\mu j^\mu=(\partial_\mu\bar\psi)\gamma^\mu\psi+\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi$.
step: The Dirac equation $i\not\partial\psi=m\psi$ gives $\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu\psi=-im\psi$.
step: Take the Dirac adjoint: the dagger of $(i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu-m)\psi=0$ is $-i\partial_\mu\psi^\dagger\gamma^{\mu\dagger}-m\psi^\dagger=0$. Multiply on the right by $\gamma^0$ and use $\gamma^{\mu\dagger}\gamma^0=\gamma^0\gamma^\mu$ to get $\partial_\mu\bar\psi\gamma^\mu=im\bar\psi$.
step: Substitute both: $\partial_\mu j^\mu=im\bar\psi\psi-im\bar\psi\psi=0$.

@mistakes
- Using $\psi^\dagger$ instead of $\bar\psi=\psi^\dagger\gamma^0$ when building invariants. $\psi^\dagger\psi$ is not a scalar.
- Mixing metric signatures in the Clifford algebra. With $(+,-,-,-)$, $(\gamma^i)^2=-1$ and $\{\gamma^\mu,\gamma^\nu\}=+2\eta^{\mu\nu}$; some texts use the opposite signature and different signs.
- Forgetting that $\not a=\gamma^0a^0-\boldsymbol\gamma\cdot\mathbf a$, which has a minus sign on the spatial part.
- Thinking the gamma matrices themselves transform. They are fixed constant matrices; the combination $\bar\psi\gamma^\mu\psi$ transforms like a vector.
