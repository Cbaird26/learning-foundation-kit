@article
Stationary perturbation theory asks how energy levels shift. When the perturbation depends on time, the question changes: **how likely is the system to jump from one level to another?** Take $H = H_0 + V(t)$, where $H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ is solved and $V(t)$ is small.

### The exact equations

Expand the state in the unperturbed eigenstates, pulling out the free evolution:

$$|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle .$$

Substituting into the Schrödinger equation gives an exact set of coupled equations,

$$i\hbar\,\dot c_m = \sum_n V_{mn}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{mn}t}\,c_n,\qquad \omega_{mn} = \frac{E_m - E_n}{\hbar},\quad V_{mn} = \langle m|V|n\rangle .$$

The probability to be found in $|m\rangle$ is $|c_m(t)|^2$. Only $V$ causes the $c_n$ to change.

### First order

If the system starts in $|i\rangle$ ($c_i(0) = 1$) and $V$ is weak, keep $c_i \approx 1$ on the right side. For $m\ne i$,

$$c_m^{(1)}(t) = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_0^t V_{mi}(t')\,e^{i\omega_{mi}t'}\,dt',\qquad P_{i\to m}(t) = \left|c_m^{(1)}(t)\right|^2 .$$

The amplitude is the Fourier component of the perturbation at the transition frequency $\omega_{mi}$. It is trustworthy only while $P_{i\to m} \ll 1$ for every $m$.

### Constant perturbation, switched on at $t = 0$

With $V_{mi}$ constant,

$$P_{i\to m}(t) = \frac{4|V_{mi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{mi}^2}\,\sin^2\frac{\omega_{mi}t}{2} .$$

The probability just oscillates between $0$ and $4|V_{mi}|^2/(E_m - E_i)^2$. It is small when $|V_{mi}| \ll |E_m - E_i|$.

### Sinusoidal perturbation and resonance

Let $V(t) = V_0\cos\omega t$. Writing the cosine as two exponentials, the term with $e^{-i\omega t}$ is nearly stationary in the amplitude when $\omega \approx \omega_{mi}$ (absorption, $E_m > E_i$). Keeping only that **resonant term** gives, with detuning $\Delta = \omega_{mi} - \omega$,

$$P_{i\to m}(t) = \frac{|V_{mi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{\Delta^2} .$$

On resonance ($\Delta = 0$) this becomes $P = |V_{mi}|^2t^2/4\hbar^2$, growing as $t^2$. For fixed $t$ the peak has width of order $2\pi/t$ in $\omega$. The dropped counter-rotating term is smaller by roughly $1/(\omega t)$, so the result needs $\omega t \gg 1$ and $|V_{mi}| \ll \hbar\omega_{mi}$. For $E_m < E_i$ the other exponential, $e^{+i\omega t}$, is the resonant one, at $\omega \approx \omega_{im}$; that is emission stimulated by the field.

### Two limits where first order is not the right tool

- **Sudden approximation.** If $V$ changes much faster than $1/\omega_{mi}$, the state has no time to respond: $|\psi\rangle$ stays the same, and the probability of being in a new eigenstate $|m'\rangle$ is the overlap $|\langle m'|\psi\rangle|^2$.
- **Adiabatic approximation.** If $H$ changes slowly compared with the gap divided by $\hbar$, the system follows the instantaneous eigenstate: level $n$ stays level $n$, apart from a phase.

@example
title: A constant perturbation switched on at t = 0
problem: A two-level system with $\hbar = 1$ has energies $0$ and $2$ and starts in $|1\rangle$. A constant perturbation with $V_{21} = 0.1$ is switched on at $t = 0$. Find the first-order probability of being in $|2\rangle$ at $t = \pi/4$, and the maximum probability.
step: The transition frequency is $\omega_{21} = 2$. The first-order amplitude is $c_2 = \frac{V_{21}}{i}\int_0^t e^{i\omega_{21}t'}dt' = \frac{V_{21}}{i}\cdot\frac{e^{i\omega_{21}t} - 1}{i\omega_{21}}$.
step: Its modulus squared uses $|e^{i\phi} - 1|^2 = 4\sin^2(\phi/2)$: $P = \frac{4V_{21}^2}{\omega_{21}^2}\sin^2\frac{\omega_{21}t}{2}$.
step: The prefactor is $\frac{4(0.1)^2}{2^2} = 0.01$. At $t = \pi/4$, $\sin^2\frac{\pi}{4} = \frac12$, so $P = 0.005$.
step: The maximum is the prefactor, $0.01$, reached at $\omega_{21}t = \pi$. First order is justified: $P \ll 1$, as expected from $|V_{21}| = 0.1 \ll \hbar\omega_{21} = 2$.

@example
title: A resonant sinusoidal drive
problem: A two-level system ($\hbar = 1$) with $\omega_{21} = 1000$ is driven by $V(t) = V_0\cos\omega t$ with $|V_{21}| = 0.4$ at resonance, $\omega = \omega_{21}$. Estimate the transition probability at $t = 0.1$.
step: Write $\cos\omega t = \frac12(e^{i\omega t} + e^{-i\omega t})$. The amplitude is $c_2 = \frac{V_{21}}{2i}\int_0^t\left[e^{i(\omega_{21} + \omega)t'} + e^{i(\omega_{21} - \omega)t'}\right]dt'$.
step: At resonance the second term equals $1$ and does not oscillate, so it integrates to $t$. The first term oscillates at $2\omega_{21}$, and its integral is at most $\frac{1}{\omega_{21}}$; here that is $0.001$ against $t = 0.1$, so it can be dropped.
step: Therefore $c_2 \approx \frac{V_{21}t}{2i}$ and $P = \frac{|V_{21}|^2t^2}{4} = \frac{(0.16)(0.01)}{4} = 4.0\times10^{-4}$.
step: Validity: $P \ll 1$, and $\omega_{21}t = 100 \gg 1$, so dropping the neglected term changes the amplitude by at most about $1\%$. If $P$ approached $1$, first order would fail and you would need the full two-level (Rabi) solution.

@mistakes
- Using $\omega_{mi}$ with the wrong sign or order: $\omega_{mi} = (E_m - E_i)/\hbar$, final minus initial, and the phase is $e^{+i\omega_{mi}t}$.
- Forgetting the square: the probability is $|c_m|^2$, not $c_m$.
- Trusting first-order results when the answer is not small, for example a probability above $1$ at long times on resonance.
- Applying the sudden approximation when the perturbation is slow, or the adiabatic one when it is fast, without comparing its time scale with $1/\omega_{mi}$.

@practice num
prompt: A harmonic oscillator with $\hbar = m = 1$ and frequency $\omega = 1$ is in its ground state when its frequency is suddenly doubled to $\omega' = 2$. What is the probability that it is found in the new ground state? Give an exact value.
answer: 2*sqrt(2)/3
check: quad(lambda x: (1/pi)**0.25*exp(-x**2/2)*(2/pi)**0.25*exp(-x**2), -10, 10)**2
hint: In the sudden approximation the state is unchanged. The probability is the squared overlap of the old and new Gaussian ground states.
hint: The ground state is $(m\omega/\pi\hbar)^{1/4}e^{-m\omega x^2/2\hbar}$.
solution: The overlap is $\frac{(\omega\omega')^{1/4}}{\sqrt\pi}\sqrt{\frac{2\pi}{\omega + \omega'}}$, so $P = \frac{2\sqrt{\omega\omega'}}{\omega + \omega'} = \frac{2\sqrt2}{3} \approx 0.943$.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from $|\psi(t)\rangle = \sum_n c_n(t)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$ and the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t|\psi\rangle = (H_0 + V)|\psi\rangle$, derive the exact equation $i\hbar\,\dot c_m = \sum_n V_{mn}e^{i\omega_{mn}t}c_n$, then obtain the first-order amplitude $c_m^{(1)}(t)$ for a system that starts in $|i\rangle$.
solution: The time derivative of the state is $\sum_n\left[\dot c_n - \frac{iE_n}{\hbar}c_n\right]e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$. Multiplying by $i\hbar$ gives $\sum_n\left[i\hbar\dot c_n + E_nc_n\right]e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle$. The right side is $\sum_n c_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}\left(E_n + V\right)|n\rangle$. The $E_nc_n$ terms cancel, leaving $\sum_n i\hbar\dot c_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}|n\rangle = \sum_n c_ne^{-iE_nt/\hbar}V|n\rangle$. Take $\langle m|$ and multiply by $e^{iE_mt/\hbar}$: $i\hbar\dot c_m = \sum_n V_{mn}e^{i\omega_{mn}t}c_n$. To first order, put $c_n(t) \approx \delta_{ni}$ on the right: $i\hbar\dot c_m = V_{mi}(t)e^{i\omega_{mi}t}$, so $c_m^{(1)}(t) = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\int_0^tV_{mi}(t')e^{i\omega_{mi}t'}dt'$.
rubric: Differentiates the expansion correctly and shows the $E_nc_n$ terms cancel against $H_0$.
rubric: Projects on $\langle m|$ and obtains the phase factor $e^{i\omega_{mn}t}$ with $\omega_{mn} = (E_m - E_n)/\hbar$.
rubric: Makes the first-order replacement $c_n \to \delta_{ni}$ and integrates from $0$ to $t$.
hint: Apply $H_0|n\rangle = E_n|n\rangle$ so the free evolution cancels.
hint: At zeroth order only $c_i = 1$ is nonzero.
