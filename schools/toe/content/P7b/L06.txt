@article
Last week's formulas have energy gaps in their denominators. If another unperturbed state $|m\rangle$ has the same energy as $|n\rangle$, the term $V_{mn}/(E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)})$ divides by zero. That is not a technicality: it signals that the perturbation mixes the degenerate states *strongly*, however small $V$ is, so the zeroth-order states themselves are not yet determined.

### The degenerate subspace

Suppose the unperturbed level $E^{(0)}$ has $g$ independent orthonormal states $|a\rangle$, $a = 1,\dots,g$. Any combination of them is an eigenstate of $H_0$ with the same energy, so $V$ gets to choose which combinations are the "good" zeroth-order states. Restrict $V$ to the subspace:

$$W_{ab} = \langle a|V|b\rangle,\qquad a, b = 1, \dots, g .$$

This is a $g\times g$ Hermitian matrix.

**The first-order energy shifts are the eigenvalues of $W$**, and the eigenvectors of $W$ are the good zeroth-order states. If the eigenvalues are all different, the perturbation has *lifted* the degeneracy at first order. If some coincide, the degeneracy survives (look to second order, or to a symmetry).

### Using symmetry

If an operator $\Omega$ commutes with both $H_0$ and $V$, choose degenerate states that are eigenstates of $\Omega$. Then $W_{ab} = 0$ between states with different $\Omega$ eigenvalues, and $W$ splits into smaller blocks. This often makes $W$ diagonal at once.

### Hydrogen in an electric field

The $n = 2$ level of hydrogen has four states, $|2s\rangle$ and $|2p, m = 0, \pm1\rangle$. An electric field along $z$ adds $V = e\mathcal E z$. Since $z$ commutes with $L_z$, only states with equal $m$ connect, and parity allows only $2s \leftrightarrow 2p_0$, with $\langle 2s|V|2p_0\rangle = -3e a_0\mathcal E$ ($a_0$ is the Bohr radius). In the basis $(|2s\rangle, |2p_0\rangle)$,

$$W = \begin{pmatrix}0 & -3ea_0\mathcal E\\ -3ea_0\mathcal E & 0\end{pmatrix}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta E = \pm 3ea_0\mathcal E ,$$

with good states $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left(|2s\rangle \mp |2p_0\rangle\right)$. The $m = \pm1$ states are unshifted at first order. The shift is *linear* in $\mathcal E$: this is the linear Stark effect, possible only because of the degeneracy.

### After the degenerate step

Once the good states are found, couple them to the *other* levels with the usual second-order formula. If the first-order shifts are all different, each good state is treated as nondegenerate from then on.

### Near-degeneracy

Exact degeneracy is not required for trouble. If two levels are separated by $\delta$ and the coupling is $|V_{12}| \gtrsim \delta$, the nondegenerate expansion is poor. Treat the close pair together and diagonalize exactly the $2\times 2$ block, including the splitting. This interpolates between both regimes.

@example
title: A degenerate pair coupled to a distant level
problem: A three-level system has $H_0 = \text{diag}(0, 0, 4)$ and $V = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2 & 0\\ 2 & 1 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, in the basis $|1\rangle, |2\rangle, |3\rangle$. Find the two energies that start at $0$ to second order in $\epsilon$.
step: States $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$ are degenerate (energy $0$), so the nondegenerate formulas would divide by zero. Restrict $V$ to this subspace: $W = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & 1\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The eigenvalues of $W$ are $\epsilon(1 \pm 2)$: $3\epsilon$ for $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle + |2\rangle)$ and $-\epsilon$ for $|-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle - |2\rangle)$. The degeneracy is lifted at first order.
step: Couple the good states to the distant level: $\langle 3|V|\pm\rangle = \pm\frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt2}$, because only $V_{23} = \epsilon$ is nonzero.
step: Second-order shift for each good state: $\frac{|\epsilon/\sqrt2|^2}{0 - 4} = -\frac{\epsilon^2}{8}$.
step: The energies are $E_+ = 3\epsilon - \frac{\epsilon^2}{8}$ and $E_- = -\epsilon - \frac{\epsilon^2}{8}$. Check with the trace: level $3$ moves to $4 + \frac{\epsilon^2}{4}$, so the three energies sum to $4 + 2\epsilon$, which equals $\text{tr}\,H$.

@mistakes
- Using the nondegenerate formulas, with a zero denominator or a basis chosen arbitrarily, on a level that is degenerate.
- Computing first-order shifts as the diagonal elements $W_{aa}$ in a basis where $W$ is not diagonal: the shifts are the *eigenvalues* of $W$.
- Including the degenerate partner states in the second-order sum, rather than only the other levels.
- Assuming $m = \pm1$ states of the $n = 2$ hydrogen level shift in an electric field, when $z$ does not connect them to anything.

@practice num
prompt: A level of $H_0$ is twofold degenerate. In the degenerate subspace the perturbation has matrix elements $W = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}1 & 3\\ 3 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. What is the larger first-order energy shift, in units of $\epsilon$?
answer: 4
check: max(np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array([[1, 3], [3, 1]])))
hint: First-order shifts are the eigenvalues of $W$.
hint: $\det(W/\epsilon - \lambda) = (1 - \lambda)^2 - 9 = 0$.
solution: $1 - \lambda = \pm 3$ gives $\lambda = -2$ or $4$. The larger shift is $4\epsilon$, with good state $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle + |2\rangle)$.

@practice num
prompt: A threefold degenerate level has $W = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 1\\ 0 & 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. What is the largest first-order shift in units of $\epsilon$? Give an exact value.
answer: sqrt(2)
check: max(np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array([[0, 1, 0], [1, 0, 1], [0, 1, 0]])))
hint: Expand $\det(W/\epsilon - \lambda)$ along the first row.
hint: The characteristic equation is $-\lambda^3 + 2\lambda = 0$.
solution: $\lambda(\lambda^2 - 2) = 0$ gives $\lambda = 0, \pm\sqrt2$. The largest shift is $\sqrt2\,\epsilon$, and the three-fold degeneracy is completely lifted.

@practice self
prompt: Consider $H = \begin{pmatrix}0 & \epsilon\\ \epsilon & \delta\end{pmatrix}$ with $\epsilon, \delta > 0$, built as $H_0 = \text{diag}(0, \delta)$ plus a coupling $V$ of size $\epsilon$. (a) Give the second-order nondegenerate shift of the lower level. (b) Give the exact lower eigenvalue. (c) Say when each of the two approaches, nondegenerate perturbation theory and the degenerate treatment ($\delta \to 0$), is accurate.
solution: (a) $E_1^{(2)} = \frac{\epsilon^2}{0 - \delta} = -\frac{\epsilon^2}{\delta}$. (b) The exact eigenvalue is $E_- = \frac{\delta}{2} - \sqrt{\frac{\delta^2}{4} + \epsilon^2}$. (c) For $\epsilon \ll \delta$, expand the square root: $E_- \approx -\epsilon^2/\delta$, which matches (a). For $\delta \ll \epsilon$, treat the levels as degenerate: the eigenvalues of $V$ are $\pm\epsilon$, matching $E_- \approx -\epsilon + \delta/2$. In the crossover $\epsilon \sim \delta$ neither simple result is accurate and you must diagonalize the $2\times 2$ matrix.
rubric: Gets $-\epsilon^2/\delta$ for the nondegenerate second-order shift.
rubric: Writes the correct exact eigenvalue of the $2\times2$ matrix.
rubric: Identifies $\epsilon \ll \delta$ as the nondegenerate regime and $\delta \ll \epsilon$ as the degenerate regime, and notes the nondegenerate result diverges as $\delta \to 0$.
hint: Expand $\sqrt{\delta^2/4 + \epsilon^2}$ for small $\epsilon/\delta$, then for small $\delta/\epsilon$.
hint: The nondegenerate shift becomes huge as $\delta \to 0$, which is a warning sign.

@quiz num
prompt: A system has $H_0 = \text{diag}(0, 0, 5)$ and $V = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 1\\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. The two lowest energies start degenerate at $0$. For the one whose first-order shift is $+\epsilon$, the energy is $\epsilon + c\,\epsilon^2 + O(\epsilon^3)$. Find $c$. Give an exact fraction.
answer: -1/10
check: (lambda g: (4*g(1e-3) - g(2e-3))/3)(lambda l: (np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array([[0, l, l], [l, 0, 0], [l, 0, 5]]))[1] + np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array([[0, -l, -l], [-l, 0, 0], [-l, 0, 5]]))[0])/(2*l**2))
hint: Find the good states from the $2\times2$ block $W = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1\\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, then couple them to the third level.
hint: The state with shift $+\epsilon$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle + |2\rangle)$, and $\langle 3|V|{+}\rangle = \epsilon/\sqrt2$.
solution: The good state is $|+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle + |2\rangle)$ with $W = +\epsilon$. Then $\langle 3|V|{+}\rangle = \frac{\epsilon}{\sqrt2}$, and the second-order shift is $\frac{\epsilon^2/2}{0 - 5} = -\frac{\epsilon^2}{10}$. So $c = -\frac{1}{10}$.

answer: (a + b)/2 + sqrt(((a - b)/2)^2 + c^2)
