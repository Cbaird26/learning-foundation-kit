@article
Few quantum systems are exactly solvable. **Perturbation theory** handles the many that are *almost* solvable: a Hamiltonian $H_0$ whose eigenvalues and eigenstates you know, plus a small extra term $V$.

### Setup

Write $H = H_0 + \lambda V$, where $\lambda$ is a bookkeeping parameter you set to $1$ at the end. The unperturbed problem is solved:

$$H_0|n\rangle = E_n^{(0)}|n\rangle ,$$

and here we assume each $E_n^{(0)}$ is **nondegenerate**. Write $|n\rangle$ for $|n^{(0)}\rangle$ and $V_{mn} = \langle m|V|n\rangle$. Expand the exact eigenvalue and eigenstate as power series in $\lambda$:

$$E_n = E_n^{(0)} + \lambda E_n^{(1)} + \lambda^2 E_n^{(2)} + \cdots,\qquad |\tilde n\rangle = |n\rangle + \lambda|n^{(1)}\rangle + \cdots .$$

Insert these into $H|\tilde n\rangle = E_n|\tilde n\rangle$ and collect powers of $\lambda$. Take $\langle n|n^{(1)}\rangle = 0$ (a choice of normalization).

### The key formulas

**First-order energy:** the shift is the expectation value of $V$ in the unperturbed state,

$$E_n^{(1)} = V_{nn} = \langle n|V|n\rangle .$$

**First-order state:** other unperturbed states mix in, weighted by the matrix element over the energy gap,

$$|n^{(1)}\rangle = \sum_{m\ne n} \frac{V_{mn}}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}}\,|m\rangle .$$

**Second-order energy:** project the order-$\lambda^2$ equation on $\langle n|$ and use the state above,

$$E_n^{(2)} = \sum_{m\ne n} \frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}} .$$

### Reading the formulas

- Levels *above* $n$ push it down, and levels *below* push it up: neighbouring levels "repel". For the ground state every denominator is negative, so $E_0^{(2)} \le 0$ always.
- A matrix element $V_{mn} = 0$ means state $m$ does not mix in. Symmetry (parity, angular momentum) often kills most terms in the sum.
- Everything is trustworthy only when the perturbation is small compared with the gaps it connects: $|V_{mn}| \ll |E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}|$. When two levels are close (or equal), these formulas fail and you need the next lesson.
- The series is usually asymptotic, not convergent: the first one or two orders are the useful ones.

### A habit worth keeping

Whenever an exact solution exists in a limit, test the formulas against it. A perturbed harmonic oscillator is the standard test bench, because shifting or stiffening the potential can be solved exactly.

@example
title: A stiffer oscillator, perturbatively and exactly
problem: A harmonic oscillator $H_0 = \frac{p^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2$ is perturbed by $V = \epsilon\cdot\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2$ with $\epsilon \ll 1$. Find the ground-state energy to second order in $\epsilon$ and compare with the exact result.
step: With $x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(a + a^\dagger)$, the perturbation is $V = \epsilon\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}\left(a^2 + a^{\dagger 2} + 2a^\dagger a + 1\right)$.
step: First order: $E_0^{(1)} = \langle 0|V|0\rangle = \epsilon\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}$, since only the constant term survives in the ground state.
step: The only other matrix element from $|0\rangle$ is $\langle 2|V|0\rangle = \epsilon\,\frac{\hbar\omega}{4}\sqrt2$, from the $a^{\dagger 2}$ term. The gap is $E_0^{(0)} - E_2^{(0)} = -2\hbar\omega$.
step: Second order: $E_0^{(2)} = \frac{\epsilon^2\hbar^2\omega^2\cdot 2/16}{-2\hbar\omega} = -\frac{\epsilon^2\hbar\omega}{16}$.
step: Exact result: the new frequency is $\omega' = \omega\sqrt{1 + \epsilon}$, so $E_0 = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega\sqrt{1+\epsilon} = \frac{1}{2}\hbar\omega\left(1 + \frac{\epsilon}{2} - \frac{\epsilon^2}{8} + \cdots\right)$. The $\epsilon$ and $\epsilon^2$ terms, $\frac{\epsilon\hbar\omega}{4}$ and $-\frac{\epsilon^2\hbar\omega}{16}$, agree with perturbation theory.

@mistakes
- Including $m = n$ in the second-order sum (the denominator is zero) or dropping the absolute-value square on the numerator.
- Getting the sign of the denominator wrong: it is $E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)}$, with the state you are correcting first.
- Applying the nondegenerate formulas when two unperturbed levels coincide or lie closer than $|V|$.
- Treating the first-order state correction as normalized: it is orthogonal to $|n\rangle$ and has norm of order $\lambda$.

prompt: A particle in the infinite square well $0 < x < L$ is in its ground state $\psi_1(x) = \sqrt{2/L}\,\sin(\pi x/L)$. A perturbation $V = V_0\sin(\pi x/L)$ is switched on. Find the first-order energy shift in units of $V_0$.
answer: 8/(3pi)
check: quad(lambda x: 2*sin(pi*x)**2*sin(pi*x), 0, 1)
hint: First order means $E^{(1)} = \langle\psi_1|V|\psi_1\rangle$, an integral over $|\psi_1|^2 V$.
hint: Use $x/L = u$, so the integral becomes $2V_0\int_0^1\sin^3(\pi u)\,du$.
solution: $E_1^{(1)} = \frac{2V_0}{L}\int_0^L\sin^3(\pi x/L)\,dx = 2V_0\cdot\frac{4}{3\pi} = \frac{8}{3\pi}V_0 \approx 0.849\,V_0$.

prompt: Starting from $(H_0 + \lambda V)|\tilde n\rangle = E_n|\tilde n\rangle$ with the series for $E_n$ and $|\tilde n\rangle$, derive the first-order energy $E_n^{(1)} = \langle n|V|n\rangle$ and the expansion coefficients of $|n^{(1)}\rangle$ in the unperturbed basis.
solution: Collecting terms of order $\lambda$ gives $(H_0 - E_n^{(0)})|n^{(1)}\rangle = (E_n^{(1)} - V)|n\rangle$. Take the inner product with $\langle n|$. Now take the inner product with $\langle m|$ for $m\ne n$: $(E_m^{(0)} - E_n^{(0)})\langle m|n^{(1)}\rangle = -V_{mn}$, so $\langle m|n^{(1)}\rangle = V_{mn}/(E_n^{(0)} - E_m^{(0)})$. The component along $|n\rangle$ is zero by the normalization choice.

@quiz num
prompt: In a three-level system $H_0 = \text{diag}(0, 1, 3)$ and $V = \epsilon\begin{pmatrix}0 & 1 & 1\\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 1 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$. The lowest level's energy is $E = c\,\epsilon^2 + O(\epsilon^4)$ for small $\epsilon$. Find $c$. Give an exact fraction.
answer: -4/3
check: (lambda f: (4*f(1e-3)/1e-6 - f(2e-3)/4e-6)/3)(lambda l: np.linalg.eigvalsh(np.array([[0, l, l], [l, 1, 0], [l, 0, 3]]))[0])
hint: First order vanishes since $V_{00} = 0$. Sum over both other levels.
hint: $V_{10} = V_{20} = \epsilon$, and the denominators are $0 - 1$ and $0 - 3$.
solution: $c\,\epsilon^2 = \epsilon^2\left(\frac{1}{0 - 1} + \frac{1}{0 - 3}\right) = -\frac{4}{3}\epsilon^2$, so $c = -\frac{4}{3}$.
