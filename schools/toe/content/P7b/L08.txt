@article
When the potential changes slowly compared with a particle's de Broglie wavelength, the particle behaves almost classically. **Semiclassical (WKB) methods** make this precise: they keep the classical momentum and add a quantum phase, giving good energies even for low-lying levels.

### The WKB wavefunction

Define the classical momentum at energy $E$,

$$p(x) = \sqrt{2m\,[E - V(x)]} .$$

In a **classically allowed** region ($E > V$), the approximate solutions are

$$\psi(x) \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{p(x)}}\exp\left(\pm\frac{i}{\hbar}\int^x p\,dx'\right).$$

The amplitude $1/\sqrt{p}$ says the particle spends more time where it moves slowly. In a **forbidden** region ($E < V$), $p$ becomes imaginary and the solutions are real exponentials,

$$\psi(x) \approx \frac{C}{\sqrt{|p(x)|}}\exp\left(\pm\frac{1}{\hbar}\int^x |p|\,dx'\right).$$

**Validity:** the momentum must change little over a wavelength, $\left|\dfrac{\hbar}{p^2}\dfrac{dp}{dx}\right| \ll 1$. This fails at a classical turning point, where $p \to 0$ and the wavelength diverges.

### Turning points and quantization

Near a smooth turning point, an exact (Airy-function) solution connects the allowed oscillation to the forbidden decay, and fixes a phase shift of $\pi/4$ at each such point. For a bound state between two **soft** turning points $x_1, x_2$ this gives the **Bohr-Sommerfeld** condition

$$\int_{x_1}^{x_2} p(x)\,dx = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\pi\hbar,\qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$$

Variants depend on the walls:

| Turning points | Condition on $\int p\,dx$ | $n$ starts at |
|---|---|---|
| two soft | $(n + \frac12)\pi\hbar$ | $0$ |
| one hard wall, one soft | $(n + \frac34)\pi\hbar$ | $0$ |
| two hard walls | $n\pi\hbar$ | $1$ |

The integral is the phase-space area enclosed by the classical orbit, divided by $2$.

### Tunneling

Through a barrier between turning points $x_1$ and $x_2$ where $E < V$, the transmission probability is

$$T \approx e^{-2\gamma},\qquad \gamma = \frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\sqrt{2m\,[V(x) - E]}\;dx .$$

This is the Gamow factor. It is accurate for thick, smooth barriers, $\gamma \gg 1$, and is exponentially sensitive to width and height.

### Accuracy

WKB is exact for the harmonic oscillator and the infinite well, and its relative error shrinks as $n$ grows. It is not a bound: unlike the variational method, it may land above or below the truth.

@example
title: The harmonic oscillator by Bohr-Sommerfeld
problem: Apply the quantization condition to $V = \frac12m\omega^2x^2$.
step: At energy $E$ the turning points are $\pm a$ with $a = \sqrt{2E/m\omega^2}$, and $p(x) = \sqrt{2mE}\sqrt{1 - x^2/a^2}$.
step: The integral is the area of half an ellipse: $\int_{-a}^{a}p\,dx = \sqrt{2mE}\cdot\frac{\pi a}{2} = \frac{\pi E}{\omega}$.
step: Set $\frac{\pi E}{\omega} = \left(n + \frac12\right)\pi\hbar$.
step: Therefore $E_n = \left(n + \frac12\right)\hbar\omega$, which happens to be exact for this potential.

@example
title: A bouncing particle: hard wall plus soft turning point
problem: A particle of mass $m$ moves in $V(x) = mgx$ for $x > 0$, with an infinite wall at $x = 0$. Find the WKB energy levels and compare with the exact ground level $2.338\,\epsilon_0$ where $\epsilon_0 = (mg^2\hbar^2/2)^{1/3}$.
step: Between the hard wall at $x = 0$ and the soft turning point $x_t = E/mg$ the condition is $\int_0^{x_t}p\,dx = \left(n + \frac34\right)\pi\hbar$.
step: With $p = \sqrt{2m(E - mgx)}$, the integral is $\frac{2\sqrt{2m}}{3mg}E^{3/2}$.
step: Solve for the energy: $E_n = \left[\frac{3\pi\left(n + \frac34\right)\hbar\,mg}{2\sqrt{2m}}\right]^{2/3} = \left[\frac{3\pi}{2}\left(n + \tfrac34\right)\right]^{2/3}\epsilon_0$.
step: For $n = 0$, $E_0 = (3.534)^{2/3}\epsilon_0 \approx 2.320\,\epsilon_0$, less than $1\%$ below the exact $2.338\,\epsilon_0$. The error is smaller still for higher $n$.

@mistakes
- Using $n + \frac12$ for every problem, instead of adjusting for hard walls ($n + \frac34$ or $n$).
- Applying WKB right at a turning point, where $p \to 0$ and the validity condition fails.
- Dropping the factor $2$ in the tunneling exponent $e^{-2\gamma}$ (it comes from squaring the amplitude) or forgetting the $1/\hbar$.
- Treating the WKB energy as an upper bound on the ground-state energy: only the variational principle gives a rigorous bound.
