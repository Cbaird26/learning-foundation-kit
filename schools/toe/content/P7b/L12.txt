@article
Most of what we know about atoms, nuclei and particles comes from scattering: fire a beam at a target and measure what comes out and in which direction. Quantum mechanics predicts the *angular distribution* by solving the stationary Schrödinger equation with a particular asymptotic form.

### The scattering amplitude

Let a beam with wave number $k$ travel along $z$ and meet a localized potential $V(\vec r)$ at the origin; the energy is $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. Far from the target the solution has the form

$$\psi(\vec r) \;\longrightarrow\; e^{ikz} + f(\theta,\phi)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}.$$

The first term is the incident plane wave and the second an outgoing spherical wave whose strength in each direction is the **scattering amplitude** $f$. Because the outgoing flux through solid angle $d\Omega$ is $|f|^2$ times the incident flux, the **differential cross section** and the **total cross section** are

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = |f(\theta,\phi)|^2,\qquad \sigma = \int|f|^2\,d\Omega .$$

A cross section has units of area.

### The first Born approximation

For a weak potential, or a fast projectile, replace the true wavefunction in the integral equation by the incident plane wave. Then $f$ is proportional to the Fourier transform of the potential at the **momentum transfer** $\vec q = \vec k' - \vec k$, with $q = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$:

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int e^{-i\vec q\cdot\vec r}\,V(\vec r)\,d^3r .$$

For a spherically symmetric potential this reduces to a radial integral,

$$f(\theta) = -\frac{2m}{\hbar^2 q}\int_0^\infty r\,V(r)\,\sin(qr)\,dr .$$

The approximation is good when $V$ only weakly distorts the incident wave: roughly, the potential's size is small compared with the kinetic energy over the range $a$ of the potential (low energy: $|V|\ll\hbar^2/2ma^2$), and it improves at high energy.

### Low energy and partial waves

At low energy only the $l = 0$ partial wave matters, the scattering is isotropic, and the cross section is $\sigma = \frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_0$ in terms of the s-wave phase shift $\delta_0$. At zero energy $\sigma = 4\pi a_s^2$ with $a_s$ the scattering length. For the Born approximation at $q \to 0$, $f$ is proportional to the volume integral of $V$.

### Identical particles

If the colliding particles are identical, detecting a particle at angle $\theta$ cannot tell you whether it was the "projectile" or the "target". In the centre-of-mass frame the second possibility corresponds to angle $\pi - \theta$. The amplitude must be symmetrized or antisymmetrized, in the same way as the two-particle wavefunctions of last week:

$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \begin{cases}|f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)|^2 & \text{spinless bosons},\\ |f(\theta) - f(\pi - \theta)|^2 & \text{fermions in the same spin state},\\ |f(\theta)|^2 + |f(\pi - \theta)|^2 - \text{Re}\left[f(\theta)f^*(\pi - \theta)\right] & \text{unpolarized spin-}\tfrac12 .\end{cases}$$

The last line weights the symmetric-spatial singlet ($\frac14$) and antisymmetric-spatial triplet ($\frac34$). Symmetrizing keeps only even partial waves $l$, antisymmetrizing only odd ones.

@example
title: Born scattering from a screened Coulomb potential
problem: Use the first Born approximation for $V(r) = \beta\,e^{-\mu r}/r$ to find $f(\theta)$ and $d\sigma/d\Omega$, and take the limit $\mu\to0$.
step: Here $rV(r) = \beta e^{-\mu r}$, so $f = -\frac{2m\beta}{\hbar^2q}\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(qr)\,dr$.
step: The integral is $\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(qr)\,dr = \frac{q}{\mu^2 + q^2}$.
step: Therefore $f(\theta) = -\frac{2m\beta}{\hbar^2(\mu^2 + q^2)}$, with $q = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$, and $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac{4m^2\beta^2}{\hbar^4(\mu^2 + q^2)^2}$.
step: As $\mu\to0$, $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}\to\left(\frac{2m\beta}{\hbar^2q^2}\right)^2$. With $q^2 = 4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$ and $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$ this is $\frac{\beta^2}{16E^2\sin^4(\theta/2)}$, the Rutherford formula.

@example
title: Symmetrizing a model amplitude
problem: A model amplitude for the scattering of two identical particles is $f(\theta) = a + b\cos\theta$ (an s-wave plus a p-wave term). Find $d\sigma/d\Omega$ for spinless bosons, for fermions in the same spin state, and for unpolarized spin-1/2 particles.
step: Since $\cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta$, we have $f(\pi - \theta) = a - b\cos\theta$.
step: The sum is $f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta) = 2a$: the odd term cancels. Spinless bosons: $d\sigma/d\Omega = 4a^2$, isotropic.
step: The difference is $f(\theta) - f(\pi - \theta) = 2b\cos\theta$: the even term cancels. Fermions in the same spin state: $d\sigma/d\Omega = 4b^2\cos^2\theta$, which vanishes at $\theta = 90^\circ$.
step: Unpolarized spin-1/2: weight the two cases $\frac14$ and $\frac34$, so $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega} = \frac14(4a^2) + \frac34(4b^2\cos^2\theta) = a^2 + 3b^2\cos^2\theta$.
step: Check with the single formula: $|f|^2 + |f'|^2 - \text{Re}(ff'^*) = 2a^2 + 2b^2\cos^2\theta - (a^2 - b^2\cos^2\theta) = a^2 + 3b^2\cos^2\theta$. They agree.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the $1/r$ in the outgoing wave, or confusing the amplitude $f$ (a length) with the cross section (an area).
- Using the scattering angle instead of the momentum transfer $q = 2k\sin(\theta/2)$ in the Born formula.
- Applying the Born approximation to a strong, deep potential at low energy where the incident wave is badly distorted.
- Treating identical particles as distinguishable: leaving out $f(\pi - \theta)$ or using the wrong sign for fermions in the same spin state.

@practice self
prompt: Explain why the differential cross section for scattering of two identical spinless bosons is $|f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)|^2$. What does it predict at $\theta = 90^\circ$ compared with distinguishable particles, and what changes for two fermions in the same spin state?
solution: In the centre-of-mass frame, the relative coordinate $\vec r$ and $-\vec r$ correspond to exchanging the particles. For identical spinless bosons the relative wavefunction must be symmetric under $\vec r\to-\vec r$, so the incident wave is $e^{ikz} + e^{-ikz}$ and the scattered wave is $\left[f(\theta) + f(\pi - \theta)\right]e^{ikr}/r$. A detector at angle $\theta$ cannot tell whether the scattered particle came from $\theta$ or $\pi - \theta$, so the amplitudes add before squaring. At $\theta = 90^\circ$, $f(\theta) = f(\pi - \theta)$ and the cross section is $4|f|^2$, twice the incoherent sum $2|f|^2$ that would hold for distinguishable particles with the same amplitude. For fermions in the same spin state the spatial wave is antisymmetric, so the amplitudes subtract: $d\sigma/d\Omega = |f(\theta) - f(\pi - \theta)|^2$, which vanishes at $90^\circ$.
rubric: Connects exchange of the particles to $\theta\to\pi - \theta$ in the centre-of-mass frame.
rubric: Explains that amplitudes, not probabilities, are added (or subtracted) before squaring, using the symmetry of the wavefunction.
rubric: States the $90^\circ$ result: $4|f|^2$ for bosons, double the distinguishable value, and zero for same-spin fermions.
hint: Think about the symmetry of the spatial wavefunction under $\vec r\to-\vec r$.
hint: At $90^\circ$ the two directions coincide.
