@article
A state vector is a geometric object; its components are just coordinates. Rotate your coordinate axes and the components change, but the vector does not. Quantum mechanics works the same way: you may describe $|\psi\rangle$ and an operator $A$ in any orthonormal basis, and every physical prediction must come out the same.

### The unitary matrix

Let $\{|n\rangle\}$ be the old orthonormal basis and $\{|\tilde m\rangle\}$ a new one. Define

$$U_{nm} = \langle n|\tilde m\rangle .$$

Column $m$ of $U$ is the new basis vector $|\tilde m\rangle$ written in the old basis. Completeness shows that $U$ is **unitary**: $U^\dagger U = U U^\dagger = 1$, so $U^{-1} = U^\dagger$.

### Transforming components and operators

Insert $\sum_n |n\rangle\langle n| = 1$ into $\tilde c_m = \langle\tilde m|\psi\rangle$:

$$\tilde c_m = \sum_n \langle\tilde m|n\rangle\langle n|\psi\rangle \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \tilde c = U^\dagger c .$$

Do the same on both sides of $\tilde A_{mk} = \langle\tilde m|A|\tilde k\rangle$:

$$\tilde A = U^\dagger A\, U .$$

This is a **similarity transformation**. The *same* abstract state and operator now have different arrays of numbers.

### What does not change

Quantities defined without reference to a basis are **invariants**:

- inner products and norms, $\tilde c^\dagger\tilde c = c^\dagger c$;
- expectation values, $\tilde c^\dagger\tilde A\,\tilde c = c^\dagger A\, c$;
- the eigenvalues, trace and determinant of $A$.

Individual components $c_n$ and matrix elements $A_{mn}$ **do** change. A statement such as "$A_{12} = 0$" is a statement about a basis, not about the physics.

### Diagonalizing a Hermitian operator

If you choose the new basis to be the orthonormal eigenvectors $A|a_k\rangle = a_k|a_k\rangle$, then $\tilde A = \text{diag}(a_1, a_2, \dots)$. In this basis, probabilities are immediate: $P_k = |\langle a_k|\psi\rangle|^2$ and $\langle A\rangle = \sum_k a_k P_k$. Diagonalizing is nothing more than a good choice of basis. If an eigenvalue is repeated, any orthonormal basis of its eigenspace will do.

### Continuous bases

The same ideas work for continuous labels. The position basis $|x\rangle$ and momentum basis $|p\rangle$ are related by $\langle x|p\rangle = e^{ipx/\hbar}/\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}$, so that the wavefunctions $\psi(x) = \langle x|\psi\rangle$ and $\tilde\psi(p) = \langle p|\psi\rangle$ are connected by a Fourier transform. A Fourier transform is a basis change.

@example
title: One state and one operator in two bases
problem: In the basis $\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ the operator is $A = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$ and the state is $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}3\\ 4\end{pmatrix}$. Find the eigenbasis of $A$, express $|\psi\rangle$ and $A$ in it, and confirm that $\langle A\rangle$ is the same in both bases.
step: Eigenvalues: $\det(A - \lambda) = (2 - \lambda)^2 - 1 = 0$ gives $\lambda = 3$ and $\lambda = 1$. The normalized eigenvectors are $|a_1\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, 1)^T$ for $3$ and $|a_2\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1, -1)^T$ for $1$.
step: The unitary matrix has these as columns: $U = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1\\ 1 & -1\end{pmatrix}$. It is real and symmetric, so $U^\dagger = U$.
step: Transform the operator: $\tilde A = U^\dagger A U = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 0\\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. It is diagonal, with the eigenvalues on the diagonal.
step: Transform the state: $\tilde c = U^\dagger c = \frac{1}{5\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}3 + 4\\ 3 - 4\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{5\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}7\\ -1\end{pmatrix}$. Probabilities: $P_{3} = \frac{49}{50}$ and $P_{1} = \frac{1}{50}$, which sum to $1$.
step: New basis: $\langle A\rangle = 3\cdot\frac{49}{50} + 1\cdot\frac{1}{50} = \frac{148}{50} = 2.96$. Old basis: $\frac{1}{25}(3, 4)\begin{pmatrix}2\cdot 3 + 4\\ 3 + 2\cdot 4\end{pmatrix} = \frac{30 + 44}{25} = 2.96$. The two agree.

@mistakes
- Using $U$ where you need $U^\dagger$ (or the reverse): components transform with $U^\dagger$, while the new basis vectors are the columns of $U$.
- Forgetting to conjugate when $U$ is complex, so that $U^\dagger$ is replaced by $U^T$.
- Normalizing eigenvectors incorrectly, so that $U$ is not unitary and probabilities no longer sum to $1$.
- Treating a zero matrix element in one basis as a physical statement valid in every basis.

@quiz num
prompt: In the basis $\{|1\rangle, |2\rangle\}$ the Hermitian operator is $B = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 2\\ 2 & -2\end{pmatrix}$ and the state is $|\psi\rangle = |1\rangle$. What is the probability that a measurement of $B$ gives its larger eigenvalue? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 4/5
check: (lambda w, v: abs(v[0, np.argmax(w)])**2)(*np.linalg.eigh(np.array([[1, 2], [2, -2]])))
hint: Find the eigenvalues of $B$ first: $\lambda^2 + \lambda - 6 = 0$.
hint: The eigenvector for $\lambda = 2$ satisfies $-x + 2y = 0$. The probability is the squared overlap of the normalized eigenvector with $(1, 0)^T$.
solution: The eigenvalues are $2$ and $-3$. For $\lambda = 2$ the normalized eigenvector is $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(2, 1)^T$, so $P = \left|\frac{2}{\sqrt5}\right|^2 = \frac{4}{5}$.
