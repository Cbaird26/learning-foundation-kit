@article
Last week gave the probability of a jump between two discrete levels: it oscillates or grows like $t^2$, never like a steady rate. Real decays, absorption into a band or ionization involve a *continuum* of final states. Summing over that continuum turns the growth into a steady, linear one: a **transition rate**.

### From probability to rate

For a perturbation $V(t) = F e^{-i\omega t} + F^\dagger e^{i\omega t}$ (a constant perturbation is the case $\omega = 0$), the absorption probability is

$$P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{|F_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2}\,\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2},\qquad \Delta = \omega_{fi} - \omega .$$

As $t$ grows this peak narrows and grows. Its area is fixed, $\int\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2}\,d\Delta = 2\pi t$, so for large $t$

$$\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2} \;\longrightarrow\; 2\pi t\,\delta(\Delta) .$$

The probability then grows linearly in $t$ and the rate $\Gamma = dP/dt$ is constant.

### Fermi's golden rule

Converting $\delta(\omega_{fi} - \omega) = \hbar\,\delta(E_f - E_i - \hbar\omega)$ and summing over final states with density $\rho(E_f)$ (states per unit energy),

$$\boxed{\Gamma_{i\to f} = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|F_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)},\qquad E_f = E_i + \hbar\omega .$$

For a constant perturbation $F = V$ and $E_f = E_i$. Energy conservation appears through $\rho$ evaluated at the final energy. The rule says that the rate is proportional to the **square** of the matrix element and to the number of available final states.

### When is it valid?

Two time scales bracket the golden rule:

- $t \gg \hbar/\delta E$, where $\delta E$ is the energy range over which $|F_{fi}|^2\rho$ varies (so the delta-function approximation is justified);
- $t \ll 1/\Gamma$, so that the initial state is barely depleted and first order is reliable.

For longer times the decay is exponential, with $P_i = e^{-\Gamma t}$ and lifetime $\tau = 1/\Gamma$.

### Selection rules

Whether $F_{fi} = 0$ often follows from symmetry. For an electric-dipole interaction $\propto\vec r\cdot\vec{\mathcal E}$ in a one-electron atom, $F_{fi}$ vanishes unless

$$\Delta l = \pm 1,\qquad \Delta m = 0, \pm 1,$$

and the parity of the state must change. So $2p\to1s$ is allowed, while $2s\to1s$ is not (at dipole order). The $\Delta m$ choices correspond to the polarization of the field.

### Strong resonant drive: Rabi oscillation

If the drive is on resonance and strong enough that $\Omega t$ is not small, the two-level system oscillates between the levels with the Rabi frequency $\Omega = 2|F_{fi}|/\hbar$ (for $V(t) = V_0\cos\omega t$ this is $|V_{fi}|/\hbar$, with $V_{fi}$ the matrix element of $V_0$):

$$P_{i\to f}(t) = \frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\frac{\sqrt{\Omega^2 + \Delta^2}}{2}\,t\right) .$$

At $\Delta = 0$ this is $\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$, which reduces to the first-order result $(\Omega t/2)^2$ at short times. A pulse with $\Omega t = \pi$ transfers the population completely (a $\pi$-pulse).

@example
title: A decay rate into a continuum
problem: A state couples to a continuum with $|V_{fi}| = 2.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV}$ and a density of final states $\rho = 1.0\times10^{3}\ \text{eV}^{-1}$ at the right energy. Find the golden-rule rate and the probability of a transition after $t = 1\ \text{ns}$. Use $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-16}\ \text{eV s}$.
step: The rate is $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|V_{fi}|^2\rho$. The squared matrix element is $(2.0\times10^{-6})^2 = 4.0\times10^{-12}\ \text{eV}^2$.
step: Multiply by $\rho$: $4.0\times10^{-12}\times1.0\times10^{3} = 4.0\times10^{-9}\ \text{eV}$, then by $2\pi$: $2.51\times10^{-8}\ \text{eV}$.
step: Divide by $\hbar$: $\Gamma = \frac{2.51\times10^{-8}}{6.582\times10^{-16}}\ \text{s}^{-1} = 3.8\times10^{7}\ \text{s}^{-1}$. The lifetime is $\tau = 1/\Gamma \approx 26\ \text{ns}$.
step: At $t = 1\ \text{ns}$, $\Gamma t \approx 0.038 \ll 1$, so the linear growth applies and $P \approx \Gamma t \approx 3.8\%$.
step: The time window also needs $t \gg \hbar/\delta E$. If $\delta E \sim 10^{-3}\ \text{eV}$ this is only $\sim 0.7\ \text{ps}$, so $1\ \text{ns}$ lies comfortably inside the window $0.7\ \text{ps} \ll t \ll 26\ \text{ns}$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that the golden-rule rate has the matrix element squared and a density of states with units of $1/\text{energy}$, so that $\Gamma$ comes out in $\text{s}^{-1}$ after dividing by $\hbar$.
- Using the rate for times where $\Gamma t$ is not small, or for times so short that $t \lesssim \hbar/\delta E$.
- Treating $2s\to1s$ as an allowed electric-dipole transition, or forgetting the parity change.
- Using first-order $P \propto t^2$ for a strong resonant drive after the population has begun to oscillate.

@practice self
prompt: Show that $\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2} \to 2\pi t\,\delta(\Delta)$ for large $t$ in the sense that both have the same integral over $\Delta$ and the left side is sharply peaked. Then use it to explain why the first-order probability for a harmonic perturbation grows linearly in $t$ and what the two validity conditions of the golden rule are.
solution: At $\Delta = 0$ the left side equals $t^2$. Its first zeros are at $\Delta = \pm2\pi/t$, so the peak has height $t^2$ and width of order $1/t$: area of order $t$. Substituting $u = \Delta/2$ gives $\int\frac{\sin^2(\Delta t/2)}{(\Delta/2)^2}d\Delta = 2\int\frac{\sin^2(ut)}{u^2}du = 2\pi t$. So for large $t$ the function acts as $2\pi t\,\delta(\Delta)$ inside any integral over final states with smooth weight. Integrating $P_{i\to f}$ over the continuum then gives a result proportional to $t$, and its slope is the rate $\Gamma = \frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|F_{fi}|^2\rho(E_f)$. Validity needs $t \gg \hbar/\delta E$, so the peak is narrower than the energy range over which the matrix element and density of states vary, and $t \ll 1/\Gamma$, so that the initial state is not depleted and first order holds.
rubric: Shows that the function has peak height $t^2$ and width of order $1/t$, and that its area is $2\pi t$.
rubric: Explains that integrating over a smooth continuum turns the function into $2\pi t\,\delta(\Delta)$ and hence a probability linear in $t$.
rubric: States both conditions: $t$ long compared with $\hbar/\delta E$, and short compared with the lifetime $1/\Gamma$.
hint: What are the height of the peak at $\Delta = 0$ and the location of its first zero?
hint: Use $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin^2 x}{x^2}dx = \pi$.
