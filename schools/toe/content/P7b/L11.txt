@article
Two electrons are not merely alike; they are *indistinguishable*: no measurement can tell you which is which. This changes the allowed states and has large consequences, from the structure of atoms to the stability of matter.

### Exchange symmetry

Label the particles $1$ and $2$ and let the **exchange operator** swap all their coordinates (positions and spins): $P_{12}\Psi(1,2) = \Psi(2,1)$. Since $P_{12}^2 = 1$, its eigenvalues are $\pm1$. For identical particles $[H, P_{12}] = 0$, so the symmetry never changes in time. Nature realizes only two cases:

- **Bosons** (integer spin): $\Psi(2,1) = +\Psi(1,2)$, symmetric.
- **Fermions** (half-integer spin): $\Psi(2,1) = -\Psi(1,2)$, antisymmetric.

This is the **symmetrization postulate**, and its link to spin is the spin-statistics theorem. Electrons, protons and neutrons are fermions; photons and helium-4 nuclei are bosons.

### Building two-particle states

Take two orthonormal single-particle states $\phi_a$ and $\phi_b$ with $a \neq b$. The product $\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)$ is not allowed by itself, because it says which particle is where. The allowed combinations are

$$\Psi_\pm(1,2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2) \pm \phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)\Big],$$

with $+$ for bosons and $-$ for fermions. If $a = b$, only $\phi_a(1)\phi_a(2)$ is possible for bosons, while the antisymmetric combination vanishes: **no two identical fermions can occupy the same single-particle state** (Pauli exclusion). For $N$ fermions the antisymmetric state is the **Slater determinant**

$$\Psi(1,\dots,N) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\big[\phi_{k}(j)\big]_{k,j=1}^{N}.$$

Counting two-particle states built from $d$ single-particle states:

| Particles | Number of states |
|---|---|
| distinguishable | $d^2$ |
| identical bosons | $d(d+1)/2$ |
| identical fermions | $d(d-1)/2$ |

### Spin and space together

The *total* wavefunction must have the right symmetry. For two spin-1/2 electrons:

- spatial part **symmetric** $\times$ spin **singlet** (antisymmetric, $S = 0$);
- spatial part **antisymmetric** $\times$ spin **triplet** (symmetric, $S = 1$, three states).

Same-spin electrons therefore avoid each other: an antisymmetric spatial function vanishes at $\vec r_1 = \vec r_2$. This **exchange effect** needs no force. For orthonormal $\phi_a, \phi_b$, the mean-square separation in one dimension is

$$\langle (x_1 - x_2)^2\rangle_\pm = \langle x^2\rangle_a + \langle x^2\rangle_b - 2\langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b \mp 2\,|\langle x\rangle_{ab}|^2 ,$$

with $\langle x\rangle_{ab} = \int\phi_a^*\,x\,\phi_b\,dx$: bosons are pulled slightly together, fermions pushed apart.

### Non-interacting particles in a well

For non-interacting identical particles, the energy is the sum of single-particle energies. In an infinite well, $E_n = n^2E_1$, and the energy of a configuration is $(n_a^2 + n_b^2)E_1$. Exclusion makes the fermion ground state much higher than the boson one.

@example
title: Two identical particles in an infinite well
problem: Two non-interacting identical particles occupy a one-dimensional infinite well with single-particle energies $E_n = n^2E_1$. Find and compare the ground states and first excited states for spinless bosons, spinless fermions, and spin-1/2 fermions (electrons).
step: Total energy is $(n_a^2 + n_b^2)E_1$. Bosons may share a level. Spinless bosons: the ground state is $\psi_1(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)$, with $E = 2E_1$. The first excited state is $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2) + \psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)]$, with $E = 5E_1$.
step: Spinless fermions cannot use $n_a = n_b$. The ground state is the antisymmetric combination of $n = 1, 2$, with $E = 5E_1$: $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2) - \psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)]$. It vanishes at $x_1 = x_2$. The next state uses $n = 1, 3$ with $E = 10E_1$.
step: Electrons: the ground state has both in $n = 1$. The spatial part $\psi_1(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)$ is symmetric, so the spin part must be the singlet. $E = 2E_1$, with one state.
step: The first excited level of electrons is at $5E_1$ (one electron in $n = 1$, one in $n = 2$). It has $1 + 3 = 4$ states: symmetric spatial $\times$ singlet (one state), and antisymmetric spatial $\times$ triplet (three states).
step: Comparison: removing spin freedom raises the lowest energy from $2E_1$ to $5E_1$. The same spatial level at $5E_1$ is a single state for spinless particles but a four-fold level for electrons.

@mistakes
- Using the product $\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)$ as a state of identical particles, or forgetting the $1/\sqrt2$ normalization.
- Concluding that two electrons cannot share a spatial orbital; they can, with opposite spins (a spin singlet).
- Mixing symmetry types: the total wavefunction (space $\times$ spin) must be antisymmetric for fermions, not each part separately.
- Counting fermion states as $d^2$ or boson states as $d(d-1)/2$ when listing allowed configurations.

@practice num
prompt: Two identical bosons (spinless) can occupy any of three single-particle states. How many distinct two-boson states exist?
answer: 6
check: sum(1 for i in range(3) for j in range(3) if i <= j)
hint: Each state is specified by which levels are occupied; both bosons may be in the same level.
solution: Both in the same level: $3$ states. In two different levels: $\binom{3}{2} = 3$ states. Total $6 = 3\cdot4/2$.

choice: $\phi_a(1)\phi_a(2)$
choice: $\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)$

choice*: $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2) - \phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)\right]$
hint: The wavefunction must change sign when particle labels are swapped.
solution: Only the antisymmetric combination changes sign under $1\leftrightarrow2$. The choice $\phi_a(1)\phi_a(2)$ is excluded by the Pauli principle, the product $\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)$ distinguishes the particles, and the combination with a plus sign is the boson state.

answer: 0.606
tol: 0.001
check: 1/np.linalg.norm(np.kron([1, 0], [0.6, 0.8]) + np.kron([0.6, 0.8], [1, 0]))

@practice self

Exchange check for the singlet: the spatial part is symmetric ($+$) and the spin part antisymmetric ($-$), so the product changes sign. For the triplet the spatial part is antisymmetric and the spin part symmetric, so again the product changes sign.

@quiz num
prompt: Two identical spinless bosons are in the infinite well $0 < x < 1$ in the symmetric state $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\left[\psi_1(x_1)\psi_2(x_2) + \psi_2(x_1)\psi_1(x_2)\right]$, where $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt2\sin(n\pi x)$. What is the probability that both particles are found in the left half, $0 < x_1, x_2 < \frac12$? Give an exact value.
answer: 1/4 + 16/(9*pi^2)
check: quad2(lambda x, y: 0.5*(2*sin(pi*x)*sin(2*pi*y) + 2*sin(2*pi*x)*sin(pi*y))**2, 0, 0.5, 0, 0.5)
hint: Each orbital has probability $\frac12$ in the left half. The new term is the squared overlap integral $\int_0^{1/2}\psi_1\psi_2\,dx$.
hint: $\int_0^{1/2}\psi_1\psi_2\,dx = \frac{4}{3\pi}$. For bosons the probability is $\frac12\left[2\cdot\frac14 + 2\left(\frac{4}{3\pi}\right)^2\right]$.
solution: $P = \frac14 + \left(\frac{4}{3\pi}\right)^2 = \frac14 + \frac{16}{9\pi^2} \approx 0.430$. Distinguishable particles would give $\frac14$, and the antisymmetric (fermion) combination would give $\frac14 - \frac{16}{9\pi^2} \approx 0.070$.
