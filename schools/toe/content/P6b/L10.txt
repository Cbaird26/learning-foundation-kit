@lesson
title: Dipole radiation
summary: Find the radiation fields, angular pattern and total power of an oscillating electric dipole, and distinguish its near field from its far field.

objective: Compute the time-averaged Poynting vector and the total radiated power $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/(12\pi c)$.
objective: Explain the roles of retardation in the near and far fields and use $kr$ to tell the zones apart.

@article
The simplest radiator is an oscillating electric dipole: charges $+q(t)$ and $-q(t)$ separated by a small distance $d$ along $z$, with dipole moment

$$\mathbf{p}(t) = p_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}},\qquad p_0 = q_0d.$$

It models a short antenna or an atom. We assume $d \ll \lambda$ and $r \gg d$. The current along the rod is $\dot{q}$, and $\int\mathbf{J}\,d^3r' = \dot{\mathbf{p}}$, so the retarded vector potential is

$$\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t) \approx \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi r}\,\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t - r/c).$$

### Radiation zone

Differentiating the retarded potentials and keeping the leading $1/r$ terms (valid for $kr \gg 1$, where $k = \omega/c$) gives, in spherical coordinates,

$$\mathbf{E} = -\frac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\,\frac{\sin\theta}{r}\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\qquad \mathbf{B} = \frac{\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\mathbf{E}}{c}.$$

Three features matter. The phase $\omega(t - r/c)$ is the **retardation**: an outgoing spherical wave. The amplitude falls as $1/r$. And $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\hat{\mathbf{r}}$ are mutually perpendicular with $E = cB$, like a local plane wave. The factor $\sin\theta$ means there is no radiation along the dipole axis.

### Power and pattern

The time-averaged Poynting vector and the total power are

$$\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2c}\,\frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2}\,\hat{\mathbf{r}},\qquad \langle P\rangle = \oint\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \frac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}.$$

The power does not depend on the radius of the sphere, as energy conservation requires, and it grows as $\omega^4$. It agrees with the Larmor formula $P = \mu_0q^2a^2/(6\pi c)$ for an accelerating charge, since $\ddot{p} = qa$ and $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \tfrac12$.

### Near and far fields

The exact dipole fields contain terms that fall as $1/r^3$ (static-like), $1/r^2$ (induction) and $1/r$ (radiation). With $k = \omega/c$ the ratio of the radiation term to the static-like term is of order $(kr)^2$, so the natural length is $\lambda/2\pi = c/\omega$.

| Zone | Condition | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| Near | $r \ll \lambda/2\pi$ | retardation negligible; $\mathbf{E}$ looks like the static dipole field, $\propto 1/r^3$; $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ about $90^\circ$ out of phase, so energy sloshes back and forth with no net outflow |
| Far (radiation) | $r \gg \lambda/2\pi$ | $\propto 1/r$, transverse, $E = cB$, in phase; net power flows outward and $\langle S\rangle \propto 1/r^2$ |

In the near zone the delay $r/c$ is a tiny fraction of the period. In the far zone it is many periods, and the pattern you see at $r$ was emitted $r/c$ earlier.

### Dimensional check

The combination $\mu_0/c$ has units $\text{kg·s/C}^2$, and $p_0^2\omega^4$ has units $\text{C}^2\text{m}^2\text{s}^{-4}$, so their product is $\text{kg·m}^2/\text{s}^3 = \text{W}$.

@example
title: A short antenna
problem: A rod antenna of length $d = 0.10\ \text{m}$ carries charge oscillating with amplitude $q_0 = 1.0\times10^{-9}\ \text{C}$ at $f = 10\ \text{MHz}$. Use $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A}$ and $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find the radiated power, the radiation electric-field amplitude at $r = 1.0\ \text{km}$ in the equatorial plane, and the zone boundary.
step: The dipole moment amplitude is $p_0 = q_0d = 1.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{C·m}$, and $\omega = 2\pi f = 6.28\times10^{7}\ \text{rad/s}$. The wavelength $\lambda = c/f = 30\ \text{m}$ is much larger than $d$, so the dipole approximation holds.
step: Total power: $\langle P\rangle = \dfrac{\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c} = \dfrac{(1.257\times10^{-6})(10^{-20})(1.56\times10^{31})}{12\pi(3.00\times10^{8})} = 1.73\times10^{-5}\ \text{W} = 17\ \mu\text{W}$.
step: In the equatorial plane $\sin\theta = 1$, so $E_0 = \dfrac{\mu_0p_0\omega^2}{4\pi r} = \dfrac{(10^{-7})(10^{-10})(3.95\times10^{15})}{1.0\times10^{3}} = 3.9\times10^{-5}\ \text{V/m}$. The magnetic amplitude is $B_0 = E_0/c = 1.3\times10^{-13}\ \text{T}$.
step: The boundary of the near zone is $c/\omega = 4.8\ \text{m}$. At $r = 1.0\ \text{km}$ we have $kr = 209 \gg 1$, so this is deep in the radiation zone and the $1/r$ formula applies.
step: Check with the intensity: $\langle S\rangle = E_0^2/(2\mu_0c) = 2.1\times10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$. Compare with the pattern formula at $\theta = 90^\circ$: $3P/(8\pi r^2) = 2.1\times10^{-12}\ \text{W/m}^2$. They agree.

@mistakes
- Using the static $1/r^3$ dipole field far from the source. At large distance the radiation field, which falls only as $1/r$, dominates.
- Forgetting the angular factor: a dipole radiates nothing along its own axis and most in the plane perpendicular to it.
- Mixing up $\omega$ and $f$. The power scales as $\omega^4 = (2\pi f)^4$.
- Using the peak $|\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}|/\mu_0$ as the average power. Averaging $\cos^2$ over a cycle gives a factor of $\tfrac12$.

@practice self
prompt: Explain how retardation shows up in the fields of an oscillating dipole, and distinguish the near field from the far field. Give the length scale that separates the zones, how the dominant field terms depend on $r$, and what happens to the energy in each zone.
hint: Start from the retarded potential $\mathbf{A} \propto \dot{\mathbf{p}}(t - r/c)/r$ and compare $r/c$ with the oscillation period.
hint: Compare the dimensionless number $kr = \omega r/c$ with $1$ and list the $1/r^3$, $1/r^2$, $1/r$ terms.
solution: The potentials depend on the dipole moment at the retarded time $t - r/c$, so the fields oscillate with the phase $\omega(t - r/c)$, an outgoing wave: what you measure at distance $r$ was emitted $r/c$ earlier. The length scale is $\lambda/2\pi = c/\omega$, i.e. $kr = 1$. Near zone ($kr \ll 1$): the delay $r/c$ is a tiny fraction of the period, so retardation is negligible and the fields are quasi-static: $\mathbf{E}$ is the dipole field $\propto 1/r^3$ oscillating in step with $\mathbf{p}$, $\mathbf{B}\propto\dot{p}/r^2$, and $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ are about $90^\circ$ out of phase, so energy flows back and forth with no net outward flux. Far zone ($kr \gg 1$): the $1/r$ radiation terms dominate by a factor of order $(kr)^2$; the fields are transverse, $E = cB$ and in phase, so $\langle S\rangle\propto 1/r^2$ and a steady net power $\mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c$ flows outward through every large sphere.

rubric: Gives the scale $\lambda/2\pi = c/\omega$ (or $kr \sim 1$) separating near and far zones.
rubric: Describes the near field: quasi-static $1/r^3$ electric field, retardation negligible, no net energy outflow (reactive).
rubric: Describes the far field: $1/r$, transverse, $E = cB$ in phase, $\langle S\rangle\propto 1/r^2$ with net outward power.
