@project
title: Radiation from an oscillating dipole
overview: You will calculate the fields, angular pattern and total power radiated by a small oscillating electric dipole, starting from the retarded vector potential. You will then test the result in three ways: by integrating the Poynting flux over a sphere, by dimensional analysis, and by comparing the near-field and far-field limits. The calculation is presented as a short written derivation with a numerical check in Python.
goal: Derive the radiation-zone fields $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ of the dipole $\mathbf{p}(t) = p_0\cos(\omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, the time-averaged Poynting vector, and the total power $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/(12\pi c)$.
goal: Check the angular dependence ($\sin^2\theta$), the dimensions of every result, and that the total power does not depend on the radius of the sphere.
goal: Distinguish the near zone from the far zone by computing $kr$ and the relative size of the $1/r^3$, $1/r^2$ and $1/r$ terms, and explain the role of retardation.
goal: Explain the whole calculation aloud, without notes, in about five minutes.
step: State the model and its assumptions: dipole size $d \ll \lambda$, observation distance $r \gg d$, harmonic motion with $\omega$, free space, SI units. Write the retarded vector potential $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t) \approx \mu_0\dot{\mathbf{p}}(t - r/c)/(4\pi r)$ and say where the retarded time enters.
step: Derive the radiation field. For $kr \gg 1$ the derivatives act on the retarded argument, so $\nabla \to -(\hat{\mathbf{r}}/c)\,\partial_t$ at leading order. Use this to get $\mathbf{B} = \nabla\times\mathbf{A} \approx -\hat{\mathbf{r}}\times\partial_t\mathbf{A}/c$ in terms of $\ddot{\mathbf{p}}$, convert to $\theta$ and $\phi$ components, and then obtain $\mathbf{E} = c\,\mathbf{B}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}$. Check that $E = cB$ and that both are transverse.
step: Compute the Poynting vector $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$, average over a cycle, and integrate over a sphere of radius $r$ to obtain $\langle P\rangle$. Confirm that $r$ cancels, and compare the result with the Larmor formula $P = \mu_0q^2a^2/(6\pi c)$ using $\ddot{p} = qa$.
step: Perform a dimensional analysis of $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$ and $\langle P\rangle$ in SI base units. Then test limiting cases: $\omega \to 0$, doubling $\omega$ (power $\times16$), doubling $p_0$ (power $\times4$), and the directions $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi/2$.
step: Write a Python script that (a) evaluates $\langle S\rangle(\theta)$ for $p_0 = 2.0\times10^{-11}\ \text{C·m}$ and $f = 100\ \text{MHz}$ at $r = 50\ \text{m}$, (b) integrates it numerically over the sphere with a trapezoid or quadrature rule, (c) prints the numerical and analytic powers with the percentage difference, and (d) plots the polar pattern.
step: Characterise the zones for this antenna: compute $\lambda$, $c/\omega$ and $kr$ at $r = 0.1\lambda$, $\lambda$ and $10\lambda$. Use the order-of-magnitude ratio $(kr)^2$ between the radiation and static-like electric-field terms to say where each dominates, and describe the energy flow in each zone.
step: Add a retardation check: for an observer at $r = 150\ \text{m}$ find the delay $r/c$ and the phase lag $kr$ of the field relative to the dipole moment, and state at what time the dipole moment that produced the field at $t = 0$ was evaluated.
step: Assemble a two- to three-page write-up and rehearse a five-minute spoken explanation covering the assumptions, the derivation, the pattern, the power, the dimensional checks and the near/far distinction.
deliverable: A written derivation (two to three pages) of the radiation fields, the Poynting vector and the total power, with every assumption and approximation stated.
deliverable: A Python script or notebook of at most about 60 lines that integrates the Poynting flux numerically, plots the $\sin^2\theta$ pattern, and prints the numerical and analytic powers side by side.
deliverable: A one-page checks table listing the SI dimensions of each result, the limiting-case tests, the $r$-independence of the power, and the near/far crossover distance for the chosen antenna.
deliverable: A recorded or live five-minute explanation, given without notes, that you can repeat to a listener and that includes one question and answer on retardation.
rubric: Derivation: the radiation fields follow logically from the retarded potential, the approximations ($d \ll \lambda$, $kr \gg 1$) are stated and used correctly, and the final expressions for $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$, $\langle\mathbf{S}\rangle$ and $\langle P\rangle$ are correct.
rubric: Checks: dimensions are verified in SI base units, limiting cases and scalings are tested, and the sphere integral shows that the power is independent of $r$.
rubric: Numerics: the script reproduces the analytic power to within about 0.1%, the polar pattern vanishes on the axis and peaks at $\theta = \pi/2$, and units and constants are documented.
rubric: Physical interpretation: retardation is explained with the delay $r/c$ and phase $kr$, and the near and far zones are separated by $kr \sim 1$ with correct field behaviour and energy flow in each.
rubric: Communication: the write-up and spoken explanation are organised, use standard names and conventions correctly, and are clear to a listener who knows only the prerequisite courses.
tutor: Here is my derivation of $\mathbf{B}$ from the retarded vector potential. Which terms did I drop, and is dropping them justified for $kr \gg 1$?
tutor: Check my dimensional analysis of the total power formula and suggest two limiting cases I have not tested.

@review
This guide follows the course in order. For each week, be able to state the result, derive it from the one before, and compute a number with it.

### Week by week

- **Weeks 1–2, Maxwell equations and conservation laws.** The four equations in SI form; the displacement current $\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t\mathbf{E}$ and the continuity equation $\partial_t\rho + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} = 0$; $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. Poynting's theorem $\partial_tu + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ with $u = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + B^2/2\mu_0$ and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$; momentum density $\mathbf{S}/c^2$; radiation pressure $I/c$ (absorber) and $2I/c$ (mirror).
- **Weeks 3–4, plane waves and polarization.** $\omega = ck$, $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$, $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$; linear, circular and elliptical polarization and helicity; Malus's law and the factor $\tfrac12$ for unpolarized light.
- **Weeks 5–6, interfaces.** Boundary conditions (normal $\mathbf{D}$, tangential $\mathbf{E}$, normal $\mathbf{B}$, tangential $\mathbf{H}$); phase matching gives $\theta_r = \theta_i$ and Snell's law; critical angle and the evanescent wave; $r = (n_1 - n_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$, $t = 2n_1/(n_1 + n_2)$, $R = r^2$, $T = (n_2/n_1)t^2$, $R + T = 1$; Brewster's angle $\tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1$.
- **Weeks 7–8, waveguides and dispersion.** Perfect-conductor conditions; TE and TM modes; $f_c = \tfrac{c}{2}\sqrt{(m/a)^2 + (n/b)^2}$; $\omega^2 = c^2k^2 + \omega_c^2$; $v_pv_g = c^2$; $v_g = d\omega/dk$; the Lorentz oscillator; the plasma frequency $\omega_p^2 = Ne^2/\varepsilon_0m$ and reflection below it.
- **Weeks 9–10, retardation and radiation.** Lorenz gauge and the sourced wave equations; $t_r = t - |\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}'|/c$; the implicit equation for a moving charge; dipole radiation with $E\propto\sin\theta\cos[\omega(t - r/c)]/r$ and $\langle P\rangle = \mu_0p_0^2\omega^4/12\pi c$; near zone $kr \ll 1$ against far zone $kr \gg 1$.
- **Weeks 11–12, relativity and gauge.** The boost of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$; the invariants $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ and $E^2 - c^2B^2$; the field of a uniformly moving charge; $\mathbf{A}' = \mathbf{A} + \nabla\lambda$, $\varphi' = \varphi - \partial_t\lambda$; Lorenz and Coulomb gauges and the residual freedom.

### Methods to practise

- Turn integral forms (pillbox, thin loop) into boundary conditions.
- Substitute a plane-wave form so that $\nabla \to i\mathbf{k}$ and $\partial_t \to -i\omega$, and let algebra do the work.
- Check every result with units, a limiting case and energy conservation.

### Self-check

- I can derive Poynting's theorem and state its integral form with the boundary flux.
- I can derive $r$ and $t$ at normal incidence and show that $R + T = 1$.
- I can list the modes that propagate in a rectangular guide at a given frequency.
- I can say what retardation does to the dipole's fields and where the near zone ends.
- I can verify gauge invariance by direct substitution.
- I can use the two invariants to decide whether a frame with $\mathbf{B}' = 0$ exists.

### Most common errors

- Dropping the displacement current, or the minus sign in Faraday's law.
- Using amplitude where intensity is needed: forgetting the $\tfrac12$ from averaging, or writing $T = t^2$ instead of $(n_2/n_1)t^2$.
- Mixing up $v_p$ and $v_g$, or $\omega$ and $f$ (the radiated power goes as $\omega^4$).
- Measuring angles from the surface instead of from the normal.
- Evaluating sources at the present time $t$ instead of the retarded time $t_r$.
- Transforming only one of $\varphi$ and $\mathbf{A}$ under a gauge change, or applying $\gamma$ to field components along the boost.

prompt: A plane electromagnetic wave in air ($n = 1.00$) with intensity $10.0\ \text{W/m}^2$ is normally incident on a flat glass surface ($n = 1.50$). Take $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ and $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find the amplitude of the electric field of the transmitted wave inside the glass, in V/m.
answer: 69.42
check: sqrt(2*10.0*(1 - lin([[1, -1], [-1.0, -1.5]], [-1, -1.0])[0]**2)/(1.5*8.854e-12*3.00e8))
tol: 1%
unit: V/m
hint: Use the boundary conditions at $z = 0$ to find $r$, then use energy conservation to find the transmitted intensity.
hint: In a medium of index $n$ the intensity is $I = \tfrac12 n\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$. Solve it for $E_0$ using the transmitted intensity.
solution: At normal incidence $r = (1.00 - 1.50)/2.50 = -0.20$, so $R = 0.04$ and the transmitted intensity is $I_T = 0.96(10.0) = 9.6\ \text{W/m}^2$. Inside the glass $E_T = \sqrt{2I_T/(n\varepsilon_0c)} = \sqrt{19.2/(1.50\cdot8.854\times10^{-12}\cdot3.00\times10^{8})} = 69.4\ \text{V/m}$. Check with amplitudes: the incident amplitude is $\sqrt{2(10.0)/(\varepsilon_0c)} = 86.8\ \text{V/m}$ and $t = 2/2.50 = 0.80$, giving $0.80(86.8) = 69.4\ \text{V/m}$.

prompt: A rectangular air-filled waveguide has inner dimensions $a = 2.5\ \text{cm}$ and $b = 1.25\ \text{cm}$. A pulse with carrier frequency $9.0\ \text{GHz}$ travels along it in the TE$_{10}$ mode. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. How much later, in nanoseconds, does the envelope of the pulse arrive after travelling $L = 12\ \text{m}$ than a pulse that travels the same distance in free space?
answer: 13.67
check: 12.0/nderiv(lambda k: sqrt((3.00e8*k)**2 + (2*pi*6.0e9)**2), sqrt((2*pi*9.0e9)**2 - (2*pi*6.0e9)**2)/3.00e8)*1e9 - 12.0/3.00e8*1e9
tol: 1%
unit: ns
hint: Find the TE$_{10}$ cutoff frequency first, and check that this carrier is above it and that the next mode is still cut off.
hint: The envelope travels at the group velocity, $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - (f_c/f)^2}$. The delay is $L/v_g - L/c$.
solution: $f_c = c/2a = 6.0\ \text{GHz}$. The next cutoffs are $c/a = 12\ \text{GHz}$ (TE$_{20}$ and TE$_{01}$), so only TE$_{10}$ propagates at $9.0\ \text{GHz}$. Then $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - (6/9)^2} = 0.7454\,c = 2.236\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. The travel time is $L/v_g = 53.67\ \text{ns}$, against $L/c = 40.00\ \text{ns}$ in free space, so the pulse is $13.67\ \text{ns}$ late.

@exam self
prompt: Charge conservation and the Lorenz gauge. (a) Starting from the Lorenz-gauge wave equations $\nabla^2\varphi - c^{-2}\partial_t^2\varphi = -\rho/\varepsilon_0$ and $\nabla^2\mathbf{A} - c^{-2}\partial_t^2\mathbf{A} = -\mu_0\mathbf{J}$, show that the quantity $L = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} + c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi$ satisfies $\Box L = -\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \partial_t\rho)$, where $\Box = \nabla^2 - c^{-2}\partial_t^2$, and explain what this implies about maintaining the Lorenz condition. (b) If a given pair of potentials has $L \neq 0$, find the equation that a gauge function $\lambda$ must satisfy to make $L' = 0$, and state the residual freedom. (c) Show that this gauge change leaves $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ unchanged.
hint: Apply $\nabla\cdot$ to the equation for $\mathbf{A}$ and $c^{-2}\partial_t$ to the equation for $\varphi$, then add. Use $c^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1$.
hint: Under $\mathbf{A}' = \mathbf{A} + \nabla\lambda$ and $\varphi' = \varphi - \partial_t\lambda$, compute how $L$ changes in terms of $\Box\lambda$.
solution: (a) The divergence of the $\mathbf{A}$ equation gives $\Box(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}) = -\mu_0\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}$. Applying $c^{-2}\partial_t$ to the $\varphi$ equation gives $\Box(c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi) = -(c^{-2}/\varepsilon_0)\partial_t\rho = -\mu_0\partial_t\rho$. Adding, $\Box L = -\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \partial_t\rho) = 0$ by the continuity equation. So $L$ obeys a source-free wave equation: if $L$ and $\partial_tL$ vanish at an initial time they vanish for all later times, and the Lorenz condition is consistent with the sourced wave equations exactly because charge is conserved. (b) Under the gauge change $L' = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{A} + \nabla^2\lambda + c^{-2}\partial_t\varphi - c^{-2}\partial_t^2\lambda = L + \Box\lambda$. Requiring $L' = 0$ means solving the sourced wave equation $\Box\lambda = -L$ (for example with a retarded solution). The residual freedom is any $\lambda_0$ with $\Box\lambda_0 = 0$. (c) $\mathbf{B}' = \nabla\times\mathbf{A} + \nabla\times\nabla\lambda = \mathbf{B}$. $\mathbf{E}' = -\nabla\varphi + \nabla\partial_t\lambda - \partial_t\mathbf{A} - \partial_t\nabla\lambda = \mathbf{E}$, because the curl of a gradient is zero and mixed partial derivatives commute.
rubric: Applies $\nabla\cdot$ to the $\mathbf{A}$ equation and $c^{-2}\partial_t$ to the $\varphi$ equation, adds them, and uses $c^2\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = 1$ to obtain $\Box L = -\mu_0(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J} + \partial_t\rho)$.
rubric: Uses the continuity equation to conclude $\Box L = 0$ and explains that the Lorenz condition is then preserved in time given suitable initial data.
rubric: Shows $L' = L + \Box\lambda$, gives $\Box\lambda = -L$ for the gauge function, and states that any $\lambda$ with $\Box\lambda = 0$ remains free.
rubric: Verifies $\mathbf{E}' = \mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}' = \mathbf{B}$ using $\nabla\times\nabla\lambda = 0$ and commuting partial derivatives.
