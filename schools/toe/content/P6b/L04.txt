@article
A plane wave is transverse, so at each point $\mathbf{E}$ lies in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{k}$. The **polarization** describes how the tip of $\mathbf{E}$ moves in that plane. For a wave along $+z$ the most general monochromatic field is

$$\mathbf{E} = E_{x0}\cos(kz - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} + E_{y0}\cos(kz - \omega t + \delta)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}.$$

### Three cases

- **Linear**: $\delta = 0$ or $\pi$. The tip oscillates along a fixed line. For $\delta = 0$ the line makes angle $\theta = \arctan(E_{y0}/E_{x0})$ with the $x$-axis.
- **Circular**: $E_{x0} = E_{y0} = E_0$ and $\delta = \pm\pi/2$. The magnitude $|\mathbf{E}|=E_0$ is constant and the direction rotates at angular rate $\omega$.
- **Elliptical**: everything else. The tip traces an ellipse.

For $\delta = +\pi/2$ the vector rotates from $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ toward $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ at a fixed point, which is counterclockwise when you look at the incoming wave. This follows the right-hand rule about $\mathbf{k}$, and we call it **positive helicity**. In complex notation it is $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} \propto (\hat{\mathbf{x}} + i\hat{\mathbf{y}})e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$. Optics and engineering books use "left" and "right" in conflicting ways, so state the helicity or write out the field.

Two decompositions are useful: circular light is the sum of two equal linear waves $90^\circ$ out of phase, and linear light is the sum of two circular waves of opposite helicity.

### The magnetic field follows

Once you know $\mathbf{E}$ and the direction of travel, $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$ fixes $\mathbf{B}$ completely. Inverting, $\mathbf{E} = c\,\mathbf{B}\times\hat{\mathbf{k}}$. In both cases $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ points along $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$, a quick check of any answer.

### Intensity and polarizers

The intensity of any plane wave is $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 c\,(E_{x0}^2 + E_{y0}^2)$, independent of $\delta$. An ideal **polarizer** passes only the component of $\mathbf{E}$ along its transmission axis. If the incident light is linearly polarized at angle $\theta$ to the axis, the transmitted amplitude is $E_0\cos\theta$, so **Malus's law** gives

$$I = I_0\cos^2\theta.$$

Unpolarized light has all orientations equally; the average of $\cos^2\theta$ is $\tfrac12$, so an ideal polarizer transmits $I_0/2$ whatever its orientation. After that, the light is linear along the axis and Malus's law applies to the next element. A quarter-wave plate shifts $\delta$ by $\pi/2$ between its two axes, turning $45^\circ$ linear light into circular light.

@example
title: Finding the direction of every field in a circular wave
problem: A plane wave in vacuum has $\mathbf{E} = E_0\big[\cos(kz - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \sin(kz - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\big]$. Describe its polarization, find $\mathbf{B}$, and find the Poynting vector.
step: The phase $kz - \omega t$ means the wave travels along $+z$, so $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}$, and $E_z = 0$ confirms that $\mathbf{E}$ is transverse. Write $\varphi = kz - \omega t$ so that $\mathbf{E} = E_0(\cos\varphi,\ \sin\varphi,\ 0)$.
step: At $z = 0$ we have $\varphi = -\omega t$, so $\mathbf{E} = E_0(\cos\omega t,\ -\sin\omega t,\ 0)$. The magnitude is constant, $E_0$, and the tip moves from $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ toward $-\hat{\mathbf{y}}$, clockwise as seen with $+z$ toward you. The wave is circularly polarized with negative helicity ($\delta = -\pi/2$).
step: Compute $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$ using $\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}} = -\hat{\mathbf{x}}$: $\mathbf{B} = (E_0/c)(-\sin\varphi,\ \cos\varphi,\ 0)$. It also rotates, always perpendicular to $\mathbf{E}$ because $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B} \propto -\cos\varphi\sin\varphi + \sin\varphi\cos\varphi = 0$.
step: The Poynting vector is $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$ with $E_xB_y - E_yB_x = (E_0^2/c)(\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi) = E_0^2/c$. So $\mathbf{S} = \dfrac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\,\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \varepsilon_0cE_0^2\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, along $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ and constant in time.
step: Check: this is twice the average intensity $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$ of one linear component alone, as expected for two orthogonal components that each carry that average.

@example
title: Light through three polarizers
problem: Unpolarized light of intensity $16\ \text{W/m}^2$ passes through ideal polarizers with transmission axes at $0^\circ$, $45^\circ$ and $90^\circ$ to the $x$-axis, in that order. Find the final intensity, and compare with the result when the middle polarizer is removed.
step: The first polarizer transmits half of unpolarized light: $I_1 = 16/2 = 8\ \text{W/m}^2$, now linearly polarized along $0^\circ$.
step: The second axis is $45^\circ$ from the incoming polarization, so Malus's law gives $I_2 = 8\cos^2 45^\circ = 4\ \text{W/m}^2$, polarized along $45^\circ$.
step: The third axis is $45^\circ$ from that, so $I_3 = 4\cos^2 45^\circ = 2\ \text{W/m}^2$.
step: Without the middle polarizer the second axis is $90^\circ$ from the first: $8\cos^2 90^\circ = 0$. The extra polarizer lets light through because it rotates the polarization in two steps.

@mistakes
- Using $\cos\theta$ instead of $\cos^2\theta$ for intensity. The amplitude scales as $\cos\theta$; the intensity as its square.
- Applying Malus's law to unpolarized light. The first ideal polarizer simply halves the intensity.
- Concluding that crossed polarizers always block everything. An element between them changes the polarization and lets light through.
- Quoting "right-" or "left-handed" without a convention. State the helicity or write the explicit field.

@practice num
prompt: Linearly polarized light of intensity $8.0\ \text{W/m}^2$ is incident on an ideal polarizer whose transmission axis is $30^\circ$ from the polarization direction. Find the transmitted intensity.
answer: 6
check: 8.0*cos(pi/6)**2
tol: 0.005
unit: W/m^2
hint: The transmitted amplitude is the component of $\mathbf{E}$ along the axis.
hint: Intensity goes as amplitude squared: $I = I_0\cos^2\theta$ with $\cos 30^\circ = \sqrt{3}/2$.
solution: $I = 8.0\cos^2 30^\circ = 8.0\,(3/4) = 6.0\ \text{W/m}^2$.

@practice mc
prompt: A wave travelling along $+z$ has $\mathbf{E} = E_0\big[\cos(kz - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} + \cos(kz - \omega t + \pi/2)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}\big]$. What is its polarization?
choice: Linear along $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$.
choice: Linear at $45^\circ$ to the $x$-axis.
choice*: Circular, positive helicity (at a fixed point $\mathbf{E}$ rotates from $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ toward $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$).
choice: Circular, negative helicity.
hint: Evaluate the field at $z = 0$ and use $\cos(\pi/2 - \omega t) = \sin\omega t$.
solution: At $z = 0$, $E_x = E_0\cos\omega t$ and $E_y = E_0\cos(\pi/2 - \omega t) = E_0\sin\omega t$. The magnitude is constant and the angle is $+\omega t$, so $\mathbf{E}$ rotates from $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ toward $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ (counterclockwise viewed with $+z$ toward you): positive helicity.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements are true for a circularly polarized plane wave in vacuum whose two components each have amplitude $E_0$? Select all that apply.
choice*: The magnitude $|\mathbf{E}|$ is the same at every instant.
choice*: It can be written as the sum of two linearly polarized waves of equal amplitude that are $90^\circ$ out of phase.
choice: $\mathbf{B}$ is parallel to $\mathbf{E}$ at every instant.
choice: The Poynting vector oscillates between zero and $2\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$ at angular frequency $2\omega$.
hint: Write out $|\mathbf{E}|^2$ and $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$ using $\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1$.
solution: $|\mathbf{E}|^2 = E_0^2(\cos^2 + \sin^2) = E_0^2$, and the two components are $90^\circ$ out of phase by definition. $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$ is perpendicular to $\mathbf{E}$, and $S = \varepsilon_0c|\mathbf{E}|^2$ is constant, unlike the linear case.

answer: 1.875
check: 10.0*cos(pi/3)**2*cos(pi/6)**2

@quiz num
prompt: A plane wave in vacuum travelling along $+z$ has $\mathbf{E} = 3.0\cos(kz - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{x}} + 4.0\cos(kz - \omega t + \pi/2)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ in V/m. Use $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ and $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find its time-averaged intensity in $\text{mW/m}^2$.
answer: 33.2
check: quad(lambda t: 8.854e-12*3.00e8*((3.0*cos(-t))**2 + (4.0*cos(-t + pi/2))**2), 0, 2*pi)/(2*pi)*1e3
tol: 1%
unit: mW/m^2
hint: For any plane wave in vacuum the instantaneous Poynting magnitude is $\varepsilon_0c\,|\mathbf{E}|^2$.
hint: Average each component over a cycle: $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \langle\sin^2\rangle = \tfrac12$. The relative phase does not change the sum.
