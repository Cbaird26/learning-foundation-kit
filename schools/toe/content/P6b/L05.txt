@article
Electromagnetic waves constantly cross boundaries between media. To handle that we need the rules that the fields obey at a surface.

### Waves in a linear dielectric

In a linear, nonmagnetic dielectric the displacement field is $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}$ with $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_r\varepsilon_0$, and $\mathbf{H} = \mathbf{B}/\mu_0$. Maxwell's equations keep their vacuum form with $\varepsilon_0 \to \varepsilon$, so waves travel at $v = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon} = c/n$ with **refractive index** $n = \sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$. The frequency is set by the source and does not change on entering the medium, so

$$k = \frac{n\omega}{c},\qquad \lambda = \frac{\lambda_0}{n}.$$

### Boundary conditions

Let $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ point from medium 1 into medium 2. With no free surface charge or free surface current:

| Field | Condition at the interface |
|---|---|
| normal $\mathbf{D}$ | $\varepsilon_1E_{1\perp} = \varepsilon_2E_{2\perp}$ |
| tangential $\mathbf{E}$ | $\mathbf{E}_{1\parallel} = \mathbf{E}_{2\parallel}$ |
| normal $\mathbf{B}$ | $B_{1\perp} = B_{2\perp}$ |
| tangential $\mathbf{H}$ | $\mathbf{B}_{1\parallel}/\mu_1 = \mathbf{B}_{2\parallel}/\mu_2$ |

They come from the integral forms. A flat **pillbox** straddling the surface and Gauss's laws give the normal conditions. A thin rectangular **loop** with long sides on either side of the surface, in Faraday's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, gives the tangential ones: as the loop's width shrinks to zero the flux terms $d\Phi_B/dt$ and $d\Phi_D/dt$ vanish. A free surface charge $\sigma_f$ makes the normal $\mathbf{D}$ jump by $\sigma_f$, and a free surface current makes tangential $\mathbf{H}$ jump.

### Phase matching

The conditions must hold at every point on the interface at every instant. The incident, reflected and transmitted waves have phases $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t$, and the only way those can all agree everywhere on the plane is for the three waves to share one frequency and one **tangential wavevector**:

$$k_i\sin\theta_i = k_r\sin\theta_r = k_t\sin\theta_t,\qquad k_i = k_r = \frac{n_1\omega}{c},\quad k_t = \frac{n_2\omega}{c}.$$

Therefore all three wavevectors lie in the plane of incidence, $\theta_r = \theta_i$ (law of reflection), and

$$n_1\sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t\quad\text{(Snell's law)}.$$

Angles are measured from the normal. We used nothing about the amplitudes, so this geometry is the same for every polarization.

### Total internal reflection

If $n_1 > n_2$, Snell's law has no real solution once $\sin\theta_i > n_2/n_1$. The **critical angle** is $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1$. Beyond it the component of $\mathbf{k}_t$ normal to the surface is imaginary, $k_{tz} = i\kappa$, and the transmitted field is **evanescent**:

$$E\propto e^{-\kappa z},\qquad \kappa = \frac{\omega}{c}\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_i - n_2^2}.$$

It decays within about a wavelength and carries no net power away from the surface.

@example
title: Refraction of 600 nm light into glass
problem: Light of vacuum wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ in air ($n = 1.00$) strikes glass ($n = 1.50$) at $40^\circ$ from the normal. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find the transmission angle, the wavelength and speed in the glass, and the critical angle for light going from the glass back into air.
step: The frequency is unchanged, but the speed and wavelength scale by $1/n$: $v = c/n = 2.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ and $\lambda = \lambda_0/n = 400\ \text{nm}$.
step: Apply Snell's law: $\sin\theta_t = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2}\sin\theta_i = \dfrac{1.00}{1.50}\sin 40^\circ = \dfrac{0.6428}{1.50} = 0.4285$.
step: So $\theta_t = \arcsin(0.4285) = 25.4^\circ$. The ray bends toward the normal on entering the denser medium.
step: For glass to air, $\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1 = 1.00/1.50 = 0.667$, so $\theta_c = 41.8^\circ$.
step: Check phase matching with $k_0 = 2\pi/\lambda_0$: the tangential wavenumber in air is $k_0\sin 40^\circ$, and in glass $1.50\,k_0\sin 25.4^\circ$. Both equal $0.643\,k_0$, as required.

@mistakes
- Treating the normal component of $\mathbf{E}$ as continuous. Without free charge it is the normal $\mathbf{D}$ that is continuous, so $E_\perp$ jumps by the ratio of permittivities.
- Measuring angles from the surface instead of from the normal.
- Believing that wavelength is preserved on entering a medium. The frequency is preserved; $\lambda$ becomes $\lambda_0/n$.
- Concluding that no field exists in the second medium past the critical angle. There is an evanescent field, but it carries no net power away.

answer: 35.17

unit: degrees
hint: Use Snell's law, $n_1\sin\theta_i = n_2\sin\theta_t$, with angles from the normal.

solution: $\sin\theta_t = (1.00)(0.7660)/1.33 = 0.5760$, so $\theta_t = 35.2^\circ$.

solution: The thin-loop argument makes $\mathbf{E}_\parallel$ continuous, and the pillbox argument makes $D_\perp$ continuous. Since $\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon\mathbf{E}$ with different $\varepsilon$, $E_\perp$ and $\mathbf{D}_\parallel$ jump.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from the integral forms of Gauss's law for $\mathbf{D}$ and Faraday's law, derive the boundary conditions that the normal component of $\mathbf{D}$ and the tangential component of $\mathbf{E}$ are continuous across an interface with no free surface charge. Describe the surface or loop you use and explain the limit you take.
hint: Draw a short cylinder (pillbox) with its flat faces just above and just below the surface, and a thin rectangle with its long sides just above and just below.
hint: Shrink the height of the pillbox and the width of the loop to zero, keeping the other dimensions fixed, and ask what happens to the side contributions and to the flux $d\Phi_B/dt$.
solution: Pillbox: take a short cylinder of face area $A$ whose faces lie on either side of the interface. Gauss's law for $\mathbf{D}$ gives $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = Q_{\text{free}}$. As the height tends to zero the side wall contributes nothing, leaving $(\mathbf{D}_2 - \mathbf{D}_1)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\,A = \sigma_fA$. With $\sigma_f = 0$ this gives $D_{1\perp} = D_{2\perp}$. Loop: take a thin rectangle of length $\ell$ along the surface, with sides parallel to the interface on either side. Faraday's law gives $\oint\mathbf{E}\cdot d\boldsymbol{\ell} = -d\Phi_B/dt$. As the width tends to zero, the end segments contribute nothing and the enclosed area, and with it $\Phi_B$ (finite $\mathbf{B}$), tends to zero. So $(\mathbf{E}_{1\parallel} - \mathbf{E}_{2\parallel})\ell = 0$ and the tangential $\mathbf{E}$ is continuous.
rubric: Defines a pillbox and applies $\oint\mathbf{D}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = Q_{\text{free}}$, with the side contribution vanishing as the height tends to zero.
rubric: Concludes $D_{1\perp} = D_{2\perp}$ when $\sigma_f = 0$ (or $D_{2\perp} - D_{1\perp} = \sigma_f$ in general).
rubric: Defines a thin loop and applies Faraday's law, arguing that the flux through the loop vanishes as its width shrinks, because $\mathbf{B}$ is finite.
rubric: Concludes $\mathbf{E}_{1\parallel} = \mathbf{E}_{2\parallel}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Light of vacuum wavelength $600\ \text{nm}$ travels in glass of index $1.50$ and strikes the glass–air interface at $60^\circ$, which is beyond the critical angle. The transmitted field in the air is evanescent, $E\propto e^{-\kappa z}$. Find the decay length $1/\kappa$ in nanometres.
answer: 115.2
check: 1/sqrt((2*pi*1.50*sin(60*pi/180)/600.0)**2 - (2*pi*1.00/600.0)**2)
tol: 1%
unit: nm
hint: The tangential wavenumber is $k_x = n_1k_0\sin\theta_i$ with $k_0 = 2\pi/\lambda_0$, and in air the total wavenumber is $k_0$.
hint: In air $k_x^2 + k_{tz}^2 = k_0^2$, so with $k_{tz} = i\kappa$ we get $\kappa^2 = k_x^2 - k_0^2$.
solution: $\kappa = k_0\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta_i - n_2^2} = \dfrac{2\pi}{600\ \text{nm}}\sqrt{(2.25)(0.75) - 1} = (0.010472\ \text{nm}^{-1})(0.8292) = 8.68\times10^{-3}\ \text{nm}^{-1}$, so $1/\kappa = 115\ \text{nm}$, a fraction of a wavelength.

@quiz ms
prompt: A plane wave in medium 1 ($n_1$) strikes a flat interface with medium 2 ($n_2$). Which statements follow from requiring the boundary conditions to hold at all points of the interface and at all times? Select all that apply.
choice*: The reflected and transmitted waves have the same frequency as the incident wave.
choice*: The component of the wavevector tangential to the interface is the same for all three waves.
choice: The wavelength is the same in both media.
choice*: The reflected and transmitted wavevectors lie in the plane of incidence.
hint: Phase matching constrains $\omega$ and the components of $\mathbf{k}$ along the surface, not the amplitudes or the magnitude of $\mathbf{k}$.
solution: Matching phases for all times forces one frequency, and matching them for all points on the plane forces equal tangential $\mathbf{k}$, which keeps all wavevectors in the plane of incidence. The magnitude $k = n\omega/c$ differs between media, so the wavelength does too.
