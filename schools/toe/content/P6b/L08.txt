@article
In vacuum all frequencies travel at $c$. In matter the refractive index depends on frequency, $n = n(\omega)$, and then waves of different frequencies travel at different speeds: the medium is **dispersive**.

### Phase and group velocity

With $k = n(\omega)\,\omega/c$, a single frequency has **phase velocity** $v_p = \omega/k = c/n$. A pulse is a superposition of frequencies around a carrier. Its envelope moves at the **group velocity**

$$v_g = \frac{d\omega}{dk} = \frac{c}{n + \omega\,dn/d\omega}.$$

The envelope also spreads, because different frequencies in it have different $v_g$. Energy and signals follow $v_g$, not $v_p$.

### The Lorentz oscillator

Model a medium as $N$ bound electrons per unit volume, each with natural frequency $\omega_0$ and damping $\gamma$, driven by $\mathbf{E}\,e^{-i\omega t}$:

$$m(\ddot{x} + \gamma\dot{x} + \omega_0^2x) = -eE.$$

The steady state is $x = -\dfrac{eE/m}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega}$. The polarization $P = -Nex$ gives

$$\varepsilon_r(\omega) = 1 + \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega},\qquad \omega_p^2 = \frac{Ne^2}{\varepsilon_0m}.$$

Well below $\omega_0$, $n > 1$ and $n$ grows with $\omega$ (**normal dispersion**: blue light bends more than red in glass). Within about $\gamma$ of $\omega_0$ the real part of $n$ falls with $\omega$ (**anomalous dispersion**), and the imaginary part, which causes absorption, peaks. Far above $\omega_0$, $\varepsilon_r \to 1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2 < 1$.

### Free electrons: the plasma

For free electrons ($\omega_0 = 0$, negligible damping), $m\,dv/dt = -eE$ gives $v = -ieE/(m\omega)$ and a current density $\mathbf{J} = -Ne\mathbf{v} = (iNe^2/m\omega)\,\mathbf{E}$. Putting this in the Ampère–Maxwell law yields an effective permittivity

$$\varepsilon(\omega) = \varepsilon_0\left(1 - \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2}\right),\qquad \omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2.$$

- For $\omega < \omega_p$, $k$ is imaginary: the wave is evanescent and a plasma slab **reflects** it. (The ionosphere reflects low-frequency radio waves.)
- For $\omega > \omega_p$ the wave propagates with $v_p = c/\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} > c$ and $v_g = c\sqrt{1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2} < c$, so $v_pv_g = c^2$.

This is the same mathematics as the waveguide, with $\omega_p$ playing the role of the cutoff. Filling a waveguide with a non-dispersive dielectric of index $n$ replaces $c$ by $c/n$, so every cutoff frequency drops by the factor $n$; for example the TE$_{10}$ cutoff becomes $f_c = c/(2na)$.

@example
title: Radio waves in the ionosphere
problem: A region of the ionosphere has free-electron density $N = 1.0\times10^{12}\ \text{m}^{-3}$. Use $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, $m = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ and $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find the plasma frequency and the group velocity of a $20\ \text{MHz}$ wave, and say what happens to a $5\ \text{MHz}$ wave.
step: For free electrons $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2)$ with $\omega_p^2 = Ne^2/(\varepsilon_0m)$. This follows from $m\,dv/dt = -eE$ and the current $\mathbf{J} = -Ne\mathbf{v}$ in the Ampère–Maxwell law.
step: Numbers: $\omega_p^2 = \dfrac{(1.0\times10^{12})(1.602\times10^{-19})^2}{(8.854\times10^{-12})(9.109\times10^{-31})} = 3.18\times10^{15}\ \text{s}^{-2}$, so $\omega_p = 5.64\times10^{7}\ \text{rad/s}$ and $f_p = \omega_p/2\pi = 8.98\ \text{MHz}$.
step: At $5\ \text{MHz}$, $f < f_p$: $k$ is imaginary, the wave is evanescent and is reflected rather than transmitted.
step: At $20\ \text{MHz}$, $f > f_p$, and $\sqrt{1 - (8.98/20)^2} = 0.894$. Therefore $v_g = 0.894\,c = 2.68\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Check: $v_p = c/0.894 = 3.36\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$, and $v_pv_g = 9.0\times10^{16}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}^2 = c^2$.

@mistakes
- Treating $v_p > c$ as faster-than-light signalling. A signal travels at $v_g$ (or more precisely, the front velocity), and $v_g < c$ here.
- Using $\omega/k$ for the group velocity. It is $d\omega/dk$, the slope of the dispersion curve.
- Assuming $n$ always increases with frequency. Inside an absorption line it decreases (anomalous dispersion).
- Saying that a wave below the plasma frequency is absorbed. It is reflected; the field in the plasma is evanescent and carries no net power.

prompt: A radio wave of frequency below the plasma frequency is aimed at a thick, uniform plasma. What happens?
choice: It propagates with a phase velocity below $c$.
choice*: Its field is evanescent inside the plasma, so the wave is reflected and carries no net power into the plasma.
choice: It is absorbed, with the energy converted to heat in the electrons.
choice: It propagates at the same speed $c$ as in vacuum.
hint: Look at the sign of $\varepsilon = \varepsilon_0(1 - \omega_p^2/\omega^2)$ and what it does to $k$.
solution: For $\omega < \omega_p$ we have $\varepsilon < 0$, so $k^2 = (\omega^2 - \omega_p^2)/c^2 < 0$ and $k$ is imaginary. The field decays exponentially into the plasma. With negligible damping no power is absorbed; the wave is reflected.

answer: 5.0

hint: In the filled guide the wavenumber is $n\omega/c$ and the transverse wavenumber is still $\pi/a$.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the dispersion relation $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$ for a transverse wave in a cold, collisionless plasma of free electrons with number density $N$. Start from the electron equation of motion and the Ampère–Maxwell law (or the wave equation with a current source), and state what happens for $\omega < \omega_p$.
hint: Solve $m\,dv/dt = -eE$ for $v$ with $\mathbf{E}\propto e^{-i\omega t}$, then form $\mathbf{J} = -Ne\mathbf{v}$.
hint: Take the curl of Faraday's law and use $\mathbf{J}$ as the source in the Ampère–Maxwell law. Then substitute plane waves $e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$.
solution: With $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{E}_0e^{i(kz-\omega t)}$, Newton's law gives $-i\omega m\mathbf{v} = -e\mathbf{E}$, so $\mathbf{v} = -ie\mathbf{E}/(m\omega)$ and $\mathbf{J} = -Ne\mathbf{v} = iNe^2\mathbf{E}/(m\omega)$. Take the curl of Faraday's law: $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\partial_t(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = -\partial_t(\mu_0\mathbf{J} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\mathbf{E})$. For a transverse wave $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$, so the left side is $-\nabla^2\mathbf{E} = k^2\mathbf{E}$. With $\partial_t \to -i\omega$ the right side becomes $i\omega\mu_0\mathbf{J} + (\omega^2/c^2)\mathbf{E} = -\mu_0Ne^2\mathbf{E}/m + (\omega^2/c^2)\mathbf{E}$. Hence $k^2 = \omega^2/c^2 - \omega_p^2/c^2$ with $\omega_p^2 = Ne^2/(\varepsilon_0m)$, i.e. $\omega^2 = \omega_p^2 + c^2k^2$. For $\omega < \omega_p$, $k^2 < 0$: $k$ is imaginary, the field decays as $e^{-\kappa z}$ with $\kappa = \sqrt{\omega_p^2 - \omega^2}/c$, and the plasma reflects the wave.
rubric: Obtains the electron velocity $\mathbf{v} = -ie\mathbf{E}/(m\omega)$ (or equivalent) from Newton's law for an oscillating field.
rubric: Forms the current density $\mathbf{J} = -Ne\mathbf{v}$ and inserts it as the source in the Ampère–Maxwell law.
rubric: Obtains $k^2c^2 = \omega^2 - \omega_p^2$ with $\omega_p^2 = Ne^2/(\varepsilon_0m)$.
rubric: States that for $\omega < \omega_p$ the wavenumber is imaginary, so the wave is evanescent and reflected.

@quiz ms
prompt: A medium has a single Lorentz resonance at $\omega_0$ with small damping. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Well below $\omega_0$ the refractive index exceeds $1$ and increases with frequency (normal dispersion).
choice*: Close to $\omega_0$, where absorption is strong, the real part of $n$ can decrease with frequency (anomalous dispersion).
choice*: Well above $\omega_0$ the real part of $n$ is less than $1$, so the phase velocity exceeds $c$.
choice: The group velocity of a pulse equals $\omega/k$ at every frequency.
choice: The refractive index of a transparent medium never depends on frequency.
hint: Examine $\varepsilon_r(\omega) = 1 + \omega_p^2/(\omega_0^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma\omega)$ in the three regions.
solution: For $\omega \ll \omega_0$ the denominator is positive and decreasing in $\omega$, so $\varepsilon_r > 1$ and rising. Across the resonance the real part falls. For $\omega \gg \omega_0$ the denominator is negative, so $\varepsilon_r < 1$. The group velocity is $d\omega/dk$, which differs from $\omega/k$ whenever $n$ depends on frequency, and real media are dispersive.
