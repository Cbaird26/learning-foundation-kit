@lesson
title: Relativistic fields
summary: Transform $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ between inertial frames, use the two field invariants, and find the field of a uniformly moving charge.
objective: Apply the boost transformation of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ and recognise that a purely electric field in one frame is partly magnetic in another.
objective: Use the invariants $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ and $E^2 - c^2B^2$ to decide which frames exist (for example, one with $\mathbf{B}' = 0$).
objective: Compute the field of a charge moving at constant velocity and explain its $\gamma$-dependent shape.
reading: David Tong, Electromagnetism lecture notes: look up electromagnetism and special relativity (Lorentz transformations of the electric and magnetic fields, the field strength tensor).

@article
Maxwell's equations hold in every inertial frame with the same $c$, so the fields of one observer must be expressible in terms of another's. Charge and current combine into a four-vector $J^\mu = (c\rho, \mathbf{J})$ and the potentials into $A^\mu = (\varphi/c, \mathbf{A})$, with metric signature $(+,-,-,-)$. Then charge conservation is $\partial_\mu J^\mu = 0$, the Lorenz gauge is $\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0$, and the wave equations are $\partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu = \mu_0J^\nu$. The fields are the antisymmetric tensor $F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu$, whose six components are $\mathbf{E}/c$ and $\mathbf{B}$.

### Transformation of the fields

Let frame $S'$ move with velocity $v$ along $+x$ relative to $S$, and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$. Components along the motion are unchanged and the transverse ones mix:

$$E'_x = E_x,\quad E'_y = \gamma(E_y - vB_z),\quad E'_z = \gamma(E_z + vB_y),$$
$$B'_x = B_x,\quad B'_y = \gamma\!\left(B_y + \frac{vE_z}{c^2}\right),\quad B'_z = \gamma\!\left(B_z - \frac{vE_y}{c^2}\right).$$

In vector form $\mathbf{E}'_\perp = \gamma(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})_\perp$ and $\mathbf{B}'_\perp = \gamma(\mathbf{B} - \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{E}/c^2)_\perp$. A pure electric field in $S$ has a magnetic part in $S'$: magnetism is the relativistic consequence of electricity plus motion.

### Invariants

Two combinations are the same in every inertial frame (they come from contracting $F^{\mu\nu}$ with itself and with its dual):

$$\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B},\qquad E^2 - c^2B^2.$$

Consequences:

- If $\mathbf{E}\perp\mathbf{B}$ and $E > cB$ in one frame, there is a frame (moving at $\beta = cB/E$ along $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}$) where $\mathbf{B}' = 0$. If $cB > E$ there is a frame with $\mathbf{E}' = 0$.
- A vacuum plane wave has both invariants zero, so in every frame it is still a plane wave with $\mathbf{E}\perp\mathbf{B}$ and $E = cB$ (with a Doppler-shifted frequency and amplitude).
- Neither $E$ nor $B$ alone is invariant, and neither is $E^2 + c^2B^2$.

### Field of a uniformly moving charge

Start from the Coulomb field in the charge's rest frame and transform. At the field point, let $b$ be the perpendicular distance from the line of motion and $x - vt$ the displacement along it, measured from the charge's present position. Then

$$\mathbf{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\,\frac{\gamma\,\mathbf{R}}{\left[\gamma^2(x - vt)^2 + b^2\right]^{3/2}},\qquad \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{E}}{c^2},$$

where $\mathbf{R}$ points from the present position of the charge to the field point. The field points away from the present position, not the retarded one. Abeam of the charge ($x = vt$) the transverse field is enhanced to $\gamma q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2)$, while on the line of motion at distance $d$ it is reduced to $q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0\gamma^2d^2)$. The field lines are squeezed into a disc perpendicular to the motion, and a passing observer sees a pulse of duration about $b/(\gamma v)$.

@example
title: A uniform electric field seen from a moving frame
problem: In frame $S$ there is a uniform electric field $E_y = 3.0\times10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$ and no magnetic field. Frame $S'$ moves along $+x$ at $v = 0.60c$. Use $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find $\mathbf{E}'$ and $\mathbf{B}'$ and check that $E^2 - c^2B^2$ is invariant.
step: The Lorentz factor is $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 1.25$.
step: Electric field: $E'_y = \gamma(E_y - vB_z) = 1.25(3.0\times10^{6}) = 3.75\times10^{6}\ \text{V/m}$, and $E'_x = E'_z = 0$.
step: Magnetic field: $B'_z = \gamma(B_z - vE_y/c^2) = -1.25\,\dfrac{0.60}{3.00\times10^{8}}\,(3.0\times10^{6}) = -7.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{T}$, with $B'_x = B'_y = 0$. A magnetic field has appeared although there was none in $S$.
step: Invariant: $E'^2 - c^2B'^2 = (3.75\times10^{6})^2 - (3.00\times10^{8}\cdot7.5\times10^{-3})^2 = 1.406\times10^{13} - 5.06\times10^{12} = 9.0\times10^{12}\ \text{V}^2/\text{m}^2$, equal to $E_y^2 = (3.0\times10^{6})^2$. Also $\mathbf{E}'\cdot\mathbf{B}' = 0$, as in $S$.

@example
title: The field of a passing charge
problem: A charge $q = 2.0\ \text{nC}$ moves at $v = 0.60c$. Take $1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0) = 8.988\times10^{9}\ \text{N·m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Find the transverse electric field at perpendicular distance $b = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ at the instant the charge is abeam, and the field on its line of motion $5.0\ \text{cm}$ ahead, and compare each with the Coulomb value.
step: The Coulomb value at $5.0\ \text{cm}$ is $\dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = (8.988\times10^{9})\dfrac{2.0\times10^{-9}}{(0.050)^2} = 7.19\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$. Here $\gamma = 1.25$.
step: Abeam, $x - vt = 0$, so $E_\perp = \gamma q/(4\pi\varepsilon_0b^2) = 1.25(7.19\times10^{3}) = 8.99\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$, stronger than Coulomb by $\gamma$.
step: Ahead on the line of motion, $b = 0$ and $x - vt = d$, so $E_\parallel = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\gamma^2d^2} = \dfrac{7.19\times10^{3}}{1.5625} = 4.60\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$, weaker by $1/\gamma^2$.
step: The field is stronger at the sides and weaker along the line of motion, which is the flattening of field lines into a disc perpendicular to the velocity.

@mistakes
- Treating $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ as separately invariant. Only $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ and $E^2 - c^2B^2$ are.
- Applying $\gamma$ to the components parallel to the boost. Those components are unchanged; only the transverse ones mix.
- Getting the sign of $v$ wrong. Frame $S'$ moves along $+x$ relative to $S$ in the formulas above; test your signs on a plane wave, which must stay $E = cB$.
- Placing the field of a moving charge along the line from its retarded position. For uniform motion the field points away from its present position.

solution: $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ and $E^2 - c^2B^2$ are the Lorentz scalars built from $F^{\mu\nu}$. Energy density and the Poynting vector are components of the energy–momentum tensor and change under boosts, and $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ mix.

answer: 60
check: (1/sqrt(1 - 0.6**2))*(120.0 - 0.6*3.00e8*(120.0/3.00e8))
tol: 1%
unit: V/m
hint: Use $E'_y = \gamma(E_y - vB_z)$ with $B_z = E_y/c$.
hint: $E'_y = \gamma E_y(1 - v/c) = \sqrt{(1-\beta)/(1+\beta)}\,E_y$.

prompt: In frame $S$ the fields are $\mathbf{E} = E_0\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and $\mathbf{B} = (E_0/2c)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. (a) Compute the two invariants. (b) Find the velocity of a frame $S'$ in which the magnetic field vanishes, and the electric field there. Check your answer against the invariant.
hint: Compute $\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{B}$ and $E^2 - c^2B^2$, then set $B'_z = \gamma(B_z - vE_y/c^2) = 0$.
