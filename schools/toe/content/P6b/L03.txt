@article
In vacuum every Cartesian component of $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ obeys $\nabla^2 f = c^{-2}\,\partial^2 f/\partial t^2$. The simplest solution is a **plane wave**, whose crests lie on flat planes moving at speed $c$.

### The solution

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) = \mathbf{E}_0\cos(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t + \phi).$$

Here $\mathbf{k}$ is the **wavevector** (it points along the direction of travel and has $|\mathbf{k}| = 2\pi/\lambda$), $\omega = 2\pi f$ is the angular frequency, and $\phi$ is a constant phase. Substituting into the wave equation gives $-k^2 = -\omega^2/c^2$, so

$$\omega = ck,\qquad c = \lambda f.$$

It is often easier to use the complex form $\tilde{\mathbf{E}} = \mathbf{E}_0 e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}$ and take the real part at the end. Then $\nabla \to i\mathbf{k}$ and $\partial_t \to -i\omega$, and Maxwell's equations become algebra.

### Field directions

The Maxwell equations in vacuum impose more than the wave equation:

- $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0$ gives $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{E}_0 = 0$: the wave is **transverse**. Likewise $\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{B}_0 = 0$.
- Faraday's law $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial_t\mathbf{B}$ gives $\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{E}_0 = \omega\mathbf{B}_0$, so

$$\mathbf{B} = \frac{1}{c}\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{E},\qquad |\mathbf{B}| = \frac{|\mathbf{E}|}{c}.$$

So $\mathbf{E}$, $\mathbf{B}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{k}}$ form a right-handed orthogonal triad, and $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ oscillate **in phase**. For $\mathbf{E} = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ the wave travels along $+x$ and $\mathbf{B} = (E_0/c)\cos(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$. If the wave travels along $-x$, the same rule gives $\mathbf{B}$ along $-\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ for $\mathbf{E}$ along $+\hat{\mathbf{y}}$.

### Energy and intensity

Because $B = E/c$, the electric and magnetic energy densities are equal, and

$$u = \varepsilon_0E^2,\qquad \mathbf{S} = \frac{E^2}{\mu_0 c}\,\hat{\mathbf{k}} = cu\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}.$$

Energy moves at speed $c$ in the direction of $\mathbf{k}$. Averaging $\cos^2$ over a cycle gives $\tfrac12$, so the **intensity** is

$$I = \langle S\rangle = \tfrac12\,\varepsilon_0 c\,E_0^2 = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0 c}.$$

Using $c = 3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$, the product $\varepsilon_0 c$ is $2.66\times10^{-3}\ \text{S}$, the reciprocal of the impedance of free space ($\approx 377\ \Omega$).

@example
title: Reading off everything from one plane wave
problem: A radio wave in vacuum has $\mathbf{E} = (120\ \text{V/m})\cos(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ and frequency $f = 100\ \text{MHz}$. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find $\omega$, $k$, $\lambda$, the magnetic field, and the intensity.
step: Frequency to angular frequency: $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi(1.00\times10^{8}) = 6.28\times10^{8}\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: Wavenumber and wavelength: $k = \omega/c = 2.09\ \text{rad/m}$ and $\lambda = c/f = 3.00\ \text{m}$. As a check, $2\pi/k = 3.00\ \text{m}$.
step: The phase $kx - \omega t$ means travel along $+x$, so $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}$. Then $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$, and since $\hat{\mathbf{x}}\times\hat{\mathbf{y}} = \hat{\mathbf{z}}$, $\mathbf{B} = (4.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{T})\cos(kx - \omega t)\,\hat{\mathbf{z}}$.
step: Intensity: $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 c E_0^2 = \tfrac12(8.854\times10^{-12})(3.00\times10^{8})(120)^2 \approx 19\ \text{W/m}^2$.
step: Check the direction of energy flow: $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B} \propto \hat{\mathbf{y}}\times\hat{\mathbf{z}} = \hat{\mathbf{x}}$, along the direction of travel.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that $\omega = ck$ is a vacuum result, then computing $k$ from $f$ with the wrong speed.
- Setting $B = E$. The amplitudes differ by a factor of $c$ and have different units.
- Omitting the $\tfrac12$ from the time-averaged intensity, which is the value for a field of amplitude $E_0$, not the peak $S$.
- Taking $\mathbf{B}$ along $+\hat{\mathbf{z}}$ for a wave travelling toward $-x$. The triad $\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B} \parallel \hat{\mathbf{k}}$ decides the sign.

hint: Read the direction of travel from the sign between $kx$ and $\omega t$.
solution: The phase $kx + \omega t$ describes a wave moving toward $-x$, so $\hat{\mathbf{k}} = -\hat{\mathbf{x}}$. choice*: The electric and magnetic energy densities are equal at every point and time.

solution: From $\mathbf{B} = \hat{\mathbf{k}}\times\mathbf{E}/c$, the two fields are proportional, so they are in phase, and $B^2/2\mu_0 = E^2/(2\mu_0c^2) = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$. They are perpendicular to each other, not parallel. In vacuum every frequency travels at $c$.

@quiz expr
prompt: A plane wave in vacuum has wavevector $\mathbf{k} = (k_x, k_y, k_z)$ and speed of light $c$. Find its angular frequency $\omega$ in terms of $c$, $k_x$, $k_y$, $k_z$.
vars: c, k_x, k_y, k_z
lead: $\omega =$
answer: c sqrt(k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2)
check: lambda c, k_x, k_y, k_z: root(lambda w: w**2/c**2 - (k_x**2 + k_y**2 + k_z**2), 0, 100)
domain: c: 0.5..2; k_x: 0.3..2; k_y: 0.3..2; k_z: 0.3..2
hint: Substitute $f = e^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r} - \omega t)}$ into $\nabla^2 f = c^{-2}\partial_t^2 f$.
hint: $\nabla^2 \to -|\mathbf{k}|^2$ and $\partial_t^2 \to -\omega^2$, with $|\mathbf{k}|^2 = k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2$.
solution: The wave equation gives $-|\mathbf{k}|^2 = -\omega^2/c^2$, so $\omega = c|\mathbf{k}| = c\sqrt{k_x^2 + k_y^2 + k_z^2}$ (taking $\omega > 0$).

@quiz num
prompt: A laser emits a uniform circular beam of radius $1.0\ \text{mm}$ with total power $5.0\ \text{mW}$. Treat it as a plane wave in vacuum and take $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ and $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$. Find the electric-field amplitude $E_0$.
answer: 1095
check: sqrt(2*(5.0e-3/(pi*(1.0e-3)**2))/(8.854e-12*3.00e8))
tol: 1%
unit: V/m
hint: Intensity is power per unit area of the beam cross-section.
hint: $I = P/(\pi r^2)$, then solve $I = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$ for $E_0$.
solution: $I = (5.0\times10^{-3})/(\pi(1.0\times10^{-3})^2) = 1.59\times10^{3}\ \text{W/m}^2$. Then $E_0 = \sqrt{2I/(\varepsilon_0c)} = \sqrt{2(1.59\times10^{3})/(2.66\times10^{-3})} \approx 1.09\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$.
