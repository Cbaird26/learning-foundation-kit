@article
Energy is not stored only in charges. It lives in the fields and it flows through space. This lesson turns Maxwell's equations into local conservation laws for energy and momentum.

### Work done on charges

The Lorentz force on a charge $q$ with velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is $q(\mathbf{E} + \mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})$. The magnetic part is perpendicular to $\mathbf{v}$, so it does no work. The power the fields deliver to charges in a volume $V$ is therefore

$$\frac{dW}{dt} = \int_V \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV,$$

and $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the power per unit volume given to the charges (Joule heating in a resistor, for example).

### Poynting's theorem

Dot Faraday's law with $\mathbf{B}$, dot the Ampère–Maxwell law with $\mathbf{E}$, and subtract using the identity $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) - \mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})$:

$$\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}) = -\mathbf{B}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} - \mu_0\,\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{E}}{\partial t}.$$

Divide by $\mu_0$ and use $\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B} = \tfrac12\partial_t B^2$ (likewise for $\mathbf{E}$):

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E},\qquad u = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2,\qquad \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\,\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}.$$

Here $u$ is the **energy density** (J/m³) and $\mathbf{S}$ is the **Poynting vector**, the energy flux density (W/m²) pointing in the direction of energy flow. Integrate over a fixed volume $V$ with boundary $\partial V$ and use the divergence theorem:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_V u\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A} - \int_V \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV.$$

Field energy inside falls when energy leaves through the boundary (positive outward flux of $\mathbf{S}$) or when the field does work on charges. This is a **field energy balance with boundary flux**.

### Momentum and radiation pressure

The fields also carry momentum, with density

$$\mathbf{g} = \varepsilon_0\,\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{S}}{c^2}.$$

A plane wave of intensity $I$ (the time-averaged magnitude of $\mathbf{S}$) delivers momentum at the rate $I/c$ per unit area. At normal incidence the **radiation pressure** is $P = I/c$ on a perfect absorber and $P = 2I/c$ on a perfect mirror, since reflection reverses the momentum. At angle of incidence $\theta$ (from the normal) the beam spreads over more surface, giving a factor $\cos\theta$, and only the normal component of momentum pushes, giving another: $P = 2I\cos^2\theta/c$ for a perfect mirror.

The full momentum balance uses the Maxwell stress tensor and has the same structure as the energy balance: field momentum changes through a boundary flux plus the force on charges.

@example
title: Where does a resistor's heat come from?
problem: A straight resistive wire of radius $a = 1.0\ \text{mm}$ carries a steady current $I = 10\ \text{A}$ along $+z$. The electric field inside the wire and at its surface is uniform, $E = 0.20\ \text{V/m}$ along $+z$. Find the Poynting vector at the surface, the power per unit length that flows through the surface, and compare it with the Joule heating per unit length.
step: Ampère's law gives the magnetic field at the surface: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(10)}{1.0\times10^{-3}} = 2.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{T}$, circulating around the wire in the $\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ direction.
step: Direction: $\hat{\mathbf{z}}\times\hat{\boldsymbol{\phi}} = -\hat{\boldsymbol{\rho}}$, so $\mathbf{S}$ points radially inward. Magnitude: $S = \dfrac{EB}{\mu_0} = \dfrac{(0.20)(2.0\times10^{-3})}{4\pi\times10^{-7}} \approx 318\ \text{W/m}^2$.
step: The surface of one metre of wire has area $2\pi a(1\ \text{m})$, so the inward power is $2\pi(1.0\times10^{-3})(318) = 2.0\ \text{W}$ per metre.
step: Joule heating per metre is $\int \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dA = E\int J\,dA = EI = (0.20)(10) = 2.0\ \text{W/m}$.
step: The two agree. The wire's fields are steady ($\partial u/\partial t = 0$), so Poynting's theorem says $-\oint\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A} = \int\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV$: the energy dissipated in the wire arrives through its surface from the surrounding fields, not along the wire.

@mistakes
- Saying the magnetic field does work on charges. The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so only $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ transfers energy.
- Treating $\mathbf{S}$ as stored energy. It is a flux, in W/m², and only its divergence changes the stored energy.
- Getting the sign wrong in the integral balance: $+\oint\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A}$ is energy leaving, which lowers the energy inside.
- Forgetting the factor $2$ for a mirror, or the factor $\cos^2\theta$ for oblique incidence.

@practice mc
prompt: In Poynting's theorem, $\partial u/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$, what does a positive value of $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ at a point represent?
choice*: Field energy is being converted into energy of the moving charges (for example heat) at that point.
choice: Energy is being carried away from that point by electromagnetic radiation.
choice: Energy is being transferred from the charges into the fields at that point.
choice: The magnetic field is doing work on the charges at that point.
hint: $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the rate of work done by the field on the charges per unit volume.
solution: $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} > 0$ means the field does positive work on the charges, so field energy decreases. Energy carried away by radiation is the divergence of $\mathbf{S}$, and magnetic forces never do work.

@practice num

@practice self
prompt: Derive Poynting's theorem in vacuum with sources. Starting from Faraday's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law, obtain $\partial u/\partial t + \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$, identify $u$ and $\mathbf{S}$, and then write the integral form for a fixed volume $V$, saying what each term represents at the boundary and inside.
hint: Combine $\mathbf{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E})$ and $\mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})$ so that the vector identity for $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B})$ appears.
hint: Write $\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{E}$ as time derivatives of $B^2/2$ and $E^2/2$, then integrate over $V$ and use the divergence theorem.
solution: Dot Faraday's law $\nabla\times\mathbf{E} = -\partial_t\mathbf{B}$ with $\mathbf{B}$: $\mathbf{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) = -\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B}$. Dot the Ampère–Maxwell law with $\mathbf{E}$: $\mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}) = \mu_0\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{E}$. Subtract and use $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}) = \mathbf{B}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}) - \mathbf{E}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{B})$: $\nabla\cdot(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}) = -\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B} - \mu_0\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} - \mu_0\varepsilon_0\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{E}$. Divide by $\mu_0$ and write $\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B} = \partial_t(B^2/2)$, $\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{E} = \partial_t(E^2/2)$: $\partial_t\left(\tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2\right) + \nabla\cdot\left(\mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0\right) = -\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$. So $u = \tfrac{\varepsilon_0}{2}E^2 + \tfrac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2$ and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E}\times\mathbf{B}/\mu_0$. Integrating over $V$ and applying the divergence theorem gives $\dfrac{d}{dt}\int_V u\,dV = -\oint_{\partial V}\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{A} - \int_V\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}\,dV$: field energy inside changes because of the net outward energy flux through the boundary and because of the work done on charges inside.

rubric: Converts $\mathbf{B}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{E}\cdot\partial_t\mathbf{E}$ into time derivatives of $B^2/2$ and $E^2/2$ and identifies $u$ and $\mathbf{S}$ with correct constants.
rubric: States the final local form with the correct sign of the $-\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ term.
rubric: Integrates and applies the divergence theorem, and interprets the outward flux of $\mathbf{S}$ through $\partial V$ and the work done on charges inside $V$.

@quiz num
prompt: A circular parallel-plate capacitor with plate radius $R = 0.050\ \text{m}$ and gap $d = 2.0\ \text{mm}$ is charging, with the uniform gap field $E = 2.0\times10^{4}\ \text{V/m}$ increasing at $1.0\times10^{9}\ \text{V/(m·s)}$. Use $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \text{F/m}$ and $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T·m/A}$. Find the total power carried by the Poynting vector through the cylindrical surface at the rim of the gap into the capacitor, in watts. Neglect edge effects.
answer: 2.782e-3
check: (lambda B: 2.0e4*B/(4*pi*1e-7)*2*pi*0.050*2.0e-3)(4*pi*1e-7*8.854e-12*(0.050/2)*1.0e9)
tol: 1%
unit: W
hint: Find $B$ at the rim from the Ampère–Maxwell law on a circle of radius $R$, using the displacement current only.
hint: $B(2\pi R) = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\pi R^2\,dE/dt$, so $B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0 (R/2)\,dE/dt$. Then $S = EB/\mu_0$, pointing inward, times the side area $2\pi R d$.
solution: $B = \mu_0\varepsilon_0(R/2)\,\dot{E} = 2.78\times10^{-10}\ \text{T}$. Then $S = EB/\mu_0 = 4.43\ \text{W/m}^2$ inward, and $P = S\,(2\pi R d) = 2.78\times10^{-3}\ \text{W}$. This equals $\dfrac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2\right)\pi R^2 d$, the rate at which the gap's stored electric energy grows.

@quiz num
prompt: A laser beam of intensity $3.0\times10^{3}\ \text{W/m}^2$ reflects off a perfect mirror with angle of incidence $60^\circ$ measured from the normal. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ and find the radiation pressure on the mirror.
answer: 5.0e-6
check: 2*3.0e3*cos(pi/3)**2/3.0e8
tol: 1%
unit: Pa
hint: A beam of intensity $I$ crossing a surface tilted by $\theta$ delivers power $I\cos\theta$ per unit surface area.
hint: Reflection reverses only the normal component of momentum, which is the fraction $\cos\theta$ of the beam's momentum; the change is twice that.
solution: Momentum arrives at a rate $(I\cos\theta)/c$ per unit area. The normal component changes by $2\cos\theta$ times this, so $P = 2I\cos^2\theta/c = 2(3.0\times10^{3})(0.25)/(3.00\times10^{8}) = 5.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{Pa}$.
