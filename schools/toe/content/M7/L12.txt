@article
Regression models how the average of a response $y$ changes with a predictor $x$. The **simple linear regression model** says

$$y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_i + \varepsilon_i, \qquad \varepsilon_i \ \text{independent, with mean } 0 \text{ and variance } \sigma^2.$$

For exact small-sample inference we also assume that the errors are normal.

### Least squares

Choose $b_0$ and $b_1$ to minimize the sum of squared **residuals** $e_i = y_i - (b_0 + b_1 x_i)$. Calculus gives

$$b_1 = \frac{S_{xy}}{S_{xx}} = r\,\frac{s_y}{s_x}, \qquad b_0 = \bar y - b_1 \bar x,$$

where $S_{xx} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$ and $S_{xy} = \sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)$. The line passes through $(\bar x, \bar y)$ and the residuals add to $0$. The slope is the estimated change in the *mean* of $y$ per one-unit increase in $x$. It describes association, and is causal only when $x$ was assigned at random. Do not extrapolate far beyond the observed range of $x$.

**$R^2$** $= 1 - \text{SSE}/\text{SST} = r^2$ is the fraction of the variation in $y$ explained by the line, where $\text{SSE} = \sum e_i^2$ and $\text{SST} = \sum (y_i - \bar y)^2$.

### Inference for the slope

The residual variance uses $n - 2$ degrees of freedom, because two parameters were fitted:

$$\hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\text{SSE}}{n - 2}, \qquad \text{SE}(b_1) = \frac{\hat\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad t = \frac{b_1}{\text{SE}(b_1)} \sim t_{n-2} \ \text{ under } H_0\colon \beta_1 = 0.$$

A 95% confidence interval for $\beta_1$ is $b_1 \pm t^*_{n-2}\,\text{SE}(b_1)$. These formulas are only as trustworthy as the assumptions behind them.

### Model checking with residuals

Plot residuals against the fitted values (or against $x$). If the model is adequate, the plot is a patternless band around $0$. Look for:

- **Curvature** (a U or an arch): the mean is not linear in $x$. Add a term or transform.
- **A funnel**: the spread grows with the fitted value, so constant variance fails and the standard errors are unreliable. Consider transforming $y$.
- **Outliers and high-leverage points** that pull the line toward themselves.
- **Trends in time order** for data collected over time: the errors may not be independent.

$R^2$ and p-values cannot detect these problems; a plot can. Do not report a regression you have not plotted.

```python
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8], dtype=float)
y = np.array([2.1, 2.9, 4.2, 4.8, 6.1, 7.2, 7.8, 9.1])
b1, b0 = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
resid = y - (b0 + b1 * x)
print(round(b1, 3), round(b0, 3), round(abs(resid.sum()), 8))   # 0.998 1.036 0.0
```

@example
title: Fitting a line and judging the slope
problem: Fit a least squares line to the eight points $x = 1, \dots, 8$ and $y = 2.1,\ 2.9,\ 4.2,\ 4.8,\ 6.1,\ 7.2,\ 7.8,\ 9.1$. Report $R^2$ and a 95% confidence interval for the slope.
step: Summaries: $\bar x = 4.5$, $\bar y = 5.525$, $S_{xx} = 42$ and $S_{xy} = 41.9$.
step: Slope and intercept: $b_1 = \frac{41.9}{42} \approx 0.998$ and $b_0 = 5.525 - 0.9976(4.5) \approx 1.036$. The fitted line is $\hat y = 1.036 + 0.998\,x$: each extra unit of $x$ goes with about one more unit of $y$ on average.
step: Fit quality: $\text{SST} = 41.995$ and $\text{SSE} \approx 0.195$, so $R^2 = 1 - \frac{0.195}{41.995} \approx 0.995$.
step: Uncertainty: $\hat\sigma = \sqrt{0.195/6} \approx 0.180$ with $n - 2 = 6$ degrees of freedom, so $\text{SE}(b_1) = \frac{0.180}{\sqrt{42}} \approx 0.0278$.
step: Interval: with $t^*_6 = 2.447$, the interval is $0.998 \pm 2.447(0.0278) \approx (0.930,\ 1.066)$.
step: Check the residuals. They are $0.07,\ -0.13,\ 0.17,\ -0.23,\ 0.08,\ 0.18,\ -0.22,\ 0.08$: they add to $0$ and change sign irregularly with no trend, so there is no sign of curvature.

@example
title: A high R-squared with the wrong model
problem: Fit a straight line to the points $(1, 1), (2, 4), (3, 9), (4, 16), (5, 25)$, which lie exactly on $y = x^2$. Compute $R^2$ and examine the residuals.
step: Summaries: $\bar x = 3$, $\bar y = 11$, $S_{xx} = 10$ and $S_{xy} = 60$. So $b_1 = 6$ and $b_0 = 11 - 6(3) = -7$, giving $\hat y = -7 + 6x$.
step: Residuals $e_i = y_i - \hat y_i$: at $x = 1, \dots, 5$ they are $2,\ -1,\ -2,\ -1,\ 2$. They add to $0$, as they must.
step: $\text{SSE} = 4 + 1 + 4 + 1 + 4 = 14$ and $\text{SST} = 374$, so $R^2 = 1 - \frac{14}{374} \approx 0.963$. The line "explains" 96% of the variation.
step: But the residuals are positive at both ends and negative in the middle: a U-shape, not random scatter. The linear form is systematically wrong.
step: Fitting $y = a + bx + cx^2$ instead removes the pattern (here the fit is exact). A high $R^2$ cannot replace looking at the residuals.

@mistakes
- Reading a slope as a causal effect without randomization, or predicting far outside the range of the observed $x$ values.
- Trusting $R^2$ or a small p-value alone. Curvature, outliers and non-constant variance can all coexist with a high $R^2$.
- Using $n$ instead of $n - 2$ in the residual variance and the degrees of freedom of the $t$ test and interval.
- Expecting the regression of $x$ on $y$ to give the same line as the regression of $y$ on $x$. The two least squares lines differ unless $|r| = 1$.

@practice self
prompt: Write Python (numpy) for two simulations. (a) Estimate the power of the two-sided $5\%$ $t$ test of $H_0\colon \beta_1 = 0$ when the true model is $y = 1 + 0.15x + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \text{Normal}(0, 2^2)$, using the fixed values $x = 1, 2, \dots, 20$ and $5000$ simulated data sets (use $t^* = 2.101$ for $18$ degrees of freedom). (b) Generate one data set from the curved truth $y = 1 + 0.5x + 0.05x^2 + \varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon \sim \text{Normal}(0, 1)$, fit a straight line, and examine the residuals. Report the power estimate, describe the residual pattern, and explain why a high $R^2$ would not reveal the problem in (b).
hint: In (a), repeat: simulate $y$, compute $b_1$, $\text{SE}(b_1)$ and $|t|$, and count how often $|t| > 2.101$; the power is that fraction.
hint: In (b), compute the residuals $y - (b_0 + b_1 x)$ and look at their signs along $x$, or plot them.
solution: ```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
x = np.arange(1, 21, dtype=float)
n, sigma, beta1, reps = len(x), 2.0, 0.15, 5000
sxx = ((x - x.mean())**2).sum()
tcrit = 2.101                      # t quantile, 18 df, two-sided 5%
reject = 0
for _ in range(reps):
    y = 1 + beta1 * x + rng.normal(0, sigma, n)
    b1 = ((x - x.mean()) * (y - y.mean())).sum() / sxx
    b0 = y.mean() - b1 * x.mean()
    resid = y - (b0 + b1 * x)
    se = np.sqrt((resid**2).sum() / (n - 2) / sxx)
    reject += abs(b1 / se) > tcrit
print("power ~", reject / reps)    # about 0.45

# (b) curved truth, straight-line fit
y = 1 + 0.5 * x + 0.05 * x**2 + rng.normal(0, 1.0, n)
b1, b0 = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
resid = y - (b0 + b1 * x)
print(np.round(resid, 1))          # positive at both ends, negative in the middle
```
The power is only about $0.45$: with this noise and sample size, the test misses a real slope of $0.15$ more than half the time, so a non-significant result would not show that the slope is zero. In (b) the residuals form a U-shape (positive for small and large $x$, negative in the middle) because the straight line cannot follow the curvature. The straight-line $R^2$ is still typically above $0.95$, because the overall upward trend dominates the variation. $R^2$ measures how much variation the line captures, not whether its form is right, so only the residual plot exposes the problem.

@quiz ms
prompt: A plot of residuals against fitted values shows a funnel: the residuals fan out as the fitted values increase. Select all statements that are correct.
choice*: The assumption of constant error variance is violated.
choice*: Standard errors, p-values and intervals that assume constant variance may be unreliable.
choice: The sign of the fitted slope must be wrong.
choice*: Transforming the response (for example, taking its logarithm) is a reasonable thing to try.
choice: A high $R^2$ shows that the problem can safely be ignored.
hint: A funnel concerns the spread of the errors, not their center.
solution: A funnel means the variance of the errors grows with the fitted value, which breaks the constant-variance assumption behind the usual standard errors. A transformation of $y$ or a weighted fit is a standard remedy. The funnel says nothing about the sign of the slope, and $R^2$ cannot detect or excuse the problem.
