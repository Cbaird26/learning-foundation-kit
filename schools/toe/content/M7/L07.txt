@article
Suppose $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_n$ are **independent and identically distributed** (iid) with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Their **sample mean** $\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + \dots + X_n)$ has

$$E[\bar X_n] = \mu, \qquad \operatorname{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \operatorname{SD}(\bar X_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

Averaging leaves the center alone and shrinks the spread like $1/\sqrt{n}$. The two big limit theorems say what happens next.

### Two inequalities

**Markov's inequality.** If $X \ge 0$ and $a > 0$, then $P(X \ge a) \le \dfrac{E[X]}{a}$.

**Chebyshev's inequality.** For any random variable with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, and any $k > 0$,

$$P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{1}{k^2}.$$

Chebyshev follows by applying Markov to $(X - \mu)^2$. Both bounds hold for *every* distribution that meets their conditions, which makes them crude: for a normal variable the true probability beyond $2\sigma$ is about $0.046$, while Chebyshev guarantees only $\le 0.25$.

### Law of large numbers

Chebyshev applied to $\bar X_n$ gives

$$P(|\bar X_n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \longrightarrow 0 \quad (n \to \infty).$$

This is the **(weak) law of large numbers**: the sample mean lands within any fixed distance of $\mu$ with probability tending to $1$. (The strong law says $\bar X_n \to \mu$ with probability $1$.) It tells you *where* the average goes, not how fast or in what shape.

### Central limit theorem

For iid variables with finite variance,

$$\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \ \longrightarrow\ \text{Normal}(0, 1) \quad \text{in distribution.}$$

Equivalently, the sum $S_n$ is approximately Normal$(n\mu,\ n\sigma^2)$. The theorem describes the *shape* of the fluctuations whatever the shape of the original distribution. For integer-valued sums, use a **continuity correction**: treat the integer $k$ as the interval $[k - \frac{1}{2},\ k + \frac{1}{2}]$, so $P(S_n \ge k) \approx P(S_n > k - \frac{1}{2})$.

### What the theorems do not promise

- Finite variance is needed. A density such as $f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}$ has tails too heavy for a mean to exist, and its sample means never settle down.
- Independence (or at least weak dependence) matters.
- "Large $n$" depends on how skewed the original distribution is. The rule $n \ge 30$ is a rough guide, not a theorem, and the approximation is worst in the far tails.
- The sum's absolute error $S_n - n\mu$ does not shrink; it grows like $\sqrt{n}$. Only the *average* error shrinks.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
means = rng.exponential(1.0, size=(20000, 50)).mean(axis=1)   # 20000 sample means, n = 50
print(round(means.mean(), 3), round(means.std(), 3))          # near 1 and 1/sqrt(50) = 0.141
```

@example
title: A Chebyshev guarantee
problem: Weekly sales have mean $100$ and standard deviation $10$, with an unknown distribution. What can you guarantee about $P(70 < X < 130)$?
step: Rewrite the interval as $|X - 100| < 30$. Since $\sigma = 10$, the half-width is $30 = 3\sigma$, so $k = 3$.
step: Chebyshev bounds the opposite event: $P(|X - 100| \ge 30) \le \frac{1}{3^2} = \frac{1}{9}$.
step: Take the complement: $P(70 < X < 130) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{9} = \frac{8}{9} \approx 0.889$.
step: For comparison, a normal variable would have probability about $0.997$ here. Chebyshev is far weaker, but it holds for every distribution with this mean and standard deviation.

@example
title: Normal approximation with a continuity correction
problem: A fair coin is flipped $100$ times. Approximate the probability of at least $60$ heads.
step: The number of heads is $S \sim \text{Binomial}(100, \tfrac{1}{2})$ with mean $np = 50$ and variance $np(1-p) = 25$, so $\sigma_S = 5$. By the central limit theorem, $S$ is approximately Normal$(50, 25)$.
step: Without a correction, $P(S \ge 60) \approx 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{60 - 50}{5}\right) = 1 - \Phi(2) \approx 0.0228$.
step: With the continuity correction, "at least $60$" starts at $59.5$: $z = \frac{59.5 - 50}{5} = 1.9$, so $P(S \ge 60) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.9) \approx 0.0287$.
step: The exact binomial tail is about $0.0284$, so the corrected approximation is far closer than the uncorrected one.

@mistakes
- Reading the law of large numbers as a "law of averages" that corrects earlier results. After a run of tails the next flip is still $50/50$; the deviation is diluted by new flips, not canceled.
- Forgetting to divide by $\sqrt{n}$: the standard deviation of the sample mean is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, not $\sigma$.
- Skipping the continuity correction when approximating a probability for an integer-valued sum.
- Applying the central limit theorem to a single observation, or to a tiny sample from a very skewed or heavy-tailed population.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement correctly describes the law of large numbers for iid variables with finite variance?
choice*: The sample mean is within any fixed distance of $\mu$ with probability approaching $1$ as $n$ grows.
choice: After a long run of tails, heads becomes more likely so that the frequencies balance out.
choice: The distribution of each individual $X_i$ becomes normal as $n$ grows.
choice: The difference $S_n - n\mu$ between the sum and its expected value shrinks to $0$.
hint: Ask what converges: the average, the individual observations, or the sum.
solution: The law of large numbers is about the average $\bar X_n$ concentrating at $\mu$. Future trials do not compensate for past results, individual observations keep their original distribution, and $S_n - n\mu$ typically grows like $\sqrt{n}$ even though the average error $(S_n - n\mu)/n$ shrinks.

@quiz num
prompt: Bulb lifetimes are independent with mean $1000$ hours and standard deviation $200$ hours. For a batch of $n = 64$ bulbs, approximate the probability that the average lifetime exceeds $1040$ hours. Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0548
check: quad(lambda z: exp(-z * z / 2) / sqrt(2 * pi), (1040 - 1000) / (200 / sqrt(64)), 12)

hint: The sample mean has standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$, and by the central limit theorem it is approximately normal.

solution: The standard deviation of the average is $\frac{200}{8} = 25$, so $z = \frac{40}{25} = 1.6$ and $P \approx 1 - \Phi(1.6) \approx 0.0548$.
