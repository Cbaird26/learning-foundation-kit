@article
Real surfaces resist sliding, and real objects often travel in circles. Both problems are solved the same way as in week 3: draw the free-body diagram, choose axes, apply $\sum \vec F = m\vec a$.

### Friction

Friction is a contact force parallel to the surface. It opposes sliding (**kinetic**) or the tendency to slide (**static**). The standard model is

$$f_k = \mu_k N, \qquad f_s \le \mu_s N.$$

Kinetic friction has a fixed size while the object slides. Static friction is different: it adjusts to whatever value keeps the object from slipping, up to the maximum $\mu_s N$. Usually $\mu_s > \mu_k$. These are empirical rules accurate to perhaps tens of percent, not exact laws.

A box on a floor pushed with $10$ N while the maximum static friction is $24.5$ N feels a friction force of exactly $10$ N, not $24.5$ N.

### Slopes with friction

For an object sliding down an incline at angle $\theta$, with $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicular to it, $N = mg\cos\theta$ and

$$ma = mg\sin\theta - \mu_k mg\cos\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).$$

An object sliding at constant speed has $a = 0$, so $\mu_k = \tan\theta$. An object just about to slip has $\mu_s = \tan\theta$.

### Uniform circular motion

When speed is constant but the direction changes, the acceleration points toward the centre of the circle:

$$a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r, \qquad \omega = \frac{v}{r}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v}.$$

Here $\omega$ is the angular speed in rad/s (one revolution is $2\pi$ rad). Newton's second law then says the **net inward force** is $mv^2/r$. "Centripetal force" is a label for that net force, not a new force. Never draw it as an extra arrow; instead ask which real forces point inward.

### What supplies the inward force

- A car on a flat curve: static friction. The limit is $\mu_s mg = mv^2/r$, so $v_{\max} = \sqrt{\mu_s g r}$.
- A ball whirled on a string: the tension.
- A vertical circle, at the bottom: $T - mg = mv^2/r$. At the top: $T + mg = mv^2/r$. The string goes slack when $T = 0$, so the slowest speed at the top is $v = \sqrt{gr}$.

```python
import math
mu, g, r = 0.50, 9.8, 40.0
print(math.sqrt(mu * g * r))   # 14.0 (m/s)
```

@example
title: Finding the friction coefficient of a ramp
problem: A crate slides down a ramp inclined at $20^\circ$ at constant speed. Find $\mu_k$.
step: Draw the crate with weight $mg$ straight down, the normal force $N$ perpendicular to the ramp, and kinetic friction $f_k$ up the slope, opposing the sliding.
step: Perpendicular to the ramp there is no acceleration: $N = mg\cos20^\circ$.
step: Along the ramp the speed is constant, so the net force is zero: $mg\sin20^\circ - \mu_k N = 0$.
step: Substitute $N$: $mg\sin20^\circ = \mu_k mg\cos20^\circ$. The mass cancels, so $\mu_k = \tan20^\circ \approx 0.36$.

@example
title: The fastest safe speed around a flat curve
problem: A car rounds an unbanked curve of radius $40$ m. The coefficient of static friction between tyres and road is $0.50$. What is the greatest speed at which it does not slide? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Draw the car from behind: weight down, normal force up, and static friction pointing toward the centre of the curve. Friction is the only inward force.
step: Vertical: $N = mg$. Inward: $f_s = mv^2/r$ with $f_s \le \mu_s N = \mu_s mg$.
step: At the limit, $\mu_s mg = mv^2/r$, so $v^2 = \mu_s g r = 0.50(9.8)(40) = 196$.
step: Therefore $v_{\max} = 14$ m/s, about $50$ km/h. The mass cancelled, so a truck and a bicycle have the same limit on the same road.

@mistakes
- Using $f = \mu N$ for static friction when the object is not about to slip; static friction only equals $\mu_s N$ at the limit.
- Adding a "centripetal force" arrow to the diagram in addition to tension, friction or gravity.
- Using $N = mg$ on a slope or when a rope pulls at an angle; always find $N$ from the perpendicular balance.
- Using revolutions per minute directly in $\omega^2 r$ without converting to rad/s.

answer: 58.8
check: 0.30*20*9.8
unit: N
hint: On a level floor with no vertical acceleration, the normal force equals the weight.

solution: $N = 20 \times 9.8 = 196$ N, so $f_k = 0.30 \times 196 = 58.8$ N.

@practice num
prompt: A point on a wheel of radius $0.50$ m turns with the wheel at $120$ revolutions per minute. Find its centripetal acceleration. Round to three significant figures.
answer: 79.0
check: (120*2*pi/60)**2*0.50
unit: m/s^2
tol: 1%
hint: Convert to rad/s first: one revolution is $2\pi$ rad and one minute is $60$ s.
hint: $\omega = 4\pi\ \text{rad/s}$. Then use $a_c = \omega^2 r$.
solution: $\omega = 120 \times 2\pi/60 = 4\pi \approx 12.57$ rad/s, so $a_c = \omega^2 r = (12.57)^2(0.50) \approx 79.0\ \text{m/s}^2$.

@practice self
prompt: A box of mass $m$ is pulled at constant velocity across a rough level floor by a rope that makes angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. The kinetic friction coefficient is $\mu_k$. Draw the free-body diagram first, then derive the rope tension $T$ in terms of $m$, $g$, $\mu_k$ and $\theta$.
hint: Four forces act on the box: weight, normal force, kinetic friction and the tension. Split the tension into horizontal and vertical components.
hint: The vertical equation gives $N$ in terms of $T$; substitute it into the horizontal equation and solve for $T$.
solution: Forces on the box: weight $mg$ down, normal force $N$ up, kinetic friction $\mu_k N$ opposite to the motion (horizontal), and tension $T$ along the rope at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. The acceleration is zero. Horizontal: $T\cos\theta - \mu_k N = 0$. Vertical: $N + T\sin\theta - mg = 0$, so $N = mg - T\sin\theta$. Substitute: $T\cos\theta = \mu_k(mg - T\sin\theta)$, which gives $T = \dfrac{\mu_k mg}{\cos\theta + \mu_k\sin\theta}$.
rubric: The diagram has exactly four forces, each with a physical source, and friction opposes the motion.
rubric: The normal force is written as $mg - T\sin\theta$, not $mg$, because the rope lifts the box partly.
rubric: Horizontal and vertical equations are both written with net force zero before they are combined.
rubric: The final expression is $T = \mu_k mg/(\cos\theta + \mu_k\sin\theta)$, and for $\theta = 0$ it reduces to $\mu_k mg$.

@quiz num
prompt: A car rounds a flat curve of radius $100$ m on a road where $\mu_s = 0.45$. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the greatest speed at which it can take the curve without sliding?
answer: 21
check: root(lambda v: 0.45*9.8*100 - v**2, 1, 100)
unit: m/s
hint: Which force points toward the centre of the curve?
hint: At the limit, $\mu_s mg = mv^2/r$.
solution: $v^2 = \mu_s g r = 0.45(9.8)(100) = 441$, so $v = 21$ m/s.

@quiz num
prompt: A $0.50$ kg ball is whirled in a vertical circle on a $1.2$ m string. At the bottom of the circle its speed is $6.0$ m/s. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. What is the tension in the string at the bottom?
answer: 19.9
check: root(lambda T: T - 0.5*9.8 - 0.5*6.0**2/1.2, 0, 100)
unit: N
hint: At the bottom, tension points up toward the centre and weight points down, away from it.
hint: Net inward force: $T - mg = mv^2/r$.
solution: $T = m(g + v^2/r) = 0.50(9.8 + 36/1.2) = 0.50(9.8 + 30) = 19.9$ N.
