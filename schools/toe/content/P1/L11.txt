@article
A number without an uncertainty is not yet a measurement. This lesson covers the three tools you need to turn raw readings into a result you can defend: statistics of repeated readings, propagation of uncertainty, and fitting.

### Two kinds of error

**Random errors** scatter unpredictably from one reading to the next, for example your reaction time on a stopwatch. Averaging reduces them. **Systematic errors** shift every reading the same way, for example a clock that runs 2% fast, a ruler whose end is worn, or air resistance on a falling ball. Averaging does **not** reduce them. You find them by calibration and by comparing independent methods.

### Repeated measurements

For $N$ readings $x_1, \dots, x_N$:

$$\bar x = \frac1N\sum x_i, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}, \qquad \delta\bar x = \frac{s}{\sqrt N}.$$

The sample standard deviation $s$ describes the spread of a single reading. The **standard error** $\delta\bar x = s/\sqrt N$ is the uncertainty of the mean, and you report $\bar x \pm \delta\bar x$. To halve the standard error you need four times as many readings, and no number of readings removes a systematic error.

### Propagation of uncertainty

If $f$ depends on independent measurements $x$ and $y$ with uncertainties $\delta x$ and $\delta y$,

$$\delta f = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\,\delta x\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\,\delta y\right)^2}.$$

Three special cases cover most of the lab:

- Sums and differences: the absolute uncertainties add in quadrature.
- Products and quotients: the **relative** uncertainties add in quadrature.
- A power $f = x^n$: $\delta f/|f| = |n|\,\delta x/|x|$.

For the pendulum, $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$ gives $(\delta g/g)^2 = (\delta L/L)^2 + (2\,\delta T/T)^2$. The squared period doubles the weight of the time error.

### Least-squares line fit

To fit $y = a + bx$, choose $a$ and $b$ to minimise $\sum (y_i - a - bx_i)^2$. The result is

$$b = \frac{\sum (x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y)}{\sum (x_i - \bar x)^2}, \qquad a = \bar y - b\bar x.$$

In Python, `np.polyfit(x, y, 1)` returns `[b, a]`.

```python
import numpy as np
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4])
y = np.array([2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8])
b, a = np.polyfit(x, y, 1)
print(round(b, 2), round(a, 2))    # 1.94 0.15
```

### Linearise the model

Straight lines are easy to judge by eye. Rearrange a model into a straight line: a pendulum gives $T^2 = (4\pi^2/g)L$, so $T^2$ against $L$ has slope $4\pi^2/g$; a fall from rest gives $d = \tfrac12 gt^2$, so $d$ against $t^2$ has slope $g/2$. A fit then returns the physical constant directly, and next week we judge how well the line describes the data.

@example
title: The mean and its standard error
problem: Five timings of the same cart run are $1.52$, $1.48$, $1.51$, $1.55$ and $1.49$ s. Report the mean with its standard error.
step: The mean is $\bar t = (1.52 + 1.48 + 1.51 + 1.55 + 1.49)/5 = 7.55/5 = 1.51$ s.
step: Deviations from the mean: $0.01, -0.03, 0, 0.04, -0.02$. Their squares sum to $0.0001 + 0.0009 + 0 + 0.0016 + 0.0004 = 0.0030$.
step: Sample standard deviation: $s = \sqrt{0.0030/(5-1)} = \sqrt{0.00075} \approx 0.027$ s.
step: Standard error: $s/\sqrt5 \approx 0.0122$ s. Report $t = 1.510 \pm 0.012$ s.

@example
title: Uncertainty in g from a pendulum
problem: A pendulum has $L = 1.000 \pm 0.002$ m and period $T = 2.007 \pm 0.004$ s. Find $g = 4\pi^2L/T^2$ with its uncertainty.
step: Value: $g = 4\pi^2(1.000)/(2.007)^2 = 39.478/4.028 \approx 9.80\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Relative uncertainties: $\delta L/L = 0.002$ and $\delta T/T = 0.004/2.007 \approx 0.0020$. The period enters squared, so its contribution is $2 \times 0.0020 = 0.0040$.
step: Combine in quadrature: $\delta g/g = \sqrt{0.002^2 + 0.0040^2} \approx 0.0045$.
step: $\delta g \approx 0.0045 \times 9.80 \approx 0.04$, so $g = 9.80 \pm 0.04\ \text{m/s}^2$. The period measurement dominates, so improving it is the best use of effort.

@mistakes
- Using $N$ instead of $N-1$ in the sample standard deviation of a small data set.
- Reporting the standard deviation as the uncertainty of the mean, or quoting more digits than the uncertainty supports.
- Adding uncertainties linearly or ignoring the power in $T^2$ when propagating through a formula.
- Believing that taking more readings cures a systematic error such as a clock that runs fast.

@practice num
prompt: Five readings are $4.0$, $5.0$, $6.0$, $7.0$ and $8.0$. What is the sample standard deviation (using $N-1$)? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 1.58
check: np.std([4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 7.0, 8.0], ddof=1)
tol: 1%
hint: The mean is $6.0$. Find each deviation from it.
hint: The squared deviations are $4, 1, 0, 1, 4$. Divide their sum by $N - 1 = 4$ before taking the root.
solution: The squared deviations sum to $10$. So $s = \sqrt{10/4} = \sqrt{2.5} \approx 1.58$.

@practice num
prompt: A cart travels a distance $d = 1.20 \pm 0.01$ m in a time $t = 2.00 \pm 0.02$ s. Find the uncertainty in its speed $v = d/t$. Round to two significant figures.
answer: 0.0078
check: (1.20/2.00)*sqrt((0.01/1.20)**2 + (0.02/2.00)**2)
unit: m/s
tol: 2%
hint: For a quotient, add the relative uncertainties in quadrature.
hint: $\delta v/v = \sqrt{(0.01/1.20)^2 + (0.02/2.00)^2} \approx 0.013$, and $v = 0.60$ m/s.
solution: $\delta v/v = \sqrt{(0.00833)^2 + (0.0100)^2} \approx 0.0130$, so $\delta v = 0.60 \times 0.0130 \approx 0.0078$ m/s.

@practice num
prompt: Fit a straight line $y = a + bx$ by least squares to the points $(1, 2.1)$, $(2, 3.9)$, $(3, 6.2)$ and $(4, 7.8)$. What is the slope $b$?
answer: 1.94
check: np.polyfit([1, 2, 3, 4], [2.1, 3.9, 6.2, 7.8], 1)[0]
tol: 0.01
hint: The means are $\bar x = 2.5$ and $\bar y = 5.0$.
hint: $\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 = 5$ and $\sum(x_i - \bar x)(y_i - \bar y) = 9.7$.
solution: $b = 9.7/5 = 1.94$, and the intercept is $a = 5.0 - 1.94(2.5) = 0.15$.

@quiz num
prompt: A student makes $25$ timings of a rolling cart. The sample standard deviation is $s = 0.10$ s. What is the standard error of the mean?
answer: 0.02
check: 0.10/sqrt(25)
unit: s
hint: The standard error is smaller than $s$ by a factor that depends on the number of readings.
solution: $\delta\bar t = s/\sqrt N = 0.10/\sqrt{25} = 0.020$ s.

@quiz num
prompt: A ball dropped from rest falls a distance $d = 1.000 \pm 0.002$ m in a time $t = 0.452 \pm 0.004$ s. The value $g = 2d/t^2$ is computed from these. What is the relative uncertainty of $g$, as a percentage? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 1.78
check: 100*sqrt((0.002/1.000)**2 + (2*0.004/0.452)**2)
unit: %
tol: 2%
hint: $g$ is a quotient with $t$ squared in the denominator, so the time contribution doubles.
hint: Add $(\delta d/d)^2$ and $(2\,\delta t/t)^2$, then take the square root.
solution: $\delta d/d = 0.0020$ and $2\,\delta t/t = 2(0.004/0.452) = 0.0177$. In quadrature, $\sqrt{0.0020^2 + 0.0177^2} \approx 0.0178$, which is $1.78\%$.
