@article
A fitted line always passes near the data, so a good-looking fit proves very little. The **residuals**, what the model fails to explain, show whether the model is right.

### Residuals

The residual of point $i$ is observed minus predicted:

$$r_i = y_i - f(x_i).$$

A positive residual means the model under-predicts. For an adequate model the residuals look like noise: scattered above and below zero, with no trend, and with a size comparable to the measurement uncertainty. Always plot residuals against $x$.

### Reading the pattern

- **Random scatter about zero**, roughly the size of the uncertainties: the model is adequate.
- **A curve** (a smile, a frown, or an S): the model is missing curvature.
- **A steady drift** or an offset: a wrong slope or a wrong intercept in the model (for example a line forced through the origin), or a systematic error that changed during the run, such as a timer error that grew from one reading to the next. A constant timing offset is a different case. For a distance $d$ covered under constant acceleration $a$, a fit of $c_0 + c_1t + c_2t^2$ absorbs the offset into $c_0$ and $c_1$ and leaves $a = 2c_2$ unbiased, whereas a straight-line fit of $d$ against $t^2$ shows it as a smile or frown.
- **A funnel** (spread growing with $x$): the uncertainty is not constant, so a weighted fit or relative uncertainties are appropriate.
- **One large residual**: possibly a reading mistake. Investigate it and record what you find; do not quietly delete a point because it spoils the fit.

### Chi-square

If each reading has uncertainty $\sigma_i$, define

$$\chi^2 = \sum_i \left(\frac{r_i}{\sigma_i}\right)^2, \qquad \nu = N - p, \qquad \chi^2_\nu = \frac{\chi^2}{\nu},$$

where $p$ is the number of fitted parameters (two for a line) and $\nu$ is the number of degrees of freedom. A reduced chi-square near $1$ means the model and the stated uncertainties are consistent. A value far above $1$ means the model is incomplete or the uncertainties are underestimated. A value far below $1$ suggests the uncertainties are overestimated. With few degrees of freedom the value scatters by about $\sqrt{2/\nu}$, so do not over-interpret small departures.

### Linearising shows the pattern

A ball falls from rest and its distance is recorded at four times. These supplied data follow $d = 4.9t^2$ exactly: $(0.2, 0.196)$, $(0.4, 0.784)$, $(0.6, 1.764)$, $(0.8, 3.136)$.

```python
import numpy as np
t = np.array([0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8])
d = np.array([0.196, 0.784, 1.764, 3.136])
b, a = np.polyfit(t, d, 1)               # a straight line in t
print(np.round(d - (a + b * t), 3))      # [ 0.196 -0.196 -0.196  0.196]
```

The straight-line residuals form a smile, which exposes the missing curvature. Fitting $d$ against $t^2$ instead gives residuals of zero and a slope of $4.9 = g/2$.

### Planning a safe experiment

Good low-risk choices are a cart or ball on a gentle, low ramp, or a pendulum with a small weight, timed by phone video with a ruler in view. Record position or time at several equally spaced marks, repeat each timing three to five times, estimate the uncertainties, fit a linearised model, and plot the residuals. Keep ramps low, with a clear floor and a stopper at the end, and keep people out of the path.

@example
title: Residuals reveal the wrong model
problem: Fit a straight line to the falling-ball data above. The least-squares line is $\hat d = 4.9t - 0.98$. Compute the residuals and decide whether a straight line is an adequate model.
step: Predictions at $t = 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8$: $\hat d = 0.00,\ 0.98,\ 1.96,\ 2.94$ m.
step: Residuals (observed minus predicted): $0.196,\ -0.196,\ -0.196,\ +0.196$ m.
step: The signs go $+,-,-,+$: a smile. That is systematic curvature, not noise, so a straight line in $t$ is not adequate even though the fitted line passes near all the points.
step: Linearise: plot $d$ against $t^2$. Then $d = 4.9\,t^2$ fits with zero residuals, and the slope gives $g = 2 \times 4.9 = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

@example
title: Is the chi-square acceptable?
problem: A line is fitted to four points. The residuals are $0.3$, $-0.2$, $0.1$ and $-0.4$ (in the units of $y$), and each reading has uncertainty $\sigma = 0.2$. Compute the reduced chi-square and interpret it.
step: Scale each residual: $r/\sigma = 1.5,\ -1.0,\ 0.5,\ -2.0$.
step: Chi-square: $\chi^2 = 2.25 + 1.0 + 0.25 + 4.0 = 7.5$.
step: A line has $p = 2$ parameters, so $\nu = 4 - 2 = 2$ and $\chi^2_\nu = 7.5/2 = 3.75$.
step: This is well above $1$. Either the model is incomplete, or the stated uncertainties of $0.2$ are too small. Check the residual plot and the uncertainty estimates before trusting the fit.

@mistakes
- Judging a fit only by how close the line looks to the points, without plotting the residuals.
- Using the number of points $N$ rather than $N-p$ when computing the reduced chi-square.
- Deleting the point with the largest residual to improve the fit without finding out why it is off.
- Treating a reduced chi-square that is far below $1$ as a sign of an excellent experiment; it usually means the uncertainties were overestimated.

@quiz num
prompt: A straight-line fit to $N = 6$ points has $\chi^2 = 10.0$. What is the reduced chi-square?
answer: 2.5
check: 10.0/(6 - 2)
hint: A line has two fitted parameters. How many degrees of freedom are left?
solution: $\nu = 6 - 2 = 4$, so $\chi^2_\nu = 10.0/4 = 2.5$. That suggests the model is incomplete or the uncertainties are too small.
