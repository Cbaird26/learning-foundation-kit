@article
**Momentum** is the quantity of motion an object carries: $\vec p = m\vec v$. It is a vector, so direction matters. Newton's second law is more generally written

$$\vec F_{\text{net}} = \frac{d\vec p}{dt}.$$

### Impulse

The **impulse** of a force is $\vec J = \int \vec F\,dt$, and integrating the second law shows $\vec J = \Delta\vec p$. The average force is $F_{\text{avg}} = \Delta p/\Delta t$, so stretching the stopping time reduces the force for the same change of momentum. That is the reason for airbags, crumple zones and bent knees on landing.

### Conservation of momentum

Inside a system, forces come in equal and opposite pairs (the third law), so their impulses cancel. If no net **external** force acts, the total momentum is constant:

$$m_1\vec v_{1i} + m_2\vec v_{2i} = m_1\vec v_{1f} + m_2\vec v_{2f}.$$

During a collision the contact forces are huge compared with friction or gravity, so for the short collision time the system behaves as isolated. This is a vector equation: write one for each component and give velocities signs.

### Types of collision

| Collision | Momentum | Kinetic energy | Total energy |
|---|---|---|---|
| elastic | conserved | conserved | conserved |
| inelastic | conserved | decreases | conserved |
| perfectly inelastic (stick together) | conserved | maximum loss | conserved |

In a **perfectly inelastic** collision the objects share one final velocity:

$$v_f = \frac{m_1v_1 + m_2v_2}{m_1 + m_2}.$$

In a one-dimensional **elastic** collision, the relative velocity reverses: $v_{2f} - v_{1f} = -(v_{2i} - v_{1i})$. Combined with momentum conservation this gives, for a target initially at rest,

$$v_{1f} = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1i}, \qquad v_{2f} = \frac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}\,v_{1i}.$$

The **coefficient of restitution** $e = -\dfrac{v_{2f} - v_{1f}}{v_{2i} - v_{1i}}$ equals $1$ for an elastic collision and $0$ for a perfectly inelastic one.

### Center of mass

The center of mass is at $x_{\text{cm}} = \sum m_i x_i / \sum m_i$, and the total momentum is $M v_{\text{cm}}$. Internal forces cannot change $v_{\text{cm}}$.

### Comparing conserved quantities

For every collision problem, compute the total momentum and the total kinetic energy before and after. Momentum must match; the kinetic-energy ratio $K_f/K_i$ tells you which kind of collision it was and catches algebra errors. In an air-track experiment these comparisons are exactly your uncertainty test: do the two totals agree within the measurement errors?

```python
def elastic(m1, v1, m2, v2):
    v1f = ((m1 - m2) * v1 + 2 * m2 * v2) / (m1 + m2)
    v2f = ((m2 - m1) * v2 + 2 * m1 * v1) / (m1 + m2)
    return v1f, v2f

print(elastic(1.0, 4.0, 3.0, 0.0))   # (-2.0, 2.0)
```

@example
title: Two carts that stick together
problem: A $2.0$ kg cart moving at $3.0$ m/s hits a $1.0$ kg cart at rest on a frictionless track, and the two couple together. Find the final velocity and the kinetic energy lost.
step: The track is frictionless, so momentum is conserved: $2.0(3.0) + 1.0(0) = (2.0 + 1.0)v_f$.
step: $6.0 = 3.0v_f$, so $v_f = 2.0$ m/s in the original direction.
step: Kinetic energy before: $\tfrac12(2.0)(3.0)^2 = 9.0$ J. After: $\tfrac12(3.0)(2.0)^2 = 6.0$ J.
step: The loss is $3.0$ J, one third of the original. It went into deformation, sound and heat. The total energy is conserved, but not the kinetic energy.

@example
title: An elastic collision, checked two ways
problem: The same $2.0$ kg cart at $3.0$ m/s now hits the $1.0$ kg cart at rest with spring bumpers, so the collision is elastic. Find both final velocities and compare the conserved quantities.
step: Use the elastic formulas for a target at rest: $v_{1f} = \dfrac{2.0 - 1.0}{3.0}(3.0) = 1.0$ m/s.
step: $v_{2f} = \dfrac{2(2.0)}{3.0}(3.0) = 4.0$ m/s.
step: Momentum: before $2.0(3.0) = 6.0$ kg m/s; after $2.0(1.0) + 1.0(4.0) = 6.0$ kg m/s. They match.
step: Kinetic energy: before $9.0$ J; after $\tfrac12(2.0)(1.0)^2 + \tfrac12(1.0)(4.0)^2 = 1.0 + 8.0 = 9.0$ J. They also match, as an elastic collision requires.

@mistakes
- Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in every collision. It is conserved only in elastic ones.
- Forgetting that momentum is a vector, and dropping the negative sign for a velocity pointing the other way.
- Applying momentum conservation over a long time interval during which friction or another external force acts.
- Believing that objects which stick together have lost momentum; their combined momentum is the same as before.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.15$ kg ball hits a wall moving at $20$ m/s and rebounds at $15$ m/s in the opposite direction. What is the magnitude of the impulse the wall exerts on the ball?
answer: 5.25
check: 0.15*(15 - (-20))
unit: N s
hint: Take the initial direction as positive; the final velocity is then negative.
hint: $J = m(v_f - v_i)$ with $v_i = +20$ and $v_f = -15$.
solution: $J = 0.15(-15 - 20) = -5.25$ N s. The magnitude is $5.25$ N s, directed away from the wall.

@practice num
prompt: In a ballistic pendulum, a $0.010$ kg bullet embeds itself in a $1.99$ kg block hanging from light strings. The block and bullet swing up together to a height of $0.20$ m. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. What was the bullet's speed? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 396
check: (0.010 + 1.99)/0.010 * root(lambda u: 0.5*u**2 - 9.8*0.20, 0.1, 10)
unit: m/s
tol: 1%
hint: Split the problem into two stages: the collision (conserve momentum) and the swing (conserve mechanical energy). Which conserved quantity applies to which stage?
hint: Swing: $\tfrac12 u^2 = gh$ gives the speed $u$ just after impact. Collision: $mv = (m + M)u$.
solution: Swing: $u = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{3.92} \approx 1.980$ m/s. Collision: $v = \dfrac{m + M}{m}u = \dfrac{2.00}{0.010}(1.980) \approx 396$ m/s. Kinetic energy is not conserved in the collision, so energy cannot be used there.

@quiz num
prompt: A $60$ kg skater at rest on frictionless ice throws a $3.0$ kg ball horizontally at $4.0$ m/s relative to the ice. The $60$ kg does not include the ball. What is the skater's recoil speed?
answer: 0.2
check: 3.0*4.0/60
unit: m/s
hint: The system of skater plus ball starts at rest, so its total momentum is zero.
hint: $0 = (3.0)(4.0) + (60)v$.
solution: $0 = 3.0(4.0) + 60v$, so $v = -0.20$ m/s. The skater recoils at $0.20$ m/s in the opposite direction.

@quiz num
prompt: A ball dropped from $1.00$ m onto a hard floor rebounds to a height of $0.64$ m. What is the coefficient of restitution of the collision?
answer: 0.8
check: sqrt(0.64/1.00)
hint: The coefficient of restitution is the ratio of rebound speed to impact speed for a fixed floor.
hint: Use $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ for the speed just before and just after the bounce.
solution: Speed before impact $\sqrt{2g(1.00)}$, after $\sqrt{2g(0.64)}$. Their ratio is $e = \sqrt{0.64/1.00} = 0.80$.
