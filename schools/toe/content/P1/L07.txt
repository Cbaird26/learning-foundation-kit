@article
Rotation about a fixed axis mirrors linear motion, with each linear quantity replaced by an angular one. Once you see the dictionary, you already know most of the equations.

### Angular quantities

Measure angle $\theta$ in **radians**, so that an arc of radius $r$ has length $s = r\theta$ and one revolution is $2\pi$ rad. Then $\omega = d\theta/dt$ and $\alpha = d\omega/dt$. For constant $\alpha$,

$$\omega = \omega_0 + \alpha t, \qquad \theta = \theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \tfrac12\alpha t^2, \qquad \omega^2 = \omega_0^2 + 2\alpha(\theta - \theta_0).$$

A point at distance $r$ from the axis has speed $v = \omega r$, tangential acceleration $a_t = \alpha r$ and centripetal acceleration $a_c = \omega^2 r$. To convert, multiply revolutions per minute by $2\pi/60$ to get rad/s.

### Moment of inertia

A spinning body has kinetic energy $\sum \tfrac12 m_i v_i^2 = \tfrac12\left(\sum m_i r_i^2\right)\omega^2$. Define the **moment of inertia**

$$I = \sum m_i r_i^2 = \int r^2\,dm, \qquad K_{\text{rot}} = \tfrac12 I\omega^2.$$

It depends on the axis, and mass far from the axis counts for much more than mass near it. Standard values for an axis through the center:

| Object (mass $M$) | $I$ |
|---|---|
| thin hoop or thin cylindrical shell, radius $R$, about its axis | $MR^2$ |
| solid disk or cylinder, radius $R$ | $\tfrac12 MR^2$ |
| solid sphere, radius $R$ | $\tfrac25 MR^2$ |
| thin rod, length $L$, axis through center, perpendicular | $\tfrac1{12}ML^2$ |
| thin rod, length $L$, axis through one end | $\tfrac13 ML^2$ |

The **parallel-axis theorem** moves the axis a distance $d$ from the center of mass: $I = I_{\text{cm}} + Md^2$. For the rod, $\tfrac1{12}ML^2 + M(L/2)^2 = \tfrac13 ML^2$.

### Rolling without slipping

The point touching the ground is momentarily at rest, so $v_{\text{cm}} = \omega R$. The kinetic energy has a translation part and a rotation part:

$$K = \tfrac12 Mv_{\text{cm}}^2 + \tfrac12 I_{\text{cm}}\omega^2.$$

Write $I_{\text{cm}} = \beta MR^2$ (so $\beta = 1$ for a hoop, $\tfrac12$ for a disk, $\tfrac25$ for a sphere). Then $K = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1 + \beta)$. Rolling from rest down a height $h$ with no losses, $Mgh = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1 + \beta)$, so

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2gh}{1 + \beta}}.$$

The mass and radius cancel. The object with the smallest $\beta$ wins a rolling race, because less of its energy is tied up in spinning.

```python
import math
g, h = 9.8, 1.0
for name, beta in [("sphere", 0.4), ("disk", 0.5), ("hoop", 1.0)]:
    print(name, round(math.sqrt(2 * g * h / (1 + beta)), 2))
# sphere 3.74, disk 3.61, hoop 3.13
```

@example
title: A spinning dumbbell
problem: Two $0.50$ kg masses are fixed at the ends of a light rod $1.0$ m long. The rod spins about its center at $6.0$ rad/s. Find the moment of inertia, the kinetic energy, and the speed of each mass.
step: Each mass is $0.50$ m from the axis. The rod's mass is negligible, so $I = \sum mr^2 = 2(0.50)(0.50)^2 = 0.25\ \text{kg m}^2$.
step: Rotational kinetic energy: $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12(0.25)(6.0)^2 = 4.5$ J.
step: Speed of each mass: $v = \omega r = 6.0(0.50) = 3.0$ m/s.
step: Check by adding the individual kinetic energies: $2 \times \tfrac12(0.50)(3.0)^2 = 4.5$ J. The two methods agree.

@example
title: A solid cylinder rolling down a slope
problem: A solid cylinder is released from rest and rolls without slipping down a slope. Its center drops by $3.75$ m. How fast is its center moving at the bottom? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Rolling without slipping means static friction does no work at the contact point, so mechanical energy is conserved.
step: For a solid cylinder $I_{\text{cm}} = \tfrac12 MR^2$, so $\beta = \tfrac12$ and $K = \tfrac12 Mv^2(1 + \tfrac12) = \tfrac34 Mv^2$.
step: Energy conservation: $Mgh = \tfrac34 Mv^2$, so $v^2 = \tfrac43 gh = \tfrac43(9.8)(3.75) = 49$.
step: $v = 7.0$ m/s. A block sliding without friction would arrive at $\sqrt{2gh} \approx 8.6$ m/s, faster, because the cylinder spent part of the energy on spinning.

@mistakes
- Using degrees or revolutions in $v = \omega r$ or $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2$; these formulas require radians.
- Using the center-axis formula for $I$ when the axis does not pass through the center, instead of adding $Md^2$.
- Counting only the translational energy $\tfrac12 Mv^2$ for a rolling object.
- Assuming that heavier or larger objects roll faster; $M$ and $R$ cancel in the rolling-speed formula.
