@project
title: Measure a pendulum or rolling cart, fit a model and report the uncertainty
overview: You will take your own timing data (or the supplied data set if you cannot run the experiment) for either a simple pendulum or a ball or cylinder rolling down a gentle ramp. You will linearise the model, fit it, extract a physical constant such as $g$ or an acceleration with an honest uncertainty, and use the residuals to judge how well the model describes the data. The report ends with the limitations of the model and of your measurements.
goal: Estimate a physical quantity ($g$ from a pendulum, or the acceleration of rolling motion) with a defensible uncertainty.
goal: Fit a linearised model and use residuals and the reduced chi-square to judge it.
goal: Explain the limitations of the experiment, connecting them to the theory of weeks 2, 7, 10, 11 and 12.
step: Choose the system and write down its prediction first. Pendulum: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, so $T^2$ against $L$ is a line with slope $4\pi^2/g$. Rolling: $x = \tfrac12 at^2$ with $a = g\sin\theta/(1 + \beta)$ for rolling without slipping, so $x$ against $t^2$ is a line with slope $a/2$. Decide in advance what will be plotted.
step: Build the setup. Pendulum: a small dense weight on thin string, length measured from the pivot to the center of the weight, at least five lengths between $0.3$ m and $1.0$ m, amplitude under $10^\circ$. Ramp: a smooth board raised a few centimetres, a ball or cylinder, at least five marked distances. Estimate the instrument resolution of each tool.
step: Take data. Time ten full swings of the pendulum (or the run along the ramp) with a phone stopwatch or video, and repeat each setting at least five times. Record every raw reading in a table before doing any calculation.
step: Reduce the data. For each setting compute the mean and standard error, convert $10T$ to $T$, and propagate the uncertainty to the plotted quantity (for example $\delta(T^2) = 2T\,\delta T$).
step: Fit. Use `np.polyfit` (weighted if the uncertainties differ), obtain the slope with its uncertainty from the covariance matrix, and convert it to $g$ or $a$ with propagated uncertainty.
step: Plot the data with error bars and the fitted line, with the residuals plotted underneath. Compute $\chi^2$ and the reduced chi-square, and describe any pattern in the residuals.
step: Compare with the accepted value ($g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$) or the predicted acceleration. Is the difference within your uncertainty? List the most likely systematic errors (finite amplitude, string mass, release and timing delay, ramp angle, slipping) and estimate the size of each.
step: Write the report and prepare to explain your main result aloud, without notes, in about two minutes.
deliverable: A raw-data table with units, instrument resolutions and every individual reading, together with the analysis script or spreadsheet.
deliverable: A report of one to two pages: model, method, result with uncertainty, one figure with error bars and residuals, and a discussion of limitations.
deliverable: A spoken explanation, without notes, of one major result and why you trust it.
rubric: Raw data are complete and honest: units, resolution, repeated readings, and no point removed or changed without a recorded reason.
rubric: Uncertainties are propagated correctly (standard error, quadrature, the squared period) and the final result is quoted with a sensible number of digits.
rubric: The model is linearised correctly, the fit is performed correctly, and the slope is converted into $g$ or $a$ with an uncertainty.
rubric: Residuals are plotted and interpreted (pattern, reduced chi-square), and the conclusion follows from them rather than from how close the line looks to the points.
rubric: Limitations are specific and quantified where possible, for example the finite-amplitude correction of about $\theta_0^2/16$ in the period, or the factor $1 + \beta$ for rolling.
safety: Use only low-energy setups. Keep the ramp a few centimetres high with a stopper at the bottom and a clear floor. Use a weight of at most a couple of hundred grams on a pendulum, keep faces and fingers away from its path, and clamp the pivot to a table or stand. Never stand on furniture to hang a pendulum.
tutor: Here are my raw timings and their spread. Which readings look inconsistent, and how should I decide whether to keep them?
tutor: My residual plot shows a pattern. Which physical effects could produce it, and how could I test each one?

@review
### Key results and methods, week by week

- **Week 1, units and vectors.** SI units; check formulas by dimensions; quote $x \pm \delta x$; add vectors by components; $\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\phi$.
- **Week 2, kinematics.** $v = dx/dt$, $a = dv/dt$; for constant $a$ use $v = v_0 + at$, $x = x_0 + v_0t + \tfrac12at^2$, $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\Delta x$; projectile components are independent; second differences amplify noise.
- **Week 3, Newton's laws.** Free-body diagram first, then $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ per axis and per object; third-law partners act on different objects.
- **Week 4, friction and circles.** $f_k = \mu_kN$, $f_s \le \mu_sN$; $a_c = v^2/r = \omega^2r$ with the net inward force; no extra "centripetal" arrow.
- **Week 5, work and energy.** $W = \int\vec F\cdot d\vec r$; $W_{\text{net}} = \Delta K$; $U = mgh$ and $\tfrac12kx^2$; friction removes mechanical energy; $P = \vec F\cdot\vec v$.
- **Week 6, momentum.** $\vec J = \Delta\vec p$; momentum is conserved when no external force acts; kinetic energy only in elastic collisions.
- **Week 7, rotation.** $v = \omega r$; $I = \sum mr^2$ and the parallel-axis theorem; $K = \tfrac12I\omega^2$; rolling gives $v = \sqrt{2gh/(1+\beta)}$.
- **Week 8, torque and angular momentum.** $\tau = rF\sin\phi$; $\sum\tau = I\alpha$; equilibrium; $L = I\omega$ is conserved without external torque.
- **Week 9, gravitation.** $F = GMm/r^2$; $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$; $T^2 \propto r^3$; $U = -GMm/r$; $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/R}$.
- **Week 10, oscillations.** $\ddot x = -\omega^2x$; $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ or $\sqrt{g/L}$; $E = \tfrac12kA^2$; the period does not depend on amplitude.
- **Week 11, measurement and fitting.** Mean, $s$, $s/\sqrt N$; add relative uncertainties in quadrature; least-squares slope; linearise.
- **Week 12, residuals.** $r_i = y_i - f(x_i)$; patterns diagnose the model; $\chi^2_\nu = \chi^2/(N-p)$.

### Self-check

- Can you pick the right tool: Newton's second law for forces and times, energy for speeds at positions, momentum for collisions?
- Can you state what each conservation law requires: no non-conservative work, no external force, no external torque?
- Can you derive the periods of the spring and the pendulum from the force or torque?
- Can you propagate an uncertainty through a formula and justify each term?
- Can you read a residual plot and say what it implies?

### Most common errors

- Starting with equations before drawing the free-body diagram.
- Using conservation of kinetic energy in inelastic collisions, or ignoring friction in energy problems.
- Measuring gravitational distances from the surface instead of the center.
- Mixing degrees, revolutions and radians.
- Adding uncertainties linearly or forgetting the square in $T^2$.
- Judging a fit by the line alone, without checking the residuals.

@exam num
prompt: A $1.0$ kg block is released from rest on a $30^\circ$ incline and slides $2.0$ m down the slope, where $\mu_k = 0.20$. At the bottom it moves onto a frictionless horizontal surface and runs into a spring with $k = 200$ N/m. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. By how much does the spring compress at its maximum? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 0.253
check: root(lambda x: 0.5*200*x**2 - (1.0*9.8*sin(pi/6)*2.0 - 0.20*1.0*9.8*cos(pi/6)*2.0), 0.01, 2)
unit: m
tol: 1%
hint: Draw the free-body diagram on the slope first: weight, normal force and kinetic friction. The normal force is $mg\cos30^\circ$.
hint: Use the work-energy theorem on the slope: gravity does positive work and friction does negative work, so $K_{\text{bottom}} = mg\sin30^\circ\,d - \mu_kmg\cos30^\circ\,d$.
hint: On the frictionless surface the kinetic energy becomes spring energy at maximum compression: $\tfrac12kx^2 = K_{\text{bottom}}$.
solution: The normal force is $N = mg\cos30^\circ \approx 8.49$ N, so friction is $\mu_kN \approx 1.70$ N and its work over $2.0$ m is $-3.39$ J. Gravity does $mg\sin30^\circ(2.0) = 9.8$ J. The kinetic energy at the bottom is $9.8 - 3.39 = 6.41$ J. At maximum compression this is all stored in the spring: $\tfrac12(200)x^2 = 6.41$, so $x = \sqrt{6.41/100} \approx 0.253$ m.

@exam num
prompt: A uniform rod of mass $2.0$ kg and length $1.2$ m hangs at rest from a frictionless pivot at its upper end. A $0.10$ kg lump of clay, moving horizontally at $6.0$ m/s, strikes the lower end and sticks to it. Treat the clay as a point mass and take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Through what largest angle, in degrees, does the rod swing from the vertical? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 10.9
check: (lambda I: math.degrees(root(lambda phi: 0.5*I*((0.10*6.0*1.2)/I)**2 - (2.0*9.8*0.6 + 0.10*9.8*1.2)*(1 - cos(phi)), 0.01, 1.5)))(quad(lambda x: (2.0/1.2)*x**2, 0, 1.2) + 0.10*1.2**2)
unit: degrees
tol: 1%
hint: Split the problem into two stages. In the collision, which quantity about the pivot is conserved, and which is not?
hint: Angular momentum about the pivot is conserved in the collision: $mvL = I\omega$ with $I = \tfrac13ML^2 + mL^2$.
hint: After the collision, mechanical energy is conserved: $\tfrac12I\omega^2 = (MgL/2 + mgL)(1 - \cos\varphi)$.
solution: Collision: angular momentum about the pivot is conserved. $I = \tfrac13(2.0)(1.2)^2 + (0.10)(1.2)^2 = 0.96 + 0.144 = 1.104\ \text{kg m}^2$ and $mvL = 0.10(6.0)(1.2) = 0.72$, so $\omega = 0.72/1.104 \approx 0.652$ rad/s. The kinetic energy just after is $\tfrac12I\omega^2 \approx 0.235$ J (kinetic energy is not conserved in the collision). Swing: the center of the rod rises $\tfrac L2(1 - \cos\varphi)$ and the clay rises $L(1 - \cos\varphi)$, so $\tfrac12I\omega^2 = (2.0 \times 9.8 \times 0.6 + 0.10 \times 9.8 \times 1.2)(1 - \cos\varphi) = 12.94(1 - \cos\varphi)$. Then $1 - \cos\varphi \approx 0.01815$, so $\varphi \approx 10.9^\circ$.

@exam self
prompt: A solid cylinder of mass $M$ and radius $R$ rolls without slipping from rest down a ramp inclined at angle $\theta$. (a) Starting from a free-body diagram, derive its acceleration and show that $a = \tfrac23 g\sin\theta$. (b) A student times the cylinder over several marked distances $x$ and fits $x$ against $t^2$, obtaining a slope $s \pm \delta s$. Explain how to find $a$ and its uncertainty, how to use the residuals to test whether the acceleration is constant, and what a measured acceleration clearly below $\tfrac23 g\sin\theta$ could mean.
hint: Draw weight, normal force and static friction at the contact point. Write $F = ma$ down the slope and $\tau = I\alpha$ about the center, with $a = \alpha R$.
hint: For constant acceleration $x = \tfrac12at^2$, so the slope of $x$ against $t^2$ is $a/2$. Think about which residual patterns would show a changing acceleration or a timing offset.
solution: (a) Three forces act: weight $Mg$, the normal force, and static friction $f$ up the slope at the contact point. Down the slope, $Mg\sin\theta - f = Ma$. About the center, $fR = I\alpha = \tfrac12MR^2(a/R)$, so $f = \tfrac12Ma$. Substituting, $Mg\sin\theta = \tfrac32Ma$ and $a = \tfrac23g\sin\theta$. Energy conservation agrees: $v^2 = \tfrac43gh$. (b) Because $x = \tfrac12at^2$, the slope is $s = a/2$, so $a = 2s$ and $\delta a = 2\,\delta s$, with $\delta s$ taken from the covariance matrix of the fit (or weighted by the timing errors). Plot the residuals against $t^2$. Random scatter of the size of the error bars, with $\chi^2_\nu$ near $1$, supports constant acceleration; a smile or frown indicates a changing acceleration or a constant timing offset (which adds a term proportional to $t$ to $x$); a steady drift indicates a wrong slope or intercept in the model, or a systematic error that changed during the run; a funnel indicates timing error that grows with $t$. Fitting $x = c_0 + c_1t + c_2t^2$ instead absorbs a constant timing offset into $c_0$ and $c_1$ and leaves $a = 2c_2$ unbiased. A measured value clearly below $\tfrac23g\sin\theta$ (compared on the scale of $\delta a$ and of the uncertainty in $\sin\theta$) may come from an overestimated ramp angle, rolling resistance or air drag, or a body with more of its mass near the rim than a uniform solid cylinder (a larger $\beta$ gives $a = g\sin\theta/(1 + \beta)$).
rubric: The free-body diagram has weight, normal force and static (not kinetic) friction, with no extra forces.
rubric: Uses both $F = ma$ and $\tau = I\alpha$ with $a = \alpha R$ and obtains $a = \tfrac23g\sin\theta$ (or gives an equivalent energy derivation).
rubric: Converts the slope to $a = 2s$ and propagates $\delta s$ to $\delta a$, noting the uncertainty from the angle measurement.
rubric: Interprets residual patterns correctly and offers at least two plausible explanations for a low measured acceleration, separating systematic from random effects.
