@article
Newton's second law tells you the acceleration at each instant. Energy methods skip the details of the path and the time, and connect speeds directly to positions. That makes them the fastest route to many answers.

### Work

For a constant force and a straight displacement $\vec d$,

$$W = \vec F\cdot\vec d = Fd\cos\theta.$$

The unit is the joule, $1\ \text{J} = 1\ \text{N m}$. Work is positive when the force has a component along the displacement, negative when it opposes it, and **zero when the force is perpendicular** to the motion. That is why a normal force on a sliding block, or the tension on a ball moving in a circle, does no work. If the force varies along a line, add up small pieces:

$$W = \int_{x_1}^{x_2} F_x\,dx,$$

which is the area under the $F_x$–$x$ graph.

### The work-energy theorem

Define the kinetic energy $K = \tfrac12 mv^2$. Writing $F = m\,dv/dt$ and $dx = v\,dt$ gives $\int F\,dx = \int mv\,dv$, so

$$W_{\text{net}} = K_f - K_i.$$

This holds for every kind of force and every path.

### Potential energy and conservation

A force is **conservative** if the work it does between two points does not depend on the path. Gravity and ideal springs are conservative. For these we define a potential energy $U$ with $W_{\text{cons}} = -\Delta U$:

$$U_{\text{grav}} = mgh, \qquad U_{\text{spring}} = \tfrac12 kx^2.$$

Here $h$ is height above any chosen reference level and $x$ is the stretch from the spring's natural length. When only conservative forces (and forces that do no work) act, the mechanical energy $E = K + U$ is constant. If friction acts, the work it does is negative, $W_{\text{f}} = -f_k d$, and

$$K_i + U_i + W_{\text{f}} = K_f + U_f.$$

The mechanical energy is not destroyed; it becomes thermal energy.

### Power

Power is the rate of doing work, in watts ($1\ \text{W} = 1\ \text{J/s}$):

$$P = \frac{dW}{dt} = \vec F\cdot\vec v.$$

### Which method?

Use energy when you want a speed at some position or height and do not need the time. Use $\sum\vec F = m\vec a$ when you need a force or a time.

```python
from scipy.integrate import quad
W, err = quad(lambda x: 3 * x**2, 0, 2)   # work done by F = 3x^2 from 0 to 2 m
print(W)                                  # 8.0 (joules)
```

@example
title: Speed at the bottom of a track
problem: A cart is released from rest at height $4.9$ m on a frictionless curved track. How fast is it moving at the bottom, at height $0$? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Choose the bottom as the zero of height. The normal force is always perpendicular to the motion, so it does no work and mechanical energy is conserved.
step: Initial energy: $K_i = 0$ and $U_i = mgh = m(9.8)(4.9)$. Final energy: $K_f = \tfrac12 mv^2$ and $U_f = 0$.
step: Set them equal: $mgh = \tfrac12 mv^2$. The mass cancels, so $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2(9.8)(4.9)} = \sqrt{96.04}$.
step: $v = 9.8$ m/s. The shape of the track did not matter, only the height difference.

@example
title: Launching a block with a spring
problem: A $0.50$ kg block rests against a spring with $k = 200$ N/m, compressed $0.10$ m, on a frictionless surface. It is released. How fast does it leave the spring?
step: The spring force is conservative and the surface is frictionless, so the spring energy becomes kinetic energy.
step: Initial spring energy: $\tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12(200)(0.10)^2 = 1.0$ J.
step: At the natural length, all of it is kinetic: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = 1.0$ J.
step: Solve: $v^2 = 2(1.0)/0.50 = 4.0$, so $v = 2.0$ m/s.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the $\cos\theta$ in $W = Fd\cos\theta$, or counting the work of forces perpendicular to the motion.
- Writing the spring energy as $\tfrac12 kx$ instead of $\tfrac12 kx^2$, or using the spring's length instead of its stretch.
- Using conservation of mechanical energy on a rough path without including the negative work done by friction.
- Giving friction a positive sign in the energy equation, which would add energy.

prompt: A rope pulls a sled $8.0$ m along level ground with a constant force of $50$ N directed $60^\circ$ above the horizontal. How much work does the rope do?
answer: 200
check: dot([50*cos(pi/3), 50*sin(pi/3)], [8.0, 0])
unit: J
tol: 1%
hint: Only the component of the force along the displacement does work.
hint: $W = Fd\cos\theta$ with $\theta = 60^\circ$.
solution: $W = (50)(8.0)\cos60^\circ = (50)(8.0)(0.5) = 200$ J.

@practice num
prompt: A $2.0$ kg block slides on a level floor and has an initial speed of $7.0$ m/s. The coefficient of kinetic friction is $0.25$. How far does it slide before stopping? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 10
check: root(lambda d: -0.25*2.0*9.8*d - (0 - 0.5*2.0*7.0**2), 0, 100)
unit: m
hint: The only force that does work is friction, and its work is negative.
hint: $-\mu_k mg\,d = 0 - \tfrac12 mv_0^2$. The mass cancels.
solution: $\mu_k g\,d = \tfrac12 v_0^2$, so $d = 49/(2 \times 0.25 \times 9.8) = 49/4.9 = 10$ m.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.20$ kg block is pressed against a spring ($k = 400$ N/m), compressing it $0.15$ m on a frictionless horizontal surface. When released, the block leaves the spring and slides up a frictionless ramp. What maximum height does it reach above its starting level? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and round to three significant figures.
answer: 2.30
check: 0.5*400*0.15**2/(0.20*9.8)
unit: m
tol: 1%
hint: Follow the energy: spring energy, then kinetic energy, then gravitational potential energy.
hint: At the highest point the block is momentarily at rest, so all the spring energy has become $mgh$.
solution: $\tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12(400)(0.15)^2 = 4.5$ J. Then $mgh = 4.5$ gives $h = 4.5/(0.20 \times 9.8) \approx 2.30$ m.

answer: 3920

@quiz num
prompt: A cart is released from rest at height $h$ on a frictionless track that leads into a vertical loop of radius $R = 4.0$ m. The cart must stay in contact with the track at the top of the loop. What is the smallest release height $h$ above the bottom of the loop? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 10
check: root(lambda h: 9.8*h - (9.8*2*4.0 + 0.5*9.8*4.0), 0, 100)
unit: m
hint: At the top of the loop the weight alone can supply the inward force when the cart is about to lose contact. What speed does that require?
hint: The limiting speed at the top is $v^2 = gR$. Then apply conservation of energy between the release point and the top, which is at height $2R$.
solution: At the top, $mg = mv^2/R$ gives $v^2 = gR$. Energy: $mgh = mg(2R) + \tfrac12 mv^2 = mg(2R) + \tfrac12 mgR$, so $h = 2.5R = 10$ m.
