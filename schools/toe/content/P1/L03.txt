@article
A **force** is a push or pull, a vector measured in newtons. Newton's laws say how forces change motion, and nearly all of mechanics follows from them.

### The three laws

1. **First law (inertia).** If the net force on an object is zero, its velocity does not change: it stays at rest or moves in a straight line at constant speed.
2. **Second law.** The net force on an object of mass $m$ produces an acceleration
$$\sum \vec F = m\vec a.$$
One newton gives a one-kilogram object an acceleration of $1\ \text{m/s}^2$.
3. **Third law.** If A pushes on B with force $\vec F_{AB}$, then B pushes on A with $\vec F_{BA} = -\vec F_{AB}$. These two forces act on **different objects**, so they never cancel inside one object's free-body diagram.

The laws hold in an inertial frame, one that is not itself accelerating.

### Forces you will meet

- **Weight** $W = mg$, directed down, with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
- **Normal force** $N$, perpendicular to a surface, with whatever size prevents the object sinking into it.
- **Tension** $T$, along a rope and pulling on whatever it is attached to. A light rope over a massless, frictionless pulley carries the same tension throughout.

### The free-body diagram method

1. Isolate **one** object.
2. Draw only the forces acting **on** it, as arrows starting at the object.
3. Choose axes, ideally with one along the direction of acceleration.
4. Resolve every force into components.
5. Write $\sum F_x = ma_x$ and $\sum F_y = ma_y$, then solve.

### Inclined plane

For a slope at angle $\theta$, put the $x$-axis down the slope. Weight splits into $mg\sin\theta$ along the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into it. With no friction and no other perpendicular forces,

$$N = mg\cos\theta, \qquad a = g\sin\theta.$$

The acceleration does not depend on the mass.

### Connected objects

Write a separate equation for each object. An inextensible rope forces the objects to share the same acceleration magnitude. For two masses on either side of a pulley, with $m_1 > m_2$, the equations are $m_1 g - T = m_1 a$ and $T - m_2 g = m_2 a$. Adding them eliminates $T$:

$$a = \frac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\,g.$$

### Apparent weight

In an elevator accelerating upward at $a$, a passenger obeys $N - mg = ma$, so the scale reads $N = m(g + a)$. You feel heavier, because the normal force has grown, not because gravity changed.

@example
title: Two masses over a pulley
problem: A light rope passes over a massless, frictionless pulley. A $3.0$ kg mass hangs on one side and a $1.0$ kg mass on the other. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the acceleration and the tension.
step: Draw one free-body diagram per mass. Each has weight down and tension up. Let the heavier mass move down, so its acceleration is downward and the lighter mass accelerates upward by the same amount.
step: Heavier mass, down positive: $3.0g - T = 3.0a$. Lighter mass, up positive: $T - 1.0g = 1.0a$.
step: Add the two equations: $2.0g = 4.0a$, so $a = 0.5g = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Substitute into the second equation: $T = 1.0(9.8 + 4.9) = 14.7$ N.
step: Check with the first equation: $3.0(9.8) - 14.7 = 14.7 = 3.0(4.9)$. Both sides agree.

@example
title: A block on a frictionless slope
problem: A $2.0$ kg block slides on a frictionless incline at $30^\circ$. Find its acceleration and the normal force. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Draw the block with two forces only: weight $mg$ straight down and the normal force perpendicular to the slope. Put $x$ down the slope and $y$ perpendicular to it.
step: Along $y$ there is no acceleration: $N - mg\cos30^\circ = 0$, so $N = 2.0(9.8)(0.866) \approx 17.0$ N.
step: Along $x$ only the weight component acts: $mg\sin30^\circ = ma$, so $a = g\sin30^\circ = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: The mass cancelled from the acceleration, so any block on this slope would accelerate the same way.

@mistakes
- Drawing a force on the diagram that has no physical source, such as a "force of motion" or a force down the slope in addition to the weight.
- Treating an action-reaction pair as two forces on the same object, then cancelling them.
- Assuming the normal force always equals $mg$; it equals $mg\cos\theta$ on a slope and changes whenever other vertical forces act.
- Starting to write equations before drawing the diagram and choosing axes.

@practice num
prompt: A $1200$ kg car speeds up uniformly from rest to $27$ m/s in $9.0$ s. What is the net force on it?
answer: 3600
check: 1200*(27.0 - 0)/9.0
unit: N
hint: First find the acceleration from the change in velocity divided by the time.
hint: Then apply $\sum F = ma$.
solution: $a = 27/9.0 = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^2$, so $F = 1200 \times 3.0 = 3600$ N.

@practice mc
prompt: A book rests on a table. Earth pulls down on the book with its weight. Which force is the third-law partner (reaction) of that weight?
choice: The normal force of the table pushing up on the book.
choice*: The gravitational pull of the book on Earth.
choice: The force of the book pressing down on the table.
choice: There is no partner force, because the book is at rest.
hint: A third-law pair has the same type of force, acts on two different objects, and swaps their roles.
solution: Earth pulls on the book, so the book pulls on Earth with an equal and opposite gravitational force. The normal force is equal to the weight only because the book is not accelerating; it is a different type of force acting on the same object.

@practice num
prompt: A $70$ kg person stands on a scale in an elevator that accelerates upward at $1.5\ \text{m/s}^2$. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. What force does the scale exert on the person (the reading in newtons)?
answer: 791
check: root(lambda N: N - 70*9.8 - 70*1.5, 0, 5000)
unit: N
hint: Draw the person with weight down and the scale's normal force up. The net force is upward.
hint: $N - mg = ma$.
solution: $N = m(g + a) = 70(9.8 + 1.5) = 791$ N, which is larger than the resting weight of $686$ N.

@practice num
prompt: A block is released from rest on a frictionless $30^\circ$ incline. How fast is it moving $2.0$ s later? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
answer: 9.8
check: 9.8*sin(pi/6)*2.0
unit: m/s
tol: 1%
hint: Find the acceleration along the slope first.
hint: $a = g\sin30^\circ = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$, then use $v = at$.
solution: $a = 9.8\sin30^\circ = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $v = at = 4.9 \times 2.0 = 9.8$ m/s.

@practice num
prompt: Two masses, $5.0$ kg and $3.0$ kg, hang from a light rope over a massless, frictionless pulley. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the tension in the rope.
answer: 36.75
check: lin([[5,1],[-3,1]], [5*9.8, 3*9.8])[1]
unit: N
tol: 1%
hint: Write one equation for each mass; the unknowns are $a$ and $T$.
hint: $5g - T = 5a$ and $T - 3g = 3a$. Add them to find $a$, then substitute.
solution: Adding gives $2g = 8a$, so $a = 2.45\ \text{m/s}^2$. Then $T = 3(9.8 + 2.45) = 36.75$ N.

@quiz num
prompt: A $2.0$ kg block A is pulled across a frictionless table by a horizontal $10$ N force. A light string connects it to a $3.0$ kg block B trailing behind, and the force acts only on A. What is the tension in the string?
answer: 6
check: lin([[2,1],[-3,1]], [10, 0])[1]
unit: N
hint: Draw each block separately. The tension pulls A backward and B forward.
hint: A: $10 - T = 2a$. B: $T = 3a$. Treat the pair as one object of $5.0$ kg to find $a$.
solution: Together $10 = 5.0a$, so $a = 2.0\ \text{m/s}^2$. Block B has only the tension acting, so $T = 3.0 \times 2.0 = 6.0$ N.

@quiz mc
prompt: A block slides down a frictionless incline. Which set of forces belongs on its free-body diagram?
choice*: Weight (straight down) and the normal force (perpendicular to the slope).
choice: Weight, the normal force, and a force pointing down the slope.
choice: Weight, the normal force, and a force of motion in the direction of the velocity.
choice: Only the weight, because the surface is frictionless.
hint: Ask for each arrow: what object exerts this force on the block?
solution: Only Earth (weight) and the surface (normal force) touch or act on the block. The "down the slope" effect is a component of the weight, not an extra force. A frictionless surface still pushes perpendicular to itself.
