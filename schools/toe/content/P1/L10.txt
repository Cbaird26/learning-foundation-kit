@article
Whenever a system is nudged from a stable equilibrium and the restoring force grows in proportion to the displacement, it oscillates in a very particular way: **simple harmonic motion**.

### The spring-mass oscillator

An ideal spring pulls back with $F = -kx$ (Hooke's law), where $x$ is the stretch from the natural length. Newton's second law gives

$$m\ddot x = -kx \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot x = -\omega^2 x, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

The general solution is

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi),$$

where the amplitude $A$ and the phase $\varphi$ come from the initial conditions. The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ and the frequency is $f = 1/T$. Differentiating gives $v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t + \varphi)$, so

$$v_{\max} = A\omega, \qquad a_{\max} = A\omega^2.$$

The period does **not** depend on the amplitude. A mass released from rest at $x = A$ has $\varphi = 0$ and $x = A\cos\omega t$. For a vertical spring, gravity only shifts the equilibrium point; the same $\omega$ describes the motion about the new equilibrium.

### Energy

The total energy is constant:

$$E = \tfrac12 mv^2 + \tfrac12 kx^2 = \tfrac12 kA^2 = \tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2.$$

At the turning points all of it is potential energy; at the equilibrium point all of it is kinetic. The energy grows as $A^2$, so doubling the amplitude quadruples the energy but leaves the period unchanged.

### The simple pendulum

A small mass on a light string of length $L$ swings through angle $\theta$. The torque about the pivot is $-mgL\sin\theta$, and the moment of inertia is $mL^2$, so $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ gives

$$mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ddot\theta = -\frac{g}{L}\sin\theta.$$

For small angles, measured in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, and the equation becomes $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, which is simple harmonic with

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}.$$

The mass cancelled, so the period does not depend on it. The approximation is good: at an amplitude of $20^\circ$ the true period is only about $0.8\%$ longer.

### How to derive an oscillator relation

1. Write the net force or torque on the system as a function of a small displacement $q$ from equilibrium.
2. Linearise: keep only the term proportional to $q$.
3. Rearrange to $\ddot q = -\omega^2 q$ and read off $\omega$.
4. Check the dimensions of $\omega$ and test limiting cases (a stiffer spring should oscillate faster; a longer pendulum slower).

@example
title: A mass on a spring
problem: A $0.50$ kg block on a frictionless table is attached to a spring with $k = 200$ N/m. It is pulled $0.05$ m from equilibrium and released from rest. Find $\omega$, the period, the maximum speed, and $x(t)$.
step: $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = \sqrt{200/0.50} = \sqrt{400} = 20$ rad/s.
step: The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega = \pi/10 \approx 0.314$ s.
step: Released from rest at $x = 0.05$ m, the amplitude is $A = 0.05$ m and $\varphi = 0$, so $x(t) = 0.05\cos(20t)$ metres.
step: Maximum speed: $v_{\max} = A\omega = 0.05(20) = 1.0$ m/s. Check with energy: $\tfrac12 kA^2 = 0.25$ J and $\tfrac12 mv_{\max}^2 = \tfrac12(0.50)(1.0)^2 = 0.25$ J.

@example
title: Designing a pendulum with a two-second period
problem: What length of pendulum has a small-angle period of $2.0$ s? Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Start from $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ and square both sides: $T^2 = 4\pi^2 L/g$.
step: Solve for the length: $L = gT^2/(4\pi^2)$.
step: Substitute: $L = (9.8)(2.0)^2/(4\pi^2) = 39.2/39.48 \approx 0.993$ m.
step: Check dimensions: $[g][T]^2 = [L][T]^{-2}[T]^2 = [L]$. A pendulum a little under one metre long ticks once per second on each side, which is why it was a historical timekeeper.

@mistakes
- Setting the pendulum equation up in degrees. The approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ requires radians.
- Believing that a larger amplitude gives a longer period for a spring-mass system. For a spring the period is independent of amplitude.
- Using the spring's length instead of its stretch from the natural length in $F = -kx$.
- Forgetting that $\omega$ is in rad/s while $f = \omega/2\pi$ is in cycles per second.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the small-angle period of a simple pendulum of length $L$ and mass $m$ starting from the torque about the pivot. State the approximation you use and where it fails.
hint: The weight component along the arc is $mg\sin\theta$ and acts at distance $L$ from the pivot. Use $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ with $I = mL^2$.
hint: For small angles in radians, replace $\sin\theta$ by $\theta$, then compare with $\ddot x = -\omega^2 x$.
solution: Take counterclockwise as positive. The tension acts along the string, through the pivot, so it has no torque. The weight has torque $-mgL\sin\theta$ about the pivot. With $I = mL^2$, $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ gives $mL^2\ddot\theta = -mgL\sin\theta$, so $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\sin\theta$. For small $\theta$ in radians, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$, so $\ddot\theta = -(g/L)\theta$, which has the form $\ddot q = -\omega^2 q$ with $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$. The period is $T = 2\pi/\omega = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$, independent of $m$. The approximation fails for large amplitudes, where $\sin\theta < \theta$ and the true period is longer.

rubric: Uses $I = mL^2$ and shows the mass cancels.
rubric: States that $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ requires small angles in radians, and arrives at $\omega = \sqrt{g/L}$.
rubric: Gives $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ and names how the result fails for large amplitude.
