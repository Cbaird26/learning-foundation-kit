@article
Rotation needs a force with the right line of action, not just a large force. **Torque** measures the turning effect of a force about an axis.

### Torque

If a force $\vec F$ acts at position $\vec r$ measured from the axis,

$$\vec\tau = \vec r\times\vec F, \qquad \tau = rF\sin\phi,$$

where $\phi$ is the angle between $\vec r$ and $\vec F$. Equivalently, torque is the force times its **lever arm** $r\sin\phi$, the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line along which the force acts. The unit is N m. In a plane, $\tau_z = xF_y - yF_x$, and counterclockwise is positive. A force that passes through the axis has zero torque.

```python
import numpy as np
r = np.array([0.30, 0.0, 0.0])    # m, from the axis to the point of application
F = np.array([0.0, 40.0, 0.0])    # N
print(np.cross(r, F))             # [ 0.  0. 12.]  (N m)
```

### The rotational second law

For a rigid body turning about a fixed axis,

$$\sum\tau = I\alpha.$$

This is $F = ma$ with force replaced by torque, mass by moment of inertia, and acceleration by angular acceleration. A rope wound around a pulley of radius $R$ exerts torque $TR$, and a non-slipping rope links the two kinds of motion through $a = \alpha R$.

### Static equilibrium

For an object that is not accelerating or rotating,

$$\sum\vec F = 0 \quad\text{and}\quad \sum\tau = 0 \text{ about any point.}$$

Choose the pivot cleverly: a force acting at the pivot has zero torque, so putting the pivot where an unknown force acts removes it from the torque equation. The weight of a uniform beam acts at its center.

### Angular momentum

For a particle, $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, with magnitude $mvd$ where $d$ is the perpendicular distance from the axis to the line of motion. For a rigid body on a fixed axis, $L = I\omega$. Newton's second law becomes

$$\sum\vec\tau = \frac{d\vec L}{dt}.$$

When the net external torque is zero, $\vec L$ is **conserved**. A skater who pulls in her arms reduces $I$, so $\omega$ rises to keep $I\omega$ fixed. The kinetic energy $L^2/2I$ rises too, because her muscles do work.

### A method for rotating systems

1. Draw a free-body diagram for every rigid body and every hanging mass.
2. Pick one consistent sense of motion (mass down means pulley clockwise).
3. Write $\sum F = ma$ for each translating object and $\sum\tau = I\alpha$ for each rotating one.
4. Add the constraint $a = \alpha R$.
5. Solve, then test a limit: with a massless pulley, the hanging mass should fall with $a = g$.

@example
title: A plank on two supports
problem: A uniform horizontal beam of mass $10$ kg and length $4.0$ m rests on two supports, one at each end. A $20$ kg load sits $1.0$ m from the left end. Find the force from each support. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
step: Draw the beam with the beam's weight $98$ N at its center ($2.0$ m from the left), the load's weight $196$ N at $1.0$ m, and upward forces $N_L$ and $N_R$ at the ends.
step: Take torques about the left end, so $N_L$ drops out: $N_R(4.0) - 98(2.0) - 196(1.0) = 0$.
step: $N_R = (196 + 196)/4.0 = 98$ N.
step: Vertical balance: $N_L + N_R = 98 + 196 = 294$ N, so $N_L = 196$ N. The support nearer the load carries more, as expected.

@example
title: A skater pulls in her arms
problem: A skater spins at $2.0$ rad/s with moment of inertia $4.0\ \text{kg m}^2$. She pulls her arms in until her moment of inertia is $1.0\ \text{kg m}^2$. Find her new angular velocity and the change in her kinetic energy.
step: Friction with the ice is negligible, so there is no external torque and $L = I\omega$ is conserved.
step: $4.0(2.0) = 1.0\,\omega_f$, so $\omega_f = 8.0$ rad/s.
step: Kinetic energy before: $\tfrac12(4.0)(2.0)^2 = 8.0$ J. After: $\tfrac12(1.0)(8.0)^2 = 32$ J.
step: The kinetic energy increased by $24$ J. That energy came from the work her muscles did pulling her arms inward against the outward motion. Angular momentum is conserved, but kinetic energy is not.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the lever arm: using $\tau = rF$ when the force is not perpendicular to $\vec r$.
- Choosing a pivot arbitrarily and then leaving a torque from an unknown force in the equation, instead of picking the pivot where that force acts.
- Writing $T = mg$ for a hanging mass that is accelerating; the tension is smaller than the weight while it falls.
- Applying conservation of angular momentum when an external torque, such as friction at the axle, acts.

@practice num
prompt: A $40$ N force acts at the end of a $0.30$ m wrench, with the force directed $30^\circ$ from the line along the wrench. What is the torque about the bolt?
answer: 6
check: cross([0.30, 0, 0], [40*cos(pi/6), 40*sin(pi/6), 0])[2]
unit: N m
tol: 1%
hint: Only the component of the force perpendicular to the wrench produces torque.
hint: $\tau = rF\sin\phi$ with $\phi = 30^\circ$.
solution: $\tau = (0.30)(40)\sin30^\circ = 6.0$ N m.

@practice ms
prompt: You push on a door with a force perpendicular to the door, and it produces some torque about the hinges. Select every change that would double the torque.
choice*: Push with twice the force at the same place.
choice*: Push with the same force at twice the distance from the hinges.
choice: Push with the same force at the same place, but at $45^\circ$ to the door instead of $90^\circ$.
choice: Push with the same force directly at the hinge.
hint: Torque is $rF\sin\phi$. Which factors change in each case, and in which direction?
solution: Doubling $F$ or $r$ doubles $\tau$. Pushing at $45^\circ$ reduces $\sin\phi$ from $1$ to $0.71$, which cuts the torque. A force at the hinge has $r = 0$ and produces no torque.

@practice num
prompt: A wheel with moment of inertia $0.50\ \text{kg m}^2$ is at rest. A constant net torque of $3.0$ N m acts on it for $4.0$ s. What is its angular velocity at the end?
answer: 24
check: quad(lambda t: 3.0/0.50, 0, 4.0)
unit: rad/s
hint: First find the angular acceleration from $\sum\tau = I\alpha$.
hint: $\alpha = 3.0/0.50 = 6.0\ \text{rad/s}^2$, then $\omega = \alpha t$.
solution: $\alpha = \tau/I = 6.0\ \text{rad/s}^2$ and $\omega = \alpha t = 6.0(4.0) = 24$ rad/s.

@practice num
prompt: A spinning skater has moment of inertia $3.0\ \text{kg m}^2$ and angular velocity $1.5$ rad/s. She pulls her arms in until her moment of inertia is $1.2\ \text{kg m}^2$. What is her angular velocity now?
answer: 3.75
check: 3.0*1.5/1.2
unit: rad/s
hint: There is no external torque, so one quantity is conserved. Which one?
hint: $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$.
solution: $\omega_2 = I_1\omega_1/I_2 = (3.0)(1.5)/1.2 = 3.75$ rad/s.

@practice num
prompt: A $2.0$ kg mass hangs from a light string wrapped around the rim of a solid disk pulley of mass $4.0$ kg and radius $0.25$ m, which turns freely about its center. The string does not slip. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the acceleration of the hanging mass.
answer: 4.9
check: lin([[2,1],[-2,1]], [2*9.8, 0])[0]
unit: m/s^2
tol: 1%
hint: Draw two diagrams. For the mass: $mg - T = ma$. For the pulley: $TR = I\alpha$ with $I = \tfrac12 MR^2$ and $\alpha = a/R$.
hint: The pulley equation reduces to $T = \tfrac12 Ma$. Substitute into the mass equation.
solution: Pulley: $TR = \tfrac12 MR^2(a/R)$, so $T = \tfrac12 Ma = 2.0a$. Mass: $2.0(9.8) - 2.0a = 2.0a$, so $a = 19.6/4.0 = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$. Limit check: a massless pulley would give $a = g$.

@quiz num
prompt: A uniform plank of mass $20$ kg and length $5.0$ m rests on supports at its two ends. A $30$ kg child stands $2.0$ m from the left end. Take $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the force exerted by the right support.
answer: 215.6
check: lin([[1,1],[0,5]], [(20+30)*9.8, 20*9.8*2.5 + 30*9.8*2.0])[1]
unit: N
tol: 1%
hint: Take torques about the left end to eliminate the left support's force.
hint: $N_R(5.0) = 20g(2.5) + 30g(2.0)$.
solution: $5.0N_R = 20(9.8)(2.5) + 30(9.8)(2.0) = 490 + 588 = 1078$, so $N_R = 215.6$ N. The left support carries the other $274.4$ N.

@quiz num
prompt: A $2.0$ kg particle moves at $3.0$ m/s along a straight line that passes $4.0$ m from the origin. What is the magnitude of its angular momentum about the origin?
answer: 24
check: abs(cross([7.0, 4.0, 0], [2.0*3.0, 0, 0])[2])
unit: kg m^2/s
hint: Use $L = mvd$ with $d$ the perpendicular distance from the origin to the line of motion.
hint: $L = (2.0)(3.0)(4.0)$.
solution: $L = mvd = 2.0(3.0)(4.0) = 24\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$. It stays the same along the whole path, because no torque acts on a free particle.
