@lesson
title: Grand canonical ensemble
summary: Use the grand partition function $\Xi$ for systems that exchange energy and particles with a reservoir, and compute $\langle N\rangle$, number fluctuations and single-orbital occupancies.
objective: Write the probability $p = e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}/\Xi$ and the grand partition function $\Xi = \sum_N \zeta^N Z_N$ with fugacity $\zeta = e^{\beta\mu}$.
objective: Obtain $\langle N\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\mu$, the variance $k_BT\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu$ and $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\Xi = -PV$.
objective: Compute $\Xi$ and the mean occupation of a single orbital for fermions, bosons and classical particles.
reading: MIT 8.044 Statistical Physics I, the lectures on the grand canonical ensemble and the chemical potential (look up "grand canonical ensemble" and "grand partition function" in the lecture notes).

@article
Many systems exchange particles with their surroundings: a patch of gas inside a larger container, molecules adsorbed on a surface, electrons in a wire, photons in a hot cavity. For these, $N$ fluctuates, and the natural description fixes $T$, $V$ and the **chemical potential** $\mu$.

### From the bath argument

Repeat the derivation of week 7 with a reservoir that exchanges both energy and particles. If the system has energy $E$ and $N$ particles, the reservoir has $E_0 - E$ and $N_0 - N$. Expanding $\ln\Omega_R$ to first order, with $\partial\ln\Omega_R/\partial E = \beta$ and $\partial\ln\Omega_R/\partial N = -\beta\mu$, gives

$$p(N,i) = \frac{e^{-\beta(E_i - \mu N)}}{\Xi}, \qquad \Xi = \sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\sum_i e^{-\beta(E_i - \mu N)} = \sum_{N=0}^\infty \zeta^N Z_N,$$

where $\zeta = e^{\beta\mu}$ is the **fugacity** and $Z_N$ is the canonical partition function with exactly $N$ particles. Particles raise a state's weight when $\mu$ is large.

### Thermodynamics from $\Xi$

$$\Phi = -k_BT\ln\Xi, \qquad \langle N\rangle = k_BT\,\frac{\partial\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu}, \qquad \langle (\Delta N)^2\rangle = (k_BT)^2\frac{\partial^2\ln\Xi}{\partial\mu^2} = k_BT\,\frac{\partial\langle N\rangle}{\partial\mu}.$$

Here $\Phi = F - \mu N$ is the **grand potential**. For a uniform system $\Phi = -PV$, so $PV = k_BT\ln\Xi$. The energy follows from $\langle E\rangle - \mu\langle N\rangle = -\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\beta$ at fixed $\mu$.

### Check: the classical ideal gas

With $Z_N = z^N/N!$ and $z = V/\lambda_T^3$,

$$\Xi = \sum_N \frac{(\zeta z)^N}{N!} = e^{\zeta z}, \qquad \langle N\rangle = \zeta z = \frac{\zeta V}{\lambda_T^3}.$$

So $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda_T^3)$, as in week 8; $PV = k_BT\ln\Xi = \langle N\rangle k_BT$; and $\langle(\Delta N)^2\rangle = \langle N\rangle$ (Poisson statistics), so the relative fluctuation is $1/\sqrt{\langle N\rangle}$.

### One orbital at a time

For independent particles, label the single-particle **orbitals** by energy $\varepsilon_k$. The weights factorize, $\Xi = \prod_k \Xi_k$, and each orbital can be treated alone with occupation $n$:

- **Fermions** (Pauli principle, $n = 0, 1$): $\Xi_k = 1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$ and $\langle n\rangle = \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1}$.
- **Bosons** ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$): the geometric series gives $\Xi_k = \dfrac{1}{1 - e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}}$ if $\mu<\varepsilon$, and $\langle n\rangle = \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}-1}$.
- **Classical limit**, $e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}\gg1$: both reduce to $\langle n\rangle \approx e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$.

Week 10 builds the full quantum statistics on these two formulas.

### Procedure

1. Decide what the reservoir exchanges: energy only, or particles too.
2. List every allowed (number, state) pair with its weight $e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}$.
3. Sum to get $\Xi$.
4. Differentiate $\ln\Xi$ for $\langle N\rangle$, fluctuations and $\Phi$.

@example
title: Adsorption on a single site
problem: A surface site can be empty ($E = 0$) or hold one molecule bound with energy $-\epsilon_b$. The surface is in equilibrium with a gas at temperature $T$ and chemical potential $\mu$. Find the mean occupation, and evaluate it for $\epsilon_b = 0.30$ eV, $\mu = -0.33$ eV and $k_BT = 0.025$ eV.
step: The site has two states. Empty: $N = 0$, weight $1$. Occupied: $N = 1$, $E = -\epsilon_b$, weight $e^{-\beta(-\epsilon_b - \mu)} = e^{\beta(\epsilon_b + \mu)}$.
step: So $\Xi = 1 + e^{\beta(\epsilon_b+\mu)}$.
step: The mean occupation is $\langle N\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\mu = \dfrac{e^{\beta(\epsilon_b+\mu)}}{1 + e^{\beta(\epsilon_b+\mu)}}$, the Langmuir form of the coverage.
step: Numerically $\beta(\epsilon_b+\mu) = (0.30 - 0.33)/0.025 = -1.2$, so $\langle N\rangle = e^{-1.2}/(1 + e^{-1.2}) = 0.3012/1.3012 \approx 0.231$.

@example
title: Number fluctuations in a classical gas
problem: A small open region of a classical ideal gas holds on average $100$ molecules. Find the rms fluctuation of the number of molecules, and compare it with a macroscopic sample of $10^{22}$ molecules.
step: For the classical gas $\Xi = e^{\zeta z}$, so $\ln\Xi = \zeta z$ and $\langle N\rangle = \zeta z$.
step: The variance is $k_BT\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu = \zeta\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\zeta = \zeta z = \langle N\rangle$.
step: For $\langle N\rangle = 100$, $\sigma_N = 10$, a relative fluctuation of $10\%$.
step: For $\langle N\rangle = 10^{22}$, $\sigma_N/\langle N\rangle = 10^{-11}$. Number fluctuations are visible in small regions and invisible in bulk, so the choice of ensemble is a matter of convenience for large systems.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that $\mu$ appears with the opposite sign to $E$: the weight is $e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}$, not $e^{-\beta(E+\mu N)}$.
- Using $\Xi = Z_N$ at fixed $N$. The grand partition function sums over all $N$.
- Using the boson formula with $\mu \ge \varepsilon$. The geometric series diverges, so $\mu$ must lie below the lowest orbital energy.
- Differentiating with respect to $\mu$ but forgetting the factor $k_BT$ in $\langle N\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\mu$.

choice: $k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial T$ at fixed $\mu$

hint: Think about which parameter in the exponent $e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}$ multiplies $N$.
solution: Since $\partial_\mu e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)} = \beta N e^{-\beta(E-\mu N)}$, we get $\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\mu = \beta\langle N\rangle$, so $\langle N\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\mu$. The $\beta$-derivative gives $-\langle E - \mu N\rangle$ instead.

@practice num

answer: 0.3100
check: (lambda E, mu, kT: math.exp(-(E - mu)/kT)/(1 + math.exp(-(E - mu)/kT)))(-0.25, -0.27, 0.025)

hint: The two weights are $1$ for the empty site and $e^{-\beta(E-\mu)}$ for the occupied one.
hint: $E - \mu = -0.25 + 0.27 = +0.02$ eV, so the occupied weight is $e^{-0.8}$.
solution: The occupied weight is $e^{-(0.02)/0.025} = e^{-0.8} = 0.4493$. So $\langle N\rangle = 0.4493/(1 + 0.4493) \approx 0.3100$.

prompt: A fermion orbital of energy $\varepsilon$ has grand partition function $\Xi_k = 1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$. Using $\langle n\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\ln\Xi_k/\partial\mu$, write the mean occupation as a function of $\beta$, $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$.
vars: beta, epsilon, mu
lead: $\langle n\rangle =$
answer: 1/(exp(beta*(epsilon - mu)) + 1)
check: lambda beta, epsilon, mu: nderiv(lambda m: log(1 + exp(-beta*(epsilon - m))), mu)/beta
hint: Differentiate $\ln\Xi_k$ with respect to $\mu$; remember $k_BT = 1/\beta$.
hint: $\partial_\mu \ln\Xi_k = \beta e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}/\big(1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}\big)$. Multiply the numerator and denominator by $e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$.
solution: $\langle n\rangle = \dfrac1\beta\cdot\dfrac{\beta e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}}{1 + e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}} = \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)} + 1}$, the Fermi–Dirac occupation. It lies between $0$ and $1$ and equals $\tfrac12$ when $\varepsilon = \mu$.

@practice self
prompt: For a classical ideal gas with $Z_N = z^N/N!$, show that $\Xi = e^{\zeta z}$. Then derive $\langle N\rangle$, show that $PV = \langle N\rangle k_BT$ from $\Phi = -PV$, and find the variance of $N$.
hint: Write $\Xi = \sum_N \zeta^N Z_N$ and recognize the series for an exponential.
hint: Use $\ln\Xi = \zeta z$, then $\langle N\rangle = \zeta\,\partial\ln\Xi/\partial\zeta$ and $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\Xi$.
solution: $\Xi = \sum_N \zeta^N z^N/N! = e^{\zeta z}$. Then $\ln\Xi = \zeta z$ and $\langle N\rangle = \zeta\,\partial_\zeta\ln\Xi = \zeta z$, since $k_BT\,\partial_\mu = \zeta\,\partial_\zeta$. The grand potential is $\Phi = -k_BT\zeta z = -\langle N\rangle k_BT$, and setting $\Phi = -PV$ gives $PV = \langle N\rangle k_BT$. The variance is $\zeta\,\partial_\zeta\langle N\rangle = \zeta z = \langle N\rangle$, which is Poisson.
rubric: Recognizes the exponential series to obtain $\Xi = e^{\zeta z}$.
rubric: Obtains $\langle N\rangle = \zeta z$ from the derivative with respect to $\mu$ (or $\zeta$).
rubric: Uses $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\Xi = -PV$ to obtain the ideal gas law.
rubric: Finds variance equal to the mean, and states the consequence for the relative fluctuation.

@quiz num
prompt: A surface site is empty ($E=0$) or holds one molecule in either of two equivalent orientations, each with energy $-0.20$ eV. The reservoir has $\mu = -0.23$ eV and $k_BT = 0.025$ eV. What is the mean number of molecules on the site? Give four decimal places.
answer: 0.3759
check: (lambda g, E, mu, kT: g*math.exp(-(E - mu)/kT)/(1 + g*math.exp(-(E - mu)/kT)))(2, -0.20, -0.23, 0.025)
tol: 0.0005
hint: The occupied state is doubly degenerate, so its total weight is twice the single-state weight.
hint: $\Xi = 1 + 2e^{-1.2}$, and $\langle N\rangle$ is the weight of the occupied states divided by $\Xi$.
solution: $E - \mu = -0.20 + 0.23 = 0.03$ eV, so each occupied state has weight $e^{-1.2} = 0.3012$. Then $\Xi = 1 + 2(0.3012) = 1.6024$ and $\langle N\rangle = 0.6024/1.6024 \approx 0.3759$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about the grand canonical ensemble.
choice*: The variance of $N$ is $k_BT\,\partial\langle N\rangle/\partial\mu$.
choice*: For a uniform system the grand potential satisfies $\Phi = -k_BT\ln\Xi = -PV$.
choice: A fermion orbital can have a mean occupation greater than $1$ if $\mu$ is large enough.
choice*: The bosonic geometric series for $\Xi_k$ converges only if $\mu < \varepsilon$.
choice: For fixed $N$ the grand partition function equals the canonical partition function $Z_N$.
hint: Think about the allowed values of $n$ for a fermion orbital, and the ratio of the geometric series for bosons.
solution: The number variance and $\Phi = -PV$ are standard results. A fermion orbital has $n \in \{0,1\}$ so $\langle n\rangle \le 1$. The boson series has ratio $e^{-\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}$, which must be below $1$. The grand partition function sums over all $N$, $\Xi = \sum_N \zeta^NZ_N$, so it is not $Z_N$.
