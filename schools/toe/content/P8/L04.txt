@article
A **thermodynamic potential** is a state function whose changes tell you what can happen under given constraints and whose derivatives give the other properties of the system. Which potential is useful depends on what you hold fixed.

### The fundamental relation

For a closed system, combining the first law with $\delta Q_{\rm rev} = T\,dS$ and $\delta W = -P\,dV$ gives

$$dU = T\,dS - P\,dV \qquad (\text{add } \mu\,dN \text{ if } N \text{ varies}).$$

So $U$ is naturally a function of $(S, V)$, with $T = (\partial U/\partial S)_V$ and $P = -(\partial U/\partial V)_S$. In a laboratory it is easier to control $T$ and $P$ than $S$ and $V$. Swapping a variable for its conjugate is done with a Legendre transform.

### The four potentials

| Potential | Definition | Differential | Natural variables |
|---|---|---|---|
| Internal energy | $U$ | $dU = T\,dS - P\,dV$ | $S, V$ |
| Enthalpy | $H = U + PV$ | $dH = T\,dS + V\,dP$ | $S, P$ |
| Helmholtz free energy | $F = U - TS$ | $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$ | $T, V$ |
| Gibbs free energy | $G = U - TS + PV$ | $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$ | $T, P$ |

### What each is good for

- **Enthalpy.** At constant pressure with only $PV$ work, $dH = \delta Q$, so $\Delta H = Q_P$ and $C_P = (\partial H/\partial T)_P$.
- **Helmholtz.** A system at fixed $T$ and $V$ in contact with a reservoir has $\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S - \Delta U/T \ge 0$, which says $\Delta F \le 0$. Equilibrium minimizes $F$, and in a reversible isothermal process the work extractable from the system is $-\Delta F$.
- **Gibbs.** At fixed $T$ and $P$, equilibrium minimizes $G$. Phase coexistence is the condition that the two phases have equal $G$ per particle.

### Maxwell relations

Each potential is a state function, so its differential is exact and mixed second derivatives agree. From $dF = -S\,dT - P\,dV$ we read $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_V$ and $P = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$, and then

$$\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial V}\right)_T = \left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial T}\right)_V .$$

The same argument gives three more:

$$\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial V}\right)_S = -\left(\frac{\partial P}{\partial S}\right)_V, \quad \left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial P}\right)_S = \left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial S}\right)_P, \quad \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial P}\right)_T = -\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P .$$

Their power is practical: an entropy derivative, which cannot be measured directly, equals a derivative of $P$, $V$ and $T$, which can. With $\alpha = \frac1V(\partial V/\partial T)_P$, the last relation reads $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -V\alpha$.

### Procedure

1. List the variables you hold fixed or control.
2. Pick the potential with those natural variables.
3. Write its differential and read off the first derivatives.
4. Equate the cross second derivatives to get a Maxwell relation.

@example
title: Heat released when a solid is compressed
problem: One mole of copper at $300$ K ($V_m = 7.1\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^3$, $\alpha = 5.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{K}^{-1}$) is compressed slowly and isothermally by $\Delta P = 1.0\times10^{8}$ Pa. Estimate the heat absorbed, treating $V_m$ and $\alpha$ as constant.
step: Fixed $T$ and varying $P$ points to $G$. Its Maxwell relation gives $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P = -V\alpha$.
step: Per mole, $-V_m\alpha = -(7.1\times10^{-6})(5.0\times10^{-5}) = -3.55\times10^{-10}$ J/(K Pa).
step: Integrate over the pressure change: $\Delta S = (-3.55\times10^{-10})(1.0\times10^{8}) = -3.55\times10^{-2}$ J/K.
step: For a reversible isothermal process $Q = T\Delta S = (300)(-3.55\times10^{-2}) \approx -10.7$ J. The solid releases heat when compressed, a result we got from measurable quantities alone.

@example
title: Checking a Maxwell relation for the ideal gas
problem: An ideal gas has Helmholtz free energy $F(T,V) = -nRT\ln(V/V_0) + g(T)$, where $V_0$ is a constant and $g$ depends on $T$ only. Find $P$ and verify $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = (\partial P/\partial T)_V$.
step: Pressure is $P = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T = nRT/V$, the ideal gas law.
step: Entropy is $S = -(\partial F/\partial T)_V = nR\ln(V/V_0) - g'(T)$.
step: Differentiate $S$ with respect to volume at fixed $T$: $(\partial S/\partial V)_T = nR/V$.
step: Differentiate $P$ with respect to $T$ at fixed $V$: $(\partial P/\partial T)_V = nR/V$. The two agree.

@mistakes
- Using $\Delta H = Q$ when the pressure is not constant or when other work is done.
- Differentiating a potential with respect to a variable that is not natural for it, such as writing $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_V$. The subscript must match the potential.
- Dropping minus signs in the Maxwell relations that come from $U$ and $G$.
- Saying "equilibrium minimizes $G$" without the constraint. That is for fixed $T$ and $P$; at fixed $T$ and $V$ it is $F$ that is minimized.

@practice mc
prompt: Which thermodynamic potential has temperature and pressure as its natural variables?
choice: The internal energy $U$
choice: The enthalpy $H$
choice: The Helmholtz free energy $F$
choice*: The Gibbs free energy $G$
hint: Look for the differential that contains $dT$ and $dP$.
solution: $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$, so $G$ is a function of $(T, P)$. $U$ depends naturally on $(S,V)$, $H$ on $(S,P)$ and $F$ on $(T,V)$.

@practice num
prompt: Boiling $0.100$ kg of water at $100\,^\circ$C (take $373.15$ K) and constant pressure requires $\Delta H = Q_P = (0.100)(2.26\times10^{6})$ J. Treat the vapor as an ideal gas, neglect the volume of the liquid, and take $M = 18.0$ g/mol and $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K). What is the change in internal energy $\Delta U$, in kJ?
answer: 208.8
check: (0.100*2.26e6 - (100.0/18.0)*8.314*373.15)/1000
unit: kJ
tol: 1%
hint: Since $H = U + PV$, at constant pressure $\Delta U = \Delta H - P\Delta V$.
hint: Neglecting the liquid, $P\Delta V = nRT$ with $n = 100\ \text{g}/18.0\ \text{g/mol}$.
solution: $n = 5.556$ mol and $P\Delta V = nRT = (5.556)(8.314)(373.15) \approx 1.72\times10^{4}$ J. So $\Delta U = 2.26\times10^{5} - 1.72\times10^{4} \approx 2.09\times10^{5}$ J $= 208.8$ kJ.

@practice expr
prompt: A gas has Helmholtz free energy $F(T,V) = -nRT\ln(V - nb) - \dfrac{a n^2}{V} + g(T)$, where $a$ and $b$ are constants and $g(T)$ depends on $T$ only. Find its pressure $P(T,V)$.
vars: n, R, T, V, a, b
domain: n: 0.5..1.5; R: 0.5..2; T: 0.5..2; V: 3..6; a: 0.1..1; b: 0.1..1
lead: $P =$
answer: n*R*T/(V - n*b) - a*n^2/V^2
check: lambda n, R, T, V, a, b: -nderiv(lambda v: -n*R*T*log(v - n*b) - a*n**2/v + 0.0, V)
hint: The pressure is a derivative of $F$ at fixed temperature.
hint: $P = -(\partial F/\partial V)_T$. The function $g(T)$ drops out.
solution: $P = -\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial V}\Big|_T = \dfrac{nRT}{V - nb} - \dfrac{a n^2}{V^2}$, the van der Waals equation of state.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$, derive the Maxwell relation $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P$. Then use $\alpha = \frac1V(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ to explain why compressing a solid with $\alpha > 0$ isothermally releases heat.
hint: Read off $S$ and $V$ as first derivatives of $G$, then differentiate each a second time with respect to the other variable.
hint: Mixed partial derivatives of a smooth function are equal. For the physical conclusion, use $Q = T\Delta S$ for a reversible isothermal process.
solution: From $dG = -S\,dT + V\,dP$: $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_P$ and $V = (\partial G/\partial P)_T$. Then $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -\partial^2 G/\partial P\,\partial T$ and $(\partial V/\partial T)_P = \partial^2 G/\partial T\,\partial P$. These mixed derivatives are equal, so $(\partial S/\partial P)_T = -(\partial V/\partial T)_P = -V\alpha$. For $\alpha > 0$, raising $P$ at fixed $T$ lowers $S$: $\Delta S < 0$. For a reversible isothermal compression $Q = T\Delta S < 0$, so heat leaves the solid.
rubric: Identifies $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_P$ and $V = (\partial G/\partial P)_T$ from the differential.
rubric: Equates the two mixed second derivatives to obtain the Maxwell relation, with the correct minus sign.
rubric: Replaces $(\partial V/\partial T)_P$ by $V\alpha$ and concludes $(\partial S/\partial P)_T < 0$ for $\alpha > 0$.
rubric: Uses $Q = T\Delta S$ to conclude that the compressed solid gives off heat.

@quiz num

Treat $V_m$ and $\alpha$ as constant. How much heat does it absorb, in joules? Give the sign; negative means heat leaves the metal.
answer: -41.4
check: quad(lambda P: 300*(-1.0e-5*6.9e-5), 0, 2.0e8)
unit: J
tol: 1%
hint: Use the Maxwell relation from $G$ to get $\Delta S$ from $\alpha$ and $V$.
