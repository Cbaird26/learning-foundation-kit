@article
### Indistinguishable quantum particles

When identical particles are exchanged, the many-particle wavefunction is either symmetric (**bosons**, integer spin) or antisymmetric (**fermions**, half-integer spin). For fermions this gives the Pauli exclusion principle: no two occupy the same single-particle state. Any number of bosons can share a state.

### The three occupation numbers

With $x = \beta(\varepsilon - \mu)$, the single-orbital results of week 9 are

$$\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD} = \frac{1}{e^{x}+1}, \qquad \langle n\rangle_{\rm BE} = \frac{1}{e^{x}-1}, \qquad \langle n\rangle_{\rm MB} = e^{-x}.$$

For the same $x>0$ they obey $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE} > \langle n\rangle_{\rm MB} > \langle n\rangle_{\rm FD}$: bosons tend to bunch and fermions to avoid each other. For $x\gg1$ all three agree with $e^{-x}$. As $x\to0^+$, $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE}\to 1/x$ diverges while $\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD}\to\tfrac12$; the Fermi–Dirac value never exceeds $1$. For bosons $\mu$ must lie below the lowest orbital energy so that $x>0$ for every orbital.

### Fixing $\mu$

Free particles in a box have the density of states (per energy), with spin degeneracy $g_s$,

$$g(\varepsilon) = \frac{g_sV}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}.$$

The chemical potential is set by $N = \int g(\varepsilon)\langle n(\varepsilon)\rangle\,d\varepsilon$, and the energy is $E = \int \varepsilon\,g\langle n\rangle\,d\varepsilon$.

### Fermions: the degenerate Fermi gas

At $T=0$ the Fermi–Dirac distribution is a step function: all orbitals below the **Fermi energy** $\varepsilon_F$ are full and the rest are empty. Setting $N = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}g\,d\varepsilon$ with $g_s = 2$ gives

$$\varepsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(3\pi^2 n\right)^{2/3}, \qquad E_0 = \tfrac35N\varepsilon_F, \qquad T_F = \frac{\varepsilon_F}{k_B}.$$

For $T\ll T_F$ the gas is degenerate: $\mu\approx\varepsilon_F$ and only electrons within about $k_BT$ of the Fermi level can change state. Conduction electrons in metals have $T_F\sim10^4$ to $10^5$ K, so they are degenerate even at room temperature.

### Bosons: condensation and photons

For a massive Bose gas with lowest orbital at zero energy, $\mu\le0$. On cooling at fixed density, $\mu$ rises to $0$, and the excited orbitals can hold at most $N_{\rm ex} = \zeta(3/2)\,V/\lambda_T^3$ particles, with $\zeta(3/2)\approx2.612$. The rest condense into the ground orbital. The condensation temperature satisfies $n\lambda_{T_c}^3 = 2.612$:

$$T_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{mk_B}\left(\frac{n}{2.612}\right)^{2/3}, \qquad \frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}\ (T<T_c).$$

Photons are bosons whose number is not conserved, so $\mu = 0$ and $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{\hbar\omega/k_BT}-1)$, the Planck distribution.

### Which statistics?

If $n\lambda_T^3\ll1$, every orbital is nearly empty and the three distributions coincide: use the classical gas. If $n\lambda_T^3\gtrsim1$ (low $T$, high density, small mass), quantum statistics matters, and fermions and bosons behave very differently.

@example
title: Comparing the three occupation numbers
problem: An orbital has $\varepsilon - \mu = k_BT$, then another has $\varepsilon-\mu = 5k_BT$. Compare the Fermi–Dirac, Maxwell–Boltzmann and Bose–Einstein occupations in each case.
step: For $x = 1$: $\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD} = 1/(e+1) = 0.269$, $\langle n\rangle_{\rm MB} = e^{-1} = 0.368$ and $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE} = 1/(e-1) = 0.582$. The order BE > MB > FD holds, and the ratio $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE}/\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD} = (e+1)/(e-1) = 2.16$.
step: For $x = 5$: $e^5 = 148.4$, so $\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD} = 0.00669$, $\langle n\rangle_{\rm MB} = 0.00674$ and $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE} = 0.00678$. They agree to within about $1.4\%$ of one another.
step: Conclusion: the quantum corrections matter only for orbitals within a few $k_BT$ of $\mu$. When every orbital has $x\gg1$, which is the condition $n\lambda_T^3\ll1$, the classical result is accurate.

@example
title: Fermi energy of conduction electrons in copper
problem: Copper has $n = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ conduction electrons ($g_s=2$). Find $\varepsilon_F$ and $T_F$, and compare $T_F$ with room temperature. Take $\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-34}$ J s, $m_e = 9.109\times10^{-31}$ kg and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}$ J.
step: Fill orbitals up to $\varepsilon_F$: $n = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}\frac{1}{2\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt\varepsilon\,d\varepsilon = \frac{1}{3\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m\varepsilon_F}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}$, so $\varepsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}$.
step: Numbers: $3\pi^2 n = 2.52\times10^{30}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, $(3\pi^2n)^{2/3} = 1.85\times10^{20}\ \text{m}^{-2}$ and $\hbar^2/2m = 6.10\times10^{-39}$ J m$^2$. So $\varepsilon_F = 1.13\times10^{-18}$ J $= 7.05$ eV.
step: The Fermi temperature is $T_F = \varepsilon_F/k_B = 1.13\times10^{-18}/1.381\times10^{-23} \approx 8.2\times10^{4}$ K.
step: At $300$ K, $T/T_F \approx 0.004$, so the electron gas is strongly degenerate. The mean energy at $T=0$, $\tfrac35\varepsilon_F \approx 4.2$ eV, is far larger than $k_BT\approx0.026$ eV.

@mistakes
- Allowing the Fermi–Dirac occupation to exceed $1$, or using the Bose–Einstein formula with $\mu$ above the lowest orbital energy, which gives negative occupations.
- Treating conduction electrons at $300$ K with classical statistics. Their $n\lambda_T^3$ is of order $10^{3}$.
- Forgetting the spin degeneracy $g_s=2$ in the density of states or in $\varepsilon_F$.
- Mixing energy units: $1$ eV corresponds to about $11\,600$ K, so a Fermi energy of a few eV is a temperature of tens of thousands of kelvin.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein occupation numbers $\langle n\rangle = 1/(e^{x}\pm1)$ with $x = \beta(\varepsilon-\mu)$. Describe their behavior as $x\to\infty$, near $x\to0^+$, and for the same $x>0$; explain the physical origin of the difference; and say what happens to $\mu$ for bosons.
hint: Expand both for large $x$, and for small $x$ keep the leading term of $e^{x}\mp1$.
hint: Think of the allowed occupation numbers of one orbital: $\{0,1\}$ for fermions and $\{0,1,2,\dots\}$ for bosons.
solution: For $x\gg1$, $e^x\gg1$, so both tend to $e^{-x}$, the Maxwell–Boltzmann value. For $x\to0^+$, $\langle n\rangle_{\rm FD}\to\tfrac12$ and $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE}\approx1/x\to\infty$, since $e^x-1\approx x$. For the same $x>0$, $\langle n\rangle_{\rm BE} > e^{-x} > \langle n\rangle_{\rm FD}$. Fermions obey the Pauli principle, so $n\le1$ and occupation is suppressed (statistical repulsion); bosons can pile into one orbital, which enhances occupation (statistical attraction). For bosons $\mu$ must stay below the lowest orbital energy, otherwise $\langle n\rangle$ would be negative or infinite; condensation occurs when $\mu$ reaches that energy.
rubric: States that both distributions reduce to $e^{-x}$ for $x\gg1$ and identifies this with the classical regime.
rubric: Gives the small-$x$ behavior: FD tends to $1/2$ and BE diverges like $1/x$.
rubric: Explains the difference via allowed occupation numbers: exclusion for fermions, bunching for bosons.
rubric: States that for bosons $\mu$ lies below the lowest orbital energy, with condensation as $\mu$ approaches it.

answer: 3.30
check: (lambda c: (lambda eF: quad(lambda e: e**1.5, 0, eF)/quad(lambda e: e**0.5, 0, eF))(root(lambda E: quad(lambda e: c*math.sqrt(e), 0, E) - 5.86e28, 0.1, 100)))((1/(2*math.pi**2))*(2*9.109e-31*1.602e-19/1.0546e-34**2)**1.5)
unit: eV
tol: 1%
hint: At $T=0$ the energy is $E_0 = \int_0^{\varepsilon_F}\varepsilon\,g\,d\varepsilon$ and the mean per electron is $E_0/N$.
hint: Since $g\propto\sqrt\varepsilon$, $E_0/N = \tfrac35\varepsilon_F$; first compute $\varepsilon_F = (\hbar^2/2m)(3\pi^2n)^{2/3} = 5.50$ eV.
