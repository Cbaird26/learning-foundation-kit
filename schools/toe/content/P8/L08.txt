@article
An ideal gas is $N$ non-interacting particles in a volume $V$ whose energy is purely kinetic. It is the classical, dilute limit of every real gas, and the first place where the machinery of the last three weeks pays off.

### One particle in a box

At high temperature the sum over a particle's states becomes an integral over position and momentum, with one state per phase-space cell of volume $h^3$:

$$z = \frac{1}{h^3}\int d^3r\,d^3p\;e^{-\beta p^2/2m} = \frac{V}{h^3}\left(\frac{2\pi m}{\beta}\right)^{3/2} = \frac{V}{\lambda_T^3}, \qquad \lambda_T = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}} .$$

The **thermal de Broglie wavelength** $\lambda_T$ is the quantum length scale of a particle at temperature $T$.

### $N$ particles and the factor $1/N!$

Identical particles are indistinguishable: swapping two does not make a new microstate. In a dilute gas almost all states have every particle in a different single-particle state, so distinguishable counting overcounts by exactly $N!$:

$$Z_N = \frac{z^N}{N!}.$$

Without this Gibbs factor the entropy would not be extensive, which is the Gibbs paradox.

### Everything from $\ln Z_N$

With Stirling's approximation, $\ln Z_N = N\ln V - \tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\beta + \text{const} - N\ln N + N$, and the free energy is

$$F = -k_BT\ln Z_N = -Nk_BT\left[\ln\frac{V}{N\lambda_T^3} + 1\right].$$

- Pressure: $P = -\left(\partial F/\partial V\right)_T = Nk_BT/V$.
- Mean energy: $\langle E\rangle = -\partial\ln Z_N/\partial\beta = \tfrac32Nk_BT$, so $C_V = \tfrac32Nk_B$.
- Entropy (Sackur–Tetrode): $S = -\left(\partial F/\partial T\right)_V = Nk_B\left[\ln\dfrac{V}{N\lambda_T^3} + \dfrac52\right]$.
- Chemical potential: $\mu = \left(\partial F/\partial N\right)_{T,V} = k_BT\ln\left(n\lambda_T^3\right)$ with $n = N/V$.

### Energy fluctuations

The variance is $\partial^2\ln Z_N/\partial\beta^2 = \tfrac{3N}{2\beta^2}$, equivalently $k_BT^2C_V$. Therefore

$$\frac{\sigma_E}{\langle E\rangle} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3N}} .$$

### When is the classical gas valid?

The classical treatment requires that thermal wave packets do not overlap: the mean spacing $n^{-1/3}$ must greatly exceed $\lambda_T$, i.e.

$$n\lambda_T^3 \ll 1 \qquad(\text{equivalently } \mu \ll 0).$$

For air at room temperature and 1 atm, $n\lambda_T^3 \sim 10^{-7}$. For conduction electrons in a metal, or for helium liquefied at a few kelvin, $n\lambda_T^3 \gtrsim 1$: quantum statistics is required (weeks 9 and 10). Cooling, compressing, or using light particles all push $n\lambda_T^3$ upward.

@example
title: Is helium at room conditions classical?
problem: Helium ($m = 6.646\times10^{-27}$ kg) is at $300$ K and $101\,325$ Pa. Take $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s and $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K. Find $\lambda_T$ and test whether the gas is classical.
step: The thermal wavelength is $\lambda_T = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT} = \dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{1.730\times10^{-46}}} = \dfrac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{1.315\times10^{-23}} = 5.04\times10^{-11}$ m.
step: The number density is $n = P/k_BT = 101\,325/(1.381\times10^{-23}\times300) = 2.45\times10^{25}\ \text{m}^{-3}$.
step: Then $n\lambda_T^3 = (2.45\times10^{25})(5.04\times10^{-11})^3 \approx 3.1\times10^{-6} \ll 1$, so the classical treatment is excellent.
step: Equivalent view: the mean spacing is $n^{-1/3} = 3.4\times10^{-9}$ m, about $68$ times $\lambda_T$.

@example
title: The temperature where classical statistics fails
problem: Liquid helium-4 has number density $n \approx 2.18\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$. Estimate the temperature at which $n\lambda_T^3 = 1$.
step: Setting $n\lambda_T^3 = 1$ means $\lambda_T = n^{-1/3}$: the thermal wavelength equals the mean particle spacing.
step: Substituting $\lambda_T^2 = h^2/(2\pi mk_BT)$ gives $T = \dfrac{h^2 n^{2/3}}{2\pi m k_B}$.
step: Numerically $h^2 = 4.390\times10^{-67}$, $n^{2/3} = 7.80\times10^{18}$ and $2\pi mk_B = 5.77\times10^{-49}$ in SI units, so $T \approx 5.9$ K.
step: Interpretation: below a few kelvin helium at liquid density cannot be treated as a classical gas, which is why quantum statistics (and not the Maxwell–Boltzmann gas) governs it there.

@mistakes
- Leaving out $1/N!$, which makes $S$ non-extensive: doubling $N$ and $V$ would not double $S$.
- Applying classical formulas when $n\lambda_T^3$ is not small. Low temperature, high density and small mass all make the classical gas fail.
- Mixing up $h$ and $\hbar$ in $\lambda_T = h/\sqrt{2\pi mk_BT}$, or putting the temperature in Celsius.
- Taking the logarithm of a quantity with units: $V/(N\lambda_T^3)$ is dimensionless only if all three factors use consistent SI units.

@practice self
prompt: A classical monatomic ideal gas of $N$ atoms has $Z_N = \dfrac{1}{N!}\left(\dfrac{V}{h^3}\right)^N\left(\dfrac{2\pi m}{\beta}\right)^{3N/2}$. Derive the mean energy $\langle E\rangle$ and the variance of the energy. Show that $C_V = \tfrac32Nk_B$ and that $\sigma_E/\langle E\rangle = \sqrt{2/(3N)}$, then evaluate this for $N = 10^{22}$.
hint: Only the $\beta$-dependence of $\ln Z_N$ matters for energy: $\ln Z_N = -\tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\beta + \text{terms independent of }\beta$.
hint: Differentiate once for $\langle E\rangle$, twice for the variance, and use $d\beta = -dT/(k_BT^2)$ to get $C_V$.
solution: $\langle E\rangle = -\partial_\beta\ln Z_N = \dfrac{3N}{2\beta} = \tfrac32Nk_BT$. The variance is $\partial_\beta^2\ln Z_N = \dfrac{3N}{2\beta^2} = \tfrac32N(k_BT)^2$. Using variance $= k_BT^2C_V$ gives $C_V = \tfrac32Nk_B$, consistent with $d\langle E\rangle/dT$. The ratio is $\sigma_E/\langle E\rangle = \dfrac{\sqrt{3N/2}\,k_BT}{\tfrac32Nk_BT} = \sqrt{\dfrac{2}{3N}}$. For $N = 10^{22}$ this is $8.2\times10^{-12}$.
rubric: Identifies that only the $-\tfrac{3N}{2}\ln\beta$ term of $\ln Z_N$ contributes to the energy derivatives.
rubric: Obtains $\langle E\rangle = \tfrac32Nk_BT$ from the first derivative and the variance $\tfrac{3N}{2\beta^2}$ from the second.
rubric: Connects the variance to $k_BT^2C_V$ and states $C_V = \tfrac32Nk_B$.
rubric: Derives $\sqrt{2/(3N)}$ and evaluates it for $N=10^{22}$, about $8\times10^{-12}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about the classical monatomic ideal gas.
choice*: Including the factor $1/N!$ makes the entropy extensive.
choice*: At fixed $V$ and $N$, the Sackur–Tetrode entropy increases with temperature.
choice: The classical treatment is justified when $n\lambda_T^3 \gg 1$.
choice*: At fixed density, lowering the temperature makes the classical approximation less accurate.
choice: The mean energy $\tfrac32Nk_BT$ depends on the mass of the atoms.
hint: Check how $\lambda_T$ changes with $T$ and what the sign of $\mu = k_BT\ln(n\lambda_T^3)$ must be for a classical gas.
solution: $S$ is extensive only with $1/N!$. Since $\lambda_T\propto T^{-1/2}$, $S$ contains $\tfrac32Nk_B\ln T$ and rises with $T$. The classical condition is $n\lambda_T^3 \ll 1$, and lowering $T$ increases $\lambda_T$ and so increases $n\lambda_T^3$. The mean energy is independent of mass, as equipartition requires.
