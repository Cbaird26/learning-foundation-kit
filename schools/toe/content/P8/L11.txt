@article
### Heat capacity from fluctuations

The heat capacity measures how much energy a system absorbs per unit temperature rise. From the canonical ensemble,

$$C_V = \frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial T} = k_B\beta^2\frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2}{k_BT^2}.$$

A large heat capacity means large energy fluctuations: many states are accessible within $k_BT$.

### Equipartition and freezing out

Classically each quadratic term in the energy contributes $\tfrac12k_B$ per particle, so $C_V = \tfrac f2Nk_B$. Quantum mechanically a motion with level spacing $\Delta$ is **active** only when $k_BT\gtrsim\Delta$; for $k_BT\ll\Delta$ it is **frozen out** and contributes almost nothing. A diatomic gas such as $\text{N}_2$ has $C_V = \tfrac52 Nk_B$ at room temperature: three translations and two rotations are active, while the vibration, with a spacing equivalent to thousands of kelvin, is frozen. $C_V$ climbs in steps as $T$ increases.

### The two-level (Schottky) system

For $N$ independent two-level systems with spacing $\epsilon$, $\langle E\rangle = N\epsilon/(e^{x}+1)$ with $x = \epsilon/k_BT$. Using $dx/dT = -x/T$,

$$C = Nk_B\,\frac{x^2e^{x}}{(e^{x}+1)^2}.$$

At low $T$ this vanishes exponentially, like $x^2e^{-x}$, since the upper level is hardly populated. At high $T$ it falls as $\tfrac14 Nk_Bx^2\propto1/T^2$, because the energy saturates at its infinite-temperature value, $N\epsilon/2$. Between these limits there is a maximum, the **Schottky anomaly**, of height $0.439\,Nk_B$ at $k_BT\approx0.42\,\epsilon$. A bounded energy spectrum always gives such a peak.

### The Einstein solid

Treat $N$ atoms as $3N$ independent oscillators of frequency $\omega$. With $x = \hbar\omega/k_BT = \Theta_E/T$,

$$\langle E\rangle = 3N\hbar\omega\left(\tfrac12 + \frac{1}{e^{x}-1}\right), \qquad C_V = 3Nk_B\,\frac{x^2e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^2}.$$

For $T\gg\Theta_E$ this tends to $3Nk_B = 3R$ per mole (**Dulong–Petit**, $24.9$ J/(mol K)). For $T\ll\Theta_E$ it vanishes exponentially. Real solids differ at low $T$ in two ways.

- **Phonons** (Debye model): sound waves of arbitrarily low frequency exist, so $C_V = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}Nk_B\,(T/\Theta_D)^3\propto T^3$.
- **Metals** add an electron term, $C_{\rm el} = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\,T/T_F\propto T$, because only a fraction $\sim T/T_F$ of the electrons lie within $k_BT$ of the Fermi level. At low $T$ the combined form is $C = \gamma T + bT^3$.

All of these obey the third law: $C\to0$ as $T\to0$.

### Reading a $C(T)$ curve

1. **Plateaus** tell you which degrees of freedom are active.
2. **A peak or step** sits where $k_BT$ is comparable to an energy scale of the system.
3. **The low-$T$ law** identifies the carriers: exponential means a gap, linear means a Fermi gas, cubic means phonons.
4. **Areas** give state-function changes: $\Delta E = \int C\,dT$ and $\Delta S = \int (C/T)\,dT$.

@example
title: Heat capacity of a two-level system
problem: $N$ two-level systems have spacing $\epsilon$ with $\epsilon/k_B = 10.0$ K. Find $C/(Nk_B)$ at $T = 5.00$ K, and compare it with the maximum possible value.
step: The mean energy per system is $\epsilon/(e^{x}+1)$ with $x = \epsilon/k_BT$. Differentiating with $dx/dT = -x/T$ gives $C/(Nk_B) = x^2e^{x}/(e^{x}+1)^2$.
step: At $T = 5.00$ K, $x = 10.0/5.00 = 2.00$ and $e^{2} = 7.389$.
step: Then $C/(Nk_B) = \dfrac{4(7.389)}{(8.389)^2} = \dfrac{29.56}{70.38} \approx 0.420$.
step: The maximum is $0.439$ at $x\approx2.40$, i.e. $T\approx4.2$ K. At $5$ K we are just on the high-temperature side of the peak, so $C$ is slightly below its maximum and will keep falling like $1/T^2$.

@example
title: Heat capacity of an Einstein solid
problem: An Einstein solid has $\Theta_E = 300$ K. Find its molar heat capacity at $T = 150$ K and compare it with the Dulong–Petit value. Take $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K).
step: There are $3N_A$ oscillators per mole, so $C_V = 3R\,x^2e^{x}/(e^{x}-1)^2$ with $x = \Theta_E/T = 2$.
step: At $x=2$: $x^2e^{x} = 4(7.389) = 29.56$ and $(e^x-1)^2 = (6.389)^2 = 40.82$, so the factor is $0.724$.
step: Then $C_V = 0.724\times3R = 0.724\times24.94 \approx 18.1$ J/(mol K).
step: This is $72\%$ of the Dulong–Petit value $3R = 24.9$ J/(mol K), as expected for $T = \Theta_E/2$: the oscillators are only partly active.

@mistakes
- Counting every degree of freedom at every temperature. A vibration with $\hbar\omega\gg k_BT$ contributes almost nothing.
- Mixing up $R$ and $k_B$ (per mole versus per particle), or $C_V$ and $C_P$. For an ideal gas $C_P - C_V = Nk_B$.
- Expecting the Schottky heat capacity to keep rising. A bounded spectrum forces $C\to0$ as $T\to\infty$ as well as $T\to0$.
- Differentiating with respect to $\beta$ without the chain-rule factor: $C_V = -k_B\beta^2\,\partial\langle E\rangle/\partial\beta$.

@practice self
prompt: The low-temperature heat capacity of a metal is plotted as $C/T$ against $T^2$ and the points fall on a straight line. Explain what physical contributions give this form, what the intercept and slope represent, and what you expect for the curve at much higher temperature.
hint: Write the total as a sum of an electron term and a phonon term, then divide by $T$.
hint: At high temperature recall the Dulong–Petit limit and the Einstein or Debye temperature.
solution: At low $T$, $C = \gamma T + bT^3$. The linear term comes from the degenerate electron gas, where only a fraction $\sim T/T_F$ of electrons can absorb energy, with $\gamma = \frac{\pi^2}{2}\frac{Nk_B}{T_F}$. The cubic term comes from phonons (Debye), with $b = \frac{12\pi^4}{5}\frac{Nk_B}{\Theta_D^3}$. Dividing by $T$ gives $C/T = \gamma + bT^2$, a straight line in $T^2$: the intercept is $\gamma$, which gives $T_F$ or the electron density of states, and the slope is $b$, which gives $\Theta_D$. At much higher temperature, $T\gg\Theta_D$, the phonon term saturates at the Dulong–Petit value $3Nk_B$, and the electron term is a small correction, so the $T^3$ rise levels off.

rubric: Shows $C/T = \gamma + bT^2$ and reads the intercept as $\gamma$ and the slope as $b$.
rubric: Relates $\gamma$ to the Fermi temperature or density of states and $b$ to the Debye temperature.
rubric: States that at high $T$ the lattice heat capacity approaches $3Nk_B$ (Dulong–Petit).
