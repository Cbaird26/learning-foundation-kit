@article
### What is a phase transition?

Water boils; iron loses its magnetization above the Curie temperature. A **phase transition** is a singularity, a non-analytic point, in the free energy as a function of $T$, $P$ or an applied field. It is classified by the lowest derivative of $G$ that misbehaves.

- **First-order:** first derivatives jump. The entropy $S = -(\partial G/\partial T)_P$ changes by $\Delta S = L/T$, where $L$ is the latent heat, and the volume jumps too.
- **Continuous:** first derivatives are continuous, but second derivatives (heat capacity, susceptibility) jump or diverge. An **order parameter** grows continuously from zero.

### Coexistence and Clausius–Clapeyron

Two phases coexist when their Gibbs energies per particle are equal. Along the coexistence line the equal changes $d\mu_1 = d\mu_2$ give $-s_1dT + v_1dP = -s_2dT + v_2dP$, so

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{s_2 - s_1}{v_2 - v_1} = \frac{L}{T\,\Delta v}.$$

Use kelvin, and keep $L$ and $\Delta v$ both per unit mass or both per mole. Ice melts at lower $T$ under pressure because $\Delta v<0$ for melting, so $dP/dT<0$.

### Landau theory

Near a continuous transition, expand the free energy density in the order parameter $m$ for a system symmetric under $m\to-m$:

$$f(m) = f_0 + a(T-T_c)\,m^2 + b\,m^4, \qquad a, b > 0.$$

Setting $\partial f/\partial m = 0$: for $T>T_c$ the only minimum is $m = 0$; for $T<T_c$ the quadratic term is negative and there are two minima,

$$m_0 = \pm\sqrt{\frac{a(T_c-T)}{2b}}, \qquad f_{\min} - f_0 = -\frac{a^2(T_c-T)^2}{4b}.$$

The order parameter grows as $(T_c-T)^{1/2}$, the entropy is continuous, and the heat capacity jumps by $\Delta C = T_ca^2/(2b)$. The system must choose one of two equivalent states: spontaneous symmetry breaking.

### Finite size

For a finite system the partition function is a finite sum of exponentials in $\beta$, which is analytic. So no singularity occurs at any finite $N$: peaks in $C(T)$ are rounded, have finite height and are shifted. As $N$ grows they sharpen; the strict singularity appears only in the **thermodynamic limit**. A finite magnet below $T_c$ also has $\langle m\rangle = 0$ exactly, because it flips between the two wells at a rate $\propto e^{-N\Delta f/k_BT}$. One uses $\langle |m|\rangle$ or $\langle m^2\rangle$ instead.

### The one-dimensional Ising chain

Take $N$ spins $s_i = \pm1$ with $E = -J\sum_{i=1}^{N-1}s_is_{i+1}$ (open ends). Each bond variable $s_is_{i+1}$ is an independent $\pm1$, so

$$Z = 2\,(2\cosh\beta J)^{N-1}, \qquad \langle E\rangle = -(N-1)J\tanh\beta J, \qquad C = (N-1)k_B(\beta J)^2\,\mathrm{sech}^2(\beta J).$$

This has a smooth, Schottky-like maximum near $k_BT\approx0.83\,J$ for any $N$, and no singularity at $T>0$: with short-range interactions, one dimension has no ordered phase.

@example
title: How much does the boiling point shift?
problem: Water boils at $100.0\,^\circ$C ($373.15$ K) at $101.325$ kPa, with $L = 2.26\times10^{6}$ J/kg and a specific-volume change $\Delta v = 1.672\ \text{m}^3/\text{kg}$ on vaporization. Estimate the boiling temperature at $95.0$ kPa.
step: Clausius–Clapeyron gives the slope of the coexistence line: $\dfrac{dP}{dT} = \dfrac{L}{T\Delta v} = \dfrac{2.26\times10^{6}}{(373.15)(1.672)} \approx 3.62\times10^{3}$ Pa/K.
step: The pressure change is $\Delta P = 95.0 - 101.325 = -6.325$ kPa.
step: For a small change, $\Delta T \approx \Delta P/(dP/dT) = -6325/3622 \approx -1.75$ K.
step: The boiling point is about $100.0 - 1.75 \approx 98.3\,^\circ$C. The linear estimate is accurate for a small $\Delta P$ like this one.

@example
title: Analyzing a Landau free energy
problem: A system has $f(m) = a(T-T_c)m^2 + bm^4$ (taking $f_0=0$) with $a = 0.50$, $b = 2.0$ and $T_c = 300$ in SI units. Find the order parameter, the free-energy density at the minimum, and the heat-capacity jump, at $T = 292$.
step: Set $\partial f/\partial m = 2a(T-T_c)m + 4bm^3 = 0$. Either $m=0$ or $m^2 = a(T_c-T)/(2b)$.
step: At $T = 292$: $m^2 = (0.50)(8)/(2\times2.0) = 1.0$, so $m_0 = \pm1.0$.
step: Check that this beats $m=0$: $f(1) = (0.50)(-8)(1) + 2.0(1) = -4 + 2 = -2.0 < f(0) = 0$. This agrees with $-a^2(T_c-T)^2/(4b) = -0.25(64)/8 = -2.0$.
step: The heat-capacity jump at $T_c$ is $\Delta C = T_ca^2/(2b) = (300)(0.25)/4 = 18.75$ (in the units of the problem): a jump, not a divergence, and the entropy is continuous.

@mistakes
- Taking a smooth peak in the $C(T)$ of a small or finite system as proof of a phase transition. A Schottky bump or the Ising-chain maximum has no singularity.
- Using Celsius temperatures in Clausius–Clapeyron, or mixing a per-mass $L$ with a per-mole $\Delta v$.
- Expecting exact singularities at finite $N$. They exist only in the thermodynamic limit.
- Using Landau theory with $b<0$ (the quartic term must be positive for stability), or forgetting that the sign change of the quadratic coefficient at $T_c$ drives the transition.

@practice self
prompt: The heat capacity of an open Ising chain of $N$ spins is $C = (N-1)k_B(\beta J)^2\,\mathrm{sech}^2(\beta J)$. (a) Describe the curve $C(T)$: its limits at low and high $T$ and the location of its maximum. (b) Does the maximum signal a phase transition? (c) How would a peak in $C(T)$ from a true continuous transition in a large system differ from this one, and how would the peak of a small finite system differ from that of the infinite system?
hint: For (a), write $x = \beta J$ and examine $x^2\,\mathrm{sech}^2x$ for $x\to0$ and $x\to\infty$.
hint: For (b) and (c), recall what the partition function of a finite system is, and what happens to the peak height and width as $N$ increases at a true transition.
solution: (a) With $x = J/k_BT$, $C/[(N-1)k_B] = x^2\mathrm{sech}^2x$. As $T\to0$ ($x\to\infty$) it falls exponentially, like $4x^2e^{-2x}$. As $T\to\infty$ ($x\to0$) it falls as $x^2\propto1/T^2$. In between there is one smooth maximum of $0.439$ per bond near $k_BT\approx0.83J$, where $x\tanh x = 1$. This is a Schottky-type bump from the energy cost $2J$ of flipping a bond. (b) No. The chain has no singularity at $T>0$ for any $N$: $Z = 2(2\cosh\beta J)^{N-1}$ is analytic in $\beta$ and the correlations decay exponentially, so there is no long-range order. The height of the maximum is proportional to $N-1$ only because $C$ is extensive; it is not growing faster than $N$. (c) At a true continuous transition in the thermodynamic limit the peak would be singular, a jump or a divergence at $T_c$, and in finite systems the peak height would grow faster than linearly in $N$ (for a divergence) while the width shrinks as $N$ grows. A small finite system has a rounded, finite, shifted peak.

rubric: Explains that singularities appear only in the thermodynamic limit, and that finite-$N$ peaks are rounded, of finite height and shifted.
rubric: Notes that a peak at a true transition sharpens as $N$ increases (it narrows, and its height per particle grows if $C$ diverges), unlike the smooth chain maximum.
