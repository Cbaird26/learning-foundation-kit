@article
### A system and a heat bath

Put a small system S in thermal contact with a very large bath R. Together they are isolated with total energy $E_0$. If S is in microstate $i$ with energy $E_i$, the bath has energy $E_0 - E_i$ and $\Omega_R(E_0 - E_i)$ microstates. By the fundamental postulate, $p_i \propto \Omega_R(E_0 - E_i)$. The bath is so large that $E_i$ is a small change for it, so expand the logarithm:

$$\ln\Omega_R(E_0 - E_i) \approx \ln\Omega_R(E_0) - E_i\,\frac{\partial\ln\Omega_R}{\partial E} = \text{const} - \frac{E_i}{k_BT},$$

because $\partial\ln\Omega/\partial E = \partial(S/k_B)/\partial E = 1/k_BT$. Hence $p_i = e^{-\beta E_i}/Z$. A low-energy state is favored because it leaves more microstates to the bath, not because the system "prefers" low energy.

### Distribution of energy

The probability that S has energy $E$ (any of its microstates) is

$$P(E) = \frac{\Omega(E)\,e^{-\beta E}}{Z}.$$

The multiplicity $\Omega(E)$ rises steeply with $E$ while the Boltzmann factor falls. Their product has a sharp peak near $\langle E\rangle$.

### Mean energy and fluctuations

Since $\partial_\beta Z = -Z\langle E\rangle$ and $\partial_\beta^2 Z = Z\langle E^2\rangle$,

$$\langle E\rangle = -\frac{\partial\ln Z}{\partial\beta}, \qquad \langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \frac{\partial^2\ln Z}{\partial\beta^2} = -\frac{\partial\langle E\rangle}{\partial\beta} = k_BT^2C_V .$$

The last step uses $d\beta = -dT/(k_BT^2)$. So $C_V > 0$ is the statement that variances are positive. Both $\langle E\rangle$ and the variance grow in proportion to $N$, so $\sigma_E/\langle E\rangle \sim 1/\sqrt N$: for a macroscopic system the canonical and microcanonical descriptions are practically the same.

### Equipartition

If the energy contains a term $aq^2$ in a classical variable $q$, then

$$\langle aq^2\rangle = \frac{\int aq^2 e^{-\beta aq^2}dq}{\int e^{-\beta aq^2}dq} = \frac{1}{2\beta} = \tfrac12 k_BT .$$

Each quadratic term contributes $\tfrac12k_BT$. This holds only when $k_BT$ is much larger than the spacing of quantum levels for that motion.

### The quantum harmonic oscillator

With $E_n = (n+\tfrac12)\hbar\omega$,

$$Z = \frac{e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}, \qquad \langle E\rangle = \hbar\omega\left(\frac12 + \frac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}\right).$$

For $k_BT\gg\hbar\omega$ this tends to $k_BT$ (two quadratic terms), and for $k_BT\ll\hbar\omega$ it tends to the zero-point energy $\hbar\omega/2$.

### The spin-$\tfrac12$ paramagnet

A moment with energies $\mp\mu B$ has $z = 2\cosh(\beta\mu B)$, so $\langle\varepsilon\rangle = -\mu B\tanh(\beta\mu B)$ and the average moment along the field is $\mu\tanh(\beta\mu B)$.

@example
title: Energy and fluctuations of a paramagnet
problem: $N$ independent spin-$\tfrac12$ moments sit in a field $B$ at a temperature with $\beta\mu B = 1$. Find $\langle E\rangle$, the energy variance, and the relative size of the fluctuations.
step: One spin has levels $\pm\mu B$, so $z = e^{\beta\mu B} + e^{-\beta\mu B} = 2\cosh(\beta\mu B)$ and $\ln z = \ln 2\cosh(\beta\mu B)$.
step: The mean energy is $\langle E\rangle = -N\,\partial_\beta\ln z = -N\mu B\tanh(1) \approx -0.762\,N\mu B$.
step: The variance of one spin is $\partial_\beta^2\ln z = (\mu B)^2\,\mathrm{sech}^2(1) = 0.420\,(\mu B)^2$. Independent variances add, so $\sigma_E^2 = 0.420\,N(\mu B)^2$.
step: The relative size is $\sigma_E/|\langle E\rangle| = \sqrt{0.420N}/(0.762N) = 0.851/\sqrt N$. For $N = 10^{22}$ this is about $9\times10^{-12}$, so the energy is effectively sharp.

@example
title: Oscillator energy when $k_BT = \hbar\omega$
problem: A quantum harmonic oscillator is at a temperature with $k_BT = \hbar\omega$. Find its mean energy and compare it with the classical equipartition value.
step: Here $\beta\hbar\omega = 1$. The mean energy is $\langle E\rangle = \hbar\omega\left[\tfrac12 + 1/(e^{1} - 1)\right]$.
step: Numerically, $1/(e-1) = 0.5820$, so $\langle E\rangle = 1.0820\,\hbar\omega$.
step: The classical value is $2\times\tfrac12k_BT = k_BT = 1.000\,\hbar\omega$. The quantum result is $8\%$ larger, because zero-point energy adds $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ while discreteness removes some thermal energy.
step: At lower temperature the discrepancy grows: for $k_BT \ll \hbar\omega$ the oscillator is frozen near $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$, far from the classical $k_BT$.

@mistakes
- Using $e^{-\beta E}/Z$ as the probability of an energy $E$. That is the probability of one microstate; the probability of the energy must include the multiplicity $\Omega(E)$.
- Applying equipartition ($k_BT$ per oscillator) when $k_BT \lesssim \hbar\omega$, where quantization matters.
- Mixing up the variance $\sigma_E^2 = k_BT^2C_V$ with the standard deviation $\sigma_E$.
- Differentiating with respect to $T$ when a formula is written for $\beta$, and forgetting the factor $-k_BT^2$ from $d\beta = -dT/(k_BT^2)$.

@practice mc
prompt: In the derivation of the canonical distribution, why does a lower-energy microstate $i$ of the system have higher probability?
choice*: Because when the system has less energy, the bath has more energy and therefore more accessible microstates.
choice: Because every physical system minimizes its energy at all temperatures.
choice: Because the bath always has a fixed number of microstates regardless of the system's energy.
choice: Because lower-energy states of the system have larger multiplicity $\Omega(E)$.
hint: Count the combined microstates: the system is in a specific state $i$, and the bath can be in any state compatible with energy $E_0 - E_i$.
solution: With the system fixed in microstate $i$, the number of combined microstates is $\Omega_R(E_0 - E_i)$, which is larger when $E_i$ is smaller because $\Omega_R$ increases with energy. Equal probability of combined microstates then gives $p_i \propto e^{-\beta E_i}$. The multiplicity of the system's own energy levels does not enter the probability of a single microstate.

@practice num
prompt: A spin-$\tfrac12$ moment in a magnetic field has energies $\mp\mu B$, with $\mu B/k_BT = 0.500$. What is its average moment along the field, in units of $\mu$? Give four decimal places.
answer: 0.4621
check: (math.exp(0.5) - math.exp(-0.5))/(math.exp(0.5) + math.exp(-0.5))
tol: 0.0005
hint: The moment is $+\mu$ in the lower-energy state and $-\mu$ in the upper one.
hint: Weight the two states by $e^{\pm\beta\mu B}$: $\langle m\rangle/\mu = \tanh(\beta\mu B)$.
solution: $p_{\text{low}} = \dfrac{e^{0.5}}{e^{0.5} + e^{-0.5}} = 0.7311$ and $p_{\text{high}} = 0.2689$. So $\langle m\rangle/\mu = 0.7311 - 0.2689 = \tanh(0.5) \approx 0.4621$.

@practice num
prompt: A block of metal at $T = 300$ K has constant-volume heat capacity $C_V = 25.0$ J/K. Take $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K. If it is in thermal contact with a heat bath, what is the rms energy fluctuation $\sigma_E$, in joules?
answer: 5.57e-9
check: 300*math.sqrt(1.381e-23*25.0)
unit: J
tol: 1%
hint: The variance of the energy is $\sigma_E^2 = k_BT^2C_V$.
hint: $\sigma_E = T\sqrt{k_BC_V}$.
solution: $\sigma_E = (300)\sqrt{(1.381\times10^{-23})(25.0)} = 300\sqrt{3.45\times10^{-22}} \approx 5.57\times10^{-9}$ J. Compared with $U \sim C_VT \approx 7500$ J, the relative fluctuation is about $10^{-12}$.

@practice num
prompt: A single two-level system has levels at $0$ and $\epsilon$ and is at a temperature with $k_BT = \epsilon$. What is the variance of its energy, $\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2$, in units of $\epsilon^2$? Give four decimal places.
answer: 0.1966
check: nderiv(lambda b: math.log(1 + math.exp(-b)), 1.0, 2)
tol: 0.0005
hint: Use $\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2 = \partial^2\ln Z/\partial\beta^2$, or compute both averages directly from $p_1 = e^{-1}/(1 + e^{-1})$.
hint: For a two-level system the variance is $p(1-p)\,\epsilon^2$ where $p$ is the upper-level probability.
solution: $p = \dfrac{e^{-1}}{1 + e^{-1}} = 0.2689$. Then $\langle E\rangle = 0.2689\,\epsilon$, $\langle E^2\rangle = 0.2689\,\epsilon^2$, and the variance is $0.2689 - 0.0723 = 0.1966\,\epsilon^2$, which equals $p(1-p)\epsilon^2$.

@practice expr
prompt: A quantum harmonic oscillator has levels $E_n = (n + \tfrac12)\hbar\omega$ for $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$. Write its mean energy as a function of $\hbar$, $\omega$ and $\beta = 1/k_BT$.
vars: hbar, omega, beta
domain: hbar: 0.8..1.5; omega: 0.8..1.5; beta: 0.5..2
lead: $\langle E\rangle =$
answer: hbar*omega*(1/2 + 1/(exp(beta*hbar*omega) - 1))
check: lambda hbar, omega, beta: -nderiv(lambda b: log(nsum(lambda n: exp(-b*hbar*omega*(n + 0.5)), 0, 400)), beta)
hint: Sum the geometric series for $Z$, then use $\langle E\rangle = -\partial_\beta\ln Z$.
hint: $Z = e^{-\beta\hbar\omega/2}/(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})$, so $\ln Z = -\tfrac12\beta\hbar\omega - \ln(1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega})$.
solution: $-\partial_\beta\ln Z = \tfrac12\hbar\omega + \dfrac{\hbar\omega\,e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}}{1 - e^{-\beta\hbar\omega}} = \hbar\omega\left(\tfrac12 + \dfrac{1}{e^{\beta\hbar\omega} - 1}\right)$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about the canonical ensemble.
choice*: The variance of the energy equals $k_BT^2C_V$.
choice: The probability that the system has energy $E$ is proportional to $e^{-\beta E}$, whatever the number of microstates at that energy.
choice*: A classical degree of freedom with energy $aq^2$ has mean energy $\tfrac12k_BT$.
choice*: For a macroscopic system with $N$ particles the relative energy fluctuation is of order $1/\sqrt N$.
choice: At low temperature the mean energy of a quantum harmonic oscillator approaches $k_BT$.
hint: Separate the probability of a microstate from the probability of an energy.
hint: At low temperature the oscillator is nearly in its ground state.
solution: The variance formula follows from $\partial_\beta^2\ln Z$. The energy distribution is $\Omega(E)e^{-\beta E}/Z$, so the multiplicity matters. Equipartition gives $\tfrac12k_BT$ per quadratic term. Variance and mean energy are both extensive, so the relative fluctuation scales as $1/\sqrt N$. At low temperature the oscillator approaches its zero-point energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$, not $k_BT$.
