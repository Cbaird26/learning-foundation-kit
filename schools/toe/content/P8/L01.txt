@article
Pick up a metal spoon and a wooden spoon that have sat on the same table. The metal feels colder, yet a thermometer reads the same for both. Your skin senses how fast heat leaves your hand, not temperature. **Temperature** is the quantity that decides whether heat will flow at all.

### Thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law

Two systems are in **thermal equilibrium** if, when placed in thermal contact, neither changes. The **zeroth law of thermodynamics** says this relation is transitive: if A is in equilibrium with C and B is in equilibrium with C, then A is in equilibrium with B. That is what makes a thermometer meaningful. System C is the thermometer, and "same reading" means "same temperature."

### Temperature scales

The kelvin scale is absolute: $T = 0$ is the lowest temperature a system can approach. The Celsius scale is just shifted:

$$T/\text{K} = T_C/{}^\circ\text{C} + 273.15 .$$

Temperature **differences** are equal in kelvin and Celsius, but every formula below that contains $T$ itself needs kelvin. A constant-volume gas thermometer defines $T = 273.16\ \text{K}\times P/P_{tp}$, where $P_{tp}$ is the pressure at the triple point of water.

### The ideal gas and what temperature means

For a dilute gas of $N$ molecules (or $n$ moles),

$$PV = Nk_BT = nRT, \qquad R = N_A k_B .$$

Here $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K is the Boltzmann constant and $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K). Kinetic theory shows that the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule depends only on temperature:

$$\left\langle \tfrac12 m v^2\right\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT, \qquad v_{\rm rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_BT}{m}} .$$

Quadrupling $T$ only doubles $v_{\rm rms}$, because energy goes as $T$ and speed as $\sqrt T$. This is a first hint of the statistical meaning of $T$ that we build in the later weeks.

### Heat

**Heat** $Q$ is energy transferred between systems because of a temperature difference. It describes a process: you can add heat to a body, but a body does not "contain" heat. In this course $Q > 0$ means energy flowing **into** the system.

With no phase change, a body's temperature change is related to heat by

$$Q = C\,\Delta T = mc\,\Delta T,$$

where $C$ is the heat capacity and $c$ the specific heat capacity. During a phase change the temperature stays fixed and

$$Q = mL,$$

where $L$ is the latent heat of fusion or vaporization. For water, $c \approx 4186$ J/(kg K) and $L_f \approx 3.34\times10^{5}$ J/kg.

### Calorimetry

In an insulated container where no work is done, the heats of all parts add to zero: whatever one part loses, another gains. Without phase changes,

$$\sum_i m_i c_i\,(T_f - T_i) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T_f = \frac{\sum_i m_i c_i T_i}{\sum_i m_i c_i}.$$

The final temperature must lie between the lowest and highest starting temperatures. If it does not, you have made a sign error. If a phase change is possible, first test whether there is enough heat available to complete it.

@example
title: A hot copper block dropped into water
problem: A $0.200$ kg copper block ($c = 385$ J/(kg K)) at $100\,^\circ$C is dropped into $0.500$ kg of water ($c = 4186$ J/(kg K)) at $20.0\,^\circ$C in an insulated container. Find the final temperature.
step: Compute the two heat capacities: $C_{\rm Cu} = 0.200 \times 385 = 77.0$ J/K and $C_{\rm w} = 0.500\times 4186 = 2093$ J/K.
step: The container is insulated, so the heats sum to zero: $C_{\rm Cu}(T_f - 100) + C_{\rm w}(T_f - 20) = 0$.
step: Solve for the final temperature: $T_f = \dfrac{77.0(100) + 2093(20.0)}{77.0 + 2093} = \dfrac{49\,560}{2170} \approx 22.8\,^\circ$C.
step: Sanity check: $T_f$ lies between $20$ and $100\,^\circ$C and is close to the water's temperature, as expected because the water has a much larger heat capacity. The copper loses $77.0(77.2)\approx 5.94$ kJ and the water gains $2093(2.84)\approx 5.94$ kJ.

@example
title: Does all the ice melt?
problem: $50.0$ g of ice at $0\,^\circ$C is added to $200$ g of water at $25.0\,^\circ$C in an insulated container. Use $L_f = 3.34\times10^5$ J/kg and $c = 4186$ J/(kg K). Find the final state.
step: Find the most heat the warm water can give up by cooling to $0\,^\circ$C: $0.200(4186)(25.0) = 20\,930$ J.
step: Find the heat needed to melt all the ice: $0.0500(3.34\times10^5) = 16\,700$ J.
step: Since $20\,930 > 16\,700$, all the ice melts and the final temperature is above $0\,^\circ$C.
step: Balance energy with the melted ice warming from $0$ to $T_f$ while the water cools from $25.0$ to $T_f$: $16\,700 + 0.0500(4186)\,T_f = 0.200(4186)(25.0 - T_f)$.
step: Collect terms: $16\,700 + 209.3\,T_f = 20\,930 - 837.2\,T_f$, so $1046.5\,T_f = 4230$ and $T_f \approx 4.04\,^\circ$C.

@mistakes
- Using Celsius temperatures in $PV = nRT$ or in $v_{\rm rms} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$. Only temperature differences are the same in kelvin and Celsius.
- Saying an object "has" a certain number of joules of heat. Heat is energy in transit; the object has internal energy.
- Applying $Q = mc\Delta T$ straight through a melting or boiling point and forgetting the $mL$ term.
- Dropping the heat capacity of the container, or getting a final temperature outside the range of starting temperatures.

@practice num
prompt: A constant-volume gas thermometer reads $80.0$ kPa when its bulb is at the triple point of water ($273.16$ K). When the bulb is placed in a hot bath it reads $110.0$ kPa. What is the bath temperature? Give the value to the nearest $0.1$ K.
answer: 375.6
check: root(lambda T: 80.0*T/273.16 - 110.0, 100, 1000)
unit: K
tol: 0.1
hint: At constant volume the pressure of a dilute gas is proportional to the absolute temperature.
hint: So $T/273.16\ \text{K} = P/P_{tp}$.
solution: $T = 273.16\ \text{K}\times\dfrac{110.0}{80.0} = 375.6$ K.

@practice num
prompt: Take $R = 8.314$ J/(mol K). One mole of an ideal gas at $300$ K occupies $0.0250\ \text{m}^3$. What is its pressure in kPa?
answer: 99.77
check: 1.00*8.314*300/0.0250/1000
unit: kPa
tol: 1%
hint: Use $PV = nRT$ with $T$ in kelvin.
hint: Solve for $P = nRT/V$ and convert pascals to kilopascals.
solution: $P = \dfrac{(1.00)(8.314)(300)}{0.0250} = 9.977\times10^{4}$ Pa $\approx 99.8$ kPa.

@practice num
prompt: A helium atom has mass $6.646\times10^{-27}$ kg. Take $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K. What is the rms speed of helium atoms in an ideal gas at $300$ K, in m/s?
answer: 1368
check: math.sqrt(3*1.381e-23*300/6.646e-27)
unit: m/s
tol: 1%
hint: The mean kinetic energy per atom is $\tfrac32 k_BT$, and it equals $\tfrac12 m v_{\rm rms}^2$.
hint: $v_{\rm rms} = \sqrt{3k_BT/m}$.
solution: $v_{\rm rms} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3(1.381\times10^{-23})(300)}{6.646\times10^{-27}}} = \sqrt{1.870\times10^{6}} \approx 1.37\times10^{3}$ m/s.

@practice num

@practice num
prompt: A $0.150$ kg metal sample at $98.0\,^\circ$C is dropped into $0.250$ kg of water at $18.0\,^\circ$C in an insulated cup whose heat capacity may be neglected. The final temperature is $22.0\,^\circ$C. Take $c_{\rm water} = 4186$ J/(kg K). What is the specific heat of the metal in J/(kg K)?
answer: 367
check: root(lambda c: 0.150*c*(22.0-98.0) + 0.250*4186*(22.0-18.0), 1, 5000)
unit: J/(kg K)
tol: 1%
hint: Heat lost by the metal equals heat gained by the water.
hint: $m_s c_s(98.0 - 22.0) = m_w c_w(22.0 - 18.0)$.
solution: $c_s = \dfrac{0.250(4186)(4.0)}{0.150(76.0)} = \dfrac{4186}{11.4} \approx 367$ J/(kg K).

@quiz num
prompt: An aluminum calorimeter cup of mass $0.150$ kg ($c = 900$ J/(kg K)) contains $0.300$ kg of water at $15.0\,^\circ$C. A $0.100$ kg iron block ($c = 450$ J/(kg K)) at $200\,^\circ$C is dropped in. Take $c_{\rm water} = 4186$ J/(kg K) and neglect heat losses. What is the final temperature, to the nearest $0.01\,^\circ$C?
answer: 20.80
check: root(lambda T: 0.150*900*(T-15.0) + 0.300*4186*(T-15.0) + 0.100*450*(T-200.0), 0, 200)
unit: °C
tol: 0.05
hint: The cup and the water start at the same temperature, so they both count as "cold" parts.
hint: Set the sum of $C_i(T_f - T_i)$ over cup, water and iron equal to zero.
solution: $C_{\rm cup} = 135$ J/K, $C_{\rm w} = 1255.8$ J/K, $C_{\rm Fe} = 45$ J/K. $T_f = \dfrac{(135 + 1255.8)(15.0) + 45(200)}{135 + 1255.8 + 45} = \dfrac{29\,862}{1435.8} \approx 20.80\,^\circ$C.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all statements that are correct.
choice*: If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, then A and B are in thermal equilibrium with each other.
choice*: Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.
choice: A hot object contains more heat than an otherwise identical cold object.
choice*: For an ideal gas, the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule is proportional to the absolute temperature.
choice: Doubling the Celsius temperature of an ideal gas doubles the rms speed of its molecules.
hint: Ask for each statement whether it describes a state (a property of the body) or a process (energy in transit).
hint: Kinetic theory involves the absolute temperature, and $v_{\rm rms}$ scales as $\sqrt{T}$.
solution: The statement about A, B and C is the zeroth law. The statement about heat and a temperature difference is the definition of heat. Heat is not stored, so the claim that a hot object contains more heat is false. The statement about translational kinetic energy is $\langle K\rangle = \tfrac32 k_BT$. The claim about doubling the Celsius temperature is false twice over: the scale must be kelvin, and $v_{\rm rms}\propto\sqrt{T}$, so doubling $T$ multiplies the speed by $\sqrt2$.
