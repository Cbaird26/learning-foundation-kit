@article
Thermodynamics takes entropy as a given. Statistical mechanics **derives** it by counting.

### Microstates and macrostates

A **microstate** specifies the system in complete microscopic detail, for example which of $N$ spins point up, or which quantum state each oscillator occupies. A **macrostate** specifies only a few large-scale variables such as $E$, $V$, $N$ or the number $n$ of up spins. Many microstates belong to one macrostate; their number is the **multiplicity** $\Omega$. For $N$ two-state spins with $n$ up,

$$\Omega(n) = \binom{N}{n} = \frac{N!}{n!\,(N-n)!}.$$

For an **Einstein solid** of $N$ oscillators sharing $q$ identical energy quanta,

$$\Omega(N,q) = \binom{q + N - 1}{q}.$$

### The fundamental postulate

For an isolated system in equilibrium with fixed $E$, $V$, $N$, every accessible microstate is equally probable, $p = 1/\Omega$. Then the probability of a macrostate is proportional to its multiplicity, so the system is found in the macrostate with the largest $\Omega$. For large $N$ that peak is extremely sharp, with relative width of order $1/\sqrt N$.

### Boltzmann entropy

$$S = k_B\ln\Omega .$$

Multiplicities of independent parts multiply, $\Omega = \Omega_1\Omega_2$, so entropies add. In a free expansion to twice the volume every molecule has twice as many positions, $\Omega \to 2^{N}\Omega$, so $\Delta S = Nk_B\ln 2 = nR\ln 2$, the same result as the thermodynamic calculation in week 3.

### Temperature from counting

Let two systems share a fixed total energy. The combined multiplicity $\Omega_1(E_1)\,\Omega_2(E-E_1)$ is largest when $\partial \ln\Omega_1/\partial E_1 = \partial\ln\Omega_2/\partial E_2$. The equal quantity is the inverse temperature:

$$\frac{1}{T} = \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial E}\right)_{V,N}.$$

Equal temperature at equilibrium is the statistical origin of the zeroth law.

### Stirling's approximation

For large $N$, $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$. It turns the two-state count into

$$\frac{S}{Nk_B} \approx -\big[x\ln x + (1-x)\ln(1-x)\big], \qquad x = \frac{n}{N},$$

which peaks at $x = \tfrac12$ with value $\ln 2$. The approximation is poor for small $N$.

### Ensembles

An **ensemble** is a collection of imagined copies of a system with the same macroscopic constraints. Averages over it give thermodynamic quantities.

- **Microcanonical:** $E$, $V$, $N$ fixed (isolated system), $p = 1/\Omega$.
- **Canonical:** $T$, $V$, $N$ fixed (contact with a heat bath). Weeks 6 and 7.
- **Grand canonical:** $T$, $V$, $\mu$ fixed (heat and particle bath). Week 9.

For large systems the ensembles agree on thermodynamic quantities; they differ in which quantities fluctuate.

@example
title: Counting microstates of ten spins
problem: Ten spins are each up or down. Find the multiplicity of the macrostate with five spins up, the probability of that macrostate if all microstates are equally likely, and its entropy.
step: A microstate is a specific up/down assignment for all ten spins, so there are $2^{10} = 1024$ microstates, all equally likely by the fundamental postulate.
step: The macrostate "five up" contains $\Omega = \binom{10}{5} = 252$ microstates.
step: Its probability is $252/1024 \approx 0.246$. By comparison the macrostate "all up" has $\Omega = 1$ and probability $1/1024$.
step: The entropy is $S = k_B\ln 252 \approx 5.53\,k_B$, versus $S = k_B\ln 1 = 0$ for all up. The evenly mixed macrostate has the largest entropy.

@example
title: Two Einstein solids sharing energy
problem: Solid A and solid B each have $3$ oscillators, and together they hold $6$ energy quanta. If all microstates are equally likely, what is the most probable number of quanta in A, and with what probability?
step: For $3$ oscillators $\Omega = \binom{q+2}{q} = \binom{q+2}{2}$. Tabulate $\Omega_A(q_A)\,\Omega_B(6-q_A)$ for $q_A = 0,\dots,6$: $28,\ 63,\ 90,\ 100,\ 90,\ 63,\ 28$.
step: Their sum is $462 = \binom{11}{6}$, which is the multiplicity of six oscillators sharing six quanta. This confirms the count.
step: The largest term is $q_A = 3$, with probability $100/462 \approx 0.216$. Equal sharing is the most probable macrostate, which is thermal equilibrium.
step: With so few oscillators the distribution is broad. For $N\sim10^{23}$ the same calculation produces a peak so narrow that the equal-sharing macrostate is the only one ever observed.

@mistakes
- Treating each macrostate as one microstate. The macrostate "$n$ up" contains $\binom{N}{n}$ microstates, and the equal-probability postulate applies to microstates.
- Using Stirling's approximation for small $N$, or dropping the $-N$ term in $\ln N! \approx N\ln N - N$.
- Using $\Omega(N,q) = \binom{q+N}{q}$ or $\binom{q}{N}$ for the Einstein solid. The correct stars-and-bars count is $\binom{q+N-1}{q}$.
- Reading the second law as absolute. For a small system, fluctuations to lower-entropy macrostates are quite likely; for macroscopic systems they are astronomically improbable.

@practice num
prompt: Six spins are each up or down. How many microstates have exactly two spins up?
answer: 15
check: comb(6, 2)
hint: Choose which two of the six spins point up.
solution: $\Omega = \binom{6}{2} = \dfrac{6\cdot5}{2} = 15$.

@practice mc
prompt: A gas is held at fixed volume in contact with a large heat bath at temperature $T$, with no exchange of particles. Which ensemble describes it?
choice: Microcanonical
choice*: Canonical
choice: Grand canonical
choice: None; ensembles apply only to isolated systems
hint: Decide what is fixed: energy, temperature, or temperature and chemical potential.
solution: Temperature, volume and particle number are fixed while the energy fluctuates through contact with the bath. That is the canonical ensemble. The microcanonical ensemble needs fixed energy, and the grand canonical ensemble also lets particles be exchanged.

@practice num
prompt: An Einstein solid has $N = 4$ oscillators sharing $q = 5$ identical quanta. How many microstates does it have? (Count the ways to distribute the quanta among the distinguishable oscillators.)
answer: 56
check: sum(1 for a in range(6) for b in range(6) for c in range(6) for d in range(6) if a + b + c + d == 5)

hint: Use $\Omega = \binom{q+N-1}{q}$.
solution: $\Omega = \binom{5+4-1}{5} = \binom{8}{5} = 56$.

@practice num
prompt: A gas of $N = 1.00\times10^{22}$ molecules expands freely into an evacuated container, doubling its volume. Use $S = k_B\ln\Omega$ with $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K to find the entropy increase, in J/K.
answer: 0.0957
check: 1.381e-23*math.log(2**1000)*(1.00e22/1000)
unit: J/K
tol: 1%
hint: Each molecule can now be in either half of the volume, so the number of position choices per molecule doubles.
hint: $\Omega_f/\Omega_i = 2^N$, so $\Delta S = k_B\ln 2^N$.
solution: $\Delta S = k_B\ln(2^N) = Nk_B\ln 2 = (1.00\times10^{22})(1.381\times10^{-23})(0.693) \approx 0.0957$ J/K.

@quiz num
prompt: Solids A and B each have $3$ oscillators and together hold $5$ quanta, all microstates being equally likely. What is the probability that A holds exactly $2$ quanta? Give a decimal to three places.
answer: 0.238
check: sum(1 for a in range(6) for b in range(6) for c in range(6) for g in range(6) for h in range(6) for m in range(6) if a + b + c + g + h + m == 5 and a + b + c == 2) / sum(1 for a in range(6) for b in range(6) for c in range(6) for g in range(6) for h in range(6) for m in range(6) if a + b + c + g + h + m == 5)
tol: 0.001
hint: Multiply the multiplicities of A with $2$ quanta and B with $3$ quanta, then divide by the total number of microstates.
hint: $\Omega_A = \binom{4}{2}$, $\Omega_B = \binom{5}{3}$, total $= \binom{10}{5}$.
solution: $P = \dfrac{\Omega_A(2)\,\Omega_B(3)}{\Omega_{\rm total}} = \dfrac{6\times10}{252} = \dfrac{60}{252} \approx 0.238$.

@quiz num

answer: 0.5004
check: (lambda N, n: ((N*math.log(N) - N) - (n*math.log(n) - n) - ((N - n)*math.log(N - n) - (N - n)))/N)(1.0e5, 2.0e4)
tol: 0.001
hint: Use $S/(Nk_B) \approx -[x\ln x + (1-x)\ln(1-x)]$ with $x = 0.2$.
hint: $-[0.2\ln0.2 + 0.8\ln0.8]$.
solution: $S/(Nk_B) = -[0.2\ln 0.2 + 0.8\ln 0.8] = 0.2(1.6094) + 0.8(0.2231) = 0.3219 + 0.1785 \approx 0.5004$, below the maximum $\ln 2 = 0.693$ reached at $x = \tfrac12$.
