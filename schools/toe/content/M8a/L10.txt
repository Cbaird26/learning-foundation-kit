@article
The residue theorem turns many real integrals into algebra. The pattern never changes: extend the integrand to the complex plane, close the real segment with an arc, show the arc contributes nothing in the limit, and sum residues. The skill lies in the third step: an arc you cannot bound is an arc you cannot ignore.

### Rational functions on the line

Let $f = P/Q$ be rational with no real poles and $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 2$. Close $[-R, R]$ with the upper semicircle $C_R$: $z = Re^{it}$, $0 \le t \le \pi$. For large $R$,

$$\int_{-R}^{R}f\,dx + \int_{C_R}f\,dz = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a>0}\operatorname{Res}(f,a).$$

**Justify the arc.** The degree condition gives $\lvert f(z)\rvert \le K/R^2$ on $C_R$, so by the ML inequality $\left\lvert\int_{C_R}f\,dz\right\rvert \le \dfrac{K}{R^2}\cdot\pi R \to 0$. (With only $\deg Q = \deg P + 1$ the bound is $\pi K$, which does not vanish.) Letting $R \to \infty$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\,dx = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a>0}\operatorname{Res}(f,a).$$

### Fourier-type integrals

For $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)e^{ikx}\,dx$ with $k > 0$ and $f(z) \to 0$ as $\lvert z\rvert \to \infty$, note that $\lvert e^{ikz}\rvert = e^{-k\operatorname{Im}z} \le 1$ in the **upper** half-plane. **Jordan's lemma** then says the arc contribution still tends to $0$, even when $f$ decays only like $1/z$. So close upward for $k > 0$; for $k < 0$ close downward (clockwise, giving $-2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a<0}$). For a real $f$, $\int f\cos kx\,dx$ and $\int f\sin kx\,dx$ are the real and imaginary parts of $\int fe^{ikx}\,dx$.

### Trigonometric integrals

For $\int_0^{2\pi}R(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)\,d\theta$, put $z = e^{i\theta}$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{z + z^{-1}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{z - z^{-1}}{2i}, \qquad d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}.$$

The integral becomes $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert=1}$ of a rational function, and only the poles **inside** the unit circle count.

### Choosing the contour

- Rational integrand over $(-\infty, \infty)$: a large semicircle, upper or lower (the answers agree, so pick the half-plane with fewer poles).
- An integrand containing $e^{ikx}$: the sign of $k$ decides the half-plane.
- A trigonometric integrand over a full period $[0, 2\pi]$: the unit circle.

These integrals are everywhere in physics. For $k > 0$ and $a > 0$, the result $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{ikx}}{x^2 + a^2}\,dx = \frac{\pi}{a}e^{-ka}$ says that the Fourier transform of the resonance line shape $\frac{1}{x^2 + a^2}$ is a decaying exponential.

### Poles on the real axis (remark)

For a simple pole on the real axis, indent the contour with a small upper semicircle; it contributes $-i\pi\operatorname{Res}$, which gives the principal value
$\operatorname{PV}\!\int f\,dx = 2\pi i\sum_{\operatorname{Im}a>0}\operatorname{Res} + \pi i\sum_{a\in\mathbb{R}}\operatorname{Res}$.

@example
title: A rational integral over the line
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^4 + 1}$.
step: Close with the upper semicircle. For $\lvert z\rvert = R > 1$, $\lvert z^4 + 1\rvert \ge R^4 - 1$, so the arc integral is at most $\dfrac{\pi R}{R^4 - 1} \to 0$ by the ML inequality.
step: The poles are the roots of $z^4 = -1$. Those in the upper half-plane are $z_1 = e^{i\pi/4}$ and $z_2 = e^{3i\pi/4}$.
step: Each pole is simple: $\operatorname{Res} = \dfrac{1}{4z^3} = \dfrac{z}{4z^4} = -\dfrac{z}{4}$, using $z^4 = -1$.
step: Sum the residues: $-\dfrac14\left(e^{i\pi/4} + e^{3i\pi/4}\right) = -\dfrac14(i\sqrt2)$.
step: The integral is $2\pi i\cdot\left(-\dfrac{i\sqrt2}{4}\right) = \dfrac{\pi\sqrt2}{2} = \dfrac{\pi}{\sqrt2}$.

@example
title: A trigonometric integral
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2 + \cos\theta}$.
step: Substitute $z = e^{i\theta}$: $2 + \cos\theta = \dfrac{z^2 + 4z + 1}{2z}$ and $d\theta = \dfrac{dz}{iz}$, so the integrand becomes $\dfrac{2}{i\,(z^2 + 4z + 1)}\,dz$ on $\lvert z\rvert = 1$.
step: The roots of $z^2 + 4z + 1$ are $z = -2 \pm \sqrt3$. Only $z_0 = -2 + \sqrt3 \approx -0.27$ is inside the unit circle.
step: This is a simple pole, with $h(z) = z^2 + 4z + 1$ and $h'(z_0) = 2z_0 + 4 = 2\sqrt3$. So $\operatorname{Res} = \dfrac{2}{i}\cdot\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt3} = \dfrac{1}{i\sqrt3}$.
step: The integral is $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{i\sqrt3} = \dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}$. Sanity check: the average of $\frac{1}{2 + \cos\theta}$ is $\frac{1}{\sqrt3} \approx 0.577$, between $\frac13$ and $1$.

@mistakes
- Saying "the arc vanishes" without justification; always give an ML estimate or cite Jordan's lemma with its hypotheses.
- Closing in the wrong half-plane for $e^{ikx}$: use the upper half-plane for $k > 0$, otherwise $\lvert e^{ikz}\rvert$ blows up on the arc.
- Forgetting to take the real part: $\int\frac{\cos kx}{x^2 + a^2}\,dx$ is the real part of the exponential integral, not the whole thing.
- In trigonometric integrals, forgetting the factor $\frac{1}{iz}$ from $d\theta$, or counting poles outside the unit circle.
- Replacing $\int_0^\infty$ by $\frac12\int_{-\infty}^\infty$ when the integrand is not even.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^2 + 4}$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi/2
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x**2 + 4), -inf, inf)
hint: Close in the upper half-plane. Where are the poles of $\frac{1}{z^2+4}$?
hint: Only $z = 2i$ is in the upper half-plane. Use $\operatorname{Res} = \frac{1}{2z}$ there.
solution: The residue at $2i$ is $\dfrac{1}{2(2i)} = \dfrac{1}{4i}$. The arc vanishes since $\lvert f\rvert \le 1/(R^2 - 4)$. So the integral is $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{4i} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$. Check: the antiderivative is $\frac12\arctan\frac x2$, giving $\frac12(\frac\pi2 + \frac\pi2) = \frac\pi2$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{x^2}{(x^2 + 1)(x^2 + 9)}\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi/4
check: quad(lambda x: x**2/((x**2 + 1)*(x**2 + 9)), -inf, inf)
hint: Upper half-plane poles: $z = i$ and $z = 3i$, both simple.
hint: $\operatorname{Res}(f,i) = \frac{i^2}{(2i)(8)}$ and $\operatorname{Res}(f,3i) = \frac{(3i)^2}{(-8)(6i)}$.
solution: $\operatorname{Res}(f,i) = \dfrac{-1}{16i}$ and $\operatorname{Res}(f,3i) = \dfrac{-9}{-48i} = \dfrac{3}{16i}$. Their sum is $\dfrac{2}{16i} = \dfrac{1}{8i}$, so the integral is $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{8i} = \dfrac\pi4$. The arc vanishes because $\deg Q - \deg P = 2$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 2x}{x^2 + 1}\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi*e^(-2)
check: 2*nsum(lambda k: quad(lambda x: cos(2*x)/(x**2 + 1), k*pi, (k + 1)*pi), 0, 1000)
hint: Consider $\int\frac{e^{2ix}}{x^2+1}\,dx$ and close in the upper half-plane, since $k = 2 > 0$.
hint: The only upper pole is $z = i$, with residue $\dfrac{e^{2i\cdot i}}{2i} = \dfrac{e^{-2}}{2i}$.
solution: $\displaystyle\int\frac{e^{2ix}}{x^2+1}\,dx = 2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{e^{-2}}{2i} = \pi e^{-2}$ (the arc vanishes by Jordan's lemma). This value is real, so it equals its real part, which is the cosine integral.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{3 + 2\sin\theta}$. Give an exact value.
answer: 2pi/sqrt(5)
check: quad(lambda t: 1/(3 + 2*sin(t)), 0, 2*pi)
hint: With $z = e^{i\theta}$, $\sin\theta = \frac{z - z^{-1}}{2i}$ and $d\theta = \frac{dz}{iz}$. Clear the fraction to get a rational function of $z$.
hint: The integrand becomes $\dfrac{1}{z^2 + 3iz - 1}\,dz$. Find its roots and decide which lies inside the unit circle.
solution: $3 + 2\sin\theta = \dfrac{z^2 + 3iz - 1}{iz}$, so the integral is $\oint\dfrac{dz}{z^2 + 3iz - 1}$. The roots are $z = \dfrac{-3 \pm \sqrt5}{2}\,i$; only $z_0 = \dfrac{-3 + \sqrt5}{2}\,i$ (modulus $\approx 0.38$) is inside. The residue is $\dfrac{1}{2z_0 + 3i} = \dfrac{1}{i\sqrt5}$. The integral is $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{i\sqrt5} = \dfrac{2\pi}{\sqrt5}$.

@practice mc
prompt: For $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\,dx$ with polynomials $P, Q$ and no real zeros of $Q$, which condition lets you show with the ML inequality that the upper semicircular arc contributes $0$ as $R \to \infty$?
choice*: $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 2$
choice: $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 1$
choice: $P$ and $Q$ have real coefficients
choice: $Q$ has at least one zero in the upper half-plane
hint: On the arc, the length is of order $R$. What decay of $\lvert f\rvert$ makes $\lvert f\rvert\cdot\pi R$ tend to $0$?
hint: You need $\lvert f\rvert = O(R^{-2})$, which means the degrees must differ by at least two.
solution: If $\deg Q \ge \deg P + 2$ then $\lvert f(z)\rvert \le K/R^2$ on the arc and the ML bound $K\pi/R \to 0$. With a gap of only $1$, $\lvert f\rvert \sim 1/R$ and the bound tends to a constant, which proves nothing. Real coefficients and the location of zeros do not control the size of the arc integral.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\infty}\frac{dx}{(x^2 + 4)^2}$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi/32
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x**2 + 4)**2, 0, inf)
hint: The integrand is even, so $\int_0^\infty = \frac12\int_{-\infty}^\infty$. Close in the upper half-plane; the pole at $2i$ is a double pole.
hint: Write $f = \dfrac{1}{(z-2i)^2(z+2i)^2}$ and differentiate $\dfrac{1}{(z+2i)^2}$ at $z = 2i$.
solution: $\operatorname{Res}(f,2i) = \dfrac{d}{dz}\dfrac{1}{(z+2i)^2}\Big|_{2i} = \dfrac{-2}{(4i)^3} = \dfrac{-2}{-64i} = \dfrac{1}{32i}$. So $\int_{-\infty}^\infty f\,dx = 2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{1}{32i} = \dfrac{\pi}{16}$, and the half-line integral is half of that: $\dfrac{\pi}{32}$.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos 3x}{x^2 + 4}\,dx$. State the closed contour you use, justify why the arc at infinity contributes nothing, and show the residue computation.
hint: Replace $\cos 3x$ by $e^{3ix}$ and take the real part at the end. Which half-plane do you close in for $k = 3 > 0$?
hint: The only upper pole is $z = 2i$. Bound the arc with Jordan's lemma (or note that $f(z) = \frac{1}{z^2+4} \to 0$).
solution: Consider $I = \int_{-\infty}^\infty\dfrac{e^{3ix}}{x^2+4}\,dx$ and close with the upper semicircle $C_R$ of radius $R > 2$. Since $k = 3 > 0$, $\lvert e^{3iz}\rvert = e^{-3\operatorname{Im}z} \le 1$ on $C_R$, and $\lvert 1/(z^2+4)\rvert \le 1/(R^2 - 4) \to 0$, so by the ML inequality (or Jordan's lemma) the arc integral is at most $\pi R/(R^2 - 4) \to 0$. The only pole inside is $z = 2i$, with residue $\dfrac{e^{3i\cdot 2i}}{2\cdot 2i} = \dfrac{e^{-6}}{4i}$. Hence $I = 2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{e^{-6}}{4i} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}e^{-6}$. Since $I$ is real, the cosine integral is its real part, $\dfrac{\pi}{2}e^{-6}$.
