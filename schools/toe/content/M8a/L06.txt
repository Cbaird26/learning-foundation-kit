@article
**Cauchy's integral formula.** Let $f$ be analytic on and inside a simple closed counterclockwise contour $\gamma$, and let $a$ be a point inside $\gamma$. Then

$$f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{z - a}\,dz.$$

The values of $f$ inside are completely determined by its values on the boundary. That is a striking rigidity, and physics has seen it before: the steady-state temperature at the center of a disk equals the average temperature around its edge, and an electrostatic potential in a charge-free region is fixed by its values on the boundary. The reason is the same, since the real and imaginary parts of an analytic function are harmonic (Week 3).

### Why it is true

By deformation (Week 5), shrink $\gamma$ to the circle $\lvert z - a\rvert = \varepsilon$. Split $f(z) = f(a) + [f(z) - f(a)]$. The first part gives $f(a)\oint\frac{dz}{z-a} = 2\pi i\,f(a)$. For the second part, the ML inequality gives a bound of at most $\max\lvert f(z) - f(a)\rvert\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\cdot 2\pi\varepsilon \to 0$ as $\varepsilon \to 0$ because $f$ is continuous. The integral does not depend on $\varepsilon$, so the second part is exactly $0$.

### Derivatives

Differentiating under the integral sign gives, for every $n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$,

$$f^{(n)}(a) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma \frac{f(z)}{(z - a)^{n+1}}\,dz.$$

So an analytic function has derivatives of **all** orders, and each is again analytic. Equivalently, $\oint \frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz = \frac{2\pi i}{n!}f^{(n)}(a)$.

### Mean value property

On the circle $z = a + re^{it}$, $dz = ire^{it}\,dt$ and $z - a = re^{it}$, so the formula reads

$$f(a) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a + re^{it})\,dt:$$

the value at the center is the average over the circle, provided $f$ is analytic on the whole disk.

### Cauchy's estimate

Suppose $f$ is analytic on the closed disk $\lvert z - a\rvert \le R$ and $\lvert f\rvert \le M$ on the circle $\lvert z - a\rvert = R$. Apply the ML inequality to the derivative formula, with $L = 2\pi R$ and $\lvert z-a\rvert^{n+1} = R^{n+1}$ on the circle:

$$\lvert f^{(n)}(a)\rvert \le \frac{n!}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{R^{n+1}}\cdot 2\pi R = \frac{n!\,M}{R^n}.$$

If $f$ is entire and bounded by $M$ everywhere, letting $R \to \infty$ with $n = 1$ gives $f' \equiv 0$. This is **Liouville's theorem**: a bounded entire function is constant.

### Using the formula for integrals

Match the integrand to $\dfrac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}$ with $f$ analytic inside, then read off $2\pi i\,f^{(n)}(a)/n!$.

@example
title: Using the formula directly
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 2}\dfrac{e^z}{z(z - 3)}\,dz$, counterclockwise.
step: The integrand has singularities at $0$ and $3$; only $0$ is inside the circle of radius $2$.
step: Write the integrand as $\dfrac{f(z)}{z - 0}$ with $f(z) = \dfrac{e^z}{z - 3}$. This $f$ is analytic on and inside the circle because its only singularity, $z = 3$, is outside.
step: By the integral formula, the integral equals $2\pi i\,f(0)$.
step: Compute $f(0) = \dfrac{e^0}{0 - 3} = -\dfrac13$, so the integral is $-\dfrac{2\pi i}{3}$.

@example
title: Using a derivative
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 1}\dfrac{\cos z}{z^3}\,dz$, counterclockwise.
step: The denominator is $(z - 0)^3$, so $n + 1 = 3$ and $n = 2$, with $f(z) = \cos z$ entire.
step: The derivative formula gives $\oint\dfrac{f(z)}{z^3}\,dz = \dfrac{2\pi i}{2!}f''(0)$.
step: Compute $f''(z) = -\cos z$, so $f''(0) = -1$.
step: The integral is $\dfrac{2\pi i}{2}\cdot(-1) = -\pi i$.

@mistakes
- Using the formula when $f$ has another singularity inside $\gamma$, or when $a$ lies outside $\gamma$ (in that case the integral is simply $0$).
- Forgetting the factorial or the shifted exponent: the $n$-th derivative goes with $(z - a)^{n+1}$ in the denominator.
- In Cauchy's estimate, bounding $\lvert f\rvert$ on the wrong circle: $M$ must bound $f$ on the circle centered at $a$, and $f$ must be analytic on the whole closed disk.
- Writing $R^{n+1}$ in the final estimate; the length $2\pi R$ cancels one power, leaving $R^n$.

@practice self
prompt: Assume $f$ is analytic on the closed disk $\lvert z - a\rvert \le R$ and $\lvert f(z)\rvert \le M$ on its boundary circle. (a) Derive the estimate $\lvert f^{(n)}(a)\rvert \le n!\,M/R^n$. (b) Use it to show that a bounded entire function is constant.
hint: Start from $f^{(n)}(a) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint_{\lvert z-a\rvert=R}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz$ and take absolute values.
hint: On the circle, $\lvert z-a\rvert = R$ and the length is $2\pi R$. For (b), take $n = 1$ and let $R \to \infty$.
solution: (a) Taking absolute values, $\lvert f^{(n)}(a)\rvert \le \frac{n!}{2\pi}\cdot\max\left\lvert\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\right\rvert\cdot 2\pi R \le \frac{n!}{2\pi}\cdot\frac{M}{R^{n+1}}\cdot 2\pi R = \frac{n!\,M}{R^n}$. (b) If $f$ is entire with $\lvert f\rvert \le M$ everywhere, the estimate holds for every $R$. With $n = 1$, $\lvert f'(a)\rvert \le M/R \to 0$ as $R \to \infty$, so $f'(a) = 0$ for every $a$ and $f$ is constant.
