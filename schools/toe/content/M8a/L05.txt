@article
**Cauchy's theorem.** If $f$ is analytic on a simply connected domain $D$ and $\gamma$ is a closed contour in $D$, then

$$\oint_\gamma f(z)\,dz = 0.$$

A domain is **simply connected** if it has no holes: every closed loop in it can be shrunk to a point without leaving it. A disk, a half-plane, and the plane with a ray removed are simply connected; an annulus and the punctured plane $\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}$ are not.

### Why it is true

Write $f = u + iv$ and $dz = dx + i\,dy$. Then

$$\oint_\gamma f\,dz = \oint_\gamma (u\,dx - v\,dy) + i\oint_\gamma (v\,dx + u\,dy).$$

Green's theorem, $\oint P\,dx + Q\,dy = \iint (Q_x - P_y)\,dA$, turns this into area integrals over the region $R$ enclosed by $\gamma$:

$$\oint_\gamma f\,dz = \iint_R (-v_x - u_y)\,dA + i\iint_R (u_x - v_y)\,dA = 0,$$

because the Cauchy-Riemann equations say $u_y = -v_x$ and $u_x = v_y$. (This argument assumes continuous partials; a more careful proof removes that assumption.)

### Consequences

- **Path independence.** On a simply connected domain, $\int_\gamma f\,dz$ depends only on the endpoints, so $F(z) = \int_{z_0}^{z}f(w)\,dw$ is well defined and $F' = f$. Every analytic function there has an antiderivative.
- **Deformation.** If $f$ is analytic on two counterclockwise closed contours $C_1$ (outer) and $C_2$ (inner) and on the region between them, then $\oint_{C_1}f\,dz = \oint_{C_2}f\,dz$. You can shrink a contour across analytic territory freely.
- **Several singularities.** If $f$ is analytic inside and on $\gamma$ except at points $a_1, \dots, a_m$ inside, then $\oint_\gamma f\,dz$ is the sum of the integrals over small disjoint circles around the $a_j$.

### The hypothesis matters

$\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 1}dz/z = 2\pi i \neq 0$ because $1/z$ is not analytic at the point $0$ inside the circle. The same integral over the circle $\lvert z - 3\rvert = 1$, which does not enclose $0$, is $0$. Singularities are exactly the places where something can be left over after a loop integral; everywhere else the contributions cancel completely.

### A strategy

1. Find the points where $f$ fails to be analytic.
2. Decide which of them lie inside $\gamma$. If none do, the integral is $0$.
3. Otherwise split $f$ (partial fractions work well for rational functions), or shrink $\gamma$ to small circles, and use $\oint (z-a)^{-1}dz = 2\pi i$ on each.

@example
title: One singularity inside, one outside
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 2}\dfrac{dz}{z(z - 3)}$, counterclockwise.
step: The integrand fails to be analytic at $z = 0$ and $z = 3$. Only $0$ lies inside the circle of radius $2$.
step: Use partial fractions: $\dfrac{1}{z(z-3)} = \dfrac{1}{3}\left(\dfrac{1}{z-3} - \dfrac{1}{z}\right)$.
step: The term $\dfrac{1}{z-3}$ is analytic on and inside the circle (a disk is simply connected), so its integral is $0$ by Cauchy's theorem.
step: The remaining term gives $-\dfrac13\oint\dfrac{dz}{z} = -\dfrac{2\pi i}{3}$.

@example
title: Two singularities inside
problem: Evaluate $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 3}\dfrac{2z}{z^2 - 1}\,dz$, counterclockwise.
step: The singularities are $z = \pm 1$, and both lie inside the circle of radius $3$.
step: Partial fractions: $\dfrac{2z}{z^2-1} = \dfrac{1}{z-1} + \dfrac{1}{z+1}$. Check: $\dfrac{(z+1) + (z-1)}{z^2-1} = \dfrac{2z}{z^2-1}$.
step: Deform the big circle into two small disjoint circles, one around $1$ and one around $-1$. On the small circle around $1$, the term $\frac{1}{z+1}$ is analytic and contributes $0$; the term $\frac{1}{z-1}$ contributes $2\pi i$. The circle around $-1$ behaves the same way with the roles swapped.
step: The total is $2\pi i + 2\pi i = 4\pi i$.

@mistakes
- Applying Cauchy's theorem when the integrand has a singularity inside the contour, as with $1/z$ around the origin.
- Believing "the integral is zero, so the function must be analytic"; for example $\oint_{\lvert z\rvert = 1}z^{-2}\,dz = 0$ even though $z^{-2}$ is singular at $0$.
- Losing track of orientation: counterclockwise is positive, and an inner boundary traversed clockwise contributes with the opposite sign.
- Drawing overlapping small circles; the circles around different singularities must be disjoint.
