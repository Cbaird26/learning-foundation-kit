@article
**Taylor's theorem.** If $f$ is analytic on the disk $\lvert z - a\rvert < R$, then on that whole disk

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z - a)^n, \qquad c_n = \frac{f^{(n)}(a)}{n!} = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\lvert z - a\rvert = r}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz,$$

for any $0 < r < R$. The second formula for $c_n$ is Cauchy's derivative formula from Week 6. Real calculus has no such guarantee; here, being analytic once is enough for the series to converge to $f$.

### Radius of convergence

The series converges in the **largest disk about $a$ on which $f$ is analytic**. So the radius is the distance from $a$ to the nearest singularity of $f$. Two consequences:

- The radius depends on the center: $\frac{1}{z - 2}$ has radius $2$ about $0$ and radius $1$ about $1$.
- Complex singularities limit real series. $\frac{1}{1 + x^2}$ is perfectly smooth for real $x$, but its series about $0$ has radius $1$ because of the poles at $z = \pm i$.

### Building series

| function | series about $0$ | radius |
|---|---|---|
| $e^z$ | $\sum z^n/n!$ | $\infty$ |
| $\sin z$ | $z - z^3/3! + z^5/5! - \cdots$ | $\infty$ |
| $\cos z$ | $1 - z^2/2! + z^4/4! - \cdots$ | $\infty$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{1 - z}$ | $\sum z^n$ | $1$ |
| $\operatorname{Log}(1 + z)$ | $\sum_{n \ge 1}(-1)^{n+1}z^n/n$ | $1$ |

Taylor series are **unique**, so any method that produces a convergent series in powers of $z - a$ produces the Taylor series. You may substitute ($e^{z^2}$ from $e^w$ with $w = z^2$), multiply, divide, and differentiate or integrate termwise inside the disk of convergence.

To expand $\frac{1}{z - b}$ about $a \neq b$, write it as a geometric series in $(z-a)$:

$$\frac{1}{z - b} = \frac{1}{(a - b) + (z - a)} = \frac{1}{a - b}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + \frac{z-a}{a-b}} = \frac{1}{a-b}\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(-\frac{z-a}{a-b}\right)^n, \quad \lvert z - a\rvert < \lvert a - b\rvert.$$

### Zeros

If $f(a) = 0$ and $f$ is not identically zero near $a$, the first nonzero Taylor coefficient is some $c_m$ with $m \ge 1$, so $f(z) = (z - a)^m g(z)$ with $g$ analytic and $g(a) \neq 0$. We say $f$ has a **zero of order $m$** at $a$. Since $g$ is continuous and nonzero at $a$, there are no other zeros nearby: zeros of a nonzero analytic function are **isolated**. Isolated zeros are the engine behind the identity theorem: two analytic functions on a connected domain that agree on a set with a limit point in the domain are equal everywhere.

@example
title: One function, two centers
problem: Expand $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{z - 2}$ in powers of $z$ and in powers of $z - 1$, and give each radius of convergence.
step: About $0$: $\dfrac{1}{z - 2} = -\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - z/2} = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$, valid for $\lvert z/2\rvert < 1$, so $R = 2$.
step: About $1$: write $z - 2 = -1 + (z - 1)$. Then $\dfrac{1}{z - 2} = -\dfrac{1}{1 - (z - 1)} = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty (z-1)^n$.
step: This is valid for $\lvert z - 1\rvert < 1$, so $R = 1$.
step: Check against the nearest-singularity rule: the only singularity is $z = 2$, at distance $2$ from $0$ and distance $1$ from $1$.

@example
title: A quotient of series
problem: Find the Maclaurin series of $\tan z$ through the $z^3$ term and its radius of convergence.
step: Write $\tan z = \dfrac{\sin z}{\cos z}$ with $\sin z = z - \dfrac{z^3}{6} + \cdots$ and $\cos z = 1 - \dfrac{z^2}{2} + \cdots$.
step: Seek $\tan z = z + c_3z^3 + \cdots$ (odd, and $\tan z \approx z$). Then $\sin z = \tan z\cos z = \left(z + c_3z^3\right)\left(1 - \tfrac{z^2}{2}\right) + \cdots = z + \left(c_3 - \tfrac12\right)z^3 + \cdots$.
step: Matching the $z^3$ coefficient with $-\tfrac16$ gives $c_3 - \tfrac12 = -\tfrac16$, so $c_3 = \tfrac13$. Thus $\tan z = z + \tfrac{z^3}{3} + \cdots$.
step: The nearest singularities are the zeros of $\cos z$ at $z = \pm\pi/2$, so $R = \pi/2$.

@mistakes
- Judging the radius of convergence from real-variable behavior alone; look for complex singularities, such as $\pm i$ for $\frac{1}{1+x^2}$.
- Measuring the distance to the singularity from the wrong center. The radius depends on $a$.
- Expanding $\frac{1}{z - b}$ about $a$ without factoring out $a - b$, so the geometric ratio is not $\frac{z-a}{b-a}$.
- Forgetting the $n!$: $c_n = f^{(n)}(a)/n!$, not $f^{(n)}(a)$.

prompt: Find the coefficient of $z^5$ in the Maclaurin series of $\sin z\cos z$. Give an exact value.
answer: 2/15
check: (lambda g: quad(lambda t: g(t).real, 0, 2*pi) + I*quad(lambda t: g(t).imag, 0, 2*pi))(lambda t: sin(0.5*exp(1j*t))*cos(0.5*exp(1j*t))*exp(-5j*t))/(2*pi*0.5**5)

solution: $\sin z\cos z = \frac12\sin 2z = \frac12\left(2z - \dfrac{(2z)^3}{3!} + \dfrac{(2z)^5}{5!} - \cdots\right)$. The $z^5$ coefficient is $\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{32}{120} = \dfrac{2}{15}$.

@practice num

answer: pi/2
check: root(lambda z: cos(z), 0.1, 3)
hint: The only singularities of $\sec z$ are the zeros of $\cos z$.
hint: Which zero of $\cos z$ is closest to $0$?

@practice mc
prompt: The real function $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{1 + x^2}$ is infinitely differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}$, yet its Taylor series about $0$ diverges for $\lvert x\rvert > 1$. What explains this?
choice: $f$ is not differentiable at $x = \pm 1$.
choice*: As a function of complex $z$, it has singularities at $z = \pm i$, at distance $1$ from the center.
choice: $f$ has a vertical asymptote on the real axis.
choice: Taylor series of rational functions always have radius of convergence $1$.
hint: Extend $f$ to complex $z$ and look for points where $1 + z^2 = 0$.
hint: The radius of convergence is the distance from the center to the nearest singularity in the complex plane.
solution: In the complex plane $1 + z^2 = 0$ at $z = \pm i$, both at distance $1$ from $0$, so $R = 1$. Nothing special happens at $x = \pm 1$ for the real function. Rational functions have radius equal to the distance to the nearest pole, which varies; for example $\frac{1}{z-2}$ has $R = 2$.

@quiz num

answer: 15/16
check: (lambda g: quad(lambda t: g(t).real, 0, 2*pi) + I*quad(lambda t: g(t).imag, 0, 2*pi))(lambda t: 1/((0.5*exp(1j*t) - 1)*(0.5*exp(1j*t) - 2))*exp(-3j*t))/(2*pi*0.5**3)
hint: Partial fractions: $\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)} = \frac{1}{z-2} - \frac{1}{z-1}$.
hint: Expand $-\frac{1}{z-1} = \frac{1}{1-z}$ and $\frac{1}{z-2} = -\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - z/2}$, then add the $z^3$ coefficients.
solution: $\dfrac{1}{1 - z} = \sum z^n$ contributes $1$, and $-\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - z/2} = -\sum \dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$ contributes $-\dfrac{1}{16}$ at $n = 3$. The coefficient is $1 - \dfrac1{16} = \dfrac{15}{16}$.

@quiz self
prompt: Starting from the geometric series $\dfrac{1}{1 + w} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty(-w)^n$ for $\lvert w\rvert < 1$, derive the Maclaurin series of $\operatorname{Log}(1 + z)$ and state its radius of convergence.
hint: Note that $\frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{Log}(1+z) = \frac{1}{1+z}$. Integrate the geometric series termwise from $0$ to $z$.
hint: Termwise integration is allowed inside the disk of convergence of the series. What fixes the constant of integration?
solution: For $\lvert z\rvert < 1$, $\operatorname{Log}(1+z) = \int_0^z\dfrac{dw}{1 + w} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty(-1)^n\int_0^z w^n\,dw = \sum_{n=0}^\infty\dfrac{(-1)^nz^{n+1}}{n+1} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty\dfrac{(-1)^{n+1}z^n}{n}$, using $\operatorname{Log}1 = 0$ for the constant. The radius is $1$, matching the distance from $0$ to the branch point $z = -1$.
rubric: Uses $\frac{d}{dz}\operatorname{Log}(1+z) = \frac{1}{1+z}$ and integrates the geometric series termwise on $\lvert z\rvert < 1$.
rubric: Determines the constant of integration from $\operatorname{Log}(1) = 0$.
rubric: Writes the series $\sum_{n\ge1}(-1)^{n+1}z^n/n$ with correct index shift.
rubric: States $R = 1$ and links it to the singularity at $z = -1$.
