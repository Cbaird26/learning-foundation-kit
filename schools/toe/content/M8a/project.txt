@review
### Week by week

1. **Complex arithmetic.** $z = x + iy = re^{i\theta}$, $z\bar z = \lvert z\rvert^2$, divide by multiplying by the conjugate. The $n$-th roots of $re^{i\theta}$ are $r^{1/n}e^{i(\theta + 2\pi k)/n}$. Principal argument is in $(-\pi, \pi]$.
2. **Analytic functions.** The complex derivative cannot depend on direction. $\log z = \ln\lvert z\rvert + i\arg z$ and $z^a = e^{a\log z}$ are multivalued; $\operatorname{Log}$ has its cut on $(-\infty, 0]$.
3. **Cauchy-Riemann.** $u_x = v_y$, $u_y = -v_x$, $f' = u_x + iv_x$; continuous partials plus CR on an open set give analyticity. Real and imaginary parts are harmonic.
4. **Contour integrals.** $\int f(z(t))\,z'(t)\,dt$; $\oint (z-c)^n\,dz = 2\pi i\,\delta_{n,-1}$; antiderivatives give path independence; $\lvert\int f\,dz\rvert \le ML$.
5. **Cauchy theorem.** Analytic on a simply connected domain gives $\oint = 0$. Deform contours across analytic territory.
6. **Integral formula.** $f^{(n)}(a) = \frac{n!}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f}{(z-a)^{n+1}}dz$, the mean value property, and $\lvert f^{(n)}(a)\rvert \le n!M/R^n$ (so bounded entire functions are constant).
7. **Taylor.** $c_n = f^{(n)}(a)/n!$; the radius is the distance to the nearest singularity; zeros of order $m$ are isolated.
8. **Laurent.** The series depends on the annulus. Singularities are removable, poles of order $m$, or essential.
9. **Residues.** $g/h'$ at simple poles, the derivative formula at poles of order $m$, series for essential singularities; $\oint = 2\pi i\sum\operatorname{Res}$.
10. **Real integrals.** Rational over the line, Fourier type (Jordan's lemma, upper half-plane for $k>0$), and trigonometric via $z = e^{i\theta}$. Always justify the arc.
11. **Argument principle.** $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f'}{f}dz = N - P$ equals the winding number of $f(\gamma)$ about $0$; Rouche compares $\lvert g\rvert < \lvert f\rvert$ on the contour.
12. **Conformal maps.** Analytic with $f' \neq 0$ preserves angles. Map boundary pieces, then test a point. Mobius maps send circles and lines to circles and lines.

### Self-check

- I can say which hypotheses Cauchy's theorem and the residue theorem need and check them for a given contour.
- I can expand a function in a Taylor or Laurent series on the correct disk or annulus.
- I can classify an isolated singularity from its series, and find a pole's order despite numerator cancellation.
- I can choose a contour for each of the three real-integral types and justify the arc.
- I can count zeros with Rouche, and say what the argument principle returns when there are poles.
- I can map a simple domain by tracking each boundary piece.

### Common errors

- Counting singularities outside the contour, or missing one inside.
- Using $\lim(z-a)f(z)$ at a higher-order pole, or forgetting $\frac{1}{(m-1)!}$.
- Declaring "the arc vanishes" without an ML bound or Jordan's lemma, or closing in the wrong half-plane.
- Forgetting the real or imaginary part after using $e^{ikx}$.
- Using one branch of $\log$ or a root without stating the cut.
- Applying Rouche with a non-strict inequality or on only part of the contour.

prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{x^2 + 2x + 2}\,dx$. Give an exact value using $e$, $\pi$ and $\cos 1$ or $\sin 1$.
answer: pi*e^(-1)*cos(1)
check: nsum(lambda k: quad(lambda x: cos(x)/(x**2 + 2*x + 2), 2*k*pi, 2*(k + 1)*pi), -500, 500)
hint: Complete the square: $x^2 + 2x + 2 = (x+1)^2 + 1$, so the poles of $\frac{e^{iz}}{z^2+2z+2}$ are $z = -1 \pm i$. Which one is in the upper half-plane?
hint: Close in the upper half-plane (since $k = 1 > 0$) and use Jordan's lemma for the arc. The residue at $z_0 = -1 + i$ is $\dfrac{e^{iz_0}}{z_0 - \bar z_0}$.
hint: $e^{iz_0} = e^{-i}e^{-1}$ and $z_0 - \bar z_0 = 2i$. Take the real part at the end.
solution: Consider $\int\dfrac{e^{ix}}{x^2+2x+2}\,dx$ and close with the upper semicircle; the arc vanishes by Jordan's lemma since $\frac{1}{z^2+2z+2} \to 0$. The only upper pole is $z_0 = -1 + i$ with residue $\dfrac{e^{i(-1+i)}}{2i} = \dfrac{e^{-1}e^{-i}}{2i}$. So the integral is $2\pi i\cdot\dfrac{e^{-1}e^{-i}}{2i} = \pi e^{-1}(\cos 1 - i\sin 1)$. The cosine integral is the real part, $\pi e^{-1}\cos 1 \approx 0.6244$. (The sine integral is $-\pi e^{-1}\sin 1$.)

prompt: Find the residue of $f(z) = \dfrac{e^z}{z^2}\sin\dfrac{1}{z}$ at $z = 0$, rounded to four decimal places.
answer: 0.4931
tol: 0.0005
check: (lambda g: quad(lambda t: g(t).real, 0, 2*pi) + I*quad(lambda t: g(t).imag, 0, 2*pi))(lambda t: exp(exp(1j*t))/exp(2j*t)*sin(exp(-1j*t))*exp(1j*t))/(2*pi)
hint: The singularity at $0$ is essential, so expand both factors in series: $e^z = \sum_k \frac{z^k}{k!}$ and $\sin\frac1z = \sum_j \frac{(-1)^j}{(2j+1)!}z^{-(2j+1)}$.
hint: Multiply by $z^{-2}$ and collect the coefficient of $z^{-1}$: you need $k - 2j - 3 = -1$.
hint: The residue is $\sum_{j\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^j}{(2j+1)!\,(2j+2)!}$. The first three terms are enough for four decimals.
solution: $\dfrac{e^z}{z^2}\sin\dfrac1z = \sum_{k\ge0}\sum_{j\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^j}{k!\,(2j+1)!}z^{k-2j-3}$. The exponent equals $-1$ when $k = 2j + 2$, so $c_{-1} = \sum_{j\ge0}\dfrac{(-1)^j}{(2j+1)!\,(2j+2)!} = \dfrac{1}{2} - \dfrac{1}{144} + \dfrac{1}{86400} - \cdots \approx 0.4931$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $p(z) = a_nz^n + a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$ with $n \ge 1$ and $a_n \neq 0$. Use Rouche's theorem to prove that $p$ has exactly $n$ zeros in the plane, counted with multiplicity, and give a radius $R$ such that all of them lie in $\lvert z\rvert < R$.
hint: Compare $p$ with its leading term $a_nz^n$ on a large circle $\lvert z\rvert = R$.
hint: Bound the lower-order terms with the triangle inequality, using $R \ge 1$ so that $R^k \le R^{n-1}$ for $k \le n - 1$.
hint: Set $S = \lvert a_{n-1}\rvert + \cdots + \lvert a_0\rvert$ and find when $S R^{n-1} < \lvert a_n\rvert R^n$.
solution: Let $S = \lvert a_{n-1}\rvert + \cdots + \lvert a_0\rvert$ and choose $R > \max\{1, S/\lvert a_n\rvert\}$. On $\lvert z\rvert = R$, take $f = a_nz^n$ and $g = a_{n-1}z^{n-1} + \cdots + a_0$. Since $R \ge 1$, $\lvert g(z)\rvert \le S R^{n-1} < \lvert a_n\rvert R^n = \lvert f(z)\rvert$ at every point of the circle. By Rouche's theorem $p = f + g$ has the same number of zeros in $\lvert z\rvert < R$ as $a_nz^n$, which has a zero of order $n$ at $0$. So $p$ has exactly $n$ zeros in $\lvert z\rvert < R$. The same argument applies to every larger radius, and the count stays equal to $n$, so $p$ has no zeros outside: all $n$ zeros lie in $\lvert z\rvert < R$.
rubric: Chooses the leading term $a_nz^n$ as the dominant function on a large circle and the remaining terms as the perturbation.
rubric: Proves $\lvert g\rvert < \lvert f\rvert$ at every point of the circle with an explicit triangle-inequality bound and an explicit $R$.
rubric: Applies Rouche correctly to conclude $p$ has $n$ zeros (with multiplicity) in $\lvert z\rvert < R$.
rubric: Explains why no zeros lie outside the disk, for example by letting the radius grow.
