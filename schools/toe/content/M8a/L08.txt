objective: Classify an isolated singularity as removable, a pole of order $m$, or essential, and find the order of a pole.

@article
A Taylor series needs analyticity on a whole disk. Near a singularity that fails, but there is a replacement. If $f$ is analytic on the **annulus** $r < \lvert z - a\rvert < R$, then there is a unique **Laurent series**

$$f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n, \qquad c_n = \frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\lvert z - a\rvert = \rho}\frac{f(z)}{(z-a)^{n+1}}\,dz,$$

for any $\rho$ with $r < \rho < R$. The terms with $n < 0$ form the **principal part**. The series depends on the annulus: one function has a different Laurent series on each region between its singularities.

### Singularities

The point $a$ is an **isolated singularity** of $f$ if $f$ is analytic on a punctured disk $0 < \lvert z - a\rvert < R$ but not at $a$ itself. Use the Laurent series on that punctured disk:

| type | principal part | behavior as $z \to a$ |
|---|---|---|
| **removable** | none | $f(z)$ tends to a finite limit |
| **pole of order $m$** | ends at $c_{-m} \neq 0$ | $\lvert f(z)\rvert \to \infty$ |
| **essential** | infinitely many terms | no limit, finite or infinite |

A **simple pole** is a pole of order $1$. Near an essential singularity $f$ takes values arbitrarily close to every complex number, which is why it is called wild.

Equivalent test for a pole: $f$ has a pole of order $m$ at $a$ exactly when $f(z) = \dfrac{g(z)}{(z-a)^m}$ with $g$ analytic at $a$ and $g(a) \neq 0$. For a quotient $h/k$, count orders: if $h(a) \neq 0$ and $k$ has a zero of order $m$ at $a$, the pole has order $m$.

### Computing Laurent series

Do not integrate; rearrange known series.

- **Geometric series.** For $\lvert z\rvert > 2$, write $\frac{1}{z - 2} = \frac{1}{z}\cdot\frac{1}{1 - 2/z} = \sum_{n\ge0}2^nz^{-n-1}$. For $\lvert z\rvert < 2$ use $-\frac12\sum (z/2)^n$ instead.
- **Substitution.** $e^{1/z} = \sum_{n\ge0}\frac{1}{n!\,z^n}$ has infinitely many negative powers.
- **Division by a power.** $\dfrac{\sin z}{z^4} = z^{-3} - \dfrac{z^{-1}}{6} + \dfrac{z}{120} - \cdots$.

The coefficient $c_{-1}$ is the **residue**, the subject of next week.

### Order is not read off the denominator

A zero in the denominator gives an upper bound on the pole order; the numerator can cancel part of it. For $\dfrac{\cos z - 1}{z^3}$ the numerator is $-\frac{z^2}{2} + \cdots$, so the function is $-\frac{1}{2z} + \cdots$, a simple pole and not a pole of order $3$.

@example
title: A Laurent series on an annulus
problem: Find the Laurent series of $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)}$ valid for $1 < \lvert z\rvert < 2$.
step: Partial fractions: $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{z-2} - \dfrac{1}{z-1}$.
step: Since $\lvert z\rvert < 2$, expand around $0$: $\dfrac{1}{z-2} = -\dfrac12\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - z/2} = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}}$.
step: Since $\lvert z\rvert > 1$, expand in powers of $1/z$: $-\dfrac{1}{z-1} = -\dfrac1z\cdot\dfrac{1}{1 - 1/z} = -\sum_{n=1}^\infty z^{-n}$.
step: Adding, $f(z) = -\sum_{n=0}^\infty\dfrac{z^n}{2^{n+1}} - \sum_{n=1}^\infty z^{-n}$ on $1 < \lvert z\rvert < 2$. The principal part is $-z^{-1} - z^{-2} - \cdots$, so $c_{-1} = -1$ on this annulus; this equals the residue of $f$ at the singularity $z = 1$ inside the inner circle.

@example
title: Three singularities at $z = 0$
problem: Classify the singularity at $z = 0$ of (a) $\dfrac{\sin z}{z}$, (b) $\dfrac{\cos z - 1}{z^3}$, (c) $e^{1/z}$.
step: (a) $\dfrac{\sin z}{z} = 1 - \dfrac{z^2}{6} + \cdots$ has no negative powers. The limit is $1$, so the singularity is **removable** (define the value at $0$ to be $1$).
step: (b) $\cos z - 1 = -\dfrac{z^2}{2} + \dfrac{z^4}{24} - \cdots$, so $\dfrac{\cos z - 1}{z^3} = -\dfrac{1}{2z} + \dfrac{z}{24} - \cdots$. The lowest power is $z^{-1}$: a **simple pole** (order $1$, not $3$).
step: (c) $e^{1/z} = \sum_{n\ge0}\dfrac{1}{n!\,z^n}$ has infinitely many negative powers: an **essential singularity**.
step: Behavior check for (c): as $z \to 0^+$, $e^{1/z} \to \infty$, but as $z \to 0^-$, $e^{1/z} \to 0$. No limit exists, consistent with an essential singularity.

@mistakes
- Reading the pole order from the denominator alone, ignoring cancellation with the numerator.
- Using one Laurent series for the whole plane; the expansion depends on which annulus you are in.
- Calling a removable singularity (like $\frac{\sin z}{z}$ at $0$) a pole because the formula has a zero in the denominator.
- Treating "infinitely many negative powers" as a pole of high order; a pole has finitely many negative powers.

@practice self
prompt: Locate and classify all isolated singularities of $f(z) = \dfrac{1}{z\,(e^z - 1)}$.
hint: Find where the denominator vanishes. Recall $e^z = 1$ exactly when $z = 2\pi i k$ for an integer $k$.
hint: Treat $z = 0$ separately from $z = 2\pi i k$ with $k \neq 0$. Expand $e^z - 1$ near $0$ to find the order of the zero of the denominator there.
solution: The denominator vanishes at $z = 0$ and wherever $e^z = 1$, i.e. $z = 2\pi i k$. For $k \neq 0$, $z \neq 0$ and $e^z - 1$ has a simple zero (its derivative $e^z = 1 \neq 0$), so $f$ has a simple pole at $2\pi i k$. At $z = 0$, $z(e^z - 1) = z\left(z + \tfrac{z^2}{2} + \cdots\right) = z^2\left(1 + \tfrac z2 + \cdots\right)$, so $f(z) = z^{-2}\left(1 + \tfrac z2 + \cdots\right)^{-1}$ has a pole of order $2$. There are no other singularities, and all of these are isolated.
rubric: Finds all singular points: $z = 0$ and $z = 2\pi i k$, $k \neq 0$.
rubric: Shows $e^z - 1$ has a simple zero at each $2\pi i k$ and concludes simple poles for $k \neq 0$.
rubric: Combines the factor $z$ with the simple zero of $e^z - 1$ at $0$ to get a double zero, hence a pole of order $2$ at $0$.

answer: 3
check: (lambda g: quad(lambda t: g(t).real, 0, 2*pi) + I*quad(lambda t: g(t).imag, 0, 2*pi))(lambda t: 1/((3*exp(1j*t) - 1)*(3*exp(1j*t) - 2))*(3*exp(1j*t))**3)/(2*pi)
hint: Use $\frac{1}{(z-1)(z-2)} = \frac{1}{z-2} - \frac{1}{z-1}$ and expand each in powers of $1/z$ (valid since $\lvert z\rvert > 2$).
hint: $\frac{1}{z-a} = \sum_{n\ge0}a^nz^{-n-1}$. The $z^{-3}$ term has $n = 2$.
solution: $\dfrac{1}{z-2} = \sum 2^nz^{-n-1}$ and $-\dfrac{1}{z-1} = -\sum z^{-n-1}$, so the coefficient of $z^{-n-1}$ is $2^n - 1$. For $z^{-3}$, $n = 2$ and the coefficient is $4 - 1 = 3$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement correctly describes $f(z) = e^{1/z}$ near $z = 0$?
choice*: It has an essential singularity: $f \to \infty$ as $z \to 0^+$ but $f \to 0$ as $z \to 0^-$.
choice: It has a simple pole, because the exponent of $z$ is $-1$.
choice: It has a removable singularity, because $e^w$ is entire.
choice: It has a pole, because $\lvert f\rvert \to \infty$ in every direction.
hint: Compare $e^{1/z}$ for small positive $z$ and small negative $z$.
hint: A pole requires $\lvert f\rvert \to \infty$ from every direction, and a removable singularity requires a finite limit.
solution: For small $z > 0$, $1/z$ is large and positive, so $e^{1/z}$ is huge; for small $z < 0$, $1/z$ is large and negative, so $e^{1/z} \approx 0$. There is no limit, finite or infinite, and the Laurent series has infinitely many negative powers: an essential singularity.
