@article
Suppose $f$ is meromorphic (analytic except for poles) on and inside a simple closed counterclockwise contour $\gamma$, with no zeros or poles on $\gamma$. Let $N$ be the number of zeros inside and $P$ the number of poles inside, **each counted with multiplicity** (order). The **argument principle** says

$$\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_\gamma\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz = N - P.$$

### Why it works

Near a zero of order $m$ at $a$, write $f(z) = (z-a)^mg(z)$ with $g$ analytic and $g(a) \neq 0$. Then

$$\frac{f'}{f} = \frac{m}{z-a} + \frac{g'}{g},$$

so $f'/f$ has a simple pole with residue $+m$. At a pole of order $m$, $f = (z-a)^{-m}g$ gives residue $-m$. Everywhere else inside $\gamma$, $f'/f$ is analytic. The residue theorem adds up the residues to give $N - P$.

### The picture: winding numbers

Since $\frac{f'}{f}\,dz = d(\ln\lvert f\rvert) + i\,d(\arg f)$ and $\ln\lvert f\rvert$ returns to its starting value around a closed loop,

$$N - P = \frac{1}{2\pi}\,\Delta_\gamma\arg f,$$

the number of times the image curve $f(\gamma)$ winds counterclockwise around $0$. You can find $N - P$ by plotting the image of the boundary and counting its turns. For example, as $z$ goes once around the unit circle, $z^3$ goes three times around the origin, matching its triple zero at $0$, while $1/z$ goes once around clockwise, matching its simple pole.

### Rouche's theorem

If $f$ and $g$ are analytic on and inside $\gamma$ and $\lvert g(z)\rvert < \lvert f(z)\rvert$ at **every** point of $\gamma$, then $f$ and $f + g$ have the same number of zeros inside $\gamma$. The reason: $\lvert g/f\rvert < 1$ keeps $(f+g)/f = 1 + g/f$ in the disk $\lvert w - 1\rvert < 1$, which does not surround $0$, so $f + g$ and $f$ wind around $0$ equally often.

To count the zeros of a polynomial in a disk, pick the dominant term on the boundary circle.

### Physics link

For a linear system, stability often means "no zeros of a characteristic function in the right half-plane" (or the upper half of the complex frequency plane). The argument principle counts them by tracking the image of the boundary of a large half-disk: this is the idea behind frequency-response stability tests. Poles and zeros of a response function in the complex frequency plane play a similar role: their locations tell you about resonances (the real part of the frequency) and damping (the imaginary part).

### A numerical check

```python
import numpy as np
t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 20001)
z = 2*np.exp(1j*t)                          # the circle |z| = 2
f = (z - 1)**2 * (z + 1) / (z * (z - 3))
phase = np.unwrap(np.angle(f))              # continuous argument of f(z)
print(round((phase[-1] - phase[0]) / (2*np.pi)))   # 2
```

@example
title: Counting zeros minus poles
problem: Let $f(z) = \dfrac{(z-1)^2(z+1)}{z(z-3)}$. Find $\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\lvert z\rvert=2}\dfrac{f'}{f}\,dz$.
step: List the zeros and poles. Zeros: $z = 1$ (order $2$) and $z = -1$ (order $1$). Poles: $z = 0$ (order $1$) and $z = 3$ (order $1$).
step: Keep those inside $\lvert z\rvert = 2$. Zeros: both $1$ and $-1$ are inside, so $N = 2 + 1 = 3$. Poles: $0$ is inside but $3$ is outside, so $P = 1$.
step: None lies on the contour, so the argument principle applies: $\dfrac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\dfrac{f'}{f}\,dz = N - P = 3 - 1 = 2$.
step: Equivalently, the image curve $f(\lvert z\rvert=2)$ winds twice counterclockwise around $0$.

@example
title: Zeros of a polynomial with Rouche
problem: How many zeros does $p(z) = z^5 + 3z + 1$ have in $\lvert z\rvert < 1$, and how many in $1 < \lvert z\rvert < 2$?
step: On $\lvert z\rvert = 1$ take $f = 3z$ and $g = z^5 + 1$. Then $\lvert f\rvert = 3$ and $\lvert g\rvert \le 2 < 3$ everywhere on the circle.
step: By Rouche, $p = f + g$ has as many zeros in $\lvert z\rvert < 1$ as $3z$ does, namely one.
step: On $\lvert z\rvert = 2$ take $f = z^5$ and $g = 3z + 1$. Then $\lvert f\rvert = 32$ and $\lvert g\rvert \le 7 < 32$, so $p$ has the same number of zeros in $\lvert z\rvert < 2$ as $z^5$: five.
step: So $5 - 1 = 4$ zeros lie in the annulus $1 < \lvert z\rvert < 2$. (None are on $\lvert z\rvert = 1$, since the strict inequality there rules it out.)

@mistakes
- Forgetting multiplicity: a double zero counts twice, and a double pole subtracts two.
- Using Rouche with a non-strict or pointwise-failing inequality; $\lvert g\rvert < \lvert f\rvert$ must hold at every point of the contour.
- Applying the argument principle when a zero or pole lies on the contour.
- Reporting $N$ when the function has poles inside; the integral gives $N - P$.
- Orientation: a clockwise contour gives $-(N - P)$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $f$ have a zero of order $m$ at $z = a$. Show that $f'/f$ has a simple pole at $a$ with residue $m$, and explain how this gives the argument principle for zeros.
hint: Write $f(z) = (z-a)^mg(z)$ with $g$ analytic and $g(a) \neq 0$, then differentiate with the product rule.
hint: Note that $g'/g$ is analytic near $a$ because $g(a) \neq 0$.
solution: Write $f(z) = (z-a)^mg(z)$ with $g$ analytic and nonzero at $a$. Then $f'(z) = m(z-a)^{m-1}g(z) + (z-a)^mg'(z)$, so $\dfrac{f'}{f} = \dfrac{m}{z-a} + \dfrac{g'(z)}{g(z)}$. The second term is analytic near $a$ because $g(a) \neq 0$, so $f'/f$ has a simple pole with residue $m$. If $f$ has zeros of orders $m_1, \dots, m_k$ inside $\gamma$ and no poles, the residue theorem gives $\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint\frac{f'}{f}\,dz = m_1 + \cdots + m_k$, the number of zeros with multiplicity.
