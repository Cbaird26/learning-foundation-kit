@article
Lifting a book stores gravitational energy; pushing a charge against an electric field stores electric energy. The electric force of static charges is **conservative**, so the work it does between two points does not depend on the path, and we can define a potential energy $U$.

### Potential and potential difference

The **electric potential** is the potential energy per unit charge, $V = U/q_0$, measured in volts ($1\ \text{V} = 1\ \text{J/C}$). Only differences matter physically:

$$V_b - V_a = -\int_a^b \vec E\cdot d\vec l.$$

The minus sign says that moving *along* the field lowers the potential: field lines point from high to low potential. A charge $q$ at potential $V$ has energy $U = qV$, so a positive charge speeds up as it moves to lower $V$, and a negative charge speeds up as it moves to higher $V$. A handy energy unit is the electron volt, $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$.

### Point charges, and why potential is easier than field

Choosing $V = 0$ at infinity, a point charge $q$ gives

$$V = \frac{kq}{r}, \qquad U_{12} = \frac{kq_1 q_2}{r}.$$

Potential is a **scalar**, so potentials of several sources add as signed numbers, with no components and no directions:

$$V = \sum_i \frac{kq_i}{r_i}, \qquad V = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r}.$$

The energy of a collection of charges is the sum over **distinct pairs**, each pair counted once.

### Energy conservation

For a particle released from rest, $\tfrac12 mv^2 = q\,(V_\text{start} - V_\text{end})$. An electron accelerated through $100\ \text{V}$ gains $100\ \text{eV}$ of kinetic energy.

### Field from potential

If you know $V$ everywhere, differentiate it:

$$E_x = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}, \quad E_y = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial y}, \quad E_z = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial z}, \qquad \vec E = -\nabla V.$$

The field points in the direction of steepest decrease of $V$ and is perpendicular to **equipotential surfaces**. Where $V$ is flat, the field is zero, even if $V$ itself is not zero. In a uniform field along $x$, $V$ changes linearly: $\Delta V = -E\,\Delta x$.

### A worked connection to week 1

For a ring of charge $Q$ and radius $R$, every element is the same distance $\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ from a point on the axis, so the integral for $V$ is trivial: $V(z) = kQ/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$. Differentiating gives $E_z = kQz/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}$, the result that took a vector argument in week 1. Doing the scalar integral first and then differentiating is often the shortcut.

@example
title: Potential and field on the axis of a ring
problem: A ring of radius $R = 0.10\ \text{m}$ carries total charge $Q = 5.0\ \text{nC}$. Find the potential and the axial field at $z = 0.20\ \text{m}$ from its center, with $V = 0$ at infinity and $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
step: Every element $dq$ of the ring is the same distance $s = \sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ from the point, so $V = \int \dfrac{k\,dq}{s} = \dfrac{kQ}{s}$ with no angular dependence.
step: Evaluate: $s = \sqrt{0.01 + 0.04} = 0.2236\ \text{m}$, so $V = (8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})/0.2236 \approx 201\ \text{V}$.
step: To get the field, keep $z$ general: $V(z) = kQ\,(R^2 + z^2)^{-1/2}$, so $E_z = -\dfrac{dV}{dz} = kQ\,z\,(R^2+z^2)^{-3/2}$.
step: Evaluate: $E_z = (8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})(0.20)/(0.05)^{3/2} \approx 804\ \text{N/C}$, positive, so the field points away from the ring along $+z$.
step: Check the limit: for $z \gg R$, $V \to kQ/z$ and $E_z \to kQ/z^2$, the point-charge results.

@mistakes
- Adding potentials as if they were vectors, or leaving out the sign of a negative charge's contribution.
- Concluding that $E = 0$ wherever $V = 0$ (or the reverse). The field depends on the slope of $V$, not its value.
- Counting each pair of charges twice when computing the potential energy of a collection.

@practice num
prompt: An electron starts at rest and is accelerated through a potential difference of $250\ \text{V}$. Using $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$ and $m = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, find its final speed. Ignore relativity.
answer: 9.38e6
check: sqrt(2*1.602e-19*250/9.109e-31)
unit: m/s
tol: 1%
hint: The kinetic energy gained equals the charge times the potential difference.
hint: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = eV$, so $v = \sqrt{2eV/m}$.
solution: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = eV = (1.602\times10^{-19})(250) = 4.005\times10^{-17}\ \text{J}$. Then $v = \sqrt{2(4.005\times10^{-17})/(9.109\times10^{-31})} \approx 9.38\times10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$.

@practice mc
prompt: At the midpoint between two equal positive point charges, which statement is correct (with $V = 0$ at infinity)?
choice: The electric field is zero and the potential is zero.
choice*: The electric field is zero and the potential is positive.
choice: The electric field is nonzero and the potential is zero.
choice: The electric field is nonzero and the potential is positive.
hint: The field is a vector sum and the potential is a scalar sum. Which one can cancel between two like charges?
hint: By symmetry the two fields at the midpoint are equal and opposite. Each potential contribution is $+kq/r$.
solution: The two field vectors point in opposite directions with equal size, so $\vec E = 0$. The potentials are scalars with the same sign, so they add: $V = 2kq/r > 0$. A zero field only says the slope of $V$ vanishes there, not that $V$ is zero.

@practice expr
prompt: A charge $+q$ is at $x = 0$ and a charge $-q$ is at $x = d$ on the $x$-axis. For $x > d$, the potential on the axis is $V(x) = kq\left(\dfrac{1}{x} - \dfrac{1}{x - d}\right)$. Find the field component $E_x(x)$.
vars: k, q, d, x
lead: $E_x =$
answer: k*q*(1/x^2 - 1/(x - d)^2)
check: lambda k, q, d, x: -nderiv(lambda s: k*q*(1/s - 1/(s - d)), x)
domain: k: 0.5..2; q: 0.5..2; d: 0.3..1.0; x: 1.5..3.0
hint: $E_x = -dV/dx$. Differentiate each term separately.
hint: $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{x} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}\dfrac{1}{x-d} = -\dfrac{1}{(x-d)^2}$.
solution: $E_x = -\dfrac{dV}{dx} = -kq\left(-\dfrac{1}{x^2} + \dfrac{1}{(x-d)^2}\right) = kq\left(\dfrac{1}{x^2} - \dfrac{1}{(x-d)^2}\right)$. For $x > d$ this is negative: the nearer negative charge dominates and the field points toward it, in $-x$.

@practice num
prompt: Three point charges of $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ each sit at the corners of an equilateral triangle with side $0.10\ \text{m}$. Find the electric potential energy of the system, with zero energy when the charges are infinitely far apart. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: 1.08
check: sum(8.99e9*(2.0e-9)**2/math.dist(a, b) for a, b in [((0, 0), (0.10, 0)), ((0, 0), (0.05, 0.05*sqrt(3))), ((0.10, 0), (0.05, 0.05*sqrt(3)))])*1e6
unit: microjoules
tol: 1%
hint: Count each distinct pair of charges once. How many pairs are there among three charges?
hint: There are three pairs, all at the same separation, each contributing $kq^2/a$.
solution: $U = 3\,\dfrac{kq^2}{a} = 3\,\dfrac{(8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-9})^2}{0.10} = 1.08\times10^{-6}\ \text{J} = 1.08\ \mu\text{J}$. The positive energy is the work an outside agent did to bring the like charges together.

@quiz num
prompt: A small sphere of mass $2.0\ \text{g}$ carrying $+5.0\ \text{nC}$ is released from rest at $0.020\ \text{m}$ from a fixed point charge of $+50\ \text{nC}$. How fast is it moving when it is very far away? Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$ and ignore gravity.
answer: 0.335
check: sqrt(2*quad(lambda r: 8.99e9*50e-9*5e-9/r**2, 0.020, 1.0e4)/2.0e-3)
unit: m/s
tol: 1%
hint: The sphere ends with kinetic energy equal to the electric potential energy it started with.
hint: $\tfrac12 mv^2 = kQq/r_0$. Convert the mass to kilograms.
solution: $U_0 = kQq/r_0 = (8.99\times10^{9})(50\times10^{-9})(5.0\times10^{-9})/0.020 = 1.124\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$. Then $v = \sqrt{2U_0/m} = \sqrt{2(1.124\times10^{-4})/(2.0\times10^{-3})} \approx 0.335\ \text{m/s}$.

@quiz num
prompt: In a uniform electric field of $1200\ \text{V/m}$ directed along $+x$, find the potential difference $V_b - V_a$ between $a = (0.10\ \text{m}, 0.20\ \text{m})$ and $b = (0.40\ \text{m}, 0.60\ \text{m})$.
answer: -360
check: -quad(lambda t: 1200*(0.40 - 0.10), 0, 1)
unit: V
hint: Use $V_b - V_a = -\int \vec E\cdot d\vec l$ along any path. Only the component of the displacement along the field contributes.
hint: The displacement along $x$ is $0.30$ m; the change in $y$ is perpendicular to $\vec E$ and contributes nothing.
solution: $V_b - V_a = -E_x\,\Delta x = -(1200)(0.30) = -360\ \text{V}$. The potential drops by $360$ V because the displacement has a component along the field.
