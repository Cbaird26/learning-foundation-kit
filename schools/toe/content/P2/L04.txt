@article
A **capacitor** is a pair of conductors that carry equal and opposite charges $+Q$ and $-Q$. Charge piles up in proportion to the voltage you apply, and the constant of proportionality is the **capacitance**:

$$C = \frac{Q}{\Delta V}, \qquad [C] = \text{F} = \text{C/V}.$$

Here $\Delta V$ is the positive potential difference between the conductors. Capacitance depends only on geometry (and the material between the conductors), never on $Q$ or $\Delta V$.

### A four-step recipe

1. Put charge $+Q$ on one conductor and $-Q$ on the other.
2. Find $\vec E$ between them, usually with Gauss's law.
3. Integrate along any path from the negative to the positive conductor: $\Delta V = \int \vec E\cdot d\vec l$ taken against the field, which is $\int E\,dl$ in magnitude.
4. Divide: $C = Q/\Delta V$.

Step 3 is exactly "a potential difference is a field integral".

### Three geometries

- **Parallel plates**, area $A$, gap $d$: $E = Q/(\varepsilon_0 A)$ is uniform, $\Delta V = Ed$, so $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$.
- **Concentric spheres**, radii $a < b$: $E = kQ/r^2$, so $\Delta V = kQ\left(\frac1a - \frac1b\right)$ and $C = \dfrac{4\pi\varepsilon_0\,ab}{b-a}$. Letting $b\to\infty$ gives an isolated sphere, $C = 4\pi\varepsilon_0 a$.
- **Coaxial cylinders**, radii $a < b$, length $\ell \gg b$: $E = 2k\lambda/r$ with $\lambda = Q/\ell$, so $\Delta V = 2k\lambda\ln(b/a)$ and $C = \dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0\,\ell}{\ln(b/a)}$.

### Combinations

**Parallel:** the capacitors share the same $\Delta V$, and charges add, so $C_\text{eq} = C_1 + C_2 + \cdots$.

**Series:** the capacitors carry the same charge $Q$, and voltages add, so $\dfrac{1}{C_\text{eq}} = \dfrac{1}{C_1} + \dfrac{1}{C_2} + \cdots$.

Reduce a network step by step, then work backward: the equivalent capacitor gives the total charge, which you split as the circuit dictates.

### Energy and dielectrics

Charging a capacitor from zero to $Q$ requires work $\int \Delta V\,dq$, which is stored as

$$U = \tfrac12 Q\,\Delta V = \tfrac12 C(\Delta V)^2 = \frac{Q^2}{2C}, \qquad u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0 E^2 \ \text{(energy density).}$$

Filling the gap with an insulator of dielectric constant $\kappa > 1$ multiplies the capacitance: $C = \kappa C_0$. What happens next depends on what you hold fixed. If a battery stays connected, $\Delta V$ is fixed and the charge increases to $\kappa Q_0$. If the capacitor is isolated, $Q$ is fixed, the voltage drops to $\Delta V_0/\kappa$, and the stored energy falls to $U_0/\kappa$.

@example
title: Capacitance of a spherical capacitor from the field
problem: An inner sphere of radius $a = 0.020\ \text{m}$ lies inside a concentric conducting shell of inner radius $b = 0.050\ \text{m}$. Find the capacitance, using $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)$.
step: Put $+Q$ on the inner sphere and $-Q$ on the shell. Gauss's law with a concentric sphere of radius $r$ between them gives $E(r) = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}$, pointing outward.
step: Integrate the field from the negative (outer) conductor to the positive (inner) one, against the field. In magnitude, $\Delta V = \int_a^b E\,dr = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\dfrac1a - \dfrac1b\right)$.
step: Form the ratio: $C = \dfrac{Q}{\Delta V} = 4\pi\varepsilon_0\,\dfrac{ab}{b-a}$. The $Q$ has canceled, as it must.
step: Numbers: $4\pi\varepsilon_0 = 1.112\times10^{-10}\ \text{F/m}$ and $\dfrac{ab}{b-a} = \dfrac{(0.020)(0.050)}{0.030} = 0.0333\ \text{m}$, so $C \approx 3.7\times10^{-12}\ \text{F} = 3.7\ \text{pF}$.
step: Check: as $b \to \infty$ the result tends to $4\pi\varepsilon_0 a = 2.2\ \text{pF}$, the isolated sphere. Our value, 3.7 pF, is larger because the nearby shell makes it easier to hold charge, as expected.

@mistakes
- Thinking $C$ depends on the charge or voltage; it depends on geometry and material only.
- Saying capacitors in series have equal voltage. They carry equal charge, and the smaller capacitance takes the larger voltage.
- Not asking what is held fixed when a dielectric is inserted: constant voltage (battery connected) and constant charge (isolated) give different energies.

@practice num
prompt: A parallel-plate capacitor has square plates of area $0.040\ \text{m}^2$ separated by $1.0\ \text{mm}$ of vacuum. Using $\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)$, find its capacitance in picofarads.
answer: 354
check: 8.85e-12*0.040/1.0e-3*1e12
unit: pF
tol: 1%
hint: Use the geometry formula for parallel plates, with the gap in meters.
hint: $C = \varepsilon_0 A/d$ with $d = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ m; then convert from farads to picofarads ($10^{-12}$ F).
solution: $C = (8.85\times10^{-12})(0.040)/(1.0\times10^{-3}) = 3.54\times10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 354\ \text{pF}$.

@practice num
prompt: How much energy is stored in a $4.7\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor charged to $12\ \text{V}$? Give the answer in microjoules.
answer: 338
check: 0.5*4.7e-6*12**2*1e6
unit: microjoules
tol: 1%
hint: Use the form of the energy that involves $C$ and $\Delta V$.
hint: $U = \tfrac12 C(\Delta V)^2$. Keep $C$ in farads, then convert joules to microjoules.
solution: $U = \tfrac12(4.7\times10^{-6})(12)^2 = 3.38\times10^{-4}\ \text{J} = 338\ \mu\text{J}$.

@practice mc
prompt: A $2\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor and a $6\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor are connected in series across a $12\ \text{V}$ battery. Which statement is correct?
choice: Each capacitor has $12\ \text{V}$ across it.
choice*: Both carry the same charge, and the $2\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor has the larger voltage.
choice: Both carry the same charge, and the $6\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor has the larger voltage.
choice: The $6\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor stores more charge because its capacitance is larger.
hint: In a series chain there is no place for charge to leave the middle plates, so what must be true of the charge on each capacitor?
hint: With equal $Q$, use $\Delta V = Q/C$ for each. Which $C$ is smaller?
solution: Series capacitors carry the same charge $Q = C_\text{eq}\Delta V = (1.5\ \mu\text{F})(12\ \text{V}) = 18\ \mu\text{C}$. Then $\Delta V_1 = Q/C_1 = 9\ \text{V}$ and $\Delta V_2 = 3\ \text{V}$. The smaller capacitance takes the larger voltage, and the voltages add to $12$ V.

@practice expr
prompt: A capacitor $C_1$ is connected in parallel with a series pair made of $C_2$ and $C_3$. Find the equivalent capacitance of the combination.
vars: C_1, C_2, C_3
lead: $C_\text{eq} =$
answer: C_1 + C_2*C_3/(C_2 + C_3)
check: lambda C_1, C_2, C_3: C_1 + 1/(1/C_2 + 1/C_3)
hint: Reduce the series pair first to a single capacitor, then add it in parallel.
hint: Two capacitors in series give $\dfrac{1}{C_2} + \dfrac{1}{C_3}$ for the reciprocal; invert it, then add $C_1$.
solution: The series pair has $C_{23} = \left(\frac{1}{C_2} + \frac{1}{C_3}\right)^{-1} = \frac{C_2C_3}{C_2+C_3}$. In parallel with $C_1$, the capacitances add: $C_\text{eq} = C_1 + \dfrac{C_2C_3}{C_2+C_3}$.

@practice num
prompt: A long coaxial capacitor has an inner conductor of radius $1.0\ \text{mm}$, an outer conductor of radius $4.0\ \text{mm}$ and length $0.50\ \text{m}$. The inner conductor carries $+20\ \text{nC}$ and the outer carries $-20\ \text{nC}$. Neglect end effects and use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Find the potential difference between the conductors by integrating the field.
answer: 997
check: quad(lambda r: 2*8.99e9*(20e-9/0.50)/r, 1.0e-3, 4.0e-3)
unit: V
tol: 1%
hint: Gauss's law with a coaxial cylinder gives $E(r) = 2k\lambda/r$ between the conductors, with $\lambda = Q/\ell$.
hint: $\Delta V = \int_a^b \dfrac{2k\lambda}{r}\,dr = 2k\lambda\ln(b/a)$.
solution: $\lambda = 20\times10^{-9}/0.50 = 4.0\times10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}$, so $2k\lambda = 719.2\ \text{V}$. Then $\Delta V = 719.2\ln 4 = 719.2(1.386) \approx 997\ \text{V}$. The capacitance is $Q/\Delta V \approx 20\ \text{pF}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A $6.0\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor $C_1$ is connected in series with a parallel pair, $C_2 = 3.0\ \mu\text{F}$ and $C_3 = 3.0\ \mu\text{F}$. The whole arrangement is connected across a $12\ \text{V}$ battery. Find the charge on $C_2$ in microcoulombs.
answer: 18
check: 3.0*lin([[6.0, -6.0], [1.0, 1.0]], [0.0, 12.0])[1]
unit: microcoulombs
tol: 1%
hint: The parallel pair acts like a single $6.0\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor in series with $C_1$. Equal charge on each means the voltages split inversely with capacitance.
hint: Find the voltage across the parallel pair, then use $Q_2 = C_2\,\Delta V_{23}$.
solution: $C_{23} = 6.0\ \mu\text{F}$, so the series pair has $C_\text{eq} = 3.0\ \mu\text{F}$ and total charge $36\ \mu\text{C}$. Both $C_1$ and the pair then have $6$ V across them. The charge on $C_2$ is $(3.0\ \mu\text{F})(6\ \text{V}) = 18\ \mu\text{C}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A parallel-plate capacitor with $C_0 = 2.0\ \text{nF}$ is charged to $100\ \text{V}$ and then disconnected from the battery. A dielectric with $\kappa = 4.0$ is inserted so that it fills the gap. Find the energy stored afterwards, in microjoules.
answer: 2.5
check: (2.0e-9*100)**2/(2*(4.0*2.0e-9))*1e6
unit: microjoules
tol: 1%
hint: Once the battery is disconnected, which quantity cannot change: the charge or the voltage?
hint: Use $U = Q^2/(2C)$ with the original $Q$ and the new capacitance $\kappa C_0$.
solution: $Q = C_0\Delta V = 2.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{C}$ stays fixed. The new capacitance is $8.0$ nF, so $U = Q^2/(2C) = (2.0\times10^{-7})^2/(1.6\times10^{-8}) = 2.5\times10^{-6}\ \text{J} = 2.5\ \mu\text{J}$, one quarter of the original $10\ \mu\text{J}$.
