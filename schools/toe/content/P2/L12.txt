@article
Everything you have learned in this course fits into four equations. In vacuum, with integral forms,

$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_\text{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}, \qquad \oint \vec B\cdot d\vec A = 0,$$

$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}, \qquad \oint \vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I_\text{enc} + \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}.$$

The first is Gauss's law and the second says there are no magnetic monopoles. The third is Faraday's law. The last is Ampere's law with Maxwell's addition, the **displacement current** $I_d = \varepsilon_0\,d\Phi_E/dt$. Without it, Ampere's law fails for a charging capacitor: a loop around the wire and a surface that passes between the plates see different enclosed currents. With it, the changing electric flux between the plates restores consistency.

### Why waves?

Faraday's law says a changing $\vec B$ makes an $\vec E$. Maxwell's addition says a changing $\vec E$ makes a $\vec B$. Each field regenerates the other, so a disturbance can propagate through empty space.

### The plane wave

A wave traveling along $+x$ with $\vec E$ along $y$ and $\vec B$ along $z$ is

$$E_y = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t), \qquad B_z = B_0\cos(kx - \omega t),$$

with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ and $\omega = 2\pi f$. The wave is **transverse**: $\vec E \perp \vec B \perp$ direction of travel, and $\vec E\times\vec B$ points along the direction of propagation. Maxwell's equations require

$$c = \frac{\omega}{k} = f\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}} \approx 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}, \qquad E_0 = cB_0.$$

That the speed computed from two constants measured in electrostatics and magnetostatics equals the measured speed of light was the great discovery that identified light as an electromagnetic wave.

### A simple derivation of $E_0 = cB_0$

Apply Faraday's law to a thin rectangle in the $xy$-plane, with height $h$ along $y$ and width $dx$ along $x$. Travel it counterclockwise, so the normal is $+z$. Only the vertical sides contribute to $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$, giving $[E_y(x+dx) - E_y(x)]\,h$. The flux is $B_z\,h\,dx$. Hence

$$\frac{\partial E_y}{\partial x} = -\frac{\partial B_z}{\partial t}.$$

Substituting the wave gives $-kE_0\sin(kx-\omega t) = -\omega B_0\sin(kx-\omega t)$, so $E_0 = (\omega/k)B_0 = cB_0$.

### Energy, intensity, pressure

The electric and magnetic energy densities of a wave are equal, so the total is $u = \varepsilon_0E^2$. The **intensity** (average power per unit area) is

$$I = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0c} = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2,$$

with $E_0$ the peak amplitude. A wave also carries momentum. Its **radiation pressure** is $I/c$ on a perfect absorber and $2I/c$ on a perfect reflector at normal incidence.

@example
title: Properties of a plane wave
problem: A plane electromagnetic wave in vacuum has electric amplitude $E_0 = 150\ \text{V/m}$ and frequency $100\ \text{MHz}$. Take $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}$ and $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$. Find $B_0$, the wavelength, the intensity and the pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface.
step: The magnetic amplitude follows from $E_0 = cB_0$: $B_0 = \dfrac{150}{3.00\times10^{8}} = 5.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{T}$.
step: The wavelength is $\lambda = \dfrac{c}{f} = \dfrac{3.00\times10^{8}}{1.00\times10^{8}} = 3.00\ \text{m}$.
step: The intensity is $I = \dfrac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0c} = \dfrac{(150)^2}{2(4\pi\times10^{-7})(3.00\times10^{8})} \approx 29.8\ \text{W/m}^2$.
step: The pressure on a perfect absorber is $\dfrac{I}{c} = \dfrac{29.8}{3.00\times10^{8}} \approx 1.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{Pa}$. It is tiny, which is why radiation pressure is hard to notice.
step: Check. Dimensions: $E_0^2/(\mu_0c)$ is $(\text{V/m})^2/[(\text{T m/A})(\text{m/s})]$, which reduces to $\text{W/m}^2$. Another check: $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2 = \tfrac12(8.85\times10^{-12})(3.00\times10^{8})(150)^2 \approx 29.9\ \text{W/m}^2$, the same value.

@mistakes
- Using $E_0 = B_0$ or comparing $E$ and $B$ directly; in SI units their amplitudes differ by the factor $c$.
- Putting an rms field amplitude into $I = E_0^2/(2\mu_0c)$, which requires the peak amplitude. In terms of rms, $I = E_\text{rms}^2/(\mu_0c)$.
- Forgetting the factor of 2 for a reflecting surface, or thinking that light waves need a medium to travel through.

choice*: Circuit B charges more slowly (larger time constant) and its capacitor ends with more charge.
choice: Circuit A charges more slowly because its resistance is larger, and its capacitor ends with more charge.
choice: Both charge with the same time constant because the products $RC$ differ only slightly.
choice: Circuit B's capacitor ends at a higher voltage because its capacitance is larger.
hint: Compute $\tau = RC$ for each circuit. Then ask what fixes the final capacitor voltage.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from Faraday's law applied to a thin rectangular loop in the $xy$-plane, derive the relation between the amplitudes of a plane wave $E_y = E_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$, $B_z = B_0\cos(kx - \omega t)$, and show that the wave speed is $\omega/k$.
hint: Choose a direction of travel around the rectangle and write down the matching normal before computing either side of Faraday's law.
hint: Only the two vertical sides contribute to the line integral of $\vec E$. Compare $E_y$ at $x$ and at $x+dx$, and equate the result to $-d\Phi_B/dt$ for the small rectangle.
solution: Take a rectangle of width $dx$ (along $x$) and height $h$ (along $y$), traveled counterclockwise, so its normal is $+z$. Only the vertical sides contribute to $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l$, which equals $[E_y(x+dx) - E_y(x)]h = (\partial E_y/\partial x)\,h\,dx$. The flux through the loop is $B_zh\,dx$, so Faraday's law gives $\partial E_y/\partial x = -\partial B_z/\partial t$. Substituting the wave gives $-kE_0\sin(kx-\omega t) = -\omega B_0\sin(kx-\omega t)$, so $E_0 = (\omega/k)B_0$. The phase $kx - \omega t$ is constant for a point moving with $dx/dt = \omega/k$, which is the wave speed $c$. Hence $E_0 = cB_0$.
rubric: States the loop orientation, the matching normal and the sign convention before applying Faraday's law.
rubric: Obtains $\partial E_y/\partial x = -\partial B_z/\partial t$ from the vertical sides of the rectangle and the flux through it.
rubric: Substitutes the wave form correctly and obtains $E_0 = (\omega/k)B_0$.
rubric: Identifies $\omega/k$ as the phase speed of the wave, which is $c$, and checks the dimensions of $E_0/B_0$.
