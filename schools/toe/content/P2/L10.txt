@article
A changing current in a coil changes the flux through the coil itself, so the coil induces an emf in itself. This effect, **self-inductance**, makes circuits resist sudden changes of current, much as mass resists sudden changes of velocity.

### Self-inductance

For a coil of $N$ turns carrying current $I$, the flux linkage is proportional to the current:

$$N\Phi_B = L\,I, \qquad [L] = \text{H} = \text{V s/A}.$$

Faraday's law then gives the self-induced emf $\mathcal{E}_L = -L\,dI/dt$. Using a clear orientation convention, suppose the current $I$ is defined positive when it flows from terminal $a$ to terminal $b$ through the inductor. Then

$$V_a - V_b = L\,\frac{dI}{dt}.$$

If the current in the direction $a\to b$ is increasing, $a$ is at higher potential (the inductor "pushes back"). If it is decreasing, $dI/dt < 0$ and $b$ is at the higher potential, which tries to keep the current flowing.

For an ideal air-core solenoid with $N$ turns, length $\ell$ and area $A$, $B = \mu_0 NI/\ell$ and so

$$L = \frac{\mu_0 N^2 A}{\ell}.$$

**Mutual inductance** $M$ describes two coils: a changing current $I_1$ in coil 1 induces $\mathcal{E}_2 = -M\,dI_1/dt$ in coil 2, and the same $M$ works in the other direction.

### Energy in the magnetic field

Building up the current from zero to $I$ against the back emf takes work, stored as

$$U = \tfrac12 L I^2, \qquad u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}.$$

You can check consistency with the solenoid: $\tfrac12 LI^2 = \tfrac12\mu_0 n^2 I^2 A\ell$, and $u_B\cdot A\ell$ with $B = \mu_0 nI$ gives the same number. The energy lives in the field, just as the capacitor energy $\tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$ lives in the electric field.

### The RL circuit

Close a switch at $t=0$ to connect a battery $\varepsilon$, a resistor $R$ and an inductor $L$ in series. Travelling around the loop in the direction of the current, the loop rule reads

$$\varepsilon - IR - L\frac{dI}{dt} = 0.$$

The solution with $I(0) = 0$ is

$$I(t) = \frac{\varepsilon}{R}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{L}{R}.$$

The current rises to $63\%$ of its final value in one time constant. Right after the switch closes, the current is still zero and the inductor carries the full emf: $dI/dt = \varepsilon/L$. At long times $dI/dt\to0$ and the inductor acts like a plain wire. If the battery is removed, the current decays as $I = I_0e^{-t/\tau}$.

The current through an inductor is **continuous**; the voltage across it can jump.

@example
title: Switching on an RL circuit
problem: A battery of $\varepsilon = 12\ \text{V}$ is connected through a switch to a $40\ \Omega$ resistor in series with a $0.20\ \text{H}$ inductor. The switch closes at $t=0$. Find the time constant, the final current, the current at $t = \tau$ and the energy stored at $t = \tau$ and at long times.
step: The time constant is $\tau = L/R = 0.20/40 = 5.0\ \text{ms}$.
step: At long times the inductor drops no voltage, so $I_\infty = \varepsilon/R = 12/40 = 0.30\ \text{A}$.
step: At $t = \tau$: $I = I_\infty\,(1 - e^{-1}) = 0.30\,(0.632) = 0.19\ \text{A}$.
step: Energy stored in the inductor: $U = \tfrac12 LI^2$. At $t = \tau$, $U = \tfrac12(0.20)(0.19)^2 = 3.6\ \text{mJ}$. At long times, $U = \tfrac12(0.20)(0.30)^2 = 9.0\ \text{mJ}$.
step: Check. At $t=0$ the loop rule gives $dI/dt = \varepsilon/L = 60\ \text{A/s}$, and the initial slope times $\tau$ gives $0.30\ \text{A}$, the final current, which is the usual geometric meaning of $\tau$.

@mistakes
- Saying the induced emf opposes the current. It opposes the change in current, so it helps when the current is falling.
- Dropping the sign in $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt$, or forgetting that the current direction defining the positive sign must be stated.
- Assuming the current through an inductor can jump. Energy $\tfrac12 LI^2$ cannot change instantly, so $I$ is continuous (the voltage across $L$ can jump).

@practice num
prompt: An air-core solenoid has $400$ turns, length $0.20\ \text{m}$ and cross-sectional area $2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. Find its inductance in microhenries. Use $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$.
answer: 201
check: 4e-7*pi*400**2*2.0e-4/0.20*1e6
unit: microhenries
tol: 1%
hint: First find the flux through one turn, $\Phi = BA$ with $B = \mu_0NI/\ell$, then multiply by $N$ and divide by $I$.
hint: $L = \mu_0N^2A/\ell$. Convert henries to microhenries at the end.
solution: $L = \dfrac{\mu_0N^2A}{\ell} = \dfrac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(400)^2(2.0\times10^{-4})}{0.20} = 2.01\times10^{-4}\ \text{H} = 201\ \mu\text{H}$.

@practice num
prompt: The current in a $0.50\ \text{H}$ inductor falls uniformly from $2.0\ \text{A}$ to $0.40\ \text{A}$ in $0.080\ \text{s}$. What is the magnitude of the self-induced emf?
answer: 10
check: 0.50*abs(0.40 - 2.0)/0.080
unit: V
hint: Find the rate of change of the current first.
hint: $dI/dt = (0.40 - 2.0)/0.080 = -20\ \text{A/s}$. Then $|\mathcal{E}_L| = L|dI/dt|$.
solution: $|\mathcal{E}_L| = L\,|\Delta I|/\Delta t = (0.50)(1.6)/(0.080) = 10\ \text{V}$.

prompt: A battery is connected through a switch to a series resistor $R$ and inductor $L$. At the instant just after the switch closes, which statement is correct?
choice*: The current is zero and the voltage across the inductor equals the battery emf.
choice: The current is $\varepsilon/R$ and the voltage across the inductor is zero.
choice: The current is zero and the voltage across the inductor is zero.
choice: The current is $\varepsilon/R$ and the voltage across the inductor equals the battery emf.
hint: Can the current in an inductor change instantaneously? Then apply the loop rule with that current.
hint: With $I = 0$ the resistor drops no voltage, so the inductor must drop the entire emf.
solution: The current through an inductor cannot jump, so $I = 0$ just after closing. The loop rule then gives $\varepsilon - 0 - L\,dI/dt = 0$, so the inductor carries the full emf and $dI/dt = \varepsilon/L$. Only after about $L/R$ does the current approach $\varepsilon/R$ with no voltage left across $L$.

@practice expr
prompt: A source of constant emf $V$ is switched onto a series circuit of resistance $R$ and inductance $L$ at $t=0$, with no initial current. Find the current $I(t)$ for $t > 0$.
vars: V, R, L, t
lead: $I =$
answer: V/R*(1 - e^(-R*t/L))
check: lambda V, R, L, t: ivp(lambda s, y: (V - R*y)/L, 0.0, 0.0, t)
hint: Write the loop equation, $V - IR - L\,dI/dt = 0$, and separate the variables.
hint: The current approaches $V/R$ with time constant $L/R$, starting from $0$.
solution: Rearranging, $L\,dI/dt = V - IR$, whose solution with $I(0) = 0$ is $I = \dfrac{V}{R}\left(1 - e^{-Rt/L}\right)$. At $t = 0$ it is zero; as $t\to\infty$ it approaches $V/R$.

@practice num
prompt: Current flows through an inductor of $0.50\ \text{H}$ from terminal $a$ to terminal $b$, and it is given by $I(t) = (4.0\ \text{A})\,e^{-2t}$ with $t$ in seconds. Taking the current direction $a \to b$ as positive, find $V_a - V_b$ at $t = 0.50\ \text{s}$.
answer: -1.47
check: 0.50*nderiv(lambda t: 4.0*exp(-2*t), 0.50)
unit: V
tol: 1%
hint: State the convention: with current positive from $a$ to $b$, $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt$.
hint: $dI/dt = -8.0\,e^{-2t}\ \text{A/s}$, which is negative at $t = 0.50$ s.
solution: $\dfrac{dI}{dt} = -8.0\,e^{-1} = -2.94\ \text{A/s}$ at $t = 0.50$ s. Then $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt = (0.50)(-2.94) = -1.47\ \text{V}$. The negative sign says $b$ is at the higher potential: the inductor opposes the decrease of the current by pushing charge forward in the $a \to b$ direction.

@quiz num
prompt: In an RL circuit with $L = 0.20\ \text{H}$ and $R = 40\ \Omega$, the current has reached its steady value of $0.30\ \text{A}$ when the battery is bypassed by a switch, so the current then decays through $R$ and $L$ alone. How long does it take for the current to fall to $0.10\ \text{A}$? Give the time in milliseconds.
answer: 5.49
check: root(lambda t: 0.30*exp(-40.0*t/0.20) - 0.10, 0, 0.1)*1e3
unit: ms
tol: 1%
hint: The current decays as $I = I_0e^{-t/\tau}$ with $\tau = L/R$.
hint: Solve $0.10 = 0.30\,e^{-t/\tau}$, which gives $t = \tau\ln 3$.
solution: $\tau = L/R = 5.0\ \text{ms}$. Setting $0.10 = 0.30\,e^{-t/\tau}$ gives $t = \tau\ln3 = (5.0)(1.099) \approx 5.49\ \text{ms}$.
