@article
Coulomb's law tells you how to add contributions from small pieces of charge. **Gauss's law** answers a different question: how is the field passing through a *closed surface* related to the charge inside it? When the charge has enough symmetry, the answer hands you the field with almost no integration.

### Electric flux

The **flux** counts how much field passes through a surface. For a flat surface of area $A$ in a uniform field,

$$\Phi_E = EA\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec E$ and the surface **normal**, not the angle with the surface itself. In general $\Phi_E = \int \vec E\cdot d\vec A$. On a closed surface the normal points outward, so field leaving counts positive and field entering counts negative. The unit is $\text{N m}^2/\text{C}$.

### Gauss's law

$$\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = \frac{Q_\text{enc}}{\varepsilon_0}.$$

The net flux through any closed surface depends only on the charge inside it. Charges outside still contribute to $\vec E$ on the surface, but their net flux through a closed surface is zero. The law is always true, yet it only *determines* $\vec E$ when symmetry lets you pull $E$ out of the integral.

### When does symmetry let you use it?

Choose a Gaussian surface through the field point so that on each part of it $\vec E$ is either perpendicular to the surface with constant magnitude, or parallel to it, or zero. Three families work:

| Symmetry | Gaussian surface | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Sphere (point, shell, ball) | concentric sphere of radius $r$ | $E = kQ_\text{enc}/r^2$ |
| Infinite line or long cylinder | coaxial cylinder of radius $r$ | $E = \dfrac{\lambda_\text{enc}}{2\pi\varepsilon_0 r} = \dfrac{2k\lambda_\text{enc}}{r}$ |
| Infinite plane sheet | pillbox straddling the sheet | $E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}$ on each side |

A finite rod, a ring or a disk does **not** qualify: $E$ is not constant over any convenient closed surface, so use integration as in week 1. Gauss's law still holds for them; it just does not give $E$ directly.

### Conductors

In electrostatic equilibrium the field inside a conductor is zero, so excess charge sits on the surface. Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface with magnitude $E = \sigma/\varepsilon_0$. This is twice the field of an isolated sheet, because a pillbox with one face inside the conductor has flux through only one face.

### Check dimensions every time

A result that fails a units check is wrong, however clean the algebra looked. For example,

$$\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon_0} \;\to\; \frac{\text{C/m}^2}{\text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)} = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{C}},$$

which is a field, whereas $\sigma/(\varepsilon_0 r)$ would carry an extra inverse length. Also test limits: just outside a sphere of radius $R$, $kQ/R^2$ should match the formula from inside.

@example
title: Field of a uniformly charged ball
problem: An insulating ball of radius $R = 0.10\ \text{m}$ carries a uniform volume charge with total charge $Q = 6.0\ \text{nC}$. Find the field magnitude at $r = 0.05\ \text{m}$ and at $r = 0.20\ \text{m}$. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
step: Choose the surface from the symmetry. The field must be radial with a magnitude that depends only on $r$, so use a concentric sphere of radius $r$. Then $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = E\,(4\pi r^2)$.
step: Find the enclosed charge. For $r < R$ the sphere contains the fraction $(r/R)^3$ of the volume, so $Q_\text{enc} = Q\,r^3/R^3$. For $r > R$ it contains everything: $Q_\text{enc} = Q$.
step: Apply Gauss's law inside: $E = \dfrac{kQ_\text{enc}}{r^2} = \dfrac{kQr}{R^3}$. At $r = 0.05\ \text{m}$ this is $\dfrac{(8.99\times10^{9})(6.0\times10^{-9})(0.05)}{(0.10)^3} \approx 2.7\times10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$.
step: Apply it outside: $E = kQ/r^2 = (8.99\times10^{9})(6.0\times10^{-9})/(0.20)^2 \approx 1.35\times10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$, exactly the field of a point charge at the center.
step: Check. Dimensions: $kQr/R^3$ is $\text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2 \cdot \text{C} \cdot \text{m}/\text{m}^3 = \text{N/C}$. At $r = R$ the inside formula gives $kQ/R^2$, matching the outside formula, so the field is continuous.

@mistakes
- Pulling $E$ out of the flux integral when the chosen surface does not make $E$ constant and perpendicular, for example using Gauss's law "for" a finite rod.
- Using $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$ just outside a conductor (it is $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$), or $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ for an isolated thin sheet (it is $\sigma/(2\varepsilon_0)$).
- Thinking charges outside the Gaussian surface do not affect $\vec E$. They do; they only contribute zero net flux.

@practice num
prompt: A uniform electric field of magnitude $600\ \text{N/C}$ makes an angle of $30^\circ$ with the plane of a square loop of side $0.20\ \text{m}$. Find the magnitude of the flux through the loop.
answer: 12
check: 600*sin(pi/6)*0.20**2
unit: N m^2/C
hint: The flux formula uses the angle between the field and the normal to the loop.
hint: The normal is perpendicular to the plane, so the angle with the normal is $90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.
solution: $\theta = 60^\circ$ with the normal, so $\Phi = EA\cos 60^\circ = (600)(0.040)(0.5) = 12\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}$.

hint: $Q_\text{enc} = \varepsilon_0\Phi$. Convert your answer from coulombs to nanocoulombs.
solution: $Q_\text{enc} = \varepsilon_0\Phi = (8.85\times10^{-12})(450) = 3.98\times10^{-9}\ \text{C} = 3.98\ \text{nC}$.

@practice ms
prompt: Gauss's law holds for every charge distribution. For which of these can you find the magnitude of $\vec E$ simply by setting the flux through a suitable Gaussian surface equal to $Q_\text{enc}/\varepsilon_0$? Select all that apply.
choice*: A uniformly charged solid sphere, at a point outside it.
choice*: An infinitely long straight line with uniform charge per unit length.
choice: A uniformly charged ring, at a point on its axis.
choice: A uniformly charged rod of length $L$, at a point on its perpendicular bisector.
hint: For each case ask two questions: is the direction of $\vec E$ known from symmetry, and is its magnitude the same everywhere on some closed surface through the field point?
hint: Spheres, infinite lines or cylinders, and infinite planes qualify. Rings and finite rods do not.
solution: The sphere (radial field, constant on a concentric sphere) and the infinite line (radial field, constant on a coaxial cylinder) work. For a ring or a finite rod the field magnitude varies over any simple closed surface through the field point, so $E$ cannot be pulled out of the integral. Gauss's law is still true there but does not determine $E$; use direct integration instead.

@practice expr
prompt: A very long solid cylinder of radius $R$ carries uniform volume charge density $\rho$. Using Gauss's law, find the field magnitude at a distance $r > R$ from the axis, in terms of $k$, $\rho$, $R$ and $r$. (Recall $\varepsilon_0 = 1/(4\pi k)$.)
vars: k, rho, R, r
lead: $E =$
answer: 2*pi*k*rho*R^2/r
check: lambda k, rho, R, r: (rho*pi*R**2*1.0)*(4*pi*k)/(2*pi*r*1.0)
domain: k: 0.5..2; rho: 0.5..2; R: 0.35..1.0; r: 1.2..2.65
hint: Use a coaxial cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ and length $\ell$. Only the curved side has flux.
hint: $Q_\text{enc} = \rho\,\pi R^2\ell$ and the side area is $2\pi r\ell$, so $E\,(2\pi r\ell) = Q_\text{enc}/\varepsilon_0$.
solution: $E\,(2\pi r\ell) = \rho\pi R^2\ell/\varepsilon_0 = 4\pi k\rho\pi R^2\ell$. Solving gives $E = \dfrac{2\pi k\rho R^2}{r}$. Dimension check: $\rho R^2$ is charge per length, so this has the form $k\lambda/r$ and, with $\lambda = \rho\pi R^2$, equals $2k\lambda/r$, the infinite-line result.

@practice num
prompt: The charge density in a sphere of radius $R = 0.20\ \text{m}$ increases with distance from the center as $\rho(s) = \rho_0\,s/R$, with $\rho_0 = 3.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{C/m}^3$. Find the field magnitude at $r = 0.10\ \text{m}$. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: 4.24e3
check: 8.99e9*quad(lambda s: 3.0e-6*(s/0.20)*4*pi*s**2, 0, 0.10)/0.10**2
unit: N/C
tol: 1%
hint: The density is not uniform, so $Q_\text{enc}$ must be found by integrating thin spherical shells of volume $4\pi s^2\,ds$.
hint: $Q_\text{enc} = \int_0^r \rho_0\dfrac{s}{R}\,4\pi s^2\,ds = \dfrac{\pi\rho_0 r^4}{R}$. Then use $E = kQ_\text{enc}/r^2$.
solution: $Q_\text{enc} = \pi\rho_0 r^4/R = \pi(3.0\times10^{-6})(1.0\times10^{-4})/0.20 = 4.71\times10^{-9}\ \text{C}$. Then $E = kQ_\text{enc}/r^2 = (8.99\times10^{9})(4.71\times10^{-9})/(0.10)^2 \approx 4.24\times10^{3}\ \text{N/C}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A point charge $+2.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the center of a thick conducting spherical shell (inner radius $0.10\ \text{m}$, outer radius $0.15\ \text{m}$) that carries a net charge of $-5.0\ \text{nC}$. Find the radial component of the electric field (positive means outward) at $r = 0.30\ \text{m}$. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: -300
check: 8.99e9*(2.0e-9 - 5.0e-9)/0.30**2
unit: N/C
tol: 1%
hint: Draw a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $0.30\ \text{m}$. What is the total charge inside it?
hint: The surface encloses the point charge and the entire shell, however the shell's charge is divided between its inner and outer faces.
solution: $Q_\text{enc} = +2.0 - 5.0 = -3.0\ \text{nC}$, so $E_r = kQ_\text{enc}/r^2 = (8.99\times10^{9})(-3.0\times10^{-9})/(0.30)^2 \approx -300\ \text{N/C}$, pointing inward. The inner face of the shell carries $-2.0$ nC and the outer face $-3.0$ nC, but only the total matters outside the shell.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which expression for the electric field magnitude at a distance $r$ from a long straight line with charge per unit length $\lambda$ is dimensionally consistent?
choice*: $\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\, r}$
choice: $\dfrac{\lambda}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\, r^2}$
choice: $\dfrac{\lambda\, r}{2\pi\varepsilon_0}$
choice: $\dfrac{\lambda^2}{2\pi\varepsilon_0\, r}$
hint: A field has units of $\text{N/C}$. Use $\varepsilon_0$ in $\text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)$ and $\lambda$ in $\text{C/m}$.
hint: Compare $\dfrac{\text{C/m}}{\text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)}$ with $\text{N/C}$ and see how many powers of length are left over.
solution: $\lambda/\varepsilon_0$ has units $\dfrac{\text{C/m}}{\text{C}^2/(\text{N m}^2)} = \dfrac{\text{N m}}{\text{C}}$, so one more inverse length is needed to get $\text{N/C}$. Only $\lambda/(2\pi\varepsilon_0 r)$ works. The other choices have the wrong power of $r$ or of the charge.
