@article
Gauss's law turned electric-field problems into algebra when the charge had symmetry. **Ampere's law** does the same for the magnetic fields of steady currents.

### The law

$$\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0\, I_\text{enc}.$$

The integral goes once around a closed **Amperian loop** that you choose. $I_\text{enc}$ is the net current through any surface bounded by the loop, counted with sign: curl the fingers of your right hand along the direction you traverse the loop, and currents along your thumb count positive. Currents outside the loop do contribute to $\vec B$ on the loop, but they add nothing net to the integral. This form holds for steady currents; in week 12 you will see Maxwell's extra term.

### When can you solve for $B$?

Exactly when symmetry lets you pull $B$ out of the integral: choose a loop on which $\vec B$ is tangent to the loop with constant magnitude, or perpendicular to it, or zero. The useful geometries are:

| Geometry | Amperian loop | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Long wire, outside ($r > R$) | circle of radius $r$ about the axis | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$ |
| Inside a uniform wire ($r < R$) | circle of radius $r$ | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I\,r}{2\pi R^2}$ |
| Ideal solenoid, $n$ turns per length | rectangle across the wall | $B = \mu_0 nI$ inside, $\approx 0$ outside |
| Toroid with $N$ turns | circle of radius $r$ in the core | $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 NI}{2\pi r}$ |
| Infinite sheet, current per width $K$ | rectangle straddling the sheet | $B = \tfrac12\mu_0K$ each side |

For the solenoid, one side of length $\ell$ of the rectangle lies inside, where $B$ is uniform and along the axis. The other sides lie outside (where $B \approx 0$) or perpendicular to $\vec B$. The loop encloses $n\ell$ turns, so $B\ell = \mu_0 n\ell I$.

### Choosing between the two methods

The **Biot-Savart law** works for any current shape but requires a vector integral. **Ampere's law** is much quicker when the symmetry is there, and useless when it is not, even though the law is still true. A single loop, a short straight segment or the ends of a finite solenoid belong to the second group. This is the same split as Gauss's law versus direct integration in week 2.

A good habit is to apply both methods wherever both work. The Biot-Savart result for a finite straight wire reduces to the Ampere result as the wire length grows, which is a strong consistency check.

### Checks

- **Units:** $\mu_0 I/(2\pi r)$ has units $(\text{T m/A})(\text{A})/\text{m} = \text{T}$.
- **Continuity at the surface:** the inside and outside wire formulas both give $\mu_0 I/(2\pi R)$ at $r = R$.
- **Limit:** $B\to0$ on the axis of a wire.

@example
title: A finite wire versus Ampere's law
problem: A straight wire $1.0\ \text{m}$ long carries $10\ \text{A}$. Find the field at $r = 5.0\ \text{cm}$ from its midpoint, on the perpendicular bisector, using the Biot-Savart law, and compare with the long-wire value from Ampere's law.
step: Ampere's law for an infinitely long wire: with a circle of radius $r$, $B\,(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I$, so $B_\infty = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{(2\times10^{-7})(10)}{0.050} = 4.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$.
step: Biot-Savart for the finite wire. Put the wire on the $x$-axis from $-L$ to $L$ with $L = 0.50\ \text{m}$. An element at $x$ has $|d\vec l\times\hat r| = dx\,\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{x^2+r^2}}$, so $dB = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\dfrac{r\,dx}{(x^2+r^2)^{3/2}}$. All elements give $d\vec B$ in the same direction (circling the wire), so the magnitudes simply add.
step: Integrate: $\displaystyle\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{r\,dx}{(x^2+r^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{2L}{r\sqrt{L^2+r^2}}$, so $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}\cdot\dfrac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+r^2}}$.
step: Numbers: $\dfrac{L}{\sqrt{L^2+r^2}} = \dfrac{0.50}{\sqrt{0.2525}} = 0.995$, so $B = 3.98\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$.
step: Compare. The finite wire gives a field only $0.5\%$ below the infinite-wire value, and the factor tends to $1$ as $L\to\infty$. So use Ampere's law when $r \ll L$, and Biot-Savart near the ends or for a short wire.

@mistakes
- Pulling $B$ out of the integral on a loop where $\vec B$ is not tangent and constant. Ampere's law is always true, but it only gives $B$ when the symmetry supports it.
- Adding currents without signs. Currents in opposite directions through the loop subtract.
- Using the full wire current inside a thick wire instead of only the part enclosed by the loop, or using the total number of turns $N$ in place of the turns per unit length $n$ for a solenoid.

@practice num
prompt: A long straight wire carries $25\ \text{A}$. Find the magnetic field magnitude at a distance of $0.10\ \text{m}$ from the wire.
answer: 5.0e-5
check: 4e-7*pi*25.0/(2*pi*0.10)
unit: T
tol: 1%
hint: Use a circular Amperian loop of radius $0.10$ m centered on the wire.
hint: $B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I$, with $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$.
solution: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r} = \dfrac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(25)}{2\pi(0.10)} = 5.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$.

check: sum(2e-7*I*cross([0, 0, 1.0], [0 - x0, 0, 0])[1]/abs(0 - x0)**2 for I, x0 in ((10.0, -0.10), (-10.0, 0.10)))
