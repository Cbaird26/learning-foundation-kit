@article
A battery is not the only way to push charge around a circuit. If the magnetic field through a loop changes, an **emf** appears in the loop, and a current flows if the loop is closed. This is **electromagnetic induction**, the principle behind generators, transformers and induction cooktops.

### Magnetic flux

The **magnetic flux** through a surface is

$$\Phi_B = \int \vec B\cdot d\vec A, \qquad \Phi_B = BA\cos\theta \ \text{(uniform field, flat loop)},$$

with units of webers ($1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T m}^2$). As with electric flux, $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec B$ and the loop's **normal**.

### Faraday's law with an explicit orientation

$$\mathcal{E} = -N\,\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$$

for a coil of $N$ turns. The signs only make sense when you fix a convention first:

1. Choose a direction of travel around the loop (for example, counterclockwise as seen from $+z$).
2. The right-hand rule gives the normal $\hat n$: curl your fingers along the direction of travel and your thumb points along $\hat n$ (here $+z$).
3. Compute $\Phi_B$ using that normal.
4. A positive $\mathcal{E}$ drives current in your chosen direction; a negative $\mathcal{E}$ drives it the other way.

Flux can change because $B$ changes, because the area changes, or because the angle changes. Any one of them induces an emf.

### Lenz's law

The minus sign is **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows in the direction whose own magnetic field opposes the *change* in flux. It does not oppose the flux itself. If the flux through a loop is decreasing, the induced field points the same way as the original field, to try to keep it up.

### Motional emf

A conducting rod of length $\ell$ moving with speed $v$ perpendicular to a uniform field $B$ (and perpendicular to itself) develops $\mathcal{E} = B\ell v$. Two derivations agree: the force $q\vec v\times\vec B$ pushes charges along the rod, and the area swept out grows at rate $\ell v$, so $d\Phi_B/dt = B\ell v$. If the rod slides on rails closing a circuit of resistance $R$, then $I = B\ell v/R$ and the magnetic force on the rod is $B I \ell$, opposing the motion. The mechanical power you supply, $Fv = B^2\ell^2v^2/R$, equals the electrical power $I^2R$ dissipated: energy is conserved.

### Induced electric fields

A changing $B$ creates an electric field even where there are no wires. The field is not conservative: $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi_B/dt$. For a long uniform field region of radius $R$ with $B$ changing at rate $dB/dt$, symmetry gives circular field lines, so $E\,(2\pi r) = \pi r^2\,|dB/dt|$ for $r<R$, or $E = \tfrac12 r\,|dB/dt|$. Outside, only the flux inside $R$ counts: $E = \dfrac{R^2}{2r}|dB/dt|$.

@example
title: Emf in a loop with a decreasing field
problem: A circular loop of radius $0.10\ \text{m}$ and resistance $2.0\ \Omega$ lies in the $xy$-plane in a field $\vec B = B(t)\,\hat z$ with $B(t) = 0.50\ \text{T} - (0.20\ \text{T/s})\,t$. Take counterclockwise (seen from $+z$) as the positive direction. Find the emf and the current, and say which way the current flows.
step: Fix the convention. Counterclockwise as seen from $+z$ gives, by the right-hand rule, the normal $\hat n = +\hat z$. So $\Phi_B = B(t)\,\pi r^2$ with a positive sign.
step: Flux: $\Phi_B(t) = \pi(0.10)^2\,(0.50 - 0.20\,t) = 0.0314\,(0.50 - 0.20\,t)\ \text{Wb}$. Differentiate: $\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = -(0.0314)(0.20) = -6.28\times10^{-3}\ \text{Wb/s}$.
step: Faraday: $\mathcal{E} = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = +6.28\times10^{-3}\ \text{V}$. It is positive, so it drives current in the chosen (counterclockwise) direction.
step: Current: $I = \mathcal{E}/R = (6.28\times10^{-3})/2.0 = 3.1\ \text{mA}$, counterclockwise as seen from $+z$.
step: Lenz check. The upward flux is decreasing, so the induced current should make its own field point up inside the loop to oppose the loss. A counterclockwise current seen from above does exactly that. The signs agree.

@mistakes
- Treating the emf as proportional to the flux itself rather than to its rate of change. A large steady flux induces nothing.
- Saying the induced field always opposes the external field. It opposes the change in flux, so it points the same way as the external field when the flux is decreasing.
- Mixing conventions: computing the flux with one normal and then reading the sign of $\mathcal{E}$ as if a different direction of travel had been chosen.

@practice num
prompt: A uniform field of $0.30\ \text{T}$ makes an angle of $60^\circ$ with the normal to a flat loop of area $0.020\ \text{m}^2$. Find the magnetic flux through the loop.
answer: 3.0e-3
check: 0.30*0.020*cos(pi/3)
unit: Wb
tol: 1%
hint: Use the angle between the field and the normal to the loop.
hint: $\Phi_B = BA\cos\theta$ with $\theta = 60^\circ$.
solution: $\Phi_B = (0.30)(0.020)\cos60^\circ = 3.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{Wb}$.

@practice num
prompt: A coil of $50$ turns has area $4.0\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$ and its normal is parallel to a field that drops uniformly from $0.80\ \text{T}$ to $0.20\ \text{T}$ in $0.30\ \text{s}$. Find the magnitude of the induced emf.
answer: 0.40
check: 50*4.0e-3*(0.80 - 0.20)/0.30
unit: V
hint: Find the rate of change of flux through one turn, then multiply by the number of turns.
hint: $|\mathcal{E}| = NA\,|\Delta B|/\Delta t$.
solution: $|\mathcal{E}| = (50)(4.0\times10^{-3})(0.60)/0.30 = 0.40\ \text{V}$.

@practice mc
prompt: The north pole of a bar magnet is pushed toward a conducting loop along the loop's axis. Looking at the loop from the magnet's side, in which direction is the induced current?
choice*: Counterclockwise, because the induced field must point toward the magnet to oppose the increasing flux.
choice: Clockwise, because the induced current must add to the magnet's field.
choice: There is no current, because the magnet does not touch the loop.
choice: Counterclockwise, but only after the magnet stops moving.
hint: The flux through the loop from the magnet points away from the north pole and is increasing. Which way must the induced field point to oppose that increase?
hint: The induced field must point back toward the magnet. A current that makes a field toward the viewer circulates counterclockwise as seen by that viewer.
solution: The magnet's field through the loop points away from the north pole and grows as the magnet approaches. Lenz's law requires an induced field pointing the other way, toward the magnet and the viewer. By the right-hand rule, that is a counterclockwise current as seen from the magnet's side. Induction needs a changing flux, not contact, and it occurs while the magnet moves.

@practice expr
prompt: A flat coil of area $A$ rotates at constant angular speed $\omega$ in a uniform field $B$. Its angle to the field is $\theta = \omega t$ (the angle between the normal and $\vec B$). Find the induced emf $\mathcal{E}(t)$ for a single turn.
vars: B, A, omega, t
lead: $\mathcal{E} =$
answer: B*A*omega*sin(omega*t)
check: lambda B, A, omega, t: -nderiv(lambda s: B*A*cos(omega*s), t)
hint: Write the flux as a function of time first: $\Phi_B = BA\cos(\omega t)$.
hint: Faraday's law gives $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi_B/dt$. Differentiate the cosine.
solution: $\Phi_B = BA\cos\omega t$, so $\mathcal{E} = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt} = BA\omega\sin\omega t$. The emf is sinusoidal with peak value $BA\omega$, which is how a generator makes ac.

@practice num
prompt: A conducting rod of length $0.40\ \text{m}$ slides at a constant $5.0\ \text{m/s}$ along rails in a uniform $0.60\ \text{T}$ field perpendicular to the plane of the rails. The circuit has a total resistance of $3.0\ \Omega$. What mechanical power must an outside agent supply to keep the rod moving at constant speed?
answer: 0.48
check: (0.60*0.40*5.0/3.0)**2*3.0
unit: W
tol: 1%
hint: Find the emf, then the current. At constant speed the agent's power equals the electrical power dissipated.
hint: $\mathcal{E} = B\ell v = 1.2$ V, so $I = 0.40$ A. The power is $I^2R$, or equivalently $Fv$ with $F = BI\ell$.
solution: $\mathcal{E} = (0.60)(0.40)(5.0) = 1.2\ \text{V}$ and $I = 0.40\ \text{A}$. The magnetic force on the rod is $F = BI\ell = 0.096\ \text{N}$, so the agent supplies $Fv = 0.48\ \text{W}$. This matches $I^2R = (0.40)^2(3.0) = 0.48\ \text{W}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A uniform field fills a long cylindrical region of radius $0.20\ \text{m}$ and is increasing at $0.50\ \text{T/s}$. Find the magnitude of the induced electric field at a distance of $0.50\ \text{m}$ from the axis, outside the field region.
answer: 0.020
check: pi*0.20**2*0.50/(2*pi*0.50)
unit: V/m
tol: 1%
hint: Apply $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec l = -d\Phi_B/dt$ on a circle of radius $0.50$ m. Which part of the circle's area contains flux?
hint: Only the area $\pi(0.20)^2$ contains field, so $E\,(2\pi r) = \pi R^2\,(dB/dt)$.
solution: $E\,(2\pi)(0.50) = \pi(0.20)^2(0.50)$, so $E = \dfrac{(0.040)(0.50)}{2(0.50)} = 0.020\ \text{V/m}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A generator coil has $100$ turns of area $0.020\ \text{m}^2$ and rotates at $60\ \text{Hz}$ in a uniform field of $0.30\ \text{T}$. Find the peak emf.
answer: 226
check: 100*0.30*0.020*2*pi*60.0
unit: V
tol: 1%
hint: The emf of a rotating coil has peak value $NBA\omega$. Convert the frequency to angular frequency.
hint: $\omega = 2\pi f = 377\ \text{rad/s}$.
solution: $\mathcal{E}_\text{max} = NBA\omega = (100)(0.30)(0.020)(2\pi\cdot60) = 0.60 \times 377 \approx 226\ \text{V}$.
