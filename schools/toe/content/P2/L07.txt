@article
A magnet or a current-carrying wire changes the space around it, and the change is described by the **magnetic field** $\vec B$, measured in tesla ($1\ \text{T} = 1\ \text{N}/(\text{A m})$). Unlike the electric field, a magnetic field exerts force only on charges that are *moving*.

### Force on a moving charge

$$\vec F = q\,\vec v\times\vec B, \qquad F = |q|\,v\,B\sin\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. The force is perpendicular to both. Right-hand rule: point the fingers along $\vec v$, curl them toward $\vec B$, and the thumb gives $\vec F$ for a positive charge (reverse it for a negative charge). Because $\vec F \perp \vec v$, the magnetic force does **no work**: it changes the direction of the velocity but never the speed or kinetic energy.

### Circular motion

For $\vec v \perp \vec B$ the magnetic force provides the centripetal force, $|q|vB = mv^2/r$, so

$$r = \frac{mv}{|q|B}, \qquad \omega = \frac{|q|B}{m}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi m}{|q|B}.$$

The period does not depend on the speed: faster particles travel larger circles in the same time. A velocity component along $\vec B$ is unaffected, so in general the path is a helix. With both fields present, $\vec F = q(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B)$. If $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ are perpendicular, a particle with $v = E/B$ passes straight through, which is a **velocity selector**.

### Force on a current

A current is a stream of moving charge, so a straight wire of length $L$ carrying current $I$ feels $\vec F = I\vec L\times\vec B$ with $\vec L$ along the current. For a wire perpendicular to the field, $F = ILB$.

### Fields produced by currents

Currents also *create* magnetic fields. The **Biot-Savart law** gives the contribution of a small current element $I\,d\vec l$:

$$d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}, \qquad \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \frac{\text{T m}}{\text{A}},$$

with $\hat r$ pointing from the element to the field point. As with Coulomb's law, you add the contributions by superposition. Two standard results:

- **Long straight wire:** $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$. Field lines circle the wire: thumb along $I$, fingers curl along $\vec B$.
- **Circular loop** of radius $R$: $B = \dfrac{\mu_0 I}{2R}$ at the center, and on the axis at distance $z$ from the center,

$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2\,(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}.$$

### Parallel wires

Wire 1 makes a field $B = \mu_0 I_1/(2\pi d)$ at wire 2, so wire 2 feels a force per unit length

$$\frac{F}{L} = \frac{\mu_0 I_1 I_2}{2\pi d}.$$

Parallel currents attract; antiparallel currents repel. Before 2019 this force between parallel currents defined the ampere. The ampere is now defined through the elementary charge, and $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m/A}$ still holds to better than one part in a billion.

@example
title: A proton circling in a magnetic field
problem: A proton ($m = 1.673\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $q = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) moves at $2.0\times10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$ perpendicular to a uniform field $B = 0.50\ \text{T}$ pointing into the page. Find the radius and period of its orbit and the sense of rotation.
step: The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, so it acts as the centripetal force: $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$.
step: Solve for the radius: $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB} = \dfrac{(1.673\times10^{-27})(2.0\times10^{6})}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(0.50)} \approx 0.042\ \text{m}$, about $4.2\ \text{cm}$.
step: The period is the circumference over the speed: $T = \dfrac{2\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB} \approx 1.3\times10^{-7}\ \text{s}$. Doubling the speed would double $r$ but leave $T$ unchanged.
step: Sense of rotation. Take $\vec v = +\hat x$ and $\vec B = -\hat z$ (into the page). Then $\vec v\times\vec B = \hat x\times(-\hat z) = +\hat y$, so the force on a positive charge points up when it moves right, and the proton circulates counterclockwise.
step: Check: the speed (and the kinetic energy) stays $2.0\times10^{6}\ \text{m/s}$ throughout, since the magnetic force does no work.

@mistakes
- Using $F = qvB$ when $\vec v$ is not perpendicular to $\vec B$; only the component of $\vec v$ perpendicular to $\vec B$ counts, which is the $\sin\theta$ factor.
- Expecting a magnetic field to speed up a charged particle. It only turns it; changing the speed needs an electric field.
- Using the wrong hand, or forgetting to reverse the force direction for a negative charge.

@practice num
prompt: A particle with charge $3.0\ \mu\text{C}$ moves at $4.0\times10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$ in a uniform $0.20\ \text{T}$ field. The velocity makes an angle of $30^\circ$ with the field. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force?
answer: 0.12
check: 3.0e-6*4.0e5*0.20*sin(pi/6)
unit: N
tol: 1%
hint: The force depends on the sine of the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$.
hint: $F = |q|vB\sin\theta$ with $q = 3.0\times10^{-6}$ C.
solution: $F = (3.0\times10^{-6})(4.0\times10^{5})(0.20)\sin30^\circ = 0.24 \times 0.5 = 0.12\ \text{N}$.

@practice num
prompt: A straight $0.50\ \text{m}$ wire carries $8.0\ \text{A}$ along $+x$ in a uniform field of $0.40\ \text{T}$ along $+y$. Find the magnitude of the force on the wire.
answer: 1.6
check: 8.0*norm(cross([0.50, 0, 0], [0, 0.40, 0]))
unit: N
hint: The wire is perpendicular to the field, so the force has its maximum value.
hint: $F = ILB$.
solution: $F = (8.0)(0.50)(0.40) = 1.6\ \text{N}$. By the right-hand rule, $\hat x\times\hat y = +\hat z$, so the force points along $+z$.

@practice expr
prompt: A particle of mass $m$ and charge $q > 0$ is accelerated from rest through a potential difference $V$ and then enters a uniform field $B$ perpendicular to its velocity. Find the radius of its circular path in terms of $m$, $q$, $V$ and $B$.
vars: m, q, V, B
lead: $r =$
answer: sqrt(2*m*V/q)/B
check: lambda m, q, V, B: (m*sqrt(2*q*V/m))/(q*B)
hint: Find the speed first from energy conservation: $qV = \tfrac12 mv^2$.
hint: Then use $r = mv/(qB)$ and substitute $v = \sqrt{2qV/m}$.
solution: $v = \sqrt{2qV/m}$, so $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB} = \dfrac{m}{qB}\sqrt{\dfrac{2qV}{m}} = \dfrac{1}{B}\sqrt{\dfrac{2mV}{q}}$. This is the principle of the mass spectrometer: at fixed $V$ and $B$, the radius reveals $m/q$.

@practice num
prompt: A circular loop of radius $5.0\ \text{cm}$ carries a current of $2.0\ \text{A}$. Use the Biot-Savart law to find the magnitude of the field on the axis of the loop, $12\ \text{cm}$ from its center. Give the answer in microteslas.
answer: 1.43
check: quad(lambda phi: 1e-7*2.0*0.05*0.05/(0.05**2 + 0.12**2)**1.5, 0, 2*pi)*1e6
unit: microteslas
tol: 1%
hint: Each element $dl = R\,d\phi$ is perpendicular to the line to the field point. Only the component along the axis survives; it is the fraction $R/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ of $dB$.

@quiz num
prompt: Alpha particles ($m = 6.64\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$, $q = 3.204\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) pass undeflected through crossed fields $E = 6.0\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m}$ and $B = 0.020\ \text{T}$. They then enter a region with only a uniform field of $0.50\ \text{T}$ perpendicular to their velocity. Find the radius of their circular path in centimeters.
answer: 1.24
check: 6.64e-27*(6.0e3/0.020)/(3.204e-19*0.50)*100
unit: cm
tol: 1%
hint: Particles that pass straight through the selector have a speed set by the ratio of the fields.
hint: $v = E/B = 3.0\times10^{5}$ m/s. Then $r = mv/(qB')$ with $B' = 0.50$ T.
solution: The speed is $v = E/B = 3.0\times10^{5}\ \text{m/s}$. Then $r = \dfrac{(6.64\times10^{-27})(3.0\times10^{5})}{(3.204\times10^{-19})(0.50)} = 1.24\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} = 1.24\ \text{cm}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Two long parallel wires $0.10\ \text{m}$ apart carry currents of $20\ \text{A}$ and $30\ \text{A}$ in the same direction. What is the magnitude of the force per unit length between them, in newtons per meter?
answer: 1.2e-3
check: 30.0*(4e-7*pi*20.0/(2*pi*0.10))
unit: N/m
tol: 1%
hint: Find the field made by one wire at the position of the other, then use $F = ILB$ on a one-meter length.
hint: $B_1 = \mu_0 I_1/(2\pi d) = 4.0\times10^{-5}$ T at wire 2. Multiply by $I_2$.
solution: $B_1 = \dfrac{\mu_0 I_1}{2\pi d} = \dfrac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(20)}{2\pi(0.10)} = 4.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{T}$, so $F/L = I_2B_1 = (30)(4.0\times10^{-5}) = 1.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{N/m}$. The wires attract because the currents are parallel.
