@article
**Current** is the rate at which charge crosses a surface:

$$I = \frac{dQ}{dt}, \qquad 1\ \text{A} = 1\ \text{C/s}.$$

By convention the direction of current is the direction positive charges would move, even though in a metal the carriers are electrons moving the other way.

### Drift and current density

Inside a wire, free electrons zigzag randomly at high speed, but the field adds a slow net motion: the **drift velocity** $\vec v_d$. If there are $n$ carriers per unit volume, each with charge $q$, the **current density** is $\vec J = nq\vec v_d$ and the current through a cross-section $A$ is

$$I = nqv_dA.$$

In copper ($n \approx 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$) a 1 A current in a 1 mm$^2$ wire gives a drift speed of only about $0.07\ \text{mm/s}$. Lights come on instantly because the electric field is established along the whole circuit at nearly the speed of light, and every carrier starts to drift at once.

### Resistivity, resistance, Ohm's law

In many materials $\vec J = \sigma\vec E$: the current density is proportional to the field. The **resistivity** $\rho = 1/\sigma$ (unit $\Omega\,\text{m}$) is a property of the material, and for a uniform wire of length $L$ and cross-section $A$,

$$R = \frac{\rho L}{A}, \qquad V = IR.$$

The second equation is **Ohm's law**: for an **ohmic** device, $R$ does not depend on $V$ or $I$, so the graph of $I$ against $V$ is a straight line. Many devices are **non-ohmic**: a filament lamp, a diode. For them $V/I$ still defines a resistance at each operating point, but it changes. Metals have resistivity that rises with temperature, approximately

$$\rho(T) = \rho_0\left[1 + \alpha\,(T - T_0)\right],$$

with $\alpha \approx 3.9\times10^{-3}\ {}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$ for copper.

### Power and energy

A charge $dq$ falling through a potential drop $V$ gives up energy $V\,dq$, so the power delivered to a device is

$$P = IV.$$

For a resistor this can be rewritten with Ohm's law as $P = I^2R = V^2/R$, which goes into heat. Which form to use depends on what is fixed: at fixed voltage, a larger $R$ dissipates **less**; at fixed current, a larger $R$ dissipates **more**. Energy is $E = Pt$; the utility unit is the kilowatt-hour, $1\ \text{kWh} = 3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J}$.

### Dimension check

$\rho L/A$ has units $\Omega\,\text{m}\cdot\text{m}/\text{m}^2 = \Omega$, as required.

@example
title: A copper wire carrying current
problem: A copper wire of length $25\ \text{m}$ and diameter $1.0\ \text{mm}$ carries $3.0\ \text{A}$. Take $\rho = 1.68\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m}$, $n = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. Find the resistance, the voltage drop, the heating power and the drift speed.
step: The cross-section uses the radius, $r = 0.50\ \text{mm}$: $A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.50\times10^{-3})^2 = 7.85\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$.
step: Resistance: $R = \dfrac{\rho L}{A} = \dfrac{(1.68\times10^{-8})(25)}{7.85\times10^{-7}} \approx 0.535\ \Omega$.
step: Ohm's law gives the voltage drop along the wire: $V = IR = (3.0)(0.535) \approx 1.6\ \text{V}$.
step: The heating power is $P = I^2R = (3.0)^2(0.535) \approx 4.8\ \text{W}$. Check with $P = IV = (3.0)(1.6) = 4.8\ \text{W}$.
step: Drift speed: $v_d = \dfrac{I}{nqA} = \dfrac{3.0}{(8.5\times10^{28})(1.602\times10^{-19})(7.85\times10^{-7})} \approx 2.8\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}$, about $0.28\ \text{mm/s}$.

@mistakes
- Confusing the slow drift speed of the carriers (fractions of a millimeter per second) with the speed at which the circuit responds.
- Using the diameter in place of the radius in $A = \pi r^2$.
- Applying $P = V^2/R$ or $P = I^2R$ without asking which of $V$ or $I$ is actually fixed, so the dependence on $R$ comes out backwards.

@practice num
prompt: A charge of $36\ \text{C}$ passes through a cross-section of a wire in $1.5$ minutes. What is the average current?
answer: 0.40
check: 36/(1.5*60)
unit: A
tol: 1%
hint: Convert the time to seconds before dividing.
hint: $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$ with $\Delta t = 90$ s.
solution: $I = 36\ \text{C}/90\ \text{s} = 0.40\ \text{A}$.

@practice num
prompt: A nichrome wire has resistivity $1.5\times10^{-6}\ \Omega\,\text{m}$, length $2.0\ \text{m}$ and diameter $0.50\ \text{mm}$. What is its resistance?
answer: 15.3
check: 1.5e-6*2.0/(pi*(0.50e-3/2)**2)
unit: ohms
tol: 1%
hint: Find the cross-sectional area from the radius, which is half the diameter.
hint: $A = \pi(0.25\times10^{-3})^2 = 1.96\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$, and then $R = \rho L/A$.
solution: $A = \pi(0.25\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})^2 = 1.96\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^2$. Then $R = (1.5\times10^{-6})(2.0)/(1.96\times10^{-7}) \approx 15.3\ \Omega$.

@practice mc
prompt: A wire of resistance $R$ is drawn out uniformly to twice its original length, with its volume (and resistivity) unchanged. What is its new resistance?
choice: $R/2$
choice: $2R$
choice*: $4R$
choice: $R$, because the material is the same.
hint: The volume is $AL$ and stays fixed. If $L$ doubles, what happens to $A$?
hint: Now use $R = \rho L/A$ with doubled $L$ and halved $A$.
solution: Since $AL$ is constant, doubling $L$ halves $A$. Then $R' = \rho(2L)/(A/2) = 4\rho L/A = 4R$. The $2R$ answer accounts for the length alone and forgets the thinner cross-section.

@practice expr

vars: I, n, q, d
lead: $v_d =$
answer: 4*I/(n*q*pi*d^2)
check: lambda I, n, q, d: I/(n*q*pi*(d/2)**2)
hint: Start from $I = nqv_dA$ and solve for $v_d$.
hint: The area of a circular cross-section of diameter $d$ is $\pi d^2/4$.
solution: $v_d = \dfrac{I}{nqA}$ with $A = \dfrac{\pi d^2}{4}$, so $v_d = \dfrac{4I}{\pi nqd^2}$.

@practice num
prompt: A copper wire has resistance $2.00\ \Omega$ at $20\ {}^\circ\text{C}$. It carries a steady current of $5.0\ \text{A}$ while its temperature is $80\ {}^\circ\text{C}$. Taking $\alpha = 3.9\times10^{-3}\ {}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$, find the power it dissipates.
answer: 61.7
check: 5.0**2*2.00*(1 + 3.9e-3*(80 - 20))
unit: W
tol: 1%
hint: First find the resistance at $80\ {}^\circ\text{C}$. The geometry is unchanged, so $R$ scales like $\rho$.
hint: $R(80) = R(20)\,[1 + \alpha\,(80 - 20)]$. Then use $P = I^2R$ because the current is given.
solution: $R(80) = 2.00\,[1 + (3.9\times10^{-3})(60)] = 2.00(1.234) = 2.468\ \Omega$. Then $P = I^2R = (5.0)^2(2.468) \approx 61.7\ \text{W}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A copper wire of diameter $2.0\ \text{mm}$ carries $10\ \text{A}$. With $n = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$, find the drift speed of the electrons in millimeters per second.
answer: 0.234
check: 10/(8.5e28*1.602e-19*pi*(1.0e-3)**2)*1e3
unit: mm/s
tol: 1%
hint: Use $I = nqv_dA$ with the cross-sectional area found from the radius, $1.0$ mm.
hint: $A = \pi(1.0\times10^{-3})^2 = 3.14\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$. Convert the final answer from m/s to mm/s.
solution: $v_d = \dfrac{I}{nqA} = \dfrac{10}{(8.5\times10^{28})(1.602\times10^{-19})(3.14\times10^{-6})} \approx 2.34\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s} = 0.234\ \text{mm/s}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of the following statements are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: Ohm's law with a constant $R$ describes certain materials and devices; it is not a universal law of nature.
choice*: The power delivered to any two-terminal device carrying current $I$ with voltage $V$ across it is $IV$.
choice: A filament lamp has the same value of $V/I$ at every operating voltage.
choice: In a wire carrying $1\ \text{A}$, electrons drift at a speed close to the speed of light.
hint: Check each statement against what you know about the shape of the $I$-$V$ graph of a lamp and about drift speeds.
hint: The power formula $P = IV$ comes from energy conservation and does not assume Ohm's law.
solution: The statements about Ohm's law and about the power $IV$ are true. A lamp filament heats up as the voltage grows, so its resistance rises and its $I$-$V$ graph is curved. Drift speeds in ordinary wires are fractions of a millimeter per second, not anywhere near the speed of light.
