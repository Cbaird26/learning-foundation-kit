@article
Electric charge comes in two kinds, called positive and negative. Like charges repel and opposite charges attract. Charge is **conserved** and **quantized** in units of the elementary charge $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{C}$. In this course we use

$$k = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \frac{\text{N m}^2}{\text{C}^2}, \qquad \varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N m}^2}.$$

### Coulomb's law

Two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ a distance $r$ apart exert forces of equal size on each other, along the line joining them:

$$F = k\,\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}.$$

The force is repulsive for like signs and attractive for opposite signs. It falls off as $1/r^2$, like gravity, but it is enormously stronger: between two protons the electric repulsion is about $10^{36}$ times the gravitational attraction. In vector form, the force on charge 2 is $\vec F_{2} = k q_1 q_2\,\hat r/r^2$, where $\hat r$ is the unit vector pointing from charge 1 toward charge 2.

### The electric field

Instead of asking what charge A does to charge B, assign a vector to every point in space. The **electric field** is the force per unit charge on a small positive test charge placed there:

$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q_0}, \qquad [E] = \text{N/C} = \text{V/m}.$$

A point charge $q$ produces $\vec E = kq\,\hat r/r^2$, pointing **away** from positive charge and **toward** negative charge. Once the field is known, the force on any charge $q'$ is $\vec F = q'\vec E$.

### Superposition

The field of several sources is the **vector** sum of the fields each would make alone. For a continuous distribution, cut it into pieces $dq$ and integrate. Use $dq = \lambda\,dx$ for a line (charge per length), $\sigma\,dA$ for a surface, and $\rho\,dV$ for a volume:

$$\vec E = \int \frac{k\,dq}{r^2}\,\hat r.$$

### Use symmetry before you integrate

A uniform rod of length $L$ and total charge $Q$ lies on the $x$-axis from $-L/2$ to $L/2$. Find the field at $(0, d)$ on its perpendicular bisector. The piece at $x$ carries $dq = \lambda\,dx$ with $\lambda = Q/L$. By mirror symmetry the $x$-components of pieces at $+x$ and $-x$ cancel, so only $dE_y$ survives:

$$dE_y = \frac{k\lambda\,dx}{x^2+d^2}\cdot\frac{d}{\sqrt{x^2+d^2}}.$$

Integrating from $-L/2$ to $L/2$:

$$E = \frac{kQ}{d\sqrt{d^2 + L^2/4}}.$$

### Sanity checks

- **Dimensions:** $L$ and $d$ are both lengths, so the denominator has dimensions of length squared, and $kQ/\text{length}^2$ is a field.
- **Far away ($d \gg L$):** $E \to kQ/d^2$, a point charge.
- **Close in ($d \ll L$):** $E \to 2k\lambda/d$, the field of an infinitely long line.

Both limits match known results, which is strong evidence the integral was done correctly.

@example
title: Net field of two opposite charges
problem: A charge $+3.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at the origin and a charge $-3.0\ \text{nC}$ sits at $(0.40\ \text{m}, 0)$. Find the electric field at the point $P = (0.20\ \text{m}, 0.15\ \text{m})$.
step: Find the distances. Each charge is $\sqrt{0.20^2 + 0.15^2} = 0.25\ \text{m}$ from $P$, so both fields have the same magnitude $E_1 = kq/r^2 = (8.99\times10^{9})(3.0\times10^{-9})/(0.25)^2 \approx 432\ \text{N/C}$.
step: Find the directions. The positive charge pushes away from itself, along $(0.20, 0.15)/0.25 = (0.8, 0.6)$. The negative charge pulls toward itself, along $(0.20, -0.15)/0.25 = (0.8, -0.6)$.
step: Write each contribution in components: $\vec E_1 = 432\,(0.8, 0.6) = (346, 259)\ \text{N/C}$ and $\vec E_2 = 432\,(0.8, -0.6) = (346, -259)\ \text{N/C}$.
step: Add as vectors. The $y$-components cancel and the $x$-components add: $\vec E = (691, 0)\ \text{N/C}$, about $6.9\times10^{2}\ \text{N/C}$ in the $+x$ direction.
step: Sanity check: $P$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of the pair, and the field there should point from the positive charge toward the negative one, i.e. along $+x$. It does.

@mistakes
- Adding field magnitudes instead of adding vectors, so cancellations and perpendicular components are lost.
- Pointing the field of a negative charge away from it; it points toward the charge.
- Forgetting to convert $\mu$C or nC to coulombs, or using the distance to the wrong point.

@practice num
prompt: Take $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$. Two point charges, $+2.0\ \mu\text{C}$ and $-3.0\ \mu\text{C}$, are $0.30\ \text{m}$ apart. What is the magnitude of the force each exerts on the other?
answer: 0.599
check: 8.99e9*2.0e-6*3.0e-6/0.30**2
unit: N
tol: 1%
hint: Coulomb's law uses the magnitudes of the charges. Convert microcoulombs to coulombs first.
hint: $F = k|q_1 q_2|/r^2$ with $q_1 = 2.0\times10^{-6}$ C, $q_2 = 3.0\times10^{-6}$ C and $r = 0.30$ m.
solution: $F = (8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-6})(3.0\times10^{-6})/(0.30)^2 = 5.394\times10^{-2}/0.090 \approx 0.599\ \text{N}$. The force is attractive because the signs differ.

@practice num
prompt: A point charge produces an electric field of magnitude $900\ \text{N/C}$ at a distance of $0.50\ \text{m}$. With $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$, find the magnitude of the charge in nanocoulombs.
answer: 25.0
check: 900*0.50**2/8.99e9*1e9
unit: nC
tol: 1%
hint: Solve $E = kq/r^2$ for $q$.
hint: $q = Er^2/k$. This comes out in coulombs; $1\ \text{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}$.
solution: $q = Er^2/k = (900)(0.25)/(8.99\times10^{9}) = 2.50\times10^{-8}\ \text{C} = 25.0\ \text{nC}$.

answer: 2*k*q*y/(a^2 + y^2)^(3/2)
check: lambda k, q, a, y: sum(k*q*y/(x0**2 + y**2)**1.5 for x0 in (-a, a))

@practice num
prompt: A thin rod lies on the $x$-axis from $x = 0$ to $x = 0.40\ \text{m}$ and carries a uniform total charge of $8.0\ \text{nC}$. Find the magnitude of the electric field at $x = 0.60\ \text{m}$ on the axis. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: 599
check: quad(lambda x: 8.99e9*(8.0e-9/0.40)/(0.60 - x)**2, 0, 0.40)
unit: N/C
tol: 1%
hint: The charge per unit length is $\lambda = Q/L$. A piece $dx$ at position $x$ is a distance $0.60 - x$ from the field point, and every piece pushes in the same direction.
hint: $E = k\lambda\int_0^{0.40}\dfrac{dx}{(0.60 - x)^2} = k\lambda\left[\dfrac{1}{0.60 - x}\right]_0^{0.40}$.
solution: $\lambda = 8.0\times10^{-9}/0.40 = 2.0\times10^{-8}\ \text{C/m}$. The bracket equals $1/0.20 - 1/0.60 = 3.33\ \text{m}^{-1}$, so $E = (8.99\times10^{9})(2.0\times10^{-8})(3.33) \approx 599\ \text{N/C}$, pointing away from the rod.

@quiz num
prompt: A thin ring of radius $0.10\ \text{m}$ carries a uniform total charge of $5.0\ \text{nC}$. Find the magnitude of the electric field on the ring's axis at a distance of $0.15\ \text{m}$ from its center. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$.
answer: 1151
check: quad(lambda phi: 8.99e9*(5.0e-9/(2*pi))*0.15/(0.10**2 + 0.15**2)**1.5, 0, 2*pi)
unit: N/C
tol: 1%
hint: Every piece of the ring is the same distance from the field point. Which components of the pieces' fields cancel around the ring, and which add?
hint: Only the component along the axis survives, giving $E = \dfrac{kQz}{(R^2 + z^2)^{3/2}}$.
solution: Each piece is $\sqrt{0.10^2 + 0.15^2}$ m away, and its axial component is the fraction $z/\sqrt{R^2+z^2}$ of its field. Summing around the ring: $E = kQz/(R^2+z^2)^{3/2} = (8.99\times10^{9})(5.0\times10^{-9})(0.15)/(0.0325)^{3/2} \approx 1151\ \text{N/C}$.
