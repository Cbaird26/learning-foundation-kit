@project
title: Analyzing a low-voltage RC circuit
overview: You will build, or simulate, a series RC circuit powered by a battery or USB supply of 9 V or less, record the capacitor voltage during charging and discharging, and extract the time constant by a straight-line fit. You then compare it with the value predicted from the component values and their tolerances, and check the energy balance. Every data set is clearly labeled as measured or simulated.
goal: Predict the time constant $\tau = RC$, with an uncertainty derived from the component tolerances.
goal: Obtain $V_C(t)$ for charging and discharging and extract $\tau$ from a linearized fit with an uncertainty.
goal: Compare prediction and fit quantitatively, and account for any disagreement with specific systematic effects.
goal: Check the energy balance of the charging process and document the whole analysis in a short report.
step: Choose the circuit. Pick $R$ between $10\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $100\ \text{k}\Omega$ and a capacitor between $10\ \mu\text{F}$ and $220\ \mu\text{F}$ so that $\tau$ is between about $0.5$ and $20$ seconds, slow enough to time by hand. Record nominal values and tolerances, and measure $R$ with a multimeter. If you cannot build the circuit, write a Python simulation that integrates $R\,dq/dt + q/C = \varepsilon$ and label every number "simulated".
step: Predict before you measure. Compute $\tau = RC$ and its uncertainty $\delta\tau/\tau = \sqrt{(\delta R/R)^2 + (\delta C/C)^2}$, and tabulate the predicted $V_C$ at the times you plan to sample, for charging from $0$ to $\varepsilon$ and for discharging from $\varepsilon$ to $0$.
step: Collect charging data. Measure the supply voltage $\varepsilon$ with the meter. Start the stopwatch (or a video of the meter display) when the circuit is connected and record $V_C$ at 10 or more times spread over about $4\tau$. Repeat the run at least three times.
step: Collect discharging data in the same way. Replace the supply with a wire (connect the supply end of $R$ to the return) so that the capacitor discharges through $R$ alone; simply unplugging the supply would leave $R$ open-ended. Record the meter's input resistance from its specification.
step: Linearize and fit. For charging, plot $\ln(1 - V_C/\varepsilon)$ against $t$; for discharging, plot $\ln(V_C/V_0)$ against $t$. Fit a straight line, take $\tau = -1/\text{slope}$, and estimate the uncertainty from the scatter of the fit or from the spread between runs. Inspect the residuals for curvature.
step: Compare with the prediction. State whether the fitted $\tau$ agrees with $RC$ within the combined uncertainties. Estimate the effect of the meter: a meter of input resistance $R_m$ in parallel with the capacitor changes the effective resistance to something close to $R\,R_m/(R+R_m)$ for the discharge and changes the final charging voltage.
step: Check energy. For one charging run compute the energy stored, $\tfrac12 C\varepsilon^2$, the energy dissipated in $R$ from $\int I^2R\,dt$ (use numerical integration of $I = (\varepsilon - V_C)/R$), and the energy supplied by the source, $\varepsilon Q$. Explain why the dissipated energy matches the stored energy.
step: Write the report (one to two pages) with the circuit diagram, a table of data, plots with axes and units labeled, the fit results, the comparison with the prediction, and a list of the main sources of error.
deliverable: A table and plot of $V_C(t)$ for charging and discharging, each labeled "measured" or "simulated", with units and uncertainties.
deliverable: A short report comparing predicted and fitted time constants, with the uncertainty analysis and a discussion of systematic effects such as meter loading, capacitor tolerance and leakage.
deliverable: The energy-balance calculation, showing source energy, stored energy and dissipated energy.
deliverable: Your written solutions to the cumulative final examination.
rubric: Data are documented clearly: units, uncertainties, number of runs, and an unambiguous label of measured versus simulated for every data set.
rubric: The predicted $\tau$ and its uncertainty are computed correctly from the nominal values and tolerances, before looking at the data.
rubric: The linearized fit is carried out correctly, with $\tau$ and an uncertainty obtained and the residuals examined.
rubric: The comparison between prediction and fit is quantitative, and any disagreement is explained by a specific, tested systematic effect rather than by "experimental error".
rubric: The energy balance is calculated and the result (half stored, half dissipated) is explained physically.
rubric: The report is clear and complete: labeled figures, stated sign and polarity conventions, and conclusions that follow from the data.
safety: Use only a battery or USB supply of 9 V or less. Never use mains-powered equipment for this project. Observe the polarity marking on electrolytic capacitors, choose a capacitor rated for at least twice your supply voltage, and discharge it through a resistor before handling. Do not short a battery. The simulation-only route is fully acceptable.
tutor: I measured these values of $V_C$ at these times. What does the pattern of residuals around my fitted line tell me about whether an exponential model is adequate?
tutor: How should I propagate the tolerances of $R$ and $C$ into the uncertainty of $\tau$, and how do I decide whether my fitted value is consistent with the prediction?
tutor: My meter has an input resistance of $10\ \text{M}\Omega$. How much would it change the time constant of my circuit, and how can I test that experimentally or in the simulation?

@review
This guide follows the course week by week. For each item, make sure you can state the key result, say when it applies, and do one calculation without notes.

### Fields and potentials (weeks 1 to 4)

- **Week 1, charge and electric field.** $\vec E = kq\,\hat r/r^2$ and superposition as a vector sum. For continuous sources integrate $k\,dq/r^2$ and use symmetry to discard components. Check dimensions and limits (for a rod, $d \gg L$ must give a point charge).
- **Week 2, Gauss's law.** $\oint \vec E\cdot d\vec A = Q_\text{enc}/\varepsilon_0$. It gives $E$ directly only for spherical, cylindrical and planar symmetry. A conductor has $E = 0$ inside and $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ just outside.
- **Week 3, potential.** $V = kq/r$ adds as a scalar. $\Delta V = -\int \vec E\cdot d\vec l$, $\vec E = -\nabla V$, $U = qV$, and energy conservation.
- **Week 4, capacitance.** $C = Q/\Delta V$ with $\Delta V$ from a field integral. Series capacitors share $Q$, parallel capacitors share $\Delta V$. $U = \tfrac12C\,\Delta V^2$ and $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$.

### Currents and circuits (weeks 5 and 6)

- **Week 5.** $I = nqv_dA$, $R = \rho L/A$, $V = IR$ for ohmic devices, and $P = IV = I^2R = V^2/R$.
- **Week 6.** Series and parallel reduction, the junction and loop rules with a written sign convention, terminal voltage $\varepsilon - Ir$, and the check that power supplied equals power dissipated.

### Magnetism (weeks 7 and 8)

- **Week 7.** $\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B$ does no work; $r = mv/(|q|B)$; $\vec F = I\vec L\times\vec B$; Biot-Savart for a wire, a loop and parallel currents.
- **Week 8.** $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0I_\text{enc}$ for wires, solenoids and toroids. Choose between Ampere's law and Biot-Savart by the symmetry available.

### Induction, AC and waves (weeks 9 to 12)

- **Week 9.** $\mathcal{E} = -N\,d\Phi_B/dt$ with a stated direction of travel and normal, Lenz's law, motional emf $B\ell v$.
- **Week 10.** $N\Phi_B = LI$, $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt$ for current from $a$ to $b$, $U = \tfrac12LI^2$, and the RL circuit with $\tau = L/R$.
- **Week 11.** RC transients with $\tau = RC$, $\omega_0 = 1/\sqrt{LC}$, $Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2}$, $P = I_\text{rms}^2R$, and resonance.
- **Week 12.** Displacement current, $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$, $E_0 = cB_0$, $I = E_0^2/(2\mu_0c)$, and radiation pressure $I/c$ or $2I/c$.

### Self-check

- Can you pick the right symmetry for Gauss's law or Ampere's law, or explain why none applies?
- Can you test any formula by its units and by a limiting case?
- Can you solve a resistor network and show that power in equals power out?
- Can you state your orientation convention before using the minus sign in Faraday's law?
- Can you explain aloud, without notes, why a capacitor charges exponentially and what sets the rate?

### Most common errors

1. Adding fields as magnitudes, or adding potentials as if they were vectors.
2. Sign errors in loop equations, Faraday's law and $V_a - V_b = L\,dI/dt$ because no convention was written down.
3. Using the wrong enclosed charge or current, or the sheet field $\sigma/2\varepsilon_0$ where the conductor value $\sigma/\varepsilon_0$ applies.
4. Series and parallel formulas swapped between capacitors and resistors.
5. Mixing peak and rms values, degrees and radians, or diameter and radius.

@exam num
prompt: A solid insulating sphere of radius $0.040\ \text{m}$ carries a uniform charge of $+12\ \text{nC}$. It is surrounded by a concentric thin conducting shell of radius $0.10\ \text{m}$ that carries $-12\ \text{nC}$. Use $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^2/\text{C}^2$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 1/(4\pi k)$. Find the total energy stored in the electric field, in microjoules.
answer: 12.9
check: (quad(lambda r: 0.5/(4*pi*8.99e9)*(8.99e9*12e-9*r/0.040**3)**2*4*pi*r**2, 0, 0.040) + quad(lambda r: 0.5/(4*pi*8.99e9)*(8.99e9*12e-9/r**2)**2*4*pi*r**2, 0.040, 0.10))*1e6
unit: microjoules
tol: 1%
hint: Use Gauss's law in each region: inside the ball, between the ball and the shell, and outside the shell. What is the net enclosed charge outside the shell?
hint: Inside the ball $E = kQr/a^3$, between the surfaces $E = kQ/r^2$, and outside the shell $E = 0$. The energy density is $u = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2$.
hint: Integrate $u\,(4\pi r^2)\,dr$ over each region where the field is nonzero, and add the two results.
solution: With $Q = 12$ nC, $a = 0.040$ m and $b = 0.10$ m: inside the ball, $U_1 = \displaystyle\int_0^a \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{kQr}{a^3}\right)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \frac{kQ^2}{10a} = 3.24\ \mu\text{J}$. Between the surfaces, $U_2 = \displaystyle\int_a^b \tfrac12\varepsilon_0\left(\frac{kQ}{r^2}\right)^2 4\pi r^2\,dr = \tfrac12kQ^2\left(\frac1a - \frac1b\right) = 9.71\ \mu\text{J}$. The field vanishes outside the shell because the net charge is zero. The total is $U = U_1 + U_2 \approx 12.9\ \mu\text{J}$.

@exam num
prompt: A $12\ \text{V}$ battery with negligible internal resistance is connected by a switch to a $20\ \Omega$ resistor. The other end of that resistor connects to a pair of parallel components, a $30\ \Omega$ resistor and an initially uncharged $100\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor, which return to the battery. The switch closes at $t = 0$. How long does it take for the capacitor voltage to reach $6.0\ \text{V}$? Give the answer in milliseconds.
answer: 2.15
check: root(lambda t: ivp(lambda s, v: ((12.0 - v)/20.0 - v/30.0)/100e-6, 0.0, 0.0, t) - 6.0, 1e-6, 0.02)*1e3
unit: ms
tol: 1%
hint: Find the final capacitor voltage from the resistor divider, because at long times no current flows into the capacitor.
hint: The capacitor sees the $20\ \Omega$ and $30\ \Omega$ resistors in parallel (the battery is a short for this purpose), so the effective resistance is $12\ \Omega$ and $\tau = R_\text{eff}C$.
hint: Then $V_C(t) = V_\infty\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$. Solve for the time at which $V_C = 6.0$ V.
solution: Final voltage: $V_\infty = 12\cdot\dfrac{30}{20+30} = 7.2\ \text{V}$. The resistance seen by the capacitor is $20\parallel30 = 12\ \Omega$, so $\tau = (12)(100\times10^{-6}) = 1.2\ \text{ms}$. Setting $6.0 = 7.2(1 - e^{-t/\tau})$ gives $e^{-t/\tau} = 1/6$, so $t = \tau\ln6 = (1.2)(1.792) \approx 2.15\ \text{ms}$.

@exam self
prompt: A conducting rod of mass $0.020\ \text{kg}$ and length $0.30\ \text{m}$ slides without friction down two long vertical conducting rails, remaining horizontal. The rails are joined at the bottom by a resistor of $0.50\ \Omega$, and the rest of the circuit has negligible resistance. A uniform field of $0.80\ \text{T}$ points horizontally into the plane of the rails. (a) State an orientation convention and find the emf as a function of the rod's speed $v$, and the direction of the induced current. (b) Write the equation of motion and find the terminal speed in symbols and then numerically with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. (c) At terminal speed, show that the power delivered by gravity equals the power dissipated in the resistor.
hint: Choose a direction of travel around the loop and the matching normal, write the flux as $B$ times the loop area, and note that the area between the rod and the resistor shrinks as the rod falls.
hint: The magnetic force on the rod is $BI\ell$, and Lenz's law tells you it opposes the fall. At terminal speed the net force is zero.
hint: Power from gravity is $mgv$; compare with $I^2R$ using the current found at terminal speed.
solution: (a) Take clockwise (as seen with $\vec B$ into the page) as the positive direction around the loop; the normal is then into the page, the same as $\vec B$. The flux is $\Phi_B = B\ell y$, where $y$ is the height of the rod above the resistor, so $\mathcal{E} = -d\Phi_B/dt = -B\ell\,\dot y = +B\ell v$ because $\dot y = -v$. The positive sign means the current is clockwise: the rod carries current to the right. Lenz's law agrees: the inward flux is decreasing, and a clockwise current makes an inward field inside the loop. The magnitude is $\mathcal{E} = B\ell v$ and $I = B\ell v/R$. (b) The force on the rod is $I\ell B$ upward (from $I\vec L\times\vec B$ with current to the right and $\vec B$ into the page), so $m\,dv/dt = mg - B^2\ell^2v/R$. The terminal speed is $v_t = \dfrac{mgR}{B^2\ell^2} = \dfrac{(0.020)(9.8)(0.50)}{(0.80)^2(0.30)^2} = 1.70\ \text{m/s}$, with $I = mg/(B\ell) = 0.82\ \text{A}$. (c) Gravity delivers $mgv_t = 0.196\times1.70 = 0.33\ \text{W}$ and the resistor dissipates $I^2R = (0.817)^2(0.50) = 0.33\ \text{W}$. The two agree, so energy is conserved and the kinetic energy of the rod is constant.
rubric: States a loop direction and matching normal, obtains $\mathcal{E} = B\ell v$ with the sign worked out from $-d\Phi_B/dt$, and gets the current direction (clockwise) consistent with Lenz's law.
rubric: Writes the equation of motion with the magnetic force $B^2\ell^2v/R$ opposing the weight, and solves for $v_t = mgR/(B^2\ell^2)$.
rubric: Evaluates the terminal speed numerically as about $1.7\ \text{m/s}$ with correct units, and the current as about $0.82\ \text{A}$.
rubric: Shows that $mgv_t = I^2R$ at terminal speed and interprets it as energy conservation.
