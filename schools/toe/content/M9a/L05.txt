@article
Closed intervals have a property that open intervals lack: nothing can leak out. A continuous function on $[0,1]$ is bounded and attains its maximum, yet $\frac1x$ on $(0,1]$ is unbounded. The property behind the difference is **compactness**.

### Open covers

A family $\{U_\alpha\}$ of open sets is an **open cover** of $K$ if $K\subseteq\bigcup_\alpha U_\alpha$. A **finite subcover** keeps only finitely many of the sets and still covers $K$.

**Definition.** $K$ is **compact** if *every* open cover of $K$ has a finite subcover.

The word "every" carries the weight. To prove compactness you must handle an arbitrary cover. To disprove it you need just one cover with no finite subcover. For instance, $\mathbb R$ is not compact: the sets $(-n,n)$ cover it, but finitely many cover only a bounded piece.

### Heine–Borel

**Theorem (Heine–Borel).** A subset of $\mathbb R$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

Both words are needed. The set $(0,1]$ is bounded but not closed; $[0,\infty)$ is closed but not bounded; neither is compact.

One direction is short. If $K$ is compact, the cover $\{(-n,n)\}$ has a finite subcover, so $K$ is bounded. If $y\notin K$, the open sets $\{x : |x-y|>\frac1n\}$ cover $K$; one of them, say for $n=M$, already covers $K$ since they increase with $n$, so $N_{1/M}(y)$ misses $K$ and the complement of $K$ is open. The other direction (closed and bounded implies compact) is proved by repeated bisection or from the sequence version below.

### Sequential compactness

**Theorem.** A set $K\subseteq\mathbb R$ is compact if and only if every sequence in $K$ has a subsequence that converges to a point *of $K$*.

For closed and bounded $K$ this is Bolzano–Weierstrass plus closedness: a bounded sequence has a convergent subsequence, and because $K$ is closed the limit stays in $K$. This is the form you will use most, because it lets you work with sequences instead of covers.

### Nested compact sets

If $K_1\supseteq K_2\supseteq\cdots$ are nonempty and compact, then $\bigcap_nK_n\ne\emptyset$. Compactness is essential. The sets $[n,\infty)$ are closed and nested, yet their intersection is empty, and so is $\bigcap_n\left(0,\frac1n\right)$.

### Why we care

Compactness converts "arbitrarily close" into "actually attained". In the coming lessons it will deliver maxima, minima and uniform estimates.

@example
title: A cover with no finite subcover
problem: Prove that $(0,1]$ is not compact.
step: We need one open cover with no finite subcover. Let $U_n=\left(\frac1n,2\right)$ for $n\in\mathbb N$. Each $U_n$ is open.
step: They cover $(0,1]$. Given $x\in(0,1]$, choose $n$ with $\frac1n<x$ (Archimedean property). Then $x\in U_n$ because also $x\le1<2$.
step: Suppose finitely many of them, $U_{n_1},\dots,U_{n_k}$, covered $(0,1]$. Let $M=\max(n_1,\dots,n_k)$. The sets increase with $n$, so their union is just $U_M=\left(\frac1M,2\right)$.
step: But $\frac1{M+1}\in(0,1]$ and $\frac1{M+1}<\frac1M$, so $\frac1{M+1}\notin U_M$. This contradicts the supposed finite subcover.
step: Hence this cover has no finite subcover, and $(0,1]$ is not compact. Notice the failure happens at $0$, a limit point missing from the set.

@mistakes
- Reading the definition as "some open cover has a finite subcover". The single cover $\{\mathbb R\}$ shows that would make every set compact.
- Thinking that "closed" alone or "bounded" alone is enough. Check both: $[0,\infty)$ and $(0,1)$ fail in different ways.
- Taking a convergent subsequence and forgetting to check that its limit lies in the set. For $(0,1)$ the sequence $\frac1{n+1}$ has a convergent subsequence, but the limit $0$ is outside.

@practice self
prompt: Let $K_1\supseteq K_2\supseteq K_3\supseteq\cdots$ be nonempty compact subsets of $\mathbb R$. Prove that $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty K_n\ne\emptyset$.
hint: Choose one point $x_n$ from each $K_n$ and look at the sequence $(x_n)$ inside the compact set $K_1$.
hint: Extract a convergent subsequence. For a fixed $m$, which terms of the subsequence lie in $K_m$, and why must the limit lie in $K_m$?
solution: Pick $x_n\in K_n$. All $x_n$ lie in $K_1$, which is compact, so some subsequence $x_{n_j}$ converges to a point $x\in K_1$. Fix $m$. For all $j$ with $n_j\ge m$ we have $x_{n_j}\in K_{n_j}\subseteq K_m$. So $x$ is a limit of points of $K_m$, and $K_m$ is closed (compact sets are closed), hence $x\in K_m$. Since $m$ was arbitrary, $x\in\bigcap_mK_m$.
rubric: Chooses a point $x_n\in K_n$ for each $n$ and notes that the whole sequence lies in $K_1$.
rubric: Uses sequential compactness of $K_1$ to get a subsequence converging to some $x$.
rubric: For a fixed $m$, shows the tail of the subsequence lies in $K_m$ and uses closedness of $K_m$ to get $x\in K_m$.
rubric: Concludes that $x$ lies in every $K_m$.

@quiz num
prompt: The set $K=\{x\in\mathbb R : |x^2-4|\le3\}$ is closed and bounded, so it is compact and has a largest element. Find $\max K$. Give an exact value.
answer: sqrt(7)
check: max(allroots(lambda x: abs(x*x - 4) - 3, 0, 10))
hint: Rewrite $|x^2-4|\le3$ as $1\le x^2\le7$.
hint: Which boundary equation gives the largest $x$ in the set?
solution: $|x^2-4|\le3$ means $-3\le x^2-4\le3$, i.e. $1\le x^2\le7$. So $K=[-\sqrt7,-1]\cup[1,\sqrt7]$, a finite union of closed intervals, and $\max K=\sqrt7$.
