@article
Differentiation and integration look like opposites. The **fundamental theorem of calculus** says precisely when they are, and "precisely" means checking hypotheses.

### The setting

Let $f$ be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$ and define
$$F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt,\qquad x\in[a,b].$$
The function $F$ is always continuous. If $|f|\le M$ and $x<y$, then $|F(y)-F(x)|=\left|\int_x^yf\right|\le M(y-x)$, so $F$ is Lipschitz. Integrability buys continuity of $F$, but nothing more: differentiability needs extra hypotheses.

### Part 1: differentiating an integral

**Theorem (FTC, part 1).** Let $f$ be integrable on $[a,b]$ and continuous at $c\in[a,b]$. Then $F$ is differentiable at $c$ and $F'(c)=f(c)$. In particular, if $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then $F'=f$ there.

*Proof.* For $h\ne0$ with $c+h\in[a,b]$,
$$\frac{F(c+h)-F(c)}{h}-f(c)=\frac1h\int_c^{c+h}\bigl(f(t)-f(c)\bigr)\,dt.$$
Given $\varepsilon>0$, continuity at $c$ gives $\delta>0$ with $|f(t)-f(c)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|t-c|<\delta$. For $0<|h|<\delta$ the right side has absolute value at most $\frac1{|h|}\cdot\varepsilon|h|=\varepsilon$. $\square$

### Part 2: evaluating an integral

**Theorem (FTC, part 2).** Let $f$ be integrable on $[a,b]$. If $G$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $G'=f$ on $(a,b)$, then
$$\int_a^bf=G(b)-G(a).$$

*Proof.* Let $P$ be any partition. By the mean value theorem there is $t_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)$ with $G(x_i)-G(x_{i-1})=f(t_i)\,\Delta x_i$. Summing over $i$ gives $G(b)-G(a)=\sum f(t_i)\,\Delta x_i$. Since $m_i\le f(t_i)\le M_i$,
$$L(f,P)\le G(b)-G(a)\le U(f,P).$$
This is true for every $P$, so $G(b)-G(a)$ lies between the lower and upper integrals, and both equal $\int_a^bf$. $\square$

### Why the hypotheses matter

- **Part 1:** integrability alone does not give $F'(c)=f(c)$. At a jump of $f$, the function $F$ has a corner.
- **Part 2:** $f$ must be integrable. A derivative need not be: $G(x)=x^2\sin(1/x^2)$ with $G(0)=0$ is differentiable on $[0,1]$, but $G'$ is unbounded, so it has no Riemann integral.
- **Part 2:** $G'=f$ must hold throughout the interval. For $f(x)=\frac1{x^2}$ on $[-1,1]$, the function $G(x)=-\frac1x$ is undefined at $0$ and $f$ is unbounded; blindly evaluating $G(1)-G(-1)$ gives $-2$, an absurd value for the integral of a positive function.

A frequent use of part 1 is the chain rule: if $f$ is continuous and $g$ is differentiable, then $\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_a^{g(x)}f=f(g(x))\,g'(x)$.

@example
title: Differentiating an integral with a variable limit
problem: Let $F(x)=\displaystyle\int_0^{x^2}\sqrt{1+t^4}\,dt$. Find $F'(x)$ and name the hypotheses you used.
step: Write $F(x)=H(g(x))$ with $H(u)=\int_0^u\sqrt{1+t^4}\,dt$ and $g(x)=x^2$.
step: The integrand $\sqrt{1+t^4}$ is continuous on $\mathbb R$, so FTC part 1 gives $H'(u)=\sqrt{1+u^4}$ at every $u$.
step: Since $g$ is differentiable with $g'(x)=2x$, the chain rule gives $F'(x)=H'(g(x))\,g'(x)=\sqrt{1+x^8}\cdot2x$.
step: Hypotheses used: continuity of the integrand at every point (for FTC part 1) and differentiability of $g$ (for the chain rule).

@example
title: A jump breaks differentiability of the integral
problem: Let $f(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge0$, and let $F(x)=\int_{-1}^xf(t)\,dt$. Is $F'(0)=f(0)$?
step: $f$ is integrable on $[-1,1]$ (monotone), so $F$ is defined. For $x\le0$ the integrand is $0$, so $F(x)=0$. For $x\ge0$, $F(x)=\int_0^x1\,dt=x$.
step: The right-hand secant slopes at $0$ are $\frac{F(h)-F(0)}h=\frac hh=1$, and the left-hand ones are $\frac{0-0}h=0$.
step: The two one-sided limits differ, so $F'(0)$ does not exist. In particular $F'(0)\ne f(0)=1$.
step: At every other point $f$ is continuous, and indeed $F'=f$ there. Continuity of $f$ at $c$ is exactly what part 1 needs.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the factor $g'(x)$ when the upper limit is a function of $x$: $\frac{d}{dx}\int_0^{x^2}f=f(x^2)\cdot2x$, not $f(x^2)$.
- Using $G(b)-G(a)$ with a formula $G$ whose derivative is not $f$ throughout the interval, for instance using $\frac{x^2}2$ for $\int_{-1}^2|x|\,dx$.
- Believing $F'=f$ at every point for every integrable $f$. It holds at points of continuity.

@practice ms
prompt: Select all true statements.
choice*: If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf$ satisfies $F'(x)=f(x)$ for every $x\in[a,b]$.
choice: If $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf$ is differentiable at every point of $[a,b]$.
choice*: If $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$ and $G'=f$ on $(a,b)$ with $G$ continuous on $[a,b]$, then $\int_a^bf=G(b)-G(a)$.
choice: If $G$ is differentiable on $[a,b]$, then $\int_a^bG'=G(b)-G(a)$.
choice*: If $f$ is integrable on $[a,b]$, then $F(x)=\int_a^xf$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.
hint: For each false statement look for a counterexample involving a jump or an unbounded derivative.
solution: The statement for continuous $f$ is part 1, the statement with $G'=f$ is part 2 and the statement that $F$ is continuous is the Lipschitz estimate. The claim that $F$ is differentiable everywhere fails at a jump of $f$. The claim $\int_a^bG'=G(b)-G(a)$ for every differentiable $G$ fails when $G'$ is not Riemann integrable, for example $G(x)=x^2\sin(1/x^2)$ with $G(0)=0$, whose derivative is unbounded.

@practice self
prompt: State the second part of the fundamental theorem of calculus with all hypotheses, and prove it.
hint: The proof applies the mean value theorem on each subinterval of an arbitrary partition.
hint: Show $L(f,P)\le G(b)-G(a)\le U(f,P)$ for every $P$, then use what the integral is.
solution: Statement: let $f$ be Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, and let $G$ be continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$ with $G'(x)=f(x)$ for all $x\in(a,b)$. Then $\int_a^bf=G(b)-G(a)$. Proof: let $P:a=x_0<\dots<x_n=b$ be any partition. On each $[x_{i-1},x_i]$ the function $G$ is continuous and differentiable inside, so the mean value theorem gives $t_i\in(x_{i-1},x_i)$ with $G(x_i)-G(x_{i-1})=G'(t_i)\Delta x_i=f(t_i)\Delta x_i$. Summing, $G(b)-G(a)=\sum f(t_i)\Delta x_i$. Because $m_i\le f(t_i)\le M_i$, we get $L(f,P)\le G(b)-G(a)\le U(f,P)$. Since $P$ was arbitrary, $G(b)-G(a)$ is at least $\sup_PL=\int_a^bf$ and at most $\inf_PU=\int_a^bf$, so it equals $\int_a^bf$.
rubric: States all hypotheses: $f$ integrable on $[a,b]$, $G$ continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, $G'=f$ on $(a,b)$.
rubric: Applies the mean value theorem on every subinterval of an arbitrary partition to write $G(b)-G(a)=\sum f(t_i)\Delta x_i$.
rubric: Bounds the sum between the lower and upper Darboux sums using $m_i\le f(t_i)\le M_i$.
rubric: Concludes that $G(b)-G(a)$ equals the integral because it lies between $\sup_PL$ and $\inf_PU$, which coincide.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $F(x)=\displaystyle\int_0^x(t^2-4t+3)\,dt$. At which $x\in(0,2)$ does $F$ have a local maximum?
answer: 1
check: root(lambda x: nderiv(lambda u: quad(lambda t: t*t - 4*t + 3, 0, u), x), 0, 2)
hint: By FTC part 1, $F'(x)=x^2-4x+3$. Where is it zero, and how does its sign change there?
solution: $F'(x)=x^2-4x+3=(x-1)(x-3)$. On $(0,2)$ the only zero is $x=1$, where $F'$ changes from positive to negative, so $F$ has a local maximum at $x=1$. (The zero at $x=3$ is a local minimum, outside the interval.)
