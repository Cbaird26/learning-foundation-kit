step: Set up an outline with five entries: continuity of limits, limits and integrals, limits and derivatives, series (the M-test), and a double limit. Give every entry the same template: Theorem (with hypotheses), Proof, Counterexample, What fails.
step: Entry 1, continuity. State and prove that a uniform limit of continuous functions is continuous, using the $\varepsilon/3$ argument. Counterexample: $x^n$ on $[0,1]$. Derive the pointwise limit and show $d_n=1$ on $[0,1)$.
step: Entry 2, integrals. State and prove that uniform convergence of Riemann integrable functions makes the limit integrable with $\int f_n\to\int f$, using $\left|\int(f_n-f)\right|\le(b-a)\,d_n$. Counterexample: the triangular spikes, which tend to $0$ pointwise while every $\int f_n=1$.
step: Entry 3, derivatives. State the theorem that needs the derivatives $f_n'$ to converge uniformly, and prove it (or prove a clearly stated special case, such as continuous $f_n'$ using the fundamental theorem). Counterexample: $\frac{\sin nx}{n}$ or $\frac{x}{1+nx^2}$, where $f_n\to0$ uniformly but $f_n'(0)=1$ for every $n$.
step: Entry 4, series. Prove the Weierstrass M-test from the Cauchy criterion. Apply it to $\sum\frac{\cos kx}{k^3}$ to justify continuity and termwise differentiation, and to $\sum\left(\frac x2\right)^k$ on $[0,1]$ to justify termwise integration.
step: Entry 5, double limits. Show with $a_{m,n}=\frac{m}{m+n}$ that $\lim_m\lim_na_{m,n}\ne\lim_n\lim_ma_{m,n}$, and explain which of your earlier theorems is an instance of swapping two limits.
step: For every counterexample, check your formulas numerically with a short Python loop that prints $d_n$ (or the failing integral or derivative) for $n=1,\dots,50$ and compare with your exact answer.
step: Assemble the summary table (continuity, integral, derivative against pointwise and uniform convergence), then rehearse a five-minute explanation of one theorem and its proof without notes before the examination.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Completeness.** Every nonempty set bounded above has a supremum. To prove $s=\sup S$, show $s$ is an upper bound and that for every $\varepsilon>0$ some element exceeds $s-\varepsilon$. The Archimedean property gives $\frac1n<\varepsilon$.
2. **Sequences.** The $\varepsilon$–$N$ definition, monotone convergence, Bolzano–Weierstrass, and Cauchy if and only if convergent in $\mathbb R$.
3. **Series.** Convergence means convergence of partial sums. Term test (one direction only), comparison, ratio and alternating series tests; absolute versus conditional convergence.
4. **Open and closed sets.** Closed means containing all limit points, equivalently having an open complement, equivalently closed under limits of sequences. Finite intersections of open sets are open; infinite ones need not be.
5. **Compactness.** Heine–Borel (closed and bounded); every sequence has a subsequence converging inside the set; nested nonempty compact sets intersect.
6. **Continuity.** The $\varepsilon$–$\delta$ and sequence forms. Continuous images of compact sets are compact, which gives the extreme value theorem; the intermediate value theorem uses completeness.
7. **Uniform continuity.** One $\delta$ per $\varepsilon$. Disprove with $|x_n-y_n|\to0$ but $|f(x_n)-f(y_n)|\not\to0$; prove with a Lipschitz bound or compactness.
8. **Differentiation.** Differentiable implies continuous; Fermat, Rolle and the mean value theorem; the false converses.
9. **Riemann integration.** Upper and lower sums, the $U-L<\varepsilon$ criterion, monotone and continuous functions are integrable, the Dirichlet function is not.
10. **Fundamental theorem.** Part 1 needs continuity of the integrand at the point; part 2 needs $f$ integrable and $G'=f$.
11. **Function sequences.** Fix $x$, then let $n\to\infty$. Pointwise limits can lose continuity and change integrals and derivatives.
12. **Uniform convergence.** $d_n=\sup|f_n-f|\to0$ preserves continuity and integrals; derivatives need $f_n'$ to converge uniformly. The M-test handles series.

### Self-check

- Can you write each definition with its quantifiers in the correct order, and negate it?
- Can you prove, directly from the definition, that a number is a supremum and that a sequence converges?
- Given a set, can you decide whether it is open, closed or compact, with a proof or a counterexample?
- Can you give a counterexample to each false converse from weeks 2 to 8?
- Can you state the second part of the fundamental theorem with every hypothesis and prove it?
- Given a function sequence, can you find the pointwise limit, compute $d_n$, and say which interchanges are justified?

### Most common errors

- Choosing $N$ or $\delta$ before $\varepsilon$, or letting a $\delta$ that must be uniform depend on $x$.
- "Proving" continuity or convergence by checking examples or a single sequence.
- Treating a supremum as a maximum.
- Quoting a theorem without verifying every hypothesis: the mean value theorem for $|x|$, the fundamental theorem with an unbounded integrand, the extreme value theorem on $(0,1)$.
- Concluding uniform convergence from pointwise convergence together with a continuous limit.
- Differentiating term by term when only $f_n$, not $f_n'$, converges uniformly.

prompt: The series $S(x)=\sum_{k=1}^\infty\dfrac{\cos kx}{k^3}$ converges for every real $x$. Use the Weierstrass M-test on the series of derivatives to justify differentiating term by term, and then find $S'\!\left(\frac\pi2\right)$. Round to 3 decimal places. (You may use Python to sum enough terms.)
answer: -0.916
check: nderiv(lambda x: nsum(lambda k: cos(k*x)/k**3, 1, 2000), pi/2)
tol: 0.0005
hint: Differentiate each term: $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{\cos kx}{k^3}=-\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}$, and $\left|\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}\right|\le\frac1{k^2}$.
hint: The M-test with $M_k=\frac1{k^2}$ shows the derivative series converges uniformly, so $S'(x)=-\sum\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}$. At $x=\frac\pi2$ the values $\sin\frac{k\pi}2$ cycle through $1,0,-1,0$.
hint: So $S'(\frac\pi2)=-\left(1-\frac19+\frac1{25}-\frac1{49}+\cdots\right)$. For this alternating series the error is at most the first omitted term; sum until that term is below $0.0005$.
solution: Termwise differentiation is justified because $\sum\frac{\cos kx}{k^3}$ converges (its terms are bounded by $\frac1{k^3}$) and the derivative series $-\sum\frac{\sin kx}{k^2}$ converges uniformly by the M-test with $M_k=\frac1{k^2}$ and $\sum\frac1{k^2}<\infty$. At $x=\frac\pi2$ only odd $k$ contribute, with signs $+,-,+,\dots$, so $S'(\frac\pi2)=-\left(1-\frac1{3^2}+\frac1{5^2}-\cdots\right)$. The alternating series estimate shows that about $23$ terms give three-decimal accuracy, and the sum is $0.9160$ to four places, so $S'(\frac\pi2)\approx-0.916$.

@exam self
prompt: Let $f_n:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be continuous for every $n$, and suppose $f_n\to f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$. Put $F_n(x)=\int_a^xf_n(t)\,dt$. (a) Prove that $f$ is continuous, hence integrable, and let $F(x)=\int_a^xf(t)\,dt$. (b) Prove that $F_n\to F$ uniformly on $[a,b]$. (c) Prove that $F'=f$ on $[a,b]$, and explain how this agrees with the theorem on differentiating a limit of functions.
hint: For (a) use the $\varepsilon/3$ argument; for (b) bound $|F_n(x)-F(x)|$ by something that does not depend on $x$.
hint: For (c) which part of the fundamental theorem applies when the integrand is continuous? What do the derivatives $F_n'$ equal?
solution: (a) Fix $c$ and $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $n$ with $\sup|f_n-f|<\frac\varepsilon3$ and then $\delta$ with $|x-c|<\delta\Rightarrow|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|<\frac\varepsilon3$. Then $|f(x)-f(c)|\le|f(x)-f_n(x)|+|f_n(x)-f_n(c)|+|f_n(c)-f(c)|<\varepsilon$, so $f$ is continuous. A continuous function on $[a,b]$ is Riemann integrable (by uniform continuity), so $F$ is defined. (b) Let $d_n=\sup|f_n-f|\to0$. For every $x\in[a,b]$, $|F_n(x)-F(x)|=\left|\int_a^x(f_n-f)\right|\le\int_a^x|f_n-f|\le(b-a)\,d_n$. The right side does not depend on $x$, so $\sup_x|F_n-F|\le(b-a)d_n\to0$. (c) Since $f$ is continuous at every point, the fundamental theorem (part 1) gives $F'=f$. By the same theorem $F_n'=f_n$, so the derivatives $F_n'$ converge uniformly to $f=F'$. This is exactly the hypothesis of the theorem on differentiating a uniform limit, and its conclusion $F'=\lim F_n'$ is consistent with what we found.
rubric: Part (a): proves continuity of $f$ with the $\varepsilon/3$ argument and justifies integrability from continuity.
rubric: Part (b): bounds $|F_n(x)-F(x)|$ by $(b-a)d_n$ independently of $x$ and concludes uniform convergence from $d_n\to0$.
rubric: Part (c): applies part 1 of the fundamental theorem (continuity of $f$) and notes that $F_n'=f_n$ converge uniformly, matching the derivative theorem.
rubric: Hypotheses and quantifier order are stated correctly throughout, with no step that assumes only pointwise convergence.

@exam self
prompt: Let $f:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R$ be continuous, and suppose $f(x)\to L$ as $x\to\infty$ for some real $L$. Prove that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $[0,\infty)$.
hint: Split the half-line into a far part where $f$ is close to $L$, and a bounded part where compactness helps.
hint: Choose $R$ so that $|f(x)-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for $x\ge R$, and use uniform continuity of $f$ on the compact interval $[0,R+1]$. Then choose $\delta$ to handle both parts at once.
solution: Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $R>0$ with $|f(x)-L|<\frac\varepsilon2$ for all $x\ge R$. Then for $x,y\ge R$, $|f(x)-f(y)|\le|f(x)-L|+|L-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The interval $[0,R+1]$ is compact and $f$ is continuous on it, so $f$ is uniformly continuous there: there is $\delta_1>0$ with $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $x,y\in[0,R+1]$ and $|x-y|<\delta_1$. Put $\delta=\min(\delta_1,1)$ and let $x,y\ge0$ with $|x-y|<\delta$. If both $x,y\ge R$, the first estimate gives $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. Otherwise $\min(x,y)<R$, and since $|x-y|<1$, both points lie in $[0,R+1]$ with $|x-y|<\delta_1$, so $|f(x)-f(y)|<\varepsilon$. The number $\delta$ depends only on $\varepsilon$, so $f$ is uniformly continuous.
rubric: Uses the limit at infinity to find $R$ such that any two points beyond $R$ have values within $\varepsilon$ of each other.
rubric: Invokes the theorem that a continuous function on a compact interval is uniformly continuous, applied on $[0,R+1]$.
rubric: Defines $\delta=\min(\delta_1,1)$ and checks both cases (both points beyond $R$, or one point below $R$ so both lie in $[0,R+1]$).
rubric: Concludes with a $\delta$ depending only on $\varepsilon$.
