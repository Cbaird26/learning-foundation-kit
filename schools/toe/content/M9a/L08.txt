@article
The derivative of $f$ at $c$ is the limit of slopes of secant lines:
$$f'(c)=\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c},$$
provided this limit exists as a real number. If it does, $f$ is **differentiable** at $c$. Everything in this lesson is a consequence of that definition together with the completeness-based results of earlier weeks.

### Differentiable implies continuous

For $x\ne c$ write $f(x)-f(c)=\dfrac{f(x)-f(c)}{x-c}\cdot(x-c)$. As $x\to c$ the first factor tends to $f'(c)$ and the second to $0$, so $f(x)\to f(c)$. The converse is false: $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous at $0$, but the secant slopes $\frac{|h|}{h}$ are $+1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$, so no limit exists.

### Extrema and Rolle's theorem

**Fermat's lemma.** If $f$ has a local maximum or minimum at an interior point $c$ and $f'(c)$ exists, then $f'(c)=0$. The converse is false: $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=0$ but is strictly increasing, so $0$ is not an extremum.

**Rolle's theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, differentiable on $(a,b)$, and $f(a)=f(b)$, then $f'(c)=0$ for some $c\in(a,b)$.

*Proof.* By the extreme value theorem $f$ attains a maximum and a minimum on $[a,b]$. If both occur at the endpoints, $f$ is constant and any $c$ works. Otherwise one of them occurs at an interior point $c$, where $f'(c)=0$ by Fermat's lemma. $\square$

### The mean value theorem

**Theorem.** If $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$, there is $c\in(a,b)$ with
$$f'(c)=\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}.$$
Apply Rolle to $g(x)=f(x)-f(a)-\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)$, which has $g(a)=g(b)=0$.

Consequences: if $f'=0$ on an interval, then $f$ is constant there; if $f'>0$ on an interval, then $f$ is strictly increasing there. Both follow by applying the theorem on a subinterval $[x,y]$.

The hypotheses matter. For $f(x)=|x|$ on $[-1,2]$ the average slope is $\frac13$, but $f'(x)=\pm1$ wherever it exists, so the conclusion fails: $f$ is not differentiable at the interior point $0$.

### False converses worth knowing

- Continuous does not imply differentiable ($|x|$).
- $f'(c)=0$ does not imply an extremum ($x^3$).
- Strictly increasing does not imply $f'>0$ everywhere ($x^3$ again). Only $f'\ge0$ survives.
- Differentiable does not imply continuously differentiable (the example below).

@example
title: Differentiable everywhere, but the derivative is not continuous
problem: Let $f(x)=x^2\sin\frac1x$ for $x\ne0$ and $f(0)=0$. Show that $f$ is differentiable at every point, but $f'$ is not continuous at $0$.
step: At $0$: the secant slope is $\dfrac{f(h)-f(0)}{h}=h\sin\dfrac1h$. Since $\left|h\sin\frac1h\right|\le|h|\to0$, the squeeze theorem gives $f'(0)=0$.
step: For $x\ne0$ the product and chain rules give $f'(x)=2x\sin\dfrac1x-\cos\dfrac1x$. So $f$ is differentiable at every point.
step: Take $x_n=\dfrac1{2\pi n}$, which tends to $0$. Then $\sin\frac1{x_n}=\sin(2\pi n)=0$ and $\cos\frac1{x_n}=1$, so $f'(x_n)=-1$ for every $n$.
step: Therefore $f'(x_n)\to-1\ne0=f'(0)$ while $x_n\to0$, so $f'$ is not continuous at $0$. Differentiable everywhere does not give continuously differentiable.

@mistakes
- Believing continuity gives differentiability. A corner, like $|x|$ at $0$, is continuous but has no derivative.
- Reading $f'(c)=0$ as "local maximum or minimum at $c$". It is only a necessary condition.
- Quoting Rolle or the mean value theorem without checking that $f$ is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable on the open interval.

@practice num
prompt: The mean value theorem applied to $f(x)=x^3$ on $[0,2]$ gives a point $c\in(0,2)$ with $f'(c)=\dfrac{f(2)-f(0)}{2-0}$. Find $c$ exactly.
answer: 2/sqrt(3)
check: root(lambda c: nderiv(lambda x: x**3, c) - (2**3 - 0**3)/(2 - 0), 0, 2)
hint: First compute the average slope $\frac{f(2)-f(0)}{2}$, then solve $f'(c)=$ that value.
solution: The average slope is $\frac{8-0}{2}=4$. Since $f'(c)=3c^2$, we need $3c^2=4$, so $c=\frac{2}{\sqrt3}\approx1.155$, which lies in $(0,2)$. The negative root is outside the interval.

@practice self
prompt: Each statement below is the converse of a true theorem, and each is false. Give a counterexample to each and justify it. (a) If $f$ is continuous at $c$, then $f$ is differentiable at $c$. (b) If $f'(c)=0$, then $f$ has a local maximum or a local minimum at $c$. (c) If $f$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$, then $f'(x)>0$ for every $x$.
hint: Think of a corner for (a) and of a cubic for (b) and (c).
hint: For each counterexample, verify the hypothesis first, and then show the conclusion fails.
solution: (a) $f(x)=|x|$ is continuous at $0$. The secant slope $\frac{|h|}{h}$ equals $1$ for $h>0$ and $-1$ for $h<0$, so $f'(0)$ does not exist. (b) $f(x)=x^3$ has $f'(0)=3\cdot0^2=0$, but for $x<0$ we have $f(x)<0=f(0)$ and for $x>0$ we have $f(x)>0$, so $0$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum. (c) $f(x)=x^3$ is strictly increasing on $\mathbb R$ (since $x<y$ implies $x^3<y^3$), yet $f'(0)=0$, not positive.
rubric: Part (a): gives a continuous function with a corner or cusp and shows that the two one-sided secant slopes disagree.
rubric: Part (b): gives a function with $f'(c)=0$ and checks that $c$ is neither a local maximum nor a local minimum.
rubric: Part (c): gives a strictly increasing function whose derivative vanishes at a point, and verifies that it is strictly increasing.
rubric: Each answer checks the hypothesis of the false statement before showing its conclusion fails.

prompt: The function $f(x)=x^3-3x+1$ satisfies $f(-\sqrt3)=f(\sqrt3)$, so Rolle's theorem applies on $[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$. Find all points $c$ in $(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)$ with $f'(c)=0$. Enter them separated by commas.
answer: -1, 1
check: allroots(lambda c: nderiv(lambda x: x**3 - 3*x + 1, c), -1.7, 1.7)
hint: Compute $f'(x)$ and solve $f'(x)=0$.
solution: $f'(x)=3x^2-3=3(x-1)(x+1)$, which vanishes at $x=-1$ and $x=1$. Both lie in $(-\sqrt3,\sqrt3)$. Rolle's theorem guarantees at least one such point; here there are two.

@quiz mc
prompt: A student applies the mean value theorem to $f(x)=|x|$ on $[-2,3]$. The average slope is $\frac{f(3)-f(-2)}{5}=\frac15$, but no point $c$ has $f'(c)=\frac15$. Which explanation is correct?
choice*: $f$ is not differentiable at the interior point $0$, so the hypotheses of the theorem are not satisfied.
choice: The mean value theorem fails for all functions that are not polynomials.
choice: $f$ is not continuous on $[-2,3]$.
choice: The interval must be symmetric about $0$ for the theorem to apply.
hint: List the hypotheses of the theorem and test each one for $|x|$.
solution: The function $|x|$ is continuous on $[-2,3]$, but it is not differentiable at $0\in(-2,3)$. Where it is differentiable, $f'(x)=\pm1$, never $\frac15$. The theorem is not wrong; its hypotheses simply fail.
