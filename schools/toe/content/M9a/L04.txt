@article
Calculus lives on intervals. Analysis needs the right notion of "a region without edges" and "a region that contains its edges" for arbitrary subsets of $\mathbb R$.

### Neighborhoods and open sets

The **neighborhood** of radius $r>0$ about $x$ is $N_r(x)=(x-r,x+r)$. A set $U\subseteq\mathbb R$ is **open** if every point of $U$ has a neighborhood inside $U$:
$$\forall x\in U\ \ \exists r>0:\ N_r(x)\subseteq U.$$
An interval $(a,b)$ is open: for $x\in(a,b)$ take $r=\min(x-a,\,b-x)$. So are $\mathbb R$ and $\emptyset$ (vacuously). The interval $[0,1)$ is not open, since no neighborhood of $0$ stays inside it. Neither is $\mathbb Q$, because every neighborhood of a rational contains irrationals.

### Limit points and closed sets

A point $x$ is a **limit point** of $E$ if every neighborhood of $x$ contains a point of $E$ other than $x$. A set is **closed** if it contains all of its limit points. Three descriptions are equivalent, and each is useful in proofs:

- $E$ contains all its limit points;
- the complement $\mathbb R\setminus E$ is open;
- whenever $x_n\in E$ and $x_n\to x$, the limit $x$ is in $E$.

The last form connects to sequences: a limit point of $E$ is exactly a limit of a sequence of distinct points of $E$.

### Examples worth remembering

$[a,b]$ is closed. The set $\{\frac1n : n\in\mathbb N\}$ is not closed, because $0$ is a limit point that it does not contain; adding $0$ gives a closed set. $\mathbb Z$ is closed since it has no limit points. $\mathbb R$ and $\emptyset$ are both open and closed. Most sets are neither: $[0,1)$ and $\mathbb Q$ are neither open nor closed. Unlike doors, sets that are not open need not be closed.

### Unions and intersections

- Any union of open sets is open, and any finite intersection of open sets is open.
- Any intersection of closed sets is closed, and any finite union of closed sets is closed.

The word "finite" matters. The intersection $\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty\left(-\frac1n,\frac1n\right)=\{0\}$ is an infinite intersection of open sets that is not open. Dually, $\bigcup_{n=1}^\infty\left[\frac1n,1\right]=(0,1]$ is an infinite union of closed sets that is not closed.

### Why this matters

Closed sets are the sets that keep limits inside them. Together with boundedness this is the heart of compactness, the topic of the next lesson.

@example
title: A set that is neither open nor closed
problem: Decide whether $A=[0,1)$ is open, closed, both or neither.
step: Open? The point $0$ is in $A$. Every neighborhood $(-r,r)$ contains $-\frac r2\notin A$. So $0$ has no neighborhood inside $A$, and $A$ is not open.
step: Closed? Consider $x_n=1-\frac1{n+1}$, which lies in $A$ and converges to $1$. The limit $1$ is not in $A$.
step: So $1$ is a limit point of $A$ that $A$ does not contain, and $A$ is not closed.
step: Conclusion: $A$ is neither open nor closed.

@example
title: A closed set via its complement
problem: Prove that $E=\{0\}\cup\left\{\frac1n : n\in\mathbb N\right\}$ is closed.
step: Take any $x\notin E$. We must find a neighborhood of $x$ that misses $E$; then $\mathbb R\setminus E$ is open.
step: If $x<0$, take $r=|x|$. If $x>1$, take $r=x-1$. In both cases $N_r(x)$ misses $E\subseteq[0,1]$.
step: If $0<x<1$ and $x\notin E$, then $x$ lies strictly between consecutive points of $E$: $\frac1{n+1}<x<\frac1n$ for some $n$. Take $r=\min\left(x-\frac1{n+1},\ \frac1n-x\right)>0$.
step: In every case $N_r(x)\subseteq\mathbb R\setminus E$. Hence the complement is open and $E$ is closed.

@mistakes
- Assuming "not open" means "closed". The set $[0,1)$ is neither.
- Forgetting that $\mathbb R$ and $\emptyset$ are both open and closed.
- Assuming an infinite intersection of open sets must be open (or an infinite union of closed sets must be closed).
- Forgetting limit points that lie outside the set, such as $0$ for $\{\frac1n\}$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $U=\{x\in\mathbb R : x^2-8x+12<0\}$, which is an open set. Find the largest $r$ such that $N_r(3)=(3-r,\,3+r)\subseteq U$.
answer: 1
check: min(abs(3 - t) for t in allroots(lambda x: x*x - 8*x + 12, 0, 10))
hint: Factor the quadratic to describe $U$ as an interval.
hint: $U=(2,6)$. How far is $3$ from each endpoint?
solution: $x^2-8x+12=(x-2)(x-6)<0$ exactly when $2<x<6$, so $U=(2,6)$. The distances from $3$ to the endpoints are $1$ and $3$. The neighborhood must stay inside, so the largest radius is the smaller distance, $r=1$.

(b) A bounded sequence that does not converge but has a convergent subsequence. (c) A convergent series that is not absolutely convergent. (d) A divergent series whose terms tend to $0$.

(c) $\sum\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{k}$ converges by the alternating series test, but $\sum\frac1k$ diverges. (d) $\sum\frac1k$: the terms tend to $0$, yet the partial sums satisfy $s_{2^m}\ge1+\frac m2$.

@quiz list
prompt: Find all limit points of the set $E=\left\{(-1)^n+\frac1n : n\in\mathbb N\right\}$. Enter them separated by commas.
answer: -1, 1
check: [round((-1)**n + 1/n) for n in (10**6, 10**6 + 1)]
hint: Split the points of $E$ according to whether $n$ is even or odd.
hint: For even $n$ the points are $1+\frac1n$; for odd $n$ they are $-1+\frac1n$. Where do these accumulate?
solution: Points with even $n$ are $1+\frac1n$, which are distinct and converge to $1$. Points with odd $n$ are $-1+\frac1n$, distinct and converging to $-1$. So $1$ and $-1$ are limit points. No other number is: away from $\pm1$, a small neighborhood contains only finitely many points of $E$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A student argues that $E=\left\{1,\frac12,\frac13,\dots\right\}$ is closed because every point of $E$ has a neighborhood containing no other point of $E$. What is wrong with the argument?
choice*: Closedness requires containing all limit points, and $0$ is a limit point of $E$ that does not belong to $E$.
choice: Having an isolated neighborhood is the definition of open, so $E$ must be open.
choice: Closed sets must be intervals.
choice: The argument is fine, because every finite-or-countable set is closed.
hint: Look for a sequence of points of $E$ whose limit is not in $E$.
solution: The sequence $\frac1n\in E$ converges to $0\notin E$, so $E$ does not contain all its limit points and is not closed. Isolated points of $E$ say nothing about points outside $E$ that are approached by $E$. Countable sets need not be closed (consider $\mathbb Q$), and closed sets need not be intervals.
