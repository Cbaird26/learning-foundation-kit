@article
Calculus teaches you to compute integrals. Analysis asks what an integral *is* and which functions have one. We use the approach of Darboux, in which the integral is trapped between two kinds of approximating sums.

### Partitions and Darboux sums

Let $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ be **bounded**. A **partition** $P$ is a finite list $a=x_0<x_1<\dots<x_n=b$. On the $i$-th subinterval put
$$M_i=\sup_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f,\qquad m_i=\inf_{[x_{i-1},x_i]}f,\qquad \Delta x_i=x_i-x_{i-1}.$$
The **upper sum** and **lower sum** are
$$U(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^nM_i\,\Delta x_i,\qquad L(f,P)=\sum_{i=1}^nm_i\,\Delta x_i.$$
Clearly $L(f,P)\le U(f,P)$. The supremum and infimum sit at endpoints when $f$ is monotone; in general they can occur in the interior.

### Refinement and the integral

Adding points to a partition (a **refinement**) can only raise $L$ and lower $U$. Comparing two partitions $P,Q$ with their common refinement gives $L(f,P)\le U(f,Q)$ for *any* pair. So every lower sum lies below every upper sum, and
$$\underline{\int_a^b}f=\sup_PL(f,P)\ \le\ \overline{\int_a^b}f=\inf_PU(f,P).$$
Completeness guarantees that the sup and inf exist. The function $f$ is **Riemann integrable** when the two numbers agree, and the common value is $\int_a^bf$.

### The criterion

**Theorem.** A bounded $f$ is integrable if and only if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a partition $P$ with $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.

This turns integrability into an $\varepsilon$ statement, which you verify by exhibiting a good partition.

### Which functions are integrable?

- **Monotone functions.** With $n$ equal pieces the differences telescope: $U-L=\dfrac{(b-a)\,(f(b)-f(a))}{n}\to0$.
- **Continuous functions.** By uniform continuity pick $\delta$ with $|x-y|<\delta\Rightarrow|f(x)-f(y)|<\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$. For a partition with pieces shorter than $\delta$, each $M_i-m_i\le\frac\varepsilon{b-a}$, so $U-L\le\varepsilon$. Running the argument with $\varepsilon/2$ gives the strict inequality the criterion asks for.
- **Bounded functions with finitely many discontinuities** are integrable (stated here without proof).
- **Not every bounded function is integrable.** The Dirichlet function ($1$ at rationals, $0$ at irrationals) has $L(f,P)=0$ and $U(f,P)=1$ for every partition of $[0,1]$, because every subinterval contains both rationals and irrationals. Its lower integral is $0$ and its upper integral is $1$.

### Properties

The integrable functions form a vector space; the integral is linear and monotone ($f\le g$ implies $\int f\le\int g$), additive over adjacent intervals, and $\left|\int f\right|\le\int|f|$.

@example
title: Darboux sums for x squared
problem: For $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ and the partition $P=\{0,\tfrac14,\tfrac12,\tfrac34,1\}$, compute $U(f,P)$ and $L(f,P)$, and explain why $f$ is integrable.
step: $f$ is increasing on $[0,1]$, so on each subinterval the supremum is at the right endpoint and the infimum at the left endpoint. Every $\Delta x_i=\frac14$.
step: Upper sum: $U=\frac14\left(\frac1{16}+\frac4{16}+\frac9{16}+\frac{16}{16}\right)=\frac14\cdot\frac{30}{16}=\frac{15}{32}$.
step: Lower sum: $L=\frac14\left(0+\frac1{16}+\frac4{16}+\frac9{16}\right)=\frac14\cdot\frac{14}{16}=\frac7{32}$.
step: The gap is $U-L=\frac{8}{32}=\frac14$. For $n$ equal pieces the same telescoping gives $U-L=\frac1n$, because $f(1)-f(0)=1$.
step: Given $\varepsilon>0$, choose $n>\frac1\varepsilon$. Then $U-L<\varepsilon$, so $f$ is integrable. Already $\frac7{32}\le\int_0^1x^2\,dx\le\frac{15}{32}$.

@mistakes
- Evaluating $f$ at endpoints instead of taking a true supremum or infimum. For a function with an interior peak, the endpoint values are wrong.
- Thinking "bounded implies integrable". The Dirichlet function is bounded and not integrable.
- Thinking "integrable implies continuous". Step functions are integrable.

prompt: Let $f(x)=x(1-x)$ on $[0,1]$ and $P=\{0,\tfrac13,1\}$. Find the upper sum $U(f,P)$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 13/54
check: sum(max(x*(1 - x) for x in np.linspace(a, b, 100001))*(b - a) for a, b in ((0, 1/3), (1/3, 1)))

prompt: Prove that every increasing function $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is Riemann integrable.
hint: Use the criterion, with the partition into $n$ equal pieces of length $\frac{b-a}n$. For an increasing function, where are $M_i$ and $m_i$ on each piece?

@quiz num
prompt: Let $f(x)=x^2$ on $[0,2]$ and let $P_n$ split $[0,2]$ into $n$ equal subintervals. Find the smallest $n$ such that $U(f,P_n)-L(f,P_n)<0.03$.
answer: 267
check: min(n for n in range(1, 2000) if np.sum(((2*(np.arange(n) + 1)/n)**2 - (2*np.arange(n)/n)**2)*(2/n)) < 0.03)
hint: Since $f$ is increasing, $U-L$ telescopes to $\frac{(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))}n$.
hint: Here $(b-a)(f(b)-f(a))=2\cdot4$. Solve $\frac8n<0.03$.
solution: $U-L=\frac{2\cdot(4-0)}n=\frac8n$. We need $\frac8n<0.03$, i.e. $n>266.67$, so the smallest integer is $n=267$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement is equivalent to "a bounded function $f$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$"?
choice*: For every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a partition $P$ with $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$.
choice: There is a partition $P$ with $U(f,P)=L(f,P)$.
choice: $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$.
choice: $f$ has an antiderivative on $[a,b]$.
hint: Recall what the integral is trapped between, and what must happen to the gap.
solution: The criterion "for every $\varepsilon>0$ there is a partition $P$ with $U(f,P)-L(f,P)<\varepsilon$" is equivalent to integrability. A single partition with $U=L$ forces $f$ to be constant on each piece, which is far too strong. Continuity is sufficient but not necessary (step functions are integrable), and having an antiderivative is neither necessary nor sufficient for Riemann integrability.
