@article
Often the coordinates you naturally write down are not independent: a bead stays on a wire, a rope has fixed length. L01 assumed independent coordinates. This lesson shows what to do otherwise.

### Holonomic constraints

A **holonomic constraint** is an equation $f_a(q, t) = 0$, $a = 1, \dots, m$, among the $n$ coordinates, and possibly the time. With $m$ independent constraints there are $n - m$ **degrees of freedom**. There are two strategies.

**Eliminate.** Choose $n - m$ independent coordinates that satisfy the constraints automatically (as with the pendulum angle in L01). This is the quickest route to the motion, but the constraint forces disappear from the equations.

**Add multipliers.** Keep all $n$ coordinates and let Lagrange multipliers $\lambda^a(t)$ carry the constraints. This route yields the constraint forces as well.

### Constrained variation

Hamilton's principle gives $\delta S = -\int E_i\,\delta q^i\,dt$ with $E_i \equiv \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}$. Now the variations cannot be arbitrary: **admissible** variations keep the constraints satisfied at each frozen time, so

$$\frac{\partial f_a}{\partial q^i}\,\delta q^i = 0 .$$

Then $E_i\,\delta q^i = 0$ only for admissible $\delta q$. The vector $E_i$ is orthogonal to the whole tangent space of the constraint surface, so it is a combination of the normals $\partial f_a/\partial q^i$:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} = \lambda^a \frac{\partial f_a}{\partial q^i}, \qquad f_a(q, t) = 0 .$$

These are the Euler–Lagrange equations of the **extended Lagrangian** $L' = L + \lambda^a f_a$, treating $q^i$ and $\lambda^a$ as independent: varying $\lambda^a$ returns the constraints. You get $n + m$ equations for $n + m$ unknown functions.

### Meaning of the multipliers

The right-hand side $Q_i = \lambda^a\,\partial f_a/\partial q^i$ is the **generalized constraint force**. In Cartesian coordinates $\lambda\nabla f$ is normal to the constraint surface. Ideal constraints do no virtual work: $Q_i\,\delta q^i = 0$ for every admissible $\delta q$. The sign of $\lambda$ depends on how you write $f$ (replacing $f \to c f$ rescales $\lambda \to \lambda/c$), but $\lambda\nabla f$ is physical.

To solve: use the second time derivative of the constraint to eliminate accelerations, solve for $\lambda$, then recover the motion.

### Velocity constraints

A constraint $a_{ai}(q,t)\,\dot q^i + a_{a0}(q,t) = 0$ that cannot be integrated to $f(q,t) = 0$ is **nonholonomic** (a blade that can only move along its own heading is an example). For ideal constraints of this type, the rule is the d'Alembert–Lagrange equation

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} = \lambda^a a_{ai}, \qquad a_{ai}\dot q^i + a_{a0} = 0 .$$

This is *not* the result of extremizing $S$ over paths that obey the velocity constraint; that "variational" prescription gives different dynamics for nonintegrable constraints, and it is not the one that matches rolling and sliding contacts. For holonomic constraints the two agree.

### Energy and one-sided constraints

If the constraints do not depend on $t$ and $L$ has no explicit time dependence, $h$ is conserved even though constraint forces act, since they do no work. A time-dependent constraint can change $h$. A particle resting on a surface satisfies only a **one-sided** constraint: the multiplier analysis holds while the normal force pushes (its sign is the physical one); when it would change sign, the particle leaves the surface.

@example
title: The Atwood machine with a multiplier
problem: Masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ hang from a massless, inextensible rope over a fixed, massless, frictionless pulley. Let $x_1, x_2$ be their downward distances below the pulley. Find the accelerations and the rope tension.
step: The rope length $\ell_0$ gives $f = x_1 + x_2 - \ell_0 = 0$, so $\partial f/\partial x_1 = \partial f/\partial x_2 = 1$. The Lagrangian is $L = \tfrac12 m_1\dot x_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot x_2^2 + m_1 g x_1 + m_2 g x_2$.
step: The multiplier equations give $m_1\ddot x_1 - m_1 g = \lambda$ and $m_2\ddot x_2 - m_2 g = \lambda$.
step: Differentiate the constraint twice: $\ddot x_1 + \ddot x_2 = 0$. Writing $\ddot x_i = g + \lambda/m_i$ and adding gives $2g + \lambda\left(\frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}\right) = 0$.
step: Hence $\lambda = -\dfrac{2 m_1 m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$ and $\ddot x_1 = -\ddot x_2 = g\,\dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}$.
step: The constraint force on each mass is $\lambda$ along $+x$ (downward). It is negative, so it points up with magnitude $T = -\lambda = \dfrac{2 m_1 m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$. That is the rope tension.

@example
title: The rod force on a pendulum
problem: A bob of mass $m$ hangs from a massless rod of length $\ell$ in a vertical plane. Use polar coordinates $(r, \phi)$, with $\phi$ measured from the downward vertical, and the constraint $f = r - \ell = 0$ to find the force of the rod on the bob.
step: $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2) + m g r\cos\phi$, with $\partial f/\partial r = 1$ and $\partial f/\partial\phi = 0$.
step: The radial equation is $m\ddot r - m r\dot\phi^2 - m g\cos\phi = \lambda$. The angular equation has no multiplier: $\frac{d}{dt}(m r^2\dot\phi) + m g r\sin\phi = 0$.
step: Impose $r = \ell$ and $\ddot r = 0$. The angular equation becomes $\ddot\phi = -(g/\ell)\sin\phi$, and the radial equation gives $\lambda = -m\ell\dot\phi^2 - m g\cos\phi$.
step: $\lambda\,\partial f/\partial r$ is the force along $+\hat r$ (outward). Since $\lambda < 0$ for small angles, the rod pulls inward with $T = -\lambda = m\ell\dot\phi^2 + m g\cos\phi$. At the bottom, $\phi = 0$, this exceeds the weight by $m\ell\dot\phi^2$.

@mistakes
- Using the constraint $f = 0$ but forgetting its time derivatives: you need $\ddot f = 0$ to eliminate the accelerations from the equations that contain $\lambda$.
- Taking $\lambda$ itself as the force. The force is $\lambda\,\partial f/\partial q^i$, which is physical, while $\lambda$ alone has a sign and size that depend on how $f$ is normalized.
- Applying $\delta q$ arbitrary to all $n$ coordinates when only $n - m$ are free; with constraints the Euler–Lagrange expressions need not vanish individually.
- Treating a nonintegrable velocity constraint as if it could be substituted into the action before varying.

tol: 1%
unit: N

prompt: A bead of mass $m$ slides on a smooth wire in the vertical plane, $z = a x^2$ with $z$ up. Using $f = z - a x^2$ and $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot z^2) - m g z$, find $\ddot x$ in terms of $x$, $v = \dot x$, $a$ and $g$.
vars: x, v, a, g
lead: $\ddot x =$
answer: -2*a*x*(g + 2*a*v^2)/(1 + 4*a^2*x^2)
check: lambda x, v, a, g: lin([[1,0,2*a*x],[0,1,-1],[-2*a*x,1,0]],[0,-g,2*a*v**2])[0]
domain: x: 0.3..2; v: 0.3..2; a: 0.3..1.5; g: 5..10
hint: The equations are $m\ddot x = \lambda\,\partial f/\partial x$ and $m\ddot z + m g = \lambda$.
hint: Differentiate $z = a x^2$ twice: $\ddot z = 2a\dot x^2 + 2 a x\ddot x$.
hint: Substitute $\lambda$ from the second equation into the first and solve for $\ddot x$.

@practice self
prompt: A small particle of mass $m$ starts essentially at rest at the top of a smooth fixed sphere of radius $R$ in uniform gravity, and slides off. Use polar coordinates $(r, \theta)$ in the vertical plane, with $\theta$ measured from the upward vertical, and the multiplier $\lambda$ for $f = r - R = 0$. Write the equations of motion, find $\lambda(\theta)$, say what $\lambda$ means physically, and find where the particle leaves the sphere.
hint: Vary $r$ and $\theta$ separately; only the radial equation has $\lambda$ on its right side, because $\partial f/\partial\theta = 0$.
hint: After forming the equations, set $r = R$ and $\ddot r = 0$, and use conservation of $h$ to eliminate $\dot\theta^2$.
solution: $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\theta^2) - m g r\cos\theta$ and $\partial f/\partial r = 1$. The radial equation is $m\ddot r - m r\dot\theta^2 + m g\cos\theta = \lambda$ and the angular equation is $\frac{d}{dt}(m r^2\dot\theta) - m g r\sin\theta = 0$. Set $r = R$, $\ddot r = 0$: $\lambda = m g\cos\theta - m R\dot\theta^2$. Since $h = \tfrac12 m R^2\dot\theta^2 + m g R\cos\theta = m g R$ is conserved, $R\dot\theta^2 = 2g(1 - \cos\theta)$, so $\lambda = m g(3\cos\theta - 2)$. The constraint force is $\lambda\nabla f = \lambda\hat r$, so $\lambda$ is the outward normal force. A real surface can only push, so contact requires $\lambda \geq 0$. It ends at $\cos\theta = 2/3$, i.e. $\theta \approx 48.2^\circ$ from the top.
rubric: Writes $L$ and the radial equation with $\lambda$ on the right side (with $\partial f/\partial r = 1$).
rubric: Sets $r = R$ and $\ddot r = 0$ and eliminates $\dot\theta^2$ with energy conservation after forming the equations.
rubric: Obtains $\lambda = m g(3\cos\theta - 2)$ and identifies it as the outward normal force.
rubric: Finds $\cos\theta = 2/3$ and explains that the multiplier description holds only while $\lambda \geq 0$.

@quiz num
prompt: A block of mass $m_1 = 4.0\ \text{kg}$ lies on a smooth horizontal table. A massless rope attached to it passes over a fixed, frictionless, massless pulley at the table's edge and carries a hanging block of mass $m_2 = 1.0\ \text{kg}$. Let $x$ be the distance of the first block from the pulley along the table and $y$ the downward distance of the second block below the pulley, with $f = x + y - \ell_0 = 0$. Use the multiplier method with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and find the rope tension.
answer: 7.84
check: -lin([[4.0,0,-1],[0,1.0,-1],[1,1,0]],[0,1.0*9.8,0])[2]
tol: 1%
unit: N
hint: Write $L = \tfrac12 m_1\dot x^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot y^2 + m_2 g y$ and one equation per coordinate, each with $\lambda$ on the right.
hint: The constraint gives $\ddot x + \ddot y = 0$. The tension is $-\lambda$.
solution: $m_1\ddot x = \lambda$ and $m_2\ddot y - m_2 g = \lambda$ with $\ddot y = -\ddot x$. Then $-m_2\ddot x - m_2 g = m_1\ddot x$, so $\ddot x = -m_2 g/(m_1 + m_2)$ and $\lambda = -\dfrac{m_1 m_2 g}{m_1 + m_2}$. The tension is $T = -\lambda = \dfrac{4.0 \times 1.0 \times 9.8}{5.0} = 7.84\ \text{N}$.

@quiz ms
prompt: A system has ideal holonomic constraints $f_a(q, t) = 0$ and is described by the multiplier equations. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: The generalized constraint force is $Q_i = \lambda^a\,\partial f_a/\partial q^i$.
choice*: For admissible variations, the constraint forces do no virtual work.
choice: If a constraint depends explicitly on time, the constraint force still does no real work along the actual motion.
choice: The multiplier $\lambda^a$ is the same number whatever normalization is chosen for $f_a$.
choice*: If the constraints and $L$ have no explicit time dependence, $h$ is conserved.
hint: Compare virtual displacements (frozen time) with actual displacements.
hint: What happens to $\lambda$ if you replace $f$ by $2f$?
solution: The statement giving $Q_i = \lambda^a\,\partial f_a/\partial q^i$ and the statement that constraint forces do no virtual work restate the definition and the ideal-constraint property. Virtual work is computed at frozen time; with a moving constraint the actual displacement has a component along the force, so real work can be done. Rescaling $f \to 2f$ halves $\lambda$, so $\lambda$ depends on the normalization. With no explicit time dependence, $dh/dt = 0$ and constraint forces do no work.
