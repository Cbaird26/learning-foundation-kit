@article
The Lagrangian gives $n$ second-order equations in the $n$ coordinates $q^i$. The Hamiltonian formulation trades them for $2n$ first-order equations in $q^i$ and their conjugate momenta $p_i$. The point is not easier arithmetic: it is a geometry (phase space) in which conservation laws, transformations and perturbation theory become clean.

### The Legendre transform

Start with a Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q, t)$ and define $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q^i$. The Lagrangian is **regular** when the Hessian matrix
$$W_{ij} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot q^i\,\partial \dot q^j}$$
is invertible. Then $p_i = p_i(q, \dot q, t)$ can be solved for $\dot q^i = \dot q^i(q, p, t)$, and the **Hamiltonian** is
$$H(q, p, t) = p_i\,\dot q^i - L,$$
with the velocities eliminated in favor of the momenta. For $L = T - V$ with $T$ quadratic in the velocities and time-independent coordinates, $H = T + V$ expressed in $(q, p)$. In general $H$ is not the energy; $H$ and the energy function $h$ of L01 are the same function, written in different variables.

### Hamilton's equations

Take the differential of $H$. The terms in $d\dot q^i$ cancel because $p_i = \partial L/\partial\dot q^i$, leaving
$$dH = \dot q^i\,dp_i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}\,dq^i - \frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\,dt .$$
Compare with $dH = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}dq^i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}dp_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}dt$ and use the Euler–Lagrange equation $\dot p_i = \partial L/\partial q^i$:
$$\dot q^i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \dot p_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t} .$$
Along a solution, $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$, so $H$ is conserved when it has no explicit time dependence. With $z = (q^1, \dots, q^n, p_1, \dots, p_n)$ the equations read $\dot z = J\,\nabla_z H$, where
$$J = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & I \\ -I & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$
We use this sign convention throughout. For $H$ independent of time, phase-space trajectories never cross.

### A variational principle in phase space

Hamilton's equations are also the stationarity conditions of
$$S[q, p] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left( p_i\dot q^i - H(q, p, t)\right) dt$$
when $q$ and $p$ are varied independently and $\delta q$ vanishes at the endpoints ($\delta p$ need not). Varying $p$ gives $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$, and varying $q$, after an integration by parts, gives $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$.

### Conserved quantities and reduction

If $q^k$ does not appear in $H$, then $\dot p_k = 0$. A conserved momentum can be substituted into $H$ directly to reduce the problem to fewer variables. This is legitimate in the Hamiltonian, unlike the substitution into $L$ warned about in L01.

### Singular Lagrangians

If $\det W = 0$, the momenta are not independent functions of the velocities and the transform fails. Examples: $L$ linear in a velocity, or $L = \tfrac12(\dot x - \dot y)^2 - V$. Such systems carry constraints among the $(q, p)$ and need the treatment of L11.

### Charged particle

In SI units, $L = \tfrac12 m\dot{\mathbf r}^2 + q\,\dot{\mathbf r}\cdot\mathbf A - q\phi$ gives $\mathbf p = m\dot{\mathbf r} + q\mathbf A$ and
$$H = \frac{(\mathbf p - q\mathbf A)^2}{2m} + q\phi .$$
The canonical momentum $\mathbf p$ depends on the gauge, whereas $m\dot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf p - q\mathbf A$ does not.

@example
title: The central-force Hamiltonian
problem: A particle of mass $m$ in a plane has $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2) - V(r)$. Find $H$ and Hamilton's equations, and identify the conserved quantities.
step: $p_r = m\dot r$ and $p_\phi = m r^2\dot\phi$. The Hessian is $\text{diag}(m,\, m r^2)$, invertible for $r > 0$, so $L$ is regular.
step: Solve $\dot r = p_r/m$ and $\dot\phi = p_\phi/(m r^2)$, then $H = p_r\dot r + p_\phi\dot\phi - L = \dfrac{p_r^2}{2m} + \dfrac{p_\phi^2}{2 m r^2} + V(r)$.
step: Hamilton's equations: $\dot r = p_r/m$, $\ \dot\phi = p_\phi/(m r^2)$, $\ \dot p_r = \dfrac{p_\phi^2}{m r^3} - V'(r)$, $\ \dot p_\phi = 0$.
step: $\phi$ is cyclic, so $p_\phi$ is conserved. $H$ has no explicit time dependence, so $H$ is conserved too.
step: Fix $p_\phi = J$ in $H$. The $(r, p_r)$ motion has the one-degree-of-freedom Hamiltonian $\dfrac{p_r^2}{2m} + V(r) + \dfrac{J^2}{2 m r^2}$. This substitution is valid in $H$.

@example
title: A charged particle in a uniform magnetic field
problem: A particle of charge $q$ and mass $m$ moves in the plane in the field $\mathbf B = B\hat z$, with no electric field. Use the symmetric gauge $\mathbf A = \tfrac12 B(-y, x, 0)$. Find $H$ and show that Hamilton's equations give the Lorentz force.
step: $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2) + \tfrac{qB}{2}(x\dot y - y\dot x)$, so $p_x = m\dot x - \tfrac{qB}{2}y$ and $p_y = m\dot y + \tfrac{qB}{2}x$.
step: Invert: $m\dot x = p_x + \tfrac{qB}{2}y$ and $m\dot y = p_y - \tfrac{qB}{2}x$. Then $H = \dfrac{1}{2m}\left[\left(p_x + \tfrac{qB}{2}y\right)^2 + \left(p_y - \tfrac{qB}{2}x\right)^2\right]$, which equals $\tfrac12 m(\dot x^2 + \dot y^2)$.
step: $\dot p_x = -\partial H/\partial x = \tfrac{qB}{2}\dot y$. Substituting $p_x = m\dot x - \tfrac{qB}{2}y$ gives $m\ddot x = qB\,\dot y$.
step: Likewise $\dot p_y = -\partial H/\partial y = -\tfrac{qB}{2}\dot x$ gives $m\ddot y = -qB\,\dot x$. These are the components of $m\ddot{\mathbf r} = q\,\dot{\mathbf r}\times\mathbf B$; the speed is constant and the angular frequency is $|qB|/m$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to eliminate the velocities: $H$ must be a function of $(q, p, t)$, not of $\dot q$.
- Assuming $p_i = m\dot q^i$ always. With a magnetic vector potential or a velocity-dependent $L$, they differ.
- Substituting a conserved momentum into $L$ instead of $H$, or differentiating $H$ with respect to $q$ at fixed $\dot q$ rather than fixed $p$.
- Trying to Legendre-transform a Lagrangian whose Hessian is singular.

answer: 2

@practice self
prompt: Starting from $S[q, p] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2}\left(p_i\dot q^i - H(q, p, t)\right)dt$ with $q$ and $p$ varied independently and $\delta q(t_1) = \delta q(t_2) = 0$, derive Hamilton's equations. Then show that $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$ on solutions.
hint: Vary $q$ and $p$ independently and integrate the $p_i\,\delta\dot q^i$ term by parts.
hint: For the last part, apply the chain rule to $H(q, p, t)$ and insert Hamilton's equations.
solution: To first order, $\delta S = \int \left(\delta p_i\,\dot q^i + p_i\,\delta\dot q^i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}\delta q^i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\delta p_i\right)dt$. Integrate $p_i\,\delta\dot q^i$ by parts: $\left[p_i\delta q^i\right]_{t_1}^{t_2} - \int \dot p_i\,\delta q^i\,dt$, and the boundary term vanishes since $\delta q = 0$ at both ends. Then $\delta S = \int\left[\left(\dot q^i - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\right)\delta p_i - \left(\dot p_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}\right)\delta q^i\right]dt$. The variations are independent and arbitrary in the interior, so $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$ and $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$. Finally $\frac{dH}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial q^i}\dot q^i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\dot p_i + \frac{\partial H}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial t}$, since the first two terms cancel.
rubric: Writes the first variation of $S$ including the $p_i\,\delta\dot q^i$ term.
rubric: Integrates by parts and uses $\delta q = 0$ at the endpoints (no condition on $\delta p$).
rubric: Concludes both Hamilton equations from independent arbitrary $\delta p_i$ and $\delta q^i$ with the correct signs.
rubric: Obtains $dH/dt = \partial H/\partial t$ by the chain rule with cancellation of the first two terms.
