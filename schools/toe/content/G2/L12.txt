@article
Everything in L03 to L11 was written in coordinates $(q, p)$. The structure behind it, the one that survives every canonical transformation, is a geometric object: the **symplectic form**. This lesson gives a short, coordinate-free summary, and a fast way to count degrees of freedom.

### Definition and conventions

A **symplectic manifold** is a manifold $M$ with a 2-form $\omega$ that is
- **closed:** $d\omega = 0$, and
- **nondegenerate:** if $\omega(X, Y) = 0$ for all $Y$, then $X = 0$.

Nondegeneracy needs an antisymmetric matrix of full rank, which exists only in even dimension, so $\dim M = 2n$. **Darboux's theorem** says that near any point there are coordinates $(q^i, p_i)$ with
$$\omega = \sum_{i=1}^{n} dq^i \wedge dp_i .$$
In these coordinates the matrix of $\omega$ is the matrix $J$ of L03. We write $\iota_X\omega = \omega(X, \cdot)$.

### Hamiltonian vector fields

Given a function $H$, its **Hamiltonian vector field** $X_H$ is defined by
$$\iota_{X_H}\,\omega = dH .$$
In Darboux coordinates, $\omega(X, \cdot) = \dot q^i\,dp_i - \dot p_i\,dq^i$, so $\iota_X\omega = dH$ gives $\dot q^i = \partial H/\partial p_i$ and $\dot p_i = -\partial H/\partial q^i$: Hamilton's equations. The Poisson bracket is
$$\{F, G\} = \omega(X_F, X_G) = X_G(F) .$$
The Jacobi identity of L04 is equivalent to $d\omega = 0$.

### Flows preserve the form

Cartan's formula $\mathcal{L}_X = d\,\iota_X + \iota_X\,d$ gives
$$\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\,\omega = d(dH) + \iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = 0 .$$
So Hamiltonian flows are **symplectomorphisms**: $\phi^*\omega = \omega$. In coordinates, this is the condition $MJM^{T} = J$ of L05, so canonical transformations are symplectomorphisms. The top power $\omega^n/n!$ is the phase-space volume, and its invariance is Liouville's theorem.

### Potentials, areas and Lagrangian submanifolds

On $T^*Q$ the 1-form $\theta = p_i\,dq^i$ satisfies $\omega = -d\theta$. By Stokes' theorem, for a closed curve $\gamma$ bounding a surface $\Sigma$, $\oint_\gamma p\,dq$ equals the symplectic area $\int_\Sigma dq\wedge dp$ (up to orientation). That is why action integrals $\oint p\,dq$ over closed orbits, like those of L08, keep their values under the canonical transformations of L05 and L06.

An $n$-dimensional submanifold on which $\omega$ restricts to zero is a **Lagrangian submanifold**. Examples: the graph $p = \partial S/\partial q$ of a Hamilton–Jacobi solution (L07), and the invariant tori of an integrable system (L08).

### Symmetry, momentum maps, reduction

Suppose a Lie group $G$ acts on $M$ by symplectomorphisms generated by Hamiltonians. The collection of these generators is the **momentum map** $\mu: M \to \mathfrak{g}^*$. Translations give the linear momentum; rotations give the angular momentum $\mathbf L$. If $H$ is $G$-invariant, $\mu$ is conserved (Noether's theorem in symplectic form).

**Reduction.** Fix a value $\mu_0$ of the momentum map and divide the level set by the symmetry that preserves it:
$$M_{\mu_0} = \mu^{-1}(\mu_0)\big/ G_{\mu_0}, \qquad \dim M_{\mu_0} = \dim M - \dim G - \dim G_{\mu_0},$$
valid when $G_{\mu_0}$ acts freely. Here $G_{\mu_0}$ is the subgroup that leaves $\mu_0$ unchanged. For an abelian group, $G_{\mu_0} = G$ and the reduction removes $2\dim G$. The reduced space again carries a symplectic form, and the number of physical degrees of freedom is half its dimension.

### Constraints seen geometrically

A set of first-class constraints defines a *coisotropic* surface: $\omega$ restricted to it has null directions (the gauge orbits), and the quotient by these orbits is symplectic. A set of second-class constraints defines a symplectic submanifold, whose induced form leads to the Dirac bracket. Both statements reproduce the counting rule $n = N - N_1 - \tfrac12 N_2$ of L11.

@example
title: A symplectomorphism checked by pullback
problem: Show that $q = \sqrt{2P}\sin Q$, $p = \sqrt{2P}\cos Q$ preserves the symplectic form: $dq \wedge dp = dQ \wedge dP$.
step: Differentiate: $dq = \sqrt{2P}\cos Q\,dQ + \dfrac{\sin Q}{\sqrt{2P}}\,dP$.
step: Likewise $dp = -\sqrt{2P}\sin Q\,dQ + \dfrac{\cos Q}{\sqrt{2P}}\,dP$.
step: Use $dQ\wedge dQ = dP\wedge dP = 0$ and $dP\wedge dQ = -dQ\wedge dP$. The coefficient of $dQ\wedge dP$ in $dq\wedge dp$ is $\sqrt{2P}\cos Q\cdot\dfrac{\cos Q}{\sqrt{2P}} - \dfrac{\sin Q}{\sqrt{2P}}\cdot\left(-\sqrt{2P}\sin Q\right)$.
step: This equals $\cos^2 Q + \sin^2 Q = 1$, so $dq\wedge dp = dQ\wedge dP$. The map is a symplectomorphism: it is the oscillator map of L05 with $m\omega = 1$.

@example
title: Counting degrees of freedom of the two-body problem by reduction
problem: Two particles in three dimensions interact through $V(|\mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2|)$. Use reduction to find the number of degrees of freedom left after removing translations and rotations, for a nonzero angular momentum.
step: The phase space is $T^*(\mathbb R^3 \times \mathbb R^3)$ with $\dim M = 12$.
step: Translations form an abelian group of dimension $3$, with momentum map the total momentum $\mathbf P$. Reduction removes $2 \times 3 = 6$ dimensions, leaving the relative variables $(\mathbf r, \mathbf p)$ with $\dim = 6$.
step: Rotations $SO(3)$ have dimension $3$, with momentum map $\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$. For $\mathbf L \ne 0$ the stabilizer is $SO(2)$, the rotations about $\mathbf L$, of dimension $1$. Reduction removes $3 + 1 = 4$ dimensions, leaving $6 - 4 = 2$.
step: The reduced space has dimension $2$: one degree of freedom, with coordinates $(r, p_r)$ and Hamiltonian $\dfrac{p_r^2}{2\mu} + \dfrac{L^2}{2\mu r^2} + V(r)$, where $\mu$ is the reduced mass, as in L01 and L03.
step: Everything removed (center of mass motion, the orientation of the orbit plane, the angle around the orbit) is recovered afterwards by quadratures.

@mistakes
- Subtracting only $\dim G$ for each symmetry. A Hamiltonian symmetry fixes the momentum map value and also quotients by the group, so the count is $\dim G + \dim G_{\mu_0}$ in total.
- Using the abelian rule ($2\dim G$) for rotations. The stabilizer of a nonzero $\mathbf L$ is only $SO(2)$.
- Forgetting the sign and ordering conventions: with $\omega = dq\wedge dp$ and $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ the flow is $(\partial_p H, -\partial_q H)$. The opposite convention for $\omega$ flips the sign of $X_H$.
- Assuming the restriction of $\omega$ to every submanifold is nondegenerate. It is not, for instance on a Lagrangian submanifold or a first-class constraint surface.

@practice num
prompt: On the plane with coordinates $(q, p)$, take the symplectic form $\omega = (1 + q^2)\,dq\wedge dp$ and $H = \tfrac12 p^2 + \tfrac12 q^2$, with $X_H$ defined by $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ and $\iota_X\omega = \omega(X, \cdot)$. Find the $p$-component $\dot p$ of $X_H$ at $(q, p) = (1, 2)$.
answer: -0.5
check: -nderiv(lambda q: 0.5*2.0**2 + 0.5*q**2, 1.0)/(1 + 1.0**2)
tol: 0.005
hint: Write $X = \dot q\,\partial_q + \dot p\,\partial_p$, so $\omega(X, \cdot) = f\,(\dot q\,dp - \dot p\,dq)$ with $f = 1 + q^2$.
hint: Match the $dq$ and $dp$ coefficients with $dH = H_q\,dq + H_p\,dp$.
solution: $f(\dot q\,dp - \dot p\,dq) = H_q\,dq + H_p\,dp$ gives $\dot q = H_p/f$ and $\dot p = -H_q/f$. At $(1, 2)$: $f = 2$ and $H_q = q = 1$, so $\dot p = -1/2$. (Also $\dot q = 2/2 = 1$.)

@practice expr
prompt: In the plane, with $\omega = \sum_{i=x,y} dq^i\wedge dp_i$, a rotation about the origin acts on phase space by the vector field $\dot x = -y$, $\dot y = x$, $\dot p_x = -p_y$, $\dot p_y = p_x$. Find the function $\mu(x, y, p_x, p_y)$ with $\iota_X\omega = d\mu$ and $\mu(0) = 0$ (the momentum map). Type $p_x$ as `px` and $p_y$ as `py`.
vars: x, y, px, py
lead: $\mu =$
answer: x*py - y*px
check: lambda x, y, px, py: quad(lambda s: s*((-y)*px + x*py - (-py)*x - px*y), 0, 1)
hint: In Darboux coordinates $X = (\partial\mu/\partial p_x, \partial\mu/\partial p_y, -\partial\mu/\partial x, -\partial\mu/\partial y)$.
hint: Integrate $\partial\mu/\partial p_x = -y$ and $\partial\mu/\partial p_y = x$, then check the $q$-derivatives.
solution: $\partial\mu/\partial p_x = \dot x = -y$ and $\partial\mu/\partial p_y = \dot y = x$, so $\mu = x p_y - y p_x + g(x, y)$. Then $-\partial\mu/\partial x = -p_y - g_x$ must equal $\dot p_x = -p_y$, so $g_x = 0$, and likewise $g_y = 0$. With $\mu(0) = 0$: $\mu = x p_y - y p_x = L_z$, the angular momentum.

@practice mc
prompt: Why must a symplectic manifold have even dimension?
choice*: Nondegeneracy of an antisymmetric 2-form requires an antisymmetric matrix of full rank, and such matrices exist only in even dimensions.
choice: Closedness of $\omega$, $d\omega = 0$, forces an even number of variables.
choice: Hamiltonians must be quadratic in the momenta, which pairs each coordinate with a momentum.
choice: Liouville's theorem is false in odd dimensions.
hint: What is the determinant of an antisymmetric $k\times k$ matrix when $k$ is odd?
hint: $\det A = \det A^{T} = (-1)^k\det A$.
solution: For antisymmetric $A$, $\det A = \det(-A^{T}) = (-1)^k\det A$. For odd $k$ this gives $\det A = 0$, so $\omega$ is degenerate. Closedness does not constrain the parity, and Hamiltonians need not be quadratic.

For a state with nonzero total angular momentum, use reduction by the translation group and the rotation group $SO(3)$ (stabilizer $SO(2)$) to find the number of physical degrees of freedom that remain.
answer: 4
check: (2*9 - 2*3 - 3 - 1)/2

@practice self
prompt: Count the physical degrees of freedom carefully. For $N \ge 2$ particles in three dimensions with interactions that depend only on mutual distances, and a state with nonzero total angular momentum, count the degrees of freedom by symplectic reduction as a function of $N$. Check the result for $N = 3$ by an independent count in terms of shape variables.
hint: Start with $\dim M = 6N$ and subtract the contributions of the translation group (abelian) and of $SO(3)$ with stabilizer $SO(2)$.
hint: For the independent check, count shape variables as $3(N-1) - 3$, and note that the reduced space also contains the sphere of possible body-frame angular momenta.
solution: $\dim M = 6N$. Translations (abelian, $\dim 3$) remove $6$, leaving $6N - 6$. Rotations remove $\dim SO(3) + \dim SO(2) = 3 + 1 = 4$, leaving $6N - 10$. Hence $n = 3N - 5$ degrees of freedom. For $N = 2$ this is $1$ (the radial motion of L01), and for $N = 3$ it is $4$. Independent check: after removing the center of mass there are $3(N-1)$ relative coordinates. Removing the three orientation angles leaves $3(N-1) - 3 = 3N - 6$ shape variables (for non-collinear configurations), which for $N = 3$ are the three side lengths of the triangle. The reduced phase space contains the cotangent space of the shape space, of dimension $2(3N - 6)$, together with a two-dimensional sphere of directions of the angular momentum as seen in the body frame, so the total is $2(3N - 6) + 2 = 6N - 10$, in agreement. For $N = 3$ this gives $6 + 2 = 8$ dimensions, i.e. $4$ degrees of freedom.

answer: 8/3
check: quad(lambda q: quad(lambda p: 1 + q**2, 0, 2), 0, 1)

hint: The area is a double integral of the coefficient of $dq\wedge dp$ over the rectangle.
hint: Integrate $1 + q^2$ over $q$ from $0$ to $1$, then multiply by the $p$ range.
solution: $\displaystyle\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^2 (1 + q^2)\,dp\,dq = 2\int_0^1(1 + q^2)\,dq = 2\left(1 + \tfrac13\right) = \tfrac83 \approx 2.667$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about symplectic geometry are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Hamiltonian flows preserve $\omega$, because $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d(dH) = 0$.
choice*: With $\iota_{X_H}\omega = dH$ and $\omega = dq\wedge dp$, the bracket is $\{F, G\} = \omega(X_F, X_G)$.
choice*: A first-class constraint surface carries a form that is degenerate along the gauge orbits.
choice: A symplectic form can exist on a manifold of odd dimension.
choice: The restriction of $\omega$ to any submanifold is nondegenerate.
hint: Lagrangian submanifolds are a counterexample to the statement about restricting $\omega$ to a submanifold.
hint: Recall why antisymmetric matrices of odd size are singular.
solution: The statements about $\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0$, about $\{F, G\} = \omega(X_F, X_G)$ and about a first-class constraint surface are the statements of this lesson.
