@article
Few mechanical problems are exactly integrable. Perturbation theory starts from one that is, and adds a small correction in a controlled way. Action-angle variables make this especially clean.

### Setup

Suppose, in action-angle variables $(\theta, J)$ of L08,
$$H(\theta, J) = H_0(J) + \epsilon\,H_1(\theta, J), \qquad \epsilon \ll 1 .$$
Here $H_0$ is the integrable part, with frequencies $\omega_0(J) = \partial H_0/\partial J$, and $H_1$ is $2\pi$-periodic in each angle. The **angle average** is
$$\langle f \rangle(J) = \frac{1}{(2\pi)^n}\oint f(\theta, J)\, d^n\theta .$$

### First-order theory

Look for a canonical transformation $(\theta, J) \to (\theta', J')$ with the type-2 function $F_2 = \theta\cdot J' + \epsilon\,\chi(\theta, J')$. Then
$$J = J' + \epsilon\,\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial\theta}, \qquad \theta' = \theta + \epsilon\,\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial J'} .$$
Since $F_2$ does not depend on time, $K = H$. Expanding to first order in $\epsilon$,
$$K = H_0(J') + \epsilon\left[\,\omega_0(J')\cdot\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial\theta} + H_1(\theta', J')\,\right] + O(\epsilon^2).$$
Choose $\chi$ so that the bracket has no angle dependence:
$$\omega_0\cdot\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial\theta} = -\left( H_1 - \langle H_1\rangle \right).$$
Then
$$K = H_0(J') + \epsilon\,\langle H_1\rangle(J') + O(\epsilon^2), \qquad \omega'(J') = \omega_0(J') + \epsilon\,\frac{\partial\langle H_1\rangle}{\partial J'} .$$
The new actions $J'$ are constant to this order, and the motion is a uniform rotation of $\theta'$ with the corrected frequency. The old action $J = J' + \epsilon\,\partial_\theta\chi$ oscillates by $O(\epsilon)$ around $J'$.

### Solving for $\chi$ and small denominators

Expand in Fourier series, $H_1 = \sum_{\mathbf k} h_{\mathbf k}(J)\,e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\theta}$. Then
$$\chi = \sum_{\mathbf k \ne 0} \frac{i\,h_{\mathbf k}}{\mathbf k\cdot\omega_0}\, e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\theta} .$$
For one degree of freedom, $\mathbf k\cdot\omega_0 = k\,\omega_0 \ne 0$, so $\chi$ always exists. For $n \ge 2$, the denominators $\mathbf k\cdot\omega_0$ can be arbitrarily small, and they vanish exactly on **resonances**. There the expansion fails, and resonances are the seed of chaos in nearly integrable systems (L10).

### The anharmonic oscillator

Take $H_0 = \omega J$ and perturb with $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}q^4$, where $q = \sqrt{2J/(m\omega)}\sin\theta$. The average of $\sin^4\theta$ is $3/8$, which gives
$$\langle H_1\rangle = \frac{3\lambda J^2}{8 m^2\omega^2}, \qquad \omega' = \omega + \frac{3\lambda J}{4 m^2\omega^2}.$$
For amplitude $A$ (at lowest order $J \approx \tfrac12 m\omega A^2$) this is $\omega' = \omega + 3\lambda A^2/(8m\omega)$. A perturbation odd in $q$, such as $q^3$, has a vanishing first-order average because $\langle \sin^3\theta\rangle = 0$; its effect appears at second order.

### Adiabatic invariance

Let $H(q, p; a(t))$ depend on a parameter $a$ that changes slowly, over many periods. Then $J$ is conserved to a very good approximation while $E$ changes. For an oscillator, $J = E/\omega$, so $E \propto \omega$ when $\omega$ changes slowly. For a pendulum whose string is shortened slowly, $\omega = \sqrt{g/\ell}$ increases, and the energy of small oscillations grows in proportion.

### Recipe

1. Write $H$ in action-angle variables of the unperturbed part.
2. Compute $\langle H_1\rangle$ with an angle average (often from Fourier expansions).
3. Read the energy $K(J')$ and the frequency $\omega' = \partial K/\partial J'$.
4. Convert $J'$ to a physical amplitude only at the end, at the order you need.

@example
title: First-order frequency shift of a quartic oscillator
problem: Treating $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}q^4$ as a perturbation of $H_0 = \omega J$, find $K(J')$ and the frequency to first order in $\lambda$.
step: With $q = \sqrt{2J/(m\omega)}\,\sin\theta$, we get $q^4 = \dfrac{4J^2}{m^2\omega^2}\sin^4\theta$, so $\tfrac{\lambda}{4}q^4 = \dfrac{\lambda J^2}{m^2\omega^2}\sin^4\theta$.
step: Use $\sin^4\theta = \tfrac38 - \tfrac12\cos 2\theta + \tfrac18\cos 4\theta$. Its angle average is $\langle\sin^4\theta\rangle = 3/8$.
step: Hence $\langle H_1\rangle = \dfrac{3\lambda J^2}{8m^2\omega^2}$ and $K(J') = \omega J' + \dfrac{3\lambda J'^2}{8m^2\omega^2}$.
step: Differentiate: $\omega' = \dfrac{\partial K}{\partial J'} = \omega + \dfrac{3\lambda J'}{4m^2\omega^2}$.
step: With $J' \approx \tfrac12 m\omega A^2$ this is $\omega' = \omega + \dfrac{3\lambda A^2}{8m\omega}$. For $\lambda > 0$ the oscillator hardens: the frequency rises with amplitude.

@example
title: The generating function and the oscillating action
problem: For the same system, find $\chi$ and show how the old action $J$ oscillates around $J'$.
step: Write $c = \lambda J'^2/(m^2\omega^2)$, so $H_1 = c\left(\tfrac38 - \tfrac12\cos 2\theta + \tfrac18\cos 4\theta\right)$ with $\epsilon$ absorbed in $\lambda$.
step: The condition $\omega\,\partial_\theta\chi = -(H_1 - \langle H_1\rangle)$ becomes $\omega\,\partial_\theta\chi = c\left(\tfrac12\cos 2\theta - \tfrac18\cos 4\theta\right)$.
step: Integrate in $\theta$: $\chi = \dfrac{c}{\omega}\left(\tfrac14\sin 2\theta - \tfrac{1}{32}\sin 4\theta\right)$.
step: Then $J = J' + \partial_\theta\chi = J' + \dfrac{c}{\omega}\left(\tfrac12\cos 2\theta - \tfrac18\cos 4\theta\right)$. The old action oscillates at twice and four times the angle frequency, with relative size of order $\lambda J'/(m^2\omega^3)$.
step: Only the angle-independent part survives in $K$, which is why $J'$ is constant and the first-order frequency is the one found above.

@mistakes
- Using $\langle H_1\rangle$ at fixed $q$ or fixed energy, instead of at fixed *action*. The average is over the angle at fixed $J'$.
- Dropping the dependence of $\langle H_1\rangle$ on $J'$ and then claiming the frequency does not shift. The shift is $\partial\langle H_1\rangle/\partial J'$.
- Applying the one-degree-of-freedom formula for $\chi$ when $\mathbf k\cdot\omega_0 = 0$ for some $\mathbf k \ne 0$ in higher dimensions.
- Interpreting $E \propto \omega$ for adiabatic change as an exact law; it holds only when the change is slow compared with the period.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle of mass $m = 0.50\ \text{kg}$ bounces elastically between two walls $1.0\ \text{m}$ apart with energy $E = 2.0\ \text{J}$. One wall is moved outward so slowly that the action stays constant, until the walls are $2.5\ \text{m}$ apart. Find the final energy of the particle.
answer: 0.32
check: (lambda J: (pi*J/2.5)**2/(2*0.5))(1.0*sqrt(2*0.5*2.0)/pi)
tol: 1%
unit: J
hint: For slow changes the action $J = \frac{1}{2\pi}\oint p\,dq$ is conserved.
hint: For a particle between walls a distance $a$ apart, $J = a\sqrt{2mE}/\pi$, so $a\sqrt{E}$ stays constant.
solution: $a\sqrt{E}$ is constant, so $E_f = E_i\,(a_i/a_f)^2 = 2.0\times(1.0/2.5)^2 = 0.32\ \text{J}$. The particle loses energy because it does work on the receding wall.
