@article
L09 asked what a *small* perturbation does to an integrable system. Nonlinear dynamics asks the global questions: where is the system at rest, which rests are stable, and what happens when the perturbation is no longer negligible.

### Equilibria and linearization

An **equilibrium** of $\dot z = J\nabla H$ is a point $z_*$ with $\nabla H(z_*) = 0$. For $H = p^2/(2m) + V(q)$ this means $p = 0$ and $V'(q_*) = 0$. Writing $z = z_* + \xi$, the linearized flow is
$$\dot\xi = A\,\xi, \qquad A = J\,\nabla^2 H(z_*),$$
where $\nabla^2 H$ is the Hessian. For one degree of freedom, $\operatorname{tr}A = 0$ and $\lambda^2 = -\det\nabla^2 H$, so:
- $\det\nabla^2 H > 0$: eigenvalues $\pm i\omega$, a **center** (elliptic point) with small-oscillation frequency $\omega$.
- $\det\nabla^2 H < 0$: eigenvalues $\pm\kappa$ real, a **saddle** (hyperbolic point), unstable.

For $H = p^2/(2m) + V(q)$, $\omega^2 = V''(q_*)/m$ at a minimum. The eigenvalues of a Hamiltonian system come in pairs $\pm\lambda$, so there are no asymptotically stable equilibria (volume preservation forbids attractors). A strict minimum of $H$ is always stable. Beyond this, a purely imaginary spectrum is only the first step, because nonlinear terms can matter.

### Phase portraits and bifurcations

Level curves of $H$ are the orbits in one degree of freedom. The **separatrix** is the level curve through a saddle: it separates oscillation (libration) from rotation, and the period of motion diverges logarithmically as the energy approaches the separatrix. For the pendulum with $H = p^2/(2m\ell^2) - m g\ell\cos\theta$, the separatrix energy is $E = m g\ell$.

When a parameter changes, equilibria can appear, disappear or exchange stability: a **bifurcation**. In a *saddle-node* bifurcation a center and a saddle merge and vanish. In a *pitchfork* bifurcation a center becomes a saddle and spawns a pair of new centers. The bead on a rotating hoop shows the second kind.

### Poincaré maps

For a periodically driven system or a system with two degrees of freedom at fixed energy, strobing the flow gives a map of a plane to itself. Canonical flow makes this map **area preserving**: its Jacobian has $\det M = 1$. A fixed point of such a map is elliptic if $|\operatorname{tr}M| < 2$ (eigenvalues $e^{\pm i\psi}$ with $\cos\psi = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M$), and hyperbolic if $|\operatorname{tr}M| > 2$.

The **standard map** (kicked rotor $H = \tfrac12 p^2 + K\cos\theta\sum_n\delta(t - n)$) is
$$p' = p + K\sin\theta, \qquad \theta' = \theta + p' \pmod{2\pi}.$$
It is integrable at $K = 0$ and becomes progressively chaotic as $K$ grows.

### Perturbed integrable systems

Start with an integrable system with a torus of frequency ratio $\omega_1/\omega_2$ and add a small perturbation.
- **KAM theorem.** If $H_0$ is nondegenerate, the perturbation is small and smooth, and the frequency ratio is sufficiently irrational (Diophantine), the torus survives, only deformed. Most tori survive for small perturbations.
- **Resonant tori** (rational ratio) break up. They are replaced by a chain of stable and unstable periodic orbits (Poincaré–Birkhoff), surrounded by islands.
- Near the unstable orbits and separatrices, a thin **chaotic layer** appears, with sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
- When neighboring resonances widen enough to overlap (**Chirikov criterion**), the layers merge and chaos spreads. For the standard map this happens near $K \approx 1$.

### Recipe

1. Solve $\nabla H = 0$ for the equilibria.
2. Evaluate the Hessian and the eigenvalues $\lambda$ (or $\det\nabla^2 H$ for one degree of freedom).
3. For small perturbations, shift equilibria with the implicit-function theorem: $\delta q = -\epsilon V_1'(q_*)/V_0''(q_*)$.
4. Track bifurcations where $\det\nabla^2 H \to 0$.

@example
title: Equilibria and a bifurcation of the rotating hoop
problem: A bead of mass $m$ on a smooth hoop of radius $R$ rotating about a vertical diameter at constant $\Omega$ has $H = \dfrac{p^2}{2mR^2} + U(\theta)$ with $U(\theta) = -\tfrac12 m R^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta - m g R\cos\theta$. Find the equilibria, classify them and locate the bifurcation.
step: Equilibria need $\dot\theta = p/(mR^2) = 0$ and $U'(\theta) = m R\sin\theta\,(g - R\Omega^2\cos\theta) = 0$. The solutions are $\theta = 0$, $\theta = \pi$, and, if $R\Omega^2 > g$, also $\cos\theta_0 = g/(R\Omega^2)$.
step: The second derivative is $U'' = m R\left[\cos\theta\,(g - R\Omega^2\cos\theta) + R\Omega^2\sin^2\theta\right]$.
step: At $\theta = \pi$: $U'' = -mR(g + R\Omega^2) < 0$, so it is a saddle for every $\Omega$ (the hoop's top).
step: At $\theta = 0$: $U'' = mR(g - R\Omega^2)$, so it is a center for $\Omega^2 < g/R$, with $\omega^2 = g/R - \Omega^2$, and a saddle for $\Omega^2 > g/R$.
step: At $\theta_0$ the first term vanishes: $U'' = m R^2\Omega^2\sin^2\theta_0 > 0$, a center with $\omega^2 = \Omega^2 - g^2/(R^2\Omega^2)$. At $\Omega_c = \sqrt{g/R}$ the center at $\theta = 0$ becomes a saddle while two centers $\pm\theta_0$ appear: a pitchfork bifurcation.

@example
title: A fixed point of the standard map
problem: For the standard map with $K = 1.0$, classify the fixed point $(\theta, p) = (\pi, 0)$ and find the rotation angle of nearby orbits.
step: A fixed point needs $p' = p$, so $K\sin\theta = 0$, and $\theta' = \theta + p'$ requires $p' = 0 \pmod{2\pi}$. At $(\pi, 0)$: $\sin\pi = 0$ and $p = 0$, so it is fixed.
step: The Jacobian of $(\theta, p) \to (\theta', p')$ is $M = \begin{pmatrix} 1 + K\cos\theta & 1 \\ K\cos\theta & 1\end{pmatrix}$, with $\det M = 1$ (area preserving).
step: At $\theta = \pi$: $\operatorname{tr}M = 2 + K\cos\pi = 2 - K = 1.0$.
step: Since $|\operatorname{tr}M| < 2$, the point is elliptic. Nearby orbits rotate by $\psi$ per iteration with $\cos\psi = \tfrac12\operatorname{tr}M = 0.5$, so $\psi = \pi/3$.
step: This fixed point stays elliptic for $0 < K < 4$. For $K > 4$, $|2 - K| > 2$ and it becomes hyperbolic (with reflection).

@mistakes
- Calling an equilibrium stable because $H$ is conserved. Conservation of $H$ alone does not stop orbits from leaving a saddle.
- Looking for eigenvalues with negative real parts. In a Hamiltonian system there are none without a mirror image with positive real part.
- Mistaking the sign of the Hessian condition: a *maximum* of $V$ gives a saddle of $H$, a minimum a center.
- Assuming KAM tori all survive. Only sufficiently irrational ones do, and the resonant ones break up.

hint: Differentiate $V$ twice and evaluate at $q_* = 1$.

choice*: Real eigenvalues $\pm\sqrt{-V''(q_*)/m}$: a saddle, which is unstable.
choice: Imaginary eigenvalues: a center, which is stable.
choice: Eigenvalues with negative real part: a stable node, since energy is conserved.
choice: A marginal case that linearization cannot decide.
hint: Write the linearized equation $m\,\delta\ddot q = -V''(q_*)\,\delta q$.
hint: A negative $V''$ turns the oscillator equation into one with exponential solutions.
solution: $\delta\ddot q = \kappa^2\,\delta q$ with $\kappa^2 = -V''(q_*)/m > 0$, so $\delta q \sim e^{\pm\kappa t}$. This is a saddle. Hamiltonian systems cannot have stable nodes or spirals, and the case $V'' = 0$, not $V'' < 0$, is the marginal one.

@practice self
prompt: Locate equilibria and assess a small perturbation. A particle with $m = 1$ has $H = \dfrac{p^2}{2} + \tfrac14 q^4 - \tfrac12 q^2 - \epsilon q$. (a) For $\epsilon = 0$, find and classify the equilibria. (b) To first order in $\epsilon$, find how each equilibrium moves and how the small-oscillation frequency about the equilibrium near $q = 1$ changes. (c) Find the value of $|\epsilon|$ at which two equilibria disappear.
hint: Equilibria satisfy $q^3 - q = \epsilon$. Differentiate implicitly to get $\delta q$ to first order.
hint: For (c), the equilibria merge where $V'(q) = \epsilon$ is tangent to the extrema of $q^3 - q$.
solution: (a) For $\epsilon = 0$, $V' = q^3 - q = 0$ gives $q_* = 0, \pm1$ (with $p = 0$). $V'' = 3q^2 - 1$: at $q = \pm1$, $V'' = 2 > 0$, centers with $\omega = \sqrt2$; at $q = 0$, $V'' = -1 < 0$, a saddle. (b) The equilibrium condition is $q^3 - q = \epsilon$. Differentiating, $(3q_*^2 - 1)\,\delta q = \epsilon$, so $\delta q = \epsilon/V''(q_*)$: $\delta q = \epsilon/2$ at $q_* = \pm1$ and $\delta q = -\epsilon$ at $q_* = 0$. The frequency about $q_* \approx 1$ is $\omega^2 = V''(q_*+\delta q) = 3(1 + \epsilon/2)^2 - 1 \approx 2 + 3\epsilon$, so $\omega \approx \sqrt2\,(1 + 3\epsilon/4)$. (c) The function $q^3 - q$ has local extrema $\mp\dfrac{2}{3\sqrt3}$ at $q = \pm 1/\sqrt3$. For $|\epsilon| < \dfrac{2}{3\sqrt3} \approx 0.385$ there are three real equilibria. At $|\epsilon| = 2/(3\sqrt3)$, a center and a saddle merge, and beyond it only one equilibrium (a center) remains: a saddle-node bifurcation.

hint: Compute the Jacobian at $\theta = 0$, its trace and its determinant.
hint: Because $\det M = 1$, the eigenvalues satisfy $\lambda^2 - (\operatorname{tr}M)\,\lambda + 1 = 0$.
