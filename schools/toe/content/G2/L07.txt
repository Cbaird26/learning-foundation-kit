@article
L06 showed that a canonical map can be built from one function. The most ambitious choice is a map to variables in which *nothing happens*: $K = 0$, so every new coordinate and momentum is constant. Then solving the dynamics reduces to finding that generating function.

### The Hamilton–Jacobi equation

Use a type-2 function $F_2 = S(q, P, t)$. From L06, $p_i = \partial S/\partial q^i$, $Q^i = \partial S/\partial P_i$ and $K = H + \partial S/\partial t$. Requiring $K = 0$ gives the **Hamilton–Jacobi equation**
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + H\!\left(q, \frac{\partial S}{\partial q}, t\right) = 0 .$$
This is a first-order partial differential equation for $S(q, t)$ in $n + 1$ variables. It is nonlinear in $\partial S/\partial q$ whenever $H$ is quadratic in the momenta.

### Complete integrals and the motion

A **complete integral** $S(q, \alpha, t)$ depends on $n$ independent constants $\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n$, in addition to an irrelevant additive constant. Take the new momenta to be $P_i = \alpha_i$. Since $K = 0$, the new coordinates are also constants, which we call $\beta^i$:
$$p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i}, \qquad \beta^i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial \alpha_i} .$$
The second set is $n$ algebraic equations that you solve for $q^i(t; \alpha, \beta)$. The $2n$ constants are fixed by the initial conditions, and then $p_i(t)$ follows from the first set.

### Meaning of $S$

Along the actual motion,
$$\frac{dS}{dt} = \frac{\partial S}{\partial q^i}\dot q^i + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t} = p_i\dot q^i - H = L,$$
so $S = \int L\,dt$ is the action evaluated along the true paths. It is called **Hamilton's principal function**.

### Time-independent Hamiltonians

If $H$ has no explicit time dependence, separate the time: $S = W(q, \alpha) - E t$, where $E$ is the energy. This gives the **time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation** for the characteristic function $W$:
$$H\!\left(q, \frac{\partial W}{\partial q}\right) = E .$$
Choose $\alpha_1 = E$ (the other $\alpha_k$ are further constants). Then
$$p_i = \frac{\partial W}{\partial q^i}, \qquad \frac{\partial W}{\partial E} = t + \beta^1, \qquad \frac{\partial W}{\partial \alpha_k} = \beta^k \ (k \ge 2) .$$

### One dimension in general

For $H = p^2/(2m) + V(q)$, $\partial W/\partial q = \pm\sqrt{2m(E - V(q))}$, and the second relation is a quadrature for the motion:
$$t + \beta = \int \frac{m\,dq}{\sqrt{2m\,(E - V(q))}} .$$

### Separation of variables

If, in some coordinates, $H$ allows the ansatz $W = \sum_i W_i(q^i; \alpha)$, then the equation splits into $n$ ordinary differential equations, one per coordinate, linked by separation constants. For instance, for a particle in a plane with $H = \dfrac{1}{2m}\left(p_r^2 + \dfrac{p_\phi^2}{r^2}\right) + V(r)$, the angle $\phi$ is cyclic and $W = \alpha_\phi\,\phi + W_r(r)$. Whether $H$ separates depends on the coordinate system; there is no general recipe.

### Cautions

- The square roots carry signs. Track which branch applies on each leg of the motion, and where the argument vanishes (turning points).
- There are infinitely many complete integrals. Any one will do, and different choices give different canonical constants.
- $S$ and $W$ are determined only up to additive constants, which drop out of $p$ and $\beta$.
- The method solves the problem exactly only when you can find a complete integral, which is why integrable systems (L08) are central.

@example
title: The oscillator by Hamilton–Jacobi
problem: Solve $H = \dfrac{p^2}{2m} + \tfrac12 m\omega^2 q^2$ by the time-independent Hamilton–Jacobi equation.
step: With $p = W'(q)$ and $H = E$: $W'(q) = \sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2 q^2}$ (on the branch with $p > 0$).
step: The conjugate constant satisfies $t + \beta = \dfrac{\partial W}{\partial E} = \displaystyle\int \frac{m\,dq}{\sqrt{2mE - m^2\omega^2 q^2}}$.
step: Substitute $q = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\sin u$. Then $dq = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\cos u\,du$ and the radical is $\sqrt{2mE}\cos u$, so the integrand reduces to $du/\omega$.
step: Hence $\omega(t + \beta) = u$, that is, $q(t) = \sqrt{\dfrac{2E}{m\omega^2}}\,\sin\omega(t + \beta)$.
step: Then $p = m\dot q = \sqrt{2mE}\cos\omega(t + \beta)$, which agrees with $W'(q)$. The constants $E$ and $\beta$ are fixed by the initial conditions.

@example
title: Projectile motion by separation of variables
problem: A particle of mass $m$ moves in the $(x, z)$ plane with $H = \dfrac{p_x^2 + p_z^2}{2m} + m g z$. Find the motion by separating variables.
step: $x$ is cyclic, so $W = \alpha_x\,x + W_z(z)$ with $\alpha_x = p_x$ constant. The equation $H = E$ gives $W_z'(z) = \sqrt{2m(E - m g z) - \alpha_x^2}$.
step: Integrate: $W_z = -\dfrac{1}{3m^2 g}\left[2m(E - m g z) - \alpha_x^2\right]^{3/2}$, and note that $W_z' = p_z$.
step: Differentiate with respect to $E$: $\dfrac{\partial W}{\partial E} = -\dfrac{p_z}{m g} = t + \beta_E$. So $p_z = -m g\,(t + \beta_E)$.
step: Differentiate with respect to $\alpha_x$: $\dfrac{\partial W}{\partial\alpha_x} = x + \dfrac{\alpha_x p_z}{m^2 g} = \beta_x$. Insert $p_z$ to get $x = \beta_x + \dfrac{\alpha_x}{m}(t + \beta_E)$, uniform horizontal motion.
step: Finally $z$ follows from $E$: $m g z = E - \dfrac{\alpha_x^2 + p_z^2}{2m}$. With $p_z$ linear in $t$ this is a parabola in time, as expected.

@mistakes
- Writing $\partial S/\partial t - H = 0$. The sign is $\partial S/\partial t + H = 0$, so that $\partial S/\partial t = -H$.
- Treating the separation constant $E$ as an extra constant on top of the $n$ constants $\alpha_i$. For time-independent $H$ one of the $\alpha_i$ is $E$ itself.
- Forgetting that the new coordinates $\beta^i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i$ are constants *and* must be solved for $q(t)$. Stopping at $p = \partial W/\partial q$ gives only the momentum as a function of position.
- Dropping the sign choice of the square root, so that turning points are mishandled.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about the Hamilton–Jacobi method are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: For a time-independent $H$ one may take $S = W(q, \alpha) - E t$.
choice*: Along the actual motion, $dS/dt = L$.
choice*: On a complete integral, the momenta are $p_i = \partial S/\partial q^i$.
choice: The Hamilton–Jacobi equation is linear in $S$.
choice: A complete integral needs only one arbitrary constant besides the additive one, whatever the number of degrees of freedom.
hint: Count the constants needed for $2n$ initial conditions.
hint: For $H = p^2/2m + V$, how does $(\partial S/\partial q)$ enter the equation?
solution: The statements about $S = W - Et$, $dS/dt = L$ and $p_i = \partial S/\partial q^i$ are standard. The equation contains $(\partial S/\partial q)^2$ for a quadratic kinetic term, so it is nonlinear. A complete integral needs $n$ independent constants $\alpha_i$; together with the $n$ constants $\beta^i = \partial S/\partial\alpha_i$ they supply the $2n$ initial conditions.
