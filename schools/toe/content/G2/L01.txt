@article
Newton's laws give one vector equation per particle. The Lagrangian formulation replaces them with a single scalar function and one variational principle. This lesson fixes the conventions used for the whole course.

### Setup and conventions

Describe the system by **generalized coordinates** $q = (q^1, \dots, q^n)$ and velocities $\dot q^i = dq^i/dt$. A repeated index is summed. For the systems of this lesson, in an inertial frame, the **Lagrangian** is $L(q, \dot q, t) = T - V$, with $T$ the kinetic energy and $V(q)$ the potential energy. The **action** of a path $q(t)$ between times $t_1$ and $t_2$ is

$$S[q] = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} L(q, \dot q, t)\, dt .$$

$S$ is a **functional**: it assigns one number to a whole path.

### Hamilton's principle

Fix the endpoints $q(t_1) = q_1$ and $q(t_2) = q_2$. **Hamilton's principle** says the physical path makes $S$ *stationary*: $\delta S = 0$ for every variation $q \to q + \delta q$ with $\delta q(t_1) = \delta q(t_2) = 0$. Stationary does not mean minimal; over a long enough time interval the physical path can be a saddle of $S$ rather than a minimum.

Expand to first order, use $\delta \dot q^i = \frac{d}{dt}\delta q^i$, and integrate the second term by parts:

$$\delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} \right) \delta q^i \, dt + \left[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i}\, \delta q^i \right]_{t_1}^{t_2} .$$

The boundary term vanishes because the endpoints are fixed. If the $q^i$ are **independent** coordinates, each $\delta q^i(t)$ is an arbitrary function, and the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations forces each bracket to vanish. The result is the **Euler–Lagrange equations**

$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i} = 0, \qquad i = 1, \dots, n .$$

The total derivative $d/dt$ acts on $q$, $\dot q$ and any explicit $t$ inside $\partial L / \partial \dot q^i$. There is one second-order equation per coordinate.

### Momenta and conservation

The **canonical momentum** conjugate to $q^i$ is $p_i = \partial L / \partial \dot q^i$. If $L$ does not contain $q^i$ (a **cyclic** coordinate), the Euler–Lagrange equation reads $\dot p_i = 0$, so $p_i$ is conserved. This is the simplest instance of Noether's theorem.

The **energy function** is

$$h = \dot q^i \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q^i} - L, \qquad \frac{dh}{dt} = -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t} \ \text{ on solutions.}$$

So $h$ is conserved when $L$ has no explicit time dependence. If the relation between Cartesian and generalized coordinates does not depend on time, $T$ is quadratic in the velocities and $h = T + V$. If a coordinate is tied to a prescribed motion (a driven support), $h$ need not equal $T + V$, and need not be conserved.

### Non-uniqueness of $L$

Adding a total derivative changes $S$ only by $F(q_2, t_2) - F(q_1, t_1)$, which is the same for every path with these endpoints, so

$$L' = L + \frac{d}{dt}F(q, t)$$

gives the same Euler–Lagrange equations. So does multiplying $L$ by a nonzero constant. Different Lagrangians can describe the same motion.

### A working routine

1. Choose independent coordinates; build any constraint in *before* varying.
2. Write $T$ and $V$ in these coordinates and form $L$.
3. Compute $p_i$, then $\dot p_i$ and $\partial L / \partial q^i$, and equate.
4. Look for cyclic coordinates and for explicit time dependence.

@example
title: A cyclic coordinate for a central force
problem: A particle of mass $m$ moves in a plane under a potential $V(r)$. Use polar coordinates $(r, \phi)$ to find the equations of motion and a conserved quantity.
step: With $\dot x^2 + \dot y^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2 \dot\phi^2$, the Lagrangian is $L = \tfrac12 m (\dot r^2 + r^2 \dot\phi^2) - V(r)$.
step: $\phi$ does not appear in $L$, so it is cyclic. Hence $p_\phi = \partial L / \partial \dot\phi = m r^2 \dot\phi \equiv J$ is conserved. This is the angular momentum about the origin.
step: The radial equation is $\frac{d}{dt}(m \dot r) - m r \dot\phi^2 + V'(r) = 0$, that is, $m \ddot r = m r \dot\phi^2 - V'(r)$.
step: Only now eliminate $\dot\phi = J / (m r^2)$: $m \ddot r = \dfrac{J^2}{m r^3} - V'(r) = -\dfrac{d}{dr}\!\left( V + \dfrac{J^2}{2 m r^2} \right)$. The motion in $r$ is one-dimensional with an effective potential.
step: $\partial L / \partial t = 0$, so $h = T + V = \tfrac12 m \dot r^2 + \dfrac{J^2}{2 m r^2} + V(r)$ is also conserved.

@example
title: A pendulum with a driven pivot
problem: A bob of mass $m$ hangs on a massless rigid rod of length $\ell$ whose pivot moves horizontally as a given function $x_0(t)$. Measure $\theta$ from the downward vertical. Find the equation of motion and decide whether $h$ is conserved.
step: The bob is at $x = x_0(t) + \ell \sin\theta$, $y = -\ell\cos\theta$. The rod constraint is built in, so $\theta$ is the only coordinate.
step: Then $\dot x = \dot x_0 + \ell\dot\theta\cos\theta$ and $\dot y = \ell\dot\theta\sin\theta$, so $T = \tfrac12 m \left( \dot x_0^2 + 2\ell \dot x_0 \dot\theta\cos\theta + \ell^2\dot\theta^2 \right)$ and $V = -m g \ell \cos\theta$.
step: The term $\tfrac12 m \dot x_0^2$ depends on $t$ alone, so it is a total derivative and can be dropped: $L = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 + m\ell\dot x_0\dot\theta\cos\theta + m g\ell\cos\theta$.
step: $p_\theta = m\ell^2\dot\theta + m\ell\dot x_0\cos\theta$, so $\dot p_\theta = m\ell^2\ddot\theta + m\ell\ddot x_0\cos\theta - m\ell\dot x_0\dot\theta\sin\theta$. Also $\partial L/\partial\theta = -m\ell\dot x_0\dot\theta\sin\theta - m g\ell\sin\theta$.
step: Equating $\dot p_\theta = \partial L/\partial\theta$, the $\dot x_0\dot\theta\sin\theta$ terms cancel: $\ell\ddot\theta + \ddot x_0\cos\theta + g\sin\theta = 0$. A fixed pivot ($\ddot x_0 = 0$) gives the ordinary pendulum.
step: For this $L$, $h = \tfrac12 m\ell^2\dot\theta^2 - m g\ell\cos\theta$, and $dh/dt = -\partial L/\partial t = -m\ell\ddot x_0\dot\theta\cos\theta \ne 0$ in general. So $h$ is not conserved while the pivot accelerates.

@mistakes
- Substituting $\dot\phi = J/(mr^2)$ into $L$ before varying $r$. This flips the sign of the centrifugal term; eliminate conserved momenta only after forming the equations of motion.
- Reading "stationary" as "minimum". Hamilton's principle only demands $\delta S = 0$, and the physical path can be a saddle of $S$.
- Dropping the boundary term $[p_i\,\delta q^i]$ without saying why: it vanishes only because $\delta q = 0$ at both ends.
- Assuming $h = T + V$ and $h$ conserved automatically. Both can fail when a coordinate is tied to a prescribed time-dependent motion.

@practice self
prompt: For one coordinate with Lagrangian $L(q, \dot q, t)$, derive the Euler–Lagrange equation from $\delta S = 0$. Say explicitly where the fixed endpoints and the arbitrariness of $\delta q$ are used.
hint: Write $\delta S$ to first order and replace $\delta\dot q$ by $\frac{d}{dt}\delta q$.
hint: Integrate the $\delta\dot q$ term by parts, then ask what the fixed endpoints do to the boundary term.
solution: To first order, $\delta S = \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\delta q + \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\delta\dot q \right) dt$ with $\delta\dot q = \frac{d}{dt}\delta q$. Integrate the second term by parts: $\int \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\frac{d}{dt}\delta q\,dt = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\delta q\right]_{t_1}^{t_2} - \int \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q}\,\delta q\,dt$. The boundary term is zero because $\delta q(t_1) = \delta q(t_2) = 0$. What remains is $\delta S = \int \left( \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} - \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} \right)\delta q\,dt = 0$. Since $\delta q(t)$ is arbitrary in the interior, the bracket must vanish at every time, giving $\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot q} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial q} = 0$.
rubric: Writes $\delta S$ to first order with $\delta\dot q = \frac{d}{dt}\delta q$.
rubric: Integrates by parts and states that the boundary term vanishes because $\delta q = 0$ at both endpoints.
rubric: Argues that $\delta q(t)$ is arbitrary in the interior, so the bracket vanishes identically (fundamental lemma).
rubric: States the final equation with the correct relative sign.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle of mass $m = 1.0\ \text{kg}$ moves in a plane with $L = \tfrac12 m(\dot r^2 + r^2\dot\phi^2) - \tfrac12 k r^2$, where $k = 4.0\ \text{N/m}$. Its conserved canonical momentum is $p_\phi = 4.0\ \text{kg m}^2/\text{s}$. Find the radius of the circular orbit.
answer: sqrt(2)
check: root(lambda r: 4.0**2/(1.0*r**3) - 4.0*r, 0.1, 5.0)
tol: 0.01
unit: m
hint: $\phi$ is cyclic, so $p_\phi = m r^2\dot\phi$ is fixed. Write the radial Euler–Lagrange equation.
hint: A circular orbit has $\ddot r = 0$. Eliminate $\dot\phi$ using $p_\phi$ after forming the radial equation.
solution: The radial equation is $m\ddot r = m r\dot\phi^2 - k r$. With $\dot\phi = p_\phi/(m r^2)$ and $\ddot r = 0$: $\dfrac{p_\phi^2}{m r^3} = k r$, so $r^4 = \dfrac{p_\phi^2}{m k} = 4$ and $r = \sqrt{2}\ \text{m} \approx 1.41\ \text{m}$.
