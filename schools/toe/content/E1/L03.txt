@article
Look at a 12-hour clock and let $H=\{0,4,8\}$. Shift $H$ by $1$ hour and you get $\{1,5,9\}$; by $2$, $\{2,6,10\}$; by $3$, $\{3,7,11\}$. Four shifted copies of $H$ tile all twelve hours with no overlap. This tiling is the idea behind cosets.

### Cosets

Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$. For $g\in G$, the **left coset** of $H$ containing $g$ is
$$gH=\{gh : h\in H\}.$$
Three facts hold.

1. **Every element lies in a coset:** $g=ge\in gH$.
2. **All cosets have the same size:** $h\mapsto gh$ is a bijection $H\to gH$, by cancellation. So $|gH|=|H|$.
3. **Two cosets are equal or disjoint.** If $x\in aH\cap bH$, write $x=ah_1=bh_2$. Then $a=bh_2h_1^{-1}$, so $aH\subseteq bH$, and symmetrically $bH\subseteq aH$.

Also $aH=bH$ exactly when $a^{-1}b\in H$. So the left cosets **partition** $G$ into pieces of size $|H|$. The number of left cosets is the **index** $[G:H]$. In an additive group the coset is written $g+H$. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the cosets of $H=n\mathbb{Z}$ are exactly the residue classes mod $n$, so $[\mathbb{Z}:n\mathbb{Z}]=n$.

### Lagrange's theorem

Counting elements piece by piece gives, for a finite group,
$$|G|=[G:H]\cdot|H|.$$
In particular **the order of a subgroup divides the order of the group**.

### Consequences

- The order of any element $g$ divides $|G|$, because $|\langle g\rangle|$ is the order of $g$. Hence $g^{|G|}=e$.
- A group of **prime order** $p$ is cyclic: pick $g\neq e$; then $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $p$ and exceeds $1$, so $\langle g\rangle=G$.
- **Fermat's little theorem:** for a prime $p$ and $a\not\equiv0\pmod p$, $a^{p-1}\equiv1\pmod p$, since $|\mathbb{Z}_p^\times|=p-1$. More generally $a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv1\pmod n$ when $\gcd(a,n)=1$.

### The converse is false

Lagrange says every subgroup order divides $|G|$. It does **not** say every divisor occurs. The group of even permutations of four objects has order $12$ and no subgroup of order $6$ (you can verify this in week 8). Right cosets $Hg$ are defined the same way; they can differ from left cosets when $G$ is not abelian, which leads to week 5.

@example
title: Listing cosets and checking Lagrange
problem: Find all left cosets of $H=\langle6\rangle$ in $(\mathbb{Z}_{18},+)$ and verify $|G|=[G:H]\,|H|$.
step: In additive notation $gH$ becomes $g+H$. We have $H=\{0,6,12\}$, so $|H|=3$.
step: Shift $H$ by $0,1,2,\dots$: $0+H=\{0,6,12\}$, $1+H=\{1,7,13\}$, $2+H=\{2,8,14\}$, $3+H=\{3,9,15\}$, $4+H=\{4,10,16\}$, $5+H=\{5,11,17\}$.
step: Shifting by $6$ returns $0+H$ again, so these six cosets are all of them: $[G:H]=6$.
step: Check: $|G|=18=6\cdot3=[G:H]\,|H|$.

@example
title: Powers modulo a prime
problem: Compute $2^{45}\bmod 11$.
step: The group $\mathbb{Z}_{11}^\times$ has order $10$, so by Lagrange $a^{10}\equiv1$ for every $a\not\equiv 0\pmod{11}$.
step: Write $45=4\cdot10+5$. Then $2^{45}=(2^{10})^4\cdot2^5\equiv 2^5\pmod{11}$.
step: $2^5=32=2\cdot11+10$, so $2^{45}\equiv10\pmod{11}$.

@mistakes
- Using Lagrange backwards: concluding that a group of order $12$ must have a subgroup of order $6$ because $6$ divides $12$.
- Mixing up the order of an element with the order of the group; the element order divides $|G|$ but is usually smaller.
- Forgetting that $aH=bH$ means $a^{-1}b\in H$, not $ab^{-1}$ or $ab\in H$ in a general (multiplicative) group.

@practice ms
prompt: Let $G$ be any group of order $12$. Select every statement that must be true.
choice*: The order of every element of $G$ divides $12$.
choice: $G$ has an element of order $12$.
choice*: $g^{12}=e$ for every $g\in G$.
choice: $G$ has a subgroup of order $5$.
hint: Which consequence of Lagrange's theorem restricts element orders?
hint: A statement is forced only if it holds for every group of order $12$. The symmetry group of a regular hexagon has order $12$ but no element of that order.
solution: Element orders divide $|G|=12$, so $g^{12}=e$. Not every group of order $12$ is cyclic, so an element of order $12$ need not exist. A subgroup of order $5$ is impossible because $5\nmid12$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $p$ be a prime and let $G$ be a group with $|G|=p$. Prove that $G$ is cyclic and that every non-identity element generates $G$.
hint: Pick $g\neq e$ and consider the subgroup $\langle g\rangle$.
hint: What does Lagrange's theorem say about $|\langle g\rangle|$, given that $p$ is prime?
solution: Let $g\in G$ with $g\neq e$. The set $\langle g\rangle$ is a subgroup of $G$ containing $e$ and $g$, so $|\langle g\rangle|\ge2$. By Lagrange, $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $p$. Since $p$ is prime, $|\langle g\rangle|=p=|G|$, so $\langle g\rangle=G$. Hence $G$ is cyclic, generated by any non-identity element.
rubric: Takes an arbitrary $g\neq e$ and uses the subgroup $\langle g\rangle$.
rubric: Applies Lagrange's theorem to conclude $|\langle g\rangle|$ divides $p$.
rubric: Uses primality of $p$ (and $|\langle g\rangle|>1$) to get $|\langle g\rangle|=p$ and hence $\langle g\rangle=G$.

@quiz num
prompt: Compute $3^{1000}\bmod 13$.
answer: 3
check: pow(3,1000,13)
hint: The group $\mathbb{Z}_{13}^\times$ has order $12$. What is $3^{12}$ mod $13$?
hint: Reduce the exponent: $1000=83\cdot12+4$. Then compute $3^4$ mod $13$.
solution: By Lagrange, $3^{12}\equiv1$, so $3^{1000}\equiv3^{4}=81=6\cdot13+3\equiv3\pmod{13}$.

@quiz self
prompt: Let $H$ be a subgroup of $G$ and $a,b\in G$. Prove that if $aH\cap bH\neq\emptyset$ then $aH=bH$.
hint: Pick an element $x$ in the intersection and write it as $ah_1$ and as $bh_2$.
hint: Express $a$ in terms of $b$, $h_1$ and $h_2$, then show every element $ah$ lies in $bH$.
solution: Let $x\in aH\cap bH$, so $x=ah_1=bh_2$ with $h_1,h_2\in H$. Then $a=bh_2h_1^{-1}$. For any $h\in H$, $ah=b(h_2h_1^{-1}h)\in bH$ because $h_2h_1^{-1}h\in H$. So $aH\subseteq bH$. By symmetry (using $b=ah_1h_2^{-1}$), $bH\subseteq aH$. Hence $aH=bH$.
rubric: Chooses a common element and writes it in two ways using $H$.
rubric: Shows one inclusion with an explicit element of $H$ and notes that it lies in $H$ because $H$ is a subgroup.
rubric: Obtains the reverse inclusion by symmetry or by a second computation, concluding equality.
