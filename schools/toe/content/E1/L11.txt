@article
Rotating a vector by any angle is a symmetry of a sphere, not just by multiples of $90^\circ$. Such symmetry groups have **continuous parameters**. A **matrix Lie group** is a group of invertible matrices cut out by smooth equations (a closed subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ or $GL_n(\mathbb{C})$). Its **dimension** is the number of independent continuous parameters.

### The classical families

| group | defining condition | dimension |
|---|---|---|
| $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ | $\det A\neq0$ | $n^2$ |
| $SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ | $\det A=1$ | $n^2-1$ |
| $O(n)$ | $A^TA=I$ | $n(n-1)/2$ |
| $SO(n)$ | $A^TA=I,\ \det A=1$ | $n(n-1)/2$ |
| $U(n)$ | $U^\dagger U=I$ | $n^2$ |
| $SU(n)$ | $U^\dagger U=I,\ \det U=1$ | $n^2-1$ |

Here $U^\dagger$ is the conjugate transpose. The condition $A^TA=I$ says the columns of $A$ are orthonormal, so $A$ preserves dot products, lengths and angles. Taking determinants gives $(\det A)^2=1$, so $\det A=\pm1$; $SO(n)$ is the subgroup of rotations ($\det=+1$) and $O(n)$ also contains reflections.

### Counting dimension

The matrix equation $A^TA=I$ is symmetric, so it imposes $n(n+1)/2$ independent conditions on the $n^2$ entries. This leaves $n^2-n(n+1)/2=n(n-1)/2$ free parameters. Requiring $\det A=1$ does not remove any (the determinant is already $\pm1$, a discrete choice). For $U(n)$ the $2n^2$ real entries are cut down by $n^2$ real conditions, leaving $n^2$; $\det U=1$ removes one more parameter (the phase of the determinant).

### The first examples

- $SO(2)=\{R(\theta)\}$ with $R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}$, and $R(\alpha)R(\beta)=R(\alpha+\beta)$. It is abelian, and isomorphic to $U(1)$ and to $\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$.
- $SO(3)$ has dimension $3$ (three angles). It is **not** abelian: rotating about different axes in different orders gives different results.
- $SU(2)$ consists of the matrices $\begin{pmatrix}a&-\bar b\\ b&\bar a\end{pmatrix}$ with $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$, a $3$-dimensional group.

### Where they appear in physics

$SO(3)$ is the rotation symmetry of space and of any central potential; $SU(2)$ governs spin; $U(1)$ is the symmetry behind electromagnetism, and $SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1)$ is the gauge group of the Standard Model. Representations of these groups (week 9) classify particles and states.

@example
title: Testing membership in $SO(3)$
problem: Show that $A=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ lies in $SO(3)$, and identify the rotation.
step: The columns of $A$ are $e_2,e_3,e_1$. These are orthonormal, so $A^TA=I$ and $A\in O(3)$.
step: Expand the determinant along the first row. Only the entry $a_{13}=1$ is nonzero, with minor $\det\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=1$ and sign $(-1)^{1+3}=+1$. So $\det A=1$ and $A\in SO(3)$.
step: $A(1,1,1)^T=(1,1,1)^T$, so the axis is the diagonal direction. The trace is $0=1+2\cos\theta$, so $\cos\theta=-\tfrac12$ and the angle is $120^\circ$.

@example
title: The dimension of $SU(2)$
problem: Count the real dimension of $SU(2)$ from its defining equations.
step: A complex $2\times2$ matrix has $4$ complex entries, i.e. $8$ real parameters.
step: $U^\dagger U=I$ is a matrix equation with Hermitian left side, which gives $4$ independent real conditions (two real diagonal entries and one complex off-diagonal entry). So $\dim U(2)=8-4=4$.
step: $\det U=1$: for a unitary matrix $|\det U|=1$ already, so only the phase of $\det U$ is constrained, which is $1$ real condition. Hence $\dim SU(2)=4-1=3$.

@mistakes
- Believing $A^TA=I$ forces $\det A=+1$. It forces $\det A=\pm1$; reflections have $\det=-1$ and lie in $O(n)$ but not $SO(n)$.
- Using $A^TA=I$ for complex matrices. For $U(n)$ the correct condition uses the conjugate transpose, $U^\dagger U=I$.
- Concluding that $SO(3)$ is abelian by analogy with $SO(2)$: two rotations about different axes generally do not commute.

@practice num
prompt: What is the dimension of the rotation group $SO(4)$?
answer: 6
check: len([(i,j) for i in range(4) for j in range(4) if i<j])
hint: The dimension of $SO(n)$ is the number of independent entries in an antisymmetric $n\times n$ matrix.
hint: Use $n(n-1)/2$, or count the pairs $i<j$.
solution: The dimension of $SO(n)$ is $n(n-1)/2$; for $n=4$ this is $4\cdot3/2=6$.

@practice num
prompt: The rotation of $\mathbb{R}^3$ through $60^\circ$ about the $z$-axis has matrix $R_z(60^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos60^\circ&-\sin60^\circ&0\\ \sin60^\circ&\cos60^\circ&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. What is its trace?
answer: 2

hint: Add the diagonal entries.
hint: $\cos60^\circ=\tfrac12$.
solution: $\operatorname{tr}=\cos60^\circ+\cos60^\circ+1=\tfrac12+\tfrac12+1=2$. In general a rotation by $\theta$ in $SO(3)$ has trace $1+2\cos\theta$.

@practice ms
prompt: Select every statement that is true.
choice*: $SO(n)$ is a subgroup of $O(n)$.
choice*: Every matrix in $O(n)$ preserves the length of every vector.
choice: The product of two matrices in $O(3)$ with determinant $-1$ again has determinant $-1$.
choice: Every matrix in $U(n)$ has determinant $1$.
hint: Determinants multiply, and $U^\dagger U=I$ gives $|\det U|^2=1$.
hint: For $A\in O(n)$, $|Av|^2=v^TA^TAv$.
solution: $|Av|^2=v^TA^TAv=|v|^2$. $SO(n)$ is the determinant-$1$ subgroup. The product of two determinant-$(-1)$ matrices has determinant $+1$. A unitary matrix has $|\det U|=1$, so its determinant can be any complex number of modulus $1$, not necessarily $1$.

@practice num
prompt: Let $A=R_x(90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&0&-1\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $B=R_z(90^\circ)=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. How many entries of $AB-BA$ are nonzero?
answer: 6
check: np.count_nonzero(np.round(np.array([[1,0,0],[0,0,-1],[0,1,0]])@np.array([[0,-1,0],[1,0,0],[0,0,1]])-np.array([[0,-1,0],[1,0,0],[0,0,1]])@np.array([[1,0,0],[0,0,-1],[0,1,0]]),8))
hint: Multiply the two matrices in both orders.
hint: $AB$ has rows $(0,-1,0)$, $(0,0,-1)$, $(1,0,0)$. Find $BA$ the same way.
solution: $AB=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&0\\0&0&-1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $BA=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $AB-BA=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1&-1\\-1&0&-1\\1&-1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has $6$ nonzero entries. Since $AB\neq BA$, rotations in $SO(3)$ do not commute.

@quiz num
prompt: Let $R$ be the $2\times2$ rotation matrix through the angle $2\pi/7$. What is the smallest positive integer $n$ with $R^n=I$?
answer: 7
check: [n for n in range(1,50) if np.allclose(np.linalg.matrix_power(np.array([[cos(2*pi/7),-sin(2*pi/7)],[sin(2*pi/7),cos(2*pi/7)]]),n),np.eye(2))][0]
hint: $R(\alpha)^n=R(n\alpha)$.
hint: When is $R(n\cdot2\pi/7)=I$? You need $n\cdot2\pi/7$ to be a multiple of $2\pi$.
solution: $R^n=R(2\pi n/7)=I$ exactly when $2\pi n/7$ is a multiple of $2\pi$, i.e. when $7\mid n$. The least such $n$ is $7$. These rotations form a copy of $\mathbb{Z}_7$ inside $SO(2)$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which pair of conditions characterizes the matrices $U$ in $SU(2)$?
choice*: $U^\dagger U=I$ and $\det U=1$
choice: $U^TU=I$ and $\det U=1$
choice: $U^\dagger=U$ and $\det U=1$
choice: $\det U=1$ only
hint: The "U" stands for unitary and the "S" for special. Which transpose does unitarity use?
hint: A unitary matrix preserves the Hermitian inner product, which involves complex conjugation.
solution: Unitarity is $U^\dagger U=I$ (conjugate transpose), and "special" means $\det U=1$. The condition $U^TU=I$ describes complex orthogonal matrices, $U^\dagger=U$ describes Hermitian matrices, and $\det U=1$ alone is $SL_2(\mathbb{C})$.
