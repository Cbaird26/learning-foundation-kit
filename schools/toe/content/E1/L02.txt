@article
A **subgroup** of a group $G$ is a subset $H\subseteq G$ that is itself a group under the operation of $G$. We write $H\le G$. Associativity is inherited for free, so what must be checked is that $H$ contains the identity, is closed under products, and is closed under inverses.

### The one-step test

A nonempty subset $H\subseteq G$ is a subgroup if and only if
$$a,b\in H\ \Longrightarrow\ ab^{-1}\in H.$$
Why does this suffice? Pick any $a\in H$: taking $b=a$ gives $e=aa^{-1}\in H$. Taking $a=e$ gives $b^{-1}\in H$. Then for $a,b\in H$ we have $b^{-1}\in H$, so $ab=a(b^{-1})^{-1}\in H$.

If $H$ is **finite** and nonempty, closure under products alone is enough: the powers $a,a^2,a^3,\dots$ of $a\in H$ must repeat, which forces $a^k=e$ for some $k\ge1$ and gives $a^{-1}=a^{k-1}\in H$. Warning: this shortcut fails for infinite sets. The set $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ is closed under addition in $\mathbb{Z}$ but is not a subgroup.

### Cyclic subgroups and order

For $a\in G$, the set $\langle a\rangle=\{a^k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}$ is the smallest subgroup containing $a$. The **order of the element** $a$ is the least $n\ge1$ with $a^n=e$ (or infinite if none exists). When $a$ has finite order $n$, $|\langle a\rangle|=n$.

In $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (written additively, so "powers" are multiples), $\langle a\rangle$ has $n/\gcd(a,n)$ elements. For instance $\langle 8\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{12}$ is $\{0,8,4\}$, of order $3$.

### Subgroups you should know

- Subgroups of $(\mathbb{Z},+)$ are exactly $n\mathbb{Z}=\{\dots,-n,0,n,2n,\dots\}$.
- $\mathbb{Z}_n$ has exactly one subgroup for each divisor $d$ of $n$, namely $\langle n/d\rangle$, of order $d$.
- $SL_n(\mathbb{R})$ (determinant $1$) is a subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ because $\det(AB^{-1})=1$.
- The **center** $Z(G)=\{z: zg=gz\text{ for all }g\}$ is a subgroup.

### Intersections and unions

If $H$ and $K$ are subgroups, so is $H\cap K$: it contains $e$, and if $a,b\in H\cap K$ then $ab^{-1}$ lies in both. But $H\cup K$ is usually **not** a subgroup. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the multiples of $2$ and the multiples of $3$ are both subgroups, yet their union contains $2$ and $3$ and not $2+3=5$.

@example
title: Testing a subset with the one-step test
problem: Let $H=\{3k:k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\cap\{x\in\mathbb{Z}: x \text{ is even}\}$ viewed inside $(\mathbb{Z},+)$. Is $H$ a subgroup? What is it?
step: In additive notation the one-step test reads: $a,b\in H\Rightarrow a-b\in H$.
step: $H$ is nonempty because $0$ is a multiple of $3$ and even.
step: If $a,b\in H$ then $a$ and $b$ are multiples of $3$ and even, so $a-b$ is a multiple of $3$ and even. Hence $a-b\in H$.
step: So $H$ is a subgroup. A number is in $H$ exactly when it is divisible by $6$, so $H=6\mathbb{Z}$.

@example
title: A subset that fails
problem: Is $H=\{x\in\mathbb{Z}_{10}:x\text{ is odd}\}$ a subgroup of $(\mathbb{Z}_{10},+)$?
step: A subgroup must contain the identity $0$.
step: But $0$ is not odd, so $0\notin H$.
step: Also $1+1=2\notin H$, so $H$ is not closed. Either failure shows $H$ is not a subgroup.

@mistakes
- Checking closure only. For infinite groups you also need the identity and inverses (for instance, $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ in $(\mathbb{Z},+)$).
- Forgetting to check that $H$ is nonempty; the identity must belong to $H$.
- Believing that the union of two subgroups is a subgroup. The intersection always is; the union is only when one subgroup contains the other.

answer: 3
check: [k for k in range(1,13) if (8*k)%12==0][0]
hint: Order means the least $k\ge1$ with $k\cdot 8\equiv0\pmod{12}$.

solution: $8$, $16\equiv4$, $24\equiv0$ (mod $12$), so $3\cdot 8\equiv 0$ and the order is $3$.

@quiz num
prompt: In the group $(\mathbb{Z}_{20}^\times,\cdot)$, how many elements does the subgroup generated by $3$ contain?
answer: 4
check: len({pow(3,k,20) for k in range(1,41)})
hint: Compute the powers $3,3^2,3^3,\dots$ mod $20$ until you reach $1$.
hint: $3^2=9$, $3^3=27\equiv7$.
solution: $3^1=3$, $3^2=9$, $3^3\equiv 7$, $3^4\equiv21\equiv1$. So $\langle3\rangle=\{1,3,7,9\}$ has $4$ elements.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which condition on a nonempty subset $H$ of a group $G$ is enough, by itself, to conclude that $H$ is a subgroup?
choice: $ab\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$.
choice: $a^{-1}\in H$ for all $a\in H$.
choice*: $ab^{-1}\in H$ for all $a,b\in H$.
choice: $H$ contains the identity of $G$.
hint: Which condition lets you derive the identity, inverses and closure in turn?
hint: Test closure under products on $\{1,2,3,\dots\}$ and closure under inverses on $\{-1,1\}$ inside $(\mathbb{Z},+)$.
solution: Closure alone fails in infinite groups ($\{1,2,3,\dots\}$), closure under inverses alone fails ($\{-1,1\}$ is closed under negation but $1+1=2$), and containing $e$ is not enough. The condition $ab^{-1}\in H$ gives $e$, then inverses, then products.
