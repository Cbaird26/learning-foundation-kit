@article
The **symmetric group** $S_n$ is the group of all bijections of $\{1,\dots,n\}$ under composition, with $|S_n|=n!$. We multiply right to left. Every finite group sits inside some $S_n$ (Cayley's theorem, below), so permutation groups are the finite groups in disguise.

### Cycles

The cycle $(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)$ sends $a_1\to a_2\to\cdots\to a_k\to a_1$ and fixes everything else. Every permutation is a product of **disjoint cycles**, unique up to order, and disjoint cycles commute. For instance $\sigma=(1\,2\,3)(4\,5)\in S_5$. To multiply, follow each symbol through the factors from right to left. In $(1\,2)(2\,3)$ we get $1\to1\to2$, $2\to3\to3$ and $3\to2\to1$, so the product is $(1\,2\,3)$.

The **order** of a permutation is the least common multiple of its cycle lengths: $\sigma$ has order $\operatorname{lcm}(3,2)=6$.

### Transpositions and sign

A $2$-cycle is a **transposition**. A $k$-cycle is a product of $k-1$ transpositions:
$$(a_1\,a_2\,\dots\,a_k)=(a_1\,a_k)\cdots(a_1\,a_3)(a_1\,a_2).$$
Every permutation is a product of transpositions, and the parity (even or odd) of the number of factors does not depend on the factorization. The **sign** $\operatorname{sgn}:S_n\to\{\pm1\}$ is a homomorphism with $\operatorname{sgn}(k\text{-cycle})=(-1)^{k-1}$. Its kernel is the group $A_n$ of even permutations, with $|A_n|=n!/2$ for $n\ge2$. Note that a $5$-cycle is even and a $4$-cycle is odd.

### Conjugacy classes

Two permutations in $S_n$ are conjugate exactly when they have the same cycle type. The number of permutations with $m_k$ cycles of length $k$ is
$$\frac{n!}{\prod_k k^{m_k}\,m_k!}.$$
For example, in $S_5$ the type $(3,2)$ has $5!/(3\cdot2)=20$ elements. In $S_4$ there are $8$ three-cycles and $3$ double transpositions.

### Cayley's theorem

For any finite group $G$, the map sending $g$ to the permutation $x\mapsto gx$ of the set $G$ is an injective homomorphism $G\to S_{|G|}$. Injective because $gx=x$ forces $g=e$.

### Orbits and stabilizers of $\langle\sigma\rangle$

The cyclic group $\langle\sigma\rangle$ acts on $\{1,\dots,n\}$. Its orbits are exactly the cycles of $\sigma$ (fixed points are orbits of size $1$). If the cycle containing $a$ has length $m$, then the stabilizer of $a$ is $\langle\sigma^m\rangle$, and $|\langle\sigma\rangle|=m\cdot|\text{stabilizer}|$.

@example
title: Cycles, order and sign
problem: Let $\sigma\in S_7$ send $1\to3,\ 3\to2,\ 2\to1,\ 4\to5,\ 5\to4,\ 6\to7,\ 7\to6$. Write $\sigma$ in disjoint cycles and find its order and sign.
step: Follow $1\to3\to2\to1$ to get the cycle $(1\,3\,2)$.
step: Then $4\leftrightarrow5$ and $6\leftrightarrow7$, so $\sigma=(1\,3\,2)(4\,5)(6\,7)$.
step: Order: $\operatorname{lcm}(3,2,2)=6$.
step: Sign: a $3$-cycle is even, each transposition is odd, so the parity is $0+1+1\equiv0$. The permutation $\sigma$ is even.

@example
title: Orbit and stabilizer of a cyclic group
problem: Let $\sigma=(1\,2\,3)(4\,5)$ and $G=\langle\sigma\rangle$. Find the orbit and stabilizer of the point $1$ and verify orbit-stabilizer.
step: $|G|=6$ since $\sigma$ has order $\operatorname{lcm}(3,2)=6$.
step: The orbit of $1$ is the cycle containing $1$: $\{1,2,3\}$, of size $3$.
step: The stabilizer of $1$ is $\langle\sigma^3\rangle$. Since $\sigma^3=(1\,2\,3)^3(4\,5)^3=(4\,5)$, it is $\{e,(4\,5)\}$, of order $2$.
step: Check: $|G|=6=3\cdot2=|\text{orbit}|\cdot|\text{stabilizer}|$.

@mistakes

- Taking the order of a permutation to be the sum or the product of the cycle lengths instead of their least common multiple.
- Using the number of entries in a cycle for parity: a cycle of odd length is even, and a cycle of even length is odd.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which of the following is NOT the order of any element of $S_5$?
choice: $4$
choice: $5$
choice: $6$
choice*: $10$
hint: The order of a permutation is the lcm of its cycle lengths, and the lengths sum to at most $5$.
hint: For lcm $10$ you would need cycles of lengths such as $5$ and $2$.
solution: Orders: $4$ from $(1\,2\,3\,4)$, $5$ from a $5$-cycle, $6$ from $(1\,2\,3)(4\,5)$. An lcm of $10$ needs cycle lengths $5$ and $2$ (or $10$), which sum to $7>5$. So $10$ does not occur.
