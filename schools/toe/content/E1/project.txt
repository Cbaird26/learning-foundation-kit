@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1-2: groups and subgroups.** A group satisfies closure, associativity, identity and inverses. Disproving needs one counterexample; proving needs all elements. From the axioms: unique identity and inverses, cancellation, $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$. The one-step test ($ab^{-1}\in H$), cyclic subgroups $\langle a\rangle$, element order, and the subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}_n$ (one per divisor). Intersections of subgroups are subgroups; unions usually are not.

**Weeks 3-4: cosets and homomorphisms.** Left cosets partition $G$ into pieces of size $|H|$, giving Lagrange: $|G|=[G:H]\,|H|$. Element orders divide $|G|$, groups of prime order are cyclic, and Fermat/Euler follow. A homomorphism has $\varphi(e)=e$ and $\varphi(a^{-1})=\varphi(a)^{-1}$; kernel and image are subgroups; $\varphi$ is injective iff $\ker\varphi=\{e\}$. There are $\gcd(n,m)$ homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}_n\to\mathbb{Z}_m$.

**Weeks 5-6: normal subgroups and quotients.** $N$ is normal if $gng^{-1}\in N$ always; kernels, centers and index-$2$ subgroups are normal. $(aN)(bN)=abN$ is well defined exactly for normal $N$. The First Isomorphism Theorem: $G/\ker\varphi\cong\operatorname{im}\varphi$.

**Weeks 7-8: actions and permutations.** Orbits partition $X$; $|G|=|Gx|\,|G_x|$; $G_{g\cdot x}=gG_xg^{-1}$; Burnside counts orbits as an average of fixed points. In $S_n$: disjoint cycles, order = lcm, sign, $A_n$, class sizes $n!/\prod k^{m_k}m_k!$, and Cayley's theorem.

**Weeks 9-10: representations.** A representation is a homomorphism $\rho:G\to GL(V)$; verify it on generators and relations. The character $\chi=\operatorname{tr}\rho$ satisfies $\chi(e)=\dim V$ and is constant on classes. Maschke: every representation splits into irreducibles. Schur: intertwiners of irreducibles are scalars or zero. $\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=\sum m_i^2$; the number of irreducibles is the number of classes; $\sum d_i^2=|G|$.

**Weeks 11-12: Lie groups and algebras.** $O(n)$, $SO(n)$, $U(n)$, $SU(n)$ via $A^TA=I$, $U^\dagger U=I$ and $\det$; dimensions $n(n-1)/2$, $n^2-1$. The Lie algebra is found by differentiating at $I$ ($\mathfrak{so}(n)$ is antisymmetric). With $[X,Y]=XY-YX$: $[L_x,L_y]=L_z$ cyclically, $e^{\theta L_z}=R_z(\theta)$, and $J_k=i\hbar L_k$ gives $[J_x,J_y]=i\hbar J_z$.

### Common errors

- Using Lagrange backwards: a divisor of $|G|$ need not be a subgroup order.
- Forming $G/N$ for a non-normal subgroup.
- Reading permutation products in the wrong order, or taking the order as a sum of cycle lengths.
- Treating the character as multiplicative.
- Confusing a Lie group with its Lie algebra, or the commutator with the anticommutator.

answer: 3

hint: First find $|S_4/V|=24/4$. A coset $pV$ has order $2$ exactly when $p\notin V$ but $p^2\in V$.
hint: Count the elements $p\in S_4\setminus V$ with $p^2\in V$, then divide by $|V|=4$ because each coset has $4$ elements.
hint: The elements with $p^2=e$ are the transpositions; the $4$-cycles square to double transpositions.
solution: The quotient has order $6$. A coset $pV$ has order $2$ iff $p\notin V$ and $p^2\in V$. The transpositions ($6$ of them, with $p^2=e$) and the $4$-cycles ($6$ of them, with $p^2$ a double transposition) qualify; $3$-cycles do not since $p^2$ is a $3$-cycle. That is $12$ elements, forming $12/4=3$ cosets. Consistently, $S_4/V\cong S_3$, which has $3$ elements of order $2$.

prompt: Let $G$ be a finite group and $\rho:G\to GL_n(\mathbb{C})$ a representation with kernel $K=\ker\rho$. (a) Prove that $K$ is a normal subgroup of $G$. (b) Prove that $\bar\rho(gK)=\rho(g)$ gives a well-defined injective homomorphism $G/K\to GL_n(\mathbb{C})$. (c) Deduce that if $|G|=p$ is prime and $\rho(g)\neq I$ for some $g$, then $\rho$ is injective.
hint: For (a) use that $K$ is the kernel of the homomorphism $\rho$ and conjugate an element of $K$.
hint: For (b) use the First Isomorphism Theorem, or check well-definedness directly: $gK=hK$ means $g^{-1}h\in K$.
hint: For (c) apply Lagrange's theorem to the subgroup $K$ and use that $K\neq G$.
solution: (a) If $k\in K$ and $g\in G$ then $\rho(gkg^{-1})=\rho(g)\,I\,\rho(g)^{-1}=I$, so $gkg^{-1}\in K$ and $K\trianglelefteq G$. (b) If $gK=hK$ then $g^{-1}h\in K$, so $\rho(g)^{-1}\rho(h)=I$ and $\rho(g)=\rho(h)$; hence $\bar\rho$ is well defined. It is a homomorphism because $\bar\rho(gKhK)=\rho(gh)=\rho(g)\rho(h)$. If $\bar\rho(gK)=I$ then $g\in K$, so $gK=K$ is the identity of $G/K$; the kernel is trivial and $\bar\rho$ is injective. (c) By Lagrange, $|K|$ divides $p$, so $|K|\in\{1,p\}$. Since some $\rho(g)\neq I$, $K\neq G$, so $|K|=1$ and $\rho$ is injective.
rubric: Proves normality of $K$ by computing $\rho(gkg^{-1})$.
rubric: Shows $\bar\rho$ is well defined and a homomorphism, and that its kernel is trivial.
rubric: Applies Lagrange's theorem and primality of $p$ in part (c), using $K\neq G$.

prompt: The group $S_4$ acts on the six two-element subsets of $\{1,2,3,4\}$, and hence on $\mathbb{C}^6$ by permuting the six basis vectors. Let $\chi(g)$ be the number of subsets fixed by $g$. Compute $\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=\frac1{24}\sum_{g\in S_4}\chi(g)^2$.
answer: 3
check: sum(len([1 for i in range(4) for j in range(i+1,4) if {p[i],p[j]}=={i,j}])**2 for p in [[a,b,c,d] for a in range(4) for b in range(4) for c in range(4) for d in range(4) if len({a,b,c,d})==4])/24
hint: Count fixed subsets class by class: identity, transpositions, double transpositions, $3$-cycles, $4$-cycles.
hint: A transposition such as $(1\,2)$ fixes $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3,4\}$; a $3$-cycle or $4$-cycle fixes no two-element subset. The class sizes are $1,6,3,8,6$.
hint: Add $\text{(class size)}\times\chi^2$ over the classes and divide by $24$.
solution: Values of $\chi$: identity $6$; transpositions (6 of them) $2$; double transpositions (3 of them) $2$; $3$-cycles (8) $0$; $4$-cycles (6) $0$. So $\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=\frac1{24}(36+6\cdot4+3\cdot4+0+0)=\frac{72}{24}=3$. Since $3=1+1+1$ is the only way to write $3$ as a sum of squares of multiplicities, the representation is a sum of three distinct irreducibles, each once (here of dimensions $1$, $2$ and $3$). The trivial one appears once because the action has a single orbit.
