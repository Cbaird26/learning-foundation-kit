@article
In the permutation representation of $S_3$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, the line spanned by $(1,1,1)$ is never moved off itself by any permutation of coordinates. The plane $x+y+z=0$ is also mapped to itself. The representation has split into smaller pieces, and each piece can be studied alone.

### Invariant subspaces and irreducibility

A subspace $W\subseteq V$ is **invariant** (or a subrepresentation) if $\rho(g)W\subseteq W$ for every $g\in G$. A representation is **irreducible** if $V\neq0$ and the only invariant subspaces are $0$ and $V$. Every one-dimensional representation is irreducible.

If $V=W\oplus W'$ with both invariant, then in a basis adapted to this splitting every matrix is block diagonal:
$$\rho(g)=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_W(g)&0\\0&\rho_{W'}(g)\end{pmatrix}.$$

### Maschke's theorem

For a finite group over $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$, every invariant subspace has an invariant complement. So every representation is a direct sum of irreducibles. The proof averages an inner product over the group, $\langle v,w\rangle_G=\sum_g\langle\rho(g)v,\rho(g)w\rangle$, which makes every $\rho(g)$ orthogonal (unitary); then the orthogonal complement of an invariant subspace is invariant.

### Schur's lemma

If $T:V\to V'$ commutes with the group action ($T\rho(g)=\rho'(g)T$) and $V,V'$ are irreducible, then $T=0$ or $T$ is an isomorphism. Over $\mathbb{C}$, a map from an irreducible $V$ to itself that commutes with all $\rho(g)$ is a scalar multiple of $I$: it has an eigenvalue $\lambda$, and $T-\lambda I$ has a nonzero kernel that is invariant, hence all of $V$.

### Characters and counting

For a finite group $G$ and complex representations, define $\langle\chi,\psi\rangle=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g}\chi(g)\overline{\psi(g)}$. The irreducible characters $\chi_1,\dots,\chi_k$ are orthonormal. If $V\cong\bigoplus m_iV_i$, then
$$m_i=\langle\chi,\chi_i\rangle,\qquad\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=\sum_im_i^2.$$
So $V$ is irreducible exactly when $\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=1$. Moreover the number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes, and
$$\sum_i(\dim V_i)^2=|G|.$$

### Example: $S_3$

$S_3$ has $3$ conjugacy classes, hence $3$ irreducibles: trivial, sign and a $2$-dimensional **standard** representation; indeed $1+1+4=6$. The permutation character is $(3,1,0)$ on classes of sizes $1,3,2$, so $\langle\chi,\chi\rangle=\frac16(9+3+0)=2$: the permutation representation is the sum of two distinct irreducibles, the trivial one and the standard one.

### Physical meaning

If a Hamiltonian $H$ commutes with every $\rho(g)$, then its eigenspaces are invariant subspaces, so each energy level carries a representation of $G$. Unless the degeneracy is accidental, this representation is irreducible, and the degeneracy of the level is the dimension of an irreducible representation. For three identical sites with hopping matrix $H=-t(J-I)$ ($J$ the all-ones matrix), the energies are $-2t$ (once, on $(1,1,1)$) and $+t$ (twice, on the plane $x+y+z=0$).

@example
title: Splitting the permutation representation of $S_3$
problem: Show that $W=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,1)\}$ and $W'=\{(x,y,z): x+y+z=0\}$ are invariant subspaces of $\mathbb{R}^3$ under the permutation representation and that $\mathbb{R}^3=W\oplus W'$.
step: A permutation matrix only rearranges coordinates. A vector $(t,t,t)$ is unchanged, so $W$ is invariant.
step: Rearranging coordinates does not change their sum, so a vector with sum $0$ stays in $W'$. Hence $W'$ is invariant.
step: If $(t,t,t)\in W'$ then $3t=0$, so $W\cap W'=\{0\}$.
step: $\dim W+\dim W'=1+2=3$, so $\mathbb{R}^3=W\oplus W'$. In an adapted basis every matrix is a $1\times1$ block (the trivial representation) plus a $2\times2$ block.

@example
title: Counting irreducibles of the symmetry group of a square
problem: The symmetry group $D_4$ of a square has $5$ conjugacy classes. Find the dimensions of its irreducible complex representations.
step: The number of irreducibles equals the number of classes, so there are $5$ of them, with dimensions $d_1,\dots,d_5$.
step: The trivial representation is one of them, and $\sum d_i^2=|D_4|=8$.
step: Each $d_i\ge1$ and $d_i^2\le 8-4=4$, so $d_i\in\{1,2\}$. If $a$ of them have dimension $2$, then $(5-a)+4a=8$, so $a=1$.
step: The dimensions are $1,1,1,1,2$.

@mistakes
- Calling a subspace invariant because it is mapped into itself by one matrix; it must be preserved by every $\rho(g)$.
- Forgetting that the number of irreducibles is the number of conjugacy classes, not the number of group elements.
- Confusing "irreducible" with "one-dimensional"; the $2$-dimensional standard representation of $S_3$ is irreducible.

@quiz num
prompt: Count the conjugacy classes of the symmetry group of a regular hexagon, whose twelve elements are the maps $i\mapsto si+k \pmod 6$ ($s=\pm1$, $k=0,1,\dots,5$). How many irreducible complex representations does this group have?
answer: 6
check: len({min((0 if s2==1 else 6)+(-s2*k+s*k2+k)%6 for s in (1,-1) for k in range(6)) for s2 in (1,-1) for k2 in range(6)})
hint: The number of irreducible complex representations equals the number of conjugacy classes.
hint: Conjugation never changes $s$, so rotations and reflections are never conjugate. Sort the rotations $i\mapsto i+k$ and the reflections $i\mapsto -i+k$ into classes separately.
solution: The classes are $\{e\}$, the half-turn $\{i\mapsto i+3\}$, the rotations $\{i\mapsto i\pm1\}$, the rotations $\{i\mapsto i\pm2\}$, the three reflections $i\mapsto -i+k$ with $k$ even, and the three with $k$ odd. That is $6$ classes, so there are $6$ irreducible representations (dimensions $1,1,1,1,2,2$, since $4\cdot1+2\cdot4=12$).
