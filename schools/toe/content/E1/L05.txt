@article
Left cosets $gH$ and right cosets $Hg$ need not agree. In $S_3$ (multiplying right to left, as in week 1) take $H=\{e,(1\,2)\}$. Then $(1\,3)H=\{(1\,3),(1\,2\,3)\}$ but $H(1\,3)=\{(1\,3),(1\,3\,2)\}$. Those are different sets. When left and right cosets always coincide, the subgroup is called normal, and that is exactly when we can build a quotient group next week.

### Conjugation

For $g\in G$, the map $x\mapsto gxg^{-1}$ is **conjugation** by $g$. It is an isomorphism $G\to G$: it is a homomorphism, because $g(xy)g^{-1}=(gxg^{-1})(gyg^{-1})$, and it is bijective, with inverse conjugation by $g^{-1}$. So conjugation preserves orders of elements. Think of $gxg^{-1}$ as the move $x$ seen from the viewpoint of $g$: undo $g$, do $x$, redo $g$. A normal subgroup is one whose moves look the same from every viewpoint.

In $S_n$ conjugation just relabels: $g\,(a\,b\,c)\,g^{-1}=(g(a)\ g(b)\ g(c))$. Two permutations in $S_n$ are conjugate exactly when they have the same cycle shape.

### Normal subgroups

A subgroup $N\le G$ is **normal**, written $N\trianglelefteq G$, if
$$gng^{-1}\in N\quad\text{for all }g\in G,\ n\in N.$$
This is equivalent to $gN=Ng$ for every $g$, and to $gNg^{-1}=N$. (If $gNg^{-1}\subseteq N$ for all $g$, apply it to $g^{-1}$ to get the reverse inclusion.)

A normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes. The conjugacy classes of $S_3$ are $\{e\}$, the three transpositions, and the two $3$-cycles, of sizes $1,3,2$.

### Where normal subgroups come from

- **Every subgroup of an abelian group** is normal, since $gng^{-1}=n$.
- **Kernels:** if $\varphi:G\to H$ is a homomorphism and $n\in\ker\varphi$, then $\varphi(gng^{-1})=\varphi(g)\,e\,\varphi(g)^{-1}=e$. So $\ker\varphi\trianglelefteq G$. Examples: $A_n\trianglelefteq S_n$ (kernel of the sign) and $SL_n\trianglelefteq GL_n$ (kernel of $\det$).
- **The center** $Z(G)$ is normal, because each $z\in Z(G)$ satisfies $gzg^{-1}=z$.
- **Intersections:** the intersection of normal subgroups is normal, since each contains $gng^{-1}$ whenever it contains $n$.
- **Index $2$:** if $[G:H]=2$, then the left cosets are $H$ and $G\setminus H$, and so are the right cosets, giving $gH=Hg$.

### Non-normal subgroups

$H=\{e,(1\,2)\}$ in $S_3$ is not normal: with $g=(1\,3)$ we get $g(1\,2)g^{-1}=(g(1)\ g(2))=(3\ 2)\notin H$. One bad conjugate settles it.

@example
title: Disproving normality
problem: Show that the subgroup $D$ of invertible diagonal matrices in $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is not normal.
step: To disprove normality we need one $g\in GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and one $d\in D$ with $gdg^{-1}\notin D$.
step: Take $g=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $g^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $d=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$.
step: Compute $gd=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$, then $gdg^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&2\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&2\end{pmatrix}$.
step: This matrix has a nonzero off-diagonal entry, so it is not in $D$. Hence $D$ is not normal.

@example
title: Proving normality with a kernel
problem: Show that $SL_2(\mathbb{R})$ is a normal subgroup of $GL_2(\mathbb{R})$.
step: $SL_2(\mathbb{R})=\ker(\det)$, where $\det:GL_2(\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^\times$ is a homomorphism.
step: For $g\in GL_2(\mathbb{R})$ and $A\in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$: $\det(gAg^{-1})=\det(g)\det(A)\det(g)^{-1}$.
step: Since $\det A=1$, this equals $\det(g)\det(g)^{-1}=1$, so $gAg^{-1}\in SL_2(\mathbb{R})$. The subgroup is normal.

@mistakes
- Testing $gng^{-1}\in N$ only for a few $g$, or only for $n=e$. Normality requires every $g\in G$ and every $n\in N$.
- Believing that "normal" means "commutes with everything": $gN=Ng$ as sets does not mean $gn=ng$ for each $n$.
- Assuming a subgroup of order $2$ in a non-abelian group is normal; that holds only if the non-identity element is central.

@practice num
prompt: The symmetries of a square with vertices $0,1,2,3$ are the eight maps $i\mapsto si+k \pmod 4$ with $s\in\{1,-1\}$ and $k\in\{0,1,2,3\}$. How many of them commute with all the others, i.e. what is the order of the center?
answer: 2
check: len([(s,k) for s in (1,-1) for k in range(4) if all(((s*k2+k)%4)==((s2*k+k2)%4) for s2 in (1,-1) for k2 in range(4))])
hint: Compose two maps: $(s,k)$ after $(s_2,k_2)$ sends $i$ to $ss_2i+sk_2+k$.
hint: A central map must satisfy $sk_2+k\equiv s_2k+k_2\pmod 4$ for all $s_2,k_2$. Try $s=1$ first.
solution: With $s=1$ the condition becomes $k\equiv s_2k\pmod4$; taking $s_2=-1$ gives $2k\equiv0$, so $k\in\{0,2\}$. With $s=-1$, taking $s_2=1$ and $k_2=1$ gives $-1+k\equiv k+1$, which is false. The center is $\{i\mapsto i,\ i\mapsto i+2\}$, of order $2$ (the identity and the half-turn).

@practice self
prompt: Let $H$ be a subgroup of index $2$ in a group $G$. Prove that $H$ is normal in $G$.
hint: List the left cosets of $H$. What must the second one be?
hint: Do the same for right cosets, then compare $gH$ and $Hg$ in the two cases $g\in H$ and $g\notin H$.
solution: Since $[G:H]=2$ there are two left cosets, one of which is $H$ itself; since cosets partition $G$, the other is $G\setminus H$. The same argument shows the right cosets are $H$ and $G\setminus H$. If $g\in H$, then $gH=H=Hg$. If $g\notin H$, then $gH=G\setminus H=Hg$. So $gH=Hg$ for all $g$, hence $gHg^{-1}=H$ and $H$ is normal.
rubric: Identifies both left cosets and both right cosets as $H$ and $G\setminus H$, using that cosets partition $G$.
rubric: Treats the cases $g\in H$ and $g\notin H$ separately.
rubric: Concludes $gH=Hg$ and translates this into $gHg^{-1}=H$ (or $gHg^{-1}\subseteq H$).

@quiz num
prompt: How many distinct conjugates does the $3$-cycle $(1\,2\,3)$ have in $S_4$?
answer: 8
check: len({sum(g[c[g.index(i)]]*4**i for i in range(4)) for g in [(a,b,c2,d) for a in range(4) for b in range(4) for c2 in range(4) for d in range(4) if len({a,b,c2,d})==4] for c in [(1,2,0,3)]})
hint: Conjugation relabels: $g(1\,2\,3)g^{-1}=(g(1)\ g(2)\ g(3))$.
hint: Count the $3$-cycles in $S_4$: choose which $3$ of the $4$ symbols appear, then count the cyclic orders.
solution: Conjugates of $(1\,2\,3)$ are all $3$-cycles in $S_4$. There are $4$ ways to choose the three symbols and $2$ distinct cyclic orders for each, so $4\cdot2=8$ conjugates.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that the kernel of a group homomorphism $\varphi:G\to H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.
hint: First show $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup with the one-step test.
hint: For normality take $n\in\ker\varphi$ and $g\in G$ and compute $\varphi(gng^{-1})$.
solution: The kernel contains $e$, so it is nonempty. If $a,b\in\ker\varphi$ then $\varphi(ab^{-1})=\varphi(a)\varphi(b)^{-1}=e\,e^{-1}=e$, so $ab^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$ and the kernel is a subgroup. Now let $n\in\ker\varphi$ and $g\in G$. Then $\varphi(gng^{-1})=\varphi(g)\varphi(n)\varphi(g)^{-1}=\varphi(g)\,e\,\varphi(g)^{-1}=e$, so $gng^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$. Hence $\ker\varphi\trianglelefteq G$.
rubric: Shows $\ker\varphi$ is a subgroup (identity and the one-step test, or closure and inverses).
rubric: Computes $\varphi(gng^{-1})$ using the homomorphism property and $\varphi(g^{-1})=\varphi(g)^{-1}$.
rubric: Concludes $gng^{-1}\in\ker\varphi$ for every $g\in G$ and $n\in\ker\varphi$.
