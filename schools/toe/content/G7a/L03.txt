@article
Until now spacetime was flat and the coordinates were Cartesian. General relativity allows any coordinates and puts the geometry into a **metric tensor**. Our conventions stay as in Week 1: signature $(-+++)$ and $c = 1$.

### The metric

A metric $g_{\mu\nu}(x)$ is symmetric and invertible, with signature $(-+++)$ at every point. It defines the line element
$$ds^2 = g_{\mu\nu}\,dx^\mu dx^\nu ,$$
the inverse metric $g^{\mu\nu}$ (with $g^{\mu\lambda}g_{\lambda\nu} = \delta^\mu_\nu$), and index raising and lowering, $V_\mu = g_{\mu\nu}V^\nu$. Under a change of coordinates $x \to x'$,
$$V'^\mu = \frac{\partial x'^\mu}{\partial x^\nu}V^\nu, \qquad g'_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial x^\alpha}{\partial x'^\mu}\frac{\partial x^\beta}{\partial x'^\nu}\, g_{\alpha\beta}.$$
The length of a curve is $L = \int\sqrt{g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu \dot x^\nu}\,d\lambda$ for a spacelike curve, and the volume element is $\sqrt{|g|}\,d^nx$ with $g = \det g_{\mu\nu}$.

Two examples to keep in mind:

- The flat plane in polar coordinates, $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2 d\theta^2$.
- A 2-sphere of radius $a$, $ds^2 = a^2\left(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\phi^2\right)$ with $\theta$ the polar angle.

The first is flat even though $r$ appears in the metric. The second is genuinely curved. Telling them apart from the metric alone is the job of Week 5.

### Why a connection is needed

To differentiate a vector field you compare vectors at neighbouring points, but components at different points refer to different local bases. The partial derivative $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ therefore does **not** transform as a tensor under position-dependent coordinate changes. The fix is the **covariant derivative**
$$\nabla_\mu V^\nu = \partial_\mu V^\nu + \Gamma^\nu_{\mu\lambda}V^\lambda, \qquad \nabla_\mu \omega_\nu = \partial_\mu\omega_\nu - \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}\,\omega_\lambda ,$$
where the connection coefficients $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$ absorb the change of the basis.

### The Levi-Civita connection

Require two properties: **torsion-free**, $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\mu}$, and **metric-compatible**, $\nabla_\lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 0$. These fix the connection uniquely, and its components are the Christoffel symbols
$$\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\, g^{\lambda\sigma}\left(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}\right).$$
Metric compatibility means raising, lowering and contracting indices commute with $\nabla$.

### What $\Gamma$ means and does not mean

$\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$ is **not a tensor**: it transforms inhomogeneously. Hence it can vanish at a point in one coordinate system and not in another. At any event $P$ one can choose **Riemann normal coordinates** with
$$g_{\mu\nu}(P) = \eta_{\mu\nu}, \qquad \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}(P) = 0 .$$
This is the mathematical form of the equivalence principle: locally, every spacetime looks flat to first order. The second derivatives of $g_{\mu\nu}$ cannot all be removed, and what remains is the curvature (Week 5).

Conversely, nonzero Christoffel symbols do not prove curvature. Polar coordinates on the flat plane have $\Gamma \neq 0$ because the coordinate basis rotates from point to point. The symbols record the geometry **and** the choice of coordinates.

@example
title: Christoffel symbols of the flat plane in polar coordinates
problem: For $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$, find all non-zero Christoffel symbols.
step: Read off $g_{rr} = 1$, $g_{\theta\theta} = r^2$, with inverse $g^{rr} = 1$, $g^{\theta\theta} = r^{-2}$. The only metric component that depends on a coordinate is $g_{\theta\theta}$, with $\partial_r g_{\theta\theta} = 2r$.
step: $\Gamma^r_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12 g^{rr}\left(2\partial_\theta g_{r\theta} - \partial_r g_{\theta\theta}\right) = -\tfrac12(2r) = -r$.
step: $\Gamma^\theta_{r\theta} = \tfrac12 g^{\theta\theta}\,\partial_r g_{\theta\theta} = \tfrac12 r^{-2}(2r) = 1/r$, and $\Gamma^\theta_{\theta r}$ has the same value by symmetry.
step: Every other component vanishes (each contains only $\partial_\theta$ of $g_{rr}$, $g_{\theta\theta}$ or $\partial_r g_{rr}$, all zero). The plane is flat, yet $\Gamma \neq 0$: the symbols reflect the curvilinear basis, not curvature.

@example
title: Christoffel symbols of the 2-sphere
problem: For the unit 2-sphere $ds^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\, d\phi^2$, find $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi}$ and $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi}$.
step: $g_{\theta\theta} = 1$, $g_{\phi\phi} = \sin^2\theta$, so $g^{\theta\theta} = 1$ and $g^{\phi\phi} = 1/\sin^2\theta$. Only $g_{\phi\phi}$ varies, with $\partial_\theta g_{\phi\phi} = 2\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
step: $\Gamma^\theta_{\phi\phi} = \tfrac12 g^{\theta\theta}\left(-\partial_\theta g_{\phi\phi}\right) = -\sin\theta\cos\theta$.
step: $\Gamma^\phi_{\theta\phi} = \tfrac12 g^{\phi\phi}\,\partial_\theta g_{\phi\phi} = \dfrac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta} = \cot\theta$.
step: All other components vanish. Unlike the plane, no coordinate choice makes all of these zero in a neighbourhood: this surface is curved.

@mistakes
- Treating $\partial_\mu V^\nu$ as a tensor, or $\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu}$ as a tensor. Neither transforms homogeneously.
- Concluding "curved" from $\Gamma \neq 0$, or "flat" from $\Gamma = 0$ at one point. Curvature needs derivatives of $\Gamma$ (Week 5).
- Using $g^{\mu\nu} = 1/g_{\mu\nu}$ component by component for a non-diagonal metric. It is the matrix inverse.
- Forgetting the minus sign and the opposite index structure in the covariant derivative of a covector.

prompt: On the plane with $ds^2 = dr^2 + r^2d\theta^2$, find the length of the circular arc $r = 3$, $0 \le \theta \le \pi/2$. Give an exact value.
answer: 3pi/2
check: quad(lambda th: sqrt(1.0*0.0**2 + (3.0**2)*1.0**2), 0, pi/2)

solution: $L = \int_0^{\pi/2} r\, d\theta = 3\cdot\pi/2 = 3\pi/2$.

@practice self
prompt: Starting from the Christoffel formula, show that the Levi-Civita connection is metric compatible: $\nabla_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} = 0$.
hint: Write $\nabla_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}$ with one $-\Gamma$ term for each of the two lower indices.
hint: Lower the upper index of $\Gamma$ with the metric, insert the Christoffel formula, and watch four of the six derivative terms cancel.
solution: Define $\Gamma_{\sigma\mu\nu} = g_{\sigma\lambda}\Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\mu g_{\sigma\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma g_{\mu\nu}\right)$. Then $\nabla_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} = \partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} - \Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\nu}g_{\sigma\lambda} - \Gamma^\sigma_{\mu\lambda}g_{\nu\sigma} = \partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} - \Gamma_{\lambda\mu\nu} - \Gamma_{\nu\mu\lambda}$. Now $\Gamma_{\lambda\mu\nu} + \Gamma_{\nu\mu\lambda} = \tfrac12(\partial_\mu g_{\lambda\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\lambda\mu} - \partial_\lambda g_{\mu\nu}) + \tfrac12(\partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} + \partial_\lambda g_{\nu\mu} - \partial_\nu g_{\mu\lambda}) = \partial_\mu g_{\nu\lambda}$, because the other four terms cancel in pairs. Hence $\nabla_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} = 0$.

rubric: Lowers an index on $\Gamma$ using the metric and inserts the Christoffel formula.
rubric: Shows the cancellation of the mixed terms and concludes $\nabla_\mu g_{\nu\lambda} = 0$.

@quiz expr
prompt: The upper half-plane metric is $ds^2 = (dx^2 + dy^2)/y^2$ with $y > 0$. Find the Christoffel symbol $\Gamma^y_{xx}$ as a function of $y$.
vars: y
lead: $\Gamma^y_{xx} =$
answer: 1/y
check: lambda y: 0.5*(y**2)*(-nderiv(lambda s: 1/s**2, y))
hint: Here $g_{xx} = g_{yy} = y^{-2}$, so $g^{yy} = y^2$. Only $-\partial_y g_{xx}$ survives in the bracket for $\Gamma^y_{xx}$.
hint: $\partial_y(y^{-2}) = -2y^{-3}$.
solution: $\Gamma^y_{xx} = \tfrac12 g^{yy}\left(-\partial_y g_{xx}\right) = \tfrac12 y^2\cdot 2y^{-3} = 1/y$.
