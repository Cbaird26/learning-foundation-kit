@article
Newton's second law uses an **inertial mass** $m_i$, and Newtonian gravity uses a **gravitational mass** $m_g$. If they are equal, every freely falling test body has the same acceleration $g$, whatever it is made of. This is the **weak equivalence principle** (universality of free fall), confirmed in torsion-balance and satellite experiments to better than one part in $10^{13}$.

### From free fall to a local inertial frame

Inside a freely falling, windowless lab, dropped objects float and no experiment reveals a gravitational field. The **Einstein equivalence principle** states this for all non-gravitational physics: in a sufficiently small freely falling region, the laws are those of special relativity. Gravity is then not a force on freely falling bodies. Their worldlines are the "straightest possible" paths, and the next weeks make that precise.

The words "sufficiently small" matter. Two nearby test bodies in a Newtonian potential $\Phi$ have relative acceleration
$$\frac{d^2 \delta x^i}{dt^2} = -\,\partial_i\partial_j \Phi\;\delta x^j .$$
This **tidal** acceleration cannot be removed by any choice of freely falling frame. It is the Newtonian shadow of spacetime curvature.

### Uniform acceleration in flat spacetime

An observer with constant **proper acceleration** $a$ (what an accelerometer reads) along $x$ follows, with $c = 1$ and the clock set so that $t = 0$, $x = 1/a$ at $\tau = 0$,
$$t(\tau) = \frac{1}{a}\sinh(a\tau), \qquad x(\tau) = \frac{1}{a}\cosh(a\tau), \qquad x^2 - t^2 = \frac{1}{a^2}.$$
This hyperbola replaces the Newtonian parabola. Its speed $v = \tanh(a\tau)$ never reaches $1$.

**Rindler coordinates** $(\eta, \xi)$ adapted to such observers are defined by
$$t = \xi \sinh\eta, \qquad x = \xi\cosh\eta, \qquad ds^2 = -\xi^2\, d\eta^2 + d\xi^2 + dy^2 + dz^2 .$$
An observer at fixed $\xi$ is at rest in these coordinates, has proper time $d\tau = \xi\, d\eta$, and proper acceleration $1/\xi$. The metric looks non-trivial, but it describes **flat** spacetime in curvilinear coordinates; Week 5 gives a test (the Riemann tensor vanishes). The inertial and accelerated descriptions differ only by a coordinate change. The lines $x = \pm t$ (where $\xi = 0$) form a **Rindler horizon**: events on the far side of $x = t$ (those with $t \ge x$) can never send a signal to the accelerating observer, a preview of black hole horizons.

### Gravitational redshift from a rocket

A rocket of height $h$ accelerates upward at $g$. Light of frequency $\nu_b$ is emitted from the floor at $t = 0$ and reaches the ceiling after $\Delta t \approx h/c$. By then the ceiling moves at $v \approx g h/c$ away from the inertial frame where the emitter was momentarily at rest, so the Doppler shift gives
$$\frac{\nu_{\rm top}}{\nu_{\rm bottom}} \approx 1 - \frac{g h}{c^2}.$$
By the equivalence principle the same holds on a tower of height $h$ in a uniform field: light climbing out of a potential difference $\Delta\Phi = gh$ is redshifted by $\Delta\Phi/c^2$, and clocks higher up tick faster by the same fraction. This suggests that the time-time component of the metric depends on $\Phi$, as Week 8 shows precisely.

### Limits of the principle

Uniform acceleration reproduces a *uniform* field only. In real gravity the field direction and strength vary, tidal terms survive, and there is no global inertial frame. So "gravity is acceleration" is true locally and false globally, and a faithful theory must be geometric.

@example
title: Properties of hyperbolic motion
problem: With $c = 1$, an observer follows $t = \sinh(a\tau)/a$, $x = \cosh(a\tau)/a$. Show that $\tau$ is proper time and that the proper acceleration is $a$.
step: Differentiate: $u^\mu = (dt/d\tau,\, dx/d\tau) = (\cosh a\tau,\; \sinh a\tau)$.
step: Normalization: $u^\mu u_\mu = -\cosh^2 a\tau + \sinh^2 a\tau = -1$. So $\tau$ is proper time.
step: Differentiate again: $a^\mu = du^\mu/d\tau = a\,(\sinh a\tau,\; \cosh a\tau)$.
step: Its squared magnitude is $a^\mu a_\mu = a^2(-\sinh^2 a\tau + \cosh^2 a\tau) = a^2$, so the proper acceleration has magnitude $a$ at every moment.

@example
title: Redshift across a tower
problem: Estimate the fractional frequency shift of light sent up a tower of height $h = 22.5$ m, using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $c = 3.00\times 10^{8}$ m/s.
step: Replace the tower by a rocket of height $h$ accelerating at $g$ (equivalence principle).
step: Light-travel time: $\Delta t = h/c = 22.5/(3.00\times10^8) = 7.5\times 10^{-8}$ s.
step: Speed gained by the receiver: $v = g\,\Delta t = 7.35\times 10^{-7}$ m/s.
step: Doppler shift: $v/c = 2.45\times 10^{-15}$. The light arrives redshifted by this fraction, $\nu_{\rm top}/\nu_{\rm bottom} \approx 1 - 2.45\times10^{-15}$.

@mistakes
- Using the Newtonian $x = \tfrac12 a t^2$ for a rocket at relativistic speed. A constant proper acceleration gives a hyperbola, not a parabola.
- Believing that Rindler coordinates describe a curved spacetime because the metric has a non-constant component. Flatness is a property of the Riemann tensor, not of the form of $g_{\mu\nu}$.
- Applying the equivalence principle over a large region. Tidal effects distinguish gravity from acceleration once the region is not small.
- Confusing coordinate acceleration $d^2x/dt^2$ with proper acceleration, which is what an accelerometer measures.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable (compare inertial and accelerated descriptions): starting from the Rindler transformation $t = \xi\sinh\eta$, $x = \xi\cosh\eta$ (with $c = 1$), derive the metric $ds^2 = -\xi^2 d\eta^2 + d\xi^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$. Then compare how a uniformly accelerated observer is described in the inertial and in the Rindler coordinates.
hint: Compute $dt$ and $dx$ with the product rule, then form $-dt^2 + dx^2$.
hint: The $d\xi\,d\eta$ cross terms cancel, and $\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta = 1$ does the rest. For the comparison, describe the worldline $\xi = $ const in both coordinate systems.
solution: Differentiate: $dt = \sinh\eta\, d\xi + \xi\cosh\eta\, d\eta$ and $dx = \cosh\eta\, d\xi + \xi\sinh\eta\, d\eta$. Then $-dt^2 + dx^2 = (\cosh^2\eta - \sinh^2\eta)\,d\xi^2 + \xi^2(\sinh^2\eta - \cosh^2\eta)\,d\eta^2 + 2\xi\sinh\eta\cosh\eta\,(-1 + 1)\,d\xi\, d\eta = d\xi^2 - \xi^2 d\eta^2$. In inertial coordinates the observer at fixed $\xi = 1/a$ moves on the hyperbola $x^2 - t^2 = \xi^2$ with changing velocity and the metric is constant. In Rindler coordinates the same observer sits at fixed $\xi$, the metric depends on $\xi$, and the proper acceleration $1/\xi$ appears as that dependence. Both describe the same flat spacetime.

rubric: States that the observer's worldline is $x^2 - t^2 = \xi^2$ in inertial coordinates but $\xi = $ const in Rindler coordinates.
rubric: Notes that the spacetime is the same flat Minkowski space, with only the coordinates changed.
