@article
When the gravitational field is weak but may vary in time, we write $g_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\nu} + h_{\mu\nu}$ with $|h_{\mu\nu}| \ll 1$ and keep only first-order terms. Indices on $h$ are raised and lowered with $\eta$, and $h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu}$. Signature $(-+++)$, the curvature conventions of Week 1, and $c = 1$ unless shown.

### The linearized equations

The linearized Riemann tensor is
$$R_{\rho\sigma\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\sigma\partial_\mu h_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\rho\partial_\nu h_{\sigma\mu} - \partial_\sigma\partial_\nu h_{\rho\mu} - \partial_\rho\partial_\mu h_{\sigma\nu}\right),$$
and the Ricci tensor is $R_{\mu\nu} = \tfrac12\left(\partial_\sigma\partial_\mu h^\sigma{}_\nu + \partial_\sigma\partial_\nu h^\sigma{}_\mu - \partial_\mu\partial_\nu h - \Box h_{\mu\nu}\right)$ with $\Box = -\partial_t^2 + \nabla^2$. For a static $h_{00}$ this gives $R_{00} = -\tfrac12\nabla^2 h_{00}$, as in Week 8.

### Gauge freedom

A small coordinate change $x^\mu \to x^\mu + \xi^\mu(x)$ shifts
$$h_{\mu\nu} \to h_{\mu\nu} - \partial_\mu\xi_\nu - \partial_\nu\xi_\mu .$$
The linearized Riemann tensor is unchanged, so it is the physical, gauge-invariant object. Individual components of $h_{\mu\nu}$ are not.

Define the **trace-reversed** perturbation $\bar h_{\mu\nu} = h_{\mu\nu} - \tfrac12\eta_{\mu\nu}h$ and choose the **Lorenz (harmonic) gauge** $\partial^\mu\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$. The linearized Einstein equation becomes
$$\Box\,\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu} .$$
For a static source $\nabla^2\bar h_{00} = -16\pi G\rho$ gives $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi$ and then $h_{00} = -2\Phi$, $h_{ij} = -2\Phi\delta_{ij}$, the weak-field metric of Week 8.

### Waves in vacuum

For $T_{\mu\nu} = 0$ we get $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = 0$: waves travelling at the speed of light. Residual gauge freedom allows the **transverse-traceless (TT) gauge**: $h_{0\mu} = 0$, $\partial_i h_{ij} = 0$, $h_{ii} = 0$. For a wave along $z$,
$$h^{\rm TT}_{ij} = \begin{pmatrix} h_+ & h_\times & 0\\ h_\times & -h_+ & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}\cos\!\big[\omega(t - z)\big].$$
There are **two polarizations**, $+$ and $\times$. In TT gauge free test masses stay at fixed coordinates, but their proper separation changes: two masses separated by $L$ along $x$ have
$$L_x(t) \approx L\left[1 + \tfrac12 h_+\cos\omega t\right],$$
and the separation along $y$ changes with the opposite sign. A gravitational-wave detector measures the strain $\Delta L/L = h/2$.

### Emission: the quadrupole formula

Conservation of energy and momentum forbids monopole and dipole radiation. The leading emission is quadrupolar. With the quadrupole moment $I_{ij} = \int\rho\left(x_ix_j - \tfrac13\delta_{ij}x^2\right)d^3x$, at distance $r$ and with $c$ restored,
$$h^{\rm TT}_{ij}(t, r) = \frac{2G}{c^4 r}\,\ddot I^{\rm TT}_{ij}\!\left(t - \frac rc\right), \qquad P = \frac{G}{5c^5}\,\big\langle \dddot I_{ij}\,\dddot I_{ij}\big\rangle .$$
Because $I_{ij}$ is quadratic in position, a circular orbit with angular frequency $\Omega$ produces a quadrupole varying at $2\Omega$.

@example
title: Trace reversal for a static weak field
problem: Given $h_{00} = -2\Phi$ and $h_{ij} = -2\Phi\delta_{ij}$, find $\bar h_{00}$ and confirm it reproduces Poisson's equation.
step: The trace is $h = \eta^{\mu\nu}h_{\mu\nu} = -h_{00} + h_{ii} = 2\Phi - 6\Phi = -4\Phi$.
step: $\bar h_{00} = h_{00} - \tfrac12\eta_{00}h = -2\Phi - \tfrac12(-1)(-4\Phi) = -4\Phi$, and $\bar h_{ij} = -2\Phi\delta_{ij} - \tfrac12\delta_{ij}(-4\Phi) = 0$.
step: The $00$ component of $\Box\bar h_{\mu\nu} = -16\pi G\,T_{\mu\nu}$ for static dust is $\nabla^2\bar h_{00} = -16\pi G\rho$.
step: Substituting $\bar h_{00} = -4\Phi$: $-4\nabla^2\Phi = -16\pi G\rho$, i.e. $\nabla^2\Phi = 4\pi G\rho$. The linearized theory reproduces Poisson's equation.

@example
title: Power radiated by a circular binary
problem: Two masses $m_1$ and $m_2$ orbit their centre of mass in a circle of separation $a$ with angular frequency $\Omega$. Find the radiated power.
step: In the centre-of-mass frame the quadrupole equals that of one reduced mass $\mu = m_1m_2/(m_1 + m_2)$ at $\vec x = a(\cos\Omega t, \sin\Omega t, 0)$. So $I_{xx} = \mu a^2\left(\tfrac16 + \tfrac12\cos2\Omega t\right)$, $I_{yy} = \mu a^2\left(\tfrac16 - \tfrac12\cos2\Omega t\right)$, $I_{xy} = \tfrac12\mu a^2\sin2\Omega t$, and $I_{zz}$ is constant.
step: Third derivatives: $\dddot I_{xx} = -\dddot I_{yy} = 4\mu a^2\Omega^3\sin2\Omega t$ and $\dddot I_{xy} = -4\mu a^2\Omega^3\cos2\Omega t$.
step: $\dddot I_{ij}\dddot I_{ij} = 16\mu^2a^4\Omega^6\left(2\sin^2 2\Omega t + 2\cos^2 2\Omega t\right) = 32\mu^2a^4\Omega^6$.
step: $P = \dfrac{32\,G}{5c^5}\mu^2a^4\Omega^6$. With Kepler's law $\Omega^2 = G(m_1 + m_2)/a^3$ this is $P = \dfrac{32G^4}{5c^5}\dfrac{m_1^2m_2^2(m_1 + m_2)}{a^5}$. The waves have frequency $2\Omega/2\pi$.

@mistakes
- Treating individual components of $h_{\mu\nu}$ as physical. Only gauge-invariant quantities, such as the Riemann tensor or the TT components, are.
- Forgetting trace reversal: a static source has $h_{ij} = -2\Phi\delta_{ij} \neq 0$ even though $\bar h_{ij} = 0$.
- Setting the gravitational-wave frequency equal to the orbital frequency. It is twice the orbital frequency for a circular binary.
- Expecting dipole (or monopole) gravitational radiation, as in electromagnetism. Momentum and mass-energy conservation forbid them.
