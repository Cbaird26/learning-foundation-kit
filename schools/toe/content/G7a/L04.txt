@article
In flat space a free particle satisfies $d^2x^\mu/d\tau^2 = 0$ in Cartesian coordinates. In curved spacetime a freely falling particle follows a **geodesic**, the curved-space version of a straight line.

### The geodesic equation

For a curve $x^\mu(\lambda)$ with tangent $u^\mu = dx^\mu/d\lambda$,
$$\frac{d^2x^\mu}{d\lambda^2} + \Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}\,\frac{dx^\nu}{d\lambda}\frac{dx^\rho}{d\lambda} = 0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad u^\nu\nabla_\nu u^\mu = 0 .$$
The tangent is parallel-transported along its own curve. The parameter $\lambda$ must be **affine**: any $\lambda' = a\lambda + b$ also works, while a nonlinear reparametrization does not.

The quantity $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu$ is constant along a geodesic, so the class of the curve never changes:

- **timelike** (massive particles): $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = -1$ and $\lambda = \tau$ is proper time;
- **null** (light): $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = 0$, with a non-unique affine parameter, since proper time does not exist for light;
- **spacelike**: $g_{\mu\nu}u^\mu u^\nu = +1$ with $\lambda$ the proper distance.

In Riemann normal coordinates $\Gamma = 0$ at a point, so geodesics look straight there. This is the equivalence principle again.

### Geodesics from a variational principle

Among nearby timelike curves joining two events, a geodesic makes the proper time $\tau = \int\sqrt{-g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu}\,d\lambda$ stationary (a local maximum). The simpler Lagrangian
$$L = \tfrac12\, g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu, \qquad \frac{d}{d\lambda}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^\mu} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial x^\mu} = 0$$
gives exactly the geodesic equation. This is a shortcut for finding Christoffel symbols: write the Euler–Lagrange equations and read $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho}$ from the coefficient of $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ (with $\Gamma^\mu_{\nu\rho} + \Gamma^\mu_{\rho\nu}$ appearing for $\nu \neq \rho$).

### Conserved quantities

If no metric component depends on a coordinate $x^\alpha$, then $\partial L/\partial x^\alpha = 0$ and
$$p_\alpha = g_{\alpha\nu}\,\dot x^\nu \quad\text{is constant along every geodesic.}$$
(This is a Killing vector, $\partial_\alpha$, at work.) Together with the normalization $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = -1, 0, +1$, conserved quantities often solve the motion without the full second-order equations. The same method yields energy and angular momentum for Schwarzschild in Week 9.

### Newton from a Christoffel symbol

Take $ds^2 = -(1 + 2\Phi)\,dt^2 + d\vec x^{\,2}$ with $|\Phi| \ll 1$ and a slow particle, $|dx^i/dt| \ll 1$, so $\dot t \approx 1$. The spatial geodesic equation is $\ddot x^i = -\Gamma^i_{tt}\,\dot t^2$ with
$$\Gamma^i_{tt} = -\tfrac12\,\partial_i g_{tt} = \partial_i\Phi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x^i}{dt^2} \approx -\partial_i\Phi .$$
This is Newton's law of free fall, derived from geometry. Only $g_{tt}$ mattered at this order. Week 8 adds the spatial metric correction that light bending needs. With $c$ restored, $g_{tt} = -(1 + 2\Phi/c^2)$.

@example
title: Free fall in a uniform potential
problem: In the metric $ds^2 = -(1 + 2gx)\,dt^2 + dx^2 + dy^2 + dz^2$ (with $c = 1$, $gx \ll 1$), find the equation of motion of a slow particle.
step: The only non-constant component is $g_{tt} = -(1 + 2gx)$, with $\partial_x g_{tt} = -2g$.
step: $\Gamma^x_{tt} = \tfrac12 g^{xx}\left(2\partial_t g_{xt} - \partial_x g_{tt}\right) = \tfrac12(2g) = g$.
step: The geodesic equation for $x$ reads $\ddot x + \Gamma^x_{tt}\dot t^2 = 0$, i.e. $\ddot x = -g\,\dot t^2$.
step: For a slow particle $\dot t = dt/d\tau \approx 1$, so $d^2x/dt^2 \approx -g$: uniform free fall, with released-from-rest motion $x = x_0 - \tfrac12 g t^2$.

@example
title: Which circles on a sphere are geodesics?
problem: On the unit sphere $ds^2 = d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\phi^2$, test whether the equator and a circle of latitude $\theta = \theta_0 \neq \pi/2$ are geodesics.
step: Use the Christoffel symbols from Week 3. The geodesic equations are $\ddot\theta - \sin\theta\cos\theta\,\dot\phi^2 = 0$ and $\ddot\phi + 2\cot\theta\,\dot\theta\,\dot\phi = 0$.
step: Equator $\theta = \pi/2$, $\dot\phi$ constant: here $\dot\theta = \ddot\theta = 0$ and $\sin\theta\cos\theta = 0$, so both equations hold. The equator is a geodesic.
step: Latitude circle $\theta = \theta_0$: $\dot\theta = \ddot\theta = 0$, so the first equation requires $\sin\theta_0\cos\theta_0\,\dot\phi^2 = 0$. That fails for $\theta_0 \neq \pi/2$ and $\dot\phi \neq 0$.
step: So latitude circles other than the equator are not geodesics. A particle on one needs a force towards the nearer pole (the centre of the circle), because a free particle would drift towards the equator.

@mistakes
- Using a non-affine parameter such as a coordinate $t$ in $\ddot x^\mu + \Gamma\dot x\dot x = 0$ without adding a term proportional to $\dot x^\mu$.
- Using proper time for a photon. Light needs an affine parameter, with $g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu = 0$.
- Reading $\Gamma$ from the Euler–Lagrange equation without dividing by the coefficient of $\ddot x^\mu$, or missing the factor $2$ for the cross terms $\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$ with $\nu \neq \rho$.
- Believing every constant-coordinate circle is a geodesic because it "looks straight" in the coordinates.

answer: 18

@practice self
prompt: Show that the Euler–Lagrange equations for $L = \tfrac12 g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\mu\dot x^\nu$ reproduce the geodesic equation with the Levi-Civita connection.
hint: Compute $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$ first, then differentiate it with respect to $\lambda$ using the chain rule on $g_{\mu\nu}(x(\lambda))$.
hint: Symmetrize the term $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$ over the two velocity indices, then multiply by $g^{\sigma\mu}$.
solution: $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu = g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu$, so $\frac{d}{d\lambda}(g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\nu) = g_{\mu\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\,\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu$. Also $\partial L/\partial x^\mu = \tfrac12\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}\,\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$. The Euler–Lagrange equation is $g_{\mu\nu}\ddot x^\nu + \partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu - \tfrac12\partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0$. Symmetrize the middle term over the dummy indices: $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\rho\dot x^\nu = \tfrac12(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho})\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho$. Then multiply by $g^{\sigma\mu}$: $\ddot x^\sigma + \tfrac12 g^{\sigma\mu}\left(\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu} + \partial_\nu g_{\mu\rho} - \partial_\mu g_{\nu\rho}\right)\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0$, which is $\ddot x^\sigma + \Gamma^\sigma_{\nu\rho}\dot x^\nu\dot x^\rho = 0$.
rubric: Computes both $\partial L/\partial\dot x^\mu$ and $\partial L/\partial x^\mu$ correctly, including the $\tfrac12$ in $L$.
rubric: Differentiates $g_{\mu\nu}(x(\lambda))$ with the chain rule to produce $\partial_\rho g_{\mu\nu}\dot x^\rho$.
rubric: Symmetrizes over the two velocity indices and contracts with $g^{\sigma\mu}$.
rubric: Identifies the result with the Christoffel formula from Week 3.

@quiz num
prompt: In the flat 1+1 metric $ds^2 = -dt^2 + t^2dx^2$ the quantity $p_x = t^2\,dx/d\tau$ is conserved along geodesics. A massive particle has $p_x = 3$. Find its coordinate velocity $dx/dt$ at $t = 1.5$. Give the value to four decimal places.
answer: 0.5963
check: (lambda pt: (pt/1.5**2)/root(lambda td: -td**2 + 1.5**2*(pt/1.5**2)**2 + 1, 0.01, 10))(3.0)
tol: 0.0005
hint: Normalization for a massive particle: $-\dot t^2 + t^2\dot x^2 = -1$ with $\dot x = p_x/t^2$.
hint: $\dot t = \sqrt{1 + p_x^2/t^2}$. Then $dx/dt = \dot x/\dot t$.
solution: $\dot x = 3/2.25 = 4/3$ and $\dot t = \sqrt{1 + 9/2.25} = \sqrt5$. So $dx/dt = \dfrac{4/3}{\sqrt5} = \dfrac{4}{3\sqrt5} \approx 0.5963$, which is below the local light speed $1/t = 2/3$, as it must be.
