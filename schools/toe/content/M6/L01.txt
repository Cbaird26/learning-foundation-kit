@article
A **statement** is a sentence that is definitely true or definitely false. "$7$ is prime" is a true statement; "$x > 3$" is not a statement until you know $x$. A sentence like that is an **open sentence** $P(x)$, and quantifiers turn open sentences into statements.

### Connectives

| symbol | read as | true exactly when |
|---|---|---|
| $P \wedge Q$ | $P$ and $Q$ | both are true |
| $P \vee Q$ | $P$ or $Q$ | at least one is true |
| $\neg P$ | not $P$ | $P$ is false |
| $P \Rightarrow Q$ | if $P$, then $Q$ | $P$ is false or $Q$ is true |
| $P \Leftrightarrow Q$ | $P$ if and only if $Q$ | both have the same truth value |

The conditional causes the most trouble. Think of $P \Rightarrow Q$ as a promise: "if it rains, I will bring an umbrella." The promise is broken only when it rains and there is no umbrella. So $P \Rightarrow Q$ is false only when $P$ is true and $Q$ is false. When $P$ is false, the conditional is **vacuously true**.

Two relatives of $P \Rightarrow Q$ appear constantly: the **converse** $Q \Rightarrow P$ and the **contrapositive** $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$. A truth table shows that the contrapositive always has the same truth value as the original, while the converse need not.

### Quantifiers

- $\forall x \in S,\ P(x)$ says $P(x)$ is true for **every** $x$ in $S$.
- $\exists x \in S,\ P(x)$ says $P(x)$ is true for **at least one** $x$ in $S$.

Order matters. Compare

$$\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\ \exists y \in \mathbb{Z},\ x + y = 0 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \exists y \in \mathbb{Z},\ \forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\ x + y = 0.$$

The first is true: given $x$, choose $y = -x$. Here $y$ is allowed to depend on $x$. The second claims that one single $y$ works for every $x$ at once. It is false, because $0 + y = 0$ and $1 + y = 0$ cannot both hold.

### Negation rules

To negate, move $\neg$ inward one symbol at a time:

$$\neg(P \wedge Q) \equiv \neg P \vee \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \vee Q) \equiv \neg P \wedge \neg Q, \qquad \neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \wedge \neg Q,$$

$$\neg\big(\forall x \in S,\ P(x)\big) \equiv \exists x \in S,\ \neg P(x), \qquad \neg\big(\exists x \in S,\ P(x)\big) \equiv \forall x \in S,\ \neg P(x).$$

Each quantifier flips, each domain stays the same, and the $\neg$ finally lands on the innermost sentence. For example, the negation of the false statement above is $\forall y \in \mathbb{Z},\ \exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\ x + y \neq 0$, which is true (take $x = 1 - y$).

The negation of "for all" is "there is at least one exception", not "for none". Precise negations are the starting point of every proof by contradiction and every counterexample, so you will use this skill in every remaining week.

@example
title: Negating a quantified conditional
problem: Negate "For every real number $x$, if $x > 2$ then $x^2 > 4$." Then decide which of the two statements is true.
step: Write the statement in symbols: $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ (x > 2 \Rightarrow x^2 > 4)$.
step: Flip the quantifier and keep the domain: $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ \neg(x > 2 \Rightarrow x^2 > 4)$.
step: Use $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \wedge \neg Q$: $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ (x > 2 \wedge x^2 \le 4)$.
step: In words: "There is a real number greater than $2$ whose square is at most $4$."
step: The original is true: if $x > 2$, then $x$ is positive, so $x^2 = x \cdot x > 2x > 4$. Therefore the negation is false.

@example
title: Two quantifiers in a row
problem: Negate $\forall \varepsilon > 0,\ \exists n \in \mathbb{N},\ \frac{1}{n} < \varepsilon$, and decide which version is true.
step: The outer $\forall \varepsilon > 0$ becomes $\exists \varepsilon > 0$; the condition $\varepsilon > 0$ is kept.
step: The inner $\exists n \in \mathbb{N}$ becomes $\forall n \in \mathbb{N}$.
step: The sentence $\frac{1}{n} < \varepsilon$ becomes $\frac{1}{n} \ge \varepsilon$. The negation is $\exists \varepsilon > 0,\ \forall n \in \mathbb{N},\ \frac{1}{n} \ge \varepsilon$.
step: The negation asks for one positive $\varepsilon$ that is at most every $\frac{1}{n}$. None exists: for any $\varepsilon > 0$, pick an integer $n > \frac{1}{\varepsilon}$, and then $\frac{1}{n} < \varepsilon$. So the original is true and the negation is false.

@mistakes
- Negating "every $x$ has property $P$" as "no $x$ has property $P$"; the correct negation is "some $x$ fails to have $P$".
- Writing the negation of $P \Rightarrow Q$ as another conditional, such as $P \Rightarrow \neg Q$, instead of $P \wedge \neg Q$.
- Changing the domain while negating: the negation of $\forall x > 0,\ P(x)$ is $\exists x > 0,\ \neg P(x)$, not a statement about $x \le 0$.
- Swapping the order of $\forall$ and $\exists$, which can turn a true statement into a false one.

@practice num
prompt: In how many of the $8$ rows of the truth table of $(P \vee Q) \Rightarrow R$ is the statement **true**?
answer: 5
check: sum(1 for P in (0, 1) for Q in (0, 1) for R in (0, 1) if (not (P or Q)) or R)
hint: A conditional is false only when its hypothesis is true and its conclusion is false.
hint: Count the rows where $P \vee Q$ is true and $R$ is false; those are the only false rows.
solution: The statement is false exactly when $P \vee Q$ is true and $R$ is false. $P \vee Q$ is true for $3$ of the $4$ combinations of $P$ and $Q$, so there are $3$ false rows and $8 - 3 = 5$ true rows.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement is logically equivalent to "If a function is differentiable, then it is continuous"?
choice: If a function is continuous, then it is differentiable.
choice: If a function is not differentiable, then it is not continuous.
choice*: If a function is not continuous, then it is not differentiable.
choice: A function is differentiable and continuous.
hint: Only one of the related conditionals always has the same truth value as the original.
hint: The contrapositive of $P \Rightarrow Q$ is $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$.
solution: The contrapositive $\neg Q \Rightarrow \neg P$ is equivalent to $P \Rightarrow Q$. The statement "If a function is continuous, then it is differentiable" is the converse and "If a function is not differentiable, then it is not continuous" is the inverse $\neg P \Rightarrow \neg Q$; neither is equivalent. Indeed $\lvert x \rvert$ is continuous but not differentiable at $0$, so the converse is false.

@practice mc
prompt: Which sentence is the negation of "Every prime number greater than $2$ is odd"?
choice: Every prime number greater than $2$ is even.
choice*: There is a prime number greater than $2$ that is even.
choice: There is a prime number less than or equal to $2$ that is odd.
choice: No prime number greater than $2$ is odd.
hint: In symbols the statement is $\forall p \in S,\ p \text{ is odd}$, where $S$ is the set of primes greater than $2$.
hint: Flip the quantifier, keep the domain $S$, and negate "odd".
solution: $\neg(\forall p \in S,\ p \text{ is odd}) \equiv \exists p \in S,\ p \text{ is even}$: "There is a prime number greater than $2$ that is even." The domain stays "primes greater than $2$". Changing the domain, or replacing "every" by "no", gives a different statement.

@practice ms
prompt: Here $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. Select every statement that is **true**.
choice*: $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ \exists y \in \mathbb{R},\ x < y$
choice: $\exists y \in \mathbb{R},\ \forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ x < y$
choice*: $\exists x \in \mathbb{R},\ \forall y \in \mathbb{R},\ x \le y^2$
choice: $\forall x \in \mathbb{R},\ \exists y \in \mathbb{R},\ y^2 = x$
choice*: $\exists m \in \mathbb{N},\ \forall n \in \mathbb{N},\ m \le n$
hint: In $\forall x\ \exists y$, the $y$ may depend on $x$. In $\exists y\ \forall x$, one $y$ must work for every $x$.
hint: Test the doubtful ones with specific values: what happens when $x = y$, or when $x = -1$?
solution: The statement $\forall x\,\exists y,\ x < y$ is true: take $y = x + 1$. The statement $\exists y\,\forall x,\ x < y$ is false: whatever $y$ is proposed, $x = y$ breaks $x < y$. The statement $\exists x\,\forall y,\ x \le y^2$ is true: $x = 0$ works because $y^2 \ge 0$. The statement $\forall x\,\exists y,\ y^2 = x$ is false: $x = -1$ has no real square root. The statement $\exists m\,\forall n,\ m \le n$ is true: $m = 1$ is at most every natural number.

@practice self
prompt: Consider the statement "Every integer $n > 1$ has a prime divisor $p$ with $p \le \sqrt{n}$." Write it in symbols, write its negation in symbols and in plain English, and decide which of the two is true, with a reason.
hint: Use the form $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \big(n > 1 \Rightarrow \exists p,\ (p \text{ prime} \wedge p \mid n \wedge p \le \sqrt{n})\big)$.
hint: Negate from the outside in. The negation of a conditional is "hypothesis and not conclusion", and $\neg(A \wedge B \wedge C) \equiv \neg A \vee \neg B \vee \neg C$.
hint: To decide which is true, try a prime value of $n$.
solution: Statement: $\forall n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \big(n > 1 \Rightarrow \exists p,\ (p \text{ prime} \wedge p \mid n \wedge p \le \sqrt{n})\big)$.
Negation: $\exists n \in \mathbb{Z},\ \big(n > 1 \wedge \forall p,\ (p \text{ not prime} \vee p \nmid n \vee p > \sqrt{n})\big)$. In words: "There is an integer $n > 1$ all of whose prime divisors are greater than $\sqrt{n}$."
The negation is true: for $n = 7$ the only prime divisor is $7$, and $7 > \sqrt{7}$. So the original statement is false. (It becomes true if "every integer $n > 1$" is replaced by "every composite integer $n$".)
rubric: The symbolic form nests the conditional inside the universal quantifier and the existential quantifier inside the conclusion.
rubric: The negation flips both quantifiers, says "$n > 1$ and ..." rather than using a conditional, and turns the inner conjunction into a disjunction.
rubric: A specific witness, such as $n = 7$ or any other prime, shows that the negation is true.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which is the negation of "For every integer $x$, if $x^2$ is even then $x$ is even"?
choice*: There is an integer $x$ such that $x^2$ is even and $x$ is odd.
choice: There is an integer $x$ such that $x^2$ is odd and $x$ is even.
choice: For every integer $x$, if $x^2$ is even then $x$ is odd.
choice: There is an integer $x$ such that if $x^2$ is even then $x$ is odd.
hint: Flip the quantifier first, then negate the conditional.
hint: $\neg(P \Rightarrow Q) \equiv P \wedge \neg Q$.
solution: $\neg\big(\forall x \in \mathbb{Z},\ (P(x) \Rightarrow Q(x))\big) \equiv \exists x \in \mathbb{Z},\ (P(x) \wedge \neg Q(x))$: some integer has an even square but is itself odd. The negation of a conditional is a conjunction, not another conditional.

@quiz num
prompt: The conditional is not associative. In how many of the $8$ rows of the truth table do $(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow R$ and $P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R)$ have **different** truth values?
answer: 2
check: sum(1 for P in (0, 1) for Q in (0, 1) for R in (0, 1) if bool((not P) or (not Q) or R) != bool((not ((not P) or Q)) or R))
hint: $P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R)$ is false in exactly one row. Which one?
hint: $(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow R$ is false when $P \Rightarrow Q$ is true and $R$ is false. List those rows, then compare the two lists.
solution: $P \Rightarrow (Q \Rightarrow R)$ is false only for $(P, Q, R) = (T, T, F)$. $(P \Rightarrow Q) \Rightarrow R$ is false when $R$ is false and $P \Rightarrow Q$ is true: $(F, F, F)$, $(F, T, F)$ and $(T, T, F)$. The two statements differ in the $2$ rows $(F, F, F)$ and $(F, T, F)$.
