@article
**Conventions.** A static field $h$ couples to an extensive observable $M$ (the total magnetization of a magnet) through $H = H_0 - hM$. Spin variables are $s_i = \pm1$ with moment $m_0$, so $M = m_0\sum_i s_i$. The isothermal susceptibility is $\chi_T = (\partial\langle M\rangle/\partial h)_T$, an extensive quantity; $\chi_T/N$ is per spin. Everything below is an equilibrium, static response.

### Response is a derivative of $\ln Z$

With $Z(h) = \text{Tr}\,e^{-\beta(H_0 - hM)}$, the mean is $\langle M\rangle = k_BT\,\partial_h\ln Z$. If $M$ commutes with $H_0$ (classical spins, Ising Hamiltonians, a conserved total spin), a second derivative gives

$$\chi_T = \frac{\partial\langle M\rangle}{\partial h} = \beta\left(\langle M^2\rangle - \langle M\rangle^2\right) = \beta\langle(\Delta M)^2\rangle.$$

When $[M, H_0]\neq0$ the variance is replaced by the Kubo integral $\int_0^\beta d\tau\,\langle\Delta M(\tau)\,\Delta M(0)\rangle$, which reduces to the formula above in the commuting case. The dynamic generalization, the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, relates the response at frequency $\omega$ to the spectrum of fluctuations; we only need the static limit.

### One pattern, three responses

| Response | Definition | Equilibrium fluctuation form |
|---|---|---|
| Heat capacity | $C_V = (\partial\langle E\rangle/\partial T)_{V,N}$ | $\langle(\Delta E)^2\rangle/(k_BT^2)$ |
| Susceptibility | $\chi_T = (\partial\langle M\rangle/\partial h)_T$ | $\langle(\Delta M)^2\rangle/(k_BT)$ |
| Compressibility | $\kappa_T = -\frac1V(\partial V/\partial P)_{T,N}$ | $V\langle(\Delta N)^2\rangle/(k_BT\langle N\rangle^2)$ |

In each row the response to a change of a control parameter equals the variance of the conjugate extensive quantity divided by $k_BT$ (or $k_BT^2$ for temperature). The mixed derivative is a covariance: $(\partial\langle M\rangle/\partial\beta)_h = -\langle\Delta M\,\Delta H\rangle$, with $H = H_0 - hM$ the full Hamiltonian.

### Positivity and exact relations

Variances are non-negative, so $C_V \ge 0$, $\chi_T \ge 0$ and $\kappa_T \ge 0$: these are the conditions of **thermodynamic stability**. Pure thermodynamics links different responses, for example

$$C_P - C_V = \frac{TV\alpha^2}{\kappa_T} \ge 0, \qquad \alpha = \frac1V\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial T}\right)_P,$$

which gives $C_P - C_V = Nk_B$ for the classical ideal gas ($\alpha = 1/T$, $\kappa_T = 1/P$).

### Susceptibility as a correlation sum

For $M = m_0\sum_i s_i$ the variance becomes a double sum over the connected correlation $G_{ij} = \langle s_is_j\rangle - \langle s_i\rangle\langle s_j\rangle$. For a translation-invariant system with $N$ sites,

$$\frac{\chi_T}{N} = \beta m_0^2\sum_r G(r).$$

The susceptibility is the integrated connected correlation, so it diverges when correlations become long-ranged. This is the bridge to critical phenomena (Week 7). For a fluid the analogous identity is the **compressibility sum rule**

$$n k_BT\kappa_T = 1 + n\int d^3r\,[g(r) - 1],$$

where $g(r)$ is the pair distribution function.

### Limits of the relations

The fluctuation form needs the equilibrium state at the field you evaluate it in. In a phase with broken symmetry the order of limits matters: take $N\to\infty$ first, then $h\to0$. The Curie law $\chi\propto1/T$ holds for independent moments only. Finally, the fluctuation formulas are for the isothermal response; adiabatic responses differ by exactly the kind of difference that $C_P - C_V$ expresses.

@example
title: Curie law from a partition function and from a variance
problem: $N$ independent spins with moment $m_0$ have $H = -m_0h\sum_i s_i$. Find $\langle M\rangle$ and $\chi_T$ both by differentiating $\ln Z$ and from the variance of $M$.
step: One spin contributes $2\cosh(\beta m_0h)$, so $Z = [2\cosh(\beta m_0h)]^N$ and $\langle M\rangle = k_BT\,\partial_h\ln Z = Nm_0\tanh(\beta m_0h)$.
step: Differentiate again: $\chi_T = N\beta m_0^2\,\text{sech}^2(\beta m_0h)$. At $h = 0$ this is the Curie law $\chi_T = Nm_0^2/(k_BT)$.
step: Variance route at $h = 0$: the spins are independent with zero mean, so $\langle(\Delta M)^2\rangle = Nm_0^2\langle s^2\rangle = Nm_0^2$, and $\beta\langle(\Delta M)^2\rangle = N\beta m_0^2$. The two routes agree.
step: At large $|h|$ the spins are nearly aligned, the variance of $M$ freezes and $\chi_T\to0$ (saturation). Positivity $\chi_T\ge0$ holds throughout.

@example
title: Susceptibility of the Ising chain from correlations
problem: In the zero-field one-dimensional Ising chain with coupling $J$ and $K = \beta J$, the connected correlation in the thermodynamic limit is $G(r) = (\tanh K)^{|r|}$ (derived next week with a transfer matrix). Find $\chi_T/N$.
step: Use $\chi_T/N = \beta m_0^2\sum_{r=-\infty}^{\infty}G(r)$ with $t = \tanh K$.
step: The sum is $1 + 2\sum_{r\ge1}t^r = 1 + \frac{2t}{1-t} = \frac{1+t}{1-t}$ since $|t|<1$.
step: With $t = \tanh K$, $\frac{1+t}{1-t} = \frac{\cosh K + \sinh K}{\cosh K - \sinh K} = e^{2K}$.
step: So $\chi_T/N = \beta m_0^2\,e^{2\beta J}$. At high temperature this reduces to the Curie law; at low temperature the susceptibility grows exponentially because correlated blocks of spins respond together.

@mistakes
- Concluding that $\chi_T = 0$ because $\langle M\rangle = 0$. The susceptibility depends on the variance of $M$, not its mean.
- Dropping the factor $\beta$ in $\chi_T = \beta\langle(\Delta M)^2\rangle$, or using the total $\langle M^2\rangle$ where the connected variance is needed in an ordered phase.
- Using the fluctuation form for a non-commuting perturbation without the Kubo integral.
- Taking $h\to0$ before $N\to\infty$ in an ordered phase, which gives a different (and wrong) susceptibility.

@quiz num
prompt: A dilute fluid of hard spheres of diameter $\sigma$ has, to lowest order in density, $g(r) = 0$ for $r < \sigma$ and $g(r) = 1$ for $r > \sigma$. Use the compressibility sum rule to find $nk_BT\kappa_T$ for $n\sigma^3 = 0.05$. Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.791
check: 1 + 0.05*quad(lambda r: -4*pi*r**2, 0, 1)
tol: 0.002
hint: Only the region $r<\sigma$ contributes to $\int[g(r)-1]\,d^3r$, where $g - 1 = -1$.
hint: The excluded volume is $\frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma^3$, so the result is $1 - \frac{4\pi}{3}n\sigma^3$.
solution: $\int d^3r\,[g-1] = -\frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma^3$, so $nk_BT\kappa_T = 1 - \frac{4\pi}{3}(0.05) = 1 - 0.209 = 0.791$. The fluid is less compressible than an ideal gas because the hard cores suppress number fluctuations.
