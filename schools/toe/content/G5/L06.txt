@article
**Conventions.** Spins $s_i=\pm1$ on a lattice with $q$ nearest neighbours per site (the coordination number; we write $q$ so as not to confuse it with the fugacity). The Hamiltonian is $H = -J\sum_{\langle ij\rangle}s_is_j - h\sum_i s_i$ with $J>0$, the moment set to $1$, $m = \langle s_i\rangle$ the magnetization per spin and $t = 1 - T/T_c$ the reduced temperature below $T_c$.

### The Weiss argument

Pick one spin $s_i$. It feels its $q$ neighbours through $-Js_i\sum_{j\in\text{nn}(i)}s_j$. **Mean-field theory** replaces each neighbour by its average $m$ and ignores their fluctuations: $s_i$ then sits in the effective field

$$h_{\rm eff} = h + qJm .$$

A spin in a field has $\langle s\rangle = \tanh(\beta h_{\rm eff})$. Demanding that this equals $m$ gives the **self-consistency equation**

$$m = \tanh\!\big[\beta(h + qJm)\big].$$

The unknown appears on both sides because the field depends on the very order it creates.

### Free energy

The equivalent variational (Bragg–Williams) form uses the mean-field free energy per spin, with the entropy of independent spins at magnetization $m$:

$$f(m) = -\tfrac12 qJ m^2 - hm + k_BT\Big[\tfrac{1+m}{2}\ln\tfrac{1+m}{2} + \tfrac{1-m}{2}\ln\tfrac{1-m}{2}\Big].$$

Setting $f'(m) = 0$, with $\frac{d}{dm}[\cdots] = \frac12\ln\frac{1+m}{1-m} = \text{artanh}\,m$, reproduces the self-consistency equation, and the physical solution minimizes $f$.

### Consequences at $h=0$

Expanding $\tanh x = x - x^3/3 + \cdots$ at $h=0$ and writing $a = T_c/T$ with

$$k_BT_c = qJ,$$

the equation becomes $m(1-a) = -a^3m^3/3$. For $T>T_c$ the only solution is $m=0$. For $T<T_c$ there is also

$$m^2 = \frac{3(a-1)}{a^3}\ \approx\ 3\,(1 - T/T_c),\qquad\text{so } m\propto t^{1/2} .$$

The order-parameter exponent is $\beta_{\rm MF} = 1/2$. Differentiating the equation with respect to $h$ gives the susceptibility per spin

$$\chi = \frac{\beta(1-m^2)}{1 - \beta qJ(1-m^2)} \;\xrightarrow{\,T>T_c\,}\; \frac{1}{k_B(T - T_c)} \quad\text{(Curie–Weiss)},$$

so $\gamma_{\rm MF} = 1$, and below $T_c$ the amplitude is half as large. On the critical isotherm $m = \tanh(\beta h + m)$ gives $m\simeq(3\beta h)^{1/3}$, i.e. $\delta_{\rm MF} = 3$. The energy per spin $-\frac12qJm^2$ makes the heat capacity jump by $\frac32k_B$ per spin on cooling through $T_c$.

### Testing the limits

Mean-field theory treats the environment of each spin as a number and discards fluctuations, so it should improve when each spin has many neighbours and degrade in low dimension:

| Lattice | $q$ | MF $k_BT_c/J$ | Exact $k_BT_c/J$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chain | 2 | 2 | no transition |
| Square | 4 | 4 | 2.269 |
| Simple cubic | 6 | 6 | 4.511 (series estimate) |

The error shrinks with dimension; it is exactly zero for the Curie–Weiss model, where every spin couples equally to all others ($J_{ij} = J/N$). The same replacement of the many-body environment by an average gives the **van der Waals equation** for fluids, $P = k_BT/(v-b) - a/v^2$, with $k_BT_c = 8a/(27b)$. Week 7 explains why the exponents are right only above four dimensions.

@example
title: Critical exponent from the self-consistency equation
problem: At $h=0$ show that mean-field theory gives $m\propto(1 - T/T_c)^{1/2}$ just below $T_c$, and find the amplitude.
step: Set $a = T_c/T$. At $h=0$ the equation is $m = \tanh(am)$.
step: Near $T_c$, $m$ is small, so $\tanh(am)\approx am - (am)^3/3$. Then $m = am - a^3m^3/3$.
step: Divide by $m\ne0$: $a^3m^2/3 = a - 1$, so $m^2 = 3(a-1)/a^3 = 3(T_c - T)T^2/T_c^3$.
step: As $T\to T_c$, $T^2/T_c^2\to1$, so $m^2\simeq3(1 - T/T_c)$ and $m\simeq\sqrt3\,(1-T/T_c)^{1/2}$.
step: The exponent is $\beta_{\rm MF} = 1/2$. The result is real only for $T<T_c$, which is how the equation encodes the transition.

@example
title: Mean-field theory against the exact square-lattice result
problem: Compare the mean-field critical temperature of the square-lattice Ising model with the exact one, which satisfies $\sinh(2J/k_BT_c) = 1$.
step: For the square lattice $q = 4$, so mean-field theory gives $k_BT_c = qJ = 4J$.
step: The exact condition $\sinh x = 1$ with $x = 2J/k_BT_c$ gives $x = \text{arsinh}\,1 = \ln(1+\sqrt2) = 0.8814$.
step: Hence $k_BT_c = 2J/0.8814 = 2.269\,J$.
step: Mean-field theory overestimates $T_c$ by the factor $4/2.269 = 1.76$: ignoring fluctuations makes ordering too easy. In one dimension the same method predicts $k_BT_c = 2J$ although the exact answer is no transition at all.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that $m$ appears on both sides, and solving $m = \tanh(\beta qJ)$ instead of $m = \tanh(\beta qJm)$.
- Double counting bonds: the field on one spin involves all $q$ neighbours, but the energy per spin is $-\frac12qJm^2$ because each bond is shared by two spins.
- Accepting the trivial solution $m=0$ below $T_c$; it exists but is a maximum of $f(m)$, not the minimum.
- Treating mean-field exponents as universal facts. They are exact only above the upper critical dimension.
