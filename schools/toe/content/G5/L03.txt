@article
**Conventions.** Free non-relativistic particles of mass $m$ and spin degeneracy $g$ ($g=2$ for electrons, $g=1$ for spinless bosons such as $^4$He) sit in a box of volume $V$ with energies $\varepsilon = \hbar^2k^2/2m$. The fugacity is $z = e^{\beta\mu}$, the thermal wavelength is $\lambda = h/\sqrt{2\pi m k_BT}$ and $n = N/V$. Quantum effects matter when $n\lambda^3/g$ is not small.

### Occupation numbers

Non-interacting modes are independent, so $\Xi = \prod_k\Xi_k$. For one mode of energy $\varepsilon$, put $x = ze^{-\beta\varepsilon}$:

- fermions (occupation $0$ or $1$): $\Xi_k = 1 + x$;
- bosons (occupation $0,1,2,\dots$): $\Xi_k = \sum_n x^n = 1/(1-x)$, which needs $\mu < \varepsilon_{\min}$.

The mean occupation is $\langle n\rangle = z\,\partial_z\ln\Xi_k$:

$$\langle n\rangle = \frac{1}{e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)} \pm 1} \qquad (+\text{ Fermi–Dirac},\ -\text{ Bose–Einstein}),$$

and the variance $k_BT\,\partial\langle n\rangle/\partial\mu$ equals $\langle n\rangle(1\mp\langle n\rangle)$. Pauli blocking suppresses fermion fluctuations below the Poisson value; for bosons the factor $1+\langle n\rangle$ is Bose enhancement. For $\mu \ll -k_BT$ both reduce to the classical $\langle n\rangle \simeq e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon)}$.

### Thermodynamics in three dimensions

Replace sums by integrals with the density of states $D(\varepsilon) = \frac{gV}{4\pi^2}\left(\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}\right)^{3/2}\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. For fermions, with $f_\nu(z) = \sum_{l\ge1}(-1)^{l-1}z^l/l^\nu$,

$$n\lambda^3 = g\,f_{3/2}(z), \qquad \frac{P\lambda^3}{k_BT} = g\,f_{5/2}(z), \qquad PV = \tfrac23E,$$

and for bosons the same with $b_\nu(z) = \sum_{l\ge1}z^l/l^\nu$. The relation $PV = \frac23E$ holds for any non-relativistic ideal gas. Expanding in $z$ gives the first quantum correction

$$\frac{P}{nk_BT} = 1 \pm \frac{n\lambda^3}{2^{5/2}g} + \cdots \qquad (+\text{ fermions},\ -\text{ bosons}).$$

With no interaction at all, statistics alone raises the pressure of fermions and lowers that of bosons.

### Degenerate Fermi gas

At $T=0$ all states below the Fermi energy $\varepsilon_F$ are filled, so $n = \frac{g}{6\pi^2}\left(2m\varepsilon_F/\hbar^2\right)^{3/2}$:

$$\varepsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{6\pi^2 n}{g}\right)^{2/3}, \qquad \frac{E}{N} = \frac35\varepsilon_F, \qquad P = \frac25 n\varepsilon_F.$$

Define $T_F = \varepsilon_F/k_B$. For $T \ll T_F$ only a shell of width $\sim k_BT$ around $\varepsilon_F$ is thermally active, a fraction $\sim T/T_F$ of the particles, each contributing $\sim k_B$. The Sommerfeld expansion makes this precise:

$$C_V = \frac{\pi^2}{2}Nk_B\frac{T}{T_F}, \qquad \mu = \varepsilon_F\left[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\Big(\frac{T}{T_F}\Big)^2 + \cdots\right].$$

$T_F$ is a scale, not a transition temperature: nothing singular happens near it.

### Bose–Einstein condensation

For bosons $n\lambda^3/g = b_{3/2}(z)$ with $z\le1$, and $b_{3/2}(1) = \zeta(3/2) = 2.612$. Once $n\lambda^3 > g\,\zeta(3/2)$ the excited states can hold only $N_{\rm ex} = gV\zeta(3/2)/\lambda^3$ particles; the rest occupy the ground state ($z\to1$, $\mu\to0^-$). This gives the condensation temperature and the condensate fraction

$$k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left(\frac{n}{g\,\zeta(3/2)}\right)^{2/3}, \qquad \frac{N_0}{N} = 1 - \left(\frac{T}{T_c}\right)^{3/2}\ (T<T_c).$$

Below $T_c$ the pressure $P = \frac{gk_BT}{\lambda^3}\zeta(5/2) \propto T^{5/2}$ is independent of density, and $C_V = \frac{15}{4}\frac{\zeta(5/2)}{\zeta(3/2)}Nk_B(T/T_c)^{3/2}$. The ground-state term must be added by hand because the integral over $D(\varepsilon)$ gives it zero weight; a sharp transition exists only in the limit $N\to\infty$ at fixed $n$.

@example
title: Fermi energy of conduction electrons
problem: Conduction electrons have $n = 8.5\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $g = 2$. Take $\hbar = 1.0546\times10^{-34}\ \text{J s}$, $m = 9.109\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}\ \text{J/K}$ and $1\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$. Find $\varepsilon_F$, $T_F$ and $C_V/(Nk_B)$ at $300\ \text{K}$.
step: For $g=2$, $6\pi^2/g = 3\pi^2$, so $\varepsilon_F = \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}(3\pi^2n)^{2/3}$.
step: $3\pi^2n = 2.52\times10^{30}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, so $(3\pi^2n)^{2/3} = 1.85\times10^{20}\ \text{m}^{-2}$. With $\hbar^2/2m = 6.10\times10^{-39}\ \text{J m}^2$ this gives $\varepsilon_F = 1.13\times10^{-18}\ \text{J} = 7.05\ \text{eV}$.
step: $T_F = \varepsilon_F/k_B = 8.18\times10^{4}\ \text{K}$, far above room temperature, so the electron gas is strongly degenerate.
step: At $300\ \text{K}$, $T/T_F = 3.67\times10^{-3}$, so $C_V/(Nk_B) = \frac{\pi^2}{2}(3.67\times10^{-3}) = 0.0181$. A classical gas would give $3/2$: the Fermi gas stores about $80$ times less heat, because only a fraction $\sim T/T_F$ of the electrons can be excited.

@example
title: Condensation temperature of an ideal Bose gas
problem: Treat $^4$He as an ideal Bose gas with $g=1$, $n = 2.18\times10^{28}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ and $m = 6.65\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}$ (same $\hbar$ and $k_B$ as above). Find $T_c$ and the condensate fraction at $2.0\ \text{K}$.
step: $k_BT_c = \frac{2\pi\hbar^2}{m}\left(n/\zeta(3/2)\right)^{2/3}$ with $\zeta(3/2) = 2.612$.
step: $n/\zeta(3/2) = 8.35\times10^{27}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, and its two-thirds power is $4.11\times10^{18}\ \text{m}^{-2}$.
step: $2\pi\hbar^2/(mk_B) = 7.61\times10^{-19}\ \text{m}^2\text{K}$, so $T_c = 7.61\times10^{-19}\times4.11\times10^{18} = 3.13\ \text{K}$.
step: At $2.0\ \text{K}$, $N_0/N = 1 - (2.0/3.13)^{3/2} = 1 - 0.511 = 0.49$. Real liquid $^4$He becomes superfluid at $2.17\ \text{K}$, the same order: interactions shift the value but the condensation mechanism is already visible here.

@mistakes
- Using $+1$ in the denominator for bosons or $-1$ for fermions, or allowing a Bose chemical potential above the lowest level (negative occupation).
- Forgetting the spin degeneracy $g$ in $\varepsilon_F$ and $T_c$ (electrons need $g=2$).
- Applying $N_0/N = 1-(T/T_c)^{3/2}$ above $T_c$, where the condensate fraction is zero in the thermodynamic limit.
- Reading $T_F$ as a critical temperature. It only marks when degeneracy sets in; the Fermi gas has no phase transition there.

@practice num
prompt: If the density of a free electron gas doubles at fixed spin degeneracy, by what factor does the Fermi energy change?
answer: 1.587
check: root(lambda E: (2/3)*E**1.5 - 2*(2/3), 0.1, 10)
tol: 0.005
hint: $\varepsilon_F\propto n^{2/3}$ because the number of filled states grows as $\varepsilon_F^{3/2}$.
hint: The factor is $2^{2/3}$.
solution: The number of states below $\varepsilon$ is proportional to $\varepsilon^{3/2}$, so doubling $n$ multiplies $\varepsilon_F^{3/2}$ by $2$, and $\varepsilon_F$ by $2^{2/3} = 1.587$.

@practice expr
prompt: A fermion level has mean occupation $\bar n = 1/(e^x+1)$ with $x = \beta(\varepsilon-\mu)$. Using $\langle(\Delta n)^2\rangle = k_BT\,\partial\bar n/\partial\mu$, find the variance of the occupation as a function of $x$.
vars: x
lead: $\langle(\Delta n)^2\rangle =$
answer: e^x/(e^x+1)^2
check: lambda x: -nderiv(lambda y: 1/(exp(y)+1), x, 1)
hint: Since $dx/d\mu = -\beta$, the operator $k_BT\,\partial_\mu$ equals $-\partial_x$.
hint: Differentiate $1/(e^x+1)$ with respect to $x$ and change the sign.
solution: $-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{1}{e^x+1} = \frac{e^x}{(e^x+1)^2} = \bar n(1-\bar n)$, the variance of a variable that is $1$ with probability $\bar n$ and $0$ otherwise.

@practice self
prompt: For a single mode of energy $\varepsilon$, derive the mean occupation number from the grand partition function for (a) fermions and (b) bosons. State the condition on $\mu$ for bosons and the classical limit.
hint: A single mode is a one-variable system: sum $x^n$ over its allowed occupations $n$.
hint: Get $\langle n\rangle$ from $x\,\partial_x\ln\Xi$ and check when the geometric series converges.
solution: Let $x = e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon)}$. (a) Occupations $0,1$: $\Xi = 1 + x$, so $\langle n\rangle = x\,\partial_x\ln\Xi = x/(1+x) = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}+1)$. (b) Occupations $0,1,2,\dots$: $\Xi = \sum_{n\ge0}x^n = 1/(1-x)$, convergent only for $x<1$, i.e. $\mu<\varepsilon$ (for the lowest mode $\mu<\varepsilon_{\min}$). Then $\langle n\rangle = x/(1-x) = 1/(e^{\beta(\varepsilon-\mu)}-1)$. Classical limit: if $x\ll1$ both give $\langle n\rangle\simeq x = e^{\beta(\mu-\varepsilon)}$, the Boltzmann factor.

rubric: States that the geometric series for bosons requires $\mu$ below the mode energy.
