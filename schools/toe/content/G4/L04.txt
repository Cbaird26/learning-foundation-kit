@article
Rotations in three dimensions form a non-Abelian group, and quantum mechanics represents that group on the state space. Its generators are the angular momentum operators. This lesson derives their spectrum from commutation relations alone, so that it applies equally to orbital motion, spin and any composite.

### Conventions

Angular momentum operators $J_x,J_y,J_z$ are Hermitian and satisfy
$$[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\,\epsilon_{ijk}J_k,\qquad [\mathbf J^2,J_i]=0 .$$
An **active** rotation of the system by angle $\phi$ about the unit vector $\hat n$ (right-hand rule) is represented by
$$R(\hat n,\phi)=e^{-i\phi\,\hat n\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar}.$$
Vector operators transform as $R^\dagger V_iR=\sum_j\mathcal R_{ij}V_j$, with $\mathcal R$ the ordinary $3\times3$ rotation matrix. Rotations about different axes do not commute, so angles about non-parallel axes do not simply add.

### The spectrum

Choose a simultaneous eigenbasis, $\mathbf J^2|j,m\rangle=\hbar^2j(j+1)|j,m\rangle$ and $J_z|j,m\rangle=\hbar m|j,m\rangle$. With $J_\pm=J_x\pm iJ_y$ one has $[J_z,J_\pm]=\pm\hbar J_\pm$, so $J_\pm$ raise or lower $m$ by one. Because $\lVert J_\pm|j,m\rangle\rVert^2\ge0$, the ladder must terminate; this forces $m\in\{-j,\dots,j\}$ with $j=0,\frac12,1,\frac32,\dots$. The norms are fixed by $J_\mp J_\pm=\mathbf J^2-J_z^2\mp\hbar J_z$, and with the standard (Condon–Shortley) phase convention that the coefficients are real and positive,
$$J_\pm|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m\pm1)}\;|j,m\pm1\rangle .$$
Orbital angular momentum $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ has only integer $l$, because $Y_{lm}(\theta,\varphi)$ must be single-valued. Spin can be integer or half-integer.

### Spin-1/2 and the sign under $2\pi$

For $j=\frac12$, $\mathbf J=\frac\hbar2\boldsymbol\sigma$ and, since $(\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)^2=1$,
$$R(\hat n,\phi)=\cos\frac\phi2-i\sin\frac\phi2\;\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma .$$
Setting $\phi=2\pi$ gives $R=-1$, and $\phi=4\pi$ gives $+1$. A $2\pi$ rotation multiplies a spinor by $-1$. This overall sign is invisible for an isolated state, but it is observable in an interference experiment where one beam is rotated and another is not.

### Rotation matrix elements

Because any rotation is a product of rotations about $z$ and $y$, it suffices to know
$$d^{\,j}_{m'm}(\beta)=\langle j,m'|e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}|j,m\rangle,$$
and then $\langle j,m'|e^{-i\alpha J_z/\hbar}e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}e^{-i\gamma J_z/\hbar}|j,m\rangle=e^{-im'\alpha}d^{\,j}_{m'm}(\beta)e^{-im\gamma}$. Examples, with rows and columns ordered from $m=+j$ downward:
$$d^{1/2}(\beta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\frac\beta2&-\sin\frac\beta2\\ \sin\frac\beta2&\cos\frac\beta2\end{pmatrix},\qquad d^{1}_{11}=\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2},\quad d^{1}_{10}=-\frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2},\quad d^1_{00}=\cos\beta .$$
A direct computation builds $J_y=(J_+-J_-)/2i$ as a matrix from the ladder formula and exponentiates it:

```python
import numpy as np
def jy_matrix(j):                         # hbar = 1, basis m = j, j-1, ..., -j
    m = np.arange(j, -j - 1, -1)
    Jp = np.zeros((len(m), len(m)))
    for k in range(1, len(m)):            # <m+1|J+|m> = sqrt(j(j+1) - m(m+1))
        Jp[k - 1, k] = np.sqrt(j * (j + 1) - m[k] * (m[k] + 1))
    return (Jp - Jp.T) / 2j
def d_matrix(j, beta):
    w, V = np.linalg.eigh(jy_matrix(j))
    return (V * np.exp(-1j * beta * w)) @ V.conj().T
print(np.round(d_matrix(1, np.pi / 3).real, 4))
```

The first row of the printed matrix is $(d^1_{11},d^1_{10},d^1_{1,-1})=(0.75,\,-0.6124,\,0.25)$, matching $\frac{1+\cos\beta}{2}$, $-\sin(\pi/3)/\sqrt2$ and $\frac{1-\cos\beta}{2}$ at $\beta=\pi/3$.

@example
title: Constructing $R_y(\beta)$ for spin 1 and one matrix element
problem: Construct $e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}$ for $j=1$ in the basis $|1,1\rangle,|1,0\rangle,|1,-1\rangle$ and evaluate $\langle1,1|e^{-i\beta J_y/\hbar}|1,0\rangle$ for $\beta=\pi/3$.
step: From the ladder formula, $J_+|1,0\rangle=\sqrt2\hbar|1,1\rangle$ and $J_+|1,-1\rangle=\sqrt2\hbar|1,0\rangle$. So $M\equiv J_y/\hbar=\frac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\ i&0&-i\\0&i&0\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The eigenvalues of $M$ are $0,\pm1$, so $M^3=M$. Hence $e^{-i\beta M}=1-i\sin\beta\,M-(1-\cos\beta)M^2$, which one checks on each eigenvalue.
step: Compute $M^2=\frac12\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&2&0\\-1&0&1\end{pmatrix}$. Its $(1,2)$ entry is $0$, and $M_{12}=-i/\sqrt2$.
step: Therefore $\langle1,1|e^{-i\beta M}|1,0\rangle=-i\sin\beta\cdot\left(-\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\right)=-\frac{\sin\beta}{\sqrt2}$.
step: At $\beta=\pi/3$ this is $-\frac{\sqrt3/2}{\sqrt2}=-\frac{\sqrt6}{4}\approx-0.612$. The probability of finding $m=1$ after the rotation is $\frac{\sin^2\beta}{2}=\frac38$.

@mistakes
- Mixing active and passive conventions: $R=e^{-i\phi\hat n\cdot\mathbf J/\hbar}$ rotates the system; a passive rotation of axes uses the opposite angle.
- Allowing half-integer $l$ for orbital angular momentum, or forgetting that $m$ runs in integer steps from $-j$ to $j$.
- Using $m(m+1)$ in the lowering coefficient; it is $m(m-1)$ for $J_-$.
- Adding rotation angles about different axes as if the rotations commuted.
