@article
Two subsystems with angular momenta $\mathbf J_1$ and $\mathbf J_2$ live in the product space $\mathcal H_1\otimes\mathcal H_2$. The total angular momentum is $\mathbf J=\mathbf J_1\otimes1+1\otimes\mathbf J_2$, and it obeys $[J_i,J_j]=i\hbar\epsilon_{ijk}J_k$, so everything from the previous lesson applies to it. The question is how the product space decomposes into irreducible blocks of definite $j$.

### Two bases

The **uncoupled basis** $|j_1m_1;j_2m_2\rangle$ diagonalizes $\mathbf J_1^2,J_{1z},\mathbf J_2^2,J_{2z}$. The **coupled basis** $|j_1j_2;j\,m\rangle$ diagonalizes $\mathbf J_1^2,\mathbf J_2^2,\mathbf J^2,J_z$. They are related by a real orthogonal matrix, the **Clebsch-Gordan (CG) coefficients**:
$$|j_1j_2;jm\rangle=\sum_{m_1+m_2=m}|j_1m_1;j_2m_2\rangle\,\langle j_1m_1;j_2m_2|jm\rangle .$$
Only terms with $m=m_1+m_2$ contribute, since $J_z=J_{1z}+J_{2z}$.

### Which $j$ occur

The **triangle rule** says $j=|j_1-j_2|,\ |j_1-j_2|+1,\ \dots,\ j_1+j_2$, each exactly once. The dimension count confirms it: $(2j_1+1)(2j_2+1)=\sum_j(2j+1)$. For $1\otimes\frac12$ the values are $\frac32,\frac12$ and $3\cdot2=4+2$.

### Constructing CG coefficients

1. Start from the top state, $|j_1+j_2,\,j_1+j_2\rangle=|j_1j_1;j_2j_2\rangle$, which is unique.
2. Apply $J_-=J_{1-}+J_{2-}$ repeatedly, using $J_-|j,m\rangle=\hbar\sqrt{j(j+1)-m(m-1)}|j,m-1\rangle$, to get all states with that $j$.
3. For the next $j$, the top state with $m=j$ is the combination orthogonal to the states already found; its overall sign is fixed by the **Condon-Shortley convention**: $\langle j_1j_1;j_2(j-j_1)|jj\rangle>0$.
4. Lower again.

CG coefficients have the symmetry $\langle j_1m_1;j_2m_2|jm\rangle=(-1)^{j_1+j_2-j}\langle j_2m_2;j_1m_1|jm\rangle$. For two spin-1/2 particles the triplet ($j=1$) is symmetric under exchange of the particles and the singlet ($j=0$) is antisymmetric:
$$|1,1\rangle=|\!\uparrow\uparrow\rangle,\quad |1,0\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle+|\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle),\quad |1,-1\rangle=|\!\downarrow\downarrow\rangle,\quad |0,0\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle-|\!\downarrow\uparrow\rangle).$$

### Interaction energies

Squaring $\mathbf J=\mathbf J_1+\mathbf J_2$ gives
$$\mathbf J_1\cdot\mathbf J_2=\tfrac12\bigl(\mathbf J^2-\mathbf J_1^2-\mathbf J_2^2\bigr),$$
which is diagonal in the coupled basis with eigenvalue $\frac{\hbar^2}{2}[j(j+1)-j_1(j_1+1)-j_2(j_2+1)]$. For two spin-1/2 particles this is $+\hbar^2/4$ in the triplet and $-3\hbar^2/4$ in the singlet. For spin-orbit coupling with $l=1$, $s=\frac12$, $\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S$ equals $+\hbar^2/2$ for $j=\frac32$ and $-\hbar^2$ for $j=\frac12$, so a term $\xi\,\mathbf L\cdot\mathbf S/\hbar^2$ splits the levels by $\frac32\xi$.

### Operators in the coupled basis

The **Wigner-Eckart theorem** states that the matrix elements of a spherical tensor operator $T^{(k)}_q$ factor into a CG coefficient and a quantity independent of the $m$ values:
$$\langle j'm'|T^{(k)}_q|jm\rangle=\langle jm;kq|j'm'\rangle\,\frac{\langle j'\Vert T^{(k)}\Vert j\rangle}{\sqrt{2j+1}} .$$
(The normalization of the reduced matrix element, here the factor $1/\sqrt{2j+1}$, is convention dependent; check which one a given text uses.) It yields selection rules at once: $m'=m+q$ and $|j-k|\le j'\le j+k$. Because the reduced element is common to all components, ratios of matrix elements within a multiplet depend only on CG coefficients.

@example
title: Coupling an orbital $p$ state to spin-1/2
problem: Construct $|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle$ and $|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle$ for $l=1$, $s=\frac12$ in the basis $|m_l;m_s\rangle$, and find the probability that $m_l=0$ in each.
step: The top state is unique: $|\tfrac32,\tfrac32\rangle=|1;\uparrow\rangle$.
step: Apply $J_-=L_-+S_-$. On the left, $J_-|\tfrac32,\tfrac32\rangle=\sqrt3\hbar|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle$. On the right, $L_-|1\rangle=\sqrt2\hbar|0\rangle$ and $S_-|\!\uparrow\rangle=\hbar|\!\downarrow\rangle$.
step: Hence $|\tfrac32,\tfrac12\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|0;\uparrow\rangle+\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|1;\downarrow\rangle$. The probability of $m_l=0$ is $\frac23$.
step: The state with $j=\frac12$, $m=\frac12$ is orthogonal to this one. The Condon-Shortley sign requires the coefficient of $|m_l=1\rangle$ (the maximal $m_1$) to be positive, so $|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle=\sqrt{\tfrac23}\,|1;\downarrow\rangle-\sqrt{\tfrac13}\,|0;\uparrow\rangle$.
step: The probability of $m_l=0$ in the $j=\frac12$ state is $\frac13$. The two probabilities sum to $1$, as they must since each uncoupled state $|0;\uparrow\rangle$ is shared between the two $j$ values.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the lower bound $|j_1-j_2|$ of the triangle rule, or listing $j$ values in steps other than $1$.
- Treating $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$ as an eigenstate of $\mathbf S^2$; it is an equal-weight superposition of the $S=1,m=0$ and $S=0$ states.
- Violating $m=m_1+m_2$ when writing a CG expansion.
- Comparing CG tables without checking the ordering of $j_1,j_2$ and the Condon-Shortley sign convention.
