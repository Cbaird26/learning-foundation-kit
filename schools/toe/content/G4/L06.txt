@article
Two electrons are not two labelled objects. No measurement can tell "particle 1" from "particle 2", so the labels carry no physical meaning, and the state space must reflect that.

### The symmetrization postulate

Define the exchange operator $P_{12}$ by $P_{12}|a,b\rangle=|b,a\rangle$, where $a,b$ stand for the full single-particle labels (position and spin). Then $P_{12}=P_{12}^\dagger$ and $P_{12}^2=1$, so its eigenvalues are $\pm1$. For identical particles every observable, including $H$, commutes with $P_{12}$, so the symmetry type of a state never changes in time. Nature realizes only one type for each species:

- **bosons** (integer spin): $P_{12}|\Psi\rangle=+|\Psi\rangle$, symmetric;
- **fermions** (half-integer spin): $P_{12}|\Psi\rangle=-|\Psi\rangle$, antisymmetric.

This is the symmetrization postulate; the link to spin is the spin-statistics theorem of relativistic field theory. For $N$ fermions occupying orbitals $\phi_{a_1},\dots,\phi_{a_N}$ the antisymmetric state is the **Slater determinant**
$$\Psi(1,\dots,N)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N!}}\det\bigl[\phi_{a_i}(j)\bigr],$$
which vanishes if two orbitals coincide: the **Pauli exclusion principle**.

### Counting states

With $d$ single-particle states, $N$ distinguishable particles have $d^N$ states, $N$ identical bosons have $\binom{N+d-1}{N}$ states, and $N$ identical fermions have $\binom{d}{N}$ states. A many-particle state is specified by occupation numbers; for fermions each is $0$ or $1$.

### Spin and space together

For two spin-1/2 fermions the total state must be antisymmetric under exchange of *all* labels. The spin singlet is antisymmetric and the triplet is symmetric (previous lesson), so there are two families:
$$\Psi=\psi_{\rm space}^{\,S}\;\chi_{\rm singlet}\qquad\text{or}\qquad \Psi=\psi_{\rm space}^{\,A}\;\chi_{\rm triplet}.$$
Two electrons in the same orbital must therefore be in the singlet.

### Exchange effects

Take orthogonal orbitals $\phi_a,\phi_b$ and the normalized spatial states $\psi_\pm=\frac1{\sqrt2}\bigl[\phi_a(1)\phi_b(2)\pm\phi_b(1)\phi_a(2)\bigr]$. For a one-dimensional coordinate $x$,
$$\langle(x_1-x_2)^2\rangle_\pm=\langle x^2\rangle_a+\langle x^2\rangle_b-2\langle x\rangle_a\langle x\rangle_b\;\mp\;2\,\bigl|\langle a|x|b\rangle\bigr|^2 .$$
The symmetric combination ($\psi_+$, which pairs with the spin singlet, or describes bosons) keeps particles closer together; the antisymmetric one ($\psi_-$, which pairs with the triplet) pushes them apart. This is not a force: it is interference between the two ways of assigning the particles to orbitals. For a repulsive interaction $V$ it makes the energies $E_\pm=J\pm K$ with a direct integral $J$ and an exchange integral $K$, so the spin states acquire an effective interaction proportional to $\mathbf S_1\cdot\mathbf S_2$.

A useful identity for spin-1/2: the spin-exchange operator is $P^{\rm spin}_{12}=\frac12(1+\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2)$, equal to $+1$ on the triplet and $-1$ on the singlet.

@example
title: Singlet and triplet electrons in an infinite well
problem: Two non-interacting spin-1/2 particles occupy orbitals $n=1$ and $n=2$ of an infinite well of width $L=1$. Compare $\langle(x_1-x_2)^2\rangle$ for the spin singlet and the spin triplet.
step: Orbitals are different, so there are four states: a spatially symmetric $\psi_+$ with the singlet (one state) and a spatially antisymmetric $\psi_-$ with the triplet (three states).
step: Single-orbital moments for $\psi_n=\sqrt2\sin n\pi x$: $\langle x\rangle_n=\frac12$ and $\langle x^2\rangle_n=\frac13-\frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}$. The off-diagonal element is $|\langle1|x|2\rangle|=\frac{16}{9\pi^2}\approx0.1801$.
step: The distinguishable-particle part is $A=\langle x^2\rangle_1+\langle x^2\rangle_2-2\cdot\frac14=\frac16-\frac{5}{8\pi^2}\approx0.1033$.
step: The exchange term is $2|\langle1|x|2\rangle|^2=\frac{512}{81\pi^4}\approx0.0649$. Singlet (symmetric space): $A-0.0649\approx0.0384$. Triplet (antisymmetric space): $A+0.0649\approx0.1682$.
step: The difference is $4|\langle1|x|2\rangle|^2=\frac{1024}{81\pi^4}\approx0.130$ in units of $L^2$. The triplet electrons stay farther apart, as the antisymmetric spatial state requires.

@mistakes
- Antisymmetrizing only the spatial part (or only the spin part) of a fermion state instead of the total state.
- Counting boson states as $d^N$ or as $\binom dN$; the boson count is $\binom{N+d-1}{N}$.
- Describing exchange as a new force instead of an interference effect between the two assignments of particles.
- Placing two electrons in the same orbital with a spin triplet; the spin state must be the singlet.

@practice num
prompt: Two spin-1/2 particles are in the product state $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$. Evaluate $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2\rangle$.
answer: -1
check: (lambda sx, sy, sz, v: float(np.real(sum(np.conj(v) @ np.kron(s, s) @ v for s in (sx, sy, sz)))))(np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]), np.array([[0, -1j], [1j, 0]]), np.diag([1.0, -1.0]), np.array([0, 1, 0, 0]))
hint: Use $\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2=2P^{\rm spin}_{12}-1$ or write $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$ in the coupled basis.
solution: $|\!\uparrow\downarrow\rangle$ is an equal mixture of triplet ($\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2=1$) and singlet ($-3$) components, so $\langle\boldsymbol\sigma_1\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma_2\rangle=\frac12(1)+\frac12(-3)=-1$. Directly, only $\sigma_{1z}\sigma_{2z}=-1$ contributes; the transverse terms flip both spins and have zero expectation value.

@quiz num
prompt: Two non-interacting electrons move in a one-dimensional harmonic trap with single-particle energies $(n+\frac12)\hbar\omega$, and each orbital carries two spin states. How many linearly independent two-electron states have total energy $3\hbar\omega$?
answer: 5
check: sum(1 for a in range(6) for b in range(a + 1, 6) if a // 2 + b // 2 == 2)
hint: The total energy is $(n_1+n_2+1)\hbar\omega$, so $n_1+n_2=2$.
hint: Treat the spin-orbitals as four distinct labels per pair of orbitals; two electrons cannot share the same spin-orbital.
solution: The orbital pairs are $(0,2)$ and $(1,1)$. For $(0,2)$ the orbitals differ, so all $2\times2=4$ spin assignments are allowed (singlet plus triplet). For $(1,1)$ both electrons are in the same orbital, so only the singlet is allowed: $1$ state. The total is $4+1=5$.
