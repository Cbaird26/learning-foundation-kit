@article
Scattering is how most of what we know about interactions was measured. A beam of particles of wave number $k$ hits a localized target, and a detector counts what comes out at each angle. We need an operator formulation that connects the Hamiltonian to that count.

### Amplitude and cross section

Consider elastic scattering of a particle of mass $m$ and energy $E=\hbar^2k^2/2m$ from a potential $V(\mathbf r)$ of finite range. A stationary scattering state has the asymptotic form
$$\psi(\mathbf r)\;\longrightarrow\;e^{ikz}+f(\theta,\varphi)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{r}\qquad(r\to\infty),$$
where $\theta$ is measured from the incident direction $\hat z$. The incident wave has unit density and current $\hbar k/m$. The current through a detector of area $r^2d\Omega$ is $\frac{\hbar k}{m}|f|^2d\Omega$. Dividing by the incident flux gives
$$\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=|f(\theta,\varphi)|^2,\qquad \sigma_{\rm tot}=\int|f|^2\,d\Omega .$$
The amplitude $f$ has dimensions of length. Normalizing the incident wave to unit density is what makes $|f|^2$ a cross section directly.

### The Lippmann-Schwinger equation and the T operator

Split $H=H_0+V$ with $H_0=\mathbf p^2/2m$. The scattering state with outgoing waves solves
$$|\psi^{(+)}\rangle=|\mathbf k\rangle+G_0^{(+)}(E)\,V\,|\psi^{(+)}\rangle,\qquad G_0^{(+)}(E)=\frac{1}{E-H_0+i\epsilon},\quad\epsilon\to0^+ .$$
The $+i\epsilon$ selects outgoing rather than incoming waves. In position space $\langle\mathbf r|G_0^{(+)}|\mathbf r'\rangle=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\frac{e^{ik|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}}{|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|}$. Define the **T operator** by $V|\psi^{(+)}\rangle=T|\mathbf k\rangle$, which satisfies
$$T=V+V\,G_0^{(+)}\,T=V+VG_0^{(+)}V+VG_0^{(+)}VG_0^{(+)}V+\cdots .$$
For large $r$ use $|\mathbf r-\mathbf r'|\approx r-\hat r\cdot\mathbf r'$ and read off the coefficient of $e^{ikr}/r$. With the final wave vector $\mathbf k'=k\hat r$ and plane waves normalized as $\langle\mathbf r|\mathbf k\rangle=e^{i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}/(2\pi)^{3/2}$,
$$f(\mathbf k',\mathbf k)=-\frac{4\pi^2m}{\hbar^2}\,\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle,\qquad |\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k| .$$
Only *on-shell* matrix elements of $T$ (equal energies before and after) matter.

### Born approximation

Keeping the first term, $T\approx V$, gives
$$f^{(1)}(\mathbf q)=-\frac{m}{2\pi\hbar^2}\int V(\mathbf r)\,e^{-i\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf r}\,d^3r,\qquad \mathbf q=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k,\quad q=2k\sin\frac\theta2 .$$
For a central potential this reduces to
$$f^{(1)}(q)=-\frac{2m}{\hbar^2q}\int_0^\infty rV(r)\sin(qr)\,dr .$$
The Born amplitude is the Fourier transform of the potential, so it is real for a real, spherically symmetric $V$. It is reliable when the potential is weak enough that the wavefunction inside the range is close to a plane wave.

### Unitarity and the optical theorem

Probability conservation makes the S operator unitary; on the energy shell $S=1-2\pi i\,\delta(E-H_0)T$ in units $\hbar=1$. Its consequence for the forward amplitude is the **optical theorem**
$$\sigma_{\rm tot}=\frac{4\pi}{k}\,{\rm Im}\,f(\theta=0).$$
It says that the total scattering removes flux from the forward direction through interference. Because $f^{(1)}$ is real, the first Born approximation cannot satisfy it by itself; the imaginary part of $f(0)$ first appears at second order in $V$.

@example
title: Born amplitude for a screened Coulomb potential
problem: Find the first-Born amplitude and differential cross section for $V(r)=V_0\,e^{-\mu r}/r$ and take the limit $\mu\to0$.
step: For a central potential, $f^{(1)}(q)=-\frac{2m}{\hbar^2q}\int_0^\infty rV(r)\sin(qr)\,dr$. Here $rV(r)=V_0e^{-\mu r}$.
step: The integral is $\int_0^\infty e^{-\mu r}\sin(qr)\,dr=\frac{q}{\mu^2+q^2}$, so $f^{(1)}(q)=-\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2(\mu^2+q^2)}$.
step: With $q^2=4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2)$, the cross section is $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\left(\frac{2mV_0}{\hbar^2}\right)^2\frac{1}{(\mu^2+4k^2\sin^2(\theta/2))^2}$.
step: For $\mu\to0$, and $\hbar^2k^2=2mE$, this becomes $\frac{d\sigma}{d\Omega}=\left(\frac{V_0}{4E\sin^2(\theta/2)}\right)^2$, the Rutherford form, which diverges as $\theta\to0$ because the Coulomb range is infinite.

@mistakes
- Using $\mathbf k'+\mathbf k$ instead of $\mathbf q=\mathbf k'-\mathbf k$, or forgetting $q=2k\sin(\theta/2)$.
- Losing the overall $-m/2\pi\hbar^2$ factor; its sign does not change the first Born cross section but matters for interference and the optical theorem.
- Applying the optical theorem with ${\rm Re}\,f(0)$, or without the $4\pi/k$ factor.
- Using the Born approximation for strong or resonant scattering.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about the T operator and the Born series.
choice*: The first Born approximation replaces $T$ by $V$.
choice*: The scattering amplitude is proportional to the on-shell matrix element $\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ with $|\mathbf k'|=|\mathbf k|$.
choice*: The prescription $E\to E+i\epsilon$ in $G_0^{(+)}$ selects outgoing spherical waves.
choice: For a real spherically symmetric potential, the first Born amplitude $f^{(1)}$ has a nonzero imaginary part in the forward direction.
hint: The first Born amplitude is a Fourier transform of a real, spherically symmetric function.
solution: The statements about the first Born approximation, the on-shell matrix element and the $+i\epsilon$ prescription are standard: $T=V+VG_0T$ starts with $V$; $f\propto\langle\mathbf k'|T|\mathbf k\rangle$ on shell; and $+i\epsilon$ gives $e^{+ikr}/r$. The statement about a nonzero imaginary part of $f^{(1)}$ is false: the Fourier transform of a real, even function is real, so ${\rm Im}f^{(1)}=0$ and the optical theorem is satisfied only after including second order.
