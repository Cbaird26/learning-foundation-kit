@article
Few Hamiltonians can be solved exactly, so most of quantum mechanics is the art of controlled approximation. The skill is to know, for each method, what is being assumed and what is being guaranteed.

### Nondegenerate perturbation theory

Write $H=H_0+\lambda V$ with known discrete levels $H_0|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$ and let $V_{mn}=\langle m|V|n\rangle$. For a nondegenerate level $n$ the Rayleigh-Schrödinger series is
$$E_n(\lambda)=E_n+\lambda V_{nn}+\lambda^2\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{|V_{mn}|^2}{E_n-E_m}+O(\lambda^3),$$
and the state is corrected by $|n\rangle+\lambda\sum_{m\ne n}\frac{V_{mn}}{E_n-E_m}|m\rangle$ (normalized to first order; the correction is orthogonal to $|n\rangle$). The method is trustworthy when $|\lambda V_{mn}|\ll|E_n-E_m|$. Two facts are worth remembering: the second-order shift of the ground state is always negative, and levels repel each other.

### Degenerate perturbation theory

If the level $E_n$ is $g$-fold degenerate, the denominators blow up and the method above fails. Instead, **diagonalize $V$ within the degenerate subspace**: the first-order energies are the eigenvalues of the $g\times g$ matrix $V_{ab}=\langle a|V|b\rangle$ and the correct zeroth-order states are its eigenvectors. A symmetry that commutes with both $H_0$ and $V$ often block-diagonalizes the problem and selects these "good" states in advance.

### The variational principle

For any normalizable $|\psi\rangle$,
$$E[\psi]=\frac{\langle\psi|H|\psi\rangle}{\langle\psi|\psi\rangle}\;\ge\;E_0 .$$
Minimizing $E[\psi]$ over a family of trial states gives the best upper bound in that family. If the trial state is orthogonal to the exact ground state, the same inequality holds with $E_1$. The bound is rigorous, but the size of the error is not controlled by the method. The energy error is second order in the error of the trial state, so energies are usually far more accurate than other quantities computed from the same trial state.

### WKB

For a slowly varying potential, the semiclassical (WKB) quantization condition for a bound state between two smooth turning points is
$$\oint p\,dx=2\pi\hbar\left(n+\tfrac12\right),\qquad p(x)=\sqrt{2m\,[E-V(x)]},\qquad n=0,1,2,\dots$$
For the harmonic oscillator the closed orbit in phase space is an ellipse of area $2\pi E/\omega$, so the condition reproduces $E_n=\hbar\omega(n+\frac12)$ exactly. WKB improves as $n$ grows; it fails near the turning points unless the connection formulas (the $+\frac12$ above) are used.

### Perturbed oscillator facts (units $\hbar=m=\omega=1$)

With $x=(a+a^\dagger)/\sqrt2$, $\langle0|x^4|0\rangle=\frac34$ and $x^3|0\rangle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\bigl(3|1\rangle+\sqrt6|3\rangle\bigr)$. For a cubic term $\lambda x^3$ the first-order shift vanishes by parity and the second-order shift of level $n$ is $-\frac{\lambda^2}{8}(30n^2+30n+11)$. These perturbation series are asymptotic, not convergent: they work for small $\lambda$ at low order.

@example
title: Second-order shift from a cubic term
problem: A harmonic oscillator (units $\hbar=m=\omega=1$) has $H=\frac12p^2+\frac12x^2+\lambda x^3$. Find the ground-state energy through order $\lambda^2$.
step: The first-order shift is $\lambda\langle0|x^3|0\rangle=0$, because $x^3$ is odd and $|0\rangle$ is even.
step: With $x=(a+a^\dagger)/\sqrt2$, repeated action gives $x^3|0\rangle=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}(3|1\rangle+\sqrt6|3\rangle)$, so $|V_{10}|^2=\frac98$ and $|V_{30}|^2=\frac68=\frac34$.
step: The denominators are $E_0-E_1=-1$ and $E_0-E_3=-3$.
step: $E^{(2)}=\lambda^2\left[\frac{9/8}{-1}+\frac{3/4}{-3}\right]=-\lambda^2\left[\frac98+\frac14\right]=-\frac{11}{8}\lambda^2$.
step: Therefore $E_0=\frac12-\frac{11}{8}\lambda^2+O(\lambda^3)$, below the unperturbed energy, as expected for a ground state.

@example
title: A variational estimate for a particle in a box
problem: Estimate the ground-state energy of a particle in a box on $[0,1]$ (units $\hbar=m=1$) using the trial function $\psi=x(1-x)$ and compare it with the exact value $\pi^2/2$.
step: The trial function vanishes at both walls, as required, and is normalizable.
step: Compute $\int_0^1\psi^2dx=\int_0^1x^2(1-x)^2dx=\frac1{30}$ and $\int_0^1\psi'^2dx=\int_0^1(1-2x)^2dx=\frac13$.
step: Inside the box $H=-\frac12\frac{d^2}{dx^2}$, so $E[\psi]=\frac12\cdot\frac{1/3}{1/30}=5$.
step: The exact ground-state energy is $\pi^2/2\approx4.9348$, so the estimate is $1.3\%$ high, consistent with the bound $E[\psi]\ge E_0$.

@mistakes
- Using the nondegenerate formulas when a level is degenerate; instead diagonalize $V$ in the degenerate subspace.
- Including $m=n$ in the second-order sum, or reversing the sign of the denominator ($E_n-E_m$, not $E_m-E_n$).
- Treating a variational result as an estimate with a known error rather than as an upper bound.
- Using a trial function that violates the boundary conditions or is not normalizable.

prompt: In units $\hbar=m=\omega=1$, a harmonic oscillator is perturbed by $V=\lambda x^4$ with $\lambda=0.1$. Find the first-order shift of the ground-state energy.
answer: 3/40
check: 0.1 * quad(lambda x: x ** 4 * np.exp(-x ** 2) / np.sqrt(np.pi), -12, 12)
hint: First-order shift is $\langle0|\lambda x^4|0\rangle$ with $|\psi_0|^2=e^{-x^2}/\sqrt\pi$.
hint: For a Gaussian density with variance $\sigma^2=\frac12$, $\langle x^4\rangle=3\sigma^4$.
solution: $\langle x^4\rangle=3\cdot\left(\frac12\right)^2=\frac34$, so $E^{(1)}=0.1\cdot\frac34=\frac3{40}=0.075$.

@practice mc
prompt: A level of $H_0$ is twofold degenerate with states $|a\rangle,|b\rangle$. A perturbation $V$ is added. What gives the first-order energy shifts?
choice*: The eigenvalues of the $2\times2$ matrix $\langle i|V|j\rangle$ with $i,j\in\{a,b\}$.
choice: The diagonal elements $\langle a|V|a\rangle$ and $\langle b|V|b\rangle$ in whatever basis is convenient.
choice: The sum $\sum_{m}|V_{ma}|^2/(E_a-E_m)$ over all other levels.
choice: The eigenvalues of $V$ in the full Hilbert space.
hint: The perturbation may mix $|a\rangle$ and $|b\rangle$ at zeroth order, because they have the same energy.
solution: In a degenerate subspace the zeroth-order states are not fixed by $H_0$; $V$ chooses them. One diagonalizes the restriction of $V$ to the subspace. The diagonal elements in an arbitrary basis are correct only if $V$ is diagonal there; the sum over intermediate states is a second-order quantity.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle in the potential $V(x)=-g\,\delta(x)$ has the exact ground-state energy $-mg^2/(2\hbar^2)$. Use the Gaussian trial function $\psi=e^{-\alpha x^2}$ and minimize over $\alpha$. What is the ratio $E_{\rm var}/E_{\rm exact}$?
answer: 2/pi
tol: 0.0001
check: (lambda E: min(E(a) for a in np.linspace(0.05, 2.0, 1001)) / (-0.5))(lambda a: (0.5 * quad(lambda x: (2 * a * x * np.exp(-a * x ** 2)) ** 2, -12, 12) - 1.0) / quad(lambda x: np.exp(-2 * a * x ** 2), -12, 12))
hint: Use units $\hbar=m=g=1$. Then $\langle T\rangle=\alpha/2$ and $\langle V\rangle=-\psi(0)^2/\int\psi^2=-\sqrt{2\alpha/\pi}$.
hint: Minimize $E(\alpha)=\alpha/2-\sqrt{2\alpha/\pi}$ and divide by $-\frac12$.
solution: $E(\alpha)=\frac\alpha2-\sqrt{\frac{2\alpha}\pi}$ has $E'(\alpha)=\frac12-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}}$, which vanishes when $\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}=2$, that is $\alpha=\frac2\pi$. Then $E_{\min}=\frac1\pi-\sqrt{\frac{4}{\pi^2}}=-\frac1\pi$. The exact value is $-\frac12$, so the ratio is $\frac{2}{\pi}\approx0.637$: the Gaussian captures $64\%$ of the binding energy.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements.
choice*: For any normalizable trial state, $\langle H\rangle/\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ is at least the ground-state energy.
choice*: The second-order perturbative shift of a nondegenerate ground state is negative.
choice*: The WKB condition $\oint p\,dx=2\pi\hbar(n+\frac12)$ gives the exact levels of the harmonic oscillator.
choice: The first-order shift of a nondegenerate level equals $\sum_{m\ne n}|V_{mn}|^2/(E_n-E_m)$.
hint: The sum $\sum_{m\ne n}|V_{mn}|^2/(E_n-E_m)$ has two matrix elements in every term, so it is of second order in the perturbation.
solution: The statements about the variational bound, the negative second-order ground-state shift and the WKB levels of the oscillator are true (variational bound; every term of the second-order ground-state sum has $E_0-E_m<0$; the phase-space ellipse has area $2\pi E/\omega$). The statement that the first-order shift equals $\sum_{m\ne n}|V_{mn}|^2/(E_n-E_m)$ is false: that sum is the second-order shift, and the first-order shift is $V_{nn}$.
