@article
Quantum mechanics rests on three ingredients: a space of states, a rule that assigns operators to measurable quantities, and a rule that turns the two into probabilities. This lesson fixes all three, with every convention stated, because every later lesson builds on them.

### Conventions

The state space is a complex Hilbert space $\mathcal H$; in this lesson it is finite-dimensional, so there are no domain subtleties. The inner product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ is **linear in the second slot and antilinear in the first**: $\langle\phi|c\psi\rangle = c\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ but $\langle c\phi|\psi\rangle = c^*\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. Forming a bra from a ket therefore **conjugates its components**. A physical state is a **ray**: a unit vector $|\psi\rangle$ with $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$, where $|\psi\rangle$ and $e^{i\alpha}|\psi\rangle$ describe the same state. A global phase is unobservable; the relative phase between components is observable.

### Observables and the Born rule

An observable is a Hermitian operator, $A^\dagger = A$. The spectral theorem writes
$$A=\sum_a a\,P_a,\qquad P_a^\dagger=P_a=P_a^2,\qquad \sum_a P_a = 1,$$
where $P_a$ projects onto the eigenspace of eigenvalue $a$. A degenerate eigenvalue has a projector of rank greater than one. For a measurement of $A$ on a system in state $|\psi\rangle$:

- the only possible results are the eigenvalues $a$;
- the probability of result $a$ is $p(a)=\langle\psi|P_a|\psi\rangle=\lVert P_a|\psi\rangle\rVert^2$ (the Born rule);
- immediately afterwards the state is $P_a|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p(a)}$ (projective, or Lüders, update).

It follows that $\langle A\rangle=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle=\sum_a a\,p(a)$ and that the variance is $(\Delta A)^2=\langle A^2\rangle-\langle A\rangle^2$. An eigenstate has $\Delta A=0$.

### Spin-1/2 as the working example

Take the basis $|\uparrow\rangle,|\downarrow\rangle$ of eigenvectors of $\sigma_z$ with eigenvalues $+1,-1$. The Pauli matrices are
$$\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad \sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix},$$
and the spin operators are $S_j=\tfrac{\hbar}{2}\sigma_j$. Every pure qubit state can be written $|\psi\rangle=\cos\frac\theta2|\uparrow\rangle+e^{i\varphi}\sin\frac\theta2|\downarrow\rangle$, and then
$$\langle\boldsymbol\sigma\rangle=(\sin\theta\cos\varphi,\ \sin\theta\sin\varphi,\ \cos\theta),$$
a unit vector called the Bloch vector. Since $\sigma_j^2=1$, the variance is $(\Delta\sigma_j)^2=1-\langle\sigma_j\rangle^2$.

### Compatibility and uncertainty

Two observables are **compatible** when they commute, $[A,B]=AB-BA=0$. Then they have a common eigenbasis, and measuring one does not disturb the statistics of the other. If they do not commute, no state is an eigenstate of both in general. The **Robertson relation** holds in every state:
$$\Delta A\,\Delta B\;\ge\;\tfrac12\bigl|\langle[A,B]\rangle\bigr|.$$
It bounds the spread of results over many identically prepared systems; it is not a statement about the accuracy of a single measurement. The right-hand side depends on the state. For $\sigma_x,\sigma_z$ we have $[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]=-2i\sigma_y$, so the bound is $|\langle\sigma_y\rangle|$, which is zero for any state whose Bloch vector lies in the $x$-$z$ plane, even though such a state is an eigenstate of neither operator in general.

Finally, a Hermitian operator is determined by its eigenvalues and eigenspaces, but a *list of probabilities* is not determined by the eigenvalues alone: the state matters. Always identify the eigenprojectors first, then apply the Born rule.

@example
title: Probabilities, expectation value and projective update
problem: A spin-1/2 is in the state $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle+2i|\downarrow\rangle\bigr)$. Find the probabilities of the two results of measuring $S_y$, the expectation value $\langle S_y\rangle$, and the state after the result $+\hbar/2$.
step: Check normalization: $\frac15(|1|^2+|2i|^2)=\frac15(1+4)=1$.
step: The eigenvectors of $\sigma_y$ are $|\pm y\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|\uparrow\rangle\pm i|\downarrow\rangle)$ with eigenvalues $\pm1$. For example $\sigma_y(1,i)^T=(-i\cdot i,\ i\cdot1)^T=(1,i)^T$.
step: The bra $\langle +y|=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle\uparrow|-i\langle\downarrow|)$ has the conjugated coefficient. So $\langle+y|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\bigl(1+(-i)(2i)\bigr)=\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}$ and $p(+)=\frac{9}{10}$.
step: Likewise $\langle-y|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\bigl(1+(i)(2i)\bigr)=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}$, so $p(-)=\frac1{10}$. The probabilities sum to $1$.
step: $\langle S_y\rangle=\frac\hbar2\left(\frac{9}{10}-\frac{1}{10}\right)=\frac{2\hbar}{5}$. After the result $+\hbar/2$ the state is $P_+|\psi\rangle/\sqrt{p(+)}=|{+y}\rangle$ up to a global phase.

@example
title: Testing the uncertainty relation
problem: For the spin-1/2 state $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\bigl(|\uparrow\rangle+2i|\downarrow\rangle\bigr)$, compute $\Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_z$ and compare it with $\frac12|\langle[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]\rangle|$.
step: Components $(c_\uparrow,c_\downarrow)=(1,2i)/\sqrt5$ give $\langle\sigma_x\rangle=2\,\mathrm{Re}(c_\uparrow^*c_\downarrow)=0$ and $\langle\sigma_z\rangle=\frac15-\frac45=-\frac35$.
step: Because $\sigma_j^2=1$, $(\Delta\sigma_x)^2=1-0=1$ and $(\Delta\sigma_z)^2=1-\frac{9}{25}=\frac{16}{25}$. The product is $\Delta\sigma_x\Delta\sigma_z=\frac45$.
step: Since $[\sigma_x,\sigma_z]=-2i\sigma_y$ and $\langle\sigma_y\rangle=2\,\mathrm{Im}(c_\uparrow^*c_\downarrow)=2\cdot\frac{2}{5}=\frac45$, the bound is $\frac12\cdot2\cdot\frac45=\frac45$. The relation holds with equality for this state.

@mistakes
- Forming a bra without conjugating the coefficients, for instance using $+i$ instead of $-i$ in $\langle+y|$.
- Treating a relative phase as unobservable, or a global phase as observable: only the global phase drops out.
- Assuming any two Hermitian operators have a common eigenbasis; this holds only for commuting operators.
- Reporting the amplitude $\langle a|\psi\rangle$ as the probability instead of its squared modulus, or summing over only one vector of a degenerate eigenspace.

prompt: A spin-1/2 is in the state $\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(|\uparrow\rangle+2|\downarrow\rangle)$. Find $\langle\sigma_x\rangle$.
answer: 4/5
check: (lambda v: float(np.real(np.conj(v) @ np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]) @ v)))(np.array([1, 2]) / np.sqrt(5))
hint: $\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\langle\psi|\sigma_x|\psi\rangle$, and $\sigma_x$ swaps the two components.
hint: For real components $(c_1,c_2)$ the result is $2c_1c_2$.
solution: $\sigma_x|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt5}(2|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle)$, so $\langle\psi|\sigma_x|\psi\rangle=\frac15(1\cdot2+2\cdot1)=\frac45$.

@practice num
prompt: A spin-1/2 is in the state $\cos\frac\pi8|\uparrow\rangle+\sin\frac\pi8|\downarrow\rangle$. What is the probability that a measurement of $\sigma_x$ gives $+1$?
answer: (2+sqrt(2))/4
check: (lambda w, V: abs(np.conj(V[:, 1]) @ np.array([np.cos(np.pi / 8), np.sin(np.pi / 8)])) ** 2)(*np.linalg.eigh(np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]])))
hint: The $+1$ eigenvector of $\sigma_x$ is $(|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle)/\sqrt2$.
hint: The probability is $\frac12(\cos\frac\pi8+\sin\frac\pi8)^2$; expand the square.
solution: $p=\frac12(\cos\frac\pi8+\sin\frac\pi8)^2=\frac12(1+2\sin\frac\pi8\cos\frac\pi8)=\frac12\bigl(1+\sin\frac\pi4\bigr)=\frac{2+\sqrt2}{4}\approx0.854$.

@practice mc
prompt: Let $A$ and $B$ be Hermitian operators. The product $AB$ is itself Hermitian if and only if:
choice*: $A$ and $B$ commute, $[A,B]=0$.
choice: $A$ and $B$ anticommute, $AB=-BA$.
choice: $A$ and $B$ have the same eigenvalues.
choice: Always, since a product of observables is an observable.
hint: Take the adjoint of the product and reverse the order.
solution: $(AB)^\dagger=B^\dagger A^\dagger=BA$, so $AB$ is Hermitian exactly when $AB=BA$. If $A$ and $B$ anticommute, $(AB)^\dagger=BA=-AB$ and $AB$ is anti-Hermitian; for example $\sigma_x\sigma_y=i\sigma_z$.

@practice num
prompt: An observable has the matrix $A=\begin{pmatrix}2&1&0\\1&2&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$ and the system is in the state $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,0,1)^T$. What is the probability that a measurement of $A$ gives the value $1$?
answer: 3/4
check: (lambda A, psi: (lambda w, V: float(sum(abs(np.conj(V[:, k]) @ psi) ** 2 for k in range(3) if abs(w[k] - 1) < 1e-9)))(*np.linalg.eigh(A)))(np.array([[2, 1, 0], [1, 2, 0], [0, 0, 1]]), np.array([1, 0, 1]) / np.sqrt(2))
hint: Find the eigenvalues first. The upper block $\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\1&2\end{pmatrix}$ has eigenvalues $3$ and $1$.
hint: The value $1$ is degenerate, with eigenvectors $(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$ and $(0,0,1)$. Add the probabilities for the whole eigenspace.
solution: The eigenspace of $1$ is spanned by $(1,-1,0)/\sqrt2$ and $(0,0,1)$. The overlaps with $\psi$ are $\frac12$ and $\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so $p=\frac14+\frac12=\frac34$. Equivalently $p=1-p(3)$ with $p(3)=|\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt2}|^2=\frac14$.

@practice num
prompt: A spin-1/2 is in the state $\cos\frac\pi6|\uparrow\rangle+\sin\frac\pi6|\downarrow\rangle$. Compute the product of standard deviations $\Delta\sigma_x\,\Delta\sigma_z$. (The Robertson bound for this state is $|\langle\sigma_y\rangle|$.)
answer: sqrt(3)/4
check: (lambda v, sx, sz: (lambda sd: sd(sx) * sd(sz))(lambda M: float(np.sqrt(np.real(np.conj(v) @ M @ M @ v - (np.conj(v) @ M @ v) ** 2)))))(np.array([np.cos(np.pi / 6), np.sin(np.pi / 6)]), np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]), np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]]))
hint: This is the Bloch-vector state with $\theta=\pi/3$, $\varphi=0$, so $\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\sin\theta$ and $\langle\sigma_z\rangle=\cos\theta$.
hint: Use $(\Delta\sigma_j)^2=1-\langle\sigma_j\rangle^2$ for each operator, then multiply the square roots.
solution: $\langle\sigma_x\rangle=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}$ and $\langle\sigma_z\rangle=\frac12$, so $(\Delta\sigma_x)^2=\frac14$ and $(\Delta\sigma_z)^2=\frac34$. The product is $\sqrt{3/16}=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\approx0.433$. The bound is $|\langle\sigma_y\rangle|=0$, so the inequality is strict here.

@quiz num
prompt: A spin-1/2 is prepared in $|\uparrow\rangle$. Its spin is first measured along the axis $\hat n=(\sin\frac\pi3,0,\cos\frac\pi3)$ and the result $+1$ is obtained. The spin is then measured along $z$. What is the probability of the sequence "$+1$ along $\hat n$, then $-1$ along $z$"?
answer: 3/16
check: (lambda n, sx, sz, I: (lambda Pn, Pdown, up: float(np.linalg.norm(Pdown @ Pn @ up) ** 2))((I + n[0] * sx + n[2] * sz) / 2, (I - sz) / 2, np.array([1, 0])))((np.sin(np.pi / 3), 0, np.cos(np.pi / 3)), np.array([[0, 1], [1, 0]]), np.array([[1, 0], [0, -1]]), np.eye(2))
hint: The projector onto $\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma=+1$ is $P_n=\frac12(1+\hat n\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma)$. After the first result the state is projected.
hint: The probability of the sequence is $\lVert P_{\downarrow}P_n|\uparrow\rangle\rVert^2$, which equals $p(+n)$ times the conditional probability of $-1$ along $z$.
solution: The state along $\hat n$ is $\cos\frac\pi6|\uparrow\rangle+\sin\frac\pi6|\downarrow\rangle$. So $p(+n)=\cos^2\frac\pi6=\frac34$, and after that result the probability of $-1$ along $z$ is $\sin^2\frac\pi6=\frac14$. The joint probability is $\frac34\cdot\frac14=\frac{3}{16}$.
