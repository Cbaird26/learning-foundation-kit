@article
Time-independent perturbation theory shifts levels; time-dependent perturbation theory asks how fast a perturbation moves probability between them. We use the interaction picture from the dynamics lesson.

### First-order amplitudes

Let $H(t)=H_0+V(t)$ with $H_0|n\rangle=E_n|n\rangle$, and let the perturbation act from $t=0$. Expand the interaction-picture state as $|\psi_I\rangle=\sum_nc_n(t)|n\rangle$. With $\omega_{fn}=(E_f-E_n)/\hbar$,
$$i\hbar\,\dot c_f=\sum_nV_{fn}(t)\,e^{i\omega_{fn}t}\,c_n,\qquad V_{fn}(t)=\langle f|V(t)|n\rangle .$$
For a system starting in $|i\rangle$ ($c_i(0)=1$) the first-order amplitude to reach $f\ne i$ is
$$c_f^{(1)}(t)=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_0^tV_{fi}(t')\,e^{i\omega_{fi}t'}\,dt',\qquad P_{i\to f}=|c_f^{(1)}|^2 .$$
The theory is valid when $P\ll1$; it can give probabilities above $1$ when it is misused.

### Constant perturbation and the golden rule

If $V$ is constant after $t=0$,
$$P_{i\to f}(t)=\frac{4|V_{fi}|^2}{\hbar^2\omega_{fi}^2}\sin^2\!\frac{\omega_{fi}t}{2}.$$
It oscillates and stays small for $\omega_{fi}\ne0$; only near $\omega_{fi}=0$ does probability accumulate. For large $t$, $\sin^2(\omega t/2)/(\omega/2)^2\to2\pi t\,\delta(\omega)$ (the integral over $\omega$ equals $2\pi t$). Summing over a quasi-continuum of final states with density of states $\rho(E)$ gives a probability linear in $t$, so a constant rate, **Fermi's golden rule**:
$$\Gamma_{i\to f}=\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}\,|V_{fi}|^2\,\rho(E_f)\Big|_{E_f=E_i}.$$
It requires $t$ long compared with $\hbar/\Delta E$, where $\Delta E$ is the energy range over which $V_{fi}$ and $\rho$ vary, and short enough that the initial state is not appreciably depleted ($\Gamma t\ll1$). For a harmonic perturbation $V(t)=We^{-i\omega t}+W^\dagger e^{i\omega t}$ the same argument gives absorption with $E_f=E_i+\hbar\omega$ and rate $\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|W_{fi}|^2\rho$, and stimulated emission with $E_f=E_i-\hbar\omega$ and rate $\frac{2\pi}{\hbar}|(W^\dagger)_{fi}|^2\rho$.

### An exactly solvable check: Rabi oscillations

Take the two-level Hamiltonian $H=\frac\hbar2(\Delta\sigma_z+\Omega\sigma_x)$ with detuning $\Delta$ and coupling $\Omega$, starting in $|\!\uparrow\rangle$. The exact probability of $|\!\downarrow\rangle$ is
$$P_{\downarrow}(t)=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\,\sin^2\!\left(\tfrac12\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}\;t\right).$$
First-order theory in the coupling (with $|V_{fi}|/\hbar=\Omega/2$ and $|\omega_{fi}|=\Delta$) gives $P^{(1)}=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Delta^2}\sin^2\frac{\Delta t}{2}$. The two agree when $\Omega\ll\Delta$. On resonance ($\Delta\to0$) the first-order result becomes $\Omega^2t^2/4$, which exceeds $1$ once $\Omega t>2$, while the exact result $\sin^2(\Omega t/2)$ never does: perturbation theory fails exactly where the transition is strongest.

### Sudden and adiabatic limits

If $H$ changes over a time $\tau\ll\hbar/\Delta E$ (the **sudden** limit), the state has no time to respond: it is unchanged, and the probability of a final eigenstate $|m'\rangle$ of the new Hamiltonian is $|\langle m'|\psi\rangle|^2$. If $H$ changes slowly, $\hbar|\langle m|\dot H|n\rangle|\ll(E_n-E_m)^2$ (the **adiabatic** limit), the system follows the instantaneous eigenstate $|n(t)\rangle$ and acquires only phases (dynamical, and a geometric phase for cyclic changes).

@example
title: First-order theory against the exact two-level answer
problem: For $H=\frac\hbar2(\Delta\sigma_z+\Omega\sigma_x)$ with $\Omega=0.5\ \text{s}^{-1}$ and $\Delta=1\ \text{s}^{-1}$, starting in $|\!\uparrow\rangle$, compare the exact and first-order probabilities of $|\!\downarrow\rangle$ at $t=3\ \text{s}$.
step: The generalized frequency is $W=\sqrt{\Omega^2+\Delta^2}=\sqrt{1.25}=1.118\ \text{s}^{-1}$, so $Wt/2=1.677$.
step: Exact: $P=\frac{\Omega^2}{W^2}\sin^2(Wt/2)=0.2\times\sin^2(1.677)=0.2\times0.989=0.198$.
step: First order: $P^{(1)}=\frac{\Omega^2}{\Delta^2}\sin^2\frac{\Delta t}{2}=0.25\times\sin^2(1.5)=0.25\times0.995=0.249$.
step: The first-order result is about $26\%$ too large. Part of the error is the prefactor, $\Omega^2/\Delta^2$ against $\Omega^2/(\Omega^2+\Delta^2)$, which differ by the factor $1+\Omega^2/\Delta^2=1.25$; the expansion parameter here is $\Omega/\Delta=0.5$, which is not very small.
step: Shrinking $\Omega$ at fixed $\Delta$ and $t$ makes the ratio $P^{(1)}/P\to1$ as $\Omega^2/\Delta^2\to0$, which is the regime where first-order theory is reliable.

@mistakes
- Dropping $\hbar$ in $\omega_{fi}=(E_f-E_i)/\hbar$ or confusing $\omega_{fi}$ with an energy.
- Applying the golden rule to a single discrete final state, where there is no density of states and no linear growth in time.
- Trusting a first-order probability that is not small, or even exceeds $1$.
- Confusing the adiabatic limit (slow change compared with $\hbar/{\rm gap}$) with the sudden limit (fast change).

prompt: A one-dimensional harmonic oscillator (mass $m$) is in its ground state when its frequency is suddenly doubled from $\omega$ to $2\omega$, with $m$ unchanged. What is the probability to find it in the new ground state immediately afterwards?
answer: 2*sqrt(2)/3
check: quad(lambda x: (1 / np.pi) ** 0.25 * np.exp(-x ** 2 / 2) * (2 / np.pi) ** 0.25 * np.exp(-x ** 2), -12, 12) ** 2
hint: Sudden limit: the wavefunction is unchanged, so the probability is $|\langle0'|0\rangle|^2$. Use $\hbar=m=\omega=1$ units.
