@article
For a central potential $V(r)$, angular momentum is conserved, so the scattering problem separates into independent channels labelled by $l$. Each channel is a one-dimensional problem whose only physical output is a single real number, the phase shift.

### Radial problem and phase shifts

Write the solution as $\psi=\sum_lA_l\,\frac{u_l(r)}{r}P_l(\cos\theta)$. Then $u_l$ obeys
$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}u_l''+\left[V(r)+\frac{\hbar^2l(l+1)}{2mr^2}\right]u_l=Eu_l,\qquad u_l(0)=0 .$$
Beyond the range of the potential the solution is a combination of free radial waves. By definition,
$$u_l(r)\propto\sin\!\left(kr-\frac{l\pi}{2}+\delta_l\right)\qquad(r\ \text{outside the range}),$$
so $\delta_l$ is the shift of the free radial wave $\sin(kr-l\pi/2)$. It is defined modulo $\pi$. An attractive potential pulls the wave in and gives $\delta_l>0$; a repulsive one gives $\delta_l<0$.

### Amplitude and cross section

Using $e^{ikz}=\sum_l(2l+1)i^lj_l(kr)P_l(\cos\theta)$ and matching the outgoing parts gives
$$f(\theta)=\frac1k\sum_{l=0}^\infty(2l+1)\,e^{i\delta_l}\sin\delta_l\;P_l(\cos\theta)=\sum_l(2l+1)f_lP_l,\qquad f_l=\frac{e^{2i\delta_l}-1}{2ik}.$$
Since $\int P_lP_{l'}\,d\Omega=\frac{4\pi}{2l+1}\delta_{ll'}$, partial waves do not interfere in the total cross section:
$$\sigma_{\rm tot}=\int|f|^2d\Omega=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l .$$
At $\theta=0$, ${\rm Im}\,f(0)=\frac1k\sum_l(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$, so $\sigma_{\rm tot}=\frac{4\pi}{k}{\rm Im}f(0)$: the optical theorem holds term by term. Each partial cross section obeys the **unitarity bound**
$$\sigma_l=\frac{4\pi(2l+1)}{k^2}\sin^2\delta_l\;\le\;\frac{4\pi(2l+1)}{k^2},$$
with equality when $\delta_l=\pi/2$ (mod $\pi$). The $1/k^2$ is the normalization to remember: the cross section is set by the de Broglie wavelength, not the size of the target.

### Low energy, hard spheres, wells

For a finite-range potential, $\delta_l\propto k^{2l+1}$ as $k\to0$, so s-waves dominate. Define the scattering length $a_s$ by $\delta_0\approx-ka_s$; then $\sigma\to4\pi a_s^2$.

- **Hard sphere** of radius $R$: $u_l(R)=0$ gives $\delta_0=-kR$ exactly, $\sigma_0=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sin^2kR$ and $\sigma\to4\pi R^2$ at low energy, four times the geometric cross section $\pi R^2$.
- **Attractive square well** (depth $V_0$, radius $a$, s-wave): inside $u=\sin k'r$ with $k'=\sqrt{k^2+2mV_0/\hbar^2}$; matching the logarithmic derivative at $r=a$ gives $k\cot(ka+\delta_0)=k'\cot(k'a)$. As $k\to0$ this gives $a_s=a-\dfrac{\tan\kappa a}{\kappa}$ with $\kappa=\sqrt{2mV_0}/\hbar$.
- **Resonance:** near an isolated resonance $\delta_l$ rises rapidly through $\pi/2$ and $\sin^2\delta_l=\dfrac{\Gamma^2/4}{(E-E_R)^2+\Gamma^2/4}$ (Breit-Wigner form), saturating the unitarity bound at $E=E_R$.

In practice only $l\lesssim kR$ contribute for a potential of range $R$, so a handful of phase shifts describe a scattering experiment:

```python
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.legendre import legval

def f_of_theta(k, deltas, theta):
    c = [(2*l + 1) * np.exp(1j*d) * np.sin(d) / k for l, d in enumerate(deltas)]
    return legval(np.cos(theta), c)          # sum_l c_l P_l(cos theta)

def sigma_total(k, deltas):
    return 4*np.pi/k**2 * sum((2*l + 1) * np.sin(d)**2 for l, d in enumerate(deltas))
```

For two identical spin-0 bosons the amplitude must be symmetrized, $f(\theta)+f(\pi-\theta)$, which keeps only even $l$ and doubles the surviving partial-wave terms.

@example
title: S- and p-wave scattering
problem: With $k=1$ (in units where lengths are measured in $1/k$), the phase shifts are $\delta_0=\pi/2$ and $\delta_1=\pi/6$, and all others vanish. Find $|f(\theta)|^2$, $\sigma_{\rm tot}$, and check the optical theorem.
step: The partial amplitudes are $e^{i\delta_0}\sin\delta_0=i$ and $e^{i\delta_1}\sin\delta_1=\frac12\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}+\frac i2\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{4}+\frac i4$.
step: Then $f(\theta)=i+3\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{4}+\frac i4\right)\cos\theta=\frac{3\sqrt3}{4}\cos\theta+i\left(1+\frac34\cos\theta\right)$.
step: Squaring, $|f|^2=\frac{27}{16}\cos^2\theta+1+\frac32\cos\theta+\frac{9}{16}\cos^2\theta=1+\frac32\cos\theta+\frac94\cos^2\theta$.
step: Integrating, $\sigma_{\rm tot}=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}\left(1+\frac32c+\frac94c^2\right)dc=2\pi\left(2+\frac32\right)=7\pi$.
step: Optical theorem: ${\rm Im}f(0)=1+\frac34=\frac74$, so $\frac{4\pi}{k}{\rm Im}f(0)=7\pi$. The phase-shift formula agrees: $4\pi[\sin^2\frac\pi2+3\sin^2\frac\pi6]=4\pi\left(1+\frac34\right)=7\pi$.

@mistakes
- Dropping the factor $(2l+1)$ or the $1/k^2$ in $\sigma=\frac{4\pi}{k^2}\sum(2l+1)\sin^2\delta_l$.
- Treating $\delta_l$ as meaningful beyond its value modulo $\pi$, or assuming a positive phase shift means repulsion.
- Adding partial-wave cross sections incoherently in $d\sigma/d\Omega$; they interfere there, and only the total cross section is a plain sum.
- Quoting the hard-sphere low-energy limit as $\pi R^2$ instead of $4\pi R^2$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select all correct statements about partial-wave scattering.
choice*: The partial cross section $\sigma_l$ cannot exceed $4\pi(2l+1)/k^2$.
choice*: For a finite-range potential, $\delta_l\propto k^{2l+1}$ as $k\to0$, so low-energy scattering is dominated by the s-wave.

solution: The statements about the unitarity bound, the $k^{2l+1}$ behaviour with s-wave dominance, and the resonance are standard results. The statement that a positive phase shift indicates a repulsive potential is false: an attractive potential advances the phase of the radial wave and gives $\delta_l>0$; a repulsive one (such as the hard sphere, $\delta_0=-kR$) gives $\delta_l<0$.
