@article
Last week the harmonic series $\sum\frac1n$ diverged even though $\frac1n\to0$. Now try $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$: the partial sums $1,\ 1.25,\ 1.361,\ 1.424,\dots$ seem to level off near $1.64$. How can we tell these apart without adding a million terms? We need **tests**. Always begin with the divergence test (does $a_n\to0$?), then choose from the toolbox.

### The toolbox for positive terms

- **Integral test.** If $f$ is positive, continuous and decreasing for $x\ge1$ and $a_n=f(n)$, then $\sum a_n$ and $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ converge or diverge together. A consequence is the **p-series rule**: $\sum\frac{1}{n^p}$ converges exactly when $p>1$.
- **Comparison.** If $0\le a_n\le b_n$, then $\sum b_n$ converging forces $\sum a_n$ to converge, and $\sum a_n$ diverging forces $\sum b_n$ to diverge. The direction rule is the same as for improper integrals.
- **Limit comparison.** If $a_n,b_n>0$ and $\lim\frac{a_n}{b_n}=c$ with $0<c<\infty$, the two series behave alike. Choose $b_n$ from the dominant terms: $\frac{3n+1}{n^3-2n+5}$ behaves like $\frac{3}{n^2}$.
- **Ratio test.** Let $\rho=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left|\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}\right|$. If $\rho<1$ the series converges absolutely; if $\rho>1$ it diverges; if $\rho=1$ the test tells you nothing. It is the tool for factorials and exponentials.

### Alternating series

For $\sum(-1)^na_n$ with $a_n\ge0$: if $a_n$ is decreasing and $a_n\to0$, the series converges. Moreover, the error after $N$ terms is at most the first omitted term:

$$|S-s_N|\le a_{N+1}.$$

### Absolute and conditional convergence

A series converges **absolutely** if $\sum|a_n|$ converges, and absolute convergence implies convergence. If $\sum a_n$ converges but $\sum|a_n|$ diverges, the convergence is **conditional**. The series $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$ is the standard example: it converges by the alternating test, but $\sum\frac1n$ diverges.

### Choosing a test

1. Does $a_n\to0$? If not, the series diverges.
2. Is it geometric or a p-series? Use the known rule.
3. Factorials, $c^n$ or $n$th powers in the terms: try the ratio test.
4. Terms that are rational or algebraic in $n$: limit comparison with a p-series.
5. Signs alternate: alternating series test, then check absolute convergence.

@example
title: The ratio test
problem: Decide whether $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n^2}{2^n}$ converges.
step: The terms contain a power of $n$ and an exponential, so use the ratio test with $a_n=\frac{n^2}{2^n}$.
step: Form the ratio: $\frac{a_{n+1}}{a_n}=\frac{(n+1)^2}{2^{n+1}}\cdot\frac{2^n}{n^2}=\frac12\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^2$.
step: Take the limit: $\rho=\frac12\cdot1^2=\frac12<1$.
step: Since $\rho<1$, the series converges absolutely. The test gives convergence only, not the value of the sum (which happens to be $6$).

@example
title: The limit comparison test
problem: Decide whether $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{3n+1}{n^3-2n+5}$ converges.
step: For large $n$ the term behaves like $\frac{3n}{n^3}=\frac{3}{n^2}$, so compare with $b_n=\frac{1}{n^2}$. All terms are positive since $n^3-2n+5>0$ for $n\ge1$.
step: Compute the ratio: $\frac{a_n}{b_n}=\frac{n^2(3n+1)}{n^3-2n+5}=\frac{3+1/n}{1-2/n^2+5/n^3}\to3$.
step: The limit is finite and positive, so the two series behave alike.
step: $\sum\frac{1}{n^2}$ is a p-series with $p=2>1$, so it converges. Therefore the given series converges. (Plain comparison would be awkward because the denominator is smaller than $n^3$.)

@mistakes
- Reading $\rho=1$ in the ratio test as a verdict. It is inconclusive: both $\sum\frac1n$ and $\sum\frac1{n^2}$ give $\rho=1$.
- Using comparison in the wrong direction, such as bounding a series above by a divergent one or below by a convergent one.
- Applying the alternating series test without checking that $a_n$ decreases and tends to $0$. The series $\sum(-1)^n$ fails the second condition.
- Calling $\sum\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n}$ absolutely convergent. It is conditionally convergent.

@practice self
prompt: Use the integral test to decide whether $\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{1}{n\ln n}$ converges. Check the hypotheses of the test, evaluate the integral, and explain why the divergence test does not settle the question.
hint: Let $f(x)=\frac{1}{x\ln x}$. Is it positive, continuous and decreasing on $[2,\infty)$?
hint: For the integral, substitute $u=\ln x$, so $du=\frac{dx}{x}$.
solution: On $[2,\infty)$ the function $f(x)=\frac{1}{x\ln x}$ is positive and continuous, and it is decreasing because the denominator $x\ln x$ is increasing. With $u=\ln x$, $\int_2^b\frac{dx}{x\ln x}=\int_{\ln2}^{\ln b}\frac{du}{u}=\ln\ln b-\ln\ln2\to\infty$ as $b\to\infty$. The integral diverges, so the series diverges. The divergence test fails to decide this because $\frac{1}{n\ln n}\to0$.
rubric: Verifies that $f$ is positive, continuous and decreasing on $[2,\infty)$.
rubric: Evaluates $\int_2^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln x}$ with $u=\ln x$ and shows that it diverges.
rubric: Concludes that the series diverges by the integral test.
rubric: Notes that $a_n\to0$, so the divergence test is inconclusive.

@quiz num
prompt: The alternating series $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n!}$ converges. Using the error bound $|S-s_N|\le a_{N+1}$, find the smallest $N$ that guarantees $|S-s_N|<10^{-4}$, where $s_N$ is the sum of the first $N$ terms.
answer: 7
check: min(N for N in range(1, 60) if 1/factorial(N+1) < 1e-4)
hint: Here $a_n=\frac{1}{n!}$, so the bound is $\frac{1}{(N+1)!}$. You need $(N+1)!>10^4$.
hint: Compute factorials until you pass $10^4$: $5!=120$, $6!=720$, and so on.
solution: $(N+1)!>10^4$ first holds when $N+1=8$, because $7!=5040<10^4<40320=8!$. So $N=7$.
