@article
Add $\frac12+\frac14+\frac18+\cdots$. Each term covers half of the distance that remains to $1$, so the running totals $\frac12,\frac34,\frac78,\frac{15}{16},\dots$ creep up to $1$. It is sensible to say that the infinite sum equals $1$, and that is exactly how we define it.

### Series and partial sums

Given a sequence $a_n$, the **series** $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$ is the limit of its **partial sums** $s_N=a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_N$. If $s_N\to S$, the series **converges** and $S$ is its **sum**; otherwise it **diverges**. A series question is therefore a sequence question about $s_N$, so last week's tools apply to $s_N$, not to $a_n$.

### Geometric series

$$\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}ar^n=\frac{a}{1-r}\quad\text{if }|r|<1,\qquad\text{and the series diverges if }|r|\ge1\ (a\ne0).$$

The reason: $s_N=a\,\frac{1-r^{N+1}}{1-r}$, and $r^{N+1}\to0$ exactly when $|r|<1$. The formula is "first term over one minus the ratio", so it matters where the sum starts: $\sum_{n=1}^\infty r^n$ has first term $r$ and sum $\frac{r}{1-r}$.

Repeating decimals are geometric series: $0.2727\ldots=\frac{27}{100}+\frac{27}{10000}+\cdots=\frac{27/100}{1-1/100}=\frac{3}{11}$.

### Telescoping series

When terms cancel in pairs, the partial sum collapses. Since $\frac{1}{n(n+1)}=\frac1n-\frac{1}{n+1}$ (partial fractions again), $s_N=1-\frac{1}{N+1}\to1$.

### The divergence test

If $\sum a_n$ converges, then $a_n\to0$. Turned around: **if $a_n\not\to0$, the series diverges.** The converse is false. The **harmonic series** $\sum\frac1n$ has $\frac1n\to0$, yet it diverges: group the terms as $1+\frac12+\left(\frac13+\frac14\right)+\left(\frac15+\cdots+\frac18\right)+\cdots$, and each bracket is at least $\frac12$, so $s_{2^k}\ge1+\frac k2$ is unbounded. Thus $a_n\to0$ is necessary for convergence but never sufficient.

### Combining series

If $\sum a_n=A$ and $\sum b_n=B$ both converge, then $\sum(ca_n+db_n)=cA+dB$. A convergent series plus a divergent one always diverges.

@example
title: A geometric series that starts at n = 1
problem: Find $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{2^{n+1}}{5^n}$.
step: Write the general term as a constant times a power: $\frac{2^{n+1}}{5^n}=2\left(\frac25\right)^n$. The ratio is $r=\frac25$ and $|r|<1$, so the series converges.
step: The sum starts at $n=1$, so the first term is $a_1=2\cdot\frac25=\frac45$.
step: Apply "first term over one minus the ratio": $\frac{4/5}{1-2/5}=\frac{4/5}{3/5}=\frac43$.
step: Sanity check: the partial sums are $0.8,\ 1.12,\ 1.248,\ 1.299,\dots$, which approach $1.33$.

@example
title: A telescoping series
problem: Find $\sum_{n=2}^\infty\frac{1}{n^2-1}$.
step: Use partial fractions: $\frac{1}{n^2-1}=\frac12\left(\frac{1}{n-1}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)$.
step: Write out the partial sum: $s_N=\frac12\left[\left(1-\frac13\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac14\right)+\left(\frac13-\frac15\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac{1}{N-1}-\frac{1}{N+1}\right)\right]$.
step: Almost everything cancels. Only the first two positive terms and the last two negative terms survive: $s_N=\frac12\left(1+\frac12-\frac1N-\frac{1}{N+1}\right)$.
step: Let $N\to\infty$: $s_N\to\frac12\cdot\frac32=\frac34$. The series converges to $\frac34$.

@mistakes
- Using the geometric formula with the wrong first term. For $\sum_{n=1}^\infty r^n$ the sum is $\frac{r}{1-r}$, not $\frac{1}{1-r}$.
- Concluding that a series converges because its terms tend to $0$. The harmonic series is the standard counterexample.
- Mixing up the sequence of terms $a_n$ with the sequence of partial sums $s_N$. A series converges when $s_N$ converges.
- Cancelling carelessly in a telescoping sum and forgetting the terms that survive at the beginning and at the end.

@practice num
prompt: Find $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n(n+2)}$.
answer: 3/4
check: nsum(lambda n: 1/(n*(n+2)), 1, 1000) + quad(lambda x: 1/(x*(x+2)), 1000.5, inf)
hint: Use partial fractions: $\frac{1}{n(n+2)}=\frac12\left(\frac1n-\frac{1}{n+2}\right)$.
hint: Write out $s_N$ and cancel. Which terms survive at the start, and which at the end?
solution: $s_N=\frac12\sum_{n=1}^N\left(\frac1n-\frac{1}{n+2}\right)=\frac12\left(1+\frac12-\frac{1}{N+1}-\frac{1}{N+2}\right)\to\frac12\cdot\frac32=\frac34$.

@quiz num
prompt: Find $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{2^n+3^n}{6^n}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 7/2
check: nsum(lambda n: (2**n + 3**n)/6**n, 0, 300)
hint: Split the general term into two pieces, $\left(\frac{2}{6}\right)^n$ and $\left(\frac{3}{6}\right)^n$.
hint: Each piece is a geometric series that starts at $n=0$. Add the two sums.
solution: $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac13\right)^n=\frac{1}{1-1/3}=\frac32$ and $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(\frac12\right)^n=\frac{1}{1-1/2}=2$. The total is $\frac32+2=\frac72$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select every series that converges.
choice*: $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\left(-\frac12\right)^n$
choice: $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{n}{n+1}$
choice*: $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}$
choice: $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n+1}$
choice: $\sum_{n=0}^\infty(1.1)^n$
hint: Check whether the terms tend to $0$ first. Then look for a geometric series, a telescoping series, or the harmonic series in disguise.
solution: The series $\sum\left(-\frac12\right)^n$ is geometric with $|r|=\frac12<1$ (sum $\frac23$). The series $\sum\frac{n}{n+1}$ has terms tending to $1$, so it diverges. The series $\sum\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}$ telescopes, since $\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}=\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+2}$, and its sum is $\frac12$. The series $\sum\frac{1}{n+1}$ is the harmonic series with its first term removed, so it diverges. The series $\sum(1.1)^n$ is geometric with $r=1.1>1$.
