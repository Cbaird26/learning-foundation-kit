title: Approximating a physical function with a Taylor polynomial
overview: Choose a physical function that is awkward to evaluate exactly, such as relativistic kinetic energy, the restoring force on a pendulum, or gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface. Replace it by a Taylor polynomial, prove a bound on the error over a stated interval, and test the bound against numerical errors computed in Python.
goal: Build Taylor polynomials of several degrees for a physical function and justify every coefficient.
goal: Derive a rigorous Lagrange error bound on a stated interval and use it to choose the degree needed for a target accuracy.
goal: Compare the guaranteed bound with the actual numerical error and explain the gap between them.
step: Pick one function and state it with SI units and constants. Options: (a) relativistic kinetic energy $K=mc^2\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\beta^2}}-1\right)$ with $\beta=\frac vc$ on $0\le\beta\le0.3$; (b) the pendulum restoring term $\sin\theta$ on $|\theta|\le0.5$ rad; (c) gravitational potential energy $U=-\frac{GMm}{R+h}=-\frac{GMm}{R}\cdot\frac{1}{1+x}$ with $x=\frac hR$ and $0\le h\le10$ km, where $R=6.371\times10^6$ m.
step: Rewrite the function in one dimensionless variable $x$, choose the center $a$ and the interval $I$ (state both), and list the first five derivatives you will need.
step: Compute the Taylor polynomials $T_1,\dots,T_4$ using derivatives or by substituting into a known series. Interpret the first two nonzero terms physically; for example, the leading term of (a) is the Newtonian $\frac12mv^2$ and the leading nonconstant term of (c) is $mgh$.
step: For each degree write the Lagrange bound $\frac{M_n\,|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$. Find $M_n$ as the maximum of $|f^{(n+1)}|$ on $I$ by analysis (for example, show that the derivative is monotone) and write the justification in a sentence.
step: In Python compute $|f(x)-T_n(x)|$ on a grid of at least 50 points of $I$ for $n=1,\dots,4$, record the maximum error for each degree, and check that it never exceeds the bound. Tabulate the results or plot them on a log scale.
step: Choose a tolerance, such as relative error at most $10^{-3}$ on all of $I$. Find the smallest degree that the bound guarantees and the smallest degree that actually meets the tolerance numerically, and compare them.
step: State the radius of convergence of the series and the physical regime in which the approximation is trustworthy. Explain what goes wrong near the edge of that regime or beyond it.
deliverable: A written report of 2 to 4 pages containing the function and interval, the derivations of $T_1,\dots,T_4$, the error bounds with the derivative bound $M_n$ justified, and the numerical comparison.
deliverable: A short Python script (or spreadsheet) that tabulates the actual error and the bound for degrees 1 to 4 on a grid.
deliverable: A one-paragraph conclusion giving the smallest degree that guarantees your tolerance and what that tolerance means in physical units. Practice explaining one result aloud without notes.
rubric: Taylor polynomials: the coefficients are correct and derived, the center and interval are stated, and the leading terms are interpreted physically.
rubric: Error bound: the Lagrange remainder uses the $(n+1)$st derivative and $(n+1)!$, and $M_n$ is justified as a bound over the whole interval, not just at one point.
rubric: Numerical comparison: actual errors are computed correctly, the bound is shown to hold at every sampled point, and the gap between bound and error is discussed.
rubric: Degree selection: the smallest degree guaranteed by the bound is found and compared with the smallest degree that works in practice.
rubric: Validity: the radius of convergence or the domain of validity is stated, and the behavior near or beyond its edge is explained.
rubric: Communication: notation, units and assumptions are stated, and a peer could follow each step of the argument.

@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2, parts and trig integrals.** $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$; let $u$ be the factor that simplifies when differentiated (logarithms and inverse trig first). For $\sin^m x\cos^n x$ peel off one factor of an odd power, and use half-angle identities when both powers are even. For $\tan^m x\sec^n x$ use $u=\tan x$ or $u=\sec x$. Different methods may give antiderivatives that differ by a constant.

**Weeks 3 and 4, trig substitution and partial fractions.** Use $x=a\sin\theta$ for $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$, $x=a\tan\theta$ for $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ and $x=a\sec\theta$ for $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$. Return to $x$ with a reference triangle, or convert the limits. For partial fractions, divide first when the numerator's degree is not smaller, then give a repeated factor one term per power and an irreducible quadratic a linear numerator. Check by differentiating.

**Weeks 5 and 6, improper integrals and geometry.** An improper integral is a limit. $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}\,dx$ converges exactly when $p>1$, and $\int_0^1x^{-p}\,dx$ exactly when $p<1$. Use comparison to decide without evaluating. Volumes use disks, washers $\pi(R^2-r^2)$ or shells $2\pi\int xf(x)\,dx$, and arc length is $\int\sqrt{1+f'(x)^2}\,dx$.

**Weeks 7 and 8, sequences and series.** Limits use algebra, the squeeze theorem and L'Hopital's rule applied to a function of $x$. A bounded monotone sequence converges, and only then may you solve $L=g(L)$. A series is the limit of its partial sums: geometric series give $\frac{a}{1-r}$, telescoping series collapse, and $a_n\to0$ is necessary but not sufficient.

**Weeks 9 and 10, tests and power series.** Choose among the p-series rule, integral, comparison, limit comparison, ratio and alternating tests. The alternating bound is $|S-s_N|\le a_{N+1}$. A power series gets its radius from the ratio test, and each endpoint needs its own test.

**Weeks 11 and 12, Taylor series and error bounds.** $T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k$, often built by substituting into the series for $e^x$, $\sin x$, $\cos x$, $\frac{1}{1-x}$, $\ln(1+x)$ or $\arctan x$. The Lagrange bound is $\frac{M|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$, where $M$ bounds $|f^{(n+1)}|$ on the whole interval.

### Self-check

- I can choose a technique for an unfamiliar integral and explain why the others fail.
- I verify every antiderivative by differentiating it.
- I decide whether an improper integral converges before evaluating it.
- I can classify a series, naming a test that works and saying why a tempting one does not.
- I can give an interval of convergence with correct decisions at both endpoints.
- I can write a Taylor polynomial, bound its error and compare the bound with the actual error.

### Most common errors

- Dropping the minus sign in $du=-\sin x\,dx$, or forgetting $dx$ in a trigonometric substitution.
- Leaving out the $\frac{A_1}{x-a}$ term for a repeated factor in partial fractions.
- Substituting $\infty$ into an antiderivative, or ignoring a singularity inside the interval.
- Concluding convergence from $a_n\to0$, or a verdict from $\rho=1$ in the ratio test.
- Using the comparison test in the wrong direction.
- Skipping the endpoints of a power series.
- Using the $n$th instead of the $(n+1)$st derivative in the error bound.

prompt: The region under $y=\frac{\ln x}{x^2}$ for $x\ge1$ is rotated about the $x$-axis. Decide whether the volume is finite and, if it is, find it. Give an exact answer.
answer: 2*pi/27
check: pi*quad(lambda x: (log(x)/x**2)**2, 1, inf)
hint: Disks of radius $\frac{\ln x}{x^2}$ give $V=\pi\int_1^\infty\frac{(\ln x)^2}{x^4}\,dx$. Since $(\ln x)^2\le x$ for $x\ge1$, the integrand is at most $\frac{1}{x^3}$, which settles convergence.
hint: Substitute $u=\ln x$, so $x=e^u$ and $dx=e^u\,du$. The integral becomes $\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-3u}\,du$.
hint: Integrate by parts twice, differentiating the power of $u$ each time.
solution: $V=\pi\int_1^\infty\frac{(\ln x)^2}{x^4}\,dx$. It converges, because $(\ln x)^2\le x$ for $x\ge1$ makes the integrand at most $\frac{1}{x^3}$, and $\int_1^\infty x^{-3}\,dx$ converges ($p=3>1$). With $u=\ln x$ the integral is $\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-3u}\,du$. Integrating by parts twice gives $\frac23\int_0^\infty ue^{-3u}\,du=\frac23\cdot\frac13\int_0^\infty e^{-3u}\,du=\frac23\cdot\frac13\cdot\frac13=\frac{2}{27}$. So $V=\frac{2\pi}{27}$.

@exam self
prompt: Consider the power series $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(x-1)^n}{n\,2^n}$. (a) Find its interval of convergence, testing both endpoints. (b) Find a closed form for its sum on that interval by relating it to a series you know. (c) Evaluate the sum at $x=-1$, and use the alternating series error bound to say how many terms are enough to guarantee an error below $0.01$.
hint: For (a) apply the ratio test and then substitute each endpoint. For (b) put $u=\frac{x-1}{2}$ and integrate the geometric series $\frac{1}{1-t}=\sum t^n$ from $0$ to $u$.
hint: For (c) at $x=-1$ the series is $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n}$. The error after $N$ terms is at most the first omitted term.
solution: (a) The ratio is $\frac{|x-1|}{2}\cdot\frac{n}{n+1}\to\frac{|x-1|}{2}$, which is less than $1$ for $-1<x<3$. At $x=3$ the series is the harmonic series $\sum\frac1n$ and diverges. At $x=-1$ it is $\sum\frac{(-1)^n}{n}$, which converges by the alternating series test. The interval is $[-1,3)$. (b) With $u=\frac{x-1}{2}$ the series is $\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{u^n}{n}=-\ln(1-u)$, obtained by integrating the geometric series. So $f(x)=-\ln\left(1-\frac{x-1}{2}\right)=\ln\frac{2}{3-x}$. (c) At the endpoint $x=-1$ the sum equals the value of $f$ by continuity of the function, so it is $f(-1)=\ln\frac24=-\ln2\approx-0.693$. The terms $\frac1n$ decrease to $0$, so after $N$ terms the error is at most $\frac{1}{N+1}$. We need $\frac{1}{N+1}<0.01$, so $N+1>100$ and $N=100$ terms suffice.
rubric: (a) Uses the ratio test to get $R=2$ and the open interval $(-1,3)$, then tests both endpoints: divergence at $3$ (harmonic series) and convergence at $-1$ (alternating series), giving $[-1,3)$.
rubric: (b) Recognizes $\sum\frac{u^n}{n}=-\ln(1-u)$ with $u=\frac{x-1}{2}$ and simplifies to $\ln\frac{2}{3-x}$.
rubric: (c) Evaluates the sum at $x=-1$ as $-\ln2$ and applies the alternating bound $\frac{1}{N+1}<0.01$ to obtain $N=100$.

@exam num
prompt: Using the Maclaurin series of $\arctan x$, the integral $\int_0^{1/2}\frac{\arctan x}{x}\,dx$ can be written as an alternating series. How many terms of that series guarantee, by the alternating series error bound, an error below $10^{-4}$?
answer: 4
check: min(N for N in range(1, 50) if 0.5**(2*N + 1)/(2*N + 1)**2 < 1e-4)
hint: Start from $\arctan x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1}$ and divide by $x$.
hint: Integrate term by term from $0$ to $\frac12$ to get $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n(1/2)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)^2}$.
hint: The terms alternate and decrease, so the error after $N$ terms is at most the next term, $\frac{(1/2)^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)^2}$. Find the first $N$ that makes it smaller than $10^{-4}$.
solution: $\frac{\arctan x}{x}=\sum\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{2n+1}$, and integrating from $0$ to $\frac12$ gives $\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n(1/2)^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)^2}=0.5-0.01389+0.00125-0.000159+0.000024-\cdots$. After $N$ terms the error is at most $a_N=\frac{(1/2)^{2N+1}}{(2N+1)^2}$. For $N=3$ this is $\approx1.6\times10^{-4}$, too big; for $N=4$ it is $\approx2.4\times10^{-5}$, small enough. So $4$ terms are needed.
