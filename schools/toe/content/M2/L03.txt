@lesson
title: Trig substitution
summary: Remove square roots of sums and differences of squares with a trigonometric substitution, convert back with a reference triangle, and check the result by differentiating.
objective: Match each of the three radical forms to its sine, tangent or secant substitution.
objective: Return to the original variable with a reference triangle, or convert the limits of a definite integral.
objective: Complete the square to bring a quadratic under a root into one of the three standard forms.
reading: MIT 18.01SC Single Variable Calculus: look up the sessions on trigonometric substitution in the integration techniques material.

@article
The area of the quarter disc under $y=\sqrt{1-x^2}$ is $\int_0^1\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx$, and geometry says it should be $\frac{\pi}{4}$. But no ordinary substitution handles that root. The picture suggests a way in: a point on the unit circle can be written $(x,y)=(\sin\theta,\cos\theta)$, so put $x=\sin\theta$. Then $\sqrt{1-x^2}=\sqrt{1-\sin^2\theta}=\cos\theta$ and the root disappears. This is **trigonometric substitution**: replace $x$ by a trig function so that a Pythagorean identity removes the radical.

### The three patterns

Each pattern matches one identity. In every row $a>0$ is a constant.

| Radical | Substitution | Becomes | Identity used |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ | $x=a\sin\theta$, $-\frac{\pi}{2}\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $a\cos\theta$ | $1-\sin^2\theta=\cos^2\theta$ |
| $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ | $x=a\tan\theta$, $-\frac{\pi}{2}<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$ | $a\sec\theta$ | $1+\tan^2\theta=\sec^2\theta$ |
| $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ | $x=a\sec\theta$, $0\le\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$ (for $x\ge a$) | $a\tan\theta$ | $\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta$ |

The angle ranges matter. On them $\cos\theta$, $\sec\theta$ and $\tan\theta$ are not negative, so the square root really equals $a\cos\theta$ and not $a|\cos\theta|$.

### The routine

1. Choose the substitution and compute $dx$.
2. Replace **everything**: $x$, $dx$ and the radical.
3. Integrate the trig integral with last week's techniques.
4. Convert back to $x$ with a **reference triangle**. If $x=2\tan\theta$, then $\tan\theta=\frac{x}{2}$: draw a right triangle with opposite side $x$, adjacent side $2$ and hypotenuse $\sqrt{x^2+4}$, and read off $\sin\theta$, $\cos\theta$ and the rest.

For a definite integral you can skip step 4 and convert the **limits** instead. If $x=3\sin\theta$ and $x$ runs from $0$ to $\frac32$, then $\theta$ runs from $0$ to $\frac{\pi}{6}$, and you never return to $x$.

### Completing the square

A quadratic such as $x^2+2x+5$ hides the pattern. Complete the square first: $x^2+2x+5=(x+1)^2+4$. Now let $u=x+1$ and you face $\sqrt{u^2+4}$, a tangent case with $a=2$.

### Check by differentiating

Substitution problems have many moving parts, so verify an antiderivative the cheap way: differentiate it and compare with the original integrand. If they agree, you are done; if not, the error is somewhere in the substitution, the integration or the triangle.

@example
title: A tangent substitution with a reference triangle
problem: Find $\int\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+4}}$ for $x>0$.
step: The radical $\sqrt{x^2+4}$ has the form $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ with $a=2$, so let $x=2\tan\theta$ with $0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$. Then $dx=2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2+4}=\sqrt{4\tan^2\theta+4}=2\sec\theta$.
step: Replace $x$, $dx$ and the radical: $\int\frac{2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta}{(4\tan^2\theta)(2\sec\theta)}=\frac14\int\frac{\sec\theta}{\tan^2\theta}\,d\theta$.
step: Switch to sines and cosines: $\frac{\sec\theta}{\tan^2\theta}=\frac{\cos\theta}{\sin^2\theta}$. With $w=\sin\theta$, $dw=\cos\theta\,d\theta$, the integral is $\frac14\int w^{-2}\,dw=-\frac{1}{4w}=-\frac{1}{4\sin\theta}+C$.
step: Draw a right triangle with $\tan\theta=\frac{x}{2}$: opposite side $x$, adjacent side $2$, hypotenuse $\sqrt{x^2+4}$. Then $\sin\theta=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}$, so the result is $-\frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{4x}+C$.
step: Check with the quotient rule: $\frac{d}{dx}\left(-\frac{\sqrt{x^2+4}}{4x}\right)=-\frac14\cdot\frac{\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+4}}-\sqrt{x^2+4}}{x^2}=-\frac14\cdot\frac{-4}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+4}}=\frac{1}{x^2\sqrt{x^2+4}}$. It matches.

@example
title: A sine substitution with new limits
problem: Evaluate $\int_0^{3/2}\frac{x^2}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}\,dx$.
step: The radical is $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ with $a=3$, so let $x=3\sin\theta$. Then $dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{9-x^2}=3\cos\theta$.
step: Convert the limits: $x=0$ gives $\theta=0$, and $x=\frac32$ gives $\sin\theta=\frac12$, so $\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}$.
step: Substitute: $\int_0^{\pi/6}\frac{9\sin^2\theta\cdot3\cos\theta}{3\cos\theta}\,d\theta=9\int_0^{\pi/6}\sin^2\theta\,d\theta$.
step: Apply the half-angle identity: $9\int_0^{\pi/6}\frac{1-\cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta=\frac92\left[\theta-\frac{\sin 2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/6}$.
step: Evaluate at the limits: $\frac92\left(\frac{\pi}{6}-\frac{\sin(\pi/3)}{2}\right)=\frac92\left(\frac{\pi}{6}-\frac{\sqrt3}{4}\right)=\frac{3\pi}{4}-\frac{9\sqrt3}{8}\approx0.408$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to replace $dx$, or its factor (such as $2\sec^2\theta$), so the integral mixes $x$ and $\theta$.
- Choosing the wrong substitution for the radical, for example $x=a\sin\theta$ for $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}$, which does not remove the root.
- Mislabelling the reference triangle: in $\tan\theta=\frac{x}{a}$ the side equal to $x$ is opposite $\theta$ and the side equal to $a$ is adjacent.
- Converting the limits to $\theta$ and then also converting the antiderivative back to $x$. Use one variable from start to finish.

prompt: Which substitution is the natural first move for an integral containing $\sqrt{x^2-16}$ with $x\ge4$?
choice*: $x=4\sec\theta$
choice: $x=4\sin\theta$
choice: $x=4\tan\theta$
choice: $x=16\sec\theta$
hint: Which Pythagorean identity has the form (something squared) minus $1$ on one side?
hint: $\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta$, so you want $x^2-16$ to look like $16(\sec^2\theta-1)$.
solution: With $x=4\sec\theta$, $x^2-16=16\sec^2\theta-16=16\tan^2\theta$, so $\sqrt{x^2-16}=4\tan\theta$ for $0\le\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$. The sine substitution gives $-16\cos^2\theta$ (negative, so no real root), the tangent substitution gives $16(\tan^2\theta-1)$, and $x=16\sec\theta$ gives $256\sec^2\theta-16$; none of these simplifies.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/6
check: quad(lambda x: 1/sqrt(4-x**2), 0, 1)
hint: The radical is $\sqrt{a^2-x^2}$ with $a=2$. Let $x=2\sin\theta$.
hint: Then $dx=2\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{4-x^2}=2\cos\theta$, so the integrand simplifies completely. Convert the limits too: $x=1$ gives $\sin\theta=\frac12$.
solution: $\int_0^{\pi/6}\frac{2\cos\theta}{2\cos\theta}\,d\theta=\int_0^{\pi/6}d\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}$. The limits are $x=0\Rightarrow\theta=0$ and $x=1\Rightarrow\theta=\frac{\pi}{6}$.

@practice expr
prompt: Find an antiderivative of $\sqrt{1-x^2}$ on $0<x<1$. Omit the $+C$.
vars: x
lead: $\int\sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx =$
answer: (asin(x) + x*sqrt(1-x^2))/2
check: lambda x: quad(lambda t: sqrt(1-t**2), 0, x)
domain: x: 0.1..0.9
const: yes
hint: Let $x=\sin\theta$, so $dx=\cos\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{1-x^2}=\cos\theta$. The integral becomes $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta$.
hint: Use $\cos^2\theta=\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}$ to get $\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\sin 2\theta}{4}$, then write $\sin 2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta$ and return to $x$ with $\theta=\arcsin x$.
solution: $\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta=\frac{\theta}{2}+\frac{\sin\theta\cos\theta}{2}$. With $\theta=\arcsin x$ we have $\sin\theta=x$ and $\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^2}$, so the antiderivative is $\frac12\left(\arcsin x+x\sqrt{1-x^2}\right)+C$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+2x+5}}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: ln(1+sqrt(2))
check: quad(lambda x: 1/sqrt(x**2+2*x+5), -1, 1)
hint: Complete the square: $x^2+2x+5=(x+1)^2+4$. Let $u=x+1$.
hint: You now have $\int_0^{2}\frac{du}{\sqrt{u^2+4}}$. Let $u=2\tan\theta$, and recall that $\int\sec\theta\,d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|$.
solution: With $u=2\tan\theta$, $du=2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{u^2+4}=2\sec\theta$, so the integral is $\int\sec\theta\,d\theta=\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|$ from $\theta=0$ to $\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}$ (because $u=2$ gives $\tan\theta=1$). This equals $\ln(\sqrt2+1)-\ln 1=\ln(1+\sqrt2)$.

@practice self
prompt: For $x>3$, find $\int\frac{\sqrt{x^2-9}}{x}\,dx$ with a trigonometric substitution, give the result in terms of $x$, and check it by differentiating.
hint: The radical has the form $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ with $a=3$. Let $x=3\sec\theta$ with $0<\theta<\frac{\pi}{2}$.
hint: You should find $dx=3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2-9}=3\tan\theta$, which leaves $3\int\tan^2\theta\,d\theta$.
solution: With $x=3\sec\theta$: $dx=3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2-9}=3\tan\theta$, so the integrand becomes $\frac{3\tan\theta}{3\sec\theta}\cdot3\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta=3\tan^2\theta\,d\theta=3(\sec^2\theta-1)\,d\theta$. Integrating gives $3\tan\theta-3\theta+C$. In the reference triangle $\sec\theta=\frac{x}{3}$ (hypotenuse $x$, adjacent side $3$, opposite side $\sqrt{x^2-9}$), so $\tan\theta=\frac{\sqrt{x^2-9}}{3}$ and $\theta=\arccos\frac{3}{x}$. The result is $\sqrt{x^2-9}-3\arccos\frac{3}{x}+C$. Check: $\frac{d}{dx}\sqrt{x^2-9}=\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2-9}}$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\left(-3\arccos\frac{3}{x}\right)=-\frac{9}{x\sqrt{x^2-9}}$; the sum is $\frac{x^2-9}{x\sqrt{x^2-9}}=\frac{\sqrt{x^2-9}}{x}$.
rubric: Chooses $x=3\sec\theta$ and replaces $x$, $dx$ and the radical correctly.
rubric: Reduces the integral to $3\int\tan^2\theta\,d\theta$ and integrates it to $3\tan\theta-3\theta$.
rubric: Converts back to $x$ with a reference triangle, giving $\tan\theta=\frac{\sqrt{x^2-9}}{3}$ and $\theta=\arccos\frac{3}{x}$.
rubric: Differentiates the final answer and recovers the original integrand.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_0^{2}\frac{dx}{(x^2+4)^2}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: pi/64 + 1/32
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x**2+4)**2, 0, 2)
hint: Let $x=2\tan\theta$, so $x^2+4=4\sec^2\theta$. Convert the limits: $x=2$ gives $\tan\theta=1$.
hint: The integrand becomes $\frac18\cos^2\theta$ on $0\le\theta\le\frac{\pi}{4}$. Use the half-angle identity.
solution: $dx=2\sec^2\theta\,d\theta$ and $(x^2+4)^2=16\sec^4\theta$, so the integrand is $\frac{2\sec^2\theta}{16\sec^4\theta}=\frac18\cos^2\theta$. Then $\frac18\int_0^{\pi/4}\frac{1+\cos 2\theta}{2}\,d\theta=\frac{1}{16}\left[\theta+\frac{\sin 2\theta}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/4}=\frac{1}{16}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}+\frac12\right)=\frac{\pi}{64}+\frac{1}{32}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\int_{2\sqrt2}^{4}\frac{dx}{x^2\sqrt{x^2-4}}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: (sqrt(3) - sqrt(2))/8
check: quad(lambda x: 1/(x**2*sqrt(x**2-4)), 2*sqrt(2), 4)
hint: The radical has the form $\sqrt{x^2-a^2}$ with $a=2$. Let $x=2\sec\theta$ and convert the limits using $\sec\theta=\frac{x}{2}$.
hint: The integrand simplifies to $\frac14\cos\theta$. The limits are $\sec\theta=\sqrt2$ and $\sec\theta=2$.
solution: $dx=2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$, $x^2=4\sec^2\theta$ and $\sqrt{x^2-4}=2\tan\theta$, so the integrand is $\frac{2\sec\theta\tan\theta}{4\sec^2\theta\cdot2\tan\theta}=\frac{\cos\theta}{4}$. The limits are $\theta=\frac{\pi}{4}$ and $\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}$, so the value is $\frac14\left(\sin\frac{\pi}{3}-\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)=\frac{\sqrt3-\sqrt2}{8}$.
