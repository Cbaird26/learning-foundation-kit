@article
A measurement is a claim: "this quantity had this value, give or take this much, under these conditions." A **measurement record** is the evidence for that claim. If a stranger (or you in six months) cannot tell what was measured, with what, and how well, the number is worthless.

### What every entry contains

Write each entry so that someone else could repeat the work:

- **Header:** date, time, your name and a one-line objective.
- **Apparatus:** instrument, model or ID, its **resolution** (the smallest step it can show) and its calibration status.
- **Conditions:** anything that might matter, such as room temperature, release angle or battery state.
- **Raw readings:** exactly what you read, in the order taken, with a **unit on every number**.
- **Observations:** anything odd, such as a draft, a slipping clamp or a reading you doubt.
- **Provenance:** names of any data files and of the script that processed them.

Never erase. If a reading is wrong, strike a single line through it and write the reason beside it. Keep raw readings and derived numbers in separate places so you can always redo the analysis.

### Reading uncertainty and scatter

Two different things limit a reading. The first is **resolution**: for a ruler with 1 mm divisions a common convention is a reading uncertainty of half the smallest division, $\pm 0.5\ \text{mm}$. The second is **random scatter**: repeating a hand timing gives different values because of reaction time, drafts and so on. When scatter is larger than resolution, repeat the measurement and let the data tell you the uncertainty.

### Summarizing repeated readings

For $N$ readings $x_1,\dots,x_N$:

$$\bar x = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i, \qquad s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(x_i-\bar x)^2}, \qquad u(\bar x) = \frac{s}{\sqrt N}.$$

The **sample standard deviation** $s$ describes the spread of single readings and does not shrink as you take more of them. The **standard error of the mean** $u(\bar x)=s/\sqrt N$ is the uncertainty of the average and shrinks like $1/\sqrt N$. In numpy the argument `ddof=1` selects the $N-1$ version:

```python
import numpy as np

t10 = np.array([14.2, 14.5, 14.1, 14.4, 14.3, 14.3])  # s, ten swings each
mean = t10.mean()
s = t10.std(ddof=1)
sem = s / np.sqrt(t10.size)
print(f"{mean:.2f} +/- {sem:.2f} s")
```

### Significant figures

Quote the uncertainty with one or two significant figures, then round the value to the same decimal place: $14.30 \pm 0.06\ \text{s}$, not $14.2983 \pm 0.0577\ \text{s}$. Do not round intermediate steps; round only the final statement.

step: Mean of the six timings: $\bar t = 85.8/6 = 14.30\ \text{s}$.
step: Deviations from the mean are $-0.1,\ +0.2,\ -0.2,\ +0.1,\ 0,\ 0$ s, so the sum of squares is $0.01 + 0.04 + 0.04 + 0.01 = 0.10\ \text{s}^2$.
step: Sample standard deviation: $s = \sqrt{0.10/(6-1)} = 0.141\ \text{s}$. This is the typical error of one timing.
step: Standard error of the mean: $0.141/\sqrt 6 = 0.058\ \text{s}$. This is the uncertainty of the average of six timings.
step: One period is one tenth of the timed interval, so divide both numbers by 10: $T = 1.430 \pm 0.006\ \text{s}$. Timing ten swings at once also shrinks the effect of reaction time by a factor of ten.

@mistakes
- Writing a number with no unit or no uncertainty, so nobody can tell what it means or how good it is.
- Erasing or overwriting a doubtful reading instead of striking it through and noting why.
- Quoting more digits than the uncertainty supports, such as $14.2983 \pm 0.06$ s.
- Reporting the standard deviation as the uncertainty of the mean, or dividing by $N$ instead of $N-1$ for a small sample.
