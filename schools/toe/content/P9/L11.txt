@article
A result depends on measured inputs and on the assumptions behind a formula or a simulation. **Sensitivity analysis** asks how much the result changes when each of them changes. The answer tells you what to measure better, what you can ignore, and how far to trust the number.

### Local sensitivity

For a result $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ the **sensitivity coefficient** of input $x_i$ is the partial derivative $S_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$ at the nominal values. Estimate it with a central difference, changing one input at a time:

$$S_i \approx \frac{f(x_i + \delta) - f(x_i - \delta)}{2\delta}.$$

Because $S_i$ has units of $f$ per unit of $x_i$, different inputs cannot be compared directly. The dimensionless **elasticity** fixes that:

$$E_i = \frac{x_i}{f}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i},$$

the percentage change in $f$ per percentage change in $x_i$. For a power law $f \propto x_i^{\,n}$ the elasticity is exactly $n$.

### Ranking by uncertainty

An input matters only if it is both influential and uncertain. Its contribution to the output variance is $(S_i u_i)^2$, the same terms as in week 2, so its share is

$$\text{share}_i = \frac{(S_i u_i)^2}{\sum_j (S_j u_j)^2}.$$

A **tornado chart** shows the output change when each input is moved by $\pm$ one uncertainty, sorted by size. The longest bar marks the first thing to improve.

### Changing an assumption

Model assumptions such as neglecting air drag, small angles or constant temperature are not random inputs, but you can treat them the same way. Change one assumption per run, rerun everything else unchanged (same step size, same seed), and report the difference in the result. Compare that difference with the statistical uncertainty: an assumption that shifts the result by much more than the measurement error is a **systematic uncertainty** and belongs in the report.

### Global sensitivity

Local derivatives describe small changes around one point. If the inputs range widely or the model is strongly nonlinear, draw many input sets from their plausible ranges (week 9), compute the output for each, and see how strongly each input correlates with the output.

```python
import numpy as np

def R(v, th_deg, g=9.8):                  # level-ground projectile range
    return v**2 * np.sin(2 * np.radians(th_deg)) / g

nom = dict(v=12.0, th_deg=40.0)
u = dict(v=0.2, th_deg=1.0)
base = R(**nom)
for k in nom:
    d = 1e-4 * nom[k]
    S = (R(**{**nom, k: nom[k] + d}) - R(**{**nom, k: nom[k] - d})) / (2 * d)
    print(k, S, S * nom[k] / base, abs(S * u[k]))   # S, elasticity, 1-sigma effect
```

The one-sigma effect of the speed is about $0.48$ m and that of the angle about $0.09$ m, so the speed matters five times more.

@example
title: Which input limits the range?
problem: A projectile is launched on level ground at $v_0 = 12.0 \pm 0.2$ m/s and $\theta = 40.0^\circ \pm 1.0^\circ$ (supplied, illustrative values). With $R = v_0^2\sin 2\theta/g$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ taken as exact, find $R$, the elasticities, the dominant input and $u_R$.
step: $R = 144\sin 80^\circ/9.8 = 14.47$ m.
step: The elasticity with respect to $v_0$ is $2$ because $R \propto v_0^2$. For the angle, $E_\theta = \theta\cdot 2\cos 2\theta/\sin 2\theta = 2\theta\cot 2\theta = 2(0.698)(0.176) = 0.246$, with $\theta$ in radians. Near $45^\circ$ this elasticity falls to zero because $R$ is at its maximum.
step: The relative uncertainties are $u_v/v = 0.2/12.0 = 1.67\%$ and $u_\theta/\theta = 1^\circ/40^\circ = 2.5\%$.
step: Their contributions to $u_R/R$ are $2 \times 1.67\% = 3.33\%$ from the speed and $0.246 \times 2.5\% = 0.62\%$ from the angle.
step: Combine in quadrature: $\sqrt{3.33^2 + 0.62^2}\,\% = 3.39\%$, so $u_R = 0.49$ m and $R = 14.5 \pm 0.5$ m. The speed carries $3.33^2/(3.33^2 + 0.62^2) = 97\%$ of the variance, so measure the speed better.

@mistakes
- Comparing raw sensitivity coefficients that have different units instead of elasticities or uncertainty-weighted effects.
- Changing several inputs or assumptions in one run, so the cause of the change cannot be identified.
- Evaluating derivatives away from the nominal operating point, or using finite-difference steps so small that round-off dominates.
- Calling an input unimportant because its sensitivity is small, without checking how uncertain it is.

prompt: The range of a projectile launched at $45^\circ$ is $R = v^2/g$ with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find the sensitivity $\partial R/\partial v$ at $v = 10$ m/s.
answer: 2.04
check: nderiv(lambda v: v**2 / 9.8, 10)
tol: 0.005
unit: m per (m/s)
hint: Differentiate $v^2/g$ with respect to $v$.
hint: $\partial R/\partial v = 2v/g$.
solution: $\partial R/\partial v = 2(10)/9.8 = 2.04$ m per m/s: each extra m/s of launch speed adds about $2.04$ m of range.

@practice num
prompt: A capacitor discharges through a resistor, so that $V = V_0\,e^{-t/RC}$. At $t = 2.0$ ms with $R = 1.0\ \text{k}\Omega$ and $C = 1.0\ \mu\text{F}$ ($RC = 1.0$ ms), find the elasticity of $V$ with respect to $R$: by how many percent does $V$ change for a $1\%$ increase in $R$?
answer: 2
check: (lambda V: nderiv(V, 1.0) / V(1.0))(lambda r: math.exp(-2.0 / r))
hint: Take the logarithm: $\ln V = \ln V_0 - t/(RC)$.
hint: $E = \partial \ln V/\partial \ln R = t/(RC)$.
solution: $\ln V = \ln V_0 - t/(RC)$ gives $E_R = \partial\ln V/\partial\ln R = t/(RC) = 2$. A $1\%$ larger $R$ slows the discharge and raises $V$ by about $2\%$ at this time.

prompt: A result is a product $f = ab$. The input $a$ has a relative uncertainty of $3\%$ and $b$ has $4\%$. What fraction of the variance of $f$ comes from $b$?
answer: 0.64
check: 0.04**2 / (0.03**2 + 0.04**2)
tol: 0.005
hint: For a product each input contributes its relative uncertainty (elasticity $1$).
hint: The variance shares are proportional to $3^2$ and $4^2$.
solution: The variances are proportional to $9$ and $16$, so the share of $b$ is $16/25 = 0.64$.

@quiz num
prompt: A density is computed as $\rho = m/(\pi r^2 h)$. The mass has a relative uncertainty of $0.5\%$, the radius $1\%$ and the height $2\%$ (supplied, illustrative values). What fraction of the variance of $\rho$ comes from the radius? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.485
check: (2 * 0.01)**2 / (0.005**2 + (2 * 0.01)**2 + 0.02**2)
tol: 0.005
hint: The radius appears squared, so its elasticity is $-2$ and its contribution is $2 \times 1\%$.
hint: The contributions to the relative uncertainty are $0.5\%$, $2\%$ and $2\%$.
solution: The squared contributions are $0.25$, $4$ and $4$ (in %²), totalling $8.25$. The radius share is $4/8.25 = 0.485$.
