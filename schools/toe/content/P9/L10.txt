@article
Computers produce **pseudo-random** numbers: a deterministic sequence that behaves statistically like independent uniform draws on $[0,1)$. A seeded generator such as `numpy.random.default_rng(seed)` makes runs repeatable. Physics usually needs other distributions, such as exponential decay times, Gaussian noise or isotropic directions, and you build them from uniform numbers.

### Inverse transform sampling

If $F(x)$ is the cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the target and $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $X = F^{-1}(U)$ has that distribution. For the exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$, $F(x) = 1 - e^{-\lambda x}$, so

$$X = -\frac{\ln(1-U)}{\lambda},$$

which simulates decay times with mean $1/\lambda$. For the density $f(x) = 2x$ on $[0,1]$ one has $F(x) = x^2$ and $X = \sqrt U$.

### Rejection sampling

When $F^{-1}$ is not available, propose $x$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ and accept it with probability $f(x)/M$, where $M \ge \max f$; otherwise draw again. The accepted values follow $f$. The fraction of proposals accepted is $1/\big(M(b-a)\big)$, so a tight bound $M$ wastes less work.

### Directions on a sphere

Isotropic directions need $\cos\theta$ uniform on $[-1,1]$ and $\phi$ uniform on $[0,2\pi)$. Taking $\theta$ itself uniform piles points up near the poles, because equal steps in $\theta$ cover less area there.

### Validate against a known answer

A sampler is a measuring instrument, so check it against a case with a known answer. Compare a statistic with its exact value and express the difference in standard errors:

$$z = \frac{\hat\mu - \mu_{\text{exact}}}{s/\sqrt N}.$$

Usually $|z| < 2$; a repeated $|z| > 3$ points to a bug. Check more than the mean: compare the variance, a histogram with the density, and a few quantiles. A passing test does not prove correctness, but a failing test shows that something is wrong, either in the sampler or in the error estimate.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(1)
lam, N = 0.5, 100_000
x = -np.log(1 - rng.random(N)) / lam        # inverse transform
se = x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N)
print(x.mean(), 1 / lam, se)
print((x.mean() - 1 / lam) / se)            # z for the mean
```

Create the generator once, outside any loop; creating it again with the same seed inside the loop repeats the same numbers.

@example
title: Simulated decay times by inverse transform
problem: A simulated source has half-life $T_{1/2} = 10$ min. A uniform random number $u = 0.25$ is drawn. Use inverse transform sampling to find the corresponding decay time, and cross-check it with the half-life formula.
step: The mean lifetime is $\tau = T_{1/2}/\ln 2 = 14.43$ min, so $\lambda = 1/\tau$.
step: Inverse transform: $t = -\tau\ln(1-u) = -14.43\,\ln(0.75) = 14.43 \times 0.2877 = 4.15$ min.
step: Cross-check: the surviving fraction is $1 - u = 0.75$, and $2^{-t/T_{1/2}} = 0.75$ gives $t = T_{1/2}\log_2(1/0.75) = 10 \times 0.4150 = 4.15$ min. The two routes agree.
step: Meaning: with $u$ uniform, a quarter of the simulated nuclei decay before $4.15$ min, as the definition of the distribution requires.

@example
title: Efficiency of rejection sampling
problem: Sample the density $f(x) = 6x(1-x)$ on $[0,1]$ by proposing $x$ uniformly on $[0,1]$. Find the best bound $M$ and the fraction of proposals accepted, and the number of proposals needed for $1000$ accepted values.
step: The density is largest at $x = 1/2$, where $f = 6(0.5)(0.5) = 1.5$. The tightest bound is $M = 1.5$.
step: A proposal $x$ is accepted with probability $f(x)/M$; draw a second uniform number $v$ and accept if $vM \le f(x)$.
step: The overall acceptance fraction is $\int_0^1 f\,dx\,/\,(M \cdot 1) = 1/1.5 = 2/3$.
step: To obtain $1000$ accepted values you need about $1000/(2/3) = 1500$ proposals.

@mistakes
- Taking $\theta$ uniform when sampling isotropic directions, which concentrates points at the poles.
- Forgetting the minus sign or confusing the rate $\lambda$ with the mean $1/\lambda$, giving negative or wrongly scaled times.
- Accepting a sampler because its mean matches, without checking the variance or the shape of the distribution.
- Re-creating the generator with the same seed inside a loop, so that every pass reuses the same numbers.

@practice num
prompt: Rejection sampling of the density $f(x) = 6x(1-x)$ on $[0,1]$ uses a uniform proposal on $[0,1]$ and the tightest bound $M = \max f$. What fraction of proposals is accepted?
answer: 2/3
check: 1 / np.max(6 * np.linspace(0, 1, 10001) * (1 - np.linspace(0, 1, 10001)))
tol: 0.001
hint: The acceptance fraction is the area under $f$ divided by the area of the box of height $M$ and width $1$.
hint: The area under $f$ is $1$, and $f$ peaks at $x = 1/2$.
solution: $\max f = f(1/2) = 1.5$, so the acceptance fraction is $1/(1.5 \times 1) = 2/3$.

prompt: Speeds in a two-dimensional gas are modeled by the Rayleigh distribution with CDF $F(x) = 1 - e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ and $\sigma = 1.5$ (arbitrary speed units). Use inverse transform sampling to convert $u = 0.8$ into a speed. Give two decimal places.
answer: 2.69
