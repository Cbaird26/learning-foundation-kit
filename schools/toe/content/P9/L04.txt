@article
Fitting estimates the parameters $\boldsymbol\theta$ of a model $f(x;\boldsymbol\theta)$ from data $(x_i, y_i)$ whose uncertainties $\sigma_i$ you know. A fit gives you three things: best parameter values, their uncertainties, and a verdict on whether the model describes the data at all.

### Chi-square

Measure the mismatch with

$$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{y_i - f(x_i;\boldsymbol\theta)}{\sigma_i}\right)^2 .$$

Each term says how many standard uncertainties a point lies from the model. Minimizing $\chi^2$ is **weighted least squares**: precise points count more. With equal $\sigma_i$ it reduces to the ordinary line fit of week 3.

Divide by the **degrees of freedom** $\nu = N - p$, where $p$ is the number of fitted parameters, to get the reduced chi-square $\chi^2_\nu = \chi^2/\nu$. If the model is right and the $\sigma_i$ are honest, $\chi^2_\nu \approx 1$, typically within $\sqrt{2/\nu}$.

- $\chi^2_\nu \gg 1$: the model is wrong, the uncertainties are too small, or a point is an outlier.
- $\chi^2_\nu \ll 1$: the uncertainties are too generous, or the model has too many parameters for the data.

### Parameter uncertainties

For a straight line with equal $\sigma$ the slope and intercept uncertainties are

$$u_b = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{S_{xx}}}, \qquad u_a = \sigma\sqrt{\frac1N + \frac{\bar x^2}{S_{xx}}}, \qquad S_{xx} = \sum_i (x_i - \bar x)^2 .$$

Spreading the $x$ values widely makes $S_{xx}$ large and the slope well determined. In general the parameter variances are the diagonal of the covariance matrix.

### Residuals

The **residual** is $r_i = y_i - f(x_i;\hat{\boldsymbol\theta})$. Plot $r_i/\sigma_i$ against $x_i$. A good fit scatters around zero with no trend, mostly within $\pm 2$. A smooth curve, a drift or a long run of same-sign values means the model is missing something, even if $\chi^2_\nu$ looks acceptable.

### In code

```python
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import curve_fit

x = np.array([0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250.0])
y = np.array([1, 52, 102, 152, 203, 254.0])
sigma = np.full_like(y, 0.5)

line = lambda x, a, b: a + b * x
popt, pcov = curve_fit(line, x, y, sigma=sigma, absolute_sigma=True)
perr = np.sqrt(np.diag(pcov))
r = (y - line(x, *popt)) / sigma
print(popt, perr, np.sum(r**2) / (len(x) - 2))
```

The option `absolute_sigma=True` tells scipy that your $\sigma_i$ are real uncertainties and not merely relative weights.

### Judging a calibration

A calibration is acceptable when (1) the residuals show no pattern, (2) $\chi^2_\nu$ is close to $1$, and (3) the parameter uncertainties are small enough for your purpose. Then you can quote the line together with its uncertainties and use it to correct readings.

@example
title: Is the scale calibration acceptable?
problem: Judge the scale calibration of week 3 (supplied, illustrative data): $x = 0, 50, \dots, 250$ g and $y = 1, 52, 102, 152, 203, 254$ g, each reading with $\sigma = 0.5$ g. The least-squares line is $y = 1.048 + 1.01029\,x$.
step: Residuals (data minus line) are $-0.05, +0.44, -0.08, -0.59, -0.10, +0.38$ g. They sum to zero and show no trend, and all lie within $\pm 1.2\,\sigma$.
step: $\chi^2 = \sum (r_i/\sigma)^2 = 0.705/0.25 = 2.82$.
step: There are $\nu = 6 - 2 = 4$ degrees of freedom, so $\chi^2_\nu = 0.70$. The expected spread is $\sqrt{2/4} = 0.71$, so this is consistent with $1$.
step: With $S_{xx} = 43\,750\ \text{g}^2$: $u_b = 0.5/\sqrt{43\,750} = 0.0024$ and $u_a = 0.5\sqrt{1/6 + 125^2/43\,750} = 0.36$ g.
step: Verdict: the fit is acceptable. The gain $1.0103 \pm 0.0024$ differs from $1$ by about four uncertainties and the offset $1.05 \pm 0.36$ g from $0$ by about three, so the systematic errors are real and the calibration is worth applying.

@mistakes
- Quoting parameter uncertainties from a fit without checking the residuals; a wrong model gives confident but meaningless errors.
- Forgetting `absolute_sigma=True`, so scipy rescales the covariance to the scatter and ignores your stated uncertainties.
- Dividing $\chi^2$ by $N$ instead of $N - p$.
- Treating $\chi^2_\nu$ far below $1$ as a sign of an excellent fit, when it usually means the uncertainties were overestimated.
