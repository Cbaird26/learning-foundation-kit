@article
**Monte Carlo** methods answer numerical questions by random sampling. If $X$ is a random variable with a known distribution, the average of $g(X)$ over many independent samples approaches the expectation $E[g(X)]$. Many laboratory quantities (integrals, areas, propagated uncertainties) can be written that way.

### The sample mean and its error

Draw $N$ independent samples $x_i$ and compute $g_i = g(x_i)$. The estimate and its standard error are

$$\hat G = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N} g_i, \qquad u(\hat G) = \frac{s_g}{\sqrt N},$$

where $s_g$ is the sample standard deviation of the $g_i$ (the formulas of week 1). The error falls like $1/\sqrt N$: to gain one more digit you need $100$ times as many samples. That slow rate is the price of a method that works in any number of dimensions and for awkward shapes.

### Example: estimating an area

To estimate $\pi$, throw random points into the unit square and count the fraction $k/N$ that land inside the quarter circle $x^2 + y^2 \le 1$. That fraction estimates $\pi/4$, so $\hat\pi = 4k/N$. Each point is a hit with probability $p = \pi/4$, so the number of hits is binomial and

$$u(\hat\pi) = 4\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{N}} \approx \frac{1.64}{\sqrt N}.$$

### Propagating uncertainty by sampling

To propagate uncertainties through a formula, draw each input from a distribution with its measured mean and standard uncertainty, compute the output for each draw, and take the mean and standard deviation of the outputs. This needs no derivatives. It also works when the formula is strongly nonlinear or the uncertainties are large, where the rule of week 2 is only approximate.

### Reproducible sampling

Always use a seeded generator and record the seed so that anyone can repeat the run exactly. A different seed gives a different estimate within the error bar, which is how you see the statistical error directly.

```python
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(12345)
N = 100_000
x, y = rng.random(N), rng.random(N)
p = np.count_nonzero(x**2 + y**2 <= 1) / N
print(4 * p, 4 * np.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / N))

# Monte Carlo propagation for g = 4 pi^2 L / T^2
L = rng.normal(0.500, 0.002, N)
T = rng.normal(1.420, 0.006, N)
g = 4 * np.pi**2 * L / T**2
print(g.mean(), g.std(ddof=1))      # compare with 9.789 +/- 0.092
```

The first line of output should be within about two printed errors of $3.1416$, and the second should match the analytic week 2 result to two digits.

@example
title: Sampling error for a simple integral
problem: Estimate $I = \int_0^1 x^2\,dx$ by averaging $x^2$ over $N = 10^4$ uniform random numbers on $[0,1]$. Predict the standard error, and find how many samples are needed for an error of $0.0003$.
step: Write the integral as an expectation: $I = E[X^2]$ with $X$ uniform on $[0,1]$. The exact value is $1/3$, which gives a known answer to validate against.
step: The variance of $g = X^2$ is $E[X^4] - (E[X^2])^2 = 1/5 - 1/9 = 4/45$, so $\sigma_g = 0.2981$.
step: At $N = 10^4$ the standard error is $0.2981/\sqrt{10^4} = 0.0030$, so expect $0.333 \pm 0.003$.
step: For an error of $0.0003$ we need $N = (0.2981/0.0003)^2 \approx 9.9 \times 10^5$, about $10^6$: ten times smaller error costs one hundred times more samples.
step: Validate by running several seeds: the run-to-run scatter should be about $0.003$, and the mean should lie within two or three standard errors of $1/3$.

@mistakes
- Quoting a Monte Carlo estimate without its statistical error.
- Expecting ten times more samples to give ten times less error; it gives only $\sqrt{10} = 3.2$ times less.
- Forgetting to set and record the seed, so that the run cannot be repeated.
- Reusing the same random array for quantities that should be independent, which builds in a false correlation.

@quiz num
prompt: To estimate the area of the ellipse $x^2/4 + y^2 \le 1$, you throw $N = 10\,000$ random points uniformly in the box $-2 \le x \le 2$, $-1 \le y \le 1$ (area $8$) and count the fraction $p$ that fall inside. The area estimate is $8p$. Use the exact $p = \pi/4$ to predict its standard error, $8\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$. Give three significant figures.
answer: 0.0328
check: 8 * math.sqrt(math.pi / 4 * (1 - math.pi / 4) / 10000)
tol: 0.001
hint: The box area times the hit fraction estimates the ellipse area.
hint: The error of $p$ is $\sqrt{p(1-p)/N}$; multiply it by $8$.
solution: The ellipse has area $\pi \cdot 2 \cdot 1$, so $p = \pi/4 = 0.785$. Then $\sqrt{p(1-p)/N} = \sqrt{0.1685/10^4} = 0.00410$ and the area error is $8 \times 0.00410 = 0.0328$.
