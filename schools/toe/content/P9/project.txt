## Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2: records and propagation.** Every number has a unit, an uncertainty, an instrument and conditions, and raw data are never erased. For repeated readings use the mean $\bar x$, the sample standard deviation $s$ (divide by $N-1$) and the standard error $s/\sqrt N$. For a result $f$, $u_f^2 = \sum (\partial f/\partial x_i)^2 u_i^2$: absolute uncertainties in quadrature for sums, relative ones for products, and the exponent times the relative uncertainty for powers.

**Weeks 3 and 4: calibration and fitting.** Fit $y = a + bx$ with $b = S_{xy}/S_{xx}$ and $a = \bar y - b\bar x$, and correct readings with $x = (y-a)/b$ inside the calibrated range. Weighted fits minimize $\chi^2 = \sum ((y_i - f_i)/\sigma_i)^2$; expect $\chi^2_\nu = \chi^2/(N-p) \approx 1$, and for a line with equal $\sigma$ the slope uncertainty is $u_b = \sigma/\sqrt{S_{xx}}$. Judge a fit by its residual pattern, $\chi^2_\nu$ and parameter uncertainties.

**Weeks 5 and 6: experiments and controls.** Cover a factor of 5 in range with at least 5 settings and 3 repeats, linearize, and respect voltage and power limits. Use controlled variables, blanks, known standards, randomized or paired runs and $z = \Delta/\sqrt{u_A^2 + u_B^2}$. Keep provenance records.

**Weeks 7 and 8: ODEs and stability.** Euler, midpoint and RK4 have global orders $1$, $2$ and $4$. Check against exact solutions and conserved quantities. Forward Euler on $y' = -\lambda y$ needs $h < 2/\lambda$. Observed order is $p = \log_2(e_h/e_{h/2})$, Richardson extrapolation is $y_{h/2} + (y_{h/2} - y_h)/(2^p - 1)$, and round-off sets a floor.

**Weeks 9 and 10: Monte Carlo and sampling.** The estimate is $\hat G \pm s/\sqrt N$, and ten times less error costs one hundred times more samples. Inverse transform uses $X = F^{-1}(U)$, for example $-\ln(1-U)/\lambda$. Rejection sampling accepts a fraction $1/(M(b-a))$. Isotropic directions need $\cos\theta$ uniform. Validate with $z = (\hat\mu - \mu)/\mathrm{SE}$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: sensitivity and reproducibility.** Use $S_i = \partial f/\partial x_i$, the elasticity $E_i = (x_i/f)\,\partial f/\partial x_i$ and the variance share $(S_iu_i)^2/\sum_j (S_ju_j)^2$. Change one assumption per run and compare with the statistical uncertainty. Keep raw data read-only with checksums, scripts, seeds, versions, tolerance-based tests and a README.

## Self-check

- Can I state for every number in my report its unit, its uncertainty and its source?
- Can I propagate uncertainty through a new formula by hand and with a short function?
- Can I say from a residual plot and $\chi^2_\nu$ whether a fit is acceptable?
- Can I find the stability limit of a step and the observed order of a method?
- Can I choose the number of samples for a target Monte Carlo error?
- Can I rerun my own project from a clean folder and get the same numbers?

## Common errors

- Mixing absolute and relative uncertainties, or forgetting an exponent.
- Dividing by $N$ instead of $N-1$, or reporting $s$ where $s/\sqrt N$ is needed.
- Trusting parameter errors from a fit whose residuals show a pattern.
- Calling a method accurate because it is stable, or reading the order from step sizes that are too large.
- Expecting ten times more samples to give ten times less error.
- Changing two assumptions at once, or leaving out the seed and the library versions.

prompt: A pendulum has length $L = 0.600 \pm 0.002$ m. Six timings of ten complete swings (supplied, illustrative data) are 15.4, 15.6, 15.5, 15.7, 15.3 and 15.5 s. Take the uncertainty of the mean timing to be its standard error, divide by 10 to get the period $T$ and its uncertainty, and propagate $L$ and $T$ through $g = 4\pi^2 L/T^2$. Find the standard uncertainty of $g$. Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.080
check: (lambda T, uT: math.hypot(nderiv(lambda L: 4 * math.pi**2 * L / T**2, 0.600) * 0.002, nderiv(lambda TT: 4 * math.pi**2 * 0.600 / TT**2, T) * uT))(np.mean([15.4, 15.6, 15.5, 15.7, 15.3, 15.5]) / 10, np.std([15.4, 15.6, 15.5, 15.7, 15.3, 15.5], ddof=1) / np.sqrt(6) / 10)
tol: 0.002
unit: m/s²
hint: Find the mean and the sample standard deviation of the six timings, then the standard error $s/\sqrt 6$.
hint: Divide the mean and its standard error by 10: $T = 1.550$ s and $u_T = 0.00577$ s.
hint: For $g \propto L\,T^{-2}$ the relative uncertainties are $u_L/L$ and $2u_T/T$; combine them in quadrature and multiply by $g$.
solution: The mean is $15.50$ s and $s = \sqrt{0.10/5} = 0.1414$ s, so the standard error is $0.1414/\sqrt 6 = 0.0577$ s. Then $T = 1.550$ s and $u_T = 0.00577$ s. The value is $g = 4\pi^2(0.600)/1.550^2 = 9.859\ \text{m/s}^2$. The relative uncertainties are $0.002/0.600 = 0.333\%$ and $2(0.00577)/1.550 = 0.745\%$, which combine to $0.816\%$, so $u_g = 0.0805 \approx 0.080\ \text{m/s}^2$. The period dominates.

prompt: The midpoint method is applied to $y' = -y$, $y(0) = 1$, up to $t = 1$ with step sizes $h = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05$ and $0.025$. For each step size find the absolute error of $y(1)$ against $e^{-1}$, then fit a straight line to $\ln(\text{error})$ against $\ln h$. Report the slope, which is the observed order, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 2.06
check: (lambda rk2: np.polyfit(np.log([0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025]), np.log([abs(rk2(h) - math.exp(-1)) for h in [0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025]]), 1)[0])(lambda h: (lambda s: s(s, 0, 1.0))(lambda s, n, y: y if n == round(1 / h) else s(s, n + 1, y + h * (-(y + 0.5 * h * (-y))))))
tol: 0.03
hint: For $y' = -y$ one midpoint step gives $k_1 = -y$, a midpoint state $y(1 - h/2)$ and $k_2 = -y(1 - h/2)$, so $y$ is multiplied by $1 - h + h^2/2$.
hint: After $1/h$ steps $y(1) = (1 - h + h^2/2)^{1/h}$. Compute the four errors; they fall by a factor of about $4$ at each halving.
hint: Use `np.polyfit(np.log(hs), np.log(errs), 1)[0]` on the four pairs.
solution: The errors at $h = 0.2, 0.1, 0.05, 0.025$ are about $2.9\times10^{-3}$, $6.6\times10^{-4}$, $1.6\times10^{-4}$ and $3.9\times10^{-5}$. Successive ratios are $4.3$, $4.2$ and $4.1$. The log-log fit has slope $2.06$, consistent with the second-order method, with the ratio approaching $4$ as $h$ shrinks into the asymptotic range.
