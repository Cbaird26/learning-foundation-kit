@article
**Literary nonfiction** tells true things with the techniques of literature: scenes, voice, images, structure. It differs from a news report because the author's perspective and style are part of the point.

### Genres

- **Memoir**: a writer's account of a part of his or her own life, shaped around a meaning. It differs from an **autobiography**, which covers a whole life in order.
- **Personal essay**: reflection on an idea through the writer's experience.
- **Speech**, **letter** and **narrative journalism** are also literary nonfiction.

### Central idea and purpose

The **central idea** is the main point about the subject. The **author's purpose** is why the author writes: to **inform**, **persuade**, **entertain** or **reflect**, often several at once. To find the purpose, ask what the author wants the reader to understand or feel by the end, and what the choice of details shows.

A memoir about a first job in a kitchen is not mainly about food; the purpose might be to show how responsibility changes a person.

### How authors develop an idea

- **Structure**: chronological, compare and contrast, problem and solution, or a pattern that begins with a scene and moves to reflection.
- **Narrator and perspective**: in memoir there are two selves, the **younger self who lived the event** and the **older narrator who interprets it**. Meaning often comes from the gap between them.
- **Techniques**: anecdote, imagery, **tone**, repetition, rhetorical questions, **juxtaposition** (placing contrasts side by side) and **analogy**.
- **Claims and evidence**: in an essay, find the claim and decide what evidence supports it.

### Summary versus analysis

A **summary** states what a text says in short form. **Analysis** explains how and why the text works: "The author opens with a vivid scene of the empty classroom to make the reader feel the loss before she states it." Aim for analysis whenever you write about a text.

### Reading steps

1. Preview the title, author and first lines.
2. Read for the main events or arguments.
3. Reread, marking turning points and shifts in tone.
4. Write the central idea in one sentence, then the purpose in one more.
5. Pick two techniques and explain how each supports the purpose.

@example
title: Finding the purpose of a memoir passage
problem: Read this original memoir paragraph. "At twelve I counted every coin in the jar, certain it would buy my mother a coat. It bought a scarf. I remember being angry at the price, then ashamed of the anger; she wore the scarf to every funeral and every wedding for twenty years." State the central idea and purpose.
step: Identify the events: a child saves coins for a coat, can buy only a scarf, and the mother wears it for decades.
step: Notice the narrator's two views: the younger self felt anger and shame; the older narrator notes the long use of the scarf.
step: Central idea: A small gift given with love can matter more than the child understood at the time.
step: Purpose: to reflect, and to persuade the reader to see the value of modest gestures. The final long list of occasions supports this.

@mistakes
- Confusing the older narrator's reflection with what the younger self knew at the time.
- Stating the topic ("family") rather than the central idea.
- Writing a summary when the prompt asks how the author achieves an effect.
- Assuming every nonfiction text is neutral; authors have perspective and purpose.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: Choose a memoir or personal-essay passage of one to three paragraphs (a free reading passage works). Write a short analysis of about 120 words that states the author's purpose, the central idea and one technique that supports them, with a quotation.
hint: Name the purpose and central idea first, then choose one technique that supports them.
solution: A model: "In the passage, the author recalls shoveling the neighbors' driveways as a teenager. The central idea is that work done for others can become a quiet form of belonging. The purpose is reflective: the older narrator looks back to explain why the job mattered. The author closes with the image of 'porch lights coming on, one by one,' a metaphor-like image that suggests the community recognizing him. This image supports the idea by showing belonging as something gradually earned rather than announced."
rubric: States a central idea as a sentence and a purpose (inform, persuade, entertain or reflect).
rubric: Includes a short, accurate quotation from the passage.
rubric: Explains how the technique supports the purpose, instead of only naming it.
