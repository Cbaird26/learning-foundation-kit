@article
Moving charges make magnetic fields, and magnetic fields push on moving charges. This single link between electricity and magnetism powers motors, generators and transformers.

### Magnetic fields

A **magnetic field** $\vec B$ is measured in **teslas** (T). Field lines leave a magnet's north pole and enter the south pole; like poles repel and opposite poles attract. A long straight current produces circular field lines around the wire, with direction given by the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl along the field.

### Force on a moving charge

A charge $q$ moving with velocity $v$ at angle $\theta$ to a field $B$ feels a force of magnitude

$$F = qvB\sin\theta .$$

The force is **perpendicular** to both the velocity and the field (right-hand rule for positive charge: fingers along $\vec v$, curl toward $\vec B$, thumb along $\vec F$; reverse for a negative charge). Because the force is always sideways, it does **no work** and changes the direction of the velocity but not the speed. A charge moving perpendicular to a uniform field travels in a circle:

$$qvB = \frac{mv^2}{r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = \frac{mv}{qB}.$$

### Force on a current-carrying wire

A straight wire of length $L$ carrying current $I$ in a field $B$ feels

$$F = BIL\sin\theta,$$

with direction again from the right-hand rule, using the current direction in place of $\vec v$. This is the force that turns an electric motor.

### Magnetic flux and Faraday's law

The **magnetic flux** through a loop of area $A$ is

$$\Phi = BA\cos\theta,$$

measured in webers ($1\ \text{Wb} = 1\ \text{T}\cdot\text{m}^2$), where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec B$ and the normal to the loop. **Faraday's law** says a changing flux induces an emf in a coil of $N$ turns:

$$\mathcal{E} = -N\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}.$$

Flux can change by changing $B$, $A$ or $\theta$. A rod of length $L$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a field has a motional emf $\mathcal{E} = BLv$.

### Lenz's law

The minus sign is **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows in the direction that **opposes the change in flux** that caused it. Pushing a magnet's north pole toward a coil makes the coil develop its own north pole facing the magnet, resisting the approach. This is conservation of energy: you must do work to produce the current.

### Transformers

A **transformer** uses a changing flux in an iron core to link two coils. For an ideal transformer, with $N_p$ primary and $N_s$ secondary turns,

$$\frac{V_s}{V_p} = \frac{N_s}{N_p}, \qquad V_pI_p = V_sI_s.$$

More secondary turns step the voltage up (and the current down). Transformers work only with changing (AC) currents; this is why power lines transmit at high voltage with lower current and less $I^2R$ loss.

@example
title: A rod moving in a magnetic field
problem: A $0.50\ \text{m}$ metal rod slides at $3.0\ \text{m/s}$ perpendicular to a uniform $0.40\ \text{T}$ field, along rails connected to a $2.0\ \Omega$ resistor. Find the emf and the current.
step: Use the motional emf: $\mathcal{E} = BLv$.
step: $\mathcal{E} = (0.40)(0.50)(3.0) = 0.60\ \text{V}$.
step: By Ohm's law, $I = \mathcal{E}/R = 0.60/2.0 = 0.30\ \text{A}$.
step: Lenz's law says this current produces a magnetic force on the rod that opposes its motion, so a steady push is needed to keep the speed constant.

@mistakes
- Treating the magnetic force as acting along the velocity or the field; it is perpendicular to both.
- Using $\sin\theta$ when the formula needs the angle with the loop's normal (flux uses $\cos\theta$ with the normal).
- Forgetting that only a **changing** flux induces an emf; a steady field through a steady loop gives none.
- Using a transformer with a battery (steady DC); no changing flux, no output.

@practice num
prompt: A straight wire $0.30\ \text{m}$ long carries a current of $4.0\ \text{A}$ perpendicular to a uniform $0.50\ \text{T}$ magnetic field. What is the magnitude of the force on the wire, in newtons?
answer: 0.6
check: 0.50 * 4.0 * 0.30
tol: 0.01
hint: Use $F = BIL\sin\theta$ with $\theta = 90^\circ$.
hint: Multiply $B$, $I$ and $L$.
solution: $F = (0.50)(4.0)(0.30)\sin 90^\circ = 0.60\ \text{N}$.
unit: N

@practice num
prompt: A proton ($q = 1.6\times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) moves at $2.0\times 10^6\ \text{m/s}$ perpendicular to a $0.10\ \text{T}$ magnetic field. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force on it, in newtons?
answer: 3.2e-14
check: 1.6e-19 * 2.0e6 * 0.10
tol: 1%
hint: Use $F = qvB\sin\theta$ with $\theta = 90^\circ$.
hint: $1.6\times 10^{-19} \times 2.0\times 10^6 = 3.2\times 10^{-13}$, then multiply by $0.10$.
solution: $F = (1.6\times 10^{-19})(2.0\times 10^6)(0.10) = 3.2\times 10^{-14}\ \text{N}$.
unit: N

@practice num
prompt: A flat loop of area $0.050\ \text{m}^2$ is perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of $0.20\ \text{T}$. What is the magnetic flux through the loop, in webers?
answer: 0.01
check: 0.20 * 0.050
tol: 0.0001
hint: Flux is $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$, and the field is along the loop's normal.
hint: $\cos 0^\circ = 1$.
solution: $\Phi = (0.20)(0.050)(1) = 0.010\ \text{Wb}$.
unit: Wb

@practice mc
prompt: The north pole of a bar magnet is pushed toward a closed loop of wire. According to Lenz's law, which describes the induced current?
choice: It flows so that the loop's field points in the same direction as the magnet's field, strengthening it.
choice*: It flows so that the loop's own field opposes the increase in flux from the approaching magnet.
choice: No current is induced because the magnet is not touching the loop.
choice: The current flows only after the magnet stops moving.
hint: Lenz's law: the induced effect opposes the change that causes it.
solution: The flux through the loop from the magnet is increasing, so the induced current creates a field opposing that increase: the loop's near face acts like a north pole, repelling the magnet.

@practice num
prompt: An ideal transformer has $100$ turns on its primary and $500$ turns on its secondary. The primary is connected to a $120\ \text{V}$ AC source. What is the secondary voltage in volts?
answer: 600
check: 120 * 500 / 100
hint: Voltage is proportional to the number of turns.
hint: $V_s = V_p N_s/N_p$.
solution: $V_s = 120(500/100) = 600\ \text{V}$. This is a step-up transformer.
unit: V

@quiz num
prompt: A coil of $50$ turns has a magnetic flux through it that falls uniformly from $0.040\ \text{Wb}$ to $0.010\ \text{Wb}$ in $0.50\ \text{s}$. What is the magnitude of the induced emf, in volts?
answer: 3
check: 50 * (0.040 - 0.010) / 0.50
tol: 0.001
hint: Use Faraday's law and ignore the minus sign when asked for the magnitude.
hint: $\Delta\Phi = 0.030\ \text{Wb}$; multiply by $N$ and divide by $\Delta t$.
solution: $|\mathcal{E}| = N|\Delta\Phi|/\Delta t = 50(0.030)/0.50 = 3.0\ \text{V}$.
unit: V

@quiz num
prompt: An electron ($m = 9.11\times 10^{-31}\ \text{kg}$, $q = 1.6\times 10^{-19}\ \text{C}$) moves at $3.0\times 10^6\ \text{m/s}$ perpendicular to a uniform $0.010\ \text{T}$ field. What is the radius of its circular path, in millimeters, to the nearest $0.01\ \text{mm}$?
answer: 1.71
check: 9.11e-31 * 3.0e6 / (1.6e-19 * 0.010) * 1000
tol: 1%
hint: The magnetic force supplies the centripetal force.
hint: $r = mv/(qB)$; convert meters to millimeters at the end.
solution: $r = (9.11\times 10^{-31})(3.0\times 10^6)/[(1.6\times 10^{-19})(0.010)] \approx 1.71\times 10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 1.71\ \text{mm}$.
unit: mm
