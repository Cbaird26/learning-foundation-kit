@article
**Kinematics** describes how things move without asking why. In one dimension, position is a signed number $x$ along a line, so direction is just a plus or minus sign.

### Displacement, velocity, acceleration

**Displacement** is the change in position, $\Delta x = x_f - x_i$. It can be negative, and it differs from **distance**, which is the total path length and is never negative. **Average velocity** is displacement per unit time, $\bar v = \Delta x/\Delta t$, while **speed** is distance per unit time. **Acceleration** is the rate of change of velocity, $a = \Delta v/\Delta t$, in $\text{m/s}^2$.

If velocity and acceleration have the same sign the object speeds up; opposite signs mean it slows down.

### Reading graphs

On a **position-time graph**, the slope is velocity. On a **velocity-time graph**, the slope is acceleration and the **area between the curve and the time axis is displacement** (area below the axis counts as negative). A horizontal line on a velocity-time graph means constant velocity, zero acceleration.

### The constant-acceleration equations

When acceleration is constant, four equations connect five quantities: $v_0$, $v$, $a$, $t$ and $\Delta x$.

$$v = v_0 + at$$
$$\Delta x = v_0 t + \tfrac{1}{2}at^2$$
$$v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$$
$$\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}(v_0 + v)\,t$$

Each equation leaves out one quantity. Strategy: list what you know, name what you want, and pick the equation that contains exactly those quantities. The third equation is the one to use when time is neither given nor asked for.

### Free fall

Near Earth's surface, ignoring air resistance, every falling or rising object has the same acceleration, $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ directed downward. If you choose up as positive, then $a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ for the whole flight, including the instant at the top where the velocity is momentarily zero. An object thrown upward returns to its starting height with the same speed it left with, and the time up equals the time down.

Always state your sign convention before you start, keep units in meters and seconds, and check that your answer has a sensible sign and size.

@example
title: Accelerating over a known distance
problem: A cyclist moving at $10\ \text{m/s}$ speeds up at a constant $2\ \text{m/s}^2$ while covering $24\ \text{m}$. Find her final speed and the time taken.
step: List the knowns: $v_0 = 10\ \text{m/s}$, $a = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$, $\Delta x = 24\ \text{m}$. Time is not given, so use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$.
step: Substitute: $v^2 = 100 + 2(2)(24) = 196$.
step: Take the positive root because she keeps moving forward: $v = 14\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Now find $t$ from $v = v_0 + at$: $t = (14 - 10)/2 = 2\ \text{s}$. Check with $\Delta x = \tfrac12(10+14)(2) = 24\ \text{m}$. It agrees.

@mistakes
- Using the kinematic equations when acceleration is not constant, or mixing units such as km/h with m/s.
- Confusing distance with displacement, or speed with velocity; a round trip has zero displacement and zero average velocity.
- Treating the top of a throw as having zero acceleration; velocity is zero there, but acceleration is still $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ downward.
- Reading the slope of a velocity-time graph as displacement instead of acceleration.

@practice num
prompt: A car starts from rest and accelerates uniformly at $3\ \text{m/s}^2$ for $8\ \text{s}$. What is its final speed in m/s?
answer: 24
check: 0 + 3 * 8
hint: Use $v = v_0 + at$.
hint: The car starts from rest, so $v_0 = 0$.
solution: $v = 0 + (3)(8) = 24\ \text{m/s}$.
unit: m/s

@practice num
prompt: A runner moving at $5\ \text{m/s}$ accelerates uniformly at $2\ \text{m/s}^2$ for $4\ \text{s}$. How far does she travel during those $4\ \text{s}$, in meters?
answer: 36
check: 5 * 4 + 0.5 * 2 * 4**2
hint: Time is given and you want displacement, so use $\Delta x = v_0 t + \tfrac12 a t^2$.
hint: The two terms are $5 \times 4$ and $\tfrac12 \times 2 \times 16$.
solution: $\Delta x = (5)(4) + \tfrac12(2)(4)^2 = 20 + 16 = 36\ \text{m}$.
unit: m

@practice num
prompt: A car traveling at $20\ \text{m/s}$ brakes with a constant acceleration of $-5\ \text{m/s}^2$ until it stops. What is its stopping distance in meters?
answer: 40
check: 20**2 / (2 * 5)
hint: Time is not given or asked for, so use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$ with $v = 0$.
hint: $0 = 400 + 2(-5)\Delta x$.
solution: $0 = 20^2 + 2(-5)\Delta x$, so $10\,\Delta x = 400$ and $\Delta x = 40\ \text{m}$.
unit: m

@practice num
prompt: A stone is dropped from rest from a height of $78.4\ \text{m}$. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, how long does it take to reach the ground, in seconds?
answer: 4
check: sqrt(2 * 78.4 / 9.8)
tol: 0.01
hint: Take down as positive, so $a = +9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and $v_0 = 0$.
hint: $\Delta x = \tfrac12 g t^2$, so $t = \sqrt{2\,\Delta x/g}$.
solution: $78.4 = \tfrac12 (9.8) t^2$ gives $t^2 = 16$, so $t = 4\ \text{s}$.
unit: s

@quiz num
prompt: A ball is thrown straight up at $19.6\ \text{m/s}$. Ignoring air resistance and using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what maximum height above the launch point does it reach, in meters?
answer: 19.6
check: 19.6**2 / (2 * 9.8)
tol: 0.01
hint: At the top the velocity is zero. Choose up as positive so $a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.
hint: Use $v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a\,\Delta x$ with $v = 0$.
solution: $0 = 19.6^2 + 2(-9.8)\Delta x$, so $\Delta x = 384.16/19.6 = 19.6\ \text{m}$.
unit: m

@quiz num
prompt: A velocity-time graph shows a train moving at a constant $4\ \text{m/s}$ for $5\ \text{s}$, then slowing uniformly to rest over the next $3\ \text{s}$. What is its total displacement in meters?
answer: 26
check: 4 * 5 + 0.5 * 4 * 3
hint: Displacement is the area between the graph and the time axis.
hint: Split the area into a rectangle and a triangle.
solution: Rectangle: $4 \times 5 = 20\ \text{m}$. Triangle: $\tfrac12 \times 3 \times 4 = 6\ \text{m}$. Total $26\ \text{m}$.
unit: m
