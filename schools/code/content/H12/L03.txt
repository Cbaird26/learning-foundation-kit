@article
Between 1803 and 1848 the United States roughly tripled in size. The **Louisiana Purchase** (1803) bought French claims to land west of the Mississippi, land long inhabited by Native nations. Texas, independent from Mexico since 1836, was annexed in 1845. After the **Mexican-American War** (1846 to 1848), the **Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo** transferred a huge territory (the Mexican Cession) to the United States. Mexican residents there were promised citizenship rights, though many later lost land and faced discrimination.

### Native nations and removal

Expansion cost Native peoples their lands. The **Indian Removal Act** (1830) authorized negotiating the exchange of Native lands in the Southeast for land west of the Mississippi. The Cherokee challenged Georgia's claims in court, and in *Worcester v. Georgia* (1832) the Supreme Court ruled that Georgia could not extend its laws over Cherokee territory. Even so, removal went ahead, and in 1838 and 1839 thousands of Cherokee people died on the forced march known as the **Trail of Tears**. Other nations, including the Choctaw, Muscogee (Creek), Chickasaw and Seminole, were also removed.

### Slavery and the sections

The cotton gin (1793) made cotton highly profitable and expanded slavery across the South. By 1860 nearly four million people were enslaved. In the North, industry and free labor grew, although Northern merchants and banks still profited from the cotton economy. Enslaved people resisted in many ways, from daily noncooperation to escape and revolt. Free Black communities, churches and newspapers, and the abolitionist movement (Black and white, women and men), pressed the case against slavery. Many white Southerners defended slavery as a legal property right and as part of their way of life; abolitionists replied that it was a moral wrong that violated the principles of the Declaration.

The fight kept returning to a single question: would slavery spread into the territories?

- The **Missouri Compromise** (1820) admitted Missouri as a slave state and Maine as a free state, and barred slavery north of 36°30′ in the rest of the Louisiana Purchase.
- The **Compromise of 1850** admitted California as a free state, allowed popular choice in other territories, and passed a stronger **Fugitive Slave Act** that required citizens to help return people who had escaped.
- The **Kansas-Nebraska Act** (1854) let settlers decide, repealing the Missouri line and leading to violence in "Bleeding Kansas."
- In *Dred Scott v. Sandford* (1857), the Supreme Court held that Black people could not be citizens and that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories.

The Republican Party, formed in the 1850s, opposed the spread of slavery. In 1858 a Republican leader warned: "A house divided against itself cannot stand." When Abraham Lincoln won the presidency in 1860 without carrying a Southern state, seven states seceded, and four more joined after the fighting began at Fort Sumter in April 1861.

### Causes: what did people say?

Historians agree that slavery was the central cause of the war. Secession documents from several states named the protection of slavery as their reason. Some Southerners and later writers emphasized "states' rights," a constitutional argument over the powers of the states; historians note that in these debates the right most at stake was the right to hold slaves and take them into territories. Economic differences, nationalism and party politics also mattered. To judge a cause, check who is speaking, when, and for what audience.

### The war

The Union had more people, industry and railroads; the Confederacy hoped to win through defense and foreign help. The **Emancipation Proclamation** (January 1, 1863) declared enslaved people in areas in rebellion to be free and opened the army to Black soldiers; about 180,000 served in the Union army. Victories at Gettysburg and Vicksburg in July 1863 turned the tide. At Gettysburg, Lincoln spoke of "government of the people, by the people, for the people." Lee surrendered at Appomattox in April 1865, and the **Thirteenth Amendment** ended slavery: "Neither slavery nor involuntary servitude, except as a punishment for crime whereof the party shall have been duly convicted, shall exist within the United States, or any place subject to their jurisdiction." About 620,000 soldiers died.

@example
title: Weighing a source on the causes of secession
problem: A state's secession declaration says that the Northern states had refused to protect slaveholders' property and had attacked the institution of slavery. How should you use it as evidence about the causes of the Civil War?
step: Identify the source: a public declaration by the secessionist government, written in 1860 to 1861 to justify leaving the Union. It is a primary source and shows what leaders chose to say at the time.
step: Note its content. It names slavery and the property claims of slaveholders as grievances, so it supports the claim that protecting slavery was central.
step: Consider limits: it represents political leaders, not every person in the state, including the enslaved and Unionists, and it was written to persuade.
step: Corroborate with other sources, such as congressional debates, election results and Lincoln's speeches. When several independent sources agree, the conclusion is stronger.

@mistakes
- Treating "states' rights" as a complete explanation without asking which rights were being defended.
- Believing the Emancipation Proclamation freed every enslaved person immediately; it applied to areas in rebellion, and the Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery nationwide.
- Ignoring Native and Mexican experiences when discussing expansion, as if the land were empty.
