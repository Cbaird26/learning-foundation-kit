@article
Scientific and medical articles are written to be checked. They report what was done, what was found and how sure the authors are. News stories and press releases, by contrast, are written to be read quickly, so details can shrink or inflate. Learning to read the original structure lets you tell the difference.

### IMRaD: where to look

Most research articles follow **IMRaD**:

- **Introduction**: the problem, what is already known and the research question.
- **Methods**: participants, design, measurements and analysis. This is where you judge whether the study can answer its question.
- **Results**: the data, in text, tables and figures, without interpretation.
- **Discussion**: what the authors think the results mean, how they fit other work, and the **limitations**.

The **abstract** summarizes all four. Read it first, but do not stop there: abstracts leave out limitations. For a quick evaluation, read the abstract, then the methods, then the limitations paragraph.

### Study design and what it can show

| Design | Description | Best for | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)** | Participants randomly assigned to treatment or control | Showing that an intervention causes an effect | Cost, short follow-up, narrow participants |
| **Cohort study** | Follows groups with and without an exposure over time | Studying risk factors and outcomes | Confounding, since groups were not randomized |
| **Case-control study** | Compares people with an outcome to people without it, looking back at exposures | Rare outcomes | Recall bias, can't give rates directly |
| **Cross-sectional study** | A snapshot at one time | Prevalence | Cannot show which came first |
| **Case report or series** | Describes one or a few patients | Raising hypotheses | No comparison group |

**Correlation is not causation.** In an observational study, people who drink coffee may also differ in income, sleep or exercise. These other factors are **confounders**. Only randomization is designed to balance confounders, which is why RCTs carry the most weight for treatment questions.

### Hedging and certainty

Scientific prose is careful: "was associated with," "suggests," "may." These verbs are honest signals of what a design allows. News headlines often drop them ("Coffee prevents heart disease"). When you summarize, preserve the hedge.

### Absolute and relative risk

Suppose an event happens in $c$ percent of the control group and $t$ percent of the treatment group.

- **Absolute risk reduction (ARR)** $= c - t$ (in percentage points).
- **Relative risk reduction (RRR)** $= \dfrac{c - t}{c}$.
- **Number needed to treat (NNT)** $= \dfrac{100}{c - t}$ when $c$ and $t$ are percentages.

A drug that cuts risk from $2.0\%$ to $1.5\%$ has ARR $= 0.5$ percentage points, RRR $= 25\%$ and NNT $= 200$. A headline might say "cuts risk by 25 percent," which is true but sounds larger than "0.5 percentage points." Neither is wrong, but a fair summary gives both.

### Statistical significance versus importance

A small **p-value** says the data would be unusual if there were no effect. It does not say the effect is large or important. A **confidence interval** shows the range of plausible values, so check its width and whether it includes no effect. Also check **sample size**: tiny samples give unstable results.

@example
title: Checking a headline against its study
problem: Invented headline: "New pill slashes stroke risk by 25%." The study reports strokes in 2.0% of the placebo group and 1.5% of the pill group over three years, in 4,000 randomized adults. Evaluate the headline.
step: Identify the design. Random assignment to pill or placebo makes this an RCT, so a causal conclusion about the pill is reasonable.
step: Compute ARR: $2.0 - 1.5 = 0.5$ percentage points.
step: Compute RRR: $\frac{0.5}{2.0} = 25\%$. The headline's number is correct but is the relative figure.
step: Compute NNT: $\frac{100}{0.5} = 200$, so about 200 people must take the pill for three years to prevent one stroke.
step: Write the fair summary: "Over three years, strokes occurred in 1.5% of people taking the pill and 2.0% of those taking placebo, a 25% relative reduction (0.5 percentage points)."

@mistakes
- Reading only the abstract or the headline and skipping the methods and limitations.
- Treating an association from an observational study as proof of cause.
- Reporting relative risk without the absolute numbers, which can exaggerate a small effect.
- Confusing statistical significance with practical importance.
