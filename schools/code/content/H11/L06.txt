@article
**Style** is the sum of choices a writer makes about words and sentences. **Syntax** is the arrangement of words into phrases and clauses. **Voice** is the personality a reader hears: formal or warm, brisk or reflective. Good style is not decoration. It is clarity with control.

### Sentence types and effects

- A **simple** sentence has one independent clause: "The test failed."
- A **compound** sentence joins independent clauses: "The test failed, and the team stopped."
- A **complex** sentence has an independent and a dependent clause: "Because the test failed, the team stopped."
- A **compound-complex** sentence has two or more independent clauses and at least one dependent clause.

A short sentence after several long ones lands with force. A long sentence can build a chain of reasoning. Monotone length, in either direction, tires the reader. Vary the pattern, and put the most important information at the **end** of the sentence, where emphasis is strongest.

### Concision

Concise does not mean short. It means every word does work. Cut:

- **Wordy phrases**: "due to the fact that" becomes "because"; "at this point in time" becomes "now."
- **Empty openers**: "There is a reason why..." becomes a direct statement.
- **Doubled words**: "each and every," "basic fundamentals."
- **Nominalizations**, verbs buried in nouns: "make a decision" becomes "decide"; "conduct an analysis of" becomes "analyze."

Revise: "It is important to note that the committee made a decision to conduct a review." (15 words) becomes "The committee decided to review." (5 words)

### Active and passive voice

In an **active** sentence the actor is the subject: "The nurse recorded the dose." In a **passive** sentence the receiver is the subject: "The dose was recorded by the nurse." Active voice is usually clearer and shorter. The passive is useful when the actor is unknown or unimportant, or when the receiver is the topic ("The samples were stored at 4 degrees Celsius" in a methods section). The problem is not the passive itself but the passive used to hide who did something: "Mistakes were made."

### Parallelism and rhythm

Items in a series should share a grammatical form. Not parallel: "The course teaches reading, to write essays and how to research." Parallel: "The course teaches reading, writing and researching." Parallel structure also creates rhythm and emphasis, as in Lincoln's "of the people, by the people, for the people."

### Voice and register

Match voice to audience. A college essay can be personal and vivid. A lab report uses precise, neutral language. A business email is courteous and direct. Choose concrete nouns and strong verbs over vague abstractions, and read your drafts aloud: your ear catches clumsy rhythm that your eye misses.

@example
title: Revising a bloated sentence
problem: Revise: "There are many students who are of the opinion that the decision that was made by the board to cancel the program was one that was made in a hasty manner." Make it concise and active.
step: Find the actor and action. The board decided to cancel the program, and students think the decision was hasty.
step: Cut the empty opener "There are" and the nominalization "are of the opinion." Replace with a strong verb: "think" or "believe."
step: Convert the passive "was made by the board" to active: "the board decided."
step: Revised: "Many students believe the board hastily canceled the program." This keeps the meaning in 9 words instead of 31.

@mistakes
- Equating concision with short, choppy sentences, and losing the connections between ideas.
- Using passive voice to avoid naming the actor when the reader needs to know.
- Faulty parallelism in lists and comparisons.
- Replacing precise words with vague ones to sound formal.
