@review
### How to use this guide

Work through the weeks in order. For each one, try to say the key idea aloud before reading the reminder, then redo any quiz items you missed. After the final, wait one to two weeks and take a fresh cumulative assessment, then explain one major result aloud without notes.

### Key results and methods, week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2: limits and continuity.** A limit describes what $f(x)$ approaches as $x \to a$, ignoring $f(a)$ itself. When substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, factor and cancel or multiply by a conjugate, and compare one-sided limits. $f$ is continuous at $a$ when $f(a)$ exists, the limit exists, and they are equal. Discontinuities are removable, jump or infinite. The Intermediate Value Theorem needs continuity on all of $[a, b]$.

**Weeks 3 and 4: the derivative.** $f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$ is the tangent slope and the instantaneous rate of change. Differentiable implies continuous, not the converse. The power, sum, product and quotient rules, with the table of basic derivatives, handle nearly everything.

**Weeks 5 and 6: chain rule, implicit differentiation, related rates.** $\frac{d}{dx}f(g(x)) = f'(g(x))\,g'(x)$. In implicit differentiation every $y$ carries a factor $y'$. In related rates, draw and label, write one relating equation, differentiate in $t$, and only then substitute values.

**Weeks 7 and 8: optimization and curves.** Extremes occur at critical numbers or endpoints. Justify with the closed interval method, the first derivative test or the second derivative test. The sign of $f'$ gives increasing or decreasing; the sign of $f''$ gives concavity; an inflection point needs a sign change of $f''$.

**Weeks 9 and 10: antiderivatives and definite integrals.** $\int f\,dx = F(x) + C$, with initial conditions fixing $C$. Riemann sums $\sum f(c_i)\,\Delta x$ approximate $\int_a^b f\,dx$, which is signed area and net change. Displacement uses $v$; total distance uses $\lvert v \rvert$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: the Fundamental Theorem and substitution.** $\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)$ and $\int_a^b f\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. Substitution is the chain rule in reverse, and the limits change along with the variable.

### Self-check list

- I can evaluate a limit that gives $\frac{0}{0}$ and decide whether a function is continuous at a point.
- I can derive the derivative of a simple function from the limit definition.
- I can differentiate products, quotients and compositions without looking at a table.
- I can set up a related-rates problem with a labelled diagram and units.
- I can solve an optimization problem and justify that the answer is a maximum or minimum.
- I can sketch a graph from sign charts of $f'$ and $f''$.
- I can solve an initial value problem and recover position from acceleration.
- I can compute a Riemann sum and say whether it over- or underestimates.
- I can evaluate a definite integral with the Fundamental Theorem, including by substitution.
- I can explain aloud why differentiation and integration are inverse processes.

### The most common errors

- Substituting $h = 0$, or an instantaneous value in a related-rates problem, too early.
- Dropping the inner derivative in the chain rule, or the factor $y'$ in implicit differentiation.
- Treating $f'(c) = 0$ as proof of an extremum, or $f''(c) = 0$ as proof of an inflection point.
- Forgetting the constant $C$ in an indefinite integral, or adding it to a definite one.
- Confusing displacement with total distance, and forgetting that area below the axis is negative.
- In substitution, leaving $x$ in the integral or keeping the old limits.

prompt: Let $$f(x) = \begin{cases} ax^2 + bx, & x \le 2, \\ 3x - 4, & x > 2. \end{cases}$$ Find the constants $(a, b)$ for which $f$ is differentiable at $x = 2$.
answer: (1, -1)
check: tuple(lin([[4, 2], [4, 1]], [3*2 - 4, 3]))
hint: Differentiability at $x = 2$ needs two conditions. First, $f$ must be continuous there, so both pieces must give the same value at $x = 2$.
hint: Second, the slopes of the two pieces must agree at $x = 2$. Differentiate each piece and set the derivatives equal there.
hint: You get two linear equations in $a$ and $b$: $4a + 2b = 2$ from continuity, and one from the slopes. Solve them together.
solution: Continuity at $2$: $4a + 2b = 3(2) - 4 = 2$. Slopes at $2$: the left piece has derivative $2ax + b$, which equals $4a + b$ at $x = 2$, and the right piece has slope $3$, so $4a + b = 3$. Subtracting the second equation from the first gives $b = -1$, and then $4a = 4$, so $a = 1$. Check: the left piece is $x^2 - x$, with value $2$ and slope $3$ at $x = 2$, matching $3x - 4$.

@exam num
prompt: Let $A(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^x (1 - 2t)\,e^{t - t^2}\,dt$ for $x \ge 0$. Find the maximum value of $A$ on $[0, \infty)$. Give an exact value.
answer: e^(1/4) - 1
check: max(quad(lambda t: (1 - 2*t)*exp(t - t**2), 0, x) for x in np.linspace(0, 3, 601))
hint: Use the Fundamental Theorem, Part 1, to write $A'(x)$ without computing the integral, and study its sign.
hint: $A'(x) = (1 - 2x)e^{x - x^2}$. The exponential factor is always positive, so the sign of $A'$ is the sign of $1 - 2x$. Where does it change?
hint: To evaluate $A$ at the critical number, use the substitution $u = t - t^2$, for which $du = (1 - 2t)\,dt$.
solution: By the Fundamental Theorem, $A'(x) = (1 - 2x)e^{x - x^2}$, which is positive for $x < \frac{1}{2}$ and negative for $x > \frac{1}{2}$. So $A$ rises and then falls, and its maximum is at $x = \frac{1}{2}$. With $u = t - t^2$ and $du = (1 - 2t)\,dt$, we get $A(x) = \int_0^{x - x^2} e^u\,du = e^{x - x^2} - 1$. Therefore $A\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = e^{1/4} - 1 \approx 0.284$.

@exam self
prompt: A particle moves along a line with acceleration $a(t) = 2t - 6\ \text{m/s}^2$ for $0 \le t \le 6$ s. Its initial velocity is $v(0) = 8$ m/s and its initial position is $s(0) = 1$ m. (a) Find $v(t)$ and $s(t)$. (b) Find the times at which the particle is at rest. (c) On which intervals is the particle speeding up, and on which is it slowing down? (d) Find the displacement and the total distance travelled on $[0, 6]$. (e) In one or two sentences, explain how part (d) uses the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
hint: Integrate $a(t)$ once and fix the constant with $v(0)$. Then integrate again and fix the constant with $s(0)$.
hint: Factor $v(t)$ to find the rest times. Then make sign charts for $v$ and for $a$: the particle speeds up when they have the same sign.
hint: Total distance needs $\lvert v \rvert$. Evaluate $s$ at $t = 0$, at each rest time and at $t = 6$, and add the sizes of the changes.
solution: (a) $v(t) = t^2 - 6t + C_1$ and $v(0) = 8$ give $v(t) = t^2 - 6t + 8$. Then $s(t) = \frac{t^3}{3} - 3t^2 + 8t + C_2$ and $s(0) = 1$ give $s(t) = \frac{t^3}{3} - 3t^2 + 8t + 1$. (b) $v(t) = (t - 2)(t - 4) = 0$ at $t = 2$ s and $t = 4$ s. (c) $v > 0$ on $[0, 2)$, $v < 0$ on $(2, 4)$ and $v > 0$ on $(4, 6]$, while $a = 2t - 6$ is negative for $t < 3$ and positive for $t > 3$. The particle speeds up when $v$ and $a$ have the same sign, on $(2, 3)$ and $(4, 6)$, and slows down when the signs differ, on $(0, 2)$ and $(3, 4)$. (d) $s(0) = 1$, $s(2) = \frac{23}{3}$, $s(4) = \frac{19}{3}$ and $s(6) = 13$. The displacement is $s(6) - s(0) = 12$ m. The total distance is $\frac{20}{3} + \frac{4}{3} + \frac{20}{3} = \frac{44}{3} \approx 14.7$ m. (e) Since $s$ is an antiderivative of $v$, Part 2 gives displacement $= \int_0^6 v\,dt = s(6) - s(0)$; the total distance is $\int_0^6 \lvert v \rvert\,dt$, found by applying $F(b) - F(a)$ on each stretch between rest times and adding the sizes.

rubric: Finds the rest times $t = 2$ and $t = 4$ and uses sign charts of $v$ and $a$ to identify the speeding-up and slowing-down intervals.
rubric: Computes the displacement $12$ m and the total distance $\frac{44}{3}$ m, splitting at the rest times.
rubric: Explains that $s$ is an antiderivative of $v$, so displacement is $s(6) - s(0)$ by the Fundamental Theorem, and that distance integrates $\lvert v \rvert$.
