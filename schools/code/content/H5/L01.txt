@article
Calculus rests on one idea: what happens to a quantity as something gets **close** to a value. That idea is the **limit**.

### Getting close without arriving

Take $f(x) = \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$. You cannot substitute $x = 1$, because that gives $\frac{0}{0}$. But you can try inputs near $1$:

| $x$ | $0.9$ | $0.99$ | $0.999$ | $1.001$ | $1.01$ | $1.1$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $f(x)$ | $1.9$ | $1.99$ | $1.999$ | $2.001$ | $2.01$ | $2.1$ |

The outputs close in on $2$ from both sides, so we write $\lim_{x\to 1} f(x) = 2$. (Indeed $f(x) = x + 1$ for every $x \neq 1$.)

**Meaning.** $\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L$ says that $f(x)$ can be made as close to $L$ as you like by taking $x$ close enough to $a$, **but not equal to** $a$. The value $f(a)$ plays no role: it may differ from $L$, or not exist at all.

### One-sided limits

$\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x)$ uses only inputs $x < a$, and $\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x)$ uses only $x > a$. The two-sided limit exists exactly when both one-sided limits exist and are equal. For $g(x) = \frac{|x|}{x}$, every input left of $0$ gives $-1$ and every input right of $0$ gives $1$, so $\lim_{x\to 0} g(x)$ does not exist.

On a graph, trace the curve toward $x = a$ from each side and read the height it approaches. A dot at $x = a$ shows $f(a)$, which the limit ignores.

### Computing limits with algebra

The **limit laws** say that limits respect sums, constant multiples, products and quotients (as long as the limit of the denominator is not $0$). So for polynomials, and for rational functions whose denominator is nonzero at $a$, you can **substitute directly**.

If substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, nothing is decided yet: simplify, then substitute again.

- **Factor and cancel:** $\lim_{x\to 3}\frac{x^2-9}{x-3} = \lim_{x\to 3}(x+3) = 6$.
- **Rationalize** with the conjugate: $\frac{\sqrt{x+4}-2}{x} = \frac{(x+4)-4}{x(\sqrt{x+4}+2)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+4}+2}$, so the limit as $x \to 0$ is $\frac14$.
- **Combine fractions** over a common denominator, then factor.

If substitution gives a nonzero number over $0$, the values grow without bound near $a$, so check the sign on each side. $\frac{1}{(x-2)^2}$ is large and positive on both sides of $2$, and we write $\lim_{x\to 2}\frac{1}{(x-2)^2} = \infty$. Strictly, that limit does not exist as a number; the symbol $\infty$ records how it fails. But $\frac{1}{x-2}$ tends to $-\infty$ from the left and to $+\infty$ from the right, so not even that description fits its two-sided limit.

### A key trigonometric limit

With $x$ in **radians**,

$$\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1.$$

For $0 < |x| < \frac{\pi}{2}$ one can show $\cos x \le \frac{\sin x}{x} \le 1$. Both bounds tend to $1$, so the middle is squeezed to $1$ (the **squeeze theorem**). Rescaling handles variants: $\frac{\sin 5x}{x} = 5\cdot\frac{\sin 5x}{5x} \to 5$.

### Limits at infinity

As $x \to \infty$, divide the numerator and denominator by the highest power of $x$ in the denominator:

$$\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x^2+1}{2x^2-x} = \lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3+\frac{1}{x^2}}{2-\frac{1}{x}} = \frac{3}{2}.$$

A table of values is a good way to **guess** a limit; algebra is how you **confirm** it.

@example
title: Factor and cancel
problem: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to -2}\frac{x^2+5x+6}{x^2-4}$.
step: Try direct substitution. The numerator is $4 - 10 + 6 = 0$ and the denominator is $4 - 4 = 0$. The form $\frac{0}{0}$ says: simplify first.
step: Factor both parts: $\frac{x^2+5x+6}{x^2-4} = \frac{(x+2)(x+3)}{(x+2)(x-2)}$.
step: For $x \neq -2$ the common factor cancels, leaving $\frac{x+3}{x-2}$. The limit never uses $x = -2$ itself, so both expressions have the same limit.
step: Now substitute: $\frac{-2+3}{-2-2} = -\frac{1}{4}$. So $\lim_{x\to -2}\frac{x^2+5x+6}{x^2-4} = -\frac{1}{4}$.

@example
title: A nonzero number over zero
problem: Describe $\lim_{x\to 1^-}\frac{x+2}{x-1}$, $\lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{x+2}{x-1}$ and $\lim_{x\to 1}\frac{x+2}{x-1}$.
step: Substitution gives $\frac{3}{0}$: the numerator approaches $3$ while the denominator approaches $0$, so the quotient grows without bound. Only the signs remain to settle.
step: From the left ($x < 1$), $x - 1$ is a small negative number, so the quotient is a large negative number: $\lim_{x\to 1^-}\frac{x+2}{x-1} = -\infty$.
step: From the right ($x > 1$), $x - 1$ is small and positive: $\lim_{x\to 1^+}\frac{x+2}{x-1} = +\infty$.
step: The two sides behave differently, so the two-sided limit does not exist. The graph has a vertical asymptote at $x = 1$.

@mistakes
- Deciding a limit from $f(a)$ alone: a hole, a misplaced dot or an undefined value at $a$ says nothing about the limit.
- Reading $\frac{0}{0}$ as $0$, as $1$ or as "does not exist", instead of as a signal to simplify.
- Cancelling terms instead of factors, such as striking out the $x$ in $\frac{x+2}{x}$.
- Using degrees with $\frac{\sin x}{x}$: the limit $1$ holds only when $x$ is in radians.

prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to 2}\left(3x^2 - 4x + 1\right)$.
answer: 5
check: lim(lambda x: 3*x**2 - 4*x + 1, 2)
hint: A polynomial has no gaps or jumps, so you may substitute $x = 2$ directly.
hint: Compute $3(2)^2 - 4(2) + 1$.
solution: Polynomials allow direct substitution: $3(4) - 8 + 1 = 5$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to 5}\frac{x^2-3x-10}{x-5}$.
answer: 7
check: lim(lambda x: (x**2 - 3*x - 10)/(x - 5), 5)
hint: Substituting $x = 5$ gives $\frac{0}{0}$, so simplify first.
hint: Factor the numerator: $x^2 - 3x - 10 = (x-5)(x+2)$.
solution: $\frac{(x-5)(x+2)}{x-5} = x + 2$ for $x \neq 5$, so the limit is $5 + 2 = 7$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}$. Give an exact answer.
answer: 1/6
check: lim(lambda x: (sqrt(x + 9) - 3)/x, 0)
hint: Substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$. Multiply the numerator and the denominator by the conjugate $\sqrt{x+9}+3$.
hint: The numerator becomes $(x+9) - 9 = x$, which cancels with the $x$ in the denominator.
solution: $\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+9}+3}{\sqrt{x+9}+3} = \frac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+9}+3)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}$. At $x = 0$ this equals $\frac{1}{3+3} = \frac{1}{6}$.

@practice mc
prompt: Let $g(x) = \frac{|x-3|}{x-3}$. Which statement is true?
choice*: $\lim_{x\to 3^-} g(x) = -1$ and $\lim_{x\to 3^+} g(x) = 1$, so $\lim_{x\to 3} g(x)$ does not exist.
choice: $\lim_{x\to 3} g(x) = 0$, the average of the two one-sided limits.
choice: $\lim_{x\to 3} g(x) = 1$, because $|x-3|$ and $x-3$ cancel.
choice: $\lim_{x\to 3} g(x)$ does not exist, and the only reason is that $g(3)$ is undefined.
hint: For $x > 3$, $|x-3| = x-3$. What does $|x-3|$ equal when $x < 3$?
hint: Compare the values of $g$ just left of $3$ with the values just right of $3$.
solution: For $x > 3$, $g(x) = \frac{x-3}{x-3} = 1$; for $x < 3$, $g(x) = \frac{-(x-3)}{x-3} = -1$. The one-sided limits are $-1$ and $1$. They differ, so the two-sided limit does not exist. Being undefined at $3$ would not, by itself, prevent a limit.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to 1}\left(\frac{1}{x-1} - \frac{2}{x^2-1}\right)$.
answer: 1/2
check: lim(lambda x: 1/(x - 1) - 2/(x**2 - 1), 1)
hint: Each term blows up at $x = 1$, but their difference might not. Write both over the common denominator $x^2 - 1 = (x-1)(x+1)$.
hint: The combined numerator is $(x + 1) - 2 = x - 1$.
solution: $\frac{1}{x-1} - \frac{2}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{x+1-2}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{x-1}{(x-1)(x+1)} = \frac{1}{x+1}$ for $x \neq 1$, so the limit is $\frac{1}{2}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to 0}\frac{\sin 3x}{2x}$, with $x$ in radians.
answer: 3/2
check: lim(lambda x: sin(3*x)/(2*x), 0)
hint: Make the denominator match the argument of sine: $\frac{\sin 3x}{2x} = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sin 3x}{3x}$.
hint: As $x \to 0$, also $3x \to 0$, so you can use $\lim_{u\to 0}\frac{\sin u}{u} = 1$.
solution: $\frac{\sin 3x}{2x} = \frac{3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sin 3x}{3x} \to \frac{3}{2}\cdot 1 = \frac{3}{2}$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(\sqrt{x^2+6x} - x\right)$.
answer: 3
check: liminf(lambda x: sqrt(x**2 + 6*x) - x)
hint: This has the form $\infty - \infty$, which settles nothing. Multiply and divide by the conjugate $\sqrt{x^2+6x} + x$.
hint: You get $\frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x}$. Divide the numerator and the denominator by $x$, using $\frac{\sqrt{x^2+6x}}{x} = \sqrt{1+\frac{6}{x}}$ for $x > 0$.
solution: $\sqrt{x^2+6x} - x = \frac{(x^2+6x) - x^2}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x} = \frac{6x}{\sqrt{x^2+6x}+x} = \frac{6}{\sqrt{1+\frac{6}{x}}+1} \to \frac{6}{1+1} = 3$.
