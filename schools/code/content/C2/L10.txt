@article
Number theory studies the integers, and it is the foundation of hashing, error-detecting codes and public-key cryptography. This week the goal is concrete: compute modular inverses and use them.

### Divisibility and the division algorithm

Recall $a \mid b$ means $b = ak$ for an integer $k$. For integers $a$ and $d > 0$ the **division algorithm** gives unique integers $q$ and $r$ with $a = qd + r$ and $0 \le r < d$. We write $r = a \bmod d$. In Python, `a % d` returns this non-negative remainder for $d > 0$, even for negative $a$, and `a // d` is $q$.

### The greatest common divisor

The **greatest common divisor** $\gcd(a, b)$ of integers that are not both zero is the largest integer dividing both. Integers with $\gcd = 1$ are **coprime**. The key fact for computation: $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$, because any common divisor of $a$ and $b$ divides $a - qb = a \bmod b$ and conversely. **Euclid's algorithm** repeats this until the remainder is $0$; the last non-zero remainder is the gcd. It runs in a number of steps proportional to the number of digits of the inputs (the number of steps is at most about $\log_\phi b$, where $\phi$ is the golden ratio), far faster than factoring.

```python
def gcd(a, b):
    while b:
        a, b = b, a % b
    return a
```

### Bézout's identity and the extended algorithm

**Bézout's identity.** For integers $a, b$ not both zero there are integers $x, y$ with $ax + by = \gcd(a, b)$. Back-substituting through the steps of Euclid's algorithm produces $x$ and $y$; this is the **extended Euclidean algorithm**. Every integer combination $ax + by$ is a multiple of $\gcd(a, b)$, and the gcd is the smallest positive such combination.

### Modular arithmetic

$a \equiv b \pmod m$ means $m \mid a - b$. Congruences can be added, subtracted and multiplied: if $a \equiv a'$ and $b \equiv b'$ then $a + b \equiv a' + b'$ and $ab \equiv a'b' \pmod m$. So you can reduce at every step: $7^{100} \bmod 10$ never needs a huge number. Division is the problem: $2 \cdot 3 \equiv 2 \cdot 8 \pmod{10}$ but $3 \not\equiv 8$.

The **modular inverse** of $a$ modulo $m$ is a number $x$ with $ax \equiv 1 \pmod m$. It **exists if and only if $\gcd(a, m) = 1$**. If $ax + my = 1$ (Bézout), then $ax \equiv 1 \pmod m$, so the inverse is $x \bmod m$; conversely if $ax \equiv 1$ then $ax - 1 = mk$ shows any common divisor of $a$ and $m$ divides $1$. Python 3.8 and later computes it as `pow(a, -1, m)`.

### Linear congruences

The congruence $ax \equiv b \pmod m$ has a solution if and only if $\gcd(a, m)$ divides $b$. When $\gcd(a, m) = 1$ the solution is unique modulo $m$: multiply by the inverse, $x \equiv a^{-1}b \pmod m$.

### Powers modulo $m$

**Repeated squaring** computes $a^e \bmod m$ in about $\log_2 e$ multiplications: use $a^{2k} = (a^k)^2$ and $a^{2k+1} = a \cdot (a^k)^2$, reducing mod $m$ after every multiplication. Python's `pow(a, e, m)` does this. **Fermat's little theorem**: if $p$ is prime and $p \nmid a$ then $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$. More generally **Euler's theorem**: if $\gcd(a, m) = 1$ then $a^{\varphi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m$, where $\varphi(m)$ counts the integers in $1, \dots, m$ coprime to $m$. Exponents can therefore be reduced modulo $p - 1$ (or $\varphi(m)$) when the base is coprime to the modulus. For a prime $p$, $\varphi(p) = p - 1$, and for distinct primes $p, q$, $\varphi(pq) = (p-1)(q-1)$.

@example
title: The extended Euclidean algorithm
problem: Find $\gcd(240, 46)$ and integers $x, y$ with $240x + 46y = \gcd(240, 46)$.
step: Run Euclid's algorithm: $240 = 5 \cdot 46 + 10$, $46 = 4 \cdot 10 + 6$, $10 = 1 \cdot 6 + 4$, $6 = 1 \cdot 4 + 2$, $4 = 2 \cdot 2 + 0$. The last non-zero remainder is $2$, so $\gcd = 2$.
step: Back-substitute from the bottom: $2 = 6 - 4$. Replace $4 = 10 - 6$: $2 = 2 \cdot 6 - 10$.
step: Replace $6 = 46 - 4 \cdot 10$: $2 = 2 \cdot 46 - 9 \cdot 10$. Replace $10 = 240 - 5 \cdot 46$: $2 = 47 \cdot 46 - 9 \cdot 240$.
step: So $x = -9$ and $y = 47$. Check: $240 \cdot (-9) + 46 \cdot 47 = -2160 + 2162 = 2$.
answer: $\gcd = 2$, with $240 \cdot (-9) + 46 \cdot 47 = 2$.

@example
title: Solving a linear congruence
problem: Solve $17x \equiv 5 \pmod{29}$.
step: Since $29$ is prime and $17 < 29$, $\gcd(17, 29) = 1$, so an inverse exists. Euclid: $29 = 1 \cdot 17 + 12$, $17 = 1 \cdot 12 + 5$, $12 = 2 \cdot 5 + 2$, $5 = 2 \cdot 2 + 1$.
step: Back-substitute: $1 = 5 - 2 \cdot 2 = 5 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 12 = 5 \cdot 17 - 7 \cdot 12 = 12 \cdot 17 - 7 \cdot 29$.
step: So $12 \cdot 17 \equiv 1 \pmod{29}$ and the inverse of $17$ is $12$. Check: $17 \cdot 12 = 204 = 7 \cdot 29 + 1$.
step: Multiply the congruence by $12$: $x \equiv 12 \cdot 5 = 60 \equiv 2 \pmod{29}$.
step: Check: $17 \cdot 2 = 34 \equiv 5 \pmod{29}$.
answer: $x \equiv 2 \pmod{29}$

@mistakes
- "Dividing" both sides of a congruence by a number that is not invertible modulo $m$, which loses or invents solutions.
- Forgetting that an inverse exists only when $\gcd(a, m) = 1$.
- Computing $a^e$ in full before reducing; with large exponents reduce modulo $m$ after each multiplication.
- Reducing the exponent modulo $m$ instead of modulo $p - 1$ (or $\varphi(m)$) when using Fermat's or Euler's theorem.

def ext_gcd(a, b):
    if b == 0:
        return (a, 1, 0)

model:
```python
def mod_inverse(a, m):
    old_r, r = a % m, m
    old_s, s = 1, 0
    while r:
        q = old_r // r
        old_r, r = r, old_r - q * r
        old_s, s = s, old_s - q * s
    if old_r != 1:
        return None
    return old_s % m
```
hint: An inverse exists exactly when `gcd(a, m) == 1`.
hint: Run the extended Euclidean algorithm and keep the coefficient of `a`; reduce it modulo `m` at the end.
solution: Euclid's algorithm on `(a % m, m)` also tracks `old_s` with `old_r ≡ old_s * a (mod m)` at every step. When the remainder reaches the gcd, a gcd other than `1` means no inverse; otherwise `old_s % m` is the inverse.

@quiz num
prompt: In a toy RSA key, $\varphi(n) = 40$ and the public exponent is $e = 7$. Find the private exponent $d$ with $0 < d < 40$ and $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{40}$.
answer: 23
check: next(d for d in range(1, 40) if (7 * d) % 40 == 1)
hint: You need the inverse of $7$ modulo $40$. Use the extended Euclidean algorithm, or test the multiples $7d$ that end in $1$ (mod $40$).
solution: $40 = 5 \cdot 7 + 5$, $7 = 1 \cdot 5 + 2$, $5 = 2 \cdot 2 + 1$. Back-substituting: $1 = 5 - 2 \cdot 2 = 5 - 2(7 - 5) = 3 \cdot 5 - 2 \cdot 7 = 3(40 - 5 \cdot 7) - 2 \cdot 7 = 3 \cdot 40 - 17 \cdot 7$. So the inverse is $-17 \equiv 23 \pmod{40}$. Check: $7 \cdot 23 = 161 = 4 \cdot 40 + 1$.
