@article
A **population** is the whole group you want to learn about, such as every student in a school. A **sample** is a smaller part of it that you actually measure. We use samples because asking everyone is often too slow or costly.

### Good samples and bad samples

A sample is **representative** if it looks like the population in the ways that matter. A **random sample** gives every member an equal chance of being chosen, for example picking names from a hat or using random numbers on a class roster. Random samples avoid **bias**, a systematic tendency to favor some members.

A **convenience sample** takes whoever is easy to reach. Asking only the school basketball team about favorite sports is biased, since it over-represents one group. A bigger random sample also tends to be more reliable than a small one.

### Estimating a population

If the sample is representative, its proportions estimate the population's. Suppose $12$ of a random $30$ students walk to school. That is $\frac{12}{30} = 40\%$, so in a school of $450$ students we estimate $0.40 \cdot 450 = 180$ walkers. This is an estimate, and another random sample would give a slightly different number.

### Center and spread

To compare data sets, describe two things.

- **Center:** the **mean** (sum divided by the number of values) or the **median** (middle value when ordered).
- **Spread:** how far the data typically lie from the center. The **mean absolute deviation (MAD)** is the average distance from the mean.

To find the MAD of $2, 4, 6, 8, 10$: the mean is $6$; the distances from $6$ are $4, 2, 0, 2, 4$; their mean is $\frac{12}{5} = 2.4$.

### Comparing two populations

When two sets have similar spread, the difference between their means can be measured in units of the MAD. If team A averages $24$ points and team B averages $18$, with a MAD of $3$ for both, the difference is $6$, which is $\frac{6}{3} = 2$ MADs. A gap of about $2$ MADs or more suggests a real difference; a gap under about $1$ MAD means the data sets overlap a lot.

### Sampling variability

Different random samples from the same population give different results. Taking several samples and seeing how much they vary tells you how much to trust a single estimate. Always say "about" or "estimate" when reporting a sample result.

@example
title: Estimating from a sample
problem: A random sample of $30$ students shows $12$ walk to school. The school has $450$ students. Estimate how many walk, and say whether asking only students on one bus route would be a better sample.
step: Find the sample proportion: $\frac{12}{30} = 0.4$.
step: Apply it to the population: $0.4 \cdot 450 = 180$ students.
step: A single bus route is a convenience sample: those students all ride the bus, so it would be biased and could not estimate the walkers.
step: The random sample is better, and the estimate is about $180$ walkers.

@mistakes
- Treating a convenience sample, such as friends or a single team, as if it were random.
- Claiming that a sample proves the exact population value instead of giving an estimate.
- Subtracting the mean from each value without taking absolute values when computing MAD, so the distances cancel.

@practice mc
prompt: A school wants to know the favorite lunch of all its students. Which sampling method is most likely to give a representative sample?
choice: Ask the first $30$ students to arrive at school.
choice: Ask only students in the cafeteria line at noon.
choice*: Choose $30$ names at random from the full school roster.
choice: Ask the $30$ students sitting closest to the principal's office.
hint: Look for a method where every student has an equal chance of being picked.
solution: Random selection from the whole roster gives everyone an equal chance. The other methods favor particular groups, such as early arrivals or students who buy lunch.

@practice num
prompt: In a random sample of $40$ students, $14$ prefer pizza. The school has $600$ students. About how many students prefer pizza?
answer: 210
check: 14 / 40 * 600
hint: Find the fraction of the sample that prefers pizza.
solution: $\frac{14}{40} = 0.35$, and $0.35 \cdot 600 = 210$ students.

@practice num

answer: 12
check: (12 + 15 + 9 + 14 + 10) / 5
hint: Add the values and divide by how many there are.
solution: The sum is $60$, and $\frac{60}{5} = 12$.

@practice num
prompt: Find the mean absolute deviation (MAD) of $4, 6, 8, 10, 12$.
answer: 2.4
tol: 0.01
check: (abs(4 - 8) + abs(6 - 8) + abs(8 - 8) + abs(10 - 8) + abs(12 - 8)) / 5
hint: The mean is $8$. Find each value's distance from it.
hint: The distances are $4, 2, 0, 2, 4$.
solution: The distances sum to $12$, so MAD $= \frac{12}{5} = 2.4$.

@practice num
prompt: Team A has a mean score of $24$ and team B has a mean score of $18$. Both data sets have a MAD of $3$. The difference between the means is how many MADs?
answer: 2
check: (24 - 18) / 3
hint: Find the difference between the means, then divide by the MAD.
solution: The difference is $6$, and $\frac{6}{3} = 2$ MADs.

@quiz num
prompt: In a random sample of $50$ households in a town, $8$ own a dog. The town has $1250$ households. About how many households own a dog?
answer: 200
check: 8 / 50 * 1250
hint: Find the sample proportion first.
solution: $\frac{8}{50} = 0.16$, and $0.16 \cdot 1250 = 200$ households.

@quiz mc
prompt: Two random samples of $20$ seventh graders each are taken from the same school, and their mean heights differ by $1.5$ cm. What is the best conclusion?
choice: The samples must have been chosen incorrectly.
choice*: Different random samples vary, so a small difference is expected.
choice: One sample must be biased against tall students.
choice: The population mean must be exactly halfway between the two sample means.
hint: Think about sampling variability: random samples do not give identical results.
solution: Random samples naturally differ from each other. A small difference such as $1.5$ cm is ordinary variability, not evidence of a mistake or bias.
