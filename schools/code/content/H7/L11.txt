@article
**Evolution** is a change in the heritable characteristics (allele frequencies) of a **population** over generations. Individuals do not evolve; populations do.

### Natural selection

Natural selection follows from four observations and inferences:
1. Individuals in a population **vary**, and some of the variation is **heritable**.
2. More offspring are produced than can survive.
3. Individuals with traits better suited to their environment have higher **fitness**, meaning more surviving, reproducing offspring.
4. So those alleles become more common in the next generation: **adaptation**.

Variation comes from mutation, and in sexual species from meiosis (crossing over, independent assortment) and fertilization. Mutations are random with respect to need; **selection is not random**. Selection acts on phenotypes, but the population evolves.

Types of selection: **directional** (one extreme favored), **stabilizing** (the average favored) and **disruptive** (both extremes favored).

### Other mechanisms of change

- **Genetic drift:** random changes in allele frequency, strongest in small populations (the **bottleneck** and **founder effects**).
- **Gene flow:** movement of alleles between populations.
- **Mutation:** the ultimate source of new alleles.
- **Sexual selection:** traits that improve mating success.

### Evidence for evolution

**Fossil record** (transitional forms and a time sequence), **comparative anatomy** (homologous structures such as the limbs of mammals; analogous structures from convergent evolution; vestigial structures), **embryology**, **biogeography**, **molecular biology** (shared genetic code and sequence similarity that matches anatomical relationships), and **direct observation** (antibiotic resistance, pesticide resistance). The relationships among species are shown on a **phylogenetic tree**.

### Species and speciation

A **species** in the biological species concept is a group of populations that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring. **Allopatric speciation** needs geographic separation; **sympatric speciation** occurs without it (often by polyploidy in plants). **Reproductive barriers** (prezygotic: timing, habitat, behavior; postzygotic: sterile hybrids) keep species separate.

### Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium

For a gene with two alleles with frequencies $p$ (dominant, $A$) and $q$ (recessive, $a$):
$$p + q = 1, \qquad p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1,$$
where $p^2$ is the frequency of $AA$, $2pq$ of $Aa$ and $q^2$ of $aa$. A population stays in equilibrium (allele frequencies do not change) only if there is **no mutation, no gene flow, random mating, no selection and a very large population**. When data do not fit the equations, evolution is occurring. Usually you start from the recessive phenotype, which is the only one whose genotype is known: $q = \sqrt{q^2}$.

@example
title: Hardy-Weinberg calculation
problem: In a population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, 16% of individuals show a recessive phenotype. What is the frequency of heterozygotes?
step: The recessive phenotype is $aa$, so $q^2 = 0.16$.
step: Take the square root: $q = 0.4$. Then $p = 1 - 0.4 = 0.6$.
step: Heterozygote frequency is $2pq = 2(0.6)(0.4) = 0.48$.

@mistakes
- Saying individuals evolve in response to need; evolution is change in populations, and mutations arise randomly.
- Treating "fitness" as strength or health instead of reproductive success.
- Using $q^2$ as the allele frequency; the allele frequency is $q = \sqrt{q^2}$.
- Saying that dominant alleles are always the most common.

@practice num
prompt: In a Hardy-Weinberg population, the frequency of the recessive homozygote $aa$ is 0.09. What is the frequency $q$ of allele $a$?
answer: 0.3
check: math.sqrt(0.09)
tol: 0.005
hint: $q^2 = 0.09$.
solution: $q = \sqrt{0.09} = 0.3$.

@practice num
prompt: A recessive disorder affects 1 in 2500 people in a population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. What fraction of the population are carriers? Give a decimal rounded to four places.
answer: 0.0392
check: 2 * math.sqrt(1/2500) * (1 - math.sqrt(1/2500))
tol: 1%
hint: $q^2 = 1/2500$, so $q = 0.02$.
hint: Carrier frequency is $2pq$ with $p = 1 - q$.
solution: $q = 0.02$, $p = 0.98$, $2pq = 2(0.98)(0.02) = 0.0392$, about 1 in 26.

@practice ms
prompt: Which conditions are required for a population to remain in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium?
choice*: No natural selection
choice*: Random mating
choice*: Very large population size
choice: High rate of mutation
choice: Frequent migration of individuals between populations
hint: Think of the absence of every evolutionary force.
solution: No selection, random mating and a very large population (as well as no mutation and no gene flow) are required. High mutation and migration would change allele frequencies.

@practice mc
prompt: A bacterial culture containing a small fraction of resistant cells is exposed to an antibiotic that kills most cells. Resistant survivors regrow the population. Which best explains the population's resistance afterward?
choice: The antibiotic caused the resistance mutation in response to exposure.
choice*: Pre-existing resistant cells survived and reproduced, increasing the frequency of the resistance allele.
choice: Individual bacteria learned to resist and taught others.
choice: The population evolved because it needed to survive.
hint: When did the resistance variants arise relative to the exposure?
solution: Resistance variants arise randomly before exposure; the antibiotic selects for them, so the resistance allele frequency increases.

@quiz num
prompt: In a Hardy-Weinberg population of 2000 individuals the frequency of allele $A$ is $p = 0.9$. How many individuals are expected to be heterozygous ($Aa$)?
answer: 360
check: 2 * 0.9 * (1 - 0.9) * 2000
tol: 1%
hint: Find $q$ first.
solution: $q = 0.1$, $2pq = 0.18$, and $0.18 \times 2000 = 360$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A severe drought leaves a population of 20 finches on an island. By chance, the survivors have a higher frequency of an allele than the original population. This is an example of
choice: natural selection for a better-adapted trait
choice*: genetic drift (bottleneck effect)
choice: gene flow
choice: directional mutation
hint: The change occurred by chance in a small population.
solution: A random change in allele frequency after a sharp reduction in population size is genetic drift (a bottleneck), not selection.
