@article
**Metabolism** is the sum of all chemical reactions in a cell. **Catabolic** pathways break molecules down and release energy; **anabolic** pathways build molecules and require energy. Energy follows the laws of thermodynamics: it cannot be created or destroyed (first law), and every transfer increases the **entropy** (disorder) of the universe (second law).

### Free energy

The **Gibbs free energy change** tells whether a reaction can proceed spontaneously:
$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S,$$
where $\Delta H$ is the change in enthalpy (heat content), $T$ is temperature in kelvin and $\Delta S$ is the change in entropy. If $\Delta G < 0$ the reaction is **exergonic** (releases free energy, spontaneous); if $\Delta G > 0$ it is **endergonic** (needs an input of energy). Spontaneous does not mean fast.

Cells couple the two. **ATP** (adenosine triphosphate) hydrolysis to ADP and inorganic phosphate has $\Delta G \approx -30.5$ kJ/mol under standard conditions, and the energy drives endergonic reactions by transferring a phosphate group to a reactant.

### How enzymes work

Even an exergonic reaction must overcome an **activation energy** ($E_A$) barrier. **Enzymes** are biological catalysts, mostly proteins, that lower $E_A$ by stabilizing the transition state. They do **not** change $\Delta G$, the equilibrium position or the products; they only speed the approach to equilibrium. Substrates bind at the **active site**; the **induced fit** model says the site changes shape slightly to grip the substrate. Enzymes are specific, reusable and often need **cofactors** (metal ions) or **coenzymes** (small organic molecules, often vitamin derivatives).

### Factors affecting rate

- **Temperature:** rate rises with temperature until the enzyme **denatures**, losing its shape.
- **pH:** each enzyme has an optimum (pepsin about 2, trypsin about 8).
- **Substrate concentration:** rate rises then saturates at $V_{max}$ when all active sites are occupied.

The **Michaelis-Menten equation** is
$$v = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}.$$
$K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which $v = V_{max}/2$; a lower $K_m$ means higher apparent affinity.

### Regulation

- **Competitive inhibitors** resemble the substrate and bind the active site. Adding more substrate can overcome them: $V_{max}$ unchanged, apparent $K_m$ increases.
- **Noncompetitive (allosteric) inhibitors** bind elsewhere and change the enzyme's shape: $V_{max}$ decreases, $K_m$ is unchanged or little changed.
- **Feedback inhibition:** the end product of a pathway inhibits an early enzyme, preventing overproduction.

@example
title: Using the Michaelis-Menten equation
problem: An enzyme has $V_{max} = 80\ \mu$mol/min and $K_m = 4$ mM. What is the rate at $[S] = 12$ mM?
step: Write the equation: $v = V_{max}[S]/(K_m + [S])$.
step: Substitute: $v = 80 \times 12 / (4 + 12)$.
step: Compute: $v = 960/16 = 60\ \mu$mol/min, which is 75% of $V_{max}$.

@mistakes
- Saying enzymes change $\Delta G$ or make an endergonic reaction spontaneous; they only lower $E_A$.
- Assuming that a negative $\Delta G$ means the reaction is fast.
- Describing a denatured enzyme as "dead"; it is a protein that has lost its shape, usually irreversibly at high temperature.
- Mixing up inhibitors: competitive inhibition can be overcome by more substrate, noncompetitive cannot.

@practice mc
prompt: Which statement about enzymes is correct?
choice: They are consumed in the reaction they catalyze.
choice: They shift the equilibrium toward the products.
choice*: They lower the activation energy of the reaction.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction has $\Delta H = -50$ kJ/mol and $\Delta S = -0.10$ kJ/(mol K) at 300 K. What is $\Delta G$ in kJ/mol?
answer: -20
check: -50 - 300 * (-0.10)
unit: kJ/mol
tol: 0.5
hint: Use $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.
hint: $T\Delta S = 300 \times (-0.10) = -30$.
solution: $\Delta G = -50 - (-30) = -20$ kJ/mol. It is exergonic.
