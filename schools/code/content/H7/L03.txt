@article
The **plasma membrane** separates the cell from its surroundings and controls what passes through. The **fluid mosaic model** describes it as a **phospholipid bilayer** (hydrophilic heads outward, hydrophobic tails inward) with proteins embedded in or attached to it. The lipids and many proteins drift sideways, so the membrane is fluid. **Cholesterol** in animal membranes buffers fluidity: it prevents the membrane from becoming too rigid when cold and too loose when warm. Carbohydrates attached to lipids and proteins on the outer surface act in cell recognition.

Because the interior of the bilayer is hydrophobic, the membrane is **selectively permeable**. Small nonpolar molecules ($O_2$, $CO_2$) and small uncharged molecules (water, slowly) cross easily; ions and large polar molecules such as glucose need help from transport proteins.

### Passive transport (no cellular energy)

Substances move **down their concentration gradient**, from high to low concentration.
- **Simple diffusion:** directly across the lipid bilayer ($O_2$, $CO_2$, steroid hormones).
- **Facilitated diffusion:** through **channel proteins** or **carrier proteins** (glucose, ions); still down the gradient and still passive.
- **Osmosis:** diffusion of **water** across a selectively permeable membrane, from lower solute concentration to higher solute concentration. Water moves toward the side with more dissolved solute that cannot cross.

### Active transport (uses energy)

Moving a substance **against** its gradient requires energy, usually ATP. The **sodium-potassium pump** uses one ATP to move 3 $Na^+$ out and 2 $K^+$ in, keeping $Na^+$ high outside and $K^+$ high inside. **Secondary active transport** uses the gradient made by a pump to drive another substance (for example glucose entering cells with $Na^+$). **Bulk transport** moves large particles in vesicles: **endocytosis** (phagocytosis, pinocytosis, receptor-mediated) brings material in, and **exocytosis** sends material out.

### Tonicity: predicting cell shape

**Tonicity** compares solutions by the concentration of solutes that cannot freely cross the membrane. Count particles: $\text{osmolarity} = i \times C$, where $i$ is the number of particles each formula unit gives (NaCl gives 2, glucose gives 1) and $C$ is molarity. Human red blood cells have an interior of about 0.30 osmol/L, equal to 0.9% NaCl ("normal saline").

| Solution relative to the cell | Net water movement | Red blood cell | Plant cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypotonic (lower solute outside) | into cell | swells, may **lyse** | turgid (healthy) |
| Isotonic | none | normal biconcave shape | flaccid |
| Hypertonic (higher solute outside) | out of cell | shrinks, **crenates** | **plasmolysis** |

Plant cells have walls, so they resist bursting; the wall pressure is **turgor pressure**. Water movement is described by **water potential**, $\psi = \psi_s + \psi_p$, where the solute potential is $\psi_s = -iCRT$ (with $R = 0.0831$ L bar mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$ and $T$ in kelvin). Water flows from higher to lower water potential. Adding solute lowers $\psi_s$; adding pressure raises $\psi_p$.

@example
title: Predicting what happens to a red blood cell
problem: A red blood cell (interior about 0.30 osmol/L of nonpenetrating solutes) is placed in 0.10 M NaCl. Predict the direction of net water movement and the cell's fate.
step: Find the solution's osmolarity. NaCl dissociates into 2 particles, so $2 \times 0.10 = 0.20$ osmol/L.
step: Compare: the outside, 0.20 osmol/L, has less solute than the cell, 0.30 osmol/L, so the solution is hypotonic.
step: Water moves toward higher solute concentration, so it flows into the cell.
step: The cell swells and, because it has no cell wall, may burst (lyse).

@mistakes
- Saying that solute moves in osmosis; osmosis is the movement of water.
- Forgetting that NaCl counts as two particles when comparing osmolarity.
- Calling facilitated diffusion active transport; it uses a protein but no energy and goes down the gradient.
- Applying "plasmolysis" or "turgor" to animal cells; those terms describe cells with walls.

solution: In 0.9% NaCl (isotonic) there is no net water movement and the cells keep their normal biconcave shape. In distilled water (hypotonic) water enters the cells because they have higher solute concentration inside; they swell and lyse. In 2% NaCl (hypertonic) water leaves the cells toward the higher solute concentration; they shrink and crenate. In all cases water moves toward the region of higher solute concentration.
