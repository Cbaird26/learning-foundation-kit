@article
A software product is never built by engineers alone. Titles and boundaries vary a lot between companies (and a small startup may have one person wearing five hats), so treat the list below as common patterns, not as a universal org chart. All teams and people in the examples are **fictional**.

### Who does what

- **Software engineers** design, build, test and operate the software. Specialties include frontend, backend, mobile, full-stack, infrastructure and machine learning. A **tech lead** is an engineer who guides the technical direction of a team's work, often without managing people.
- **Engineering managers (EMs)** are responsible for the team's health: hiring, growth, performance, staffing and delivery. They usually code little or not at all. Your EM is the person who helps you grow and removes obstacles.
- **Product managers (PMs)** own the *what* and the *why*: which problems to solve for which users, how to prioritize them and how to know it worked. A good PM brings evidence (user research, data, business goals); the engineers bring feasibility, cost and risk.
- **Designers** (product, UX, interaction, visual, content designers and user researchers) own how the product works and feels for people, including accessibility. Involve them before the code is written, not after.
- **QA and test engineers** build quality into the process: test strategy, automation, exploratory testing. On many teams developers own most testing and QA coaches.
- **Site reliability engineers (SREs), DevOps and platform engineers** keep services reliable and give product teams the tools to deploy and operate safely.
- **Data roles:** data analysts answer questions with data, data engineers build pipelines, data scientists and ML engineers build models.
- **Security, privacy and legal** specialists review designs and advise on risk and compliance.
- **Technical program managers (TPMs)** coordinate large efforts that span teams. **Support and customer success** hear the user's pain first; befriend them.

### How decisions get made

Confusion about *who decides* causes more delay than any bug. A **RACI** chart assigns each decision four kinds of participation:

| Letter | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **R**esponsible | does the work |
| **A**ccountable | makes the final call; exactly one per decision |
| **C**onsulted | gives input before the decision (two-way) |
| **I**nformed | told after the decision (one-way) |

The one-**A** rule is the heart of it. Zero accountable people means the decision drifts; two means a stalemate. A related framework, **DACI**, names a Driver, an Approver, Contributors and Informed people. Use whichever your company uses, but always be able to answer: *who has the final say here?*

Also sort decisions by how easy they are to undo. A button label is cheap to change; a public API or a database schema is expensive. Spend your consultation effort on the hard-to-reverse ones.

### Working with product managers and designers

Do not say "that's impossible." Say what it would cost and offer options: "We can ship the full export in six weeks, or a CSV-only version in two; the CSV version covers the cases in the research." Ask what problem a request solves before debating the solution. When you disagree after the decision is made, say your concern clearly once, record it, and then support the decision (often called *disagree and commit*) unless it is unethical or unsafe; Week 9 covers those cases.

### Reading a project

When you inherit or join a project, make a one-page map: the roles (with names), the key decisions made and coming, and who is accountable for each. It takes an hour and saves weeks. Your first questions to your manager should be "who decides priorities?" and "who do I ask about X?"

@example
title: Mapping one decision
problem: At a fictional company, the team must decide whether the new "parcel photo" feature stores images for 30 days or 1 year. Roles: Product Manager (PM), Engineer (Eng), Designer, Privacy Lead and Support. Assign RACI.
step: Who makes the final call? Retention is a product decision with privacy implications, so the PM is **Accountable**.
step: Who does the work? The Engineer implements the storage rule: **Responsible**.
step: Whose input is required before deciding? The Privacy Lead (legal and risk) is **Consulted**. The Designer is consulted about how deletion appears to users.
step: Who just needs to know afterward? Support, so they can answer customers: **Informed**.
answer: PM: A; Eng: R; Designer: C; Privacy Lead: C; Support: I. Exactly one A.

@example
title: Finding a gap in a decision map
problem: A fictional RACI chart lists the decision "Choose the payment provider" with Eng: R, PM: C, Finance: C and nobody marked A. What is wrong and how do you fix it?
step: Count the accountable people: zero. Nobody can make the final call, so the decision will stall or be made implicitly.
step: Decide who should own it. It has cost, contract and product impact, so the PM, or a finance owner if your company works that way, is a natural A.
step: Ask the manager or sponsor to confirm out loud, and update the chart so everyone sees the same owner.
answer: There is no Accountable owner. Assign exactly one (for example the PM) and confirm it with the team.

@mistakes
- Assuming one company's titles and boundaries apply everywhere; ask what each person actually owns.
- Treating the PM as the enemy or as a ticket-writing service; the best results come when engineers join the problem discussion early.
- Leaving a decision with two Accountable people, or none, and then wondering why it stalls.
- Raising design or accessibility concerns after implementation, when changes are expensive.

Write `invalid_decisions(raci)` that returns a sorted list of decision names that break either rule: exactly one role must have an `A` in its letters, and at least one role must have an `R`.
starter:
```python

    pass
```
tests:
```python
good = {"Pick DB": {"Eng": "R", "PM": "A", "Sec": "C"}}
assert invalid_decisions(good) == []
two_a = {"Pick DB": {"Eng": "A", "PM": "A", "Sec": "R"}}
assert invalid_decisions(two_a) == ["Pick DB"]
no_r = {"Launch": {"PM": "A", "Eng": "C"}}
assert invalid_decisions(no_r) == ["Launch"]
combo = {"Launch": {"PM": "AR", "Eng": "C"}, "Rename": {"PM": "I"}}
assert invalid_decisions(combo) == ["Rename"]
assert invalid_decisions({}) == []
```
model:
```python
def invalid_decisions(raci):
    bad = []
    for decision, roles in raci.items():
        a_count = sum(1 for letters in roles.values() if "A" in letters)
        has_r = any("R" in letters for letters in roles.values())
        if a_count != 1 or not has_r:
            bad.append(decision)
    return sorted(bad)
```
hint: For each decision, count the roles whose letters contain `"A"`.
hint: A role with `"AR"` counts as both accountable and responsible.
solution: For each decision, count roles with an `A` and check that some role has an `R`. A decision is invalid if the A count is not exactly one or there is no R. Return the names sorted. The `"AR"` test checks that combined letters count for both rules.

@quiz code
prompt: In a RACI chart (a dict mapping each decision to a dict of role to letters such as `"A"`, `"R"`, `"AR"` or `"C"`), write `accountable_load(raci)` that returns a dict mapping **every role that appears anywhere in the chart** to the number of decisions for which that role's letters contain `"A"` (zero if none).
starter:
```python
def accountable_load(raci):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
chart = {
    "Pick DB": {"Eng": "A", "PM": "C"},
    "Launch": {"PM": "A", "Eng": "R"},
    "Rename": {"PM": "AR", "Design": "C"},
}
assert accountable_load(chart) == {"Eng": 1, "PM": 2, "Design": 0}
assert accountable_load({}) == {}
assert accountable_load({"X": {"A": "C"}}) == {"A": 0}
```
model:
```python
def accountable_load(raci):
    load = {}
    for roles in raci.values():
        for role, letters in roles.items():
            load.setdefault(role, 0)
            if "A" in letters:
                load[role] += 1
    return load
```
hint: Make sure a role gets an entry even when it is never accountable.
hint: `dict.setdefault(role, 0)` creates the entry without overwriting an existing count.
solution: Walk every decision and every role, create the role's counter if needed, and add 1 when its letters contain `A`. Roles that are only consulted or informed still appear with `0`.
