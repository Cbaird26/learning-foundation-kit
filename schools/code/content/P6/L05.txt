@article
Every engineer is asked, "How long will it take?" The honest answer is almost always "it depends," and your job is to say *on what* and give a useful number anyway. Names, teams and numbers below are **fictional** and illustrative.

### Why estimates go wrong

Humans are optimistic planners: we imagine the smooth path and forget reviews, meetings, flaky tests, waiting for answers, and the bug found halfway. This is the **planning fallacy**. Uncertainty is also largest at the start: the **cone of uncertainty** says early estimates can be off by a large factor, narrowing as you learn. Treat early estimates as ranges and refine them.

Three words people confuse:

- An **estimate** is your best prediction, with uncertainty.
- A **target** is what the business would like.
- A **commitment** is a promise you make *after* you understand the work and can control the scope.

If a manager says "we need it by Friday" and your estimate says "two to three weeks," the right response is to negotiate scope, resources or date, not to quietly change your estimate.

### Decompose, then estimate

Break a feature into tasks small enough to reason about (a day or two each). Include the invisible work: reading existing code, tests, code review, documentation, deployment and monitoring. Items you cannot estimate are your biggest risks; schedule a short **spike** (a time-boxed investigation) first.

### Three-point estimates

For each task give three numbers: **optimistic** $O$ (things go well), **most likely** $M$, and **pessimistic** $P$ (a realistic bad case, not a disaster). The PERT approximation is

$$\text{mean} = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}, \qquad \sigma \approx \frac{P - O}{6}.$$

For a whole feature, add the means. If tasks are roughly independent, **variances add, not standard deviations**: $\sigma_{\text{total}} = \sqrt{\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 + \cdots}$. A rough range is the mean plus or minus two standard deviations, which is about a 95 percent range *if* the normal approximation holds; treat that as a guide, not a guarantee. Report a range ("about 6 days, likely between 3 and 9") rather than a single number.

### Relative sizing and velocity

Many teams size work in **story points**, a relative scale, and measure **velocity**, the points the team completes per sprint. Points are not hours and should not be converted one-for-one or used to compare teams. Your **focus factor** (the share of a day available for focused work after meetings and interruptions) is often well below 100 percent; measure yours instead of assuming.

### Tracking and communicating

Track progress against tasks daily. A **burndown chart** plots remaining work over time: if the line is flat, you are not finishing things. When you discover you will slip, say so as soon as you know and bring options (cut scope, add help, move the date). Update the estimate when you learn something, and keep a note of what surprised you; that is how your estimates improve.

### Managing your own work

- **Prioritize** with the urgent/important idea: do important and urgent first, schedule important but not urgent, delegate or limit urgent but unimportant, drop the rest.
- **Limit work in progress.** Finishing one thing beats starting three. Close the loop on reviews quickly; blocked reviewers block the team.
- **Time-box** investigations (for example 90 minutes) and decide what you will do if it is not solved.
- **Protect focus time**: batch chat checks, block calendar time for deep work, and make interruptions visible rather than silently absorbing them.
- **Keep a log**: a daily note of what you did and what is next makes stand-ups and performance reviews easy.
- **Say no or "yes, and"**: when asked for something new, show what it displaces: "I can take that if we move the export work to next sprint."

@example
title: A three-point estimate for one task
problem: A fictional task "add CSV export" has optimistic 2 days, most likely 3 days and pessimistic 10 days. Compute the PERT mean and standard deviation.
step: Apply the mean formula: $(2 + 4 \cdot 3 + 10)/6 = 24/6 = 4$ days.
step: The mean is above the most likely value because the pessimistic case is much farther away than the optimistic one.
step: The standard deviation is $(10-2)/6 \approx 1.33$ days.
step: Communicate it: "About 4 days, plausibly 2 to 7."
answer: Mean 4 days, standard deviation about 1.33 days.

@example
title: Combining two tasks
problem: Task A has mean 4 and $\sigma = 1.33$ days. Task B (fictional) has mean 2 and $\sigma = 0.33$ days. Estimate the total, treating them as independent.
step: Means add: $4 + 2 = 6$ days.
step: Variances add: $1.33^2 + 0.33^2 \approx 1.78 + 0.11 = 1.89$.
step: Take the square root: $\sqrt{1.89} \approx 1.37$ days. Note this is less than $1.33 + 0.33 = 1.67$, because independent errors partly cancel.
step: Report a range: 6 days, plus or minus about 2.7 days, so roughly 3 to 9 days.
answer: About 6 days with a standard deviation of about 1.37, so roughly 3 to 9 days.

@mistakes
- Giving a single number with no range or assumptions, then treating it as a promise.
- Adding standard deviations instead of variances when combining independent tasks.
- Forgetting review, testing, documentation and deployment time in the task list.
- Hiding a slip until the deadline instead of renegotiating scope or dates as soon as you know.

@practice code
prompt: Write `feature_estimate(tasks)` where `tasks` is a list of `(o, m, p)` three-point tuples (independent tasks). Return `(mean, sigma)` for the whole feature: the sum of the task means, and the square root of the sum of the task variances (each task's sigma squared), both rounded to 2 decimals. An empty list returns `(0.0, 0.0)`. Use the PERT formulas `mean = (o + 4*m + p) / 6`, `sigma = (p - o) / 6`.
starter:
```python
import math


def feature_estimate(tasks):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert feature_estimate([(2, 3, 10), (1, 2, 3)]) == (6.0, 1.37)
assert feature_estimate([]) == (0.0, 0.0)
assert feature_estimate([(1, 4, 13)]) == (5.0, 2.0)
assert feature_estimate([(3, 3, 3), (6, 6, 6)]) == (9.0, 0.0)
assert feature_estimate([(0, 6, 12)] * 4) == (24.0, 4.0)
```
model:
```python
import math


def feature_estimate(tasks):
    mean = 0.0
    variance = 0.0
    for o, m, p in tasks:
        mean += (o + 4 * m + p) / 6
        variance += ((p - o) / 6) ** 2
    return (round(mean, 2), round(math.sqrt(variance), 2))
```
hint: Keep two running totals: the sum of means and the sum of variances.
hint: Take the square root only at the end, after summing the variances.
solution: Sum the means and sum the squared sigmas, then take the square root of the total variance. Four identical tasks with sigma 2 each give a combined sigma of $\sqrt{4 \cdot 4} = 4$, not 8; that is the case that catches adding standard deviations.

@quiz code
prompt: Write `remaining_by_day(total, done_per_day)` that returns the list of remaining work after each day, for plotting a burndown. `total` is the starting amount and `done_per_day` is a list of amounts completed each day. Remaining never drops below `0`. An empty `done_per_day` returns an empty list.
starter:
```python
def remaining_by_day(total, done_per_day):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert remaining_by_day(10, [3, 2, 4]) == [7, 5, 1]
assert remaining_by_day(5, [3, 4]) == [2, 0]
assert remaining_by_day(5, []) == []
assert remaining_by_day(6, [0, 0, 6, 3]) == [6, 6, 0, 0]
```
model:
```python
def remaining_by_day(total, done_per_day):
    remaining = total
    result = []
    for done in done_per_day:
        remaining = max(0, remaining - done)
        result.append(remaining)
    return result
```
hint: Keep a running `remaining` value and append it after each day.
hint: Use `max(0, ...)` so extra work done does not make the value negative.
solution: Subtract each day's work from the running total, clamp at zero with `max`, and record the value. The test with two zero days shows a flat burndown, which signals no progress.
