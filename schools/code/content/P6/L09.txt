Software now touches health, money, elections, transport and children's lives. Engineers make choices that affect people who will never meet them: what data to collect, how a model is evaluated, whether a safety check ships late. Ethics for engineers is not abstract philosophy; it is a practical skill, like debugging, that you can practice. The cases and companies below are **fictional**.

### The ACM Code of Ethics (2018)

The Association for Computing Machinery's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct, revised in 2018, is a widely used statement of professional responsibility for computing professionals. It is organized in four sections. Here is a paraphrase (read the original for the exact text):

**1. General Ethical Principles**
- 1.1 Contribute to society and to human well-being, acknowledging that all people are stakeholders in computing.
- 1.2 Avoid harm.
- 1.3 Be honest and trustworthy.
- 1.4 Be fair and take action not to discriminate.
- 1.5 Respect the work required to produce new ideas, inventions, creative works and computing artifacts (credit and licenses).
- 1.6 Respect privacy.
- 1.7 Honor confidentiality.

**2. Professional Responsibilities** include striving for high quality (2.1), maintaining competence and professional conduct (2.2), knowing and respecting rules and laws (2.3), accepting and giving professional review (2.4), giving thorough evaluations of systems and their risks (2.5), working only within your competence (2.6), fostering public understanding of computing (2.7), accessing resources only when authorized (2.8), and designing systems that are robustly and usably secure (2.9).

**3. Professional Leadership Principles** cover ensuring the public good is a central concern (3.1), articulating responsibilities, managing personnel and resources well, creating opportunities for growth, and being careful with changes to systems (3.2 to 3.7).

**4. Compliance** asks members to uphold and promote the Code (4.1) and to treat violations as inconsistent with membership (4.2).

The Code is a guide to judgment, not an algorithm. Principles can conflict (honesty versus confidentiality, for example) and the Code itself says to weigh them in context, giving special attention to the people most likely to be harmed.

### A process for ethical analysis

When something feels wrong, slow down and work through it:

1. **Get the facts.** What exactly is being asked, what is known, what is assumed? Do not accuse before you understand.
2. **Identify stakeholders**, including people who are not in the room: users, non-users affected, colleagues, the company, the public.
3. **Name the possible harms and benefits** and who bears them. Consider the vulnerable and the long term.
4. **Find applicable principles and rules**: the ACM Code, your company's policy, the law (ask legal; do not guess).
5. **List options**, including middle paths: ask a question, propose an alternative design, add a safeguard, raise it with your manager or an ethics channel, refuse a part, escalate, or in extreme cases report externally.
6. **Decide and act**, then document what you raised and when. Seek advice from someone you trust.

### Different lenses, honest disagreement

Reasonable people use different frameworks. A **consequence-focused** view asks which option produces the best overall outcome. A **duty- or rights-focused** view asks which actions respect obligations and rights regardless of outcomes (for example, a duty not to deceive). A **virtue** view asks what a trustworthy and caring professional would do. On many questions they agree; on some they do not. Examples where thoughtful people differ: how much privacy should be traded for safety features; whether and when to report concerns outside the company; whether to work on defense or surveillance products; how to define a fair algorithm when different fairness measures cannot all be satisfied at once; how much persuasion in design is acceptable before it becomes manipulation. A good analysis states the strongest versions of more than one view.

### Typical situations

- **Honesty about quality and risk**: reporting test results and estimates truthfully, including bad news.
- **Safety-critical work**: a missing safety check is not a normal bug; raise it forcefully.
- **Data and privacy**: collect what you need, say what you do, and protect it (Week 10).
- **Manipulative design**: interfaces that trick people into choices they would not otherwise make, such as hidden cancellation, fake urgency or confusing consent.
- **Automated decisions**: models can encode and amplify bias; measure outcomes for different groups, test with affected users and keep human review for high-stakes decisions.
- **AI-assisted work**: you remain responsible for code and text you ship, however it was produced; understand it, test it, check its licenses and do not paste confidential data into tools your company has not approved.
- **Speaking up**: start with the least drastic effective step (ask, document, escalate internally). Many companies have channels for ethics or compliance concerns; legal protections for people who report wrongdoing vary by country and situation, so learn about them before you need them.

### A fairness measure you can compute

A common first check compares **selection rates** across groups (the share of each group receiving a favorable outcome). The "four-fifths" rule of thumb from US employment guidance flags a group whose rate is under 80 percent of the highest group's rate. It is a **screening heuristic**: it is neither a legal conclusion nor proof that a system is fair or unfair, and it ignores other measures such as error rates per group.

@example
title: Analyzing a dilemma
problem: A fictional product manager asks you to make the "Cancel subscription" button gray, small and placed below three screens of offers, "to cut churn." What do you do?
step: Facts: confirm the request and the goal. The goal (reduce cancellations) is legitimate; the means is hiding a control people need.
step: Stakeholders and harms: customers who cannot easily cancel are charged for something they no longer want (honesty, 1.3; avoiding harm, 1.2), and the company risks complaints and regulator attention.
step: Options: (a) comply silently; (b) propose alternatives that address churn honestly, such as a visible cancel button with a brief retention offer or a pause option; (c) escalate if the request stands.
step: Views differ on how much retention persuasion is acceptable, but hiding the cancel control is toward the deceptive end. Choose (b), put your concern and proposal in writing, and measure the outcome.
answer: Raise the concern with an alternative that serves the goal honestly, document it, and escalate if needed.

@example
title: Computing selection rates
problem: In a fictional screening tool, Group A had 10 applicants and 4 were advanced; Group B had 20 applicants and 12 were advanced. Compare using the four-fifths rule of thumb.
step: Selection rates: A is $4/10 = 0.40$; B is $12/20 = 0.60$.
step: Ratio of the lower rate to the higher: $0.40/0.60 \approx 0.67$.
step: This is below $0.80$, so the heuristic flags the difference for review.
step: Interpret carefully: the flag asks for investigation (job-relevance, data quality, sample size), not a verdict. With only 10 applicants the rate is also noisy.
answer: Rates 0.40 and 0.60; ratio about 0.67, below 0.80, so flag for review (not a proof of bias).

@mistakes
- Treating an ethical question as a purely legal one ("it is legal, so it is fine") or purely personal ("it is not my job").
- Presenting only your own view; a sound analysis also states the strongest case for the other side.
- Jumping straight to going public, or to doing nothing, without trying the least drastic effective steps and documenting them.
- Using the four-fifths rule or any single fairness number as proof that a system is fair or unfair.

tests:
```python
recs = [("A", True), ("A", False), ("B", True), ("B", True), ("B", False)]
assert selection_rates(recs) == {"A": 0.5, "B": 0.667}
assert selection_rates([]) == {}

assert selection_rates([("A", True)]) == {"A": 1.0}
```
model:
```python
def selection_rates(records):
    totals = {}
    chosen = {}
    for group, selected in records:
        totals[group] = totals.get(group, 0) + 1
        chosen[group] = chosen.get(group, 0) + (1 if selected else 0)
    return {g: round(chosen[g] / totals[g], 3) for g in totals}
```
hint: Keep two counters per group: how many records, and how many selected.
hint: Divide at the end and round to 3 decimals.
solution: Count totals and selected per group, then divide. Use `get` with a default so a group's first record starts the count. The group `"B"` has 2 of 3 selected, which rounds to `0.667`.

model:
```python
def flag_groups(rates, threshold=0.8):
    if not rates:
        return []
    top = max(rates.values())
    if top == 0:
        return []
    return sorted(g for g, r in rates.items() if r / top < threshold)
```
hint: Find the highest rate first and guard against an empty dict and a highest rate of zero.
hint: Compare `rate / top` with `threshold`.
solution: Compute the maximum rate, return an empty list when there are no rates or the maximum is zero, and otherwise keep groups whose ratio to the maximum is below the threshold. In the second test the ratio is about 0.83, which is not below 0.8, so nothing is flagged; the last test shows that a lower threshold flags fewer groups.

prompt: A classifier's fairness is often examined using error rates per group. Write `false_positive_rates(records)` where `records` is a list of `(group, predicted, actual)` with bool values. For each group, the false positive rate is (predicted positive among actual negatives) divided by (number of actual negatives). Return a dict of group to rate rounded to 3 decimals, or `None` for a group with no actual negatives.
starter:
```python
def false_positive_rates(records):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
recs = [
    ("A", True, False), ("A", False, False), ("A", False, False), ("A", True, True),
    ("B", False, False), ("B", False, False), ("B", True, True),
    ("C", True, True),
]
assert false_positive_rates(recs) == {"A": 0.333, "B": 0.0, "C": None}
assert false_positive_rates([]) == {}
assert false_positive_rates([("X", True, False), ("X", True, False)]) == {"X": 1.0}
```
model:
```python
def false_positive_rates(records):
    negatives = {}
    false_pos = {}
    for group, predicted, actual in records:
        negatives.setdefault(group, 0)
        false_pos.setdefault(group, 0)
        if not actual:
            negatives[group] += 1
            if predicted:
                false_pos[group] += 1
    return {
        g: (round(false_pos[g] / negatives[g], 3) if negatives[g] else None)
        for g in negatives
    }
```
hint: Only records whose `actual` is `False` count toward the denominator.
hint: Make sure every group appears in the result, even if it has no actual negatives.
solution: Track per group how many actual negatives there are and how many of those were predicted positive. Divide where the denominator is nonzero, otherwise use `None`. Group `C` has no negatives, so its rate is undefined.
