@article
If you run software that people depend on, sooner or later something breaks at an inconvenient time. **On-call** is how teams make sure a prepared person responds. It feels scary the first time; it becomes manageable with preparation. Every incident, company and person in this lesson is **fictional**.

### On-call basics

An **on-call rotation** assigns one engineer (the *primary*) to respond to **pages** for a period, commonly a week, with a *secondary* as backup and an *escalation path* to a manager or specialist. Good practice:

- **Shadow first.** Before you are on call, shadow someone, read the runbooks, and do a practice page.
- **Runbooks** are short step-by-step guides for each alert: what it means, how to check, how to fix, whom to call.
- **Handoffs** at rotation change: open issues, risky deploys, flaky alerts.
- **Escalate early.** Asking for help is part of the job. Nobody expects you to know every system.
- **Alerts must be actionable.** An alert that fires and needs no action trains people to ignore it. Alert on symptoms users feel (error rate, latency) rather than on every internal cause, and tune noisy alerts afterward.
- **Look after yourself:** teams should cap pages per shift, give time off after a bad night, and how on-call work is recognized varies by company, so ask early.

### SLIs, SLOs and error budgets

A **service level indicator (SLI)** is a measurement, such as the fraction of requests that succeed. A **service level objective (SLO)** is the target for it, such as 99.9 percent over 30 days. A **service level agreement (SLA)** is a contract with customers, often with penalties. The gap between perfect and the SLO is the **error budget**: with a 99.9 percent SLO over 30 days, the allowed unavailability is

$$0.001 \times 30 \times 24 \times 60 = 43.2 \text{ minutes}.$$

Teams use the budget to balance reliability against speed: while budget remains, ship quickly; when it is spent, focus on reliability.

### Responding to an incident

An **incident** is an unplanned event that degrades service. The classic lifecycle: **detect, triage, mitigate, communicate, resolve, learn.**

1. **Declare it** and set a **severity** (many teams use levels such as SEV1 for a major outage to SEV4 for minor issues; definitions vary by company).
2. **Assign roles**: an **incident commander** coordinates and decides, a **communications lead** updates stakeholders, and **operations or subject-matter experts** investigate. One person keeps a **timeline** of what was seen and done.
3. **Mitigate first.** Stop the bleeding (roll back, turn off a feature flag, fail over) before you hunt for the root cause. Diagnosis can wait; customer impact cannot.
4. **Communicate regularly** even when there is no news: "Still investigating, next update in 30 minutes."
5. **Resolve** when the service is healthy, then schedule a review.

Useful measures: **time to detect** (start to detection), **time to mitigate** (detection to mitigation) and total duration.

### Blameless postmortems

A **postmortem** (or incident review) documents what happened and what the team will change. **Blameless** does not mean nobody is accountable; it means we assume people acted sensibly with the information they had, and we ask *how did our system make this mistake easy and the detection slow?* instead of *who messed up?* Blame teaches people to hide problems; curiosity teaches the organization to improve.

A good postmortem contains:

- **Summary and impact**: what users experienced, for how long, how many.
- **Timeline** with times and sources.
- **Contributing factors**: several, usually; avoid a single "root cause" or the conclusion "human error." Ask why the human action was possible without a guard.
- **What went well, what went poorly, where we got lucky.**
- **Action items**: specific, owned and dated, each tied to a contributing factor, plus a way to check later that they were done.

Compare. Blameful: "Priya pushed a bad config." Blameless: "A config change reached production without validation because the deploy pipeline lacks a schema check; the on-call engineer's rollback took 20 minutes because the rollback steps were not in the runbook."

@example
title: Computing an error budget
problem: A fictional service has a 99.5 percent monthly availability SLO (30-day month). A 50 minute outage has occurred. How much error budget has been used?
step: Allowed unavailability is $0.5\% \times 43{,}200 = 216$ minutes (there are $30 \times 24 \times 60 = 43{,}200$ minutes).
step: Used: 50 of 216 minutes, which is $50/216 \approx 23\%$.
step: Remaining: $216 - 50 = 166$ minutes. The team can still ship normally but should watch the risk of another large incident.
answer: Budget 216 minutes; 50 used (about 23 percent); 166 remaining.

title: Making a sentence blameless
problem: A draft postmortem (fictional) says "Dev should have tested the migration and deleted the wrong table." Rewrite it as a blameless statement with a contributing factor and an action.
step: Replace the person with the system: what let the wrong table be deleted?
step: Describe the facts neutrally: "A migration deleted the `orders_archive` table in production; the script ran against production because the target was chosen by an environment variable that defaults to production."
step: State contributing factors: no confirmation prompt for destructive statements, staging and production share naming, and migrations were not reviewed by a second person.

@mistakes
- Hunting for the root cause during an outage instead of mitigating first.
- Writing "human error" as the conclusion; the real question is why the system allowed the error and delayed detection.
- Postmortem action items with no owner or date, so nothing changes.
- Staying silent while investigating; stakeholders need regular updates even without news.

ev = [("09:00", "started"), ("09:12", "detected"), ("09:40", "mitigated"), ("11:05", "resolved")]
assert incident_metrics(ev) == {"time_to_detect": 12, "time_to_mitigate": 28, "duration": 125}

model:
```python
def incident_metrics(events):
    at = {}
    for stamp, label in events:
        hours, minutes = stamp.split(":")
        at[label] = int(hours) * 60 + int(minutes)
    return {
        "time_to_detect": at["detected"] - at["started"],
        "time_to_mitigate": at["mitigated"] - at["detected"],
        "duration": at["resolved"] - at["started"],
    }
```
hint: Convert each `"HH:MM"` to minutes since midnight and store it by label.
hint: Look times up by label rather than by position, because the list may be shuffled.
solution: Turn each time into minutes since midnight, keep a dict from label to minutes, and subtract. The shuffled test catches solutions that rely on list order, and `09:55` to `10:05` style crossings of the hour are handled by converting to total minutes.

model:
```python
def error_budget_minutes(slo_percent, days):
    total_minutes = days * 24 * 60
    return round((100 - slo_percent) / 100 * total_minutes, 2)
```
hint: There are `days * 24 * 60` minutes in the period.
hint: The allowed fraction of downtime is `(100 - slo_percent) / 100`.
solution: Multiply the allowed failure fraction by the total minutes in the period and round. At 99.9 percent over 30 days that is `0.001 * 43200 = 43.2` minutes, and at 100 percent the budget is zero.

@quiz code
prompt: Write `oncall_rotation(people, weeks)` that returns a list of `(primary, secondary)` pairs, one per week, for a weekly round-robin rotation. In week 0 the primary is `people[0]` and the secondary is `people[1]`; each following week moves one person along, wrapping around at the end of the list. If there is only one person, the secondary is `None`. If `weeks` is `0`, return an empty list. Assume `people` is not empty.
starter:
```python
def oncall_rotation(people, weeks):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert oncall_rotation(["Ana", "Bo", "Cy"], 4) == [("Ana", "Bo"), ("Bo", "Cy"), ("Cy", "Ana"), ("Ana", "Bo")]
assert oncall_rotation(["Solo"], 2) == [("Solo", None), ("Solo", None)]
assert oncall_rotation(["Ana", "Bo"], 3) == [("Ana", "Bo"), ("Bo", "Ana"), ("Ana", "Bo")]
assert oncall_rotation(["Ana", "Bo"], 0) == []
```
model:
```python
def oncall_rotation(people, weeks):
    n = len(people)
    schedule = []
    for week in range(weeks):
        primary = people[week % n]
        secondary = people[(week + 1) % n] if n > 1 else None
        schedule.append((primary, secondary))
    return schedule
```
hint: Use the remainder operator `%` with `len(people)` to wrap around.
hint: The secondary is the next person after the primary, but only when there is more than one person.
solution: The primary for week `w` is `people[w % n]` and the secondary is `people[(w + 1) % n]`, with a special case for one person (otherwise that person would be both). Modulo arithmetic handles the wrap-around.
