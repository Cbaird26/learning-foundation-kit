@article
Welcome to the team. Everything in this course is written the way a senior engineer would coach a new hire in the first month: what actually happens, why, and what to do about it. All companies, teams and people in the examples are **fictional** and invented for teaching.

Imagine you have just joined the platform team at *Fernwood Freight*, a fictional logistics company. In your first week you will not write a clever algorithm. You will read a ticket, change a few lines, open a pull request, answer review comments, watch automated checks run, and see your change reach production behind a feature flag. Understanding that loop is the most useful thing you can learn on day one.

### Companies and teams

Software companies differ widely, but most organize engineers into **teams** of roughly five to ten people who own a product area or a service: a checkout flow, a mobile app, a payments service, an internal developer platform. Some teams are **product teams** (they build things customers touch), some are **platform teams** (they build tools and infrastructure other teams use), and some are **internal-tools** or **data** teams. Every team has an owner you can ask, a backlog of work, and a set of services or repositories it is responsible for. Learn the names of those early.

### The life of a change

Almost every team follows a loop like this one:

1. **Ticket.** Work starts as an item in a tracker (a bug, a story, a task) with a description and acceptance criteria.
2. **Branch and commit.** You create a short-lived branch and make small, focused commits.
3. **Pull request (PR).** You describe what changed and why. A teammate reviews it.
4. **Automated checks.** Continuous integration builds the code and runs tests, linters and security scans.
5. **Merge.** After approval and green checks, the change joins the main branch.
6. **Deploy.** A pipeline ships it to staging and then production, often behind a **feature flag** that lets the team turn it on gradually.
7. **Observe.** Dashboards and alerts show whether the change behaves. If not, you roll back or switch the flag off.

### Agile frameworks: Scrum and Kanban

**Scrum** organizes work in fixed-length **sprints** (commonly one to four weeks). Its events are sprint planning, the daily scrum, the sprint review and the sprint retrospective. Its accountabilities are the product owner (ordering the backlog), the developers (building the increment) and the Scrum Master (helping the team follow the framework). **Kanban** has no sprints: work flows across a board (To do, In progress, In review, Done), and the team limits **work in progress (WIP)** so that unfinished work does not pile up. Many real teams blend them: "we do two-week sprints but we also cap work in review." Do not be dogmatic; ask what your team actually does.

### CI, CD and branching

**Continuous integration (CI)** means merging small changes into the main line frequently, with an automated build and tests on every change. **Continuous delivery** means every merged change is kept in a releasable state and can be shipped with a button press. **Continuous deployment** goes further: every change that passes the pipeline is deployed to production automatically. Teams that merge to main at least daily often practice **trunk-based development**; others use longer-lived feature branches and pay for it in painful merges.

### Measuring delivery: DORA

Research on software delivery popularized four measures, often called the **DORA metrics**: **deployment frequency**, **lead time for changes** (commit to production), **change failure rate** (the share of deployments that need remediation such as a rollback or hotfix) and **time to restore service**. (Recent DORA reports renamed the last one *failed deployment recovery time* and added a fifth measure, *deployment rework rate*; the four classic measures remain the most widely used vocabulary.) They are team-level health indicators, not scorecards for individuals; using them to rank people breaks them. A simpler number you can compute yourself is **cycle time**: days from starting a ticket to finishing it.

### Forecasting with velocity

Scrum teams often track **velocity**, the points completed per sprint. A rough forecast divides remaining points by recent average velocity and rounds up. It is a conversation starter, never a promise.

@example
title: Following a change from ticket to production
problem: At Fernwood Freight (fictional), ticket FF-212 says "Show estimated delivery window on the tracking page." Describe what happens from your first commit to customers seeing it.
step: You branch from main, implement the change behind a flag named `delivery_window_v2`, and commit in small steps with clear messages.
step: You open a PR that explains the user-visible change, how you tested it and how to roll it back (turn the flag off). A teammate reviews and asks for one extra test.
step: CI builds the service, runs unit and integration tests, a linter and a dependency scan. All checks must be green before merging.
step: After merge the pipeline deploys to staging, then to production with the flag **off**. The team enables it for 5 percent of users, watches error rate and latency, then raises it to 100 percent.
answer: Ticket, branch, PR and review, CI checks, merge, deploy behind a flag, gradual rollout, observation, with a fast rollback path at each stage.

@example
title: Forecasting sprints from velocity
problem: A team (fictional) has 41 story points left. Its last four sprints completed 6, 8, 10 and 12 points. Using the average of the last three sprints, how many sprints are needed?
step: The last three sprints are 8, 10 and 12. Their average is $(8+10+12)/3 = 10$ points per sprint.
step: Divide the remaining work by the average: $41/10 = 4.1$ sprints.
step: You cannot run a fraction of a sprint, so round up to 5.
step: Say it as a forecast: "About five sprints, assuming scope and capacity hold." Re-forecast every sprint.
answer: 5 sprints

@mistakes
- Treating Scrum or Kanban as a rulebook to enforce rather than a tool; ask how your team actually works before proposing changes.
- Opening a very large PR; reviewers skim it, bugs slip through, and merges conflict. Keep changes small and focused.
- Using DORA metrics or velocity to compare or rank individual engineers; they describe a team's system, and people will game them.
- Confusing continuous delivery (always releasable, a human chooses when) with continuous deployment (every passing change ships automatically).

prompt: Write `cycle_days(opened, closed)` that takes two dates as ISO strings (`"YYYY-MM-DD"`) and returns the whole number of days from `opened` to `closed`. Use the standard `datetime` module. A ticket opened and closed on the same day has cycle time `0`.
starter:
```python
from datetime import date


def cycle_days(opened, closed):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert cycle_days("2026-03-02", "2026-03-05") == 3
assert cycle_days("2026-03-02", "2026-03-02") == 0
assert cycle_days("2026-02-27", "2026-03-02") == 3
assert cycle_days("2025-12-30", "2026-01-02") == 3
```
model:
```python
from datetime import date


def cycle_days(opened, closed):
    start = date.fromisoformat(opened)
    end = date.fromisoformat(closed)
    return (end - start).days
```
hint: `date.fromisoformat("2026-03-02")` turns a string into a `date`.
hint: Subtracting two dates gives a `timedelta`, which has a `.days` attribute.
solution: Parse both strings with `date.fromisoformat`, subtract, and return `.days`. Using real dates handles month ends and year boundaries that string arithmetic would get wrong.

@practice code
prompt: Write `sprints_needed(points_remaining, velocities)` that forecasts the number of sprints left. Use the average of the **last three** values in `velocities` (or all of them if there are fewer than three) and round the result **up** to a whole number. If `points_remaining` is `0`, return `0`. Assume `velocities` is non-empty and its average is positive.
starter:
```python
def sprints_needed(points_remaining, velocities):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert sprints_needed(40, [8, 10, 12]) == 4
assert sprints_needed(41, [8, 10, 12]) == 5
assert sprints_needed(30, [5, 100, 10, 8, 12]) == 3
assert sprints_needed(0, [10]) == 0
assert sprints_needed(5, [10]) == 1
```
model:
```python
def sprints_needed(points_remaining, velocities):
    recent = velocities[-3:]
    total = sum(recent)
    # ceiling division without floating point
    return -(-points_remaining * len(recent) // total)
```
hint: `velocities[-3:]` gives the last three items, or all of them if fewer.
hint: Remaining divided by the average is `points_remaining * len(recent) / sum(recent)`; round up with `math.ceil` or ceiling division.
solution: Slice the last three velocities, compute remaining divided by their average, and round up. The model uses the integer trick `-(-a // b)` for ceiling division. The third test checks that old sprints (the 100) are ignored.

Write `wip_violations(counts, limits)` where `counts` maps column names to the number of cards currently in them and `limits` maps column names to WIP limits. Return a sorted list of the names of columns whose count is **strictly greater** than their limit. Columns that have no entry in `limits` have no limit and are never reported.
starter:
```python
def wip_violations(counts, limits):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
assert wip_violations({"Doing": 4, "Review": 2}, {"Doing": 3, "Review": 3}) == ["Doing"]
assert wip_violations({"Doing": 3}, {"Doing": 3}) == []
assert wip_violations({"Review": 5, "Doing": 9}, {"Review": 2, "Doing": 4}) == ["Doing", "Review"]
assert wip_violations({"Backlog": 50}, {}) == []
```
model:
```python
def wip_violations(counts, limits):
    return sorted(
        col for col, n in counts.items()
        if col in limits and n > limits[col]
    )
```
hint: Check that the column has a limit before comparing.
hint: A count equal to the limit is allowed; only strictly greater is a violation.
solution: Loop over `counts`, keep columns that appear in `limits` with a count above the limit, and sort the names. The equal case (`3` of `3`) is the common off-by-one trap.
