@project
title: Professional portfolio: design, postmortem, ethics, open source and growth
overview: You will produce five short professional documents to the standard a senior engineer would expect: a one-page design document, a blameless postmortem, an ethics case analysis using the ACM Code, a plan for contributing to an open-source project, and a one-year growth plan. Together they form a portfolio you can reuse in your job search and in your first year of work. All scenarios supplied below are fictional.
goal: Write clearly and concretely for a reader who has little time, with evidence, trade-offs and explicit decisions.
goal: Apply blameless analysis, ethical reasoning and license knowledge to realistic situations.
goal: Plan your own growth with measurable goals, dates and support.
step: Create a folder named `portfolio` on your computer (put it in a Git repository if you like) with five Markdown files: `design-doc.md`, `postmortem.md`, `ethics-case.md`, `oss-plan.md` and `growth-plan.md`.
step: Design document. Using the supplied design brief, write a document of at most 550 words with status, context and problem, goals and non-goals, proposal, at least two alternatives, risks and open questions, and rollout and testing. Run a README-style check yourself: every required heading present, every open question listed as an unchecked task item.
step: Postmortem. Using the supplied incident record, write a blameless postmortem with summary and impact, a timeline in order, contributing factors (more than one, none that blames a person), what went well, what went poorly, where you got lucky, and at least five action items with an owner role and a due date.
step: Ethics case. Using the supplied case, write an analysis of 500 to 800 words: facts and unknowns, stakeholders, ACM Code principles cited by number, at least three options, at least two defensible views stated fairly, and your recommended action. Do not quote the Code beyond a few words; paraphrase.
step: Open-source plan. Choose a real open-source project you use. Read its README, contribution guide, code of conduct and license. step: Growth plan. Write a one-year plan for yourself using the supplied template: current state, two or three goals with a measure and due date each, quarterly milestones, learning actions with scheduled time, support needed, risks and review dates.
step: Review each document as a stranger would: read it aloud, cut sentences that do not help the reader, check numbers, and ask a friend or colleague to point out the first confusing sentence. Revise, and add a short `README.md` to the folder that lists the five documents with one sentence each.

@review
**Week by week**

1. **Teams and companies.** A change travels ticket, branch, pull request, review, CI checks, merge, deploy (often behind a flag), observe. Know Scrum (sprints and events), Kanban (WIP limits), CI, continuous delivery versus continuous deployment, and the four classic DORA measures (deployment frequency, lead time for changes, change failure rate, time to restore; recent DORA reports rename the last one failed deployment recovery time and add deployment rework rate).
2. **Roles.** Engineers, managers, product managers, designers, QA, SRE, data, security and legal each own different things. A RACI chart has exactly one Accountable person per decision.
3. **Technical writing.** Know your reader; lead with the point. A design doc has context and problem, goals and non-goals, proposal, alternatives, risks and open questions. A README answers what, why, install, usage, contributing and license.
4. **Communication.** Status updates lead with status, risks and asks; meetings need a purpose, agenda and action items; ask for help with the goal, what you tried and a specific question.
5. **Estimating.** Estimates are ranges, not commitments. PERT mean is $(O+4M+P)/6$, sigma is $(P-O)/6$, and independent task variances add. Track progress and renegotiate early.
6. **Legacy code.** Run it, trace it, use `git blame` and `git log -S`, write characterization tests, refactor in small steps, prefer the strangler fig to a rewrite.
7. **On-call and incidents.** Mitigate first; alerts must be actionable; the error budget is the allowed unavailability, for example 43.2 minutes for 99.9 percent over 30 days. Blameless postmortems find several contributing factors and owned, dated actions.
8. **Open source.** Contribute with discussion and focused pull requests. Permissive licenses (MIT, BSD, Apache-2.0) versus copyleft (MPL file-level, LGPL library-level, GPL-3.0 whole work, AGPL-3.0 adds network use). Apache-2.0 and GPL-3.0 include patent terms; MIT and BSD have no express patent grant. General information, not legal advice.
9. **Ethics.** The ACM Code (2018): general principles 1.1 to 1.7, professional responsibilities 2.1 to 2.9, leadership 3.x, compliance 4.x. Use facts, stakeholders, harms, principles, options and the least drastic effective step; show more than one defensible view.
10. **Accessibility and privacy.** WCAG 2.2 (POUR; AA contrast 4.5:1 normal text and 3:1 large; target size 24 by 24 CSS pixels). Data minimization, purpose limitation and retention; GDPR basics in general terms.
11. **Careers.** Levels are company-specific; scope and independence distinguish them; IC and management are different tracks; keep evidence; make measurable goals.
12. **Learning and mentoring.** Practice, read code, space your reviews, teach; use SBI feedback; use a growth plan with measures and review dates.

**Self-check**
- Can I trace a change from ticket to production and name each safeguard?
- Can I turn three-point estimates into a range for a whole feature?
- Can I rewrite a blameful sentence as a blameless one with an action?
- Can I explain what each license I know requires when I distribute code?
- Can I analyze a case with the ACM Code and state both sides fairly?
- Can I check a color pair's contrast and a target's size?

**Common errors**
- Treating an estimate as a commitment, or adding standard deviations instead of variances.
- Blaming people in a postmortem, or listing actions with no owner or date.
- Believing GPL obligations arise from private use, or that no license means free to use.
- Confusing "legal" with "ethical," or presenting only one view of a contested case.
- Writing documents with no reader, no alternatives, or no measurable goals.

@exam code
prompt: A team plans a feature from three-point estimates. Write `feature_plan(tasks, focus_factor, start)` where `tasks` is a list of `(o, m, p)` tuples in focused days, `focus_factor` is the fraction of a working day available for focused work (for example `0.6`), and `start` is an ISO date string. Compute the feature mean (sum of `(o + 4*m + p) / 6`) and sigma (square root of the sum of squared `(p - o) / 6`), both rounded to 2 decimals; the number of working days `n` needed, which is `mean / focus_factor` rounded **up** to a whole number (round the quotient to 9 decimals first to avoid floating-point noise); and the finish date: count working days (Monday to Friday) starting from `start` as day 1 if it is a weekday (a weekend start begins on the following Monday), and finish on the date of the `n`-th working day. Return a dict with keys `"mean"`, `"sigma"`, `"working_days"` and `"finish"` (an ISO date string). If `n` is `0` (no tasks), `"finish"` is `None`.
starter:
```python
import math
from datetime import date, timedelta


def feature_plan(tasks, focus_factor, start):
    pass
```
tests:
```python
r = feature_plan([(2, 3, 10), (1, 2, 3)], 0.6, "2026-03-02")
assert r == {"mean": 6.0, "sigma": 1.37, "working_days": 10, "finish": "2026-03-13"}
r2 = feature_plan([(3, 6, 15)], 0.5, "2026-03-02")
assert r2 == {"mean": 7.0, "sigma": 2.0, "working_days": 14, "finish": "2026-03-19"}
r3 = feature_plan([(1, 1, 1)], 1.0, "2026-03-07")
assert r3 == {"mean": 1.0, "sigma": 0.0, "working_days": 1, "finish": "2026-03-09"}
r4 = feature_plan([], 0.6, "2026-03-02")
assert r4 == {"mean": 0.0, "sigma": 0.0, "working_days": 0, "finish": None}
assert feature_plan([(1, 1, 1)] * 3, 1.0, "2026-03-05")["finish"] == "2026-03-09"
```
model:
```python
import math
from datetime import date, timedelta


def feature_plan(tasks, focus_factor, start):
    mean = 0.0
    variance = 0.0
    for o, m, p in tasks:
        mean += (o + 4 * m + p) / 6
        variance += ((p - o) / 6) ** 2
    days = math.ceil(round(mean / focus_factor, 9))
    finish = None
    if days > 0:
        day = date.fromisoformat(start)
        counted = 0
        while True:
            if day.weekday() < 5:
                counted += 1
                if counted == days:
                    break
            day += timedelta(days=1)
        finish = day.isoformat()
    return {
        "mean": round(mean, 2),
        "sigma": round(math.sqrt(variance), 2),
        "working_days": days,
        "finish": finish,
    }
```
hint: Reuse the PERT formulas: sum the means and the variances, and take the square root only at the end.
hint: For the date, loop from `start` one day at a time, counting only days where `date.weekday() < 5`.
solution: Compute the mean and the combined sigma, convert the mean to working days with the focus factor and round up, then walk forward day by day counting weekdays until you reach day `n`. A weekend start is handled naturally because Saturday and Sunday are not counted. The three-task test starts on Thursday 5 March: Thursday, Friday and Monday are days 1, 2 and 3.

The logs are readable by about 200 employees and are copied to a third-party logging vendor. Write a response of 10 to 14 sentences covering: your first actions during the incident and their order (mitigation, communication, roles), how you handle the privacy exposure and whom you involve, how you would describe the cause in a blameless way, two contributing factors and two action items with owners, and one ACM principle you apply (cite its number). Mention at least one decision on which reasonable people could differ.
hint: Mitigate first (roll back or disable the change), but also stop the exposure and tell the right people.
hint: Blameless wording describes the system conditions that let the logging change reach production.
solution: Example. I acknowledge the page, declare an incident with a severity, and ask for an incident commander and a communications lead so I can focus on diagnosis. The quickest mitigation is to roll back Alex's deploy (or switch off its flag), which should end both the errors and the new logging; I do this first and confirm that the error rate recovers, posting a status update every 30 minutes. Because the logs now hold personal data, I also notify the security and privacy owners right away, ask for access to the affected log indices to be restricted, and ask whether the vendor can purge or isolate the affected entries; I record what was exposed, for how long and who had access, since privacy rules may require assessment and possibly notification within strict deadlines, and I let the privacy lead and legal decide on that (this is general information, not legal advice). Actions: (a) add a redaction filter that removes known sensitive fields from all logs (owner: platform team, due in 3 weeks); (b) add a log-exposure step to the incident runbook and a pre-deploy checklist item for logging changes (owner: reliability lead, due in 2 weeks). The principle I apply is respecting privacy (1.6), together with avoiding harm (1.2). A decision on which reasonable people differ is whether to delay the rollback by a few minutes to capture more diagnostic data first: one view prioritizes learning the cause, while another prioritizes ending customer impact and the exposure immediately; I would roll back first and keep the logs for analysis under restricted access.
