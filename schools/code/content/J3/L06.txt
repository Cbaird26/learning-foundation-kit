@article
A **system of linear equations** is two or more linear equations considered together. A **solution** is an ordered pair $(x, y)$ that makes **every** equation true. Because each equation graphs as a line, a solution is a point where the lines meet.

### Three outcomes

- The lines cross at one point: **exactly one solution**.
- The lines are parallel (same slope, different intercept): **no solution**.
- The lines are the same line: **infinitely many solutions**.

### Method 1: graphing

Graph both lines and read the intersection. This shows the picture, but it is only as precise as your graph, so always check the answer in both equations. For $y = x + 1$ and $y = -2x + 7$, the lines meet at $(2, 3)$.

### Method 2: substitution

Use when one equation already solves for a variable. Replace that variable in the other equation. For $y = 2x + 1$ and $x + y = 7$: substituting gives $x + (2x + 1) = 7$, so $3x = 6$ and $x = 2$. Then $y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$. The solution is $(2, 5)$.

### Method 3: elimination

Add or subtract the equations so that one variable cancels. For $x + y = 10$ and $x - y = 2$, add the equations: $2x = 12$, so $x = 6$. Then $6 + y = 10$ gives $y = 4$. If the coefficients do not match, first multiply an equation by a number to make them match (or be opposites).

### Always check

Substitute the pair into **both** original equations. Many wrong answers satisfy only one.

### Spotting special cases

If after substituting or eliminating you get a false statement, such as $0 = 5$, the system has no solution. If you get a true statement, such as $0 = 0$, there are infinitely many. For $y = 2x + 1$ and $y = 2x - 4$ the slopes are equal but the intercepts differ, so the lines are parallel and never meet.

### Modeling with systems

Systems answer "when are two things equal?" questions. If tickets cost \$8 for adults and \$5 for children, then with $a$ adults and $c$ children, a total of $a + c$ people and a total cost $8a + 5c$ give two equations in two unknowns.

@example
title: Substitution
problem: Solve the system $y = 2x + 1$ and $x + y = 7$.
step: The first equation gives $y$ already, so substitute $2x + 1$ for $y$ in the second: $x + (2x + 1) = 7$.
step: Combine like terms: $3x + 1 = 7$, so $3x = 6$ and $x = 2$.
step: Put $x = 2$ in the first equation: $y = 2(2) + 1 = 5$.
step: Check in both: $5 = 2(2) + 1$ is true, and $2 + 5 = 7$ is true. The solution is $(2, 5)$.

@mistakes
- Finding only $x$ and stopping; a solution is an ordered pair.
- Subtracting equations and forgetting to subtract every term, especially negative ones.
- Checking the answer in only one equation.
- Concluding "no solution" because the lines look close; compare the slopes exactly.

prompt: Solve the system $y = 2x - 1$ and $y = x + 3$. Give the answer as $(x, y)$.
answer: (4, 7)
check: lin([[-2, 1], [-1, 1]], [-1, 3])
hint: Both expressions equal $y$, so set them equal to each other.
hint: $2x - 1 = x + 3$.
solution: $2x - 1 = x + 3$ gives $x = 4$, and $y = 4 + 3 = 7$. Check: $2(4) - 1 = 7$.

solution: $4x = 12$ so $x = 3$, and $y = 3(3) = 9$. Check: $3 + 9 = 12$.

@practice tuple

answer: (6, 4)

hint: Add the two equations so that $y$ cancels.
hint: $2x = 12$.
solution: Adding gives $2x = 12$, so $x = 6$, and $y = 10 - 6 = 4$. Check: $6 - 4 = 2$.

@practice self
prompt: Solve the system $y = x + 1$ and $y = -2x + 7$ in three ways: by graphing, by substitution and by elimination. Show that all three methods agree and check the solution in both equations.
hint: Plot two points on each line, or set the two expressions for y equal.
hint: Find the intersection first, then confirm it with a second method.
solution: Graphing: plot $y = x + 1$ (through $(0,1)$ and $(1,2)$) and $y = -2x + 7$ (through $(0,7)$ and $(3,1)$); the lines cross at $(2, 3)$. Substitution: $x + 1 = -2x + 7$ gives $3x = 6$, $x = 2$, and $y = 3$. Elimination: rewrite as $-x + y = 1$ and $2x + y = 7$; subtract the first from the second: $3x = 6$, so $x = 2$, then $y = 3$. Check: $3 = 2 + 1$ and $3 = -2(2) + 7$ are both true. The solution is $(2, 3)$.
rubric: Both lines are graphed accurately and the crossing point is read as $(2, 3)$.
rubric: The substitution method sets the expressions for $y$ equal and solves for $x$, then finds $y$.
rubric: The elimination method cancels a variable by adding or subtracting the equations.
rubric: The solution is checked in both original equations.

@quiz tuple
prompt: Solve the system $3x + y = 11$ and $x - y = 1$. Give the answer as $(x, y)$.
answer: (3, 2)
check: lin([[3, 1], [1, -1]], [11, 1])
hint: Add the equations; the $y$ terms are opposites.
hint: $4x = 12$.
solution: Adding gives $4x = 12$, so $x = 3$, and $y = 3 - 1 = 2$. Check: $3(3) + 2 = 11$.

@quiz num
prompt: A theater sells adult tickets for \$8 and child tickets for \$5. It sells $10$ tickets for a total of \$62. How many adult tickets were sold?
answer: 4
check: lin([[1, 1], [8, 5]], [10, 62])[0]
hint: Let $a$ be adult tickets and $c$ child tickets; write one equation for the count and one for the money.
hint: $a + c = 10$ and $8a + 5c = 62$; replace $c$ with $10 - a$.
solution: $c = 10 - a$, so $8a + 5(10 - a) = 62$, which gives $3a + 50 = 62$ and $a = 4$. Check: $4(8) + 6(5) = 62$.
