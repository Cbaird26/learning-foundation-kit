@article
Our **solar system** is the Sun and everything held in orbit around it by gravity. The Sun contains about 99.9% of all the mass in the solar system. Its gravity holds the planets in nearly circular, slightly **elliptical** orbits.

### The planets

In order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

- **Terrestrial planets** (Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars): small, rocky, dense, with few or no moons.
- **Gas giants** (Jupiter, Saturn): huge, made mostly of hydrogen and helium, with rings and many moons.
- **Ice giants** (Uranus, Neptune): large planets with interiors rich in water, ammonia and methane, and cold atmospheres.

### Smaller bodies

The **asteroid belt** lies between Mars and Jupiter and contains rocky leftovers from planet formation. **Comets** are balls of ice and dust that grow glowing tails when they approach the Sun. The **Kuiper belt** beyond Neptune holds icy bodies and **dwarf planets** such as Pluto. A dwarf planet orbits the Sun and is round, but it has not cleared other objects from its orbit (Ceres, in the asteroid belt, is another).

### Distances: the astronomical unit

Kilometers get awkward at this scale, so astronomers use the **astronomical unit (AU)**, the average Earth–Sun distance, about 150 million km. Approximate average distances are: Mercury 0.39 AU, Venus 0.72, Earth 1.00, Mars 1.52, Jupiter 5.2, Saturn 9.58, Uranus 19.2 and Neptune 30.1 AU. Light travels at about 300,000 km/s and takes about **499 s, or 8.3 minutes**, to travel 1 AU.

### Kepler's third law

Planets farther out orbit more slowly. For anything orbiting the Sun, with the period $T$ in years and the average distance $a$ in AU,
$$T^2 = a^3.$$
Jupiter at $a = 5.2$ AU has $T = \sqrt{5.2^3} \approx 11.9$ years.

### Building a scale model

The solar system is mostly empty space, and a model shows it best. If $1\ \text{AU} = 10$ cm, each planet's distance in centimeters is $10 \times$ its distance in AU. Neptune would then be about 3 m from the Sun, while Earth is only 10 cm away. The planets themselves would be specks smaller than a grain of sand.

@example
title: Orbital period from distance
problem: Mars is at an average distance of 1.52 AU from the Sun. Estimate Mars's orbital period in Earth years.
step: Write Kepler's third law: $T^2 = a^3$.
step: Cube the distance: $1.52^3 \approx 3.51$.
step: Take the square root: $T = \sqrt{3.51} \approx 1.87$ years.
step: This is close to the known value of about 1.88 years, so Mars takes almost two Earth years to orbit the Sun.

@mistakes
- Thinking Mercury is the hottest planet because it is closest to the Sun. Venus is hotter because of its thick carbon dioxide atmosphere.
- Calling Pluto a planet. It is a dwarf planet.
- Forgetting to cube $a$ before taking the square root in Kepler's law.
- Picturing planets as crowded close together. Real distances are huge compared with the planets' sizes.

solution: $T = \sqrt{4^3} = \sqrt{64} = 8$ years.

@quiz num
prompt: A comet-like object orbits the Sun with an average distance of 9 AU. Find its orbital period in years using $T^2 = a^3$.
answer: 27
check: math.sqrt(9 ** 3)
unit: years
hint: Cube 9 and then take the square root.
solution: $T = \sqrt{9^3} = \sqrt{729} = 27$ years.
