@article
Earth's lithosphere is broken into about a dozen large plates and several smaller ones. The plates move a few centimeters per year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. That is slow, but over millions of years it moves continents and builds mountains. This idea is the theory of **plate tectonics**.

### Evidence

Early scientists noticed that the coasts of South America and Africa fit together like puzzle pieces. Matching rock layers, mountain chains and fossils (such as the freshwater reptile *Mesosaurus*) appear on continents now separated by ocean. This led to the idea of **continental drift**. The missing piece was a mechanism, which came from the ocean floor. New crust forms at **mid-ocean ridges**. The seafloor gets older and thicker with distance from a ridge, and its magnetic stripes are mirror images on both sides of it. This is **seafloor spreading**.

### What moves the plates

Heat from Earth's interior drives slow **convection** in the mantle: hot rock rises, spreads, cools and sinks. Plates also move because old, cold, dense oceanic plate sinks into the mantle and pulls the rest of the plate along (**slab pull**), and because new crust at a ridge pushes outward (**ridge push**).

### Three kinds of boundary

1. **Divergent boundaries:** plates move apart. Magma rises and forms new crust. Examples: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge and the East African Rift.
2. **Convergent boundaries:** plates move together. The result depends on the plates.
   - Oceanic plate meets continental plate: the denser oceanic plate **subducts** (sinks) beneath, making a deep **trench** and a line of volcanoes (the Andes).
   - Oceanic meets oceanic: one subducts, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanic islands (Japan, the Aleutians).
   - Continental meets continental: neither sinks easily, so the crust crumples into huge mountains (the Himalayas).
3. **Transform boundaries:** plates slide past each other horizontally. Crust is neither made nor destroyed, but earthquakes are common. Example: the San Andreas Fault.

### Rates and distance

Plate speeds are small, so we use big time spans. A handy conversion: **1 cm per year equals 10 km per million years**. Then
$$\text{distance} = \text{rate} \times \text{time}, \qquad \text{rate} = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}.$$
Always convert units before you divide: $1\ \text{km} = 100{,}000\ \text{cm}$.

@example
title: How wide does an ocean grow?
problem: The Atlantic Ocean widens by about 2.5 cm per year. If the rate stayed constant, how many kilometers wider would it become in 100 million years?
step: Convert the rate: $2.5$ cm per year is $2.5 \times 10 = 25$ km per million years.
step: The time is $100$ million years, so multiply: $25 \times 100 = 2500$ km.
step: Check with the long way: $2.5 \times 100{,}000{,}000 = 250{,}000{,}000$ cm, and $250{,}000{,}000 \div 100{,}000 = 2500$ km. The answers agree.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to convert centimeters to kilometers (there are 100,000 cm in 1 km).
- Saying new crust forms at a transform boundary. At transform boundaries plates slide past each other.
- Thinking continents plow through the ocean floor. The continents ride along on plates that include ocean floor.
- Confusing divergent (apart) with convergent (together).

@quiz num
prompt: Two plates move apart at 5 cm per year. How many million years will it take for the gap to grow to 3,000 km?
answer: 60
check: 3000 * 100000 / 5 / 1000000
tol: 1%
unit: million years
hint: Convert 3,000 km to centimeters, then divide by the rate.
hint: 5 cm per year is 50 km per million years.
solution: $5$ cm/yr $= 50$ km per million years, so the time is $\frac{3000}{50} = 60$ million years.
