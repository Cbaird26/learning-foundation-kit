@article
Two lines in a plane are **parallel** if they never meet; equivalently, they stay the same distance apart. A third line that crosses both is a **transversal**. Picture two horizontal lines, one above the other, and a slanted line crossing both. The transversal creates eight angles: four at each crossing point.

### Naming the pairs

Label the crossings $P$ (on the upper line) and $Q$ (on the lower line). At each crossing, there are angles above and below the line, and to the left and right of the transversal.

- **Corresponding angles** sit in the same position at each crossing, for example both above the line and to the right of the transversal.
- **Alternate interior angles** lie between the two lines, on opposite sides of the transversal.
- **Alternate exterior angles** lie outside the two lines, on opposite sides of the transversal.
- **Same-side interior angles** lie between the lines on the same side of the transversal.

### The parallel-line theorems

If the two lines are parallel, then:

1. Corresponding angles are congruent.
2. Alternate interior angles are congruent.
3. Alternate exterior angles are congruent.
4. Same-side interior angles are supplementary (they add to $180^\circ$).

The first fact is taken as a **postulate**, and the others follow from it with vertical angles and linear pairs. For instance, if corresponding angles are equal and vertical angles are equal, then alternate interior angles are equal: each interior angle at the upper intersection is vertical to the angle that corresponds to its alternate interior partner at the lower intersection, so both equal that angle.

### The converses

Each theorem has a true converse, which lets you **prove** lines are parallel: if corresponding angles (or alternate interior angles, or alternate exterior angles) are congruent, or same-side interior angles are supplementary, then the lines are parallel.

### Writing a proof

A **two-column proof** lists statements on the left and a reason on the right: a given fact, a definition, a postulate or a theorem already proved. Each line must follow from earlier lines. Here is a model.

*Given:* line $a \parallel$ line $b$, cut by transversal $t$. *Prove:* the two same-side interior angles are supplementary.

| Statement | Reason |
|---|---|
| 1. $a \parallel b$ | Given |
| 2. Alternate interior angles $\angle 1 \cong \angle 2$ | Alternate interior angles theorem |
| 3. $\angle 2$ and $\angle 3$ form a linear pair, so $m\angle 2 + m\angle 3 = 180^\circ$ | Linear pair is supplementary |
| 4. $m\angle 1 + m\angle 3 = 180^\circ$ | Substitution (lines 2, 3) |

Here $\angle 1$ and $\angle 3$ are the same-side interior angles. The pattern "convert to an angle you know, then use a linear pair or vertical angles" solves most angle problems in this unit.

@example
title: Angles with algebra on parallel lines
problem: Lines $a$ and $b$ are parallel and are cut by a transversal. Two alternate interior angles measure $(4x + 7)^\circ$ and $(6x - 25)^\circ$. Find $x$, and then find the measure of the interior angle that forms a linear pair with the $(6x - 25)^\circ$ angle (it is the same-side interior partner of the $(4x + 7)^\circ$ angle).
step: Alternate interior angles are equal when lines are parallel: $4x + 7 = 6x - 25$.
step: Solve: $32 = 2x$, so $x = 16$. Each alternate interior angle measures $4(16) + 7 = 71^\circ$.
step: The angle that forms a linear pair with an interior angle, at the same crossing, is that crossing's other interior angle on the opposite side of the transversal, and it is the same-side partner of the interior angle at the other crossing.
step: Supplementary: $180 - 71 = 109^\circ$.

@mistakes
- Using "alternate interior angles are equal" when the lines are not known to be parallel.
- Mixing up same-side interior angles (supplementary) with alternate interior angles (equal).
- Giving a reason such as "it looks equal" or "by the diagram" in a proof.
- Using a theorem and the converse interchangeably: to prove lines parallel you need the converse, not the original.

prompt: Line $a \parallel$ line $b$, and transversal $t$ crosses both. An exterior angle on the upper line measures $(7x - 20)^\circ$ and the alternate exterior angle on the lower line measures $(3x + 40)^\circ$. Find the measure of the angle in degrees.
answer: 85
check: 7 * ((40 + 20) / 4) - 20
hint: Alternate exterior angles are equal when the lines are parallel.
hint: $7x - 20 = 3x + 40$ gives $x = 15$.
solution: $4x = 60$ so $x = 15$. The angle measures $7(15) - 20 = 85^\circ$ (and $3(15) + 40 = 85^\circ$).

@practice self
prompt: Given: lines $a \parallel b$, cut by transversal $t$. Let $\angle 1$ and $\angle 2$ be corresponding angles at the two crossings, and let $\angle 3$ be the angle vertical to $\angle 2$. Write a short two-column proof that $\angle 1 \cong \angle 3$.
hint: Link $\angle 1$ to $\angle 2$ with the parallel lines, then $\angle 2$ to $\angle 3$ with the crossing lines.
solution: Statements and reasons. 1. $a \parallel b$ (Given). 2. $\angle 1 \cong \angle 2$ (Corresponding angles postulate, since $a \parallel b$). 3. $\angle 2 \cong \angle 3$ (Vertical angles are congruent). 4. $\angle 1 \cong \angle 3$ (Transitive property of congruence, lines 2 and 3).
rubric: Starts from the given $a \parallel b$ and uses it to justify the corresponding-angles step.
rubric: Uses vertical angles for the second step.
rubric: Combines the two equalities with the transitive property in the last line.
rubric: Every statement has a named reason (no "it looks equal").

@quiz num
prompt: Two parallel lines are cut by a transversal. A pair of alternate interior angles measure $(5x - 9)^\circ$ and $(2x + 36)^\circ$. Find the measure of each of them in degrees.
answer: 66
check: 5 * ((36 + 9) / 3) - 9
hint: Alternate interior angles are equal for parallel lines.
hint: $5x - 9 = 2x + 36$ gives $3x = 45$.
solution: $x = 15$, so each angle is $5(15) - 9 = 66^\circ$ (and $2(15) + 36 = 66^\circ$).

@quiz mc
prompt: Lines $m$ and $n$ are cut by a transversal. Two same-side interior angles measure $112^\circ$ and $68^\circ$. Which conclusion is correct?
choice*: $m \parallel n$, because same-side interior angles are supplementary.
choice: $m$ and $n$ intersect, because the angles are not equal.
choice: $m \parallel n$, because the angles are alternate interior angles.
choice: Nothing can be concluded, because the angles are not congruent.
hint: Check what the two measures add to.
solution: $112 + 68 = 180$, so the same-side interior angles are supplementary, and the converse theorem gives $m \parallel n$. Equality of the angles is not required for this pair.
