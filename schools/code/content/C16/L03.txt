@article
Bayes' rule turns around a conditional probability. We often know how likely the evidence is under each hypothesis ("how often do spam emails contain the word *free*?") but want the reverse ("how likely is spam given that *free* appears?").

### The rule

From the definition of conditional probability, $P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)P(B) = P(B \mid A)P(A)$. Dividing by $P(B) > 0$ gives **Bayes' rule**:

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(B \mid A)\,P(A)}{P(B)}.$$

For a hypothesis $H$ and evidence $E$, the terms have standard names: $P(H)$ is the **prior**, $P(E \mid H)$ is the **likelihood**, and $P(H \mid E)$ is the **posterior**. The denominator is usually computed with the law of total probability over $H$ and $H^c$:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid H^c)\,P(H^c)}.$$

### The base rate fallacy

Suppose a condition affects 1% of a population and a test detects it 90% of the time (**sensitivity**) but also gives a false positive for 5% of healthy people. Most people guess that a positive result means about a 90% chance of having the condition. In fact the posterior is only about 15%, because the 99% of healthy people produce many more false positives than the 1% of sick people produce true positives. Ignoring the prior (the **base rate**) is the *base rate fallacy*. Always ask: how common was the hypothesis before the evidence?

### Odds form

The **odds** of an event are $P/(1-P)$. Dividing the Bayes formula for $H$ by the one for $H^c$ gives

$$\underbrace{\frac{P(H \mid E)}{P(H^c \mid E)}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{P(H)}{P(H^c)}}_{\text{prior odds}} \times \underbrace{\frac{P(E \mid H)}{P(E \mid H^c)}}_{\text{likelihood ratio}}.$$

Evidence multiplies the odds by the likelihood ratio, so several pieces of evidence can be applied one after another. Convert odds $o$ back to a probability with $o/(1+o)$.

### Naive Bayes spam filtering

A spam filter estimates $P(\text{spam} \mid \text{words})$. Modeling the joint behavior of all words is impractical, so the **naive Bayes** classifier assumes the words are **conditionally independent given the class**: $P(w_1, \dots, w_k \mid \text{spam}) = \prod_i P(w_i \mid \text{spam})$, and likewise for ham (non-spam). The assumption is rarely exactly true, but the classifier is simple, fast and often effective. Its score is

$$P(\text{spam} \mid w_1, \dots, w_k) = \frac{P(\text{spam})\prod_i P(w_i \mid \text{spam})}{P(\text{spam})\prod_i P(w_i \mid \text{spam}) + P(\text{ham})\prod_i P(w_i \mid \text{ham})}.$$

In practice implementations add logarithms instead of multiplying many small numbers, to avoid floating-point underflow, and smooth the word counts so that no estimated probability is exactly zero.

@example
title: A diagnostic test
problem: A condition has prevalence 1%. A test is positive for 90% of people who have it and for 5% of people who do not. A randomly chosen person tests positive. What is the probability they have the condition?
step: Let $D$ be "has the condition" and $+$ "tests positive". We are given $P(D) = 0.01$, $P(+ \mid D) = 0.9$ and $P(+ \mid D^c) = 0.05$.
step: The numerator is $P(+ \mid D)P(D) = 0.9 \cdot 0.01 = 0.009$.
step: The denominator adds the false positives: $0.009 + P(+ \mid D^c)P(D^c) = 0.009 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99 = 0.009 + 0.0495 = 0.0585$.
step: So $P(D \mid +) = 0.009 / 0.0585 = 2/13 \approx 0.154$.
answer: $2/13 \approx 0.154$

@example
title: One word in a spam filter
problem: 30% of incoming mail is spam. The word "free" appears in 40% of spam messages and in 2% of non-spam messages. What is the probability that a message containing "free" is spam?
step: Let $S$ be spam and $F$ the event that "free" appears. Then $P(S) = 0.3$, $P(F \mid S) = 0.4$ and $P(F \mid S^c) = 0.02$.
step: Numerator: $0.4 \cdot 0.3 = 0.12$. The non-spam term is $0.02 \cdot 0.7 = 0.014$.
step: $P(S \mid F) = \frac{0.12}{0.12 + 0.014} = \frac{0.12}{0.134} = \frac{60}{67}$.
step: This is about $0.896$. The odds check agrees: prior odds $3:7$ times likelihood ratio $0.4/0.02 = 20$ gives posterior odds $60:7$, and $60/67$ is the matching probability.
answer: $60/67 \approx 0.896$

@mistakes
- Reporting the likelihood $P(E \mid H)$ as if it were the posterior $P(H \mid E)$, as in "the test is 90% accurate, so I am 90% likely to be sick".
- Forgetting the prior (base rate) and the false-positive term in the denominator.
- Using the wrong complement: the denominator needs $P(E \mid H^c)P(H^c)$, not $P(E \mid H^c)(1 - P(E \mid H))$.
- Applying naive Bayes to words that are strongly dependent (such as repeated copies of one phrase) and treating each copy as independent evidence.

model:
```python
def posterior(prior, p_e_given_h, p_e_given_not_h):
    numerator = p_e_given_h * prior
    denominator = numerator + p_e_given_not_h * (1 - prior)
    return numerator / denominator
```
hint: The numerator is `p_e_given_h * prior`.
hint: The denominator is the numerator plus the false-alarm term `p_e_given_not_h * (1 - prior)`.
solution: Compute the numerator $P(E \mid H)P(H)$, add $P(E \mid H^c)P(H^c)$ to get $P(E)$, and divide. With evidence that is equally likely under both hypotheses (the last test) the posterior equals the prior.

model:
```python
def spam_probability(prior_spam, p_spam, p_ham, words):
    spam_score = prior_spam
    ham_score = 1 - prior_spam
    for w in words:
        if w in p_spam and w in p_ham:
            spam_score *= p_spam[w]
            ham_score *= p_ham[w]
    return spam_score / (spam_score + ham_score)
```
hint: Keep two running products: the spam score starts at the prior and the ham score at one minus the prior.
hint: For each usable word, multiply the spam score by its spam likelihood and the ham score by its ham likelihood. The answer is the spam score divided by the sum of both.
solution: Multiply each class score by the likelihood of every usable word, then normalize: `spam_score / (spam_score + ham_score)`. Skipping unknown words leaves both scores at the priors, so the result is `prior_spam`.

model:
```python
def sequential_posterior(prior, evidence):
    odds = prior / (1 - prior)
    for p_h, p_not_h in evidence:
        odds *= p_h / p_not_h
    return odds / (1 + odds)
```
hint: Convert the prior to odds first, and convert back at the end.
hint: Each pair multiplies the odds by `p_e_given_h / p_e_given_not_h`.
solution: Start with `odds = prior / (1 - prior)`, multiply by each likelihood ratio, and return `odds / (1 + odds)`. In the last test the ratios are $3$ and $1/3$, which cancel and leave the prior.

@quiz num
prompt: A bag holds 1 two-headed coin and 9 fair coins. One coin is chosen at random and tossed 3 times, giving 3 heads. What is the probability that the chosen coin is the two-headed one? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 8/17

solution: The prior is $1/10$ and the likelihood is $1$ for the two-headed coin and $1/8$ for a fair coin. So $P = \frac{1/10}{1/10 + (9/10)(1/8)} = \frac{1/10}{17/80} = \frac{8}{17}$.
