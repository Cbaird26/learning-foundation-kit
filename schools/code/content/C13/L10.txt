@article
An **estimate** is a prediction under uncertainty; a **commitment** is a promise. Confusing them causes most planning trouble: if a manager hears "about three weeks" as "exactly three weeks", the team is blamed for physics. Software is hard to estimate because the work is new each time, requirements change and unknown problems appear. The **cone of uncertainty** describes how estimates made at the start of a project can be wrong by a large factor in either direction, and narrow only as decisions are made and risks are resolved. The honest response is to estimate in **ranges**, re-estimate as you learn, and plan with a buffer.

### Techniques

- **Expert judgment and analogy**: compare with similar past work ("the last report took six days").
- **Decomposition**: break a large item into pieces small enough to estimate with some confidence, then add the pieces. Errors in individual pieces partly cancel, but shared mistakes (underestimating all the integration work) do not.
- **Story points**: a relative size for a story, reflecting effort, complexity and uncertainty, not hours. A team picks a reference story ("this is a 3") and sizes others against it, often on a Fibonacci-like scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13). Many teams find relative sizing faster and more consistent than estimating hours, because people are usually better at comparing than at predicting.
- **Planning poker**: each team member privately picks a card, all reveal at once, and the highest and lowest explain their reasoning; the discussion exposes hidden assumptions, and the team re-votes. The conversation is the point, not the number.
- **Three-point (PERT) estimates**: give an optimistic $o$, most likely $m$ and pessimistic $p$ value, and use the weighted mean

$$E = \frac{o + 4m + p}{6}.$$

For $o = 2$, $m = 4$, $p = 12$ days, $E = (2 + 16 + 12)/6 = 5$ days, larger than the most likely value because the pessimistic tail is long.

### Velocity and forecasting

**Velocity** is the number of story points a team completes per sprint, measured over past sprints (only fully finished stories count). The average of recent velocities forecasts the future: with remaining backlog $B$ points and average velocity $v$, expect about $\lceil B / v \rceil$ sprints. This works only for a stable team using consistent sizing; velocity is a planning aid for one team, never a measure for comparing teams or for rewarding people, because it is easily inflated by relabeling points. Show forecasts as ranges (use the best and worst recent sprints) rather than a single date.

### Capacity and sprint planning

**Capacity** is the time available for work in a sprint. A simple calculation multiplies team members by working days by productive hours per day, subtracting planned absences. Not all hours are productive; meetings, support and interruptions take time, so use a focus factor or productive hours (a rule of thumb is 60 to 80 percent of the day, but the right figure varies by team and should come from your own history). For **sprint planning**, the team:

1. agrees a **Sprint Goal**, one sentence on the value of the sprint;
2. pulls stories from the top of the ordered Product Backlog until the total is near the forecast (recent velocity) and within capacity;
3. breaks stories into tasks and checks that the work is understood and meets the **Definition of Done**;
4. leaves a **buffer** for the unexpected (10 to 20 percent is a typical rule of thumb, not a standard), and does not commit to fill every hour.

If the scope is at risk mid-sprint, the team renegotiates scope with the Product Owner; the length of the sprint is not extended and quality is not cut.

@example
title: Forecasting the number of sprints
problem: A team completed 30, 25 and 35 points in its last three sprints. The remaining backlog is 150 points. How many sprints should it expect to need, and what range?
step: Compute the average velocity: `(30 + 25 + 35) / 3 = 30` points per sprint.
step: Divide the backlog by it: `150 / 30 = 5`, so about 5 sprints.
step: Compute a range from the extremes. At the best velocity 35: `150 / 35 = 4.29`, rounded up to 5 sprints. At the worst velocity 25: `150 / 25 = 6` sprints.
step: Report "about 5 sprints, between 5 and 6, if the backlog and the team stay stable" rather than a single date.
answer: About 5 sprints (range 5 to 6).

@example
title: Planning a sprint within capacity
problem: A team of 4 has a 10-working-day sprint. One member is absent for 2 days. Each person has 6 productive hours per day. The team's average velocity is 24 points. The top of the backlog holds stories of 8, 5, 5, 3 and 3 points. Which stories fit?
step: Capacity in person-days: `4 * 10 - 2 = 38`; in hours: `38 * 6 = 228`.
step: The forecast from velocity is 24 points; take stories in backlog order: 8, then 5 (13), then 5 (18), then 3 (21), then 3 (24). Total 24 equals the forecast.
step: With a 10 to 20 percent buffer the team should plan a little under 24, so it drops the last 3-point story to a stretch goal: committed 21 points.
step: Check capacity: 228 hours must cover the tasks of the 21 points; if the task breakdown exceeds it, remove a story.
step: Write a Sprint Goal covering the committed stories, not a list of tickets.
answer: Commit to 21 points (8, 5, 5, 3) with the last 3-point story as a stretch.

@mistakes
- Treating a point estimate as a promise, and giving a single date without a range.
- Using story points as hours, or comparing teams' velocities and rewarding higher numbers.
- Counting partly finished stories toward velocity, which inflates it and misleads the forecast.
- Planning every available hour, leaving no buffer for bugs, meetings and the unexpected.

```python
def sprints_needed(points, velocities):
    if points < 0:
        raise ValueError("points must not be negative")
    if not velocities or sum(velocities) <= 0:
        raise ValueError("need a positive velocity")
    total = sum(velocities)
    # ceil(points / (total / n)) using integer arithmetic only
    return -(-points * len(velocities) // total)
```
hint: Average velocity is `sum(velocities) / len(velocities)`; the number of sprints is points divided by that, rounded up.
hint: `math.ceil` rounds up; alternatively use integer arithmetic with `-(-a // b)`.
solution: Validate, then compute `ceil(points / average)`. With integers, `points * n / total` rounded up is `-(-points * n // total)`, which avoids floating-point error; zero points give zero.
