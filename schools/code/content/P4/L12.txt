@article
Coding rounds decide whether you *can* do the work. Behavioral rounds and the offer conversation decide whether people want to work with you, at what level, and on what terms. Candidates who prepare for these as seriously as for algorithms stand out.

### What behavioral interviews test

Interviewers ask about the past ("Tell me about a time you...") because past behavior predicts future behavior. They are looking for ownership, collaboration, handling conflict and failure, dealing with ambiguity, learning, and communicating clearly. Expect questions like: a disagreement with a teammate, a project that went wrong, a time you led without authority, a tight deadline, feedback you received.

### The STAR structure

Answer in four parts, spending most of the time on Action and Result:

- **Situation.** One or two sentences of context: the project, team, stakes.
- **Task.** Your specific responsibility or goal.
- **Action.** What **you** did, step by step. Say "I", not "we", for your own decisions; credit the team where it is due.
- **Result.** The outcome, measured if possible, plus what you learned or would change.

Aim for about two minutes. A story with no result, or with only "we", leaves the interviewer unable to score you.

### Build a bank of five stories

You cannot memorize a dozen answers, but five well-prepared stories can be adapted to almost any question. Choose stories that cover these themes, and note which questions each could answer:

1. A conflict or disagreement you resolved.
2. A failure or mistake, and what changed afterwards.
3. Taking ownership beyond your assignment, or leading others.
4. Working through ambiguity or changing requirements.
5. A technical achievement you are proud of, with the trade-offs you weighed.

Honesty matters: interviewers probe with follow-ups ("What would you do differently? What did the other person say?"). Real details survive follow-ups; embellishment does not. Never present someone else's work as yours. In your failure story, take real responsibility; blaming others reads as a red flag.

### The one-page résumé

For early-career applicants, one page is the standard. Use a plain single-column layout in a common file format so that both people and automated parsers can read it; avoid tables, text boxes and images for key content. Put education, experience, projects and skills in a sensible order (projects higher if you have little experience).

Each bullet should follow a pattern: **strong action verb + what you did + result with a number**. Compare: "Responsible for the search feature" versus "Reduced median search latency from 800 ms to 200 ms by adding a query cache, improving checkout conversion by 3%." Numbers can be time saved, users, speed, errors, cost or scale; if you cannot measure impact, state the scope ("for a service handling 5,000 requests per second"). Keep bullets under about two lines, and only include what you can discuss in depth, since any line can become an interview question. If you used AI tools in your work, describe what you decided and verified, accurately.

### Offers and negotiation

An offer has several parts: **base salary**, an annual **bonus** target, **equity** (often restricted stock units, which vest over time, commonly over four years, frequently with a one-year **cliff**: nothing vests until 12 months, then the portion earned so far vests at once, a quarter of the grant on a four-year schedule), a **sign-on bonus**, and benefits, leave and remote policy. Compare offers by total compensation per year and by what you value, not base salary alone. Equity in a private company is uncertain, so discount it.

Negotiating is normal and expected. Good practice: be enthusiastic and polite; ask for the details in writing; take a reasonable time to decide; ask what flexibility exists in each component; and **base your request on evidence** such as market data for your level and location and any competing offers. Never lie about other offers. Decide your priorities and your walk-away point in advance. All salary, bonus and equity figures in this lesson are illustrative assumptions, not market data. This is general guidance, not legal or financial advice.

### Talking through it

For behavioral answers, practice aloud and time yourself. Write each story as five bullets, not a script. For the offer conversation, rehearse a sentence such as: "I am excited about the role. Based on my other conversations and the market for this level, I was hoping for a base nearer X. Is there flexibility?"

@example
title: Comparing two offers by first-year value
problem: Using these illustrative figures, Offer A: base 140000, bonus target 10%, equity grant worth 200000 vesting over 4 years, sign-on 20000. Offer B: base 155000, bonus target 5%, equity grant worth 120000 vesting over 4 years, no sign-on. Which is worth more in year one?
step: Offer A: bonus is 10% of 140000, which is 14000; equity per year is 200000 / 4 = 50000.
step: Offer A year-one total: 140000 + 14000 + 50000 + 20000 = 224000.
step: Offer B: bonus is 5% of 155000, which is 7750; equity per year is 120000 / 4 = 30000.
step: Offer B year-one total: 155000 + 7750 + 30000 = 192750.
step: Offer A is worth more in year one by 31250, but the sign-on bonus does not repeat, and equity may carry risk or a cliff. Also compare year-two totals and non-money factors before deciding.
answer: Offer A at 224000 versus Offer B at 192750 in year one; the gap shrinks after the one-time sign-on bonus.

@mistakes
- Telling a story only with "we", so the interviewer cannot tell what you personally did.
- Ending with no result, or an unmeasured result ("it went well"), instead of a concrete outcome and a lesson.
- Résumé bullets that list duties ("Responsible for...") instead of actions and measurable impact.
- Accepting or rejecting an offer on base salary alone, ignoring bonus, equity, vesting and cliff, or inventing competing offers.

```python
assert bullet_issues("Responsible for the search feature") == ["weak_opening", "no_number"]
assert bullet_issues("Cut p95 latency from 800 ms to 200 ms with a cache") == []
assert bullet_issues("  worked on 3 services") == ["weak_opening"]

assert bullet_issues(long_text) == ["too_long"]
```
model:
```python
def bullet_issues(bullet):
    issues = []
    text = bullet.strip().lower()
    weak = ("responsible for", "worked on", "helped with")
    if text.startswith(weak):
        issues.append("weak_opening")
    if not any(ch.isdigit() for ch in bullet):
        issues.append("no_number")
    if len(bullet.split()) > 25:
        issues.append("too_long")
    return issues
```
hint: `str.startswith` accepts a tuple of prefixes, and `str.split()` with no argument splits on any whitespace.
hint: Build the list in the required order: weak opening, then missing number, then length.
solution: Pattern: simple rule checks. Normalize with `strip().lower()` for the opening test, use `any(ch.isdigit() ...)` for the number test and count `split()` words for length. The long-text test has a digit in "40" and no weak opening, so only `"too_long"` appears.

tests:
```python
assert vested_shares(4800, 6) == 0
assert vested_shares(4800, 12) == 1200
assert vested_shares(4800, 24) == 2400
assert vested_shares(4800, 60) == 4800
assert vested_shares(1000, 13) == 270

```
model:
```python
def vested_shares(grant, months, cliff_months=12, total_months=48):
    if months < cliff_months:
        return 0
    return min(grant, grant * months // total_months)
```
hint: Check the cliff first and return `0` if it has not been reached.
hint: Use integer division for whole shares, and cap with `min(grant, ...)` for employees who stay beyond the full schedule.
solution: Pattern: guard clause then formula. The cliff test comes first, so month 6 returns `0` even though the proportional amount would be positive. Integer arithmetic keeps the result exact: `1000 * 13 // 48` is `270`.

```python
def first_year_comp(base, bonus_pct, equity_total, vest_years, sign_on):
```
