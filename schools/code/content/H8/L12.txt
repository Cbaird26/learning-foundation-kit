@article
Thermodynamics tells you whether a reaction can happen; **kinetics** tells you how fast. **Equilibrium** tells you where the reaction ends up.

### Reaction rate

The **rate** of a reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time, for example $\text{M/s}$. Rates depend on:

- **Concentration:** more reactant particles means more collisions.
- **Temperature:** a higher temperature gives particles more energy, so a larger fraction collide with at least the **activation energy** $E_a$. A common rule of thumb is that rate roughly doubles for each $10\,^\circ\text{C}$ rise.
- **Surface area** of solids, and **catalysts**, which provide an alternative path with lower $E_a$ and are not consumed.

### Rate laws

A **rate law** is determined by experiment, not from the balanced equation. For $\text{A}\rightarrow$ products,

$$\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^n,$$

where $k$ is the rate constant and $n$ the **order**. If $n=1$, doubling $[\text{A}]$ doubles the rate; if $n=2$, doubling $[\text{A}]$ quadruples it; if $n=0$, concentration has no effect. To find $n$ from data, compare how the rate changes when $[\text{A}]$ changes by a known factor: $\text{rate ratio}=(\text{concentration ratio})^n$.

For a **first-order** reaction, concentration falls exponentially and the **half-life** is constant:

$$t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln 2}{k}=\frac{0.693}{k}.$$

### Equilibrium

Many reactions are reversible. At **equilibrium**, the forward and reverse rates are equal and concentrations stop changing, although both reactions continue. For $a\text{A}+b\text{B}\rightleftharpoons c\text{C}+d\text{D}$ the **equilibrium constant** is

$$K=\frac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}.$$

Pure solids and pure liquids are left out, and for gases one can use partial pressures ($K_p$). A large $K$ means products are favored; a small $K$ means reactants are favored. $K$ depends only on temperature. Reversing a reaction inverts $K$.

### The reaction quotient

$Q$ has the same form as $K$ but uses the **current** concentrations. If $Q<K$, the reaction proceeds forward (toward products) to reach equilibrium; if $Q>K$, it proceeds in reverse; if $Q=K$, the system is at equilibrium.

### Le Chatelier's principle

When a system at equilibrium is disturbed, it shifts in the direction that partially counteracts the change.

- **Add reactant** (or remove product): shifts toward products. Add product (or remove reactant): shifts toward reactants.
- **Decrease volume** (raise pressure): shifts toward the side with fewer moles of gas. Increase volume: toward more moles of gas. If the moles of gas are equal, there is no shift. Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not shift it.
- **Raise temperature:** shifts in the endothermic direction (treat heat as a reactant). For an exothermic forward reaction, heating shifts toward reactants and **changes $K$** (it decreases). Cooling does the reverse.
- **Catalyst:** speeds both directions equally, so it reaches equilibrium faster but does not change the position or $K$.

Changes in concentration and pressure change the position of equilibrium but not the value of $K$. Only temperature changes $K$.

@example
title: Using Q to find the direction
problem: For $\text{H}_2+\text{I}_2\rightleftharpoons2\,\text{HI}$ at a temperature where $K=50$, a mixture has $[\text{H}_2]=0.10\ \text{M}$, $[\text{I}_2]=0.10\ \text{M}$ and $[\text{HI}]=0.20\ \text{M}$. Which way does the reaction go?
step: Write $Q=\dfrac{[\text{HI}]^2}{[\text{H}_2][\text{I}_2]}$.
step: Substitute: $Q=\dfrac{(0.20)^2}{(0.10)(0.10)}=\dfrac{0.040}{0.010}=4.0$.
step: Since $Q=4.0<K=50$, there are too few products, so the reaction shifts forward (toward HI) until $Q=K$.

@example
title: Predicting a shift
problem: The reaction $\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g)\rightleftharpoons2\,\text{NO}_2(g)$ is endothermic. Predict the shift when the temperature is raised and when the volume is increased.
step: Treat heat as a reactant for an endothermic reaction: $\text{heat}+\text{N}_2\text{O}_4\rightleftharpoons2\,\text{NO}_2$. Raising the temperature shifts it toward products, and $K$ increases.
step: Count moles of gas: $1$ on the left and $2$ on the right.
step: Increasing the volume lowers the pressure, so the system shifts toward more moles of gas, again toward $\text{NO}_2$ (here $K$ is unchanged).

@mistakes
- Writing a rate law directly from the balanced equation's coefficients; reaction order must come from experiment.
- Including pure solids or liquids in $K$, or putting reactants over products.
- Saying a catalyst shifts the equilibrium or changes $K$.
- Saying that any disturbance changes $K$; only a temperature change does.

choice: $K=\dfrac{[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3}{[\text{NH}_3]^2}$

@practice num
prompt: A reaction is second order in A, so $\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^2$. By what factor does the rate change when $[\text{A}]$ is doubled?
answer: 4
check: 2 ** 2
hint: Replace $[\text{A}]$ with $2[\text{A}]$ in the rate law.
hint: $(2)^2$.
solution: $\text{rate}_\text{new}=k(2[\text{A}])^2=4k[\text{A}]^2$, so the rate increases by a factor of $4$.

@practice num
prompt: At equilibrium, $[\text{NH}_3]=0.20\ \text{M}$, $[\text{N}_2]=0.10\ \text{M}$ and $[\text{H}_2]=0.30\ \text{M}$ for $\text{N}_2+3\,\text{H}_2\rightleftharpoons2\,\text{NH}_3$. Calculate $K$ (concentration units), to three significant figures.
answer: 14.8
check: 0.20 ** 2 / (0.10 * 0.30 ** 3)
tol: 1%
hint: Write the expression: products squared over $[\text{N}_2][\text{H}_2]^3$.
hint: The denominator is $(0.10)(0.30)^3=0.0027$.
solution: $K=\dfrac{(0.20)^2}{(0.10)(0.30)^3}=\dfrac{0.040}{0.0027}=14.8$.

@quiz num
prompt: For $2\,\text{NO}_2(g)\rightleftharpoons\text{N}_2\text{O}_4(g)$ at equilibrium, $[\text{NO}_2]=0.020\ \text{M}$ and $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_4]=0.060\ \text{M}$. Calculate $K$.
answer: 150
check: 0.060 / 0.020 ** 2

hint: The coefficient $2$ in front of $\text{NO}_2$ becomes an exponent in the denominator.

solution: $K=\dfrac{0.060}{(0.020)^2}=\dfrac{0.060}{0.00040}=150$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which changes increase the rate of a reaction between a solid and a dissolved reactant?
choice*: Raising the temperature
choice*: Grinding the solid into a fine powder
choice*: Adding a suitable catalyst
choice: Lowering the concentration of the dissolved reactant
