### Week by week

- **Weeks 1 and 2, describing data.** Classify variables (categorical or quantitative). Use bar charts for categories and histograms, dotplots or stemplots for numbers. Describe **shape, center, spread, outliers**. Use the mean and standard deviation for symmetric data and the median and IQR for skewed data. Sample SD divides by $n - 1$; quartiles here use median-of-halves; outlier fences are $Q_1 - 1.5\,\text{IQR}$ and $Q_3 + 1.5\,\text{IQR}$.
- **Week 3, normal model.** $z = (x - \mu)/\sigma$; areas come from $\Phi$; percentiles go backward with $x = \mu + z\sigma$.
- **Week 4, regression.** $b = r\,s_y/s_x$, $a = \bar{y} - b\bar{x}$, residual $= y - \hat{y}$, $r^2$ is the explained fraction. Association is not causation; avoid extrapolation.
- **Weeks 5 and 6, design and probability.** Random sampling allows generalizing; random assignment with a control group allows causal conclusions. Probability rules: complement, addition (subtract the overlap), multiplication, conditional, independence, Bayes for predictive values.
- **Weeks 7 and 8, random variables.** $E(X) = \sum xP(x)$, variances add for independent variables, $\sigma_{a+bX} = |b|\sigma_X$. Binomial: $\binom{n}{k}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$, mean $np$, SD $\sqrt{np(1-p)}$.
- **Weeks 9 and 10, inference for estimation.** Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ or $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$; CLT; interval is statistic $\pm$ critical value $\times$ SE; $t$ for means; round sample sizes up.
- **Weeks 11 and 12, tests.** Hypotheses, test statistic, P-value, decision, conclusion in context; Type I and II errors; chi-square tests use $\sum (O - E)^2/E$ with upper-tail P-values.

### Common errors

Using $n$ instead of $n - 1$ for a sample SD; adding standard deviations rather than variances; confusing $P(A \mid B)$ with $P(B \mid A)$; saying the P-value is the probability that $H_0$ is true; "accepting" $H_0$; reading a confidence interval as covering $95\%$ of individuals; claiming causation from observational data.

@exam num
prompt: Systolic blood pressure in a large adult population is modeled as normal with mean $125$ mm Hg and standard deviation $15$ mm Hg. For a random sample of $25$ adults from this population, what is the probability that the sample mean exceeds $130$ mm Hg? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0478
check: 1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(((130 - 125) / (15 / sqrt(25))) / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: First find the standard error of the sample mean.
hint: $\text{SE} = 15/5 = 3$, so $z = (130 - 125)/3$.
solution: $\text{SE} = 15/\sqrt{25} = 3$ and $z = 5/3 \approx 1.667$. Then $P(\bar{x} > 130) = 1 - \Phi(1.667) \approx 0.0478$.

prompt: In a random sample of $300$ patients on a new drug, $66$ had an adverse event. Find the upper limit of a $95\%$ confidence interval ($z^* = 1.96$) for the proportion of all such patients with an adverse event. Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.267
check: 66 / 300 + 1.96 * sqrt((66 / 300) * (1 - 66 / 300) / 300)
tol: 0.002
hint: $\hat{p} = 66/300 = 0.22$. Compute the standard error and then the margin of error.
hint: $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.22(0.78)/300} \approx 0.0239$.
solution: $\hat{p} = 0.22$, $\text{SE} \approx 0.0239$, margin $= 1.96(0.0239) \approx 0.0469$. The upper limit is $0.22 + 0.0469 \approx 0.267$.

@exam num
prompt: A disease has prevalence $5\%$. A screening test has sensitivity $90\%$ and specificity $95\%$. Three different people who tested positive are chosen independently. What is the probability that at least one of them truly has the disease? Round to three decimal places.
answer: 0.865
check: 1 - (1 - 0.05 * 0.90 / (0.05 * 0.90 + 0.95 * 0.05))**3
tol: 0.003
hint: First find the positive predictive value of the test using Bayes' rule.
hint: $\text{PPV} = 0.045/(0.045 + 0.0475) \approx 0.4865$. "At least one" is the complement of "none".
solution: $\text{PPV} = 0.045/0.0925 \approx 0.4865$. The probability that none of the three has the disease is $(1 - 0.4865)^3 \approx 0.1354$, so the answer is $1 - 0.1354 \approx 0.865$.
