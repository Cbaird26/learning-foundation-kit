@article
Many biological measurements (adult heights, hemoglobin in healthy adults, birth weights) pile up in a symmetric, bell-shaped curve. The **normal distribution** is a model for such data, completely determined by its mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$. It is a model: no real measurement is exactly normal, but the approximation is often excellent.

### The z-score

To compare values from different scales, count how many standard deviations a value lies from the mean:

$$z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}.$$

A positive $z$ is above the mean, a negative $z$ below. A hemoglobin of $13.5$ g/dL in a population with $\mu = 15$ and $\sigma = 1.5$ has $z = (13.5 - 15)/1.5 = -1$: one standard deviation below average. Converting every value in a data set to $z$-scores gives a new set with mean $0$ and standard deviation $1$. A normal variable converted this way has the **standard normal distribution**.

### The 68-95-99.7 rule

For a normal distribution, about $68\%$ of values lie within $1$ standard deviation of the mean, $95\%$ within $2$ and $99.7\%$ within $3$. These are quick estimates; a value more than $2$ or $3$ standard deviations from the mean is unusual.

### Normal probabilities

The proportion of a normal distribution below $z$ is the **cumulative probability** $\Phi(z)$. You can read it from a table, a calculator's normal CDF, or software. Useful values: $\Phi(-1) = 0.1587$, $\Phi(0) = 0.5$, $\Phi(1) = 0.8413$, $\Phi(2) = 0.9772$. The steps are always:

1. Sketch the curve and shade the region.
2. Convert the boundary values to $z$-scores.
3. Use $\Phi$: for "below $z$" use $\Phi(z)$; for "above $z$" use $1 - \Phi(z)$; for "between" subtract, $\Phi(z_2) - \Phi(z_1)$.

### From a percentile to a value

For the **$p$-th percentile** go backward: find the $z$ with $\Phi(z) = p$, then use $x = \mu + z\sigma$. The $90$th percentile has $z \approx 1.2816$, the $95$th has $z \approx 1.645$, the $97.5$th has $z \approx 1.96$ and the $99$th has $z \approx 2.326$.

### Checking whether normal is reasonable

Look at a histogram: it should be roughly symmetric and single-peaked, with no big outliers. Skewed variables such as triglycerides or length of stay are poorly modeled by a normal curve, and normal probabilities for them can mislead.

@example
title: Systolic pressure in a population model
problem: Suppose systolic blood pressure in a certain adult population is modeled as normal with $\mu = 120$ and $\sigma = 15$ mm Hg. Find the $z$-score of $150$ and the proportion of adults below $135$.
step: For $x = 150$: $z = (150 - 120)/15 = 2$. The value is two standard deviations above the mean, which is unusual (only about $2.3\%$ of adults lie above it).
step: For $x = 135$: $z = (135 - 120)/15 = 1$.
step: The proportion below is $\Phi(1) \approx 0.8413$, so about $84\%$ of adults have a systolic pressure below $135$ mm Hg under this model.

@example
title: Finding a cutoff for the top 10%
problem: Using the same model ($\mu = 120$, $\sigma = 15$), what systolic pressure marks the $90$th percentile?
step: The $90$th percentile has $\Phi(z) = 0.90$, so $z \approx 1.2816$.
step: Convert back: $x = \mu + z\sigma = 120 + 1.2816(15) \approx 120 + 19.2$.
step: The cutoff is about $139.2$ mm Hg: roughly $10\%$ of this population exceeds it.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to convert to $z$-scores before using a standard normal table or computing with $\Phi$.
- Using $\Phi(z)$ for "above" a value instead of $1 - \Phi(z)$.
- Applying the normal model to a clearly skewed variable without checking the shape.
- Giving the $z$-score a unit, or forgetting that its sign tells the direction from the mean.

@practice num
prompt: In a population, hemoglobin has mean $15$ g/dL and standard deviation $1.5$ g/dL. Find the $z$-score of a patient whose hemoglobin is $13.5$ g/dL. Note that a z-score has no units.
answer: -1
check: (13.5 - 15) / 1.5
hint: Subtract the mean from the value, then divide by the standard deviation.
solution: $z = (13.5 - 15)/1.5 = -1.5/1.5 = -1$.

@practice num
prompt: Birth weights are modeled as normal with mean $3400$ g and standard deviation $500$ g. What proportion of babies weigh less than $2400$ g? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0228
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf(((2400 - 3400) / 500) / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: Convert $2400$ g to a $z$-score.
hint: $z = -2$. The proportion below is $\Phi(-2)$.
solution: $z = (2400 - 3400)/500 = -2$ and $\Phi(-2) \approx 0.0228$, about $2.3\%$ of babies.

@practice num
prompt: Serum cholesterol in a population is modeled as normal with mean $200$ mg/dL and standard deviation $30$ mg/dL. What proportion of people have cholesterol between $170$ and $260$ mg/dL? Round to four decimal places.
answer: 0.8186
check: 0.5 * (1 + erf(((260 - 200) / 30) / sqrt(2))) - 0.5 * (1 + erf(((170 - 200) / 30) / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: Convert both boundaries to $z$-scores: one is below the mean and one above.
hint: $z$ runs from $-1$ to $2$. Compute $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-1)$.
solution: $z_1 = -1$ and $z_2 = 2$. The proportion between is $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-1) \approx 0.9772 - 0.1587 \approx 0.8186$.

@practice num
prompt: Birth weights are modeled as normal with mean $3400$ g and standard deviation $500$ g. What weight is the $95$th percentile? Round to the nearest gram.
answer: 4222
check: 3400 + 500 * special.ndtri(0.95)
tol: 5
unit: g
hint: Find the $z$ with $\Phi(z) = 0.95$.
hint: $z \approx 1.645$. Use $x = \mu + z\sigma$.
solution: $x = 3400 + 1.645(500) = 3400 + 822.5 \approx 4222$ g.

solution: A $z$-score counts standard deviations from the mean, so $+1.5$ means $1.5$ standard deviations above average. It is not a difference in original units, a ratio or a percent.

@quiz num
prompt: For a normal distribution, what proportion of values lie within $2$ standard deviations of the mean? Give the value to four decimal places (the 68-95-99.7 rule gives only about $0.95$).
answer: 0.9545
check: erf(2 / sqrt(2))
tol: 0.001
hint: The proportion within $\pm 2$ is $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-2)$.
solution: $\Phi(2) - \Phi(-2) \approx 0.9772 - 0.0228 = 0.9545$, consistent with the rule of thumb of about $95\%$.

hint: Subtracting the mean re-centers the data; dividing by the standard deviation rescales it.
solution: Subtracting the mean shifts the center to $0$, and dividing by the standard deviation rescales the spread to $1$. This is why $z$-scores let you compare values from different scales.
