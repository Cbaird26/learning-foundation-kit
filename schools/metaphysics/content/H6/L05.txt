@article
We almost never measure an entire **population**; we study a **sample** and use it to learn about the population. How the sample is chosen decides whether conclusions can be trusted.

### Sampling methods

- **Simple random sample (SRS):** every group of $n$ individuals has the same chance of being chosen, for example by drawing names from a hat or using random numbers. The number of different SRSs of size $n$ from $N$ individuals is $\binom{N}{n}$.
- **Systematic sample:** pick every $k$-th individual from a list after a random start, with $k = N/n$.
- **Stratified sample:** split the population into groups (**strata**) that are alike inside, such as age bands, then take an SRS from each stratum. Proportional allocation samples each stratum in proportion to its size.
- **Cluster sample:** split the population into natural groups (clinics, classrooms), randomly choose whole clusters, and measure everyone in them.
- **Convenience sample:** take whoever is easiest to reach. It is biased in general and cannot be used to generalize.

### Bias

**Bias** is a systematic tendency to miss the truth in one direction; a larger sample does not fix it. Common sources include **voluntary response** (people who choose to respond, such as an online poll, tend to have strong opinions), **undercoverage** (some groups are left out of the list), **nonresponse** (chosen people do not take part) and **response bias** (wording or interviewer effects, or inaccurate self-report).

### Observational studies and experiments

In an **observational study** the researcher records data without imposing a treatment. It can show associations, but because the groups differ in many ways, **confounding** (a lurking variable mixed up with the explanatory variable) prevents a causal conclusion. In an **experiment** the researcher assigns **treatments** to subjects, which is the only way to support cause and effect.

### Principles of a good experiment

1. **Control:** include a comparison group, often receiving a **placebo** (an inactive treatment that looks identical) or the current standard treatment, so that effects of the treatment can be separated from expectation, natural recovery and the passage of time.
2. **Randomization:** assign subjects to groups by chance, which balances known and unknown lurking variables on average.
3. **Blinding:** in a **single-blind** experiment subjects do not know their treatment; in a **double-blind** experiment neither subjects nor those who evaluate them know. This prevents expectations from influencing outcomes.
4. **Replication:** use enough subjects that chance differences between groups are small.

In a **block design**, subjects are first grouped by a variable known to affect the response (for example, disease severity), and randomization happens separately within each block. Blocking is to experiments what stratifying is to sampling. A **matched-pairs** design is a block design with blocks of two, such as two similar patients, or the same patient tested twice.

A related point: a **random sample** supports generalizing to a population, while **random assignment** supports cause and effect. A study can have either, both or neither.

@example
title: Systematic sampling and counting samples
problem: A clinic has $1200$ patient records and wants a systematic sample of $40$. Find $k$, and also find how many different simple random samples of size $3$ exist from a list of $10$ records.
step: For a systematic sample, $k = N/n = 1200/40 = 30$. Pick a random start between $1$ and $30$, then take every $30$th record.
step: The number of SRSs of size $3$ from $10$ is $\binom{10}{3} = \frac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 120$.

@example
title: Spotting the flaw in a study
problem: Patients who chose to join an exercise program had lower blood pressure after a year than patients who did not. Can we conclude the program lowered blood pressure?
step: This is an observational study because patients chose their own group.
step: Patients who volunteer for exercise may be healthier, more motivated or younger to begin with, so those factors are confounded with the program.
step: To test cause and effect, randomly assign volunteers to the program or to a control group and compare outcomes.

@mistakes
- Believing that a very large voluntary response sample is unbiased because it is large.
- Confusing random sampling (choosing who is studied) with random assignment (choosing who gets which treatment).
- Using a placebo comparison but not randomizing, so the groups differ in other ways.
- Calling a study an experiment when the researcher only observed groups that formed on their own.

@practice num
prompt: A systematic sample of $60$ is to be drawn from a list of $1500$ patients. What is the sampling interval $k$?
answer: 25
check: 1500 / 60
hint: Divide the population size by the sample size.
solution: $k = N/n = 1500/60 = 25$, so take every $25$th patient after a random start.

@practice num
prompt: How many different simple random samples of size $4$ can be drawn from a group of $12$ volunteers?
answer: 495
check: comb(12, 4)
hint: Order does not matter, so count combinations.
solution: $\binom{12}{4} = \frac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10 \cdot 9}{4 \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 495$.

@practice mc
prompt: In a drug trial, the control group receives an inactive pill that looks identical to the study drug. What is the main purpose of this placebo?
choice*: To separate the drug's effect from effects of expectation and natural improvement
choice: To guarantee that the drug works
choice: To increase the sample size
choice: To make the study an observational study
hint: What would happen to a patient who believes they are being treated even though they are not?
solution: Patients often improve simply because they expect to or because many conditions improve with time. A placebo group experiences these effects, so any extra improvement in the drug group can be attributed to the drug.

@practice self
prompt: Forty patients with mild hypertension agree to take part in a study of whether a six-week diet program lowers systolic blood pressure. Describe how you would design a randomized comparative experiment. Include the groups, how patients are assigned, what is measured, and one way to reduce bias.
hint: You need a comparison group, chance-based assignment, a measured outcome, and a plan for avoiding biased measurement.
solution: Randomly assign $20$ patients to the diet program and $20$ to a control group that continues usual diet (or receives general health advice). Random assignment is done with random numbers or by drawing names. Measure systolic blood pressure for every patient before and after six weeks, using the same device and procedure. Reduce bias by having the person who measures blood pressure not know which group each patient is in (blinding the evaluator). Compare the average change in the two groups.
rubric: States a control or comparison group.
rubric: Uses random assignment of patients to groups, with a method.
rubric: Names the response variable and how it is measured.
rubric: Describes blinding or another specific way to reduce bias.

@quiz num
prompt: A hospital has $600$ nurses and $400$ physicians. A stratified sample of $50$ staff uses proportional allocation. How many nurses are in the sample?
answer: 30
check: 50 * 600 / (600 + 400)
hint: Nurses are what fraction of all staff?
solution: Nurses are $600/1000 = 0.6$ of the staff, so the sample contains $0.6 \cdot 50 = 30$ nurses (and $20$ physicians).

@quiz mc
prompt: In a double-blind trial, who does not know which treatment each subject receives?
choice*: Both the subjects and the people who evaluate their outcomes
choice: Only the subjects
choice: Only the statistician who plans the study
choice: No one, because assignment is random
hint: Double means two groups of people are kept uninformed.
solution: In a double-blind trial both the subjects and the evaluators are unaware of the assignments, so neither group's expectations can influence the results. Random assignment does not by itself keep people uninformed.
