@article
How fast is something changing *right now*? Average speed is easy: distance divided by time. The derivative makes "right now" precise by shrinking the time interval toward zero.

### Secant lines to tangent lines

Pick a point $(a, f(a))$ on a graph and a nearby point $(a + h, f(a + h))$. The line through them is a **secant line**, and its slope is the **difference quotient**

$$\frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h},$$

the average rate of change of $f$ over the interval from $a$ to $a + h$. As $h$ shrinks, the second point slides toward the first and the secant rotates into the **tangent line**.

### The definition

The **derivative of $f$ at $a$** is the limit of the difference quotient, when it exists:

$$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}.$$

If the limit exists, $f$ is **differentiable** at $a$. The number $f'(a)$ is the slope of the tangent line and the instantaneous rate of change of $f$. Replacing $a$ by a variable $x$ gives the **derivative function** $f'(x)$, also written $\frac{dy}{dx}$ or $\frac{d}{dx} f(x)$.

The tangent line at $x = a$ is

$$y = f(a) + f'(a)(x - a).$$

Units matter: the units of $f'$ are the units of $f$ divided by the units of $x$. If $f$ is position in metres and $x$ is time in seconds, $f'$ is velocity in metres per second.

### A recipe for first principles

1. Write $f(x + h)$ by replacing **every** $x$ with $x + h$.
2. Form $f(x + h) - f(x)$ and simplify. The terms without $h$ cancel.
3. Divide by $h$ and cancel it. You may not set $h = 0$ before this step.
4. Let $h \to 0$.

For $f(x) = x^2$:

$$\frac{(x + h)^2 - x^2}{h} = \frac{2xh + h^2}{h} = 2x + h \;\longrightarrow\; 2x.$$

So the slope of $y = x^2$ at $x = 3$ is $6$.

### When a derivative fails to exist

A graph with a corner, a vertical tangent or a break has no derivative there. For $f(x) = \lvert x \rvert$ at $0$, the secant slopes are $-1$ from the left and $+1$ from the right, so the limit does not exist. Differentiability is stronger than continuity: **a differentiable function is continuous**, but $\lvert x \rvert$ shows that the converse is false.

@example
title: First principles for a quadratic
problem: Use the limit definition to find $f'(x)$ for $f(x) = 3x^2 - 2x$, then write the equation of the tangent line at $x = 1$.
step: Replace every $x$ by $x + h$: $f(x + h) = 3(x + h)^2 - 2(x + h) = 3x^2 + 6xh + 3h^2 - 2x - 2h$.
step: Subtract $f(x)$. The terms $3x^2$ and $-2x$ cancel, leaving $f(x + h) - f(x) = 6xh + 3h^2 - 2h$.
step: Divide by $h$ and cancel: $\dfrac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} = 6x + 3h - 2$.
step: Let $h \to 0$: $f'(x) = 6x - 2$.
step: At $x = 1$ we have $f(1) = 1$ and $f'(1) = 4$. The tangent line is $y = 1 + 4(x - 1) = 4x - 3$.

@example
title: A rational function from the definition
problem: Use the limit definition to show that the derivative of $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x}$ is $-\dfrac{1}{x^2}$ for $x \neq 0$.
step: Write the difference quotient: $\dfrac{1}{h}\left(\dfrac{1}{x + h} - \dfrac{1}{x}\right)$.
step: Combine the fractions over the common denominator $x(x + h)$: $\dfrac{1}{x + h} - \dfrac{1}{x} = \dfrac{x - (x + h)}{x(x + h)} = \dfrac{-h}{x(x + h)}$.
step: Divide by $h$ and cancel it: $\dfrac{1}{h} \cdot \dfrac{-h}{x(x + h)} = \dfrac{-1}{x(x + h)}$.
step: Let $h \to 0$: the limit is $\dfrac{-1}{x \cdot x} = -\dfrac{1}{x^2}$.

@mistakes
- Writing $f(x + h)$ as $f(x) + h$. You must replace every $x$ in the formula with $x + h$.
- Substituting $h = 0$ before simplifying, which always produces $\frac{0}{0}$.
- Dividing only one term of the numerator by $h$ instead of the whole numerator.
- Confusing $f'(a)$, a single number (the slope at one point), with $f'(x)$, a function.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement about $f(x) = \lvert x \rvert$ at $x = 0$ is correct?
choice*: $f$ is continuous at $0$ but not differentiable there, because the secant slopes from the left and right tend to different values.
choice: $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with $f'(0) = 0$, because $0$ is the minimum point.
choice: $f$ is not continuous at $0$, because its graph has a corner.
choice: $f$ is differentiable at $0$ with $f'(0) = 1$, because $\lvert x \rvert = x$ on the right.
hint: Compute the difference quotient $\frac{\lvert h \rvert - 0}{h}$ for small positive and small negative $h$.
solution: For $h > 0$ the quotient is $\frac{h}{h} = 1$; for $h < 0$ it is $\frac{-h}{h} = -1$. The one-sided limits differ, so $f'(0)$ does not exist. But $\lvert x \rvert \to 0 = f(0)$, so $f$ is continuous at $0$.

prompt: Let $f(x) = \dfrac{2}{x + 1}$. Find the $y$-intercept of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at $x = 1$.
answer: 3/2
check: 2/(1 + 1) - nderiv(lambda x: 2/(x + 1), 1)*1
hint: You need the point $(1, f(1))$ and the slope $f'(1)$. Start with $f(1) = 1$.
hint: Use the difference quotient $\frac{1}{h}\left(\frac{2}{2 + h} - 1\right)$ and combine it into one fraction to find $f'(1)$.
hint: Then write the tangent line $y = f(1) + f'(1)(x - 1)$ and set $x = 0$.
solution: $\frac{2}{2 + h} - 1 = \frac{-h}{2 + h}$, so the difference quotient is $\frac{-1}{2 + h} \to -\frac{1}{2}$. The tangent line is $y = 1 - \frac{1}{2}(x - 1)$, and at $x = 0$ we get $y = \frac{3}{2}$.
