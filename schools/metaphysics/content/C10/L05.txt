@article
There are infinitely many prime numbers. This is a theorem, and nobody doubts it. But what is it a theorem about? On the face of it, it is about numbers, and says that there are infinitely many of them with a certain property. Numbers are not in space, since there is no answering the question "Where is the number 7?" They do not seem to be involved in causal processes: the number 7 does not push or pull anything. If numbers exist, they would be **abstract objects**. The literature does not agree on a single definition of "abstract". The usual characterization is negative: abstract objects are not located in space and time and do not enter into causal relations. Standard candidates include numbers, sets and functions; whether propositions and properties belong on the list is disputed.

**Platonism** about mathematics is the view that there are abstract mathematical objects, existing independently of us and of our language. **Nominalism**, in this context, denies that there are any abstract objects.

### Arguments for platonism

**The semantic argument.** Frege held that numbers are objects, since number words behave like names: "The number of planets" is a singular term and "There are four prime numbers between 10 and 20" is an existential claim. If mathematical sentences are true and have the form they seem to have, their singular terms refer and their quantifiers range over numbers. Neo-Fregeans such as Crispin Wright and Bob Hale use Hume's principle, that the number of $F$s equals the number of $G$s just when the $F$s and $G$s can be paired one to one, to defend this.

**The indispensability argument**, associated with Quine and Hilary Putnam:

| P1 | We ought to believe in all and only the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories. |
| P2 | Mathematical objects are indispensable to our best scientific theories. |
| C | So we ought to believe in mathematical objects. |

The argument is valid. Premise P1 rests on naturalism and on **confirmational holism**, the thesis that a theory is confirmed or disconfirmed as a whole, mathematics included. Hartry Field attacked premise P2 in *Science Without Numbers* (1980), where he tried to restate Newtonian gravitation without quantifying over numbers, using spacetime points and their relations. Others, such as Penelope Maddy, have objected to premise P1: scientists seem not to treat all idealized mathematics as literally true.

### The epistemological challenge

Paul Benacerraf's "Mathematical Truth" (1973) puts the problem as a tension between two demands. The first is a **uniform semantics**: the truth conditions of mathematical sentences should resemble those of other sentences, with singular terms referring to objects. The second is a **reasonable epistemology**: an account of mathematical knowledge should fit with an account of how we know anything. Benacerraf worked with a causal theory of knowing, on which one knows a fact only if one is causally connected to it. A platonist semantics satisfies the first demand but seems to fail the second, since we cannot be causally connected to abstract objects. Views that explain mathematical truth by proof or convention satisfy the second demand but give a different semantics for mathematics.

A common reconstruction of the argument against platonism runs as follows:

| P1 | If platonism is true, mathematical objects are abstract, so they have no causal effects. |
| P2 | We can know facts about a kind of thing only if we stand in some causal relation to it. |
| P3 | We do not stand in a causal relation to anything that has no causal effects. |
| C1 | So if platonism is true, we cannot know facts about mathematical objects. |
| P4 | We do know many facts about mathematical objects, such as that there are infinitely many primes. |
| C | So platonism is false. |

The argument is valid. Premise P2 is the weak point, since the causal theory of knowing is widely rejected: we know about the future, and about logical truths, without being causally affected by them. Field restated the challenge in a form that does not need a causal premise (*Realism, Mathematics and Modality*, 1989). The platonist must explain the **reliability** of mathematicians' beliefs: why do the beliefs they form about mathematical objects match the facts about those objects, given that the objects have no influence on them? The challenge is to explain the correlation, not to rule out causal contact by decree.

### Replies

- **Mathematical intuition.** Kurt Gödel suggested that we have something like a perception of mathematical objects, a view that critics find obscure.
- **Holism.** If mathematical claims are confirmed along with science, we have indirect empirical grounds for them, and no causal contact is needed.
- **Full-blooded platonism.** Mark Balaguer holds that every consistent mathematical theory truly describes some objects, so that mathematical knowledge needs only knowledge of consistency.
- **Rejecting platonism.** Fictionalists such as Field deny that mathematical claims are literally true. **Structuralists** hold that mathematics is about structures and not individual objects.

@example
title: Which sets are the numbers?
problem: Benacerraf noted that different set-theoretic constructions can serve as the natural numbers. In the von Neumann construction, $0 = \varnothing$ and each number is the set of all smaller numbers. In the Zermelo construction, $0 = \varnothing$ and the successor of $n$ is $\{n\}$. Is $1 \in 3$ on each construction?
step: Von Neumann: $0 = \varnothing$, $1 = \{0\}$, $2 = \{0, 1\}$, $3 = \{0, 1, 2\}$. The set 3 has 0, 1 and 2 as members, so $1 \in 3$ is true.
step: Zermelo: $0 = \varnothing$, $1 = \{\varnothing\}$, $2 = \{\{\varnothing\}\}$, $3 = \{\{\{\varnothing\}\}\}$. The only member of 3 is $\{\{\varnothing\}\}$, which is 2, so $1 \in 3$ is false.
step: Both constructions satisfy the laws of arithmetic. They disagree about "$1 \in 3$", a question that arithmetic itself does not ask.
step: This is one way of putting the worry: if numbers are objects, which objects are they? Nothing in our arithmetical practice selects one construction. Benacerraf concluded that numbers are not objects at all, which is a motivation for structuralism.
answer: True on the von Neumann construction and false on the Zermelo construction.

@example
title: Locating the weak premise
problem: A student says: "Benacerraf's challenge refutes platonism, because we have no causal contact with numbers." Using the reconstruction in the article, identify the premise that a critic would attack and say what the student has assumed.
step: The student relies on premise P2, that knowledge of a kind of thing requires causal contact with it, together with premises P1 and P3.
step: P2 is the premise most often rejected. Knowledge of the future, of logical truths and of general laws does not seem to depend on causal contact with the facts known.
step: So the student has assumed a causal theory of knowing. A platonist can accept P1 and P3 and reject P2, but must then say what explains the reliability of mathematical belief.
answer: The weak premise is P2; the student assumed that knowledge requires causal contact.

@mistakes
- Saying that Benacerraf proved that platonism is false. His paper states a tension between a uniform semantics and a reasonable epistemology.
- Treating the indispensability argument as an argument that mathematics is useful. It argues from indispensability to science to ontological commitment.
- Saying that Field accepted that mathematical objects exist. He denied that mathematics is indispensable to science and defended nominalism.
- Assuming that "abstract" has a single agreed definition. The usual definition is negative, and borderline cases are disputed.

solution: The argument needs the premise that one can know facts about a kind of thing only if one stands in a causal relation to it. A platonist can reject it and keep abstract objects. Field's version of the challenge asks instead for an explanation of reliability. Mathematicians' beliefs agree with the mathematical facts, but if the objects do not affect the believers, the agreement looks like a coincidence, and the platonist owes an explanation.
