@article
A friend looks at a wooden table and says: "That is just a lot of particles arranged tablewise. There is no extra thing, the table." Another friend replies: "Of course there is a table; I am eating off it." Is this a disagreement about the facts, or only about how to talk? No experiment seems to settle it, because both friends accept every observation. Questions of this kind, about what there is and what it is like at the most general level, are the subject of **metaphysics**.

### What metaphysics studies

Aristotle described a science that studies being as being, and not any particular kind of being as physics or biology do (*Metaphysics* Book Gamma, chapter 1). The name "metaphysics" is traditionally explained by the place his treatise on this subject held in the ordering of his works, after the writings on nature. Today the field has two broad tasks:

- **Ontology** asks what exists and what categories of thing there are: objects, properties, numbers, events, persons.
- **Fundamental questions** ask what the nature of those things is, how they are related, and which of them are more basic than others: what it is for a thing to persist through change, what makes a whole out of its parts, whether properties are real.

Kit Fine and Jonathan Schaffer, among others, have argued that the central question is not simply what exists but what **grounds** what: what is more fundamental, and what depends on it for its existence or nature. Many hold that facts about tables obtain in virtue of facts about arrangements of smaller things, and that the reverse does not hold. Whether this is the best way to understand the subject is itself debated.

### Methods

No single method defines metaphysics. Four are common.

1. **Analysis of cases.** We state a definition or principle and test it against thought experiments, as in the statue and the clay (Lesson 7). The data are judgments about cases, which carry weight but can be revised.
2. **Naturalism.** Following Quine, many hold that our best guide to what exists is our best science, and that metaphysics should be continuous with it rather than an independent source of knowledge. Critics reply that science does not settle questions such as what a property is.
3. **Theory choice.** Metaphysical theories are compared as a whole, by their **theoretical virtues**: simplicity, explanatory power, fit with what we already believe and with the sciences, and economy. David Lewis distinguished **quantitative parsimony**, positing fewer individuals of a kind already accepted, from **qualitative parsimony**, positing fewer fundamental kinds of thing, and argued that a theory may be worth a high price in one if it pays in explanatory power.
4. **Formal tools.** First-order logic, modal logic and mereology (the logic of parts and wholes) make claims precise and reveal what follows from what.

### The charge that it is meaningless

Hume ended his *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* by saying that books containing neither abstract reasoning about quantity or number nor experimental reasoning about matters of fact should be thrown on the fire. In the twentieth century the logical positivists sharpened the charge:

| P1 | A sentence is meaningful only if it is analytic (true by meaning alone) or empirically verifiable. |
| P2 | Metaphysical sentences, such as "Numbers exist" or "Material objects have temporal parts", are neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. |
| C | So metaphysical sentences are meaningless. |

The argument is valid. The standard objection attacks premise P1: P1 is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, so by its own standard it is meaningless. A defender can reply that P1 is a proposal about how to use "meaningful", but then it is a practical choice and no longer a discovery about language. Premise P2 is also doubtful: many metaphysical claims bear on what our best theories say.

Rudolf Carnap offered a subtler challenge in "Empiricism, Semantics and Ontology" (1950). He distinguished **internal questions**, asked within a linguistic framework and answered by its rules or by evidence ("Is there a prime number between 10 and 20?"), from **external questions** about whether the framework as a whole is correct ("Are there numbers?"). He held that an external question is either a practical question about whether to adopt the framework, judged by its usefulness, or a pseudo-question. Quine replied that the line between framework choice and ordinary theory choice is not sharp. Later, Eli Hirsch argued that many ontological disputes are verbal, because the disputants' quantifiers can mean different things (**quantifier variance**), while Theodore Sider holds that some ways of quantifying match the structure of reality better than others and that metaphysics seeks that structure.

You need not settle these meta-questions before beginning. But keep them in mind: for each dispute in this course, ask whether the parties disagree about the world or only about words.

@example
title: Sorting questions
problem: For each question say whether it is a metaphysical question, an empirical scientific question, or a matter of definition, and state what would count as evidence. (a) "How many moons does the planet Lumen have?" (b) "If a ship has every plank replaced over the years, is it the same ship?" (c) "Is a bachelor an unmarried man?"
step: (a) is an empirical question about a particular planet. Telescopic observation decides it, and no philosophical theory of objects is needed.
step: (b) asks what persistence through change requires, a general question about objects that holds for ships, organisms and cities. Observations tell us exactly what happened to the planks, but they do not say which theory of identity over time to use, so this is metaphysical.
step: (c) is settled by the meaning of "bachelor". It is a question of definition, though a philosopher may ask what makes such a truth analytic.
answer: (a) empirical; (b) metaphysical; (c) definitional.

@example
title: Testing the verificationist argument
problem: Reconstruct the argument that metaphysics is meaningless, check its validity, and apply the argument's own principle to its first premise.
step: The conclusion C is that metaphysical sentences are meaningless. The premises: P1, meaningful only if analytic or verifiable; P2, metaphysical sentences are neither.
step: Validity: let $M$ be "is meaningful", $A$ "is analytic or empirically verifiable". P1 says $\forall s\,(Ms \to As)$. P2 says that no metaphysical sentence satisfies $A$. So for any metaphysical sentence $m$, $\neg Am$, and by P1 $\neg Mm$. The argument is valid.
step: Apply P1 to itself. Is P1 analytic? It is not true by the meaning of "meaningful" alone, since many competent speakers reject it. Is it empirically verifiable? No observation confirms or refutes it. So by P1's own standard, P1 is meaningless, and an argument cannot rest on a meaningless premise.
answer: The argument is valid, but premise P1 fails its own test, so its soundness is in doubt.

@mistakes
- Treating "metaphysics" as meaning the occult or the supernatural. In philosophy it is the general study of what exists and its nature.
- Saying that the verificationist argument is invalid. It is valid; the objection is to premise P1, which is self-undermining.
- Thinking that naturalism makes metaphysics impossible. Naturalists such as Quine did metaphysics; they hold that it should answer to our best theories.
- Confusing quantitative with qualitative parsimony: the first counts individuals, the second counts kinds.

@practice self
prompt: Choose a dispute from everyday life that may be only verbal (for example, whether a hot dog is a sandwich). Say in three or four sentences what the parties agree about, what they disagree about, and what would show that the dispute is not merely verbal.
hint: List the facts both sides accept, then ask whether any further fact is left to be right or wrong about.
solution: Take the question whether a hot dog is a sandwich. Both parties agree on everything about the food: what it is made of, how it is served and eaten. They disagree only about whether the word "sandwich" applies. That suggests a verbal dispute, since fixing the meaning of "sandwich" settles it. It would not be merely verbal if the parties had a common, fixed meaning and still disagreed, for example if a regulation defined "sandwich" and they disputed whether the food met it. In metaphysics, someone who says there really is a table over and above the particles must explain which fact the other party is missing.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the claim "Metaphysical disputes are only verbal." Say what would make a dispute verbal, give one dispute from this lesson that might be verbal and one that seems substantive, and say how you would tell them apart.
hint: A verbal dispute disappears once the parties agree on how the key words are used.
solution: A dispute is verbal when the parties agree about all the facts and differ only on what a key term means, so that stipulating the meaning settles it. The dispute about whether there is a table over and above the particles may be verbal if both agree on the particles and on how "table" is used. The question whether a person survives if their body is destroyed and their psychology is transferred seems less clearly verbal, because the parties disagree about what matters in survival and not only about a word. A test is whether the parties could state a shared language in which they still disagree. A defender of substantive metaphysics, such as Sider, says that some candidate meanings of the quantifiers fit reality better. The strongest reply is that this fit is hard to specify without begging the question.
