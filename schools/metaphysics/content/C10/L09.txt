@article
A ship is built of timber. Over the years its planks rot and are replaced one by one, until no original plank remains. It is still in the harbor, still carries passengers, still has the name painted on its bow. Is it the same ship? Most of us say yes: ships, like cats and cities, survive replacement of their parts. But what is it for a thing to persist through time, and through change? This is the question of **persistence**.

### Three theories

Perdurantism and stage theory are normally combined with **eternalism**, the view that past and future things are as real as present ones; endurantism can be combined with eternalism or with presentism. The three theories differ over how a thing is related to the times at which it exists.

**Endurantism.** A thing **endures** if it persists by being **wholly present** at each time at which it exists. The ship at noon and the ship at midnight are one and the same whole thing, not different parts of it. Endurantists, such as David Wiggins and E. J. Lowe, deny that things have temporal parts.

**Perdurantism** (the worm view). A thing **perdures** if it persists by having different **temporal parts** at different times, no one of which is wholly present at more than one time. David Lewis gave this definition of the contrast (*On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986). On this view an ordinary object is a four-dimensional **spacetime worm**, extended in time as it is in space, and the ship's persistence is like a road's extension: the road is not wholly in each place. Quine, Lewis and Theodore Sider defend the view.

**Stage theory.** The things we talk about, such as ships and people, are **stages**: momentary (or brief) things. A stage persists by having **temporal counterparts**, earlier and later stages related to it by the relation of resemblance and causal connection. "The ship was built in 1990" is true of a stage if it has a past counterpart that was built in 1990. Sider and Katherine Hawley defend it.

| | Endurantism | Perdurantism | Stage theory |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temporal parts? | No | Yes | Yes |
| What is the ordinary thing? | A whole present at each time | A spacetime worm | A stage |
| Is the thing wholly present at each time? | Yes | No: only a part | Yes: the stage is momentary |
| How does it persist? | By being the same whole at many times | By having parts at many times | By having counterparts at other times |

Sider's definition of a temporal part: $x$ is an **instantaneous temporal part** of $y$ at $t$ if and only if $x$ is a part of $y$, $x$ exists at $t$ and only at $t$, and $x$ overlaps everything that is a part of $y$ at $t$. A temporal part is thus the whole of $y$ "at an instant", cut off from the rest of its history.

### The ship of Theseus

Plutarch reports that the Athenians preserved Theseus's ship, replacing its timbers, and that philosophers disputed whether it remained the same ship. Hobbes added a twist: suppose someone collects the discarded planks and reassembles them into a ship. Call the original ship $O$, the continuously repaired ship $R$, and the reassembled ship $S$.

| P1 | $R$ is identical to $O$: gradual replacement of parts preserves identity. |
| P2 | $S$ is identical to $O$: a ship is the very same ship if it is made of the same parts in the same arrangement. |
| P3 | $R$ is not identical to $S$: they are in different places, and one can be sunk while the other floats. |

These three cannot all be true, because identity is transitive. If $R = O$ and $S = O$, then $R = S$, contradicting P3. So at least one has to go.

- **Reject P2** (the repaired ship is the original): continuity of form and function matters more than sameness of matter. Many ordinary judgments about ships and organisms agree.
- **Reject P1** (the reassembled ship is the original): on this view sameness of parts is what matters. It seems odd for organisms, which constantly replace their matter.
- **Reject both:** the original ceased to exist when the planks were taken apart, and both $R$ and $S$ are new ships.
- **Accept a perdurantist treatment.** There are two worms, one running through $R$ and the other through $S$. They share their early stages, up to the time of division, and diverge afterwards. Asking "which is the original?" then has no unique answer: the name "the original ship" fits two overlapping worms, in the way that fission cases are treated (Lesson 12).
- **Say that the answer is conventional**: nothing in the world decides it, and our practices do.

@example
title: Showing that the three claims conflict
problem: Show that P1, P2 and P3 in the ship of Theseus cannot all be true, and say what each of three solutions gives up.
step: Suppose P1 and P2. By P1, $R = O$. By P2, $S = O$. Identity is symmetric and transitive, so from $R = O$ and $O = S$ we get $R = S$.
step: But P3 says $R \neq S$. So P1, P2 and P3 are jointly inconsistent: the contradiction is $R = S$ and $R \neq S$.
step: Solutions: giving up P2 keeps $R$ as the original; giving up P1 makes $S$ the original; giving up both says that $O$ is neither. Each is consistent, since only one of $R = O$ and $S = O$ (or neither) is asserted.
step: The perdurantist solution keeps both P1 and P2 by reading "the original ship" as ambiguous between two overlapping worms, and so changes the reading of the claims, not the logic.
answer: P1 and P2 give $R = S$ by transitivity, contradicting P3.

@example
title: Counting temporal parts
problem: An object exists at five consecutive instants $t_1$ to $t_5$. If a temporal part can be any non-empty run of consecutive instants of the object's life, how many different temporal parts does it have?
step: A temporal part is a run of consecutive instants. Count the runs by length.
step: Length 1: 5 runs. Length 2: 4. Length 3: 3. Length 4: 2. Length 5: 1.
step: The total is $5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15$. This equals $\binom{5}{2} + 5 = 15$, the number of ways to choose a start and an end.
answer: 15 temporal parts (counting the whole life as a temporal part of itself).

@mistakes
- Saying that perdurantists deny that things persist. They say a thing persists by having parts at different times.
- Saying that endurantism requires presentism. The two are different theses: an endurantist can accept that past and future things are real.
- Confusing a temporal part with a spatial part. A temporal part of a ship is not its bow; it is the ship at a time.
- Saying that stage theory and perdurantism agree about what a ship is. The worm theorist says the ship is the whole worm; the stage theorist says the ship is a stage.

unit: temporal parts
check: sum(1 for i in range(6) for j in range(i, 6) if i <= 2 <= j)

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how a perdurantist might say that the ship of Theseus case is like a fission case, and say what this implies about the question "which ship is the original?"
hint: Count the worms and ask where they overlap.
solution: The perdurantist can say that there are two ship-worms: one runs through the repaired ship and the other through the reassembled ship. They share all their stages up to the time when the planks are taken apart, and then diverge. So the structure is one of branching, as in fission. The question "which is the original?" presupposes that there is exactly one worm in the early part of the story, but there are two overlapping ones, so the name "the original ship" is ambiguous or indeterminate in reference. If we want an answer, we can stipulate which one we mean, or we can follow our ordinary practices. This implies that there is no further fact of the matter beyond the description of the two worms. A critic will say that this multiplies the ships in the early part of the story, so that there were two ships in the harbor all along.
