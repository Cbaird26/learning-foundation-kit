@article
Two scarlet balls sit on a table. They are two things, with two locations, two weights, two histories. Yet they are alike: each is scarlet. What is it for them to be alike in this way? One answer says that there is one thing, scarlet, that both of them have. If so, scarlet is a strange sort of thing: it is in two places at once, and it is not a ball. This is the **problem of universals**, which asks what, if anything, makes it the case that many different things are the same in nature. The older name for the underlying phenomenon is the **one over many**: one character, many things that have it.

### Terms

A **particular** is a single, unrepeatable thing: this ball, this electron. A **universal** is a repeatable entity: one thing that can be wholly present in many different particulars, which then **instantiate** it. **Realism** about universals says that universals exist. The opposing views, which hold that there are only particulars, are **nominalism** in its various forms (Lesson 4).

Properties are often divided into **abundant** and **sparse**. On the abundant conception there is a property for every way a thing might be described or grouped, however gerrymandered, such as being a ball or a planet. On the sparse conception, which David Lewis articulated, only a few properties are fundamental: those that make for objective resemblance and figure in causal and scientific explanation. Most realists about universals take universals to be sparse.

### The argument for realism

| P1 | Some distinct particulars are alike in a respect: these two balls are both scarlet. |
| P2 | If distinct particulars are alike in a respect, there is something that they share and that explains the likeness. |
| P3 | Anything shared by distinct particulars is repeatable, so it is not a particular. |
| C | So there are repeatable entities, that is, universals. |

The argument is valid. Nominalists attack premise P2: they can accept that things are alike without accepting that the likeness needs any entity to explain it, or they can offer an account in terms of classes, predicates, resemblance or tropes. Premise P1 is hard to deny, though nominalists differ about what it amounts to.

### Platonic and Aristotelian realism

Plato held that **Forms** exist apart from the sensible things that resemble them, outside space and time; the Form of the Beautiful, for instance, is what beautiful things share (see the *Phaedo* and *Republic* V to VII). On this **Platonic realism**, universals exist whether or not anything instantiates them, and they exist in a realm that is not the world of sensible things. Aristotle rejected separate Forms: a universal exists only in the particular things that have it (see *Metaphysics* Book Zeta and the *Categories*). On this **immanent realism**, which David Armstrong defended in *Universals and Scientific Realism* (1978), there is a **principle of instantiation**: there are no uninstantiated universals. Armstrong held that the right universals are discovered by science, and he proposed that laws of nature are relations between universals.

| Question | Platonic realism | Immanent realism |
|---|---|---|
| Do universals exist? | Yes | Yes |
| Where are they? | Outside space and time | Wholly present in each instance |
| Are there uninstantiated universals? | Yes, possibly | No |
| Main cost | How do we know about, or how do abstract Forms relate to, sensible things? | A single thing is in many places at once (multiple location) |

### The third man

Plato himself raised the best-known objection to Forms in the *Parmenides* (132a to 132b), and Aristotle later named it the "third man". A common modern reconstruction, due to Gregory Vlastos, uses three assumptions:

| P1 | One over many (OM): if a number of things are all $F$, there is a Form of $F$-ness in virtue of which each of them is $F$. |
| P2 | Self-predication (SP): the Form of $F$-ness is itself $F$. |
| P3 | Non-identity (NI): nothing is $F$ in virtue of itself. |
| C1 | By OM, the large things $a$, $b$ and $c$ have a Form, Largeness$_1$; by SP, Largeness$_1$ is large. |
| C2 | So $a$, $b$, $c$ and Largeness$_1$ are all large, so by OM there is a Form Largeness$_2$ in virtue of which they are all large; by NI, Largeness$_2$ is different from Largeness$_1$. |
| C | The reasoning repeats without end, so there are infinitely many Forms of Largeness, where we expected one. |

The regress is taken to be vicious because it multiplies Forms without explaining anything new. The three assumptions are inconsistent with the claim that there is only one Form for each kind, and each has been rejected: some say that Forms are not themselves $F$ in the way their instances are (denying SP), others that a Form can be $F$ in virtue of itself (denying NI), and others restrict OM. A related regress, modeled on F. H. Bradley's regress of relations, concerns instantiation itself: if $a$ instantiates scarlet, is instantiation another relation that $a$ and scarlet stand in, requiring a further relation, and so on? Armstrong replied that instantiation is not a relation in the problematic sense, and that the basic unit is a state of affairs.

@example
title: Reading the third man
problem: Suppose three things $a$, $b$ and $c$ are all large. Walk through the third man argument to show how the second Form arises, and say which premise a philosopher denies who says that the Form of Largeness is not itself large.
step: By OM, because $a$, $b$ and $c$ are large, there is a Form Largeness$_1$ in virtue of which they are large.
step: By SP, Largeness$_1$ is itself large. So there are now four large things: $a$, $b$, $c$ and Largeness$_1$.
step: Apply OM to these four. There is a Form in virtue of which all four are large. By NI, it cannot be Largeness$_1$, since nothing is large in virtue of itself. So it is a new Form, Largeness$_2$. The argument can be repeated for Largeness$_2$.
step: A philosopher who says that the Form of Largeness is not itself large denies self-predication, premise SP. Without SP the first step does not add Largeness$_1$ to the large things, and the regress does not start.
answer: Largeness$_2$ is generated from the four large things $a$, $b$, $c$ and Largeness$_1$; the philosopher denies SP.

@example
title: Platonist or immanent realist?
problem: Consider the claim: "There is a shade of blue that has never been instantiated by anything, and it exists." Which kind of realist accepts the claim, and which rejects it, and why?
step: The Platonist holds that universals exist independently of their instances, so an uninstantiated shade can exist.
step: The immanent realist accepts the principle of instantiation: a universal exists only if something instantiates it. Since nothing is that shade, there is no such universal.
step: Note that the immanent realist does not deny that the shade is possible or that we can think of it. The claim is that nothing in the world corresponds to it.
answer: The Platonist accepts it; the immanent realist rejects it, by the principle of instantiation.

@mistakes
- Saying that realism about universals is the view that "red" is a word that refers. Realists hold that the things two red objects share is a real entity, not just that the word is general.
- Confusing a universal with a class. A class is individuated by its members, so coextensive classes are identical; a universal is what the members are supposed to share, and on most views distinct universals can have the same instances.
- Attributing the third man to Aristotle as his own view. Plato raised the problem; Aristotle gave it its name when discussing Plato's Forms.
- Thinking that immanent realists deny that universals are real. They accept universals but locate them in the things that have them.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain how the argument from the one over many leads to universals, and state one way that a nominalist might resist it.
hint: Say which premise says that likeness needs something shared, and how a nominalist could deny it.
solution: Two particulars, such as two scarlet balls, are alike in being scarlet. The realist argues that if distinct things are alike in a respect, there must be something they share that explains the likeness. What distinct particulars share cannot be a particular, since a particular is unrepeatable, so it is a repeatable entity, a universal. A nominalist can resist the second premise. For example, the resemblance nominalist says that the balls are scarlet because each resembles paradigm scarlet things, and that no shared entity is needed. The realist may reply that resemblance is itself a repeatable relation, so a universal has returned.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why the claim that a universal is "wholly present in each of its instances" is puzzling, and give one response that an immanent realist might offer.
hint: Think of what it would be for one entity to be in two places at once, and of how a particular differs from a universal.
solution: The immanent realist says that the universal scarlet is wholly present in each of two balls in different places. But ordinary objects are in one place at a time, and if a single thing is entirely present here and entirely present there, then it is in two places at once, which seems to violate a principle of location. One response: universals are not ordinary objects, and the principle applies only to particulars; the universal is multiply located by its nature, as a repeatable. Another response, which Armstrong favored, is to treat the basic unit as a state of affairs in which the particular and the universal are both constituents. The cost is that we must accept a new kind of entity with a new mode of location.
