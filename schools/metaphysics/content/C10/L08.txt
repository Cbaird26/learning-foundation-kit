@article
A brick is made of smaller parts, and a wall is made of bricks. But is there anything made of the front half of a trout and the back half of a turkey? Is there an object made of your left shoe and the Eiffel Tower? Common sense says that bricks compose walls, and that nothing is composed of the trout and the turkey, but it is hard to say what the difference is. This is the **special composition question**, posed by Peter van Inwagen in *Material Beings* (1990).

### Composition

Write "$x$ is a **part** of $y$" for parthood. Two things **overlap** if they have a part in common. Some things, the $xs$, **compose** $y$ if and only if

1. each of the $xs$ is a part of $y$,
2. no two of the $xs$ overlap, and
3. every part of $y$ overlaps at least one of the $xs$.

The special composition question asks: **under what conditions do some things compose something?** The general composition question asks what composition is, and the special question asks for the conditions of its occurring. An answer states a condition on the $xs$, for example "the $xs$ are in contact". Here are the main answers.

| Answer | Claim | Main cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Nihilism** | The $xs$ never compose anything (except when there is one of them) | Denies tables, planets and organisms, so is highly revisionary |
| **Universalism** | Any things whatever compose something | Admits trout-turkeys and many objects that seem arbitrary |
| **Contact** | The $xs$ compose something iff they are in contact | Objects with spaced parts, such as a spacecraft's fragments, and two touching people |
| **Fastening** | The $xs$ compose something iff they are fastened together | Hard to state without vagueness, and many wholes are not fastened |
| **Organicism** | The $xs$ compose something iff their activity constitutes a life (van Inwagen) | Denies tables and statues; what counts as a life is vague |
| **Brutal composition** | There is a fact about when the $xs$ compose, but it has no informative statement (Ned Markosian) | Arbitrary-looking and unexplained |

Universalism is defended by David Lewis (*Parts of Classes*, 1991) and by Theodore Sider (*Four-Dimensionalism*, 2001). Nihilism is defended in different forms by Peter Unger, who argued that no ordinary objects exist ("Why There Are No People", 1979), and by Cian Dorr and Gideon Rosen ("Composition as a Fiction", 2002), who argue that composition never occurs but that talk of composite objects is useful. Ned Markosian defended brutal composition in "Brutal Composition" (1998). On universalism, the sum of any things exists; if we add that things with the same parts are identical (**uniqueness of composition**), we have classical extensional mereology.

### The vagueness argument

Lewis and Sider argue that composition is either universal or never occurs:

| P1 | If composition is neither universal nor nihilist, then there is a series of cases, each differing from the next in tiny ways, that runs from cases in which the $xs$ compose something to cases in which they do not, and in some case it is vague whether they compose something. |
| P2 | If it is vague whether the $xs$ compose something, then it is vague whether there exists something composed of the $xs$, so existence is vague. |
| P3 | It cannot be vague whether something exists. |
| C | So composition is either universal or nihilist. |

The argument is valid: by P1 and P2, a restricted answer implies that existence is vague, and P3 denies this. Critics reject each premise. Against P1, a restricted view may place a **sharp cutoff** (brutal composition says that there is one, though it cannot be stated). Against P2, some say that vagueness about composition is vagueness in the *words* we use to describe the case, not in what exists. Against P3, van Inwagen accepts that existence can be vague: whether some particles compose an organism depends on whether their activity constitutes a life, and "life" is vague. Sider argues for P3 by claiming that the quantifier is not vague; this is controversial, because we can ask why the existential quantifier should be the one precise expression.

The argument supports universalism or nihilism disjunctively, not either one. A further consideration is needed to choose, and each side appeals to the costs in the table: the universalist accepts many strange objects but keeps ordinary ones, the nihilist rejects the strange and the ordinary alike.

@example
title: Counting objects under universalism
problem: A world has exactly five simples (things without proper parts). Under universalism, how many objects are there in all, counting the simples and every thing composed of two or more of them? How many are composite?
step: By universalism, every non-empty collection of simples composes something, and by uniqueness of composition different collections compose different things.
step: A set of 5 simples has $2^5 = 32$ subsets, of which one is empty, so there are $32 - 1 = 31$ objects in all.
step: Five of these are the simples themselves, so there are $31 - 5 = 26$ composite objects.
step: By contrast, nihilism allows only the 5 simples, and a restricted view allows only those collections that meet its condition.
answer: 31 objects in all; 26 are composite.

@example
title: Evaluating three answers
problem: Compare nihilism, universalism and the contact answer on the question whether a pair of gloves, one on each of two tables in different rooms, composes anything.
step: Nihilism: the gloves compose nothing, and neither does anything else, so the answer is no for the same reason as for a single glove's parts.
step: Universalism: yes, any two things compose something, so the pair is a sum, though it may not be an object we have a use for.
step: Contact: no, since the gloves are not touching. But the contact view must also say whether a glove, with its scattered threads, is one object, and what to say about a pair that touches briefly.
step: The three answers differ in their costs: nihilism and contact are revisionary about ordinary things, and universalism is generous about the things it admits.
answer: Nihilism says no; universalism says yes; contact says no.

@mistakes
- Treating the special composition question as asking what parts are. It asks when some things compose a further thing.
- Saying that universalism implies that all these objects are equally interesting. It claims only that they exist.
- Confusing nihilism with eliminating matter. Nihilists accept simples; they deny composite objects.
- Saying that the vagueness argument shows that universalism is true. It shows that composition is universal or never occurs, given its premises.

@quiz num
prompt: A world has 4 simples, $A$, $B$, $C$ and $D$, and under universalism every non-empty collection of them composes exactly one object. How many of these objects have the simple $A$ as a part, counting $A$ itself?
answer: 8
unit: objects
check: sum(1 for r in product([0, 1], repeat=4) if r[0] == 1)
hint: An object has $A$ as a part just when its collection of simples includes $A$. Decide for each of the other three simples whether it is in the collection.
solution: Each collection that includes $A$ is fixed by choosing which of $B$, $C$ and $D$ to add, so there are $2^3 = 8$ such collections, and each composes exactly one object. They include $A$ itself, which is the collection with nothing added.

@quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct the vagueness argument for universalism or nihilism in standard form, and say which premise you would reject if you wanted to defend a restricted answer, with your reasons.
hint: The argument moves from vague composition to vague existence.
solution: The argument is: P1, if composition is neither universal nor nihilist, there is a series of cases with a borderline case; P2, if it is vague whether the xs compose something, then existence is vague; P3, existence cannot be vague; so composition is universal or nihilist. To defend a restricted answer, one can reject P3 as van Inwagen does: the answer "life" yields borderline cases of composition, and vague existence is then just a consequence of a vague criterion. The objection is that existence is expressed by the quantifier, and it is hard to see how a quantifier can be vague. A reply is that vague criteria of persistence for ordinary things are already accepted, and quantification over ordinary things inherits their vagueness. Alternatively one can reject P1 by adopting a sharp cutoff, with the cost of arbitrariness.
