@article
Suppose that each hemisphere of your brain is enough to carry your memories, character and plans. A surgeon divides your brain and places each half in a different body, whose own brain has been removed. Two people wake up, each with your psychology. Call them Left and Right. Which of them is you? This is a **fission** case, and a version of it is described in Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984).

### The puzzle

We know what we would say if only one hemisphere were transplanted and the other destroyed: the recipient would be you. Fission differs only in that the other half is saved too.

| P1 | If only your left hemisphere were transplanted and the right destroyed, the recipient would be you. The same holds for the right hemisphere alone. |
| P2 | Whether a later person is you depends only on facts about you and that person, and not on whether another person also exists. |
| P3 | So in fission, Left is you and Right is you. |
| P4 | Left and Right are two distinct people: they are in different places and can have different experiences. |
| P5 | Identity is transitive and symmetric. |

These cannot all be true: P3, P5 and P4 give that you are Left and you are Right, so Left is Right, contradicting P4. Each claim has a defender who gives it up. P2 is sometimes called the "only $x$ and $y$" principle.

### Responses

1. **You are one of them.** You are Left, say, and Right is a new person. The reply seems arbitrary, since nothing distinguishes the two relations. It rejects P3 by rejecting the symmetric treatment of P1.
2. **You do not survive.** You cease to exist at the division, and Left and Right are new people. This denies P2, since it makes the existence of Right bear on whether Left is you. Robert Nozick's **closest continuer** theory is of this kind: a later person is you if that person is the closest continuer of you, and if there is a tie, there is no identity.
3. **Multiple occupancy** (David Lewis, "Survival and Identity", 1976). Before the division there were two people, who shared a single stage in the way that two roads can share a stretch of highway. Left and Right are each such a person, and each survives. This denies the assumption that there was one person before the division; when we say "you" before the division we ordinarily count by sharing a stage at a time, so we seem to count one. Lewis keeps P1 to P5, with the first-person pronoun ambiguous between two people before the fission.
4. **Stage theory** (Sider, Hawley). The stage that is you has two future counterparts, Left and Right. "You will be Left" and "You will be Right" are both true, because "will be" is analyzed in terms of counterparts. There is no violation of transitivity, since identity relates stages, and the stage that is you is not identical to either future stage. The cost is that "you" refers to a stage, which seems strange to the ordinary view of what we are.
5. **Parfit: identity is not what matters.** Parfit argued that the fission case shows that identity is not what matters in survival. You cannot be identical to both Left and Right, and nothing favors one of them, so he held that you are identical to neither. What matters in survival is not identity but **relation R**, psychological connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause. In fission R holds between you and each of Left and Right, so you have what matters in survival, in two ways, though you are not identical to either. On this view fission is about as good as ordinary survival, and no worse than if one of the hemispheres had been destroyed. Parfit's view is part of **reductionism**, the claim that a person's continued existence consists in physical and psychological facts, with no further fact.

### Evaluating the replies

Parfit's claim has critics. Some say that identity does matter, as when I anticipate future pain: I fear my own pain and not merely pain connected with me by relation R. Parfit replies that branching shows that R can have all the importance we thought identity had. A second criticism is that relation R, restricted to one-one relations, is what matters, so that fission with its branching is a loss. A third is that the multiple occupancy and stage views change the subject: they redescribe our counting but do not answer what the case is like for the person undergoing it.

@example
title: Showing the inconsistency
problem: Show that P3, P4 and P5 conflict, and say what Lewis and Nozick each do about it.
step: By P3, you are Left and you are Right. By symmetry, Left is you; by transitivity, Left is Right.
step: P4 says that Left and Right are two distinct people, so Left is not Right. This is the contradiction.
step: Nozick: a tie for closest continuer means that neither is you, so P3 is false. P2 is denied, since the existence of Right affects whether Left is you.
step: Lewis: there were two people before the division, so "you" does not name one person. Each of the two is Left or Right in later life, and P3 is true of each separately, without contradiction.
answer: P3, P4 and P5 jointly entail that Left is Right and is not Right; Nozick denies P3, and Lewis denies that there was one person before the division.

@example
title: What matters
problem: Parfit holds that, in fission, you would not be identical to either Left or Right, yet it is as good as ordinary survival. What does he mean, and what is the objection?
step: On his view relation R, psychological continuity and connectedness with the right kind of cause, holds between you and each of Left and Right.
step: Because R is what matters, you have in fission everything that matters in ordinary survival, even though identity fails, since you cannot be identical to both.
step: Objection: when I anticipate my future, what I care about seems to be my own survival, which is identity, and a relation that holds to two people at once does not seem to be that.
step: Parfit's reply is that the cases show that identity cannot be what matters, since identity in fission cannot be one to one while what matters does not change.
answer: Relation R matters, not identity; the objection is that survival seems to require identity.

@mistakes
- Saying that Parfit held that you die in fission. He held that you are identical to neither, but that what matters in survival is preserved.
- Confusing Lewis's view with the view that two people exist after fission only. Lewis holds that there were two people before the fission as well.
- Saying that the fission puzzle arises only for the psychological view. Any view that gives you survival in single-hemisphere cases faces a version of it.
- Thinking that the puzzle shows that fission is impossible. It is a thought experiment, and the question is what to say if it happened.

hint: Use Parfit's relation R, the objection from identity, and your own judgment.
solution: Plan and model. Case: your brain is divided and each half is placed in a body, producing Left and Right, each psychologically continuous with you. Claim: fission can be nearly as good as ordinary survival. Argument: in single-hemisphere cases we say the recipient is you, and what makes that case good is relation R: continuity of memory, character and intention with the right cause. Fission preserves R to each recipient, and the existence of a second recipient adds nothing that removes the relation that held in the single case. Hence, if survival in the single case matters, fission gives what matters twice over. Objection: what I care about in anticipating my future is that I will exist, which is identity, and in fission I will not be identical to either. Reply: the objection assumes identity is what matters, but the single-case survival we valued was valued for R, since a case with identity and no R would give us nothing we care about, and a case with R and no identity, as fission, gives most of what we care about. A critic may say that there is a loss in having to share one's life with a duplicate, which suggests that something more than R matters. I would concede that fission changes how we relate to others, such as family, while holding that it does not make the anticipation of the future worthless.
