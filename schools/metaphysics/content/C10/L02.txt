@article
Suppose I say: "Pegasus does not exist." This seems true. But to say that something does not exist, I must be talking about something. And if I am talking about Pegasus, then there is something I am talking about, and so Pegasus has some sort of being. Plato's puzzle of non-being, in the Sophist, has this shape, and Quine revived it in his paper "On What There Is" (1948), where he gave it the name "Plato's beard", since it keeps growing back however often it is shaved.

### The puzzle in standard form

The puzzle is a set of three claims that cannot all be true:

| P1 | "Pegasus does not exist" is true. |
| P2 | If a sentence containing a name is true, the name refers to something. |
| P3 | If "Pegasus" refers to something, then Pegasus exists. |

Together P2 and P3 give that if P1 holds, Pegasus exists, which contradicts what P1 says. Each of the three has been rejected by someone.

- **Russell** rejects premise P2. Following his theory of descriptions (1905), a name like "Pegasus" is a disguised description, and "the winged horse caught by Bellerophon does not exist" is not about any winged horse. Russell analyzed "The $F$ is $G$" as $\exists x\,(Fx \wedge \forall y\,(Fy \to y = x) \wedge Gx)$. Saying that the $F$ does not exist is then simply denying that anything is uniquely $F$, that is, negating $\exists x\,(Fx \wedge \forall y\,(Fy \to y = x))$, and no winged horse is needed to make that intelligible.
- **Quine** adopted Russell's move and went further: any name can be turned into a predicate, so "Pegasus" becomes "pegasizes" (is a Pegasus). Then "Pegasus does not exist" is regimented as $\neg\exists x\,Px$, with $Px$ for "$x$ pegasizes". The sentence is true, and it quantifies over nothing winged.
- **Meinongians** reject premise P3. Alexius Meinong held that there are objects that do not exist; Terence Parsons and Graham Priest have defended versions of this view. "There is" is broader than "exists", and Pegasus is something that has properties but does not exist.

### Quine's criterion

Quine's answer generalizes into a criterion of **ontological commitment**. His slogan is that to be is to be the value of a bound variable. Spelled out:

- A **theory** (a set of sentences we accept) is committed to those entities that must be among the values of its bound variables for the theory to be true.
- To read off the commitments we first **regiment** the theory, that is, restate it in first-order logic with names eliminated in favor of predicates and descriptions.
- Then we look at the existential quantifiers: $\exists x\,(Dx \wedge Bx)$, "Some dogs are brown", commits us to dogs.

Several points are often missed.

1. The criterion tells us what a **theory** is committed to; it does not tell us what exists. Whether to accept the theory is a further question.
2. **Universal sentences** alone carry no existential commitment. "Every sphere in the box is red", $\forall x\,((Sx \wedge Ix) \to Rx)$, is true when the box has no spheres, so it does not commit us to spheres.
3. Commitment depends on the **regimentation**. "Ana and Ben have a trait in common" regiments, with a first-order quantifier over traits, as $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge Hax \wedge Hbx)$, which commits to traits. Quine resisted this and urged that we look for a paraphrase that avoids them, if one can be found.
4. **Paraphrase** is therefore a method of avoiding commitment. If a sentence apparently about averages, holes or numbers can be restated without quantifying over them, the theory need not be committed. Whether the paraphrase succeeds is a matter of dispute.

### Is existence a property?

Kant argued that being is not a "real predicate": saying that a hundred thalers exist adds nothing to the concept of a hundred thalers (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598/B626). Frege and Russell turned this into the view that existence is not a first-order property of objects but is expressed by the quantifier: "Tigers exist" says that the concept *tiger* has an instance, $\exists x\,Tx$. Friends of nonexistent objects reject this, and **free logics** weaken the assumption that every name refers. On the Quinean view, "exists" is not a predicate of individuals, which is why $\neg\exists x\,Px$ involves no object that fails to exist.

@example
title: Regimenting five sentences
problem: For each sentence give a regimentation in first-order logic and say what it is committed to. (a) "Some dogs are brown." (b) "There is a prime number between 10 and 20." (c) "Vulcan does not exist." (d) "Ana and Ben share a trait." (e) "Every sphere in the box is red."
step: (a) $\exists x\,(Dx \wedge Bx)$, with $Dx$ for "$x$ is a dog" and $Bx$ for "$x$ is brown". Committed to dogs.
step: (b) $\exists x\,(Nx \wedge Px \wedge 10 < x \wedge x < 20)$. For this to be true something that is a number must be in the range of the variable, so it is committed to numbers unless a paraphrase is found.
step: (c) Turn the name into a predicate: $\neg\exists x\,Vx$, with $Vx$ for "$x$ vulcanizes". True if nothing vulcanizes, and committed to nothing that is Vulcan.
step: (d) $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge Hax \wedge Hbx)$, with $Tx$ for "$x$ is a trait" and $Hxy$ for "$x$ has $y$". Committed to traits, which is the point of dispute for nominalists.
step: (e) $\forall x\,((Sx \wedge Ix) \to Rx)$, with $Ix$ for "$x$ is in the box". This is a universal sentence; it is true when there are no spheres in the box, so it is committed to nothing in particular.
answer: (a) dogs; (b) numbers; (c) nothing; (d) traits; (e) nothing.

@example
title: Diagnosing the Pegasus puzzle
problem: Show that the three claims of the puzzle are jointly inconsistent, and say what Russell does.
step: Suppose P1 is true. By P2, "Pegasus" refers to something. By P3, Pegasus exists. But P1 says that Pegasus does not exist, so P1 is false. Hence P1 cannot be true if P2 and P3 are, so the three are inconsistent.
step: The set is inconsistent, so at least one claim must go, and each choice gives a different view: reject P2 (Russell and Quine), reject P3 (Meinongians), or reject P1 (say that "Pegasus does not exist" is false or truth-valueless).
step: Russell's analysis converts "Pegasus does not exist" into the denial that anything uniquely has the property that "Pegasus" abbreviates. The sentence is true, but it contains no genuine name, so P2 is not triggered.
answer: P1, P2 and P3 are inconsistent; Russell rejects P2 by treating the name as a disguised description.

@mistakes
- Reading Quine's criterion as a test for what exists. It tells us what a theory is committed to; whether to accept the theory is a separate matter.
- Thinking that every sentence mentioning a kind of thing commits to it. A universal sentence is vacuously true if there are none, and a paraphrase may remove an apparent commitment.
- Saying that the Meinongian rejects the claim that Pegasus is a myth. The Meinongian says that Pegasus does not exist but that there is such a thing, and so rejects premise P3.
- Treating "exists" as an ordinary predicate like "is brown". On the Fregean and Quinean view it is expressed by the quantifier.
