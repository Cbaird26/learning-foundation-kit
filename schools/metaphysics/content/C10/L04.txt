@article
Return to the two scarlet balls. A **nominalist** about universals holds that everything that exists is particular, so that there is nothing which is wholly present in both balls. But the nominalist cannot just deny that the balls are alike. The task is to say what their likeness comes to without a shared universal. There are several ways to try.

### Four nominalist strategies

**Predicate nominalism.** The balls are scarlet just because the predicate "scarlet" applies to each. The objection: this seems to explain the wrong way round. We apply the same predicate to the balls because they are alike, not the reverse. And if a language lacked the predicate, the balls would still be alike.

**Class nominalism.** Properties are classes: being scarlet is being a member of the class of scarlet things. This works for a given class, but it faces the **problem of coextensive properties**. If every creature with a heart is a creature with kidneys, and conversely, the class of cordates and the class of renates are the same class, so the view says that the two properties are the same. Yet they differ. A standard repair uses classes of possible things, but that adds possible individuals to the ontology.

**Resemblance nominalism.** A thing is scarlet by resembling certain paradigm scarlet things closely enough. H. H. Price developed the view, and Gonzalo Rodriguez-Pereyra defended it at length. Nelson Goodman raised two difficulties. In the **companionship** difficulty, a set of things might share a property only because they all happen to share another one as well, so that resembling the paradigms does not single out the right property. In the **imperfect community** difficulty, a group of things may each resemble many of the others while no single feature is common to the whole group.

**Trope theory.** Here properties are real but particular. A **trope** is an individual instance of a property, such as the scarlet of this ball, which is a different trope from the scarlet of that ball. Trope theory was developed by Donald C. Williams in "On the Elements of Being" (1953), who called tropes "abstract particulars", and by Keith Campbell. The balls are alike because their two tropes **exactly resemble** each other. Trope theory rejects universals, but it does not reject properties.

### Russell's regress

Bertrand Russell argued in *The Problems of Philosophy* (chapter 9) that a nominalist who appeals to resemblance still needs a universal:

| P1 | The resemblance nominalist explains the likeness of two scarlet things $a$ and $b$ by the fact that $a$ resembles $b$. |
| P2 | The resemblance of $a$ to $b$ and the resemblance of $c$ to $d$ are likenesses of the same kind, so they too need to be explained. |
| P3 | If they are explained by resemblance, then resemblance between resemblance facts requires a further explanation, and so on without end. |
| C | So either resemblance is a universal, which the nominalist denies, or the explanation never ends. |

The nominalist has replies. One is that resemblance is a primitive, so the first step is not an explanation that needs a further one. Another is that the regress is **benign**: each stage is true given the one before, and nothing requires that we complete an infinite chain before the first stage is explained. Realists answer that primitive resemblance is just as unexplained as the shared universal.

### Realism and tropes on one case

| | Universals | Tropes |
|---|---|---|
| What is in each ball? | The same universal, scarlet, wholly present | A distinct trope, the scarlet of this ball |
| Why are the balls alike? | They instantiate one universal | Their tropes exactly resemble |
| Primitive (unexplained) | Instantiation | Exact resemblance of tropes |
| Cost | One thing in many places at once | Resemblance between tropes is not further explained |
| Number of kinds of entity | Particulars and universals | Tropes (and sets of them) only |

The two views are comparable. A trope theorist may say that the scarlet of this ball is a particular that is located where the ball is, and that trope theory avoids multiple location. The realist may say that trope theory merely relocates the primitive, since "exactly resemble" has the same job as "instantiate the same universal". Philosophers weigh such trades against further considerations, such as which view better handles causation, laws and mathematics.

@example
title: Coextension and class nominalism
problem: Suppose a small world has exactly four things. Every thing that is round is also red, and every thing that is red is also round. Show how this causes difficulty for class nominalism.
step: According to class nominalism, the property of being round is the class of round things and the property of being red is the class of red things.
step: In this world every round thing is red and every red thing is round, so the two classes have the same members.
step: Classes with the same members are identical, so the view must say that roundness and redness are one property. But they seem to be different properties: something could have been round without being red.
step: The repair that uses classes of possible things distinguishes them, but only by adding merely possible things to the ontology.
answer: Class nominalism identifies coextensive properties, which are intuitively distinct, so it must either accept that identification or add possibilia.

@example
title: Resemblance and the imperfect community
problem: Three tokens are such that each pair shares exactly one of the properties $F$, $G$ and $H$: $a$ and $b$ share $F$, $b$ and $c$ share $G$, and $a$ and $c$ share $H$, but no property is shared by all three. Why is this a difficulty for resemblance nominalism?
step: Each of $a$, $b$ and $c$ resembles the other two to a considerable degree, since each pair shares a feature.
step: A resemblance nominalist who says that things are alike in a respect when they resemble each other will therefore count $a$, $b$ and $c$ as a class of things all alike in some one respect.
step: But there is no one respect in which all three are alike. The imperfect community shows that pairwise resemblance does not guarantee a common feature.
answer: Pairwise resemblance does not guarantee a feature shared by the whole group, which is Goodman's imperfect community difficulty.

@mistakes
- Saying that trope theory is just realism under another name. Tropes are particulars: they are not repeatable and each is in one place.
- Assuming that a nominalist about universals must deny that things have properties. Trope theorists accept properties as particular tropes.
- Thinking that the problem of coextension shows that no two properties are coextensive. It shows that an account which identifies properties with classes cannot tell coextensive properties apart.
- Saying that Russell's regress proves that resemblance nominalism is false. It is an objection that the nominalist can answer, and the replies are debated.

prompt: Two plums are both purple. Compare how the realist about universals and the trope theorist each explain why the plums are alike, say what each takes as primitive, and state one cost of each.

What is primitive is exact resemblance. The cost is that the likeness of the tropes is left unexplained, and the realist says it plays the role that the shared universal plays. Which view is better depends on how the two handle further matters such as laws and mathematical objects.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain Russell's regress against resemblance nominalism in your own words and give one reply.
hint: Ask whether the resemblance between two scarlet things is itself something that needs explaining.
solution: The resemblance nominalist says that two scarlet things are alike because they resemble each other. But the resemblance between them is a likeness of the same type as the resemblance between any other two resembling things. If that needs an explanation, the nominalist would say it is a matter of resemblance between resemblances, which in turn is another likeness, and so on. Russell concludes that the nominalist must either admit that resemblance is a universal or accept an endless chain of explanations. One reply is that resemblance is primitive and need not be explained. Another is that the regress is benign, since each stage is true given the previous one and nothing depends on completing an infinite explanation first.

rubric: Gives a reply: primitive resemblance or a benign regress.
