@article
Suppose a surgeon removes your cerebrum, the part of the brain that carries memory, character and thought, and puts it into another person's skull after that person's own cerebrum has been removed. The person who wakes up has your memories, your character and your plans. Is that person you? Or are you the living body left on the table, which still breathes but has lost its psychology? The question is one of **personal identity**: what makes a person at one time the same person as a person at another time?

This is the **persistence question** about persons. It differs from the question of what makes someone the sort of person they are (their character or self-conception), and from the question of what a person is made of. The answers are usually divided into three families.

### Psychological views

On **psychological views**, a person persists through psychological relations between earlier and later stages: memory, intention, character and belief. John Locke's account in *An Essay concerning Human Understanding* (II.xxvii) takes a person to be a thinking, intelligent being that can regard itself as itself at different times, and holds that what makes a person the same over time is sameness of consciousness, which reaches back as far as memory does. His case of the prince whose consciousness enters the body of a cobbler (II.xxvii.15) suggests that the person goes with the consciousness.

Thomas Reid objected with the case of the **brave officer**, in *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785). A boy is flogged for stealing fruit. Later, as a young officer, he captures a standard, and remembers being flogged. Later still, as a general, he remembers capturing the standard but no longer remembers the flogging.

| P1 | Memory criterion: a person at a later time is the same person as someone at an earlier time if and only if the later person remembers the earlier person's experiences. |
| P2 | The officer remembers the boy's flogging, and the general remembers the officer's capture of the standard. |
| P3 | The general does not remember the flogging. |
| P4 | Identity is transitive. |
| C | So the memory criterion implies both that the general is the boy (the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, by P4) and that he is not (by P3), which is a contradiction. |

So the simple memory criterion must go. Joseph Butler had earlier objected that memory presupposes personal identity, since to remember an experience is to remember *one's own* experience, so the criterion is circular. Contemporary psychological views reply to both. Against Reid, they say that identity requires **continuity**, an overlapping chain of connections, not direct memory. Derek Parfit distinguished **psychological connectedness**, the holding of direct psychological connections such as memories, persisting intentions, beliefs, desires and character traits, from **psychological continuity**, the holding of overlapping chains of strong connectedness. Connectedness is **strong** when there are at least half as many direct connections as hold over every day in the lives of nearly every actual person. Against Butler, Sydney Shoemaker and Parfit proposed **quasi-memory**: an apparent memory of an experience that was caused in the right way by that experience, whether or not it was one's own, so that the notion does not presuppose identity.

### The biological view

On the **biological view**, or **animalism**, we are human animals, and we persist as long as the animal does, by the continuation of its life. Eric Olson defends this in *The Human Animal* (1997). It is supported by the following argument:

| P1 | There is a human animal sitting where you are. |
| P2 | The human animal thinks. |
| P3 | If you are not that animal, then two beings sitting where you are are thinking your thoughts. |
| P4 | It is not the case that two beings are thinking your thoughts. |
| C | So you are that animal. |

The argument is valid. Psychological theorists reject P2 or P4: the animal thinks only derivatively, in virtue of being constituted by or related to the person, so there is only one thinker in the primary sense. Animalists reply that this is unmotivated. Animalism says that the transplant recipient is not you, since the animal does not go with the transplanted cerebrum but stays behind. It also says that you were once a fetus and might survive in a permanent vegetative state, claims that a psychological view denies.

### Further-fact views

**Further-fact** (non-reductionist) views, such as those of Richard Swinburne and Roderick Chisholm, hold that personal identity is not reducible to psychological or physical relations: it is a further fact, perhaps the persistence of an immaterial soul or a simple self. They hold that continuity cannot settle whether a future person is you. Critics ask what this further fact is and how we could tell whether it holds.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 44% of respondents accepted or leaned toward a psychological view, 19% the biological view and 15% the further-fact view. The share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

@example
title: Applying Reid's objection
problem: A girl, Mara, breaks a window at age 7. At 25 Mara remembers breaking the window. At 60 Mara remembers her life at 25 but not the window. A simple memory theorist says: x is the same person as y iff x remembers y's experiences. Show the problem, and say how the continuity theorist responds.
step: By the simple memory criterion, the 60-year-old is the 25-year-old, since she remembers the 25-year-old's experiences, and the 25-year-old is the 7-year-old, since she remembers the window.
step: By transitivity, the 60-year-old is the 7-year-old. But the 60-year-old does not remember the 7-year-old's experiences, so by the criterion she is not the 7-year-old.
step: Contradiction: the criterion says both that she is and that she is not. So it cannot be correct as stated.
step: The continuity theorist replaces direct memory with a chain: the 60-year-old is the 7-year-old because they are linked by overlapping chains of strong connectedness. Direct memory is not required between the endpoints.
answer: The simple criterion gives a contradiction; the continuity view requires only a chain of connections.

@example
title: Strong connectedness
problem: On Parfit's definition, connectedness is strong when at least half as many direct connections hold as hold over every day in the lives of nearly every actual person. If 80 direct connections hold on a typical day, and 55 hold between Dev at 30 and Dev at 40, is Dev at 30 strongly connected with Dev at 40?
step: The threshold is half of 80, which is 40 connections.
step: 55 is at least 40, so the connections between Dev at 30 and Dev at 40 are strong.
step: Strong connectedness between neighbors in a chain, overlapping, is what psychological continuity requires.
answer: Yes; 55 is above the threshold of 40.

@mistakes
- Saying that Locke thought a person is a body. He distinguished the person, the man (the animal) and the substance, and held that the person goes with consciousness.
- Treating Reid's objection as showing that psychological views are false. It refutes the simple memory criterion, and continuity views are a response.
- Saying that animalism denies that humans think. It says that we are animals, and animals can think.
- Confusing connectedness with continuity. Connectedness concerns direct connections; continuity is an overlapping chain of strong connectedness.

hint: Consider whether the animal in your chair thinks and whether there could be two thinkers.
solution: The argument says that a human animal sits where you are and thinks, and that if you are not it, two beings are thinking your thoughts, which is unacceptable. I would challenge premise P4, the denial that two beings are thinking your thoughts. A defender of the psychological view can say that there are two beings, a person and an animal, in the same place, and that they differ in kind: the person thinks in the primary sense and the animal derivatively.
