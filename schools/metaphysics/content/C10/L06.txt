@article
Take an apple. It is red, round, sweet, heavy. Remove the red, the roundness, the sweetness and the weight in thought, and what is left? One answer: nothing; the apple is just its properties together. Another answer: something is left, the thing that has those properties. This is the question of what an ordinary object, or **substance**, is over and above its properties.

### Three conceptions

**Aristotelian substance.** Aristotle's primary substances are individual things such as this man and this horse, which are neither said of a subject nor present in a subject (*Categories*, chapter 5). A substance is the basic thing, and properties depend on substances and not the other way around. The substance has an essence: some properties are had essentially, and the thing could not exist without them. Many contemporary philosophers use "substance" in this sense of a thick, independent, property-bearing particular with a nature.

**The substratum theory.** On this view, a thing has properties by having a **substratum**, an underlying bearer that is distinct from all its properties. Locke wrote of a notion of substance in general as something we suppose to support properties but have no clear idea of. In modern discussion the substratum is called a **bare particular** (the term comes from Gustav Bergmann) or, in David Armstrong's terminology, a **thin particular**: a particular considered apart from its properties. The thing itself is then a bare particular together with its properties, a **thick particular**.

**The bundle theory.** On this view, an object is nothing but a bundle of properties, held together by a relation of **compresence**, the relation that properties bear when they are instantiated together in the same place. Hume's remarks on the idea of substance and on the self are often read as a bundle view. Russell developed the idea for objects, and in the trope version of the theory, which Donald C. Williams and Keith Campbell defended, an object is a bundle of tropes (Lesson 4).

### Problems for the bundle theory

If the bundle consists of universals, then two objects with the same universals have the same constituents, so they would be one and the same bundle. Max Black ("The Identity of Indiscernibles", 1952) used this point against the bundle theory:

| P1 | If the bundle theory with universals is true, then necessarily, objects that have exactly the same properties are identical. |
| P2 | It is possible that there are two distinct objects with exactly the same properties: in a universe containing only two iron spheres of the same size, temperature and color, a mile apart, each has the same properties as the other. |
| C | So the bundle theory with universals is false. |

The argument is valid. Its conclusion follows from premise P2 together with P1. Premise P1 is the **identity of indiscernibles**, applied to this theory, and should be kept distinct from **Leibniz's Law**, the **indiscernibility of identicals**: if $x = y$, then $x$ and $y$ have the same properties. Leibniz's Law is uncontroversial; the identity of indiscernibles is not.

Replies:

1. **Deny premise P2.** Leibniz held that two things cannot share all properties. A defender of this reply says that Black's world only seems possible. A sphere's relational properties may distinguish it from the other: being exactly a mile from *that* sphere. But the bundle theorist cannot easily use properties that involve the identity of the other sphere, since this presupposes that the spheres are already individuated.
2. **Use tropes.** If each sphere is a bundle of its own tropes, then the two bundles are distinct even though their tropes exactly resemble.
3. **Add bare particulars or haecceities.** A bare particular, or a primitive "thisness", distinguishes the spheres even if all their qualities are the same.

### Problems for bare particulars

Bare particulars are said to be unknowable: since they have no qualities of their own, we could not detect them. A further objection is that a bare particular cannot be bare, because it has at least the properties of being a particular and of being a bearer of properties. Defenders reply that "bare" means that the particular has no property as a *constituent* or no nature, not that it has no properties in any sense. Other issues: what the tie between the bearer and its properties is, and whether two worlds that differ only by swapping bare particulars are really different.

@example
title: Counting bundles
problem: A world has three properties, $F$, $G$ and $H$. Suppose every non-empty collection of them can be the complete nature of an object, and that objects are identified with their bundles. How many different objects are possible?
step: A bundle is a non-empty subset of $\{F, G, H\}$.
step: A set of three things has $2^3 = 8$ subsets. Excluding the empty set, which is not an object, leaves $8 - 1 = 7$.
step: The seven bundles are $\{F\}$, $\{G\}$, $\{H\}$, $\{F,G\}$, $\{F,H\}$, $\{G,H\}$ and $\{F,G,H\}$.
step: Note what this shows about the bundle theory with universals: at most one object can have each bundle. So in this model there can be at most one object with exactly the properties $F$, $G$ and $H$, which is the consequence that Black's spheres are meant to refute.
answer: 7 possible objects, and no two of them have exactly the same properties.

@example
title: Responding to Black
problem: A philosopher replies to Black: "Each sphere is a mile from the other, so they differ in a property: being a mile from sphere A versus being a mile from sphere B." Evaluate the reply.
step: The reply uses relational properties. In Black's world, sphere A is a mile from sphere B, and sphere B is a mile from sphere A.
step: To tell the two apart by these properties, we have to say that A has "being a mile from B" and B has "being a mile from A". These properties are identified by reference to the particular spheres they involve.
step: But the bundle theory aims to say what makes each sphere the individual it is, and the properties used presuppose that the individuals are already distinct. The reply is circular unless the properties can be specified qualitatively, and the only qualitative property, "being a mile from some sphere", is shared.
answer: The reply is circular for a bundle theory of universals, since the distinguishing properties already involve the identity of the spheres.

@mistakes
- Confusing the identity of indiscernibles with Leibniz's Law. The first says things with all the same properties are identical; the second says that identical things have the same properties.
- Saying that the bundle theory is Hume's view of objects without qualification. Hume's remarks are often read that way, but this is an interpretation.
- Supposing that "bare" means having no properties at all. Defenders mean that the particular has no constituent properties or nature.
- Thinking that the trope bundle theory faces Black's objection in the same way. Two bundles of exactly resembling tropes are distinct bundles.

@practice self
prompt: Reconstruct the epistemological argument against platonism about mathematical objects in standard form, naming each premise's role, and say which premise is most often rejected and why.
hint: The argument has premises about what platonism implies about causation, what knowledge requires, what we lack and what we know.
solution: The argument is:

| P1 | If platonism is true, mathematical objects are abstract and have no causal effects. |
| P2 | We can know facts about a kind of thing only if we stand in some causal relation to it. |
| P3 | We do not stand in a causal relation to anything that has no causal effects. |
| C1 | So if platonism is true, we cannot know facts about mathematical objects. |
| P4 | We know many facts about mathematical objects. |
| C | So platonism is false. |

Premise P1 says what platonism implies, P2 states a causal theory of knowledge, P3 applies it and P4 says mathematical knowledge is real. Premise P2 is most often rejected, because knowledge of the future, of logical truths and of laws does not seem to require causal contact. Field's reliability version replaces P2 with a demand to explain why mathematicians' beliefs are reliable.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare the bundle theory and the substratum theory of ordinary objects. State one advantage and one problem of each, and say which problem you find more serious, with a reason.
hint: Consider economy of kinds for the bundle theory, and individuation for the substratum theory.
solution: The bundle theory is economical: it needs only properties and a compresence relation, with no further bearer. Its problem is individuation: if the properties are universals, two objects with the same properties, like Black's spheres, would be one bundle. The substratum theory explains how two objects can share all their properties, since they have different bare particulars. Its problem is that the substratum seems to be unknowable and, because it has no nature, it is hard to say what it contributes. I find individuation less serious for the bundle theorist, who can switch to trope bundles. The unknowability of the substratum is harder to remove, but a defender can say that we infer it as the best explanation of distinctness.
