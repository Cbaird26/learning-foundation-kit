title: A metaphysics paper on one puzzle
overview: Write a paper of about 2,500 words on one puzzle from the course: the statue and the clay, the special composition question, the ship of Theseus, temporary intrinsics or fission. The paper states the puzzle precisely as an argument, compares two solutions and their costs, and defends one of them against its best objection.
goal: State a metaphysical puzzle as an argument in standard form, so that each premise can be assessed.
goal: Present two solutions accurately, with their attributions, and say which premise each one rejects.
goal: Compare the costs of the solutions and defend one of them in an argument that answers the strongest objection.
step: Choose one puzzle from the list in the material. Read the lesson for that puzzle again and the relevant Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry. Search PhilPapers for two or three further sources, and keep a list of what you use.
step: Write the puzzle as an argument in standard form with numbered premises and a conclusion, or as an inconsistent set of claims. Say which claims are intuitively compelling and why. This is about 400 words.
step: Choose two solutions that reject different premises. State each one accurately, name its main defenders only where you are sure of the attribution, and say what it keeps and what it gives up. This is about 800 words.
step: Compare the costs. For each solution, list the cost in terms of the theoretical virtues from Lesson 1 (simplicity, explanatory power, fit with common sense and with science), and say which costs you judge to be heavier and why. This is about 400 words.
step: Choose one solution to defend. State the strongest objection to it in the form a critic would give, and answer it. Do not claim more than your argument shows. This is about 600 words.
step: Write an introduction of about 150 words that states your thesis, and a conclusion of about 150 words that says what you have shown and what remains open. Check that every attribution can be traced to a source.
step: Revise. Check each premise of your argument for the puzzle, cut anything that does not serve the thesis, read the paper aloud, and cite every source you used.

## Week-by-week

**Week 1: aims and methods.** Metaphysics asks what exists and what is fundamental. Methods: analysis of cases, naturalism, theory choice by virtues (quantitative versus qualitative parsimony), formal tools. The verificationist argument is valid but its first premise is self-undermining. Carnap's internal and external questions; Hirsch's quantifier variance and Sider's realism.

**Week 2: existence and commitment.** The puzzle of non-being; Russell's descriptions; Quine's criterion (to be is to be the value of a bound variable); regimentation; universal sentences carry no existential commitment; existence is expressed by the quantifier; Meinongianism.

**Week 3: universals.** One over many; Platonic and immanent realism; the principle of instantiation; sparse and abundant properties; the third man (one over many, self-predication, non-identity); Bradley's regress.

**Week 4: nominalism and tropes.** Predicate, class and resemblance nominalism; coextension; companionship and imperfect community; Russell's regress; tropes as particular property instances with exact resemblance as primitive.

**Week 5: abstract objects.** Indispensability argument (Quine and Putnam); Field's nominalization; Benacerraf's tension; the causal premise and the reliability version of the challenge; replies.

**Week 6: substance and bundles.** Aristotelian substance, substratum and bare particulars, bundle theory; Black's spheres; the identity of indiscernibles versus Leibniz's Law.

**Week 7: constitution.** Lumpl and Goliath; Leibniz's Law; constitution view and the grounding problem; counterpart theory and contingent identity; four-dimensionalism; eliminativism.

**Week 8: composition.** The definition of composition; nihilism, universalism, contact, fastening, organicism, brutal composition; the vagueness argument.

**Weeks 9 and 10: persistence and change.** Endurance, perdurance, stages; Sider's definition of a temporal part; the ship of Theseus; temporary intrinsics and the replies: relationalism, adverbialism, presentism, perdurance.

**Weeks 11 and 12: persons.** Locke, Reid's brave officer, Butler's circularity, continuity and quasi-memory, animalism and the thinking animal argument, further-fact views; fission, multiple occupancy, closest continuer, stage theory and Parfit's relation R.

## Key arguments to be able to state

Verificationist argument; Pegasus triad; argument from the one over many; third man; Russell's regress; indispensability; epistemological argument against platonism; Black's spheres; Leibniz's Law argument for statue and clay; vagueness argument for composition; ship of Theseus triad; problem of temporary intrinsics; brave officer; thinking animal argument; fission.

- Can you regiment a sentence and read off what it is committed to, including when it is committed to nothing?
- Can you distinguish the identity of indiscernibles from Leibniz's Law, and connectedness from continuity?
- Can you state the difference between endurance, perdurance and stage theory in one sentence each?

## Common errors

Treating the Quinean criterion as a test of what exists; saying that a valid argument is unsound without naming a premise; confusing coincidence with overlap; reading the third man as Aristotle's own view; saying that Parfit held that fission is death; and attributing a view to a philosopher without being sure of the attribution.

prompt: A philosopher says: "The lump and the statue exist at exactly the same times and share all their temporal parts, so they are one thing. Their modal differences are only differences in how we describe it." Which combination of views does this philosopher hold?
choice*: Four-dimensionalism with a counterpart-theoretic treatment of modal predicates.
choice: The constitution view with the grounding problem.
choice: Eliminativism about composite objects.
choice: Endurantism with relationalism about shape.
hint: Identity based on shared temporal parts, with modal claims treated as context sensitive.
solution: Sharing all temporal parts makes the lump and the statue one worm, which is four-dimensionalism, and treating modal differences as context sensitive descriptions is the counterpart-theoretic reply. The constitution view keeps two coincident things, and eliminativism denies that there are composite objects.

@exam num
prompt: A world contains exactly 5 simples. Under universalism with uniqueness of composition, every non-empty collection of simples composes exactly one object. How many of these objects are composed of exactly three simples?
answer: 10
unit: objects
check: sum(1 for r in product([0, 1], repeat=5) if sum(r) == 3)
hint: Each such object corresponds to a choice of 3 of the 5 simples.
solution: Distinct collections compose distinct objects, so we count the ways to choose 3 simples from 5: $\binom{5}{3} = 10$ objects.

@exam self
prompt: Use what you have learned about composition, persistence and personal identity to answer: if a four-dimensionalist holds that persons are spacetime worms, how does the view treat the fission of a person into two? Compare it with the stage view, say which you find more plausible, and state the strongest objection to your choice.
hint: Count the worms before the division, and recall what the stage view says about "will be".
solution: The worm theorist can say that two overlapping worms share their pre-fission stages, so there were two people before the division (Lewis's multiple occupancy), and each survives as one of Left and Right. This keeps identity and transitivity, but it counts two people where we seemed to see one, and so needs the idea of counting by sharing a stage at a time. The stage theorist says that the person is a stage, which has two future counterparts, so "I will be Left" and "I will be Right" are both true with no violation of transitivity; the cost is that ordinary things are momentary. I find the stage view somewhat more plausible because it does not multiply people, though it requires taking "I" to refer to a stage, and the strongest objection is that this misdescribes what we mean when we say that we persist through time: a thing that exists only at an instant does not seem to be what we take ourselves to be. The reply is that the stage theory can paraphrase persistence claims through counterparts and that ordinary speech does not settle what we are.
