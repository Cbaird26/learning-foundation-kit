@article
A poker is straight on Monday and, after heating and bending, bent on Tuesday. This is a case of **change**: one and the same thing has a property at one time that it lacks at another. Change is the paradigm of persistence, and it seems to need nothing special. But the example has a feature that makes trouble for endurantism: being straight and being bent are shapes, and shape seems to be an **intrinsic** property.

### Intrinsic properties

A property is **intrinsic** if a thing has it in virtue of how that thing is in itself, regardless of what else exists; it is **extrinsic** if it depends on the thing's relations to other things. Shape is the standard example of an intrinsic property, and being an uncle is an extrinsic property. Two perfect duplicates agree in their intrinsic properties. A **temporary intrinsic** is an intrinsic property that a thing has at one time and lacks at another. Change in shape, mass or charge consists in gaining or losing temporary intrinsics.

### The argument

David Lewis put the problem in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), calling it the principal and decisive objection to endurance. It can be set out as follows:

| P1 | Some persisting things change their intrinsic shape: the poker is straight at one time and bent at another. |
| P2 | Being straight and being bent are incompatible shapes. |
| P3 | Shapes are intrinsic properties, so a thing has its shape without relation to times or anything else. |
| P4 | Nothing has incompatible intrinsic properties, simply and without qualification. |
| P5 | If the poker endures, then it is wholly present on Monday and wholly present on Tuesday, and it is the poker itself, not a part of it, that has each shape. |
| C | So if the poker endures, it has incompatible intrinsic properties simply, which is impossible. |

The argument is valid. It does not show that endurantism is false unless its premises are accepted, and each of the endurantist's replies denies one of them.

### The endurantist replies

1. **Relationalism.** Shapes are not intrinsic properties but relations to times: the poker bears the relation "being straight at" to Monday and "being bent at" to Tuesday. This denies P3. The cost is that shape seems to be a matter of how the poker is in itself, and the reply makes it extrinsic.
2. **Adverbialism.** (Mark Johnston, E. J. Lowe.) The poker has the property of being bent, but it has it in a time-relative way: it is bent-*on-Tuesday*, with the time modifying how it has the property. This denies the second half of P3, that a thing has its shape without relation to times, since having a property at a time in this way is not having it simply. Lewis objected that the notion of having a property "at a time" in a way that is not a relation to the time is obscure.
3. **Presentism.** Only the present exists, so the poker has only its present shape. This denies P1 or P5 as stated, since there is no past straight poker for the present poker to differ from. The cost is that presentism faces its own problems, such as what makes true claims about the past.

### The perdurantist solution

The perdurantist says that the poker is a four-dimensional worm, with one temporal part on Monday and another on Tuesday. The Monday part is straight and the Tuesday part is bent, and each has its shape simply, without relation to a time. This denies P5, since it is not the whole poker that is present on each day. The poker itself changes in the way a road changes: it is narrow at one end and wide at the other because its parts differ. Change is **qualitative difference between the temporal parts of a persisting thing**. The sentence "the poker is bent on Tuesday" is true because the poker has a bent temporal part on Tuesday. On the stage view, the sentence "the poker was straight" is true because the present stage has a straight past counterpart.

The perdurantist faces objections. One is that it is odd that the poker never is wholly present, and that the thing in your hand is a part of the thing you take yourself to hold. A second is that the account makes change a matter of difference between *different things* (the parts), not of one thing varying. The perdurantist replies that the whole worm does differ from itself in the way a road does, and that endurantist accounts, in which shape is relational or adverbial, pay a larger price. A third is a parity objection: adopting temporal parts does not by itself avoid the problem, because the perdurantist must also say how a thing has its shape at a time, so the worry may return in some form.

title: A perdurantist account of change
problem: A rod is straight on Monday and Tuesday and bent on Wednesday. Explain what the perdurantist says about the rod, which temporal parts it has, and in what sense the rod changes.
step: The rod is a worm with temporal parts $r_{\text{Mon}}$, $r_{\text{Tue}}$ and $r_{\text{Wed}}$ (taking days as the unit of time).
step: $r_{\text{Mon}}$ and $r_{\text{Tue}}$ are straight and $r_{\text{Wed}}$ is bent, each simply, with no relation to a time.
step: The rod changes between Tuesday and Wednesday because its earlier part is straight and its later part is bent, a qualitative difference between temporal parts.
step: There is no contradiction, since no single thing is both straight and bent. The rod is straight at Tuesday in virtue of having a straight part at Tuesday.
answer: The rod changes because its temporal parts differ in shape; no one thing is both straight and bent.

@example
title: Locating the denied premise
problem: A philosopher replies to the problem of temporary intrinsics by saying: "The poker is not straight simpliciter. It stands in the straight-at relation to Monday and the bent-at relation to Tuesday." Which premise does this reject, and what is the cost?
step: The claim makes straightness a relation between the poker and a time, so the poker's shape depends on its relation to something other than itself.
step: Premise P3 says that shape is intrinsic and not relational. The reply rejects P3.
step: The cost is that shape then looks extrinsic, although a perfect duplicate of the poker would seem to share its shape regardless of when it exists.
answer: It rejects P3; shape becomes a relation to times, which is counterintuitive for an intrinsic property.

@mistakes
- Thinking that the problem arises for any change. It arises for changes in intrinsic properties; a change in being an uncle involves a relation to another person and is not a problem in the same way.
- Saying that the perdurantist denies that change occurs. Change is a difference between temporal parts.
- Saying that the argument shows that endurantism is false. It is valid, but each reply denies a premise.
- Confusing the perdurantist solution with presentism. The perdurantist accepts that past and future parts are real.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the claim that the problem of temporary intrinsics gives us good reason to accept perdurantism. State what you would say to someone who responds that the perdurantist has the same problem.
hint: Compare the costs of the endurantist replies and of temporal parts, and consider the parity objection.
solution: The problem is that an enduring poker would be wholly present at two times with incompatible shapes. The endurantist can reply with relationalism, adverbialism or presentism, each with a cost, whereas the perdurantist avoids contradiction by giving different parts the different shapes. That is a point in favor of perdurantism, but it is not decisive, because temporal parts have costs of their own. The parity objection says that the perdurantist must also say how a thing has its shape at a time, so the relativization to times may return. A reply is that the temporal part exists at only one time, so it has its shape simply, and the relation to times belongs only to the whole worm's way of having parts. I think that the problem supports perdurantism modestly: it raises the cost of the endurantist replies but does not by itself settle the matter.
