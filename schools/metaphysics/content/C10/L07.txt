@article
A sculptor takes a lump of clay and shapes it into a statue of a giant. Call the lump Lumpl and the statue Goliath, after the names used in Allan Gibbard's discussion of the case ("Contingent Identity", 1975). Suppose that the sculptor makes the statue at noon and that, at the end of the day, the statue is crushed, so that statue and lump cease to exist together. From noon to the crushing, are there two things in the studio, or one?

There seem to be two: the lump and the statue are different in some ways. But they have the same location, the same matter, the same shape, the same weight, the same color, and the same beginning and end. It seems odd to say that two things fill exactly the same place with exactly the same matter. This is the puzzle of **material constitution**: the clay **constitutes** the statue, and the question is whether constitution is identity.

### The argument

The case for distinctness runs as follows:

| P1 | Lumpl could have survived being squashed into a ball. |
| P2 | Goliath could not have survived being squashed into a ball. |
| P3 | Leibniz's Law: if $x = y$, then whatever is true of $x$ is true of $y$. |
| C | So Lumpl is not identical to Goliath. |

The argument is valid. It does not need any claim about the actual history of the two things; the difference is in what they could have survived, so it is a difference in modal properties, and the premises P1 and P2 claim that a lump is the kind of thing that survives reshaping and a statue is not. But the conclusion is awkward, since it seems to conflict with the intuition that

| P4 | No two distinct material things can be in the same place, made of the same matter, at the same time. |

So the options are to deny P4 and accept coincident things, to deny P1 or P2, to deny that Leibniz's Law applies to the modal claims, or to deny that there are two things at all.

### Responses

**The constitution view** (David Wiggins, Lynne Rudder Baker) accepts the conclusion and rejects P4. The statue and the lump are distinct things that coincide. Constitution is a relation different from identity. They differ because they belong to different kinds with different persistence conditions: something is a statue only if it has a certain shape and an artistic origin, but a lump persists through change of shape. The cost is the **grounding problem**: if the statue and the lump are alike in all their physical properties and their surroundings, what grounds their different modal properties? A further cost is that there will be many coinciding things, and the view must say why the sculptor's work has not simply created a duplicate. Also, by parity, a person would coincide with the matter making up the body.

**Identity views** reject the conclusion by rejecting P1 or P2 or by qualifying Leibniz's Law. David Lewis argued, using counterpart theory, that modal claims like "could survive squashing" are context sensitive: different counterpart relations are salient when we call the thing a statue and when we call it a lump, so one object can truly be described both ways in different contexts ("Counterparts of Persons and Their Bodies", 1971). Gibbard defended **contingent identity**: Lumpl and Goliath are identical in the actual world but are not necessarily identical, since they could have been distinct. The cost is that the first requires modal predicates to shift their meaning, and the second gives up the standard view that identity is necessary.

**Four-dimensionalism** says that objects persist by having temporal parts (Lesson 9). If the lump and the statue exist together from noon to the crushing, they share all their temporal parts, and are identical. If the clay existed before noon, the statue is a proper temporal part of the clay: they overlap but are not wholly coincident, so they are different objects without being two things in the same place. The modal difference is handled by counterpart theory.

**Eliminativism** denies that there are two things, and denies that there are statues at all. Peter van Inwagen holds that there are no inanimate composite objects: there are only simples arranged statue-wise. Statue talk is a convenient paraphrase. The cost is that this is far from common sense and that it needs a paraphrase of the sentences we ordinarily assert.

| View | Rejects | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Constitution | P4 (no coincidence) | Grounding problem; many coincident things |
| Counterpart-theoretic identity | P1 or P2, read as context-sensitive | Modal predicates change meaning with context |
| Contingent identity | The necessity of identity | Departs from a standard view of identity |
| Eliminativism | That there are statues or lumps | Revisionary; paraphrase required |

@example
title: Applying Leibniz's Law
problem: Suppose a clay lump and a vase begin and end together. The lump could have survived being melted and re-formed as a bowl, but the vase could not. Show that this implies that the lump and the vase are distinct, and name the assumption that a defender of identity would question.
step: Premise: the lump has the property of being able to survive melting and re-forming, and the vase lacks that property.
step: By Leibniz's Law, identical things have the same properties. The lump and the vase differ in this property, so they are not identical.
step: A defender of identity can question the premise that the properties differ. On Lewis's counterpart theory, "could survive melting" is true or false relative to the way the thing is described, so the same thing may have it under the description "lump" and lack it under "vase".
step: Alternatively the defender can deny that there is a single modal property that the two have different values for.
answer: By Leibniz's Law the two are distinct, unless the modal premise is read as context-sensitive or is denied.

@example
title: Mapping responses to premises
problem: A philosopher says: "There really is only a collection of simples arranged vase-wise; the vase and the lump are both convenient ways of talking." Which premise of the argument does this philosopher reject, and what is the cost?
step: The claim denies that there are two things, indeed that there is a vase or a lump as a single object over and above the simples.
step: This is eliminativism, and it rejects the existence presupposed by premises P1 and P2: there is no Lumpl or Goliath of which they could be true.
step: The cost is that the view is revisionary: it denies ordinary claims such as "there is a vase on the table" if they are read at face value, and it needs a paraphrase to say what such sentences mean.
answer: It rejects the existence of the lump and the vase as composite objects (eliminativism), at the cost of revisionary paraphrase.

@mistakes
- Saying that the constitution view identifies the statue with the clay. It says they are distinct things, one of which constitutes the other.
- Saying that the Leibniz's Law argument depends on the statue and the clay having different weights. They are stipulated to be alike in every ordinary physical property; the difference is modal.
- Confusing coincidence with overlap. Coincident things share all their parts at a time; overlapping things share only some.
- Treating eliminativism as the view that matter does not exist. It denies composite objects, not simples.
