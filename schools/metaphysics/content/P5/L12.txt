@article
The PhD is a long commitment, and the application is a small document that has to carry a lot of weight. Admissions committees in philosophy are made of working faculty who read a large number of files. Most of what they learn about you comes from three sources: the writing sample, the letters and the statement of purpose. This lesson explains what each is for and how to prepare them, and it does so without pretending that there is one process: programs differ, and their rules and the field's conditions change.

### What applications commonly contain

Common components of a US philosophy PhD application include a **writing sample**, a **statement of purpose**, a **CV**, **letters of recommendation** (often three), **transcripts** and an application form with a fee (waivers are sometimes available). Some programs ask for standardized test scores; many do not, and policies change, so do not assume. Some ask for additional statements. Deadlines, length limits, anonymization rules and whether a particular area (such as metaphysics) is a strength all vary by program. Outside the US, systems differ: a research master's degree often precedes the doctorate, and the application may involve a research proposal. Always take the requirements from each program's own page and, for current information about graduate study, from the department and the APA.

A philosophy PhD in the United States commonly takes about five to seven years: coursework, a qualifying or comprehensive requirement whose form varies by program, a prospectus and a dissertation. Many programs fund their students, with terms that vary. Admission to doctoral programs is highly competitive, and the academic job market is also highly competitive, with far more new PhDs than permanent academic positions in most years, so many philosophy PhDs build careers outside academia. These facts are reasons to apply with open eyes and to choose programs for the training and the people, not only for a hoped-for job.

### The writing sample

The sample is usually the most important part of the file, because it shows what you can do. The argument for what it should contain is this:

| P1 | Admissions readers use the writing sample to judge whether you can do original philosophical research. |
| P2 | A sample shows that you can do original research only if it contains an original thesis, an argument for it, and replies to objections. |
| P3 | A pure survey of the literature contains no original thesis. |
| C | So a pure survey of the literature does not show that you can do original research. |

The argument is valid: P3 says a survey lacks the original thesis, P2 says that without one the sample does not show research ability, and P1 says that this is what readers are looking for. The premise that a reader could test is P1: some programs also look for evidence of breadth or of writing under course conditions, so check what a program says it wants.

Practical guidance:

- **Choose your best piece**, ideally a paper with a clear thesis, a tight argument and a fair treatment of an objection, written on a topic in an area of the program's strength. It need not be in the area you will finally work in, but it should show the skills.
- **Respect the length limit.** Programs set limits, often a number of pages or words. Do not exceed them, and do not send an excerpt that stops mid-argument; revise the whole paper down to length so that it is self-contained. Cut exposition first.
- **Make it self-contained.** A reader outside your sub-field should see the problem, the thesis and why it matters in the first page.
- **Anonymize if asked**, as in Lesson 8, including file metadata.
- **Have it read** by someone who has not seen it, and revise as in Lesson 10. A sample that has been through referee-style feedback shows.
- **Do not include** an unrevised class paper, a book review or a survey unless a program asks for one.

### The statement of purpose

A statement of purpose, commonly one to two pages (check the limit), is not an autobiography or a statement of passion for philosophy. It tells the committee what you want to work on, how you have prepared and why this program fits. A good one contains: **specific philosophical interests** stated as questions or theses ("I am interested in whether the grounding relation is irreflexive and whether this can be defended without a theory of explanation"), **evidence of preparation** (courses, papers, a thesis, the writing sample, research experience), **a sense of where you might go**, and **fit**: named faculty whose published work connects to yours and what in it engages you. Read the faculty's work, so that you can say something specific and accurate. Avoid clichés about wonder, lists of famous philosophers you admire, and claims that you will solve a major problem. Tone should be professional and plain. Say what you are not sure of: committees expect interests to change.

### Letters, CV and timing

**Letters** come from people who know your philosophical work: ask early (commonly several weeks before the deadline), say what you are applying for, and give your writers your CV, statement draft and writing sample. A letter that speaks specifically about a paper you wrote is stronger than a general one. A **CV** for an application is short: education, relevant papers, presentations, honors, teaching or research assistantships. **Timing**: work backward from the earliest deadline, since the sample takes the longest. Allow time to revise it with feedback.

### Choosing programs

Select programs by **faculty in your area** whose recent work you have read, the **training** and the department's culture, the **funding** offered, the program's **record of placing graduates** in the careers you might want (programs often publish this; read it with care about what is counted), and your own circumstances. Apply to a range. Ask current students and your supervisors for candid advice. If you are not yet ready, a master's program, additional coursework or time to build the sample are legitimate paths, and your advisors can say what suits you.

@example
title: Choosing a writing sample
problem: Suppose a program asks for a sample of up to 4,500 words (this limit is hypothetical). You have (a) a 6,300-word course paper arguing for an original thesis about the problem of the many, with an objection and reply; (b) a 3,000-word excerpt from a long chapter, which begins in the middle of an argument; (c) a 4,400-word survey of five accounts of causation with no thesis of your own. Which would you submit, and what would you do with it?
step: Test each against the argument: does it contain an original thesis, an argument and replies to objections? (a) does; (b) contains part of an argument but is not self-contained; (c) has no thesis.
step: Test against the length limit: (a) is 1,800 words over; (b) is within it; (c) is within it.
step: Decide: choose (a), the only one that shows research ability, and revise it to fit: cut the exposition and any secondary argument, rather than stopping at 4,500 words.
step: Do the arithmetic and plan: 6,300 minus 4,500 is 1,800 words to cut, or about 28.6%; keep the thesis, the main argument, the best objection and its reply, and make the first page self-contained.
answer: Submit (a), revised down to 4,500 words by cutting about 1,800 words of exposition and secondary material, keeping the thesis, argument, objection and reply.

@example
title: Revising a statement-of-purpose paragraph
problem: A draft says: "I have been fascinated by philosophy since I first read the great thinkers, and I want to solve the problem of what exists." Rewrite it as a paragraph of about 80 words that is specific, shows preparation and gives a reason for the program (the faculty member, her work and the program are invented).
step: Find what is wrong: "fascinated", "great thinkers" and "solve the problem of what exists" are clichés and overclaims; there is no specific interest, preparation or fit.
step: Choose a specific question that you could actually pursue: whether ordinary objects exist if composition is restricted.
step: Add evidence of preparation: a paper on the problem of the many, a course in metaphysics, the writing sample.
step: Add fit: name an invented faculty member and a specific connection, accurately and without flattery.
answer: My interest is in whether a restrictive answer to the special composition question can be defended against the argument from vagueness. In a paper on the problem of the many, which I submit as my sample, I argued that supervaluation does not fix the count of candidates. I would like to pursue this with Professor Ansel Brightwater, whose work on vague existence bears directly on my question, in a program with strength in metaphysics and philosophy of logic.

@mistakes
- Treating the process as uniform: requirements, deadlines, limits and policies on tests differ by program and change, so each program's own page is the authority.
- Submitting a survey, an unrevised class paper or an excerpt that stops mid-argument as the writing sample.
- Writing a statement of purpose that is autobiography or praise of philosophy, without a specific question or an accurate reference to faculty work.
- Asking for letters at the last minute, or giving writers nothing to work from.

Write a one-paragraph revision summary (about 120 words) for your own file and the reader, saying what the draft's main problems were, what you changed to address them, and what you decided not to change and why. Half of it was exposition of modal realism and its rivals, which delayed the argument; I cut about 1,500 words of exposition, moving the thesis and the main argument to the first third. I did not add the suggested discussion of counterpart theory, since it does not bear on the argument.

rubric: Says concretely what changed for each, including the narrowed thesis.
rubric: Records at least one decision not to change, with a reason.
