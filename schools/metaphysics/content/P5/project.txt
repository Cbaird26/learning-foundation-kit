> **Title:** The Persistence of Statues Without Sortals
>
> **Introduction.** I argue that the sortal-relative view of identity, on which "same statue" and "same lump" are different relations, cannot be right, since identity is not relative. Many philosophers have discussed the problem of material constitution, and I will say something about several of their views.
>
> **Section 1.** Suppose that a statue is made by pressing a lump of clay. Before pressing, there is a lump; after, there is a statue and the lump. The lump existed before the statue. So the statue is not the lump. (I take this to be the standard argument.)
>
> **Section 2.** The relativist says that the statue and the lump are the same lump but not the same statue. But identity is just identity: if a is the same lump as b, then a is b. So the relativist must be wrong. The view is a confusion.
>
> **Section 3.** It might be thought that the relativist has a reply, by saying that "same lump" means "coincides with at some time". But this is not what the relativist means, and I will not discuss it further.
>
> **Conclusion.** The sortal-relative view fails, and so we should accept that the statue is identical to the lump. In a footnote I note that this has consequences for persons.

@review
## Week by week

1. **What makes a paper good.** A thesis that could be denied, an argument for it, treatment of the strongest objections, accurate attribution, a contribution and clarity. A topic is not a thesis.
2. **Finding and reading the literature.** PhilPapers is an index; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy are free, peer-reviewed encyclopedias. Seed, chain backward and forward, vary vocabulary, check dates, triage in passes. An annotated entry has a citation, summary, evaluation and use note.
3. **Reconstruction.** Conclusion first, then premises and hidden premises; valid as written; keep reconstruction apart from evaluation.
4. **Original contribution.** Counterexample, hidden premise, premise attack, distinction, transfer, diagnosis, synthesis, reversing the burden. Test for novelty, defensibility and significance; cite overlap.
5. **Thesis and structure.** Commitment, precision, scope, support; standard architecture; roadmap, topic sentences, reverse outline.
6. **Objections and replies.** An objection attacks a premise, the inference, a presupposition or the conclusion; rebutting differs from undercutting. Replies: deny, distinguish, concede and limit, bite the bullet, parity, dissolve. State the cost.
7. **Clarity.** Stipulative, lexical and analytic definitions; test both directions of a definition; equivocation and scope; fixed terms; minimal, named cases.
8. **Citation and integrity.** Cite ideas, not only words; paraphrase is not patchwriting; plagiarism, self-plagiarism, fabrication, misrepresentation, confidentiality; verify every source; policies on submission, preprints and writing tools vary.
9. **Refereeing.** Summary, assessment, numbered major concerns, minor points, recommendation; fatal against fixable; courtesy; confidentiality and conflicts of interest. As an author: sort comments, take misreadings seriously, respond point by point.
10. **Revising.** Argument, structure, paragraphs, sentences, surface, in that order; feedback triage; revision plan; abstract; cutting to length.
11. **Talks.** One main argument, thesis early, handout in standard form, words fit the time, restate questions, concede honestly.
12. **Applications.** Writing sample (original thesis, self-contained, within the limit), statement of purpose (specific, accurate on fit), letters, program choice. Check every program's own rules.

## Self-check

- Can I state my thesis in one sentence that an informed reader could deny?
- Can I put my main argument in valid standard form and say which premise is least defended?
- Have I named the strongest objection, said which premise it attacks, and said what my reply costs?
- Is every source verified, every quotation exact, every idea credited?

## Common errors

Mistaking a topic for a thesis; surveying instead of arguing; reconstructing a weaker argument than the author's; replying to an objection that is not the one made; patchwriting; citing an unread or unverifiable source; polishing sentences before fixing the argument; assuming that one journal's or program's rules apply to all.

@exam mc
prompt: Invented argument against identifying a statue with its lump of clay. P1: The statue is essentially statue-shaped. P2: The lump is not essentially statue-shaped. P3: If x is identical to y, then x and y have the same properties. C: So the statue is not identical to the lump. A critic replies: "'Is essentially statue-shaped' is true of an object only relative to how it is picked out, so it is not one of the properties that the principle that identical things share all their properties covers. Your argument applies the principle where it does not hold." Which premise does this reply attack?
choice: P1, because it denies that the statue has any essential properties.
choice: P2, because it says that the lump is essentially statue-shaped.
choice*: P3, as applied here, because it denies that the principle covers the predicate "is essentially statue-shaped".
choice: The conclusion alone, because it accepts the premises and denies the conclusion without giving a reason.
hint: The argument is valid as written, so a reply must attack a premise. Ask which premise the reply says is misapplied.
solution: The argument is valid, so the reply must attack a premise. The reply does not deny that the statue is described as essentially statue-shaped, nor that the lump is not; it says that the predicate does not express a property to which the principle about identical things applies. That restricts the scope of P3, so it attacks P3 as it is used in the argument. A critic would then ask what makes a predicate property-expressing, and whether the restriction is principled.

@exam self
prompt: Timed essay. You have this invented abstract and two referee comments. Abstract: "Many philosophers hold that the passage of time is an illusion. I argue that this view is incoherent, because an illusion of passage requires a succession of experiences, and a succession is itself a passage. I consider the reply that the succession is only a succession in a static series, and I argue that this reply makes the illusion unexplainable." Comment 1: "The reply you consider is the main position in the literature and you give it one paragraph; the argument that it makes the illusion unexplainable is asserted, not given." Comment 2: "The first premise, that an illusion requires a succession of experiences, is doubtful: one could have a single experience that represents motion." (a) Reconstruct the paper's central argument in standard form, valid as written, marking any supplied premise. (b) Say which premise or step each comment attacks and the type of reply you would give, with its cost. (c) Write a prioritized revision plan of at least four items, with a rough word budget for a 6,500-word paper that is 500 words over a 6,000-word limit.
hint: For (a) find the conclusion first, chain the stated premises, and add the step that sets the result against what the view itself says. For (b) match each comment to a premise or to the dialectical weakness it points out. For (c) rank by effect on the argument and make the budget sum correctly.
solution: (a) P1: If passage of time is an illusion, then there is an illusion of passage. P2: Any illusion of passage requires a succession of experiences. P3: Any succession of experiences is a passage of time (the abstract's own claim). C1: So if passage is an illusion, then there is a passage of time (P1 to P3, chaining). P4 (supplied): If passage is an illusion, then there is no passage of time (this is what the illusion view asserts). C: So passage is not an illusion: on the illusion view there would both be and not be a passage of time, which is the incoherence the abstract claims (a reductio from C1 and P4). Valid as written; P4 is the only supplied premise and merely states the content of the view under attack, and P3 is the premise on which the argument depends. (b) Comment 2 attacks P2: reply by distinction or concession: distinguish an illusion of passage as a single experience that represents motion from an illusion of passage over time, and limit the thesis to the second, at the cost of weakening the claim that all illusions of passage are impossible. Comment 1 attacks the support for P3 against the static-succession reply, an objection that the premise is not adequately defended: reply by deny and develop: give the argument that a static succession cannot explain the content of the experience of passage, at the cost of committing to a view about what explains experiential content, and say that the cost is accepted. (c) Revision plan: 1. Develop the argument against the static-succession reply, giving it a full section (add about 700 words). 2. Restate P2 and restrict the thesis, adding about 200 words in the argument section. 3. Cut about 1,400 words of exposition and history elsewhere (500 for the limit, 900 for the additions). 4. Reverse-outline for structure and move the thesis to the first page. 5. Rewrite sentences and abstract, which must state the narrower thesis. 6. Check citations and anonymization. Budget: 6,500 plus 700 plus 200 minus 1,400 equals 6,000.
rubric: The reconstruction is valid as written, identifies the conclusion correctly and marks supplied premises.
rubric: Each comment is matched to the premise or step it attacks (P2, and the support for P3 against the static-succession reply), with a named type of reply and a stated cost.
rubric: The revision plan is prioritized from argument to structure to sentences to surface, and the word budget sums to the stated limit.
rubric: The answer keeps reconstruction, objection and reply, and revision separate, and does not invent sources.
