@article
Peer review is the main quality filter in academic philosophy, and it is also one of its main teachers. Most professional philosophers spend years receiving reports before they write many, and the habits they form as authors (defensiveness, or learning from criticism) shape their careers. In this lesson you learn both sides of the process, because writing a good report teaches you what a paper needs, and reading one well teaches you how to revise.

### How review commonly works

Journals differ, so what follows is common practice, not a rule. An author submits a manuscript, often prepared for **anonymous review** so that referees do not know the author's identity (and some journals also keep referees anonymous). An editor may **desk-reject** a paper that is out of scope, not ready, or not a fit. Otherwise the editor invites one or more referees, who read the paper and send a report and a recommendation, and the editor decides. Common outcomes include accept, accept with minor revisions, **revise and resubmit** (a real chance, not a polite rejection, though not a promise of acceptance), and reject. Timelines and the number of referees vary widely, so check each journal's own description of its process. Review is imperfect: referees disagree, and a rejection may say more about fit or a referee's taste than about the paper's quality.

### The referee's responsibilities

A referee owes the editor an honest, timely judgment, and the author a useful report. Accept an invitation only if you know the area, have time and have no **conflict of interest** (a close relationship, a rivalry, a direct stake). Declining promptly helps the editor. The manuscript is **confidential**: do not share it, use its ideas or discuss it, unless the journal permits. Judge the paper that was written, not the one that you would have written, and judge it on its merits and not on the author's reputation or position, which you should not try to discover.

### The structure of a report

A useful report has a standard shape.

1. **Summary.** In a paragraph, say what the paper argues and how. This shows the editor and author that you understood it, and often exposes a misreading that is itself a finding: if a careful referee misread the paper, other readers will.
2. **Overall assessment.** Say what the contribution is, what is strong, and whether the central argument works. Be specific about strengths: "the case in section 3 is a genuine counterexample" is more useful than "interesting paper".
3. **Major concerns.** Number them. For each, say what the problem is, where it is in the paper, why it matters for the thesis, and, when you can, what would fix it. Keep the list to what truly matters.
4. **Minor points.** Smaller matters: a missing reference, an unclear sentence, a typo. Keep them brief.
5. **Recommendation.** Often addressed to the editor and sometimes in a separate box. Say what you recommend and why, in terms of the criteria in the journal's instructions. Make sure that your recommendation is consistent with your comments: major unresolved concerns do not go with "accept".

Referees look for the same questions that Lesson 1 listed: Is the thesis clear? Is the argument valid and are the premises defended? Are the views discussed accurately? Is the contribution new? Is it clear?

### Useful and useless criticism

Compare "the argument is unconvincing" with "premise P3 of the argument in section 2, that every candidate is equally a cat, is asserted but not supported; the objection in section 4 depends on it, so without support for P3 the reply fails". The second tells the author what to change. The reason is this:

| P1 | A report helps an author revise only if the author can tell what to change. |
| P2 | If a criticism is vague, then the author cannot tell what to change. |
| C | So if a criticism is vague, the report does not help the author revise. |

The argument is valid (by contraposition on P1, chained with P2: vague criticism leaves the author unable to tell what to change, and so the report does not help). A reader may object that a report can help in other ways, for example by showing that readers are not persuaded; that objection attacks P1, and it succeeds only in a minimal sense: the report helps by signaling a problem, though not by saying how to fix it.

### Tone and fairness

Write about the paper and not the author: "the argument in section 2 does not establish" and not "the author fails to see". Distinguish **fatal** problems (the argument is invalid, a key premise is false, the thesis is already in the literature) from **fixable** ones (a gap that a further paragraph could fill). Do not ask for a different paper: "this would be better as a paper about X" is not a criticism of the paper that was submitted, unless the paper's own claims require X. Say what you are not sure of, and say if a point is outside your expertise.

### Receiving a report

Read it, put it down for a day or two, and read it again. Then **sort** the comments into four groups: **fatal** (the thesis or main argument fails), **substantial** (needs a new argument or restructuring), **fixable** (a clearer paragraph, an added citation) and **misreadings** (the referee understood the paper otherwise than you meant). Take misreadings seriously: they are evidence that something is unclear, even if the referee is wrong about what you said. Then write a plan and a **response**. Journals commonly want a covering letter to the editor with a point-by-point response: quote each comment, say what you changed and where (by section), and where you disagree, say so politely and give reasons. Never respond to a report angrily; the referee is helping you, even when unfairly, and the editor reads your response.

@example
title: Writing a referee report
problem: Invented submission: "Gunk and the Whole", by an anonymous author. It argues that the principle that every object has a minimal part cannot be motivated by the claim that composition must bottom out, because the claim confuses explanatory priority with the existence of a smallest part. The author gives one argument, in section 2, that depends on the premise that explanation requires a base level. Section 3 replies to the objection that gunk makes composition unintelligible, using an analogy with the real line. The writing is clear. The paper cites two articles from 1990-2000 and nothing later. Write a referee report in five to seven sentences with a summary, an assessment, one major concern and one minor point, and a recommendation.
step: Summary: state the thesis and the argument in a sentence or two, with the key premise, to show that you understood it.
step: Assessment: say what is strong (the clarity; the distinction between explanatory priority and having a smallest part is a promising contribution).
step: Major concern: choose the concern that matters most and say where it is and how it could be fixed: the argument depends on the premise that explanation requires a base level, which is not defended; the author should argue for it or show the argument goes through without it.
step: Minor point: the literature coverage stops about two decades back; the author should check recent work on the topic. Then the recommendation: revise and resubmit, with the major concern as the condition.
answer: The paper argues that the claim that composition must bottom out cannot motivate minimal parts, because it confuses explanatory priority with a smallest part. It is clearly written and the distinction is promising. Major concern: section 2 relies on the undefended premise that explanation requires a base level; the author should defend it or show that the argument does not need it. Minor: the coverage of the literature stops with work from more than twenty years ago and should be updated. Recommendation: revise and resubmit.

@example
title: Sorting and answering a report
problem: You receive these comments on your paper. (1) "The argument in section 3 is invalid: the conclusion does not follow from P2 and P3." (2) "The author seems to think that the view she opposes is committed to X, but no defender holds X." (3) "Please cite the recent literature." (4) "I would have liked a paper on a different topic." Sort them into fatal, substantial, fixable, misreading or not actionable, and say what you would do.
step: Comment 1 is potentially fatal: check validity yourself. If it is invalid as written, either add the missing premise (and defend it) or restructure; if you disagree, show the derivation and say where the reviewer could be misreading it.
step: Comment 2 is either a misreading or a real problem with attribution: check the cited sources. If a defender does hold X, add the citation; if not, restate the target view accurately, since the objection would then be to a straw man.
step: Comment 3 is fixable: search the recent literature and cite what bears on the argument, and say in the response letter what you added.
step: Comment 4 is not actionable. In the response, thank the referee and say that the paper's scope is as stated, with no changes.
answer: (1) potentially fatal: check and repair or defend; (2) misreading or an attribution error: check and clarify or correct; (3) fixable: update the literature; (4) not actionable: note politely.

@mistakes
- Writing a report that only says whether the paper is liked ("interesting but not convincing") without saying why or where.
- Asking for a different paper instead of assessing the one submitted.
- Responding to a referee defensively, or ignoring comments one disagrees with instead of answering them with reasons.
- Letting the recommendation conflict with the comments (major unresolved flaws with "accept"), or sharing a manuscript that you received in confidence.

@practice self
prompt: Write the major-concern paragraph (about 100 words) of a referee report on this invented paper: "The paper argues that the A-theory of time is false, using the premise that the present moment would have to move along the series of times, and a moving present requires a second time dimension. The author does not consider A-theorists who deny that the present moves, saying only that such views are 'hardly A-theories'."
hint: Say where the problem is, why it matters to the thesis and what would fix it.
solution: Major concern 1. The central argument of section 2 assumes that on the A-theory the present moves along the series of times, and then argues that movement requires a second time dimension. But some A-theorists, including those who hold that the present is a feature of how things are and not something that moves, reject the premise that the present moves. The author dismisses them in one sentence ("hardly A-theories") without argument. Since the thesis is that the A-theory as such is false, the author must either argue that a non-moving present is not an A-theory, or restrict the thesis to moving-present views and say what the argument shows against the rest.
rubric: Locates the problem (the premise that the present moves, section 2 and the dismissal).
rubric: Explains why it matters to the thesis (the thesis is about the A-theory as such).
rubric: Suggests a concrete fix and keeps the tone about the paper, not the author.

@practice self
prompt: A referee wrote: "The reply to the first objection in section 4 is a non sequitur, since the distinction between the two senses of 'dependence' that the author draws is never used in the reply." You think the referee has overlooked a sentence at the start of the reply. Write a response (about 80 to 100 words) that is polite, says what you will change and does not simply restate the disagreement.
hint: Treat the misreading as evidence of unclarity and say how you will make the use of the distinction visible.
solution: We thank the referee for this comment. The reply does rely on the distinction between the two senses of "dependence": the first sentence of the reply (p. 14) says that the objection trades on the second sense. We see, however, that the connection is too easy to miss. In the revision we have moved the distinction into the opening of the reply, added a sentence saying that the objection is true only on the second sense, and cross-referred to section 2, where the distinction is introduced. We hope that the reply is now clearly an application of that distinction.
rubric: Thanks the referee and says where the distinction is used, without being defensive.
rubric: Treats the misreading as a sign of unclarity and says what will change in the text.
rubric: Is specific about the change (where it is and what it adds).

@quiz self
prompt: Write a short referee report (about 200 words) on this invented abstract: "Many hold that persons are essentially thinkers. I argue that this is false, since a person could survive in a permanent coma. The best reply is that coma cases are cases of a person who has lost the capacity to think but retains the capacity to acquire it; I reject this reply because the capacity is not in fact retained after long enough. I conclude that persons are not essentially thinkers." Include a summary, an assessment, one major concern, one minor point and a recommendation.
hint: Find the premise on which the argument rests and ask whether the best reply has been answered.
solution: Summary: The paper argues that persons are not essentially thinkers, on the ground that a person could survive in a permanent coma, and it rejects the reply that coma patients retain a capacity to think. Assessment: The thesis is clear and the target is a real view. The use of the coma case is a recognizable and legitimate move, and the discussion of the best reply is a strength. Major concern: the argument needs the premise that the person survives in a permanent coma, and the abstract does not say what supports it; those who hold that persons are essentially thinkers will deny it, and the reply to the capacity view depends on a contested claim about when capacities are lost ("long enough"). The author should defend the premise that the person survives and say what is meant by the capacity being lost. Minor: define "essentially" (modal or real-definition sense). Recommendation: revise and resubmit, conditional on supporting the survival premise.
rubric: Summarizes the thesis and the argument accurately.
rubric: Identifies a specific major concern and says why it matters and what would fix it.
rubric: Includes a minor point and a recommendation consistent with the comments.
