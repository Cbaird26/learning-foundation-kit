@article
Nobody can respond to an argument they have not understood. Yet much weak philosophical writing responds to a view the author never reconstructed: a position described by its slogan, an argument attacked at the premise that is easiest to attack. The skill of **reconstruction** is to state, in standard form, the argument that an article actually gives, strong enough that its author would say "yes, that is my argument." Only then does criticism have anything to bite on.

### Reconstructing is not summarizing, and not evaluating

A **summary** reports what a paper covers. A **reconstruction** sets out the central argument: its conclusion, its premises and the support for each. An **evaluation** says whether the argument is any good. Keep them separate. If you start criticizing while you are still reconstructing, you will weaken the argument to make it easier to refute, and your readers will not trust your criticism.

### A procedure

1. **Find the conclusion.** Look at the abstract, the end of the introduction ("I argue that...") and the final section. A paper usually has one main conclusion and several intermediate ones. Look for indicator words: "so", "therefore", "it follows that", "hence", "this shows".
2. **Find the premises.** Ask of each sentence near the conclusion: is this offered as a reason for it? Look for "since", "because", "for", "given that". Also identify **sub-arguments**: a premise may itself be defended by an argument, which becomes an intermediate conclusion C1 in your reconstruction.
3. **Separate the argument from its surroundings.** Authors state views to oppose, report objections, give illustrations and draw consequences. None of these are premises unless the author relies on them.
4. **Make hidden premises explicit.** Many arguments are **enthymemes** (arguments with a suppressed premise). Ask: what must be added for the conclusion to follow? Add the *weakest* premise that makes the argument valid and is plausibly what the author means, and mark it as supplied by you.
5. **Check validity as written.** If the conclusion follows from the premises, say so. If it does not, you have either missed a premise or found a gap in the paper; decide which, honestly, and say which.
6. **Label the support.** For each premise, note whether the author defends it (and where), takes it as common ground, or leaves it undefended.
7. **Note the dialectic.** Say whom the argument is aimed at and what the author expects the opponent to deny.

### The charity constraint

State the argument in the strongest form that the text allows. Reconstruction is often described as an exercise in **charity**, but charity here has a limit: you may not attribute to an author a premise that she rejects, or ignore a premise that she plainly relies on. Where the text is ambiguous between two readings, say so and give both, as a good referee would.

### The one-page reconstruction

A good one-page reconstruction usually contains: the author's thesis and target in a sentence; the main argument in standard form; a line on how each premise is supported; the best objection the author considers and her reply; and, if the assignment calls for it, a short separate assessment clearly marked as yours. Keep your own evaluation to a few sentences. The page is for your reader to see exactly what the article claims.

### A worked specimen

Here is a short invented passage: "Numbers cannot enter into causal relations, since they are not located in space. So no one has knowledge of numbers." The passage states one premise and one intermediate conclusion, and suppresses two premises. Set out in valid standard form:

| P1 | Numbers are not located in space. |
| P2 | Whatever is not located in space cannot enter into causal relations. |
| C1 | So numbers cannot enter into causal relations. |
| P3 | No one can have knowledge of whatever cannot enter into causal relations. |
| C | So no one has knowledge of numbers. |

Premise P1 is the author's stated support for the intermediate conclusion C1, and P2 is the hidden premise that links them. P3 is the second hidden premise: without it the conclusion simply does not follow, and it is the premise that a critic of the argument would most likely contest (for example, by denying that knowledge requires a causal connection). That is an evaluative point. In the reconstruction you say that P3 is supplied and why the passage needs it; whether P3 is true is a question for the next stage.

### When an argument will not reconstruct

Some passages do not yield a valid argument even after you add the most charitable hidden premise. Then there are three honest options: report that the text leaves a gap, report that the best available premise is implausible, or report that the passage is better read as an explanation or an illustration than as an argument. Do not invent an argument the author did not give, and do not pass over a gap in silence.

@example
title: Reconstructing a short argument
problem: Reconstruct this invented argument in standard form, valid as written: "If the statue's persistence were explained by the persistence of its clay, then the statue would survive whatever the clay survives. But the clay survives squashing, and the statue does not. So the statue's persistence is not explained by the clay's persistence."
step: Find the conclusion: "the statue's persistence is not explained by the clay's persistence." The indicator word is "So".
step: Find the premises: a conditional (if explained, then the statue would survive whatever the clay survives), a claim that the clay survives squashing, and a claim that the statue does not.
step: Write E for "the statue's persistence is explained by the clay's persistence". P1: if E, then the statue survives whatever the clay survives. P2: the clay survives squashing. P3: the statue does not survive squashing.
step: Check validity: from P1 and P2, if E then the statue survives squashing, since squashing is something the clay survives; with P3 that is false, so E is false by modus tollens. So no hidden premise is needed.
step: Note the contestable premise (an evaluative remark, kept separate): P1 can be denied by someone who says that explanation of persistence is sensitive to the sortal under which a thing persists.
answer: P1: If E, the statue survives whatever the clay survives. P2: The clay survives squashing. P3: The statue does not survive squashing. C: So not-E. The argument is valid as written. P1 is the premise a critic would press.

@example
title: Finding the hidden premise
problem: A paper says: "Time cannot be an illusion, for an illusion needs a succession of mental states, and a succession is a temporal order." Reconstruct the argument, valid as written, and say which premise is hidden.
step: Conclusion: "Time is not an illusion." Stated support: an illusion needs a succession of mental states, and succession is a temporal order.
step: Draft the premises. P1: If time were an illusion, there would be an illusion. P2: Any illusion requires a succession of mental states. P3: Any succession of mental states is a temporal order.
step: Chain them: if time were an illusion, there would be a succession of mental states (P1, P2), hence a temporal order (P3). That says there would be time, which does not yet contradict "time is an illusion" unless you add that an illusion of time presupposes that time exists.
step: Supply the hidden premise: P4: If there is a temporal order, then time is not an illusion. Then the argument is valid: if time were an illusion, then there would be a temporal order and so time would not be an illusion, so time is not an illusion. Mark P4 as supplied, and note that a critic might say it conflates the temporal order of mental states with the time of physics.
answer: The hidden premise is that the existence of a temporal order of mental states suffices for time not being an illusion (P4). With it added the argument is valid, and the contestable step is exactly that premise.

@mistakes
- Reconstructing a position rather than the argument: "The author is a nominalist" is not a premise.
- Adding a hidden premise that is far stronger than the text needs, so that the argument becomes easy to refute.
- Criticizing while reconstructing, so that the reader cannot tell what the author said from what you think.
- Treating objections that the author reports, or views she opposes, as her premises.

prompt: Consider this argument: P1: If properties are universals, then two things can share a property by being numerically identical in that respect. P2: Two things can share a property by being numerically identical in that respect. C: So properties are universals. A critic replies: "That a shared property requires identity in that respect does not show that properties are universals; the resemblance theorist can also explain sharing." Which premise or step does the reply attack?

choice: It attacks nothing; it agrees with the argument.

@practice self
prompt: Reconstruct the following invented passage as an argument in standard form, valid as written, adding any hidden premise and marking it as yours: "The world cannot contain gunk, that is, matter with no smallest parts, because a gunky object would have to be composed of parts that are themselves composed of parts without end, and nothing can be composed of an endless regress of composition."
hint: Identify the conclusion and the stated reasons; then see what is needed to link the reasons to the conclusion.
solution: P1: If the world contained gunk, then some object would be composed of an endless regress of composition. P2: Nothing can be composed of an endless regress of composition. C: So the world does not contain gunk. The argument is valid by modus tollens: if gunk then regress; no regress; so no gunk. P1 states the first clause of the passage, though it is itself supplied as a connecting premise (the passage says "a gunky object would have to be composed of parts..."), so mark it as a paraphrase of that clause. P2 is the premise that the author relies on and does not defend, and is the one an objector would deny.

prompt: Write a one-page-style reconstruction (about 200 words) of this invented article summary. Include the thesis and target, the argument in standard form, how each premise is supported, and the objection the author considers. Summary: Marguerite Oyelaran argues that ontological pluralism, the view that there are different ways of being, cannot be stated without using a univocal notion of being. She argues that to say that some things exist in one way and others in another, the pluralist must quantify over the things that exist in these ways, and the quantifier she uses must range over all of them. Oyelaran supports this by considering the pluralist's two best ways of stating the view, by using restricted quantifiers for each way of being and by using a primitive operator, and argues that each presupposes a univocal quantifier. She considers the objection that the pluralist may introduce a generic quantifier as a defined notion and replies that the definition would need a notion of being that is common to the ways. (The article is invented.)
hint: Write the argument so that it is valid, with the supported premises first and the conclusion last, then add a sentence on how the author supports the key premise.
solution: Oyelaran's thesis is that ontological pluralism cannot be stated without a univocal notion of being; her target is the pluralist who hopes to state the view in neutral terms. The argument: P1: To state ontological pluralism one must say of all the things there are that they exist in one way or another. P2: To say this one must use a quantifier ranging over all of them. P3: Such a quantifier expresses a univocal notion of being. C: So ontological pluralism cannot be stated without a univocal notion of being. Support: P2 is defended by examining two formulations, restricted quantifiers for each way of being and a primitive operator; Oyelaran argues that each presupposes an all-ranging quantifier to say that these exhaust the things there are. P3 is defended by the reply to the objection: if the pluralist defines the all-ranging quantifier from the restricted ones, the definition itself needs something common to the ways of being. Objection and reply: the pluralist may take the generic quantifier as defined, but Oyelaran answers that the defining condition requires a notion common to the ways, which is what univocity amounts to.
rubric: States the thesis and the target accurately and in the learner's own words.
rubric: Gives a valid standard-form argument that matches the summary without adding claims the summary does not state.
rubric: Says how the premises are supported, using the two formulations and the reply.
rubric: Reports the author's own objection and reply, and keeps any evaluation separate or absent.

@quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct this invented argument in standard form, valid as written, supplying and marking any hidden premise, and then, in a separate sentence labeled "Assessment", say which premise you would press. Argument: "The statue is made of clay, and the clay existed before the statue was made. But the statue did not exist before it was made. So the statue is not identical to the clay."
hint: Think of the principle that identical things have the same properties.
solution: P1: The clay existed before the statue was made. P2: The statue did not exist before it was made. P3 (supplied): If x is identical to y, then x and y have all the same properties, including the property of having existed before the statue was made. C: So the statue is not identical to the clay. Valid: if the statue were the clay, the statue would share the clay's property of having existed before the statue was made (P3, P1), contradicting P2. The supplied premise is an instance of Leibniz's Law, and I mark it as supplied. Assessment: I would press P2, since a defender of identity can say that "the statue" and "the clay" refer to the same thing, so that the statue did exist before it was made, in the sense that it existed before it was shaped as a statue.
rubric: The argument is valid as written, and the supplied premise (Leibniz's Law or an equivalent) is marked.
rubric: Does not add other premises beyond what the argument needs.
rubric: The assessment is kept separate and names a specific premise with a reason.
