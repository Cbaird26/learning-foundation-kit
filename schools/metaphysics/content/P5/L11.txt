@article
A paper is written to be read slowly, with the page open and the footnotes at hand. A talk is heard once, at the speaker's pace, by people who cannot scroll back. The skills of presenting are therefore different from the skills of writing, and a philosopher who is good at the first is not automatically good at the second. Conferences are also where much of a career's networking, testing of ideas and early feedback happens, so presenting well repays the effort.

### The kinds of event

Formats vary across the profession. Common ones include **society meetings** (such as the divisional meetings of the American Philosophical Association, and meetings of specialist societies), **specialist conferences and workshops** on a topic such as metaphysics or logic, **graduate conferences** run by students, and **invited talks** at departments. Typical features are a talk of a set length followed by questions; at some events a **commenter** (an assigned respondent who gives a short reply before the floor opens); and at some a **poster** session. Timetables are often tight, and chairs enforce them.

**Submission** works in several ways: a call for papers may ask for an abstract, an extended abstract or a full paper; review may be anonymous; acceptance rates and deadlines vary and change from year to year. Do not rely on a summary like this one. Read each call for its requirements, length limits, anonymization rules and dates, and ask your supervisor about which events suit your stage. Many programs and societies have travel funding, with its own rules: check yours.

### From paper to talk

A 20-minute slot, at a comfortable speaking pace, holds far fewer words than a paper. As a rough planning rule, many speakers use something like 120 to 140 words per minute, so a 20-minute talk is about 2,400 to 2,800 words *including* any time for slides or handout. A 7,000-word paper must therefore lose well over half its text. What to cut: nearly all exposition of the literature, most of the objections (keep the best one), all secondary arguments, and the detail that a reader could check on the page.

Build the talk around **one main argument**. The reason is the nature of listening:

| P1 | A listener to a talk cannot go back and reread what was said. |
| P2 | If a listener cannot go back and reread, then the main argument must be short enough to be held in memory in one hearing. |
| C | So the main argument of a talk must be short enough to be held in memory in one hearing. |

The argument is valid by modus ponens. The premise to test is P2: a handout or slide that displays the argument takes much of the memory load off the listener, which is why many philosophers put the premises on a handout and say the argument aloud as well. The conclusion for the speaker is practical: choose an argument with few premises, state it in standard form, number the premises, and refer to them by number.

### The structure of a good talk

1. **The thesis in the first minute.** Say what you will argue and why it matters. Do not save it for the end.
2. **The target, briefly.** State the view or argument you address, as in the reconstruction lesson, in a minute or two.
3. **Your argument, with the premises displayed.** Spend most of the time here. Use an example early: a concrete case lets listeners see the point.
4. **One objection and reply.** Choose the one that you expect, and be ready to say that you have others in the paper.
5. **Where it leaves us.** Summarize what has been shown and what has not, in a minute.

Signpost more than you would in writing: "That is the first premise; now the second", and repeat the thesis at the middle and the end. Many philosophers read from a text, which is acceptable at many events, but a text written for the eye can be hard to follow by ear; write the talk version for the ear, with shorter sentences, and rehearse it aloud with a clock. A **handout** or slides, where the event expects them, should carry the argument in standard form, key definitions and any example. Keep them to a page or a few slides, in a readable size. Check the event's norms.

### Handling questions

Questions are the point of the event: they are the free feedback that a referee would give you six months later. Prepare by writing the five questions you most fear and your answers. During the session:

- **Listen to the whole question**, and write it down if you can.
- **Restate it** in a sentence if it was long or unclear: "Let me check that I have it: you are asking whether premise two is true on the weaker reading?" This gives you time and shows that you understood.
- **Answer the question asked**, briefly, and do not use it as a prompt for a second talk.
- **Concede what should be conceded.** "That is a good point; it would require me to restrict the thesis to X" is a strength, not a weakness.
- **Say when you do not know.** "I haven't thought about that; my first reaction is..., but I would want to work through it" is honest and respected. Do not bluff.
- **Treat a hostile question as a question.** Reply to its content, calmly. If a questioner is rude, the chair can step in, and the audience notices who stays polite.

Types of question include **clarification** (what does X mean?), **objection to a premise**, **an objection to the inference**, **a counterexample**, **"have you considered..."** (which is often a pointer to literature: note the reference), and **comments** that are really speeches. Take each in turn, as in Lesson 6: which premise does it attack, and which type of reply fits?

When you are a questioner, be brief, ask a question and not a speech, ask something that helps the speaker, and let others have a turn.

### After the talk

Write down every question and comment that day, while you remember them, and use them in your revision. Thank the commenter and the organizers, and follow up on the references you were given. If your paper is going to a journal, an acknowledgment note can thank the audience, in the form the journal allows.

@example
title: Planning a 20-minute talk
problem: You have a 7,000-word paper and a 20-minute slot, and you speak at about 130 words per minute. (a) About how many words can the talk contain? (b) What fraction of the paper must be cut? (c) Give a minute-by-minute plan.
step: Words: 20 minutes times 130 words per minute is 2,600 words.
step: Cut: 7,000 minus 2,600 is 4,400 words, and 4,400 divided by 7,000 is about 63%, so nearly two-thirds of the paper must go.
step: Plan, in minutes: thesis and motivation, 2; the target view, 2; an example, 2; the main argument with premises on the handout, 8; one objection and reply, 4; where it leaves us, 2. The total is 20; in practice, shorten to leave a minute of slack.
step: Decide what to cut: exposition, secondary objections, and technical detail; keep a short pointer ("this is treated in section 5 of the paper").
answer: (a) 2,600 words; (b) about 63%; (c) 2 + 2 + 2 + 8 + 4 + 2 = 20 minutes, with the main argument getting the most time.

@example
title: Answering a question from the floor
problem: In the discussion of your talk, someone says: "I'm not sure your second premise is true: couldn't the endurantist just say that shapes are relations to times? You seem to ignore that." How should you respond?
step: Restate the question: "You are asking whether my second premise survives the reply that treats shapes as relations to times."
step: Identify what it attacks: premise 2, and the reply is of a known kind. Check whether the paper addresses it: it does, in section 4, so say that briefly.
step: Answer directly in a few sentences: "I discuss that reply in section 4. My concern is that it gives up the idea that the thing itself is bent or straight, which was what the endurantist wanted. I can say more in the paper; if you think there is a version of the reply that avoids this cost, I would like to hear it."
step: Do not claim more than you can show, and do not turn it into a mini-lecture; offer to continue after the session.
answer: Restate; identify the premise the question targets; say how the paper addresses it, in a few sentences; invite further discussion without being defensive.

@mistakes
- Reading a 7,000-word paper at speed in a 20-minute slot, instead of writing a talk.
- Saving the thesis for the end, so that listeners spend the talk guessing what it is for.
- Treating a question as an attack and answering with a long defense of the whole paper, or bluffing when you do not know.
- Packing the handout with more text than listeners can read while you speak.

@practice self
prompt: Invented thesis: "The argument from the vagueness of composition to unrestricted composition depends on the premise that existence cannot be vague." Three audience questions follow. (1) "What do you mean by 'vague existence'?" (2) "Isn't that premise just obviously true?" (3) "Haven't you ignored the reply that vagueness is purely semantic?" Write a short reply (about 40 words each) to each, naming the type of question.
hint: A clarification question needs a definition; an objection to a premise needs an argument; a "have you considered" needs a pointer to where you address it, or an honest admission.
solution: (1) Clarification: "By vague existence I mean that there are cases in which it is indeterminate whether something exists, not merely cases where a word like 'table' is vague. I'll say more on the handout." (2) Objection to a premise: "Many find it plausible, but I think the plausibility comes from confusing it with the claim that we can't be unsure whether something exists; I argue in the third section that nothing independent supports it." (3) A "have you considered" question: "The semantic view is the main rival and I discuss it briefly in the objections section, but I take your point that it deserves more; I'll expand it in the paper."
rubric: Each reply is brief and answers the question asked.
rubric: The types are identified correctly: clarification, premise objection, and "have you considered".
rubric: The replies are honest and non-defensive, conceding where fitting.

prompt: Deliverable exercise. Write a plan for a 20-minute talk on a paper of your choice (or an invented paper in metaphysics): the thesis in one sentence, the main argument in standard form with at most four premises (as it would appear on the handout), a minute-by-minute outline that adds up to 20 minutes with a minute of slack, and the one objection you will include.

Outline: 0-2 thesis and motivation; 2-4 the target objection; 4-6 an example of a necessary truth and its truthmaker; 6-13 the argument with premises on the handout; 13-17 one objection (that the example misdescribes truthmaking) and reply; 17-19 where it leaves us; 19-20 slack.

rubric: The argument is in standard form, valid as written and has no more than four premises.
rubric: The timing adds up to 20 minutes or less and gives most time to the argument.
rubric: One objection is chosen and justified as the one most likely to be raised.

@quiz self
prompt: Design a one-page handout for a 20-minute talk on this invented argument: "If grounding is irreflexive, then nothing grounds itself. Some philosophers propose a case in which a fact seems to ground itself. If the case is genuine, then grounding is not irreflexive. The case is not genuine, because the fact in it has a different ground. So grounding is irreflexive." List what goes on the handout: the argument in valid standard form (check that it is valid), a key definition, the example, and one sentence of the objection to be discussed.
hint: Put the argument in standard form first and check validity, then choose the content that the listener needs to see.
solution: Handout. Definition: grounding is irreflexive iff no fact grounds itself. Argument: P1: If the fact in the case has a different ground, then the case is not genuine. P2: The fact in the case has a different ground. C1: So the case is not genuine (P1, P2). P3 (supplied): If grounding is not irreflexive, then the proposed case is genuine (that is, the proposed case is the only candidate for a self-grounding fact). C: So grounding is irreflexive. (Valid: C1 by modus ponens; C by modus tollens on P3 and C1. P3 is supplied, and it is the premise a critic would press, since the failure of one proposed counterexample does not by itself show irreflexivity. The converse, that a genuine case would show grounding not to be irreflexive, is stated in the passage but is not needed for validity.) Example: the proposed case, named "the case of the self-grounding fact", stated in two lines, with its alternative ground. Objection: "the claim that the fact has a different ground is just what a defender of the case would deny", with the reply that the burden is on the proponent to say what the fact grounds it in.
rubric: The argument is in standard form, valid as written and short enough for a handout.
rubric: Includes the definition, the named example and the objection, and nothing else of length.
rubric: The handout is realistically one page and supports the spoken argument.
