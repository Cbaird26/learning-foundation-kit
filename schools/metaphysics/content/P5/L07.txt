@article
Philosophy has a reputation for obscurity, and some of it is deserved. But the obscurity that matters most to a referee is a different kind: a claim that cannot be tested because its terms are unfixed, an argument that seems valid only because a word shifts meaning in the middle, a case that is so elaborate that the reader cannot tell which feature is doing the work. Clarity is not a matter of style. A claim that is not clear enough to be wrong is not yet a claim.

### Definitions

There are three kinds of definition, and a paper should say which it is giving.

- A **stipulative** definition introduces a term for your own use: "Call a property *thin* iff it is had by something in virtue of nothing but its location." It cannot be false, though it can be useless or misleading, and you must keep to it.
- A **lexical** (reportive) definition says how a term is used in the literature or in ordinary speech, and can be wrong: "As the term is used in the literature on grounding, 'fundamental' means ungrounded." Cite where it is used so.
- An **analytic** definition (an analysis) claims to give the necessary and sufficient conditions for something to fall under a concept: "x knows that p iff ...". It can be tested by counterexample, and the counterexamples are the paper.

State which sense you are using when it matters, and do not slide from one to another: a stipulative definition cannot be refuted by a counterexample, and an analysis cannot be defended by saying "that is how I use the word".

### Testing a definition

For a definition of the form "x is F iff C", test both directions. **Sufficiency:** is there something that satisfies C and is not F? That is a counterexample to the "if" direction. **Necessity:** is there something that is F and does not satisfy C? That is a counterexample to the "only if" direction. Also test the edge cases: the empty case, the case where x is y, the case with infinitely many items. Many reported definitions fail only at an edge.

### Equivocation and scope

An argument **equivocates** when a term has two senses and the argument is valid only if it has one sense throughout. Here is an invented example:

| P1 | Whatever is fundamental is ungrounded. |
| P2 | Quarks are fundamental. |
| C | So quarks are ungrounded. |

As written the argument is valid. But if "fundamental" in P1 means "ungrounded" (a metaphysical notion) and in P2 means "a basic constituent in current physics", then P1 is trivially true and P2 does not say that quarks are ungrounded: the argument has four terms. The fix is to distinguish the two senses, say F1 for ungrounded and F2 for basic in physics, restate P2 as "quarks are F1", and notice that this is now the controversial premise that needed defending all along.

**Scope ambiguities** work similarly. "Every event has a cause" can mean that for each event there is some cause or that some one cause is the cause of all events; "possibly, everything is contingent" can be read with the possibility operator inside or outside a quantifier. In metaphysics the ambiguity between **de re** and **de dicto** readings of modal claims is a common source. Write sentences so that one reading is forced, and use symbols where they remove the doubt.

### Precision in claims

Loose quantifiers hide disagreement. "Philosophers think that..." and "this is widely accepted" are claims about the field that need a citation or a hedge. "Some", "most" and "all" are different claims. "Is" can mean identity, predication, constitution or existence. Watch for **hedges** that conceal commitment ("it might be thought that", "in a sense"): use them only when you can say in what sense. Avoid **intensifiers** ("clearly", "obviously", "of course") that signal that you do not have an argument for the next sentence.

### Examples and cases

A good example is **minimal** (it contains only what the point needs), **controlled** (one feature varies and the rest are held fixed) and **motivated** (the reader can see what the case is a test of). A counterexample must satisfy the analysis's stated conditions and fail the analyzed concept for a reason a reader can see. Give an example a name, so that you can refer to it, and then use the same name each time. Resist the temptation to build the example until it is vivid: details that do no work give a critic something to dispute.

### Plain prose

Technical terms should be kept **fixed**: once you call something "the grounding relation", do not call it "the explanatory link" in the next paragraph for the sake of variety, since a reader will suppose that you mean two things. Prefer short sentences for hard points. Introduce a term only when you will use it at least several times. Put the subject and verb near the front of a sentence. Use a formal notation when it removes a doubt and not otherwise; a symbol that the reader must look up costs attention. Read your draft aloud: where you stumble, the reader will.

### A test of precision

Take any sentence of your draft and ask: what would show this to be false? If you cannot answer, the sentence is not yet a claim. If you can, you have found the evidence that the reader needs, and you may need to supply it.

problem: Rewrite this invented passage in clear prose: "The relation of dependence that grounds the explanatory order is, of course, fundamental in the sense that it is clearly not itself derivative, and this is sometimes thought to be ontological dependence, but that is not what is meant, since it is plainly something else which is more like explanation, though it is not exactly that either."
step: Find the terms: "dependence", "fundamental", "derivative", "ontological dependence", "explanation". Decide what the author means by each: the passage seems to say that the grounding relation is not itself grounded, and that it is neither ontological dependence nor explanation.
step: Replace the intensifiers ("of course", "clearly", "plainly"): they signal missing arguments, so either supply an argument or delete the word.
step: Split into short sentences and fix the terms: use "grounding" for the relation throughout, and define "fundamental" as "ungrounded" for this paper.
step: Write: "I use 'fundamental' to mean 'ungrounded'. On one view, the grounding relation is fundamental in this sense. Some take it to be ontological dependence; I argue that it is not, since grounding connects facts and ontological dependence connects entities. It is also not the same as explanation, though it supports explanation."
answer: "I use 'fundamental' to mean 'ungrounded'. On one view, grounding is fundamental in this sense. Some take it to be ontological dependence; I argue that it is not, since grounding connects facts and ontological dependence connects entities. It is also not the same as explanation, though it supports explanation."

@example
title: Testing a definition
problem: Test this definition: "A mereological sum of x and y is an object z such that x and y are parts of z." Is it sufficient? Is it necessary? Repair it if needed.
step: Test sufficiency: is there a z with x and y as parts that is not their sum? Yes: a table that has a leg x and a top y as parts also has other parts (the screws). The definition counts the whole table as a sum of the leg and the top, but the sum of the leg and the top should have nothing else in it.
step: Test necessity: in the case where x is y, the sum is x itself, and "x and y are parts of z" is satisfied with z = x if parthood is reflexive, so the edge case is fine on that assumption; state the assumption.
step: Repair: require that z have x and y as parts and that every part of z overlap x or overlap y. This excludes the screws as parts of the sum, since a screw overlaps neither the leg nor the top.
step: Retest: the repaired definition says that the sum is the thing that has x and y as parts and nothing disjoint from both. State it as: z is a sum of x and y iff x and y are parts of z and every part of z overlaps x or overlaps y.
answer: The original definition is not sufficient (too many things satisfy it). The repaired definition adds that every part of z overlaps x or y.

@mistakes
- Sliding from a stipulative definition to a claim about how the word is used in the literature, or defending an analysis by saying that this is how you use the word.
- Equivocating on a technical term between a metaphysical and an everyday or scientific sense.
- Using "clearly" or "obviously" where an argument is needed.
- Varying technical terms for style, so that readers suppose that you refer to different things.

prompt: Test this invented definition: "x is a sibling of y iff x and y have the same mother." Give (a) a counterexample to sufficiency, (b) a counterexample to necessity, and (c) a repaired definition, saying which notion of sibling you adopt.
