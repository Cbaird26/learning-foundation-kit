@article
Most of what makes a published paper good was done in revision. First drafts are for finding out what you think; the second and third are for finding out what you can show. Revising is therefore not proofreading. It is the discipline of treating your draft as someone else's work: reading it as a referee would, deciding what must change, and changing it in the right order.

### Revise top-down

The order matters, because work done at a lower level is wasted if the higher level then changes. Here is the argument:

| P1 | Whatever polishing is applied to a paragraph that is later cut is wasted. |
| P2 | If you polish before you fix the argument, then a polished paragraph may be cut when the argument is fixed. |
| C | So if you polish before you fix the argument, some of the polishing may be wasted. |

The argument is valid. It does not say that polishing is worthless; it says that polishing should come last. The order is therefore: **argument** (is the thesis right, are the premises defended, are the objections answered?), **structure** (does the order of sections serve the argument?), **paragraphs** (does each have one point, with a topic sentence?), **sentences** (clarity, concision, fixed terms), and **surface** (citations, formatting, typos).

### Revision at each level

**Argument.** Reconstruct your own argument in standard form, as in Lesson 3. If you cannot, the draft has a problem that no revision of prose will fix. Check each premise: is it defended or flagged as an assumption? Check the objections: have you answered the strongest? If a referee or reader has found a gap, decide whether it is fatal, or fixable by adding an argument, or fixable by narrowing the thesis. Narrowing is often the best fix: a smaller claim fully defended beats a larger claim half defended.

**Structure.** Make a reverse outline (Lesson 5). Move material so that each section depends only on what came before it. Cut anything that does not serve the thesis, even if it is true and you like it; keep it in a file for another paper.

**Paragraphs.** Each paragraph has one point, stated early. Merge paragraphs that make the same point and split any that make two.

**Sentences.** Remove throat-clearing ("It is important to note that"), intensifiers, passive constructions that hide who does what, and variations in technical terms. Read aloud.

**Surface.** Check every citation, quotation and page number against the sources (Lesson 8), the reference list against the in-text citations, and the manuscript against the journal's formatting and anonymization requirements.

### Cutting to length

Word limits vary and are sometimes enforced strictly; read the journal's. To cut: first, remove anything that does not serve the thesis; second, shorten exposition of other people's views to what the argument needs; third, compress objections to the strongest; fourth, tighten sentences. Cut in proportion: the exposition usually has the most slack.

### Using feedback

Feedback comes from peers, supervisors, workshops and referees. **Triage** it: group comments into those that bear on the thesis or argument, those about structure, those about clarity and those about detail. Two readers who raise the same point are a strong signal. A comment that surprises you ("this is not what I meant") shows where the draft is unclear. Do not revise on every comment; decide what the paper is trying to do and use comments that serve that. Then write a **revision plan**: a short list in priority order, each item saying what will change, where and roughly how many words it will take.

### The abstract and the title

An **abstract** is usually a short paragraph, often of about 100 to 200 words, though journals set their own limits. A good abstract states the question, the thesis, the main argument or method, and the upshot, in that order, in plain words and without citations or abbreviations that are not explained. Write it after revising, since the thesis may have changed. A **title** should say what the paper is about, ideally naming the view or argument, and should avoid vague puns that a search engine cannot match to its topic.

### Knowing when to stop

A paper is never perfect; it is ready when its argument is complete for its stated thesis, the strongest objections are answered, and a reader can follow it without you present. Ask a reader who has not seen it to try. If they can say what the thesis and the main argument are, you are ready to submit. Keep a record of versions, and keep the referee comments and your responses, since they will help with later papers.

@example
title: Writing an abstract
problem: Write an abstract of about 100 words for this invented paper: it argues that the claim that persons are numerically identical to animals (animalism) faces a problem from the possibility of a person's brain being transplanted, because the claim must say that the person goes with the animal's remainder, not with the brain. The author argues that animalism can avoid the problem by distinguishing the animal from its organs, and defends this against the objection that the distinction makes the animal depend on its organs.
step: State the question: what does animalism say about a brain transplant?
step: State the thesis: animalism faces the problem but can avoid it by distinguishing an animal from its organs.
step: State the main argument: the distinction means that the person goes with the animal even if a vital organ is transplanted.
step: State the upshot and the objection handled: the distinction does not make the animal depend on its organs. Write in plain words and no citations.
answer: Animalism says that each of us is numerically identical to a human animal. A familiar problem is that, if a person's brain were transplanted, the animalist must say that the person stays with the remaining animal and not with the brain. I argue that animalists can avoid the problem by distinguishing an animal from its organs: the animal remains the same animal when an organ is replaced, and the person goes with it. I defend the distinction against the objection that it makes the animal depend on its organs, and I conclude that the transplant case is not a counterexample to animalism.

@example
title: Cutting to a word limit
problem: A draft is 8,650 words and the limit is 7,500 words including notes. The author decides to take 60% of the necessary cut from the exposition and the rest from the objections. How many words should come from the objections?
step: Compute the excess: 8,650 minus 7,500 is 1,150 words to cut.
step: Take 60% of the excess from the exposition: 0.6 times 1,150 is 690 words.
step: The rest comes from the objections: 1,150 minus 690 is 460 words.
step: Check: 690 plus 460 is 1,150, which brings 8,650 down to 7,500, and decide in each section what to cut (examples, repetition, sentences that restate), keeping the argument intact.
answer: 460 words (and 690 words from the exposition).

@mistakes
- Polishing sentences before the argument and structure are settled, then cutting the polished passages.
- Revising on every comment instead of deciding what the paper is trying to do and using the comments that serve it.
- Writing the abstract before the paper's thesis is final, so that it describes a draft that no longer exists.
- Cutting the objections to meet a word limit while keeping long exposition of other people's views.

The argument is: if every truth has a truthmaker, then 'there are no unicorns' has one; the only candidates are absences or totality facts; absences are not entities and totality facts are mysterious; so there is no truthmaker for it; so the principle is false. solution: Summary: The paper argues that the principle that every truth has a truthmaker is false, because negative existentials such as "there are no unicorns" have no acceptable truthmaker. Assessment: The question is well chosen and the argument is stated clearly. The paper's strength is that it commits to a thesis. Major concerns: (1) The argument is not valid as stated: from "absences are not entities and totality facts are mysterious" it does not follow that neither can be a truthmaker, so the conclusion that the principle is false needs a premise that nothing that is not an entity, or that is mysterious, can be a truthmaker, and that premise is only asserted. The author should argue for it or weaken the conclusion. (2) The rival view that truth supervenes on being is relegated to a footnote, but it is the most natural alternative that a truthmaker theorist would offer; it should be discussed in the body. Minor: about half of the paper is history; much of it could be cut in favor of (1) and (2). Recommendation: major revision.

rubric: Notes the proportion of space on history and suggests a fix.
rubric: The recommendation is consistent with the comments.
