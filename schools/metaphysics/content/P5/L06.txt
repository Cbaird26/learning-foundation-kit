@article
The worst feature of an unsympathetic referee report is often that it says "you have not considered the obvious objection that...". An objection section is a paper's insurance against that sentence, but more than that, it is where the paper shows that its thesis survives pressure. Adding an objections section does not help if the objections are weak, unnamed or mischaracterized. The craft is to choose the right objections, state them in a form their proponents would accept, and reply in a way that actually matches what the objection attacks.

### What an objection attacks

Given an argument in standard form, an objection can go four ways.

- **Attack a premise.** Deny that a premise is true, or show that it is unsupported. This is the most common.
- **Attack the inference.** Deny that the conclusion follows from the premises: the argument is invalid as stated, or it equivocates.
- **Attack a presupposition.** Deny something the argument takes for granted, such as that the question is well formed or that a term has a single sense.
- **Attack the conclusion directly.** Offer a case or a further argument that the conclusion is false. This works as a counterexample to the thesis, and its force depends on how strongly one believes the conclusion false.

Distinguish **rebutting** an argument (giving reason to think its conclusion false) from **undercutting** it (giving reason to think its premises do not support the conclusion, without saying the conclusion is false). The two require different replies.

### Types of reply

A reply to an objection is of one of these kinds, and good papers know which they are making.

- **Deny.** The objection's key premise is false, and you say why.
- **Distinguish.** The objection trades on two senses of a term; on the sense in which its premise is true, it does not touch your thesis.
- **Concede and limit.** Grant that the objection shows something, and restrict the thesis so that it survives.
- **Bite the bullet.** Accept the objection's counterintuitive consequence and argue that its cost is worth paying.
- **Parity.** Show that the objection applies equally to the objector's own view, so it cannot favor the objector. Parity shows that the objection does not discriminate; it does not by itself show that your thesis is true.
- **Dissolve.** Show that the objection rests on a mistake about what your thesis says, usually by clarifying the thesis.

Each reply has a **cost**, and you should state it. A bitten bullet is a commitment; a limitation shrinks the claim; a parity reply may leave both views in difficulty. A reader trusts an author who says "this is what the reply costs me, and here is why I accept it."

### Choosing objections

Include the objections that a well-informed referee would raise first, not the ones you can answer best. Ask three questions: Who would object, and could I name them? What would they say in their strongest form? What does the objection force me to give up if it is right? If the strongest objection is one you cannot answer, say so: a paper that states a hard objection honestly and shows that the thesis survives in a restricted form is stronger than one that pretends the objection is not there.

Attribute objections accurately. "One might object" is acceptable for a natural objection that no one has pressed in print, but where an objection appears in the literature, cite it. Never invent an opponent to defeat, and never make an objection weaker than its proponents' version.

### The shape of an objection section

For each objection, a section typically does four things: **state** the objection as a short argument in standard form; **show its force** by saying what it would show if it succeeded; **reply**, naming the type of reply; and **say what remains**, i.e. what the thesis is now committed to or what has been limited. Take the objections in the order that makes the dialectic clearest, usually the most forceful first.

### Matching objection to premise

Consider an argument against the thesis that composition is restricted:

| P1 | If composition is restricted, then there is a sharp boundary between the cases in which composition occurs and the cases in which it does not. |
| P2 | There is no sharp boundary between such cases. |
| C | So composition is not restricted. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). A reply that denies P2, by accepting a sharp boundary that we cannot know, is a **deny** reply. A reply that distinguishes sharp from knowable boundaries, and says that P1 requires only a sharp boundary while P2 is plausible only of a knowable one, is a **distinction** that also attacks P2, but by a different route (it charges P2 with equivocating). A reply that says the universalist also faces a sharp boundary (between where things exist and where they do not, if existence is sharp) is a **parity** reply. Notice that each reply attacks a particular premise or the argument's relevance, and that they differ in type, which is why it matters to be able to say which premise a reply attacks and what kind of reply it is.

@example
title: Matching replies to premises
problem: Argument against a thesis (invented): P1: If properties are sets of possible individuals, then coextensive-in-all-worlds properties are identical. P2: Being a triangle and being trilateral are necessarily coextensive and are not identical. C: So properties are not sets of possible individuals. Three replies: (a) "They are identical; the intuition that they differ is an artifact of different modes of presentation." (b) "P1 is true only if sets are individuated extensionally, and that is not what I mean by set." (c) "Every theory has some pair it cannot distinguish; the rival theory has its own such pair." Say what each reply attacks and name its type.
step: Check validity first: P1 and P2 together give the conclusion by modus tollens (if properties were such sets, necessarily coextensive properties would be identical; these are necessarily coextensive and not identical; so not). The argument is valid, so each reply must attack a premise, or the relevance of the argument.
step: Reply (a) denies the second conjunct of P2, that the two properties are not identical. It is a deny reply, and it pays the cost of explaining away the apparent difference.
step: Reply (b) attacks P1, by distinguishing two readings of "set", extensional and intensional. It is a distinction reply. Its cost is to show that the intensional reading is still a set theory of properties and not a covert relabelling of properties as primitives.
step: Reply (c) attacks neither premise: it is a parity reply. It does not show that P1 or P2 is false; it says that the objection does not tell against this theory in particular, and the objector must still say why the pair is a cost for this theory and not for the other.
answer: (a) denies P2 (deny); (b) attacks P1 (distinction); (c) attacks the objection's dialectical force (parity).

@example
title: Writing an objection and reply
problem: Thesis (invented): "The truthmaker principle is false for negative existentials." Write one objection, stated fairly in standard form, and a reply of one type, with its cost.
step: Choose the objection a well-informed critic would raise: that without truthmakers for negative existentials, the principle that truth depends on being is abandoned for a whole class of truths, which looks ad hoc.
step: State it in standard form: P1: If the truthmaker principle is false for negative existentials, then truth for them does not depend on being. P2: Truth for every class of truths depends on being. C: So the truthmaker principle is not false for negative existentials. It is valid by modus tollens.
step: Reply by distinguishing: truth depends on being in two ways, by having a truthmaker or by the absence of a falsemaker; negative existentials are true because nothing makes them false, and truth still depends on being, namely on what there is and is not. So P1 is false: denial of the principle for negatives is compatible with dependence on being.
step: State the cost: the reply replaces "truthmaker" with a weaker notion, so the thesis is that the strong principle fails and a weaker one holds. The reader should be told that this is a concession to the objector, who may say that the weaker principle is the one that mattered.
answer: Objection (P1, P2, so not-thesis) answered by a distinction between two ways truth depends on being, attacking P1, at the cost of weakening the principle that the thesis denies.

@mistakes
- Replying to a weaker version of the objection than its proponents would give (a straw man).
- Answering with a reply that does not match what the objection attacks: denying a premise the objection does not use.
- Treating a parity reply as if it showed that the thesis is true.
- Never saying what a reply costs: every reply has a cost, and silence reads as not having noticed it.

@practice self
prompt: Write one objection to the following invented thesis, stated in standard form and valid as written, and a reply to it of a named type, stating what the reply costs. Thesis: "Dispositional essentialism does not need to postulate laws of nature as further entities."
hint: Think of what a critic who believes in laws would say; state it as premises and a conclusion, then choose a type of reply.
solution: Objection: P1: If dispositional essentialism does not need laws as further entities, then the regularities in nature are explained by the dispositions alone. P2: The regularities in nature are not explained by the dispositions alone, since dispositions manifest only given suitable stimulus and absent interference, and the claim that these conditions are regular needs something more. C: So dispositional essentialism does need laws as further entities, or something that plays their role. Valid by modus tollens. Reply (distinction): P2 treats the conditions of manifestation as a further regularity needing explanation, but on the dispositionalist view the conditions are part of the specification of the disposition, so there is no extra regularity. Cost: the reply makes the stimulus and interference conditions part of each disposition's essence, which is a strong commitment about dispositions, and one that critics think makes the view hard to test.
rubric: The objection is stated as a valid argument and is one that a critic might actually give.
rubric: The reply names its type correctly and matches what the objection attacks.
rubric: The cost of the reply is stated and is a real cost.

@quiz self
prompt: Take the argument: P1: If time travel is possible, then someone could kill their own grandfather before their parent is conceived. P2: No one could kill their own grandfather before their parent is conceived. C: So time travel is not possible. Write three replies, one denying a premise, one distinguishing senses, and one by parity or concession, and say what each costs.
hint: Think about what "could" means in each premise: compossibility with the facts as stated, or ability in a relevant sense.
solution: Deny P1: if time travel is possible, consistency of the timeline requires that the attempt fail; so there is no possibility of success; cost: you must explain why the traveler seems able, and the reply says only that her ability is limited by facts about the world. Distinguish: "could" in P1 means that nothing about her abilities stops her, while in P2 it means that the whole story is consistent; on that reading the argument equivocates; cost: you must say how the abilities and the consistency are related, which opens the problem of what ability is. Concede and limit: concede that the argument shows that time travel with free change of the past is impossible, and limit the thesis to time travel without changing the past; cost: you give up the more dramatic version of time travel.
rubric: The three replies are of the three different types asked for and each matches a premise or step.
rubric: Names the equivocation on "could" correctly.
rubric: States a cost for each reply.
