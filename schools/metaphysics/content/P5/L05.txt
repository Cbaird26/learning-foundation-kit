@article
A reader opens your paper after a long day and has fifteen other things to read. In the first page they decide whether to continue, and in the rest they decide whether to trust you. You help them do both by saying early and exactly what you will show, by arranging the paper so that each part is visibly needed for that, and by telling them, at each turn, where they are. This is structure and **signposting**. It is not decoration; it is how an argument becomes followable.

### The thesis statement

A good thesis statement has four features.

- **Commitment.** It asserts something that could be denied.
- **Precision.** Its key terms are fixed, and its quantifiers are right: "no reply of kind K works", not "replies of kind K have problems".
- **Scope.** It says what it covers and what it leaves aside: "for the case of artifacts, I argue...", "granting for the sake of argument that...".
- **Support.** It signals the kind of argument: by counterexample, by a premise attack, by a distinction.

Compare "Grounding is important for metaphysics" (a topic) with "I argue that the claim that grounding is irreflexive cannot be supported by the intuition that explanation is asymmetric, because that intuition is explained by a feature of explanations that grounding lacks" (a thesis with precision, scope and support).

### The standard architecture

Most research articles in metaphysics follow a recognizable plan, which readers expect. Journals and subfields vary, so treat this as a default, not a rule.

1. **Introduction.** Motivation (why the question matters), the thesis, a statement of what is new, and a **roadmap**: a sentence or two on the order of the sections.
2. **Setting up the target.** The view or argument you address, stated in standard form and fairly (see the reconstruction lesson).
3. **The main argument.** Your case, with its premises defended in turn.
4. **Objections and replies.** The strongest objections and your answers (the next lesson).
5. **Conclusion.** What has been shown, what has not (the limits), and where the question goes next. A conclusion is not a place for new arguments.

A rough guide is that the introduction and conclusion are short, and the main argument and the objections together take most of the paper. How you divide the space depends on the paper; the guide for a 7,000-word paper might be about 500 to 700 words for the introduction and about 2,000 for the main argument, but such numbers are a planning tool, not a standard.

### Signposting

**Signposts** are the sentences that tell the reader where they are and why. A roadmap sentence ("In section 2 I state the argument; in section 3 I present my objection; in section 4 I reply to two replies") tells the reader the order. A **topic sentence** at the start of each paragraph states the paragraph's point, so that a reader who read only topic sentences would see the shape of the argument. **Transitions** ("This objection assumes that... but...") state the logical relation between steps: a consequence, a contrast, an example. A signpost that says only "Next, I turn to..." names a topic; a better one states a relation: "This reply has a cost: it requires...".

Here is the argument for stating the thesis early:

| P1 | If a reader does not know what the paper is trying to show, then she cannot judge whether the material in a paragraph is relevant. |
| P2 | If the thesis is stated only in the conclusion, then the reader does not know what the paper is trying to show while reading the body. |
| P3 | If a reader cannot judge whether the material in a paragraph is relevant, the paper is hard to follow. |
| C | So if the thesis is stated only in the conclusion, the paper is hard to follow. |

The argument is valid: P2 and P1 give that a late thesis means the reader cannot judge relevance, and P3 gives the rest. The premise most open to dispute is P2; a title or abstract can tell the reader the thesis even if the body does not, which is itself a reason to state the thesis in the title or abstract and again in the introduction.

### The reverse outline

When you have a draft, write a **reverse outline**: for each paragraph, write one sentence stating its point (not its topic), then read the list. The questions are: Does the list tell the story of the argument? Is there a paragraph whose point does not serve the thesis (cut or move it)? Is there a step that the argument needs and that no paragraph supplies (add it)? Do any two paragraphs make the same point (merge them)? The reverse outline is the quickest way to see a draft's structure, and professional writers use it in revision (Lesson 10).

### Common structural faults

Common faults include a long introduction that is really a literature survey; an exposition of the opposing view that runs longer than the argument against it; a main argument that is announced but never stated in standard form; objections added in a last section as an afterthought; and a conclusion that merely repeats the introduction. Each of these has the same cure: decide what the reader must believe at each point in order to accept the next, and build the structure around that.

@example
title: Budgeting and outlining a paper
problem: A student has the thesis "The reply to the grounding objection that treats grounding as a primitive relation cannot answer the objection that primitives must be explanatorily idle." The paper is capped at 7,000 words. Draft an outline with a word budget for each part.
step: Intro (about 600 words): motivation, the thesis, what is new, a roadmap sentence.
step: Section 1, the target (about 1,000 words): the grounding objection and the primitivist reply, in standard form.
step: Section 2, the main argument (about 2,200 words): the argument that a primitive must do explanatory work to earn its place, and that grounding as a primitive does none.
step: Section 3, objections and replies (about 2,400 words): two objections, one about the nature of explanation and one about parity with other primitives, with replies.
step: Conclusion (about 500 words), and check the budget: 600 plus 1,000 plus 2,200 plus 2,400 plus 500 equals 6,700, leaving about 300 words of slack for the bibliography-adjacent notes and the inevitable overrun.
answer: Intro 600; target 1,000; main argument 2,200; objections and replies 2,400; conclusion 500; total 6,700 of 7,000.

@example
title: A reverse outline
problem: A draft has these paragraph points. (1) Composition is a central topic. (2) The argument from vagueness against restricted composition, in standard form. (3) The author's objection that the argument's second premise is unmotivated. (4) A long history of the problem of the many. (5) A reply to the objection that the second premise is supported by sorites cases. (6) The conclusion that restricted composition survives. Which paragraph should be cut or moved, and what is missing?
step: Check each against the thesis, which is that the second premise of the argument is unmotivated. Paragraph 1 states a topic and not a point; paragraph 2 is the target; 3 is the thesis; 5 is a reply; 6 is the conclusion.
step: Paragraph 4 is a history that does not serve the thesis: cut it, or move a sentence of it to the introduction if the history is needed for motivation.
step: Look for the missing step: paragraph 3 asserts that the premise is unmotivated, but no paragraph gives the argument for that; add a paragraph before 5 that argues for it.
step: Revise paragraph 1 so that it states a point: why the vagueness argument matters for restricted composition.
answer: Cut or move paragraph 4; add a paragraph that argues that the second premise is unmotivated; turn paragraph 1 into a motivated point.

@mistakes
- Hiding the thesis until the conclusion, as if the paper were a mystery story.
- Writing a roadmap that lists topics ("then I discuss objections") instead of stating what each section will show.
- Spending more space on exposition of the opposing view than on your own argument.
- Using the conclusion to introduce new arguments or objections.
