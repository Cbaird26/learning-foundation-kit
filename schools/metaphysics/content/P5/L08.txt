@article
In philosophy the main thing you produce is a chain of reasoning in which other people's ideas play a part. Citation records which parts are theirs and where to find them. It lets readers check your report, lets authors receive the credit that is their due, and keeps the conversation of the field honest. Violations of academic integrity are among the few mistakes in a philosophical career that cannot be undone by a better argument, so it pays to learn the norms exactly and early.

### What to cite

Cite when you use **another person's idea, argument, distinction, term of art or example**, whether or not you use their words. Cite when you **report what an author says or argues**, so the reader can check your report. Cite the **source of a quotation** with a location. Cite **background** for claims that a reader would reasonably doubt or want to follow up. You need not cite each use of what is common knowledge in the field (that Leibniz's Law is a principle of identity, or that the standard argument for X has a certain form); the line depends on your audience, and when in doubt, cite.

Cite what you have read. If you rely on a source only through another author's report of it, say so ("as reported in..."), and prefer to find and read the original.

### Quotation, paraphrase and patchwriting

Philosophers paraphrase far more than they quote. **Paraphrase** restates the content in your own words and your own sentence structure, and still needs a citation. Quote only when the exact wording matters (when you will analyze it, when its phrasing is the point, or when you want to be sure that you are not putting words in an author's mouth). A **quotation** must be exact, in quotation marks or set off as a block, and give a location such as a page number. Use brackets for words you add and ellipses for words you omit, and do not let either change the meaning.

**Patchwriting** is the habit of changing a few words in a source's sentences, or keeping its structure while substituting synonyms. It counts as plagiarism even when a citation is present, because the wording and structure are not yours, and many institutions treat it as a breach. The safe method is to read the passage, close the source, write what the passage argues from memory and then check your version against the source for accuracy.

### Citation styles

Journals and publishers require particular styles: author-date, notes with a bibliography, and others. They vary, and they change. Do not memorize one; read the style guide in the journal's instructions to authors, and use a **reference manager** to keep consistent records. The important norms are the same across styles: author, year, title, venue, pages, and enough information to find the source.

### Breaches of integrity

- **Plagiarism**: presenting another's words or ideas as your own. It includes uncited paraphrase and uncited use of an argument, not only copied sentences.
- **Self-plagiarism and duplicate publication**: reusing your own published or submitted text without disclosure, or submitting substantially the same paper to several journals at once. Most journals prohibit simultaneous submission, but policies differ, so read each journal's rules, and tell an editor about related work of yours.
- **Fabrication and falsification**: inventing a source, a quotation or a page number, or misreporting what a source says. This includes a citation that you took from a list or a software tool's output without checking that the work exists and says what is claimed.
- **Misrepresentation**: attributing to an author a view she does not hold, or leaving out a qualification that changes her claim.
- **Breach of confidentiality**: using or sharing a manuscript you received as a referee. Refereed manuscripts are confidential unless the journal says otherwise.
- **Authorship and credit**: philosophy papers are often single-authored, but conventions for co-authorship and acknowledgment vary: credit people who gave substantial comments, in an acknowledgment note, and follow the venue's rules about authorship.

**Tools that generate text.** Policies on the use of writing and generative tools vary by journal, program and course, and they are changing. Read the policy that applies, follow it and disclose what it requires. A tool's output is not a source: any claim or citation that you take from it must be verified against the actual publication, and you are responsible for the accuracy and originality of your paper.

### Why uncited paraphrase is plagiarism

The point is sometimes misunderstood by students who think that plagiarism is copying. Here is the argument:

| P1 | If a passage of yours reports another person's idea, in any wording, then you are using that person's idea. |
| P2 | If you use another person's idea, then you owe that person acknowledgment. |
| P3 | If you owe someone acknowledgment and you give none, you commit plagiarism. |
| C | So if a passage of yours reports another person's idea, in any wording, and you give no acknowledgment, you commit plagiarism. |

The argument is valid: chain P1 and P2 to get that you owe acknowledgment, and P3 gives plagiarism. The premise that a critic would test is P2, with the exception of common knowledge: an idea that is the shared background of the field is not "another person's idea" in the relevant sense. That is why the common-knowledge judgment matters and why, when you are unsure whether something is common knowledge, you cite.

### Anonymity and review

Many philosophy journals use anonymous review, in which referees do not know the author's name, and some also keep referees anonymous to authors. Practices vary. When a journal asks for an anonymized manuscript, remove names, acknowledgments and any self-citation that reveals the author (write "the author" or cite in the third person where the journal's guidance says so), and check the file's metadata.

### Keeping records

Keep a **quotation log** (exact text, source, page) and a reference file as you read. Most integrity problems come from a note written in a hurry that no longer distinguishes your words from the source's. Mark every note as "quote", "paraphrase" or "my idea" at the time you take it.

@example
title: Rewriting a patchwritten passage
problem: Invented source (by an invented author, V. Kestrel, 2021, p. 14): "A disposition is not manifested merely by being triggered; it is manifested only if the trigger produces the characteristic outcome in the absence of interfering conditions." A student's draft reads: "A disposition is not manifested only by being triggered; it is manifested only if the trigger produces the characteristic result when interfering conditions are absent." Say what is wrong and write a better version.
step: Compare the two. The draft keeps the source's structure and swaps "merely" for "only", "outcome" for "result" and "in the absence of" for "when ... are absent". There is no quotation marks and no citation.
step: Name the fault: patchwriting without citation, and so plagiarism, even though some words changed.
step: Read the source and close it; ask what it argues: a trigger is not sufficient for manifestation; interference can prevent it.
step: Write a version in your own words with a citation: "As Kestrel (2021, p. 14) argues, a trigger does not suffice for manifestation: if something interferes, the disposition can be triggered and yet fail to manifest." Then check it against the source for accuracy.
answer: The draft is patchwriting and has no citation. A better version restates the content in new wording and cites Kestrel (2021, p. 14); if the exact wording matters, quote it with quotation marks and the page number.

@example
title: Classifying cases
problem: Say whether each is a breach of academic integrity or acceptable practice: (a) A student reuses two paragraphs from her own earlier published article in a new paper, without saying so. (b) A student cites a book on the strength of an index listing, having read only the title. (c) A student thanks two colleagues in an acknowledgment note for comments on a draft. (d) A student includes a reference whose title and author came from a software tool's suggestion and which she cannot find.
step: (a) Reusing published text without disclosure is self-plagiarism; the right course is to cite the earlier article, rewrite, or get the editor's permission as the journal requires.
step: (b) Citing an unread work risks misreporting it; if you rely on it, read it, and otherwise do not cite it for a claim about what it says.
step: (c) Acknowledging helpful comments is expected and acceptable.
step: (d) A reference that cannot be verified is a fabrication risk; an unverified citation must be found and checked or removed.
answer: (a) breach (self-plagiarism); (b) a breach of the norm of citing what you have read, and a risk of misreporting; (c) acceptable; (d) breach (an unverifiable citation, a fabrication risk).

@mistakes
- Believing that changing a few words makes a passage your own.
- Citing a source you have not read, or have only seen reported by someone else, as if you had read it.
- Pasting in notes without marking which are quotations, and later presenting them as your own words.
- Treating the policy of one journal or program as the rule for all: policies on simultaneous submission, preprints and the use of tools vary, so check each.

prompt: A colleague draft includes this passage: "As the literature agrees, the best account of ontological dependence is the modal-existential account." It has no citation. List three problems with the sentence from the point of view of citation and precision, and say how you would revise it.

rubric: Notices that "best" is an unsupported evaluation.
rubric: The revision is accurate, cites a source (as a placeholder) and does not claim agreement in the literature.
