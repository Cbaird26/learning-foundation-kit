@article
Beginning researchers often believe that original work requires a new theory of everything. In practice almost all published contributions are smaller and more exact: a counterexample to a widely used analysis, a premise shown to be doing hidden work, a distinction that makes two views compatible. Originality in philosophy is usually **relative to a specific literature**, and it is found by reading closely, not by waiting for inspiration.

### The repertoire

Here are common kinds of contribution, each with the move that produces it.

- **Counterexample.** Find a case that satisfies an analysis's conditions but not the analyzed concept, or the reverse.
- **Hidden premise.** Show that an argument relies on a premise its author does not state, and that the premise is doubtful or that the argument fails without it.
- **Premise attack.** Argue that an accepted premise of a standard argument is false or unmotivated, and say what follows.
- **Distinction.** Show that a term is used in two senses and that an apparent dispute disappears, or an argument equivocates, once they are separated.
- **Transfer.** Take a successful argument or technique from one debate and apply it to another (for example, an argument for the vagueness of one thing applied to another).
- **Diagnosis.** Explain why two views seem to conflict, or why a problem has seemed intractable.
- **Synthesis with a new twist.** Combine elements of two positions into a view that avoids the objections to each, and show that it can.
- **Reverse the burden.** Argue that an argument said to put pressure on one view puts equal pressure on its rival.

A single paper often combines two: a distinction used to defend a new reply.

### Reading for openings

Original work begins with dissatisfaction. When you read, ask four questions of each article.

1. **What must be true for this argument to work?** List the premises and the hidden premises, as in Lesson 3. The least defended is a candidate.
2. **What case would test it?** Construct a case at the edge of the author's analysis: a degenerate case, a case with a different category of thing, a case where two principles diverge.
3. **What does this view say about a neighboring problem?** Views that are plausible in one debate sometimes have awkward consequences in another.
4. **Do two authors use the same word differently?** Terms like "fundamental", "dependence" and "essence" are used in several ways; a disagreement may be verbal or an argument may equivocate.

Keep a running log of **questions** and **dissatisfactions**, separate from your notes on what papers say. The log is where your own work starts.

### Testing a candidate

A candidate becomes a paper only if it passes three tests.

1. **Novelty.** Search under the vocabulary of the debate and its neighbors (Lesson 2), follow citations forward from the most closely related papers, and ask someone who knows the area. You cannot prove a negative: write "I know of no discussion of this in the literature" only after real searching, and expect a referee to know of something you missed. If you find a version, you often still have a paper: yours may differ in its target, its argument or its consequences.
2. **Defensibility.** Can you state the claim exactly and defend it in the space available? Can you state the best objection to it and answer it? A claim you cannot defend against the first objection you think of is not ready.
3. **Significance.** Does it matter to anyone besides you? Say what changes for a neighboring debate or an opposing view.

### An example of the move from dissatisfaction to claim

Suppose you read a proposed analysis: "x is a part of y just in case x is wholly located within the region occupied by y." You wonder what it says about the contents of a sealed box. That question is a counterexample candidate:

| P1 | If the analysis is correct, then whatever is wholly located within the region occupied by y is a part of y. |
| P2 | The marble in the sealed box is wholly located within the region occupied by the box. |
| P3 | The marble is not a part of the box. |
| C | So the analysis is not correct. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens on P1 after instantiating with P2). Its force depends on P2, where the best reply is a distinction: perhaps the region occupied by the box is only the region of its walls, not its cavity, so the marble is not within it. A paper built on this case must therefore start by fixing what "the region occupied by the box" means, and ask whether the analysis can be repaired by a clause about connection or about the occupying region. That is the shape of many small, real contributions: one case, one objection, one reply, one repaired claim.

### Honesty about priority

You may rediscover an idea that others have published. When you find the earlier source, cite it and adjust your claim to what is actually yours. Do not hide the overlap. A referee who knows the area will find it, and open acknowledgment of a predecessor looks much better than apparent ignorance of it.

@example
title: From an argument to three candidate contributions
problem: Invented argument: "If a thing's essence includes its origin, then the table could not have been made from a different block of wood. The table's essence includes its origin. So it could not have been made from a different block." (a) Is the argument valid? (b) List three candidate contributions (a counterexample, a premise attack, a distinction), each as a claim.
step: Validity. It has the form: if A then B; A; so B. That is modus ponens, so it is valid. Truth depends on the premises.
step: Counterexample candidate: a case where a thing is plausibly made from a different block but is the same thing, such as a table whose legs are replaced by pieces from another block over years and then entire planks. The claim: the premise connecting origin to modal profile needs a threshold for how much of the origin matters, and the case shows that no sharp threshold is available.
step: Premise attack candidate: attack the second premise by arguing that the evidence for origin essentialism is the intuition about tables, and that the intuition is explained by the way we use the word "this table" to pick out one particular piece of furniture, not by a fact about essence.
step: Distinction candidate: distinguish "the table could not have originated from a different block" from "the table could not have been made from mostly different matter", and argue that the first is more plausible than the second, so the argument is valid and its premises are acceptable only on the first reading.
answer: (a) Valid, by modus ponens. (b) A counterexample showing that origin essentialism needs a threshold; a premise attack on the second premise based on how reference to the table works; and a distinction between two origin claims, one plausible and one not.

@example
title: Testing a candidate for novelty
problem: You think that an argument from the problem of the many can be used to attack the claim that persons are numerically identical to their organisms. Describe how you would test whether this is new and whether it is worth a paper.
step: Search. Read the encyclopedia entries on the problem of the many and on personal identity, then search the index for variations ("problem of the many" with "animalism", "organism", "persons").
step: Chain. Find the most closely related papers and read who cites them, to see whether anyone has already linked these debates.
step: Compare. If a similar argument exists, write one sentence stating how yours differs: different premises, different target or different consequences. If none exists, say only that you know of none.
step: Test defensibility. Write the argument in standard form, find its weakest premise, and write the best objection and your reply.
step: Test significance. Say what changes for the view you attack and for neighboring positions if your argument works.
answer: A search through the encyclopedias and index under several terms, forward citation chasing, a stated difference from any near neighbors, a standard-form argument with an objection and reply, and a statement of significance.

@mistakes
- Waiting for a grand idea instead of looking for a precise gap or a premise that carries more weight than it was shown to bear.
- Claiming "no one has noticed this" without searching under the vocabulary of neighboring debates.
- Producing a counterexample without checking the case against the obvious rival reading of the analysis.
- Hiding overlap with an earlier paper instead of citing it and saying what is different.

@practice self
prompt: Invented analysis: "A person knows that p just in case p is true, the person believes p, and the person's belief is caused by the fact that p." Give (a) a case that is plausibly a counterexample, (b) the argument from the case in standard form, valid as written, and (c) one sentence on the best reply by a defender of the analysis.
hint: Think of knowledge of mathematical or future facts, where the causal link is doubtful.
solution: (a) A person who knows that 17 is prime by working through a proof: the fact that 17 is prime does not cause her belief, since numbers do not enter into causation, yet she plausibly knows it. (b) P1: If the analysis is correct, then anyone who knows that p has a belief that p caused by the fact that p. P2: The person knows that 17 is prime. P3: The person's belief that 17 is prime is not caused by the fact that 17 is prime. C: So the analysis is not correct. This is valid by modus tollens. (c) The defender can restrict the analysis to contingent empirical knowledge, or say that proof gives a derivative kind of causal connection (the belief is caused by the proof, which is explained by the fact), and the critic must then ask whether the restriction is principled.
rubric: The case is described clearly and satisfies the analysis's conditions or fails them in the right way.
rubric: The argument is valid as written, with the denial of the analysis as the conclusion.
rubric: The reply is a real option for a defender (a restriction or a redefinition) and is stated fairly.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page-style reconstruction (about 200 words) of the following invented recent article, and add a two-sentence assessment that proposes one possible contribution you could make in response. Article summary: Priya Haldane argues that the A-theory of time, on which there is an objective present, cannot account for the apparent speed of time's passage. She argues that to say time passes at one second per second is to say that the rate of passage is unity, but that this makes sense only if rate is measured against a second time-dimension; the A-theorist has no such dimension, and so the claim that time passes is not a claim about rate. She considers the reply that the question of rate is confused and answers that if so, the A-theorist cannot reply to the claim that passage is merely metaphorical. (The article is invented.)
hint: Reconstruct first (thesis, argument, premise support, her objection and reply); then add the assessment as your own, in separate sentences.
solution: Haldane's thesis is that the A-theory cannot give content to the claim that time passes at a rate. Argument: P1: If time passes at a rate, then the rate is given by a quantity of time per unit of time that is measured against some further dimension. P2: The A-theorist admits no further time dimension. C1: So, for the A-theorist, passage does not have a rate. P3: If passage has no rate, then the A-theorist's claim that time passes is not a claim about rate and may be merely metaphorical. C: So the A-theorist's claim that time passes is not a claim about rate. Support: P1 is defended by analysis of what rates are; P2 is common ground; P3 is a conditional definition. Objection and reply: the A-theorist may call the question about rate confused; Haldane replies that this concedes that passage is only metaphorical. Assessment: the argument is valid as stated, though P1 may be contested, since a rate of unity might be a limiting case of rates that needs no measurement against a second dimension. A possible contribution: argue that P1 equivocates between measured rates and a primitive rate of passage, and say what a primitive rate would have to be.
rubric: States the thesis and builds a valid standard-form reconstruction faithful to the summary.
rubric: Reports the premise support and the author's own objection and reply.
rubric: Keeps the assessment separate and short.
rubric: Proposes a specific contribution (a premise attack, distinction or counterexample), not a vague wish to discuss the topic.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose a standard argument in metaphysics (the argument from vagueness for unrestricted composition, the argument for the B-theory from relativity, the indispensability argument, or another). (a) State it in standard form, valid as written. (b) Identify its least defended premise. (c) State one candidate contribution as a precise claim and say which kind of contribution it is.
hint: Pick an argument you can state in four or five lines and ask which premise is merely assumed.
solution: (a) Indispensability argument. P1: We ought to be committed to all and only the entities indispensable to our best scientific theories. P2: Numbers are indispensable to our best scientific theories. C: So we ought to be committed to numbers. (b) The least defended premise is P1: it combines a claim about confirmation with a view about which parts of a theory are confirmed. (c) Candidate: I argue that P1 conflates two things, that a theory is confirmed as a whole and that each of its posits is confirmed, and that on the weaker reading, which only the whole-theory view supports, the conclusion fails. This is a distinction: it divides P1 into a strong reading that is not independently supported and a weak reading that does not yield the conclusion.
rubric: The argument is correct, in standard form and valid as written.
rubric: Names a specific premise as least defended and gives a reason.
rubric: The candidate contribution is a precise claim, not a topic, and is classified correctly.
