@article
Two papers arrive on a referee's desk. Both are about material constitution: the puzzle of how a statue and the lump of clay it is made from can occupy the same place and share the same parts. Paper A surveys four solutions and ends by saying that "each has merits and demerits, and the debate continues." Paper B argues that one specific reply to one specific argument fails, for one specific reason, and says what follows for the view that reply was meant to protect. Paper A is more comprehensive. The referee recommends Paper B, and most experienced referees would agree. Why?

### The five questions

A good research article answers five questions, usually in its first two pages, and keeps answering them to the end.

1. **What is the claim?** A thesis is a statement that an informed person could deny. "Material constitution is an interesting problem" is not a thesis; "the best reply to the grounding objection makes the constitution relation too weak to do its job" is.
2. **Why believe it?** The paper gives an **argument**: premises that support the thesis, not a list of considerations that gesture at it.
3. **What are the rivals and objections?** The paper says which views it opposes and which objections it expects, and answers the strongest, not the weakest.
4. **What is new?** The paper's **contribution** is what a reader will know, or be able to do, after reading it that they could not before.
5. **Is it accurate and clear?** Other people's views are reported correctly, terms are defined, and the prose lets a specialist reader follow every step without guessing.

### Topic, thesis, contribution

A **topic** is an area ("persistence"). A **thesis** is a claim about it ("the argument from temporary intrinsics does not favor perdurantism over endurantism"). A **contribution** is the thesis's relation to the literature: a new argument, a new objection to a known argument, a new distinction that dissolves an apparent disagreement, a counterexample, a diagnosis of why two views seem to conflict, a new reply, or a defense of an unfashionable view with new resources. Beginning writers often have a topic and mistake it for a thesis; promising writers often have a thesis and cannot yet say what is new about it. Both are fixable, but only if you can tell which problem you have.

Survey articles, handbook chapters and review essays are legitimate genres with different aims. This course concerns the **research article**, the standard unit of professional philosophy, which is judged by its contribution. Lengths and formats vary by journal, so read each journal's instructions to authors before writing for it.

### The virtues, and their tensions

Referees tend to prize **clarity** (the reader always knows where the paper is and why), **rigor** (valid arguments, defined terms, no equivocation), **charity** (the opposing view in its best form), **depth** (an objection pressed until it either lands or is answered) and **significance** (a result that matters to people working on neighboring questions). These pull against each other. A broad thesis is significant but hard to defend in a fixed word limit; a narrow one can be defended completely but may seem small. Charity to a rival costs you some drama. The craft is calibration: choose the largest claim you can defend in the space you have.

Consider the reasoning behind that advice about contribution:

| P1 | A research article is publishable only if it makes a contribution that the existing literature does not already contain. |
| P2 | A research article that only summarizes existing positions makes no contribution that the existing literature does not already contain. |
| C | So a research article that only summarizes existing positions is not publishable. |

The argument is valid. Its soundness depends on the restriction to research articles in both premises: a critic who points to a respected survey article attacks neither premise, because surveys are another genre. A critic who says that a good summary can itself be a contribution (by unifying a scattered literature) attacks premise P2, and has a point: the line between synthesis and survey is real, which is why a summary that proposes a new organizing distinction can count as new.

### What "good" does not mean

A good paper need not be right. It needs to be a serious attempt that moves the discussion even if its thesis is later rejected: a sharp mistake is more useful than a vague truth. It need not be difficult, either; difficulty that does not buy anything is a fault. And it need not mention everything you read. Part of the skill is the discipline to leave out true and interesting material that does not serve the thesis.

### A working test

Before drafting, write two sentences. The first begins "I argue that" and finishes with a claim someone could deny. The second begins "This matters because" and says what changes if the claim is true (for an opposing view, for a neighboring debate, for a standard argument). If you cannot write both, you do not yet have a paper; you have a topic and some reading. Lesson 4 of this course shows how to find the missing second sentence.

@example
title: Diagnosing an opening paragraph
problem: A student's draft begins: "Persistence through time is among the oldest problems in metaphysics. Many philosophers have written on it. In this paper I will look at some of the main views, including endurantism and perdurantism, and say something about whether they are plausible." Say what is missing, then rewrite the opening so that it states a thesis and a reason it matters. (The rewrite is an invented sample thesis, not a claim about the literature.)
step: Look for the thesis. "Say something about whether they are plausible" promises a topic, not a claim. No reader could disagree with it, so no reader could be persuaded by it.
step: Look for the argument and the contribution. The paragraph announces no premises and nothing new: "many philosophers have written" is true of every topic and tells the reader nothing about this paper.
step: Pick one specific, defensible claim from the draft's own material. Suppose the student's real interest is the argument from temporary intrinsics, which says that one thing can be bent at one time and straight at another, and that this seems to require time-indexing of shapes. Choose the reply that makes shapes relations to times, and ask what it costs.
step: Write the rewrite: "I argue that the endurantist reply that treats shapes as relations to times gives up the intuition that motivated endurantism, namely that a persisting thing is wholly present at each moment and has its shape intrinsically. This matters because it shows that the temporary-intrinsics problem cannot be set aside by a mere change of logical form: the endurantist must either pay this price or adopt a different reply."
answer: The draft has a topic but no thesis, argument or contribution. The rewrite commits to a claim that can be denied, names the target (one reply to one argument) and says what follows.

@example
title: Calibrating the scope of a thesis
problem: A student proposes to write a 7,000-word paper with the thesis "Mereological nihilism is true." A supervisor suggests "The argument from vagueness for unrestricted composition fails even if its premise that composition cannot be vague is granted." Which thesis is better suited to the paper, and why?
step: Estimate what each thesis demands. To defend nihilism in full you must handle every major argument against it (the apparent existence of tables and persons, the problem of gunk, the appeal to common sense) and every major argument for rival views. That is a book, not an article.
step: Estimate what the supervisor's thesis demands. It concerns one argument, one premise and one reply. The paper can state the argument fairly, show where the reply attacks it, answer two or three objections, and stop.
step: Check the contribution. A claim about a single argument can be a real contribution if it is non-obvious. The narrower thesis even grants an opponent's premise, which makes the paper's point harder to dismiss.
step: Check what is lost. The narrower thesis is less dramatic, but the student can say in the introduction how a success here bears on the larger debate, without promising to settle it.
answer: The narrower thesis is better: it can be fully defended in the space available, it commits to a claim that can be denied, and its significance can be argued without overreaching. The broad thesis is a research program, not a paper.

@mistakes
- Treating a topic ("I will discuss the problem of universals") as a thesis. Test: can an informed reader disagree with it?
- Promising more than the word limit can deliver ("I will refute nominalism"), then answering only one or two objections.
- Confusing comprehensiveness with quality: surveying every position in order to avoid committing to a claim.
- Saying "this paper makes an important contribution" instead of showing what the contribution is.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of these sentences, appearing in an introduction, states a thesis?
choice: This paper is concerned with the nature of fundamentality.
choice: Fundamentality has been discussed by many philosophers in recent years.
choice*: I argue that no account of fundamentality that identifies the fundamental with the ungrounded can also count a grounded but unanalyzable entity as fundamental.
choice: In what follows I examine several accounts of fundamentality.
hint: A thesis is a claim that an informed reader could deny.
solution: Only the sentence that identifies the fundamental with the ungrounded commits to a claim that could be disputed. The others announce a topic, report that others have written about it, or promise an examination without saying what it will conclude.

@practice ms
prompt: Referees commonly look for which of these features in a research article? Select all that apply.
choice*: A thesis that is stated early and can be denied
choice*: An argument that supports the thesis rather than merely mentioning relevant considerations
choice*: Accurate reports of the views the paper opposes
choice: A citation for every philosopher who has written on the topic, whether or not they bear on the argument
choice: A thesis broad enough that no reader could find an objection to it
hint: Think about what a reader needs in order to assess whether the paper succeeds.
solution: Referees look for a thesis, an argument, and accurate and charitable reports of rivals. They do not want an exhaustive list of everyone who has written on the topic (relevance matters), and a thesis so broad that nobody could object to it is a topic, not a claim.

@practice mc
prompt: Paper A surveys five accounts of causation and concludes that "each has strengths and weaknesses." Paper B argues that a particular counterfactual analysis fails because of a specific kind of case, and proposes a repair. A referee prefers Paper B. Which explanation is best?
choice: Paper B is shorter, and shorter papers are always preferred.
choice*: Paper B commits to a claim, supports it with an argument and offers something new, while Paper A's conclusion is one no one would deny.
choice: Paper B is more likely to be true.
choice: Referees prefer papers that discuss fewer philosophers.
hint: Ask which paper makes a contribution that the literature did not already contain.

@practice self
prompt: Here is an invented abstract: "Modal metaphysics is a rich area with many competing views. I consider the debate between modal realists and ersatzers and discuss some of the strengths of each. I conclude that there is much to be said on both sides." In two or three sentences, say what is wrong with it as an abstract of a research article, and then write a replacement of at most three sentences that commits to a claim (the claim may be an invented sample).
hint: Look for a thesis, a reason to believe it and a statement of what is new.
solution: The abstract states a topic and a conclusion that no one could deny ("much to be said on both sides"), so it gives a reader no reason to read on and no claim to assess. A replacement: "Ersatz theories of possible worlds are often said to be ontologically cheaper than modal realism. I argue that this advantage disappears for ersatzers who need a primitive modal notion to say which representations are consistent, since the primitive then plays the role that the realist's worlds were meant to play. The result shows that the dispute is better framed as a choice between two primitives than as a choice between an extravagant and a frugal ontology."
rubric: Identifies that the original has no thesis and an uncontroversial conclusion.
rubric: The replacement states a claim that an informed reader could deny, and the claim is specific (it names a view, a feature of it and a consequence).
rubric: The replacement says or implies why the claim matters or what is new, in no more than three sentences.

@practice self
prompt: A student writes this thesis for a 6,000-word paper: "Free will is compatible with determinism." Explain in three or four sentences why this thesis is too broad for the paper, and propose a narrower thesis, on the same topic, that could be defended in that space.
hint: Count the objections a full defense would have to answer, then pick one argument to target.
solution: The thesis is one of the central claims in a whole subfield; a full defense must answer the consequence argument, the manipulation arguments, the objections from alternative possibilities and more, and 6,000 words cannot answer them all well. A narrower thesis: "The consequence argument's rule for transferring powerlessness (having no choice about a truth) across entailment fails when applied to the laws of nature, because it treats the laws as fixed in the same way as the past." This targets one argument and one rule, can be defended with a counterexample and an answer to two objections, and still bears on the larger debate.
rubric: Explains that the broad thesis demands answers to many distinct arguments, more than the space allows.
rubric: Proposes a narrower thesis that is still a claim someone could deny, about a specific argument, premise or case.
rubric: The narrower thesis is realistically defensible in about 6,000 words, and the answer says briefly why.

@quiz mc
prompt: A paper begins: "In this paper I discuss whether there are numbers." Which description of this sentence is most accurate?
choice*: It names a topic but commits to no claim, so a reader cannot yet tell what the paper will argue.
choice: It states a thesis in the form of a question.
choice: It states a contribution, because the question is important.
choice: It is a good thesis, because both answers are controversial.
hint: A thesis is an answer, not a question.
solution: The sentence announces a topic. That the question is controversial is a reason for the topic to matter, but a thesis is a position on the question, and a paper must say what it will argue about it.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these are legitimate kinds of contribution for a research article in metaphysics? Select all that apply.
choice*: A counterexample to a widely accepted analysis
choice*: A distinction that shows two apparently conflicting views to be compatible
choice*: A new objection to a well-known argument
choice: A report that many philosophers disagree about the topic
choice: An announcement that the paper's topic is important
hint: A contribution is something the reader can use that the literature did not already offer.
solution: A counterexample, a dissolving distinction and a new objection are each something new that others can respond to or build on. That philosophers disagree, and that a topic matters, are things readers already know and are not contributions.

@quiz self
prompt: Take a debate in metaphysics that you know: for example, the problem of the many, the grounding of modal facts, or the truthmaker principle. (1) Name one specific argument in it. (2) State a thesis about that argument that an informed reader could deny. (3) Write the "This matters because" sentence. (4) In one sentence, say which kind of contribution your thesis is (new objection, counterexample, distinction, diagnosis, new reply or other).
hint: Start from one premise of a known argument that you find weak or under-defended.
solution: (1) The argument from vagueness for unrestricted composition. (2) I argue that the argument's premise that composition cannot be vague is not needed, because a parallel argument runs from the weaker premise that there is no sharp cutoff to composition, so the argument's force should be assessed using that weaker premise. (3) This matters because restrictivists about composition who answer the argument by questioning the stronger premise (that composition cannot be vague) have not yet answered the version of the argument that does not use it. (4) It is a diagnosis: the argument's conclusion is supported by a weaker premise than has been assumed, so one familiar reply does not reach it.
rubric: Names a specific argument, not just a topic.
rubric: The thesis is a claim that could be denied and is about the named argument.
rubric: The significance sentence says what changes for another view or debate if the thesis is true.
rubric: Correctly classifies the contribution and is consistent with the thesis.
