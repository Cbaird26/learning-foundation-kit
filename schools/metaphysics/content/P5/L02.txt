@article
You have a topic: whether composition can be vague. Within an afternoon you can have a map of the debate or a pile of fifty unread PDFs. The difference is method. Professional philosophy has a small set of tools for finding what has been written, and a set of habits for reading it in proportion to its usefulness.

### The tools and what each is for

**PhilPapers** is the field's main index of philosophical research. It lists journal articles, books, chapters and many unpublished or preprint items, organized into a subject taxonomy that you can browse and search. It is an **index**, not a publisher: a listing tells you that something exists and where it can be found, and does not certify that it was peer reviewed. Many listings link to a publisher's page or to an open-access copy, and PhilArchive, the open-access repository associated with PhilPapers, hosts papers that authors deposit. Features such as alerts for new items in a category and personal bibliographies change over time, so check the site's help pages for what is currently offered.

The **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (SEP) and the **Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy** (IEP) are free, peer-reviewed online encyclopedias. Their entries are written by specialists and are the best place to get a map of an unfamiliar debate: its main positions, standard arguments and canonical papers. Each entry's bibliography is a ready-made reading list. But an entry is a survey as of its last revision and reflects its author's choices about what to include. It is a starting point, and rarely a source you cite for a claim about what a specific paper says.

**Journals** are where most new work appears. General philosophy journals publish across the field; specialist journals publish a particular area, such as metaphysics or logic. Check a journal's own website for its scope, its review process and whether you can read the article with your institution's access. **Books and edited volumes** matter in metaphysics too, and handbooks and companions give overviews of an area by many authors.

### A search strategy

1. **Seed.** Start from an entry in the SEP or IEP on your topic and read its overview and its section headings.
2. **Backward chaining.** Take the two or three papers the entry treats as central, and read their bibliographies for what they respond to.
3. **Forward chaining.** For each key paper, find the later work that cites it. Citation counts and "cited by" lists are available on several services, including some PhilPapers listings, but coverage is uneven, so treat the lists as leads, not complete records.
4. **Vary vocabulary.** The same problem goes by different names in different sub-literatures: "material constitution" and "coincident objects", "problem of the many" and "vagueness of ordinary objects". If a search returns little, try other terms before concluding that nothing has been written.
5. **Check dates.** A debate may have moved on since the entry's last revision. Sort by date and read the most recent items for the current state of play.
6. **Stop when it saturates.** You have found enough when new searches return mostly items you have already seen, or items that are plainly off-topic.

### Triage: reading in proportion

You cannot read everything closely. Read in three passes. **Pass one** (five minutes): the abstract, the introduction's roadmap, the section headings and the conclusion. Ask what the thesis is and whether the paper bears on your topic. **Pass two:** for papers that matter, read the sections that state the main argument and its objections, and mark the premises. **Pass three:** for the few papers that your own argument depends on, read everything closely and reconstruct the argument in standard form (Lesson 3). Record what you learn as you go, because a literature you cannot remember is a literature you will re-read.

### Why novelty claims need current reading

Consider an argument that explains why a survey entry cannot be your only source:

| P1 | I am justified in claiming that my thesis is new only if I have checked that the current literature does not already contain it. |
| P2 | If I have read only a survey entry on the debate, then I have not checked the current literature. |
| C | So if I have read only a survey entry on the debate, I am not justified in claiming that my thesis is new. |

The argument is valid: P1 says that justification requires checking, P2 says that reading only the entry means not checking, and the conclusion follows by modus tollens on P1 and a chain of conditionals. The point of the argument is practical. Someone who denies P2 may say that a recently revised entry is thorough; the reply is that it is still selective and still lags the newest work, which is why the search strategy goes beyond the entry.

### The annotated bibliography

An **annotated bibliography entry** has four parts. The **citation** is complete and in one consistent style. The **summary** says what the source argues, in two to four sentences, stating its thesis and its main line of argument in your own words. The **evaluation** says what is strong or weak about it: does the argument work, which premise is vulnerable, is the target fairly described. The **use note** says what you will do with the source: it is the opponent, the source of a distinction, a background survey, a counterexample, a position you will defend.

Keep entries short (about 100 to 150 words) so that you will keep writing them. Ten good entries on a topic are worth more than fifty bare citations, because writing the entry forces you to find out what the paper actually argues.

title: Writing an annotated bibliography entry
problem: Invented article: Ilse Varnholt, "Parts Without Priority," Annals of Hypothetical Ontology 14 (2023), pp. 101-124. In it, Varnholt argues that the thesis that wholes are metaphysically prior to their parts cannot be motivated by cases of organisms, because the cases support only that organisms have parts that depend on the organism's functioning, and functional dependence is not priority. She replies to two objections: that functional dependence is a species of ontological dependence, and that her view cannot explain why an organism's death changes what its parts are. Write an annotated bibliography entry for a student working on priority monism and priority pluralism. (The article and author are invented.)
step: Write the citation in one consistent style: Varnholt, Ilse. 2023. "Parts Without Priority." Annals of Hypothetical Ontology 14: 101-124.
step: Summarize in your own words, starting with the thesis: Varnholt argues that organism cases do not motivate the priority of wholes over parts, because they show only functional dependence of parts on the organism, which is not metaphysical priority.
step: Evaluate. Note what succeeds and what is vulnerable: the argument turns on distinguishing functional dependence from ontological dependence, so the first reply carries the weight; the second reply is brief and does not say what the parts' identity conditions are.
step: Add the use note: say how the student will use it: as a counterargument to the organism-based argument for priority monism, and as a source of the distinction between functional and ontological dependence.
answer: Varnholt, Ilse. 2023. "Parts Without Priority." Annals of Hypothetical Ontology 14: 101-124. Argues that organism cases do not support the priority of wholes, since they show only functional dependence of parts on the organism, which is not metaphysical priority. Replies to the objection that functional dependence is a species of ontological dependence by denying that dependence on a whole's functioning makes the whole prior. Evaluation: the key move is the distinction between functional and ontological dependence; the reply about death is brief and leaves the parts' identity conditions open. Use: the main opponent for my section on organism-based arguments for priority monism.

@mistakes
- Treating PhilPapers as a guarantee of quality: it lists items; it does not certify that they were peer reviewed.
- Citing an encyclopedia entry for a claim about what a particular paper argues, instead of reading the paper.
- Searching under one term and concluding that nothing has been written.
- Writing annotations that only paraphrase the abstract; the evaluation and the use note are what make an entry useful.
