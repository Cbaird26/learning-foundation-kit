@article
The **Industrial Revolution** was a shift from hand production on farms and in workshops to machine production in factories, powered first by water and then by steam. It began in Britain in the late 1700s and spread to Belgium, the United States, Germany, Japan and elsewhere during the 1800s.

### Why Britain first?

Historians point to several factors that combined: accessible coal and iron, a growing agricultural output, trading ports and colonial markets, capital from trade, banks, and legal protection for property, and a culture of practical invention. Textile making was the first major industry: machines such as the spinning jenny and the power loom increased cloth output, and cotton came from plantations in the Americas that used enslaved labor. The improved steam engine, developed in the 1700s, powered mines and then factories, and later **railways** (from the 1820s) and steamships.

### The factory system and the city

In the **factory system** workers followed the clock and the machine rather than the seasons. Hours were long, wages were low, and many women and children worked in mills and mines. The population of industrial cities rose quickly as people moved from the countryside, a process called **urbanization**. Housing was often crowded, with shared water pumps and open sewers.

Historians debate living standards: optimists point to long-run rises in income and consumption, pessimists point to crowding, pollution and disease in the early decades. Both can be partly true at different times and for different groups.

### Industrial cities and disease

Crowded cities spread infectious diseases. Tuberculosis, typhoid, typhus and **cholera** were common. Cholera arrived in Britain in 1831 to 1832, and large outbreaks followed in 1848 to 1849 and 1853 to 1854. The dominant theory of the time was the **miasma theory**, that disease came from foul air from decaying matter. An 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the working population in Britain linked poor drainage, water supply and filth to illness and argued for sanitary reform. The Public Health Act of 1848 created a national board of health. During the 1854 cholera outbreak in London's Soho district, a physician mapped deaths around a water pump on Broad Street and convinced local authorities to remove the pump handle, supporting the view that cholera was spread by contaminated water. Later, in the 1860s to 1880s, **germ theory** replaced miasma. Sanitary reform (sewers, clean water, waste collection) reduced cholera and typhoid long before most effective drugs existed.

### Responses to industrial society

- **Reform:** Factory Acts limited hours and child labor; trade unions bargained for wages.
- **Liberal economics:** supporters of free markets argued that competition and trade create wealth.
- **Socialism and Marxism:** critics argued that factory owners controlled production at the expense of workers and proposed collective or state ownership.
- **Second industrial revolution (c. 1870 to 1914):** steel, chemicals, electricity and mass production spread, along with new corporations.

@example
title: Growth of an industrial city
problem: A city of 25,000 people grows by 4 percent per year for 20 years. About how many people live there? Then say what kind of public-health problem such growth causes.
step: Use compound growth: $25000 \times 1.04^{20}$.
step: Compute $1.04^{20} \approx 2.191$.
step: Multiply: $25000 \times 2.191 \approx 54{,}800$ people.
step: Rapid growth strained water supply and sewage disposal, so infections such as cholera and typhoid spread easily.

@mistakes
- Claiming industrialization began everywhere at once; it spread unevenly over the 1800s.
- Saying that germ theory explained cholera in 1832; the dominant theory was miasma.
- Treating industrial capitalism as only good or only bad; sources and historians differ.
- Forgetting that the cotton for early mills depended on enslaved labor.
