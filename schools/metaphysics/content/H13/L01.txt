@article
For most of human history, people lived as **foragers**: small groups that moved with the seasons to hunt animals and gather plants. Beginning roughly 12,000 to 10,000 years ago, in several parts of the world, some groups began to plant crops and raise animals. This slow change is called the **Neolithic Revolution** or the **agricultural revolution**. It happened independently in places such as the Fertile Crescent (wheat, barley, sheep, goats), China (millet, rice), Mesoamerica (maize) and the Andes (potato). Calling it a "revolution" is a convenience; in most places it took many centuries.

### From surplus to cities

Farming can produce a **surplus**: more food than the farmers themselves need. A surplus can be stored, taxed or traded, and it feeds people who do not farm: priests, soldiers, scribes, potters, metalworkers and rulers. This is **specialization of labor**. As populations grew and settled, villages grew into cities, and cities needed ways to settle disputes, collect grain and organize defense. Those needs encouraged **governments**.

Historians often define a **civilization** by a cluster of features rather than a single test: cities, a food surplus, specialized jobs, organized government, record keeping or writing, a shared religion or belief system, and monumental building. Not every early society had every feature, and the word is a tool for comparison, not a ranking of peoples. Many societies without cities, such as pastoral herders, had rich cultures and complex knowledge.

### Four river-valley examples

**Mesopotamia** ("between the rivers") lies between the Tigris and Euphrates. Floods there were unpredictable, so communities built canals and levees. Sumerian cities such as Uruk and Ur developed **cuneiform**, wedge-shaped writing pressed into clay, around 3400 to 3200 BCE. Temples called ziggurats stood at city centers. Around 1754 BCE a Babylonian king issued a law collection, the **Code of Hammurabi**, which listed offenses and penalties that varied by social class.

**Egypt** grew along the Nile, whose predictable annual flood left fertile silt. Tradition dates the unification of Upper and Lower Egypt to about 3100 BCE. Egyptians were ruled by a **pharaoh**, regarded as divine or semi-divine, and built pyramids as royal tombs; the Great Pyramid at Giza dates to roughly 2560 BCE. Writing in **hieroglyphs** and a cursive script on papyrus supported a large bureaucracy. Egyptian medical papyri describe wounds, drugs and disease.

**The Indus Valley** civilization (c. 2600 to 1900 BCE; also called Harappan) built planned cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro with grid streets, standardized brick sizes and covered drains. Its script has not been deciphered, so many questions about its government and beliefs remain open.

**Shang China** (c. 1600 to 1046 BCE) arose along the Yellow River. Kings consulted ancestors by cracking heated animal bones or turtle shells and inscribing the questions on them. These **oracle bones** are the earliest well-attested Chinese writing.

### Evidence and caution

Dates before about 1000 BCE are approximate and often revised. Written sources usually come from rulers and scribes, so they show an elite point of view; archaeology of houses, tools and graves adds the lives of ordinary people.

@example
title: How many specialists can a farming village support?
problem: In a village, 60 farming households each grow 500 kg of grain per year beyond what they need to eat. A non-farming household (potter, scribe, soldier) needs 600 kg of grain per year. How many non-farming households can the village support, and which feature of civilization does this illustrate?
step: Find the total surplus: $60 \times 500 = 30000$ kg of grain per year.
step: Divide by the grain needed per non-farming household: $30000 \div 600 = 50$.
step: The village can support 50 non-farming households.
step: This illustrates **surplus** leading to **specialization of labor**: because farmers produce more than they eat, other people can spend their time on crafts, record keeping, religion or defense.

@mistakes
- Treating "civilization" as meaning "better" and calling societies without cities "uncivilized"; the term describes a set of features, nothing more.
- Assuming that all early civilizations were connected or copied each other; several developed farming and writing independently.
- Counting BCE years as if they increase: 3100 BCE is earlier than 1754 BCE, so you subtract the smaller from the larger to find the gap.
- Presenting approximate dates as exact facts.

The Nile flooded on a fairly predictable yearly schedule and Egypt was protected by deserts, which supported a unified state under one pharaoh. The Tigris and Euphrates flooded unpredictably and Mesopotamia had few natural barriers, so many competing city-states built canals and levees, wrote laws and fortified their cities.
