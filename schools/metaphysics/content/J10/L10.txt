@article
Dogs, rocks and stars exist. But what about the number seven? The color red? The hole in a doughnut? The shadow of a tree? You cannot hold any of them, but we talk about them as if they exist. **Ontology** is the part of philosophy that asks what exists and what the different kinds of things are.

### Numbers

Is there a number seven, apart from seven apples, seven days or the word "seven"? **Platonism** about numbers (named after Plato, although he did not hold exactly this view) says yes: numbers are **abstract objects**. They are real, but they are not in space and time and they do not cause anything. **Nominalism** says no: only particular things exist, and numbers are not additional things. A Platonist's argument:

| P1 | "There are prime numbers between 10 and 20" is true. |
| P2 | If that sentence is true, then there really are numbers. |
| C | So there really are numbers. |

Most nominalists deny premise P2 (a few deny P1 instead, saying that such sentences are useful stories and not literally true). They say that talk about numbers is a useful way to talk about counting and measuring, not a description of strange objects. The Platonist asks how mathematics could be so useful if numbers are not real, and the nominalist asks how we could know about objects that have no effect on us. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, the two groups were close (about 38% Platonism and 42% nominalism), which tells you the question is open.

### Colors

Is the redness of a tomato in the tomato, or in your mind? The philosopher John Locke distinguished **primary qualities**, such as shape and size, which things have whatever anyone perceives, from **secondary qualities**, such as color and taste, which are powers of things to produce experiences in us. On one view, color is a property of surfaces that reflect light in certain ways. On another, colors depend on how our eyes and brains work, so the same surface might look different to a different animal. Philosophers still discuss how these fit together.

### Holes and shadows

A hole in a doughnut seems to be there: you can count them, and a doughnut with one hole differs from one with two. But a hole seems to be a missing piece, not a piece of anything. Is it a thing at all, or only a way of describing the doughnut? A shadow is similar. It is a dark patch that moves, and you can step on it, but it is just a place where light is blocked. Some philosophers say holes and shadows are real but unusual things, and others say that sentences about them are shorthand for sentences about the things that have them.

A useful question for each case is: "Could I say everything I want to say without pretending that this thing exists?"

@example
title: Platonist or nominalist?
problem: Priya says: "The number four does not exist as a thing. When I say 'there are four chairs', I only mean that there are some chairs, one, another, another and another." Which view is this, and which premise of the Platonist argument does it reject?
step: Priya denies that there is a thing called the number four apart from the chairs. That is nominalism.
step: The Platonist argument says that if sentences about numbers are true, numbers must exist. Priya says that the sentence is only about chairs.
answer: Nominalism. It rejects the premise that true statements about numbers require numbers to exist.

@example
title: Is a shadow a thing?
problem: Give one reason to say a shadow is a real thing and one reason to say it is not.
step: For: a shadow can be seen, measured, photographed and tracked as it moves across the ground.
step: Against: a shadow has no material of its own. It is just a place where light is blocked, so "there is a shadow" may only be shorthand for "light is blocked here".
answer: It is real because it can be observed and measured. It may not be a thing because it is only the absence of light.

@mistakes
- Saying that nominalists deny that mathematics is useful. They agree that it is useful, and most say that its truth does not need abstract objects.
- Saying that abstract objects are imaginary. Platonists hold that they are real, but not in space or time.
- Thinking that the primary and secondary qualities distinction says that colors are not real. Locke said that they are real powers to produce experiences.
- Assuming that "I can talk about it" shows it exists. That is the question: whether talk about holes or shadows is about things or shorthand.
