@article
People in every part of the world have asked whether there is a God, usually meaning a being who is all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good, and who made the universe. Philosophers have tried to give reasons on both sides. In philosophy we study the **arguments** whatever our own beliefs are. People of many faiths and of none take part in this discussion with respect, and thoughtful people end up in different places. You do not have to say what you believe.

### Three arguments for

**The first-cause (cosmological) argument.** Thomas Aquinas gave versions of it in the 1200s, and many others have developed it. A simple form:

| P1 | Everything in the world has a cause or explanation outside itself. |
| P2 | A chain of causes cannot go back forever without a first one. |
| C | So there is a first cause that is not caused by anything else, and this is God. |

Objections: why should the first cause be God, rather than something else, such as the universe itself? And if everything in the world needs a cause (P1), why does the first cause not need one? Replies say that the first cause is not a thing in the chain, and that it is special because it exists necessarily.

**The design argument.** William Paley (1802) compared the world to a watch. If you found a watch on a heath, with its parts fitted together for telling time, you would conclude that someone made it. The universe and living things show even more order and purpose, so they too must have a designer. Objection: Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection explains how complex living things can arise without a designer. Reply: even so, perhaps the laws of nature that allow evolution to work have a designer.

**The ontological argument.** Anselm of Canterbury (1000s) argued that God is a being than which nothing greater can be thought of. Such a being, he said, must exist in reality, and not only in the mind, because a being who exists in reality is greater than one who exists only in thought. Objection: you cannot define something into existence. Gaunilo, a monk and contemporary of Anselm, made this objection with an imaginary perfect island. Reply: Anselm's defenders say that God is a special case, because a greatest conceivable being cannot be merely imagined.

### An argument against: the problem of evil

| P1 | If God exists, then God is all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good. |
| P2 | A being like that would prevent needless suffering if it could. |
| P3 | There is a great deal of needless suffering. |
| C | So God does not exist. |

Replies come in several forms. The **free will defense** says that a world with free beings who can choose good is more valuable, even though they can also choose evil. Others say that suffering can lead to growth, or that we are in no position to know which suffering is needless, which attacks premise P3.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about two-thirds of professional philosophers accepted or leaned toward atheism and about one in five toward theism. The share of people who hold a view is not evidence that it is true. Philosophers still discuss all four arguments.

@example
title: What does the objection attack?
problem: Someone objects to the first-cause argument: "Maybe the first cause was just the universe itself, not God." Which part of the argument does this attack?
step: The argument concludes that there is a first cause and that it is God. The two parts are the existence of a first cause and its identity.
step: The objection does not say that there is no first cause. It says that the first cause might be something other than God.
answer: The objection attacks the last step, from "there is a first cause" to "this is God".

@example
title: Which premise does the reply attack?
problem: A reply to the problem of evil says: "We cannot know which suffering is really needless, since we see only a small part of the whole." Which premise of the argument does this reply question?
step: The premises are: P1, God is all-powerful, all-knowing and good; P2, such a being would prevent needless suffering; P3, there is a great deal of needless suffering.
step: The reply says that we cannot know that the suffering is *needless*, only that it seems so. That is a challenge to what we can conclude from P3.
answer: It questions premise P3, because it says that suffering that seems needless to us may not be.

@mistakes
- Saying that one argument proves or disproves God. Each argument has premises that thoughtful people accept or reject, and philosophers still disagree about all of them.
- Thinking that the argument against God is an attack on believers. The problem of evil is a philosophical argument that many believers have tried to answer.
- Mixing up the design argument with evolution. Paley's argument comes before Darwin, and Darwin's theory is the best-known objection to it.
- Assuming that the first-cause argument says that the universe is the first cause. It says there is a first cause, and the debate is about what it is.
