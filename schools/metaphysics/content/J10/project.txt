@project
title: A philosophy dialogue
overview: Write a dialogue of three to four pages between two characters who disagree about one big question from the course. Each character gives at least two real arguments and answers the other's objections, and then you add a half-page note on which argument you find strongest and why.
goal: Present two sides of a big question fairly, each in its strongest form.
goal: Practice stating arguments with premises and conclusions, and giving objections and replies.
goal: Reflect on which argument is strongest and explain your reasons.
step: Choose one question: free will, minds (could a robot think?), personal identity, time, or God's existence. Write the question as one sentence.
step: Choose two characters with names and a short setting, such as two friends on a bus or a student and a grandparent. Give each a different answer to the question.
step: Use the planning table in the material below. For each character, list two arguments in the form "premises, so conclusion", and the best objection to the other character's arguments.
step: Draft the dialogue. Each character should explain an argument, hear an objection and reply. Include at least one thought experiment or example from the course. Use plain, natural speech.
step: Check fairness. Ask whether each character would be happy with how their view is presented. If one character sounds foolish, rewrite so that both make strong points.
step: Write the half-page note, in your own voice, saying which argument you find strongest, which objection worried you most, and what you are still unsure about.
step: Revise for clarity, spelling and length (three to four pages for the dialogue, about half a page for the note), and list the readings you used.
material: **Planning table.** Copy this and fill it in before you write.

material: **Some starting points** (use any, or your own). Free will: determinism and the argument that choices are not up to us, the libertarian, the compatibilist. Minds: the Turing test, the Chinese room, the systems reply. Personal identity: the body view, the memory view, Reid's objection, the teletransporter. Time: presentism, eternalism, the growing block. God: first cause, design, the ontological argument, the problem of evil and the free will defense. Everyone should treat these topics respectfully, and you do not have to say what you believe.
deliverable: The finished dialogue (three to four pages) with both characters giving at least two arguments each and answering each other's objections.
deliverable: A half-page note saying which argument you find strongest and why.
deliverable: A short list of the readings and sources you used.
rubric: Both characters give at least two real arguments, with reasons and not just opinions.
rubric: Each character states and answers at least one objection, and the objections are real ones.
rubric: Both views are presented fairly, with no straw man, and the facts about the arguments are accurate.
rubric: The note explains which argument is strongest with reasons, and says what is uncertain.
rubric: The writing is clear, organized and in the writer's own words, with sources named.
safety: Treat all views, including religious and non-religious ones, with respect. Do not put real classmates in your dialogue, and do not share personal or private details about yourself or your family.
tutor: Can you check whether each of my characters is stating an argument, with premises and a conclusion, or just an opinion?

@review
## Review guide for J10

### Key ideas, week by week

1. **What is philosophy?** Philosophical questions are not settled by measuring or look-up alone. An argument has premises and a conclusion. A thought experiment imagines a case to test an idea.
2. **Reading and discussion.** Find the question, conclusion, reasons and key words. Be charitable, avoid straw men and ad hominem, and give objections with reasons.
3. **Knowledge and skepticism.** Knowing is traditionally true belief with good reasons. The dream argument (Descartes) is valid; replies reject a premise (we can tell the difference, or knowledge does not require certainty).
4. **Appearance and reality.** Plato's cave, the brain in a vat, Putnam's reply, and the difference between idealism (Berkeley) and skepticism.
5. **Minds.** Dualism and physicalism; the Turing test; Searle's Chinese room and the systems reply. There is no agreement on whether any AI system understands.
6. **Free will.** Determinism; the argument that it rules out free will; libertarianism, compatibilism and hard determinism.
7. **Personal identity.** The body, psychological (memory) and soul views; Locke's prince and cobbler; Reid's objection; Parfit's teletransporter; the ship of Theseus.
8. **Time.** Presentism, eternalism and the growing block; A- and B-theories; relativity and the debate; two answers to the ship of Theseus.
9. **God.** First cause, design (Paley), the ontological argument (Anselm, Gaunilo) and the problem of evil, with replies.
10. **What exists.** Platonism and nominalism, primary and secondary qualities, and whether holes and shadows are things.
11. **Right and wrong.** Consequentialism (utilitarianism), Kantian deontology and virtue ethics, with objections.
12. **Meaning and how to live.** Aristotle's flourishing, divine purpose, Camus and the absurd, and Nozick's experience machine.

### Key distinctions

- Premise or conclusion; valid or invalid; objection to a premise or to the link.
- Skepticism (what we can know) or idealism (what exists).
- Determinism, libertarianism and compatibilism.
- Presentism (what exists) or the A-theory (whether time passes).

### Self-check

- I can put an argument in standard form and say which premise a reply attacks.
- I can retell each thought experiment accurately and say what it tests.
- I can state a view fairly before I criticize it.
- I can give a reason, an objection and a reply on at least three big questions.

### Common errors

- Treating a famous view as proved, or an objection as a refutation.
- Confusing views that sound alike: idealism and skepticism, libertarianism and the political view, presentism and the A-theory.
- Using the share of philosophers who hold a view as evidence that it is true.
- Attacking a straw man or the person instead of the reasons.

@exam mc
prompt: Which of these is the best summary of the dream argument that Descartes raised in his Meditations?
choice: It proves that we are always dreaming.
choice*: It argues that, since a dream could seem just as real as waking life, we cannot be sure we are awake, and so we may not know what we think we know.
choice: It shows that the physical world is made of ideas.
choice: It is an argument that time does not pass.
hint: Think about what the argument concludes, and compare it with idealism.
solution: The dream argument claims that we cannot be certain that we are not dreaming, so our knowledge is in doubt. It does not claim that we are dreaming, and the view that the world is made of ideas is idealism.

@exam self
prompt: Choose one of these pairs: (a) the Chinese room and the systems reply; (b) the problem of evil and the free-will defense; (c) the experience machine and the hedonist reply. In a short essay of about 200 words, explain the argument or thought experiment accurately, give the reply, say what you think of the reply and why, and say what someone who disagrees with you would say.
hint: First state the argument with its premises, then the reply and which premise it attacks, then give your own judgment with a reason.
solution: I choose the Chinese room and the systems reply. Searle imagines a person who does not understand Chinese in a closed room with a rule book that tells them which Chinese symbols to send out for the symbols that come in. To people outside, the room seems to understand Chinese, but the person understands nothing. Searle concludes that following a program, which is just rules for changing symbols, is not enough for understanding. The systems reply says that the person is only one part of the system, and that the whole system, with the person, the rule book and the room, might understand, in the way that one brain cell does not understand English but a whole brain does. I think the reply is partly successful, because it shows that the case does not prove that the whole system lacks understanding. But Searle's response, that the person could memorize the book and still understand nothing, is hard to dismiss, and I am unsure how to settle it. Someone who disagrees might say that behaving exactly like a speaker of Chinese is all that understanding is, or might say that the room does not understand at all. This is an open question, and nobody has shown that either view is right.

rubric: States the reply accurately and says what it attacks.
rubric: Gives a judgment on the reply with a reason, and any view is acceptable if it is supported.
rubric: Includes a fair statement of what someone who disagrees would say.
