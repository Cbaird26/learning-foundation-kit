@article
Look at a photo of yourself from when you were a baby. Almost everything about you has changed: your size, your face, your thoughts, even most of the cells in your body. Yet you say, "That is me." What makes the baby and you the same person? This is the question of **personal identity**: what makes a person at one time the *same person* as a person at another time?

This is a different question from "What is your personality like?" In philosophy, **identity** means being one and the same thing, not being similar.

### Three answers

**The body view.** You are the same person as long as you are the same living human body, or animal. A baby and an adult are one continuing organism that grew. A challenge: imagine that your brain, with all your memories and character, were put into another body. Many people feel that *you* would go with your brain, which seems to count against the body view.

**The memory and psychological view.** John Locke argued in his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* that a person is a thinking thing that can think of itself as the same thinking thing at different times, and that this depends on **consciousness**, in particular on remembering. Locke imagined a prince whose mind enters the body of a cobbler: the person with the prince's memories would be the prince, even in the cobbler's body. Later philosophers broadened this to the **psychological view**: what matters is the continuation of memories, character, beliefs and intentions. A challenge came from Thomas Reid. A boy is punished for stealing apples, later becomes a brave officer who remembers the punishment, and later still a general who remembers being the officer but has forgotten the punishment. If memory makes you the same person, then the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, but the general is not the boy. That cannot be right, because identity is **transitive**: if A is B and B is C, then A is C. Defenders reply that the view should count chains of overlapping memories and other links, not only direct memories.

**The soul view.** Some philosophers and many religious traditions say that you are a non-physical soul that continues through changes in body and memory. A challenge: how could we know whether the soul in a body is the same one from one day to the next?

### A modern puzzle

Derek Parfit described a **teletransporter**: a machine scans you, destroys your body and builds an exact copy of you elsewhere, with all your memories. Is that a way of traveling, or the end of you? On the psychological view you survive. On the body view you do not. Parfit used cases like this to argue that identity may matter less than we think.

### The ship of Theseus

The ancient writer Plutarch reports a puzzle about the ship of the hero Theseus, kept by the Athenians. As planks rotted, they were replaced one by one, until none of the original wood remained. Is it still the same ship? The philosopher Thomas Hobbes added a twist: suppose someone collected the old planks and built them back into a ship. Now there are two candidates for being the original: the repaired ship and the rebuilt one. We will return to this puzzle next week.

@example
title: Testing the body view
problem: Imagine that a surgeon puts Lily's brain, with all her memories and her character, into the body of another person, whose own brain is removed. The person who wakes up says, "I am Lily, and I remember my family." What does the body view say, and what does the psychological view say?
step: The body view says that you are the same person as long as you are the same living body. The person who woke up has the other person's body.
step: So the body view says that the person who woke up is not Lily; it is the body's original owner, with a transplanted brain.
step: The psychological view says that what matters is the continuation of memories and character. The person who woke up has Lily's, so it says that she is Lily.
answer: The body view says that the person who woke up is not Lily. The psychological view says that she is. Many people find the second answer natural, which is a challenge for the body view.

@example
title: Reid's objection to the memory view
problem: A person at age 10 breaks a window. At age 30 the person remembers the window. At age 60 the person remembers being 30 but has forgotten the window. State how this is supposed to be a problem for the view that being the same person is having the memory of being that person.
step: The 30-year-old remembers the 10-year-old, so on the memory view they are the same person.
step: The 60-year-old remembers the 30-year-old, so they are the same person too.
step: Identity is transitive, so the 60-year-old should be the same person as the 10-year-old. But the 60-year-old does not remember being the 10-year-old, so the memory view says they are not the same person.
answer: The view seems to give contradictory answers: the 60-year-old both is and is not the 10-year-old. One reply is to count chains of overlapping memories.

@mistakes
- Confusing identity with similarity. Two identical-looking twins are not the same person; one person who changes a lot is.
- Thinking that the psychological view needs a perfect memory. Its defenders count overlapping links of memory, character and intention.
- Saying that Locke thought you are your body. He argued that a person is defined by consciousness, especially memory.
- Thinking that thought experiments such as the teletransporter prove one view. They test the views and show what each view says.

@practice self
prompt: In four to six sentences, explain the puzzle of the ship of Theseus and say whether you think that it is the same ship after all the planks are replaced. Give a reason.
hint: Say what happens to the ship, and then choose an answer, such as "same ship, because it changed gradually" or "not the same, because none of the matter remains".
solution: In the ship of Theseus puzzle, a ship's planks are replaced one by one as they rot, until every plank is new. The question is whether it is the same ship. I think it is the same ship, because it was a gradual process in which the ship stayed in use and kept its shape and function at each step, and we call things the same when they change slowly. Someone could reply that if no part of the original remains, then nothing makes it the original and it is a new ship. Hobbes's twist makes it harder: if the old planks were collected and rebuilt into a ship, which one is the original? I would say the repaired one, because continuity of use matters more to ships than the matter does.

@quiz self
prompt: Imagine that your memories and character were copied into a new body while your old body kept on living. In four or five sentences, say who you think is you, and what this shows about the views of personal identity.
hint: Compare what the body view and the psychological view would say about each of the two people.
solution: I think that I am the person with my original body and continuing life, and the copy is a new person who starts with my memories. The body view agrees with this, because only one has my body. The psychological view would have trouble, because both people have my memories and character, so it cannot say that one of them is me without picking one by a different test. This suggests that the psychological view needs an extra rule, or that identity may matter less than we thought, as Parfit suggested.
