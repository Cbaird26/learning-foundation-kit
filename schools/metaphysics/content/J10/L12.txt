@article
Sooner or later almost everyone wonders, "What is the point of it all?" Philosophers ask this carefully. It is a serious question, and thoughtful people answer it in different ways. It helps to split it into parts: Does life as a whole have a purpose? What makes a *particular* life meaningful? How should we live?

### Some answers

**Flourishing.** Aristotle, in his *Nicomachean Ethics*, said that the best life is one of **eudaimonia**, often translated "flourishing" or "living well". It is not just feeling pleased. It is using our abilities well, in activities such as friendship, learning and acting with virtues like courage and honesty, over a whole life.

**Purpose from God.** Many religious traditions teach that life has meaning because of a divine purpose, for example to know and love God and to care for others. People inside these traditions describe this meaning in their own terms, and for many it is central to how they live.

**Meaning we make.** Some thinkers say that the universe does not come with a built-in purpose, so meaning is something we create by what we care about and do. Albert Camus, in *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942), called the clash between our wish for meaning and a silent universe **the absurd**. He compared us to Sisyphus, who must push a rock up a hill forever, and he suggested that we can face this without despair and live fully anyway.

### The experience machine

Is a good life just a life of pleasant feelings? **Hedonism** says that pleasure is the only thing good in itself. Robert Nozick, in 1974, asked you to imagine an **experience machine** that could give you any experiences you wished, such as writing a great novel or having wonderful friends, while you float in a tank, with no memory that you chose it. Would you plug in for life? Nozick thought that many people would say no, because we want to *do* things and *be* certain kinds of people, not just have experiences, and that this counts against hedonism. A hedonist can reply that our reluctance comes from habit or fear of the unknown, and not from what is truly good.

| P1 | If hedonism is true, then a life in the experience machine is as good as any other life with the same pleasant feelings. |
| P2 | A life in the experience machine is not as good as a real life with the same feelings. |
| C | So hedonism is not true. |

The argument is valid. The hedonist must reject P2, and the objector must defend it.

### Writing your own argument

@example
title: Which view is this?
problem: Rita says: "Nothing in the universe tells me what my life is for, so I decide: I will spend my life helping animals, and that is what makes it meaningful." Which approach is she expressing?
step: She says that the universe does not supply a purpose, so there is no built-in meaning.
step: She says that she creates the meaning by what she chooses to care about and do.
answer: She is expressing the view that we make our own meaning, which is close to what some existentialist thinkers say.

@example
title: What does Nozick's machine test?
problem: Explain what Nozick's experience machine is meant to test, and what a person who says "no, I would not plug in" is thereby suggesting.
step: The machine gives you the experiences of a wonderful life with no real events behind them. If pleasure were all that mattered, you should be glad to plug in.
step: Someone who would not plug in is suggesting that other things matter too, such as really doing things, really being a certain kind of person, and being in contact with reality.
answer: It tests hedonism. A "no" suggests that pleasant experiences are not the only thing that matters.

@mistakes
- Thinking that Aristotle's flourishing is just feeling good. It is a whole life of using our abilities well and acting virtuously.
- Saying that Camus tells us to give up. He suggests that we can live fully while admitting that the universe gives us no answer.
- Thinking that the experience machine proves hedonism false. It is an argument, and hedonists have replies.
- Assuming that there is one correct answer that everyone must adopt. The question is open, and a strong answer gives reasons and faces objections.
