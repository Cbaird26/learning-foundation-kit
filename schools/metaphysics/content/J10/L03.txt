Have you ever had a dream so real that you were sure it was happening? When you woke up, it was a relief or a disappointment. What if you are dreaming right now? This puzzle is at the heart of **epistemology**, the part of philosophy that studies knowledge.

### What is knowledge?

Suppose you guess that a coin will land heads and it does. You were right, but you did not *know*. A traditional answer says that to **know** something, three things must be true: you **believe** it, it is **true**, and you have **good reasons** for believing it. Philosophers still argue about whether this is enough, because there are tricky cases where someone has a true belief with good reasons and still seems to be right only by luck. But the three parts are a good place to start.

### Skepticism

A **skeptic** (in philosophy) is someone who argues that we know much less than we think, perhaps almost nothing about the world outside our own minds. Most skeptics are not trying to convince you to give up on everything. They are testing our ideas about knowledge: what would we need in order to *really* know something?

### The dream argument

In the first of his *Meditations* (1641), René Descartes noticed that he sometimes dreamed that he was sitting by the fire when he was really asleep in bed. In a dream, everything seems as real as it does now. So how could he be sure he was awake? Here is the argument in standard form:

| P1 | If I cannot tell for sure that I am not dreaming, then I do not know that I am really sitting here reading. |
| P2 | I cannot tell for sure that I am not dreaming, because anything I experience now could also happen in a dream. |
| C | So I do not know that I am really sitting here reading. |

The argument is **valid**: if P1 and P2 are true, C must be true. So the only way to resist is to reject a premise. The same idea appears elsewhere. The Chinese thinker Zhuangzi wrote about dreaming that he was a butterfly and then wondering whether he was now a man who had dreamed of being a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming of being a man.

### Two replies

**Reply 1: Dreams are different.** Waking life is connected and orderly: events follow from one another, and you can check what you see by touching, asking someone or looking again. Dreams are often strange and disconnected. Descartes himself says something like this near the end of the *Meditations*. This reply attacks premise P2: we *can* tell the difference, at least most of the time. The skeptic answers that a dream can seem orderly too, and that the question is whether you can be *sure*.

**Reply 2: Knowing does not require being certain.** Many philosophers say you can know something even if you cannot rule out every wild possibility. I know I have a sandwich in my hand, even though I cannot prove I am not dreaming. This reply attacks premise P1. G. E. Moore argued in this spirit that he was more certain that he had hands than he could be of any premise the skeptic used. The skeptic answers that if you cannot rule out the dream, you are only guessing, and that guessing is not knowing.

Both replies take the argument seriously. Neither is accepted by everyone.

@example
title: Which premise does the reply attack?
problem: Someone says: "I cannot rule out that I am dreaming, but I still know that two plus two is four and that I am holding a pencil. Knowledge does not need absolute certainty." Which premise of the dream argument does this reply attack, and how?
step: Recall the two premises. P1 links "cannot be sure I am not dreaming" to "do not know". P2 says "I cannot be sure I am not dreaming."
step: The speaker admits P2: "I cannot rule out that I am dreaming." So the reply does not attack P2.
step: The speaker denies that failing to rule out the dream means you do not know. That is a denial of P1.
answer: The reply attacks P1, by saying that knowledge does not require ruling out every possibility.

@example
title: Believing, luck and knowing
problem: Mia wakes up and, with no reason at all, feels sure it is raining. It happens to be raining. Does Mia know it is raining?
step: Check the three parts. Mia believes it is raining, and it is true.
step: But Mia has no good reason for the belief. She would have felt sure of rain on a sunny day too.
step: Being right by luck is different from knowing, so on the traditional account Mia does not know.
answer: On the traditional account, Mia does not know, because her true belief has no good reason behind it.

@mistakes
- Thinking the skeptic claims that you are dreaming. The claim is only that you cannot be sure you are not, and that this threatens what you know.
- Saying that the dream argument is invalid. The conclusion follows from the premises, so a reply must reject a premise.
- Saying that knowledge is just true belief. A lucky guess is true, but it is not knowledge.
- Treating a reply as having "won". A reply raises a new question, and the skeptic usually has a response.
