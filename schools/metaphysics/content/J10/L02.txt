@article
Philosophy can be hard to read, not because the words are long, but because every sentence is doing work. Slow down and read with a pencil. Ask four questions as you go.

### Four questions for a text

1. **What is the question?** What is the author trying to figure out?
2. **What is the conclusion?** What answer does the author give?
3. **What are the reasons?** Which premises are supposed to support it?
4. **What do the key words mean?** If the author uses *free*, *know* or *same*, how do they mean it?

Try putting the argument in your own words in two or three sentences. If you cannot, you have not understood it yet, and that is useful to find out.

### Discussing fairly

Philosophers disagree a lot, and good disagreement has rules.

- **Be charitable.** Before you criticize a view, state it in the strongest, most sensible form you can. Philosophers call this the **principle of charity**.
- **Avoid the straw man.** A **straw man** is a weak, silly version of someone's view that is easy to knock down. If you attack a straw man, you have not answered the real view.
- **Attack the argument, not the person.** Saying "you only think that because you are young" is an **ad hominem**, an attack on the person. It does not show that the reasons are bad.
- **Ask for reasons.** "Why do you think that?" is the most useful question in philosophy.
- **Change your mind when you should.** Changing your view because of a good reason is a success, not a loss.

### Objections and replies

An **objection** says something is wrong with an argument. It can attack a premise ("that premise is false") or the link between the premises and the conclusion ("even if the premises are true, the conclusion does not follow"). A **reply** answers the objection. Much philosophy goes like this: claim, reason, objection, reply.

### A paragraph that works

A good short philosophical paragraph has four parts: a **claim** (your answer), a **reason** for it, the best **objection** you can think of, and your **reply**. Writing the objection yourself, instead of hoping nobody notices it, is what makes the paragraph strong.

@example
title: Reading a short passage
problem: Read this passage and find the question, the conclusion and the reasons: "Should students be allowed to use calculators in math class? I think so. A calculator does the boring arithmetic, so students can spend their time on understanding the ideas. Also, adults use calculators all the time, so learning to use one well is a real skill."
step: The question is asked in the first sentence: should students be allowed to use calculators in math class?
step: The conclusion is "I think so": yes, they should be allowed.
step: The reasons are two premises. P1: A calculator does the boring arithmetic, so students can spend their time on understanding. P2: Adults use calculators all the time, so using one well is a real skill.
answer: Question: should students be allowed calculators in math class? Conclusion: yes. Reasons: it frees time for understanding, and using a calculator well is a real skill.

@example
title: Spotting a straw man
problem: Ana says: "Students should be allowed to listen to music quietly while doing independent work." Ben replies: "So you think students should be allowed to blast music and ignore the teacher all day!" What has Ben done wrong?
step: Compare what Ana said with what Ben says she said. Ana said quietly and only during independent work.
step: Ben turned her view into a much sillier one (loud music, ignoring the teacher) that is easy to reject.
answer: Ben attacked a straw man. A fair reply would answer Ana's actual claim, for example by giving a reason why even quiet music might distract others.

@mistakes
- Arguing against a weaker version of someone's view than the one they hold. First say their view in a form they would agree with.
- Treating "I disagree" as an objection. An objection needs a reason, such as a premise that is false or a step that does not follow.
- Confusing the person with the argument. A good argument can come from someone you dislike, and a bad one from someone you admire.
- Writing an objection that is easy to answer on purpose. A good objection is one that really worries you.

choice*: Some dogs bark and bite, so dogs are not always calming.

@practice self
prompt: Write a short paragraph (five to seven sentences) about a big question that puzzles you, such as whether you can be sure of anything, what makes a friend a real friend, or whether animals have minds. Include a claim, a reason, an objection and a reply.
hint: Start with "I think that..." and then ask yourself, "What is the best thing someone could say against this?"
solution: I think that animals such as dogs can feel happy. My reason is that a dog wags its tail, plays and seems eager when its owner comes home, which are the signs we use to tell that a person is happy. Someone might object that we are only reading our own feelings into the dog, so these signs show nothing about what the dog feels. I reply that we also cannot see inside other people's heads, and we trust their feelings because of how they behave and because they are like us. Dogs have brains and bodies similar to ours in many ways, so it would be strange to trust the signs for people but not for dogs. The objection shows that we cannot be certain, but it does not show that the dog is not happy.
rubric: States a clear claim about a real big question.
rubric: Gives at least one reason for the claim.
rubric: States an objection that a thoughtful person might really make, in a fair form.
rubric: Gives a reply that answers the objection, and any view is acceptable if it is supported by reasons.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose a view about a school rule that you disagree with. In four to six sentences, first state that view in the strongest, fairest way you can. Then give one objection to it.
hint: Try to say the view so well that someone who holds it would say, "Yes, that is what I think."
solution: One view is that students should wear school uniforms. The strongest form of this view says that uniforms reduce pressure about clothes and let students focus on learning, because nobody is judged by what they can afford. Uniforms may also make it easier to feel part of a community. My objection is that clothes are one way students express who they are, so a uniform takes away a harmless kind of self-expression. A supporter of uniforms could reply that students can still express themselves in other ways, which is why the disagreement depends on how much each of these matters.
rubric: States the opposing view fairly, in a form its defenders would accept.
rubric: Includes at least one reason that a supporter of that view would give.
rubric: Gives an objection with a reason, not just "I disagree".
