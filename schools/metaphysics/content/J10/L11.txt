@article
You find a wallet with money and an ID card. Nobody saw you. You could keep the money, and you would enjoy having it. Is it wrong to keep it? Most people say yes. But *why* is it wrong? What makes an action right or wrong? This is the central question of **ethics**, or moral philosophy.

### Three big answers

**Consequentialism** says that what makes an action right is its **consequences**: the right action is the one that produces the best results. The best-known version is **utilitarianism**, defended by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, which says to do what produces the most happiness and the least suffering for everyone affected, counting everyone equally. A utilitarian says that keeping the wallet is wrong because the owner loses more than you gain. An objection: it seems to allow terrible acts if they add up well. Suppose a doctor could save five patients by secretly taking the organs of one healthy visitor. The numbers favor it, but it seems deeply wrong.

**Deontology** says that some actions are right or wrong in themselves, whatever the results, because of duties and rights. Immanuel Kant is the most famous defender. He argued that we should act only on principles that we could will to be universal laws, and that we should treat people always as ends in themselves and never merely as a means. A Kantian says that keeping the wallet is wrong because you would be using the owner's loss as a means to your gain, and because a world in which everyone kept lost property would be a world where no one could trust anyone. An objection: rigid duties can lead to bad results, and it is not clear what to do when duties conflict.

**Virtue ethics** goes back to Aristotle. It asks less "What should I do?" and more "What kind of person should I be?" The right action is what a good person, with virtues such as honesty, courage and kindness, would do. A virtue ethicist says that keeping the wallet is dishonest. An objection: it is not always clear what the virtuous person would do in a hard case, so it may not guide action.

### The survey

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, professional philosophers were split among these views (about 37% virtue ethics, 32% deontology and 31% consequentialism). The shares are not evidence of which is true.

### Testing views with cases

A good way to compare views is to take a case where they disagree and ask which answer seems right and why.

| P1 | An action is right if and only if it produces at least as much overall happiness as any other action available. |
| P2 | Keeping the lost wallet would produce less overall happiness than returning it. |
| C | So keeping the lost wallet is not right. |

This is a utilitarian argument. It is valid, so objections must attack P1 (the theory) or P2 (the facts about the case).

@example
title: Three reasons for one verdict
problem: Maya promised to help a friend move on Saturday, but she was invited to a party that she would enjoy a bit more than helping. Give the reasoning a utilitarian, a Kantian and a virtue ethicist might each give for keeping the promise.
step: Utilitarian: count the results. The friend will be left without help and may be upset, and trust is weakened; this may outweigh Maya's extra enjoyment.
step: Kantian: ask whether she could will that everyone break promises when something nicer comes up. Then promises would mean nothing, so she should keep this one.
step: Virtue ethicist: ask what a trustworthy, loyal person would do. Such a person keeps their word.
answer: All three can recommend keeping the promise, but for different reasons: the results, the universal principle and the kind of person she would be.

@example
title: Which view is this?
problem: A student says: "It is wrong to lie, even if the lie would make everyone happier, because it treats the other person as if they cannot handle the truth." Which view is closest to this, and why?
step: The student says that lying is wrong even when the results are good. That means the results are not what decides.
step: The reason given is about how the person is treated, which fits Kant's idea of respecting people as ends.
answer: Kantian ethics (deontology), because it looks at duty and respect for persons, not the results.

@mistakes
- Thinking that utilitarianism says to do whatever makes you happy. It counts everyone's happiness equally, not just your own.
- Thinking that Kant said that only intentions matter and results never do. He held that a good will and duty are central, but he did not say that results are irrelevant.
- Saying that virtue ethics has no rules or no answer. It answers with the virtues and the example of good people, and it faces the objection that this can be unclear.
- Treating a case where a view gives an odd verdict as a refutation. It is an objection that the view's defenders may answer.
