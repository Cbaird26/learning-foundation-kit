@article
You can feel pain, see red, remember last summer and decide what to eat. Your brain is a wet, gray organ made of cells. How are the two connected? This is the **mind-body problem**.

### Two big answers

**Dualism** says that the mind and the body are two different kinds of thing. The best-known dualist is René Descartes, who argued that the mind is a thinking thing that is not made of matter. A big question for dualists is how a non-physical mind could move a physical body. **Physicalism** says that everything, including your mind, is physical: thinking is something that brains do. A big question for physicalists is why a brain's activity should *feel* like anything from the inside. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about half of professional philosophers accepted or leaned toward physicalism and about a third non-physicalism. That shows the debate is alive, not who is right.

### Could a computer think?

In 1950 the mathematician Alan Turing asked whether a machine could think, and proposed a test. In his **imitation game**, a judge types questions to a hidden human and a hidden machine, and tries to tell which is which. If the machine is good enough that the judge cannot reliably tell, Turing suggested we should be willing to say that it thinks. This is now called the **Turing test**.

In 1980 John Searle gave a famous reply, the **Chinese room**. Imagine you are in a closed room and do not understand Chinese. You have a huge rule book, in English, that tells you which Chinese symbols to send out when particular symbols come in. People outside slip in questions in Chinese and you pass back answers by following the rules. To them, the room understands Chinese. But you understand nothing: you are just moving shapes. Searle's argument is:

| P1 | A computer program is a set of rules for changing symbols according to their shapes. |
| P2 | Following such rules is not enough for understanding what the symbols mean (as the person in the room shows). |
| C | So running a program is not enough for understanding. |

The most common reply is the **systems reply**: the person is only one part of the system. The person plus the rule book plus the room may understand Chinese, even though the person does not, in the way that a single neuron in your brain does not understand English but you do. Searle answered that the person could memorize the whole rule book and still understand nothing. Others reply that the argument shows only that the *person* does not understand, or that the test was not enough.

### Where things stand

As of now there is no agreement among scientists or philosophers about whether any computer system understands or is conscious, or about what test would settle it. The question is being actively researched. The best approach is to be careful about words: *think*, *understand* and *feel* may come apart.

@example
title: Which part does the systems reply attack?
problem: Someone replies to the Chinese room: "The person does not understand Chinese, but the whole room, with the person, the rules and the symbols, might." Which premise of Searle's argument does this reply question?
step: Recall the premises. P1: programs are rules for changing symbols. P2: following such rules is not enough for understanding.
step: The reply accepts that the person understands nothing, but says this does not settle whether the whole system understands. So it questions the step from the person in the room to the claim in P2.
answer: The reply questions P2 (or the move from "the person does not understand" to "following rules is not enough"), because it says that understanding might belong to the whole system.

@example
title: Dualist or physicalist?
problem: A student says: "My thoughts are not made of anything physical, but they can still make my arm move when I decide to wave." Which view is the student expressing, and what question does it face?
step: The student says thoughts are not physical, and that they can still cause physical movements.
step: A view that says the mind is not physical is a form of dualism.
step: The question it faces is how a non-physical thing could make a physical arm move.
answer: Dualism. Its big question is how a non-physical mind could act on a physical body.

@mistakes
- Thinking that the Turing test shows that machines do think. Turing proposed it as a way to approach the question, and critics such as Searle argue that passing it is not enough for understanding.
- Saying that Searle claims that machines could never think. His claim is about running a program, and he held that brains are machines of a kind that do think.
- Confusing dualism with the claim that minds exist. Physicalists agree that minds exist; they say minds are physical.
- Saying that the question about AI is settled. There is no agreement, so be careful with claims in either direction.
