@article
Your last birthday party is over. Dinosaurs are gone. The year you turn thirty has not happened yet. Do the past and the future exist in any sense, or does only the present exist? This is a question about the **philosophy of time**.

### Three views

**Presentism** says that only the present exists. The past used to exist and the future will exist, but neither exists now. Dinosaurs do not exist; they did. This fits the feeling that "now" is special. A challenge: if only the present exists, what makes statements about the past true? "Dinosaurs lived" seems to be about something real.

**Eternalism** says that the past, present and future all exist equally. "Now" is like "here": it picks out where you happen to be. On this view, which is often called the **block universe**, you are as real in your childhood as you are today, although they are at different times. A challenge: it seems to leave out how time passes, and what makes the present special.

**The growing block view** says that the past and the present exist but the future does not yet: the universe grows as new moments are added. A challenge: if the past is real, how do we know that *we* are at the edge, in the present, and not in the past?

### Passing time and relativity

Philosophers also ask whether time really *passes*. Those who say it does hold the **A-theory**, that there is an objective "now" that moves. Those who deny it hold the **B-theory**, that events are just ordered earlier and later, and that "now" depends on who is speaking. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, more philosophers accepted or leaned toward the B-theory (about 38%) than the A-theory (about 27%), but that does not decide who is right.

Physics enters the debate. Albert Einstein's special theory of relativity, which is extremely well confirmed, shows that two observers moving differently can disagree about which distant events are happening "at the same time". Many philosophers think this makes a single, universal "now" hard to defend, and favors eternalism. Presentists reply that relativity does not decide the matter, and that they can pick a preferred way of defining "now". The question is still open.

### The ship of Theseus

This puzzle (see last week) is also about time: what does it take for a thing to stay the same *through* time? A ship has all its planks replaced gradually. Later, someone builds a second ship from the original planks. Which ship, if either, is the original?

- **Answer 1: the repaired ship is the original.** It has been continuous at each stage, kept the same form and was always in use. A ship is not defined by its matter, which any ship swaps over time.
- **Answer 2: the rebuilt ship is the original.** It has all the original material, and the repair simply replaced the ship with another.
- **Answer 3: neither, or it depends.** "Same ship" is partly a matter of how we choose to use the words, so there may be no single fact to find.

@mistakes
- Confusing presentism with the A-theory. Presentism is about what exists; the A-theory is about whether time objectively passes. The views are related but not identical.
- Saying that relativity proves eternalism. Many find it a reason to prefer eternalism, but philosophers still disagree.
- Treating the block universe as saying that you cannot change anything. Eternalism says that all times exist, not that your choices make no difference.
- Mixing up the two ships. The repaired ship has the original ship's history of continuous use; the rebuilt ship has its original wood.
