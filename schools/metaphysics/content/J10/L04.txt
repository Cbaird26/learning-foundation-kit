@article
A straight stick looks bent when you put it half in a pool. The Moon looks as large as a dinner plate. A table feels solid, yet science tells us it is made of tiny particles with space between them. Things do not always seem as they are. The **appearance** of something is how it looks, sounds or feels to us. The **reality** is how it is. **Metaphysics**, the part of philosophy about what exists and what it is like, begins with that gap.

### Plato's cave

In Book 7 of Plato's *Republic*, a character called Socrates describes prisoners who have lived all their lives chained in a cave, facing a wall. Behind them is a fire, and people carry objects past it, so the prisoners see only shadows on the wall. They take the shadows for the real things. If one prisoner were freed and led outside, the sunlight would hurt at first, but eventually that prisoner would see the real world and realize how limited the shadow-world was. The story is a picture of how we can mistake appearances for reality, and of how learning can feel like being turned around.

### The brain in a vat

Modern philosophers use a science-fiction version of the dream argument. Imagine that your brain has been taken out of your body and kept alive in a tank, with a computer feeding it exactly the signals it would get from a normal life. Your experiences would be the same as they are now. So how do you know you are not a **brain in a vat**? The skeptic argues that you cannot tell, so you cannot know what the world outside is like.

One reply, from Hilary Putnam, says that the story defeats itself. Words get their meaning from connections to things. A vat-brain that has only ever been connected to a computer would use the word "tree" to talk about computer images, not real trees. So when it thinks "I am a brain in a vat", it would not mean what we mean, and the thought would be false. This reply is clever, and philosophers still argue about whether it works.

### Idealism

Some philosophers have gone further and said that the world may not be as the skeptic thinks at all. **Idealism** is the view that reality is made of minds and their ideas. George Berkeley argued that physical things are collections of ideas in minds, and that for such things "to be is to be perceived". This is not the same as the dream argument. The skeptic says we cannot know whether there is a physical world, and the idealist says that what we call the physical world is itself made up of ideas. The opposite view, often called **realism** about the external world, holds that the physical world exists whether or not anyone perceives it.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey of professional philosophers, about four in five accepted or leaned toward realism about the external world, but that does not tell us which view is true.

@example
title: Appearance or reality?
problem: A friend says: "The road ahead looks wet on a hot day, but when I get there it is dry." Which part is the appearance and which is the reality?
step: Ask what the friend experienced. From far away, the road looked wet. That is the appearance.
step: Ask what turned out to be so when the friend checked. The road is dry. That is the reality.
step: The example shows that appearances can be wrong, but it also shows that we found the reality by checking, which is what skeptics ask us to explain.
answer: Appearance: the road looks wet. Reality: the road is dry.

@example
title: What does the brain-in-a-vat story ask?
problem: Explain in two steps how the brain-in-a-vat story is supposed to threaten knowledge.
step: The story says a brain in a vat could have exactly the same experiences as you do now, because a computer feeds it the signals.
step: If you could not tell the difference from the inside, then your experiences alone do not show that you are not a vat-brain, and so it seems you cannot know that the world is as it seems.
answer: Same experiences in both cases, so experience alone cannot tell you which case you are in. That is why it seems to threaten knowledge.

@mistakes
- Saying that appearances are always false. A stick looks bent in water, but most appearances (the cup in front of you) are correct.
- Confusing idealism with skepticism. The skeptic says we cannot know whether the world is physical, and the idealist says what exists is minds and ideas.
- Thinking that Plato's cave is about a real cave. It is a story that stands for the way we can take shadows for the real thing.
- Treating Putnam's reply as a proof. It is one argument, and many philosophers think it leaves something unanswered.
