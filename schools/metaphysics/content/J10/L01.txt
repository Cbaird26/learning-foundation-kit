@article
Is a color the same for everyone? Could you be wrong about something you are sure of? Is it ever right to break a promise? You can have a good talk about each of these questions, but no lab test and no encyclopedia will simply hand you the answer. These are **philosophical questions**.

The word **philosophy** comes from Greek words meaning "love of wisdom". Philosophers ask big questions about knowledge, reality, minds, freedom, right and wrong, and how to live. They do not just give opinions. They give **reasons**.

### Questions that experiments can settle, and questions that they cannot

"How many moons does Mars have?" is answered by looking through a telescope. "How long does it take sound to cross a gym?" is answered by measuring. These are questions of science and fact. A question like "What is it to be fair?" is different. People who agree on every fact about a case can still disagree about whether it was fair. Philosophy begins where facts alone do not settle the matter, and often it is about the words and ideas we use every day: *knowledge*, *mind*, *time*, *fair*, *right*.

Philosophy and science are not enemies. Many sciences began as parts of philosophy, and good philosophers pay close attention to what science finds out.

### Arguments

In everyday life an "argument" is a fight. In philosophy an **argument** is a set of reasons for believing something. The reasons are called **premises** and the claim they are meant to support is the **conclusion**. A philosopher sets out an argument like this:

| P1 | Every living thing needs energy to stay alive. |
| P2 | A flame needs fuel to keep burning, but a flame is not alive: it does not grow, reproduce or breathe. |
| C | So needing energy is not enough to make something alive. |

Words like *because*, *since*, *so* and *therefore* often signal an argument. *Because* and *since* usually introduce a premise; *so* and *therefore* usually introduce the conclusion. To judge an argument you ask two questions: if the premises were true, would the conclusion have to be true or likely to be true? And are the premises really true?

### Thought experiments

Sometimes the best way to test an idea is to imagine a case. A **thought experiment** is an imagined situation, with the details chosen carefully, used to see what we would say about it. In Plato's *Republic* (Book 2), a character tells the story of a shepherd who finds a ring that makes him invisible, and asks whether a person who could never be caught would still bother to be just. The story is not meant to be real. It is meant to make you think about why people behave well.

A thought experiment works when you notice what you would say about the case, and then ask why. Other philosophers may answer differently, and then you have a real disagreement to work on.

@example
title: Finding the conclusion
problem: Find the conclusion and the premises: "You should not copy a friend's homework, because it is not fair to the students who did their own work, and because you would not learn the lesson yourself."
step: Look for the signal word. "Because" tells you that what follows gives reasons, so the claim before it is probably the conclusion.
step: The conclusion is "You should not copy a friend's homework."
step: There are two premises. P1: Copying is not fair to the students who did their own work. P2: If you copy, you will not learn the lesson yourself.
answer: Conclusion: you should not copy a friend's homework. Premises: it is unfair to other students, and you would not learn the lesson.

@example
title: Fact question or philosophical question?
problem: Sort these two questions: (a) "How many bones does an adult human hand have?" (b) "Is a person who tells a lie to protect a friend's feelings doing something wrong?"
step: Ask whether a careful measurement or look-up could settle it. For (a), yes: count them in an anatomy book or an X-ray.
step: For (b), two people can agree on exactly what was said, who was hurt and who was helped, and still disagree. The disagreement is about what makes an action wrong.
answer: Question (a) is a fact question for science and look-up. Question (b) is a philosophical question about right and wrong.

@mistakes
- Thinking that a philosophical question is just a matter of opinion. Some answers are better than others because they have better reasons.
- Using "argument" only to mean a quarrel. In philosophy it means reasons given for a conclusion.
- Confusing the conclusion with a premise. The conclusion is the claim the reasons are meant to support, not the reasons themselves.
- Thinking that a thought experiment is pointless because it could never happen. It tests our ideas, not our travel plans.

@practice self
prompt: Write one philosophical question of your own, then explain in two or three sentences why a lab experiment or an encyclopedia could not simply settle it.
hint: Try a question that starts with "What is..." or "Is it ever...", about something like fairness, friendship or knowledge.
solution: Question: Can two people be best friends if one of them never tells the other the truth? An experiment could measure how many people say they have such friendships, and an encyclopedia could report what people think. But neither could tell us what a friend is or what friendship requires. That depends on which reasons for and against seem strongest, so it has to be worked out by giving arguments and testing them against cases.

@quiz self
prompt: Describe a short thought experiment of your own (two or three sentences) about a question such as fairness, honesty or friendship. Then say what you would answer about it, and give one reason.
hint: Imagine a case, change one detail, and notice whether your answer changes.
solution: Imagine a game in which one team is allowed to see the other team's secret plan before the match. Both teams still play hard and the rules are the same for everyone. I would say the game is not fair, because fairness is not only about following the written rules but also about each side having an equal chance to compete. Someone could reply that if both teams are allowed to peek, it is fair again, which shows that whether the rules apply equally matters to the idea of fairness.
rubric: Describes an imagined case with enough detail to think about.
rubric: States what the writer would say about the case and gives at least one reason.
rubric: Connects the case to the idea it tests, such as fairness, without needing to agree with the model answer.
