@article
Ana is a bachelor, so she is unmarried. That is not just true: it could not have been otherwise, given what "bachelor" means. But it does not follow that Ana is *necessarily* unmarried. She could have married last year, in which case she would not have been a bachelor. Ordinary logic cannot even state this difference: both claims are about the same facts, and the only thing that changes is how strongly they are claimed. **Modal logic** adds two operators that let us say how things are: that they must be so, or might be so.

### The language

The **basic modal language** has sentence letters $P, Q, R, \ldots$, the constant $\bot$, the connectives $\neg$, $\wedge$, $\vee$, $\to$, $\leftrightarrow$, and two new operators: $\Box$, read "necessarily", and $\Diamond$, read "possibly". Formulas are built by these rules:

- every sentence letter, and $\bot$, is a formula;
- if $A$ is a formula, so are $\neg A$, $\Box A$ and $\Diamond A$;
- if $A$ and $B$ are formulas, so are $(A \wedge B)$, $(A \vee B)$, $(A \to B)$ and $(A \leftrightarrow B)$.

The **modal depth** of a formula is the greatest number of modal operators nested inside one another: letters have depth 0, connectives take the maximum of their parts, and $\Box A$ and $\Diamond A$ have depth one more than $A$. The formula $\Box(\Diamond P \wedge Q)$ has depth 2, because the $\Diamond$ is nested inside the $\Box$, while $\Box P \wedge \Diamond Q$ has two operators but depth 1.

The symbol $\Box$ has several readings, and the same logic can serve them all: metaphysical necessity ("it could not have been otherwise"), knowledge ("Ana knows that"), obligation ("it ought to be that"), and time ("it will always be that"). Lessons 3, 5 and 10 return to these. The metaphysical reading is the main one in this course.

### Not truth-functional

The connective $\wedge$ is truth-functional: the truth value of $A \wedge B$ is fixed by the truth values of $A$ and $B$. The operator $\Box$ is not. Take two true sentences: "$2 + 2 = 4$" and "Ana owns a watch". Prefix "necessarily" to each: the first stays true, the second becomes false. Two true inputs give different outputs, so no truth table defines $\Box$. This is why modal logic needs a richer semantics (Lesson 2): we must look at *how else things could have been*, not only at how things are.

### Duality

Necessity and possibility are interdefinable. "It is possible that $A$" means that it is not necessary that $A$ fails:

$$\Diamond A \leftrightarrow \neg\Box\neg A \qquad\qquad \Box A \leftrightarrow \neg\Diamond\neg A$$

From these come $\neg\Box A \leftrightarrow \Diamond\neg A$ and $\neg\Diamond A \leftrightarrow \Box\neg A$. The pattern mirrors the quantifiers, $\exists x \leftrightarrow \neg\forall x\neg$, and on the possible-worlds reading it is no accident: $\Box A$ says that $A$ holds in *all* the relevant worlds and $\Diamond A$ that it holds in *some*. Many presentations take $\Box$ as primitive and write $\Diamond A$ for $\neg\Box\neg A$.

### Scope

Where the operator sits matters. $\Box(B \to U)$ ("necessarily, if Ana is a bachelor she is unmarried") and $B \to \Box U$ ("if Ana is a bachelor, she is necessarily unmarried") are different formulas; the first is plausible, the second is not. Confusing them is the **scope fallacy** of modal reasoning. What does hold is the **necessity of the consequence** argument:

| P1 | $\Box(P \to Q)$: necessarily, if the vase falls then it breaks. |
| P2 | $\Box P$: necessarily, the vase falls. |
| C | $\Box Q$: necessarily, the vase breaks. |

This argument is valid (we prove it in Lesson 6). It does not show that the vase must break, because the work is done by premise P2, which a critic will deny unless fatalism is true. The *invalid* variant with P2 replaced by the plain "the vase falls" would, if valid, turn every contingent fact that has a necessary consequence into a necessity. Lesson 2 gives a model that shows it is invalid.

@example
title: Two forms of the same claim
problem: Show that $\neg\Box(P \to Q)$ and $\Diamond(P \wedge \neg Q)$ are equivalent, using duality.
step: Start from the right-hand side and unpack the diamond: $\Diamond(P \wedge \neg Q)$ is $\neg\Box\neg(P \wedge \neg Q)$.
step: Inside the box, $\neg(P \wedge \neg Q)$ is equivalent to $P \to Q$ by propositional logic.
step: Equivalent formulas may replace one another under $\Box$ (if $A \leftrightarrow B$ is valid, so is $\Box A \leftrightarrow \Box B$), so $\neg\Box\neg(P \wedge \neg Q)$ is equivalent to $\neg\Box(P \to Q)$.
answer: $\neg\Box(P \to Q) \leftrightarrow \Diamond(P \wedge \neg Q)$. In words: it is not necessary that if $P$ then $Q$ exactly when it is possible that $P$ holds and $Q$ fails.

@example
title: Reading and rewriting a formula
problem: Let $R$ be "the vase is dropped" and $S$ be "the vase survives". Read $\Box(R \to \Diamond S)$ in English, give its modal depth, and rewrite it using only $\Box$ and $\neg$.
step: In English: necessarily, if the vase is dropped then it is possible that it survives.
step: Depth: $\Diamond S$ has depth 1, so $R \to \Diamond S$ has depth 1, and the outer $\Box$ adds one: the depth is 2.
step: Replace $\Diamond S$ by $\neg\Box\neg S$. The formula becomes $\Box(R \to \neg\Box\neg S)$, which uses only $\Box$, $\neg$ and $\to$ (and $\to$ is definable from $\neg$ and $\vee$ or $\wedge$ if you want to go further).
answer: Depth 2; equivalent to $\Box(R \to \neg\Box\neg S)$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\Box$ as a truth table: "$P$ is true, so $\Box P$ is true" is the fallacy that everything true is necessary.
- Mixing up $\neg\Diamond A$ with $\Diamond\neg A$: "it is impossible that $A$" is $\Box\neg A$, while $\Diamond\neg A$ says only that $A$ could fail.
- Moving $\Box$ across a conditional: $\Box(A \to B)$ is not $A \to \Box B$ (the scope fallacy).
- Counting modal depth by counting operators: $\Box P \wedge \Diamond Q$ has two operators but depth 1.

@practice self
prompt: Let $B$ be "Ana is a bachelor" and $U$ be "Ana is unmarried". Translate (a) "It is necessary that if Ana is a bachelor then she is unmarried" and (b) "If Ana is a bachelor, then it is necessary that she is unmarried". Explain why (a) is plausible and (b) is not.
hint: Ask whether Ana could have married, and what "bachelor" means.
solution: (a) is $\Box(B \to U)$ and (b) is $B \to \Box U$. Being a bachelor means being an unmarried man, so in every possible situation in which Ana is a bachelor she is unmarried: (a) holds because of what "bachelor" means. (b) says that, given that Ana is in fact a bachelor, she is unmarried in every possible situation. But Ana could have married, so there are possible situations in which $U$ is false, and $\Box U$ is false even though $B$ is true. The error in reading (a) as (b) is a scope mistake: the necessity attaches to the whole conditional, not to its consequent.

rubric: Explains that (a) is true because of what "bachelor" means, in every possible situation.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which formula says "It is impossible that Ana wins and Ben wins", with $W$ for "Ana wins" and $V$ for "Ben wins"?
choice*: $\neg\Diamond(W \wedge V)$
choice: $\Diamond\neg(W \wedge V)$
choice: $\neg\Diamond W \wedge \neg\Diamond V$
choice: $\Box W \wedge \Box V$
hint: "Impossible that" is $\neg\Diamond$ of the whole claim.
solution: Impossibility is the negation of possibility, applied to the whole conjunction: $\neg\Diamond(W \wedge V)$, equivalently $\Box\neg(W \wedge V)$. The formula $\neg\Diamond W \wedge \neg\Diamond V$ says that each win is impossible separately, which is much stronger, and $\Diamond\neg(W \wedge V)$ says only that they could fail to co-occur.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, using two true sentences of your own choosing, why the operator "necessarily" is not truth-functional. Then say what this shows about the kind of semantics modal logic needs.
hint: Find one true sentence that is necessarily true and one that is true but not necessarily true.
solution: Take the true sentences "Seven is a prime number" and "It is raining in Lisbon today" (or any contingent truth). Both are true. "Necessarily, seven is a prime number" is true, but "Necessarily, it is raining in Lisbon today" is false, since it could have been dry. A truth-functional operator has one output for each input, but here the input T gives both T and F, so "necessarily" has no truth table. Its truth depends on more than the actual truth value of the sentence: on how the sentence fares in other possible situations. So the semantics must provide those situations, as possible-worlds models do.
rubric: Gives two true sentences, one necessary and one contingent.
rubric: Concludes correctly that the same input value gives different outputs, so there is no truth table.
rubric: Says that the semantics must look at how things stand in other possible situations.
