@article
Semantic arguments, with models and frames, show what is valid. A **proof** shows that a formula is a theorem, using only rules that can be checked line by line. By soundness and completeness (Lesson 5) the two notions agree, but proofs are often shorter than a check over all frames, and they show *why* a formula holds.

### Axiomatic proofs

A **proof** of a formula $A$ in a normal system is a finite list of formulas ending in $A$, each of which is justified as one of:

- **PL**: a tautological consequence of the lines cited (or a tautology, citing nothing);
- an **axiom**: K, or in the stronger systems T, 4, B or 5;
- **MP**: modus ponens on two cited lines;
- **Nec**: if a cited line is a theorem proved without premises, its box is also a theorem.

Throughout, $\Diamond A$ is an abbreviation of $\neg\Box\neg A$, so a step such as $\Box\neg P \to \neg\Diamond P$ is just the tautology $X \to \neg\neg X$ with $X = \Box\neg P$. Since these proofs prove theorems, with no premises, Nec is always safe. It is not safe with assumptions: from the assumption $P$ one cannot conclude $\Box P$.

Two derived rules save work. **RM** (monotonicity): from a proved $A \to B$ infer $\Box A \to \Box B$.

```text
1  A → B                        (proved)
2  □(A → B)                     Nec 1
3  □(A → B) → (□A → □B)         K
4  □A → □B                      MP 2, 3
```

**RE**: from a proved $A \leftrightarrow B$ infer $\Box A \leftrightarrow \Box B$: apply RM to each direction and combine with PL.

### Strict subproofs

A proof from premises is easier in a Fitch-style system with **strict subproofs**. Such a subproof, drawn as a box, begins with the line `box` and has no assumption. Inside it you may write $A$ whenever $\Box A$ stands on an earlier line outside the box (rule $\Box$R, reiteration). When the last line of the box is $B$, you may close the box and write $\Box B$ outside (rule $\Box$I). Here is the argument of Lesson 1, the necessity of the consequence:

```text
1 | □(P → Q)         PR
2 | □P               PR
  |---
3 | | box
  | |---
4 | | P → Q          □R 1
5 | | P              □R 2
6 | | Q              →E 4, 5
7 | □Q               □I 3–6
```

Nothing but boxed formulas may enter the box, which is why $P$ alone (without a box) is not available: this is the proof-theoretic form of the scope fallacy. To get the system for T, add the rule "from $\Box A$ infer $A$"; for S4, allow $\Box A$ itself to be reiterated into a box.

### Five theorems of S4 and S5

Each proof is a schema: $P$ stands for any formula.

**1. S4 proves $\Box P \leftrightarrow \Box\Box P$.**

```text
1  □P → □□P        4
2  □□P → □P        T (with A := □P)
3  □P ↔ □□P        PL 1, 2
```

**2. S4 proves $\Diamond\Diamond P \to \Diamond P$.**

```text
1  □¬P → □□¬P      4 (with A := ¬P)
2  □¬P → ¬◇P       PL
3  □□¬P → □¬◇P     RM 2
4  □¬P → □¬◇P      PL 1, 3
5  ¬□¬◇P → ¬□¬P    PL 4
```

Line 5 is $\Diamond\Diamond P \to \Diamond P$ written out.

**3. S5 proves $P \to \Box\Diamond P$ (axiom B).**

```text
1  □¬P → ¬P        T (with A := ¬P)
2  P → ¬□¬P        PL 1
3  ¬□¬P → □¬□¬P    5 (with A := P)
4  P → □¬□¬P       PL 2, 3
```

Line 4 is $P \to \Box\Diamond P$. For the next theorem we need a lemma, which holds in S5 for any $A$: **$\neg\Box A \to \Box\neg\Box A$**.

```text
1  ¬□¬¬A → □¬□¬¬A   5 (with A := ¬A)
2  ¬¬A ↔ A          PL
3  □¬¬A ↔ □A        RE 2
4  ¬□¬¬A ↔ ¬□A      PL 3
5  □¬□¬¬A ↔ □¬□A    RE 4
6  ¬□A → □¬□A       PL 1, 4, 5
```

**4. S5 proves $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box P$.**

```text
1  ¬□P → □¬□P      lemma (with A := P)
2  ¬□¬□P → ¬¬□P    PL 1
3  ¬□¬□P → □P      PL 2
```

Line 3 is $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box P$.

**5. S5 proves $\Box P \to \Box\Box P$ (axiom 4).**

```text
1  □P → □◇□P       theorem 3 (with P := □P)
2  ◇□P → □P        theorem 4
3  □◇□P → □□P      RM 2
4  □P → □□P        PL 1, 3
```

So S5 as T plus 5 proves both B and 4, and it proves everything S4 does. The proofs also show how axioms combine: theorem 5 is the proof-theoretic counterpart of "reflexive and Euclidean implies transitive".

@example
title: A proof in K by RM
problem: Prove in K that $\Box(P \wedge Q) \to \Box P$.
step: The formula $(P \wedge Q) \to P$ is a tautology, so line 1 is justified by PL.
step: Apply RM to line 1: from $A \to B$ with $A := P \wedge Q$ and $B := P$ we get $\Box(P \wedge Q) \to \Box P$.
step: The finished proof has two lines:

```text
1  (P ∧ Q) → P        PL
2  □(P ∧ Q) → □P      RM 1
```
answer: Line 2 is the theorem; RM stands for Nec, K and MP applied to line 1.

@example
title: A proof in S5 with the lemma
problem: Prove in S5 that $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box\Box P$.
step: Theorem 4 gives $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box P$, and theorem 5 gives $\Box P \to \Box\Box P$.
step: The two conditionals chain by propositional logic, since the consequent of the first is the antecedent of the second.
step: The proof, citing the theorems already proved:

```text
1  ◇□P → □P        theorem 4
2  □P → □□P        theorem 5
3  ◇□P → □□P       PL 1, 2
```
answer: $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box\Box P$ is a theorem of S5. (Semantically: on an equivalence frame, if some accessible world has $\Box P$ then every accessible world does.)

@mistakes
- Applying Nec to an assumption or premise: from $P$ one cannot infer $\Box P$. Nec applies only to theorems.
- Citing PL when a modal step is needed: $\Box A \to \Box B$ does not follow tautologically from $A \to B$; you need RM (or Nec and K).
- Forgetting that $\Diamond$ is an abbreviation: $\Diamond\Diamond P$ is $\neg\Box\neg\neg\Box\neg P$, and the double negation matters when you use RM or RE.
- Reiterating an unboxed formula into a strict subproof, which is exactly the scope fallacy.

@practice num

answer: 2
unit: valuations
check: sum(1 for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=3) if all(bits) or not any(bits))

@quiz mc
prompt: In a proof in S5, line 2 is $\Diamond\Box P \to \Box P$ and line 3 is $\Box\Diamond\Box P \to \Box\Box P$. Which justification fits line 3?
choice*: RM, applied to line 2
choice: Nec, applied to line 2
choice: PL, applied to line 2

@quiz num
prompt: Consider the frame with worlds 1, 2 and 3 and accessibility $\{(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,2), (2,3), (1,3)\}$. For how many of the 8 valuations of $P$ is $P \to \Box\Diamond P$ false at some world?
answer: 3
unit: valuations
check: sum(1 for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=3) if any(bits[w - 1] == 1 and any(not any(bits[u - 1] for u in {1: [1, 2, 3], 2: [2, 3], 3: [3]}[v]) for v in {1: [1, 2, 3], 2: [2, 3], 3: [3]}[w]) for w in (1, 2, 3)))
hint: The formula fails at $w$ if $P$ holds at $w$ and some successor of $w$ has no successor where $P$ holds. Ask what world 3 sees.
solution: World 3 sees only itself, and every world sees 3. So $\Diamond P$ is true at 3 only if $P$ is true at 3, and $\Box\Diamond P$ at any world requires $\Diamond P$ at 3, hence $P$ at 3. If $P$ is true at 3, every world sees a $P$-world (every world sees 3), so the formula is true everywhere. If $P$ is false at 3, the formula fails at any world where $P$ is true, that is, whenever $P$ is true at 1 or at 2. With $P$ false at 3 there are 4 valuations of the other two worlds, and 3 of them make $P$ true at 1 or 2. So the answer is 3.
