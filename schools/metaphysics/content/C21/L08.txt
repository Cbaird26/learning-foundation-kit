@article
Ana has no children. Yet she might have had a child. Is there something that could have been Ana's child? If you say yes, what is it? If you say no, how can it be possible that she has a child? This is the problem of **possible objects**, and the logic of the quantifiers and modal operators together settles how it must be answered, or at least what each answer costs.

### The language and the semantics

Add to the basic modal language predicate letters ($F$, $G$, a binary $R$), variables ($x$, $y$, $z$), the identity sign $=$ and the quantifiers $\forall x$ and $\exists x$. A **model** is $M = (W, R, D, I)$:

- $W$ and $R$ are as before;
- $D$ assigns to each world $w$ a non-empty set $D(w)$, the objects that **exist** at $w$, and $U = \bigcup_w D(w)$ is the set of all objects;
- $I$ assigns to each predicate and each world an extension, a set of objects (or tuples of objects) from $U$.

An **assignment** $g$ maps variables to objects of $U$. Truth at a world under an assignment is defined by the earlier clauses, with these new ones:

- $M, w, g \vDash Fx$ iff $g(x) \in I(F, w)$, and $M, w, g \vDash x = y$ iff $g(x) = g(y)$;
- $M, w, g \vDash \forall x A$ iff $M, w, g[x \mapsto a] \vDash A$ for every $a \in D(w)$, and $\exists x A$ iff for some $a \in D(w)$;
- $M, w, g \vDash \Box A$ iff $M, v, g \vDash A$ for every $v$ with $wRv$, with the same assignment.

The quantifiers at a world range over the objects that *exist at that world*, and variables name objects rigidly. Two features are worth noting. Since $g$ is held fixed, $x = y \to \Box\, x = y$ is valid: the necessity of identity. And we allow $I(F, w)$ to contain objects that do not exist at $w$; some authors require $I(F, w) \subseteq D(w)$ (so that only existing things have properties, a view called serious actualism), which changes some correspondences below. This lesson uses the unrestricted convention.

A **frame** now includes the domains: $(W, R, D)$. It is **constant-domain** if $D(w) = D(v)$ for all $w, v$; **increasing** if $wRv$ implies $D(w) \subseteq D(v)$ (things persist from a world to its alternatives, and new ones can appear); **decreasing** if $wRv$ implies $D(v) \subseteq D(w)$.

### The Barcan formula and its converse

$$\text{BF: } \forall x\,\Box Fx \to \Box\,\forall x\,Fx \qquad\qquad \text{CBF: } \Box\,\forall x\,Fx \to \forall x\,\Box Fx$$

BF is named after Ruth Barcan Marcus, who proposed it in her early work on quantified modal logic. In the presence of duality, BF is equivalent to $\Diamond\exists x\,Fx \to \exists x\,\Diamond Fx$ (if possibly something is $F$, then something is possibly $F$), and CBF to $\exists x\,\Diamond Fx \to \Diamond\exists x\,Fx$. These say something about possible objects, which is why they are controversial.

**Theorem.** A frame $(W, R, D)$ validates BF iff it is decreasing, and validates CBF iff it is increasing. So both are valid on constant-domain frames.

*Proof for CBF.* ($\Leftarrow$) Let the frame be increasing, let $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ be true at $w$, let $a \in D(w)$ and $wRv$. Then $a \in D(v)$, and $\forall x\,Fx$ is true at $v$, so $a \in I(F, v)$. Hence $\Box Fx$ holds of $a$, and $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ holds at $w$. ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $wRv$ and $a \in D(w) \setminus D(v)$. Let $I(F, u) = D(u)$ at every world $u$. Then $\forall x\,Fx$ is true at every world, so $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is true at $w$; but $a \notin I(F, v)$, so $\Box Fx$ fails of $a$ and $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ is false at $w$.

*Proof for BF.* ($\Leftarrow$) Let the frame be decreasing, let $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ be true at $w$, $wRv$ and $a \in D(v)$. Then $a \in D(w)$, so $\Box Fx$ holds of $a$ at $w$, hence $a \in I(F, v)$. So $\forall x\,Fx$ holds at $v$, and $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ at $w$. ($\Rightarrow$) Suppose $wRv$ and $a \in D(v) \setminus D(w)$. Let $I(F, u) = U \setminus \{a\}$ at every world. Every $x \in D(w)$ differs from $a$, so $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ is true at $w$; but $a \in D(v)$ and $a \notin I(F, v)$, so $\forall x\,Fx$ is false at $v$, and $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is false at $w$. $\square$

If $R$ is also symmetric, as in S5, then BF and CBF each force the domains within a cluster of worlds to be equal.

### An argument from the Barcan formula

| P1 | $\Diamond\exists x\,Cx$: it is possible that Ana has a child ($Cx$: $x$ is a child of Ana), although she has none. |
| P2 | $\Diamond\exists x\,Cx \to \exists x\,\Diamond Cx$ (BF, in its equivalent form). |
| C | $\exists x\,\Diamond Cx$: there is something that could have been Ana's child. |

The argument is valid (modus ponens). P1 is plausible. If the conclusion is unacceptable, because nothing that exists is such that it could have been Ana's child, then P2 must go. A **contingentist** (someone who holds that what exists could have been different) denies P2 and has a ready semantics, varying domains with a possible child existing only at the other worlds. A **necessitist** such as Timothy Williamson accepts the conclusion, understanding the possible child as an object that exists but is not concrete, a view Lesson 12 develops. A further reply says that the conclusion, read de re, is false but that its de dicto cousin $\Diamond\exists x\,Cx$ is true, and BF is the principle that illegitimately converts the one into the other.

@example
title: Domains and the Barcan formula
problem: Let $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w, v)\}$, $D(w) = \{a\}$, $D(v) = \{a, b\}$, and let $F$ have extension $\{a\}$ at both worlds. Evaluate BF and CBF at $w$.
step: The domains increase along $R$ ($D(w) \subseteq D(v)$), so CBF is valid on this frame and is true at $w$.
step: BF at $w$: the only object existing at $w$ is $a$, and $\Box Fx$ holds of $a$ because $a \in I(F, v)$. So $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ is true at $w$.
step: But $\forall x\,Fx$ at $v$ requires $F$ of both $a$ and $b$, and $b \notin I(F, v)$. So $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is false at $w$.
step: Hence BF is false at $w$: the antecedent is true and the consequent false. The new object $b$ at $v$ is exactly what the proof of the theorem exploits.
answer: CBF is true at $w$ and BF is false at $w$, as the theorem predicts for increasing domains.

@example
title: A countermodel for CBF
problem: Show that CBF can fail when domains decrease.
step: Take $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w, v)\}$, $D(w) = \{a, b\}$, $D(v) = \{a\}$, with $I(F, w) = \{a, b\}$ and $I(F, v) = \{a\}$.
step: At $v$, the only existing object is $a$ and $a \in I(F, v)$, so $\forall x\,Fx$ is true at $v$; hence $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is true at $w$.
step: But $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ at $w$ needs $\Box Fx$ to hold of $b$, and $b \notin I(F, v)$. So it is false at $w$.
step: The antecedent of CBF is true and the consequent false at $w$.
answer: CBF fails at $w$. The object $b$ exists at $w$ but not at the alternative $v$, and there it lacks the property.

@mistakes
- Treating $\Diamond\exists x\,Fx$ and $\exists x\,\Diamond Fx$ as the same: the first is de dicto (possibly something is $F$), the second de re (something is such that it could be $F$).
- Reversing the domain conditions: BF needs *decreasing* domains and CBF *increasing* ones (BF fails when a new object can appear; CBF when an object can disappear).
- Forgetting that a quantifier inside the box looks at the domain of the world where it is evaluated.
- Assuming that the Barcan formula is a theorem of every quantified modal logic: it holds on constant domains, but varying-domain semantics invalidates it.

@practice num
prompt: A model has worlds $p$, $q$ and $r$ with $R = \{(p, q), (q, r)\}$ and domains $D(p) = \{a\}$, $D(q) = \{a, b\}$ and $D(r) = \{a, b, c\}$. The predicate $F$ has extension $\varnothing$ at $p$, $\{a\}$ at $q$ and $\{a, b\}$ at $r$. At how many of the three worlds is the Barcan formula $\forall x\,\Box Fx \to \Box\forall x\,Fx$ false?
answer: 2
unit: worlds
check: sum(1 for u in 'pqr' if (lambda D, S, F: all(all(x in F[v] for v in S[u]) for x in D[u]) and not all(all(x in F[v] for x in D[v]) for v in S[u]))({'p': {'a'}, 'q': {'a', 'b'}, 'r': {'a', 'b', 'c'}}, {'p': ['q'], 'q': ['r'], 'r': []}, {'p': set(), 'q': {'a'}, 'r': {'a', 'b'}}))
hint: Check each world separately; a world with no accessible worlds makes every box formula true.
solution: At $p$, the antecedent is true (the one existing object $a$ is $F$ at $q$), and the consequent is false (at $q$, $b$ exists and is not $F$): the formula is false. At $q$, the antecedent is true ($a$ and $b$ are both $F$ at $r$), and the consequent is false (at $r$, $c$ exists and is not $F$): the formula is false. At $r$, which sees no world, both $\Box Fx$ and $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ hold vacuously, so the conditional is true. The count is 2.

prompt: A model has worlds $1, 2, 3$ with $R = \{(1, 2), (2, 3)\}$, domains $D(1) = \{a, b, c\}$, $D(2) = \{a, b\}$, $D(3) = \{a\}$, and $F$ has at each world exactly the existing objects as its extension. At how many worlds is the converse Barcan formula $\Box\forall x\,Fx \to \forall x\,\Box Fx$ false?
answer: 2
unit: worlds
check: sum(1 for u in (1, 2, 3) if (lambda D, S: all(all(x in D[v] for x in D[v]) for v in S[u]) and not all(all(x in D[v] for v in S[u]) for x in D[u]))({1: {'a', 'b', 'c'}, 2: {'a', 'b'}, 3: {'a'}}, {1: [2], 2: [3], 3: []}))
hint: $\Box Fx$ holds of an object $a$ at $w$ when $a$ exists at every world accessible from $w$.
solution: Since $F$ is true of exactly the existing objects, $\forall x\,Fx$ is true at every world, so $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is true everywhere. The consequent $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ at world 1 fails for $c$, which does not exist at world 2; at world 2 it fails for $b$, which is missing at world 3; at world 3 it holds vacuously. So the formula is false at worlds 1 and 2: 2 worlds.

@quiz self
prompt: Construct a model with two worlds $w$ and $v$, $wRv$, and domains $D(w) = \{a, b\}$ and $D(v) = \{a, c\}$ (so objects both disappear and appear along the relation), and two predicates $F$ and $G$, such that the Barcan formula fails for $F$ and the converse Barcan formula fails for $G$ at $w$. Say in plain words what your model says about $b$ and about $c$.
hint: For $F$, make $b$ and $a$ have $F$ at $v$ while the new object $c$ lacks it. For $G$, make $G$ true of everything that exists at $v$, so that $b$, which is absent at $v$, lacks it.
solution: Let $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w, v)\}$, $D(w) = \{a, b\}$, $D(v) = \{a, c\}$, $I(F, v) = \{a, b\}$ and $I(G, v) = \{a, c\}$ (the extensions at $w$ do not matter). Barcan formula for $F$: $\forall x\,\Box Fx$ is true at $w$, since $a$ and $b$ are both $F$ at $v$; but $\forall x\,Fx$ is false at $v$, since $c$ exists there and is not $F$; so $\Box\forall x\,Fx$ is false at $w$ and the Barcan formula fails. Converse Barcan formula for $G$: everything that exists at $v$ is $G$, so $\forall x\,Gx$ is true at $v$ and $\Box\forall x\,Gx$ is true at $w$; but $b$ is not $G$ at $v$, so $\Box Gx$ fails of $b$ and $\forall x\,\Box Gx$ is false at $w$. In words: $c$ is a new object that could have existed without our $F$-property, which defeats the Barcan formula; $b$ might have failed to exist, and is therefore not necessarily $G$ although everything that exists at the alternative is, which defeats the converse.
