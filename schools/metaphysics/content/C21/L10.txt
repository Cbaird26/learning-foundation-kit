@article
The same technique serves many readings of the box: change what the accessible worlds are, and you change the logic. Two readings matter to metaphysicians in particular. If the accessible worlds are the later times, $\Box$ becomes "it will always be that", and we have **tense logic**, founded by Arthur Prior. If they are the ideal worlds, $\Box$ becomes "it ought to be that", and we have **deontic logic**.

### Tense logic

A **tense model** is $(T, <, V)$: a non-empty set $T$ of times, an earlier-than relation $<$ and a valuation. There are four operators:

| operator | reading | true at $t$ iff |
|---|---|---|
| $G A$ | $A$ will always be true | $A$ is true at every $s$ with $t < s$ |
| $F A$ | $A$ will be true at some time | $A$ is true at some $s$ with $t < s$ |
| $H A$ | $A$ was always true | $A$ is true at every $s$ with $s < t$ |
| $\mathsf{P} A$ | $A$ was true at some time | $A$ is true at some $s$ with $s < t$ |

$F A$ is $\neg G \neg A$ and $\mathsf{P} A$ is $\neg H \neg A$. Here $G$ and $F$ are the box and diamond of the relation $<$, and $H$ and $\mathsf{P}$ those of its converse. For that reason $A \to G\mathsf{P} A$ and $A \to HFA$ are valid on every frame: if $A$ holds now, then at any later time it was true at some earlier time (now).

Take $T = \{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ with the usual order and $Q$ true at times 3 and 4. At time 2, $GQ$ is true (3 and 4 are the later times), and $FQ$ is true. At time 4, $GQ$ is true vacuously (there is no later time) and $FQ$ is false. At time 1, $H$ anything is true and $\mathsf{P}$ anything false.

**Frame conditions.** As in Lesson 4, each corresponds to a property of $<$: $GA \to GGA$ to transitivity; $GGA \to GA$ to **density** (if $s < t$ then there is $u$ with $s < u < t$); $GA \to FA$ to the absence of a last moment (every time has a later one). Whether time is transitive, dense or unending is a question of physics and metaphysics, and the logic shows exactly which formulas depend on the answer.

### From future truth to fatalism

Aristotle's discussion of the sea battle tomorrow (*De Interpretatione* 9) raises the worry that if it is already true that there will be a sea battle, then the battle is unavoidable. Here is a modern, tense-logical version, with $UA$ for "$A$ is now unpreventable":

| P1 | $FA \to HFA$: if $A$ will be the case, then it was always going to be the case. |
| P2 | $HFA \to UA$: what was always going to be the case is now unpreventable, since the past is fixed. |
| C | $FA \to UA$: if $A$ will be the case, then $A$ is unpreventable. |

The argument is valid (chain the two conditionals). P1 is a theorem of tense logic on transitive time, so denying it means denying that "was always going to be" is simply a fact about the past. The standard replies target P2. **Ockhamists** distinguish **hard facts** about the past (what really happened then) from **soft facts** (facts about the past that depend on the future, like "it was going to be that $A$"); only hard facts are fixed, so P2 is false. **Peirceans** instead deny the antecedent: on their reading, "will" holds only of what is already settled, so $FA$ is not true for an open future. Each reply has costs: Ockhamism needs a clear hard/soft line, and the Peircean view gives up that "it will rain or it will not" is a logical truth for an open future.

### Deontic logic

In **deontic logic** the operator $O$ reads "it ought to be that" (obligation), $\mathsf{Pm}A$ ("$A$ is permitted") is $\neg O\neg A$, and "$A$ is forbidden" is $O\neg A$. **Standard deontic logic** (SDL) is the system KD: K and necessitation, plus the axiom D, $OA \to \mathsf{Pm}A$ (what is obligatory is permitted; equivalently, no conflicts of obligation). Its semantics: $wRv$ means that $v$ is an **ideal alternative** to $w$, a world where everything is as it ought to be. Axiom D corresponds to seriality: some ideal world exists. SDL is not T: $OA \to A$ is false in a world where nobody does what they ought to do, so $R$ is not reflexive.

Two problems for SDL. **Ross's paradox:** $OQ \to O(Q \vee R)$ is valid, since it is monotonicity in K, so if you ought to mail the letter then you ought to mail it or burn it. **Chisholm's paradox** (1963) is a set of premises, each natural:

| P1 | $OG$: Ana ought to go and help her neighbors. |
| P2 | $O(G \to T)$: it ought to be that if she goes, she tells them she is coming. |
| P3 | $\neg G \to O\neg T$: if she does not go, she ought not tell them she is coming. |
| P4 | $\neg G$: she does not go. |
| C | $OT \wedge O\neg T$ |

The argument is valid in K: from P1 and P2, $OT$ by axiom K and modus ponens; from P3 and P4, $O\neg T$. But D makes $OT \wedge O\neg T$ impossible in SDL, so in SDL the four sentences are jointly inconsistent although the English story is consistent. The usual reactions locate the fault in the formalization: P3 should be a *conditional obligation*, not a conditional with a plain obligation in the consequent, and **dyadic deontic logics**, in which obligation is relative to a condition, were developed for this purpose.

@example
title: Evaluating tense formulas
problem: In the model with times $1, 2, 3, 4$ in their usual order and $Q$ true at times 2 and 3 only, evaluate $FQ$, $GQ$, $H Q$ and $G F Q$ at time 1 and at time 3.
step: Time 1: the later times are 2, 3, 4. $FQ$ is true (2 has $Q$). $GQ$ is false (4 lacks $Q$). There are no earlier times, so $HQ$ is true vacuously. $GFQ$ asks for $FQ$ at 2, 3 and 4: true at 2 (via 3), false at 3 (4 lacks $Q$), so false.
step: Time 3: the later time is 4. $FQ$ is false and $GQ$ is false (4 lacks $Q$). The earlier times 1 and 2 give $HQ$ false (1 lacks $Q$). $GFQ$ needs $FQ$ at 4, where it is false: false.
answer: At time 1: $FQ$ true, $GQ$ false, $HQ$ true, $GFQ$ false. At time 3: all four false.

@example
title: Chisholm's set is inconsistent in SDL
problem: Derive $OT \wedge O\neg T$ from P1 to P4 above and explain why SDL treats the premises as inconsistent.
step: From P2, $O(G \to T)$, and P1, $OG$: axiom K gives $O(G \to T) \to (OG \to OT)$. Two applications of modus ponens give $OT$.
step: From P4, $\neg G$, and P3, $\neg G \to O\neg T$, modus ponens gives $O\neg T$.
step: So $OT \wedge O\neg T$ follows. Axiom D gives $OT \to \neg O\neg T$, so the two together contradict D.
step: Hence the formalization of the story is inconsistent in SDL, which is a problem if the story is consistent. The fault may lie in P3.
answer: The premises entail $OT \wedge O\neg T$, which D rules out, so they are inconsistent in SDL.

@mistakes
- Evaluating $GA$ as false at the last moment of a finite time: it is vacuously true there, and $FA$ is false.
- Assuming that $OA \to A$ holds: obligations can go unmet, so deontic accessibility is not reflexive. D, not T, is the axiom of SDL.
- Reading the fatalist argument as invalid: the chain of conditionals is valid, and the replies attack P2 (Ockhamism) or the antecedent $FA$ (Peirceanism).
- Treating soft facts as fixed by the past: the Ockhamist's point is that "it was always going to be that $A$" depends on what happens later.

answer: 2
unit: times
check: sum(1 for t in range(1, 6) if all(s in {3, 5} for s in range(t + 1, 6)))

prompt: Which of these formulas are theorems of standard deontic logic (the system KD)? Select all that apply.
choice*: $O(Q \wedge R) \to OQ$
choice*: $OQ \to O(Q \vee R)$
choice*: $OQ \to \neg O\neg Q$
choice: $OQ \to Q$
choice: $\neg O\neg Q \to OQ$

solution: The first two are valid in every normal logic (box over conjunction, and monotonicity); the second is the formula behind Ross's paradox. The third is axiom D. prompt: Reconstruct the argument from future truth to fatalism (P1 $FA \to HFA$, P2 $HFA \to UA$, C $FA \to UA$) and state the Ockhamist reply: which premise does it deny, and why? Give one cost of this reply.
hint: Ask what kind of fact "it was always going to be that $A$" is, and whether the past is fixed in every respect.
solution: The argument is valid by chaining the two conditionals. The Ockhamist denies P2. The claim "it was always going to be that $A$" is a soft fact about the past: it is about the past in form but depends on what happens later. Only hard facts, facts that are wholly about the past, are fixed and so unpreventable; soft facts are not. So $HFA$ does not make $A$ unpreventable. One cost: the Ockhamist needs a principled way to separate hard from soft facts, and critics doubt that such a line can be drawn without circularity (facts about the past that "entail" something about the future are the hard cases).

@quiz self
prompt: Let $Q$ be "you mail the letter" and $R$ be "you burn the letter". Show that $OQ \to O(Q \vee R)$ is valid in standard deontic logic, say why it looks paradoxical (Ross's paradox), and give one proposed response.
hint: Use the tautology $Q \to (Q \vee R)$ with necessitation and axiom K, or look at what holds in every ideal world.
solution: The formula $Q \to (Q \vee R)$ is a tautology, so by necessitation $O(Q \to (Q \vee R))$ holds, and axiom K gives $OQ \to O(Q \vee R)$. Semantically, if $Q$ is true at every ideal alternative, so is $Q \vee R$. It looks paradoxical because "you ought to mail the letter or burn it" seems to follow from "you ought to mail the letter", although the first sentence suggests that burning the letter would be a way of doing what you ought. One response is pragmatic: $O(Q \vee R)$ is true but misleading to assert, because a speaker who knows the stronger fact $OQ$ should say so. Another is to change the logic, for example by treating the disjunction in the scope of $O$ as a free choice between options, or by moving to a logic of actions or a dyadic logic.

rubric: Gives a genuine response, such as a pragmatic explanation or a revised logic of disjunctive obligation.
