@article
In the *Proslogion*, Anselm of Canterbury argues from the idea of "that than which nothing greater can be conceived" to the existence of God. Philosophers have read chapter 2 as one argument, which treats existence as a feature that makes a being greater, and chapter 3, which says that such a being cannot be conceived not to exist, as another. In the twentieth century, Charles Hartshorne and Norman Malcolm drew on the second to propose **modal** ontological arguments, and Alvin Plantinga gave the best-known version in the 1970s. The modal language gives us the tools to say exactly what the argument needs.

### Plantinga's argument, informally

Plantinga defines **maximal excellence** in a world as being omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect there, and **maximal greatness** as maximal excellence in every possible world. He then argues: it is possible that there is a maximally great being; but a maximally great being would exist in every world; so there is a maximally great being. Plantinga himself presented the argument as showing that it is *rational to accept* theism, since the key premise is at least as reasonable as its denial, and not as a proof that compels every rational person.

### The formal skeleton

Let the sentence letter $M$ stand for "there is a maximally great being". The argument becomes:

| P1 | $\Diamond M$: possibly, there is a maximally great being. |
| P2 | $\Box(M \to \Box M)$: necessarily, if there is a maximally great being, then necessarily there is one. |
| C | $M$: there is a maximally great being. |

P2 expresses that maximal greatness, if instantiated at all, is instantiated necessarily. It follows from the definition (maximal excellence in *every* possible world), provided that the possible worlds are the same from every world's point of view, as they are in S5. P1 is the premise that carries the philosophical weight.

**What logic does the argument need?** From P1 and P2 the system K already derives $\Diamond\Box M$: there is a world $v$ with $M$, and at $v$ the conditional $M \to \Box M$ holds because $\Box(M \to \Box M)$ is true at the actual world, so $\Box M$ is true at $v$. To get from $\Diamond\Box M$ to $M$ we need axiom B, in its equivalent form $\Diamond\Box A \to A$.

**Theorem.** The argument from P1 and P2 to $M$ is valid on every symmetric frame.

*Proof.* Suppose P1 and P2 are true at $w$. By P1 there is $v$ with $wRv$ and $M$ true at $v$. By P2, $M \to \Box M$ is true at $v$, so $\Box M$ is true at $v$. By symmetry $vRw$, so $M$ is true at $w$. $\square$

So the argument is valid in B and S5. It is **not** valid in K, T or S4. Take $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w,w), (w,v), (v,v)\}$, which is reflexive and transitive, and $M$ true at $v$ only. At $w$: $\Diamond M$ is true (via $v$); $\Box(M \to \Box M)$ is true ($M \to \Box M$ holds at $w$ because $M$ is false there, and at $v$ because $v$ sees only itself and $M$ holds); but $M$ is false at $w$. So the premises are true and the conclusion false in a model of S4. The argument thus uses **symmetry** (axiom B) at its key step, and nothing stronger. It does not need transitivity, so Salmon's table argument, which tells against transitivity and, with it, S5, does not by itself block this argument: whether metaphysical possibility is symmetric is a separate question that the logic leaves open.

### What validity leaves open

The formalization is **valid** in B and S5, and so a person who accepts the premises and that logic must accept the conclusion. But a valid argument leaves the truth of its premises open. In S5 there is a sharper point. With P2, $\Diamond M$ is equivalent to $\Box M$: the premises together say that necessarily there is a maximally great being. So the premise P1, together with P2, already comes to the claim that a maximally great being exists necessarily, and the argument converts that claim into the claim that one exists.

**The parody objection.** Consider the mirror-image premise $\Diamond\Box\neg M$: possibly, necessarily, there is no maximally great being. In B, $\Diamond\Box\neg M \to \neg M$ is valid, so the same reasoning that gets us from $\Diamond M$ and P2 to $M$ gets us from $\Diamond\Box\neg M$ to $\neg M$. The two premises cannot both be true in S5. If the possibility of God is the more plausible, the argument is fine; if the parallel premise is equally plausible, the argument does not move a neutral person. Defenders reply that the concept of a maximally great being is coherent and the parody premise lacks any such support; critics reply that conceivability is a poor guide to metaphysical possibility. Each side is working with a premise about possibility that the logic leaves entirely open.

### Gödel's argument and modal collapse

Kurt Gödel's version, usually presented in Dana Scott's form, defines a God-like being by *positive properties* and concludes that, in S5, if it is possible that a God-like being exists then necessarily one does. It is known (a result of Jordan Howard Sobel) that Gödel's axioms imply **modal collapse**: every truth is necessary, $P \to \Box P$. This formula is valid on a frame exactly when every world sees at most itself: if $wRv$ with $v \neq w$, make $P$ true at $w$ only, and $\Box P$ fails at $w$; conversely, if each world sees only itself or nothing, $\Box P$ and $P$ agree wherever there is a successor. Since most philosophers want some truths to be contingent, the collapse is a serious cost, and Anderson and others have proposed revised axioms to avoid it.

@example
title: Showing the argument fails in S4
problem: Show that $\Diamond M$ and $\Box(M \to \Box M)$ do not entail $M$ in the sense of S4, by giving a reflexive transitive countermodel, and say what this shows about the role of axiom B.
step: Let $W = \{w, v\}$, $R = \{(w,w), (w,v), (v,v)\}$. This relation is reflexive (loops at both worlds) and transitive (the only chain $(w,v), (v,v)$ gives $(w,v)$). Let $M$ be true at $v$ only.
step: At $w$, $\Diamond M$ is true, via $v$.
step: At $w$, $\Box(M \to \Box M)$ needs $M \to \Box M$ at $w$ and at $v$. At $w$, $M$ is false, so the conditional is true. At $v$, $M$ is true and the only successor of $v$ is $v$, so $\Box M$ is true. So the premise is true.
step: But $M$ is false at $w$. So the premises are true and the conclusion false at $w$ in a model on a reflexive transitive frame. The frame is not symmetric: $w$ sees $v$ but $v$ does not see $w$.
answer: The argument is invalid in S4 (and so in T and K). Axiom B, the frame condition of symmetry, is the one extra ingredient it needs.

@example
title: The premises collapse into $\Box M$ in S5
problem: Show that in S5 the formula $\Diamond M \wedge \Box(M \to \Box M)$ is equivalent to $\Box M$.
step: ($\to$) Suppose both conjuncts hold at $w$. As in the proof of the theorem, some accessible $v$ has $\Box M$; in an equivalence frame, $\Diamond\Box M \to \Box M$ is valid (Lesson 5), so $\Box M$ holds at $w$.
step: ($\leftarrow$) Suppose $\Box M$ holds at $w$. By reflexivity, $M$ holds at $w$, so $\Diamond M$ holds at $w$.
step: For the second conjunct, take any $u$ with $wRu$. Then $M$ holds at $u$, and by transitivity every world accessible from $u$ is accessible from $w$, so $\Box M$ holds at $u$. So $M \to \Box M$ holds at every accessible world: $\Box(M \to \Box M)$ holds at $w$.
answer: In S5 the two premises together say exactly $\Box M$: the argument's premises already contain its conclusion's necessity.

@mistakes
- Thinking that a valid formalization shows that God exists: validity shows only that the conclusion follows if the premises are true, and the truth of the premises is exactly what is debated.
- Confusing the possibility premise with a harmless one: in S5, $\Diamond M$ together with P2 is equivalent to $\Box M$.
- Assuming that the modal argument works in any modal logic: it needs axiom B (symmetric accessibility), which T and S4 lack.
- Treating the parody objection as a refutation: it shows that logic alone cannot choose between $\Diamond M$ and $\Diamond\Box\neg M$, which is a different claim.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the parody objection to the modal ontological argument. Why is it not a refutation, and what does it show about where the argument's weight rests?
hint: Compare $\Diamond M$ with $\Diamond\Box\neg M$ and apply axiom B to each.
solution: The parody replaces the premise $\Diamond M$ with $\Diamond\Box\neg M$: possibly, necessarily, there is no maximally great being. The step to the conclusion uses axiom B ($\Diamond\Box A \to A$), so from $\Diamond\Box\neg M$ one derives $\neg M$ by exactly the same reasoning. Both premises cannot be true in S5, so someone who finds the two equally credible has no reason to accept the argument's conclusion over its negation. The parody is not a refutation: it does not show that $\Diamond M$ is false, only that logic alone cannot decide between the two possibility claims, and a defender may argue that $\Diamond M$ has better support (the coherence of the concept) than the parallel premise. It shows that the weight of the argument rests on the possibility premise, which in S5 is equivalent to the necessity of God's existence.
