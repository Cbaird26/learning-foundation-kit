@article
When one billiard ball strikes another, something is passed from the first to the second: motion, momentum. Counterfactual theories say that causation is a matter of making a difference. **Process theories** say instead that causation is a matter of *connection*: a cause is linked to its effect by a physical process, with something transferred along the way. The two views disagree on important cases, and the disagreement suggests that "cause" may name more than one thing.

### Causal processes and pseudo-processes

Wesley Salmon distinguished **causal processes** from **pseudo-processes**. A baseball in flight is a causal process: it carries its own properties from place to place. A spot of light moving across a wall, produced by a rotating lamp, is a pseudo-process. The spot can move faster than light, which no causal process may do, because it is not the same thing carried along. At each moment a different part of the wall is lit by a new beam of light.

Salmon's first test was **mark transmission**: a process is causal iff it can transmit a mark, a modification, once introduced, without further intervention. A scratch on the baseball stays on it. A red filter put in front of the moving spot changes the spot only where the filter is, and the spot is white again on the rest of the wall. The mark is not transmitted, so the spot is a pseudo-process. Salmon later gave up the mark method, since marks are described in counterfactual terms, and, following a proposal by Phil Dowe, moved to a **conserved quantity theory**, which Dowe developed in his own way: a causal process is the world line of an object possessing a conserved quantity such as energy, momentum or charge, and a **causal interaction** is an intersection of such processes involving an exchange of a conserved quantity. A billiard collision exchanges momentum, so it is a causal interaction.

### Advantages

The process theory handles cases that trouble counterfactual theories. In late preemption (week 6), the first stone is linked to the jar by a process that carries momentum to it and shatters it, while the second stone never reaches the jar and transfers nothing. So the first stone causes the shattering. It also seems to explain causal asymmetry by an account that uses no counterfactuals and no similarity ordering.

### Absences, prevention and double prevention

Process theories have difficulty with causation by **omission** (a gardener's failure to water the plant caused it to die), **prevention** (a guard prevented the theft) and especially **double prevention**: a causes e by preventing something that would have prevented e. Suppose Mo has set a bomb on a timer. A guard, Ned, would have defused it. Ana locks Ned out. The bomb explodes. Ana's locking is a cause of the explosion, and there is no process from her act to the explosion: she did not transfer anything to the bomb.

| P1 | If the conserved quantity theory is correct, then $c$ is a cause of $e$ only if there is a causal process or chain of interactions linking $c$ to $e$. |
| P2 | In the bomb case, Ana's locking Ned out is a cause of the explosion, but there is no causal process or chain of interactions linking her act to the explosion. |
| C | So the conserved quantity theory is not correct (as a theory of all causation). |

The argument is valid: P2 describes a cause with no link, which P1 says is impossible. There are two main replies. The first attacks P2. Dowe himself distinguished **causation** proper, which requires a process, from **quasi-causation**, which covers omissions, prevention and double prevention: these are not genuine causal relations but are counterfactually related to genuine ones, and our judgments that they are causal are explained by that. A critic says that the reply is ad hoc and that much of ordinary and scientific causal talk concerns absences, such as the absence of a vaccine. The second reply accepts the conclusion, taken as a verdict on the theory as an account of all causation, and holds that the process theory describes only part of causation.

### Causal pluralism

Ned Hall argued that there are **two concepts of causation**: **dependence** (the effect counterfactually depends on the cause, as in omissions and double prevention) and **production** (the cause generates the effect through a process, as in billiard collisions). Cases such as late preemption are cases of production without dependence, and double prevention is dependence without production. Hall thinks the two concepts are both causal, and our intuitions are inconsistent only if we assume there is a single concept. More generally, **causal pluralism** says that causation is not one relation: Nancy Cartwright has argued that science uses a family of specific causal notions, such as pushing and attracting, and not a single one.

The pluralist faces two objections. One is that pluralism is less unified and less economical than a monist theory and may be a way of avoiding hard cases. The other is that we need an explanation of why the concepts go by one name; the pluralist may reply that they share a role in explanation and in guiding action, and that ordinary use is vague.

@example
title: Mark transmission and pseudo-processes
problem: A lamp rotates and sweeps a beam of light across a long wall, so a bright spot moves along it. You hold a red filter in front of the spot at one place. Is the moving spot a causal process by Salmon's mark criterion?
step: Introduce a mark: the filter turns the spot red where the filter is.
step: Check transmission. After the spot has passed the filter, it is white again on the rest of the wall, because each new bit of wall is lit by white light from the lamp. The mark is not carried along by the spot.
step: A process is causal iff it can transmit a mark. The spot cannot, so it is a pseudo-process. Contrast a red mark scratched onto a rolling ball, which stays on the ball for as long as it rolls.
answer: No. The spot is a pseudo-process: it fails to transmit the mark. The lamp's rotating beam and the light itself are the causal processes.

@example
title: Double prevention against the process theory
problem: Mo sets a bomb on a timer, and Ned would have defused it, but Ana locks Ned out and the bomb explodes. Say what the counterfactual theory and the process theory each say about Ana's act, and give the dispute's upshot.
step: Counterfactual theory: if Ana had not locked Ned out, he would have defused the bomb and there would have been no explosion. The explosion depends on her act, so on a dependence theory it is a cause.
step: Process theory: nothing physical passes from Ana's act to the bomb, since the explosion's energy comes from the bomb itself. So there is no process from her act, and on a strict process theory it is not a cause.
step: Replies: Dowe says it is quasi-causation, so our judgment tracks counterfactual facts. Hall says it is genuine causation of the dependence kind. Either way, a single account needs to handle both billiards and double prevention.
answer: The counterfactual theory counts the lock as a cause, the strict process theory does not, and the pluralist says that both are right about different concepts.

@mistakes
- Saying that a pseudo-process cannot exist: a moving spot of light really does occur, but it does not carry a mark or a conserved quantity along with it.
- Saying that process theories use counterfactuals: Dowe's version is meant to avoid them, though Salmon's mark criterion was criticized for implicitly using them.
- Treating double prevention as a case in which the effect does not occur: the explosion occurs, and the question is whether the lock caused it.
- Treating pluralism as the view that no causal claim is true: it says that there are several legitimate causal relations.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the counterfactual and process theories on the following case: a fuse is lit and burns to a bomb, which explodes; a second fuse, lit later, would have burned to the same bomb if the first had been absent. Which theory handles the case more easily, and why?
hint: Consider whether the explosion depends on the first fuse and whether a process links it to the bomb.
solution: This is a case of late preemption. The explosion does not depend on the first fuse, because the second would have set off the bomb anyway, so the simple counterfactual theory says that the first fuse is not a cause, which is the wrong verdict. The process theory handles it directly: the burning of the first fuse reaches the bomb and transfers energy to it, while the second fuse is not yet burned through, so nothing from it reaches the bomb. So the process theory gets the intuitive verdict without added machinery, while the counterfactual theory needs fragility or influence. The process theory pays elsewhere: omissions and double prevention.
