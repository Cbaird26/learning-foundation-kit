@article
Suppose the consequence argument works. Then someone who wants free will must reject determinism, or else must give up the idea that free will requires the ability to do otherwise. Both routes are open, and so is a third: that free will is impossible whatever the laws are. This week covers each.

### Libertarianism

**Libertarians** hold that free will is incompatible with determinism and that we have free will, so determinism is false of free actions. They need an account of how an undetermined action can still be controlled by the agent.

- **Event-causal** views, such as Robert Kane's, say that free actions are caused by prior mental events, such as reasons, but the causation is indeterministic. In Kane's account the key cases are *self-forming actions*, in which an agent torn between competing motives makes an effortful choice whose outcome is undetermined.
- **Agent-causal** views, such as Roderick Chisholm's and, in a different form, Timothy O'Connor's, say that the agent, a substance, causes the action, and this causation is not reducible to events causing events.
- **Non-causal** views, such as Carl Ginet's, say that free actions are not caused by anything but have an intrinsic feature of being actively done.

### The luck objection

Indeterminism seems to give chance, not control. Call this the **luck objection**, or the *mind argument* in one of its forms. Consider replaying a choice from the same past and laws: if the choice is undetermined, it may go one way on one replay and the other on another, and nothing about the agent distinguishes the replays.

| P1 | If an action is undetermined, then given exactly the same past and laws, it is a matter of chance whether the agent performs it. |
| P2 | If it is a matter of chance whether an agent performs an action, then the agent does not control whether she performs it. |
| P3 | A free action is one that the agent controls. |
| C | So no undetermined action is free. |

The argument is valid. The libertarian replies attack P1 or P2. Agent-causalists reject P1 in the intended sense: the agent's causing is not a chance event; the outcome is settled by the agent, though not by prior events. Kane rejects P2 in the case of self-forming actions: whichever option the agent chooses, she was trying to do it, and her choice expresses her will, so she has control even if the result is not determined. Critics reply that agent-causation is obscure and does not explain why the agent causes one thing in one replay and another in the next, and that Kane's account leaves a gap between the effort and the outcome.

### Frankfurt cases

The **principle of alternate possibilities** (PAP) says that an agent is morally responsible for what she did only if she could have done otherwise. Harry Frankfurt argued against it with a kind of case that now bears his name. In a typical case, a neuroscientist, Black, wants Jones to choose to do $X$ and secretly monitors his deliberation. If Jones begins to decide otherwise, Black will intervene and make him choose $X$. But Jones decides to do $X$ on his own, and Black never intervenes.

| P1 | (PAP) If an agent is morally responsible for what she did, then she could have done otherwise. |
| P2 | In the Frankfurt case, Jones is morally responsible for choosing $X$. |
| P3 | In the Frankfurt case, Jones could not have done otherwise. |
| C | So PAP is false. |

The argument is valid. Replies attack P3 or P2. The **flicker of freedom** reply attacks P3: Jones could have done something else, such as showing the sign that would have triggered Black, so there is an alternative. Defenders, notably Fischer, answer that the flicker is too slight to ground responsibility. The **dilemma defense**, due to Widerker, Ginet and Kane, attacks P2: if Jones's decision is determined, then the case simply assumes that a determined agent is responsible, which begs the question against incompatibilists; if it is undetermined, then there is no reliable sign for Black to use, and so Black cannot guarantee the outcome and Jones had an alternative. Frankfurt cases matter for the consequence argument because if PAP is false, then losing the ability to do otherwise under determinism need not remove responsibility. John Martin Fischer and Mark Ravizza developed **semicompatibilism**: moral responsibility is compatible with determinism whether or not free will in the sense of leeway is, because it depends on reasons-responsive mechanisms.

### Free will skepticism

**Free will skeptics** hold that no one is morally responsible in the sense that would make them deserve blame or praise. Galen Strawson's **basic argument** says that responsibility requires being responsible for the way one is, and this leads to a regress.

| P1 | You do what you do because of the way you are, in certain mental respects. |
| P2 | To be ultimately responsible for what you do, you must be ultimately responsible for the way you are in those respects. |
| P3 | To be ultimately responsible for the way you are, you must have brought it about by an earlier action for which you were ultimately responsible, and so, by P1 and P2, for the way you were then, and so on without end. |
| P4 | No one can complete such an endless regress. |
| C | So no one is ultimately responsible for what they do. |

The argument is valid. Compatibilists attack P2: responsibility does not require ultimate, self-creating responsibility, only that one's action flow from reasons-responsive mechanisms. Derk Pereboom's four-case argument is another route. Skeptics also argue that important things survive, such as deliberation, forward-looking responses to wrongdoing and meaningful relationships. Empirical results are often cited, but it remains disputed what experiments on the timing of decisions show about free will, and they do not settle the question.

@example
title: Analyzing a Frankfurt case
problem: Black will force Jones to choose $X$ if Jones begins to choose otherwise. Jones chooses $X$ on his own and Black does nothing. Present the case as an argument against PAP and say what the flicker reply says.
step: P1 is PAP. P2 says Jones is responsible, since he chose on his own for his own reasons. P3 says he could not have chosen otherwise, since Black would have intervened.
step: P1 with P3 implies that Jones is not responsible. P2 says that he is. So P1 must be false if P2 and P3 are true.
step: Flicker: Jones could have *started* to decide otherwise, and that is an alternative. So P3 is false as stated.
step: Reply to the flicker: the alternative is a tiny event, not the kind of power responsibility needs, and Jones is not responsible for it in any way. So the case still shows what it was meant to.
answer: The flicker reply attacks P3. It is not clear that the flicker is enough to ground responsibility, and the dispute turns on that.

@example
title: The luck objection and the agent-causal reply
problem: A libertarian says that Mo's choice to donate was undetermined but free. Present the luck objection and the agent-causal reply, identifying the premise the reply attacks.
step: The objection: given the same past and laws, Mo might have chosen not to donate, so which choice she made was a matter of chance (P1). Chance is not control (P2). Free action requires control (P3).
step: The conclusion is that no undetermined action is free. The argument is valid.
step: The agent-causal reply: Mo herself, as a substance, caused the choice, and this is not an event whose occurrence is a matter of chance. So P1's claim that it was a matter of chance in the relevant sense is rejected.
step: Critics respond that this replaces the problem with a mystery: what explains why Mo caused one choice on this occasion and not another?
answer: The reply attacks P1. The remaining question is whether agent-causation can explain the difference between the replays.

@mistakes
- Saying that Frankfurt cases show that determinism is compatible with free will: they target PAP, and support only the weaker claim that responsibility does not require alternatives. They leave the consequence argument's conclusion about leeway standing.
- Treating the luck objection as a proof that indeterminism cannot be free: it is an argument with premises that libertarians reject.
- Confusing free will skepticism with hard determinism: the skeptic denies free will whether or not determinism is true.
- Saying that science has refuted free will: whether neuroscience or physics bears on free will is disputed, and philosophers disagree about what experiments show.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain what a free will skeptic can say about the practices of holding people responsible, and give one objection. Do not assume that the skeptic's argument is sound.
hint: Think of forward-looking responses such as protection, rehabilitation and moral education.
solution: A skeptic who denies that people deserve blame in the ultimate sense can still hold that we may protect others, rehabilitate offenders and teach, since these responses do not require desert. Pereboom, for example, argues that meaningful relationships and moral improvement do not depend on desert-based blame. An objection is that this does not fit our reactive attitudes of resentment and gratitude, which seem to express an assumption of responsibility, so abandoning them would change the relationships the skeptic wants to preserve. Another objection is that the skeptical premises are contested, and that a compatibilist can reject the demand for ultimate responsibility. Whether the skeptical conclusion is true is a live question, and the point here is only what follows if it is.
