@article
Did the dinosaurs exist? Will your grandchildren? Most people say yes to the first and "probably" to the second, but a theory of time must say what these claims are about. The question is **ontological**: which things exist, and does that depend on when? It is different from the question of week 9, whether tensed facts are fundamental, though the two are often combined.

### Four pictures

- **Presentism**: only present things exist. Past things *did* exist and future things *will*, but they do not exist now.
- **Eternalism**: past, present and future things exist, in the same sense. All events are equally real; none is privileged. The picture is called the **block universe**. Most B-theorists are eternalists.
- **The growing block**: past and present things exist, and future things do not. The block grows as new moments come into being. C. D. Broad and Michael Tooley defended versions of it.
- **The moving spotlight**: eternalism about what exists, together with an A-theoretic claim that one moment is objectively present, and the privilege moves. This view lets the A-theorist have the whole block.

Presentism fits the idea that the past is gone and the future open. Eternalism fits the physics of spacetime more easily. Each faces objections.

### Relativity and presentism

Special relativity is extremely well confirmed: time dilation is measured with fast-moving particles and with atomic clocks in aircraft and satellites, and GPS must correct for it. In it, whether two distant events are simultaneous depends on the frame of reference: two flashes simultaneous in one frame are not simultaneous in a frame moving relative to it. This is the **relativity of simultaneity**. An argument goes back to Putnam and Rietdijk, and to Stein's reply:

| P1 | If presentism is true, then there is an absolute fact about which distant events exist now, and so a privileged frame of reference. |
| P2 | Special relativity makes simultaneity relative to a frame, and recognizes no privileged frame. |
| P3 | We should not accept a metaphysical thesis that requires a privileged frame which our best physics does not recognize. |
| C | So presentism should not be accepted. |

The argument is valid: P1 says that presentism requires a privileged frame, P2 says that physics recognizes none, and P3 says that such a requirement should not be accepted. The replies are mostly attacks on P2 and P3. A **neo-Lorentzian** presentist says that there is a privileged frame that the physics cannot detect, as in Lorentz's older interpretation of the same equations: this rejects P3 by saying that metaphysics can go beyond what physics requires. Another reply notes that in the standard cosmological model there is a natural preferred slicing, **cosmic time**, defined by the matter of the universe; this attacks P2's claim that nothing is privileged, though critics say it is a contingent feature of the universe and not part of the laws. A third reply notes that special relativity is not our final theory: there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity, and whether spacetime is fundamental or emergent is open.

### Other objections

The **truthmaker objection** to presentism: "dinosaurs existed" is true, but nothing present seems to make it true. Presentists reply that truth need not be grounded in what exists, or that present facts about the world's history do the work. The **epistemic objection** to the growing block: if past things exist and are as real as present ones, then past people are as conscious as we are, and each of them would seem to be in the same position as we are in thinking "I am at the edge". How do we know that we are at the edge? Defenders reply that being present is a special feature of the edge and not the same as past states.

### Time travel

**Time travel**, as David Lewis explained in "The Paradoxes of Time Travel", involves a gap between **external time**, the time of the world, and **personal time**, the time on the traveller's own life, so a traveller can depart in one year and arrive in an earlier one, while living on in personal time. It is easiest to describe on eternalism, since the earlier time exists to be visited; presentists have to say where the traveller goes. The famous puzzle is the **grandfather paradox**:

| P1 | If backward time travel is possible, then someone could travel back and kill her own grandfather before her parent was conceived. |
| P2 | Nobody could kill her own grandfather before her parent was conceived. |
| C | So backward time travel is impossible. |

The argument is valid, but Lewis argued that "could" is ambiguous. Relative to the traveller's skills, her rifle and the opportunity, she *can* kill him. Relative to the whole history of the world, which includes that he survived and had a child, she *cannot*. Use the same sense in both premises and the argument fails: if "could" is relative to her abilities and circumstances, P2 is false; if it is relative to all the facts, P1 does not follow from the possibility of time travel. So the grandfather paradox does not show that time travel is impossible, though it shows that a traveller cannot change the past, since the past is fixed.

@example
title: The grandfather paradox and Lewis's reply
problem: Tim travels to the time before his father was conceived. Lewis says that Tim can kill his grandfather and cannot. How is this consistent?
step: Fix the facts of the world, which include that Tim's grandfather lived on and became Tim's father's father. These facts are a single, unchanging history.
step: Relative to Tim's abilities, his rifle, his skill and the opportunity, Tim can kill his grandfather: nothing in these facts prevents it.
step: Relative to the totality of facts, which include that the grandfather survived, Tim cannot, since the conjunction of Tim's shot killing him and his surviving is contradictory.
step: Since "can" is relative to a set of facts, the two claims do not conflict. Something always stops Tim: a jam, a change of heart or a miss.
answer: Tim can in the sense relative to his abilities and opportunity, and cannot in the sense relative to all the facts. There is no contradiction, and no change to the past.

@example
title: Relativity of simultaneity
problem: Two flashes are simultaneous in frame S and are 2 light-seconds apart along the line of motion of a spaceship traveling at 0.6 times the speed of light relative to S. By how many seconds do the flashes differ in time in the spaceship's frame?
step: The time difference in the moving frame is $\Delta t' = \gamma\, v\, \Delta x / c^2$ in magnitude, when $\Delta t = 0$ in S.
step: With $v = 0.6c$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.6^2} = 1.25$. With $\Delta x = 2$ light-seconds, $v\Delta x / c^2 = 0.6 \times 2 = 1.2$ seconds.
step: So $|\Delta t'| = 1.25 \times 1.2 = 1.5$ seconds.
answer: The flashes are 1.5 seconds apart in the spaceship's frame. Simultaneity is relative to the frame, which is the physics behind the argument against presentism.

@mistakes
- Identifying the A-theory with presentism: the growing block and the moving spotlight are A-theories, and presentism is one of several.
- Thinking that the block universe says that time does not pass in the sense of changing: the eternalist says that things change by having different properties at different times; what is denied is a privileged present.
- Taking the relativity argument to refute presentism outright: it assumes P3, and presentists reject it or P2.
- Reading Lewis as saying that a time traveller can change the past: he says that the past is fixed and what happens is consistent, though "can" is relative to the facts.

prompt: State the truthmaker objection to presentism and a reply. Say whether the reply makes presentism worse off than eternalism on this point.
hint: Ask what makes "dinosaurs existed" true if dinosaurs do not exist.
solution: The objection: "dinosaurs existed" is true, and truths need truthmakers, but if only present things exist, nothing present seems to make it true, since the dinosaurs are gone. A presentist can reply that the claim is made true by present facts about the world, such as how things are now, including a tensed property of the world of having been home to dinosaurs, or deny that truth needs truthmakers. The eternalist has an easy truthmaker, the dinosaurs themselves, located in the past, so the presentist pays by adding tensed properties or giving up the truthmaker principle. Whether that is a serious cost depends on the independent standing of the principle and of the tensed properties.
