@article
Every coin in Ana's pocket is copper. Every lump of pure plutonium-239 is smaller than a mountain. Both generalizations are true and, as far as anyone knows, have no counterexamples. But the first looks like an accident of Ana's pocket, and the second looks as if it might be explained by laws, since a lump of plutonium-239 beyond a certain size would not stay in one piece. A **law of nature** seems to differ from a true generalization: it supports counterfactuals ("had this coin been put in her pocket, it would have been copper" is false; "had this lump been that large, it would have exploded" seems true), it figures in explanations, and it is confirmed by its instances. The metaphysics of laws asks what the difference between laws and accidents consists in.

### The Humean picture

**Humeans** hold that there are no necessary connections between distinct existences. David Lewis's **Humean supervenience** says that the world is a vast **mosaic** of particular matters of fact, local qualities at points of spacetime, and that everything else, including laws, supervenes on the mosaic. If so, laws cannot be extra ingredients that govern events: they must be fixed by the pattern of events.

The leading Humean account is the **best-system account**, developed from suggestions by Mill and Ramsey, and by Lewis. Consider all the deductive systems that describe the mosaic truly. Systems differ in **simplicity** (a few elegant axioms) and **strength** (how much they say about what happens); Lewis added **fit** for chancy worlds. The **laws** are the generalizations that belong to the system that best balances these virtues. Which predicates may be used is restricted to **natural** properties: otherwise a gerrymandered vocabulary could make anything simple. A generalization such as "all coins in Ana's pocket are copper" would add little strength to the best system at too great a cost in simplicity, so it is not a law.

### Governing views

**Non-Humeans** hold that laws are more than patterns. Armstrong, Dretske and Tooley independently proposed that laws are **relations among universals**: that being $F$ necessitates being $G$ is a contingent fact written $N(F, G)$, from which the regularity "all $F$s are $G$s" follows. Laws then explain their instances, and the difference between laws and accidents is that only laws have an underlying relation of necessitation. On the **powers** view of week 4, laws derive from the essential dispositional natures of properties, so they are metaphysically necessary. Tim Maudlin defended **primitivism**: laws are fundamental and govern how the world evolves from one state to the next, so they are not reducible to the mosaic or to universals. What these views share is that laws *govern*, or *produce*, their instances. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, more respondents leaned toward non-Humean than Humean views of laws (about 54% to 31%), but the share of philosophers holding a view is not evidence that it is true.

### Two objections

**The inference problem.** Van Fraassen named it, and Lewis stated its core: if $N(F, G)$ is a relation between universals, why does it follow that every $F$ is $G$? Calling the relation "necessitation" does not make it necessitate. A contingent relation between two universals might be present without the regularity. The governing theorist may reply that necessitation is a primitive that has this nomic role by its nature. The Humean answers that this is just to name what needs to be explained.

**Circularity.** The Humean account faces a problem about explanation:

| P1 | If the laws explain their instances, then the laws are not themselves fixed, even in part, by those instances. |
| P2 | On the best-system account, the laws are fixed by the whole mosaic of particular facts, and the mosaic includes their instances as part of what fixes them. |
| P3 | The laws explain their instances. |
| C | So the best-system account is false. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P3 imply that the laws are not fixed even in part by their instances, which contradicts P2. The standard Humean reply attacks P1 or P3 by distinguishing two kinds of explanation, as Barry Loewer did. A law can scientifically explain an instance by subsuming it under a pattern, while the law is in turn metaphysically fixed by the whole mosaic. The circularity arises only if both claims concern the same kind of explanation. Critics reply that scientific explanation then loses its depth, since the law adds nothing beyond a summary of what happens.

### Comparing the views

The best-system account is economical and fits Hume's dictum, but it has trouble saying why laws explain. Governing views make laws explanatory, but they add something, necessitation, a primitive, or powers, whose nature is hard to specify.

@example
title: Law or accident?
problem: Under the best-system account, why is "all electrons repel each other" a law but "all the cars on Ana's street today are blue" not?
step: Consider the best system: the set of generalizations that together give the best balance of simplicity and strength. Electron repulsion belongs to a compact set of axioms that says a great deal about the mosaic, including how electrons behave in all places and times.
step: The generalization about the cars adds one small fact about one street at one time. To include it would add an axiom at a cost in simplicity and with almost no gain in strength.
step: So the best system includes the first and excludes the second. The Humean says that this is all the difference between a law and an accident, and it shows why laws figure in explanations of many instances and accidents in none.
answer: The first generalization is in the best system and is a law. The second is not in it, so it is an accident. The difference is a matter of how the generalization fits in an economical systematization of the whole mosaic.

@example
title: Reconstructing the circularity objection
problem: A critic says: "On the best-system account, the law that all electrons repel is fixed by what all electrons do. So it cannot explain why this electron repels." Put it in standard form and give the Humean reply.
step: The conclusion is that the best-system account is false. The explanation claim is P3: laws explain their instances. The Humean claim is P2: laws are fixed by the whole mosaic, including the instances.
step: The needed hidden premise is P1: if laws explain their instances, they are not fixed by them. With P1, P2 and P3 conflict, so the argument is valid.
step: The Humean's reply, due to Loewer, is to distinguish scientific explanation, which subsumes instances under laws, from metaphysical explanation, which says what fixes the laws. P1 is true only if both are the same kind, and they are not, so the reply attacks P1.
step: A critic replies that scientific explanation by a summary is weak, so P3 is not satisfied in a robust sense.
answer: The objection is valid; the Humean reply attacks P1 (or the sense of explanation in P3), and the dispute is about whether summary explanation is enough.

@mistakes
- Saying that the best-system account makes laws mere accidental regularities: a law is a generalization in the best system, which is a special status that most true generalizations lack.
- Saying that non-Humeans deny that laws are regularities: they hold that laws entail or produce regularities and are more than them.
- Identifying the powers view with the Armstrong view: on the powers view laws are necessary, and on Armstrong's view they are contingent relations among universals.
- Confusing the inference problem, which concerns whether necessitation entails the regularity, with the circularity problem, which concerns the explanatory relation of laws to instances.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the best-system account with a governing view (necessitation, powers or primitivism) in about 150 words. Say what each says a law is, what each says about explanation, and one cost of each.
hint: Think about the circularity objection for the Humean and the inference problem or the problem of primitives for the governing view.
solution: On the best-system account, a law is a generalization in the system that best balances simplicity and strength, so laws are fixed by the mosaic of particular facts. The view is economical and respects Hume's dictum, but it faces the circularity objection: laws that depend on their instances seem unable to explain them, and the reply, which distinguishes scientific from metaphysical explanation, makes scientific explanation a matter of summary. On a governing view such as Armstrong's, a law is a relation between universals that necessitates a regularity; laws explain their instances because they produce them. The cost is the inference problem: it is unclear why a contingent relation should entail the regularity, and calling it necessitation does not answer this. A powers theorist avoids the problem by making laws necessary, at the cost of denying the contingency of laws.
