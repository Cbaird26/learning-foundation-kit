@article
Suppose you accept that there are possible worlds. What are they? Three answers dominate the literature, and each is a response to the same worry: worlds are meant to explain modality, so they should be understood without already presupposing it.

### Modal realism

David Lewis defended **genuine modal realism** in *On the Plurality of Worlds*. On his view, possible worlds are concrete things of the same kind as our own universe: each is a maximal sum of spatiotemporally related objects, and there are countless of them, none spatiotemporally related to any other. Our world is the only one in which we are located, but it is not metaphysically special. The word "actual" is **indexical**, like "here" or "now": "actual" means "belonging to the same world as the speaker". Someone at another world rightly calls her own world actual.

This gives a clean account of modality: a sentence is possible if it is true at some world, necessary if true at all. For individuals, Lewis rejected **transworld identity**: nothing is part of two worlds. A thing's de re modal properties come from its **counterparts**, things at other worlds that resemble it closely enough in the relevant respects. "Dana could have lost" is true iff Dana has a counterpart who loses. Which things count as counterparts depends on context. Kripke objected that this gets de re modality wrong. Humphrey, he said, cares about what could have happened to *him*, not about what happens to someone merely similar to him in another world.

Lewis's positive case is an argument from **theoretical utility**:

| P1 | If positing a kind of entity gives the best systematic account of a wide range of philosophical phenomena, and its costs are outweighed by its benefits, then it is rational to believe that the entities exist. |
| P2 | Positing a plurality of concrete possible worlds gives the best systematic account of modality, counterfactuals, properties and propositions, and its costs are outweighed by its benefits. |
| C | So it is rational to believe that there is a plurality of concrete possible worlds. |

The argument is valid, and the contest is over P2. The most common objection is the **incredulous stare**: the view is so far from common sense that its benefits cannot outweigh the cost of believing in innumerable concrete universes. Lewis accepted that this is the objection he felt most keenly and that he could not refute a stare. He replied that it is a cost to be weighed, not a refutation. He added that what is surprising is the number of entities, whereas the economy that matters is economy in *kinds* of things, and worlds are just more of the kind we already accept. A critic can answer that the stare tracks something deeper than quantity, namely that the plurality is unlike anything our evidence supports; and a second objection says that causally isolated worlds make modal knowledge mysterious. Lewis replied that modal knowledge comes from a principle of recombination, that duplicates of parts of worlds can be put together in any arrangement, together with reasoning about what is possible.

### Ersatzism

**Ersatz** theories keep worlds but make them abstract: a world is a representation of a way things might have been, and the actual world is the one representation that is correct. **Linguistic ersatzism** takes worlds to be maximal consistent sets of sentences. **Plantinga's actualism** takes them to be maximal possible states of affairs, which exist necessarily and only one of which obtains. The main cost is that ersatz theories need modal notions to select the right representations: "consistent" or "possible state of affairs" is itself modal, so the account appears circular. Defenders reply that consistency can be taken as logical, or that modality is primitive and the theory still earns its keep by organizing modal facts. A second cost is expressive: a finite vocabulary cannot describe worlds with unnamed individuals or distinguish worlds that differ only in which individuals occupy which roles.

### Fictionalism

**Modal fictionalists** such as Gideon Rosen say that we need not believe in worlds. Talk of worlds is a useful story, and a modal sentence such as "Dana could have lost" is true iff, according to the story of the plurality of worlds, there is a world where she loses. The objection is that "according to the story" is a modal notion (what must hold in the story, given its content), so the account may smuggle in the modality it set out to explain.

### Which is best?

Each view takes a different cost. Modal realism pays in ontology, ersatzism in primitive modality or limited expressiveness, fictionalism in the status of "according to the story". A fair evaluation keeps these costs and benefits in view and asks which a theorist can best afford.

@example
title: Locating the incredulous stare
problem: A critic says: "Modal realism must be false, because it says that there are talking donkeys in universes I can never reach, and nobody could believe that." Reconstruct this as an objection to Lewis's argument and give his best reply.
step: Identify the target. The critic accepts that the argument is valid and does not attack P1, the general principle that beneficial posits should be believed. The complaint concerns the cost of the posit, so it targets P2's claim that the costs are outweighed by the benefits.
step: State the objection as a claim about weighing: the cost, belief in a vast number of concrete universes, is so great that no theoretical benefit could outweigh it.
step: Lewis's reply grants that the stare is a real cost and not a refutation. He says that the surprising feature is quantity, but the principle of parsimony restrains new kinds of entity and not new instances, and the other worlds are of the same kind as ours.
step: Evaluate. The reply depends on whether the worlds *are* of our kind and whether the cost lies in quantity or in the unknowable nature of the other worlds. The critic's best response is that unobservable, causally isolated universes are a new kind of commitment, whatever Lewis says about kinds.
answer: The incredulous stare attacks P2 by saying the costs outweigh the benefits. Lewis's reply is that the cost is real but outweighed, and that parsimony concerns kinds, not quantity. It is not decisive in either direction, and the debate turns on how to weigh common sense against theoretical gain.

@mistakes
- Saying that Lewis denies that our world is actual: he says that "actual" is indexical, so our world is actual *for us*, and other worlds are actual for their inhabitants.
- Thinking that Lewis's worlds are abstract or are mere stories: modal realist worlds are concrete, and ersatz and fictionalist worlds are not.
- Treating the incredulous stare as a proof that modal realism is false, or as no objection at all: it is a claim about weighing costs and benefits, aimed at premise P2.
- Saying that ersatzism avoids all modal primitives: its worlds are selected by a consistency or possibility notion, and the question is whether that notion can be explained without circularity.

@quiz self
prompt: A student says: "Lewis's argument is invalid because modal realism is incredible." Explain what is wrong with this, and then state what the incredulous stare does show.
hint: Distinguish validity from soundness, and then say which premise the stare challenges.
solution: Validity concerns whether the conclusion follows from the premises, not whether the premises are credible. Lewis's argument is valid as stated: if his two premises are true, the conclusion follows. The stare is an objection to soundness: it says that premise P2, that the benefits of modal realism outweigh its costs, is false, because the cost of believing in innumerable concrete universes is too high. That is a legitimate objection that has to be answered by weighing the costs and benefits, but it does not show invalidity.
