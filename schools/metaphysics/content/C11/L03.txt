@article
Take a particular wooden chair. It is brown, it stands in the kitchen, it was made from a certain batch of timber, and it is a chair. Which of these could it have lacked? Some seem easy to lose: it might have been painted white or kept in the garage. Others seem harder: could this very chair have been, say, a number, or a river? **Essentialism** is the view that things have some properties **essentially**, so that they could not exist without them, and others **accidentally**. Claims of this kind are **de re** modal claims: they attribute necessity to a thing and not merely to a sentence (week 1).

### Quine's challenge

Quine criticized "Aristotelian essentialism", the idea that a thing has some properties essentially independently of how it is described. His example, in my words: suppose every mathematician is necessarily rational and not necessarily two-legged, while every cyclist is necessarily two-legged and not necessarily rational. Now take a person who is both a mathematician and a cyclist. Is *that person* necessarily rational? Necessarily two-legged? Quine's point is that the answer seems to depend on whether we think of the person as a mathematician or as a cyclist, whereas essentialism says that the person has some properties essentially, whatever we call her. One reply is that the example runs together de dicto claims, which are about what is true of whoever satisfies a description, with de re claims about the individual, so it shows only that descriptions do not settle the de re facts. Kripke's work on rigid designators and the necessary a posteriori helped to make de re modality respectable again.

### Origin essentialism

A famous candidate for an essential property is **origin**. **Origin essentialism**, associated with Kripke, says that a thing that came from a particular source could not have come from a wholly different one: a particular person could not have had different parents, and a particular table could not have been made from a wholly different hunk of wood. The thesis is not that every detail of origin is essential: small differences seem tolerable, and an objection says that if small changes are tolerable then it is hard to see why a series of them could not lead anywhere, a sorites-style worry. Origin essentialism is also a source of examples of the **necessary a posteriori**: that this table came from this particular block of wood, if it did, is learned by investigation, yet on the view it could not have been otherwise.

### The modal account of essence

The traditional account is the **modal account**: a property $F$ is essential to $x$ iff, necessarily, if $x$ exists then $x$ has $F$. In symbols, $\Box(Ex \to Fx)$, where $Ex$ says that $x$ exists. On this account, essential properties are those a thing has in every world in which it exists. This is precise and reduces essence to modality. Kit Fine argued that it gets the order of explanation backwards.

### Fine's counterexamples

Consider Socrates and his singleton set, $\{\text{Socrates}\}$. If a set exists, its members exist, and (on standard set theory) a singleton exists whenever its member does. So necessarily, if Socrates exists, then he is a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$. The modal account then says that being a member of that set is essential to Socrates. But intuitively it is no part of what Socrates is, of his nature, that a set he belongs to exists: the set depends on him, not the other way round. Fine gave a similar case: necessarily, if Socrates exists, he is distinct from the Eiffel Tower, but that distinctness is not part of his essence. As for necessary truths, such as that $2 + 2 = 4$, they hold in every world, so the modal account makes them essential to everything.

| P1 | If the modal account is true, then any property that $x$ has in every world where $x$ exists is essential to $x$. |
| P2 | Necessarily, if Socrates exists, then Socrates is a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$. |
| P3 | Being a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$ is not essential to Socrates. |
| C | So the modal account is false. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 together imply that, if the modal account is true, membership is essential to Socrates; P3 denies the consequent, so by modus tollens the modal account is false. There are two principal replies. A modal theorist can **bite the bullet** and say that membership is essential to Socrates after all, rejecting P3, perhaps because the property, though odd, does belong to what he is. Or she can **refine the account**, restricting it to a class of properties, such as those that are not about relations to other things; the unrestricted account is then given up, which concedes the force of the case against the unrestricted view that P1 describes.

### The definitional account

Fine proposed a different account, which he took to go back to Aristotle: the essence of a thing is what it *is*, given by a real definition, and some truths are true in virtue of the nature of the thing. It is part of the essence of Socrates to be human (if it is) in a way in which it is not part of his essence to belong to a set. On this view modality is derived from essence: a necessary truth is one that holds in virtue of the natures of things. Week 4 compares the two accounts.

@example
title: Reconstructing Fine's argument against the modal account
problem: Put the singleton objection in standard form and decide which premise a modal theorist who says "it is essential to Socrates to be in his singleton" rejects.
step: State the link between the account under attack and essence as P1: if the modal account is true (that $F$ is essential to $x$ iff $\Box(Ex \to Fx)$), then whatever $x$ has in every world where it exists is essential to $x$.
step: The necessity claim is P2: $\Box(E\text{Socrates} \to \text{Socrates} \in \{\text{Socrates}\})$. It holds because a singleton exists whenever its member exists, and sets have their members in every world where they exist.
step: Fine's intuition is P3: membership of the singleton is not essential to Socrates. From P1 and P2, if the modal account were true, membership would be essential, contradicting P3. So the modal account is false.
step: The modal theorist who accepts that membership is essential to Socrates accepts P1 and P2 and rejects P3. She pays the price that "essential" no longer tracks "part of what the thing is".
answer: The reply attacks P3. Fine's argument is valid, and the dispute is about whether the intuition in P3 is reliable.

@example
title: Applying the modal account to a case
problem: The modal account says that a property is essential to $x$ iff $x$ has it at every world where $x$ exists. A vase $v$ exists at worlds $w_1, w_2, w_3$ and not at $w_4$. The vase is fragile at $w_1, w_2, w_3$, blue at $w_1, w_2$, and has the property of being such that 2+2=4 at all four worlds. Which of these three properties are essential to it by the modal account?
step: List the worlds where $v$ exists: $w_1$, $w_2$, $w_3$. Only these matter, because the account conditions on existence.
step: Being fragile holds at all three worlds where $v$ exists, so by the modal account it is essential.
step: Being blue fails at $w_3$, so it is accidental.
step: Being such that 2+2=4 holds at all four worlds, including all three where $v$ exists, so it is essential by the modal account. This is one of Fine's complaints: the account makes trivial necessities essential to everything.
answer: Fragility and being such that 2+2=4 are essential by the modal account; being blue is not.

@mistakes
- Treating "necessarily $F$" and "essentially $F$" as the same: Fine's examples are necessary properties that are not essential.
- Reading the modal account as saying that a thing has an essential property at worlds where it does not exist: the account conditions on the thing's existing.
- Thinking that Quine's mathematician-cyclist case shows that de re modality is incoherent, when it targets the claim that a thing has properties essentially independently of description.
- Saying that origin essentialism holds that every detail of origin is essential, rather than that a wholly different origin is impossible.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why Quine's mathematician-cyclist case does not by itself refute essentialism. Use the distinction between de dicto and de re.
hint: Ask what the example shows about descriptions and what the essentialist claims about individuals.
solution: Quine's case shows that "necessarily, mathematicians are rational" and "necessarily, cyclists are two-legged" are true as de dicto claims about whoever satisfies those descriptions. The essentialist claims something different: of a particular individual, that she has some properties in every world where she exists. The essentialist can accept that descriptions do not settle de re claims, and insist that the facts about the individual are fixed by what she is. So the example works against a view that infers de re necessity from de dicto necessity, but not against essentialism proper. A defender of Quine will reply that the essentialist owes an account of what fixes the de re facts apart from our ways of describing the individual.
