@article
A counterfactual theory says that causes make a difference: take the cause away and the effect goes too. But some causes make no difference, because something else would have done the job. These cases are the main test of the theory. They also decide what the theory should say about backup systems, redundant safety measures and responsibility: if a second assassin would have fired had the first not, did the first assassin cause the death?

### Three kinds of case

**Symmetric overdetermination.** Two assassins fire at the same instant and both bullets hit the heart, each sufficient for death. Had either not fired, the victim would still have died, so the death does not depend on either shot. Many people judge that each shot caused the death, though intuitions are less clear than in other cases, and Lewis suggested that they are unclear.

**Early preemption.** A backup process is ready to bring about the effect if the main process fails, but the main process succeeds and cuts the backup off *before* the effect occurs. Ana throws a stone at a window. Bo stands ready to throw if Ana does not, watches the stone in flight and, seeing it will hit, stays his hand. The window shatters. Had Ana not thrown, Bo would have, so the shattering does not depend on Ana's throw. But Ana's throw is plainly a cause.

**Late preemption.** The backup process is not cut off before the effect: it is merely too slow. Two stones fly toward a jar. One arrives first and shatters it; the second would have arrived a moment later, but the jar is already gone. The first thrower is the cause, though the jar would have shattered anyway.

### Lewis's reply to early preemption

Recall that Lewis defines causation as the ancestral of causal dependence. In the early case, there is a chain: Ana's throw, then the stone in flight at a time after Bo has already decided not to throw, then the shattering. The stone in flight depends on the throw, and, since the backup is already cut off by that time, the shattering depends on the stone in flight. So there is a chain of dependence from the throw to the shattering, and the throw is a cause.

### Late preemption

No such chain exists in the late case. At every stage of the first stone's flight, the second stone is still on its way, so the shattering does not depend on any intermediate event. The argument against the theory is therefore as follows:

| P1 | $c$ causes $e$ iff there is a chain of causal dependence from $c$ to $e$ (Lewis's theory). |
| P2 | In the late preemption case, the first thrower's throw causes the jar's shattering. |
| P3 | In the late preemption case, there is no chain of causal dependence from the first throw to the shattering. |
| C | So Lewis's theory is false. |

The argument is valid: P2 and P3 describe a cause with no chain, which P1 forbids. There are three main replies. The first attacks P3. In *Postscripts* to his papers on causation Lewis discussed, and in the end judged inadequate, the suggestion that events are **fragile**: the shattering that would have been caused by the second stone, a moment later and in slightly different fragments, would be a *different event*. So the actual shattering does depend on the first throw. Critics say that fragility cannot be made precise without proliferating causes: if every small change in timing or manner makes a different event, then almost everything is a cause of almost everything. The second reply is a new theory. In "Causation as Influence" Lewis proposed that $c$ is a cause of $e$ when alterations of $c$ would bring about alterations of $e$, in time or manner, so the first throw is a cause since a different throw would have produced a different shattering. The third reply, from Ned Hall, is **pluralism**: there are two concepts of causation, dependence and production, and the counterfactual theory is a theory of the first only. A fourth family of responses uses **structural equation models**, as in the work of Judea Pearl and of Joseph Halpern and Pearl, which test dependence while holding other variables fixed at chosen values.

### Overdetermination

For symmetric overdetermination, a theory that finds no dependence says that neither shot causes the death. The reply is either to hold, as Lewis suggested, that our intuitions in such cases are unclear, so that the theory's verdict is not clearly wrong, or to say that each shot is a cause because it would be if the other were absent: an approach that uses holding fixed or influence.

@example
title: Analyzing early preemption
problem: Ana throws a stone at a window; Bo is ready to throw if Ana does not, but stays still once he sees the stone will hit. The window breaks. Show that the breaking does not depend on Ana's throw, and show how Lewis's theory still counts the throw as a cause.
step: Direct dependence: take the closest world in which Ana does not throw. Bo throws and the window breaks anyway. So the breaking does not depend on Ana's throw.
step: Find an intermediate event: the stone in flight after Bo has decided to stay still. That event depends on Ana's throw, because if she had not thrown there would be no such stone.
step: The breaking depends on the stone in flight: holding fixed that Bo has decided not to throw, if the stone had not been in flight, the window would not have broken.
step: So there is a chain of dependence from the throw to the breaking, and Lewis's theory counts Ana's throw as a cause.
answer: No direct dependence, but a chain of dependence, so the throw is a cause. The chain works because the backup is cut off before the intermediate event.

@example
title: Why the chain fails for late preemption
problem: Two stones are thrown at a jar. The first reaches it and shatters it; the second would have shattered it a moment later. Set out the objection to Lewis's theory and give the fragility reply.
step: The first throw is a cause, so P2 holds. At every moment before the shattering, the second stone is still on its way, so the shattering does not depend on any event in the first stone's flight: P3.
step: With P1, the theory implies that the first throw is not a cause. The two together give the conclusion that the theory is false.
step: The fragility reply attacks P3: a shattering at a later moment, with different fragments, would have been a different event. The shattering that actually happens then depends on the first throw.
step: The objection to the reply is that treating events as this fragile makes the theory yield too many causes, such as the thrower's choice of shirt, which changes the details of the throw.
answer: The argument is valid. Fragility attacks P3, and the objection to fragility is overgeneration of causes.

@mistakes
- Treating early and late preemption as the same: in early preemption the backup is cut off before the effect and the chain strategy works, in late preemption it is not and the chain strategy fails.
- Saying that overdetermination is a case of preemption: in symmetric overdetermination both processes complete, and neither pre-empts the other.
- Reading the failure of the simple counterfactual test as showing that the preempting cause is not a cause: the cases are counterexamples because we judge that it is.
- Saying that the fragility reply denies that the shattering happens: it says that a differently timed shattering would be a different event.

solution: Early: a gardener waters a plant at noon; her assistant would water it at one o'clock if the soil were still dry, but checks and finds it wet. The plant survives. The survival does not depend on the noon watering, since the assistant would have watered it. The survival does not depend on the first sprinkler, nor on any intermediate event, since the second is still running. So there is no chain, and the theory has to turn to fragility or influence.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, in about 150 words, why Lewis's chain strategy handles early preemption but not late preemption, and say which reply to late preemption you find most promising.
hint: Identify the intermediate event on which the effect depends in the early case.
solution: In early preemption, the backup is cut off before the effect, so there is an intermediate event after the cutoff on which the effect depends. The first event in the chain depends on the preempting cause and the effect depends on the intermediate event, so a chain of dependence exists. In late preemption, the backup continues until the effect occurs, so at each intermediate stage the effect would have occurred anyway, and no link in the chain is a dependence. I find the influence account most promising: it treats the first throw as a cause because changing the throw would have changed the shattering's timing and manner, and it avoids multiplying events. Its weakness is that it needs an account of which alterations count as the relevant ones, and that is hard to specify without circularity.
