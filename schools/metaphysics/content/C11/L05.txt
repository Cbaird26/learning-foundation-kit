@article
Ana flips a switch and her kettle boils. Mo's barometer needle falls and a storm arrives. In both cases one event is followed by another, and we say that the first caused the second in only one of them. What is the difference? **Causation** is one of the most used and least understood notions in philosophy. We use it to explain events, assign responsibility, and judge what would have happened otherwise. Metaphysics asks what causation *is*: a relation between events or facts, and what grounds it.

### Hume's two definitions

Hume looked for the impression from which our idea of cause derives and found only that causes are followed by their effects, regularly. In the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (Section VII) he gave two definitions of "cause". The first is a **regularity** definition: a cause is an object followed by another, where objects like the first are always followed by objects like the second. The second is, in effect, **counterfactual**: where, had the first not existed, the second would never have existed. Hume seems to treat the two as equivalent, though readers dispute whether he did, and the two lines of thought have developed into rival theories.

### The regularity theory

The **regularity theory** says that $c$ causes $e$ when $c$ and $e$ are events of types that are universally conjoined: events of the type of $c$ are always followed by events of the type of $e$. J. L. Mackie refined it. A particular cause is rarely sufficient or necessary alone: a short circuit starts a fire only with flammable material nearby and no sprinkler. A cause is therefore an **INUS condition**: an *insufficient* but *non-redundant* part of an *unnecessary* but *sufficient* condition for the effect. The short circuit is a non-redundant part of a package, which is itself sufficient for the fire but not necessary, since other packages could produce a fire.

### The common-cause problem

A falling barometer reading is reliably followed by a storm, and no one thinks the reading causes the storm: both are effects of a drop in air pressure. The simple theory counts the reading as a cause.

| P1 | If events of type $C$ are always followed by events of type $E$, then each $C$ event is a cause of the $E$ event that follows it (the simple regularity theory, as a sufficient condition). |
| P2 | In a barometer case, drops in the reading are always followed by storms, though no drop in the reading causes a storm. |
| C | So the simple regularity theory is false. |

The argument is valid: P2 describes an instance in which the antecedent of P1 holds and the consequent fails. A reply that keeps the regularity theory can attack P1 by adding conditions, for instance that the regularity be a law of nature, but the barometer-storm regularity may itself be lawful (it follows from laws plus the common cause), so this reply needs more. Another attacks P2 by saying that the regularity is not exceptionless. The deeper problem is that regularity is symmetric while causation is not.

### The counterfactual theory

David Lewis's **counterfactual theory** begins with **causal dependence** between distinct actual events. Event $e$ **causally depends on** $c$ iff both occur and:

> if $c$ had not occurred, $e$ would not have occurred.

The counterfactual is evaluated by the semantics for counterfactuals: it is true iff $e$ is absent from the closest worlds, those most similar to the actual world, in which $c$ is absent. Lewis ordered worlds by similarity with a special rule that avoids "backtracking": the closest not-$c$ world keeps the actual past up to just before $c$ and lets $c$ be absent by a small miracle. Then **causation** is the *ancestral* of causal dependence: $c$ causes $e$ iff there is a chain $c = c_0, c_1, \dots, c_n = e$ in which each event depends on the one before. The chain makes causation transitive even when dependence is not.

The theory deals neatly with the barometer. If the reading had not fallen, because someone had tampered with the needle, the storm would still have come, so the storm does not depend on the reading. It also handles asymmetry: effects do not usually make a difference to their causes in the non-backtracking sense. A backtracking reading ("if the storm had not come, the pressure would not have dropped, so the barometer would not have fallen") is true on a different resolution of similarity, but it is not the one that fixes causal claims.

### Evaluation

The counterfactual theory explains why causes matter to us: they make a difference. It also reduces causation to something with a semantics, but the semantics depends on a similarity ordering that must be specified without presupposing causal notions. Week 6 considers cases where an event is a cause without being a difference-maker.

@example
title: Testing causal claims counterfactually
problem: Ana flips her kettle's switch and the kettle boils. Mo's barometer falls and then a storm arrives, both because the air pressure dropped. Say, with the counterfactual test, whether the flip caused the boiling and whether the barometer's fall caused the storm.
step: For the flip: take the closest world where Ana does not flip the switch, keeping the past the same until just before. The kettle stays cold there, so the boiling depends on the flip. The flip causes the boiling.
step: For the barometer: take the closest world where the reading does not fall, say because the needle sticks. The air pressure still dropped there, so the storm still arrives.
step: The storm does not depend on the reading, so the reading does not cause the storm. The common cause, the pressure drop, does: without the drop, there would be no fall in the reading and no storm.
answer: The flip causes the boiling, since the boiling depends on it. The barometer's fall does not cause the storm, since the storm would have come anyway.

@example
title: Causation as a chain of dependence
problem: Event $a$ is a spark, $b$ a fire, $c$ an alarm and $d$ an evacuation. Each of the three later events depends on the one before it, and it is stipulated that $d$ does not depend directly on $a$. On Lewis's theory, does $a$ cause $d$?
step: Check the chain: $b$ depends on $a$, $c$ depends on $b$, $d$ depends on $c$. Each link is a case of causal dependence.
step: Lewis defines causation as the ancestral of dependence: $a$ causes $d$ iff there is a chain of dependence from $a$ to $d$.
step: The chain $a, b, c, d$ is such a chain, so $a$ causes $d$. This holds even though $d$ does not depend directly on $a$.
answer: Yes. The chain makes causation transitive although dependence need not be.

@mistakes
- Saying that Lewis analyses causation as counterfactual dependence: causal dependence is sufficient for causation, and causation is the ancestral of dependence, which allows causes that do not make a difference by themselves.
- Treating a regularity between effects of a common cause as causal: the simple regularity theory does that, which is the problem.
- Reading the counterfactual in the barometer case as backtracking: the standard resolution holds the past fixed and lets the antecedent come about by a small miracle.
- Confusing an INUS condition with a necessary or sufficient condition: it is a non-redundant part of a sufficient condition, which need not be necessary.

@practice self
prompt: Invent a case of your own in which two events are regularly conjoined but the first does not cause the second. Say what the simple regularity theory says about it and what the counterfactual theory says.
hint: Use a common cause, as in a case of symptoms of one disease.
solution: Two clocks in a house are synchronized and the first to chime is always followed by the second. The chime of the first does not cause the second: both are caused by the arrival of the hour. The simple regularity theory says that the first chime causes the second, since chimes of the first are always followed by chimes of the second. The counterfactual theory says that if the first clock had not chimed, because it was stopped, the second would still have chimed, so there is no dependence and no causation. The cause of both chimes is the hour, through the clocks' mechanisms.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how Lewis's counterfactual theory distinguishes the causal counterfactual "if the pressure had not dropped, the storm would not have come" from the backtracking counterfactual "if the storm had not come, the pressure would not have dropped". Why does the difference matter?
hint: Ask what is held fixed in the closest worlds.
solution: Lewis's similarity ordering for causal counterfactuals holds the past fixed up to just before the antecedent and lets the antecedent come about by a small departure. For the causal counterfactual, the closest world where the pressure does not drop is one where the storm does not come. For the backtracking one, the closest world where the storm does not come is one where the past is the same up to just before and the storm is prevented by a small miracle, so the pressure still dropped. So the backtracking counterfactual is false on this ordering. The difference matters because if backtracking counterfactuals counted, effects would make a difference to their causes, and the counterfactual analysis would not capture the asymmetry of causation.

rubric: Explains that the ordering holds the past fixed until shortly before the antecedent.
rubric: Explains why the distinction matters for the asymmetry of causation.
