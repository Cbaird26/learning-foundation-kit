@article
Mo raises her hand to vote. Could she have kept it down? We hold people responsible for what they do on the assumption that they could have done otherwise, or at least that their actions came from them in the right way. But physics, or the laws of any deterministic world, might seem to leave no room for this. The question of **free will** is whether we have the kind of control over our actions that responsibility requires, and whether that control is possible in a deterministic world.

### Determinism and the options

**Determinism** is the thesis that the state of the world at any time, together with the laws of nature, entails the state at every other time. Whether our world is deterministic is not settled by philosophy: some physical theories are deterministic and others are not, and the interpretation of quantum mechanics is still disputed. The question here is conditional: *if* determinism were true, could we be free?

- **Compatibilism**: free will is compatible with determinism.
- **Incompatibilism**: free will is not compatible with determinism. Incompatibilists divide into **libertarians**, who think we have free will and determinism is false, and **hard determinists**, who think determinism is true and there is no free will. **Free will skeptics** deny free will whether or not determinism is true (week 12).

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 59% of respondents accepted or leaned toward compatibilism, about 19% libertarianism and about 11% no free will. The share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

Two conceptions shape the debate. On the **leeway** conception, free action requires that the agent could have done otherwise. On the **source** conception, it requires that the action originate in the agent in the right way, whether or not alternatives exist. The consequence argument targets leeway; Frankfurt cases (week 12) target the claim that leeway is needed.

### The consequence argument

Peter van Inwagen's **consequence argument** is the best-known case for incompatibilism. Informally: if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past. But what happened before we were born is not up to us, and neither are the laws. So the consequences of these things, including our acts, are not up to us. Formally, let $P_0$ be a complete description of the world in the remote past, $L$ the conjunction of the laws, and $P$ any true proposition about the agent's act. Write $N Q$ for "$Q$ is true and no one has, or ever had, any choice about whether $Q$".

| P1 | $\Box((P_0 \wedge L) \to P)$ (determinism) |
| P2 | $N P_0$: no one has any choice about the remote past. |
| P3 | $N L$: no one has any choice about the laws of nature. |
| P4 | Rule Alpha: for any $Q$, if $\Box Q$ then $N Q$. |
| P5 | Rule Beta: for any $Q$ and $R$, if $N Q$ and $N(Q \to R)$, then $N R$. |
| C1 | $\Box(P_0 \to (L \to P))$ (from P1 by logic) |
| C2 | $N(P_0 \to (L \to P))$ (from C1 and P4) |
| C3 | $N(L \to P)$ (from P2, C2 and P5) |
| C | $N P$ (from P3, C3 and P5) |

The argument is valid. Given determinism, no one has any choice about the act $P$. To get incompatibilism, add that free action requires some choice about the act, the leeway premise. Each compatibilist reply attacks a particular element.

- **Lewis's reply attacks P3.** David Lewis distinguished a *weak thesis*, that we are able to do something such that, if we did it, a law would have been broken, from a *strong thesis*, that we are able to break a law. He accepted the weak thesis and rejected the strong one. Had Mo kept her hand down, a small miracle would have occurred in the run-up, which is not a law broken by her act. So she has a choice about whether a law holds, in the weak sense, and P3 is false in the relevant reading. Critics reply that this makes ability depend on a miracle that no one could cause.
- **The attack on rule Beta.** McKay and Johnson showed that Beta, with Alpha, entails that if $N Q$ and $N R$ then $N(Q \wedge R)$, and gave a case in which that fails. A fair, chancy coin is not tossed, so it lands neither heads nor tails. No one could have made it land heads, since even a toss would give only a chance of heads, so no one has a choice about its not landing heads; likewise for tails. But someone could have tossed it, and then it would have landed one way or the other, so someone did have a choice about whether it landed neither heads nor tails. Thus $N Q$ and $N R$ hold while $N(Q \wedge R)$ fails, with $Q$ for "it does not land heads" and $R$ for "it does not land tails". Van Inwagen replied by repairing the rule. The dispute over the repaired rule continues.
- **The attack on leeway.** Some compatibilists deny that free will requires choice about one's act. Week 12 takes this up.

### Classical compatibilism

Hobbes and Hume held that freedom is the absence of external constraint: a person acts freely when she does what she wants and nothing stops her. G. E. Moore gave the **conditional analysis**: "$S$ could have done otherwise" means "$S$ would have done otherwise if $S$ had chosen to". This is compatible with determinism, since it is a claim about what would have followed from different choices. The objection is that the conditional can be true although the agent could not have chosen otherwise: someone with a compulsion to keep her hand down would, if she had chosen to raise it, have raised it, yet she cannot choose to. The conditional analysis then counts her as able to do otherwise, which seems to get her ability wrong. Later compatibilists, including Frankfurt, Fischer and Ravizza, and Strawson, say that freedom is better understood in terms of the agent's own motivations, responsiveness to reasons or the reactive attitudes.

@example
title: Following the consequence argument
problem: Suppose the world is deterministic. Explain, step by step, how the consequence argument reaches the conclusion that no one has any choice about whether Mo raises her hand, and where a compatibilist might object.
step: Determinism says the remote past $P_0$ and the laws $L$ entail $P$, that Mo raises her hand. By rule Alpha, no one has a choice about that conditional truth: $N(P_0 \to (L \to P))$.
step: No one has a choice about $P_0$. With rule Beta, no one has a choice about $L \to P$.
step: No one has a choice about $L$. With rule Beta again, no one has a choice about $P$.
step: A compatibilist objection: Lewis denies that no one has a choice about $L$ in the weak sense, and McKay and Johnson deny that rule Beta is valid as stated.
answer: Given determinism, rule Alpha and two uses of rule Beta, no one has a choice about whether Mo raises her hand. Replies attack P3 or rule Beta.

@example
title: The conditional analysis and its objection
problem: Mo has a compulsion that makes her unable to choose to raise her hand, though if she were to choose, she would raise it. Does the conditional analysis say she could have raised her hand? Is that the right verdict?
step: The analysis says "she could have raised it" iff "she would have raised it if she had chosen to".
step: The conditional is true by stipulation. So the analysis says she could have raised her hand.
step: The objection is that she cannot choose to, so she could not have raised it in the sense that matters. The analysis counts as able someone who intuitively is not.
step: A defender replies that the analysis should be adapted so that the choice must be one she could have made, but then the analysis uses a notion of ability that it was meant to analyze.
answer: The analysis says she could have, which seems wrong. The reply of requiring that she could have chosen is circular.

@mistakes
- Thinking that incompatibilism says that determinism is true: it says that free will and determinism cannot both hold. Van Inwagen, for one, accepts the argument and holds that determinism is false.
- Confusing compatibilism with determinism: compatibilists need not say that the world is deterministic, only that free will would survive if it were.
- Reading the consequence argument as proving that we lack free will: it shows only that, if determinism is true, there is no choice about our acts, and a leeway premise is needed to conclude that there is no free will.
- Saying that Lewis's reply denies that the laws are laws: he accepts that they are, and says that we can do things such that, were we to do them, a law would have been broken.

@practice self
prompt: Explain Lewis's reply to the consequence argument. Say which premise it attacks, state the weak and strong theses, and give one objection.
hint: Lewis distinguishes being able to break a law from being able to do something such that a law would have been broken.
solution: Lewis's reply attacks P3, that no one has any choice about the laws. He distinguishes the strong thesis, that we are able to do something that would itself break a law, from the weak thesis, that we are able to do something such that, if we did it, a law would have been broken. He rejects the strong thesis but accepts the weak one: if Mo had kept her hand down, the past would have differed in a small way from the actual past, and a small miracle would have occurred, so a law would have been broken by an event that did not cause it. The compatibilist can therefore say that Mo could have done otherwise. An objection is that this makes her ability depend on a miracle that is not in her power, and the incompatibilist can say that the ability to do something such that a miracle would have occurred is not the ability that matters for responsibility.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the McKay and Johnson objection to rule Beta in your own words, using their coin case. Say what the objection shows about the consequence argument, and how a defender of the argument can respond.
hint: Show that Beta together with Alpha entails that if $N Q$ and $N R$ then $N(Q \wedge R)$, then find a case where $N Q$ and $N R$ are true and $N(Q \wedge R)$ is false.
solution: From $N Q$ and $N R$, rule Alpha gives $N(Q \to (R \to (Q \wedge R)))$, and two uses of Beta give $N(Q \wedge R)$, so Beta with Alpha entails agglomeration. Now take a fair coin that is not tossed. Let $Q$ be "it does not land heads" and $R$ be "it does not land tails". Both are true, and no one has a choice about either, since no one could make a chancy coin land heads, or tails, by tossing it. But "it lands neither" is false if someone tosses the coin, and someone could have tossed it, so $N(Q \wedge R)$ is false. So Beta, as stated, is not valid, and the consequence argument, which uses it twice, is not shown valid by its form. A defender can repair the rule, as van Inwagen did, with a modified Beta that blocks this case but still licenses the steps of the argument, and then the dispute is over whether the repaired rule is as plausible as the original; a critic can reply that the repair is ad hoc.
