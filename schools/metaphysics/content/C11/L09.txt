@article
On Monday you say "the exam is next week". On the following Monday you say "the exam is today", and after it "the exam was last week". The exam does not move, but it seems to change in a way no date can express: it is first future, then present, then past. Is this change a feature of reality, or only of how we think about it? That question divides the two main theories of time.

### Three ways of ordering events

J. M. E. McTaggart, in "The Unreality of Time" (1908), distinguished two series of positions in time.

- The **A-series** orders events as **past, present or future**. These properties are **tensed**: they change as time goes by. An event that is future becomes present and then past.
- The **B-series** orders events by **earlier than, later than and simultaneous with**. These relations are **tenseless**: if event $e$ is earlier than event $f$, it always is.
- The **C-series** is an order without direction, such as the order of the numbers. McTaggart himself concluded that although time is unreal, reality has a non-temporal order of this kind.

The **A-theory** holds that tensed properties and facts are real and fundamental: there is an objective present, and time passes. The **B-theory** holds that the B-series is all there is: tensed talk can be explained without tensed facts, and there is no objective passage or privileged present.

### McTaggart's argument

McTaggart argued that time is unreal. His argument has two parts. First, **change** requires the A-series: if only the B-series existed, nothing would change, since each earlier-than relation is permanent. What changes is an event's A-properties. Second, the A-series is **contradictory**: every event has all of the A-properties, past, present and future, which are incompatible. The obvious reply is that an event has them at different times: it is future now, will be present and will be past. But then the A-properties are had at times, and times are themselves ordered in an A-series, so the account generates a further A-series and the same problem, in an endless regress.

| P1 | Time exists only if there is change. |
| P2 | There is change only if the A-series exists. |
| P3 | The A-series is contradictory: it requires that each event be past, present and future, which are incompatible. |
| P4 | Nothing contradictory exists. |
| C1 | So the A-series does not exist. (From P3 and P4.) |
| C2 | So there is no change. (From P2 and C1.) |
| C | So time does not exist. (From P1 and C2.) |

The argument is valid. Replies are classified by the premise they attack.

- **Against P2: the B-theorist.** Change is a thing's having a property at one time and lacking it at another: a poker is hot on Monday and cool on Tuesday. That requires only a B-series of times, not an A-series. McTaggart's reply is that nothing changes on this account, since the series of events is fixed, and that the poker's heat at each time is permanent. The B-theorist answers that this is the only change there is and that the demand for more rests on a view of change that mistakes the question.
- **Against P3: the A-theorist.** The contradiction arises only if past, present and future are treated as properties an event has at once. An A-theorist who takes tense seriously says that the tenses are not predicates but sentence operators, as in Prior's tense logic: "it was the case that $e$ is present" and "it will be the case that $e$ is present" are each true, and no event has incompatible properties at once. McTaggart's rejoinder is that this account takes tense as basic and so presupposes the A-series, and that the regress arises in explaining what it is for tense to be true. A-theorists reply that the regress is not vicious, since they do not claim to reduce tense to anything.
- A few say that McTaggart is right, accepting C.

### The tenseless account of tensed sentences

The **old B-theory** (Russell, Smart) said that tensed sentences can be **translated** into tenseless ones: "The exam is past" means "the exam is earlier than this utterance". The **new B-theory**, associated with Mellor, drops translation and says only that a tensed sentence token is true iff its event stands in the matching B-relation to the token. A. N. Prior's objection, in "Thank Goodness That's Over", is that we care whether an unpleasant event is over, not merely whether it is earlier than a token: relief at its being past cannot be relief that one thing is earlier than another. B-theorists reply that this concerns the *beliefs* we express with tensed sentences, which can be explained by the way we locate ourselves in time, and not the content of the facts.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, more philosophers accepted or leaned toward the B-theory than the A-theory (about 38% to 27%). That share is not evidence that the B-theory is true, and the debate has not been settled by physics or by argument.

@example
title: Reconstructing McTaggart's argument
problem: Put the argument "there is no change without the A-series, and the A-series is contradictory, so there is no time" in standard form, and say what a B-theorist rejects.
step: Find the conclusion: time does not exist. Find the stated premises: change requires the A-series, and the A-series is contradictory.
step: Add the hidden premises needed for validity: time exists only if there is change; nothing contradictory exists.
step: Order them: P1 time requires change, P2 change requires the A-series, P3 the A-series is contradictory, P4 nothing contradictory exists, so no A-series, so no change, so no time.
step: The B-theorist accepts P3 or at least the lesson of the regress, and rejects P2: change is a thing's having different properties at different times, which needs only the B-series.
answer: The B-theorist rejects P2. The argument is valid, so rejecting any premise is enough.

@example
title: Tensed and tenseless truth
problem: Ana says on Monday "the exam is tomorrow". On the old B-theory, give a tenseless equivalent. On the new B-theory, say what makes her token true.
step: Old B-theory: the sentence means "the exam is on the day after the day of this utterance".
step: New B-theory: the meaning is not given by a translation. The token is true iff the exam occurs on the day after the token occurs, which is a tenseless B-relation between the exam and the token.
step: Prior's objection applies to both: when Ana feels relief that an unpleasant event is over, she is not relieved that it is earlier than this utterance. B-theorists reply that tensed beliefs are explained by locating oneself in time.
answer: The old theory gives a translation. The new theory gives truth conditions for the token without claiming synonymy. Both face Prior's objection.

@mistakes
- Treating the B-series as unchanging in every sense: the B-theorist says that things change, by having different properties at different times, but the order of earlier and later does not change.
- Confusing the A-theory with presentism: the A-theory says that tensed facts are fundamental, and presentism, discussed in week 10, says that only the present exists. Growing-block and moving-spotlight views are A-theories without presentism.
- Saying that McTaggart's argument is invalid: it is valid, and the replies reject premises P2, P3 or both.
- Saying that the B-theory denies that tensed sentences can be true: it holds that they can, but that their truth does not need tensed facts.
