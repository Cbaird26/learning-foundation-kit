@article
Ana's watch stopped at noon. It might have kept running; it could not have been a prime number. We say such things constantly, and we also say that the watch must have been stopped by a flat battery, or that visitors must sign in. All of these use words of **modality**: ways things could, must or might be, as opposed to how they actually are. Metaphysics asks what makes modal claims true. This course begins there, because causation, laws, time and free will are all discussed with modal vocabulary: a cause makes a difference to what *would* happen, a law *has to* hold, and an agent is free only if she *could have done otherwise*.

### Kinds of modality

"Possible" and "necessary" have several senses, and many apparent disagreements disappear once you fix the sense.

- **Metaphysical** (or alethic) modality concerns how things could have been, in the broadest sense of "could" that is not just about what we know. On the standard view, whatever is metaphysically possible is logically possible, but not conversely: "Water is not H$_2$O" is logically consistent, yet on Kripke's view it is metaphysically impossible.
- **Nomological** modality concerns what is compatible with the laws of nature. A faster-than-light signal would be nomologically impossible whatever one thinks about its metaphysical status.
- **Epistemic** modality concerns what is compatible with what someone knows: "The library might be closed" can be true because I have not checked, even if it is open and could not have been closed today.
- **Deontic** modality concerns what is permitted or required: "Visitors must sign in."

The rest of the course concerns metaphysical modality unless stated otherwise. Write $\Box A$ for "necessarily $A$" and $\Diamond A$ for "possibly $A$". They are interdefinable: $\Diamond A$ is equivalent to $\neg\Box\neg A$, and $\Box A$ to $\neg\Diamond\neg A$.

### De dicto and de re

A modal word can attach to a whole sentence (**de dicto**) or to a thing (**de re**). "Necessarily, the oldest member of the choir is a member of the choir" is true on its de dicto reading: whoever fits the description is a member. On its de re reading it says of the person who is in fact the oldest, Bea, that she is necessarily a member of the choir. That is false, since she might never have joined. Confusing the two readings is the commonest mistake in this part of metaphysics. De re modality is the subject of week 3.

### Possible worlds

The standard tool is **possible-worlds semantics**. A **possible world** is a complete way things might have been. A sentence is **possible** if it is true at some world, and **necessary** if true at every world. In the refined, Kripke-style version, a model has a set of worlds, an **accessibility relation** saying which worlds are possible *relative to* which, and an assignment of truth values to sentence letters at each world:

- $\Box A$ is true at $w$ iff $A$ is true at every world accessible from $w$.
- $\Diamond A$ is true at $w$ iff $A$ is true at some world accessible from $w$.

If every world is accessible from every world, the logic is S5, the usual choice for metaphysical modality. A world with no accessible worlds makes every $\Box$ sentence true and every $\Diamond$ sentence false. This is a formal fact about the definitions, not a claim about reality.

### From semantics to ontology

The semantics is a mathematical model. Does the metaphysics need worlds? Here is the argument from the indispensability of worlds, which applies Quine's criterion of ontological commitment (a theory is committed to what its quantifiers must range over for the theory to be true):

| P1 | Some modal claims are true. |
| P2 | The best account of what makes modal claims true quantifies over possible worlds. |
| P3 | If some modal claims are true and the best account of what makes them true quantifies over possible worlds, then there are possible worlds. |
| C | So there are possible worlds. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 together give the antecedent of P3, and modus ponens yields C. P1 is hard to deny, though some deny that modal sentences are literally true. Premise P2 is attacked by those who say modal truths are grounded in the essences of things, in dispositions, or in modal primitives, with no appeal to worlds. Premise P3 is attacked by those who say that a semantics is an instrument, like a model in physics, and that quantifying over worlds in the model does not commit us to them. Whichever way one replies, the argument shows that the question "are there worlds?" is a question about what best explains modal truth. Week 2 compares the three main answers.

@example
title: De dicto and de re
problem: Dana won the chess tournament, but the final could have gone to Eli. Consider "Necessarily, the winner of the tournament won the tournament." Give its de dicto and de re readings and say which is true.
step: De dicto: at every possible world, whoever wins the tournament at that world wins the tournament at that world. The modal operator governs the whole sentence.
step: This is true because it follows from what "the winner" means. It does not depend on who the winner is.
step: De re: the person who is in fact the winner, Dana, is such that at every possible world she wins the tournament. The description picks out Dana in the actual world and the modal claim is about her.
step: This is false. At the world where Eli wins the final, Dana does not win, and Dana herself exists there.
answer: The de dicto reading is true and the de re reading is false. The sentence is ambiguous, and an argument that moves from the first to the second is invalid.

@example
title: Evaluating $\Diamond$ and $\Box$ in a model
problem: A model has worlds $w_1, w_2, w_3$. Accessibility: $w_1$ sees $w_2$ and $w_3$; $w_2$ sees $w_3$; $w_3$ sees no world. The letter $P$ is true at $w_2$ only. At which worlds are $\Diamond P$, $\Box P$ and $\Box\Diamond P$ true?
step: $\Diamond P$ needs an accessible world where $P$ is true. $w_1$ sees $w_2$, where $P$ is true, so $\Diamond P$ is true at $w_1$. $w_2$ sees only $w_3$, where $P$ is false, and $w_3$ sees nothing, so $\Diamond P$ is false at both.
step: $\Box P$ needs $P$ at every accessible world. At $w_1$, $P$ fails at $w_3$; at $w_2$ it fails at $w_3$. At $w_3$ there are no accessible worlds, so the requirement holds vacuously and $\Box P$ is true.
step: $\Box\Diamond P$ needs $\Diamond P$ at every accessible world. $\Diamond P$ is true only at $w_1$, which no world sees. So $w_1$ and $w_2$ fail, and $w_3$ passes vacuously.
answer: $\Diamond P$ is true at $w_1$ only; $\Box P$ is true at $w_3$ only; $\Box\Diamond P$ is true at $w_3$ only.

@mistakes
- Treating epistemic and metaphysical possibility as the same: "It might be that Hesperus is not Phosphorus" can be true epistemically even if the identity is metaphysically necessary.
- Reading a de dicto necessity as a de re one, and so concluding that a thing has a property essentially because a description of it necessarily applies.
- Forgetting that a world with no accessible worlds makes every $\Box A$ true and every $\Diamond A$ false.
- Taking the semantics itself to show that there are worlds: the argument needs the extra premises about the best account of modal truth.

solution: "Not possible that $P$" is $\neg\Diamond P$, which is equivalent to $\Box\neg P$: in no accessible world is $P$ true. The choice $\neg\Box P$ says only that $P$ fails somewhere, which is the weaker claim that $\neg P$ is possible, and that choice is $\Diamond\neg P$ in other notation.

@quiz mc
prompt: In the indispensability argument for possible worlds, someone replies that modal truths are made true by the essences of things and the dispositions they have, so that the best account of modal truth does not quantify over worlds. Which premise does this reply attack?
choice: P1: Some modal claims are true.
choice*: P2: The best account of what makes modal claims true quantifies over possible worlds.
choice: P3: If modal claims are true and the best account of their truth quantifies over worlds, then there are worlds.
choice: The validity of the argument.
hint: The reply offers a rival account of what makes modal claims true.
solution: The reply grants that modal claims are true but denies that the best account of their truth mentions worlds, so it rejects P2. Rejecting P1 would deny that modal claims are literally true, and rejecting P3 would deny that the semantics commits us to what it quantifies over. The argument is valid as stated, so the reply does not target validity.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why a philosopher might accept possible-worlds semantics as a useful model of modal logic and still reject premise P3 of the indispensability argument. Give one reply the defender of P3 could make.
hint: Compare the model with a scientific model that is useful without being literally true.
solution: A semantics assigns truth conditions to modal sentences by quantifying over worlds, and it gives a systematic account of which modal inferences are valid. The skeptic about P3 says this shows only that the model is an effective tool, as an idealized frictionless plane is a tool in mechanics, and that quantifying over worlds in the model does not show that the best metaphysics of modality contains them. The defender can reply that, by Quine's criterion, we cannot pick which parts of our best theory to take literally without a principled reason, and that the semantics is not merely idealized but explains the validity of modal arguments and the truth of modal claims in a way no rival does.
