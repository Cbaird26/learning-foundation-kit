@article
A sugar cube dissolves when placed in water. It is **soluble** even in the cupboard, where it is not dissolving. Fragility, flammability, courage and elasticity are likewise **dispositions**: properties a thing can have without manifesting them. Dispositions matter to metaphysics because they look like a bridge between actual and merely possible events, and because some philosophers hold that *all* fundamental properties are dispositional. They also bear on the essence of things, the topic of week 3.

### The simple conditional analysis

The obvious analysis is the **simple conditional analysis**. Call the triggering circumstance the **stimulus** $S$ and the characteristic response the **manifestation** $M$:

> $x$ is disposed to $M$ when $S$ iff, if $x$ were to undergo $S$, $x$ would $M$.

So a glass is fragile iff it would break if struck. The analysis explains dispositions in terms of counterfactuals (week 5 returns to those). It looks innocuous but has well-known counterexamples.

### Finks, masks and mimics

A **fink**, in the sense introduced by C. B. Martin, is something that would remove a disposition at the moment of the stimulus. A live electrical wire is disposed to conduct if touched by a conductor. Suppose a device is attached that cuts the current when, and only when, the wire is touched. The wire is live and so disposed to conduct, but it is false that it would conduct if touched, because the device would make it dead first. The disposition is present and the counterfactual is false.

A **mask** is something that stops the manifestation without removing the disposition. A fragile glass, packed in thick foam, is still fragile: but if it were struck, it would not break. This case is credited to Mark Johnston. A **mimic** works the other way: an object that lacks the disposition would nonetheless give the manifestation. A sturdy plate, with a hidden mechanism that smashes it whenever it is struck, would break if struck, though it is not fragile.

The argument against the simple analysis can be set out so that its structure is clear:

| P1 | Necessarily, $x$ is disposed to $M$ when $S$ iff $x$ would $M$ if $x$ were to undergo $S$ (the simple conditional analysis). |
| P2 | It is possible for a wire to be disposed to conduct when touched, even though it is not the case that it would conduct if touched (the fink case). |
| C | So the simple conditional analysis is false. |

The argument is valid: if the analysis held of necessity, no possible wire could be disposed without the counterfactual being true, and P2 says there is such a wire. Replies divide. Some defenders of the analysis attack P2: they say that the finked wire is not really disposed to conduct, since a wire that would lose its charge when touched lacks the disposition in the relevant sense. Others accept P2 and attack P1 by revising the analysis. David Lewis proposed a **reformed conditional analysis**: $x$ is disposed to $M$ when $S$ iff $x$ has an intrinsic property $B$ such that, if $x$ were to undergo $S$ and retain $B$ for long enough, then $S$ and $B$ together would cause $x$ to $M$. The reformed analysis lets the finked wire count as disposed, since the counterfactual holds when the fink is held off by stipulating that it retains $B$. Critics reply that new finks and masks can be built against the reformed version, and that appealing to "the intrinsic basis" quietly presupposes a prior grasp of the disposition.

### Powers and dispositional essentialism

A deeper question is what dispositions are. On **Humean categoricalism**, fundamental properties are **categorical**: their nature is what they are like in themselves (their quiddity), and what they do depends on the laws, which are contingent. On **powers** views, fundamental properties are dispositional or power-like through and through, and being the property it is consists in conferring certain dispositions. Philosophers such as Alexander Bird and Brian Ellis defended **dispositional essentialism**: a fundamental property has its dispositional profile essentially, so the same property could not have had a different causal role in another world. A consequence is that laws of nature, which describe what the powers do, come out metaphysically necessary, not contingent.

The two sides differ on what grounds modality in the world. The categoricalist locates necessity in how worlds can be recombined (week 2). The power theorist locates it in the natures of properties, which fits Fine's view that necessity flows from essence (week 3). Objections to powers views include the worry that if a power is individuated by its manifestation, which is another power, the account may be regressive or circular, and that necessary laws seem to clash with the thought that the laws might have been different.

### Essence again

The week 3 views give different accounts of what it is for a disposition to belong to something essentially. On the modal account, a sugar cube is essentially soluble iff it is soluble at every world where it exists. On the definitional account, the question is whether solubility figures in what the cube *is*, given by its real definition.

@example
title: Classifying a counterexample to the conditional analysis
problem: A vase is fragile. It sits in a box packed with foam, so that if it were struck inside the box it would not break. Which kind of counterexample is this, and does it show that the analysis is false?
step: Identify the facts. The vase is fragile, so it has the disposition. The counterfactual "if struck, it would break" is false because of the foam.
step: Classify. The foam interferes with the manifestation and does not remove the disposition. That is a **mask**, not a fink.
step: Apply the analysis. P1 says the vase is disposed iff the counterfactual holds. Here the left side is true and the right side false.
step: Conclude. If the vase is fragile in the box, the simple analysis is false. A defender may deny the fragility or point out that a quick fix, such as "if struck and unmasked", presupposes the notion of masking.
answer: The case is a mask. If we accept that the vase is fragile in the box, it refutes the simple analysis.

@example
title: Comparing categoricalism and dispositional essentialism
problem: Suppose a property $Q$ in our world confers a disposition to repel other $Q$-bearers. Is there a world with $Q$ in which $Q$-bearers attract each other? Give each view's answer.
step: The categoricalist says that $Q$'s nature is fixed independently of what it does. So $Q$ could have been connected with attraction by different laws: there is a world of the kind asked about.
step: The dispositional essentialist says that repelling is part of what $Q$ is. A property that attracts would be a different property, so the described world is impossible.
step: Both views agree about our world. They disagree about which worlds are possible.
answer: The categoricalist says yes, the essentialist says no. The case shows that the dispute is about modality and the identity of properties, not about the actual laws.

@mistakes
- Saying that a mask removes the disposition: a mask blocks the manifestation, and a fink removes (or would remove) the disposition itself.
- Taking the failure of the simple analysis to show that there are no dispositions, when it shows that counterfactuals alone do not capture them.
- Treating the powers view as the claim that dispositions are real: categoricalists can accept dispositions as real but grounded in categorical bases.
- Confusing dispositional essentialism with the claim that all properties are dispositions: the view concerns fundamental properties and their essential natures.

prompt: Compare the modal and the definitional accounts of essence by applying both to the claim "being soluble is essential to this sugar cube". State which of the two accounts you find better supported and why.
hint: Apply the modal account to every world in which the cube exists, and the definitional account to what it is to be this cube.
solution: On the modal account, solubility is essential iff the cube is soluble at every world where it exists. That seems plausible, since a cube that was not soluble would have to be made of something different, though it depends on how much change of composition the cube tolerates. On the definitional account, the question is whether solubility figures in the real definition of the cube, or only of sugar; the cube may be essentially a piece of sugar and soluble because sugar is soluble. The modal account is simpler, but Fine's cases show that necessity given existence does not suffice for essence, so I prefer the definitional account's distinction between what follows from the nature of a thing and what merely accompanies it. The best reply is that the definitional account needs a primitive notion of "what a thing is".
