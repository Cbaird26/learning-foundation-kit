title: Metaphysics paper: argue one debate in modality, causation, laws, time or free will
overview: You will write a 2,500-word paper on one debate from this course: modal realism, the counterfactual theory of causation, Humean laws, presentism or the consequence argument. The paper states the strongest argument on each side in standard form, uses at least one case or thought experiment of your own, and defends a conclusion. Weeks 13 and 14 are for drafting, revising and preparing for the unseen final.
goal: Reconstruct the strongest argument for and against a position in standard form, with any hidden premises added so that each argument is valid.
goal: Invent a case or thought experiment of your own and use it to test the arguments.
goal: Defend a conclusion that says which premise you accept or reject and why, and respond to the best objection to your own view.
step: Choose one of the five debates in the first material. Read the relevant Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry and search PhilPapers for one or two recent articles on the debate. Record full citations for everything you use.
step: Write the strongest argument for each side in standard form (premises P1, P2, ..., conclusion C), adding hidden premises so that each argument is valid. Check each one by asking whether the conclusion could be false while all the premises are true.
step: For each argument, say which premise the other side attacks, and state the best reply. Decide which premise is carrying the dispute.
step: Invent a case or thought experiment of your own, different from the cases in the lessons, and work out what each side says about it. A good case makes the two sides give different verdicts.
step: Decide your conclusion. It should be a claim you can defend in 2,500 words, such as "the incredulous stare does not refute modal realism, but it shifts the burden", and not a survey. Write a one-sentence thesis and a one-paragraph outline.
step: Draft the paper using the structure suggested in the second material. Write for an educated reader who has not taken this course, define each technical term when you first use it, and paraphrase rather than quote.
step: Revise. Check that every argument is in valid form, every objection is attributed to the premise it attacks, the case does real work, and the conclusion is the one you defended. Cut anything that does not serve the thesis. Check the word count and the citations.
step: Prepare for week 14: review your own paper and the lessons, and practice setting out one argument from each week from memory. The final is unseen and lasts 120 minutes.
material: **The five debates.**

| Debate | Main argument for | Main argument against |
|---|---|---|
| Modal realism | The argument from theoretical utility: positing a plurality of concrete worlds gives the best systematic account of modality and related notions. | The incredulous stare (the costs outweigh the benefits), the Humphrey objection to counterparts, and the epistemological objection about causally isolated worlds. |
| The counterfactual theory of causation | Causes make a difference: the theory explains the barometer case and the asymmetry of causation without regularities. | Late preemption: the preempting cause is a cause although the effect depends on no intermediate event. |
| Humean laws (best-system account) | The account respects Hume's dictum, uses no necessary connections, and says how laws differ from accidents. | The circularity objection: laws fixed by their instances seem unable to explain them. (The matching objection to non-Humean views is the inference problem.) |
| Presentism | Only the present is real: it fits the sense that the past is gone and the future open. | The relativity argument (simultaneity is frame-relative) and the truthmaker objection to truths about the past. |
| The consequence argument | If determinism is true, no one has a choice about the past or the laws, so none about their acts: an argument using rules Alpha and Beta. | Lewis's reply that denies we have no choice about the laws, and the McKay-Johnson objection to rule Beta. |

You may take either side. The assignment is the quality of the argument, not the side you take.

## Week-by-week review

**Weeks 1 and 2: modality and worlds.** Necessity and possibility, with metaphysical, nomological, epistemic and deontic senses; $\Diamond A \leftrightarrow \neg\Box\neg A$; de dicto and de re; evaluating $\Box$ and $\Diamond$ in a Kripke-style model, with vacuous truth at dead ends. Lewis's modal realism, ersatzism and fictionalism, with the incredulous stare aimed at the claim that costs are outweighed.

**Weeks 3 and 4: essence and powers.** The modal account of essence, Fine's singleton and Eiffel Tower counterexamples, the definitional account, Quine's challenge, origin essentialism. The simple conditional analysis of dispositions, finks, masks and mimics, Lewis's reformed analysis, categoricalism and dispositional essentialism.

**Weeks 5 and 6: causation.** Hume's two definitions, the regularity theory and INUS conditions, the common-cause objection, Lewis's counterfactual theory with causal dependence and its ancestral. Symmetric overdetermination, early preemption (the chain works), late preemption (the chain fails), fragility and influence.

**Weeks 7 and 8: processes and laws.** Mark transmission, conserved quantities, double prevention, Dowe's quasi-causation, Hall's dependence and production. Laws against accidents, the best-system account, necessitation and powers views, the inference problem and the circularity objection.

**Weeks 9 and 10: time.** The A-, B- and C-series, McTaggart's argument, the A-theory and B-theory, Prior's objection. Presentism, eternalism, the growing block and moving spotlight, the relativity of simultaneity, and Lewis on the grandfather paradox.

**Weeks 11 and 12: free will.** Determinism, the consequence argument with rules Alpha and Beta, Lewis's weak thesis, the conditional analysis. Libertarianism and the luck objection, Frankfurt cases and the flicker and dilemma replies, Strawson's basic argument.

## Self-check

- For each argument, I can write the premises, add the hidden premise that makes it valid, and say which premise each named reply attacks.
- I can classify a case as overdetermination, early preemption or late preemption, and say what Lewis's chain strategy does with it.
- I can evaluate $\Box$ and $\Diamond$ in a small model, and use the modal account of essence on a list of worlds.
- I can say what a view claims without saying that it is true, and I know the share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence for it.

## Common errors

- Treating validity as truth: the arguments in this course are valid, and the dispute is about premises.
- Mixing de dicto and de re readings, or epistemic and metaphysical possibility.
- Treating necessary properties as essential, or dependence as causation.
- Running together the A-theory with presentism, compatibilism with determinism, and skepticism with hard determinism.
- Attributing a reply to the wrong premise: say which premise it rejects, not just that it is a reply.

@exam mc
prompt: Two assassins fire at the same instant and each bullet would suffice to kill. Separately, a lamp's moving spot of light travels along a wall. Which pairing of the two cases with a theory is correct?
choice*: The death is a case where the counterfactual theory finds no dependence on either shot, and the moving spot is a pseudo-process because it carries no mark or conserved quantity.
choice: The death shows that the process theory is false, and the spot shows that the counterfactual theory is false.
choice: The death is a case of early preemption, and the spot of light is a causal process.
choice: Neither case troubles any theory of causation.
hint: Ask in the first case whether the death depends on either shot, and in the second whether anything is carried along.
solution: In symmetric overdetermination, the death does not counterfactually depend on either shot, since the other would have sufficed. The moving spot is a pseudo-process, since it does not carry a mark or a conserved quantity. The first case troubles the simple counterfactual theory and the second is an example used to define causal processes. Overdetermination is not preemption, and the spot is not a causal process.

@exam num
prompt: A clock moves at 0.8 times the speed of light relative to a frame S, and 10 seconds pass on the moving clock. How many seconds pass in S during that time? Give the answer to two decimal places.
answer: 16.67
unit: seconds
tol: 0.01
check: 10 / sqrt(1 - 0.8 ** 2)
hint: Use time dilation: the time in S is the proper time multiplied by $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.
solution: With $v = 0.8c$, $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 1/0.6 \approx 1.667$, so 10 seconds on the moving clock correspond to about 16.67 seconds in S. Time dilation is measured with fast-moving particles and atomic clocks in aircraft and satellites. It is part of the physics behind the argument from relativity against presentism: relativity makes simultaneity, and so the present, depend on the frame.

@exam self
prompt: Dispositional essentialists hold that laws of nature are metaphysically necessary. Explain how this view bears on Lewis's reply to the consequence argument, in about 300 words. State the consequence argument's premise about the laws, state Lewis's reply, say what changes if the laws are necessary, and say what a compatibilist could do in response.
hint: Rule Alpha says that necessary truths are ones no one has a choice about. Lewis's weak thesis concerns worlds in which a law is broken.
solution: The consequence argument has a premise that no one has any choice about the laws of nature ($N L$). Lewis rejects it in a weak sense: Mo is able to do something such that, if she did it, a law would have been broken, by a small miracle in the run-up. This needs the laws to be contingent: there must be a world, close to the actual one, in which the laws are different. A dispositional essentialist says that the laws flow from the essential natures of properties, so they hold in every world. Then rule Alpha applies directly to the laws and gives $N L$: if $L$ is necessary then no one has any choice about it. There is no world in which a law is broken, so the weak thesis cannot be true, and Lewis's reply loses its footing. Counterfactuals with impossible antecedents are controversial in any case. So the view makes the consequence argument harder to resist at premise P3. A compatibilist who accepts necessary laws would need another reply: she might attack rule Beta, as McKay and Johnson do, or deny that responsibility needs the ability to do otherwise, as Frankfurt-style semicompatibilists do, or hold that ability is analyzed by the conditional analysis, which does not require power over the laws. A Humean about laws, by contrast, finds Lewis's reply natural, since laws are contingent summaries of the mosaic.
rubric: States the premise about the laws and Lewis's reply, including the weak thesis, accurately.
rubric: Explains correctly that the reply needs the laws to be contingent, and that necessary laws make $N L$ follow by rule Alpha.
rubric: Names at least one alternative compatibilist reply, such as attacking Beta or denying that responsibility requires leeway.
rubric: Does not claim that the dispositional essentialist view settles the free will question, and notes that Humeans about laws are better placed for Lewis's reply.
