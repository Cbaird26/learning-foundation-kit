@article
A **narrative** tells a story, real or imagined. Strong narratives feel alive because the writer controls structure, details and voice.

### Plot structure

Most narratives follow the **plot diagram**:

1. **Exposition**: introduces the characters, setting and situation.
2. **Rising action**: a conflict appears and tension builds through a series of events.
3. **Climax**: the turning point, the most intense moment.
4. **Falling action**: events after the climax.
5. **Resolution**: how the conflict ends and what changes.

Short narratives focus on one conflict and one scene or a few connected moments. Do not try to tell a whole year in a page.

### Start strong

Open with action, dialogue, a striking detail or a question, not "My story is about..." Put readers inside a moment right away.

### Show, don't tell

**Telling** names a feeling: "I was nervous." **Showing** lets the reader see it: "My palms were damp, and I read the same line of my speech three times." Use the senses and specific details. Replace general words (*walked, said, nice*) with precise ones (*shuffled, whispered, welcoming*).

### Dialogue

Dialogue reveals character and moves the story. Start a new paragraph each time the speaker changes. Put the speaker's exact words in quotation marks, and put punctuation inside: "Come with me," she said. Use a comma before a dialogue tag after a quotation, but a period when the quote is a complete sentence followed by an action: "I can't." He looked away.

### Pacing and transitions

Slow down at important moments by adding detail; speed past unimportant ones with a sentence of summary. Use transitions of time (*Later that night, By noon, Suddenly*) to guide readers.

### Point of view and voice

Choose first person or third person and stay consistent. Let your characters' words and thoughts sound like them.

### Ending

A resolution should answer the conflict. It does not have to be happy, but it should feel earned. Avoid endings like "and then I woke up" or "it was all a dream." A good ending shows how the character or situation has changed.

### Revising

After drafting, check: Is the conflict clear? Is there a climax? Which lines tell where they could show? Did you use at least two senses? Is each dialogue paragraph punctuated correctly?

@example
title: Turning telling into showing
problem: Revise the telling sentence "Jonah was scared of the dark basement" into two or three sentences that show his fear without using the word scared.
step: Choose senses and body reactions: a sound, a physical sign, an action.
step: Draft: "Jonah's hand hovered over the light switch. Somewhere below, a pipe clanked, and the hairs on his arms stood up."
step: Add a choice that reveals feeling: "He decided the laundry could wait until morning."
step: Check: the passage never names the fear, but sound, body reaction and a decision all show it.
@mistakes
- Starting with a long background paragraph, so the conflict does not appear until late.
- Telling feelings ("I was sad") instead of showing them with details and actions.
- Starting a new speaker's words in the same paragraph, or placing punctuation outside the quotation marks.
