@article
**Point of view** is the perspective from which a story is told. **Tone** is the author's attitude toward the subject. Together they shape what you learn and how you feel as a reader.

### Types of point of view

- **First person**: the narrator is a character and uses *I*, *me*, *my*. You learn only what that narrator knows, notices or chooses to say. First-person narrators can be unreliable, meaning they may be mistaken or biased.
- **Third person limited**: an outside narrator uses *he*, *she*, *they* and tells the thoughts of only **one** character.
- **Third person omniscient**: an outside narrator knows the thoughts of **many** characters (and may comment on the story).

Nonfiction has a point of view, too. An author's own account of an event is first person; a news report usually stays in third person.

### Why it matters

Imagine a story about a lost homework assignment. In first person from the student, you feel panic. From the teacher's limited view, you might see only a messy desk. An omniscient narrator could show both. Point of view controls **what information you get** and **how much you sympathize**.

**Point of view is not the same as opinion.** A character's point of view may differ from other characters' views or the author's. Always ask who is speaking and what they cannot know.

### Tone

Tone is shown through **word choice (diction)**, details, sentence length and the images chosen. Tone words include *sarcastic, joyful, formal, bitter, hopeful, nostalgic, humorous, critical, objective*.

Compare: "The old house crouched at the end of the lane, its shutters hanging like tired eyelids" (gloomy, eerie) with "The old house waited at the end of the lane, its shutters open like friendly eyes" (warm, welcoming). The same house, different words, different tone.

**Mood** is the feeling the *reader* experiences; tone is the *author's* attitude. They are often close but not identical.

### Finding tone

1. Underline words with strong feelings (**connotations**, the emotions a word carries beyond its dictionary meaning).
2. Notice sentence rhythm: short, clipped sentences can feel tense; long, flowing sentences can feel calm.
3. Ask what attitude those choices add up to, and name it with a precise word. "Good" or "bad" is too vague.

@example
title: Point of view and tone together
problem: Read: "I waited at the window all afternoon. Every car that rolled past was the wrong one, and each one made the house seem emptier. When the headlights finally turned in, I pretended I hadn't been watching at all." Identify the point of view and the tone.
step: The narrator uses "I" and describes only personal observations and feelings, so this is first person.
step: Note the words: "waited all afternoon," "wrong one," "emptier," "pretended." They carry longing and disappointment.
step: The sentences begin slowly and build, mirroring patient waiting; the final line adds a hint of embarrassment and hidden hope.
step: Tone: wistful and anxious. Evidence: "each one made the house seem emptier" and "pretended I hadn't been watching."
@mistakes
- Treating "first person" as "the author's real life"; the narrator is a created voice.
- Choosing a vague tone like "sad" or "nice" with no word evidence, instead of a specific tone such as wistful or hopeful.
- Confusing tone (the author's attitude) with mood (the reader's feeling) or with the topic.

solution: Cheerful: "The rain danced on the town and set every puddle sparkling." (words: danced, sparkling; tone: playful, cheerful). Gloomy: "The rain crawled over the town and soaked every empty street." (words: crawled, soaked, empty; tone: gloomy).
rubric: Two rewritten sentences keep the same event (rain falling on a town).
rubric: Word choices clearly differ in connotation.
