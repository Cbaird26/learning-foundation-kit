@article
You believe that your keys are on the kitchen table. Do you know it? If you saw them there a minute ago, probably yes. If you are only guessing and they happen to be there, no. **Epistemology** studies this difference: what knowledge is, what justifies a belief, and whether we know as much as we think.

### The traditional analysis

On an old and natural proposal, which goes back to Plato's discussion in the *Theaetetus*, **knowledge is justified true belief**. To know that $p$ you must (1) believe that $p$, (2) $p$ must be true, and (3) your belief must be **justified**, backed by good reasons or evidence. Each condition seems needed: you cannot know what you do not believe, you cannot know what is false, and a lucky guess is not knowledge.

### Gettier cases

In 1963 Edmund Gettier published a short paper giving cases in which someone has a justified true belief that is not knowledge. Here is a case in the same spirit. Mira checks a weather app that says rain is falling at her house. She believes it is raining, and she is justified: the app is usually accurate. But the app froze a week ago and shows an old reading. By coincidence, it is raining at her house right now. Her belief is true and justified, yet it seems a matter of luck that she is right, so she does not know. A **Gettier case** is a counterexample to the claim that justified true belief is sufficient for knowledge. Philosophers have proposed fourth conditions, such as that the justification must not depend on a false assumption, or that the belief must be reliably produced; none has won general agreement.

### Skepticism

**Skepticism** about the external world holds that we do not know (or cannot know) that the world around us is as it seems. In his *Meditations* (1641), Descartes considers that he might be dreaming, or that an all-powerful deceiving being, an **evil demon**, might be producing all his experiences. A modern version imagines a **brain in a vat** fed experiences by a computer. The argument can be put like this:

| P1 | If I know that I have hands, then I know that I am not a brain in a vat with only the experience of having hands. |
| P2 | I do not know that I am not a brain in a vat with only the experience of having hands. |
| C | So I do not know that I have hands. |

This is valid (it is modus tollens, with P1 resting on the idea that knowledge is closed under known implication). The question is which premise to reject.

### Three replies

- **The Moorean reply** (after G. E. Moore): I am more certain that I have hands than I am of either premise of the skeptic's argument, so it is more reasonable to reject a premise (P2) than to accept the conclusion.
- **Contextualism** says that "know" is context-sensitive: in ordinary conversation the standards for knowing are modest, and I do know that I have hands; in a philosophy seminar the standards go up and the skeptic's claim is true in that context, without showing that I fail to know in everyday contexts.
- **Externalism** holds that justification or knowledge can depend on facts outside one's own mind, such as whether one's belief was produced by a reliable process. If I am not in a vat, my beliefs are reliably formed and count as knowledge, even though I cannot prove from the inside that I am not in a vat.

Some philosophers instead deny P1, which means giving up closure. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 80% of respondents accepted or leaned toward non-skeptical realism about the external world; but how many philosophers hold a view is no evidence that it is true. To evaluate a reply, ask whether it explains why the skeptic's argument seems compelling and whether it pays a cost.
@example
title: Explaining a Gettier case
problem: Explain why this case shows that justified true belief is not knowledge: Omar reads on a printed timetable that his bus leaves at 8:10. It is last year's timetable, and the company has since moved the regular departure to 8:30. But today a road closure has put the bus back on its old time, and it does leave at 8:10.
step: Check the three conditions. Omar believes the bus leaves at 8:10; it does leave then, so the belief is true; and he has a reason, the timetable, which seems good evidence, so he is justified.
step: But his justification depends on an out-of-date source, since the regular schedule has changed. It is only by luck that the road closure made the old time right today.
step: Intuitively, Omar does not know the departure time, though all three conditions are met. So justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge.
answer: The case satisfies belief, truth and justification, yet success is a matter of luck, so it is a counterexample to sufficiency.
@example
title: Locating the target of a reply
problem: A contextualist says: "In everyday life I know I have hands. In a seminar, when the possibility of being a brain in a vat is raised, the standards for 'know' rise, and then the skeptic speaks truly." Which premise of the argument above does the contextualist reply to, and what does the contextualist grant to the skeptic?
step: The skeptical argument is: P1 (knowing I have hands requires knowing I am not a vat brain), P2 (I do not know that I am not), C.
step: The contextualist accepts that, in the high-standards context, P2 is true and so is the conclusion: in that context, "I know I have hands" is false.
step: But the contextualist denies that this shows that ordinary claims to know are false: in ordinary contexts, the standards are lower, and "I know I have hands" is true.
step: So the contextualist does not reject a premise outright. The reply is that the argument's conclusion is true only in contexts where the standards for "know" are very high.
answer: The reply targets the move from the conclusion to the claim that we lack everyday knowledge; it grants the skeptic the high-standards context.
@mistakes
- Thinking that skeptics claim the external world does not exist: the claim is that we cannot know (or lack justification) that it is as it seems.
- Saying that a Gettier case shows that knowledge needs no justification: it shows that justified true belief is not enough.
- Confusing "justified" with "true": a belief can be justified but false, or true but unjustified.
- Treating a reply to skepticism as a refutation: each reply has costs, and philosophers still debate them.
