@article
Suppose a friend says, "Of course you should tell the truth to a friend, even when it hurts." Another replies, "No: kindness matters more." They could look up a fact to settle a dispute about the height of a mountain. Nothing like that will settle this one. Yet they can still do better or worse: they can give reasons, test each other's reasons, and notice when a view leads somewhere they cannot accept. That activity is the heart of **philosophy**.

The word comes from Greek words meaning "love of wisdom." Today we can describe philosophy as the careful, reasoned study of very general questions: What is knowledge? What is real? What is a person? Are we free? Is there a God? What is good? What do we owe one another? A question is **philosophical** when it is very general or basic, when it cannot be settled just by collecting more observations or running an experiment, and when it can still be investigated by argument.

### The main areas

- **Logic** studies good and bad reasoning.
- **Epistemology** studies knowledge, evidence and justification.
- **Metaphysics** studies what exists and what it is like at the most general level: substance, causation, time, minds, freedom.
- **Philosophy of mind** asks what minds and consciousness are.
- **Philosophy of religion** examines arguments about God and the divine.
- **Ethics** asks what we ought to do and what makes lives and acts good; **value theory** asks what goodness is.
- **Political philosophy** asks about justice, rights and the legitimacy of the state.

Philosophy also overlaps with other subjects. Science, mathematics and law all began with questions that philosophers asked first, and many of their foundations still raise philosophical questions: what counts as a good explanation, what numbers are, what makes a law fair.

### Claims and arguments

A **claim** (or statement) is something that is true or false: "Lying is always wrong." Questions and commands are not claims. An **argument**, in the philosopher's sense, is not a quarrel. It is a set of claims in which some, the **premises**, are offered as reasons to accept another, the **conclusion**. Words that often signal a conclusion are "therefore," "so," "thus" and "hence." Words that often signal a premise are "because," "since" and "given that." Look for the conclusion first: ask, "What is this person trying to convince me of?"

### How philosophers work

Philosophers make a claim, give an argument for it, consider **objections**, and reply. An objection can attack a premise (it is false or unsupported), attack the link between premises and conclusion (the conclusion does not follow), or offer a **counterexample**, a case that fits a general claim's wording but plainly makes it false. A **thought experiment** is an imagined case used to test a view: if you would judge the case one way, what does that show about the view? Thought experiments are not proofs, and people can disagree about how to judge the case, but they make hidden assumptions visible.

Philosophy does not require you to agree with anyone. It requires that you say what you think, why, and what the best reply to you would be. A conclusion defended well is worth more than a conclusion held loudly. Throughout this course you will retell views fairly, set out arguments in **standard form**, assess them, and write about them.
@example
title: Finding the conclusion and the premises
problem: Identify the conclusion and premises: "Our school should not ban phones in class. Teachers can use phones for quick quizzes, and a ban would be hard to enforce. Also, I like my ringtone."
step: Ask what the writer wants us to accept. The first sentence takes a position: "Our school should not ban phones in class." That is the conclusion.
step: The next sentence gives two reasons: phones can be used for quick quizzes, and a ban would be hard to enforce. These are premises.
step: The last sentence is about the writer's ringtone. It says nothing about whether the school should ban phones, so it is not a reason for the conclusion. Leave it out of the standard form.
step: Standard form is shown below.

| P1 | Teachers can use phones for quick quizzes. |
| P2 | A ban on phones in class would be hard to enforce. |
| C | So the school should not ban phones in class. |
answer: The conclusion is the first sentence; two premises support it; the ringtone remark is irrelevant.
@example
title: Is the question philosophical?
problem: Decide whether each question is mainly empirical (settled by observation) or philosophical: (a) How many moons does Mars have? (b) Is a person who gives to charity only to be admired really generous?
step: (a) You can find the number by observing the sky. Facts settle it, so it is an empirical question.
step: (b) Suppose we know exactly what the person did and why. We can still ask what generosity is and whether motives matter. More facts will not decide it; we need to analyze the concept and give reasons. That makes it philosophical.
answer: (a) empirical; (b) philosophical.
@mistakes
- Treating "argument" as meaning an angry dispute: in philosophy it is premises offered as reasons for a conclusion.
- Thinking philosophy has no answers because people disagree: philosophers disagree about many things but agree about much, and arguments can still be better or worse.
- Taking the first sentence of a passage to be the conclusion: conclusions can come first, last or in the middle, so look for what the writer wants you to accept.
- Treating a thought experiment as an experiment that proves something: it tests a view against what we are willing to say about a case.
