@article
Is a good meal good in the same way that a good friend is? We say "good" of knives, grades, people and acts. **Value theory** asks what it is for something to be good, what has value, and whether goodness is a feature of the world or a matter of attitude.

### Kinds of value

Something has **instrumental value** if it is good as a means to something else: money, medicine. Something has **intrinsic value** if it is good in itself, for its own sake. Philosophers also ask what is good **for a person**, their **wellbeing**. Three leading theories:

- **Hedonism**: wellbeing is pleasure and the absence of pain.
- **Desire-satisfaction theories**: wellbeing is getting what you desire.
- **Objective-list theories**: wellbeing consists of certain goods, such as friendship, knowledge and achievement, whether or not you desire them.

Robert Nozick's **experience machine** tests hedonism: would you plug in to a machine that gives you any experiences you like, if you could not tell it was a simulation? Many say no, which suggests that we care about more than experiences; but others reply that this reflects bias in favor of the familiar.

### Facts and values

David Hume noted that writers often move from claims about what *is* to claims about what *ought* to be, without explaining the step (*Treatise*, Book III, Part I). The point is usually called the **is-ought gap**: a conclusion about what we ought to do does not follow validly from premises that are purely descriptive. G. E. Moore's **open question argument** says that for any natural description, such as "pleasant," it remains an open question whether things with that description are good, which suggests that "good" does not mean any natural property.

### Realism and anti-realism

**Moral realism** holds that there are objective moral facts, true whatever anyone thinks. **Anti-realism** denies this; versions include **subjectivism** (moral claims report attitudes), **expressivism** (they express attitudes rather than describe facts) and the **error theory** (moral claims aim at objective facts but none exist). In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 62% accepted or leaned toward moral realism and 26% anti-realism; this share is not evidence about which is right.

### The moral argument for God

Some philosophers link ethics and religion in the **moral argument**:

| P1 | If God does not exist, there are no objective moral values. |
| P2 | There are objective moral values. |
| C | So God exists. |

The argument is valid (it is modus tollens). Objections attack its premises. A **secular moral realist** rejects P1: objective moral facts, like mathematical facts, might hold without God (and the Euthyphro dilemma of Lesson 3 asks whether goodness depends on God at all). An **anti-realist** rejects P2. A theist replies that P1 is plausible because objective values need a ground, such as God's nature, but this reply needs argument to meet the objection that other grounds are possible.
@example
title: Reconstructing and objecting
problem: State the moral argument and an objection, and say which premise the objection targets.
step: The argument is displayed above: P1 (no God, no objective values), P2 (there are objective values), C (God exists).
step: Check validity: if P1 and P2 are true, the conclusion follows by modus tollens. If there were no God, there would be no objective values, but there are, so there is a God.
step: An objection: moral truths may need no divine ground. Many philosophers hold that moral facts can be basic. This objection rejects P1.
step: A reply: P1 holds that objective duties need a source with authority, and only God could fill that role. The objector can respond that this claims too much without showing why moral facts could not be basic.
answer: The argument is valid; the secular realist's objection targets P1.
@example
title: Applying the is-ought gap
problem: Is this argument valid? "Most people enjoy gossip. So gossip is good."
step: The premise is descriptive (an "is"): it says what people enjoy. The conclusion is evaluative (an "ought" or good claim).
step: To make the argument valid, add a bridge premise: "Whatever most people enjoy is good."
step: With the bridge premise, the argument is valid, but the bridge is doubtful: people enjoy many things that are not good.
answer: As stated it is not valid; the missing bridge premise carries all the evaluative weight.
@mistakes
- Treating all value as instrumental: some things (on many views, friendship or happiness) are valued for their own sake.
- Saying the is-ought gap means that facts are irrelevant to ethics: it says that facts alone do not entail evaluative conclusions, not that facts do not matter.
- Confusing moral realism with a claim about what is right: realism is a view about what kind of claims moral claims are.
- Taking the moral argument to show that atheists cannot be moral: it concerns the nature of moral values, not whether non-believers act well.

hint: Say what each theory predicts about the machine.
solution: Hedonism says that wellbeing is pleasure, so a life in the machine, full of pleasure, is as good as any other life with the same pleasure. An objective-list theory says that wellbeing includes goods such as real friendship and achievement, so a machine life lacks something valuable. Many people hesitate to plug in, which favors the objective list. I find the objective list more plausible on this case, but a hedonist can reply that our reluctance reflects fear of the unfamiliar, and that the objective list gives no principled account of why these goods are on the list.
