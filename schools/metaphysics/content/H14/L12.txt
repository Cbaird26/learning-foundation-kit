Why should you obey a government? A state forces people to pay taxes and follow laws. **Political philosophy** asks what, if anything, justifies this: what makes a state **legitimate**, what is **justice**, and what **rights** people have against the state and one another.

### The social contract

Early modern philosophers imagined a **state of nature**, life without government, and asked why people would leave it. In *Leviathan* (1651), Thomas Hobbes argued that without a common power, people would be in a war of all against all, and life would be, in his words in chapter 13, "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." Rational people would therefore agree to give up their liberty to a sovereign who keeps the peace. John Locke, in the *Second Treatise of Government*, held that the state of nature is governed by natural law and that people have natural rights to life, liberty and property; they form government to protect these rights, and a government that violates them can lose its legitimacy. Jean-Jacques Rousseau, in *The Social Contract*, held that legitimate law expresses the general will of the people. These **social contract theories** share the idea that political authority is justified by the (real or hypothetical) **consent** of the governed. A standard objection: most people never actually consented; defenders say hypothetical consent is what matters.

### Rawls: justice as fairness

In *A Theory of Justice* (1971), John Rawls asked what principles we would choose for society from an **original position**, behind a **veil of ignorance**: you do not know your class, talents, gender, race or conception of the good. Not knowing where you will end up, Rawls argued, you would choose two principles: (1) equal basic liberties for all; and (2) social and economic inequalities are allowed only if they are open to all under fair equality of opportunity and work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the **difference principle**). The reasoning is often illustrated by **maximin**: choose the option whose worst outcome is best.

### Nozick: entitlement

Robert Nozick, in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974), replied that justice concerns how holdings arise, not how they are patterned. If holdings were acquired justly and transferred justly, they are just, however unequal. Redistribution by taxation takes what people are entitled to, and Nozick argued that only a minimal state (protecting against force, theft and fraud) is justified. Critics reply that initial acquisition of property is hard to justify and that his view lets large inequalities of opportunity stand.

### Liberty and the harm principle

In *On Liberty* (1859), John Stuart Mill argued that the only purpose for which power can rightly be used against a person against their will is to prevent harm to others; a person's own good is not a sufficient warrant. Critics ask what counts as harm and whether society may protect people from themselves.

### Writing a four-page essay

An argumentative essay of about four pages (roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words) has an **introduction** with a clear thesis; a section that **explains the view** or argument fairly, in standard form; your **argument** for your answer; the **strongest objection**, stated fairly; and your **reply**, followed by a short conclusion. Before drafting, write the thesis in one sentence and outline the argument as premises and a conclusion.
@example
title: Maximin choice
problem: Three social arrangements give the least advantaged, the middle and the most advantaged groups these incomes (thousands per year): Arrangement 1: 10, 40, 90; Arrangement 2: 18, 30, 50; Arrangement 3: 15, 35, 60. Which would a maximin chooser pick, and which arrangement does the difference principle favor?
step: Maximin looks at the worst-off position in each arrangement: 10, 18 and 15.
step: The highest of these is 18, in Arrangement 2.
step: The difference principle also asks that inequalities benefit the least advantaged, so it favors Arrangement 2 among these three.
step: A critic notes that maximin ignores the large gains at the top in Arrangement 1; Rawls's reply is that behind the veil you would not gamble with your basic position.
answer: Arrangement 2.

title: Planning an essay
problem: Plan a four-page essay answering "Is the state justified in taxing people to help the poor?"
step: Thesis: Yes. Taxation to help the poor is justified, because people behind a veil of ignorance would agree to it.
step: Argument: P1, principles of justice are those that free and equal people would choose from a fair starting position; P2, people who do not know their own position would choose to protect the worst-off; C, so taxation to help the poor is justified.
step: Objection: Nozick's: people are entitled to what they justly acquire, and taxation is a form of taking.
step: Reply: Entitlements depend on a just system of property to begin with; taxation is part of the rules that set up property, so it does not take what was fully theirs. Concede that the reply has costs and note them. (A writer who answered "No" would use the same structure, with Nozick's argument as the thesis and Rawls's view as the objection.)
answer: Thesis, argument in standard form, strongest objection, reply.
@mistakes
- Saying that Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau agreed: all used the social contract idea, but reached different conclusions about the state's powers.
- Saying that the original position is a historical event: it is a thought experiment about fair choice.
- Equating Rawls with socialism or Nozick with indifference to the poor: summarize their arguments and avoid labels.
- Writing a four-page essay that only lists views: take a position and defend it, with an objection and a reply.

hint: Say what each says justice is about, then give Nozick's reply.
solution: Rawls holds that just inequalities are those that work to the benefit of the least advantaged, so justice is assessed by the overall pattern of distribution. Nozick replies that justice depends on how holdings came about: if they were acquired and transferred justly, the resulting distribution is just, whatever its pattern. Taxation to improve the position of the worst-off, on his view, takes what people are entitled to and violates their rights. A Rawlsian can answer that the question of how property is justly acquired cannot be separated from the fair terms of cooperation, so patterns matter.
