@article
Right now, you are having experiences: you see these words and perhaps feel a little tired. Meanwhile, your brain is carrying out electrical and chemical processes. How are the two related? That is the **mind-body problem**, and the answers divide into a few families.

### Dualism

**Substance dualism**, defended by Descartes in the *Meditations*, holds that mind and body are two different kinds of substance: the body is extended in space and divisible, the mind is a thinking thing without spatial extension. One of his arguments is that he can clearly conceive of himself existing as a thinking thing without a body, and he concludes that mind and body are really distinct. Critics reply that being able to imagine something does not show that it is possible. A famous objection came from Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, who asked Descartes in letters how a non-physical mind could move a physical body: causing motion seems to require contact, and a mind with no extension cannot touch anything. This is the **interaction problem**. **Property dualism** is a milder view: there is one kind of substance, but mental properties are not reducible to physical ones.

### Physicalism

**Physicalism** holds that everything is physical, so the mind is part of the physical world. Two versions:

- The **identity theory** (defended in the twentieth century by U. T. Place and J. J. C. Smart) says that mental states are brain states: pain is the same thing as a certain neural process, as lightning is an electrical discharge.
- **Functionalism** says that a mental state is defined by what it does: its causal relations to inputs, outputs and other states. Pain is whatever state is caused by injury and causes distress and avoidance. Since the same function could be carried out by different materials, functionalism allows in principle that minds could be built from something other than neurons, though whether any artificial system actually has a mind is a further question that is disputed. Searle's **Chinese room** argues that running a program, which is what the room's occupant does by following rules to manipulate symbols he does not understand, is not enough for understanding; functionalists reply in several ways, and the question of whether a machine could understand remains open.

### Consciousness

Even if a complete physical account explained behavior, there is a further puzzle about experience. Thomas Nagel asked what it is like to be a bat: there seems to be a subjective character to experience that objective description leaves out. David Chalmers calls the problem of explaining why physical processes are accompanied by experience at all the **hard problem of consciousness**.

### The knowledge argument

Frank Jackson imagined **Mary**, a scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has lived in a black-and-white room.

| P1 | Before leaving the room, Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision. |
| P2 | When she first sees a red tomato, Mary learns a new fact: what it is like to see red. |
| C | So there are facts about experience that are not physical facts, and physicalism is false. |

Physicalists reply in different ways, and most of them deny P2: Mary gains an **ability** (to imagine or recognize red), not a new fact; or she comes to know a **familiar physical fact in a new way**. Non-physicalists accept the argument, although Jackson himself later rejected it and became a physicalist. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 52% accepted or leaned toward physicalism and 32% non-physicalism, but this share does not show which side is right.
@example
title: Locating the target of a reply to Mary
problem: A physicalist says: "When Mary leaves the room she acquires a new ability, to recognize and imagine red, but she learns no new fact." Which premise of the knowledge argument is this reply denying?
step: The premises are P1 (Mary knows all the physical facts) and P2 (she learns a new fact, what it is like to see red).
step: The reply grants that something happens to Mary on leaving, but says that what she gains is a skill, not a fact.
step: So the reply denies P2: Mary learns something, but not a new fact. Without P2, the argument gives no reason to think there are non-physical facts.
answer: P2: the reply denies that Mary learns a new fact, while accepting that she gains a new ability.
@example
title: The interaction problem
problem: Explain the interaction problem for substance dualism in standard form.
step: Dualism says the mind is non-physical and the body physical, and also (in Descartes's case) that they interact: decisions cause bodily movements.
step: Objection: causation between things so unlike each other is mysterious.

| P1 | If the mind is a non-physical substance with no location, it is unclear how it could cause changes in a physical body. |
| P2 | Minds and bodies do causally interact: decisions lead to movements, and injuries to pains. |
| C | So dualism owes an account of how a non-physical mind can move a body, and without one it is in difficulty. |
step: Dualists reply in different ways, for example that we do not need to understand how a relation works to know that it holds, or by changing the view (property dualism, or the claim that mind and brain are two aspects of one thing).
answer: The problem is explaining causal interaction between non-physical and physical.
@mistakes
- Saying that physicalism denies that people have experiences or minds: physicalists claim minds are physical, not that they are absent. (Eliminativists go further, but that is a separate view.)
- Confusing substance dualism, which says mind and body are different substances, with property dualism.
- Saying that the Chinese room shows that no machine could ever think: Searle's argument is about whether running a program is sufficient for understanding.
- Treating the knowledge argument as a proof: it relies on a premise about what Mary learns, which physicalists dispute.
