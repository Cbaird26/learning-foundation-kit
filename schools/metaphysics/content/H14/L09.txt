@article
Philosophy of religion does not ask you to believe or disbelieve anything. It asks what arguments can be given for and against belief in God, and how well they work. Philosophers on all sides, believers and non-believers alike, have contributed to it. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey about 19% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward theism and 67% atheism; a share of opinion is not evidence that either view is true. By "God" this lesson means, as in the main Western traditions, a being who is all-powerful (omnipotent), all-knowing (omniscient) and perfectly good.

### The cosmological argument

Arguments of this family start from the existence of the world and reason to a first cause or a necessary being. Aquinas gave several versions in his *Five Ways*. A version from **contingency**, in the style of Leibniz:

| P1 | Every contingent thing, anything that might not have existed, has an explanation of its existence. |
| P2 | The universe, the whole collection of contingent things, is itself contingent. |
| P3 | The explanation of the whole collection of contingent things cannot lie in any of its members; it must lie in something outside the collection, which is therefore not contingent but necessary. |
| C | So there is a necessary being outside the universe that explains why there is a universe. |

Objections: one may reject P1, the principle that everything has an explanation, or say that the universe as a whole might be a brute fact. A critic may say that P2 assumes that what is true of the parts is true of the whole (a **fallacy of composition**): each person has a mother, but the human race does not. Following Hume, others reply to P3 that if each member of the collection is explained by earlier members, nothing more needs explaining. Others question whether a necessary being is coherent.

### The ontological argument

Anselm argued, from the idea of God alone:

| P1 | God is a being than which none greater can be conceived. |
| P2 | A being that exists in reality is greater than one that exists only in the understanding. |
| P3 | If God existed only in the understanding, a greater being could be conceived (one that exists in reality). |
| C | So God exists in reality. |

Gaunilo, a contemporary monk, replied with a parody: the same reasoning would prove that a perfect island exists. Later, Kant argued that existence is not a property that makes a thing greater or lesser. Defenders respond that the argument applies only to a being of maximal greatness, where the island parody does not.

### The design argument

William Paley compared the world to a watch: finding a watch on a heath, we infer a designer from its complex parts working together for a purpose; the world shows similar adaptation, so it has a designer. Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* raises objections: the analogy between the world and a machine is weak, and the same reasoning would not obviously lead to one perfect designer. Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection later gave a natural explanation of adaptation in living things; some theists respond that design operates at the level of the laws of nature themselves.

### The problem of evil

Evil and suffering are, for many, the strongest argument against the existence of God. The **logical problem of evil** (put forcefully by J. L. Mackie) says:

| P1 | If God exists, God is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good. |
| P2 | A being that is omnipotent, omniscient and perfectly good would prevent all evil. |
| P3 | There is evil. |
| C | So God does not exist. |

Responses: the **free will defense** (Alvin Plantinga) denies P2: a world with free creatures who sometimes choose evil may be better than a world without freedom, and God cannot cause free creatures always to choose rightly. **Soul-making theodicy** (John Hick) holds that a world with challenges lets people grow morally. **Skeptical theism** says that we are not in a position to know that no good reason exists. Critics reply that natural evils such as disease are not explained by free will. The **evidential** form (William Rowe) argues that the amount and kind of evil makes theism improbable, even if not impossible.
@example
title: Which premise does the response target?
problem: A theist replies to the problem of evil: "God could have a morally sufficient reason for allowing some evils, such as giving creatures real freedom." Which premise of the argument does this deny?
step: The premises are P1 (God is omnipotent, omniscient and good), P2 (such a being would prevent all evil), P3 (there is evil).
step: The reply grants that evil exists (P3) and the definition of God (P1).
step: It says that a good being might allow some evil for a greater good, so P2 is false.
answer: P2.
@example
title: Gaunilo's parody
problem: Explain how Gaunilo's island parody is meant to refute the ontological argument.
step: Gaunilo imagined an island than which no greater island can be conceived. Following Anselm's form of reasoning: an island that exists is greater than one that exists only in the mind, so the perfect island exists.
step: But it is absurd to claim that this proves such an island exists. So the form of reasoning must be flawed somewhere, if it proves absurd results.
step: Replies: a defender of Anselm says that islands have no maximum of greatness, whereas God, by definition, is the maximally great being, so the reasoning does not carry over.
answer: Parallel reasoning yields an absurd conclusion, so the reasoning is suspect; defenders deny that the parallel is exact.
@mistakes
- Saying that the problem of evil shows that no evil exists or that believers are foolish: it is an argument with premises that can be challenged.
- Confusing arguments for God (what a premise says) with proof: each has defenders and critics, and none commands general agreement.
- Confusing the cosmological argument (from the world's existence) with the ontological argument (from the concept of God alone).
- Saying that Darwin refuted every religious belief: evolution answers a question about the origin of species, and theists and non-theists disagree about what follows.
