@article
Philosophy did not begin in only one place. In many regions, people asked about knowledge, the self, the good life and the right way to live together, and they argued about the answers. Each tradition has many schools that disagree with one another, so this lesson gives samples, not summaries of whole cultures.

### India

Indian philosophy includes schools that accept the authority of the Vedas and schools that do not. The **Nyaya** school developed a theory of knowledge in which there are reliable sources of knowledge (**pramanas**): perception, inference, comparison and testimony. It also studied the structure of good argument. **Buddhist** philosophers, following the Buddha, taught that there is no permanent, unchanging self (**anatta**); what we call a person is a changing stream of experiences and processes, and clinging to a fixed self is a source of suffering. In **Advaita Vedanta**, whose best-known teacher is Shankara, the individual self (**atman**) is ultimately identical with the ultimate reality (**Brahman**), and the world of many separate things is appearance in a sense that the tradition explains in detail.

### China

**Confucius** (Kongzi) stressed becoming a good person through cultivating virtues such as **ren** (humaneness, benevolence) and following **li** (ritual and proper conduct) within relationships like parent and child. **Mozi** argued for impartial concern: we should care for everyone alike, and judge actions by whether they benefit the people. Confucians, by contrast, held that care properly begins close to home and extends outward. **Daoist** writers such as Laozi and Zhuangzi turned to the **Dao** (the way) and recommended acting in harmony with it (**wu wei**, effortless action) instead of forcing things, and Zhuangzi explored how our views depend on our standpoint.

### Africa

African philosophy includes oral traditions and written works. Many Southern African thinkers discuss **ubuntu**, often summarized in the saying that a person is a person through other persons: personhood is realized in community, and moral life centers on relationships. In Akan thought, philosophers such as Kwame Gyekye and Kwasi Wiredu have written about the concept of a person and about community, and about how traditional ideas can be critically examined. Zera Yacob, a seventeenth-century Ethiopian philosopher, is traditionally credited with the *Hatata* (its authorship has been debated, though many scholars accept it), an inquiry which reasons from human reason about God, ethics and the equality of persons.

### The Islamic world

Philosophers writing in Arabic, such as al-Farabi and Ibn Sina (Avicenna), drew on Greek thought and developed it. Ibn Sina imagined a **floating man**, created in an instant, suspended in air with senses blocked: such a person, he argued, would still be aware of himself, which suggests that the self is distinct from the body. Al-Ghazali criticized some philosophers' claims in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, while Ibn Rushd (Averroes) defended philosophy in reply. Their debate shows a lively argument inside the tradition about how reason and revelation relate.

### Comparing traditions

To compare two traditions, choose one **question**, such as "What is a person?" or "How should we live together?"; state each answer in its own terms; then note one real **similarity** and one real **difference**, and say what is at stake between them. Beware of false simplicity: schools within a tradition disagree, and a comparison tells you about two answers, not about two civilizations.
@example
title: Comparing two answers to "What is a self?"
problem: Compare the Buddhist no-self view with Ibn Sina's floating man on the question of what a self is.
step: State each view. The Buddhist view: there is no permanent self; a person is a changing stream of experiences and processes. Ibn Sina's: even a floating person with no sensation would be aware of himself, which suggests that the self is something distinct from the body.
step: Find a similarity. Both start from reflection on what we are aware of in experience, and both ask what the self is when we examine it, instead of assuming the answer.
step: Find a difference. Ibn Sina concludes that the self is a distinct, lasting reality; the Buddhist tradition denies a permanent self and says the sense of one is misleading.
step: Say what is at stake: whether the persistence and unity of a person need an underlying self, or only a continuing process. A good comparison ends by stating the disagreement clearly instead of declaring a winner.
answer: Both examine self-awareness; they disagree about whether it reveals a lasting self or only a process.
@example
title: Impartial care versus graded care
problem: Explain the disagreement between Mozi and the Confucians over how much we should care for others.
step: Mozi: we should have impartial concern for all people and evaluate practices by whether they benefit everyone.
step: The Confucian view, in broad outline: we begin with care for those close to us, such as family, and extend it outward; a good person recognizes special duties within relationships.
step: The disagreement: impartial concern versus special obligations. Mozi's critics said it asked too much and ignored the special ties; his defenders said that partiality causes conflict and harm.
answer: Mozi favors impartial concern; Confucian thinkers stress care graded by relationships.
@mistakes
- Treating a tradition as one voice: Indian, Chinese, African and Islamic philosophy each include many schools that disagree with each other.
- Treating a tradition as only religion or wisdom sayings: each includes arguments, objections and replies.
- Comparing at the level of slogans ("the East is about community and the West about individuals"): compare specific views on one question.
- Assuming that similar-sounding ideas mean the same thing: say what each means in its own context before comparing.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, compare Confucian and Daoist answers to "How should a person act?"
hint: Give each school's central idea and name one difference.
solution: Confucian thinkers answer that a person should cultivate virtues such as ren and follow li, fulfilling the duties of relationships like parent and child, so that a good character shapes society. Daoist writers such as Laozi and Zhuangzi recommend acting in harmony with the Dao through wu wei, effortless action, and doubt that rigid rules and conventions capture the way. Both are concerned with living well and in tune with a larger order. The difference is that Confucians emphasize deliberate cultivation and social roles, while Daoists warn against forcing things and stress spontaneity and harmony with nature.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare two traditions from this lesson on one question (for example, "What is a person?" or "How should we live together?"). State each answer, one real similarity, one real difference, and one sentence on what is at stake.
hint: Choose one question and avoid summarizing whole cultures.
solution: Question: How should we live together? Ubuntu holds that a person is a person through other persons, so moral life centers on community and relationships. Mozi argued that we should have impartial concern for all and judge practices by how they benefit people. Similarity: both treat the good of others as central to morality, not just personal advantage. Difference: ubuntu locates obligation in the ties and relationships that make a person, while Mozi asks for the same concern for everyone regardless of relationship. At stake is whether closeness gives special moral reasons or whether such reasons are a bias to be overcome.
