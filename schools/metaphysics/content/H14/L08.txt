@article
You decide to raise your hand. It feels as if you could have kept it down. But if the universe is governed by laws, and everything that happens follows from earlier events together with those laws, then your choice was settled long before you were born. Can you still be free?

### Determinism and freedom

**Determinism** is the thesis that the state of the world at one time, together with the laws of nature, fixes everything that happens afterward. **Free will** is, roughly, the capacity to act in a way that makes us responsible for what we do. The question is whether the two are **compatible**. **Compatibilists** say yes; **incompatibilists** say no.

Three main positions:

- **Hard determinism** (incompatibilist): determinism is true, so no one has free will.
- **Libertarianism** (incompatibilist; nothing to do with politics here): we have free will, so determinism is false, at least for our free choices.
- **Compatibilism**: freedom is compatible with determinism. Hume, for instance, held that to act freely is to act according to one's own will, without constraint: if you had chosen otherwise, you would have acted otherwise.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 59% accepted or leaned toward compatibilism, 19% libertarianism and 11% no free will; the share who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

### The consequence argument

Peter van Inwagen's **consequence argument** is the best-known case for incompatibilism:

| P1 | If determinism is true, every act of mine is a consequence of the laws of nature and the state of the world before I was born. |
| P2 | It is not up to me what the laws of nature are, or what the state of the world was before I was born. |
| P3 | If something is not up to me, and another thing is a necessary consequence of it, then that consequence is not up to me either. |
| C | So if determinism is true, my acts are not up to me. |

The argument is valid. Compatibilists must reject a premise or the way it is read, often by arguing that "up to me" or "could have done otherwise" has a meaning that fits determinism. Libertarians accept the argument and conclude that determinism is false.

### Frankfurt cases

Another traditional idea is that moral responsibility requires being able to do otherwise: the **principle of alternate possibilities**. Harry Frankfurt described cases meant to refute it. Suppose a neurosurgeon will force you to choose A if you show signs of choosing B, but you freely choose A on your own; the surgeon never acts. You could not have done otherwise, yet you seem responsible. If that is right, responsibility does not require alternatives. Critics argue that the cases fail to show this.

### Writing a short essay

For this lesson's practice, you will write a short essay of about 250 words on the mind-body problem. A good short essay has a **thesis** (your answer), an **argument** for it in two or three steps, the **strongest objection** to your view, and a **reply**. Say what you mean by key terms. Do not just list positions: take one and defend it, and treat the opposing view fairly.
@example
title: Reading the consequence argument
problem: A compatibilist says: "Determinism is true, and I still act freely, because I do what I want without being forced." Which view does this express, and which part of the consequence argument does it contest?
step: The speaker accepts that determinism is true, and also that people act freely. That is compatibilism.
step: The consequence argument concludes that if determinism is true, our acts are not up to us. The speaker disagrees.
step: The compatibilist will say that "up to us" means acting from our own wants without constraint, and that this is fully consistent with the acts having earlier causes. So the compatibilist contests the reading of "not up to me" in the conclusion, and perhaps the way P3 uses it.
answer: Compatibilism; it contests the interpretation of "up to me" the argument depends on.
@example
title: A Frankfurt case in a new setting
problem: Explain how a Frankfurt case challenges the principle of alternate possibilities, using this case: Rin freely decides to return a lost wallet. Unknown to her, a device in her brain would have forced the same decision if she had begun to decide otherwise, but it never switched on.
step: The principle says that a person is responsible only if she could have done otherwise.
step: In the case, Rin could not have done otherwise, since the device would have ensured the same outcome. But the device played no role: she decided on her own, for her own reasons.
step: Many judge that Rin is still responsible and deserves credit. If so, responsibility does not require alternatives. Critics reply that there was an earlier alternative: she could have begun to decide otherwise.
answer: The case aims to show responsibility without alternatives; critics dispute whether the case really removes all alternatives.
@mistakes
- Equating determinism with fatalism: determinism says that our choices have causes, not that outcomes happen whatever we choose.
- Thinking compatibilists deny determinism: they accept that it may be true and say that freedom still exists.
- Thinking libertarians in this debate are about politics: it names a view about free will.
- Saying the consequence argument proves that we lack free will: it proves only that if determinism is true, we lack it (given the premises).

@practice self
prompt: Write an essay of about 250 words on the mind-body problem: state a thesis, give an argument, state the strongest objection and reply.
hint: Choose one position (for example dualism, identity theory or functionalism) and one argument for it.
solution: The mind-body problem asks how conscious mental states relate to physical states of the brain. I defend functionalism. A mental state, on this view, is individuated by its causal role: what causes it, what it causes, and how it connects to other states. My argument: P1, the same kind of mental state, such as pain, can in principle be realized by different physical structures; P2, the identity theory ties each type of mental state to one kind of brain state, so it would rule this out; therefore functional organization, not the stuff, is what matters. This explains how minds can be studied scientifically without a mysterious extra substance. The strongest objection is the knowledge argument: Mary knows all about the function of color vision but seems to learn something when she sees red, so function leaves out experience. I reply that Mary's new learning may be an ability or a familiar fact known in a new way, though I accept that this reply does not fully explain why experience is felt at all. So functionalism is the best available account, with the hard problem still open.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the consequence argument in your own words and give one way a compatibilist might respond.
hint: State the premises, then pick one for the compatibilist to resist.
solution: The consequence argument says: if determinism is true, my acts follow from the laws and the distant past; I have no control over those; and what follows from things I have no control over is also beyond my control; so if determinism is true, my acts are not up to me. A compatibilist can respond that "up to me" does not require that I could have acted against the laws: it requires that my act flows from my own desires and reasoning, free from coercion. On this reading, the argument's step from lacking control over the past and the laws to lacking control over acts is not warranted. A critic would say this changes the subject from the ability to do otherwise to acting on one's desires.
