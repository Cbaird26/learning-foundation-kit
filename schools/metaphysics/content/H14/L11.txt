@article
A runaway cart is heading toward five workers on a track. You can pull a lever to send it to a side track, where it will hit one worker. Should you? Many people say yes; many who say yes hesitate if the way to save the five is to push a stranger in front of the cart. Why does the difference matter? **Normative ethics** gives general theories about what makes acts right or wrong. Philippa Foot and Judith Jarvis Thomson used cases like this one (the "trolley problem") to test such theories. Three families dominate.

### Consequentialism: utilitarianism

**Consequentialism** says that the rightness of an act depends only on its consequences. The best-known version is **utilitarianism**, developed by Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill: the right act is the one that produces the greatest overall happiness (or wellbeing) for everyone affected, counting each person equally. In practice you compare the total welfare each option produces. A famous objection: it seems to allow terrible acts if they add up to more total good, such as punishing an innocent person to calm a mob, and it seems to ignore rights and special obligations. Utilitarians reply by pointing to the long-run harm of such acts or by refining the theory.

### Deontology: Kant

**Deontological** theories hold that some acts are required or forbidden whatever their consequences. Immanuel Kant's version rests on the **categorical imperative**, a command binding all rational beings regardless of their wishes. One formula, the **formula of universal law**: act only on a maxim (a personal rule of action) that you can will to become a universal law. Another, the **formula of humanity**: treat humanity, in yourself and others, always as an end and never merely as a means. A standard application:

| P1 | An act is permissible only if its maxim can consistently be willed as a universal law. |
| P2 | The maxim "I will make a promise I do not intend to keep whenever it gets me money" cannot be consistently willed as a universal law, because if everyone acted on it nobody would trust promises and the practice of promising would collapse. |
| C | So making a false promise to get money is impermissible. |

Objections: rules can conflict, and rigid duties yield odd results, such as the claim that one may never lie even to protect an innocent person; defenders reply that the theory can be refined.

### Virtue ethics

**Virtue ethics**, which goes back to Aristotle, shifts the question from "What should I do?" to "What kind of person should I be?" Aristotle holds that the good life is **eudaimonia** (flourishing), achieved by developing the virtues, such as courage and honesty, which lie between the vices of excess and deficiency: courage between cowardice and recklessness. A virtuous person has practical wisdom, the ability to see what a situation calls for. Objections: it may not give clear guidance in hard cases, and it may be unclear how to say who is virtuous without a prior idea of right action.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 37% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward virtue ethics, 32% deontology and 31% consequentialism; many accept elements of more than one. This is no evidence for any theory. To apply a theory, ask what it says about a case, then check your reaction against it.
@example
title: A utilitarian comparison
problem: A town can fund Plan A or Plan B. Plan A gives 3 units of benefit to each of 12 people and costs 5 units of harm to each of 2 people. Plan B gives 4 units of benefit to each of 8 people and no harm. Which does a utilitarian choose?
step: Plan A: total benefit 12 x 3 = 36; total harm 2 x 5 = 10; net 26.
step: Plan B: total benefit 8 x 4 = 32; no harm; net 32.
step: A utilitarian chooses the plan with the greatest net welfare: Plan B (32 against 26).
step: A critic of utilitarianism might note that this ignores who bears the cost; here Plan B also happens to avoid harming anyone, so the verdict is not in conflict with fairness concerns.
answer: Plan B, with a net of 32.
@example
title: The formula of humanity
problem: Explain why Kant's formula of humanity forbids a manager from tricking an employee into working unpaid overtime by a false promise of a bonus.
step: The formula says to treat people never merely as means but always also as ends.
step: The manager uses the employee's labor and deceives him, getting his agreement by a promise he does not mean to keep, so that the employee cannot share the purpose.
step: A person who is deceived cannot rationally consent to be used this way, so the manager treats the employee merely as a means.
answer: The deception treats the employee merely as a means, which the formula forbids.
@mistakes
- Saying that utilitarianism allows anything: it requires maximizing overall welfare, which is demanding and subject to what the best predictions say.
- Confusing Kant's categorical imperative with the golden rule: the universal-law test asks whether a maxim can be consistently willed as a law, not how you would like to be treated.
- Thinking that virtue ethics has no concern with action: it holds that right actions are those a virtuous person would perform, and that character matters.
- Treating the average of the three theories as a "right answer": each theory has costs, and philosophers continue to argue.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences how a utilitarian and a Kantian would each approach a case of lying to a friend to spare their feelings.
hint: Ask the utilitarian about consequences and the Kantian about the maxim.
solution: A utilitarian compares the total welfare of lying with that of telling the truth: if the lie spares pain and causes little harm, the lie may be right, though one must count effects on trust over time. A Kantian asks whether the maxim "I will lie when the truth would upset someone" can be willed as a universal law; if everyone did so, assurances would lose their point, and lying also treats the friend as a person who is not trusted with the truth. So a Kantian is likely to forbid the lie, while a utilitarian leaves it open. Each faces objections, such as intuitions that a harmless kind lie is fine or that honesty sometimes costs too much.

solution: Case: you promised to help a friend move, but an hour before, a stranger nearby needs help with a minor injury that others could also handle. A utilitarian compares the welfare of keeping the promise with that of helping the stranger; if the others could help, the promise wins on trust grounds, but if no one else could, helping might be better. A virtue ethicist asks what a person of practical wisdom and fidelity would do: likely keep the promise unless the injury is serious, and notice what is at stake. They may agree here, though for different reasons: one counts totals and the other looks to character. I find the virtue verdict more convincing because it explains why we feel the pull of both considerations, but it gives less definite guidance than the utilitarian count.
