@article
Look at a red apple. There seems to be a thing, the apple, and a way it is, red. Another apple is red too. Is the same redness in both? Is the apple more than its color, shape and weight? And is the apple there when no one looks? **Metaphysics** asks such questions about what exists and what it is like at the most general level.

### Substance and properties

A **substance**, in a traditional sense, is an independent thing that has properties: an apple, a person. A **property** is a way a thing is: red, round, heavy. Aristotle took individual things like this horse to be the basic substances. Two views of what a thing is:

- The **substratum view** (associated with Locke's discussion of our idea of substance in general as the idea of an unknown support of qualities; scholars disagree about how far Locke himself endorsed a bare substratum) holds that an object is a bearer, a "something" in which properties inhere, beyond the properties themselves.
- The **bundle theory** says that an object is nothing over and above a collection of properties that occur together. Hume's remarks about the self are the best-known version for minds.

Objection to bundle theory: two things might share all their properties and yet be two. Objection to the substratum view: a bare bearer with no properties of its own seems unknowable and mysterious.

### Universals

Both apples are red, and many things are circular. A **universal** is something that can be shared by many particulars: redness, circularity. The **problem of universals** asks whether there are such things. Three main answers:

- **Realism** about universals holds that there really are universals. **Platonic realism** says they exist apart from particulars (the Forms of Lesson 3); **Aristotelian realism** says they exist only in the particulars that have them.
- **Nominalism** denies that there are universals: only particulars exist, and general words apply to many things without any shared entity.
- **Resemblance nominalism** is a version: two things are red because they resemble one another more than they resemble blue things, with no further shared thing.

The realist's argument is the **one over many**: the best explanation of how many things can be red is that something is common to them. The nominalist replies that postulating universals is extravagant and raises its own puzzles: where is redness, and how can the same thing be wholly in many places?

### Appearance and reality

George Berkeley (1685–1753) defended **idealism**: the view that what exists are minds and their ideas. His slogan is that to be is to be perceived (or to perceive). Berkeley argued that we only ever experience our own ideas, and that the notion of a material thing existing apart from any mind is empty. This might seem to open the door to skepticism, but Berkeley claimed the reverse: if ordinary objects just are collections of ideas, there is no gap between how things seem and how they are, so there is nothing for the skeptic to doubt. Critics ask what makes the world continue when no human perceives it; Berkeley answered by appealing to God's perception. Realists about matter say the idealist's reply gives up too much. Which metaphysics is true is disputed; for universals in the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, nominalism (about 42%) and Platonism (about 38%) were close, and the share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.
@example
title: Using the one-over-many argument
problem: Set out the realist's one-over-many argument for the property "being square" in standard form and say how a nominalist could reply.
step: Premises: many different things (tiles, windows, boards) are square. There needs to be an explanation of what they all have in common.
step: Realist's reasoning: the best explanation is that they all share a universal, squareness.

| P1 | Many different things are square. |
| P2 | The best explanation of how many different things can be square is that they share one universal, squareness. |
| C | So there is a universal, squareness. |
step: A nominalist can reply that P2 is false: the squares need only resemble one another, or fall under the same general word, without any shared entity. It adds that universals raise puzzles of their own, such as how one thing can be fully present in many places.
answer: The argument is an inference to the best explanation; the nominalist denies that universals are the best explanation.
@example
title: Evaluating the idealist reply to skepticism
problem: Evaluate Berkeley's idealism as a reply to skepticism about the external world.
step: The skeptic worries that our experiences might not match a mind-independent world. Berkeley's reply: ordinary objects are collections of ideas, so there is no mind-independent world to be mistaken about.
step: Strength: it removes the gap between appearance and reality that the skeptical argument needs.
step: Cost: it changes what we mean by "object", and to explain why objects persist unperceived it appeals to God's perception, which many find a high price.
step: A fair evaluation says what the reply solves and what it pays, instead of calling it simply right or wrong.
answer: It removes the gap the skeptic exploits, but at the cost of a revisionary account of objects.
@mistakes
- Saying that nominalists deny that things are red: they deny that there is a universal redness, not that red things exist.
- Confusing a universal with a particular: a particular apple is one thing in one place, a universal can be shared.
- Reading Berkeley as saying that the table is an illusion: he held that the table is real, but consists of ideas.
- Confusing idealism in metaphysics (reality is mental) with idealism as optimism or high ideals.

solution: Many things are blue: a flag, a sky, a book. The realist says that they share something, the universal blueness, which explains how they can all be blue. The nominalist denies that there are universals; the things are simply blue, or they resemble each other, and the word "blue" applies to them without any shared entity. The realist points to the success of the explanation, and the nominalist replies that universals are extra entities with puzzling features, such as being wholly present in many places at once.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate one reply to skepticism about the external world (the Moorean reply, contextualism, externalism or Berkeley's idealism): say what the reply claims, what it solves, and what its main cost is.
hint: Choose one reply and say which premise or assumption of the skeptical argument it addresses.
solution: Take externalism. It claims that knowledge depends partly on facts outside the knower's mind: if my beliefs are produced by reliable processes, I know, even if I cannot rule out from the inside that I am deceived. This solves the problem by denying that I must prove I am not in a vat before I know I have hands. Its main cost is that it seems to leave unanswered what the skeptic asks, namely how I can tell that my processes are reliable; and the resulting kind of knowledge may not satisfy the wish for good reasons I can offer. So externalism changes what knowing requires instead of showing that the skeptic's scenario is false.
