## Week by week

1. **What philosophy is.** Philosophy studies very general questions by argument. Know the branches, and claim, premise, conclusion, objection, counterexample and thought experiment.
2. **Logic.** Valid means it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false; sound means valid with true premises; strong and cogent are the inductive counterparts. Modus ponens and modus tollens are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are not. Reconstruct arguments in standard form, adding implicit premises fairly.
3. **Greece.** The elenchus, the Euthyphro dilemma, Plato's Forms and the cave, Aristotle's substance, form and syllogism.
4. **World traditions.** Nyaya pramanas, Buddhist no-self, Advaita, Confucius and Mozi, Daoism, ubuntu, Zera Yacob, Ibn Sina's floating man, al-Ghazali and Ibn Rushd. Compare two traditions on one question.
5. **Epistemology.** Justified true belief, Gettier cases, the brain-in-a-vat argument and the Moorean, contextualist and externalist replies.
6. **Metaphysics.** Substratum and bundle theories, realism and nominalism about universals, Berkeley's idealism.
7. **Mind.** Dualism and the interaction problem, identity theory, functionalism, the Chinese room, the knowledge argument, the hard problem.
8. **Free will.** Determinism, compatibilism, libertarianism, hard determinism, the consequence argument, Frankfurt cases.
9. **Religion.** Cosmological, ontological and design arguments; the problem of evil and the free will defense.
10. **Value.** Intrinsic and instrumental value, theories of wellbeing, the is-ought gap, realism and anti-realism, the moral argument.
11. **Ethics.** Utilitarianism, Kant's universal law and humanity formulas, virtue ethics.
12. **Politics.** Social contract, Rawls, Nozick, Mill's harm principle.

## Key distinctions

- Valid versus sound; deductive versus inductive.
- A view versus an objection to it; which premise an objection attacks.
- Compatibilism versus determinism; substance dualism versus property dualism; realism versus nominalism.

## Self-check

- Can you set out three arguments from the course in standard form?
- Can you name the premise a given reply rejects?
- Can you retell a view so that its defender would accept your account?
- Can you state the strongest objection to your own thesis?

## Common errors

Calling a valid argument sound; treating a view's popularity as evidence for it; presenting a contested view as settled; attributing to a philosopher a claim you are not sure they made; replying to a weaker version of the opposing view.
@exam mc
prompt: "If objective moral values exist, then God exists. Objective moral values exist. So God exists." Which statement about this argument is correct?
choice*: It is valid, because it has the form of modus ponens.
choice: It is invalid, because its conclusion is controversial.
choice: It is sound, because it is valid.
choice: It commits the fallacy of affirming the consequent.
hint: Identify the form first; then recall that soundness also needs true premises.
solution: The argument has the form "If P then Q; P; so Q," which is modus ponens and is valid. Validity does not depend on the conclusion's being uncontroversial, and soundness would additionally require that the premises be true, which is disputed.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of about 400 words in which you take a position on one question from the course (free will, the mind-body problem, God, skepticism, ethics or justice); you are asked to argue, not to reveal your own beliefs, and you may defend a position for the sake of argument. Reconstruct one argument that opposes your position in standard form, state the premise you reject and why, and consider the strongest reply to you.
hint: Choose your position first. Then find the best argument against it and write that argument in standard form.
solution: Question: Do we have free will? Position: compatibilism. Opposing argument (the consequence argument): P1: If determinism is true, every act of mine is a consequence of the laws of nature and the state of the world before I was born. P2: It is not up to me what the laws are or what the world was like before I was born. P3: If something is not up to me, then what is a necessary consequence of it is not up to me either. C: So if determinism is true, my acts are not up to me. The argument is valid, so I must reject a premise or a reading of its terms. I reject the reading of "up to me" that P3 relies on: acts are up to me when they flow from my own desires and reasoning without coercion or manipulation, and this can be true although those desires have causes. If I had wanted to act otherwise, I would have; that is the sense of freedom that matters for responsibility. The strongest reply is that I have changed the subject: the original notion of freedom required that I could have done otherwise, given the same past and laws, and my reading drops that. I answer that the notion of freedom we need for praise and blame is the ability to respond to reasons, and that Frankfurt cases suggest that the demand for alternatives under identical conditions is not needed for responsibility, though I acknowledge that the dispute depends on how we understand "could have done otherwise."
rubric: Takes a clear position and states the opposing argument accurately in valid standard form.
rubric: Identifies a specific premise or reading that is rejected and gives a reason.
rubric: States the strongest reply fairly and answers it or acknowledges its force.
rubric: Does not treat a contested claim as settled or confuse validity with soundness.
