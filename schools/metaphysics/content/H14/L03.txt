@article
Before philosophy had its name, Greek thinkers around the sixth century BCE asked what the world is made of and tried to answer without appealing only to myths. Thales, according to Aristotle's report, said that everything is water; later thinkers proposed air, fire, atoms and more. What matters is the style: give an account, and expect others to criticize it.

### Socrates and the elenchus

Socrates (469–399 BCE) wrote nothing; we know him mainly through Plato's dialogues, so scholars distinguish what Plato's Socrates says from what the historical Socrates thought. In the early dialogues Socrates asks people what virtues such as courage, piety and justice are, and tests each answer with questions. This is the **elenchus**, or Socratic method. A speaker offers a definition; Socrates gets agreement to other claims; and these turn out to contradict the definition. The speaker is left puzzled, aware of not knowing what he thought he knew. In the *Apology*, Socrates says that he is wiser only in that he does not think he knows what he does not know.

### The Euthyphro dilemma

In Plato's *Euthyphro*, Socrates asks whether what is pious is loved by the gods because it is pious, or is pious because the gods love it. Set out as a dilemma, in the form usually applied to the relation between gods and goodness:

| P1 | Either the gods love the pious because it is pious, or it is pious because the gods love it. |
| P2 | If the gods love the pious because it is pious, then piety is independent of the gods' love, and their love does not explain what piety is. |
| P3 | If it is pious because the gods love it, then piety depends on the gods' love. |
| C | So either the gods' love does not explain what piety is, or piety depends on that love. |

The dilemma is a challenge for any view that explains goodness by appeal to divine will, and philosophers have given replies to each horn; the dialogue itself ends without agreed answer.

### Plato's Forms

Plato (c. 428–348 BCE) asked what makes many things be the same kind of thing. Many acts are just, many things are beautiful. His answer: there is a single **Form** (or Idea) of Justice and of Beauty, an unchanging, non-physical standard that the many things share in. This is the **one over many** argument. In the *Republic* (Book VII) the image of the cave describes people chained in a cave who take shadows for reality; the philosopher is the one who turns toward the light and learns of the Forms, the highest being the Form of the Good. Plato held that knowledge concerns the Forms, while the changing physical world offers only opinion.

### Aristotle

Aristotle (384–322 BCE), who studied in Plato's Academy, agreed that there are real kinds but rejected Forms existing apart from things. For him, universals exist in the things that have them: the **form** of a horse is in each horse, and primary reality is the individual **substance**, a particular horse or person, made of **matter** and **form**. He also founded formal logic with the **syllogism**, an argument with two premises and a conclusion in which each of three terms appears twice. In ethics he held that a good life is a life of virtue, exercised through reason (Lesson 11 returns to this).

| P1 | All humans are mortal. |
| P2 | All Greeks are human. |
| C | So all Greeks are mortal. |

This is valid. The pattern "All A are B; all C are A; so all C are B" is valid whatever the terms are.
@example
title: Testing a syllogism
problem: Is this argument valid? "All violinists are musicians. Some musicians are teachers. So some violinists are teachers."
step: Let the terms be violinists, musicians and teachers. The premises say that the violinists are inside the group of musicians, and that the musicians and teachers overlap.
step: Find a possible case in which both premises are true and the conclusion false. Suppose all violinists are musicians and some musicians are teachers, but only the pianists among the musicians are teachers.
step: Then no violinist is a teacher. The premises are true and the conclusion false, so the argument is invalid. A true middle term does not carry a "some" claim down to a subgroup.
answer: Invalid; a case where the teachers among the musicians are all pianists is a counterexample.
@example
title: The one over many in a new case
problem: Use Plato's one-over-many reasoning to explain why someone might believe in a Form of Triangle, and say how Aristotle would reply.
step: Many drawn triangles differ in size and ink, and none is perfectly straight-sided, yet we call them all triangles. Plato asks what they have in common that makes each a triangle.
step: His answer: the Form of Triangle, a perfect, unchanging pattern that each drawing imitates or shares in, and which geometry is really about.
step: Aristotle replies that the common nature is in the triangular things themselves: the form of triangle is found in each triangle, and does not exist in a separate realm. We can think about triangles in general without a separate world of triangles.
answer: Plato posits a separate Form explaining what the many triangles share; Aristotle holds that the shared form exists in the particulars.
@mistakes
- Attributing writings to Socrates: he wrote nothing, and we know his views mainly through Plato (and others), so say "Socrates in Plato's dialogues."
- Reading the Euthyphro dilemma as showing that the gods do not exist: it is a question about the relation between goodness or piety and divine will, not about whether there are gods.
- Saying that Plato thought the physical world is unreal nonsense: he held that it is a changing copy of the Forms and so a poor object of knowledge, not that it is nothing.
- Saying that Aristotle rejected universals altogether: he held that they exist, but only in the things that have them.

@quiz self
prompt: State the Euthyphro dilemma for goodness (replacing "pious" with "good") and give one reply a believer in a divine-command view might make to one horn.
hint: Begin with the two options: God commands what is good because it is good, or it is good because God commands it.
solution: The dilemma: either God commands what is good because it is good, or it is good because God commands it. On the first horn, goodness is independent of God's commands and God's will does not explain what goodness is. On the second, goodness depends on God's will, and it can seem arbitrary. One reply to the second horn: God's commands are not arbitrary, since God's will flows from God's own good nature, so what is good is fixed by what God is, not by a whim. A critic can ask whether this just moves the question to why God's nature is good.
