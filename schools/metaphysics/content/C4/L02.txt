@article
Everyone knows how to use words like "yesterday" and "tomorrow", and everyone can tell a long wait from a short one. Yet when Augustine asks in *Confessions* XI what time is, he finds that he cannot say. His puzzle is among the most influential in the metaphysics of time, and his treatment of evil and free choice in other works is among the most influential in the philosophy of religion. This lesson takes both.

### The puzzle about time

Augustine begins with a simple observation: time is made of past, present and future. Then the trouble begins.

| P1 | Time consists of the past, the present and the future. |
| P2 | The past no longer exists, and the future does not yet exist. |
| P3 | The present is time only if it passes into the past; a present that always stayed would be eternity, not time. |
| P4 | Any stretch of time that is truly present has no parts that are past or future, and so no duration: whatever has duration has an earlier and a later part, one of which is not present. |
| C | So time seems to exist only by tending not to exist: its only real part is an instantaneous present that has no length. |

The argument leads to a second puzzle, about measurement. We say that one interval is longer than another. But what is measured? The past is gone, the future is not yet, and the present has no length. Yet we do measure and compare times, so there must be something that we measure.

### The reply: the stretching of the mind

Augustine's answer relocates time into the mind. He speaks of three "presents": a present of past things, which is **memory**; a present of present things, which is **attention**; and a present of future things, which is **expectation**. A long future is not a long future that exists, but a long expectation of it; a long past is a long memory. When we measure a syllable, we measure the impression that stays in the mind as the syllable passes. Time is therefore a kind of **distentio animi**, a stretching out of the soul, as it holds together what it remembers, attends to and expects.

There are at least two readings. On a **psychological** reading, Augustine holds that time is mind-dependent. On a more modest reading, he gives an account of how we *experience and measure* time without denying that events are really past or future. Interpreters disagree about which fits his text, and you should report the disagreement rather than choose silently. A further point is that the account has not removed the original worry: the mind's "present" of attention still seems to need extension, which returns the puzzle. Later philosophers have compared this with discussions of how experience of the present can have some duration.

### Evil as privation

Augustine had been attracted to the Manichees, who held that evil is a rival substance opposed to God. He replies as follows (*Confessions* VII; *Enchiridion*).

| P1 | Everything that exists as a substance was created by God, who is wholly good, so every substance is good. |
| P2 | If evil were a substance, it would be good. |
| P3 | Evil is not good. |
| C1 | So evil is not a substance. |
| P4 | What we call evil is corruption, the lack of a good that a thing ought to have. |
| C2 | So evil is a **privation** (a lack) of good in something that is good. |

Blindness is the lack of sight in a being that should see, and it is not a thing of its own. Corruption depends on something good to corrupt: if the good were wholly removed, nothing would remain to be corrupted. A common objection is that pain and cruelty seem to be positive, not mere absences; a defender replies that what is bad about pain is the damage or disorder that it signals or that cruelty expresses, which is the lack of due order.

### Free choice

If God made everything good, where does moral evil come from? In *On Free Choice of the Will* Augustine answers that it arises from the misuse of the **will**. God gave free choice because it is a good, since without it no one could act rightly; but it is a good that can be used badly. The wrongdoer turns from a higher good to a lower one. This is the root of the **free will defense**: God is not the author of sin, because sin comes from the creature's choice. Augustine's later writings give a larger role to divine grace, and the exact relation between his earlier and later views on freedom is disputed among scholars.

### Evaluating the reply to the problem of evil

The privation account does not by itself solve the problem of evil, since a lack that God allows still needs explaining, and the free will defense is open to the reply that God could have created beings who freely choose the good. Notice that these are objections to Augustine's use of the arguments, not to their validity.

@example
title: Reading the argument that evil is not a substance
problem: A student writes: "Augustine says that if evil were a substance it would be good, and since evil is not good, evil is not a substance." Identify the hidden step that explains why a substance would be good, and say what the argument has shown and what it has not.
step: The conclusion is that evil is not a substance. The student's reasoning requires that any substance would be good. Augustine's reason is that every substance is created by God, who is wholly good, so that every substance, as such, is good.
step: With that premise added, the argument is valid: substance implies good; evil is not good; so evil is not a substance (modus tollens).
step: It has shown that evil is not a *thing*; it has not shown what evil is. For that Augustine adds that evil is a privation, a lack of a good that should be present. Nor has it shown that God had no reason to permit evil.
answer: The hidden premise is that every created substance is good. The argument rules out evil as a rival substance but leaves open the further questions of what evil is and why God permits it.

@example
title: Explaining the puzzle about time
problem: Explain in a few steps why the present seems to be a problem for the existence of time.
step: Past and future do not exist: the past is no longer and the future is not yet. If time exists, it must be because of the present.
step: But a stretch of time with parts, such as an hour, has earlier and later parts, so most of it is past or future. Shrink it down to a day, an hour, a minute, and the same is true. A present with no past or future part has no length at all.
step: Moreover, if the present stayed and never passed into the past, it would not be time at all, but eternity. So the present is time only by becoming past, that is, by ceasing to be.
answer: The only part of time that exists is the present, but the present has no duration and counts as time only by passing away. So time exists only by tending not to be.

@mistakes
- Treating Augustine's remark that he knows what time is until he is asked to explain it as the claim that time is unreal: the remark expresses the puzzle, and he goes on to give a positive account.
- Saying that he proves evil does not exist: the privation view says evil is a lack and not a substance, not that evil is an illusion.
- Confusing the free will defense with a claim that God does not know what the creature will do: that is a different issue, which the next lesson takes up.
- Presenting the psychological reading of time as the only reading: interpreters disagree.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, explain what the second puzzle about time (measurement) adds to the first (existence), and say whether Augustine's reply, that time is measured in the mind, succeeds in removing the problem.
hint: The first puzzle is about whether time exists; the second is about what we compare when we say that one time is longer.
solution: The first puzzle suggests that time hardly exists, since the past and future do not exist and the present has no duration. The second asks how, given that, we can measure time at all, for there seems to be nothing to measure. Augustine replies that we measure an impression in the mind: memory holds what has passed, attention what is present, expectation what is to come, and the mind's stretching among them is what has length. This succeeds in explaining how we compare lengths without a real past. It does not clearly remove the first puzzle, since the mind's present of attention seems to need some duration, and it leaves open whether time is mind-dependent or only our measurement of it. A defender can say that attention spans a short stretch of experience, not an instant, so the problem is reduced and not removed.
