@article
Most of Aristotle's works and many Neoplatonic texts were translated into Arabic between the eighth and tenth centuries, and philosophers writing in Arabic, among them Muslims, Christians and Jews, turned them into new systems. Their metaphysics reached the Latin West in translation in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries and shaped it profoundly. This lesson looks at two figures: al-Farabi (tenth century), who built an Aristotelian and Neoplatonic system around a First Cause and a descending series of intellects, and Avicenna (Ibn Sina, died 1037), who made the most influential distinction in medieval metaphysics.

### Al-Farabi: a first being and the order of the world

Al-Farabi, known as a logician and as a commentator on Aristotle, presents the world as an ordered emanation from a **First Cause**, which he describes as a necessarily existent being, wholly simple and perfect. From it proceed a series of intellects and, with them, the heavenly spheres, down to the active intellect that governs the sublunary world. His picture owes much to Neoplatonism. Scholars also credit him with early use of the idea that existence is something over and above what a thing is, but whether he made the full distinction is a matter of interpretation; the full and explicit development is Avicenna's.

### Avicenna: necessary and possible

Avicenna begins from the most basic notions: *existent*, *thing* and *necessary*, which, he says, are grasped immediately. Considering any being, we can ask whether its essence by itself requires that it exist.

| Status | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Necessary in itself** | Its essence requires existence: it cannot fail to exist. |
| **Possible in itself** | Its essence neither requires nor excludes existence. |
| **Impossible in itself** | Its essence excludes existence (a square circle). |

A possible thing that exists is **necessary through another**: given its cause, it must exist. Avicenna argues that what is possible in itself needs something outside it to tip the balance toward existence, and this yields the following argument (a reconstruction of his proof, sometimes called the proof of the truthful).

| P1 | Something exists, and every existent is either necessary in itself or possible in itself. |
| P2 | Whatever is possible in itself has existence only through a cause outside itself, since its essence does not settle whether it exists. |
| P3 | If there are existents that are possible in itself, then the whole series of them and their causes, even if infinite, is itself possible in itself, and so requires a cause outside the series. |
| P4 | That cause cannot be possible in itself, or it would belong to the series; so, by P1, it is necessary in itself. |
| C | So there is a being that is necessary in itself (if no existent is possible in itself, then by P1 every existent is already necessary in itself). |

Avicenna goes on to argue that this necessary existent is unique, simple and without a cause, and that it has no essence distinct from its existence. The argument does not rest on the impossibility of a temporal beginning: it is compatible with an eternal world that depends at every moment on a necessary cause. The most contested step is premise P3, since it treats a whole as having the modal status of its members, and critics ask whether that holds.

### Essence and existence

A thing's **essence** (or quiddity) is what it is: the answer to "what is it?". Avicenna argues as follows for the claim that, in things other than the necessary existent, existence is not part of the essence.

| P1 | We can grasp what a thing is, for example what a phoenix or a triangle is, without thereby knowing whether any such thing exists. |
| P2 | If existence were part of the essence, grasping the essence would include grasping that the thing exists. |
| C | So, for such things, existence is not part of the essence; it is added to the essence, from outside, by a cause. |

This is an argument from what can be understood, and a critic can say that it only shows that the *concept* of existence is not contained in our concept of a phoenix, and not that existence is really distinct from the essence in the thing. Avicenna also has a modal route to the same conclusion: if existence belonged to the essence of a possible thing, that thing would be necessary in itself and would need no cause. How far Avicenna means the distinction to be real and not conceptual is debated, and later thinkers disagreed. Averroes criticized Avicenna's treatment of existence as an attribute added to essence, and Aquinas later adopted a distinction between essence and the act of being. In God, Avicenna holds, essence and existence are one: this is why God is necessary in itself.

### The essence considered in itself

Avicenna also holds that an essence taken just in itself, for example **horseness**, is merely horseness: it is neither one nor many, neither universal nor particular. It exists as particular in individual horses and as universal in the mind that thinks it, but in itself it is neither. This connects with Boethius's solution (last lesson), since it gives a more explicit account of how one nature can be particular in things and universal in thought; the medieval slogan is that "horseness is only horseness".

### Evaluation

The distinction between essence and existence is useful because it explains how a thing can be understood without being known to exist, and it supports the argument for a necessary cause. The chief objections are that existence is not a feature that a thing can lack in the way that it can lack a property, and that the argument from conceivability proves less than needed.

@example
title: Classifying beings by Avicenna's modal status
problem: Classify each as necessary in itself, possible in itself or impossible in itself, according to Avicenna's scheme: (a) a round square; (b) a particular horse; (c) the First Cause, whose essence is its existence.
step: Ask whether the essence requires, permits or excludes existence.
step: (a) A round square has an essence that excludes existence: it is **impossible in itself**.
step: (b) The essence of a horse neither requires nor excludes existence: a horse might or might not exist. It is **possible in itself**, and, given its causes, necessary through another.
step: (c) The First Cause has an essence that is identical with its existence, so it cannot fail to exist: **necessary in itself**.
answer: (a) impossible in itself; (b) possible in itself (and necessary through another); (c) necessary in itself.

@example
title: Testing the argument that existence is not part of essence
problem: A critic replies: "You can grasp what a triangle is without knowing whether any triangle exists, but that only shows we lack information, not that existence is distinct from triangularity." Which premise does this question, and how might Avicenna reply?
step: The critic accepts that we can grasp the essence without knowing existence (premise P1), and says this shows a limit on our knowledge only. This targets the move from P1 to the conclusion, resting on P2.
step: Avicenna's reply is that if existence belonged to the essence, then understanding the essence would include understanding that it exists, as understanding a triangle includes understanding that it has three sides. Since we do not find existence among the contents of the concept, it is not part of the essence.
step: The critic can respond that this proves only that the concept differs, not the thing. The dispute is over whether a conceptual distinction reflects a real one.
answer: The criticism targets the move from a conceptual distinction to a real one (premise P2's use). Avicenna replies that essence includes whatever is needed to understand what the thing is.

@mistakes
- Saying that Avicenna's proof depends on the world having a beginning: it is compatible with an eternal world that depends on a necessary being at every moment.
- Treating "possible in itself" as "possibly not existing in fact": it means that the essence alone does not settle existence, and the thing may exist necessarily through its cause.
- Reading the essence-existence distinction as the claim that things could have existence without essence: Avicenna holds that every existing thing has an essence, and that in God the two are identical.
- Attributing the full distinction unqualifiedly to al-Farabi: scholars disagree on how far he held it.
