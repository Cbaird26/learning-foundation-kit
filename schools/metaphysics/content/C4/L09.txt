@article
What does it take for a horse to be, as opposed to what it takes for something to be a horse? And what, in a living human being, accounts for the fact that a body is alive and thinks? Aquinas answers both questions with a few central ideas drawn from Aristotle and from Avicenna: that there is a real composition of essence and being in every creature, and a composition of matter and form in every material thing.

### Essence and the act of being

An **essence** is what a thing is, its nature: what the definition of "human being" says. **Esse** is the act of being, the fact that the thing is. In *On Being and Essence* (chapter 4) Aquinas argues as follows, in a reconstruction of the argument that follows Avicenna's.

| P1 | Whatever is not part of the concept of an essence comes to that essence from outside it. |
| P2 | We can understand the essence of a thing, such as a human being or a phoenix, without knowing that any such thing exists. |
| C1 | So existence is not part of the essence of such things; it is something other than the essence. |
| P3 | What has existence that is not identical with its essence has that existence from another cause. |
| P4 | A chain of such causes cannot go on without end. |
| C2 | So there is a first being in which essence and existence are identical, which causes the existence of everything else. |

For Aquinas, esse is not another kind of property in the thing but its actuality: in creatures the essence is related to esse as potency is to act. In God alone, essence and esse are identical (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 3, a. 4), which is why God is called "subsistent being itself" (*ipsum esse subsistens*). Whether the distinction is "real" (a distinction in things) or conceptual was debated by Aquinas's successors, and the interpretation of Aquinas himself is still disputed. A critic can say that the argument only shows a difference in what we can understand; a defender replies that what is added by a cause must differ from what it is added to.

### Hylomorphism

Following Aristotle, Aquinas holds that every material substance is a composite of **matter** and **form**. Form is the principle that makes the substance the kind of thing it is; matter is what receives the form and is potentially other things. A bronze statue is bronze (matter) shaped as a horse (form). A natural substance has one **substantial form**, which makes it the kind of thing it is, and not several. A human being is a composite of body and soul, where the **soul** is the substantial form of a living body. This differs from the view often associated with Plato, and with Augustine on some readings, on which the soul is a complete substance that uses the body, and it differs from Descartes's later view that mind and body are really distinct substances. The thesis is called **hylomorphism**, from the Greek words for matter and form.

### The human soul

Aquinas argues that the human soul has an operation, understanding, that does not use any bodily organ, and from this he argues that it can exist on its own. The argument is a reconstruction of *Summa Theologiae* I, q. 75, a. 2.

| P1 | The intellect can know the natures of all bodies. |
| P2 | Whatever has a nature of its own that is bodily would be hindered from knowing other bodily natures, as an eye that was itself colored would not see all colors. |
| C1 | So the intellect has no bodily nature of its own, and its operation does not use a bodily organ. |
| P3 | A thing operates in the way that it exists: what can operate through itself can subsist through itself. |
| C2 | So the intellectual soul is subsistent: it can exist apart from the body. |

This does not make the soul the whole person: for Aquinas the human being is the composite, and the separated soul is incomplete, which is one reason he gives for the resurrection of the body. The argument is controversial. Critics, ancient and modern, point out that intellectual activity depends on the brain, and that P2 and P3 are not obvious; Aquinas's own reply is that the intellect depends on the body for its objects, since it works with images from the senses, but not as an organ. Whether the mind is a physical thing is a question on which philosophers still disagree, and the argument should be assessed on its premises.

### Individuation in Aquinas

How is this horse a different one from that horse, if they share an essence? Aquinas's answer is that the principle of individuation is **matter marked out by quantity** (*materia signata quantitate*): matter, taken as having definite dimensions, is what divides one form into many individuals. Next week's lesson sets this beside Scotus's rival answer.

@example
title: Applying hylomorphism
problem: Identify the matter and the form in each: (a) a bronze statue of a horse; (b) a living cat, according to Aquinas.
step: In (a) the matter is the bronze, which can receive other shapes; the form is the shape of a horse.
step: In (b) the substantial form is the cat's soul, the principle that makes the body a living cat; the matter is the body in potency to being a cat.
step: The difference is that the statue's form is an accidental arrangement of bronze, while the cat's soul is a substantial form: the cat is one thing, not a heap of parts that is organized.
answer: (a) matter: bronze; form: the shape. (b) matter: the body; form: the soul as substantial form.

@example
title: Reading the argument that the soul subsists
problem: A student objects: "A damaged brain impairs thought, so the intellect uses a bodily organ and premise C1 is false." What would Aquinas reply, and what does the reply give up?
step: The objection targets C1. Aquinas accepts that thought depends on the body in one way: the intellect needs images supplied by the senses and imagination.
step: His reply is that depending on the body for its objects is not the same as using a bodily organ to understand. Damage to the brain disturbs the supply of images.
step: The reply fits Aquinas's account but takes on the task of showing that understanding itself, as distinct from imagining, has no organ; critics say this is what is in dispute.
answer: He distinguishes dependence on the body for objects from using a bodily organ, and the burden remains to show that understanding has no organ.

@mistakes
- Saying that for Aquinas the soul is the whole person: it is the form of the body, and the person is the composite.
- Treating esse as a property alongside others such as color: Aquinas takes it to be the act by which an essence exists.
- Saying that Aquinas holds Cartesian dualism: for him the soul is the form of the body, and he rejects the view that the soul is a complete substance.
- Presenting the "real distinction" reading as settled: scholars disagree about how Aquinas and his successors understood the distinction.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, explain how Aquinas can say both that the soul is the form of the body and that the intellectual soul subsists, and state one difficulty.
hint: Distinguish the soul's relation to the body from its operation of understanding.
solution: Aquinas holds that the soul is the substantial form of the living body, so the human being is one substance, a composite. But he also argues that understanding is an operation with no bodily organ, and that what operates through itself can subsist, so the intellectual soul can exist apart from the body after death, although it is then incomplete and the person is not fully present until the resurrection. The two claims fit together because being the form of the body does not require that every operation of the soul uses a bodily organ. The main difficulty is the premise that understanding uses no organ, which critics deny on the basis of its dependence on the brain; Aquinas replies that the dependence concerns images and not the act of understanding.
