@project
title: A medieval disputation
overview: Write a disputed question (quaestio disputata) in the medieval scholastic style on one of three topics: universals, essence and existence, or divine foreknowledge and human freedom. Your question states the problem, gives three objections, a sed contra, your answer (the respondeo) with arguments from at least two medieval thinkers, and a reply to each objection. The finished piece is about 2,000 words.
goal: Use the scholastic form to make arguments precise: each objection is an argument in standard form, and each reply says which premise or inference fails.
goal: Draw accurately on at least two medieval thinkers from this course, and report disagreements about interpretation as disagreements.
goal: Give your own reasoned answer to a question on which informed philosophers disagree, while stating the opposing views fairly.
step: Choose a topic and a question that can be answered "yes" or "no", for example "Whether universals exist outside the mind", "Whether existence is part of the essence of a created thing" or "Whether divine foreknowledge is compatible with human freedom". Use the material below to get started, then read the relevant encyclopedia entries and a primary text through the course reading.
step: Draft three objections. Each begins "It seems that..." and gives an argument, set out in standard form on scratch paper first, for the answer you will reject. Draw on real positions: use a different thinker or a different premise for each.
step: Write the sed contra ("On the contrary..."): a short authority or argument pointing toward your answer. Cite a text or thinker you have read, and paraphrase it unless you are sure of the exact wording of a public domain source.
step: Write the respondeo ("I answer that..."), the main body, of about 800 words. State a distinction or principle, then argue for your answer using at least two medieval thinkers, and say what each contributes. State clearly where interpreters disagree.
step: Reply to each objection in order ("Reply to Objection 1..."). For each, say which premise is false, or which inference is invalid, or which distinction the objector missed. Where an objection has force, concede what is true in it.
step: Check accuracy: every attribution, every claim about a text and every definition. Remove any quotation that you cannot check, and cite the sources you used.
step: Revise for the form: the objections should be the strongest you can give, and the replies should address the objection as written. Check the length, about 2,000 words in all.

material: **The form.** A scholastic article has this order: the question; "Objection 1", "Objection 2", "Objection 3"; "On the contrary"; "I answer that"; "Reply to Objection 1", "Reply to Objection 2", "Reply to Objection 3". Objections give the strongest case for the answer you will reject, and the replies show that you have understood each one.
deliverable: A disputed question of about 2,000 words in the structure above, with a bibliography of the sources you used (course reading, encyclopedia entries and PhilPapers items).
deliverable: A half-page note listing which medieval thinker you relied on for each objection, for the sed contra and for the respondeo, with one sentence on the interpretive disagreement you reported.
rubric: Form: the question, three objections, a sed contra, a respondeo and three replies are all present and in order.
rubric: Accuracy: attributions, claims about texts and definitions are correct, and disputed interpretations are reported as disputed.
rubric: Argument: objections are strong and in standard form, and each reply identifies the exact premise, inference or distinction at issue.
rubric: Use of sources: at least two medieval thinkers contribute real arguments to the answer, and the sources are cited.
rubric: Independence: the answer takes a clear position, states the opposing view fairly and does not rely on a count of philosophers' opinions as evidence.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Late antiquity.** Plotinus: the One, Intellect, Soul, by emanation. Augustine: creation out of nothing, the Forms as ideas in God, the argument from eternal truths, and the point that even a mistaken person exists.
2. **Augustine.** The puzzle about time (past and future do not exist, the present has no duration) and the reply in terms of memory, attention and expectation; evil as privation; free choice and the free will defense.
3. **Boethius.** The foreknowledge argument; conditional versus simple necessity; eternity as the complete possession of unending life; providence and fate.
4. **Universals.** Porphyry's three questions; Boethius's dilemma and his abstractionist solution; realism, conceptualism and nominalism.
5. **Al-Farabi and Avicenna.** Necessary and possible in itself; the argument for a necessary being; essence and existence; essence in itself.
6. **Averroes and Maimonides.** The *Decisive Treatise*, interpretation when demonstration conflicts with scripture; Maimonides on the eternity of the world and on negative theology.
7. **Anselm.** The ontological argument as a reductio; Gaunilo's island and Anselm's reply.
8. **Aquinas.** The five ways; essentially versus accidentally ordered series; the first way; why the ways do not require a beginning in time.
9. **Aquinas on being and the soul.** Essence and esse; hylomorphism; the argument that the intellectual soul subsists; matter signed by quantity.
10. **Scotus.** Univocity of being and the certain-and-doubtful argument; haecceity and the formal distinction.
11. **Ockham.** Universals as signs; the identity argument against a common nature; parsimony.
12. **Legacy.** Universals, existence, haecceitism, hylomorphism and the hard/soft fact distinction.

### Key arguments and distinctions

- Augustine's argument from eternal truths: the step from "eternal and higher than the mind" to God (P5) is the contested one.
- The foreknowledge argument: the necessity of the consequence differs from the necessity of the consequent.
- Boethius's dilemma: a universal cannot be one thing wholly in many, and cannot be an empty thought.
- Avicenna: possible in itself, necessary through another; existence is not part of the essence of contingent things.
- Anselm's reductio and the reply to Gaunilo; Aquinas's a posteriori route.
- Aquinas versus Scotus: analogy versus univocity; designated matter versus haecceity.
- Ockham's razor is a guide to theory choice, not a proof.

### Self-check

- Can you set out Augustine's puzzle about time, the foreknowledge problem and the ontological argument in standard form?
- Can you say which premise each reply rejects?
- Can you state Avicenna's, Aquinas's and Scotus's views on essence, existence and individuality without mixing them up?
- Can you explain a disputed interpretation without choosing silently?

### Common errors

- Treating a historian's reading as the only reading.
- Attributing modern terms (haecceitism, soft fact) to medieval authors.
- Confusing the necessity of the consequence with the necessity of the consequent.
- Saying that the first way needs a beginning of the universe.
- Treating the razor or a survey as a proof.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of 400 to 600 words that connects at least three weeks of the course: choose one of (a) the problem of universals from Boethius to Ockham, (b) essence and existence from Avicenna to Scotus, (c) divine knowledge and freedom from Augustine to Ockham. State the problem, give the main positions in order with accurate attributions, and evaluate one of them with an objection and a reply.
hint: Pick a thread, then place each thinker's contribution in order, noting what each keeps and what each changes.
solution: Sample for (a). The problem arises from Porphyry's three questions, which he did not answer. Boethius argues that a universal can be neither a single thing wholly in many individuals nor an empty thought, and holds that the likeness exists in things as sensible and in the mind as intelligible, by abstraction. Avicenna adds that an essence in itself is neither universal nor particular. Aquinas, a moderate realist, places the nature in individuals and explains individuation by matter signed by quantity. Scotus holds a common nature with less than numerical unity, contracted by a haecceity to which it is formally distinct, and rejects individuation by quantity. Ockham denies any common nature: only individuals exist, and a universal is a sign; his identity argument, as usually reconstructed, says that a nature really identical with Socrates and with Plato would make them identical. Evaluating Ockham: his razor favors the sparser theory, but a realist objects that resemblance is left unexplained; Ockham replies that resemblance is a basic fact about individuals, and the realist's instantiation is equally basic. So the dispute turns on what needs explaining, and the razor alone does not decide it.
rubric: Sets out the chosen thread in order, with correct attributions of at least three thinkers.
rubric: Describes each position accurately and shows how it relates to the others.
rubric: Evaluates one position with an objection and a reply, without claiming the question is settled.
