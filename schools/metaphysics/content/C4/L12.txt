@article
The medieval period did not end with a single break. Its questions, vocabulary and arguments passed into early modern philosophy and are still active in contemporary metaphysics, often under new names. This lesson surveys five threads, so that you can read modern work and see what it inherits.

### Universals

The medieval positions on universals map onto contemporary ones. Platonic realism has descendants among those who hold that properties are abstract objects; Aristotelian realism is close to views on which properties are immanent, existing in the things that have them; Ockham's austerity is echoed by nominalists who accept only individuals, and by **trope theorists**, who hold that properties are particular, as this particular redness, an idea with medieval roots in the discussion of individual qualities. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey of professional philosophers, about 38% accepted or leaned toward Platonism about abstract objects and about 42% nominalism; this shows that the debate is alive, and it is not evidence that either view is true.

### Existence and essence

Avicenna's and Aquinas's distinction between essence and existence reappears in debates about whether existence is a property and about essential versus accidental properties. Kant's remark that being is not a "real predicate", that is, not a feature that adds to the content of a concept (Critique of Pure Reason, A598/B626), is aimed at the ontological argument and has influenced a tradition that treats existence as a matter of whether a concept is instantiated. The distinction between what a thing is and that it is also lies behind modern necessary-being arguments, such as Leibnizian cosmological arguments from contingency, which are related to Avicenna's and Aquinas's reasoning about possible beings and their causes (the line runs through later scholastic and early modern authors, and is a family resemblance more than a single inheritance). Modal versions of the ontological argument, using possible worlds and S5, descend from Anselm; whether they are sound depends on whether a maximally great being is possible, and philosophers on both sides of the question have argued for their own answers.

### Individuation and haecceity

Scotus's haecceity survives as **haecceitism** in the metaphysics of modality: the view that two possible worlds can differ merely in which individuals play which roles, so that individuals have a "thisness" that is not reducible to their qualities. The word "haecceity" is associated with Scotus, but "haecceitism" is a twentieth-century term, and its modern meaning is not his: Scotus asked what makes individuals distinct within the actual world and did not use possible-worlds semantics. The line from Scotus is therefore one of theme and vocabulary (a primitive individuating "thisness") and not a direct historical inheritance, so be careful to separate what Scotus said from what is now called by his name. Opponents, such as David Lewis, deny haecceitism. The question of whether qualities can fix individuality is also taken up in the debate over the **identity of indiscernibles**.

### Matter, form and the soul

Aristotelian metaphysics has had a revival. Contemporary **hylomorphists** hold that some objects are composed of matter and a principle of structure or form, in answer to questions such as the special composition question, and some neo-Aristotelians defend a metaphysics of kinds and powers. Aquinas's view of the soul as form is cited in discussions of the mind-body problem, which remains open: physicalists, dualists and others disagree.

### Foreknowledge, time and eternity

Augustine's puzzle about time bears on the debate between **presentism** (only the present exists) and the **eternalist** views, and on whether the past and the future are real. Boethius's eternity doctrine is a precursor to views on which God is timeless, and the question of how a timeless being relates to time remains disputed. Ockham gave an influential reply to the foreknowledge problem: statements such as "God knew yesterday that Ana will leave tomorrow" are, he argued, **only apparently about the past**, since their truth depends on what will happen in the future. Today philosophers speak of **hard facts**, which are genuinely and wholly about the past and are fixed, and **soft facts**, which only seem to be about the past. This vocabulary comes from the modern discussion revived in the 1960s and after (for example Nelson Pike's 1965 paper on divine omniscience and Marilyn McCord Adams's 1967 paper "Is the Existence of God a 'Hard' Fact?"), which deliberately drew on Ockham. The argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | God knew yesterday that Ana will leave tomorrow, and that fact entails that Ana will leave tomorrow, since knowledge is factive. |
| P2 | The fact that God knew yesterday is a fact about the past, and the past is now fixed and beyond our control. |
| P3 | If a fact about the past entails that Ana will leave, and that fact is fixed, then it is fixed that Ana will leave. |
| C | So Ana cannot do otherwise than leave, and she is not free to stay. |

Ockham-style replies reject premise P2: that God knew something yesterday is a soft fact, since it depends on what Ana will freely do tomorrow. Critics reply that it is hard to give a principled test for which past-tense claims are soft; a defender of the Ockhamist reply must supply one. Alternatively, a Boethian rejects the idea that God's knowledge is located in the past at all, and a Molinist and an open theist give other replies. All of these are live options.

### Reading across the periods

When you read a modern paper, ask which medieval question it is a version of, and what has changed. Often the change is a formal tool, such as possible worlds or a theory of properties, and sometimes it is a shift in what is at stake. The deliverable for this block, a two-page defense of realism or nominalism, is a way to do the same thing yourself.

@example
title: Tracing a medieval idea into a contemporary debate
problem: Identify the medieval source of each contemporary position: (a) trope theory; (b) haecceitism; (c) the soft-fact reply to the foreknowledge argument.
step: (a) Trope theory holds that properties are particulars; the medieval source is the discussion of individual qualities, in the tradition of Ockham's austerity, though trope theory's modern form is a development.
step: (b) Haecceitism: the medieval source of the term is Scotus's haecceity, a primitive individual difference, though the modern thesis concerns possible worlds.
step: (c) The soft-fact reply: Ockham's claim that some past-tense statements about God's knowledge are only apparently about the past.
answer: (a) the medieval discussion of individual qualities and Ockham's individualism; (b) Scotus's haecceity; (c) Ockham on future contingents and God's knowledge.

@example
title: Hard and soft facts
problem: Classify each as a hard fact or a soft fact about the past, using the Ockhamist test (does its truth depend on what will happen later?): (a) "The coin was minted in 1990." (b) "Ten years ago, it was true that the coin will be spent next week." (c) "God knew yesterday that the coin will be spent next week."
step: (a) Its truth depends only on what happened in 1990: a hard fact.
step: (b) Its truth depends on whether the coin is spent next week, so it is only apparently about the past: a soft fact.
step: (c) On the Ockhamist view, God's knowing depends on what will happen, so it is a soft fact; others deny this, which is the point of the dispute.
answer: (a) hard; (b) soft; (c) soft on the Ockhamist view, though disputed.

@mistakes
- Saying that the medieval problems have been solved or abandoned: they continue under new names, and the answers are disputed.
- Using the modern term "haecceitism" as if it were Scotus's own doctrine: its modern meaning concerns possible worlds.
- Treating survey percentages as evidence for a view: the PhilPapers figures describe what professionals believe, and not what is true.
- Saying that Ockham's reply denies that God knows the future: it says that God's past knowledge is a soft fact about the past.

prompt: Plan a two-page defense of realism or nominalism about universals: state your thesis, give two arguments (one drawing on a medieval thinker), state the strongest objection and your reply.
hint: Use Boethius's dilemma, Scotus's formal distinction or Ockham's razor and identity argument.
solution: Thesis (nominalism): only individuals exist; "red" and "man" are signs that apply to many. Argument 1 (from Ockham): if a common nature were really identical with Socrates and with Plato, Socrates would be identical with Plato; the formal distinction does not remove the difficulty, so there is no real common nature. Argument 2 (parsimony): an account in terms of resemblances among individuals explains why the word applies, and universals add entities without further explanation. Objection: resemblance is itself a universal, or else is unexplained, so nominalism leaves the sameness of the red things unexplained. Reply: resemblance is a basic fact about individuals, and a realist account only relocates it, since "instantiation" is a primitive relation as well; so the cost is a draw, and the razor decides. A model for realism would answer in the opposite direction.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, explain how one medieval argument from this course is used or answered in contemporary philosophy, and say what has changed.
hint: Choose among the ontological argument, the contingency argument, the problem of universals and the foreknowledge problem.
solution: Anselm's ontological argument is now often given in a modal form: if a maximally great being is possible, then, in the logic S5, such a being exists in every world, so exists actually. This keeps Anselm's idea that God is the greatest conceivable being and that necessary existence is part of greatness, but replaces the claim that existence adds to greatness with the claim about possibility. What has changed is the formal tool, possible worlds semantics; what is unchanged is that the argument's force depends on a premise that critics dispute: whether a maximally great being is possible. Opponents offer parodies, such as claiming that it is possible that no maximally great being exists, which shows that the key premise cannot be accepted just because it is coherent.
