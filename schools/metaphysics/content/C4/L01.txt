@article
Why does anything exist at all, and why is the world made of many things rather than one? Late antique philosophers answered with a picture that shaped the next thousand years: reality is a hierarchy that descends from something perfectly simple to the many changing things of ordinary experience. Medieval metaphysics in the Christian, Islamic and Jewish traditions is largely the story of how that picture was inherited, argued over, and revised.

### Plotinus and the three principles

Plotinus (third century AD), whose lectures were collected by his student Porphyry as the *Enneads*, rebuilt Plato's philosophy into a system that later readers call **Neoplatonism**. He posits three **hypostases** (basic principles of reality):

- **The One**, absolutely simple, with no parts, no duality of thinker and thought, and, in Plotinus's famous phrase, "beyond being". It is the source of everything else, and it is called "the Good" because everything seeks it.
- **Intellect** (*Nous*), the first product of the One. It contains the Platonic Forms and thinks them; in it, thinking and what is thought are one, but there is already plurality, because there are many Forms.
- **Soul**, which is produced by Intellect, animates the physical cosmos and mediates between the intelligible and the sensible. Matter lies at the bottom of the series.

The relation between them is **emanation** (or "procession"): the One produces Intellect, and Intellect produces Soul, not by deliberating, choosing or acting in time, but as heat radiates from a fire or light from the sun, without the source being diminished. The lower principle then "turns back" toward its source and is completed by contemplating it. A reconstruction of one of Plotinus's motives for the One runs as follows.

| P1 | Whatever is composite, made of distinct parts or aspects, depends for its being on those parts and on whatever unites them. |
| P2 | Intellect is composite, because it is both thinker and thought and contains a plurality of Forms. |
| C1 | So Intellect depends on something prior to it. |
| P3 | A chain of dependence of this kind cannot run on forever, and what stops it must itself not be composite. |
| C2 | So there is something absolutely simple on which all composite things depend: the One. |

### Augustine: the Platonists, corrected

Augustine (354–430), bishop of Hippo, tells us in the *Confessions* (Book VII) that "books of the Platonists" showed him how to think of a non-bodily reality and of God as an immaterial, unchangeable being. He took much from them, but he changed three central things.

1. **Creation, not emanation.** For Augustine the world is freely created by God out of nothing (*ex nihilo*), by an act of will and in no way necessary to God. This differs from Plotinus's necessary overflow, and he also rejects any eternal, uncreated matter.
2. **The Forms are God's ideas.** Plato's Forms and Plotinus's Intellect become the ideas in the divine mind, according to which God makes creatures (Augustine's discussion of ideas is in *Eighty-Three Different Questions*, question 46).
3. **A personal God, and the soul's own existence.** Augustine answers skeptics with a point that anticipates Descartes: even if I am mistaken, I exist (*City of God* XI.26).

### The argument from the eternal truths

In *On Free Choice of the Will*, Book II, Augustine argues that the existence of necessary truths leads to God. Here is a standard reconstruction.

| P1 | There are truths, for example in arithmetic, that are necessary and unchangeable. |
| P2 | These truths are common to all minds that grasp them: they are discovered, not made, by any individual mind. |
| P3 | Our minds are changeable, and what is changeable cannot be the source of, or superior to, what is unchangeable. |
| P4 | So the truths are higher than our minds. |
| P5 | Whatever is eternal and unchangeable and higher than the human mind is God, or is subordinate to something that is God. |
| C | So God exists. |

Augustine himself says that if there is something higher than truth, that is God, and if there is not, then truth itself is God; either way, God exists. Note that P3 and P5 carry most of the weight. An objector can reply that necessary truths need no source beyond themselves, or that they are products of how any minds must reason, or that they are abstract facts and not a mind at all. A defender can answer that abstract truths do not account for the fact that they are *thought* and that there is a reality which we can know. Premise P5 is the step from "something eternal" to "God", and it is the one that critics have pressed hardest.

### Exegesis and evaluation

When you read a medieval argument, first say what the author is claiming (exegesis), then ask whether it is sound (evaluation). Augustine's argument is a good first test: its structure is clear, and the philosophical dispute lies in how to think about necessary truths.

@example
title: Placing claims in the hierarchy
problem: For each claim, say whether it describes the One, Intellect or Soul in Plotinus's system: (a) it has no parts and is beyond being and thought; (b) it is the realm of the Forms, where thinker and thought are one but many Forms exist; (c) it gives life and order to the physical cosmos and mediates between the intelligible and the sensible.
step: (a) Absolute simplicity, with no distinction even between thinker and thought, belongs to the **One**. Anything that thinks has at least the duality of thinker and thought.
step: (b) Many Forms form a plurality, so this is not the One; but thinker and thought are identical here, so this is not Soul either. It is **Intellect**.
step: (c) Animating the cosmos and linking the intelligible and the sensible is the work of **Soul**.
answer: (a) the One; (b) Intellect; (c) Soul.

@example
title: Finding the work done by premise P5
problem: A student objects to Augustine: "A mathematical truth like 'seven plus three is ten' is eternal and unchangeable, but it is just an abstract fact, so nothing about it requires God." Which premise of the reconstruction does this reject, and what must Augustine's defender add?
step: Check the premises one by one. The student does not deny that the truths are necessary (P1) or that they are discovered (P2). The student does not dispute that our minds are changeable (P3) or that the truths are higher than our minds (P4).
step: The student grants that the truths are eternal and unchangeable but denies that this makes them God or dependent on God. That rejects premise P5.
step: To keep the argument, the defender needs a reason to think that eternal truths are not self-standing. One reply is that truths are *thought* and *known*, and that a reality which is knowable and necessary is best explained as grounded in a divine mind; this is what Augustine's identification of the Forms with God's ideas is meant to supply.
answer: The objection targets premise P5. The defender must give an independent reason for treating eternal truth as grounded in God rather than as a self-standing abstract fact.

@mistakes
- Saying that Plotinus's One creates the world by choice, as a craftsman: for Plotinus emanation is necessary and not an act of deliberation, which is exactly where Augustine and the Christian tradition diverge.
- Treating the Neoplatonic One as the same as Intellect: the One is beyond even the duality of thinker and thought.
- Saying that Augustine's argument from truth proves God from the premise that truths are many: the key premises are that truths are necessary, are not made by us, and are higher than our changeable minds.
- Attributing the claim "I think, therefore I am" in those words to Augustine: his point (if I am mistaken, I exist) is related, but it is made in a different setting and for a different use.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain one way in which Augustine's account of creation differs from Plotinus's account of emanation, and say why the difference matters for the relation of God to the world.
hint: Think about will and necessity, and about what the lower level depends on.
solution: For Plotinus the One produces Intellect and Soul by a necessary overflow, as light comes from the sun, with no deliberation, and the lower levels are eternal consequences of the One. For Augustine God creates the world out of nothing by a free act of will, so the world is not a necessary outflow of God's nature and could have been otherwise or not have been at all. This matters because it makes the world contingent and wholly dependent on God, while God is complete without it. It also allows a God who acts and wills, which fits the Christian doctrine of creation, whereas emanation presents a continuous, impersonal procession.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate Augustine's argument from the eternal truths. Write the argument in standard form, name the premise you consider weakest, and give one objection and the best reply to it, in a paragraph of about 150 words.
hint: Look at the step from eternal truths to God; ask what else could explain their necessity.
solution: P1: There are necessary, unchangeable truths. P2: They are discovered and not made by any mind. P3: Our minds are changeable. P4: So the truths are higher than our minds. P5: Whatever is eternal and higher than our minds is God or subordinate to God. C: So God exists. The weakest premise is P5. An objector says that necessary truths are self-standing abstract facts, or reflect only the structure of any possible reasoning, so no further source is needed. The best reply is that truths are not merely facts but things that can be known and thought, and that necessary, knowable truth is best explained by an eternal mind; this is the point of treating the Forms as God's ideas. The objector can answer that this only relocates the necessity into God, who is then also constrained by it. The argument is therefore as strong as the reasons for rejecting self-standing abstract truth.
