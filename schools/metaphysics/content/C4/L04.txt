@article
Socrates and Plato are two different men, and yet both are men. What is it that they share? If we answer that it is **humanity**, a nature that each of them has, then we seem to have admitted something that exists in two places at once, or something that exists apart from both of them. If we answer that they share nothing but a word, then it is hard to explain why that word fits them and not a horse. The problem of what, if anything, our general terms answer to is the **problem of universals**, and its medieval form begins with a few sentences in a short introduction to Aristotle's logic.

### Porphyry's three questions

Porphyry (third century AD) wrote the *Isagoge* ("Introduction") to Aristotle's *Categories*. It presents the five **predicables**: genus, species, difference, property and accident. Porphyry says that he will not discuss deeper matters, and then he lists three questions and declines to answer them:

1. Do genera and species exist in themselves, or only in bare thoughts?
2. If they exist, are they bodies or incorporeal?
3. Are they separate from sensible things, or in them and dependent on them?

The *Isagoge* was translated into Latin by Boethius (about 480–524), who wrote two commentaries on it. Through them these questions became the starting point of a thousand years of debate.

### The main positions

| Position | What it says about the universal *man* |
|---|---|
| **Platonic (extreme) realism** | The universal exists in itself, apart from individual men, as a Form. |
| **Aristotelian (moderate) realism** | The universal exists, but only in individual things that have it. |
| **Conceptualism** | The universal exists only as a concept in the mind, which applies to many things. |
| **Nominalism** | There are no universals: only individuals exist, and "man" is a general term applying to many of them. |

The terms are modern labels for a spectrum, and in the Middle Ages the exact meaning of each was itself debated. Learn them as tools for classifying a view, not as something each author accepted.

### Boethius's dilemma

In his second commentary on the *Isagoge*, Boethius argues that each of the two simplest answers fails. The argument can be reconstructed in two parts.

| P1 | If a genus or species is a single thing, then that one thing is common to many individuals: it is wholly in each of them at once. |
| P2 | A single thing cannot be wholly present in many things at once and remain numerically one. |
| C1 | So a genus or species cannot be a single thing common to many individuals. |
| P3 | If a genus or species is merely a thought that corresponds to nothing in things, then it is an empty or false thought. |
| P4 | Our thoughts of man or animal, which group like things, are not empty or false. |
| C2 | So a genus or species cannot be merely an empty thought. |

Boethius's own diagnosis of the first horn is also that if a universal were shared by *parts*, then it would not be common to the whole, and if shared wholly it would be one yet many. The second horn is plausible because we do not take it to be a mistake to think of Socrates and Plato as both men.

### Boethius's solution

Boethius resolves the dilemma by saying that universals have a double mode of being. In individual things the likeness exists as something **sensible**, tied to each individual. In the mind the same likeness is **intelligible** and general: the mind **abstracts** from the differences among individuals and attends only to what they have in common. There is no single entity shared by two men, and yet the thought of man is not empty, since it is grounded in a real likeness among men. This solution is Aristotelian in spirit, since it places universals in things and gives the mind the work of abstraction.

Interpretation is not settled. Some readers take Boethius to be a moderate realist, since the likeness is real and in things; others stress that it is the mind that makes it universal, so that his view is closer to conceptualism; and some argue that he did not intend to resolve the problem at all. A good answer says which reading it follows and why.

### Evaluating the solution

The strongest objection is that a "likeness" is itself a universal, so the solution seems to assume what it needs to explain: why do things resemble one another in just the way they do? A realist reply is that the likenesses are real features of things; a nominalist reply is that resemblance is a primitive fact about individuals, which needs no further explanation. Later lessons follow how Avicenna, Aquinas, Scotus and Ockham answered.

@example
title: Classifying views on universals
problem: Classify each view as Platonic realism, Aristotelian realism, conceptualism or nominalism. (a) Only individual things exist; "red" is just a word we apply to many things. (b) Redness exists, but only in red things, and would not exist if nothing were red. (c) Redness exists in itself, apart from red things. (d) "Red" stands for a concept in the mind that applies to many things, and there is nothing in things beyond the individuals.
step: Ask first whether the universal is held to exist at all outside the mind. In (a) and (d) it is not; in (b) and (c) it is.
step: Between (a) and (d): in (a) the only general thing is a word, which is nominalism; in (d) a concept is the general item, which is conceptualism.
step: Between (b) and (c): in (c) the universal is separate from things (Platonic), while in (b) it exists only in things (Aristotelian).
answer: (a) nominalism; (b) Aristotelian realism; (c) Platonic realism; (d) conceptualism.

@example
title: Using the dilemma
problem: A student says: "The universal *animal* is just one thing that is in a horse and in a dog." Explain, using Boethius's dilemma, what difficulty he would press.
step: Identify which horn this is: the universal is a single thing common to many individuals. This is premise P1's supposition.
step: Apply P2: a single thing cannot be wholly in a horse and in a dog at once and still be one numerically; if each has only a part, then it is not common as a whole.
step: So the student's answer faces the objection that it makes one thing both one and many.
answer: The student's view is the first horn, which Boethius presses against as making a numerically one thing be wholly in many.

@mistakes
- Saying that Porphyry answered his three questions: he raised them and declined to answer them.
- Calling Boethius a nominalist: on the usual readings he holds that the likeness is real in things and universal in the mind, and he rejects both the single shared thing and the empty thought.
- Equating the problem of universals with the question of whether general words are useful: it is about what, if anything, makes different things alike.
- Treating "realism" as one view: Platonic and Aristotelian realism differ on whether universals exist apart from things.

prompt: Reconstruct the foreknowledge problem in standard form (three or four premises and a conclusion) and explain, in a few sentences, how Boethius replies to it.
hint: Recall the distinction between the necessity of the consequence and of the consequent, and God's eternity.
solution: P1: God infallibly foreknows every action. P2: If God foreknows an action, that action will necessarily occur. P3: An action that will necessarily occur is not free. C: No action is free. Boethius rejects P2 as simple necessity: from God's knowing it follows only that the act occurs, with a conditional necessity, as someone seen walking is necessarily walking while he is seen. He adds that God is eternal and knows all things in an eternal present, so God's knowledge does not come before the act.
