Can you prove that something exists simply by thinking about what it would be like? Most philosophers think the answer is no. Yet Anselm of Canterbury (1033–1109) thought that one case is special: in Chapter 2 of the *Proslogion* he offers a short argument that God exists, using only the meaning of "God". Immanuel Kant later called arguments of this kind **ontological**, and the label stuck. It is among the most studied and disputed arguments in the history of philosophy, and for good reason: it is very short, and it is hard to say exactly what is wrong with it, if anything is.

### Anselm's argument

Anselm starts from a definition of God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived". He then argues as follows, in the form of a **reductio ad absurdum** (an argument that supposes the denial of the conclusion and derives a contradiction).

| P1 | God is, by definition, a being than which nothing greater can be conceived. |
| P2 | Even someone who denies that God exists understands this description, so such a being exists in the understanding. |
| P3 | A being that exists in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than one that exists in the understanding alone. |
| P4 | Suppose, for reductio, that the being than which nothing greater can be conceived exists in the understanding alone. |
| C1 | Then we could conceive of a being that exists in reality too, and is greater, from P3. |
| C2 | So we could conceive of something greater than that than which nothing greater can be conceived, which is a contradiction. |
| C | So that than which nothing greater can be conceived exists in reality as well as in the understanding. |

Premise P2 is reported as a point about the fool of Psalm 14, who "says in his heart" that there is no God: even the fool understands the phrase. Premise P3 treats existence as a respect in which something can be greater, a premise that has attracted much discussion. The conclusion C is reached by rejecting P4, so the form of the argument is valid.

### Necessary existence

In Chapter 3 Anselm argues that this being cannot even be conceived not to exist: a being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than one that can, so that than which nothing greater can be conceived must be such that it cannot be conceived not to exist. Interpreters disagree about whether Chapter 3 is a second argument or a development of the first, and many modern versions of the argument, using modal logic and the system S5, descend from this part rather than from Chapter 2.

### Gaunilo's reply

Gaunilo, a monk of Marmoutier, replied *On Behalf of the Fool*. His best-known objection is a **parody**: suppose we speak of a lost island more excellent than any other. By Anselm's reasoning, since an island that exists is more excellent than one that exists only in the understanding, the most excellent island must exist. But it is absurd that we can prove an island exists in this way. So, Gaunilo argues, Anselm's form of reasoning is unreliable. Gaunilo also questions premise P2: understanding a phrase may not amount to having something in the understanding in the way the argument requires, since we cannot really form a conception of God as we can of a man.

### Anselm's reply

Anselm responds that the argument applies only to "that than which nothing greater can be conceived". An island, or any other thing, can be conceived not to exist, whereas that than which nothing greater can be conceived cannot (the point of Chapter 3). Later defenders add that "the greatest island" has no definite maximum, since for any island one can conceive a better one, so the notion is incoherent, while the notion of a being than which nothing greater can be conceived has a maximum built in. Many philosophers regard this reply as the beginning of a long dispute about which "greatest" notions can be used in this way, and whether Gaunilo's parody can be repaired to avoid Anselm's reply.

### Later criticism

Aquinas (next lesson) denied that Anselm's argument works, because we do not know God's essence well enough to see that God's existence follows. Kant argued that existence is not a "real predicate", a property that adds to the concept of a thing, and so cannot make one concept greater than another (though this reading is itself debated). Others reply that the argument can be reformulated as a modal one, so that the question becomes whether a maximally great being is possible. Whether any version is sound is not settled, and philosophers of every view on God have written about it.

@example
title: Showing that the argument is a reductio
problem: Identify the supposition that Anselm reduces to absurdity, the contradiction he derives, and the conclusion.
step: The supposition is P4: that than which nothing greater can be conceived exists in the understanding alone.
step: With P3 this lets us conceive a greater being, one that also exists in reality. So we can conceive something greater than that than which nothing greater can be conceived: a contradiction.
step: Since the supposition leads to a contradiction, it is rejected, and what is left is that such a being exists in reality as well as in the understanding.
answer: The supposition is that God exists in the understanding alone; it yields the contradiction that something greater than the greatest conceivable can be conceived; so God exists in reality.

@example
title: Testing a parody
problem: A student offers a parody: "Consider the perfect pizza, that than which no pizza can be better. Existing pizzas are better than merely imagined pizzas, so the perfect pizza exists." Use Anselm's reply to Gaunilo to say what he would answer, and what a defender of the parody would need to show.
step: Anselm's reply is that the argument works only for that than which nothing greater can be conceived, which cannot be conceived not to exist, whereas an island or a pizza can be conceived not to exist. Later defenders add that things such as islands or pizzas can always be improved, so there is no maximum.
step: Apply this: for any pizza we can conceive a better one, so "that than which no pizza can be better" does not pick out a coherent maximum, and the argument cannot start.
step: A defender of the parody would need to show either that such a maximum does exist for pizzas, or that Anselm's argument has a flaw independent of the maximum, so the reasoning proves too much.
answer: Anselm would say that the argument applies only to that than which nothing greater can be conceived, and defenders add that "the best pizza" has no maximum, so the parody fails. A defender needs a parody with a coherent maximum or a general flaw in the form of the argument.

@mistakes
- Saying that the argument assumes that God exists: premise P1 is a definition, and the supposition P4 is the denial of the conclusion.
- Describing Gaunilo as an atheist: he wrote on behalf of the fool, to show the form of the argument unreliable, and he was a believer.
- Treating the parody as a proof that the island exists: its point is that if the form of reasoning were good, it would prove too much.
- Confusing the ontological argument with the cosmological arguments, which begin from facts about the world such as motion or contingency.
