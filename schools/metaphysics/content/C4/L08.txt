@article
Anselm tried to reach God from the meaning of a word. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) takes the opposite route. In the *Summa Theologiae* (I, q. 2, a. 3) he offers five short arguments, the **five ways**, each starting from an ordinary fact about the world and reasoning to a first cause, mover or source. Because they begin from experience, they are called **a posteriori** arguments. In the previous article (I, q. 2, a. 1), Aquinas had said that the existence of God is not self-evident to us, since we do not know God's essence, which is why he rejects Anselm's argument.

### The five ways in outline

| Way | Starting point | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|
| First: motion | Some things are in motion (change). | A first mover that is not moved by anything else. |
| Second: efficient cause | There is an order of efficient causes. | A first efficient cause. |
| Third: possibility and necessity | There are things that can exist or not exist. | Something that is necessary of itself, and the source of necessity in other things. |
| Fourth: gradation | Things differ in degrees of goodness, truth and being. | A maximum that is the cause of these perfections in others. |
| Fifth: governance | Natural things without knowledge act for ends. | An intelligent being that directs them to their ends. |

Each way ends with a version of the sentence: "and this everyone understands to be God", or something similar. Aquinas does not claim that the argument by itself shows that this being is the God of scripture; the later questions (q. 3 onward) argue for God's simplicity, perfection and other attributes.

### The first way

Aquinas uses "motion" for change in general, and understands change as the reduction of something from **potency** (potentiality) to **act** (actuality): wood that is potentially hot becomes actually hot when fire acts on it. This is the framework of Aristotle's physics. The argument can be reconstructed as follows.

| P1 | Some things are in motion: they are changing, being reduced from potency to act. |
| P2 | Whatever is moved is moved by something else, because nothing can be both in potency and in act in the same respect at the same time. |
| P3 | A series of movers in which each moves only because it is itself moved by another cannot go on to infinity. |
| C1 | So there is a first mover that is moved by nothing else. |
| C2 | This is what everyone understands to be God. |

The crucial premise is P3, and it helps to see what kind of series Aquinas has in mind. Medieval authors distinguish an **essentially ordered** series (per se), in which each member acts only in virtue of the activity of the one before it at the same time, as a hand moves a stick that moves a stone, from an **accidentally ordered** series (per accidens), in which each member acts independently, as a father begets a son who begets another. Aquinas thinks that an infinite regress is impossible in the first kind, since if there were no first member that acts of itself, nothing would be moving at all, as a stick moves nothing unless a hand moves it. He does not claim that an accidentally ordered series, such as generations of animals reaching back in time, cannot be infinite. In fact he holds that natural reason cannot show that the world had a beginning in time (I, q. 46, a. 2). So the first way is not an argument from a beginning of the universe, and it does not require one.

### The other ways

The second way uses efficient causation in the same way, now about what causes the existence of things and not their motion. The **third way** starts from things that are generated and perish, hence can exist or not exist. Aquinas reasons that if everything were such that it can fail to exist, then at some time nothing existed, and then nothing would exist now. A common objection charges this with a mistake: from "for each thing, there is a time when it does not exist" it does not follow that "there is a time when nothing exists". Interpreters disagree about whether the argument really needs that step, and some read it more charitably as an argument from the dependence of contingent beings. The **fourth way** takes the "more and less" of perfections to point to a maximum; this relies on a principle that Aquinas takes from Aristotle's *Metaphysics* (with roots in Platonism) that whatever has a perfection to a degree gets it from what has it most, and many modern readers find this the least persuasive. The **fifth way** is an argument from final causes, which is sometimes confused with modern design arguments; Aquinas's version rests on the claim that natural bodies act for ends, whereas the modern one often begins from complexity.

### What the ways do and do not show

The five ways are not meant as a single combined argument. They differ in premises, and a critic who rejects one need not reject all. The first three ways in particular assume that there cannot be an infinite regress of *dependence*, and that is where critics press: they say either that an infinite chain is coherent, or that the first member need not be God, or that even if a first mover exists it is not obvious that there is only one or that it has the attributes of God. A defender replies that Aquinas deals with these in later questions, in which he argues for uniqueness and simplicity.

@example
title: Essentially or accidentally ordered?
problem: Say whether each series is essentially ordered (each member acts only in virtue of the one before it, now) or accidentally ordered: (a) a hand moving a stick that moves a stone, all at once; (b) a grandfather who fathers a father who fathers a son; (c) a magnet that holds a nail that holds a second nail.
step: Ask whether each member's activity depends on the previous member's activity at the same time.
step: (a) The stick moves the stone only while the hand moves the stick: **essentially ordered**.
step: (b) The father can father a son whether or not the grandfather is still alive or acting: **accidentally ordered**.
step: (c) The second nail hangs only because the first nail is held by the magnet, now: **essentially ordered**.
answer: (a) essentially ordered; (b) accidentally ordered; (c) essentially ordered.

@example
title: Finding the work done by premise P3 of the first way
problem: A student says: "The first way fails because the universe might have always existed, so there might have been no first moment." Does this refute the first way as reconstructed?
step: Identify what the first way needs: P3 says that a series of essentially ordered movers cannot be infinite, not that time has a beginning.
step: The student's objection is about a series stretching back in time, which is the accidentally ordered kind; Aquinas himself thought that reason cannot show that the world had a beginning.
step: To press the objection, the student would have to argue that an essentially ordered series, with simultaneous dependence, could be infinite, or that a first member is not needed to explain any motion now.
answer: It does not refute the first way as it stands, because the first way concerns simultaneous dependence and not a beginning in time. The student needs a different objection to premise P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that the first way argues that the universe had a beginning: Aquinas holds that reason cannot show this, and the first way concerns present dependence.
- Treating all five ways as one argument: each has its own premises and each may be assessed separately.
- Treating "motion" as only movement of place: Aquinas means any change, the reduction of potency to act.
- Saying that Aquinas accepts Anselm's argument: he denies that God's existence is self-evident to us.
