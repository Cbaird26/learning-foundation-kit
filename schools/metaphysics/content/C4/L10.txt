@article
When we say "God is good" and "Socrates is good", do we use "good" in the same sense? When we say that God exists and that a horse exists, is "exists" the same word in both cases? And what makes Socrates this particular man and not just a man? John Duns Scotus (about 1266–1308) gives two famous answers, each of which departs from Aquinas.

### Three ways of speaking

A term is used **univocally** of two things if it has one and the same meaning in both, **equivocally** if it has two unrelated meanings (the "bank" of a river and the "bank" for money), and **analogically** if its meanings are different but systematically related. Aquinas argues (*Summa Theologiae* I, q. 13, a. 5) that terms said of God and creatures are said neither univocally nor merely equivocally but analogically: "healthy" is said of an animal, of its complexion and of its medicine in related ways, with the animal as the primary case. Without this, he thinks, either we would claim to know God as we know creatures, or we could say nothing true about God at all.

### Scotus on the univocity of being

Scotus holds that there is at least one concept, the concept of **being**, that is common to God and creatures in a single sense. His argument, in the *Ordinatio* (Book I, distinction 3), runs as follows in outline.

| P1 | A person can be certain that God is a being while being in doubt whether God is a finite being or an infinite being. |
| P2 | If the concept of "being" were the same as the concept of "finite being" or of "infinite being", the person could not be certain of the one while doubting the other. |
| C1 | So the concept of "being" in the first claim is distinct from both the concept of finite being and the concept of infinite being, and is common to both. |
| P3 | A concept that is one in this way, so that affirming and denying it of the same thing would be a contradiction, and that can serve as the middle term of a syllogism, is univocal. |
| C2 | So "being" is said univocally of God and creatures. |

Scotus adds a second consideration: if all our concepts applied to God only equivocally or analogically, we could draw no inference from creatures to God, since an argument with a term that shifts meaning would be an equivocation. Univocity does not mean that God and creatures are alike in kind. For Scotus God is **infinite** and creatures finite; "infinite" and "finite" are **intrinsic modes** of being, and being is not a genus. A defender of Aquinas replies that Scotus's concept is abstract enough to say little, and that real differences are lost; and others hold that Aquinas's analogy and Scotus's univocity are less far apart than they sound. Scholars disagree about how far.

### Individuation: Aquinas and its problems

If Socrates and Plato share a human nature, what makes them two? Aquinas's answer (last week) is **matter marked out by quantity**. Scotus argues that this cannot be right.

| P1 | What individuates a substance must be prior to, or at least no later than, its other features. |
| P2 | Quantity is an accident, and accidents come after the substance they belong to. |
| P3 | Matter, form and the composite of the two all belong to the common nature, which is not of itself individual. |
| C | So neither matter nor quantity can be what makes something an individual. |

There is a further difficulty. If matter individuates, then things without matter, such as angels, would not be individuals within a species. Aquinas accepted this consequence, holding that each angel is a distinct species, but Scotus rejected it.

### Scotus's positive view: haecceity

Scotus holds that the nature that is common (humanity) is not by itself individual, since it has what he calls a "lesser unity". What makes it this one is an **individual difference**, often called **haecceity** ("thisness", from Latin *haec*, "this"). The haecceity is not matter, not form and not a composite of the two, but a positive feature that contracts the common nature to one individual. It is not a different thing from the nature; the two are united in a way Scotus describes as a **formal distinction**, a distinction less than a real distinction between two things and more than a mere distinction in our thinking. We can think of humanity without thinking of Socrates's haecceity, but they are not two things.

### Evaluation

Scotus's view explains why individuality is not a mere accident and accommodates the case of angels. Critics say that the haecceity is unexplained: it seems to name the problem and not to solve it, and the formal distinction is obscure. Defenders reply that individuality has to be a basic feature of things, so that a primitive "thisness" is the honest answer. Aquinas's defenders say that quantity-signed matter does explain something, since parts of matter occupy different places, and that the objection from priority rests on a different account of what is prior.

@example
title: Comparing Aquinas and Scotus on what individuates
problem: Two horses, Bucephalus and Incitatus, are of the same species. Say what Aquinas and what Scotus would say makes them two horses.
step: Aquinas: the species and its form are shared; what makes them two is that each is a different portion of matter, marked out by its own quantity, in a different place.
step: Scotus: the common nature of the horse is shared and is not enough for individuality; each horse has its own haecceity, a positive individual difference that makes it this one.
step: A difference: for Aquinas individuation is explained by something already in the thing, matter with quantity; for Scotus it is a primitive feature added to the nature. Scotus objects that quantity is an accident and so cannot be the prior principle of the substance's unity.
answer: Aquinas: different designated matter. Scotus: different haecceities, not reducible to matter or quantity.

@mistakes
- Saying that for Scotus God and creatures are the same: univocity concerns one concept, "being", and God remains infinite where creatures are finite.
- Saying that the haecceity is matter or form: it is neither, and is distinguished from the common nature by a formal distinction.
- Treating Aquinas's analogy as equivocation: analogical terms have related meanings, and Aquinas denies that talk about God is merely equivocal.
- Saying that for Aquinas angels share a species with other angels: he holds each angel is its own species, since there is no matter to individuate it.
