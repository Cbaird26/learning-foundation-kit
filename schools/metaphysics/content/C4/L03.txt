@article
Suppose that God knows today everything that you will do tomorrow. Then, it seems, what you will do tomorrow is already settled. If it is settled, how can it be up to you? This is the problem of **divine foreknowledge and human freedom**, and Boethius (about 480–524) gave it its classic medieval statement and its most influential reply, in Book V of the *Consolation of Philosophy*, written in the form of a dialogue between Boethius and Lady Philosophy.

### The problem

The worry in the *Consolation* is put in the mouth of Boethius himself. It can be reconstructed as follows.

| P1 | God infallibly foreknows everything that will happen, including every human action. |
| P2 | If God foreknows that an action will occur, then that action will necessarily occur: it cannot fail to occur. |
| P3 | If an action will necessarily occur, it is not free. |
| C | So no human action is free. |

The argument is valid. A defender of free action must deny a premise. Few deny P1 within the tradition, since divine knowledge is held to be perfect, and P3 seems close to the definition of an unfree action. The usual target is premise P2. There is also a further worry that Boethius presses: if our actions were not free, there would be no point in prayer or in praise and blame.

### The modal distinction behind the reply

Consider the claim "if God knows that you will sit, then you will sit". It is certainly true, since knowledge is factive: you cannot know what is false. But there are two ways to add "necessarily":

| Reading | Form | Is it true? |
|---|---|---|
| Necessity of the consequence | $\Box(K \to S)$: necessarily, if God knows that you will sit, then you will sit | Yes, by the nature of knowledge. |
| Necessity of the consequent | $K \to \Box S$: if God knows you will sit, then it is necessary that you will sit | Does not follow from the first. |

Writing $K$ for "God knows that you will sit" and $S$ for "you will sit", the inference from $\Box(K \to S)$ and $K$ to $\Box S$ is invalid: it confuses the two readings. This is the **modal fallacy** that the medieval tradition diagnosed in premise P2. In Boethius's terms, the necessity involved is **conditional necessity**, which attaches to a thing given a condition (as when you see someone walking, he must be walking while you see it) and is different from **simple necessity** (as for the fact that the sun rises). Conditional necessity does not remove freedom: someone who walks of his own accord is walking necessarily only in the sense that he is walking while you see him walking.

### Eternity and the eternal present

A critic can reply that the conditional claim is not enough, since God's knowledge occurred *before* the act, so that the past fact of God's knowing is now fixed. Boethius meets this by denying that God's knowledge is foreknowledge at all. His reply has three parts.

1. **Knowledge depends on the knower.** Things are known according to the nature of the knower and not simply according to the nature of the thing known. Sense, imagination, reason and intellect grasp the same thing in different ways.
2. **God is eternal, not everlasting.** Boethius defines eternity as the whole, simultaneous and perfect possession of unending life. God does not exist at every time in turn, as an everlasting being would; God is outside time and has all of life at once. The world, by contrast, exists in time and is at most perpetual.
3. **God's knowledge is of an eternal present.** From the standpoint of eternity every event is present. So God does not *fore*know what you will do; God sees it, as you see someone walking. And seeing does not make the act necessary, except with the conditional necessity just discussed.

Boethius adds a distinction between **providence** and **fate** (Book IV): providence is the divine plan as it is in the divine mind, simple and outside time, while fate is the same plan as it unfolds in the order of events in time.

### Objections

Critics reply that the eternal present only changes the metaphor: if everything is eternally present to God, then your act is eternally fixed. Defenders respond that being known is not being caused, and that fixedness of this kind is conditional. Other critics ask whether an eternal being can know *what time it is now* or can act in time. These questions are still discussed, and later medieval thinkers, especially Aquinas, Scotus and Ockham, each develop their own reply.

@example
title: Diagnosing the modal fallacy
problem: A student argues: "Necessarily, if God knows that I will walk, then I will walk. God knows that I will walk. So it is necessary that I will walk." Say what is wrong with the reasoning, using $K$ for "God knows that I will walk" and $W$ for "I will walk".
step: Symbolize: the first premise is $\Box(K \to W)$, the second is $K$, and the conclusion is $\Box W$.
step: The first premise is true: knowledge is factive. But from $\Box(K \to W)$ and $K$ we can only conclude $W$ (by modus ponens inside the box, the conclusion is $W$ and not $\Box W$). To reach $\Box W$ we would also need $\Box K$.
step: $K$ is a contingent fact about what God knows given what I will do; it is not necessary on its own. So the step to $\Box W$ is invalid: it confuses the necessity of the consequence with the necessity of the consequent.
answer: The argument slides from $\Box(K \to W)$ and $K$ to $\Box W$. This is invalid unless $K$ is itself necessary, and whether it is is just what the argument was supposed to establish.

@example
title: Where does Boethius reply?
problem: In the foreknowledge argument (P1 God infallibly foreknows everything; P2 if God foreknows an action, it necessarily occurs; P3 a necessary action is not free), which premise does Boethius's appeal to eternity and conditional necessity reject, and how?
step: Not P3: Boethius accepts that what occurs by simple necessity is not free. Not P1 in the sense of denying that God knows everything.
step: He rejects P2 in its simple form: God's knowledge makes an action necessary only conditionally, as seeing someone walk makes it necessary that he walks while he is seen walking.
step: He also qualifies the phrase "fore-knows": God's knowledge is of an eternal present, so it is not located before the act.
answer: He rejects premise P2 (read as simple necessity), by distinguishing conditional from simple necessity and by treating God's knowledge as eternal rather than as foreknowledge.

@mistakes
- Saying that Boethius denies that God knows the future: he holds that God knows everything, but that God's knowing is eternal and not located in time.
- Confusing eternity with endless time: for Boethius God's eternity is a complete present, not an unending sequence of days.
- Treating the modal fallacy as a failure of modus ponens: the inference from $\Box(K \to W)$ and $K$ to $\Box W$ is invalid because the box does not distribute over the conditional in that way.
- Treating conditional necessity as a claim that actions are necessary: it says only that what is known (or seen) is the case while it is known.
