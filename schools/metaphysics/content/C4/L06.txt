@article
Is it permissible, or even required, for a believer to do philosophy? And if philosophy seems to say that the world has always existed while scripture seems to say that God created it, which gives way? Medieval Muslims, Jews and Christians faced this problem with similar tools, and the answers of two twelfth-century thinkers, Averroes (Ibn Rushd, died 1198) and Maimonides (died 1204), became models for others.

### Averroes: the Law and demonstration

Averroes, a jurist as well as a commentator on Aristotle, wrote the *Decisive Treatise* (*Fasl al-maqal*) as a legal opinion on whether the study of philosophy is permitted by Islamic law. His argument can be reconstructed as follows.

| P1 | The Law encourages reflection on beings, in order to learn about their maker. |
| P2 | The most complete form of such reflection is demonstration, that is, philosophy. |
| C1 | So the Law commands or permits the study of philosophy by those able to do it. |
| P3 | Truth does not contradict truth: what demonstration establishes cannot conflict with the truth of the Law. |
| P4 | Where the apparent meaning of scripture conflicts with a demonstration, the apparent meaning must be interpreted figuratively (*ta'wil*). |
| C2 | So apparent conflicts between scripture and demonstration are conflicts only in appearance. |

Averroes adds that people differ in their capacities, and that scripture speaks to all through rhetorical, dialectical and demonstrative arguments. Interpretations that rest on demonstration should not be taught to those who cannot follow them, since they may be confused. He replied in a longer work, the *Incoherence of the Incoherence*, to al-Ghazali's attack on the philosophers, the *Incoherence of the Philosophers*, in which al-Ghazali had argued that the philosophers' proofs of the world's eternity and of other doctrines fail. Averroes held that the philosophers' position was more defensible than al-Ghazali claimed. On the eternity of the world, he argued that the gap between the philosophers and their opponents is narrower than it appears, since both agree that God is the cause of the world and differ over whether the world has a beginning in time. Averroes himself sided with the philosophers: he held that the world is eternal, understood as depending at every moment on God as its cause, and he thought that this does not conflict with the Law properly interpreted. The critics' main objection is to P4: who decides when a demonstration has succeeded, and may a believer be asked to reinterpret scripture?

### Maimonides: reason, scripture and the eternity of the world

Maimonides, a rabbi and physician writing in Arabic, composed the *Guide of the Perplexed* for readers educated in philosophy and troubled by the apparent conflict between it and the Torah. On the **eternity of the world** he distinguishes three views: (1) the world was created out of nothing by God, which is the view of the Torah as he understands it; (2) the world was formed from eternal matter, as in Plato's *Timaeus*; (3) the world, matter and all, is eternal, as in Aristotle. His treatment (*Guide* II.13–25) has this shape.

| P1 | If the eternity of the world were demonstrated, the apparent sense of scripture would have to be reinterpreted, as it has been where it seems to ascribe a body to God. |
| P2 | Aristotle's arguments for eternity describe how the world works now, and cannot establish how it began: the present nature of a thing is no guide to its origin. |
| P3 | The arguments of the theologians for creation are not demonstrations either. |
| P4 | When neither side has a demonstration, the view supported by scripture, and not refuted by reason, may reasonably be accepted. |
| C | So it is reasonable to hold that the world was created. |

Notice that Maimonides does not claim to prove creation. He says the question is open to reason, and decides on other grounds. Interpreters disagree about how seriously he meant this, and some think he privately held a different view; the exegetical question is still discussed, so treat it as one. Premise P2 is his best-known point: he compares the case to a boy raised from birth by a father on an island who tries to work out how he was born from his present observations of the world.

### Maimonides on divine attributes

Maimonides also argues that God is absolutely simple. If God were composed of a nature and attributes such as wisdom, that would introduce plurality in God. So statements such as "God is wise" must not be read as giving a positive description of God; they say what God is not (God is not ignorant) or describe God's effects. This is the **negative theology** of the *Guide* (I.50–60). A standard objection is that negation alone seems unable to distinguish God from nothing at all; a standard reply is that each further negation narrows our grasp.

### The Avicennian background

Both thinkers lived in a world shaped by Avicenna's doctrine that, apart from the necessary being, essence is distinct from existence. Averroes rejected the view that existence is a feature added to an essence, and an important strand of the later Latin tradition followed one side or the other.

@example
title: Applying Averroes's rule of interpretation
problem: A text of scripture, read literally, says that the world began at a definite time. Suppose that someone claimed to have a demonstration that the world is eternal. According to Averroes's argument in the *Decisive Treatise*, what should follow?
step: By premise P3, truth does not contradict truth, so a true demonstration cannot really conflict with a true scripture.
step: By premise P4, where the apparent sense conflicts with a demonstration, the sense must be interpreted figuratively.
step: But the rule is conditional on there being a genuine *demonstration*. Averroes did not say that any argument, only that conclusive demonstration, can require reinterpretation.
answer: If the demonstration is genuine, the apparent meaning of the verse should be interpreted figuratively; if not, no reinterpretation is required.

@example
title: Using Avicenna's distinction
problem: A student says: "A unicorn is something whose existence is part of what it is to be a unicorn, because anything that is a unicorn exists." Use Avicenna's distinction between essence and existence to say what is wrong with this.
step: Identify the claim: existence belongs to the essence of a unicorn.
step: Apply Avicenna's test: grasping what a unicorn is, a horse-like animal with one horn, does not by itself tell us that there are unicorns. If existence belonged to the essence, grasping the essence would include grasping its existence.
step: The student's reasoning runs together "if something is a unicorn, it exists" (a claim about any unicorn that is actual) with "existence is part of the nature of a unicorn". The first can be true of whatever is actual, without the second being true.
answer: Avicenna would say that existence is not part of the essence of a unicorn, because the essence can be understood without settling whether any unicorn exists.

@mistakes
- Saying that Averroes held a "double truth" theory, that something can be true in philosophy and false in religion: he held that truth does not contradict truth, and that apparent conflicts are resolved by interpretation.
- Saying that Maimonides proved creation: he argued that eternity was not demonstrated and that creation is supported by scripture and is reasonable.
- Reading Maimonides's negative theology as saying that God is nothing: it holds that positive attributes mislead and that what we can say truly is what God is not.
- Treating the three views of the world's origin as only two: Maimonides distinguishes creation out of nothing, formation from eternal matter and the eternity of the world.
