@article
Is there anything that Socrates and Plato both are, other than Socrates and Plato themselves? For William of Ockham (about 1287–1347) the answer is no. Everything that exists is an individual, and no universal is a thing outside the mind. He draws this out against a background of realist views, above all those of Scotus, and in doing so sets the terms in which later philosophers discuss universals, parsimony and the cost of positing entities.

### Ockham's account of universals

Ockham accepts that there are general words and concepts: "man" applies to Socrates and to Plato. A **universal**, he says, is a sign that signifies many individuals: either a word in a spoken or written language, which signifies only by convention, or a **concept** in the mind, which signifies naturally. A concept is what makes a general word apply. In his mature view, a concept is a mental act or quality of the mind, itself an individual thing, which is "universal" only in the way it signifies many things. On this view the world contains only individual substances and their individual qualities. This is often called **nominalism**, and it is sometimes called conceptualism or terminism, because the generality belongs to signs. The labels differ in how they stress words and concepts, and some historians reserve "nominalist" for the later movement that followed Ockham.

Two consequences are worth noticing. First, there is no need to ask what makes two men share a nature, because they share nothing beyond being similar in the way that makes the concept apply; Ockham does not explain similarity by anything further. Second, knowledge is of individuals, but science is expressed in universal terms, which stand for (*supponunt pro*) the individuals they signify.

### The argument against realism

Ockham argues against Scotus's moderate realism. What follows is a reconstruction, in standard form, of the core of his argument; it is not his own wording, and scholars reconstruct it in different ways.

| P1 | The common nature (humanity) that is in Socrates is really identical with Socrates, and the common nature in Plato is really identical with Plato. |
| P2 | If the common nature is one and the same thing in both, then it is one thing that is really identical with Socrates and with Plato. |
| P3 | Things that are really identical with one and the same thing are identical with each other. |
| P4 | Socrates is not identical with Plato. |
| C | So there is no one common nature that is in both: either the nature in Socrates is a different thing from the nature in Plato, in which case nothing real is common, or there is no real common nature at all. |

The argument uses the principle that if A is identical with B and B is identical with C then A is identical with C. A realist reply, in Scotus's spirit, is that the nature and the individual difference (haecceity) are really one thing but only **formally** distinct, two formalities of one thing; the nature has a lesser unity than numerical unity, so being really one with Socrates does not make it numerically identical with Socrates, and the transitivity step does not apply to it. Ockham answers that this distinction is itself unintelligible: if two items are really the same thing, then anything true of the one is true of the other, and one cannot be common while the other is not. The dispute, which is a good case for studying how Leibniz's Law (the indiscernibility of identicals) is applied to formal distinctions, still divides philosophers.

### The razor

Ockham is associated with the maxim that plurality should not be posited without necessity (*pluralitas non est ponenda sine necessitate*), now called **Ockham's razor**. The wording "entities are not to be multiplied beyond necessity" is a later formulation, and the idea was not new to him. In his hands, it works as a principle about explanation: do not assume more kinds of things than are required to explain what we observe. He applied it to reduce Aristotle's list of categories: he argued that we need not suppose that quantity, relation and motion are things distinct from substances and qualities, since talk of them can be paraphrased in terms of individuals.

The razor is a rule of **parsimony**, and its status needs care. It is not a proof that the simpler theory is true. Two standard understandings are that it gives a reason to prefer the simpler of two theories that explain the data equally well, and that it guides where to put the burden of proof. A realist can reply that universals are not an extra cost, since they are needed to explain resemblance and the truth of general laws; Ockham's reply is that the explanation is not required. A good use of the razor must say what is explained and what the extra entity adds.

### Other connections

Ockham also holds that God's power is limited only by contradiction, and that many things that seem necessary are in fact contingent, a theme that affects his treatment of foreknowledge, which we meet in the next lesson. His concern for what can be said with the fewest commitments echoes in modern discussions of ontological commitment.

@example
title: Applying the razor to a case
problem: A theory says that there is a universal "redness" that all red things share. A rival theory says that there are only red individual things and a concept "red" that applies to them. Explain how Ockham would apply the razor, and what the realist can say in reply.
step: Compare what each theory posits: the first posits individuals and universals; the second only individuals and concepts, which exist in the mind.
step: Ockham would say that if the second theory explains why we call things red, then the extra entity, redness, is not needed, so it should not be posited.
step: The realist replies that the second theory leaves unexplained why things resemble each other in the way that makes "red" apply, so the universal is needed to explain something.
answer: Ockham prefers the theory with fewer entities because it explains the same facts; the realist denies that the facts are explained without universals. The razor does not settle the question until it is clear what needs explaining.

@example
title: Following the identity argument
problem: In Ockham's argument against a really common nature, say which premise a realist who uses the formal distinction rejects, and what she says.
step: The target is P1 read as numerical identity (the nature is the very same thing as each individual), and the use of P3 that depends on it.
step: The realist says that the nature is really one with the individual difference in a single thing but formally distinct from it, a weaker relation than numerical identity, so the nature can be common to many while each individual is numerically distinct from the others.
step: Ockham replies that this makes the nature both the same as and different from the individual, a position he says is unintelligible; the realist replies that formal distinction is a weaker relation than real distinction, not a contradiction.
answer: The realist rejects the reading of P1 on which the nature is numerically identical with each individual (and so the use of P3), by appeal to formal distinction.

@mistakes
- Saying that Ockham denies there are general words or concepts: he holds that they exist and are signs, but not things outside the mind.
- Treating the razor as a proof that the simpler theory is true: it is a methodological principle about what to posit.
- Attributing to Ockham the exact slogan "entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity": the common formulation is later, though the idea is his.
- Treating nominalism as the claim that there are no similarities: Ockham accepts that individuals resemble one another and denies that resemblance requires a universal.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, compare Ockham's nominalism with Boethius's solution to the problem of universals.
hint: Both avoid a single thing in many places; they differ over whether there is a real likeness in things that grounds the concept.
solution: Boethius and Ockham both deny that a universal is a single thing wholly present in many individuals. Boethius holds that the likeness among individuals is real in them, as something sensible, and that the mind, by abstraction, grasps it as universal; the universal is thus in things and in the mind in different ways. Ockham holds that only individuals exist, that a universal is a sign that signifies many of them, and that no common nature exists in things. He thus rejects what a realist reading of Boethius treats as the real basis of the concept: the likeness in individuals is just their resemblance, with no further entity. They differ, then, on whether the resemblance needs a real, shared basis, and Ockham's use of the razor makes his view the more austere.
