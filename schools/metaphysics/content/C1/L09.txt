@article
Is the morning star the same planet as the evening star? "Venus is Venus" is trivial; "the morning star is the evening star" was a discovery. First-order logic treats both with a special two-place predicate, **identity**, written $=$. The sentence $a = b$ is true when the names $a$ and $b$ refer to one and the same object, and $\neg\, a = b$ says they refer to different objects. (We write the negation of an identity as $\neg\, a = b$, with the negation sign in front.) Unlike $F$, $G$ and $R$, the identity sign has a fixed meaning in every interpretation.

### Leibniz's Law

The principle of the **indiscernibility of identicals**, usually called **Leibniz's Law**, says that if $a = b$, then whatever is true of $a$ is true of $b$. It is the basis of the rule $=$E in natural deduction, and of countless philosophical arguments: to show that $a \neq b$, find something true of $a$ and not of $b$. It must be kept apart from the much more contested **identity of indiscernibles**, which says that things sharing all their properties are identical.

### Counting with identity

Without identity, we cannot say how many things there are. With it we can.

- At least one $F$: $\exists x\,Fx$.
- At least two $F$s: $\exists x\exists y(Fx \wedge Fy \wedge \neg\, x = y)$.
- At least three: $\exists x\exists y\exists z(Fx \wedge Fy \wedge Fz \wedge \neg\, x = y \wedge \neg\, x = z \wedge \neg\, y = z)$.
- At most one: $\forall x\forall y((Fx \wedge Fy) \to x = y)$.
- At most two: $\forall x\forall y\forall z((Fx \wedge Fy \wedge Fz) \to (x = y \vee x = z \vee y = z))$.
- Exactly one: $\exists x(Fx \wedge \forall y(Fy \to y = x))$, equivalent to "at least one and at most one".
- Exactly two: $\exists x\exists y(Fx \wedge Fy \wedge \neg\, x = y \wedge \forall z(Fz \to (z = x \vee z = y)))$.

"At least $n$" uses $n$ existentials and all the distinctness claims; "at most $n$" uses $n + 1$ universals and says that some two coincide; "exactly $n$" combines the two.

### Definite descriptions

"The present king of France is bald." What is its subject? Russell analyzed a sentence "the $F$ is $G$" as saying three things: there is an $F$; there is at most one $F$; and it is $G$:

$$\exists x(Fx \wedge \forall y(Fy \to y = x) \wedge Gx).$$

On this analysis a description is not a name but a quantified claim, and the sentence is false if there is no $F$, or more than one. Negation can then take wide or narrow scope. "The $F$ is not $G$" can mean $\exists x(Fx \wedge \forall y(Fy \to y = x) \wedge \neg Gx)$ (there is exactly one $F$ and it is not $G$) or $\neg\exists x(Fx \wedge \forall y(Fy \to y = x) \wedge Gx)$ (it is not the case that the $F$ is $G$). If nothing is $F$, the first is false and the second true.

### An argument with Leibniz's Law

The classic puzzle of the statue and the clay:

| P1 | The lump of clay existed before the sculptor shaped it. |
| P2 | The statue did not exist before the sculptor shaped it. |
| C | So the lump of clay is not identical to the statue. |

Write $Fx$ for "$x$ existed before the shaping", $l$ for the lump and $s$ for the statue. The form is $Fl$, $\neg Fs$, so $\neg\, l = s$. It is valid by Leibniz's Law: if $l = s$, then whatever is true of $l$ would be true of $s$, so $Fs$, contradicting P2. Both premises do work. The best objection denies P2: the statue existed before it was a statue, as the same lump of clay, and only acquired its shape. The reply is that this makes "statue" a way the clay can be rather than a thing in its own right, and many find it odd that a statue could exist in the form of a shapeless lump. Others accept the conclusion and say that the lump and the statue are two things in the same place, which raises the further question of how two things can share all their matter. The logic is not in dispute; the question is which premise to give up.

@example
title: Counting claims
problem: Domain: all things. $Px$: $x$ is a planet. Symbolize (a) "There are at least two planets." (b) "There is at most one planet." (c) "There are exactly two planets."
step: (a) Two existentials with a distinctness clause, so that the two planets are different: $\exists x\exists y(Px \wedge Py \wedge \neg\, x = y)$.
step: (b) Any two planets are the same one: $\forall x\forall y((Px \wedge Py) \to x = y)$.
step: (c) At least two planets, and every planet is one of them: $\exists x\exists y(Px \wedge Py \wedge \neg\, x = y \wedge \forall z(Pz \to (z = x \vee z = y)))$.
answer: (a) $\exists x\exists y(Px \wedge Py \wedge \neg\, x = y)$; (b) $\forall x\forall y((Px \wedge Py) \to x = y)$; (c) $\exists x\exists y(Px \wedge Py \wedge \neg\, x = y \wedge \forall z(Pz \to (z = x \vee z = y)))$.

@example
title: Scope in descriptions
problem: Domain: all people. $Kx$: $x$ keeps the lighthouse; $Sx$: $x$ is asleep. Symbolize "The lighthouse keeper is not asleep" on both readings, and say which one is true if the lighthouse has no keeper.
step: Russell's analysis of "the keeper is asleep" is $\exists x(Kx \wedge \forall y(Ky \to y = x) \wedge Sx)$.
step: Narrow-scope negation puts $\neg$ on the predicate: $\exists x(Kx \wedge \forall y(Ky \to y = x) \wedge \neg Sx)$. It says there is exactly one keeper and that keeper is awake.
step: Wide-scope negation negates the whole analysis: $\neg\exists x(Kx \wedge \forall y(Ky \to y = x) \wedge Sx)$. It says that it is not the case that there is exactly one keeper who is asleep.
step: If there is no keeper, the narrow reading is false (it claims there is one) and the wide reading is true (there is no sleeping keeper).
answer: Narrow: $\exists x(Kx \wedge \forall y(Ky \to y = x) \wedge \neg Sx)$, false. Wide: $\neg\exists x(Kx \wedge \forall y(Ky \to y = x) \wedge Sx)$, true.

@mistakes
- Writing "at least two" without the distinctness clause: $\exists x\exists y(Fx \wedge Fy)$ is true when there is only one $F$ (take $x$ and $y$ to be the same object).
- Confusing "at most one" with "exactly one": "at most one" is also true when there is none.
- Treating "the $F$" as a name. Russell's analysis makes the sentence false, not meaningless, if there is no $F$.
- Confusing Leibniz's Law (identicals are indiscernible) with the identity of indiscernibles (indiscernibles are identical), which is much more controversial.

prompt: Domain: all things. $Mx$: $x$ is a moon. Which symbolizes "There is at most one moon"?
choice*: $\forall x\forall y((Mx \wedge My) \to x = y)$
choice: $\exists x\exists y(Mx \wedge My \wedge \neg\, x = y)$
choice: $\exists x(Mx \wedge \forall y(My \to y = x))$
choice: $\forall x(Mx \to \forall y(My \to \neg\, x = y))$
hint: "At most one" says that any two moons are the same moon.
solution: "At most one moon" says that any two things that are moons are identical: $\forall x\forall y((Mx \wedge My) \to x = y)$. The sentence $\exists x\exists y(Mx \wedge My \wedge \neg\, x = y)$ says at least two, and $\exists x(Mx \wedge \forall y(My \to y = x))$ says exactly one (it requires a moon to exist). The sentence $\forall x(Mx \to \forall y(My \to \neg\, x = y))$ is false whenever there is a moon, since taking $y = x$ gives $\neg\, x = x$.

@practice num
prompt: The domain has four objects. An interpretation of a one-place predicate $F$ chooses which objects it is true of. For how many of the $2^4 = 16$ choices is "there are exactly two $F$s" true?
answer: 6
unit: interpretations
