@article
Last week's rules let you use what you have. They cannot yet *establish* a conditional or a negation from nothing. Take the claim "if it snows, the road is closed", which you want to prove from "if it snows, the pass is blocked" and "if the pass is blocked, the road is closed". The natural way is to suppose it snows and see what follows. That is the idea of a **subproof**: an assumption made for the sake of argument, whose consequences are used and then discharged.

### The rules with subproofs

| Rule | What it needs | What it gives |
|---|---|---|
| $\to$I | a subproof $i$–$j$ that assumes $A$ and ends with $B$ | $A \to B$ |
| $\leftrightarrow$I | a subproof $i$–$j$ from $A$ to $B$ and a subproof $k$–$l$ from $B$ to $A$ | $A \leftrightarrow B$ |
| $\neg$I | a subproof $i$–$j$ that assumes $A$ and ends with $\bot$ | $\neg A$ |
| IP | a subproof $i$–$j$ that assumes $\neg A$ and ends with $\bot$ | $A$ |

You cite the *whole subproof* with its first and last line numbers, written $i$–$j$. $\neg$I and IP are close cousins. $\neg$I **proves a negation**: assume $A$ and derive a contradiction. **Indirect proof** (IP) proves a sentence by assuming its negation and deriving a contradiction. IP is stronger: it is what lets you reach a sentence like $P \vee \neg P$, which has no negation to introduce. Together with the other rules, IP gives a system that proves every tautology and every valid argument.

### Strategy

Look at the goal's main connective, and work backward.

- Goal $A \to B$: assume $A$, aim for $B$, and finish with $\to$I.
- Goal $A \leftrightarrow B$: two subproofs, then $\leftrightarrow$I.
- Goal $\neg A$: assume $A$, aim for $\bot$, finish with $\neg$I.
- Goal $A \wedge B$: prove each, then $\wedge$I.
- Goal $A \vee B$: try to prove one disjunct and use $\vee$I; if that is impossible, use IP and assume $\neg(A \vee B)$.
- Any other goal: if you are stuck, assume its negation and aim for $\bot$ with IP.

Also work forward: use $\wedge$E on every conjunction premise, and use $\to$E when you have an antecedent. Keep a clear list of what you have and what you need.

### An argument to prove

Epicurus's argument that death is not bad for the one who dies, put in a standard form, is:

| P1 | If death is bad for a person, there is a time at which it is bad for her. |
| P2 | If there is a time at which death is bad for her, then she exists at a time at which she is dead. |
| P3 | She never exists at a time at which she is dead. |
| C | So death is not bad for her. |

With $D$: "death is bad for her", $T$: "there is a time at which it is bad for her", $E$: "she exists at a time at which she is dead", the form is $D \to T$, $T \to E$, $\neg E$, so $\neg D$.

```text
1 | D → T        PR
2 | T → E        PR
3 | ¬E           PR
  |---
4 | | D          AS
5 | | T          →E 1, 4
6 | | E          →E 2, 5
7 | | ⊥          ¬E 6, 3
8 | ¬D           ¬I 4–7
```

The argument is valid, so the debate is over the premises. The best objection, associated with Nagel's **deprivation account**, rejects P2 (or the idea behind it): death is bad for a person because it deprives her of the good she would otherwise have had, and a deprivation can be bad without there being any time at which the person exists in a bad state. The reply is that this requires a subject, a time and a harm, and it needs an account of whom the deprivation harms and when. Whether that reply succeeds is a question for philosophy of death; the formalization shows exactly where it must be fought.

@example
title: Conditional proof
problem: Prove $P \to R$ from the premises $P \to Q$ and $Q \to R$.
step: The goal is a conditional, so assume its antecedent $P$ in a subproof and aim for $R$.
step: Inside the subproof, use $\to$E twice: $P \to Q$ with $P$ gives $Q$, and then $Q \to R$ with $Q$ gives $R$.
step: Close the subproof and apply $\to$I to lines 3–5.
```text
1 | P → Q        PR
2 | Q → R        PR
  |---
3 | | P          AS
4 | | Q          →E 1, 3
5 | | R          →E 2, 4
6 | P → R        →I 3–5
```
answer: $P \to Q$, $Q \to R$ $\vdash P \to R$, as proved.

@example
title: Indirect proof of excluded middle
problem: Prove $P \vee \neg P$ from no premises.
step: The goal is a disjunction and neither disjunct can be proved from nothing, so use IP: assume $\neg(P \vee \neg P)$ and aim for $\bot$.
step: Inside, assume $P$. Then $P \vee \neg P$ follows by $\vee$I, and it contradicts the assumption, so $\neg$I gives $\neg P$ on line 5.
step: From $\neg P$, apply $\vee$I again to get $P \vee \neg P$ on line 6, contradicting the assumption a second time. That yields $\bot$ and closes the IP subproof.
```text
  |---
1 | | ¬(P ∨ ¬P)    AS
2 | | | P          AS
3 | | | P ∨ ¬P     ∨I 2
4 | | | ⊥          ¬E 3, 1
5 | | ¬P           ¬I 2–4
6 | | P ∨ ¬P       ∨I 5
7 | | ⊥            ¬E 6, 1
8 | P ∨ ¬P         IP 1–7
```
answer: $\vdash P \vee \neg P$, as proved.

@mistakes
- Using a line from inside a closed subproof outside it. After $\to$I, only the conditional on the later line is available.
- Confusing $\neg$I and IP: $\neg$I assumes $A$ to prove $\neg A$; IP assumes $\neg A$ to prove $A$.
- Forgetting to end an indirect subproof with $\bot$: both $\neg$I and IP need $\bot$ as the last line.
- Assuming the conclusion you want to prove, as if it were a premise. Assumptions are only for subproofs and are discharged by the rule that closes them.

@practice num
prompt: The sentence $P \to (Q \to P)$ is provable from no premises, so by soundness it is a tautology. In how many of the four rows of its truth table, with letters $P$ and $Q$, is it true?
answer: 4
unit: rows
check: sum(1 for r in tt_rows(2) if implies(r[0], implies(r[1], r[0])))
hint: Soundness says that everything provable from no premises is true on every row.
solution: A sentence provable from no premises is true on every row, by soundness. A direct check agrees: the inner conditional $Q \to P$ is true whenever $P$ is true, so the whole sentence is true on all 4 rows.

@practice self

```text
1 | (P ∧ Q) → R      PR
  |---
2 | | P              AS
3 | | | Q            AS
4 | | | P ∧ Q        ∧I 2, 3
5 | | | R            →E 1, 4
6 | | Q → R          →I 3–5

```

rubric: Builds $P \wedge Q$ and uses $\to$E to reach $R$ inside the inner subproof.
rubric: Closes the two subproofs with $\to$I, citing the correct line ranges.
