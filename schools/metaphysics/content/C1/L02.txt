@article
Suppose a note says: "Ana will bring the map, unless it rains." Does it say that Ana brings the map if it does not rain? That she does not bring it if it does rain? Everyday sentences are loose, and a formal language is useful because it is not. To use it, we must translate: pick a **symbolization key** that assigns a sentence letter to each atomic English sentence, then rebuild the English sentence out of connectives.

### The connectives as truth functions

Each connective is **truth-functional**: the truth value of a compound is fixed by the truth values of its parts. That is the whole meaning we give them.

| $A$ | $B$ | $\neg A$ | $A \wedge B$ | $A \vee B$ | $A \to B$ | $A \leftrightarrow B$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | F | T | T | T | T |
| T | F | F | F | T | F | F |
| F | T | T | F | T | T | F |
| F | F | T | F | F | T | T |

The disjunction is **inclusive**: $A \vee B$ is true when both are true. The conditional $A \to B$ is the **material conditional**: it is false in exactly one case, when $A$ is true and $B$ false. $A$ is its **antecedent** and $B$ its **consequent**.

### Translating

Some English phrases map cleanly. "And", "but", "although" and "however" all go to $\wedge$ (the difference between "and" and "but" is not truth-functional). "$A$ if $B$" is $B \to A$. "$A$ only if $B$" is $A \to B$: it forbids $A$ without $B$. "$A$ if and only if $B$" is $A \leftrightarrow B$. "Neither $A$ nor $B$" is $\neg(A \vee B)$, or equivalently $\neg A \wedge \neg B$. "Not both $A$ and $B$" is $\neg(A \wedge B)$, which is different. "$A$ unless $B$" is $A \vee B$: it is true when $A$ is true, or when $B$ is true, and at least one of them must hold. It is also equivalent to $\neg B \to A$.

Always find the **main connective** first. "Ana brings the map and Ben brings the compass or it rains" is ambiguous, with two readings: $(A \wedge B) \vee R$ and $A \wedge (B \vee R)$. Translation exposes the ambiguity, and you must choose.

### Is the material conditional "if"?

An argument often raised against the translation of "if" as $\to$:

| P1 | An English conditional "if $A$, then $B$" is true only when there is some connection between $A$ and $B$. |
| P2 | $A \to B$ is true whenever $A$ is false, whether or not there is any connection between $A$ and $B$. |
| P3 | A correct translation of a sentence is true in exactly the situations in which the original is true. |
| C | So $A \to B$ is not a correct translation of "if $A$, then $B$". |

The argument is valid. P1 and P2 make the two sentences differ in truth value in some situation (false $A$, no connection), and P3 then forbids calling the translation correct. P3 is a bridge premise; the dispute is over P1. Critics point to "If the moon is made of cheese, then 2 + 2 = 5", which sounds false because nothing connects the parts. The standard reply is Grice's: a conditional is only *conversationally* odd, not false, when asserted without a connection, because a speaker who knew that $A$ was false would ordinarily just say so, and the connection is implied, not part of the truth conditions. Others accept the bridge premise P3 and instead give conditionals non-truth-functional truth conditions, in terms of the closest situations in which the antecedent is true: this is Stalnaker's proposal for conditionals, and Lewis's for counterfactual conditionals. In this course $\to$ is the material conditional, the weakest truth-functional reading on which modus ponens is valid.

@example
title: Symbolizing with a key
problem: Use the key $A$: Ana brings the map; $B$: Ben brings the compass; $R$: it rains. Symbolize (a) "Ana brings the map only if Ben brings the compass." (b) "Unless it rains, Ben brings the compass." (c) "Neither does Ana bring the map nor does Ben bring the compass." (d) "Ana brings the map and Ben brings the compass or it rains", on both readings.
step: (a) "$A$ only if $B$" forbids the map without the compass: $A \to B$. The mistake to avoid is $B \to A$, which would be "Ana brings the map if Ben brings the compass".
step: (b) "$X$ unless $Y$" is $X \vee Y$ with $X$ the main clause: "Ben brings the compass, unless it rains" gives $B \vee R$. Equivalent: $\neg R \to B$.
step: (c) "Neither $A$ nor $B$" is true exactly when both are false: $\neg(A \vee B)$, or $\neg A \wedge \neg B$.
step: (d) Grouping the first two clauses gives $(A \wedge B) \vee R$; grouping the last two gives $A \wedge (B \vee R)$. The two differ when $A$ is false and $R$ true: the first is then true, the second false.
answer: (a) $A \to B$; (b) $B \vee R$; (c) $\neg(A \vee B)$; (d) $(A \wedge B) \vee R$ or $A \wedge (B \vee R)$.

@example
title: Computing a compound
problem: With $P$ true, $Q$ false and $R$ true, find the truth value of $(P \to Q) \leftrightarrow \neg(R \vee Q)$.
step: Compute the left side: $P \to Q$ with $P$ true and $Q$ false is false.
step: Compute the right side: $R \vee Q$ is true, so $\neg(R \vee Q)$ is false.
step: The main connective is $\leftrightarrow$, and it is true when both sides have the same value: false and false.
answer: True.

@mistakes
- Symbolizing "$A$ only if $B$" as $B \to A$. "Only if" introduces the necessary condition: $A$ cannot hold without $B$, so it is $A \to B$.
- Treating "neither $A$ nor $B$" as $\neg A \vee \neg B$, which is "not both". Neither means both fail: $\neg(A \vee B)$.
- Symbolizing "$A$ unless $B$" as a biconditional. The usual reading is just $A \vee B$; add the exclusion of "both" only when the sentence clearly means "one or the other, not both".
- Dropping parentheses so that the main connective is unclear; "A and B or C" needs a decision.

choice: $S \to D$
choice: $D \leftrightarrow S$
choice: $D \wedge S$

@practice ms
prompt: Using the key $L$: the door is locked; $W$: the window is closed, which of these are correct symbolizations of "Neither the door is locked nor the window is closed"? Select all that apply.
choice*: $\neg(L \vee W)$
choice*: $\neg L \wedge \neg W$
choice: $\neg L \vee \neg W$

solution: "Neither...nor" says both are false, which is $\neg(L \vee W)$ and, equivalently, $\neg L \wedge \neg W$. The choices $\neg L \vee \neg W$ and $\neg(L \wedge W)$ are equivalent to each other and say only that at least one of the two is false.

@practice num
prompt: In how many of the eight rows of its truth table is $(P \vee Q) \to R$ true?
answer: 5
unit: rows
check: count_models(lambda p, q, r: implies(p or q, r))
hint: A conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false.
solution: The conditional is false when $P \vee Q$ is true and $R$ is false: $R$ false and at least one of $P$, $Q$ true, which is 3 rows. So it is true in $8 - 3 = 5$ rows.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which sentence is equivalent to "The ferry runs unless the sea is rough", with $F$: the ferry runs and $S$: the sea is rough?
choice*: $\neg S \to F$
choice: $F \to \neg S$
choice: $F \leftrightarrow \neg S$
choice: $S \to F$
hint: "Unless" amounts to "if not".
solution: "$F$ unless $S$" is $F \vee S$, which is equivalent to $\neg S \to F$: if the sea is not rough, the ferry runs. The choice $F \to \neg S$ says that the ferry only runs in calm seas, which is a different claim.

@quiz num
prompt: In how many of the eight rows of its truth table is $\neg(P \leftrightarrow Q) \vee R$ true?
answer: 6
unit: rows
check: count_models(lambda p, q, r: (not iff(p, q)) or r)
hint: Find the rows where the disjunction is false first.
solution: The disjunction is false only when $\neg(P \leftrightarrow Q)$ is false and $R$ is false: that is, $P$ and $Q$ agree and $R$ is false, which is 2 rows. So it is true in $8 - 2 = 6$ rows.

@quiz self
prompt: Symbolize "The vote passes if and only if a majority votes yes and the quorum is met, but it does not pass if the chair abstains and the quorum is met", giving your key, and say which connective is main in the first clause.

In the first clause the main connective is $\leftrightarrow$, with $M \wedge Q$ as its right side; "but" is translated as the conjunction joining the two claims.
