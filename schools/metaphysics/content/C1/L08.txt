@article
"Everyone loves someone." Does that mean there is one person whom everyone loves, or only that nobody is left unloving, each with their own beloved? English can leave this open. First-order logic cannot: the two readings have different symbolizations.

### Relations and nested quantifiers

A **two-place predicate** names a relation: $Lxy$ for "$x$ loves $y$", with the *order of the places fixed by the key*. $Lab$ says that $a$ loves $b$, which is not the same as $Lba$. Sentences can have several quantifiers, one inside the scope of another. The **scope** of a quantifier is the formula it applies to: in $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$, the quantifier $\exists y$ is inside the scope of $\forall x$.

### The order of quantifiers

Compare the two sentences, with domain all people and $Lxy$: $x$ loves $y$.

- $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$: for every person $x$ there is a person $y$ whom $x$ loves ("everyone loves someone"). The loved person may depend on $x$.
- $\exists y\forall x\,Lxy$: there is a person $y$ such that everyone loves $y$ ("someone is loved by everyone"). One beloved works for all.

The second entails the first, since a single beloved loved by everyone is, for each person, somebody that person loves. The first does not entail the second. Swapping *unlike* quantifiers can change the meaning; swapping *like* quantifiers ($\forall x\forall y$ with $\forall y\forall x$, or $\exists x\exists y$ with $\exists y\exists x$) never does.

### Reading and writing

To symbolize, read the quantifiers in the order the English commits to them, and restrict each with a predicate where needed. "Every student read some book" becomes $\forall x(Sx \to \exists y(By \wedge Rxy))$: the universal is restricted by $Sx$ with a conditional, the existential by $By$ with a conjunction. Negations can be pushed inward by quantifier negation: "no one loves everyone" is $\neg\exists x\forall y\,Lxy$, equivalent to $\forall x\exists y\,\neg Lxy$, "everyone fails to love someone".

### The quantifier-shift fallacy

Critics of some cosmological arguments say that they commit the **quantifier-shift fallacy**, inferring $\exists y\forall x$ from $\forall x\exists y$. The argument in question, put as simply as possible with $Cyx$: $y$ causes $x$, is:

| P1 | Everything has a cause. |
| C | So there is something that is the cause of everything. |

The form is $\forall x\exists y\,Cyx$, so $\exists y\forall x\,Cyx$, and it is invalid. A countermodel: domain $\{1, 2\}$, with $C$ true of the ordered pairs $(1, 2)$ and $(2, 1)$ and no others (1 causes 2 and 2 causes 1). Then everything has a cause (2 is caused by 1, and 1 by 2), but nothing causes everything: 1 does not cause 1 and 2 does not cause 2. The point of the countermodel is purely logical: the premise leaves it open that causes differ from one thing to the next. Defenders of cosmological arguments reply that their best versions do not rest on this inference at all, but on a principle of sufficient reason together with the claim that an infinite regress of causes explains nothing, so the criticism targets a weak version. The critic's reply is to ask what, in the stronger version, rules out a chain of causes that goes back without end, each member explained by the one before; that is a question about the premises, and the logic shows where it has to be answered.

@example
title: Nested quantifiers
problem: Domain: all people. $Lxy$: $x$ loves $y$. Symbolize (a) "Everyone loves someone." (b) "Someone is loved by everyone." (c) "No one loves everyone." (d) "Everyone who loves someone is loved by someone."
step: (a) For each $x$ there is some $y$ that $x$ loves: $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$.
step: (b) The existential comes first: $\exists y\forall x\,Lxy$, one $y$ loved by every $x$.
step: (c) "No one loves everyone" is the negation of "someone loves everyone": $\neg\exists x\forall y\,Lxy$, equivalent to $\forall x\exists y\,\neg Lxy$.
step: (d) The antecedent restricts the universal: $\forall x(\exists y\,Lxy \to \exists z\,Lzx)$; each quantifier gets its own variable, and the two existentials $\exists y$ and $\exists z$ may pick different people.
answer: (a) $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$; (b) $\exists y\forall x\,Lxy$; (c) $\neg\exists x\forall y\,Lxy$; (d) $\forall x(\exists y\,Lxy \to \exists z\,Lzx)$.

@example
title: Restricted nested quantifiers
problem: Domain: all things. $Sx$: $x$ is a student; $Bx$: $x$ is a book; $Rxy$: $x$ read $y$. Symbolize (a) "Every student read some book." (b) "Some book was read by every student."
step: (a) Restrict the universal by $Sx$ with a conditional and the existential by $By$ with a conjunction: $\forall x(Sx \to \exists y(By \wedge Rxy))$.
step: (b) The book comes first, as one book for all students: $\exists y(By \wedge \forall x(Sx \to Rxy))$.
step: Sentence (b) entails (a), since the one book that every student read is, for each student, a book that student read; (a) does not entail (b), since different students may read different books.
answer: (a) $\forall x(Sx \to \exists y(By \wedge Rxy))$; (b) $\exists y(By \wedge \forall x(Sx \to Rxy))$.

@mistakes
- Swapping unlike quantifiers as if the order did not matter: $\forall x\exists y\,Rxy$ does not entail $\exists y\forall x\,Rxy$.
- Reversing the order of the places in a two-place predicate: $Lab$ ("$a$ loves $b$") and $Lba$ are different claims.
- Reusing a variable for two different quantifiers in a nested sentence. Give each quantifier its own variable when they are meant to pick different objects.
- Using a conjunction after a universal quantifier ($\forall x(Sx \wedge \ldots)$) or a conditional after an existential, as in the one-quantifier case.

@practice mc
prompt: Domain: all people. $Kxy$: $x$ knows $y$. Which symbolizes "Someone knows everyone"?
choice*: $\exists x\forall y\,Kxy$
choice: $\forall x\exists y\,Kxy$
choice: $\forall y\exists x\,Kxy$
choice: $\exists x\exists y\,Kxy$
hint: The person who knows is the first place of $K$, and the sentence mentions that person first.
solution: Someone ($x$) is such that for every $y$, $x$ knows $y$: $\exists x\forall y\,Kxy$. The sentence $\forall x\exists y\,Kxy$ says everyone knows someone, $\forall y\exists x\,Kxy$ says everyone is known by someone, and $\exists x\exists y\,Kxy$ says only that someone knows someone.

@practice ms
prompt: Domain: the natural numbers $0, 1, 2, \ldots$. $Lxy$: $x < y$. Which of these sentences are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$
choice: $\exists y\forall x\,Lxy$
choice: $\forall x\exists y\,Lyx$
choice*: $\exists x\forall y\,\neg Lyx$
choice*: $\exists x\exists y\,Lxy$
hint: Read each sentence aloud: "for every number there is a bigger one", and so on.
solution: Every number has a larger one, so $\forall x\exists y\,Lxy$ is true. No number is larger than all numbers (it would be larger than itself), so $\exists y\forall x\,Lxy$ is false. $\forall x\exists y\,Lyx$ says every number has a smaller one, which fails for 0, but $\exists x\forall y\,\neg Lyx$ ("some number has nothing below it") is true, witnessed by 0. And $\exists x\exists y\,Lxy$ is true: $0 < 1$.

@practice list
prompt: Consider this interpretation: the domain is $\{1, 2, 3, 4\}$ and $R$ is true of exactly the ordered pairs $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$, $(3, 3)$ and $(2, 4)$. List the objects $x$ for which $\exists y\,Ryx$ is true, as numbers separated by commas, or write none.
answer: 2, 3, 4

hint: $\exists y\,Ryx$ is true when something stands in $R$ to $x$: look for $x$ in the second place of a pair.
solution: The second places of the pairs are 2, 3, 3 and 4. So $\exists y\,Ryx$ is true for $x = 2$, $3$ and $4$, and false for $x = 1$, since no pair has 1 in its second place.

@practice num
prompt: The domain is $\{1, 2, 3\}$. An interpretation of a two-place predicate $R$ chooses which of the nine ordered pairs $R$ is true of. For how many of the $2^9 = 512$ interpretations is $\forall x\exists y\,Rxy$ true?
answer: 343
unit: interpretations
check: sum(1 for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=9) if all(any(bits[3 * x + y] for y in range(3)) for x in range(3)))
hint: For each $x$, the three pairs $(x, 1)$, $(x, 2)$, $(x, 3)$ must not all be false.
solution: For each of the three objects $x$, the sentence requires at least one of its three pairs $(x, y)$ to be in $R$: $2^3 - 1 = 7$ ways. The three objects choose independently, so there are $7^3 = 343$ interpretations.

@practice self
prompt: Domain: all things. $Px$: $x$ is a person; $Dx$: $x$ is a desire; $Hxy$: $x$ has $y$. Symbolize (i) "Every person has some desire", (ii) "Some desire is had by every person" and (iii) "No person has every desire". Then explain with a small model why (i) does not entail (ii).
hint: Restrict each quantifier by its predicate. For the model, use two persons and two desires.
solution: (i) $\forall x(Px \to \exists y(Dy \wedge Hxy))$. (ii) $\exists y(Dy \wedge \forall x(Px \to Hxy))$. (iii) $\neg\exists x(Px \wedge \forall y(Dy \to Hxy))$. Model: persons 1 and 2, desires 3 and 4, with $H$ true of the pairs $(1, 3)$ and $(2, 4)$. Each person has a desire, so (i) is true; but desire 3 is not had by person 2 and desire 4 is not had by person 1, so no single desire is had by every person, and (ii) is false.
rubric: Symbolizes (i) and (ii) with the quantifiers in the correct order and correct restrictions.
rubric: Symbolizes (iii) as the negation of an existential over persons with a universal over desires.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which sentence is entailed by $\exists y\forall x\,Rxy$?
choice*: $\forall x\exists y\,Rxy$
choice: $\forall y\exists x\,Rxy$
choice: $\forall x\forall y\,Rxy$
choice: $\exists x\forall y\,Rxy$
hint: If one $y$ works for every $x$, what can you say about each $x$?
solution: If some $y$ is such that every $x$ stands in $R$ to it, then each $x$ stands in $R$ to at least one object, which is $\forall x\exists y\,Rxy$. The others do not follow: take a domain $\{1, 2\}$ with $R$ true only of $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 1)$, so the premise is true (object 1 works), while $\forall y\exists x\,Rxy$ is false (nothing stands in $R$ to 2), $\forall x\forall y\,Rxy$ is false, and $\exists x\forall y\,Rxy$ is false (neither 1 nor 2 stands in $R$ to everything).

@quiz num
prompt: The domain is $\{1, 2\}$. An interpretation of a two-place predicate $R$ chooses which of the four ordered pairs $R$ is true of. For how many of the 16 interpretations is $\exists y\forall x\,Rxy$ true?
answer: 7
unit: interpretations
check: sum(1 for bits in product([0, 1], repeat=4) if any(all(bits[2 * x + y] for x in range(2)) for y in range(2)))
hint: Count the interpretations in which $y = 1$ works, those in which $y = 2$ works, and subtract the overlap.
solution: For $y = 1$ to work, $(1, 1)$ and $(2, 1)$ must be in $R$, leaving the other two pairs free: $2^2 = 4$ interpretations. The same holds for $y = 2$. Both work in exactly 1 interpretation (all four pairs). So there are $4 + 4 - 1 = 7$.
