@article
"Every philosopher is wise. Ana is a philosopher. So Ana is wise." This is valid, but not because of anything the truth-functional language can see: it would symbolize the three sentences with three unrelated letters $P$, $Q$, $R$, and $P$, $Q$, so $R$ is invalid. We need to look inside sentences. **First-order logic** (also called predicate logic or quantificational logic) does this.

### The vocabulary

- **Names** (constants): lowercase letters from the start of the alphabet, $a, b, c, \ldots$, each standing for one object.
- **Predicates**: capital letters, $F, G, H, \ldots$, with places filled by names or variables: $Fa$ ("$a$ is $F$"), $Gb$.
- **Variables**: $x, y, z, \ldots$, which hold places for objects.
- **Quantifiers**: $\forall x$ ("for every $x$") and $\exists x$ ("for some $x$", "there is an $x$ such that").
- The connectives of truth-functional logic.

A quantifier is always read relative to a **domain** (domain of discourse): the things the variables range over. Unless a key says otherwise, "everything" means everything in the domain.

### Translating

For "All $F$s are $G$s" use a conditional: $\forall x(Fx \to Gx)$. It says that every object that is $F$ is also $G$, and nothing more. For "Some $F$s are $G$s" use a conjunction: $\exists x(Fx \wedge Gx)$. Two translations that look natural are wrong:

- $\forall x(Fx \wedge Gx)$ says that *everything* is both $F$ and $G$, which is far too strong.
- $\exists x(Fx \to Gx)$ is true as soon as something is not $F$ (a false antecedent makes the conditional true), so it says almost nothing.

"No $F$ is $G$" is $\forall x(Fx \to \neg Gx)$ or, equivalently, $\neg\exists x(Fx \wedge Gx)$. "Not every $F$ is $G$" is $\neg\forall x(Fx \to Gx)$, equivalently $\exists x(Fx \wedge \neg Gx)$.

### Quantifier negation

Two equivalences hold for any predicate: $\neg\forall x\,Fx$ is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg Fx$ ("not everything is $F$" means "something is not $F$"), and $\neg\exists x\,Fx$ is equivalent to $\forall x\,\neg Fx$ ("nothing is $F$").

### An argument from traditional logic

Traditional logic, in the Aristotelian tradition, treated "All $F$s are $G$s" as implying that there are $F$s. Modern logic does not. The difference shows in this argument:

| P1 | All unicorns have horns. |
| C | So some unicorn has a horn. |

In first-order logic the form is $\forall x(Ux \to Hx)$, so $\exists x(Ux \wedge Hx)$, and it is **invalid**. A countermodel: a domain with one object, $d$, where $U$ is true of nothing. Then $Ux \to Hx$ is true for $d$ (a false antecedent), so P1 is true, while $\exists x(Ux \wedge Hx)$ is false. The premise works only as a statement about unicorns *if there are any*. The defender of the traditional reading says that general terms in ordinary reasoning are assumed to be non-empty, so that the premise carries an **existential import**. The modern reply is that this makes "All $F$s are $G$s" false when there are no $F$s, which is wrong for "All trespassers will be prosecuted" and for the laws of science, which are meant to hold in cases that never occur. Modern logic keeps the quantifier claim weak and adds $\exists x\,Ux$ as an explicit premise when it is meant.

@example
title: Symbolizing with a key
problem: Domain: all people. $Sx$: $x$ studies; $Px$: $x$ passes; $a$: Ana. Symbolize (a) "Everyone who studies passes." (b) "Someone who studies fails." (c) "Ana passes only if someone studies." (d) "Not everyone passes."
step: (a) A universal claim about a restricted group: $\forall x(Sx \to Px)$. The mistake to avoid is $\forall x(Sx \wedge Px)$, which says everyone studies and passes.
step: (b) An existential claim, with "fails" as "does not pass": $\exists x(Sx \wedge \neg Px)$.
step: (c) "$Pa$ only if $\ldots$" is a conditional $Pa \to \ldots$, and "someone studies" is $\exists x\,Sx$: $Pa \to \exists x\,Sx$.
step: (d) Negate the universal: $\neg\forall x\,Px$, which is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg Px$.
answer: (a) $\forall x(Sx \to Px)$; (b) $\exists x(Sx \wedge \neg Px)$; (c) $Pa \to \exists x\,Sx$; (d) $\neg\forall x\,Px$.

@example
title: Pushing a negation inward
problem: Find a sentence equivalent to $\neg\forall x(Sx \to Px)$ that has no negation in front of a quantifier, and say what it means in English.
step: Apply quantifier negation: $\neg\forall x\,A$ is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg A$, so $\neg\forall x(Sx \to Px)$ is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg(Sx \to Px)$.
step: A conditional is false exactly when its antecedent is true and its consequent false: $\neg(Sx \to Px)$ is equivalent to $Sx \wedge \neg Px$.
step: So the sentence is equivalent to $\exists x(Sx \wedge \neg Px)$: "Someone studies and does not pass."
answer: $\exists x(Sx \wedge \neg Px)$: some student does not pass.

@mistakes
- Translating "All $F$s are $G$s" as $\forall x(Fx \wedge Gx)$, which says that everything is both an $F$ and a $G$.
- Translating "Some $F$s are $G$s" as $\exists x(Fx \to Gx)$, which is true whenever something is not an $F$.
- Forgetting the domain: "everyone" and "everything" mean different things unless the domain is all people or you add a predicate like "is a person".
- Treating "all $F$s are $G$s" as implying that there are $F$s. In first-order logic it does not.

@practice mc
prompt: Domain: all animals. $Bx$: $x$ is a bird; $Fx$: $x$ flies. Which symbolizes "Some birds do not fly"?
choice*: $\exists x(Bx \wedge \neg Fx)$
choice: $\exists x(Bx \to \neg Fx)$
choice: $\forall x(Bx \to \neg Fx)$
choice: $\neg\exists x(Bx \wedge Fx)$
hint: "Some" goes with a conjunction.
solution: "Some birds do not fly" says that there is something that is a bird and does not fly: $\exists x(Bx \wedge \neg Fx)$. The conditional version is true if anything at all is not a bird, $\forall x(Bx \to \neg Fx)$ says that no bird flies, and $\neg\exists x(Bx \wedge Fx)$ says the same.

@practice ms
prompt: Domain: all animals. $Px$: $x$ is a penguin; $Fx$: $x$ flies. Which of these are equivalent to "No penguin flies"? Select all that apply.
choice*: $\forall x(Px \to \neg Fx)$
choice*: $\neg\exists x(Px \wedge Fx)$
choice*: $\forall x(\neg Px \vee \neg Fx)$
choice: $\exists x(Px \wedge \neg Fx)$
choice: $\neg\forall x(Px \to Fx)$
hint: Say what each sentence says in words, and push negations inward.
solution: "No penguin flies" can be put as $\forall x(Px \to \neg Fx)$, as the negation of "some penguin flies", $\neg\exists x(Px \wedge Fx)$, and as $\forall x(\neg Px \vee \neg Fx)$, which is equivalent to the first by the equivalence of $A \to B$ with $\neg A \vee B$. The other two say only that some penguin does not fly: $\exists x(Px \wedge \neg Fx)$ is that directly, and $\neg\forall x(Px \to Fx)$ is equivalent to it.

@practice list
prompt: Consider this interpretation. The domain is $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$; $F$ is true of 1, 2, 3 and 4; $G$ is true of 2, 4 and 6. List the objects $d$ in the domain for which $Fd \to Gd$ is false, as numbers separated by commas, or write none.
answer: 1, 3
check: [d for d in range(1, 7) if d in {1, 2, 3, 4} and d not in {2, 4, 6}]
hint: A conditional is false when its antecedent is true and its consequent false.
solution: $Fd \to Gd$ is false exactly for objects that are $F$ but not $G$. The objects that are $F$ are 1, 2, 3 and 4, and of these 2 and 4 are $G$. So 1 and 3 are the only counterexamples, and $\forall x(Fx \to Gx)$ is false in this interpretation.

@practice num
prompt: The domain has four objects. An interpretation of a one-place predicate $F$ chooses which of the four objects it is true of (any subset, including none or all). For how many of these choices is $\exists x\,Fx \wedge \exists x\,\neg Fx$ true?
answer: 14
unit: interpretations
check: sum(1 for s in product([0, 1], repeat=4) if any(s) and not all(s))
hint: The sentence needs at least one object that is $F$ and at least one that is not.
solution: There are $2^4 = 16$ subsets. The sentence fails only when $F$ is true of nothing (the empty subset) or of everything, so it is true on $16 - 2 = 14$ subsets.

@practice self
prompt: Domain: all beings. $Mx$: $x$ is a mind; $Px$: $x$ is physical. Symbolize (i) "Every mind is physical", (ii) "Some mind is not physical" and (iii) "It is not the case that every mind is physical". Then say which pairs among the three are equivalent or contradictory, and why.
hint: Apply quantifier negation to (iii).
solution: (i) $\forall x(Mx \to Px)$. (ii) $\exists x(Mx \wedge \neg Px)$. (iii) $\neg\forall x(Mx \to Px)$. By quantifier negation and the equivalence of $\neg(A \to B)$ with $A \wedge \neg B$, (iii) is equivalent to $\exists x\,\neg(Mx \to Px)$, which is equivalent to (ii). Sentences (i) and (ii) are contradictory: on every interpretation exactly one is true, since (ii) is (iii), the negation of (i). The translations take no side on whether any mind is physical: they only state the claims.
rubric: Gives $\forall x(Mx \to Px)$, $\exists x(Mx \wedge \neg Px)$ and $\neg\forall x(Mx \to Px)$ for the three sentences.
rubric: Shows that (iii) and (ii) are equivalent by quantifier negation and the behavior of $\neg(A \to B)$.
rubric: Concludes that (i) and (ii) are contradictory and gives the reason.

@quiz mc
prompt: Domain: all animals. $Bx$: $x$ is a bird; $Fx$: $x$ flies. Which symbolizes "Only birds fly" (nothing that is not a bird flies)?
choice*: $\forall x(Fx \to Bx)$
choice: $\forall x(Bx \to Fx)$
choice: $\forall x(Bx \wedge Fx)$
choice: $\exists x(Bx \wedge Fx)$
hint: "Only $B$s are $F$" means being $F$ requires being $B$.
solution: "Only birds fly" says that anything that flies is a bird: $\forall x(Fx \to Bx)$. The choice $\forall x(Bx \to Fx)$ says that all birds fly, which is a different claim.

@quiz num
prompt: The domain has three objects. An interpretation chooses, for each of the two one-place predicates $F$ and $G$, which objects it is true of. For how many of the $4^3 = 64$ choices is $\forall x(Fx \to Gx)$ true?
answer: 27
unit: interpretations
check: sum(1 for fg in product(product([0, 1], repeat=2), repeat=3) if all((not f) or g for f, g in fg))
hint: Each object has four possibilities for the pair ($Fx$, $Gx$). How many of them make $Fx \to Gx$ true?
solution: Each object independently has four possible pairs of values, and three of them make the conditional true (all except $F$ true with $G$ false). The universal sentence is true when all three objects do, so there are $3 \times 3 \times 3 = 27$ interpretations.

@quiz self
prompt: Show that "Some student passed the exam. Ana is a student. So Ana passed the exam." is invalid in first-order logic by giving a countermodel. Then say what extra premise would make the argument valid.
hint: Use two objects: let the student who passed be someone other than Ana.
solution: Use the domain $\{1, 2\}$ with $a$ naming 1, and the key $Sx$: $x$ is a student; $Px$: $x$ passed. The form is $\exists x(Sx \wedge Px)$, $Sa$, so $Pa$. Let $S$ be true of 1 and 2 and $P$ be true of 2 only. Then $\exists x(Sx \wedge Px)$ is true (witness 2) and $Sa$ is true, but $Pa$ is false because 1 is not in the extension of $P$. So the argument is invalid. An extra premise that repairs it is "every student passed", $\forall x(Sx \to Px)$, or "Ana is the only student", $\forall x(Sx \to x = a)$.
rubric: Symbolizes the argument as $\exists x(Sx \wedge Px)$, $Sa$, so $Pa$.
rubric: Gives a countermodel in which a different object from the one named by $a$ is the student who passed, and checks that both premises are true and the conclusion false.
rubric: Names a suitable extra premise, such as that every student passed or that Ana is the only student.
