@article
A truth table tests an argument by brute force. A **proof** shows *why* a conclusion follows, by a chain of small, obviously correct steps. In **natural deduction** each connective has an **introduction rule** (how to establish a sentence with that main connective) and an **elimination rule** (what you may infer from one). We write $\Gamma \vdash A$ ("$A$ is provable from $\Gamma$") when there is a proof of $A$ from premises $\Gamma$. A proof is trustworthy because it can be checked line by line: the proof system is **sound**, which means that everything provable is valid. (We return to this in week 12.)

### How a proof is laid out

A proof is a numbered list of lines. Premises come first, marked PR, above a short horizontal rule. Each later line is a sentence with a **justification**: the rule used and the line numbers it cites. A **subproof** starts with an **assumption** (AS) and is marked off by an extra vertical bar; lines inside it may use earlier lines outside, but once a subproof is closed, its lines are not available to later lines, only the subproof as a whole.

### The basic rules

| Rule | Form |
|---|---|
| $\wedge$I | from $A$ and $B$ (lines $m$, $n$), infer $A \wedge B$ |
| $\wedge$E | from $A \wedge B$ (line $m$), infer $A$, or infer $B$ |
| $\vee$I | from $A$ (line $m$), infer $A \vee B$, or $B \vee A$, for any $B$ |
| $\vee$E | from $A \vee B$ (line $m$), a subproof $i$–$j$ assuming $A$ and ending in $C$, and a subproof $k$–$l$ assuming $B$ and ending in the same $C$, infer $C$ |
| $\to$E | from $A \to B$ and $A$ (lines $m$, $n$), infer $B$ |
| $\leftrightarrow$E | from $A \leftrightarrow B$ and $A$, infer $B$; from $A \leftrightarrow B$ and $B$, infer $A$ |
| $\neg$E | from $A$ and $\neg A$ (lines $m$, $n$), infer $\bot$ (absurdity) |
| X | from $\bot$ (line $m$), infer any sentence |
| R | repeat a sentence from an earlier line that is still available |

The symbol $\bot$ stands for contradiction. Rule X (from a contradiction, anything) lets you leave a dead-end case, as in the proof of disjunctive syllogism below.

### An argument to prove

The Euthyphro dilemma, as it is usually put to divine command theories, can be set out as follows.

| P1 | Either the gods love the pious because it is pious, or it is pious because the gods love it. |
| P2 | If the gods love the pious because it is pious, then what is pious is settled independently of the gods' will. |
| P3 | If it is pious because the gods love it, then what is pious is arbitrary. |
| C | So either what is pious is settled independently of the gods' will, or it is arbitrary. |

Write $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ for the four atomic claims in order of occurrence ("the gods love it because pious", "pious because the gods love it", "settled independently of the gods' will", "arbitrary"). The form is $A \vee B$, $A \to C$, $B \to D$, so $C \vee D$.

```text
 1 | A ∨ B        PR
 2 | A → C        PR
 3 | B → D        PR
   |---
 4 | | A          AS
 5 | | C          →E 2, 4
 6 | | C ∨ D      ∨I 5
 7 | | B          AS
 8 | | D          →E 3, 7
 9 | | C ∨ D      ∨I 8
10 | C ∨ D        ∨E 1, 4–6, 7–9
```

The argument is valid, and P1 is the premise doing the work in the sense that it supplies the two cases. The best objection attacks P1 as a **false dilemma**: a defender of divine command theory may say that what is pious is grounded neither in the gods' mere will nor independently of them but in the gods' nature, a third option. The reply from the dilemma's defender is that this third option needs its own argument and can be pressed again: is the nature itself good because it is good, or arbitrary? The logic of the proof is not at issue; the dispute is over whether P1 is true.

@example
title: Conjunction and the conditional
problem: Prove $R \wedge P$ from the premises $P \wedge Q$ and $Q \to R$.
step: Set up the premises, then work toward the goal $R \wedge P$. It is a conjunction, so aim to get $R$ and $P$ separately and join them with $\wedge$I.
step: Get $R$ from $Q \to R$ by $\to$E, which needs $Q$. Get $Q$ from the first premise by $\wedge$E.
step: Get $P$ by $\wedge$E from line 1 as well, then join.
```text
1 | P ∧ Q        PR
2 | Q → R        PR
  |---
3 | Q            ∧E 1
4 | R            →E 2, 3
5 | P            ∧E 1
6 | R ∧ P        ∧I 4, 5
```
answer: $P \wedge Q$, $Q \to R$ $\vdash R \wedge P$, as proved.

@example
title: Disjunctive syllogism by cases
problem: Prove $Q$ from the premises $P \vee Q$ and $\neg P$.
step: The only way to use a disjunction is $\vee$E: assume each disjunct in turn and show $Q$ in both subproofs.
step: In the first subproof assume $P$. It contradicts the premise $\neg P$, giving $\bot$ by $\neg$E, and X lets you infer $Q$ from $\bot$.
step: In the second subproof assume $Q$; the goal is already there, so repeat it with R. Then $\vee$E closes the proof.
```text
1 | P ∨ Q        PR
2 | ¬P           PR
  |---
3 | | P          AS
4 | | ⊥          ¬E 3, 2
5 | | Q          X 4
6 | | Q          AS
7 | | Q          R 6
8 | Q            ∨E 1, 3–5, 6–7
```
answer: $P \vee Q$, $\neg P$ $\vdash Q$, as proved.

@mistakes
- Using $\to$E "backwards": from $A \to B$ and $B$ you cannot infer $A$. The rule needs the antecedent.
- Citing a line inside a closed subproof from outside it. Once a subproof ends, only the subproof as a whole can be cited.
- Ending the two subproofs of $\vee$E with different sentences: both must end with the sentence you infer.
- Using $\wedge$E on a disjunction or $\vee$I as if it told you which disjunct holds. $A \vee B$ gives you neither $A$ nor $B$ by itself.

@practice mc
prompt: Which rule lets you infer $S$ from the two lines $R \to S$ and $R$?
choice*: $\to$E
choice: $\to$I
choice: $\vee$E
choice: $\wedge$E
hint: Which rule uses a conditional together with its antecedent?
solution: From a conditional and its antecedent, conditional elimination ($\to$E) gives the consequent. $\wedge$E needs a conjunction, $\vee$E a disjunction with two subproofs, and $\to$I needs a subproof, not two lines.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these are correct single-step applications of the basic rules, given the lines cited? Select all that apply.
choice*: From $A \wedge B$, infer $B$ by $\wedge$E.
choice: From $A \vee B$ and $A$, infer $B$.
choice: From $A \to B$ and $B$, infer $A$ by $\to$E.
choice*: From $A$, infer $A \vee (B \wedge C)$ by $\vee$I.
choice*: From $A \leftrightarrow B$ and $B$, infer $A$ by $\leftrightarrow$E.
hint: Check what each rule actually allows, and whether the cited lines match the rule's pattern.
solution: $\wedge$E, $\vee$I and $\leftrightarrow$E each allow the step as described: the biconditional rule works in both directions, and $\vee$I lets you add any disjunct. From $A \vee B$ and $A$ you cannot infer $B$ (that would need the negation of $A$), and from $A \to B$ and $B$ you cannot infer $A$ (affirming the consequent).

  |---

@practice num
prompt: The sequent $(P \vee Q) \wedge R \vdash (P \wedge R) \vee (Q \wedge R)$ is provable, and the proof system is sound. In how many of the eight rows of the truth table is the premise $(P \vee Q) \wedge R$ true?
answer: 3
unit: rows
check: count_models(lambda p, q, r: (p or q) and r)
hint: $R$ must be true, and at least one of $P$, $Q$ must be true.
solution: With $R$ true, the disjunction $P \vee Q$ is true on three of the four assignments to $P$ and $Q$. So the premise is true on 3 of the 8 rows (and by soundness the conclusion is true on each of them).

@quiz mc
prompt: Which is the error in this attempted proof?
```text
1 | P → Q        PR
2 | Q            PR
  |---
3 | P            →E 1, 2
```
choice*: $\to$E needs the antecedent $P$, not the consequent $Q$; the step affirms the consequent.
choice: $\to$E may only be used when the conclusion is a conditional.
choice: Premises must not be cited in later lines.
choice: Line 3 should be justified by $\wedge$E.
hint: What two lines does conditional elimination need?
solution: Conditional elimination takes a conditional and its *antecedent* and gives the consequent. Here line 2 is the consequent, so the step is the fallacy of affirming the consequent; indeed $P \to Q$, $Q$ do not entail $P$ (take $P$ false and $Q$ true).
