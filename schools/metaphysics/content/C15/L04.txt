@article
A visitor finds a perfectly smooth glass sphere on a deserted beach and asks where it came from. Suppose someone answers, "Nowhere in particular; it has no explanation, and there is nothing more to say." The visitor would find this answer strange. The **principle of sufficient reason** (PSR) says that this reaction is right in general, that everything has an explanation. Leibniz named it as one of the two great principles of reasoning, alongside non-contradiction (*Monadology*, section 32): no fact is true or existent without a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise. The contingency argument rests on it, so this week we study the argument and then the principle.

### Versions of the principle

The PSR comes in strengths:

- **Strong PSR**: every truth has an explanation.
- **PSR for contingent facts**: every contingent fact has an explanation. A fact is **contingent** if it could have failed to obtain, and **necessary** if it could not have failed; necessary truths may explain themselves or be explained by other necessary truths.
- **PSR for beings**: every being has an explanation of its existence, either in its own nature or in something else.

Weaker versions are easier to defend and do less work; stronger ones do more work and face more objections.

### The contingency argument

Here is a modern formulation in the Leibnizian tradition (the term "Big Conjunctive Contingent Fact" comes from the work of Alexander Pruss). Let the **BCCF** be the conjunction of all contingent facts.

| P1 | Every contingent fact has an explanation. |
| P2 | The BCCF is a contingent fact. |
| C1 | So the BCCF has an explanation. |
| P3 | Any explanation of the BCCF is either a contingent fact or a necessary fact. |
| P4 | No contingent fact explains the BCCF. |
| C2 | So some necessary fact explains the BCCF. |
| P5 | If some necessary fact explains the BCCF, then there is a necessary being that explains it. |
| C | So there is a necessary being that explains the BCCF. |

The argument is valid. P1 and P2 give C1 by instantiation. C1, P3 and P4 give C2 by disjunctive syllogism. C2 and P5 give C by modus ponens. P4 rests on the thought that any contingent fact is a part of the BCCF, so it would have to help explain itself. The **key premise** is P1, the PSR for contingent facts; without it nothing requires an explanation of the BCCF.

The conclusion is modest: a necessary being. It does not say that this being is personal, good or unique, and some critics suggest the universe itself, or some physical law, might be the necessary thing. A defender must add further arguments to reach the God of theism.

### Objections

1. **Brute facts (against P1).** David Hume held that we can conceive of events without causes, and Bertrand Russell, in his 1948 radio debate with Frederick Copleston, said that the universe is simply there and that is all. On this view some contingent facts, perhaps the BCCF, are brute: they have no explanation. Defenders reply that the PSR is a presupposition of all inquiry: we always search for explanations, and an exemption for the biggest fact looks unmotivated.
2. **The Hume-Edwards principle (against P4).** Hume's *Dialogues* (Part IX) and later Paul Edwards suggest that if each member of a collection is explained, the collection is explained, and each contingent fact might be explained by an earlier one, forever. This denies P4: the contingent facts jointly explain the BCCF. William Rowe and others reply that the principle is false in general: explaining why each person in a group of travelers was born does not explain why the group exists; likewise, explaining each fact by another leaves unexplained why there are any contingent facts at all.
3. **Modal collapse (against P1, in its strong form).** Peter van Inwagen argued that a strong PSR entails that every truth is necessary. In standard form:

| P1 | If every truth has an explanation, then the BCCF has an explanation. |
| P2 | If the BCCF has an explanation, then that explanation is a necessary truth. |
| P3 | Any truth that explains another truth necessitates it. |
| P4 | If a necessary truth necessitates the BCCF, then the BCCF is necessary. |
| P5 | If the BCCF is necessary, then there are no contingent truths. |
| P6 | There are contingent truths. |
| C | So not every truth has an explanation: the strong PSR is false. |

This is valid: assume the strong PSR, and P1 to P5 yield that there are no contingent truths, contradicting P6. Defenders deny **P3**. A free choice may be explained by an agent's reasons without being necessitated by them, and so the explanation of the contingent facts could be a free act of a necessary being. Critics reply that such an account makes "explanation" too loose or that a free choice is itself arbitrary.
4. **Against P5.** Necessary truths such as mathematical truths seem abstract; they do not cause anything. So a necessary fact might explain nothing about contingent facts, and a defender must say how a necessary being explains contingent things.

### Where things stand

Neither side wins by default. A defender of the argument needs a PSR strong enough to explain the BCCF yet weak enough to avoid modal collapse; a critic needs a reason to exempt some facts from explanation that is more than the observation that explanations must end somewhere. That is the real point at issue, and informed philosophers disagree.

@example
title: Reconstructing and locating an objection
problem: A student summarizes: "Every contingent thing needs an explanation. The totality of contingent things is contingent. So the totality needs an explanation, and it cannot be one of the contingent things. So there must be a necessary being." Put this in standard form and say which premise is attacked by the objection that "each fact is explained by an earlier fact, so the whole series is explained".
step: Identify the premises: contingent things need explanation (P1); the totality is contingent (P2); the totality needs an explanation (C1); a contingent explanation is excluded (P4); the explainer is not contingent so it is necessary (C2); so there is a necessary being (C).
step: Check what is missing. The step from "needs an explanation that is not contingent" to "necessary" needs P3, that any explanation is contingent or necessary. The final step needs P5, that a necessary explainer is a necessary being. These hidden premises make the argument valid.
step: The objection "each fact is explained by an earlier fact, so the whole series is explained" says that the contingent facts do explain the totality, so it denies P4.
answer: The argument is valid with the hidden premises P3 and P5 added. The objection attacks P4.

@example
title: Contingent propositions in a small model
problem: A model has four worlds, w1 to w4. Proposition A is true at all four worlds; B at w1 and w2; C at w1 and w3; D at w3 only. Consider only these four propositions. At how many worlds is the conjunction of all the contingent propositions true at w1 itself true?
step: A proposition is contingent if it is true at some worlds and not at others. A is true at all worlds, so it is necessary; B, C and D are contingent.
step: At w1, B and C are true, and D is false. So the contingent propositions true at w1 are B and C.
step: The conjunction of B and C is true exactly where both are true: B holds at w1 and w2, C at w1 and w3, and both hold only at w1.
step: So the conjunction of the contingent truths at w1 is true at 1 world, which shows that it is itself contingent, as P2 of the argument requires.
answer: 1 world

@mistakes
- Treating P1 as "every thing has a cause". The PSR for contingent facts says that contingent facts have explanations; the necessary being is not said to be explained by something else.
- Reading the conclusion as the God of any particular tradition. The conclusion is a necessary being that explains the BCCF, and further steps are needed to reach more.
- Attacking P4 when you mean P1, or the reverse. The brute-fact objection denies that the BCCF needs an explanation (P1); the Hume-Edwards objection says the contingent facts explain it (P4).
- Saying that modal collapse refutes any PSR. The argument targets the strong PSR, and because it uses only the explanation of the BCCF it threatens the PSR for contingent facts too; either version survives only if a defender can deny that explanation necessitates.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the modal-collapse objection to the strong principle of sufficient reason and give one reply. Say which premise of the objection the reply denies.
hint: Recall the premise that explanation necessitates what it explains, and how a free choice might be thought to explain without necessitating.
solution: The objection says that if every truth has an explanation, then the conjunction of all contingent truths has one; the explanation must be necessary (a contingent one would be part of the conjunction); anything that explains a truth necessitates it, so the conjunction is necessary; but then every contingent truth is necessary, which contradicts the existence of contingent truths. A reply denies the premise that anything that explains a truth necessitates it: free agents may explain by choosing for reasons that incline without necessitating, and probabilistic explanations also do not necessitate. The objection's critics respond that this makes the explanation of a free choice seem arbitrary, so the dispute is over what an explanation is.
