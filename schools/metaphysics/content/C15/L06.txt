@article
Imagine a machine with many dials, each of which could be set to any of an enormous range of values, and suppose that only if each dial is set within a tiny window does anything interesting happen. If you saw that every dial was in its window, you would want an explanation. Some physicists and philosophers say that our universe is like this. The values of certain physical constants, and the initial conditions of the universe, appear to lie in narrow ranges that permit stars, chemistry and life. Weinberg argued in 1987, for example, that a cosmological constant much larger than the observed value would have prevented galaxies from forming. This is the **fine-tuning** of the universe for life, and the **fine-tuning argument** says that it is evidence of design.

### What is, and is not, claimed

The first thing to separate is the **physical claim** (that life-permitting values are a tiny fraction of the possible values) from the **philosophical interpretation**. The physical claim is contested in its details: it is not settled which constants could have varied independently, whether other kinds of life might exist in other conditions, or whether a deeper theory, which we lack, fixes the values. The philosophical question is what follows if the claim is correct.

### The argument

The best version is a **likelihood** argument. Let $F$ be the fact that the universe is fine-tuned, $T$ theism and $N$ naturalism (the view that there is no God and that nothing supernatural exists, with or without other universes).

| P1 | The universe is fine-tuned for life: the life-permitting values of the constants and initial conditions are a very small fraction of the values they could have had. |
| P2 | The fine-tuning of the universe is much more probable if theism is true than if naturalism is true. |
| P3 | If a piece of evidence is much more probable on one hypothesis than on another, then it confirms the first hypothesis over the second (the likelihood principle). |
| C | So the fine-tuning of the universe confirms theism over naturalism. |

The argument is valid. Its conclusion is about **confirmation**, not about how probable theism is. By Bayes's theorem, the odds of two hypotheses after evidence are their prior odds multiplied by the **likelihood ratio** $\Pr(E \mid H_1) / \Pr(E \mid H_2)$. A large likelihood ratio can raise the odds a great deal and still leave a hypothesis improbable if its prior was low enough, or leave it probable if the prior was high. How to assess priors for theism and naturalism is itself disputed, and people who accept the conclusion C can disagree about the final probability.

### Objections to the premises

1. **Against P1: the measure problem.** The claim that the life-permitting range is "small" needs a way to compare it with the range of possible values, and when the possible values are unbounded there is no natural, normalizable probability distribution over them. This has been pressed in the literature as the normalizability problem. Defenders reply that the argument needs only comparisons over finite ranges or a looser, epistemic notion of probability.
2. **Against P2: what would God choose?** Why would God make life depend on precise values? An omnipotent God needs no fine-tuning, so the probability of fine-tuning given theism is hard to assess. Defenders reply that a God who values complex, embodied life, beauty and stable laws has reason to make a universe in which the laws permit it; critics reply that this assumes a great deal about God's aims.
3. **Against P2: the anthropic selection effect.** We could only ever observe a universe that permits observers, so finding ourselves in one is no surprise. This is the **weak anthropic principle**, due to Brandon Carter. John Leslie replied with the image of a firing squad: a prisoner who survives when all the marksmen miss would still ask why they all missed; being alive to ask the question does not remove the need for an explanation.
4. **The multiverse reply (against P2).** Suppose there are very many universes, with the constants varying from one to the next, as some physical theories, such as eternal inflation and the string landscape, suggest (neither is established). Then it is likely that some universe is life-permitting, and observers will find themselves in such a one. On this view naturalism makes fine-tuning probable, which denies P2 because $N$ includes multiverse versions of naturalism.

### Objections to the multiverse reply

- **No independent evidence.** Other universes are not observable, and the theories that predict them are unconfirmed. Defenders say that they are consequences of otherwise well-motivated physics.
- **Parsimony.** The multiverse posits a vast number of unobserved entities. Defenders say the theory is simple: one mechanism, many outcomes.
- **Fine-tuning of the generator.** Some argue that a mechanism that produces universes with varying constants would itself need special features, so the fine-tuning is moved up a level. Many physicists dispute this.
- **The inverse gambler's fallacy.** Ian Hacking argued that reasoning from the fact that our universe is life-permitting to the existence of many universes is like inferring from a double six on the throw you walk in on that there were many earlier throws. John Leslie and others replied that the charge fails when the evidence is only that some universe supports life, while Roger White defended Hacking's side; the question remains debated.
- **Theism is not excluded.** A theist can accept a multiverse: God could create many universes. The multiverse reply, if it works, weakens the fine-tuning argument for theism without refuting theism.

The result is a clean map of the debate: the argument is valid; the physical claim P1 may be questioned; the weight falls on P2, where the multiverse and the selection effect press, and on what is to be assumed about God's aims.

@example
title: From likelihood ratio to posterior probability
problem: Two exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses, H and K, have prior probabilities of 1/3 and 2/3. Evidence E has probability 0.6 on H and 0.1 on K. What is the probability of H after learning E? (These are artificial numbers to illustrate the method, not estimates about any real hypothesis.)
step: Compute the likelihood ratio: 0.6 / 0.1 = 6, so E favors H six to one.
step: Prior odds of H to K are (1/3) : (2/3) = 1 : 2. Posterior odds are 6 × (1/2) = 3, that is, 3 : 1.
step: Convert the odds to a probability: 3 / (3 + 1) = 0.75.
step: Check by Bayes's theorem: $\Pr(H \mid E) = (0.6 \cdot 1/3) / (0.6 \cdot 1/3 + 0.1 \cdot 2/3) = 0.2 / (0.2 + 0.0667) = 0.75$.
answer: The probability of H after learning E is 0.75, so E confirms H over K even though H started out less probable.

@example
title: Matching objections to the fine-tuning argument
problem: Which premise does each objection attack? (a) There is no natural, normalizable probability distribution over the possible values of the constants. (b) If there are many universes with varying constants, naturalism makes a life-permitting universe probable. (c) A deeper theory may fix the constants at the values they have. (d) The likelihood principle is a standard principle of confirmation, but the argument shows only confirmation, not that theism is probable.
step: Recall the premises: P1 is the physical claim; P2 is the likelihood claim; P3 is the likelihood principle.
step: (a) challenges the meaning of "very small fraction" of the possible values, so it attacks P1. (c) says that the constants could not have varied across a large range, so it also attacks P1.
step: (b) says that the fine-tuning is not much less probable on naturalism, so it attacks P2.
step: (d) accepts P3 and every premise; it says only that the conclusion is about confirmation, not posterior probability, so it attacks no premise.
answer: (a) P1; (b) P2; (c) P1; (d) no premise.

@mistakes
- Treating the conclusion as "theism is probable". The argument concludes only that fine-tuning confirms theism over naturalism, and posterior probability depends on priors and all other evidence.
- Assuming that the multiverse reply refutes theism. At most it weakens the fine-tuning argument; theists can accept a multiverse.
- Saying that the anthropic principle explains why the universe is fine-tuned. It explains why we observe a life-permitting universe, which is a different question that Leslie's firing-squad example presses.
- Taking the claims about physical constants as settled. The details are disputed, and no accepted theory says whether the constants could have varied.

prompt: The prior odds of H to K are 1 : 9, and evidence E has likelihood ratio 20 in favor of H (that is, $\Pr(E \mid H) / \Pr(E \mid K) = 20$). What is the probability of H after learning E, as a percent to one decimal place? Assume that H and K are exclusive and exhaustive.

prompt: Explain the weak anthropic principle as an objection to the fine-tuning argument, and John Leslie's firing-squad reply to it. Say which premise the objection is aimed at, and then weigh the example: what does it show, and what are its limits? Answer by assessing the reasoning, not by stating your own beliefs about God.
hint: Consider what we can observe and what needs to be explained. Then ask whether being alive to ask a question removes the need for an explanation.
solution: The weak anthropic principle says that we can only observe universes that permit observers, so it is no surprise to find ourselves in one. The objection is aimed at P2: it says that fine-tuning is not improbable given that we exist to observe it. Leslie's firing squad: a prisoner faces a hundred marksmen, and all miss. His survival is a precondition of his reflecting on it, but he would still ask why they all missed, and "you could not be here otherwise" does not answer that. The example shows that a selection effect explains why we observe a life-permitting universe, not why there is one. Its limit is that it is an analogy: a critic can say that the universe was not set up like an execution, and a defender of the objection can respond that if there are many universes, the selection effect together with the many trials does explain why there is a life-permitting one. So the objection is strongest when combined with the multiverse reply, and the example shows why it is weak alone.
