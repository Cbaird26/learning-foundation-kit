@article
Few questions in the philosophy of religion are as old, or weigh on as many people, as this one: if there is a God who is all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good, why is there so much suffering in the world? The question is raised in many traditions and has been pressed with great force from Hume's *Dialogues* (Part X) to the present. Philosophers divide evils into **moral evil**, which results from the wrongful free actions of agents, and **natural evil**, which results from natural processes such as disease and earthquakes. They also divide the arguments. The **logical problem of evil** says that the existence of God is *inconsistent* with the existence of evil. The **evidential problem** (Week 10) says that evil is strong evidence against God without claiming inconsistency.

### Mackie's argument

J. L. Mackie ("Evil and Omnipotence", *Mind*, 1955) argued that theism is not just unsupported but positively irrational, because its core claims are inconsistent: God is omnipotent, God is wholly good, and yet evil exists. To get an inconsistency, he said, we need extra "quasi-logical rules": that a good thing eliminates evil as far as it can, and that there are no limits to what an omnipotent thing can do. Here is a valid version in standard form:

| P1 | If God exists, then God is omnipotent and wholly good. |
| P2 | If God is omnipotent, then God could have made a world that contains free creatures and no evil. |
| P3 | If God is wholly good and could have made a world containing free creatures and no evil, then any world God makes contains no evil. |
| P4 | If God exists, then the actual world is a world that God made. |
| P5 | The actual world contains evil. |
| C | So God does not exist. |

It is valid. Suppose God exists. By P1, God is omnipotent and wholly good. By P2, God could have made a world with free creatures and no evil. By P3, any world God makes has no evil, and by P4 the actual world is one God made, so the actual world has no evil, contradicting P5. The argument is therefore a reductio of God's existence. Mackie held that free will does not help: there is no contradiction in someone's freely choosing the good on every occasion, so an omnipotent God could have made creatures who always freely do right.

### Plantinga's free will defense

Alvin Plantinga (*God, Freedom, and Evil* and *The Nature of Necessity*, both 1974) answered by denying **P2**. His reply makes a distinction between what is logically possible and what an omnipotent being can bring about. It is logically possible that a world exists in which free creatures always choose rightly. But God cannot *cause* a creature to freely choose the right, since a choice that is caused in that way is not free. What a free creature would freely do in a given circumstance is up to the creature. So whether God could create a world with free creatures and no evil depends on what free creatures would in fact freely do, and that is not up to God. Plantinga introduced the idea of **transworld depravity**: a creature suffers from it if, in every circumstance God could put her in, she would freely do at least one wrong thing. If every possible creature suffers from transworld depravity, then God, though omnipotent, could not have created a world with free creatures and moral good but no moral evil. Then P2 is false, and the inconsistency disappears.

This is a **defense**, not a **theodicy** (the term is from Leibniz's *Theodicy*, 1710). A theodicy tries to say what God's actual reasons for permitting evil are. A defense need not say what the reasons are, or even that its story is true or likely. It needs only to be *possible*: if the story is consistent with God's existing and evil's existing, then the claim that they are inconsistent is refuted. This is why a possibility claim can answer a charge of inconsistency.

Plantinga's treatment of natural evil is more speculative. He suggested that it is possible that natural evil results from the free actions of non-human persons (such as fallen angels). Critics find this implausible, though the defense needs only possibility.

### Objections

1. **Counterfactuals of freedom.** The defense relies on there being facts about what free creatures would freely do in circumstances that do not arise. Critics such as Robert Adams deny that there are such facts, since nothing makes them true; this would alter the account of transworld depravity but not the claim that freedom limits what God can bring about.
2. **Implausibility of transworld depravity.** It seems unlikely that every possible creature is depraved in this way. Defenders reply that the defense needs only the possibility.
3. **Mackie's demand for a stronger notion.** Some critics argue that an omnipotent God could have made it so that creatures freely choose the good as the only thing they would choose. This is a denial of the premise that what free creatures would do is not up to God.
4. **Beyond the logical problem.** Even if the defense succeeds, it does not show that evil is not evidence against God; it answers only the charge of inconsistency.

William Rowe, a leading critic of theism, wrote in 1979 that it was reasonable to think that the logical problem had been answered, and he turned to the evidential problem. Most philosophers on both sides now agree that the logical problem, in its original form, does not succeed; the evidential problem remains debated.

@example
title: Validity and the premise denied
problem: Consider Mackie's argument as set out above (P1 to P5, C). A student says: "The free will defense shows the argument is invalid." Evaluate this, and say which premise the free will defense denies, and which premise a "greater goods" reply that a wholly good God may permit some evil for the sake of a greater good would deny.
step: Check validity. Assume God exists, and chain P1, P2, P3 and P4 to derive that the actual world contains no evil. This contradicts P5, so the argument is valid. The student is wrong: the defense does not claim invalidity.
step: The free will defense says that God could not have brought about a world of free creatures without evil, because of transworld depravity. This denies the claim in P2 that God could have made such a world.
step: A greater-goods reply says that a wholly good being need not exclude all evil from the worlds it makes. This denies P3, which says that a wholly good God who could make a world with free creatures and no evil makes only worlds with no evil.
answer: The argument is valid; the free will defense denies P2; the greater-goods reply denies P3.

@example
title: A toy model of transworld depravity
problem: God can create creature A, creature B, both or neither, and must put each created creature in circumstance S1 or S2. God decides whom to create and where, and then each creature chooses freely. The facts about what each would freely do are: A would do wrong in S1 and in S2; B would do wrong in S1 and right in S2. Assume each creature's choices do not depend on the other's. How many options of creating at least one creature contain no wrong action?
step: List the options. A can be absent, in S1 or in S2; B can be absent, in S1 or in S2; that gives 9, and 8 if we exclude creating no one.
step: A would do wrong in both circumstances, so any option that creates A contains a wrong action. So A must be absent.
step: With A absent, B must be created, and B does no wrong only in S2. That leaves one option: B alone, in S2.
step: Here A suffers from transworld depravity, B does not. If B also did wrong in S2, no option with at least one creature would be free of wrong, which is what the defense says about every possible creature.
answer: 1 option

@mistakes
- Saying that the free will defense is a theodicy. It does not claim to state God's actual reasons, only a possible reason consistent with the existence of both God and evil.
- Saying that Plantinga showed that God exists. The defense answers the charge of inconsistency only.
- Saying that the defense denies that God is omnipotent. It keeps the definition of omnipotence and denies that God can bring about any logically possible world; free choices are not caused by God.
- Treating the logical and evidential problems as the same. The first claims inconsistency; the second claims that evil is evidence against theism.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why Plantinga's free will defense needs only to show that its story is possible, and say what it leaves open for the evidential problem of evil.
hint: Think about what a claim of inconsistency requires, and about what the evidential problem claims.
solution: The logical problem says that two claims, that God exists and that evil exists, cannot both be true. A claim that two statements cannot both be true is refuted by any consistent scenario in which both are true. The free will defense gives such a scenario: it is possible that God could not have made a world with free creatures and no moral evil. It needs no claim that the scenario is actual or likely. But the evidential problem makes a different claim: that the amount and distribution of evil make it unlikely that God exists. A merely possible story does not touch that claim, since the question is what is probable on the evidence, so the evidential problem remains.
