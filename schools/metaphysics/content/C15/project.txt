@project
title: A balanced dialogue on one argument
overview: You will write a dialogue of about 2,500 words in which a theist, an atheist and an agnostic examine one argument (the kalam cosmological argument, the fine-tuning argument, the modal ontological argument or the evidential argument from evil). Each speaker gives the strongest version of their view and replies to the others. You then write a one-page assessment, in your own voice as an evaluator, of which considerations carry the most weight and why.
goal: State one argument in valid standard form and use it to give each speaker a precise target.
goal: Give the strongest version of the theist's, the atheist's and the agnostic's views, with replies that engage what the others have actually said.
goal: Judge which premises and objections carry the most weight, in a way that a reader of any view could recognize as fair.
step: Choose one argument from the four argument cards below and copy its standard form. Check that you can say why it is valid.
step: For each premise, note the best case for it and the best objection to it, naming which premise the objection attacks, and the best reply to the objection. Use the course reading, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy and PhilPapers to find the standard formulations, and cite everything you use.
step: Decide what each speaker holds. The theist defends the argument's premises, or at least its force. The atheist presses the strongest objections. The agnostic suspends judgment for stated reasons, for example that the premises seem about as plausible as their negations, and must also argue, replying to both of the others, not merely agree with each in turn.
step: Write an outline of about twelve exchanges so that each speaker states a position, hears an objection, replies, and is replied to. Make sure each speaker concedes at least one good point.
step: Draft the dialogue (about 2,500 words). Keep each speaker's position consistent. Let each speaker name the premise being discussed ("that attacks premise P3"), so that the reader can follow the logic.
step: Write the one-page assessment (about 400 words). Say which premises are doing the most work, which objections seem strongest and why, what considerations remain unresolved, and what evidence or argument would change your assessment. Do not report your own religious beliefs; assess the reasoning.
step: Revise using the rubric. Check that each speaker's strongest points are as well stated as the others', that every attribution to a philosopher or tradition is accurate, and that you have paraphrased rather than quoted any modern source. Read one exchange aloud without notes to test that you can explain the idea.
material: **Argument card A (kalam).**

| P1 | Whatever begins to exist has a cause of its beginning to exist. |
| P2 | The universe began to exist. |
| C | So the universe has a cause of its beginning to exist. |

Points to develop: support for P2 from the impossibility of an actual infinite and from cosmology; objections to P1 and P2; the step from C to any further claim about the cause.
material: **Argument card B (fine-tuning).**

| P1 | The universe is fine-tuned for life: the life-permitting values of the constants and initial conditions are a very small fraction of the values they could have had. |
| P2 | The fine-tuning of the universe is much more probable if theism is true than if naturalism is true. |
| P3 | If a piece of evidence is much more probable on one hypothesis than on another, then it confirms the first hypothesis over the second. |
| C | So the fine-tuning of the universe confirms theism over naturalism. |

Points to develop: the measure problem, the selection effect, the multiverse reply and its objections, and the role of prior probabilities.
material: **Argument card C (modal ontological argument).** Let $Q$ be "there is a maximally great being" and $E$ be "there is an omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect being".

| P1 | $\Diamond Q$ |
| P2 | $\Box(Q \to \Box E)$ |
| C1 | $\Diamond\Box E$ |
| P3 | $\Diamond\Box E \to \Box E$ (a theorem of S5) |
| C2 | $\Box E$ |
| P4 | $\Box E \to E$ |
| C | $E$ |

Points to develop: why the force lies in P1, the parallel argument from $\Diamond\Box\neg E$, conceivability and possibility, and the status of S5.
material: **Argument card D (evidential argument from evil).**

| P1 | There exist instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. |
| P2 | An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. |
| C | So there does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being. |

Points to develop: the inductive support for P1, skeptical theism and its objections, theodicies, and what the free will defense does and does not show.
material: **Layout for the dialogue.** Name three fictional speakers (for example, Theo, Alex and Agnes), give each a short opening statement, and let the exchanges follow the argument's premises in order. The assessment is a separate page, written outside the dialogue, in the first person, as an evaluator of the arguments.
deliverable: A dialogue of about 2,500 words with three speakers, with a reference list.
deliverable: A one-page assessment of which considerations carry the most weight and why.
rubric: Accuracy: the argument is in valid standard form, and every attribution to a philosopher, text or tradition is correct.
rubric: Strength and parity: each speaker gives the strongest version of their view, and none is a straw figure or a mere foil.
rubric: Engagement: replies address what the other speakers have said, and each objection is matched to the premise it attacks.
rubric: Judgment: the assessment names the considerations carrying the most weight, gives reasons, and says what would change it.
rubric: Respect and sourcing: traditions and believers of every view and none are described fairly; sources are cited and modern works are paraphrased.
safety: This project asks you to assess arguments, never to state what you personally believe. The speakers are fictional and should be written with respect. If a topic, such as the problem of evil, is distressing to you, you may choose another argument.
tutor: Which premise of my chosen argument do the best objections attack, and what is the strongest reply to each?
tutor: Is my agnostic speaker arguing, or just agreeing with each of the others in turn? How can I make the position stronger?
tutor: Have I stated each speaker's strongest case, or is one side easier to answer than the other?

@review
This is the cumulative review for the course. Use it to check that you can state each argument in standard form, say why it is valid, and say which premise each objection attacks.

### Key ideas, week by week

- **Week 1.** Philosophy of religion evaluates arguments; it is not theology or a description of belief. Theism, atheism (narrow and broad) and agnosticism; the evidentialist argument, attacked at P1 by reformed epistemology and James, and at P2 by those who claim evidence.
- **Week 2.** Omnipotence as the power to do the logically possible; the stone paradox is valid, and its replies deny P3 (the task is incoherent) or P2 (omnipotence is not bound by logic). Omniscience and moral perfection; the Euthyphro question.
- **Week 3.** The kalam argument (P1: what begins has a cause; P2: the universe began); contingency arguments need no beginning.
- **Week 4.** The principle of sufficient reason and the contingency argument; brute facts and modal collapse attack the PSR; the Hume-Edwards principle attacks P4.
- **Week 5.** Paley's design argument; Hume's objections attack the analogical principle P3, and Darwin's natural selection attacks P4.
- **Week 6.** The fine-tuning argument in likelihood form; the multiverse attacks P2; the conclusion is confirmation, not probability.
- **Week 7.** Anselm, Descartes and the modal argument; Gaunilo's parody, Kant's claim that existence is not a real predicate, and the role of P1 and S5.
- **Week 8.** The argument from religious experience; credulity, defeaters and diversity.
- **Week 9.** Mackie's logical problem; the free will defense denies that God could have made a world of free creatures without evil; a defense needs only possibility.
- **Week 10.** Rowe's evidential argument; skeptical theism attacks the support for P1; theodicies deny P1.
- **Week 11.** Hume on miracles; Pascal's wager; faith and reason.
- **Week 12.** Exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism; the diversity argument; non-theistic traditions; writing a balanced assessment.

### Key distinctions

Validity and soundness; truth and rationality; defense and theodicy; the logical and the evidential problem; confirmation and probability; a premise attacked and a conclusion's reach (what follows about a designer or a necessary being).

### Self-check

- Can you state each argument in valid form, with its hidden premise, from memory?
- For each of ten standard objections, can you name the premise attacked?
- Can you do a Bayesian update with a likelihood ratio and prior odds?
- Can you state the strongest case for and against each argument in terms its defenders would accept?
- Can you describe a tradition without caricature and in its own terms?

### Common errors

Calling an argument invalid when only a premise is disputed; saying that an argument proves or refutes God when its conclusion is narrower; running the kalam and contingency arguments together; treating the multiverse as refuting theism; saying that the free will defense is a theodicy; attacking P1 when you mean the support for P1; and "balance" that gives equal space but not equal strength.

@exam mc
prompt: Which pairing of an argument and the premise that a standard objection attacks is correct? (Kalam: P1, whatever begins to exist has a cause; P2, the universe began to exist. Fine-tuning: P1, the universe is fine-tuned; P2, fine-tuning is much more probable on theism than on naturalism; P3, the likelihood principle. Rowe's argument: P1, there are instances of intense suffering that could have been prevented without losing a greater good; P2, a wholly good being would prevent such suffering unless doing so loses a greater good.)
choice*: Hume's point that an uncaused beginning is conceivable attacks P1 of the kalam argument.
choice: The multiverse reply attacks P3 of the fine-tuning argument.
choice: Skeptical theism attacks P2 of Rowe's argument.
choice: The claim that some cosmological models have no first moment attacks P1 of the kalam argument.
hint: Ask what each objection says: is it about what begins, about the probability of the evidence, or about the support for a premise?
solution: Hume's point concerns the general principle that whatever begins has a cause, which is P1 of the kalam argument. The multiverse reply says that fine-tuning is not improbable on naturalism, so it attacks P2, not P3. Skeptical theism attacks the support for P1 of Rowe's argument, not P2. The claim about models without a first moment attacks P2 of the kalam argument.

@exam num
prompt: Two hypotheses H and K are exclusive and exhaustive, with prior odds of 1 : 4 (H to K). Evidence E1 has likelihood ratio 5 in favor of H, and independent evidence E2 has likelihood ratio 5 in favor of K (that is, E2 is five times as probable on K as on H). What is the probability of H after both pieces of evidence, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 20.0
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * ((1 / 4) * 5 * (1 / 5)) / (1 + (1 / 4) * 5 * (1 / 5))
hint: Multiply the prior odds by each likelihood ratio in turn, then convert the odds to a probability.
solution: The posterior odds of H to K are (1/4) × 5 × (1/5) = 1/4, so the evidence on the two sides cancels and the odds are unchanged. The probability of H is (1/4) / (1 + 1/4) = 0.2, or 20.0%. This illustrates a cumulative case: evidence for and against must be weighed together, and priors still matter.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of 400 to 500 words giving a balanced assessment of the evidential argument from evil (Rowe's argument). State it in standard form, give the best defense and the best objection for the premise that carries the most weight, say what the free will defense does and does not show, and end with a verdict that names the premise or inference doing the most work and what would change your assessment. Assess the reasoning without stating your own beliefs.
hint: Use the five-part structure: standard form, strongest case for each side, best objections matched to premises, replies, and a verdict.
solution: P1: There exist instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. P2: An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent any such suffering. C: So there is no omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being. The argument is valid, and the dispute is over P1, which is supported by an inference from "we see no justifying good" to "there is none". The strongest case for the inference is that after reflection on cases such as a fawn dying slowly in a fire, no proposed good seems to justify the suffering, and a failure to find something after looking is ordinarily evidence that it is absent. The best objection is skeptical theism: if God exists, God's knowledge of goods exceeds ours, so our failure to see a justifying good is weak evidence that none exists. Bayes's theorem shows how much depends on how likely we would be to see a good if it existed. The best objection to skeptical theism is that it leads to moral skepticism and undermines confidence in claims about God's goodness; the reply is that our duties rest on other grounds and that the skepticism is limited. Theodicies deny P1 by naming goods, such as free will or soul-making, though critics doubt that these justify horrendous evils and animal suffering. The free will defense shows only that God and evil are logically compatible, by denying the premise that God could have made a world of free creatures without evil; it leaves the evidential problem untouched, because it is a claim about possibility. Verdict: the inference supporting P1 does the most work. Its strength depends on how strongly one thinks we would see a justifying good if there were one, and on the other evidence for and against theism. Evidence about what goods are accessible only to such a being, or an account of why humans are poorly placed to see them, would change the assessment either way.
rubric: States Rowe's argument in valid standard form and identifies the inductive support for P1.
rubric: Presents the strongest case for each side on the key premise and matches each objection to the right premise or inference.
rubric: Explains correctly what the free will defense does and does not show.
rubric: Ends with a verdict that names the premise or inference carrying the most weight and what would change the assessment, without stating personal belief.
