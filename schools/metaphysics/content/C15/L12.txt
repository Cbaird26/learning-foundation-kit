@article
Most people hold the religion, or the lack of one, of the community in which they grew up. This is a fact about how beliefs spread, and it is not by itself an argument. But philosophers ask what, if anything, follows. Does the existence of many traditions with incompatible teachings give anyone a reason to doubt their own? And what do the traditions that do not teach a creator God show about the questions this course has asked?

### Three positions

A common typology distinguishes three answers to the question of how religions relate. **Exclusivism** holds that the central claims of one religion are true and that incompatible claims of others are false (and, in some versions, that salvation or liberation is available only through that religion). **Inclusivism** holds that one religion's claims are true but that other religions may grasp part of the truth, or that salvation is available through them. **Pluralism** holds that several religions are equally valid responses to the ultimate. John Hick, in *An Interpretation of Religion* (1989), proposed a pluralist hypothesis, drawing on Kant: the ultimate reality, "the Real", is beyond the concepts of any one tradition, and the great traditions are different human responses to it, shaped by their cultures. Objections: if the Real cannot be described, we cannot say that it is personal or impersonal, and then it is unclear what the traditions are said to be responding to; many traditions understand themselves as making claims that exclude others, so the pluralist reinterprets them; and critics say pluralism is itself a substantive position that conflicts with exclusivist teachings. Hick's defenders reply that the hypothesis is offered as the best explanation of the similar moral and spiritual fruits of the traditions.

### The argument from religious diversity

| P1 | There are religious traditions whose central doctrines are incompatible and whose adherents are, on the whole, equally sincere, informed and intelligent. |
| P2 | If P1 holds and a person has no independent, non-question-begging reason to think her own tradition better placed to be right, then she should reduce her confidence in her tradition's distinctive claims. |
| P3 | Many adherents of each tradition have no independent, non-question-begging reason to think their own tradition better placed to be right. |
| C | So many adherents of each tradition should reduce their confidence in their tradition's distinctive claims. |

The argument is valid (P1 and P3 yield the antecedent of P2, and modus ponens gives C). Replies attack different premises. One reply denies **P2**: it applies a general principle about disagreement, which philosophers dispute. **Conciliationists** hold that learning that a peer disagrees should lower your confidence; **steadfast** views hold that you may sometimes keep it. The reply adds that P2's principle, applied across the board, would also tell the naturalist to reduce confidence, since naturalism is disputed too, and would threaten almost every philosophical view, including P2 itself. Another reply denies **P3**: believers may have evidence that others lack, such as religious experience or a tradition's long record, and this is not question-begging because personal evidence is evidence. Alvin Plantinga defended exclusivism in this way. A critic replies that adherents of the other traditions can say the same, and that this symmetry is what P2 is about. Note that the argument does not say which tradition is right, or that none is; it says only that confidence should be moderated.

### Outlooks that do not teach a creator God

A fair philosophy of religion describes these traditions in their own terms.

- **Buddhism.** The early teachings, preserved in the Pali canon, and the later schools do not present a creator God. Buddhist cosmology includes gods (*devas*), but they are within the cycle of rebirth and are not creators or saviors. Central teachings are impermanence, **no-self** (*anatta*: there is no unchanging self), dependent arising, and the path to the end of suffering. Buddhist philosophers developed arguments against a creator and rich accounts of mind and ethics.
- **Jainism.** The universe is taken to be uncreated and eternal, and each soul works toward liberation by ethical discipline, especially non-violence (*ahimsa*). Jain thinkers argued against a creator God.
- **Advaita Vedanta.** Shankara taught that ultimate reality, Brahman, is one without a second, and that the individual self (*atman*) is not different from it. How the world relates to Brahman is interpreted in different ways within the school. Other Vedanta schools differ: Ramanuja taught a personal God, Vishnu, with whom souls are united while remaining distinct, and Madhva taught a stronger distinction between God and souls.
- **Nyaya.** Indian philosophy also contains theistic arguments. Udayana (*Nyayakusumanjali*) argued that the world's features, as effects, have an intelligent maker, in an argument comparable to design arguments.
- **Confucian and Daoist thought.** Confucian thought centers on moral cultivation, ritual and the idea of Heaven (*Tian*), interpreted in different ways; Daoist texts speak of the *dao*, the way that is the source of things. Whether these are best called religions, philosophies or both is itself discussed, and the question of a God does not play the same role as in the Abrahamic traditions.
- **Naturalism.** Naturalists hold that the natural world is all there is. They appeal to the explanatory success of science, to parsimony, to the problem of evil and to explanations of why religious beliefs arise; they also give positive accounts of morality, meaning and community. Whether explanations of how religious belief arises undermine it is disputed. **Spinoza**, who identified God with Nature (*Ethics*), was called both an atheist and a pantheist in his time and since. Some philosophers hold that religious language is not mainly about stating facts but expresses commitment or a way of life.

These traditions bear on the arguments of the course in two ways. They show that the questions about theism are one part of a wider conversation about ultimate reality, the self and the good life. And they bear on the argument from religious experience and the argument from diversity, since they report different experiences and different doctrines.

### Writing a balanced assessment

The course asks you to evaluate arguments, not to announce a creed. A balanced essay on one argument does five things: states the argument in standard form, making it valid; states the strongest case for each premise in the language its defenders would use; states the best objection to each contested premise, saying which premise it attacks; gives the best reply to each objection; and ends with a verdict that says which premise carries the most weight and what evidence or argument would change the assessment. It treats each tradition and each side with the same care.

@example
title: Sorting the three positions
problem: Classify each statement as exclusivist, inclusivist or pluralist. (a) "Only my tradition's central claims are true, and incompatible claims in other traditions are false." (b) "My tradition has the fullest truth, but other traditions grasp part of it and may lead to the same end." (c) "The great traditions are different responses to one ultimate reality, and none is the uniquely right one."
step: Recall the definitions: exclusivism says that only one religion's central claims are true; inclusivism says that one is true but that others may share in the truth or the end; pluralism says that several are equally valid responses.
step: (a) says that only one tradition's claims are true, and others false: exclusivism.
step: (b) holds that one tradition has the fullest truth but others grasp part of it: inclusivism.
step: (c) holds that several traditions are valid responses to one reality with none uniquely right: pluralism, as in Hick's hypothesis.
answer: (a) exclusivism; (b) inclusivism; (c) pluralism.

@example
title: Matching replies to the diversity argument
problem: Use the argument from diversity (P1 to P3, C). Which premise does each reply attack? (a) "The principle that disagreement should lower confidence would apply to every philosophical view, including itself." (b) "My own religious experience is evidence that people in other traditions do not have." (c) "Adherents of other traditions are not, on the whole, equally informed or sincere."
step: P1 is about the facts of diversity and the parity of adherents; P2 is the principle linking parity of reasons to reduced confidence; P3 says that many adherents lack an independent reason to favor their own tradition.
step: (a) attacks the general principle, so it attacks P2.
step: (b) says that believers do have an independent reason, so it attacks P3.
step: (c) denies that adherents are equally sincere, informed and intelligent, so it attacks P1.
answer: (a) P2; (b) P3; (c) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the three positions as about whether religions are all the same. Pluralism says that several are valid responses to the ultimate, and it does not say that their doctrines are the same.
- Saying that Buddhism, Jainism and Advaita Vedanta are all atheism. Early Buddhism and Jainism do not teach a creator God, Advaita takes ultimate reality to be non-dual, and other Indian schools are theistic; each should be described in its own terms.
- Treating the argument from diversity as showing that all religions are false. It claims at most that confidence should be moderated, and a naturalist faces a parallel argument from disagreement.
- Writing a "balanced" essay that gives equal space to each side but states one side's case weakly. Balance means stating the strongest case for each.

@practice self
prompt: Write a balanced assessment, in 250 to 350 words, of the contingency argument (P1, every contingent fact has an explanation; P2, the conjunction of all contingent facts is contingent; C1, so it has an explanation; P3, any explanation is contingent or necessary; P4, no contingent fact explains it; C2, so a necessary fact explains it; P5, if a necessary fact explains it, there is a necessary being that explains it; C, so there is a necessary being). Follow the five-part structure of a balanced essay, and do not say which side you favor beyond which premise you think carries the most weight.
hint: State the argument, give the strongest case for P1 and one objection to it, one reply, and a verdict that names the premise doing the most work and what would change the assessment.
solution: The argument (P1 to P5) is valid; its key premise is P1, a principle of sufficient reason for contingent facts. The strongest case for P1 is that the search for explanations underlies all inquiry, so exempting the largest fact seems arbitrary; Leibniz's question, why there is something rather than nothing, expresses this. The best objection is that some facts may simply be brute, as Hume and Russell suggested, and that a strong principle of sufficient reason leads to modal collapse (van Inwagen), since a necessary explanation would seem to make everything necessary. The best reply is that the collapse argument requires that explanation necessitates, which fails for free choices and probabilistic explanation, and that, with that premise denied, the principle for contingent facts survives. Against P4, the Hume-Edwards principle says that explaining each fact explains the whole, and the best reply is that this leaves unexplained why there are any contingent facts at all. Verdict: the premise carrying the most weight is P1, and the verdict depends on how credible a principle strong enough to explain the whole yet weak enough to avoid collapse is; a theory of explanation that allows brute facts without arbitrariness, or a defense of free explanation, would change the assessment. The conclusion is a necessary being, and further steps are needed to reach any particular tradition's God.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how the existence of religious traditions that do not teach a creator God bears on the argument from religious experience, and name the premise it bears on.
hint: Think about what experiences are reported in such traditions, and whether they conflict with experiences of God.
solution: Traditions such as Buddhism and Advaita Vedanta report experiences, for example the dropping away of the sense of a separate self, or a non-dual awareness, that are not experiences of a personal creator. If experiences conflict, someone may argue that they defeat one another, which attacks P3 of the argument from religious experience (that there is no undefeated reason to doubt). A defender can reply that these experiences may be compatible: they might be partial or differently described experiences of one reality, as pluralists say, or the conflict may lie only in the doctrines that interpret them. A critic answers that these replies reinterpret what the experiences claim. The tradition's reports thus bear on how P3 is assessed.
