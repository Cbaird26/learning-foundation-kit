@article
Most arguments for God start from some feature of the world: its order, its existence, its beginning. The **ontological argument** starts from the concept of God alone and tries to show, by reasoning about what that concept contains, that God exists. It is the most striking argument in the subject, because if it worked, no observation of the world would be needed, and the most disputed, because many people feel that existence cannot be proved from a definition.

### Anselm's argument

Anselm of Canterbury, in the *Proslogion* (chapter 2), defined God as a being than which nothing greater can be conceived. Call this being G. Set out as a reductio, in standard form:

| P1 | G exists in the understanding (even one who denies that God exists understands the idea). |
| P2 | A being that exists in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than an otherwise identical being that exists in the understanding alone, and such a being can be conceived. |
| P3 | No being greater than G can be conceived. |
| C1 | So it is not the case that G exists in the understanding alone. |
| C | So G exists in reality. |

The argument is valid. Suppose G existed in the understanding alone. Then by P2, a being just like G but existing also in reality could be conceived, and it would be greater than G, contradicting P3. So C1 holds. Since G exists in the understanding (P1) and not in the understanding alone, it also exists in reality. In *Proslogion* 3, Anselm argues further that such a being cannot even be conceived not to exist.

### Descartes's argument

In Meditation V (1641), Descartes argued that the idea of a supremely perfect being contains existence just as the idea of a triangle contains the property of having three angles that sum to two right angles:

| P1 | God is a supremely perfect being, that is, a being that has every perfection. |
| P2 | Existence is a perfection. |
| C | So God has existence: God exists. |

This is valid: if God has every perfection and existence is one of them, God has existence. Objections in the original *Objections and Replies* include Caterus's (First Objections) that the argument shows only that existence belongs to the concept of an existent God, not that anything answers to the concept, and Gassendi's (Fifth Objections) that existence is not a perfection or property of a thing at all, but what is needed for it to have properties.

### Gaunilo and Kant

**Gaunilo**, a contemporary monk, replied on behalf of "the fool" (*On Behalf of the Fool*) with a parody: consider an island than which no greater island can be conceived. Reasoning like Anselm's would prove that this perfect island exists, which seems absurd; so the reasoning must be faulty. Anselm replied that his argument applies only to a being than which nothing greater can be conceived, and a defender adds that for an island there is no maximum, since we can always imagine a better one, whereas for G the notion of a maximum is built in. Critics reply that the notion of a greatest conceivable being is no more clearly coherent. The parody does not directly attack a single premise: it asks whether the form of reasoning proves too much, and the dispute is over whether the parallel premises hold for islands.

**Kant** (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598 to A602 / B626 to B630) argued that **existence is not a real predicate**: saying that something exists does not add a further property to its concept. A hundred real thalers contain no more than a hundred possible thalers in concept, though my finances differ. If so, P2 of Anselm's argument fails, because real existence adds nothing to the concept that could make a being greater, and P2 of Descartes's argument fails because existence is not a perfection. Defenders reply that necessary existence, which differs from mere existence, may be a property.

### The modal version

Charles Hartshorne and Norman Malcolm, and most influentially Alvin Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974), developed an argument in the language of possible worlds. Let a being be **maximally great** if it has **maximal excellence** (omnipotence, omniscience and moral perfection) in every possible world. Let $E$ be "there is an omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect being" and $Q$ be "there is a maximally great being". Then:

| P1 | $\Diamond Q$: possibly, there is a maximally great being. |
| P2 | $\Box(Q \to \Box E)$: necessarily, if there is a maximally great being, then in every possible world there is an omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect being. |
| C1 | $\Diamond\Box E$: possibly, it is necessary that there is such a being. |
| P3 | $\Diamond\Box E \to \Box E$ (a theorem of the modal system S5). |
| C2 | $\Box E$: necessarily, there is such a being. |
| P4 | $\Box E \to E$ (whatever is necessary is actual). |
| C | $E$: there is an omnipotent, omniscient and morally perfect being. |

It is valid. C1 follows from P1 and P2: in a world where $Q$ holds, $Q \to \Box E$ holds too, so $\Box E$ holds there. C2 follows by modus ponens from C1 and P3, and C by P4. The force of the argument lies in **P1**. Critics note a parallel: if it is possible that it is necessary that there is no such being, that is, $\Diamond\Box\neg E$, then by the same reasoning $\Box\neg E$ and so $\neg E$. The two possibility claims cannot both be true, so the argument is only as strong as the case for P1 over its rival. Plantinga himself said that the argument does not compel every rational person, but shows that belief in theism is rationally acceptable for one who finds P1 plausible. A second line of objection challenges **P3**: it rests on S5, which treats what is necessary as the same from every world. Most philosophers accept S5 for metaphysical modality, though some dispute it. Others deny P1 because they think that the existence of a being with every perfection is conceivable but not possible, a point about the gap between conceivability and possibility.

@example
title: Validity and the premise at issue
problem: Consider the modal argument above. A student says: "The argument is invalid, because P1 only says that a maximally great being is possible." Evaluate this, and say where a person who denies that God exists would object.
step: Check what follows from P1 and P2. If a world exists where $Q$ is true, then in that world $\Box E$ is true, so $\Diamond\Box E$ holds. That is C1, so the step is valid.
step: Then C1 with P3 gives $\Box E$ by modus ponens, and P4 gives $E$. Every step is valid, so the argument is valid, and its force depends on its premises.
step: A person who denies that God exists must deny a premise. The natural target is P1 (it is not possible that there is a maximally great being), or P3 (S5 does not hold for the relevant sort of necessity). If P1 and P3 are granted, C follows.
answer: The argument is valid. The natural objections target P1 and P3.

@example
title: Matching objections to premises
problem: Which premise does each objection attack? (a) Kant: existence adds nothing to the concept of a being, so a being that exists is not greater by virtue of existing (Anselm's argument). (b) A thing's being conceivable does not show that it is possible (modal argument). (c) Existence is not a perfection (Descartes's argument). (d) S5 is not the right logic for metaphysical necessity (modal argument).
step: Identify the argument and its premise in each case. Anselm's P2 says real existence makes a being greater. Descartes's P2 says existence is a perfection. The modal argument's P1 is possibility and P3 is the S5 principle.
step: (a) denies that existence is a great-making property, so it attacks P2 of Anselm's argument.
step: (b) is an objection to supporting P1 of the modal argument by conceivability.
step: (c) denies P2 of Descartes's argument, and (d) denies P3 of the modal argument, the S5 step.
answer: (a) P2 of Anselm; (b) P1 of the modal argument; (c) P2 of Descartes; (d) P3 of the modal argument.

@mistakes
- Saying that Gaunilo showed that the ontological argument is invalid. His parody is meant to show that the form of reasoning proves too much; the parallel premises about islands are what a defender denies.
- Reading Kant as saying that God does not exist. His point is about whether existence is a property that can be part of a concept, and so a premise in the argument.
- Thinking the modal argument avoids all controversy because it is valid. Its force depends on P1, and a parallel argument with $\Diamond\Box\neg E$ is valid by the same rules.
- Confusing $\Box E \to E$ (necessity implies truth) with $E \to \Box E$ (truth implies necessity); only the first is a valid principle.

answer: 0
unit: worlds
check: sum(1 for w in range(4) if any(all(v in {0, 1, 2} for v in range(4)) for u in range(4)))
hint: First find where $\Box E$ is true: that needs $E$ true at all four worlds. Then find where $\Diamond\Box E$ is true.
