@article
Four people are asked about God. The first says, "I am sure there is a God." The second says, "There is no God." The third says, "I have no idea, and I don't think anyone does." The fourth says, "I'm not sure what you mean by God, and anyway my religion is mostly about how I live and what we do together on holy days." Each of these answers raises a different question: Is the claim true? Is it reasonable to hold it? What would the question even mean? The **philosophy of religion** studies questions of this kind by the methods of philosophy: it clarifies concepts, sets out arguments in standard form, and asks which premises are true and which inferences are good.

### What the field is, and is not

The philosophy of religion is not **theology**, which in the narrow sense works out the doctrines of a particular tradition from within it, and it is not the **history, sociology or psychology of religion**, which describe what people believe and do and why. It is a branch of philosophy, and it can be done by believers of any tradition, by non-believers and by people who are undecided. Its main questions are:

- **Truth**: Is there a God, or an ultimate reality of some other kind? What would the evidence be?
- **Rationality**: Is it reasonable to believe, or to disbelieve, without proof? What counts as evidence?
- **Meaning**: What do religious statements mean? Are they claims about reality at all?
- **Diversity**: What should we make of the many religious and non-religious answers?

The field began mostly with the monotheistic traditions of Judaism, Christianity and Islam, but it now draws on Indian, Chinese, African and other traditions as well. Several of these (Theravada Buddhism and Jainism, for example) do not teach a creator God, and many religious lives center on practice and community rather than doctrine. So a good philosophy of religion does not assume that belief in a God is what every religion is about.

### Key terms

**Theism** is the view that there is a God: a supreme, personal being who created and sustains the world, usually taken to be all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good. **Atheism** has two common senses: in the narrow sense it is the belief that there is no God; in the broad sense it is simply the lack of belief that there is one. **Agnosticism** is the suspension of judgment about whether there is a God, whether because one finds the considerations evenly balanced or because one thinks the question cannot be settled. Since the broad sense of atheism asks only about lacking belief, a person can be both a broad atheist and an agnostic; no one can be both a narrow atheist and an agnostic about the same claim. Other views include **pantheism** (God is identical with the universe or nature), **panentheism** (the world is in God, but God is more than the world), **deism** (God created the world but does not act in it) and **polytheism** (there are many gods).

**Natural theology** tries to support its claims by reason and ordinary experience of the world; **revealed theology** relies on a claimed revelation. The arguments in this course belong mostly to natural theology and to its critics.

### The evidentialist challenge

Does belief in God need evidence? Here is the **evidentialist argument**, in the spirit of W. K. Clifford's essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877). Take a person, Susan, who believes that God exists:

| P1 | For any person S and proposition p, S's believing p is rationally permissible only if S has sufficient evidence for p. |
| P2 | Susan does not have sufficient evidence that God exists. |
| C | So Susan's believing that God exists is not rationally permissible. |

The argument is valid: it is universal instantiation of P1 followed by modus tollens. Whether it is sound depends on both premises, and the same argument form, with "God does not exist" in place of "God exists", would apply to Susan's atheist neighbor. There are two broad replies.

1. **Deny P1.** William James, in "The Will to Believe" (1896), argued that when a choice between beliefs is live, forced and momentous, and the evidence cannot settle it, we may let our "passional nature" decide. **Reformed epistemologists**, notably Alvin Plantinga, argue that belief in God can be **properly basic**: justified without being inferred from other beliefs, much as belief in other minds or in the past may be. A standard objection to P1 is that it is itself not supported by evidence of the kind it demands.
2. **Deny P2.** Others say that Susan, or at least some believers, do have sufficient evidence: arguments (Weeks 3 to 7), religious experience (Week 8) or both. A non-theist who accepts P1 will in turn ask whether the arguments and experiences really are sufficient.

Notice that the two replies attack different premises, so a response to the argument must say which premise it denies. Notice too what the argument leaves open: it concerns whether belief is rational, not whether it is true. A true belief can be held irrationally, and a false belief can be held rationally.

### Where things stand

In a 2020 survey of professional philosophers, about two thirds accepted or leaned toward atheism and about one fifth toward theism. The share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that the view is true, and the survey shows that serious thinkers disagree. In this course you will meet the strongest arguments on each side, and you will be asked to evaluate them, never to say what you yourself believe.

title: Sorting positions with the definitions
problem: Using the definitions in this lesson, classify each speaker. Amir: "There is a God." Bea: "I do not believe there is a God, but I do not claim there isn't one; I withhold judgment." Chen: "There is no God." Dara: "The universe is itself divine, and the divine is nothing over and above it."
step: Amir believes that there is a God, so Amir is a theist.
step: Bea lacks the belief that there is a God, so Bea is an atheist in the broad sense. Bea also withholds judgment about whether there is one, so Bea is an agnostic. Bea is not an atheist in the narrow sense, because Bea does not believe that there is no God.
step: Chen believes that there is no God, so Chen is an atheist in the narrow sense, and therefore also in the broad sense. Chen is not an agnostic about this claim.
step: Dara identifies the divine with the universe, which is pantheism. Whether Dara counts as a theist depends on whether "God" is taken to mean a personal creator distinct from the world; on that definition, Dara is not a theist.
answer: Amir: theist. Bea: broad atheist and agnostic. Chen: narrow (and so broad) atheist. Dara: pantheist, not a theist on the personal-creator definition.

@example
title: Matching replies to premises
problem: Susan's argument is: P1, S's believing p is rationally permissible only if S has sufficient evidence for p; P2, Susan lacks sufficient evidence that God exists; C, Susan's belief is not rationally permissible. Which premise does each reply attack? (a) Belief in God can be properly basic. (b) Susan's life contains a vivid experience of the divine, which is evidence. (c) A momentous choice that evidence cannot settle may be made on other grounds.
step: Find the form: P1 is a general principle about rational belief; P2 is a claim about Susan's evidence.
step: (a) Properly basic beliefs are justified without evidence from other beliefs, so this reply denies that evidence is always required. It attacks P1.
step: (b) This reply says Susan does have evidence. It attacks P2.
step: (c) This is James's reply: when evidence cannot settle a live, forced, momentous choice, belief without sufficient evidence is permissible. It attacks P1.
answer: (a) P1; (b) P2; (c) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating agnosticism and atheism as always exclusive. In the broad sense of atheism (lack of belief), an agnostic is also an atheist; in the narrow sense (belief that there is no God), the two are exclusive.
- Assuming that the philosophy of religion is either a defense of religion or an attack on it. It is the evaluation of arguments, and its results can favor either side or neither.
- Confusing the truth of a belief with its rationality. Evidentialism is about whether a belief is rationally permissible, not about whether it is true.
- Assuming that every religion centers on belief in a God. Some traditions have no creator God, and many put practice before doctrine.

@practice self
prompt: Philosophers disagree about who has the "burden of proof" in debates about God. Give the strongest reason you can for the view that the burden lies on the theist alone, and the strongest reason you can for the view that each side must support its own claims. Then say in one sentence which of the two views is better supported by the structure of this evidentialist argument: P1, a person's believing p is rationally permissible only if that person has sufficient evidence for p, for any proposition p; P2, Susan lacks sufficient evidence that God exists; C, Susan's believing that God exists is not rationally permissible.
hint: Think about whether "there is no God" is a claim that needs support in the same way as "there is a God", and about what P1 says about belief in general.
solution: For the first view: a claim that something exists is usually held to need support before it is accepted, as with any new entity, so the one who adds God to our picture of the world bears the burden. For the second view: "There is no God" is also a substantive claim about how things are, and to believe it rationally one needs evidence, such as the problem of evil; so each side must support its claim, and agnostics suspend judgment because neither side has met its burden for them. The evidentialist argument's P1 is stated for any proposition p, so it treats a belief that God does not exist exactly as it treats the belief that God exists; that supports the view that each side must support its claim.
rubric: States a reasonable argument for the view that the burden lies on the theist alone.
rubric: States a reasonable argument for the view that each side bears a burden.
rubric: Notes that P1 applies to any proposition, and so to disbelief as well as belief.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the difference between asking whether a religious belief is true and asking whether it is rational to hold it, and give one neutral example, with no reference to your own beliefs, in which the two questions come apart.
hint: Think of a case where someone has good evidence for something false, or a lucky guess that is true.
solution: A belief is true if things are as it says; it is rational to hold if it fits the believer's evidence and reasoning. They can come apart. A traveler who has been given a reliable-looking but mistaken timetable rationally believes that the train leaves at nine when it leaves at ten: the belief is rational and false. Someone who picks a number by whim and believes it will win a lottery that it does win holds a true belief irrationally. So evidentialism, which concerns rationality, cannot by itself tell us whether theism or atheism is true.
rubric: Defines truth as correspondence with how things are, and rationality as fit with the believer's evidence.
rubric: Gives a case of a rational false belief or an irrational true belief.
rubric: Applies the point to religion without taking a side.
