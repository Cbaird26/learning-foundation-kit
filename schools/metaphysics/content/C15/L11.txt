@article
A report arrives that someone was healed in a way that doctors cannot explain. How should we weigh it? And, more generally, what is the proper relation between faith and the evidence we have? This lesson takes two questions that are often linked: the credibility of miracle reports, and the standards that govern religious belief.

### Hume on miracles

David Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), Section X, defines a **miracle** as a violation of a law of nature, brought about by a particular act of the Deity or by some invisible agent. His argument has two parts: the first argues in general that testimony can seldom establish a miracle, and the second lists historical circumstances that, he says, weaken actual reports: few witnesses of education and integrity, the human love of the marvelous, the tendency of such reports to come from earlier, less critical ages, and the fact that rival religions report miracles for incompatible doctrines, so that these reports tend to cancel one another. Here is the argument of the first part, in a form that is valid as stated:

| P1 | One should believe that a reported event occurred only if, on all the evidence, its occurrence is more probable than its nonoccurrence. |
| P2 | If a reported event is a miracle, then, unless the falsity of the testimony would be even more improbable than the event's occurrence, the event's nonoccurrence is more probable than its occurrence on all the evidence. |
| P3 | For every actual miracle report, the falsity of the testimony is not more improbable than the occurrence of the miracle. |
| C | So one should not believe any report of a miracle. |

The argument is valid. Take a miracle report. By P3, the condition in the "unless" clause of P2 fails, so by P2 the event's nonoccurrence is more probable than its occurrence. By P1, one should then not believe it. Hume supports P2 by the claim that the laws of nature rest on uniform experience, which makes a violation extremely improbable. It is important to see that the argument does not say that miracles are impossible; it says that the probability that testimony is false is usually higher than the probability of the miracle.

### Objections

1. **Circularity (against P2's support).** If the experience supporting a law is called "uniform" only by excluding the very reports at issue, Hume assumes what he must show. John Earman has argued in detail that Hume's argument fails. Defenders of Hume reply that the relevant uniform experience is the wide experience of ordinary events, which no report of a miracle has overturned.
2. **Multiple witnesses (against P3).** A low prior probability can be overcome by evidence. Suppose an event has a prior probability of one in a million. A single witness who is right 99% of the time but who would also report such an event falsely one time in a thousand leaves the probability of the event at about 0.1%, which supports Hume. But two or three independent witnesses can raise it a great deal, because the chance that all of them are mistaken in the same way falls fast. So P3 is not secured by the argument; it depends on the particular case, and on the background probability of the event, which depends in turn on what one thinks about whether a God exists.
3. **Laws and interference (against the definition).** Laws of nature describe what happens when no outside agent interferes, so a miracle on this view is not a violation of any law but an event of a kind that the laws do not cover. Critics reply that on this definition what makes an event miraculous is its supposed divine cause rather than its violating a law, so the question of whether the cause was divine returns, and the probability of that depends on what one thinks of the background evidence about God.
4. **Rival religions.** The claim that miracle reports of rival religions cancel one another is accurate only to the extent that the reports support incompatible doctrines; many reports do not. Hume's critics add that the reports must still be examined one by one.

### Faith and reason

Philosophers hold several views about how faith relates to evidence. **Evidentialists** (Week 1) say that belief in God needs sufficient evidence. **Reformed epistemologists**, such as Alvin Plantinga and William Alston, say that belief in God can be properly basic, or can be formed by a reliable faculty, so it needs no supporting argument. **Fideists** hold, in various forms, that faith does not need support from reason, or that reason is limited in matters of faith; the label covers different views and some theologians so called deny it. **Pragmatists**, such as William James, say that in some conditions practical reasons can justify belief where evidence is silent. Traditions also differ on what faith is: assent to doctrine, trust in a person, or loyalty and commitment.

### Pascal's wager

Blaise Pascal's *Pensées* (published after his death in 1670) contains the **wager**, a practical argument: even if reason cannot settle whether God exists, one must act, and acting as a believer is the better bet. In a form that is valid as stated:

| P1 | The only live possibilities are that God exists and that no god exists, and one must either wager for God (live as a believer) or wager against. |
| P2 | If God exists, wagering for God yields an infinite reward, while wagering against yields only a finite payoff. |
| P3 | If God does not exist, both wagers yield only finite payoffs. |
| P4 | The probability that God exists is greater than zero. |
| P5 | A rational person chooses so as to maximize expected utility, and an option with a positive probability of an infinite reward has greater expected utility than any option whose payoffs are all finite. |
| C | So it is rational to wager for God. |

The argument is valid: by P1 to P4, wagering for God has a positive probability of an infinite reward, and wagering against has only finite payoffs; P5 then implies that wagering for God has greater expected utility. For finite stakes, **expected utility** is the sum of each outcome's utility multiplied by its probability. Objections include these. The **many gods objection**: there are many possible gods and rewards, including a deity who punishes believers or one who rewards honest unbelievers, so P1's claim that only two possibilities are live is too simple. **Infinite utilities**: decision theory with infinite payoffs behaves strangely, since every option with any positive probability of an infinite reward ties, so the argument for the particular wager under P5 may fail; Alan Hájek has pressed this. **Belief and the will**: one cannot simply decide to believe, so "wagering for God" may not be an available option; Pascal suggested that one can cultivate belief by habit and practice, and critics ask whether that produces belief or only behavior. And the argument gives only a practical reason, not evidence that God exists.

@example
title: Weighing a report with Bayes's theorem
problem: An event has a prior probability of one in a million. A witness reports it. The probability of a report if the event occurred is 0.99, and the probability of the same report if it did not is 0.001. What is the probability that the event occurred, given the report? (Artificial numbers, to illustrate how the argument works.)
step: Let E be the event and R the report. $\Pr(E) = 0.000001$, $\Pr(R \mid E) = 0.99$, $\Pr(R \mid \neg E) = 0.001$.
step: By Bayes's theorem, $\Pr(E \mid R) = 0.99 \cdot 0.000001 / (0.99 \cdot 0.000001 + 0.001 \cdot 0.999999)$.
step: The numerator is 0.00000099 and the other term is about 0.001, so $\Pr(E \mid R)$ is about 0.00099, or about 0.1 percent.
step: A very reliable witness still leaves the event improbable when the prior is tiny, which is Hume's point. Independent witnesses would change this, since each additional report multiplies the odds by the same likelihood ratio.
answer: About 0.1 percent.

@example
title: Matching objections to premises
problem: Hume's argument has P1 (believe only if the occurrence is more probable), P2 (for a miracle, nonoccurrence is more probable unless the falsity of the testimony would be more improbable than the event), P3 (for every actual report, the falsity of the testimony is not more improbable) and C. Which premise does each objection attack? (a) Several independent, reliable witnesses can make the falsity of the testimony more improbable than the event. (b) The uniform experience on which the laws are based is uniform only because reports of miracles are excluded. (c) Probable belief is not required for a reasonable belief; sometimes one may believe on practical grounds.
step: P3 is about the strength of the actual testimony. (a) says that in some cases the falsity of the testimony would be more improbable than the event, so it attacks P3.
step: (b) says that the evidence against a miracle is not as strong as P2 assumes, so it attacks the support for P2.
step: (c) denies that one should believe only when the occurrence is more probable, which is P1.
answer: (a) P3; (b) the support for P2; (c) P1.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hume claims miracles are impossible. His argument is that testimony seldom, if ever, outweighs the improbability of a violation of a law.
- Treating a low prior probability as a reason to dismiss all evidence. With enough independent evidence, a low prior can be overcome, though how much is needed depends on the prior.
- Treating Pascal's wager as evidence that God exists. It is a practical argument about what to do under uncertainty.
- Confusing "expected utility" with the most likely outcome. It weighs each outcome's value by its probability.

prompt: In a version of Pascal's wager with finite payoffs, the probability that God exists is 0.1. Wagering for God gives +60 if God exists and -3 if not. Wagering against gives -20 if God exists and +6 if not. What is the expected utility of wagering for God?
answer: 3.3
tol: 0.01
check: 0.1 * 60 + 0.9 * (-3)
hint: Multiply each outcome by its probability and add: the probability that God does not exist is 0.9.
solution: The expected utility is 0.1 × 60 + 0.9 × (-3) = 6 - 2.7 = 3.3. For comparison, wagering against has expected utility 0.1 × (-20) + 0.9 × 6 = 3.4, so on these finite numbers wagering against is slightly better, which shows that the wager's force depends on the payoffs being infinite.

@practice num
prompt: An event has a prior probability of 0.00001. Two independent witnesses each report it. Each would report it with probability 0.9 if it occurred, and each would report it falsely, independently of the other, with probability 0.002 if it did not. What is the probability that the event occurred, given both reports, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 66.9
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * (1e-5 * 0.9 ** 2) / (1e-5 * 0.9 ** 2 + (1 - 1e-5) * 0.002 ** 2)
hint: Under each hypothesis, multiply the probabilities of the two reports, then use Bayes's theorem.
solution: If the event occurred, both report with probability 0.9² = 0.81; if not, both report falsely with probability 0.002² = 0.000004. The posterior is (0.00001 × 0.81) / (0.00001 × 0.81 + 0.99999 × 0.000004) = 0.0000081 / (0.0000081 + 0.00000399996), which is about 0.669, or 66.9%.

@practice self
prompt: Evaluate Pascal's wager. State it in standard form, say which premise the many gods objection attacks, and give the strongest reply a defender could make and the strongest rejoinder.
hint: Use a two-by-two table of options and outcomes. The many gods objection says that the options are not exhausted by "God" and "no God".
solution: P1: The only live possibilities are that God exists and that no god exists, and one must wager for God or against. P2: If God exists, wagering for yields an infinite reward and wagering against only a finite payoff. P3: If God does not exist, both wagers yield finite payoffs. P4: The probability that God exists is greater than zero. P5: A rational person maximizes expected utility, and an option with a positive probability of an infinite reward has greater expected utility than any option whose payoffs are all finite. C: So it is rational to wager for God. The many gods objection attacks P1 (and P2): there are many possible gods and rewards, and a deity who punishes believers or rewards honest unbelievers is as possible as the God of the wager, so the two-way partition is not exhaustive. A defender may reply that the wager can be restricted to the live options for the person, and that the options differ in probability or in the kind of reward, so they do not cancel. The rejoinder is that if there are infinite rewards under several rival options, expected utility cannot discriminate among them, and any assignment of probabilities that favors one looks like it depends on the evidence the wager was meant to do without.

prompt: Use the payoffs: wagering for God gives +50 if God exists and -4 if not; wagering against gives -10 if God exists and +5 if not. For what probability p that God exists do the two wagers have equal expected utility? Give p as a percent to one decimal place.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, with a numerical illustration, why Hume's argument about miracles does not show that testimony could never make a miracle report credible, and say which premise your point attacks.
hint: Show how several independent witnesses change the posterior probability of an event with a very low prior.
solution: Hume's argument concludes that one should not believe any miracle report because the falsity of the testimony is not more improbable than the miracle (P3). But whether this holds depends on the case. Suppose an event has a prior of one in ten thousand and each independent witness would report it with probability 0.95 if it occurred and falsely with probability 0.01 if not. One witness leaves the event at about 0.9% probable. Two independent witnesses give a posterior of 0.95² × 10⁻⁴ divided by that plus 0.01² × (1 - 10⁻⁴), which is about 0.474, and three give about 0.988. So, with enough independent evidence, a low prior can be overcome. This attacks P3: the falsity of the testimony can be more improbable than the event. A Humean can reply that real cases rarely meet the independence and reliability conditions, and that the prior of a violation of a law is lower than a million to one.
