@article
Can God make a stone so heavy that God cannot lift it? If the answer is yes, then there is a stone God cannot lift; if no, then there is something God cannot make. Either way, it seems, God is not all-powerful. The puzzle is old, and it is a good entry point to a deeper question: before we ask whether God exists, what exactly are we asking about?

### The concept of God in classical theism

Many arguments in this course concern the God of **classical theism**, a view developed by thinkers in the Jewish, Christian and Islamic traditions, among them Maimonides, Avicenna (Ibn Sina), Anselm and Thomas Aquinas. On this view God is a personal being who is the creator and sustainer of everything else, and is **omnipotent** (all-powerful), **omniscient** (all-knowing) and **morally perfect**. Many classical theists add that God is eternal, necessary (cannot fail to exist), **simple** (without parts) and independent of everything else. One method, **perfect being theology**, starts from Anselm's idea of God as a being than which nothing greater can be conceived and asks what attributes such a being would have.

Not every theist accepts the classical package. **Process theologians**, following Whitehead and Hartshorne, deny that God has unlimited coercive power; they hold that God influences the world by persuasion. Maimonides held that we can say what God is not more safely than what God is. Hindu traditions differ: Ramanuja taught a personal God with attributes, while Shankara's Advaita Vedanta takes ultimate reality, Brahman, to be without attributes. A good argument must say which concept of God it targets.

### Omnipotence

A first definition: a being is omnipotent if it can do anything. This cannot be right, because "anything" includes the impossible. Can God make a four-sided triangle, or make it true that 2 + 2 = 5? Aquinas (Summa Theologiae I, question 25, article 3) replied that God can do everything that is possible absolutely, and that what implies a contradiction is not something that can be done at all: such descriptions say nothing, so failing to do them does not limit God. A refined definition runs: **a being is omnipotent if and only if it can bring about any state of affairs whose obtaining is logically possible**. Others add a restriction to what is consistent with God's nature; Anselm (Proslogion 7) argued that an inability to lie or to be corrupted is no lack of power, but a mark of it. A minority view, sometimes attributed to Descartes, though interpreters disagree about what he meant, holds that God's power is not bound even by logic.

### The paradox of the stone

| P1 | Either God can create a stone that God cannot lift, or God cannot create such a stone. |
| P2 | If God can create such a stone, then there is something God cannot do (lift it). |
| P3 | If God cannot create such a stone, then there is something God cannot do (create it). |
| C1 | So there is something God cannot do. |
| P4 | If there is something God cannot do, God is not omnipotent. |
| C | So God is not omnipotent. |

The argument is valid: P1 to P3 give C1 by a constructive dilemma, and C1 with P4 gives C by modus ponens. Since validity is not in doubt, a reply must deny a premise.

**Reply 1: the task is incoherent.** On the definition of omnipotence as the power to bring about whatever is logically possible, the description "a stone that an omnipotent being cannot lift" is self-contradictory when the being in question is omnipotent: whatever can be lifted, an omnipotent being can lift. So there is nothing here for God to be unable to do, and this reply denies **P3**. George Mavrodes defended a reply of this kind, calling the task a pseudo-task.

**Reply 2: omnipotence is not bound by logic.** If God's power extends even to the logically impossible, then God can create a stone and also lift it, and the supposed inability does not follow. This reply denies **P2**. Harry Frankfurt discussed it as a way out of the paradox. Its cost is that it appears to abandon the intelligibility of the concept: if God can do the logically impossible, it is unclear what "God can do X" excludes.

There are further replies, for instance that God could limit or give up power by free choice; a classical theist will answer that a being that can lose its power is not omnipotent in the first place. A critic of the whole project may reply in turn that refining the concept until the paradox disappears makes omnipotence a weak notion; a defender answers that the refined definition does not weaken God at all, since the excluded tasks are not tasks.

### Omniscience and goodness

**Omniscience** is usually defined as follows: a being is omniscient if and only if for every true proposition it knows that proposition and it believes no false proposition. This definition raises questions about knowledge of tensed and first-person facts (what it is for God to know what time it is now) and about foreknowledge of free actions, a topic beyond this course. **Moral perfection** raises the Euthyphro question, from Plato's dialogue, adapted to God: is something good because God wills it, or does God will it because it is good? If the first, goodness seems arbitrary; if the second, goodness seems independent of God. Replies include views on which God's own nature is the standard of goodness.

@example
title: Checking validity and finding the denied premise
problem: A student says: "The paradox of the stone is invalid, because C1 does not follow from P1 to P3." Evaluate this, and then say which premise is denied by the claim that "God can create such a stone and still lift it."
step: Set out what P1 to P3 say. P1: A or B. P2: if A then something God cannot do. P3: if B then something God cannot do.
step: Whichever of A and B holds, P2 or P3 yields that there is something God cannot do. This is a constructive dilemma, so C1 follows. The student is wrong.
step: The claim that God can create the stone and still lift it denies that creating the stone leaves God unable to lift it. That is exactly the connection asserted in P2.
answer: C1 follows validly. The claim denies P2.

@example
title: Two definitions of omnipotence
problem: Apply two definitions: Def A, "an omnipotent being can do anything whatever"; Def B, "an omnipotent being can bring about any state of affairs whose obtaining is logically possible". For each of (i) create a galaxy and (ii) create a four-sided triangle, say whether the being can do it.
step: A galaxy's existing involves no contradiction, so on Def B the being can create one. Def A also says it can.
step: A triangle with four sides is a contradiction, since a triangle by definition has three sides. On Def B the being cannot, and it is no limit on its power. On Def A the being can.
step: So the two definitions agree about the galaxy and differ about the four-sided triangle.
answer: (i) both definitions: yes. (ii) Def A: yes; Def B: no, and this is no limit on power.

@mistakes
- Saying that the stone paradox shows that the concept of omnipotence is contradictory. It shows that a naive definition ("can do anything") generates trouble; the refined definition escapes it.
- Saying that the refined definition limits God. On Def B, the excluded tasks are logically impossible, so not being able to do them is not lacking any power.
- Treating omniscience as only knowledge of the future. It covers every true proposition.
- Assuming that all theists accept the classical attributes. Process theology and some other traditions reject some of them.
