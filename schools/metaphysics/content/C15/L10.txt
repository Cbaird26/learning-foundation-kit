@article
The free will defense shows only that God and evil are logically compatible. Compatibility does not settle the question. A person can believe that it is possible that no one in a village committed a crime, and still think the evidence shows that someone did. The **evidential problem of evil** says that the kinds and amounts of suffering in the world are strong evidence against the existence of an all-powerful, all-knowing and perfectly good God, without claiming inconsistency.

### Rowe's argument

William Rowe (1979, "The Problem of Evil and Some Varieties of Atheism") gave the best-known form. He asked us to consider a fawn trapped in a forest fire started by lightning, which suffers terribly for days before dying, and no one is aware of it. In standard form:

| P1 | There exist instances of intense suffering that an omnipotent, omniscient being could have prevented without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. |
| P2 | An omniscient, wholly good being would prevent the occurrence of any intense suffering it could, unless it could not do so without thereby losing some greater good or permitting some evil equally bad or worse. |
| C | So there does not exist an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being. |

The argument is valid: if such a being existed, P2 says it would prevent every instance of suffering it could prevent without losing a greater good or permitting an equal or worse evil, and P1 says that there are instances it could have prevented in that way. It is called *evidential* because of how P1 is supported. No one can show that P1 is true; Rowe supports it by an inductive inference: we cannot think of any greater good that would justify allowing the fawn's suffering, and so, probably, there is none. Sometimes this is called a **"no-see-um" inference** (from the name of tiny biting insects: if you look closely and see none, you infer there are none). If that inference is good, P1 is probable, and the conclusion is, to that degree, supported.

P2 can also be challenged. Some theists hold that a good being may allow some suffering that serves no specific further good, if, for example, a world governed by regular natural laws could not avoid it. Most discussion, though, accepts P2 and attacks the support for P1.

### Skeptical theism

**Skeptical theism** attacks the inference that supports P1. Its main claim is that, if God exists, God's knowledge of goods and of how they connect is vastly greater than ours, so that our inability to see a justifying good gives little reason to think that none exists. Stephen Wykstra (1984) developed the idea in terms of a condition on when "it appears that there is no good" is a reasonable basis for inference; William Alston (1991) argued from our cognitive limits; Michael Bergmann (2001) applied the position to Rowe's later arguments. Compare a child who cannot see any reason for a painful medical procedure: her failure to see a reason is poor evidence that there is none. In the argument, skeptical theism attacks the support for **P1**, not P1's role in a valid argument.

Objections include these. First, the **moral skepticism objection**: if we cannot judge whether a good would justify suffering, then we cannot tell whether we should prevent suffering, and we may stand by when we should help. Skeptical theists reply that our moral duties rest on other grounds, such as duties not to harm and to help, that do not depend on knowing God's reasons. Second, the position seems to **undermine confidence in God's goodness** or in revelation: if we cannot know what a good God would do, we cannot rely on any claim about God's purposes. Skeptical theists reply that the skepticism is limited to the specific question of whether a good would justify a particular evil. Third, critics say that the same reasoning would block no-see-um inferences everywhere, but defenders reply that they are weak only where the inferrer lacks reason to think she would see the good, as in this case.

### Theodicies

A theodicy replies to P1 directly by naming a good that justifies the evil. The **free will theodicy** appeals to the value of free choice. **Soul-making theodicies**, associated with John Hick (*Evil and the God of Love*, 1966), who drew on a tradition he traced to Irenaeus, say that a world in which creatures can grow in character through difficulty is better than a world without difficulty. **Natural law theodicies** say that stable laws, needed for rational agency, sometimes produce suffering. Objections concern **horrendous evils** (Marilyn McCord Adams) and animal suffering, which, critics say, no such good justifies, especially suffering that no one could learn from. Defenders reply that goods may be realized in an afterlife or for other kinds of creatures.

### A probabilistic version

Paul Draper (1989) formulated the argument comparing theism with a rival, the **hypothesis of indifference**: that the world was not shaped by the moral concerns of any powerful being. He argued that the pattern of pleasure and pain we find is more probable on indifference than on theism. A theist can reply that the likelihoods are hard to assess for the reasons skeptical theism gives, or that other evidence, such as the arguments of earlier weeks, favors theism. The question becomes how such considerations are to be weighed together.

@example
title: Validity, the key premise, and the objection
problem: Consider Rowe's argument (P1, P2, C). (i) Is it valid? (ii) Which premise does the skeptical theist's reply target, and what exactly is attacked? (iii) Which premise does a theodicy naming a justifying good target?
step: (i) Suppose an omnipotent, omniscient, wholly good being exists. By P2 it prevents any intense suffering it could prevent without a greater good being lost or an equal or worse evil permitted. By P1, there is some suffering of that kind that it could have prevented. So such a being would have prevented it, yet it occurs, which contradicts the existence of such a being. The argument is valid.
step: (ii) The skeptical theist grants the argument's form but attacks the inference that we have reason to accept P1, namely that because we see no justifying good there is none. So the target is the support for P1.
step: (iii) A theodicy says that a good does justify the evil, so the antecedent of P1 fails. It denies P1.
answer: The argument is valid. Skeptical theism targets the support for P1; a theodicy denies P1.

@example
title: How much does failing to see a good lower its probability?
problem: Suppose, as an illustration, that the probability that a justifying good exists for a given evil is 0.5 before we look. If a justifying good exists, the probability that we would fail to see it is 0.5; if none exists, we certainly fail to see one. What is the probability that a justifying good exists, given that we do not see one?
step: Name the events: G is "a justifying good exists" and N is "we do not see one". Then $\Pr(G) = 0.5$, $\Pr(N \mid G) = 0.5$ and $\Pr(N \mid \neg G) = 1$.
step: Bayes's theorem gives $\Pr(G \mid N) = \Pr(N \mid G)\Pr(G) / (\Pr(N \mid G)\Pr(G) + \Pr(N \mid \neg G)\Pr(\neg G))$.
step: The numerator is 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 and the other term is 1 × 0.5 = 0.5, so the probability is 0.25 / 0.75 = 1/3.
step: So not seeing a good lowers the probability from 0.5 to about 0.33. How far it falls depends on how likely we would be to see the good if it existed; the skeptical theist says that this likelihood is low, and the critic says it is not low enough to remove the force. The numbers are illustrations, not estimates.
answer: 1/3, about 33.3 percent.

@mistakes
- Saying that the evidential argument is invalid because it is probabilistic. In Rowe's form the argument is valid, and the evidence concerns how P1 is supported.
- Saying that skeptical theism denies that evil exists, or that God exists. It attacks the inference from "we see no justifying good" to "there is none".
- Confusing a defense with a theodicy. A theodicy tries to say what God's reasons are; skeptical theism says only that we are in a poor position to rule them out.
- Taking the evidential problem to be answered by the free will defense. The defense shows only logical compatibility.
