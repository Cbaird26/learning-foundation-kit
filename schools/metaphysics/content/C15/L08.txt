@article
A woman walking alone in the hills is suddenly overwhelmed by a sense that she is in the presence of something vast and good. She cannot put it fully into words, and the sense stays with her for years. A man in a quite different tradition sits in meditation and reports a state in which the sense of being a separate self drops away. Both report experiences that they call religious, and both treat them as a reason for their religious outlook. The **argument from religious experience** asks whether such experiences give reasons that the rest of us can weigh.

### Kinds of religious experience

William James in *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902) described mystical states by four marks: **ineffability** (they resist adequate description), a **noetic quality** (they seem to give knowledge), **transiency** (they do not last) and **passivity** (the subject feels grasped by a power not their own). Rudolf Otto, in *The Idea of the Holy* (1917), described the **numinous**: an experience of a *mysterium tremendum et fascinans*, a mystery that inspires awe and attraction, felt as wholly other. W. T. Stace distinguished **extrovertive** mysticism, in which unity is perceived in the outer world, from **introvertive** mysticism, in which all content of consciousness falls away. Experiences also differ in what they seem to be of: a personal God, an impersonal absolute, emptiness, or nothing at all; and in how they arise, whether spontaneously, in prayer, in practice or under the influence of drugs.

Two views of their relation to tradition compete. **Perennialists** hold that there is a common core of mystical experience beneath differences of interpretation. **Constructivists**, such as Steven Katz, hold that the beliefs and practices of a tradition shape the experience itself, so that experiences in different traditions are different, and cannot independently confirm one another.

### The argument

Richard Swinburne has defended a principle of **credulity**: in the absence of special considerations, it is rational to believe that things are as they seem to be. William Alston, in *Perceiving God* (1991), argued that the experience of God is a way of perceiving, and that the practice of forming beliefs from such experience is rationally acceptable, in the same way as the practice of forming beliefs from the senses. Set out as a valid argument with a modest conclusion:

| P1 | If it seems to a subject, in an experience, that something is present, and the subject has no undefeated reason to doubt this, then the subject has some reason to believe that it is present. |
| P2 | Many people have had experiences in which it seemed to them that God was present. |
| P3 | For at least some of these people, there is no undefeated reason to doubt that experience. |
| C1 | So at least some people have some reason to believe that God was present. |
| P4 | Anyone who has some reason to believe that God was present has some reason to believe that God exists. |
| C | So at least some people have some reason to believe that God exists. |

The argument is valid. P1 is the **principle of credulity**; it is the key premise, and P3 is its partner: the argument supplies reason only so long as the experience is undefeated. The conclusion is modest. It says that some people have some reason, not that everyone does or that the reason is decisive; and it does not say what the experience was of.

### Objections and replies

1. **Against P1: checkability.** In sense perception we can corroborate experiences by other senses, by other observers and by prediction; religious experiences lack such checks. Alston replied that the reliability of sense perception cannot be shown without relying on sense perception, so every basic practice is in the same position; religious practice has its own internal checks. Critics reply that a practice without independent checks is not as well supported as perception, whose results converge across people.
2. **Against P3: naturalistic explanation.** Religious-type experiences can be produced by drugs, by stimulating the brain, or by fatigue and expectation, and they are correlated with brain activity. The objection says that if the experience would have occurred whether or not God exists, it is not evidence. Defenders reply that all experience, including ordinary sight, is accompanied by brain activity, and that the cause of a belief does not decide its truth; it is a mistake to infer falsehood from origin. A debunker can answer that the objection does not rely on origin alone but on the claim that the experiences are produced by the same causes whatever is real, which would defeat them; defenders reply that this must be shown, not assumed.
3. **Against P3: diversity.** Experiences in different traditions seem to conflict: a personal God, an impersonal absolute, emptiness, the absence of any divine presence. If they conflict, they cannot all be right, so they defeat each other. Replies include **pluralism** (John Hick held that they are responses to one ultimate reality interpreted differently, which Week 12 evaluates), the view that some descriptions are partial, and the view that conflict is limited to doctrines, not experiences. Critics reply that the conflicts are real and that these replies reinterpret the experiences.
4. **Against the strength of the conclusion.** Even if the experience gives the experiencer reason, others hear only testimony, which is weaker; and the lack of such experiences in other people, which some atheists press, shows only that the evidence is not available to everyone.

### What the argument shows

The argument is better at showing that religious belief can be rational for those who have such experiences than at persuading outsiders. This is also its limit: its strength depends on P1, on whether defeaters apply, and on how the diversity of experience is read.

@example
title: Locating the objections
problem: Take the argument above with P1 (credulity), P3 (no undefeated reason to doubt) and C. Which premise does each objection attack? (a) Religious experiences can be reproduced by drugs and brain stimulation. (b) Experiences in different traditions contradict one another. (c) Unlike sense experience, religious experience cannot be checked by other observers, so a credulity principle for it is not justified.
step: P1 is the general credulity principle; P3 says that, for some experiences, no defeater applies.
step: (a) proposes a defeater: a naturalistic explanation that would apply whether or not God exists. It attacks P3.
step: (b) proposes another defeater: conflict among experiences. It attacks P3.
step: (c) says that credulity is not justified for an unchecked kind of experience, so it attacks P1.
answer: (a) P3; (b) P3; (c) P1.

@example
title: Classifying experiences
problem: Classify each report using the terms of the lesson. (i) "I felt dread and fascination at once, before something utterly unlike anything in the world." (ii) "In deep meditation every content of my mind dropped away and only awareness remained." (iii) "Looking at the forest, I saw all things as one."
step: (i) combines awe, fear and attraction before something wholly other, which is the numinous in Otto's sense.
step: (ii) is an experience in which all content of consciousness falls away, which Stace calls introvertive mysticism.
step: (iii) is a unity perceived in the outer world, which Stace calls extrovertive mysticism.
answer: (i) numinous; (ii) introvertive mystical; (iii) extrovertive mystical.

@mistakes
- Treating the conclusion as "God exists". The argument shows only that some people have some reason to believe it, and the reason can be defeated.
- Committing the genetic fallacy in either direction. That an experience has a natural cause does not by itself show it is false, and that it feels real does not show it is true; what matters is whether the cause would operate whatever is real.
- Treating all religious experiences as experiences of a God. Traditions report experiences of an impersonal absolute, of emptiness and of other things, and this is why the argument from diversity matters.
- Confusing James's four marks of mystical experience with Otto's account of the numinous; they are different analyses.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how the argument from religious diversity attacks the argument from religious experience, state the premise it attacks, and give the best reply of a defender.
hint: Think of the conflicting reports (a personal God, an impersonal absolute, emptiness) and of the idea of a defeater.
solution: The experiences reported in different traditions seem to conflict: they present God as a person, as an impersonal absolute, as emptiness, or as absent. If they conflict, they cannot all be accurate, so the existence of conflicting experiences gives each experiencer a reason to doubt their own, which would be a defeater, so the objection attacks P3. The best reply is that conflict between interpretations does not show conflict between experiences: pluralists say that the experiences are responses to one ultimate reality, differently conceptualized; or a defender may say that the traditions' descriptions overlap and complement one another, or that the experiencer's own tradition gives them reasons to rank their experience. A critic answers that these replies reinterpret what the experiences claimed and that the doctrines do conflict.
