@article
A hiker, walking across open country, kicks a stone and wonders how it came to be there; the answer, "it has always lain there," satisfies her. Then she finds a watch. It has many parts, each shaped to fit the others, and together they keep time. The answer "it has always lain there" would no longer satisfy her: the watch looks made. William Paley opened his *Natural Theology* (1802) with this contrast and argued that living things show the same kind of adaptation, only more intricate, so they too must have a maker. **Design arguments**, also called **teleological arguments** (from Greek *telos*, "end" or "purpose"), reason from order, purpose or adaptation in nature to an intelligent designer.

### The argument

Paley's reasoning is an argument from analogy. In standard form, with the analogical principle stated explicitly:

| P1 | Artifacts such as watches display complex adaptation of parts to a function, and they are produced by intelligent design. |
| P2 | Organisms display complex adaptation of parts to a function. |
| P3 | If two kinds of things display such adaptation, one kind is produced by intelligent design, and the adaptation of the other kind has no better-supported explanation than design, then the other kind is probably also produced by intelligent design. |
| P4 | The adaptation of organisms has no better-supported explanation than design. |
| C | So organisms are probably produced by intelligent design. |

The argument is valid: P1, P2 and P4 give the three conditions in the antecedent of P3, and modus ponens delivers C. Paley's heath example supports P1 and P2; the whole weight rests on P3 and P4. The conclusion is that organisms are probably designed, not that the designer is the God of any tradition.

### Hume's objections

David Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* (published 1779, after his death) criticizes design arguments through the character Philo, who answers Cleanthes, the defender of the argument. Philo's objections include these. The analogy is weak: the universe is unlike a house or a machine in many ways, and an analogy is only as strong as the similarity. We have experience of the making of artifacts but none of the making of worlds, and a world is a unique effect, so we have no repeated cases to generalize from. And even if the argument worked, it would give no reason to think the designer is infinite, unique, perfect or good: worlds are made by many hands or by a finite one, and the dialogue suggests alternatives. Another worry is whether a designer would need its own explanation. These objections attack **P3** (the strength of the analogical principle), and the last attacks **P4**, if a designer is claimed to be no better explanation than the thing explained. Scholars disagree about which of Philo's remarks Hume himself endorsed, especially in Part XII.

### The Darwinian objection

Charles Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859) offered an explanation of adaptation without a designer: **natural selection**. Organisms vary, some variations are heritable, and those that help organisms survive and reproduce become more common over many generations. Selection is not mere chance: variation is random with respect to what is useful, but selection is not. Small improvements accumulate, so complex adaptation can arise step by step. This attacks **P4**: if natural selection explains adaptation at least as well as design does, then design is not unrivaled. It does not attack P1, P2 or P3, and it does not by itself refute theism: many theists accept evolution (**theistic evolution**), holding that God works through natural processes.

### Replies and further moves

1. **Irreducible complexity.** Michael Behe (*Darwin's Black Box*, 1996) argued that certain biochemical systems, whose parts all seem needed for the function, could not arise by gradual selection, so Darwin's explanation fails for them. This is the **intelligent design** movement's main argument. The great majority of biologists reject it, citing detailed gradual pathways for systems of the sort Behe discussed, and scientific bodies do not treat it as established science; defenders reply that the pathways are speculative.
2. **Design at the level of laws.** Richard Swinburne argues that evolution presupposes laws of nature that are simple and orderly, and that this order, not the adaptation of individual organisms, is what a designer best explains. Critics reply that the laws may be brute, or that a multiverse or a deeper theory may explain them.
3. **Complexity of the designer.** Richard Dawkins argued that a designer capable of designing life would itself be complex and improbable, so design explains little (his "ultimate Boeing 747" argument, in *The God Delusion*, 2006). Defenders reply that classical theism takes God to be simple, not a complex assembly of parts, and that a personal explanation can stop at an intelligent agent.
4. **Aquinas's Fifth Way** (*Summa Theologiae* I, question 2, article 3) argues from the goal-directed behavior of natural bodies, which act for ends although they lack intelligence, to an intelligent director. It differs from Paley's argument: its target is the ordinary directedness of natural processes, not the complexity of organisms.

Thoughtful people disagree about whether any of these succeeds. A fair evaluation says what each objection attacks, and does not call design arguments refuted merely because evolution exists, or confirmed merely because order exists.

title: Finding the weak premise
problem: Use the design argument in the lesson (P1 to P4, C). A critic says: "A watch is built from known materials by known craftspeople, but nothing is known about how worlds or organisms are made, so the resemblance is too thin for the analogy to be reliable." Which premise does the critic attack, and is the argument valid?
step: Check validity first. P1, P2 and P4 together supply the three conditions in the antecedent of P3, and P3 then gives C by modus ponens. So the argument is valid, and any reply must deny a premise.
step: The critic accepts that watches are designed and that organisms display complex adaptation, so P1 and P2 are not attacked. The critic does not say that a rival explanation is better, so P4 is not attacked either.
step: The critic says that the resemblance between watches and organisms is too thin to support the inference, which is the claim made by P3.
answer: The argument is valid. The critic attacks P3.

@example
title: Matching four objections to premises
problem: Which premise, if any, does each objection attack? (a) Natural selection explains complex adaptation at least as well as intelligent design. (b) A designer would itself need to be explained, so design is no better an explanation than what it explains. (c) Even if organisms were designed, the designer need not be omnipotent or good. (d) The world is a unique effect, so we cannot reliably extend what we know about artifacts.
step: List the premises: P1 and P2 are about artifacts and organisms; P3 is the analogical principle; P4 says that no explanation is better than design.
step: (a) and (b) both say that design is not the best explanation, so they attack P4. (a) offers a better rival; (b) says that design is no better.
step: (c) accepts that organisms are probably designed and says only that it does not follow that the designer is the God of theism, so it attacks no premise and concerns what the conclusion shows.
step: (d) says that the basis for the analogy is too weak, which is a challenge to P3.
answer: (a) P4; (b) P4; (c) no premise (the step to theism); (d) P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that Darwin refuted the design argument. His theory attacks P4 if it explains adaptation better than design, but many theists accept evolution, and arguments from the laws of nature remain.
- Describing natural selection as pure chance. Variation is random with respect to usefulness, but selection is not random.
- Treating the conclusion of Paley's argument as a proof of God. It says that organisms are probably designed, not that the designer is omnipotent, unique or good.
- Confusing Paley's argument from adaptation with Aquinas's Fifth Way, which argues from goal-directed behavior of natural bodies.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how Swinburne's argument from the regularity of nature differs from Paley's argument from adaptation, and say why the Darwinian objection does not touch it directly.
hint: Ask what each argument says needs to be explained, and what natural selection explains.
solution: Paley's argument starts from the adaptation of parts to function in organisms and infers a designer from that. Swinburne's argument starts from the fact that nature is governed by simple, orderly laws, and holds that this order is better explained by an intelligent agent than by brute chance or necessity. Natural selection explains how adaptive complexity can arise given the laws and conditions, so it attacks the premise that adaptation has no better explanation than design. It does not explain why there are such laws at all, because it presupposes them. So the Darwinian objection does not directly touch an argument whose explanandum is the laws. Critics reply that the laws might be brute, might be explained by a deeper theory, or might be one among many in a multiverse.
