@article
Two questions about the universe are easy to run together. Did it have a beginning? And why does it exist at all, rather than nothing? **Cosmological arguments** reason from a very general feature of the world, such as that it began, or that it might not have existed, to a first cause or a necessary being. They fall into two families. The **kalam** argument starts from the claim that the universe began to exist. **Contingency** arguments start from the claim that the universe, or the things in it, depend on something else, and they do not need a beginning at all. This week covers the first family and introduces the second; Week 4 studies the principle that the second depends on.

### The kalam argument

*Kalam* is the Arabic name for a tradition of theological reasoning in Islam. Thinkers in that tradition, such as al-Kindi and al-Ghazali, and earlier the Christian John Philoponus, argued that the world cannot be eternal. The argument was revived in the 20th century by William Lane Craig. In standard form:

| P1 | Whatever begins to exist has a cause of its beginning to exist. |
| P2 | The universe began to exist. |
| C | So the universe has a cause of its beginning to exist. |

The argument is valid (the universe is among the things that begin to exist, so P1 applies to it, by instantiation and modus ponens). Defenders then argue that a cause of the whole universe, including all space, time and matter, would have to be uncaused, immaterial, timeless and immensely powerful, and, on Craig's argument, personal, since only a free agent could produce a first effect from a timeless cause that does not change. That second stage is a further argument with its own premises; critics ask why the cause could not be some impersonal process, or why there should be only one. The conclusion C by itself says only that the universe has a cause.

### Support for the premises

For **P1**, defenders appeal to the principle that something cannot come from nothing, and to our experience that things that begin have causes.

For **P2**, two kinds of support are offered. The first is philosophical. Defenders argue that an actual infinity of past events is impossible, because an actual infinite collection leads to absurdities, such as **Hilbert's Hotel**, a hotel with infinitely many rooms that is full but can still accommodate new guests, by moving each guest from room $n$ to room $n+1$ (or any number of new guests, by moving each from room $n$ to room $n+k$). They add that a beginningless series of events could not be completed by successive addition. The second kind is scientific. The standard cosmological model describes the expansion of the universe from an extremely hot and dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. But that model's earliest moments are not described by any accepted theory, since there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity, so whether the Big Bang was an absolute beginning or a transition from something earlier remains open. Some proposed models have no first moment at all.

### Objections and replies

1. **Against P2 (philosophical support).** Hilbert's Hotel is strange but not contradictory. After Cantor, mathematicians accept that an infinite set can be matched one-to-one with a part of itself, which is exactly what the hotel does. The reply is that mathematical infinity is one thing and an infinite collection of real events another, and that the second leads to absurdity even if the first does not. Critics respond that this assumes what is to be shown.
2. **Against P2 (scientific support).** The science does not establish an absolute beginning, and some models avoid one. Defenders reply that they are speculative and that known results suggest that inflating universes cannot be past-eternal; critics reply that those results do not cover all models.
3. **Against P1.** Hume held that we can conceive of something beginning without a cause, so the causal principle is not known a priori; defenders reply, as Elizabeth Anscombe argued, that Hume's argument does not actually show this. Some interpretations of quantum mechanics treat some events as uncaused; defenders reply that such events occur within a given physical setting and are not beginnings from nothing. A further objection is that P1 is known only for things within the universe, and the universe is not a thing of that kind.
4. **Against the further steps.** The cause need not be a personal God. This attacks the inference from C to theism, not P1 or P2.

### The contingency argument, in outline

A being is **contingent** if it might not have existed, and **necessary** if it could not have failed to exist. Leibniz asked why there is something rather than nothing (*Principles of Nature and Grace*, section 7) and answered that the ultimate reason must lie in a necessary being. An argument of this family does not need a beginning of the universe: even a universe that had always existed would, if contingent, call for an explanation. Aquinas's Third Way (*Summa Theologiae* I, question 2, article 3) and Avicenna's argument from necessary and possible existence belong to this family. Because it runs differently from the kalam argument, an objection to one need not touch the other.

@example
title: Which premise does the objection attack?
problem: Take the kalam argument: P1, whatever begins to exist has a cause of its beginning to exist; P2, the universe began to exist; C, the universe has a cause of its beginning to exist. For each objection, say which premise it attacks, or whether it attacks neither. (a) Some models of cosmology have no first moment of time. (b) Even if the universe has a cause, that cause might be an impersonal process. (c) Hume held that an uncaused beginning is conceivable, so P1 is not known a priori.
step: Match the claim of each objection to the content of a premise. P1 is about all beginnings; P2 is about the universe in particular.
step: (a) says the universe may not have begun to exist, so it attacks P2.
step: (b) accepts the conclusion C that there is a cause and denies only that the cause must be a personal God, so it attacks neither premise; it attacks the extended inference to God.
step: (c) denies that we know P1 a priori, so it attacks P1.
answer: (a) P2; (b) neither premise (the step from C to God); (c) P1.

@example
title: The arithmetic of Hilbert's Hotel
problem: A hotel has rooms numbered 1, 2, 3, ... without end, and every room is occupied. Four new guests arrive. Each current guest moves from room $n$ to room $n+4$. Which rooms are free for the newcomers, and where does the guest from room 15 go? Why is this not a contradiction?
step: The guest from room $n$ moves to $n+4$, so the guest from room 15 moves to room 19.
step: Every room number $m$ greater than 4 is now occupied by the guest who came from $m-4$, and rooms 1, 2, 3 and 4 are occupied by no one, so those four rooms are free.
step: No guest is left without a room, since $n \mapsto n+4$ gives each guest a different room. The hotel is full before and after; the shift is a one-to-one matching of the infinite set of rooms with a proper part of itself.
step: Matching an infinite collection one-to-one with a part of itself is the standard mark of an infinite set in modern set theory (Dedekind made it the definition of an infinite set), so the hotel is paradoxical only to intuition drawn from finite collections. Whether this carries over to a collection of real events is the point in dispute.
answer: Rooms 1 to 4 are free; the guest from room 15 goes to room 19; there is no contradiction, only an infinite set matched with a part of itself.

@mistakes
- Saying the kalam argument proves the existence of God. Its conclusion C is that the universe has a cause; later steps are needed to reach any further claim.
- Confusing "whatever begins to exist has a cause" with "everything has a cause". P1 is about things that begin; it does not imply that the cause itself needs a cause.
- Treating the Big Bang as established to be an absolute beginning. The standard model describes expansion from a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago, but no accepted theory describes the earliest moments.
- Running together the kalam argument and the contingency argument. The first needs a beginning; the second does not.

hint: Compare the form "everything that F is G; a is G; so a is F" with the correct form of the kalam argument.
solution: The argument infers that something began to exist from the claim that it has a cause. Something might have a cause without having begun to exist (a cause could be eternal), so the premises could be true while the conclusion is false. That is affirming the consequent, which is invalid. The kalam argument runs the other way: it infers that the universe has a cause from the claim that it began.

prompt: Give the strongest objection you can to P1 of the kalam argument (whatever begins to exist has a cause), and then the strongest reply a defender could give. State the premise your objection attacks.
hint: Decide first whether your objection is about our knowledge of P1 or about its truth.
solution: Objection (to P1): The principle is supposed to be known a priori or by experience. A priori, Hume held that we can conceive of something beginning without a cause, and conceivability is at least some evidence of possibility; by experience, we have only observed things beginning within the universe, from earlier states, which gives no basis for extending the principle to the beginning of the universe itself. Reply: Conceivability alone is weak evidence about metaphysical possibility; the principle that being cannot arise from nothing is better supported than any rival, since an uncaused beginning would leave unexplained why things begin at one time and place and not elsewhere; and the universe as a whole is not shown to be exempt, since nothing is. The objection attacks P1.
