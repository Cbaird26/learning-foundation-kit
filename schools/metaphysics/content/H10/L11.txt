@article
A **research paper** answers a question using evidence from several sources and your own reasoning. It is built on a process, not a single sitting.

### From topic to question

A broad topic ("space") is too big. Narrow it with **who, what, where, when, why**: "How did the Apollo missions influence engineering careers in the 1970s?" A good **research question** is focused, answerable with evidence, and open to more than yes or no. Your **thesis** is your tentative answer, and it may change as you learn.

### Finding and recording sources

Search with **keywords** and synonyms, use quotation marks for phrases, and use library databases for scholarly material. Use the evaluation criteria from the last unit: authority, accuracy, purpose, currency and corroboration. For each source, record the full citation information at once (author, title, publisher or website, date, URL or page numbers) so you can cite it later.

### Notes: quote, paraphrase, summarize

- **Direct quotation**: exact words in quotation marks, used when the wording itself matters.
- **Paraphrase**: your own wording and sentence structure for a specific passage, still credited. Changing a few words is not a paraphrase.
- **Summary**: a short version of the main idea of a whole source.

Mark in your notes which words are copied and which are yours.

### Plagiarism and citation

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own, intentionally or not. Avoid it by quoting exactly, paraphrasing honestly and **citing** every borrowed idea, fact or quotation (except common knowledge). Two common styles:

- **MLA** (humanities): parenthetical citation with author and page, like (Rivera 45), and a **Works Cited** list.
- **APA** (social sciences): author and year, like (Rivera, 2021), and a **References** list.

Follow the style your teacher assigns, and be consistent.

### Organizing and drafting

1. Write a working thesis.
2. Group notes by idea and make an **outline** with main points and supporting evidence.
3. Draft with a clear introduction, body paragraphs that each make a point supported by cited evidence and your analysis, and a conclusion.
4. Integrate sources with signal phrases ("According to Rivera,") and then **explain**: your voice should stay in control, with sources as support.

### Revising and editing

Check that every paragraph advances the thesis, every source is cited in the text and listed at the end, and sentences are polished. Read it aloud and have someone else read it.

@example
title: Paraphrasing without plagiarizing
problem: Original: "Coral reefs cover less than one percent of the ocean floor but support about a quarter of all marine species." Write an acceptable paraphrase and note the citation.
step: Understand the idea: reefs are small in area yet hold a large share of ocean life.
step: Write it in your own structure: "Although reefs occupy a tiny part of the sea floor, a large share of marine species depends on them."
step: Credit the source: add a citation, for example (Chen 12), because the idea and fact are not yours.
step: Compare with the original: no long phrases are copied, and the meaning is the same.

@mistakes
- Changing a few words of the original and calling it a paraphrase.
- Forgetting to record citation details while researching.
- Dropping in quotations without introducing or explaining them.
- Treating the first search results as the best sources.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: Write a research question, a working thesis and a four-point outline for a short paper on how reading aloud affects young children's vocabulary. Name two types of source you would use.
hint: Narrow the topic, make the thesis a tentative answer, and outline by idea.
solution: Question: How does regular reading aloud to preschoolers affect vocabulary growth by the time they enter school? Working thesis: Regular reading aloud increases preschoolers' vocabulary because it exposes them to rarer words than everyday talk. Outline: (1) Define vocabulary growth and how it is measured; (2) Evidence that reading aloud exposes children to more varied words; (3) Studies comparing children with and without daily reading; (4) Limits: the role of interaction and family circumstances. Sources: peer-reviewed studies from a library database and a report from a reputable education or pediatric organization.
rubric: The research question is focused and open to analysis.
rubric: The thesis is a tentative, arguable answer.
rubric: The outline has four logical points, and the named sources are credible.
