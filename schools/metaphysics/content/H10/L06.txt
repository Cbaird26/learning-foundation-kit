@article
Not all information is equally reliable. In research you must decide which sources to trust and whether the evidence they give truly supports their claims.

### Kinds of sources

- A **primary source** is original evidence from the time or event: a diary, speech, photograph, raw data, an interview.
- A **secondary source** interprets primary sources: a history book, a review article, a biography.
- A **tertiary source** summarizes secondary ones: an encyclopedia or textbook.
- **Scholarly** (peer-reviewed) sources are written by experts and reviewed by other experts before publication. **Popular** sources (magazines, news sites) are written for a general audience.

### Judging credibility

Ask five questions of any source (a handy name is the CRAAP test, or just remember these):

1. **Authority**: Who wrote this? What are their qualifications? Who published it?
2. **Accuracy**: Are claims supported by evidence, citations and links? Can you verify them elsewhere?
3. **Purpose and bias**: Why does this exist: to inform, sell, persuade or entertain? Who benefits? Every author has a point of view, but undisclosed interests lower trust.
4. **Currency**: When was it published or updated? Some topics (medicine, technology) go out of date quickly; others (a historical event) do not.
5. **Relevance**: Does it actually address your question at the right depth?

**Corroboration** is the strongest single habit: check whether independent, reliable sources report the same fact. A claim found in only one place deserves caution.

### Judging evidence

Evidence supports a claim only if it is **relevant**, **sufficient** and **representative**. A story about one person is an anecdote, not proof of a trend. Check statistics for the **sample** (how many, how chosen) and the **source** of the numbers.

**Correlation is not causation.** Two things can rise together without one causing the other. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer because of heat, not because ice cream causes drowning. To argue causation you need more: a plausible mechanism, a comparison group, and ruling out other explanations.

### Lateral reading

Rather than reading a website deeply on its own terms, open new tabs and **read laterally**: search the author and the organization and see what independent sources say about them. This is how professional fact-checkers work.

### Recording your judgment

When you use a source in a paper, note why you trust it. A short evaluation ("Written by a pediatric researcher, published in a peer-reviewed journal in 2023, cites 40 studies") is a defensible reason.

@example
title: Judging a source quickly
problem: A website called HealthWins.example claims "Drinking lemon water cures colds in one day." The article is unsigned, undated, sells lemon supplements, and cites no studies. Evaluate its credibility.
step: Authority: no author or credentials, so the claim has no expert behind it.
step: Purpose and bias: the page sells lemon supplements, a financial interest.
step: Accuracy and currency: no citations to check, no date.
step: Corroboration: a quick search of health agencies and review articles would show whether any reliable source supports the claim. Verdict: low credibility; do not rely on it.

@mistakes
- Judging a source by how professional the website looks.
- Treating any source with an agenda as worthless; instead check whether claims are verifiable.
- Confusing correlation with causation.
- Relying on one source for a major fact.
