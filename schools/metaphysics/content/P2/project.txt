title: Seminar paper on a question in the metaphysics of mind
overview: You will write a 5,000- to 7,000-word seminar paper on one question: the hard problem, illusionism, the combination problem or AI consciousness. The paper surveys the relevant literature accurately, reconstructs the strongest opposing view and offers an original argument, and you revise it after written feedback.
goal: Argue for a definite thesis in a form that a reasonable reader could reject.
goal: Reconstruct at least one argument in standard form, valid as stated, and match each objection or reply to the premise it targets.
goal: Represent the literature and any science accurately, and treat the question about artificial intelligence as open, presenting the arguments on each side.
goal: Revise in response to written feedback and explain what you changed and why.
step: Choose one of the four questions in the material below, or a narrower question of your own within one of them, and write a one-paragraph proposal: the question, your tentative thesis, the view you will reconstruct and the plan of the paper (the deliverable of weeks 7 and 8 applies here).
step: Read the course lesson on your topic and the relevant entries of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Use PhilPapers to find recent work, and read at least six primary sources in full. Keep a bibliography of what you have actually read.
step: Reconstruct your target argument in standard form with every premise explicit and the argument valid as stated. Reconstruct the strongest opposing view in the form its best defender would give it, and write a paragraph for each saying which premise is at issue.
step: Write a full draft of 5,000 to 7,000 words, following the structure in Lesson 12: introduction with thesis and roadmap, background, target argument, strongest opposing view, your argument, the best reply to you and your response, conclusion.
step: Check every attribution and quotation against the source, make sure that every reference is to a work you have read, and review claims about experiments and about artificial intelligence for overstatement. Do not state as settled that any AI system does or does not understand or is or is not conscious.
step: Submit the draft for review and, on receiving written feedback (from a tutor, peer or instructor), sort each comment into misunderstanding, gap or disagreement. Decide what to fix, what to supply and what to answer or narrow.
step: Write a revised paper and a one-page response to feedback saying what you changed, what you did not change and why.
step: Prepare a 20-minute version of the talk of about 2,600 words with a one-page handout giving your argument in standard form, and practice answering the three strongest objections.

### Week by week

1. **Problems of consciousness.** Creature, state and transitive consciousness; phenomenal and access consciousness (Block); Nagel's what-it-is-like criterion; Chalmers's easy problems and hard problem; the structure-and-function argument.
2. **Physicalism and the gap.** Levine's explanatory gap is epistemic; the zombie argument (P1 conceivable, P2 conceivability implies possibility, P3 possibility implies physicalism false) and types A, B and C; the knowledge argument and its replies; the phenomenal concept strategy.
3. **Higher-order and representationalist theories.** Rosenthal's transitivity principle; HOT, HOP, dispositional and self-representational variants; the empty-HOT objection; Dretske and Tye; the transparency argument and its premises.
4. **Workspace and integrated information.** Baars, Dehaene and Changeux: broadcast and ignition; Tononi's IIT and Φ; consequences for feed-forward systems and digital hardware; adversarial collaboration.
5. **Illusionism and the meta-problem.** Frankish and Dennett; the meta-problem; the debunking argument; the illusion problem and the denial that seeming is phenomenal.
6. **Panpsychism and combination.** Russellian monism, panpsychism, panprotopsychism, cosmopsychism; the argument from the limits of physics; subject, quality and structure combination; replies.
7. **Mental causation.** Kim's exclusion argument (P1 to P4); replies by premise; the paradox of phenomenal judgment.
8. **The self.** Psychological, animalist and brain-based accounts; the fission argument and Relation R; the thinking-animal argument; split-brain cases.
9. **AI and understanding.** The Chinese room and its replies; symbol grounding; Blockhead; arguments for and against understanding in language models.
10. **AI consciousness.** Functionalist, biological and IIT verdicts; fading qualia; indicators; the gaming problem.
11. **Moral status.** Grounds of status; Singer's argument; the precautionary argument for AI; the expected-weight model.
12. **Writing.** Topic, question, thesis; structure; charitable reconstruction; accuracy; integrity; presenting and refereeing.

### Key skills

State each argument so that it is valid, then say which premise a reply denies. Know the standard names (type-A, type-B, type-C; HOT; PANIC; Φ; the exclusion principle; Relation R; Blockhead) and who is associated with each.

### Self-check

- Can I state the zombie, knowledge, debunking, combination, exclusion, fission, Chinese room, fading qualia and precautionary arguments in standard form?
- For each, can I name a reply to every premise and say who gives it?
- Can I say what each theory of consciousness implies for an artificial system, and why behavior alone is not decisive?
- Can I describe the state of play on artificial intelligence without claiming that any system is or is not conscious or understanding?

### Common errors

Calling a valid argument invalid because its premises are disputed; treating the explanatory gap as a proof; running together HOT and HOP, panpsychism and panprotopsychism, or the systems and robot replies; overclaiming about AI in either direction; citing a source you have not read.

@exam mc
prompt: A philosopher says: "I grant that a zombie world is conceivable, but the explanatory gap is a feature of our phenomenal concepts and not of reality, so conceivability does not show that a zombie world is possible." In the zombie argument (P1 a zombie world is conceivable; P2 if conceivable then metaphysically possible; P3 if possible then physicalism is false), which premise does she deny, and what is her position called?
choice*: P2; a type-B physicalist
choice: P1; a type-A physicalist
choice: P3; a type-C physicalist
choice: P2; a type-A physicalist
hint: She accepts conceivability and denies the step from conceivability to possibility.
solution: She grants P1 and denies P2, the step from conceivability to metaphysical possibility. That is the type-B response, which grants the gap and denies that it shows that physicalism is false. Type-A physicalists deny the conceivability of zombies, and type-C physicalists expect the gap to be closed by future science.

@exam self
prompt: Timed essay (about 45 minutes, 700 to 900 words). Do the debunking argument for illusionism and the paradox of phenomenal judgment pose the same challenge to realism about phenomenal consciousness? Does Russellian monism answer either? Set out each argument in standard form, say which premise a realist should deny, and defend a view.
hint: Begin by stating both arguments in standard form, then ask what each concludes: one is about justification, the other about coincidence.
solution: The debunking argument runs: P1, our judgments that we have phenomenal consciousness can be fully explained without phenomenal consciousness; P2, if a belief can be fully explained without its truth and nothing else supports it, it is unjustified; P3, nothing else supports the belief; C, the belief is unjustified. The paradox of phenomenal judgment starts from the exclusion argument's setting: if phenomenal properties are not physical, our judgments about them are fully caused by physical processes that would occur without them, so the truth of the judgments looks like a coincidence. The two are closely related: both exploit the causal sufficiency of the physical story for our judgments. They differ in their conclusions: the debunking argument concludes that the belief is unjustified, the paradox that, on non-physicalism, its truth would be an unexplained coincidence, which is a cost to the view and not a refutation. A realist should deny P3 of the debunking argument by appealing to acquaintance, since that supports the belief independently of its causal history; the paradox is better met by showing why the judgments track experience. Russellian monism offers exactly that: if the intrinsic natures that realize physical dispositions are phenomenal or protophenomenal, then experience is part of the causal basis of the judgments, so their truth is not a coincidence. It does not by itself answer the debunking argument, since the argument says that the explanation need not mention experience, and the realist must still say why acquaintance survives it. Russellian monism also takes on the combination problem. I conclude that the two arguments share a root but differ in force, and that Russellian monism answers the second better than the first.
rubric: States both arguments accurately in standard form, valid as stated, and identifies at least one premise a realist should deny.
rubric: Distinguishes the conclusions (unjustified belief versus coincidence) and explains their relation.
rubric: Evaluates Russellian monism's answer to each, noting its cost, and defends a view without claiming that either side has won.
