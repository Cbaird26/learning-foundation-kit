Before a trip to Mars, a device scans you, destroys your body and builds an exact copy from new matter on the other planet. Has your journey ended in death or in survival? Questions of this kind, which Derek Parfit made central in *Reasons and Persons* (1984), join the metaphysics of persons to the philosophy of mind, since they ask what the self is, what its persistence consists in and how it relates to the brain.

### What are we?

Three families of answers compete.

- **Psychological accounts**, descending from Locke, say that you persist as long as there is psychological continuity, the overlapping chains of memory, intention, belief and character. Locke's own view is the focus of interpretive debate, and contemporary versions are usually non-circular accounts of continuity with the right sort of cause.
- **Animalism**, defended by Eric Olson in *The Human Animal* (1997) and by Paul Snowdon, says that you are a human animal, an organism, and persist as long as that organism's life continues, whatever happens to your psychology.
- **Brain-based and embodied-mind views** say that you are, or are essentially, the part of the animal that supports mental life: on Jeff McMahan's embodied mind account, you are a mind, essentially a capacity for consciousness, embodied in a brain. These views occupy a middle position between the psychological and the animalist accounts.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, the psychological view drew about 44% of respondents who answered, the biological view about 19%, and the further-fact view about 15%. Those are shares of opinion, not evidence for any view.

### The fission argument

Parfit asks us to suppose that your brain is divided, each hemisphere is transplanted into a new body, and each of the resulting people has your psychology. Call yourself A and the two resulting people B and C. The following argument shows that A cannot be identical to both and that nothing in the case favors one over the other.

| P1 | If A is identical to B and A is identical to C, then B is identical to C. |
| P2 | B is not identical to C (two people who live separate lives). |
| P3 | A is identical to B if and only if A is identical to C (the case is symmetrical). |
| C | So A is identical to neither B nor C. |

The argument is valid. P1 and P2 show that A is not identical to both; P3 says that if A is identical to either it is identical to both; so A is identical to neither. Parfit draws a further moral: A's relation to B and to C contains everything that matters in survival, though it is not identity, so identity is not what matters. He calls the relation **Relation R**, psychological connectedness and continuity with the right kind of cause. Replies attack different premises. David Lewis, in "Survival and Identity" (1976), rejects the description of the case: before fission there were already two persons, sharing their stages, so A is not a single person who becomes two. Others deny **P3** and defend a "closest continuer" view, or hold that A does not survive in either case, or say that the case shows only that identity is not what we should care about. Those who think that Parfit's moral goes too far deny that Relation R is all that matters.

### The thinking-animal argument

Animalists argue as follows.

| P1 | There is a human animal located where you are, and it thinks. |
| P2 | You are a thinking being located where you are. |
| P3 | There is only one thinker located where you are. |
| C | So you are that human animal. |

The argument is valid, since the thinker where you are must be both you and the animal. Psychological theorists reply in different ways. Some deny **P1**, saying that the animal does not think, or that it thinks only derivatively. Lynne Baker's constitution view and Sydney Shoemaker's account deny **P3**: the animal and the person coincide, and either may be said to think in a derivative sense. Animalists reply that this multiplies thinkers and raises the worry of how you could know that you are the person and not the animal.

### What neuroscience adds

Cases in which the two cerebral hemispheres have been disconnected by cutting the corpus callosum offer a natural experiment. Under special laboratory conditions, the hemispheres can be presented with different information and appear to act independently. Thomas Nagel's "Brain Bisection and the Unity of Consciousness" (1971) argued that these cases show that there may be no determinate number of minds in such a subject. Others, including Tim Bayne, have defended a view on which consciousness switches between hemispheres, and still others interpret the cases as two streams. The empirical details and the interpretation are both debated, and the cases are best used as constraints on a theory of unity, not as proofs. Theories that deny a persisting self altogether, from Hume's report in the *Treatise* (1.4.6) that he finds only perceptions when he looks within, to Thomas Metzinger's self-model theory in *Being No One* (2003), treat the sense of a unified self as a construction of the brain's self-model.

### Where things stand

There is no consensus on what persons are, and the debate is closely linked with views of consciousness: a theory of the unity of consciousness has implications for what a self could be, and illusionists, panpsychists and higher-order theorists have each extended their views to the self. @example
title: Checking the fission argument
problem: In a fission case A becomes B and C. Show that the fission argument is valid and say what each of Lewis and an advocate of the closest-continuer view does with it.
step: P1 says that A=B and A=C together would make B=C; P2 says that B≠C. So A=B and A=C cannot both hold.
step: P3 says that A=B iff A=C. If A=B were true, A=C would be true, and then both would hold, which is impossible. So A=B is false, and by the same reasoning A=C is false.
step: Lewis rejects the picture of a single person who divides: there were two persons all along, sharing stages up to the fission.
step: A closest-continuer theorist denies P3 in the case where one of B and C is a better continuer. In a symmetric case, she says, neither is the closest, so A survives as neither.
answer: The argument is valid. Lewis rejects the description of the case, and the closest-continuer view qualifies the symmetry premise.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply deny?
problem: A constitution theorist says: "The animal and the person are two coinciding things, both located where you are. Each thinks, in its own way." Which premise of the thinking-animal argument does she deny?
step: Recall P1 (the animal thinks), P2 (you think), P3 (only one thinker is located where you are).
step: She accepts that the animal thinks and that you think, so she does not deny P1 or P2.
step: She says that there are two thinkers where you are, which denies P3.
answer: P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that the fission argument proves that you cannot survive fission: it shows that you are not identical to both successors, and Parfit adds that what matters in survival is preserved.
- Confusing animalism with the view that you are your brain: the animalist says you are the whole organism.
- Reading the split-brain cases as showing that there are two selves: the interpretations differ and the data underdetermine them.
- Treating Relation R as identity: Parfit's point is that R and identity can come apart.

@quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct the fission argument in standard form and explain in four to five sentences how it supports Parfit's claim about what matters, and one way to resist the move from the argument to that claim.
hint: Remember that the argument itself concludes that A is identical to neither, and the moral about what matters is a further step.
solution: P1: If A is identical to B and to C, then B is identical to C. P2: B is not identical to C. P3: A is identical to B iff A is identical to C. C: A is identical to neither B nor C. If that is so, then what matters can be present without identity, so it is not identity. One way to resist the step is to say that what matters in ordinary survival depends on identity, and that the fission case is an anomaly where our concerns have no determinate object. Another is to deny that the relations to B and C contain everything that matters, as when we think that being the unique continuer matters.
