@article
Ana decides to raise her hand, and her hand rises. We naturally say that her decision caused the movement. Now add the physical story: a pattern of neural activity in her motor cortex caused the muscles to contract, and that activity had a sufficient physical cause of its own. It looks as if the physical story leaves nothing for the decision to do. Jaegwon Kim made this worry into an argument, and it has shaped discussion of non-reductive physicalism for three decades.

### The setting

**Non-reductive physicalism** holds that mental properties are realized by physical properties but are not identical to them, since the same mental property can be realized in different ways (multiple realizability). It promised to keep the autonomy of the mental and the primacy of physics. Donald Davidson's **anomalous monism** ("Mental Events", 1970) was an early version: every mental event is a physical event, though there are no strict psychophysical laws. Kim and others objected that on such views mental *properties* do no causal work, because events cause things by virtue of their properties and the physical properties do all of it.

### The exclusion argument

Kim's argument, developed in "The Myth of Nonreductive Materialism" (1989) and in *Mind in a Physical World* (1998), can be set out as follows, with $m$ a mental event, $p$ its physical realizer and $p^*$ a later physical event.

| P1 | The mental event $m$ causes the physical event $p^*$. |
| P2 | $p^*$ has a sufficient physical cause $p$ (the causal closure of the physical). |
| P3 | If $p^*$ has a sufficient physical cause $p$, then any cause $m$ of $p^*$ that is distinct from $p$ is a case of overdetermination, that is, $p^*$ has two distinct sufficient causes (the exclusion principle). |
| P4 | $p^*$ is not overdetermined in this way (overdetermination of this kind is not systematic). |
| C | So $m$ is not distinct from $p$: $m$ is identical to the physical cause $p$. |

The argument is valid. Its conclusion is acceptable to a reductive physicalist, but a non-reductive physicalist holds that $m$ is not identical with its realizer, so she faces a trilemma: reduce, accept epiphenomenalism or deny a premise. Kim concluded that mental properties that can be functionally defined can be reduced, while he was pessimistic about the reduction of qualia.

### Responses, and the premises they deny

- **Interactionist dualists** deny **P2**: the physical world is not causally closed, so the mental event can supply a cause without competing with a sufficient physical one. The cost is a conflict with a widely accepted principle of the sciences, though how well supported closure is, and what it really says, is debated.
- **Proportionality** (Stephen Yablo, "Mental Causation", 1992) and **interventionist** accounts of causation (following James Woodward) deny **P3**. A cause should be proportional to its effect: the mental property, being more general, is the right level of cause for a behavior that could have been produced by many physical realizers, and the physical property is too specific. An interventionist says that causal claims at different levels can both be true when they describe difference-making relations among variables, so there is no competition. The cost is the need to show that levels of cause do not compete.
- **Defenders of benign overdetermination** (Karen Bennett and others) deny **P4**, or deny that the case is overdetermination in the troubling sense, because $m$ and $p$ are not independent of each other in the way two separate bullets are.
- **Reductive physicalists** accept the conclusion, as the type-identity theorist does.
- **Epiphenomenalists**, for instance Frank Jackson in "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), deny **P1**: the mental event does not cause any physical event, and the apparent causal role is explained by the physical realizer.

### Why this matters for consciousness

If phenomenal properties are not physical, the argument seems to bear on them with extra force, because physical events then have sufficient physical causes and the experience does no work. Chalmers states the result as the **paradox of phenomenal judgment**: our judgments that we have experiences, and our reports of them, would be fully caused by physical processes that would have occurred without the experiences. It looks like a coincidence that the judgments are true, and that is also the motivation for the meta-problem (Lesson 5). Options for the non-physicalist include interactionism, a Russellian view on which the intrinsic natures that realize the physical dispositions are themselves phenomenal or protophenomenal, so that experience is part of the causal story (Lesson 6), and acceptance of epiphenomenalism with an appeal to acquaintance.

### Where things stand

No response commands consensus. Many philosophers think the exclusion principle is the weak point; others hold that closure is the premise that has the least independent support; and some accept that mental causation requires reduction. Whichever reply you adopt, say what you take causation to be, since the argument turns on what causal competition between levels amounts to.

problem: Ana's decision $m$ to raise her hand is realized by a neural state $p$ that is a sufficient cause of her arm's rising ($p^*$). Apply the exclusion argument to show what it concludes and what a non-reductive physicalist must say.
step: P1: the decision causes the arm's rising. P2: the neural state is a sufficient physical cause of the same event.
step: P3 says that if the decision is a cause distinct from the neural state, the arm's rising has two sufficient causes: it is overdetermined. P4 says it is not.
step: So the decision is not distinct from the neural state: it is identical with it.
step: A non-reductive physicalist holds that the decision is distinct from its realizer, so she must deny one of the premises (typically P3 or P4) or accept that the decision does no causal work.
answer: The argument concludes that the decision is identical to the neural state. A non-reductive physicalist must deny a premise or accept epiphenomenalism.

@mistakes
- Saying that the exclusion argument shows that mental causation is impossible: it shows that mental events must be identical to physical events, or that a premise must be denied.
- Treating causal closure as the same as exclusion: closure says that physical effects have sufficient physical causes, and exclusion says what follows if they have another cause.
- Running epiphenomenalism together with the denial of mental states: it holds that mental states exist and have physical causes but do not themselves cause physical events.
- Thinking that proportionality says physical properties are not causes: it says they are not the best-fitting causes of certain effects.

@practice self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, say which premise of the exclusion argument is, in your view, the best one to deny, and state the strongest objection to doing so.
hint: Pick one premise, say why it is the most vulnerable and what denying it commits you to.
solution: The exclusion principle (P3) is the best premise to deny. It says that a second cause distinct from a sufficient physical cause is a case of overdetermination, but this assumes that causes at different levels compete for the same causal work. In the sciences, we cite macro-level causes freely while accepting that micro-level processes realize them, and an interventionist account shows how both claims can be true without competition, since they describe different difference-making relations. The strongest objection is that this seems to give mental properties only the causal relevance that the macro properties of any physical system have, and it leaves open whether phenomenal properties in particular, as opposed to functional ones, are among those that make a difference. A further objection is that interventionism assumes that the variables are independent enough to be manipulated, which is exactly what is in dispute for mental and realizing variables. I take these to show that the reply works best for functional properties and must be supplemented for phenomenal ones.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a response-paper paragraph (six to eight sentences) explaining how the exclusion argument bears on the causal role of phenomenal consciousness, and what a Russellian monist could say in reply.
hint: Connect the argument to the paradox of phenomenal judgment, and then ask how intrinsic natures could play a causal role.
solution: If phenomenal properties are not identical to physical properties, then the exclusion argument applies to them with special force: physical events have sufficient physical causes, so a non-physical experience is either excluded or overdetermines. The result is the paradox of phenomenal judgment: our judgments about experience would occur even without it, and their correctness would be a coincidence. A Russellian monist offers a different picture. Physics describes dispositions, and the intrinsic natures that ground these dispositions are phenomenal or protophenomenal. Then the physical causes of our judgments are realized by intrinsic natures that are themselves experiential, so experience is part of the causal story and does not compete with it. The proposal faces objections: it needs an account of why the structure of the causal story seems independent of the phenomenal character of its grounds, and it must say how micro-level phenomenal natures add up to the macro-level experience that figures in the judgment (the combination problem). So the Russellian reply is a promising way to respond to the paradox, but one that takes on the commitments of the view.
