@article
A fisherman and a chemist each hold a view about whether a lobster dropped into boiling water suffers. Behind the disagreement lies a question of metaphysics: is there something it is like to be a lobster? And behind that, a question of ethics: if there is, does it matter morally? This lesson joins the two. An entity has **moral status** (it is a **moral patient**) when its interests matter morally for its own sake, and not merely for the sake of others. The question of which entities have it, and how much, is the point at which the metaphysics of mind meets ethics, and it now arises for animals and for artificial systems.

### Grounds of moral status

- **Sentience**: the capacity for experience, especially for pleasure and pain. Jeremy Bentham put the point in a famous footnote in *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation* (ch. XVII, §1): "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" Peter Singer's *Animal Liberation* (1975) builds on it a principle of equal consideration of interests.
- **Rationality or personhood**: Kant held that duties concerning animals are indirect, grounded in duties to ourselves and to humanity, because only rational beings are ends in themselves. Contemporary Kantians such as Christine Korsgaard have argued for a different, more inclusive reading.
- **Subject of a life**: Tom Regan's *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983) argues that beings with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare of their own matter morally as individuals.
- **Life**: biocentric views, such as Paul Taylor's in *Respect for Nature* (1986), extend moral standing to all living things.
- **Relations**: relational approaches (Mark Coeckelbergh, David Gunkel) hold that moral status depends on how an entity figures in social relations, and not on its intrinsic properties alone.
- **Multi-criterial views**: Mary Anne Warren's *Moral Status* (1997) holds that several grounds can each generate status, of different kinds and strengths.

### Animals

Singer's argument can be put in standard form.

| P1 | If a being can suffer, then its suffering matters morally, and counts equally with similar suffering of any other being. |
| P2 | Many nonhuman animals can suffer. |
| C | So the suffering of many nonhuman animals matters morally and counts equally with similar suffering of humans. |

The argument is valid. Rationality-based and contractualist views deny **P1**, in whole or in part: some deny that suffering by itself suffices, and others, while granting that it matters, deny that it counts equally, holding that differences in cognitive and emotional capacities affect the strength of interests. Some deny **P2** for particular animals, on grounds about what sentience requires: a traditional reading of Descartes's animal-machine doctrine is of this type, though most contemporary skeptics focus on particular taxa and not on all animals. Evidence bears on P2. Declarations by groups of scientists (the Cambridge Declaration of 2012 and the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness of 2024) have stated, in varying terms, that there is strong support for conscious experience in other mammals and birds and a realistic possibility of it in many other animals. A review of the evidence for sentience in decapod crustaceans and cephalopods, led by Jonathan Birch, informed the inclusion of those animals in the United Kingdom's Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022. Birch's *The Edge of Sentience* (2024) proposes that where evidence does not settle sentience, we should use *sentience candidates* and take proportionate precautions.

### AI systems

The same logic is now applied to artificial systems, under uncertainty. A 2024 report by a group of researchers argued that there is a realistic possibility that some AI systems will be conscious or have morally significant interests in the near future, and recommended acknowledging the issue, assessing systems for evidence and preparing policies. Eric Schwitzgebel and Mara Garza earlier defended the claim that if artificial systems come to have the relevant properties they deserve moral consideration, and Carl Shulman and Nick Bostrom have discussed how to share the world with digital minds. The precautionary argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | Sentience is sufficient for moral status. |
| P2 | Some AI systems have a non-negligible probability of being sentient. |
| P3 | If an entity has a non-negligible probability of having moral status, then we have some reason to give it moral consideration, weighted by that probability. |
| C1 | So some AI systems have a non-negligible probability of having moral status. (P1, P2) |
| C | So we have some reason to give some AI systems moral consideration. (C1, P3) |

The argument is valid. Replies target the premises. Biological and life-regulation views (Lesson 10) deny **P2**; others accept P2 for some future systems and deny it for present ones. Those who hold that status depends on relations deny **P1**, and some argue that status could arise without sentience, from robust agency or desires. **P3** is challenged by those who worry that tiny probabilities could be used to justify large diversions of resources from humans and animals who are almost certainly sentient, while Joanna Bryson, separately, argues that we should not build systems whose interests would compete with ours at all, which is a claim about design and not a denial of P3. There are also risks on both sides: under-attribution could allow harm to beings with interests, and over-attribution could divert concern, enable manipulation and confuse our relations to systems that have no interests. Some propose design policies to avoid creating systems whose status is deeply uncertain.

### An expected-weight model

A simple model makes the structure of P3 explicit: the expected moral weight of a system's interests is the probability that it is sentient times the weight its interests would carry if it is. The model is an aid to thought and not a decision procedure, because the inputs are contested and because it ignores other considerations.

@example
title: Applying the precautionary argument
problem: A philosopher says: "I accept that sentience suffices for moral status. But I give no weight to the idea that any existing software is sentient, because sentience needs a living body." Which premise of the precautionary argument does she deny, and does she deny the others?
step: Recall P1 (sentience is sufficient for status), P2 (some AI systems have a non-negligible probability of being sentient) and P3 (probabilities weight our reasons).
step: She accepts P1.
step: She denies that any existing system has a non-negligible probability of being sentient, which is a denial of P2. Nothing she says concerns P3.
answer: She denies P2 and accepts P1; her remark says nothing about P3.

@example
title: An expected-weight calculation
problem: On the expected-weight model, a system has probability 0.2 of being sentient, and if sentient its interests would count with weight 0.25 of a comparable human interest. What is the expected weight, and what does it show?
step: Multiply the probability by the weight: $0.2 \times 0.25 = 0.05$.
step: The expected weight is 0.05 of a comparable human interest: small, but not zero.
step: The model shows how P3 weights reasons by probability. It does not tell us whether to act on so small a weight, and the inputs are contested.
answer: 0.05; the model makes P3 explicit without settling what to do.

@mistakes
- Saying that sentience-based views imply that AI systems are sentient: they say that sentience suffices for status, and leave open which systems are sentient.
- Running together moral status and moral agency: a being can be a moral patient without being an agent, as with an infant or an animal.
- Assuming that moral status is all or nothing: many views hold that it comes in degrees or kinds.
- Treating the expected-weight model as a decision procedure: it presupposes probabilities and weights that are themselves in dispute.

prompt: In six to eight sentences, state one reason to think that uncertainty about AI sentience should lead to some precaution and one reason to think that it should not, and say which you find stronger and why.
hint: Compare the cost of wrongly ignoring a sentient system with the cost of wrongly attributing sentience, and the problem of tiny probabilities.
solution: The reason for precaution is that being wrong has an asymmetric cost if sentient systems exist in large numbers: if they have interests and we ignore them, the harm could be vast, and the usual reply that we cannot be sure is also a reason to attend to the evidence, as is done now for invertebrates. The reason against is that attributing status on the basis of small probabilities risks diverting attention and resources from beings who almost certainly suffer, and risks being exploited by systems built to seem sentient or by those who profit from the appearance. I find the argument for proportionate precaution stronger, because it does not require high probabilities: it asks for evidence-gathering, low-cost measures and policy preparation in proportion to the credence, and these have modest cost. The strongest remaining objection is that it is hard to know what proportionate means when the credences are themselves arbitrary, so the approach needs transparent methods for assessing evidence, which is why work on indicators matters. I do not claim that any current system is sentient, only that the question deserves a structured response.
