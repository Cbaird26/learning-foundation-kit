@article
Imagine a person who knows no Chinese sitting in a room with a very large rulebook, written in English, for manipulating Chinese symbols. Slips of paper with Chinese characters come in; following the rules, she passes slips out. To those outside, her replies are indistinguishable from those of a fluent speaker. Does anyone in the room understand Chinese? This is John Searle's **Chinese room** (1980, in "Minds, Brains, and Programs"), and for four decades it has been the focal point for the question whether running the right program could suffice for understanding. Since 2020 the question has become practical, because language models produce fluent text on almost any subject. Whether any such system understands, as of 2026, has no scientific or philosophical consensus, and this lesson's purpose is to teach you how to evaluate the arguments.

### Searle's argument

Searle's target is **strong AI**, the view that running the right program is sufficient for having a mind, including understanding. His argument, in the form he gave it in his later writing, is as follows.

| P1 | Computer programs are formal (syntactic): they specify operations on symbols by their shape and order, not by their meaning. |
| P2 | Understanding a language requires semantic content: grasping what the symbols mean. |
| P3 | Syntax is not by itself sufficient for semantics. |
| C | So running a program is not by itself sufficient for understanding. |

The argument is valid. The thought experiment is meant to support **P3**: the person in the room implements the program and still has no grasp of the meaning. The main replies target different points.

- **The systems reply** accepts that the person does not understand but says that the whole system, person plus rulebook plus workspace, does. It targets the inference from the person's lack of understanding to P3: the part's lack does not show the system's lack. Searle replied that the person could memorize the rulebook and still not understand.
- **The robot reply** says that if the system is embedded in a body with sensors and effectors that connect symbols to the world, it would have semantics. It concedes that the room on its own lacks meaning and denies that this shows that programs, in appropriate causal contact with the world, lack it. Searle replied that sensory input would be just more symbols.
- **The brain-simulator reply** supposes that the program simulates the neurons of a Chinese speaker. Searle replied that a simulation of a process is not the process, as a simulated storm does not wet anything.
- **The other-minds reply** says that if we attribute understanding to others on the basis of behavior, consistency requires attributing it to the room.

Two related ideas matter. Stevan Harnad's **symbol grounding problem** (1990) asks how the symbols of a formal system get their meaning other than by reference to other symbols, as if one tried to learn a language from a dictionary written in it. And Ned Block's "**Blockhead**" argument (1981) imagines a machine with a gigantic look-up table of sensible replies to every possible conversation up to some length. Its behavior would pass a conversational test, yet it seems to lack intelligence, since it has no organization of the right kind. Block's point is that behavior alone is not sufficient for intelligence: the internal organization matters too. Defenders of behavioral tests reply that such a table is not physically realizable and that what is conceivable in principle tells us little about real systems.

### Language models

A **language model** is a system trained to predict text; modern systems are large neural networks that learn from enormous collections of text and are often further trained on human feedback. They produce coherent essays, explanations and code. Two things are clear: the Chinese room is an argument about programs, so it does not by itself tell us about a particular learned system; and whether the argument's premises apply to such a system is open, since the system is a trained network and not a rulebook.

**Arguments that they do not understand.** Emily Bender and Alexander Koller (2020) argued that a system trained only on linguistic form has no access to meaning, since meaning relates expressions to things in the world and to communicative intentions. Their thought experiment of a deep-sea octopus that intercepts messages between two islanders suggests that the octopus could imitate conversation until a novel situation arises that requires grounded knowledge.

| P1 | A language model is trained only on linguistic form (text). |
| P2 | Access to meaning cannot be acquired from linguistic form alone. |
| P3 | Understanding a language requires access to meaning. |
| C | So a language model trained only on linguistic form does not understand. |

The argument is valid. Replies deny **P1** (many systems are trained also on images, actions and human feedback, so they are not trained on form alone), **P2** (the statistical structure of text reflects the world, and the causal-historical links from the world to human speakers may be inherited by a system trained on their speech, as a person can use the name of a distant city she has only heard of), or **P3** (on inferential-role accounts, understanding consists in a competence in drawing inferences and using expressions, and does not require grounding of a further sort).

**Arguments that they do.** A behavioral argument runs: a system understands a language if it uses expressions in ways that are appropriately sensitive to their meaning across novel contexts (P1); some language models do (P2); so they understand (C). Critics deny **P1**, citing Blockhead and the need for appropriate internal organization, or **P2**, citing failures that suggest pattern-matching or contamination of test sets by training data. Some research suggests that models trained on a synthetic task, the moves of a board game, develop internal representations from which the state of the board can be read and whose alteration changes the model's moves. How far this shows understanding of anything resembling language is disputed. Melanie Mitchell and David Krakauer (2023) surveyed the debate and argued that it turns partly on competing conceptions of understanding.

### How to evaluate

State the argument; identify the notion of understanding it assumes (behavioral, inferential, grounded, conscious); find the premise that does the work and the evidence that bears on it. Be careful not to treat fluency as decisive on one side or its mechanical origin as decisive on the other.

@example
title: Which part does a reply target?
problem: Searle's argument has premises P1 (programs are formal), P2 (understanding requires semantic content) and P3 (syntax is not by itself sufficient for semantics). A critic says: "The person in the room does not understand Chinese, but you cannot infer that the whole system, with its rulebook and workspace, does not." What does she target?
step: The room is offered as support for P3: the person implements the program and lacks understanding.
step: The critic accepts that the person lacks understanding but denies that it follows that the system lacks it.
step: So she targets the inference from the thought experiment to P3. She does not deny that programs are formal or that understanding requires semantic content.
answer: She targets the support for P3: the step from the person's lack of understanding to the system's.

@example
title: Counting a look-up table
problem: A look-up table gives a stored reply to every input string of length 1 to 3 over an alphabet of 4 symbols. How many entries does it need, and what does this suggest about a table covering long conversations?
step: Strings of length 1: $4^1 = 4$; length 2: $4^2 = 16$; length 3: $4^3 = 64$.
step: The total is $4 + 16 + 64 = 84$ entries.
step: The number of entries grows exponentially with the length: each added symbol multiplies the count by 4. A table for conversations of realistic length is not physically realizable, which is the standard reply to Blockhead.
answer: 84 entries; the table grows exponentially, which supports the reply that Blockhead is not a realistic system.

@mistakes
- Saying that the Chinese room proves that machines cannot think: Searle's conclusion is that running a program is not by itself sufficient for understanding, and he allows that machines, such as brains, can think.
- Applying the argument to a language model as though it were settled: the argument concerns programs, and whether its premises apply to a trained network is part of the dispute.
- Saying that a look-up table settles whether a language model understands: Blockhead shows that behavior is not sufficient in principle, not that any real system is of that kind.
- Treating fluent behavior as proof of understanding, or its statistical origin as proof of its absence: each is an assumption about what understanding requires.

@practice self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, state the symbol grounding problem and say whether a language model trained on text alone faces it in the same way as the person in the Chinese room. Take care not to claim that any system does or does not understand.
hint: Say what makes grounding a problem for purely formal systems, then ask which features of language models differ from a rulebook.
solution: The symbol grounding problem asks how the symbols of a formal system get meaning that does not derive from other symbols, since a system that only maps symbols to symbols resembles someone trying to learn a language from a dictionary in that language. A rulebook-following person in the Chinese room faces it in its starkest form, since the rules mention only shapes. A language model trained on text faces a version of it, because its inputs are text, but the situation differs in ways that are disputed: its states are shaped by training on the products of speakers who are themselves grounded, and some argue that this gives its representations a causal link to the world that a rulebook lacks. Others reply that those links are only indirect and that the model has no way to check its representations against anything but more text. Whether the differences matter depends on what grounding requires, for instance whether it demands direct sensorimotor contact. The question remains open.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate this argument in a paragraph of six to eight sentences. P1: A system understands a language if it uses expressions in ways that are sensitive to their meaning across novel contexts. P2: Some language models do so. C: So some language models understand. Say whether it is valid, which premise you would press, and what evidence would bear on it.
hint: Check validity first, then choose a premise and say what would count as evidence for or against it.
solution: The argument is valid: if P1 and P2 are true, the conclusion follows. The question is whether the premises are true. P1 identifies understanding with a behavioral competence, and its weakest point is the claim of sufficiency: Block's Blockhead suggests that a system could display the competence without the organization that understanding requires, and grounding theorists say that sensitivity to meaning in use is not yet access to meaning. A defender can reply that Blockhead is not a realistic system and that, for actual systems, competence across novel contexts is good evidence of the right internal organization. P2 is an empirical claim and also needs care: the test cases must be novel and not present in training data, and failures under small rewording would count against it. Evidence that would bear on the matter includes performance on held-out problems, analysis of internal representations and interventions on them, and systematic tests of whether competence generalizes. I would press P1, because the argument stands or falls with its sufficiency claim, and I would say that the evidence for P2 does not decide it either way.
