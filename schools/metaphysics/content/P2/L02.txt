@article
Water is H2O. Once the chemistry is in, there is no leftover puzzle about why this stuff is water: the molecular story explains the liquidity, the solvency and the boiling point. Compare "pain is such-and-such neural firing". Even with the neurophysiology complete, it seems an open question why *that* should feel like *this*. Joseph Levine's 1983 paper "Materialism and Qualia: The Explanatory Gap" named the difference. A physical account of a phenomenon is explanatory when it makes the phenomenon intelligible, so that it would be no surprise that the physical facts give rise to it. For experience, Levine argued, there is a gap: identity claims such as "pain is the firing of C-fibers" leave something unexplained, even if they are true.

### Epistemic gap, metaphysical conclusion?

Keep two claims apart. The **explanatory gap** is epistemic: we cannot see how physical facts explain phenomenal facts. **Physicalism** is a metaphysical thesis: roughly, that everything is physical, or that every fact is fixed by (supervenes on, or is grounded in) the physical facts. The central question is whether an epistemic gap yields a metaphysical conclusion. Three arguments try to bridge them.

### The conceivability argument

Chalmers's version rests on **zombies**: creatures physically identical to us (and so behaviorally and functionally identical) with no experience. The argument has the following form.

| P1 | A zombie world, one physically identical to ours in which no one has experience, is conceivable. |
| P2 | If a zombie world is conceivable, then a zombie world is metaphysically possible. |
| P3 | If a zombie world is metaphysically possible, then physicalism is false. |
| C | So physicalism is false. |

The argument is valid (two applications of modus ponens). Its premises divide the physicalists, in the taxonomy of Chalmers's "Consciousness and Its Place in Nature" (2003):

- **Type-A materialists** deny P1, or its substantive form: on careful reflection a zombie is not coherently conceivable, because the functional facts leave nothing to be absent. Dennett takes roughly this line.
- **Type-B materialists** accept P1 and deny P2: the epistemic gap is real, but conceivability does not carry us to metaphysical possibility, as with "water is not H2O", which was conceivable before the chemistry and is nevertheless not possible. This view usually appeals to Kripke's necessary a posteriori identities and to **phenomenal concepts** (see below).
- **Type-C materialists** think the gap is currently deep, but that it will be closed by future science.

Notice that P3 is also open to question: someone might hold that a world with our physics but no experience would not be a counterexample if physicalism is formulated more loosely. Most of the debate has centered on P1 and P2.

### The knowledge argument

Frank Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) asks us to consider Mary, a scientist who knows every physical fact about color vision but has lived in a black-and-white room. When she first sees a ripe tomato, does she learn something new? If so, there are facts about experience that are not physical facts.

| P1 | Before her release, Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision. |
| P2 | On her release, Mary learns something new about what seeing red is like. |
| P3 | If someone who knows all the physical facts learns something new, then there are facts that are not physical facts. |
| C | So there are non-physical facts, and physicalism is false. |

Replies attack different premises. The **ability hypothesis** (associated with David Lewis and Laurence Nemirow) denies P2 in its factual form: Mary gains abilities, to imagine, recognize and remember, and not new propositional knowledge. The **acquaintance** reply (Earl Conee) says that she gains acquaintance with a known fact. The **old fact, new guise** reply accepts P2 but denies P3: she learns an old physical fact under a new, phenomenal concept. Others deny P1, holding that knowing all the physical facts would include more than Mary can possess. Jackson himself later rejected the conclusion, a reminder that one can state an argument without endorsing it.

### The phenomenal concept strategy

The most-discussed physicalist reply to both arguments, developed by Brian Loar, David Papineau, Block and Robert Stalnaker and others, holds that **phenomenal concepts**, the concepts we use to think about experiences from the first-person standpoint, are special: they pick out physical states directly, without any descriptive or functional route. This explains why identity claims linking them to physical concepts seem open, and why Mary learns something, without positing non-physical properties. Critics, including Chalmers, press the **master argument**: either phenomenal concepts can be explained physically or functionally, in which case the gap should be closable a priori, or they cannot, in which case a new puzzle arises. Whether the strategy really explains the gap, or merely redescribes it, is where the present debate stands.

### How to read this

When you read a paper in this area, identify (1) whether the author treats the gap as epistemic or metaphysical, (2) which premise of which argument is under attack, and (3) whether the reply to one argument also works against the others. A reply that blocks the knowledge argument may leave the conceivability argument untouched, and the reverse.

@example
title: Locating a reply to the zombie argument
problem: Ben says: "I can conceive of a zombie world, but conceivability is not a guide to possibility here. People once conceived of heat without molecular motion, and that was not possible. Phenomenal concepts mislead us in the same way." Which premise of the zombie argument does he deny, and which type of physicalist is he?
step: State the premises: P1, conceivability of a zombie world; P2, conceivability implies metaphysical possibility; P3, if possible then physicalism is false.
step: Ben grants that a zombie world is conceivable, so he accepts P1.
step: He denies that conceivability carries over to possibility, citing a case in which it did not. That is a denial of P2.
step: A physicalist who accepts that the gap is real but denies the step from conceivable to possible is a type-B materialist.
answer: He denies P2 and is a type-B physicalist.

@example
title: Telling the replies to the knowledge argument apart
problem: Three replies to the knowledge argument: (i) "Mary only gains an ability." (ii) "Mary gains new knowledge, but of an old physical fact under a new concept." (iii) "Mary did not have all the physical facts before release." Which premise does each reply attack?
step: Recall P1, Mary knows all physical facts; P2, she learns something new; P3, learning something new while knowing all physical facts implies non-physical facts.
step: (i) says she does not gain propositional knowledge of a new fact, only know-how: it attacks P2 in its factual reading.
step: (ii) grants that she learns, but denies that what she learns is a non-physical fact: it attacks P3.
step: (iii) says Mary did not have the full physical story: it attacks P1.
answer: (i) attacks P2; (ii) attacks P3; (iii) attacks P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the explanatory gap as already a proof that physicalism is false: Levine's gap is epistemic, and the step to a metaphysical conclusion is the point at issue.
- Saying that type-B physicalists deny that zombies are conceivable: they grant conceivability and deny the inference to possibility.
- Running the ability hypothesis together with the old-fact-new-guise reply: the first denies that Mary learns a new fact, the second grants it.
- Reading the knowledge argument as showing that Mary lacks some information about the brain: it concerns whether complete physical information would leave something about experience unlearned.

@practice self
prompt: In one paragraph of 5 to 7 sentences, give the strongest objection you can to the claim that the phenomenal concept strategy closes the explanatory gap, and the best reply a defender could give.
hint: Think about Chalmers's dilemma for phenomenal concepts: can they be explained physically or not?
solution: The strategy is meant to explain why identities between phenomenal and physical concepts seem open, while holding that the properties are physical. The best objection is the master argument: if phenomenal concepts can themselves be explained in physical or functional terms, then the facts about them should be knowable a priori from the physical facts, and the gap, by the strategy's own lights, should not seem to arise; if they cannot be so explained, then the strategy leaves an unexplained residue of exactly the kind it was meant to remove. A defender can reply that a concept may be physically realized and yet fail to be derivable a priori by a concept-user, so the gap is explained by features of the concept without being closed for us. The objector can press that this explains why we are puzzled but not why there is something it is like. The exchange shows that the strategy explains the appearance of the gap better than it explains experience.

@quiz self
prompt: Write the opening paragraph (five to seven sentences) of a response paper on the explanatory gap. It should state the gap, say what it argues (a thesis about which physicalist reply best handles it), and indicate the strongest opposing view.
hint: Open with the contrast of water and pain, then commit to a thesis in one sentence.
solution: Levine's explanatory gap is the observation that, unlike the identity of water with H2O, the identity of a phenomenal state with a physical state leaves it unintelligible why the physical state should feel as it does. I argue that the type-B response, which grants the gap but denies that it shows physicalism false, gives the best account of the data, because it explains why the gap persists without positing a second realm of properties. Its key resource is that our phenomenal concepts are direct and not derived from physical concepts, so no a priori path leads between the two vocabularies. The strongest opposing view is Chalmers's dilemma for this strategy: if phenomenal concepts are physically explicable, the gap should close, and if they are not, a new residue remains. I shall argue that the dilemma equivocates between a concept being explicable and its being derivable by the concept-user. If that is right, the gap is a fact about our concepts and not about the world, and the zombie argument loses its step from conceivability to possibility.
