Physics tells us what things do: how they attract, repel, move and combine. It says remarkably little about what things *are* in themselves. Bertrand Russell, in *The Analysis of Matter* (1927), drew a striking conclusion: physics describes only the structural and dispositional properties of matter, so the intrinsic nature of matter is something about which physics is silent, and it may be the place where consciousness fits. This idea, **Russellian monism**, and the view that the intrinsic nature of matter is experiential, **panpsychism**, are among the more discussed options in contemporary metaphysics of mind.

### The views

**Panpsychism** says that mentality, or experience, is fundamental and ubiquitous: the basic physical entities have some form of experience. **Russellian monism** is a more general view: the intrinsic natures that underlie the dispositions physics describes are the basis of consciousness. It has two forms, as Chalmers sets out in "Panpsychism and Panprotopsychism" (2013): panpsychism proper, on which the intrinsic natures are themselves experiential, and **panprotopsychism**, on which they are *protophenomenal*, not experiences themselves but such that, suitably combined, they constitute experience. **Cosmopsychism** reverses the order: the universe as a whole is the fundamental conscious entity, and individual minds are derived from it. Defenders of panpsychism in the recent literature include Galen Strawson and Philip Goff, who has also defended cosmopsychism-friendly views.

### The argument

One way of presenting the case, in the spirit of Strawson's "Realistic Monism" (2006) and Goff's *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017), runs as follows.

| P1 | Consciousness is not wholly grounded in the structural and dynamic properties that physics describes. |
| P2 | If P1, then either (a) there are fundamental non-physical properties that are not intrinsic natures of physical things, or (b) consciousness is strongly emergent, or (c) the intrinsic natures of physical things are themselves experiential or protophenomenal. |
| P3 | Neither (a) nor (b) is acceptable. |
| C | So the intrinsic natures of physical things are experiential or protophenomenal. |

The argument is valid, by modus ponens and disjunctive syllogism. Its premises are all contested. Those who deny **P1**, whether type-A physicalists, illusionists or functionalists who think that consciousness is wholly structural, reject the starting point. Interactionist and epiphenomenalist **dualists** deny **P3** with respect to (a), and strong emergentists deny it with respect to (b); they say that fundamental laws linking physical and phenomenal properties are no worse than other fundamental laws. And **P2** assumes that those are the only options, and that the intrinsic natures play the explanatory role the argument gives them; critics ask why a structural theory of matter needs intrinsic natures, and why they should be experiential rather than inert.

### The combination problem

Panpsychism faces a problem of its own. If basic entities have experience, how do the experiences of micro-level subjects constitute the unified experience of a human being? William James, in *The Principles of Psychology* (1890), argued that no combination of separate feelings is itself a single feeling: a group of feelings in separate minds is just a group of feelings. Chalmers's later treatment distinguishes the **subject combination** problem (how many subjects can constitute one subject), the **quality combination** problem (how micro-level qualities yield macro-level qualities such as the red of a tomato) and the **structure combination** problem (how the structure of macro-level experience, with its field, perspective and unity, is related to micro-level structure).

| P1 | Constitutive panpsychism, the view that human experience is constituted by the experiences of micro-level subjects, is acceptable only if there is an intelligible account of how micro-level subjects and experiences combine into a macro-level subject with a unified experience. |
| P2 | There is no intelligible account of how micro-level subjects and experiences combine into a macro-level subject with a unified experience. |
| C | So constitutive panpsychism is not acceptable. |

The argument is valid. The main responses are as follows. Defenders deny **P1** by appealing to parity: physicalism has an explanatory gap of its own, and no a priori derivation of macro-level experience from micro-level facts should be required. Goff and others deny **P2** by proposing **phenomenal bonding**, a fundamental relation under which micro-level experiences constitute a macro-level one, or turn to cosmopsychism, which starts from the whole and so avoids combining parts. **Panprotopsychists** avoid subject combination, since proto-properties are not subjects, but face the question of why protophenomenal properties, which are not experiences, should constitute experience at all, a version of the original gap.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, panpsychism attracted about 8% of respondents who answered the question on consciousness. Whether the combination problem is worse than the gap panpsychism was meant to close remains a central question in the literature, and so is whether the view is testable. Integrated information theory (Lesson 4) has panpsychist-leaning consequences, but it is a scientific theory with its own commitments.

title: Subject, quality or structure combination?
problem: Classify each worry: (a) How could many tiny subjects of experience add up to one subject, with one point of view? (b) How could the structure of the visual field, with its geometry, come from the structure of micro-level experience? (c) How could micro-level qualities, none of them red-like, yield the experience of red?
step: Recall Chalmers's three: subject combination (many subjects, one subject), quality combination (micro-level qualities yielding macro-level ones) and structure combination (the structure of experience).
step: Worry (a) is about the number of subjects: subject combination.
step: Worry (b) concerns the structure of experience: structure combination. Worry (c) concerns how qualities yield qualities: quality combination.
answer: (a) subject combination; (b) structure combination; (c) quality combination.

@mistakes
- Equating panpsychism with the claim that rocks and tables think: the view attributes some form of experience to fundamental entities and not to every aggregate.
- Treating panprotopsychism as a form of panpsychism: its basic properties are not themselves experiences.
- Saying that the combination problem refutes panpsychism: the argument is valid, and its premises can be denied.
- Confusing Russellian monism with idealism: it holds that matter has intrinsic natures, and it does not say that only minds exist.

@practice self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, say whether the demand that panpsychism supply an intelligible account of combination is fair, given that physicalism faces its own explanatory gap. State a view and its strongest objection.
hint: Consider the parity reply, and ask what makes an explanatory demand reasonable.
solution: The demand is fair to a point. The parity reply is right that no view has an a priori derivation of macro-level experience from micro-level facts, so a demand for one would count against every view equally. But panpsychism's motive was to avoid the brute emergence of experience from the non-experiential, and the combination problem threatens to reintroduce a version of it, since macro-level subjects must somehow arise from many micro-level ones. So the panpsychist must say more than that combination is a brute fact: she needs a relation such as phenomenal bonding, which can be assessed for coherence and for fit with the rest of her view. The strongest objection is that positing such a relation by fiat looks no better than positing strong emergence. A reasonable conclusion is that parity limits the force of the demand without removing the obligation to explain why combination is not just emergence under another name.
