@article
Suppose a complete physical story of a bat's flight is on the table: the echo-location calls, the auditory cortex, the motor commands, the interception of a moth. You can read all of it and still ask a further question: is there anything it is like to be that bat? Thomas Nagel's 1974 paper "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" made that question the touchstone of the field. He proposed that an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something it is like to be that organism, something it is like for the organism. This lesson sorts the many things "consciousness" can mean and then asks which of them make trouble for a scientific, and perhaps a physicalist, picture of the mind.

### Kinds of consciousness

Philosophers separate several uses of the word.

- **Creature consciousness**: a creature is conscious when it is awake and sentient, as opposed to anesthetized or in dreamless sleep. It can be **transitive** (conscious *of* the smell of smoke) or **intransitive** (simply conscious).
- **State consciousness**: a mental state is conscious when it is a state the creature is in consciously. David Rosenthal's 1986 paper "Two Concepts of Consciousness" drew the line between creature and state consciousness, and he used it to argue that state consciousness needs explaining in terms of the creature's awareness of its own states (Lesson 3).
- **Phenomenal consciousness**: the "what it is like" aspect of states such as the look of red or the ache of a bruise. The qualitative character of an experience is often called its **qualia**, though the term is contested.
- **Access consciousness**: Ned Block's term (in "On a Confusion about a Function of Consciousness", 1995) for a state's availability for use in reasoning, report and the rational control of action.

Block holds that the two notions are conceptually distinct and argues that they can come apart in fact: he thinks there can be phenomenal consciousness without access, for instance in partial-report experiments where subjects seem to see more detail than they can report. Others, including Daniel Dennett and Stanislas Dehaene, dispute this reading of the data, and whether phenomenal consciousness ever outruns access is one of the live empirical-philosophical questions of the field.

### Easy problems and the hard problem

David Chalmers's 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" sorted the problems into two classes. The **easy problems** concern the ability to discriminate and react to stimuli, to integrate information, to report mental states, to focus attention, to control behavior, and the difference between wakefulness and sleep. "Easy" is a technical label: they are the problems we know how to *pose* as questions about mechanisms, and they are not easy to solve. The **hard problem** is why the performance of these functions is accompanied by experience at all.

Chalmers's reasoning can be set out in standard form.

| P1 | Physical (neuroscientific and cognitive) explanations explain only structure and function: how a system is organized and what it does. |
| P2 | Explaining structure and function does not suffice to explain experience. |
| C | So physical explanations of this kind do not suffice to explain experience. |

The argument is valid, and each type of reply denies a premise or the use made of the conclusion. Those who deny **P2** are what Chalmers later called type-A physicalists: once the functions are explained, nothing remains, or what remains is an illusion (Dennett's position in *Consciousness Explained*, 1991, and illusionists generally, Lesson 5). Those who deny **P1** hold that physical science may describe more than structure and function, for instance the intrinsic natures that Russellian monists posit (Lesson 6). A third group accepts the argument as stated but denies that the conclusion refutes physicalism, by treating the gap as a feature of our concepts (Lesson 2).

### Other problems

The hard problem is one of several. There is the **problem of other minds** (how can we know which systems are conscious?), the **unity** problem (why experience at a time is a single field), the problem of **intentionality** (how states are about things), the problem of **self-consciousness**, and the **problem of mental causation** (Lesson 7). Chalmers has also posed the **meta-problem**: explain why we judge that there is a hard problem (Lesson 5). Which of these is fundamental is itself contested.

### Where things stand

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about half of respondents accepted or leaned toward physicalism about the mind and about a third non-physicalism; functionalism was the most popular view of consciousness. That is a report on opinion, not evidence for any view. For the rest of the course, ask of each theory two questions: which of these problems does it claim to solve, and which does it dissolve or defer?

@example
title: Easy or hard?
problem: Chalmers's distinction sorts questions, not subject areas. Classify each as an easy problem or as the hard problem: (a) How does the visual system tell a vertical edge from a horizontal one? (b) How can a person report that she is in pain? (c) Why is there a felt quality to the sensation of a cold drink, rather than mere processing of temperature signals?
step: Ask of each question whether a successful answer would be an account of a mechanism performing a function: discriminating, integrating, reporting, controlling.
step: Question (a) asks for a mechanism of discrimination, and (b) for a mechanism of reportability. Both are easy problems in Chalmers's technical sense, however hard the neuroscience.
step: Question (c) would remain even after every discrimination, report and control function had been accounted for, because it asks why that functioning is accompanied by felt quality. That is the hard problem.
answer: (a) easy; (b) easy; (c) the hard problem.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply deny?
problem: A philosopher replies to the structure-and-function argument: "Once you have explained how the brain discriminates, integrates, attends and reports, there is nothing left over that is worth calling experience; the feeling that something is left over is a mistake." Which premise does she deny?
step: Recall the premises: P1 says physical explanations explain only structure and function; P2 says that explaining structure and function does not suffice to explain experience.
step: She does not dispute that physical explanation is explanation of structure and function, so she does not deny P1.
step: She says that explaining structure and function does suffice, with nothing important left over. That is the denial of P2.
answer: She denies P2, as a type-A physicalist or illusionist does.

@mistakes
- Treating "easy problem" as meaning "simple": the label marks a kind of question (about mechanisms of function), not how hard the science is.
- Running together phenomenal and access consciousness: a view that explains access (availability for report and control) has not thereby explained phenomenal character unless it also says that the two coincide.
- Reading Nagel's criterion as a claim that bats are conscious or that we can know what it is like to be one: it is a statement of what consciousness is, and it leaves open which creatures have it.
- Taking the hard problem to be an argument for dualism: it is a challenge, and the replies include physicalist ones.

@practice self
prompt: In four to six sentences, explain why the hard problem is not simply the observation that neuroscience is unfinished.
hint: Compare an unsolved easy problem with the hard problem: what would a complete solution of the former still leave open?
solution: Neuroscience has many unsolved problems, such as how memories are consolidated or how attention is implemented. Each is an easy problem in Chalmers's sense: we know what a solution would look like, namely a mechanism that performs the function. The hard problem is meant to be different in kind. Even a complete mechanistic account of discrimination, integration, report and control would, Chalmers argues, leave open why any of it is accompanied by experience, so more data of the same kind would not close it. A critic may reply that this overrates the force of our current intuitions, and that the question may fade as the mechanisms are understood, as questions about life faded once biochemistry advanced. The disagreement is exactly whether experience is a further explanandum.

rubric: Notes a reply, such as the analogy with life or the claim that the felt gap may dissolve, rather than asserting that the hard problem is settled.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a short response-paper paragraph (five to seven sentences) that distinguishes phenomenal from access consciousness and explains why a theory of access consciousness alone would not settle the hard problem unless the two coincide.
hint: Say what each notion is, then ask what would follow if there were phenomenal consciousness that no theory of access describes.
solution: Phenomenal consciousness is the felt, qualitative character of experience, what it is like for the subject. Access consciousness, in Block's sense, is a state's availability for reasoning, report and the control of action, and it can be characterized entirely in terms of function. A theory of access therefore explains how information becomes globally usable by a system. Whether it also explains experience depends on a further claim: that being a state with global availability is, or suffices for, being a state with phenomenal character. Those who accept this claim, as many functionalists do, treat the hard problem as largely solved by a theory of access. Those who reject it, or who think access and phenomenality can come apart, as Block does, take the question of phenomenality to remain open. So the real dispute lies in the bridge claim, and a good paper should state it explicitly and say what motivates it.
rubric: Defines both notions correctly, with access tied to availability for report, reasoning and control.
rubric: Identifies the bridge claim that the two coincide as the crux, rather than assuming it.
rubric: Notes that views differ on the bridge claim, with Block as one who denies coincidence, and does not claim that either side is established.
