@article
A viewer is shown a faint pattern for a few milliseconds, followed at once by a mask. Her forced-choice guesses about the pattern's orientation are better than chance, yet she says she saw nothing. The visual system has processed the stimulus. What is missing when the processing is unconscious? Two families of theory give very different answers, and both are cast as accounts of what makes a state conscious in terms of **representation**.

### Higher-order theories

**Higher-order theories** begin from a principle that David Rosenthal calls the **transitivity principle**: a mental state is conscious only if the subject is, in some suitable way, aware of being in it. Conscious states are states we are conscious *of*. The theory then says what awareness of a state consists in: a representation of that state, hence a higher-order state. It can be set out as follows.

| P1 | A mental state is conscious if and only if the subject is aware of being in it. |
| P2 | A subject is aware of being in a state if and only if she has a suitable higher-order representation of herself as being in it. |
| C | So a mental state is conscious if and only if the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of herself as being in it. |

The argument is valid: P2 lets us substitute for the right-hand side of P1. P1 is the contested heart of the theory. The variants differ over P2.

- **Higher-order thought (HOT) theory** (Rosenthal): the representation is a thought, occurrent, assertoric and arising without conscious inference.
- **Higher-order perception (HOP) or inner-sense theory** (David Armstrong, William Lycan): the representation is a quasi-perceptual scanning of one's own states.
- **Dispositional HOT theory** (Peter Carruthers): it is enough that the state is *available* to a higher-order thought.
- **Self-representationalism** (Uriah Kriegel and others, with roots in Brentano): the conscious state represents itself, so awareness of it is built into it and not separate from it.

On the empirical side, Hakwan Lau and Rosenthal (2011) argued that experiments in which subjects' discrimination performance is matched while their reported awareness differs support the theory, since what changes is the subject's higher-order stance, not the first-order processing. Critics reply that such differences may reflect shifts in the criterion for reporting, not in awareness, and the interpretation remains disputed.

### Objections to higher-order theories

The best-known challenge is the **misrepresentation** or **empty higher-order thought** objection (pressed by Karen Neander and by Block). Suppose a subject has a higher-order thought that she is seeing a red patch when no first-order state of seeing red exists. There is a representation, but nothing it represents. Rosenthal's answer is that what it is like for the subject is fixed by how she represents herself as being, so she consciously experiences red even though no first-order red state exists. Critics find it incredible that a thought could produce an experience with no state for it to be about. A second worry is **over-intellectualization**: if higher-order *thoughts* need concepts, infants and many animals may lack conscious experience. Defenders reply that the concepts required are minimal.

### Representationalism

**First-order representationalism**, developed by Fred Dretske and Michael Tye, makes no appeal to higher-order states. It says that phenomenal character is identical with, or supervenes on, a kind of representational content. Tye's version holds that it is content that is **poised, abstract, nonconceptual and intentional** (PANIC): content is poised when it is available to the cognitive system, nonconceptual when it does not require the subject to have the concepts used to describe it. The best-known route to the view is the **transparency** argument, which goes back to G. E. Moore's remark that experience is "diaphanous" and was revived by Gilbert Harman.

| P1 | When you introspect an experience, you find only the features of what it represents (the blueness of the sky, not any blueness of the experience). |
| P2 | Introspection reveals all of an experience's phenomenal character. |
| P3 | If introspection reveals all phenomenal character and reveals only represented features, then phenomenal character consists in represented features. |
| C | So phenomenal character consists in represented features. |

The argument is valid. Block's "Mental Paint and Mental Latex" (1996) attacks **P1**: we can attend to features of the experience itself, "mental paint", as in blur or the way a color seems different in a dim room. Introspective unreliability would attack **P2**. Ned Block's **Inverted Earth** case (1990) tests the conclusion: two people with the same experience but different represented colors, because of differences in their environment and language, seem to differ in content but not in phenomenal character. And Christopher Peacocke's well-known example of two trees of equal height at different distances suggests experiences with the same represented size but different visual appearance.

### Comparing the two

Both theories say that the masked stimulus is unconscious, for different reasons. For a HOT theorist, it is unconscious because the subject has no thought that she is seeing it; for a first-order representationalist such as Tye, because its content is not poised for the use of the cognitive system. The theories give different verdicts on cases where the two come apart, which is why they matter for experimental design.

@example
title: Two theories, one forced-choice experiment
problem: A subject views a very faint tilted bar and then a mask. She says she saw nothing, but chooses the correct tilt on 70% of 200 trials. Say what a HOT theorist and a PANIC representationalist say about why her visual experience of the bar was absent.
step: Both theories grant that the visual system processed the bar: the first-order state carried information about its tilt, which explains the better-than-chance choices.
step: The HOT theorist says that the state was unconscious because she had no higher-order thought to the effect that she was seeing a tilted bar. First-order information without such a thought gives no experience.
step: The PANIC theorist says that the state's content was not poised, that is, not available to the cognitive systems of belief and rational control. It affected forced-choice guesses but not her reasoning or report.
answer: HOT: no higher-order thought. PANIC: content not poised for use by the cognitive system.

@example
title: Which premise does an objection attack?
problem: A critic of the transparency argument says: "When I look at a red patch through a smudged lens, I can attend to the blur itself, and that blur is no feature of the patch." Which premise is attacked?
step: Recall P1 (introspection finds only represented features), P2 (introspection reveals all phenomenal character) and P3 (the bridging conditional).
step: The critic claims that attention can find a feature of the experience, the blur, that is not a feature of what is represented.
step: That is the denial of P1.
answer: P1.

@mistakes
- Saying that higher-order theories require a first-order state to be *represented correctly*: the empty-HOT debate is exactly about misrepresentation.
- Running HOT and HOP together: a thought is conceptual and propositional, perception-like scanning is not, and the debate between them is about which is right.
- Treating first-order representationalism as a higher-order theory: it holds that phenomenal character is a first-order content and needs no awareness of the state.
- Reading transparency as showing that experiences have no intrinsic features: the argument concludes that character consists in represented features only given P2 and P3.
