Why does one stimulus reach awareness while another, equally well processed, does not? Neuroscientific theories of consciousness are attempts to say. Two of them, the global workspace theory and integrated information theory, are the most discussed, and they disagree about almost everything: what consciousness is, where it is in the brain and which machines could have it.

### Global workspace theory

**Global workspace theory** (GWT) was proposed by Bernard Baars. The brain is pictured as many specialized, parallel and mostly unconscious processors. Consciousness corresponds to a limited-capacity **workspace**: information that wins the competition for it is **broadcast** to the many specialized systems, such as memory, planning, language and motor control, so that it becomes globally available. Stanislas Dehaene and Jean-Pierre Changeux developed a neural version, the **global neuronal workspace** theory, in which large-scale, long-range networks (with a prominent role for prefrontal and parietal cortex) undergo a sudden, self-sustaining "**ignition**" when a stimulus becomes conscious. Weak stimuli produce brief, local activity, while conscious ones produce late, widespread and sustained activity.

On GWT, consciousness is a functional property of information: being broadcast. That makes it a version of **functionalism**, and a natural partner of the view that phenomenal consciousness comes with access, so that it is a theory first of access consciousness (Lesson 1). Its predictions are, in principle, testable: a stimulus that is consciously perceived should show ignition and late widespread activity; one that is masked should not.

### Integrated information theory

**Integrated information theory** (IIT), developed by Giulio Tononi and championed by Christof Koch and others, starts from the other end. It begins with axioms about experience, claiming that every experience exists intrinsically, is structured, is specific, is unified and is definite, and then asks what physical systems could satisfy corresponding postulates. The theory's central quantity, **integrated information** (Φ, "phi"), measures, roughly, how much a system's causal structure specifies over and above its parts taken independently. A system is conscious to the degree that it has a maximum of irreducible cause-effect structure; the experience just is that structure. The theory is not about functions such as broadcast: it is about the physical causal structure of the substrate.

Several striking consequences follow, and they are the source of much of the debate.

- A system of simple elements with the right kind of feedback connectivity could have some consciousness, so IIT has panpsychist-leaning implications (Lesson 6).
- Purely feed-forward systems have no integrated information, so they have no consciousness, however intelligent their behavior.
- Because Φ depends on physical causal structure, a conventional digital computer running a simulation of a brain would, according to IIT's proponents, have very little consciousness whatever its software does. A neuromorphic design could differ.
- The cerebellum, which has many neurons but a largely feed-forward, modular architecture, is predicted to contribute little to consciousness.

Critics point out that exact Φ is intractable to compute for realistic systems and that some of these implications are strange, and in 2023 a large group of researchers signed a letter calling the theory pseudoscience, while others defended it as a serious, if unfinished, theory. The dispute concerns testability and the status of the axioms.

### What an adversarial collaboration shows

A consortium of researchers organized an **adversarial collaboration** in which proponents of both theories pre-registered predictions about the same experiments. The results reported so far did not simply vindicate either theory: some predictions of each were not borne out, and both camps have disputed how to interpret the findings. The lesson for philosophers is twofold. Theories of consciousness can make contact with data. And data underdetermine theory, because each theory can revise its auxiliary assumptions, such as which brain regions matter or what counts as report.

### The philosophical stakes

Both theories face the hard problem in its own way. GWT identifies consciousness with a function and can be accused of explaining access and not experience. IIT begins with phenomenology and so seems to take experience seriously, but it identifies it with a physical structure, which raises the question why that structure should be experience.

| P1 | A theory of consciousness is acceptable only if it explains why the physical or functional property it identifies is accompanied by experience. |
| P2 | GWT identifies consciousness with global broadcast and offers no further account of why broadcast is accompanied by experience. |
| C | So, by the standard in P1, GWT is not acceptable. |

The argument is valid. A defender of GWT can deny **P1**, saying that no theory could meet so strong a standard and that the right test is whether the theory explains the pattern of data about which states are conscious; or can deny **P2**, claiming that broadcast explains the reportability and the unity that experience seems to have. IIT's defenders would say that their approach avoids the issue by starting from experience; critics say that it simply relocates the problem.

@example
title: Two theories, one experiment
problem: In a masking experiment a subject sees a word on half of the trials and does not see it on the other half, with the same stimulus shown each time. Say what GWT and IIT each say distinguishes the two kinds of trial.
step: GWT: on seen trials the word's representation wins the competition for the workspace and is broadcast widely, with ignition in large-scale networks; on unseen trials the activation stays local and decays.
step: IIT: the question is whether the physical substrate engaged has a high degree of integrated, irreducible cause-effect structure. IIT's proponents link this to sustained, integrated activity in posterior cortex.
step: The theories can thus disagree about which brain areas to examine, and about whether report is needed: GWT ties consciousness closely to broadcast to prefrontal systems, while IIT does not.
answer: GWT: broadcast and ignition. IIT: a maximum of irreducible cause-effect structure in the substrate, with no role for broadcast as such.

@example
title: Counting candidate systems in IIT
problem: In IIT's analysis, every nonempty subset of a system's units is considered as a candidate for a conscious system, and the subset with the maximum of integrated information is selected. A toy device has four binary units. How many candidate subsets are there?
step: Four units give $2^4 = 16$ subsets of the set of units.
step: The empty set is not a candidate, so there are $16 - 1$ subsets to consider.
answer: 15 candidate subsets.

@mistakes
- Treating IIT as a theory of function: it says that consciousness depends on the causal structure of the substrate, so two systems with the same function may differ in Φ.
- Saying that GWT claims that the workspace is a place in the brain where experience is "displayed": it is a functional architecture of broadcast, not a screen.
- Saying that the adversarial collaboration refuted one of the theories: the reports described predictions of both as not borne out, and the interpretation is disputed.
- Assuming that a theory's popularity among scientists is evidence of its truth.

@practice self
prompt: In five to six sentences, explain why a global workspace theorist and an integrated information theorist might disagree about whether a large feed-forward neural network that talks fluently is conscious.
hint: Ask what each theory takes to be the basis of consciousness, a function or a causal structure.
solution: For a global workspace theorist, what matters is whether the system has an architecture in which information is broadcast from a limited-capacity workspace to many specialized modules. The question is then whether the network has such an architecture, and fluent speech alone does not settle it; a theorist of this kind would look at whether there is competition for a workspace, global broadcast and the sustained, recurrent dynamics that go with it. For an integrated information theorist, a purely feed-forward system has no integrated information whatever its behavior, so it would not be conscious, and the same would hold for a digital implementation of a recurrent system. So the two theories ask different questions, one about functional organization, the other about the physical causal structure, and could return different verdicts about the same system. Both would insist that behavior alone is not decisive.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a paragraph of five to seven sentences comparing how the global workspace theory and integrated information theory would each explain what a patient with blindsight can and cannot do, naming the claim of each theory you use. (Blindsight: after damage to primary visual cortex, patients report no visual experience in part of the visual field yet can, when forced to guess, point to or discriminate stimuli there better than chance.)
hint: For each theory, ask what is missing in the blind field: broadcast, or integrated causal structure?
solution: In the blind field the patient's visual system still carries information that guides forced-choice guessing, but she reports nothing, so the information is not used to control report and deliberate action. A global workspace theorist says that the information in the damaged field is processed by specialized systems but does not win access to the workspace, so it is not broadcast and no experience results; the information is available to guesses only because those can draw on local processing. An integrated information theorist says that the relevant question is whether the damaged pathways still contribute to the integrated, maximal cause-effect structure in the substrate that supports the patient's experience; the loss of experience in that field would correspond to the loss of a contribution, not to the absence of broadcast. The two theories make different predictions, and in practice the interpretation of blindsight is itself contested, including how much residual awareness patients have. A careful paper would flag this and say which data would discriminate.
