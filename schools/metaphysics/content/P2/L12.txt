@article
A seminar paper is a short argumentative essay, usually of 5,000 to 7,000 words in this course, that answers one question. It is not a survey of everything said about a topic, and it is not a report on what you think. It presents an argument for a thesis that a reasonable reader could reject, shows that you have understood the best opposing view and gives reasons to prefer yours. This lesson is about the craft, and it prepares the deliverable for this block: a draft submitted for review.

### From topic to thesis

A **topic** ("AI and consciousness") is not a **question** ("Does the fading qualia argument support the claim that a language model could be conscious?"), and a question is not a **thesis** ("It does not, unless the argument is supplemented by a premise about which organization matters, and the best candidate for that premise faces a dilemma"). A good thesis is arguable, specific and modest enough to defend in the space. Test it by asking who would disagree and what they would say. If no one would, you have a truism; if everyone would, you have a research program.

### The shape of the paper

A common and effective structure is the following.

1. **Introduction**: the question, the thesis, and a roadmap in a few sentences. State what you will not argue.
2. **Background**: only what the reader needs, with accurate attributions.
3. **The target view or argument**, reconstructed in standard form with all premises explicit and the argument valid as stated, so that objections can be matched to premises.
4. **The strongest opposing view**, in the form its best defender would give it. Reconstructing it charitably is a requirement of the project, not a courtesy.
5. **Your argument**: an objection, a distinction or a new argument, with the premise it targets stated.
6. **The best reply to you**, and your response.
7. **Conclusion**: what has been shown, what has not, and what follows.

### Accuracy in the literature

Find sources through the course reading, the encyclopedias and PhilPapers, and read the primary sources for any claim you attribute. Cite what you use. Attribute a view to a philosopher only if you have checked it in their work, and say "on one reading" where interpretation is disputed. Report experiments as their authors do, say what was measured, and flag where interpretation is contested. On artificial intelligence, do not write that a system does or does not understand or is or is not conscious as though settled: state the argument you are discussing, the theory it assumes and what would count as evidence. Never include a reference you have not seen. Tools that generate text can invent plausible citations, and you are responsible for every claim in your paper.

### Academic integrity and tools

Programs and journals differ on the use of writing and generation tools, and policies change. Check your own program's rules, disclose any use that they require, and do not present text or ideas that are not yours as your own. Whatever help you have had, you must be able to explain and defend every sentence aloud.

### Presenting

A paper is not a talk. For a 20-minute talk at about 130 words per minute, plan for about 2,600 words, which means one main argument, one running example and a short handout that sets out the argument in standard form. Say where you are in the argument. Prepare for the three strongest objections and say before the talk which premise each targets. In questions, restate the objection in your own words before answering, and say "I don't know" when you do not.

### Revising and refereeing

After written feedback, sort comments into those that show a misunderstanding (fix the presentation), those that identify a gap (supply the missing argument) and those that disagree (decide whether to answer or to narrow the claim). A short **referee report** says what the paper claims, whether the argument supports it, the most important objection and what would improve it. You will write one for another draft, and the habit of reading your own work as a referee improves it.

problem: Turn the topic "mental causation" into a question and a thesis for a 6,000-word paper, and name the view you will have to reconstruct.
step: Narrow: choose one argument, Kim's exclusion argument, and one reply, the proportionality account.
step: Question: does proportionality block the exclusion argument for phenomenal properties as well as for functional ones?
step: Thesis: it blocks the argument for functional properties but not for phenomenal ones, unless phenomenal properties can be shown to be proportional causes of behavior.

@example
title: Diagnosing a weak paragraph
problem: Here is a draft paragraph: "Many philosophers have written about the Chinese room. Some think it works and some don't. The systems reply is one reply. It is hard to say who is right, and there is more research to do." Say what is wrong and how to improve it.
step: It has no claim: it says that views differ and then says that nothing is settled.
step: It has no argument: the premises and the point at issue are not stated, so no premise is matched with a reply.
step: Improve it by stating the argument in standard form, naming the premise the systems reply targets, giving the strongest form of Searle's response and saying whether it succeeds and why.
answer: The paragraph has neither a thesis nor an argument; revise it by reconstructing the argument, matching the reply to a premise and taking a position.

@mistakes
- Writing a survey in place of an argument: a paper that reports views without a thesis gives the reader nothing to evaluate.
- Reconstructing the opposing view weakly: an objection to a weak version shows nothing about the strong one.
- Overclaiming about artificial intelligence, in either direction: say what an argument shows and what it assumes.
- Citing sources you have not read or that a text generator supplied without checking: every reference must exist and say what you say it says.

@practice self
prompt: Write an abstract of 120 to 150 words for a seminar paper on one of these questions: the hard problem, illusionism, the combination problem or AI consciousness. It should state the question, the thesis, the route of the argument and what is shown.
hint: Use four moves: question, thesis, argument and upshot.
solution: Does solving the meta-problem of consciousness favor illusionism over realism? I argue that it does not settle the dispute, though it raises the cost of realism. The paper reconstructs the debunking argument for illusionism in standard form and shows that, to reach its conclusion, it needs the premise that nothing other than a causal explanation of our judgments supports belief in phenomenal consciousness. I then reconstruct the realist reply from acquaintance in its strongest form, which denies that premise, and argue that the reply is sound only if acquaintance can be shown to be a source of justification that the explanation of our judgments cannot undermine. I conclude that the debate turns on the epistemology of acquaintance, not on the meta-problem, and I identify what further argument each side owes.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a referee-report paragraph (six to eight sentences) on this invented draft summary: "The paper argues that because language models are trained on text alone, they cannot understand language. It surveys the Chinese room and the symbol grounding problem, notes that models are fluent, and concludes that understanding requires bodies." Say what the paper claims, whether the argument supports it, the most important objection and what would improve it.
hint: Look for hidden premises, for views not engaged and for overreach in the conclusion.
solution: The paper claims that language models cannot understand language because they are trained only on text, and that understanding requires bodies. The argument as summarized is not valid: it needs premises that training on text alone cannot yield access to meaning and that access to meaning requires bodily experience, and neither is argued for. The most important objection is that the paper does not engage with replies that deny the first premise (many systems are trained on more than text), that deny that meaning cannot be acquired from text (through inherited causal links or inferential structure) or that deny that understanding requires bodies. The conclusion also overreaches in stating as settled what the literature treats as open. To improve the paper, the author should put the argument in standard form, identify the premise that does the work, reconstruct the strongest opposing view and say what evidence would bear on the premise. I would recommend major revision, with a narrower thesis, for example that the grounding argument shows what a defender of understanding in these systems owes.
rubric: States the claim accurately and notes the lack of explicit premises.
rubric: Identifies a central objection such as unengaged replies or overreach.
rubric: Gives concrete advice for improvement.
