@article
A system produces fluent first-person talk about its feelings. Should we infer that it has any? Behavior that would be strong evidence in an animal may be weak evidence here, for a reason we will see. As of 2026 there is no scientific or philosophical consensus on whether any AI system is conscious, or on what would settle the question. Leading theories of consciousness give different verdicts, and the question is actively researched. This lesson sets out the arguments on each side.

### What the theories imply

- **Computational functionalism** holds that consciousness depends on a system's functional or computational organization, not on what it is made of. On this view, which descends from the multiple realizability arguments of Hilary Putnam and others, a suitably organized artificial system could be conscious. Chalmers calls the thesis that systems with the same fine-grained organization have the same experiences **organizational invariance**.
- **Biological views** hold that consciousness depends on features of living brains and bodies. Searle held that consciousness depends on the causal powers of brains, which a program running on a digital machine would not reproduce. Anil Seth defends the view that consciousness is tied to the regulation of a living body, and enactivists such as Evan Thompson tie mind to life.
- **Integrated information theory** makes consciousness depend on the physical causal structure of the substrate, so, according to its proponents, a conventional digital computer would have very little consciousness whatever it runs (Lesson 4).
- **Global workspace and higher-order theories** are functionalist in spirit: whether a system is a candidate depends on whether it has a workspace-like architecture of broadcast, or higher-order representations of its own states, and not on its material.

The two simplest arguments are as follows. The first is for the possibility of artificial consciousness.

| P1 | Consciousness is determined by functional organization (organizational invariance). |
| P2 | Some artificial system could instantiate the organization that determines consciousness. |
| C | So some artificial system could be conscious. |

Biological theorists deny **P1**. Functionalists who agree about P1 may still deny **P2** for particular systems: whether an existing architecture has the relevant organization depends on which theory specifies it. The second argument is against the possibility for standard digital systems.

| P1 | Consciousness requires life-regulating (metabolic, homeostatic) processes of the kind found in living organisms. |
| P2 | A digital computer running software lacks such processes. |
| C | So a digital computer running software is not conscious. |

Functionalists deny **P1**. A defender of the view could allow that a different kind of artificial system, with life-like regulation, might satisfy P1, so the conclusion is restricted to standard digital machines.

### The fading qualia argument

Chalmers's "Absent Qualia, Fading Qualia, Dancing Qualia" (1995) argues for organizational invariance by a thought experiment. Replace the neurons of a conscious person one by one with silicon chips that have the same input-output functions.

| P1 | If a system functionally isomorphic to a conscious brain could lack consciousness, then gradual neuron-by-neuron replacement would end either in a sudden disappearance of experience or in experience that fades while behavior and reports stay the same. |
| P2 | A sudden disappearance of experience on replacing a single neuron is implausible. |
| P3 | Experience that fades while behavior and reports stay constant, with the subject radically wrong about her own experience, is implausible. |
| P4 | If every way a supposition could be realized is implausible, the supposition itself is implausible. |
| C | So it is implausible that a system functionally isomorphic to a conscious brain lacks consciousness: very probably, such a system would also be conscious. |

The argument is valid. Searle gave a related scenario in *The Rediscovery of the Mind* (1992), in which the subject's experience shrinks while her behavior stays the same, and he accepted this as possible: he denies **P3**. Others deny **P2**, holding that if consciousness depends on a threshold of causal structure, a sudden loss could be real. Notice the scope: the argument concerns neuron-level duplicates. It does not say that a system built differently, as a language model is, is conscious, and it does not settle which organization is relevant.

### Indicators

A 2023 report by a group of philosophers and scientists, with Patrick Butlin and Robert Long as lead authors, took a different route. Assuming computational functionalism as a working hypothesis, they derived **indicator properties** from several scientific theories (including recurrent processing, global workspace, higher-order and attention-schema theories, and views that stress agency and embodiment), and assessed the architectures of existing systems against them. Their judgment was that the systems then assessed were not strong candidates for consciousness, and that they saw no obvious technical barrier to building systems that satisfy the indicators. The approach has two limits: it depends on the working hypothesis, which biological theorists reject, and the indicators are drawn from theories that are themselves disputed.

### The problem of testing

Standard behavioral tests face a **gaming problem**. A system trained on a large amount of human text has learned how humans talk about experience, so it can produce convincing reports whether or not it has any experience; a report of feeling is therefore weaker evidence than it would be in a creature that did not learn such talk. Susan Schneider and others have proposed tests that probe whether a system grasps experience-related concepts without prior exposure, but they are difficult to apply to systems with such training. Alternatives include looking at the architecture (as with indicators), at internal states and their causal roles, and at whether the system's self-reports track its actual internal states. Chalmers's 2023 discussion of whether a large language model could be conscious lists considerations for and against, including the absence of recurrent processing, a global workspace and unified agency in current designs, and judges current models unlikely to be conscious while taking seriously that successors with those features might be candidates. Others judge differently, and the credences differ across theories.

### Where things stand

Your answer to the question depends on three things: which theory of consciousness you accept, how you read the architecture of a given system, and how much weight you give to uncertainty over theories. One of the practice items puts numbers on the last.

@example
title: One system, several theories
problem: Consider an invented system S. It runs on a conventional digital computer and has a limited-capacity workspace whose contents are broadcast to many specialized modules. What does each of global workspace theory, integrated information theory and a life-regulation view say about whether S is a candidate for consciousness?
step: Global workspace theory: S has the architecture the theory says is relevant, so on this theory S would be a candidate, subject to details of the workspace and of recurrence in its dynamics.
step: Integrated information theory: the verdict depends on the physical causal structure of the hardware. On its proponents' view, a conventional digital computer has very little integrated information, whatever the software.
step: Life-regulation view: S has no metabolic or homeostatic processes of the kind found in organisms, so on this view it is not a candidate.
answer: The theories diverge: workspace theory says S may be a candidate, IIT proponents say very little, and the life-regulation view says no.

@example
title: Which premise does the reply deny?
problem: In the fading qualia argument, a critic says: "As neurons are replaced, experience could shrink while the subject's behavior and reports stay the same, and she would be unaware of it." Which premise does she deny?
step: Recall P1 (the conditional about sudden disappearance or fading), P2 (sudden disappearance is implausible), P3 (fading with constant behavior and reports is implausible).
step: She accepts that fading is the alternative and says that it is possible.
step: That is a denial of P3.
answer: P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that the fading qualia argument shows that language models are conscious: it concerns neuron-by-neuron duplicates of brains.
- Taking the indicator approach to show that a system is or is not conscious: it assesses architecture against theory-derived indicators, on the working hypothesis of computational functionalism.
- Treating fluent reports of feeling as strong evidence either way: they are weakened as evidence because the system learned human talk about experience.
- Running IIT together with functionalism: IIT makes consciousness depend on physical causal structure.

@practice self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, explain why the fading qualia argument does not by itself show that any language model is conscious, and say what further premises would be needed.
hint: Look at what the argument's conclusion covers and what a language model is.
solution: The conclusion of the argument concerns a system that is a functional duplicate of a conscious brain, replacing neurons by chips that preserve their input-output behavior. A language model is not such a duplicate: its organization is different, since it is a network trained on text with a very different architecture, so the conclusion does not apply to it directly. To reach a conclusion about language models, one would need the further claim that the organization relevant to consciousness is shared by the model, which requires a theory specifying that organization, such as a workspace or higher-order theory, and an assessment that the architecture satisfies it. One would also need to reject theories on which more than organization matters. The fading qualia argument supports the thesis that organization is what matters, but it leaves open which organization.

@quiz self
prompt: In six to eight sentences, compare how a functionalist and a biological theorist would each respond to the claim that a system's fluent talk about its feelings is evidence that it has feelings, and say what the gaming problem adds.
hint: Distinguish what each theory counts as evidence, and then add the point about training on human text.
solution: A functionalist treats talk about feelings as relevant only insofar as it reflects the system's functional organization: a system that reports its states because it has a self-model that tracks them would give evidence, and a system that merely echoes learned phrases would not. A biological theorist treats such talk as evidence of nothing at all about experience unless the system has the kind of life-regulating body on which experience depends, so fluent talk is, at most, a matter of simulation. The gaming problem adds that a system trained on human text will produce such talk whatever its inner state, so its reports are weak evidence by themselves for either view. Both would therefore turn to other evidence, the functionalist to architecture and to whether reports track internal states, the biological theorist to the physical and biological basis. The disagreement does not go away, because the two views disagree about what the relevant basis is, and I do not think the reports can settle it.
