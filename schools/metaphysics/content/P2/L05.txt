@article
Ask a thoughtful person whether she is conscious and she will say that of course she is, that she has a vivid inner life and that it is not a mere matter of behavior, brain activity or information processing. Almost everyone makes judgments of this kind, and the hard problem is built from them. A striking move is to ask where these judgments come from, and to suggest that the best explanation of them does not need the properties they describe.

### Illusionism

**Illusionism**, as Keith Frankish named and defended it in his 2016 paper "Illusionism as a Theory of Consciousness" (with a target article and replies in the *Journal of Consciousness Studies*), is the thesis that **phenomenal consciousness**, understood as the realist understands it, as an intrinsic, ineffable, private and non-physical-seeming property of experiences, does not exist. What exists is the *introspective illusion* that it does. Our introspective mechanisms represent some of our states as having such properties. Daniel Dennett's earlier "Quining Qualia" (1988) and *Consciousness Explained* (1991) argue in the same direction, treating qualia as a mistaken theory of our own states, and on Dennett's view the "user illusion" of an inner theater is the way the brain presents itself to itself.

Illusionism is not the claim that nobody has experiences in any sense, and it does not deny that people feel pain in the ordinary sense: they are in states that cause them to wince, to report, to avoid. It denies that those states have *phenomenal properties* in the realist's sense. Frankish speaks of **quasi-phenomenal** properties: the real, physical properties that introspection misrepresents as phenomenal. The task then is to explain the illusion: what Frankish calls the **illusion problem** replaces the hard problem.

### The meta-problem

David Chalmers's "The Meta-Problem of Consciousness" (2018) asks the question to which illusionism is one answer: why do we think there is a hard problem of consciousness, and why do we make judgments of the kind we do about consciousness? This is a problem about the behavior and cognition of conscious beings and is, in Chalmers's terms, an *easy* problem, solvable by the methods of science. Chalmers's thought is that every position has to engage with it. A **realist** has to say why a solution of the meta-problem does not simply explain away the hard problem; an illusionist can say that the solution is the whole story. One candidate type of solution is Michael Graziano's attention schema theory: the brain builds a simplified model of its own attention, and reports of an inner, non-physical awareness are reports of that model.

### The debunking argument

The best-known argument for illusionism from the meta-problem is a debunking argument.

| P1 | Our judgments that we have phenomenal consciousness can be fully explained by mechanisms that do not involve phenomenal consciousness. |
| P2 | If a belief can be fully explained by mechanisms that do not involve the truth of the belief, and nothing else supports it, then the belief is unjustified. |
| P3 | Nothing else supports our belief that we have phenomenal consciousness. |
| C | So our belief that we have phenomenal consciousness is unjustified. |

The argument is valid. Note that C is epistemic, and an illusionist needs a bridge to the metaphysical thesis (that the belief is false). Replies target each premise. Many realists deny **P3**: they say we have *direct acquaintance* with experience, which supports the belief independently of any causal explanation of our judgments (a line Chalmers has pressed). Others deny **P1**, arguing that no explanation of the judgments will be complete without appeal to experience, or that the explanation can mention phenomenal consciousness without being circular. Some deny **P2**, citing cases in which a reliable belief has an explanation that does not mention its truth, as with mathematical beliefs on some views.

### The illusion problem

The most common objection to illusionism is that it is self-undermining: an illusion is a way things *seem*, and seeming is itself an experience. Galen Strawson, for one, has argued forcefully that denying the existence of experience is not a credible option. The objection can be stated as follows.

| P1 | If phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then it seems to subjects that they have phenomenal consciousness. |
| P2 | Something can seem some way to a subject only if the subject has phenomenal consciousness. |
| C | So if phenomenal consciousness is an illusion, then there is phenomenal consciousness. |

The argument is valid, and so a proponent of illusionism must deny a premise. They deny **P2**: "seems" is read as a functional or representational state, not a phenomenal one. A system can be disposed to judge that it has a mysterious inner glow without anything being phenomenally given to it. Whether that account can be given without smuggling in the phenomenal remains the central dispute: the realist asks what the illusionist can mean by "seems" that is not experiential.

### Where things stand

Illusionism was a minority view, and the 2020 PhilPapers Survey recorded eliminativism about consciousness at about 5%. It has been influential because it makes the meta-problem central: whatever one's view, a theory of consciousness should explain why we talk as we do about it. Whether the fact that the meta-problem is an easy problem should reduce the pull of the hard problem is exactly what divides the parties.

@example
title: Reconstructing a debunking argument
problem: Put in standard form: "Our belief that we are free to choose is produced by a mechanism that would produce it whether or not we were free. So this belief is unjustified." Identify the hidden premise needed for validity.
step: The stated premise is that the belief is produced by a mechanism that would produce it whether or not it were true; the stated conclusion is that the belief is unjustified.
step: The argument needs a principle connecting the two: if a belief is produced by a mechanism that does not track its truth, and nothing else supports it, it is unjustified.
step: The argument needs a further premise: nothing else supports the belief. Writing both gives P1 (the belief is produced by a mechanism that would produce it whether or not it were true), P2 (the debunking principle), P3 (nothing else supports it) and C.
answer: The hidden premises are the debunking principle and the claim that nothing else supports the belief.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply deny?
problem: A realist says: "Whatever explains my judgments, I am directly acquainted with my experience, and that acquaintance gives me reason to believe I have it." Which premise of the debunking argument does she deny?
step: Recall P1 (judgments fully explained without phenomenal consciousness), P2 (debunking principle), P3 (nothing else supports the belief).
step: She does not dispute the explanation of her judgments, so she does not deny P1.
step: She claims that acquaintance supports the belief independently of the causal explanation. That is the denial of P3.
answer: She denies P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that illusionists deny that anything hurts or that people have mental states: they deny that experiences have phenomenal properties in the realist's sense.
- Taking the meta-problem to be a hard problem: it is about explaining judgments and reports, and Chalmers classes it as an easy problem.
- Reading the debunking argument as concluding that experience does not exist: its conclusion is that a belief is unjustified, and a further bridge is needed.
- Treating the illusion problem as a refutation: the argument is valid, and illusionists deny its second premise.

prompt: In five to seven sentences, state one reason to think that solving the meta-problem would help the illusionist and one reason to think that it would not settle the dispute with the realist.
hint: Consider what a complete functional explanation of our judgments would show, and what a realist could say about the relation of that explanation to experience.
solution: A complete explanation of why we judge that we have inner, ineffable experiences, in terms of introspective mechanisms that do not mention experience, would support the illusionist's claim that the judgments are produced whether or not there is anything corresponding to them. It would not settle the dispute because the realist can accept the explanation and still hold that the judgments are true, either because acquaintance with experience supports them or because the explanation is compatible with experience's existing and playing no role in it. On this view the meta-problem's solution would show only that the judgments are explicable, not that they are false. So the meta-problem raises the cost of realism without by itself refuting it.
