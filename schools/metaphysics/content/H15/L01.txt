@article
A friend says, "You should bring an umbrella. The sky is dark and the forecast says rain." Your friend is not just describing the weather. They want you to believe something, that you should bring an umbrella, and they give you reasons. In logic, that is an **argument**: a set of statements in which some, the **premises**, are offered as reasons to accept another, the **conclusion**.

### What counts as an argument

A **statement** is a sentence that is true or false. Questions, commands and exclamations are not statements. An argument needs at least one premise and exactly one conclusion that the premises are meant to support. Many passages that look like arguments are not:

- A **description** reports facts without trying to prove anything: "The bus was late and the road was wet."
- An **explanation** takes a fact for granted and says why it happened: "The road was wet because it had rained." Nobody doubts the road was wet; the passage tells you its cause.
- A **command** or **request** is not true or false: "Bring an umbrella."

The test is to ask what the author is trying to convince you of. If there is a claim that the passage tries to get you to accept, that claim is the conclusion and the rest is premises. If every claim is already accepted and the passage says why or how, it is an explanation.

### Indicator words

Certain words often mark the parts of an argument. **Premise indicators**: *because*, *since*, *for*, *given that*, *as shown by*. **Conclusion indicators**: *therefore*, *so*, *thus*, *hence*, *it follows that*, *consequently*. These are clues, not proofs: "because" also appears in explanations, and many arguments have no indicators at all.

### Standard form

To evaluate an argument, set it out in **standard form**: premises listed one per line and numbered, then the conclusion, each as a complete statement in plain wording, with no indicator words.

| P1 | Every mammal has a backbone. |
| P2 | A bat is a mammal. |
| C | So a bat has a backbone. |

Putting an argument in standard form often means supplying small pieces the author left out. A premise the author takes for granted and does not state is **implicit**. Add it only if the argument clearly needs it, and say that you did.

### Diagrams

Number each statement in the passage, then draw arrows from the statements that support to the statement they support. Since the site shows no pictures, describe the diagram in words. There are three basic structures:

| Structure | What it looks like | Example pattern |
|---|---|---|
| **Linked** | Premises work together: neither supports the conclusion alone. One combined arrow. | 1 + 2 → 3 |
| **Convergent** | Each premise gives separate support, and would still give some if the other were dropped. Two arrows. | 1 → 3 and 2 → 3 |
| **Serial** | A premise supports an intermediate conclusion, which in turn supports the final conclusion. | 1 → 2 → 3 |

Real arguments mix these. The question to ask about two premises is: if I remove one, does the other still give some reason for the conclusion? If yes, convergent; if no, linked.

@example
title: From passage to standard form
problem: Put this passage in standard form and describe its structure: "Dara will win the chess tournament. She has won it three years running, and she has just beaten the only player who ever defeated her."
step: Ask what the author wants you to believe. It is "Dara will win the chess tournament"; the other two sentences are offered as reasons.
step: Write the premises as complete statements: P1, Dara has won the tournament three years running. P2, Dara has just beaten the only player who ever defeated her. The conclusion is C, Dara will win the tournament.
step: Check structure. If you drop P2, P1 still gives some reason to expect a win, and the same holds if you drop P1. So the premises are independent: the structure is convergent (1 → 3 and 2 → 3).
answer: P1: Dara has won the tournament three years running. P2: Dara has just beaten the only player who ever defeated her. C: Dara will win the tournament. Structure: convergent.

@example
title: Diagramming a serial argument
problem: Number the statements and diagram: "(1) The supports of the footbridge have deep cracks. (2) So the footbridge is unsafe. (3) Any unsafe bridge should be closed, so (4) the footbridge should be closed."
step: Find the final conclusion: statement 4, "the footbridge should be closed", marked by "so".
step: Statement 2 is a conclusion too ("So"), supported by statement 1. That gives the first stage: 1 → 2.
step: Statement 2 and statement 3 are both needed to reach statement 4: neither alone says the footbridge should be closed. They are linked: 2 + 3 → 4.
step: Put the stages together: 1 → 2, and then 2 + 3 → 4. Statement 2 is an intermediate conclusion (C1), so in standard form it is labeled C1 and the final line is C.
answer: Diagram: 1 → 2; then 2 + 3 → 4. Standard form: P1 (cracks), C1 (the footbridge is unsafe), P2 (any unsafe bridge should be closed), C (the footbridge should be closed). The structure is serial, with a linked step at the end.

@mistakes
- Treating every "because" as a sign of an argument. "The road is wet because it rained" explains a fact nobody doubts; it does not try to prove that the road is wet.
- Thinking the conclusion always comes last. It can come first ("You should bring an umbrella. The sky is dark.") or in the middle.
- Writing premises as fragments or questions. Each line of a standard form must be a complete statement that is true or false.
- Calling two premises convergent just because there are two of them. Ask whether each would still give some support alone; if not, they are linked.
