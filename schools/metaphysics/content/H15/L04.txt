@article
"All drummers are musicians. All musicians are artists. So all drummers are artists." This feels right. But "All oaks are trees. All maples are trees. So all oaks are maples" feels wrong, and both have the same shape of premises. A diagram can tell us exactly why.

### The anatomy of a syllogism

A **categorical syllogism** is an argument with two categorical premises and a categorical conclusion, using exactly three terms, each appearing twice. The **major term** $P$ is the predicate of the conclusion, the **minor term** $S$ is its subject, and the **middle term** $M$ appears in both premises but not in the conclusion. The premise containing $P$ is the major premise; the one with $S$ is the minor premise.

| P1 | All musicians are artists. (major premise: $M$, $P$) |
| P2 | All drummers are musicians. (minor premise: $S$, $M$) |
| C | So all drummers are artists. ($S$, $P$) |

### The Venn test

Draw three overlapping circles, $S$, $P$ and $M$. They divide the page into **eight regions**: $S$ only, $P$ only, $M$ only, $S$ and $P$ only, $S$ and $M$ only, $P$ and $M$ only, all three, and none (outside all circles). Recall that shading means "empty" and an X means "something is here".

1. Draw the **universal premises first** (A and E statements), by shading. "All $M$ are $P$" shades the two regions inside $M$ but outside $P$ ($M$ only and $S$ with $M$ only). "No $M$ are $P$" shades the two regions where $M$ and $P$ overlap ($P$ with $M$ only, and all three).
2. Then draw any particular premise (I or O) with an X in the region it requires. If one of the two regions has been shaded, the X goes in the other. If neither is shaded and the X could be in either, put it **on the line** between the regions, which means "in one or the other".
3. **Look at the conclusion without adding anything.** If the diagram already shows it, the syllogism is valid: for A or E conclusions, the needed regions are shaded; for I or O, the needed X is in the region (not merely on a line). Otherwise it is invalid.

This method uses the modern reading: a universal statement does not guarantee that anything exists, so two universal premises can never prove a particular conclusion.

### Why some forms fail

The failing example above, "All oaks are trees; all maples are trees; so all oaks are maples", shades only the regions where oaks or maples lie outside trees. The oak-and-not-maple region inside trees is untouched, so oaks and maples are not forced to coincide. Traditionally the fault is called **undistributed middle**: the middle term ("trees") is not said of all of anything on either premise in a way that connects the two other classes. The diagram shows the same fault as an unshaded region.

@example
title: A valid syllogism
problem: Test with a Venn diagram: "All drummers are musicians. All musicians are artists. So all drummers are artists."
step: Let $S$ be drummers, $M$ musicians and $P$ artists. Shade the premise "All $M$ are $P$": the regions $M$ only and $S$ with $M$ only.
step: Shade "All $S$ are $M$": the regions $S$ only and $S$ with $P$ only (the parts of $S$ outside $M$).
step: The conclusion "All $S$ are $P$" needs the regions of $S$ outside $P$ to be empty: these are $S$ only and $S$ with $M$ only. Both are already shaded.
answer: Valid: the premises alone force the conclusion.

@example
title: An invalid syllogism
problem: Test: "All poodles are dogs. All terriers are dogs. So all poodles are terriers."
step: Let $S$ be poodles, $P$ terriers and $M$ dogs. Shade "All $S$ are $M$": the regions $S$ only and $S$ with $P$ only. Shade "All $P$ are $M$": the regions $P$ only and $S$ with $P$ only.
step: The conclusion "All $S$ are $P$" needs the regions $S$ only and $S$ with $M$ only to be empty. $S$ only is shaded, but $S$ with $M$ only (poodles that are dogs but not terriers) is not.
step: A counterexample fills that region: take a world where poodles and terriers are both kinds of dog, so the premises hold, yet poodles are not terriers.
answer: Invalid: the region of poodles that are dogs but not terriers is left open.

@mistakes
- Shading an I or O premise, or placing an X before drawing the universal premises. Always shade first, then place the X.
- Putting an X on a region that has already been shaded. Shaded means empty, so the X goes in the other region (or on the line if neither is shaded).
- Treating a conclusion as shown because it is plausible. The diagram of the premises alone must already show it, with the X inside the region, not on a line.
- Using the traditional reading in a modern-reading problem, so that two universal premises seem to give a particular conclusion such as "Some $S$ are $P$".

@practice num
prompt: A Venn diagram of three circles has eight regions. After you shade the premises "All $S$ are $M$" and "No $M$ are $P$", how many of the eight regions remain unshaded?
answer: 4
unit: regions
check: sum(1 for s, p, m in product([0, 1], repeat=3) if not ((s and not m) or (m and p)))
hint: "All $S$ are $M$" shades the two regions of $S$ outside $M$; "No $M$ are $P$" shades the two regions where $M$ and $P$ overlap.
solution: "All $S$ are $M$" shades $S$ only and $S$ with $P$ only. "No $M$ are $P$" shades $P$ with $M$ only and all three. These are four different regions, so $8 - 4 = 4$ remain: $P$ only, $M$ only, $S$ with $M$ only and the outside.

@quiz num
prompt: After you shade the premises "All $S$ are $M$" and "All $S$ are $P$" on a three-circle Venn diagram (eight regions), how many regions remain unshaded?
answer: 5
unit: regions
check: sum(1 for s, p, m in product([0, 1], repeat=3) if not ((s and not m) or (s and not p)))
hint: Each premise shades the regions of $S$ outside one other circle. Some regions are shaded by both.
solution: "All $S$ are $M$" shades $S$ only and $S$ with $P$ only. "All $S$ are $P$" shades $S$ only and $S$ with $M$ only. The union has 3 regions ($S$ only is counted once), so $8 - 3 = 5$ regions remain.
