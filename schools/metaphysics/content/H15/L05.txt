@article
"If you study, you will pass." "You will pass only if you study." These sound alike, but they say different things: the first says that studying is enough to pass, the second that studying is needed. To reason with such sentences exactly, logic translates them into a small formal language, **propositional logic**, which only cares about how whole statements combine.

### Atomic and compound statements

An **atomic statement** contains no other statement as a part ("It is raining"). We give each one a letter: $r$ for "It is raining". A **compound statement** is built from atomic ones with **connectives**. A **key** says what each letter stands for.

### The five connectives

| Symbol | Name | Reads | Typical English |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\neg p$ | negation | not $p$ | not, it is not the case that |
| $p \wedge q$ | conjunction | $p$ and $q$ | and, but, although, also |
| $p \vee q$ | disjunction | $p$ or $q$ (or both) | or, either ... or |
| $p \to q$ | conditional | if $p$ then $q$ | if, only if, provided that |
| $p \leftrightarrow q$ | biconditional | $p$ if and only if $q$ | if and only if, just in case |

In $p \to q$, $p$ is the **antecedent** and $q$ the **consequent**. Our "or" is **inclusive**: $p \vee q$ is true when either or both is true. "But" translates as $\wedge$ because logic keeps only the truth conditions, not the contrast.

### The tricky English patterns

- "If $p$, then $q$" and "$q$ if $p$" both mean $p \to q$: the antecedent is the part that follows "if", wherever it appears in the sentence.
- "$p$ **only if** $q$" is $p \to q$: $p$ cannot happen without $q$. "You will pass only if you study": $p \to s$.
- "$p$ is **sufficient** for $q$" is $p \to q$; "$p$ is **necessary** for $q$" is $q \to p$.
- "$p$ if and only if $q$" is $p \leftrightarrow q$.
- "$p$ **unless** $q$" is treated in this course as $p \vee q$, which is equivalent to $\neg q \to p$.
- "**Neither** $p$ **nor** $q$" is $\neg p \wedge \neg q$ (equivalently $\neg(p \vee q)$).
- "**Not both** $p$ and $q$" is $\neg(p \wedge q)$, which is different from "both not": $\neg p \wedge \neg q$.

### Main connective and parentheses

The **main connective** is the one that holds the whole formula together. In $(p \wedge q) \to r$ it is $\to$; in $\neg(p \wedge q)$ it is $\neg$. Parentheses show grouping, and they matter: $\neg p \wedge q$ negates only $p$, while $\neg(p \wedge q)$ negates the conjunction. To translate, first find the main connective in the English ("if ... then", "or", "and"), and then translate each part.

### What translation loses

Propositional logic keeps only truth conditions. "She took the medicine and got well" and "She got well and took the medicine" are both $m \wedge w$, though the order in English suggests different things. Translation is a judgment, and sometimes an English sentence has more than one reasonable symbolization.

@example
title: A conditional with a conjunction
problem: Translate with the key $r$: it rains, $c$: the game is cancelled, $w$: we get wet. "If it rains and the game is not cancelled, we will get wet."
step: The main connective is "if ... then", so the formula is a conditional: antecedent $\to$ consequent.
step: The antecedent is "it rains and the game is not cancelled": $r \wedge \neg c$. The consequent is $w$.
step: Put them together, keeping parentheses so that the whole conjunction is the antecedent.
answer: $(r \wedge \neg c) \to w$

@example
title: Only if, unless and not both
problem: Use the key $a$: Ana comes, $b$: Ben comes, $c$: Cy comes. Translate: (a) "Ana comes only if Ben comes." (b) "Cy comes unless Ben comes." (c) "Ana and Ben do not both come."
step: (a) "Only if" introduces the necessary condition: Ana cannot come without Ben. That is $a \to b$, not $b \to a$.
step: (b) "Unless" is $\vee$ in this course: $c \vee b$. The equivalent form $\neg b \to c$ says that if Ben does not come, Cy does.
step: (c) "Not both" negates the conjunction: $\neg(a \wedge b)$. This is not $\neg a \wedge \neg b$, which would say that neither comes.
answer: (a) $a \to b$; (b) $c \vee b$ (or $\neg b \to c$); (c) $\neg(a \wedge b)$.

@mistakes
- Reversing "only if": "$p$ only if $q$" is $p \to q$, not $q \to p$. "You can vote only if you are 18" means voting requires being 18.
- Translating "neither $p$ nor $q$" as $\neg p \vee \neg q$. That says at least one is false; "neither" says both are false.
- Losing parentheses: $\neg p \wedge q$ and $\neg(p \wedge q)$ are different formulas with different meanings.
- Putting the parts in the order they are spoken. In "The picnic is on if it is sunny" the antecedent is "it is sunny", so the formula is $s \to p$, not $p \to s$.

choice*: $p \to q$
choice: $q \to p$
choice: $p \leftrightarrow q$
choice: $p \wedge q$

prompt: Using $b$: the bus is running and $t$: the train is running, which formula translates "Neither the bus nor the train is running"?
choice*: $\neg b \wedge \neg t$
choice: $\neg(b \wedge t)$
choice: $\neg b \vee \neg t$
choice: $b \vee t$
hint: "Neither" says that both are not running.
solution: "Neither ... nor" says each fails: $\neg b \wedge \neg t$ (equivalent to $\neg(b \vee t)$). $\neg(b \wedge t)$ and $\neg b \vee \neg t$ only say that they are not both running.

choice: $t \to e$
choice*: $e \to t$

choice: $t \wedge e$

@practice self
prompt: Give a key and translate: (a) "Either Lena sings or Omar plays, but not both." (b) "If the roads are icy and the buses are late, school opens late." (c) "The cake is sweet unless it has no sugar."
hint: Find the main connective of each sentence first, then the parts.
solution: Key: $l$: Lena sings; $o$: Omar plays; $i$: the roads are icy; $b$: the buses are late; $s$: school opens late; $c$: the cake is sweet; $n$: the cake has no sugar. (a) $(l \vee o) \wedge \neg(l \wedge o)$. (b) $(i \wedge b) \to s$. (c) $c \vee n$, or equivalently $\neg n \to c$.
rubric: States a key with one letter for each atomic statement and no letter for a compound.
rubric: Translates (a) with an inclusive "or" and a separate "not both" clause, and (b) with a conjunction in the antecedent.
rubric: Translates (c) as a disjunction or as the equivalent conditional.

@quiz mc
prompt: In this course "$p$ unless $q$" is symbolized as $p \vee q$. Which formula is equivalent to it?
choice*: $\neg q \to p$
choice: $q \to p$
choice: $p \to q$
choice: $\neg p \to \neg q$
hint: A conditional $\neg q \to p$ is false only when $\neg q$ is true and $p$ false.
solution: $\neg q \to p$ is false only when $q$ is false and $p$ is false, which is exactly when $p \vee q$ is false, so they are equivalent. The others are true or false on different rows (for example $p \to q$ is true when both letters are false, but $p \vee q$ is not).

@quiz mc
prompt: What is the main connective of $\neg(p \wedge q) \to r$?
choice: $\neg$
choice: $\wedge$
choice*: $\to$
choice: $\vee$
hint: Ask which connective holds the two big halves of the formula together.
solution: The formula is a conditional whose antecedent is $\neg(p \wedge q)$ and whose consequent is $r$. The negation sits inside the antecedent, so the main connective is $\to$.
