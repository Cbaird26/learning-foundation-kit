@article
Three friends get sick after a party. What made them ill? Everyone at the party ate something different and stood in different places. Working out what caused the illness is **causal reasoning**, and the 19th-century philosopher John Stuart Mill described a set of methods for finding **candidate causes** in tables of cases. They do not prove a cause, but they show where to look.

### Mill's methods

Each method compares cases in which an effect does or does not occur, and the factors present in each.

- **Method of agreement**: if two or more cases where the effect occurs have just one factor in common, that factor is a candidate cause. Look at what the cases share.
- **Method of difference**: if a case where the effect occurs and a case where it does not are alike in every factor except one, that factor is a candidate cause. Look at what separates two otherwise matching cases.
- **Joint method** (agreement and difference together): the factor present in every case with the effect and absent in cases without it. This is stronger than either alone.
- **Method of concomitant variation**: if the effect rises and falls as a factor rises and falls, the factor is a candidate cause.
- **Method of residues**: subtract the effects of causes already known; the remaining effect is due to the remaining factor.

### Why the methods only give candidates

The methods assume we have listed all the relevant factors. If we miss one, the common factor may be a coincidence. They also assume a single cause: the effect might come from several factors together, or from different causes in different cases. This is why the strongest evidence comes from experiments, where we control the factors.

### Correlation is not causation

Two things are **correlated** when they tend to vary together. If $A$ and $B$ are correlated, there are several explanations:

1. $A$ causes $B$.
2. $B$ causes $A$ (**reverse causation**).
3. A **common cause** $C$ causes both. Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer because of hot weather; ice cream does not cause drowning.
4. **Coincidence**, especially with small samples or many comparisons.

The fallacy of **post hoc ergo propter hoc** ("after this, therefore because of this") assumes that because $B$ followed $A$, $A$ caused $B$.

### Experiments

In a **randomized controlled trial**, participants are assigned by chance to a **treatment group** or a **control group**, which differ only in the treatment. Randomization spreads other factors evenly, so differences in outcome are probably due to the treatment. Using a placebo and keeping participants and assessors from knowing who has the real treatment (**blinding**) guard against expectation effects.

To compare groups, use rates, not raw counts. The **relative risk** is the rate in one group divided by the rate in the other: if 12% of exposed people and 4% of unexposed people get ill, the relative risk is $0.12/0.04 = 3$. The difference in rates, 8 percentage points, tells you how many people are affected in absolute terms.

@example
title: Method of agreement
problem: Three diners were ill after a meal. Which factor does the method of agreement point to?

| Diner | Soup | Salad | Fish | Pie | Ill? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | yes | yes | yes | no | yes |
| B | no | yes | yes | yes | yes |
| C | yes | no | yes | yes | yes |
step: Look at the factors present in every case where the effect (illness) occurs. Soup: A and C but not B. Salad: A and B but not C. Pie: B and C but not A.
step: Fish is the only factor present in all three cases.
step: So fish is the candidate cause. The result is weak: we have no cases of people who ate fish and were well, and only three cases in all.
answer: Fish is the candidate cause by the method of agreement; the evidence is only suggestive.

@example
title: Method of difference
problem: Two identical seedlings are planted in the same soil, kept in the same light and watered the same amount. One gets fertilizer and grows 20 cm; the other gets none and grows 12 cm. What does the method of difference say?
step: The cases are alike in soil, light and water, and differ only in fertilizer.
step: The effect (extra growth) occurs with the fertilizer and not without it.
step: The fertilizer is the candidate cause of the extra 8 cm of growth. A single pair is weak evidence, since seedlings can vary for other reasons, so repeat with many pairs.
answer: Fertilizer is the candidate cause; replicate with more seedlings to reduce the chance of coincidence.

@mistakes
- Concluding from agreement that the common factor is certainly the cause. It is a candidate: other factors you did not list could be responsible.
- Treating a correlation as proof of causation, or "B came after A" as proof that A caused B.
- Comparing raw counts instead of rates when the groups are different sizes.
- Ignoring reverse causation and common causes: when two things move together, check whether a third thing drives both.

@practice num
prompt: In one group, 21 of 150 people developed a rash after using a product. In another group, 14 of 400 people who did not use it developed a rash. What is the relative risk (the rate in the first group divided by the rate in the second)?
answer: 4
check: (21 / 150) / (14 / 400)
hint: Compute each group's rate before dividing.
solution: The first rate is $21/150 = 0.14$, and the second is $14/400 = 0.035$. The relative risk is $0.14/0.035 = 4$. This alone does not show that the product causes rashes, since the groups may differ in other ways.

@practice mc

choice: Sending more fire trucks causes more damage.
choice: Damage that has not yet happened causes more trucks to be sent.
choice*: Bigger fires both call for more trucks and cause more damage.
choice: The correlation must be a coincidence, since it could not be a real pattern.

solution: A common cause, the size of the fire, raises both the number of trucks dispatched and the damage done. The other explanations are implausible, and a strong correlation across many fires is unlikely to be pure coincidence.

@practice self
prompt: Describe a randomized controlled trial to test whether a new study app improves test scores. Say how you would form the groups, what you would keep the same, and what you would measure.
hint: Think about how to make the two groups alike except for the app.
solution: Recruit 200 students and assign each by a coin flip or random number to the app group or the control group. Both groups follow the same course, teacher and number of study hours; the control group uses a neutral alternative such as ordinary notes. After six weeks, give both groups the same test, graded without knowing who used the app, and compare average scores (and rates of passing). Random assignment makes it unlikely that differences in motivation or ability account for any difference.
rubric: Assigns participants to groups by chance, not by choice.
rubric: Keeps other factors the same and uses a comparison group.
rubric: Measures the same outcome for both groups and compares them, ideally with blinded grading.

@quiz num
prompt: In a trial, 220 of 400 people given a treatment recovered, and 160 of 400 people given a placebo recovered. By how many percentage points is the recovery rate higher in the treatment group?
answer: 15
unit: points
check: (220 / 400 - 160 / 400) * 100
hint: Compute each group's recovery rate as a percent.
solution: The treatment rate is $220/400 = 55\%$ and the placebo rate is $160/400 = 40\%$, a difference of 15 percentage points.
