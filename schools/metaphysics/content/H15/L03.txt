@article
"All dolphins are mammals." "No snakes are mammals." "Some pets are mammals." "Some pets are not mammals." Each of these says how two classes relate. Aristotle's logic was built on such statements, and they are still the simplest way to reason about "all", "none" and "some".

### The four forms

A **categorical statement** relates two classes: the **subject** class $S$ and the **predicate** class $P$. There are four standard forms, labeled by the vowels of the Latin words *affirmo* and *nego*.

| Letter | Form | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | All $S$ are $P$ | universal affirmative | All dolphins are mammals. |
| E | No $S$ are $P$ | universal negative | No snakes are mammals. |
| I | Some $S$ are $P$ | particular affirmative | Some pets are mammals. |
| O | Some $S$ are not $P$ | particular negative | Some pets are not mammals. |

In logic, "some" means "at least one", so "Some pets are mammals" does not say that some pets are not.

### Venn diagrams of single statements

Draw two overlapping circles, $S$ and $P$. This makes four regions: $S$ only, both $S$ and $P$, $P$ only, and neither (outside the circles). **Shading** a region says it is empty; an **X** in a region says something is there.

| Form | Diagram |
|---|---|
| A: All $S$ are $P$ | Shade the region $S$ only (no $S$ outside $P$). |
| E: No $S$ are $P$ | Shade the region where $S$ and $P$ overlap. |
| I: Some $S$ are $P$ | Put an X in the overlap. |
| O: Some $S$ are not $P$ | Put an X in the region $S$ only. |

### The square of opposition

Compare the diagrams. A shades exactly the region where O puts its X, and E shades exactly the region where I puts its X. So A and O are **contradictories**: they always have opposite truth values, as are E and I. The traditional square adds three more relations:

- **Contraries** (A and E): not both true.
- **Subcontraries** (I and O): not both false.
- **Subalternation**: A implies I, and E implies O.

### Existential import: modern and traditional readings

The traditional relations assume that the subject class has at least one member, so "All $S$ are $P$" would be false if there were no $S$. This assumption is called **existential import**. The modern (Boolean) reading, which most logic courses use, says "All $S$ are $P$" only means that no $S$ lies outside $P$. Then "All unicorns are white" is true when there are no unicorns, whereas "Some unicorns are white" is false. On the modern reading only the contradictory relations (A with O, and E with I) survive; contraries, subcontraries and subalternation fail. Each reading is a coherent logic: they answer a slightly different question about what "all" commits you to.

### Translating everyday sentences

Rewrite sentences in standard form with a plural subject and predicate noun and one of the four quantifiers. "Every" and "any" mean A; "Not a single" and "There are no" mean E; "Only $S$ are $P$" means "All $P$ are $S$" (the order reverses); "Some $S$ aren't $P$" is O.

### Converse, obverse, contrapositive

Three operations rewrite a statement. The **converse** swaps subject and predicate: it is equivalent for E and I, but not for A and O. The **obverse** changes quality and replaces the predicate with its complement ("All $S$ are $P$" becomes "No $S$ are non-$P$"): equivalent for all four forms. The **contrapositive** swaps and complements both terms: equivalent for A and O, not for E and I.

@example
title: Translating into standard form
problem: Put each in standard categorical form and give its letter. (a) "Only members may vote." (b) "Not a single whale is a fish." (c) "Some of the students aren't athletes."
step: (a) "Only members may vote" means everyone who may vote is a member. Subject: voters; predicate: members. Form: All voters are members. Letter A (the order reverses with "only").
step: (b) "Not a single whale is a fish" says nothing in the class of whales is in the class of fish: No whales are fish. Letter E.
step: (c) "Some of the students aren't athletes" is already in the shape of O: Some students are not athletes.
answer: (a) All voters are members (A); (b) No whales are fish (E); (c) Some students are not athletes (O).

@example
title: Subalternation depends on existential import
problem: Suppose there are no dragons. Is "All dragons are green" true, and is "Some dragons are green" true, on the modern reading? What does this show about subalternation?
step: Modern reading: "All dragons are green" says there are no dragons that are not green. There are no dragons, so none lies outside the green things, and the statement is true.
step: "Some dragons are green" requires at least one dragon that is green. There are no dragons, so it is false.
step: So A is true while I is false. Subalternation (A implies I) fails on the modern reading; it holds only if we assume the subject class is not empty.
answer: "All dragons are green" is true and "Some dragons are green" is false, so on the modern reading A does not imply I.

@mistakes
- Translating "Only $S$ are $P$" as "All $S$ are $P$". It means "All $P$ are $S$".
- Thinking "Some $S$ are $P$" implies "Some $S$ are not $P$". "Some" means at least one, and all the $S$ might be $P$.
- Treating the converse of "All $S$ are $P$" as equivalent to it. "All dogs are mammals" is true, but "All mammals are dogs" is false.
- Applying the traditional square (contraries, subalternation) when a problem says to use the modern reading. Only the contradictory pairs survive there.

prompt: In the traditional square of opposition (which assumes the subject class is not empty), suppose "All $S$ are $P$" is true. How many of the other three standard forms (E, I and O) are then true?
answer: 1
unit: forms
check: (lambda S, P: sum([not (S & P), bool(S & P), bool(S - P)]))({1, 2}, {1, 2, 3})
hint: A true A makes E false (contraries) and O false (contradictory); what does subalternation say about I?
solution: A true makes E false (contraries), O false (contradictories) and I true (subalternation). So exactly 1 of the three, I, is true.
