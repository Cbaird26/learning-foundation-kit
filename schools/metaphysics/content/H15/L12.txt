@article
"A new medicine reduced tumors in mice, so it may help people." "The window is broken, the lock is intact and muddy footprints lead away, so someone climbed in." The first argues from a resemblance, the second from what would best explain the clues. Both are inductive, and both are used constantly in science, medicine, law and everyday life.

### Arguments from analogy

An **argument from analogy** has this form:

| P1 | Case $A$ has properties $F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_n$ and also property $G$. |
| P2 | Case $B$ has properties $F_1, F_2, \ldots, F_n$. |
| C | So $B$ probably has $G$ too. |

How strong is such an argument? Look at:

- **Relevance**: do the shared properties actually bear on $G$? Resemblance in irrelevant features (hair color) counts for nothing.
- **Number and diversity**: more relevant similarities, and more varied cases that have $G$, make the argument stronger.
- **Dissimilarities**: relevant differences between $A$ and $B$ weaken it, because they may block $G$.
- **Modesty**: a modest conclusion ("probably", "may") is better supported than a bold one.

An analogy can be attacked by pointing out a relevant difference, or by offering a **counter-analogy**, an equally similar case that lacks $G$.

### Inference to the best explanation

**Inference to the best explanation** (IBE) reasons from evidence to the hypothesis that would explain it best. It is not deductive: the best explanation may still be false. The usual criteria for comparing hypotheses are:

1. **Explanatory scope and power**: explaining more of the evidence, and more precisely.
2. **Simplicity**: fewer unsupported assumptions.
3. **Consistency with what we already know**, including well-established theories.
4. **Testability**: making predictions that could prove it wrong, and surviving them.
5. **Not being ad hoc**: it does not add special assumptions only to escape refutation.

Philosophers disagree about how these criteria should be justified, and whether "best explanation" is a distinct kind of inference or can be reduced to probability. Everyone agrees that we should weigh rival explanations rather than seize the first one that fits.

### Linking explanation to probability

One way to make the weighing exact uses **Bayes's theorem**. With two exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses $H_1$ and $H_2$ and evidence $E$:

$$P(H_1 \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H_1)\,P(H_1)}{P(E \mid H_1)\,P(H_1) + P(E \mid H_2)\,P(H_2)}.$$

The **likelihood** $P(E \mid H)$ is how well $H$ predicts $E$, which is explanatory power in numbers; the **prior** $P(H)$ is how plausible $H$ was beforehand, which is consistency with background knowledge. A hypothesis that predicts the evidence well can still lose if its prior is very low.

@example
title: Judging an analogy
problem: "School A and School B have the same class sizes, funding, curriculum and teacher training. School A's students improved their reading after a tutoring program. So School B's students will probably improve if they take the same program." What would weaken this argument?
step: Identify the shared properties: class size, funding, curriculum, teacher training. These are relevant to how well a tutoring program works.
step: Look for relevant differences. Suppose most of School B's students are still learning the language of instruction while School A's are not. This difference bears directly on reading improvement.
step: That dissimilarity weakens the analogy: the program may need adjusting for School B, and the conclusion should be more modest.
answer: A relevant dissimilarity, such as a different language background, weakens it; irrelevant differences (the color of the buildings) would not.

@example
title: Weighing two explanations
problem: A gardener finds her tomatoes eaten overnight, hoofprints in the soil and a gap in the fence. H1: a deer came through the gap. H2: a neighbor ate the tomatoes and faked the prints. Compare the explanations.
step: Scope: H1 explains the eaten tomatoes, the hoofprints and the gap. H2 explains the first and the prints only by adding an assumption that they were faked.
step: Simplicity and consistency: H1 needs no unusual assumptions and fits what we know about deer. H2 adds a motive and a deception with no independent evidence.
step: H1 is the better explanation. It could still be wrong: a trail camera or checking for deer hair would test it.
answer: H1 (the deer) is the better explanation on scope, simplicity and consistency; it remains open to test.

@mistakes
- Treating similarity in any respect as support. Only similarities relevant to the conclusion count.
- Treating the best explanation as proved. IBE is inductive: the best of the available explanations may be false, or the true one may not yet have been thought of.
- Choosing the hypothesis that explains the evidence at all without comparing it with rivals.
- Rewarding an explanation for fitting the evidence after special assumptions were added to protect it; ad hoc additions make a hypothesis worse.

@practice num
prompt: Two exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses are equally likely before the evidence. The evidence has probability 0.8 given the first and 0.2 given the second. What is the probability of the first hypothesis after the evidence, as a percent?
answer: 80
unit: %
check: 100 * 0.5 * 0.8 / (0.5 * 0.8 + 0.5 * 0.2)
hint: Use Bayes's theorem with both priors equal to 0.5.
solution: $0.5 \times 0.8 = 0.40$ and $0.5 \times 0.2 = 0.10$, so the posterior is $0.40/0.50 = 0.8$, or 80%.

@practice self
prompt: Evaluate the causal claim in this news report: "A study of 5,000 adults over ten years found that those who drank three or more cups of coffee a day were 15% less likely to die during that time. Experts say people should drink more coffee." Say what kind of evidence this is, give at least three alternative explanations for the result, and say what further evidence you would want.
hint: Think of reverse causation, common causes, how the groups were formed and what "15% less likely" means.
solution: The study is observational: the researchers followed people who chose their own habits, so it shows a correlation. Alternative explanations: (1) a common cause, such as income or general health, might lead people both to drink more coffee and to live longer; (2) reverse causation, since people who are already ill may drink less coffee; (3) coincidence or measurement error, since coffee habits were probably self-reported. Also, "15% less likely" is a relative figure; the absolute difference may be small. Further evidence: a randomized trial, or an observational study that adjusts for age, health and income and finds a similar effect across several groups, along with a plausible mechanism. The claim that people should drink more coffee goes beyond the evidence.
rubric: Identifies the study as observational and the result as a correlation rather than a demonstrated cause.
rubric: Gives at least three alternative explanations, such as a common cause, reverse causation and coincidence or measurement error.
rubric: Says what further evidence would help, such as a randomized trial, controlling for confounders or a mechanism, and notes that the advice goes beyond the evidence.

@quiz num
prompt: Two exclusive and exhaustive hypotheses have prior probabilities 0.1 for $H_1$ and 0.9 for $H_2$. The evidence has probability 0.8 given $H_1$ and 0.2 given $H_2$. What is the probability of $H_1$ after the evidence, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 30.8
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * 0.1 * 0.8 / (0.1 * 0.8 + 0.9 * 0.2)
hint: Multiply each prior by its likelihood, then divide $H_1$'s product by the sum.
solution: $0.1 \times 0.8 = 0.08$ and $0.9 \times 0.2 = 0.18$, so the posterior is $0.08/0.26 \approx 0.308$, about 30.8%. The evidence favors $H_1$, but its low prior keeps it less likely than $H_2$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement about inference to the best explanation is correct?
choice: It is deductively valid, since the best explanation must be true.
choice*: It is inductive: the best available explanation could still be false.
choice: It only applies in science and never in everyday reasoning.
choice: It requires that the hypothesis explain one fact only.
hint: Recall whether true premises guarantee the conclusion.
solution: The premises (the evidence and the comparison of hypotheses) do not guarantee that the winning hypothesis is true: the true explanation may be one nobody has considered. So IBE is inductive. It is used in everyday reasoning as well as in science.
