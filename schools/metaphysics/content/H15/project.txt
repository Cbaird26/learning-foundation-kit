title: Logic casebook
overview: You will collect four real arguments from public sources, put each in standard form, test or evaluate it with the tools of this course, name its fallacies and write a one-page verdict on each. The casebook shows that you can apply deduction, induction, causal reasoning and the study of fallacies to arguments you did not write.
goal: Put real arguments in standard form with premises and conclusion stated as complete statements.
goal: Choose and apply the right test: a Venn diagram or truth table for deductive arguments, and strength, sampling, causal and analogy criteria for inductive ones.
goal: Identify fallacies precisely and give a fair verdict that separates "the argument fails" from "the conclusion is false".
step: Choose four arguments from public sources: one from an opinion column, one from a court opinion (a short passage of a published ruling), one from a classic philosophical text (a short public-domain passage, for example from Plato, Descartes or Hume) and one scientific claim from a news report or a science article. Find them through the course reading, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, PhilPapers, and public sources such as news sites and court websites, and cite every source you use.
step: For each argument, copy out the key passage (a few sentences), and number its statements. Ask what the author wants the reader to believe, and mark premise and conclusion indicators.
step: Write each argument in standard form, with premises P1, P2, ... and conclusion C, and add any implicit premise you need, labeled as implicit. Draw its structure (linked, convergent, serial) in words.
step: Decide whether each argument is meant as deductive or inductive. For each deductive argument, translate it into symbols or into categorical statements and test it with a truth table (or short truth table) or a three-circle Venn diagram. State validity and, if possible, soundness.
step: For each inductive argument, evaluate its strength with the criteria of weeks 10 to 12: sample size and bias, margin of error, causal alternatives (reverse causation, a common cause, coincidence), relevant similarities and differences in an analogy, and rival explanations.
step: Name every informal fallacy in each of the four arguments, quoting the words that show it, and explain each in a sentence. Note any passage that looks like a fallacy but is acceptable, and say why.
step: Write a one-page verdict on each argument: what it argues, whether it is valid or strong, whether it is sound or cogent, its fallacies, and what evidence would improve it. Keep the verdict about the argument, and say clearly when a conclusion may be true although the argument fails.
step: Check your work: recompute every truth table, recount every row, and make sure each Venn diagram description lists which regions are shaded or marked.
material: **Casebook template.** Use this table for each argument, and attach your working below it.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| Source | Title, author, date, where you found it |
| Passage | The key sentences, quoted exactly and briefly |
| Standard form | P1, P2, ... and C (mark implicit premises) |
| Structure | Linked, convergent or serial, with a one-line description |

material: **Sample worked case.** Passage (invented for practice): "If the bridge is unsafe, the city will close it. The city has not closed it. So the bridge is safe." Standard form: P1, if the bridge is unsafe then the city closes it; P2, the city has not closed it; C, the bridge is safe. Symbols with $u$: the bridge is unsafe and $c$: the city closes the bridge: $u \to c$, $\neg c$ $\therefore$ $\neg u$. This has the form of modus tollens, so it is valid. Soundness depends on whether P1 is true: the city might fail to close an unsafe bridge, so P1 may be false. Verdict: the argument is valid, and it is as good as its first premise.
deliverable: A casebook with four cases, each with source, passage, standard form, structure, test, result and fallacies, using the template.

rubric: Inductive arguments are evaluated with the right criteria (sample, margin, causal alternatives, analogy, rival explanations) and the verdict explains why the support is strong or weak.

@review
## Week 13 review guide

### Key ideas, week by week

1. **Arguments and standard form.** Premises give reasons for a conclusion; explanations, descriptions and commands are not arguments. Structures: linked, convergent, serial.
2. **Validity, soundness, strength.** Valid: no possible case with true premises and a false conclusion. Sound: valid with all premises true. Strong: the premises make the conclusion likely. Cogent: strong with true premises.
3. **Categorical statements.** A, E, I, O; contradictories A-O and E-I; existential import separates the modern and traditional readings.
4. **Syllogisms.** Three circles, eight regions; shade universal premises first, then place the X; the conclusion must already be shown.
5. **Propositional logic.** Five connectives. "Only if" is $p \to q$; "unless" is $\vee$; "neither" is $\neg p \wedge \neg q$; "not both" is $\neg(p \wedge q)$.
6. **Truth tables.** $2^n$ rows in the standard order. Tautology, contradiction, contingency. The conditional is false only on T, F.
7. **Testing arguments.** Valid when no row makes all premises true and the conclusion false.
8. **Short tables and forms.** Modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical and disjunctive syllogism are valid; affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent are not.
9. **Fallacies.** Ad hominem, straw man, popularity, ignorance, authority, red herring, false dilemma, begging the question, slippery slope, equivocation.
10. **Statistics.** Sample size, representativeness and bias; margin of error $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$; gambler's fallacy; base rates.
11. **Causation.** Mill's methods; reverse causation, common cause and coincidence; randomized trials; relative risk.
12. **Analogy and IBE.** Relevant similarities and differences; scope, simplicity, consistency, testability and not being ad hoc; priors and likelihoods.

### Self-check

- Can I put any short passage in standard form and say whether it is deductive or inductive?
- Can I build a 3-letter truth table, and find the counterexample row for an invalid argument?
- Can I test a syllogism on a Venn diagram, and explain the result in words?
- Can I name a fallacy and say exactly what is wrong with it?
- Can I compute a margin of error and a probability with a base rate?
- Can I explain to someone without notes why a correlation does not prove a cause?

### Most common errors

- Calling an argument invalid because its conclusion is false, or valid because its premises are true.
- Reversing "only if", or translating "neither" as $\neg p \vee \neg q$.
- Treating a false antecedent as making a conditional false.
- Judging validity from only some rows of a truth table.
- Placing an X before shading, or on a shaded region.
- Concluding that a conclusion is false because the argument for it is fallacious.
- Taking a large sample to cure bias, or a streak to change independent odds.
- Reading a correlation as a cause.

@exam text
prompt: Fill in the main column of the truth table for $(p \wedge q) \to r$, with the eight rows in the standard order TTT, TTF, TFT, TFF, FTT, FTF, FFT, FFF for $p, q, r$. Type eight letters, T or F, with no spaces.
answer: TFTTTTTT
check: tt_column(lambda p, q, r: implies(p and q, r))
hint: A conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false.
solution: The antecedent $p \wedge q$ is true only on TTT and TTF. On TTT, $r$ is true, so the conditional is T; on TTF, $r$ is false, so it is F. On all other rows the antecedent is false, so the conditional is T. The column is TFTTTTTT.

@exam mc
prompt: "No amphibians are mammals. Some pets are amphibians. So some pets are not mammals." Which verdict and reason is correct, on the modern reading?
choice*: Valid: the first premise shades the region where amphibians and mammals overlap, so the pets that are amphibians must lie outside mammals.
choice: Invalid: a syllogism with a negative premise can never be valid.
choice: Invalid: the conclusion is false in the actual world.
choice: Valid, because its premises and conclusion are all true.
hint: Shade the universal premise first, then see where the X for "some pets are amphibians" must go.
solution: With the overlap of amphibians and mammals shaded, the X for a pet that is an amphibian can lie only in the part of the amphibians circle outside mammals. That X lies inside "pets but not mammals", which is exactly what the conclusion needs, so the syllogism is valid. A negative premise does not make an argument invalid, and validity does not depend on the conclusion's actual truth.

@exam self
prompt: Read this passage from a letter: "A recent poll of 50 people leaving a pizza restaurant found that 45 of them think pizza is the best food. So most people in our city think pizza is the best food. Anyone who disagrees is just a food snob. Since the new pizza tax will put this restaurant out of business and then no one will have any good food left, we must repeal it." Put the main argument in standard form, say whether it is deductive or inductive and weak or strong, and name two fallacies.
hint: Find the conclusion first. Then examine the sample and the chain of consequences.
solution: Standard form: P1: In a poll of 50 people leaving a pizza restaurant, 45 said pizza is the best food. C1: Most people in the city think pizza is the best food. (Further premises about the pizza tax then lead to the final conclusion that the tax must be repealed.) The step from P1 to C1 is inductive and weak: the sample is small and biased, since people leaving a pizza restaurant are more likely than others to like pizza. Fallacies: hasty generalization (a biased sample of 50 for a whole city); ad hominem ("anyone who disagrees is just a food snob"), and the claim that the tax would leave "no one with any good food" is a slippery slope.
rubric: Gives a standard form with the poll result as a premise and the claim about the city as the conclusion, as complete statements.
rubric: Classifies the argument as inductive and weak, citing the small sample and the bias from where the sample was taken.
rubric: Names at least two fallacies correctly (hasty generalization, ad hominem, slippery slope or false dilemma) and links each to the words in the passage.
