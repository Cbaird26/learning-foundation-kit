@article
"Everyone is buying this phone, so it must be good." It is persuasive, and it is not a good argument. A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning that seems to support its conclusion but does not. A **formal fallacy** (like affirming the consequent) is bad because of its logical form, as week 8 showed. An **informal fallacy** is bad because of its content: the premises are irrelevant to the conclusion, they assume what is in question, or a word slides between meanings. Informal fallacies are not found by truth tables, but by asking what the premises really have to do with the conclusion.

### Fallacies of relevance

- **Ad hominem**: rejecting a claim by attacking the person who makes it instead of the reasons. "You cannot trust her budget, because she was fined for speeding." The person's character is irrelevant to the arithmetic.
- **Straw man**: replacing an opponent's view with a weaker version and attacking that. "She wants shorter homework, so she thinks students should learn nothing at home."
- **Appeal to popularity** (bandwagon): "Everyone believes it, so it is true."
- **Appeal to ignorance**: "Nobody has proved it false, so it is true" (or the reverse). Lack of proof one way is not proof the other way.
- **Illegitimate appeal to authority**: citing someone with no expertise in the matter, or an expert outside their field, or treating authority as decisive. Citing a qualified expert on their own subject is a good inductive argument (weeks 10 and 12).
- **Red herring**: changing the subject. Asked about pollution, the manager talks about jobs.

### Fallacies of presumption

- **False dilemma**: presenting two options as the only ones when others exist. "Either we ban all phones or students will never learn."
- **Begging the question** (circular reasoning): the conclusion is assumed in a premise. "The rule should be obeyed, because it must be obeyed." Such an argument is valid, but it cannot persuade anyone who doubted the conclusion.
- **Slippery slope**: claiming that one step will lead to a chain of bad results without support for the links. "If we allow one late assignment, soon nobody will hand anything in."
- **Hasty generalization**: drawing a general claim from too few or unrepresentative cases (week 10).

### Fallacies of ambiguity

- **Equivocation**: using a word in two senses in one argument. "A feather is light. Whatever is light cannot be dark. So a feather cannot be dark." "Light" means low weight in the first premise and bright in the second.

### Care in applying labels

Three cautions. First, a fallacy shows that an argument fails, not that its conclusion is false: a conclusion can be true although it was defended badly. Second, the same words can be fair or fallacious. If a witness stands to profit from the verdict, that is a good reason to check the testimony more closely; it is fallacious only if used as the sole reason to say the testimony is false. A slippery slope is acceptable when someone gives evidence for each step. Third, many passages contain more than one fallacy, and some labels overlap. Name the fallacy by saying what is wrong: what the premise has to do with the conclusion.

@example
title: Naming and explaining a fallacy
problem: Identify the fallacy: "The senator's library budget must be wrong, because he was fined for parking illegally last year."
step: Find the conclusion: the library budget is wrong. Find the premise: the senator was fined for parking illegally.
step: Ask whether the premise bears on the conclusion. A parking fine says nothing about whether the budget's numbers are right.
step: The argument attacks the person instead of the budget: ad hominem.
answer: Ad hominem: the premise concerns the senator's conduct, not the budget.

@example
title: A false dilemma
problem: Identify the fallacy: "Either we ban phones completely or students will never learn. We do not want that, so we must ban phones."
step: The first premise is a disjunction: ban phones completely, or students never learn.
step: Other options exist, such as limiting phones to certain times, so the disjunction leaves them out.
step: The argument is valid in form (disjunctive syllogism), but the disjunctive premise is not justified: false dilemma.
answer: False dilemma: it presents two options as exhaustive when others exist.

@mistakes
- Calling every use of authority a fallacy. Citing a real expert in their field is a reasonable inductive argument; the fallacy is citing someone who lacks the relevant expertise.
- Concluding that a claim is false because it was argued fallaciously. The conclusion may still be true.
- Calling every attack on a person an ad hominem. Doubting a witness who has a financial stake is acceptable when it prompts further checking; it is fallacious when the stake is treated as proof that the claim is false.
- Naming a fallacy without explaining it. A good answer says what is wrong with the premise's relation to the conclusion.

@practice self
prompt: Write your own short argument that begs the question, and explain in two sentences why it is a fallacy even though it may be valid.
hint: Make the premise say the same thing as the conclusion in different words.
solution: Example: "Honesty is the best policy because being honest always works out better than lying." The premise is just the conclusion restated, so anyone who doubted the conclusion would doubt the premise as well. The argument is valid, since a statement follows from itself, but it gives no independent reason and so cannot persuade.
rubric: Gives an argument whose premise restates or presupposes its conclusion.

@quiz mc
prompt: "Nothing is better than lifelong happiness. A cheese sandwich is better than nothing. So a cheese sandwich is better than lifelong happiness." Which fallacy is this?
choice: Ad hominem
choice*: Equivocation
choice: False dilemma
choice: Appeal to popularity
hint: Check whether the same word has the same meaning in both premises.
solution: "Nothing" means "no thing" in the first premise and "an empty plate, having nothing" in the second, so the argument slides between two meanings: equivocation.

choice: Red herring
choice: Slippery slope
choice*: Begging the question
choice: Straw man

solution: The premise ("it is a lovely work of art") says in other words what the conclusion ("this painting is beautiful") says, so it gives no independent reason: begging the question.
