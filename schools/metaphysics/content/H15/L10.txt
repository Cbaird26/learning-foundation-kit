@article
A poll of four people says that three of them prefer the green logo. Should the company switch? Most people feel the answer is "no", but it is worth knowing why, and how many people would make the poll worth trusting.

### Inductive generalization

An **inductive generalization** argues from a sample to a population: "In a sample, $x$% have property $F$; so about $x$% of the **population** have $F$." The population is the whole group; the **sample** is the part examined. Strength depends on:

1. **Sample size**: larger samples vary less.
2. **Representativeness**: a sample is representative if its members are like the population in the ways that matter. A **random sample**, where every member has an equal chance of being picked, tends to be representative.
3. **Absence of bias**: a **biased sample** is unrepresentative in a way that skews the result. Asking only gym members about exercise habits, letting people volunteer to answer, or using loaded questions ("Do you agree that wasteful spending should end?") produce bias. A larger sample does not cure bias.

A generalization from too few or unrepresentative cases is a **hasty generalization**.

### Margin of error

Even a random sample is only approximately right. For a proportion $p$ estimated from a random sample of size $n$, the 95% **margin of error** is about

$$1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.$$

If 54% of 900 voters back a plan, the margin is $1.96\sqrt{0.54 \times 0.46/900} \approx 0.033$, about 3.3 percentage points. We say we are 95% confident that the population value lies within that margin of the sample value, which means that polls built this way would capture the true value about 95% of the time. The margin shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so to halve it you need four times the sample, and it says nothing about bias.

### Probability traps

The **gambler's fallacy** says that after a run of heads a tail is "due". Coin flips are independent: for a fair coin the next flip is heads with probability 0.5 whatever came before. The probability of four heads in a row is $0.5^4 = 0.0625$, but after three heads, the chance of a fourth is still 0.5.

**Base-rate neglect** ignores how common something is in the first place. Suppose 2% of people have a condition, a test detects it in 80% of those who have it, and it wrongly flags 10% of those who do not. Out of 10,000 people, 200 have the condition and 160 of them test positive; 9,800 do not, and 980 of them test positive. So of the $160 + 980 = 1{,}140$ positives, only 160 are true, about 14%. A positive result is far from conclusive, because the condition is rare. Working with counts out of a large round number ("natural frequencies") makes such calculations easier.

### Correlation and averages

Be careful, too, with "average": the mean, median and mode can differ a lot in skewed data, and a claim about an average says little about any individual. Next week asks whether a correlation shows cause.

@example
title: A margin of error
problem: A random poll of 1,600 students finds that 30% prefer a later start time. Give the 95% margin of error in percentage points to one decimal place.
step: Use the formula with $p = 0.30$ and $n = 1600$: $1.96\sqrt{0.30 \times 0.70 / 1600}$.
step: $0.30 \times 0.70 = 0.21$; $0.21/1600 = 0.00013125$; its square root is about 0.01146.
step: Multiply by 1.96: about 0.02245, or 2.2 percentage points.
answer: About 2.2 percentage points, so the population value is probably between about 27.8% and 32.2%.

@example
title: Base rate with natural frequencies
problem: A condition affects 1% of a group. A test is positive for 90% of those who have it and for 9% of those who do not. A person tests positive. What is the probability that they have the condition?
step: Imagine 10,000 people. Then 100 have the condition and 9,900 do not.
step: Positives among those with the condition: 90% of 100 is 90. Positives among those without it: 9% of 9,900 is 891.
step: Total positives: $90 + 891 = 981$. The fraction with the condition is $90/981 \approx 0.092$.
answer: About 9.2%: most positives are false alarms because the condition is rare.

@mistakes
- Treating a large sample as a cure for bias. A million self-selected respondents can be worse than a few hundred chosen at random.
- Treating a margin of error as covering every kind of mistake. It describes only random sampling variation, not biased wording or an unrepresentative sample.
- Thinking a coin that has landed heads several times is "due" for tails. Independent events have no memory.
- Reading "the test is 90% accurate" as "a positive result is 90% likely to be right". That depends on the base rate.

@practice num
prompt: A random poll of 400 voters finds that 50% support a proposal. Using $1.96\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, what is the 95% margin of error, in percentage points to one decimal place?
answer: 4.9
unit: points
tol: 0.1
check: 1.96 * sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 400) * 100
hint: With $p = 0.5$, $p(1-p) = 0.25$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.25/400} = \sqrt{0.000625} = 0.025$, and $1.96 \times 0.025 = 0.049$: 4.9 percentage points.

@practice num
prompt: A fair coin is tossed five times. What is the probability that all five tosses are heads?
answer: 0.03125
check: 0.5 ** 5
hint: The tosses are independent, so multiply the probabilities.
solution: $0.5^5 = 0.03125$, or $1/32$. If four heads have already come up, the probability that the fifth is heads is still 0.5.

@practice num
prompt: A disease affects 4% of a population. A test is positive for 75% of people who have it and for 6% of people who do not. Someone tests positive. What is the probability that they have the disease, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 34.2
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * 0.04 * 0.75 / (0.04 * 0.75 + 0.96 * 0.06)
hint: Imagine 10,000 people and count the true positives and the false positives separately.
solution: Of 10,000 people, 400 have the disease and 300 of them test positive. Of the 9,600 without it, 576 test positive. So $300/(300 + 576) \approx 0.3425$, about 34.2%.

@practice self
prompt: A letter to the editor reads: "Dear Editor, our town should close its public pool. I asked five of my friends and not one of them swims there, so clearly nobody uses it. And anyone who defends the pool is just a lifeguard protecting a paycheck. Either we close the pool or taxes will skyrocket and the town will go bankrupt. Besides, Councilor Reyes wants to keep it, and she failed to repair a road last year." Identify at least four fallacies, quote the words involved and explain each in a sentence.
hint: Look for a sample problem, an attack on people, a missing option, and a premise unrelated to the pool.
solution: (1) Hasty generalization: five friends is a tiny, unrepresentative sample, so "nobody uses it" does not follow. (2) Ad hominem (circumstantial): "anyone who defends the pool is just a lifeguard protecting a paycheck" attacks the defenders' motives instead of their reasons. (3) False dilemma (with a slippery slope): "either we close the pool or taxes will skyrocket" ignores other options and asserts the chain to bankruptcy without support. (4) Ad hominem: the councilor's failure to repair a road is irrelevant to whether the pool should stay open.
rubric: Names at least four distinct fallacies correctly, such as hasty generalization, ad hominem, false dilemma and slippery slope.
rubric: Quotes or points to the words in the letter that exhibit each one.
rubric: Explains for each why the premise fails to support the conclusion.

@quiz num
prompt: A condition affects 5% of a group. A test is positive for 95% of people who have it and for 5% of people who do not. Someone tests positive. What is the probability that they have the condition, as a percent?
answer: 50
unit: %
check: 100 * 0.05 * 0.95 / (0.05 * 0.95 + 0.95 * 0.05)
hint: Imagine 10,000 people. How many true positives and how many false positives are there?
solution: Of 10,000 people, 500 have the condition and 475 test positive; of the 9,500 without it, 475 test positive. So the fraction is $475/(475 + 475) = 0.5$, or 50%.

@quiz mc
prompt: A website invites visitors to vote in an online poll on whether the town needs a new stadium, and 12,000 people vote. Why can't we conclude that most residents want a stadium if most votes say yes?
choice*: The sample is self-selected, so it may be biased even though it is large.
choice: The sample is too large to be representative.
choice: The margin of error is zero for large samples.
choice: Polls cannot be used to learn about a population.
hint: Think about who chooses to take part in an online poll.
solution: People who volunteer to vote may care more about the issue than other residents, so the sample may not represent the population. A large sample reduces random error but does nothing about this kind of bias.

@quiz mc
prompt: A roulette wheel has landed on red six times in a row. Which statement about the next spin is correct, assuming the wheel is fair and spins are independent?
choice: Black is now more likely, since it is due.
choice: Red is now more likely, since the wheel is on a streak.
choice*: The chances are the same as on any other spin.
choice: The next result cannot be predicted at all, so no probability applies.
hint: Independent events do not remember earlier results.
solution: If spins are independent, the earlier results do not change the probabilities of the next one. Believing that black is due is the gambler's fallacy; believing that red is favored by the streak is the opposite mistake.
