@article
A film of an egg breaking is easy to tell from the same film run backward. A film of two billiard balls colliding is not. Yet the laws governing the molecules of the egg are, in Newtonian mechanics, just as symmetric as the laws governing the billiard balls. Where does the difference between past and future come from? The standard answer is **entropy**, and the interesting question is what, exactly, makes entropy rise.

### Boltzmann's statistical entropy

A **microstate** specifies the exact state of every molecule (positions and velocities). A **macrostate** specifies only coarse-grained quantities (volume, temperature, pressure, or which half of a box each molecule is in). Many microstates realize the same macrostate. The **Boltzmann entropy** of a macrostate with $W$ compatible microstates is
$$S = k_B \ln W, \qquad k_B = 1.380649\times 10^{-23}\ \text{J/K},$$
where $k_B$ has this exact value in SI units. The second law, which says that the entropy of an isolated system does not decrease, becomes a statement about probability: systems move toward macrostates with more microstates because there are overwhelmingly more of them.

A toy model shows the effect. Put $N$ distinguishable molecules in a box divided into a left and a right half; a macrostate is the number $n$ in the left half, and a microstate says which molecules are where, so $W(n) = \binom{N}{n}$ and the total number of microstates is $2^N$. For $N = 100$, the even split has $\binom{100}{50} \approx 1.01 \times 10^{29}$ microstates, and "all on the left" has just one. A real gas has about $10^{23}$ molecules, and the imbalance is overwhelming: decreases of entropy are possible but fantastically improbable.

### Two objections

**Loschmidt's reversibility objection** (1876) says that the dynamics cannot by itself make entropy increase, because it makes no distinction between the two directions of time:

| P1 | The fundamental dynamics of the system (Newtonian mechanics, or unitary quantum evolution) is time-reversal invariant: the time-reverse of any possible history is also a possible history. |
| P2 | If the dynamics is time-reversal invariant, then for every possible history in which an isolated system's entropy rises there is a possible history, the time-reverse, in which it falls. |
| C | So no dynamical law alone entails that the entropy of an isolated system never decreases. |

The argument is valid. A reply that denies P1 holds that the fundamental dynamics is irreversible. Some physicists propose this (certain collapse theories of quantum mechanics are time-asymmetric); weak interactions do show a small violation of time-reversal symmetry, but it is generally thought to play no role in entropy increase. The standard reply accepts the conclusion and replaces the dynamical law with a statistical one: entropy increase is not exceptionless but overwhelmingly typical. Boltzmann's early *H*-theorem derived increase from dynamics only by adding a premise ("molecular chaos") that is itself not time-symmetric. **Zermelo's recurrence objection** (1896) says that a finite isolated system will eventually return near its initial microstate (Poincaré recurrence); the reply is that the recurrence times are astronomically long.

### The Past Hypothesis

The statistical account has a further problem. If we reason from the present macrostate alone, assuming that every compatible microstate is equally likely, then entropy would be very likely higher in the future *and* higher in the past. But the past had lower entropy: ice cubes were frozen before they melted, and the evidence of records says so. This is the **retrodiction problem**, and it gives an argument:

| P1 | For a system in a non-equilibrium macrostate, nearly all compatible microstates lie on trajectories along which entropy is higher in the future and also higher in the past. |
| P2 | Entropy was in fact lower in the past. |
| C1 | So statistical reasoning from the present macrostate alone gives false retrodictions. |
| P3 | If statistical reasoning from the present macrostate and the dynamics alone gives false retrodictions, then something else must be added to explain why entropy was lower in the past. |
| C2 | So something else must be added: for example, that the universe began in a macrostate of very low entropy. |

C1 follows from P1 and P2; C2 from C1 and P3. The addition is the **Past Hypothesis** (associated especially with David Albert's *Time and Chance*, 2000): the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago in a macrostate of extremely low entropy. On the standard cosmological model, the early universe was very smooth, which counts as low entropy when gravity is taken into account. The Past Hypothesis is then what grounds the arrow: from a low-entropy past, entropy increase in the future is overwhelmingly typical, and the asymmetry of records, and plausibly of causation, follows.

Replies attack different premises. One denies P1: the assumption of equal likelihood on a uniform measure over microstates is itself contested, as is its status as a probability. One denies P3: some say that we should not do retrodiction by statistics at all and should simply use records. The main cost of C2 is that it leaves a brute fact: why was the entropy low? Some, such as Humean "best system" theorists, include it among the laws; others look to cosmology for an explanation; no explanation is accepted. And an entropic arrow does not by itself settle whether time *passes*; that is the question from Lesson 5.

@example
title: Counting a six-molecule gas
problem: Six distinguishable molecules are in a box with two halves. For each number $n$ in the left half, find the number of microstates $W(n) = \binom{6}{n}$ and the probability of the even split $n = 3$, assuming each microstate is equally likely.
step: Compute the binomial coefficients for $n = 0, 1, \dots, 6$: 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1.
step: Their sum is $2^6 = 64$, the total number of microstates.
step: The probability of $n = 3$ is $20/64 = 0.3125$, that is, 31.25%.
step: The probability of "all on one side" ($n = 0$ or $n = 6$) is $2/64 = 3.1\%$. With six molecules this is not negligible; with $10^{23}$ it is.
answer: $W(n) = 1, 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1$; the even split has probability 31.25%.

@example
title: Entropy differences
problem: Using $S = k_B \ln W$ with $k_B = 1.380649\times 10^{-23}$ J/K, find the entropy of the macrostate with three of the six molecules on the left, and the entropy change when the system moves from the macrostate with one molecule on the left to that one.
step: The macrostate with $n = 3$ has $W = 20$, so $S = k_B \ln 20 = 1.380649\times 10^{-23} \times 2.9957 = 4.14\times 10^{-23}$ J/K.
step: The macrostate with $n = 1$ has $W = 6$, so the change is $\Delta S = k_B \ln(20/6) = 1.380649\times 10^{-23} \times 1.2040 = 1.66\times 10^{-23}$ J/K.
step: The change is positive: the system has moved to a macrostate with more microstates.
answer: $S = 4.14\times 10^{-23}$ J/K for $W = 20$, and $\Delta S \approx 1.66\times 10^{-23}$ J/K.

@mistakes
- Saying that the second law is a law of dynamics that forbids entropy decrease. In Boltzmann's account decreases are possible but overwhelmingly improbable for macroscopic systems.
- Thinking that statistical reasoning explains the past/future asymmetry on its own. It gives the same answer toward the past unless a low-entropy past boundary condition is added.
- Confusing the entropy of a microstate with that of a macrostate. Entropy belongs to a macrostate, relative to a choice of coarse-graining.
- Treating the Past Hypothesis as a derived result. It is an added assumption, and its status is debated.

@practice num
prompt: Fifty distinguishable molecules in a box with two halves go from the macrostate "all 50 on the left" to the macrostate "25 on each side". Use $S = k_B \ln W$ with $k_B = 1.380649\times 10^{-23}$ J/K. Find the entropy increase $\Delta S$ in units of $10^{-22}$ J/K, to two decimal places.
answer: 4.48
unit: 10^-22 J/K
tol: 0.01
check: 1.380649e-23 * log(comb(50, 25) / comb(50, 50)) / 1e-22
hint: Compute $W$ for each macrostate and use $\Delta S = k_B \ln(W_2/W_1)$.
solution: $W_1 = \binom{50}{50} = 1$ and $W_2 = \binom{50}{25} \approx 1.264\times 10^{14}$, so $\ln(W_2/W_1) = 32.47$ and $\Delta S = 1.380649\times 10^{-23} \times 32.47 = 4.48\times 10^{-22}$ J/K.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in a paragraph why the statistical account of the second law needs the Past Hypothesis in order to explain the arrow of time.
hint: Ask what the statistical reasoning says about the past, given only the present macrostate.
solution: The statistical account says that, among the microstates compatible with the present non-equilibrium macrostate, nearly all evolve to higher entropy in the future. But because the dynamics is time-symmetric, nearly all of these microstates also came from higher entropy in the past. Applied to the past, the reasoning gives the wrong answer, since we know that entropy was lower: ice was frozen before it melted. So statistics from the present macrostate cannot explain the asymmetry; something must break the symmetry, and the best candidate is a boundary condition on the past: the Past Hypothesis, that the early universe had very low entropy. With it, entropy increase toward the future is typical and entropy decrease toward the past is not. The cost is that the Past Hypothesis is an extra assumption with no accepted explanation.

@quiz self
prompt: Someone argues: "We remember the past and not the future because entropy increases, so the entropy arrow explains the memory arrow." Evaluate this argument briefly, saying what additional assumption it needs.
hint: Ask what makes a record reliable and where its low-entropy origin comes from.
solution: A record, such as a footprint or a memory trace, is a correlation that is reliable only if the past state that produced it had low entropy; the processes that create records increase entropy. Statistical reasoning from the present alone would not make records reliable, because it would suggest that the present records are fluctuations. So the argument needs the Past Hypothesis, the assumption that entropy was very low in the past. With it, the argument is plausible: the direction of entropy increase fixes the direction in which records form. Some philosophers question whether the entropic arrow really grounds the causal or epistemic one or only correlates with it.

rubric: Gives a balanced assessment, noting that some dispute the grounding claim.
