@article
Two lightning bolts strike the ends of a long train. A woman standing on the platform, halfway between the strike points, sees the two flashes arrive at the same moment, and concludes that the strikes were simultaneous. A man sitting in the middle of the train is moving toward the front flash and away from the back flash, so he meets the front flash first. Light travels at the same speed in both directions for him as well, so he concludes that the front strike came first. Who is right? In special relativity, both are: simultaneity of distant events is relative to a frame. This lesson explains why, and what it does to the idea of a single "now" for the universe.

### Conventions and the postulates

We use **inertial frames**: frames in which free bodies move at constant velocity. Distances are in meters and times in seconds in SI units, or we set $c = 1$ and measure time in years and distance in light-years (the distance light travels in one year), so that light has speed 1 ly/yr. The speed of light in vacuum is exactly $c = 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s, by the definition of the meter. Einstein's two postulates (1905):

- **Relativity principle.** The laws of physics have the same form in every inertial frame.
- **Light postulate.** Light in vacuum travels at speed $c$ in every inertial frame, whatever the motion of its source.

Special relativity is extremely well confirmed experimentally, and GPS satellites must correct for its effects.

### The Lorentz transformation

Let frame $S'$ move at velocity $v$ in the $+x$ direction relative to $S$, with origins coinciding at $t = t' = 0$. Define $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2}$ (with $c = 1$). Then
$$t' = \gamma\,(t - v x), \qquad x' = \gamma\,(x - v t).$$
(In SI units, $t' = \gamma(t - vx/c^2)$ and $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$.) For two events with $\Delta t = 0$ and $\Delta x \neq 0$, we get $\Delta t' = -\gamma v \Delta x \neq 0$: events simultaneous in $S$ are not simultaneous in $S'$, and the one farther in the direction of motion occurs earlier in $S'$. At $v \ll c$, $\gamma \approx 1$ and $v\Delta x/c^2$ is tiny, which is why we never notice.

### Spacetime diagrams and the interval

A **spacetime diagram** plots events by position and time. Since the site shows no images, describe one with coordinates: an event is a pair $(t, x)$, and light from the origin follows the lines $x = \pm t$. The **interval** between two events is
$$s^2 = \Delta t^2 - \Delta x^2 \quad (c = 1),$$
and every inertial frame gives it the same value (check it with the Lorentz transformation). Events are:

| Interval | Name | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| $s^2 > 0$ | timelike | A slower-than-light signal could connect them; $s$ is the proper time between them |
| $s^2 = 0$ | lightlike | Only light could connect them |
| $s^2 < 0$ | spacelike | No signal could connect them; the order of the events depends on the frame |

Causally connectible events (timelike or lightlike) have the same time order in all frames, so the relativity of simultaneity never reverses a cause and its effect. The set of events lightlike separated from an event $O$ is the **light cone** of $O$; the events spacelike separated from $O$ are its "elsewhere".

### An argument about simultaneity

Does physics contain an objective relation of simultaneity between distant events? One argument says no:

| P1 | Inertial frames in relative motion disagree about the simultaneity of some pairs of distant events, so no relation of distant simultaneity agrees with the simultaneity of every inertial frame. |
| P2 | If the laws of physics recognized an objective relation of distant simultaneity, then it would agree with the simultaneity of every inertial frame, or the laws would single out one inertial frame whose simultaneity it matches. |
| P3 | The laws of physics single out no inertial frame. |
| C | So the laws of physics recognize no objective relation of distant simultaneity. |

The argument is valid. P1 follows from the postulates by the Lorentz transformation; P3 is the relativity principle. Replies: a **conventionalist** (following Reichenbach and Grünbaum) says that only the round-trip speed of light can be measured, so which distant events count as simultaneous is partly a matter of convention; if so, distant simultaneity is not an objective relation at all, and the conclusion records a choice of convention rather than a discovery about the world. Against the conventionalist, Malament proved that, under assumptions that critics have questioned, the standard simultaneity is the only one definable from causal structure and an inertial observer's path. A **neo-Lorentzian** denies P3 or its relevance: there may be a privileged frame that the laws do not reveal, for instance the frame in which the cosmic microwave background is isotropic, a feature of our universe's matter and not of its laws. Lesson 5 puts this argument to work against presentism.

@example
title: Simultaneous in one frame, not in another
problem: In frame $S$, event $A$ is at $(t, x) = (0, 0)$ and event $B$ is at $(0, 4)$ (years and light-years, $c = 1$). Frame $S'$ moves at $v = 0.6$ in the $+x$ direction. Find the coordinates of both events in $S'$.
step: Compute $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.36} = 1/0.8 = 1.25$.
step: For $A$: $t' = 1.25 \times (0 - 0) = 0$ and $x' = 0$.
step: For $B$: $t' = 1.25 \times (0 - 0.6 \times 4) = -3$ years and $x' = 1.25 \times (4 - 0) = 5$ light-years.
step: In $S'$, $B$ happens 3 years before $A$. The separation in $S$ is spacelike ($s^2 = -16$) and in $S'$ it is $s^2 = 3^2 - 5^2 = -16$ as well: the interval is invariant.
answer: In $S'$, $A$ is at $(0, 0)$ and $B$ is at $(-3, 5)$, so $B$ occurs 3 years before $A$.

@example
title: Proper time from the interval
problem: In frame $S$, event $O$ is at $(0, 0)$ and event $P$ is at $(5, 3)$ (years and light-years, $c = 1$). Classify the separation, and find the time between the events in the frame $S'$ moving at $v = 0.6$.
step: The interval is $s^2 = 5^2 - 3^2 = 16 > 0$: timelike, with proper time $s = 4$ years.
step: In $S'$ with $\gamma = 1.25$: $t' = 1.25 \times (5 - 0.6 \times 3) = 1.25 \times 3.2 = 4$ years and $x' = 1.25 \times (3 - 0.6 \times 5) = 0$.
step: In $S'$ the two events occur at the same place, and the time between them is 4 years: the proper time, which is the interval and the same in every frame that agrees on the events.
answer: The separation is timelike, with proper time 4 years; in $S'$ the events happen at the same place, 4 years apart.

@mistakes
- Saying that relativity of simultaneity is about when light reaches the observers. It concerns when the events happen after correcting for light travel times; the platform and train observers agree on what they see and disagree on what happened simultaneously.
- Concluding that everything is relative. The interval, the speed of light and the order of causally connected events are the same in all inertial frames.
- Thinking that cause and effect can be reversed. Only spacelike separated events change order between frames.
- Mixing units: with $c = 1$ time is in years and distance in light-years; with SI units keep the factor $c^2$ in $vx/c^2$.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in four or five sentences, why the relativity of simultaneity does not allow an effect to occur before its cause in some frame.
hint: Think about which pairs of events can be connected by a signal, and what the interval says about them.
solution: Two events can be connected by a signal only if the interval between them is timelike or lightlike, that is, if $\Delta t^2 \ge \Delta x^2$ ($c = 1$). The Lorentz transformation preserves the interval, and for such pairs it also preserves the sign of $\Delta t$ for every $v$ with $|v| < 1$, because $\Delta t' = \gamma(\Delta t - v \Delta x)$ and $|v \Delta x| < |\Delta x| \le |\Delta t|$ whenever $|v| < 1$, so $\Delta t'$ has the same sign as $\Delta t$. Only spacelike separated events, which no signal can connect, change their order. So any pair that could be related as cause and effect has the same order in every inertial frame.
rubric: Says that only causally connectible (timelike or lightlike) pairs could be cause and effect.
rubric: Explains that the order of such pairs is the same in all frames, while only spacelike pairs change order.
rubric: Relates the claim to the interval or to the inequality $|v \Delta x| < |\Delta t|$.
