@article
Lesson 9 ended with a challenge. EPR's conclusion was that quantum mechanics is incomplete and that a local, deterministic theory with extra variables would fill the gap. In 1964 John Bell showed that this can be tested. The test is a number, and quantum mechanics says that nature will not stay below the bound that such a theory implies.

### The setting and the model

Alice and Bob each receive one particle of a pair from a source. Alice chooses one of two measurement settings, $a$ or $a'$; Bob chooses $b$ or $b'$. Each measurement gives $+1$ or $-1$. Let $E(a,b)$ be the average of the product of the two outcomes when the settings are $a$ and $b$. The **CHSH quantity** (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969) is
$$S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b').$$
A **local hidden-variable model** says that each pair carries a hidden variable $\lambda$ (with some probability distribution) fixed at the source, and that the outcomes are $A(\text{setting},\lambda) = \pm 1$ for Alice and $B(\text{setting},\lambda) = \pm 1$ for Bob. Two assumptions are built in. **Locality**: Alice's outcome depends only on her setting and $\lambda$, not on Bob's setting, and vice versa. **Measurement independence**: the distribution of $\lambda$ does not depend on the settings chosen. (Bell's later formulation allows stochastic outcomes, with joint probabilities that factorize given $\lambda$; the bound is the same.)

### Deriving the bound

For a fixed $\lambda$ write $A = A(a,\lambda)$, $A' = A(a',\lambda)$, $B = B(b,\lambda)$, $B' = B(b',\lambda)$, each $\pm 1$. Then
$$AB - AB' + A'B + A'B' = A(B - B') + A'(B + B').$$
Since $B$ and $B'$ are each $\pm 1$, either $B - B' = 0$ and $B + B' = \pm 2$, or $B - B' = \pm 2$ and $B + B' = 0$. In both cases exactly one bracket is nonzero, and the whole expression is $\pm 2$. Averaging over $\lambda$ (using measurement independence, so that the same distribution applies to all four terms), $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$. Note the number of deterministic assignments: there are $2^4 = 16$ ways to fix $A, A', B, B'$, and each gives $\pm 2$.

### The quantum value

For the singlet state with measurement axes in a plane, $E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = -\cos\theta_{ab}$, where $\theta_{ab}$ is the angle between them. Take the axes at angles $0^\circ$ for $a$, $90^\circ$ for $a'$, $45^\circ$ for $b$ and $135^\circ$ for $b'$:

| Pair | Angle between axes | $E$ |
|---|---|---|
| $(a, b)$ | $45^\circ$ | $-\cos 45^\circ = -0.7071$ |
| $(a, b')$ | $135^\circ$ | $-\cos 135^\circ = +0.7071$ |
| $(a', b)$ | $45^\circ$ | $-0.7071$ |
| $(a', b')$ | $45^\circ$ | $-0.7071$ |

So $S = -0.7071 - 0.7071 - 0.7071 - 0.7071 = -2\sqrt{2} \approx -2.83$, and $\lvert S\rvert = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83 > 2$. **Tsirelson's bound** says that no quantum state and measurements give more than $2\sqrt{2}$. Theories that merely forbid signaling would allow up to 4. Experiments confirm violations: tests in 2015 closed the main loopholes (in detection efficiency and in the separation of the measurements) together in a single experiment, and the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics recognized experiments with entangled photons that established violations of Bell inequalities.

### Bell's argument in standard form

| P1 | If the measured correlations are produced by a local hidden-variable model satisfying measurement independence, then $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$. |
| P2 | In experiments on entangled pairs, $\lvert S\rvert > 2$. |
| C | So the measured correlations are not produced by a local hidden-variable model satisfying measurement independence. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens); P1 is a theorem, and P2 is an experimental result. The conclusion is a denial of a conjunction, so the interesting question is which conjunct to give up, along with the assumption that each run has a single definite outcome that the model builds in:

- **Give up locality.** Pilot-wave theory and collapse theories are nonlocal, in different ways (Lesson 11). The cost: tension with relativity, mitigated by no-signaling.
- **Give up measurement independence.** *Superdeterminism* says that the hidden variable is correlated with the settings, through common causes in the past; *retrocausal* models let later settings influence earlier hidden states. The cost: apparent conspiracy, or a rethinking of causation.
- **Give up single outcomes.** On the many-worlds interpretation, there is no single definite outcome for each run, and its defenders argue that the dynamics is local (whether it is local in every sense that matters is debated). The cost: the interpretation of probability in a branching universe.

These are costs, not refutations; informed physicists and philosophers disagree on which to pay. What the theorem does not do: it does not say that signals travel faster than light (no-signaling holds), and it does not say that quantum mechanics is the final theory.

@example
title: One deterministic strategy
problem: Suppose a hidden variable fixes $A = +1$, $A' = -1$, $B = +1$, $B' = -1$ for one pair. Compute the value of $AB - AB' + A'B + A'B'$ and check it against the factored form.
step: Direct: $AB = (+1)(+1) = 1$, $AB' = (+1)(-1) = -1$, $A'B = (-1)(+1) = -1$, $A'B' = (-1)(-1) = 1$.
step: The sum is $1 - (-1) + (-1) + 1 = 2$.
step: Factored form: $A(B - B') + A'(B + B') = (1)(1 - (-1)) + (-1)(1 + (-1)) = 2 + 0 = 2$.
step: Since $B \ne B'$ here, $B + B' = 0$ and the first bracket is $\pm 2$; if $B = B'$ the second would be. Either way, one term is $\pm 2$ and the other 0.
answer: The value is $2$, in agreement with the factored form.

@example
title: A quantum computation with other settings
problem: For the singlet state, with $E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}) = -\cos\theta_{ab}$, take the settings $a = 0^\circ$, $a' = 90^\circ$, $b = 135^\circ$ and $b' = 45^\circ$ (axes in one plane). Find $S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b')$.
step: $E(a,b) = -\cos 135^\circ = +0.7071$.
step: $E(a,b') = -\cos 45^\circ = -0.7071$, so $-E(a,b') = +0.7071$.
step: $E(a',b) = -\cos 45^\circ = -0.7071$ (angle between $90^\circ$ and $135^\circ$), and $E(a',b') = -\cos 45^\circ = -0.7071$ (angle between $90^\circ$ and $45^\circ$).
step: $S = 0.7071 + 0.7071 - 0.7071 - 0.7071 = 0$.
answer: $S = 0$ for these settings: the settings matter, and only suitably chosen settings give $\lvert S\rvert > 2$.

@mistakes
- Saying that Bell's theorem proves that nature is nonlocal. It proves that locality, measurement independence and the other assumptions of the model cannot all hold with the quantum correlations.
- Thinking that a violation of the CHSH bound allows faster-than-light signaling. Quantum correlations obey no-signaling.
- Confusing $E$ with $S$: $E$ is one correlation, and $S$ is the sum of four with one sign flipped.
- Thinking that $2\sqrt{2}$ is the largest value allowed by any theory. It is the largest quantum value; the algebraic maximum is 4.

@practice self
prompt: Show that for fixed values $A, A', B, B' \in \{+1, -1\}$, the quantity $AB - AB' + A'B + A'B'$ equals $+2$ or $-2$, and explain how this yields $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$ for local hidden-variable models.
hint: Factor the expression as $A(B - B') + A'(B + B')$ and consider the cases $B = B'$ and $B \ne B'$.
solution: Factor: $AB - AB' + A'B + A'B' = A(B - B') + A'(B + B')$. If $B = B'$, then $B - B' = 0$ and $B + B' = \pm 2$, so the expression is $A' \times (\pm 2) = \pm 2$. If $B \ne B'$, then $B + B' = 0$ and $B - B' = \pm 2$, so the expression is $A \times (\pm 2) = \pm 2$. So for every $\lambda$ the expression is $\pm 2$. In a local model with measurement independence, $S$ is the average of this expression over the same distribution of $\lambda$, and an average of numbers each in $\{-2, +2\}$ lies between $-2$ and $2$. Hence $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$.

rubric: Concludes that the expression is $\pm 2$ for each value of the hidden variable.

@quiz self
prompt: Bell's argument runs: P1, if the correlations are produced by a local hidden-variable model satisfying measurement independence, then $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$; P2, in experiments $\lvert S\rvert > 2$; C, the correlations are not produced by such a model. A defender of the many-worlds interpretation says that Bell's theorem does not show nonlocality. Say which assumption of the model the defender denies and assess the reply.
hint: The model assumes that each run of the experiment has one definite outcome at each wing.
solution: The model assumes that each pair has definite single outcomes $A$ and $B$ fixed by a local rule. The many-worlds defender denies this: on that interpretation both outcomes occur in different branches, the dynamics is (its defenders argue) local, and the correlations arise only when records are compared inside a branch. This respects locality and measurement independence, so the theorem does not force a nonlocal action. The cost is the need to make sense of probabilities and of the observed single-outcome statistics in a branching universe, and critics say that the reply changes the question by dropping the assumption that experiments have outcomes the model must reproduce.
