@article
Suppose that overnight everything in the universe moved three kilometers east: every star, every atom, you. Nothing would be different in any distance, any angle, any experience you could have. Was there a change at all? If you say yes, you think there is something for the bodies to move *relative to* besides one another: space itself. If you say no, you think that space is nothing over and above the spatial relations among bodies. This is the oldest question in the philosophy of physics, and it set the agenda for the study of motion that you will follow through relativity.

### Two theories of space

**Substantivalism** (absolute space) holds that space is an entity in its own right, a "container" with points or regions that exist whether or not any body occupies them. Newton states this view in the Scholium to the Definitions of the *Principia* (1687): "Absolute space, in its own nature, without relation to any thing external, remains always similar and immovable" (in Motte's translation of 1729). On this view a body's **absolute place** is the part of absolute space it occupies, and a body moves truly when it changes absolute place. Newton contrasts this with **relative place**, fixed by the position of a body with respect to other bodies.

**Relationism** holds that there are bodies and spatial relations among them (distances, angles, betweenness), and nothing more. Leibniz, in his correspondence with Samuel Clarke (who defended Newton's view), called space an order of coexisting things: a system of relations, not a thing. For the relationist, "the distance between the Earth and the Moon" is a fact about two bodies, and "where the whole universe is" is not a fact at all.

Careful: the two theories are not about whether distances exist. Both accept that two bodies are 384,000 km apart. They disagree about whether there is more to space than such facts.

### Leibniz's shift argument

Let $W$ be the actual world and $W^*$ a world exactly like it except that every body is located 3 km east of its location in $W$. The substantivalist seems committed to saying that these are different possibilities: in $W^*$ the bodies occupy different points. Leibniz argues that this is absurd. Set out as an argument (the **identity of indiscernibles** is the principle that no two distinct things are alike in every respect; here it is applied to worlds):

| P1 | If substantivalism is true, then $W$ and $W^*$ are two distinct possible worlds. |
| P2 | $W$ and $W^*$ agree in every qualitative property of bodies and every relation among bodies (the same bodies, the same distances, the same angles, the same causal histories). |
| P3 | Possible worlds that agree in every qualitative property of bodies and every relation among bodies are not distinct. |
| C | So substantivalism is false. |

The argument is valid: P2 and P3 give that $W$ and $W^*$ are not distinct, which contradicts the consequent of P1, so by modus tollens the antecedent of P1 fails. Leibniz's own version appeals instead to the **principle of sufficient reason** (there is a sufficient reason for everything): God could have had no reason to place the universe here rather than 3 km east. Clarke replied that the mere will of God is a sufficient reason.

### Where the replies attack

- **Deny P3.** The substantivalist can say that worlds can differ merely in which points of absolute space the bodies occupy, a difference in the relation of bodies to points of space rather than in any qualitative property of bodies or relation among them. A general objection to P3 says that the identity of indiscernibles itself is doubtful: a famous thought experiment imagines a universe containing nothing but two qualitatively identical iron spheres at a fixed distance from each other, which seems possible and contains two things alike in every respect.
- **Deny P1.** A *sophisticated substantivalist* accepts that space exists but denies that points have an identity beyond their structural roles. Then $W^*$ is just $W$ described a second time, there is only one possibility, and the argument's conclusion does not follow.
- **Deny P2.** Someone who claims that absolute position or velocity makes a detectable difference to bodies denies that the worlds are alike. Newton's Scholium argues that absolute motion shows itself through its dynamical effects, the topic of Lesson 2, though a uniform shift of every body's position has no such effects.

The shift argument is not decisive, but it shows what is at stake: whether physical theories are committed to quantities, such as absolute position, that make no observable difference.

@example
title: Reconstructing and locating a reply
problem: A critic says: "Black's two-spheres universe shows that indiscernible things can be distinct, so the shift argument fails." Which premise of the shift argument is this criticism aimed at, and does it show that substantivalism is true?
step: Recall the premises. P1: if substantivalism, then $W$ and $W^*$ are distinct. P2: they agree in all qualitative properties of and relations among bodies. P3: worlds that agree in all those respects are not distinct.
step: The two-spheres case is offered as a possible situation with two distinct things that agree in every property and relation. That is a counterexample to the general principle that indiscernibles are identical, and P3 applies that principle to worlds. So the criticism targets P3.
step: Rejecting a premise of a valid argument only removes the support for its conclusion: it shows that this argument does not establish that substantivalism is false. It does not give a reason to think that substantivalism is true.
answer: The criticism attacks premise P3. It undermines the argument against substantivalism without by itself supporting substantivalism.

@example
title: What a shift leaves unchanged
problem: Three bodies lie in a plane (coordinates in meters): $A = (0, 0)$, $B = (6, 8)$ and $C = (12, 0)$. World $W^*$ shifts every body by $(5, -3)$ m, and world $W^{**}$ rotates every body by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin. Compare the three pairwise distances in the three worlds.
step: In $W$: $AB = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$ m, $BC = \sqrt{6^2 + 8^2} = 10$ m, $AC = 12$ m.
step: In $W^*$ the bodies are at $(5, -3)$, $(11, 5)$ and $(17, -3)$. Each difference of coordinates is unchanged, so the distances are again 10 m, 10 m and 12 m.
step: In $W^{**}$ the map $(x, y) \to (-y, x)$ gives $(0, 0)$, $(-8, 6)$ and $(0, 12)$. Then $AB = 10$ m, $BC = \sqrt{8^2 + 6^2} = 10$ m and $AC = 12$ m.
step: All three worlds have the same distances among bodies, though the absolute coordinates differ. The relationist says they are one world; the substantivalist who accepts P1 says they are three.
answer: The distances are 10 m, 10 m and 12 m in every case; only the absolute coordinates differ.

@mistakes
- Saying that the relationist denies that distances exist. Relationists accept spatial relations; they deny that space is an entity over and above them.
- Treating Leibniz's argument as showing that absolute space is undetectable. The conclusion is that substantivalism is false, and it depends on the identity of indiscernibles or the principle of sufficient reason.
- Attributing the shift argument's premises to Newton. Newton defends absolute space; the argument comes from Leibniz's side of the correspondence, and Clarke speaks for Newton.
- Thinking that rejecting one premise proves the opposite view. A reply to P3 blocks the argument but does not establish substantivalism.

@practice self
prompt: In one paragraph, explain how the principle of sufficient reason is used in Leibniz's shift argument, and state how Clarke replied.
hint: Ask what reason there could be for the universe being placed here rather than elsewhere.
solution: Leibniz holds that everything has a sufficient reason. If space were an absolute container, God could have created the universe 3 km east of where it is, with every distance unchanged. Nothing could then give God a reason to choose one location over the other, so a choice between them would have no sufficient reason. Since there is a sufficient reason for everything, there is no such choice, and the supposed difference between the two locations is not real: space is nothing but relations among bodies. Clarke replied that the mere will of God is a sufficient reason, so the principle does not rule out a free choice between indiscernible alternatives.
rubric: States the principle of sufficient reason correctly and applies it to a choice between two locations with identical relations.
rubric: Explains that the lack of any reason for choosing is what makes the supposed difference illusory for Leibniz.
rubric: States Clarke's reply that the divine will itself suffices as a reason.

hint: P3 applies the identity of indiscernibles to worlds that agree in all qualitative properties of and relations among bodies.
solution: P3 says that two possible worlds that agree in every qualitative property of bodies and every relation among bodies are really one world.
