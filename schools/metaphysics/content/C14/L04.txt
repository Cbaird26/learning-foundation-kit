@article
Special relativity says that a clock moving relative to you runs slow. But motion is relative, so the moving clock should say the same about yours. Suppose that one of two twins travels to a distant star and back while the other stays home. Each can say that the other's clock runs slow. When they meet again, who is younger? The question looks like a contradiction. It is not, and seeing why is the best way to learn what time dilation is.

### The light clock and time dilation

Take a clock made of two mirrors a distance $L$ apart, with a pulse of light bouncing between them. In its own rest frame it ticks once every $\Delta\tau = 2L/c$ (SI units). This $\Delta\tau$ is the **proper time**: the time between two events measured by a clock present at both. Now let the clock move at speed $v$ perpendicular to the line between the mirrors, as seen from frame $S$. During one tick, the light travels a diagonal path, and by the light postulate its speed is still $c$. Each half-tick takes $\Delta t/2$, and
$$\left(\frac{c\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 = L^2 + \left(\frac{v\,\Delta t}{2}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta t = \frac{2L/c}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}} = \gamma\,\Delta\tau.$$
So the moving clock's tick is longer by the **Lorentz factor** $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}$: **time dilation**. Likewise, a rod of proper length $L_0$ (its length in its own rest frame) moving along its length at speed $v$ is measured to have length $L = L_0/\gamma$: **length contraction**. Both are measured effects: unstable particles called muons, made by cosmic rays at the top of the atmosphere, reach the ground in numbers that fit time dilation and not their rest-frame lifetime (about 2.197 microseconds); atomic clocks flown on aircraft and clocks on GPS satellites, whose orbital speed alone makes them lose about 7 microseconds a day (an effect more than offset by a larger gravitational effect of the opposite sign, which general relativity describes, so that the net is a gain of roughly 38 microseconds a day that GPS corrects for), agree with the theory.

### The twin scenario

A traveling twin leaves Earth, flies at $v = 0.8c$ to a star 4 light-years away (distance in the Earth frame), turns around at once and returns at the same speed. We use $c = 1$, with years and light-years. In the Earth frame each leg takes $4/0.8 = 5$ years, so the Earth twin ages 10 years. The traveler's clock runs at the rate $1/\gamma = \sqrt{1 - 0.64} = 0.6$ of Earth's, so the traveler ages $0.6 \times 10 = 6$ years. The traveler comes home younger.

### Why it is not symmetric

During each inertial leg, each twin does find the other's clock slow. But the situation is not symmetric overall. The Earth twin stays in a single inertial frame. The traveler changes from an outbound inertial frame to an inbound one at the turnaround, and feels the acceleration. There is also a geometrical way to put it: the proper time elapsed along a worldline is the "length" of the worldline in spacetime, measured with the interval, and among all timelike paths between two events the straight (inertial) one has the *longest* proper time. The detour through the star is a shorter path in this sense.

From the traveler's side, the distance to the star is contracted to $4 \times 0.6 = 2.4$ light-years, so each leg takes $2.4/0.8 = 3$ years: 6 years in all, agreeing with the Earth account. In the outbound frame Earth's clock advances at rate 0.6, only $0.6 \times 3 = 1.8$ years on the outbound leg, and by symmetry 1.8 years on the return: 3.6 in all. The remaining $10 - 3.6 = 6.4$ years comes from the traveler's change of frame at the turnaround, which changes which events on Earth count as simultaneous with the traveler's turning point.

### The symmetry argument

| P1 | If special relativity's time dilation is correct, then each twin finds the other's clock running slow throughout the journey. |
| P2 | If each twin finds the other's clock running slow throughout the journey, then at reunion each twin is younger than the other. |
| P3 | It is impossible that at reunion each twin is younger than the other. |
| C | So special relativity's time dilation is not correct. |

The argument is valid. The standard reply denies P1: it is not true that time dilation makes each twin find the other's clock slow throughout the journey. Time dilation says that a clock moving inertially runs slow *in the inertial frame of the observer*; it holds only while each twin is inertial, and only the Earth twin is inertial for the whole trip. (A reader who takes P1 to mean only that each twin finds the other slow during each inertial leg should instead deny P2.) Comparing ages at reunion is a comparison at a single place, so it is frame-independent, and it is settled by the proper times of the two worldlines, not by either twin's "finds the other's clock slow". Some of the interest of the case for philosophers lies in the clock hypothesis, that acceleration as such does not change the rate of an ideal clock, which is an assumption supported by experiment rather than a consequence of the postulates.

@example
title: Cosmic-ray muons
problem: A muon has proper mean lifetime $2.197\ \mu$s. Cosmic-ray muons are created high in the atmosphere and move at $0.995c$ relative to the ground ($c = 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s). Find the mean lifetime in the ground frame and the mean distance traveled before decay, with and without time dilation.
step: $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.995^2} = 10.01$.
step: Ground-frame mean lifetime: $\gamma \times 2.197 = 22.0\ \mu$s.
step: Distance with dilation: $0.995 \times 299{,}792{,}458 \times 22.0 \times 10^{-6} \approx 6.56 \times 10^3$ m, about 6.6 km.
step: Without dilation the distance would be $0.995 \times 299{,}792{,}458 \times 2.197 \times 10^{-6} \approx 655$ m. Muons that reach the ground from several kilometers up are explained by dilation.
answer: In the ground frame the mean lifetime is about 22.0 microseconds and the mean distance is about 6.56 km, ten times the 655 m expected without dilation.

@example
title: The twins' ages from the Earth frame
problem: A traveling twin flies out at $v = 0.8$ (units with $c = 1$) to a star 4 light-years from Earth in the Earth's frame and returns at the same speed with no time spent at the star. How much does each twin age?
step: Time on each leg in the Earth frame: $4/0.8 = 5$ years, so the Earth twin ages $2 \times 5 = 10$ years.
step: The Lorentz factor is $\gamma = 1/\sqrt{1 - 0.8^2} = 1/0.6 = 1.667$.
step: The traveler's proper time on each leg is $5/\gamma = 3$ years, so the traveler ages 6 years.
step: Check by the interval: each leg has $\Delta t = 5$ and $\Delta x = 4$, so $\sqrt{25 - 16} = 3$ years per leg.
answer: The Earth twin ages 10 years and the traveler ages 6 years.

@mistakes
- Saying that the paradox arises because "motion is relative". Time dilation is a relation between a clock and the inertial frame in which it moves; the twins differ in whether they stay inertial.
- Attributing the asymmetry only to acceleration as if acceleration "causes" the missing time. The proper time is determined by the shape of the worldline; the acceleration marks the change of frame, and the age difference could be made as large as you like for the same acceleration.
- Using the dilation formula in the wrong direction: the proper time is the shorter one, $\Delta\tau = \Delta t/\gamma$.
- Applying length contraction to lengths perpendicular to the motion: only lengths along the direction of motion contract.

@quiz self
prompt: A traveling twin flies from Earth to a star 6 light-years away (Earth frame) at $v = 0.6$ ($c = 1$) and returns at the same speed. Explain, from the traveler's own frame, how the traveler accounts for Earth's clock showing 20 years at reunion, using numbers.
hint: In the traveler's frame the distance is contracted; Earth's clock runs slow during each leg, so something else must account for the rest.
solution: In the traveler's frame the distance is contracted to $6 \times 0.8 = 4.8$ light-years, so each leg takes $4.8/0.6 = 8$ years, and the traveler ages 16 years. During each leg Earth's clock runs at the rate 0.8, so it advances $0.8 \times 8 = 6.4$ years per leg, or 12.8 years over both legs. Earth's clock reads 20 years at reunion, so 7.2 years remain. They come from the turnaround: when the traveler changes from the outbound to the inbound frame, the set of events on Earth that count as simultaneous with the traveler's turning point jumps forward by 7.2 years. So the traveler's account agrees with Earth's, and the asymmetry shows up as the change of frame.
