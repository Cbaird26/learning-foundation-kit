@article
Two particles are prepared together and sent in opposite directions to Alice and Bob, who are far apart. Whenever Alice measures her particle's spin along some axis and gets "up", Bob, measuring along the same axis, gets "down", and vice versa. Quantum mechanics predicts this for every axis they might choose. Did the particles carry the answers from the source, like a pair of gloves sealed in boxes? Or did Alice's measurement bring Bob's answer into being? In 1935 Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen used this kind of case to argue that quantum mechanics is incomplete.

### Entanglement and the singlet

The state of two spin-1/2 particles, A and B, is **entangled** if it is not a product of a state of A and a state of B. The standard example is the **singlet state**
$$\lvert\Psi^-\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\lvert\uparrow\rangle_A\lvert\downarrow\rangle_B - \lvert\downarrow\rangle_A\lvert\uparrow\rangle_B\bigr).$$
Alice measures the spin of A along an axis $\mathbf{a}$ and Bob that of B along $\mathbf{b}$, with an angle $\theta$ between the axes. Each outcome is $\pm\hbar/2$, which we record as $+1$ or $-1$. Quantum mechanics predicts
$$P(+,+) = P(-,-) = \tfrac12\sin^2(\theta/2), \qquad P(+,-) = P(-,+) = \tfrac12\cos^2(\theta/2).$$
At $\theta = 0$ (the same axis) the results are always opposite. The **correlation** $E(\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b})$, the average of the product of the two outcomes, is $P(\text{same}) - P(\text{opposite}) = \sin^2(\theta/2) - \cos^2(\theta/2) = -\cos\theta$. Taken alone, each of Bob's outcomes is a fair coin toss: his probability of $+1$ is $\tfrac12$, whatever Alice does. So Bob cannot tell from his own results what axis Alice chose, or whether she measured at all: the correlations cannot be used to send a message. This is the **no-signaling** property. Quantum correlations are therefore compatible with the relativistic limit on signals, but they are still puzzling, because they hold between events that are spacelike separated: for stations 1.3 km apart, as in one of the 2015 Bell tests, light needs about 4.3 microseconds to cross, and the choices and recordings were completed in less time.

### The EPR argument

EPR (1935) used position and momentum; David Bohm later gave the spin version above. They defined three ideas:

- **Criterion of reality**: if, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty the value of a physical quantity, then there is an element of physical reality corresponding to that quantity.
- **Locality** (their "no action at a distance" assumption): if two systems are no longer interacting, nothing done to the first produces a real change in the second.
- **Completeness**: every element of physical reality has a counterpart in the theory.

In standard form, for the singlet:

| P1 | For any axis $\mathbf{n}$ that Alice might choose, a measurement of A's spin along $\mathbf{n}$ lets her predict with certainty the result of a measurement of B's spin along $\mathbf{n}$. |
| P2 | Whichever axis Alice chooses, her measurement does not disturb B (locality), since A and B are spacelike separated. |
| P3 | If the value of a quantity can be predicted with certainty without disturbing the system, then there is an element of reality corresponding to it (criterion of reality). |
| C1 | So B has an element of reality corresponding to its spin along every axis $\mathbf{n}$, before anything is done to B. |
| P4 | If quantum mechanics is complete, then every element of reality of B has a counterpart in a quantum state of B. |
| P5 | No quantum state of B has a counterpart for definite spin values along two different axes at once. |
| C2 | So quantum mechanics is not complete. |

C1 follows from P1, P2 and P3; C2 follows from C1, P4 and P5. The argument is valid. Replies:

- **Deny P3.** Bohr's reply (1935) accepted that Alice's measurement mechanically disturbs nothing in B, but held that the choice of her measurement arrangement affects the conditions that define what kinds of predictions can meaningfully be made about B, so the criterion's "without disturbing" cannot be used as EPR use it. A reply in this spirit says that a quantity has physical meaning only relative to a complete experimental arrangement.
- **Deny P2.** Alice's measurement does change B's state instantly, as the collapse postulate says. This keeps completeness and gives up locality in the sense of P2, although the no-signaling property means that special relativity's ban on signals is not violated.
- **Accept the conclusion.** Agree with EPR: quantum mechanics is incomplete, and hidden variables fix the outcomes in advance.

John Bell showed in 1964 that the last option, with locality, has testable consequences that differ from those of quantum mechanics. The next lesson derives them. The upshot is that EPR's own conclusion (a local, deterministic completion of quantum mechanics) cannot be maintained if the quantum predictions are correct, and they are.

@example
title: Correlations at a right angle
problem: Alice and Bob measure the spins of a singlet pair along axes at an angle $\theta = 90^\circ$. Find $P(\text{same})$, $P(\text{opposite})$ and the correlation $E$.
step: $P(+,+) = P(-,-) = \tfrac12\sin^2(45^\circ) = \tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$, so $P(\text{same}) = \tfrac14 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac12$.
step: $P(+,-) = P(-,+) = \tfrac12\cos^2(45^\circ) = \tfrac14$, so $P(\text{opposite}) = \tfrac12$.
step: $E = P(\text{same}) - P(\text{opposite}) = 0$, in agreement with $-\cos 90^\circ = 0$: the results are uncorrelated.
answer: $P(\text{same}) = P(\text{opposite}) = \tfrac12$ and $E = 0$.

@example
title: Spacelike or not?
problem: Alice's and Bob's stations are 1.3 km apart (SI units, $c = 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s). Alice's measurement is completed at $t_A = 0$ and Bob's at $t_B = 3.0$ microseconds. Could a light signal from one event reach the other?
step: Light needs $1300/299{,}792{,}458 = 4.34\times 10^{-6}$ s, that is, 4.34 microseconds, to cross the distance.
step: The time difference is $3.0 < 4.34$ microseconds, so no light signal leaving one event could arrive at the other event in time.
step: The interval (with $c = 1$) is negative, so the events are spacelike separated: in some inertial frames Alice's measurement comes first and in others Bob's.
answer: No: the events are spacelike separated, so no signal at or below the speed of light can connect them.

@mistakes
- Saying that entanglement lets Alice send information to Bob. Bob's outcomes alone are fair coin tosses; the correlation shows up only when the two records are compared.
- Treating EPR as showing that quantum mechanics is wrong. EPR argue that it is incomplete, and the predictions they discuss are correct.
- Confusing the criterion of reality with locality. The first says when a quantity corresponds to something real; the second says that distant operations do not change a system.
- Thinking that perfect correlation along one axis proves entanglement. A pair of sealed gloves shows perfect anticorrelation too; what a classical model cannot reproduce is the set of correlations at different angles (Lesson 10).

@practice self
prompt: Explain in three or four sentences why the correlations of the singlet state cannot be used to send a message from Alice to Bob.
hint: Consider what Bob's results look like if he does not know what Alice did.
solution: For the singlet, Bob's probability of $+1$ along any axis is $\tfrac12$, whatever axis Alice measured along and even if she did not measure at all, so his sequence of results is a random series of equal frequencies. The correlation shows up only when Alice's and Bob's records are compared, which requires an ordinary signal that travels at or below the speed of light. Her choice of axis therefore changes nothing that Bob can observe on his own. This is why quantum correlations do not conflict with the relativistic ban on signals, although they remain puzzling.

rubric: Concludes that no message can be sent, so there is no conflict with the signal limit.

@quiz mc
prompt: Alice and Bob are each sent one sealed box, one containing a left glove and one a right glove, chosen at random at the source. Whenever they open their boxes the gloves are opposite. What does this analogy show about the EPR case?
choice: It shows that entanglement is just ordinary classical correlation, and so there is nothing to explain.
choice: It shows that opening a box changes the glove in the other box.
choice*: Perfect correlation along one axis can be explained by values fixed at the source, as in the EPR reading; what such a model must also reproduce are the correlations at different axes, which Bell's theorem shows it cannot.

hint: The EPR argument itself reads the correlation as evidence of pre-existing values; ask what the model cannot do.
solution: Pre-assigned values explain perfect anticorrelation along a single axis, which is why EPR read it as showing that the values were fixed at the source. The singlet's correlations at other angles, $-\cos\theta$, cannot be reproduced by values fixed at the source and independent of the distant setting; this is Bell's theorem, the topic of the next lesson. The other choices wrongly identify the singlet with a mixture or claim that opening a box acts on the other glove.
