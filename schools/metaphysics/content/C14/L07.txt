@article
Send a beam of silver atoms through a magnet whose field varies in strength along the vertical direction. Classically, the atoms' little magnetic moments point in any direction, and we expect a smear on a detecting screen. Instead we see exactly two spots: this is the Stern-Gerlach experiment. For each atom, the measurement of the spin component along the magnet's axis gives one of two values, $+\hbar/2$ or $-\hbar/2$, where $\hbar = h/2\pi$ and $h = 6.62607015 \times 10^{-34}$ J s exactly. To understand what such a case says about reality, we need the mathematics of the theory.

### States and the Born rule

We work with the simplest quantum system: the spin of a spin-1/2 particle. Its **state** is a unit vector in a two-dimensional complex vector space. Use the **z-basis** $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ (spin $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ along $z$), and write a general state as
$$\lvert\psi\rangle = a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle, \qquad \lvert a\rvert^2 + \lvert b\rvert^2 = 1,$$
with complex amplitudes $a$ and $b$. This is a **superposition** of the two basis states. The condition on the sum is **normalization**. The **Born rule** says that if the spin along $z$ is measured, the probability of the outcome $+\hbar/2$ is $\lvert a\rvert^2$ and of $-\hbar/2$ is $\lvert b\rvert^2$. For another axis, the same rule applies with that axis's basis: the probability of the outcome associated with a basis vector $\lvert\phi\rangle$ is $\lvert\langle\phi\vert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$. The conventions for the other axes are
$$\lvert{+x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle),\quad \lvert{-x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle - \lvert\downarrow\rangle),$$
$$\lvert{+y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + i\lvert\downarrow\rangle),\quad \lvert{-y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle - i\lvert\downarrow\rangle).$$
For an axis $\mathbf{n}$ in the $x$-$z$ plane at angle $\theta$ from $+z$, $\lvert{+\mathbf{n}}\rangle = \cos(\theta/2)\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \sin(\theta/2)\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. So a particle in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ has probability $\cos^2(\theta/2)$ of the outcome $+\hbar/2$ along $\mathbf{n}$. Between measurements, an isolated system evolves by the **Schrödinger equation**, $i\hbar\,\partial_t\lvert\psi\rangle = H\lvert\psi\rangle$, which is linear and deterministic.

The overall phase of a state is irrelevant, but the **relative phase** between $a$ and $b$ matters: $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle - \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$ give the same probabilities along $z$ (each 1/2) but opposite outcomes along $x$.

### Superposition is not ignorance

Suppose a source prepares particles in $\lvert{+x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$. A measurement along $z$ gives up or down with probability 1/2 each. Might each particle already be in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$, and we just do not know which? Compare two sources: the first prepares $\lvert{+x}\rangle$; the second flips a fair coin and prepares $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ (a **mixture**). Along $z$ they agree. Along $x$ they differ:

| Source | Probability of $+x$ when spin along $x$ is measured |
|---|---|
| Superposition $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ | 1 |
| 50/50 mixture of $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ | $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac12$ |

The difference is observed. This is interference: the amplitudes for the two $z$-values add before they are squared. In argument form:

| P1 | If each particle prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ really were in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or in $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ (with us ignorant of which), then the probability of $+x$ in an $x$-measurement would be $\tfrac12$. |
| P2 | The probability of $+x$ in an $x$-measurement on particles prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ is 1. |
| C | So it is not the case that each particle prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ really is in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or in $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). P2 is a prediction of quantum mechanics confirmed in experiments of this kind. P1 uses the quantum prediction that either $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ gives $+x$ with probability $\tfrac12$. The conclusion is limited: it rules out one kind of ignorance, namely that the particle has a definite $z$-spin that we do not know. It does not rule out all hidden variables. A hidden-variable theorist replies that the hidden state need not be a value of $z$-spin, and that the outcomes of spin measurements may depend on the whole measuring arrangement. Whether quantum probabilities reflect ignorance of something is exactly what Lessons 9 to 11 take up.

@example
title: Normalizing and measuring
problem: A spin-1/2 particle is in the state $\lvert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \tfrac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. Check normalization and find the probabilities of $+\hbar/2$ along $z$ and of $+\hbar/2$ along $x$.
step: Normalization: $\lvert 1/\sqrt{5}\rvert^2 + \lvert 2/\sqrt{5}\rvert^2 = 1/5 + 4/5 = 1$.
step: Along $z$: $P(\uparrow) = 1/5 = 0.2$ and $P(\downarrow) = 4/5 = 0.8$.
step: Along $x$: the amplitude is $\langle{+x}\vert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1/\sqrt{5} + 2/\sqrt{5}) = 3/\sqrt{10}$, so $P(+x) = 9/10 = 0.9$.
step: Then $P(-x) = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1$; check: $\langle{-x}\vert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1 - 2)/\sqrt{5} = -1/\sqrt{10}$, with squared modulus 0.1.
answer: $P(\uparrow) = 0.2$ along $z$, and $P(+x) = 0.9$ along $x$ (so $P(-x) = 0.1$).

@example
title: An axis at an angle
problem: A spin-1/2 particle is in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$. A Stern-Gerlach magnet is aligned along an axis in the $x$-$z$ plane at $\theta = 60^\circ$ from $+z$. Find the probabilities of $+\hbar/2$ and $-\hbar/2$ along that axis.
step: The axis state is $\lvert{+\mathbf{n}}\rangle = \cos 30^\circ\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \sin 30^\circ\lvert\downarrow\rangle$.
step: $\langle{+\mathbf{n}}\vert\uparrow\rangle = \cos 30^\circ$, so $P(+\mathbf{n}) = \cos^2 30^\circ = 3/4$.
step: Therefore $P(-\mathbf{n}) = 1 - 3/4 = 1/4 = \sin^2 30^\circ$.
answer: $P(+\hbar/2) = 0.75$ and $P(-\hbar/2) = 0.25$ along the axis at $60^\circ$ from $z$.

@mistakes
- Treating the amplitude as the probability. The probability is the squared modulus of the amplitude, and amplitudes are in general complex.
- Treating a superposition as a mixture. They agree along the basis axis but differ in interference along other axes.
- Ignoring the relative phase. $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$ and $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle - \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$ differ in their $x$-measurement outcomes.
- Forgetting to normalize: the squared moduli of the amplitudes must sum to 1 before using the Born rule.

@practice mc
prompt: A spin-1/2 source A prepares particles in the state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$, and source B flips a fair coin for each particle and prepares $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ accordingly. Which measurement distinguishes the two sources?
choice: Measuring the spin along $z$, since A gives only up and B gives both.
choice*: Measuring the spin along $x$, since A gives $+x$ with certainty and B gives $+x$ with probability $\tfrac12$.
choice: Measuring the spin along $z$, since A gives up with probability $\tfrac12$ and B gives up with probability 1.
choice: No measurement can distinguish them, since both give up and down with probability $\tfrac12$ along $z$.
hint: Compute the probability of $+x$ for each source, using $\lvert{+x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$.
solution: Along $z$ both sources give up and down with probability $\tfrac12$, so $z$ cannot distinguish them. Along $x$, source A's state is $\lvert{+x}\rangle$, which gives $+x$ with certainty, while source B's particles are in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$, each of which gives $+x$ with probability $\tfrac12$.

@practice num
prompt: A spin-1/2 particle is in the state $\lvert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{3}{5}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \tfrac{4i}{5}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. Using the Born rule in the z-basis, what is the probability of the outcome $+\hbar/2$ along $z$?
answer: 0.36
check: abs(3 / 5) ** 2
hint: The probability is the squared modulus of the coefficient of $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$.
solution: $\lvert 3/5\rvert^2 = 9/25 = 0.36$. (The factor $i$ in the other amplitude does not matter along $z$; its squared modulus is $16/25 = 0.64$, and $0.36 + 0.64 = 1$.)

@practice self
prompt: Explain in three or four sentences why the reading "the particle is really in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or in $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ and we do not know which" is not a correct account of the state $\lvert{+x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$.
hint: Compare what the two accounts predict for a measurement of the spin along $x$.
solution: If each particle were in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ with probability one half each, then an $x$-measurement would give $+x$ with probability $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$, since each of those states gives $+x$ with probability $\tfrac12$. But particles prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ give $+x$ with probability 1. The amplitudes for the two $z$-values interfere, which a mixture cannot reproduce. So the state is not a statistical mixture of $z$-states; this does not exclude all hidden variables, only a definite pre-existing $z$-spin.
rubric: Computes or states the probability of $+x$ for the mixture ($\tfrac12$) and for the superposition (1).
rubric: Concludes that the observed difference rules out the ignorance reading.
rubric: States the limit of the conclusion, namely that other hidden variables are not excluded.

@quiz num
prompt: A spin-1/2 particle is in the state $\lvert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \tfrac{3i}{\sqrt{10}}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. With $\lvert{+y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle + i\lvert\downarrow\rangle)$, what is the probability of the outcome $+\hbar/2$ along $y$?
answer: 0.8
check: abs((1 + (-1j) * (3j)) / sqrt(2) / sqrt(10)) ** 2
hint: Compute $\langle{+y}\vert\psi\rangle$, remembering to take the complex conjugate of the coefficient of $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ in $\lvert{+y}\rangle$.
solution: $\langle{+y}\vert = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\langle\uparrow\vert - i\langle\downarrow\vert)$, so $\langle{+y}\vert\psi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{10}} + (-i)\tfrac{3i}{\sqrt{10}}\bigr) = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\cdot\tfrac{4}{\sqrt{10}}$. The squared modulus is $\tfrac{16}{20} = 0.8$.

@quiz mc
prompt: According to the Born rule, a spin-1/2 particle is in the state $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ with $\lvert a\rvert^2 + \lvert b\rvert^2 = 1$. What is the probability that a measurement along $z$ gives $+\hbar/2$?
choice: $a$, the amplitude itself.
choice: $\lvert a\rvert$, the modulus of the amplitude.
choice*: $\lvert a\rvert^2$, the squared modulus of the amplitude.
choice: $a + b$, the sum of the amplitudes.
hint: Probabilities are real numbers between 0 and 1 that add up to 1.
solution: The Born rule gives $\lvert a\rvert^2$, the squared modulus of the amplitude. The amplitude itself may be complex or negative, and the moduli $\lvert a\rvert$ and $\lvert b\rvert$ do not sum to 1 in general, whereas $\lvert a\rvert^2 + \lvert b\rvert^2 = 1$.

@quiz self
prompt: Consider the argument: P1, if each particle prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ really were in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ (we are ignorant which) then $P(+x) = \tfrac12$; P2, $P(+x) = 1$ for particles prepared in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$; C, the particles are not really in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. State what the conclusion shows and what it does not show about hidden variables.
hint: The conclusion is about definite values of $z$-spin, not about all possible hidden variables.
solution: The conclusion shows that the system does not have a definite $z$-spin value of which we are merely ignorant, since the observed $x$-statistics could not then arise. It does not show that there are no hidden variables: a theory could add other variables whose values, together with the whole measuring arrangement, determine the outcomes, without any pre-existing $z$-spin value. The argument therefore constrains what the hidden variables can be, which is the starting point for Bell's theorem and for contextuality results; it does not by itself establish that quantum states describe reality completely.
rubric: Says that the conclusion rules out pre-existing definite $z$-spin that is merely unknown.
rubric: Says that other hidden variables, or measurement-dependent outcomes, remain possible.
rubric: Connects the point to the later debate about hidden variables.
