@article
The interpretations of Lesson 11 all try to say what the world is like, so that the quantum state describes it. A different family of views says that this is the wrong way to read the state. On these views the quantum state is not a description of a system as it is in itself; it is tied to a perspective. This lesson presents two such views, and the argument that shows why they are live options: the Wigner's friend argument.

### Ontic and epistemic readings of the state

A reading of the quantum state is **ontic** (or $\psi$-ontic) if the state is a real feature of the system, as in many-worlds, GRW collapse theories, and (on the usual reading) pilot-wave theory. It is **epistemic** ($\psi$-epistemic) if the state represents information, knowledge or credence about something else. In 2012 Pusey, Barrett and Rudolph proved a theorem (the **PBR theorem**): suppose that systems have underlying physical states, and that independently prepared systems have independent underlying states (preparation independence). Then different pure quantum states correspond to non-overlapping sets of underlying states, so the quantum state cannot be merely partial knowledge of an underlying state in the overlapping way that epistemic models assumed. The theorem is conditional on its assumptions, and views that reject the framework are not touched.

### QBism

**QBism** (quantum Bayesianism; developed by Christopher Fuchs, Rüdiger Schack, David Mermin and others) says that a quantum state is an agent's personal degrees of belief about the consequences of the agent's own actions on the world, in the form of the experiences that result. Different agents may assign different states to the same system, and none is the correct one. The **Born rule** is not a law about the world but a normative addition to ordinary coherence (Bayesian) constraints on an agent's beliefs. A measurement outcome is a personal experience of the agent who acts. **Collapse** is not a physical process: it is the updating of beliefs on learning the outcome. Nonlocality becomes mild: when Alice learns her outcome and updates her probabilities about Bob's, nothing changes in Bob's world. **What exists**: agents and the world they act on; the theory says little about the world apart from agents. **Costs**: it is accused of anti-realism or solipsism (QBists deny this, saying that the world exists and constrains outcomes); it makes no account of what the world is like; and it gives agents a special role that physics did not previously give them.

### Relational quantum mechanics

**Relational quantum mechanics** (Carlo Rovelli, 1996) holds that the state of a system, and the values of its properties, are always relative to another system. Any physical system can serve as the "observer"; consciousness is not required. After the friend measures a particle, the outcome is definite relative to the friend; relative to an outside system that has not interacted with the lab, the particle and friend are in an entangled state. There is no absolute, observer-independent state of a system and no absolute collapse. **What exists**: systems and the relative facts about them. **Costs**: it must explain how the relative facts of different systems fit together, and some critics say that relative facts are the same as no facts.

### Wigner's friend

Eugene Wigner (1961) imagined a friend in a sealed laboratory who measures the spin of a particle prepared in an equal superposition of up and down. The friend sees an outcome. Wigner, outside, treats the lab (friend, apparatus, particle) as one isolated quantum system. The argument:

| P1 | If the quantum state is a complete description and isolated systems always evolve unitarily, then Wigner assigns to the sealed lab the entangled state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{sees up}\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{sees down}\rangle)$, which does not settle which outcome the friend obtained. |
| P2 | If the friend's outcome is an absolute fact (a fact that does not depend on who describes it), then a complete description of the lab says which outcome occurred. |
| C | So it is not the case that all three hold: the quantum state is a complete description, isolated systems always evolve unitarily, and the friend's outcome is an absolute fact. |

The argument is valid: if all three held, P1 would make Wigner's complete description silent about the outcome, while P2 would require it to say which outcome occurred. Each interpretation gives up a different claim:

| Interpretation | Claim given up |
|---|---|
| Collapse theories | Unitary evolution of isolated systems: the lab collapses before Wigner looks |
| Pilot-wave theory | Completeness: the positions of the particles settle the outcome |
| Many-worlds | An absolute single outcome: the outcome is relative to a branch |
| Relational QM | An absolute outcome: it is relative to the friend |
| QBism | That the state is a complete description of the world: it is Wigner's belief, and the friend's outcome is the friend's experience |

In 2018 Daniela Frauchiger and Renato Renner published an "extended" Wigner's friend argument that, from the universal validity of quantum mechanics, single outcomes, and the consistency of agents' reasoning, derives a contradiction; interpretations respond by rejecting different assumptions, and what the argument establishes is disputed.

### What quantum mechanics says about reality

Is the state real, as in the collapse, pilot-wave and many-worlds views, or perspectival, as in QBism and relational views? Is the world local? Does it contain one outcome per measurement or many? These questions remain open. Bell's theorem constrains every answer; the measurement problem has no agreed solution; and experiments cannot currently decide among the leading options, which agree on the standard predictions. A defensible conclusion is that each interpretation is a live option with specific costs, and a good report says which costs one is prepared to pay and why.

@example
title: The test that separates collapse from unitary evolution
problem: In an idealized Wigner's friend test, the sealed lab ends in the state $\lvert\Phi\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle + \lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle)$ if the evolution is unitary. If instead the friend's measurement collapsed the state, the lab would be in the mixture of $\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. Wigner tests whether the lab is in $\lvert\Phi\rangle$. Find the probability of "yes" in each case.
step: Unitary evolution: the lab is in $\lvert\Phi\rangle$, so the probability of "yes" is $\lvert\langle\Phi\vert\Phi\rangle\rvert^2 = 1$.
step: Collapse: the lab is in $\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle$ or $\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each. The probability of "yes" in each is $\lvert\langle\Phi\vert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle\rvert^2 = \tfrac12$ and likewise $\tfrac12$ for the other.
step: The probability of "yes" is $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 + \tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12 = \tfrac12$.
step: So the two accounts predict different frequencies (1 and $\tfrac12$) for a test that Wigner could in principle run. Such a test is far beyond what can be done with a human friend, which is why it is a thought experiment; experiments with much simpler "friends" have been done.
answer: Unitary evolution gives "yes" with probability 1, collapse gives $\tfrac12$.

@example
title: Mapping interpretations to the claim they give up
problem: In the Wigner's friend argument, the three claims are: (A) the quantum state is a complete description; (B) isolated systems always evolve unitarily; (C) the friend's outcome is an absolute fact. Say which claim each of GRW collapse, pilot-wave theory and many-worlds gives up.
step: GRW collapse adds a stochastic collapse that acts on the lab before Wigner looks, so isolated systems do not always evolve unitarily: it gives up (B).
step: Pilot-wave theory says that the particle positions are part of the full description, so the state alone is not complete: it gives up (A).
step: Many-worlds keeps (A) and (B), and says that the friend's outcome is relative to a branch, not absolute: it gives up (C).
answer: GRW gives up (B), pilot-wave theory gives up (A), many-worlds gives up (C).

@mistakes
- Treating QBism as solipsism. QBists say that outcomes are personal experiences of agents acting on a world that exists and constrains them; whether this is enough for realism is the subject of the debate.
- Treating "observer" in relational quantum mechanics as requiring a conscious being. Any physical system can be the system relative to which facts are defined.
- Thinking that the PBR theorem proves that quantum states are real. It proves a conditional result: given its assumptions (including preparation independence), they cannot be merely epistemic in the overlapping sense.
- Saying that experiments favor one of these interpretations. They agree on the standard predictions, and most disputes are over assumptions, ontology and costs.

@practice num
prompt: In a Wigner's friend test, the lab's state would be $\lvert\Psi\rangle = 0.8\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle + 0.6\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ under unitary evolution (a unit vector, with $\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ orthonormal). If the friend's measurement had instead collapsed it, the lab would be in $\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle$ with probability $0.64$ or in $\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ with probability $0.36$. Wigner tests whether the lab is in $\lvert\Psi\rangle$. What is the probability of "yes" in the collapse case?
answer: 0.5392
check: 0.64 * abs(0.8) ** 2 + 0.36 * abs(0.6) ** 2
hint: In each collapsed state, the probability of "yes" is the squared overlap with $\lvert\Psi\rangle$.
solution: $\lvert\langle\Psi\vert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle\rvert^2 = 0.64$ and $\lvert\langle\Psi\vert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle\rvert^2 = 0.36$. So the probability of "yes" is $0.64\times 0.64 + 0.36\times 0.36 = 0.4096 + 0.1296 = 0.5392$, whereas unitary evolution gives 1.

@quiz num
prompt: In a Wigner's friend test, unitary evolution leaves the lab in $\lvert\Psi\rangle = \sqrt{0.75}\,\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle + \sqrt{0.25}\,\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ (orthonormal terms), so Wigner's test "is the lab in $\lvert\Psi\rangle$?" gives "yes" with probability 1. If the friend's measurement had collapsed the state, the lab would be in $\lvert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle$ with probability 0.75 or $\lvert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle$ with probability 0.25. By how much do the probabilities of "yes" in the two cases differ (unitary minus collapse)?
answer: 0.375
check: 1 - (0.75 * 0.75 + 0.25 * 0.25)
hint: Find the probability of "yes" in the collapse case from the squared overlaps, then subtract it from 1.
solution: In the collapse case $\lvert\langle\Psi\vert\uparrow,\text{up}\rangle\rvert^2 = 0.75$ and $\lvert\langle\Psi\vert\downarrow,\text{down}\rangle\rvert^2 = 0.25$, so the probability of "yes" is $0.75\times 0.75 + 0.25\times 0.25 = 0.625$. Unitary evolution gives 1, so the difference is $1 - 0.625 = 0.375$.

choice: That quantum mechanics is complete and that hidden variables are impossible.
choice*: That, if systems have underlying physical states and independently prepared systems have independent underlying states, then distinct pure quantum states correspond to non-overlapping sets of underlying states, so the quantum state cannot be merely partial knowledge in the overlapping sense.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, answer: what, if anything, does quantum mechanics tell us about the nature of reality? Defend one view or explain why the question remains open, and state the strongest reply to your view.
hint: Name the options (ontic reading, perspectival reading, or open question) and what each costs.
solution: The question remains open, because the leading interpretations agree on all observed predictions and differ about what exists. The theory together with experiment does tell us something robust: Bell-inequality violations, including the loophole-free tests of 2015, show that no local hidden-variable model with measurement independence and single outcomes reproduces the correlations. That rules out one familiar picture of reality but leaves a choice among nonlocal theories (pilot-wave, collapse), a local branching theory (many-worlds) and perspectival views (QBism, relational quantum mechanics). Each pays a price: nonlocality and a preferred foliation, the probability problem, or a limited account of the world apart from perspectives. The strongest reply to the view that the question is open is that simplicity or theoretical virtue may eventually favor one option, as it did in earlier episodes of physics; the reply to that is that no such decisive consideration is currently available.
