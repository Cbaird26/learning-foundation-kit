@article
Lesson 1 left a question open: if absolute space makes no difference to distances among bodies, does it make a difference to anything? Newton thought it does: it makes a difference to the shape of water in a spinning bucket. This is **Newton's bucket argument** (Scholium to the Definitions in the *Principia*), and the replies to it shaped the philosophy of physics for three centuries.

### The bucket

Hang a bucket of water from a rope, twist the rope tight, and release the bucket. There are four stages:

| Stage | What is happening | Water moves relative to the bucket? | Surface of the water |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bucket and water both at rest | No | Flat |
| 2 | Bucket spinning, water not yet moving | Yes | Flat |
| 3 | Bucket and water spinning together | No | Concave |
| 4 | Bucket stopped, water still spinning | Yes | Concave |

The shape of the surface does not follow the water's motion relative to the bucket. Stages 1 and 3 have the same relative motion (none) and different shapes; stages 2 and 4 have the same relative motion (present) and different shapes. In steady rotation at angular velocity $\omega$ (radians per second), the water at distance $r$ from the axis needs a centripetal acceleration $\omega^2 r$, so the surface slope is $dh/dr = \omega^2 r/g$ and the surface is a paraboloid with $h(r) - h(0) = \omega^2 r^2/(2g)$ (SI units, $g = 9.81$ m/s$^2$). For $\omega = 2$ rad/s and $r = 0.10$ m this gives a rise of about 2.04 mm.

### The argument

Newton's target is the view that the true motion of a body is its motion relative to the bodies immediately surrounding it (a view usually associated with Descartes). The argument has two stages:

| P1 | If the rotation of the water is motion relative to the bodies immediately surrounding it, then the water has the same surface shape whenever it has the same motion relative to the bucket. |
| P2 | In stage 1 and in stage 3 the water has no motion relative to the bucket, yet its surface is flat in stage 1 and concave in stage 3. |
| C1 | So the rotation of the water is not motion relative to the bucket, the body immediately surrounding it. |
| P3 | The concavity is an effect of the water's rotation, which is a real motion of the water relative to something. |
| P4 | Whatever the water rotates relative to is either the bodies immediately surrounding it or absolute space. |
| C2 | So the water rotates relative to absolute space. |

C1 follows from P1 and P2 by modus tollens; C2 follows from C1, P3 and P4 by disjunctive syllogism. The argument is valid, so a reply must deny a premise. The most important reply denies P4.

### The relationist reply: relative to what?

**Ernst Mach** (*The Science of Mechanics*, 1883) replied that Newton's experiment never showed that the water would be concave if there were nothing but the water and the bucket. The bucket's walls are a tiny mass; the real comparison may be with the fixed stars, or with all the matter in the universe. On this reply, the water rotates relative to the distant stars, which is a relation among bodies, so P4's disjunction is not exhaustive: there is a third option. This is the root of what is called **Mach's principle**, which has been formulated in many ways, none of them easily derived from current physics, and a straightforward proof that general relativity satisfies it is not available.

Newton had a counter-case: two globes joined by a cord, rotating about their center in an otherwise empty universe. The cord would be taut if they rotate and slack if they do not, although the distance between the globes is constant either way. The relationist can reply that it is unclear what the physics of such an empty universe would be, or can treat the cord's tension as grounded in the relations of the globes to all possible bodies or to the cord itself. Substantivalists reply that the simplest explanation of the tension is absolute rotation.

### Galilean relativity

Newton himself noted (Corollary V of the *Laws of Motion*) that the motions of bodies within a space are the same whether the space is at rest or moves uniformly in a straight line. In modern notation, let frame $S'$ move at constant velocity $v$ along the $x$-axis relative to frame $S$. The **Galilean transformation** is
$$x' = x - vt, \qquad t' = t,$$
with distances in meters, times in seconds and velocities in m/s. Then a body's velocity transforms as $u' = u - v$, but its acceleration is unchanged, $a' = a$. Newton's second law, $F = ma$, has the same form in both frames. So no mechanical experiment can tell whether a laboratory is at rest or in uniform motion: **absolute velocity is undetectable**. Absolute **acceleration** is detectable: the water's concavity, a taut cord and the push you feel in a turning car are the same in every frame that is not accelerating.

This suggests a repair. **Neo-Newtonian** (Galilean) spacetime has absolute time intervals, the distance between simultaneous events, and a distinction between accelerated and unaccelerated motion, but no fact about which inertial frame is at rest. It has enough structure for rotation but not enough for absolute velocity; the shift argument's analogue for velocity (a world where everything moves at a constant extra velocity) is then no objection to it.

@example
title: Testing the shape against relative motion
problem: Using the four stages of the bucket, decide for each pair of stages whether the water's surface shape follows its motion relative to the bucket.
step: Record each stage as (relative motion of water to bucket, shape): stage 1 (none, flat), stage 2 (present, flat), stage 3 (none, concave), stage 4 (present, concave).
step: If the shape followed relative motion, stages with the same relative motion would have the same shape. Stages 1 and 3 both have none, but flat differs from concave.
step: Stages 2 and 4 both have relative motion present, but flat differs from concave again.
step: So the surface shape is not a function of the water's motion relative to the bucket. It does follow the water's rotation relative to the room, the Earth and the stars: it is flat in stages 1 and 2, where the water is not turning with respect to them, and concave in stages 3 and 4, where it is. That is why Mach's reply is available.
answer: The shape does not follow motion relative to the bucket, but it does follow motion relative to the surroundings farther away, so the experiment refutes only the claim that the bucket is the right reference body.

@example
title: Velocity changes but acceleration does not
problem: A train moves at 20 m/s along a straight track (frame $S'$, with $S$ the ground and $x$ pointing forward). A ball on the train is thrown forward at 5 m/s relative to the train and, 2 s later, moves at 7 m/s relative to the train. Find its velocities and acceleration in each frame.
step: Apply $u = u' + v$. At the start, $u = 5 + 20 = 25$ m/s relative to the ground. Two seconds later, $u = 7 + 20 = 27$ m/s.
step: In $S'$: $a' = (7 - 5)/2 = 1$ m/s$^2$. In $S$: $a = (27 - 25)/2 = 1$ m/s$^2$.
step: The two frames disagree about the velocity (5 against 25 m/s) but agree on the acceleration (1 m/s$^2$), so they agree on the force $F = ma$ that acted on the ball.
answer: The ball's velocity is 25 m/s then 27 m/s on the ground and 5 m/s then 7 m/s on the train; its acceleration is 1 m/s$^2$ in both frames.

@mistakes
- Saying that the bucket argument proves absolute space. It shows that the shape does not follow motion relative to the bucket; to reach absolute space it needs P4, which the Machian denies.
- Thinking that Galilean relativity makes all motion relative. Velocity is relative to a frame, but acceleration, including rotation, has the same value in every inertial frame.
- Treating Mach's principle as an established result of general relativity. It is a family of proposals with a disputed status.
- Forgetting the sign convention in $x' = x - vt$: $S'$ moves in the positive $x$ direction at speed $v$ relative to $S$.

@quiz self
prompt: Newton's two globes are joined by a cord and spin about their center in an otherwise empty universe; the cord is taut. State the argument from this case against the relationist who holds that all motion is relative motion among bodies, and give one reply.
hint: The distance between the globes is constant whether they spin or not.
solution: The relationist holds that motion is change of relation among bodies. The distance between the globes does not change whether they spin or not, and no other bodies exist, so a purely relational account has no difference between a spinning and a non-spinning pair. But the cord is taut only if they spin, so there is a physical difference that needs absolute rotation. One reply is that the empty universe is a different physical situation and our judgments about it are unreliable; the relationist may say that the cord's tension must be explained by relations to all possible or actual matter. Another reply accepts primitive absolute acceleration while denying absolute space and velocity, which is close to neo-Newtonian spacetime.
