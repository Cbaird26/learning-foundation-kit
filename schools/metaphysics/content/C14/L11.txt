@article
Lesson 8 showed that three claims cannot all be true: the wave function is complete, it always evolves linearly, and measurements have determinate outcomes. Lesson 10 added that quantum correlations cannot come from a local hidden-variable model with measurement independence and single outcomes. This lesson presents three leading ways to respond. Each is a live option with a price, none is established, and informed physicists and philosophers still disagree about the interpretation of quantum mechanics. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey (only 556 respondents answered this question) 22% accepted or leaned toward hidden-variable views, 19% many-worlds, 17% collapse and 13% epistemic views; the share who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

### Collapse theories

**Collapse theories** deny that the Schrödinger evolution is always linear. In the model of Ghirardi, Rimini and Weber (**GRW**, 1986), each particle's wave function undergoes, at random times, a sudden **spontaneous localization**: it is multiplied by a narrow Gaussian centered at a random point, with the probability of each center given by the Born rule. In the values originally proposed, the mean rate is $\lambda \approx 10^{-16}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ per particle and the width is about $10^{-7}$ m. A single particle then almost never collapses (once in $10^{16}$ s, about 317 million years), so microscopic systems behave quantum mechanically. But a system of $N$ particles whose positions are entangled collapses at rate $N\lambda$, so a macroscopic pointer with $N \sim 10^{20}$ or more collapses within a fraction of a second, which gives the measurement a determinate outcome. **What exists**: the wave function, plus (depending on the version) a mass density or discrete "flashes". **Costs**: new constants that are not derived from anything else; small predicted departures from quantum mechanics (heating, for instance) that experiments constrain; nonlocality (the collapse of an entangled pair is instantaneous in the formalism); and difficulty in building fully relativistic versions, although some exist.

### Pilot-wave theory

In **pilot-wave** (de Broglie–Bohm) theory, de Broglie 1927 and Bohm 1952, the wave function is not the complete description: there are also particles with definite positions $Q_k(t)$. The wave function evolves by the Schrödinger equation and **guides** the particles: $dQ_k/dt = (\hbar/m_k)\,\mathrm{Im}(\nabla_k\psi/\psi)$ evaluated at the actual configuration. For a plane wave $\psi = e^{ikx}$ the velocity is $\hbar k/m$, in SI units. The theory is deterministic: given the initial positions and the wave function, everything follows. Probabilities arise because the initial positions are distributed according to $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ (**quantum equilibrium**), and then the theory agrees with the standard predictions. Other observables such as spin are not extra properties of the particle: outcomes depend on the whole measuring arrangement. **What exists**: a wave function (on configuration space) and particles. **Costs**: the guidance of one particle depends instantly on the positions of the others, so the theory is nonlocal; a relativistic version appears to need a preferred foliation of spacetime; and the wave function has a role that some find odd (it guides but is not guided), as well as "empty" branches that never influence the particles.

| P1 | In standard pilot-wave theory, the velocity of each particle depends on the instantaneous positions of all the others, which requires a preferred division of spacetime into simultaneity slices (a preferred foliation). |
| P2 | If a theory's fundamental dynamics requires a preferred foliation, then its fundamental laws are not Lorentz invariant. |
| C | So the fundamental laws of standard pilot-wave theory are not Lorentz invariant. |

The argument is valid. Replies: deny P2, by letting the foliation be determined by the wave function or other dynamical variables in a way that is itself covariant, so that the laws remain Lorentz invariant even though a foliation is used; or accept C and deny that it is a cost, because in quantum equilibrium the theory's observable predictions are Lorentz invariant and the preferred structure is undetectable. Critics answer that an undetectable preferred structure resembles the surplus structure (absolute position, a privileged frame) discussed in Lessons 1 to 5; defenders reply that a theory may contain structure that is not directly observable if it explains the observed phenomena.

### Many-worlds

The **many-worlds** (Everettian) interpretation, from Hugh Everett's 1957 relative-state formulation, keeps completeness and linearity and drops the claim that measurements have a *single* outcome. The universal wave function always evolves by the Schrödinger equation. When a measuring device interacts with a superposition, the result is the entangled state of Lesson 8, and decoherence makes its terms evolve independently, like separate quasi-classical **branches**, each containing an observer who sees one outcome. All outcomes occur. **What exists**: the universal wave function (and, derivatively, the branches it contains). **Costs**: the **probability problem**: if every outcome occurs, what do the Born probabilities mean? Everettians such as David Wallace and David Deutsch argue, using decision theory, that a rational agent should weight branches by squared amplitude; critics deny that the argument succeeds. Naive branch counting gives the wrong answers: ten measurements of an equal superposition give $2^{10} = 1024$ branches, but if the amplitudes are unequal, counting branches does not match the Born weights. There are also questions about the nature of the branches (they are emergent, not fundamental) and about wave-function realism: a wave function on a very high-dimensional space is the fundamental thing.

### How they answer Bell

Collapse and pilot-wave theories give up locality; many-worlds gives up single outcomes and keeps locality. None allows signaling. Choosing among them involves weighing nonlocality, ontology, probability and relativity, and no consensus exists.

@example
title: A collapse rate
problem: In the GRW model with $\lambda = 10^{-16}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ per particle, find the collapse rate and mean waiting time for a system of $N = 10^{23}$ particles whose positions are entangled, and for a single particle, in years (1 year $= 3.15576\times 10^{7}$ s).
step: The collapse rate for $N$ entangled particles is $N\lambda = 10^{23}\times 10^{-16} = 10^{7}\ \text{s}^{-1}$.
step: The mean waiting time is $1/(N\lambda) = 10^{-7}$ s.
step: For one particle the mean waiting time is $10^{16}$ s, which is $10^{16}/3.15576\times 10^{7} = 3.17\times 10^{8}$ years.
step: So the same law that leaves an atom alone for hundreds of millions of years collapses a macroscopic pointer within about a tenth of a microsecond, which is how GRW gets definite outcomes at large scales.
answer: The system collapses at $10^7$ per second (every $10^{-7}$ s on average); a single particle collapses about once in $3.17\times 10^8$ years.

@example
title: Branch weights
problem: A spin-1/2 particle prepared in $\sqrt{0.6}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \sqrt{0.4}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ is measured three times in a row (each time a fresh particle in the same state). On the many-worlds picture, how many branches are there, and what is the Born weight of the branch in which all three outcomes are "up"? Compare with the fraction of branches that are all-up.
step: Each measurement splits every branch in two, so there are $2^3 = 8$ branches.
step: The Born weight (squared amplitude) of the all-up branch is $0.6^3 = 0.216$.
step: The fraction of branches that are all-up is $1/8 = 0.125$.
step: These differ, so counting branches does not reproduce the Born rule; the weights, not the number of branches, must carry the probabilities, which is the heart of the probability problem.
answer: There are 8 branches; the all-up branch has weight 0.216, although it is only 1/8 = 0.125 of the branches.

@mistakes
- Saying that pilot-wave theory is a theory with "no probabilities". Its dynamics is deterministic, but the probabilities arise from the distribution of initial positions.
- Saying that many-worlds has no collapse and therefore needs no account of probability. It must explain what the Born weights mean.
- Treating GRW as a reinterpretation. It is a different theory with new constants and slightly different predictions.
- Assuming that nonlocality in pilot-wave or collapse theories permits signaling. All of these theories give the quantum statistics, which obey no-signaling.

@practice num
prompt: A spin-1/2 particle whose probability of "up" is 0.7 is measured four times in a row (a fresh particle each time, same state). On the many-worlds picture, the Born weight of the branch in which all four outcomes are "up" is the product of the four squared amplitudes. What is it?
answer: 0.2401

solution: $0.7^4 = 0.2401$. There are $2^4 = 16$ branches, of which this is one, so its weight, not the fraction of branches 1/16 = 0.0625, is what the Born rule requires to be its probability.

@practice self
prompt: Compare two of the three interpretations (collapse, pilot-wave, many-worlds) in a paragraph: say how each answers the measurement problem and state the main cost of each.
hint: Say which of the three claims (complete, linear, determinate) each gives up, then name the cost.
solution: A collapse theory such as GRW denies that evolution is always linear: it adds spontaneous, random localizations whose rate grows with the number of entangled particles, so measurement outcomes are determinate. Its costs are new constants, small testable deviations from quantum mechanics and difficulty in making a relativistic version. Many-worlds denies that there is a single outcome: the universal wave function evolves linearly, and every outcome occurs in some branch. Its main cost is the probability problem: it must explain what the Born weights mean if all outcomes occur, and decision-theoretic derivations are disputed. Both agree with the standard statistics; they differ in what exists and in what they cost.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the following claim: "The probability problem shows that many-worlds is false." Say what the problem is and what the strongest reply is.
hint: Consider an agent who knows that every outcome will occur on some branch, and ask what it could mean to expect one.
solution: The problem: on the many-worlds picture every outcome occurs, so it is unclear what the Born probabilities mean and why an agent should expect outcomes with frequencies given by the squared amplitudes rather than by the number of branches. For three measurements with probability 0.6 of "up", 1/8 of the branches are all-up although the Born weight is 0.216. The strongest reply is the decision-theoretic one: a rational agent who accepts the theory and certain symmetry and rationality principles should weight future branches by squared amplitude, and uncertainty can arise because the agent does not know which branch it is in. The claim that the problem refutes the theory is too strong: critics say that the reply smuggles in assumptions, and defenders say it justifies the Born rule, so the problem is a serious open cost, not a refutation.
