@article
Lesson 7 left two rules for how a quantum state changes. Between measurements, the state evolves by the Schrödinger equation: smoothly, deterministically and linearly. When a measurement is made, textbooks say that something else happens. This lesson is about the gap between the two rules, and about why the standard theory does not tell you when each applies.

### The collapse postulate

The **collapse postulate** (also called the projection postulate) says: when an observable is measured and the outcome is associated with the basis vector $\lvert\phi\rangle$, the state afterward is $\lvert\phi\rangle$, and the probability of that outcome beforehand was $\lvert\langle\phi\vert\psi\rangle\rvert^2$. So a spin measurement along $x$ on a particle in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$ gives $+x$ or $-x$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, and leaves the particle in $\lvert{+x}\rangle$ or $\lvert{-x}\rangle$. A second measurement along $z$ then gives up or down with probability $\tfrac12$ each: the first measurement erased the earlier certainty about $z$. The collapse is not linear, not deterministic and not reversible, unlike Schrödinger evolution. But a measuring device is itself made of atoms that, one would think, obey the Schrödinger equation.

### Why this is a problem

Take an ideal measuring device for $z$-spin, with a pointer that starts in a "ready" state. By being a good device, it makes the pointer read "up" for a spin-up particle and "down" for a spin-down particle: $\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{ready}\rangle \to \lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{ready}\rangle \to \lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$. Now send in a particle in $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. If the evolution is linear, the result is
$$\bigl(a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle\bigr)\lvert\text{ready}\rangle \;\to\; a\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle,$$
an **entangled** state of particle and pointer in which neither pointer reading is singled out. Yet we see one outcome. This is the **measurement problem**. Tim Maudlin (1995) put it as an inconsistent triad: (A) the wave function is a complete description of a system; (B) it always evolves linearly, as the Schrödinger equation says; (C) measurements have determinate outcomes. In standard form:

| P1 | There is a device (the ideal measuring device above) that sends $\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{ready}\rangle$ to $\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{ready}\rangle$ to $\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$. |
| P2 | If the evolution is linear, then that device sends $(a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle)\lvert\text{ready}\rangle$, with $a$ and $b$ both nonzero, to $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$. |
| P3 | If the wave function is a complete description, then in the state $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$ the pointer is determinately at neither "up" nor "down". |
| C | So it is not the case that all three hold: the wave function is complete, evolution is always linear, and the pointer determinately ends at "up" or at "down". |

The argument is valid: suppose all three hold; P1 and P2 give the entangled state; P3 says the pointer is then determinately at neither reading, contradicting the third claim. Each family of solutions denies one claim. **Collapse theories** deny (B): they add a nonlinear, stochastic process. **Hidden-variable theories** deny (A): the wave function is not the whole story. **Many-worlds** accepts (A) and (B) and reads (C) differently: all outcomes occur, in different branches. Lesson 11 takes up each at length. The measurement problem has no agreed solution, and physicists and philosophers still disagree about the interpretation of quantum mechanics.

Schrödinger's cat (1935) is the same problem with a macroscopic pointer: a cat correlated with a radioactive atom would be in an entangled superposition of alive and dead.

### Decoherence

Real devices are not isolated. Suppose the particle-pointer state interacts with an environment (air molecules, photons) that records which pointer reading occurred: $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert E_1\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert E_2\rangle)$. The state of the particle alone, obtained by ignoring the environment, is described by a **reduced density matrix** whose diagonal entries are $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ and whose off-diagonal entries have magnitude $\tfrac12\lvert\langle E_1\vert E_2\rangle\rvert$. For a macroscopic environment the overlap $\langle E_1\vert E_2\rangle$ falls to practically zero extremely fast, so interference between the two pointer readings becomes unobservable in practice. This is **decoherence**: it explains why we do not see superpositions of macroscopic pointer positions, and why the preferred measurement bases are what they are. It does not by itself pick out one outcome: the global state is still an entangled superposition, and the reduced density matrix looks like a mixture only when we ignore the environment. Whether it solves the measurement problem depends on the interpretation; most agree that it does not solve it alone.

@example
title: A sequence of measurements
problem: A spin-1/2 particle is in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$. Its spin along $x$ is measured, and the outcome is $-x$. Then its spin along $z$ is measured. What is the probability of the sequence ($-x$, then $\downarrow$)? Compare with measuring $z$ directly.
step: Measured directly along $z$, the particle gives up with probability 1 (it is in $\lvert\uparrow\rangle$).
step: The probability of $-x$ is $\lvert\langle{-x}\vert\uparrow\rangle\rvert^2 = (1/\sqrt{2})^2 = \tfrac12$, and afterward the state is $\lvert{-x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle - \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$.
step: The probability of $\downarrow$ along $z$ in this state is $\lvert -1/\sqrt{2}\rvert^2 = \tfrac12$.
step: The probability of the sequence is $\tfrac12 \times \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$. The intervening $x$-measurement destroyed the certainty about $z$.
answer: The sequence has probability $\tfrac14$; without the $x$-measurement, $\downarrow$ along $z$ would have probability 0.

@example
title: Decoherence and interference
problem: A particle and its environment are in the state $\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert E_1\rangle + \lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert E_2\rangle)$ with $\langle E_1\vert E_2\rangle = 0.1$ (a real number). Find the magnitude of the off-diagonal element of the particle's reduced density matrix in the $z$-basis, and the probability that the particle gives $+x$.
step: The reduced density matrix has diagonal entries $\tfrac12, \tfrac12$ and off-diagonal entries $\tfrac12\langle E_1\vert E_2\rangle$, of magnitude $\tfrac12 \times 0.1 = 0.05$.
step: The probability of $+x$ is $\tfrac12(1 + \langle E_1\vert E_2\rangle) = \tfrac12(1.1) = 0.55$.
step: With overlap 1 (no record) it would be $1$; with overlap 0 (a perfect record) it is $\tfrac12$, the value for a mixture.
answer: The off-diagonal magnitude is 0.05 and $P(+x) = 0.55$: interference is mostly, but not completely, lost.

@mistakes
- Thinking that the measurement problem is about whether quantum mechanics predicts the right statistics. It does, to great precision; the problem is what the theory says happens in a single measurement.
- Saying that decoherence turns a superposition into a mixture. The reduced state looks like a mixture, but the global state is still entangled, and no single outcome has been selected.
- Taking the cat as a claim that cats are in superposition. Schrödinger posed it as a reductio of a naive reading of the formalism applied to large objects.
- Forgetting that the collapse postulate changes the state: the second of two measurements is described by the post-measurement state.

Using $\lvert{\pm x}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle \pm \lvert\downarrow\rangle)$ and $\lvert{\pm y}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\lvert\uparrow\rangle \pm i\lvert\downarrow\rangle)$, what is the probability that the three outcomes are $+x$, $+y$ and then spin down along $z$?
answer: 0.125
check: abs(1 / sqrt(2)) ** 2 * abs((1 / sqrt(2)) * (1 / sqrt(2)) * (1 + (-1j) * 1)) ** 2 * abs(1j / sqrt(2)) ** 2

@practice self
prompt: Apply the measurement problem to Schrödinger's cat: a cat is placed in a box with a device that kills it if a radioactive atom decays, and the atom is in an equal superposition of "decayed" and "not decayed". Explain in a short paragraph where the argument's premises enter and what each family of solutions says.
hint: Treat the atom as the particle, the cat as the pointer, and ask which of the three claims each solution denies.
solution: The atom plays the role of the spin and the cat plays the role of the pointer: "decayed" leads to a dead cat and "not decayed" to a living cat. If the device-cat system evolves linearly (P2), the final state is an equal-amplitude entangled superposition of (decayed, dead) and (not decayed, alive). If the wave function is complete (P3), the cat is determinately neither dead nor alive. But we expect to find one or the other. A collapse theory denies linearity: the superposition is destroyed, at a rate that grows with the number of particles, so the cat is determinately in one state. A hidden-variable theory denies completeness: the particles' positions determine whether the cat is alive. A many-worlds theory keeps both and says that both outcomes occur in different branches.

@quiz num
prompt: An ideal device measures the $z$-spin of a particle in the state $\tfrac{3}{5}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \tfrac{4}{5}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$. By linear evolution, the final state is $\tfrac{3}{5}\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert\text{up}\rangle + \tfrac{4}{5}\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$. By the Born rule, what is the probability that the pointer is found reading "down"?
answer: 0.64
check: abs(4 / 5) ** 2
hint: The probability is the squared modulus of the amplitude of the term in which the pointer reads "down".
solution: The amplitude of $\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert\text{down}\rangle$ is $\tfrac45$, so the probability is $(4/5)^2 = 16/25 = 0.64$. Linear evolution fixes the amplitudes; the Born rule turns them into probabilities, and a solution to the measurement problem has to say what, in addition, picks out one outcome.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement about decoherence is correct?
choice: It shows that wave functions collapse when a macroscopic environment is present.
choice*: It explains why interference between macroscopically distinct pointer states is practically unobservable, but by itself does not select a single outcome.

solution: Decoherence makes the reduced state of the system look like a mixture, so interference is practically unobservable, but the global state remains an entangled superposition unless something else (collapse, hidden variables, many worlds) is added. @quiz self
prompt: "Decoherence solves the measurement problem." Evaluate this claim in a short paragraph, using the three-claim triad.
hint: Ask whether decoherence denies any of the three claims, and what it leaves unexplained.
solution: Decoherence uses only linear Schrödinger evolution and the completeness of the wave function, so it denies neither (A) nor (B). It shows that interference between pointer states is lost in practice and that certain bases are preferred. But it leaves the global state an entangled superposition, so unless the third claim is reinterpreted, there is still no determinate outcome. The claim is therefore true only if decoherence is combined with an interpretation: with collapse, it explains why collapse looks basis-specific; with hidden variables, it explains why the extra variables behave classically; with many worlds, it helps to define the branches. Taken alone, it explains the appearance of a mixture but not why one term occurs.
