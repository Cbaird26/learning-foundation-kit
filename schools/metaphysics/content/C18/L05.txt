@article
When you write with a pen, you do not notice the pen. You notice the words, the page, the thought. If the nib snags, the pen suddenly stands out as a thing: long, thin, scratched, uncooperative. Heidegger takes this ordinary shift as a clue to the structure of human existence and to what is wrong with a picture of the mind inherited from Descartes.

### Being-in-the-world

Descartes's picture begins with a subject, an inner mind with ideas, that must work out whether an outer world exists. Heidegger holds that this starts from the wrong place. In *Being and Time* he describes Dasein's basic constitution as **being-in-the-world**, a single phenomenon, not a subject plus a world added to it. The "in" does not mean being inside, as water is in a glass. It means dwelling, being engaged with and familiar with: the sense of "in" in "I am *in* love" or "in business" (an illustration commentators often use; Heidegger traces "in" to an old sense of dwelling). Heidegger adds that Dasein is also always **being-with** other Dasein, and that it is always already in some mood or attunement. On the skeptic's demand for a proof of the external world, Heidegger says that the real scandal is that people go on asking for one: the question presupposes a subject cut off from a world, which is what he disputes.

### Equipment, readiness-to-hand, presence-at-hand

What are the entities we deal with first? Not, Heidegger says, mere objects with properties but **equipment** (*Zeug*): things for something, such as hammers, pens, doors and kettles. A piece of equipment is what it is only within a **totality of involvements**: the hammer is for hammering, for fastening boards, for building a shed, for sheltering, and finally for the sake of some way of being of Dasein, which Heidegger calls the **for-the-sake-of-which**. Equipment is never a single isolated thing.

He distinguishes two ways of being:

| Mode | German | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ready-to-hand** | *zuhanden* | Equipment in use, unobtrusive, withdrawn into the task | The hammer in the hands of a carpenter absorbed in the work |
| **Present-at-hand** | *vorhanden* | An entity regarded as a bare object with properties, detached from use | The same hammer, examined as a thing of a certain weight and shape |

The more closely we use a tool, Heidegger says, the less it stands out as an object. And his claim is that the ready-to-hand is the primary way entities are encountered, while the present-at-hand is a derivative mode that arises when we change our stance to a theoretical one.

### Breakdown

When equipment fails, it shows itself differently. Heidegger gives three modes of **breakdown**:

- **Conspicuousness**: the tool is damaged or unsuitable, so it stands out (the snagging nib).
- **Obtrusiveness**: the tool is missing, so the task halts and the lack stands out (no pen on the desk).
- **Obstinacy**: something is in the way and will not cooperate (a box blocking the doorway).

In each, the thing is more like a present-at-hand object, and the surrounding totality of involvements becomes visible. Breakdown is the clue that equipment belonged to a web of practice that was invisible while all went well.

Heidegger applies the same view to space. Nearness is measured by what is useful, not by meters: the spectacles on your nose are, in a practical sense, farther from you than the person on the street whom you see through them. Space as measured by geometry is a derived abstraction.

### The argument against the Cartesian picture

| P1 | If entities are first encountered as present-at-hand objects, and uses are added to them afterward, then in skilled use of a tool we would first have to attend to the tool as an object with properties. |
| P2 | In skilled use of a tool, we do not attend to it as an object with properties; it withdraws from attention. |
| C | So entities are not first encountered as present-at-hand objects with uses added afterward. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). Interpreters differ on P2 and on what "attend" means. **Hubert Dreyfus** read Heidegger as describing a skilled, **non-conceptual coping** that needs no representation. An objector can deny P1: a Cartesian can say that what is present at first need not be attended to, since an implicit or unconscious representation might guide the action without being noticed. Another line of reply, from philosophers such as John McDowell, holds that absorbed skill is shaped by concepts even when we are not thinking about them. Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson have argued, in the debate about **knowing how**, that skilled competence is a species of propositional knowledge. If so, readiness-to-hand might not show a break with representation. The disagreement is partly about what the evidence from experience can show at all.

### Why it matters

The result is an alternative to the picture of the mind as an inner theater. Cognition becomes a way of being involved in a meaningful world, and detached observation becomes one attitude among others. This is one source of later embodied and situated approaches to cognition.

@example
title: Classifying three failures of a tool
problem: Classify each as conspicuousness, obtrusiveness or obstinacy: (a) A chef reaches for the whisk and finds that its wires are bent. (b) A cyclist cannot find the pump in the garage while the tire sags. (c) A parked cart is in the doorway while you are carrying shopping in.
step: (a) The tool is present but unfit: it stands out because it is damaged. This is conspicuousness.
step: (b) The tool is missing. The task halts and the missing item pushes itself forward. This is obtrusiveness.
step: (c) The thing is in the way and resists the task. This is obstinacy.
answer: (a) conspicuousness; (b) obtrusiveness; (c) obstinacy.

@example
title: Tracing a totality of involvements
problem: Trace the totality of involvements for a kettle, ending in a for-the-sake-of-which.
step: The kettle is for boiling water. Boiling water is for making tea.
step: Making tea is for offering a visitor a cup; offering a cup is for welcoming a guest.
step: Welcoming a guest is for the sake of being a certain kind of host: a way in which Dasein takes a stand on its own being. This is the for-the-sake-of-which.
step: At each link the item makes sense only through the next. The kettle is not first a metal object with a shape and only later "used" in this chain; in use, the chain is what the kettle is in.
answer: Kettle, boiling water, making tea, offering a cup, welcoming a guest, being a host: the host-role is the for-the-sake-of-which.

@mistakes
- Reading "being-in-the-world" as "a person located inside a room". The "in" is engaged dwelling.
- Calling the ready-to-hand merely "useful" in the everyday sense. It is equipment as encountered in absorbed activity, within a totality of involvements.
- Saying that Heidegger regards the present-at-hand as an illusion. He claims it is derivative, and that theory has a legitimate place.
- Treating breakdown as showing that tools are unreal. It shows how a normally invisible web of use appears when use fails.

prompt: Explain in four or five sentences one objection to the claim that skilled coping needs no conceptual content, and give a reply on Heidegger's behalf.

solution: One objection is that the carpenter's skill is knowledge of how to hammer well, and this may be knowledge of facts about the right way to do it that the carpenter cannot state.
