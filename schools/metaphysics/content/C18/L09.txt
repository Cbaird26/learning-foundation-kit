@article
Reach for a cup of tea while talking to a friend. You do not compute the cup's distance or give your arm instructions, and you do not need to look at your hand. Your body simply goes toward the cup. Maurice Merleau-Ponty argues in *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) that this ordinary skill shows something that two traditional pictures of perception miss: the body is not just an object we have, but the subject through which we have a world.

### Two rival pictures

The first picture is **empiricism**, taken in the sense of the classical theory of sensation: perception is built from simple, punctate sensations caused by stimuli, which are combined by association. The second is **intellectualism**: perception is an act of judgment or understanding that interprets raw data; Merleau-Ponty associates it with a Kantian tradition. Both treat the perceived world as a finished object that can be analyzed from outside, and they differ about whether the work is done by causes or by thought. Merleau-Ponty argues that both overlook the **lived perceptual field**, where things appear already organized, meaningful and bound up with our bodily possibilities.

### The argument from context

A reconstruction of one argument against atomistic empiricism uses the phenomena studied by Gestalt psychologists:

| P1 | If perception were a sum of punctate sensations, each fixed by the local stimulus at its point, then how a patch looks would depend only on the local stimulus, not on its surroundings. |
| P2 | How a patch looks depends on its surroundings: the same gray appears lighter on a dark ground than on a light one, and two equal lines in the Müller-Lyer figure appear unequal. |
| C | So perception is not a sum of punctate sensations each fixed by the local stimulus. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). An empiricist can reply by keeping sensations but adding that neural processes such as lateral interaction between neighboring regions modify them. Merleau-Ponty's point would then be that the structured whole, and not the atoms, has become the explanatory unit, which concedes his claim about organization while keeping a causal account.

### The lived body and motor intentionality

Merleau-Ponty distinguishes the **body as object**, which science studies and which can be seen from outside, from the **lived body** (*corps propre*, the body as one's own), which is the point of view from which I perceive and act. I can walk around a house but not around my own body; it is always with me, my means of access to things. He speaks of the **body schema**: a practical, pre-reflective sense of what my body can do and where it is, not an image I look at. In skilled action, the body shows **motor intentionality**, a directedness toward tasks that is a practical "I can" and not a thought that something is the case. This idea develops Husserl's notion of the "I can" and Heidegger's practical involvement (week 5).

**Habit** extends the body schema: the typist's fingers find the keys without searching, and a blind person's stick becomes part of how they sense the pavement, no longer an object at the end of the hand. The **phantom limb** is a case where neither a purely physiological account (a nerve disturbance) nor a purely psychological one (a memory) seems enough: the person remains oriented to a world of tasks that the missing limb would perform. Merleau-Ponty reads it as a failure of the body to give up its habitual way of being in the world.

### Schneider and the argument against intellectualism

In the clinical literature on a patient called Schneider (studied by the psychologists Adhémar Gelb and Kurt Goldstein), a man with a brain injury could do **concrete movements**, such as reaching to the spot where he had been stung, but could not do **abstract movements**, such as pointing to a named part of his body on command or miming an action with no object. Merleau-Ponty argues:

| P1 | If concrete grasping and abstract pointing were both applications of one capacity for objective representation of one's body, then a person who retained one would retain the other. |
| P2 | As reported in the clinical literature, Schneider retained concrete movement but lost abstract movement. |
| C | So concrete and abstract movement do not both depend on one capacity for objective representation. |

The argument is valid. His interpretation is that concrete movement rests on the body schema and motor intentionality, whereas abstract movement requires projecting a neutral, objective space. The interpretation can be questioned. The clinical details of such cases have been revisited by later researchers, and a cognitive scientist can reply that the case shows two systems that both involve representations. The two-visual-systems hypothesis of David Milner and Melvyn Goodale, in which vision for action and vision for report dissociate, is cited both as support for Merleau-Ponty and as a representational alternative. The disagreement is whether the dissociation shows a non-representational body or two kinds of representation.

### The primacy of perception and the incomplete reduction

Merleau-Ponty holds that perception is the primary contact with the world, which science and reflection presuppose. He also adopts and revises Husserl's method. Describing the lived world, he says, we cannot bracket our embodied situation, so a complete reduction is impossible; its chief lesson is that it can never be completed. In the book's final part he also criticizes Sartre's picture of an absolute freedom: our choices are shaped by habits and a sedimented situation. His work influenced later embodied and enactive approaches to cognition and critics of the representational picture, though the interpretation of his claims remains disputed.

@example
title: Sorting abstract and concrete movements
problem: In the Schneider literature, which of these would a patient like Schneider be expected to do, and which not? (a) Swat a mosquito that is biting his arm. (b) Point on command to his left elbow. (c) Take a handkerchief from a pocket and blow his nose. (d) Mime hammering a nail when there is no hammer.
step: Concrete movements are tied to a real situation and a familiar task. (a) and (c) fit: the patient can do them.
step: Abstract movements are performed on command or for show, with no situation that calls for them. (b) and (d) fit: the patient cannot do them.
step: The contrast suggests that what is lost is the ability to project a neutral, merely possible space of movement, while the practical body schema remains.
answer: He can do (a) and (c); he cannot do (b) and (d).

@example
title: Testing the argument from context
problem: Someone replies: "Neighboring regions of the retina interact, which explains why a gray patch looks lighter on a dark ground. So the sensation-based theory survives." Which premise of the argument from context does this challenge, and what is left of Merleau-Ponty's point?
step: The argument has P1 (if perception were a sum of local sensations, context would not matter) and P2 (context matters). The reply accepts P2.
step: The reply denies that P1 follows from the sensation theory once the theory is enriched with interactions among neighboring regions. It targets the theory P1 describes: not the strictly atomistic theory, but one with relations built in.
step: Merleau-Ponty's point can be restated: the atomistic theory is refuted, and what explains perception is a structured whole. Whether that supports his positive account of a lived perceptual field is a further question.
answer: The reply changes the target of P1 from a strictly atomistic theory to an enriched one. The refutation of the atomistic version stands, and the dispute moves to what the enriched version shows.

@mistakes
- Treating the lived body as the physical body studied by anatomy. The lived body is the body as the perspective of perceiving and acting.
- Reading motor intentionality as unconscious reasoning. It is a bodily directedness toward tasks, not an inference.
- Identifying Merleau-Ponty with Husserl's transcendental idealism. He insists that a complete reduction is impossible.
- Presenting the Schneider case as settled evidence. The argument's logic is valid, but its clinical interpretation has been debated.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare Merleau-Ponty's incomplete reduction with Husserl's epoché in five or six sentences. What does each aim at, and why does Merleau-Ponty think the aim cannot be fully achieved?
hint: Consider what the describer's body does to bracketing.
solution: Husserl's epoché suspends the natural attitude's belief in the world so that experience can be described as it is given. Merleau-Ponty adopts the aim of describing the lived world but says that complete bracketing cannot be achieved, since the describer is embodied and situated, and no standpoint is outside that situation. The lesson of the reduction is then the impossibility of a complete reduction: it reveals our embeddedness. A Husserlian can reply that the epoché is an ideal that is approached in stages, and that incompleteness does not make it pointless. The two agree on description, but differ on whether a transcendental standpoint is available. I think that Merleau-Ponty's point shows a limit, not a failure of the method.
