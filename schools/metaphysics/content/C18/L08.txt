@article
Human beings are free, and they are also vulnerable: they are the source of meaning in their lives, yet they have bodies that age, can be injured and die; they act on the world and are acted on by others. Simone de Beauvoir calls this condition **ambiguity** and argues that the task of ethics is not to resolve it but to live it honestly. Her *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) and *The Second Sex* (1949) draw on Sartre's existentialism and on phenomenological ideas of the body and situation, but they develop an ethics and a social analysis that go beyond his earlier work.

### Ambiguity and its evasions

In her view, a human being is at once a **subject** (a free consciousness that gives meaning to things) and an **object** (a being in a world, seen and used by others, with a body and a limited time). Many attempts to live well try to escape one side of this. She describes characteristic types:

| Type | The evasion |
|---|---|
| The **sub-man** | Retreats from freedom and wills nothing, taking life as it comes |
| The **serious man** | Accepts ready-made values (the party, the nation, success) as absolutes and gives up his own freedom to them |
| The **nihilist** | Sees that values are not given and concludes that nothing matters, so denies all value |
| The **adventurer** | Loves action for its own sake and is indifferent to its ends |
| The **passionate man** | Makes a single object of love absolute, in a way that removes it from the open future |

Against these, she proposes an attitude that accepts that there are no absolute given values and that freedom must still choose, taking responsibility for ends and keeping them open to revision.

### Willing oneself free and willing others free

Sartre's early work depicts the encounter with another person as a conflict, in which the other's look turns me into an object. Beauvoir argues instead that freedom has an ethical structure. Reconstructed in standard form:

| P1 | Willing oneself free is willing one's freedom as an open source of meaning for the future. |
| P2 | Whoever wills an end wills its necessary conditions and does not act to destroy them. |
| P3 | Freedom can be an open source of meaning only if other free beings are able to take up and extend what one does. |
| P4 | Oppressing others prevents them from being able to take up and extend what one does. |
| C | So whoever wills himself free does not oppress others. |

The argument is valid. Interpreters present it in different ways, and this is one reconstruction. The most pressed objection targets P3: a person's projects might have meaning for him without any other free beings, or even through the labor of unfree people. Beauvoir's reply is that a freedom that needs others to be unfree has made its meaning depend on being merely a thing for them, so it fails to will itself as open. Whether that reply works depends on how much "open source of meaning" requires.

### The Second Sex

The Second Sex has two volumes, "Facts and Myths" and "Lived Experience". Beauvoir examines biology, psychoanalysis and historical materialism, then history, and then myths of the feminine, before turning to the stages of a woman's life. Her central category is the **Other**. Man has been taken as the Subject, the neutral and essential, and woman defined in relation to him, as the Other, secondary and inessential. This is not peculiar to women: Beauvoir notes that other groups, such as ethnic groups, have been treated as Other, but she remarks that there is a particular lack of reciprocity in the case of women.

She distinguishes **transcendence**, projects that go beyond the given into the future, from **immanence**, being confined to repeating life and maintaining it. Her claim is that women's situation, shaped by law, education, economic dependence and expectation, has tended to confine them to immanence. A reconstruction:

| P1 | Women have been defined as the Other relative to men and denied equal access to projects in the world (law, work, education, status). |
| P2 | A group so defined and so denied is held in immanence by that definition and denial. |
| P3 | Immanence imposed by others on a freedom is oppression. |
| C | So women's situation has been one of oppression. |

The argument is valid. Objections test each premise. P1 is a thesis about history that varies across societies and periods, and a critic can say that in a particular society women have equal access, which denies P1 for that society. A critic of P3 might say that some immanence is chosen. Beauvoir's answer is that she treats choice within an imposed situation as constrained. She also discusses women's **complicity**: the temptation to accept the security of immanence. Later feminist writers argued that Beauvoir's category of "woman" hides differences of class, race and sexuality.

Her famous formula, which paraphrased says that one becomes a woman and is not born one, expresses the claim that the ways of being that are called feminine are products of a situation, of education and expectation, not a fixed female nature. She does not deny biology: she examines it at length and says it does not settle what a woman must be. Later feminist theorists disagree about whether she anticipates the distinction between sex and gender; some read her as a source of it, others argue that she does not use that distinction and that her view should not be reduced to it.

### Existence precedes essence, applied

The slogan from Sartre (week 7) says that humans first exist and then define themselves. Beauvoir uses it to resist the idea of a fixed essence of woman, an "eternal feminine". If no human has a prior essence, then "woman" is not a nature that determines what any woman is or must do. But Beauvoir adds an emphasis that Sartre's early work lacks: freedom is exercised in a **situation**, and social structures can narrow it.

@example
title: Locating a challenge to the argument about oppression
problem: A critic says: "In Norland, women and men have had the same legal rights and access to schooling for a century. So the claim that women are denied equal access to projects does not hold there." Which premise does this challenge, and does it make the argument invalid?
step: The argument has P1 (defined as Other and denied equal access), P2 (such a group is held in immanence), P3 (imposed immanence is oppression), and C.
step: The critic denies that women in Norland are denied equal access. That challenges P1 for that society.
step: Validity is unaffected: if the three premises were true of a group, the conclusion would follow. The critic's point limits the argument to societies where P1 is true.
step: Beauvoir could reply that formal equality does not remove expectations and myths that continue to define women as Other, which would require evidence about the society in question.
answer: The critic challenges P1 for Norland. The argument remains valid, and the dispute is about the facts.

@mistakes
- Treating Beauvoir as simply repeating Sartre. She develops a reciprocal ethics and a theory of situation that go beyond his early view.
- Reading her claim that one becomes a woman rather than being born one as denying that there are bodies or sexes. She discusses biology at length and denies that it settles what a woman must be.
- Treating "Other" as meaning "foreign". In her usage, the Other is the inessential, relative to the Subject taken as essential.
- Saying that the ethics of ambiguity resolves the tension between freedom and facticity. It asks us to live it honestly.

prompt: Explain Sartre's claim that existence precedes essence in about 150 words, using the paper-knife contrast and one example of your own of how a person might define themselves by action. Say what the claim does not deny.
hint: Contrast an artifact whose purpose is fixed before it exists with a person who has no such prior purpose.
solution: A paper-knife is made by someone who first conceives its purpose and form, so its essence precedes its existence. Sartre says that for human beings there is no such prior plan: a person first exists and then becomes what she is through her choices. For example, a young woman who has no fixed destiny might become a doctor by committing to training and care, and what she is, in Sartre's sense, is shown in what she does and chooses. The claim does not deny that she has a body, a history, a language and a shared human condition: these are her facticity. What it denies is a fixed nature that settles in advance what she must be or value. A theist could reject the premise that no mind conceived humans; Sartre treats that as the assumption of atheistic existentialism.
