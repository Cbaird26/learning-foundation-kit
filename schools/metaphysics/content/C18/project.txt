## Course review: phenomenology, existentialism and process philosophy

### Key ideas by week

1. **Brentano**: intentionality, or directedness toward an object, marks the mental; thoughts about nonexistent things make it puzzling. Responses: immanent objects, Meinong's nonexistent objects, content or sense.
2. **Husserl's method**: the natural attitude, the epoché (suspension, not doubt), the phenomenological and eidetic reductions, noesis and noema.
3. **Husserl on time and the lifeworld**: primal impression, retention, protention; retention is not recollection. The lifeworld is the source of the sense of science; objectivism forgets it.
4. **Heidegger on being**: the ontological difference, Dasein, the hermeneutic circle.
5. **Heidegger on equipment**: being-in-the-world, ready-to-hand and present-at-hand, three modes of breakdown, totality of involvements.
6. **Heidegger on time and death**: care, temporality, anxiety, the they, authenticity, being-toward-death.
7. **Sartre**: in-itself and for-itself, nothingness, freedom, facticity, bad faith, existence precedes essence.
8. **Beauvoir**: ambiguity, willing oneself free and others free, woman as Other, situation.
9. **Merleau-Ponty**: the lived body, body schema, motor intentionality, Schneider (a case whose clinical interpretation is debated), the incomplete reduction.
10. **Bergson**: duration versus clock time, spatialization, the free will argument, the 1922 debate.
11. **Whitehead**: actual occasions, prehension, concrescence, societies, misplaced concreteness.
12. **Dialogue**: perdurantism and process ontology, Carnap's criticism of Heidegger, what each tradition can learn.

### Key arguments and distinctions

- Intentionality and nonexistence: the modus tollens argument that directedness is not always a relation, and which premise each response denies.
- Husserl's argument for the epoché (denied at the premise that presuppositionless description is attainable), the argument from melody to retention, and the argument against objectivism.
- Heidegger's argument for beginning with Dasein, the argument against the Cartesian priority of the present-at-hand, and the argument for being-toward-death (denied by Sartre at the premise that the end can be grasped as a whole).
- Sartre's argument for freedom (determinists deny that taking a stand is undetermined) and for existence preceding essence (theists deny the second premise).
- Beauvoir's arguments about freedom and others and about oppression.
- Merleau-Ponty's argument from context and from Schneider.
- Bergson's arguments about clock time and free will.
- Whitehead's argument from misplaced concreteness.
- Carnap's argument against Heidegger, with replies.
- Distinctions: retention versus recollection; fear versus anxiety; sex versus situation in Beauvoir; perdurantism versus process ontology.

### Self-check

- Can you state each argument in standard form, add the hidden premise, and name the premise its best critic denies?
- Can you explain each technical term in plain English, with an example?
- Can you say, for each thinker, what the view is and what it does not claim?
- Can you describe an experience using the phenomenological method?
- Can you explain readiness-to-hand with three examples?
- Can you compare duration with clock time and process with substance views?

### Common errors

- Treating the epoché as doubt, retention as memory, or protention as prediction.
- Identifying Dasein with the Cartesian mind, or authenticity with moral praise.
- Treating bad faith as an ordinary lie, or existence precedes essence as denying bodies and histories.
- Taking the Einstein debate as a refutation of special relativity, or the share of philosophers who hold a view as evidence for it.

@exam self
prompt: Compare two of Husserl, Heidegger and Bergson on time, in a short essay of about 400 words. For each, state the central thesis, reconstruct one argument in standard form that is valid as stated, name the premise a critic would most plausibly deny, and say what the account of time captures that the other leaves out.
hint: Choose two accounts with a clear contrast, such as Husserl's retention and Bergson's duration, and use the lesson's reconstructed arguments.
solution: Husserl and Bergson both start from lived time. Husserl argues that we hear melodies as unities of successive tones (P1); that hearing a unity of succession requires, at each phase, consciousness of the present tone and of the just-past tones as just past (P2); that this consciousness is neither inference nor recollection (P3); and that any such consciousness that is part of present perceiving is retention (P4); so there is retention. An extensionalist would not so much deny a premise as reject the retentionalist reading of what P4 calls retention, and a snapshot theorist would deny P2. Bergson argues that clock time is a series of homogeneous, juxtaposed instants (P1); that duration is a flow in which each moment carries the past and never recurs (P2); and that a series of juxtaposed instants lacks interpenetration and unrepeatability (P3); so clock time omits what is essential to duration. A critic would deny that duration has the character P2 claims, saying lived time may be explained by processes that clocks measure. Husserl's account is more precise about the structure of the present and fits into a method of description, while Bergson's makes a broader claim about spatialization and creativity but is less precise about structure. Each leaves open how lived time relates to the time of physics.
