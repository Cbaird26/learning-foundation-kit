@article
You arrive at a cafe expecting to meet Pierre. He is not there. Your experience is not a mere lack of experience of Pierre: the cafe is organized around his absence, and the empty chair is full of it. Sartre takes this as a sign that consciousness brings a peculiar kind of "nothing" into the world, and that this fact shapes what a human being is. *Being and Nothingness* (1943), subtitled "An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology", builds on Husserl's intentionality and Heidegger's analysis of existence while departing from both.

### Two kinds of being

Sartre divides being into two regions. **Being-in-itself** (*être-en-soi*) is the being of things: solid, complete, simply what it is. A stone does not question itself or lack anything. **Being-for-itself** (*être-pour-soi*) is the being of consciousness. Consciousness is intentional, always consciousness *of* something other than itself, and it has no content of its own that makes it a thing. It is also aware of itself in a non-reflective way: when I count cigarettes, I am aware of counting without making counting an object. Sartre says in a deliberately paradoxical formula that the for-itself is what it is not and is not what it is: it is never simply identical with its past, its role or its character, because it is always able to take up a stand toward them.

### Nothingness and negation

The cafe case illustrates **nothingness** (*néant*). A negative fact such as "Pierre is not here" is not a thing in the world, yet we perceive absences and make negative judgments. Sartre argues that this requires a being that can step back from what is given and question it, and that being is consciousness. Sartre concludes that human reality is the being through which nothingness comes into the world. Consciousness is not a nothing in the sense of being empty or unreal; it is nothing in the sense that it is no thing, and it **nihilates** (holds at a distance) what is given.

### Freedom, facticity and anguish

If consciousness always stands back from its situation, then it is free: freedom is not a property that consciousness has but its very structure. Sartre's central argument for this can be set out as follows:

| P1 | Consciousness is never identical with any of its contents or with its situation; it can always take a stand toward them by negating, interpreting or ignoring them. |
| P2 | A being that can always take a stand toward every feature of its situation is not determined by those features alone to respond in any one way. |
| P3 | A being that is not determined by its situation to respond in any one way is free. |
| C | So consciousness is free. |

The argument is valid. A determinist denies P2: taking a stand toward one's situation can itself be the product of causes, such as upbringing and brain states, so the ability to take a stand does not show that the stand is undetermined. Sartre's reply is that no cause has its significance except through the stand taken toward it: a hard childhood is a motive only for someone who sees it as one. Critics respond that this describes how causes figure in experience, not whether they act. Note that Sartre's freedom is freedom to choose one's attitude and project; it is not the power to get whatever one wants. Sartre's own example is the prisoner: he is not free to be free of prison, but he is always free to try to escape, that is, to choose his project within the situation, and so also how to take his imprisonment.

Freedom is always **situated**. **Facticity** is the given aspect of one's situation: one's body, birthplace, past, and the fact that others see one in certain ways. **Transcendence** is the going beyond the given toward possibilities. A person is both: not fixed by facts, not free of them. **Anguish** is the consciousness of one's freedom, as in the vertigo of a person on a narrow path who dreads not so much falling as the possibility of throwing themselves off. It differs from fear because its object is oneself and one's own possible choices.

### Bad faith

**Bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*) is the attempt to escape freedom by lying to oneself. It takes two main forms: treating oneself as pure facticity ("I am just a person who cannot change") or as pure transcendence, as if one's past and body had no weight. Sartre's example is the waiter who plays at being a waiter with exaggerated precision, as if his being were a fixed essence like a thing's, not a role he continues to take up. Bad faith is not an ordinary lie: a liar knows the truth and misleads another, whereas in bad faith one person is both deceiver and deceived. That raises a paradox about self-deception. Sartre rejects the Freudian unconscious as a solution, since a censor that selects what to repress must know what is repressed, and he appeals to the consciousness that is not what it is. Many critics find the account of self-deception incomplete, and the paradox has a large literature.

### Existence precedes essence

The slogan comes from Sartre's 1945 lecture, published in 1946 as *Existentialism Is a Humanism*. Take a paper-knife: an artisan conceives its purpose and form before making it, so its essence (what it is for and how it is made) precedes its existence. Sartre says that for human beings the order is reversed: first one exists, turns up in the world, and only afterward defines oneself through what one does. There is no human nature that a designer fixed in advance:

| P1 | A thing has an essence prior to its existence only if some mind conceived what it was to be before it was made. |
| P2 | No mind conceived what human beings were to be before they existed. |
| C | So human beings have no essence prior to their existence. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). P2 is contested: Sartre states it as the assumption of atheistic existentialism and notes that some existentialists are Christians, who would deny it. P1 can also be denied by those who hold that a natural kind, such as a species, has an essence without any designer. Sartre does not deny that there is a human condition: we are all born, must act and die. What he denies is a fixed nature that settles what any person must be.

@example
title: Facticity, transcendence and bad faith
problem: Marta says: "I am a shy person. It is just how I am. I could never speak up at a meeting, so there is no point trying." Analyze her statement in Sartre's terms.
step: She states her character as a fixed fact, like the properties of a stone. That treats herself as being-in-itself.
step: But she is a for-itself: she can take a stand toward her past shyness, interpret it and act otherwise, even if doing so is hard. Her past is her facticity, not her destiny.
step: By presenting her past as a settled essence that excludes choice, she flees the freedom she has. That is bad faith in its first form, treating oneself as pure facticity.
step: Sartre would not say that she can easily change or that her shyness is not real. He says that it is not a fixed essence that rules out choice.
answer: Marta is in bad faith by treating herself as pure facticity, though her situation is real and not easy to change.

@example
title: Locating an objection to the argument for freedom
problem: A psychologist says: "Everything a person does has causes in genes and upbringing, so the ability to take a stand does not show that the stand is free." Which premise does this challenge, and how might Sartre reply?
step: The argument has P1 (consciousness can always take a stand), P2 (such a being is not determined by its situation alone), P3 (such a being is free).
step: The psychologist grants P1 but denies that taking a stand is undetermined. That denies P2.
step: Sartre's reply is that causes figure in a person's life only through the significance the person gives them: the same childhood motivates one person and not another because they take it differently.
step: The psychologist can answer that how a person takes a childhood is itself caused. The disagreement turns on whether the stand-taking is a further cause or the point where causal explanation gives out.
answer: The objection targets P2. Sartre's reply points to the role of significance, and the dispute remains open.

@mistakes
- Saying that Sartre denies all constraints on a person. Facticity is real; freedom is situated.
- Reading "nothingness" as the claim that consciousness does not exist. It means that consciousness is no thing and can negate what is given.
- Treating bad faith as an ordinary lie to another person.
- Taking "existence precedes essence" to deny that people have bodies, histories or a shared condition. It denies a prior fixed nature that settles what one must be.
