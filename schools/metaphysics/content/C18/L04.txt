@article
Many philosophers ask what exists: numbers, minds, God, tables, events. Heidegger asks something that, he thinks, comes before: what do we understand when we say that anything *is*? We use the word "is" constantly and understand it well enough to get through a day, but when we try to say what it means, we find that we cannot. Martin Heidegger's *Being and Time* (1927) begins with the claim that this is the forgotten question, and that philosophy has not yet properly asked it.

### The structure of the question

In its opening pages, Heidegger analyzes any act of questioning into three elements:

| Element | German | In the question of being |
|---|---|---|
| What is asked about | *das Gefragte* | Being |
| What is interrogated | *das Befragte* | Beings: the entities through which being shows itself |
| What is to be found out | *das Erfragte* | The sense or meaning of being |

He gives reasons why the question has been neglected. Three prejudices, he says, make it seem unnecessary: that being is the most universal concept, so it needs no further inquiry; that it cannot be defined, so there is nothing to ask; and that it is self-evident, since everyone understands "is". Heidegger replies that the universality of being is not that of a highest genus (a genus must be marked off by differences, and being is not a kind of thing), that indefinability invites inquiry rather than forbidding it, and that its supposed self-evidence conceals our ignorance of what we are using.

### The ontological difference

The question depends on what is now called the **ontological difference**: being (*Sein*) is not itself a being (*Seiendes*, an entity). Trees, numbers and atoms are beings. That they *are*, and in what manner each is, is their being. To explain that being by pointing to another being (say, God as the highest being) would only add one more entity to the list. Heidegger's thesis is that being is always the being of beings, so the inquiry must go through beings; but it asks about what makes them intelligible as beings, not about which ones there are. Compare Quine's criterion of ontological commitment, on which to be is to be the value of a bound variable: Quine tells us how to find out what a theory says exists, but not what understanding of being is at work when we reason with a quantifier. Heidegger would say that a criterion presupposes such an understanding. Analytic metaphysicians reply that the quantifier gives all the meaning "exists" needs.

### Dasein

If the inquiry must go through some being, which one? Heidegger's answer is the one that each of us is, which he calls **Dasein** (German for "being there", used in ordinary German for existence). Dasein is the entity for whom its own being is an issue and which already has some understanding of being. Heidegger avoids "subject", "consciousness" and "human being" to avoid carrying over the Cartesian picture of a mind facing a world. Dasein's way of being is called **existence**: Dasein is not a fixed thing with properties but a being that is its possibilities, taking a stand on how to be. Its being is **in each case mine** (*Jemeinigkeit*). The structures of Dasein's being are **existentials**, in contrast to the **categories** that fit non-Dasein entities.

The argument for beginning with Dasein may be reconstructed:

| P1 | Any inquiry into the meaning of being must start from an understanding of being that the inquirer already has. |
| P2 | The understanding of being belongs to the way of being of Dasein, which is the inquirer. |
| P3 | An inquiry that must start from something belonging to the inquirer's way of being must begin with an analysis of that way of being. |
| C | So the inquiry into the meaning of being must begin with an analysis of Dasein. |

The argument is valid. Heidegger accepts the **hermeneutic circle** that P1 suggests: we interpret what we already understand in a vague way, and the aim is to enter the circle rightly, not to escape it. Objectors say that the circle is vicious: if the inquiry starts with a pre-understanding, it may only extract its own assumptions. Heidegger replies that there is no assumption-free starting point, and that the pre-understanding is revised in the course of the analysis. A second objection denies P3: that "being" is something we understand does not show that the analysis should start with the human, and the move from the question of being to an analysis of ourselves looks anthropocentric. Heidegger's reply is that Dasein is not privileged as a subject but as the site where being is disclosed, and he says his analysis is preparatory for the question of being. Whether the reply works is much discussed. A third criticism, made by Rudolf Carnap in 1932, is that the sentences of Heidegger's later lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" (1929) are pseudo-statements. We return to this in week 12.

### Phenomenology and time

Heidegger uses Husserl's method but changes it. A phenomenon, for him, is what shows itself from itself, and the aim is to make visible what usually stays hidden because it is too close. His procedure is **hermeneutic** (interpretive) and begins with Dasein as it is in ordinary **average everydayness**, not with a transcendental ego. The title announces the thesis that time is the horizon for understanding being. *Being and Time* was published as an unfinished work: only the first two divisions of the planned first part appeared. Week 6 takes up time.

@example
title: Beings versus being
problem: Sort each question into a question about beings or a question about being: (a) "How many bridges cross the river?" (b) "What is it for a bridge to be, as opposed to being merely imagined?" (c) "Is the bridge made of steel?" (d) "What do we understand when we say that something is?"
step: (a) and (c) ask what beings there are and what they are like, which are questions that science or counting can in principle answer. They are about beings.
step: (b) and (d) ask what it means for anything to be, or what understanding of "is" we use. They are questions about being.
step: Note that (b) and (d) still pass through beings (a bridge is the example), since being is always the being of beings. They do not ask which beings there are.
answer: (a) and (c) concern beings; (b) and (d) concern being.

@example
title: Testing the argument for starting with Dasein
problem: Someone says: "We understand being only because we speak a language, and many animals and machines respond to their surroundings without any such understanding. But that shows only that people have an understanding of being; it does not show that the inquiry must begin with an analysis of ourselves." Which premise does this target, and does it make the argument invalid?
step: The argument is P1 (the inquiry starts from a pre-understanding), P2 (this understanding belongs to the inquirer's way of being), P3 (such an inquiry must analyze that way of being), C.
step: The remark accepts that we have such an understanding but denies that this requires starting with ourselves. That targets P3, which says an inquiry resting on the inquirer's way of being must analyze it.
step: Validity is untouched, since from P1, P2 and P3 the conclusion follows. The objector must show P3 false, for example by a route to being that does not pass through the inquirer's own understanding.
answer: The remark targets P3. The argument is still valid, so the objector must deny a premise.

@mistakes
- Treating being as a very general kind of thing, a highest being. Heidegger insists that being is not itself a being.
- Identifying Dasein with the human body, or with the Cartesian mind. It is the way of being of the entity that we are, characterized by understanding being and by existing as possibilities.
- Reading the hermeneutic circle as a plain logical fallacy. Heidegger holds that understanding is circular and that the circle should be entered correctly, and critics are free to say it is still vicious.
- Saying that Heidegger answers the question of being in the first chapter. The book begins by posing it and starts the preparatory analysis.

prompt: In about 120 words, explain Husserl's retention and protention and give an example from your own experience that is not a melody (for example, catching a thrown ball or listening to a sentence). Say which phase is retention and which is protention.
hint: Retention keeps the just-past in the present perceiving; protention is the forward-leaning openness.
solution: In hearing someone say "I'll meet you at the cafe on the corner of ..." I hear the last word with the earlier words still held as just-past in the hearing; this is retention, which is why the sentence makes sense as I hear it and is not something I reconstruct afterward. At the same time I am open to a street name that will complete the sentence; that forward-leaning openness is protention, and it shows when a surprising word frustrates it. The word now being said is the primal impression. Recollection would be a different act, such as later recalling the conversation. Retention and protention are structures of the present itself, not separate memories or predictions.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare Heidegger's question of being with Quine's criterion of ontological commitment in four or five sentences. What does each ask, and why might a defender of each say the other's question comes second?
hint: Quine asks what a theory says exists; Heidegger asks what we understand by being.
solution: Quine's criterion says that a theory is committed to whatever must exist for its sentences to be true, as shown by the values of its bound variables. It tells us how to read off the ontology of a theory. Heidegger asks about the sense of being that we already understand when we use "is" and quantify at all, and he says a criterion presupposes this. A Heideggerian says the meaning question comes first because the criterion rests on it. A Quinean says the criterion is all the meaning "exists" requires, so the further question is either answered by the quantifier or is not clear enough to be asked. They disagree about what the inquiry into being should be.
