@article
Look at a blue bowl on a shelf. You take it to be a real bowl, there whether or not anyone looks, made of clay, with a back you cannot see. Almost all of life, and all of natural science, proceeds on assumptions of this kind. Husserl asks a different question: what is it for the bowl to *appear* to you as real, three-dimensional and partly hidden? To answer, he thinks, you must first stop using the assumptions as a background and start describing them.

### From the Logical Investigations to the epoché

Edmund Husserl's *Logical Investigations* (1900–01) argued against **psychologism**, the view that the laws of logic are laws about how people actually think. Logic, he said, concerns ideal meanings, not mental events; but meanings are still given in experience, and we need a careful description of how. The slogan "to the things themselves" calls for this: begin from how matters show themselves, not from theory. In *Ideas I* (1913), Husserl developed a method for such description. Its central tool is the **epoché**, a Greek word for suspension of judgment taken from ancient skeptics.

### The natural attitude and its suspension

Husserl calls our ordinary orientation the **natural attitude**. In it we take the world to exist independently, out there, with ourselves as things within it. He calls this assumption the "general thesis" of the natural attitude. The **epoché** is the deliberate suspension, or **bracketing**, of that thesis. You neither affirm nor deny that the bowl exists; you set the question aside and attend to the bowl as experienced. This is not Cartesian doubt. Descartes doubted in order to find a certain foundation and in doing so treated the world as possibly false. Husserl does not deny the world: he refrains from using the assumption of its existence in his description, and he leaves the world's existence as it was. Bracketing also covers scientific theories: the description of how a bowl looks may not borrow from optics or neuroscience.

Husserl distinguishes two reductions (a "reduction" is a leading back to the experience in which something is given):

- The **phenomenological** (or transcendental) **reduction**, produced by the epoché, redirects attention from the things in the world to the experiences in which they are given.
- The **eidetic reduction** moves from a particular case to its **essence** (Greek *eidos*). By **free imaginative variation**, you alter features of an example in thought and ask which changes make it cease to be an example of the kind; what survives all variations is essential. Varying a perceived bowl, you can imagine other shapes and colors, but a visible bowl always shows itself in perspectives, as having sides not now seen. That structure is essential to spatial things perceived, not to bowls in particular.

### Noesis and noema

Husserl takes **intentionality** from Brentano but redescribes it. Every experience has two inseparable aspects. The **noesis** is the act side: the perceiving, remembering or judging. The **noema** is the object as it is meant in that act: the bowl *as perceived*, with its sense and its manner of presentation. Husserl noted that the bowl itself can crack or burn, but the bowl-as-perceived cannot. How to read the noema is disputed. Some interpreters, following Dagfinn Føllesdal, read it as akin to Frege's sense, an abstract content that determines the object; others, such as Robert Sokolowski, read it as the object itself as it is given. The disagreement matters for whether Husserl's account is a theory of mediating contents or of direct presence.

### The argument for the epoché

Why take the epoché at all? A reconstruction of Husserl's methodological argument:

| P1 | A description of experience that is free of presuppositions must not rely on assumptions about whether the objects of experience exist. |
| P2 | The natural attitude relies on the assumption that the world exists independently of experience. |
| P3 | Philosophical description of experience should aim to be free of presuppositions, and so should not rely on anything that a presupposition-free description must not rely on. |
| C | So, in describing experience, one should not rely on the natural attitude's assumption that the world exists independently. |

The argument is valid. P1 and P3 are the contentious ones. Against P3, Maurice Merleau-Ponty argued in the 1940s that a complete reduction is impossible: the describer is always already embodied and in the world, so the most instructive lesson of the reduction is its incompleteness. Martin Heidegger argued that understanding always proceeds from some prior interpretation; he took description itself to be interpretive. A Husserlian reply is that the aim is regulative: we can reduce presuppositions step by step and test the rest. A further worry concerns the limits of the method rather than any premise: even if we describe how things are presented, we may need to know when the presentation is accurate, and the epoché cannot say.

### Why it matters

The method is a discipline of attention. It trains you to describe the structure of experience, not to explain it by causes. Many later movements (existential, hermeneutic, feminist and embodied approaches to mind) borrow the descriptive style while rejecting Husserl's transcendental commitments.

@example
title: Describing the experience of hearing a doorbell
problem: Use the steps of the phenomenological method to describe your experience of hearing a doorbell ring while you are reading.
step: Name the experience in the natural attitude: "A doorbell rang, caused by a visitor pressing a button." This already contains causal and existence claims.
step: Perform the epoché. Set aside whether there is a visitor, how sound travels, and what the doorbell is made of. Attend only to how the ringing is given.
step: Describe the noesis and the noema. The act: a sudden orienting of attention away from the page, with a slight tension. The noema: a ringing, as coming from the direction of the hall, as the sort of sound that signals someone at the door, and as ending.
step: Vary the case. If the sound had no direction, would it still be a doorbell? Perhaps not as a ring "at the door". A sound is given with a direction and duration, which is what survives the variation.
answer: The description records the act, the object-as-meant (with its sense as a signal) and its essential features, without asserting the existence of the visitor.

@example
title: Reconstructing and testing the argument for the epoché
problem: Which premise of the argument for the epoché does the claim "no description can be presuppositionless, since the describer is always already situated in a world" attack, and is the argument still valid?
step: The argument is P1 (presupposition-free description avoids existence assumptions), P2 (the natural attitude makes one), P3 (philosophy should aim at presupposition-free description), C (so describe without relying on that assumption).
step: Validity: from P1 and P2, relying on the natural attitude's assumption conflicts with presupposition-free description. P3 says we should aim at the latter, so we should not rely on the assumption. Nothing is missing.
step: The objection says presuppositionless description is unattainable. That attacks P3, because an aim can be reasonable only if it can in some measure be pursued. P1 and P2 are not touched.
answer: The objection attacks P3 (and a defender would reply that the aim is regulative, pursued by degrees). The argument stays valid.

@mistakes
- Treating the epoché as doubt or denial of the world. It suspends the use of the assumption without taking a view on its truth.
- Identifying the noema with a mental image or with the physical object. It is the object as meant: the bowl-as-perceived.
- Thinking that the eidetic reduction is a generalization from many observed cases. Husserl's variation is carried out in imagination and seeks what cannot be varied away.
- Taking phenomenology to be introspective psychology. It aims at the essential structures of experience, not at reports of what particular people feel on particular days.

prompt: In the eidetic reduction, a philosopher imagines varying a particular tune: made faster, louder, in another key, played on another instrument. All these remain the same tune. Imagining the pitches in a different order does not. What is the philosopher learning?

@quiz self
prompt: Compare Cartesian doubt and the Husserlian epoché in four or five sentences: what is each for, and what does each do to the belief that the world exists?
hint: One is a method for finding certainty by testing beliefs; the other is a change of attitude for describing experience.
solution: Descartes treats a belief as false if it can be doubted, to find something indubitable on which to rebuild knowledge; his doubt temporarily counts the world as possibly non-existent. Husserl's epoché is aimed at description, not at a foundation of certainty in that sense. It suspends the general thesis, neither affirming nor denying it, so that the world as experienced can be studied. The belief in the world is not rejected but set out of play and, Husserl says, remains as it was. A critic could say that the difference is smaller than Husserl claims because the transcendental turn also makes consciousness prior to the world. I think the contrast in purpose is real, though the debate about priority is genuine.
