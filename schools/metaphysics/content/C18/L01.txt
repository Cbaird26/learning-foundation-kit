@article
You can think about your grandmother's kitchen, about the number seven, about next Tuesday, about a unicorn. Each thought is *of* or *about* something, even when nothing of that kind exists. A stone is not about anything, and neither is a puddle. This directedness of thought toward something looks like the feature that sets minds apart from the rest of nature, and it is the starting point of the whole phenomenological tradition.

### Brentano's thesis

In *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), Franz Brentano revived a scholastic phrase, the "intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object", to name what he took to be the mark of the mental. "Inexistence" here means existence *in*: in every mental act, something is present as its object. The word **intentionality** has nothing to do with intending in the sense of planning. It means directedness, aboutness, or reference to a content. Brentano's thesis is that every mental phenomenon is directed toward an object in this way, and no physical phenomenon is.

He sorted mental phenomena into three basic classes, each a different way of being directed at an object:

| Class | Way of being directed | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Presentation | Having something before the mind | Seeing a lamp, imagining a harbor |
| Judgment | Accepting or rejecting something | Affirming that the harbor is cold |
| Emotion (interest, love and hate) | Taking up an attitude of liking or disliking | Dreading the winter |

Judgments and emotions both rest on presentations: you cannot affirm, reject or dread what is not presented to you. By **physical phenomena** Brentano meant what appears in sensory presentation: a color, a sound, a shape, a warmth, a landscape as seen. These are the contents of sensing, and by his account they show no directedness of their own. Brentano also held that mental acts are known through **inner perception**, with a special evidence, and that each act is also, in a secondary way, aware of itself.

The thesis, set out as an argument, runs as follows:

| P1 | Every mental phenomenon is directed toward an object. |
| P2 | No physical phenomenon is directed toward an object. |
| P3 | If a property belongs to every member of one kind and to no member of another, it distinguishes the two kinds. |
| C | So being directed toward an object distinguishes the mental from the physical. |

The argument is valid. P1 and P2 are the contested ones. Against P2, one can say that words, maps and pictures are about things. Brentano's side replies that these have aboutness only derivatively, because minds use and interpret them; the underlying directedness is in the minds. Against P1, it has been argued that some states, such as a diffuse mood or a bodily pain, are not directed at anything. Defenders reply that moods have objects in a wider sense (the world as a whole, the sore place), or that these are not purely mental. Neither side wins by definition.

### The puzzle of nonexistent objects

Intentionality becomes a puzzle once we notice that thought can be directed at what does not exist. Consider this argument:

| P1 | If thinking of Pegasus is a genuine relation to Pegasus, then Pegasus exists. |
| P2 | Pegasus does not exist. |
| C | So thinking of Pegasus is not a genuine relation to Pegasus. |

It is valid by modus tollens. P1 rests on the principle that a relation needs both of its relata: you cannot kick, hug or sit beside what is not there. Since thinking about Pegasus is nonetheless thinking *of* Pegasus, intentional directedness cannot always be an ordinary relation.

The main responses each deny something:

- **Immanent objects.** The early Brentano's language suggests that the object is in the mind as a content, so the relation is to something that does exist. The cost is that you now think of a mental item rather than of Pegasus, and a winged horse is not in your head. Brentano's later work moved toward the view that only individual things exist and that talk of intentional objects is not talk of further entities; how to read this development is itself disputed.
- **Nonexistent objects.** Alexius Meinong held that there are objects that lack existence, and that such objects still have characteristics: Pegasus is winged whether or not he exists. This denies P1's principle that relations need existing relata. The cost is an ontology many find extravagant.
- **Content and sense.** Kazimierz Twardowski distinguished the content of a presentation from its object, and Husserl later developed a sense-like noema (next week). The cost is explaining how a content can fix an object that is not there.

### Two logical marks

Roderick Chisholm, in the 1950s, proposed testing sentences rather than states of mind. In a typical **intentional sentence** (i) existential generalization fails: from "Ana is looking for the Fountain of Youth" you cannot infer "there is something Ana is looking for that exists"; and (ii) substitution of co-referring terms can fail: Ana may look for the Morning Star without looking for the Evening Star, although they are the same planet. Compare "Ana is polishing the Morning Star", which supports both inferences. W. V. Quine took the irreducibility of intentional idioms to non-intentional ones as a reason to keep them out of a scientific vocabulary. Others take it as a sign that science must make room for the mind.

@example
title: Testing two sentences for intentionality
problem: Apply the two tests to (a) "Rui is painting the garden gate" and (b) "Rui is imagining a perfectly silent concert hall". Which sentence behaves intentionally?
step: Test (a) for existential generalization. If Rui is painting the gate, there is a gate he is painting. The inference to "there is something Rui is painting" goes through.
step: Test (a) for substitution. If the garden gate is the oldest object on the property, then Rui is painting the oldest object on the property. Substitution of identicals works.
step: Test (b) for existential generalization. Rui can imagine a perfectly silent hall although there may be no such hall (perhaps there could not be one). "There is something Rui is imagining that exists" does not follow.
step: Test (b) for substitution. Suppose the hall he imagines is, unknown to him, the hall where his sister will play next year. It does not follow that he is imagining the hall where his sister will play. Substitution fails.
answer: Sentence (b) behaves intentionally, since both inferences fail. Sentence (a) supports both and is an ordinary extensional sentence.

@example
title: Which premise does each response deny?
problem: In the Pegasus argument, say which premise a Meinongian denies, how an immanent-object theorist responds, and why the argument is still valid.
step: The form is modus tollens: "If R then E; not E; so not R", where R is "thinking of Pegasus is a genuine relation to Pegasus" and E is "Pegasus exists". Any argument of this form is valid, so a reply must deny a premise or else accept the conclusion in some qualified sense.
step: The Meinongian accepts that Pegasus does not exist (P2) but denies P1: thinking can be a genuine relation to an object that has no existence.
step: The immanent-object theorist can accept P1 and P2 and even the conclusion as worded: thinking of Pegasus is not a relation to the winged horse. But the theorist denies that directedness is therefore not a genuine relation: it is a relation to a content present in the mind, which exists. What is denied is the step from "not a relation to Pegasus" to "not a relation at all".
answer: The argument is valid. Meinong denies P1's principle that relations need existing relata; the immanent-object theorist grants that the relation is not to the horse and reinterprets the relatum as a mental content that does exist.

@mistakes
- Treating "intentionality" as meaning "done on purpose". In this tradition it means aboutness, and a belief or a fear is intentional in this sense.
- Saying that Brentano thinks every mental state has an existing object. His point is the opposite: directedness does not require that its object exist.
- Counting a book as a counterexample to Brentano's thesis without noting his reply that its aboutness is derived from minds.
- Confusing Brentano's physical phenomena (the colors and sounds that appear) with the brain events or physical objects that physicists study.

@practice self
prompt: A critic says that a road sign is about the upcoming bend, so aboutness is not special to minds and Brentano's thesis is false. In three or four sentences, give Brentano's best reply and say what the reply leaves open.
hint: Ask where the sign's aboutness comes from.
solution: The reply is that the sign is about the bend only because people designed it, read it and use it that way: its aboutness is derived from the intentionality of the minds that interpret it. Without such minds it is just painted metal, whereas a person's thought about the bend is about the bend in its own right. This keeps P2 true of physical phenomena in the sense that matters. What the reply leaves open is whether minds have any intentionality that is not itself derived from something, and whether the derived-versus-intrinsic distinction can be drawn cleanly, for example for the states of a machine.
