@article
Stir a glass of water and drop in a lump of sugar. You must wait until it dissolves. That waiting, Bergson says in *Creative Evolution* (1907), is not an interval you can shorten and still have the same experience: it is bound up with your own impatience and with the sugar's dissolving. You could measure it with a stopwatch, but the stopwatch does not capture what the waiting is like or what it is for the sugar. This gap between measured time and lived time is the heart of Henri Bergson's philosophy.

### Two kinds of multiplicity

In *Time and Free Will* (1889; its French title is *Essay on the Immediate Data of Consciousness*), Bergson distinguishes two kinds of multiplicity. A **quantitative multiplicity** is made of distinct, homogeneous units that can be counted and set side by side, like sheep in a flock: each is separate, and the count is the same however we group them. A **qualitative multiplicity** is a succession of states that interpenetrate: in a melody, each note colors the next, and you cannot take one out without altering the others. **Duration** (*durée*) is Bergson's name for time as it is lived: a qualitative multiplicity in which the past is carried into the present, and the whole is continually changing and never repeats.

### Clock time as spatialized time

Bergson argues that our usual idea of time, a line of equal instants, is a **spatialization**: we picture time as space, with moments laid side by side like points on a ruler. This is useful for measuring and acting, but it replaces flow with juxtaposition. When I count the swings of a pendulum, Bergson says, I really count simultaneities: each swing coincides with a state of my consciousness. The units of clock time are tied to positions in space; the passage between them is left out. He holds that the **intellect** is shaped by practical action on solid bodies and so spatializes. Philosophy needs **intuition**, a way of entering into duration from within, as one coincides with the flow of one's own experience.

An argument to the point:

| P1 | Clock time represents time as a series of homogeneous, juxtaposed instants that can be counted. |
| P2 | Lived duration is a qualitative flow in which each moment carries the accumulated past and never recurs, and these features are essential to it. |
| P3 | A series of homogeneous, juxtaposed instants lacks the interpenetration of moments and the unrepeatability of duration. |
| C | So clock time omits what is essential to duration. |

The argument is valid. Its force depends on P2, a claim about experience. An objector says that no one holds that a clock measures lived experience: clocks serve the purposes of coordination and physics, and so duration and clock time answer different questions. Bergson would agree and reply that his target is the claim that clock time is the whole of time. Another objector says that the lived flow itself might be explained by neural processes that clocks can measure, so duration is something science can explain after all. The disagreement is about whether lived time needs its own metaphysics.

### Free will and duration

Bergson argues that the debate between determinists and defenders of free will pictures deliberation as a fork in space: two paths, A and B, with the agent at the junction, who could go either way. A reconstruction:

| P1 | The question "could the same agent in the very same state have done otherwise?" presupposes that the moment of choice could recur with the agent in exactly the same state. |
| P2 | In duration no state of the self recurs, since each moment includes the accumulated past. |
| C | So the question presupposes something that is false of real duration. |

The argument is valid. Bergson's positive claim is that a free act expresses the whole self, which develops through its history. A critic can deny P1: the question can be read as a question about what laws and prior conditions entail, a hypothetical that does not require literally re-running the choice. Bertrand Russell, in 1912, criticized Bergson's views of number, space and time as resting on confusions. Whether Bergson dissolves the problem or only redescribes it is much discussed.

### Memory, life and the 1922 debate

In *Matter and Memory* (1896) Bergson distinguishes habit memory, the body's learned routines, from pure memory, in which the past is preserved as a whole and selected by present needs. In *Creative Evolution* he holds that life is a creative process that cannot be predicted, and names its impetus the **élan vital**; most biologists do not use this term as an explanation, though philosophers of biology discuss his critique of purely mechanistic and purely goal-directed accounts of evolution.

In 1922 Bergson published *Duration and Simultaneity* and discussed relativity with Albert Einstein in Paris. On the usual account, he accepted the mathematics of special relativity but argued that the times it compares are measured times, and that they presuppose a lived duration shared among observers. Einstein reportedly replied that physics and psychology have times, but that there is no separate time of the philosophers. Special relativity is extremely well confirmed: moving clocks run slow relative to one another in a way measured with fast particles and with atomic clocks in aircraft and satellites, and GPS must correct for it. Many physicists and historians judged that Bergson's arguments about relativity, in particular about the traveler of Paul Langevin's twin scenario, contained errors. Others, more recently, have argued that his distinction between lived and measured time deserves a fairer hearing. The episode illustrates a standing issue: whether the physical description of time leaves something out.

@example
title: Duration or clock time?
problem: For each, say whether it describes duration or clock time in Bergson's sense: (a) "The kettle took 4 minutes and 10 seconds to boil." (b) "The wait for the bus seemed to stretch and stretch, and by the end I was a different mood from the start." (c) "The recording is 3 minutes 20 seconds long." (d) "In hearing the final chord, I still hear the whole song as present."
step: (a) and (c) give a measured quantity: a number of equal units, applicable to any process. These describe clock time.
step: (b) describes a flow in which the experience changes as it goes, with a mood carried forward. This is duration.
step: (d) describes the interpenetration of earlier and later notes in one experience: duration.
answer: (a) clock time; (b) duration; (c) clock time; (d) duration.

@example
title: Locating an objection to the free will argument
problem: A philosopher says: "The question 'could she have done otherwise?' does not need the same moment to recur. It asks whether, given the laws and the state of the world just before, a different choice was physically possible. That is a claim about what is entailed, not about re-running time." Which premise does this challenge?
step: The argument has P1 (the question presupposes recurrence of the same state) and P2 (no state recurs in duration), with the conclusion that the question presupposes something false of duration.
step: The philosopher accepts P2 for the sake of argument but denies that the question requires recurrence. That is a denial of P1.
step: The argument remains valid. The dispute is whether the question about alternatives is a question about re-running, as Bergson says, or about entailment.
answer: The reply denies P1; the argument stays valid.

@mistakes
- Treating duration as just a feeling that time passes faster or slower. Bergson's claim is about the structure of lived time: interpenetration and unrepeatability.
- Saying that Bergson denies that clocks work. He accepts clock time as useful for action and measurement, and denies that it is the whole of time.
- Taking the Einstein debate to show that relativity is false. Special relativity is extremely well confirmed, and the debate concerns whether it leaves out lived time.
- Treating the élan vital as an accepted scientific hypothesis. It is a philosophical idea that biologists do not use as an explanation.
