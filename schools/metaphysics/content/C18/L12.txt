@article
Two philosophers might each ask what a person is, or what time is, and give very different kinds of answer. One describes how a life is lived through and what it is like to be a self in time; the other asks what the identity conditions of persons are across possible situations. Do they disagree, talk past each other, or answer different parts of one question? This final lesson of the course looks at where the traditions meet.

### Persistence: endurance, perdurance and process

Analytic metaphysics has its own debate about how things last. **Endurantism** holds that an object is wholly present at each moment of its existence, so the same thing, not a part of it, is there at different times. **Perdurantism**, defended by David Lewis and by Theodore Sider among others, holds that an object is a four-dimensional whole extended in time, with different **temporal parts** at different times, as a road has different spatial parts in different places. These views take things as basic and ask how things last. A **process ontology**, as defended in Whitehead's tradition and in more recent work such as Johanna Seibt's general theory of processes, reverses the priority: processes or events are basic, and things are patterns or stabilities in them. Perdurantism is sometimes seen as friendly to process thought because it makes time a dimension of objects, but the two differ. A perdurantist still takes the worm to be a thing composed of parts; a process thinker denies that substances in any form are basic. Process ideas have also been applied outside metaphysics, for instance by philosophers of biology who argue that organisms are better understood as stabilized processes than as fixed things.

### Time, the self and existence

On time, the analytic debate between the A-theory and the B-theory (from McTaggart's 1908 argument about the A-series) asks whether temporal passage is real. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 27% of philosophers accepted or leaned toward the A-theory and about 38% the B-theory, but a share of opinion is not evidence for either. Bergson is sometimes cited for the A-theory side, and phenomenological descriptions of time generally portray it as experienced as flowing. A B-theorist can accept every phenomenological description and explain the felt passage as a feature of how a temporally extended mind represents the world. The phenomenological work is therefore evidence about the structure of experience, and what it shows about the nature of time is disputed.

On the self, phenomenologists describe a minimal sense of self given in the pre-reflective awareness that accompanies every experience (compare Sartre's non-positional awareness), while analytic philosophers study personal identity over time through psychological continuity, bodily continuity or a further fact. These are different questions, and a full account would need both: what it is to have a first-person perspective, and what makes a later person the same as an earlier one.

On existence, we have contrasted Heidegger's question about the sense of being with Quine's criterion of ontological commitment (week 4). A good bridge is to ask whether the quantifier's meaning is exhausted by its logic.

### Carnap's challenge

In 1932, Rudolf Carnap's paper "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" used a passage from Heidegger's lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" (1929) as an example of a pseudo-statement. An argument:

| P1 | In correct logical syntax, "nothing" is a quantifier expression ("there is nothing that..."), not the name of an object. |
| P2 | Heidegger's sentence, which says that the Nothing itself performs an activity named by a verb built from the word "nothing", uses "the Nothing" as the name of an object that acts. |
| P3 | A sentence that uses a quantifier expression as the name of an object violates logical syntax and is meaningless. |
| C | So Heidegger's sentence is meaningless. |

The argument is valid. Replies target each premise. A reply to P3 says that meaning is not exhausted by the syntax of a formal language and that natural language has other uses of "nothing" that need not be reducible to the quantifier. A reply to P2 says that Heidegger is trying to name an experience, the experience of anxiety in which things as a whole recede, and not to refer to an object, and that his sentence has an interpretable use in context. A third reply says that Heidegger rejects the authority of logic over philosophy, so the criticism begs the question against him. Carnap's critics also pointed out that his general criterion of meaning, the verification principle, later ran into problems of its own. Michael Friedman's book *A Parting of the Ways* (2000) examines the 1929 Davos encounter between Cassirer and Heidegger, which Carnap attended, and Carnap's relation to Heidegger as a point at which the analytic and continental traditions diverged. Michael Dummett argued, in *Origins of Analytical Philosophy*, that analytic philosophy and phenomenology have common roots in the Austro-German philosophy of Brentano's time, so that their later separation hides a shared origin.

### Method and what each can learn

Both traditions appeal to how things seem: phenomenologists to a disciplined description of experience, analytic philosophers to intuitions about cases, which they test against theory. Each can complain of the other: phenomenology can be accused of obscure vocabulary and of drawing metaphysical conclusions from descriptions, and analytic metaphysics of ignoring the lived experience that gives its concepts content. Some have tried to connect them, for example Francisco Varela's proposal of a neurophenomenology that correlates first-person descriptions with brain data. A fair comparison of two views, for instance process and substance, asks four questions: what problem does each aim to solve, what are its basic entities, what does it explain well, and what must it leave unexplained or treat as primitive?

@mistakes
- Treating perdurantism as the same as a process ontology. Perdurantism still takes things to be basic.
- Saying that phenomenological descriptions settle the nature of time. They describe experience; what they show about the world is disputed.
- Treating Carnap's argument as a refutation of the whole tradition. It targets particular sentences and relies on premises that critics deny.
- Equating the share of philosophers who hold a view with evidence for it.

@quiz self
prompt: In five or six sentences, say what a phenomenological and an analytic account of the self might each capture that the other misses, and how they might be combined.
hint: One concerns the first-person perspective, the other identity over time.
solution: A phenomenological account captures what it is to have a first-person perspective: the pre-reflective awareness that goes with each experience and the way a self is embodied and situated in a world. An analytic account of personal identity captures what makes a person at one time the same as a person at another, and it handles cases of fission and memory loss with precision. The phenomenological account says little about identity conditions across time, and the analytic account can leave the first-person character of experience unexplained or treat it as a given. They could be combined by treating the first-person perspective as the explanandum that an account of identity must respect, while letting identity theories test the phenomenological claims against hard cases. A skeptic would ask whether the two sets of questions really are separate.

rubric: Proposes a way to combine them or argues that they cannot be combined.
