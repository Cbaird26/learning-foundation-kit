@article
A candle flame looks like one steady thing. But the wax is constantly burning and the gases constantly being replaced: no particle stays in the flame. What makes it the same flame? A traditional answer says there is an underlying thing, a substance, that persists through change. Alfred North Whitehead proposes the opposite order: process is basic, and what we call a persisting thing is a pattern in a continuing succession of events. His *Process and Reality* (1929), based on his Gifford Lectures of 1927–28, is the most systematic statement of this view. Whitehead had earlier been a mathematician and had co-written *Principia Mathematica* with Bertrand Russell; his later metaphysics grew out of reflection on the new physics and on the nature of experience.

### Actual occasions and prehension

For Whitehead, the final real things are **actual entities**, also called **actual occasions**: momentary events of becoming, which he likens to momentary units of experience in a very broad sense. This does not mean that every occasion is conscious: "experience" is meant in a very general sense of taking account of other things. Each occasion comes to be by **prehending** (grasping, feeling) earlier occasions and the possibilities open to it, and by unifying these into a single new whole; this process is called **concrescence**, a growing together. When the occasion reaches its completion, it perishes as a subject and becomes a datum for later occasions, a kind of **objective immortality**. In Whitehead's own summary of the process, a multiplicity of occasions is unified in a new occasion, and the world is thereby increased by one more. The most general principle is **creativity**, the ultimate feature of reality by which novelty enters the world.

Besides occasions, there are **eternal objects**, pure potentials such as a particular shade of red or a geometric form, which "ingress" into occasions without being events themselves. They are close to Platonic forms, though the exact relation is debated. Whitehead also gives **God** a role in his system, with a **primordial nature** that orders possibilities and a **consequent nature** that takes in the world. God is not an omnipotent creator who coerces, but a source of persuasion. Some process philosophers accept this part of the system and others set it aside, and discussion of it should not be confused with the rest of the framework.

### Persisting things as societies

How, then, are there tables, electrons and persons? Whitehead says that these are **societies**: chains or nexuses of occasions, each inheriting a defining characteristic from its predecessors. A flame, a stone and a person are all such historical routes of occasions, differing in how stable and complex they are. Persistence is **inheritance**, and not the continued presence of an underlying subject. Change is not what happens to a substance but the replacement of one occasion by another that takes account of it.

### The fallacy of misplaced concreteness

In *Science and the Modern World* (1925), Whitehead criticizes the picture of nature as bare matter, located at points of space and instants of time. He calls this **simple location**: the view that a bit of matter is wholly where it is, at a place and a time, with no essential reference to other places and times. He argues:

| P1 | Notions such as bare matter at a point-instant are abstractions from concrete, extended and interrelated events. |
| P2 | To treat an abstraction as if it were the concrete and fully real thing is the fallacy of misplaced concreteness. |
| P3 | The scientific materialism of simply located matter treats such abstractions as the concrete and fully real things. |
| C | So scientific materialism of that kind commits the fallacy of misplaced concreteness. |

The argument is valid. P2 is a definition. A defender of the targeted view denies P3: physicists use point particles and instants as idealizations and do not claim that these are concrete, so the charge hits a position few hold. Another reply questions P1: it is not obvious that events are more concrete than objects, since an event may also be an abstraction from objects and their properties. Whitehead's reply is that the standard to apply is experience, in which we find process and relatedness first.

### Process and substance

The process view and the substance view are two ways of ordering the same concerns. The table sets out a rough contrast, remembering that both families have many versions:

| Question | Substance view (Aristotelian type) | Process view (Whitehead) |
|---|---|---|
| What is basic? | Enduring individual substances | Events of becoming (occasions) |
| What is persistence? | The same thing continues through change | Inheritance along a chain of occasions |
| What is change? | A substance gains or loses properties | One occasion succeeds another that takes account of it |
| How are things related? | Relations are often taken as external to the relata | Relations are constitutive: an occasion is made by what it prehends |
| What is the mind? | A kind of substance, or a property of one | A pattern of occasions, with a mental pole in each |

Substance theorists have replies. They say that the unity of a persisting thing, such as a person, is better explained by a subject than by inheritance, that the unity and identity conditions of events are as obscure as those of substances, and that the "mental pole" of every occasion is an extravagant claim that goes well beyond what the physical sciences require. Process theorists reply that substances are an abstraction from patterns of events and that process views fit modern physics and biology, in which things are processes. How much support these sciences give is itself disputed. Week 12 returns to this comparison and asks you to write it up.

@example
title: A candle flame as a society
problem: Explain in Whitehead's terms what makes a candle flame the same flame from one moment to the next, and say what it is not.
step: The flame is a society: a chain of occasions, each of which prehends the preceding ones and inherits their pattern (a particular shape, brightness and motion).
step: Persistence is therefore inheritance along the chain: each new occasion carries the defining characteristic forward, even though none of the matter in it remains.
step: It is not the continued presence of an underlying flame-substance that stays the same while its properties change.
step: A critic from the substance side says that the inheritance needs an account of why the pattern continues, which Whitehead gives in terms of prehension and creativity.
answer: The flame's identity is the inheritance of a pattern along a chain of occasions, not a persisting substrate.

@example
title: Locating an objection to the argument from misplaced concreteness
problem: A physicist says: "No one regards a point particle as a concrete thing. We use it as an idealization whose limits we know." Which premise does this deny, and does it show that Whitehead's argument is invalid?
step: The argument has P1 (such notions are abstractions), P2 (treating abstractions as concrete is the fallacy), P3 (scientific materialism treats them as concrete), and C.
step: The physicist grants P1 and P2 and denies P3: she says that science uses the idealization consciously.
step: Validity is unaffected, since if all three premises were true the conclusion would follow. What remains to be shown is whether some scientists or philosophers do take the idealization as the whole truth.
answer: The reply denies P3. The argument stays valid, so the dispute is about whom the charge applies to.

@mistakes
- Saying that Whitehead holds that everything is conscious. An actual occasion prehends and feels in a very general sense; consciousness is a rare, complex achievement.
- Treating process philosophy as the claim that nothing is stable. Stable things are societies of occasions that inherit patterns.
- Reading the fallacy of misplaced concreteness as a rejection of abstraction. Abstraction is necessary; the fallacy is mistaking it for the concrete.
- Identifying Whitehead's God with the creator of classical theism. His God acts by persuasion and is affected by the world.

@quiz self
prompt: In five or six sentences, explain the argument from the fallacy of misplaced concreteness and say which premise a scientist who uses point particles would deny.
hint: Three premises, one of which is a definition.
solution: Whitehead argues that notions such as bare matter at a point-instant are abstractions from concrete events, that treating an abstraction as the concrete thing is the fallacy of misplaced concreteness, and that scientific materialism treats such abstractions as concrete. So it commits the fallacy. The second premise is a definition. A scientist who uses point particles as idealizations would deny that she treats them as concrete, which rejects the third premise. If she is right, the argument is still valid but its target is narrower than Whitehead supposed. Whitehead would then ask whether the broader picture of nature in which the idealization is used still treats the abstract as the whole.
