@article
When you hear a tune, you do not hear a single note followed by the memory of notes already gone. You hear the phrase as a phrase, unfolding. Yet, strictly speaking, only one note sounds at a time. How can a note that has stopped still be part of what you are now hearing? Husserl's lectures of 1905, published in 1928 as *On the Phenomenology of the Consciousness of Internal Time*, take this question as their starting point.

### The structure of the living present

Husserl does not ask what time is in physics. He asks how time shows up in experience. In experience, a moment is not a point. The **living present** has three aspects, which Husserl treats as moments of one structure and not as three separate acts:

| Term | What it is | In hearing a tune |
|---|---|---|
| **Primal impression** | The phase of consciousness aimed at what is sounding now | The note now sounding |
| **Retention** | The just-elapsed phase held in consciousness as just past | The notes that have just sounded, still present as fading |
| **Protention** | An openness to what is about to come | The expectation of a next note, of a cadence |

Retention is not memory in the ordinary sense. **Recollection** (re-presentation) is a separate act in which you bring back something that is no longer perceived: recalling last year's concert. It requires that you were not still perceiving it. Retention is part of the perception itself; without it, there would be nothing to recollect. Protention is likewise not a prediction made by reasoning: it is the forward-leaning character of experience, which shows itself when it is disappointed. A wrong note in a familiar song jars because the protended note did not arrive.

Husserl also marks a **double intentionality** in retention. *Transverse* intentionality is directed at the object in time, the tone as it fades. *Longitudinal* intentionality is directed at the flow itself, the earlier phases of the consciousness. Because of this, the flow of consciousness appears to itself without a second, higher flow behind it, which would raise an infinite regress.

### The argument from melody

A reconstruction of the main argument:

| P1 | We hear melodies as unities of successive tones. |
| P2 | If we hear a melody as a unity of successive tones, then at each phase of the hearing we are conscious of the tone now sounding and also of the tones just past as just past. |
| P3 | Being conscious of tones just past as just past, in the course of hearing, is neither an inference nor a separate act of recollection. |
| P4 | Any consciousness of the just past that is part of present perceiving, and is neither inference nor recollection, is retention. |
| C | So there is retention: a consciousness of the just-past that belongs to the perceptual present. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 give the consciousness of both present and past tones; P3 and P4 identify its kind. The key premises are P2 and P3. A rival, **extensionalism** (defended by philosophers such as Barry Dainton), accepts that we are directly conscious of the just past but disputes how that is possible: experience itself, it holds, is temporally extended, so that one experience can literally include a succession and no momentary phase with retentional content is needed. So it does not deny a premise so much as reject the retentionalist reading of what P4 calls retention. On the usual classification, Husserl is a **retentionalist**: he holds that each phase is a momentary one with a retentional structure. Another rival, discussed by Husserl, is a **snapshot** or "cinematographic" view in which consciousness is a series of instantaneous present-only states; if so, it would be hard to say how we hear change and persistence directly. Husserl also criticized the view, associated with his teacher Brentano, that our awareness of the just-past is produced by imagination. The tone that just ended is not imagined.

Compare William James's **specious present**: the short stretch of time, a few seconds at most, that is experienced as present. Husserl's account is more structured and treats the present as having an inner form.

### The lifeworld and the critique of objectivism

Husserl's last major work, *The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology* (written 1934–37; the first part appeared in 1936 and the whole in 1954), introduces the **lifeworld** (*Lebenswelt*): the pre-given world of everyday experience, of tables, streets, colors and persons, in which we live before any scientific theory. Husserl argues that modern natural science since Galileo built its ideal objects (perfect geometrical shapes, measurable magnitudes) by idealizing the lifeworld's shapes and measuring practices. The danger is **objectivism**: taking the idealized, mathematical picture to be the true being and the lifeworld to be mere subjective appearance. Husserl did not oppose science. He opposed forgetting where its sense comes from.

| P1 | The ideal objects of science get their meaning from idealizing practices carried out in the pre-given lifeworld. |
| P2 | Whatever gets its meaning from a source cannot coherently be used to show that the source is mere appearance to be replaced. |
| P3 | Objectivism uses the scientific picture to show that the lifeworld is mere appearance to be replaced. |
| C | So objectivism is incoherent: it undermines the meaning of the very picture it defends. |

This is valid. Defenders of the scientific view deny P3: a scientist can say that the lifeworld is a useful, accurate way of dealing with medium-sized things, while physics describes the underlying structure, without calling the lifeworld mere appearance. A second reply denies P2, holding that a theory can outgrow its origins, as chemistry outgrew alchemy. Husserl's point survives in a weaker form: any account of experience and meaning must leave room for the lifeworld.

@example
title: Labeling the parts of a heard phrase
problem: You hear a three-tone doorbell sequence: low, middle, high. You are just hearing the high tone while the middle tone is fading, and you are leaning toward a final tone you expect to complete the sequence. Later you recall, over dinner, how the sequence sounded. Label each: (a) the high tone, (b) the fading middle tone, (c) the leaning toward a final tone, (d) the later dinner-time remembering.
step: (a) The high tone is the content of the primal impression: what is sounding now.
step: (b) The fading middle tone, still present as just past, belongs to retention. It is not a new act and is not a memory over dinner.
step: (c) The leaning toward a completing tone is protention, an openness to what is about to come that need not involve any conscious prediction.
step: (d) The dinner-time remembering is recollection (re-presentation): a separate act bringing back what is no longer perceived.
answer: (a) primal impression; (b) retention; (c) protention; (d) recollection.

@example
title: Testing the argument from melody against a snapshot view
problem: A snapshot theorist says experience consists of instantaneous present-only states, with no retention. Which premise of the argument from melody does this deny, and what would Husserl say about the cost?
step: The snapshot theorist agrees that we seem to hear melodies (P1), but denies P2 or P3, since at each instant consciousness would hold only the present tone.
step: Such a theorist must say that our impression of hearing a melody is produced by memory or inference added afterwards, in other words by recollection or judgment.
step: Husserl replies that this misdescribes the experience: the just-passed tone is not recalled or inferred but still present in the hearing, which is why you can hear that a note is rising or falling, not merely infer it.
answer: The snapshot view denies P2 (or P3, if it concedes awareness of the past but explains it by recollection or inference). Husserl takes the cost to be a false description of experience, since the melody is heard, not reconstructed.

@mistakes
- Identifying retention with memory. Retention is part of perceiving the present; recollection is a separate later act.
- Treating protention as a guess or prediction. It is a structural openness, and it shows itself when it is frustrated.
- Thinking that Husserl's lifeworld is a nostalgic return to nature or an attack on science. He claims that science depends on it for its sense.
- Saying that Husserl is giving a physical theory of time. He describes time as experienced.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about 150 words Husserl's retention and protention using a melody of your own choice (not the doorbell), and say which of the two is easier to defend against the objection that experience consists of snapshots.
hint: Describe what is held in the hearing and what is anticipated, then ask which of the two the snapshot theorist can account for more easily.
solution: Take the opening of a lullaby: four notes that rise, then a fifth that falls. When the fourth note sounds, it is the primal impression. The first three, still present as just past, are retained, and that is why the phrase is heard as rising. Meanwhile, I lean toward the falling fifth: that is protention, as I notice if the fifth fails to come or comes wrongly. The objection from snapshots says each instant holds only the current note. Protention may be easier for the snapshot theorist to explain, since expectation could be a learned disposition. Retention is harder to reduce, because the rising shape is heard, not inferred. But a determined snapshot theorist can say both are produced by fast processing.
