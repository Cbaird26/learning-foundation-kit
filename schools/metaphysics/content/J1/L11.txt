@article
A **statistical question** is one that expects the answers to **vary**. "How many pets does Ana have?" has one answer. "How many pets do the students in our school have?" does not: different students give different numbers. Data about variation is what statistics studies.

### Collecting data

The whole group you want to learn about is the **population**. Sometimes you ask everyone in it, and sometimes only a part. To learn anything useful, ask every person the same question the same way, and record each answer. Each recorded answer is a **data value**.

### Dot plots

A **dot plot** (or line plot) puts a dot above a number line for each data value. Equal values stack. Dot plots work for small data sets and show every value.

Suppose a class records the number of siblings: $0$ siblings has $3$ dots, $1$ has $5$, $2$ has $4$ and $3$ has $2$. Count the dots to find how many students answered: $3 + 5 + 4 + 2 = 14$. To find how many have at least $2$ siblings, add the dots at $2$ and $3$: $4 + 2 = 6$.

### Histograms

A **histogram** groups data into equal-width **bins** (intervals) and uses bar heights for the **counts**. Bars touch, since the bins fill the number line without gaps. A histogram shows the overall shape of larger data sets, but you cannot read individual values from it.

### Shape and variation

Look for where the data are **clustered**, whether it is **symmetric** or skewed to one side, and for any **gaps** or unusually high or low values. Describing these features is how you summarize a display in words.

### Choosing a display

- A **dot plot** is best for small sets of numerical data.
- A **histogram** is best for many values grouped in intervals.
- A **bar graph** compares categories, such as favorite sports.
- A **box plot**, which you will meet next week, summarizes the spread using five numbers.

### Planning a survey

State your question, decide who to ask, collect at least $20$ answers, and choose a display before you look at the results.

@example
title: Reading a dot plot
problem: A dot plot of quiz scores shows these dots: score $6$ has $2$ dots, score $7$ has $4$, score $8$ has $6$, score $9$ has $5$ and score $10$ has $3$. How many students took the quiz, and how many scored $9$ or more?
step: Count all the dots: $2 + 4 + 6 + 5 + 3 = 20$ students took the quiz.
step: Scores of $9$ or more are the dots at $9$ and $10$: $5 + 3 = 8$.
step: Describe the shape: the data cluster around $8$ and $9$, with fewer scores at the low and high ends.

@mistakes
- Calling a question statistical when it has one fixed answer, like "How tall is Ana?".
- Counting the values on the number line instead of the dots.
- Reading a histogram bar height as one data value; the height is the count in that bin.
