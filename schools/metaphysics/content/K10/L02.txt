@lesson
title: Asking good questions
summary: Make up good questions about a picture book and circle the big ones.
objective: Use the question words who, what, where, why and how.
objective: Tell an open question from a yes-or-no question.
objective: Write or say five questions about a picture book and circle the big ones.
reading: Teaching Children Philosophy (Prindle Institute for Ethics): the pages about asking questions with a picture book.

@article
Grown-up helper: choose a picture book you both like and keep it next to you.

Good questions start with small words: **who**, **what**, **where**, **when**, **why** and **how**. Each word opens a different door.

"Who is in the story?" asks about a person or animal. "Where does it happen?" asks about a place. These are often easy questions, because the picture book shows you.

"Why did the bear share his berries?" and "How do you think the bear felt?" ask you to think. The book may not say the answer. You need to look at the pictures, think about people and give a reason.

Some questions can be answered with just "yes" or "no". "Was the bear kind?" is a **yes-or-no question**. Yes-or-no questions can be good too, but you can make them bigger by adding "why": "Was the bear kind? Why do you think so?"

An **open question** has room for many answers. "What would you do if you were the bear?" is an open question. Open questions are great for talking.

Here is a plan for any picture book:
1. Look at the cover and ask one question.
2. Read a page and ask one more.
3. Read to the end and ask a "why" question.
4. Ask your helper to ask one too.

When you have five questions, circle the big ones. Remember: a big question has more than one answer and each answer can have a reason.

@example
title: Making a question bigger
problem: A picture book is about a rabbit who finds a lost mitten. You want to make the question "Did the rabbit give the mitten back?" into a bigger question. How?
step: The question has a yes-or-no answer. That is fine, but it is a small question.
step: Add "why": "Why do you think the rabbit gave the mitten back?" Now there is room for reasons.
step: Try a "what would you do" question: "What would you do if you found a mitten?" Now everyone can say something different.
answer: Add "why" or ask "What would you do?" to turn a yes-or-no question into a bigger one.

@mistakes
- Asking only questions that the page already answers. Look for questions the book does not answer.
- Thinking a question must be hard to be good. A short question like "Why?" can be a very good one.
- Forgetting to listen. After you ask, wait quietly for answers.

prompt: Which question word asks about a place?
choice: Who
choice: Why
choice*: Where
choice: How many
hint: Think about "Where is my hat?"
solution: "Where" asks about a place. "Who" asks about a person, "why" asks for a reason, and "how many" asks for a number.

@practice mc
prompt: Which question is a yes-or-no question?
choice*: Is the dog in the picture brown?
choice: Why do you think the dog is sad?
choice: How would you help the dog?
choice: What happens next in the story?
hint: Could you answer with just "yes" or "no"?
solution: "Is the dog in the picture brown?" can be answered with yes or no. The other questions need a longer answer.

@practice ms
prompt: Which of these questions are open questions, with room for many answers? Pick all that are open.
choice*: Why do you think the bear shared?
choice: Is the bear brown?
choice*: What would you do if you were the bear?
choice: Did the story end?
hint: An open question cannot be answered with one word like yes or no.
solution: The "why" question and the "what would you do" question have room for many answers. The questions about the bear's color and the end of the story are yes-or-no questions.

@practice self
prompt: Pick a picture book and say or write five questions about it. Try a who, a where, a why and a how question. Then circle the big ones.
hint: Start with the cover, then ask something at the middle and the end of the story.
solution: Example for a book about a fox: "Who is the fox?" (easy), "Where does the fox live?" (easy), "Why was the fox sad?" (big), "How would you help the fox?" (big), "Is a fox's den a home?" (big). Any five honest questions work, with big ones circled for a reason.
rubric: The child says or writes five questions about the book.
rubric: The child circles some questions as big and gives a reason for at least one.
rubric: The helper listens and asks one question too.
