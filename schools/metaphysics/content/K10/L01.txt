@lesson
title: Wondering: what is a big question?
summary: Tell a big question, which has many possible answers, from an easy question, which you can answer by looking or counting.
objective: Say what it means to wonder.
objective: Tell a big question from an easy question.
objective: Make up a big question of your own about something you see.
reading: Teaching Children Philosophy (Prindle Institute for Ethics): the pages about starting a conversation with a picture book.

@article
Grown-up helper: read this aloud, slowly, and stop to talk whenever the child has something to say.

Have you ever looked up at the sky and thought, "Why is it blue?" or looked at a puddle and thought, "Where does it go?" When you do that, you are **wondering**. Wondering is thinking about something just because you want to know more.

Some questions are **easy questions**. You can find the answer by looking, counting or asking someone who knows. "How many legs does a spider have?" is an easy question. You can count: eight. "What color is this book?" is an easy question too. You look, and you know.

Some questions are **big questions**. A big question does not have one answer that you can just look up. Grown-ups and children can think about it for a long time, and different people may give different answers. "Is a dream real?" is a big question. "What makes a good friend?" is a big question. Nobody can count the answer or find it by looking.

Here is a trick. Ask yourself, "Could two kind, smart people answer this in different ways, and each have a good reason?" If yes, it is probably a big question. If one answer is clearly right, it is probably an easy question.

Big questions are not scary. They are like puzzles that you work on together. You do not have to find the final answer. Wondering and talking is the fun part.

Today's job: ask your helper a question about the room you are in, and decide together whether it is easy or big.

@example
title: Sorting questions
problem: Your helper reads you four questions. Sort each one into easy or big: "How many wheels does a bike have?", "Is a robot alive?", "What color is a banana?", "What is the best way to spend a rainy day?"
step: Take the first question. You can count the wheels on a bike, so it is an easy question.
step: Take "Is a robot alive?" Ask: could two smart people answer it differently and each give a reason? Yes, so it is a big question.
step: A banana is yellow when you look at it, so "What color is a banana?" is easy.
step: "The best way to spend a rainy day" depends on what you think and like, and people give reasons. It is a big question.
answer: Easy: the bike wheels and the banana color. Big: the robot and the rainy day.

@mistakes
- Thinking a big question means a hard word or a long question. A big question can be very short, like "What is a friend?"
- Thinking a big question has a secret right answer that the grown-up knows. Everyone can wonder and give reasons.
- Thinking an easy question is a bad question. Easy questions are useful. They just have one answer you can find.

@practice num
prompt: You and your helper write 9 questions about a picture book. Of these, 6 are easy questions and the rest are big questions. How many big questions did you write?
answer: 3
unit: questions
check: 9 - 6
hint: The big questions are the ones left after you take away the easy ones.
hint: Count down from 9 by 6, or use your fingers.
solution: There are 9 questions in all and 6 are easy, so 9 minus 6 is 3 big questions.

@practice self
prompt: Look around the room or out of a window. Wonder out loud about one thing you see, and make up one question. Ask your helper: is it easy or big?
hint: Start with "I wonder why..." or "I wonder if..." and say what the thing is.
solution: Example: "I wonder if the rain feels happy when it falls." This is a big question, because people can answer in different ways and give reasons. Any question about something you really see is a fine start.
rubric: The child makes up a question about something real that they can see or know about.
rubric: The child and helper talk about whether the question is easy or big and give a reason, any reason.
rubric: The helper listens and accepts any honest question.

@quiz self
prompt: Tell your helper one big question you wonder about, and one reason you like to wonder about it.
hint: It can be about animals, the sky, friends or toys.
solution: Example: "Can a stuffed animal feel loved? I like wondering about it because my bear is my favorite." Any big question with a reason works.
rubric: The child says a question that is big, with more than one possible answer.
rubric: The child gives a reason for liking the question, even a small one.
rubric: The helper listens and does not correct the child's answer.
