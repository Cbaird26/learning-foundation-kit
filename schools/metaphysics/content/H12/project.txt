title: Document-based essay: Could Congress charter a national bank?
overview: You will write a document-based essay on a historical constitutional question: whether the Constitution allowed Congress to create a national bank in 1791. You will use the five primary sources below and present the main arguments on each side fairly before reaching a reasoned conclusion that you support with evidence.
goal: Analyze primary sources for their meaning, context and limits.
goal: Present both sides of a constitutional dispute in terms each side would accept.
goal: Write a clear, evidence-based argument that connects the sources to the ideas of federalism and enumerated powers.
step: Read the background and the five sources below. Write one sentence in your own words saying what each source claims.
step: Make a two-column chart. In one column list the arguments that Congress had the power to charter a bank. In the other list the arguments that it did not. Cite the source for each.
step: Note the context for each source: who wrote or issued it, when, and for what purpose. Mark which sources are the text of the Constitution and which are summaries of arguments.
step: Draft a thesis. It should answer the question and mention the strongest argument on the other side, for example "Although the Tenth Amendment suggests ..., the Necessary and Proper Clause ...". Your thesis may be either position, or a qualified one.
step: Write the essay in five parts: introduction with thesis, a paragraph presenting the argument against the bank, a paragraph presenting the argument for the bank, a paragraph weighing the evidence, and a conclusion. Use at least five sources, quoting or paraphrasing each.
step: Revise. Check each quotation against the source text exactly, check that each side is described fairly, and add one sentence about how this question is related to later disputes over federal power.
material: Background and sources (the numbered summaries 3, 4 and 5 are written for this course, not quotations).
material: Background: In 1791 Congress passed a bill to create the Bank of the United States, a national bank that would hold federal funds, make loans and issue paper money. The Constitution does not mention a bank. President Washington asked his advisers for written opinions on whether it was constitutional.
material: Source 1, Constitution, Article I, Section 8 (1787): Congress has power "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof."
material: Source 2, Tenth Amendment (1791): "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people."
material: Source 3, summary written for this course of the Secretary of State's 1791 opinion against the bank: The Constitution lists the powers of Congress. A bank is not listed. "Necessary" should mean truly needed, not merely convenient, because a broad reading would let Congress do almost anything and erase the limits in the Tenth Amendment.
material: Source 4, summary written for this course of the Secretary of the Treasury's 1791 opinion for the bank: Every government has the power to use means suited to its ends. A bank helps collect taxes, borrow money and regulate the currency, which are listed powers. "Necessary" means useful or appropriate, and the Constitution gives Congress the means to carry out its powers.
material: Source 5, summary written for this course of McCulloch v. Maryland (1819): The Supreme Court upheld the second national bank. It held that Congress has implied powers as well as enumerated ones, that a state may not tax the national bank, and that under the Supremacy Clause valid federal law prevails over conflicting state law.
deliverable: A document-based essay of 600 to 900 words that cites all five sources.
deliverable: Your two-column chart and a source table listing author, date and purpose.

rubric: It uses all five sources accurately, with exact quotations where it quotes.
rubric: It presents the main argument of each side fairly, in terms its supporters would accept.
rubric: It explains the connection to federalism, enumerated powers and the Supremacy Clause.
rubric: It is organized, clear and free of unsupported claims about modern politicians or parties.

### Week by week

1. **Colonial foundations and the Revolution.** Colonial regions differed; Native nations, enslaved Africans and colonists shaped each other. After 1763 Britain taxed and regulated more, and colonists protested "no taxation without representation." Independence was declared in 1776 and recognized in 1783.
2. **Constitution and federalism.** Articles of Confederation were weak. The Constitution used compromises, separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism (enumerated, reserved, concurrent powers; Supremacy Clause). Amendments need two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states.
3. **Expansion, slavery and the Civil War.** Expansion displaced Native nations and Mexico's claims, and fights over slavery in the territories led to compromises, Dred Scott, secession and war. Slavery was central to the conflict. The Thirteenth Amendment ended slavery.
4. **Reconstruction and the Gilded Age.** The Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments; backlash; Plessy. Industrialization, unions, immigration, and Native dispossession.
5. **Progressive Era and World War I.** Muckrakers, the 1906 food, drug and meat laws, constitutional amendments, women's suffrage, and wartime civil liberties.
6. **Depression and New Deal.** Causes, relief, recovery and reform programs, the 1938 drug law, and debates over federal power.
7. **World War II.** Home front, Executive Order 8802, Japanese American incarceration, the GI Bill.
8. **Cold War.** Containment, Korea, Vietnam, the postwar economy and the end of the Cold War.
9. **Civil rights.** Brown, direct action, the 1964 and 1965 laws, and other movements.
10. **Modern America.** Politics, economy, September 11, the financial crisis; separate fact from interpretation and value.
11. **Branches and courts.** The path of a bill, veto override, cloture, judicial review.
12. **Rights, elections, participation.** Incorporation, the Electoral College, the expansion of voting, fair treatment of contested questions.

### Self-check

- Can you explain federalism and separation of powers with an example of each?
- Can you give three causes of the Civil War and cite evidence?
- Can you trace a bill and give the vote thresholds?
- Can you explain judicial review and name the case?
- Can you describe two sides of a contested question fairly?

### Common errors

- Treating dates as the whole story; explain causes and effects.
- Confusing federalism with separation of powers, or a majority with a two-thirds vote.
- Presenting one group's experience as the whole story.
- Stating opinions about current politics as facts.

@exam self
prompt: Write a short essay (about 300 words) answering: How did the meaning of equality in the United States change from the Declaration of Independence to the civil rights laws of the 1960s? Use at least four events or documents from different periods, include the experiences of at least two groups, and acknowledge one disagreement about how far equality extends.
hint: Make a timeline of four documents or events first, such as the Declaration, the Fourteenth Amendment, the Nineteenth Amendment and the Civil Rights Act of 1964.
hint: For each, ask who was included and who was not.
solution: The Declaration of Independence said that all men are created equal, but in 1776 slavery was legal and women and most Native people were excluded from politics. Abolitionists and women's rights advocates used the Declaration's language to argue for change. After the Civil War, the Thirteenth, Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments ended slavery, defined citizenship and equal protection, and barred race-based denial of the vote, but Reconstruction's protections weakened and Plessy allowed segregation. The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) extended the vote regardless of sex, yet many Black and Native Americans were still blocked from voting in practice. The civil rights movement used lawsuits like Brown, boycotts and marches to win the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965. Americans still disagree about questions such as how to remedy past discrimination and how rights should be balanced, but each expansion rested on arguments that the promise of equality should apply more widely.
rubric: Uses at least four accurate events or documents from different periods.
rubric: Describes the experiences of at least two groups.
rubric: Shows change over time, not only a list.
rubric: Acknowledges a real disagreement fairly.
