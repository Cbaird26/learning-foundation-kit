@article
In summer, ice cream sales go up. In summer, sunburns go up too. Does eating ice cream cause sunburn? That sounds silly, but why is it silly? Because we know there is a better explanation: hot, sunny weather makes people buy ice cream and also makes them burn.

### Correlation and causation

Two things are **correlated** when they tend to go up and down together (or one goes up when the other goes down). One thing **causes** another when it helps bring the other about. **Correlation does not prove causation.** A correlation is a clue, and sometimes a good clue, but it needs more thought.

### Four explanations for a correlation

Suppose A and B are correlated. Possible explanations:

1. **A causes B.** Studying more may cause better grades.
2. **B causes A** (**reverse causation**). Cities with more firefighters have more fires. The fires do not come from the firefighters; large fires bring more of them.
3. **A common cause C produces both.** Hot weather produces both ice cream sales and sunburns. Such a hidden C is also called a **lurking** or **confounding** factor.
4. **Coincidence.** With enough things to compare, some will line up by chance.

Often more than one explanation can be at work.

### Post hoc

"I wore my lucky socks, and we won, so the socks caused the win." This mistake, from the Latin *post hoc ergo propter hoc* ("after this, therefore because of this"), treats coming before as causing. One time after another is not enough.

### Testing a cause

To learn whether A causes B, scientists try a **controlled experiment**. They take a group of subjects and **randomly assign** some to get A and others not to, then keep everything else the same and compare the results. Randomness makes the two groups alike, so a difference in B is likely due to A. When an experiment is not possible, researchers collect many studies and look for hidden factors.

### Asking good questions

When you read "people who do X have more Y", ask: Could Y cause X? Could something else cause both? Could it be chance? How many cases were there?

@example
title: Hunting for a common cause
problem: A report finds that students with bigger shoe sizes score higher on reading tests. Does having bigger feet cause better reading?
step: Look for a third factor that could cause both. Older students tend to have bigger feet, and older students have had more years of reading practice.
step: So age is a common cause of both shoe size and reading score.
step: Among students of the same age, we would expect shoe size to tell us little about reading.
answer: No. Age is a common cause, so the correlation is not good evidence that big feet cause good reading.

@example
title: A fair test
problem: A gardener thinks a new fertilizer helps plants grow. She gave it to her best-looking plants and compared them to the rest. What is wrong, and what would be better?
step: She chose the plants herself, and picked the healthiest. They might have grown more anyway, so the groups were different from the start.
step: A better test randomly decides which plants get the fertilizer, for example by drawing numbers from a hat, and treats all plants otherwise alike.
step: If the fertilized plants then do better, the fertilizer is a likely cause.
answer: The groups were not alike to begin with. Random assignment would fix this.

@mistakes
- Saying "A and B go together, so A causes B." Check reverse causation, a common cause and coincidence first.
- Saying "correlation is useless". It is a real clue and often the first sign of a true cause.
- Assuming that if A came before B, A caused B.
- Thinking that an experiment with hand-picked groups is fair. Random assignment is what makes the groups comparable.

@practice ms
prompt: Months when more people wear scarves also have more colds. Which of these are possible explanations? Select all that apply.
choice*: Cold winter weather leads to both.
choice*: It is a coincidence in the data.
choice: Scarves cause colds, which has been proved by the correlation alone.
choice*: People who feel a cold coming on start wearing scarves.
hint: A correlation can have several explanations. None is proved by the correlation alone.
solution: Winter weather is a possible common cause, a coming cold leading to scarves is possible reverse causation, and coincidence is always possible. The claim that the correlation proves scarves cause colds is exactly what a correlation cannot do.

prompt: A gardener randomly gives fertilizer to 20 of 40 plants and none to the other 20. Of the fertilized plants, 17 grow taller than 30 cm. Of the unfertilized plants, 9 grow taller than 30 cm. By how many percentage points is the share of tall plants higher in the fertilized group?
answer: 40
unit: percentage points
check: (17 / 20 - 9 / 20) * 100
hint: Find the percent of tall plants in each group, then subtract.
solution: In the fertilized group 17 out of 20 is 85%. In the other group 9 out of 20 is 45%. The difference is 85 - 45 = 40 percentage points.

@practice self
prompt: A news story says, "Children who eat breakfast get better grades, so breakfast makes children smarter." Explain why a correlation alone does not prove this, giving at least two other explanations.
hint: Think of a common cause and of reverse causation.
solution: The correlation shows that breakfast eaters and good grades go together, but it does not show why. A common cause is possible: families with more stable routines may both provide breakfast and help with schoolwork. Reverse causation is also worth checking: students who are doing well and feel good about school may find it easier to get up on time and eat breakfast, so good grades could make breakfast more likely rather than the other way round. A controlled experiment that randomly gives some children breakfast would give better evidence.

@quiz mc
prompt: Every morning the rooster crows and then the sun rises. Ben concludes that the crowing makes the sun rise. What is the main problem?
choice*: Something happening before another thing does not show that it caused it.
choice: Roosters cannot crow.
choice: Ben should wake up earlier.
choice: The sun does not rise.
hint: Think of the "post hoc" mistake.
solution: Coming before is not causing. The sunrise has its own cause, and the rooster crows because dawn is near. The argument treats a sequence as proof of a cause.

@quiz num
prompt: On 15 hot days a town had sunburn cases on 12 of them. On 15 cool days it had sunburn cases on 3 of them. How many more hot days than cool days had sunburn cases?
answer: 9
unit: days
check: 12 - 3
hint: Subtract the two counts.
solution: 12 hot days minus 3 cool days is 9 more. The pattern is a correlation between hot weather and sunburn, and here the weather is a plausible cause of the sunburns.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, in your own words and with your own example, why a correlation does not prove a cause.
hint: Choose two things that go together and explain a common cause or reverse causation.
solution: Two things can go together without one causing the other. Example: people who carry umbrellas are more likely to be seen on wet days. Carrying an umbrella does not cause rain; rain causes people to carry umbrellas, which is reverse causation. Similarly, a hidden common cause or pure coincidence can make two things line up. So a correlation is a clue to investigate, not a proof.
