@article
A sneaker ad shows a famous runner crossing the finish line. A voice says, "Champions wear Zoom-Step." No one says, "Buy these shoes," but that is exactly what the ad wants you to do. Ads, news and chats are full of arguments, and many of them leave part of the argument unsaid.

### Where arguments hide

**Ads.** The conclusion is nearly always "buy this" or "choose this". The premises are the reasons offered: a famous face, a low price, "9 out of 10 users are happy". Ask: what does this ad want me to do or believe, and what is it giving me as the reason?

**News.** A news report mainly **describes**: who, what, when and where. But some pieces argue. An **editorial** or **opinion** piece has a conclusion ("The city should build a new bridge"), and even a headline can be a claim ("Phones make students less focused"). Ask whether the writer is reporting an event or trying to persuade you of something.

**Conversation.** People often argue in a hurry: "You're not coming? Everyone from our class is going!" The conclusion ("You should come") is implied.

### Unstated parts

An argument can leave out its conclusion or a premise. A missing premise that the argument needs is a **hidden** (or **unstated**) **premise**. In the sneaker ad, the hidden premise is something like "If champions wear a shoe, it is a good shoe for me." Writing the hidden premise out lets you decide whether you accept it. Do this fairly: add the premise the speaker most likely has in mind, not a silly one.

### Two cautions

- Not every ad, headline or post is an argument. "New flavor now in stores" only describes.
- Do not add premises the person never meant. Stay close to what they said.

### A method

1. Ask what the person wants me to accept or do. That is the conclusion.
2. List the reasons given. Those are the premises.
3. Ask whether a hidden premise connects the reasons to the conclusion.
4. Write it all in standard form.

@example
title: An ad with a hidden conclusion
problem: Set out the ad: "Brightsmile toothpaste. Used by thousands of families. Dentists in our town recommend it."
step: What does the ad want? You to use Brightsmile. The conclusion is never said, so supply it: "You should use Brightsmile toothpaste."
step: The reasons given are "Thousands of families use it" and "Dentists in our town recommend it."
step: A hidden premise links reasons to conclusion: "A toothpaste that many families use and dentists recommend is a good choice."
step: Standard form:

| P1 | Thousands of families use Brightsmile. |
| P2 | Dentists in our town recommend it. |
| P3 (hidden) | A toothpaste that many families use and dentists recommend is a good choice. |
| C | So you should use Brightsmile toothpaste. |
answer: The conclusion is that you should use Brightsmile; the argument also needs the hidden premise P3.

@example
title: Report or argument?
problem: Is this an argument? "The council met on Tuesday and voted 5 to 4 to close the old pool. Opponents say it is a loss for the town. Our view: the council was wrong, because the pool served thousands of children."
step: The first sentence only reports what happened, and the second reports what others say. Neither is an argument.
step: The last sentence has a clue word, "because", and a claim, "the council was wrong". It is the writer's own opinion backed by a reason.
step: So the passage contains one short argument inside a report.

| P1 | The pool served thousands of children. |
| C | So the council was wrong to close the pool. |
answer: Only the last sentence is an argument, with conclusion that the council was wrong.

@mistakes
- Assuming every ad is an argument. Some only describe a product, though many do try to get you to buy something.
- Inventing a weird hidden premise. State the one the speaker most likely believes.
- Calling a news report an argument just because it contains the word "because" or reports someone else's opinion.
- Missing an implied conclusion because nobody says "so".

@practice num
prompt: Six short passages are listed. (1) "Take the 8:15 bus." (2) "We should adopt a cat, because a cat is calm and our apartment is small." (3) "The match ended 2 to 1." (4) "Our mayor is wrong about the park, since a park helps children." (5) "Do you have a pencil?" (6) "You can trust this bakery: its reviews are all good." How many of the six are arguments?
answer: 3
unit: arguments
check: len([2, 4, 6])
hint: Look for a claim plus a reason offered for it. Commands, questions and plain reports do not count.
solution: Passages 2, 4 and 6 each state a claim and give reasons for it. Passage 1 is a command, 3 is a report and 5 is a question, so 3 passages are arguments.

@practice self
prompt: Mark the premises and the conclusion in each passage by writing P1, P2, ... and C. (a) "The lake is probably unsafe to swim in today. There was a big storm last night, and the sign at the gate says to stay out." (b) "Don't buy that jacket. It's expensive, and it won't be warm enough for winter." (c) "Our class should do a bake sale, because it is easy to run and everyone likes cookies."
hint: In each passage, first find what the speaker wants you to accept, then the reasons for it.
solution: (a) C: The lake is probably unsafe to swim in today. P1: There was a big storm last night. P2: The sign at the gate says to stay out. (b) C: Don't buy that jacket. P1: It is expensive. P2: It won't be warm enough for winter. (c) C: Our class should do a bake sale. P1: A bake sale is easy to run. P2: Everyone likes cookies.
rubric: Correctly identifies the conclusion in each of the three passages, including the one that comes first.
rubric: Lists the stated reasons as premises without adding reasons the passage does not give.
rubric: Writes each argument with premises first and the conclusion last or labels them clearly.
