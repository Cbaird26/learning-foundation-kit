@article
Ella says the town needs more crosswalks near the school. Ben replies, "Why listen to her? She failed science." Did Ben answer her? No. He said nothing about crosswalks. He moved the talk from her claim to her.

### Fallacies

A **fallacy** is a common mistake in reasoning that can seem convincing but does not give good support for a conclusion. **Fallacies of relevance** give reasons that have little or nothing to do with the claim. Three are very common.

### 1. Attacking the person (ad hominem)

An **ad hominem** ("against the person") argument attacks the speaker instead of the speaker's argument.

| P1 | Ella is bad at science. |
| C | So her claim that we need more crosswalks is wrong. |

Whether Ella is good at science has nothing to do with whether the street is dangerous. Be careful, though: it is **not** a fallacy to ask whether a source is trustworthy. If a witness did not see the event, or a company is paid to praise its own product, that is a good reason to look harder at what they say (as in week 6). The fallacy is to treat the attack as if it showed the claim false.

### 2. The straw man

A **straw man** misstates someone's position, usually making it weaker or sillier, and then attacks that version. Mia says, "We should have shorter recess breaks on test days." Leo answers, "So you want to cancel recess forever? That's unfair!" Leo attacked a view Mia never held. To avoid a straw man, state the other view in a way its holder would accept.

### 3. The appeal to popularity (bandwagon)

The **appeal to popularity** says a claim is true, or a choice is good, because many people believe it or do it. "Everyone at school has the Zing scooter, so it must be the best." Many people can be wrong, and fashions can spread without being good. Popularity may be a clue in some cases, for example that a restaurant is busy, but it is not proof, and it does not settle questions of truth.

### The fallacy fallacy

If an argument commits a fallacy, its **conclusion may still be true**. Ella may be right that crosswalks are needed. The fallacy shows only that Ben gave no good reason against it. After naming a fallacy, ask for a better argument, or look for one yourself.

### How to reply

Bring the talk back to the question: "My science grades have nothing to do with the street. Can we talk about crosswalks?" or "That is not what I said. I said shorter breaks only on test days."

@example
title: Naming a fallacy
problem: Which fallacy is this? "Kai says that we should recycle more at school. But Kai once left his trash on the bus, so he is a hypocrite and we should ignore him."
step: What does Kai claim? That the school should recycle more.
step: What is the reason offered against it? Something about Kai's own behavior, not about recycling.
step: That is an attack on the person, so it is an ad hominem.
step: The conclusion "ignore Kai's claim" does not follow. Whether or not Kai litters, recycling may still be a good idea.
answer: Ad hominem.

@example
title: Spotting a straw man
problem: Nora says, "I think we should be allowed to use calculators on part of the math test." Omar says, "Nora wants us to never learn any math, which is silly." What is wrong?
step: State what Nora said: calculators on part of the test.
step: State what Omar attacked: a view that nobody learns math at all.
step: Omar's version is far more extreme than Nora's, so he attacked a straw man.
answer: Straw man. A fair reply would address calculators on part of the test.

@mistakes
- Calling any criticism of a person ad hominem. Questioning a source's expertise or honesty can be a fair part of judging evidence; the fallacy is using the attack as the whole reply.
- Saying that a conclusion is false because the argument for it commits a fallacy. It may still be true.
- Thinking that popularity never matters. It can be a clue about taste or about what others have found useful, but it does not show that a claim is true.
- Confusing a straw man with a weak argument. A straw man puts words in someone else's mouth.

@practice self
prompt: Name the fallacy in each reply and write a short answer that returns to the real question. (a) Rosa: "We should add a quiet room to the library." Jay: "Rosa is just a bookworm, so ignore her." (b) Eli: "I think the dress code should allow hats indoors on cold days." Sam: "Eli wants everybody to wear whatever they like all day, so there would be chaos." (c) Pia: "Since half the class is voting for the zoo trip instead of the museum trip, the zoo trip must be the better choice."
hint: Ask in each case what the real claim was and whether the reason given bears on it.
solution: (a) Ad hominem. Answer: "Whether or not I like books, does a quiet room help students concentrate?" (b) Straw man. Answer: "I only said hats on cold days, not any clothes at any time. Is there a problem with hats on cold days?" (c) Appeal to popularity. Answer: "Many people voting for it does not show it is better. What are the reasons for the zoo trip over the museum?"
rubric: Names the correct fallacy in each of the three cases.
rubric: Each reply addresses the real claim and does not attack, distort or appeal to the crowd in turn.
rubric: Stays calm and polite.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a short example of an appeal to popularity about something students might do, such as a game, a snack or a trend. Then explain what is wrong with it and write a better argument for the same conclusion, if one exists.
hint: The better argument should give reasons about the thing itself, not about how many people like it.
solution: Fallacy: "Everyone in our grade is playing Block Quest, so it must be a great game." What is wrong: that many people play a game does not show it is well made or good for you; people join in because friends do. Better argument: "Block Quest is a great game because it can be played in short rounds, it needs teamwork, and reviewers who tested it found it fun and fair."
rubric: The first example is a real appeal to popularity, with the crowd as the only reason.
rubric: Explains why many people believing or doing something does not by itself make it true or good.
rubric: Offers a better argument with reasons about the thing itself, or explains why none is available.
