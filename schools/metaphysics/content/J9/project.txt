title: Argument portfolio
overview: You will collect five real arguments from ads, news articles or conversations, and study each one like a detective. You will map each into premises and a conclusion, judge it, name any fallacies, and finally rewrite the weakest as the best argument for its conclusion that you can make.
goal: Pick out premises, hidden premises and conclusions in real examples.
goal: Judge whether each argument is valid or strong, and whether its premises are true.
goal: Name fallacies and biases accurately, and explain what goes wrong.
goal: Build the strongest version of an argument, even one you disagree with.
step: Collect five arguments from different places: at least one ad, one news or opinion piece and one conversation or speech that you have heard. Write down each one exactly as it was said or written, or copy the link or name of the source. Choose short ones, 2 to 6 sentences each.
step: For each argument, find the conclusion first, then the premises. Write it in standard form (P1, P2, ..., C). Add any hidden premise the argument needs, and mark it "hidden".
step: Decide what kind of argument it is. If it aims to guarantee its conclusion, ask whether it is valid (could the premises be true and the conclusion false?). If it only aims to make its conclusion probable, ask whether it is strong (is the sample large and fair? is the evidence good?).
step: Judge the premises. For each one, say whether it is true, false or unknown, and why. Use what you learned about sources: expertise, evidence, interests and independent agreement. For causal claims, ask about correlation, common causes and reverse causation. For numbers, check the chances.
step: Look for fallacies and biases: attacking the person, straw man, appeal to popularity, equivocation, false dilemma, begging the question, confirmation bias, availability, anchoring, sunk cost. Name each one you find and quote the words that show it. If there is none, say so.
step: Choose the weakest of your five arguments, the one with the biggest problems. Rewrite it as the strongest argument you can for the same conclusion: fix the premises, add better evidence, remove fallacies, and state what a fair critic could still say against it.
step: Write a short reflection of 4 to 6 sentences on what you learned, including one belief that you were less sure about after the project.
deliverable: A portfolio with five entries, each with the source, the standard form, your judgments about validity or strength and about the premises, and any fallacies or biases.
deliverable: The rewritten strongest version of your weakest argument, with a short note on what changed and what objection remains.
deliverable: A reflection of 4 to 6 sentences.
rubric: Each of the five arguments has its conclusion and premises correctly identified, with hidden premises added only when needed.
rubric: Judgments about validity or strength and about the truth of the premises are reasoned, not just stated.
rubric: Every fallacy or bias named is the right one and is backed by quoted words and an explanation.
rubric: The rewritten argument is clearly stronger than the original and is fair about its remaining weaknesses.
rubric: The portfolio is respectful in tone and attacks arguments, not people.
safety: Use arguments from public ads, news and everyday talk. Do not include private information about classmates, friends or family; leave out names, and ask an adult before recording any conversation. If a topic upsets you, choose another example.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Arguments.** An argument gives premises as reasons for a conclusion. Commands, questions, descriptions and explanations are not arguments. Clue words: so, therefore (conclusion); because, since (premise).
2. **Real life.** Ads, news and chats hide conclusions and premises. Find the conclusion first, then the reasons, then any hidden premise.
3. **Validity.** An argument is valid when its premises cannot all be true while its conclusion is false. A valid argument with all true premises is sound. A counterexample shows invalidity.
4. **Patterns.** Modus ponens: if P then Q, P, so Q. Modus tollens: if P then Q, not Q, so not P. Syllogism: all A are B, all B are C, so all A are C. Invalid look-alikes: affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent.
5. **Induction.** A strong inductive argument makes its conclusion probable. Samples must be large enough and fair (representative, ideally random). A biased sample or a tiny one gives a hasty generalization.
6. **Sources.** Ask about expertise, evidence, first-hand knowledge, interests, independent agreement and recency. Bias is a reason to check, not proof of falsehood.
7. **Cause.** Correlation is not causation. Consider A causes B, B causes A, a common cause and coincidence. Controlled experiments with random assignment test causes.
8. **Chance.** Probability is favorable outcomes over all equally likely outcomes. The complement rule, expected counts and independent events (the gambler's fallacy).
9. **Relevance fallacies.** Ad hominem, straw man, appeal to popularity.
10. **Ambiguity and presumption.** Equivocation, false dilemma, begging the question.
11. **Biases.** Confirmation, availability, anchoring, sunk cost.
12. **Disagreeing well.** Charity, the strongest version of a view, kinds of disagreement (facts, values, meanings) and changing your mind for good reasons.

### Self-check

- Can I find the conclusion and premises of a short passage?
- Can I explain valid, sound, strong and weak, and give a counterexample?
- Can I name the four patterns and say which are valid?
- Can I estimate from a sample and spot a biased one?
- Can I explain why a correlation does not prove a cause?
- Can I calculate a simple probability?
- Can I name each fallacy and bias and say why it fails?
- Can I state a view fairly before replying to it?

### Common errors

- Confusing valid with true, and sound with valid.
- Treating a fallacy as proof that the conclusion is false.
- Calling every attack on a source an ad hominem.
- Thinking a big sample is good even if it is biased.
- Believing tails is "due" after a run of heads.
- Letting money already spent decide what to do next.

@exam mc
prompt: Consider this argument. "If the library is open, the lights are on. The lights are off. So the library is not open." Which description is correct?
choice*: It is valid, because it is modus tollens: the consequent is denied and the antecedent is rejected.
choice: It is invalid, because it affirms the consequent.
choice: It is invalid, because it denies the antecedent.
choice: It is unsound whatever the facts, because its conclusion is negative.
hint: Find the "if" statement and see which of its two parts the second premise denies.
solution: The second premise denies the "then" part, and the conclusion denies the "if" part. That is modus tollens, which is valid. Whether it is sound depends on whether its premises are in fact true, and a negative conclusion is no problem.

@exam num
prompt: A school has 900 students. In a random sample of 60 students, 21 have a pet. Use the sample to estimate how many of the 900 students have a pet.
answer: 315
unit: students
check: 21 / 60 * 900
hint: Find the fraction of the sample with a pet, then apply it to the whole school.
solution: In the sample, 21 out of 60 is 0.35. Applying 35% to 900 students gives 0.35 times 900 = 315. It is an estimate, and its quality depends on the sample being large enough and fair.

@exam self
prompt: Read this passage. "We should cut the school's outdoor time to add more math. Anyone who disagrees is just lazy. Besides, every other school in town already has more math time, so it must be the right thing to do. Anyway, either we add more math, or our test scores will drop." (a) Identify the conclusion, then name two fallacies in the passage and say what each one does wrong. (b) Write a stronger argument for the conclusion that the school should add more math time, and say one reply that a person who prefers outdoor time could make.
hint: Look for the claim the speaker wants you to accept, then look for reasons that are not relevant, attacks on a person, and options that are left out.
solution: (a) The conclusion is that the school should add more math time (by cutting outdoor time). Three fallacies occur; any two earn credit. Ad hominem: "Anyone who disagrees is just lazy" attacks people instead of answering their reasons. Appeal to popularity: other schools having more math time does not show that it is right for this school. False dilemma: adding math or watching test scores drop are not the only options, because other changes, like tutoring or better lessons, might raise scores. (b) Stronger version: P1: Students who spend more time on math practice tend to improve in math. P2: Test scores in math have been falling for two years. P3: Short, extra math lessons could be added without removing most outdoor time. C: So the school should add some math time. Reply: Outdoor time helps students pay attention and stay healthy, so cutting it might hurt learning too.
rubric: Identifies the conclusion correctly and names two fallacies that really occur in the passage (ad hominem, appeal to popularity or false dilemma), explaining each.
rubric: Builds a stronger argument in standard form that uses relevant premises instead of attacks and false choices.
rubric: States a fair reply that someone who prefers outdoor time could make.
