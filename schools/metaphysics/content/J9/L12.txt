@article
Two friends disagree about whether the school should have a longer recess. Lena says yes. Arjun says no. If each only shouts a weak version of the other's view, nobody learns anything. If each first says, "Here is what I think you mean," they may find out what the real disagreement is.

### The principle of charity

The **principle of charity** says: when you interpret someone's argument, choose the **most reasonable version** that fits what they said. Do not pick the silliest reading. Charity is not agreement. You can understand a view fairly and still disagree with it. It is the opposite of the straw man from week 9.

### The strongest version (steel man)

A **steel man** is the strongest version of a view that you can build: add the best reasons, fix gaps and drop weak points. If you can state the other side's view so well that its supporters say, "Yes, that is what I think," you really understand the disagreement. Then your reply, or your change of mind, is on firmer ground.

### What are you disagreeing about?

Disagreements come in different kinds:

- **Facts:** "Does a longer recess improve afternoon focus?" Evidence can help here.
- **Values:** "Is fun more important than more math time?" People can weigh things differently.
- **Meanings:** "What counts as a 'longer' recess?" Sometimes agreeing on what a word means ends the argument.

Finding the kind of disagreement tells you what to do next: look at evidence, discuss what matters, or define terms.

### Changing your mind

Changing your mind because of **new evidence** or a **better argument** is a sign of good thinking, not weakness. Changing it because someone is louder, because many people disagree, or because you are embarrassed is not the same. Good questions: What would convince me? What evidence would I accept? It is also reasonable to say, "I am not sure yet."

### Useful habits

1. Restate the other person's view and ask, "Have I got it right?"
2. Ask for reasons, and give yours.
3. Look for what you agree on.
4. Say what would change your mind.
5. Stay calm. Attack arguments, never people.

@example
title: A charitable restatement
problem: Ravi says, "Homework is pointless." Give a straw man restatement and a charitable restatement.
step: A **straw man** reading: "Ravi thinks students should never learn anything outside school." This is far more extreme than what he said.
step: A **charitable** reading: "Ravi thinks that a lot of the homework we get does not help us learn." This is a more reasonable view that fits his words.
step: Check with him: "Do you mean most homework does not help, or that there should be none at all?" His answer decides what you reply to.
answer: Charitable: much homework does not help learning. Then ask Ravi to confirm.

@example
title: What kind of disagreement is it?
problem: Two students agree that a new game costs 40 dollars and takes 10 hours to finish. One says it is worth it and the other says it is not. What kind of disagreement is this?
step: They already agree on the facts: the price and the length.
step: They differ on how to weigh them: how much 40 dollars and 10 hours of fun is worth.
step: That is a disagreement about values, so more facts will not settle it. They can compare what each cares about.
answer: A disagreement about values, not facts.

@mistakes
- Thinking charity means agreeing. You can state a view fairly and still reject it.
- Building a steel man of only your own side. The point is to understand the other side at its best.
- Changing your mind to avoid an argument or because many people disagree. Good reasons should do the work.
- Never changing your mind. Holding on only to win is a different mistake.
