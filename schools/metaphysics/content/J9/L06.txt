@article
A video says a certain berry cures colds. A friend shares it. A website selling the berry powder repeats it. Should you believe it? You cannot check everything yourself, so you must decide whom to trust. Good thinkers do this with questions.

### Evidence

**Evidence** is something that gives a reason to think a claim is true: an observation, a measurement, a record, or the testimony of someone who knows. A claim with no evidence is only an assertion. More and better evidence makes you more confident.

### Questions to ask about a source

1. **Expertise.** Is the person or group knowledgeable about this topic? A doctor knows more about medicine than a famous singer does.
2. **Evidence.** Does the source show what it relies on, such as data, records or named studies?
3. **First-hand or second-hand?** A **first-hand** source saw or measured the thing. A **second-hand** source is repeating what someone else said, and mistakes can creep in with each retelling.
4. **Interests.** Does the source gain from your believing the claim? A company has a reason to praise its own product.
5. **Agreement.** Do other **independent** sources, ones not simply copying each other, say the same thing?
6. **Recency.** Is the information up to date? Some facts change.

### Bias is a reason to check, not a proof of falsehood

A source with something to gain may still be right. The company might really have a good product. A reason to be careful is not a reason to say the claim is false. It is a reason to look for evidence from sources without that interest. (Later, you will see that attacking the speaker instead of the claim is a fallacy.)

### How much to trust a claim

Trust comes in **degrees**. A claim from an expert with open evidence, confirmed by independent sources, deserves a lot of trust. An unsigned post with no evidence deserves little. When sources disagree, ask which has better reasons. If experts disagree, it may be wise to hold the claim loosely.

### Check sideways

Instead of studying only one page, open other sources and see what they say about it and about its author. This is often the quickest way to learn whether a source can be trusted.

@example
title: Rating two sources
problem: You want to know if the school pool will reopen in June. Source A is a post by the city's pool manager on the official city site, with the repair schedule. Source B is a classmate who says, "My cousin heard it will reopen." Which deserves more trust?
step: Expertise and first-hand knowledge: the pool manager runs the pool and writes the schedule. The classmate is passing on what a cousin heard, a second- or third-hand report.
step: Evidence: Source A shows a schedule. Source B shows nothing.
step: Interests: neither seems to gain much from a false claim. So interests do not change the ranking much.
step: Source A is the better source, though a schedule can still change.
answer: Source A deserves more trust, because it is expert, first-hand and backed by a schedule.

@example
title: Reading a bias without jumping to a conclusion
problem: A juice company's website says its juice is "the healthiest in the world". What should you conclude?
step: The company gains from your believing this claim, so you should be careful.
step: That does not prove the claim false. Ask whether the page gives evidence and whether independent experts agree.
step: If there is no evidence and no independent support, trust the claim very little. It may be false, but you cannot tell from this source alone.
answer: Treat the claim cautiously and look for independent evidence.

@mistakes
- Trusting a source because it sounds sure of itself or is popular. Confidence is not evidence.
- Rejecting a claim only because the source has a motive. Check the evidence.
- Counting several sources that copy one another as independent agreement.
- Treating an expert in one field as an expert in every field.

choice*: FizzUp's own advertising team
choice: A government nutrition office that tests many drinks
choice: A scientist with no link to any drink company

solution: The advertising team earns money if you believe it, so it has a clear interest. The others do not gain from this claim. Having an interest means a need to check, not that the claim is false.

@practice num
prompt: A class rates five sources for a claim on a scale from 1 to 5. The scores are 4, 2, 5, 3 and 1. What is the mean (average) score?
answer: 3
check: statistics.mean([4, 2, 5, 3, 1])
hint: Add the five scores and divide by five.
solution: The scores add up to 4 + 2 + 5 + 3 + 1 = 15, and 15 divided by 5 is 3.

@practice self
prompt: Claim: "Eating breakfast helps students do better on tests." Source 1 is a university study of 2,000 students, published with its data. Source 2 is a cereal company's ad that says the same thing. Rate each source from 1 (low trust) to 5 (high trust) and explain each rating in one or two sentences.
hint: Use the questions about expertise, evidence, interests and agreement.
solution: Source 1: 4 out of 5. It comes from researchers, uses a large number of students and shares its data, so others can check it. I do not give 5 because one study is not the last word. Source 2: 2 out of 5. A cereal company gains if people eat breakfast cereal and shows no evidence, so I would not rely on it, although it could be right.

@quiz mc
prompt: Dana says, "I saw the accident myself." Eli says, "My neighbor told me about the accident." Which statement is correct?
choice*: Dana is a first-hand source and Eli is a second-hand source.
choice: Eli is a first-hand source and Dana is a second-hand source.
choice: Both are second-hand sources.
choice: Neither can be a source.
hint: Who observed the event?
solution: Dana observed the accident herself, which makes her a first-hand source. Eli is repeating what someone else said. First-hand reports can still be mistaken, but they have fewer links in the chain.

@quiz mc
prompt: A store's website says its mattress is the best. Which response is most reasonable?
choice*: Be careful, since the store gains from the claim, and look for evidence from independent reviewers.
choice: Believe it, because stores know their products.

solution: The store has an interest, so a cautious reader looks for independent evidence. Believing it automatically ignores the interest, and rejecting it automatically treats the motive as proof of falsehood.
