@article
Maya says, "We should get a dog. Dogs make you exercise, and Dad wants to walk more." Is Maya just talking, or is she doing something more? She is **arguing**: she gives reasons to get you to accept a claim.

### Statements and arguments

A **statement** is a sentence that is either true or false: "Dogs need walks" is a statement. Questions ("Who wants a dog?") and commands ("Please feed the dog") are not true or false, so they are not statements.

An **argument** is a group of statements in which some of them, the **premises**, are offered as reasons to accept another one, the **conclusion**. In everyday life "argument" can mean a quarrel, but in logic it means reasons plus the claim they are meant to support.

In Maya's argument, the conclusion is "We should get a dog". The premises are "Dogs make you exercise" and "Dad wants to walk more".

### Not everything is an argument

- **A description** tells what is so: "The gym has two doors and a clock." No claim is being supported.
- **A command or a question** is not a statement.
- **An explanation** tells why something happened, when everyone already agrees that it did: "The game was cancelled because of thunder." An argument tries to show that something is true; an explanation tries to show why a known fact is so.

### Clue words

**Conclusion clues:** so, therefore, thus, hence, it follows that. **Premise clues:** because, since, for, given that. Be careful: "because" can start either a reason in an argument or an explanation. Clue words help, but the best test is to ask, **"What is this person trying to get me to accept, and what are the reasons?"** The conclusion can come last, first or in the middle.

### Standard form

To see an argument clearly, write the premises first and the conclusion last:

| P1 | Dogs make you exercise. |
| P2 | Dad wants to walk more. |
| C | So we should get a dog. |

Once an argument is written like this, you can ask the two big questions of this course: do the premises really support the conclusion, and are the premises true?

@example
title: Finding the conclusion
problem: Write this in standard form: "The cafeteria should add a salad bar. Many students want healthier food, and a salad bar is healthy."
step: Ask what the speaker wants you to accept. "The cafeteria should add a salad bar" is the claim the rest of the passage backs up, so it is the conclusion.
step: The other two sentences give reasons for it: "Many students want healthier food" and "a salad bar is healthy". There are no clue words, but each one answers "why should I accept the conclusion?".
step: Write the premises first and the conclusion last.

| P1 | Many students want healthier food. |
| P2 | A salad bar is healthy. |
| C | So the cafeteria should add a salad bar. |
answer: Two premises and the conclusion that the cafeteria should add a salad bar.

@example
title: Conclusion in the middle of the passage
problem: Find the conclusion: "Since the library closes at 5:00 and it is already 4:45, we should hurry, and I think the bus will be late too."
step: "Since" signals a premise, so "the library closes at 5:00" and "it is already 4:45" are reasons.
step: What do they support? "We should hurry." That is the claim being backed.
step: "I think the bus will be late too" is an extra opinion with no reason given, so it is not part of this argument.
answer: The conclusion is "We should hurry"; the premises are the closing time and the time now.

@mistakes
- Treating every sentence as a premise or conclusion. Some sentences are questions, commands or side remarks that do no work in the argument.
- Assuming the conclusion is always the last sentence. Many arguments begin with the conclusion.
- Thinking that "because" always marks an argument. "The game was cancelled because of thunder" explains a fact; it does not try to prove it.
- Thinking an argument is a fight. In logic, an argument can be calm, polite and even in your own head.
