@article
You taste one spoonful of soup from a big pot and say, "This soup is too salty." You did not taste the whole pot, but if it was stirred, you are probably right. You went from a small part to the whole. That is **inductive reasoning**.

### Induction

In a **deductive** argument, the premises are meant to guarantee the conclusion. In an **inductive** argument, the premises make the conclusion **probable**, but not certain. Inductive arguments are not valid or invalid; they are **strong** or **weak**. A strong inductive argument gives good reason to expect the conclusion, but the conclusion could still be false.

### Samples and populations

A very common kind of induction is a **generalization**: you study a **sample**, a small part of a group, and draw a conclusion about the whole **population**.

| P1 | In a sample of 40 students, 28 take the bus. |
| C | So about 70% of all students at the school take the bus. |

Here the population is every student at the school. Two things decide how strong the argument is.

**1. Size.** A bigger sample usually tells you more. Meeting two rude people does not tell you much about a whole town.

**2. Fairness.** A good sample is **representative**: it looks like the population. The best way to get one is a **random** sample, where everyone has an equal chance of being picked. A **biased** sample leaves out some kinds of members. If you ask only the chess club what the school's favorite hobby is, you will get a skewed answer.

### Hasty generalization

Concluding something about a whole group from a tiny or unfair sample is a **hasty generalization**. "My two cousins are bad at math, so everyone in that family is" is hasty.

### Counterexamples

A generalization like "all ducks are brown" can be defeated by one counterexample, a single white duck. A generalization like "most ducks are brown" survives a few exceptions. Notice what the word "all" demands.

### Using a sample

If a random sample has a fraction that does something, you can estimate that fraction for the population. If 28 of 40 sampled students take the bus, that is $28/40 = 70\%$. At a school of 500 students, you would expect about 350 bus riders. It will not be exact, but a fair sample gets you close.

@example
title: Estimating a whole group
problem: A school has 600 students. In a random sample of 40 students, 14 are in a club. About how many of the 600 students are in a club?
step: Find the fraction in the sample: 14 out of 40 is 0.35, or 35%.
step: Apply it to the population: 0.35 times 600 equals 210.
step: This is an estimate, because a different random sample might give a slightly different fraction.
answer: About 210 students.

@example
title: Is the sample fair?
problem: A poll asks only the students waiting in line at the pizza window, "Is the cafeteria food good?" Is the sample good?
step: Who is in the sample? Students who chose to buy pizza, who probably like the cafeteria's food.
step: Students who do not like the food may bring lunch from home and are missing.
step: The sample is biased, so the conclusion "most students think the food is good" is weak, no matter how large the sample is.
answer: The sample is biased, so the generalization is weak.

@mistakes
- Thinking a large sample is automatically good. A huge but biased sample can mislead.
- Treating an inductive conclusion as certain. A strong argument still leaves room for error.
- Calling inductive arguments "invalid". Use strong and weak for them, and valid and invalid for deductive ones.
- Forgetting that "all" claims are broken by one exception, while "most" claims are not.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why a larger sample is usually more reliable, and then explain why a large sample is still not enough on its own.
hint: Think of one extra case at a time, and of who might be left out.
solution: A larger sample is less likely to be thrown off by a few unusual cases, so its percentages tend to be closer to the population's. But if the sample leaves out some kinds of members, such as asking only 1,000 people at a sports stadium about their favorite sport, adding more people of the same kind does not fix the bias. Size helps only when the sample is also fair.
