@article
Imagine you believe your favorite team is the best. You read ten articles about the season. Which ones do you remember? Probably the ones that praise your team. Your mind did not lie to you, but it quietly leaned one way. That lean is called a bias.

### Bias

A **cognitive bias** is a regular tendency in how our minds work that can push our thinking away from good reasoning. Everyone has biases, including scientists and teachers. Knowing about them does not make them vanish, but it helps us catch them. Four common ones follow.

### Confirmation bias

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to notice, remember and believe evidence that agrees with what we already think, and to overlook evidence against it. **Habit:** actively look for evidence that could show you are wrong, and ask someone who disagrees to explain why.

### The availability heuristic

A **heuristic** is a mental shortcut. With the **availability heuristic**, we judge how common or likely something is by how easily examples come to mind. Dramatic or recent stories, such as a rare accident on the news, are easy to recall, so we may think such events are more common than they are. **Habit:** look at real numbers instead of what comes to mind first.

### Anchoring

**Anchoring** is the tendency to let the first number or fact we meet influence later guesses. A shirt marked "Was 100 dollars, now 60 dollars" feels like a bargain, but the 100 was only an anchor. If the shirt is worth 30 dollars, 60 is not a good price. **Habit:** make your own estimate before looking at the offered number, and compare prices elsewhere.

### The sunk cost trap

**Sunk costs** are money, time or effort already spent that cannot be recovered. The **sunk cost** bias is continuing something just because we have already spent on it. You paid for a movie ticket, and the movie is terrible. Staying means losing another two hours as well as the price. The ticket money is gone either way, so the sensible question is: **from now on, which choice is better?** **Habit:** ask what you would choose if you had not paid anything yet.

### Reasoning about bias

Spotting a bias in someone else, or yourself, does not show that a conclusion is false. Your team may really be the best. It shows only that the thinking behind the belief needs another look. Be gentle: biases are normal, and the point is to improve.

@example
title: Which bias?
problem: A student fears flying after seeing a news story about a plane problem. She says, "Flying must be very dangerous." Which bias could be at work, and what would help?
step: A vivid news story is easy to recall, so she judges flying by that memory.
step: That is the availability heuristic.
step: A helpful habit is to look for real figures that compare how often problems happen with how many flights take place.
answer: The availability heuristic; check real numbers.

@example
title: Spotting the sunk cost
problem: Theo paid 15 dollars for a game and has played ten hours without enjoying it. He says, "I must keep playing so my 15 dollars is not wasted." What is the mistake?
step: The 15 dollars is gone whether he continues or stops: it is a sunk cost.
step: The useful question is whether the next hour of playing is worth more to him than the next hour of something else.
step: If it is not, quitting is the better choice, even though the money cannot be recovered.
answer: Sunk cost bias: only future costs and benefits should decide.

@mistakes
- Thinking that you are not biased. Everyone is, and believing otherwise makes it worse.
- Saying that someone's belief is false because they are biased. Bias is a reason to check, not a proof of error.
- Mixing up the availability heuristic with confirmation bias. One is about what comes to mind easily, the other about favoring evidence that agrees with you.
- Treating the money or time already spent as a reason to keep going.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why knowing that you have a bias does not prove that your belief is false. Then say what a person with that bias should do next.
hint: Compare "my thinking may be biased" with "my conclusion is wrong".
solution: A bias shows that the way I reached the belief may have been unreliable, not that the belief is false. For example, I might favor evidence for my team because I love them, and my team might still be the best. The right step is to check the belief with better evidence, such as full records, and to ask someone who disagrees.
