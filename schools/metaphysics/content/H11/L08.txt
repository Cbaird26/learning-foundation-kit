@article
Numbers feel objective, but how they are chosen, displayed and framed is part of an argument. A reader who can interpret data and notice distortion is harder to mislead, and a writer who presents data honestly earns credibility.

### Percent, percentage point and percent change

A **percentage point** is a difference between two percentages. If a rate rises from 20% to 25%, it rose by 5 percentage points. It rose by 25 **percent**, because the relative change is
$$\frac{25 - 20}{20} = 0.25.$$
In general, **percent change** $= \dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100$. The base matters: a fall of 50 percent followed by a rise of 50 percent does not return to the start (100 becomes 50, then 75).

### Counts, rates and denominators

Raw counts mislead when populations differ. A city with 600 cases and a population of 1,200,000 and a town with 30 cases and 10,000 people: the city has more cases but the town has a higher rate (30 per 10,000 versus 5 per 10,000). Always ask for the denominator, the time period and the definition of the thing counted.

### Averages

The **mean** is the sum divided by the number of values. The **median** is the middle value of the ordered list. A few extreme values pull the mean but not the median, so for skewed data such as incomes or hospital stays, the median can describe a typical case better. Report which one you are using.

### Reading graphs honestly

Check these before trusting a graph:

- **Axes**: Do they start at zero? A truncated bar chart makes a small difference look large. (Line graphs may start above zero if the change, not the level, is the point, but the scale should be stated.)
- **Range**: Does the time window hide a longer trend? Choosing the start year can reverse a story.
- **Scale**: Are two graphs on different scales next to each other?
- **Labels and units**: Are they present and clear?
- **Source**: Where did the data come from, and who made the graph?

Correlation in a graph does not prove cause. Two lines that rise together may both follow a third factor, or be coincidence.

### Using visuals in your own writing

Introduce a table or figure in the text, point the reader to the key feature, number and title it, cite its source, and make sure the text says what the figure shows. A graph should simplify an idea, not decorate a page.

### Checking a science news article against its study

1. Find the original study (title, authors, journal).
2. Identify the design, sample, and main outcome.
3. List each claim in the news article, then locate the matching result.
4. Check wording: did "associated with" become "causes"? Did a result in mice or one small group become "people"?
5. Compare numbers, including whether a relative figure was presented without the absolute one.
6. Summarize accurately, with the hedges and limitations the authors give.

@example
title: Checking a claim with percent change and rate
problem: Invented news line: "Emergency visits for sports injuries jumped 40% at Lakeview High." The study reports 50 visits in 2022 and 70 visits in 2023, from a school of 1,000 students both years. Check the claim and state a fair summary.
step: Compute percent change: $\frac{70 - 50}{50} \times 100 = 40\%$. The number is correct.
step: Check the base. The enrollment was the same, so the rate went from 50 to 70 per 1,000 students, an increase of 20 per 1,000 (2 percentage points).
step: Check wording and context. The study covers one school and two years, so it cannot show a trend or a cause.
step: Fair summary: "At one school, sports-injury emergency visits rose from 50 to 70 per 1,000 students between 2022 and 2023, a 40% increase; the data come from two years and cannot show why."

@mistakes
- Saying "percent" when you mean "percentage point," or the reverse.
- Comparing raw counts for groups of very different sizes.
- Judging a graph by its shape without checking the axes and range.
- Repeating a news claim without checking it against the study's actual result.

@practice num
prompt: Town A had 45 cases among 15,000 residents. Town B had 60 cases among 40,000 residents. How many cases per 10,000 residents does Town A have?
answer: 30
check: 45 / 15000 * 10000
tol: 0.005
hint: Divide cases by population, then scale to 10,000.
solution: $\frac{45}{15000} \times 10000 = 30$ per 10,000. (Town B has 15 per 10,000, so Town A has the higher rate despite fewer cases.)

@practice self
prompt: Use this invented material. News article: "Walking 10,000 steps a day cuts the risk of heart disease in half." Study summary: An observational study followed 3,000 adults for five years. Of those who averaged fewer than 4,000 steps daily, 8% developed heart disease. Of those who averaged more than 8,000, 4% did. The authors note that active people may differ in diet and health. Write a five-sentence check that compares the article's claims with the study and gives a fair summary.
hint: Compare each claim in the article with the study: cause versus association, the 10,000-step threshold versus the 8,000 and 4,000 groups, and the 8% and 4% rates.
hint: Work out the absolute difference (8% minus 4%) and the relative change (that difference divided by 8%), then end with one hedged sentence summarizing what the study can support.
solution: The article says walking "cuts" heart disease risk "in half," which implies cause. The study was observational, so it shows an association, and the authors themselves warn that diet and baseline health may differ. The halving is a relative figure: 8% to 4% is a reduction of 4 percentage points, or 50 percent. The article also changed the exposure from "more than 8,000" steps to "10,000" steps, a threshold the study did not test. A fair summary: In a five-year study of 3,000 adults, those averaging more than 8,000 steps a day had heart disease at 4%, compared with 8% for those under 4,000, but the design cannot show that walking caused the difference.
rubric: Notes the design and the difference between association and cause.
rubric: Reports both the absolute and the relative figure correctly.
rubric: Identifies at least one mismatch between article and study.
rubric: Ends with a fair, hedged summary.

@quiz num
prompt: A hospital's infection rate fell from 5% to 4%. By what percent did the infection rate fall? Give the number.
answer: 20
unit: %
check: (5 - 4) / 5 * 100
tol: 0.005
hint: This asks for percent change, not percentage points.
solution: The change is $1$ percentage point, and $\frac{1}{5} = 20\%$.
