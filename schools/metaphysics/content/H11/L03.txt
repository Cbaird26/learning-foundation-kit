@article
**Rhetoric** is the study of how language persuades or moves an audience in a particular situation. A **rhetorical analysis** does not ask "Is this argument right?" It asks "How does this text try to work, and how well does that fit its situation?" Think of it as explaining how a magic trick is done, not whether you liked the show.

### The rhetorical situation

Every text sits inside a situation with five parts:

- **Speaker or writer**: who is talking, and with what credibility?
- **Audience**: who is meant to be persuaded, and what do they already believe?
- **Purpose**: what is the text trying to get the audience to think, feel or do?
- **Context**: when, where and in what medium?
- **Exigence**: the problem or occasion that makes the text necessary.

Change any part and the best choices change. A hospital's notice to patients, a campaign speech and a letter to the editor about the same clinic would sound different because the audience and purpose differ.

### The appeals

Aristotle named three means of persuasion, and a fourth idea, kairos, completes the toolkit.

| Appeal | What it works on | Typical moves |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethos** | Trust in the speaker | Credentials, shared values, fairness, correct and careful language |
| **Pathos** | Emotion and imagination | Vivid examples, stories, loaded words, imagery |
| **Logos** | Reason | Evidence, statistics, logical structure, definitions |
| **Kairos** | Timing | Why now? Using the moment or occasion |

A strong text usually blends them. When Lincoln closes his Second Inaugural Address (1865) with "With malice toward none, with charity for all", the balanced phrasing and the plea for reconciliation at the end of a war combine pathos and ethos: the speaker presents himself as generous and asks the audience to share the posture. The occasion itself, a president speaking as the war nears its end, supplies the kairos.

### Moving from labeling to analysis

The beginner's trap is **listing**: "The author uses ethos, pathos and logos." That names tools without explaining anything. Analysis connects a choice to an effect on a specific audience, using a verb of function (frames, narrows, softens, disarms, escalates).

Compare:

- Weak: "The writer uses pathos by telling a story about a child."
- Stronger: "By opening with a single child waiting alone in a clinic, the writer makes a statistic about waiting times personal, which prepares skeptical readers to accept the numbers that follow."

Also look beyond appeals to **structure** (order, repetition, parallelism, transitions), **diction** (word choice and connotation), **tone** and **evidence selection**.

### Writing the analysis

A good thesis names the speaker's purpose, the main strategies and the effect: "To persuade a skeptical school board to fund a nursing program, the author pairs local data with a patient story, so the audience sees both the scale of the problem and its human cost." Each body paragraph takes one strategy, quotes a specific piece of the text, and explains how it works on the audience.

@example
title: From a list to an analysis
problem: Invented passage by a community health director addressing a town council: "Last year, 312 residents waited more than six hours in our emergency room. I have worked in this town for twenty years, and I have never been prouder of our staff or more worried about their limits. Tonight I am asking you to approve funding while the budget is still open." Write one analytical sentence about ethos.
step: Find the choice. The speaker mentions twenty years in the town and pride in the staff.
step: Name the audience and purpose. The audience is a town council deciding on funding, and the purpose is to win approval.
step: Use a verb of function. The long service establishes familiarity and loyalty, which makes the warning about limits credible rather than alarmist.
step: Write it. "By stressing twenty years of service and pride in the staff, the director presents himself as a loyal insider, so his warning about the staff's limits reads as concern rather than complaint."

@mistakes
- Listing appeals ("uses ethos and pathos") without explaining what they do for this audience.
- Arguing for or against the position instead of analyzing the strategy.
- Quoting without explaining, or explaining without quoting.
- Ignoring the audience, so that every choice looks equally effective.

@practice self
prompt: Read this invented passage from a pharmacist writing to patients: "You trust us with your medicines every month. We have checked every refill twice, and last year we caught 41 possible interactions before they reached a patient. Please read the new label carefully; it was redesigned to prevent mistakes." Write a thesis plus two sentences of analysis explaining how the pharmacist persuades the audience to accept the new label.
hint: Write the thesis first: name the pharmacist's purpose, two strategies (for example, the relationship in "You trust us" and the figure of 41 interactions) and their effect on patients.
hint: For each analysis sentence, quote a few words, use a verb such as builds, proves or reassures, and say what it does to a patient reading a new label.
solution: Thesis: To persuade patients to trust and read the redesigned label, the pharmacist combines relational language with concrete evidence of care. The opening "You trust us" builds ethos by acknowledging an existing relationship, which makes the request feel collaborative. The figure of 41 caught interactions is logos that proves the double-checking is real, so the redesign reads as a continuation of safety, not a sudden change.
rubric: Thesis names purpose, strategies and effect, not just appeals.
rubric: Quotes or cites specific words from the passage.
rubric: Uses verbs of function and ties each choice to the audience.
