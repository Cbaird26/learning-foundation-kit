@project
title: A researched argument on a health-policy question, plus a personal essay draft
overview: You will write an argument of about 1,500 words on a health-policy question, using five cited sources and presenting the main positions fairly, and you will draft a personal essay of up to 650 words. The project uses everything in the course: reading sources critically, synthesizing them, building and defending a claim, writing clearly, citing correctly and revising.
goal: Build a focused, arguable claim about a health-policy question and defend it with evidence from five credible sources.
goal: Present at least two opposing positions fairly and answer the strongest objection to your claim.
goal: Cite sources accurately in APA or MLA style, with an in-text citation for every borrowed idea and a matching reference list.
goal: Draft a personal essay that uses a specific scene and honest reflection.
step: Choose a question that a reasonable person could answer in more than one way, such as "Should schools start later?", "Should sugary drinks carry a tax?", "Should vaccines be required for school attendance?" or "Should nurses be allowed to prescribe more medicines independently?" Narrow it by place, population or policy detail, and write it as a research question.
step: Plan and run a search with keywords and Boolean operators. Collect at least eight candidate sources, then choose five that include at least two scholarly sources and at least two that represent different positions. Evaluate each for authority, accuracy, currency, purpose and relevance.
step: Take notes on each source, labeled as quotation, paraphrase or your own thought, with page numbers. For each source write a short annotation (summary, evaluation, relevance) in APA style, and check whether any claim you plan to use is stronger than the source's own wording.
step: Write an outline: a claim, two or three reasons, the strongest objection and your response. For any statistic you use, compute the absolute and the relative change, and state the denominator, time period and study design.
step: Draft the researched argument of about 1,500 words. Open with the question and why it matters, present the main positions fairly, state your claim, develop reasons organized by idea (not source by source), include a counterargument section with a concession or rebuttal, and end with what follows from your position. Cite as you write.
step: Revise for style: cut wordiness and empty openers, prefer active verbs, check parallelism, vary sentence length and read the draft aloud. Then verify every citation against your reference list and your notes.
step: Draft a personal essay (up to 650 words) that begins in a specific moment, develops a turning point and ends with honest reflection. Revise it using a trusted reader's answer to "What did you learn about me?"
deliverable: The researched argument (about 1,500 words), with in-text citations and a References or Works Cited list of five sources.
deliverable: An APA annotated bibliography of the five sources.
deliverable: A personal essay draft of up to 650 words, plus a short note naming one change you made after revising.
rubric: The claim is specific and arguable, and the essay stays focused on it throughout.
rubric: Sources are credible, varied and used as evidence; the paragraphs are organized by idea, with the writer's own analysis.
rubric: At least two opposing positions are stated fairly, and the strongest objection is answered by conceding, qualifying or rebutting.
rubric: Citations are accurate and consistent in one style, with no uncited borrowed ideas and no overstated source claims.
rubric: The prose is clear and concise, with varied sentences and an appropriate voice; the personal essay uses a specific scene and real reflection.
tutor: Here is my claim and my strongest objection. Is my objection a fair version of what critics would say, or a straw man?
tutor: Here is a paragraph where I paraphrase a source. Is it genuinely in my own words, and have I kept the source's level of certainty?
tutor: Here are the opening and ending of my personal essay. What is a reader likely to learn about me from them, and what is missing?

@review
### Week by week

- **Weeks 1 and 2, literature in context.** Context is specific history, audience and literary convention tied to a detail of the text. Use detail, context, interpretation. Know the broad periods of American and British literature and how satire and translation shape meaning.
- **Week 3, rhetorical analysis.** Describe the rhetorical situation (speaker, audience, purpose, context, exigence). Explain how ethos, pathos, logos and kairos work on a specific audience, using verbs of function. Analyze strategy; do not judge the position.
- **Week 4, synthesis.** Organize by idea, not by source. Make your own claim, integrate sources with signal phrases, and do not strengthen a source's claim.
- **Week 5, argument.** An arguable claim, reasons, evidence and warrant. Answer the strongest objection by conceding, qualifying or rebutting. Know the main fallacies.
- **Week 6, style.** Concision, active verbs, parallelism and sentence variety; emphasis falls at the end of a sentence.
- **Week 7, science and medical writing.** IMRaD, study designs and what they can show. $ARR = c - t$, $RRR = (c - t)/c$, $NNT = 100/(c - t)$ with percentages. Association is not causation.
- **Week 8, data and visuals.** Percent versus percentage point, percent change, rates with denominators, mean versus median, and honest graphs. Check news claims against the study.
- **Weeks 9 and 10, research and citation.** Research question, Boolean search, source evaluation, careful notes, and APA and MLA formats for in-text citations and reference lists; annotated bibliographies.
- **Weeks 11 and 12, personal and professional writing.** Show with a specific scene, then reflect; avoid clichés. Emails need a specific subject line, early purpose, a clear request and protection of private information.

### Self-check

- Can I state my claim and the strongest objection in one sentence each?
- Is every statistic given with its base, denominator and design?
- Does every paraphrase have a citation, and do citations match the reference list?
- Did I read the essay aloud and cut filler?

### Common errors

- Listing appeals or sources instead of explaining how they work.
- Treating observational findings as proof of cause.
- Mixing up percent and percentage point.
- Using APA in-text style with an MLA list, or the reverse.
- Burying the request in an email.

@exam num
prompt: In a randomized trial, 2,000 adults received a placebo and 2,000 received a medicine. Over two years, 5% of the placebo group and 4% of the medicine group had a heart attack. About how many fewer heart attacks occurred in the medicine group than would be expected if it had the placebo group's rate? Give the number.
answer: 20
check: 2000 * (0.05 - 0.04)
tol: 0.005
hint: Find the absolute risk reduction in percentage points.
hint: Convert the ARR to a proportion and multiply by the number of treated adults.
solution: ARR $= 5\% - 4\% = 1$ percentage point $= 0.01$. Among $2000$ treated adults, $2000 \times 0.01 = 20$ fewer heart attacks.

@exam mc
prompt: A student writes a synthesis paragraph on a school policy. It opens, "Source 1 says the policy raised attendance by 3 percentage points, Source 2 says costs rose, and Source 3 says parents liked it." The paragraph then describes each source in turn and ends without a conclusion. Which revision would most improve it?
choice: Add a fourth source with another summary.
choice*: Open with an original claim about the policy's benefits and costs, then group sources by idea and end with the writer's conclusion.
choice: Replace the sources' names with the word "research."
choice: Remove the numbers so the paragraph sounds less technical.
hint: The weakness is organization by source with no claim.
solution: The paragraph is a source parade. A claim, grouping by idea and the writer's own analysis turn it into a synthesis. Adding sources, hiding names or removing numbers would not fix the structure.

@exam self
prompt: Use this invented excerpt from a speech by a county health director: "Last year, 410 children in our county missed more than twenty days of school because of untreated asthma. I have spent fifteen years in this county's clinics, and I know these families. Approve the school nurse program this month, before the budget closes." (a) Write a two-sentence analysis of how the speaker persuades the council, naming at least two appeals and their effect. (b) State the strongest objection a council member could raise, and write a three-sentence response that concedes a true point and rebuts or qualifies.
hint: Tie each appeal to a quoted detail and to what it does for a council deciding on funding.
hint: Choose an objection a thoughtful critic would find serious, such as cost, and answer it with a reason.
solution: (a) The director uses logos by giving a specific figure, 410 children missing more than twenty days, which makes the problem measurable for a council that must justify spending. He builds ethos with "fifteen years in this county's clinics," presenting himself as an experienced insider whose request is credible, and he uses kairos by asking for approval "this month, before the budget closes." (b) Objection: the program costs money the county may not have, and not every absence is caused by asthma. Response: The cost is a real concern, so the council could fund the program for one year and require a report on attendance and emergency visits. Although absences have several causes, the figure concerns untreated asthma specifically, which a nurse can address directly. A time-limited trial lets the county test the benefit before committing long term.
rubric: Names at least two appeals and ties each to a quoted detail and an effect on the audience.
rubric: States a fair, strong objection rather than a straw man.
rubric: Response concedes, qualifies or rebuts with a reason.
rubric: Uses analysis verbs and avoids merely agreeing or disagreeing with the speaker.
