@article
If you say "I promise to come", you do not report anything: you promise. If you say "he did it by accident" and "he did it by mistake", you may be marking different things. Around the middle of the century, philosophers at Oxford and Cambridge asked whether the problems of philosophy could be cleared up by paying attention to such everyday distinctions. This is **ordinary language philosophy**. It is not a claim that ordinary language is infallible. J. L. Austin said, in "A Plea for Excuses" (1956 to 1957), that ordinary language is the first word and not the last: it embodies distinctions that generations found worth drawing, and philosophers should start from them and may then improve on them.

### Austin and speech acts

In *How to Do Things with Words* (1962, based on his 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard) Austin began by distinguishing **constatives**, which state facts and are true or false, from **performatives** such as "I name this ship the *Dawn*" and "I bet you it will rain", which are not true or false but do something. Performatives can go wrong in ways other than falsehood: they can **misfire** (the procedure is not accepted or is wrongly carried out, as when a bystander names the ship) or be an **abuse** (the speaker has no intention to do what he says). These are **felicity conditions**. Austin ended by abandoning the neat contrast, since even stating is a kind of act, and distinguished three aspects of any utterance. The **locutionary act** is saying something with a sense and reference; the **illocutionary act** is what one does in saying it (warning, promising, ordering); the **perlocutionary act** is what one brings about by saying it (persuading, alarming). In *Sense and Sensibilia* (published 1962) Austin attacked A. J. Ayer's argument from illusion for sense-data by examining carefully how words like "seems" and "looks" are used.

### Ryle and the category mistake

Gilbert Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) argues that the Cartesian picture of the mind as a ghostly inner theater, which he called the dogma of the ghost in the machine, rests on a **category mistake**: treating a concept as if it belonged to a logical category to which it does not. Someone shown the stalls, the stallholders and the customers of a market who asks "but where is the market?" has made such a mistake. Ryle holds that mental concepts are chiefly about dispositions and abilities to behave in certain ways, not inner events. A central chapter distinguishes **knowing how** from **knowing that**: knowing how to ride a bicycle is not a matter of knowing propositions. His argument against the "intellectualist legend":

| P1 | If every intelligent act required a prior act of contemplating a proposition or rule that guides it, then there would be an infinite regress of prior acts. |
| P2 | There is no infinite regress of prior acts that must be completed before an intelligent act. |
| C | So not every intelligent act requires a prior act of contemplating a guiding proposition. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). In 2001 Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson, in "Knowing How", denied the link between intellectualism and the regress: knowing how to do something is knowing a proposition (that a certain way is a way to do it), but this need not involve a prior act of contemplation, so the regress does not arise. This attacks P1: intellectualism about know-how does not entail that each act is preceded by a contemplation.

### Strawson and referring

Peter Strawson's "On Referring" (1950) replied to Russell's theory of descriptions. He distinguished a sentence from the *use* of it to say something: whether "The King of France is wise" is true or false depends on whether anyone uses it to refer to a king, and if there is no king the question of truth does not arise, since the sentence presupposes one. Russell's reply (1957) was that ordinary use is not decisive for logic. The dispute illustrates the method and its limits.

### The private language argument

Wittgenstein's *Investigations* contains the **private language argument**, in sections 243 to 315. Imagine a language whose words refer to what only the speaker can know, his immediate private sensations. In section 258 someone keeps a diary, writing "S" for a recurring sensation. He concentrates his attention on it and "defines" S. Wittgenstein says that no criterion of correctness has been given. Here is a reconstruction (there are several):

| P1 | If a private language is possible, then some word in it has meaning although its correct use is fixed only by the speaker's own private sensations and impressions. |
| P2 | If a word has meaning, then there is a standard of correct use that differs from what seems correct to the speaker. |
| P3 | If a word's correct use is fixed only by the speaker's own private sensations and impressions, then there is no standard of correct use that differs from what seems correct to the speaker. |
| C | So a private language is not possible. |

The argument is valid: if a private language were possible, some word would have meaning (P1), so there would be a standard distinct from what seems right (P2), yet its use is fixed only privately, so there is none (P3). Wittgenstein's argument for P3 is that the diarist's memory cannot supply the standard, since it is exactly what is in question; he compares it to buying a second copy of the morning paper to check that what the first said is true (section 265).

**Replies.** A. J. Ayer, "Can there be a private language?" (1954), argued that a solitary person, such as a Robinson Crusoe, could develop a language, since he could check his usage against his other experiences and his own memory: this denies P3. Others hold that a private language is possible if the standard is provided by non-linguistic regularities, or that memory can be an independent check. A defender replies that what is at issue is a language whose words refer to what *only the speaker can know*. The argument does not say that sensations do not exist, and Wittgenstein is not a behaviorist: he says that a sensation is not a something, but not a nothing either. How much the argument shows about the role of a community is disputed: Kripke's reading says it shows that meaning requires a community, and others deny this.
@example
title: Three acts in one utterance
problem: A doctor says to a patient, "You should stop smoking." Distinguish the locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary acts, and say what would make the illocutionary act misfire.
step: The locutionary act is saying the words with their ordinary sense and reference: that the patient (the person addressed) should stop smoking.
step: The illocutionary act is what the doctor does in saying it: advising, perhaps warning the patient.
step: The perlocutionary act is the effect brought about: persuading the patient to stop, or perhaps alarming or annoying the patient.
step: The act could misfire if the speaker lacked the standing to advise, for example if a stranger overheard and said it to a patient as if he were the doctor; such a failure of the procedure is a misfire, whereas advising while not caring about the patient's health would be an abuse.
answer: Saying with a sense is locutionary, advising is illocutionary, persuading is perlocutionary; lack of standing is a misfire.
@example
title: Where does a reply strike the private language argument?
problem: A critic says that the diarist who writes "S" can check each use by memory, which is as reliable as any check. Which premise of the reconstructed argument does the critic deny, and how might Wittgenstein reply?
step: The critic is saying that something other than the diarist's momentary impression, namely his memory, supplies a standard of correct use. That denies P3, which says that a privately fixed use has no standard other than seeming correct.
step: Wittgenstein's reply, in section 265, is that checking one memory by another memory is like buying a second copy of the same newspaper to see whether the first was right: it does not provide an independent check.
step: The critic can respond that memory is routinely an independent check, as when we correct a recollection by another recollection, and that the newspaper analogy begs the question about the case of sensations.
answer: The critic denies P3. Wittgenstein replies that memory used to check memory is not independent, and the critic questions that analogy.
@mistakes
- Saying that ordinary language philosophers held that ordinary language cannot be improved on: Austin called it the first word, not the last.
- Confusing the illocutionary act (what one does in saying something, such as promising) with the perlocutionary act (the effect on the hearer, such as persuading).
- Saying that the private language argument shows that there are no private sensations: it concerns whether words for them can have meaning in a language that only the speaker can understand.
- Reading Ryle as denying that there are mental states altogether: he says that mental concepts have been misclassified.

prompt: Take the utterance "I bet you ten coins that the bus will be late." Explain why Austin would classify it as a performative and not a constative, and give one way in which it could be infelicitous.
hint: Ask whether saying it is describing something or doing something, and recall misfires and abuses.
solution: By saying "I bet you ten coins that the bus will be late", the speaker does not describe a bet but makes one, provided the circumstances are right. It is not true or false in the way a report is. It is therefore a performative. It can be infelicitous as a misfire if the hearer does not accept the bet, since the conventional procedure of betting requires acceptance, or as an abuse if the speaker has no intention of paying if he loses.
