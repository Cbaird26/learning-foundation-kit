@article
How do you know what "slab" means if you are a builder's assistant and your boss calls out "Slab!"? You bring a slab. The word works because of the way it is embedded in the activity of building. That image, from the first pages of the *Philosophical Investigations*, marks a break with the picture theory of the Tractatus. The *Investigations* was published posthumously in 1953, with an English translation by G. E. M. Anscombe, and its author spent his later work criticizing the assumptions of his early book.

### The target: a picture of language

The *Investigations* opens with a passage from Augustine's *Confessions* describing how a child learns words by being shown objects and hearing them named. Wittgenstein says that this **Augustinian picture** is not false but describes only a narrow part of language, and that it supposes that every word names something and that learning language is ostensive definition. The Tractatus, in which names stand for simple objects, is a refined version of that picture.

### Meaning as use and language-games

In its place he offers **language-games**: simple, complete activities such as the builders' language, in which words are woven into actions. In section 43 he says that for a large class of cases (though not all) the meaning of a word is its use in the language. To ask what a word means is to ask how it is used, what role it plays in the activities. **Family resemblance** (sections 66 and 67): there is nothing common to all the things we call "games", but a network of overlapping similarities, as members of a family resemble one another without one feature shared by all. This undermines the demand, from Socrates through Russell's analysis, for necessary and sufficient conditions. Concepts may be open-textured, and may still be usable. These ideas are not a theory so much as a way of dissolving philosophical puzzles by reminding us how words are used.

### Rule-following

If meaning is use, and use is correct or incorrect, then using a word is following a rule. But what makes a use accord with a rule? In section 185 a pupil has been taught to add 2 and, after 1000, writes 1004, 1008, 1012. We say the pupil has not understood, but the pupil can claim that this is how he understood "add 2". Section 201 states the **rule-following paradox**: no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be made to accord with the rule under some interpretation. If so, there would be neither accord nor conflict. Wittgenstein replies that there is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation, shown in what we call "obeying" and "going against" the rule in actual cases; following a rule is a practice. Interpreters differ about what exactly he affirms.

### Kripke's skeptical argument

In *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language* (1982) Saul Kripke presented a version of the paradox, as he says, as it struck him in reading Wittgenstein, a skeptical argument that has been discussed as a problem in its own right ("Kripkenstein"). Define **quus**: $x \oplus y = x + y$ if both $x$ and $y$ are less than 57, and $x \oplus y = 5$ otherwise. You have never computed with numbers as large as 57. Perhaps by "plus" you always meant quus. Nothing in your past use rules this out, since all the sums you ever computed were less than 57. The argument:

| P1 | If anyone means addition by "+", then some fact about that person (their past use, dispositions or mental states) makes it the case that they mean plus and not quus. |
| P2 | No fact about a person's past use, dispositions or mental states makes it the case that they mean plus and not quus. |
| C | So no one means addition by "+". |

The argument is valid (modus tollens), and the conclusion is so hard to accept that most replies deny P2. Kripke argues for P2 case by case. Past use is finite and compatible with quus. **Dispositions** are finite too, since people err and die before computing large sums, and they say only what one *would* answer, not what one *ought* to; this is the **normativity** objection. A mental image or an instruction would itself need interpretation.

Replies. The **dispositionalist** denies P2: meaning is fixed by idealised dispositions (to compute correctly under good conditions), but critics ask how to say which dispositions are idealised without presupposing the meaning. The **primitivist** also denies P2 by holding that facts about meaning are irreducible: there is a fact that I mean plus, although nothing more basic constitutes it. The **skeptical solution**, which Kripke ascribes to Wittgenstein, accepts that there is no fact of the kind P1 demands and says that attributions of meaning are not fact-stating but are licensed by a community's practice of accepting a person as following a rule if their answers agree with the community's. Many Wittgenstein scholars deny that this is Wittgenstein's own view, and the community reading is disputed.
@example
title: Plus and quus on small numbers
problem: Using $x \oplus y = x + y$ if $x, y < 57$ and $5$ otherwise, compute $68 \oplus 57$, and say why no past use of "+" settles whether the speaker meant plus or quus.
step: Both numbers are at least 57, so the second clause applies and $68 \oplus 57 = 5$; the sum $68 + 57$ is 125.
step: The speaker has only ever added numbers below 57. For any such pair the two functions agree, so the speaker's answers are exactly what both plus and quus predict.
step: So the finite record of past use is compatible with both rules, and nothing in the past tells us that "+" meant plus. Only a case such as $68$ and $57$ would separate them, and that case has never arisen.
answer: $68 \oplus 57 = 5$. The finite past record is compatible with both plus and quus.
@example
title: Sorting replies to the skeptic
problem: For each reply, say which premise of Kripke's argument it denies: (a) meaning is fixed by the speaker's disposition to compute correctly in ideal conditions; (b) there is a basic, irreducible fact that I mean plus; (c) attributions of meaning are not statements of fact but are licensed by community agreement.
step: (a) The dispositionalist says some fact does make it the case that I mean plus: an idealised disposition. This denies P2.
step: (b) The primitivist holds that there is such a fact, though it is not reducible to anything else. This also denies P2.
step: (c) The skeptical solution grants that no fact of the kind in P1 and P2 exists, and then says that the conclusion does not need to be as alarming as it seems, because attributions are justified by the community's practice rather than by facts. It accepts the argument and changes how the conclusion is to be understood.
answer: (a) and (b) deny P2; (c) accepts the argument and reinterprets its conclusion.
@mistakes
- Saying that the later Wittgenstein holds that the meaning of every word is its use: section 43 says "for a large class of cases".
- Attributing Kripke's skeptical solution to Wittgenstein without qualification: it is Kripke's reading of Wittgenstein, and interpreters dispute it.
- Confusing family resemblance with vagueness: the thesis is that there is no feature common to all the cases, not that the boundary is fuzzy.
- Treating the quus argument as an argument about arithmetic: it concerns what fixes the meaning of any word, with "+" as the example.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about a paragraph how the rule-following paradox of section 201 challenges the view that meaning is use, and how Wittgenstein responds.
hint: Start from the thought that using a word is following a rule, and ask what makes a use accord with a rule.
solution: If the meaning of a word is its use, then using a word correctly is following a rule for its use. The paradox of section 201 says that any course of action can be made to accord with a rule, given a suitable interpretation, so that nothing could determine what accords with the rule, and then there would be neither accord nor conflict, and "use" could not be correct or incorrect. Wittgenstein responds that the paradox arises from supposing that following a rule is always a matter of interpretation. There is a way of grasping a rule that is not an interpretation, and it shows itself in what we call obeying and going against the rule in actual cases: following a rule is a practice. Interpreters disagree about whether this is a solution that uses facts about a community, as in Kripke's reading, or something different.
