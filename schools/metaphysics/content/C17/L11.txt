@article
Could Hesperus have failed to be Phosphorus? Could there have been more planets than there are? To the logical positivists such questions were suspect, and in 1953 Quine argued that quantified modal logic, the logic of "necessarily" and "possibly" with quantifiers, was committed to an unintelligible essentialism. Within two decades, metaphysics about possibility and necessity had become respectable again, and much of the credit goes to a mathematical tool and to two philosophers.

### Quine's challenge

Quine's "Reference and Modality" and "Three Grades of Modal Involvement" (both 1953) pressed an objection. "9 is greater than 7" is necessary, and 9 is the number of planets (as it was then thought), but "the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7" seems false: substituting identicals fails in modal contexts, which are **referentially opaque**. He also said that quantifying into modal contexts presupposes **Aristotelian essentialism**, that an object has some properties necessarily and others accidentally independently of how it is described. A mathematician is necessarily rational and not necessarily two-legged; a cyclist is the reverse; if the same man is both, which is it? Quine found such talk unacceptable.

### Possible worlds semantics

C. I. Lewis had created modal systems in *A Survey of Symbolic Logic* (1918) and *Symbolic Logic* (1932, with C. H. Langford), with the five systems S1 to S5, and Ruth Barcan (later Barcan Marcus) added quantifiers in 1946. In 1959 and 1963 Saul Kripke gave them a semantics. A **Kripke model** has a set $W$ of worlds, an **accessibility relation** $R$ on $W$, and an assignment of truth-values at each world. $\Box A$ is true at $w$ if $A$ is true at every world accessible from $w$; $\Diamond A$ if $A$ is true at some accessible world. A world with no accessible worlds makes every $\Box A$ true and every $\Diamond A$ false. Axioms correspond to conditions on $R$: $\Box A \to A$ (T) to reflexivity; $\Box A \to \Box\Box A$ (4) to transitivity; $A \to \Box\Diamond A$ (B) to symmetry; $\Diamond A \to \Box\Diamond A$ (5) to the Euclidean condition (if $wRv$ and $wRu$ then $vRu$); $\Box A \to \Diamond A$ (D) to seriality. S5 corresponds to an equivalence relation. The axiom $\Box(A \to B) \to (\Box A \to \Box B)$ (K) is valid on every frame.

### Kripke: rigid designators and the necessary a posteriori

In *Naming and Necessity* (lectures of 1970, published in 1972 and as a book in 1980), Kripke defines a **rigid designator** as an expression that designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. Proper names are rigid; descriptions such as "the teacher of Alexander" typically are not. His **modal argument** against the view that a name means a description:

| P1 | If "Aristotle" meant the same as "the teacher of Alexander", then "If Aristotle existed, he taught Alexander" would be necessary. |
| P2 | It is not necessary that, if Aristotle existed, he taught Alexander: he might have done something else. |
| C | So "Aristotle" does not mean the same as "the teacher of Alexander". |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). The usual replies attack P1: if the description is read as taking wide scope over the modal operator, or is **rigidified** with "actual" ("the actual teacher of Alexander"), the sentence is not predicted to be necessary. Kripke also pressed epistemic and semantic arguments against descriptivism, and offered a causal-historical picture of reference.

His argument for the **necessity of identity** (Ruth Barcan Marcus had given an earlier version in modal logic):

| P1 | If Hesperus is Phosphorus, then if Hesperus has the property of being necessarily identical with Hesperus, Phosphorus has it too. |
| P2 | Hesperus has the property of being necessarily identical with Hesperus. |
| P3 | Hesperus is Phosphorus. |
| C | So Phosphorus is necessarily identical with Hesperus: necessarily, Hesperus is Phosphorus. |

The argument is valid. P1 is an instance of Leibniz's Law, and Quine-style cases attack it for modal contexts. Kripke's reply is that the substitution in Quine's example involves a non-rigid description; with rigid names, Leibniz's Law holds. So "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is a **necessary a posteriori** truth: it could not have been otherwise, yet it was discovered by astronomy. Kripke also argued that "the standard meter bar is one meter long at $t_0$" is **contingent a priori** for one who fixes the reference of "meter" by it. These cases separated necessity from apriority.

### Lewis: modal realism

David Lewis, in *Counterfactuals* (1973) and *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), held that possible worlds are as real as ours: concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes, and "actual" is an indexical word like "here". Since no individual exists in two worlds, an individual's modal properties are given by its **counterparts**, similar individuals in other worlds ("Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic", 1968). The theoretical gains are analyses of modality, counterfactuals, properties and propositions. Objections: Lewis acknowledged the **incredulous stare** this view meets; there is an **epistemological** worry about how we know about causally isolated worlds; and Kripke's **Humphrey objection** says that what happens to a counterpart is not what matters to the man himself, so counterpart theory seems not to capture what he could have done. **Ersatz** theorists (Plantinga, Adams, Stalnaker) take worlds to be abstract objects such as maximal consistent sets of propositions. The share of philosophers who accept any view is no evidence of its truth.
@example
title: Evaluating box and diamond in a model
problem: Let $W = \{w_1, w_2, w_3\}$, with accessibility $w_1 R w_2$, $w_1 R w_3$, $w_2 R w_3$ and no others, and let $P$ be true at $w_2$ and $w_3$ only. Where are $\Box P$ and $\Diamond \neg P$ true?
step: At $w_1$, the accessible worlds are $w_2$ and $w_3$. $P$ is true at both, so $\Box P$ is true at $w_1$.
step: At $w_2$, the only accessible world is $w_3$, where $P$ is true, so $\Box P$ is true at $w_2$. At $w_3$ no world is accessible, so $\Box P$ is true there vacuously.
step: $\Diamond\neg P$ needs an accessible world where $P$ is false. $P$ is false only at $w_1$, which no world accesses. So $\Diamond \neg P$ is false at all three worlds.
answer: $\Box P$ is true at $w_1$, $w_2$ and $w_3$; $\Diamond \neg P$ is true at none.
@example
title: Quine's planet argument and the rigid reply
problem: Explain why "the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7" does not threaten the necessity of identity.
step: The argument runs: 9 is necessarily greater than 7; 9 is the number of planets; so the number of planets is necessarily greater than 7. The conclusion seems false, since there might have been fewer planets.
step: The identity "9 is the number of planets" has a rigid term "9" on one side and a non-rigid description on the other. The description picks out different numbers in different worlds.
step: Leibniz's Law, in Kripke's use, concerns the same object under rigid designators. Substituting a non-rigid description changes what is said about other worlds, so the reasoning is not an instance of valid substitution of identicals.
answer: The failure shows that descriptions are not rigid, not that identity between rigid designators is contingent.
@mistakes
- Saying that Kripke showed that all necessary truths are a posteriori or that all a priori truths are necessary: he argued that the two distinctions come apart.
- Confusing a rigid designator with one that designates a necessary existent: it designates the same object in every world in which the object exists.
- Attributing the claim "worlds are abstract" to Lewis: he held that they are concrete, like our universe.
- Reading $\Box A$ as true at a world with no accessible worlds as false: it is vacuously true.

@quiz self
prompt: Kripke separated necessity (a metaphysical notion) from apriority (an epistemic one). Explain the two distinctions and give, with brief reasons, one example of a necessary a posteriori truth and one example of a contingent a priori truth from the literature.
hint: Ask what the truth could have been like in other worlds, and separately how one could come to know it.
solution: A truth is necessary if it could not have been false, and contingent if it could have; it is a priori if it can be known independently of experience and a posteriori if experience is needed. Kripke argued that these come apart. On Kripke's view (and Putnam's), "Water is H2O" is necessary, since "water" rigidly designates the stuff of that chemical kind and that stuff could not have had a different chemical constitution, yet it was discovered empirically, so it is necessary a posteriori. For the contingent a priori, suppose someone fixes the reference of "one meter" by the length of a particular bar at a time $t_0$. She can know without measuring that the bar is one meter long at $t_0$, since that is how the term was introduced, yet the bar could have had a different length, so the sentence is contingent. Many philosophers dispute some of these cases, so they should be presented as Kripke's claims.
