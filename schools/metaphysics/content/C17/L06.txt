Do numbers exist? One answer: of course, since there is a prime number between 10 and 20. Another: that is a fact about our mathematics, but whether numbers really exist, apart from our theories, is a different question, and it is hard to say what would settle it. Rudolf Carnap thought the second question has no answer of the kind philosophers wanted, and the reason is the distinction on which his later philosophy of language and ontology turns.

### From syntax to frameworks

In *The Logical Syntax of Language* (1934) Carnap proposed the **principle of tolerance**: there is no uniquely correct logic or language, and one is free to construct whatever language, with whatever rules, is useful for one's purposes, provided the rules are stated exactly. Debates about which logic is "right" are replaced by questions about which system works best. In "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950) he applied this to ontology.

### Frameworks

A **linguistic framework** is a language (or part of a language) introduced by rules for forming sentences and for deciding which are true. Carnap's examples include the framework of **things** (the everyday spatiotemporal thing language), of **numbers**, of **propositions**, of **properties** and of **space-time points**. To accept a framework with variables for a kind of entity is to adopt rules for talking about entities of that kind.

### Internal and external questions

Carnap then distinguished two kinds of questions about existence:

- **Internal questions** are asked within a framework and are answered by its rules together with, where appropriate, empirical evidence. "Is there a white piece of paper on my desk?" is answered by looking. "Is there a prime number between 10 and 20?" is answered by calculation. "Are there numbers?" asked inside the number framework is answered trivially in the affirmative, since "5 is a number" follows from the rules.
- **External questions** concern the framework as a whole: "Are there really numbers, or things?" taken as a question about whether the entities of the system exist in some sense beyond the framework. Carnap held that such a question, if it is meant as a theoretical question with a true-or-false answer, lacks cognitive content. If it is meant as a practical question, whether to adopt the framework, it can be answered by considering the framework's **efficiency, fruitfulness and simplicity**, which are matters of degree and not of truth.

Accepting the number framework does not commit one to a metaphysical doctrine of Platonism, since the acceptance is a practical decision, not a belief in an abstract realm. Carnap called the philosophical question a pseudo-question when taken to be a factual one.

### The argument

| P1 | Every question of the form "Are there $F$s?" is either internal to some framework or external to every framework. |
| P2 | No external question is a theoretical question with a true-or-false answer: it is either a practical question about which framework to adopt or, if meant as theoretical, lacks cognitive content. |
| P3 | An internal existence question about numbers is answered trivially by the rules of the number framework. |
| C | So the question "Do numbers exist?" is either trivially answered yes or is not a theoretical question. |

The argument is valid: by P1 the question is internal or external; if internal, P3 settles it trivially, and if external, P2 removes its theoretical status.

### Objections

Quine's reply, in "On Carnap's Views on Ontology" (1951) and in "Two Dogmas" (next week), attacks P1: the sharp line between questions answered by the rules of a framework and questions answered by evidence rests on the distinction between analytic and synthetic, which Quine rejects. If there is no such distinction, the choice of a framework is continuous with the choice of a scientific theory, and so what Carnap called external questions are not a separate kind. Quine agrees that choice of a conceptual scheme is pragmatic, but he does not conclude that ontological questions are therefore pseudo-questions.

A second objection attacks P2: metaphysicians such as those of the 1970s and later say that an external question may be a perfectly good question, about what there is, answerable by considering which theory best explains the facts. Defenders of Carnap's side (the **neo-Carnapians**, such as Eli Hirsch, Amie Thomasson and David Chalmers, in different ways) say that disputes about whether, say, composite objects exist are verbal or are settled by the use of the words. The debate continues in week 12.

title: Platonism and acceptance
problem: Explain why on Carnap's view someone who says "There are prime numbers between 10 and 20" is not thereby a Platonist.
step: The sentence is an internal statement in the number framework, true by the rules and calculation: 11, 13, 17 and 19 are primes.
step: Platonism in the philosophical sense says that numbers exist in a realm independent of thought and language. That is an external claim about the framework as a whole.
step: Carnap holds that the external claim, taken as theoretical, lacks cognitive content, and that accepting the number language is a practical decision. So uttering the internal statement commits the speaker to no metaphysical doctrine.
answer: The internal statement is true by the framework's rules; the external Platonist claim is not a cognitively meaningful addition.
@mistakes
- Saying that Carnap denies that numbers exist: he says the internal question has a trivial yes, and the external question as a theoretical question lacks content.
- Saying that Carnap holds that external questions can be settled by empirical observation: external questions, if practical, are settled by efficiency, fruitfulness and simplicity.
- Mixing up internal and external with "easy" and "hard": an internal question can be difficult (the answer to a deep mathematical problem), and what makes it internal is the framework's rules.
- Attributing the objection from the analytic-synthetic distinction to Carnap's defenders: it is Quine's.
