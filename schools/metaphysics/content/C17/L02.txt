@article
In the 1890s the dominant philosophy in British universities was idealism, influenced by Hegel: F. H. Bradley's *Appearance and Reality* (1893) argued that the world as we ordinarily describe it is appearance and that reality is a single unified whole. Around 1898 two young philosophers at Cambridge, G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell, turned against it. Their revolt was both a rejection of idealist conclusions and a change of method: take statements one at a time, ask exactly what they mean, and demand arguments. Frege's new logic gave Russell a tool for doing this, and it also gave him a problem.

### Russell's paradox

Frege's system rested on Basic Law V, which in effect lets every concept determine a collection, its extension. The informal version is **unrestricted comprehension**: for any condition there is a set of exactly the things that meet it. Russell found in 1901 that this is inconsistent, and in June 1902 he wrote to Frege, who had just finished the second volume of the *Grundgesetze*. Take the condition "is not a member of itself". The argument is:

| P1 | For any condition $\varphi(x)$ there is a set whose members are exactly the things that satisfy $\varphi$. |
| P2 | "$x \notin x$" is a condition. |
| C1 | So there is a set $R$ such that, for every $x$, $x \in R \leftrightarrow x \notin x$. |
| C2 | So $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$ (apply C1 to $R$ itself). |
| C3 | So a contradiction follows: no statement can be equivalent to its own negation. |

The step from C2 to C3 needs only elementary reasoning, and it does not even need excluded middle: from $A \leftrightarrow \neg A$ one can derive a contradiction intuitionistically too. The culprit is therefore P1, not the logic. Frege acknowledged in an appendix to the second volume that the foundations of his system had been shaken, and the repair he proposed did not work.

**Why it mattered.** First, it showed that the naive idea of a collection for every condition is inconsistent, so mathematics needed a new foundation. Two families of response followed. Russell's **theory of types** (developed in the 1900s and used in *Principia Mathematica*, by Whitehead and Russell, 1910 to 1913) stratifies entities into levels so that "$x \in x$" is ill-formed. **Zermelo's axiomatization** (1908), the ancestor of ZFC, replaces comprehension by **Separation**: given a set $A$ and a condition, there is a set of the members of $A$ that satisfy it. Second, it undermined Frege's logicism in its original form. A later **neo-Fregean** programme, led by Crispin Wright and Bob Hale, shows that second-order arithmetic can be derived from Hume's Principle (numbers of two concepts are the same exactly when the concepts are equinumerous), which is consistent; whether this counts as logicism is debated. Third, a minority of logicians keep naive comprehension and weaken the logic instead (paraconsistent set theory).

### Moore and common sense

In "The Refutation of Idealism" (*Mind*, 1903) Moore attacked the idealist slogan *esse is percipi*, "to be is to be perceived". He argued that it confuses the act of being aware of something (the sensation of blue) with the object it is an awareness of (the blue), which are distinct. Later he defended common sense: in "A Defense of Common Sense" (1925) he lists propositions he claims to know with certainty, and in "Proof of an External World" (1939) he gives a famous argument, held up while he shows his hands:

| P1 | I know that here is one hand and here is another. |
| P2 | I know that "here is one hand and here is another" entails that at least two things external to my mind exist. |
| P3 | If I know $p$ and I know that $p$ entails $q$, then I know $q$. |
| C | So I know that there are things external to my mind. |

The argument is valid. A skeptic who points to dreams or an evil demon attacks P1: Moore cannot know he has hands unless he can rule out that he is dreaming. Dretske and Nozick-style replies deny P3, the closure principle, and so accept that Moore may know about hands without knowing the consequence. Wright and others say that the proof is valid but cannot *transmit* warrant, because Moore's grounds for P1 already presuppose the conclusion. Moore's own reply to the first objection is that he is more certain of P1 than he could be of any premise of a skeptical argument that contradicts it.

### Russell on descriptions

In "On Denoting" (*Mind*, 1905) Russell analyzed phrases like "the present King of France". On his theory, "The $K$ is bald" says that there is exactly one $K$ and it is bald:

$$\exists x\,(Kx \wedge \forall y\,(Ky \to y = x) \wedge Bx).$$

Since there is no present King of France, the sentence is false. It is neither meaningless nor about a non-existent object. The negation has two readings: "there is a unique king and he is not bald" is false, while "it is not the case that there is a unique king and he is bald" is true. The apparent failure of excluded middle comes from conflating the two. In 1950 Peter Strawson replied that such sentences presuppose existence and, if the presupposition fails, are neither true nor false; the dispute is a topic for week 10.
@example
title: Why no set contains everything
problem: Use Separation to show that no set has every set among its members.
step: Suppose, for reductio, that $V$ is a set and every set is a member of $V$. By Separation, since $V$ is a set and "$x \notin x$" is a condition, there is a set $R = \{x \in V : x \notin x\}$.
step: Since $R$ is a set, $R \in V$. By the definition of $R$, for any $x$ in $V$, $x \in R$ exactly when $x \notin x$; applying this to $x = R$ gives $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$.
step: That is a contradiction, so the supposition fails. Russell's argument, once comprehension is restricted to members of a given set, no longer produces a paradoxical set; it proves instead that there is no universal set.
answer: There is no set of all sets. Separation turns the paradox into a theorem.
@example
title: Scope in Russell's analysis
problem: Take the description "the largest prime number" (there is none, since there are infinitely many primes). Analyze "The largest prime number is odd" and distinguish the two readings of "The largest prime number is not odd".
step: Write $Tx$ for "$x$ is a largest prime" in the sense "no prime is greater than $x$", and $Ox$ for "$x$ is odd". "The largest prime is odd" is $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge Ox)$. Since nothing satisfies $T$, it is false.
step: Reading one (narrow scope negation): $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge \neg Ox)$. It also needs a largest prime, so it is false.
step: Reading two (wide scope negation): $\neg\,\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge Ox)$. It denies the whole claim in the first step, so it is true.
answer: The unnegated sentence and the narrow-scope negation are false; the wide-scope negation is true. Excluded middle is intact, since the unnegated sentence and its wide-scope negation are contradictories.
@mistakes
- Saying that Russell's paradox shows that classical logic is inconsistent: it shows that unrestricted comprehension is inconsistent with classical logic.
- Saying that the paradox makes "the set of all sets that are not members of themselves" an ordinary set that we cannot describe: if comprehension is restricted, there is no such set.
- Treating Moore's proof as a refutation that the skeptic must accept: whether it answers the skeptic, or only shows that skeptical arguments are less credible than common sense, is exactly what is disputed.
- Saying that on Russell's theory "The King of France is bald" is meaningless: it is meaningful and false.
