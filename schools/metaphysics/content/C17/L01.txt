@article
Two sentences: "Samuel Clemens is Samuel Clemens" and "Samuel Clemens is Mark Twain". Both are true, and both say, on the face of it, that one man is identical with himself. Yet the first is trivial and the second can be news. That small difference is the start of the philosophy of language in the analytic tradition, and it was noticed by Gottlob Frege, a German mathematician and logician who did more than anyone to create the tools on which that tradition runs.

### The new logic

Before Frege, the standard logic was Aristotle's syllogistic, refined over two millennia. It handles sentences of the forms "All S are P" and "Some S are P" well, but it cannot represent relations or sentences with more than one quantifier. In his *Begriffsschrift* (1879) Frege gave the first full formal system of what we now call quantificational logic (its completeness was proved only later, by Gödel). He analyzed sentences on the model of a mathematical function applied to an argument: "Ana admires Ben" is a **concept** ("admires Ben") applied to an **object**, or a two-place relation applied to two objects. With quantifiers binding variables, he could write what traditional logic could not. Compare, over a domain of people with $Axy$ for "$x$ admires $y$":

| Formula | Reading |
|---|---|
| $\forall x\,\exists y\,Axy$ | Everyone admires someone (possibly different people). |
| $\exists y\,\forall x\,Axy$ | There is someone whom everyone admires. |

The two formulas differ only in the order of the quantifiers, and the second entails the first but not the converse. This **multiple generality** underlies the definitions of continuity and limit in analysis, and it is how Frege could give a gapless formal notion of proof. The logic of the *Begriffsschrift* is a second-order system; the first-order fragment is what logic courses teach as standard.

### Logicism

In *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (*Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik*, 1884) Frege defended **logicism**: arithmetic is part of logic, so its truths are analytic in Kant's sense and not synthetic a priori. Three methodological principles frame the book: separate the psychological from the logical; ask for the meaning of a word only in the context of a sentence (the **context principle**); and keep the distinction between concept and object. A key idea is that a statement of number says something about a concept: "Jupiter has four moons" says that the concept *moon of Jupiter* has the number four. Frege then defined numbers through equinumerosity of concepts and, in *Basic Laws of Arithmetic* (*Grundgesetze der Arithmetik*, volume I 1893, volume II 1903), tried to derive arithmetic in a formal system. That system needed **Basic Law V**: the extension of the concept $F$ is the same as the extension of the concept $G$ if and only if everything falls under $F$ exactly when it falls under $G$. Next week we will see what went wrong.

### Sense and reference

In "On Sense and Reference" ("Über Sinn und Bedeutung", 1892) Frege asks how an identity $a = b$ can differ in **cognitive value** from $a = a$. Here is his argument, put in standard form with the hidden connection made explicit:

| P1 | If the meaning of a name were just its referent, then any true identity sentence "$a = b$" would have the same cognitive value as "$a = a$". |
| P2 | Some true identity sentences, such as "Hesperus is Phosphorus", differ in cognitive value from "Hesperus is Hesperus": one can be informative and the other cannot. |
| C | So the meaning of a name is not just its referent. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). Frege's positive proposal is that a name has a **sense** (a mode of presentation of its referent) as well as a **reference** (the object). "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" present Venus differently, so the two identity sentences express different thoughts. Sense determines reference but not conversely. A declarative sentence has a **thought** as its sense and a **truth-value** as its reference. In indirect contexts such as "Mira believes that…", Frege held, words refer to their customary senses, which is why co-referring names cannot always be swapped there.

### The best objection

The target of the objection is premise P1. A **Millian**, following John Stuart Mill's view that a name just denotes its bearer (a view later defended in different forms by Saul Kripke and Nathan Salmon, among others), accepts the data in P2 but denies P1: the difference in informativeness arises from what speakers know about the names and how they think of the object, not from a difference in meaning. The Fregean replies that cognitive value is exactly what a theory of meaning must explain, and that the Millian owes a story about why substituting names changes what a belief report says. The debate is still open.

### Why it matters

Frege's logic gave philosophers a precise instrument for analyzing the form of sentences; his logicism set the problem that Russell, the Vienna Circle and Wittgenstein tried to solve; and sense and reference started a research programme on meaning that runs to Kripke and beyond.
@example
title: Two quantifiers, two models
problem: Let the domain be Ana, Ben and Cy, and let $Rxy$ mean "$x$ respects $y$", with these facts: Ana respects Ben, Ben respects Cy, and Cy respects Ben, and no other respect holds. Evaluate $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$ and $\exists y\,\forall x\,Rxy$.
step: For $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$ check each person in turn for someone they respect: Ana respects Ben, Ben respects Cy, Cy respects Ben. Everyone passes.
step: For $\exists y\,\forall x\,Rxy$ we need one person whom every one of the three respects. Try Ben: Ana respects Ben and Cy respects Ben, but Ben does not respect himself. Try Cy: Ana does not respect Cy. Try Ana: nobody respects Ana. No candidate works.
step: So the first formula is true and the second false in this model. The order of quantifiers matters, and the model shows that $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$ does not entail $\exists y\,\forall x\,Rxy$.
answer: $\forall x\,\exists y\,Rxy$ is true; $\exists y\,\forall x\,Rxy$ is false.
@example
title: Sense, reference and a belief report
problem: Suppose "Dusk" and "Dawn" are the titles of two novels by the same author, and Mira has read both but does not know they share an author. Use Frege's distinctions to explain why "The author of Dusk is the author of Dawn" is informative to Mira and why replacing one of these descriptions by the other in a belief report can change its truth.
step: The descriptions "the author of Dusk" and "the author of Dawn" have different senses (different modes of presentation) but the same reference, the one author. Frege treated definite descriptions, like proper names, as expressions with a sense and a reference.
step: The identity sentence is true, so its reference is the truth-value True, but its sense, the thought it expresses, differs from the thought of "The author of Dusk is the author of Dusk". Mira can grasp the first and doubt it, so cognitive value tracks sense.
step: In "Mira believes that the author of Dusk wrote Dusk", the words after "that" refer to their customary senses. Replacing "the author of Dusk" by "the author of Dawn" changes the sense and so the thought she is said to believe, and "Mira believes that the author of Dawn wrote Dusk" may be false even though the two descriptions have the same reference.
answer: The two descriptions differ in sense and share a reference. Substitution preserves truth only where reference is what matters, and in belief reports it is sense.
@mistakes
- Saying that Frege thinks "Hesperus" and "Phosphorus" refer to different things: they have one referent and two senses.
- Confusing the sense of a sentence (the thought) with its reference (a truth-value), or saying that the reference of a name is its sense.
- Reading $\forall x\,\exists y\,Axy$ and $\exists y\,\forall x\,Axy$ as equivalent: the second entails the first, not the converse.
- Saying that Frege's logicism is the view that arithmetic is about psychology: the Grundlagen insists on separating the logical from the psychological.

@quiz self
prompt: Frege's logic can distinguish "everyone admires someone" from "someone is admired by everyone". Explain, using a small model with a domain of three things, why traditional syllogistic logic has trouble with this distinction and how a model shows the two differ.
hint: Write both sentences with quantifiers, then build a model in which one is true and the other false.
solution: Traditional syllogistic treats sentences of the forms "All S are P" and "Some S are P" with one quantifier and cannot represent a relation between two quantified phrases, so it cannot mark the difference in order. In quantificational logic the two sentences are $\forall x\,\exists y\,Axy$ and $\exists y\,\forall x\,Axy$. Take a domain of three people $a$, $b$, $c$ in which $a$ admires $b$, $b$ admires $c$ and $c$ admires $a$ and nothing else holds. Everyone admires someone, so the first formula is true; but no one person is admired by all three, so the second is false. Hence the two sentences differ in truth-conditions, and the second entails the first but not conversely.
