Suppose I say "The cat is on the mat". The sentence is a string of marks or sounds, and the cat's being on the mat is a state of the world. How can one stand for the other? And how can I say "The cat is on the mat" when there is no cat, and say something false instead of nothing at all? Two philosophers tried to answer with a single logic-driven picture: Bertrand Russell in his 1918 lectures "The Philosophy of Logical Atomism", and Ludwig Wittgenstein in the *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*, published in German in 1921 and in English, with an introduction by Russell, in 1922.

### Logical atomism

**Logical atomism** is the view that the world consists of a plurality of independent, simple facts, and that complex sentences are built from sentences about those simple facts by logical operations. Language analyzed completely would reveal the structure of the world. For Russell, the atoms were facts about particulars known by acquaintance; the theory of descriptions was the model of analysis, since it shows that a phrase like "the largest prime" disappears on analysis into a logical structure of simpler claims. Wittgenstein's version is more abstract.

### The Tractatus: world, picture, proposition

The book is numbered in a decimal system. Its main theses are these.

- **The world is the totality of facts, not of things** (1.1). A fact is the obtaining of a **state of affairs** (*Sachverhalt*), a combination of **objects** (2.01).
- Objects are **simple** and form the unalterable **substance** of the world (2.021).
- A **proposition** is a **picture** of reality: its elements stand for objects, and the way the elements are arranged in the sentence mirrors a possible arrangement of objects. Picture and pictured share a **logical form**. The proposition shows its **sense**, a possible state of affairs, and is true if things are as it shows.
- An **elementary proposition** pictures a state of affairs and consists of names in immediate combination. Elementary propositions are logically independent of one another.
- Every proposition is a **truth-function** of elementary propositions (5), and all the truth-functions can be generated by repeated application of one operation, joint denial of a set of propositions (5.5).
- **Logical constants do not represent** (4.0312). Logic does not describe a realm of logical objects.
- **Tautologies and contradictions** are not nonsense but are **senseless** (*sinnlos*): a tautology is true under every possible assignment to the elementary propositions and so excludes no possibility, and a contradiction is false under every assignment (4.461 and following). They say nothing about the world. Logic is therefore not a body of truths about reality, and the propositions of logic show the logical properties of language and world.

### The argument for simple objects

At 2.0211 and 2.0212 Wittgenstein argues that there must be simple objects. In standard form:

| P1 | If the world had no simple objects, then whether a proposition has sense would depend on whether some other proposition is true. |
| P2 | Whether a proposition has sense never depends on whether some other proposition is true. |
| C | So the world has simple objects. |

The reasoning behind P1 is that a sentence about a complex (say a particular watch) says something only if the complex exists, since its parts must be arranged in the way the word for the complex assumes; if the parts were themselves complexes, the same would hold at the next level, and sense would depend on contingent facts. P2 expresses the Tractatus's demand for **determinate sense**: it must be settled in advance, by the structure of language alone, what is the case if the proposition is true. The argument is valid (modus tollens). Objections attack the premises. Peter Strawson in 1950 argued that sentences with empty descriptions presuppose the existence of their referents and, when the presupposition fails, are neither true nor false. Strawson himself held that such sentences are meaningful, so his view denies P1 (sense does not depend on the existence of the complex); a critic who instead said that the sentence lacks sense when the presupposition fails would be denying P2. Wittgenstein himself later rejected the demand for absolutely simple elements, arguing that "simple" has no absolute sense but is relative to a system of description or language-game (*Philosophical Investigations* sections 46 to 64), that a name's meaning is not its bearer, so that a sentence using the name keeps its sense if the bearer is destroyed (sections 39 to 44), and that sense need not be sharply fixed in advance (sections 88 to 99); he came to deny that sense must be fixed in advance in the way the Tractatus assumed, which is a rejection of the picture behind P1 and P2.

### Saying and showing

The picture theory implies that a proposition cannot say what it has in common with the reality it pictures: its logical form. Logical form can only be **shown**, in the way the proposition is built. So the book's own claims about language and world, which concern logical form, cannot be said. At 6.54 Wittgenstein calls his propositions **nonsensical** (*unsinnig*) and says that whoever understands him will use them as steps to climb up and then throw away the ladder. At 7 he concludes that what cannot be spoken about must be passed over in silence. Interpreters disagree about what this means. On the **traditional** reading there are ineffable truths that are shown. On the **resolute** reading, associated with Cora Diamond and James Conant, there are no ineffable truths and the book aims to dissolve the temptation to think there are. The Vienna Circle did not share the book's interest in the mystical, but took from it the ideas that logic is empty and that a sentence has sense only if it pictures a possible fact.
@example
title: Building connectives from joint denial
problem: Let $P \downarrow Q$ mean "neither $P$ nor $Q$" (joint denial). Show how to express $\neg P$ and $P \wedge Q$ with $\downarrow$ alone, and check the second with a truth table.
step: Joint denial of $P$ with itself is "neither $P$ nor $P$", which is true exactly when $P$ is false. So $\neg P$ is $P \downarrow P$.
step: Conjunction: $P \wedge Q$ is true exactly when both are true, that is, when neither $\neg P$ nor $\neg Q$ is true. So $P \wedge Q$ is $(P \downarrow P) \downarrow (Q \downarrow Q)$.
step: Check on the rows TT, TF, FT, FF for $P$ and $Q$. Here $P \downarrow P$ is F, F, T, T and $Q \downarrow Q$ is F, T, F, T. Joint denial of these two columns is true only where both are false: T, F, F, F. That is the column of $P \wedge Q$.
answer: $\neg P = P \downarrow P$ and $P \wedge Q = (P \downarrow P) \downarrow (Q \downarrow Q)$.
@example
title: Counting possibilities
problem: With 2 elementary propositions, how many truth-possibilities are there, and how many different truth-functions of them (counting a tautology and a contradiction)?
step: Each elementary proposition is true or false independently, so the number of truth-possibilities is $2^2 = 4$: TT, TF, FT, FF.
step: A truth-function is fixed by saying, for each of the 4 possibilities, whether the proposition agrees with it (true there) or not. That gives $2^4 = 16$ truth-functions.
step: Among them is the one true under all 4 possibilities (the tautology) and the one true under none (the contradiction); the others say that the world is in one of a proper subset of the possibilities.
answer: 4 truth-possibilities and 16 truth-functions.
@mistakes
- Saying that the Tractatus calls tautologies nonsense: it calls them senseless (*sinnlos*) but not nonsensical (*unsinnig*); they are part of the symbolism and say nothing.
- Attributing "meaning is use" to the Tractatus: that is the later Wittgenstein; the Tractatus has the picture theory.
- Treating the Tractatus as a book that proves its claims: its own propositions are declared nonsensical by the book, and how to read that is disputed.
- Saying that the world, for the Tractatus, is the totality of things: it is the totality of facts.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the Tractatus's distinction between saying and showing, with an example, and say why the distinction is a problem for the book's own propositions.
hint: Start from the claim that a proposition and the fact it pictures share a logical form.
solution: A proposition pictures a possible state of affairs, and the picture and the fact pictured share a logical form. A proposition cannot step outside language to say what that form is, since it already uses the form to say anything at all; the form is shown in the way the proposition is built. For example, "the cat is on the mat" says that the cat is on the mat, and its structure shows that a relation holds between two objects, but the sentence does not say that. The problem is that the book's own theses, such as that propositions are pictures with a logical form, are about logical form and so by the book's own standards cannot be said. Wittgenstein accepts this at 6.54 by calling them nonsensical. Interpreters disagree whether they point to ineffable truths or aim to dissolve the wish to state them.
