Is "The Absolute enters into evolution but is not itself subject to it" true? False? Or is there nothing there to be true or false? Some philosophers in Vienna in the 1920s and 1930s thought the third answer is correct for much of what had been called metaphysics, and they thought a precise criterion could show it.

### The Circle

The **Vienna Circle** was a group of scientists and philosophers who met in Vienna from the mid-1920s around Moritz Schlick, professor of the philosophy of the inductive sciences. Its members included Otto Neurath, Hans Hahn, Rudolf Carnap, Herbert Feigl and Friedrich Waismann; related groups worked in Berlin (Hans Reichenbach) and elsewhere. Its 1929 manifesto, *The Scientific Conception of the World*, written by Neurath, Hahn and Carnap, announced a program: **empiricism** (all knowledge comes from experience), **logical analysis** of language with the new logic of Frege and Russell, and the **unity of science**. Their approach is called **logical positivism** or logical empiricism. A. J. Ayer brought the doctrine to English readers in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936).

### Sources

The Circle drew on three sources. **Hume's fork** divides all genuine knowledge into relations of ideas (a priori, concerning meanings) and matters of fact (a posteriori); "commit it then to the flames", Hume said of books containing neither, in the last paragraph of the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding*. **Mach** supplied a program of reducing science to the description of sensations. And the **Tractatus** suggested that logic and mathematics say nothing (they are tautologies) and that a significant sentence pictures a possible fact. A slogan associated with Schlick and Waismann, who credited Wittgenstein's conversations with them, held that the meaning of a sentence is its method of verification.

### The principle

The **verification principle** in the form Ayer gave it says that a declarative sentence is **factually** (or **cognitively**) **meaningful** only if it is either **analytic** (true or false in virtue of the meanings of its words, including contradictions) or **empirically verifiable**, that is, some possible observation would bear on its truth. Two clarifications matter.

- **Strong versus weak.** A sentence is strongly verifiable if experience could conclusively establish it. It is weakly verifiable if some possible experience could make it probable. Ayer adopted the weak form, since "All metals expand when heated" can never be conclusively verified. And verifiability is **in principle**: "There are mountains on the far side of the Moon" was meaningful before anyone could go and look, since we know what observation would settle it.
- **Meaning is not the same as significance.** A sentence that is neither analytic nor verifiable lacks *factual* meaning, but may still express feeling. Ayer held that sentences such as "Cruelty is wicked" are neither true nor false but express the speaker's attitude (**emotivism**).

### The argument against metaphysics

The Circle used the principle in an argument against traditional metaphysics. Carnap's paper "The Elimination of Metaphysics through Logical Analysis of Language" (1932) is the classic statement. In standard form:

| P1 | A sentence is cognitively meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. |
| P2 | The sentence "The Absolute enters into evolution but is not itself subject to it" is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. |
| C | So that sentence is not cognitively meaningful. |

The argument is valid. If P1 is granted, everything depends on P2, and so on whether one can show of each metaphysical sentence that no observation could bear on it. A critic of the argument can attack P2 (perhaps the sentence has consequences for experience) or P1 (the principle itself). Next week turns to P1.

### A method for applying the principle

1. Is the sentence analytic or contradictory, true or false by meaning alone? If so, it is meaningful and says nothing about the world.
2. If not, does it, perhaps together with auxiliary premises, imply some observation sentence, or is it itself one? If so, it is empirically verifiable and meaningful.
3. If neither, it is not factually meaningful, but ask whether it expresses an attitude (emotive meaning).
4. Distinguish *practically* unverifiable (nobody can currently check) from unverifiable in principle.
@example
title: The principle applied to five sentences
problem: Apply the verification principle to these sentences: (a) "Every square has four sides." (b) "There is a lake on the north side of that hill." (c) "Betrayal is vile." (d) "The Absolute enters into evolution but is not itself subject to it." (e) "Copper conducts electricity."
step: (a) Analytic: true by the meaning of "square". By clause one it is meaningful, and it says nothing about the world.
step: (b) Not analytic, but an observer could walk to the north side of the hill and look, so it is verifiable in principle and meaningful. It does not matter that nobody has looked.
step: (c) Not analytic, and no observation would bear on whether betrayal is vile. For Ayer it lacks factual meaning and expresses an attitude of disapproval, so it has emotive meaning.
step: (d) Neither analytic nor tied to any possible observation. It is not factually meaningful.
step: (e) Not analytic. It cannot be conclusively verified, since it covers every piece of copper, but experiments can make it probable. On the weak principle it is meaningful; on the strong principle it would fail.
answer: (a) analytic; (b) verifiable, meaningful; (c) emotive, not factual; (d) not factually meaningful; (e) meaningful on the weak principle only.
@example
title: Strong versus weak
problem: Under the strong principle and under the weak principle, is "All swans on this lake are white" meaningful, if the lake has 40 swans and new ones may hatch?
step: Strong principle: the sentence is a universal generalization covering all swans on the lake, now and later. No finite set of observations conclusively establishes it, since a new swan might be brown.
step: But it is conclusively refutable: seeing one non-white swan would show it false. Popper later made this asymmetry central to his own criterion of demarcation, but refutability is not verifiability.
step: Weak principle: observing many white swans makes it probable, and a non-white swan would count against it. So it is weakly verifiable and meaningful.
answer: Meaningless on the strong principle (if conclusive verification is required), meaningful on the weak principle.
@mistakes
- Treating "verifiable" as "verified" or "practically checkable": the principle concerns what observation could in principle bear on a sentence.
- Saying that the Circle held that ethical sentences are false: Ayer said they are not factual at all and express attitudes.
- Confusing "not cognitively meaningful" with "false": a sentence that fails the test is neither true nor false.
- Forgetting that analytic sentences pass the test: mathematics and logic are meaningful, and say nothing about the world.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the difference between the strong and the weak form of the verification principle, and say why Ayer chose the weak form.
hint: Think of universal generalizations in science and what can be done with a finite set of observations.
solution: The strong form requires that a sentence be conclusively verifiable by experience; the weak form requires only that some possible experience be relevant to its truth, in that it could make it probable. Scientific laws such as "All metals expand when heated" are generalizations over unlimited instances, and no finite set of observations establishes them conclusively. The strong form therefore counts the laws of science as meaningless, which defeats a program devoted to the scientific conception of the world. Ayer chose the weak form to keep scientific generalizations. The cost is that the weak form is harder to state without admitting too much, which is the subject of the next lesson.
