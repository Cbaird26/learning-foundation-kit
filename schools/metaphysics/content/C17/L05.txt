@article
Last week the verification principle looked like a clean test. This week it breaks. The problems have a pattern: the clear versions rule out too much (including science), the liberal versions let in too much (including the metaphysics they were meant to exclude), and the principle is hard to place by its own standard.

### Problem 1: the principle applied to itself

The most famous objection is that the principle fails its own test:

| P1 | A sentence is cognitively meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. |
| P2 | The verification principle (P1 itself) is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. |
| C | So the verification principle is not cognitively meaningful. |

The argument is valid, and its conclusion is embarrassing. There are two main replies, and both deny P2. The first says that the principle is a **stipulation or definition**: a proposal about how to use the words "meaningful" and "cognitive", so that it is analytic in the way a definition is. Ayer took roughly this line: he called the principle a definition and not an empirical hypothesis, while denying that it was arbitrary, since it fits the use of "meaningful" in science and ordinary thought. Carnap treated such criteria as proposals for the construction of a language, to be judged by their fruitfulness. The second says that the principle is an **explication**, a replacement of a vague everyday notion with an exact one. The cost of the first reply is that a stipulation seems unable to show that metaphysicians *ought* to be silent: they may simply decline the definition. A stipulated criterion gives no argument against anyone who does not accept it. The cost of reading the principle instead as a claim about how speakers use "meaningful" is that, so read, it looks false, since metaphysicians and their critics evidently understand each other's sentences.

### Problem 2: too strong

If verification must be conclusive, then **universal generalizations** such as scientific laws ("All metals expand when heated") are unverifiable, and **existential claims** such as "There is a planet with twice the mass of Jupiter in some galaxy" cannot be conclusively refuted. Conclusive verification would exclude much of science. Karl Popper's *Logik der Forschung* (1934) proposed falsifiability as a criterion that distinguishes science from non-science, but he said it was a criterion of demarcation, not of meaning.

### Problem 3: too weak

The weak form, as Ayer stated it in 1936, says that a sentence is verifiable if some possible experience is relevant to it. A more exact version says: $S$ is verifiable if there are auxiliary premises $A$ and an observation sentence $O$ such that $S$ together with $A$ entails $O$, but $A$ alone does not. In the introduction to the second edition (1946), Ayer accepted that such a formulation is too liberal, for this reason:

| P1 | $S$ counts as verifiable if, for some observation sentence $O$ and some auxiliary premises $A$, $S$ together with $A$ entails $O$ while $A$ alone does not entail $O$. |
| P2 | For any sentence $S$ that is neither analytic nor contradictory there is an observation sentence $O$ that is logically independent of $S$: neither $S$ nor $\neg S$ entails it. |
| P3 | If $O$ is independent of $S$, then with $A$ = "if $S$ then $O$", $S$ together with $A$ entails $O$ but $A$ alone does not. |
| C | So every sentence that is neither analytic nor contradictory counts as verifiable by that criterion. |

The argument is valid. Premise P3 holds because $S$ and "if $S$ then $O$" give $O$ by modus ponens, while "if $S$ then $O$" is equivalent to "$\neg S$ or $O$", which entails $O$ only if $\neg S$ does, and $\neg S$ does not by the independence of $O$. Ayer's revised version of 1946 allowed only auxiliary premises that are analytic, directly verifiable or independently verifiable; Alonzo Church argued in a review of 1949 that this version also fails, since it still admits any sentence or its negation. Attempts at a precise criterion continued, and none won general acceptance.

### Problem 4: theory and holism

Scientific theories use **theoretical terms** ("electron") and **dispositions** ("soluble") that are not definable from observation words. Carnap's "Testability and Meaning" (1936 to 1937) accordingly replaced verifiability by **confirmability**, and used reduction sentences to link dispositional words to tests. Carl Hempel's "Problems and Changes in the Empiricist Criterion of Meaning" (1950) concluded that a theory's significance belongs to the system as a whole, since individual sentences do not have their own observational consequences. This holism leads directly to the Duhem and Quine thesis that sentences face experience together.

### What survived

Verificationism did not die from one argument but from a long failure to formulate a criterion that excluded metaphysics without excluding science. Its motivation survives. Philosophers still ask what difference a claim would make to experience or to use, and some views in the philosophy of language, such as Michael Dummett's anti-realism, treat meaning in terms of what can be recognized as established. Whether the Circle's rejection of metaphysics was correct is another question: many later metaphysicians (see weeks 11 and 12) work in the Circle's logical style without accepting its criterion.
@example
title: Building the trivialising premise
problem: Show how the liberal criterion counts "The Absolute is idle" as verifiable, with $O$ = "A red light is on in the laboratory at noon".
step: Let $S$ = "The Absolute is idle". It is logically independent of $O$: neither $S$ nor its negation entails anything about the laboratory's lights.
step: Take the auxiliary premise $A$ = "If the Absolute is idle, then a red light is on in the laboratory at noon".
step: Then $S$ together with $A$ entails $O$ by modus ponens. And $A$ alone does not entail $O$: if the Absolute is not idle, $A$ is true while the red light may be off.
step: So by the liberal criterion, "The Absolute is idle" is verifiable, and since the construction works for any sentence, the criterion excludes nothing.
answer: The liberal criterion makes every sentence verifiable, since a suitable conditional can always be supplied as the auxiliary premise.
@example
title: The two horns for the self-application objection
problem: Say what the defender of the verification principle must say about its status, and what the cost is on each of two options.
step: Option one: the principle is a **definition** of "cognitively meaningful". Then it is analytic and passes its own test. The cost: a definition cannot show that someone who rejects it is mistaken, so it gives no argument against metaphysics.
step: Option two: the principle is a **claim about ordinary use** of "meaningful". Then it is a claim of fact about language that should be testable. The cost: it looks false, since speakers understand sentences such as "The Absolute is idle" well enough to dispute them.
step: A third option, Carnap's, treats the principle as a proposal about how to build a language, to be assessed by its fruitfulness. The cost: it no longer shows that metaphysics is nonsense, only that a given language excludes it.
answer: The defender must choose between a status on which the principle is true but toothless and one on which it has force but is doubtful.
@mistakes
- Saying that the self-refutation objection shows the verification principle is false: it shows that the principle is not meaningful by its own standard, unless it is reclassified as a definition or proposal.
- Confusing Popper's falsifiability with verifiability or with a criterion of meaning: it was offered as a demarcation of science.
- Saying that the failure of one formulation shows that every empiricist theory of meaning fails: the objections concern particular formulations.
- Forgetting that analytic sentences pass the criterion by clause one, so the objection about mathematics does not arise.
