@article
Suppose we give up the analytic-synthetic distinction. What then is philosophy? Quine's answer: continuous with science. There is no standpoint outside our best theory of the world from which to certify it, so philosophy works from within that theory. This week we follow three consequences: a naturalized epistemology, a criterion of ontological commitment with an argument for mathematical objects, and a theory of the limits of what facts can fix about meaning and reference.

### Epistemology naturalized

In "Epistemology Naturalized" (1969, in *Ontological Relativity and Other Essays*) Quine reviews the empiricist project of founding knowledge. The project had two sides: a **conceptual** side, to translate talk about the world into talk of sense experience, logic and set theory, and a **doctrinal** side, to justify our beliefs from sensory evidence. Carnap's *Aufbau* (1928) was the great attempt at the first. Quine argues that translation cannot be completed, since sentences about bodies do not individually translate into sentences about sense data, and that Hume had already shown that general beliefs go beyond the evidence. The argument:

| P1 | If a foundation for science prior to science were achievable, then a rational reconstruction of our knowledge of the world from sense experience, logic and set theory would succeed. |
| P2 | No such rational reconstruction succeeds. |
| C1 | So a foundation for science prior to science is not achievable. |
| P3 | If no such foundation is achievable, then either epistemology should be abandoned or it should be pursued within science, as the study of how evidence leads to theory. |
| P4 | Epistemology should not be abandoned. |
| C2 | So epistemology should be pursued within science: it becomes a chapter of empirical psychology. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens, then disjunctive syllogism). Quine says we should study how human beings, with their sensory input, produce their theory as output. Jaegwon Kim objected in "What Is Naturalized Epistemology?" (1988) that epistemology is about **justification**, which is normative, and that psychology describes how we do reason, not how we ought to; so what Quine proposes is not a continuation of epistemology. This is an attack on P3: it denies that the study of belief-formation can count as pursuing epistemology. Quine's later reply was that epistemology within science is a branch of engineering concerned with how to attain truth, so normative questions about method have a place in it; critics also raised a circularity charge, which he answered by denying that there is any standpoint outside science from which to ask for non-circular justification.

### Ontological commitment and indispensability

In "On What There Is" (1948) Quine proposed a criterion: a theory is committed to those entities that must be among the values of its variables, if the theory is formulated in first-order logic, for its sentences to be true. What there is according to a theory is what its existential quantifiers range over, which is a matter of regimenting the theory, not of consulting the words one uses. Together with confirmational holism, this supports the **indispensability argument**, developed by Quine and Hilary Putnam:

| P1 | We ought to be ontologically committed to all and only the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories. |
| P2 | Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories. |
| C | So we ought to be ontologically committed to mathematical entities. |

The argument is valid. Hartry Field's *Science Without Numbers* (1980) denies P2: he tried to reformulate Newtonian gravitation without quantifying over numbers. Penelope Maddy denied P1: scientists do not treat the success of a theory as confirming every part of its mathematics, and mathematical practice has its own standards. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 38% of respondents leaned toward Platonism about abstract objects and 42% toward nominalism: a split that is no evidence for either view.

### Indeterminacy and ontological relativity

In *Word and Object* (1960) Quine imagines **radical translation**: a linguist learning a language with no prior help, who hears the native say "gavagai" when a rabbit passes. All the evidence is behavioral. Quine argues that the evidence leaves open translating "gavagai" as "rabbit", "undetached rabbit part" or "rabbit stage", and that different **translation manuals** may fit all the evidence yet be incompatible with each other: the **indeterminacy of translation**. This is not a lack of knowledge about a determinate fact; Quine's point is that no further fact exists. Related is the **inscrutability of reference**: what our own terms refer to cannot be fixed by behavior. In "Ontological Relativity" (1968) he concludes that it makes sense to say what a theory's objects are only relative to a background language or translation manual. A **proxy function** is a one-to-one mapping of the domain onto itself; reinterpreting every predicate by its image under the map leaves the truth of all sentences unchanged. For example, each object might be replaced by its cosmic complement, the rest of the universe apart from that object. The regress ends, he says, in acquiescence in our home language. Critics reply that the argument relies on a behaviorism about the evidence that is itself contested, and that this threatens the realism Quine wishes to keep.
@example
title: Reconstructing Quine's case in one paragraph
problem: Summarize Quine's case against analyticity from the explanations he considers to the lesson of holism, in a short paragraph that identifies the key premise.
step: The case begins by asking what "analytic" means. Kantian containment is too narrow and metaphorical; the better gloss is truth by meaning, which in turn requires a notion of synonymy.
step: Definition, interchangeability *salva veritate*, semantical rules and verification are each considered. Interchangeability in an extensional language is too weak; with "necessarily" it is circular; semantical rules say "analytic for $L$" without explaining it; verification leads to reductionism.
step: Reductionism fails because statements are tested as a group, so no single statement has its own confirming experiences; this undermines the verification account of synonymy and removes the motive for the distinction.
step: The key premise is that no available explanation of analyticity is both clear and independent of analyticity; the objection of Grice and Strawson targets the preceding conditional premise, that a respectable distinction needs such an explanation.
answer: Quine argues that none of the explanations works and that holism undermines the last, so the distinction is an unexplained article of faith; the main replies deny that clarity requires a non-circular explanation or offer a new explanation.
@example
title: Proxy functions on a small domain
problem: A theory has the domain $\{a, b, c\}$ and one predicate $F$ true of $a$ and $b$. Use the proxy function $f$ with $f(a) = b$, $f(b) = c$, $f(c) = a$ to build an alternative interpretation with the same truth-values.
step: Reinterpret each object as its image: the new domain is $\{f(a), f(b), f(c)\} = \{b, c, a\}$, the same set, and each name for an object now names its image.
step: Reinterpret the predicate $F$ as applying to the images of the things it applied to: $F'$ is true of $f(a) = b$ and $f(b) = c$.
step: Check a sentence: "Fa" was true; on the new interpretation the name "a" names $f(a) = b$ and $F'$ is true of $b$, so it is still true. "Fc" was false; now "c" names $a$ and $F'$ is not true of $a$, so it is still false.
step: Every sentence keeps its truth-value, so the evidence cannot distinguish the two interpretations, which differ in what the words refer to.
answer: The proxy function gives a systematically different reference scheme with identical truth-values, illustrating the inscrutability of reference.
@mistakes
- Saying that Quine's naturalism is the claim that only science yields knowledge: the claim is that there is no first philosophy prior to science, so philosophy works within our best theory.
- Reading the indeterminacy of translation as ignorance about a hidden fact: Quine's point is that there is no further fact to know.
- Treating the criterion of ontological commitment as a claim about what exists: it says what a theory is committed to, given its regimentation.
- Confusing the indispensability argument's premises: Field denies that mathematics is indispensable, Maddy denies that indispensability brings commitment.

@quiz self
prompt: The indispensability argument has the premises P1: We ought to be ontologically committed to all and only the entities that are indispensable to our best scientific theories, and P2: Mathematical entities are indispensable to our best scientific theories. Explain how the replies of Hartry Field and of Penelope Maddy differ: which premise each denies, and what each must show to make the reply good.
hint: Ask what each reply says about the role of mathematics in science and about what the success of a theory confirms.
solution: The conclusion, that we ought to be committed to mathematical entities, follows from P1 and P2. Field denies P2: he holds that mathematics is a useful tool but not indispensable, and he must show that our best physical theories can be reformulated without quantifying over numbers, as he attempted for Newtonian gravitation; critics doubt that the method extends to the rest of modern physics. Maddy denies P1: she holds that the success of a theory does not confirm every part of the mathematics it uses, since scientists treat much of it as an idealisation or tool, and she must show that this matches actual scientific practice and that mathematics has its own standards of justification. So Field accepts the principle linking indispensability to commitment but denies the indispensability, while Maddy grants the indispensability and denies that it brings commitment.
