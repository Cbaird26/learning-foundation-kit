## Week-by-week review

**Weeks 1 and 2: Frege, Russell, Moore.** Frege's quantificational logic (1879), logicism, and sense and reference (1892): the argument that meaning is not just reference, in which the Millian denies the premise linking meaning to cognitive value. Russell's paradox: unrestricted comprehension gives a set $R$ with $R \in R \leftrightarrow R \notin R$; responses are types, Separation and paraconsistent logic. Moore's proof of an external world needs closure of knowledge under known entailment. Russell's theory of descriptions: existence, uniqueness and predication, with two scopes of negation.

**Weeks 3 and 4: the Tractatus and verificationism.** The world is the totality of facts; propositions picture states of affairs; logic is senseless, not nonsensical; saying versus showing; the argument for simples. The verification principle: a sentence is factually meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable; strong versus weak; emotivism; the argument against metaphysics.

**Weeks 5 and 6: problems and Carnap.** The principle is not itself analytic or verifiable (reply: it is a proposal); liberal weak criteria admit every sentence; Church's 1949 review; Hempel's holism. Carnap: frameworks, tolerance, internal versus external questions; external questions are practical or lack cognitive content.

**Weeks 7 and 8: Quine.** The two dogmas; the circle of analyticity, synonymy, interchangeability and necessity; holism and the web of belief; naturalized epistemology and Kim's objection; the criterion of ontological commitment; the indispensability argument (Field denies that mathematics is indispensable, Maddy denies that indispensability brings commitment); indeterminacy of translation and proxy functions.

**Weeks 9 and 10: the later Wittgenstein and ordinary language.** Meaning as use for a large class of cases; family resemblance; the rule-following paradox and Kripke's quus argument (dispositionalists and primitivists deny that no fact fixes meaning); Austin's speech acts; Ryle's category mistake and regress argument; Strawson's reply to Russell; the private language argument and the premise about the standard of correctness.

**Weeks 11 and 12: metaphysics returns.** Kripke semantics, frame conditions (T reflexive, 4 transitive, B symmetric, 5 Euclidean, D serial), rigid designators, the necessary a posteriori and the modal argument against descriptivism; Lewis's modal realism and counterparts; naturalism, scientism, neo-Carnapians and heavyweight realists, and the debate about intuitions.

## Key distinctions

- Validity versus soundness; a premise denied versus an argument said to be invalid.
- Sense versus reference; analytic versus synthetic; internal versus external questions; strong versus weak verification.
- Senseless (*sinnlos*) versus nonsensical (*unsinnig*) in the Tractatus; constative versus performative; illocutionary versus perlocutionary.
- Methodological naturalism versus ontological naturalism versus scientism.

## Common errors

Treating a view as refuted because an objection exists; confusing exegesis with evaluation; attributing Kripke's reading to Wittgenstein without qualification; saying that Quine proved there are no analytic sentences; saying that the Tractatus calls logic nonsense; and treating survey percentages as evidence for a view.

@exam self
prompt: In an essay of about 500 words, explain how Quine's attack on the analytic-synthetic distinction bears on (a) the verification principle and (b) Carnap's distinction between internal and external questions. State the arguments you rely on in standard form and say which premise Quine's objection attacks in each case, and give your own assessment of one reply.
hint: Ask where each position uses the notion of analyticity, and what holism says about verifying sentences one at a time.
solution: The verification principle has two clauses: a sentence is meaningful only if it is analytic or empirically verifiable. The first clause depends on a clear notion of analyticity, which Quine says has not been explained without circularity. His holism also bears on the second clause: sentences do not face experience singly, so it is doubtful that each has its own verification conditions, as the principle requires (Hempel reached a related conclusion). In standard form: P1: If the analytic-synthetic distinction is respectable, "analytic" can be explained in terms that are clear and do not presuppose analyticity. P2: If "analytic" can be so explained, it can be explained by one of the available proposals (containment, definition, interchangeability, semantical rules, verification). P3: None of these proposals works. C: So the distinction is not respectable. Applied to the principle, the first clause is left without a respectable basis. For Carnap: P1: Every existence question is internal or external. P2: No external question is theoretical. P3: Internal questions about numbers are trivially answered by the rules. C: So the philosopher's question about numbers is trivial or not theoretical. Quine attacks P1: the division of questions into those answered by a framework's rules and those about which framework to adopt depends on the analytic-synthetic distinction, so without it the choice of framework is continuous with scientific theory choice. Assessment: Grice and Strawson reply, against the premise that a respectable distinction needs a non-circular explanation, that a family of concepts that speakers use and agree about can be respectable, and that Quine's standard of clarity is too demanding. I think this reply shows that Quine's argument does not establish that there is no distinction at all, but it does not restore the sharp, principled line that Carnap's argument needs. A neo-Carnapian can respond that a vague line is enough for much of Carnap's purpose, though then the conclusion is weaker.
