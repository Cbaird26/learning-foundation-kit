@article
Is "No bachelor is married" true in the same way as "No bachelor is more than six feet tall"? Most people say the first is true because of what "bachelor" means and the second, if true, because of how things are. This is the distinction between **analytic** and **synthetic** truths, and in the first half of the twentieth century it was the foundation of the empiricists' account of logic, mathematics and philosophy: analytic truths are knowable by understanding alone, synthetic truths need experience. In 1951 W. V. Quine attacked it in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism", published in *The Philosophical Review* and reprinted in *From a Logical Point of View* (1953).

### The two dogmas

Quine named two articles of faith of empiricism. The first is the belief in a fundamental cleavage between analytic truths, which are grounded in meanings independently of matters of fact, and synthetic truths, which are grounded in fact. The second is **reductionism**: the belief that each meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construction out of terms that refer to immediate experience. Quine argues that both are mistaken and that they are at root the same.

### The case against analyticity

Quine does not say that no sentence is ever called analytic. His claim is that "analytic" has never been made clear in terms that do not presuppose it. He considers the available explanations:

- **Kant's** definition, that the predicate is contained in the subject, applies only to sentences of subject-predicate form and relies on a metaphor of containment. The better idea is that a sentence is true by virtue of meaning and not fact.
- Analytic sentences come in two classes: **logical truths** ("No unmarried man is married"), true on every reinterpretation of their non-logical words, and sentences such as "No bachelor is married", which become logical truths when synonyms are put for synonyms. The second class depends on **synonymy**.
- **Definition** does not explain synonymy: a dictionary reports synonymies that were already there, and a stipulated definition creates synonymy only for new notation.
- **Interchangeability *salva veritate*** (putting one expression for another without changing truth-value) is not enough in an extensional language: "creature with a heart" and "creature with kidneys" are interchangeable in that way but not synonymous. To strengthen it one adds "necessarily", but "necessarily" applied to a statement is true only if it is analytic, so the explanation is circular.
- **Semantical rules** of an artificial language, as in Carnap's work, list sentences as analytic *for* a language $L$, but they leave "analytic" unexplained as a general term and give no help with natural language.
- The **verification theory** says that sentences are synonymous when they are confirmed by the same experiences, which leads to the second dogma.

The reconstruction in standard form:

| P1 | If the analytic-synthetic distinction is respectable, then "analytic" can be explained in terms that are clear and do not presuppose analyticity. |
| P2 | The only available ways of explaining analyticity are Kantian containment, definition, interchangeability, semantical rules and verification. |
| P3 | Each of these either is too narrow, or is circular (it presupposes synonymy, necessity or analyticity), or does not apply to natural language. |
| C1 | So "analytic" cannot be explained in clear terms that do not presuppose analyticity. |
| C2 | So the analytic-synthetic distinction is not respectable: it is an unempirical dogma, a doctrine held on faith and not on evidence. |

The argument is valid: P2 and P3 give C1, and P1 with C1 gives C2 by modus tollens.

### Holism

Against the second dogma Quine uses an idea he credits to Pierre Duhem. Statements do not face the tribunal of experience one at a time but as a **group**: a test involves a theory together with auxiliary assumptions, and if a prediction fails, logic says only that something in the whole is false. Total science is like a web of belief, connected to experience only at its edges, in which any statement can be kept true if enough is revised elsewhere, and no statement, not even a law of logic, is immune from revision. Quine mentions proposals to revise the law of excluded middle to simplify quantum mechanics. Choice among revisions is guided by conservatism and simplicity, which are pragmatic. So there is no sharp line between statements that hold come what may and those that are open to experience.

### Replies

H. P. Grice and P. F. Strawson, "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956), attack P1: Quine demands a definition of "analytic" in terms outside its family of related notions (synonymy, self-contradiction, necessity), but a circle of concepts need not be vicious, since speakers can apply them and agree about many cases. Carnap and others respond that a notion that can be taught by examples and applied by speakers has sufficient clarity. Paul Boghossian later separated **metaphysical analyticity** (truth in virtue of meaning alone, which Quine attacks) from **epistemic analyticity** (grasp of meaning alone suffices for justified belief) and defended the second, which attacks P2's claim that there are no other explanations. Quine's own reply to defenders is that behavioral facts do not fix the line.
@example
title: Interchangeability and the circle
problem: On the planet Lumen, exactly the same creatures have a "glim organ" and a "tuning fork". Does the interchangeability of "creature with a glim organ" and "creature with a tuning fork" in an extensional language show that the expressions are synonymous? What happens if we add "necessarily"?
step: In an extensional language, truth depends only on which things an expression applies to. Since the two expressions apply to exactly the same creatures, either can be put for the other in any extensional sentence without changing truth-value.
step: But they are not cognitively synonymous: someone could understand both and not know that they apply to the same creatures. So interchangeability in an extensional language is not enough for synonymy.
step: Add "necessarily": say that the expressions are synonymous if "Necessarily, all and only creatures with a glim organ are creatures with a tuning fork" is true. That would exclude the Lumen case if the coincidence is contingent.
step: But Quine holds that "necessarily" applied to a statement is true only if the statement is analytic. So the explanation of synonymy presupposes analyticity, the notion we wanted to explain, and the explanation is circular.
answer: Interchangeability in extensional language is too weak; strengthening it with "necessarily" makes the explanation circular.
@example
title: Holism and a failed prediction
problem: A theory $T$ with auxiliary assumptions $A_1$ (the instrument is calibrated) and $A_2$ (no stray light enters the room) predicts $O$. We observe $\neg O$. What does logic tell us, and what does Quine's holism add?
step: Logic gives $\neg (T \wedge A_1 \wedge A_2)$, that is, at least one of the three is false. It does not say which.
step: So we may reject $T$, or reject $A_1$ (the instrument was not calibrated), or reject $A_2$; or we might even reject the observation report.
step: Holism adds that the choice between these revisions is not settled by the experience alone but by overall considerations of simplicity and conservatism, and that in principle no statement is immune, including very general ones.
answer: Logic says only that the conjunction fails; holism says that the choice of what to revise is pragmatic and that no statement is exempt in principle.
@mistakes
- Saying that Quine argued that "bachelor" and "unmarried man" are not synonymous: his claim is that "synonymy" has not been explained without presupposing analyticity.
- Confusing the first dogma (analytic-synthetic) with the second (reductionism): they are distinct, though Quine argues they are connected.
- Saying that Quine's holism implies that all beliefs are equally likely to be revised: he says that revisions are guided by conservatism and simplicity.
- Reading P1 as claiming that every concept must be definable: Quine's demand is for a clear explanation not presupposing analyticity, and Grice and Strawson deny that this is required.

prompt: Explain, in your own words, why Quine thinks that explaining analyticity by synonymy and synonymy by interchangeability moves in a circle.
hint: Follow the chain from "analytic" to "synonymy" to "interchangeability" to "necessarily".
solution: Quine says that a sentence like "No bachelor is married" is analytic because replacing "bachelor" by its synonym "unmarried man" yields a logical truth, so "analytic" depends on synonymy. To explain synonymy by interchangeability *salva veritate*, one finds that interchangeability in an extensional language is too weak, since coextensive predicates are interchangeable. To strengthen it one says that the expressions are interchangeable within a context governed by "necessarily", but "necessarily" applied to a statement is true only if the statement is analytic. So analyticity is explained by synonymy, synonymy by interchangeability with "necessarily", and "necessarily" by analyticity, and the circle returns to the starting point.
