@article
Analytic philosophy began as a movement against metaphysics, and by the 1970s it was a movement that produced a great deal of it, in Kripke's essentialism, Lewis's plurality of worlds and a revival of theories of universals, time, composition and causation. Meanwhile Quine's naturalism told philosophers to work in step with science. These trends sit uneasily together, and today's debates about how philosophy should be done are in large part debates about how they can be reconciled.

### Varieties of naturalism

**Methodological naturalism** says that philosophy should proceed in continuity with the methods of the sciences and take scientific results seriously. **Ontological** (or metaphysical) **naturalism** says that there is nothing but the natural world, and that it is the world described by science. Quine's naturalism is primarily the first: there is no first philosophy prior to science from which to judge it. A stronger doctrine is **scientism**, that only the methods of empirical science yield knowledge. Wilfrid Sellars, in "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956) and "Philosophy and the Scientific Image of Man" (1962), argued that the **manifest image** of persons and things and the **scientific image** need to be reconciled; he attacked the "myth of the given" in empiricist epistemology. Critics of naturalism include those who defend a **liberal naturalism** that makes room for non-scientific forms of understanding (a view discussed in the collection *Naturalism in Question*, edited by Mario De Caro and David Macarthur, 2004).

### The scientism argument

A familiar objection to scientism is self-undermining:

| P1 | If scientism is true, then a thesis is known only if it is established by the methods of empirical science. |
| P2 | The thesis of scientism is not established by the methods of empirical science. |
| C | So if scientism is true, its thesis is not known. |

The argument is valid. Replies attack P2: a defender of scientism says that the thesis is supported by an empirical, inductive argument from the success of science and the failure of other methods. Others note that the objection does not touch Quine's methodological naturalism, which does not say that only science yields knowledge. The objection resembles the self-application argument against verificationism in week 5, and its force is again disputed.

### Metaontology: what are ontological questions?

The Carnap-Quine debate has been revived in what is called **metametaphysics**, the study of what metaphysical questions are and how to answer them. The main positions, in outline:

| Position | Main claim | Key figures |
|---|---|---|
| Quinean | Ontological questions are substantive and answered by what our best total theory quantifies over | Quine, and many later metaphysicians |
| Neo-Carnapian | Many ontological disputes are verbal, or answered easily by the use of our words | Eli Hirsch (quantifier variance), Amie Thomasson (easy ontology), in a different way David Chalmers |
| Heavyweight realist | Ontological questions are substantive and need not be settled by science alone: some structure is fundamental ("joint-carving") | Ted Sider, Kit Fine and others, with views on fundamentality and grounding |

Philosophers disagree about which of these is correct. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 38% of professional philosophers leaned toward Platonism about abstract objects and 42% toward nominalism, and 52% toward physicalism about the mind and 32% toward non-physicalism, so there is no consensus even about the ontology; and the proportion of philosophers holding a view is not evidence that it is true.

### Naturalists against metaphysics, and the method of intuitions

James Ladyman and Don Ross, in *Every Thing Must Go* (2007), argue that much recent analytic metaphysics fails because it rests on intuitions and neglects physics, and that metaphysics should be a unification of scientific results. Defenders of the metaphysical tradition reply that science does not settle questions about, for example, grounding, composition or modality, and that scientific theories themselves carry metaphysical assumptions.

A related debate concerns **intuitions**, the judgments about cases (Gettier cases, the Chinese room, Kripke's Gödel case) that philosophers treat as data. Experimental philosophers, beginning with studies by Jonathan Weinberg, Shaun Nichols and Stephen Stich (2001) and by Edouard Machery and colleagues (2004), reported variation in such judgments across demographic and cultural groups; some of the early findings have been disputed in later replication attempts. Timothy Williamson, in *The Philosophy of Philosophy* (2007), defended armchair methods and argued that the use of intuitions is not special to philosophy. The question what the method of philosophy is, and whether it can yield knowledge, remains open.
@example
title: Reading ontological commitment off a regimented sentence
problem: Regiment "Every dog has a tail" as $\forall x\,(Dx \to \exists y\,(Ty \wedge Hxy))$. According to Quine's criterion, what kinds of entity is the theory committed to?
step: The criterion says that a theory is committed to whatever must be among the values of its bound variables in order for the theory's sentences to be true.
step: The variable $x$ ranges over things that can be dogs and the variable $y$ over things that can be tails, and the quantifier $\exists y$ requires that if there are dogs then there are tails belonging to them.
step: So the theory is committed to dogs and tails. It is not committed to the property of being a dog, because there is no quantification over properties; the predicate letter $D$ is not a bound variable.
answer: Dogs and tails, not properties.

@mistakes
- Saying that Quine's naturalism is scientism: he says there is no first philosophy outside science, not that only science yields knowledge.
- Treating the neo-Carnapian view as the view that metaphysics is meaningless: it holds that many ontological disputes are verbal or easy, not that they are cognitively meaningless in the verificationist sense.
- Reading survey percentages as evidence for a view: the share of philosophers who hold a view is not a reason to think it true.
- Presenting the debate over experimental philosophy as settled: the findings and their significance are disputed.

@quiz self
prompt: Compare Carnap and Quine on the status of ontological questions in about four sentences. Say what they agree on and what they disagree on.
hint: They agree about something concerning the choice of a language or theory; they disagree about whether there is a principled division.
solution: Carnap and Quine agree that there is no standpoint outside all language or theory from which to settle what exists, and that the choice of a language or theory is guided by pragmatic considerations such as simplicity and fruitfulness. They disagree about whether there is a principled line between questions answered within a framework by its rules and questions about which framework to adopt: Carnap draws such a line and treats the external question as practical, whereas Quine, rejecting the analytic-synthetic distinction on which it depends, treats ontological questions as continuous with questions of scientific theory, so that they are as legitimate and as theoretical as any other. As a consequence Quine thinks existence questions are answered by what our best total theory quantifies over, whereas Carnap thinks the philosophical existence question is not a factual one.
