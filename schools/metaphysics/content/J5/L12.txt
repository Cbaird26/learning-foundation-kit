@article
People are part of ecosystems, and our activities change them. Some changes are small, and some threaten whole populations.

### Major impacts

- **Habitat loss**: clearing forest, draining wetlands or building cities removes the place organisms live. It is the leading cause of species decline.
- **Pollution**: chemicals, litter and extra nutrients such as fertilizer running into water can poison organisms or cause algae blooms that use up oxygen.
- **Invasive species**: organisms moved by people to places where they did not evolve can outcompete native species because their usual predators and diseases are missing.
- **Overharvesting**: taking organisms faster than populations can recover, as in overfishing.
- **Climate change**: burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gases that trap heat, raising global temperatures and changing habitats.

### Biomagnification

Some pollutants are not broken down and build up in living tissue. At each higher trophic level, predators eat many prey, so the pollutant concentration rises. This is **biomagnification**. If a pollutant increases about tenfold at each level, a level-4 predator has $10^3$ times the concentration found in the producers.

### Measuring change

A **percent change** compares a new value with the original:

$$\text{percent decrease} = \frac{\text{original} - \text{new}}{\text{original}} \times 100.$$

Data like this help scientists judge whether an impact is small or serious and whether a solution works.

### What can be done?

Actions work at several scales:

- **Individuals**: reduce, reuse and recycle; save energy; walk, bike or share rides; avoid releasing pets or plants into the wild.
- **Communities**: protect parks and wetlands, restore habitats and build wildlife corridors.
- **Governments**: set fishing limits, clean-air and clean-water rules and support renewable energy.

Because the energy pyramid shows how little energy reaches the top, eating lower on the food chain, for example more plants, uses less land and energy per meal. Every solution has trade-offs in cost, convenience and effects on people, so scientists weigh evidence rather than relying on a single fix.

@example
title: Percent decrease in habitat
problem: A wetland shrinks from 200 hectares to 170 hectares over 10 years. What is the percent decrease?
step: Find the amount lost: $200 - 170 = 30$ hectares.
step: Divide by the original value: $30 \div 200 = 0.15$.
step: Multiply by $100$ to get a percent: $15\%$.
step: Check: 15% of 200 is 30, which matches the loss.

@mistakes
- Dividing by the new value instead of the original value when finding a percent change.
- Thinking natural causes never matter; people are one of many factors, and scientists use data to tell them apart.
- Assuming one solution has no downsides; real solutions have costs and trade-offs to weigh.

prompt: A forest covers 120 hectares in 2005 and 90 hectares in 2025. What is the percent decrease?
answer: 25
check: (120 - 90) / 120 * 100
unit: %
tol: 0.2
hint: Subtract to find the loss, then divide by the original area.
solution: The loss is $30$ ha, and $30 \div 120 = 0.25$, so 25%.

@practice num
prompt: A household produces 12 kg of waste each week and recycles 30% of it. How many kilograms of waste are recycled in 52 weeks?
answer: 187.2
check: 12 * 0.30 * 52
unit: kg
tol: 1%
hint: Find the weekly recycled amount, then multiply by the weeks.
solution: Each week: $12 \times 0.30 = 3.6$ kg. For 52 weeks: $3.6 \times 52 = 187.2$ kg.

answer: 500
check: 0.5 * 10 ** 3
unit: ppm
hint: How many steps are there from level 1 to level 4?
