@article
Parmenides and Zeno seemed to have shown that a world of many changing things is impossible. Yet no one doubts that wood burns and runners overtake tortoises. One way out is to refuse the argument's premises; another, which we meet this week, is to keep the premises that matter and re-describe the world so that the conclusion no longer follows. The atomists did the second. A different group, the Sophists, shifted the question away from nature and toward human beings: what can we know, and what is merely our convention?

### Atoms and void

**Leucippus** and **Democritus**, the latter of Abdera, in the later fifth century BCE, held that reality consists of two things: **atoms** (from the Greek *atomos*, "uncuttable") and **void**, empty space. The atoms are infinite in number, indivisible, unchanging and in constant motion. According to Aristotle (Metaphysics I.4), they differ in shape, in arrangement and in position. His illustration is about letters: A differs from N in shape, AN from NA in arrangement, and Z from N in position (Z is N turned). Everything we see comes about by atoms joining, separating and rearranging in the void.

Aristotle (On Generation and Corruption I.8) reports that the atomists accepted the evidence of the senses that there is motion and plurality, and that they answered Parmenides directly. Each atom is like a Parmenidean being: full, uniform, uncreated and unchanging. What Parmenides denied was the void, which atoms need in order to move apart; so they said that what is not (the void) exists no less than what is. Change is not an atom becoming different but atoms rearranging. This is a pluralist answer to Parmenides, and it is the origin of the idea that the world's variety comes from the arrangements of a few kinds of simple parts.

Aristotle also reports two arguments for the atoms' existence. The first is for void.

| P1 | There is motion. |
| P2 | Motion requires empty space for bodies to move into: in a completely full world nothing could move. |
| C | So there is void. |

The second argument, reported at On Generation and Corruption I.2, is for indivisible bodies.

| P1 | If a body is divisible through and through, then it could be divided everywhere at once. |
| P2 | If it were divided everywhere at once, nothing would be left: no body, since a body cannot be made of points or of nothing. |
| P3 | A body cannot be reduced to nothing by division. |
| C1 | So no body is divisible through and through. |
| P4 | If no body is divisible through and through, then there are indivisible magnitudes. |
| C | So there are indivisible magnitudes. |

This is an answer to the problem Zeno raised about infinite division, and Aristotle's reply (Week 2) distinguishes potential from actual divisions, rejecting the assumption in P1 that a body divisible without limit could actually be divided everywhere at once.

The atomists also divided qualities. Democritus is reported as saying that sweet and bitter, hot and cold, and color exist by convention (*nomos*), while in truth there are atoms and void. This is an ancestor of the later distinction between primary and secondary qualities, which later courses in the pathway take up.

### The Sophists

The **Sophists** were itinerant teachers in the fifth century BCE, who charged fees for teaching skills for public life, above all persuasive speech. They were not a school with a doctrine, and generalizations about them mostly come from Plato, who wrote against them, so we should read his descriptions critically. A recurring theme was the contrast between **nomos** (law, custom, convention) and **physis** (nature). Is justice a matter of nature or merely of human agreement? Is the Greek way of life natural, or one of several customs? The contrast gave a vocabulary for criticizing inherited norms, and some writers used it to argue that justice is a convention that a clever person can ignore when no one is watching.

**Protagoras** of Abdera is credited with the saying that man is the measure of all things, of those that are that they are, and of those that are not that they are not (as Plato reports it in the Theaetetus, 152a). In Plato's presentation this means that things are for each person as they appear to that person: if the same food tastes sour to one and sweet to another, then it is sour for the one and sweet for the other. This is **relativism** about truth. **Gorgias** of Leontini is reported (in Sextus Empiricus and in a work attributed to Aristotle's school) to have argued in a work on not-being that nothing exists; that if anything did, it could not be known; and that if it could be known, it could not be communicated. Whether this is serious metaphysics, a parody of the Eleatics or a display of rhetorical skill is disputed.

### The self-refutation argument

Plato's Socrates argues against Protagoras in the Theaetetus (170a–171c) that the doctrine undermines itself. A reconstruction:

| P1 | For each person, whatever seems true to that person is true for that person (Protagoras's thesis). |
| P2 | It seems to many people that Protagoras's thesis is false. |
| C1 | So, by P1, it is true for them that the thesis is false. |
| P3 | Protagoras's thesis is supposed to be true for everyone, and not merely true for those who hold it. |
| C | So the thesis is not true for everyone, contrary to what it is supposed to be: by his own principle Protagoras must concede that it is false for those who reject it. |

The best reply is that relativism is meant to be relativized all the way down: the thesis is true for Protagoras and for those who accept it, and false for those who reject it, and there is no inconsistency in that. Plato's answer is that the relativist has now given up the thesis's claim to be a truth for everyone, and that the Protagorean who wants to teach or to claim wisdom must hold that some people are better judges than others. Whether the argument succeeds is still debated.

@example
title: The argument for indivisible bodies
problem: A student says that the atomists' argument for indivisible bodies "just assumes there are atoms". Reconstruct the argument and say which premise Aristotle questions.
step: State the argument as above: P1 says a body that is divisible through and through could be divided everywhere at once; P2 says that then nothing would remain; P3 says a body cannot be reduced to nothing by division; so C1, no body is divisible through and through, and with P4 (the only alternative is indivisible magnitudes), C. The conclusion is not assumed in the premises.
step: Check validity. If P1 and P2 are true, then a body divisible through and through could be reduced to nothing, which P3 denies; so, by reductio, no body is divisible through and through. The step from there to "there are indivisible magnitudes" is P4, which assumes bodies exist and that the only alternative to unlimited divisibility is indivisible parts.
step: Find the weak premise. P1 says that being divisible without limit means being dividable everywhere simultaneously. Aristotle denies this: divisibility is potential, so at no time are all the divisions actually made.
step: The student is mistaken that the conclusion was assumed, but is right that a further assumption is needed, namely that bodies exist and that actual divisions are the only kind.
answer: The argument is a reductio and does not assume atoms; Aristotle rejects premise P1, which treats unlimited divisibility as simultaneous actual division.

@example
title: A Protagorean case
problem: Ana says the corridor is warm and Ben says it is cold. On Plato's presentation of Protagoras's doctrine, what is the status of each claim, and why is it not a case of one of them being mistaken?
step: Apply P1: whatever seems true to a person is true for that person. The corridor seems warm to Ana, so it is warm for Ana; it seems cold to Ben, so it is cold for Ben.
step: There is no contradiction, because each claim is relative to a perceiver, and "warm for Ana" and "cold for Ben" can both be true.
step: Neither is mistaken, since neither can be wrong about how things seem to them. There is nothing further, on this doctrine, that makes one of them right.
step: A critic can press: if Ana and Ben seem to disagree, what are they disagreeing about? On the Protagorean view, perhaps nothing, which looks like a cost.
answer: Both claims are true, each relative to its speaker; no one is mistaken, which is both the doctrine's attraction and a cost, since it seems to leave nothing to disagree about.

@mistakes
- Treating the Sophists as a single school or as people who all denied the existence of truth. They differed, and the sources are mostly their critics.
- Saying that atomism is the view that matter is infinitely divisible. It is the view that some bodies are indivisible.
- Saying that the atomists denied that void exists. They affirmed it, in answer to Parmenides.
- Saying that Plato's argument proves Protagoras false in every case. It gives a reason to doubt that the thesis can be both relative and meant universally, and defenders reply.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the contrast between nomos and physis and give one way that a Sophist could use it to criticize an inherited rule, and one reason a critic might resist that use.
hint: Think about a rule that is a custom of one city and ask whether it is rooted in nature.
solution: Nomos is law, custom or convention, which varies from city to city; physis is nature, which is the same everywhere. A Sophist could argue that a rule such as a particular city's dress code is merely nomos, since other cities have different rules, and that it therefore has no authority beyond agreement and enforcement. A critic might resist by saying that the fact that customs vary does not show that none is better than another: variation in beliefs about the shape of the earth does not make the shape a matter of convention. Some rules, such as a ban on needless cruelty, might be grounded in human nature even though cities differ in how they apply them.

@quiz self
prompt: State the best reply a Protagorean could give to Plato's self-refutation argument, and say whether it succeeds in your judgment, with a reason.
hint: Ask whether the thesis can be true for everyone while denying that anything is true for everyone.
solution: The Protagorean can say that the thesis is relativized like every other claim: it is true for those who accept it and false for those who reject it, so the fact that many people think it false refutes it only for them. I think this reply avoids the charge of formal inconsistency, since "true for me" and "false for you" can both hold. But it has costs: the relativist can no longer say that those who reject the thesis are mistaken, and Protagoras, who taught for fees, seems to claim a wisdom his thesis cannot support. So the reply saves consistency at the price of making the thesis idle as a thesis about how things are. A defender could answer that the thesis is a proposal about how to understand truth, not a claim to be argued against opponents.
