@article
After the conquests of Alexander the Great in the fourth century BCE, the small Greek city-states lost much of their independence, and philosophy turned to a personal question: how can one live well and be undisturbed in an uncertain world? Three schools in Athens and elsewhere gave answers that lasted for centuries. They shared a conviction that philosophy is a kind of therapy for the soul, and they disagreed about the cure.

### The Epicureans

**Epicurus** (341–270 BCE) taught in his school, the Garden. He was a materialist who adopted and modified atomism (Week 3): everything is atoms and void, including the soul. The swerve, a small random deviation in atoms' paths, is reported by Lucretius (On the Nature of Things, Book II) and Cicero (for example in On Fate); none of Epicurus's surviving writings mentions it. Lucretius gives it two roles: to start collisions among atoms that would otherwise fall in parallel, and to break the chain of fated causes, which later writers connect with free action. In ethics he was a **hedonist**: pleasure is the highest good. But the greatest pleasure is the absence of pain in the body (*aponia*) and of disturbance in the soul (*ataraxia*), not a life of luxury. He sorted desires into natural and necessary (food, shelter), natural but unnecessary (luxurious food), and empty (fame, unlimited wealth), and advised satisfying chiefly the first. A later summary of his teaching, the "fourfold cure", says: do not fear the gods, do not worry about death, what is good is easy to get, and what is terrible is easy to endure. Friendship is the greatest means to a secure life.

His argument about death is set out in the *Letter to Menoeceus*:

| P1 | Death is the end of sensation: the soul is atoms that disperse at death. |
| P2 | Good and bad consist in sensation: nothing is good or bad for someone unless they can feel it. |
| C1 | So death, which is the absence of sensation, is neither good nor bad for the person who dies. |
| P3 | What is not bad when present is not rightly feared in advance. |
| C | So the fear of death is irrational. |

The slogan is that where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not. The Roman Epicurean **Lucretius** adds a symmetry argument: we do not regret the endless time before our birth, so we should not regret the time after death. The best objection, associated with Thomas Nagel's 1970 paper "Death", targets P2: death is bad because it **deprives** us of the goods that continued life would have given, and a thing can be bad for us without our experiencing it, as when we are betrayed behind our backs. Epicureans reply that deprivation requires a subject to be deprived, and after death there is none. Deprivation theorists meet the symmetry argument by denying the symmetry: Nagel observes that one could not have been born much earlier than one was, so prenatal nonexistence takes nothing away, whereas death takes away future life one would otherwise have had; others add that our concern is naturally directed toward the future.

### The Stoics

**Zeno of Citium** (c. 334–262 BCE) founded the Stoa, named after the painted colonnade in Athens where he taught; **Cleanthes** and **Chrysippus** developed it, and later Roman Stoics include **Epictetus** and **Marcus Aurelius**. The Stoics held that the cosmos is a rationally ordered whole, governed by *logos*, and that a good life is one lived in agreement with nature. Virtue, which is wisdom exercised in action, is the **only good**, and vice the only evil; health, wealth and reputation are **indifferents**, though some are **preferred** and some dispreferred. The passions, such as fear and anger, are on their view mistaken judgments that something indifferent is good or evil, and the wise person is free of them, while having rational "good feelings" such as joy. Epictetus begins his handbook with the distinction between what is **up to us** (our judgments, impulses, desires and aversions) and what is not (our bodies, property and reputations). Death is an indifferent: not an evil, since it is not a vice, and the fear of it rests on a false judgment. Marcus Aurelius presents death as a natural process of change.

### The Skeptics

**Pyrrhonian** skepticism, whose main source is Sextus Empiricus, holds that for any argument there is an opposing argument of equal strength; the skeptic then **suspends judgment** (*epoche*), and finds that tranquility follows. The **Agrippan modes**, which Sextus reports, give a trilemma against justifying any claim:

| P1 | If a claim is adequately justified, it is justified by an infinite series of reasons, by a circle of reasons, or by an unjustified starting point. |
| P2 | None of these three provides adequate justification: an infinite series never completes, a circle assumes what it is meant to prove, and an unjustified starting point is arbitrary. |
| C | So no claim is adequately justified. |

Sextus uses these modes dialectically, against anyone who claims to know; the Pyrrhonist does not assert the conclusion as a dogma but, finding the arguments for and against equally persuasive, suspends judgment.

The objection that the skeptic cannot act is answered by Sextus: he lives by appearances, following nature's guidance, the compulsion of feelings, the customs of his city and the teaching of crafts, without asserting that things are so. The **Academic** skeptics, **Arcesilaus** and **Carneades**, argued in Plato's Academy; Carneades proposed that we guide action by what is persuasive without claiming knowledge.

@example
title: Testing the Epicurean argument on death
problem: Reconstruct the argument that death is nothing to us, and say which premise a critic who thinks death can be bad must reject.
step: State the premises: death is the end of sensation; good and bad consist in sensation; so death is neither good nor bad for the person who dies; what is not bad when present is not to be feared; so the fear of death is irrational.
step: Check validity: C1 follows from P1 and P2 if "death is the absence of sensation" counts as not involving good or bad sensation; the last step follows from C1 and P3.
step: The critic who holds that death can be bad, such as a deprivation theorist, rejects P2: something can be bad for a person without being felt, as when a person is deprived of future goods.
step: The Epicurean can reply that deprivation presupposes someone to be deprived, and that after death there is no one; the critic answers that the person who would have lived is the one deprived, even though the loss is not experienced.
answer: The critic rejects the premise that good and bad consist in sensation, which is the premise on which the argument stands or falls.

@example
title: Sorting what is up to us
problem: Using Epictetus's distinction, classify (a) your judgment about whether a remark was an insult, (b) whether your colleagues respect you, (c) whether you will fall ill next winter, and (d) how hard you prepare for an exam.
step: Epictetus says the things up to us are our judgments, impulses, desires and aversions: in short, our own acts of mind and will.
step: (a) A judgment about a remark is up to us. (d) How hard we prepare is an act of our own will, so it is also up to us, though whether we pass is not.
step: (b) Others' respect depends on their attitudes, so it is not up to us. (c) Illness is a matter of the body, which is not up to us.
step: The Stoic advice follows: care about the things that are up to us, and treat the others as indifferent in the sense of not being what makes a life good.
answer: (a) and (d) are up to us; (b) and (c) are not.

@mistakes
- Equating Epicurean hedonism with indulgence. Epicurus's highest pleasure is tranquility and freedom from pain, and he recommends simple living.
- Saying that Stoics are unfeeling. They aim to remove passions that rest on false judgments, and they recommend good feelings and attachment to family and community.
- Saying that the Skeptic claims that nothing can be known. The Pyrrhonian suspends judgment even on that claim.
- Attributing the death argument to the Stoics. Epicurus's argument rests on annihilation and on sensation as the measure of good, while the Stoic one rests on virtue as the only good.

prompt: Compare the Epicurean and Stoic views of death. State what each says about whether death is bad, what supports each view, one point of agreement, and one objection that applies to one or both.
hint: Epicurus uses annihilation and sensation; the Stoics use virtue as the only good.
solution: The Epicurean says that death is nothing to us: it is the end of sensation, and since good and bad lie in sensation, death is neither, so the fear of it is irrational. The Stoic says that death is an indifferent: since virtue is the only good and vice the only evil, death, which is neither, is not an evil, and the fear of it rests on the false judgment that it is. They agree that the fear of death rests on a mistake and that philosophy can free us from it, and both aim at tranquility. They differ in their reasons: the Epicurean depends on a materialist view of the soul and on hedonism, while the Stoic depends on the claim that only virtue is good and on a view of the cosmos as rationally ordered. An objection to both is that they seem to dismiss the intuition that dying early is a loss, which a deprivation theorist presses; the Stoic must also defend the claim that health and life are not goods at all.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the Agrippan trilemma: state it, then give the best reply that a non-skeptic could make to it, and say what the reply gives up.
hint: Consider foundationalist and coherentist responses to the three options.
solution: Trilemma: P1: If a claim is adequately justified, it is justified by an infinite series of reasons, a circle or an unjustified starting point. P2: None of these provides adequate justification. C: So no claim is adequately justified. A foundationalist replies by rejecting P2's treatment of the third option: some claims, such as basic perceptual or self-evident claims, can justify others without needing support from further claims, so the starting point is not arbitrary. This gives up the demand that every justification be supported by a further reason, and the skeptic can ask why the supposed basic claims are trustworthy. A coherentist instead accepts the circle as a web of mutually supporting claims, and gives up the idea that justification flows in one direction from basic claims. Neither reply convinces everyone, and the trilemma remains a standard statement of the regress problem.
