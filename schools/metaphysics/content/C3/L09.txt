@article
A sculptor melts down a bronze statue. The bronze is still there, and the statue is gone. If the statue were simply the bronze, it could not have ceased to exist while the bronze remained; yet the statue is not something over and above the bronze, like an extra ingredient. What is a statue, then, and how does anything come to be or cease to be without coming from nothing, as Parmenides's argument forbids? Aristotle's answer is **hylomorphism** (from *hyle*, matter, and *morphe*, form): ordinary substances are composites of matter and form.

### Matter, form and privation

In Physics I.7, Aristotle analyzes every change into three principles. There is an underlying **subject** that persists, a **form** it gains, and a **privation**, the lack of that form before the change. When an unmusical man becomes musical, the subject is the man, the form is being musical, and the privation is being unmusical. What comes to be does not come from what is not at all, as Parmenides assumed, but from what is not yet F, and "not yet F" is true of something that exists. In this way he rejects the premise in Week 2's reconstruction that every coming to be is from nothing.

He distinguishes two kinds of change. In **accidental change** a substance persists and gains or loses a property, as when a pale man tans or water is heated. In **substantial change** a substance comes to be or passes away: a log is burned, and the log ceases to be while its matter, now ash and smoke, persists in a new form. **Matter** is what can take on a form, and so is potentiality; **form** is what makes a thing the kind of thing it is, and so is actuality (a theme taken up in Week 10).

### What is substance?

In Metaphysics Z, Aristotle asks what substance, the primary kind of being, is. He considers four candidates: the essence (what it is for a thing to be what it is), the universal, the genus and the **substratum**, the ultimate subject of which everything else is predicated. In Z.3 he tests the substratum. If substance is the ultimate subject, then matter should qualify: strip away every attribute and all that remains is matter. But he thinks this is the wrong result. A reconstruction of the argument:

| P1 | If substance is whatever is the ultimate subject of predication, then matter is substance. |
| P2 | If something is a substance, it is separable and a determinate "this something". |
| P3 | Matter by itself is neither separable nor a determinate "this". |
| C1 | So matter by itself is not a substance. |
| C | So substance is not simply whatever is the ultimate subject of predication: the criterion of being the ultimate subject is not enough. |

Aristotle goes on to say in Z.3 that form seems to be substance more than matter or the composite is, though the argument above does not by itself establish that. It is a matter of continuing interpretation how he settles between form and the composite in Z, H and Θ.

In Z.13, he gives several arguments that **no universal is a substance**. A reconstruction of the first:

| P1 | The substance of a thing is peculiar to it and does not belong to anything else. |
| P2 | A universal is, by nature, common: it belongs to many things. |
| C | So no universal is the substance of anything. |

This creates a puzzle, because the form of a kind, such as the form of man, looks like a universal. Interpreters disagree about whether Aristotle's forms are universal or particular (this man's form), and how Z.13 and the claim that form is substance fit together.

### Against separate Forms

Aristotle criticizes Plato's theory that Forms exist apart from their instances (Metaphysics A.9; Z.13–16). One objection is the **Third Man argument**, which also appears in Plato's own *Parmenides* (132a–b), where Plato lets Parmenides raise it against the young Socrates. A standard modern reconstruction runs as follows:

| P1 | If a number of things are F, there is a Form of F by which they are all F (one over many). |
| P2 | The Form of F is itself F (self-predication). |
| P3 | If a thing is F by a Form of F, it is not identical with that Form (non-identity). |
| C | So there is a second Form of F, by which the Form of F and the many F things are F, and then a third, and so without end. |

The regress is thought to be vicious because the Forms are supposed to explain what the many things have in common, and the explanation is never completed. A defender of Forms can reject P2, saying that the Form of Large is not large in the way ordinary large things are; or P3, saying that the Form is the paradigm that is F by being F-ness itself; or P1, limiting which groups have Forms. Aristotle's own alternative is that forms are in the things and do not exist apart from them.

@example
title: Classifying changes with subject, form and privation
problem: Classify each change as accidental or substantial, and name the subject, the form gained and the privation. (a) A cold bowl of soup is warmed. (b) A log is burned to ash.
step: (a) The subject is the soup, which persists through the change. The privation is being cold; the form gained is being warm. The soup remains soup, so this is an accidental change.
step: (b) The log does not persist as a log. What persists is the matter, which first had the form of a log and now has the form of ash. The privation is the lack of the ash-form; the form gained is ash.
step: Check by asking what ceases to exist. In (a), nothing ceases to exist except a temperature state. In (b), the log ceases to exist. So (b) is a substantial change: a substance passes away.
step: In (b) the matter is the subject, and the substance (the log) is the composite that does not survive. This shows why Aristotle can say that something comes to be from what is not yet it without coming from nothing.
answer: (a) accidental: subject, the soup; form, warmth; privation, coldness. (b) substantial: subject, the matter; form gained, ash; privation, lack of ash-form; the log ceases to be.

@example
title: Locating a reply to the Third Man
problem: A defender of Forms says: "The Form of Large is not large in the way that large things are; it is Large-ness itself, which is not another large thing." Which premise of the Third Man argument does this reject, and why does that block the regress?
step: The reply says that the Form is not large in the way that ordinary things are. That denies self-predication, premise P2 in the standard reconstruction, at least on the reading where "large" is applied to the Form and to ordinary things in the same sense.
step: The regress needed P2 to put the Form in the same group as the many large things: the group of things that are large, which then needs a further Form.
step: Without P2, the many large things and the Form do not form a single group of large things, so no second Form is required and the regress does not start.
step: The cost: the defender must say in what way the Form is "large" or how it can serve as a paradigm for large things, and some of the Forms' roles in earlier dialogues seemed to rely on self-predication.
answer: The reply rejects self-predication (premise P2); it blocks the regress but at the cost of explaining how the Form is a paradigm.

@mistakes
- Saying that Aristotle thinks matter is the only thing that persists through all change. In accidental change the substance persists, and in substantial change the matter does.
- Treating "privation" as a positive thing. It is the lack of a form in something that can have it.
- Saying that Aristotle simply denies that there are universals. He holds that universals exist as predicated of things, but argues that no universal is a substance.
- Attributing the Third Man to Aristotle as its inventor. It is raised in Plato's Parmenides, and Aristotle gives his own version.

@practice self
prompt: A potter makes a lump of clay into a bowl and later crushes the bowl back into a lump. Describe each change using matter, form and privation, and say what a hylomorphist would say about whether the lump and the bowl are identical.
hint: Ask what persists through each change, and what ceases to exist when the bowl is crushed.
solution: In making the bowl, the clay is the subject and matter, the bowl-shape is the form gained, and the privation is the lack of that shape; the bowl comes to be, so this is a substantial change for the bowl. In crushing, the clay persists as matter, the bowl-form is lost, and the bowl ceases to exist. The lump and the bowl differ in what they survive: the clay survives crushing and the bowl does not, so by Leibniz's Law they are not identical. The hylomorphist says the bowl is a composite of clay and form, with the clay as the matter that constitutes it; the lump is not the bowl, but it is what the bowl is made of. A rival view holds that the bowl and the clay are the same thing described differently, and has to explain the difference in what they survive.
