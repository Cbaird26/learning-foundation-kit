## Week-by-week

**Week 1: Thales to Heraclitus.** The Milesians seek an *arche*: water (Thales), the unlimited *apeiron* (Anaximander), air with condensation and rarefaction (Anaximenes). The inference from "every change has something that persists" to "one thing persists through every change" is a quantifier shift. Heraclitus: flux, the unity of opposites, the logos; two readings of the river.

**Week 2: Parmenides and Zeno.** Parmenides argues that what is cannot come to be or change. Zeno's Dichotomy, Achilles and Arrow defend him. Replies: Aristotle's potential and actual infinity, the convergent series, the at-at theory of motion.

**Week 3: Atomists and Sophists.** Atoms and void answer Parmenides. Protagoras's relativism and Plato's self-refutation argument; Gorgias's three theses; nomos and physis.

**Week 4: Socrates.** The elenchus and "What is F?"; the Euthyphro argument, which uses Leibniz's Law; "no one does wrong willingly" and the akrasia objection.

**Week 5: Forms and the divided line.** Forms are intelligible, unchanging and purely F; the argument from relativity; the sun, the line and the cave.

**Week 6: Knowledge, recollection and the soul.** Meno's paradox, recollection, true belief and knowledge, the three parts of the soul.

**Week 7: Justice and the Republic.** Thrasymachus, Glaucon, the city-soul analogy, and objections to the argument that justice benefits its possessor.

**Week 8: Logic and the categories.** Ten categories; said of and present in; syllogistic moods and counter-models; non-contradiction.

**Week 9: Substance, form and matter.** Hylomorphism, privation, substantial and accidental change; Z.3 and Z.13; the Third Man regress.

**Week 10: Four causes, nature and the soul.** Material, formal, efficient and final causes; the argument for natural teleology; the soul as form.

**Week 11: Happiness and virtue.** Eudaimonia, the function argument, the mean, habituation and practical wisdom.

**Week 12: Hellenistic philosophy.** Epicurean hedonism and the argument on death; Stoic virtue and indifferents; Pyrrhonian suspension and the Agrippan trilemma.

## Key distinctions

- Validity versus soundness; invalid forms such as the quantifier shift and the undistributed middle.
- Accidental versus substantial change; first versus second actuality.
- Said of versus present in; primary versus secondary substance.
- Exegesis (what the text says) versus evaluation (whether it is right); a reading versus the text.

## Common errors

Quoting from memory; treating a report from Aristotle as a quotation; confusing a view with the objection to it; saying that an analogy is an identity (the sun and the Good); attributing Glaucon's or Thrasymachus's view to Plato.

@exam ms
prompt: Select the arguments that are invalid because they shift a quantifier.
choice*: Every river has a source; so there is a source of every river.
choice*: Every student has a teacher; so there is a teacher who teaches every student.
choice: There is a teacher who teaches every student; so every student has a teacher.
choice: All stars are bodies; all planets are stars; so all planets are bodies.
hint: Compare the order of "every" and "some" in the premise and the conclusion.
solution: The arguments about rivers and sources and about students and teachers move from "for every x there is a y" to "there is a y for every x", which does not follow. The argument from a teacher of every student to each student's having a teacher goes the other way, which is valid, and the argument about stars, planets and bodies is Barbara.

@exam self
prompt: In about 300 words, compare Plato's and Aristotle's answers to the question "what is most real?", state one objection that bears on one or both, and judge which answer handles it better, giving your reasons.
hint: Use the Forms and the divided line for Plato, the primary substances and form for Aristotle, and the Third Man regress or the unity of form and matter as an objection.
solution: Plato answers that the Forms are most real: they are unchanging, purely F and the objects of knowledge, and sensible things are less real because they are F only in a respect and change. Aristotle replies that the primary substances, individual things such as this man or this horse, are most basic, since everything else is said of them or present in them, and that in the account of substance in Metaphysics Z, form, or the composite of form and matter, is substance, while no universal is. They agree that form is central to explanation; they disagree about whether forms exist apart from particulars. The Third Man regress is an objection to Plato: if the Form of F is itself F and the many F things are F by it, then a further Form is needed for the Form and the many, and so on. A defender of Forms can deny self-predication, and Aristotle faces a parallel puzzle about whether forms are universal. On balance I judge that Aristotle's view handles the regress better, since it places forms in things and so needs no further Form to unite them, though it pays the price of a puzzle about the universality of form, and a Platonist who rejects self-predication keeps the explanatory role of Forms without the regress.
