@article
How can you search for something you do not already know? If you do know it, you have no need to search; if you do not, you would not recognize it if you found it. This puzzle, put to Socrates by Meno in the Meno (80d–e), is a challenge to the idea of inquiry itself, and Plato's response, **recollection**, is the foundation of his epistemology.

### Meno's paradox and the reply

| P1 | Either you know what you are looking for, or you do not. |
| P2 | If you know it, you cannot inquire into it, since there is nothing to find out. |
| P3 | If you do not know it, you cannot inquire into it, since you would not know what to look for or recognize it if you found it. |
| C | So you cannot inquire into anything. |

The argument is valid. Socrates answers by rejecting the dichotomy in P1: there is a third possibility, that we have knowledge we are not aware of. The soul, he says, is immortal and has learned everything before, so that what we call learning is **recollection** (*anamnesis*), prompted by questions. He demonstrates this (82b–85b) with a slave of Meno's household who has never studied geometry. Asked how to construct a square with twice the area of a given square with sides of 2 units, the slave says first that the side should be doubled, which would give a square of area 16, four times the original, not twice; then, that it should be 3, which gives 9. After being brought to see that he does not know, and with Socrates's questions, he sees that the square built on the **diagonal** of the original has twice the area: the original square has area 4 and the diagonal square has area 8, because it is made of four triangles, each of which is half of a copy of the original square (each has area 2).

How much does this show? An objector notes that Socrates asks leading questions, so the slave is being guided, not recalling. A defender replies that the slave comes to see the truth himself, which is the point. Even a critic might grant that the demonstration shows something real: we can come to know mathematical truths by reasoning, without observation. Whether this requires learning before birth is a further step, and in the Meno Socrates does not claim that the demonstration alone proves the doctrine of a previous life; he says only that he would stake much on the conclusion that we are better for believing we must inquire (86b–c).

### Knowledge and true belief

If true belief guides action as well as knowledge does, why prize knowledge? Socrates compares a guide who knows the road to Larissa with one who has only a true belief about it (97a–98a). Both lead you correctly, but true opinions, like the statues of Daedalus, run away unless tied down, and they are tied by **working out the reason** (*aitias logismos*): that is what makes them knowledge. In the *Theaetetus* the question of what knowledge is receives three proposals, each of which is found wanting: knowledge is **perception**; knowledge is **true judgment**; and knowledge is **true judgment with an account** (*logos*). The third is an ancestor of the modern "justified true belief" analysis, which Gettier's 1963 counterexamples challenged, though the dialogue ends in perplexity and gives no confident definition.

### The three parts of the soul

In Republic IV (435b–441c) Plato divides the soul into **reason**, **spirit** (*thumos*, the seat of anger and honor) and **appetite**. The argument rests on a principle of opposites:

| P1 | The same part of the soul cannot, at the same time and in the same respect and in relation to the same object, do or undergo opposite things. |
| P2 | Sometimes a person, at one time, desires to drink and also refuses to drink that very drink, and these are opposite attitudes in the same respect. |
| C | So these two cannot belong to the same part: one part desires and another part holds back. |

Plato adds that the part that holds back is **reason**, and that spirit is a third thing, because spirit can side with reason against appetite. The objection is that a single part might desire and refuse in different respects, which would deny P2's claim that the opposition is in a single respect. A defender replies that Plato requires opposition within one respect and object: the person wants to drink and does not want to drink, and so the two attitudes belong to different parts.

### The cave and the Forms

The cave (Week 5) fits these themes. The prisoners are like ordinary people who take images for realities. The freed prisoner's ascent is education, a turning around of the soul to see what is, rather than putting knowledge into an empty vessel (518b–d). The theory of recollection gives a reason that education can only direct the soul to what it, in some sense, already has the power to see.

@example
title: The slave-boy argument with new numbers
problem: Suppose the original square has sides of 3 units. Follow the stages of the slave boy's reasoning and find the side of a square with twice the area.
step: Original area: $3^2 = 9$. The target area is $2 \times 9 = 18$.
step: First guess, doubling the side to 6: the area would be $6^2 = 36$, which is four times 9, not twice.
step: Second guess, a side of 4.5 would give $20.25$, which is more than 18; a side of 4 gives 16, which is too little. So the answer lies between 4 and 4.5, and it is not a whole number or any fraction of the side (it is irrational).
step: The side of the square with area 18 is $\sqrt{18} = 3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24$, which is the length of the diagonal of the original 3-by-3 square, since the diagonal is $\sqrt{3^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{18}$.
answer: The square on the diagonal, with side $3\sqrt{2} \approx 4.24$, has twice the area; doubling the side gives four times the area.

@example
title: Applying the principle of opposites
problem: A student wants to read for the exam and also wants to go to a party. A critic says this does not show that the soul has parts. Use the argument for the three parts to state when it does and when it does not.
step: The principle says the same part cannot, in the same respect and about the same object, do opposite things. Wanting to read and wanting to party are about different objects, so on its face they do not violate the principle and need not belong to different parts.
step: But suppose the student has a strong urge to go out and also a judgment that she should not, so that she goes out at the same moment as she refuses to. Here the same object, going out, is both desired and refused.
step: In this second case, the principle requires two sources: one that is drawn to going and another that holds back, so there are two parts.
step: A critic can still reply that the student has one mind that is simply of two minds about the matter, and that "parts" is a metaphor. The argument is a good reason to distinguish sources of motivation only if the principle of opposites is accepted.
answer: The case supports parts only when the same object is both desired and refused; two wants for different things do not.

@mistakes
- Saying that the slave-boy demonstration proves the soul's pre-existence. It shows that he can reach a mathematical truth under questioning, and Plato presents pre-existence as a further claim.
- Thinking that the Theaetetus defends "justified true belief". It considers a proposal that resembles it, and ends in perplexity.
- Describing Plato's three parts as three persons or homunculi. Whether they are literally parts or aspects is debated.
- Confusing the claim that knowledge is more valuable with the claim that true belief is useless. In the Meno, true belief guides action well and knowledge is more stable.

prompt: Explain the allegory of the cave in terms of the theory of Forms. Say what the shadows, the objects carried before the fire, the world outside, the sun and the prisoner's ascent represent, and mention one way the mapping to the divided line is uncertain.
hint: Use the divided line: images, visible things, the intelligible, and the Good. The text says to apply the image to what was said before.
solution: The prisoners represent ordinary people who take what they see to be reality. The shadows on the wall stand for images, the lowest state of mind, imagination, and the objects carried before the fire stand for ordinary visible things, which are known only by belief. The fire stands for the sun of the visible world. The ascent out of the cave is the education that turns the soul toward what is intelligible: the world outside stands for the Forms, which are grasped by reason; and the sun there stands for the Form of the Good, the source of the Forms' intelligibility, as the sun is of visibility. Plato says (517b) that the image is to be applied to what has been said before, which supports reading the cave through the line. The uncertainty is how closely each stage of the cave maps onto a segment: for example, whether the shadows and reflections seen outside correspond to the mathematical objects of the third segment is a matter of interpretation.

prompt: Evaluate the argument for the tripartite soul: state it, then give the best objection and a reply.
hint: Look at the principle of opposites, and ask whether "the same respect" does the work.
solution: Argument: P1: The same part cannot, at the same time and in the same respect and about the same object, do or undergo opposite things. P2: A person may at one time both desire a drink and refuse that very drink, in the same respect. C: So desiring and refusing belong to different parts. The best objection targets P2's claim of a single respect: the person might desire the drink in one respect (it would quench thirst) and refuse it in another (it is poisoned), so one part could be involved in both, and no division is needed. The reply is that Plato's cases are chosen so that the same object is both wanted and refused, as when a thirsty person wants to drink and judges that she should not, and that what is refused is the same drink the same person desires; the best the objector can then say is that the soul is one thing with conflicting states, and this leaves it open whether the states are best described as coming from parts.
