@article
Ask why there is a house on the hill, and you can answer in four ways. Because it is made of stone and timber. Because it has the shape of a dwelling, with walls, a roof and rooms. Because a builder built it. Because people needed shelter. Aristotle thinks all four answers are correct and different, and that a complete explanation of anything needs them all.

### The four causes

The Greek word is *aitia*, better translated "explanation" or "the because" than "cause" in the modern sense. In Physics II.3 (and Metaphysics A.3), Aristotle distinguishes four.

| Cause | Answers | Aristotle's example |
|---|---|---|
| material | what is it made of? | the bronze of a statue |
| formal | what is it, what is its form or definition? | for the octave, the ratio 2:1 |
| efficient | what started the change? | the father of a child |
| final | what is it for? | health, as the reason for taking a walk |

The same thing may have several causes of different kinds; a single thing can also be a cause of different kinds (a sculptor is the efficient cause of the statue). He also considers **chance** and **spontaneity** (Physics II.4–6): a man goes to the market for another reason and happens to meet someone who owes him money. The meeting has efficient causes, but is not done for the sake of collecting the debt, so it is by coincidence. Many later philosophers keep only efficient causation as "cause", which is why the modern conception of causation (taken up in other courses) differs from Aristotle's.

### Nature

Aristotle defines **nature** as an internal principle of change and rest (Physics II.1, 192b21–23). A natural thing (an animal, a plant, a stone, fire) has within it a source of change in the way it grows, moves or rests. An artifact has no such principle in so far as it is an artifact, though it has one in so far as it is made of a natural material. His example is a bed: if you buried a bed and it sprouted, what would grow would be wood, not a bed. The wood's tendency to grow is natural; its being shaped as a bed comes from the craftsman.

### Teleology: do natural things act for an end?

In Physics II.8, Aristotle argues that nature acts for the sake of something. A reconstruction:

| P1 | Natural processes occur always, or for the most part, in the same way (an acorn grows into an oak). |
| P2 | What happens always or for the most part is not by chance or coincidence. |
| P3 | What is not by chance happens for the sake of something. |
| C | So natural processes happen for the sake of something. |

He considers the rival view of Empedocles, who suggested that natural structures arose by chance and that only those that happened to be well fitted survived, and rejects it by appeal to P1: chance cannot give regular results. The ancient argument has a modern descendant in the debate over natural selection. The usual objection is that selection explains the regular, well-fitted features of organisms without final causes, since fitness results from heritable variation and differential survival, which is exactly the kind of alternative Empedocles proposed. Philosophers disagree about what follows. Some hold that selection eliminates final causes from biology, others that function statements such as "the heart is for pumping blood" are teleological and are explained by selection history, and neo-Aristotelians defend a more robust role for ends in living things. The argument's bridge, P3, is the premise most open to question.

### The soul

In De Anima II.1 (412a27–28), Aristotle defines the **soul** as the first actuality of a natural body that has life potentially. "First actuality" means having a capacity (as someone who knows French has it while asleep), as opposed to exercising it (speaking French), which is second actuality. The soul is not a thing in the body, but the form of the living body: the set of its capacities for nutrition, growth and reproduction, perception, movement and thought. His analogies are the axe, whose "soul", if it were a natural body, would be its capacity to cut, and the eye, whose soul is sight (412b10–413a3). There is no more need to ask whether soul and body are one than whether the wax and its shape are one (412b6–9).

He distinguishes grades of soul: the **nutritive**, common to all living things; the **perceptive**, found in animals; and the **rational**, which, among known animals, belongs to human beings. This contrasts with Plato's Phaedo, where the soul is a separable, immortal thing temporarily joined to the body. Aristotle treats **intellect** (*nous*) as possibly different (De Anima II.2, 413b24–27; III.5), and what he says about an "active intellect" is among the most disputed passages in his work; interpreters disagree about whether any part of the human soul survives death.

@example
title: Four causes of a violin
problem: Give the four causes of a particular violin and say which is the final cause.
step: The material cause is the wood, glue and strings from which it is made.
step: The formal cause is its form: the structure and shape that make it a violin, the arrangement of body, neck and strings that defines what it is to be a violin.
step: The efficient cause is the violin-maker, who put the materials into that form.
step: The final cause is the end for the sake of which it exists: to be played, making music. The final cause explains why the other three are as they are, since the shape was chosen to suit this end.
answer: Material, wood and strings; formal, the violin structure; efficient, the maker; final, to be played to make music.

@example
title: First and second actuality
problem: Marcus knows how to swim, but is asleep in bed. Classify his swimming ability and his swimming now, and say what this shows about the definition of soul.
step: The ability to swim, which he has even when not swimming, is a first actuality: a developed capacity.
step: Swimming at this moment would be second actuality, the exercise of the capacity. Marcus is not in that state.
step: Aristotle's definition of the soul uses first actuality, because a sleeping person is still alive and has the capacities of a living body even though he is not exercising them.
step: If the soul were only second actuality, a sleeping person would have no soul, which is absurd; the definition avoids that result.
answer: His ability is a first actuality and his present swimming would be second actuality; the soul is first actuality, so it belongs to the sleeper too.

@mistakes
- Reading "final cause" as meaning that something in the future causes the present: the end explains the process by being what it is for; it does not act backward in time.
- Treating the four causes as four efficient causes. Only one is efficient; the others are different kinds of explanation.
- Taking Aristotle's soul to be a ghost in a machine. It is the form of the living body, not a separate component.
- Saying that Aristotle simply rejects chance. He holds that chance exists but that regular outcomes are not by chance.

prompt: Apply Aristotle's four causes to three examples: a clay bowl, an oak growing from an acorn, and a city's building a defensive wall. For each, give all four causes, and note one case in which the final cause is more contestable than the others.
hint: Ask for each: what is it made of, what form does it have, what started it, and what is it for?
solution: Bowl: material, clay; formal, the shape and structure that make it a bowl; efficient, the potter; final, to hold food or drink. Oak: material, the acorn's matter and what it takes up from soil and water; formal, the form of an oak, which the mature tree realizes; efficient, the parent oak that produced the acorn (Aristotle says that a man begets a man); final, the mature, flourishing oak, or the continuation of the species. Wall: material, stone and mortar; formal, the design of the wall; efficient, the builders and the council that commissioned it; final, the defense of the city. The final cause is most contestable for the oak: a bowl and a wall have ends assigned by makers, but whether natural growth is for an end is exactly what the debate over natural teleology concerns, and a modern biologist might explain the oak's development without ends.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the argument from regularity to natural teleology: state the strongest objection and say what a defender of Aristotle could reply.
hint: Ask whether a regular outcome without ends could be explained in another way, such as by selection, and whether P3 is the weak premise.
solution: The strongest objection is that the argument's third premise, that whatever is not by chance is for the sake of something, is unsupported: regularity can be explained by laws of nature and by heritable variation with differential survival, without ends. The objector adds that natural selection is the sort of alternative that Empedocles gestured at, and that Aristotle's rejection by appeal to regularity does not touch a theory in which regularity comes from the stable operation of selection. A defender can reply in two ways. One says that function ascriptions in biology are teleological, and selection history explains why a trait has the function it has, so ends are not eliminated; the other, more Aristotelian, says that the regular development of an organism toward its mature form still requires an internal directedness that mechanism does not capture. Neither reply settles the question, and the first concedes more to the objector.
