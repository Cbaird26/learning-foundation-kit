@article
Last week ended with a puzzle. If every change has something that persists, what persists when a thing comes to be from nothing at all? Parmenides of Elea, writing in the early fifth century BCE, pressed this puzzle so hard that he concluded that change is an illusion. His follower Zeno of Elea then argued that motion, plurality and change lead to absurdity. These are among the first sustained arguments in metaphysics, and every theory of change since, Aristotle's included, can be read as a response to them.

### Parmenides: the two ways

Parmenides's poem is narrated by a goddess who sets out two "ways" of inquiry. The **way of truth** says that what **is** is, and what is not is not. The way that holds that what is not exists, or must be, is called unthinkable: you cannot know or point to what is not. The poem also gives a **way of opinion**, an account of the world of appearances that it presents as deceptive, in which mortals wander because they treat being and not-being as the same and not the same.

From this, the goddess draws conclusions about what is. It is not generated and not destroyed. If it came to be, it would have to come from what is not, and what is not is nothing at all, and there is no reason why it should have begun to be at one time rather than another. It is whole and of one kind, so it is not divisible; it does not move or change; and it is complete, like the mass of a well-rounded sphere. A common reconstruction runs as follows.

| P1 | Only what is can be thought of or spoken of; what is not cannot be. |
| P2 | If something came to be, it would come from what is not; and if something perished, it would pass into what is not. |
| P3 | Nothing can come from what is not or pass into what is not. |
| C1 | So nothing comes to be and nothing perishes. |
| P4 | Change is coming to be and perishing: the coming to be of a new feature and the passing away of an old one. |
| C | So there is no change. |

The argument is valid if P4 is read strictly (premise P1 is the ground offered for P3, and the further conclusions that what is, is one, whole and unchanging are drawn from the poem's other arguments, not from this one). Its weak points are P2 and P4: they treat every change as an emergence from nothing at all. Aristotle's reply, in Week 9, is that a change is the emergence of one feature from the absence of that feature in a thing that continues to exist; the thing that persists is not nothing. Interpreters also disagree about what "is" means in the poem: whether it is existence, predication ("is F") or something like truth, and whether Parmenides is a strict **monist** who holds that only one thing exists.

### Zeno's defense

According to Plato's *Parmenides*, Zeno wrote his arguments to defend Parmenides: if believing in many things and in motion leads to consequences more absurd than the consequences of denying them, the defender of Parmenides has reason to deny them. Zeno's own book is lost. Our main report of the paradoxes of motion comes from Aristotle's *Physics* (VI.9, with the discussion of divisibility in VI.2), and Aristotle names four.

**The Dichotomy.** Before the runner can reach the end of a course, she must reach the half-way point; before that, the quarter-way point; and so on without end.

| P1 | To complete a course, a runner must first complete the first half of it, then half of what remains, and so on without end. |
| P2 | That is an infinite series of tasks. |
| P3 | No one can complete an infinite series of tasks in a finite time. |
| P4 | If anyone moves across a distance, she completes it in a finite time. |
| C | So no one moves across a distance. Motion is impossible. |

**Achilles and the tortoise.** The faster runner can never overtake the slower one who starts ahead, because he must first reach the point where the slower one started, by which time the slower has moved on, and so on.

**The Arrow.** At any instant, a flying arrow occupies a space exactly equal to its own size. What occupies a space equal to itself is at rest. A moment of time is an instant, and the whole of time is made of instants. So the arrow is at rest at every instant of its flight, and so it never moves. The argument assumes that time is made of indivisible instants and that being in motion at an instant means traveling during it.

### Replies

Aristotle (Physics VI.2, 9; VIII.8) distinguished a **potential** infinity, something that can always be divided further, from an **actual** infinity of completed parts. A line is divisible without end, but it is not made up of an actual infinity of parts that have to be traversed one after another. Time is divisible in the same way, so a finite stretch of time can be divided just as the distance is. That blocks P3's use in the Dichotomy: there is no infinite series of separate tasks, only a continuous motion that could be divided as one chose.

The modern reply uses mathematics that Zeno lacked. The distances $\tfrac12, \tfrac14, \tfrac18, \dots$ form a convergent series that sums to 1, and the times needed for them sum in the same way to a finite total. So P3 is false if it says that an infinite series of ever shorter tasks must take infinite time. Some philosophers reply that this answers a mathematical question, not the philosophical one of whether an infinite series of tasks can be completed one after another; this debate continues. For the Arrow, the standard modern reply, associated with Bertrand Russell's "at-at" theory of motion, says that to move is to be at different places at different times; there is no property of "being in motion at an instant". Aristotle replied by denying that time is made of indivisible instants.

@example
title: Reconstructing Achilles and the tortoise with numbers
problem: Achilles runs at 10 meters per second and the tortoise at 1 meter per second, and the tortoise starts 100 meters ahead. Set out the paradox, then show where in the series he catches it.
step: Zeno's argument: when Achilles reaches the tortoise's starting point, it has moved ahead; when he reaches that point, it has moved again; the gaps never reach zero, so he never catches it.
step: Compute the gaps. Achilles covers 100 m in 10 s, in which time the tortoise covers 10 m. The next gap is 10 m, which takes him 1 s while the tortoise covers 1 m. The gaps are 100, 10, 1, 0.1 and so on, each one tenth of the previous.
step: Add the series. The total distance Achilles runs before catching the tortoise is $100 + 10 + 1 + 0.1 + \cdots = 100/(1 - 0.1) = 111.\overline{1}$ meters. The total time is $111.\overline{1}/10 = 11.\overline{1}$ seconds. Check with relative speed: the gap closes at 9 meters per second, so $100/9 = 11.\overline{1}$ seconds.
step: So Achilles passes the tortoise after about 11.1 seconds, though the argument breaks his journey into infinitely many stages. What the argument needs is the premise that infinitely many stages must take an infinite time, and the convergent sum shows that premise is false.
answer: He catches the tortoise after $100/9 \approx 11.1$ seconds, having run about 111.1 meters; the paradox fails because infinitely many ever shorter stages can add up to a finite time.

@example
title: Reconstructing Parmenides and locating Aristotle's reply
problem: A learner says: "Aristotle just ignores Parmenides." Use the standard-form reconstruction above to show where Aristotle's reply targets the argument, and state what the reply gives up.
step: The argument's key moves are P2 ("if something came to be, it would come from what is not") and P3 ("nothing can come from what is not"). Aristotle accepts P3, understood as: nothing comes to be from what is absolutely nothing.
step: He denies P2. When a man becomes musical, he comes from what is not musical, but he is not nothing: there is a persisting subject, the man, who lacks the feature and then gains it. So what comes to be comes from a thing that is, together with the lack of a feature.
step: Check that the reply does not give up the argument's logic. The reconstruction is valid, so the reply must reject a premise, and it rejects P2 and the strict reading of P4.
step: What the reply gives up is the claim that "is not" has only one sense. It distinguishes "is not at all" from "is not yet F".
answer: Aristotle accepts that nothing comes from nothing and denies that every change is a coming-to-be from what is not at all; he does so by distinguishing senses of "is not".

@mistakes
- Saying that Zeno or Parmenides doubted that things appear to move. They argued that the appearances cannot be reality; they do not deny the experience.
- Saying the convergent series is a complete answer to Zeno. It answers the mathematical claim that infinitely many shrinking stages must take infinite time; whether a completed infinity of tasks is possible is a further philosophical question.
- Reading "what is not cannot be" as a claim about unicorns. In Parmenides, "is not" is an absolute notion with debated meaning, and not a claim about particular nonexistent things.
- Treating Aristotle's report of a paradox as Zeno's exact words.
