@article
Suppose you could do whatever you liked without ever being caught: take what you wanted, break promises, harm rivals. Would you still have a reason to be just? If the only reason to be just is the fear of punishment or the loss of reputation, then justice is a cost we pay for protection. The Republic is Plato's long answer to that suspicion, and its question is both political (what is a just city?) and personal (what is justice in a person, and does it make a life go better?).

### The challenge

In Book I, **Thrasymachus** says that justice is the advantage of the stronger. In every city, he says, the ruling group makes laws in its own interest, and calls obeying them just; so justice is whatever benefits the rulers, and injustice on a large scale pays better than justice. Socrates replies by appeal to **craft**: a doctor as a doctor, or a pilot as a pilot, aims at the good of the patient or the crew and not at his own. A ruler in the strict sense, a craftsman of ruling, likewise aims at the advantage of the ruled. Socrates also points out that rulers sometimes err about their own interest, so that obeying them can harm them; Thrasymachus replies that a ruler in the strict sense does not err, as no craftsman does qua craftsman. He then objects that a shepherd fattens his sheep for the sake of his own profit, not theirs.

The dialogue then restates the challenge in a sharper form. In Book II, **Glaucon**, who says he will argue for the common view only to hear it refuted, asks where justice belongs among three kinds of good: goods we want for their own sake, goods we want for their results, and goods we want for both. Socrates says justice belongs in the highest class; Glaucon says most people put it in the second, as a burden that is endured for its rewards. The popular view, which Glaucon presents, holds that justice is a compromise between doing wrong without being punished, which is best, and suffering wrong without being able to retaliate, which is worst. The **Ring of Gyges**, which makes its wearer invisible, is a thought experiment about this: with such a ring, a person who is just and a person who is unjust would act alike. Glaucon asks Socrates to show that justice is good in itself, even if the just person were thought unjust and suffered for it.

### The city and the soul

Socrates answers by first "writing large": he builds a city in speech to see justice in it, then looks for it in the individual. The city has three classes: **rulers** (guardians), who have wisdom; **auxiliaries** (soldiers), who have courage; and **producers**, craftsmen and farmers. The four cardinal virtues appear as follows (Book IV): wisdom is the rulers' knowledge of what is good for the whole city; courage is the auxiliaries' preservation of right belief about what is to be feared; moderation is the agreement of all classes on who should rule; and **justice** is each class doing its own work without meddling in the others'.

The soul has three parts too (Week 6): reason, spirit and appetite. A just person is one in whom each part does its own work: reason rules, with the help of spirit, and appetite is kept in its place. Plato compares this inner order to **health**, and injustice to disease.

| P1 | Justice in the soul is a condition in which each part does its own work, with reason ruling and spirit as its ally. |
| P2 | Such a condition is the health of the soul, as bodily health is the proper ordering of the body. |
| P3 | Health is good for its possessor, whatever its further rewards. |
| C | So justice is good for its possessor. |

Plato continues in Books VIII and IX with the decline of regimes from aristocracy through timocracy, oligarchy and democracy to tyranny, and the corresponding souls, and gives three arguments in Book IX that the just life is the most pleasant. In the middle books, he argues that cities will not be just until philosophers rule or rulers philosophize (473c–d), which ties the political proposal to the theory of Forms and the cave. Among the Republic's proposals are ones many readers find troubling: strict censorship of poetry, communal living for the guardians, and rule by an educated few. Plato also holds, unusually for his time, that women can be guardians and rulers if they have the right nature and education (Book V).

### The best objection

The argument has two famous weak points. First, the **city-soul analogy**: Plato assumes that the individual is just in the same way as the city, and that the soul has the same three-part structure. Why should the soul's structure match the city's? Second, an objection associated with David Sachs's 1963 paper "A Fallacy in Plato's Republic": Plato set out to show that justice in the ordinary sense, not cheating, stealing or betraying, is good for the agent; but his argument shows only that an inner harmony is good for the agent. It has not been shown that a person with a harmonious soul will not steal, or that those who do steal have disordered souls. Plato's reply (442d–443b) is that a person with a harmonious soul would not embezzle, betray a friend or break an oath, because these acts come from a part of the soul that has escaped reason's rule. Critics reply that this claim is asserted, not shown, and that it needs an argument.

@example
title: Socrates's reply to Thrasymachus in standard form
problem: Reconstruct Thrasymachus's definition and Socrates's craft-based reply as two arguments, and say where they clash.
step: Thrasymachus: P1, justice is obeying the laws; P2, in every city the laws are made by the rulers for their own advantage; C, so justice is what is to the advantage of the stronger (the rulers).
step: Socrates: P1, a ruler in the strict sense is a craftsman of ruling; P2, every craft aims at the good of its subject and not of the craftsman as such; C, so a ruler in the strict sense aims at the good of the ruled.
step: The clash is over P2 of Thrasymachus's argument, that rulers make laws for their own advantage: Socrates's conclusion says that rulers qua rulers aim at the advantage of the ruled.
step: Thrasymachus can reply that rulers as actually found are not craftsmen in the strict sense but profit-makers, like the shepherd who fattens his flock for the market. Then the dispute is about whether Socrates's "strict sense" describes real rulers.
answer: The two arguments clash over what rulers aim at; Thrasymachus describes actual rulers, Socrates describes rulers in the strict sense of the craft, and neither argument settles which description is the relevant one.

@example
title: Diagnosing a disordered soul
problem: Mina knows that she should rest before a hard exam but eats sweets all night. Use the three-part soul to describe her state in Plato's terms and say whether she is just.
step: Identify the parts: her reason judges that she should rest; her appetite pushes her to eat.
step: Identify who rules: appetite wins, so each part is not doing its own work, and reason is not ruling. Spirit, which should be reason's ally, is either too weak to help or is being used by appetite.
step: Apply the definition: justice in the soul is each part doing its own work with reason ruling. Mina's soul is out of order, so, on Plato's account, it is not just at that moment.
step: Qualify the verdict: a single lapse does not settle her character, and Plato's account concerns the settled order of the soul, as health concerns the settled condition of the body.
answer: Appetite is ruling reason, so by Plato's definition her soul is out of order at that time and not just; a settled condition would be a matter of character, not one night.

@mistakes
- Attributing Glaucon's popular view to Plato. Glaucon states it so that Socrates can answer it.
- Saying that justice in the Republic is simply obedience to the laws. It is each part, of the city and the soul, doing its own work.
- Saying that Plato proves that justice pays by showing it is rewarded. The challenge was to show that justice is good in itself.
- Treating the Republic's political proposals as simple recommendations. They are part of an image of the soul, and readers disagree about how literally they were meant.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how Socrates uses the analogy of crafts against Thrasymachus, and say whether Thrasymachus's shepherd example is a good reply.
hint: Ask what a shepherd as a shepherd aims at, and whether Thrasymachus is describing the craft or the person who practices it.
solution: Socrates argues that every craft aims at the good of what it is a craft over: medicine at the patient's health, navigation at the crew's safety. A ruler, in so far as he is a ruler, has a craft over the city, so aims at its good and not his own. Thrasymachus replies that the shepherd fattens the sheep for the sake of his own profit or the master's. This is a good reply if it shows that practitioners aim at their own advantage, but Socrates can answer that the profit is gained by a separate craft, the wage-earning craft, which accompanies every craft, and that the shepherd's craft, as such, aims at the flock's good. A critic could say that this makes Socrates's claim true by definition: it describes the ideal ruler, not the actual ones. So the reply is partly successful: it shows that Thrasymachus's definition does not describe rulers as such, but it leaves open how far actual rulers resemble them.
