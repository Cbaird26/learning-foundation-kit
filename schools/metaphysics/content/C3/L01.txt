@article
A log burns and becomes ash, smoke and heat. A seed becomes a tree. A glacier becomes a river. In each case something is gone and something new has appeared, and yet we are inclined to say that something persisted: the stuff, perhaps, in a different form. The first Greek philosophers, working in the sixth and fifth centuries BCE in the Greek cities of Ionia and southern Italy, asked whether there is one underlying nature behind all such transformations. They did so by giving natural explanations rather than appealing to the actions of gods, and that move is why Aristotle and later writers treat them as the first philosophers.

### A caution about the sources

No writings of Thales survive, and we have only fragments of Anaximander, Anaximenes and Heraclitus, quoted by later authors who often had their own interests. Much of what we say about the Milesians comes from Aristotle, writing more than two centuries later, and from the later reports he inspired. Aristotle reads his predecessors as groping toward his own theory of causes, so his reports are evidence to be weighed, not neutral records. Scholars therefore disagree about many details, and a careful writer says "Aristotle reports that Thales held..." rather than "Thales held...".

### The question of the arche

Aristotle says (Metaphysics I.3) that most early thinkers thought the principles of everything were of the nature of matter: that from which all things are, from which they first come to be and into which they finally perish, the substance persisting while its attributes change. The Greek word for such a first principle is **arche**. The Milesians gave three different answers.

- **Thales** of Miletus is reported to have said that the arche is **water**. Aristotle offers a guess at his reasoning, not a quotation: the nourishment of all things is moist, and the seeds of all things have a moist nature.
- **Anaximander** held that the arche is the **apeiron**, the unlimited or indefinite. It is not water, air or any other familiar stuff. A reason reported in ancient sources is that if one of the opposed stuffs (hot, cold, wet, dry) were unlimited, it would long ago have overwhelmed the others; the source of the opposites must therefore be none of them. His one surviving fragment speaks of things paying penalty to one another, according to the ordering of time, for their injustice, which suggests a picture of opposites that encroach on each other and are balanced.
- **Anaximenes** said the arche is **air**, and gave a mechanism: air that is thinned becomes fire, air that is thickened becomes wind, then cloud, then water, then earth and stones. This is the first explanation we know of in which differences of quality (hot, wet, solid) are explained by differences of quantity (how dense the stuff is).

### From change to a single stuff

The Milesian reasoning can be reconstructed as an argument. It is a reconstruction, not a text.

| P1 | Ordinary things come to be and pass away by changing into one another. |
| P2 | For every change, there is something that persists through it as the same underlying nature. |
| P3 | Any thing can, through some chain of changes, become any other, and each thing has only one underlying nature. |
| C | So there is one underlying nature that persists through all change: the arche. |

Premise P3 is what the argument needs and what the Milesians did not argue for. Without it, the step from P2 to C commits a **quantifier shift**: "for every change, there is something that persists" does not imply "there is something that persists through every change". Different changes might have different substrata.

### Heraclitus: flux and the unity of opposites

Heraclitus of Ephesus wrote in deliberately riddling prose. Three themes matter here. First, **flux**: Plato and Aristotle report that he said that all things are in motion and nothing stays still. His fragments about rivers are the evidence. One has the form: on those who step into the same rivers, different and different waters flow. Second, the **unity of opposites**: the road up and the road down are one and the same, and the bow's name is life while its work is death. Opposites are not independent things at war; they are interdependent aspects of one process. Third, the **logos**, an ordering account or formula according to which everything happens, which most people fail to understand even though it is common to all. Fire, in his cosmos, is less a stuff than an image of orderly process: measures kindle and measures go out.

### Two readings of the river

The river fragments pose a puzzle about persistence that we can state as an argument.

| P1 | A thing is the same thing at two times only if it has the same constituents at both times. |
| P2 | The waters of a river at the second time are different from the waters at the first. |
| C | So the river at the second time is not the same thing as the river at the first. |

On the **radical flux** reading, which is Plato's and Aristotle's, Heraclitus accepts the conclusion and generalizes it: everything is always changing in every respect, so nothing stays the same. On a **stability-through-change** reading, defended by some modern interpreters, the fragment says the opposite: it is because new waters keep flowing that there is a river at all, so P1 is false for rivers and flames, which are processes whose identity is a pattern maintained through the replacement of their parts. Which reading is right is a matter of interpretation, and this question is the same as the one that the metaphysics of persistence still debates for ships, organisms and persons. Next week, Parmenides will argue that change itself is impossible.

@example
title: Finding the gap in the argument from change
problem: Someone argues: "Every change has an underlying something that persists through it. So there is one something that persists through every change." Explain why this argument is invalid, and say what extra premise would be needed.
step: Write the argument's form. Let $C$ range over changes and $S$ over underlying things: the premise says that for every $C$ there is some $S$ that persists through $C$; the conclusion says that there is some $S$ such that for every $C$, $S$ persists through $C$.
step: Show that the premise is weaker than the conclusion with a model. Suppose there are exactly two changes: wood into ash and ice into water. Suppose that the wood's matter persists through the first and the water's matter persists through the second, and that these are different. Then the premise is true, since each change has a persisting something, but the conclusion is false, since nothing persists through both.
step: So the inference is a quantifier shift and is invalid. To repair it, add a premise that links the changes, such as: all things can change into one another, each thing has only one underlying nature, and what persists through a change is the underlying nature of the thing before and after. Then every change shares its substratum with every other, and the conclusion follows.
step: Notice the repair is a substantive claim about nature that has to be argued for, which is why Anaximander and Anaximenes could accept the same form of reasoning yet disagree about what the single stuff is.
answer: The argument is invalid because it shifts a universal-existential claim into an existential-universal one. It needs an additional premise connecting all changes through a single underlying nature.

@example
title: Two readings of the river applied to a flame
problem: A candle flame burns steadily for an hour while the wax is consumed. Using the river argument's pattern, state the puzzle about the flame and say what each reading of Heraclitus would say.
step: Apply P1 and P2 to the flame: a thing is the same only if it has the same constituents; the matter in the flame at the end of the hour is entirely different from the matter in it at the start. So, by C, the flame at the end is not the same thing as the flame at the start.
step: The radical flux reader accepts this and says it shows that the flame, like everything, has no fixed identity through time: it is only a continuous succession of changing events.
step: The stability-through-change reader rejects P1 for flames. A flame is a process, kept going by the steady replacement of its fuel, and the same flame is the pattern that continues: the pattern is stable because the matter keeps changing.
step: Both readings agree that the constituents change; they differ over whether sameness requires constant constituents. That is a disagreement in the metaphysics of persistence, not a fact about flames that one more observation could settle.
answer: The radical flux reading accepts the conclusion and generalizes it; the stability reading denies premise P1 for processes such as flames and rivers.

@mistakes
- Treating Aristotle's reports as direct quotations from Thales or Anaximander. Present them as reports that come through Aristotle's own framework.
- Saying the apeiron is just "infinite water" or any other familiar stuff. The point of the idea is that the source of the opposites is not itself one of them.
- Reading Heraclitus as claiming that nothing at all is stable or that the world is chaos. Even on the radical flux reading he affirms a **logos** governing change, and on the other reading he affirms that stability depends on flux.
- Treating "every change has a persisting substratum" as if it implied "one substratum persists through all changes". That is the quantifier shift.
