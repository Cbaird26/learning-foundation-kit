@article
Many people want money, honor, pleasure or health. Yet if you keep asking "and what is that for?", the answers must end somewhere, and Aristotle thinks they end in a single aim that everyone, whatever else they disagree about, names the same way: **eudaimonia**. The word is usually translated "happiness", but it is closer to "flourishing" or "living well": it is not a feeling that comes and goes but a condition of a whole life.

### The highest good

In Nicomachean Ethics I.4 and I.7, Aristotle says that people agree that the highest good is eudaimonia and disagree about what it consists in. A highest good must be **final**, chosen for its own sake and never for the sake of anything else, and **self-sufficient**, such that by itself it makes a life lacking in nothing. Honor, pleasure and virtue are chosen partly for their own sake, but also for the sake of being happy; eudaimonia is chosen only for itself.

### The function argument

To say what eudaimonia is, Aristotle asks what the characteristic activity, or **function** (*ergon*), of a human being is (I.7, 1097b22–1098a20). His reasoning, reconstructed:

| P1 | For anything that has a function, its good consists in performing that function well. |
| P2 | A human being has a function: some activity characteristic of a human being. |
| P3 | Nutrition and growth are shared with plants, and perception with other animals, so the human function is an activity of the soul in accordance with reason. |
| P4 | A good performer of a function performs it in accordance with the proper excellence (virtue). |
| C | So the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with reason, in accordance with the best and most complete virtue, over a complete life. |

The argument is valid if P3 is read as saying that the human function is rational activity and P4 as stating what performing well consists in. Two objections are standard. First, why think that a human being, unlike a flautist or an eye, has a function at all? Artifacts have functions because they were made for a purpose, and organs have functions in the body, but it is not obvious that a whole person has one. Second, even if we have a characteristic activity, why would performing it well be good for **us**? A defender replies that Aristotle's premise is grounded in his biology: living things have characteristic forms of life, and what is good for a living thing is the full exercise of its form of life (compare Week 10); the objector answers that the move from what is characteristic to what is good needs more argument.

### Virtue and the mean

Aristotle distinguishes the **virtues of thought** (such as wisdom and practical wisdom) from the **virtues of character** (such as courage and generosity). Character virtues are acquired by **habituation**: we become just by doing just acts (II.1). This raises an apparent circle: to do just acts one must already be just. Aristotle answers (II.4) that a person can do what is just without being just, by chance or under instruction, and that an act is done virtuously only if the agent knows what he is doing, chooses it for its own sake, and acts from a firm and settled character.

A virtue of character is a **state** concerned with choice, lying in a **mean** relative to us, determined by reason, as the person of practical wisdom would determine it (II.6). The mean is not the arithmetic midpoint: Aristotle observes that if ten units of food are too much and two too little for a given athlete, the trainer will not simply prescribe six; the right amount depends on the person. Each virtue lies between a vice of excess and a vice of deficiency. Courage lies between rashness and cowardice about fear; temperance lies between self-indulgence and insensibility about pleasures. Not everything has a mean: some actions and feelings, such as theft, murder and spite, are bad in themselves (II.6).

**Practical wisdom** (*phronesis*), discussed in Book VI, is the intellectual virtue of deliberating well about what promotes living well. Aristotle holds that there is no full virtue of character without practical wisdom and no practical wisdom without virtue of character (VI.13). In Book X, he argues that the best activity is contemplation, the exercise of theoretical wisdom, and interpreters disagree about whether this means that eudaimonia consists only of contemplation or includes the ethical virtues as well.

@example
title: Finding the mean relative to the agent
problem: A trained firefighter in protective gear and an untrained bystander both face a burning house in which a child may be trapped. Explain why Aristotle says the mean may be different for each.
step: Identify the virtue: courage, concerned with fear and confidence, with cowardice as the deficiency and rashness as the excess.
step: Identify the circumstances that are relevant: the person's training, equipment, the actual danger and the chance of success.
step: For the firefighter, entering may be the mean: the risk is proportionate and success is likely. For the bystander, entering may be rash, since it risks a second victim without a good chance of rescue; calling for help and warning others may be the courageous act.
step: This is what Aristotle means by a mean relative to us: the standard is fixed by what the practically wise person would judge right for this agent in this situation, not by a single arithmetic quantity of risk.
answer: The mean for courage depends on the agent's capacities and the situation, so the same act can be courageous for one and rash for another.

@example
title: Why not nutrition or perception?
problem: In the function argument, how does Aristotle reach the conclusion that the human function is rational activity of the soul?
step: Look for what is peculiar to humans. A function is the activity characteristic of a kind, so something humans share with other kinds cannot be the human function in particular.
step: Nutrition and growth are shared with plants, so they are not peculiar to humans.
step: Perception is shared with the other animals, so it is not peculiar to humans either.
step: What remains is an active life of the part of the soul that has reason, which Aristotle takes to be peculiar to human beings; so the function is activity of the soul in accordance with reason.
answer: He rules out activities shared with plants and animals, and what remains as peculiar to humans is rational activity; this depends on the claim that reason is peculiar to humans and that a function must be peculiar.

@mistakes
- Translating eudaimonia as a mood. It is a way a whole life goes, not a feeling.
- Reading the mean as moderation in everything or as a mathematical midpoint. The mean is relative to the agent and fixed by practical wisdom, and some actions have no mean.
- Saying that virtue is just habit. Habituation is how it is acquired, but a virtue also involves knowing, choosing the act for its own sake and acting from a firm character.
- Confusing the function argument with an argument from the purpose of human beings given by a creator. Aristotle's premise concerns the characteristic activity of a kind of living thing.

solution: P1: For anything that has a function, its good consists in performing that function well. P2: A human being has a function, an activity characteristic of a human being. P3: What is characteristic of humans is not nutrition (shared with plants) or perception (shared with animals), so it is activity of the soul in accordance with reason. P4: A good performer of a function performs it in accordance with the proper excellence. C: So the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with reason and excellence. The best objection is to P2: a flautist's function comes from his craft and an eye's from its role in the body, but nothing makes the whole human being a tool for a task, so the assumption that we have a function is unsupported; and even if we have a characteristic activity, it does not follow that it is good for us. A defender replies that Aristotle's function is not assigned by a maker but is the characteristic activity of a kind of living thing, determined by its form of life, and that what is good for a living thing is to flourish in that form of life. The objector can answer that the move from what is characteristic to what is good still needs an argument.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain Aristotle's response to the objection that virtue cannot be acquired by habituation, since to do just acts one must already be just.
hint: Distinguish doing what is just from doing it as a just person does.
solution: The objection says that to become just we must do just acts, but doing just acts requires already being just, so the process cannot begin. Aristotle replies (II.4) that a person can do a just act without being just, for instance by chance or on instruction, as someone can write grammatically without being a grammarian. An act is done as a just person does it only if the agent knows what he is doing, chooses it for its own sake, and does it from a firm and unchanging character. Habituation produces that character by repeated performance of the acts, first under guidance, and so the circle is avoided: the early acts are just in content, and later acts are just in the full sense. A remaining question is how the learner is guided to the right acts at the start, which Aristotle answers by appeal to good upbringing and law.
