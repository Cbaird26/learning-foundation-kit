@article
Two sticks can be equal in length, and yet no two sticks are perfectly equal: put them under a microscope and they differ. How then do we have the idea of Equal, and what is it that sticks fall short of? Plato's answer is that there is an object of thought, Equality itself, which is perfectly equal, which our senses never present, and which sensible things imitate more or less well. This is the **theory of Forms** (or Ideas, from *eidos* and *idea*), one of the most influential positions in the history of philosophy.

### What a Form is

For each of many general terms (Beauty, Justice, Equal, Large) Plato posits a Form, a single thing that the many particular beautiful, just or equal things are said to **participate in** or **imitate**. A Form, in the works usually called middle-period (Phaedo, Republic, Symposium), is:

- **intelligible, not sensible**: grasped by reason, not by the senses;
- **unchanging and eternal**: it does not come to be or pass away or vary from time to time;
- **purely F**: the Form of Beauty is beautiful without being ugly in any respect, unlike beautiful things, which are beautiful in one respect and not in another;
- **a model and an explanation**: particular things are F because of their relation to the Form of F (Phaedo 100).

The theory was meant to answer several questions: how general thought is possible when the world of sense is always changing (a problem of **universals**); what the objects of knowledge are, if sensible things are always in flux; and what grounds our standards in mathematics and ethics.

### An argument from knowledge

At the end of Republic V, Socrates distinguishes the lovers of sights and sounds, who admire many beautiful things, from the philosopher, who loves the Beautiful itself. He argues that these two have different cognitive states with different objects. In outline:

| P1 | Knowledge is set over what is; ignorance over what is not; opinion over what is and also is not. |
| P2 | Each of the many beautiful things is also ugly, and so not beautiful, in some respect (and similarly for the just, the large and the equal). |
| P3 | What is both F and not F is between what purely is and what purely is not. |
| C1 | So the many beautiful things are objects of opinion, not of knowledge. |
| P4 | If there is knowledge of what beauty is, its object is something that purely is, and so is beautiful without being ugly in any respect. |
| P5 | There is knowledge of what beauty is. |
| C2 | So the object of that knowledge is something other than the many beautiful things (by C1) and is only beautiful: the Beautiful itself. |

The argument is a reconstruction, valid as set out; P4 and P5 supply the bridge from C1 to C2 that the text leaves implicit (that knowledge needs an object that purely is, and that the philosopher has such knowledge). Objectors question P2: a thing can be beautiful **in one respect** and ugly **in another** without being both beautiful and ugly in the same respect, and so it need not be "between" being and not-being. A defender replies that Plato's examples are meant to show that the sensible particulars are F only relative to a comparison or a standard, whereas the Form is F without qualification.

### The sun, the line and the cave

Republic VI compares the **Form of the Good** with the sun. As the sun gives visible things their visibility and the eye its power of seeing, and also brings them into being and growth, the Good gives the knowable things their knowability, and in some way their being, though it is itself beyond being in dignity and power (508e–509b). This is an image, not an identification: the Good is not the sun.

The **divided line** (509d–511e) is a line divided unequally into two parts, one for the **visible** and one for the **intelligible**, and each part divided again in the same ratio. This gives four segments, with the states of mind corresponding to each:

| Segment | Objects | State of mind |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | images: shadows, reflections | imagination (*eikasia*) |
| 2 | visible things: animals, plants, artifacts | belief (*pistis*) |
| 3 | mathematical objects, studied with the help of diagrams and from hypotheses | thought (*dianoia*) |
| 4 | the Forms, studied by dialectic, up to the first principle that needs no hypothesis | knowledge or understanding (*noesis*) |

Plato does not say which of the two main parts is longer, though the usual convention makes the intelligible part longer. Whichever it is, the proportion has a consequence: the two **middle** segments are equal in length. If the whole line has length $L$ and is divided in ratio $a:b$ and then each part in the same ratio, the middle segments both have length $L\,ab/(a+b)^2$.

The **cave** (514a–517a) is a story. Prisoners are chained since childhood facing a wall, seeing only shadows cast by objects carried in front of a fire behind them. One is freed, is dazzled, gradually learns to see the objects, then things outside in the light, and finally the sun. If he returned, he would struggle to see in the dark, and the others would mock him. Plato says the image is to be applied to what was said before (517b): the cave is the visible world, the fire is the sun, the ascent is the soul's journey to the intelligible, and the sun outside stands for the Good. How closely each stage of the cave matches a segment of the line is a matter of interpretation.

@example
title: Dividing the line
problem: A line of length 18 is divided in the ratio 1:2 into a visible part and a longer intelligible part, and each part is divided in the same ratio. Find the length of each segment and check that the middle segments are equal.
step: The visible part is $18 \times 1/3 = 6$ and the intelligible part is $18 \times 2/3 = 12$.
step: Divide the visible part in the ratio 1:2: imagination is $6 \times 1/3 = 2$ and belief is $6 \times 2/3 = 4$.
step: Divide the intelligible part in the same ratio: thought is $12 \times 1/3 = 4$ and knowledge is $12 \times 2/3 = 8$.
step: The segments are 2, 4, 4 and 8, which sum to 18. Belief (4) and thought (4) are equal, matching the formula $L\,ab/(a+b)^2 = 18 \times 2/9 = 4$.
answer: The segments are 2 (imagination), 4 (belief), 4 (thought) and 8 (knowledge); the two middle segments are equal.

@example
title: Placing cases on the line
problem: Place on the divided line: (a) a tree's reflection in a pond; (b) the tree; (c) a geometer's proof about circles that uses a drawn circle; (d) a philosopher's account of what Justice itself is.
step: (a) A reflection is an image of a visible thing, so it belongs to the first segment, and the state of mind concerned with it is imagination.
step: (b) The tree is an ordinary visible thing, so it belongs to the second segment, and the state of mind is belief.
step: (c) A geometer reasons about circles, using a drawn circle as an image, and starts from hypotheses she does not justify. That is the third segment, thought. She is reasoning about the circle itself, not the drawing.
step: (d) An account of Justice itself, reached by dialectic without relying on images, is the fourth segment, knowledge.
answer: (a) imagination; (b) belief; (c) thought; (d) knowledge.

@mistakes
- Saying that Plato held that the Forms are located in a second place, "a heaven". The Forms are not in space or time; "place" language is metaphor in the dialogues.
- Saying that the divided line's lengths prove how much more real each part is. The proportion is an image of degrees of clarity and truth, and Plato gives no numbers.
- Identifying the sun with the Form of the Good. The sun is an image of the Good, and an analogy is not an identity.
- Forgetting that Plato later criticizes his own theory of Forms, in the Parmenides.

prompt: State the "one over many" argument for Forms, as it appears in Republic X (596a), in standard form, and give one reply that a nominalist could make.
hint: Begin from a group of things called by the same name, such as many beds, and ask what, according to Plato, accounts for their sharing the name.
solution: P1: Whenever there are many things to which we apply the same name (many beds), there is a single Form corresponding to them. P2: There are many beds that we all call "bed". C: So there is a single Form of Bed. This is Plato's pattern of reasoning in 596a, and it needs the claim in P1 that a shared name requires a single object. A nominalist replies that P1 is the point at issue: things can fall under the same word because they resemble one another or because we use the word of them, without there being any further entity, the Form, in which they all share. Plato's defender may answer that resemblance itself requires a respect of resemblance, which looks like a universal.

@quiz self
prompt: Give one objection to the claim that the argument from relativity establishes that Forms exist, and say how a defender of Plato could reply.
hint: Consider whether sensible things must be F only "in a respect" and whether there could be other objects of knowledge than Forms.
solution: Objection: even if sensible things are F only in respect of a comparison or standard, it does not follow that there is a separately existing Form that is F without qualification. Knowledge could be of general truths about sensible kinds, or of mathematical structures, without any further entities. Reply: Plato's defender can say that the point of the argument is that the objects of knowledge cannot be things that are always F in one way and not-F in another, since then knowledge would not be stable; something must be F without qualification for there to be a standard by which we judge sensible things to be more or less F. The objector can answer that a standard need not be an entity of a different kind, and this dispute led to later debates about universals.
