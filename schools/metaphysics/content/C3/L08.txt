@article
"All Greeks are mortal" and "Socrates is a Greek" together yield "Socrates is mortal". Everyone sees that, but Aristotle was the first to ask what makes such inferences good, and to build a system out of the answer. His logical works are collected as the **Organon** (the "instrument"): the Categories, On Interpretation, the Prior and Posterior Analytics, the Topics and the Sophistical Refutations. Two tools from them matter most for metaphysics: the categories and the syllogism.

### The categories

In Categories 4, Aristotle lists ten kinds of things that can be said without combination. Each is a kind of answer to a question about a thing:

| Category | Example (Aristotle's, or close to it) |
|---|---|
| substance | man, horse |
| quantity | two cubits long |
| quality | white, grammatical |
| relation | double, half |
| place | in the Lyceum |
| time | yesterday |
| position | lying, sitting |
| state (having) | shod, armed |
| action | cutting, burning |
| passion (being affected) | being cut, being burned |

Whether the categories classify words, concepts or kinds of being is a matter of scholarly debate; it is safest to say that they are the highest kinds of predicate, and, on most readings, correspond to basic ways of being.

Chapter 2 draws two distinctions. Something is **said of** a subject when it is a species or genus predicated of it, as "man" is said of Callias. Something is **present in** a subject when it is in it, not as a part, and cannot exist apart from it, as a particular paleness is present in a body. The two distinctions cross to give four cases.

| | not present in a subject | present in a subject |
|---|---|---|
| **not said of a subject** | individual man, individual horse | an individual piece of grammatical knowledge in a soul; a particular white |
| **said of a subject** | man, horse (said of individuals) | knowledge (present in the soul and said of the individual knowledge); color |

**Primary substances** are the individuals in the top left cell: this man, this horse. **Secondary substances** are the species and genera of primary substances (man, animal). Chapter 5 argues that primary substances are the most basic: everything else is either said of them or present in them, so if there were no primary substances, nothing else could exist (2a34–b6). Week 9 asks whether this individual-first view survives in Aristotle's later work.

### The syllogism

In the Prior Analytics (I.1, 24b18–20), Aristotle defines a **syllogism** as a discourse in which, certain things being laid down, something other than what is laid down follows of necessity. He studies syllogisms built from four kinds of categorical sentence. The letters A, E, I, O come from a medieval mnemonic, not from Aristotle himself:

| Letter | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A | All S are P | All stars are bodies |
| E | No S are P | No stars are stones |
| I | Some S are P | Some stars are bright |
| O | Some S are not P | Some stars are not bright |

A syllogism has two premises and a conclusion, and three terms: the **major** (the predicate of the conclusion), the **minor** (the subject of the conclusion) and the **middle**, which appears in both premises but not in the conclusion. The position of the middle term gives the **figure**; Aristotle recognized three figures, and a fourth was added later. The first figure is "perfect": its validity is evident, and the others are shown valid by reducing them to it. The four valid moods of the first figure have the traditional names

| Barbara (AAA) | All M are P; all S are M; so all S are P |
| Celarent (EAE) | No M are P; all S are M; so no S are P |
| Darii (AII) | All M are P; some S are M; so some S are P |
| Ferio (EIO) | No M are P; some S are M; so some S are not P |

To show that a syllogism is **invalid**, give a counter-model: sets for S, P and M that make both premises true and the conclusion false. Aristotle does this too, with terms. His syllogistic assumes that terms are not empty, so it differs from modern predicate logic on cases such as "All S are P" with an empty S, and it handles only simple predicates of one subject, so it cannot capture arguments about relations: "every horse is an animal, so the head of a horse is the head of an animal", a well-known example due to De Morgan, is valid but lies outside it. Frege's logic of 1879 overcame this limitation.

### First principles

In Metaphysics Gamma, Aristotle states the **principle of non-contradiction**: the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same thing in the same respect (1005b19–20). He holds it to be the firmest principle, which cannot be demonstrated, because every demonstration relies on it, but which can be defended against someone who denies it by showing that the denier must say something determinate in order to deny it. He also states the **law of excluded middle** (Gamma 7): of one thing, we must either affirm or deny any one attribute. Whether this holds for statements about the future, as in his discussion of the sea battle in On Interpretation 9, is a matter of long-running interpretive dispute.

@example
title: Testing a syllogism with a counter-model
problem: Test this syllogism: "All philosophers are readers; all poets are readers; so all poets are philosophers."
step: Identify the terms. The conclusion's predicate is "philosophers" (major), its subject is "poets" (minor), and "readers" is the middle term, which appears in both premises.
step: The form is All P are M, All S are M, so All S are P. The middle is the predicate of both premises, so it is not distributed in either; this is the pattern of an invalid syllogism.
step: Build a counter-model. Let the philosophers be {1}, the poets be {2} and the readers be {1, 2}. Both premises are true: every philosopher (1) is a reader, and every poet (2) is a reader.
step: The conclusion is false in this model, since poet 2 is not a philosopher. So the syllogism is invalid.
answer: Invalid: the counter-model with philosophers {1}, poets {2} and readers {1, 2} makes the premises true and the conclusion false.

@example
title: Said of and present in
problem: Classify (a) Callias, (b) the species horse, (c) this particular paleness of Callias's skin, and (d) color in general, as said of a subject and present in a subject.
step: (a) Callias is an individual man. He is not said of anything, and he is not present in anything: he is a primary substance.
step: (b) The species horse is said of individual horses and is not present in them as an accident. It is said of a subject but not present in one: a secondary substance.
step: (c) This paleness is a particular quality that exists in Callias's body and could not exist apart from it, so it is present in a subject. It is not said of any further subject.
step: (d) Color is present in bodies, and it is said of particular colors such as this paleness. It is both present in a subject and said of a subject.
answer: (a) neither; (b) said of only; (c) present in only; (d) both.

@mistakes
- Treating the categories as a list of kinds of things in the world and nothing else. They are the highest kinds of predicate, and how closely they track kinds of being is debated.
- Saying that "present in" means "a part of". An accident is in a subject without being a part and cannot exist apart from it.
- Calling a syllogism valid because its conclusion is true. Validity concerns the form: a valid syllogism with true premises has a true conclusion, but a true conclusion does not make the form valid.
- Saying that Aristotle's logic is the same as modern predicate logic. It treats only one-place categorical sentences and assumes that terms are not empty.

prompt: Thrasymachus argues: "To be just is to obey the laws; in every city the ruling group makes the laws in its own interest; so to be just is to do what is in the interest of the ruling group, the stronger." Put this in standard form, say whether the inference is valid, and name one suppressed premise that Socrates challenges.
hint: Treat "just acts" and "acts of obeying the law" as terms, and notice what the word "stronger" adds.
solution: P1: All just acts are acts of obeying the laws. P2: All acts of obeying the laws are acts that serve the interest of the ruling group. C: So all just acts serve the interest of the ruling group. This has the form of Barbara and is valid. P2 combines two claims: that rulers make laws in their own interest and that they never mistake it. Socrates challenges the suppressed premise that rulers do not err about their own interest, since if they sometimes err, then obeying a law may harm the ruling group, and justice would not be its advantage. A second suppressed premise is that the ruling group is the stronger, which is needed for the conclusion as Thrasymachus states it.

prompt: Explain why Aristotle, in Categories 5, treats individual substances (this man, this horse) as primary, and give one question one might raise about that claim.
hint: Consider what he says about everything else being said of or present in primary substances.
solution: Aristotle argues that everything other than primary substances is either said of a primary substance (species and genera, such as man and animal) or present in one (qualities, quantities and the like, such as a particular paleness), so that without primary substances nothing else could exist. That makes individual substances the basic subjects of the world. A question one might raise is whether individuals, rather than their forms or their kinds, are the best candidates for what is most basic, since he later says (Metaphysics Z) that substance is form, and it is disputed how the later account fits the Categories. Another is whether the claim about dependence gives priority in being or only in what is predicated.
