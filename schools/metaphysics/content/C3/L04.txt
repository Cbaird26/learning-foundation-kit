@article
Imagine you ask a neighbor what courage is and she says, "Standing your ground in battle." You agree, and then a pilot comes to mind who flew away from an ambush to save her crew. Was that cowardice? If not, the definition seems too narrow. Socrates built a way of philosophizing out of such exchanges, and most of what we call the history of ethics begins with the questions he asked.

### The sources and the method

Socrates wrote nothing. We rely on Plato's dialogues, especially the early ones; on Xenophon's memoirs; on Aristophanes's comedy *The Clouds*; and on scattered remarks of Aristotle. These sources differ, and the "Socratic problem" is how much of Plato's Socrates is the historical man and how much is Plato's own development, the more so in the middle dialogues. The usual practice is to treat the early dialogues (Euthyphro, Laches, Charmides, Apology, Crito and others) as the best evidence for the Socratic method and a core of views, and to say so when we do.

The method is the **elenchus**, "testing" or "refutation". Socrates asks for a definition ("What is courage?" "What is piety?"). He gets an answer, and then secures the agreement of the respondent to further claims, from which he derives a contradiction of the original answer. The respondent is thereby shown to hold inconsistent beliefs and usually ends in **aporia**, perplexity. A logical point is worth noticing: an inconsistency shows that something in the set of beliefs must go, but not which one; whether the elenchus can establish truths or only expose inconsistency is a long-debated question.

Socrates's question has a special form: "What is F?" He wants not examples of F but what makes all the F things F, one and the same in every case. A good definition should be neither too narrow (leaving out some F things) nor too broad (including some non-F things). Some scholars call the assumption that we cannot know whether anything is F until we can define F the "Socratic fallacy", a name due to Peter Geach; whether Socrates held that priority of definition is itself disputed.

### A definition and its refutation: the Euthyphro

Euthyphro is on his way to prosecute his own father for a killing, and he claims to have exact knowledge of piety. Socrates asks him what piety is. After an example instead of a definition, Euthyphro proposes that the pious is what is loved by the gods. Socrates points out that the gods, as the stories say, quarrel, so the same act might be loved by some gods and hated by others and so be both pious and impious. Euthyphro revises: the pious is what is loved by **all** the gods. Then comes the famous question: is the pious loved by the gods because it is pious, or is it pious because they love it?

| P1 | The pious is loved by the gods because it is pious. |
| P2 | The god-loved is god-loved because the gods love it, and it is not loved because it is god-loved. |
| P3 | If two things are one and the same, then whatever is true of one is true of the other (Leibniz's Law). |
| C | So the pious is not the same as the god-loved. |

Premise P1 is what Euthyphro agrees to after reflection, and premise P2 is a general point about the passive "being loved". The conclusion does not show that the gods do not love the pious things; it shows that being god-loved is a feature the pious things have (Socrates says a *pathos*, an affection) and not what piety is (the *ousia*, its essence). One objection is that the "because" in P1 and P2 creates an intensional context, in which substituting identicals may fail; the usual reply is that "because" claims are used here as claims about what explains what, and that if piety were identical with god-lovedness the explanation could not run in only one direction. The same argument is now applied to **divine command theory** in ethics, and defenders of that theory have replies, for instance that goodness is grounded in God's nature rather than in arbitrary commands.

### Virtue and wrongdoing

In the Apology, Socrates, on trial for impiety and corrupting the young, says that he knows he is not wise, and that the god's oracle meant that the wisest person is the one who recognizes the limits of their wisdom; that he will go on examining himself and others; and that the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being. Whether his profession of ignorance is sincere or ironic is disputed.

Socrates also holds, in the early dialogues, that virtue is a kind of knowledge and that no one does wrong willingly. A reconstruction of the argument, in a form drawn from the Meno and Protagoras (it is not a single passage), is:

| P1 | No one chooses what they believe will harm them. |
| P2 | Wrongdoing harms the wrongdoer, by damaging the soul. |
| P3 | If someone knows that an act is wrong, then they believe that it will harm them. |
| C | So no one knowingly does wrong; wrongdoing comes from ignorance. |

The argument is valid: if someone knowingly did wrong, then by premise P3 they would believe the act would harm them, and so would be choosing what they believe will harm them, against premise P1. (Premise P2 is not needed for validity; it is what makes P3 plausible.) The best objection, pressed by Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics VII.2) who reports that Socrates's view contradicts the plain appearances, is **akrasia**, weakness of will: people seem to act against their own better judgment. The objection rejects premise P1. The Socratic reply is that people who seem to do so are really in the grip of a false belief about what is best at the moment of action, and so are ignorant after all.

@example
title: Analyzing a definition by counterexample
problem: In the Laches, the general Laches defines courage as staying at one's post in battle and not fleeing. Socrates replies by pointing to Scythian cavalry, who fight while retreating. Analyze the definition and the refutation.
step: State the definition: x is courageous if and only if x stays at its post and does not flee in battle. This is a claim about what courage is, so it must cover all and only courageous acts.
step: Test it against the counterexample. A Scythian rider who retreats while still fighting does not stay at his post, yet is plausibly courageous. So the definition is too narrow: it leaves out a courageous case.
step: Notice Socrates's further move. He observes that there is courage in danger at sea, in illness, in poverty and in politics, none of which involves holding a post in battle. So even if the definition were correct for infantry, it gives an example of courage rather than what is common to all cases.
step: A reasonable repair is to state something about the soul's endurance or about wise handling of danger, which Laches next tries (a definition in terms of endurance), and the dialogue tests it again.
answer: The definition is too narrow, because the Scythian cavalry case is courageous without holding a post, and it is a species of courage rather than the common nature the question asks for.

@example
title: Testing the argument that no one does wrong willingly
problem: A person knows that eating a third dessert is bad for them, and eats it anyway. Which premise of the Socratic argument does this case challenge, and what reply is open to a Socratic?
step: Set out the argument: P1 says no one chooses what they believe will harm them, P2 says wrongdoing harms the doer, P3 connects knowing that an act is wrong to believing it harms them, and C says no one knowingly does wrong. Our case is about harm to health, not wrongdoing, but it has the same structure: knowing that something is bad for oneself and still choosing it.
step: In the case, the person believes that dessert will harm them and eats it anyway. That is a case of choosing what one believes will harm one, so it challenges premise P1.
step: A Socratic can reply that the person at that moment believes, or is carried by an appearance, that eating is on balance best, and so does not really hold the belief that it harms them: the knowledge is not present when it is needed.
step: The objector can answer that this redescribes the case to fit the theory, which is what Aristotle suspected. The disagreement is about how to describe the person's state at the moment of choice.
answer: The case challenges premise P1; the Socratic denies that the person genuinely holds the belief at the time of choice, and the objector says this ignores how things seem from the inside.

@mistakes
- Treating everything Plato's Socrates says as the historical Socrates's view. Name the source and say that the Socratic problem exists.
- Saying that the elenchus proves the definition false. It shows that the respondent's beliefs are inconsistent, and it takes a further step to say which belief should go.
- Reading the Euthyphro as showing that the gods do not exist or do not love what is pious. It targets a definition of piety.
- Confusing "no one does wrong willingly" with "no one does wrong". Socrates thinks people do wrong, but through ignorance.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain what an elenchus does and does not show, using the Euthyphro as an example.
hint: Say what is established about the respondent's beliefs and what is left open.
solution: An elenchus takes a respondent's definition and, from other things the respondent agrees to, derives a contradiction of it. In the Euthyphro, Euthyphro agrees that the pious is what all the gods love, that the pious is loved because it is pious, and that the god-loved is loved because it is loved by the gods. These together are inconsistent with identifying piety with being god-loved. What this shows is that Euthyphro's beliefs cannot all be true; it does not by itself show which belief should be given up. Euthyphro could have resisted the claim that the pious is loved because it is pious, and so the conclusion depends on his agreeing to it. The elenchus is therefore best seen as exposing inconsistency and pushing the discussion forward, and whether it can establish positive truths is a matter of dispute.
