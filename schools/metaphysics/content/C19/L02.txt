@article
You see a brown cow and a white cow. Both are cows; one is brown and one is white; each is this cow and not that one; and the brownness belongs to the first cow in some intimate way that is not the way the cow is standing near the white one. A realist about the world will want a theory of what the cows share, how qualities belong to things, and what makes each thing an individual. **Nyaya-Vaisheshika**, the realist system of classical India, gives a detailed theory of all three.

### Two schools, one system

**Vaisheshika**, traditionally traced to Kanada's *Vaisheshika Sutra*, is mainly an ontology: a list of what there is. **Nyaya**, traditionally traced to Gautama's *Nyaya Sutra*, is mainly an epistemology and a theory of debate: it analyzes the means of knowledge (*pramana*): perception, inference, comparison and testimony. In later centuries the two were combined, so people speak of Nyaya-Vaisheshika; a late and very technical phase is called Navya-Nyaya ("new Nyaya"), which is usually associated with Gangesa around the thirteenth or fourteenth century. The central commitment is **realism**: the world exists independently of our minds, and what we know through our means of knowledge is really there.

### The categories (padarthas)

A *padartha* ("referent of a word") is a basic kind of thing. Early Vaisheshika lists six; later authors add a seventh, absence.

| Category | Gloss | Example |
|---|---|---|
| dravya | substance, the bearer of qualities and motion | a pot, a lump of clay, a self |
| guna | quality, residing in a substance | the redness of the pot, its number |
| karma | motion or action, residing in a substance | the pot's falling |
| samanya | universal, the repeatable feature shared by many | potness |
| vishesha | ultimate particularity, what individuates the eternal substances | what distinguishes one atom from another |
| samavaya | inherence, the relation of inseparable connection | the relation of the redness to the pot |
| abhava | absence, the lack of something | the absence of a pot on this table |

There are **nine substances**: earth, water, fire and air (each with atoms, four kinds in all), ether (*akasha*, the medium of sound), time, space, the self (*atman*) and the mind (*manas*, an internal organ of attention). Qualities include color, taste, number, size, conjunction and also cognitions, pleasure and desire: Vaisheshika counts these mental states as qualities of the self.

### Inherence and wholes

Two things can be connected by **contact** (*samyoga*), which is a quality and can come to an end, as when a book rests on a table. Other connections cannot be dissolved without destroying the thing: a quality and its substance, a motion and its substance, a universal and its instances, a whole and its parts. These are held together by **inherence** (*samavaya*), a single relation counted as a category of its own. Nyaya-Vaisheshika holds that a whole, such as a cloth, is a new entity (*avayavin*) that inheres in its threads, and is something over and above them. Buddhist opponents deny this, which prepares the ground for weeks 4 and 5.

### The argument for universals

| P1 | Our single cognition "cow" is accurate when applied to many different cows. |
| P2 | If one cognition is accurate when applied to many things, then those things share a real feature that makes it accurate. |
| P3 | A real feature shared by many things is a universal. |
| C | So there are universals, such as cowness. |

The argument is valid. Its first premise is a datum of ordinary cognition, and its third is a definition, so the weight rests on **P2**. The standard nominalist reply attacks P2: the cognition is accurate because the animals resemble each other, and resemblance is a primitive relation among individuals that needs no extra entity. A Buddhist version, due to Dignaga and developed by Dharmakirti, is the **apoha** (exclusion) theory: a general word works by excluding things that are not of its kind, not by naming a shared entity. Nyaya replies that exclusion presupposes the positive feature it is supposed to replace, and that resemblance itself needs something in common.

### Comparing the categories with Aristotle's

Aristotle's *Categories* lists ten kinds of thing that can be said of or about a subject: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action and being acted on. The lists overlap but do different jobs. Aristotle classifies the kinds of predicates a being can have; the Vaisheshika list classifies everything that is knowable and nameable, including universals and absences. Universals are not a category for Aristotle. Number and size are qualities in Vaisheshika, so Aristotle's quantity has no separate place. Vaisheshika's substances include space, time, self and mind, whereas Aristotle's primary substances are individual living things and objects. Aristotle says qualities are "present in" a substance, which resembles inherence, but inherence is itself a category of Vaisheshika. These points are about the lists, not about who was right.

@example
title: Classifying a case with the categories
problem: Apply the Vaisheshika categories to the sentence "The blue lamp is on the shelf, and there is no oil in it." Identify one item of each relevant category.
step: The lamp is a **dravya**: a substance. The shelf is also a substance.
step: Blueness is a **guna**, a quality, and it is connected to the lamp by **samavaya**: inherence, because the quality cannot exist apart from the substance it inheres in.
step: The lamp's being on the shelf is **samyoga**, contact, a quality of the two substances; it can end when the lamp is moved.
step: What all lamps share, lampness, is a **samanya**. The lack of oil in the lamp is an **abhava**, an absence: for later Nyaya-Vaisheshika, absences are real and are known by perceiving the place where something is not.
answer: Lamp and shelf are substances; blueness is a quality inhering in the lamp; the lamp's position is contact; lampness is a universal; the missing oil is an absence.

@example
title: Mapping Vaisheshika categories onto Aristotle's
problem: Where, if anywhere, do the Vaisheshika categories "quality" and "universal" appear in Aristotle's list of ten?
step: Vaisheshika guna is a very broad category (color, taste, number, size, conjunction, cognition, pleasure and others). Aristotle's quality covers colors and dispositions like health, but his quantity, relation and some of the other categories would put number, size and conjunction elsewhere.
step: So a Vaisheshika quality corresponds only in part to Aristotle's quality, and part of what Aristotle files under quantity and relation is a quality in Vaisheshika.
step: Vaisheshika samanya (universal) has no counterpart: in Aristotle, universals such as "man" are said of individual substances, but they are not an extra category on the list of ten.
answer: Quality overlaps only partly; the universal has no corresponding category in Aristotle's ten.

@mistakes
- Calling inherence "a kind of contact": contact is a quality that can end, whereas inherence relates inseparable items and is a category of its own.
- Equating dravya with the Western notion of substance in every respect: it includes space, time, self and mind, and it is defined as the bearer of qualities and motion.
- Saying that Nyaya-Vaisheshika has no place for absence: later authors count absence (abhava) as a seventh category.
- Treating the argument for universals as invalid because nominalists disagree: it is valid; the nominalist denies a premise.

solution: Sentence: "The green mango hangs from the tree, and there is no ripeness in it yet." The mango and the tree are substances (dravya); greenness is a quality (guna) inhering in the mango by inherence (samavaya); the mango's hanging from the tree is a contact (samyoga) of two substances; mangoness is a universal (samanya) shared by all mangoes; the lack of ripeness in the mango is an absence (abhava).
