@article
You hold a piece of cotton in a flame, and it burns. Everyone says that the fire burned the cotton. But what exactly did you observe? You saw the flame touch the cotton and then you saw the cotton burn. Did you also see the burning being produced by the fire, or did you see one thing after another? This is the question at the center of the *Tahafut al-Falasifa* ("The Incoherence of the Philosophers"), written by al-Ghazali (1058 to 1111), one of the most influential thinkers of the Islamic world.

### Al-Ghazali on causation

In the seventeenth discussion of the *Tahafut*, al-Ghazali attacks the claim, associated with Avicenna and the *falasifa*, that there is a **necessary connection** between a natural cause and its effect: given the cause and the right conditions, the effect must follow. He denies that this is established. His argument:

| P1 | If the claim that fire necessarily produces burning is established, then it is established by observation or by reason. |
| P2 | Observation does not establish it: it shows only that burning follows contact with fire. |
| P3 | Reason does not establish it: the affirmation of one thing does not entail the affirmation of the other. |
| C | So the claim that fire necessarily produces burning is not established. |

The argument is valid: P1 gives two sources, P2 and P3 deny each, so the claim is not established. Note what it does not conclude: that the claim is false. Al-Ghazali's own positive proposal is that the connection is due to God's **habit** (*'ada*): God creates the burning at the moment of contact with the fire, and does so regularly, so that we have reliable expectations, yet God could do otherwise (as in miracles such as Abraham's escaping unharmed from fire). He also considers a weaker alternative, in which fire has a nature that God can remove or override. Scholars disagree about whether al-Ghazali himself endorses occasionalism in this discussion or only argues that the philosophers cannot rule it out, since he offers both options.

Critics attack **P3**: knowing what fire is, they say, reveals its power to burn, so reason, by grasping the nature of fire, does establish the necessity (the view of the *falasifa*, according to whom natures ground necessary connections). Others attack **P2**, saying that regular observation of many cases reveals natures and so necessity.

### Compared with Hume

Hume, in the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (sections 4 to 7) and the *Treatise* (1.3.14), argues similarly that observation shows only one event following another, and that the contrary of any matter of fact involves no contradiction. The two views are alike in these two points. They differ in at least three: Hume offers a psychological account (the idea of necessary connection arises from the habit of expecting the second event after the first), while al-Ghazali offers a metaphysical one (God's creative habit); al-Ghazali's purpose is partly to secure the possibility of miracles, whereas Hume's discussion of miracles (Enquiry, section 10) is skeptical about reports of them; and al-Ghazali holds that there is a real agent who produces the effect, while Hume's own view of causal powers is disputed by interpreters (some read him as denying that there are necessary connections, others as denying only that we can know or conceive them beyond the felt expectation).

### Avicenna on essence and existence

Avicenna (Ibn Sina, c. 980 to 1037) distinguished the **essence** or **quiddity** (*mahiyya*, "what it is") of a thing from its **existence** (*wujud*). A classic argument associated with him:

| P1 | If existence belonged to the essence of a thing, then knowing what the thing is would suffice to know that it exists. |
| P2 | We can know what a thing is (for example, a phoenix, the illustration Aquinas later used when he took up the argument) without knowing that it exists. |
| C | So existence does not belong to the essence of such things. |

It is valid by modus tollens. For Avicenna only one being, the Necessary Existent, has an essence that is not distinct from its existence; everything else receives existence from another.

### Mulla Sadra: the primacy of existence

Mulla Sadra (Sadr al-Din Shirazi, c. 1571 to 1640) built on this. His main work is *The Transcendent Wisdom of the Four Intellectual Journeys*. He held the **primacy of existence** (*asalat al-wujud*): existence is what is real, and quiddities are concepts the mind abstracts from the limits of existence. One argument (a reconstruction in the spirit of his view, not a quotation):

| P1 | Whatever is real in itself is not indifferent to existing. |
| P2 | Any quiddity considered in itself is indifferent to existing. |
| P3 | Whatever is real in itself is either a quiddity or existence. |
| P4 | Something is real in itself. |
| C | So existence is real in itself. |

It is valid: P1 and P2 show that no quiddity is real in itself; P3 and P4 leave existence. A critic denies **P1**: a quiddity might be real in itself as an intelligible nature although it is indifferent to whether it is instantiated, which is how a rival tradition of Persian philosophy thought of it. A critic can deny **P3**, saying that the real is the concrete individual, which is neither. Sadra also held that existence comes in **degrees** (*tashkik*, gradation or systematic ambiguity), a single reality with more and less intense levels, and the doctrine of **substantial motion** (*al-haraka al-jawhariyya*): change occurs in the substance itself, not only in accidents, so the natural world is being continually renewed. Avicenna and Aristotle had limited motion to accidents.

step: Premises: if the claim is established, it is established by observation or reason. Observation shows only that thirst ceases after drinking. Reason shows no entailment between "water is drunk" and "thirst ceases."
step: So the claim of necessary connection is not established. The argument is valid.
step: The argument does not show that water does not quench thirst, or that we cannot rely on it: it concerns the grounds for the claim of necessity.
step: A critic could attack the premise about reason by saying that knowing what water is and what thirst is shows why water quenches it.
answer: The claim of necessity is not established by the argument's two sources; the argument does not deny the regularity or justify distrust of it.

@example
title: Essence and existence for a made-up animal
problem: Use Avicenna's argument to show that existence is not part of the essence of the "glass-winged moth," a species that we are told about but know nothing about otherwise.
step: Premise 1: if existence belonged to the essence of the glass-winged moth, then knowing what it is would suffice to know that it exists.
step: Premise 2: you can grasp what a glass-winged moth is (a moth with glass-like wings) without knowing that there are any.
step: By modus tollens, existence does not belong to its essence.
step: The same reasoning works for any kind of thing whose essence can be understood without settling its existence. Avicenna excepted the Necessary Existent.
answer: Existence is not part of the moth's essence, since the essence can be grasped without knowing that the moth exists.

@mistakes
- Saying that al-Ghazali concludes that causal claims are false: the argument concludes that the necessity claim is not established.
- Treating al-Ghazali and Hume as the same: they agree that observation shows only succession, but they differ in their positive accounts and aims.
- Equating Avicenna's essence-existence distinction with Mulla Sadra's view: Sadra turns it into the claim that existence is primary and quiddity is abstracted.
- Treating substantial motion as the claim that only accidents change: it is the claim that substances themselves change.
