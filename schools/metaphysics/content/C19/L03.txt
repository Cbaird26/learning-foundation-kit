@article
When you feel a pain, what is it that is aware of the pain? One answer is that awareness is a product of the body's workings. Another is that awareness is a distinct kind of thing. A third is that there is, in the end, only one awareness, and the many persons and things we perceive are appearances within it. Classical Indian philosophy contains rigorous defenses of the second and third answers, in **Samkhya** and **Advaita Vedanta**.

### Samkhya: two ultimate realities

Classical Samkhya, systematized in Ishvarakrishna's *Samkhyakarika*, is a dualism of **purusha** (conscious witness) and **prakriti** (primal matter or nature). Purusha is pure consciousness: it does not act, change or know in the way minds do; it only witnesses. Purushas are many, one for each liberated or bound self. Prakriti is unconscious and active, made of three strands (**gunas**): **sattva** (lucidity), **rajas** (activity) and **tamas** (inertia). When prakriti is in balance nothing evolves; when the balance is disturbed it evolves into the world in an ordered series of 23 products: **buddhi** (intellect), **ahamkara** (ego-sense), **manas** (mind as coordinator of the senses), five sense capacities, five action capacities, five subtle elements and five gross elements. With purusha and prakriti, these make 25 principles.

Two features stand out. First, mind and intellect are material in Samkhya: thinking, deciding and feeling belong to prakriti, and only the witnessing of them belongs to purusha. This is unlike Descartes, for whom the mind is the thinking, non-extended substance. Second, Samkhya holds **satkaryavada**: the effect exists in a latent form in its cause (the pot in the clay), so evolution is the manifestation of what prakriti already contains. Classical Samkhya is usually read as having no creator god. Liberation is the discriminating knowledge that purusha is distinct from prakriti.

The *Samkhyakarika* gives several arguments for purusha. One, from the purposiveness of aggregates, can be set out like this:

| P1 | Every aggregate exists for the sake of something other than itself. |
| P2 | The body-mind complex is an aggregate. |
| P3 | What an aggregate exists for is not itself an aggregate. |
| C | So there is something that is not an aggregate for whose sake the body-mind complex exists. |

This is valid. It reasons from P1 and P2 to the existence of some beneficiary, and P3 then says the beneficiary is not composite. An opponent can attack **P3**: perhaps one aggregate exists for the sake of another, with no ultimate beneficiary. Samkhya replies that a chain of aggregates serving aggregates never reaches a purpose, so there must be a non-composite beneficiary, and that this beneficiary is purusha, the conscious witness.

### Advaita Vedanta: only Brahman

**Advaita** means "non-dual." Its classic exponent is Shankara, traditionally dated to around the eighth century CE (his dates are debated); its texts are commentaries on the Upanishads and the *Brahma Sutras*. The central thesis is that **Brahman**, pure consciousness and being, is the only ultimate reality, and that the **atman**, the true self in each of us, is identical with Brahman. The Upanishadic sentence usually cited is *tat tvam asi*, "you are that" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7).

If only Brahman is real, what are the many things we perceive? Advaita answers with **superimposition** (*adhyasa*): we mistake one thing for another, as someone in dim light takes a rope for a snake. The snake appears, is not nothing as an appearance, and is cancelled when we see the rope. Ignorance (**avidya**) superimposes the world's multiplicity on Brahman, and the power by which this happens is called **maya**. Later Advaitins distinguish two standpoints: the everyday (*vyavaharika*), in which the world functions and is real enough to act in, and the ultimate (*paramarthika*), in which only Brahman is real. The world is called neither being nor non-being in the full sense (*anirvacaniya*, "indefinable"). Interpreters disagree about how far Shankara himself held that the world is illusory rather than dependent on Brahman, and Advaitin schools after him (the Bhamati and Vivarana traditions) differ on details of ignorance.

A central argument is the argument from **sublation** (*badha*), the cancelling of one cognition by a later one:

| P1 | Whatever is ultimately real is never liable to sublation. |
| P2 | Everything other than Brahman is liable to sublation. |
| C | So nothing other than Brahman is ultimately real. |

It is valid by contraposition. Objectors attack **P2**: a rope-snake or a dream is sublated by waking, but nothing sublates the waking world, so the step from familiar sublations to all of the world is unjustified. The Advaitin replies that liberating knowledge of Brahman is exactly the cognition that sublates the world's multiplicity. An opponent may also attack **P1**, saying that being cancelled by a later cognition does not make an object unreal. The theistic Vedantin Ramanuja (eleventh to twelfth century) rejected the thesis that the world is illusory and held a qualified non-dualism in which the world and selves are real parts of Brahman.

### Where they differ

Samkhya has many conscious selves and an unconscious matter; Advaita has one consciousness. Samkhya takes prakriti to be real and active; Advaita takes the world to be dependent on ignorance. Both locate consciousness outside the changing mind-body complex, and both treat liberation as a kind of knowledge.

@example
title: Testing the sublation criterion on a mirage
problem: A traveler sees water on the road ahead. On arriving, she finds dry asphalt. Use the Advaita criterion to say what is sublated and what the case shows about the argument from sublation.
step: The cognition "there is water ahead" is cancelled by the later cognition "there is only dry asphalt here." By P1's criterion, the water was not ultimately real.
step: That is one case. To reach C the Advaitin needs P2: that everything other than Brahman is liable to such cancellation.
step: The mirage supports P2 only for mirages. A defender of P2 appeals to a different kind of sublation (the knowledge of Brahman), and a critic denies that the analogy extends to the whole world.
answer: The water is sublated, so it is unreal on the criterion; but the case supports P2 only for items like mirages, which is why P2 is the premise a critic attacks.

@example
title: Counting the Samkhya principles
problem: Classical Samkhya counts 25 principles. Which are conscious, which are products of prakriti, and how many of each?
step: Purusha is the only conscious principle: 1.
step: Prakriti is the unevolved matter that is not itself a product: 1.
step: The remaining principles are evolutes: 23 in all. Among them are buddhi, ahamkara, manas, five sense capacities, five action capacities, five subtle and five gross elements (3 + 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 23).
step: So the intellect and mind are among the products of prakriti, hence unconscious in themselves; their apparent awareness is due to the reflection or proximity of purusha.
answer: One conscious principle (purusha), one unevolved matter (prakriti) and 23 evolutes, including intellect and mind.

@mistakes
- Equating purusha with the Cartesian mind: in Samkhya, thinking and feeling belong to material prakriti, and purusha is only the witness.
- Saying that Advaita holds that the world is nothing at all: it holds that the world is neither fully real (it is sublated by knowledge of Brahman) nor fully unreal (it appears and functions), which is the force of "indefinable."
- Saying that Samkhya is monist like Advaita: it counts many purushas as well as prakriti.
- Treating "illusion" as a settled translation of maya: interpreters dispute how far maya makes the world illusory rather than dependent.
