@article
The Abhidharmika of week 5 says that a pot is conventional and its momentary dharmas are ultimate. Nagarjuna, who is traditionally dated to around the second century CE, asks whether the dharmas themselves can bear that weight. If each dharma has its own **intrinsic nature** (*svabhava*, "own-being"), a nature that does not depend on anything else, how can dharmas be caused, combined or changed? His answer, in the *Mulamadhyamakakarika* (the *Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way*), is that nothing has such a nature. Everything is **empty** (*shunya*) of it.

### Emptiness is not nothingness

**Emptiness** (*shunyata*) is the lack of svabhava. It does not say that nothing exists. It says that whatever exists depends on causes, parts and the concepts we use to designate it. Nagarjuna identifies dependent origination, emptiness, dependent designation and the middle way (verse 24.18). The middle way is between **eternalism**, the view that things have intrinsic natures, and **nihilism**, the view that nothing exists at all. Opponents charged him with nihilism; his reply is that emptiness is what makes anything possible, since a thing with an unchangeable own-nature could neither arise nor cease nor change.

### The argument against arising

The first chapter of the *Mulamadhyamakakarika* examines whether things with intrinsic nature could arise from causes:

| P1 | If a thing with intrinsic nature arises, then it arises from itself, from another, from both, or from no cause. |
| P2 | It does not arise from itself (it would already exist, and its production would be pointless). |
| P3 | It does not arise from another (if cause and effect each had a fixed own-nature, there would be no connection between them, and anything could produce anything). |
| P4 | It does not arise from both (that inherits the faults of both). |
| P5 | It does not arise without a cause (anything could arise anywhere at any time). |
| C | So no thing with intrinsic nature arises. |

The argument is valid by modus tollens: P2 to P5 deny each alternative in P1's consequent. It is a **tetralemma**, a four-way case analysis. A common reply, from an opponent who holds that causes and effects have svabhava, attacks **P3**: seeds and sprouts are different and yet seeds produce sprouts. Nagarjuna accepts that the seed produces the sprout, but argues that this works only if neither has intrinsic nature, since dependent things can be related while independent ones cannot. The conclusion is not that nothing arises but that nothing arises *with* svabhava.

### The self-refutation objection

An old objection, put by an opponent in Nagarjuna's *Vigrahavyavartani* ("Averting the Arguments"), holds that emptiness defeats itself:

| P1 | If everything is empty of intrinsic nature, then the statement "everything is empty" is itself empty of intrinsic nature. |
| P2 | A statement empty of intrinsic nature cannot refute anything. |
| P3 | If a thesis, were it true, would prevent itself from refuting its rivals, then the thesis is self-defeating. |
| C | So the thesis that everything is empty is self-defeating. |

This is valid: P1 and P2 give that if everything is empty, the statement cannot refute; P3 then yields the conclusion. Nagarjuna's reply attacks **P2**: refuting requires only that a statement can function dependently, as in conventional practice, and emptiness is what permits functioning. He accepts that his own statement is empty and denies that this is a problem. A different reply says that Nagarjuna asserts no thesis and so has nothing to defend, but interpreters disagree about whether that is his position.

### The two truths

Nagarjuna says (verses 24.8 to 24.10) that the Buddha's teaching rests on two truths: the **conventional** (*samvriti*) and the **ultimate** (*paramartha*). Conventionally there are tables, persons and causes, and we act in a world of them. Ultimately, all of these lack intrinsic nature. The ultimate truth is not another realm behind the world. It is the emptiness of the conventional things. A table is a good example: conventionally, the table holds cups and can be bought and sold; ultimately, it has no table-nature of its own, since it exists in dependence on its parts, its causes (wood, carpenter), and our practice of calling it a table. Emptiness is itself empty: it is not an entity but a way things are, so there is no hidden absolute.

### Interpreting Nagarjuna

Scholars disagree about what Nagarjuna holds. On one reading he is an **anti-realist** about svabhava who nonetheless accepts conventional reality (and, on several such readings, rejects the reductionism of the Abhidharma). On another he is a **skeptic** who holds no thesis. Some, including Jay Garfield and Graham Priest, have read him as accepting that some contradictions are true (a **paraconsistent** reading), which other scholars reject. The tradition divides later: Candrakirti and the school Tibetan writers call Prasangika argue by exposing absurd consequences of opponents' views; Bhaviveka and the school called Svatantrika used independent arguments. These labels come from later Tibetan classification.

### Compared with the West

Nagarjuna's rejection of intrinsic nature resembles some Western views that deny substance as an independent bearer of properties, but his target is any independent existence, including that of the parts, and the argument is an *a priori* analysis of causal relations, not a Humean appeal to experience.

@example
title: The two truths with a river
problem: Explain the two truths with the example of a river, stating what is conventionally true and what is ultimately true.
step: Conventionally, there is a river: it has a name, it flows past the town, it can be crossed by a bridge, and we can say true and false things about it.
step: Ultimately, the river has no intrinsic nature. It depends on its water, which is constantly replaced, on the banks and rainfall that shape it, and on our practice of marking some stretch of water off as "the river."
step: The ultimate truth is not that there is no river, and it does not reveal a different thing behind the river. It is the fact that the river lacks intrinsic nature, which is exactly what allows it to change and be used.
step: So the two truths are two ways of describing one thing: the river is conventionally real and ultimately empty.
answer: Conventionally there is a river that flows and can be crossed; ultimately it lacks intrinsic nature, being dependent on water, banks, rainfall and designation. Emptiness describes how the river is, not another place.

@example
title: Replying to the self-refutation objection
problem: The objection says: if everything is empty, then the statement "everything is empty" is empty and cannot refute anything. State Nagarjuna's reply and the premise it attacks.
step: The objection's premises: P1, if everything is empty then the statement is empty; P2, an empty statement cannot refute anything; P3, a thesis that would prevent itself from refuting is self-defeating; C, emptiness is self-defeating.
step: Nagarjuna accepts P1: his statement is empty too.
step: He denies P2. Something lacking intrinsic nature can still function: a reflection can be seen, a magician's illusion can be an illusion, and an empty statement can refute because refuting is a conventional, dependent activity.
step: He adds that only if functioning required svabhava would the objection succeed, and that is what he denies.
answer: The reply attacks P2: a statement can refute without having intrinsic nature, since refutation is a dependent and conventional practice.

@mistakes
- Equating emptiness with nothingness or nihilism: it is the absence of intrinsic nature in things that conventionally exist.
- Treating the ultimate truth as a separate realm: for Nagarjuna it is the emptiness of the conventional.
- Reading the argument against arising as a proof that nothing arises: it concludes that nothing arises with svabhava.
- Claiming that scholars agree on a single reading of Nagarjuna: they disagree about whether he is an anti-realist about svabhava, a skeptic or a paraconsistent thinker.
