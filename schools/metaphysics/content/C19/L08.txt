@article
Two oak seedlings grow side by side from acorns. Each becomes an oak and not a pine, and each has its own shape, size and fate. What makes both of them oaks? What makes them two oaks rather than one? And why do people, like trees, differ in character although they share a nature? The Neo-Confucians of China's Song and Ming dynasties gave an answer in terms of **li** (pattern, principle) and **qi** (vital stuff, energy).

### Background

Classical Confucianism (Confucius, Mencius, Xunzi) was mainly concerned with ethics, ritual and the cultivation of persons, though it shared the *Book of Changes* (*Yijing*) picture of a world of yin and yang transformations. Between the eleventh and seventeenth centuries, thinkers who are collectively called Neo-Confucians built a metaphysics partly in response to the challenge of Buddhism, with its doctrines of emptiness and impermanence, and Daoism, with its non-being. Zhou Dunyi (eleventh century) wrote a short text on the **Supreme Ultimate** (*taiji*). Zhang Zai (1020 to 1077) taught that the world is made of **qi**, which condenses into things and disperses again, passing through a state of great void that is not an absolute emptiness. Cheng Yi (1033 to 1107) put **li** at the center. Zhu Xi (1130 to 1200) synthesized these into the system called the Cheng-Zhu school. Wang Yangming (1472 to 1529) criticized it from within, in the "learning of the mind."

### Li and qi

**Li** is the pattern or principle that makes a thing the kind of thing it is and makes it act as it does: the li of an oak is what makes it grow as an oak. **Qi** is the stuff or energy of which things are made, which condenses and disperses, and which comes in varieties: clearer or more turbid, finer or coarser. Every concrete thing is a combination of li and qi. In Zhu Xi's system, the totality of li is the Supreme Ultimate. The principle is expressed in the slogan **"principle is one, manifestations are many"** (*li yi fen shu*), which comes from Cheng Yi and which Zhu Xi emphasized: one all-embracing pattern is realized differently in different things, as the same moral principle shows up in different relations and roles.

The two are, in a standard phrase of the school, neither separate nor mixed. In concrete things, li never exists apart from qi, but li and qi are not the same. Scholars disagree about priority: Zhu Xi says that li is in some sense prior, and that without it there would be no world, yet he also denies that there is any time at which li existed without qi. Many readers take the priority to be logical or explanatory, not temporal, and others find in him a stronger dualism.

Li has a moral dimension. A person's nature (*xing*) is li as it is present in the person, and it is good, as Mencius says. Differences in character arise from the qi in which li is embodied: clear qi lets li show through, turbid qi obstructs it. Cultivation therefore includes the **investigation of things** (*gewu*) and reverence, by which one clears one's qi and learns the patterns of things.

### The argument for li

| P1 | The transformations of qi are orderly: seeds of an oak always yield oaks, never pines. |
| P2 | If the transformations of qi are orderly, then either qi itself explains the order or something other than qi does. |
| P3 | Qi, considered simply as the stuff of change, does not explain why its transformations follow one pattern rather than another. |
| C | So something other than qi explains the order of its transformations: li. |

This is valid. The argument depends on **P3**, which later qi-emphasizing thinkers, such as Dai Zhen (1724 to 1777), reject: on their view the patterns are the qi's own internal structure, and li is only the orderly pattern of qi, not a distinct principle. Zhang Zai's emphasis on qi anticipates this, and some interpreters read Zhu Xi in a way that makes li dependent on qi. Defenders reply that qi's own character varies (clear and turbid, dense and thin) and cannot account for the sameness of pattern across all the qi that makes up all oaks.

### Wang Yangming's challenge

Wang Yangming held that li is not outside the mind: investigating bamboo outside oneself will not reveal the principle, since the principle of things is found in the mind's own moral awareness. This is a disagreement about where li is found and how it is known, not simply about whether it exists.

### Li and qi compared with form and matter

There are similarities. Both li and Aristotelian form explain why things are of a kind and act as they do, and both qi and matter are what is shaped. There are also differences. Qi is not passive or characterless: it has qualities and is dynamic, whereas Aristotle's matter is potentiality. Li is one pattern realized in many things, with a moral dimension; Aristotle's forms are the forms of particular kinds and are not usually described as one principle. Zhu Xi's li is also the source of ethical norms. The comparison is useful for each side, and must not be taken as an identity.

@example
title: Applying li and qi to a case
problem: Two people share a human nature but differ in how well they act on it. Explain, using li and qi, what Zhu Xi would say.
step: Both people have the same li: the nature that makes them human, which is good.
step: They differ in qi: one has clear qi, through which li shows readily, and the other has turbid qi, which obstructs it.
step: So the difference in character is a difference in qi, not in li. A person with turbid qi can improve by investigating things and cultivating reverence, which clears the qi.
answer: Same li, different qi: character varies with the clarity of the qi, and cultivation clears it.

@example
title: Comparing the roles in a table
problem: Compare li and qi with form and matter on four points: what explains kind, whether the stuff is characterless, whether the principle is one or many, and whether the principle has an ethical role.
step: Kind: li explains kind (what makes an oak an oak); Aristotelian form likewise explains kind. These roles match.
step: The stuff: qi has qualities (clear, turbid, dense) and moves; Aristotle's matter is what can take on form, defined by its potentiality for it. They differ.
step: One or many: Zhu Xi's li is one pattern in many manifestations (*li yi fen shu*). Aristotle's forms are of particular kinds, and one need not hold that they are one principle. They differ.
step: Ethical role: li includes the moral principles that make up human nature; Aristotle's form of a human is tied to its characteristic activity and so to a theory of human flourishing, but the two accounts of ethics differ in detail.
answer: They match on explaining kind and differ on the activity of qi, on the unity of li and on the explicitly moral character of li.

@mistakes
- Equating li with Platonic Forms: li is found in things and is never separate from qi in concrete cases, although scholars disagree about how strong its priority is.
- Treating qi as inert matter: it is dynamic and comes in varieties.
- Attributing the doctrine of "principle one, manifestations many" to Wang Yangming: it comes from Cheng Yi and was used by Zhu Xi; Wang disputes where principle is found.
- Treating Neo-Confucianism as only ethics or only metaphysics: li links the structure of things with moral nature.

@quiz self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, contrast Zhu Xi's and Wang Yangming's views on where li is found and how it is known, and say which problem of knowledge each view solves or creates.
hint: Zhu Xi recommended investigating things; Wang held that principle is not outside the mind.
solution: Zhu Xi holds that li is in each thing, so that one learns it by investigating things, one at a time, and by reverence that clears the qi. This explains how we can learn the pattern of a bamboo or of a social relation, but it raises the worry of how one's accumulated knowledge of external things becomes moral motivation. Wang Yangming says that li is not outside the mind: the principle of things is found in the mind's own moral awareness, and one need not seek it by studying external things. This ties knowledge directly to action, since knowing and acting are not separate, but it raises the worry of how the mind's awareness can be corrected by the world and of what to say about the patterns of nature. Both agree that li is real and moral, and disagree about its location and the route to it.
