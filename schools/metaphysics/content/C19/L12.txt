@article
Three ordinary things raise three questions: a pot, a person and a fire. Is the pot something over and above its parts? What makes you the same person over time, or are you not one? Does the fire make the cotton burn, or does the burning merely follow? The traditions you have studied answered each of these, and the best way to see what they can learn from each other is to put the answers side by side, question by question, and ask what argument supports each.

### A map of the questions

| Question | Views from the traditions | Western counterparts |
|---|---|---|
| Parts and wholes | Nyaya-Vaisheshika: a whole is a new entity inhering in its parts. Buddhist chariot argument: "chariot" is a designation depending on the parts. Nagarjuna: neither wholes nor parts have intrinsic nature. | The special composition question; universalism, nihilism and moderate answers. |
| Causation | Nyaya: causes necessitate effects. Al-Ghazali: necessity is not established; God's habit. Nagarjuna: nothing with intrinsic nature arises. | Aristotle's causes, Hume's regularity, necessitarian accounts of powers. |
| Persons | Buddhist no-self; Samkhya and Advaita: the self is a witness or Brahman; ubuntu: persons through persons. | Locke, Parfit, Aristotle, relational accounts. |
| The ultimate | Brahman; emptiness; the Dao; li and the Supreme Ultimate; the Necessary Existent and the primacy of existence. | God, matter, substance, the laws of nature. |

The table shows two features. First, each question receives several answers *within* a tradition (Nyaya and Buddhists disagree about wholes; Wang Yangming and Zhu Xi about li), so the traditions are not monoliths. Second, there are structural likenesses across traditions that suggest a shared problem space, such as the Buddhist and Nyaya debate and the Western debate about composition.

### What can be learned

- **New options.** Nagarjuna's two truths offer a way to say that composites are real in one sense and lack a fundamental nature in another, which clarifies debates about composition. Ubuntu-influenced accounts provide a relational view of persons that tests assumptions of individualist accounts. Sadra's primacy of existence makes one ask whether existence is a property at all. Daoist non-being makes one ask what an ontology of absences should say.
- **Hidden assumptions.** The Buddhist argument against substance and property alike shows that the Western pairing of substance and property is not forced.
- **Testing generality.** A claim that seems obvious ("causes necessitate their effects") may be local to a framework.

### The convergence argument

A tempting argument is that agreement among independent traditions supports a view:

| P1 | If traditions that argue independently reach the same conclusion, then their agreement gives some support to that conclusion. |
| P2 | Buddhist and Humean writers reach the same conclusion: that there is no unified persisting self over and above a collection of changing items. |
| P3 | Buddhist and Humean writers argued independently of each other. |
| C | So their agreement gives some support to the conclusion that there is no unified persisting self. |

This is valid. Each premise can be attacked. **P2**: the conclusions are not the same. The Buddhist keeps the conventional person, the continuity of karma and a path to liberation, and Hume's own appendix to the *Treatise* expresses dissatisfaction with his account; this is the assimilation pitfall of week 1. **P3**: some scholars have suggested channels through which Buddhist ideas could have reached Hume; the claim is disputed, and for most of the argument's historical interest it does not matter. **P1**: agreement can reflect shared cognitive habits or shared mistakes, and a conclusion may be reached from the same flawed premise in different dress, so convergence is weak evidence unless the routes really differ. The learner should note that a convergence argument, even if it succeeds, supports only "some" weight, and that it does not replace evaluating the arguments on each side.

### How to write a comparison

A three-page comparison follows a pattern.

1. **Question and thesis.** State the shared question and your thesis: for example, that al-Ghazali's and Hume's arguments agree on what observation shows and differ on what follows.
2. **Exposition of each side.** Reconstruct one argument from each tradition in standard form, with hidden premises, in each thinker's own terms.
3. **Premise-level comparison.** Say which premises are shared, which differ, and what hangs on the difference.
4. **Evaluation.** Say what is strongest and weakest in each argument, using replies from within each tradition.
5. **Scope.** Note scholarly disagreement and what you are not claiming.

Avoid the pitfalls of week 1: assimilation, essentialism and unglossed technical terms.

@example
title: Testing the convergence argument on causation
problem: Al-Ghazali and Hume agree that observation shows only succession. Does this convergence support the claim that necessary connection is not established? Identify the weakest premise.
step: Convergence argument: P1, independent agreement supports the conclusion; P2, they agree that observation shows only succession; P3, they argued independently; C, their agreement supports the claim.
step: P2 is true as far as it goes: both appeal to the fact that we observe one event after another. But they differ on what follows (al-Ghazali: the connection is not established and God's habit explains it; Hume: our idea of necessity comes from custom).
step: P3 is doubtful: there may be no historical link between the two thinkers, but independence also means independent routes, and the two arguments share a premise: that the contrary of the effect involves no contradiction, so that reason cannot supply the necessity.
step: The weakest link is therefore P3, read as independence of premises: a shared assumption means the agreement reflects one starting point, not two independent routes.
answer: The convergence gives little support beyond the shared premises about what observation and reason can show; the weakest link is whether the two routes really start from independent premises.

@example
title: Mapping two views onto one question
problem: Place the Nyaya-Vaisheshika and the Buddhist chariot views on the question of whether a whole is something over and above its parts, and state the shared question.
step: Shared question: "Is a whole, such as a cloth or a chariot, a thing distinct from its parts?"
step: Nyaya-Vaisheshika: yes, the whole is a new entity that inheres in its parts. Buddhist chariot argument: no, "chariot" is a designation that depends on the parts.
step: The premises where they differ: the realist denies the premise that the chariot is not something apart from the parts; the Buddhist accepts it.
step: Each side also has a Western counterpart in the debate about composition.
answer: The shared question is whether wholes are distinct from parts; Nyaya says yes and the Buddhist says no, and the disagreement is located in the premise about whether the whole is something apart.

@mistakes
- Treating agreement of conclusion as agreement of argument: the Buddhist and Humean views of the person rest on different premises and purposes.
- Using a table of "equivalents" as an argument: a table of likenesses is a starting point for comparison, not a result.
- Evaluating the other tradition only from the Western side: replies and objections from within each tradition should be used.
- Forgetting that a convergence argument supports a conclusion only to a degree and only if the routes are independent.

prompt: Plan a three-page comparison across two traditions you have studied. State the shared question, your thesis, the argument you would reconstruct from each tradition (name the premises in a sentence each), where the premises differ, and one strength and one weakness of each argument.
hint: Choose a pair from the map in the lesson, such as al-Ghazali and Hume or the chariot argument and the Nyaya whole.

solution: Question: does observation establish a necessary connection between cause and effect? Thesis: al-Ghazali and Hume agree on what observation shows but differ on what follows. Al-Ghazali's argument: if the necessity claim is established it is by observation or reason; observation shows only succession; reason shows no entailment; so it is not established. Hume's argument: our ideas come from impressions; we observe no impression of necessary connection in single instances; so the idea of necessity must arise from the habit of expecting the effect. The premises differ at the positive account: al-Ghazali adds God's habit, Hume adds psychological custom. Strength of al-Ghazali: a valid argument whose conclusion is modest. Weakness: a critic can say that reason grasps natures. Strength of Hume: a naturalistic explanation of our idea of necessity. Weakness: it depends on the empiricist premise about the origin of ideas, which is open to challenge.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate in four to six sentences the claim that the agreement of Nagarjuna and Hume on the absence of a fixed, independent self shows that there is no such self. Say which premise of the convergence argument is the weakest and why.
hint: Check whether the conclusions are the same and whether the routes are independent.
solution: Nagarjuna's argument is that nothing has intrinsic nature, since everything depends on causes, parts and designation; Hume's is that introspection finds only particular perceptions. They agree in doubting a fixed independent self, but their conclusions differ: Nagarjuna's concerns every thing, including the parts and emptiness itself, whereas Hume's concerns the self, and he expressed dissatisfaction with his own account. So the weakest premise is P2, that they reach the same conclusion. Even if the conclusions were the same, the convergence would support the claim only weakly, since the routes may share assumptions about what experience can show, and the argument cannot replace assessing each argument.
