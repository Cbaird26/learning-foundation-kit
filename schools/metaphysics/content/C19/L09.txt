@article
Suppose the world were created anew at every instant, so that what exists now is not the survivor of what existed a moment ago but a fresh creation that resembles it. Would anything be different? This is not an idle puzzle. It is close to the picture that many theologians of the early Islamic centuries defended, and the arguments they gave raise questions about persistence, causation and divine action that remain live.

### The kalam tradition

**Kalam** (Arabic, "speech" or "discussion") is the rational theology of Islam; its practitioners are the **mutakallimun**. Two schools are central: the **Mu'tazila** and the **Ash'ariyya** (the school of al-Ash'ari, who died in 936). They argued among themselves about divine justice, attributes and human action, but shared a method: they used metaphysical arguments about bodies and time to defend the claim that the world was created and depends on God.

### Atoms and accidents

Most kalam thinkers were **atomists**: bodies are composed of indivisible parts (*jawhar fard*, "the indivisible substance"). An atom has accidents: color, motion, rest, life, knowledge and the like, each of which is an **accident** (*'arad*), a quality that comes to be in a substance and is not itself a substance. No body exists without accidents. Many kalam thinkers also held **time atomism**: time is made up of indivisible moments. Maimonides reports their propositions, including these, in his *Guide of the Perplexed* (I.73) and attacks them. Scholars disagree about whether the kalam atoms were extended (had a minimal size) or were point-like, and the sources vary from thinker to thinker.

The Ash'arite thesis that matters here is that an accident **does not last for two moments**: when an accident exists, God creates it, and in the next moment it is gone, so that a continuing quality such as the whiteness of snow is a series of newly created accidents. Mu'tazilite thinkers generally allowed that some accidents persist, although the details vary by thinker.

### From accidents to a beginning

A standard kalam argument that bodies, and so the world, have a beginning:

| P1 | Every body is never without some accident. |
| P2 | Every accident has a beginning. |
| P3 | Whatever is never without things that have a beginning itself has a beginning. |
| P4 | Whatever has a beginning is brought into being by a cause. |
| C | So every body is brought into being by a cause. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 give that every body is never without things that began; P3 gives that every body began; P4 gives a cause. Objections attack **P3**. The philosophers (the *falasifa*), including Avicenna, held that the world is eternal, with an unending series of accidents each of which begins and each of which is replaced by the next, so that a body can be eternal even though no accident is. The kalam defenders replied that an actual infinite series of past events is impossible; Al-Ghazali would later press this in the *Tahafut* (week 10). Others attack **P4** by asking why a beginning needs a cause, or deny that the argument gives more than a cause of the bodies.

### Occasionalism

If accidents last a moment and bodies are mere collections of atoms with accidents, what is the power by which one thing affects another? **Occasionalism** is the doctrine that God directly creates every event; creatures supply only the *occasions*. A reconstruction in the spirit of the Ash'arite view (not a quotation from any one text):

| P1 | Every event is brought about either by God directly or by the power of a creature. |
| P2 | Any power of a creature would be an accident, and an accident exists for only one moment. |
| P3 | Whatever exists for only one moment cannot bring about an effect that arises after that moment. |
| P4 | Every effect arises after the moment in which its supposed cause exists. |
| C | So every event is brought about by God directly. |

It is valid: P2, P3 and P4 together exclude the power of a creature, and P1 leaves God. **P2** is the likeliest target: Mu'tazilites and the falasifa hold that some powers and natures persist. **P4** is denied by anyone who allows simultaneous causation, and **P1** by those who hold that God and creatures cooperate (a view later called concurrentism). The Ash'arites also had to say what is left of human responsibility, and their answer was **acquisition** (*kasb*): God creates the act and the human acquires it. The Mu'tazila held that humans author their own acts, mainly to preserve divine justice in punishing them.

### An objection to geometrical atomism

If a line consists of a finite number of indivisible atoms, the diagonal of a square seems to consist of as many atoms as a side, yet geometry says that the diagonal is longer than the side by a factor of the square root of 2. This is a standard objection to any atomism that takes space to be built of atoms, and the kalam reply is to deny that geometry applies to atoms.

### Comparison

Descartes (Meditation III) argued that the time of my life can be divided into parts that do not depend on one another, so that preservation requires the same power as creation: this resembles continuous creation. Malebranche (seventeenth century) is the best-known Western occasionalist. The kalam versions differ in being tied to an atomist ontology of accidents.

@example
title: Testing the geometrical objection
problem: A square is made of atoms placed 6 to a side. A walker steps along the diagonal from corner to corner, stepping from atom to atom. Compare the number of atoms along the diagonal with the geometrical length of the diagonal, measured in atom-widths.
step: Counting atoms along the diagonal of a 6-by-6 arrangement gives 6, the same as along a side.
step: Geometry gives the diagonal as $6\sqrt{2}$ atom-widths, which is about 8.5.
step: The atomist count (6) and the geometrical length (8.5) disagree, so one of the two must be given up.
step: The atomist can reject the claim that the diagonal passes through exactly 6 atoms, or can deny that Euclidean geometry applies to a world of atoms.
answer: The count is 6 atoms, the geometrical length about 8.5 atom-widths, and so the objection shows a conflict between simple atomism and Euclidean geometry.

@example
title: Locating the target of a reply
problem: A Mu'tazilite says: "Some accidents last more than one moment: the sweetness of honey endures for a while." Which premise of the argument for occasionalism does this deny?
step: The argument: P1 (God or a creature's power), P2 (a creature's power is an accident that lasts one moment), P3 (a one-moment thing cannot bring about a later effect), P4 (every effect comes after its cause's moment), C (so God).
step: The reply says that accidents can last more than one moment.
step: That denies the claim in P2 that every accident exists for only one moment. If a power can persist, P3 does not apply to it.
answer: The reply attacks P2.

@mistakes
- Equating occasionalism with the denial of regularities: occasionalists say the regularities are real, but they are due to God's consistent creating, not to powers in creatures.
- Saying that all kalam thinkers held the same views: the Mu'tazila and Ash'ariyya differed on accidents, human action and divine attributes.
- Treating the argument from accidents as invalid: it is valid; the objection is to P3 or P4.
- Taking the reconstruction of the argument for occasionalism as a quotation: it is a reconstruction in the spirit of Ash'arite doctrine.
