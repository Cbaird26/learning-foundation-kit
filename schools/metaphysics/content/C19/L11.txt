@article
A newborn child is welcomed into a family. Is she a person? On some Western accounts a person is a being with certain capacities, such as memory, reason and self-awareness, so the answer depends on when these arise. On a family of views developed in African philosophy, personhood is not just a matter of having capacities: it is bound up with a person's relations to others and with a community, and it is something a human being can grow into. This week studies those views as philosophical positions.

### Plurality and method

Africa is a continent of many peoples, languages and traditions, so "African philosophy" names a field with many sources, among them the Akan, Yoruba, Igbo and Nguni traditions, written traditions such as the scholars of Timbuktu and the Ethiopian *Hatata*, which is attributed to Zera Yacob in the seventeenth century (some scholars dispute its authorship), and contemporary academic philosophy. An early book, Tempels's *Bantu Philosophy* (1945), described a Bantu ontology of "vital force," and it was criticized for presenting a unanimous worldview of a whole people. Paulin Hountondji argued that such **ethnophilosophy** treats a supposed collective belief system as philosophy, whereas philosophy requires individual, critical argument. Henry Odera Oruka's "sage philosophy" project sought critical thinkers among individual sages. These methodological debates connect to week 1: do not attribute one view to "African thought" without naming a thinker or tradition.

### The Akan person

Kwame Gyekye and Kwasi Wiredu, both writing about the Akan of Ghana, describe the person as having several elements: the **okra** (a life-giving or soul-like element, said to be sent by God), the **sunsum** (a spirit or personality element that is the source of character and effectiveness) and the body (*honam*). They disagree about the okra. Gyekye reads it as immaterial and so as close to a soul, whereas Wiredu argues that it is better understood as quasi-physical, since the Akan do not draw the sharp division of mental and physical that the question assumes. This is a disagreement about interpretation and also about what the conceptual scheme commits us to.

### Personhood and community

The Nguni proverb *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu* says that a person is a person through other persons, and it is often linked to **ubuntu** (or **botho**), a notion of humaneness. John Mbiti summarized the outlook in a much-cited formula to the effect that the individual's existence is grounded in the group's existence (paraphrased here, not quoted); his broader generalizations about African religion and time have been criticized as overgeneralizing. Ifeanyi Menkiti argued that in many African traditions personhood is not a status that an individual has at birth by having certain capacities but something acquired in stages through incorporation into the community and moral maturation, so that one may be more or less a person, and one may fail to become one. This is a **normative** conception: "person" is partly an achievement and a standard.

An argument in this spirit:

| P1 | If personhood were possessed independently of community, then an individual would fully possess it before and apart from participating in communal life. |
| P2 | An individual does not fully possess personhood before and apart from participating in communal life. |
| C | So personhood is not possessed independently of community. |

It is valid by modus tollens. Replies attack different premises. Gyekye's **moderate communitarianism** holds that communal life shapes persons, but that persons also have intrinsic worth grounded in capacities such as rationality and moral sense, which can be exercised and used to evaluate communal values. Such a view attacks **P2**: a newborn or a person cut off from community already has a status, and the extreme view makes personhood too easy to withdraw. A different reply attacks **P1** by distinguishing **causal** from **constitutive** dependence: even if the capacities for personhood develop only through social interaction (causal dependence), once present they may give a status that is not constituted by community. The communitarian answers that the status itself, and not only its development, is constituted by relations.

### Comparisons

Locke (*Essay*, Book II, chapter 27) takes a person to be a thinking, intelligent being able to consider itself the same thinking thing at different times, which makes memory and self-consciousness central. Parfit's reductionism makes psychological connectedness central. Relational views of persons also have Western antecedents: Aristotle's claim that the human is by nature a political animal, and later accounts of recognition (Hegel) and of relational autonomy. Thaddeus Metz has developed ubuntu into a moral theory in which right action honors relationships of identity and solidarity. The contrast between African and Western accounts is therefore not a contrast of whole civilizations, but of emphasis and argument, and it is best studied question by question.

@example
title: Causal versus constitutive dependence
problem: A learner says: "A child learns language only by being raised among speakers, so personhood depends on community." Is this the claim in the argument for P2, or a different claim?
step: The learner's claim is about how the capacities arise: language develops only in community. That is **causal dependence** (community is a cause of the capacities).
step: The argument's claim is that personhood is not possessed independently of community: community constitutes the status. That would be **constitutive dependence**.
step: Causal dependence does not imply constitutive dependence: a plant needs sunlight to grow but is not constituted by sunlight. So the learner's premise does not yet support the argument's conclusion.
step: The communitarian must argue further that the status of personhood, not just its development, consists in relations.
answer: The claim is of causal dependence; the argument needs constitutive dependence, so an extra step is required.

@example
title: Descriptive and normative personhood
problem: Classify the statements "A human being is a person if she has the capacity for self-reflection" and "A human being becomes a person as she takes up the duties of a family member and neighbor" as descriptive or normative conceptions, and explain.
step: The first states a condition (a capacity) that a being has or lacks. This is a descriptive, capacity-based conception, close to Locke's.
step: The second states that personhood is attained through a process and by meeting standards of conduct. This is normative and processual, like Menkiti's view.
step: On the second, one may be more or less of a person and may fail to become one; on the first, one either has the capacity or does not.
answer: The first is descriptive and capacity-based; the second is normative and processual.

@mistakes
- Attributing one view to "all of Africa": the traditions are many, and thinkers disagree (Gyekye and Wiredu on the okra, Menkiti and Gyekye on the community).
- Treating the ubuntu proverb as a theory of persons by itself: it is a starting point for several different theories.
- Running together causal and constitutive dependence on community.
- Treating ethnophilosophy criticism as a claim that African philosophy does not exist: Hountondji criticized a method, not the field.

@practice self
prompt: Explain the difference between causal and constitutive dependence on community in three to five sentences, and say which one the communitarian needs for the argument that personhood is not independent of community.
hint: Think of what a plant needs in order to grow compared with what constitutes it.
solution: Causal dependence means that the community is a cause of something coming into being, as when children acquire language only among speakers. Constitutive dependence means that the thing is partly made up of or consists in relations to the community, so that without those relations it would not be what it is. A human could depend causally on community for developing capacities and yet, once she has them, possess a status that does not consist in her relations. The communitarian needs constitutive dependence, because the conclusion is about what personhood is and not merely about how its capacities arise. An opponent will say that this extra step is not supported by the fact that we learn in communities.
