@article
Watch a candle flame from dusk to dawn. We say it is the same flame, but it is fed by fresh wax and air at every instant, and nothing in it stays put. A Buddhist philosopher of the **Abhidharma** tradition pushes this thought as far as it will go: what we call things are continuous streams of momentary events, and a stable thing is a name for a stream. The tradition thereby supplies the metaphysical background to the no-self doctrine of week 4.

### The Abhidharma project

**Abhidharma** ("higher teaching") is the systematic analysis of experience into its ultimate constituents, called **dharmas**: momentary events of matter, feeling, thought and mental factors, each with its own characteristic. The classic synthesis is Vasubandhu's *Abhidharmakosha* (usually dated to the fourth or fifth century CE; dating is uncertain). A key idea is the distinction of two kinds of existence. Something exists **conventionally** (*samvriti-sat*) if awareness of it disappears when it is physically broken up or mentally analyzed into other things: a pot (smash it and the cognition "pot" ends) or water (analyze it into color, taste and touch). Something exists **ultimately** (*paramartha-sat*) if awareness of it survives such breaking and analysis: the color or the touch that is left. Wholes, persons and chariots are conventional; dharmas are ultimate. This is the same move as the chariot argument, now generalized into a theory of what is basic.

### Momentariness

Dharmas are **momentary** (*ksanika*): each lasts for one instant and is replaced by a successor. A continuing thing is a **stream** (*santana*), a causal series in which each moment conditions the next. What holds a stream together is causal dependence, not an underlying substance. Schools differ on time. The **Sarvastivadins** held that dharmas exist in the past, present and future (their name means "those who say everything exists"), which looks like a kind of eternalism about time, although they explain change by differences in a dharma's activity. The **Sautrantikas** denied this and held that only the present dharma exists, which looks like presentism. These comparisons with the Western debates on time are helpful, but each school had its own reasons.

### The argument from causal efficacy

A well-known argument, developed by Dharmakirti and his successors, ties momentariness to what it is to be real:

| P1 | Whatever is real is causally efficient (it can produce effects). |
| P2 | Whatever is causally efficient is not unchanging. |
| P3 | Whatever is not unchanging is momentary (replaced from moment to moment). |
| C | So whatever is real is momentary. |

It is valid: P1, P2 and P3 chain together. The support for P2 is a dilemma. Suppose an unchanging thing produces its effects. Either it produces all of them at once, in which case it has nothing left to do, or it produces them in succession, which means that it changes between the moments, since it acts differently at different times. In either case it is not both unchanging and efficient. The support for P3 is the Buddhist claim that destruction needs no cause: a thing perishes by its own nature, so if it were not destroyed at the very next moment, nothing would ever destroy it.

Realist critics, especially Nyaya, attack different premises. The reply that an unchanging self or substance can produce effects, with the help of auxiliary conditions that arrive at different times, attacks **P2**. The reply that a jar can change in some respects and yet be the same jar, as when we recognize it as "the same jar I saw yesterday," attacks **P3**, since it denies that change requires replacement. Recognition is the main Nyaya evidence for lasting things, and the Buddhist reply is that recognition is the work of a similar later moment together with memory, which wrongly presents it as the same.

### Problems and replies

A theory of momentary dharmas must still explain how memory, action and karmic consequence link moments to one another. Abhidharma answers with the causal continuity of the stream. A later school, Yogacara, posited a store consciousness (*alaya-vijnana*) to carry dispositions between moments. Critics ask whether causal continuity is enough to account for ownership of an action's results, and some Buddhists, such as the Pudgalavadins, answered that it is not.

### Comparisons

Process philosophies in the West, such as Heraclitus's flux and Whitehead's process thought, also deny that stable substances are basic. Perdurantists hold that a thing is a four-dimensional whole made of temporal parts. The Buddhist view differs from perdurantism in saying that the whole is only a convention: the real items are the momentary dharmas and their causal relations.

@example
title: The Abhidharma criterion applied
problem: Apply the criterion of conventional and ultimate existence to a forest, a single leaf's green color and a sentence.
step: The criterion asks: does awareness of the thing disappear when it is broken up physically or analyzed mentally?
step: A forest: remove the trees and the cognition "forest" is gone; mentally divide it into trees and it dissolves into the cognition of trees. So a forest is conventional.
step: A sentence: break it into words or analyze it into sounds, and the awareness "this sentence" is gone. A sentence is conventional too.
step: The green of the leaf: it is not further analyzable into other kinds of thing in the way the forest is, so awareness of it persists under analysis. It is a candidate for an ultimate (dharma-level) item.
answer: The forest and the sentence are conventional; the green color is a candidate for an ultimate dharma.

@example
title: The dilemma for a permanent cause
problem: Reconstruct the dilemma behind premise P2 of the argument for momentariness and say how a Nyaya critic responds.
step: Suppose a permanent, unchanging thing T is efficient. Then T either produces all its effects at once or in succession.
step: If all at once, T has nothing left to do afterward, so it is not efficient at later times; yet it persists unchanged, which makes its later inactivity unexplained.
step: If in succession, T acts differently at different times, so T is not unchanging (or the difference of timing is due to something other than T).
step: A Nyaya critic denies the second horn: T can act in succession because the auxiliary conditions it needs arrive at different times, while T itself stays the same. The Buddhist replies that T's efficacy then depends on those conditions, not on T.
answer: The dilemma concludes that an unchanging thing cannot be efficient; the Nyaya critic attacks the premise that succession of effects requires change in the cause.

@mistakes
- Treating momentary as meaning unreal: the dharmas are the ultimately real items, and it is stable things that are conventional.
- Saying that Abhidharma denies causation: it says that causal conditioning between momentary events replaces a persisting substance.
- Equating the Sarvastivada with presentism: the school held that past, present and future dharmas all exist, which is closer to eternalism.
- Treating the argument from causal efficacy as invalid: it is valid, and critics attack P2 or P3.

@practice self
prompt: In three to five sentences, explain how the Abhidharma doctrine of momentary dharmas supports the Buddhist doctrine of no-self, and state one question that a defender of a persisting self would press.
hint: Recall that the person is a stream of momentary events.
solution: If the ultimately real items are momentary dharmas, then there are no persisting substances, and a person is only a stream of such events connected by causal dependence. The self, taken as a single enduring owner of experiences, is then a conventional designation for a stream, like the chariot for its parts. This gives the chariot argument a general theory of what is basic. A defender of a persisting self would press the question of what unifies the moments: if each dharma lasts an instant, what makes this moment of awareness mine rather than someone else's, and how does memory link moments that never coexist? The Buddhist answer appeals to causal continuity within a stream, and the defender will ask whether that is enough.
