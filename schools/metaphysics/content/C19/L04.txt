@article
Point to yourself. Are you pointing at your body, at the stream of feelings, at your memories, at the awareness doing the pointing? Early Buddhist teachers asked this question in earnest and answered that you will not find a self in any of these, and yet that there is a person who lives, acts and is responsible. The doctrine is **anatman** (Pali *anatta*), "not-self" or "no-self."

### The five aggregates

Buddhist analysis divides a person into five **aggregates** (*skandhas*): **form** (the body and matter), **feeling** (pleasant, painful or neutral), **perception** (recognizing and labelling), **volitional formations** (intentions, dispositions, habits) and **consciousness** (awareness through the senses and the mind). The claim is that these, and their causal interactions, are all there is to a person: nothing permanent and unitary stands behind them. Scholars of the early texts disagree about how to read this. On one reading the texts deny that there is any self at all; on another they refuse only to identify the self with any aggregate, and decline to say more. Later Buddhist schools mostly took the first reading. One group of schools, the Pudgalavadins, held that there is a "person" (*pudgala*) neither identical to nor different from the aggregates, and other Buddhists rejected this as a covert self.

### The chariot argument

In the *Milindapanha* ("Questions of King Milinda"), the monk Nagasena explains to the king that "Nagasena" is only a name, and uses a chariot as the model. The argument can be set out for the chariot first:

| P1 | If "chariot" names something over and above its parts, then that thing is identical to one of the parts, or identical to the collection of the parts, or something apart from the parts. |
| P2 | The chariot is not identical to any single part (not the axle, the wheels or the pole). |
| P3 | The chariot is not identical to the collection of the parts. |
| P4 | The chariot is not something apart from the parts. |
| C | So "chariot" does not name something over and above its parts: it is a conventional designation. |

The argument is valid: P1 is a conditional whose antecedent is denied by contraposition, since P2, P3 and P4 deny each disjunct of its consequent. Applied to persons, "I" or "Nagasena" would name something over and above the aggregates only if it were identical to one aggregate, to their collection or to something apart; none holds, so the word is a designation (*prajnapti*) that depends on the aggregates. This does not make persons disappear: the conventional person is as real as the chariot you ride in. What goes is the extra, unified, permanent entity.

Replies attack different premises. A philosopher who says that a chariot is the parts *organized* so as to carry a rider, and that this organization is real, attacks **P3**: the "collection" is not a heap but a structured whole. The Nyaya-Vaisheshika realist who holds that a whole is a new entity inhering in its parts attacks **P4**. Someone who accepts the conclusion for chariots but resists it for persons denies P1's application to selves, claiming that the person is unlike a chariot because it has a unity of consciousness.

### The control argument

Another argument comes from the early discourse on non-self (the Anattalakkhana Sutta). If an aggregate were the self, it would be under the owner's control: one could say "let my body be thus." But no aggregate is under that control. So none is the self. As a valid argument: P1, if an aggregate were the self, we could control it; P2, we cannot control any aggregate; so no aggregate is the self. The replies attack P1, saying that being the self does not require control.

### Dependent origination

If there is no unchanging self, what makes a life continuous? **Dependent origination** (*pratityasamutpada*): everything arises in dependence on conditions, summarized as: when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. The standard list has **twelve links**: ignorance, volitional formations, consciousness, name-and-form, the six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and aging-and-death. Ignorance conditions formations, which condition consciousness, and so on, so that the chain explains suffering without a self. The traditions disagree about how to read the chain: as spanning three lives, as describing the structure of a single moment of experience, or both.

### Hume and Parfit

Hume (Treatise 1.4.6) reports that when he looks within he finds only particular perceptions, never a self, and concludes that a person is a bundle of perceptions linked by relations. Parfit later held that personal identity is not what matters and remarked on the resemblance to Buddhism. The resemblances are real, but there are differences: the Buddhist argument is embedded in a theory of causation and of liberation, includes the claim that clinging to a self is the root of suffering, and denies that even the bundle is a unified thing.

@example
title: The control argument stated and tested
problem: Reconstruct the control argument for the aggregate of feeling and say which premise a critic would deny.
step: Write the argument: P1, if feeling were the self, then one could control it; P2, one cannot control feeling (one cannot choose to feel no pain on demand); C, feeling is not the self.
step: Validity: this is modus tollens. P1 is "if self then controllable," P2 denies the consequent, so C denies the antecedent.
step: A critic can deny P1, saying that the self need not be a controller: it may be a subject that undergoes feelings. Or the critic can deny P2, saying that people exert some control over their feelings.
step: The Buddhist response to the first is that an owner of the aggregates should have mastery over them, and to the second that partial influence is not the control that selfhood would require.
answer: The argument is valid; the likeliest target is P1, the claim that selfhood implies control.

@example
title: Reading the chain of dependent origination
problem: In the standard list of twelve links, ignorance is the first and aging-and-death the last. Use the principle "when this exists, that comes to be" to read the stretch from craving to becoming.
step: The links in order: ignorance, formations, consciousness, name-and-form, six sense bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, aging-and-death.
step: Craving is the eighth link and clinging is the ninth, becoming the tenth. Feeling conditions craving, craving conditions clinging, and clinging conditions becoming.
step: Read the principle on the middle links: when feeling exists, craving comes to be; when craving exists, clinging comes to be; when clinging exists, becoming comes to be. If craving ceased, clinging would not arise, and the stretch from clinging onward would cease.
answer: Craving conditions clinging, and clinging conditions becoming: each link depends on the one before, so removing a condition stops what depends on it.

@mistakes
- Saying that Buddhism denies that persons exist in any sense: the person is a conventional designation depending on the aggregates, as real as a chariot.
- Confusing anatman with the claim that the aggregates are permanent or that consciousness is the self: the texts reject identifying the self with any aggregate.
- Treating the chariot argument as invalid: it is valid; critics attack P3 or P4, or the application to persons.
- Assuming Hume and the Buddhist traditions are the same: the bundle view resembles the no-self doctrine, but the arguments and aims differ.
