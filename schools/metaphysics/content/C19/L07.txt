@article
A wheel is made of spokes, a bowl of clay, a room of walls and a door. Yet a passage in the *Daodejing* observes that what makes the wheel turn, the bowl hold and the room usable is the empty space in them. We think of the clay and the walls as what there is, and of the emptiness as a mere lack. The text turns this around: what is *not* there is what the useful thing uses. This image introduces the deepest idea in Daoist metaphysics, that **non-being** (*wu*) is not nothing in the sense of a mere absence, and that it is somehow prior to **being** (*you*).

### The texts

The *Daodejing* is traditionally attributed to Laozi, and the *Zhuangzi* to Zhuang Zhou. Scholars disagree about authorship and date: the *Daodejing* took shape in the Warring States period, probably from earlier sayings, and its early manuscripts (the Guodian bamboo slips from around 300 BCE and the Mawangdui silk copies from the second century BCE) differ from the received text. The *Zhuangzi* is generally taken to be the work of several hands, with the "inner chapters" usually thought earliest.

### The Dao, being and non-being

**Dao** means "way" or "path," and comes to mean both the way things go and whatever the way comes from. *You* and *wu* are literally "having" and "lacking," and are usually translated "being" and "non-being." The *Daodejing* says that the things of the world arise from *you* and that *you* arises from *wu*. Interpreters disagree about what this means. One reading takes *wu* to be a literal nothing from which things arise. A second takes it to be the undifferentiated: not a nothing but the absence of any particular, determinate feature. The commentator Wang Bi (third century CE) develops the second reading, treating *wu* as the ground of the particular things.

### The ineffability argument

The opening line of the *Daodejing* is often read as saying that the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao. An argument that supports this reading:

| P1 | Any name that picks out something does so by marking it off from other things. |
| P2 | Whatever is marked off from other things is a determinate thing among others. |
| P3 | The Dao is not a determinate thing among others. |
| C | So no name picks out the Dao. |

It is valid: names pick out only determinate things, the Dao is not one, so no name picks it out. Replies attack **P1**: a description such as "the source of all things" picks out something without marking it off as one thing among others. An objection to the argument as a whole is that the text itself uses the word "Dao." The text's own reply is that "Dao" is a makeshift pointer used reluctantly (chapter 25 says it is forced to give it a name), which in effect restricts P1 to names of ordinary things. Interpreters also disagree about the line itself: on some readings it is about ineffability, on others it says only that a way that can be fixed in words and followed rigidly is not the constant way, which would make the claim about the limits of fixed guidance.

### Change and spontaneity

For Daoism the world is in constant **transformation** (*hua*). The *Zhuangzi* illustrates this in its story of a man who dreams that he is a butterfly and wakes unsure whether he is a man who dreamt of being a butterfly or a butterfly dreaming of being a man. Taken as a point about the fluidity of distinctions, it asks whether our fixed categories of self, dream and waking are as firm as they seem. The text elsewhere recommends seeing things from many perspectives, and interpreters disagree about whether this is skepticism, relativism or a practical attitude.

Daoists also speak of **ziran**, "so of itself" or spontaneity, and of **wu wei**, "non-doing." Wu wei is not doing nothing: it is acting without forcing, in accord with the way things go, as a skilled swimmer follows the current. The metaphysical support is the Dao's own way: it produces and sustains things without striving or commanding them.

### Wang Bi and Guo Xiang

Wang Bi held that wu is the ground that makes the things of the world possible. Guo Xiang (died 312 CE) rejected this: non-being cannot generate anything, he argued, so things transform themselves (*duhua*, "self-transformation") with no source. This is a real disagreement about whether the world has a source.

### Compared with the West

The Dao has been compared with negative theology and with Plotinus's One, which is beyond being and description, and the *wu* of Wang Bi with Anaximander's boundless. The comparisons are suggestive, but the Dao is not generally treated as a creator who wills, so each comparison should name its differences.

@example
title: An argument for wu as the source
problem: Reconstruct an argument in the spirit of Wang Bi's commentary for the claim that the source of all things is not itself a determinate thing, and say who might reject it.
step: Suppose the source were determinate, say hot. Then it would exclude the opposite, cold. P1: If the source of all things were a determinate thing, then it would exclude some determinations.
step: P2: If the source excluded some determinations, it could not give rise to things with those determinations. P3: There is a source of all things, and it gives rise to things with every determination. C: so the source is not a determinate thing.
step: The argument is valid by two applications of modus tollens: P3 and P2 give that the source excludes no determination; with P1, it is not a determinate thing.
step: Guo Xiang attacks P3: he denies that there is a source at all, since things transform themselves. A critic can also attack P2 by saying that a determinate source could yield opposites at different times.
answer: The source must be undetermined (wu). Guo Xiang attacks P3, and another critic might attack P2.

@example
title: Two readings of the opening line
problem: The line "the Dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao" can be read as ineffability or as the limits of fixed guidance. Explain what each reading claims and what evidence is relevant.
step: The ineffability reading: the Dao is beyond description, so any word fails to capture it. It fits the claims that the Dao is without determinate features and that the text uses "Dao" only provisionally.
step: The fixed-guidance reading: the "way" in question is a path to follow, and the line says that a way written down in a code or doctrine is not the constant way, since the right way adapts to circumstances. It fits the text's emphasis on wu wei and its criticism of rigid rules.
step: The words support both readings, and interpreters disagree, so the best course is to state both, note which passages support each, and say which you adopt and why.
answer: One reading is about the limits of words (ineffability), the other about the limits of fixed guidance; the line is compatible with both.

@mistakes
- Equating wu wei with doing nothing: it is acting without forcing, in harmony with how things are.
- Reading wu as plain nothingness without noting the interpretations: some take it as the undifferentiated, not a literal nothing.
- Saying that the Daodejing was written by one author at one time: authorship and date are disputed and the early manuscripts differ from the received text.
- Treating the Dao as a god who creates by will: the text does not present it that way.

prompt: In four to six sentences, explain how the idea that the Dao works without striving supports the ethical and political ideal of wu wei, and state one objection to the inference from a metaphysical claim to a way of acting.
hint: Think about what it would mean for a person to imitate the way the Dao operates.
solution: The Dao is said to produce and sustain all things without commanding or forcing them, so a person who wants to act well is invited to follow the same pattern: to respond to circumstances as they are, without imposing a fixed plan on them. A ruler who governs this way interferes little and lets things take their course, and a craftsperson who acts skillfully follows the grain of the material rather than fighting it. The inference is from how the world is to how one should act. An objection is that it is a version of the is-ought gap: that the Dao works without striving does not by itself show that people ought to, since human aims may require effort and planning that the Dao does not need. A defender can reply that the claim is about effective action, not obligation: forcing things works against the way they go and so tends to fail.
