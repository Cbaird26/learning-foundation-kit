You will practice the method of the course: a shared question, accurate attribution, arguments in standard form with hidden premises, and replies matched to the premises they attack.
goal: Reconstruct one argument from a non-Western tradition in standard form so that it is valid as stated, and explain it in the tradition's own terms.
goal: Set it beside a Western view of the same question and locate the premise at which the two views differ.

step: Choose one topic from the list in the material below (the Buddhist argument for no-self, Nagarjuna on causation, al-Ghazali on causation, or a Neo-Confucian account of li and qi) and the Western view you will compare it with. State the shared question in one neutral sentence.
step: Read the course lesson on your topic and the relevant encyclopedia entries (the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy), and search PhilPapers for one or two scholarly articles. Note the technical terms and where scholars disagree. Cite everything you use.
step: Reconstruct the non-Western argument in standard form (P1, P2, ..., C), adding the hidden premise that makes it valid. Check validity by asking whether the conclusion could be false while the premises are true.
step: Do the same for the Western view. Then make a short table of the premises of the two arguments and mark which are shared, which differ and which are about different things.
step: Draft the paper in five parts: question and thesis (about 300 words), the non-Western argument (about 600), the Western argument (about 600), premise-level comparison (about 500) and evaluation with strongest objections and replies (about 500).
step: Revise for accuracy and fairness. ## Week-by-week key ideas

**Week 1, methods.** Compare question first, with named attributions. Pitfalls: assimilation, exoticizing, essentialism, anachronism, translation drift. The "not philosophy" argument is valid; its premises are in doubt.

**Week 2, Nyaya-Vaisheshika.** Seven categories in the later lists (early Vaisheshika lists six, and absence is added later): substance, quality, motion, universal, particularity, inherence, absence. Nine substances. Inherence versus contact. The argument for universals (the nominalist attacks P2). Comparison with Aristotle's ten categories.

**Week 3, Samkhya and Advaita.** Purusha and prakriti; 25 principles; satkaryavada. Advaita: Brahman, atman, superimposition, sublation, two standpoints. Ramanuja's objection.

**Week 4, Buddhist no-self.** Five aggregates, the chariot argument (P3 and P4 are the targets), the control argument, twelve links, comparison with Hume.

**Week 5, momentariness.** Abhidharma, conventional and ultimate existence, Sarvastivada versus Sautrantika, the argument from causal efficacy (Nyaya attacks P2 or P3).

**Week 6, Nagarjuna.** Emptiness as the absence of svabhava, the tetralemma, the self-refutation objection (reply: P2 is denied), the two truths.

**Week 7, Daoism.** Dao, you and wu, the ineffability argument, wu wei, Wang Bi versus Guo Xiang.

**Week 8, Neo-Confucianism.** Li and qi, the Supreme Ultimate, Zhang Zai, Zhu Xi, Wang Yangming, comparison with form and matter.

**Week 9, kalam.** Atoms and accidents, the argument from accidents, occasionalism, kasb, Mu'tazila versus Ash'ariyya.

**Week 10, al-Ghazali and Mulla Sadra.** Necessary connection not established; comparison with Hume. Avicenna's essence-existence argument; Sadra's primacy of existence, gradation, substantial motion.

**Week 11, African philosophy.** Okra and sunsum; Menkiti and Gyekye; ubuntu; causal versus constitutive dependence; ethnophilosophy.

**Week 12, comparison.** Question-first mapping, the convergence argument, how to write a comparison.

## Key distinctions

- Valid versus sound; which premise a reply attacks.
- Conventional versus ultimate existence.
- Causal versus constitutive dependence.
- Descriptive versus normative personhood.
- Report of scholarly disagreement versus choosing a reading silently.

## Self-check

- Can you state each tradition's central argument in standard form and name the premise each reply denies?
- Can you gloss the technical terms (svabhava, samavaya, li, qi, kasb, wujud, ubuntu) in a sentence?
- Can you compare two views at the level of premises?

## Common errors

- Equating emptiness with nothingness, occasionalism with the denial of regularity, wu wei with doing nothing, or li with Platonic Forms.
- Treating one thinker's view as the view of a whole civilization.
- Declaring a winner without argument.

@exam self
prompt: In a timed essay of about 500 words, compare the Buddhist chariot argument and the Nyaya-Vaisheshika view of wholes. Reconstruct the chariot argument in standard form, state the Nyaya-Vaisheshika view and the premise it denies, evaluate each side with its best objection and reply, and say what a careful comparison should avoid.
hint: Start with a shared question about whether a whole is something over and above its parts.
hint: Identify which premise of the chariot argument the realist denies and what each side says about inherence and designation.
solution: Question: is a whole, such as a cloth or a chariot, something over and above its parts? The chariot argument: P1, if "chariot" names something over and above its parts, then it is identical to a part, to the collection, or to something apart from them; P2, it is not identical to any single part; P3, it is not identical to the collection; P4, it is not something apart from the parts; C, so "chariot" is a conventional designation depending on the parts. It is valid by modus tollens. Nyaya-Vaisheshika holds that a whole is a new entity (avayavin) that inheres in its parts by inherence, so it is something over and above them: this denies P4. A second reply, that the parts organized so as to carry a rider are the chariot, denies P3. The best objection to the realist is that a whole as an additional entity seems redundant and invites the question of what inherence is; the realist replies that wholes have properties their parts lack, such as being able to cover a body, and that inherence is a single primitive relation. The best objection to the Buddhist is that the organization is real and not mere convention; the Buddhist replies that organization is a further feature of the parts and not an extra thing, and that the designation is explained by dependence on the parts. A careful comparison attributes each view to its school, avoids assimilating either to a Western mereology, glosses terms such as avayavin and prajnapti, and notes that the debate continued among later Indian philosophers.
rubric: Reconstructs the chariot argument correctly in standard form and says that it is valid.
rubric: States the Nyaya-Vaisheshika view and names the premise it denies (P4), with the organization reply targeting P3.
rubric: Gives the best objection and reply for each side.
rubric: Says what a careful comparison should avoid (assimilation, essentialism, unglossed terms) and notes scholarly or later debate.
