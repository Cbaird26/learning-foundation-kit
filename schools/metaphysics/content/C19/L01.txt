@article
A student reads that the Sanskrit word *brahman* is "God," that *dharma* is "duty" or "religion" or "law," and that the Chinese word *li* is "principle." Each gloss is partly right and partly misleading, and each brings with it a whole Western framework. Before you study what Indian, Chinese, Islamic and African thinkers argued about being, substance, causation and persons, you need a method for comparing them that is fair to the sources and does not simply read them as bad versions of Plato or Hume.

### Why compare at all

Metaphysics asks what exists and what it is like. If a question is a good one, such as what makes a thing the same thing over time or whether causes necessitate their effects, it would be odd if only one cultural lineage had anything to say about it. Comparison does three jobs: it shows you arguments you would not have invented, it tests the claim that a view is "obvious" by showing sophisticated people who denied it, and it clarifies your own tradition's assumptions. "Western" and "non-Western" are rough labels used in this course for convenience. They hide a great deal: Greek philosophy was developed in the Islamic world in Arabic and returned to Latin Europe through translation, so Avicenna and Averroes belong to the history of Western philosophy as well as to Islamic philosophy.

### Is it philosophy?

Some readers have held that a tradition is not philosophy if it is entangled with religion. Here is the argument in standard form:

| P1 | A body of thought counts as philosophy only if its arguments do not depend on religious commitments. |
| P2 | The arguments of the Indian traditions depend on religious commitments. |
| C | So the Indian traditions are not philosophy. |

The argument is valid: from P1 and P2, the Indian traditions fail the necessary condition in P1. Its soundness is the issue. A reply that points to medieval Christian, Jewish and Islamic thinkers, whose arguments often use theological premises yet who are standardly studied as philosophers, attacks **P1**: the criterion would exclude much of the Western canon too, so it is not the criterion we actually use. A reply that points to schools whose methods do not presuppose a creed attacks **P2**. Nyaya philosophers, for example, give an account of the sources of knowledge, of inference, of fallacies and of the rules of debate that opponents of every religious stripe could use. P2 generalizes over many centuries and schools: Indian thought includes materialists (the Carvaka or Lokayata school), Buddhists, Jains and theists, who argued with each other using reasons.

### Pitfalls

- **Assimilation**: collapsing a view into a Western one because of surface similarity ("Nagarjuna is just Hume"). Similarities are real and worth noting, but the arguments, aims and background assumptions differ.
- **Exoticizing**: treating a tradition as mystical or intuitive rather than argumentative, which makes it a curiosity instead of a conversation partner.
- **Essentialism**: attributing one view to "the Indian mind" or "African thought." Traditions contain schools that disagree sharply; much Indian philosophy is a debate between realists (Nyaya-Vaisheshika) and their Buddhist opponents.
- **Anachronism and neglect of history**: reading a modern problem back into an ancient text, or ignoring influence between traditions (Buddhism's entry into China shaped Neo-Confucianism, a theme in week 8).
- **Translation drift**: letting one English word stand for a family of senses. A good habit is to keep the technical term and give a short gloss.

### A method

1. **Start from a question**, stated neutrally enough that each side can answer it: "What, if anything, makes a person at one time the same as a person at another?" The shared question is the **tertium comparationis**, the third thing in common that makes comparison possible.
2. **Attribute carefully.** Say "the Nyaya school" or "Nagarjuna" or "Zhu Xi" only when you are sure; otherwise say "one tradition holds."
3. **Reconstruct arguments** in standard form, adding hidden premises, so that each reply can be matched with the premise it denies.
4. **Interpret on the tradition's own terms first**, then evaluate. Where scholars disagree about what a text means, report that as a disagreement rather than picking a reading silently.
5. **Compare differences as well as likenesses**, and do not treat the Western view as the standard that others are measured against: premises and validity can be checked without assuming either tradition's metaphysics.

Treat each tradition as you would treat Plato or Descartes: as a source of arguments that may be sound or unsound, and whose conclusions you are free to reject after understanding them.

@example
title: Testing the "not philosophy" argument
problem: A classmate replies to the argument that Indian thought is not philosophy: "Aquinas's proofs of God start from religious premises, and no one says Aquinas is not a philosopher." Which premise of the argument does this reply attack, and does it show that the conclusion is false?
step: Restate the argument: P1 (philosophy requires argument independent of religious commitment), P2 (Indian arguments depend on religious commitment), C (so Indian thought is not philosophy).
step: The reply says that a thinker whose arguments use religious premises is still a philosopher. That denies the necessary condition in P1, since Aquinas would fail it yet is counted as a philosopher.
step: The reply does not touch P2, so it does not by itself show that Indian thought is philosophy; it shows only that this argument fails, because P1 is not a criterion anyone applies consistently.
step: To show that the conclusion is false you would need a positive case, such as a reconstruction of an Indian argument whose premises are open to any reasoner. The rest of the course supplies such cases.
answer: The reply attacks P1. It shows the argument is unsound as it stands; it does not settle the status of Indian thought by itself.

@example
title: Diagnosing a comparison
problem: A student writes: "Nagarjuna's emptiness is basically Hume's skepticism, so there is nothing new in it." Identify the pitfall and rewrite the claim as a fairer comparison.
step: The sentence moves from a resemblance (both deny that we can find a certain kind of thing in experience) to an identity, and uses the Western view as the standard that makes the other redundant. That is **assimilation**, with a touch of anachronism.
step: Find the differences. Nagarjuna's target is intrinsic nature (svabhava) in anything, not only necessary connections; his arguments are aimed at views held by other Indian schools; and his aim is soteriological as well as philosophical.
step: State a shared question: "Do things have natures of their own, independent of other things?" Then say what each thinker's answer is and what argument supports it.
step: A fairer sentence: "Nagarjuna and Hume both resist the idea that there is an independent, intrinsic something behind what we observe, but they argue for it differently and generalize differently; comparing them shows what each argument can and cannot establish."
answer: The pitfall is assimilation; the fix is a question-first comparison that attributes each view accurately and keeps both the likenesses and the differences.

@mistakes
- Treating "valid" as "true": the not-philosophy argument is valid; what is wrong with it is a premise.
- Taking a family resemblance for an identity of doctrine, or one school's view for the view of a whole civilization.
- Using one English word for a technical term without a gloss, so that "dharma" or "li" silently carries a Western meaning.
- Thinking that a respectful comparison requires agreeing with the tradition; respect means reconstructing the argument accurately and then assessing it.

prompt: Which of these sentences commit a pitfall of comparison? Select all that apply.

prompt: Choose two views from different traditions on one metaphysical question (for example, whether things have natures of their own). State a neutral question, say in one sentence per view what each tradition or school holds, and name one difference that a hasty comparison might miss.
hint: Start with the question, then attribute each answer to a named school or thinker you are sure about.
solution: Question: "Does anything have a nature of its own, independent of everything else?" The Nyaya-Vaisheshika school holds that substances such as a pot have qualities that inhere in them and that universals such as potness are real and shared. Nagarjuna argues that nothing has intrinsic nature (svabhava), since whatever exists depends on causes, parts and designation. A hasty comparison might say that both are theories of properties; the difference it would miss is that Nagarjuna's argument is aimed at the very idea of an independent own-nature, which the Abhidharma analysts, Nagarjuna's main Buddhist target, treated as the mark of what is ultimately real.
