@article
Two globes joined by a taut cord are alone in an otherwise empty Newtonian universe. In one possible history they turn about their midpoint, and the cord is taut. In another they do not turn, and the cord is slack. In both histories the distance between the globes is the same at every moment, and there is nothing else to be at a distance from. Newton drew from this case in the *Principia* an argument that motion is not just relative to bodies. If he is right, there is something, space, relative to which the globes turn.

### The positions

**Substantivalism** holds that spacetime (or space, or its points and regions) exists as an entity in its own right, not reducible to relations among bodies and fields. Variants differ on the entity: the **manifold** of points; the manifold with its metric field; or, in **supersubstantivalism**, the material objects themselves are spacetime regions (Schaffer defends a version). **Relationalism** holds that there are only bodies and fields and the spatiotemporal relations among them; talk of space is talk of those relations. **Leibnizian** relationalists allow only relations among bodies; **Machian** relationalists (the program of Barbour and Bertotti) formulate dynamics with only relative configurations as physical data. A third approach, the **dynamical** or **functional** view associated with Harvey Brown's *Physical Relativity* (2005), says that spacetime structure is a codification of how matter behaves, so asking whether it exists "over and above" matter is the wrong question.

The positions are tied to the structure each theory ascribes to spacetime:

| Spacetime | Absolute velocity | Absolute acceleration | Light-cone structure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Newtonian (absolute space) | Yes | Yes | No |
| Galilean (neo-Newtonian) | No | Yes | No |
| Minkowski | No | Yes | Yes |

In Galilean spacetime there is absolute simultaneity and absolute spatial distance between simultaneous events, and a notion of straight (unaccelerated) paths, but no fact about which inertial frame is at rest. In Minkowski spacetime the invariant is the **interval**. In a coordinate system with one space dimension, with signature $(+,-,-,-)$ and speed of light $c = 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s, the squared interval between two events is
$$I^2 = c^2\,\Delta t^2 - \Delta x^2.$$
If $I^2 > 0$ the events are **timelike** separated, and the **proper time** along a straight path joining them is $\tau = I/c$. If $I^2 < 0$ they are **spacelike** separated; if $I^2 = 0$, lightlike. Every inertial observer computes the same $I^2$.

### Leibniz's shift argument

Leibniz and Clarke exchanged letters in 1715–16 on exactly this issue. Leibniz's shift argument can be stated validly as follows (the Principle of Sufficient Reason or the identity of indiscernibles is behind P3):

| P1 | If substantivalism is true, the world in which every body and event is shifted three meters east is a distinct possibility from the actual world. |
| P2 | If the shifted world is a distinct possibility from the actual world, then two possibilities differ in a way that makes no difference to any spatial relation among bodies and no difference to any possible observation. |
| P3 | No two possibilities differ in a way that makes no difference to any relation among bodies and none to any possible observation. |
| C | So substantivalism is false. |

It is valid: P1 and P2 give "if substantivalism then such a difference exists" and P3 denies that. The replies target different premises. Against **P1**, a substantivalist says the shifted world is the same possibility described twice: there is no haecceitistic difference between worlds that differ only in which point plays which role (this is the anti-haecceitist, "sophisticated" substantivalism of the kind Pooley and others discuss, and the hole argument next week turns on it). Against **P3**, a substantivalist notes that P3 is a version of verificationism or of the Principle of Sufficient Reason, which many reject.

### Newton's globes, and Mach

The argument against relationalism from the globes runs as follows.

| P1 | If relationalism is true, then facts about inertial effects, such as the tension in a cord, supervene on the relative distances and motions of bodies. |
| P2 | In Newtonian mechanics, the tension in the cord does not supervene on the relative distances and motions of the globes: models with the same relative distances at all times differ in tension. |
| C | So relationalism is not true as an account of Newtonian physics. |

This is valid by modus tollens. A **Machian** answers against P2: Newtonian mechanics should be reformulated so that only relative configurations are physical, accepting some changes (for example, that the total angular momentum of the universe must vanish). A relationalist can also grant P2 and take the lesson to be that Newtonian theory is wrong about the world, not that space is a substance. Whether general relativity vindicates Mach, whose "principle" has many non-equivalent formulations, is contested.

### Where the discussion stands

Which view survives depends on which theory you take as your target. For Minkowski spacetime, Brown's dynamical view and neo-Leibnizian relationalists give different accounts of what the interval is. General relativity makes it hard to separate matter from metric: the metric is a dynamical field, which supports a substantivalist reading that treats it as an entity, and a relationalist reading in which it is a way of encoding relations. This is the background to the hole argument.

@example
title: Newton's globes argument, step by step
problem: Reconstruct the argument from the two globes and say what a Machian denies.
step: State the relationalist thesis as a supervenience claim: inertial effects supervene on relative distances and motions among bodies. That is premise P1, and the thesis itself is not under attack, it is what the argument tests.
step: State the case. In a Newtonian model with only two globes, the distance between them is constant in two models, and the cord is taut in one (rotation about the midpoint) and slack in the other. So the tension differs where relative distances match: premise P2.
step: Conclude by modus tollens: if P1 is true, the tensions would have to match; they do not; so relationalism, understood as P1, fails for Newtonian physics.
step: The Machian answers that P2 is true only of Newtonian mechanics as ordinarily formulated. Reformulating the dynamics (the Barbour–Bertotti approach) so that rotation of the whole universe is not a physical possibility removes the model where the cord is taut with no other matter, but at the price of changing the theory's predictions.
answer: The Machian denies P2 for the reformulated theory; a relationalist could instead deny that the case is physically possible by rejecting Newtonian mechanics as a model of the world.

@example
title: A Minkowski interval and the proper time
problem: Two events are separated by $\Delta t = 3$ s and $\Delta x = 6.0 \times 10^8$ m in one inertial frame, along one spatial axis. Classify the separation and find the proper time between them for a clock moving freely from one to the other.
step: Compute $c\,\Delta t = 299{,}792{,}458 \times 3 \approx 8.994 \times 10^8$ m.
step: Compute $I^2 = (8.994 \times 10^8)^2 - (6.0 \times 10^8)^2 \approx 8.089 \times 10^{17} - 3.6 \times 10^{17} \approx 4.489 \times 10^{17}\ \text{m}^2$. It is positive, so the separation is timelike.
step: $\tau = \sqrt{I^2}/c = \sqrt{4.489 \times 10^{17}}\ \text{m} / c \approx 6.700 \times 10^8\ \text{m} / 2.998 \times 10^8\ \text{m/s} \approx 2.23$ s.
step: The clock aging $2.23$ s, less than the $3$ s of coordinate time, is not a paradox: the proper time along a straight path between two events is the longest of any timelike path joining them (the twin effect).
answer: Timelike; $\tau \approx 2.23$ s.

@mistakes
- Treating the debate as "does space exist?" Relationalists accept spatiotemporal relations and structure; the question is whether spacetime is an entity over and above bodies and fields, or whether the structure is in the relations.
- Saying that Galilean spacetime has no absolute notion of motion. It has no absolute velocity but it does have absolute acceleration, which is why Newton's globes and the bucket carry over.
- Mixing signature conventions. With $(+,-,-,-)$ the timelike interval is positive in $c^2\Delta t^2 - \Delta x^2$; with $(-,+,+,+)$ the sign flips. State your convention.
- Reading the shift argument as refuting every substantivalism. It uses a haecceitistic reading of P1, which a sophisticated substantivalist denies.

solution: The shift argument rests on P3, a principle that no two possibilities differ in a way that makes no difference to any relation or possible observation. A naturalist should be wary of it for two reasons. First, it is not a result of physics; it descends from the Principle of Sufficient Reason or from verificationism, and by the lesson 1 argument its verdicts are suspect unless they answer to our best science. Second, physics itself offers a better-supported analog: gauge and diffeomorphism invariance say that models related by certain transformations represent the same physical situation, so physics endorses a restricted indiscernibility principle, but only where the formalism supports it. That analog supports the anti-haecceitist reply to P1 (the shifted world is the same possibility) without a general verificationist premise. So the naturalist can reject the shift argument as stated while accepting its moral in a physical form, which is that physical significance is attached to what is invariant. The strongest reply is that this uses the invariance of one formalism as a metaphysical guide, and that a formalism with more structure (a preferred rest frame, say) would give a different answer: so the naturalist inherits the underdetermination worry. I conclude that the argument fails against sophisticated substantivalism, and that the naturalist is right to distrust P3 as a general principle, though not for the reason that all such principles are unscientific.
