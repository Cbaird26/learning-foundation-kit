@article
A glass of water freezes at 0 degrees Celsius at ordinary pressure. Nothing in a single water molecule freezes, and the free energy of any finite collection of molecules, as statistical mechanics defines it, is a smooth function of temperature. Yet water freezes sharply, and the phase transition looks like a new, robust behavior of the whole. Is this emergence, and if so, of what kind?

### Senses of emergence

Philosophers distinguish **epistemic** from **ontological** emergence, and, within the latter, **weak** from **strong**. Chalmers ("Strong and weak emergence", 2006) says a high-level phenomenon is **weakly emergent** if it is unexpected but deducible in principle from low-level truths and laws, and **strongly emergent** if it is not deducible even in principle, so that new fundamental laws are needed. The British emergentists (Broad, for example) defended strong emergence, and Anderson's "More is different" (1972) is the classic physicist's statement that large aggregates show behavior that needs its own concepts. **Reduction**, on Nagel's model, is derivation of the higher-level theory from the lower-level one together with bridge laws; **functional reduction** in Kim's sense identifies a higher-level property with whatever plays a role at the lower level. **Multiple realizability**, stressed by Fodor and Putnam, says that a higher-level kind can be realized by different lower-level kinds, which is the main argument against type-identity.

### The exclusion argument

Kim's causal exclusion argument, adapted to emergent properties, runs as follows.

| P1 | Every physical event that has a cause has a sufficient cause that is a property instance at the physical base level (causal closure). |
| P2 | If emergent property instances $M$, distinct from the physical base instances, are sufficient causes of physical events $E$, then those events have two distinct sufficient causes: $M$ and a physical base instance $P$ (by P1). |
| P3 | If physical events have two distinct sufficient causes in this way, they are systematically overdetermined. |
| P4 | Systematic overdetermination of physical events does not occur. |
| C | So emergent property instances distinct from their physical base are not sufficient causes of physical events. |

The argument is valid. Replies and the premises they deny:

- **Against P1:** strong emergentists reject causal closure, holding that emergent properties have novel causal powers and that the base level is not causally complete. This is a claim about what physics shows: critics say that physics supports closure for the processes we have studied, and defenders say that it has not been shown for the complex systems at issue.
- **Against P4 (or P3):** compatibilists such as Bennett, in work on why the exclusion problem seems intractable, say that the overdetermination in question is not objectionable because the two causes are related by dependence, unlike the independent causes (two assassins) in ordinary overdetermination. This denies that the systematic duplication is a cost.
- **Interventionist approaches** to causation (Woodward) allow causes at several levels, since a variable at either level can be a cause if interventions on it change the effect; this rejects the assumption, behind P3 and P4, that the two causes compete.
- **Accepting the conclusion:** the emergentist can accept that the higher-level property is not a distinct cause and say that it is identical to, or constituted by, the base; this concedes reduction for causal purposes.

### Limits, singularities and universality

For a finite system of $N$ particles with a finite number of states, the partition function is a finite sum of exponentials and the free energy is analytic in temperature, so there is no mathematical singularity. Sharp phase transitions, with singularities in the free energy per particle, appear only in the **thermodynamic limit**, $N \to \infty$ with density fixed. Every real system is finite. Batterman (*The Devil in the Details*, 2002, and later work) argues that the infinite idealization is **indispensable** to the explanation of **universality**: very different substances share the same critical exponents (the liquid-gas critical point and the three-dimensional Ising ferromagnet are in one universality class), and the renormalization group explains this by showing that microscopic details are irrelevant in the limit. Butterfield ("Less is different", 2011) argues that emergence and reduction are **compatible**: reduction is deduction of the higher-level behavior in a limit, and emergence is novel and robust behavior in that limit, so there is no conflict. Norton distinguishes idealizations that approximate and those that mislead. The dispute is over whether the infinite system is a convenient approximation to a finite one, or an indispensable part of the explanation.

### Presenting the tension

Your block deliverable is a presentation of the tension between nonlocality and relativity (lesson 9). The point of this lesson for it is the question of **levels**: is the thing in tension a feature of the fundamental dynamics, or of a higher-level description that may be emergent? A good presentation states the argument, names the premises, and says which level each reply operates at.

@example
title: The size of fluctuations
problem: The number of molecules in a small region of a gas has a mean $N$ and, in a Poisson model, a standard deviation of $\sqrt{N}$. How large is the relative fluctuation $\sqrt{N}/N$ for $N = 10^6$, and what does this show about the emergence of a stable macroscopic density?
step: The relative size is $\sqrt{N}/N = 1/\sqrt{N}$.
step: For $N = 10^6$, $1/\sqrt{10^6} = 10^{-3}$, a tenth of a percent.
step: For $N \sim 10^{23}$, as in a macroscopic sample, the relative fluctuation is about $3 \times 10^{-12}$, far below any measurable threshold. A stable density therefore emerges from large numbers without any new law.
answer: $10^{-3}$ for $N = 10^6$. Stable macroscopic quantities come from the suppression of relative fluctuations as $N$ grows; this is weak emergence in Chalmers's sense.

@example
title: Locating a reply to the exclusion argument
problem: A philosopher writes: "The overdetermination in the exclusion argument is not objectionable. A mental event and its neural base are not two independent causes, like two assassins, since the higher-level cause depends on the base." Which premise does this deny, and what is the strongest worry?
step: Find what is accepted. The philosopher accepts that the higher-level property is distinct and sufficient (the antecedent of P2) and accepts closure (P1).
step: Find what is denied. The philosopher does not deny that there are two sufficient causes; the claim is that this is not objectionable. This targets P4: it grants that the duplication occurs systematically and says it does no harm (or targets P3, if one says the dependence means it is not overdetermination at all).
step: The strongest worry: a dependence between the two causes does not yet explain why the higher-level cause does any causal work in addition to the base, so the emergent property seems epiphenomenal.
answer: It denies P4 (or the characterization in P3). The worry is that dependence alone leaves the higher-level cause idle.

@mistakes
- Saying that weak emergence is merely epistemic and so of no interest. It concerns robust higher-level patterns, such as universality, which need their own explanation.
- Saying that finite systems have phase transitions with singular free energies. The singularities arise in the limit, which is the source of the dispute.
- Attributing the claim that reduction and emergence are incompatible to Butterfield. He argues that they are compatible.
- Treating the exclusion argument as showing that higher-level properties do not exist. It concerns their causal efficacy, assuming they are distinct from the base.
