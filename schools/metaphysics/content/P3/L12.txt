@article
A seminar paper in the philosophy of physics fails in a few predictable ways: the physics is wrong or stated in a form no physicist would recognize; the thesis is a topic ("this paper is about the hole argument") and not a claim; the paper surveys the literature and never says anything of its own; or the argument is valid and its premises are not defended. This lesson is a checklist for avoiding them.

### Choosing and scoping the thesis

A thesis is a claim that someone could deny, and it should be narrow enough to defend in 5,000 to 7,000 words. Compare "this paper is about probability in many worlds" (a topic) with "I argue that the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule succeeds only if branching indifference is taken as a rationality constraint, and that this makes the derivation weaker than its defenders claim" (a thesis with a target, a claim and a consequence). A useful form is: **target** (a specific argument or view), **claim** (what is wrong with it, or how it can be repaired, or what follows), **because** (the main reason). State at once what you will not do.

### Getting the physics right

Wrong physics is the most avoidable error, and referees notice it first. Checks to run on every draft:

- **State the theory and the formulation.** Say which theory you mean (non-relativistic quantum mechanics for $N$ particles, a version of general relativity, a given model), and which formulation, since some philosophical claims hold for one formulation and fail for another.
- **Give units and conventions.** Write the signature of the metric, the units, whether $\hbar = c = 1$, and the sign conventions used in any formula.
- **Compute every number.** A claim such as "the interval is timelike" or "the CHSH value is 2.6" should be checked by computation, not memory.
- **Distinguish what is established from what is conjectured.** The Bell inequalities are violated; the interpretation of quantum mechanics is contested; there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity.
- **Check against a standard source** at the right level (a graduate textbook or review) and, where possible, ask a physicist to read the physics sections. State your own limits.
- **Attribute carefully.** Say whose argument you are discussing, cite the work, and say "a reconstruction in the spirit of" when you put an argument into standard form that the author did not.

### Scope and the form of an argument

A common mistake is to prove a claim about a model and state it as a claim about a theory. Consider this argument, as a referee might set it out:

| P1 | If a paper's physical premises hold only in non-relativistic quantum mechanics with a fixed number of particles, then its argument establishes its thesis at most for non-relativistic quantum mechanics. |
| P2 | The paper's premises about configuration space hold only in non-relativistic quantum mechanics with a fixed number of particles. |
| C | So the paper's argument establishes its thesis at most for non-relativistic quantum mechanics. |

It is valid (modus ponens). The author can reply against **P2** by showing that the premises have a counterpart in quantum field theory (a wave functional on a space of field configurations, for example), or against **P1** by saying that the premises are structural and do not depend on the particle formulation. Either way, you have a duty to state the scope or to argue that it extends.

### Structure and a word budget

A paper of this kind usually has: an introduction with the thesis and a roadmap (about 10% of the words); the physics set out at the level the argument needs (about 20%); the relevant literature and the argument you target (about 20%); your argument, set out in standard form with each premise defended (about 35%); and objections and replies, followed by a short conclusion (about 15%). These are guides, not rules. Put the strongest objection in, say which premise it denies, and reply to it; a paper that ignores its best objection reads as if the author did not see it.

### Presenting

A twenty-minute talk is about 2,400 to 3,000 words at a speaking rate of 120 to 150 words per minute, and that is the length of a short paper; a talk should therefore cover the problem, the thesis, one argument and one objection, not the whole paper. Put the argument in standard form on a slide, with premises numbered. Expect questions to target a premise. When asked, restate the question, say which premise it targets, concede what is right, and answer or say what you would need to do to answer. Revising after written feedback is part of the project: for each comment, say what you changed, or why you did not.

### Integrity

Cite every source you use. Rules about the use of AI tools, collaboration and the sharing of drafts vary between programs and courses: check your own program's policy.

@example
title: Auditing a paragraph for physical accuracy
problem: A draft says: "The speed of light, exactly 299,792,458 m/s only in air, is the same for all observers. Bell's theorem shows that quantum mechanics allows signals to travel faster than light, and a wave function for N particles is a field on ordinary three-dimensional space." Find three errors and correct each.
step: The speed of light in vacuum is exactly 299,792,458 m/s by the definition of the meter, not in air. (In air the speed of light is slightly lower.)
step: Bell's theorem concerns what local hidden-variable theories cannot reproduce. The no-signaling theorem shows that quantum correlations cannot be used to send signals faster than light.
step: The wave function for $N$ particles is a function on the $3N$-dimensional configuration space (with spin degrees of freedom added), not a field on three-dimensional space. Wave function realists draw philosophical conclusions from exactly this fact.
answer: Errors: "in air" (it is vacuum); the claim about signaling (no-signaling holds); the three-dimensional space (configuration space).

@example
title: From topic to thesis
problem: Improve the claim "I will discuss whether the past hypothesis explains the arrow of time" into a thesis with a target, a claim and a reason, and state one limit on scope.
step: Choose the target: the Albert and Loewer view that a past hypothesis, with a uniform measure, grounds the arrow.
step: State a claim someone could deny: that the past hypothesis grounds the arrow only given a choice of macrostate partition that is not itself fixed by the dynamics.
step: Add a reason and a limit: because the entropy of a macrostate is relative to the partition, the claim that the past hypothesis is low entropy needs a privileged partition; the paper will treat classical statistical mechanics and will not address general relativity.
answer: Thesis: the past hypothesis grounds the arrow of time only relative to a privileged macrostate partition, which the dynamics do not supply; I argue this for classical statistical mechanics and leave general relativity aside.

@mistakes
- Writing a survey in which no sentence is your own claim. Say what you think and defend it.
- Stating a result for a toy model and generalizing to a theory without saying so.
- Omitting units, signature or conventions, so that a physicist cannot check a formula.
- Burying the strongest objection, or answering a weaker objection than the one a referee would raise.

solution: The paper argues that wave function realism fails because the three-dimensional world cannot be recovered from a field on configuration space, and it considers one reply. My main concern is that the argument, as stated, supports at most a conditional conclusion: if wave function realism is true, it owes an account of how three-dimensional space and its contents arise, and the literature contains functional accounts (Albert, Ney) that aim to pay this debt.
