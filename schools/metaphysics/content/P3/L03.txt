@article
Einstein reasoned in 1913–1914 that a theory whose equations hold in every coordinate system could not determine the field uniquely from initial data, and for that reason he temporarily abandoned generally covariant equations. He returned to them in November 1915 and replied to his own worry in 1916 with the **point-coincidence** remark: the only physical content of a spacetime description lies in coincidences of events, which survive any relabeling of points. John Stachel brought the argument back to attention in the 1980s, and John Earman and John Norton (1987) turned it into an argument against one form of spacetime substantivalism. It is now the standard test case for how metaphysics should read a gauge symmetry.

### The mathematics

A spacetime model of general relativity is a triple $(M, g_{ab}, T_{ab})$: a smooth four-dimensional manifold $M$, a Lorentzian metric $g_{ab}$ and a matter stress-energy tensor $T_{ab}$ that satisfy Einstein's equations $G_{ab} = (8\pi G/c^4)\,T_{ab}$ (with $G_{ab}$ the Einstein tensor and $G$ Newton's constant). A **diffeomorphism** $d : M \to M$ is a smooth bijection with a smooth inverse. It moves the fields around the manifold: the **drag-along** metric $d^*g_{ab}$ and matter $d^*T_{ab}$ are obtained by relabeling each point $p$ as $d(p)$ (so for a scalar field, $(d^*\phi)(p) = \phi(d^{-1}(p))$). The key fact is that the field equations are **diffeomorphism invariant**: if $(M, g, T)$ solves them, then so does $(M, d^*g, d^*T)$. A **hole diffeomorphism** is one that is the identity outside a compact region $H$ (the hole) and not the identity inside it, and smooth throughout.

Take a hole $H$ lying to the future of a spacelike surface $\Sigma$ on which initial data are given. The models $(M,g,T)$ and $(M,d^*g,d^*T)$ are then identical on $\Sigma$ and everywhere outside $H$, and both solve the equations, yet they assign different field values at some points inside $H$.

### The argument

The target is **manifold substantivalism**: the view that the points of the manifold exist as entities, with identities independent of the fields on them, so that the same point can carry different fields in different possibilities. (The argument is one about this view, not about spacetime realism in general.)

| P1 | If manifold substantivalism is true, then the models $(M,g,T)$ and $(M,d^*g,d^*T)$ represent distinct physical possibilities. |
| P2 | Both models solve Einstein's equations, they are identical outside the hole $H$ (in particular on a spacelike surface $\Sigma$ to the past of $H$), and they differ at some points inside $H$. |
| P3 | If those two models represent distinct possibilities and are identical on $\Sigma$ but differ in $H$, then general relativity is indeterministic: the laws and the data on $\Sigma$ do not fix the future. |
| P4 | General relativity, as a theory of the physical world, is not indeterministic in that way. |
| C | So manifold substantivalism is false. |

This is valid. Assume substantivalism; P1 gives distinct possibilities; P2 supplies the agreement, the difference inside $H$ and the solutions; P3 then gives indeterminism, contradicting P4. Each family of response denies one premise.

### Responses

- **Relationalism** accepts the conclusion: the argument is a reason to reject manifold substantivalism, and Earman and Norton drew exactly this moral.
- **Sophisticated substantivalism** (Butterfield, Brighouse, Hoefer, Pooley and others) denies **P1**: the two models differ only in which point plays which role, so they represent the same possibility. This is an anti-haecceitist stance about points (the **Leibniz equivalence**: diffeomorphic models represent the same situation). It then owes an account of what makes points the entities they are if their identity is wholly given by their role.
- **Metric essentialism** (Maudlin) also denies **P1**, differently: the points have their metric relations essentially, so the point at which $d^*g$ would place different metric values is not a possible way for those very points to be.
- **The mathematical-practice response** (Weatherall's work on the argument is the best-known statement) denies **P1**: physicists and mathematicians already treat isomorphic models as representing the same situation, and the argument gets "distinct" only by an unmotivated reading of the formalism.
- **Accepting indeterminism** denies **P4**. Some argue that this is a harmless indeterminism, because it concerns only which point is which and nothing observable, but critics say that then the ordinary notion of determinism has been changed.

### What to take from it

The argument shows that a symmetry of the equations (diffeomorphism invariance) forces a decision about which differences in the mathematics are differences in the world. That is the general lesson, and it recurs in gauge theory and in quantum gravity. A careful paper says which version of substantivalism it targets, whether it assumes the models are individuated up to isomorphism, and whether the determinism it uses is the physicist's (a well-posed initial value problem) or a metaphysical one.

@example
title: Dragging a field along a diffeomorphism
problem: On the real line $M = \mathbb{R}$ take the scalar field $\phi(p) = p$ and the diffeomorphism $d(p) = 2p$. Find $d^*\phi$ using $(d^*\phi)(p) = \phi(d^{-1}(p))$, and say where the value 3 of the original field is found in the dragged field. Why does this toy not yet give a hole argument?
step: The inverse is $d^{-1}(p) = p/2$, so $(d^*\phi)(p) = \phi(p/2) = p/2$.
step: The original field has value 3 at the point $p = 3$. The dragged field has value 3 where $p/2 = 3$, that is at $p = 6$. The diffeomorphism has moved each field value from $p$ to $2p$.
step: The two fields are different functions on the same manifold ($p$ versus $p/2$), but they are related by a relabeling of points, so a relationalist treats them as the same configuration.
step: The map $d(p) = 2p$ changes the field everywhere, including far from any initial surface, and is not the identity outside a compact region. A hole argument needs a map that is the identity outside a region, so that the two models agree on all initial data.
answer: $(d^*\phi)(p) = p/2$; the value 3 sits at $p = 6$. The toy lacks the identity-outside-a-hole property, which is what makes the hole argument bear on determinism.

@example
title: Matching responses to premises
problem: A sophisticated substantivalist says: "The two models represent one situation, since they differ only by a relabeling of points that have no identity apart from their role." Match this to the argument, and say what question it leaves open for the speaker.
step: Locate the target. The speaker says the models do not represent distinct possibilities, so the speaker denies that substantivalism implies distinct possibilities.
step: That is premise P1. The speaker accepts P2 (the models agree outside the hole and differ inside it), accepts that P3 is a good conditional, and need not deny P4; since the models are not distinct, no indeterminism follows.
step: The open question: if points have no identity apart from their role in the structure, in what sense are they entities that exist over and above the fields? A critic says the view has become relationalism about points.
answer: The reply denies P1. It leaves open what makes the view still a form of substantivalism.

@mistakes
- Saying that the hole argument shows that general relativity is indeterministic. It shows that if the models are distinct possibilities, the theory would be, so something has to give.
- Confusing the hole diffeomorphism (identity outside a compact region) with an arbitrary coordinate change or with any diffeomorphism.
- Treating the argument as a refutation of all spacetime realism. It targets manifold substantivalism with distinct points; metric essentialism and sophisticated substantivalism escape.
- Thinking that diffeomorphism invariance of the equations is itself in dispute. It is a mathematical fact; the dispute is about what it shows.

prompt: Which property makes a diffeomorphism a hole diffeomorphism?
choice*: It is the identity outside a compact region and is not the identity inside it.
choice: It maps every point of the manifold to a point that is spacelike separated from it.
choice: It preserves the metric at every point.
choice: It changes the field equations to a different set of equations.
hint: The point of the hole is that the two models agree on initial data.
solution: A hole diffeomorphism is smooth, the identity outside a compact region (the hole) and not the identity inside it. Because it is the identity outside the hole, the dragged-along models agree on all initial data placed before the hole, which gives the argument its bearing on determinism. It does not preserve the metric inside (if it did, the second model would be the same as the first), and it leaves the field equations unchanged.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, explain why the hole argument requires the hole to lie in the future of a surface on which the data are given, and what would go wrong with the argument if the diffeomorphism changed the fields everywhere.
hint: Ask what the two models share, and what premise about determinism needs.
solution: Determinism concerns whether data on a surface fix what happens afterward. For the argument to threaten determinism, the two models must be identical on and before some surface (the same data) and differ afterward, which a diffeomorphism that is the identity outside a compact region to the future of the surface guarantees. If the diffeomorphism changed the fields everywhere, including on the surface, the two models would have different initial data, and there would be nothing for determinism to fail to fix: the second model would simply be a different history with different data. The compactness also keeps the transformation local, so that the models can differ in the hole without differing anywhere that a physicist would regard as already fixed. The argument then runs: they agree on the data, both solve the equations, but they differ later, so if they are distinct possibilities the future is not fixed by the past.
rubric: Says that determinism asks whether data on a surface fix the later fields, so the two models must share the data.
rubric: Explains that a map changing the fields on the surface would give different initial data and so no failure of determinism.
rubric: Connects the compact hole to the identity outside it and to premise P2.
