@article
An ice cube dropped in warm water melts. Statistical mechanics says that it is overwhelmingly probable that it melts, and that there is a tiny probability that it would grow instead. Yet on classical mechanics the dynamics are deterministic: the exact microstate of the water and the ice fixes what happens. In what sense is there a probability of anything here? And what about quantum mechanics, where the Born rule assigns probabilities to outcomes: is that chance at the fundamental level, or something else?

### Accounts of probability

- **Subjective** (Bayesian) probabilities are degrees of belief constrained by coherence.
- **Frequentism** identifies probability with relative frequency, actual or hypothetical; it faces the single-case problem and the reference-class problem.
- **Propensity** accounts take chance to be a physical tendency of a setup to produce outcomes.
- **Humean** chances (Lewis) are the probabilities that figure in the best system of the world, the one that best balances simplicity, strength and **fit**: how probable the system makes the actual history.

What links chance to credence is Lewis's **Principal Principle** (1980). Write $C$ for a reasonable initial credence function, $\mathrm{ch}_t(A)$ for the chance at time $t$ of $A$, and $E$ for evidence that is **admissible**, meaning that it bears on $A$ only by way of the chance of $A$ (historical information up to $t$ is typically admissible; a reliable report of the outcome is not). The principle says
$$C\bigl(A \mid \mathrm{ch}_t(A) = x \wedge E\bigr) = x.$$
If you are sure the chance is 0.3, your credence should be 0.3 unless you have inadmissible information, such as a crystal ball.

Humean chances face the **problem of undermining**: since the chances supervene on the whole history, there is a small chance that the future will go in a way that would make the best system, and therefore the chances, different. The Principal Principle then conflicts with Humean supervenience, which led Lewis (1994), Hall (1994) and Thau (1994) to propose a modified principle. How well that works is a live debate.

### Probability in statistical mechanics

In **Boltzmannian statistical mechanics** a macrostate is a set of microstates. The standard probabilities come from a **statistical postulate**: a uniform measure (the Liouville measure on phase space) over the microstates compatible with the macrostate. Counting is the simplest case. A system of $N$ two-state units has $2^N$ microstates, and the macrostate with $k$ units "up" has $\binom{N}{k}$ of them, so its probability under the uniform measure is $\binom{N}{k}/2^N$.

### Can chance be deterministic?

Lewis held that if determinism is true, all chances are 0 or 1. The argument:

| P1 | If determinism holds, then for any event $E$ and time $t$ the laws together with the complete state at $t$ entail either $E$ or its negation. |
| P2 | If the laws together with the complete state at $t$ entail $E$ or entail $\neg E$, then the chance at $t$ of $E$ is 1 or 0. |
| C | So if determinism holds, then every event has chance 0 or 1 at every time. |

The argument is valid (hypothetical syllogism). P2 is supported by the Principal Principle: the laws and the complete past are admissible, so credence should be 1 in anything they entail. Schaffer (2007) argues for incompatibility from several platitudes about the role of chance.

The **compatibilist** reply, defended by Loewer and by Albert, denies **P2**, or rather its premise that the complete microstate is admissible. On their **Mentaculus**, a package of three things (the fundamental dynamical laws, a **past hypothesis** (next lesson), and a uniform probability distribution over initial conditions compatible with it), the probabilities are defined by conditionalizing the distribution on a macrostate. The microstate then has the role of inadmissible information: knowing it would make the chance irrelevant, as with the crystal ball, so the Principal Principle gives no verdict. Chances in deterministic worlds are then not trivial. The cost, which Schaffer presses, is that "chance" now has a different role: an event that is entailed by the laws and state has chance below 1 in a sense that does not track what could have happened. Glynn (2010) defends a related view on which special-science chances are compatible with determinism, relative to a level of description.

### Quantum chances

In collapse theories such as GRW, probabilities are fundamental and dynamical. In Bohmian mechanics the dynamics are deterministic, and the Born probabilities arise from a **typicality** or **quantum equilibrium** analysis (Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì): for typical initial configurations, the statistics of outcomes follow $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$. In Everettian quantum mechanics, probability is the problem of week 8.

### Where this leaves your paper

Say which role probability is meant to play (explaining frequencies, guiding credence, ensuring typicality) and test the account against that role. A statistical-mechanical probability that guides credence about macroscopic events is not thereby a chance about what the world is disposed to do.

@example
title: Counting microstates for a macrostate
problem: A toy system has six independent two-state units, each "up" or "down". Under the uniform measure over microstates, what is the probability of the macrostate "exactly four up"? Compare it with the macrostate "all six up".
step: The number of microstates is $2^6 = 64$.
step: The macrostate "exactly four up" contains $\binom{6}{4} = 15$ microstates, so its probability is $15/64 \approx 0.234$, about 23.4%.
step: The macrostate "all six up" contains exactly one microstate, so its probability is $1/64 \approx 1.6\%$.
step: The ratio is $15 : 1$. Even with six units, the more mixed macrostate is much more likely, and the ratio grows rapidly with $N$. This is the combinatorial source of the second law's statistical character.
answer: 23.4% for "exactly four up" and about 1.6% for "all six up".

@example
title: Admissibility and the Principal Principle
problem: A coin has chance 0.4 of landing heads at noon, in a theory where chance is a physical property of the setup. At 12:05 you read a note from a reliable colleague who watched the toss at 12:01: "it landed heads." What should your credence in heads be at 12:05, and why does the Principal Principle not say 0.4?
step: Identify the evidence. The note reports the outcome, so it is evidence about the toss that does not run through the chance at noon.
step: Admissible evidence bears on the outcome only through the chance. A reliable report of the outcome is a paradigm of inadmissible evidence.
step: The Principal Principle conditional on the chance of 0.4 and on admissible evidence gives credence 0.4. With the inadmissible note, the principle is silent. Ordinary reasoning gives a credence close to 1, as high as your trust in the note.
answer: Your credence should be close to 1. The Principal Principle applies only to admissible evidence and the note is inadmissible.

@mistakes
- Treating the Principal Principle as saying that credence equals chance whatever else you know. It holds only given admissible evidence.
- Saying that statistical mechanics gives probabilities only because we are ignorant. Whether the probabilities are objective is the point of dispute; the Mentaculus view makes them chances of a sort, and the ignorance reading is one among several.
- Saying that Bohmian mechanics is indeterministic because it uses probabilities. Its dynamics are deterministic, and the probabilities enter through the distribution over initial configurations.
- Using the uniform measure without saying over what: it is over microstates compatible with a macrostate, and which macrostate you conditionalize on matters (next lesson).

@practice self
prompt: In 200 words, argue for or against the claim that the probabilities of statistical mechanics are objective chances in a deterministic world. State the strongest objection to your view and your reply.
hint: Say what role you want chance to play (guide credence, explain frequencies), and test the Mentaculus account against that role.
solution: I argue that they are objective chances of a kind, though not the kind Lewis had in mind. On the Mentaculus, the probability distribution over initial conditions is part of the best system of the world because it greatly increases the system's informativeness about macroscopic frequencies at small cost in simplicity. The resulting probabilities explain why we observe the frequencies we do, and they guide credence, since they satisfy a version of the Principal Principle for agents without microscopic information. Those are the roles of objective chance, and a Humean can take chance to be just whatever fills them. The strongest objection, due to the incompatibilist, is that an event entailed by the laws and the state cannot have a nontrivial chance: the chance then does not describe what could have happened. My reply is that the objection assumes a role for chance (tracking genuine possibility of different outcomes) that Humeans reject for all chances; its force depends on a modal conception of chance that is not shared by those who take chance to be a feature of the best system. The cost is that these chances are relative to a macrostate and a measure, which is a real limitation but not one that stops them from being objective.

prompt: What is the problem of undermining for Humean theories of chance?
choice*: The Humean chances supervene on the whole history, so there is a small chance of futures that would make the best system, and hence the chances, different, which conflicts with the Principal Principle.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in 120 to 160 words why the Principal Principle needs the restriction to admissible evidence, using an example of your own where the evidence is inadmissible.
hint: Build a case where you know the chance and also know something that bears on the outcome in another way.
solution: Suppose a radioactive atom has a chance of 0.5 of decaying within an hour, and I know this. The Principal Principle says my credence that it decays within the hour, given that chance and nothing else, should be 0.5. Now suppose a detector that I know to be reliable beeps after fifty minutes to report that the atom has decayed. This evidence bears on the outcome, but it does not do so by way of the chance at the start of the hour. If the principle applied to it, I would still have to keep my credence at 0.5, which is absurd. The restriction to admissible evidence captures the intuition that chance is the best guide to the outcome for someone who has no information about the outcome except through the chance; historical information about the setup is typically admissible, a report from the future is not.
