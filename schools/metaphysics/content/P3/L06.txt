@article
Run a film of a drop of ink spreading in water backward and you know at once that you are watching something wrong. Run a film of two billiard balls colliding backward and you may not. The microscopic laws of classical mechanics, and the Schrödinger equation (with time reversal represented by an antiunitary operator), are **time-reversal invariant**: if a sequence of states is a solution, so is the reversed sequence. (Weak-interaction processes, such as certain neutral kaon and B meson decays, violate CP symmetry and, given the CPT theorem, therefore violate time-reversal invariance, but these effects are tiny and are not thought to be the source of the thermodynamic asymmetry.) So where does the arrow come from?

### Boltzmann's account and Loschmidt's objection

Boltzmann defined the entropy of a macrostate as $S = k_B \ln W$, where $W$ is the number of microstates compatible with the macrostate and $k_B = 1.380649 \times 10^{-23}$ J/K, the Boltzmann constant, whose value is exact in the SI since 2019. Macrostates of high entropy contain overwhelmingly many microstates. Boltzmann's idea: a typical microstate in a low-entropy macrostate evolves into a high-entropy macrostate simply because there are so many more of them.

Loschmidt's **reversibility objection** says that this cannot be the whole story. For every microstate that evolves from low to high entropy, its time reversal evolves from high to low, so the dynamics cannot by themselves single out the entropy-increasing trajectories. The recurrence theorem of Poincaré gives a second reminder that the statistical result is not a theorem about all trajectories.

### The retrodiction problem and the past hypothesis

Suppose we apply the uniform measure over the microstates compatible with the present macrostate, an ice cube half melted in a glass. Because the dynamics are reversible, the measure makes it overwhelmingly probable that the system's entropy was higher in the past as well as in the future: the ice was more likely to be a fluctuation out of a pool of water. This contradicts everything we know. David Albert (*Time and Chance*, 2000) and Barry Loewer draw the lesson that the statistical postulate has to be supplemented by a **past hypothesis** (PH): the universe began in a macrostate of very low entropy. Conditionalizing the uniform measure on the PH and the present macrostate then makes it probable that entropy was lower in the past and higher in the future. (Feynman made a similar remark, and Penrose's Weyl curvature hypothesis is a proposal about what the low-entropy beginning consisted in.)

The argument:

| P1 | If the statistical postulate (a uniform probability over microstates compatible with the macrostate) is applied to the present macrostate alone, with time-reversal-invariant dynamics, then it is overwhelmingly probable that entropy was higher in the past as well as in the future. |
| P2 | Entropy was lower in the past: our memories, records and cosmology are reliable on that, and they would not be if the past had higher entropy. |
| P3 | If P1 and P2 are both true, then the theory must include a constraint on the past, such as a low-entropy initial macrostate of the universe, beyond the dynamics and the statistical postulate applied to the present macrostate. |
| C | So the theory must include a constraint on the past (a past hypothesis). |

It is valid. The replies:

- **Against P2:** one may suppose, with Boltzmann's fluctuation proposal, that our region is a fluctuation. The standard objection is that this makes it overwhelmingly likely that we are a minimal fluctuation and that our records are unreliable, which undermines the evidence for the proposal itself.
- **Against P1:** one may reject the uniform measure over the whole macrostate as the right probability, or deny that probabilities apply to the past in this way. This gives up the Mentaculus's simple structure.
- **Not a denial of any premise:** the cosmologist's response (Sean Carroll's, for instance) that the PH might be explained by a deeper cosmological theory accepts the conclusion and asks whether the constraint is brute.

### How the PH grounds the arrows

The PH is meant to ground **the thermodynamic arrow**: entropy increases away from the low-entropy boundary. Albert and Loewer also hold that it grounds the **epistemic arrow** (we have records of the past and not the future, because records are correlations that a low-entropy past makes probable) and, on a Humean view, the **causal and counterfactual arrows** (Lewis's asymmetry of overdetermination is a consequence of the statistical asymmetry). On the best-system view, the PH is a **law**: it increases the strength of the system enormously at little cost in simplicity.

### Objections

Huw Price (*Time's Arrow and Archimedes' Point*, 1996) argues that the issue is a **temporal double standard**: we require an explanation of a low-entropy state at one end and take the high-entropy state at the other as natural, though the reverse description is equally available. For him the question is why the early universe had low entropy, and a PH that simply states it explains the arrow only by relocating the puzzle. Earman, in a 2006 paper, argues that the PH cannot be stated with the needed precision in general-relativistic cosmology, since there is no agreed definition of entropy for the gravitational field in cosmological spacetimes, and concludes that, as usually formulated, the hypothesis is too ill-defined to count even as false. His target is the PH as a precise physical hypothesis in that setting, not the Boltzmannian program in nongravitational statistical mechanics. Others ask whether special initial conditions need explanation at all.

@example
title: A toy calculation behind the problem
problem: Four gas molecules each sit in the left or right half of a box, giving 16 equally weighted microstates. The macrostate is the number on the left. How many microstates does each macrostate contain, and what does the uniform measure say about the chance that the system is in the "all four left" macrostate?
step: The macrostate "$k$ on the left" has $\binom{4}{k}$ microstates: $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$ for $k = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4$. The total is $16$.
step: The "all four left" macrostate has one microstate, so under the uniform measure its probability is $1/16 = 6.25\%$; the "two and two" macrostate has $6/16 = 37.5\%$.
step: Boltzmann entropy $S = k_B \ln W$, so the "two and two" macrostate has entropy $k_B \ln 6$ and the "all left" macrostate has entropy $k_B \ln 1 = 0$.
step: The point of the toy: the measure favors the macrostate with the largest $W$ whichever direction in time we look, so a system found in the "all left" macrostate is much more probable to have come from, and to go to, a higher-entropy macrostate.
answer: Counts $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$; probability $1/16$ for "all four left"; entropy $0$ for "all left" and $k_B\ln 6$ for "two and two".

@example
title: Does the cosmologist's reply deny a premise?
problem: A cosmologist says: "The low-entropy beginning need not be a brute law. A deeper theory of the early universe may explain it." Which premise of the standard-form argument does this reply deny?
step: Check P2. The cosmologist accepts that entropy was lower in the past.
step: Check P1 and P3. The cosmologist accepts that the dynamics and the statistical postulate alone do not yield the low-entropy past, and that a constraint on the past is required.
step: The reply is about the status of the constraint, derived or brute, not about whether one is needed. The conclusion asks that the theory include such a constraint; a deeper theory that entails it would still include it.
answer: It denies no premise. It accepts the argument and asks whether the past hypothesis is a fundamental law or a consequence of one.

@mistakes
- Saying that the second law is violated by the laws being time-reversal invariant. The second law is a statistical claim about typical behavior given a low-entropy past.
- Applying the uniform measure to the present macrostate and concluding that the past had lower entropy. It concludes the opposite, which is why the PH is needed.
- Saying that CP violation in meson decays explains the arrow of time. Those processes are real but are not the source of the thermodynamic asymmetry.
- Attributing to Albert and Loewer the claim that the PH is explained by a deeper theory. They treat it as a candidate fundamental law of the best system.

@practice self
prompt: In 200 words, explain how the past hypothesis is meant to ground the arrow of time (the thermodynamic arrow and at least one other arrow), and say what the objector who invokes a temporal double standard replies.
hint: Say what the PH adds to the dynamics and the statistical postulate, what follows for entropy, and how that bears on records or causation.
solution: The dynamics are time-reversal invariant, and the statistical postulate treats past and future alike, so neither distinguishes a direction. The past hypothesis adds a boundary condition at one end of time: the universe began in a macrostate of very low entropy. Conditionalizing the uniform measure on this macrostate and on the present macrostate, entropy is probably lower in the past and higher in the future: this is the thermodynamic arrow, a difference between the two directions that comes from the boundary condition alone. The same asymmetry underwrites the epistemic arrow: records (a footprint, a memory trace) are correlations that a low-entropy past makes probable, so we have records of the past and not the future. On a Humean view the causal arrow also follows, since the asymmetry of overdetermination is a statistical consequence of the PH. The objector (Price) says that the PH relocates the puzzle: if a low-entropy end needs explanation it needs it at the beginning too, and treating the early end as given while the late end needs the statistical explanation is a double standard. The defender replies that every explanation stops somewhere, and that the PH earns its place as a law by the strength it adds.
