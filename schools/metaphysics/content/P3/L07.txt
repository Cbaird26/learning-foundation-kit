@article
Quantum mechanics, in its textbook form, assigns a system of $N$ particles a **wave function** $\psi(\mathbf{x}_1, \dots, \mathbf{x}_N, t)$, a complex-valued function on the $3N$-dimensional **configuration space**, evolving by the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial_t \psi = \hat H \psi$. Born's rule gives $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$ as a probability density for the particle positions. What is the wave function? A physical field? A piece of law? A summary of our knowledge? And what, in a world described by it, are the tables and chairs?

### The measurement problem

Following Maudlin ("Three measurement problems", 1995), the problem is that three claims are jointly inconsistent:

| P1 | The wave function of a system is a complete description of its physical state. |
| P2 | The wave function always evolves in accordance with a linear dynamical equation (the Schrödinger equation). |
| P3 | Measurements have determinate outcomes. |

Suppose a spin measurement apparatus is coupled to a system in a superposition of two spin states. Linear evolution (P2) gives a superposition of the pointer states, and by completeness (P1) there is nothing more to the apparatus than that superposition, so no determinate outcome (P3). The three families of solution deny one of them. **Hidden-variable** theories deny P1 (Bohmian mechanics adds particle positions). **Collapse** theories deny P2 (GRW adds spontaneous nonlinear localization). **Everettian** theories keep P1 and P2 and reinterpret P3: all outcomes occur, each in its own branch (next lesson). Nothing here says which is correct, and the interpretation of quantum mechanics remains contested.

### Wave function realism

**Wave function realism** (Albert, in "Elementary quantum metaphysics", 1996, and later work; Ney, *The World in the Wave Function*, 2021; Lewis, "Life in configuration space", 2004) takes the wave function as a physical field. If it is the complete description in a collapse or Everettian theory, then it is a field on configuration space. The fundamental space is then $3N$-dimensional, and three-dimensional space and the objects in it are in some way derivative.

| P1 | If wave function realism is true, then fundamental physical space is the $3N$-dimensional configuration space. |
| P2 | If fundamental space is $3N$-dimensional, then the three-dimensional space of ordinary experience is derivative, not fundamental. |
| P3 | If three-dimensional space is derivative, then wave function realism owes an account of how three-dimensional space and its contents arise from the dynamics of the wave function. |
| C | So if wave function realism is true, it owes an account of how three-dimensional space and its contents arise from the dynamics of the wave function. |

The argument is valid (a chain of conditionals), and the conclusion is a debt, not a refutation. Critics, notably Monton (2002, 2006), argue the debt cannot be paid: nothing in the Hamiltonian marks out three dimensions. Defenders argue that it can: Albert holds that the three-dimensional appearance is recovered **functionally**, since the interaction terms in the Hamiltonian couple coordinates in groups of three; Ney develops a related view on which the dynamics select a three-dimensional structure; Lewis argues that the configuration space picture is empirically adequate and that the apparent loss of ordinary objects is no worse than for other reductions. The reply that denies P3 says the theory need only be empirically adequate and that recovery is optional, which turns the question into one about scientific realism.

### Primitive ontology

Bell's term **beables** names what a theory says there is. The **primitive ontology** program (Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì; Allori and collaborators) says that a physical theory must have, among its fundamental posits, an ontology of matter in three-dimensional space or in spacetime, which the macroscopic objects are made of. The wave function then plays a different role.

| Theory | Primitive ontology | Role of the wave function |
|---|---|---|
| Bohmian mechanics | $N$ point particles with positions $Q_k(t)$ | Guides the particles by the guidance equation |
| GRW with flashes | Discrete space-time events ("flashes"), after Bell | Fixes the probability of collapse events |
| GRW with matter density | A continuous mass density in space (Ghirardi, Grassi and Benatti) | Determines the density through collapses |

In Bohmian mechanics the guidance equation is
$$\frac{dQ_k}{dt} = \frac{\hbar}{m_k}\,\mathrm{Im}\!\left(\frac{\nabla_k \psi}{\psi}\right)\Bigg|_{(Q_1,\dots,Q_N)},$$
and the Born probabilities arise from quantum equilibrium, the statement that initial configurations are typically distributed with density $\lvert\psi\rvert^2$. In the original GRW proposal each particle undergoes spontaneous localization at a rate of about $10^{-16}$ per second, with a localization width of about $10^{-7}$ m; these are free parameters, so an object of $N$ particles has a localization about $N \times 10^{-16}$ times per second, which is large for macroscopic $N$.

The **nomological** reading (Dürr, Goldstein and Zanghì; Goldstein and Zanghì) says that in Bohmian mechanics the wave function is not a field in addition to the particles, but represents a law, like the Hamiltonian in classical mechanics. This avoids configuration-space fields, at the cost of making a law depend on time and, on some versions, of requiring an account of how a law can be that complicated.

### Where to push

State the **cost** of each: wave function realism owes the recovery; Bohmian mechanics has a preferred foliation or a nonlocal guidance (lesson 9); GRW changes the dynamics and has free parameters; the primitive ontology demand can be questioned as an unargued commitment to three-dimensional spatiality.

@example
title: Which claim does each approach deny?
problem: Classify (a) Bohmian mechanics, (b) GRW, (c) a theory that keeps the Schrödinger equation and the completeness of the wave function and says all outcomes occur, using Maudlin's three claims.
step: Recall the claims: P1, the wave function is complete; P2, it always evolves linearly; P3, measurements have determinate outcomes.
step: (a) Bohmian mechanics supplies particle positions in addition to the wave function, so it denies P1.
step: (b) GRW adds a stochastic, nonlinear collapse, so it denies P2.
step: (c) A theory keeping P1 and P2 must either deny P3 or reinterpret it; the Everettian reinterprets: each outcome is determinate within a branch, and all occur.
answer: (a) denies P1; (b) denies P2; (c) keeps P1 and P2 and reinterprets P3.

@example
title: The guidance equation for a plane wave
problem: A single particle of mass $m$ in one dimension has wave function $\psi(x) = e^{ikx}$ (a plane wave, up to a constant). Find the velocity the Bohmian guidance equation assigns, and evaluate it for an electron with $k = 2.0 \times 10^{10}\ \text{m}^{-1}$, using $\hbar = 1.054571817 \times 10^{-34}$ J s and $m = 9.1093837 \times 10^{-31}$ kg.
step: In one dimension the guidance equation gives $dQ/dt = (\hbar/m)\,\mathrm{Im}(\psi'/\psi)$ evaluated at $Q$.
step: For $\psi = e^{ikx}$, $\psi'/\psi = ik$, with imaginary part $k$, so $dQ/dt = \hbar k/m$.
step: The velocity is the same everywhere: $\hbar k / m = (1.0546 \times 10^{-34})(2.0 \times 10^{10}) / (9.1094 \times 10^{-31}) \approx 2.32 \times 10^{6}$ m/s.
step: This agrees with the de Broglie relation $p = \hbar k$ and $v = p/m$, as it should for a plane wave. The electron speed is well below the speed of light, so the nonrelativistic equation is consistent here.
answer: $v = \hbar k/m \approx 2.32 \times 10^6$ m/s.

@mistakes
- Saying that wave function realism holds that the wave function is a field in ordinary three-dimensional space. For a many-particle system it is a field on $3N$-dimensional configuration space.
- Saying that Bohmian mechanics is a collapse theory, or that it has no wave function. It has both a wave function and particles, and its evolution of the wave function is linear.
- Reading the recovery argument as a refutation. Its conclusion is that wave function realism owes an account; whether the account can be given is the debate.
- Saying that the primitive ontology program is the same as hidden-variable theories. GRW with flashes has no hidden variables, and it has a primitive ontology.

@practice self
prompt: In 200 words, compare wave function realism and Bohmian mechanics on one question: how do macroscopic objects get located in three-dimensional space? State what each owes.
hint: Say what is fundamental in each theory and what ordinary objects are made of.
solution: In Bohmian mechanics the fundamental matter is $N$ particles with definite positions in three-dimensional space, and macroscopic objects are collections of particles; their location is simply where the particles are, so ordinary objects are located at the outset. What Bohmian mechanics owes is an account of the wave function (a law or a field on configuration space) and of how the particles' guidance by it is compatible with relativity. In wave function realism the fundamental entity is a field on $3N$-dimensional configuration space, and nothing in the fundamental ontology is a located object. Location must be recovered: the realist says the dynamics of the field contain a pattern that functionally plays the role of three-dimensional objects, since the interaction terms couple coordinates in threes. What it owes is that functional account, and an explanation of why we should count a pattern in a high-dimensional field as our tables. The difference in burden is real, but each side can say the other begs a question: the Bohmian assumes the primitive ontology demand, and the wave function realist assumes that functional recovery suffices.
rubric: States correctly what is fundamental in each (particles in three-dimensional space; a field on configuration space).
rubric: States what each owes (the nature of the wave function and relativistic compatibility; the functional recovery of three-dimensional objects).
rubric: Notes that each side can charge the other with an unargued assumption.

@quiz self
prompt: Show that Maudlin's three claims about measurement are jointly inconsistent by describing a measurement of a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle, and then say which claim a Bohmian, a GRW theorist and an Everettian each rejects or reinterprets.
hint: Start with a particle in a superposition of spin-up and spin-down, couple it linearly to a pointer, and ask what the final state is.
solution: Prepare a particle in $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle$, and couple it to a pointer in a ready state $\lvert R\rangle$. A measurement interaction is designed so that $\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert R\rangle \to \lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert P_\uparrow\rangle$ and $\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert R\rangle \to \lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert P_\downarrow\rangle$. By linearity (claim 2), the initial superposition evolves to $a\lvert\uparrow\rangle\lvert P_\uparrow\rangle + b\lvert\downarrow\rangle\lvert P_\downarrow\rangle$. If the wave function is complete (claim 1), this is all there is to the system and pointer, and it is not a state in which the pointer points to one result, contradicting claim 3. The Bohmian denies claim 1: there are also particle positions, and the pointer's position is determinate. The GRW theorist denies claim 2: collapses end the superposition at high rate for a macroscopic pointer. The Everettian keeps claims 1 and 2 and reinterprets claim 3: both outcomes occur, each in its own branch, and each branch has a determinate outcome.
rubric: Derives the entangled superposition correctly from linearity.
rubric: Explains why completeness of the wave function gives no determinate pointer reading.
rubric: Matches Bohm to the denial of completeness, GRW to the denial of linearity, and Everett to a reinterpretation of determinate outcomes.
