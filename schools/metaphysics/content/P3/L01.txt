@article
Start with a collision between two doctrines. David Lewis defended **Humean supervenience**: all facts supervene on the spatiotemporal arrangement of local qualities, a "mosaic" of particular matters of fact. Lewis presented it as a contingent thesis that empirical science might overturn. Tim Maudlin, in *The Metaphysics Within Physics* (2007), argues that quantum mechanics, read in the standard way, does just that: an entangled state of two particles carries information about the pair that is not fixed by the states of the parts taken separately. A metaphysician who wants to keep the mosaic must therefore reinterpret the physics. A critic asks: why should a doctrine hammered out by reflecting on everyday concepts get to veto what our best physics seems to say?

That question is the center of the debate over **naturalized metaphysics** (also called scientific metaphysics): how far should metaphysical theorizing be constrained by, or consist in the interpretation of, our best science?

### Three things "naturalism" can mean

- **Quinean naturalism.** There is no first philosophy prior to science. We learn what exists by finding what our best total theory, regimented in first-order logic, quantifies over: Quine's criterion of ontological commitment. This is a method, not a ban on metaphysics, and the "best theory" includes mathematics.
- **Constraint naturalism.** Metaphysics must answer to physics. Ladyman and Ross, in *Every Thing Must Go* (2007), attack what they call neo-scholastic metaphysics and impose two constraints. The **primacy of physics constraint**: when a hypothesis of fundamental physics conflicts with a metaphysical hypothesis, the metaphysical one is rejected. The **principle of naturalistic closure**: a metaphysical hypothesis is admissible only if it serves to unify at least two scientific hypotheses, at least one of them drawn from fundamental physics. They defend ontic structural realism, which we meet in week 4.
- **Interpretive naturalism.** Maudlin treats metaphysics as the attempt to say what the world is like if our best theories are true; the work is done inside the physics, and the right guide to the nature of laws or time is what the theories require, not a prior analysis.

Bas van Fraassen attacks from a different side. His constructive empiricism (*The Scientific Image*, 1980) says science aims at empirical adequacy, and in *The Empirical Stance* (2002) he criticizes both traditional metaphysics and the claim that science itself mandates a metaphysical picture.

### The argument for constraint

The case for constraint can be reconstructed as follows (a reconstruction in the spirit of Ladyman and Ross, not a quotation):

| P1 | A method for discovering what fundamentally exists is reliable only if its verdicts answer to our best current science. |
| P2 | The method of analyzing everyday concepts and testing the analyses against intuitions about cases is a method whose verdicts do not answer to our best current science. |
| C | So that method is not a reliable way to discover what fundamentally exists. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). Everything depends on the premises.

### Replies, and the premise each denies

- **Against P1:** the demand that verdicts "answer to science" is itself a philosophical thesis, not a result of science, and science is silent on many questions about what exists (how objects are individuated, whether there are properties). So P1 is either self-undermining or too strong.
- **Against P2:** analytic metaphysics is not insulated from science. Philosophers have argued that presentism conflicts with special relativity, and L. A. Paul has argued that metaphysics proceeds by building and comparing theoretical models, as science does, so the contrast between the methods is overdrawn.
- **The underdetermination worry** is better seen as pressure on the program than as a reply to the argument. Physics does not fix its own ontology: standard quantum mechanics is compatible with a wave function alone, with particles guided by it, with branching worlds, and with spontaneous collapse (whose extra predictions, if any, lie beyond what has been tested). To choose among them you must weigh simplicity, unity and fit with other theories, and that is metaphysical work. Defenders answer that this is the point: naturalized metaphysics is that work, done with full knowledge of the physics.

### Where the live questions are

Two further problems matter for your papers. **Which science?** Fundamental physics is incomplete, and the special sciences have their own ontologies; a metaphysics tied to today's physics inherits the risk that the physics will change. And **fit with the program's own norms**: Ladyman and Ross's closure principle rules out hypotheses that do not unify scientific hypotheses, yet many conceptual questions (what it is for one fact to explain another) are plausibly not of that kind. Read the best work on each side as a disagreement about where the burden of proof falls, not as a contest between "science" and "armchair".

@example
title: Locating an objection in the argument
problem: A critic writes: "Your claim that metaphysics must answer to physics is not itself a claim of physics. If only physics-answerable claims are reliable, your own claim is not reliable." Match this to the standard-form argument above and say what the naturalist can reply.
step: Find the target. The critic is talking about the general demand that reliable methods answer to science. That demand is exactly premise P1.
step: State the objection precisely. If P1 is true, P1 is a claim about reliable methods for finding what exists. P1 is not a verdict of physics. So by P1's own standard, the method that produced P1 is unreliable, and the argument undermines itself. The objection does not say that P2 or the inference is bad.
step: Give the best reply. The naturalist can restrict P1 to first-order ontological questions ("what fundamentally exists") and treat the claim about methods as a meta-level claim defended by the track record of scientific inquiry, an empirical argument, not by intuition. The critic may then ask whether a track-record argument is available without already assuming that science is the arbiter.
answer: The objection attacks P1 (it claims self-undermining). The naturalist's reply restricts P1's scope and offers an empirical, track-record defense of the meta-level claim.

@example
title: Testing hypotheses against the two constraints
problem: Apply Ladyman and Ross's two constraints to two hypotheses. (H1) Fundamental particles are tiny hard balls with definite trajectories at all times, whatever quantum theory says. (H2) A single structural hypothesis that is used to unify the treatment of identical particles in quantum mechanics and the treatment of spacetime points in general relativity, as both being individuated only by relations.
step: H1 contradicts a hypothesis of fundamental physics as ordinarily understood (the quantum state does not assign definite trajectories). Under the primacy of physics constraint it is rejected. (Note that a Bohmian replies that the theory, interpreted as the guidance theory, does supply trajectories; the dispute is then about what physics says, not about the constraint.)
step: H2 does not conflict with the physics it addresses. It is also built to unify two scientific hypotheses, in different theories, so it can satisfy the closure principle.
step: Neither constraint says H2 is true. The constraints are admissibility conditions, not confirmations.
answer: H1 fails the primacy constraint (on the standard reading of the physics); H2 can pass both constraints, which makes it admissible, not established.

@mistakes
- Saying that naturalized metaphysics is just "doing physics" or opposing all metaphysics. Quine, Maudlin, and Ladyman and Ross all do metaphysics; they differ about its sources of evidence and its constraints.
- Identifying Quinean naturalism with Ladyman and Ross's two constraints. Quine gives a method for reading off ontological commitment; the constraints are far stronger, and Quine did not state them.
- Treating the underdetermination of ontology by physics as a refutation. It shows the program cannot be "reading off", and the program's defenders answer that choosing among interpretations is exactly what the program has to do.
- Presenting a reconstruction as the author's own words. Say "a reconstruction in the spirit of" when you set out an argument the author did not state in that form.

@practice self
prompt: In one paragraph, state the Humean supervenience thesis and explain why entanglement is said to threaten it. Then say which of the three senses of naturalism (Quinean, constraint, interpretive) a defender of Humean supervenience is most likely to resist, and why.
hint: State what supervenes on what, then ask what entanglement says about wholes and parts.
solution: Humean supervenience holds that all facts supervene on the spatiotemporal distribution of local, intrinsic qualities, the "mosaic". For a pair of particles in an entangled state, the quantum state assigns the pair properties (for instance a definite joint spin state) that are not determined by the states assigned to each particle alone, so, if the state is physically real, the whole has a feature not fixed by the arrangement of local qualities of the parts. A Humean can reply by reading the state as a summary of the pattern of outcomes or as part of the laws, not as a quality of the pair. The Humean is most likely to resist constraint naturalism: the primacy of physics constraint would reject the doctrine whenever the physics, on its standard reading, conflicts with it, whereas the Humean wants the freedom to reinterpret the physics. The interpretive naturalist also reads off the doctrine's falsity, but only after the physics has been interpreted, and the Humean can contest the interpretation.

rubric: Explains the threat from entanglement: joint states not fixed by the separate states, assuming the state is physically real.
rubric: Identifies a sense of naturalism that the Humean would resist and gives a reason tied to what that sense requires.
