@article
Physicists treat laws and symmetries as the heart of a theory: the Schrödinger equation, Maxwell's equations, the invariance of the laws under translations and rotations. Metaphysicians ask what a law is, and what, if anything, symmetries tell us about the world. The two questions are linked because symmetries constrain which laws are possible, and because one popular response to what physics seems to say, **structural realism**, makes structure rather than objects the basic stuff.

### Humean and non-Humean laws

On the **best-system account** (from Mill and Ramsey, developed by Lewis in *Counterfactuals* (1973) and later work), the laws are the generalizations that belong to the deductive system of truths about the world that best balances simplicity and strength (Lewis later added "fit" for chancy laws). Laws are then **Humean**: they supervene on the whole pattern of particular matters of fact, and nothing in the world necessitates or produces the pattern. **Non-Humean** views include Armstrong, Dretske and Tooley's account of laws as relations of necessitation among universals; **dispositional essentialism** (Bird, among others), on which properties have their causal powers essentially and the laws flow from them; and **primitivism** about laws, on which Maudlin takes law as a basic notion that governs or produces the evolution of the world. In the 2020 PhilPapers survey, 54% of respondents accepted or leaned toward non-Humean views and 31% Humean ones; the share holding a view is not evidence that it is true.

The central argument against the Humean concerns explanation:

| P1 | If laws are nothing over and above the best summary of the particular matters of fact, then laws do not explain particular matters of fact (since those facts partly constitute the laws, the explanation would be circular). |
| P2 | Laws explain particular matters of fact. |
| C | So laws are not nothing over and above the best summary of the particular matters of fact. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). A Humean can deny **P1** by distinguishing scientific from metaphysical explanation, as Loewer does: the law explains an instance as a unifying generalization, even though the instance partly grounds the law in the order of metaphysical constitution, and these are different relations. Or the Humean can deny **P2** and say that laws unify but do not explain. A non-Humean answers that scientific explanation is what we care about, so the Humean has given up the explanatory role.

### Symmetries and Noether's theorem

A **symmetry** of a physical theory is a transformation that maps solutions to solutions. **Noether's theorem** (1918) says that for a physical system with an action principle, each continuous symmetry of the action yields a conserved quantity: invariance under translations in time gives conservation of energy, under translations in space gives conservation of linear momentum, and under rotations gives conservation of angular momentum. The theorem has important qualifications (it concerns the action, and the treatment of gauge symmetries is subtle), so state it as above and no more.

The symmetries of Minkowski spacetime form the **Poincaré group**: four translations (one in time, three in space), three rotations and three boosts, so ten generators and ten conserved quantities in special relativistic theories with the full symmetry. The philosophical question is about **direction of explanation**. Do the symmetries of spacetime explain the form of the laws, as the **spacetime** view has it? Or do the dynamical laws of matter explain why spacetime has the structure it appears to have, as in Harvey Brown's **dynamical** approach? And are **gauge** symmetries (which relate descriptions of one physical situation, as the previous lesson showed) reasons to say that the described features are not physically real?

### Structural realism

Scientific realists face two worries. The pessimistic induction says that past successful theories were later abandoned, so current ones will be too. Yet the mathematical structure of past theories (Fresnel's wave equations, for instance) was largely retained. **Epistemic structural realism** (Worrall, 1989) concludes that we should believe in the structure of our best theories, while the nature of what has that structure is unknown. **Ontic structural realism** (Ladyman, 1998; French and Ladyman) makes a stronger claim about the world: structure is all there is, or objects are derivative of structure. Its main motivation is quantum mechanics: identical particles, whose permutation does not change the physical state, are said not to be individuals with a primitive identity.

The main objection is that relations require relata:

| P1 | A relation exists only if there are things that stand in it, with some identity or nature of their own. |
| P2 | Ontic structural realism says there are relations (structure) but no objects with an identity or nature of their own that stand in them. |
| C | So ontic structural realism is incoherent. |

This is valid. The ontic structural realist denies **P1**: relations may be fundamental and the relata derivative, so that objects are nodes in the structure and have no identity apart from it. A **moderate** structural realist (Esfeld and Lam, for instance) denies **P2** as stated, accepting objects but holding that their identity is wholly given by their relations, with the objects and relations being on a par. Critics reply that an object with no identity apart from its relations makes the explanation circular, as when a node is individuated by relations to nodes that are individuated by relations to it.

### What is open

There is no consensus on whether Humean laws can ground the explanatory role of physics, on whether symmetries are explanatory or merely descriptive, or on what structure is. When you write about one of these, state which theory you consider (Newtonian, special relativistic, quantum) because the answers differ.

@example
title: Counting the Poincaré generators in other dimensions
problem: In $n$ spacetime dimensions the Poincaré group has $n$ translations and the rotations and boosts of the Lorentz group, which together number $n(n-1)/2$. How many generators does it have for $n = 2$, and how many conserved quantities follow by Noether's theorem if the full symmetry is a symmetry of the action?
step: Translations: $n = 2$ gives 2 (one in time, one in space).
step: Lorentz transformations: $n(n-1)/2 = 2 \cdot 1 / 2 = 1$. With one space dimension there are no rotations and just one boost.
step: Total: $2 + 1 = 3$ generators.
step: By Noether's theorem each continuous symmetry of the action gives a conserved quantity, so there are three: energy, momentum and the boost charge (which, in the 3+1 case, is the one associated with the motion of the center of energy).
answer: 3 generators and 3 conserved quantities.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: Take the explanatory argument against Humeanism. A Humean writes: "A law explains an instance by subsuming it under a pattern; that the instance is part of what makes the pattern the best system does not make the explanation circular, because scientific explanation and metaphysical grounding are different relations." Say which premise this denies, and give a non-Humean's best response.
step: Find what the reply grants. It grants that laws explain instances (P2) and grants that instances partly constitute the laws.
step: Find what it denies. It says that constitution of the law by its instances does not stop the law from explaining them, since the explanation is of a different kind. That is a denial of P1, the conditional linking being a summary to failing to explain.
step: The non-Humean's best response: if scientific explanation is just subsumption under a pattern, then the pattern's explanatory force is the force of the best summary of the facts, and one wants to know why the pattern holds. A Humean can say the question has no answer.
answer: It denies P1. The non-Humean replies that subsumption alone is a thin form of explanation that leaves the pattern itself unexplained.

@mistakes
- Saying that the best-system account makes laws "mere regularities". The best system must be simple and strong, so not every regularity is a law; the question is what makes the best system better than others.
- Stating Noether's theorem as "every symmetry yields a conservation law" without the restriction to continuous symmetries of the action.
- Confusing epistemic and ontic structural realism: the first limits what we can know, the second says what there is.
- Citing the proportion of philosophers who are non-Humeans as an argument for non-Humeanism.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in 150 to 200 words how a Humean about laws and a primitivist about laws would each reply to the claim that Noether's theorem shows that laws of conservation are explained by symmetries.
hint: Say what each takes a law to be, and then what each says explains the symmetry or the conservation.
solution: The claim is that conservation of energy is explained by the time-translation symmetry of the action. A Humean says laws are the best summary of the pattern of events. The symmetry is then a feature of the best system: it is a reason why the system is simple, and Noether's theorem is a mathematical relation among laws within the system. It does not explain the conservation by anything outside the pattern, so the Humean reads the explanation as unification, not as production. A primitivist, such as Maudlin, holds that the laws govern the evolution of the world and are not reducible to the pattern. For the primitivist the symmetry is a property of the laws, so it is a fact about how the laws govern, and the conservation is a consequence of that governance. Noether's theorem shows how two features of the law are related, but the primitivist can add that what explains the conservation is the law itself. Both can accept the theorem; they differ about what it adds to the explanation of the conserved quantity.
