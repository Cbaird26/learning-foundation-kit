@article
Suppose the Schrödinger equation is the whole story: no collapse, no added particles. A spin measurement then leaves a superposition of the observer seeing "up" and seeing "down". Hugh Everett's relative-state proposal (1957) was that this is exactly what happens, and that we should take each term of the superposition to describe the observer's experience in a branch. The view, later called the **many-worlds interpretation** (Bryce DeWitt's popularization), faces two questions: what are the branches, and what could probability be if every outcome occurs?

### Branches and decoherence

A term-by-term reading of a superposition needs a preferred basis. The contemporary answer, developed by Zurek, by Saunders, and by Wallace in *The Emergent Multiverse* (2012), is **decoherence**: when a system interacts with a large environment, interference between certain macroscopically distinct components becomes negligible, and the dynamics of each component are, for all practical purposes, independent. Branches are then **emergent** and approximate: patterns in the universal wave function with a quasi-classical dynamics, real in the way that a tiger is real (Dennett's "real patterns"), not part of the fundamental ontology. Because decoherence is approximate and continuous, there is no fact about exactly how many branches exist, a point that will matter.

### The probability problems

In a Born-rule experiment, suppose a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle in the state $\alpha\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \beta\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ with $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2 + \lvert\beta\rvert^2 = 1$ is measured. The **Born rule** says the probability of "up" is $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2$. The Everettian says that both outcomes occur, so there are two problems. The **incoherence problem** is that probabilities are measures of uncertainty about which outcome will occur and there is no such uncertainty. The **quantitative problem** is why the weights should be $\lvert\alpha\rvert^2$ and not $1/2$ for each branch, as naive branch counting would suggest. The branch weight of a branch is the squared norm of its component.

The incoherence argument:

| P1 | If Everettian quantum mechanics is true, then after a measurement every outcome occurs in some branch, with certainty. |
| P2 | If every outcome occurs in some branch with certainty, then there is nothing about which outcome occurs that an agent could be uncertain about. |
| P3 | If there is nothing about which outcome occurs that an agent could be uncertain about, then probability cannot play its ordinary role as a measure of uncertainty about which outcome will occur. |
| C | So if Everettian quantum mechanics is true, probability cannot play its ordinary role as a measure of uncertainty about which outcome will occur. |

It is valid. The replies divide as follows.

- **Against P2: self-locating uncertainty.** Saunders (1998) argued that an agent before a measurement can treat her future branch-selves much as in fission cases, and Vaidman held that after a splitting but before observing the result, the agent is uncertain which branch she is in. Sebens and Carroll (2018) derive the Born rule from an assumption of **epistemic separability**: the credence one should assign to being one of several observers with identical experiences does not depend on the details of those observers' environment. This is uncertainty about location, not about what the world contains.
- **Against P3: decision theory.** Deutsch (1999) and Wallace (*The Emergent Multiverse*, with Greaves's related work) argue that the role of probability is to guide rational action. Given axioms of rationality (for example, that the agent is indifferent to branching as such and that preferences are consistent across different ways of dividing up the same outcomes), the agent's preferences must be those of an expected-utility maximizer with Born weights. The incoherence is dissolved by redefining what probabilities are for.
- **Critics:** Albert, Kent and Baker, among others, argue that the axioms smuggle in the result (for example, that rationality requires caring about branches in proportion to their weight), or that it is unclear why the branch weight, rather than the number of branches, should matter to an agent who is certain all will occur.

### Branch counting

The quantitative problem is sharp. If a state $\sqrt{0.2}\lvert\uparrow\rangle + \sqrt{0.8}\lvert\downarrow\rangle$ is measured, branch counting gives probability $1/2$ for each outcome, but the observed statistics follow $0.2$ and $0.8$. Wallace replies that branches are not countable, since decoherence does not determine their number, so counting has no content; critics answer that this defends the weights only by making branch counting unavailable, not by explaining why weights matter.

### Alternatives and costs

A **many-minds** theory (Albert and Loewer) makes minds, not worlds, the things that branch. The Everettian faces two further costs: the **ontological** cost of an emergent multiverse, and the **epistemic** question of how the theory is confirmed if probabilities are in doubt. The Everettian replies that other interpretations pay costs of their own, as we saw last week.

@example
title: Born weights versus branch counting
problem: A spin state has amplitudes $\sqrt{0.2}$ for "up" and $\sqrt{0.8}$ for "down", and the same preparation is measured twice in succession. Give the Born weights of the four branches, say what branch counting gives for "up, up", and say what the discrepancy shows.
step: The branches are UU, UD, DU, DD, with weights $0.2 \times 0.2 = 0.04$, $0.2 \times 0.8 = 0.16$, $0.8 \times 0.2 = 0.16$ and $0.8 \times 0.8 = 0.64$. They sum to $1$, as they should.
step: Branch counting gives each of the four branches weight $1/4 = 0.25$, so "up, up" has $25\%$ against the Born weight of $4\%$.
step: An observer repeating the experiment many times sees relative frequencies of "up" near $0.2$ if the Born weights apply. Under counting, frequencies near $0.5$ would be expected, contradicting the quantum statistics.
step: The discrepancy shows that if the theory is to account for the statistics, the Everettian must justify the use of branch weights, not branch counts; this is the quantitative problem.
answer: Born weights $0.04, 0.16, 0.16, 0.64$; counting gives $25\%$ for "up, up", which would conflict with observed statistics.

@example
title: Matching a reply to a premise
problem: A defender writes: "Immediately after the measurement but before I look at the pointer, I do not know which branch I am in, so there is something I am uncertain about." Match this to the incoherence argument and say what a critic might say.
step: The defender grants P1: all outcomes occur in some branch.
step: The defender denies P2: there is something to be uncertain about, namely which branch the agent herself is in. The uncertainty concerns self-location, not what the world contains.
step: A critic says that this uncertainty only arises after the branching and says nothing about the situation before the measurement, which is when the Born rule is used for prediction. And the critic asks why the credences about location should follow the Born weights.
answer: The reply denies P2. The critic questions whether it covers pre-measurement prediction and whether it derives the weights.

@mistakes
- Saying that the many-worlds interpretation adds worlds to the theory. It adds nothing to the Schrödinger equation: branches are said to be emergent structure in the wave function.
- Saying that decoherence by itself solves the probability problem. It addresses the preferred basis problem; the Born-rule problem is separate.
- Treating branch counting as the Everettian view. Most Everettians use weights, and argue that counting is ill-defined.
- Attributing the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule to Everett. It is due to Deutsch, developed by Wallace and Greaves.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in 150 to 200 words why the self-locating reply to the incoherence problem does not by itself solve the quantitative problem, and what an Everettian needs to add.
hint: Say what the reply gives you (uncertainty) and what it does not give you (the numbers).
solution: The self-locating reply says that, after splitting, I am uncertain which branch I am in, and so there is something to which credence can attach. That answers the incoherence problem. It does not tell me what credences to have. If I am one of two branches and I have no further information, indifference would give $1/2$ for each, regardless of their weights, which is the counting answer and conflicts with the Born statistics. To get the Born weights the Everettian needs an additional principle connecting credence to branch weight: for example, Sebens and Carroll's epistemic separability principle, together with a symmetry argument for cases of equal amplitude, or Wallace's decision-theoretic axioms. The reply thus changes the problem from "what is probability about?" to "why do the weights matter?" and the second question has to be answered separately.
