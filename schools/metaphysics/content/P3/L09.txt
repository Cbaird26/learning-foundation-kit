@article
Two particles are prepared in a singlet state and sent to distant laboratories. Each lab chooses one of two measurement settings at random and records a binary outcome. Quantum mechanics predicts correlations between the outcomes that depend on the angle between the settings. Einstein, Podolsky and Rosen (1935) argued that such correlations show that quantum mechanics is incomplete, assuming locality. John Bell (1964) showed that no theory that is local in a precise sense can reproduce the quantum predictions, and the experiments agree with quantum mechanics.

### The mathematics

For a spin-$\tfrac12$ singlet, with measurement directions in a common plane at angles $x$ and $y$, the quantum correlation is $E(x,y) = -\cos(x - y)$. The **CHSH quantity** (Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt, 1969) for settings $a, a'$ in one lab and $b, b'$ in the other is
$$S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b').$$
Local hidden-variable theories imply $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$. Quantum mechanics allows up to $\lvert S\rvert = 2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ (**Tsirelson's bound**), reached for example with $a = 0^\circ$, $a' = 90^\circ$, $b = 45^\circ$, $b' = 135^\circ$. Experiments with entangled photons, beginning with Clauser's and Aspect's tests, violate the inequality as quantum mechanics predicts; tests in 2015 by three groups (Delft with electron spins, Vienna and NIST with photons) closed the main loopholes together, and the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for experiments with entangled photons that established violations of Bell inequalities.

### Bell's argument

Let $\lambda$ be the complete state in a hidden-variable theory, $A$ and $B$ the outcomes, and $x$ and $y$ the settings. Bell's derivation uses three assumptions. **Single outcomes**: each run has one definite outcome. **Factorizability** (Bell's local causality): $P(A,B \mid x,y,\lambda) = P(A \mid x,\lambda)\,P(B \mid y,\lambda)$. **Measurement independence** (also called freedom of choice): $\lambda$ is statistically independent of the settings $x$ and $y$.

| P1 | If every run has a single definite outcome, the outcome probabilities factorize given the complete state and the settings, and the complete state is statistically independent of the settings, then $\lvert S\rvert \le 2$. |
| P2 | In suitable experiments, $\lvert S\rvert > 2$. |
| C | So at least one of the three assumptions (single outcomes, factorizability, measurement independence) fails. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). The positions differ about which assumption goes.

| Approach | Gives up | How |
|---|---|---|
| Bohmian mechanics, collapse theories | Factorizability | The probability of one wing's outcome depends on the other wing's setting or outcome |
| Everettian quantum mechanics | Single outcomes | Everettians say that all outcomes occur in branches, so that, on their view, Bell's derivation does not apply |
| Superdeterminism (Bell raised it; 't Hooft defends a version) | Measurement independence | The hidden state is correlated with the later choice of settings |
| Retrocausal models (Price, Wharton) | Measurement independence | The settings influence the hidden state by an influence that runs backward in time |

Everettians hold, in addition, that their theory is local in a sense that avoids a conflict with relativity (Deutsch and Hayden; Wallace and Timpson discuss it); critics dispute whether the sense is the relevant one.

### Two ways to fail factorizability

Jarrett and Shimony split factorizability into **parameter independence** (the probability of $A$ does not depend on $y$, given $\lambda$ and $x$) and **outcome independence** (it does not depend on $B$, given $\lambda$, $x$ and $y$). Standard quantum mechanics satisfies parameter independence and violates outcome independence, which is why it does not permit **signaling**: the **no-signaling theorem** says that the statistics at one lab are unaffected by the choice at the other. Shimony called this **peaceful coexistence** between quantum mechanics and special relativity. Bohmian mechanics violates parameter independence at the level of the hidden variable (one particle's motion depends on the other's setting), but the quantum equilibrium distribution hides this from operational statistics.

### The tension

The question is whether no-signaling suffices for compatibility with relativity. Tim Maudlin (*Quantum Non-Locality and Relativity*) argues that it does not, since relativity is a claim about the fundamental structure of spacetime and not only about what signals can be sent. The argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | If the violation of factorizability is a causal influence from one wing to the other, then the influence is directed: one measurement influences the other. |
| P2 | If a causal influence is directed between two spacelike separated events, then there is a fact about which of the two events is the cause, and therefore occurs first. |
| P3 | If there is a fact about which of two spacelike separated events occurs first, then spacetime has a preferred foliation or frame beyond the Minkowski structure. |
| C | So if the violation of factorizability is a causal influence, spacetime has a preferred foliation or frame beyond the Minkowski structure. |

It is valid (a chain of conditionals). Special relativity has no invariant temporal order for spacelike separated events, which is what motivates P3. Replies: against **P1**, the correlation may be a **holistic** feature of the joint state and not a causal influence ("passion at a distance" without action); against **P2**, the dependence may be symmetric or undirected, as in a relativistic GRW flash theory for non-interacting particles (Tumulka, 2006), whose joint law does not single out an order; or one may accept the conclusion, as Bohmian mechanics with a foliation does. The cost of each is the topic of the presentation you are to prepare.

@example
title: A CHSH value from correlation functions
problem: Use $E(x,y) = -\cos(x-y)$ with settings $a = 0^\circ$, $a' = 60^\circ$, $b = 30^\circ$, $b' = 90^\circ$ in $S = E(a,b) - E(a,b') + E(a',b) + E(a',b')$. Does the value violate the local bound?
step: $E(a,b) = -\cos(0^\circ - 30^\circ) = -\cos 30^\circ \approx -0.866$.
step: $E(a,b') = -\cos(0^\circ - 90^\circ) = -\cos 90^\circ = 0$.
step: $E(a',b) = -\cos(60^\circ - 30^\circ) = -0.866$ and $E(a',b') = -\cos(60^\circ - 90^\circ) = -0.866$.
step: $S = -0.866 - 0 - 0.866 - 0.866 = -2.598$, so $\lvert S\rvert \approx 2.60 > 2$: the local bound is violated, though the value is below Tsirelson's $2.83$.
answer: $\lvert S\rvert \approx 2.60$, which exceeds 2 and is below $2\sqrt{2}$.

@example
title: Spacelike separation and the light-travel time
problem: Two measurement stations are $20$ km apart. Using $c = 299{,}792{,}458$ m/s, how long does light take to travel between them, and what must be true of the two measurement events for them to be spacelike separated?
step: The time is $d/c = 20{,}000 / 299{,}792{,}458 \approx 6.67 \times 10^{-5}$ s, which is about $66.7\ \mu$s.
step: Two events are spacelike separated if $c\,\lvert\Delta t\rvert < \lvert\Delta x\rvert$, that is, if the time between them is less than $66.7\ \mu$s in this frame.
step: If the events satisfy this, no signal limited by $c$ can connect them, and different inertial frames disagree about their order.
answer: About $66.7\ \mu$s; the events must occur within $66.7\ \mu$s of each other.

@mistakes
- Saying that Bell's theorem proves that faster-than-light signaling is possible. The no-signaling theorem says that it is not, in quantum mechanics.
- Saying that Bell's theorem refutes quantum mechanics. Its predictions are confirmed; what is ruled out are local hidden-variable theories satisfying the three assumptions.
- Saying that "nonlocality" means the same in every approach. Bohmian mechanics, collapse theories, and holistic or relational readings of nonlocality differ in what they say is nonlocal.
- Forgetting the third assumption. Superdeterministic and retrocausal views deny measurement independence, not factorizability.

@practice self
prompt: In 200 words, explain the difference between parameter independence and outcome independence, say which one standard quantum mechanics violates, and explain why the answer matters for whether quantum mechanics is compatible with special relativity.
hint: Say what each condition says about the probability of $A$, and connect to the no-signaling theorem.
solution: Parameter independence says that, given the complete state $\lambda$ and the local setting $x$, the probability of the outcome $A$ does not depend on the distant setting $y$. Outcome independence says that, given $\lambda$, $x$ and $y$, it does not depend on the distant outcome $B$. Together they give factorizability. Standard quantum mechanics, with the quantum state as the complete state, satisfies parameter independence and violates outcome independence: the marginal statistics at one lab do not depend on the distant setting (the no-signaling theorem), but the outcomes are correlated even given the state. This is why Shimony spoke of peaceful coexistence: an influence that depends on outcomes cannot be controlled, so it cannot be used to signal. It matters because parameter dependence would allow, in principle, a controllable influence, and would be a more direct violation of the spirit of relativity. It is not the end of the question, since Maudlin and others argue that compatibility with the letter of no-signaling does not show compatibility with relativity as a thesis about the structure of spacetime, which is the topic of the standard-form argument above.
