A town has money for only one project: a new playground, repairs to the bus station, or a heating subsidy for a few elderly residents. How should it choose? A natural answer is to ask what will do the most good. That is the core of **consequentialism**: the rightness of an action depends only on its consequences. The best-known version is **utilitarianism**.

### The classical theory

Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, the founders of classical utilitarianism, held that the right action is the one that produces the greatest happiness for the greatest number. In its modern form the **principle of utility** says: an action is right if and only if it produces at least as much total well-being, counting everyone affected equally, as any alternative open to the agent. Three ideas are packed into this.

1. **Consequentialism**: only outcomes matter. Motives and rules matter only through their effects.
2. **Welfarism**: what counts as a good outcome is the well-being of those affected. (Bentham and Mill identified well-being with pleasure and the absence of pain, a view called **hedonism**; later versions count satisfied preferences instead.)
3. **Impartiality and aggregation**: everyone's well-being counts the same, regardless of who they are, and it is summed.

Bentham proposed that pleasures can be compared by features such as intensity and duration, and the number of people affected. Mill, in *Utilitarianism* (first published in 1861), added that pleasures differ in quality: he argued that the pleasures of the intellect, of feeling and imagination are more valuable than mere bodily ones, and that those who have experienced both prefer the higher ones. Critics ask whether this is consistent with a view that says only pleasure matters.

### Act and rule utilitarianism

**Act utilitarianism** applies the principle of utility directly to each action. **Rule utilitarianism** says an action is right if it follows a rule whose general acceptance would produce the most well-being. The difference matters in cases where breaking a rule would produce a slightly better result once.

### Objections and replies

**The objection from justice.** Suppose a crime has occurred and the town will be calmer if someone is blamed. A utilitarian who adds up consequences might favor punishing an innocent person if that really produces more well-being. Critics say this shows utilitarianism ignores justice and rights. *Replies:* an act utilitarian can say that such cases are unrealistic, since the deception is likely to be found out and trust would suffer; a rule utilitarian says we should follow a rule against punishing the innocent because the rule's acceptance does well overall. Critics respond that the rule version risks collapsing into the act version when it allows exceptions, or else is irrational rule-worship when it does not.

**The demandingness objection.** If every person counts equally, then buying a luxury when the same money could greatly help strangers seems wrong. Critics say this demands too much. Utilitarians reply either that morality *is* demanding, or that a sensible version allows personal projects.

**Hedonism and the experience machine.** Nozick's thought experiment asks whether you would plug into a machine that gives you any experience you like. Many people say no, which suggests that something other than pleasant experience matters. Hedonists can reply that the intuition reflects status quo bias or that other theories of well-being fit utilitarianism too.

**Predicting consequences.** We cannot know all the consequences of what we do. Utilitarians reply that we can use reasonable expectations, and that the **expected value** of an act can be estimated.

### Using the principle

To apply the theory, list the options, estimate how each affects everyone, add up the well-being, and choose the option with the highest total. Notice that the theory does not say that the majority wins: a small group with a very large stake can outweigh a large group with a small stake.

@example
title: Counting well-being
problem: A school can fund one of three activities. The table shows the estimated gain in well-being, on a scale of 0 to 10, for each group. Which does the principle of utility favor?

| Option | 10 chess players | 40 football players | 4 students with disabilities |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chess room | 5 each | 0 each | 0 each |
| Pitch repairs | 0 each | 2 each | 0 each |
| Accessible entrance | 0 each | 1 each | 9 each |

step: Compute the totals per option: multiply each group's gain by the number of people. Chess room: 10 x 5 = 50.
step: Pitch repairs: 40 x 2 = 80.
step: Accessible entrance: 40 x 1 + 4 x 9 = 40 + 36 = 76.
step: Compare: pitch repairs (80) beat the accessible entrance (76) and the chess room (50). The principle of utility favors the pitch repairs on these estimates.
step: Note what the calculation depends on: the estimates. If the football players' gain from the entrance were 0 instead of 1, the entrance would total 36 and still lose. If the disabled students' gain were 11 each, it would total 84 and win. Utilitarians therefore need good estimates, and critics ask whether such numbers can be given at all.
answer: Pitch repairs (total 80), though the result depends on the estimates.

@example
title: Act versus rule
problem: A shopkeeper could cheat a customer out of a small amount without ever being found out, and the extra money would make the shopkeeper slightly happier than the customer is unhappy. What do act and rule utilitarianism say?
step: Act utilitarianism: look only at the consequences of this one act. If, as stipulated, the net total is positive and no one finds out, the act utilitarian seems to have to approve, unless the effects on the shopkeeper's character, or other effects, change the total.
step: Rule utilitarianism: ask which rule, if generally accepted, would produce the most well-being: "do not cheat customers" or "cheat when it benefits you and you will not be caught". A society with the first rule has trust and efficient trade, so the first rule does better overall.
step: So the rule utilitarian says do not cheat, even if this one act would have the better outcome.
step: The act utilitarian can answer that real acts of cheating erode trust and the shopkeeper's own habits, so even the act version often agrees. The disagreement is over the stipulated case in which cheating really has the best consequences.
answer: Act utilitarianism may permit it if cheating really maximizes well-being; rule utilitarianism forbids it because the rule against cheating does better.

@mistakes
- Saying that utilitarianism means "the majority wins". It sums well-being, so a minority with a large stake can outweigh a majority with a small stake.
- Thinking utilitarians only care about their own happiness. The theory is impartial: everyone affected counts equally.
- Confusing consequentialism (the general idea that outcomes decide) with utilitarianism (one version that counts well-being and sums it).
- Saying that an act that turns out badly was therefore wrong on utilitarianism. Many versions judge by expected consequences, or distinguish the act from the agent's blameworthiness.

Program B raises the well-being of 12 people by 4 points each and lowers that of 3 people by 2 points each. What is the total change in well-being under the better option for the classical utilitarian?
answer: 47
check: max(6 * 7 + 5 * 1, 12 * 4 - 3 * 2)
hint: Work out each program's total: gains minus losses.
solution: Program A: 6 x 7 + 5 x 1 = 42 + 5 = 47. Program B: 12 x 4 - 3 x 2 = 48 - 6 = 42. The classical utilitarian favors Program A, with a total of 47.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain the objection from demandingness against utilitarianism, and give one reply a utilitarian could make.
hint: Think about how much the theory asks of someone with spare money, when the same money could help others a great deal.
solution: The objection says that utilitarianism asks too much. If everyone's well-being counts equally and we must always produce the most, then buying a concert ticket while the same money could save someone from serious hardship seems forbidden, and ordinary life with projects and special concern for family looks nearly always wrong. Critics say a theory that makes almost everything we do wrong cannot be right about what morality requires. A utilitarian could reply that morality really is demanding, and that our comfort is not evidence against the theory but evidence that we do too little. Another reply is that a sensible utilitarian recognizes that people who are allowed their own lives and projects are more productive of well-being overall, so the best rules leave room for personal life.
rubric: States the objection correctly: equal weight to everyone's well-being appears to demand most of our resources and time.
rubric: Explains why this is thought to be a problem, for example that ordinary life with personal projects would be almost always wrong.
rubric: Gives a genuine utilitarian reply, such as accepting the demand or arguing that rules leaving room for personal life produce more well-being.

answer: 1

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in a short paragraph how a utilitarian would decide whether a student who knows the answer to a test question should tell it to a friend who will otherwise fail. Then state one objection to the utilitarian method and one reply.
hint: List the effects on the friend, the student, the class and the school, and recall the objection that predictions are uncertain or that fairness is ignored.
solution: A utilitarian would list the likely effects: the friend avoids failing, which is a benefit, but the other students who studied are disadvantaged if grades are curved, the test no longer measures what it was meant to, the student risks punishment, and if cheating becomes common the value of grades erodes for everyone. Adding these up, most versions would likely judge that helping the friend cheat lowers total well-being, especially if one accepts that a rule of tolerating cheating would spread. An objection is that the method depends on predictions and numbers that no one can really know, so the result can be manipulated. A reply is that decisions in daily life always involve uncertainty, and the utilitarian can use reasonable expected values and general rules of thumb.
