@article
When you say "Cruelty is wrong", are you describing a fact, like "Water boils at 100 degrees Celsius at sea level", or doing something else, such as expressing disapproval or telling people what to do? **Metaethics** studies the nature of moral claims. Its questions are: what do moral statements mean, can they be true, and if so, what makes them true, and how could we know?

### Two questions, four positions

**Cognitivism** says moral statements express beliefs and can be true or false. **Non-cognitivism** says they do not express beliefs but something else, such as attitudes or commands. **Moral realism** says there are objective moral facts, true independently of what anyone thinks. Positions combine these answers:

| Position | What it says |
|---|---|
| **Moral realism** | Moral statements can be true, and some are true because of objective moral facts. |
| **Error theory** | Moral statements aim to describe objective facts, but there are none, so they are all false. |
| **Expressivism** (non-cognitivism) | Moral statements express attitudes (approval, disapproval) rather than describe facts. |
| **Subjectivism / relativism** | Moral statements can be true, but their truth depends on the attitudes of individuals or groups. |

Realists divide into **naturalists**, who say moral facts are natural facts (such as facts about well-being) that can be studied empirically, and **non-naturalists**, who say moral facts are a distinct kind. Some philosophers, called **constructivists**, hold that moral truths are what would result from an idealized process of reasoning or agreement; whether they count as realists is itself discussed.

Many philosophers also ask about moral **motivation**: sincerely judging that you ought to give aid seems to move you to do it, as Hume argued that reason alone cannot move us, and expressivists use this to support their view.

### Arguments against realism

**Mackie's argument from queerness.** In *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977), J. L. Mackie defended an error theory. He argued that objective moral facts would be strange: unlike anything else in the universe, they would have an inherent action-guiding quality that gives everyone a reason to act, whatever they want, and we would need a strange faculty to know them. This can be set out as:

| P1 | If there were objective moral facts, they would be metaphysically and epistemologically strange (intrinsically action-guiding, and knowable by an odd faculty). |
| P2 | We should not believe in entities that are that strange without strong reason. |
| P3 | There is no strong reason to believe in them. |
| C | So we should not believe in objective moral facts. |

Replies: realists say that moral facts are not stranger than facts about numbers or logic, and that their evidence is our considered moral judgments, such as that cruelty is wrong. Some argue that reasons for action are not strange once we think of normative facts in general.

**Disagreement.** Mackie also used the argument from relativity: moral disagreement is better explained by differing ways of life than by perceiving objective facts. We saw in week 2 that disagreement alone is a weak argument, though defenders reply that the best explanation of moral disagreement matters.

### Arguments for realism and against naturalism

**The open question argument.** G. E. Moore, in *Principia Ethica* (1903), argued that goodness cannot be identified with any natural property. For any natural property, such as pleasure, it makes sense to ask "this is pleasant, but is it good?" The question is *open*, meaning it is not trivial in the way "this is a bachelor, but is he unmarried?" is. So "good" does not mean "pleasant". Critics say that two terms can refer to the same thing without meaning the same (as "water" and "H2O" do), so the argument at most shows that the terms differ in meaning, not that the properties differ.

**The argument from moral conviction.** Some realists argue that we are more certain that torturing someone for fun is wrong than we are of any philosophical premise against realism, so if the premises of an anti-realist argument conflict with that, it is the premises that should go. Critics reply that confidence is a psychological fact that can be explained, for example by evolution and upbringing, without there being a moral fact.

### Where the field stands

In a 2020 survey of professional philosophers, about 62% accepted or leaned toward moral realism and about 26% anti-realism. The share of philosophers holding a view is not evidence that the view is true.

@example
title: Reconstructing an argument against realism
problem: Reconstruct this reasoning in standard form: "Moral facts would be unlike any physical facts. We could never see or measure one. So we should not believe in them."
step: Find the conclusion: "We should not believe in moral facts."
step: Find the premises: moral facts would be unlike physical facts and not observable or measurable.
step: Add the hidden premise: we should believe only in kinds of facts we can observe or measure, or that are otherwise supported by a good explanation.
step: Set out the argument.

| P1 | Moral facts would be unlike physical facts and could not be observed or measured. |
| P2 | We should believe only in kinds of facts that can be observed or measured, or that figure in good explanations. |
| C | So we should not believe in moral facts. |
step: Evaluate: the argument is valid. A realist can attack premise P2: it appears to rule out mathematical and logical facts, and P2 itself is not something observed or measured.
answer: The hidden premise P2 does the work, and a realist's best objection is that P2 would also rule out mathematics and itself.

@example
title: Sorting positions
problem: Four people respond to "Cruelty is wrong". Ana: "True, and it is true whatever anyone thinks." Ben: "It means 'Boo to cruelty!'; it expresses disapproval." Cai: "It aims to state an objective fact, but there are none, so it is false." Dee: "It is true because my society disapproves of cruelty." Name each person's position.
step: Ana says it is true independently of what anyone thinks, so she is a **moral realist**.
step: Ben says that the statement expresses an attitude and does not describe a fact, so he is an **expressivist**, a kind of non-cognitivist.
step: Cai says moral statements aim at objective facts but fail, so he holds an **error theory**.
step: Dee makes the truth depend on society's attitudes, so she is a **relativist** or subjectivist.
answer: Ana is a realist, Ben an expressivist, Cai an error theorist and Dee a relativist.

@mistakes
- Treating anti-realism as the view that morality does not matter. Anti-realists can hold strong moral commitments and treat them as attitudes or as dependent on human responses.
- Confusing error theory (moral claims are false) with expressivism (moral claims are not in the business of being true or false in the same way).
- Thinking that realism implies that people agree about morality or that moral facts are easy to know.
- Thinking that the open question argument shows that moral facts exist. It was an argument about the meaning of "good" and against naturalism.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the difference between moral realism and error theory by applying each to the statement "It is wrong to humiliate someone for fun". Then give one consideration that favors realism and one that favors error theory.
hint: Say whether each view counts the statement as true or false and why.
solution: The realist says the statement is true, and true because of an objective moral fact that does not depend on what anyone thinks. The error theorist agrees that the statement aims at an objective fact but says no such fact exists, so the statement is false, however much we may feel it is right. A consideration favoring realism is that we are very confident about the wrongness of humiliating someone for fun, more so than of any philosophical premise against it. A consideration favoring error theory is Mackie's argument from queerness: objective facts that guide action by their nature would be unlike anything else and hard to know. Each side has replies, and philosophers disagree about which is stronger.
