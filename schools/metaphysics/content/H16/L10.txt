@article
You are walking past a shallow pond and see a small child in danger of drowning. You could wade in and save her, but your new shoes would be ruined. Almost everyone says you must save her. Now suppose that for the price of the shoes you could help save the life of a child far away through a reliable charity. Is there any difference? This is the challenge of **Peter Singer's** essay "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972).

### The argument

Singer's argument starts from a principle he thought few could reject: if it is in our power to prevent something very bad from happening, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it. He then applied it to people in affluent countries and those facing famine and extreme poverty.

| P1 | Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad. |
| P2 | If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to do it. |
| P3 | It is in our power to prevent some of this suffering by giving money, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance (for example, by giving up luxuries). |
| C | So we ought to give. |

Singer also argued that distance should not matter: the fact that someone is far away, or that many others could help too, does not change the *moral* importance of the suffering. The drowning child analogy supports premise P2 by appealing to a case where nearly everyone accepts the principle.

### Objections and replies

1. **Distance.** The child at the pond is in front of you; the strangers are far away. Singer replies that distance does not reduce the badness of suffering, and that modern communication and aid make distant help possible. A critic can reply that special relationships and nearness may carry some moral weight, though perhaps not as much as people assume.
2. **Other people.** If many people could help, why should I? Singer replies that others' failure to do their share does not remove my duty, though it may change what I should do.
3. **Demandingness.** If the argument is right, we must give until giving more would sacrifice something of comparable importance, which seems to require a drastic change in how we live. Singer accepted that the conclusion is demanding; critics say a theory should not demand so much. A weaker version of the principle, requiring us to give up only what is *not very* important, is less demanding but still requires a lot.
4. **Rights and property.** Some argue that people have a right to what they have justly acquired, and that others cannot claim it. Robert Nozick's *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) defended an entitlement theory of justice on which holdings acquired and transferred justly are not subject to redistribution. On this view giving may be admirable, but it is charity and not a duty. Replies: even if property rights are strong, they may not be unlimited, and extreme poverty may weigh against them.
5. **Effectiveness.** Does aid work? Critics have argued that some aid is wasted or harmful; defenders respond that evidence-based programs have measurable success, and that the question is empirical and affects what we ought to do, not whether we have a duty.
6. **Justice or charity.** The ethical tradition distinguishes duties of **justice**, which others can claim as owed, from **charity**, which is good to do but not owed. Some philosophers argue that rich countries have duties of justice because of their role in global institutions; others hold that the duty is only of beneficence. An act that goes beyond duty is called **supererogatory**.

### Balanced analysis

To analyze an argument fairly, state it in standard form, say what each premise means, give the strongest objection to each, and give the best reply. Then say which premise carries the most weight and what would have to be true for the argument to fail.

@example
title: Testing the principle with the pond
problem: Does the pond case support Singer's principle? Consider the objection: "In the pond case I am the only one who can help; in the case of famine, millions of others could help too."
step: In the pond case, only you can save the child. In the famine case, many others can help.
step: Singer's reply: the number of others who could help does not change that each of them could help at a small cost. If the child at the pond were in danger and ten others stood by without acting, you would still have a duty to help.
step: The critic can respond that the cases differ: with ten others, perhaps you should share the burden, and your marginal difference to the outcome is smaller. A defender replies that your gift still makes a real difference to someone.
step: So the objection does not refute the principle but forces a more careful statement of what each person should do when others fail to.
answer: The number of other potential helpers does not remove the duty on Singer's reply, though critics say it may affect how much each must do.

@example
title: Calculating cost per life
problem: Suppose (using a figure invented for this example, not a real estimate) that a charity can save one child's life for every 4,500 dollars donated, and a person has 9,000 dollars of discretionary savings. How many lives could the savings help save, and why might a critic still disagree about whether the person must give it all?
step: Divide: 9,000 / 4,500 = 2 lives.
step: The numbers show what is at stake, and a utilitarian would say the person should give if nothing of comparable importance is lost.
step: A critic could still say that the person has a right to the savings, that giving it all is more than duty requires (supererogatory), or that the estimate is uncertain.
step: The numbers settle a factual question, but not the moral question of how much we owe.
answer: Two lives, with the moral disagreement remaining.

@mistakes
- Saying that Singer argues that we should give until we are as poor as the people we help. His argument is that we should give until we would sacrifice something of comparable moral importance, and he accepts that this is demanding.
- Treating the drowning child analogy as an argument that distance is irrelevant for every moral purpose; it is one of the supports for premise P2, and critics dispute whether the cases are alike.
- Confusing a duty of justice (what others can claim as owed) with charity (good but not owed).
- Assuming that objections from property rights show the argument is invalid. They target premise P2, and the argument remains valid.

hint: Say what the principle seems to require, why that seems excessive, and why one might accept or modify the conclusion.
solution: The objection is that if we must give whenever we can prevent something bad without sacrificing anything of comparable importance, ordinary life with comfort, hobbies and savings would almost always be forbidden, and a moral theory should not require that. A critic can respond that morality should leave room for personal projects and special relationships, and that an account that denies this misdescribes ethics. I think the reply succeeds in showing that the affluent owe much more than is usual, but it does not obviously show that the duty is unlimited, so the strongest position may be a middle one that recognizes a substantial duty with room for personal life.

prompt: Write a balanced analysis of Singer's argument in about eight sentences: state it in standard form, say which premise you think carries the most weight, give the best objection to it, give the best reply, and say what would have to be true for the argument to fail.
hint: Use the standard form from the lesson, and keep the objection and reply fair.
solution: The argument has three premises: suffering from deprivation is bad (P1), we ought to prevent bad things when we can without sacrificing anything of comparable importance (P2), and affluent people can prevent some such suffering by giving without that sacrifice (P3), so we ought to give. Premise P2 carries most weight, since the argument is valid and P1 is hardly disputed. The best objection comes from rights and the demandingness of the principle: people have claims to what they have justly acquired, and a principle that requires nearly everything seems to leave no room for a life of one's own. Singer's reply is that the drowning child shows the principle is accepted in the case at hand, and that the discomfort of a demanding conclusion is not an argument against it. The critic can reply that the pond case differs, since it involves an emergency, a single identifiable victim and no collective responsibility. The argument would fail if the pond case and the global case differed in a morally relevant way, if aid could not reliably prevent suffering, or if property rights set a limit on what can be required. Even then, a weaker duty of beneficence might survive.
