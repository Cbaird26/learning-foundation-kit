The aim is to show that you can reason about a hard question, not that you agree with anyone in particular.

step: Choose a question from the list in the material below, or one of your own from weeks 9 to 12. Write it as a yes-or-no question ("Should ...?", "Do we have a duty to ...?").
step: Describe the practice first. In one paragraph, say what happens and what the people involved believe, without judging it. Check that every claim in this paragraph is a fact, not an evaluation.
step: Pick two theories from weeks 3 to 8 (for example utilitarianism and Kantian ethics, or virtue ethics and care ethics). For each, write down what it asks about the question and what answer it points to. Where the two disagree, say why.

step: Draft the essay in this order: question and description of the practice; case for one answer; case for the other; your conclusion and argument; objections and replies; what the other side gets right. Aim for about 1,500 words.
step: Revise. Check that you have not said "everyone thinks" or "it is natural" in place of an argument, that your terms are used as in the course, and that you attributed positions only to the philosophers you read. Cite the sources you used.

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1: What ethics studies.** Descriptive questions ask what is the case; normative questions ask what should be done; metaethical questions ask about the nature of moral claims. The is-ought gap: factual premises alone do not give a moral conclusion, so find the hidden moral premise.
- **Week 2: Relativism.** Descriptive relativism (cultures differ) is not ethical relativism (right is relative). The cultural differences argument needs a doubtful premise; relativism is not the same as tolerance.
- **Week 3: Utilitarianism.** Right action maximizes total well-being, impartially. Act and rule versions. Objections: justice, demandingness, the experience machine.
- **Week 4: Kant.** Good will; hypothetical and categorical imperatives; the universal law test (contradiction in conception or in will) and the humanity formula; perfect and imperfect duties. Objections: conflicting duties, the description of maxims, rigidity.
- **Week 5: Virtue ethics.** Eudaimonia, virtue as a mean, habituation, practical wisdom. Objections: action guidance, situationism, which virtues.
- **Week 6: Contract and care.** Hobbes's state of nature; Rawls's original position, veil of ignorance, maximin and the difference principle; Scanlon's contractualism. Care ethics: relationships and responsiveness.
- **Week 7: Natural law and religious ethics.** Aquinas's four laws, double effect and its four conditions, the Euthyphro dilemma.
- **Week 8: Metaethics.** Realism, error theory, expressivism, relativism; Mackie's queerness argument, Moore's open question argument.
- **Weeks 9 to 12: Applications.** Animals (Singer, Regan, indirect duties), the environment (anthropocentrism, sentientism, ecocentrism), the duty to give aid (Singer, Nozick), privacy and algorithmic fairness, justice and rights.

### Key distinctions and arguments

Describing versus judging; normative versus metaethical; validity versus soundness; act versus rule utilitarianism; perfect versus imperfect duties; instrumental versus intrinsic value; negative versus positive rights; justice versus charity; sentience versus rationality as the ground of moral status.

### Self-check

- Can you put an argument from any week in standard form and name its hidden premise?
- Can you apply two theories to the same case, and say where they agree and differ?
- Can you state an objection and a reply for each theory?
- Can you state a view fairly enough that someone who holds it would agree with your summary?

### Common errors

Treating belief or popularity as proof of rightness; calling relativism the same as tolerance; saying that a Kantian ignores consequences altogether; saying utilitarianism means majority rule; reading the mean as an average; treating the Euthyphro dilemma as an argument that God does not exist; treating a contested claim as settled; and ending an essay without answering the objections.

@exam mc
prompt: A student argues: "Eating meat is natural, many people do it, and it is legal. So it is morally permissible." Which statement best identifies the problem with the argument as stated?
choice: Its premises are false.
choice*: All its premises are factual or legal, so a moral conclusion needs a hidden moral premise, such as "whatever is natural, common or legal is permissible", which is open to challenge.
choice: It is invalid only because it has three premises.
choice: It proves that eating meat is impermissible.
hint: Compare what kind of claim each premise and the conclusion makes.
solution: The premises describe what is natural, common and legal; the conclusion is a moral claim. A moral premise must be added to reach it, and that premise can be challenged (many natural, common or legal things have been wrong). The argument does not settle whether eating meat is permissible either way.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of these pairings of a thinker or tradition with a view or argument are accurate? Select all that apply.
choice*: Kant: the universal law formula tests whether a maxim can be willed as a law for everyone.
choice*: Rawls: principles of justice are those that would be chosen behind a veil of ignorance.
choice: Mackie: moral statements express attitudes, so they are neither true nor false.
choice*: Singer: the interests of all sentient beings deserve equal consideration.
choice*: Aquinas: natural law is humans' rational participation in the eternal law.
hint: Mackie defended an error theory.
solution: The pairings for Kant, Rawls, Singer and Aquinas are accurate. Mackie defended an error theory: moral statements aim to describe objective facts, but there are none, so they are false. The view that moral statements express attitudes is expressivism.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of 300 to 400 words on this question: "Is it permissible to break a promise to a friend in order to help a stranger in serious need?" Apply two ethical theories from the course, give the strongest case on each side, answer the best objection to the conclusion you reach, and treat the other side fairly.
hint: Pick two theories first (for example a consequentialist and a Kantian one, or virtue ethics and care ethics) and write down what each says before you decide.
solution: Utilitarianism points to weighing the harm of the broken promise (disappointment, lost trust) against the benefit to the stranger. If the need is serious and the cost to the friend small, the stranger's need outweighs it, so the promise may be broken. Kantian ethics asks whether the maxim "I will break a promise when something more urgent comes up" could be universal, and notes that promises are not meant to be broken for convenience; but Kant also recognized an imperfect duty of beneficence, and most Kantians hold that promises do not bind when keeping them would require something seriously wrong. The utilitarian can say that the friend would likely release the promisor, so the harm is small. The Kantian can say that even a good outcome does not remove the wrong of treating the friend's trust as a mere means. My conclusion is that breaking it is permissible when the stranger's need is serious and immediate, the cost to the friend is modest, and the promisor tells the friend and makes amends; this is where the two theories converge, since the Kantian can see an imperfect duty of aid and the exceptions to promising. The best objection is that this weakens promises, since any promisor can claim an urgent reason; my reply is to require that the need be serious and specific, and the friend be told. The other side is right that trust is valuable and that breaking a promise creates a debt to the friend, which is why amends are owed.

rubric: Reaches a reasoned conclusion, treats the other side fairly and separates facts from moral premises.
