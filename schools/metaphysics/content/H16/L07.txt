Many people through history have understood morality in relation to something beyond human opinion: either the order of nature or the will of a god. This lesson looks at two such ideas, **natural law** and **divine command theory**, and at how thoughtful religious and non-religious philosophers have argued about them. Believers of many faiths, and people with none, take part in these debates, and the arguments can be assessed without taking a side on whether God exists.

### Natural law

**Thomas Aquinas** (thirteenth century), in the *Summa Theologiae*, Part I-II, questions 91 to 94, distinguished four kinds of law: **eternal law**, the rational plan by which God governs the universe; **natural law**, humans' participation in the eternal law through reason; **human law**, made by communities; and **divine law**, revealed in scripture. Natural law is the key to his ethics. Its first precept is that good is to be done and pursued, and evil avoided (I-II, q. 94, a. 2). From this he derived more specific precepts by asking what human beings by nature are inclined toward. He listed goods such as preserving life, family and the raising of children, knowing the truth (including about God), and living in society. Actions are wrong when they act against these basic goods.

Two points matter. First, natural law is supposed to be knowable by reason, not only by revelation, so it can be discussed with people of other traditions. Second, on this view, human law that contradicts natural law is not truly law, or lacks the binding force of law: Aquinas said an unjust law is "a perversion of law" (I-II, q. 95, a. 2).

**The doctrine of double effect.** Aquinas's discussion of killing in self-defense (II-II, q. 64, a. 7) is usually cited as the source of the **doctrine of double effect**, which holds that it can be permissible to do an act with a good effect even if you foresee a bad side effect, provided that:

1. the act itself is not wrong;
2. the bad effect is not your means to the good effect;
3. you intend only the good effect, not the bad one;
4. the good is proportionate to the bad.

It is applied in medicine, for example, to a doctor who gives pain relief that may shorten life, and in the ethics of war. Critics doubt that the line between intending and merely foreseeing is as morally significant as the doctrine claims, and note that it can be hard to say what someone intends.

**Objections to natural law.** Critics raise (1) the **is-ought** worry: how does a fact about what is natural yield a moral claim? (2) that "natural" is ambiguous, since many natural things are bad and many good things are artificial; (3) disagreement about what human nature requires. Defenders reply that the theory concerns what *fulfills* human nature, which is a normative notion from the start, not what merely occurs.

### Divine command theory and the Euthyphro dilemma

**Divine command theory** says that what makes an action right is that God commands it. In Plato's dialogue *Euthyphro*, Socrates asks a question, in the context of the gods (plural) and piety, that has become a challenge for any such view:

| P1 | Either God commands an action because it is right, or it is right because God commands it. |
| P2 | If God commands it because it is right, then rightness is independent of God's commands, and divine command theory is false. |
| P3 | If it is right because God commands it, then rightness is arbitrary, since God could have commanded cruelty and it would then be right. |
| C | So divine command theory is false or makes morality arbitrary. |

Replies: some philosophers say God's commands flow from a perfectly good nature, so they are neither arbitrary nor independent of God, since goodness is part of what God is. Others hold a modified version in which right and wrong are constituted by the commands of a loving God. Critics say the reply makes goodness prior to the commands or leaves the question of why God's nature is good.

Many religious ethicists do *not* hold a divine command theory. Natural law, virtue ethics and other approaches, within Christian, Jewish, Islamic, Buddhist and other traditions, treat morality as grounded in reason, nature or character.

@example
title: Testing double effect
problem: A doctor treats a patient in severe pain with a drug dose that will relieve the pain and, as a foreseen side effect, may shorten the patient's life slightly. Check the four conditions of double effect.
step: Condition 1: the act (giving a pain-relieving drug) is not wrong in itself.
step: Condition 2: the bad effect (a shortened life) is not the means by which the pain is relieved; the drug relieves pain directly.
step: Condition 3: the doctor intends the relief of pain, not the shortening of life.
step: Condition 4: relief from severe suffering is a serious good, plausibly proportionate to a small foreseen risk to length of life.
step: So on the doctrine, the act is permissible. Notice that the verdict changes if the doctor *intended* to shorten life as the means of relief, since that would violate condition 2 or 3.
answer: The act satisfies all four conditions, so the doctrine permits it; the same outcome pursued as a means would not be permitted.

@example
title: The Euthyphro dilemma
problem: A believer says, "Cruelty is wrong because God forbids it." Show how the Euthyphro dilemma applies and give one reply.
step: First horn: God forbids cruelty because it is wrong. Then cruelty's wrongness is independent of God's command, and the command does not explain it.
step: Second horn: cruelty is wrong because God forbids it. Then it seems that God could have commanded cruelty, and then cruelty would be right; critics say this makes morality arbitrary.
step: Reply: God's commands express God's perfectly good nature, so God could not command cruelty. The commands are neither arbitrary nor independent of God.
step: Remaining question: is "good" then defined by God's nature, or does the nature conform to a standard of goodness? Critics press this, and defenders have more replies.
answer: The dilemma asks whether God's command or the independent wrongness comes first; one reply grounds the commands in God's good nature.

@mistakes
- Thinking that natural law means "what happens in nature". For Aquinas it concerns what fulfills human nature as rational.
- Saying that double effect allows any bad outcome if it was merely a side effect. All four conditions, including proportionality, must be met.
- Thinking that all religious ethics is divine command theory. Many religious ethicists reject it.
- Treating the Euthyphro dilemma as proving that God does not exist. It is an argument about the relation of morality to commands.

@practice self
prompt: A critic says that natural law moves illegitimately from what is natural to what is right. In four or five sentences, explain the objection and give a reply a natural law theorist could make.
hint: Recall the is-ought gap and what Aquinas means by natural inclinations that fulfill our nature.
solution: The objection is that facts about what humans are naturally inclined to do do not tell us what we ought to do: many natural tendencies, such as aggression, seem bad, and pointing to what is natural looks like the hidden premise that whatever is natural is right. A natural law theorist can reply that the theory does not say that whatever occurs naturally is right, but that certain goods, such as life, knowledge and friendship, are what fulfills a rational human being, and that action which flourishing requires is morally required. The normative notion of fulfillment is built into the account from the beginning, so the account does not slip from a purely factual premise to a moral one. The critic can respond that the claim about what fulfills human nature is itself contestable.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why a moral argument based on natural law might be understood by someone who does not accept revelation, and explain one difficulty for that thought.
hint: Think about what Aquinas says about reason and natural law.
solution: Aquinas held that natural law is knowable by reason because it concerns what fulfills the rational nature that all humans share, so one need not accept a revealed text to see, for example, why preserving life or telling the truth is a human good. A non-believer can therefore evaluate arguments about whether a practice undermines basic human goods, using ordinary reasoning and shared experience, and some secular philosophers defend neo-Aristotelian views of this kind. A difficulty is that people disagree about what human fulfillment consists in, and the natural law account has been used to defend conflicting conclusions, so that appealing to reason about human nature may not settle disputes without further argument.
