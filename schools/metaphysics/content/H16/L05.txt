@article
Imagine a friend who has been mocked at school and asks whether to stand up to the bully. A utilitarian would ask which action has the best consequences; a Kantian would test the maxim. A third tradition asks a different question: *what sort of person do I want to be?* **Virtue ethics** makes character, rather than rules or outcomes, central. Its classic source is Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics*.

### The good life: eudaimonia

Aristotle began by asking what the highest human good is. Nearly everyone, he says, calls it **eudaimonia**, usually translated "happiness", "flourishing" or "living well". It is not a feeling but a way of living: an activity of the soul in accordance with excellence over a complete life. To say what human excellence is, Aristotle appeals to the **function argument**: as the excellence of a knife is cutting well, the excellence of a human being is exercising what is distinctive of humans, namely reason, well. Whether this argument succeeds is disputed, since it is not obvious that humans have one function in the way tools do.

### Virtue and habit

A **virtue** (Greek *aretē*, "excellence") is a settled trait of character that disposes a person to feel and act well. Aristotle distinguishes the **intellectual virtues**, learned by teaching, from the **moral virtues** such as courage, temperance, generosity and justice, acquired by **habituation**: we become just by doing just acts, as we become musicians by playing. Good character is a skill built by practice, which is why upbringing matters.

### The doctrine of the mean

In Book II, Aristotle says each moral virtue is a **mean** between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency. The table below is our own simplified paraphrase of Aristotle's lists, using modern names (he notes that some of his extremes have no ordinary name).

| Sphere | Deficiency | Virtue (the mean) | Excess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fear and confidence | Cowardice | Courage | Rashness |
| Giving and taking money | Stinginess | Generosity | Wastefulness |
| Anger | Spinelessness | Good temper | Irascibility |

The mean is not an arithmetic average. It is relative to the person and the situation: what is courageous for a soldier may differ from what is courageous for a student, and it depends on feeling the right thing, at the right time, toward the right people, for the right reason and in the right way.

### Practical wisdom

How do you find the mean in a given case? Aristotle's answer is **practical wisdom** (*phronēsis*): the intellectual capacity to see what a situation calls for and to act on it. No rulebook can replace it, since situations vary. The virtuous person perceives what matters in the case and responds. This is why virtue ethicists often say that an action is right when it is what a virtuous person would characteristically do in the circumstances. Aristotle also holds that the virtues form a unity, in that a fully virtuous person has practical wisdom.

### A revival

In the twentieth century, philosophers such as Elizabeth Anscombe (in her 1958 essay "Modern Moral Philosophy") and Philippa Foot argued that modern ethics had neglected character and the question of what a flourishing life is, and virtue ethics became again a major alternative to the other theories.

### Objections

1. **Action guidance.** "Do what the virtuous person would do" seems unhelpful to someone who is unsure what to do. Virtue ethicists reply that virtues generate rules ("be honest", "do not be cruel") and that all theories need judgment in applying principles.
2. **Which virtues?** Cultures prize different traits. Defenders say that a flourishing life has some common requirements for beings like us.
3. **Situationism.** Some psychologists and philosophers argue that experiments show behavior depends heavily on circumstances, so stable character traits may be rarer than virtue ethics assumes. Defenders reply that the experiments show that virtue is difficult and that most of us are not fully virtuous, not that it is impossible.
4. **Conflicting virtues.** Honesty and kindness can seem to pull apart. Aristotle's answer is that practical wisdom resolves such conflicts.

@example
title: Courage and the mean
problem: Tariq's team is losing badly, and a teammate is being mocked by others. Tariq could stay silent, speak up calmly, or start a fight. Which of these corresponds to the deficiency, the mean and the excess in the sphere of fear and confidence?
step: Identify the sphere: Tariq must decide how to act where there is some fear of social cost, so the virtue at stake is courage.
step: Staying silent out of fear of being mocked is the deficiency, cowardice.
step: Starting a fight regardless of the danger or the point is the excess, rashness.
step: Speaking up calmly and firmly, in a way suited to the situation, is the mean, courage. Notice that the mean is not the midpoint between silence and fighting but whatever the situation actually calls for, and Aristotle would add that Tariq needs practical wisdom to see it.
answer: Silence is cowardice (deficiency), calm speaking up is courage (the mean), a fight is rashness (excess).

@example
title: Three theories, one question
problem: Rosa has found out that a friend has been telling people a rumor that is false. What question does each of utilitarianism, Kantian ethics and virtue ethics lead Rosa to ask?
step: Utilitarian: which action (say nothing, confront the friend, correct the rumor) will produce the most well-being for everyone affected?
step: Kantian: what is the maxim of each option, and could it be willed as a universal law? Does correcting it respect the people deceived as ends?
step: Virtue ethics: what would an honest, brave and caring person do here, and what will acting this way make me?
step: All three may agree on the action (correct the rumor, kindly) but they give different reasons, and they may differ in harder cases.
answer: The utilitarian asks about outcomes, the Kantian about universalizable maxims and respect for persons, and the virtue ethicist about what a virtuous person would do and become.

@mistakes
- Treating the mean as a mathematical average. It is relative to the person and the situation, and some actions (like cruelty for fun) have no mean.
- Thinking that virtue ethics ignores action. It judges actions by whether they express virtue.
- Equating eudaimonia with a pleasant mood. It is a life lived well over time.
- Thinking virtue ethics gives no guidance. It offers guidance through the virtues and the exercise of practical wisdom, though critics doubt it is enough.

hint: List the virtues in tension (honesty, loyalty, justice) and say how practical wisdom might help.
solution: Honesty, loyalty and justice are in play, along with courage. A virtuous person would not simply protect the friend by lying, since that betrays honesty and justice to those harmed, nor would they betray the friend carelessly. With practical wisdom, they might first urge the friend to own up, give the teacher an honest answer if asked directly, and stay supportive of the friend afterwards, so that honesty and loyalty are both honored as far as possible. A critic could say that "do what the virtuous person would do" gives no clear guidance when virtues conflict, and that different people will disagree about what the virtuous person would do; the virtue ethicist can answer that all theories need judgment in applying principles.
