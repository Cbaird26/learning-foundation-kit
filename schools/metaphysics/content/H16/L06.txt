@article
Why should you follow rules at all? One answer is that rules are what rational people would agree to for mutual benefit. A very different answer says that morality starts not with rules but with relationships: with those who depend on us and those we depend on. This lesson looks at both.

### Contract theories

**Social contract theory** explains the authority of moral or political rules by what people would agree to. **Thomas Hobbes**, in *Leviathan* (1651), imagined a **state of nature** without government. People are roughly equal and mostly self-interested, and resources are scarce, so each has reason to fear the others. Life there would be, in his famous words, "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" (chapter 13, spelling modernized). To escape this, rational people would agree to give up some freedom to a sovereign who enforces peace and agreements. John Locke offered a more optimistic version in which people have natural rights to life, liberty and property that government exists to protect, and Rousseau wrote of a "general will". For Hobbes, morality is a set of rules that self-interested people would agree to for mutual benefit.

**John Rawls** gave the best-known modern version in *A Theory of Justice* (1971). He asked what principles of justice free and equal persons would choose if they did not know their place in society. This hypothetical choice situation is the **original position**, and the ignorance is the **veil of ignorance**: behind it, no one knows their class, talents, sex, race or conception of the good. Because you might turn out to be anyone, you would choose fair rules. Rawls argued that parties would choose two principles: equal basic liberties for all, and social and economic inequalities permitted only if they come with fair equality of opportunity and work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the **difference principle**). He argued that people behind the veil would reason by something like **maximin**: choose the arrangement whose worst-off position is best. Critics, including some who accept the original position, dispute whether the parties would reason this way, and some prefer to maximize the average.

A related theory, **contractualism**, developed by T. M. Scanlon, says that an act is wrong if it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject.

**Objections.** (1) Nobody actually signed a contract. Defenders say the contract is hypothetical and justifies by showing what is fair. (2) Contract theories seem to leave out those who cannot bargain, such as animals, infants and those with severe disabilities, since they cannot take part. (3) Hobbes's picture of human nature may be too bleak.

### Care ethics

**Care ethics** grew out of work by Carol Gilligan, *In a Different Voice* (1982), who described a "care" orientation in moral thinking, focused on relationships and responsiveness, alongside a "justice" orientation centered on rights and rules, and by Nel Noddings, *Caring* (1984). Care ethicists hold that we are not isolated individuals making contracts but people who begin life dependent on others and who live within networks of care. Morality, on this view, is centrally about **attentiveness and responsiveness** to the particular needs of particular people. They criticize ethical theories that demand strict impartiality for undervaluing the moral importance of relationships such as parent and child, friend and friend.

Objections: care ethics may be parochial, favoring those near to us, and say little about our duties to distant strangers or about justice in institutions; it may also place burdens on caregivers. Care ethicists reply that care can be extended into a theory of justice and politics, and that impartial theories hide the labor of care.

@example
title: Behind the veil
problem: Behind the veil of ignorance, you must choose among three societies, knowing only the incomes of the five equal-sized groups in each (in thousands). Which would a maximin chooser pick, and which would one who maximizes the average pick?

| Society | Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 3 | Group 4 | Group 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 10 | 20 | 30 | 40 | 100 |
| Y | 25 | 26 | 27 | 28 | 29 |
| Z | 5 | 30 | 40 | 50 | 60 |

step: Maximin looks at the worst-off group in each society: X has 10, Y has 25, Z has 5.
step: The highest minimum is 25, so a maximin chooser picks Y.
step: Averages: X is (10 + 20 + 30 + 40 + 100) / 5 = 40; Y is (25 + 26 + 27 + 28 + 29) / 5 = 27; Z is (5 + 30 + 40 + 50 + 60) / 5 = 37.
step: The highest average is 40, so an average maximizer picks X.
step: The theories give different answers because they weigh the worst-off differently; the debate is over which attitude to risk is rational behind the veil.
answer: Maximin picks Y; maximizing the average picks X.

@example
title: A dilemma between friends
problem: Nia knows that her close friend Omar has been secretly copying homework from a classmate who is not his friend. If the teacher finds out, Omar will be punished. Should Nia report him, speak to him privately, or say nothing? Compare how three theories approach it: social contract, care ethics and virtue ethics.
step: Contract: ask what principles people could not reasonably reject. A rule that allows copying would unfairly burden the classmate and undermine the grading system everyone relies on; a rule requiring reporting every lapse might also be rejected as too harsh. This points to a fair rule such as "do not copy" and some proportionate response, such as speaking privately first.
step: Care ethics: Nia's relationship with Omar matters, and so does the classmate, who has been wronged. Care is attentive to what each needs: Omar needs honesty and support to stop; the classmate needs fairness. This suggests talking to Omar first, listening to why he did it, and, if he does not stop, finding a way to protect the classmate.
step: Virtue ethics: honesty, justice, loyalty and courage are all in play. A virtuous Nia would not simply protect Omar or turn him in without thought, but would use practical wisdom to speak to him honestly and, if necessary, take the matter further.
step: Comparison: all three may recommend speaking to Omar first, but they arrive there for different reasons: fair principles, responsive care for those involved, and the traits of a good person. They can differ in what they say if Omar refuses.
answer: Contract theory stresses fair principles no one could reasonably reject; care ethics stresses responsiveness to the people affected; virtue ethics stresses what a person of good character would do. All three can favor speaking to Omar first.

@mistakes
- Thinking that the social contract was an actual historical agreement. The contract is a thought experiment that is used to justify rules.
- Saying the veil of ignorance means that people do not know any facts. The parties know general facts about society and psychology but not their own position.
- Saying that care ethics has no place for justice or that it is only about women. Gilligan described an orientation present in people generally, and care ethicists argue about how care and justice relate.
- Confusing Hobbes's state of nature (fearful, self-interested people) with Locke's (where natural rights already exist).

prompt: Explain in four or five sentences how the veil of ignorance is meant to support fair principles, and state one objection to Rawls's argument.
hint: Say what parties know and don't know, and what they would then want from the rules.
solution: Rawls asks us to imagine choosing rules for society without knowing our own talents, wealth, race, sex or beliefs. Because any of us could turn out to be in the worst position, no one can tailor the rules to favor a group they belong to, so whatever the parties agree on is fair by the procedure. Rawls argued that they would secure equal basic liberties and permit inequalities only if they benefit the worst-off. An objection is that the parties might not reason the way Rawls assumes: if they were willing to gamble, they might maximize the average instead of protecting the worst-off, and so the argument for the difference principle depends on a contestable attitude to risk.

prompt: Two close friends, Kai and Lee, applied for the same place on a team. Kai discovers that Lee has exaggerated an achievement on the application. Compare how two of the theories from this unit (choose from utilitarianism, Kantian ethics, virtue ethics, contract theory and care ethics) would guide Kai, and say which you think gives the better guidance and why.
hint: State what each theory asks Kai to consider, and whether they agree.
solution: Kantian ethics asks Kai to consider the maxim behind each option and whether Lee's exaggeration could be universalized. It could not: if applicants routinely inflated their achievements, applications would not be believed. Kai also owes respect to the selectors and to other applicants as persons not to be deceived. Care ethics asks what the people involved, including Lee, need: Lee needs honesty from a friend and a chance to correct the error, while other applicants need fairness. It suggests speaking to Lee first and asking him to fix the application, and only then reporting if he refuses. The two theories could agree on talking to Lee first, but they differ on why and on what to do if he refuses: the Kantian emphasizes a duty that holds regardless of the friendship, while the care ethicist weighs the relationship more heavily. I think the care approach gives better guidance on what to say, while the Kantian approach clarifies why Kai cannot simply stay silent; a good answer needs both.
