@article
Most people feel that causing a dog needless pain is wrong. Many also eat meat, wear leather or support laboratory research that uses animals. Is that consistent? To answer, we need to ask who or what counts morally. **Moral status** is the property of having interests or value that matter morally for their own sake, so that we can wrong the being and owe it something. The disagreement over which beings have moral status is at the center of animal and environmental ethics.

### The case for taking animals seriously

**Peter Singer**, in *Animal Liberation* (1975), applied utilitarian reasoning. His argument rests on the **principle of equal consideration of interests**: the interests of all beings affected count equally, whatever their species. The relevant feature is **sentience**, the capacity to suffer and to experience pleasure. Bentham made the point long before: the question, he wrote, is not "Can they reason?" nor "Can they talk?" but "Can they suffer?" (*Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, chapter XVII). Singer argued that giving less weight to interests merely because the sufferer belongs to another species is **speciesism**, a prejudice analogous to racism or sexism. A standard-form version:

| P1 | If a being can suffer, its suffering matters morally, and should be weighed equally with the like suffering of any other being. |
| P2 | Many animals used for food can suffer. |
| P3 | Much of the suffering inflicted on animals in modern farming is not needed to meet any comparable human interest. |
| C | So that suffering is morally wrong. |

**Tom Regan**, in *The Case for Animal Rights* (1983), took a rights-based route. He argued that many animals are **subjects-of-a-life**, with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare that matters to them, which gives them **inherent value** and rights not to be treated merely as means to human ends. On this view, the problem is not only the amount of suffering but the use itself.

### Replies

1. **Rationality and moral agency.** Some argue that moral status depends on rationality or on being able to take part in moral relations, so animals have little or none. Critics respond with the **argument from marginal cases**: human infants and people with severe cognitive disabilities lack these capacities, yet they clearly have moral status. Defenders reply that humans belong to a kind whose normal members are rational, or that relationships matter.
2. **Indirect duties.** Kant held that we have no direct duties to animals, but that cruelty to them is wrong because it damages our character and makes us more likely to be cruel to humans. Critics say this misses what is wrong: the animal's own suffering.
3. **Contractualism.** Because animals cannot reason about agreements, some contract theorists hold that duties toward them are indirect; others extend contractualism to include them.
4. **Practical objections.** Defenders of current practices appeal to human nutrition, culture, livelihoods and the benefits of research. Critics reply that many of these interests can be met by other means, while others hold that research and diet involve real tradeoffs.

Thoughtful people end up in different places: some reject using animals for food, others accept it with strong welfare rules, and others hold more permissive views.

### The environment

Environmental ethics asks what we owe to the natural world, including species and ecosystems, and to future generations. A first distinction is between **instrumental value** (valuable as a means) and **intrinsic value** (valuable for its own sake). Three broad views:

| View | What has moral standing | Typical claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Anthropocentrism** | Human beings only | Nature is valuable for what it gives people, such as resources, health and beauty; the environment matters because humans need it. |
| **Sentientism / biocentrism** | Sentient beings, or all living things | Animals (or all organisms) have interests or goods of their own. |
| **Ecocentrism** | Ecosystems, species and the biotic community | Wholes have value; Aldo Leopold's land ethic, in *A Sand County Almanac* (1949), says an action is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community. |

Critics of ecocentrism worry that it could permit sacrificing individuals, human or animal, for the sake of a whole. Critics of anthropocentrism say it cannot explain why the extinction of a species no one will ever see seems a loss. Defenders say an enlightened anthropocentrism can protect nature strongly, since future humans depend on it. Climate change raises the further question of what we owe to **future generations**, who cannot bargain with us but whose lives our choices will shape.

@example
title: Reconstructing Singer's argument
problem: A student says: "Pigs can suffer, and farming makes them suffer for cheaper bacon. So it's wrong to buy cheap bacon." Reconstruct it and say what a critic could challenge.
step: Conclusion: "It is wrong to buy cheap bacon from farms that make pigs suffer."
step: Premises stated: pigs can suffer; the farming causes suffering; the benefit is cheapness.
step: Hidden premise: the suffering of pigs matters morally and outweighs the benefit of cheapness.

| P1 | Pigs can suffer. |
| P2 | The farming causes pigs significant suffering to make bacon cheaper. |
| P3 | Suffering that matters morally should not be inflicted for a small benefit such as lower cost. |
| C | So it is wrong to buy bacon produced this way. |
step: A critic can challenge P3 (is lower cost a small benefit for poor families, and does the suffering of pigs count as much as human interests?), or can say that one person's purchase makes no difference. A reply to the second point is that purchases add up, and that complicity matters.
answer: The argument depends on the premise that animal suffering matters morally and outweighs the benefit; a critic can dispute the weight or the effect of one purchase.

@example
title: Three views of a forest
problem: A company wants to log a rare forest. How would an anthropocentrist, a sentientist and an ecocentrist begin to reason?
step: Anthropocentrist: weigh the benefits to humans (jobs, timber) against the costs to humans (loss of water protection, recreation, future options).
step: Sentientist: ask how the logging affects the animals living there, including how many suffer or lose their homes.
step: Ecocentrist: ask whether the logging damages the integrity and stability of the forest ecosystem and the survival of its species.
step: Notice that all three might oppose the logging, and for different reasons, while they could differ in a case where a human benefit is large.
answer: They ask about human benefits and costs, the effects on sentient animals, and the integrity of the ecosystem, respectively.

@mistakes
- Saying that Singer argues animals have the same rights as humans. His argument concerns equal consideration of interests, which can imply different treatment where interests differ.
- Confusing sentience (the capacity to feel) with intelligence. Singer's criterion is the capacity to suffer.
- Thinking that the Kantian indirect duty view says cruelty to animals is permissible. Kant held it wrong, but for reasons concerning character and duties to humanity.
- Assuming environmental ethics is only about animals. It also considers plants, species, ecosystems and future people.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in a short paragraph the difference between instrumental and intrinsic value, and give an example from environmental ethics of a view that treats nature as only instrumentally valuable and one that treats it as intrinsically valuable. State one objection to the second.
hint: Use anthropocentrism and ecocentrism or biocentrism.
solution: Something has instrumental value when it is valuable as a means to something else, and intrinsic value when it is valuable for its own sake. An anthropocentrist treats nature as valuable only instrumentally: a forest is worth protecting for the water, timber, recreation and climate stability that humans get from it. An ecocentrist, following Leopold, says that the integrity of the ecosystem is valuable in itself. One objection to the second is that it could justify sacrificing individual humans or animals for the sake of the whole, or that it is unclear what the good of an ecosystem is, since an ecosystem has no interests the way a sentient being does.
