@article
A friend finds a wallet with 200 dollars in it and a student ID. Nobody saw. Should the friend keep the money? Notice how many different questions are packed into that one. Does everyone in your town think keeping it is wrong? Would keeping it make the friend happier? Is keeping it actually wrong? And what would it even mean for something to be "actually wrong"? **Ethics** (or **moral philosophy**) is the part of philosophy that studies such questions: how we should live, what we owe to others, and what makes actions and people good or bad.

### Three kinds of question

**Descriptive** questions ask what is the case. "Do most people in this town think keeping the wallet is wrong?" is answered by surveys and observation. Anthropologists and psychologists describe moral beliefs and practices, and this is a respectable and useful kind of inquiry. But a description of what people believe does not tell you whether they are right.

**Normative** questions ask what we *should* do or what is good or bad. "Is it wrong to keep the wallet?" is normative. **Normative ethics** develops general theories that answer such questions: consequentialism, Kantian ethics, virtue ethics and others, which later weeks study. **Applied ethics** uses those theories on particular issues such as animals, wealth or technology.

**Metaethical** questions ask about ethics itself. Can moral claims be true or false? If so, what makes them so? Do moral facts exist independently of what anyone thinks? How can anyone know them? "What does it mean to say that something is wrong?" is metaethical. Week 8 returns to these.

Telling these apart is the first skill of the course. A speaker who says "Everyone does it, so it is fine" has slid from a descriptive claim to a normative one, and that step needs support.

### The gap between "is" and "ought"

Hume observed, in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (Book 3, Part 1, Section 1), that writers often move from claims about what *is* to claims about what *ought* to be without saying how. Philosophers still discuss this **is-ought gap**. Whatever one concludes about whether it can be bridged, the practical lesson is clear: an argument with only factual premises cannot reach a moral conclusion unless a moral premise is hidden somewhere. Common examples are the appeals to what is natural ("It is natural, so it is permissible"), what is legal ("It is legal, so it is right") and what is popular. Each depends on a hidden premise ("whatever is natural is permissible") that is open to counterexamples: disease is natural, and some legal practices have been deeply unjust.

### Moral reasoning

Moral reasoning is giving reasons for and against moral claims. Set it out as an argument with premises and a conclusion, using the same tools as in H14. An example:

| P1 | Promises create a duty to do what was promised. |
| P2 | Jo promised to meet Sam at noon. |
| C | So Jo has a duty to meet Sam at noon. |

The argument is valid, and it is sound if both premises are true. Premise P1 is a general **moral principle**; P2 is a fact. Two things can be doubted: a principle (are there never exceptions, for instance to prevent a disaster?) and its application to a case.

Where do principles get tested? Often against **considered judgments** or **intuitions** about cases: judgments we hold confidently, on reflection, such as that torturing someone for fun is wrong. If a principle implies something that seems clearly wrong, that counts against it. Equally, a firm principle can lead us to revise a judgment about a case. Rawls called the process of adjusting principles and judgments until they fit together **reflective equilibrium**. Moral reasoning also draws on facts, which is why good ethics needs good information, and on **consistency**: like cases should be treated alike unless there is a relevant difference between them.

### Why study it

Disagreement is not a reason to give up. People disagree about the sciences too, yet argument and evidence get somewhere. The aim of this course is not to tell you what to conclude but to show you the strongest case for each side, so that you can reason about hard questions well.

@example
title: Finding the hidden premise
problem: Put this reasoning in standard form and find its hidden premise: "Plenty of people copy answers from friends, so copying answers is not really wrong."
step: Find the conclusion: "Copying answers is not wrong." This is a normative claim.
step: Find the stated premise: "Plenty of people copy answers from friends." This is descriptive, a claim about what people do.
step: A descriptive premise cannot yield a normative conclusion alone, so something is missing. Add the hidden premise that links them: "Whatever many people do is not wrong."
step: Set it out in standard form.

| P1 | Many people copy answers from friends. |
| P2 | Whatever many people do is not wrong. |
| C | So copying answers is not wrong. |
step: The argument is now valid. Its weakness is premise P2: many people have done things that were wrong, so a popular practice can still be criticized. The argument's soundness depends on a claim that is easy to doubt.
answer: The hidden premise is "Whatever many people do is not wrong", and it is the premise to challenge.

@example
title: Sorting questions
problem: Classify each as descriptive, normative or metaethical: (a) "What percentage of students admit to cheating?" (b) "Is cheating on a test wrong?" (c) "Is there any fact of the matter about whether cheating is wrong?"
step: (a) asks for a count of what people report. It can be answered by a survey, so it is **descriptive**.
step: (b) asks whether an action is wrong, and wants an evaluation. It is **normative**.
step: (c) asks whether moral claims are true or false at all, which is a question about the nature of morality. It is **metaethical**.
answer: (a) descriptive; (b) normative; (c) metaethical.

@mistakes
- Treating "most people believe it" as showing that something is right (or wrong). It describes belief and does not settle what is true.
- Thinking that normative ethics only tells people what to do. It studies the reasons for and against moral claims, and does not issue orders.
- Missing hidden moral premises, such as "whatever is natural is acceptable", and so treating a factual argument as if it had proved a moral conclusion.
- Running together normative ethics (what is right?) and metaethics (what is rightness, and can claims about it be true?).

@practice self
prompt: A classmate says: "My grandparents' village has always punished lying by shaming the liar in public. That is just their way, so there is nothing to discuss about whether it is a good way." Explain in three or four sentences the difference between describing this practice and judging it, and say which one your classmate's last remark leaves out.
hint: Say what a description of the practice would report, and what a judgment of it would ask.
solution: Describing the practice means saying what the village does and why they do it: they shame liars in public, and perhaps they think it protects trust. That is a factual report, and it is open to anyone to check. Judging the practice means asking whether it is good or fair: does public shaming treat people with appropriate respect, and does it work better than alternatives? Those are normative questions that need reasons. The classmate's last remark moves from "that is their way" to "there is nothing to discuss", but the fact that a practice exists never settles whether it is good, so the evaluation is still open.
rubric: Correctly says that describing reports what the practice is, does and means, and can be checked by observation.
rubric: Correctly says that judging asks whether the practice is good, fair or right, and needs moral reasons.
rubric: Identifies that the classmate's remark treats the existence of the practice as settling the evaluation, which leaves the evaluative question out.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose a practice you know about from school, sport, family life or the news (not cheating or the wallet case). Write one descriptive question, one normative question and one metaethical question about it. Then set out in standard form a short argument that gives a reason for answering your normative question one way, and name its moral premise.
hint: Start with the practice, then ask first what happens, then what should happen, then what "should" means.
solution: Practice: schools giving prizes only for top scores. Descriptive: Do students who receive prizes work harder, according to surveys? Normative: Is it fair to give prizes only for top scores? Metaethical: Is "fair" a property that exists independently of what people think, or does it depend on our attitudes? Argument. P1: Prizes only for top scores reward natural advantages that students did not earn. P2: It is unfair to reward what people did not earn. C: So it is unfair to give prizes only for top scores. The moral premise is P2; a critic could reply that top scores reflect effort as well as luck, so P1 is doubtful, or that rewarding achievement has other benefits that outweigh unfairness.
