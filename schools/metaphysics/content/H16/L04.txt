@article
You borrow a friend's bike and promise to return it on Friday. On Friday you find that keeping the promise is inconvenient, and no one would ever know if you "forgot". The utilitarian asks what outcome is best. Kant asks something different: what *kind* of act is this, and could I will that everyone act the same way? This is the central idea of **deontology** (from the Greek *deon*, "duty"): some actions are required or forbidden whatever their consequences. Immanuel Kant's *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) is its most influential statement.

### The good will

Kant begins by asking what is good without qualification. Talents like intelligence, and gifts like wealth, can all be used badly. The only thing good in itself is a **good will**: the will to act from duty, that is, because the act is right. An act has moral worth, Kant says, only when it is done *from* duty, not merely in accordance with duty. A shopkeeper who gives correct change only because honesty is good for business acts in accordance with duty but, on Kant's view, not from it.

### Hypothetical and categorical imperatives

An **imperative** is a command of reason. A **hypothetical imperative** is conditional on a desire: "If you want to pass, study." A **categorical imperative** applies to you whatever you want. Kant argued that moral requirements are categorical: "Do not lie" does not depend on whether lying would serve your goals.

### The formula of universal law

Kant gave several formulations of the categorical imperative. The first, the **formula of universal law**, says in effect: act only on a maxim that you can at the same time will to be a universal law. A **maxim** is the principle behind an action: "In situation S, I will do A in order to achieve goal G." The test has steps.

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | State the maxim: what am I doing, and why? |
| 2 | Imagine that everyone adopts this maxim, as a universal law. |
| 3 | Is this contradictory? Either the maxim cannot even be conceived as universal (a **contradiction in conception**), or I cannot rationally will it to be universal (a **contradiction in will**). |
| 4 | If so, the action is forbidden. |

Kant's example is the **false promise**. A man in need of money considers promising to repay, knowing he cannot. If everyone made false promises when convenient, no one would believe promises, and the institution of promising would collapse: the maxim defeats itself. So making a false promise is forbidden.

### The formula of humanity

A second formulation says to treat humanity, whether in yourself or in another, never merely as a means but always at the same time as an end. People are rational beings with dignity, not mere tools. Using someone merely as a means is acting in a way to which they could not consent. Lying to deceive someone for your own gain uses their rational agency as a tool.

### Duties

Kant distinguished **perfect duties** (strict, allowing no exceptions, such as not lying or not killing oneself) from **imperfect duties** (such as helping others, which leave room for choice about when and how).

### Objections

1. **Conflicts of duty.** If duties are absolute, what do you do when two conflict? Kant's own essay "On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy" (1797) argued that it is wrong even to lie to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding. Many find that absurd; some Kantians reply that the maxim should be described more carefully, or that Kant's answer isn't forced by his theory.
2. **Describing the maxim.** The result depends on how the maxim is described; a very specific maxim might pass the test when a general one fails.
3. **Rigidity.** Critics say the theory ignores consequences, even terrible ones.
4. **Empty formalism.** Critics also say the test can forbid trivial things and permit monstrous ones, depending on the maxim.

Kantians respond that the humanity formula supplies substance and that the test is applied with care.

@example
title: Lying about a gift
problem: Lina receives a scarf she dislikes from a friend who is hoping to hear she loves it. Lina is considering saying "I love it!" Apply the formula of universal law and a utilitarian test.
step: Kant, step 1: the maxim is "When it would spare someone's feelings, I will say something false about my opinion."
step: Kant, steps 2 and 3: if everyone adopted it, compliments would no longer be trusted, since you could never know whether praise was sincere. The maxim undermines the practice it relies on, so it fails the test. The humanity formula adds that Lina would be managing her friend's beliefs without her consent, treating her as someone to be handled.
step: Utilitarian: the lie spares the friend's feelings now (a gain), but if it is discovered, trust suffers; repeated lying may lead to more scarves; and the friend might prefer honesty. A utilitarian weighs these and may find that a gentle truth ("I'm so touched you thought of me") does better, or may find the white lie does better in a one-off case.
step: Compare: Kant's verdict does not depend on how the numbers come out, while the utilitarian's does. This is the key difference between the theories.
answer: Kant forbids the lie; the utilitarian's verdict depends on the estimated consequences.

@example
title: Testing a maxim
problem: Test the maxim "When I am tired of helping someone, I will refuse, in order to rest" with the formula of universal law. Does it pass?
step: Imagine everyone adopting it: whenever tired, no one helps anyone. This does not obviously contradict itself, so there is no contradiction in conception.
step: Is there a contradiction in will? Kant argued that a rational being knows it will sometimes need help from others, so it cannot consistently will a world in which no one helps when tired.
step: So, on Kant's reasoning, the maxim of refusing help whenever it suits you is ruled out. Because helping is an *imperfect* duty, though, it leaves room: you can help at other times and in other ways, and resting is not forbidden.
step: This shows how the contradiction in will differs from the contradiction in conception.
answer: The maxim fails by a contradiction in will, but it leaves latitude about how and when to help.

@mistakes
- Thinking that Kant's test asks "what if everyone did it?" in the sense of bad consequences. The question is whether the maxim can be universalized without contradiction.
- Thinking that a Kantian cares nothing about consequences. Consequences matter to what act is done and to the imperfect duty to help; they do not decide whether a lie is permitted.
- Confusing acting in accordance with duty with acting from duty. Only the latter has moral worth on Kant's view.
- Treating the formula of humanity as saying you may never use anyone; it forbids using someone *merely* as a means.

prompt: Choose a case in which someone considers breaking a promise to a friend in order to do something more enjoyable. Apply a Kantian test and a utilitarian test, and say in a short paragraph where they agree or differ and which analysis you find more convincing, with a reason.

solution: Case: Dev promised to help his friend move on Saturday but is invited to a concert. Kantian: maxim "When a more enjoyable offer arises, I will break a promise."
