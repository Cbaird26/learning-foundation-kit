@article
Your phone knows where you have been, a school app records how long you spend on each page, and a computer program may help decide whether a loan is approved. New technology gives new powers to help and to harm, and old moral questions return in new forms: what are we entitled to keep to ourselves, when is a decision fair, and who is responsible when a machine decides?

### Privacy

**Privacy** is the ability to control who has access to information about you and to your personal space. Philosophers and lawyers give several reasons for valuing it. In an 1890 article, "The Right to Privacy", Samuel Warren and Louis Brandeis argued for a legal right to privacy and made famous the phrase "the right to be let alone", which they took from earlier legal writing (it is usually traced to the judge Thomas Cooley). The main reasons for valuing privacy are:

- **Autonomy**: if you are always watched, you may shape your behavior to please observers instead of choosing for yourself.
- **Dignity and intimacy**: there are parts of life, such as health, relationships and thoughts, that people share only with those they choose.
- **Protection from harm**: information can be used to discriminate, manipulate or steal.
- **Power**: those who know much about you have power over you.

**The "nothing to hide" argument.** Some people say: if you have done nothing wrong, you have nothing to fear from surveillance.

| P1 | Privacy matters only to protect wrongdoing that people want to hide. |
| P2 | Innocent people have no wrongdoing to hide. |
| C | So innocent people lose nothing important when they are watched. |

Critics reply that premise P1 is false: people have good reasons to conceal many things that are not wrongdoing, such as medical conditions, finances or private conversations; that information can be misused or misread by those who have it; and that being watched changes behavior. Defenders of surveillance point to real benefits for safety and the prevention of serious crime, and argue that rules and oversight can limit abuses. A reasoned position weighs these: how serious is the threat, how effective is the measure, are there less intrusive means, and who oversees it?

### Fairness in algorithms

Computer programs are increasingly used to make or advise on decisions: hiring, lending, medical triage, and risk assessments. They are trained on past data, which can reflect past bias, and they can be unfair even when no one intends it. Consider a program that flags people as "high risk". A **false positive** is a person flagged who would not have had the outcome. The **false positive rate** is the number of false positives divided by the number of people who would not have had the outcome. If one group has a much higher false positive rate than another, many see that as unfair to that group.

There are several ideas of fairness: equal error rates across groups, equal accuracy of the scores, or treating similar individuals similarly. Researchers have shown that when two groups have different underlying rates of the outcome, a program cannot generally satisfy several of these measures at once. This means that choosing among them is a **value judgment**, not only a technical one. Transparency matters too: people affected by a decision may be owed an explanation.

### Responsibility and AI

When an automated system causes harm, who is responsible: the designers, the owners, the users, or no one? Some philosophers worry about a **responsibility gap**, where no human seems fully responsible; others say responsibility can be traced to the choices of people who design, deploy and oversee the system.

Another question is whether an AI system could ever have **moral status**: whether it could be a being whose interests matter morally. As of now there is no scientific or philosophical consensus on whether any AI system understands, is conscious or has moral status, or on what would settle it. Theories of consciousness give different verdicts, and the question is actively researched. Some argue that if a system could suffer, its suffering would matter whatever it is made of; others argue that only beings with certain biological or experiential features can be conscious. Many think that the right response to the uncertainty is to take the question seriously while we investigate. This is a question for you to think through, not one the lesson can settle.

@example
title: Measuring unfairness
problem: A program flags people as high risk. Among 200 people in Group A who would not have had the outcome, the program flagged 30. Among 100 people in Group B who would not have had the outcome, it flagged 5. Compare the false positive rates.
step: Group A: 30 / 200 = 0.15, or 15%.
step: Group B: 5 / 100 = 0.05, or 5%.
step: Group A's false positive rate is three times Group B's: 0.15 / 0.05 = 3.
step: Many people would call this unfair to Group A, since innocent members of A are flagged more often. But the program's designers might say the scores are equally accurate for both groups, and when the underlying rates differ, one cannot usually achieve both equal error rates and equal accuracy. The moral question is which kind of fairness should take priority.
answer: 15% against 5%, a ratio of three to one; deciding what to do about it is a value judgment.

@example
title: Cameras in the corridor
problem: A school proposes installing cameras in all corridors to reduce bullying. Evaluate it using a consequence-based test and a Kantian test.
step: Consequences: list the benefits (fewer incidents, quicker response) and costs (loss of privacy, stress, cost, data that could be misused or leaked), and estimate which dominates. The answer depends on how much the cameras actually reduce bullying and how well the data is protected.
step: Kantian: ask whether students are treated as persons who deserve respect for their autonomy and whether they are told. A maxim such as "Watch everyone all the time to catch the few" treats all as suspects, so a Kantian might ask whether less intrusive means exist and whether students are consulted.
step: Compare: the two analyses use different questions. The first depends on measured results; the second on respect, and both can support safeguards such as limits on who views the footage and for how long.
answer: A consequence-based analysis weighs benefits and costs; a Kantian analysis asks about respect and consent; both can support limits and oversight.

@mistakes
- Saying that privacy matters only to people who have done something wrong. Privacy protects autonomy, dignity and safety for everyone.
- Assuming that a computer program is automatically objective. It can reflect bias in its data and in the choices of its designers.
- Thinking that the fairness of algorithms is a purely technical matter. Choosing among fairness measures that conflict is a value judgment.
- Stating as settled that AI systems are, or are not, conscious or moral patients. There is no consensus, and the question is open.

@practice self
prompt: A company uses a program to rank job applicants, trained on past hiring decisions. In four or five sentences, explain how the program could be unfair even though no one intended it, and say what the company might do about it.
hint: Think about what the training data reflect.
solution: The program learns patterns from past hiring decisions. If past decisions favored some groups, whether through bias or unequal access to opportunities, the data encode these patterns and the program may reproduce them: for example, by ranking down applicants with features that correlate with a group that was previously rejected more often. No one needs to intend this for it to happen. The company could check the program's results for differences in error rates and selection between groups, remove or adjust inputs that act as proxies, keep humans in the loop and explain decisions to applicants. It should also decide in advance which fairness measure it will prioritize, since the measures can conflict.
rubric: Explains that bias in training data or proxies can be reproduced without intent.
rubric: Proposes at least one concrete response, such as auditing results or human oversight.
rubric: Recognizes that choosing a fairness standard is a value judgment.

solution: Case: location tracking by a parental app for a 14-year-old. For: it can help keep a young person safe, parents are responsible for their children, and some risks are real; a consequence-based view would count prevented harm. Against: it limits the teen's autonomy and development of judgment, may damage trust, can be misused or leak, and a Kantian view says that persons should be treated as ends who are owed some respect for their own decisions. A reasonable position would be that the case for tracking is stronger when the risk is specific and serious and the teen knows about it; the case against is stronger as the teen grows older and where less intrusive means, such as talking and agreed check-ins, would work. Safeguards include telling the teen, limiting use to defined situations, setting an end date, protecting the data and reviewing the arrangement as the teen matures. The main remaining disagreement concerns how much weight to give safety compared with autonomy at different ages.
