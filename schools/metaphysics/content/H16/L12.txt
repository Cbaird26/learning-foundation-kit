@article
A teacher has 60 tickets for a school trip and 80 students want to go. Should they be given out by lottery, by need, by merit, or first come, first served? Each way seems fair in some situations and unfair in others. **Justice** is the part of ethics that concerns what people are owed, and fairness is closely related.

### Kinds of justice

**Distributive justice** concerns how benefits and burdens (money, jobs, tickets, opportunities) are shared. **Retributive justice** concerns the proper response to wrongdoing, in punishment. **Procedural justice** concerns the fairness of the process by which decisions are made, such as hearing both sides. Aristotle's formal principle, in the *Nicomachean Ethics* Book V, is that equals should be treated equally and unequals unequally, in proportion to their relevant differences. The hard question is: which differences are relevant?

### Principles of distribution

| Principle | Idea | Strength | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Equality** | Everyone gets the same share | Respects equal worth | Ignores differing needs and efforts |
| **Need** | Give most to those who need it most | Responds to urgent suffering | Hard to measure, may ignore effort |
| **Desert / merit** | Give according to what people deserve, such as by effort or achievement | Rewards effort | Talent and effort depend partly on luck |
| **Contribution** | Reward what people produce | Gives incentives | Contributions depend on circumstances |
| **Entitlement** | Whatever arises from just acquisition and voluntary exchange is just | Respects freedom and choice | May allow great inequality from unequal starting points |
| **Utility** | Distribute to maximize total well-being | Concerned with results | May sacrifice individuals |

**Rawls** proposed two principles of justice for the basic structure of society: equal basic liberties, then fair equality of opportunity, and inequalities allowed only when they work to the benefit of the least advantaged. **Nozick** criticized pattern-based principles: if people freely exchange what they own, any pattern will be upset, so redistribution to fit a pattern interferes with liberty. Some **luck egalitarians** argue that people should not be worse off because of bad luck for which they are not responsible, but may be responsible for their own choices.

### Retributive justice

Why punish? **Retributivists** say wrongdoers deserve punishment because of what they did; **deterrence** theorists say punishment is justified by preventing future crime; **rehabilitation** theorists focus on reforming offenders. Critics of retributivism ask why suffering is good in itself; critics of deterrence say it might justify punishing an innocent person if that deterred others, which is an objection to the pure consequentialist version.

### Rights

A **right** is a claim that others have duties to respect. A **moral right** exists whatever the law says; a **legal right** is one recognized by law. Rights can be **negative** (rights not to be interfered with, such as freedom of speech) or **positive** (rights to be provided with something, such as education). The *Universal Declaration of Human Rights* (adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1948) lists rights that all people are said to have, simply as human beings. Supporters say rights protect individuals from being sacrificed for the good of others; critics, including Bentham, who called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts" in *Anarchical Fallacies*, doubted that rights exist apart from law, and others ask how rights are grounded. Rights can also **conflict**: a right to free speech may conflict with a right to protection from harassment, and then we must weigh them.

@example
title: Dividing the tickets
problem: Sixty tickets for 80 students. Apply the principles of equality, need and desert to this case and say which seems most appropriate.
step: Equality: a lottery gives every student an equal chance, so each has probability 60 / 80 = 0.75 of getting a ticket.
step: Need: tickets might go first to students who cannot otherwise access such a trip, such as those who have never been on one. This responds to need but requires a fair way to judge need.
step: Desert: tickets might go to those who raised money for the trip. This rewards effort but could disadvantage those who had no time.
step: Which is appropriate depends on the purpose of the trip: for a reward for effort, desert; for an educational opportunity, need or a lottery. A mixed rule, such as reserving some tickets for need and using a lottery for the rest, could combine them.
answer: A lottery gives each student a 0.75 chance; need and desert give different results, and the best choice depends on the purpose.

@example
title: Conflicting rights
problem: A student newspaper wants to print a story naming a student accused of cheating before the school's investigation is complete. Identify the rights in conflict and one way to weigh them.
step: Right of the newspaper: freedom of expression and of the press.
step: Right of the accused student: a fair process, which includes not being treated as guilty before an investigation is finished, and privacy.
step: Weighing: ask how serious the harm is, whether the story is accurate, whether there is a public interest in naming the student, and whether the story can be printed without the name.
step: A possible resolution: report that an investigation is under way without naming the student.
answer: Freedom of the press conflicts with the accused student's right to a fair process and privacy; weighing seriousness of harm and public interest may suggest reporting without the name.

@mistakes
- Treating one principle of distribution as obviously correct. Each has strengths and problems, and different cases seem to call for different principles.
- Confusing equality of outcome with equality of opportunity or equal treatment.
- Assuming that a legal right is the same as a moral right. A law can fail to recognize a moral right, or recognize a right that is not a moral one.
- Thinking that rights can never conflict. They often do, and conflicts have to be weighed.

@practice num
prompt: A coach has 105 identical medals to share among 7 teams in proportion to the number of players. Team sizes are 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14 and 16 players. How many medals does the team of 14 players receive?
answer: 21
unit: medals
check: 105 * 14 / sum([4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16])
hint: Add up all the players first, then find the 14-player team's share.
solution: The total number of players is 4 + 6 + 8 + 10 + 12 + 14 + 16 = 70. Each player corresponds to 105 / 70 = 1.5 medals, so the team of 14 gets 14 x 1.5 = 21 medals.

@practice self
prompt: A school has 10 places on a competitive science program and 25 applicants. Explain in four or five sentences how two different principles of distribution (equality, need, desert or entitlement) would select students, and say what is the main objection to each.
hint: Choose two principles and say what each would look at, such as a lottery or past effort.
solution: A desert principle would choose the students who have worked hardest or achieved the most, for example by test results and projects. The objection is that achievement partly reflects luck and unequal opportunities, such as better schools or tutoring. A need-based principle would give places to students who would benefit most or who have the fewest other chances to pursue science. The objection is that it is hard to measure need fairly and that it may disregard effort and talent, which matter for a competitive program. A school might combine them, for example by setting aside some places for students with less access and filling the rest by merit.
rubric: Applies two principles accurately to the case.
rubric: States a real objection to each.
rubric: Does not simply assume one principle is correct.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a balanced essay of about 250 words on one applied question from this course (for example, whether people in rich countries have a duty to give aid, whether schools should use surveillance, or whether farming animals for food is wrong). State the question, give the strongest case on each side using at least two ethical theories, answer the best objection to the side you favor, and reach a conclusion that treats the other side fairly.
hint: Pick a question and two theories first, then write the case for and against before choosing a conclusion.
solution: Question: Do schools do wrong by using surveillance cameras in corridors? A consequentialist case for cameras: fewer incidents of bullying and quicker responses, so more well-being; the cost to privacy is small for most students. A Kantian case against: constant watching treats all students as suspects and undermines their ability to act autonomously, and students should be told and consulted. An objection to the case against: respect for persons also requires protecting victims, and the cameras can be limited. An objection to the case for: the benefits are uncertain, and data can be misused. Conclusion: cameras might be justified in places with a specific, serious risk, with notice, limited access and an end date; the other side's concern for autonomy is real and weighs against cameras elsewhere, such as in bathrooms or classrooms, so the policy should be targeted and reviewed. This position gives weight to both safety and respect, and I hold it with the recognition that estimates of the cameras' effects could change my view.

rubric: Answers the best objection to the side chosen and reaches a clear conclusion.
