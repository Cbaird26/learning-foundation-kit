@article
Suppose a world contains exactly two simples, $a$ and $b$, and nothing else is in it but what they compose. Ana says there are three things: $a$, $b$ and their sum. Ben says there are two. Both have counted correctly by their own standards, both know all the physical facts, and neither is making an observational mistake. Is one of them wrong about how many things there are, or are they using "there are" differently?

### Quantifier variance

**Quantifier variance**, defended by Eli Hirsch in a series of papers and in his book *Dividing Reality*, says that the existential quantifier ("there is", "something") could have had several meanings, each coherent and each satisfying the usual rules of introduction and elimination, though they differ in what they count as a thing. This descends from Carnap's idea of alternative frameworks and from Putnam's conceptual relativity. Hirsch adds a view about interpretation: a language should be interpreted charitably, so that most of its speakers' sentences come out true. If Ana's "there are three things" is true in her language and Ben's "there are two" is true in his, and no meaning of the quantifier is privileged, their dispute is **merely verbal**: they could settle it by deciding whose words to speak. The argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | If the parties to a dispute use the quantifier with different meanings, each making that party's sentences true, and neither meaning is privileged, then the dispute is merely verbal. |
| P2 | The parties to the dispute over composition use the quantifier with different meanings, each making that party's sentences true. |
| P3 | Neither of these meanings is privileged. |
| C | So the dispute over composition is merely verbal. |

The argument is valid. Notice what it does not say. It does not say that everything is a matter of convention, since Hirsch is a realist about the physical facts, and it does not say that ordinary speakers say anything false; it says that there is no substantive question left over when the meanings are fixed.

### The reply from a privileged meaning

Ted Sider's "Ontological Realism" (2009) and *Writing the Book of the World* (2011) attack premise P3. A quantifier meaning can be more **natural**, more joint-carving, than others, and he holds that one meaning of the existential quantifier is the most natural, the one that "carves reality at its joints". If so, the true ontological question is what exists in the fundamental, joint-carving sense of "exists", and Ana and Ben may differ about that even though each speaks truly in a less natural language. Hirsch replies that naturalness is not a constraint on interpretation: charity to speakers matters, and reference magnetism of this sort has no place in ordinary meaning. A second line of reply attacks premise P2. A **collapse argument** holds that the standard rules of introduction and elimination already fix a single meaning for the quantifier, so there is no variation to appeal to. Defenders of variance reply that those rules are satisfied by many meanings and so do not fix one.

### Easy ontology

**Easy ontology**, developed by Amie Thomasson in *Ordinary Objects* (2007) and *Ontology Made Easy* (2015), draws on Schiffer's "pleonastic" entities and on neo-Fregean work on number. Its central idea is that a term comes with **application conditions**, rules that say when it applies, and that from a truth stated without the term one can validly reach a truth that uses it. Here is a standard easy argument.

| P1 | There are exactly five apples in the bowl. |
| P2 | If there are exactly five apples in the bowl, then the number of apples in the bowl is five. |
| P3 | If the number of apples in the bowl is five, then there is a number. |
| C1 | So the number of apples in the bowl is five. |
| C | So there is a number. |

The argument is valid (C1 follows from P1 and P2 by modus ponens, C from C1 and P3). The easy ontologist says that P2 and P3 are trivial conceptual truths, so the existence question about numbers is answered easily, and that heavy ontological debates misunderstand what the terms mean. Unlike Hirsch, Thomasson does not say that the parties speak different languages: she says that most disputants share the ordinary concepts and the existence questions are, in the ordinary sense, easy to answer. Critics reply that the trivial steps do too much: either P3 is not analytic, because the move from a "the number of" construction to a quantifier over numbers is the very point at issue, or the easy method generates existence claims that the ontologist should refuse.

### Where things stand

Neither side is in a majority position. The anti-deflationary tradition leads to the next weeks: if the quantifier question is not substantive, or is easy, then the substantive questions may be about structure, fundamentality and what depends on what.

@example
title: A verbal dispute reconstructed
problem: In a world with exactly three simples, a universalist counts every non-empty sum of simples as a thing, and a nihilist counts only the simples. Reconstruct the dispute using the argument from quantifier variance, say what each party counts, and say which premise a Siderian denies.
step: Count the things. With three simples the universalist counts $2^3 - 1 = 7$ things (three simples, three pairs and the whole), and the nihilist counts 3.
step: Apply the argument. P2 holds if the universalist's quantifier and the nihilist's quantifier are different meanings that each make that party's sentences ("there are 7 things", "there are 3 things") true. P3 says neither meaning is privileged. Then C: the dispute over how many things there are is merely verbal.
step: A Siderian says that one of the two quantifier meanings is more natural than the other, or that some third meaning is the most natural of all. That denies P3, since a privileged meaning exists, and it opens the possibility that one party is wrong about what there is in the joint-carving sense.
answer: Universalist 7 things, nihilist 3. The Siderian denies P3, the premise that neither meaning is privileged.

@example
title: Testing an easy argument
problem: Consider this easy argument: "There are three chess pieces on the board; so the number of chess pieces on the board is three; so there is a number." State it in standard form, check validity, and say which premise a critic of easy ontology would most plausibly deny.
step: Standard form: P1 There are three chess pieces on the board. P2 If there are three chess pieces on the board, the number of chess pieces on the board is three. P3 If the number of chess pieces on the board is three, there is a number. C1 The number of pieces is three. C There is a number.
step: Validity: C1 follows from P1 and P2 by modus ponens, and C follows from C1 and P3. The argument is valid, so a critic must deny a premise.
step: P1 is an ordinary truth that anyone can check. P2 turns a numerical quantifier into a singular term. P3 moves from a singular term to a quantifier over numbers. A critic, such as one who thinks numerals in "the number of" phrases are not referring terms, would deny P3 (or P2), saying that such expressions may be only a way of speaking about the pieces.
answer: The argument is valid; the critic would most plausibly deny P3, the step from "the number of pieces is three" to "there is a number".

@mistakes
- Saying that quantifier variance makes ontological claims false or merely conventional. It says that the disputants may speak truly, each in a different language, and that the dispute is verbal when no meaning is privileged.
- Treating Sider as denying that the quantifier can take different meanings. He allows many meanings and denies that all are on a par: one is the most natural.
- Running together quantifier variance and easy ontology. The first says the parties speak different languages; the second says that existence questions have trivial answers in a shared language.
- Calling an easy argument invalid. Each step is a plain inference, so the debate concerns whether the conditional premises are trivial or hide the question.

prompt: Which of the following are valid inferences in the easy argument "There are exactly four chairs in the room; so the number of chairs in the room is four; so there is a number"? Select all that apply.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, compare the way the quantifier variance argument and the easy argument each try to deflate the question "are there numbers?", and say what their critics most plausibly deny in each.
hint: Say which of the two claims the disputants speak different languages, and which claims the existence question is trivial.
solution: The quantifier variance argument deflates the question by saying that disputants about whether there are numbers use the quantifier with different meanings, and no meaning is privileged, so the dispute is verbal; the easy argument deflates it by saying that in a shared language the answer follows trivially from truths about, for example, how many apples there are. The first locates the deflation in meaning, the second in the triviality of the inference. A critic of the first, like Sider, denies that no meaning is privileged, or, with a collapse argument, denies that the quantifier has several meanings. A critic of the second denies that the conditional premise from "the number of apples is five" to "there is a number" is trivial, saying that it is where the ontological question lies. The two views are compatible in one respect, since both reject a heavy-duty reading of the existence question, but they differ about what the disputants share: Hirsch's disputants speak different languages, while Thomasson's share the ordinary one.
