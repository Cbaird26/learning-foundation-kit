@article
A table is made of wood and the wood of cells, and so on down to whatever physics says is at the bottom. So the table is less fundamental than its parts, one might think. But the cosmos, the whole of everything, has the table as a part. Is the cosmos less fundamental than its parts, or more? The question is not about what exists but about what is **fundamental**: what depends on nothing else, and what is derivative.

### Fundamentality and levels

Using ground, call an entity **absolutely fundamental** if nothing grounds it, and say that one entity is **more fundamental** than another (**relative fundamentality**) when it grounds it or lies earlier on a chain of grounding. The picture of **levels**, from the macroscopic to the microscopic, orders things by the direction of grounding. Two assumptions about chains of priority matter. **Foundationalism** (well-foundedness) says that every chain of priority ends in something basic. **Infinitism** says that a chain may descend without end, with nothing at the bottom. Jonathan Schaffer ("Is there a fundamental level?", 2003) argued that the existence of a fundamental level is an empirical assumption, not a conceptual necessity, and Ross Cameron ("Turtles all the way down", 2008) defended the coherence of endless descent.

### Three views

**Existence monism** says that exactly one thing exists. **Priority monism**, defended by Schaffer in "Monism: The Priority of the Whole" (2010), says that many things exist, but exactly one is basic: the cosmos, the maximal whole. Its parts, such as particles and tables, exist and are derivative from it, as an arch's halves are derivative from the arch. **Priority pluralism** says that many things are basic, typically the smallest parts, and that wholes are derivative from them. The disagreement is about the direction of dependence between whole and part, not about what exists. Counting shows this: in a world with three simples, mereological sums are $2^3 - 1 = 7$ things; the pluralist takes 3 of them as basic and 4 as derivative, and the monist takes only the whole as basic and 6 as derivative.

### The argument from gunk

An object is **gunky** if every part of it has proper parts, so that there are no smallest parts; a world is gunky if everything in it is. Whether the actual world is gunky is open: there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity, and whether spacetime and its parts are fundamental or emergent is unresolved. Schaffer argues that the pluralist is hostage to this, and the monist is not. Here is the argument in a form that is valid.

| P1 | Either every whole is posterior to its proper parts (pluralism), or the cosmos is prior to its proper parts (monism). |
| P2 | If every whole is posterior to its proper parts and the world is gunky, then some chain of priority descends without end. |
| P3 | No chain of priority descends without end (foundationalism). |
| C | So if the world is gunky, the cosmos is prior to its proper parts. |

Validity: suppose the world is gunky. If pluralism held, P2 would give an endless chain, contradicting P3; so pluralism fails and, by P1, monism holds. The conclusion is conditional. Schaffer's wider case adds that monism is therefore safe whatever physics finds, whereas pluralism must bet on atoms, and he offers other arguments, for instance from the entanglement of quantum systems and from the structure of spacetime, which depend on empirical claims and on interpretations of quantum mechanics that remain in dispute.

### Replies

A critic can deny P1: there are intermediate views on which neither the cosmos nor the smallest parts are basic, such as the priority of middle-sized things. A critic can deny P3: infinitists such as Cameron hold that endless descent is not vicious. A critic can accept both and deny that the world is gunky, or say that a pluralist may take points of spacetime, if there are any, as basic. A final reply is that priority monism makes every thing derivative from one whole, so the ordinary parts depend on the cosmos in a way that needs separate argument; the argument from gunk shows only that the pluralist's candidate fails in a gunky world.

@example
title: Counting basic and derivative entities
problem: Suppose a world has exactly five simples, and every non-empty sum of simples exists. How many entities are basic and how many derivative according to (a) priority pluralism, with the simples basic, and (b) priority monism?
step: The entities are the non-empty sums: $2^5 - 1 = 31$ in all, from the five simples up to the one sum of all five (the cosmos).
step: (a) The pluralist takes the five simples as basic, so $31 - 5 = 26$ are derivative.
step: (b) The monist takes only the cosmos as basic, so $31 - 1 = 30$ are derivative, including the simples.
answer: (a) 5 basic and 26 derivative; (b) 1 basic and 30 derivative.

@example
title: Where does a reply attack the gunk argument?
problem: For each reply, name the premise of the gunk argument it attacks. (i) "Some chain of priority could descend forever with nothing wrong." (ii) "There could be basic entities in the middle, neither the smallest parts nor the cosmos." (iii) "The world may not be gunky."
step: Recall the premises: P1 (pluralism or monism), P2 (pluralism with gunk gives an endless chain), P3 (no endless chain).
step: (i) denies P3, since it allows endless descent.
step: (ii) denies P1, since it describes a view that is neither pluralism nor monism as defined.
step: (iii) does not deny a premise. The conclusion is conditional on gunk, so (iii) accepts the argument and denies its antecedent, which limits what it shows.
answer: (i) P3; (ii) P1; (iii) no premise: it denies the condition.

@mistakes
- Confusing priority monism with existence monism. The priority monist accepts that tables and electrons exist; she holds that they are derivative.
- Saying that priority monism is the view that the whole is bigger than the parts. It is a claim about the direction of dependence, not about size.
- Treating the gunk argument as showing that the world is a single basic whole. Its conclusion is conditional on the world being gunky.
- Treating foundationalism as a truth of logic. It is a substantive assumption, and infinitists reject it.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, explain why the gunk argument gives priority monism only conditionally, and say what extra premise or consideration the monist needs to turn it into an argument that monism is true.
hint: Look at the conclusion of the argument and ask what has been assumed about the world.
solution: The conclusion is "if the world is gunky, the cosmos is prior to its proper parts". The argument tells us nothing about the world unless we know that it is gunky, so it supports monism only given that antecedent. To reach monism the monist needs either a premise that the world is gunky, which physics has not established, or a further argument that monism is the safe option: it is true in a gunky world, and in an atomic world it is at least as good as pluralism, so monism is a better bet whichever way the world is. That second route needs a claim that in an atomic world the cosmos is also basic, which the argument from gunk does not supply. The monist therefore adds other arguments, for example from entanglement or from the unity of space and time. A pluralist can reply that these depend on a contested interpretation of quantum mechanics.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, evaluate the infinitist reply to the gunk argument: is endless descent in the chain of priority an acceptable cost? Say what it would take for the pluralist to defend it.
hint: Ask what a foundationalist thinks is wrong with chains that never end, and what an infinitist says in response.
solution: The infinitist denies P3, the premise that no chain of priority descends without end. A foundationalist says that if each entity depends on something prior, then nothing would ever be fully explained or fully real: dependence would be like a debt that is never paid. The infinitist replies that a chain is not a debt, since each member is fully grounded by the next, and that explanation can be complete at each step; what is wrong with unpaid debts does not carry over, because grounding is not a transfer of something. This reply is plausible if we treat fundamentality as relative and do not require an absolute base. The pluralist who takes it must give up the idea that there is a fundamental level of parts, so the view is no longer the picture of basic smallest things, and she must explain what it means to call the whole structure real. I think the infinitist reply is a respectable position, but it concedes that the gunk argument does not favor monism without a premise that excludes endless chains.
