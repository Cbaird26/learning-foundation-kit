@article
A coin has landed heads on every one of the ten thousand tosses in a long experiment. Is it a **law** that the coin must land heads, or a coincidence? Whatever the answer, it must rest on something beyond the pattern of outcomes, or else on that pattern alone. This is the question that divides Humeans from their rivals, and it is the starting point for this week and next.

### Humean supervenience

David Lewis called **Humean supervenience** the doctrine that the world is, as he describes it in the introduction to *Philosophical Papers II*, a mosaic of local particular facts: it is a thesis about the spatiotemporal distribution of perfectly natural local qualities, and everything else, including laws, chances, causation and the modal facts, supervenes on that distribution. It is a development of Hume's denial of necessary connections between distinct existences. Lewis stated his Humeanism as a thesis he was willing to give up if physics showed that fundamental properties are not local, and said that what mattered most to him was the denial of necessary connections.

The Humean account of laws is the **best-system analysis**, with roots in Mill and Ramsey and developed by Lewis in *Counterfactuals* (1973) and "New Work for a Theory of Universals" (1983). Take every true deductive system about the mosaic. The best balances **simplicity** and **strength** (informativeness), and later versions add **fit** for chancy systems. The laws are the theorems of the best system. Since the mosaic fixes which system is best, it fixes the laws. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 54% of respondents accepted or leaned toward non-Humean laws and 31% Humean; the share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

### The possibility argument

Tooley and others argue that laws are not fixed by the mosaic, using cases of worlds that seem to match in the mosaic and differ in laws, for instance a world in which two kinds of particle never meet, with different laws governing what would happen if they did. The argument can be set out in valid form.

| P1 | If Humean supervenience is true, then any two possible worlds exactly alike in the distribution of local particular fact are exactly alike in their laws. |
| P2 | There are two possible worlds exactly alike in the distribution of local particular fact that differ in their laws. |
| C | So Humean supervenience is false. |

The Humean replies by denying P2. The cases describe two worlds incompletely; what we can imagine does not show what is metaphysically possible; and a world's whole mosaic, not a sub-part of it, fixes its best system, so worlds with the same mosaic have the same laws.

### The circularity argument

Armstrong and Maudlin press a second argument: laws explain their instances, but on Humeanism the instances partly make up the laws.

| P1 | Laws explain their instances. |
| P2 | If laws explain their instances, then their instances do not ground them, not even in part. |
| P3 | If Humeanism is true, then laws are partly grounded in their instances. |
| C | So Humeanism is false. |

The argument is valid. Loewer's reply ("Two accounts of laws and time", 2012) denies P2: scientific explanation, in which a law explains why an event occurred, and metaphysical explanation, in which the mosaic grounds the laws, are different relations, so no circle arises. Lange ("Grounding, scientific explanation, and Humean laws", 2013) replied that the distinction leaves the Humean without an adequate account of how the two kinds of explanation are related.

### The rivals

Armstrong (1983), Dretske (1977) and Tooley (1977) take laws to be relations of **necessitation** among universals. Lewis's complaint was that calling the relation "necessitation" does not give it the force to necessitate, and van Fraassen put the inference problem: how does the law relation get us from a fact about universals to a regularity among particulars? Maudlin (*The Metaphysics Within Physics*, 2007) takes laws to be **primitive** and to govern how the world evolves. **Dispositional essentialists** (next week) derive laws from the powers of properties.

### Physics

Violations of Bell inequalities show that no local hidden-variable theory reproduces the quantum predictions, and the experiments have been confirmed with the main loopholes closed together (2015). Some philosophers, among them Maudlin, argue that entangled states are a challenge to a local base: the state of an entangled pair does not seem to supervene on the intrinsic properties of its parts. Whether this refutes Humeanism depends on the interpretation of quantum mechanics, on which there is no consensus, and Humeans have proposed bases that include non-local quantities.

@example
title: Counting mosaics
problem: A toy class of worlds has exactly 3 spacetime points, and each point has exactly one of two qualities, A or B. (a) How many distinct mosaics are there? (b) If Humean supervenience holds for this class, what is the largest number of distinct sets of laws the class can contain? (c) A non-Humean claims that three worlds in this class share one mosaic but differ in laws. Does that violate Humean supervenience?
step: (a) Each of the 3 points has 2 options, so there are $2^3 = 8$ mosaics.
step: (b) If the mosaic fixes the laws, worlds with the same mosaic have the same laws, so there is at most one set of laws per mosaic: at most 8.
step: (c) Yes. Humean supervenience says worlds with the same mosaic are alike in their laws, and the claim is that three such worlds differ. That is the sort of case P2 of the possibility argument asserts.
answer: (a) 8; (b) at most 8; (c) yes, it contradicts Humean supervenience.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: In the circularity argument, say which premise each reply attacks. (i) "Scientific explanation of an event by a law and metaphysical grounding of a law by the mosaic are different kinds of explanation, so there is no circle." (ii) "On my view laws are not grounded in their instances at all; they are primitive." (iii) "Laws do not explain their instances; they only summarize them."
step: Recall P1 (laws explain instances), P2 (if so, instances do not ground them), P3 (if Humeanism, laws are partly grounded in instances).
step: (i) accepts that laws explain and that the mosaic grounds them, and denies that the two cannot go together, so it attacks P2.
step: (ii) is a view on which P3's antecedent is false. It does not deny a premise of the argument as a response from the Humean, and it states the rival view that the argument supports.
step: (iii) denies that laws explain their instances, so it attacks P1.
answer: (i) P2; (ii) none: it is the rival view itself; (iii) P1.

@mistakes
- Saying that the Humean denies that there are laws. The Humean holds that there are laws and that they are the theorems of the best system.
- Treating Humean supervenience as the claim that the world is only particles. It is a claim about the distribution of local qualities and what supervenes on it.
- Reading Bell-inequality violations as refuting Humeanism. They bear on whether the base is local, and the verdict depends on how quantum mechanics is interpreted.
- Treating the best system as the most complete system. It balances simplicity, strength and, for probabilistic systems, fit.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, say whether the existence of entangled quantum systems is a good argument against Humean supervenience. State what the argument assumes and what a Humean can reply.
hint: Say what the local base of Humean supervenience is and what an entangled state seems to need.
solution: The argument says that the quantum state of an entangled pair is not fixed by the intrinsic local properties of its two parts, since measurements on the pair are correlated in ways that local hidden variables cannot reproduce, as Bell-inequality experiments confirm. If the state is a fundamental feature of the world, then it is not a local matter of particular fact, and the local mosaic is insufficient. The argument assumes that the state is part of fundamental reality and that it should not be treated as a mere summary of local facts. A Humean can reply that the argument depends on an interpretation of quantum mechanics, which is disputed. She can also enlarge the base to include non-local quantities such as relations among points, a move that keeps the Humean denial of necessary connections even if it drops locality. Lewis said that his doctrine was one that physics could refute, so a Humean can take entanglement to call for a revision of the base and not of the Humean outlook.
