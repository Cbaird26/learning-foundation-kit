@article
Last week grounding looked like a well-behaved notion with a clear logic. Not everyone accepts that it is needed. A skeptic about grounding grants that there are dependence relations in the world: parts compose wholes, sets have members, physical states realize mental ones. What the skeptic doubts is that there is a single, general relation of grounding over and above these, or that we need a theory of one.

### Wilson's skeptical argument

Jessica Wilson's "No Work for a Theory of Grounding" (*Inquiry*, 2014) argues that the jobs grounding is supposed to do, such as capturing the way the less fundamental depends on the more fundamental and marking out the structure of reality, can be done by more specific dependence relations, which she calls **small-g relations**: composition, set formation, realization, and the determinate/determinable relation, among others. She does not deny that these relations exist. She argues that a general theory of Grounding with a capital G is idle. The argument can be set out in valid form as follows.

| P1 | A theory of grounding is needed only if there is some philosophical job that cannot be done by the specific dependence relations. |
| P2 | There is no philosophical job that cannot be done by the specific dependence relations. |
| C | So a theory of grounding is not needed. |

The argument is valid, by modus tollens on P1. Everything turns on P2, which is a claim about every job that has been proposed, and replies divide by which job they point to.

### Replies

**Logically complex facts.** The introduction rules of last week assign grounds to conjunctions, disjunctions, negations and existential generalizations. No small-g relation of composition, set formation or realization seems to govern these. If a job is to say why a disjunctive fact obtains in virtue of its true disjunct, a general relation seems to be needed, so P2 is false. A Wilsonian reply is that this is a matter of logic and truth conditions and does not need a metaphysical relation.

**Unification.** Karen Bennett's *Making Things Up* (2017) proposes **building** as a general category: composition, constitution, set formation, grounding of facts and realization are all ways in which some things build another. Defenders of grounding add that the specific relations share features, such as being directed, that call for a general explanation. This attacks P2 by saying that unification is a job the specific relations cannot do. A skeptic can reply that a family resemblance does not need a single relation.

**Explanation.** Some, Fine among them, express ground with a sentential operator ("because") and treat it as a notion of metaphysical explanation, without committing to a relation in the world between facts. On this view the question whether grounding is a relation of a special kind may be set aside, and Wilson's complaint that grounding is not needed misses a practice that cannot be done without explanation.

### Alternatives to taking ground as primitive

Even those who accept grounding disagree about its relation to other notions. **Essence**: many hold that facts about the essences of things partly explain why grounding facts hold, so that ground is understood in terms of what it is to be a thing of a given kind (see next week). **Metaphysical laws**: Schaffer ("Laws for metaphysical explanation", 2017) proposes laws that connect grounds to what they ground, on the model of laws of nature, and Sider in later work seeks to explain, rather than assume, the facts about ground. **Truthmaking** relates a truth to some entity in virtue of whose existence it is true, and is often taken to be a special case of or an input to grounding. **Fundamentality-first approaches** take structure or naturalness as primitive and ground as derived. None of these is orthodoxy.

### Formal properties again

@example
title: Which job can small-g relations do?
problem: For each of the following, say whether a specific, small-g relation could do the explanatory job, or whether a more general notion of ground is the natural candidate. (a) "The committee has five members" because five individuals compose it. (b) "The ball is scarlet, so it is red." (c) "Either the cheese is mouldy or the bread is stale" holds because the bread is stale.
step: (a) Composition is a specific relation: the members compose the committee. This is a job for a small-g relation.
step: (b) Scarlet is a determinate of the determinable red, and the determinate/determinable relation relates them. This too can be done with a small-g relation.
step: (c) A disjunction is explained by its true disjunct. No relation of composition, set formation or realization is the obvious candidate, so this is the type of case a defender of general ground cites against P2. A Wilsonian replies that the explanation is given by the truth conditions of "or" and does not need a metaphysical relation.
answer: (a) and (b) can be handled by small-g relations; (c) is the case the defender of grounding uses against P2, and the skeptic replies that logic, not a relation, does the work.

@example
title: Assigning a reply to a premise
problem: Match each of these to the premise of Wilson's argument it attacks: (i) "There are logically complex facts that no specific relation explains." (ii) "A general theory is needed because the specific relations have common structure." (iii) "Even if every job were done by specific relations, a theory of grounding might still be needed for unity."
step: Recall P1: grounding is needed only if some job cannot be done by specific relations; P2: no job is beyond the specific relations.
step: (i) and (ii) each cite a job that the specific relations cannot do, so they deny P2.
step: (iii) accepts that every job might be done by specific relations and denies that this would show a theory is not needed, which is a denial of P1, since P1 says need requires an undone job.
answer: (i) and (ii) attack P2; (iii) attacks P1.

@mistakes
- Saying that Wilson denies that dependence relations exist. She accepts specific dependence relations and doubts that there is a general relation of grounding that does work they do not.
- Treating the argument as invalid. It is valid; replies deny P1 or P2.
- Treating essence, metaphysical laws and truthmaking as rival theories that cannot be combined. Many writers combine them in different ways.
- Assuming that the skeptic's challenge removes the formal properties. It asks what they are properties of and whether they are needed.

prompt: Prepare a five-minute presentation outline on the formal properties of grounding. List four properties, give an example of each, and mark which one is most contested and why.
hint: Choose properties such as irreflexivity, transitivity, factivity and hyperintensionality, and use a conjunction, disjunction or existential for the examples.
solution: Outline. (1) Irreflexivity: nothing grounds itself; the fact that the vase is fragile does not ground itself, and a view on which it did would trivialize explanation. (2) Transitivity: if the vase being fragile grounds its being fragile-or-heavy and that grounds its being fragile-or-heavy-or-red, then the first grounds the third. (3) Factivity: the true disjunct grounds "fragile or heavy"; a false disjunct grounds nothing. (4) Hyperintensionality: two necessary truths, that 3 is odd and that 3 is odd and 4 is even, are necessarily equivalent, but the conjunction is grounded in its conjuncts and not conversely, so grounding is not necessitation. The most contested is transitivity, since Schaffer offers putative counterexamples and defenders reply that the cases equivocate on what the facts are. I would add one slide on the skeptic's challenge, Wilson's argument, to show where the properties are needed.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, say whether the existence of logically complex facts is a good reply to Wilson's argument. Name the premise attacked, and give the best reply of a skeptic.
hint: Decide whether a disjunction's ground is a job for a metaphysical relation or for logic.
solution: The reply attacks P2, the claim that no job is beyond the specific relations. A disjunction is true because a disjunct is true, and no composition, set formation or realization relation seems to supply this. If grounding is what explains it, there is a job for a general relation. The skeptic's best reply is that this explanation is given by the truth conditions of "or", a semantic matter, so no worldly relation is required; the fact "p or q" does not depend on a further entity. The defender can answer that the same pattern recurs in existential facts about the world, where truth conditions do not exhaust the explanation, and that the introduction rules have the same formal profile as those for composition, so they call for a common treatment. I find the reply good but not decisive: it puts the burden on the skeptic to show why logical cases differ from the metaphysical ones, but it leaves open that the job may be done by a theory of logical consequence and not by a general grounding relation.
