@article
A glass is fragile. What makes that true? The first idea is a conditional: it is fragile because, if it were struck, it would break. But a glass packed in foam would not break when struck and is still fragile. This small difficulty opens a large question: are dispositional properties, or **powers**, basic, with other properties dependent on them, or are they grounded in categorical properties that have no tendency of their own?

### The conditional analysis and its counterexamples

The **simple conditional analysis** says that $x$ is disposed to $M$ when $C$ just in case, if $x$ were in $C$, $x$ would $M$. It faces three kinds of counterexample. A **fink**, a case described by C. B. Martin in "Dispositions and Conditionals" (1994), is one in which stimulus alters the disposition: a live wire connected to a device that cuts the current the instant it is touched would not conduct if touched, yet is live. A **mask**, discussed by Mark Johnston (1992), is one in which something interferes with the manifestation: the fragile glass in foam would not break if struck. A **mimic** is a case in which the conditional is true but the disposition is absent: a sturdy glass rigged so that a hidden device shatters it whenever it is struck would break if struck, but is not fragile. Lewis's "Finkish Dispositions" (1997) proposed a revised analysis to handle finks. These cases show that the link between disposition and conditional is not analytic, not that dispositions are unreal.

### Dispositional essentialism

**Dispositional essentialism** holds that fundamental properties have their causal and nomic roles essentially: what it is to be charge is to have the powers that charge has. Sydney Shoemaker's "Causality and Properties" (1980) proposed that properties are individuated by the causal powers they confer. Brian Ellis and Caroline Lierse (1994), Ellis (*Scientific Essentialism*, 2001), Alexander Bird (*Nature's Metaphysics*, 2007) and Stephen Mumford (*Laws in Nature*, 2004) develop versions. A consequence is that the laws of nature are **metaphysically necessary**: they hold in every world where the properties exist. This contrasts with Humeanism and with Armstrong's view, on which laws are contingent. **Categoricalism** says that fundamental properties are categorical, with no essential dispositions, and that powers are grounded in them together with the laws. **Quidditism** is the thesis that properties have identities that are independent of their nomic roles, so that two properties could swap roles.

### The argument from quidditism and humility

Bird presses an epistemological argument against the view that nomic roles are contingent. A valid version is as follows.

| P1 | If fundamental properties are categorical, then two of them could swap their nomic roles while everything qualitative stays the same. |
| P2 | If two properties could swap their nomic roles with everything qualitative the same, then no possible observation distinguishes the actual world from the swap world. |
| P3 | If no possible observation distinguishes the actual world from the swap world, then we cannot know which property occupies which nomic role. |
| P4 | We do know which property occupies which nomic role. |
| C | So fundamental properties are not categorical. |

The argument is valid: from P1 to P3 a chain of conditionals leads from categoricalism to ignorance, which P4 denies. The categoricalist can deny P4. David Lewis's "Ramseyan Humility" (2009) accepts the consequence: we know which roles exist and that they are occupied, and are ignorant of the identity of the occupants, which he treats as a tolerable cost. A second reply denies P3, saying that structural knowledge suffices for the sort of knowledge we have.

### Objections to powers

Armstrong (1997) objected that if a property is wholly a power, with its manifestations further powers, nothing is ever actual, and the account is a regress of promissory notes. Bird's reply is that the identity of a fundamental property is given by its place in a network of nomic relations, so no categorical base is needed. A different worry is whether metaphysically necessary laws are plausible given that physics can consider alternative laws. Barbara Vetter (*Potentiality*, 2015) develops a view on which possibility itself is grounded in potentialities.

@example
title: Finks, masks and mimics
problem: Classify each case as a fink, a mask or a mimic with respect to the simple conditional analysis. (a) A rubber ball is soft, but when squeezed a hidden thermostat heats it so quickly that it becomes hard before it deforms. (b) A fragile lamp is held in a gel that absorbs every blow. (c) A steel bar is not fragile, but a hidden device is rigged to snap it whenever it is tapped.
step: Ask what happens to the conditional "if it were stimulated, it would manifest" and whether the object has the disposition.
step: (a) The stimulus (squeezing) changes the object so that it loses the disposition and the conditional is false. That is a fink. The ball is, in the ordinary sense, soft; it would not yield when squeezed.
step: (b) The disposition remains but something prevents its manifestation when struck: a mask. The lamp is fragile but would not break if struck.
step: (c) The conditional is true (it would snap if tapped), but the disposition to snap when tapped is not the bar's own: a mimic.
answer: (a) fink; (b) mask; (c) mimic.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: Say which premise of the argument from quidditism and humility each reply attacks. (i) "We cannot know which property occupies which role, and that is a cost we can accept." (ii) "Knowing the structure of the roles is all the knowledge we need." (iii) "Properties could not swap roles with everything qualitative the same."
step: Recall P1 (categoricalism allows a swap), P2 (swap leaves no observable difference), P3 (no observable difference means no knowledge of which property fills which role), P4 (we know which does).
step: (i) accepts the ignorance, so denies P4.
step: (ii) says that the knowledge we have is structural and enough, so denies P3 as a claim about the sort of knowledge in question.
step: (iii) says that no swap is possible, which denies P1, since categoricalism is said to allow swaps. It is the dispositional essentialist's own view.
answer: (i) P4; (ii) P3; (iii) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the failure of the conditional analysis as showing that dispositions do not exist. It shows that they are not simply analysable as conditionals.
- Confusing a fink with a mask. In a fink, the stimulus changes the disposition; in a mask, the disposition stays and its manifestation is blocked.
- Saying that dispositional essentialism makes laws contingent. It makes them metaphysically necessary, given the properties.
- Running together categoricalism and Humeanism. A categoricalist may accept necessitation laws, and a Humean may be a categoricalist about properties.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, evaluate the claim that the argument from quidditism and humility favors dispositional essentialism over categoricalism. Name the premise you attack or defend.
hint: Decide whether we are right to think that we know which property occupies which role.
solution: The argument is valid, so its force depends on P4, that we know which property occupies which role. I think P4 is stronger than the argument needs. What science tells us is what the roles are: how a property behaves, which laws involve it, and how it relates to others. If that is all we know, then the swap case shows only that the roles can be occupied by different things and that we would not notice, which is the humility Lewis accepts. For the dispositional essentialist, what it is to be a property is exhausted by the role, so there is nothing further to be ignorant of. That is a real advantage, but it is gained at the price of making properties nothing over and above their relations, which raises the objection of regress that Armstrong pressed. So the argument shifts the dispute to whether ignorance of quiddities matters, and I conclude that it favors dispositional essentialism only for those who think that knowledge of identity beyond role is required.

rubric: Explains what the dispositional essentialist gains and what it costs.
rubric: Reaches a conclusion with a reason, not a summary.
