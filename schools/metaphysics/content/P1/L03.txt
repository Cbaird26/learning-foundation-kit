@article
The vase is fragile or the vase is heavy. Why is that true? Suppose the vase is fragile and not heavy. The answer is plain: the disjunction is true because the vase is fragile. That "because" is not the causal one. Nothing about the vase's fragility came earlier and brought about the disjunction; the fact that the vase is fragile *makes it the case* that it is fragile-or-heavy. Philosophers call this relation **grounding**, or metaphysical ground, and describe it as a form of non-causal, metaphysical explanation. Grounding is now central to the way metaphysicians put questions: instead of asking only what exists, we ask what is more fundamental than what, and in virtue of what other things are so.

### Three ways to put it

The main presentations are Kit Fine's "The Question of Realism" (2001) and his "Guide to Ground" (2012), Gideon Rosen's "Metaphysical Dependence: Grounding and Reduction" (2010) and Jonathan Schaffer's "On What Grounds What" (2009). Fine takes the best form of the idea to be a **sentential operator**: "A is true because of B", which makes no reference to facts. Rosen treats grounding as a relation between **facts**, and Schaffer as a relation between **entities**, with a view on which more fundamental entities ground less fundamental ones. Fine distinguishes **full ground**, which is enough on its own to explain a truth, from **partial ground**, a contribution to a full ground: that the vase is fragile and that it is heavy are partial grounds of their conjunction, and together a full ground. He also distinguishes **strict ground**, where the grounded fact is distinct from its grounds, from **weak ground**, which allows a fact to ground itself trivially.

### The logic of ground

For the strict notion, Fine and Rosen give introduction rules for logically complex truths.

| Fact | Ground |
|---|---|
| Conjunction $A \wedge B$ | $A$ and $B$ together (full ground) |
| Disjunction $A \vee B$ | $A$ alone, if $A$ is true; $B$ alone, if $B$ is true |
| Existential $\exists x\,Fx$ | An instance $Fa$ |

Grounding is **factive**: only truths ground and are grounded. It is also **hyperintensional**: necessarily equivalent facts can differ in ground, which is why grounding cannot be defined as necessitation or supervenience. The standard picture treats it as a strict partial order. It is **irreflexive** (nothing grounds itself) and **transitive** (if $A$ grounds $B$ and $B$ grounds $C$, then $A$ grounds $C$), and these yield **asymmetry**.

| P1 | Grounding is transitive: for any $A$, $B$ and $C$, if $A$ grounds $B$ and $B$ grounds $C$, then $A$ grounds $C$. |
| P2 | Grounding is irreflexive: nothing grounds itself. |
| C | So grounding is asymmetric: if $A$ grounds $B$, then $B$ does not ground $A$. |

The argument is valid. Suppose $A$ grounds $B$ and $B$ grounds $A$. By P1 (taking $C$ to be $A$), $A$ grounds $A$, which contradicts P2. So the supposition fails.

### Challenges

Each premise has been challenged. Schaffer ("Grounding, transitivity, and contrastivity", 2012) offers putative counterexamples to transitivity, to which defenders reply that the cases involve equivocation on what the grounded fact is or are better described in other terms. Carrie Jenkins ("Is metaphysical dependence irreflexive?", 2011) argued that irreflexivity should not simply be assumed, and Rodriguez-Pereyra ("Grounding is not a strict order", 2015) argued that grounding is not a strict order at all. There are also disputes about **grounding necessitarianism**, the claim that if some facts ground another, then necessarily the grounds suffice for it, and about whether grounding is a worldly relation or a feature of explanation. Treat the strict-partial-order package as orthodoxy and not a theorem.

### How to work with ground

A good seminar habit is to state a grounding claim in the operator form, say whether the claim is full or partial, and check it against the rules. A claim that violates a rule needs a stated reason. When you cite a grounding fact, say which of the formal properties you rely on.

@example
title: Grounds for complex facts
problem: Suppose it is true that Ana is in Lyon, true that Ben is in Oslo, and false that Cleo is in Rome. Using the introduction rules, give a full ground for each of (a) "Ana is in Lyon and Ben is in Oslo", (b) "Cleo is in Rome or Ben is in Oslo", (c) "Someone is in Lyon".
step: (a) Both conjuncts are true, so the full ground is the pair of facts that Ana is in Lyon and that Ben is in Oslo, taken together. Each alone is only a partial ground.
step: (b) Only the second disjunct is true, since Cleo is not in Rome, so the full ground is that Ben is in Oslo. The first disjunct is false, so it grounds nothing.
step: (c) The existential is grounded in an instance. Ana's being in Lyon is such an instance, so it is a full ground of "someone is in Lyon".
answer: (a) Ana is in Lyon and Ben is in Oslo, together; (b) Ben is in Oslo; (c) Ana is in Lyon.

@example
title: From transitivity to asymmetry
problem: A theorist proposes that fact $X$ grounds fact $Y$, and that $Y$ grounds $Z$, and that $Z$ grounds $X$. Show, using the premises of the argument above, that this is inconsistent with the standard view.
step: Apply transitivity to the first two claims: $X$ grounds $Y$ and $Y$ grounds $Z$, so $X$ grounds $Z$.
step: Apply transitivity again with $Z$ grounds $X$: $X$ grounds $Z$ and $Z$ grounds $X$ give that $X$ grounds $X$. That contradicts irreflexivity.
step: So the cycle of three is impossible on the standard view, just as the cycle of two is. Asymmetry is the case of two facts; the general result is that a strict partial order has no cycles.
answer: The proposal entails $X$ grounds $X$, contradicting irreflexivity.

@mistakes
- Treating grounding as causation. Grounds do not precede their groundeds in time, and the relation is explanatory in a non-causal way.
- Saying that the true disjunct and the false disjunct both ground a disjunction. Only truths ground, so a false disjunct grounds nothing.
- Defining grounding as necessitation. Necessary equivalents can differ in ground: any two necessary truths necessitate each other, yet a conjunction of them is grounded in them and not conversely.
- Saying that the argument for asymmetry is invalid. It is valid; what is disputed is whether grounding is transitive and irreflexive.

@practice num
prompt: Six facts form a chain in which the first grounds the second, the second the third, and so on to the sixth. If grounding is transitive, how many grounding facts hold in total, counting each ordered pair (earlier, later) once?
answer: 15
unit: pairs
check: comb(6, 2)
hint: Each pair of distinct facts in the chain stands in the grounding relation in exactly one direction.
solution: Transitivity makes every earlier fact ground every later fact, so each pair of distinct facts gives one grounding fact: $\binom{6}{2} = 15$. Five of them are the direct links, and the remaining ten are derived by transitivity.

@practice self
prompt: Give an example of your own of a three-step grounding chain, writing each step as "A because B" and naming the grounding rule or intuition used. Then say which formal property of grounding guarantees that the first fact grounds the third, and how a critic might challenge it.
hint: Build the chain from a conjunction or an existential, so that you can name the rule at each step.
solution: Chain: (1) "The barn has a red wall" because "the north wall of the barn is red"; (2) "the north wall of the barn is red" because "every panel of the north wall is red" (the wall's color is fixed by its panels'); (3) so "the barn has a red wall" because "every panel of the north wall is red". Step one is an existential generalization, grounded in an instance; step two is the claim that the whole is red in virtue of how its parts are. Transitivity guarantees step three: if the first holds in virtue of the second and the second in virtue of the third, the first holds in virtue of the third. A critic of transitivity, following Schaffer, might say that the explanatory link breaks when the middle fact is chosen at a level that is not relevant to the contrast we care about; a defender replies by re-describing the cases so that the facts in the chain are not equivocal.

@quiz num
prompt: Four facts $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$ stand in these direct grounding relations: $a$ grounds $b$, $a$ grounds $c$, $b$ grounds $d$ and $c$ grounds $d$. If grounding is transitive, how many grounding facts hold in total, counting ordered pairs?
answer: 5
unit: pairs
check: (lambda E: (lambda R1: len(R1 | {(x, w) for (x, y) in R1 for (v, w) in R1 if y == v}))(E | {(x, w) for (x, y) in E for (v, w) in E if y == v}))({('a', 'b'), ('a', 'c'), ('b', 'd'), ('c', 'd')})
hint: Start with the four direct pairs and add any pair obtained by chaining.
solution: The four direct pairs are $(a,b)$, $(a,c)$, $(b,d)$ and $(c,d)$. Chaining gives one more: $a$ grounds $d$ via $b$ (and via $c$, the same pair). So there are 5 in all.

@quiz self
prompt: In 120 to 180 words, explain why grounding is not defined as necessitation, using one pair of necessarily equivalent facts that differ in ground, and say what this shows about the way grounding is individuated.
hint: Pick facts that are necessarily true together, but one explains the other.
solution: Necessitation is not enough because necessarily equivalent facts necessitate each other while grounding is not symmetric, and because it cannot tell apart facts that hold in exactly the same worlds. Take the fact that 3 is odd and the fact that 3 is odd and 4 is even. Both are necessary truths, so they are necessarily equivalent. But the conjunction is grounded in its two conjuncts, one of which is the first fact, and the first fact is not grounded in the conjunction. What this shows is that grounding is hyperintensional: it distinguishes facts that no possible world separates, so it is individuated by what the facts are about and how they are built, not by the sets of worlds where they hold. That is why necessitation, supervenience and strict implication cannot be what grounding is, even if (as grounding necessitarians hold) they are necessary conditions on it, and why grounding is regimented with a sentential operator or relation among finely individuated facts.

rubric: States that grounding is hyperintensional and not definable as necessitation or supervenience.
rubric: Draws the right conclusion about individuating facts, not worlds.
