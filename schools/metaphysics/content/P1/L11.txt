@article
Last week's arguments left a question hanging. If composition is unrestricted, or the cosmos is basic, or laws are necessitation relations, how would we ever find out? There is no laboratory for mereology. Some of the evidence in metaphysics is the data of science and some is what seems right about cases. This lesson looks at how metaphysical claims are supported and at the challenges to each method.

### Three methods

**Intuitions about cases.** Metaphysicians consider a case, such as a statue and the clay it is made of, and ask what we should say. George Bealer (*Intuition and the Autonomy of Philosophy*, 1998) defends a view on which intuitions are a basic source of evidence. Timothy Williamson (*The Philosophy of Philosophy*, 2007) argues that the judgments made in thought experiments are ordinary judgments, using the capacity we use in everyday thought to evaluate counterfactuals, with no special faculty, and that they can be as reliable as other judgments. Herman Cappelen (*Philosophy Without Intuitions*, 2012) argues that philosophers do not in fact rely on intuitions as evidence: they argue for their verdicts on cases.

**Theoretical virtues.** Theories are compared by simplicity, explanatory power, unity and fit with other things we know, as in science. David Lewis was willing to pay a cost in the number of entities to gain a better theory: he distinguished **qualitative parsimony**, which counts the kinds of thing a theory posits, from **quantitative parsimony**, which counts the individual things, and said that the first is the one that matters. Jonathan Schaffer ("What Not to Multiply Without Necessity", 2015) argues for a **laser**: the preference is against positing extra *fundamental* entities, and derivative entities are cheap. Theodore Sider (*Writing the Book of the World*, 2011) argues that metaphysics should be judged by the same virtues as the sciences. L. A. Paul ("Metaphysics as Modeling", 2012) proposes that metaphysical theories are models to be compared by their virtues.

**Naturalism.** On a **naturalist** approach, metaphysics is continuous with science. Quine's view and its descendants say that we should believe in what our best scientific theories require. James Ladyman and Don Ross (*Every Thing Must Go*, 2007) are much more severe: they propose that metaphysics is acceptable only when it is constrained by current science and that most analytic metaphysics, which is not so constrained, should be set aside. Tim Maudlin (*The Metaphysics Within Physics*, 2007) argues that physics is the proper source of metaphysical information.

### The experimental challenge

Experimental philosophers, starting with Weinberg, Nichols and Stich ("Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions", 2001), reported that intuitions about some philosophical cases varied with culture, with the order in which cases were presented, or with other factors that seem irrelevant to the truth. Later work failed to replicate some of the early findings, and the field disputes how much variation there is and what it shows. The argument can be set out in valid form.

| P1 | If intuitions about a type of case vary with factors irrelevant to the truth, then they are unreliable. |
| P2 | Intuitions about the cases of metaphysics vary with factors irrelevant to the truth. |
| P3 | If intuitions about the cases of metaphysics are unreliable, then relying on them as evidence is unjustified. |
| P4 | Metaphysicians rely on intuitions about cases as evidence. |
| C | So metaphysicians' reliance on intuitions about cases as evidence is unjustified. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 make the intuitions unreliable, P3 makes reliance unjustified, and P4 says that reliance occurs. Cappelen denies P4. Others deny P2: the early findings did not always replicate, and the judgments of trained philosophers, after reflection, may vary less than first reactions (the expertise defense). Replies to P1 point out that variation does not by itself show unreliability, since judgments in other fields vary too.

### Assessing the methods

Chalmers ("Why Isn't There More Progress in Philosophy?", 2015) notes that disagreement on the big questions persists, and each method must say how it can make progress. A good seminar paper states what kind of evidence it relies on, and defends it. If it uses an intuition, it says whether the case is one where intuitions are stable. If it appeals to a virtue, it says which kind of parsimony, and why that cost is the relevant one.

@example
title: Three kinds of parsimony
problem: Theory T1 posits 3 kinds of entity, 40 entities in all, of which 2 are fundamental. Theory T2 posits 5 kinds, 20 entities in all, all of which are fundamental. The theories fit the data equally. Which does each of (a) qualitative parsimony, (b) quantitative parsimony and (c) the laser prefer?
step: (a) Qualitative parsimony counts kinds: 3 against 5, so it prefers T1.
step: (b) Quantitative parsimony counts individuals: 40 against 20, so it prefers T2.
step: (c) The laser counts fundamental entities: 2 against 20, so it prefers T1, since derivative entities are cheap.
answer: (a) T1; (b) T2; (c) T1.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: Here is a naturalist argument. P1: a metaphysical theory is legitimate only if it is constrained by current science. P2: much analytic metaphysics is not constrained by current science. C: much analytic metaphysics is not legitimate. Say which premise each reply attacks. (i) "The requirement that metaphysics be constrained by science is not itself a scientific result." (ii) "Many metaphysicians of composition and time draw on physics." (iii) "Even if it is not constrained by science, a theory may be legitimate because it clarifies the concepts science uses."
step: The argument is valid: if legitimacy requires constraint and much of the work lacks it, much of the work is not legitimate. A reply must deny a premise.
step: (i) says that the requirement is self-undermining, so it denies P1.
step: (ii) says that much analytic metaphysics is constrained by science, so it denies P2.
step: (iii) says that legitimacy does not require constraint by science, so it denies P1.
answer: (i) P1; (ii) P2; (iii) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the experimental argument as showing that intuitions are worthless. Its conclusion is about reliance on intuitions that vary with irrelevant factors, and its premises are contested.
- Saying that Cappelen thinks intuitions are unreliable. He denies that philosophers rely on intuitions as evidence, which is a claim about philosophical practice.
- Running together qualitative parsimony, quantitative parsimony and the laser. They count kinds, individuals and fundamental entities respectively.
- Treating naturalism as the claim that only science is knowledge. Ladyman and Ross's view is about what constrains metaphysics, not about all knowledge.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, say which virtues you would use to choose between a nihilist and a universalist about composition, and give the best objection to your choice.
hint: Decide whether you count kinds, individuals or fundamental entities, and what else matters, such as fit with science or explanatory power.
solution: I would use qualitative parsimony and the laser. A universalist posits more individuals, but every sum is derivative from the simples, so she adds no new fundamental entity and no new kind, and the laser counts her theory as no more costly than the nihilist's. The nihilist, in turn, must say what to make of ordinary claims about tables and people, and paraphrase has costs in explanatory power and in fit with common thought and with the sciences, which talk of organisms and cells. So on those virtues I favor the universalist or the moderate. The best objection is that the laser assumes that derivative entities are cheap, and that is the thing in dispute: if sums are something over and above the simples, they are an added commitment, and quantitative parsimony counts against them. I reply that this is a question about what dependence is, and that the issue between the laser and quantitative parsimony shows why a paper must say which cost it takes to be relevant.

@quiz self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, assess the claim that metaphysics should be constrained by current science. Name the premise of the naturalist argument you would attack or defend, and say what the best reply is.
hint: Consider whether the constraint is itself scientific and how much current science tells us about the questions metaphysicians ask.
solution: I attack P1 as stated. A weaker claim is true, that metaphysical theories should be consistent with and informed by current science, but it does not make most analytic metaphysics illegitimate. Science is the best guide we have to what exists and how things depend on each other, so a theory of composition that ignores what physics says about particles and fields is open to the objection that it lacks evidence. The weaker claim is not the same as a requirement that every legitimate theory be constrained by science. Our best physics does not settle whether there are smallest parts, since there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity, or what a quantum state represents, so metaphysics has questions to ask about the structures that science leaves open. The best reply, from the naturalist, is that to the extent that these questions are not answered by science they have no answer we can reach. I think that this reply assumes a verificationist standard that the naturalist should not adopt, and that conceptual work on what dependence and fundamentality are is useful to scientists too.

rubric: Gives a reason that engages with what science does and does not settle.
rubric: States and answers the best reply from the other side.
