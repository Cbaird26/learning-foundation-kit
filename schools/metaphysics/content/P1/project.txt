@review
## Week-by-week review

**Weeks 1 and 2: metaontology.** Quine's criterion says a theory is committed to what must be in the range of its bound variables, after regimentation. Carnap separates internal questions (answered inside a framework) from external questions (empty or practical). Quine replies by attacking the analytic/synthetic basis of the distinction and the idea that "practical" means "not factual". Quantifier variance says the quantifier can mean different things, so some disputes are verbal unless one meaning is privileged (Sider denies this). Easy ontology says existence claims follow trivially from application conditions.

**Weeks 3 and 4: grounding.** Grounding is non-causal, hyperintensional explanation. Rules: a conjunction is grounded in its conjuncts, a disjunction in a true disjunct, an existential in an instance. Orthodoxy: irreflexive, transitive (so asymmetric), factive. Contested: transitivity, necessitarianism. Wilson's skeptical argument denies that a general theory is needed; replies cite logically complex facts and unification (Bennett's building).

**Weeks 5 and 6: fundamentality and essence.** Priority monism says the cosmos is the one basic entity. The gunk argument is conditional on the world being gunky and needs foundationalism. Fine's argument shows that necessity is not sufficient for essence. Essence is hyperintensional, and facts about essence may explain ground.

**Weeks 7 and 8: laws and powers.** Humean supervenience and the best-system analysis face the possibility and circularity arguments. Dispositional essentialism makes laws necessary and faces the regress objection; the quidditism argument assumes that we know which property fills which role.

**Weeks 9 and 10: indeterminacy and time.** Precisifications of the world model metaphysical indeterminacy. Evans's argument needs a determinacy-transfer step. McTaggart's argument and Fine's four-way inconsistency organize the tense debate.

**Weeks 11 and 12: method and writing.** Intuitions, virtues and naturalism, with the experimental and naturalist challenges. A paper makes a premise-level contribution.

## Key distinctions and habits

- Validity is not soundness: an objection attacks a premise of a valid argument.
- Epistemic, semantic and metaphysical indeterminacy differ in what they locate the indeterminacy in.
- Qualitative parsimony counts kinds, quantitative parsimony individuals, and the laser fundamental entities.
- A share of philosophers holding a view is not evidence that it is true.

## Self-check

- Can you state each week's main argument in valid standard form and name the premise each reply denies?
- Can you explain one idea aloud without notes?
- Can you say what the field has and has not settled, including the open questions in physics?

## Common errors

- Reading Quine's criterion as saying what exists, or priority monism as existence monism.
- Treating grounding as causation, or defining it as necessitation.
- Giving an objection without naming a premise.

@exam mc
prompt: Which of these pairs correctly matches a reply to the premise of the argument it attacks?
choice*: An infinitist about priority chains attacks the premise of the gunk argument that no chain of priority descends without end.
choice: Sider's appeal to a joint-carving quantifier attacks the premise of the quantifier-variance argument that the parties use different meanings.
choice: Pointing to logically complex facts attacks the premise of Wilson's argument that a theory of grounding is needed only if some job cannot be done by specific relations.
choice: Cappelen's claim that philosophers do not rely on intuitions attacks the premise that intuitions about metaphysical cases vary with irrelevant factors.
hint: For each argument, recall its premises and ask which one the reply denies.
solution: The infinitist denies foundationalism. Sider denies that no meaning is privileged, not that the meanings differ. Logically complex facts attack the premise that no job is beyond the specific relations, not the conditional about need. Cappelen denies that metaphysicians rely on intuitions as evidence, not that intuitions vary.

@exam num
prompt: A world has exactly 7 simples and every non-empty sum of simples exists. How many more entities does a priority monist count as derivative than a priority pluralist who takes the simples as basic?
answer: 6
unit: entities
check: (2 ** 7 - 2) - (2 ** 7 - 1 - 7)
hint: Each view counts every non-empty sum as an entity and subtracts its basic entities.
solution: There are $2^7 - 1 = 127$ entities. The monist takes one as basic, so 126 are derivative; the pluralist takes 7 as basic, so 120 are derivative. The difference is 6, which is one less than the number of simples.

@exam self
prompt: In 400 to 500 words, answer this timed question. "Does moving from the question what exists to the question what grounds what escape the challenge posed by deflationary views of ontology such as quantifier variance and easy ontology?" Reconstruct one argument in standard form, name the premise you attack or defend, and give the best reply to your position.
hint: Use the argument from quantifier variance, and ask whether the grounding theorist's question can also be called verbal or easy.
solution: I argue that it largely escapes the quantifier-variance challenge but not the skeptical challenge about grounding. The argument from quantifier variance runs: P1, if parties use the quantifier with different meanings, each making their sentences true, and neither meaning is privileged, the dispute is merely verbal; P2, the parties to the composition dispute use different meanings, each making their sentences true; P3, neither is privileged; C, the dispute is verbal. The argument is valid. The grounding theorist's question is not about the meaning of the quantifier: "what is more fundamental than what?" asks about priority among things and facts, not about how many things there are, and Schaffer's turn to ground is motivated by exactly this. So the argument has no direct application, since the parties to a priority dispute, for example the monist and the pluralist, can agree on what exists and differ on what depends on what. A deflationist can reply that "fundamental" or "grounds" might also be given different meanings, which is a version of the variance argument for the new vocabulary. I deny the premise that none of the candidate meanings of "grounds" is privileged: Sider's idea that a joint-carving notion exists applies to fundamentality at least as well as to the quantifier, since the notion of what is basic is the notion of what carves at the joints. So the first defense is that the grounding question may have a privileged reading. The second line of challenge is Wilson's: that no general theory of grounding is needed, since specific dependence relations do all the work. If so, the grounding question, while not verbal, may be idle. My view is that the shift to grounding escapes the verbal-dispute charge, provided that a privileged notion of fundamentality is available, and that it is exposed instead to the claim that the specific relations make the general question otiose, which is why the debate in weeks 3 and 4 matters.
