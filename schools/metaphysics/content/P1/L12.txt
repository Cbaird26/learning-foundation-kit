@article
A good seminar paper is not a survey, and it is not a list of objections. It makes one claim that an informed reader could reasonably deny, defends it against the strongest reply, and shows exactly where it stands in a debate. For the project in this course you will write 5,000 to 7,000 words on one current debate, revise after written feedback, and present the result in 20 minutes. This lesson is the practical guide to doing that. Requirements differ among programs and instructors, so check your own.

### What kind of contribution

Each of these is a recognizable contribution. An **objection** attacks a premise of a published argument. A **reply** defends an argument against an objection. A **refinement** repairs a principle that has counterexamples, as Wildman's sparse restriction repairs the modal account of essence. A **distinction** shows that a debate runs together two questions. A **counterexample** refutes a general claim. A **synthesis** shows that two positions are compatible. Pick one. A paper that tries to do all of them in 6,000 words does none of them well.

### The premise-level principle

The method of this course rests on a simple principle, which you should apply in every paper.

| P1 | The argument you discuss is valid as you have reconstructed it. |
| P2 | If an argument is valid, then any successful resistance to its conclusion denies at least one of its premises. |
| C | So any successful resistance to the argument's conclusion, in your paper, denies at least one premise of your reconstruction. |

The argument is valid, and P2 is a truth of logic. Its use is practical. It tells you to reconstruct the argument first, with the hidden premises added so that P1 holds, and then to say which premise your paper denies or defends. An objection that does not name a premise has not yet been stated. If a reader replies that your reconstruction is not what the author argued, the question becomes whether a charitable reading supplies your premise, so the exposition has to justify it.

### Reading the literature

Start from the encyclopedia entries and the primary reading, then follow their bibliographies to the central papers, and then search PhilPapers for recent work that cites them. Read a paper by finding its thesis, its argument in standard form and its replies to objections before reading the details. Keep a table of the debate: the positions, the premises, who denies which. Your own contribution should be a line in that table. Report the literature accurately: attribute a view to an author only for what the author wrote, and say "a critic might hold" when you are constructing a reply.

### Drafting

The **abstract** (usually 100 to 150 words) states the question, the thesis and the main argument, with no promises: say what you argue, not that you will discuss it. The **introduction** states the problem, locates it in the literature, states the thesis and gives the roadmap. The **exposition** reconstructs the argument you discuss, fairly and in standard form. The **body** gives your argument, then the best objection, then your reply. The **conclusion** says what has been shown and what has not, and where the debate stands. Every section should answer the question "so what?".

### Feedback, integrity and venues

Revise after feedback by answering every comment: accept it and change the text, or explain why not. Keep a short note of changes for your reader. Cite every source of an idea, a quotation or an argument, put quotation marks round exact words, and do not present a paraphrase of someone else's structure of argument as your own. Follow your program's and your instructor's rules on the use of AI tools, and disclose any use that the rules require you to disclose. Journals commonly use double-anonymous review and reply with acceptance, a request for revision, or rejection, and timelines vary; check each venue's guidelines. Seminar papers often become writing samples or dissertation chapters, and a doctoral program's rules, such as the qualifying requirement and the prospectus, differ, so check yours with your advisor.

### Presenting in 20 minutes

Written prose does not fit a talk. At a speaking pace of about 125 words per minute, 20 minutes is about 2,500 words, well under half of a 6,000-word paper. Present the question, one argument, one objection and your reply. Use a handout or a slide with the argument in standard form, so the audience can follow which premise you target. Plan for questions: list the three hardest and your answers.

@example
title: Reading an abstract critically
problem: A draft abstract reads: "This paper discusses grounding and the objections to it. I will consider several views and say something about what is at stake." What is wrong with it, and how would you revise it into a version a reader could use?
step: Check for a thesis. The abstract says only that the paper discusses and considers; it states no claim a reader could deny.
step: Check for an argument. There is no premise or conclusion, so the reader cannot tell what kind of contribution the paper makes.
step: Revise. Choose a claim and a premise: "I argue that the gunk argument for priority monism supports monism only if the world is gunky, and that the monist therefore needs either a further premise excluding atoms or an independent argument; I consider the entanglement argument as a candidate and argue that it depends on an interpretation of quantum mechanics."
answer: The draft states no thesis and no argument; the revision names a claim, the premise attacked and the main reply.

@example
title: Fitting a paper to a talk
problem: A conference gives you 12 minutes. You speak at 130 words per minute, and your paper is 6,240 words. What fraction of the paper can be read aloud, and what does that imply for the talk?
step: Words available: $12 \times 130 = 1{,}560$.
step: Fraction of the paper: $1{,}560 / 6{,}240 = 0.25$, so one quarter.
step: Implication: you cannot read the paper. Choose one argument, state its premises on a handout, and give your objection and one reply.
answer: One quarter (25%); the talk must present one argument and one objection.

@mistakes
- Writing a survey and calling it a paper. A seminar paper needs a thesis that an informed reader could deny.
- Attacking an argument without naming a premise. If the argument as reconstructed is valid, a successful objection must deny a premise.
- Reading a paper aloud as a talk. A talk has room for about a third to a half of the words, and for one argument.
- Paraphrasing a source's argument without citing it. Paraphrase needs a citation as much as a quotation does.

prompt: Which of these count as plagiarism? Select all that apply.
choice*: Copying a sentence from a paper word for word, with a citation but without quotation marks.
choice*: Presenting a paraphrase of another author's argument as your own idea, with no citation.

solution: Exact words need quotation marks as well as a citation, and an uncited paraphrase presents another's idea as yours. A quotation with marks and a citation, and a cited paraphrase, are proper.

@practice mc
prompt: Suppose you have reconstructed an argument so that it is valid. Which statement about your objection to it is correct?
choice*: A successful objection must deny at least one premise of the reconstruction.
choice: An objection can succeed without denying any premise, since the conclusion may still be false.
choice: A successful objection must show that the argument is invalid.
choice: A successful objection must deny the conclusion and need not mention the premises.
hint: A valid argument with true premises has a true conclusion.
solution: If the argument is valid, the conclusion can be false only if some premise is false, so a successful objection denies a premise. Showing invalidity is not available once validity is secured, and denying the conclusion alone does not say where the argument goes wrong.

@practice self
prompt: Write a 120 to 150 word abstract for a seminar paper draft on a debate from this course. State the question, the thesis, the premise you target and the main argument, in the form a reader could use to decide whether to read the paper.
hint: Choose a published argument, name the premise you attack or defend, and say what your reason is.
solution: Wilson argues that a theory of grounding is unnecessary, because the jobs assigned to grounding can be done by specific dependence relations such as composition and realization. I argue that her argument fails at its second premise, the claim that no job lies beyond the specific relations. The explanation of why a disjunctive or existential fact obtains in virtue of a true disjunct or instance is a job that composition, set formation and realization do not do, and the natural candidate for it is a general notion of ground. I consider the reply that truth conditions of the connectives do the work and no worldly relation is needed, and argue that this reply cannot explain why the same pattern governs facts about composition, where truth conditions are not available. I conclude that the skeptic should accept a general notion of ground for logically complex facts.
rubric: States a question and a thesis that an informed reader could deny.
rubric: Names the premise targeted and gives the reason for the attack.
rubric: Mentions the main reply and the paper's answer, in a form that does not promise but argues.
rubric: Fits the length and is written in specific, accurate terms.

@quiz self
prompt: A reader of your draft writes: "Your objection attacks a premise that the author never asserts." In 120 to 180 words, write a response that says how you would check this, and what you would change in the paper.
hint: Say how you would test your reconstruction against the text, and what happens to the objection if the premise is not the author's.
solution: I would first reread the passage and list what the author actually says, and then ask whether my premise is stated or is a hidden premise the argument needs to be valid. If the author states it, I would add the page reference to the exposition and answer the reader. If it is a hidden premise, I would say so, argue that it is the most charitable way to make the argument valid, and say what the alternatives are, so that the reader can see that the premise is not mine. If the author does not need it, because the argument is valid with a weaker premise, I would retarget the objection to the premise the author does assert, since an objection aimed at a premise the author never asserts does not touch the argument. I would add a short note to the revision summary saying which of these cases applies and which paragraphs changed.
rubric: Says how the reconstruction would be checked against the text.
rubric: Distinguishes stated premises, hidden premises needed for validity and unneeded premises, and says what to do in each case.
rubric: Describes concrete changes to the paper and to the revision note.
