@article
You are waiting for a long and painful procedure to end. When it is over you think, with relief, "Thank goodness that's over." Arthur Prior's 1959 paper of that name argues that what you are glad about is not that the procedure is earlier than your thought (a tenseless fact, true of the event at all times), but that it is *past*. If so, tensed facts are ineliminable. This is the heart of the dispute between the **A-theory** and the **B-theory** of time.

### The positions

J. M. E. McTaggart ("The Unreality of Time", 1908) distinguished the **A-series**, which orders events as past, present and future, from the **B-series**, which orders them as earlier and later. The A-theory says that tense is a feature of reality. Its main versions are **presentism**, on which only present things exist (Prior, and Markosian's "A Defense of Presentism", 2004); the **growing block**, on which past and present things exist and the future does not (Broad, *Scientific Thought*, 1923); and the **moving spotlight**, on which all times exist and one is present. The B-theory, with **eternalism** (past, present and future things all exist), takes tense to be a feature of perspective, not of reality: Smart, D. C. Williams ("The Myth of Passage", 1951) and Mellor (*Real Time II*, 1998) defend versions. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 27% accepted or leaned toward the A-theory and 38% the B-theory; the share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence that it is true.

### McTaggart's argument

McTaggart argued that time is unreal. On one reading the argument is as follows.

| P1 | Time requires change. |
| P2 | Change requires the A-series. |
| P3 | The A-series is contradictory: each event is past, present and future, and these properties are incompatible. |
| P4 | Nothing contradictory is real. |
| C | So time is unreal. |

The argument is valid: time requires the A-series by P1 and P2, and the A-series is not real by P3 and P4. B-theorists deny P2: change is a thing's having different properties at different times, which the B-series suffices for. A-theorists deny P3: an event is not past, present and future at once; it *is* future, *will be* present and *will be* past, and McTaggart replied that this explanation uses tense again and so generates a regress.

### Fine's argument about tense

Kit Fine ("Tense and Reality", 2005) argues that four plausible claims cannot all hold. **Realism**: reality is constituted, at least in part, by tensed facts. **Neutrality**: no time is privileged, so the tensed facts of one time are no more part of reality than those of another. **Absolutism**: the constitution of reality is absolute, and not relative to a time or perspective. **Coherence**: reality is not contradictory.

| P1 | If Realism and Neutrality hold, then reality contains the tensed facts of every time, including incompatible facts. |
| P2 | If Absolutism holds, then these incompatible facts are constituents of reality absolutely. |
| P3 | If incompatible facts are absolutely constituents of reality, then reality is incoherent. |
| C | So Realism, Neutrality, Absolutism and Coherence do not all hold. |

The argument is valid. Suppose all four held: P1 and P2 give absolutely incompatible facts, P3 gives incoherence, and that contradicts Coherence. Each position drops one claim. The standard A-theorist drops Neutrality, since the present is privileged. The B-theorist drops Realism. The relativist drops Absolutism: reality is relative to a time. The fragmentalist, a view Fine develops in detail, drops Coherence in the sense that reality is not a single coherent whole but is divided into fragments, each coherent in itself.

### Relativity and the open future

Special relativity is extremely well confirmed, and simultaneity depends on the frame of reference, so physics does not distinguish a unique present in the way presentism seems to need. Presentists reply that a privileged slicing of spacetime may exist even if physics does not detect it, and the cost is that such a structure would be empirically idle. Whether spacetime is fundamental or emergent is open, since there is no accepted theory of quantum gravity. The tense debate meets last week's topic in the **open future**: some argue that future contingents are metaphysically indeterminate (Barnes and Cameron's work on the open future is the main recent treatment), which would be a case of metaphysical indeterminacy of the kind that the Barnes and Williams model describes.

### Presenting an argument

The deliverable for this block is to present a recent argument about metaphysical indeterminacy. A good presentation states the question, sets out the argument in standard form with premises labeled, says which premise is most contestable, and ends with a clear take-away. In five minutes, one argument and one reply is enough.

@example
title: Which claim does each position reject?
problem: For each position, say which of Fine's four claims (Realism, Neutrality, Absolutism, Coherence) it rejects. (a) Presentism. (b) The view that only tenseless, earlier-than facts constitute reality. (c) The view that the facts constituting reality are relative to a time. (d) The view that reality contains incompatible tensed facts, none of which is privileged, in a fragmented whole.
step: (a) Presentism privileges the present, so it rejects Neutrality.
step: (b) It says tensed facts do not constitute reality, so it rejects Realism.
step: (c) Relativizing reality to a time rejects Absolutism.
step: (d) It accepts Realism, Neutrality and Absolutism, so it must reject Coherence.
answer: (a) Neutrality; (b) Realism; (c) Absolutism; (d) Coherence.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: For McTaggart's argument, say which premise each reply attacks. (i) "Change is just a thing's having different properties at different times." (ii) "An event is not past, present and future at once; it is future, will be present, and will be past." (iii) "Time does not require change: a changeless period is still a stretch of time."
step: Recall P1 (time requires change), P2 (change requires the A-series), P3 (the A-series is contradictory), P4 (nothing contradictory is real).
step: (i) says change needs no A-series, so it denies P2.
step: (ii) says the A-properties are not incompatible when tensed correctly, so it denies P3.
step: (iii) denies that time requires change, so it denies P1.
answer: (i) P2; (ii) P3; (iii) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the A-theory as presentism. The growing block and the moving spotlight are also A-theories.
- Saying that eternalism is the B-theory. Eternalism is a thesis about what exists; one can hold it with a tensed view of reality, as in the moving spotlight.
- Reading special relativity as refuting presentism by itself. It removes a physically privileged present, and the argument needs the premise that a privileged present would have to be physically detectable.
- Treating McTaggart's argument as invalid. Each step follows; the replies deny a premise.

@practice self
prompt: Prepare a five-minute presentation outline of one argument about metaphysical indeterminacy: the argument from vagueness to unrestricted composition. State the question, the argument in standard form, the most contestable premise and a take-away.
hint: Use a premise saying that intermediately restricted composition yields indeterminate composition, one linking this to indeterminate existence, one denying that existence can be indeterminate, and one excluding nihilism.
solution: Question (30 seconds): must composition be unrestricted, if existence cannot be indeterminate? Argument (90 seconds). P1: if composition is restricted in an intermediate way (some things compose something and others do not), there is a possible case in which it is indeterminate whether some things compose something. P2: if it is indeterminate whether some things compose something, it is indeterminate how many things exist. P3: it cannot be indeterminate how many things exist. P4: composition is not nihilistic: some things compose something. C: composition is unrestricted. I note that it is valid: intermediate restriction would give indeterminate existence, which P3 forbids, so composition is universal or nihilistic, and P4 excludes nihilism. Most contestable premise (90 seconds): P3. The Lewis-Sider tradition motivates it by saying that vagueness is semantic, and the existential quantifier is too basic to be semantically indecisive. Barnes and Williams's model allows indeterminate existence, so a metaphysical indeterminist denies P3. The sharp-restriction view of Markosian denies P1 instead. Take-away (60 seconds): the argument forces a choice between unrestricted composition, nihilism, a brute sharp restriction and metaphysical indeterminacy in existence, and which is cheapest depends on how far one trusts the claim that vagueness is only semantic.

rubric: Identifies the most contestable premise and gives the main reply to it.
rubric: Is organized as a presentation, with a timed structure and a clear take-away.

@quiz self
prompt: Consider this argument against presentism. P1: If presentism is true, there is a unique, objective present. P2: If there is a unique, objective present, there is a privileged, frame-independent relation of simultaneity. P3: If special relativity is correct, there is no privileged, frame-independent relation of simultaneity. P4: Special relativity is correct. C: So presentism is false. In 150 to 200 words, say which premise a presentist should attack and what the cost is.
hint: Ask what a presentist can say about a structure that physics does not need.
solution: The argument is valid, so a presentist must deny a premise, and the best target is P3. Special relativity's predictions are confirmed to great precision, but its empirical content is compatible with a privileged slicing of spacetime into instants that is not detectable, as in neo-Lorentzian versions. The presentist can then say that relativity as a physical theory is correct while a privileged simultaneity exists in a deeper description. The cost is that the privileged foliation does no work in physics: any physical quantity can be computed without it, so it is empirically idle, and the presentist has to argue that metaphysical considerations, such as the reality of tense, are strong enough to justify an idle structure. A second cost is that the open question of whether spacetime is fundamental, in the absence of a theory of quantum gravity, makes it harder to say what the privileged slicing is a slicing of.

rubric: Explains the reply in terms of a privileged foliation or similar.
rubric: States a real cost of the reply and does not overstate what relativity shows.
