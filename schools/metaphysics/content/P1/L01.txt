@article
A mathematician says that there is a prime number between 20 and 30. A nominalist says that there are no numbers. Are they contradicting each other? If they are, someone is making a mistake about what exists, and metaphysics has a subject matter: the question what there is. If they are not, perhaps because the mathematician is speaking within arithmetic and the nominalist is asking something else, then a good deal of ontology may rest on a confusion. This is the question of **metaontology** (a term from Peter van Inwagen's 1998 paper "Meta-ontology"): what are ontological questions, can they be answered, and by what method? Much of current metaphysics, including the grounding debate of weeks 3 and 4, is a reaction to the positions in this lesson.

### Quine: commitment and the method of regimentation

Quine's 1948 paper "On What There Is" begins with the old puzzle of nonbeing: if "Pegasus does not exist" is true, what is it about? Quine answers with Russell's theory of descriptions and a more general proposal. Say what a theory says in the notation of first-order logic (**regimentation**). Then the theory is **committed** to exactly those things that must be among the values of its bound variables for the theory to be true. This **criterion of ontological commitment** does not say what exists. It says what a theory says exists, so that the question "what is there?" is replaced by two: what does our best theory say, and should we believe it? On the second, Quine offers no special metaphysical faculty: we choose among theories as in science, by simplicity, explanatory power and fit with evidence.

Regimentation matters because surface grammar misleads. "Every unicorn has one horn" regiments as $\forall x\,(Ux \to Hx)$, which is true when nothing is a unicorn: no commitment to unicorns. "Some dog is white" regiments as $\exists x\,(Dx \wedge Wx)$, which needs a white dog in the domain.

### Carnap: frameworks and the two kinds of question

Carnap's 1950 paper "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" distinguishes questions **internal** to a linguistic **framework** (the thing language, the number language) from **external** questions about the framework as a whole. "Is there a prime between 20 and 30?" is internal to arithmetic and is answered by calculation. "Are there numbers?" asked internally is trivially yes. Asked externally, as a question about whether numbers belong to reality over and above the framework, Carnap says it is either a pseudo-question with no cognitive content or, taken charitably, the practical question whether to adopt the number framework, to be settled by its usefulness. His argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | Every existence question about a kind of entity is either internal to a framework or external to every framework. |
| P2 | If an existence question is internal, it is settled by the framework's rules together with logic or empirical evidence. |
| P3 | If an existence question is external, it is either a pseudo-question without cognitive content or a practical question about whether to adopt the framework. |
| P4 | A question that is settled by a framework's rules and evidence, or is a pseudo-question, or is a practical question about adopting a framework, is not a substantive factual question of metaphysics. |
| C | So no existence question is a substantive factual question of metaphysics. |

The argument is valid: by P1 each question falls under P2 or P3, and either way its status falls under the antecedent of P4.

### Quine's reply

Quine's 1951 paper "On Carnap's Views on Ontology" attacks the argument at two places. First, the internal/external contrast depends on a distinction between framework-fixing, analytic conventions and factual claims, which Quine rejects in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism": this challenges P1 as a principled dichotomy. Second, he denies that "pragmatic" means "not factual": choosing to quantify over numbers is like choosing a scientific hypothesis, answered on grounds of simplicity and fit, and no less a question about what the world is like. That challenges P4. The line from Quine through van Inwagen is **neo-Quinean**.

### The Carnapian revival

Recent writers revive Carnap without his verificationism: Hirsch (quantifier variance), Thomasson (easy ontology) and Chalmers, whom we meet next week. Others, such as Schaffer, accept Carnap's complaint about the existence question but turn to a different one: not what exists but what is more fundamental than what, which is the subject of grounding.

@example
title: What does a theory commit us to?
problem: Regiment each sentence and say what, if anything, a Quinean takes it to be committed to. (a) "All phoenixes are red." (b) "There is a prime number between 40 and 50." (c) "Ana and Ben share a hobby."
step: (a) Regiment as $\forall x\,(Px \to Rx)$. This is true when nothing is a phoenix, so it requires nothing about phoenixes. There is no commitment to phoenixes; the sentence is committed only if some other sentence of the theory says there is a phoenix.
step: (b) Regiment as $\exists x\,(Nx \wedge Px \wedge 40 < x \wedge x < 50)$ with $Nx$ for "is a number" and $Px$ for "is prime". For the sentence to be true something in the domain must be a number, so the theory is committed to numbers (the primes between 40 and 50 are 41, 43 and 47).
step: (c) At face value, $\exists x\,(Hx \wedge Axa \wedge Axb)$ with $Hx$ for "is a hobby" and $Axy$ for "y engages in x": a commitment to hobbies. But a Quinean may paraphrase: "there is an activity $y$ such that Ana and Ben both do it and it is a leisure pursuit", with the quantifier ranging over activities or over kinds of activity. Commitment depends on the regimentation we judge best, and paraphrase can lower the cost.
answer: (a) no commitment; (b) numbers; (c) hobbies if taken at face value, though paraphrase may reduce this to activities.

@example
title: Which premise does a reply attack?
problem: In the course of discussion someone replies to Carnap: "Choosing a framework for talking about numbers is no more arbitrary than choosing a physical theory, so being a practical question does not stop it being a question about what is true." Which premise of Carnap's argument (set out above) does this reply attack, and does it threaten the argument's validity?
step: Find what the reply denies. It does not deny that the questions are of the kinds listed in P3, or that internal questions are settled by rules and evidence. It denies that being a practical question about adopting a framework makes a question non-substantive.
step: That is a denial of P4, which says a practical question about adopting a framework is not a substantive factual question.
step: Validity is untouched: the conclusion still follows from P1 to P4. A reply attacks a premise, and the argument remains valid with the premise denied; it is just no longer sound.
answer: The reply attacks P4. It does not affect validity, only soundness.

@mistakes
- Reading Quine's criterion as saying what exists. It says what a theory is committed to; whether to believe the theory is a further question settled by theoretical virtues.
- Saying Carnap thinks numbers do not exist. His internal answer to "are there numbers?" is yes, and the external question is either empty or practical.
- Reading "pragmatic" as "arbitrary". Carnap's framework choice is judged by usefulness, and Quine's reply is that the same is true of theory choice in science.
- Treating a conditional such as "all phoenixes are red" as committing a theory to phoenixes. The universally quantified conditional is true in an empty extension.

prompt: A theory regiments as $\exists x\,Fx \wedge \exists x\,Gx \wedge \neg\exists x\,(Fx \wedge Gx)$. What is the smallest number of things that a domain must contain for the theory to be true?
answer: 2
unit: things
check: min(n for n in range(1, 5) for F in range(1, 2 ** n) for G in range(1, 2 ** n) if F & G == 0)
hint: Something must be $F$ and something must be $G$, and nothing is both.
solution: One thing cannot be both $F$ and $G$, so the $F$ and the $G$ must be two different things. A domain of two things, one $F$ and one $G$, makes all three conjuncts true, so the theory is committed to at least two things.

@practice self
prompt: Ana says "There are holes in this cheese." Regiment the sentence at face value, say what a Quinean takes it to be committed to, and describe one way of paraphrasing it so as to avoid that commitment. State one cost of your paraphrase.
hint: Quantify over holes at face value first, then try to say the same thing using only a predicate of the cheese.
solution: At face value, $\exists x\,(Hx \wedge Ix c)$, with $Hx$ for "x is a hole" and $Ixc$ for "x is in cheese c": the sentence is committed to holes among the values of its variables. A paraphrase avoids this by taking the cheese as the only thing quantified over and saying that it is perforated, a predicate of the cheese and its parts, so that "there are holes in the cheese" becomes "the cheese is perforated". One cost is that ordinary talk about holes supports inferences the paraphrase must also capture, such as "the hole in this cheese is bigger than the one in that cheese" or "there are three holes, and the largest is near the rind", so the paraphrase needs separate resources for counting and comparison. If it recovers them by quantifying over regions of the cheese that are not filled, it has traded one commitment for another.
rubric: Gives a correct face-value regimentation with a quantifier over holes and says that the commitment is to holes.
rubric: Offers a genuine paraphrase that avoids quantifying over holes, using a predicate of the cheese or similar.
rubric: States a real cost, such as difficulty capturing counting, comparison or inference, or a new commitment to regions.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement best describes Quine's view of the question "what is there?" after regimentation?
choice*: It is answered, like questions in science, by choosing the best overall theory and then reading off what that theory is committed to.
choice: It is a pseudo-question until a framework has been adopted, and then it is answered trivially.

solution: For Quine, ontology is continuous with science: we choose among regimented theories by simplicity and explanatory power and then read off commitments. The view that the question is a pseudo-question until a framework is adopted describes Carnap's picture. The view that a special faculty answers it is one Quine rejects, and the view that it cannot be asked misdescribes the criterion, which attributes commitments to theories.

prompt: In one paragraph, say whether Quine's reply to Carnap succeeds in showing that the question "are there numbers?" is a substantive question. Name the premise of Carnap's argument you are attacking or defending, and give the strongest reply to your own position.
hint: Decide first whether a question can be both practical and factual, and then say what a Carnapian would reply.
solution: I defend Quine's reply to premise P4: a question is not made non-factual merely because its answer is reached by pragmatic considerations such as simplicity and fit, for those are the considerations that also decide among scientific theories, and nobody concludes that physics is not about the world. I answer that the dependence of physical theory on mathematics is itself part of what is observed to succeed together, so the evidence bears on the whole theory and cannot be neatly divided, which is Quine's holism; the Carnapian can still insist that this assumes the very conception of evidence in dispute.
