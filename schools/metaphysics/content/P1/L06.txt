@article
Socrates is necessarily human, if he exists. He is also necessarily a member of the set whose only member is Socrates, and necessarily distinct from the Eiffel Tower. Should we say that all three are part of what it is to be Socrates? Most people feel that the first is, and the other two are not. The question is what the difference is, and what **essence** is.

### The modal account and its trouble

The traditional **modal account** says that a property is part of an object's essence when the object could not exist without having it: $F$ is essential to $x$ just in case necessarily, if $x$ exists then $x$ is $F$. Kit Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994) argues that this is not an account of essence at all. He observes that it counts as essential to Socrates being a member of the singleton $\{\text{Socrates}\}$ and being distinct from the Eiffel Tower, and it makes every necessary truth essential to everything. The singleton is the other way round: it belongs to the nature of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$ to have Socrates as a member, not to the nature of Socrates to belong to a set. The argument can be set out in valid form.

| P1 | If the modal account is correct, then whatever an object has necessarily, given that it exists, is part of its essence. |
| P2 | Necessarily, if Socrates exists, then Socrates is a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$. |
| P3 | It is not part of Socrates's essence to be a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$. |
| C | So the modal account is not correct. |

The argument is valid: if the account were correct, P1 and P2 would make membership essential, contradicting P3. A defender of the modal account can deny P3, accepting the consequences, or can deny P2 by denying that sets exist necessarily given their members. A third response does not deny a premise but replaces the account: it restricts the modal account to a class of **sparse** properties so that membership of a set and distinctness from arbitrary things are not counted. Ryan Wildman's "Modality, Sparsity, and Essence" (2013) develops such an account. Fine's own conclusion is that essence is a finer notion than necessity: necessity cannot be what essence is analyzed by.

### Essence as hyperintensional

Fine takes the **essence** of a thing to be what it is, stated by propositions true in virtue of the nature of that thing. Statements of the form "it lies in the nature of $x$ that $A$" are not closed under necessary equivalence, so essence is **hyperintensional**, like ground. Fine distinguishes **constitutive** essence, the core of what a thing is, from **consequential** essence, which includes whatever follows from the constitutive part ("Senses of Essence", 1995). He also proposes that metaphysical necessity itself is a consequence of essence: what is necessary is what is true in virtue of the natures of all things together.

### Real definition

A **real definition** says what a thing or kind is, as opposed to a **nominal definition**, which gives the meaning of a word. "To be a prime number is to be a natural number greater than 1 whose only divisors are 1 and itself" is offered as a real definition. Gideon Rosen's "Real Definition" (2015) treats statements of the form "to be $F$ is to be $G$" as a distinct, hyperintensional kind of claim, and proposes that real definition can be taken as the basic notion in a theory of essence.

### Essence and ground

Essence and ground are closely linked. The fact that Socrates is a member of $\{\text{Socrates}\}$ is grounded in the fact that Socrates exists, and the plausible explanation of why is that it lies in the nature of the singleton to have its member. A number of writers, among them Rosen, and Dasgupta in "The Possibility of Physicalism" (2014), have proposed that facts about essence help to explain ground. Dasgupta holds that essentialist facts are **autonomous**: they are not apt to be grounded and need no further explanation. Whether essence or ground is the more basic notion is open.

### Reconstructing priority monism

The deliverable for weeks 5 and 6 is to reconstruct the argument for priority monism. A good reconstruction does three things: it states premises so that the conclusion follows (as in the gunk argument of last week), it labels the premises, and it says which of them each reply denies. Fine's argument is a model of the form: a single decisive case, an explicit bridge premise, and replies matched to premises.

@example
title: Replies to the Socrates argument
problem: For each response to Fine's argument, say which premise it attacks, if any. (i) "It is part of Socrates's essence to belong to the set of which he is the only member." (ii) "Sets do not necessarily exist given their members, so the necessity claim fails." (iii) "The modal account should count only sparse properties."
step: Recall P1 (modal account makes necessary properties essential), P2 (necessarily, if Socrates exists, he is a member of his singleton) and P3 (that membership is not essential).
step: (i) says that membership is essential, which denies P3. It keeps the modal account but accepts an odd consequence.
step: (ii) denies that the necessity holds, which denies P2.
step: (iii) does not deny a premise: it grants that the unrestricted account fails and proposes a modified account. It accepts the conclusion for the account Fine attacks.
answer: (i) P3; (ii) P2; (iii) no premise; it replaces the account.

@example
title: Telling essence from necessity
problem: Which of these are necessary truths about the number 7 but, on Fine's view, plausibly not part of its essence? (a) 7 is prime. (b) 7 is such that it is raining or it is not raining. (c) 7 is distinct from Mount Everest.
step: A modal test counts all three as necessary and so essential, since each holds of 7 in every world where 7 exists.
step: (a) Being prime is plausibly part of what it is to be 7, so it is part of its essence.
step: (b) and (c) are necessary but concern things other than what 7 is, and Fine's point is that the modal account cannot tell them from (a).
answer: (b) and (c) are necessary but plausibly not essential; (a) is essential.

@mistakes
- Saying that Fine denies that Socrates is necessarily a member of his singleton. He accepts the necessity and denies that it shows essence.
- Confusing a real definition (what a thing is) with a nominal definition (what a word means).
- Treating the modal account as a view no one defends. It is the starting point, and there are refinements of it.
- Reading Fine's claim that essence is finer than necessity as the claim that essence is unrelated to necessity. He proposes that metaphysical necessity derives from essence.

prompt: In 150 to 200 words, explain how a fact about essence might explain why a grounding fact holds, using the case of the planet Venus and the set whose only member is Venus.
hint: Ask what it is to be a singleton, and what follows about what grounds its existence.
solution: The fact that the set whose only member is Venus exists is grounded in the fact that Venus exists. What explains this grounding claim? One proposal links ground and essence: it lies in the nature of a singleton to be the set that has a given thing as its only member, so the singleton's existence is explained by the existence of its member. This connects with Fine's observation that the modal account counts membership as essential to the member: the essentialist account gets the direction right because essence is not symmetric.
