@article
Tomorrow a fair coin will be tossed, and suppose that the future is not yet settled: nothing in the present makes it the case that the coin will land heads or that it will land tails. Is "the coin will land heads" true now, false now, or neither? One answer is that it is true or false and we cannot know which. Another says that the sentence is not yet determinate because our words are not precise enough. A third says that the world itself is unsettled. This lesson is about the third answer. The material here is a different topic from the sorites paradox; it concerns what the world, and not just our words, could be like.

### Three sources of indeterminacy

**Epistemic** indeterminacy is ignorance of a fact that is settled. **Semantic** indeterminacy is indecision in what our words mean, so that more than one precise meaning fits our use. **Metaphysical indeterminacy** is the claim that the world itself fails to settle some matter. Lewis, in *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), argued that the only intelligible vagueness is semantic, since the way the world is cannot be vague, only our representations of it. Elizabeth Barnes ("Ontic Vagueness: A Guide for the Perplexed", 2010) and Barnes with J. R. G. Williams ("A Theory of Metaphysical Indeterminacy", 2011) argue that metaphysical indeterminacy is coherent and gives it a formal model.

### The Barnes and Williams model

A **precisification** of the world is a complete, classically consistent way for the world to be that is left open. The actual world is, as it were, one of these, but it is **unsettled** which. A sentence is **determinately true** if it is true on every open precisification, **determinately false** if false on every one, and **indeterminate** otherwise. Classical logic is retained: every precisification makes "$P$ or not $P$" true, so excluded middle is determinately true even when $P$ is not. Unlike supervaluationism, where precisifications are ways of making a word precise, these are ways the world might be. The model is a representation of indeterminacy and is not an analysis of it, and its authors say that the notion of unsettledness is taken as primitive.

Other accounts exist. Jessica Wilson ("A Determinable-Based Account of Metaphysical Indeterminacy", 2013) says that an object is indeterminate when it has a determinable property without any unique determinate under it. There is also work on indeterminacy in quantum mechanics, by Skow (2010), Darby (2010) and Calosi and Wilson (2019), on the idea that a system in superposition lacks a determinate value. This is controversial: the measurement problem has no agreed solution, and some interpretations, such as many-worlds and the Bohmian one, do not take the state this way.

### Evans's argument

Gareth Evans's short paper "Can There Be Vague Objects?" (1978) is usually read as an argument that there can be no indeterminate identity. Write $\nabla A$ for "it is indeterminate whether $A$" and $\Delta A$ for "it is determinate that $A$".

| P1 | If $\nabla(a = b)$, then $b$ has the property of being such that $\nabla(a = x)$ holds of it. |
| P2 | $a$ lacks that property: $\neg\nabla(a = a)$. |
| P3 | If $b$ has a property that $a$ lacks, then $a \neq b$ (Leibniz's Law). |
| C1 | So if $\nabla(a = b)$, then $a \neq b$. |
| P4 | If $\nabla(a = b)$, then it is not determinate that $a \neq b$. |
| P5 | If C1 holds, then: if $\nabla(a = b)$, it is determinate that $a \neq b$. |
| C | So it is not the case that $\nabla(a = b)$. |

The argument is valid. C1 follows from P1 to P3; then P5 gives $\nabla(a = b) \to \Delta(a \neq b)$ and P4 gives $\nabla(a = b) \to \neg\Delta(a \neq b)$, so $\nabla(a = b)$ is excluded. P5 packs in two assumptions that should be made explicit: that the derivation of C1 is itself determinate, so $\Delta(\nabla(a = b) \to a \neq b)$, and that $\nabla(a = b) \to \Delta\nabla(a = b)$, that is, that any indeterminacy of identity is itself determinate. Only with both can $\Delta(a \neq b)$ be detached, and the second is the natural target for anyone who allows higher-order indeterminacy. Replies attack P5, the transfer of determinacy across a derivation, or P1, the property-abstraction step: Terence Parsons (*Indeterminate Identity*, 2000) and Barnes (2009) both reject steps in the argument. Lewis (1988) read Evans as targeting indeterminacy that is not due to semantic indecision in names.

@example
title: Determinacy in a model of precisifications
problem: A region contains the simples $x$ and $y$, and it is unsettled whether they compose a thing $z$. Five precisifications are open. In the first, second and fifth, $z$ exists; in the third and fourth it does not. Say whether each sentence is determinately true, determinately false or indeterminate. (a) "$x$ exists." (b) "$z$ exists." (c) "$z$ exists or $z$ does not exist." (d) "There are exactly three things in the region." In how many precisifications is (d) true?
step: (a) $x$ exists on all five precisifications, so the sentence is determinately true.
step: (b) $z$ exists on three and not on two, so "$z$ exists" is indeterminate.
step: (c) On each precisification, either $z$ exists or it does not, so the disjunction is true on all five: determinately true, although neither disjunct is.
step: (d) Three things ($x$, $y$, $z$) exist exactly where $z$ exists, which is three precisifications; on the other two there are two things. So (d) is true on 3 of the 5, and indeterminate.
answer: (a) determinately true; (b) indeterminate; (c) determinately true; (d) indeterminate, true on 3 of the 5 precisifications.

@example
title: The argument from vagueness to unrestricted composition
problem: Lewis (1986) and Sider (2001) argue that composition is unrestricted. State the argument in valid form and say which premise each of these replies attacks. (i) "Composition may be restricted by a sharp, brute condition, so no case is borderline." (ii) "It can be indeterminate how many things exist."
step: The argument. P1: if composition is restricted in an intermediate way (some things compose something and others do not), then there is a possible case in which it is indeterminate whether some things compose something. P2: if it is indeterminate whether some things compose something, then it is indeterminate how many things exist. P3: it cannot be indeterminate how many things exist. P4: composition is not nihilistic: some things do compose something. C: so composition is unrestricted.
step: Validity. If composition were restricted in an intermediate way, P1 and P2 would make the number of things indeterminate, contradicting P3. So composition is either universal or nihilistic, and P4 excludes nihilism, leaving unrestricted composition.
step: (i) A brute but sharp restriction, of the kind Markosian's "Brutal Composition" (1998) allows, has no borderline cases, so it denies P1.
step: (ii) Accepting indeterminate existence, as the Barnes and Williams framework permits, denies P3.
answer: The argument is valid. Reply (i) attacks P1 and reply (ii) attacks P3.

@mistakes
- Saying that metaphysical indeterminacy is ignorance. It is a claim that the world leaves the matter unsettled, and there is nothing to be ignorant of.
- Confusing precisifications of the world with the sharpenings of a word. In the model they are worldly alternatives, and the semantics of the word may be perfectly precise.
- Thinking that indeterminacy requires giving up excluded middle. In the model "$P$ or not $P$" is determinately true even when $P$ is indeterminate.
- Reading Evans as showing that there are no vague words. His argument concerns identity in the world.

@practice self
prompt: In 150 to 200 words, say which premise of Evans's argument you would attack, and defend that choice against the best reply.
hint: Pick one of P1, P2 or P5, state what it assumes, and say what a defender of the argument would answer.
solution: I would attack P5, the claim that if C1 holds then, given $\nabla(a = b)$, it is determinate that $a \neq b$. The defender's reason is that C1 is a logical consequence of P1 to P3 and so is itself determinate: $\Delta(\nabla(a = b) \to a \neq b)$. But from that one gets only $\Delta\nabla(a = b) \to \Delta(a \neq b)$. To detach the consequent we need $\nabla(a = b) \to \Delta\nabla(a = b)$: if it is indeterminate whether $a = b$, then that very indeterminacy is determinate. That is a claim that there is no higher-order indeterminacy here, and it is exactly what a theorist who allows higher-order indeterminacy denies. A defender replies that Leibniz's Law is logical and that I have only relocated the problem to P1, where "being indeterminately identical to $a$" is treated as a genuine property. I answer that P1 is one place the argument can be resisted but P5 is a better target, because it needs an extra premise that is not among P1 to P4, and the premise is false on any account that countenances higher-order indeterminacy, as most accounts of vagueness do.

@quiz self
prompt: Construct a case, not about heaps, baldness or any other sorites series, in which a matter might be settled by the world, unsettled by the world, or settled but unknown, and explain in 150 to 200 words how an epistemic, a semantic and a metaphysical view of indeterminacy would each describe it.
hint: Choose a case such as a border, a future event or a measurement, and say what each view locates the indeterminacy in.
solution: Case: a treaty places the border "at the river's mouth", and a flood has left the river with two outlets. On the epistemic view, there is a fact about which outlet is the mouth, fixed by how we use the words and the physical facts, and no one can know it. On the semantic view, "the river's mouth" is indecisive between the two outlets: both are acceptable precisifications of the phrase, and the sentence "the border is at outlet one" is neither supertrue nor superfalse, but the river itself is exactly as it is. On the metaphysical view, the world does not settle which outlet is the mouth, so that the river's geography is unsettled, in the way the Barnes and Williams model represents with open precisifications of the world. The semantic view seems the most plausible for this case, since the unsettledness is explained by our word choice. A case for the metaphysical view would need a matter, such as a quantum measurement outcome on some interpretations, where no precise way of using words removes the unsettledness.
