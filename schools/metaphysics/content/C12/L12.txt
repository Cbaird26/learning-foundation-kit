@article
Two people say "I am hungry." The same words, the same linguistic rule, and yet one has said something that might be true while the other has said something false. And a hiker who says "The water is cold" at 12 degrees Celsius may be right in one company and wrong in another. A semantics that assigns each sentence fixed truth-conditions must be extended to handle sentences whose truth depends on the occasion of use and on how sharply a word draws its lines.

### Character and content

David Kaplan's "Demonstratives" (written in the 1970s and published in 1989) separates two levels of meaning. The **character** of an expression is a rule that takes a **context of use**, with its agent, time, place and world, to a content. The **content** is what is said in that context: roughly a function from circumstances of evaluation (worlds and times) to extensions. The character of "I" is the rule "the agent of the context." In Ana's mouth its content is Ana, and in Ben's mouth it is Ben. Pure indexicals such as "I", "here", "now" and "today" need no accompanying gesture; true demonstratives such as "that" need a demonstration or an intention to fix their referent. For Kaplan, indexicals are **directly referential**: they contribute their referent to the content, and not a descriptive condition, so they are rigid.

| P1 | If the character of an indexical sentence were the same as what is said by an utterance of it, then two speakers who each sincerely and correctly utter "I am hungry" would say the same thing. |
| P2 | Ana and Ben, uttering "I am hungry," do not say the same thing: what Ana says can be true while what Ben says is false. |
| C | So the character of a sentence is not what is said by an utterance of it. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). Premise P1 is the only place for a reply, and few take it: the claim that the two speakers say the same thing runs into the fact that each is describing a different person.

Kaplan's logic of demonstratives has a consequence. "I am here now" is true in every context, since the agent of a context is located at its place at its time, so it is **logically true**. But the content it expresses in a given context, that Ana is in Lisbon at noon, is not necessary: Ana might have been elsewhere. So a sentence can be true in every context and still express a contingent content, which separates the logical truth of a sentence from the necessity of what it says.

### Vagueness

A predicate is **vague** when it has borderline cases that more information would not settle. Consider "is cold" for water at a temperature of $n$ tenths of a degree Celsius, written $C(n)$. Water at 2.0 degrees is cold; water at 40.0 degrees is not. Yet a tenth of a degree seems to make no difference:

| P1 | $C(20)$: water at 2.0 degrees is cold. |
| P2 | $\forall n\,(C(n) \to C(n+1))$: if water at $n$ tenths of a degree is cold, so is water a tenth of a degree warmer. |
| C | $C(400)$: water at 40.0 degrees is cold. |

The argument is valid: P2 gives 380 conditionals, from $C(20) \to C(21)$ to $C(399) \to C(400)$, and 380 steps of modus ponens connect P1 to C. P1 is true and C is false, so P2 must be false in classical logic. The reason that P2 looks true is that vague predicates seem **tolerant**, and one argument for tolerance is as follows:

| P1 | If competent speakers cannot tell two samples of water apart by feeling them, then the word "cold" applies to one just in case it applies to the other. |
| P2 | Competent speakers cannot tell by feeling between water at $n$ tenths and water at $n + 1$ tenths of a degree. |
| C | So "cold" applies to water at $n$ tenths just in case it applies to water at $n + 1$ tenths. |

The argument is valid. The epistemicist attacks P1: meaning is fixed by use in a way that can draw lines finer than our powers of discrimination. The epistemicist adds that the argument proves too much: applied along any series of indiscriminable steps, it leads to the sorites conclusion.

### Four semantic responses

- **Epistemicism** (Timothy Williamson) keeps classical logic and bivalence: there is a sharp boundary, but a margin-for-error principle makes it unknowable.
- **Supervaluationism** (Kit Fine) says a sentence is true when it is true on every admissible **sharpening** of the vague words. Fine emphasized **penumbral connections**: relations between vague predicates that hold however they are sharpened. "No water is both cold and hot" comes out supertrue because every admissible sharpening puts the line for "hot" above the line for "cold", though neither "water at 20 degrees is cold" nor "water at 20 degrees is hot" is supertrue or superfalse.
- **Degree theories** give sentences degrees of truth between 0 and 1. If the connectives are truth-functional in the usual min-max way, with $v(\neg A) = 1 - v(A)$, $v(A \wedge B) = \min(v(A), v(B))$ and $v(A \vee B) = \max(v(A), v(B))$, then a conjunction of two borderline sentences, each with degree 0.4, has degree 0.4, although "water at that temperature is both cold and hot" seems plainly false. A degree theorist can reply by using a different conjunction, such as $\max(0, v(A) + v(B) - 1)$.
- **Contextualism**, developed by Diana Raffman and Stewart Shapiro among others, says that the boundary of a vague predicate shifts with the context of judgment, for instance as one moves along a series; no single context makes all the conditionals true while the base premise is true and the conclusion false.

A further difficulty for the sharpening approach is **higher-order vagueness**: it can be vague which sharpenings are admissible, and so vague which sentences are borderline. And gradable adjectives, as in "cold for a bath" against "cold for a drink", depend on a comparison class, which is a further kind of context-sensitivity.

@example
title: A new sorites and what responses say
problem: Show that this reasoning is valid and say what an epistemicist and a contextualist each say about its second premise: "A person standing 1 meter from a campfire is near it. Moving a meter farther away never takes someone from near to not near. So a person standing 600 meters from the fire is near it."
step: Write $N(n)$ for "a person standing $n$ meters from the fire is near it". The premises are $N(1)$ and $\forall n\,(N(n) \to N(n+1))$, and the conclusion is $N(600)$.
step: Validity: there are $600 - 1 = 599$ conditionals from $N(1) \to N(2)$ to $N(599) \to N(600)$, and 599 applications of modus ponens take us from the first premise to the conclusion.
step: The first premise is true and the conclusion is false, so classically the second premise must be false.
step: The epistemicist accepts this: there is a last distance at which someone is near, though it cannot be known which. The contextualist says that in each context the conditionals seem true because the cutoff moves as one considers neighboring cases, but no single context makes all 599 conditionals true.
answer: The argument is valid; the epistemicist says the second premise is false with an unknowable boundary; the contextualist says it holds only context by context.

@example
title: Character and content
problem: Ana says "I am in Lisbon today" on a Monday, and Ben says the same sentence on a Tuesday. Give the character of the sentence, the content of each utterance, and say whether the sentence's character differs between them.
step: The sentence has one character: a rule taking a context to a content, where "I" picks the agent and "today" the day of the context.
step: In Ana's context, the content is that Ana is in Lisbon on that Monday.
step: In Ben's context, the content is that Ben is in Lisbon on that Tuesday.
step: The character is the same in both utterances, but the contents differ, and so can their truth-values.
answer: One character; two different contents (Ana in Lisbon on Monday, Ben in Lisbon on Tuesday).

@mistakes
- Identifying character with content. The character is a rule, and the content is what the rule delivers in a context.
- Taking "I am here now" to be necessary. It is true in every context, but its content in a context is contingent.
- Saying that supervaluationism denies excluded middle. "A or not A" is supertrue even when neither disjunct is.
- Treating vagueness as ambiguity or ignorance in every theory. Epistemicists locate it in ignorance, while others do not.

@quiz self
prompt: Use the predicates "cold" and "hot" for water to explain what a penumbral connection is, and say why it is a problem for a degree theory with the min rule for conjunction and a point in favor of supervaluationism. Then give one reply the degree theorist can make.
hint: Compare "Water at 20 degrees is both cold and hot" on the two theories.
solution: A penumbral connection is a logical relation between vague predicates that holds however they are sharpened, as with "cold" and "hot": nothing is both. On supervaluationism every admissible sharpening puts the line for "hot" above the line for "cold", so "Water at 20 degrees is both cold and hot" is false on every sharpening and is superfalse, although each conjunct on its own is borderline. On a degree theory with the min rule, if each conjunct has degree 0.4, the conjunction has degree 0.4, so a sentence that seems plainly false is partly true. A reply: use a different conjunction, such as $\max(0, v(A) + v(B) - 1)$, which gives 0 when the two degrees sum to at most 1, or add constraints linking the degrees of "cold" and "hot". The cost is a departure from the simplest min-max rules.
