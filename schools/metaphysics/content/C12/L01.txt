@article
Suppose a friend tells you, "Vulcan is hotter than Mercury." Astronomers once proposed a planet called Vulcan inside Mercury's orbit, and there is no such planet. You still understand the sentence. You know what it would take for it to be true, and you know that, as things are, it is not. How can a word contribute to a sentence's meaning when nothing answers to it? This question is where philosophy of language begins.

### Meaning, reference and extension

Four notions must be kept apart.

- The **referent** of a singular term is the object it stands for. "Mercury" refers to the planet.
- The **extension** of a predicate is the set of things it is true of. The extension of "is a planet with a ring system" is the set of such planets.
- The **truth-value** of a sentence is True or False. On Frege's picture (next week) it is the sentence's reference.
- The **meaning** of an expression is whatever a competent speaker grasps in understanding it. What this is, is the main question of the course.

A leading idea, and the one this course follows, is that to know the meaning of a declarative sentence is at least to know its **truth-conditions**: the circumstances in which it would be true. "There are exactly 17 fish in Lake Orlo" has clear truth-conditions even if nobody has counted. Knowing the truth-conditions of a sentence is not knowing whether it is true.

### Use and mention

Words can be used to talk about things or mentioned in order to talk about the words. "Mercury is small" is about a planet. "'Mercury' has seven letters" is about a word, and the quotation marks are what show it. Without them, "Mercury has seven letters" is false, because the planet has no letters. Semantic claims are claims about expressions, so they need quotation: "'Mercury' refers to Mercury."

### Three views of what a name contributes

1. **Millianism**, from John Stuart Mill's *A System of Logic* (Book I, chapter 2): a proper name denotes its bearer but has no further meaning ("connotation"). Its contribution to a sentence is just the bearer.
2. **Descriptivism**: a name is associated with a description, and the description gives its meaning and fixes its reference. Frege and Russell are the classic sources (weeks 2 and 3).
3. **Ideationalism**: words signify ideas in the speaker's mind (a view found in Locke's *Essay*, Book III). A standard objection is that meanings are public and shared, while ideas are private and differ from speaker to speaker.

### An argument against the simplest Millian view

The Millian claim, that the meaning of a name is its bearer, faces the problem of empty names:

| P1 | If the meaning of a name is its bearer, then a name that has no bearer is meaningless. |
| P2 | "Vulcan" is a name that has no bearer. |
| P3 | "Vulcan" is not meaningless. |
| C | So the meaning of a name is not its bearer. |

The argument is valid. Suppose the meaning of a name is its bearer. Then by P1 and P2, "Vulcan" is meaningless, which contradicts P3. Premise P2 is hard to resist: there is no such planet. The live replies attack P3 or P1. A Millian who attacks P3 says that "Vulcan" really is meaningless, and that speakers only seem to understand sentences containing it, perhaps because they think of descriptions or of a pretense. This faces the apparent meaningfulness of "Vulcan does not exist," which seems true and informative. A different reply accepts the conclusion and moves to a Fregean view in which a name also has a sense (week 2). Premise P1 is attacked by theorists who say that a name can contribute something other than a bearer, such as a descriptive condition, which is already to leave simple Millianism.

### Looking ahead

Two more problems for the simple view will drive weeks 2 and 3. A true identity sentence such as "Mercury is Mercury" is trivial, while "Mercury is the planet nearest the Sun" is informative. And replacing a name with another name for the same thing seems to change truth-value in some contexts, as in "Ana believes that ..." Any theory of meaning must say how these data fit together with truth-conditions.

@example
title: Telling referent, extension and truth-value apart
problem: Take the sentence "Ana's cat sleeps", where "Ana's cat" picks out one particular cat. For each of "Ana's cat", "sleeps" and the whole sentence, say which semantic notion applies: referent, extension or truth-value. Then say what knowing its truth-conditions requires.
step: "Ana's cat" is a singular term. Its **referent** is the cat itself, an animal, not a word or an idea.
step: "sleeps" is a predicate. Its **extension** is the set of things that are asleep (at the time in question). The cat is or is not a member of that set.
step: The sentence is true when the cat is in the extension of "sleeps" and false otherwise. Its **truth-value** is therefore True or False, depending on how things are.
step: To know the sentence's truth-conditions you need to know that it is true just in case the cat Ana owns is asleep. You do not have to know whether the cat is asleep right now.
answer: "Ana's cat" has a referent, "sleeps" has an extension, the sentence has a truth-value; understanding it requires knowing its truth-conditions, not its truth-value.

@example
title: Locating the objection in the Vulcan argument
problem: Consider this argument. P1: If the meaning of a name is its bearer, then a name with no bearer is meaningless. P2: "Vulcan" has no bearer. P3: "Vulcan" is not meaningless. C: So the meaning of a name is not its bearer. A Millian says: "Sentences with 'Vulcan' in them are not really meaningful, and our sense that we understand them is an illusion." Which premise does this attack, and is the argument still valid?
step: The argument's premises are P1 (if meaning is bearer, bearerless names are meaningless), P2 ("Vulcan" has no bearer) and P3 ("Vulcan" is not meaningless).
step: The Millian grants P1 and P2 and says "Vulcan" is meaningless, so denies P3.
step: Rejecting a premise does not make a valid argument invalid. The argument remains valid; the Millian claims only that it is unsound, because P3 is false.
step: The debate then turns on whether the apparent meaningfulness of "Vulcan does not exist" can be explained away.
answer: The reply attacks P3. The argument stays valid, and the question is whether P3 is true.

@mistakes
- Treating the meaning of a name as the same thing as its referent in every theory. That is Millianism, one view among several.
- Confusing truth-conditions with truth: you can understand a sentence without knowing whether it is true.
- Omitting quotation marks: "Mercury has seven letters" is about the planet, and "'Mercury' has seven letters" is about the word.
- Saying a valid argument is refuted by a reply that denies a premise. A reply that denies a premise makes the argument unsound in the replier's view; it does not make it invalid.

answer: 8
unit: things
check: 4 + 6 - 2

@quiz self
prompt: Write a sentence about a word and a sentence about the thing the word names, using quotation marks correctly, and explain in two or three sentences why dropping the quotation marks in the first changes its truth-value.
hint: Choose a word whose letters or syllables are clearly different from the properties of the thing it names.
solution: "Bread" has five letters. Bread is made from flour. If the quotation marks are dropped from the first sentence, it says that bread, the food, has five letters. A food has no letters, so the sentence becomes false, while the quoted sentence, which is about the word, is true. Quotation marks turn the expression into a name of itself, so they change what the sentence is about.
