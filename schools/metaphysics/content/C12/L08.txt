@article
Imagine a planet exactly like Earth in every respect but one: where Earth has water, a clear liquid that fills the lakes and falls as rain, this planet, Twin Earth, has a different clear liquid with the same appearance and uses, whose chemical formula is abbreviated XYZ. The people there speak a language indistinguishable from English and call the liquid "water." Hilary Putnam's thought experiment, in "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), asks what the Twin Earthers' word means.

### The case

Set the time in 1750, before anyone on either planet knows the chemistry. Oscar lives on Earth, and his duplicate, Twin Oscar, lives on Twin Earth. They are molecule for molecule alike, and every internal state, including what each would say about what he is thinking, is alike. When Oscar says "water", the word applies to H$_2$O. When Twin Oscar says "water", it applies to XYZ. Putnam concludes that the two words differ in extension, and so in meaning, although the speakers' psychological states are the same. The setting in 1750 is there so that no speaker has beliefs from chemistry that would distinguish the two.

### The argument

Putnam identifies two assumptions that, he argues, cannot both hold: that knowing the meaning of a term is a matter of being in a certain psychological state (in the narrow sense, a state that does not depend on the environment), and that the meaning of a term determines its extension. The argument runs:

| P1 | If two terms have the same meaning, they have the same extension. |
| P2 | Oscar and Twin Oscar are in the same narrow psychological state. |
| P3 | Oscar's word "water" and Twin Oscar's word "water" differ in extension. |
| C1 | So the two words differ in meaning. |
| C2 | So meaning is not fixed by narrow psychological state alone. |

The argument is valid: C1 follows from P1 and P3 by modus tollens, and since the psychological states are the same (P2) while the meanings differ (C1), meaning is not determined by narrow psychological state, which is C2. The conclusion is often put by saying that meanings, or contents, are not "in the head". Premise P3 depends on the claim from last week that "water" refers to a kind whose nature is set by the environment: it is an **indexical** or deferential component in the word. In Putnam's formulation, a term like "water" applies to stuff that bears the same-liquid relation to the samples around us. A second part of Putnam's view is the **division of linguistic labor**: many speakers use "elm" and "beech" and cannot tell the trees apart but defer to experts who can, so the extension of the term for them is fixed by the community.

### Replies

One reply rejects P2, by saying that the relevant states differ, perhaps by taking the content of thoughts to depend on the environment. This leads to **externalism about mental content**, not only meaning. A second reply denies P3 for "water", holding that XYZ is water, or that the words do not differ in extension when the speakers use them. A third reply keeps both: it accepts that the ordinary notion of meaning is externalist and supplies a second, **narrow content**, shared by Oscar and Twin Oscar, that plays a role in explaining behavior; this is the two-factor approach that Jerry Fodor and others developed in different ways. Critics have also noted that human bodies are mostly water, so the twins could not be molecule-for-molecule duplicates; defenders reply that the case can be adjusted to avoid the problem without affecting the point.

### Burge and social externalism

Tyler Burge's arthritis case (1979) pushes in a different direction. A man thinks that he has arthritis in his thigh, which is a misapplication of the term, since arthritis is a condition of the joints. Burge asks us to imagine a counterfactual community where "arthritis" is used for a wider range of ailments, including thigh ailments. There his duplicate would not have the belief that he has arthritis. The difference is not in the physical environment but in the linguistic community, so mental contents depend on social facts. This is **social externalism**, and it is distinct from the natural-kind externalism of Twin Earth. Both raise a problem about **self-knowledge**: if the content of my thought depends on facts I do not know, how can I know a priori what I am thinking?

@example
title: Spelling out the Twin Earth argument
problem: Put Putnam's reasoning in standard form and identify what each premise says. Say which claims the argument shows are inconsistent.
step: P1: same meaning, same extension. P2: Oscar and Twin Oscar share narrow psychological state. P3: their uses of "water" differ in extension.
step: From P1 and P3, by modus tollens, the words differ in meaning (C1).
step: Since the speakers have the same narrow state (P2) and their words differ in meaning (C1), narrow state does not fix meaning (C2).
step: Taken together, the assumption that narrow state fixes meaning, P1, P2 and P3 cannot all be true.
answer: The assumption that meaning is fixed by narrow psychological state is inconsistent with P1, P2 and P3, so Putnam rejects it.

@example
title: Using the argument on a new case
problem: Suppose that on one planet the tall green trees called "elms" are one species, while on a duplicate planet, the corresponding trees are a different species, though no one can tell them apart. A speaker on each planet says "elm". Apply Putnam's reasoning to say what an externalist concludes.
step: Take the two speakers to be duplicates in narrow psychological state, with no botanical knowledge.
step: The extension of the term on the first planet is the first species, and on the second the second species.
step: By the premise that same meaning means same extension, the two words differ in meaning.
step: Since the speakers' narrow states are alike, meaning is not fixed by narrow state: the externalist conclusion.
answer: The words differ in extension and so in meaning, though the speakers are alike in narrow state.

@mistakes
- Saying that Putnam concludes that meaning is entirely outside the head. He concludes that meaning is not determined by the speaker's narrow psychological state alone.
- Forgetting the setting in 1750. It is chosen so that no speaker has knowledge of chemistry that would differentiate the two.
- Confusing natural-kind externalism (Putnam) with social externalism (Burge). They concern different features of the environment.
- Treating the Twin Earth argument as proving externalism. It is an argument, and its premises, especially P3, can be disputed.
