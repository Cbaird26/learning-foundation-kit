@article
"The present King of France is bald." France has no king. Is the sentence true? If we say false, then "The present King of France is not bald" seems to be true, and yet there is no king to have hair of any kind. If we say it is neither true nor false, we have given up the principle that every statement is true or false. And if we say that the sentence is about something, then the thing it is about must be somewhere, as a king who does not exist. Bertrand Russell's paper "On Denoting" (1905) answers by rejecting the assumption that "the present King of France" is a name for anything.

### Russell's analysis

A **definite description** is a phrase of the form "the F". Russell's claim is that "The F is G" is not a statement of the simple subject-predicate form with "the F" as subject. It says three things at once:

1. There is at least one F.
2. There is at most one F.
3. Whatever is F is G.

In logical notation, with $Fx$ for "x is F" and $Gx$ for "x is G":

$$\exists x\,\bigl(Fx \wedge \forall y\,(Fy \to y = x) \wedge Gx\bigr)$$

The first conjunct says that something is F, the middle says that anything F is identical with it (uniqueness), and the last says that it is G. Russell called descriptions **incomplete symbols**: they have no meaning in isolation, but each sentence containing one has a meaning that the analysis spells out. The phrase is a **quantifier phrase**, not a referring expression. He extended the view in later work: ordinary proper names such as "Romulus" are, he held, abbreviated descriptions, and only expressions that refer to things with which we are directly acquainted, which he called logically proper names, refer without description.

### Scope

A description interacts with negation, and the order matters. "The King of France is not bald" is ambiguous:

- **Narrow scope for the description** (negation outside, so negation has wide scope): $\neg\exists x\,(Kx \wedge \forall y\,(Ky \to y = x) \wedge Bx)$. This says that it is not the case that there is a unique king who is bald. It is true if there is no king.
- **Wide scope for the description** (description outside, so negation has narrow scope): $\exists x\,(Kx \wedge \forall y\,(Ky \to y = x) \wedge \neg Bx)$. This says there is a unique king and he is not bald. It is false if there is no king.

With no king in France, "The King of France is bald" is false, the narrow reading of its negation is true, and the wide reading is false. Excluded middle is respected: of "the King is bald" and its narrow-scope negation, one is true and the other false.

### Negative existentials

"The golden mountain does not exist" seems to say of something that it lacks existence, which suggests there is something, the golden mountain, that fails to exist. On Russell's analysis the sentence is $\neg\exists x\,(Gx \wedge \forall y\,(Gy \to y = x))$, where $Gx$ is "x is golden and a mountain": it denies that there is exactly one golden mountain. No object is mentioned, so no nonexistent object is needed, which was the aim against the view of Alexius Meinong that there are objects that do not exist. Combined with the view that ordinary names are disguised descriptions, the theory also analyzes "Vulcan does not exist" as the denial that one thing satisfies the associated description.

### An argument that descriptions are not names

| P1 | If a phrase like "the golden mountain" were a name whose meaning is its bearer, then a sentence containing it would lack a meaning when the phrase has no bearer. |
| P2 | "The golden mountain does not exist" contains "the golden mountain", which has no bearer, and the sentence is meaningful (and true). |
| C | So "the golden mountain" is not a name whose meaning is its bearer. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). Its soundness depends on P2. A Meinongian denies that the phrase lacks a bearer, since there is a nonexistent golden mountain. Frege-style theorists accept that the sentence is meaningful because the phrase has a sense.

### Objections

Strawson argued that the analysis gets the data wrong: when there is no king, "The King of France is bald" is not false but fails to make a statement at all (next week). Another worry is that the theory replaces the grammatical form with a logical one; defenders reply that logical form need not mirror grammar.

@example
title: Two readings of a negation
problem: Greenvale has two towers of exactly the same greatest height, both red. Let $Tx$ be "x is a tallest tower in Greenvale" and $Rx$ "x is red". What are the truth-values, on Russell's account, of (a) "The tallest tower in Greenvale is red" and (b) "The tallest tower in Greenvale is not red", read first with the description taking narrow scope (negation outside) and then with the description taking wide scope (negation inside)?
step: Russell's analysis includes uniqueness. Since two towers satisfy "tallest tower", the uniqueness clause fails.
step: (a) is $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge Rx)$, false because the middle conjunct is false.
step: (b) narrow: $\neg\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge Rx)$ is the negation of (a), so true.
step: (b) wide: $\exists x\,(Tx \wedge \forall y\,(Ty \to y = x) \wedge \neg Rx)$ contains the same failed uniqueness clause, so false.
answer: (a) false; (b) true on the narrow reading, false on the wide reading.

@example
title: A negative existential
problem: Analyze "The cave under Brook Hill does not exist" on Russell's theory and say why it does not commit us to a nonexistent cave.
step: Let $Cx$ be "x is a cave under Brook Hill".
step: "There is a cave under Brook Hill" in Russell's form is $\exists x\,(Cx \wedge \forall y\,(Cy \to y = x))$.
step: The sentence denies this: $\neg\exists x\,(Cx \wedge \forall y\,(Cy \to y = x))$.
step: This is a general statement about what exists: it says that nothing is the unique cave under Brook Hill. It names no object, so no nonexistent object is needed.
answer: $\neg\exists x\,(Cx \wedge \forall y\,(Cy \to y = x))$; the sentence quantifies over actual things and mentions no cave.

@mistakes
- Dropping the uniqueness clause, which gives "some F is G" instead of "the F is G".
- Forgetting that negation can take wide or narrow scope: the two readings can differ in truth-value.
- Saying that Russell's theory makes "The King of France is bald" meaningless. On the theory it is meaningful and false.
- Saying that Russell held every name to be a disguised description. He held this of ordinary names such as "Romulus", in his later discussions, and excepted logically proper names.

prompt: In each of five cases, F is the set of things that are F and G the set of things that are G. Case 1: F = {1}, G = {1, 2}. Case 2: F = {2, 3}, G = {2, 3}. Case 3: F = {}, G = {1}. Case 4: F = {4}, G = {1, 2, 3}. Case 5: F = {5}, G = {5}. In how many cases is "The F is G" true on Russell's analysis?
answer: 2
unit: cases
check: sum(1 for F, G in [({1}, {1, 2}), ({2, 3}, {2, 3}), (set(), {1}), ({4}, {1, 2, 3}), ({5}, {5})] if len(F) == 1 and F <= G)
hint: The sentence needs exactly one F, and that F must be in G.

@practice self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain how Russell's theory of descriptions lets us say truly that "The golden mountain does not exist" without there being a golden mountain for the sentence to be about.
hint: Write the sentence in logical form and ask what, if anything, the formula names.
solution: Russell analyzes "the golden mountain" as a quantifier phrase and not as a name. The sentence becomes $\neg\exists x\,(Gx \wedge \forall y\,(Gy \to y = x))$, which says that it is not the case that exactly one thing is a golden mountain. The formula contains no name for a golden mountain, so it is true when nothing satisfies the condition, and its truth does not require an object for the sentence to be about. This removes the need for a Meinongian realm of nonexistent objects. A cost is that the account makes the grammatical subject of the sentence disappear in the analysis.

rubric: Says that the description is not a name and that the formula names nothing.

prompt: On Russell's view, what is the logical status of a definite description such as "the author of the novel"?
choice*: A quantifier phrase that is an incomplete symbol, with no meaning of its own in isolation
choice: A name of an object, whose meaning is that object
choice: A predicate with the same logical form as "is tall"
choice: A name of a nonexistent object when no one wrote the novel
hint: Recall what Russell does with the phrase when he analyzes the whole sentence.
solution: For Russell, a description is an incomplete symbol: its contribution is given by the analysis of whole sentences containing it, in terms of quantifiers. It is not a name, and it does not name nonexistent objects; that last idea is the Meinongian view Russell rejected.

prompt: The domain is {1, 2, 3, 4} and G is true of 1, 2 and 3. For how many of the 16 subsets of the domain, taken as the extension of F, is "The F is G" true on Russell's analysis?
answer: 3

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, with the sentence "The ferry is not late" in a town where there is no ferry, the difference between narrow-scope and wide-scope negation, and give the truth-value of each reading.
hint: One reading denies that there is a unique ferry that is late; the other says there is a unique ferry that is not late.
solution: Narrow scope, with negation outside: it is not the case that exactly one ferry exists and is late. This is true, since no ferry exists. Wide scope, with the description outside: exactly one ferry exists and it is not late. This is false, since there is no ferry. The ambiguity arises because the description can take scope over negation or under it. So, on Russell's theory, the English sentence can be heard as true or false depending on the reading.
