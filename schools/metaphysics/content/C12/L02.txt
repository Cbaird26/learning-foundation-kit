@article
"The Morning Star is the Morning Star" tells you nothing. "The Morning Star is the Evening Star" was an astronomical discovery. Yet both sentences are true, and both say, of one planet, that it is identical with itself. If the meaning of "the Evening Star" were simply the planet it stands for, the two sentences would say the same thing in the same way, and it would be a mystery that one is trivial and the other is news. This is **Frege's puzzle**, which opens Gottlob Frege's 1892 paper "On Sense and Reference."

### Frege's solution

Frege distinguishes two things an expression may have.

- **Reference** (*Bedeutung*): the object (for a name), the concept (for a predicate) or the truth-value (for a sentence) the expression stands for.
- **Sense** (*Sinn*): the **mode of presentation** of the reference, the way the referent is given. The sense of "the Evening Star" presents Venus as the bright body seen in the evening sky; the sense of "the Morning Star" presents it as the bright body seen before dawn. The two expressions share their reference and differ in sense.

Sense determines reference: given the sense, there is at most one thing that fits it. But the same thing can be presented in different ways. This is why an identity statement can be informative. Its informativeness lies in the difference of sense. Sense is objective and shareable, not a private mental image: two people can grasp the same sense, while the ideas they associate with a word may differ. Frege's own example is the point at which the three medians of a triangle meet, which can be given as the intersection of two of them or of another pair.

For a sentence, Frege holds that its sense is a **thought** (a proposition, as we would say), and its reference is its **truth-value**. This fits a principle of compositionality for reference: replacing a part of a sentence by an expression with the same reference leaves the sentence's reference, its truth-value, unchanged.

### The argument for sense

The reasoning for the distinction can be set out like this:

| P1 | The cognitive value of a sentence (what it tells us) is determined by the meanings of its parts. |
| P2 | If the meaning of a name were just its referent, then two names with the same referent would have the same meaning. |
| P3 | The Morning Star and the Evening Star are the same planet. |
| P4 | "The Morning Star is the Evening Star" differs in cognitive value from "The Morning Star is the Morning Star." |
| C | So the meaning of a name is not just its referent. |

The argument is valid. Suppose the meaning of a name were just its referent. By P3 and P2 the two names would have the same meaning. The two sentences differ only in which of the two names occurs in the second place, so by P1 they would have the same cognitive value, contradicting P4. Premise P1 is the hidden premise that does the work. A Millian attacks P1: the difference between the two sentences, the Millian says, lies in how a speaker thinks of the planet and not in the meanings of the names, so the sentences say the same thing and differ only in the way they are grasped or in what is conveyed. Frege's own reply is that this makes the difference in cognitive value unexplained.

### Indirect contexts and empty names

Substitution of co-referring expressions preserves truth-value in ordinary contexts. It can fail in **indirect contexts**, such as "Ben believes that the Morning Star is visible at dawn." Ben may not know that the two are one body. Frege's account: inside "believes that", an expression refers not to its usual referent but to its **customary sense**. Different senses are then in play, so substitution is not expected to preserve truth.

A name can have a sense without a referent. Frege held that a sentence containing such a name has a sense but no truth-value, because its parts lack reference. He also allowed that the senses of ordinary names may differ between speakers, and he suggested that a name's sense can be given by a description, so that "Aristotle" may be associated with a description such as "the pupil of Plato and teacher of Alexander." This suggestion, rather than any explicit theory of names, is the origin of **descriptivism** about names.

@example
title: Informative and trivial identities with new names
problem: Mara has three sisters. The oldest is Ines. Say why "Mara's oldest sister is Ines" can be informative while "Ines is Ines" cannot, using sense and reference, and say what Frege says about the reference of each sentence.
step: "Mara's oldest sister" and "Ines" refer to the same woman. But the sense of the first is given by a condition (being the oldest sister of Mara), while the sense of the second is a different way of being given the same person.
step: A hearer who does not know that Ines is Mara's oldest sister grasps both senses but does not know that they present the same woman. Learning the identity extends her knowledge.
step: "Ines is Ines" presents the same sense on both sides, so it can be known without any such discovery.
step: On Frege's view both sentences refer to the True, since each has a referent for every part and is true. Their senses, the thoughts they express, differ.
answer: The two names share a referent and differ in sense; the informativeness lies in the difference of sense; both sentences have the True as their reference.

@example
title: A failure of substitution
problem: Lena knows that "Corin Hale" wrote the novel Salt Road. She does not know that Corin Hale is also the person who writes under the name "R. Vane." Is it valid to infer "Lena believes that R. Vane wrote Salt Road" from "Lena believes that Corin Hale wrote Salt Road" and "Corin Hale is R. Vane"? Explain in Frege's terms.
step: The two names have the same referent, so substitution in an ordinary sentence ("Corin Hale was born in spring") would preserve truth-value.
step: Inside "Lena believes that ...", Frege says each name refers to its customary sense. The two names have different senses.
step: So the premises do not guarantee that Lena believes the thought expressed with "R. Vane." The inference is invalid.
answer: The inference is invalid: in belief contexts the names refer to their senses, which differ.

@mistakes
- Identifying Frege's sense with a private mental image or idea. Senses are objective, and ideas are not.
- Saying that for Frege a sentence refers to a fact. Its reference is its truth-value, and its sense is a thought.
- Thinking that sense and reference are two kinds of referent. Sense is the mode of presentation of the referent.
- Concluding that a name without a referent is meaningless for Frege. It has a sense but no reference, and the sentence containing it lacks a truth-value.

prompt: Explain Frege's puzzle with two examples of your own, neither of which is the Morning Star and the Evening Star or the medians of a triangle. For each, say which expressions share a reference, how their senses differ, and why the identity sentence is informative.

Both refer to one mountain, but the first gives it through a condition (the highest point above sea level) and the second through its name. Someone who has heard of the tallest mountain and of Everest may not know they are one, so "Everest is the tallest mountain on Earth" is informative, while "Everest is Everest" is not. Example 2: "the number of days in a leap year" and "366". In each case, Frege's explanation is that the senses differ though the reference is shared, and the informativeness of the identity lies in the difference of sense.
