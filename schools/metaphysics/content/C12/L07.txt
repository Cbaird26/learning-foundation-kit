@article
Most of us know very little about many people we can name. You may know only that Feynman was a physicist, and that is not enough to single him out from other physicists, yet when you say "Feynman" you speak of him. If descriptions in the speaker's head fixed reference, this would be a mystery. Saul Kripke's alternative is a **causal-historical picture** of reference.

### The picture for names

On this picture, a name is introduced at an **initial baptism**: someone points to an object, or fixes the reference by a description ("let 'Zed' name the planet that causes the irregularities"). The name then passes from speaker to speaker in a **chain of communication**. A speaker who hears the name from another and intends to use it with the reference it had for the one from whom she learned it becomes a link in the chain. Her use refers to the object at the start of the chain, whatever she believes about it, even if all her beliefs about it are false. This explains the Gödel–Schmidt intuition of last week: the name goes with the chain and not with the description.

The picture also fits the necessary a posteriori and rigid designation. The name designates the object at the start of the chain in every world in which it exists.

### Objections to the picture for names

Gareth Evans's well-known objection concerns reference change. "Madagascar" was apparently once used for part of the African mainland, and, through a misunderstanding, came to be used for the island. If so, the causal chain leads back to the mainland, but the name now refers to the island. So the chain cannot be the whole story: Evans proposed that the present use of a name, and the dominant source of the information associated with it, matters too. A second problem is the **qua problem**: at a baptism a speaker points at something that is at once an animal, a dog, a puppy and a collection of cells, and the baptism must be directed at the right one. A third is the status of **empty names** and names of fictional characters, which have no object to start a chain.

### An argument from ignorance

| P1 | A speaker who associates with the name "Feynman" only the description "a physicist" succeeds in referring to Feynman. |
| P2 | If the description theory were true, a speaker would refer to an object by a name only if the speaker associated with it a description that the object uniquely satisfies. |
| P3 | The description "a physicist" is not uniquely satisfied by Feynman. |
| C | So the description theory is false. |

The argument is valid. Premise P2 is what descriptivists dispute: the cluster theory, or a view on which reference may be fixed by what the community associates with the name, relaxes the requirement of a unique description in the individual speaker's head. A causal theorist replies that the community's descriptions fall prey to the same objection, and appeals to deference to those from whom the name was learned.

### Natural kind terms

Kripke and Hilary Putnam extended the picture to terms for natural kinds: "gold", "water", "tiger". The reference of such a term is fixed by **paradigm samples** together with a relation of being the same kind (Putnam called it "same liquid" for water): "gold" applies to whatever is of the same kind as the stuff in the samples. What it is to be of that kind is a matter for science to discover: atomic number 79 in the case of gold, H$_2$O (in the main) for water. Because the essence is discovered by investigation, "Gold has atomic number 79" is, on this view, necessary and a posteriori.

| P1 | If "gold" were synonymous with a description in terms of observable properties (yellow, heavy, malleable), then anything satisfying the description would be gold and nothing else would. |
| P2 | Some substances, such as iron pyrites, satisfy a description in terms of observable properties without being gold. |
| C | So "gold" is not synonymous with such a description. |

The argument is valid. A descriptivist replies that the relevant description may be more refined: gold is whatever has the chemical makeup scientists specify. Kripke's and Putnam's reply is that this is not what ordinary speakers, who know no chemistry, associate with the word, and that they could be wrong about the properties without losing reference. There is also the difficulty that some terms do not name a single microstructural kind, which is why jade, which is either of two different minerals, is a standard test case.

@example
title: Following a chain of reference
problem: The name "Kestrel Ridge" was introduced by a surveyor, Ada, who pointed at a hill. She told Ben, who told Cal, who told Dee. Dee has false beliefs about the hill: she thinks it is in the north, when it is in the south, and that it is a lake. Does Dee's use of "Kestrel Ridge" refer to the hill? Say what the causal-historical picture says and what a pure descriptivist would say.
step: The chain runs from the baptism (Ada) through Ben and Cal to Dee. Each intends to use the name as the one before did.
step: On the causal picture, Dee's use refers to the hill, since her use is linked to the baptism, whatever her beliefs.
step: A pure descriptivist would say that the name refers to whatever satisfies Dee's description: a northern lake. If there is none, the name would fail to refer, or it would refer to a different thing.
step: The causal picture handles ignorance and error better, but Evans's Madagascar case warns that a chain may not always settle the reference.
answer: On the causal picture it refers to the hill; a pure descriptivist predicts failure or a different referent.

@example
title: Gold and pyrite
problem: A prospector describes "gold" as "the shiny yellow metal that is heavy and does not tarnish". Why do Kripke and Putnam deny that this description gives the meaning of "gold"?
step: Suppose the description gave the meaning. Then any shiny yellow heavy untarnishing metal would be gold.
step: Iron pyrites resembles gold on the surface and is not gold, and a test shows it has a different internal composition.
step: So the description does not match the extension of "gold" as speakers use the word. Speakers treat their paradigm samples as instances of a kind with a hidden nature.
step: The word applies to whatever shares that nature, which science may reveal to be atomic number 79.
answer: Because anything fitting the surface description would count as gold, while speakers treat pyrite as a counterfeit and not as gold.

@mistakes
- Thinking that the causal-historical picture says that speakers must know the history of a name. They need only be link in a chain with the right intentions.
- Saying that causal theorists hold that reference can never change. Evans's example is that it can, and that any causal theory has to allow for this.
- Treating "water is H$_2$O" as true by definition. On the view, it is a discovery, and the meaning of "water" is not given by the formula.
- Taking the natural kind account to imply that every kind term has a simple chemical essence. Terms such as "jade" and "tiger" show that the cases are more complicated.

@quiz self
prompt: Jade is a name for a material that is in fact either of two chemically different minerals, jadeite or nephrite. In four or five sentences, say what this case shows about the claim that natural kind terms name a single kind with a hidden essence, and give a reply the defender of the claim might make.
hint: Ask whether one chemical essence could underlie the word "jade", and what that implies for a theory that looks for one.
solution: The case shows that a term used for centuries by speakers who could not tell the two minerals apart may not correspond to a single microstructural kind, so the claim that every natural kind term has one hidden essence is too simple. A defender can reply in two ways. She can say that "jade" is not a strict natural kind term, but a term for a class of materials grouped by appearance and use, so it falls outside the thesis. Or she can say that the term names a broader kind, covering both minerals, as a category such as a family of minerals. Either reply concedes that the picture applies only to terms for kinds with a unified underlying nature.
