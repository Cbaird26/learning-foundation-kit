@article
You are in a meeting and someone says, "The head of the department is on leave." There is no single head of the department: the post is vacant. Did the speaker say something false? Russell says yes: the existence clause fails, so the sentence is false. But most people hesitate to call it false in the way "The head of the department is a fish" would be. It seems as though the speaker made a mistake of another kind, as if the question whether what was said is true or false never arose. P. F. Strawson's paper "On Referring" (1950) develops this reaction into an objection.

### Strawson's objection

Strawson distinguishes a **sentence**, the type; a **use** of it on an occasion; and the **statement** made. "The king of France is wise" is a meaningful sentence. Whether it makes a true or false statement depends on who uses it and when: said under Louis XIV it was about a particular man. **Referring** is something a speaker does with an expression on an occasion, and an expression does not refer by itself. When someone uses the sentence and there is no king, the use does not refer to anyone, and the question of truth or falsity does not arise.

Strawson's term for the relation is **presupposition**: a statement S presupposes S′ when the truth of S′ is a precondition of S's being either true or false. The tell-tale mark is that presuppositions survive negation. "The king of France is wise" and "The king of France is not wise" both presuppose that there is a king; and both lack a truth-value if there is none. By contrast an entailment does not survive negation: "The king is wise" entails that someone is wise, but "The king is not wise" does not.

The argument against Russell can be set out as follows.

| P1 | If Russell's analysis is correct, then "The king of France is wise," said when there is no king, makes a false statement. |
| P2 | When there is no king, a use of "The king of France is wise" does not make a false statement. |
| C | So Russell's analysis is not correct. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens). A Russellian rejects P2: the use is false, and what seems odd about it is explained pragmatically, since the speaker conveys that there is a king, which is misleading, but misleading is not the same as lacking a truth-value. Russell himself replied to Strawson in a paper of 1957, defending his view that ordinary language is a poor guide to logical form for the purposes of the theory.

### Attributive and referential uses

Keith Donnellan, in "Reference and Definite Descriptions" (1966), argued that definite descriptions have two uses. In an **attributive** use the speaker says something about whoever or whatever fits the description, as a detective does who finds a brutal crime and says "The person who took the silver is left-handed" without any idea who it is. In a **referential** use the speaker has a particular individual in mind and uses the description as a way to pick that individual out for the audience. At a party you may say "The woman in the green coat is a pianist," meaning a particular woman, and if her coat is in fact blue, you may still have said something true of her. Donnellan thought this shows that Russell's analysis, with its uniqueness and existence clauses, does not capture referential uses, and that Strawson was also wrong to say that the description must be satisfied for the speaker to say something about someone.

| P1 | If Russell's analysis gives what is said by every use of "the F is G", then what is said is true only if exactly one thing is F and it is G. |
| P2 | In a referential use, the speaker can say something true even when no unique F is G, provided the individual meant is G. |
| C | So Russell's analysis does not give what is said by every use of a description. |

The argument is valid. Saul Kripke's reply, in "Speaker's Reference and Semantic Reference" (1977), attacks P2: what the speaker meant, or **speaker's reference**, differs from the **semantic reference** of the expression in the language. In the blue coat case the speaker meant something true about the woman, but the sentence she uttered, on Russell's analysis, was false, and the phenomenon is pragmatic. Donnellan's distinction, in this view, is not a semantic ambiguity but a difference between what the sentence says and what the speaker means by it.

@example
title: Strawson's negation test
problem: Does "The curator of the Orlo museum has retired" presuppose that Orlo's museum has a curator, in Strawson's sense, or does it only entail it? Use the negation test.
step: Negate the sentence: "The curator of the Orlo museum has not retired."
step: Ask what the negated sentence requires. A hearer who learns that the museum has no curator would not take the negation to be true; she would reject the question.
step: Since the thought that there is a curator survives negation, it is a presupposition, not merely an entailment.
step: An entailment such as "someone has retired" fails the test: the negation "the curator has not retired" does not carry it.
answer: It presupposes it: the existence of the curator survives negation, which an entailment would not.

@example
title: Attributive or referential?
problem: A teacher sees a note pinned to the notice board that reads "Your essay is the best in the class" and says to a colleague: "Whoever wrote this note likes my teaching." Another teacher, pointing at a student she watched pin the note, says: "The student with the red scarf wrote this note." Classify the two descriptions.
step: In the first case the teacher does not know who wrote the note. She says something about whoever fits "whoever wrote this note".
step: This is an attributive use: any individual who satisfied the description would make the statement true.
step: In the second case the second teacher has a particular student in mind, and the description helps the audience to identify her.
step: This is a referential use. If the scarf were in fact pink, the speaker might still have said something true of that student.
answer: The first is attributive; the second is referential.

@mistakes
- Saying that Strawson held that "The king of France is wise" is false when there is no king. His view is that the question of truth does not arise.
- Confusing presupposition with entailment: a presupposition survives negation, while an entailment does not.
- Taking Donnellan's distinction to be Kripke's own view. Kripke argues that the distinction is pragmatic, in terms of speaker's reference and semantic reference.
- Treating the attributive-referential distinction as a distinction between two kinds of expressions. It is a distinction between uses of the same description.

@quiz num
prompt: Six occasions of the sentence "The F is G" are as follows. Occasions 1, 4 and 6: there is no F. Occasions 2 and 5: exactly one thing is F, and it is G. Occasion 3: exactly one thing is F, and it is not G. On how many occasions does Russell's analysis count the statement as false, while Strawson's view counts it as neither true nor false?
answer: 3
unit: occasions
check: sum(1 for n, g in [(0, None), (1, True), (1, False), (0, None), (1, True), (0, None)] if n == 0)
hint: Find the occasions where there is no F, and ask how each theory classifies them.
solution: Russell makes every occasion without an F false, so it counts occasions 1, 4 and 6 as false. Strawson holds that these three occasions fail a presupposition, so they are neither true nor false. For the occasions with exactly one F, both theories agree: occasions 2 and 5 are true and occasion 3 is false. So the disagreement is over the 3 occasions with no F.
