@article
A guest at a dinner says "It is cold in here." Taken as a statement about the temperature, it is true or false. But the host will probably get up and close the window. The guest has done more than describe: she has made a request. And if a friend asks "Did you enjoy the meal?" and you reply "The bread was excellent", you have said something about the bread and conveyed something about the rest. Truth-conditional semantics tells us about what sentences say; **pragmatics** is the study of what speakers do and convey with them.

### Austin and speech acts

J. L. Austin, in *How to Do Things with Words* (lectures given in 1955, published in 1962), began by noting that some utterances, **performatives**, do not describe but do something: "I promise to return the book", "I name this boat the Heron." They are not true or false but **happy** or **unhappy** (felicitous or infelicitous), depending on whether conditions are met: the right person, the right circumstances, sincere intentions, and the following through of the procedure. Austin came to see that all utterances are acts, and distinguished three levels:

- the **locutionary act**: uttering words with a certain sense and reference ("It is cold in here");
- the **illocutionary act**: what is done in saying it, such as asserting, requesting, warning or promising, which has a conventional **force**;
- the **perlocutionary act**: the effect achieved by saying it, such as persuading, alarming or getting someone to close the window.

The same sentence can be used with different illocutionary forces. "I will be there at eight" can be a promise, a prediction or a warning, depending on context.

John Searle's classification of illocutionary acts (1975) has five categories: **assertives** (committing the speaker to the truth of a proposition), **directives** (trying to get the hearer to act), **commissives** (committing the speaker to a future act), **expressives** (expressing an attitude, as in thanking or apologizing) and **declarations** (bringing about a state of affairs by the utterance itself, as in naming or pronouncing). Searle also discussed **indirect speech acts**: "Can you pass the salt?" is in form a question about ability and in force a request.

### Grice and implicature

H. P. Grice's "Logic and Conversation" (1975) starts from the observation that conversation is a cooperative activity. Participants are assumed to follow a **cooperative principle**: make your contribution as is required by the purpose of the exchange. Grice sorted this into four categories of **maxims**: Quantity (be as informative as required, and no more), Quality (try to say only what you believe true and have evidence for), Relation (be relevant) and Manner (be clear, brief and orderly). A **conversational implicature** is something a speaker conveys by saying what she says, derived by the hearer from the assumption that she is cooperating. A speaker can observe the maxims, or **flout** one openly so that the hearer will look for an implicature. Irony flouts Quality: "What lovely weather!" said in a downpour.

The standard example is **scalar implicature**. "Some of the students passed" suggests that not all of them did.

| P1 | The speaker said "Some of the students passed" and not "All of the students passed." |
| P2 | The speaker is observing the maxim of Quantity. |
| P3 | If the speaker knew that all of the students passed, and is observing Quantity, she would have said "All of the students passed." |
| P4 | The speaker knows whether all of the students passed. |
| C | So the speaker believes that not all of the students passed. |

The argument is valid. From P1, P2 and P3, the speaker does not know that all passed (otherwise she would have said so). By P4, she knows whether all passed, and so she knows that not all did, and therefore believes it. Each premise can be attacked. If the speaker is not well informed, P4 fails, and the hearer can conclude only that she does not know that all passed. If she is not cooperating, or has a reason to be reticent, P2 fails.

### Implicature, entailment and presupposition

A conversational implicature is not an **entailment**. "Some passed" does not entail "not all passed": the speaker can add "In fact, all of them did" without contradiction. Grice's tests: implicatures are **cancellable** (they can be explicitly denied without contradiction), **calculable** (the hearer can derive them from what is said and the maxims) and **non-detachable** (saying the same thing in other words keeps them, unless the manner of expression is what generates them). An entailment cannot be cancelled: "Some passed, in fact none did" is contradictory. A presupposition, as week 4 showed, survives negation, while an implicature of this kind does not. Neo-Griceans and relevance theorists, such as Sperber and Wilson, disagree about how much of what is conveyed is derived in this way and how much is part of what is said.

@example
title: Deriving an implicature
problem: At a meeting, Dana asks "Is the new report finished?" and Eli answers "Part of it is." Derive what Eli implicates and say how you could cancel it.
step: Eli said "Part of it is" and did not say "All of it is."
step: Assuming that Eli is cooperative and observing Quantity, if he knew that all of it was finished, he would have said so.
step: Assuming that he knows the state of the report, he must know that it is not all finished. So he implicates that the report is not entirely finished.
step: The implicature is cancellable: "Part of it is, and in fact all of it is" is not a contradiction. So it is not an entailment.
answer: Eli implicates that the report is not entirely finished; the implicature is cancellable, so it is not an entailment.

@example
title: Three levels of act
problem: A lifeguard shouts "The current is strong here!" and swimmers leave the water. Identify the locutionary, illocutionary and perlocutionary acts, and name the Searle category of the illocutionary act on the most natural reading.
step: The locutionary act is uttering the sentence "The current is strong here" with its sense and reference to the place.
step: The illocutionary act is a warning, performed in saying it. Warnings are classified as directives or assertives, depending on the analysis; the most natural reading is that she is trying to get the swimmers to leave, which is a directive.
step: The perlocutionary act is alarming or persuading the swimmers, with the effect that they left the water.
step: The perlocutionary effect may fail while the other acts succeed: the swimmers may ignore the warning.
answer: Locutionary: uttering the sentence; illocutionary: warning (a directive on the natural reading); perlocutionary: causing the swimmers to leave.

@mistakes
- Confusing illocutionary acts with perlocutionary acts. Warning is done in saying; alarming someone is an effect of saying.
- Treating an implicature as an entailment. An implicature can be cancelled without contradiction.
- Saying that performatives are false. Performatives are neither true nor false but happy or unhappy.
- Treating Grice's maxims as rules that speakers always follow. The point is that hearers assume the cooperative principle and so derive implicatures when it seems to be flouted.
