@article
You can understand a sentence you have never heard before: "The ferry across Lake Orlo carries seventeen bagpipers on Tuesdays." No one taught you this sentence, and probably no one has said it. You understand it because you know the meanings of its words and how they combine. How is this possible, and what must a theory of meaning look like to explain it?

### Compositionality

The principle of **compositionality** says that the meaning of a complex expression is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. A theory built on it assigns meanings to the finitely many words, and finitely many rules of combination then yield meanings for the infinitely many sentences. Frege's **context principle**, in the Introduction to *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884), says that one should not ask for the meaning of a word in isolation but only in the context of a sentence. Many philosophers read this as a complement to compositionality: words have meanings by contributing to the truth-conditions of the sentences they occur in.

### Truth-conditional semantics

In a **truth-conditional semantics**, the meaning of a sentence is given by its truth-conditions, and the truth-conditions of a complex sentence are computed from the semantic values of its parts. A toy case: in a model with a domain of individuals, a name has an individual as its value, a one-place predicate has a set of individuals, and "$a$ is $F$" is true when the individual is in the set. A quantifier phrase such as "every poet" relates two sets: "Every poet is G" is true when the set of poets is a subset of the set of Gs. Richard Montague's work in the early 1970s showed that fragments of English can be treated this way with the methods of logic, and textbooks of formal semantics now use function application as the basic mode of combination.

Donald Davidson's 1967 paper "Truth and Meaning" proposed that a theory of meaning for a language is a finitely axiomatized, Tarski-style truth theory, which entails for every sentence of the language a T-theorem of the form "s is true if and only if p". Learning the language is then, he argued, the learning of finitely many axioms, from which the meaning of each of the infinitely many sentences can be derived.

### The argument for compositionality

| P1 | Competent speakers can understand indefinitely many sentences that they have never encountered. |
| P2 | Speakers have finite capacities: a finite memory and a finite stock of experience. |
| P3 | If speakers can understand indefinitely many new sentences with only finite capacities, then they do so by grasping a finite stock of word meanings and finitely many rules of combination, which determine the meanings of the sentences. |
| C | So the meaning of a sentence is determined by the meanings of its parts and the way they are combined. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 give the antecedent of P3, and the conclusion follows by modus ponens, with P3's consequent restated. The premise that does the work is P3, and it is the target of most objections. One objection says that speakers might understand new sentences by analogy, or by using context and background knowledge, and not by compositional rules. Defenders reply that analogy among sentences needs to track their structure. Another is that **idioms**, such as "kick the bucket", have meanings not determined by those of their parts; defenders reply that idioms can be stored as lexical items. A third, from context-sensitivity, is that the contribution of a word can vary with the occasion, which a compositional account must handle by making the value depend on a parameter of the context (week 12).

### Problems for the program

A first problem is that a T-theorem such as "'Snow is white' is true if and only if grass is green" is a true biconditional, since both sides are true, but it does not give the meaning of "Snow is white". J. A. Foster raised the difficulty, and Davidson's reply is to require that the theory as a whole be the best interpretation of speakers' behavior, so that its theorems work as laws. A second problem is that truth-conditions seem too coarse: "Seven is prime" and "Two plus two is four" are true in all the same possible worlds, yet they do not seem to mean the same. Responses include structured propositions, in which meanings keep track of the parts, and an appeal to what speakers do with sentences (a use-based approach). A third problem is belief reports, such as "Ben believes that the Morning Star is bright", where replacing a word with another of the same reference seems to change the truth-value (week 2).

@example
title: Composing the truth-value of a sentence
problem: A model has domain {1, 2, 3, 4}. The name "Ana" names 1. The set of poets is {1, 2, 3} and the relation "admires" holds of the pairs (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 3) and (4, 1). Is "Ana admires some poet" true? How about "Every poet admires some poet"?
step: The first sentence is true if there is a poet y with (1, y) in the admiring relation. The pair (1, 2) is in it, and 2 is a poet. So it is **true**.
step: For the second, check each poet x in {1, 2, 3} for a poet y with (x, y) in the relation.
step: Poet 1 admires 2, a poet. Poet 2 admires 3, a poet. Poet 3 admires 3, a poet. Each has a poet he or she admires.
step: The pair (4, 1) concerns a non-poet and does not matter for a sentence about poets. The second sentence is **true**.
answer: Both sentences are true in the model.

@example
title: A problem with truth-conditions
problem: Explain why the T-theorem "'Salt is soluble' is true if and only if grass is green" is a problem for the claim that a truth theory gives meanings, and how Davidson answers.
step: Both sides of the biconditional are true, so the biconditional is true.
step: But it does not give the meaning of "Salt is soluble", since the right side is about something else.
step: So the truth of the biconditional is not enough for it to serve as an interpretation.
step: Davidson requires that the whole theory be the best account of the speakers' behavior, so that the theorems have the status of empirical laws, not accidents, and that the axioms be finite and compositional. The deviant theorem would not be derivable from such a theory.
answer: The T-theorem is true but not meaning-giving; Davidson adds constraints on the theory as a whole.

@mistakes
- Treating compositionality as the claim that the meaning of a sentence is the sum or list of the meanings of its words. The way the parts are combined matters.
- Saying that Davidson identifies the meaning of a sentence with its truth-value. He uses the truth theory to derive the sentence's truth-conditions.
- Thinking that a true T-theorem always gives the meaning of the sentence mentioned. True but deviant biconditionals show that more is needed.
- Believing that idioms refute compositionality without argument. Defenders say that idioms can be treated as stored units, and the dispute is about how widespread such cases are.

@quiz num
prompt: A toy language has sentences of the form "NP admires NP" and "NP avoids NP". Each NP is one of the three names a, b, c. How many different sentences does it have?
answer: 18
unit: sentences
check: 3 * 2 * 3
hint: Choose the first name, the verb, and the second name independently.
solution: There are 3 choices of subject, 2 of verb and 3 of object, so $3 \times 2 \times 3 = 18$. Adding a rule that lets a sentence be embedded in a larger one would make the number infinite, and a compositional theory still needs only finitely many axioms.
