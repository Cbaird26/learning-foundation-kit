@article
Two students sit a true/false exam. Both write "True" beside the same question, and both are right. One remembers the lecture and the textbook; the other flipped a coin. Their answers are the same, their beliefs are both true, and yet only one of them seems to *know*. **Epistemology**, the theory of knowledge, begins by asking what the difference is.

### Three kinds of knowing

English uses "know" for several things. **Propositional knowledge** (knowledge that) takes a proposition as its object: you know that Lisbon is the capital of Portugal. **Ability knowledge** (knowing how) is a skill: you know how to ride a bicycle. **Knowledge by acquaintance** is familiarity with a person, place or thing: you know your neighbor. Epistemology's central question concerns the first kind, and philosophers disagree about whether knowing how reduces to it.

### The tripartite analysis

A philosophical **analysis** of a concept states conditions that are individually **necessary** and jointly **sufficient** for its application. The traditional analysis of propositional knowledge, the **justified true belief** (JTB) or **tripartite** analysis, says:

$$S \text{ knows that } p \iff (\text{T}) \; p \text{ is true}, \;\; (\text{B}) \; S \text{ believes that } p, \;\; (\text{J}) \; S \text{ is justified in believing that } p.$$

Plato's *Theaetetus* considers the proposal that knowledge is true belief together with an account (*logos*), and the *Meno* (97–98) says that true belief becomes knowledge when it is tied down by reasoning about the explanation; the dialogues are not simply stating the modern analysis, but they are the ancestors of it. In twentieth-century epistemology versions of the analysis were defended by A. J. Ayer and Roderick Chisholm.

Why these three conditions?

- **Truth.** Knowledge is **factive**: if you know that $p$, then $p$. You can believe falsehoods, but you cannot know them. We say "she thought she knew" of someone who was wrong.
- **Belief.** To know that $p$ you must take $p$ to be so. Someone who is agnostic about $p$ does not know $p$. Colin Radford argued that there are cases of knowing without believing (a nervous examinee who answers a history question correctly while insisting she is only guessing, though she has learned the date); most philosophers reply that she does believe it, perhaps weakly or unconsciously.
- **Justification.** The next argument explains why a third condition is wanted.

| P1 | Some true beliefs are not knowledge (a lucky guess, a coin-flip answer). |
| P2 | What separates knowledge from a lucky true belief is that the believer has good reason, evidence or some other positive epistemic standing for the belief. |
| C | So knowledge requires more than true belief: it requires justification. |

The argument is valid, and premise P1 is hard to deny. Premise P2 does the work, and it is where the disagreements begin. Is the positive standing a matter of reasons the believer can cite (**internalists** say yes), or of how the belief was produced, whether or not she can cite anything (**externalists**; see week 4)? And is "has good reasons" enough? Week 2 shows that it is not.

### How to test an analysis

The analysis is a biconditional, so it can fail in two ways. A counterexample to **necessity** is a case of knowledge that lacks one of the conditions. A counterexample to **sufficiency** is a case that meets all three conditions but is not knowledge. Philosophers test analyses by intuitions about cases, and this method is itself debated: if intuitions about a case differ, the case settles little.

Note also that justification, in this tradition, is **fallible**: you can be justified in believing something false. A justified belief based on strong evidence can still be mistaken, which is why truth must be a separate condition rather than something justification guarantees.

@example
title: Testing three cases against the analysis
problem: Apply the tripartite analysis to each case. (a) Ravi guesses that a jar holds 212 marbles, has no evidence, and the jar does hold 212. (b) Ilse reads a reliable timetable saying the 9:40 train leaves from platform 3, believes it, and the train does leave from platform 3. (c) Oren reads the same timetable and believes the same thing, but the platform was changed that morning and the train leaves from platform 5.
step: Check each condition for each case. In (a), $p$ is true and Ravi believes it, but he has no justification.
step: In (b), $p$ is true, Ilse believes it, and a reliable timetable justifies her belief: all three conditions hold, so the analysis says she knows.
step: In (c), Oren believes $p$ and is justified (the same timetable), but $p$ is false: the truth condition fails. Justified false belief is possible because justification is fallible.
answer: (a) no knowledge, since justification is missing; (b) knowledge; (c) no knowledge, since the belief is false.
@example
title: What would count against the analysis
problem: A critic wants to refute the claim that justification is necessary for knowledge, and another critic wants to refute the claim that the three conditions are jointly sufficient. What kind of case does each need?
step: Write the analysis as "$S$ knows $p$ iff T, B and J". Necessity of J means: if $S$ knows $p$, then J. Sufficiency of T, B and J means: if T, B and J, then $S$ knows $p$.
step: To refute "if $S$ knows $p$ then J" the first critic needs a case where $S$ knows $p$ yet is not justified. Externalists with a view such as simple reliabilism may count some cases this way, for example a reliable process that gives the believer no reasons she can cite.
step: To refute "if T, B and J then $S$ knows" the second critic needs a case with a true, justified belief that is not knowledge. This is the kind of case Edmund Gettier published in 1963, the subject of week 2.
answer: Necessity is refuted by knowledge without the condition; sufficiency is refuted by a case with all three conditions and no knowledge.
@mistakes
- Treating "justified" as "guaranteed true": on the tripartite analysis justification is fallible, so truth is a separate condition.
- Treating the analysis as a single claim: it has three necessary conditions and a sufficiency claim, and a case can threaten one without threatening the others.
- Thinking a lucky guess fails the belief condition: the guesser may believe her guess; what she lacks is justification.
- Saying that Plato simply defended the JTB analysis: the *Theaetetus* examines a proposal of this kind and ends without endorsing it.

prompt: Reconstruct in standard form the argument that knowledge requires more than true belief, name the premise that an externalist and an internalist would interpret differently, and say how.
