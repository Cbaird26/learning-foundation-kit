@article
Every morning so far, the sun has risen. Bertrand Russell's chicken is fed every morning by the farmer, and on that evidence expects to be fed tomorrow, until the day the farmer wrings its neck. The chicken's inference was a good one from the evidence it had, and it led to disaster. What, then, entitles *us* to expect the future to resemble the past?

### Deductive and inductive inference

In a **deductive** argument, if the premises are true the conclusion must be. In an **inductive** (or *ampliative*) argument the conclusion goes beyond the premises: "all observed ravens are black, so the next raven will be black." No deductive guarantee is offered, yet inductive reasoning underlies science and daily life.

### Hume's problem

David Hume (*Treatise* 1.3.6; *Enquiry* IV) asks what justifies reasoning from observed to unobserved cases. His argument can be set out as follows.

| P1 | Inductive inferences from the observed to the unobserved assume that nature is uniform: that unobserved cases will resemble observed ones. |
| P2 | The uniformity principle cannot be established by demonstrative reasoning, since its denial is conceivable and involves no contradiction. |
| P3 | The uniformity principle cannot be established by experience without circularity, since any argument from experience to the principle is itself an inductive argument that assumes it. |
| C | So the assumption behind induction cannot be given a non-circular rational justification. |

The argument is valid. Hume's own positive account is that inductive inference is driven by custom or habit and not by reason in the sense of demonstration. Responses accept the argument but reinterpret its significance, or reject a premise.

- **Reichenbach's vindication** (1938): we cannot show that induction will work, but if *any* method can discover the long-run frequency of a type of event, induction can, and so it is the best bet. Critics note that many other rules also converge in the limit and give different predictions in finite samples.
- **Strawson's dissolution** (1952): being reasonable *means*, in part, proportioning belief to inductive evidence; asking whether induction as a whole is justified is like asking whether the law is legal. Critics say this makes the question trivially answered rather than answering it.
- **Popper's** rejection of induction: science proceeds by conjecture and attempted refutation, not by induction; theories are not confirmed but only *corroborated* by surviving tests. Critics say that relying on corroborated theories for prediction is itself an inductive step.
- **Externalist** replies say that induction yields knowledge when it is reliable, even though we cannot show non-circularly that it is. A Bayesian reply is considered in week 11.

### Goodman's new riddle

Nelson Goodman, in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955), argued that the problem is worse. Define **grue**: an object is grue if it is examined before a time $t$ and is green, or is not examined before $t$ and is blue. Every emerald examined so far (before $t$) is green, and so every one is also grue. So the same evidence supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue". The second predicts that emeralds first examined after $t$ will be blue. The principle that the future will resemble the past does not choose between them, because they disagree about what resemblance is. Any reply must explain why *green* is **projectible** and *grue* is not. Goodman's own answer appeals to the *entrenchment* of predicates in past projection; others say that grue is positional, or that its properties are less natural.

### Inference to the best explanation

**Inference to the best explanation** (IBE, closely related to Peirce's *abduction*; the name is Gilbert Harman's, 1965) infers that a hypothesis is true because it would, if true, explain the evidence better than the available alternatives. Its schema:

| P1 | Evidence $E$ is observed. |
| P2 | Hypothesis $H$ would, if true, explain $E$ better than any available rival. |
| C | So $H$ is (probably) true. |

IBE is widespread: detectives, doctors and scientists reason this way. Peter Lipton, in *Inference to the Best Explanation* (1991), distinguishes the **likeliest** explanation from the **loveliest**: the one that would give the most understanding, and argues that loveliness is a guide to likeliness.

Two standard objections. First, **the bad lot**: Bas van Fraassen argued that the best of our available hypotheses might be the best of a bad lot, so being best does not make it likely; to infer truth we must already have reason to think the true hypothesis is among those we have considered. Second, **why should explanatory virtues track truth?** Simplicity, scope and unification are attractive, but showing they indicate truth seems to require an inductive argument that itself relies on IBE. Defenders say that IBE is compatible with Bayesian updating, with explanatory considerations affecting priors and likelihoods.

title: Hume's problem applied to a simple case
problem: A baker's loaves have risen in each of the last 300 baking days, so she expects tomorrow's loaf to rise. She defends the inference: "In the past, expecting loaves to rise has worked, so it will work tomorrow." What does Hume's argument say about this defense?
step: Her defense is itself an inference from past success of the method to future success. It assumes the future will resemble the past.
step: So it presupposes the uniformity principle that is the question, and by premise P3 it is circular.
step: Hume's conclusion is not that she should stop expecting the loaf to rise, but that her expectation cannot be shown by non-circular argument to be rational; he says it rests on custom.
answer: The defense is circular, which is just what premise P3 predicts.

title: Grue-style predicates
problem: Define "rellow": an object is rellow if it is examined before 2040 and is red, or is not examined before 2040 and is yellow. Poppies examined so far are all red. Why does this support "all poppies are rellow" as much as "all poppies are red", and what do the two hypotheses predict about a poppy first examined in 2041?
step: Every poppy examined so far was examined before 2040 and is red, so each satisfies the first clause of "rellow". The evidence fits both hypotheses equally.
step: "All poppies are red" predicts that a poppy first examined in 2041 will be red.
step: "All poppies are rellow" predicts that a poppy first examined in 2041 is not examined before 2040, so it must be yellow.
answer: The evidence fits both equally and they make incompatible predictions about the 2041 poppy: red versus yellow.
@mistakes
- Thinking that Hume denies that we reason inductively: he describes that we do and asks about justification.
- Saying that grue shows that the future will not resemble the past: the point is that "resemblance" is not defined independently of the predicate we choose.
- Treating IBE as the same as induction: it infers an explanation, not a generalization, though the two overlap.
- Saying that Popper solved the problem by showing that induction is justified: Popper denied that science uses induction.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in a paragraph why the grue predicate shows that the claim "inductive reasoning assumes that the future will resemble the past" does not by itself explain good induction.
hint: Show that two hypotheses fit the same evidence and make different predictions.
solution: The claim says induction assumes that unobserved cases will resemble observed ones, but "resemble" depends on which respects count. Every emerald examined before the time $t$ is both green and grue. If the future resembles the past in being green, new emeralds are green; if it resembles the past in being grue, new emeralds are blue. Both are projections of the same pattern, so a bare uniformity principle does not say which to make. We need an account of which predicates are projectible, which Goodman tried to supply with entrenchment.
