material: **Gettier problem: starting argument**

| P1 | If the tripartite analysis is correct, any justified true belief is knowledge. |
| P2 | In case X, the belief is justified, true and believed. |
| P3 | In case X, the believer does not know. |
| C | So the tripartite analysis is not correct. |

Responses to compare: no false lemmas, defeasibility, safety, sensitivity, knowledge first.
material: **Skepticism and closure: starting argument**

| P1 | (Closure) If I know $H$ and competently deduce $\neg$SH from $H$, then I know $\neg$SH. |
| P2 | $H$ entails $\neg$SH. |
| P3 | I do not know $\neg$SH. |
| C | So I do not know $H$. |

Responses to compare: Moore's rejection of P3, the denial of closure (Dretske, Nozick), contextualism.
material: **The a priori: starting argument**

| P1 | If a statement is known a priori, it is immune to revision in light of experience. |
| P2 | No statement is immune to revision in light of experience. |
| C | So no statement is known a priori. |

Responses to compare: the reply that a priori justification need not be indefeasible, and an empiricist or Quinean view of logic and mathematics.
material: **Peer disagreement: starting argument**

| P1 | You and your friend are epistemic peers on the question. |
| P2 | Your friend's disagreement is evidence that you have made an error, as strong as the evidence of your own conviction that she did. |
| P3 | Rationality requires that you respond to your total evidence. |
| P4 | If your total evidence makes it about as likely that you erred as that she did, then responding to it requires you to lower your confidence substantially. |
| C | So you should substantially lower your confidence. |

Responses to compare: the equal weight view, the total evidence view, permissivism.

@review
### Key ideas by week

1. **Analysis of knowledge.** Knowledge is analyzed as justified true belief: truth, belief and justification. The conditions are tested by cases; justification is fallible.
2. **Gettier.** Justified true belief that is true by luck is not knowledge. Responses: no false lemmas, defeasibility, causal theory, sensitivity, safety, knowledge first.
3. **Foundationalism and coherentism.** The regress argument leads to basic beliefs (foundationalism), holistic support (coherentism), endless chains (infinitism) or skepticism.
4. **Reliabilism, internalism and externalism.** Reliabilism faces the generality problem, the clairvoyant cases and the new evil demon problem; evidentialism is an internalist rival.
5. **Skepticism.** Skeptical hypotheses are incompatible with ordinary belief and leave the evidence unchanged; the underdetermination argument depends on a principle linking evidence and knowledge.
6. **Replies.** The closure argument; Moore rejects P3, Dretske and Nozick reject closure, contextualists make "know" context-sensitive.
7. **The a priori.** A priori, necessary and analytic are different distinctions; Kripke's cases; Quine's challenge; rational intuition.
8. **Perception.** The argument from illusion; direct and indirect realism; disjunctivism; Sellars's dilemma for the given.
9. **Induction and IBE.** Hume's argument; Reichenbach, Strawson, Popper; grue; inference to the best explanation and the bad lot.
10. **Testimony.** Reductionism and anti-reductionism; transmission; epistemic injustice; the jury theorem.
11. **Credences.** Axioms, conditional probability, Bayes's theorem, the Dutch book argument, the lottery paradox.
12. **Disagreement and belief.** Conciliationism and steadfast views; Clifford and James.

### Formulas to know

- $P(A \mid B) = P(A \wedge B)/P(B)$.
- $P(H \mid E) = P(E \mid H)P(H)/P(E)$, with $P(E) = P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H)$.
- Posterior odds = prior odds times likelihood ratio.
- A majority of three independent voters, each correct with probability $p$, is right with probability $p^3 + 3p^2(1-p)$.

### Common errors

- Confusing validity with soundness, and a view with the objection to it.
- Treating the a priori as the necessary or the innate.
- Forgetting the prior (base rate) when updating.
- Attributing to a philosopher a view held by someone else, such as Dretske's denial of closure and the contextualist's retention of it.

choice*: It classifies a Gettier-case belief that would still be held if false as not knowledge, and it implies that closure fails when the belief that one is not a handless brain in a vat is insensitive.
choice: It implies that closure holds, so the skeptical argument is sound.
choice: It is a condition on justification and says nothing about knowledge.
choice: It was proposed by Gettier to repair the tripartite analysis.
hint: Recall what happens to the belief that one is not a vat-brain, and to Gettier-case beliefs that would be held even if false.
solution: Sensitivity classifies Gettier-style beliefs that would be held even if false as non-knowledge, and it makes the belief that one is not a handless BIV insensitive, so it allows knowing $H$ without knowing what $H$ entails: closure fails. @exam num
prompt: A hypothesis $H$ has prior probability 0.1. A witness says "yes" with probability 0.9 if $H$ is true and with probability 0.2 if $H$ is false. Assume that the witness's answer depends only on whether $H$ is true and that you have no other evidence. The witness says "yes". What is your posterior credence in $H$? Give the answer as a decimal to three places.
answer: 0.333
tol: 0.001
check: 0.1 * 0.9 / (0.1 * 0.9 + 0.9 * 0.2)
hint: Treat the witness's report as evidence $E$ and compute $P(E)$ from both cases.
solution: $P(E) = 0.9 \times 0.1 + 0.2 \times 0.9 = 0.09 + 0.18 = 0.27$. The posterior is $0.09/0.27 = 1/3 \approx 0.333$. Testimony from a reasonably reliable witness raises the credence from 0.1 to one third, without making the claim probable, because of the low prior.
@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of about 500 words: reconstruct the closure argument for skepticism in standard form, explain how Moore and the contextualists each respond, and say which response you find more plausible and why. Include your own example of an entailment of the kind the argument uses.
hint: Name the premise each response rejects or reinterprets, and compare the costs.
solution: Let $H$ be "my car is in the driveway" and $\neg$T "my car has not been stolen and replaced by a convincing replica". P1 (Closure): if I know $H$ and competently deduce $\neg$T, I know $\neg$T. P2: $H$ entails $\neg$T. P3: I do not know $\neg$T. C: I do not know $H$. The argument is valid. Moore denies P3, holding that he is more certain that $H$ than of any philosophical premise against it; the cost is that this seems to beg the question against the skeptic. The contextualist says that "know" has standards that vary with context, so P3 is true when skeptical possibilities are salient and false in ordinary contexts, and closure holds within each context; the cost is that it seems to explain the puzzle by semantics and leaves open whether I really know. I find contextualism more plausible as a diagnosis of why the argument has pull, though a Moorean might reply that it concedes too much to the skeptic. Either choice earns credit if the reasoning is sound.
