@article
A straight oar looks bent where it enters the water. The oar is not bent, so what you are aware of cannot be the oar as it really is. Some philosophers conclude that what you are directly aware of is something else: an appearance, an image, a *sense-datum*. Others deny it. The question matters for epistemology because we want perceptual experience to justify our beliefs about the world, and the theory of what experience is shapes how it could do that.

### Theories of perception

- **Direct (naive) realism**: in veridical perception, we are directly aware of mind-independent objects and their properties, and the experience is constituted by that awareness.
- **Indirect realism** (representationalism): we are directly aware of mental items, such as ideas or sense-data, which represent or resemble external objects. Locke's theory of ideas is a historical example, and Russell in *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912) defended a sense-datum view.
- **Phenomenalism**: physical objects are constructions out of actual and possible experiences (a view associated with J. S. Mill's "permanent possibilities of sensation" and with Berkeley's idealism, which differs in details).
- **Intentionalism** (representationalism in the current sense): experiences have representational content, and what it is like to have an experience is fixed by that content.
- **Disjunctivism**: the good case (seeing a tree) and the bad cases (hallucination) are different in kind, even if they seem the same to the subject.

### The argument from illusion

| P1 | In illusions and hallucinations, the subject is aware of something that has sensible qualities that no physical object in the scene has (a bent look, a dagger-shape where there is none), and that something is a mental item. |
| P2 | A veridical experience and an illusory or hallucinatory one can be subjectively indistinguishable. |
| P3 | Experiences that are subjectively indistinguishable are of the same fundamental kind. |
| C1 | So what the subject is aware of in veridical experience is the same kind of thing as in illusions. |
| C | So what we are directly aware of is mental (sense-data), not physical objects. |

The argument is valid given its premises. Its replies are classified by the premise they reject:

- **Adverbialism and intentionalism** reject P1: the subject is not aware of an odd *object* but is sensing in a certain way, or representing a world that is not as it represents.
- **Disjunctivism** rejects P3: indistinguishability does not imply sameness in kind, so veridical perception can consist in direct awareness of the object and hallucination be something else.
- **Naive realism** about the good case relies on the disjunctivist rejection of P3.

Indirect realism faces a threat of skepticism: if we are aware only of mental items, how can we know anything about the objects beyond them? This **veil of perception** worry is one source of the skeptical arguments of weeks 5–6. A disjunctivist response matters for skepticism: if in the good case one sees *that* there is a tree, the evidence differs from the bad case (a version of **epistemological disjunctivism**, defended by John McDowell and Duncan Pritchard), which challenges premise P1 of the underdetermination argument.

### The given and the dispute about foundations

The **given** is the idea that experience provides a foundation: non-inferential, justified states that justify our other beliefs without themselves needing justification. In "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind" (1956), Wilfrid Sellars attacked the **myth of the given** with a dilemma:

| P1 | If an experience is to justify a belief, it must have propositional content, so that it can stand in rational relations (support, contradiction) to beliefs. |
| P2 | If an experience has propositional content, then it is itself a candidate for justification, so it is not an epistemically independent foundation: having it presupposes concepts and the ability to be a reliable reporter. |
| P3 | If an experience lacks propositional content, then it cannot be a reason for belief, since it can only cause belief. |
| C | So there is no experience that is both epistemically independent and able to justify belief. |

The dilemma is valid, and responses reject a premise or reinterpret it:

- **Donald Davidson** accepted the horn that experience cannot justify, and held that only a belief can justify a belief: experience causes beliefs but gives no reason. This is a coherentist view.
- **John McDowell** (*Mind and World*, 1994) argued that experience has conceptual content, so it can be a reason, and that a coherentist view without constraint from the world is like a mind spinning in the void. He accepts Sellars's critique of the Given and rejects the demand for a foundation of non-conceptual givens: experience is already conceptual, so it can still rationally constrain belief, and he does not treat it as a belief in need of independent justification. He is not seeking an independent foundation, so he reinterprets the dilemma rather than denying one of its premises.
- **Modest foundationalists and dogmatists** such as James Pryor, and defenders of **phenomenal conservatism** such as Michael Huemer, say that an experience in which it seems that $p$ gives immediate, defeasible justification for believing $p$ unless there are defeaters; they reject P2 by denying that this kind of justification requires prior justification.
- Some foundationalists appeal to **acquaintance** with the fact or experience itself rather than propositional content (a version is Richard Fumerton's, on which a belief is non-inferentially justified by one's acquaintance with the fact that makes it true), rejecting P3.

@example
title: Locating the reply
problem: Which premise of the argument from illusion does each reply reject? (a) "The oar looks bent, but that is a way I am representing the oar, not an object I am aware of." (b) "Seeing a real oar is a different state from having a hallucination, even if the two feel the same."
step: (a) denies that the subject is aware of an odd object, so it rejects P1.
step: (b) accepts that the two can be subjectively the same, so it accepts P2, and rejects P3: sameness of appearance is not sameness of kind.
answer: (a) rejects P1 (intentionalism or adverbialism); (b) rejects P3 (disjunctivism).
@example
title: Applying Sellars's dilemma
problem: A foundationalist says that your experience of redness gives you a basic belief that "there is something red here". Use the dilemma to say what the foundationalist must now say.
step: If the experience has propositional content ("there is something red here"), then, by P2, it seems to need justification of its own, since having it depends on concepts such as "red" and on being a competent user of them.
step: If it has no propositional content, it is a mere sensation, and by P3 it can cause but not justify.
step: The foundationalist must therefore accept one horn and explain it: say that propositional experience justifies immediately without needing a further justification (a modest foundationalist or dogmatist), or appeal to acquaintance with experience.
answer: The foundationalist must reject P2 or P3 and give an account of non-inferential justification.
@mistakes
- Taking the argument from illusion to prove sense-data: it is valid but its premises are rejected by several views.
- Saying that disjunctivism denies that hallucinations seem the same as veridical perception: it accepts indistinguishability and denies that it fixes the kind.
- Reading Sellars as rejecting the idea that experience matters for knowledge: he rejects experience as an independent epistemic foundation, and he accepts that experience figures in observation reports.
- Attributing to McDowell the view that experience is a belief: he holds that experience has conceptual content, which is different.

solution: P1: If an experience is to justify a belief, it must have propositional content. P2: If an experience has propositional content, it is not epistemically independent and itself needs justification. P3: If it lacks propositional content, it can cause but not justify. C: So there is no experience that is both independent and justifying. I find the first horn easier to resist: a modest foundationalist can say that an experience with propositional content can justify immediately and defeasibly, and that the fact that having it depends on concepts does not mean it needs further justification, since justification can be immediate yet dependent on a background of conceptual capacities.
