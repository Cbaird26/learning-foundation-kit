@article
You and a friend each add up the same restaurant receipt in your heads. You reach 252 and she reaches 256. You have always been equally good at arithmetic, and neither of you has a calculator. What should you now think? You were confident before; now you know that someone with the same evidence and the same skill disagrees. This is the problem of **peer disagreement**.

### Epistemic peers

Two people are **epistemic peers** on a question if they are equally well informed (they have the same evidence) and equally capable of assessing it (equally intelligent, careful and free from bias), as far as you can tell. The question is how to respond when you learn that a peer disagrees.

### Three responses

**Conciliationism** says you should move toward your peer's view, perhaps all the way to the midpoint. The **equal weight view**, defended by Adam Elga (2007) and David Christensen, says that you should give your peer's opinion as much weight as your own. An important constraint is the **independence principle**: in deciding how far to trust your peer, you may not rely on the very reasoning in dispute. If you could reason, "she disagrees, so she is wrong, since my answer is right", then disagreement could never matter.

**Steadfast** views say that you may sometimes stay with your view. Thomas Kelly's **total evidence view** (2010) holds that the first-order evidence still counts: if one party in fact reasoned correctly from the evidence, her belief is better supported, though the fact of disagreement is also evidence and should reduce her confidence. The **right reasons** view says that what the first-order evidence supports determines what is rational.

**Permissivism** holds that the same evidence can rationally support more than one attitude, so that two peers could differ rationally; Roger White has argued against it, and others have defended it.

| P1 | You and your friend are epistemic peers on the question. |
| P2 | Your friend's disagreement is evidence that you made an error, as strong as the evidence of your own conviction that she did. |
| P3 | Rationality requires that you respond to your total evidence. |
| P4 | If your total evidence makes it about as likely that you erred as that she did, then responding to it requires you to lower your confidence substantially. |
| C | So you should substantially lower your confidence (conciliationism). |

The argument is valid. Steadfasters reject P2 or P4, on the ground that your first-order evidence is part of the total evidence that P3 refers to and may favor your answer. Two further problems: **self-undermining** (if conciliationism is itself disputed among peers, it seems to recommend lowering confidence in itself), and whether the independence principle is too strong.

Averaging is not the only way to combine opinions. If two peers have gathered *independent* evidence, then agreement, and not only disagreement, should affect confidence. Suppose each begins at 0.5 and each independently gets evidence that is three times as likely if $H$ is true as if it is false. Each moves to odds of 3, a credence of 0.75. If they combine their evidence, the total likelihood ratio is $3 \times 3 = 9$, so the odds are 9 and the combined credence is 0.9, higher than either alone.

### The ethics of belief

Is there a duty about what we ought to believe? W. K. Clifford, in "The Ethics of Belief" (1877), told of a shipowner who sends an emigrant ship to sea, silencing his doubts about its seaworthiness. Clifford held that his belief was wrong even if the ship arrived safely, because it was formed without adequate evidence, and he concluded: "it is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence." This is **evidentialism** in its ethical form.

William James, in "The Will to Believe" (1896), replied that when an option is *genuine* (live, forced and momentous) and by its nature cannot be settled on evidence, we may rightly let our "passional nature" decide. He did not hold that we may believe anything we like: his claim concerns a restricted class of questions, such as some religious or moral ones. Critics say that the class is narrow or that evidence is rarely absent.

A prior question is whether belief is **voluntary**. Bernard Williams ("Deciding to Believe," 1970) argued that we cannot believe at will, since belief aims at truth, and one cannot take something as true while knowing that one acquired the belief by a decision made without regard to whether it is true. If **doxastic involuntarism** is right, "ought" claims about belief must concern how we should *manage* evidence and inquiry, or what beliefs are rational, and not what we can choose to believe.

@example
title: Evaluating conciliationism
problem: You and a peer disagree about the receipt total (252 versus 256). State what the equal weight view says, and what the total evidence view says if your sum was in fact correct.
step: Equal weight: you have no independent reason to prefer your own answer, so you give your friend's answer the same weight as yours, and you should be about equally unsure which of you made the mistake.
step: Total evidence view: your sum, if correctly computed, reflects the first-order evidence correctly; the disagreement is further evidence that lowers your confidence but need not eliminate it, so you may remain somewhat more confident than your friend.
step: A practical resolution that both views agree on: add up again, use a calculator, or check each line, since new evidence beats a stalemate.
answer: Equal weight: treat the answers equally and become about equally unsure. Total evidence: the correct first-order reasoning still counts, though your confidence should fall.
@example
title: Combining independent evidence
problem: Two analysts independently assess a hypothesis $H$ with prior 0.4. Analyst A's evidence is twice as likely given $H$ as given $\neg H$; B's is three times as likely. Assume their evidence is independent given $H$ and given $\neg H$. What credence in $H$ should someone who learns both pieces have?
step: Prior odds are $0.4/0.6 = 2/3$.
step: The combined likelihood ratio is $2 \times 3 = 6$, so the posterior odds are $(2/3) \times 6 = 4$.
step: The posterior probability is $4/5 = 0.8$.
answer: 0.8
@mistakes
- Thinking that conciliationism says to always average: it says that disagreement is evidence that lowers confidence, and that the weight of a peer's view depends on her reliability.
- Using the disputed reasoning to rate your peer's reliability: the independence principle forbids it.
- Reading James as saying that one may believe whatever is comforting: his claim is limited to genuine options that evidence cannot settle.
- Confusing the ethical claim that one ought not believe without evidence with the psychological claim that one cannot believe at will.

prompt: Two peers start with the same prior of 0.5 in $H$. Peer A's private evidence is twice as likely given $H$ as given $\neg H$, and peer B's is five times as likely. Assume the pieces of evidence are independent given $H$ and given $\neg H$. What credence in $H$ is warranted by both pieces of evidence taken together? Give the answer as a decimal to three places.
answer: 0.909
tol: 0.001
check: (2 * 5) / (1 + 2 * 5)
hint: With a prior of 0.5 the prior odds are 1, and likelihood ratios of independent evidence multiply.
solution: The combined likelihood ratio is $2 \times 5 = 10$, so the posterior odds are 10 and the credence is $10/11 \approx 0.909$. Notice that this is higher than the average of the credences each would have alone (about 0.667 and 0.833, with average 0.75), because the evidence is independent.

(3) Prior 0.60; $P(E \mid H) = 0.2$ and $P(E \mid \neg H) = 0.5$. (4) Prior 0.5; two pieces of evidence, each three times as likely if $H$ is true as if it is false, independent given $H$ and given $\neg H$.

(4) Odds $1 \times 3 \times 3 = 9$, posterior $9/10 = 0.9$, using the independence assumption.
