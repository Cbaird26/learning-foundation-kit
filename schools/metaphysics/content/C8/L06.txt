@article
Suppose you know that your car is in the driveway. The car's being in the driveway entails that it has not been stolen and driven away in a way you did not notice. If you know the first, it seems you can know the second by simple deduction. Yet it also seems that you cannot know, from where you stand, that the car has not been stolen. This tension between two plausible claims is the core of the **closure argument** for skepticism, and it is the best-known way of making skepticism a threat to ordinary knowledge.

### The closure principle

The idea is that knowledge is preserved by competent deduction. A common statement is:

**Closure (single-premise version).** If $S$ knows that $p$, and $S$ competently deduces $q$ from $p$ and thereby comes to believe $q$, then $S$ knows that $q$.

Closure is attractive: if you could know the premises and validly deduce the conclusion and still not know the conclusion, deduction would fail to extend knowledge. Let $H$ be "I have hands" and $\neg$BIV be "I am not a handless brain in a vat that is being stimulated to seem to have hands."

| P1 | (Closure) If I know that $H$, and I competently deduce $\neg$BIV from $H$, then I know that $\neg$BIV. |
| P2 | $H$ entails $\neg$BIV. |
| P3 | I do not know that $\neg$BIV. |
| C | So I do not know that $H$. |

The argument is valid: suppose I knew $H$; I could deduce $\neg$BIV by P2, and by P1 I would know $\neg$BIV, which contradicts P3. Premise P3 is supported by the underdetermination argument of week 5: nothing in my evidence favors $\neg$BIV over BIV.

Each reply rejects or reinterprets a premise.

### Moore: reject P3

G. E. Moore (in "A Defense of Common Sense", 1925, and "Proof of an External World", 1939) held that he knew he had hands, and the proof is to hold up his hands and say "here is one hand, and here is another". He argued that he was more certain of the premises of this proof than he could be of any philosophical premise offered against it. On the **Moorean shift**, one turns the skeptic's modus tollens into a modus ponens: if I know that I have hands, then I know I am not a handless BIV; I do know that I have hands; so I know that I am not a handless BIV. The cost: critics say that the reply is question-begging, and Moore's proof seems to give no reason for someone who doubts that there are hands; a related worry is that the evidence for $H$ cannot transmit to $\neg$BIV when the evidence presupposes $\neg$BIV (a *failure of transmission*, discussed by Crispin Wright). Defenders such as James Pryor argue that perceptual experience gives immediate justification for $H$ that does not depend on first ruling out BIV.

### Dretske and Nozick: reject P1

Fred Dretske (1970) and Robert Nozick (1981) denied closure. On Nozick's tracking account, you know $p$ only if (i) $p$ is true, (ii) you believe $p$, (iii) if $p$ were false you would not believe it (**sensitivity**), and (iv) if $p$ were true you would still believe it in close variations. I know I have hands: if I had none I would not believe I had. But if I were a handless BIV I would still believe $\neg$BIV, so my belief that $\neg$BIV is not sensitive and I do not know it. Thus I know $H$ without knowing something it entails, and closure fails. The cost: Keith DeRose called the resulting statement "I know that I have hands, but I do not know that I am not a handless BIV" an **abominable conjunction**; and giving up closure threatens the value of deduction as a way of extending knowledge.

### Contextualism: both P1 and P3 are true, but not in the same context

**Epistemic contextualists** (Stewart Cohen, Keith DeRose, David Lewis) hold that "know" is context-sensitive: the standards for the truth of a sentence like "S knows that $p$" depend on the speaker's context. In ordinary contexts the standards are modest and "I know that I have hands" is true. When skeptical possibilities are raised, standards rise and P3 comes out true and "I know $H$" comes out false. Closure holds within each context. The skeptic's argument is thus correct in its own context, and the ordinary claim is correct in ordinary contexts; the dispute is a matter of shifting standards. Costs: it seems to leave unanswered whether I *really* know; "know" does not seem to behave like an indexical in ways tests suggest it should (Jason Stanley); and the view explains the paradox by semantics rather than by the epistemology of the case. An alternative, **interest-relative invariantism** (Hawthorne, Stanley), says that the truth of "S knows $p$" depends on the subject's stakes, not on the speaker's context.

@example
title: Reconstructing a closure argument
problem: Reconstruct the closure argument for the belief "I know that the bird on the pond is a mallard", using the hypothesis that it is a lifelike decoy that moves by remote control.
step: Let $M$ be "the bird on the pond is a mallard" and $\neg$D be "it is not a remote-controlled decoy". $M$ entails $\neg$D.
step: Premises: P1, closure: if I know $M$ and deduce $\neg$D, I know $\neg$D; P2, $M$ entails $\neg$D; P3, I do not know $\neg$D, since from this distance a good decoy would look the same. Conclusion: I do not know $M$.
step: Check validity: if I knew $M$, then by P2 and P1 I would know $\neg$D, contradicting P3. Replies: reject P3 (Moore: I know it isn't a decoy), reject P1 (sensitivity: if the bird were a different species I would not believe $M$, but I would still believe $\neg$D if it were a decoy), or say the standards for P3 are high only in some contexts.
answer: The argument is valid; the philosophical issue is which premise to reject.
@example
title: Sensitivity applied
problem: On Nozick's account, do you know that you are not a handless brain in a vat if you believe it, it is true, and you would believe it even if it were false?
step: Sensitivity requires that if $\neg$BIV were false (I were a handless BIV) I would not believe it.
step: But a vat-brain stimulated to have the same experiences would believe $\neg$BIV, so the belief is not sensitive.
step: So Nozick's account says you do not know it, even though it is true and believed.
answer: No: the belief fails sensitivity, so it is not knowledge on Nozick's account; this is how his view gives up closure.
@mistakes
- Saying that Moore refutes the skeptic by deduction alone: his proof rests on being more certain of the premises than of the skeptical premise, which critics find question-begging.
- Treating contextualism as relativism about truth: the claim is that the content of "know" depends on the context of the speaker, while the truth of each use is not relative.
- Taking denial of closure to be an arbitrary choice: Dretske and Nozick are driven by a theory of knowledge (sensitivity) that predicts the failure.
- Confusing contextualism (the speaker's context fixes the standard) with interest-relative invariantism (the subject's stakes fix the standard).
