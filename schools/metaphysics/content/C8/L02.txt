@article
Kofi has excellent evidence that his colleague Bela drives a blue hatchback: he sees her arrive in it every morning, and she has told him she owns it. From this he infers, and comes to believe, "Someone in my office drives a blue hatchback." Unknown to Kofi, Bela sold the car yesterday. But another colleague, Ines, whom Kofi barely knows, drives a blue hatchback. Kofi's belief is true. It is justified. He holds it. Does he *know* it?

Most people say no, and that is the **Gettier problem**. In a three-page paper of 1963, "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?", Edmund Gettier presented cases of justified true belief that are not knowledge, refuting the claim that the three conditions are jointly sufficient.

### The structure of a Gettier case

Gettier relied on two assumptions. First, justification is **fallible**: one can be justified in believing a falsehood. Second, justification is **closed under competent deduction**: if you are justified in believing $p$, and you validly deduce $q$ from $p$ and accept $q$, you are justified in believing $q$. Gettier's own cases involve a job candidate and a set of coins, and a second case with a disjunction; Kofi's case has the same shape:

1. S has strong evidence for a false proposition $p$ (Bela drives a blue hatchback).
2. S validly infers a proposition $q$ from $p$ (someone in the office does), so S is justified in believing $q$.
3. $q$ is true, but because of facts unconnected to S's reason (Ines's car).

The recipe is general: **take a justified false belief, deduce something that is true for an unrelated reason**. Other standard cases do not have this inferential structure. A version of Russell's stopped-clock case has a man look at a clock that he takes to be working but that has in fact stopped, at the one moment of the day when it shows the right time, so that he forms a true belief about the time by luck. Roderick Chisholm described a farmer who sees what looks like a sheep in a field and believes there is a sheep there; it is a dog, but a sheep is hidden behind a rock. Goldman's fake-barn case (1976) has a driver who sees a real barn in a district full of facades, with no false inference at all. In every case the belief is true and justified, and its truth is a matter of **luck** with respect to the evidence.

| P1 | If the tripartite analysis is correct, then any justified true belief is knowledge. |
| P2 | Kofi's belief is justified, true and believed. |
| P3 | Kofi does not know that someone in his office drives a blue hatchback. |
| C | So the tripartite analysis is not correct. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens after P1 and P2 give that Kofi knows). The premise at issue is P3, which rests on the intuition that the truth of Kofi's belief is an accident.

### Responses

**No false lemmas.** Knowledge requires that the belief not be inferred from a falsehood (an early version is due to Michael Clark, 1963). This handles Kofi, whose reasoning runs through the false claim about Bela. But the fake-barn and sheep cases involve no inference from a falsehood, and the view must say what counts as reasoning at all.

**Defeasibility.** Knowledge is justified true belief with justification that no further true information would defeat (versions by Keith Lehrer and Thomas Paxson, Peter Klein, and others). Kofi's justification would be defeated if he learned that Bela sold the car. The problem: almost any evidence is defeated by some misleading true information, so the condition must be restricted to exclude misleading defeaters, and that is hard to do without circularity.

**Causal theory.** Alvin Goldman (1967) proposed that the fact that $p$ must figure in the causal explanation of the belief. Ines's car plays no role in Kofi's belief. The theory struggles with knowledge of the future, of mathematics and of generalizations, where causes are not obviously available.

**Sensitivity.** Robert Nozick (1981) added that if $p$ were false, S would not believe $p$. Kofi would still believe it if Ines had sold her car and no one drove a blue hatchback, so his belief is insensitive. Problem: sensitivity seems to fail for some obvious knowledge, and it makes closure fail (week 6).

**Safety.** Knowledge requires that S could not easily have been wrong in forming the belief this way (developed by Ernest Sosa and Timothy Williamson). Kofi's belief is unsafe: very similar circumstances would give a false belief. Problems arise in giving a precise account of "easily".

**Knowledge first.** Williamson (2000) argues that knowledge cannot be analyzed into more basic conditions; we should take knowledge as primitive and explain justification and belief in terms of it. Linda Zagzebski argued that any account on which justification does not entail truth will face Gettier-style cases. These responses give up the project of analysis.

@example
title: Diagnosing a Gettier case
problem: Mina sees a parcel on her neighbor's step with a label that reads "Rao" and believes "There is a parcel for Rao on Rao's step." In fact the visible label is for a different person called Rao, and the parcel was delivered to the wrong house, but Rao's own parcel, which Mina cannot see, is also on the step, hidden behind a plant pot. Is this a Gettier case? Which responses classify it correctly?
step: Test the conditions. The belief is true (Rao's own parcel is there) and held. Mina's justification is the label she sees, which is good evidence. So all three conditions hold.
step: Is her success a matter of luck? The label she sees is not for Rao, so her evidence is unconnected to the true state of affairs. Her belief is true by accident: it is a Gettier case.
step: No false lemmas: she infers from the belief that the visible parcel is for her neighbor Rao, which is false, so the response gives the right verdict. Defeasibility: learning that the label is for a different Rao would defeat her justification, so the verdict is right again. Safety: a nearby case where the plant pot is absent and the other parcel not delivered gives a false belief, so the belief is unsafe.
answer: It is a Gettier case, and each of the three responses correctly says that Mina does not know.

title: Constructing a Gettier case
problem: Build a new Gettier case from the recipe and check that it works.
step: Choose a justified false belief. Dalia sees her housemate's bicycle chained in the yard each night and believes that her housemate is home when the bicycle is there. Tonight the bicycle is there but the housemate left it and went out.

answer: A valid Gettier case: justified, true, believed, and true by luck relative to her evidence.
@mistakes
- Saying that the Gettier cases show that justified belief is unnecessary: they target sufficiency, not necessity.
- Describing Gettier's cases as cases where the justified belief is false: the belief is true, though the justifying evidence supports it only by coincidence.
- Treating "no false lemmas" as a complete solution: the barn and sheep cases involve no inference from a falsehood.
- Thinking that every Gettier case needs an inference: the standard cases of Russell, Chisholm and Goldman have none.

The argument is valid, and the controversial premise is P3, which rests on the intuition that the truth of the belief is accidental. A strong defense of P3 notes that nearly everyone shares it and that the response philosophers give is to repair the analysis, not to deny the verdict.
