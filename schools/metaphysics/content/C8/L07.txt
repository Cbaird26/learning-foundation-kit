@article
You know that no bachelor is married, and you did not need to survey bachelors to find out. You know that if every dog is a mammal and every mammal is an animal then every dog is an animal, and no experiment is needed. Other things you know only by looking: that it is raining in Oslo, or that water boils at a lower temperature on a mountain. Is there a real difference in the *way* these are known, and can we explain how we know the first kind?

### Three distinctions

Three distinctions are easily run together, because they belong to different fields.

- **A priori / a posteriori** is **epistemic**: it concerns how a proposition can be known or justified. A proposition is knowable **a priori** if it can be justified independently of experience; it is **a posteriori** if its justification depends on experience. Independence is of the *justification*, not of what is needed to acquire the concepts: one needs experience to learn the word "bachelor", but not to justify "no bachelor is married". "A priori" does not mean "innate" or "before experience in time".
- **Necessary / contingent** is **metaphysical**: a truth is necessary if it could not have been false.
- **Analytic / synthetic** is **semantic**: roughly, a sentence is analytic if it is true in virtue of meaning (for Kant, if the predicate concept is contained in the subject concept), and synthetic otherwise.

Kant held that these come apart. In the Introduction to the second edition of the *Critique of Pure Reason* he says that the marks of the a priori are necessity and strict universality, because experience can tell us how things are but not that they must be so. He argued that arithmetic and geometry are **synthetic a priori**: they extend our knowledge, yet are known independently of experience. Hume's fork, in the *Enquiry*, divided objects of reason into *relations of ideas*, known by thought alone and certain, and *matters of fact*, known by experience.

Saul Kripke argued in *Naming and Necessity* (1980) that the distinctions do not line up:

| | a priori | a posteriori |
|---|---|---|
| **necessary** | "No bachelor is married" | "Hesperus is Phosphorus"; "Water is H2O" (on Kripke's view) |
| **contingent** | the length of the standard meter bar, for someone who fixes the unit by it | "It is raining in Oslo" |

Kripke's example of the contingent a priori is a person who fixes the meaning of "one meter" as the length of a particular bar at a time, and so knows without measurement that the bar is one meter long, though the bar might have had a different length. These are Kripke's claims, and some philosophers dispute them.

### An argument for the a priori

| P1 | We know some truths, such as those of simple arithmetic and logic, that are necessary and universal. |
| P2 | Experience can tell us only how things are, not that they must be so or that they hold without exception. |
| C | So our knowledge of such truths is not based on experience: it is a priori. |

This is a version of the reasoning in Kant's Introduction. The argument is valid. Premise P2 is the target: an empiricist such as J. S. Mill held that even arithmetic is a very well confirmed generalization from experience, and answered that the necessity we feel is the strength of our inductive habit.

### Quine's challenge

W. V. Quine, in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), rejected the analytic/synthetic distinction and argued that our beliefs form a web that faces experience as a whole, so that any belief, even in logic, could be revised if the web required it. He noted that the revision of logic had been proposed to simplify quantum mechanics.

| P1 | If a statement were known a priori, it would be immune to revision in light of experience. |
| P2 | No statement is immune to revision in light of experience. |
| C | So no statement is known a priori. |

The argument is valid. Premise P1 is the weak point: **a priori justification need not be indefeasible**. A statement may be justified independently of experience and yet be given up if its consequences are refuted, as a theorem may be given up when a proof is found to rest on a false axiom. A reply to Quine thus distinguishes independence from experience in *justification* from immunity to *revision*. Quine's holism also faces the objection that the revision of logic is itself guided by logical reasoning.

### Rational intuition

How can we know anything a priori? One answer, from the rationalist tradition and defended in recent decades by Laurence BonJour, is **rational intuition** or **rational insight**: a faculty by which we grasp that a proposition is true by understanding it. Descartes spoke of clear and distinct perception. Intuition in this sense is an *intellectual seeming*: when you consider "if $p$ and $q$, then $p$", it seems true, and not merely because you have memorized it. Rival accounts say that a priori knowledge derives from understanding concepts (the conceptual or analytic account), from linguistic convention, or from a reliable cognitive process.

Objections: the faculty is mysterious, since it is hard to say how mere understanding can be a source of information about necessary truths; intuitions have sometimes been wrong (naive set theory's comprehension principle seemed obvious and led to Russell's paradox); and experimental philosophers have reported that intuitions about some philosophical cases vary among people, though how far this undermines their use is disputed. Defenders reply that revisable fallible intuitions can still justify, as perceptual seemings do.

title: Cross-classifying propositions
problem: Classify each as a priori or a posteriori, and necessary or contingent, according to the standard view in this lesson. (a) "Every triangle has three sides." (b) "The Pacific is larger than the Atlantic." (c) "Water is H2O," on Kripke's view.
step: (a) Known by understanding the concept of a triangle, with no survey of triangles: a priori. It could not be false: necessary.
step: (b) Known by looking at atlases and measurements: a posteriori. The oceans' sizes are facts that could have been different: contingent.

answer: (a) a priori, necessary; (b) a posteriori, contingent; (c) a posteriori, necessary (on Kripke's view).

@mistakes
- Defining the a priori as innate or as known before experience in time: the a priori concerns the independence of the justification from experience.
- Identifying the a priori with the necessary: Kripke's cases (the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori) are put forward to show that they differ.
- Taking "a priori" to mean "certain" or "unrevisable": a priori justification can be fallible.
- Saying Kant held that arithmetic is analytic: he held that it is synthetic a priori.
