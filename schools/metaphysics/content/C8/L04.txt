@article
A thermometer registers the temperature because of how it is built, not because it has reasons for its reading; yet we trust it, and say it carries information about the room. The **reliabilist** says that human belief can be like this. A belief is justified when it is produced by a process that tends to yield truths, whether or not the believer has any reasons she can state. Others reply that a belief produced by a reliable process but with no support in the believer's perspective is no better than a lucky thermometer reading.

### Process reliabilism

In "What Is Justified Belief?" (1979), Alvin Goldman proposed, in outline, that a belief is justified if and only if it is produced by a reliable belief-forming process. For beliefs inferred from other beliefs, the process must be conditionally reliable: it must tend to yield truths when its inputs are true. Processes include perception under good conditions, memory, and sound reasoning; wishful thinking and guessing are unreliable. This is an **externalist** theory: what makes the belief justified lies at least partly outside the believer's own perspective.

**Internalism** says that the factors that justify are accessible to the believer by reflection. There are two forms: **access internalism** (the believer can tell by reflection what justifies) and **mentalism** (justification supervenes on the believer's mental states, even if she cannot always tell what they are). **Evidentialism**, defended by Earl Conee and Richard Feldman, is a mentalist view: a belief is justified when it fits the believer's evidence. **Externalism** denies that justification is fixed by such internal factors. Reliabilism is one version; others include proper-function views (Alvin Plantinga) and tracking theories of knowledge (Nozick).

### Objections to reliabilism

**The generality problem.** Every belief is produced by many processes of different breadth: "vision", "vision at dusk", "vision by this person with her eyes in this state". The reliability of the process depends on how it is described, and a theory of justification must say which description counts. Goldman himself noted the problem, and it was pressed by Earl Conee and Richard Feldman.

**Reliable but unjustified.** Laurence BonJour imagined Norman, who has a reliable clairvoyant power and no evidence for or against it; he forms the belief that the President is in New York. Many judge that Norman is not justified. Keith Lehrer's Mr Truetemp has had a device implanted that causes him to form accurate beliefs about the temperature, without his knowing. The cases suggest that reliability is not sufficient.

**The new evil demon problem.** The cases above target sufficiency. The next targets necessity. It is due to Stewart Cohen and Keith Lehrer (1983):

| P1 | The victim of a deceiving demon has perceptual beliefs produced by processes that are unreliable in the victim's world. |
| P2 | The victim is an internal duplicate of an ordinary perceiver, and the ordinary perceiver's perceptual beliefs are justified. |
| P3 | Internal duplicates are alike in which of their beliefs are justified. |
| C1 | So the victim's perceptual beliefs are justified. |
| C | So justification does not require reliable production in the believer's own world. |

The argument is valid. Reliabilists reject P3, or add that the victim's processes would be reliable in normal worlds (Goldman's later "normal worlds" proposal), or distinguish being justified from being blameless. Internalists reply that P3 is exactly what the case shows: justification supervenes on the internal.

### Comparing the families

| | what justifies | main strength | main problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidentialism (internalist) | the believer's evidence | explains the demon victim's justification | seems to over-intellectualize; unclear how infants and animals fit |
| Reliabilism (externalist) | reliable production | explains how unsophisticated believers know | generality problem; Norman; demon victim |

Many philosophers combine the two: one can think there are two kinds of epistemic status, one that depends on internal rationality and one that depends on reliability.

@example
title: One case, three theories
problem: Ines has just arrived in a town and sees a clock tower. Without thinking about it, she forms the belief that it is 3:15, since the clock reads 3:15. She has no information about the clock's reliability, but it is in fact a very accurate clock. Say what each of foundationalism (modest), coherentism and reliabilism says about whether her belief is justified.
step: Modest foundationalism: her belief is a perceptual belief formed directly from her visual experience of the clock face. It is the kind of belief that can be basic, so it is justified unless she has a defeater. She has none, so it counts as justified.
step: Coherentism: justification depends on coherence with her other beliefs. She holds no beliefs against the clock and her background beliefs (clocks usually tell the time, towns put clocks up to be read) support it. It coheres, so it is justified, though the verdict depends on whether she counts as having those background beliefs.
step: Reliabilism: reading a well-functioning clock in good light is a reliable process, and her belief is produced by it. It is justified, whether or not she knows anything about the clock.
answer: All three can count the belief as justified, for different reasons: basic perceptual justification, coherence with background beliefs, and reliable production. A case where the theories diverge needs a change to the case: for example, a broken clock.
@example
title: Where the theories diverge
problem: Change the case: the clock in fact has been stopped at 3:15 for a week, and Ines forms the same belief on the same basis, though it happens to be 3:15 by luck. What do reliabilism and evidentialism say about justification, and about knowledge?
step: Reliabilism: reading a stopped clock is not a reliable process, so the belief is not justified.
step: Evidentialism: her evidence is exactly as before: the clock reads 3:15 and she has no sign it has stopped. The belief fits her evidence, so it is justified.
step: Knowledge: the belief is true, but its truth is lucky (a Gettier case in the style of Russell's clock), so she does not know on either view.
answer: Reliabilism says unjustified; evidentialism says justified; both deny knowledge.
@mistakes
- Saying that reliabilism requires the believer to know that her process is reliable: it requires only that the process be reliable.
- Treating externalism as the claim that justification has nothing to do with the mind: externalists say that some justifiers lie outside the believer's perspective.
- Confusing the clairvoyant case (reliable but intuitively unjustified, against sufficiency) with the demon case (justified but unreliable, against necessity).
- Taking the new evil demon problem to show that the victim knows: it concerns justification, not knowledge.
