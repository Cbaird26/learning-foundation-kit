You seem to be sitting somewhere reading these words. You seem to feel the chair and to see the screen. Could all of this be a vivid dream? You would not be able to tell from inside. And if you cannot rule that out, how can you claim to know that you are reading? This is the problem of **external-world skepticism**, and it has produced some of the most durable arguments in philosophy.

### Varieties of skepticism

**Global skepticism** says we know nothing (or nearly nothing); **local skepticism** targets a domain, such as the external world, other minds, the past or morality. **Pyrrhonian skepticism**, from the ancient tradition associated with Sextus Empiricus, is a practice of suspending judgment when opposing arguments seem equally strong. **Cartesian skepticism**, the subject of this week, is an argument that we lack knowledge or justification because we cannot rule out certain possibilities. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 80% of respondents accepted non-skeptical realism about the external world and about 5% skepticism; the share of philosophers who hold a view is not evidence about whether it is true, and skeptical arguments matter because they are hard to answer, not because many accept them.

### Descartes's doubts

In Meditation I, Descartes sets out to find what he can be certain of and uses doubt as a method. He gives two main arguments. First, the **dream argument**: he has often been convinced in dreams that he was sitting by the fire, and he finds no certain mark that separates waking from dreaming. Second, the **evil demon** hypothesis: a supremely powerful and malicious deceiver might be arranging all his experiences, and even his beliefs about simple arithmetic could be false. Descartes does not end as a skeptic: he finds the cogito in Meditation II and aims to rebuild knowledge. Whether his reconstruction succeeds is a different question.

The modern version is the **brain in a vat**: a brain kept alive in a tank and stimulated by a computer so that it has exactly the experiences of a normal life. The brain's beliefs about its surroundings would be false.

### What makes a skeptical hypothesis work

A **skeptical hypothesis** (SH) such as dreaming, the demon or the vat must satisfy two conditions. It must be **incompatible** with the ordinary belief (if you are in a vat, you are not holding a book), and it must be **subjectively indistinguishable**: your experiences would be the same whether or not it were true. Hypotheses that merely suggest a mistake, such as "perhaps you misread the sign", are ordinary doubts that evidence can settle.

### The underdetermination argument

Let $H$ be an ordinary belief, such as "I am reading in a library", and SH a skeptical hypothesis.

| P1 | SH is incompatible with $H$, and my evidence is the same whether $H$ is true or SH is true. |
| P2 | If my evidence does not favor $H$ over a hypothesis incompatible with $H$, then I do not know $H$. |
| C | So I do not know $H$. |

The argument is valid. P1 is stipulated by the way the hypothesis is built (though some deny that perceptual evidence is identical in the two cases; see week 8 on disjunctivism). P2 is the **underdetermination principle**, and nearly all replies attack it or the closure principle behind a second skeptical argument (week 6). Three observations help.

1. P2 can seem too strong. If it applies to every possible incompatible hypothesis, however far-fetched, then we know almost nothing, including claims we are sure of. Skeptics say that is the point; **fallibilists** say it shows the standard for knowledge is too demanding, since we can know while not having evidence that rules out every possibility of error.
2. The principle is about *evidence*, so externalists may deny it: on a reliabilist view, I can know $H$ by a reliable process without having evidence that favors $H$ over SH.
3. The argument concerns *knowledge*; a separate argument could target *justification*, saying that the evidence also fails to justify belief in $H$. That is a stronger skepticism.

### The dreaming argument in standard form

| P1 | If I know that I am awake and sitting by the fire, then I can tell that I am not now dreaming of it. |
| P2 | There is no certain mark that distinguishes waking experience from dreaming experience. |
| P3 | If there is no such mark, I cannot tell that I am not now dreaming. |
| C | So I do not know that I am awake and sitting by the fire. |

This argument is valid. P2 is the premise most often questioned, since dreams seem to differ from waking in coherence and vividness, though a better dream would remove the difference. The vat case removes it by stipulation.

title: Building and testing a skeptical hypothesis
problem: A student proposes the "five-minute hypothesis": the world was created five minutes ago, complete with memories, books and apparent ruins. Does it meet the conditions for a skeptical hypothesis against the belief "The castle ruins were built in the Middle Ages"? Reconstruct the argument.
step: Incompatibility: if the world began five minutes ago, the castle was not built in the Middle Ages, so the hypothesis is incompatible with the belief.
step: Indistinguishability: the hypothesis says the evidence (the stones, the guidebook, the memories) is exactly as it would be if the belief were true, so the evidence does not favor one over the other.

answer: The hypothesis works as a skeptical hypothesis about the past, giving a local skepticism about history.
@example
title: Ordinary doubt versus skeptical hypothesis
problem: Which of these is a skeptical hypothesis in the sense of this lesson? (a) "Perhaps the sign said 'Dover' and I misread it as 'Dorset'." (b) "Perhaps every apparent perception I have is produced by a computer feeding a disembodied brain."
step: (a) is incompatible with the belief but evidence can settle it: I could look again, or ask. It is not subjectively indistinguishable in the relevant way.
step: (b) is incompatible with ordinary beliefs about my surroundings and is designed so that my experiences are the same either way.
answer: Only (b) is a skeptical hypothesis; (a) is an ordinary doubt.
@mistakes
- Saying that Descartes was a skeptic: he used skeptical arguments as a method to find certainty, though he thought dreaming and demon hypotheses could not be refuted at the first stage.
- Thinking that a skeptical hypothesis must be probable: the argument needs only that it cannot be ruled out by one's evidence.
- Treating the skeptical conclusion as that the external world does not exist: the conclusion is that we do not know (or are not justified in believing) that it does.
- Confusing the underdetermination argument with the closure argument of week 6: the first rests on a principle about evidence, the second on a principle about knowledge and deduction.

hint: Make it incompatible with the belief and make your evidence the same in both cases.

@quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct the brain-in-a-vat version of the underdetermination argument in standard form, and explain why a fallibilist thinks premise P2 is too strong.
hint: Use $H$ for an ordinary belief and SH for the vat hypothesis.
solution: P1: My evidence is the same whether $H$ ("I am holding a book") is true or I am a brain in a vat. P2: If my evidence does not favor $H$ over a hypothesis incompatible with $H$, I do not know $H$. C: So I do not know $H$. A fallibilist holds that knowledge does not require evidence that rules out every possible way of being wrong. If P2 applied to every incompatible hypothesis, however remote, it would imply that we do not know much that we are in practice quite sure of, which suggests that the standard for knowledge it assumes is too high. The skeptic replies that in practice we do not take remote possibilities seriously because we ignore them, not because we have shown them to be false.
