@article
A cargo scanner is installed at a port. One container in a hundred holds contraband. The scanner sounds its alarm for 95% of containers that hold contraband and for 5% of containers that do not. A container sets off the alarm. How likely is it that it holds contraband? Most people answer "about 95%". The correct answer is about 16%. Seeing why is the first lesson of **formal epistemology**, which uses probability to model rational belief.

### Credences

Beliefs come in degrees. You are more confident that the sun will rise tomorrow than that a fair coin will land heads, and more confident of heads than of a ten-sided die showing 7. A **credence** is a degree of belief, represented by a number from 0 to 1. **Bayesian epistemology** holds that rational credences obey the laws of probability and are updated by conditionalization. For propositions $A$ and $B$ and a credence function $P$:

1. $0 \le P(A) \le 1$.
2. $P(A) = 1$ if $A$ is a tautology.
3. If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, $P(A \vee B) = P(A) + P(B)$.

From these follow $P(\neg A) = 1 - P(A)$, and that $P(A \wedge B) \le P(A)$. The **conditional probability** of $A$ given $B$, when $P(B) > 0$, is

$$P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \wedge B)}{P(B)}.$$

### Bayes's theorem and conditionalization

From the definition, $P(H \mid E)\,P(E) = P(E \mid H)\,P(H)$, which gives **Bayes's theorem**:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E)}, \quad \text{where } P(E) = P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H).$$

Here $P(H)$ is the **prior**, $P(E \mid H)$ is the **likelihood** of the evidence on the hypothesis, and $P(H \mid E)$ the **posterior**. **Conditionalization** is the rule that on learning exactly $E$ (and nothing stronger), your new credence in $H$ is your old $P(H \mid E)$. In odds form, posterior odds equal prior odds times the **likelihood ratio** $P(E \mid H)/P(E \mid \neg H)$.

For the scanner: prior $P(H) = 0.01$, $P(E \mid H) = 0.95$ and $P(E \mid \neg H) = 0.05$ (assuming the scanner's alarm depends only on whether the container holds contraband). Then $P(E) = 0.95 \times 0.01 + 0.05 \times 0.99 = 0.059$ and $P(H \mid E) = 0.0095/0.059 \approx 0.161$. Ignoring the low prior, the **base rate**, is the *base-rate fallacy*. The evidence confirms the hypothesis (the credence rises from 1% to 16%) without making it probable.

**Confirmation.** On the Bayesian account, $E$ **confirms** $H$ if $P(H \mid E) > P(H)$, and the degree of confirmation can be measured by the likelihood ratio.

### Why obey the axioms? The Dutch book argument

Frank Ramsey (1926) and Bruno de Finetti (1937) argued that incoherent credences are irrational because they can be exploited.

| P1 | An agent's credence in $A$ fixes a betting price she regards as fair: she will pay $P(A)$ units for a bet that pays 1 unit if $A$ is true. |
| P2 | If her credences violate the axioms of probability, there is a set of bets, each priced as fair by her, that guarantees she loses whatever happens (a *Dutch book*). |
| P3 | An agent who regards as fair a set of bets that guarantees a loss is irrational. |
| C | So rational credences obey the axioms of probability. |

The argument is valid. Objections: P1 identifies credences with betting dispositions, though risk-aversion and stakes may distort betting behavior; P3 is questioned on the ground that she is exposed to a loss only if she actually bets, and *depragmatized* versions try to avoid this. A different defense, the **accuracy argument** (James Joyce, 1998), shows that incoherent credences are *accuracy-dominated*: some coherent credences are closer to the truth in every possible situation.

### Belief and credence

How do belief and credence relate? The **Lockean thesis** says that to believe $p$ is to have credence above some threshold. The **lottery paradox** (Henry Kyburg, 1961) challenges it: in a fair 1,000-ticket lottery with exactly one winner, your credence that each ticket loses is 0.999, so you would believe of each ticket that it loses, yet you do not believe that all lose. If belief were closed under conjunction, the threshold view would be inconsistent. The **preface paradox** (David Makinson, 1965) poses a similar problem for an author who believes each claim in a book but also that some claim is wrong.

### Problems

The **problem of priors**: Bayes's theorem takes the prior as an input, and nothing in the probability axioms fixes it, so *subjective* Bayesians allow any coherent prior, while *objective* Bayesians seek further constraints. David Lewis's *Principal Principle* ties credence to known chance. The **problem of old evidence** (Clark Glymour, 1980) asks how evidence already known can confirm a theory when $P(E) = 1$.

title: Updating on a spam filter
problem: Assume 20% of incoming emails are spam, the word "prize" appears in 60% of spam emails and in 5% of non-spam emails, and the word's appearance depends on spam status only. Given that an email contains "prize", what is the probability that it is spam?
step: Let $H$ be "spam" and $E$ be "contains prize". Then $P(H) = 0.2$, $P(E \mid H) = 0.6$ and $P(E \mid \neg H) = 0.05$.
step: $P(E) = 0.6 \times 0.2 + 0.05 \times 0.8 = 0.12 + 0.04 = 0.16$.
step: $P(H \mid E) = 0.12/0.16 = 0.75$.
answer: 0.75
@example
title: Building a Dutch book
problem: An agent's credences are $P(A) = 0.6$ and $P(\neg A) = 0.6$. Show how she can be made to lose whatever happens, assuming she will pay her credence for a bet paying 1 unit if the proposition is true.
step: She pays 0.6 for a bet that pays 1 if $A$, and 0.6 for a bet that pays 1 if $\neg A$. Her total outlay is 1.2.
step: Exactly one of $A$ and $\neg A$ is true, so exactly one bet pays 1. Her total return is 1 whatever happens.
step: Her net result is $1 - 1.2 = -0.2$ in every case: a sure loss of 0.2.
answer: A sure loss of 0.2 units, because her credences sum to 1.2 instead of 1.
@mistakes
- Treating $P(A \mid B)$ as the same as $P(B \mid A)$: in the scanner case, $P(\text{alarm} \mid \text{contraband}) = 0.95$ but $P(\text{contraband} \mid \text{alarm}) \approx 0.16$.
- Ignoring the prior, the base-rate fallacy: a highly accurate test can still give a low posterior when the hypothesis is rare.
- Running together assumptions: Bayes's theorem needs the prior and both likelihoods, and multi-step updates need an independence assumption.
- Saying that the Dutch book argument shows that rational agents must bet: it shows that incoherent credences expose one to guaranteed loss if bets are made.

prompt: Use the lottery paradox to explain why the Lockean thesis conflicts with the requirement that belief be closed under conjunction.
hint: Take a fair lottery with exactly one winner and a threshold below 1.
solution: In a fair lottery with 1,000 tickets and exactly one winner, the credence that a given ticket loses is 0.999. If the Lockean threshold is 0.99, you believe of each ticket that it loses. If belief were closed under conjunction, you would believe that all 1,000 tickets lose, but you are certain that one wins, so your credence in that conjunction is 0. So a threshold view of belief, with belief closed under conjunction, would make you believe something you are certain is false. Either belief is not closed under conjunction or belief is not just high credence.
