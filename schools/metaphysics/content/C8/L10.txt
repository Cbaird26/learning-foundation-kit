@article
You know your date of birth, that Australia is a continent, and that the Earth is billions of years old. For almost none of these have you verified the facts yourself: you were told. Much of what any of us knows depends on other people. **Testimony**, in epistemology, is the transmission of belief by assertion: you come to believe that $p$ because someone told you $p$. The central question is when testimony justifies belief.

### Reductionism and anti-reductionism

**Reductionism** holds that testimonial justification reduces to other sources: perception, memory and inference. To be justified in believing a speaker, you need positive reasons for thinking she is reliable, such as past experience of how reports and facts correlate. David Hume's discussion of testimony in his essay on miracles (*Enquiry*, section X) is usually read this way: our confidence in witnesses comes from experience of their conformity to fact. **Global** reductionism needs evidence for the reliability of testimony in general; **local** reductionism (Elizabeth Fricker) requires reasons about *this* speaker on *this* topic.

**Anti-reductionism** holds that testimony is a basic source of justification, like perception. Thomas Reid said that we have a natural tendency to speak truly (a principle of veracity) and a tendency to believe what others say (a principle of credulity). C. A. J. Coady and Tyler Burge defended modern versions: Burge's **acceptance principle** says that one is entitled to accept something presented as true that is intelligible, unless there are stronger reasons not to.

| P1 | Much of what we know (history, geography, our own birthdate) we know only by testimony. |
| P2 | If reductionism were true, we would need independent evidence of the reliability of testimony before testimony could justify us. |
| P3 | We have, and could have, only a small amount of independent evidence for the reliability of testimony: we can check very few reports, and checking mostly uses further testimony. |
| C | So reductionism implies that we know far less than we do; so reductionism is false. |

This is the standard *argument from large-scale dependence*, and is associated with Coady. The argument is valid, and P1 and P3 are open to dispute. A reductionist replies that the amount of checkable testimony is large enough to support a global induction, that we can check coherence among independent reports, and that children are not justified in the way adults are, though they become so. An anti-reductionist worries that this makes ordinary hearers' justification too intellectual.

### Transmission

Does testimony pass on knowledge, so that you know only if the speaker does? Jennifer Lackey's case of a teacher who does not herself believe in evolution but teaches the biology syllabus carefully, because of her professional duty, is a standard challenge. Her students acquire knowledge from reliable materials though the speaker lacks the belief. If this is right, a speaker's knowing or believing is not necessary for her testimony to give knowledge; reliability of the testimony is what matters.

### Experts and trust

When we face experts, we often cannot assess the arguments. Alvin Goldman asked how a novice can choose between experts; he lists clues such as agreement among other experts, track record, apparent interests and biases, and the argumentative performance seen in a debate. A hearer should be more cautious when a speaker has an interest in being believed.

### Epistemic injustice

Miranda Fricker (*Epistemic Injustice*, 2007) identified two wrongs. **Testimonial injustice** occurs when prejudice leads a hearer to give a speaker less credibility than she deserves. **Hermeneutical injustice** occurs when a gap in shared interpretive resources puts someone at a disadvantage in making sense of their own experience, as when a group lacks a word for a kind of harm it suffers. These cases show that trust in testimony is not only a question of evidence, but also of fairness.

### The jury theorem

When many people report on the same yes/no question, can we trust the majority? The **Condorcet jury theorem** (1785) states: if each of an odd number $n$ of voters is independently correct with the same probability $p > 1/2$, then the probability that the majority is correct increases as $n$ grows and approaches 1. If $p < 1/2$, the probability that the majority is correct falls as $n$ grows. The assumptions are strong: **independence** (each voter's reliability does not depend on the others) is often violated when people share sources, and **competence above chance** may fail.

@example
title: A jury of three
problem: Three advisers independently answer a yes/no question. Each is right with probability 0.6. The majority answer is taken. What is the probability that the majority is right?
step: Assumptions: the advisers are independent, each has probability 0.6 of being right, and the majority of three decides. The majority is right if all three are right or exactly two are right.
step: All three right: $0.6^3 = 0.216$. Exactly two right: there are three ways to choose which two, so $3 \times 0.6^2 \times 0.4 = 0.432$.
step: Adding: $0.216 + 0.432 = 0.648$. A single adviser has 0.6, so the majority of three does better.
answer: 0.648
@example
title: Reductionist and anti-reductionist verdicts
problem: A visitor asks a passer-by for directions to the station. The passer-by says "second left." The visitor has no evidence about this person but sees no sign of insincerity. Compare the verdicts of local reductionism and of Burge's acceptance principle.
step: Local reductionism requires positive reasons about this speaker: she appears competent, no cues of lying, and people in general give correct directions in such circumstances, so the visitor may have such reasons without noticing them.
step: The acceptance principle says that the visitor is entitled to accept what she is told unless there are stronger reasons not to, and there are none.
step: Both views therefore count the visitor as justified, but they explain it differently. They diverge in a case with no background reasons, such as a child's trust, where only the anti-reductionist counts the belief as justified.
answer: Both give justification here; they differ in whether the justification requires positive reasons.
@mistakes
- Saying that anti-reductionism holds that testimony is always justified: defeaters can remove the entitlement.
- Thinking the jury theorem shows that majorities are always right: it needs independence and competence above chance.
- Describing epistemic injustice as a matter of speakers being wrong: it concerns unfair credibility deficits and gaps in interpretive resources.
- Confusing global and local reductionism: global concerns the reliability of testimony in general and local concerns this speaker on this topic.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why the independence assumption of the jury theorem matters for the epistemology of expert consensus, with an example.
hint: Ask what happens if the voters share one source.
solution: The theorem's result depends on voters' errors being independent. If many experts all rely on one flawed study or a common source, their agreement is little more than one opinion repeated, and a large majority is not much better than one adviser. For example, if twenty commentators each repeat the same unchecked report, their agreement gives almost no extra reason to believe it. So when assessing consensus, a hearer should ask how independent the sources are, not just how many there are.
