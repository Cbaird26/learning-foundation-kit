@article
Ask a child "why?" enough times and the answers run out. You believe the bridge is safe because the sign says it passed inspection; you believe the sign because inspections are posted by the council; you believe that because... At some point you either stop, circle back, or keep going. This is the **epistemic regress problem**, and it is the central question about the *structure* of justification. The ancient skeptical tradition summarized it in the **Agrippan trilemma**: a chain of reasons must end dogmatically, go on forever, or run in a circle. (The Pyrrhonist Sextus Empiricus reports five modes of this kind, which Diogenes Laertius credits to Agrippa and his followers.)

### The regress argument

A belief is **inferentially justified** if its justification depends on other beliefs from which it is inferred or on which it is based, and **non-inferentially justified** (or **basic**) if not.

| P1 | Some beliefs are justified, and an inferentially justified belief is justified only if the beliefs it is based on are themselves justified. |
| P2 | The chain of justifying beliefs behind any belief must end in beliefs not justified by other beliefs, or circle back on itself, or go on without end. |
| P3 | A circular chain cannot justify anything, since a belief cannot support itself. |
| P4 | No finite thinker has an endless chain of justifying beliefs. |
| P5 | The beliefs at the end of a chain of justification are themselves justified (otherwise the chain would pass on no justification). |
| C | So some beliefs are justified non-inferentially. |

The argument is valid, and each rival theory of justification rejects a premise. The **skeptic** accepts the structure but rejects P5, and with it the claim in P1 that some beliefs are justified: the chains end in unjustified beliefs, so nothing is justified. The **coherentist** rejects P3. The **infinitist** (Peter Klein) rejects P4 and says that justification comes from an endless supply of available reasons.

### Foundationalism

**Foundationalism** accepts the conclusion: justification has the structure of a building, with **basic beliefs** at the base and inferential beliefs above them. Two versions matter.

- **Classical foundationalism**, associated with Descartes and many empiricists, requires basic beliefs to be certain, infallible or self-evident: for instance, a belief about one's present experience, or an elementary truth of logic.
- **Modest foundationalism** allows basic beliefs to be fallible and defeasible. A perceptual belief such as "there is a door in front of me" can be justified by the experience that prompts it, without inference, and yet could be overturned by further evidence.

The main objections: that no beliefs are certain enough for the classical version, and that classical foundations support too little to yield the rest of what we know. Against both, it is said that picking basic beliefs is arbitrary unless something explains why they, and not others, are justified. A deeper objection, Sellars's dilemma, is discussed in week 8.

### Coherentism

**Coherentism** rejects the building picture. Justification is holistic: a belief is justified by belonging to a system of beliefs that **cohere**. Coherence is more than consistency: it includes mutual support, explanatory connections, and the absence of unexplained anomalies. Laurence BonJour defended a coherence theory in *The Structure of Empirical Knowledge* (1985), and Keith Lehrer developed another. The coherentist answers the regress by denying that support is linear: beliefs support each other in a web, and this is not the vicious circle of P3.

The standard objections:

1. **The isolation objection.** A system could be perfectly coherent yet disconnected from the world, like a consistent and detailed novel. If coherence alone justified, a coherent fantasy would be justified.
2. **The alternative-systems objection.** There may be many incompatible, equally coherent systems. Coherence cannot tell us which to believe.
3. **The input problem.** Experience seems to give us reasons, yet on a pure coherence theory experience is not an independent source of justification.

Coherentists reply that a suitable coherence theory can include beliefs about one's own reliability and spontaneous observational beliefs as part of the system, so that coherence is not disconnected from experience.

### Summary

| | what justification looks like | what it rejects in the argument |
|---|---|---|
| Foundationalism | finite chains ending in basic beliefs | nothing (it accepts the conclusion) |
| Coherentism | mutual support within a system | P3 |
| Infinitism | endless chains of available reasons | P4 |
| Skepticism | chains ending in unjustified beliefs | P5 |

@example
title: Classifying structures of support
problem: Three thinkers describe how Tomas's belief that the lake is frozen is justified. (X) It rests on his belief that the surface is white and hard, which he forms directly on looking and which needs no further support. (Y) It rests on the belief that the ice looks thick, which rests on his belief that the lake is frozen, and both rest on a large network of beliefs about weather, past winters and the lake. (Z) It rests on a reason, which rests on a further reason, which rests on a further one, with no last reason. Name the theory each describes.
step: In (X), the chain stops at a belief that needs no further support: a basic belief. This is foundationalism.
step: In (Y), support runs in several directions inside a system and includes the belief itself supporting its own supports: holistic mutual support. This is coherentism, and the objector must show it differs from the vicious circle of premise P3.
step: In (Z), there is no end to the chain and no circle: this is infinitism, which rejects premise P4.
answer: (X) foundationalism, (Y) coherentism, (Z) infinitism.
@example
title: The isolation objection applied
problem: Tessa has constructed a large set of beliefs about a life as an airship navigator in an invented country: maps, routes, weather, colleagues. Her beliefs are consistent and strongly interconnected. She has never checked them against anything and none is caused by observation. Does a pure coherence theory count them as justified, and what does that show?
step: A pure coherence theory makes justification depend only on the relations among the beliefs. These are highly coherent, so the theory counts them as justified.
step: Intuitively they are not justified, since they are cut off from the world. This is the isolation objection.
step: A reply: coherence must include the believer's observational beliefs and her track record, and Tessa's system, which has no input, is not coherent in the required sense. Whether this reply keeps the theory coherentist is disputed.
answer: A pure coherence theory seems to count the beliefs as justified, which most take to be a mistake; coherentists respond by building experience into the system.
@mistakes
- Treating coherence as mere consistency: coherentists require mutual support and explanatory connection.
- Saying foundationalists claim that basic beliefs are unjustified: basic beliefs are justified, but not by other beliefs.
- Equating foundationalism with classical foundationalism: modest versions allow fallible basic beliefs.
- Saying coherentism accepts the vicious circle of P3: the coherentist denies that holistic support is a circle of the kind P3 rules out.

@practice self
prompt: In a short paragraph, explain how infinitism differs from coherentism in its response to the regress argument, and state one objection to infinitism.
hint: Identify which premise of the regress argument each view rejects.
solution: The infinitist rejects premise P4: a belief is justified by an endless chain of reasons, each of which is available to the thinker even if she never actually reaches the end. The coherentist rejects premise P3: support is holistic, running among beliefs in a system, so that mutual support is not vicious circularity. One objection to infinitism is that finite minds cannot have infinitely many beliefs, or at least cannot actually hold reasons for each belief in an endless series; the infinitist replies that it is enough that further reasons are available and that justification comes in degrees as the chain is extended.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate the claim that the alternative-systems objection is more damaging to coherentism than the isolation objection. State both objections, and give a view with the best reply to it.
hint: Say what each objection shows about what coherence can and cannot do.
solution: The isolation objection says that a coherent system could be completely cut off from the world; the alternative-systems objection says that there could be many incompatible systems, each equally coherent. I suggest that the alternative-systems objection is the more serious one because the coherentist can answer the isolation objection by building observation into the system, but cannot so easily explain why coherence picks out one system over its equally coherent rivals, which is what a theory of justification must do. A coherentist can reply that the rivals are unlikely to be equally coherent once observational input is counted, and that we need only claim that justification is relative to the system we have. This reply gives up some of the force of calling the beliefs justified, not merely coherent.
