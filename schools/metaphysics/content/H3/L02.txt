@article
Imagine a point that starts at $(1, 0)$ and runs counterclockwise around a circle of radius $1$ centred at the origin. After it has travelled an arc length $\theta$, it sits at a point we call $P(\theta)$. Because the radius is $1$, arc length and angle in radians are the same number, so $P(\theta)$ is simply "the point at angle $\theta$".

### Definition

The **unit circle** is the set of points with $x^2 + y^2 = 1$. For any angle $\theta$ in standard position, the terminal side meets the circle at exactly one point, and we **define**

$$\cos\theta = x\text{-coordinate}, \qquad \sin\theta = y\text{-coordinate}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}.$$

So $P(\theta) = (\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Since this point lies on the circle, for every $\theta$

$$\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta = 1.$$

The quantity $\tan\theta$ is the slope of the terminal side. It is undefined where $\cos\theta = 0$, that is at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$.

### The exact values to know

Two right triangles inscribed in the first quadrant give everything:

- **$\theta = \frac{\pi}{4}$:** an isosceles right triangle with hypotenuse $1$ has two equal legs $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.
- **$\theta = \frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\frac{\pi}{3}$:** halving an equilateral triangle of side $1$ gives a right triangle with sides $\frac{1}{2}$, $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and hypotenuse $1$.

| $\theta$ | $0$ | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $\cos\theta$ | $1$ | $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $0$ |
| $\sin\theta$ | $0$ | $\frac{1}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ | $1$ |

A memory aid: the sines are $\frac{\sqrt{0}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{1}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}, \frac{\sqrt{4}}{2}$, and the cosines are the same list in reverse.

### Every other angle: reference angles and signs

The circle is symmetric under reflection in both axes, so every angle has the same coordinates as an acute angle, up to signs. The **reference angle** $\alpha$ is the acute angle between the terminal side and the $x$-axis. To evaluate at any angle:

1. Find the quadrant and the reference angle $\alpha$.
2. Read $\cos\alpha$ and $\sin\alpha$ from the table.
3. Attach signs to $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$: Quadrant I is $(+,+)$, II is $(-,+)$, III is $(-,-)$, IV is $(+,-)$.

For $\theta = \frac{5\pi}{6}$ (Quadrant II) the reference angle is $\pi - \frac{5\pi}{6} = \frac{\pi}{6}$, so $\cos\theta = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin\theta = +\frac{1}{2}$.

### Reciprocal functions

$$\sec\theta = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}, \qquad \csc\theta = \frac{1}{\sin\theta}, \qquad \cot\theta = \frac{\cos\theta}{\sin\theta}.$$

Coterminal angles give the same point, so $\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta$ and $\cos(\theta + 2\pi) = \cos\theta$.

@example
title: Exact values at a third-quadrant angle
problem: Find the exact values of $\cos\frac{7\pi}{6}$, $\sin\frac{7\pi}{6}$ and $\tan\frac{7\pi}{6}$.
step: Locate the angle: $\pi < \frac{7\pi}{6} < \frac{3\pi}{2}$, so it is in Quadrant III, where both coordinates are negative.
step: The reference angle is $\frac{7\pi}{6} - \pi = \frac{\pi}{6}$, with $\cos\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{\pi}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$.
step: Attach the Quadrant III signs: $\cos\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$ and $\sin\frac{7\pi}{6} = -\frac{1}{2}$.
step: Divide: $\tan\frac{7\pi}{6} = \frac{-1/2}{-\sqrt{3}/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}$. The two negatives cancel, so the tangent is positive in Quadrant III.

@example
title: Finding cosine from sine and the quadrant
problem: The angle $\theta$ lies in Quadrant II and $\sin\theta = \frac{3}{5}$. Find $\cos\theta$ and $\tan\theta$.
step: Use the Pythagorean identity: $\cos^2\theta = 1 - \sin^2\theta = 1 - \frac{9}{25} = \frac{16}{25}$.
step: Taking roots gives $\cos\theta = \pm\frac{4}{5}$. The identity cannot decide the sign; the quadrant does.
step: In Quadrant II the $x$-coordinate is negative, so $\cos\theta = -\frac{4}{5}$.
step: Then $\tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} = \frac{3/5}{-4/5} = -\frac{3}{4}$.

@mistakes
- Using the reference angle's value without adjusting the sign for the quadrant.
- Swapping sine and cosine. Remember $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta) = (x, y)$: at $\theta = 0$ the point is $(1, 0)$, so $\cos 0 = 1$ and $\sin 0 = 0$.
- Measuring the reference angle from the positive $x$-axis instead of from the nearest part of the $x$-axis. The reference angle of $\frac{5\pi}{6}$ is $\frac{\pi}{6}$, not $\frac{5\pi}{6}$.
- Dropping the sign when solving $\cos^2\theta = \frac{16}{25}$. There are two roots and the quadrant picks one.

@quiz num
prompt: Find the exact value of $\sec\frac{5\pi}{3}$.
answer: 2
check: 1/cos(5*pi/3)
hint: Secant is the reciprocal of cosine. Find $\cos\frac{5\pi}{3}$ first.
hint: $\frac{5\pi}{3}$ is in Quadrant IV with reference angle $\frac{\pi}{3}$, and cosine is positive there.
solution: $\cos\frac{5\pi}{3} = \cos\frac{\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{2}$ (positive in Quadrant IV), so $\sec\frac{5\pi}{3} = \frac{1}{1/2} = 2$.

@quiz num
prompt: The point $\left(-\frac{8}{17}, y\right)$ lies on the unit circle with $y > 0$. It is the point at angle $\theta$. Find $\tan\theta$.
answer: -15/8
check: sqrt(1 - (8/17)**2)/(-8/17)
hint: First find $y$ from $x^2 + y^2 = 1$, choosing the sign that matches $y > 0$.
hint: Then $\tan\theta = y/x$.
solution: $y^2 = 1 - \frac{64}{289} = \frac{225}{289}$, so $y = \frac{15}{17}$. Then $\tan\theta = \frac{15/17}{-8/17} = -\frac{15}{8}$.
