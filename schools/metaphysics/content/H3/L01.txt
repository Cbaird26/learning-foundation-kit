@article
An **angle** measures an amount of rotation. To compare angles we place them in **standard position**: the vertex sits at the origin and the **initial side** lies along the positive $x$-axis. Rotating the **terminal side** counterclockwise gives a positive angle; rotating clockwise gives a negative one.

### Two ways to count a turn

You already know **degrees**: one full turn is $360^\circ$, a number chosen long ago because it divides evenly in so many ways. Mathematics and physics prefer a unit that comes straight from the circle itself.

Take a circle of radius $r$ and walk along its edge a distance equal to the radius. The angle you have swept out at the centre is **one radian**. More generally, if an arc of length $s$ subtends a central angle $\theta$, then

$$\theta = \frac{s}{r}.$$

Both $s$ and $r$ are lengths, so a radian is a ratio of lengths: a pure number. We write "rad" only for clarity.

### Converting

The whole circumference is $2\pi r$, so one full turn is $\theta = 2\pi r / r = 2\pi$ radians. Hence

$$2\pi\text{ rad} = 360^\circ, \qquad \pi\text{ rad} = 180^\circ.$$

To convert, multiply by a form of $1$:

$$\text{degrees to radians: } \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ}, \qquad \text{radians to degrees: } \times \frac{180^\circ}{\pi}.$$

A sanity check: $1\text{ rad} \approx 57.3^\circ$, so the number of degrees is always the larger number in size. Memorise these anchors; every other common angle is a multiple of one of them.

| Degrees | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ | $90^\circ$ | $180^\circ$ | $270^\circ$ | $360^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Radians | $\pi/6$ | $\pi/4$ | $\pi/3$ | $\pi/2$ | $\pi$ | $3\pi/2$ | $2\pi$ |

### Arc length and sector area

An angle $\theta$ (in radians) takes up the fraction $\theta/2\pi$ of a full circle. The arc length and the area of the **sector** (the pie slice) are that same fraction of the circumference and of the disc area:

$$s = \frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot 2\pi r = r\theta, \qquad A = \frac{\theta}{2\pi}\cdot \pi r^2 = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta.$$

These tidy formulas are the reason radians exist. They are valid **only** when $\theta$ is in radians.

### Coterminal angles

Angles that differ by whole turns end on the same terminal side. The angles $\theta$ and $\theta + 2\pi k$, for any integer $k$, are **coterminal**. To find the representative in $[0, 2\pi)$, add or subtract multiples of $2\pi$.

```python
import math
print(math.radians(150))             # about 2.618, which is 5*pi/6
print(round(math.degrees(math.pi/6), 6))   # 30.0
print(-17*math.pi/4 % (2*math.pi))   # about 5.498, which is 7*pi/4
```

Python's `%` returns a result in $[0, 2\pi)$ here, which is exactly the "add turns until it fits" step.

@example
title: Arc length and sector area from a degree measure
problem: A circular sector has radius $12$ cm and central angle $150^\circ$. Find its arc length and its area.
step: Convert the angle to radians: $150^\circ \times \frac{\pi}{180^\circ} = \frac{5\pi}{6}$.
step: Arc length: $s = r\theta = 12\cdot\frac{5\pi}{6} = 10\pi \approx 31.4$ cm.
step: Area: $A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta = \frac{1}{2}(144)\cdot\frac{5\pi}{6} = 60\pi \approx 188.5\ \text{cm}^2$.
step: Check with fractions of a circle. Since $150/360 = 5/12$, the sector is $\frac{5}{12}$ of the circle. The circumference is $24\pi$ and $\frac{5}{12}(24\pi) = 10\pi$. The disc area is $144\pi$ and $\frac{5}{12}(144\pi) = 60\pi$. Both agree.

@example
title: A coterminal angle in one turn
problem: Find the angle in $[0, 2\pi)$ that is coterminal with $-\frac{17\pi}{4}$.
step: Coterminal angles differ by multiples of $2\pi = \frac{8\pi}{4}$. The angle is negative, so add whole turns until it is non-negative.
step: One turn gives $-\frac{9\pi}{4}$ and two turns give $-\frac{\pi}{4}$. Both are still negative.
step: Three turns give $-\frac{17\pi}{4} + \frac{24\pi}{4} = \frac{7\pi}{4}$, which lies in $[0, 2\pi)$. As a check, $-\frac{17\pi}{4}$ is $-765^\circ$ and $-765^\circ + 3(360^\circ) = 315^\circ = \frac{7\pi}{4}$.

@mistakes
- Using $s = r\theta$ or $A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ with $\theta$ in degrees. Convert to radians first.
- Multiplying by $\frac{180}{\pi}$ when you should multiply by $\frac{\pi}{180}$. Remember that a radian measure is usually the smaller number.
- Leaving a calculator in degree mode while working in radians, or the reverse.

@quiz num
prompt: The minute hand of a clock is $9$ cm long. How far does its tip travel in $20$ minutes? Give an exact answer.
answer: 6pi
check: 9*(2*pi*20/60)
unit: cm
hint: What fraction of a full turn does the minute hand sweep in $20$ minutes?
hint: That fraction of $2\pi$ is the angle in radians. Then use $s = r\theta$.
solution: Twenty minutes is $\frac{1}{3}$ of an hour, so $\theta = \frac{2\pi}{3}$. Then $s = r\theta = 9\cdot\frac{2\pi}{3} = 6\pi \approx 18.85$ cm.
