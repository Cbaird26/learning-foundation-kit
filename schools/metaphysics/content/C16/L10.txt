@article
Why do societies have the laws, religions and ideas they have? A medieval manor and a modern factory town differ not only in what they produce but in what people think they owe each other. Karl Marx proposes an answer: the way a society produces its means of life shapes, in the end, its political institutions and forms of consciousness. This is **historical materialism**. It is a theory of history, and it also marks Marx's break with Hegel.

### From Hegel to Marx

Marx began among the Young Hegelians, who drew critical conclusions from Hegel's philosophy, and he was influenced by Feuerbach's claim that religion projects human powers onto God. In the *Theses on Feuerbach* (written in 1845, published by Engels in 1888) Marx says that earlier materialism treated the world only as an object of contemplation, and not as a field of human practice, and that philosophers have only interpreted the world while the point is to change it. In the Afterword to the second German edition of *Capital* (1873) he says that Hegel's dialectic stands on its head and must be turned right side up: its "rational kernel" is to be freed from its mystical, idealist shell. For Hegel ideas move history; for Marx the real basis is human productive activity.

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts* of 1844 (published only in 1932) Marx describes **alienation** under capitalist wage labor in four forms: from the product of labor, from the activity of laboring, from one's species-being (the capacity for free, conscious, creative production) and from other people.

### Historical materialism

In the Preface of 1859, Marx summarizes the theory. At a given stage, people enter **relations of production** that correspond to the stage of development of their **productive forces** (tools, skills and labor power, and raw materials). The sum of the relations of production is the economic structure, the real foundation, on which a legal and political **superstructure** rises, and to which definite forms of social consciousness correspond. Social being determines consciousness, not the reverse. When the forces develop beyond what the relations can accommodate, the relations become fetters, and an epoch of social revolution begins.

One influential reconstruction, in G. A. Cohen's *Karl Marx's Theory of History: A Defense* (1978), adds that the productive forces tend to develop over time, since people are rational and face scarcity, and that the relations of production are as they are because they promote that development, an explanation by the function they serve. Cohen's version of the argument:

| P1 | The relations of production persist when and because they promote the development of the productive forces. |
| P2 | The legal and political superstructure persists when and because it stabilizes the relations of production. |
| P3 | If the forces explain the relations, and the relations explain the superstructure, then the forces explain the superstructure. |
| C | So the development of the productive forces ultimately explains the character of the legal and political superstructure. |

The argument is valid: P1 says that the forces explain the relations, P2 that the relations explain the superstructure, and P3 completes the chain. Objections attack different premises. Jon Elster, in *Making Sense of Marx* (1985), argued that functional explanations of the P1 kind are acceptable only when a mechanism is given, and that Marx does not supply one: this attacks **P1**. Defenders of the theory reply that evolutionary selection of relations that fit the forces could supply such a mechanism. Against **P2**, Engels, in a letter to Joseph Bloch (1890), said that the economic element is only the ultimately determining one (*in letzter Instanz*, the phrase usually rendered "in the last instance"), not the only determining one, and that political, legal and ideological factors act back on the base; if the superstructure has real independent influence, then it is not explained simply by the relations. Against **P3**, one can say that "explains" in this sense need not be transitive. Interpreters also disagree about whether Cohen's technological and functional reading is Marx's own view, or whether class struggle and human practice play a more central part.

### Further disputes

Marx's analysis of **commodity fetishism** in *Capital* (1867) says that under commodity production social relations between people appear as relations between things. Interpreters disagree whether Marx thought capitalism unjust: Allen Wood argued that Marx did not criticize it for injustice, since justice is relative to a mode of production, and Norman Geras and others argued that Marx did hold that it was unjust. This is an exegetical dispute, and not a question of whether one should accept Marx's politics.

### Two breaks with Hegel

| Question | Schopenhauer | Marx |
|---|---|---|
| What replaces Hegelian spirit? | A blind, irrational will, the thing in itself. | The material activity of production and its social relations. |
| View of history | No rational progress: the same striving repeats. | Developmental, driven by changes in the productive forces and class conflict. |
| What is the basic problem? | The suffering inherent in willing. | Alienation and exploitation in particular social forms. |
| Response | Contemplation, compassion, denial of the will. | Transforming the social order through practice. |
| Place of reason | Subordinate to the will. | Social and historical, shaped by conditions of life. |

@example
title: Base and superstructure in an invented society
problem: In the island society of Marel, farmers use iron plows and oxen, and nearly all land is owned by a few families who rent it to tenants for a share of the harvest. A court enforces the tenancy contracts, and a priesthood teaches that the landlords' status is divinely ordained. Sort these items into forces of production, relations of production and superstructure, in the vocabulary of the 1859 Preface.
step: The plows, oxen and farmers' skills are productive forces: the instruments and labor power of production.
step: The ownership of land by a few families and the tenancy for a share of the harvest are relations of production: who controls the forces and how the product is shared.
step: The court that enforces the contracts is part of the legal superstructure, and the priesthood's doctrine is a form of social consciousness, an ideological form.
step: On Cohen's reconstruction, the relations of production are explained by their fit with the development of the forces, and the court and the doctrine by the way they stabilize the relations. Whether that explanation is right in this case is an empirical question.
answer: Forces: plows, oxen, skills. Relations: land ownership and tenancy. Superstructure: the court and the doctrine.

@example
title: Which premise does Engels's remark attack?
problem: A critic points to Engels's letter of 1890: political, legal and ideological factors act back on the economic base, and the economic element is determining only in the last instance. Which premise of Cohen's argument does this attack, and what is the reply?
step: Identify what the argument claims about the superstructure. P2 says that the superstructure persists when and because it stabilizes the relations of production, which makes the superstructure explained by the relations.
step: If the superstructure has real, independent effects on the economy and is partly explained by its own dynamics, then P2 is false as an exhaustive explanation. So the remark attacks P2.
step: A defender replies that the argument concerns what ultimately explains the form of the superstructure, and that independent influence is compatible with ultimate economic explanation. The critic answers that "ultimately" must then be spelled out, or it becomes an empty qualification.
answer: The remark attacks P2. The reply appeals to the difference between ultimate explanation and independent influence.

@mistakes
- Calling historical materialism the claim that only matter exists. It is a theory of social explanation, and Marx's materialism concerns the role of material production.
- Reading "social being determines consciousness" as saying that ideas have no effects. Interpreters, including Engels in his late letters, allow that they act back.
- Identifying Marx's break with Hegel with Schopenhauer's. Schopenhauer drops rational history for an irrational will, and Marx keeps a developmental history based on production.
- Treating "alienation" as a feeling of boredom. In the 1844 Manuscripts it names four relations in which workers are separated from product, activity, species-being and other people.

@practice self
prompt: Compare Schopenhauer's and Marx's breaks with Hegel in eight to ten sentences. Say what each replaces Hegel's spirit with, what each says about history, and what each takes the central human problem to be.
hint: Use the table of the lesson: what replaces spirit, view of history, the basic problem, and the response.
solution: Hegel holds that reason is realized in history through the development of spirit. Schopenhauer replaces spirit with a blind, irrational will, which is the thing in itself, and denies that history makes rational progress: it repeats the same striving. For him the central problem is the suffering inherent in willing, and the responses are aesthetic contemplation, compassion and the denial of the will. Marx replaces spirit with human material production and its social relations, and keeps a developmental view of history, driven by changes in the productive forces and by class conflict. For him the central problem is alienation and exploitation in particular social forms, and the response is to change those forms through practice. Both reject Hegel's idea that the real is the rational, but Schopenhauer ends in resignation and Marx in a program of transformation. One disagreement between them is whether the problem is a feature of existence as such or of a historical order.

@quiz self
prompt: In six to eight sentences, say how the phrase "in the last instance" bears on the argument from forces of production to superstructure, and state one reason a critic might still find the argument unsatisfying.
hint: Consider whether ultimate explanation is compatible with real independent influence of the superstructure.
solution: The phrase allows the superstructure to have real influence on the economy, while holding that in the long run the character of the legal and political order is explained by the economic base. That helps the argument by letting it avoid the crude claim that the superstructure is a mere reflex. It also weakens it: P2 can no longer say that the superstructure is explained only by the relations of production, so the argument must say what "ultimately" adds. A critic might say that if independent factors can shape the outcomes in any given case, the claim of ultimate explanation becomes hard to test, and so does the argument's conclusion. A defender can say that the claim is testable at the level of long historical patterns, for example by asking whether changes in productive forces predict changes in legal and political forms.
