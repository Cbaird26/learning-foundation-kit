The aim is to practice what this course has taught: stating an argument so that it is valid as written, saying which premise an objection attacks, and reporting disagreements between interpreters as disagreements.
goal: Reconstruct a Kantian argument in standard form, valid as written, with every hidden premise made explicit.
goal: Present a nineteenth-century response accurately, attributing it to the right philosopher and text, and say exactly which premise or step it attacks or replaces.
goal: Evaluate the response with reasons, giving the strongest reply for each side and reporting interpretive disputes fairly.
step: Choose one argument from material A. Read the relevant part of the Critique through the course reading (Meiklejohn's translation is free) and at least one encyclopedia entry or article found through PhilPapers, and note where your reconstruction differs from the version supplied.

step: Choose a response from material B, or one of your own that you can support from the sources. Read the response in a primary text or in a reliable secondary source, and write down in two sentences what it claims and which premise or step of your reconstruction it targets.
step: Draft the paper using the plan in material C. Use your own words: paraphrase unless you are sure of the exact wording of a public-domain text, and give the location (a section, chapter or standard page reference) for every claim about a text.
step: Evaluate. State the best objection to your chosen response and the best reply to that objection, and say whether the response succeeds, partly succeeds or fails, and why. Where interpreters disagree (for example, over two-world and two-aspect readings of Kant, or over how to read Hegel), say so and say how your evaluation depends on the choice.
step: Revise. Check that each paragraph does one job, that every term (analytic, a priori, category, appearance, thing in itself) is used in the sense you gave, that no premise changes meaning between its first and last appearance, and that you have cited what you used.
material: **A. Three arguments to choose from.** Each is valid as written. You may improve on them, but then explain what you changed and why.

**The transcendental deduction (B edition, simplified)**

| P1 | For any manifold of intuition to be mine, it must be possible for me to be aware, with "I think", of it as belonging to one consciousness. |
| P2 | It is possible for me to be aware of a manifold as belonging to one consciousness only if I synthesize it, that is, combine it according to rules. |
| P3 | Combining a manifold according to rules into the unity of one consciousness is bringing it to the objective unity of judgment, so that it represents an object. |
| P4 | Whatever is brought to the unity of judgment stands under the categories, so that the categories apply to the objects it presents. |
| P5 | Every object of experience for us is given in a manifold of intuition that can be mine. |
| C | So the categories apply necessarily to every object of our experience. |

**The second analogy (simplified)**

| P1 | To represent a succession as objective, we must represent the order of our perceptions as determined by the object and not up to us. |
| P2 | We can represent that order as determined by the object only if we represent the later state as following the earlier necessarily, according to a rule. |
| P3 | Representing the later state as following the earlier necessarily according to a rule is representing the two states as causally connected. |
| C1 | So to represent any objective succession, we must represent its states as causally connected. |
| P4 | All our experience of alterations is experience of objective successions. |
| P5 | What we must represent as true of every objective succession in experience holds of all objects of our experience. |
| C | So every alteration among the objects of our experience takes place according to the law of cause and effect. |

**The refutation of idealism (simplified)**

| P1 | I am conscious of my own existence as determined in time. |
| P2 | I can be conscious of my existence as determined in time only if I perceive something permanent, against which succession and change are determined. |
| P3 | No representation in me can be that permanent. |
| P4 | If the permanent that I perceive is not any representation in me, then it is a thing existing outside me, and not a mere representation of one. |
| C | So there exists a permanent thing outside me, in space. |

## Review: Kant and nineteenth-century philosophy

### Week by week

1. **The project of critique.** Analytic and synthetic (content), a priori and a posteriori (justification), the central question of how synthetic a priori judgments are possible, the Copernican turn.
2. **The Aesthetic.** Intuitions and concepts, space and time as forms of sensibility, empirical reality and transcendental ideality, the neglected alternative and non-Euclidean geometry.
3. **The deduction.** The categories, the question of right, the unity of apperception, Henrich's two-step reading of the B edition (one reading among several), Stroud's objection.
4. **The analogies.** Persistence, succession and coexistence; the second analogy as a reply to Hume; Strawson's non sequitur.
5. **The refutation and the paralogisms.** Problematic and dogmatic idealism, the argument from the permanent, the equivocation in the first paralogism.
6. **Transcendental idealism.** Two-world and two-aspect readings, noumenon in the negative and positive senses, the affection problem.
7. **Fichte and Schelling.** Dogmatism and idealism, the self-positing I, the check, the summons, philosophy of nature, the identity of subject and object.
8. **Hegel.** Determinate negation, sublation, the metaphysical and non-metaphysical readings, lordship and bondage.
9. **Schopenhauer.** Representation and will, the argument from the body, pessimism, three routes of relief.
10. **Marx.** Alienation, forces and relations of production, base and superstructure, the argument for explanatory primacy.
11. **Kierkegaard and Nietzsche.** Existence and the system, the stages, the true world as a fable, genealogy, perspectivism.
12. **Mill and the pragmatists.** Induction and the methods, the pragmatic maxim, truth for Peirce and James, the will to believe.

### Key skills

- State an argument so that it is valid as written, with the hidden premise added, and say which premise each objection attacks.
- Report disagreements between interpreters (two-world and two-aspect readings of Kant, metaphysical and non-metaphysical readings of Hegel, how to read Nietzsche's perspectivism) as disagreements, and do not make one the answer.
- Attribute views carefully: Trendelenburg's neglected alternative, Stroud on transcendental arguments, Strawson on the second analogy, Jacobi on the thing in itself, Elster on functional explanation.

### Self-check

- Can you explain why "synthetic a priori" is not a contradiction?
- Can you state the second analogy's argument from memory and say what Hume would deny?
- Can you give the two-aspect and two-world answers to the affection problem?
- Can you say how Schopenhauer's and Marx's breaks with Hegel differ?
- Can you apply the pragmatic maxim to a new concept?

### Common errors

- Confusing analytic/synthetic with a priori/a posteriori.
- Treating "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" as Hegel's method.
- Calling "the leap of faith" Kierkegaard's phrase, or treating The Will to Power as a book Nietzsche published.
- Reading the pragmatic maxim as demanding actual verification, and identifying James's view of truth with Peirce's.
- Writing "objection" for what is really a rejection of the premises' form: an objection to a valid argument attacks a premise.

@exam mc
prompt: Kant holds that the principle that every event has a cause is a condition of experiencing objective succession and is known a priori. Mill, by contrast, holds that
choice*: the law of universal causation is itself established by induction from experience.
choice: causal laws are known by pure reason alone.
choice: the causal principle is analytic.
choice: there are no causal regularities.
hint: Mill's empiricism extends to the most general laws, including the law of causation.
solution: In the System of Logic Mill treats even the law of universal causation as a generalization from experience, which is the opposite of Kant's claim that it is a priori. Mill is a regularity theorist and does not deny that there are causal regularities, and neither he nor Kant takes the principle to be analytic.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of these statements about the thing in itself are accurate? Select all that apply.
choice*: Fichte holds that a consistent idealism must dispense with the thing in itself as the cause of sensation.
choice*: Schopenhauer holds that the thing in itself is will, known through the inner awareness of one's own body.
choice*: On the two-aspect reading, things in themselves are the same objects as appearances, considered independently of our cognitive conditions.
choice: Kant holds that the categories give us knowledge of things in themselves.
choice: Peirce defines truth as agreement with things in themselves.
hint: Two of the statements misreport a philosopher's view.
solution: Fichte rejects the thing in itself as the cause of sensation, Schopenhauer identifies it with the will, and the two-aspect reading holds that appearances and things in themselves are the same objects considered in two ways. Kant denies that the categories give knowledge of things in themselves, and Peirce's account of truth is the opinion fated to be agreed on at the end of inquiry.

@exam self
prompt: Does the thing in itself threaten the coherence of Kant's transcendental idealism? In an essay of 350 to 450 words, state the affection argument in standard form (valid as written), explain how a two-world interpreter and a two-aspect interpreter each respond, and say how Fichte takes the problem. Do not treat either reading as the settled answer.
hint: Use premises about what Kant says of affection, what affection is, the limits on the categories, and what the things in themselves are not.
solution: The affection argument runs: P1, Kant holds that sensation is produced by things in themselves affecting our sensibility; P2, affecting is a causal relation; P3, causality is a category that Kant holds may legitimately be applied only to objects of possible experience; P4, things in themselves are not objects of possible experience; C, so Kant is not entitled, by his own principles, to assert that things in themselves affect us. The argument is valid, so the question is which premise to deny. The two-aspect interpreter, following Prauss and Allison, denies P1 as stated: what affects us is the empirical object, one and the same thing considered in two ways, so no extra thing in itself is needed. The cost is that Kant's talk of things in themselves as the ground of sensation must be read as loose, and that the claim that things in themselves are not spatial seems stronger than an epistemic distinction can support. The two-world interpreter may deny P2, saying that affection is a relation of ground that does not use the schematized category, or restrict P3 to cognition, so that the thought of a ground is allowed. The cost is a notion of ground that Kant does not explain and that seems to be causation under another name. Fichte accepts the point: an explanation of experience that appeals to something outside possible consciousness is inconsistent with the Critique, so he derives experience from the activity of the I, with a check on its striving. Critics say that the check is the thing in itself under another name. Neither reading is the consensus. So the problem is most pressing on the two-world reading, where its force depends on whether the replies succeed; the two-aspect reading avoids it at what some take to be the cost of weakening the claims about things in themselves, and others deny that there is any such cost.
