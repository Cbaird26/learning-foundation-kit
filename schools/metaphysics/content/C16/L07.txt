@article
Within a decade of the Critique, readers pressed a problem. Kant says that sensation arises when things in themselves affect us, yet he also says that nothing can be known of them and that the categories of cause and ground do not apply beyond experience (week 6). G. E. Schulze and Salomon Maimon pressed versions of the objection, and Jacobi's complaint became famous. **German idealism** is the family of systems that tries to keep Kant's insight, that the mind contributes to the structure of experience, while removing the thing in itself and finding a single first principle.

### Fichte: philosophy from the I

Johann Gottlieb Fichte, in the *Wissenschaftslehre* ("Science of Knowledge", first presented 1794–95), says that Kant's system lacks a single principle, and builds one on the activity of the I. In the later First Introduction (1797) he contrasts two ways of explaining experience. **Dogmatism** explains the content of consciousness from a thing independent of it, and, Fichte argues, can never account for consciousness itself. **Idealism** explains experience from the activity of intelligence. Fichte holds that neither can refute the other by argument alone, since each starts from a different first principle, and that the choice depends on what sort of person one is, and in particular on whether one's sense of freedom is stronger than one's attachment to things. He chooses idealism.

The first principle is not a fact but an act: the I **posits itself**. It is a **Tathandlung** (deed-act), the self-making activity that the I consists in, in contrast to a **Tatsache**, a given fact. The I then posits a not-I in opposition, and posits within the I a limited I and a limited not-I. Fichte says that the I's activity is a striving without end, and that experience arises when this striving meets a limit, a check (**Anstoß**) that the I does not itself produce. The check lets him say that the I is finite without bringing back a thing in itself as its cause. Critics say that the check is the thing in itself under another name; defenders say that it is only the condition under which the I's activity becomes conscious of itself, and is no entity at all. The primacy of the practical is central: the world is first of all the field of the I's moral task, and theoretical knowledge is its form.

The argument against the thing in itself, as Fichte and the critics press it, goes like this:

| P1 | The thing in itself, as Kant introduces it, is meant to explain the matter of experience as its ground. |
| P2 | Being the ground of something in experience is a relation that holds only between items within possible consciousness. |
| P3 | The thing in itself lies outside all possible consciousness. |
| C | So the thing in itself cannot be the ground of the matter of experience, and no explanation of experience can appeal to it. |

The argument is valid. A defender of Kant can deny **P2**, saying that Kant's restriction applies to cognition and that the relation of ground can be thought of a thing in itself. A critic of Fichte can accept all this and argue that his own check does the same job. Kant himself, in a public declaration of 1799, disowned the *Wissenschaftslehre* as an interpretation of his philosophy.

Fichte also argues that self-consciousness is social. In *Foundations of Natural Right* (1796) he says that a finite rational being becomes self-conscious as a free agent only when it is **summoned** (the *Aufforderung*) by another rational being to act freely. The idea, together with the idea of recognition, is often thought to have influenced Hegel's treatment of recognition (week 8).

### Schelling: nature and the Absolute

F. W. J. Schelling began as a follower of Fichte and then criticized him. In works from 1797 to 1799, including *Ideas for a Philosophy of Nature*, he argues that nature cannot be a mere obstacle or not-I: it is productive and self-organizing, and the same activity is at work in nature and in mind. His *System of Transcendental Idealism* (1800) starts from the I and traces the stages by which it becomes conscious of itself, and ends with art: the work of art shows the identity of conscious and unconscious activity that philosophy can only describe. In the **identity philosophy** (the *Presentation of My System*, 1801) he says that the Absolute is the indifference or identity of subject and object, ideal and real, and that nature and mind are two expressions of it. In the Preface to the *Phenomenology* (1807) Hegel complains of an Absolute in which all differences vanish, a night in which all cows are black. The phrase is commonly read as aimed at Schelling's identity philosophy, though Hegel does not name him. In later work, including the 1809 essay on human freedom and the Berlin lectures, Schelling turns to will, freedom and the existence of things, and criticizes Hegel's logic as a merely "negative" philosophy of essences.

@example
title: Locating the objection to the thing in itself
problem: A Kantian says, "Kant never claims to know the thing in itself. He only thinks it, as a ground of sensation, and a thought is not a use of a category for cognition." Which premise of the argument above does this deny, and what is the cost?
step: List the premises. P1 says the thing in itself is meant as the ground of the matter of experience, P2 that being a ground holds only between items within possible consciousness, P3 that the thing in itself lies outside possible consciousness.
step: The Kantian says that the relation of ground can be thought of the thing in itself though not used for knowledge. That denies P2: a ground need not be within possible consciousness in order to be thought.
step: The cost: the thought of a ground that explains, and so is used in the account of experience, begins to look like cognition. Fichte would say that Kant uses the relation when he says that the thing affects us.
answer: The reply denies P2. The cost is that thinking of a thing in itself as a ground begins to look like using the category to explain.

@example
title: Fichte's argument for the summons
problem: Fichte's reasoning about other persons can be put in valid form: P1, a finite rational being is self-conscious only if it takes itself to be a free agent whose action is its own. P2, it can take itself to be a free agent only if it has a determinate sphere of possible actions that it did not itself fix and that was given to it without compelling it. P3, a sphere of possible actions that is given to a free being without compelling it can be given only by a summons from another rational being to act freely. C, so a finite rational being is self-conscious only if it is summoned by another rational being. Which premise would a critic deny who says that the world itself can present options without anyone else?
step: Check validity. P1 and P2 give a need for a given sphere of actions, and P3 says that the only non-compelling source is another rational being.
step: The critic says that obstacles and opportunities in nature can also be given without compelling the agent, so the source need not be a person. That denies P3's claim that only a summons can do it.
step: Fichte's reply is that natural obstacles merely cause; a summons addresses the agent as free and invites a response. Whether this is persuasive depends on whether the difference is more than a difference in how we describe the case.
answer: The critic denies P3. The reply appeals to the difference between causing and summoning.

@mistakes
- Saying that Fichte believes that the individual person creates the world. The I is not an individual's mind but the activity of rational self-positing, and the check is not produced by it.
- Treating the thesis-antithesis-synthesis triad as the key to Fichte's text. He uses the language of positing and opposing, and reducing his method to a formula obscures his argument.
- Calling Schelling's identity philosophy a version of Fichte's. Schelling gives nature an independent positive role, which Fichte's not-I lacks.
- Saying that Hegel named Schelling in the remark about the night in which all cows are black. The target is commonly read as Schelling's Absolute, though the text does not name him.
