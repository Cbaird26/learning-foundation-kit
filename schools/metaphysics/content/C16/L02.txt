@article
Imagine two surveyors who disagree about a field's boundaries but never about whether the angles of a triangle drawn on it add up to two right angles. Geometry seems to hold of anything we can measure, and to be knowable from the armchair. Kant takes that combination to need an explanation. The **Transcendental Aesthetic** (from the Greek *aisthēsis*, perception) gives it: space and time are not features of things independent of us, and not concepts we abstract from experience, but the forms of our own sensibility.

### Intuitions and concepts

Kant distinguishes two sources of cognition. **Sensibility** is our receptive capacity to be affected by objects, and its representations are **intuitions**: immediate and singular, like seeing this patch of red. **Understanding** is our capacity to think objects through **concepts**: general and mediate, like the concept of red. Neither suffices alone. Intuitions without concepts are blind, and concepts without intuitions are empty (a slogan developed in week 3). The Aesthetic asks what in intuition is contributed by us rather than given by sensation. The **matter** of appearance is sensation; the **form** is what lets the manifold be ordered in relations. Kant argues that the form is space for outer sense and time for inner sense, and that each is a **pure intuition**: a priori and yet singular.

### Arguments about space

Kant's "metaphysical exposition" of space (usually counted as four arguments) shows that space is a priori and an intuition. A transcendental exposition then explains how geometry is possible. Here is the first argument, that space is not derived from outer experience, in valid form:

| P1 | Having outer experience requires representing objects as outside me and as in different places. |
| P2 | Representing things as in different places requires already having the representation of space. |
| C1 | So having outer experience requires already having the representation of space. |
| P3 | A representation required for having outer experience cannot be derived from outer experience. |
| P4 | Every representation is either derived from experience or a priori. |
| C | So the representation of space is a priori. |

Premises P1 and P2 give C1; C1 and P3 give that space is not derived from experience; P4 then gives the conclusion. The "a priori" here means presupposed by experience, not innate. Objection to P2: an empiricist can say that tactile and visual sensations, together with bodily movement, build up the representation of place, so no prior representation of space is needed. Objection to P3: the learner may say that a capacity can be needed for experience and still be developed through earlier experience. Kant would reply that the objection already uses spatial ideas in describing the movement it appeals to.

The third and fourth arguments, that space is an intuition and not a discursive concept, say we represent only one space, whose parts are limitations of it, not pieces from which it is composed; and we represent it as an infinite given magnitude, which no concept does. The transcendental exposition adds that the propositions of geometry are synthetic and known a priori, and that this is possible only if space is a pure intuition: an a priori form of outer sense.

| P1 | The propositions of geometry are synthetic and are known a priori with necessity. |
| P2 | If geometry's propositions are synthetic and known a priori, there is an a priori intuition of space on which they rest. |
| P3 | An intuition can be had a priori, before any object is given, only if it is the mere form of our sensibility. |
| C | So space is the form of our outer sensibility. |

### Consequences: empirical reality and transcendental ideality

If space is the form of our outer intuition, geometry holds necessarily of all objects of outer experience: that is the **empirical reality** of space. But space tells us nothing about things in themselves: it is **transcendentally ideal**. Kant's position differs from Newton's, for whom space is an absolute container, and from Leibniz's, for whom space is an order of relations among things. On time, Kant argues in parallel that it is the form of inner sense, our way of intuiting ourselves and our states, and, since all representations are states of mind, the condition of all appearances.

### Objections

The **neglected alternative** objection is associated with Adolf Trendelenburg: the arguments show that space is a priori, but not that it is *only* a form of intuition. Space might be both an a priori form of our intuition and a real feature of things in themselves. This attacks P3 of the geometry argument above, and it presses on the step from "a priori" to "merely subjective". A second objection concerns **non-Euclidean geometry**. Geometries that drop Euclid's parallel postulate were developed in the nineteenth century, and general relativity describes spacetime with a non-Euclidean geometry. This attacks P1 of the geometry argument if it is meant to cover Euclid in particular: Kant took Euclidean geometry to be necessarily true of space. Replies distinguish pure geometry from claims about physical space, or propose, with later neo-Kantians and in more recent work by Michael Friedman, a "relativized" a priori. Whether these replies preserve what Kant wanted is disputed.

@example
title: A parallel argument for time
problem: Kant argues that time, too, is not derived from experience. Put in valid standard form the argument that simultaneity and succession could not be perceived unless the representation of time were already given, and identify the premise an empiricist would deny.
step: State the claim. Perceiving that two events are simultaneous or that one follows another requires representing them in a temporal order. So the representation of time is presupposed by perceiving temporal relations.
step: Write the argument.
```text
P1  Perceiving events as simultaneous or successive requires representing them in time.
P2  Representing events in time requires already having the representation of time.
C1  So perceiving temporal relations requires already having the representation of time.
P3  A representation required for perceiving temporal relations cannot be derived from perceiving them.
P4  Every representation is either derived from experience or a priori.
C   So the representation of time is a priori.
```
step: Check validity. P1 and P2 give C1; P3 shows time is not derived from experience; P4 excludes every alternative but a priori.
step: An empiricist denies P2 or P3: the sense of temporal order might be built up from the felt order of memories and sensations, so it need not be presupposed. A Kantian reply is that remembering an order as an order is already a temporal representation.
answer: The argument has the same form as the argument for space. The empiricist denies P2 or P3.

@example
title: Which premise does the objection target?
problem: A critic says, "Non-Euclidean geometries are consistent and general relativity treats physical space as non-Euclidean, so Euclidean geometry is not necessarily true of space." Which premise of the transcendental exposition does this attack, and what is one reply?
step: The transcendental exposition begins from the claim that the propositions of geometry are synthetic and known a priori with necessity. Kant's examples are Euclidean.
step: The objection says that Euclidean claims are not necessary truths about physical space, so it attacks P1 (the starting point), which makes the geometry argument unsound if the propositions are not necessarily true of space.
step: One reply says that pure geometry is a body of consequences of axioms, which can be analytic, while the claim that physical space is Euclidean is empirical. Kant's defender then has to say what remains synthetic a priori about space itself.
answer: The objection attacks P1. One reply separates pure geometry from the geometry of physical space.

@mistakes
- Saying that Kant thinks space is "all in the head" and does not exist. He holds that space is empirically real: geometry applies to every object we can experience.
- Confusing an intuition with a hunch. For Kant an intuition is an immediate, singular representation, such as the representation of one space.
- Taking the neglected alternative to refute Kant's claim that space is a priori. It targets the further claim that space is only a form of our sensibility.
- Reading "a priori" in the Aesthetic as "innate idea". Kant says that the representation of space is presupposed by experience, and does not say that it is present from birth.

solution: The objection says that Kant's arguments show space to be a priori, but not that it is only a form of our sensibility: space might be both a priori in us and a real feature of things in themselves. The dispute is a standard example of an objection that identifies a gap in one step of an argument without refuting its other premises.
