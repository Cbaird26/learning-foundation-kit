@article
Kant said that we should examine our faculty of knowledge before relying on it. Hegel asked how that could be done. To examine knowledge we must already know something, and he compared the demand to refusing to enter the water until one has learned to swim. His alternative, in the *Phenomenology of Spirit* (1807), is to follow forms of consciousness as they test themselves by their own standards and, by failing, give way to richer forms. The book's route runs from the simplest claim to knowledge up to what Hegel calls absolute knowing.

### The method of the Phenomenology

Each **shape of consciousness** claims to know its object and holds a standard for success. When the claim fails by that standard, the failure is not empty: it is a **determinate negation**, a negation of a particular content that points to a specific next shape. Consider sense-certainty, the claim that the richest knowledge is of the immediate "this, here, now". Write "now it is night". Later, when the sentence is read at noon, it is false, and "now" turns out to be a general term that applies to every time. The claim to give particular knowledge fails on its own terms, and consciousness moves to perception, which deals in general properties. The main stages are consciousness (sense-certainty, perception, understanding), self-consciousness, reason, spirit, religion and absolute knowing.

### Dialectic and sublation

In the *Encyclopedia Logic* (§§79–82) Hegel describes logical thought as having three moments: the understanding, which fixes determinations; the **dialectical** moment, in which each determination turns into its opposite; and the **speculative** moment, which grasps the unity of the opposites. The movement is called **sublation** (*Aufhebung*), a word that means both cancelling and preserving, and also lifting up. The popular formula "thesis, antithesis, synthesis" is not Hegel's own description of his method. Kant used "thesis" and "antithesis" for the two sides of an antinomy, Fichte used a triad of positing, opposing and uniting, and later commentators turned it into a label for Hegel. A standard example from the *Science of Logic* (1812–16) is the move from being to nothing to becoming: pure being, with no determination, is indistinguishable from nothing, and the passage of each into the other is becoming.

### The absolute and the disagreement about it

In the Preface to the *Phenomenology* Hegel says that the true is the whole, and that substance must be grasped as subject. Interpreters disagree about what this commits him to. On the **traditional metaphysical reading**, associated with Charles Taylor among others, Hegel holds that reality is the self-development of a cosmic spirit that comes to know itself through finite minds and history. On the **non-metaphysical or post-Kantian reading**, advanced by Robert Pippin and Terry Pinkard in different forms, Hegel continues Kant's project of examining the categories, and the absolute is the self-justifying system of concepts, whose life is a social practice of giving and asking for reasons, not a mind outside nature. Each reading has to explain the texts the other cites, and neither is a consensus. After Hegel's death the school divided: the "right" Hegelians read the system as compatible with Christianity and the existing state, and the "left" or Young Hegelians drew critical conclusions from it (this is where Marx in week 10 begins).

### Lordship and bondage

In the chapter on self-consciousness (§§178–196), two self-consciousnesses meet. Each wants to be recognized by the other as an independent being, and each stakes its life to prove that it is not bound to mere life. One yields and becomes the bondsman, and the other becomes the lord. The standard reconstruction:

| P1 | A self-consciousness is satisfied only if it is recognized as an independent being by another self-consciousness. |
| P2 | Recognition satisfies a self-consciousness only if it comes from one whom it recognizes as itself independent. |
| P3 | The lord treats the bondsman as a dependent thing, and is recognized only by him. |
| C1 | So the lord does not get the recognition that would satisfy him. |
| P4 | The bondsman, through fear and through the formation of things in labor, comes to see his own activity embodied in the world, and so becomes aware of himself as a source of form. |
| P5 | The bondsman is not recognized as independent by the lord. |
| C | So neither position satisfies the demand for recognition by an independent other, and what P1 and P2 require is mutual recognition. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 together say that satisfaction requires recognition between independent beings, P3 and P5 say that neither position has it, and C1 is its first application. Disputes concern each premise. A critic can deny **P1**, saying that self-consciousness needs no recognition from another and that the lord can secure what he wants by the bondsman's labor. A critic can deny **P2**, saying that the lord can be satisfied by the recognition of an inferior, an objection Nietzsche's picture of the strong (week 11) would sharpen. **P4** makes the bondsman's position the more advanced, which some find a surprising and unargued reversal. Interpreters also disagree about the status of the story. Alexandre Kojève's influential lectures of the 1930s read it as an account of a historical struggle and as the engine of human history, and many interpreters, such as Pippin, read it as an analysis of the conceptual structure of recognition and not a historical event.

@example
title: Determinate negation in a new case
problem: Consider the claim of perception: "this salt is a thing with several properties, white and cubic and tart, and the thing is one." Using the idea of determinate negation, state what such a claim is committed to and how a Hegelian would say it fails by its own standard.
step: State the claim and its standard. Perception takes an object to be one thing that has many properties. Its standard is that the thing is one and the properties many.
step: Test it. Each property (white, cubic, tart) is a general feature that other things share, and the thing seems to be only their collection. Yet the thing must also be exclusively one. Consciousness oscillates between taking the object as one and as many.
step: This failure is a determinate negation: it tells consciousness exactly what was wrong, the opposition of unity and plurality in the object, and so points to the next shape, which considers forces and laws as what unifies the manifold.
answer: The claim that the object is one thing with many properties fails on its own standard, and the failure points to the shape of understanding.

@example
title: Which premise does the objection attack?
problem: A critic says: "The lord does not need recognition from the bondsman, and wants only obedience and enjoyment. So the demand for recognition is not part of self-consciousness." Which premise of the argument above does this attack, and what could a Hegelian reply?
step: Find the premise that says what self-consciousness requires. P1 says that satisfaction requires recognition by another self-consciousness.
step: The critic denies this, saying that obedience and enjoyment are enough. That is a denial of P1.
step: A Hegelian replies that the lord seeks obedience as a sign that he is independent, and that by Hegel's analysis independence is a matter of how one is regarded by others who count, which is why he stakes his life. The critic can say that this assumes the very thing in dispute.
answer: The critic denies P1. The Hegelian reply says that the lord's demand shows that he does seek recognition.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hegel's method is thesis, antithesis, synthesis. That formula is not Hegel's description; he speaks of moments, determinate negation and sublation.
- Reading sublation as plain cancellation. The word means both cancelling and preserving.
- Reading lordship and bondage as a defense of slavery or as a straightforward history of masters and slaves. It is a stage in the development of self-consciousness, whose interpretation is disputed.
- Treating the metaphysical and non-metaphysical readings of the absolute as the textbook Hegel. They are competing interpretations.

He becomes aware of himself as a source of form, which the lord, who only consumes, does not. Yet the bondsman is not recognized as independent either. @quiz self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, state Hegel's objection to Kant's idea that we should examine our faculty of knowledge before using it, and give a reply a Kantian might make.
hint: Think of the swimming analogy and what examining knowledge already requires.
solution: Hegel argues that examining the faculty of knowledge before using it would require a knowledge that is the very thing under examination, so the project is incoherent: it is like refusing to enter the water until one can swim. His alternative is to examine knowledge as it appears in the shapes of consciousness, each tested by its own standard. A Kantian can reply that Kant's critique is not a prior act of knowing but reason's self-examination, which uses reason's own resources, and that this is exactly the kind of internal test Hegel recommends. The Hegelian can respond that Kant's results, such as the limits set by the thing in itself, are imposed from outside the shapes of consciousness.
