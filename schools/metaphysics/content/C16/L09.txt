@article
When you decide to raise your arm and it rises, you are aware of the event in two ways. You can look at the arm as a body among bodies, moving through space and following causal laws. You are also aware of the rising from the inside, as your own doing. Arthur Schopenhauer builds a metaphysics on this double access. His main work, *The World as Will and Representation* (first published 1818–19, with a second edition in 1844 that added substantial supplements), starts from Kant and ends with a very different picture of what lies behind appearances.

### Representation

"The world is my representation": for Schopenhauer this is a truth that holds for every living being, though only humans can raise it to reflective consciousness. He accepts Kant's transcendental idealism: space, time and causality are forms of the subject's representation, and every object stands under the **principle of sufficient reason** (the topic of his earlier *Fourfold Root*, 1813), so there is no object without a subject. He cuts Kant's table of twelve categories down, holding that causality is the only category the understanding really needs, along with space and time, and he is sharply critical of Kant's elaborate architecture.

### The break with Kant: will as thing in itself

Kant says we can never know things in themselves. Schopenhauer says there is one exception: our own body. It is given to us as representation, and also from within, in the immediate awareness of our actions as acts of will. So we know what the body is in itself, and the thing in itself is **will**. The reasoning, in valid form:

| P1 | I know my body in two ways: from outside, as an object among objects in space, and from within, as the immediate awareness of my actions as acts of will. |
| P2 | The inner awareness of my action as will gives me knowledge of what my body is in itself, and not only as appearance. |
| C1 | So my body, in itself, is will. |
| P3 | As representation, my body is an object like any other body, and it differs from other bodies in no way that would make it the only body with an inner nature. |
| P4 | If my body is in itself will and other bodies do not differ from it in kind as representations, then other bodies are also in themselves will. |
| C | So the thing in itself, throughout nature, is will. |

The argument is valid. It is a version of an argument by analogy, and each of its premises is disputed. **P2** is the most exposed: Schopenhauer himself grants that even our inner awareness of will is in time, and that it is therefore not a completely unveiled knowledge of the thing in itself; a Kantian objects that the claim to know anything of the thing in itself breaks Kant's limits, and a critic can say that inner awareness is already interpreted by concepts. **P3** and **P4** are attacked as anthropomorphic: to infer from one case, my own body, that gravity or crystal growth is will stretches the word beyond any content it has in my experience. A defender replies that "will" is used for the inner nature of striving in general, not for conscious desire, but critics ask whether this leaves the claim empty.

### What the will is like

For Schopenhauer the will is blind, without goal, without ground and not subject to the principle of sufficient reason. It is one: space and time are the **principle of individuation**, so the will, which lies outside them, is not divided into many things. It appears at grades of objectification, from forces of nature through plants and animals to human beings, and the grades correspond to what he calls Platonic Ideas, the timeless forms in which the will is represented.

### Pessimism

If the world is will, the central fact of life is striving. The argument for pessimism:

| P1 | All life consists of striving, that is, of willing. |
| P2 | All willing springs from lack or need, and lack is suffering. |
| P3 | Satisfaction is only the removal of a lack: it brings no lasting positive good, and is followed by new willing or by boredom. |
| C | So life, as willing, is essentially suffering, and its satisfactions do not redeem it. |

The argument is valid. Critics deny **P2**, pointing to willing that arises from abundance, such as play, or that is itself enjoyable, and deny **P3**, saying that positive pleasures exist. Defenders reply that these apparent exceptions presuppose some prior lack.

### Relief and the break with Hegel

Schopenhauer offers three ways out. In **aesthetic contemplation** (above all in music) we are absorbed in an Idea and for a time freed from willing. In **compassion**, which he treats as the basis of morality, we see through the principle of individuation and recognize the same will in others. In **asceticism** the will turns against itself and denies itself. Schopenhauer rejected Hegel's philosophy: for him the essence of the world is an irrational will, not a rational spirit coming to know itself, and history shows no progress toward freedom, only the repetition of the same striving. Marx (week 10) also breaks with Hegel, but in the opposite direction, from idealism to the material conditions of history, and he keeps a view of history as development.

title: Locating an objection to the body argument
problem: A critic says: "It is a leap from my own case to gravity and chemical affinity. For all I know, striving is a feature of animals only, and calling the fall of a stone 'will' is a metaphor." Which premise of the argument above does this attack, and how might a Schopenhauerian reply?
step: List the steps from my own case to nature at large. C1 is about my body. P3 says other bodies do not differ in kind as representations, and P4 says that if so, they too are in themselves will.
step: The critic accepts the inner awareness of striving in my own case, and denies that it can be generalized to nonliving things. That targets P4, and also P3 if stones are said to differ from living bodies in the relevant respect.
step: The Schopenhauerian replies that "will" names the inner nature of any striving force, so the generalization is not metaphorical but a claim that all appearances have a common inner nature.
step: The critic can respond that this makes "will" cover so much that it has lost its explanatory content.
answer: The critic attacks P4 (and P3). The reply stretches the sense of "will", which the critic says empties the claim.

@example
title: Testing the pessimism argument
problem: A critic says: "A child building a sand castle for its own sake wills without any lack, and enjoys the activity." Which premise of the pessimism argument does this attack, and what does the defender say?
step: Identify the premises about the source of willing. P2 says that all willing springs from lack or need, and lack is suffering.
step: The child's building is willing, yet is said to spring from no lack. That denies P2.
step: The defender replies that even play has a prior lack, such as restlessness or a wish to do something, so the premise stands. The critic says that this stretches "lack" until P2 can no longer be tested.
step: The argument remains valid, so the dispute is about whether P2 is true.
answer: The critic attacks P2. The defender says that play also springs from a lack, and the critic says that this makes P2 unfalsifiable.

@mistakes
- Treating Schopenhauer's will as a conscious plan or God-like agent. The will is blind and aimless.
- Saying that he rejects Kant entirely. He accepts transcendental idealism about space, time and causality and revises the claim that the thing in itself is unknowable.
- Equating his pessimism with a recommendation of suicide. He rejects suicide as an affirmation of the will in its individual form, and offers contemplation, compassion and asceticism.
- Confusing the aims of his break with Hegel and Marx's: Schopenhauer rejects rational progress in history, while Marx keeps a developmental view of history.

hint: Start with the two ways I know my body, move to what the inner awareness gives, and then to the step that extends it to nature.
solution: Schopenhauer notes that I know my body in two ways: as an object in space among others, and from within, as my own acts of will. Since the second access is not a representation of an external object, he argues that it reveals what my body is in itself, namely will. Because my body is, as representation, an object like others, he infers that every body is also in itself will, so the thing in itself throughout nature is will. The strongest objection is that the extension from my own body to inanimate nature is a leap: from a single case of conscious striving it infers an inner nature for gravity or crystal formation, and the word "will" is stretched to a point where it no longer says anything. A defender replies that will in the argument is not conscious desire but the inner nature of striving in general. The critic answers that this does not show that stones strive.
