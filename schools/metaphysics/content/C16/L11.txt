@article
Hegel's system promised to comprehend everything, including the history of thought and religion. Two thinkers answered it from different directions. Søren Kierkegaard said that a person who exists cannot be absorbed into a system, because being one's own existing self is a task and not a topic. Friedrich Nietzsche said that the idea of a true world behind appearances, from Plato to Kant, is a symptom to be diagnosed, not a discovery. Both are critics of metaphysics, but neither is simply an enemy of thinking, and each is easy to caricature.

### Kierkegaard: existence and the system

Kierkegaard wrote many works under pseudonyms, so the views of "Johannes Climacus" (author of the *Concluding Unscientific Postscript*, 1846) or "Johannes de silentio" (*Fear and Trembling*, 1843) are not automatically his own. Interpreters disagree about how far to read the pseudonyms as speaking for him. Climacus's argument against Hegelian system-building, in standard form:

| P1 | A system of existence could be given only from a standpoint of completion, outside the process of existing. |
| P2 | Every human thinker is an existing individual, in the midst of existing, and cannot take a standpoint outside it. |
| C | So no human thinker can give a system of existence. |

The argument is valid. Climacus accepts that a *logical* system is possible, since logic abstracts from existence, and denies that a system of existence is. A Hegelian denies **P1**: Hegel says that philosophy comprehends the actual after the fact, and absolute knowing is not a standpoint outside time; the point is that thought can comprehend its own history. Interpreters also disagree about the target. Some think Kierkegaard aimed at Hegel himself, and others at the Danish Hegelians of his day; some hold that Hegel never claimed a completed system of existence in the sense P1 requires.

Kierkegaard distinguishes three **stages** or spheres of existence: the aesthetic (living for enjoyment and the interesting), the ethical (living by universal duty and commitment) and the religious. *Fear and Trembling* uses the story of Abraham and Isaac to raise the question whether there can be a "teleological suspension of the ethical", and presents faith as a paradox that reason cannot mediate. His claim that "truth is subjectivity" in the *Postscript* is easily read as relativism, but many interpreters hold that it is about how an individual must relate to truths that concern existence, such as faith. The phrase "leap of faith" is common in summaries, but interpreters note that it is not Kierkegaard's own wording, though he speaks of a leap.

### Nietzsche: the true world as a fable

Nietzsche attacks "the prejudices of philosophers": in *Beyond Good and Evil* (1886) he suggests that metaphysicians believe in opposite values, and asks what the will to truth is for. In *Twilight of the Idols* (1888), the section "How the 'True World' Finally Became a Fable" sketches the history of an error in stages, from Plato's true world attainable by the sage, through Christianity and Kant's unknowable thing in itself, to the abolition of the distinction between the true and the merely apparent world. In *The Gay Science* (1882, §125) a madman announces that God is dead. The claim is not a piece of theology but a cultural diagnosis: belief in God has ceased to be credible, and the moral and metaphysical structure that rested on it is threatened.

In the *Genealogy of Morality* (1887) Nietzsche traces moral values to their origins in ressentiment and the ascetic ideal. The argument, put in valid form:

| P1 | The belief in a true world beyond appearance arose from motives of ressentiment and a wish to devalue life, not from the pursuit of truth. |
| P2 | If a belief arose from motives that do not track truth and are hostile to life, we have reason to be suspicious of its claim to truth. |
| C | So we have reason to be suspicious of the belief in a true world. |

The conclusion is only that suspicion is warranted. The standard objection, the **genetic fallacy**, says that the origin of a belief is irrelevant to whether it is true. It attacks **P2**. A defender replies that origin matters when it shows that the belief was not formed by a process sensitive to truth, as a debunking argument does, and that the argument does not claim to show falsity. A different objection attacks **P1**: Nietzsche's historical story is contested, and genealogy is an empirical claim that requires evidence.

### Perspectivism and the will to power

Nietzsche says in the *Genealogy* (third essay, §12) that there is only a perspectival seeing and knowing, and that more eyes give a more complete objectivity. Interpreters disagree: Maudemarie Clark argues that in his later work he rejects the thing in itself while keeping an ordinary notion of truth, and others read him as denying that there are any truths. The **will to power** is also disputed. *The Will to Power* is a compilation from his notebooks, assembled after his death, not a book he published, so interpreters differ over how much weight to give those notes. Some take the will to power to be a metaphysical thesis about all things; others take it to be a psychological hypothesis about human motives. The **eternal recurrence**, presented in *The Gay Science* (§341) as a thought experiment, asks whether one could affirm one's life if it returned, unchanged, endlessly.

@example
title: Testing the argument against a system of existence
problem: A Hegelian says, "Thought does not stand outside history to survey it. It understands the shapes of history by looking back on them, and that comprehension is itself part of history." Which premise of Climacus's argument does this deny, and what could Climacus say?
step: List the premises. P1 says a system of existence needs a standpoint outside the process of existing. P2 says that no human thinker can take such a standpoint.
step: The Hegelian accepts that thinkers are within history and denies that completing a system requires a standpoint outside it. That denies P1.
step: Climacus can reply that comprehension looking back is still unfinished for the individual who exists forward, so that the individual's own existence, in which decisions are still to be made, remains outside the system.
step: The Hegelian can respond that this shifts the claim from the impossibility of a system to the observation that an individual has decisions to make, which Hegel does not deny.
answer: The Hegelian denies P1. Climacus replies that the system cannot include the individual's own unfinished existence.

@example
title: The genetic fallacy and Nietzsche's argument
problem: A critic says, "Even if belief in a true world arose from ressentiment, that does not show it is false. Origins are irrelevant to truth." Which premise does this attack, and what is the best reply?
step: The conclusion of Nietzsche's argument is that suspicion is warranted. The premise that connects origin to suspicion is P2.
step: The critic says that origin is irrelevant to truth, so P2 is false as stated. That targets P2.
step: The reply: the argument does not conclude that the belief is false. It says that if the belief was formed by a process not sensitive to truth, the belief lacks the support its holder supposed it to have, as with a belief produced by wishful thinking.
step: The critic can respond that the claim in P1, that the belief arose in this way, is the contested part, and that independent arguments are needed to show the belief is false.
answer: The critic attacks P2. The reply says the argument is debunking, and does not claim to show falsity.

@mistakes
- Treating the pseudonymous authors as simply Kierkegaard. How to read them is a matter of interpretive dispute.
- Reading "truth is subjectivity" as saying that anything one believes sincerely is true. Interpreters take it to concern how one relates to truths about existence.
- Taking "God is dead" as Nietzsche's announcement that a deity died. It is a diagnosis of a cultural change in belief.
- Treating The Will to Power as a book Nietzsche wrote. It is a posthumous compilation of notebook material.
