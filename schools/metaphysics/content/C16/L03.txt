@article
Suppose the sensations reaching you were so irregular that no concept of substance or cause fitted them. You would still have appearances, but nothing you could call a world of objects. Why must the world that appears to us be one to which our basic concepts apply? Hume gave a reason to doubt that we are entitled to the concept of cause. Kant's **transcendental deduction** is the longest and most difficult argument of the Critique, and it tries to show that the pure concepts of the understanding, the **categories**, apply necessarily to every object of our experience.

### The categories and the question of right

Kant draws the categories from the logical forms of judgment, an argument usually called the **metaphysical deduction**. Each way of judging corresponds to a basic concept of an object in general. The table has four headings, each with three categories.

| Heading | Categories |
|---|---|
| Quantity | unity, plurality, totality |
| Quality | reality, negation, limitation |
| Relation | inherence and subsistence (substance and accident), causality and dependence (cause and effect), community (reciprocity between agent and patient) |
| Modality | possibility and impossibility, existence and non-existence, necessity and contingency |

These concepts do not come from experience, so we cannot justify them by pointing to cases. Borrowing legal vocabulary, Kant separates the question of fact (*quid facti*: how did we come to have the concept?) from the question of right (*quid juris*: by what right do we apply it?). The deduction is an answer to the second question. It needs to show that the categories have **objective validity**: that they apply to objects.

### The idea: unity of apperception

The second edition (B, 1787) builds the deduction on a single fact about thinking. It must be possible for the "I think" to accompany all my representations; otherwise something would be represented in me that could not be thought at all, and so would be nothing to me. This is the **transcendental unity of apperception**. Kant does not claim that it gives knowledge of a substantial soul; it is a formal condition. My representations belong together as mine only if I can combine them, or **synthesize** them, according to rules. Synthesis aimed at a unity of consciousness is what judging does: a judgment brings given representations to the unity of one consciousness as representing an object. The functions of that unity are the categories.

### The argument

| P1 | For any manifold of intuition to be mine, it must be possible for me to be aware, with "I think", of it as belonging to one consciousness. |
| P2 | It is possible for me to be aware of a manifold as belonging to one consciousness only if I synthesize it, that is, combine it according to rules. |
| P3 | Combining a manifold according to rules into the unity of one consciousness is bringing it to the objective unity of judgment, so that it represents an object. |
| P4 | Whatever is brought to the unity of judgment stands under the categories, which are the functions of that unity applied to intuition in general, so that the categories apply to the objects it presents. |
| P5 | Every object of experience for us is given in a manifold of intuition that can be mine. |
| C | So the categories apply necessarily to every object of our experience. |

The argument is valid: P1 to P4 chain from "can be mine" to "stands under the categories", and P5 applies this to every object of experience. In the B-edition, interpreters following Dieter Henrich's influential reading find two steps in one proof: §20 shows that any intuition already unified by apperception falls under the categories, and §26 extends this to all appearances, since the unity of space and time is itself produced by synthesis. In the first edition Kant instead describes a threefold synthesis (apprehension, reproduction in imagination and recognition under a concept), and scholars disagree about how far the two versions agree.

### Where it can be attacked

Each premise has been disputed. **P2** is attacked by someone who says that a unified consciousness might arise from association, which is not rule-governed synthesis. **P3** is challenged by those who think Kant slides from the unity of my consciousness to the unity of an object. **P4** depends on the metaphysical deduction. Because Kant took his table of judgments from the logic of his day, critics doubt that the list of twelve is complete or necessary. **P5** and the conclusion carry the most weight. Barry Stroud, in "Transcendental Arguments" (1968), argued that such arguments show only how we must think or what we must believe, and not how things are. P5 speaks of objects of experience; if these are appearances whose unity is produced by our synthesis, there is no gap, and Kant can reply that the objection assumes the world is independent of our synthesis. But that reply uses transcendental idealism, which is the topic of week 6, and P. F. Strawson, in *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), proposed keeping the argument and rejecting the idealism. Whether that is possible is disputed.

title: Judgment forms and categories
problem: Kant links each form of judgment to a category. Match the following judgments to the heading and category they correspond to: (a) "If the kettle is on the stove, the water will heat." (b) "The water is heating." (c) "Every kettle is made of metal." Explain one way a critic could challenge the match.
step: Find the form of each judgment. (a) is hypothetical (if... then), (b) is categorical (a predicate ascribed to a subject) and (c) is universal.
step: Match them. Hypothetical judgment corresponds to the category of causality and dependence (cause and effect); categorical judgment to inherence and subsistence (substance and accident); universal judgment to totality, under quantity.
step: A critic can say that a hypothetical judgment states a ground and a consequence and need not state a causal connection: "if 4 is even, then 2 divides 4" is hypothetical without any cause. Kant would need to explain how the category differs from the logical form, and the distinction between the form of judgment and the category applied to intuition is meant to do this.
answer: (a) causality (relation); (b) substance and accident (relation); (c) totality (quantity). The challenge: logical form does not obviously fix a unique category.

@example
title: Applying Stroud's objection
problem: A critic says, "The deduction shows that we must think of experience as falling under the categories. It does not show that the world is so." Which premise or step does this attack, and how might a defender of Kant reply?
step: Locate the claim. The objection targets the step from "what is required for my representations to be mine" to "what holds of objects", the step that P5 and the conclusion carry.
step: The objection attacks P5 together with the conclusion: if objects of experience are not constituted by our synthesis, then showing what our synthesis requires does not show how objects are.
step: A defender replies that for Kant objects of experience are appearances, whose order is partly produced by synthesis, so the objection presupposes objects independent of it. The cost is that the argument now relies on transcendental idealism.
answer: The objection attacks P5 and the conclusion. The reply appeals to transcendental idealism, and so shifts the dispute to week 6's question.

@mistakes
- Calling the deduction a proof that the self is a substance. The "I think" is a formal condition of thought, and the Paralogisms (week 5) deny that it yields knowledge of a soul substance.
- Treating the categories as concepts we learn from experience. Their justification cannot rest on experience, which is why the question is one of right.
- Confusing the metaphysical deduction with the transcendental deduction. The first derives the list of categories from the forms of judgment; the second shows that they apply to objects of experience.
- Thinking the deduction claims that every judgment we make is true. It claims that experience of objects requires the categories, not that every use of them is correct.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about six sentences what the "I think" in the B-edition deduction is and is not: say what Kant uses it to prove and what he denies it shows about the self.
hint: Contrast a formal unity of consciousness with a substantial soul.
solution: The "I think" is the thought that must be able to accompany all my representations: the formal unity of self-consciousness, the transcendental unity of apperception. Kant uses it to argue that a manifold can belong to one consciousness only if it is synthesized according to rules, and that this synthesis is judging, whose functions are the categories. It is not an intuition of a persisting self, and it does not show that the self is a substance, simple or an enduring person: the Paralogisms argue that rational psychology goes wrong by treating this formal unity as the knowledge of a thing. The "I think" reveals that I exist as thinking but gives me no further cognition of what I am.
