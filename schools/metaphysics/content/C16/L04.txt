@article
You watch a vase slip from a shelf and shatter. Then you walk along the shelf, looking at the books from left to right. In both cases your perceptions come one after another. But in the first you take the order to belong to the vase: the breaking comes after the falling, and nothing you do could reverse it. In the second you take the order to be an accident of how you looked. Kant's **analogies of experience** claim that this difference rests on a priori principles about substance, causation and interaction, and that experience of objects is impossible without them.

### The principle and the three analogies

The general principle (B edition) is that experience is possible only through the representation of a necessary connection of perceptions. Perceptions alone give no necessity, so the connection must come from rules of the understanding, applied to time. Time cannot be perceived by itself, so the rules are rules for determining the temporal relations of appearances. There are three, one for each mode of time: **persistence**, **succession** and **coexistence**.

| Analogy | Principle (paraphrased) | Mode of time |
|---|---|---|
| First | In all change of appearances substance persists, and its quantity in nature is neither increased nor diminished. | persistence |
| Second | All alterations take place in conformity with the law of the connection of cause and effect. | succession |
| Third | All substances, so far as they can be perceived as coexisting in space, are in thoroughgoing reciprocity. | coexistence |

The first analogy argues that change is possible to represent only against something that persists: an alteration is a change in the states of something that remains, and the permanent is the substratum in which time is determined. The third argues that we can perceive coexistence only if we could perceive things in either order, which requires them to stand in reciprocal causal connection.

### Hume and the second analogy

Hume held that we perceive one event following another but never a necessary connection between them. What we call causation is, on his account, constant conjunction together with a habit of expecting the second event after the first. Kant said that Hume had awakened him from a dogmatic slumber. He agrees that necessary connection cannot be read off perceptions, but denies that this leaves the causal principle without foundation: it is a condition for experiencing events at all.

The second analogy argues like this. Apprehension is always successive, even when the object is not: we take in the parts of a house one after another, and we could have taken them in the opposite order. When a ship moves downstream, the order of our perceptions (upstream position, then downstream position) is fixed by the ship, not by us. We distinguish these two cases. How can we? Only by representing the later state as following the earlier according to a rule.

| P1 | Our apprehension of any manifold is always successive. |
| P2 | We distinguish between a merely subjective succession of perceptions and the perception of an objective succession of states in the object. |
| P3 | If we represent a succession as objective, we must represent the order of our perceptions as determined by the object and not up to us. |
| P4 | We can represent the order of our perceptions as determined by the object only if we represent the later state as following the earlier necessarily, according to a rule. |
| P5 | Representing the later state as following the earlier necessarily according to a rule is representing the two states as causally connected. |
| C1 | So to represent any objective succession, we must represent its states as causally connected. |
| P6 | All our experience of alterations is experience of objective successions. |
| P7 | What we must represent as true of every objective succession in experience holds of all objects of our experience, since objective order in appearances consists in what is so represented. |
| C | So every alteration among the objects of our experience takes place according to the law of cause and effect. |

The argument is valid: P3, P4 and P5 give C1; C1, P6 and P7 give C. Notice what it does not say. It does not say that the particular laws of nature can be known a priori, only that every event falls under some causal law, and it does not claim to derive the idea of necessity from experience. On Kant's view Hume was right that necessity cannot be perceived and wrong to conclude that the causal principle lacks a justification.

### Objections and disagreements

In *The Bounds of Sense* (1966) P. F. Strawson charged the argument with a non sequitur: from the need for an objective temporal order, it does not follow that each event is necessitated by a prior state according to a law. This attacks **P4** (and **P5**). A Humean may also deny P4 by saying that the order of perceptions can be marked as objective by coherence with the rest of our experience, not by necessitating laws. A third objection attacks **P7**: if objects of experience are not constituted by our representations, then what we must represent need not hold of them (the same issue as Stroud's objection in week 3). Interpreters differ about how to reconstruct the argument. Paul Guyer and Henry Allison, among others, offer different versions, and Eric Watkins reads Kant's notion of cause as involving causal powers of substances, not only regular succession.

@example
title: Subjective or objective succession?
problem: Ana looks at the front of a cathedral, moving her eyes from the left tower to the right tower. Later she watches a candle burn down from a tall stub to a flame at the base. Which of these is, on Kant's criterion, a perception of an objective succession, and why?
step: Apply the test: could the order of perceptions have been reversed, at Ana's choice, without a change in the object? The cathedral's towers coexist, and Ana could look right to left.
step: For the cathedral, the order of perception is up to Ana. It is a merely subjective succession, and the towers do not succeed one another.
step: The candle's states (tall, shorter, flame at the base) occur in an order that Ana cannot reverse. Perceiving the flame at the base before perceiving the tall candle would be a different experience, not a different way of looking at the same event.
step: By the second analogy, Ana must represent the candle's later state as following the earlier according to a rule: the burning of wax. She represents a causal connection, though she need not know the physics.
answer: The candle's burning is an objective succession; the cathedral glance is a subjective succession of perceptions of coexisting parts.

@example
title: Which premise does Strawson's objection attack?
problem: A critic says that the second analogy moves from "we need some way of distinguishing objective from subjective order" to "every event must be necessitated by a prior state according to a law", and that this does not follow. Which premise of the argument above is it, and how might a defender respond?
step: Find the step that makes the move. P3 says what we must represent (order not up to us). P4 says we can do so only by representing the later state as following the earlier necessarily according to a rule.
step: The objection says that "not up to us" does not require "necessitated by a rule". It attacks P4. If P4 fails, C1 does not follow from P3 alone, so the argument is unsound.
step: A defender can say that the only alternative to a rule is mere chance, and that if later states followed earlier ones by no rule, then nothing in the object would fix the order of our perceptions. The critic can reply that fixing the order may require only a pattern in the experience, not a necessitating law.
answer: The objection attacks P4. The reply argues that the only alternative to a rule is a lack of determination.

@mistakes
- Saying that Kant agrees with Hume that causation is only habit. Kant agrees that necessity cannot be perceived and denies that the causal principle rests on habit: it is a condition of experience.
- Reading the second analogy as saying that we can know all causal laws a priori. It argues that every event falls under some causal law, and particular laws are found by experience.
- Treating the analogies as claims about things in themselves. They concern objects of experience, as the statement of P7 makes explicit.
- Confusing the three analogies with one another: persistence belongs to the first, succession to the second and coexistence to the third.

@practice self
prompt: Using an example of your own (not a house, a ship, a vase or a candle), explain in six to eight sentences how the second analogy replies to Hume's view of causation. Say what Kant concedes to Hume and what he denies.
hint: Choose a case with a fixed order of events and a case of looking at coexisting parts, and then say what the contrast requires.
solution: Take a drawbridge. When I look from one end of the bridge to the other, the order of my perceptions is up to me: the planks coexist and I could have looked in the other direction. When the bridge rises, I perceive the lowered span and then the raised span, and I cannot reverse the order at will. Kant argues that to distinguish these cases I must represent the raised state as following the lowered state according to a rule, that is, as caused. Hume holds that I observe only a succession and a constant conjunction, and that necessity is a projection of habit. Kant concedes that necessity is not observed in the sequence of perceptions. He denies that this leaves the principle without justification: the principle that every alteration has a cause is a condition of my experiencing an objective order, so it holds necessarily of all objects of experience.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in six to eight sentences the difference between the claim that every event has a cause and the claim that we can know a priori what causes each event, and say which of the two the second analogy is meant to establish.
hint: Think about the difference between the general principle and particular laws of nature.
solution: The claim that every event has a cause is a general principle, and the claim that we can know a priori what causes a given event is a claim about particular laws. Kant's second analogy is meant to establish the first: that every alteration among objects of experience falls under some causal law. It does not claim that we can know in advance which law, so particular causal laws are discovered by experience, though always under the general principle. This answers Hume only in part: it justifies the principle that events have causes and leaves open how we find out which causes. A critic can say that even the general principle is not established, since the argument may show only that we must look for objective order, not that every event is necessitated.
