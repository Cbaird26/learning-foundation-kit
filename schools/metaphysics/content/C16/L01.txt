@article
Mathematics and natural science, Kant thought in the 1780s, had found a secure path: their results accumulate and practitioners agree. Metaphysics, the attempt of reason alone to say what God, the soul and the world as a whole are like, had not. Its history, as he tells it in the first Preface, is a battlefield of rival systems with no settled progress. The **Critique of Pure Reason** (first edition 1781, second edition 1787) asks why. Is the failure bad luck, or does reason have limits that it keeps overstepping?

### What "critique" means

A critique is not an attack on a book or a school. It is a trial of reason's own powers: an inquiry into what reason can know **independently of experience**, and what it cannot. Kant calls this inquiry **transcendental**: it concerns not objects directly but our way of cognizing objects, insofar as that way is possible a priori. The Critique has a doctrine of elements (the Transcendental Aesthetic on sensibility, then the Transcendental Logic, divided into the Analytic and the Dialectic) and a short doctrine of method. Weeks 2 to 5 follow the Aesthetic, the Analytic and the Dialectic in turn.

### Two distinctions

The first distinction is about **knowledge**. A judgment is **a priori** if it can be known independently of all experience; Kant gives two marks of it, strict necessity and strict universality. It is **a posteriori** if it rests on experience. The second is about **content**. In an **analytic** judgment the predicate is already thought in the subject concept: it only unpacks the concept, so denying it contradicts the concept. In a **synthetic** judgment the predicate adds something not thought in the subject. Kant's own examples: "all bodies are extended" is analytic; "all bodies are heavy" is synthetic.

| | a priori | a posteriori |
|---|---|---|
| analytic | yes: unpacking a concept | none (Kant) |
| synthetic | the contested cell | yes: ordinary empirical claims |

Every analytic judgment is a priori, and every empirical judgment is synthetic. The interesting question is the remaining cell. Kant holds that there are **synthetic a priori** judgments, and that they include the propositions of arithmetic ("7 + 5 = 12"), those of geometry, the principles of natural science (the quantity of matter is conserved; every event has a cause) and the claims of metaphysics. Hume had treated the causal principle as neither intuitively certain nor demonstrable, and so as a matter of habit; Kant wants to say how a principle can be informative and yet known without experience.

### The central question and the Copernican turn

Kant's question is: **How are synthetic a priori judgments possible?** His proposal, offered as an experiment and compared to Copernicus, reverses the usual picture. Instead of supposing that our cognition must conform to objects, suppose that objects, as we experience them, must conform to the structure of our cognition. If space, time and the basic concepts are contributed by the mind, then we can know a priori that everything we experience will fit them. The price is steep: we know objects only as they appear to us, not as they are in themselves. Interpreters dispute how apt the Copernican comparison is; the point to keep is the reversal of the order of conformity.

### The argument that metaphysics needs the synthetic a priori

Here is the reasoning behind the project, in valid form:

| P1 | If metaphysics is a genuine science, its judgments extend our knowledge beyond what is already thought in our concepts. |
| P2 | Any judgment that extends our knowledge beyond what is thought in its concepts is synthetic. |
| P3 | If metaphysics is a genuine science, its judgments hold necessarily and universally, independently of experience. |
| P4 | Any judgment that holds necessarily and universally, independently of experience, is a priori. |
| C | So if metaphysics is a genuine science, its judgments are synthetic a priori. |

The argument is valid: P1 and P2 give "synthetic", P3 and P4 give "a priori". Each premise can be denied. A critic of the analytic/synthetic distinction, such as Quine in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), denies the line that P2 relies on. Frege held that arithmetic is analytic, so on his view the extension of knowledge does not require synthetic judgments, which also pushes against P2. Naturalists who treat metaphysics as continuous with empirical science deny P3. And Kripke's later work on the necessary a posteriori, for example that Hesperus is Phosphorus, shows that necessity does not always go with a priori knowability, which puts pressure on P4's tie between the two.

@example
title: Classifying four judgments
problem: Using Kant's two distinctions, classify each judgment: (a) "Every vixen is a fox." (b) "The kettle on my stove is dented." (c) "In Euclidean geometry, the angles of a triangle sum to two right angles." (d) "Every change has a cause." For (c) and (d), say what Kant holds and what a critic could say.
step: Test (a) by asking whether the predicate is already thought in the subject concept. A vixen is a female fox, so "fox" is contained in "vixen". The judgment is analytic, and its truth can be seen without checking any vixens, so it is a priori.
step: (b) The concept of a kettle on my stove does not contain "dented", and I can learn it only by looking. It is synthetic and a posteriori.
step: (c) On Kant's view the concept of a triangle contains the three sides and angles but not their sum, so the judgment is synthetic. It is known with necessity and without measuring, so it is a priori. He therefore counts it as synthetic a priori. A critic can say that in pure geometry the sum follows from the axioms, so it is analytic relative to them, and that whether it is true of physical space is a separate empirical question.
step: (d) For Kant the concept of an event (something happening) does not contain the concept of a cause, so the judgment is synthetic; he holds it is strictly universal and known a priori. A critic in Hume's tradition says that we can conceive an uncaused event without contradiction, so it is not a priori at all.
answer: (a) analytic, a priori. (b) synthetic, a posteriori. (c) and (d) synthetic a priori on Kant's view, each with a standard objection.

@example
title: Which premise does the objection attack?
problem: A critic says, "There is no principled way to separate judgments that merely unpack concepts from judgments that add to them, because what a concept contains is fixed only by the whole web of our beliefs." Which premise of the argument for the synthetic a priori does this attack, and why does the argument stay valid?
step: Locate what the objection targets. It denies that "extends knowledge beyond what is thought in the concepts" marks off a distinct class, which is the distinction between analytic and synthetic.
step: Premise P2 says that any judgment that extends our knowledge beyond the concepts is synthetic. P2 presupposes that the analytic/synthetic line exists, so the objection attacks P2. It also puts pressure on P1, which uses the same notion, but P2 is where the distinction does its work.
step: The argument is still valid. An objection to a premise is a reason to deny that premise, not a flaw in the form of the argument. If P2 is rejected the argument is unsound, and the defender of Kant must either defend the distinction or restate the claim without it.
answer: The objection attacks premise P2 (and the notion in P1). The argument is valid but would be unsound if P2 fails.

@mistakes
- Treating "a priori" and "analytic" as the same thing. Kant's whole point is that some judgments are a priori and synthetic. The first distinction is about how a judgment is known, the second about what its predicate adds.
- Reading "a priori" as "innate" or "before birth". For Kant it means independent of experience in its justification, not earlier in time.
- Reading the Critique as an attack on reason. It is an inquiry into reason's powers and limits, and Kant thinks it secures mathematics and natural science while limiting metaphysics.
- Saying that the Copernican turn means the mind creates the things themselves. The claim is about how objects of experience must conform to our forms of cognition, and how to read it is the topic of week 6.

@practice self
prompt: In four to six sentences, explain why Kant holds that "everything that happens has a cause" is a synthetic judgment, and how Hume's view of the causal principle differs from Kant's.
hint: Ask whether the concept of "something happening" already contains the concept of a cause, and what Hume thinks we can and cannot conceive.
solution: For Kant the concept of an event, something that happens or begins, does not contain the concept of a cause: one can analyze the first without finding the second. So the principle adds a predicate to the subject and is synthetic. Kant also holds that the principle is strictly universal and necessary, so it is known a priori and is therefore synthetic a priori. Hume agrees that the principle is not analytic, since we can conceive an event without a cause without contradiction, and he concludes that it is not something we can know by reason alone; our confidence in it comes from habit formed by experience. Kant's reply is that the principle is a condition of experience of events, which is the argument of the second analogy.
