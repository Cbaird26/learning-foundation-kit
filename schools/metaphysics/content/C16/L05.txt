@article
Descartes's cogito leaves a gap. I can be certain that I exist as a thinking thing, but the existence of a world of bodies in space seems to need further support, and he supplies it by an argument about God. Kant thinks that this gap is a scandal for philosophy, and also that Cartesian certainty about the inner self is overrated. The **Refutation of Idealism**, added to the second edition of the Critique, argues that our inner experience itself presupposes outer objects. The **Paralogisms** argue that the "I think" gives us no knowledge of a thinking substance. Together they reverse the Cartesian order: outer experience is not less secure than inner, and the self is not better known than the world.

### Two kinds of idealism

Kant distinguishes **problematic idealism** (Descartes), which holds that the existence of objects in space outside us is doubtful or unprovable, and **dogmatic idealism** (Berkeley, as Kant reads him), which holds that space and the things in it are impossible or merely imaginary. Kant says that the Aesthetic disposes of the dogmatic kind, since space is the form of outer intuition and objects in it are empirically real. The Refutation answers the problematic kind. His own view, transcendental idealism, is something else, and he insists that it is compatible with the existence of bodies in space (week 6).

### The Refutation

The theorem is that the mere, but empirically determined, consciousness of my own existence proves the existence of objects in space outside me. Here is the argument, in valid form:

| P1 | I am conscious of my own existence as determined in time: I can place my inner states in a temporal order. |
| P2 | I can be conscious of my existence as determined in time only if I perceive something permanent, against which succession and change are determined. |
| P3 | No representation in me can be that permanent, since all my representations, including the "I think", are themselves in flux or give no intuition of a permanent. |
| P4 | If the permanent that I perceive is not any representation in me, then it is a thing existing outside me, and not a mere representation of such a thing. |
| C | So there exists a permanent thing outside me, in space. |

The argument borrows from the first analogy: change can be represented only against something that persists. The Cartesian skeptic is given a premise that he cannot refuse, an awareness of his own states in time, and is shown that it depends on what he doubts. Kant was not satisfied with the original statement, and the second edition's Preface adds a long note meant to clarify the proof.

Each premise has been attacked. A skeptic who doubts memory denies **P1**: an evil deceiver might arrange the apparent order of my past states. Others deny **P2**, saying that the order of my states might be fixed by causal links among them, with no outer permanent. **P3** is challenged by those who say that the persistence of an enduring self or of a stable inner state could serve as the permanent. **P4** raises the question of what "outside me" means. Interpreters disagree: on the empirical reading it means in space, so the Refutation shows that bodies exist as appearances; on a transcendental reading it points to things in themselves, but that would conflict with Kant's own limits on what we can say of them. A further question is whether the argument shows more than that I must represent the permanent as an outer object. Paul Guyer's reading, in *Kant and the Claims of Knowledge* (1987), treats the Refutation as an argument that determining the order of my own states requires an objective, spatial order, though not everyone agrees.

### The paralogisms

The **Paralogisms of Pure Reason** are the first chapter of the Dialectic. Rational psychology, as Kant knew it from the school of Wolff, tried to derive the soul's nature from the single thought "I think": that it is a substance, simple, a person with identity over time, and distinct from bodies. A **paralogism** is a formally invalid inference, and Kant argues that each argument commits a fallacy, an equivocation in its middle term. The first edition (A) has four paralogisms; the second edition (B) condenses them and adds a reply to Moses Mendelssohn's proof, from the soul's simplicity, that it cannot perish.

The first paralogism, of substantiality, can be written as a valid argument if "subject" is read in one fixed sense:

| P1 | That which can be thought only as subject, and never as predicate of anything else, is a substance. |
| P2 | I, as a thinking being, can be thought only as subject, and never as predicate of anything else. |
| C | So I, as a thinking being, am a substance. |

Kant says that this appearance of validity is a trick. In P1 "subject" means an object that is given in intuition and can persist; in P2 it means the logical subject of my judgments, the "I" of the "I think". On the first reading P2 is unsupported, because the "I think" is not an intuition of anything permanent. On the second reading P1 is false, since being the grammatical or logical subject of a thought does not make something a substance. The inference trades on both meanings. The argument for the others, the soul's simplicity and personal identity, follows the same diagnosis: the formal unity of the "I think" is mistaken for knowledge of a thing.

@example
title: Reconstructing the Refutation
problem: A skeptic says, "I am certain that I exist and that I have thoughts in a temporal order. But I can imagine that all my experiences are produced without any outer objects." How does the Refutation reply, and which premise would the skeptic have to deny to resist it?
step: Take the skeptic's own certainty: that I am conscious of my states as determined in time. This is P1, which the skeptic accepts.
step: Kant argues that determining my states in time needs something permanent in perception (P2), and that nothing in me is permanent (P3), so the permanent must be a thing outside me (P4).
step: To resist, the skeptic must deny a premise. The most natural options are P2 (denying that ordering my states needs a permanent) or P3 (claiming that something in me is permanent).
step: Notice that the skeptic's imagined scenario of experiences produced without outer objects is not a counterexample to the argument's validity. It is a claim that its premises fail.
answer: The Refutation replies that inner experience of time-order presupposes a permanent outer thing. The skeptic must deny P2 or P3.

@example
title: Diagnosing a parallel equivocation
problem: Consider: "A bank is a place where money is kept. The edge of a river is a bank. So the edge of a river is a place where money is kept." State what has gone wrong, and say how this parallels Kant's diagnosis of the first paralogism.
step: Identify the middle term. The word "bank" occurs in both premises and drops out of the conclusion.
step: Say that "bank" means a financial institution in the first premise and a riverside in the second, so the term is used in two senses, and the argument has four terms and not three.
step: In the first paralogism "subject" plays the part of "bank". It means an object given in intuition in the first premise and the logical subject of thought in the second. The argument looks valid and is not once the senses are kept apart.
step: The parallel is not complete. In the first paralogism there is a further question: whether any single reading makes both premises true, and Kant says none does.
answer: The argument equivocates on "bank". The first paralogism equivocates on "subject" in the same way.

@mistakes
- Saying that Kant's Refutation proves that there is a world of things in themselves. Its conclusion concerns objects in space outside me, which for Kant are empirically real and, on the transcendental idealist view, appearances.
- Confusing Kant's own idealism with Berkeley's. Kant says that his view is transcendental or critical idealism, and that it allows the empirical reality of bodies.
- Reading the Paralogisms as showing that there is no self. Kant denies that the "I think" yields knowledge of the soul's nature, and he does not deny that I think.
- Treating a paralogism as an argument with false premises. It is a faulty inference, by Kant's diagnosis one that equivocates.

@practice self
prompt: In five to seven sentences, explain why on Kant's view the cogito ("I think, therefore I exist") does not establish that I am a persisting thinking substance, and state a reply a defender of Descartes might make.
hint: Contrast the formal "I think" with an intuition of something permanent, and recall the equivocation on "subject".
solution: For Kant the "I think" is the formal unity that accompanies all my representations. It is not an intuition of anything permanent, since inner sense presents only changing states, so it gives no cognition of a persisting thing. To infer a substance I need a premise about things given in intuition, and the first paralogism trades on the ambiguity between the logical subject of thought and a substance. Kant does not deny that I exist as thinking, but he says that nothing about my nature follows. A defender of Descartes can reply that in the cogito I am directly aware of myself as a thinking thing, an intellectual awareness that does not depend on sensible intuition, and that Kant's insistence on intuition simply begs the question against it. Kant's defender can answer that the awareness is of the act of thinking, which is not yet an awareness of an enduring subject.
