@article
Two people argue about whether a vase is "really" fragile. One says that fragility is a hidden inner nature, the other that it is only the tendency to break when struck. A pragmatist asks: what difference would it make which of them is right? If neither answer predicts anything different about what happens when the vase is dropped, the dispute may have no content beyond words. This question is the core of pragmatism, and it comes down to us from an empiricist tradition that Mill helped to set.

### Mill: induction and causation

John Stuart Mill's *System of Logic* (1843) argues that all knowledge of the world, including mathematics and logic, rests on induction from experience. Many philosophers reject the claim about mathematics, and Frege's criticism in *The Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884) is the best known. On causation Mill holds a regularity view: the cause of an event is the sum of the conditions, positive and negative, which together are invariably followed by it. His **methods of experimental inquiry** are rules for finding causes: the methods of agreement, difference, joint agreement and difference, residues and concomitant variation. In the **method of difference**, if two cases agree in every circumstance except one, and the effect occurs only where that circumstance is present, then that circumstance is the cause, or an indispensable part of it. In his *Examination of Sir William Hamilton's Philosophy* (1865), Mill defines matter as a permanent possibility of sensation, a phenomenalist view of bodies.

### Peirce: the pragmatic maxim

Charles Sanders Peirce, in "The Fixation of Belief" (1877), says that doubt is an irritation we want to end by settling belief, and compares four ways of doing so: **tenacity** (holding on to what we believe), **authority**, the **a priori method** (believing what seems agreeable to reason) and the **method of science**, which alone is checked by something external to our wishes. In "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878) he proposes the **pragmatic maxim**: to clarify a concept, consider what effects, with conceivable practical bearings, we take its object to have; our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object. His example is *hard*: to say a diamond is hard is to say that it would not be scratched by many other things, even if it is never tested. The maxim is therefore about conditionals and not only actual events, and Peirce did not mean it to reduce meaning to what is observed here and now. He later called his own view "pragmaticism" to distinguish it from others' uses. On truth, he holds that the true opinion is the one fated to be agreed to by all who investigate, and that the real is the object represented in it.

A use of the maxim to dispose of apparent disputes:

| P1 | Two conceptions differ in meaning only if they differ in conceivable practical effects. |
| P2 | The two sides in a given dispute hold conceptions that do not differ in any conceivable practical effects. |
| C | So the dispute between the two sides concerns no difference in meaning: it is merely verbal. |

The argument is valid. A critic can deny **P1**, arguing that meaning can outrun any practical difference, for example a claim about an unobservable event in the distant past; Peirce answers that "conceivable" and "practical bearings" are broad, and that past events have consequences in what we might find. A critic can also deny **P2** for a given dispute, by finding a difference in effects the two sides had overlooked, which is how the maxim is meant to be used.

### James: truth and belief

William James, in *Pragmatism* (1907), applies the method to metaphysical disputes and gives a view of truth: true ideas are those we can assimilate, validate and verify, and truth is what works in the long run of experience. Critics, including Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore, argued that this confuses truth with usefulness. James replied that "working" is constrained by how things are and by the agreement of ideas with reality. Peirce, in turn, was unhappy with James's use of the word, since for Peirce truth is the end of inquiry, not what satisfies an individual. In "The Will to Believe" (1896), James replies to W. K. Clifford, who wrote in "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) that it is wrong always, everywhere and for anyone to believe anything upon insufficient evidence. James argues for a permission:

| P1 | If an option is live, forced and momentous, and cannot by its nature be decided on evidential grounds, then it is permissible to decide it by one's passional nature. |
| P2 | This option is live, forced and momentous, and cannot by its nature be decided on evidential grounds. |
| C | So it is permissible to decide this option by one's passional nature. |

The argument is valid. Clifford's side denies **P1**, since on his view evidence is always required. James's critics also deny **P2** in particular applications, by saying that further evidence can be found. Whether the argument supports religious belief depends on whether religious options satisfy P2 for a person, and on this informed philosophers disagree.

@example
title: Applying the pragmatic maxim to "fragile"
problem: Apply Peirce's maxim to the concept "fragile". What conceivable practical effects does it comprise, and why does this not reduce it to what has actually happened to the vase?
step: Ask what we expect of a fragile thing. If it were struck sharply or dropped onto a hard floor, it would break.
step: The effects are conditional: a fragile vase that is never dropped is still fragile, because the maxim concerns what would happen under conceivable tests.
step: On the maxim, our whole conception of fragility consists of such conditional expectations, including those about how it would respond to different kinds of knocks. A claim about an inner nature that predicted nothing more would add no content.
answer: "Fragile" means that the thing would break if struck or dropped. The maxim concerns conditionals about conceivable tests and not only actual events.

@example
title: Applying James's permission argument
problem: Priya must decide whether to join a new orchestra. She cannot know in advance whether the players will get along, and part of the outcome depends on whether she trusts them. She believes that trust will help. Does James's argument permit her to decide by her passional nature, and what might a critic say?
step: Check the conditions in P2. The option is live (she takes it seriously), forced (not joining is declining) and momentous (a significant commitment). Whether it can be decided on evidence is the contested part.
step: James would say that the outcome partly depends on her trust, so the evidence cannot settle it in advance; given P1, she is permitted to decide on her passional nature.
step: A critic in Clifford's spirit denies P2 by saying that she can attend a trial rehearsal and gather evidence. This leaves P1 intact and shows that the option is not of the kind that cannot be decided evidentially.
answer: If P2 holds, the argument permits her decision. The critic denies P2 by pointing to available evidence.

@mistakes
- Saying that the pragmatic maxim reduces meaning to what has actually been observed. It concerns conceivable effects, and so includes conditionals.
- Treating James's and Peirce's views of truth as the same. Peirce ties truth to the end of inquiry, while James's account stresses what works in experience.
- Reading James's "The Will to Believe" as permitting any belief one wants. It applies to options that are live, forced and momentous and that cannot by their nature be decided on evidence.
- Saying that Mill's methods prove causes with certainty. They are rules for eliminating candidates and are fallible, as inductions are.

@practice self
prompt: Explain Peirce's pragmatic maxim in your own words and illustrate it with three examples of your own (not "hard" or "fragile"), saying for each what conceivable practical effects make up the concept.
hint: Choose concepts like "acidic", "heavier than", or "magnetic", and state the conditionals that give them their content.
solution: The maxim says that to make a concept clear, we ask what conceivable effects with practical bearings we attribute to its object, and that our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object. Example 1: "acidic". A liquid is acidic if litmus paper would turn red in it, if it would react with a carbonate to give off gas, and so on; these are conditional on the tests being done. Example 2: "heavier than". A is heavier than B if, were both put on opposite pans of a balance, A's pan would go down. Example 3: "magnetic". A piece of iron is magnetic if iron filings would cling to it when it was brought near. None of these requires that the test is actually carried out, so the maxim concerns conditionals. If two accounts of "acidic" predicted exactly the same tests, the maxim says that they differ only in words.

@quiz self
prompt: In six to eight sentences, compare Peirce's and James's views of truth, and say what each would say about a claim that no one could ever test.
hint: Think about Peirce's end of inquiry and James's emphasis on what works in experience.
solution: For Peirce the true opinion is the one that all inquirers would agree on at the end of inquiry, and a concept's content consists of its conceivable practical effects. For James, true ideas are those that can be assimilated, validated and verified in experience, and truth is what works in the long run, constrained by how things are. On a claim that no one could ever test, Peirce would ask whether it has any conceivable practical effects: if not, it has no content beyond words, although he allows broad conditionals. James would ask whether believing it makes a difference in experience, and, if it does not, would say that it makes no difference to truth; but for options of a certain kind that evidence cannot settle, he allows belief on passional grounds. The two differ because Peirce's account is about the community's long-run opinion, while James's is about the believer's experience of validation. Critics say that James's account is closer to confusing truth with usefulness.
