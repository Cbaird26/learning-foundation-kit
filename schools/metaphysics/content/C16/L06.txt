@article
Kant says that we know only appearances and never things as they are in themselves. Does this mean that the world has two layers: the ordinary world of tables and trees, and behind it a hidden world of things that cause our experience? Or does it mean that the one world of tables and trees can be considered in two ways, as it appears to creatures like us and as it would be independently of our cognitive conditions? Kant's readers have split on this question since the first reviews, and the split matters for everything else in the Critique.

### The thesis

**Transcendental idealism** is the doctrine of the Aesthetic and the Analytic combined. Space and time are forms of our sensibility, and the categories are conditions of our experience of objects, so what we cognize are **appearances**, objects as they are given to us, and not **things in themselves**. Kant says that this is needed to avoid the absurd result that there would be appearance without anything that appears. He distinguishes two senses of "noumenon". In the **negative** sense it is a thing so far as it is not an object of our sensible intuition. In the **positive** sense it would be an object of a non-sensible, intellectual intuition. Kant allows the negative concept as a limiting concept and denies that we have knowledge of the positive.

### Two readings

On the **two-world** (or two-object, or ontological) reading, appearances and things in themselves are distinct kinds of entities: things in themselves exist independently of us and are not spatial or temporal, and appearances are mind-dependent entities in us, representations or constructions out of them. Some defenders of this view stress Kant's passages that call outer objects mere representations. On the **two-aspect** (or one-world, or methodological) reading, defended by Gerold Prauss and, in English, by Henry Allison, there is one set of objects, which can be considered in two ways: as they appear under our cognitive conditions, and as they are in themselves, independently of those conditions. This reading appeals to the Preface to the second edition, where Kant speaks of the same objects taken as appearances and as things in themselves. A third view, Rae Langton's in *Kantian Humility* (1998), keeps one set of objects and says we know only their relational properties and are ignorant of their intrinsic properties. This is a one-object view of a different kind from Allison's.

| Question | Two-world reading | Two-aspect reading |
|---|---|---|
| Are appearances and things in themselves the same objects? | No: two kinds of entity. | Yes: one set of objects, considered in two ways. |
| What are appearances? | Mind-dependent entities in us. | Ordinary objects as given under our cognitive conditions. |
| How does sensation arise? | Things in themselves affect our sensibility. | Empirical objects affect our senses; no extra realm is needed. |
| Main support in the text | Passages calling objects mere representations. | The Preface's "same objects" in two senses. |
| Main difficulty | The affection problem; an unknowable hidden realm. | Making sense of claims that things in themselves are not spatial. |

### The affection problem

Friedrich Jacobi complained in 1787 that without the presupposition of the thing in itself he could not enter Kant's system, and with it he could not remain. Kant says that sensation arises when objects affect us. But affection looks like causation, and causality is a category, which on Kant's principles may legitimately be applied only to objects of possible experience.

| P1 | On Kant's account, sensation is produced by things in themselves affecting our sensibility. |
| P2 | Affecting is a causal relation. |
| P3 | Causality is a category, and Kant holds that categories may legitimately be applied only to objects of possible experience. |
| P4 | Things in themselves are not objects of possible experience. |
| C | So Kant is not entitled, by his own principles, to assert that things in themselves affect our sensibility. |

The argument is valid. The two readings respond differently. A two-aspect interpreter denies **P1** as stated: what affects us is the empirical object, so the cause is something in the world of appearance, and no extra thing is needed. A two-world interpreter has other options: to deny **P2**, by saying that affection is a relation of ground and consequence that does not use the schematized category, or to read **P3** as a restriction on cognition (what we can know), leaving room for a merely thought, non-cognitive use of the category. Critics say that these replies strain Kant's own wording. The point of the exercise is not to settle which reading is right but to see what each costs: the two-world reading risks making the thing in itself an unwarranted hidden cause, and the two-aspect reading finds it difficult to explain why Kant says that space and time do not belong to things in themselves.

### Why the dispute continues

Each reading finds support in passages the other must explain. It is common to find defenders of one reading saying that Kant's wording is loose and that his considered view is theirs. Neither reading is the consensus of Kant scholars, and a good paper states both fairly before arguing for one.

@example
title: Which reading says it?
problem: Classify each statement as most naturally belonging to the two-world reading, the two-aspect reading or both: (a) "A table as it appears and the table in itself are one and the same table, considered in different ways." (b) "The things that exist in themselves are not in space or time, and the spatial table exists only as a representation in us." (c) "Kant's thing in itself is a limiting concept, and we cannot know it."
step: (a) says that appearance and thing in itself are the same object, which is the two-aspect view's central claim.
step: (b) says that there are two kinds of thing, those outside space and time and the representations in us, which is the two-world view.
step: (c) is common ground: both readings accept that we cannot know things in themselves, and that the concept limits our knowledge claims, though they explain it differently.
answer: (a) two-aspect; (b) two-world; (c) both.

@example
title: Replying to the affection problem
problem: A defender of the two-aspect reading and a defender of the two-world reading each respond to the affection argument given above. For each, say which premise is denied and what the denial costs.
step: Find the premises that could be denied. P1 says things in themselves affect us, P2 that affection is causal, P3 that categories apply only to possible experience, P4 that things in themselves are not objects of possible experience.
step: The two-aspect defender denies P1 as stated: what affects us is the empirical object. The cost is that Kant's talk of things in themselves as the ground of sensation has to be read as loose or as about the same objects.
step: The two-world defender denies P2, saying that affection is a relation of ground, not the schematized category, or reads P3 as a limit on cognition. The cost is a notion of ground that Kant has not clearly explained, and that seems to be causation under another name.
step: Neither reply refutes the argument, which is valid: each denies a premise and must pay for it.
answer: The two-aspect defender denies P1; the two-world defender denies P2 or restricts P3 to cognition.

@mistakes
- Saying that transcendental idealism denies that there are real tables and trees. For Kant these are empirically real, and the dispute is over what "appearance" means.
- Treating the two-world and two-aspect readings as Kant's own labels. They are names for competing interpretations, and neither has the status of a settled result.
- Confusing the negative and positive senses of "noumenon". The positive sense would require intellectual intuition, which Kant says we lack.
- Taking the affection problem to show that Kant's philosophy is incoherent. It is an objection that each reading must answer, and the replies are disputed.
