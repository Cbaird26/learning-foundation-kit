@project
title: Kitchen chemistry and motion lab
overview: In this project you will rank household liquids by acidity using a red-cabbage indicator and measure how the height of a ramp changes the speed of a toy car. You will collect your own data, make calculations, and explain your results using ideas from the whole course. If you cannot do a part at home, use the sample data given in the steps and label it as sample data.
goal: Rank at least four colorless household liquids from most acidic to most basic using a red-cabbage indicator, and link each color to an approximate pH.
goal: Measure a toy car's average speed at three different ramp heights and describe the relationship between height and speed.
goal: Explain your results using pH, conservation of energy ($PE = mgh$ and $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$) and Newton's laws.
step: Part A, make the indicator. Chop a few red-cabbage leaves, soak them in warm (not boiling) tap water for ten minutes with an adult's help, and pour off the purple liquid into a jar. Label it.
step: Part A, test the liquids. Put 2 tablespoons of each colorless liquid (for example white vinegar, lemon juice, water, and a solution of 1 teaspoon baking soda in a cup of water) in separate labeled clear cups. Add 1 tablespoon of indicator to each. Record the color. Sample data if you cannot test: vinegar is pink, lemon juice is red, water is purple, baking soda solution is blue-green. Rank from most acidic to most basic and assign each liquid an approximate pH from the color chart (red or pink 1 to 3, purple-pink 4 to 6, purple 7, blue 8 to 9, blue-green 10 to 12; these pH values are only approximate).
step: Part B, build the ramp. Prop one end of a smooth board or large book at heights of 5 cm, 10 cm and 15 cm above the table. Measure a $1.00\ \mathrm{m}$ track starting at the bottom of the ramp, and mark a start line at the top and a finish line at $1.00\ \mathrm{m}$ past the bottom of the ramp.
step: Part B, time the car. Release the toy car from the top at each height without pushing it, and time the run from the bottom of the ramp to the finish line with a phone stopwatch. Do three trials per height and average them. Sample data if you cannot test: average times of 2.00 s, 1.43 s and 1.18 s for the $1.00\ \mathrm{m}$ track at 5 cm, 10 cm and 15 cm.
step: Part B, calculate. For each height find the average speed $v = d/t$. Make a table of height, average time and average speed, and plot speed against ramp height.
step: Analyze with energy. Explain the trend. A higher ramp gives the car more gravitational potential energy $PE = mgh$, which is converted to kinetic energy $\tfrac12 mv^2$, so the car moves faster at the bottom. Mention friction and why the numbers are not perfect.
step: Write up. Assemble your results into a short report with a table for each part, a graph, and a paragraph for each part that states your claim, evidence and reasoning. Include one sentence on possible sources of error and one improvement you would make.
deliverable: A table of the household liquids with colors, a ranking from most acidic to most basic, and an approximate pH for each.
deliverable: A table of ramp height, average time and average speed for three heights, with a graph and a sentence describing the trend.
deliverable: A one-page explanation using pH, Newton's laws and energy, including sources of error and an improvement.
rubric: The ranking of liquids matches the colors observed and uses the indicator color chart correctly.
rubric: Speeds are calculated correctly from distance and the average time, with units, and the three-trial averages are shown.
rubric: The explanation uses $PE = mgh$ and $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$ correctly and mentions friction or other sources of error.
rubric: A fair test is described, with only one variable changed at a time and the other conditions kept the same.
rubric: Safety steps are followed and clearly stated.
safety: Use only safe kitchen materials with an adult nearby. Do not taste any of the test liquids, keep them away from your eyes, and wash your hands afterward. Do not use boiling water; use warm tap water. Keep the ramp away from stairs, and tidy up any spilled liquid at once.
tutor: Why does the red-cabbage indicator change color for different liquids, and how can I make my ranking more precise?
tutor: How can I reduce timing error when I time a toy car with a stopwatch?
tutor: Can you check my explanation of how potential energy and kinetic energy relate to my ramp results?

@review
### Week by week

- **Week 1, matter and atoms.** Matter has mass and takes up space. Atomic number = protons; mass number = protons + neutrons; a neutral atom has equal protons and electrons. Density = mass / volume.
- **Week 2, periodic table.** Periods are rows (shells), groups are columns (similar valence electrons). Electron shells hold 2, then up to 8.
- **Week 3, physical and chemical changes.** A chemical change forms a new substance; clues are gas, precipitate, color, heat or light, and smell. No single clue is proof.
- **Week 4, reactions.** Atoms are conserved, so mass of reactants = mass of products. Balance equations with coefficients only. A sealed system keeps all its gas.
- **Week 5, acids, bases and pH.** pH below 7 is acidic, 7 is neutral, above 7 is basic. Each unit is a factor of $10$. $\mathrm{pH} = -\log_{10}[\mathrm{H^+}]$.
- **Week 6, motion.** $v = d/t$, $a = (v_f - v_i)/t$. Velocity has direction; speed does not. Average several trials.
- **Week 7, forces.** Net force adds along a line. $F = ma$, $W = mg$. Action and reaction act on different objects.
- **Week 8, energy.** $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$, $PE = mgh$. Energy changes form but is conserved.
- **Week 9, heat.** Temperature is average particle energy; heat is transfer from hot to cold. $Q = mc\Delta T$. Conduction, convection, radiation.
- **Week 10, waves.** $v = f\lambda$, $T = 1/f$. Sound needs a medium; light does not.
- **Week 11, circuits.** $V = IR$, $P = IV$. Series resistances add; parallel gives lower total resistance.
- **Week 12, magnetism.** Like poles repel. Current makes a magnetic field. Electromagnets are stronger with more current, more turns and an iron core.

### Self-check

- I can find protons, neutrons and electrons from the atomic and mass numbers.
- I can classify a change and give evidence for my choice.
- I can balance a simple equation and explain mass conservation in a sealed bag.
- I can explain what a pH difference of $2$ means.
- I can calculate speed, acceleration, force, energy, heat and current with units.
- I can explain a crash test using Newton's laws.

### Common errors

- Mixing up the mass number and atomic number, or periods and groups.
- Changing subscripts instead of coefficients when balancing.
- Treating each pH unit as a small step instead of a factor of ten.
- Forgetting to square the speed in kinetic energy, or to use $\Delta T$ in $Q = mc\Delta T$.
- Adding resistances in parallel, or forgetting units and unit conversions.

prompt: A toy car with mass $0.050\ \mathrm{kg}$ rolls the last $1.00\ \mathrm{m}$ of a track in $1.25\ \mathrm{s}$ at a steady speed. What is the car's kinetic energy in joules?
answer: 0.016
check: 0.5 * 0.050 * (1.00 / 1.25) ** 2
tol: 1%
unit: J
hint: First find the speed from $v = d/t$.
hint: The speed is $1.00 \div 1.25 = 0.80\ \mathrm{m/s}$. Then use $KE = \tfrac12 mv^2$.
solution: $v = 1.00 \div 1.25 = 0.80\ \mathrm{m/s}$. $KE = \tfrac12 (0.050)(0.80)^2 = 0.016\ \mathrm{J}$.

prompt: Liquid C has a pH of $8$. Liquid D has a hydrogen ion concentration of $1 \times 10^{-5}$ mol/L. How many times greater is the hydrogen ion concentration of liquid D than that of liquid C?
answer: 1000
check: 1e-5 / 10 ** (-8)
tol: 1%
hint: Convert liquid D's concentration to a pH first.
hint: Liquid D has pH $5$. The pH values differ by $3$ units, and each unit is a factor of $10$.
solution: Liquid D has $\mathrm{pH} = 5$. A difference of $3$ pH units is $10^3 = 1000$, so liquid D has $1000$ times the hydrogen ion concentration.

@exam self
prompt: A student seals $5.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of baking soda and $50.0\ \mathrm{g}$ of vinegar (kept separate) in a bag of mass $3.0\ \mathrm{g}$, mixes them, and the bag inflates. (a) State the total mass before and after mixing and justify it. (b) Say whether the change is physical or chemical and give two pieces of evidence. (c) Explain what the student would see on the scale if the experiment were done in an open cup, and why.
solution: (a) Before mixing the total is 5.0 + 50.0 + 3.0 = 58.0 g. After mixing it is also about 58.0 g, because the bag is sealed and atoms are conserved: the gas produced stays inside. (b) It is a chemical change. Evidence: gas bubbles inflate the bag, and the temperature of the mixture drops, which shows that new substances (carbon dioxide, water and a salt) formed. (c) In an open cup the carbon dioxide escapes into the air, so the scale reading goes down. The mass has not been destroyed; it left as gas.
hint: Include everything that is in the system, and decide whether anything can enter or leave.
rubric: Gives 58.0 g before and after for the sealed bag and cites conservation of mass.
rubric: Classifies the change as chemical and gives two valid pieces of evidence, such as gas and temperature change.
rubric: Explains that the open cup loses mass because the gas escapes.
rubric: Uses correct terms, such as reactants, products and conservation of mass.
