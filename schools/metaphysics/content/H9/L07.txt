@article
Gravity is the force that holds you to the ground, keeps the Moon in orbit and shapes the solar system. Newton's insight was that one law explains all three.

### Universal gravitation

Every two masses attract each other with a force along the line joining them:

$$F = \frac{G\,m_1 m_2}{r^2}, \qquad G = 6.67\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2.$$

Here $r$ is the distance between the **centers** of the masses. The force is an inverse-square law: doubling the distance divides the force by 4, and tripling it divides by 9. The two forces form a third-law pair, equal in magnitude even if the masses differ. The tiny size of $G$ is why we feel gravity only when one object is as large as a planet.

### Gravitational field strength

The acceleration of a small object due to a large mass $M$ at distance $r$ from its center is

$$g = \frac{GM}{r^2}.$$

At Earth's surface, with $M = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ and $R = 6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$, this gives $9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. At a height of one Earth radius above the surface ($r = 2R$) it is a quarter as large. The acceleration does not depend on the falling object's mass, which is why all objects fall alike in a vacuum.

### Circular orbits

A satellite in a circular orbit is in continuous free fall: gravity supplies exactly the centripetal force needed to keep it on a circle. Setting $GMm/r^2 = mv^2/r$ gives

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi r}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}.$$

The orbital speed does not depend on the satellite's mass. Larger orbits are slower and have longer periods. Astronauts in orbit feel "weightless" not because gravity is absent (at 400 km it is still about 90% of its surface value) but because they and their spacecraft fall together, so no normal force is needed.

### Kepler's laws

Kepler described planetary motion before Newton explained it:

1. Planets move in **ellipses** with the Sun at one focus.
2. A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**, so a planet moves fastest when closest to the Sun.
3. The square of the period is proportional to the cube of the orbit's average radius: $T^2 \propto r^3$.

Using years and astronomical units (Earth: $T = 1$ year, $r = 1$ AU), the third law becomes simply $T^2 = r^3$ for anything orbiting the Sun.

### Method

Draw the situation, identify which mass is the large central body, measure $r$ from its center (add the planet's radius to any altitude), and be consistent with SI units. For ratio questions, avoid the constants: write a ratio of $r^{-2}$ or $r^3$ terms.

@example
title: Surface gravity from G, M and R
problem: Use $G = 6.67\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$, $M = 5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$ and $R = 6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$ to find the gravitational acceleration at Earth's surface.
step: The field strength is $g = GM/R^2$ with $R$ measured to the center of Earth.
step: Numerator: $GM = (6.67\times 10^{-11})(5.97\times 10^{24}) \approx 3.98\times 10^{14}$.
step: Denominator: $R^2 = (6.37\times 10^6)^2 \approx 4.06\times 10^{13}$.
step: $g \approx 3.98\times 10^{14}/4.06\times 10^{13} \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, matching the familiar value.

@mistakes
- Using the altitude instead of the distance from the center of the planet for $r$.
- Forgetting to square $r$, or squaring only part of the expression.
- Thinking gravity vanishes in orbit; astronauts are weightless because they are in free fall.
- Believing a heavier satellite must orbit faster; orbital speed depends only on $M$ and $r$.

answer: 1.334e-6
check: 6.67e-11 * 1000 * 2000 / 10**2
tol: 1%
hint: Use $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$.
hint: $m_1m_2 = 2\times 10^6$ and $r^2 = 100$.
solution: $F = (6.67\times 10^{-11})(2\times 10^6)/100 \approx 1.33\times 10^{-6}\ \text{N}$, a tiny force.
unit: N

@practice num

answer: 2.45

@practice num
prompt: A satellite orbits Earth in a circle at an altitude of $400\ \text{km}$. Taking Earth's radius as $6.37\times 10^6\ \text{m}$ and mass as $5.97\times 10^{24}\ \text{kg}$, find its orbital speed in m/s, to the nearest $1\ \text{m/s}$. Use $G = 6.67\times 10^{-11}\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2/\text{kg}^2$.
answer: 7669
check: sqrt(6.67e-11 * 5.97e24 / (6.37e6 + 4.0e5))
tol: 1%
hint: The orbital radius is measured from Earth's center: add the altitude to Earth's radius.
hint: $r = 6.77\times 10^6\ \text{m}$ and $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$.
solution: $r = 6.37\times 10^6 + 0.40\times 10^6 = 6.77\times 10^6\ \text{m}$. $v = \sqrt{(3.98\times 10^{14})/(6.77\times 10^6)} \approx 7669\ \text{m/s}$.
unit: m/s

@quiz num
prompt: A planet has twice Earth's mass and twice Earth's radius. If $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ on Earth's surface, what is the gravitational acceleration at the planet's surface, in $\text{m/s}^2$?
answer: 4.9
check: 9.8 * 2 / 2**2
tol: 0.01
hint: Write $g = GM/R^2$ and compare the planet with Earth as a ratio.
hint: The mass factor is $2$ and the radius factor enters squared.
solution: $g_{planet}/g_{Earth} = 2/2^2 = 1/2$, so $g = 4.9\ \text{m/s}^2$.
unit: m/s^2
