@article
Ana believes that Venus is visible tonight. Her belief is about a planet millions of kilometers away. It can be true or false. It can also be about something that does not exist: she can fear a monster under the bed that is not there. How can a state of a physical creature reach out to things that are far away or non-existent? This is the problem of **intentionality**, the mind's directedness toward things.

### Brentano's thesis

Franz Brentano, in *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), proposed that intentionality, which he described as the reference to a content or direction toward an object, is the mark of the mental: all and only mental phenomena exhibit it. Many philosophers accept that intentionality is central to the mind and doubt that it is exclusive to it: some think that conscious sensations such as a dull ache, or moods, have no object. Others hold that some non-mental things, such as words and pictures, represent too, though perhaps derivatively.

### Features of intentional states

The **propositional attitudes**, such as believing, desiring and hoping, relate a subject to a content, a proposition. Three features matter:

- **Aboutness**: the state is directed at something.
- **Misrepresentation**: the state can be false or can fail to correspond to anything. A theory of content must allow error.
- **Opacity**: substituting co-referring terms inside "believes that..." can change truth value. Ana may believe that the morning star is visible without believing that the evening star is visible, although both are Venus. This is why a content must be finer-grained than the object it concerns.

### Narrow and wide content

If two people are physically alike, must they have the same contents? **Narrow content** is content fixed by what is in the head; **wide content** depends on the environment. Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth argument supports the claim that, at least for natural-kind terms, meaning is wide. Imagine a planet exactly like Earth except that the clear drinkable liquid in its lakes is not H$_2$O but a different compound, XYZ. Oscar on Earth and Twin Oscar on Twin Earth are molecule-for-molecule duplicates (setting aside that human bodies contain water), and they use the word "water." Putnam argued that Oscar's word refers to H$_2$O and Twin Oscar's to XYZ, so that their words differ in meaning although they are alike inside.

| P1 | Oscar and Twin Oscar are intrinsic physical duplicates. |
| P2 | Oscar's "water" thoughts are about H$_2$O, and Twin Oscar's are about XYZ, so their contents differ. |
| C | So content is not fixed by intrinsic physical state alone: it depends partly on the environment. |

The argument is valid. Its premises can be contested: some philosophers say there is a narrow component that the two share, such as a way of thinking, while others accept the conclusion about wide content. Tyler Burge's argument about "arthritis" extends the point to the social environment: a person who wrongly thinks arthritis can occur in the thigh still has thoughts about arthritis because of the linguistic community, and a counterfactual duplicate in a community with a different usage would have different contents. Externalism has consequences for self-knowledge and for mental causation, which week 8 takes up.

### Naturalizing content

Can we explain content in non-mental terms? Two families of theories try.

**Causal-informational theories** (Fred Dretske, Jerry Fodor) say that a state represents what reliably causes it. A "cow" tokening represents cows because cows cause it. The **disjunction problem** challenges this: if on some dark nights a horse causes the same tokening, then the state seems to represent "cow or horse," and misrepresentation becomes impossible. Fodor proposed an asymmetric dependence condition: horse-caused tokenings depend on cow-caused ones, not the reverse.

**Teleological theories** (Ruth Millikan, among others) say that a state represents what it was selected to carry information about, given its history of evolution or learning. Misrepresentation is then a malfunction. A worry is that they make content depend on history: a being that came into existence by accident would have no content, and some think this is the wrong result.

Jerry Fodor also defended the **language of thought** hypothesis: thinking is computation over a system of mental symbols with syntax and semantics. Daniel Dennett's **intentional stance** takes a different line: to have beliefs is to be a system whose behavior is well predicted by attributing beliefs and desires to it.

@example
title: Reading the Twin Earth argument
problem: Put the Twin Earth argument for wide content in standard form, then say what an internalist could deny.
step: P1: Oscar and Twin Oscar are intrinsic duplicates, so they are alike in everything inside their skins.
step: P2: The word "water" in Oscar's mouth refers to H$_2$O, and in Twin Oscar's to XYZ.
step: C: So their meanings or contents differ although they are intrinsic duplicates: content is wide.
step: An internalist can accept P2 for reference but deny that reference is content: they may say the two share a narrow content (a way of thinking about the clear drinkable liquid) and differ only in what that content picks out.
answer: The argument is valid. The internalist rejects the step from differing reference to differing content.

@example
title: The disjunction problem
problem: A simple causal theory says a state represents whatever causes it. Show how misrepresentation becomes a problem.
step: Suppose Ana's "cow" state is tokened when she sees a cow, and also, on a dark night, when she sees a horse.
step: The theory says the state represents whatever causes it, so it represents "cow or horse."
step: But then her tokening of "cow" on seeing a horse is not a mistake: it correctly represents a cow-or-horse.
step: The theory must find a way to say that the horse-caused tokenings are errors, as in Fodor's asymmetric dependence proposal.
answer: Simple causal theories make misrepresentation impossible, which is the disjunction problem.

@mistakes
- Treating intentionality as a feeling. It is aboutness, not a kind of sensation.
- Thinking that wide content means that the mind is outside the head. It means content depends partly on the environment.
- Saying Twin Earth shows that water is XYZ. Twin Earth is a different planet with a different liquid.
- Confusing opacity with falsity. Opaque contexts allow substitution to change truth value but are not false by that fact.
