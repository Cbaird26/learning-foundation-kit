@article
Two arguments try to move from the apparent gap between physical information and experience to the conclusion that physicalism is false. One begins with a scientist who has never seen color, the other with a creature that looks like you in every physical respect but has no inner life.

### The knowledge argument

Frank Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982) describes Mary, a brilliant scientist raised in a black-and-white room who learns everything physical there is to know about color vision: the physics of light, the neurophysiology of the visual system, the functional organization of the brain. When she steps out and sees a ripe tomato, does she learn something?

| P1 | Before her release, Mary knows all the physical facts about color vision. |
| P2 | On her release, Mary learns something new: what it is like to see red. |
| P3 | If she learns something new, there is a fact she did not know before. |
| P4 | If she knew all the physical facts and yet there is a fact she did not know, that fact is not a physical fact. |
| C | So there are facts about experience that are not physical facts, and physicalism is false. |

The argument is valid. Jackson himself later came to reject its conclusion, but it remains the standard formulation. The replies target different premises.

**The ability reply** (David Lewis and Laurence Nemirow). Deny P3: what Mary gains is not a new fact but new abilities: to imagine, recognize and remember the experience of red. This is know-how, not knowledge-that. Critics reply that her gain seems to include learning that this is what seeing red is like, a truth, and that abilities alone do not explain her ability to recognize a truth.

**The acquaintance reply.** Mary gains acquaintance with a quality she already knew about in the abstract; acquaintance is a different kind of knowledge, not knowledge of a new fact.

**The old-fact, new-mode reply** (the phenomenal concept strategy). Deny P3: Mary learns something in a new way but not a new fact. She learns an old physical fact under a new concept, a phenomenal one. She knew that some brain state occurs when one sees red; now she grasps the same state in a new way. Critics ask whether the phenomenal concept can be explained physically.

**Denying P2.** Some, like Daniel Dennett, argue that Mary would not be surprised, or that we cannot imagine knowing all the physical facts.

### The zombie argument

A **philosophical zombie** is a being that is physically identical to a conscious person but has no phenomenal consciousness. It behaves as we do, and says it feels pain, but there is nothing it is like to be it. David Chalmers argued:

| P1 | Zombies are conceivable: it is conceivable that there be a being physically identical to a conscious person without consciousness. |
| P2 | If zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible. |
| P3 | If zombies are metaphysically possible, then consciousness is not entailed by the physical facts, and physicalism is false. |
| C | So physicalism is false. |

It is valid, and the debate concerns P1 and P2. Philosophers who deny P1 say that the zombie scenario is incoherent when examined closely, because a complete physical duplicate would have the same ground of experience. Type-B materialists deny P2: conceivability is not a guide to possibility for a posteriori identities. "Water is not H$_2$O" seems conceivable, but water is necessarily H$_2$O (Kripke). Chalmers replies with a two-dimensional account of the semantics of concepts like "water," on which the case of consciousness differs because there is no appearance-reality gap for experience. This reply is contested.

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, about 24% of respondents held zombies to be metaphysically possible, 37% conceivable but not metaphysically possible, and 16% inconceivable. These figures say what philosophers believe, not what is true.

### How the arguments differ

The knowledge argument is about **knowledge and facts**: what could be known from physical information. The zombie argument is about **possibility**: what could be true given the physical facts. The two share a structure: both claim that the physical facts leave consciousness open, and both face the same reply, that conceptual or epistemic gaps need not be gaps in reality.

@example
title: Which premise does each reply deny?
problem: For each reply to the knowledge argument, name the premise it denies: (a) "Mary only gains abilities." (b) "Mary learns an old fact under a new concept." (c) "Mary would not learn anything."
step: (a) The ability reply says that no new fact is learned, so it denies P3.
step: (b) The new-mode reply accepts that she learns something but holds that it is an old physical fact grasped under a new concept, so it also denies P3: learning something new does not require a fact she did not know before.
step: (c) Denying that she learns anything denies P2.
step: Each reply leaves the argument valid and rejects a premise. (a) and (b) both deny P3 in different ways: the first says what Mary gains is know-how, the second that it is an old fact known in a new way.
answer: (a) P3; (b) P3; (c) P2.

title: Testing the zombie argument
problem: Ana says: "I can imagine a physical duplicate of me with no experience. So physicalism is false." Put in standard form and say what is needed.
step: P1: I can conceive of a physical duplicate of me with no consciousness.
step: P2: What is conceivable is metaphysically possible.
step: P3: If such a duplicate is possible, physicalism is false.
step: A critic can deny P1, by saying careful reflection shows incoherence, or P2, by saying that conceivability does not guarantee possibility.
answer: Ana needs both the conceivability-possibility bridge and the claim that zombie possibility refutes physicalism.

@mistakes
- Saying Mary learns that color exists. She already knew facts about color; the question is whether she learns what it is like.
- Thinking the ability reply denies that Mary gains anything. It says that she gains abilities and no new fact.
- Treating conceivability as the same as possibility.
- Saying the zombie behaves differently. By stipulation it behaves just as a person does.
