@article
Your examiners will not ask you whether you have read a list; they will ask what the author of one of its entries argued and whether the argument works. A reading list is therefore less an inventory than a promise: of each entry, you can state the thesis, give the argument in standard form, name the best objection, and say what changed because of it. A list built that way can be revised, examined and defended aloud (see Week 7).

### How lists are usually set

Practice varies: some programs publish a departmental list, others expect each student to build a list with advisors, and some give areas and let students choose. The rest of this lesson assumes that you build or adapt a list, and that your program's rules, and your advisors, settle its final form. Many students work from forty to one hundred entries across areas; the figure forty here is simply this course's target for a first draft.

### Principles

1. **Coverage.** Include the areas your program will examine. For a metaphysician these usually include metaphysics itself, philosophy of mind, philosophy of language, epistemology and the history of philosophy, and often logic and some value theory. A metaphysician who cannot discuss the semantics of names or the analysis of knowledge will be exposed by the questions that cross areas: modality depends on reference, causation on explanation, and mind on the metaphysics of properties.
2. **Anchors and frontier.** Combine works that define the debate with recent work that shows where it now stands. Anchor works are usually named in the entries and bibliographies of encyclopedia articles; recent work is found through PhilPapers, journals and your advisors.
3. **Depth over completeness.** Forty works read as arguments beat a hundred skimmed. Choose each so that it earns its place by being the best statement of a view, the best objection to one, or the work that changed the question.
4. **Both sides.** For each major debate include a work on each main side, so that you can state the best case against your own sympathies.
5. **Your research.** Weight the list toward the area in which you expect to write your dissertation, with enough sources to speak at the level of a specialist there.

### Annotating by argument

The annotation that helps in an exam is not a summary but a reconstruction. For each work write four lines: **thesis**, **argument** (premises, with the hidden premise), **best objection** and **where it sits** in the debate. The reason is simple:

| P1 | An examiner's question about a work asks for its thesis, its argument, the objections to it or its standing in the debate. |
| P2 | Notes that record only a summary of what the author says do not contain the premises, objections or standing of the argument. |
| P3 | If your notes do not contain what the examiner will ask for, they will not help you answer. |
| P4 | You should keep notes that will help you answer the examiner's questions. |
| C | So annotations should record thesis, argument, best objection and place in the debate, not a summary alone. |

A reader might object that summaries are quicker to write. That is true, but a summary is exactly the thing that cannot be turned into an answer under time pressure; the premise-by-premise reconstruction can.

### A balanced forty

An example of a distribution for a student whose dissertation will be in metaphysics (this is an illustration, not a requirement):

| Area | Works |
|---|---|
| Metaphysics: ontology, composition, persistence | 6 |
| Metaphysics: modality, essence and grounding | 6 |
| Metaphysics: causation, laws, time | 4 |
| Philosophy of mind | 6 |
| Philosophy of language | 6 |
| Epistemology | 6 |
| History of philosophy | 6 |

Classic anchors you might include, to give the flavor: Quine's "On What There Is" (1948), Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* (1980), Lewis's *On the Plurality of Worlds* (1986), Fine's "Essence and Modality" (1994), Jackson's "Epiphenomenal Qualia" (1982), Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974), Frege's "On Sense and Reference" (1892), Russell's "On Denoting" (1905), Gettier's 1963 paper on justified true belief, and the *Meditations* of Descartes. Add recent work from your own areas on the advice of your advisors.

### Checking a list

Ask: Is every area examined by your program covered? Does each debate have both sides? Does a good part of the list come from recent work, so that you know the present debate? Could you give a five-minute talk on each entry? Anything you cannot defend aloud should be read again or replaced.

@example
title: Annotating one work by argument
problem: Make a four-line annotation of Gettier's 1963 paper that an examiner could use as a prompt, without quoting it.
step: Thesis: justified true belief is not sufficient for knowledge, so the traditional analysis fails.
step: Argument: in each of two cases a subject has a justified true belief that is true by luck, because the justification supports a false belief from which the true belief is validly inferred. Hidden premise: if a belief is justified and one validly infers a conclusion from it, the conclusion is also justified.
step: Best objection or response: deny that the subject has knowledge-level justification (for example, because the justification relies on a false premise), or add a fourth condition; later cases show that many such repairs face new counterexamples.
step: Place in the debate: starts the search for a fourth condition and eventually helps motivate views that do not analyze knowledge at all, such as taking knowledge as basic.
answer: The annotation records thesis, argument with its hidden premise, best objection and the work's place in the debate.

@example
title: Balancing a draft list
problem: A student's draft of forty works has 14 on modality, 9 on mind, 8 on language, 5 on epistemology and 4 on history. Find the imbalances and repair them.
step: Count: 14 + 9 + 8 + 5 + 4 = 40, so the total is right, but two areas look thin.
step: Epistemology (5) and history (4) are the least covered, while modality (14) takes more than a third of the list.
step: If the student's dissertation will be on modality, weight toward it is acceptable, but fourteen modality works leave only 26 for four other areas that will be examined.
step: Repair: move four modality works out and add two to epistemology and two to history, giving 10, 9, 8, 7 and 6, which still totals 40.
answer: Move four entries from modality to epistemology and history so that every examined area is covered.

@mistakes
- Annotating with a plot summary instead of thesis, argument, best objection and place in the debate.
- Filling the list with works from one's own dissertation area and leaving other examined areas thin.
- Including only anchors from the twentieth century, so that the list cannot support a conversation about the current debate.
- Listing works you have only read about: if you could not give a five-minute talk on an entry, it is not yet on the list.

@practice self
prompt: Write the four-line annotation (thesis, argument with hidden premise, best objection, place in the debate) for Frege's "On Sense and Reference" or Russell's "On Denoting", in no more than 150 words.
hint: Start from the puzzle the work solves, then give the argument that the puzzle forces a distinction or an analysis.
solution: Frege. Thesis: a name or expression has both a reference (what it picks out) and a sense (the mode of presentation under which it picks it out). Argument: if the meaning of "Hesperus is Phosphorus" were only its reference, it would say the same as "Hesperus is Hesperus", but the first is informative and the second is trivial; hidden premise: sentences that differ in informativeness differ in cognitive significance and so in something semantically relevant. So there is something beyond reference. Best objection: Kripke and others argue that names refer without descriptive senses, and that identity statements between names are necessary though knowable only a posteriori, so the puzzle should be handled otherwise. Place in the debate: the origin of the contrast between descriptivist and direct-reference theories of names, still central in discussions of modality and content.
rubric: States the thesis correctly and distinguishes sense from reference.
rubric: Gives the informativeness argument with its hidden premise.
rubric: Names a genuine objection or rival, such as direct reference.
rubric: Says where the work sits in the debate in a sentence, without biography.
