@article
A prospectus is a proposal, a plan and an argument at once. The committee reads it to decide three things: whether the question is **worth answering**, whether your approach is **likely to work**, and whether you can **finish** in the time and with the resources you have. Practice varies in length and form (this course's target is 6,000 to 8,000 words), so use your program's rules as the authority, and use this lesson for the parts that most prospectuses share.

### The parts

1. **Research question and overview** (about a tenth of the length): the question, why it matters, and a summary of your answer.
2. **Literature review**: not a survey of everything, but a map of the debate that ends in a **gap**: what the literature leaves open, and why that matters.
3. **Thesis**: what the dissertation will argue, in one or two sentences, with the main argument in outline.
4. **Chapter outline**: for each chapter, its question, its argument, what it uses from earlier chapters and what it contributes.
5. **Timeline**: dated stages with buffers, and a plan for what to do if a chapter fails.
6. **Bibliography**: the works you cite and the core works you will use.

### The gap claim

The key sentence of a prospectus is the claim that the literature leaves open something that matters. It has to be established, not asserted: show where the positions leave a question unanswered, and why existing replies fail or do not apply. An example of the argument form:

| P1 | Essentialist accounts of metaphysical necessity hold that necessity is grounded in essence. |
| P2 | If an account holds that necessity is grounded in essence, then on that account knowledge of what is necessary derives from knowledge of essence. |
| C1 | So, on essentialist accounts, knowledge of what is necessary derives from knowledge of essence. |
| P3 | If, on essentialist accounts, knowledge of what is necessary derives from knowledge of essence, then an essentialist account of modality is adequate only if it explains how essences are known. |
| C2 | So an essentialist account of modality is adequate only if it explains how essences are known. |
| P4 | Existing essentialist accounts do not explain how essences are known. |
| C | So existing essentialist accounts of modality are not yet adequate. |

This argument (from an invented project) is valid, and the chapters of the dissertation would each defend or develop one of its steps. The premise that does the work in a prospectus is P4, the gap claim: the literature review exists to show that it is true. An examiner will ask: "Is P4 true of every essentialist account, or only some? Did you check?" Be ready to answer.

### The literature review as an argument

Organize a review by **debate**, not by author: the positions, the arguments that divide them, and the point at which your question arises. Attribute accurately and fairly, in the way you would want your own work to be treated. End each section by saying what it contributes to the gap. A review that lists summaries gives the committee no reason to think you know where the work needs to be done.

### The chapter outline

Each chapter entry should answer: What question does it address? What is its main argument (in two to four premises)? Which objection will it consider? What does it need from earlier chapters, and what does the next chapter need from it? A committee reads this to see whether the dissertation is a unified argument or a collection.

### Timeline and contingency

Date each chapter's draft, each round of feedback, and the submission, working backward from the end of funding and, if you plan to go on the job market, the dates of applications. Include a **contingency**: what you will do if a central argument fails (for example, narrow the claim, or turn a chapter into an objection that the dissertation answers). Committees often respond well to a plan that admits what might go wrong.

### Style

Write plainly, state commitments, and avoid promising what you cannot deliver ("I will solve the problem of..."). Use specific, modest verbs: argue, defend, show that, develop.

@example
title: Writing a chapter entry
problem: Write the entry for a chapter of the invented project above that argues that knowledge of essence can come from understanding definitions of kinds.
step: Question: "Can knowledge of essence be explained as understanding the real definition of a kind?"
step: Argument: "P1: Knowing what a thing essentially is consists in grasping its real definition. P2: Understanding the concept of a kind can give grasp of its real definition. C: So knowledge of essence can be explained as an exercise of conceptual understanding."
step: Objection: "Conceptual understanding may give only nominal definitions of our words, not real definitions of things."
step: Role: "Draws on the gap established in Chapter 1 and provides the epistemology that Chapter 3 will use for specific necessities."
answer: The entry has a question, an argument in premises, an objection and a role in the whole.

@example
title: Testing a gap claim
problem: A student writes, "No one has explained how essences are known." What should she do before putting this in a prospectus?
step: Narrow it to something testable: which accounts, in which sense of "explain"?
step: Search the literature (encyclopedia entries, PhilPapers, recent journals) for essentialist accounts of modal knowledge, and read the best candidates.
step: Restate the claim accurately: for instance, "Existing accounts either give a conceptual story that applies only to some essences or leave the epistemology unspecified."
answer: Test the claim against the literature, then state it in a narrower form that is true.

@mistakes
- Treating the literature review as a list of summaries: organize by debate and end in the gap.
- Asserting a gap without having searched the literature and read the strongest candidates.
- Promising to solve a large problem instead of making a specific claim you can defend.
- A timeline with no buffers and no plan for what to do if an argument fails.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: draft an outline of a prospectus on a project of your own (or an invented one) with these headings: question; gap claim (in standard form with at least three premises); thesis; chapter list (four chapters, each with a question and a one-sentence argument); a timeline in months with one contingency.
hint: Make each chapter answer a step of your gap or thesis argument.
solution: (Invented project.) Question: Does a Humean account of laws explain why regularities hold? Gap claim: P1 If an account makes laws mere summaries of regularities, it faces the circularity objection that laws cannot explain the regularities they summarize. P2 Humean accounts make laws the axioms of the best system, a summary of the regularities. C1 So Humean accounts face the circularity objection. P3 An account that faces that objection is complete only if it says what explanatory work laws do if they do not explain the regularities. P4 Existing Humean replies deny that laws must explain regularities but do not say what explanatory work laws do instead. C So existing Humean accounts are not yet complete. Thesis: Humeans should accept a deflationary account of the explanatory role of laws, on which laws explain by unification, and this answers the circularity objection. Chapters: 1. What is the circularity objection, and what does it assume about explanation? Argument: the objection requires that explanation be causal or generative. 2. Does unification explain? Argument: unification explanations are non-causal and work in science. 3. Does best-system unification meet the standard? Argument: the best system meets standards of strength and simplicity that give it explanatory force. 4. Objections: whether the deflationary account is stable, and what follows for counterfactuals. Timeline: months 1 to 3 chapter 1 draft; months 4 to 7 chapters 2 and 3; months 8 to 10 chapter 4; contingency: if chapter 2's argument fails, reframe the dissertation as a defense of explanatory pluralism in the laws debate.
rubric: The gap claim is in valid standard form with the gap premise identified.
rubric: Each chapter has a question and an argument that serves the thesis.
rubric: The timeline has dates and a realistic contingency.
rubric: The thesis is specific and could be wrong.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, write the gap-claim paragraph of a prospectus for an invented project, stating the gap, how you checked it, and why it matters.
hint: Say which accounts you examined and what they leave open.
solution: (Invented project, illustrating the form; check any real claim against the literature.) The literature on essentialist accounts of modality, in the tradition of Fine's work on essence, holds that metaphysical necessity is grounded in essence. In this invented project, suppose the claim to be defended is that the literature has said too little about how we come to know essences. Suppose I examined the leading essentialist treatments and the main surveys of modal epistemology and found that they either appeal to a conceptual route that applies to the essences of kinds such as water and gold only through scientific discovery, or they leave the epistemology as a further question. This matters because an account of modality that makes our knowledge of necessity depend on knowledge of essence is adequate only if the latter is explained. Without such an account, essentialism has an epistemological debt that rivals, such as possible-worlds accounts, do not obviously have in the same form. My project addresses this debt.

rubric: Explains why the gap matters to the debate.
