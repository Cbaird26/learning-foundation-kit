@article
In 2020 PhilPapers surveyed professional philosophers on a long list of questions. The survey had 1,785 respondents, though fewer answered each question. The figures reported on each question are the percentages of respondents who **accept or lean toward** each answer, and respondents could choose more than one option, or none, so the percentages on a question need not add up to 100. The survey's own pages caution against comparing its figures directly with those of its 2009 survey, because the populations and the question sets are not the same.

### A selection of the figures

The table gives a few results, rounded, for orientation. Do not memorize them.

| Question | Results |
|---|---|
| Abstract objects | Platonism 38%, nominalism 42% |
| Free will | Compatibilism 59%, libertarianism 19%, no free will 11% |
| God | Theism 19%, atheism 67% |
| Mind | Physicalism 52%, non-physicalism 32% |
| Time | A-theory 27%, B-theory 38% |
| Personal identity | Psychological view 44%, biological view 19%, further-fact view 15% |
| Laws of nature | Non-Humean 54%, Humean 31% |
| Zombies | Metaphysically possible 24%, conceivable but not metaphysically possible 37%, inconceivable 16% |
| External world | Non-skeptical realism 80%, skepticism 5%, idealism 7% |
| Quantum mechanics (556 answered) | Hidden variables 22%, many worlds 19%, collapse 17%, epistemic 13% |
| Consciousness | Functionalism 33%, dualism 22%, identity theory 13%, panpsychism 8%, eliminativism 5% |

Many of these questions are divided, with no answer near a clear majority; a few, such as the external world, are lopsided. On the interpretation of quantum mechanics, in particular, there is no majority view.

### What the figures do not show

The share of philosophers who hold a view is **not evidence that the view is true**, and nothing in this course should be read in that way. Consider the argument that a student might be tempted to make:

| P1 | If a view is accepted or leaned toward by most professional philosophers, then it is true. |
| P2 | View V is accepted or leaned toward by most professional philosophers. |
| C | So V is true. |

The argument is valid, but P1 should be rejected as stated: a majority's acceptance is not an argument, views are correlated by training and by fashion, and the same group of experts divides on almost every deep question. The figures also depend on the wording of the options, on who responded, and on what "lean toward" means. And the survey reports what philosophers believe, not why: two people who both accept physicalism may hold it for different reasons, and a view's popularity says nothing about whether the best arguments for it are sound. Your answer to an examiner who cites the survey is to say what the best argument for each side is.

### What the figures are good for

1. **Knowing your audience.** If you defend a view that few hold, expect to have to say why the usual objections fail; if you defend a majority view, expect to be asked what the minority sees.
2. **Finding live debates.** Questions with divided answers are the ones where argument still does work, and where a dissertation can contribute.
3. **Calibrating a literature review.** The survey shows which positions you must be able to state fairly.
4. **Understanding disagreement as data about the field.** It shows that informed philosophers do not agree, which is a reason for modesty in how you present your thesis.

### Placing your project

For your dissertation topic, write three sentences: the question; the main positions and who holds them; and where you stand and why. Then check the survey for the nearest question. If your question is not in the survey (most research questions are narrower), say which survey question it falls under.

### The mock oral exam

This week's block deliverable is a mock oral exam on your reading list. Ask two faculty members or advanced students to examine you for an hour: a short opening on your thesis, questions on each area, and one question about where the field stands and why. Record it, listen to it, and write a one-page report on what to fix.

@example
title: Reading a survey result correctly
problem: The survey reports, for the mind, physicalism 52% and non-physicalism 32%. A classmate says, "So 84% of philosophers take a side, and 16% are undecided." Is that right?
step: Check what the figures are: the percentages of respondents who accept or lean toward each view.
step: Respondents could select more than one option or none, and there may be other answers than these two, so the figures need not be exhaustive or exclusive.
step: The sum 52 + 32 = 84 therefore does not show that 16% are undecided: the remainder may include other options, those who chose none, or those who chose more than one.
answer: No: the figures need not sum to 100, so the remainder is not the undecided.

@mistakes
- Treating the percentages as evidence of truth: they show what philosophers believe, not whether it is correct.
- Adding the percentages to 100 and calling the rest "undecided": respondents could choose more than one option or none.
- Comparing the 2020 figures directly with the 2009 survey: the survey's own pages caution against this.
- Memorizing the numbers: use them only for orientation, and do not let them replace arguments.

Why believe it?" Use an invented topic if you do not yet have one.
hint: Do not answer with numbers; answer with the best argument and say what the figures show.
solution: Placement (invented topic): The question is whether we are human animals or persons whose identity over time consists in psychological continuity. Psychological theorists in the tradition of Locke and Parfit say that a person survives where the right psychological connections do; animalists such as Olson say that we are organisms; and further-fact views say that identity is a deep fact beyond both. I defend animalism because of the thinking-animal argument: there is a human animal where I am, it has the brain and seems to think, so if I were something else there would be two thinkers in my chair and I could not tell which one I am. Reply: The figures show that the biological view is a minority position and that the question is divided, and they could not show that the minority view is mistaken, since the survey reports what philosophers believe, not what is true. What matters is whether the argument works. The best reply is that the animal thinks only derivatively, or that the person is constituted by the animal without being a second thinker; I answer that this gives up the simple premise that whatever has the brain's thinking does the thinking, and I admit that animalism faces the transplant and fission cases, which I would have to meet. If the examiner thinks the psychological view handles those cases better, we can compare the costs.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says in a seminar: "The survey shows most philosophers are compatibilists about free will, so the libertarian should drop the view." Respond in about 150 words, in the way you would in a mock oral exam.
hint: Distinguish what the survey shows from what an argument would show.
solution: I think the survey shows something about where the field is, and nothing about which view is correct. The figures, as I recall, show a majority leaning toward compatibilism and a smaller group leaning toward libertarianism, which tells us that the question is live and that each side owes the other an answer. But the share is the share of respondents who accept or lean toward a view, and a view's popularity does not show that its arguments work. If the libertarian should drop the view, it should be because the best objection succeeds: that an indeterministic act is a matter of luck rather than control. Libertarians have replies, such as agent-causal accounts, and compatibilists must in turn answer the consequence argument. So I would ask us to compare the arguments. I am happy to say that the survey tells me what the burden of explanation looks like in the room.
rubric: Separates what the survey shows (belief) from what would show a view false (argument).
rubric: States an actual argument for or against the view rather than appealing to numbers.
rubric: Keeps a tone suitable to a seminar or oral exam.
