@article
A first-year doctoral student in metaphysics usually has a good sense of what the first semester asks (read, write, speak up in seminar) and almost no sense of what the fourth year asks. This course is built for that gap. The milestones of a PhD are less like a staircase of hurdles than like a series of changes in what you are trusted to do: first to master what others have said, then to show that mastery under pressure, then to propose something of your own, and finally to carry it out.

### The common shape

In the United States a philosophy PhD usually takes about five to seven years, and many programs fund their students through some or all of that time. The stages below are common practice, not a rule: programs differ in names, order, timing and format, and programs in other countries often differ a great deal (many have no formal coursework or qualifying exam at all).

| Stage | What it usually involves | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Coursework | Seminars across the field, often with distribution requirements in logic, history and value theory | Breadth, and the habits of seminar reading, writing and discussion |
| Qualifying or comprehensive requirement | Varies: timed written exams on a reading list, a qualifying paper or portfolio, an oral exam, or a mix | Showing command of the field and the ability to write and argue under constraint |
| Prospectus | A written plan for the dissertation, often followed by a meeting with the committee | Getting approval for a feasible, original project |
| Dissertation | Sustained research, often a book-length argument or a connected set of papers, depending on the program | Making an original contribution |
| Defense and degree | A final examination of the finished work and any required revisions | Certifying the work |

Between the qualifying requirement and the defense, many programs call a student a candidate (sometimes "ABD", all but dissertation). Funding, teaching duties and time limits frequently change at that point.

### What varies, and why you should check

Four things vary most: the **form** of the qualifying requirement (exam, paper, portfolio), its **timing** (end of the second year, the third year), its **consequences** (a retake, a master's degree, dismissal) and the **composition of committees**. Nothing in this course can tell you your program's rules. Treat the following argument as the reason for starting with the handbook:

| P1 | Programs differ in the form, timing and consequences of their milestones. |
| P2 | If programs differ, then what you heard about another program's milestones may not hold in yours. |
| P3 | If what you heard may not hold in yours, you should rely on your own program's written rules and your advisors. |
| C | So you should rely on your own program's written rules and your advisors. |

The argument is valid, and P1 is a plain fact about the profession. The best objection targets P3: senior students' accounts of how rules are applied in practice are real evidence that the handbook does not contain. The reply is that the conclusion survives with a small amendment: use stories as a supplement to the written rules and to your advisors, never a substitute. When the two conflict, ask a faculty member or the director of graduate studies.

### Reading a handbook like a philosopher

Treat the handbook as a set of premises. Underline every date, number and verb: "must", "may", "by the end of". Note what is **required** and what is merely customary. Ask who decides each judgment (the committee, the director, the whole faculty) and what the appeal route is. Then write the answers in your own words and check them with the director of graduate studies.

### Backward planning

Work backward from the fixed points: the end of funding, the last date to hold a prospectus defense, the date of the qualifying exam. Reading for a comprehensive exam takes many weeks of steady work, and a prospectus takes several drafts, so schedule each with a buffer. Many students also plan around the academic job market (see Week 12), whose application deadlines usually fall in the autumn: a dissertation draft that is far along by then makes a job application stronger. For current facts about the profession, consult the American Philosophical Association and your own program; conditions change.

@example
title: Turning a handbook into a calendar
problem: An invented handbook says: students take 12 courses at 3 per semester; the qualifying exam must be taken within two semesters after coursework ends; the prospectus must be defended within two semesters after the exam; funding lasts 5 years of two semesters each. Work out the latest semester in which each milestone can occur and how much slack is left for the dissertation.
step: Coursework: 12 courses at 3 per semester takes 12 / 3 = 4 semesters, so it ends at the close of semester 4.
step: The exam must be taken within two semesters after that, so by semester 6 at the latest.
step: The prospectus must be defended within two semesters after the exam, so by semester 8 at the latest.
step: Funding covers 5 x 2 = 10 semesters. If every milestone is taken at its last permitted moment, only 10 - 8 = 2 semesters remain for writing and defending the dissertation, which is unrealistic.
step: So the sensible plan takes each milestone earlier than its deadline, and the point of the exercise is to see that the deadlines are ceilings, not targets.
answer: Exam by semester 6 and prospectus by semester 8 at the latest, leaving two funded semesters; aim well ahead of both deadlines.

@example
title: Sorting hearsay from rules
problem: A senior student tells you, "Nobody ever fails the exam here, and they only read the first page of your answers." Decide what to do with this.
step: Classify the claims. "Nobody fails" is a claim about outcomes; "only the first page" is a claim about how graders read. Neither is a rule, and both may be false or out of date.
step: Check each against the written handbook and ask the director of graduate studies, or a faculty member who has graded the exam, what the exam is graded on.
step: Even if the claims are true, preparing as if they were false costs little and protects you if they are not.
answer: Treat the stories as hints to be checked against the handbook and the faculty, and prepare to the stated standard.

@mistakes
- Treating common practice as a rule: the qualifying requirement, its timing and the committee structure vary by program, and some programs differ in kind (no exam, a paper instead).
- Planning each milestone for its last permitted date: deadlines are ceilings, and delay in one stage cascades into the next and into funding.
- Relying on hearsay about "how it really works" instead of the written rules and the director of graduate studies.
- Assuming the dissertation is the first original work: the prospectus and often a qualifying paper already ask you to take a position.

prompt: Which statement about doctoral milestones is the most accurate?
choice*: The order and form of milestones are common practice that varies by program, so each student should confirm them in their own program's rules.
choice: Every philosophy PhD requires a three-hour written qualifying exam.

@quiz num
prompt: An invented program funds 11 semesters. It requires coursework of 4 semesters, a qualifying exam in the semester right after, and a prospectus defense one semester after the exam. If the dissertation then takes 6 semesters, how many semesters of funding are missing?
answer: 1
unit: semesters
check: max(0, 4 + 1 + 1 + 6 - 11)
hint: Add the semesters used by each stage.
solution: The stages take 4 + 1 + 1 + 6 = 12 semesters if each follows the last without a gap, and funding covers 11, so 12 - 11 = 1 semester is unfunded. A student who wants slack must plan for it, and should talk to the director of graduate studies early about what happens at the end of funding.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences why a graduate student should plan backward from the deadlines of the qualifying requirement and prospectus rather than forward from the end of coursework, and state one cost or risk of planning backward.
hint: What does a deadline do to the usable time before it, and what could go wrong if your estimates are too optimistic?
solution: Planning backward starts from the fixed points, such as the last permitted semester for the exam and the end of funding, and asks what must be finished by when. That makes slack visible: if every stage takes its last permitted moment, nothing remains for the dissertation, as in a plan that uses all ten funded semesters before the writing begins. Planning forward from the end of coursework hides this, because each stage seems to have time. The risk of backward planning is false precision: reading and drafting speeds are uncertain, and an apparently tight schedule may collapse after one bad semester, so a good plan includes buffers and reviews with an advisor every term.
rubric: Explains that deadlines are ceilings and backward planning reveals whether slack exists.
rubric: Connects the point to a concrete stage such as funding or the exam date.
rubric: States a real cost or risk, such as false precision or an optimistic estimate.
