The three areas of this lesson are tested together because their problems meet in metaphysics. The mind-body problem is about the relation of two kinds of property; the semantics of names is about how thought and language hook onto the world, and epistemology asks what we can know of either. As in Lesson 3, the aim is a map, with the standard attributions, rather than a replacement for the readings.

### Mind

The **knowledge argument** (Jackson, "Epiphenomenal Qualia", 1982) is set out as follows, with its hidden premises supplied:

| P1 | Before her release, Mary knows every physical fact about color vision. |
| P2 | On her release, Mary learns a fact she did not know before. |
| P3 | If someone knows every physical fact, then any fact she does not know is not a physical fact. |
| C1 | So the fact Mary learns on release is not a physical fact. |
| P4 | If there is a fact that is not physical, then physicalism is false. |
| C | So physicalism is false. |

It is valid, and each reply denies a premise. The **ability hypothesis** (Lewis, Nemirow) denies P2: Mary gains abilities (to imagine, recognize, remember) and no new fact. The **acquaintance** and **phenomenal concept** replies deny P2 in a more careful form: Mary comes to know an old fact under a new, phenomenal concept, so what she gains is not a new fact. (P3 is close to trivial, since a fact a person does not know cannot be among the facts she knows.) Some deny P1, saying that we cannot imagine what knowing every physical fact would be like. Jackson has since rejected the conclusion, a good reminder that the argument and its author's later view are different things.

**Searle's Chinese room** (1980) targets strong AI, the view that running the right program suffices for understanding. A person who does not understand Chinese follows a rule book to produce fluent replies; Searle says he understands nothing, and the same goes for any program. Replies include the **systems reply** (the whole room understands) and the **robot reply**; Searle answers that the person could internalize the system. As of 2026 there is no consensus about whether any artificial system understands or is conscious, and the argument is a way of structuring that debate, not of closing it. On the **hard problem**, Chalmers (1995) distinguishes the easy problems of explaining functions from the problem of why there is experience at all.

### Language

**Frege** distinguishes sense from reference to explain how "Hesperus is Phosphorus" can be informative. **Russell's theory of descriptions** ("On Denoting", 1905) analyzes "The F is G" as "there is exactly one F, and it is G", which handles empty descriptions without a mysterious non-existent object. **Kripke** (*Naming and Necessity*) argues that ordinary names are **rigid designators** that refer to the same object in every world in which it exists, and that the descriptions speakers associate with names do not fix their reference (the modal, epistemic and semantic arguments against descriptivism). Kripke's picture links names to their bearers by a causal-historical chain. Rival views include two-dimensional semantics and neo-descriptivism.

### Epistemology

**Gettier** (1963) gave cases of justified true belief that is not knowledge, ending the dominance of the justified-true-belief analysis. The main families of response are: add a **fourth condition** (no false lemmas, defeasibility), a **reliabilist** account where knowledge is true belief produced by a reliable process (Goldman), a **safety** or **sensitivity** condition (Nozick on sensitivity; safety is the thought that one could not easily have been wrong), and **knowledge-first** epistemology (Williamson), which treats knowledge as basic and unanalyzable and uses it to explain other notions, such as evidence. Contextualists hold that "knows" varies with the conversational context; invariantists deny it.

### Using the map in an exam

An answer on a core topic does four things in about twenty to thirty minutes: states the question and your thesis in the first paragraph; presents the best argument for a position; presents the best objection and the best reply; and says what you conclude and what would change your mind. Practice with the items below, and keep the two timed answers for your block's deliverable.

@example
title: Which premise does each reply deny?
problem: Match three replies to Jackson's argument (as set out in the article) with the premise each denies: (a) Mary only gains an ability; (b) Mary knows an old physical fact under a new concept; (c) Mary could not truly know every physical fact without having had color experience.
step: Reply (a) says that nothing she learns is a fact: she gains know-how. That denies P2.
step: Reply (b) accepts that she learns something but says it is a physical fact known in a new way, so it is not a new fact. That also denies P2 in a refined form: the thing she gains is not a new fact. (P3 is close to trivial, so it is not the premise at issue.)
step: Reply (c) denies that her prior knowledge was complete, so it denies P1.
step: Notice that none of the three denies validity: the argument is valid, so every reply must reject a premise.
answer: (a) P2, (b) P2, (c) P1.

@mistakes
- Treating the knowledge argument as invalid: it is valid, and replies must deny a premise.
- Saying that Searle shows that no machine could think: his target is the claim that program-running alone suffices for understanding, and he allows that a machine might think.
- Saying that rigid designation means that a name has a necessary referent: it means the name refers to the same object in every world in which that object exists.
- Treating Gettier as showing that knowledge is impossible: it shows that justified true belief is insufficient.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: set a timer for 30 minutes and write a timed answer to "Does the Chinese room show that running a program cannot suffice for understanding?" Then compare it with a model of about 220 words.
hint: State what the argument is, which premise carries the weight and which reply is strongest.
solution: Searle's argument is that a person following a rule book to manipulate Chinese symbols produces fluent replies without understanding Chinese; since the program is only the rule book, and executing it does not give understanding, running a program does not suffice for understanding. The weight rests on the claim that if the person does not understand, nothing in the system does. The systems reply denies this: the person is a part, and the whole system might understand. Searle responds that the person could memorize the rule book and still not understand. A defender of the systems reply says that this puts the system in the person's head without changing what is doing the understanding. I would say that the argument shows that syntax by itself, as we ordinarily characterize it, is not obviously sufficient for semantics, but it does not show that no system built from a program could understand, because it relies on an intuition about the person which the systems reply can contest. As of now, no consensus exists on whether any artificial system understands, so the argument clarifies where the disagreement lies rather than settling it.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 200 words, as in a timed exam, state the best objection to the safety condition on knowledge and say how a defender could respond.
hint: Think of cases in which a belief seems safe but is not knowledge, or the reverse.
solution: Safety says that S knows p only if S's belief could not easily have been false. One objection is that necessary truths are trivially safe: a belief in a mathematical truth formed by guesswork could not have been false, since the truth could not have been false, yet it is not knowledge. So safety is not sufficient, and without a way of restricting the comparison to the way the belief was formed, it does not rule out luck. The defender responds by relativizing safety to the method or basis: the question is whether S could easily have formed a false belief on the same basis, and a lucky guess could easily have produced a false belief about a different proposition. This reply moves the work to how bases are individuated, which is itself contested, so the objection shows that safety needs a theory of bases rather than defeating it.
rubric: States safety correctly and gives a real objection with a concrete case.
rubric: Gives a defender's response and says what it costs or what remains open.
rubric: Is concise and organized for a timed setting.
