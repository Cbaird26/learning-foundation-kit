@article
An oral exam is a conversation conducted under conditions you would not choose: examiners may interrupt, the questions may move quickly between areas, and you cannot reread. Programs vary in how they use oral examinations (a stand-alone exam on a reading list, a discussion of written exams, or a part of the prospectus meeting), so find out the format, the length and who will be in the room from your own program. What the formats share is that the examiners are trying to see how you think, and the skill is largely trainable.

### The questions you will meet

| Type | Example in outline | What it asks for |
|---|---|---|
| Explain | "What is Kripke's modal argument against descriptivism?" | A clear, accurate statement of the argument |
| Defend | "Why do you think composition is restricted?" | A position and the reasons for it |
| Object | "Doesn't that view make facts about origin arbitrary?" | A fair reading of the objection and a reply |
| Connect | "How does your view of reference bear on essentialism?" | Seeing links between areas |
| Apply | "What does your theory say about a ship whose planks are replaced?" | Using a theory on a case |
| Evaluate | "Is the knowledge argument sound?" | A verdict with reasons |

### The ninety-second answer

Most questions can be answered well in about ninety seconds if you use four moves: **claim** (say your answer), **reason** (the best argument for it in two or three steps), **example** (one case that makes it concrete) and **limit** (where the view is weakest or what it does not claim). Stop there and let the examiner ask for more. A short, structured answer invites a follow-up; a ten-minute monologue spends the examiners' time on the topic you chose and not the one they want to test.

### Handling an objection

Set out the response in four moves: restate the objection in its strongest form ("As I understand it, you are asking whether..."), concede what is true, reply to the rest, and say what cost remains. Restating shows that you have understood, and it gives you thinking time.

Consider the **Humphrey objection** to Lewis's modal realism: Kripke's point is that if what makes it true that Humphrey might have won is that a counterpart of Humphrey wins in another world, then why should Humphrey care? Lewis's reply is that the counterpart relation does not need identity: representing him as possibly winning only requires a counterpart who is similar in the relevant respects, and he accepts that the cost of that is the strangeness of the theory.

### When you do not know

Do not bluff. Say what you do know and reason aloud: "I'm not sure of the details of that paper, but the question seems to be about X, and I'd expect the argument to go like this." Examiners generally prefer a candidate who identifies the gap and reasons well from the knowledge they have to one who invents a citation. If you realize you made a mistake, correct it plainly; this is a sign of strength.

### Preparing

Practice aloud, not silently, because speaking and thinking silently are different skills. Use a **mock oral** with two or three faculty or advanced students: ask them to interrupt, to change topic suddenly and to press the best objection. Make a set of cards, one for each work on your list, with a thesis, premises, an objection and a link to another work, and answer them aloud with a timer. Record yourself and listen for filler words, for long openings and for the point where your explanation loses clarity. For notes on your program's rules, ask the director of graduate studies, since no general advice can replace that.

### An argument for concession

| P1 | An examiner who raises an objection wants to see whether you can tell a decisive point from a limited one. |
| P2 | If an objection is correct about a limited point, denying it shows that you cannot tell the two apart. |
| P3 | If you concede the limited point and defend the thesis, you show the discrimination the examiner is looking for. |
| P4 | You should act so as to show the examiner the discrimination that he or she is looking for. |
| C | So you should concede correct limited points while defending the thesis. |

The argument is valid. The best objection to P1 is that some examiners press merely to test your resolve, and conceding looks like weakness. The reply is that a concession followed by a reason is firmness: you yield only the point that is right.

@example
title: Answering the Humphrey objection aloud
problem: An examiner says: "Under Lewis's view, what makes it true that Humphrey might have won is something about a counterpart in another world. Why should that matter to Humphrey?" Give a ninety-second answer.
step: Restate: "You are pressing the worry that a counterpart is not Humphrey, so facts about him seem irrelevant to what Humphrey could have done."
step: Concede: "That's a fair statement of the cost, and Kripke raised it against the view."
step: Reply: "Lewis's response is that being possibly F is analyzed as having a counterpart that is F, where counterparthood is similarity in respects that matter in the context, and that this is our ordinary way of representing what could have been, not a strange extra demand."
step: Limit: "I think this shows that the view is coherent, but the cost, the sharp divide between what is relevant to Humphrey and what happens elsewhere, remains."
answer: Restate, concede the real cost, give the reply, and name the remaining cost.

@example
title: Reasoning aloud about an unfamiliar case
problem: An examiner asks: "Could this table have been made from a different block of wood?" You have not prepared this. Respond.
step: Say what the question is about: "This is the question of the necessity of origin, which Kripke defends for biological and material origins."
step: Give a view and a reason: "I'd say probably not from a wholly different block, on the thought that a small difference in matter might be tolerable but a large one is not."
step: Note the difficulty: "But that raises a sorites-style worry about how much difference is permissible, so the principle may be less definite than it looks."
answer: Name the issue, commit to a view with a reason, and flag the best worry.

@mistakes
- Giving a ten-minute reply without letting the examiner follow up: use claim, reason, example and limit, then stop.
- Bluffing or inventing a citation: say what you know and reason aloud instead.
- Treating every objection as an attack: restate it fairly and concede what is correct.
- Practicing only in writing: spoken explanation is a separate skill that needs spoken practice.

@practice self
prompt: An examiner says: "The ability hypothesis dissolves the knowledge argument, doesn't it? Mary only gains a skill." Write what you would say in about ninety seconds (roughly 180 words), using restate, concede, reply and limit.
hint: Say which premise the ability hypothesis denies, and why some find that denial incomplete.
solution: As I understand it, you are asking whether the ability hypothesis shows that Mary learns no new fact. That is a fair point: if what she gains is know-how, such as the ability to imagine and recognize red, then the second premise, that she learns a fact she did not know, is false, and the argument's conclusion does not follow. I think the hypothesis is a serious reply, and it is attractive because know-how is an acknowledged kind of knowledge that does not seem to require a new fact. But it faces a standard worry: when Mary sees red, she seems to learn that this is what seeing red is like, and this looks like knowledge of a fact, expressible in a sentence. Defenders respond that the sentence expresses an old physical fact under a new concept. So I would say that the hypothesis does not by itself dissolve the argument; it needs support from an account of what Mary learns. The remaining cost is that it must explain why her knowledge seems propositional.

rubric: Is the right length and style for speech: short sentences, no citation without confidence.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a script of about 200 words for a two-minute oral explanation of Frege's puzzle about identity to an examiner who is not a specialist in language. Include a claim, reasons, an example and a limit.
hint: Start from the contrast between two identity statements.
solution: Frege's puzzle asks why "Hesperus is Phosphorus" is informative while "Hesperus is Hesperus" is not, if both names refer to the same planet and meaning is just reference. My claim is that reference alone cannot be the whole of meaning. Reason: if the two names contributed only their reference, the two sentences would say the same thing, and so someone who understands both would find them equally trivial. But astronomers did not know the first before discovering it, and that discovery was informative. Frege concludes that each name also has a sense, a way of presenting the planet, and that the senses differ. Example: one name was introduced for the planet seen in the evening and the other for the planet seen in the morning. Limit: Kripke and others argue that names do not have descriptive senses and that the puzzle should be explained by other means, for instance by differences in how speakers use names, so Frege's solution is influential, not uncontested.
rubric: States the puzzle correctly with the two identity sentences.
rubric: Explains that sense is added to reference, and gives a clear example.
rubric: Flags the direct-reference objection as a limit, with accurate attribution.
