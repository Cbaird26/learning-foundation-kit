@article
Timed writing is a different skill from the careful writing of a seminar paper. Under the clock you cannot polish, and you cannot read everything again: what you can do is plan quickly, commit to a thesis, and write clear, correct paragraphs. Faculty readers of qualifying exams generally want to see that you know the debate, can argue for a position, and can recognize the best objection; they rarely want a catalogue of everything you have read. Formats differ by program (some use take-home papers, others several hours in a room), so confirm yours and use this lesson as a training method.

### Why an answer needs a thesis

| P1 | A qualifying exam rewards answers that show you could advance a debate. |
| P2 | Advancing a debate requires a position that is supported by argument and could be wrong. |
| P3 | A survey of positions without a thesis takes no position that could be wrong. |
| C | So a good exam answer states and defends a thesis rather than only surveying positions. |

The argument is valid. The best objection is to P2: an exam is about breadth, and a fair survey shows breadth. The reply is that you can show breadth inside an argument: each position you survey is introduced because the thesis must answer it. A survey with no thesis cannot show that you know which positions matter.

### Reading the question

Underline the **task word** (explain, assess, compare, defend) and the **scope** (one argument, one debate, two views). "Assess Lewis's argument from vagueness" asks you to reconstruct and judge a single argument, not to survey composition. "Compare and evaluate" requires a verdict, not two summaries. If a question has two parts, answer both, and use the proportions of marks if they are stated.

### Planning in five to ten minutes

1. **Thesis** in one sentence (a verdict).
2. **Three moves**: the best argument for the position you reject, the strongest objection to your thesis, and your reply.
3. **A defined term** or distinction that your answer needs.
4. **One example** or case to make an abstract point concrete.
5. **A closing line** that says what would change your mind or what remains open.

### Pacing a three-hour exam

Suppose three questions in three hours. Reserve about ten minutes at the start for reading and choosing, about twenty at the end for review, and divide the rest equally. Stop writing each answer when its time is up and leave a note of what you would add; an unfinished third answer costs more than a slightly shorter first one. Practice this with a clock until the pacing is automatic. Keep the same routine each time: read, plan, write, check.

### Common failures

The recurring faults of timed answers are: a long introduction that restates the question; **name-dropping** without argument; no objection, or an objection to a weak version; an ending that runs out of time; and unclear terms ("physicalism" used in two senses). Practice fixes these by naming your most frequent fault after each mock exam and targeting it in the next.

### Scoring yourself

After each answer, read it against a rubric: Does it state a thesis in the first paragraph? Is the argument valid as stated? Does it present the best objection? Are the attributions accurate? Is it finished? Then rewrite its weakest paragraph in ten minutes.

@example
title: Planning an answer from a question
problem: Question: "Assess Lewis's argument from vagueness for unrestricted composition." Produce a five-line plan.
step: Reconstruct the argument briefly: any restriction on composition would be vague; vague composition would make existence vague; existence cannot be vague; so composition is unrestricted (or never occurs).
step: Thesis: the argument is strong against vague restrictions, but its premise that existence cannot be vague carries the weight and can be resisted.
step: Moves: state the argument; give the best objection (existence may be vague, for instance if some things only borderline compose something, as in views that allow metaphysical vagueness); give the reply (the vagueness of a restriction would have to be in the world rather than in language).
step: Concept to define: "vague existence", distinguishing semantic from ontic vagueness.
answer: A plan with a thesis, three moves, a defined distinction and a closing note.

@example
title: Allocating time
problem: A three-hour exam has four questions, of which you must answer three. You reserve 10 minutes for reading and 20 for review. How long does each answer get?
step: Subtract the reserved time from the total: 180 - 10 - 20 = 150 minutes.
step: Divide by three answers: 150 / 3 = 50 minutes each.
step: Set checkpoints: planning by minute 8 of each answer, the objection by minute 30, and conclusion started by minute 45.
answer: 50 minutes for each answer.

@mistakes
- Opening with a long summary of the question's topic rather than a thesis.
- Surveying every position instead of arguing for one: breadth is shown by selecting what the argument needs.
- Giving the objection to the weak version of a view, or leaving out the reply.
- Spending the whole time on the first answer and leaving the last unfinished.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: take a timed three-hour written exam as described in this lesson (three questions from your own areas, with the plan, the pacing and the checks). Before you begin, write the one-sentence thesis and three-move plan for this question: "Is it a problem for the counterfactual account of causation that it is hard to handle preemption?" Then compare with the model.
hint: Name which kind of preemption you will discuss and what a fix would cost.
solution: Thesis: preemption is a real problem for the simple counterfactual account, but it does not defeat the program, since refinements show how to preserve counterfactual dependence along chains of events; the price is that the account becomes more complicated and late preemption remains harder. Move one: set out the case (a rock thrown by Mira breaks a window and would have been broken by Noor's rock), and say that the effect does not counterfactually depend on the actual cause. Move two: the best objection to my thesis is that every refinement is ad hoc and leaves new counterexamples; the reply is that the refinements track a principled feature (dependence along a chain of intermediate events), which handles early preemption, while late preemption shows the limits. Move three: say what this suggests about the rival accounts: interventionist and power-based views take a different starting point and avoid the problem in different ways, with their own costs. Close by saying that what would change my mind is a refined account that handles late preemption without ad hoc adjustment, or a principled argument that no counterfactual analysis could.
rubric: The thesis is a verdict that could be wrong, not a statement of the topic.
rubric: The three moves include the case, the best objection and the reply.
rubric: Notes that the answer is timed, and sets the plan within the pacing of the exam (reading, writing, review).

@quiz self
prompt: In about 300 words, as in a 45-minute exam, answer: "Does the Gettier problem show that knowledge cannot be analyzed?"
hint: Take a position, say what "analyzed" means, and consider the best reply.
solution: Thesis: the Gettier problem does not show that knowledge cannot be analyzed, but the history of failed repairs gives real inductive support for the claim that no informative analysis in terms of belief and truth-making conditions exists. By an analysis I mean a set of individually necessary and jointly sufficient conditions stated without using the concept of knowledge. Gettier's cases show that justified true belief is not sufficient: a subject can have excellent but misleading evidence and a belief that is true by luck. This shows that the particular analysis fails, not that all fail. The natural response adds a fourth condition (for example, that the belief not be inferred from a false lemma, or that its justification not be defeated by true information). The best objection is that each such condition has met new counterexamples, so the project looks like a degenerating one; this supports the stronger thesis. A reply: this is an inductive argument, and it does not rule out a sophisticated analysis, such as a modal one (safety) or a causal one. A second reply: the knowledge-first program of Williamson accepts the failure and inverts the order of explanation, treating knowledge as basic. I think the best conclusion is modest: the problem refutes the traditional analysis and gives good, but not conclusive, reason to doubt that any reductive analysis will succeed. What would change my mind is a stable analysis that survives the standard counterexamples.
rubric: States a clear thesis and defines what "analysis" means.
rubric: Explains why the Gettier cases refute the traditional analysis but not every analysis.
rubric: Presents an objection and a reply, with accurate attributions.
