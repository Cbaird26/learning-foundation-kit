### Ontology

**Quine's criterion** says that a theory is committed to the entities that must be values of the bound variables of its best regimentation in first-order logic (in Quine's well-known slogan, to be is to be the value of a bound variable). The criterion tells you how to find a theory's commitments; it does not tell you which theory to believe. Nominalists respond by paraphrasing away apparent quantification over abstracta, or by denying that the theory needs them.

**Universals.** The **realist** about universals holds that the properties things share are entities that are wholly present in each instance (Armstrong's immanent realism) or exist apart from instances (Platonism). **Trope theorists** hold that properties are particular, individual instances (this redness, not redness as such). **Resemblance nominalists** hold that resemblance between particulars does the work, and **class nominalists** analyze properties as sets. The main pressures: realism faces the question of how a universal can be wholly in many places; resemblance nominalism faces the regress of resemblances and the problem of coextensive properties.

**Composition and persistence.** The **special composition question** (van Inwagen's phrasing) asks when some things compose another. The three families of answers are **nihilism** (never: only simples exist), **universalism** (always) and **restricted composition** (sometimes, as with van Inwagen's organicism: when the activity of the parts constitutes a life). Universalism is supported by the argument from vagueness: any restriction on composition would be vague, but existence cannot be vague (Lewis and Sider). On persistence, **endurantists** say that an object is wholly present at each time; **perdurantists** say that it has temporal parts; **stage theorists** say that the things we talk about are instantaneous stages. The argument from **temporary intrinsics** pressures endurantism: a thing that is bent and later straight must have both shapes, and relativizing the shape to times seems either to make shapes relations or to push the problem elsewhere.

### Modality

**Modal realism** (Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds*) says that possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally isolated universes; "actual" is indexical, and possibility is truth at some world. Its virtue is reductive analysis of modal talk in non-modal terms. Its cost is the "incredulous stare" and Kripke's **Humphrey objection**: a counterpart in another world is not the person whose possibilities are in question, so why should his fate matter to what is possible for Humphrey? **Ersatzists** take worlds to be abstract representations (sets of sentences, states of affairs, properties) and face the question of what the representation relation is without modal notions. **Essentialism about modality** (Fine, "Essence and Modality", 1994) reverses the order: Fine's example is that, necessarily, Socrates exists if and only if his singleton does, yet it is no part of Socrates's essence to belong to a set, so necessity cannot be what essence is.

A standard argument you must know in full is the argument for the **necessity of identity**, associated with Kripke's work:

| P1 | For all x and y, if x = y, then every property of x is a property of y (Leibniz's Law). |
| P2 | For all x, x has the property of being necessarily identical to x. |
| P3 | For all x and y, y has the property of being necessarily identical to x if and only if, necessarily, x = y. |
| C | So for all x and y, if x = y, then necessarily x = y. |

It is valid. Attack P1 only by restricting Leibniz's Law in modal contexts (rarely persuasive) or deny P2 or P3 by denying that the relevant property abstraction is legitimate; the more common route is to accept it and explain apparent contingent identities (a statue and its clay, on Gibbard's well-known case) by counterpart theory or by distinguishing what the terms designate.

### Causation, laws

Hume's analysis is usually read as a **regularity** account: causes are followed by effects of a kind, with constant conjunction. **Counterfactual** accounts (Lewis, "Causation", 1973) say e depends causally on c if e would not have occurred had c not occurred, with causation as the ancestor of that dependence. **Preemption** is the standard problem: a backup cause would have produced the effect had the actual cause failed, so the effect does not counterfactually depend on the actual cause even though it caused it. **Interventionist** accounts (Woodward) tie causation to what would happen under idealized manipulations. **Power-based** views take dispositions as basic. On laws, **Humean** views (Lewis's best-system analysis) hold that laws summarize patterns in the mosaic of particular facts, while **non-Humean** views make laws govern (Armstrong's necessitation between universals) or flow from the powers of things (dispositional essentialism).

@example
title: Finding a theory's commitments with Quine's criterion
problem: A theory says: "Some electrons are in the box, and the number of those electrons is prime." Apply Quine's criterion and state two replies available to a nominalist.
step: Regiment: there is an x such that x is an electron and x is in the box, and there is a number n such that n is the number of those electrons and n is prime. The second quantifier ranges over numbers.
step: Quine's criterion then says that the theory is committed to electrons and to numbers, since both are values of bound variables.
step: Reply one: paraphrase. Express "the number of electrons is three" in purely logical terms ("there are x, y, z, distinct, each an electron in the box, and nothing else is"), so that no quantifier ranges over numbers.
step: Reply two: deny that the theory needs numbers, or take the number talk as a useful fiction that the science does not literally assert.
answer: The theory commits to electrons and numbers; the nominalist paraphrases or treats the number talk as non-literal.

@example
title: A preemption case against the counterfactual account
problem: Mira throws a rock that shatters a window. Noor, a moment behind, had thrown a rock that would have shattered it had Mira's missed. Show that the simple counterfactual analysis gives the wrong verdict.
step: Counterfactual dependence: if Mira had not thrown, would the window have shattered? Yes, because Noor's rock would have done it.
step: So the shattering does not counterfactually depend on Mira's throw, and the simple analysis says her throw did not cause it.
step: But intuitively Mira's throw did cause the shattering, since her rock actually hit the window and Noor's did not.
step: This is late preemption: Noor's rock was still in flight when the window broke, so the backup process was not cut off before the effect. In early preemption the backup is cut off earlier, and Lewis's chains of dependence handle it; late preemption is harder and has motivated further revisions of the account.
answer: The analysis denies that Mira caused the shattering, which is intuitively false, so it is incomplete.

@mistakes
- Treating Quine's criterion as a test of which things exist, rather than as a test of what a theory says exists.
- Saying that modal realists believe in "many actual universes": for Lewis, "actual" is indexical, so every world is actual from its own standpoint.
- Treating the necessity of identity as the claim that identity statements are knowable a priori: Kripke's claim is that true identities between names are necessary and may be known only a posteriori.
- Saying that Lewis's counterfactual theory fails because of correlation without causation: its main problem is preemption.

@practice self
prompt: Write a 150-word answer: does the necessity of identity make "Hesperus is Phosphorus" a priori? State the argument in your own words and give a clear verdict.
hint: Separate the modal status of the identity from the way it is known.
solution: The argument for the necessity of identity uses Leibniz's Law: since each thing is necessarily identical to itself, whatever is identical to it is also necessarily identical to it. Applied to names, if Hesperus is Phosphorus, then necessarily Hesperus is Phosphorus. But necessity and a priority are different: the identity is necessary, yet discovered by astronomy, not by thinking alone. On Kripke's view, both names are rigid and designate Venus in every world in which it exists, but the speaker knows each name through different ways of fixing its reference, so the identity can be informative. Therefore the necessity of identity does not make the identity a priori; it is a necessary a posteriori truth. The best reply is the descriptivist's: if the names have different descriptive senses, there is no need for a necessary a posteriori truth.
rubric: Distinguishes necessity (a modal status) from a priority (an epistemic one).
rubric: States the argument from Leibniz's Law correctly and applies it to the names.
rubric: Gives a clear verdict and acknowledges a real alternative, such as descriptivism.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 250 words, as in a timed exam, explain the argument from temporary intrinsics against endurantism and state the best reply open to the endurantist.
hint: Identify the premise that creates the trouble, then say which premise each reply denies.
solution: Take an object that is bent at one time and straight at another. Shape is an intrinsic property, one an object has in virtue of how it is, not its relations. If the object is wholly present at each time and has both shapes, then it is bent and straight, which are incompatible. The argument: P1, shapes are incompatible intrinsic properties. P2, an enduring object is wholly present at each time at which it exists. P3, the object is bent at one time and straight at another. C, so the object has incompatible properties, unless the properties are relativized to times. The endurantist replies by relativizing: shapes are relations to times (so being bent at t1 and straight at t2 is not incompatible), or by treating the having of properties as time-indexed, so that the object has a shape in a bent-at-t1 way (adverbialism). The perdurantist's objection is that relations to times make shape extrinsic, contrary to intuition. The adverbialist's reply is that bending is a way of having a shape, and it keeps shape intrinsic. The choice depends on whether the cost of non-intrinsic shapes or of a new mode of having properties is smaller than the cost of temporal parts.
rubric: States the argument with an incompatibility premise and the claim that enduring things are wholly present at each time.
rubric: Names a real reply and says which premise it denies, such as relativizing shape to time or adverbialism.
rubric: Notes a cost of that reply, and writes in connected exam prose.
