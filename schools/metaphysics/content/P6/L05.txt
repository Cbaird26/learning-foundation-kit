@article
Exams in the history of philosophy test a skill that is easy to lose in a metaphysics program: reading an argument in its own terms before judging it. An examiner will ask, "What does Aristotle say a substance is?" and then, "Is that a good answer to the problem of change?" The first asks for exegesis, the second for evaluation, and an answer that confuses them loses marks on both. Where scholars disagree about what a text means, say so and say which reading you adopt.

### A map by period

**Ancient.** Plato's theory of Forms (central in the *Republic*, with the divided line at the end of Book VI and the cave in Book VII) holds that the objects of knowledge are unchanging, intelligible Forms, of which sensible things are imperfect participants. Plato himself raises objections to the theory in the *Parmenides*, notably the **third man regress**: if a Form of Largeness makes large things large, and the Form is itself large, then a further Form is needed to explain what the Form and the large things share, and so on. **Aristotle** rejects separate Forms. In the *Categories* the primary substances are individual things such as this man; in the *Metaphysics* (the Zeta books especially) he asks what a substance is and gives an account in terms of form, matter and the compound, a view called **hylomorphism**. He distinguishes four kinds of explanation (material, formal, efficient, final).

**Medieval.** The problem of universals, handed on through Boethius's discussion of Porphyry, sets realism, conceptualism and nominalism against each other. **Aquinas** distinguishes essence from existence in created things, so that God alone is such that his essence is existence. **Scotus** posited a haecceity, an individuating feature of each particular; **Ockham** defended a nominalism that admits only particulars and rejects the need for common natures.

**Early modern.** **Descartes** argues in the *Meditations* (the cogito is in Meditation II) for a real distinction between mind and body, and counts God, minds and bodies as substances. **Spinoza** (*Ethics*) argues that there can be only one substance, God or Nature, of which minds and bodies are modes. **Leibniz** holds that the world consists of simple, mind-like substances called monads, and affirms the principle of sufficient reason (see the *Monadology*, around sections 31 and 32) and the identity of indiscernibles. **Locke** distinguishes primary from secondary qualities; **Berkeley** argues that to be is to be perceived or to perceive, rejecting material substance; **Hume** analyzes causation and the self in terms of impressions and associations, with the discussion of causal inference in *Treatise* 1.3.6.

**Kant.** The *Critique of Pure Reason* asks how synthetic a priori knowledge is possible. In the Transcendental Deduction of the B edition, Kant argues that the categories, such as causation, are conditions of the possibility of experience. On a common reading, called transcendental idealism, we know objects only as they appear to us under the forms of space and time, not as they are in themselves; scholars disagree about whether this means two worlds or two aspects of one world.

**Early analytic.** Frege and Russell give the new logic and the theory of descriptions; Carnap's "Empiricism, Semantics and Ontology" (1950) separates questions internal to a linguistic framework from external questions about the framework; Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951) attacks the analytic/synthetic distinction on which that separation relies.

### One argument in full

Hume's problem of induction, on a standard reconstruction (the *Treatise* and the *Enquiry* give it in different forms):

| P1 | If our belief that the future will resemble the past is justified, then it is justified by demonstrative reasoning or by probable reasoning. |
| P2 | It is not justified by demonstrative reasoning, since the contrary is conceivable without contradiction. |
| P3 | It is not justified by probable reasoning, since such reasoning already presupposes that the future will resemble the past and would be circular. |
| C | So our belief that the future will resemble the past is not justified. |

The argument is valid. Replies target the premises: reliabilists and pragmatists question the demand for non-circular justification (P3); Kant's reply, on the standard reading, is that causal order is a condition of experience, so the uniformity of nature need not be justified from experience but is necessary for there to be experience at all, which takes the question out of P1's framework.

### In the exam

Put the claim first, then support it from the text, then evaluate. A good answer to "Is Spinoza's monism a good response to Descartes's problem of interaction?" begins with a thesis and uses the *Ethics* for exegesis. Cite locations only when you are sure of them, and say "on one reading" when scholars differ.

@example
title: How many substances?
problem: Compare Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz on the number and kinds of substances, and say what each is responding to.
step: Descartes: God, plus two kinds of created substance, minds and bodies, defined by thought and extension. His problem is how two such different substances interact.
step: Spinoza: one substance, God or Nature, with thought and extension as attributes of it; minds and bodies are modes. This removes interaction by making the two parallel aspects of one thing.
step: Leibniz: infinitely many simple substances, monads, with no real interaction between them, and a pre-established harmony between their states.
step: Note the pattern: each position answers the interaction problem differently, and each pays a cost (Descartes with interaction, Spinoza with the apparent loss of individual substances, Leibniz with the harmony).
answer: Descartes holds three kinds of substance (God, minds and bodies), Spinoza one substance and Leibniz infinitely many.

@example
title: Separating exegesis from evaluation
problem: Write one exegetical and one evaluative sentence about Plato's third man regress.
step: Exegesis: in the *Parmenides*, Plato has Parmenides press the objection that, if a Form of Largeness is large like the things it makes large, then a second Form is needed to explain what they have in common, and so on without end.
step: Evaluation: the regress arises only if the Form is itself an instance of the property it explains (self-predication) and the Form is just one more thing that shares the property with the others; deny either assumption and the regress stops.
answer: One sentence says what Plato's text argues; the other judges whether it works, naming the premises that a defender denies.

@mistakes
- Attributing to a historical figure a view for which you cannot give the textual basis; say "on one reading" when scholars differ.
- Treating Aristotle as a Platonist about universals: he rejects separate Forms.
- Treating Kant's transcendental idealism as simple subjectivism: Kant claims that experience has a necessary structure, and scholars dispute what the distinction between appearances and things in themselves amounts to.
- Evaluating before stating the view accurately: an examiner cannot give credit for a good objection to a view the text does not hold.

@practice self
prompt: In about 120 words, state exegetically what Descartes argues in Meditation II about his existence, and then in two sentences evaluate whether the argument establishes anything about what kind of thing he is.
hint: Separate "I exist" from "I am a thinking thing".
solution: In Meditation II, Descartes supposes that an evil deceiver is deceiving him about everything and notes that even then he must exist in order to be deceived: the proposition "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever he states or conceives it. He then asks what the "I" is and concludes that he is a thing that thinks. Evaluation: the first step is widely accepted as secure against doubt, but the second is contested, since the argument shows only that thinking occurs, and it is a further step to say that a substance that thinks exists and that it is distinct from the body; critics have pressed that the inference from "there is thinking" to "there is a thinker" needs a premise.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a 200-word exam answer: explain Hume's problem of induction in standard form, and say which premise a reply of your choice targets and whether that reply succeeds.
hint: Give the premises, then pick one reply and say which premise it denies.
solution: Hume's argument runs: P1, if our belief that the future will resemble the past is justified, it is justified by demonstrative or probable reasoning; P2, it is not justified demonstratively, since its denial is conceivable; P3, it is not justified by probable reasoning, since that would presuppose it; C, so it is not justified. The argument is valid. I consider the reply that inductive reasoning is justified by its own track record, which targets P3 by denying that circularity makes the justification worthless. This succeeds in showing that a rule-circular justification of induction is not obviously incoherent, since we accept such circularity for other basic rules of inference. But it does not meet Hume's challenge in full, because a counterinductive rule could be justified by the same method by its own track record, so rule-circularity cannot distinguish good rules from bad. I conclude that the reply weakens P3 without removing the problem, and that a stronger answer must say what makes some rules reasonable, as Kant, reliabilists and pragmatists in different ways attempt.
rubric: States the argument correctly in standard form and says it is valid.
rubric: Identifies the premise the chosen reply targets.
rubric: Evaluates the reply with a real objection (for example, that a counterinductive rule could claim the same support).
