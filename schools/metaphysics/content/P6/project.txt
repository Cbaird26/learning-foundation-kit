title: Dissertation prospectus, mock qualifying exam and mock oral defense
overview: You will write a 6,000- to 8,000-word prospectus for a dissertation in metaphysics, pass a mock qualifying exam of three timed essays, and defend the prospectus in a recorded mock oral examination. The three parts rehearse the milestones of a PhD: showing command of the field, proposing original work, and defending the proposal. Programs differ in form, length and rules, so adapt each part to your own program's handbook.
goal: Write a prospectus with a research question, literature review, thesis, chapter outline, timeline and bibliography that a committee could judge to be original, sound and feasible.
goal: Show command of the core areas by writing three timed essays under exam conditions and scoring them against a rubric.
goal: Explain and defend your project aloud, answering objections about scope, originality and feasibility.
step: Choose a question. Use your reading list and the tests of scope, originality, tractability and fit, and write three candidates in one sentence each. Pick one, and say why.
step: Build the map. Use the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, PhilPapers and the readings from this course to find the main positions and the nearest work to your question, and cite everything you use.
step: Draft the argument. State your thesis and your gap claim in standard form, with premises, and check that each argument is valid as written.
step: Write the prospectus using the template in the materials, in about 6,000 to 8,000 words, with the chapter outline and timeline last. Revise it once against the rubric.
step: Take the mock qualifying exam. Choose three questions from the bank in the materials, one from each of three different areas, and write three timed essays in a three-hour sitting, with the pacing of Lesson 6. Do not look at notes unless your program allows them. Score each essay with the exam rubric below.
step: Hold the mock oral. Ask two or more faculty members or advanced students to read your prospectus and examine you for about an hour, using the question bank in the materials as a starting point; record it with their permission.
step: Review the recording. List the questions you answered well, the ones you did not, and the three changes to make to the prospectus. Write a one-page revision plan with dates.

material: **Exam rubric for scoring each timed essay.** Give 0 to 3 points for each: (a) a thesis in the first paragraph; (b) accurate exposition with correct attributions; (c) an argument in valid form with the key premise identified; (d) the best objection and a reply; (e) a finished answer with clear prose. material: **Mock oral question bank.** (1) State your thesis in two sentences. (2) Which premise of your argument is weakest, and why? (3) How does your project differ from the nearest existing work? (7) What if your thesis is false? (8) What can you finish in your funded time?

### The course in one page

**Weeks 1 and 2: how the PhD works and the reading list.** The usual stages are coursework, a qualifying or comprehensive requirement, a prospectus, the dissertation and the defense. Programs differ in form, timing and consequences, so rely on your own handbook and advisors. A reading list of about forty works should cover every examined area, include both sides of each debate and be annotated by thesis, argument, best objection and place in the debate.

**Weeks 3 to 5: core review.** Ontology: Quine's criterion, universals, composition (nihilism, universalism, restriction) and persistence. Modality: modal realism, ersatzism, essentialism, and the argument for the necessity of identity. Causation and laws: regularity, counterfactual, interventionist and power views, preemption, Humean and non-Humean laws. Mind, language and epistemology: the knowledge argument, the Chinese room, sense and reference, descriptions, rigid designation, Gettier and its responses. History: Plato and Aristotle, the medieval debate on universals, Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz, the empiricists, Kant, Hume's problem of induction. Keep exegesis (what the text says) separate from evaluation.

**Weeks 6 and 7: exams.** A written answer states a thesis, gives the best argument, the best objection and a reply, and is finished. Allocate time before you start. An oral answer uses claim, reason, example and limit; restate objections, concede what is right, and do not bluff.

**Week 8: the field.** The 2020 PhilPapers Survey reports the share of respondents who accept or lean toward each view; the shares need not sum to 100, and they are not evidence that a view is true.

**Weeks 9 to 12: the project and the profession.** A dissertation needs a question, not a topic, tested for scope, originality, tractability and fit. A prospectus gives the question, a review ending in a gap, the thesis, the chapters, a timeline and contingencies. In the defense, answer scope, originality, method and feasibility challenges, and "what if your thesis is false?" with honesty. The academic job market is competitive, with far more new PhDs than permanent positions in most years, and many PhDs build careers elsewhere: check the APA, PhilJobs and your department for current information.

### Common errors

Treating common practice as a rule; surveying instead of arguing; evaluating before stating a view accurately; reading survey percentages as evidence of truth; presenting a topic as a question; promising to solve a large problem; defending a chapter without saying what it does for the thesis.

prompt: Kripke's Humphrey objection is aimed at which view?
choice*: Lewis's modal realism with counterpart theory, which analyzes what is possible for a person by what happens to counterparts in other worlds.
choice: Fine's essentialism about modality.
choice: Quine's criterion of ontological commitment.
choice: Descartes's real distinction between mind and body.
hint: The objection asks why facts about someone else in another world should matter to what is possible for a given person.
solution: The objection is that if a person's possibilities are analyzed by what happens to counterparts, then what happens to a distinct individual seems irrelevant to what could have happened to him. It targets counterpart-theoretic modal realism, not the other views listed.

@exam self
prompt: Write a timed essay (about 45 minutes, roughly 400 words) answering: "Kripke argued that some truths are necessary but knowable only a posteriori. Does this undermine the zombie argument against physicalism, which moves from the conceivability of zombies to their metaphysical possibility?" Begin with a thesis, and include the best objection to it.
hint: Start from the gap between conceivability and metaphysical possibility illustrated by "water is H2O".
solution: Thesis: Kripke's necessary a posteriori shows that conceivability does not by itself entail metaphysical possibility, so the zombie argument needs more than a bare conceivability premise; but it does not refute the argument, because defenders can supply a form of conceivability that is meant to track possibility. The zombie argument, as given by Chalmers, runs: P1, zombies, physical duplicates of us without consciousness, are conceivable. P2, if zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible. P3, if zombies are metaphysically possible, physicalism is false. C, so physicalism is false. Kripke's lesson bears on P2: it is conceivable, in one sense, that water is not H2O, yet it is metaphysically impossible. So a physicalist can say that the conceivability of zombies is like that: an epistemic possibility that is not a metaphysical one, because the link between physical and phenomenal facts is a necessary a posteriori identity that we cannot see a priori. This is the line of the phenomenal concept strategy. Chalmers replies that the water case involves a gap between how a thing is picked out and what it is, which an appeal to a more careful kind of conceivability, in which we consider the scenario as actual, closes; and that in the phenomenal case there is no such gap, since the appearance of experience is the experience. The best objection to my thesis is that this reply restores P2, so Kripke's point is irrelevant. I think it does not: it shows that P2 needs argument via the two-dimensional framework, and the physicalist can contest that framework or deny that the scenario is positively conceivable. So the argument is not refuted, but it carries the burden of its second premise, and its force depends on a contested view about how conceivability relates to possibility.
rubric: States a thesis and the zombie argument in valid standard form.
rubric: Explains the Kripkean point that conceivability and metaphysical possibility can come apart (the water and H2O case), and applies it to the premise linking conceivability to possibility.
rubric: Presents the best objection (the two-dimensional or scenario-based reply) and a response, with accurate attributions.
rubric: Is organized as a timed essay with a thesis up front and a conclusion.
