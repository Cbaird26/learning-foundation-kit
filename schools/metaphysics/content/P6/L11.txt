@article
A prospectus meeting is usually shorter and friendlier than students fear, and more useful than they expect. Its purpose is to improve the project and to give you the committee's approval to proceed. Formats vary: some programs hold a formal "defense" in which you present and are questioned, others a conversation about the document, and some have no meeting at all. Outcomes also vary: approval, approval with required revisions, a request to revise and resubmit. Find out from your own program what it expects, what the possible outcomes are and what follows from each.

### Preparing the talk

If you are asked to present, a common structure for about ten minutes is:

1. **The question and why it matters** (one to two minutes).
2. **The gap**: what the literature leaves open (two minutes).
3. **The thesis** and its main argument in standard form (two minutes).
4. **The chapters**: one sentence each on the question and the argument (two minutes).
5. **Timeline and risks**: what you will do and what you will do if an argument fails (one to two minutes).

Do not read the prospectus aloud. Practice with a clock, and give the committee the structure at the start ("I will give the question, the gap, the thesis, the chapters and the plan"). Write the main argument on one slide or on paper so that questions can refer to it.

### What the committee will ask

| Challenge | What it tests | Your move |
|---|---|---|
| Scope: "Isn't chapter three a different dissertation?" | Unity of the project | Show how it serves the thesis, or restructure |
| Originality: "How is this different from X?" | Knowledge of the literature | Name the nearest work and state the difference exactly |
| Method: "How will you argue for premise two?" | Feasibility of the argument | State the strategy and what would count as success |
| Feasibility: "Can this be finished in your time?" | Planning | Point to the timeline and the contingency |
| Falsity: "What if your thesis is false?" | Intellectual honesty | Say what you would then argue, and why the project is still of value |
| Position: "Why not accept the Humean view?" | Understanding of rivals | Give the best case for the rival, and say where your argument differs |

### Handling the scope challenge as an argument

An examiner's challenge to a chapter can be put as an argument:

| P1 | If a chapter does not serve the dissertation's central thesis, then the dissertation contains two projects. |
| P2 | Chapter 3, as written, does not serve the central thesis. |
| C | So the dissertation, as written, contains two projects. |

The argument is valid, and you can reply in two ways. You can deny P2 by showing the link (the chapter supplies a premise the thesis needs, or answers an objection that the thesis must meet). Or you can accept the conclusion and propose to cut the chapter, or to turn it into a section or a separate paper. Either reply is good; the bad reply is to defend the chapter without saying what it does for the thesis.

### What if the thesis is false?

This question is a gift. A good answer has three parts: say which premise you think is most at risk; say what you would argue if it failed (a weaker thesis, a conditional thesis, or a negative result such as "the best accounts fail for this reason"); and say why even those results would be valuable. Doing so shows the committee that the project is a real inquiry and not an attempt to defend a conclusion at any cost.

### After the meeting

Write down every comment during the meeting, or ask someone to do so. Within a few days, turn them into a list: **required changes**, **recommended changes** and **comments you disagree with**, and for each a decision and a date. Send a short message to the chair summarizing your understanding and your plan and ask whether it matches theirs. Disagreeing with a comment is allowed; ignoring it is not. Check your program's rules on any formal paperwork, since some require signatures or a filed record.

@example
title: Answering "What if your thesis is false?"
problem: The thesis is that Humean laws explain by unification. An examiner asks what happens if this is false. Answer in about a minute.
step: Name the risk: "The premise most at risk is that unification explanations count as genuine explanations of regularities."
step: Say what follows if it fails: "Then I would argue for a weaker result: that the Humean view is stable only if it accepts a deflationary view of explanation, which is a cost for the view."
step: State the value: "That conclusion would still sharpen the debate by identifying exactly what the Humean must give up."
answer: Name the premise at risk, say what you would argue if it fails, and say why the result would still be valuable.

@example
title: Turning feedback into a plan
problem: A committee says: (a) chapter 3 seems to belong to a separate project; (b) you have not engaged with recent work on grounding; (c) the timeline for chapter 4 is too short. Make a revision plan.
step: Classify, and ask the chair which items the committee treats as required: (a) is probably required since it bears on unity; (b) is probably required because it is a gap in the literature review; (c) is probably a recommended change that the chair can help judge.
step: Decide: for (a) either show the link or cut or reframe the chapter, after talking with the chair; for (b) read the recent work and add a section; for (c) lengthen the timeline by two months and re-check the funding limit.
step: Date: set dates for each and send the chair the plan.
answer: Classify each comment, decide a response, set a date and confirm with the chair.

@mistakes
- Reading the prospectus aloud instead of presenting its structure and argument.
- Defending a chapter without saying what it does for the thesis.
- Treating "what if your thesis is false?" as a threat rather than a chance to show the value of the inquiry.
- Ignoring comments you disagree with instead of answering them.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: an examiner says, "Chapter 3 looks like a different dissertation." Write a reply of about 150 words for an invented project in which chapter 3 discusses the metaphysics of properties and the thesis concerns the explanatory role of laws.
hint: Say what chapter 3 contributes to the thesis, and offer a fallback.
solution: That is a fair worry, and I should have made the link clearer in the document. Chapter 3 is there because the thesis depends on a premise about what laws relate: if laws are relations between universals, as the non-Humean says, the thesis that Humean laws can explain by unification has to meet an objection from the metaphysics of properties, namely that unification does not explain why properties are arranged as they are. Chapter 3 gives my account of properties that makes the Humean answer coherent, and chapter 4 relies on it when it answers the circularity objection. If the committee finds the link still too thin, I would be willing to reduce the chapter to a section of chapter 4 and move the rest to a separate paper after the dissertation. I would want to discuss this with my chair before deciding.
rubric: Concedes what is right in the challenge and states what the chapter contributes to the thesis.
rubric: Names the premise or chapter that depends on it.
rubric: Offers a specific fallback and a way of deciding.
