@article
The academic job market in philosophy is highly competitive: in most years there are far more new PhDs than permanent academic positions, and many people with philosophy PhDs build careers outside academia. This is true even for strong candidates, and it is a feature of the field's structure, not a verdict on any individual's work. Requirements, calendars and conditions vary by institution and change over time, so this lesson describes common practice and sends you to the American Philosophical Association, PhilJobs and your own department for current facts. It gives no figures because figures go out of date.

### What an academic search usually involves

- **Listings.** PhilJobs is the field's main job listing site, and the APA's pages explain the process and its norms.
- **Materials.** Common application materials are a cover letter, a CV, a writing sample, a research statement, a teaching statement and letters of recommendation; some institutions ask for additional documents, such as teaching evidence or statements on other topics. Each advertisement says what it wants, and you should follow it exactly.
- **Timing.** Many applications are due in the autumn for positions starting the next year, so a dissertation far along by the summer makes applications stronger. Because of that timeline, many students plan to have a writing sample and several chapters ready then.
- **Stages.** A typical process has an initial screening of files, a first-round interview (the format varies, and video interviews are common), and a campus visit with a talk and a teaching demonstration for the shortlist. Teaching-focused institutions may emphasize teaching in each stage.
- **Kinds of position.** Tenure-track and permanent posts, postdoctoral fellowships, visiting and temporary positions, and teaching-focused posts. Each has different expectations for research and teaching.

### What committees look for

Hiring committees usually look for evidence of **research potential** (a coherent project, a strong writing sample, a record of publications or work in progress), **teaching ability** (courses taught, evaluations, a teaching philosophy tied to practice), and **fit** with the department's needs. Letters from your committee are central, which is one reason to keep the committee informed all the way through.

### Planning with a parallel track

The reasoning for a dual plan is simple:

| P1 | If academic positions are far fewer than new PhDs in most years, then not everyone who wants an academic position will get one. |
| P2 | Academic positions are far fewer than new PhDs in most years. |
| C1 | So not everyone who wants an academic position will get one. |
| P3 | If not everyone who wants an academic position will get one, then a prudent person who wants one also prepares options that do not depend on getting one. |
| C | So a prudent person who wants an academic position also prepares options that do not depend on getting one. |

The argument is valid, and P2 is the field's standard description. The best objection is that dividing attention lowers the chance of success on the academic track. The reply is that the skills the two tracks need overlap to a large extent, so the cost is smaller than it seems, and that the alternative is to bear the whole risk on a single outcome.

### Other paths

A philosophy PhD builds skills of analysis, writing, argument and teaching that are useful in many settings. Graduates work in universities in other roles (administration, advising, research support), in teaching at schools and colleges, in law and policy, in government and non-profit organizations, in technology and data work, in publishing and journalism, and in consulting. None of these is a "second prize". Prepare by describing your skills in the vocabulary of the field you are applying to, by finding people who work in it, and by practicing a short summary of your work for non-specialists.

### Your project, three ways

Prepare three versions of your project summary: a **two-minute** version for a non-specialist (no jargon), a **five-minute** version for a department (question, gap, thesis, chapters), and a **one-paragraph** research statement. Each should state the question, why it matters, what you argue and what you have done. Practicing these is also practice for the defense of the prospectus.

### Care of yourself

A long, uncertain process is hard. Keep a record of what you have achieved, talk with advisors and with peers, and decide in advance what counts as a good outcome for you. The stage at which the market is hardest is also the stage at which you most need a plan that does not depend on it.

@example
title: Building an application calendar
problem: A student plans to apply for positions advertised in the autumn of her fifth year, and funding ends at the close of that year. Sketch a backward plan.
step: Fix the dates: applications are usually due in the autumn, so the writing sample, CV, statements and letter requests must be ready by late summer.
step: Work backward: finish a polished writing sample in the spring, have two chapters drafted by the end of the fourth year, and ask letter-writers in the summer.
step: Add a second track: identify two non-academic fields, speak to people in them in the spring, and prepare a version of the CV for each.
step: Check against the program's rules and the placement officer's advice, since calendars and norms vary.
answer: A calendar working back from the autumn deadline with a parallel non-academic track, checked with the placement officer.

@example
title: A two-minute summary for a non-specialist
problem: Write two or three sentences summarizing the invented project "Do laws of nature explain why there are regularities?" for a non-specialist.
step: State the question in plain words: "Scientists say that the planets move as they do because of laws of nature. Some philosophers think laws are merely summaries of what happens."
step: State the problem: "But if laws only summarize what happens, how can they explain it?"
step: State the claim: "I argue that summaries can explain when they unify many facts under a simple pattern, which is what scientists seem to count as explanation."
answer: A plain-language question, problem and claim, with no jargon.

@mistakes
- Treating one source's description of the market, or last year's, as current: check the APA, PhilJobs and your department.
- Sending the same materials to every advertisement without following what each asks for.
- Treating non-academic careers as a fallback to be thought of after the market: plan them in parallel.
- Describing your project in jargon, so that non-specialist readers cannot see what is at stake.
