@article
"My dissertation is about time" is a topic; "Does the growing-block theory have a principled answer to the question of how we know that it is now?" is a question. Topics are what you are interested in; questions are what a dissertation can answer. Choosing well is among the most important decisions of a doctorate, and it is made with imperfect information, which is why this lesson gives tests and a process instead of a formula.

### Why a dissertation needs a question

| P1 | A dissertation must make a contribution that a committee can judge to be original and sound. |
| P2 | A contribution can be judged only if the question it answers has identifiable rival answers that the work assesses. |
| P3 | A broad topic does not by itself have identifiable rival answers that can be assessed within the span of a dissertation. |
| C | So a dissertation needs a focused question, not only a broad topic. |

The argument is valid. A fair objection to P3 is that many dissertations have a broad unifying theme. The reply is that a theme can organize chapters, but each chapter still needs a question, and the dissertation needs a central question that the chapters jointly answer.

### Four tests for a candidate question

1. **Scope.** Can it be answered in the space of a dissertation, in a few connected chapters? If answering it requires settling the whole of an area, narrow it.
2. **Originality.** Is there a gap that matters, not only a gap in what you have read? A question is not original because you have not seen it discussed; search the encyclopedias, PhilPapers and recent journals, and ask advisors.
3. **Tractability.** Do you know what a successful argument would look like, and what work (formal, historical, empirical) it would need? A question with no imaginable resolution is a poor basis for a dissertation.
4. **Fit.** Do you have, or can you acquire, the background it needs, and is there a faculty member who can supervise it? Is it a question that will sustain your interest for years?

A fifth practical test is **publishability**: could each chapter, with revision, be a paper of the kind journals accept? Many programs allow, or even expect, the dissertation to be a set of connected papers, and many hiring committees will look at a writing sample drawn from it; some programs prefer a book-style argument. Ask your program.

### Where candidate questions come from

Use your reading list and exam preparation. Look for **tensions** (two views you accept that seem to conflict), **gaps** (a debate that has not been connected to a nearby one), **unexamined assumptions** (a premise everyone shares in a debate), **new tools** (a result in logic, physics or cognitive science that bears on a metaphysical question) and **neglected objections**. Write each candidate as one sentence of the form "Does X, given Y?" or "What explains X?" and a paragraph on why it matters.

### Sharpening a topic

The move from topic to question is a series of narrowing steps. Start with an area (grounding), narrow to a debate (the formal features of grounding), then to a puzzle (whether grounding is asymmetric) and finally to a question that has rival answers (whether the asymmetry of grounding is explained by its being a form of explanation or must be taken as primitive).

### The committee

A dissertation committee usually has a **chair** (often called the advisor) and several members; many programs require at least one member from outside the department or from another subfield. Rules about size and composition vary, so read your handbook. Choose people for:

- **Expertise** in your question (not only its general area).
- **Availability and reliability**: how quickly they read, and how long they will be at the institution.
- **Working style**: how they give feedback, how often they meet, how they respond to drafts.
- **Complementarity**: a committee with different strengths covers more of the project's weak points.
- **Fairness and engagement**: someone willing to read the whole dissertation and to give honest criticism.

Approach potential members with a short written summary (one page) and a specific request, and give them time. Your chair should be someone with whom you can talk frankly about problems. Keep the committee informed of your progress, and see them separately, not only at milestones.

@example
title: Sharpening a topic into a question
problem: A student says "I want to work on personal identity." Sharpen this into a researchable question and test it.
step: Topic to debate: the dispute between psychological and biological views of what we are.
step: Debate to puzzle: the case of fission, in which one person's psychology is divided into two, and what it implies for the view that identity consists in psychological continuity.
step: Puzzle to question: "Given fission, should a psychological view of our identity be revised to say that what matters is psychological continuity and not identity, as Parfit argued, or can it keep identity as what matters, for example by holding, as Lewis did, that two persons shared their stages before the fission?"
step: Test: scope is a few chapters; originality needs a literature search on the second option; tractability needs a statement of what would count as success; fit depends on faculty and on the student's formal background.
answer: The topic is narrowed through debate and puzzle to a question with rival answers, then tested on scope, originality, tractability and fit.

@example
title: Choosing between two possible chairs
problem: A student must choose a chair. Professor A is a leading expert in the student's question but is often away and reads drafts after months. Professor B knows the area but not the specific question, meets every two weeks and returns comments within a fortnight. What should the student consider?
step: Weigh expertise against availability: neither factor alone settles the choice.
step: Look for a combination: Professor B as chair for regular supervision, with Professor A as a member for expertise on the central question.
step: Ask about the practicalities: A's plans to be present, each professor's willingness, and the program's rules for committees.
answer: Consider B as chair and A as a member, subject to the rules and to their availability.

@mistakes
- Stating a topic ("the metaphysics of time") as if it were a question: a topic has no rival answers to assess.
- Treating a question as original because you have not seen it discussed, without a search.
- Choosing a committee only for fame or for friendliness, and ignoring expertise and reliability.
- Waiting until the prospectus is written to speak to potential committee members.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: write three candidate dissertation questions in metaphysics (they may be invented), evaluate each in one or two sentences on scope, originality, tractability and fit, and say which you would pursue and why.
hint: Write each as "Does X, given Y?" or "What explains X?" and use the four tests.
solution: (Illustrations.) Q1: Do the possibility of finks and masks undermine the dispositional essentialist's claim that the laws of nature are metaphysically necessary? Scope: good, a few chapters. Originality: requires a literature check on dispositional essentialism, where the question may already be well discussed, so I would need a new angle. Tractability: success would be an argument that conditions on manifestation accommodate finks and masks, or that they cannot. Fit: needs faculty in metaphysics and in philosophy of science. Q2: Does the necessity of origin hold for artifacts, given the possibility of gradual replacement? Scope: narrow, perhaps one or two chapters. Originality: probably well discussed, so I would need a new angle. Tractability: clear. Fit: good. Q3: What is the metaphysics of time? This is a topic: too broad. I would pursue Q1, because it has rival answers, a clear success condition and a literature I can survey, and it connects to my exam reading in modality and causation. I would revisit Q2 as a possible chapter.

rubric: Each evaluation applies the four tests in a way specific to the question.
rubric: The choice is justified with reasons and identifies a risk.
