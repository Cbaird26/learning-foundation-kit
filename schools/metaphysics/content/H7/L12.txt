@article
**Ecology** is the study of interactions among organisms and their environment. It is organized in levels: **organism**, **population** (one species in an area), **community** (all populations), **ecosystem** (community plus the nonliving environment) and **biosphere**.

### Populations

**Density** is the number of individuals per unit area. When counting every individual is impossible, ecologists use **mark and recapture**: mark $M$ animals and release them; later capture $C$ animals, of which $R$ are marked. Then
$$N \approx \frac{M \times C}{R}.$$

Growth models:
- **Exponential growth** (unlimited resources): $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = rN$, giving a J-shaped curve, $N = N_0 e^{rt}$.
- **Logistic growth** (limited resources): $\dfrac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(1 - \dfrac{N}{K}\right)$, an S-shaped curve that levels off at the **carrying capacity** $K$.

**Density-dependent** limits (competition, disease, predation) intensify as populations crowd; **density-independent** factors (storms, fires) act regardless of density. **r-selected** species have many offspring and little parental care; **K-selected** species have few offspring with more care.

### Communities

Species interactions include **competition** (the **competitive exclusion principle**), **predation**, **herbivory**, **mutualism** (both benefit), **commensalism** (one benefits, the other is unaffected) and **parasitism** (one benefits, the other harmed). A **keystone species** has an effect on its community out of proportion to its abundance. **Succession** is the change in community composition over time.

### Energy flow and matter cycles

Energy enters most ecosystems as sunlight, is captured by **producers** (autotrophs) and passes to **consumers** through food chains and webs: primary consumers eat producers, secondary consumers eat primary consumers, and so on. **Decomposers** recycle nutrients. Only about **10%** of energy is transferred from one **trophic level** to the next; the rest is lost as heat in respiration or remains unused or undigested. This is why food chains are short and why there are fewer top predators.

Matter is recycled: the **carbon cycle** moves carbon among the atmosphere, producers (photosynthesis), consumers (respiration), decomposers and fossil fuels; burning fossil fuels adds $CO_2$, increasing the greenhouse effect. The **nitrogen cycle** involves nitrogen fixation by bacteria, nitrification and denitrification. Energy flows through ecosystems; matter cycles within them.

### Antibiotic resistance as natural selection

Ecology and evolution meet in a bacterial population. Bacteria divide quickly, so a large population contains random variation, including rare mutations (or plasmid genes) that make a cell resistant. An antibiotic is a strong selective pressure: it kills susceptible cells, while resistant cells survive, reproduce and (by **horizontal gene transfer**, such as conjugation) can share resistance genes. The resistance allele frequency rises in the next generations. Incomplete courses of antibiotics and overuse increase this selection pressure.

@example
title: Estimating population size
problem: Researchers mark 60 fish in a lake. A later sample of 50 fish contains 10 marked fish. Estimate the population.
step: Use $N = M \times C / R$ with $M = 60$, $C = 50$, $R = 10$.
step: Compute $N = 60 \times 50 / 10 = 300$.
step: The lake holds about 300 fish, assuming marks do not fall off and marked fish mix fully.

@mistakes
- Saying energy is recycled; matter is recycled but energy is lost as heat at each step.
- Treating "10% of energy transferred" as 10% of organisms.
- Confusing a population with a community.
- Describing antibiotic resistance as bacteria "adapting" within an individual cell's lifetime rather than selection changing the proportion of resistant cells.

@practice self
prompt: Explain antibiotic resistance as natural selection. Use the four requirements of selection (variation, heritability, differential survival and reproduction, change in allele frequency) and describe one human practice that increases resistance.
solution: A bacterial population has variation; a few cells carry a mutation or plasmid gene for resistance, and this is heritable (passed by division and sometimes by horizontal gene transfer). When an antibiotic is applied, susceptible cells die and resistant cells survive and reproduce much more, so the proportion of resistant cells and the resistance allele frequency rise in the next generations. The resistance mutations arose randomly before exposure; the antibiotic only selected for them. Stopping a course of antibiotics early or using them when not needed leaves the strongest survivors and applies selection pressure, increasing resistance.
hint: Ask when the resistance variants first appeared and what the antibiotic does to each type of cell.
rubric: Identifies variation, inheritance, differential survival and rising allele frequency.
rubric: States that resistance mutations arise before and independent of antibiotic exposure.
rubric: Names one practice such as overuse, incomplete courses or unneeded prescriptions.
