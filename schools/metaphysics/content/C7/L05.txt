@article
Why should you obey the law, share the costs of public goods or refrain from harming strangers? One answer is that these are rules everyone could agree to, since a world in which they are followed is better for each person than a world without them. That thought, that morality or political authority rests on agreement among equals, is the core of **contractualism**.

### The social contract tradition

Thomas Hobbes argued in *Leviathan* (1651) that in a **state of nature**, with no common authority, life would be a war of all against all, and he describes it as "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" (chapter 13). Rational people would therefore agree to submit to a sovereign. John Locke started from natural rights and argued that government is founded on consent and exists to protect those rights, while Rousseau, in *The Social Contract* (1762), asked how people can be both subject to law and free, and answered with the idea of the general will. These authors differ greatly, but all use agreement to explain the legitimacy of political authority.

### Rawls's original position

John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), revived the contract idea as a device for choosing the principles of justice for society's basic structure. In the **original position** you choose principles behind a **veil of ignorance**: you do not know your class, talents, sex, race or conception of the good, though you know general facts about society and psychology. Because no one can tailor principles to their advantage, the choice is fair. Rawls argues that the parties would choose two principles: **equal basic liberties** for all, and a principle on social and economic inequalities requiring **fair equality of opportunity** and that inequalities work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged (the **difference principle**). The first principle has priority over the second.

The reasoning for the difference principle can be set out like this.

| P1 | Principles of justice are those that would be chosen in the original position, where the parties are rational, self-interested and ignorant of their own position. |
| P2 | In the original position, the rational choice is to adopt the arrangement whose worst-off position is best (the maximin rule). |
| P3 | The difference principle selects the arrangement whose worst-off position is best. |
| C | So the difference principle is a principle of justice. |

The argument is valid. Critics, beginning with John Harsanyi, deny P2: behind the veil, a rational chooser who assigns equal probability to each position should maximize *expected* (average) welfare, not the minimum. Rawls replied that the conditions of the original position (no basis for probabilities, a grave downside, and a guaranteed satisfactory minimum) make maximin sensible. A second objection, from Ronald Dworkin, is that a hypothetical contract cannot bind anyone: an agreement you never made gives you no reason to honor it. Defenders reply that the device models fairness; it does not claim that anyone consented.

### Scanlon's contractualism

T. M. Scanlon's *What We Owe to Each Other* (1998) offers contractualism as a theory of the part of morality concerned with what we owe to each other, not of politics. Its central claim is that an act is wrong if its performance under the circumstances would be disallowed by any set of principles for the general regulation of behavior that no one could **reasonably reject**. The test is to compare principles, asking, for each person, what burdens the principle would impose on them, and whether anyone has a strong complaint against it compared with the complaints against alternative principles. It is **individualist**: complaints are those of individual persons, so it does not simply add up benefits and burdens as a utilitarian does. A principle permitting you to let one person die in order to save a larger number from minor headaches would be reasonably rejected by that one person, since the burden on her is far greater than any single headache. Scanlon argues that this explains why aggregation of small benefits cannot always outweigh a large burden to one person.

Critics ask whether "reasonable" smuggles in the moral content the theory is meant to explain, and how the theory deals with beings who cannot take part in agreements, such as animals and some humans. Scanlon replies that contractualism concerns one part of morality and that other considerations also bear on how we may treat those beings. Another challenge is the problem of numbers: when many people face small burdens and one faces a larger one, the theory must say when complaints combine.

### Comparing contract views

Rawls uses the contract to select principles of justice for institutions; Scanlon uses it to explain moral wrongness between persons. The contract is also hypothetical in both: it is a device for ascertaining what principles are justifiable, not a historical agreement.

@example
title: Maximin and expected value behind the veil
problem: Three social arrangements give these incomes (in units) to four equally likely positions: Arrangement 1: 12, 12, 12, 12. Arrangement 2: 8, 14, 20, 30. Arrangement 3: 4, 20, 40, 60. Which arrangement does the maximin rule choose, and which maximizes expected income?
step: Find the minimum of each arrangement: 12 for Arrangement 1, 8 for Arrangement 2 and 4 for Arrangement 3.
step: Maximin picks the arrangement whose minimum is highest: Arrangement 1.
step: Compute the average income. Arrangement 1: 12. Arrangement 2: $(8 + 14 + 20 + 30)/4 = 18$. Arrangement 3: $(4 + 20 + 40 + 60)/4 = 31$.
step: Expected income is highest in Arrangement 3. So the two rules disagree, which is the point of the Harsanyi–Rawls dispute: it matters whether the parties should be risk averse or should maximize the average.
answer: Maximin chooses Arrangement 1; expected income is highest in Arrangement 3.

@example
title: Applying reasonable rejection
problem: Compare two principles for employers: (A) "An employer may dismiss a worker at any moment without notice or reason" and (B) "An employer must give a month's notice and a reason before dismissing a worker". Which could be reasonably rejected, and by whom?
step: Identify who is affected and how. Under A, a worker may suddenly lose income and security. Under B, an employer bears some cost and loses some flexibility.
step: Compare complaints person by person. A worker's complaint against A concerns livelihood, which is serious. An employer's complaint against B concerns inconvenience and cost, which are less serious.
step: The contractualist test says that a principle is ruled out if someone could reasonably reject it. A worker could reasonably reject A given the alternative B, whereas an employer's complaint against B is not as strong.
step: So A is disallowed. Note the test compares individuals' complaints, without adding up total benefits; a reader who thinks the employer's complaints are also serious must argue that B imposes a comparable burden.
answer: Principle A can be reasonably rejected by workers; principle B is the one that no one could reasonably reject on these facts.

@mistakes
- Treating the original position as a historical event. It is a hypothetical device for modeling fairness.
- Confusing Rawls's theory of justice, which concerns the basic structure of society, with Scanlon's theory of what we owe to each other.
- Saying the veil of ignorance removes all knowledge. The parties know general facts about society, economics and psychology.
- Saying that contractualism is a version of utilitarianism because it considers everyone's interests. It compares individual complaints and does not simply add benefits.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain how Scanlon's contractualism differs from utilitarianism in how it treats the claims of many people with small stakes against one person with a large stake, and assess whether the difference is an advantage.
hint: Focus on the individualist character of the complaint model.
solution: A utilitarian adds up all the benefits and burdens, so enough small benefits can outweigh a large burden to one person. A contractualist asks, for each principle, whether any individual could reasonably reject it, comparing that person's complaint with the complaints of others one by one. A principle that lets one person suffer grave harm for the sake of trivial gains to many can be reasonably rejected by the one, so contractualism avoids the verdict that numbers always win. This looks like an advantage in cases of grave harm. But it faces the problem of numbers: if the stakes for each of many people are only slightly smaller than the harm to the one, it seems that numbers should count, and the theory needs an account of when complaints may be combined.
