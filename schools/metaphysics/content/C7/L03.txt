@article
You walk past a shallow pond and see a child drowning. Wading in will ruin your shoes and make you late. Almost everyone thinks you must save the child. Peter Singer's 1972 paper "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" asks you to accept that distance does not change the moral picture: if you can save a life far away at a modest cost, you must do so too. The conclusion is that most affluent people are doing wrong, every day. For some this shows how serious our obligations are; for others it shows that a theory generating such a conclusion has gone wrong. This tension is the **demandingness objection**, one of three classic complaints against consequentialism.

### Objection 1: demandingness

Singer's argument itself has a standard form.

| P1 | Suffering and death from lack of food, shelter and medical care are bad. |
| P2 | If it is in our power to prevent something bad from happening without thereby sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought, morally, to do it. |
| P3 | It is in our power to prevent some of this suffering and death without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance. |
| C | So we ought, morally, to prevent some of this suffering and death. |

This is valid. Premise P2 is much weaker than maximizing utilitarianism, since it requires sacrifice only up to the point of comparable moral importance, but the argument shows how quickly ordinary life comes under moral demand. The **demandingness objection** says that maximizing consequentialism requires so much of agents that it leaves no room for personal projects, partiality to family and friends, or leisure. Replies come in two kinds. One bites the bullet: our intuitions are shaped by self-interest, and moral theories should be revisionary. The other limits the theory. Samuel Scheffler, in *The Rejection of Consequentialism* (1982), proposed **agent-centered prerogatives**: permissions to give extra weight to your own projects, so that you need not always produce the best outcome. The cost, which critics press, is that a prerogative of this kind seems to permit you to refuse a small sacrifice that would save someone's life.

### Objection 2: rights and the separateness of persons

Imagine a surgeon with five patients each needing a different organ, and a healthy visitor whose organs could save all five. If only outcomes matter, five lives outweigh one. Most people think the surgeon must not do this. The objection:

| P1 | If consequentialism is true, then an act is right whenever it produces the best total outcome. |
| P2 | Killing the visitor to save the five produces a better total outcome than not doing so. |
| C1 | So if consequentialism is true, killing the visitor is right. |
| P3 | Killing the visitor is wrong. |
| C | So consequentialism is false. |

John Rawls, in *A Theory of Justice* (1971), put a related complaint in terms of the **separateness of persons**: utilitarianism treats society like a single person whose gains and losses can be traded off, and so fails to take seriously that there are many persons, each with a life of his or her own. Robert Nozick drew the conclusion that individual rights function as **side constraints**: limits on what may be done to people whatever the benefits. Consequentialists reply in several ways. They may say that in realistic settings the act would not produce the best outcome (it would undermine trust in doctors), though the case can be stipulated to avoid this. They may build rights into the account of value, giving weight to the minimization of rights violations, but then they face a further puzzle: such a theory may require you to violate one right to prevent two violations by others, which constraints-based theorists reject.

### Objection 3: integrity

Bernard Williams, in "A Critique of Utilitarianism" (in *Utilitarianism: For and Against*, 1973), asks you to imagine Jim, a traveler who arrives in a town where a captain is about to shoot twenty captives. The captain offers Jim a deal: if Jim himself shoots one, the other nineteen will go free. Otherwise all twenty will die. Williams's point is not that Jim should refuse, but that utilitarianism treats the answer as obvious and gives no weight to Jim's relation to his own actions, and more generally to the commitments and projects that make up his life. Utilitarianism's **negative responsibility**, on which you are responsible for outcomes you could have prevented as much as for those you cause, makes your own projects just one more source of outcomes. Williams calls this an attack on **integrity**. Defenders reply that Jim's feelings of revulsion are real but that they are not reasons that outweigh nineteen lives, or they are costs that count in the calculation.

### Taking stock

Each objection targets a different feature: demandingness attacks maximization, the rights objection attacks aggregation across persons, and the integrity objection attacks agent-neutrality together with negative responsibility. Note that the strength of an objection is relative to the version of consequentialism it targets.

@example
title: Which feature does the objection target?
problem: A critic says: "A theory that requires Maya to give up her music lessons whenever the money would save more lives elsewhere gives her no room to have a life of her own." Which objection is this, which component does it target, and what would an agent-centered prerogative say?
step: The complaint is that the theory leaves no room for personal projects. That is the demandingness objection, with some overlap with integrity.
step: The component targeted is maximization: the requirement that Maya always produce the best outcome. Agent-neutrality is also at issue, since her projects count for no more than anyone else's.
step: An agent-centered prerogative says Maya may give extra weight to her own projects, so she is permitted but not required to give up the lessons. Critics reply that the prerogative must be limited, or it will permit her to refuse a trivial sacrifice that would save a life.
answer: The demandingness objection; it targets maximization; the prerogative permits partiality at the cost of a difficult question about where permission ends.

@example
title: Replying to the transplant case
problem: Evaluate the reply "The transplant case is unrealistic: if doctors killed patients, no one would trust hospitals, so the act would not produce the best outcome."
step: The reply attacks premise P2 of the rights argument, the claim that killing the visitor produces the best outcome.
step: It works against the case as ordinarily told in real settings, where the long-term effects on trust are bad.
step: But the critic can stipulate that the act would be secret and without side effects. Then P2 holds, and the question is whether the verdict that the act is right is acceptable. The reply therefore shows that real-world consequentialists need not endorse such acts, but does not remove the objection to the theory's account of why they are wrong.
answer: The reply attacks P2 and succeeds for realistic versions, but the objection survives in the stipulated version.

@mistakes
- Treating the three objections as one. Demandingness concerns how much the theory asks, rights concern what may be done to individuals, and integrity concerns the agent's relation to her own projects and actions.
- Saying that Williams argues that Jim should not shoot. His point concerns how the theory treats the question, not the answer.
- Thinking Singer's argument requires maximizing utilitarianism. Premise P2 as stated needs only a comparable-importance threshold.
- Assuming that a reply which works in the realistic case settles the objection. Critics can modify the case to remove the replies.

choice: A rule that is followed because following it usually has good consequences

solution: A side constraint, in Nozick's usage, restricts what may be done to people whatever the benefits. A rule justified by its usual consequences is a rule-consequentialist device, a weight on respecting rights is a goal-based consequentialist device, and a permission to favor your projects is an agent-centered prerogative.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose either the demandingness objection or the rights objection. State it in standard form, and write a paragraph on the strongest consequentialist reply and whether it succeeds.
hint: Pick the premise that the reply attacks and say whether the reply survives the strongest version of the objection.
solution: Demandingness objection: P1: If maximizing consequentialism is true, then you are required to make any sacrifice of your own projects, leisure and relationships that would produce a better outcome overall. P2: Some such sacrifices (giving up everything that makes your life your own, for a small gain to others) are not required. C: So maximizing consequentialism is false. One strong reply is Scheffler's agent-centered prerogative, which denies P1 by allowing you to give extra weight to your own projects, so that you need not always produce the best outcome. This reply is substantial, because it fits the ordinary thought that morality leaves room for a life of one's own. It faces the problem of where the permission ends: a prerogative generous enough to protect your projects seems also to permit refusing a small sacrifice that would save a life. A rival reply bites the bullet and denies P2, holding that our intuitions about what is required are distorted by self-interest and that moral theory should be revisionary. Whether either reply succeeds depends on whether the prerogative can be bounded in a principled way and on how much weight intuitions about permissions should carry.
