@article
A shopkeeper gives correct change to every customer, children included. He is not cheating anyone. Does that make him morally admirable? Immanuel Kant's answer in the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals* (1785) is: it depends on his reason. If he is honest only because honesty is good for business, his act conforms to duty but, Kant holds, has no genuine **moral worth**. An action has moral worth when it is done from duty, that is, because it is morally required. This is the starting point of Kant's ethics: what makes an act good is the principle on which the agent acts, not the results it produces.

### Imperatives

An **imperative** is a command of reason. A **hypothetical imperative** commands an action as a means to an end you have: "If you want to be a doctor, study anatomy." It applies to you only if you have the end. A **categorical imperative** commands an action unconditionally, whatever you happen to want. Kant argues that morality consists of categorical imperatives: a moral requirement does not disappear because you lack the relevant desire. Whether Kant has shown that there are such imperatives, or only that morality as we understand it presupposes them, is debated.

### The formula of universal law

Kant gives several formulations of the categorical imperative, which he regards as equivalent. The first, the **formula of universal law (FUL)**, tells you to act only on a maxim that you can at the same time will to be a universal law. A **maxim** is the principle on which you act: it states what you will do, in what circumstances, and for what end. The test is usually presented in four steps:

1. Formulate your maxim: "I will do A in circumstances C in order to achieve end E."
2. Imagine a world in which everyone in C does A to achieve E, as if by a law of nature.
3. Ask whether that world is conceivable. If not, the maxim fails: a **contradiction in conception**.
4. If it is conceivable, ask whether you can rationally will to live in it. If not, the maxim fails: a **contradiction in the will**.

Kant illustrates the test with four examples in *Groundwork* II. The maxim of making a lying promise to get money you cannot repay fails by a contradiction in conception, since in a world in which everyone did this, promises would be disbelieved and the practice of promising would not exist. The maxim of refusing to help others in need fails, by contrast, by a contradiction in the will: a world of universal non-help is conceivable, but a rational being who knows she will sometimes need help cannot will it. Kant's other two examples are suicide from self-love and neglecting one's talents; interpreters disagree about how exactly these arguments run. Kant also distinguishes **perfect duties**, which allow no exceptions in favor of inclination (his examples of suicide and the lying promise), from **imperfect duties**, which require pursuing an end, such as developing one's talents or helping others, but leave latitude about when and how.

The argument for the wrongness of a maxim has this form:

| P1 | An act is morally permissible only if its maxim can be universalized without a contradiction in conception or in the will. |
| P2 | The maxim of making a lying promise to obtain money cannot be universalized without a contradiction in conception. |
| C | So making a lying promise to obtain money is not morally permissible. |

### The formula of humanity

A second formulation tells you to treat humanity, whether in your own person or that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means. To use someone **merely as a means** is to involve them in a plan they could not share the end of, as when a deceiver relies on his victim's ignorance. Kant grounds this in the claim that rational beings have dignity, an unconditional worth beyond price. Treating someone as an end includes respecting their capacity to make their own choices.

### Objections

**The maxim-description problem.** Whether a maxim passes can depend on how it is described. "I will keep my promise unless I am tempted" can be made more or less specific, and a clever description can make nearly anything universalizable. A response is that Kant requires the maxim to include your actual reasons and circumstances, so that it cannot be redescribed to hide them; but specifying how much detail is allowed is difficult.

**Rigorism.** Kant argued, in a 1797 essay, that you must not lie even to a murderer asking where your friend is hiding. Many consider this a reductio. Defenders reply in several ways: that the essay concerns a narrow question about truthfulness in public declarations and is not representative of Kant's considered view; that applying the formula of humanity (or a maxim that honestly states the murderer's aim) shows that deceiving a would-be murderer does not treat him as an end in the way ordinary lying does; or that Kant's strict duties describe ideal conditions and a two-level reading can allow for nonideal circumstances, where others act wrongly. Critics reply that the logic of perfect duties excludes exceptions and that Kant himself endorsed the strict conclusion. How far Kant's theory can avoid the conclusion remains contested.

**Conflicting duties.** If duties are absolute, what is to be done when they conflict? Kant says genuine conflicts of duties are impossible, but critics doubt this.

@example
title: Applying the formula of universal law
problem: Apply the FUL test to this maxim: "When I can easily get away with it, I will copy another student's answers in an exam in order to get a better grade."
step: State the maxim: Circumstance: an exam in which I can easily copy undetected. Act: copy another student's answers. End: a better grade.
step: Universalize: suppose everyone in such circumstances copies. Exams could no longer show anyone's own work, so a grade would no longer indicate a student's achievement, and the point of copying (getting a better grade as a sign of achievement) would be defeated.
step: Classify: the maxim undermines the practice on which its own success depends, which is the structure of a contradiction in conception. The maxim cannot be universalized without defeating its own purpose.
step: Conclude: by FUL the maxim is impermissible. A critic may say that the failure is more a practical than a logical contradiction. Defenders say the test is about whether the maxim could be a law while still achieving its end.
answer: The maxim fails the universalization test, by a contradiction in conception on the standard reading.

@example
title: Testing a maxim of non-assistance
problem: Apply FUL to the maxim: "I will never give any time to anyone else's projects, whatever they need and whatever it costs me, so that I keep all my time to myself."
step: State the maxim, including circumstances and end: in all circumstances, refuse help, in order to keep my time.
step: Universalize: a world in which no one ever gives time to another is conceivable; people could survive and even do their own work.
step: Ask whether I can will it: I am a finite rational being who will sometimes need others' help to achieve my own ends. Willing a world without help conflicts with my other willing, so I cannot will it.
step: Classify: a contradiction in the will, not in conception. It supports an imperfect duty of beneficence, not a duty to help in every case.
answer: The maxim passes the conception test and fails the will test; it supports the imperfect duty of beneficence.

@mistakes
- Treating the categorical imperative as "What if everyone did that?" in the sense of bad consequences. The test is about contradiction in conception or in the will, not about outcomes.
- Applying the test to an act instead of a maxim. The maxim states the circumstances and end, and different maxims can underlie the same outward act.
- Saying Kant thinks consequences are irrelevant to morality. He denies that consequences make an act morally worthy, but beneficence aims at others' welfare.
- Confusing hypothetical with categorical imperatives: a hypothetical imperative depends on your having an end.
