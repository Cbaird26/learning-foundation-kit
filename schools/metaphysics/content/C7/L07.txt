@article
Two drivers leave a party after the same number of drinks. Each drives equally carelessly. One gets home safely. The other, just as she turns a corner, finds a child has stepped into the road, and the child is killed. Most of us judge the second driver far more harshly, and she will judge herself more harshly too. Yet what separated them was luck: whether a child happened to be there. If we are only to be morally judged for what we control, this difference seems unjustified. The puzzle is the problem of **moral luck**: the apparent fact that how morally good or bad someone is, or how much they are to blame, depends on factors beyond their control.

### The control principle and the problem

The problem was set out in two essays, both called "Moral Luck": Bernard Williams's, who introduced the term, and Thomas Nagel's, written as a companion and reprinted in his book *Mortal Questions* (1979); both first appeared in 1976. The tension arises between our ordinary practice and a principle that seems very plausible.

| P1 | (Control principle) A person is morally assessable only for what is within her control. |
| P2 | Many of the things for which we ordinarily assess people, such as the results of their actions, the circumstances they face, and the traits they have, depend significantly on factors beyond their control, and so are not within their control. |
| P3 | We do ordinarily assess people for these things, and often think we are right to. |
| C | So either our ordinary assessments are mistaken, or the control principle is false. |

The conclusion is a dilemma, not a refutation: which side gives way is what responses disagree about.

### Four kinds of luck

Nagel distinguished four kinds.

- **Resultant luck**: luck in how actions and projects turn out. The two drivers above differ in resultant luck.
- **Circumstantial luck**: luck in the situations one faces. A person who is never offered a bribe cannot show she would refuse it; someone in a morally ruinous regime is tested in ways that most of us never are.
- **Constitutive luck**: luck in the kind of person one is: one's temperament, capacities and inclinations.
- **Antecedent (causal) luck**: luck in how one is determined by antecedent circumstances, which connects the problem to free will.

Nagel argued that, as we examine each factor, the control principle erodes the area in which we are responsible until little is left, yet we cannot give up our ordinary judgments of persons. He described this as a real paradox.

### Responses

**Deny that luck affects moral assessment.** The Kantian tradition holds that the moral worth of an act lies in the will and not in its results. On this view the two drivers are equally blameworthy; we may feel differently about them, but this feeling is not a moral judgment. The cost is that this requires revising common practice, such as the more severe treatment of those who cause harm.

**Accept moral luck.** Williams argued that there are cases where moral assessment properly depends on how things turn out. His imagined painter, Gauguin, abandons his family to pursue painting, and whether his decision was justified depends on whether he succeeds, which he could not know in advance. This is *intrinsic* luck, Williams says, luck that enters into the justification itself. He also stressed **agent-regret**: a driver who, through no fault of her own, runs over a child has a distinctive regret that spectators lack. The existence of such regret, he suggested, shows that we care about what we do and not only about what we intended.

**Reinterpret.** Others accept that luck affects what happens but deny that it affects *blameworthiness*: the second driver is no more to blame, but she may have a different obligation (to make amends, to bear legal liability) and a different appropriate emotional response. The challenge is to say why the same blameworthiness leads to different legal or emotional treatment.

### Responsibility and the reactive attitudes

P. F. Strawson, in "Freedom and Resentment" (1962), argued that our practice of holding people responsible is rooted in **reactive attitudes** such as resentment and gratitude, and that these are not the sort of thing we could give up on the basis of a theory that they are unjustified. Applied here, a Strawsonian may say that the attitudes that make the second driver feel guilty are part of our practice of responsibility and need not be abolished because they depend on luck. Whether practice can justify itself in this way is disputed.

### Where this leaves ethics

The problem of moral luck bears on each theory in this course. Consequentialists evaluate outcomes and can accept that luck affects results while taking the best decision to be the one with best expected value. Kantians locate worth in the will and tend toward the first response. Virtue ethicists, for whom character is central, must face constitutive luck: if flourishing depends on good fortune, is it fully in our control?

@example
title: Classifying kinds of luck
problem: Classify the luck in each case. (a) A cautious surgeon's operation fails because of an undetectable defect in the equipment. (b) Anna has a naturally calm temper, which makes it easy for her to be patient. (c) Ben was never in a situation where lying would have benefited him greatly, so he has never been tempted. (d) Two negligent engineers, one of whose bridges collapses.
step: (a) The failure is in how the action turns out, beyond her control: resultant luck.
step: (b) A calm temper is part of the kind of person she is, not something she chose: constitutive luck.
step: (c) The luck lies in the situations Ben has faced: circumstantial luck.
step: (d) The difference between the engineers is a difference in how their negligent acts turned out: resultant luck.
answer: (a) resultant; (b) constitutive; (c) circumstantial; (d) resultant.

@example
title: Reconstructing the dilemma
problem: Explain in terms of the control-principle argument why the following claim by a student is an incomplete response: "The control principle is obviously true, so the two drivers are equally to blame."
step: The student accepts premise P1, the control principle, and draws the conclusion that our ordinary assessments are mistaken, which is the first horn of the dilemma.
step: This is a coherent response, but it must still address premise P3: we ordinarily think that the second driver is more to blame, and she herself seems to feel that way. The student owes an explanation of why those reactions are mistaken or can be reinterpreted.
step: A fuller response says that the reactions track something other than blame, such as agent-regret, liability or the badness of the outcome. That fills the gap between the control principle and ordinary practice.
answer: The student takes one horn of the dilemma but still needs to explain away or reinterpret the ordinary judgments that conflict with it.

@mistakes
- Treating moral luck as the claim that morality is arbitrary. It is a problem: a conflict between the control principle and ordinary practice.
- Saying that Williams denies that there is any such thing as luck in morality. He argues that it plays a real role.
- Confusing resultant luck (how things turn out) with circumstantial luck (what situations one faces).
- Assuming a Kantian cannot recognize that outcomes matter in any way. The Kantian holds they do not affect moral worth.
