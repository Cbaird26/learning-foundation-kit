Last week's realists said that moral claims describe moral facts. Two families of anti-realists reject this in different ways. The **error theorist** agrees that moral claims *try* to describe facts and says they fail: the facts do not exist. The **expressivist** says moral claims were never trying to describe facts in the first place. Each faces a serious challenge, and this lesson sets out the central argument for each view and the central objection to it.

### Error theory and the argument from queerness

J. L. Mackie's *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977) opens with the claim that there are no objective values. His error theory has a semantic part and a metaphysical part.

| P1 | Moral claims, as ordinary people understand them, presuppose that there are objective moral properties that give anyone reasons to act, whatever their desires. |
| P2 | There are no such properties. |
| P3 | A positive moral claim whose presupposition that there are such properties fails is false. |
| C | So positive moral claims (such as "Cruelty is wrong") are systematically false. |

The semantic premise P1 is the claim that ordinary moral thought has a commitment to **objective prescriptivity**: a property that is part of the way the world is, and that is also authoritative for action. The metaphysical premise P2 is supported by the **argument from queerness**.

| P1 | If there were objective moral properties, they would be entities or qualities of a very strange sort, unlike anything else in the universe, because they would be both objective and intrinsically action-guiding. |
| P2 | If there were such properties, we would need a special faculty of moral perception or intuition, unlike our other ways of knowing, to be aware of them. |
| P3 | We should not believe in entities or faculties that are this strange, without strong reason. |
| P4 | There is no strong reason to believe in them. |
| C | So we should not believe that there are objective moral properties. |

Mackie also gave the **argument from relativity**: the variation in moral codes between societies is better explained by the view that codes reflect ways of life than by the view that people perceive objective values with varying success. We return to disagreement in Week 12.

Realists reply in several ways. Naturalists deny P1: moral properties need not be strange, since they are natural properties of a familiar kind. Non-naturalists such as David Enoch accept that the properties are distinctive but deny P4, arguing that they are indispensable to deliberation and so give us strong reason to believe in them. A related non-naturalist reply attacks P3: the charge of queerness would equally condemn normative facts about reasons for belief. This is a **companions in guilt** reply: if the argument eliminated moral facts, it would eliminate epistemic normativity too, including the norms that the error theorist relies on when she asks us to believe her argument. The error theorist can respond that the cases differ, or accept the consequence.

### Expressivism and the Frege-Geach problem

**Expressivism** (Ayer's emotivism, and the more developed views of Simon Blackburn and Allan Gibbard) says that to call something wrong is to express an attitude toward it, not to describe it. This explains the connection to motivation and seems to avoid the metaphysical queerness of moral facts. Its central challenge is the **Frege-Geach problem**, which Peter Geach pressed (drawing on a point of Frege's), and John Searle made a related point about speech acts. The challenge begins with the observation that moral sentences occur in contexts where they are not asserted: "If lying is wrong, then getting your brother to lie is wrong." In such a conditional no one expresses disapproval of lying, so the meaning of "lying is wrong" cannot simply be "boo to lying", and yet the argument below is plainly valid.

| P1 | If lying is wrong, then getting someone else to lie is wrong. |
| P2 | Lying is wrong. |
| C | So getting someone else to lie is wrong. |

A valid modus ponens requires that "Lying is wrong" mean the same in P1 as in P2. But in P2 it is asserted and (the expressivist says) expresses disapproval; in P1 it is merely embedded and expresses nothing. If the meaning is given by what asserting expresses, the meaning differs between the premises, and the argument seems to equivocate. The expressivist needs an account of meaning, for negation, conditionals and other connectives, that does not take the meaning of a sentence to be the state it expresses *when asserted*.

Blackburn's **quasi-realism** aims to earn the right to the ordinary language of truth, belief and logic from within an expressivist base: it says that moral sentences have the logical behavior of descriptive ones because the underlying attitudes have a structure, so that someone who accepts P1 and P2 and rejects the conclusion has attitudes that clash. Gibbard developed a more formal treatment (norm-expressivism), on which a sentence's meaning is given by the combinations of attitudes it rules out. Critics doubt that the account succeeds, in particular for negation (a problem sometimes called the negation problem): what is the attitude of disapproving of lying, as opposed to not disapproving of it?

### Comparing the two

The error theorist keeps cognitivist semantics and gives up truth; the expressivist gives up the cognitivist semantics and tries to keep as much of moral discourse as possible. The error theorist faces the question of what to do with moral thought, abolish it or keep it as a useful fiction. The expressivist faces the Frege-Geach problem.

@example
title: Locating the target of a reply
problem: A naturalist says: "Moral properties like harm and fairness are no stranger than the properties studied in psychology or economics." Which premise of Mackie's argument from queerness does this attack?
step: The premise that moral properties would be of a very strange sort is P1 of the argument from queerness.
step: The naturalist's claim is that moral properties are natural and so not especially strange. That denies P1.
step: A non-naturalist would instead let P1 stand and deny P3, the claim that strange entities should not be believed in without strong reason.
answer: The naturalist attacks P1; a non-naturalist would attack P3 instead.

@example
title: Spotting the equivocation
problem: Explain why the Frege-Geach problem is a problem for the claim that "Stealing is wrong" simply means "Boo to stealing".
step: Consider "If stealing is wrong, then taking a friend's bike is wrong." The antecedent is not asserted, so no one is saying "boo to stealing" in uttering it.
step: If the meaning of "stealing is wrong" were just the attitude expressed in asserting it, the antecedent would have no meaning in this conditional, or a different one.
step: But the argument "If stealing is wrong, taking a bike is wrong; stealing is wrong; so taking a bike is wrong" is valid, and validity requires the same meaning in each place.
answer: The simple expressivist account makes the argument seem to equivocate; the expressivist needs a more sophisticated account of embedded sentences.

@mistakes
- Treating the error theory as the claim that morality is unimportant or that anything is permitted. It is a thesis about the truth of moral claims, and Mackie himself went on in the same book to discuss first-order questions about how to live.
- Saying that expressivists think moral claims are meaningless. They think they have a different function.
- Confusing the Frege-Geach problem, which is about embedding and validity, with the objection that expressivism cannot explain motivation. Expressivism handles motivation well.
- Saying the argument from queerness shows only that we cannot know moral facts. It also concerns what kind of property they would have to be.
