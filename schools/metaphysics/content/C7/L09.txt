@article
A mathematician checks a proof and finds it correct. No one thinks the proof's correctness depends on what anyone wants it to be. A physician finds that a patient is dehydrated: the fact does not depend on the physician's mood. **Moral realism** says that moral claims can be like this. In its standard form it has two parts: (1) some moral claims are true, and (2) their truth does not depend on the attitudes, conventions or opinions of any person or group. Most realists add that moral claims are beliefs about real moral properties. Realism is a thesis in metaphysics and semantics at once: there are moral facts, and moral sentences describe them.

### Naturalist and non-naturalist realism

The question dividing realists is what kind of property goodness or wrongness is.

**Reductive naturalism** says that moral properties are identical to natural properties, those studied by the natural and social sciences, or at least discovered by empirical inquiry. A utilitarian naturalist might say that rightness just is the property of maximizing well-being. **Non-reductive naturalism**, as in the "Cornell realism" of Richard Boyd, Nicholas Sturgeon and David Brink, holds that moral properties are natural, but constituted by natural properties without being reducible to any simple description, much as "being a good thermostat" might be realized by many different mechanisms. **Non-naturalism**, defended by G. E. Moore in *Principia Ethica* (1903), holds that moral properties are real but distinct from any natural property. Moore thought goodness is a simple, unanalyzable property we grasp by intuition. W. D. Ross's *The Right and the Good* (1930) combined non-naturalism with **pluralism**: there are several irreducible *prima facie* duties, among them fidelity, reparation, gratitude, justice, beneficence, self-improvement and non-maleficence, and we weigh them by judgment in each case. Recent non-naturalists include Russ Shafer-Landau and David Enoch, who argue that moral facts are objective and irreducibly normative, and that we can accept them for the same reasons we accept other facts that cannot be reduced to science.

### The open question argument

Moore's main argument against reducing goodness to anything else is the **open question argument**. Suppose someone proposes that being good is the same as being N, where N is some natural property, such as producing pleasure. Moore says that we can always sensibly ask: "I grant that this is N, but is it *good*?" A question of that kind is *open*: it is not trivially answered by understanding the words.

| P1 | If "good" were synonymous with a natural term N, then the question "Is what is N good?" would be closed: anyone who understood the terms would see that it has an obvious answer. |
| P2 | For every natural term N that has been proposed, the question "Is what is N good?" is open. |
| C | So "good" is not synonymous with any natural term that has been proposed. |

The argument is valid. Its conclusion covers the proposals tested; Moore generalized it to the claim that no *definition* of "good" in natural terms is correct, and took it to establish that goodness is a distinct property. Whether it shows that is the main issue, and there are several replies.

### Replies

**Synthetic identity.** The argument shows that two *concepts* differ in meaning, not that the *properties* differ. "Is heat the motion of molecules?" is a sensible, open question, though heat turned out to be molecular motion; the identity is discovered by science and is not a matter of meaning. Naturalists such as Richard Boyd and Peter Railton argue that moral terms may pick out natural properties without being synonymous with natural terms. This weakens the argument's conclusion without denying that the question is open.

**A reply to the reply.** Non-naturalists note that scientific identities like heat and molecular motion are accepted because they best explain observations. They ask whether moral identifications have this kind of explanatory support. Also, the "moral twin earth" argument of Terence Horgan and Mark Timmons suggests that if "good" referred to whatever natural property regulates our moral use, then communities with different regulating properties would be talking past one another when they seem to disagree.

**Other concerns about realism.** Gilbert Harman argued in *The Nature of Morality* (1977) that moral facts seem unnecessary to explain our observations, in contrast to scientific facts. Sturgeon replied that moral facts do explain, for instance, that a leader's cruelty explains the uprising against him. Non-naturalists face the worry about **supervenience**, since moral properties seem to depend on natural properties yet be distinct from them, and about **epistemology**: how do we know non-natural facts? Naturalists face the worry that moral properties look too *normative* to be identical to descriptive ones. Each version has a characteristic cost.

@example
title: Applying the open question argument
problem: A philosopher proposes: "Being right is the same as being what a majority of one's society approves." Apply the open question argument to this proposal.
step: Form the question: "I grant that this act is approved by a majority of my society, but is it right?"
step: The question appears open: someone might sensibly doubt whether a majority-approved practice, say one that harms a minority, is right.
step: By the argument, if "right" meant what the majority approves, the question would be closed: it would be like asking "Is this approved by the majority, but is it approved by the majority?" Since it is open, "right" does not mean that.
answer: The proposal fails as a definition of the meaning of "right" by the open question argument.

@example
title: The synthetic identity reply applied
problem: A naturalist says that "wrong" refers to the property of reducing the well-being of conscious creatures. Explain how she responds to the open question argument and what a non-naturalist can say in return.
step: She grants that "Is what reduces well-being wrong?" feels open, but denies that this shows the properties differ. It shows only that the concepts differ.
step: As "Is lightning an electrical discharge?" could be asked sensibly before the discovery and yet lightning is an electrical discharge, so the property of wrongness might be identical to a natural property without synonymy.
step: The non-naturalist replies that such identities are earned by explanation and prediction, and it is unclear that a moral identity is earned in this way. He may also press that the same naturalist should expect moral disputes to be settled by empirical investigation, which seems wrong.
answer: The naturalist distinguishes concepts from properties; the non-naturalist asks what supports the proposed moral identity and whether it fits the way moral disagreement works.

@mistakes
- Saying the open question argument shows that moral claims are not true. It is aimed at definitions of "good" in natural terms.
- Equating naturalism with the claim that moral claims are about what is "natural" in the sense of untouched by humans. The term means that moral properties are among those studied by empirical inquiry.
- Treating all moral realists as intuitionists. Naturalist realists need not rely on intuition.
- Treating non-naturalism as supernaturalism. Moore's goodness is a non-natural property, not a divine one.

solution: The argument concludes that "good" is not synonymous with any natural term: the concept of goodness is not the same as any natural concept. So the argument alone does not rule out a naturalist who claims that goodness is identical to a natural property discovered by inquiry. To reach non-naturalism, Moore needed an additional premise, that goodness is a simple property, or an argument that the proposed identities lack support. This matters because the debate is about the nature of moral properties, and the open question argument speaks directly only to meanings.
