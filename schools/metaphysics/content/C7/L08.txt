@article
Consider the claim "Torturing someone for fun is wrong." A normative ethicist asks *why* it is wrong, and what a general theory of right and wrong would say about it. A metaethicist asks questions *about* the claim itself. What is someone doing when she says it: describing a fact, expressing a feeling, or issuing a command? Could it be true? If so, what makes it true, and how could anyone know? **Metaethics** is the study of the nature, meaning and status of moral thought and talk. It differs from **normative ethics**, which asks what is right and wrong and why (Weeks 2 to 6), and from **applied ethics**, which applies those theories to specific issues.

### Four families of questions

- **Metaphysical:** Are there moral facts or properties? If so, are they mind-independent, and are they natural properties or something distinctive?
- **Semantic:** What do moral sentences mean? Do they state facts and so aim at truth, or do they do something else?
- **Epistemic:** Can we have moral knowledge, and how?
- **Motivational:** How do moral judgments relate to motivation? Can someone sincerely judge that she ought to do something and not be at all moved to do it?

The questions are linked: an answer to one constrains the others.

### The map

The basic division is semantic. **Cognitivism** holds that moral judgments express beliefs and are apt to be true or false. **Non-cognitivism** holds that they express non-belief states, such as attitudes or desires, so they are not (at least in the most straightforward sense) truth-apt.

Among cognitivists:

- **Moral realism** holds that some moral claims are true, and that their truth does not depend on anyone's attitudes. **Naturalist realists** say moral properties are natural properties, of the kind studied by science or ordinary empirical inquiry (Week 9). **Non-naturalist realists** say moral properties are real but distinct from natural properties.
- **Error theory** accepts the cognitivist semantics but denies that any moral claim is true, because moral claims presuppose something, such as objective prescriptivity, that does not exist. J. L. Mackie defended this in *Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong* (1977).
- **Subjectivism** and relativism hold that moral claims are true or false, but their truth depends on the attitudes of individuals or cultures.
- **Constructivism** holds that moral truths are determined by what would result from a suitable procedure of reasoning or agreement (Week 11).

Among non-cognitivists: the **emotivism** of A. J. Ayer (*Language, Truth and Logic*, 1936) and Charles Stevenson, on which moral sentences express emotions and try to influence others; **prescriptivism**, R. M. Hare's view that they are universalizable prescriptions; and the more sophisticated **expressivism** of Simon Blackburn (**quasi-realism**) and Allan Gibbard, which tries to earn the right to talk of truth, belief and fact within an expressivist framework (Week 10).

The terms cut across each other: "realism" is a metaphysical thesis, "cognitivism" a semantic one. Error theorists are cognitivists but not realists. Constructivists differ over whether they count as realists.

### The motivational puzzle

Michael Smith, in *The Moral Problem* (1994), set out three claims that seem individually plausible but are jointly inconsistent:

| P1 | Moral judgments express beliefs about an objective matter of fact. |
| P2 | If someone sincerely judges that she ought to do something, then, other things equal, she is motivated to do it. |
| P3 | Motivation requires a desire as well as a belief, and a belief alone cannot produce motivation. |

If P1 and P3 hold, then a moral judgment alone could not motivate, contradicting P2. Positions can be sorted by which claim they reject. **Non-cognitivists** reject P1: moral judgments are partly desire-like states and so do motivate. **Externalists** reject P2: a person can sincerely judge that she ought to act and lack motivation, and motivation comes from a separate desire to do what is right. Some cognitivists reject P3, holding that certain beliefs, about what there is reason to do, motivate without any prior desire. Smith himself tries to keep all three by arguing that moral judgments are beliefs about what we would desire if fully rational.

### Where the field stands

In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey of professional philosophers, about 62% accepted or leaned toward moral realism and about 26% toward anti-realism. That is a fact about the profession, not evidence that realism is true, and the survey's questions group a range of views together.

@example
title: Placing a position on the map
problem: A philosopher says: "When I call an act wrong, I am expressing disapproval of it and inviting others to share my attitude. Such sentences are not true or false." Place this view, and say which claim in the motivational triad she rejects.
step: The claim is that moral sentences express attitudes and are not truth-apt. That is non-cognitivism, and more specifically an emotivist-style view.
step: Because she denies that moral judgments are beliefs about an objective matter of fact, she rejects P1, the claim that they express beliefs.
step: She can accept P2 and P3: moral judgments, being expressions of attitudes, motivate, and motivation requires desire-like states. This is one of the traditional attractions of non-cognitivism.
answer: Non-cognitivism (emotivism); she rejects P1.

@example
title: Sorting the claims
problem: For the claim "Cruelty for fun is wrong", say what a naturalist realist, a non-naturalist realist, an error theorist and an expressivist would each say.
step: The naturalist realist says the claim is true, and wrongness is a natural property (for example, a property tied to harm and flourishing).
step: The non-naturalist realist says the claim is true, but wrongness is a distinctive property not identical to any natural property.
step: The error theorist says the claim, like all positive moral claims, is false, because moral claims presuppose an objective authority that does not exist.
step: The expressivist says that the sentence expresses an attitude of disapproval, and that she can still call the claim "true" in a deflated sense if she shares the attitude.
answer: Realists say true (differing on the nature of the property), the error theorist says false, and the expressivist says it expresses an attitude and may be called true in a deflated sense.

@mistakes
- Confusing normative and metaethical questions. "Is lying wrong?" is normative; "What are we doing when we say it is?" is metaethical.
- Treating "anti-realism" as meaning moral nihilism or that anything goes. Expressivists and constructivists may hold firm moral views.
- Treating error theory as non-cognitivism. The error theorist agrees that moral claims aim at truth, and says they all fail.
- Assuming that the survey percentages show which view is correct.

@practice self
prompt: Analyze the following moral luck case. A pharmacist, Tomasz, fills a prescription without the usual check of the label, which he skips because he is in a hurry. On one day the omission has no effect. On another day, with a different customer, the missing check lets a dangerous mistake through, and a patient is badly harmed. Identify the kind of luck at work, state the control principle, and explain how a Kantian and a defender of moral luck would each evaluate Tomasz.
hint: Decide which kind of luck distinguishes the two days, then apply the control principle.
solution: The luck is resultant luck: Tomasz's acts are the same on both days, but one result depends on factors beyond his control. The control principle says that people are morally assessable only for what is within their control, which suggests that Tomasz is equally blameworthy on both days. A Kantian says exactly this: his moral worth lies in his will, and the will was equally careless on both days, though he may bear legal liability and should feel regret for the harm. A defender of moral luck, following Williams, may say that the harm properly affects how he is assessed and how he should feel, since what we do includes what results from our acts. The strongest reply to the latter is that it conflicts with the control principle; the strongest reply to the Kantian is that it does not explain why harm calls for a distinctive form of responsibility.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain the difference between a metaphysical and a semantic question in metaethics, and say how the error theorist's answers to each differ from the expressivist's.
hint: The error theorist agrees with the realist about what moral sentences mean, and disagrees about whether the facts they describe exist.
solution: A metaphysical question asks whether there are moral facts or properties; a semantic question asks what moral sentences mean and whether they aim at truth. The error theorist answers the semantic question like the realist: moral sentences purport to state facts. She answers the metaphysical question negatively: there are no such facts, so positive moral claims are false. The expressivist answers the semantic question differently, saying moral sentences express attitudes rather than beliefs, and so need not be false for lack of facts. This lets the expressivist keep moral talk while denying that it needs to describe a special domain of fact.
