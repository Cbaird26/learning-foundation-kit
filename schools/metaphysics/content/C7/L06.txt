@article
Ask a consequentialist what to do and she will point to outcomes. Ask a Kantian and he will point to maxims, and ask a contractualist and she will point to principles. Ask a virtue ethicist, and the first answer is about *what kind of person to be*. A person who is honest, courageous, generous and fair will, on this view, usually act well, and what makes an act good is that it expresses good character. **Virtue ethics** puts the question "How should I live?" and the concept of character first, ahead of duty and outcomes.

### Aristotle's framework

In the *Nicomachean Ethics*, Aristotle begins from the thought that every action aims at some good, and that the highest good is **eudaimonia**, usually translated "flourishing" or "living well." His **function argument** (Book I, chapter 7) tries to say what flourishing consists in.

| P1 | The good of a thing with a characteristic function consists in performing that function well. |
| P2 | The characteristic function of a human being is activity of the soul in accordance with reason. |
| C1 | So the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with reason, performed well. |
| P3 | To perform an activity well is to perform it in accordance with its proper excellence. |
| C | So the human good is activity of the soul in accordance with reason and in accordance with virtue (excellence). |

Critics doubt P2 (why should humans have a single function, and why should it be reasoning?) and the inference from what a thing is good *at* to what is good *for* it. Defenders read the argument as an account of what a distinctively human flourishing involves, drawing on a view of human nature.

**Virtues** are stable dispositions of character, acquired by **habituation**: we become just by doing just acts, as we become musicians by playing. Aristotle's **doctrine of the mean** (Book II) says that a moral virtue is a state that hits the mean relative to us between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency: courage lies between rashness and cowardice. The mean is not an arithmetic average; it depends on the situation and is determined by reason. What identifies the mean is **practical wisdom** (*phronesis*, Book VI), the intellectual virtue of seeing what to do in particular circumstances. Aristotle holds that the virtues are unified: you cannot have the moral virtues fully without practical wisdom, and practical wisdom requires the moral virtues.

### Modern virtue ethics

G. E. M. Anscombe's paper "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) argued that the notions of moral obligation and the moral "ought" belong to a legal conception of ethics that has lost its framework, and she recommended returning to concepts of virtue and human flourishing. Philippa Foot (*Virtues and Vices*, 1978) developed a naturalistic account of the virtues. Rosalind Hursthouse (*On Virtue Ethics*, 1999) answered the charge that virtue ethics gives no guidance by stating its account of right action.

| P1 | An action is right if and only if it is what a virtuous agent would characteristically do in the circumstances. |
| P2 | A virtuous agent is one who has and exercises the virtues. |
| P3 | The virtues are the character traits that human beings need in order to flourish. |
| C | So right action is what an agent with the character traits that human beings need in order to flourish would characteristically do in the circumstances. |

Hursthouse's account comes with important qualifications: in hard cases, even a virtuous agent might act in a way she regrets, and virtue ethics can give guidance through rules such as "do what is honest" and "avoid what is cruel," which she calls v-rules.

### Objections

**Action guidance.** "Do what the virtuous person would do" seems of little use to someone who does not yet know what virtue requires. Replies point to the v-rules and to the possibility of learning from exemplars.

**Situationism.** Some psychologists and philosophers (Gilbert Harman and John Doris are the best known) argue that experiments show that behavior depends strongly on situational factors, so that stable character traits of the kind Aristotle describes may be rarer than virtue ethics assumes. Replies say that the experiments do not show that no one has robust traits, and that virtue ethics describes an ideal that most people approximate only partly.

**Self-centeredness.** Is the aim of virtue my own flourishing? Defenders say that the virtues of justice and benevolence involve concern for others as part of what flourishing is.

**Cultural variation.** Different societies prize different traits; critics ask whether there is a single account of human flourishing. Defenders point to widely shared human needs and to the role of reason in criticizing local custom.

### A different starting point

A contractualist's main question about a situation is whether a principle could be justified to each person affected; a virtue ethicist asks what a person of good character would do and how the act expresses who one is. The two can give the same verdict, but for different reasons, and they can differ in what they notice: the virtue ethicist is interested in the agent's motives, attention and emotions.

@example
title: Aristotle's mean applied to a situation
problem: Karim's friend has asked for honest feedback on a business plan that is weak. Karim can flatter the plan, rip it apart without care, or say what is good and what is weak, with tact. Identify the excess, deficiency and mean in the virtue of honest kindness here, and say what practical wisdom contributes.
step: Identify the virtue: truthful and tactful communication toward a friend. This scheme is the example's own simplification, not Aristotle's classification: in his account of friendliness, obsequiousness or flattery is the excess and quarrelsomeness the deficiency, with truthfulness a separate virtue. Here the deficiency is flattery, saying what pleases without regard to truth. The excess is harshness, saying hard truths without regard to the friend's feelings.
step: The mean is to say what is true, including the weak points, in a way that serves the friend and is sensitive to her feelings. The mean is relative to the situation and person: what is tactful for one friend might be too gentle for another.
step: Practical wisdom is the capacity to see which features of the situation matter (the friend's investment, how much time she has to change course) and to find the action that fits. It is not a formula but a developed sensitivity.
answer: Flattery is the deficiency, harshness is the excess, and honest tact is the mean, which practical wisdom identifies in the particular case.

@example
title: Contractualism and virtue ethics on the same case
problem: Compare the two approaches on this case: a colleague has been taking credit for a team's work, and you have the chance to say so in a meeting.
step: Contractualism: ask what principle for credit-taking could be reasonably rejected. A principle allowing people to take credit for others' work could be reasonably rejected by the colleagues who did the work, since it destroys their ability to be recognized for their contribution. So it is wrong to take credit, and a principle permitting you to report it is hard to reject.
step: Virtue ethics: ask what an honest, just and courageous person would do. She would be motivated by concern for fairness and truthfulness, and would raise the matter in a way that is neither cowardly (staying silent) nor rash (publicly humiliating the colleague), guided by practical wisdom about the particulars.
step: Compare: both agree that taking credit is wrong and that speaking up can be appropriate. The contractualist account focuses on the justifiability of a principle to each person; the virtue ethicist's focuses on the agent's character and on how and why she acts.
answer: They agree on the verdict but differ in explanation: justifiability of principles to each person versus what a virtuous agent would do and the character the act expresses.

@mistakes
- Saying virtue ethics ignores action. It gives an account of right action (what a virtuous agent would do) and gives guidance via v-rules.
- Reading the doctrine of the mean as moderation in everything. Some feelings and actions, such as spite, have no mean, and the mean depends on the situation.
- Confusing eudaimonia with a feeling of happiness. It is an activity of living well, not a mood.
- Treating virtue ethics as relativist. Its defenders argue that virtues are grounded in facts about human flourishing.

@practice self
prompt: You find a wallet containing cash and an identity card on a quiet street, and you could easily keep the cash without anyone knowing. Explain in one paragraph how a contractualist and a virtue ethicist would each approach this case, and say whether their verdicts or their explanations differ.
hint: For the contractualist, think about principles that could be reasonably rejected; for the virtue ethicist, think about the agent's character and the virtues involved.
solution: A contractualist asks whether a principle permitting people to keep found property when the owner is easily identified could be reasonably rejected. The owner could reasonably reject it, since losing money and documents is a serious burden, while the finder's burden of returning them is small, so keeping the cash is wrong. A virtue ethicist asks what an honest, just and considerate person would do. She would return the wallet, and not out of calculation of rules: honesty and concern for the owner would lead her to see the situation as one of returning someone's property, and the temptation as something she does not seriously entertain. The verdicts agree. The explanations differ: the contractualist grounds the wrong in what could be justified to the owner, while the virtue ethicist grounds it in the traits the act would express or betray.
