If moral truths are not facts of nature or of a special non-natural realm, and not mere expressions of attitude, what could they be? **Constructivism** offers a third idea: moral truths are the outputs of a procedure. What is right is what would be endorsed, or could not be rejected, from a standpoint defined by reasoning, agreement or the point of view of an agent. An analogy is the rules of a game. There is no pre-existing fact about what is a legal move in chess apart from the rules; yet it is not a matter of personal opinion what is legal, since the rules settle it.

### Constructivism

**Metaethical constructivism** says that there are moral truths, but they are not independent of all attitudes: they are determined by what results from some specified procedure. Rawls's "Kantian Constructivism in Moral Theory" (1980) presents the principles of justice as constructed from the original position, a procedure that models fairness and reasonableness. Christine Korsgaard, in *The Sources of Normativity* (1996), develops a Kantian constructivism about practical reason itself: the demands of morality are those we must accept if we are to regard ourselves as agents who can reflectively endorse our reasons for action. Sharon Street has defended a *Humean* constructivism, on which the standard is the agent's own evaluative attitudes, tested by whether they cohere with one another under reflection.

Constructivism differs from **realism** because it denies that moral truths hold independently of the procedure, and from **simple subjectivism** because the procedure imposes standards: your actual opinions are not automatically right. Whether it counts as a form of realism is disputed, and depends on how "independence from attitudes" is understood.

Two objections are standard. The first, in the style of the Euthyphro question, asks: does the procedure's output make an act right, or does the procedure track what is already right? If the first, the procedure seems arbitrary, since a different procedure would give other results, and we need a reason to prefer ours. If the second, constructivism collapses into realism. The constructivist replies that the procedure is justified by what is built into practical reasoning, not by any independent moral fact, though critics (for example David Enoch in his discussion of "shmagency") ask what favors the procedure over rivals. The second objection is that the procedure either presupposes moral content, which makes the account circular, or lacks content, which makes it unable to deliver substantive principles.

### Moral knowledge

**Intuitionism** holds that we can know basic moral truths by direct intellectual insight, without inferring them from anything more basic. Ross held that we can know that promise-keeping and non-injury are *prima facie* duties by reflecting on cases and principles. Critics ask what makes such intuitions reliable. **Reflective equilibrium**, the method Rawls described in *A Theory of Justice*, seeks coherence between our considered judgments about cases and general principles, adjusting each in light of the other until they fit together. It is a coherentist method; its critics say that coherence is not enough, since a coherent set of beliefs may be mistaken, and that the judgments we start with may be products of upbringing.

### Evolutionary debunking

Sharon Street's "A Darwinian Dilemma for Realist Theories of Value" (2006) raises a challenge to realists about how our attitudes would be related to independent moral truths. The argument can be put as a dilemma.

| P1 | Evolutionary forces have had a large influence on the content of our basic evaluative attitudes. |
| P2 | Either there is no relation between those evolutionary influences and independent evaluative truths, or there is one. |
| P3 | If there is no relation, then it would be a massive coincidence if our evaluative attitudes matched the independent truths, so realism implies that most of our moral beliefs are probably false or unjustified. |
| P4 | If there is a relation, it is a *tracking* relation (we evolved attitudes because they track the truth), and this account is scientifically worse than the alternative on which attitudes were selected because they promoted survival and reproduction. |
| C | So realism must either accept moral skepticism or adopt a scientifically inferior explanation of our evaluative attitudes. |

Realists have several replies. A **third-factor** reply, developed in different forms by Enoch and others, says that survival and reproduction are themselves good, so that attitudes which promote them tend to fit evaluative truths without any tracking of the truth by selection: a third factor explains the correlation. Critics respond that this assumes a substantive moral claim, and that it may not explain the whole content of our moral views. Another reply denies that debunking arguments can be restricted to moral beliefs: if they show too much, they threaten beliefs in logic and mathematics. Debunking arguments are also used against other views: Richard Joyce has used them to support an error theory.

title: Locating a reply in the debunking argument
problem: A realist says: "Survival is good, so it is no coincidence that attitudes promoting survival tend to be correct." Which premise of Street's argument does this target, and what is the challenge to it?
step: The reply targets P4, which says that if there is a relation between evolution and evaluative truth, it is a tracking relation, and that a tracking account is scientifically inferior.
step: It supplies a relation that is not tracking, since both are connected to survival, so that the correspondence is not a coincidence, while denying that selection tracks moral truth directly.
step: The challenge is that the reply assumes a substantive moral claim (that survival is good) in order to defend moral knowledge, and does not explain why many of our other moral attitudes would match the truth.
answer: It targets P4; the challenge is that it presupposes a moral claim and may not extend to the full range of our moral beliefs.

@example
title: Constructivism and the Euthyphro question
problem: A student asks: "On constructivism, is an act right because it comes out of the procedure, or does the procedure come out right because it tracks what is right?" Explain why each answer is a problem and how a constructivist might respond.
step: If the act is right because it comes out of the procedure, the procedure looks arbitrary: why this procedure and not another that gives different results? A constructivist needs an argument for the procedure.
step: If the procedure is right because it tracks what is right, then there are moral facts independent of the procedure and constructivism collapses into realism.
step: The constructivist takes the first horn and says the procedure is not arbitrary because it is built from the standpoint of practical reason, which no agent can avoid. Critics reply that this is exactly what must be shown.
answer: The first answer risks arbitrariness, the second collapses into realism, and the constructivist takes the first and appeals to the nature of practical reason.

@mistakes
- Treating constructivism as the view that each person's opinion makes things right. The procedure imposes standards.
- Treating reflective equilibrium as intuitionism. It is a coherentist method that adjusts principles and judgments together.
- Saying the debunking argument shows that moral beliefs are false. It aims to show that realists cannot explain why they would be true, and so threatens justification.
- Thinking that an argument about how our beliefs originated by itself refutes them. The argument needs a premise that the origin makes truth coincidental.

@practice self
prompt: In a paragraph, explain how reflective equilibrium works, and state one objection to it as a method for gaining moral knowledge.
hint: Describe the back-and-forth between particular judgments and principles.
solution: In reflective equilibrium we start with considered judgments about particular cases, such as that it is wrong to punish an innocent person to calm a mob, and propose general principles that fit them. When a principle conflicts with a judgment we revise one or the other, depending on which we trust more, until we reach a stable fit. The method gives moral thought a coherentist structure and does not require infallible intuitions. An objection is that coherence does not guarantee truth: a coherent set of views may rest on biases from upbringing, and so reflective equilibrium may only systematize prejudice. A defender replies that the method asks us to give weight only to judgments we hold with confidence after reflection, and to revise in light of arguments.
