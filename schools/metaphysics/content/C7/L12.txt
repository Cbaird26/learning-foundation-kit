@article
You read a news report on a medical study: smoking raises the risk of lung cancer, and the effect is large. Does it follow that you ought not to smoke? Not yet: you need a further premise, such as "you ought to avoid serious risks to your health when the benefits are small." This is a typical case of what is called the **is-ought gap**: the thesis that no set of purely factual (descriptive) premises logically entails an evaluative or normative conclusion.

### Hume's observation

The source is a short passage near the end of the first section of Book III of David Hume's *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739-40). Hume remarks that in every system of morality he has met, the author proceeds for a while in the usual way of reasoning, establishing the existence of God or making observations about human affairs, and then, suddenly, instead of the usual "is" and "is not" he finds propositions connected by "ought" or "ought not." Hume says that this change is imperceptible but important, since the new relation needs to be observed and explained, and a reason must be given for how it can be a deduction from others that are entirely different. Hume does not state a formal law; the common formulation, sometimes called Hume's law, is a reconstruction. Interpreters disagree about what Hume himself meant, with some taking him to be making a point about logic, and others a point about the psychology of moral motivation or the nature of moral judgment.

A reconstruction runs as follows.

| P1 | In a deductively valid argument, the conclusion contains no content that is not already contained in the premises. |
| P2 | A normative conclusion contains content (a prescription or evaluation) that is not contained in any set of purely descriptive premises. |
| C | So no normative conclusion can be validly deduced from purely descriptive premises. |

Premise P2 is the contested one. It is not obvious, and a realist naturalist can deny it by holding that normative claims are descriptive claims about natural properties (Week 9).

### Challenges

**Prior's counterexample.** A. N. Prior (1960) pointed out that from the premise "Tea-drinking is common in England" one can validly infer the disjunction "Either tea-drinking is common in England or all New Zealanders ought to be shot." The conclusion contains "ought", yet follows validly from a purely descriptive premise. The standard response is that the "ought" occurs *inessentially*: replace the normative disjunct with any sentence at all and the inference stays valid. Hume's law is then restated to say that no *essentially* normative conclusion follows. Whether this can be stated precisely has been much discussed.

**Searle's derivation.** John Searle, in "How to Derive 'Ought' from 'Is'" (1964), argued that from the fact that someone uttered the words "I hereby promise to pay you" one can derive that he ought to pay, using facts about the institution of promising. Critics reply that the derivation relies on a hidden evaluative premise, namely that one ought to keep promises, or that the institutional facts are already normative.

### Moral disagreement

Moral disagreement also raises a challenge to realism, in the form of Mackie's argument from relativity (Week 10), now often put as the **argument from disagreement**.

| P1 | There is widespread and persistent moral disagreement, which would remain even if all parties were fully informed and reasoning well. |
| P2 | If such disagreement would remain under ideal conditions, then there are no objective moral facts to be discovered, since that is its best explanation. |
| C | So there are no objective moral facts. |

The argument is valid, with premises that realists deny. They attack P1 by arguing that much apparent disagreement stems from differences in non-moral beliefs (about religion, or about the effects of policies), or from bias and self-interest, and that there would be more convergence under ideal conditions than appears. They attack P2 by noting that deep disagreement exists in areas where realism is not in question, such as history, philosophy or cosmology: disagreement alone does not show that there are no facts. A different reply says that the argument supports a more limited conclusion, such as a limit to our knowledge of moral truths, but not the denial that there are any.

### Three relativisms

It helps to separate three claims often run together. **Descriptive relativism** says that societies in fact differ in their basic moral views. This is an empirical claim, and may be true. **Metaethical relativism** says that the truth of moral claims is relative to a standpoint, such as a culture, so that a claim can be true for one group and false for another. **Normative relativism** (or the thesis of tolerance) says that we ought to tolerate the practices of other cultures. Descriptive relativism does not entail metaethical relativism, since differences in belief do not show that there is no fact of the matter, and metaethical relativism does not entail the normative claim, since if all moral claims are relative to cultures, that applies also to the claim that we should be tolerant.

@example
title: Spotting the hidden premise
problem: Someone argues: "Most people in this region prefer to settle disputes by retaliation. So retaliation is the right way to settle disputes here." Identify the gap, and the premise needed to close it.
step: The premise is descriptive (a fact about what people prefer). The conclusion is normative (a claim about what is right).
step: The argument as stated is not valid, since the facts could be as stated and retaliation could still be wrong. By the is-ought gap, a normative premise is needed.
step: One premise that would close the gap is "Whatever most people in a region prefer is right there." This is a substantive and controversial moral claim, a form of relativism, and it should be evaluated on its own.
answer: The argument moves from "is" to "ought" and needs a normative bridge premise, such as a relativist principle, which is itself open to challenge.

@example
title: Which premise of the disagreement argument does the reply attack?
problem: A realist says: "Many societies disagree about how to treat animals largely because they hold different beliefs about animal minds." Which premise of the argument from disagreement does this attack?
step: The argument's first premise says that disagreement would remain under ideal conditions, including full information.
step: The realist suggests that much disagreement comes from differences in non-moral belief, so with full information it would shrink.
step: So the reply attacks P1. It does not deny that the disagreement exists, only that it would persist under ideal conditions.
answer: It attacks premise P1.

@mistakes
- Saying Hume proved that moral claims are not true. At most, the passage claims that a gap needs to be explained, and what follows is disputed.
- Reading the is-ought gap as saying that facts are irrelevant to ethics. Facts matter as part of arguments with normative premises.
- Equating descriptive relativism (cultures differ) with metaethical relativism (truth is relative).
- Taking the fact that people disagree to show that no one is right. Disagreement is common in science.
