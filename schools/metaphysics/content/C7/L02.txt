@article
You have one free afternoon and can spend it in one of three ways: tutor a struggling classmate, volunteer at a food bank or rest. A natural thought is that you ought to do whatever will make things go best. **Consequentialism** takes that thought and makes it a theory: the rightness of an act (or rule, or motive) depends only on the goodness of its consequences. Everything else, including who is affected, whether you made a promise or whether you caused the harm directly, is irrelevant except insofar as it changes the outcome.

### The ingredients

The classical theory, found in Bentham, Mill and Sidgwick, bundles several separable claims. It is worth separating them, because later variants keep some and drop others.

- **Consequentialism** proper: the right act is determined by the value of outcomes.
- **Welfarism**: the only thing of value in an outcome is the well-being of individuals (the subject of Week 1).
- **Aggregation** (sum-ranking): outcomes are compared by adding up everyone's well-being.
- **Impartiality** or **agent-neutrality**: everyone's well-being counts equally, and the reasons are the same for every agent.
- **Maximization**: the right act is the one with the best outcome, not merely a good one.

Classical **utilitarianism** combines all five with hedonism. A consequentialist need not be a utilitarian: she could count equality or the fair distribution of goods as valuable in outcomes, and so drop pure welfarism or aggregation.

### Variants

**Act versus rule.** *Act consequentialism* assesses each act by its own consequences. *Rule consequentialism*, defended by Brad Hooker in *Ideal Code, Real World* (2000), says an act is wrong if it is forbidden by the code of rules whose general acceptance would have the best consequences. Rule consequentialism can forbid an act that would produce slightly better results in a single case, for example breaking a promise when no one would ever learn of it, because a general practice of breaking promises in such cases would do worse. Its critics offer two standard objections: that it collapses into act consequentialism once rules are allowed to include enough exceptions, and that it requires obeying a rule even when you know that breaking it would do better ("rule worship"). **Two-level** views, such as R. M. Hare's in *Moral Thinking* (1981), use intuitive rules in everyday life and critical, act-level thinking to choose and revise them.

**Actual versus expected.** *Actual* consequentialism says the right act is the one that in fact produces the best outcome. But you cannot know the actual outcome in advance, so *prospective* (or *expected-value*) versions say the right act is the one with the highest expected value given the evidence available to the agent. Imagine a doctor who gives a patient the treatment that, on all available evidence, has the best prospects, and the patient has an extremely rare reaction. On the actual view she acted wrongly; on the prospective view she acted rightly.

**Maximizing versus satisficing.** *Satisficing consequentialism*, defended by Michael Slote in *Common-Sense Morality and Consequentialism* (1985), says you need only produce an outcome that is good enough. Critics ask what could justify the cut-off, and why it is acceptable to choose a worse option when a better one costs you nothing.

**Scalar views** abandon the binary of right and wrong and say only that acts are better or worse according to their outcomes.

### An argument for consequentialism

A common route to the view is the following reconstruction.

| P1 | What ultimately matters in any situation is how well the lives of the individuals affected go. |
| P2 | If what ultimately matters is how well lives go, and each life matters equally, then the best act is the one that makes lives go best overall. |
| P3 | Each life matters equally. |
| C1 | So the best act is the one that makes lives go best overall. |
| P4 | An act is right if and only if it is the best act available. |
| C | So an act is right if and only if it makes lives go best overall. |

The argument is valid but its premises are contested. Premise P1 is denied by those who hold that outcomes are not all that matters (for example, that it also matters whether you violate a rights-based constraint). Premise P4 is the maximizing assumption, which satisficers deny and scalar views bypass. The next lesson takes up objections that target P1 and P4 from the side of rights and integrity.

### Why the variants matter

Whenever someone says that consequentialism has an implausible implication, ask: which version? An objection to maximizing act-utilitarianism may leave rule or satisficing views untouched, and a response may purchase safety at the price of ceasing to be recognizably consequentialist.

@example
title: Actual versus expected value
problem: A relief agency can send supplies by road or by air. By road, there is a 0.8 chance that 50 people will be helped and a 0.2 chance that none will be. By air, there is a 0.5 chance that 90 people will be helped and a 0.5 chance that none will be. The road trip succeeds. Which option is right according to expected-value consequentialism, and which was right according to actual consequentialism?
step: Compute the expected number helped by road: $0.8 \times 50 + 0.2 \times 0 = 40$.
step: Compute the expected number helped by air: $0.5 \times 90 + 0.5 \times 0 = 45$.
step: Expected-value consequentialism says choose the higher expectation, so air was the right choice at the time of decision, by 45 to 40.
step: Actual consequentialism looks at what happened. By road, 50 people were helped. Had the air option been taken, the result would have been either 90 helped or 0, and we are told only that road succeeded; if air would have failed, then road was actually better. So the actual verdict depends on facts that the agency could not know.
answer: Expected-value consequentialism says air; actual consequentialism's verdict depends on how the air trip would have gone, which the agency could not know in advance.

@example
title: Rule versus act in a promise case
problem: You promised a friend you would return a borrowed book on Friday. On Friday you could instead use the time to help a stranger whose small project would gain slightly more good than the book's return, and no one would find out. What do act and rule consequentialism say?
step: Act consequentialism compares the outcomes of this single act. If helping the stranger really gives slightly more good, then it says to help the stranger.
step: Rule consequentialism asks which code of rules would have the best results if generally accepted. A code allowing promise-breaking whenever it would slightly improve outcomes would erode trust and make cooperation harder, so the best code plausibly includes a promise-keeping rule.
step: So rule consequentialism says return the book, even though in this case that does slightly worse. The standard objection is that this is rule worship if you know the outcome will be worse.
answer: Act consequentialism says help the stranger; rule consequentialism says keep the promise, subject to the rule-worship objection.

@mistakes
- Equating consequentialism with utilitarianism. Utilitarianism is one consequentialist theory (consequences, welfare, sum, impartiality, maximizing).
- Taking consequentialism to be a doctrine of self-interest. The standard theory is impartial: your own interests count the same as anyone else's.
- Saying a consequentialist must calculate before every act. Two-level and rule views, and the standard classical defenses, hold that rules of thumb are usually the best procedure.
- Confusing a criterion of right with a decision procedure: a theory can say what makes acts right without telling agents to think in those terms.

prompt: A clinic chooses between a screening program with a 0.7 chance of preventing 20 serious illnesses and a 0.3 chance of preventing none, and an education program that surely prevents 12. By how many illnesses does the expected number prevented by the better program exceed that of the other?
answer: 2
unit: illnesses
check: abs(0.7 * 20 + 0.3 * 0 - 12)
hint: Compute the expectation of the risky program first.
solution: The screening program has expectation $0.7 \times 20 = 14$. The education program has 12. So the screening program exceeds the education program by $14 - 12 = 2$ illnesses.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, in four or five sentences, the difference between a criterion of right and a decision procedure, and say why this distinction helps a consequentialist reply to the objection that no one could calculate the consequences of every act.
hint: Ask what the theory says makes acts right and what it recommends agents think about.
solution: A criterion of right says what makes an act right; a decision procedure is the method an agent actually uses to choose. A consequentialist can hold that outcomes are the criterion while recommending that agents rely on rules of thumb, habits and virtues in everyday decisions, since these tend to produce good outcomes and calculation is costly and error prone. So the objection that people cannot calculate shows only that calculation is not a good decision procedure, not that outcomes fail as the criterion. A critic may reply that a theory should be action-guiding, and a view whose criterion cannot be used directly gives less guidance.
