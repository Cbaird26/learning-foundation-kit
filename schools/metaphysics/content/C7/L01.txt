@article
Two people have lived to ninety. One has had a gentle, comfortable life, full of pleasant days, but has been deceived about almost everything that mattered to her. The other has had a harder life, with real friendships, real work and real struggles. Whose life went better *for the person who lived it*? Your answer depends on your **theory of well-being**: an account of what makes a life good for the one living it. This is a different question from what makes a life morally good, or what we owe to others, though most normative theories (Week 2 will show this for consequentialism) need an answer to it.

### Three families of theory

Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) gave the standard three-way classification, and the literature still uses it.

**Hedonism** says that well-being consists in pleasant experience and the absence of painful experience, and nothing else is good for you in itself. Bentham's version measured pleasure by features such as intensity and duration. Mill, in *Utilitarianism* (1863), kept hedonism but held that pleasures differ in quality as well as quantity, so that a pleasure of the intellect can be worth more than a larger quantity of a bodily one. Critics say this is hard to square with hedonism's own monistic idea that only pleasantness matters; defenders say quality can be a way of measuring how much enjoyment a pleasure involves.

**Desire-satisfaction theories** say that your life goes well to the extent that your desires are satisfied. The simplest version counts actual desires. Most defenders move to **informed-desire** versions, on which what counts is what you would desire if you were fully informed and thinking clearly. This handles a person who desires to drink what he wrongly believes is water. Two problems remain. **Adaptive preferences** arise when someone who has been deprived learns to want little, so that satisfying his desires seems too cheap a measure of how well he is doing. And what about desires whose objects never touch your experience, such as a wish that a scientific problem be solved after you are dead? Theories that count these treat your well-being as partly dependent on events you will never know about.

**Objective-list theories** say that some things are good for you whether or not you want them or enjoy them: typically knowledge, friendship, achievement, autonomy and pleasure itself. Martha Nussbaum's list of central capabilities is a well-known political application. The standard objection is that such a list can seem arbitrary and alienating, since a life could contain items from the list that the person finds meaningless. A reply is that the goods on the list are things people typically find worth wanting because they are good, rather than good merely because wanted.

### The experience machine

Robert Nozick's argument in *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* (1974) is the classic challenge to hedonism. Imagine a machine that could give you any experiences you chose, indistinguishable from real ones, for the rest of your life, while you float in a tank. Should you plug in?

| P1 | If hedonism is true, then how well a life goes depends only on the pleasant and painful experiences in it. |
| P2 | A life in the machine and a life outside it could contain exactly the same pleasant and painful experiences. |
| P3 | Plugging in would make your life go worse for you than staying out. |
| C | So hedonism is false. |

The argument is valid: by P1 and P2 the two lives are equally good for you, which P3 contradicts. The weight falls on premise P3. Replies include the claim that our reluctance is a **status quo bias** (we would also hesitate to leave the machine if we were already in it), the claim that we dislike the machine for reasons that hedonism can accommodate (the fear that it will malfunction, or that others will suffer if we leave), and the concession that hedonism may have to be replaced by a pluralistic view. Nozick himself suggests that we care about doing things and being a certain sort of person, not only about how it feels. Note that the argument, even if sound, refutes only the claim that *experience alone* matters. It does not show that desire satisfaction or an objective list is correct.

### Comparing the theories

A useful method is to build a case in which the theories give different verdicts, then ask which verdict is more plausible and whether the theory has principled ways to resist it. Do not treat a single case as decisive: theorists often respond by revising the theory, and each of the three families has been refined many times.

@example
title: Testing three theories on a posthumous desire
problem: Tomas has spent twenty years writing a history of his village. He desires that it be read and valued after his death. He dies believing it will be forgotten, but after his death it is widely read. Did the later success make Tomas's life go better for him? Give the verdict of hedonism, an actual-desire theory and an objective-list theory that includes achievement.
step: Identify what changed. Nothing in Tomas's experiences differs: he died believing his book would be forgotten. What changed is whether his desire was satisfied and whether his project had a lasting effect.
step: Hedonism counts only his pleasant and painful experiences, which are the same whether or not the book succeeds. So hedonism says the later success made no difference to how his life went for him.
step: An actual-desire theory counts the satisfaction of his desire that the book be read and valued, even though he never learned of it. So it says the success made his life go better for him, though some defenders of the theory restrict it to desires about his own life.
step: An objective-list theory that includes achievement can say the book's success contributes to the value of his achievement, so his life went better. But it need not credit the success to his life if it holds that the good lies in the work he did and not in later events.
answer: Hedonism says no difference. The actual-desire theory says his life went better (unless restricted to desires about his own life). The objective-list theory can say it went better, depending on how achievement is understood.

@example
title: Where does the experience machine argument bite?
problem: A student says: "The experience machine shows that pleasure does not matter at all." Evaluate the claim using the standard-form argument.
step: Restate what the argument concludes: that hedonism, the view that only experience matters, is false. This is the claim that something besides experience matters, not that experience does not.
step: An objective-list theory can include pleasure on its list, and a desire theory can count the desire to enjoy oneself. Both are compatible with the argument's conclusion.
step: So the student's claim goes beyond what the argument can show. Even a successful argument tells us only that pleasure is not the whole story.
answer: The student overreaches: the argument targets the sufficiency of experience for well-being, not the value of pleasure.

@mistakes
- Treating well-being as the same as moral goodness: a theory of what is good for a person is not yet a theory of what you ought to do.
- Taking the experience machine to show that pleasure is worthless, when at most it shows that pleasure is not all that matters.
- Saying that desire theories count every desire: informed-desire versions, and versions restricted to desires about one's own life, exist precisely to avoid this.
- Describing an objective-list theory as the view that people must be forced to pursue the listed goods. The theory is about what is good for a person, not about what others may impose.

prompt: Construct your own case in which an informed-desire theory and an objective-list theory give different verdicts about whether a person's life is going well. State the verdict of each theory.
hint: Imagine a person who, even when fully informed, does not want something that a typical objective list counts as good, or who wants something not on the list.
solution: Case: Elena, fully informed and thinking clearly, has no desire for friendship; she prefers solitary work on mathematics and is content. An informed-desire theory says her life can go well, since her informed desires are satisfied. The difference shows what each theory takes to be the source of the value: the person's own informed attitudes, or the goods themselves.
