@project
title: Metaethics paper: reconstruct, evaluate and defend
overview: Write a 2,500-word paper that reconstructs one central argument in metaethics (the argument from disagreement, the argument from queerness, the open question argument or the Frege-Geach problem), evaluates the strongest reply, and defends a conclusion. You must treat the other side fairly: a reader who disagrees with you should recognize their own best case.
goal: Reconstruct an argument in standard form, with premises that a defender of it would accept.
goal: Identify the premise or inference each main reply attacks, and judge which reply is strongest.
goal: Defend a clear conclusion that is narrow enough to argue for in 2,500 words.
step: Choose one argument from the four candidates (see the material below for a starting statement of each) and read the relevant encyclopedia entries and the course reading. Find at least four sources through the course reading, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy and PhilPapers, and note where each source states the argument.
step: Write a one-paragraph statement of the argument in your own words, then put it in standard form with numbered premises, and check that the conclusion follows. Mark which premises are controversial.
step: List the three most important replies in the literature. For each, say which premise or inference it attacks and what the reply asserts.
step: Choose the reply you think strongest and write a paragraph on why it is stronger than the others. Then write the best objection to that reply, and decide whether it survives.
step: Decide your thesis. It may be that the argument succeeds, that it fails, or that it succeeds only against a restricted target. State it as one sentence that someone could disagree with.
step: Draft the paper in four parts: the argument, the replies, your evaluation and the thesis defended, and the strongest remaining objection with your response. Write for an intelligent reader who has not taken this course.
step: Revise. Check that each premise is stated in the same words each time, that every attribution is accurate, that you paraphrase and cite instead of quoting modern works, and that you describe the other side's position the way its defenders would.
step: Submit the paper with a bibliography of at least four sources, and keep a one-page outline in case you need to explain it aloud.
material: Starting statements of the four arguments, which you should improve rather than copy.

| Argument | Starting statement |
|---|---|
| Disagreement | P1: There is widespread, persistent moral disagreement that would remain under ideal conditions. P2: If so, there are no objective moral facts, since that is the best explanation of it. C: So there are no objective moral facts. |
| Queerness | P1: Objective moral properties would be strange, both objective and intrinsically action-guiding. P2: Knowing them would require a special faculty. P3: We should not believe in such entities and faculties without strong reason. P4: There is no such reason. C: So we should not believe that there are objective moral properties. |
| Open question | P1: If "good" were synonymous with a natural term N, "Is what is N good?" would be closed. P2: For every proposed N it is open. C: So "good" is not synonymous with any natural term that has been proposed. |
| Frege-Geach | P1: A modus ponens argument is valid only if its components mean the same in each occurrence. P2: On simple expressivism a moral sentence's meaning is the attitude it expresses when asserted. P3: In the conditional premise, the moral clause is not asserted, so it expresses no attitude, and by P2 it has a different meaning there than in an asserted premise. C: So simple expressivism cannot explain the validity of moral modus ponens. |
deliverable: A paper of about 2,500 words, with a title, an argument in standard form, and a bibliography of at least four sources.
deliverable: A one-page outline of the paper showing the argument, the reply you evaluate and your thesis.
deliverable: A two-minute spoken explanation, without notes, of the argument and your evaluation of it.
rubric: Accuracy: the argument is stated in valid standard form, and the views and attributions are correct.
rubric: Analysis: the paper identifies which premise or inference each reply attacks and evaluates the strongest reply fairly.
rubric: Argument: the thesis is clear, narrow and defended with reasons, and the strongest remaining objection is answered.
rubric: Fairness: the opposing side is presented as its defenders would present it.
rubric: Writing and sources: the paper is organized, paraphrases and cites sources instead of quoting modern works, and uses at least four sources.
safety: Metaethics can feel unsettling when it questions whether morality is objective. Remember that these are open questions on which thoughtful people disagree, and that your own moral commitments are not at stake in reconstructing an argument. If a topic troubles you, talk it over with a teacher or someone you trust.

## Week by week

- **Week 1, well-being.** Hedonism, desire satisfaction (actual and informed) and objective lists; Nozick's experience machine targets hedonism through the judgment that plugging in is worse.
- **Week 2, consequentialism.** Separate consequentialism, welfarism, aggregation, impartiality and maximization; act and rule, actual and expected, maximizing and satisficing; expected value.
- **Week 3, objections.** Demandingness (maximization), rights and the separateness of persons (aggregation), integrity and negative responsibility (agent-neutrality); Singer, Rawls, Nozick, Williams, Scheffler.
- **Week 4, Kant.** Moral worth, hypothetical and categorical imperatives, the formula of universal law (contradiction in conception and in the will), perfect and imperfect duties, the formula of humanity.
- **Week 5, contract.** Hobbes, Rawls's original position, maximin against average utility, Scanlon's reasonable rejection.
- **Week 6, virtue.** Function argument, the mean, practical wisdom, Hursthouse's account; action guidance and situationism.
- **Week 7, moral luck.** The control principle; resultant, circumstantial, constitutive and antecedent luck; Kantian, accepting and reinterpreting responses.
- **Week 8, the map.** Metaphysical, semantic, epistemic and motivational questions; cognitivism and non-cognitivism; Smith's triad.
- **Week 9, realism.** Naturalism and non-naturalism; the open question argument and the synthetic identity reply.
- **Week 10, anti-realism.** Mackie's error theory and the argument from queerness; expressivism and the Frege-Geach problem.
- **Week 11, constructivism and knowledge.** Constructivism and the Euthyphro question; intuitionism; reflective equilibrium; Street's debunking dilemma.
- **Week 12, gap and disagreement.** Hume's gap, Prior and Searle; the argument from disagreement; three relativisms.

## Key arguments to be able to state in standard form

The experience machine; the argument for consequentialism; Singer's argument; the transplant objection; the formula of universal law test; the difference principle argument; Aristotle's function argument; the moral luck dilemma; the open question argument; the argument from queerness; the Frege-Geach problem; the Darwinian dilemma; the argument from disagreement.

## Self-check

- Can you say, for each argument, which premise each main reply attacks?
- Can you distinguish normative from metaethical questions, cognitivism from realism, and descriptive from metaethical relativism?
- Can you apply the universalization test to a new maxim and classify the failure?
- Can you compute an expected value and a maximin choice?

## Common errors

Treating a view and an objection to it as the same thing; confusing which philosopher holds which view; saying the experience machine shows pleasure is worthless; saying the open question argument shows moral claims are not true; calling error theory non-cognitivism; running together the three relativisms; and treating survey percentages as evidence that a position is true.

@exam mc
prompt: Which of the following correctly pairs a metaethical position with the claim in Smith's triad that it rejects?
choice: Non-cognitivism rejects the claim that moral judgments motivate
choice*: Non-cognitivism rejects the claim that moral judgments express beliefs about an objective matter of fact
choice: Error theory rejects the claim that belief alone cannot produce motivation
choice: Externalism rejects the claim that moral judgments express beliefs
hint: Recall the three claims: beliefs about objective fact, sincere judgments motivate, and belief alone does not motivate.
solution: Non-cognitivists deny that moral judgments express beliefs, so they can accept that such judgments motivate. Externalists reject the claim that sincere moral judgments motivate, and error theory is a view about truth and not about motivation.

@exam ms
prompt: Select every pairing of an objection with the view it is aimed at that is correct.
choice*: The demandingness objection is aimed at maximizing consequentialism.
choice*: The Frege-Geach problem is aimed at simple expressivism.
choice: The argument from queerness is aimed at emotivism.
choice: The maxim-description problem is aimed at rule consequentialism.
hint: Match each objection to the theory whose commitments it targets.
solution: Demandingness targets the requirement to maximize, and Frege-Geach targets expressivist accounts of meaning. The argument from queerness is aimed at objective moral properties (realism), and the maxim-description problem is aimed at Kant's universalization test.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of 500 to 700 words answering this question: "Does the open question argument or the argument from queerness pose the greater threat to moral realism?" State one of the arguments in standard form, identify the strongest reply to each, and defend a conclusion while treating the other side fairly.
hint: Decide first what each argument is supposed to show: the open question argument targets definitions of "good", and the argument from queerness targets the existence of objective moral properties.
solution: A strong essay notes that the arguments have different targets. The open question argument (P1: if "good" were synonymous with natural N, the question "Is what is N good?" would be closed; P2: for every proposed N it is open; C: "good" is not synonymous with any natural term that has been proposed) targets analytic naturalism and, on its own, leaves open synthetic identity (heat and molecular motion), so it does not threaten realism as such. The argument from queerness (objective, intrinsically action-guiding properties would be strange; knowing them would need a special faculty; we should not believe in such things without strong reason) targets non-naturalist realism and, by P1, does not obviously touch naturalism, whose properties are not strange. An essay might conclude that queerness poses the greater threat to non-naturalism while the open question argument poses the lesser threat to realism overall, and that companions in guilt and indispensability replies are the main non-naturalist responses to queerness. It should concede that the replies carry costs: the naturalist must say why the identified property is normative, and the non-naturalist must explain how we know non-natural facts.
rubric: States an argument accurately in valid standard form.
rubric: Identifies the strongest reply to each argument and the premise it attacks.
rubric: Defends a clear conclusion with reasons that depend on what each argument targets.
rubric: Presents the opposing view fairly, noting at least one cost of the preferred view.
