Our best physical theories say that there are electrons, that DNA carries heritable information and that the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago on the standard cosmological model. None of these things can be seen with the naked eye. Should we believe what the theories say about them, or only that the theories work? That is the question between **scientific realism** and its rivals.

### What realists claim

Scientific realism is a family of views, and philosophers distinguish three components. The **metaphysical** component says that there is a world that exists independently of what anyone thinks about it, including the unobservable parts of it. The **semantic** component says that scientific theories should be read literally, as making claims that are true or false, including claims about unobservable things. The **epistemic** component says that we have good reason to believe that our best, mature scientific theories are approximately true, and that their central theoretical terms refer. Anti-realists can accept the first component and reject the epistemic one. Next week, the main anti-realist positions are discussed; this week looks at the main argument for realism.

### The no-miracles argument

Hilary Putnam stated the idea in the 1970s: realism is the only philosophy of science that does not make the success of science a miracle. Writers since then, including Richard Boyd and Stathis Psillos, have developed it into an **inference to the best explanation** (IBE): we believe a hypothesis because it explains the data better than its rivals. The argument runs as follows.

| P1 | Our best current theories are very successful empirically: they make precise predictions, including predictions of novel phenomena, that turn out to be correct. |
| P2 | The best explanation of the empirical success of a theory is that it is approximately true. |
| P3 | If a hypothesis is the best explanation of a fact, then we are justified in believing that hypothesis. |
| C | So we are justified in believing that our best current theories are approximately true. |

The argument is valid. Its power depends on P1 being restricted to success of the right kind. A theory that merely accommodated known data could be successful through adjustment, which is why realists emphasize **novel predictions**, such as a correct prediction of a phenomenon not yet observed when the theory was formulated.

### Objections and the premises they attack

**Against P2: the selection explanation.** Bas van Fraassen argued in *The Scientific Image* (1980) that success is not a miracle that needs an explanation by truth. Theories compete, and those that do not succeed are abandoned, so the survivors are successful for the same reason that surviving organisms are well adapted: selection. The realist replies that this explains why we have successful theories now, but not why any particular theory is successful.

**Against P2: false but successful theories.** Larry Laudan (1981) listed theories that were successful and are now regarded as false, such as the caloric theory of heat and the ether theories of light. If truth is not needed for success, then truth is not the best explanation of it. Next week looks at this in more detail, as the pessimistic induction.

**Against P3: the status of IBE.** Van Fraassen also questioned whether being the best of the available explanations is a mark of truth: the best may be "the best of a bad lot." Arthur Fine and others argued that the argument is **circular**, since it uses an inference to the best explanation to support the reliability of inference to the best explanation; realists reply that the circularity is of a benign kind, since the argument uses a rule and does not assume its reliability as a premise.

**Against the argument as a whole: the base-rate objection.** Colin Howson and, later, P. D. Magnus and Craig Callender argued that the no-miracles argument ignores the base rate of true theories. Success is likely if a theory is true, but it can also be likely for false theories, and how much the success confirms truth depends on how many theories were approximately true to begin with. This objection attacks P2: if most theories in a domain are false, then the best explanation of the success of a theory may still be that it is false but lucky or tuned to the data. The Bayesian calculation in the example shows the point.

@example
title: Reconstructing the argument from a case
problem: In a certain field, a theory was formulated before an instrument existed that could test it. When the instrument was built, it recorded exactly the signal that the theory predicted for a quantity that had not been measured before. A realist concludes that the theory is approximately true. Put the argument in standard form and say which premise a selection explanation attacks.
step: P1: The theory is empirically successful: it made a novel prediction that was confirmed. This is the success that needs explaining.
step: P2: The best explanation of this kind of success is that the theory is approximately true. P3: If a hypothesis is the best explanation of a fact, we are justified in believing it. C: So we are justified in believing that the theory is approximately true.
step: The selection explanation says that the success is explained by the fact that unsuccessful theories are abandoned, so that truth is not the best explanation. That denies P2.
step: Note that the argument is valid, so every reply is aimed at a premise. A reply about the reliability of inference to the best explanation targets P3.
answer: P1, P2, P3 and C as above; the selection explanation attacks P2, and doubts about IBE attack P3.

@example
title: The base-rate objection in numbers
problem: Suppose that in some domain 25% of proposed theories are approximately true. A true theory is successful with probability 0.9, and a false theory is successful with probability 0.3 (for example because it was tuned to the data). A theory is observed to be successful. What is the probability that it is approximately true?
step: Let $T$ be "the theory is approximately true" and $S$ be "the theory is successful." Then $P(T) = 0.25$, $P(S \mid T) = 0.9$ and $P(S \mid \neg T) = 0.3$.
step: By Bayes's theorem, $P(T \mid S) = (0.9 \times 0.25)/(0.9 \times 0.25 + 0.3 \times 0.75) = 0.225/0.45 = 0.5$.
step: Success confirms truth, since the probability rises from 0.25 to 0.5, but it does not make it probable that the theory is true. The answer depends on the base rate and on how likely false theories are to succeed.
step: The realist replies that this model understates the case for novel predictions, because for a false theory the probability of a successful novel prediction may be very small; the objector replies that this is the very thing in dispute.
answer: The probability is 0.5. How strongly success supports truth depends on the base rate of true theories and on how often false theories succeed.

@mistakes
- Saying that realism is the claim that scientific theories are true. The epistemic claim is that our best, mature theories are approximately true, and we have reason to believe so.
- Confusing the no-miracles argument with the pessimistic induction. The first is an argument for realism, the second is an argument against it.
- Treating the argument as invalid. Its conclusion follows from the premises, and the objections attack P2 or P3 or the argument's use of the base rate.
- Thinking that the argument shows that all successful theories are true. It claims approximate truth, for theories that are successful in the right way.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in one paragraph why the no-miracles argument is a case of inference to the best explanation, and why that makes the argument vulnerable to the charge of circularity. Give the realist's best reply.
hint: Consider what justifies the use of inference to the best explanation.
solution: The argument infers that theories are approximately true because that is the best explanation of their success, which is an inference to the best explanation. The charge of circularity is that realists defend this style of inference by pointing out that it produces true theories, and the claim that it produces true theories is itself reached by this style of inference, so the argument seems to assume what it is meant to establish. The realist's best reply is that the circularity is rule-circularity and not premise-circularity: the argument uses the rule but does not assume as a premise that the rule is reliable, and an anti-realist who relies on other rules, such as induction, is in the same position. The objector can reply that the argument then cannot persuade anyone who doubts the rule.
