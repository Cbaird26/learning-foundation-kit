@article
A geologist is fairly sure that a rock sample is not from a certain ancient formation. A test comes back positive, but the test sometimes gives positive results for samples from other formations. How much should the geologist's confidence change? **Bayesian confirmation theory** answers with the probability calculus. It treats a scientist's confidence as a **degree of belief** or **credence** between 0 and 1, and holds that rational credences obey the axioms of probability and change by **conditionalization**: on learning evidence $E$ (and nothing stronger), the new credence in $H$ is the old credence in $H$ given $E$.

### Bayes's theorem

The conditional probability of $H$ given $E$ is $P(H \mid E) = P(H \wedge E)/P(E)$ when $P(E) > 0$. Bayes's theorem, which follows from this definition and the axioms, says:

$$P(H \mid E) = \frac{P(E \mid H)\,P(H)}{P(E \mid H)\,P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)\,P(\neg H)}.$$

$P(H)$ is the **prior**, $P(H \mid E)$ the **posterior**, and $P(E \mid H)$ and $P(E \mid \neg H)$ are the **likelihoods**. An equivalent form uses odds: the posterior odds equal the prior odds times the **likelihood ratio** $P(E \mid H)/P(E \mid \neg H)$. For several pieces of evidence that are independent given $H$ and given $\neg H$, the likelihood ratios multiply.

### What confirmation means

The Bayesian definition is **incremental**: $E$ **confirms** $H$ when $P(H \mid E) > P(H)$, **disconfirms** it when $P(H \mid E) < P(H)$ and is **irrelevant** to it when they are equal. When $0 < P(H) < 1$ and $P(E) > 0$, evidence confirms $H$ exactly when $P(E \mid H) > P(E \mid \neg H)$. Confirmation is not the same as a high posterior: a rare hypothesis can be confirmed and still be improbable. This sorts out some earlier puzzles. Hempel's white shoe confirms "All ravens are black" only if it raises its probability, and in plausible models it raises it by a very small amount, since non-black non-ravens are so common; a black raven raises it more. So the Bayesian accepts the conclusion of the raven argument while explaining why it feels paradoxical.

### Why believe the probability axioms?

The best-known argument is the **Dutch book argument**, due to Frank Ramsey and Bruno de Finetti. Treat an agent's credence in $A$ as the price at which she regards a bet paying one unit if $A$ as fair.

| P1 | If an agent's credences violate the probability axioms, then there is a set of bets, each of which she regards as fair given her credences, that guarantees her a net loss. |
| P2 | Any agent who regards as fair a set of bets that guarantees a net loss is irrational. |
| C | So a rational agent's credences obey the probability axioms. |

The argument is valid: by P1 and P2, every agent whose credences violate the axioms is irrational. Objections attack the premises. One says that P1 requires a close link between credence and betting prices, which fails for agents who are risk averse or who do not bet; this attacks the betting assumption built into P1. Another says that P2 confuses a pragmatic defect (losing money) with an epistemic one, since one can avoid the loss by declining the bets; accuracy-based arguments try to replace it with a claim about expected closeness to the truth.

### Problems for Bayesianism

The **problem of priors**: Bayes's theorem needs prior probabilities, and where they come from is disputed. **Subjective Bayesians** allow any coherent prior, and appeal to the **washing out** of priors, the fact that under suitable conditions agents who start with different priors but share likelihoods tend to converge as evidence accumulates. **Objective Bayesians** add constraints, such as principles of indifference, which have their own difficulties. The **problem of old evidence**, raised by Clark Glymour in 1980, is this: Einstein knew the anomalous advance of Mercury's perihelion before he had general relativity, so $P(E) = 1$ for him, and then $P(H \mid E) = P(H)$, which implies that the known fact gave no confirmation to the theory. Yet the explanation of Mercury's orbit is regarded as strong support. Replies include evaluating the probabilities as if one did not know $E$, or modeling the learning of the logical fact that $H$ entails $E$.

@example
title: One update by Bayes's theorem
problem: A lab suspects with credence 0.10 that a powder is compound K. A test reads positive with probability 0.9 if it is K and with probability 0.2 if it is not. The test is positive. What is the posterior probability that it is K, and does the result confirm the hypothesis?
step: Write $H$ for "the powder is K" and $E$ for "the test is positive." The given values are $P(H) = 0.10$, $P(E \mid H) = 0.9$ and $P(E \mid \neg H) = 0.2$.
step: The numerator is $0.9 \times 0.10 = 0.09$. The second term of the denominator is $0.2 \times 0.90 = 0.18$.
step: So $P(H \mid E) = 0.09/(0.09 + 0.18) = 1/3$, about 0.333.
step: Since $0.333 > 0.10$, $E$ confirms $H$. The posterior is still below one half, because the prior was low.
answer: The posterior is $1/3$. The result confirms the hypothesis, though the hypothesis is still probably false.

@example
title: The raven paradox in a small model
problem: Suppose a universe has 10 ravens and 900 non-black non-ravens. Hypothesis $H$ says that all ravens are black. The rival $\neg H$ says that exactly one raven is non-black. Your prior for $H$ is 0.5. Compare two observations. (a) You pick a random raven and it is black. (b) You pick a random non-black object and it is not a raven (a white shoe). How much does each raise your credence in $H$?
step: (a) $P(E \mid H) = 1$ and $P(E \mid \neg H) = 9/10$. The posterior is $0.5/(0.5 + 0.5 \times 0.9) = 1/1.9 \approx 0.526$.
step: (b) If $H$ is true, every non-black object is a non-raven, so $P(E \mid H) = 1$. If $\neg H$ is true, there are 901 non-black objects, of which 900 are non-ravens, so $P(E \mid \neg H) = 900/901$. The posterior is $901/1801 \approx 0.50028$.
step: Both observations confirm $H$, since both posteriors exceed 0.5, but the raven raises the credence by about 0.026 and the shoe by about 0.0003. The shoe confirms, to a tiny degree.
answer: Both confirm; the black raven raises the credence from 0.5 to about 0.526, and the white shoe to about 0.5003.

@mistakes
- Treating $P(E \mid H)$ as $P(H \mid E)$ (the fallacy of the transposed conditional), which usually goes with ignoring the base rate. A positive result from an accurate test can still leave a rare hypothesis improbable.
- Saying that confirmation means a high posterior. Incremental confirmation only requires that the probability goes up.
- Multiplying likelihood ratios when the pieces of evidence are not independent given the hypotheses.
- Thinking that Bayes's theorem settles the priors. It only shows how credences should change given the priors and likelihoods.

prompt: State the problem of old evidence in two or three sentences and give one reply.
hint: Say what happens to $P(H \mid E)$ when $P(E) = 1$.
solution: If a scientist already knows $E$, then $P(E) = 1$, and then $P(H \mid E) = P(H)$, so on the incremental account $E$ does not confirm $H$. @quiz self
prompt: A critic says that Bayesian confirmation is "subjective, because it depends on arbitrary priors." In one paragraph, state the objection fairly and give the best Bayesian reply, noting what the reply does not show.
hint: Think about washing out of priors, and about when it applies.
solution: The objection is that Bayes's theorem needs a prior, the axioms allow any coherent prior, and two scientists with different priors can reach different posteriors on the same evidence, so the method does not yet yield objective conclusions. The best reply is that, under suitable conditions (shared likelihoods, priors that do not assign zero to the truth), differing priors tend to converge as evidence accumulates, so with enough evidence the disagreement shrinks. The reply does not show that the priors are rational or that agents converge quickly with limited evidence, and it does not help when the priors assign zero to the true hypothesis or the agents disagree about the likelihoods.
rubric: States the objection that priors are not fixed by the axioms and can lead to different posteriors.
rubric: Gives the washing-out reply and states a condition for it, such as shared likelihoods or non-zero priors.
rubric: Notes a limit of the reply.
