@article
Every day of your life the sun has risen, and you expect it to rise tomorrow. You also expect the next piece of bread you eat to nourish you, and the next flame to be hot. Science is full of inferences of this sort: from the observed to the unobserved, from some cases to all. These are **inductive** inferences, and their conclusions are not guaranteed by their premises. This week asks two questions. Can such inferences be justified at all? And when does an observation **confirm** a hypothesis?

### Hume's problem of induction

David Hume raised the problem in the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1.3.6) and the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (section IV). The argument is usually put as follows. Let the **uniformity principle** (UP) be the claim that unobserved cases will resemble observed cases.

| P1 | An inductive inference is justified only if the uniformity principle is justified. |
| P2 | The uniformity principle can be justified only by a deductive argument or by an inductive argument. |
| P3 | No deductive argument justifies the uniformity principle, since its denial is not a contradiction. |
| P4 | No inductive argument justifies the uniformity principle, because any such argument is circular: it assumes the principle. |
| C1 | So the uniformity principle is not justified. |
| C | So no inductive inference is justified. |

The argument is valid: P2, P3 and P4 give C1, and P1 gives C. Replies map onto the premises. Deny P1: Peter Strawson argued that asking whether induction is justified is like asking whether the law is legal, because being reasonable in matters of fact just is, in part, proportioning belief to inductive evidence. Deny P4: some philosophers say that an inductive justification of induction is circular only in a harmless, rule-circular way, since the argument uses the rule but need not assume that the rule is reliable as a premise. Accept the conclusion and look for a different sort of vindication: Hans Reichenbach argued pragmatically that if any method can predict successfully, induction will eventually find the pattern, so there is nothing to lose by using it. Karl Popper accepted the conclusion and denied that science relies on induction.

### Confirmation and Hempel's paradox

What is it for evidence to **confirm** a hypothesis? An attractive idea, associated with Jean Nicod, is that a **positive instance** confirms a generalization: a black raven confirms "All ravens are black." Carl Hempel, in "Studies in the Logic of Confirmation" (1945), showed that this idea, with a second plausible principle, leads to a paradox.

| P1 | (Nicod's criterion) An object that is $F$ and $G$ confirms "All $F$ are $G$." |
| P2 | (Equivalence condition) Whatever confirms a hypothesis confirms every hypothesis logically equivalent to it. |
| P3 | "All non-black things are non-ravens" is logically equivalent to "All ravens are black." |
| P4 | A white shoe is a non-black thing that is a non-raven. |
| C | So a white shoe confirms "All ravens are black." |

P1 applied to the object and the hypothesis "All non-black things are non-ravens" shows that the shoe confirms that hypothesis, and P2 and P3 transfer the support. The argument is valid, and its conclusion is strange: you seem to be able to investigate ravens by looking at shoes without leaving your room. The responses are these. **Accept the conclusion**: Hempel held that the sense of paradox arises because we import background knowledge, such as that there are far fewer ravens than non-black things. **Deny P1**: some philosophers say that positive instances confirm only relative to background assumptions. **Deny P2 or P3**: the equivalence condition is rarely denied, and P3 is a theorem of classical logic. Bayesian confirmation theory, the topic of next week, accepts the conclusion but says that the shoe confirms the hypothesis very little.

### Goodman's new riddle

Nelson Goodman, in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast* (1955), argued that confirmation is not a purely formal matter. Define "grue": an object is grue if it is examined before a time $t$ and is green, or is not examined before $t$ and is blue. All emeralds examined before $t$ are green, and therefore also grue. So the evidence seems equally to support "All emeralds are green" and "All emeralds are grue," which tell us different things about emeralds examined after $t$. The hypotheses are **projectible** or not, and Goodman's own proposal appeals to the **entrenchment** of predicates in past use. Others say that grue is a gerrymandered predicate and that naturalness matters. No consensus exists, and this is why the problem is called "new": it concerns which generalizations are the lawlike ones that evidence can confirm, not whether induction is justified.

@example
title: Deriving the raven paradox for a new hypothesis
problem: Apply Hempel's argument to the hypothesis "All glass vessels are fragile." What object confirms it by the combination of Nicod's criterion and the equivalence condition, though it is neither glass nor a vessel?
step: Find the logically equivalent contrapositive: "All non-fragile things are non-glass-vessels."
step: By Nicod's criterion, an object that is non-fragile and is not a glass vessel confirms the contrapositive. A steel hammer, which is not fragile and is not a glass vessel, qualifies.
step: By the equivalence condition, whatever confirms the contrapositive confirms "All glass vessels are fragile," so the steel hammer confirms it.
step: Say which premise a reply would deny. A reply that a hammer is irrelevant evidence denies Nicod's criterion (P1) as stated, not the equivalence condition.
answer: A steel hammer confirms the hypothesis on these two principles. The paradox comes from Nicod's criterion together with the equivalence condition.

@example
title: A grue-like predicate for new evidence
problem: Call a lump "bliss" if it is examined before the year 2200 and is soft, or is not examined before 2200 and is hard. All lumps of the substance Orlite examined so far are soft. Show that the evidence fits two generalizations that disagree about unexamined lumps.
step: Each examined lump is soft, so "All Orlite lumps are soft" has only positive instances.
step: Each examined lump was also examined before 2200 and soft, so it is bliss. So "All Orlite lumps are bliss" has only positive instances too.
step: For lumps not examined before 2200, the first hypothesis says soft and the second says hard. They give conflicting predictions.
step: So the formal rule that positive instances confirm cannot choose between them. Goodman's question is what distinguishes lawlike (projectible) generalizations like the first from the second.
answer: The same evidence fits "all soft" and "all bliss", which disagree about unexamined lumps; a theory of confirmation must say why the first is projectible.

@mistakes
- Confusing the problem of induction with the claim that induction usually gives false conclusions. The question is whether it can be given a non-circular justification.
- Calling Hempel's argument invalid. It is valid; the replies deny Nicod's criterion or accept the conclusion.
- Saying that Goodman's riddle is solved by noting that "grue" mentions a time. Goodman pointed out that "green" can itself be defined from "grue" and "bleen" using the same time, so a mention of time alone does not show that a predicate is unnatural.
- Treating the problem of induction as saying that inductive inferences are invalid. They are not deductively valid, which is not the same as being unreasonable.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose any two of the following and explain in a paragraph how they differ: the old problem of induction (Hume), the raven paradox (Hempel) and the new riddle of induction (Goodman). Say what each asks and what kind of answer would count as solving it.
hint: Ask whether each problem is about justification, about the logic of confirmation or about which predicates to project.
solution: Hume's problem asks whether inductive inference can be justified without circularity; a solution would be a non-circular justification or a good argument that none is needed. Hempel's paradox asks about the logic of confirmation: from two plausible principles it follows that a white shoe confirms the hypothesis that all ravens are black, so a solution would either accept the conclusion with an explanation of why it seems odd, or give up one of the principles. Goodman's riddle asks which generalizations can be confirmed by their instances; a solution would be a principled distinction between projectible and non-projectible predicates. They differ because Hume's problem concerns the legitimacy of the whole practice, Hempel's the structure of confirmation and Goodman's the content of the predicates.
