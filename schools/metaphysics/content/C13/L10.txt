@article
Suppose a finite body of data, such as the positions of a planet on twelve nights. Many different mathematical curves pass through all the points. Does something similar hold for theories and evidence, however much evidence we collect? If it does, then evidence alone could never tell us which theory to believe, and the realist's confidence about unobservable things would need more support.

### Underdetermination

The thesis that theories are **underdetermined by evidence** comes in several forms. **Holist** underdetermination follows from the Duhem-Quine point of Lesson 2: since a theory meets observation only with auxiliary assumptions, when a prediction fails one can retain the theory by revising the auxiliaries. **Contrastive** underdetermination says that for any theory there are rival theories that are equally well supported by all possible evidence. Two theories are **empirically equivalent** if they have the same observable consequences. A standard candidate is the pair of Newtonian mechanics with a state of absolute rest and the same mechanics with no such state, since all observable consequences are the same under uniform motion. Another is a set of rival interpretations of quantum mechanics, which agree on the observable predictions that have so far been tested, though which pairs are strictly empirically equivalent is itself disputed.

The argument for an anti-realist conclusion is:

| P1 | For any theory about unobservable things, there are incompatible rival theories that are empirically equivalent to it. |
| P2 | If two theories are empirically equivalent, then the evidence provides no reason to believe one rather than the other. |
| P3 | If the evidence provides no reason to believe one of two incompatible theories rather than the other, then belief in either one is not justified by the evidence. |
| C | So belief in any theory about unobservable things, beyond its observable consequences, is not justified by the evidence. |

The argument is valid. The replies:

**Against P1.** Larry Laudan and Jarrett Leplin (1991) argued that empirical equivalence is relative to what auxiliary assumptions and instruments we have, and so it can change, and that two theories with the same observable consequences today may differ tomorrow. Realists add that the claim is shown only for toy cases, not for real theories. This also reminds us that the thesis needs "empirically equivalent" to be defined with care.

**Against P2.** Realists say that evidence is not the only source of reasons: simplicity, explanatory power, fit with other theories and unification can be **epistemic virtues**, signs of truth, and not merely practical conveniences. Van Fraassen replies that virtues of this kind are **pragmatic**, relevant to our use of a theory, and give no reason to think it is true. The two sides disagree about whether the virtues are connected with truth.

**Against P3.** Some say that the support for a theory includes more than its empirical consequences, such as its coherence with background beliefs, and that to count this as "evidence" is the issue. This reply, like the last one, depends on how narrowly "evidence" is understood.

### Structural realism

John Worrall, in 1989, proposed **structural realism** as a way of keeping the best of the realist argument from success and the anti-realist argument from theory change. The core observation is that when Maxwell's electromagnetic theory replaced Fresnel's theory of light as vibrations in an elastic ether, Fresnel's equations for the intensity of reflected and refracted light were retained. What continues across theory change is mathematical **structure**, the relations expressed by the equations, and not the picture of what the underlying things are. On **epistemic structural realism**, we can know the structure of the world but not the intrinsic nature of the things that instantiate it. On **ontic structural realism**, advocated in different forms by James Ladyman and by Ladyman and Don Ross, the world is structure: there are no objects with intrinsic natures behind the structure. This is motivated by the physics of identical particles and by the philosophy of quantum theory.

The best-known objection is **Newman's problem**. In 1928 M. H. A. Newman objected to Russell's view that we know only the structure of the world, and in 1985 Michael Demopoulos and Michael Friedman revived the objection; it was later pressed against Worrall's structural realism. Any collection of objects of the right number can be given any structure with that number of elements: we can define a relation on them that fits. So the claim that the unobserved world has a given structure says no more than that it has a certain number of objects, unless the relation is required to be a particular, natural one. Structural realists reply that the relations are not arbitrary but are those that figure in laws, and the objection then becomes the question what makes a relation natural.

@example
title: Underdetermination by finite data
problem: Three observations record that a quantity $y$ is 2, 4 and 6 when $x$ is 1, 2 and 3. Theory $A$ says $y = 2x$. Theory $B$ says $y = 2x + (x-1)(x-2)(x-3)$. Show that both fit the data, and say what the case does and does not show about the underdetermination thesis.
step: Theory $A$ gives 2, 4 and 6 at $x = 1, 2, 3$, so it fits.
step: In theory $B$ the added product is zero at each of $x = 1, 2, 3$, so $B$ gives the same values 2, 4 and 6 and also fits.
step: They disagree at $x = 4$: $A$ gives 8, and $B$ gives $8 + 3 \times 2 \times 1 = 14$. A new observation at $x = 4$ would discriminate between them.
step: So finite data underdetermine theory, but the observation at $x = 4$ shows that these two are not empirically equivalent for all possible evidence. The contrastive thesis needs rivals that agree on all possible observations, which is much stronger.
answer: Both theories fit the three data points but differ at $x = 4$. This shows underdetermination by present data and not empirical equivalence for all data.

@example
title: Newman's problem in a small case
problem: A structural realist says that three unobserved things stand in a strict linear order: $a$ before $b$, $b$ before $c$, and $a$ before $c$. Show how Newman's objection makes this claim cheap.
step: Take any three objects, say three stones $s_1, s_2, s_3$.
step: Define a relation $R$ by $R = \{(s_1, s_2), (s_2, s_3), (s_1, s_3)\}$. This relation is a strict linear order on the stones.
step: So the claim that there are three things with a relation that is a strict linear order is true of any three objects, since such a relation can always be defined.
step: The claim says more only if the relation is required to be a special one, for example the actual causal relation. That raises the question of what makes a relation the one that the theory refers to.
answer: The structural claim holds of any three objects. It has content only if the relation is restricted to a natural relation, and structuralists must say which.

@mistakes
- Treating underdetermination as the claim that no evidence bears on theories. It concerns whether the evidence picks out one theory from among rivals.
- Confusing holist with contrastive underdetermination. The first concerns the role of auxiliary assumptions; the second concerns rival theories that agree on all possible observations.
- Saying that structural realists deny that there is an external world. They hold that what we know of it, or what it consists in, is structure.
- Thinking that epistemic and ontic structural realism are the same. The first is about what we can know; the second is about what there is.

@quiz self
prompt: In one paragraph, explain Newman's problem for structural realism and give one way a structural realist might reply.
hint: Use the fact that a set of objects of the right size can be given any structure of that size.
solution: Newman's problem is that if all we know about unobserved things is that they instantiate a certain structure, then this knowledge is trivial, because any collection of objects with the right number of members instantiates every structure of that size, by a suitable choice of relation. So the structural claim reduces to a claim about how many things there are. A structural realist can reply that the relation is not arbitrary but is the one that figures in the laws of the theory, so the claim says that things stand in that relation and not just some relation. The objection then moves to what makes a relation natural or law-governed, and whether specifying it requires more than structure.
