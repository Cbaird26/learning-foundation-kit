@article
Two people claim to predict the future. One, an astronomer, says a comet will return on a stated date. The other, a horoscope writer, says that the coming month will "bring change to those who are open to it." If the comet returns, the astronomer's theory gains credit; if it does not, the astronomer has a problem. Whatever happens, the horoscope writer was right. Most of us feel that there is a difference in kind between the two, and the **demarcation problem** asks what it is: what distinguishes science from non-science and, in particular, from **pseudoscience**, which presents itself as science without earning the title?

### Why it matters

The question is not an academic game. Courts, school boards, funding bodies and medical regulators have to decide what counts as scientific evidence or science education. A good answer would also tell us something about why science has the authority it has. Notice, though, that there are two different questions in the neighborhood. One asks which **statements** are meaningful or factual at all. The other asks which **theories and practices** are scientific. A theory can be unscientific and perfectly meaningful: the claim that there are 14 doors in a particular old castle is a meaningful claim, though no science studies it.

### The positivist answer: verifiability

The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle and their followers, such as A. J. Ayer in *Language, Truth and Logic* (1936), proposed a **verification principle** as a criterion of cognitive meaning: a declarative sentence is meaningful only if it is analytic (true or false by meaning alone, as in logic and mathematics) or empirically verifiable. Statements of metaphysics, they held, are neither, so they are not false but meaningless. The principle was meant to separate science from metaphysics, not science from pseudoscience, and it ran into two famous problems.

First, a strong version demanding conclusive verification excludes scientific laws: "all electrons have the same charge" cannot be conclusively verified by any finite set of observations. A weaker version demanding only that observations be relevant is too liberal. Ayer's first formulation, which he revised in the introduction to the 1946 second edition, let in nonsense. Take the invented sentence "The Hollow is sleepy" and add the auxiliary premise "if the Hollow is sleepy, then the kettle is cold." Together they entail "the kettle is cold," which is observable and does not follow from the auxiliary alone, so the sentence counts as meaningful.

Second, the principle seems to refute itself. The argument can be set out in standard form:

| P1 | Every declarative sentence that is meaningful is either analytic or empirically verifiable. |
| P2 | The verification principle (the sentence in P1) is a declarative sentence that is neither analytic nor empirically verifiable. |
| C | So the verification principle is not meaningful. |

The argument is valid: P1 is a universal claim about all declarative sentences, and P2 says that the principle is a declarative sentence outside both categories. Replies attack P2. One says that the principle is not a declarative claim at all but a proposal for how to use the word "meaningful." Another says that it is analytic, a definition of "meaningful." Each reply has a price: a proposal needs reasons to be adopted, and a definition that is stipulated loses its force against metaphysics.

### Popper's answer and the doubts about any single criterion

Karl Popper, in *Logik der Forschung* (1934), proposed **falsifiability** as the mark of science, and, importantly, as a demarcation of science from non-science that is **not** a criterion of meaning: he thought unfalsifiable statements, including metaphysical ones, can be meaningful and even fruitful. Next week examines his view in detail.

Larry Laudan, in a 1983 paper whose title announces "the demise of the demarcation problem," argued that the search for a single criterion should be abandoned. The argument is:

| P1 | If there is a demarcation criterion, then there is a set of conditions that are individually necessary and jointly sufficient for being scientific. |
| P2 | There is no such set of conditions: any proposed set misclassifies some clear case of science or of pseudoscience. |
| C | So there is no demarcation criterion. |

Defenders of demarcation deny P1. A **cluster** or **multi-criterial** approach holds that being scientific is a matter of having enough of a range of marks (testability, progress over time, openness to criticism by a community, responsiveness to evidence) without any single mark being necessary. Paul Thagard's 1978 paper on astrology argued along these lines that pseudoscience is a matter of lack of progress combined with a community's neglect of alternatives and selective use of evidence. Others deny P2 by defending a particular criterion. Whichever you choose, the lesson is that "science" may be a family of practices with no sharp edge, and that demarcation may be a matter of degree.

@example
title: A weak verifiability test lets in nonsense
problem: A critic says that the sentence "The Wend is hungry" is meaningful on Ayer's first formulation of the criterion (a sentence is meaningful if, together with some auxiliary premises, it entails an observation sentence that the auxiliaries do not entail alone). Show how, and say what is wrong with the result.
step: Choose an auxiliary premise that links the sentence to something observable: "If the Wend is hungry, then the river is rising."
step: Together, the sentence and the auxiliary entail "the river is rising" by modus ponens.
step: The auxiliary alone does not entail "the river is rising." So the criterion counts the sentence as meaningful.
step: But the auxiliary was chosen freely, and the same trick works for any sentence at all. A criterion that classifies everything as meaningful no longer separates science from metaphysics, so it needs tightening, as Ayer himself saw.
answer: The sentence passes with the auxiliary "if the Wend is hungry, then the river is rising", but because such an auxiliary can be invented for any sentence, the criterion is too liberal.

@example
title: Which premise does the reply deny?
problem: Reconstruct the reply "the verification principle is a recommendation about how to use the word 'meaningful', not a claim" against the self-refutation argument, and say which premise it denies.
step: The argument has P1 (every meaningful declarative sentence is analytic or verifiable), P2 (the principle is a declarative sentence that is neither) and C (the principle is not meaningful).
step: If the principle is a proposal and not a declarative sentence, then P1, which concerns only declarative sentences, does not apply to it.
step: That means the reply denies the first conjunct of P2, that the principle is a declarative sentence. P1 stays as it is.
step: The argument stays valid; the reply shows that one premise is false.
answer: The reply denies P2. A fair follow-up objection asks what reasons there are to adopt the proposal.

@mistakes
- Treating Popper's falsifiability as a theory of meaning. Popper proposed it to mark off science, and held that unfalsifiable statements can be meaningful.
- Saying that the self-refutation argument is invalid. It is valid; the replies attack P2.
- Assuming that rejecting a single criterion shows there is no difference between science and pseudoscience. Laudan's conclusion concerns a criterion of a certain form, and cluster views keep the distinction.
- Running together "unscientific" and "meaningless": the 14-doors claim is meaningful and unscientific.
