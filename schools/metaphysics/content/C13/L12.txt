Should scientists' values, such as their ethical commitments or political hopes, have any role in their work? On one familiar view, science is at its best when it is **value-free**: evidence and logic alone decide what to believe, and values come in only afterward, when society decides what to do with the results. Much recent philosophy of science has questioned this ideal. It matters in practice, because science informs medicine, regulation and public policy.

### Kinds of values

Philosophers distinguish **epistemic values**, which are marks of a good theory and are thought to promote truth or understanding, such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness (the list that Kuhn offered in Lesson 6), from **non-epistemic** (ethical, social, political) values, such as concern for human welfare. Almost no one doubts that non-epistemic values properly influence which questions science investigates and which methods are ethically allowed. The controversy is about whether they have a legitimate role inside the **justification** of hypotheses, in deciding whether the evidence is good enough.

### The argument from inductive risk

Richard Rudner argued in 1953 that scientists qua scientists make value judgments, and Heather Douglas has developed the argument in recent decades. Evidence never entails a hypothesis, so accepting one always risks error. The argument:

| P1 | Whenever scientists accept or reject a hypothesis on the basis of evidence, there is some risk of error. |
| P2 | How strong the evidence must be before a hypothesis is accepted properly depends on how serious the consequences of an error would be. |
| P3 | Judging how serious the consequences of an error would be is a value judgment of an ethical or social kind. |
| C | So scientists' acceptance of hypotheses properly involves non-epistemic value judgments. |

The argument is valid. Two replies are standard. Richard Jeffrey argued in 1956 that scientists need not accept or reject hypotheses at all: their job is to assign probabilities, and those who use the results can apply their own values. This challenges P1's presupposition that acceptance is part of the scientist's role, and the C follows only for scientists who accept or reject. Defenders of inductive risk answer that deciding how to state probabilities, which data count as reliable and how to classify borderline results also carries risk. Isaac Levi argued in 1960 that the standards for acceptance are set by the epistemic values of science, and not by ethical values, so that how strong the evidence must be does not depend on the seriousness of practical consequences. This denies P2. Douglas replies that the epistemic values do not settle how much risk is tolerable in a case, because the consequences of errors differ with the use of the claim.

### Objectivity as a social achievement

If values play a role, is science still **objective**? Helen Longino, in *Science as Social Knowledge* (1990), argued that objectivity is not the absence of values in individual scientists, which cannot be guaranteed, but a feature of a community whose practices expose background assumptions to criticism. Her conditions for **transformative criticism** include recognized venues for criticism, uptake of criticism by the community, shared public standards and a rough equality of intellectual authority among its members. This is a view called **contextual empiricism**. Earlier, Robert Merton described norms of science, including universalism (claims are judged by impersonal criteria), organized skepticism and disinterestedness. Feminist and standpoint philosophers of science, such as Sandra Harding, argued that including diverse perspectives can improve objectivity by revealing assumptions that a homogeneous community does not see. Naomi Oreskes has argued that trust in science rests on its social processes of vetting and criticism. These are debated views, and critics ask whether diversity in itself improves reliability, and how to identify which values are illegitimate.

### Replication and statistical reliability

A concern in some fields is that a large share of published findings do not replicate. In 2015 the Open Science Collaboration attempted to replicate 100 psychology studies and found that a minority of the replications (about 36%) were statistically significant, though 97% of the original studies had reported significant results. In 2005 John Ioannidis argued that, given low prior probabilities of tested hypotheses, modest statistical power and a conventional significance level, a substantial fraction of significant findings can be false. The calculation is Bayesian, as in Lesson 4: if $\pi$ is the prior probability that a tested hypothesis is true, $1-\beta$ the power and $\alpha$ the significance level, the probability that a significant result is true is $\pi(1-\beta)/(\pi(1-\beta) + (1-\pi)\alpha)$. The remedies, such as preregistration, larger samples and replication studies, are partly institutional, which links the technical problem to Longino's social picture of objectivity.

@example
title: Inductive risk in a threshold decision
problem: A lab must decide whether the evidence is strong enough to declare a food additive harmful to the public. Explain how inductive risk arises and which premise of Rudner's argument is at stake if a critic says that the lab should only report the evidence.
step: Evidence of harm will be uncertain whatever the lab does, so declaring the additive harmful risks a false alarm (the additive is safe), and declining to declare it risks a missed hazard (it is harmful). This is P1.
step: Where to put the threshold depends on how bad each error would be, for example the cost to producers of a false alarm and the harm to consumers of a missed hazard. This is P2.
step: Weighing those costs is an ethical and social judgment, not a purely epistemic one. This is P3, and C follows: deciding whether to declare the additive harmful involves a value judgment.
step: The critic who says that the lab should only report probabilities is following Jeffrey. That avoids the value judgment inside the lab, but the lab still has to decide how to measure and report the evidence, and the public must decide what to do, so the value judgment is moved and not removed.
answer: P1-P3 and C as in the argument; the critic's reply is Jeffrey's, which challenges the scope of P1 by denying that the scientist should accept or reject.

@example
title: How likely is a significant result to be true?
problem: Suppose that 20% of the hypotheses a field tests are true, studies have power 0.5 (a true effect is detected with probability 0.5), and the significance level is 0.05 (a false hypothesis yields a significant result with probability 0.05). A study reports a significant result. What is the probability that the hypothesis is true?
step: Let $\pi = 0.2$, power $= 0.5$ and $\alpha = 0.05$. The probability of a significant result for a true hypothesis is 0.5, and for a false one 0.05.
step: By Bayes's theorem, $P(\text{true} \mid \text{significant}) = (0.2 \times 0.5)/(0.2 \times 0.5 + 0.8 \times 0.05) = 0.10/0.14 \approx 0.714$.
step: So about 71% of significant results in this field would be true. A smaller prior or lower power would lower the figure, which is why the prior and the power matter as much as the significance level.
answer: The probability is about 0.714.

@mistakes
- Saying that the inductive-risk argument shows that science is subjective. It claims that non-epistemic values properly enter decisions about the sufficiency of evidence, not that evidence is irrelevant.
- Confusing epistemic and non-epistemic values. Simplicity and scope are epistemic values; concern for public welfare is not.
- Treating a significance level of 0.05 as the probability that a significant result is false. The probability depends on the prior and the power as well.
- Saying that Longino's view makes objectivity depend on each scientist being unbiased. It locates objectivity in the community's critical practices.
