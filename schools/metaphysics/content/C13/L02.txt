@article
Suppose a theory says that every sample of a certain alloy conducts electricity. Ten thousand conducting samples do not prove it, because the next sample might fail to conduct. But one non-conducting sample shows that it is false. Popper built a philosophy of science on this **logical asymmetry**. Universal claims cannot be conclusively verified by finitely many observations, but they can be conclusively contradicted by one. The inference is a plain case of modus tollens: if the theory says that every sample conducts, and this sample does not conduct, then the theory is false.

### Popper's view

Karl Popper, in *Logik der Forschung* (1934; English version *The Logic of Scientific Discovery*, 1959) and later in *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963), drew three consequences.

First, a theory is scientific only if it is **falsifiable**: it forbids some possible observable outcomes, so that there are conceivable observations, called **potential falsifiers**, that would contradict it. The more a theory forbids, the more **empirical content** it has and the more falsifiable it is. A tautology such as "it will rain or it will not" forbids nothing and says nothing about the world.

Second, science proceeds by **conjectures and refutations**: bold theories are proposed and then subjected to severe tests. Popper rejected the idea that induction justifies theories. A theory that survives severe tests is not made probable; it is **corroborated**, which reports its past performance under test and is not a measure of its likelihood of truth.

Third, Popper was concerned with how theories can be shielded from refutation. He contrasted Einstein's general relativity, which risked refutation by predicting the bending of starlight by the Sun (tested in the solar eclipse expeditions of 1919), with theories such as Marx's theory of history, Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology, which, he held, could interpret any outcome as confirmation. He also criticized **ad hoc** rescues, such as adding an assumption that has no independent test and serves only to save a theory from a failed prediction. His methodological rule is to prefer rescues that increase testability and to forbid those that reduce it.

### What falsification cannot do alone

A pure falsificationism has a problem identified by Pierre Duhem and later generalized by W. V. O. Quine. Theories do not meet observation alone. A prediction comes from a theory together with auxiliary assumptions about instruments, initial conditions and background theory. The argument:

| P1 | In every real test, the predicted observation O follows from the theory T together with auxiliary assumptions A, but not from T alone. |
| P2 | If O follows from T together with A but not from T alone, then the falsity of O logically refutes only the conjunction of T and A, not T by itself. |
| C | So no failed prediction logically refutes T by itself. |

The argument is valid. It matters because the history of science contains cases in which a failed prediction was blamed on an auxiliary assumption, with different results. When the observed orbit of Uranus departed from the prediction of Newtonian mechanics, Le Verrier and Adams each proposed that an unseen planet was pulling on it, and in 1846 Galle found Neptune close to the predicted position. When the orbit of Mercury was found to advance more than Newtonian calculation allowed, Le Verrier proposed another unseen planet, nicknamed Vulcan, and searches never found it; the anomaly was explained in 1915 by Einstein's general relativity, which changed the theory. The same logical situation had different outcomes, and the logic of refutation alone does not say which way to go.

Popper accepted C. He never held that falsification is conclusive. In the *Logic of Scientific Discovery* he said that no theory can be conclusively refuted, because one can always doubt the observation or the auxiliaries. His reply is that falsifiability is a **methodological** rule: scientists adopt decisions about which statements to accept as basic and which to blame, and they ought to avoid blame-shifting that makes theories less testable. Critics ask whether a rule of this kind can capture the actual practice of science, and whether "corroboration" can do without a covert use of induction: if past survival gives a reason to rely on a theory in the future, that looks like induction. Popper's position remains a main reference point for the questions that follow.

@example
title: Same logic, different outcomes
problem: Apply the Duhem-Quine argument to the Uranus and Mercury cases. What is T, what is A, and what does this show about what the failed prediction demands?
step: In each case, T is Newtonian mechanics and gravitation. A includes the claim that the only bodies significantly affecting the orbit are the known planets, plus claims about the data and calculations.
step: The prediction O is the planet's orbit. In each case the observed orbit differed from O, so by modus tollens it is false that T and A are both true.
step: In the Uranus case, giving up the assumption about the number of planets (adding Neptune) saved T and yielded a new prediction that was confirmed. In the Mercury case, adding Vulcan failed its own test, and later the theory T was replaced.
step: So the logic shows that something in T and A is false, but not which part; further tests and alternative theories decided.
answer: T is Newtonian mechanics; A includes the claim that no further bodies matter. The failed prediction shows that T and A are not both true, and only further testing decides which to revise.

@example
title: Finding a potential falsifier
problem: For each claim, say whether it is falsifiable in Popper's sense and, if it is, give a potential falsifier: (a) "Every sample of this resin melts below 120 degrees Celsius." (b) "Somewhere in the universe there is a planet with seas of liquid methane." (c) "Either this resin melts below 120 degrees or it does not."
step: (a) It is a universal claim. One sample that did not melt below 120 degrees, tested correctly, would contradict it. So it is falsifiable.
step: (b) It is purely existential. No finite set of observations of planets without such seas contradicts it, since the planet might be somewhere unexamined. Finding such a planet would verify it. So it is not falsifiable, although it is meaningful and could be verified.
step: (c) It is a tautology and is compatible with every possible observation, so it has no potential falsifier.
answer: (a) falsifiable, with a non-melting sample as potential falsifier; (b) not falsifiable though verifiable; (c) not falsifiable.

@mistakes
- Saying that Popper held falsification to be conclusive. He accepted that tests rely on fallible auxiliary assumptions and basic statements, and made falsifiability a methodological rule.
- Taking "corroborated" to mean "probably true". For Popper corroboration reports a theory's record under severe tests and is not a measure of probability.
- Thinking that every unfalsifiable statement is meaningless. Purely existential claims and metaphysical claims may be meaningful.
- Reading the Duhem-Quine argument as showing that no evidence ever bears on a theory. It shows that a failed prediction does not by itself indicate which element to give up.

prompt: A botanist's theory says that seeds of the species Ilex nova sprout within ten days in any soil kept above 15 degrees Celsius. In a trial, none sprout in ten days. Using the Duhem-Quine argument, list two auxiliary assumptions the botanist might blame, and explain how Popper would judge a decision to blame an auxiliary assumption.
hint: Think about what had to be true about the soil, the thermometer and the seeds for the prediction to follow.
solution: The prediction follows from the theory together with auxiliary assumptions, for example that the thermometer was accurate, that the seeds were viable and had not been damaged in storage, and that the soil was kept above 15 degrees throughout. The failure shows only that the theory and these assumptions are not all true. Popper would not say that blaming an auxiliary is always illegitimate. Blaming a testable auxiliary, such as seed viability, is acceptable because it can be checked and yields a new prediction (a viable batch should sprout). Blaming something untestable, such as an "unobservable soil inhibitor" invented for the purpose, is ad hoc and lowers the theory's testability.
