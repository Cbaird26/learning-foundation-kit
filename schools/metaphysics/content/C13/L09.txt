Last week's argument for realism turned on the success of science. Anti-realists reply that success is less than it seems. Two anti-realist ideas are central. One says that science does not need to aim at truth about unobservable things; the other says that history gives us reason to doubt that success shows truth.

### Constructive empiricism

Bas van Fraassen's *The Scientific Image* (1980) defends **constructive empiricism**. It has a thesis about the aim of science and a thesis about acceptance. The aim of science, he says, is to give us theories that are **empirically adequate**: what a theory says about observable things and events, past, present and future, is true. To **accept** a theory is to believe that it is empirically adequate, and to commit oneself to working within its framework. It is not to believe that what it says about unobservable things is true. The constructive empiricist is not an instrumentalist about meaning: theories are to be read literally, so a theory that mentions electrons says that there are electrons, and the constructive empiricist remains agnostic about whether it is true. This is the difference from the older positivist view that talk of unobservables should be reinterpreted.

The view depends on the distinction between observable and unobservable. Van Fraassen's line is that something is **observable** if a human being could observe it with unaided senses under suitable circumstances. The moons of Jupiter are observable, since an astronaut near Jupiter could see them, whereas a bacterium is not, even though we see its image in a microscope. There are three standard objections. First, Grover Maxwell argued in 1962 that there is a continuum from seeing with the naked eye, through spectacles and binoculars, to microscopes, with no sharp line, so the distinction is arbitrary. Van Fraassen replied that vagueness does not prevent the distinction from having clear cases on each side. Second, all observation is laden with theory, so there is no theory-free observational base; he replied that the claim concerns what is observable, not the language of description. Third, Ian Hacking's entity realism argues from experimental practice: when we can manipulate unobservable entities to produce effects on other things, we have good reason to believe in them, whether or not we believe the theories about them.

### The pessimistic induction

Larry Laudan's 1981 paper "A Confutation of Convergent Realism" is the source of the **pessimistic (meta-)induction**. Laudan compiled a list of theories that were once empirically successful and are now regarded as false, including the caloric theory of heat, phlogiston chemistry, and nineteenth-century ether theories of light. The argument:

| P1 | Most past scientific theories that were empirically successful were later judged false, including in the parts that were credited with producing their success. |
| P2 | Our current best theories are not relevantly different from those past successful theories. |
| P3 | If most past successful theories were false and current ones are not relevantly different, then most current successful theories are probably false. |
| C | So most of our current successful theories are probably false. |

The argument is valid, and the strength of the conclusion depends on how good P3 is, since it is an inductive step. Each premise has been attacked. Against P1, realists such as Philip Kitcher and Stathis Psillos argue that the parts of past theories that did the work producing success were retained (a strategy sometimes called **divide and conquer**): for example, Fresnel's equations for the intensity of reflected and refracted light were taken over, reinterpreted as claims about electromagnetic fields, in Maxwell's theory. This claims that the past theories were not wholly false in the relevant sense. Against P2, realists say that today's theories are more mature, have more diverse confirmation and make novel predictions, so the cases differ. Against P3, Peter Lewis (2001) and later P. D. Magnus and Craig Callender argued that the induction can commit a **base-rate** error. Counting false theories among successful ones does not tell us the probability that a theory is false unless we also know how many successful theories there were and how they were selected. Kyle Stanford (2006) offered a variant, the **problem of unconceived alternatives**: past scientists failed to conceive of alternatives that later scientists accepted, and we should expect the same of ourselves.

### How the two ideas fit together

Constructive empiricism does not rely on the pessimistic induction, and the induction does not entail it. Together they push in the same direction: belief should extend only as far as the evidence reaches, and for claims about unobservables, the evidence is contested. The realist reply is that past failures are compatible with a partly true picture that improves, which is why the next lesson turns to structural realism.

title: What the constructive empiricist believes
problem: A theory says that a certain infection is caused by a bacterium too small to see, that its colonies grow visibly on a dish in a lab, and that a newly discovered moon orbits a distant planet. Which of these does a constructive empiricist take to be observable, and what does she believe when she accepts the theory?
step: The colonies on the dish are visible with the unaided eye, so they are observable. The moon is too far away to see from Earth, but an astronaut near it could see it, so on van Fraassen's definition it is observable. The individual bacterium is not observable unaided, though we can see an image in a microscope.
step: In accepting the theory she believes that it is empirically adequate: that what it says about observable things, such as the colonies and the moon, is true.
step: She does not believe or disbelieve what it says about the unobservable bacterium. She takes the claim literally and remains agnostic about it, while using the theory.
step: Contrast the realist, who believes also the claim about the bacterium, and the instrumentalist, who reads the claim not as literally true or false.
answer: The colonies and the moon are observable and the bacterium is not; she believes the theory's claims about the first two and stays agnostic about the third.

problem: A realist says: "Fresnel's equations, which did the work in his wave theory of light, were retained in Maxwell's theory, so the theory's success-producing parts were not false." Which premise of the pessimistic induction does this deny?

step: The reply is about the past theory: it says that the part responsible for success was kept, so it was not judged false.

@mistakes
- Describing constructive empiricism as the view that unobservable entities do not exist. Van Fraassen's view is agnosticism about them, with a literal reading of theories.
- Confusing acceptance with belief. A constructive empiricist accepts a theory, which involves believing that it is empirically adequate, without believing everything it says.
- Saying that the pessimistic induction shows that current theories are false. At most it gives a reason to expect many of them to be false, and its strength is disputed.
- Treating the inference as invalid. Its premises, together with the inductive premise, do yield the conclusion, so replies attack premises.

prompt: Explain Maxwell's objection to the observable/unobservable distinction in two or three sentences and give van Fraassen's reply.
hint: Think of a series from looking with the naked eye to using a microscope.
solution: Grover Maxwell pointed out that there is a continuum of cases from seeing with the naked eye, through glasses, binoculars and low-power microscopes, to high-power instruments, so there is no principled place to draw a line between observable and unobservable, and a distinction that matters for belief should not be arbitrary. Van Fraassen replied that observable is a vague predicate, but vague predicates have clear cases and clear non-cases, and the distinction is fixed by what unaided humans can observe. The objector can respond that this makes a limit of human biology decisive for what we may believe.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in one paragraph the difference between acceptance of a theory and belief in a theory for a constructive empiricist, and say why this difference allows her to use theories of unobservable things in science.
hint: Say what she believes when she accepts a theory and what she does not.
solution: For van Fraassen, to accept a theory is to believe that it is empirically adequate and to commit oneself to working within its framework, using its concepts in explaining, predicting and designing experiments. It is not to believe that everything it says about unobservables is true. Because she reads theories literally, she does not treat claims about unobservable things as meaningless, but she suspends judgment on them. Since scientific practice needs a framework for explanation and further research, not belief in every claim, she can use theories of unobservables as fully as a realist does, while believing less. The realist can reply that this attitude is hard to tell apart from belief, or that the empirical adequacy claim itself goes beyond what has been observed.
