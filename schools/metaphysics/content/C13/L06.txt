The traditional picture of science is cumulative: facts and theories are added to a growing stock, and each theory is a refined version of the last. Thomas Kuhn's *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (1962) challenged this picture with a view trained on the history of science. On his account, science does not grow steadily. Long periods of consensus are interrupted by episodes in which a field's basic commitments are replaced.

### Normal science

For most of a field's history, Kuhn says, scientists engage in **normal science**: they work within a **paradigm**, a shared set of achievements, methods, examples and commitments that tells practitioners what problems are worth solving and what a solution looks like. Normal science is **puzzle-solving**: the aim is not to test the paradigm but to extend it, matching theory to observation and solving problems that the paradigm itself defines as soluble. In the 1969 postscript to the book, Kuhn distinguished two senses of "paradigm": the **disciplinary matrix**, the entire constellation of shared beliefs, values and techniques, and **exemplars**, the concrete problem solutions that students learn and imitate. The critic Margaret Masterman counted over twenty different uses of the word in the original text, which is one reason for the distinction.

### Anomaly, crisis and revolution

Puzzles sometimes resist solution. An **anomaly** is a result that conflicts with what the paradigm leads scientists to expect. Most anomalies are set aside or are eventually resolved within the paradigm. When anomalies accumulate, or bear on something fundamental, the field enters a **crisis**: confidence in the paradigm weakens, rules for solving puzzles become loose, and rival proposals multiply. A **scientific revolution** occurs when a rival paradigm gains the allegiance of the community and replaces the old one, after which a new period of normal science begins. Kuhn compared the transition to a **gestalt switch** and sometimes to a political revolution: it is not forced by logic and evidence alone, and he said that a change of paradigm changes what scientists see.

### Incommensurability

Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend, independently, used the term **incommensurability**, from the mathematical sense of having no common measure. For Kuhn, competing paradigms differ in the problems they take to be important, in the standards for solutions, and in the meanings of key terms, so that there is no neutral language in which to state both and no neutral algorithm to decide between them. His standard example is the concept of mass, which in Newtonian mechanics is conserved and in relativity can be converted into energy. The argument can be stated this way:

| P1 | If two paradigms are incommensurable, then there is no neutral observation language or set of shared standards that is sufficient to decide between them. |
| P2 | Paradigms on either side of a scientific revolution are incommensurable. |
| P3 | If there is no neutral language or shared set of standards sufficient to decide between two paradigms, then the choice between them is not determined by logic and evidence alone. |
| C | So the choice between paradigms across a revolution is not determined by logic and evidence alone. |

The argument is valid, and the conclusion alone does not mean that the choice is irrational. Kuhn insisted on this. In "Objectivity, Value Judgment, and Theory Choice" (a 1973 lecture, published in *The Essential Tension*, 1977), he argued that scientists share values, such as accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness, which guide choice, but that the values can be interpreted and weighed differently, so they do not amount to an algorithm. This reply to the charge of irrationality denies that "not determined by an algorithm" means "not rational," so it accepts the conclusion C and denies the inference to irrationality that critics draw from it.

Replies to the argument itself attack other premises. Donald Davidson, in "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme" (1974), argued that the idea of a language wholly untranslatable into ours is unintelligible, which attacks P2 in its strong form. Kuhn himself later restricted his claim to **local incommensurability**: only a subset of interrelated terms fails to translate, leaving much common ground. Others deny P3 on the ground that shared values and common observations are enough for rational choice, even if the choice is not forced.

### Exegesis and evaluation

Be careful to separate what Kuhn claims from whether the claim is true. That the history of science contains episodes like Kuhn's descriptions is an empirical claim, and historians disagree about how well his scheme fits. Whether this implies relativism is a philosophical claim that Kuhn disputed, and the debate continues.

@example
title: The Ptolemaic-Copernican episode in Kuhn's terms
problem: Describe the change from Ptolemaic to Copernican astronomy using Kuhn's concepts, and say which features of the history do and do not fit the story of a quick, decisive switch.
step: Normal science: for many centuries, astronomers worked within the Ptolemaic paradigm, an Earth-centered system using circles (deferents), smaller circles (epicycles) and an off-center point (the equant). The puzzles were to fit the observed positions of the planets by adjusting the sizes and speeds of the circles.
step: Anomaly and crisis: predictions of planetary positions and of the calendar did not fit observation well, and astronomers' attempts to fix them produced competing and increasingly complicated variants. Kuhn stresses that calendar problems, such as the date of Easter, gave the difficulties practical urgency.
step: Revolution: in 1543 Copernicus published *De revolutionibus*, placing the Sun near the center. His system still used epicycles and was not much more accurate in prediction than Ptolemy's. Acceptance was gradual: Kepler's ellipses (published in 1609) and Galileo's telescopic observations (from 1610) strengthened the case, and the change was complete only with Newton's synthesis in 1687.
step: What fits and what does not: the replacement of a framework with different assumptions fits Kuhn's account, but the long duration, the contributions of several figures and the lack of an immediate predictive advantage show that the transition was not a single switch. This is an instance of history testing a theory of science.
answer: The episode has normal science, anomaly, crisis and revolution, but acceptance took more than a century and the new system was not obviously more accurate at first.

@example
title: Which premise does the reply deny?
problem: A critic says: "Newtonian and relativistic physicists can agree on observations such as the positions of light spots on photographic plates, so there is a neutral observation language." In the incommensurability argument (P1, P2, P3, C), which premise does this reply deny?
step: The reply says that a neutral observation language is available to both sides.
step: P1 does not itself say that there is a neutral language; it is conditional. The claim that the paradigms are incommensurable is in P2.
step: So the reply denies P2: it denies that the paradigms are incommensurable in a way that rules out a neutral observation language. P1 and P3 can stay.
answer: The reply denies P2. Kuhn's own response was to accept shared observations in some cases but to say that they do not suffice to decide, and he later restricted his claim to local incommensurability.

@mistakes
- Saying that Kuhn held that science is irrational. He argued that theory choice relies on shared values and judgment, not an algorithm, and he denied that this makes it irrational.
- Using "paradigm" for any big idea. In Kuhn it is a shared achievement and set of commitments that guide puzzle-solving in a community.
- Reading the stages as a necessary law of history. They are Kuhn's description of a pattern, which historians have both used and criticized.
- Treating incommensurability as the claim that rival paradigms are logically contradictory. It is the claim that there is no common measure, neutral language or algorithm.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in four or five sentences the difference between a disciplinary matrix and an exemplar, and say why Kuhn thought that learning exemplars matters for how normal science works.
hint: Think of a student learning to solve textbook problems.
solution: A disciplinary matrix is the whole constellation of shared commitments of a scientific community: its symbolic generalizations, models, values and exemplars. An exemplar is a concrete problem solution, such as a standard worked problem, that students study and learn to imitate. Kuhn thought that exemplars matter because students learn what counts as a problem and a solution by practicing on examples, not by memorizing rules, so scientists apply the paradigm by perceiving new problems as similar to the exemplars. This explains how a community can agree in practice while being unable to state complete rules, and how normal science extends the paradigm to new puzzles.
