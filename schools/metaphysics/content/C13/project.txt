@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1-2.** The demarcation problem asks what separates science from non-science; the verification principle was a criterion of meaning, not a demarcation of science from pseudoscience, and it faces the self-refutation objection. Popper's falsifiability rests on the logical asymmetry between verifying and falsifying a universal claim; corroboration is not probability; the Duhem-Quine argument shows that a failed prediction refutes only a theory together with auxiliaries.

**Weeks 3-4.** Hume's problem of induction, Hempel's raven paradox (Nicod's criterion plus the equivalence condition) and Goodman's new riddle of induction. Bayesian confirmation: $E$ confirms $H$ when $P(H \mid E) > P(H)$; Bayes's theorem and the odds form; the Dutch book argument; the problems of priors and of old evidence.

**Weeks 5-6.** The DN model and the flagpole and irrelevance counterexamples; causal, unificationist and pragmatic rivals. Kuhn: paradigm, normal science, puzzle, anomaly, crisis, revolution, incommensurability, and the shared values reply to the charge of irrationality.

**Weeks 7-8.** Lakatos's research programmes (hard core, protective belt, heuristics, progressive and degenerating); Feyerabend's argument against universal method. Scientific realism has metaphysical, semantic and epistemic components; the no-miracles argument and its objections (selection, false successful theories, the status of inference to the best explanation, the base rate).

**Weeks 9-10.** Constructive empiricism (empirical adequacy, acceptance versus belief); the pessimistic induction and its replies; underdetermination; epistemic and ontic structural realism; Newman's problem.

**Weeks 11-12.** Nagel reduction, multiple realizability, weak and strong emergence and Kim's exclusion argument; the value-free ideal, inductive risk, social objectivity and the replication problem.

### Key arguments and skills

- For each argument in the course (Hume on induction, the raven argument, the flagpole argument, the incommensurability argument, the no-miracles argument, the pessimistic induction, underdetermination, multiple realizability, exclusion, inductive risk), write it in standard form, check that it is valid, and match each reply to the premise it denies.
- Bayes's theorem: $P(H \mid E) = P(E \mid H)P(H)/[P(E \mid H)P(H) + P(E \mid \neg H)P(\neg H)]$, and the odds form with likelihood ratios.
- Distinguish: demarcation from meaning; refutation from methodological rule; confirmation from high probability; holist from contrastive underdetermination; weak from strong emergence; epistemic from non-epistemic values.

### Self-check

- Can you explain each term in a sentence and give a case?
- Can you say which premise a given objection attacks, without checking?
- Can you describe the Copernican, oxygen, continental drift and atomic cases accurately, and say what each is used to show?

### Common errors

Calling a valid argument invalid when you mean that its conclusion is doubtful; attributing to Popper a theory of meaning; saying that Kuhn thought science irrational; confusing acceptance with belief in constructive empiricism; treating "anything goes" as Feyerabend's method; forgetting the base rate in Bayesian problems; and stating a historical claim you have not checked.

prompt: A researcher's credence in a theory $T$ is 0.3. Two independent pieces of evidence arrive, independent given $T$ and given $\neg T$. A novel prediction is confirmed, with $P(E_1 \mid T) = 0.9$ and $P(E_1 \mid \neg T) = 0.2$. A second result occurs, with $P(E_2 \mid T) = 0.6$ and $P(E_2 \mid \neg T) = 0.4$. What is her credence in $T$ after conditionalizing on both, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 74.3
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: (lambda o: 100 * o / (1 + o))(0.3 / 0.7 * (0.9 / 0.2) * (0.6 / 0.4))
hint: Update on one piece of evidence at a time, or multiply the prior odds by the two likelihood ratios.
hint: The prior odds are $0.3/0.7$ and the likelihood ratios are $4.5$ and $1.5$.
solution: The posterior after $E_1$ is $0.27/(0.27 + 0.14) \approx 0.659$, and after $E_2$ it is $0.395/(0.395 + 0.137) \approx 0.743$. In odds form, $(0.3/0.7) \times 4.5 \times 1.5 \approx 2.893$, so the probability is $2.893/3.893 \approx 0.743$, or 74.3%. Both pieces of evidence confirm $T$, since both likelihood ratios exceed 1.

@exam self
prompt: Write an essay of about 500 words answering this question: "Does the history of science support scientific realism or anti-realism?" Use the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic induction in standard form, say which premise of each is most vulnerable, and consider how structural realism responds. Take a position and defend it, while presenting the strongest reply to it.
hint: State both arguments with premises, match objections to premises, and use a case from history, such as the replacement of Fresnel's theory of light.
solution: The no-miracles argument holds that our best theories are very successful (P1), that the best explanation of such success is approximate truth (P2), and that the best explanation of a fact is justified (P3), so that we are justified in believing that they are approximately true (C). The pessimistic induction holds that most past successful theories were later judged false, including in the parts credited with success (P1), that current theories are not relevantly different (P2), and that if so most current ones are probably false (P3), so that most current successful theories are probably false (C). Both are valid, and the debate is about their premises. The weakest premise in the no-miracles argument is P2, which the selection explanation and the base-rate objection both attack; in the pessimistic induction it is P2, relevant similarity, which realists say fails because of the greater maturity of current theories, or P1, since the working parts of the past theories may have survived. A defensible position is selective or structural realism: the best explanation of novel success is that the parts of a theory doing the work are approximately true, and history, as in the retention of Fresnel's equations in Maxwell's theory, is taken to show that what is retained is the mathematical structure. This position takes the induction seriously by restricting belief. The strongest reply is Newman's problem, which says that structure alone is trivial unless the relations are restricted, and a second reply that identifying the working parts is easier in hindsight than in advance. The position is therefore modest: it commits to structure, concedes that the intrinsic nature of unobservables is left open, and treats the question of which relations count as genuine as unfinished work.
rubric: States both the no-miracles argument and the pessimistic induction accurately in standard form.
rubric: Identifies a vulnerable premise in each and names the objection that attacks it.
rubric: Takes a position, uses a historical example correctly, and discusses structural realism and at least one objection to it.
