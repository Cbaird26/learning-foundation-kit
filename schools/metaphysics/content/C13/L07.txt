@article
Popper's falsificationism is too simple, since a failed prediction does not by itself refute a theory, and Kuhn's account seemed to some to leave theory choice to persuasion and sociology. How, then, can the rationality of science be understood? Imre Lakatos tried to combine Popper's concern with rational appraisal and Kuhn's historical sensitivity. Paul Feyerabend argued that the attempt to give science a fixed method is mistaken.

### Lakatos: research programmes

In "Falsification and the Methodology of Scientific Research Programmes" (1970), Lakatos proposed that the unit of appraisal is not a single theory but a **research programme**: a series of theories that share a **hard core** of fundamental assumptions. The **negative heuristic** tells scientists not to direct the logic of refutation at the hard core; when a prediction fails, blame falls on the **protective belt**, the auxiliary hypotheses and initial conditions surrounding the core. The **positive heuristic** is a set of suggestions about how to develop the programme and modify the belt. Lakatos took the Newtonian gravitational programme as an illustration: its core was the three laws of motion and the law of gravitation, and its positive heuristic directed physicists to build ever more realistic models of the planets, starting from simple ones.

What makes a programme good? A programme is **theoretically progressive** if each change in its belt predicts some new fact that was not already known, and **empirically progressive** if some of these predictions are corroborated. It is **degenerating** if its changes are only ad hoc adjustments that accommodate known facts. This is a refinement of Popper's rule against ad hoc rescues: what matters is the trend over time, not one test. Lakatos denied that there is **instant rationality**: because a programme can recover, it can be rational to continue working on a degenerating programme in the hope of a comeback, though the cost of doing so rises as it degenerates. Critics, including Feyerabend, asked what the advice amounts to if there is no time limit on how long a degenerating programme may be pursued.

### Feyerabend: against method

In *Against Method* (1975), Feyerabend defended what he called epistemological anarchism. His core claim is that every methodological rule that has been proposed, such as "reject theories that contradict observations" or "prefer theories that explain more," has been violated in episodes of science that we count as successes, and that those violations were part of the reason for the successes. He recommended **counterinduction**, the introduction of hypotheses that conflict with well-confirmed theories, and **proliferation**, the multiplication of rival theories. He argued that Galileo's defense of the Copernican view relied on rhetoric and on new interpretations of observation, and not on a clear application of a fixed method; historians have disputed this reading. Feyerabend presented "anything goes" as the only principle that does not hinder progress, and later said that it was meant as a summary of the predicament of a rationalist who looks at the historical record, not as a rule that he endorsed.

The argument can be stated as follows:

| P1 | If there is a universal methodological rule that scientists ought always to follow, then following it would never have blocked scientific progress. |
| P2 | For every proposed universal methodological rule, there are episodes in the history of science in which following it would have blocked progress. |
| C | So there is no universal methodological rule that scientists ought always to follow. |

The argument is valid. To resist it, one can deny P2 by reading the historical episodes differently, showing that the rule was followed or that the progress was due to something else. One can also deny P1, which assumes that progress is the sole standard of a good rule. A rule might bind scientists because it is truth-conducive in the long run, even if obeying it sometimes slows the field down. Another reply says that rules are conditional advice (if you want to find the truth, then do such-and-such) and are binding in context. Feyerabend's own reply to the objection that this leaves science without guidance was that a plurality of approaches serves science better than a single method.

### Where this leaves rationality

Lakatos, Kuhn and Feyerabend ask in different ways what remains of the idea that science is a rational enterprise. A common middle view says that science involves rational norms, such as responsiveness to evidence, but that these norms are defeasible, depend on context and do not form an algorithm.

title: Classifying changes to a programme
problem: A research programme in the field of tidal modeling has a hard core: the water in a basin obeys a certain set of dynamical equations. Its model predicts a tide height of 1.2 m, but 1.5 m is observed. The modelers then (a) add a new parameter for seabed friction and, using it, predict the tides in a second basin that they have not yet measured, and the prediction is confirmed; (b) later, a prediction fails and they add a correction factor that fits the new observation but implies nothing else. Classify (a) and (b) on Lakatos's criterion.
step: Identify the hard core and protective belt: the equations are the hard core, and the friction parameter and correction factor are additions to the belt.
step: For (a), the change accommodates the known 1.5 m reading and also predicts something new (the tides in the second basin), and the prediction was confirmed. It is theoretically and empirically progressive.
step: For (b), the change was introduced only to fit the new observation and predicts nothing further. It is ad hoc, and a series of such changes would mark degeneration.
step: Say what follows: one degenerating step does not refute the programme, and Lakatos allowed a degenerating programme to recover. What matters is the trend over time.
answer: (a) is progressive; (b) is ad hoc and a sign of degeneration if the pattern continues.

@example
title: Which premise does the reply deny?
problem: A critic says: "Feyerabend's examples are cherry-picked, and in each of them the successful scientist was in fact following the rule 'prefer the theory that explains more.'" Which premise of Feyerabend's argument does this deny?
step: The reply is about the historical episodes, and says that following the rule did not block progress.
step: P2 claims that for every proposed rule there are episodes where following it would have blocked progress.
step: The critic is denying P2 for the rule "prefer the theory that explains more."
step: The argument stays valid. P2 says that every proposed rule has been violated productively, so showing that even one rule never blocked progress is enough to deny P2; to defeat C as well, the critic must also argue that this rule is universal and binding.
answer: The reply denies P2, since one rule that never blocked progress would falsify the claim that every rule has. It shows the argument can be resisted rule by rule.

@mistakes
- Treating "anything goes" as Feyerabend's recommended method. He presented it as the only "principle" that does not hinder progress and later said it summarized a predicament.
- Saying that Lakatos defended falsification of the hard core. The negative heuristic tells scientists to protect it.
- Confusing a progressive programme with a true one. Progress is a matter of predicting novel facts, which does not guarantee truth.
- Treating Lakatos and Kuhn as the same view. Lakatos tried to give a rational criterion for appraising programmes over time, which Kuhn, on Lakatos's reading, did not.

@quiz self
prompt: In one paragraph, say whether Lakatos's criterion of progressive and degenerating programmes answers the Duhem-Quine problem for falsificationism, and what remains unanswered.
hint: Think about what the criterion says about where to place blame and about how long to wait.
solution: Lakatos's methodology gives a partial answer: the negative heuristic says where to place blame after a failed prediction (on the belt, not the hard core), and the progressive-degenerating distinction gives a way to appraise how well a series of adjustments is doing, so that ad hoc rescues are marked as bad without being forbidden outright. What remains unanswered is how long a degenerating programme may be pursued before it is rational to abandon it, since the criterion gives no time limit, and how to tell in advance whether a programme will recover. Critics, including Feyerabend, said that this leaves the choice to judgment, which suggests that the methodology offers advice that is retrospective and does not determine decisions.
