@article
Chemistry is made of atoms, biology of cells and cells of molecules, and economies of people. Does it follow that chemistry is "really" physics, biology is "really" chemistry and economics is "really" psychology? Or does each science have its own laws and kinds that cannot be reduced to those of the science below? This is the question of **reduction** and **emergence**, and it bears on whether the sciences form a unified hierarchy.

### Nagel's model of reduction

Ernest Nagel, in *The Structure of Science* (1961), gave the classic account. A theory $T_2$ is **reduced** to a theory $T_1$ when the laws of $T_2$ are logically derived from the laws of $T_1$, together with **bridge laws** (or "connecting principles") that link the terms of $T_2$ not found in $T_1$ to terms of $T_1$. The standard illustration is the reduction of thermodynamics to statistical mechanics. The law relating the pressure, volume and temperature of an ideal gas is derived from the kinetic theory of molecules, using a bridge law that connects temperature to the mean translational kinetic energy of the molecules. On this model, reduction is a kind of explanation: the higher-level laws are explained by the lower-level ones. The model has been criticized because real cases of reduction involve approximations and corrections, so that the old theory is often derived only approximately, or a corrected version of it is.

### Multiple realizability and the autonomy of the special sciences

Hilary Putnam (1967) and Jerry Fodor (1974) argued that psychology and other **special sciences** cannot be reduced in this way. The argument is:

| P1 | If a special-science kind is reducible to physics in Nagel's sense, then there is a physical kind that is coextensive with it, as stated in a bridge law. |
| P2 | Special-science kinds are multiply realizable: the same kind (for example, being a thermostat or being a currency) can be realized by physically very different kinds of systems or states. |
| P3 | If a special-science kind is multiply realizable by physically very different states, then there is no single physical kind coextensive with it. |
| C | So special-science kinds are not reducible to physics in Nagel's sense. |

The argument is valid: P2 and P3 give that no single physical kind is coextensive with it, and P1 then yields the conclusion. Replies have attacked each premise. Against P2, William Bechtel and Jennifer Mundale (1999) argued that the claims of multiple realization are overstated: when the individuation of kinds is done at the same level of grain, the realizations are often similar. Against P3, one can say that a disjunction of physical kinds is a physical kind, so that the bridge law may be disjunctive; Fodor replied that such disjunctions are not the kinds of things that figure in laws. Jaegwon Kim (1992) argued instead that multiple realizability supports **local reductions**, species-specific or structure-specific reductions, such as the reduction of pain in humans to a human neural state, and that the special science kind is then not a genuine kind.

### Emergence

A property is **emergent** if it arises from the properties of a system's parts in a way that is not simply the sum of what the parts do. The British emergentists, such as C. D. Broad (1925), defended a strong version. David Chalmers (2006) distinguishes **weak emergence**, in which high-level phenomena are unexpected or hard to predict but are in principle deducible from the low-level facts (as the flocking of birds is from simple rules for each bird), from **strong emergence**, in which the high-level truths are not deducible even in principle. Weak emergence is widely accepted and is compatible with reductionism. Strong emergence is controversial, and the main objection to it concerns causation.

Jaegwon Kim's **causal exclusion argument** is usually stated as follows. A higher-level property $M$ is supposed to have effects on physical events, which is called **downward causation**.

| P1 | (Causal closure) Every physical event that has a sufficient cause has a sufficient physical cause. |
| P2 | (Exclusion) If an event has two distinct sufficient causes, then it is overdetermined. |
| P3 | The physical effects of higher-level properties are not overdetermined. |
| P4 | A higher-level property $M$ is distinct from the physical property $P$ on which it depends. |
| C | So $M$ is not a sufficient cause of a physical event that $P$ causes. |

The argument is valid. Replies deny P1 (strong emergentists deny that physical causal closure is true), deny P2 and P3 (theories of causation on which causes at different levels are compatible, so that two causes need not compete), or deny P4 and identify $M$ with $P$, which gives up the autonomy of the special sciences.

@example
title: A Nagel-style derivation
problem: Kinetic theory says that for $N$ molecules of an ideal gas in volume $V$, the pressure satisfies $PV = \tfrac{2}{3} N \bar{E}$, where $\bar{E}$ is the mean translational kinetic energy per molecule. Show how a bridge law gives the ideal gas law $PV = NkT$.
step: The bridge law connects temperature to a quantity of the lower theory: $\bar{E} = \tfrac{3}{2}kT$, where $k$ is Boltzmann's constant.
step: Substitute into the kinetic-theory law: $PV = \tfrac{2}{3} N \times \tfrac{3}{2} kT$.
step: Simplify: $PV = NkT$, which is the ideal gas law of thermodynamics.
step: Note the structure: laws of the lower theory plus a bridge law yield a law of the higher theory. The bridge law does the work of linking a term ("temperature") that the lower theory did not contain.
answer: $PV = \tfrac{2}{3}N \cdot \tfrac{3}{2}kT = NkT$, given the bridge law $\bar{E} = \tfrac{3}{2}kT$.

@example
title: Multiple realization and the reply from local reduction
problem: A "cold-detector" is any system that registers when a room is too cold and switches on a heater. One is a bimetallic strip that bends, another a digital sensor with a chip, another a biological tissue that triggers a response. Explain why this seems to block a Nagel-style bridge law, and give the local-reduction reply.
step: The kind is defined by its function (registering cold and triggering heat), and the three systems have very different physical states.
step: A Nagel bridge law for the kind needs a physical kind that holds of all of them and only them. A list of the three physical states is a disjunction, which is not obviously a physical kind that figures in laws (P3).
step: A local reductionist says that for each type of system there is a reduction: the strip's behavior is derived from properties of metals, the chip's from properties of circuits.
step: What remains is a question about the status of the general kind: whether it is merely a convenient classification or a real kind that earns explanations of its own.
answer: The kind is multiply realizable by physically different states, so no single physical kind is coextensive with it; a local reductionist replies that reduction can be made type by type.

@mistakes
- Confusing weak and strong emergence. Weak emergence is compatible with in-principle deducibility from the low-level facts; strong emergence is not.
- Saying that multiple realizability shows that the special sciences are not physical. It is an argument about the form of the relation between kinds, and Putnam and Fodor accepted that special-science systems are made of physical parts.
- Treating the causal exclusion argument as showing that higher-level properties do not exist. Its conclusion concerns causal efficacy, given its premises.
- Thinking that reduction means that the higher-level science can be discarded. A reduced theory may remain indispensable for explanation and practice.

@practice self
prompt: Explain in a paragraph why multiple realizability is supposed to block Nagel-style reduction, and give one reply.
hint: Think of what a bridge law needs and of a kind realized in different materials.
solution: A Nagel-style reduction needs bridge laws that link each kind of the higher-level theory to a kind of the lower-level theory with the same instances. If a kind, such as being a thermostat, is realized in very different physical ways (a metal strip, a circuit, a tissue), no single physical kind seems to have the same instances, so no bridge law of the required form is available and the laws of the special science cannot be derived. One reply is that a disjunction of the physical realizers can serve as the kind in a bridge law. Fodor's counter is that such disjunctions are not the kinds that figure in laws, which leaves the status of disjunctive kinds as the point in dispute. Another reply is that reduction can be local, within a single type of system.

@quiz self
prompt: Distinguish weak from strong emergence, and give an example of a phenomenon that is a plausible case of weak emergence. Say why strong emergence faces the exclusion argument.
hint: Use deducibility in principle and downward causation.
solution: Weak emergence is the arising of high-level phenomena that are surprising or hard to predict but are in principle deducible from the low-level facts, as the V-shaped flocking pattern of a flock of birds is deducible from simple rules each bird follows. Strong emergence is the arising of high-level truths that are not deducible even in principle, which would require high-level properties to have a further, independent role. Strong emergence faces the exclusion argument because such properties are supposed to cause physical events, but if every physical event has a sufficient physical cause and the higher-level property is distinct from its base, then the event seems to have two sufficient causes and to be overdetermined, which is implausible as a general pattern. The strong emergentist can deny physical closure or deny that two-level causation is overdetermination.
