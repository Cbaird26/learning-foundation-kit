@article
Science does not only describe and predict. It explains. Why does the tide come in twice a day? Why did the bridge fail? A theory of **scientific explanation** says what makes an answer to a why-question a good one, and what is the difference between explaining a fact and merely deriving it or predicting it.

### The deductive-nomological model

Carl Hempel and Paul Oppenheim set out the **deductive-nomological (DN) model** in "Studies in the Logic of Explanation" (1948). An explanation of a fact (the **explanandum**) is an argument whose premises (the **explanans**) include at least one law of nature and statements of antecedent conditions, whose conclusion is a description of the explanandum. The conditions are that the argument is deductively valid, that the explanans contains a general law that is needed for the derivation, and that the explanans is true. A law is a lawlike generalization, not an accident: "all samples of iron expand when heated" qualifies, but "all the coins in my pocket are copper" does not support counterfactuals ("if this aluminum token were in my pocket, it would be copper") and is not a law. Explanation on this model has the same logical form as prediction, so an explanation is what would have allowed the event to be predicted. For explanations with statistical laws Hempel proposed a related **inductive-statistical (IS)** model, on which the explanans must make the explanandum highly probable.

### Counterexamples

The model faces two famous kinds of counterexample. The first concerns **asymmetry**. A flagpole stands on level ground. The Sun is at a given elevation, and the pole casts a shadow of a certain length. From the length of the shadow, the elevation of the Sun and the laws of optics, one can validly derive the height of the pole. The example is due to Sylvain Bromberger. The argument against the DN model is:

| P1 | If the DN model is correct, then any valid derivation of a fact's description from true laws and true statements of conditions is an explanation of that fact. |
| P2 | The derivation of the flagpole's height from the length of its shadow, the Sun's elevation and the laws of optics is a valid derivation from true laws and true conditions. |
| P3 | The length of the shadow does not explain the height of the flagpole. |
| C | So the DN model is not correct. |

The argument is valid, by modus tollens. A defender of the DN model must deny P2 or P3. Denying P3 is hard: it is clear that the height explains the shadow and not the other way around. Denying P2 would require showing that the derivation violates one of the DN conditions, but all of them are met. Hence critics conclude that the model is incomplete: it leaves out the asymmetry of explanation, which many take to be causal.

The second kind concerns **irrelevance**. A sample of table salt dissolves in water, and one can derive that it dissolved from the facts that it was hexed by a person with a ritual and that all hexed salt dissolves in water. The premises are true and lawlike (they hold of all salt, hexed or not), yet the hexing is irrelevant. Wesley Salmon gave a similar example about a man who takes his partner's contraceptive pills and does not become pregnant.

### Rivals

**Causal accounts** say that to explain is to give information about the causes of the explanandum. Salmon developed a **causal-mechanical** account, in which explanation traces causal processes and interactions, and David Lewis held that to explain an event is to provide information about its causal history. These accounts handle asymmetry and irrelevance, since the height of the pole causes the shadow and hexing does not cause dissolving. They face a problem with explanations in mathematics and with non-causal explanations in physics, and with the question of what causation is.

**Unificationist accounts**, from Michael Friedman (1974) and Philip Kitcher (1981), say that explanation unifies: to explain is to show how many phenomena follow from a small stock of argument patterns. Critics ask whether unification can capture asymmetry: it seems that one can unify as well with the flagpole derivation reversed.

**Pragmatic accounts**, notably Bas van Fraassen's in *The Scientific Image* (1980), treat a why-question as having a **contrast class**. "Why did the bridge fail in March?" may mean "why in March and not April?" or "why this bridge and not the one next to it?", and a good answer depends on the question asked and on what is already known. This accommodates the variety of explanations, but critics ask whether it makes explanation too subjective.

@example
title: Testing a derivation against the DN conditions
problem: Evaluate these two derivations as DN explanations. (1) Law: all samples of iron expand when heated. Condition: this bar is iron and was heated. Conclusion: this bar expanded. (2) Generalization: all the coins in my pocket are copper. Condition: this coin is in my pocket. Conclusion: this coin is copper.
step: Check validity. Both derivations are deductively valid: the conclusion follows from the generalization and the condition.
step: Check truth and the presence of a law. In (1) the generalization is a lawlike statement that supports counterfactuals, and it is true. In (2) the generalization may be true but is accidental: it would not support "if this aluminum token were in my pocket, it would be copper."
step: So (1) satisfies the DN conditions. (2) fails the requirement that the explanans contain a law, so it does not count as a DN explanation.
step: Note that even (1) is not guaranteed to be a good explanation on rival accounts, but it passes the test of the DN model.
answer: (1) is a DN explanation; (2) is not, because the generalization is accidental and not lawlike.

@example
title: The irrelevance problem and the hexed salt
problem: Show that the hexed-salt derivation meets the DN conditions, say what is wrong with it, and say how a causal theorist diagnoses the problem.
step: The explanans is "this salt was hexed" and "all hexed salt dissolves in water when placed in it," and the explanandum is "this salt dissolved." The derivation is valid, the explanans is true, and the generalization is true and lawlike, since it holds of all salt.
step: So the derivation meets the DN conditions. But hexing plays no role in the dissolving, so the derivation does not explain it.
step: A causal theorist says that to explain the dissolving, one has to cite its causes, such as the interaction between water molecules and the salt's ions. Hexing is not among them.
step: The DN model lacks a relevance condition. Adding one, in statistical or causal form, is how its rivals proceed.
answer: The derivation satisfies the DN conditions but cites an irrelevant factor, and a causal account repairs this by requiring causal relevance.

@mistakes
- Treating the flagpole argument as showing that the derivation is invalid. It is a valid derivation; the objection is that validity and true lawlike premises are not enough for explanation.
- Calling a true general statement a law. Accidental generalizations do not support counterfactuals and are not laws in the DN sense.
- Reading Hempel's model as saying that prediction and explanation are the same thing in practice. The claim is about logical form: an explanation would have made the explanandum predictable.
- Taking van Fraassen's pragmatic view to mean that any answer is a good explanation. The contrast class and background knowledge constrain what counts as a good answer.

@quiz self
prompt: Construct your own case in which a derivation from a true law and true conditions fails to explain, say whether it illustrates the asymmetry problem or the irrelevance problem, and say how a causal account would treat it.
hint: Use a law that is symmetric, such as one linking two quantities, or add an irrelevant condition.
solution: Take the ideal gas law $PV = nRT$. From the pressure, volume and amount of gas in a sealed rigid container, one can derive its temperature, and from the temperature, pressure and amount one can derive the volume. Suppose a heater raised the temperature of the gas and so raised the pressure at constant volume. Deriving the temperature from the pressure meets the DN conditions but runs against the causal order: the heating explains the pressure and not the reverse. This is an asymmetry case. A causal account says that the pressure rise is explained by the heating, because heating is its cause, and that the derivation of the temperature from the pressure is not an explanation of why the gas became hot.
