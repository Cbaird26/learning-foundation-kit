@article
Could Socrates have been a carpenter? In Lewis's modal realism there are other worlds, and Socrates is in only one of them. So the possibility that he was a carpenter is the existence of a carpenter in another world who resembles him sufficiently: a **counterpart**. The formal system for this is **counterpart theory**, presented in Lewis's 1968 paper "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic", and the question for this lesson is what it says about the logic of identity.

### The language and the postulates

Counterpart theory is a first-order theory in a non-modal language with four primitive predicates: $Wx$ ($x$ is a possible world), $Ixy$ ($x$ is in world $y$), $Ax$ ($x$ is actual) and $Cxy$ ($x$ is a counterpart of $y$). The postulates in Lewis's paper, numbered here for reference, are:

| CT1 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Ixy \to Wy)$ |
| CT2 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,((Ixy \wedge Ixz) \to y = z)$ |
| CT3 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Cxy \to \exists z\,Ixz)$ |
| CT4 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Cxy \to \exists z\,Iyz)$ |
| CT5 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,((Ixy \wedge Czx \wedge Izy) \to z = x)$ |
| CT6 | $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Ixy \to Cxx)$ |
| CT7 | $\exists x\,(Wx \wedge \forall y\,(Iyx \leftrightarrow Ay))$ |
| CT8 | $\exists x\,Ax$ |

In words: only worlds contain things (CT1); nothing is in two worlds (CT2); counterparts and what they are counterparts of are in worlds (CT3, CT4); nothing is a counterpart of anything else in its own world (CT5); everything is its own counterpart (CT6); some world contains exactly the actual things (CT7); something is actual (CT8). Note that the counterpart relation is *not* postulated to be symmetric, transitive, or one-to-one.

### Translating modal sentences

A modal sentence is translated relative to a world $w$. Quantifiers are restricted to the world, and modal operators quantify over worlds and counterparts. For a formula with the single free variable $x$:

| $(\exists x\,\varphi)^w$ | $\exists x\,(Ixw \wedge \varphi^w)$ |
| $(\Diamond\varphi(x))^w$ | $\exists v\,\exists y\,(Wv \wedge Iyv \wedge Cyx \wedge \varphi(y)^v)$ |
| $(\Diamond\varphi)^w$, $\varphi$ a sentence | $\exists v\,(Wv \wedge \varphi^v)$ |
| $(\Box\varphi)^w$ | $\neg(\Diamond\neg\varphi)^w$ |

With several free variables, each is replaced by one of its counterparts in the same world $v$. A sentence is true simpliciter when its translation relative to the actual world holds, and CT7 and CT8 say there is one.

### Two theorems

**Theorem 1 (the actual world is unique).** Any two worlds that contain exactly the actual things are identical.

*Proof.* Let $w$ and $w'$ be worlds with $\forall y\,(Iyw \leftrightarrow Ay)$ and $\forall y\,(Iyw' \leftrightarrow Ay)$. By CT8 some $a$ is actual, so $Iaw$ and $Iaw'$. By CT2, $w = w'$. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (the T principle).** If $Ixw$ then $(\Box Fx)^w \to Fx$.

*Proof.* Assume $Ixw$ and $(\Box Fx)^w$, which unpacks to: for every world $v$ and every $y$, if $Wv$, $Iyv$ and $Cyx$ then $Fy$. By CT1, $Ww$. By CT6, $Cxx$. Taking $v = w$ and $y = x$ gives $Fx$. $\square$

### The necessity of identity

The following argument, due in its modal-logical form to Barcan Marcus and put to work by Kripke, shows that identity is necessary.

| P1 | For all $x$ and $y$: if $x = y$ and $\Box(x = x)$, then $\Box(x = y)$ (an instance of Leibniz's Law, with the property of being necessarily identical to $x$). |
| P2 | For all $x$: $\Box(x = x)$. |
| C | So for all $x$ and $y$: if $x = y$ then $\Box(x = y)$. |

It is valid: for the arbitrary $x$ and $y$ with $x = y$, P2 gives $\Box(x=x)$ and P1 gives $\Box(x = y)$. In counterpart theory P1 fails. Under the translation, $\Box(x = x)$ asks that every counterpart of $x$ in a world be identical to itself, which is trivially true. But $\Box(x = y)$ with distinct variables asks, of each world $v$ and each pair of a counterpart $z_1$ of $x$ in $v$ and a counterpart $z_2$ of $y$ in $v$, that $z_1 = z_2$. If $x$ and $y$ are the same thing with *two* counterparts in some world, the conclusion fails even though $x = y$. Lewis accepts this: the two variables can be associated with different counterparts, so Leibniz's Law cannot be applied to a modal context blindly. In later work (from 1971) he answered the apparent oddity by allowing that different counterpart relations may be in play for different ways of representing the same thing. A critic replies that the necessity of identity seems to be one of the firmest modal intuitions, and that a theory which denies it for a single thing has a cost.

### A countermodel

Let the worlds be $@$ and $w$, with $a$ in $@$ and $b_1$, $b_2$ in $w$. Let $A$ hold of $a$ alone. Let $C$ hold of each of the reflexive pairs $(a,a)$, $(b_1,b_1)$, $(b_2,b_2)$ together with $(b_1, a)$ and $(b_2, a)$. All eight postulates hold (CT5 holds since no two things in the same world are counterparts). Yet $a = a$ and $\Box(a = a)$ hold while the translation of $\Box(x = y)$ at $x = y = a$ fails at $w$, because $b_1 \neq b_2$ are both counterparts of $a$ in $w$. So the necessity of identity is not a theorem of counterpart theory.

@example
title: Checking a model against the postulates
problem: In the countermodel above, check CT2 and CT5, and decide whether any thing has two counterparts in one world.
step: CT2: each of $a$, $b_1$, $b_2$ is in exactly one world ($@$, $w$, $w$). So the postulate holds.
step: CT5: take $x$, $z$ in the same world with $Czx$. The only counterpart pairs are reflexive (where $z = x$) or $(b_i, a)$, whose members are in different worlds. So no counterexample, and CT5 holds.
step: Fission: in $w$ the thing $a$ has two counterparts, $b_1$ and $b_2$. Distinct things in the same world are not counterparts of each other (CT5 permits this since $C b_1 b_2$ is false), so the model is consistent.
answer: CT2 and CT5 hold, and the model contains a case where one thing has two counterparts in one world.

@example
title: Proving that possibility follows from truth
problem: Prove from the postulates: if $Ixw$ and $Fx$, then $(\Diamond Fx)^w$.
step: Assume $Ixw$ and $Fx$. The translation of $(\Diamond Fx)^w$ is $\exists v\,\exists y\,(Wv \wedge Iyv \wedge Cyx \wedge Fy)$.
step: Choose $v = w$ and $y = x$. We need $Ww$, $Ixw$, $Cxx$ and $Fx$.
step: $Ww$ follows from $Ixw$ by CT1. $Ixw$ and $Fx$ are assumed. $Cxx$ follows from $Ixw$ by CT6.
step: So the witnesses work, and $(\Diamond Fx)^w$ holds.
answer: Take the world $w$ itself and $x$ as its own counterpart.

@mistakes
- Assuming that the counterpart relation is an equivalence relation. Lewis's postulates make it reflexive on things in worlds, but nothing makes it symmetric or transitive, and it may be many-one or one-many.
- Reading CT2 as a claim about the actual world only: it is the postulate that nothing is in two worlds, which is why counterparts replace identity across worlds.
- Treating $\Box(x = y)$ as a single predicate of the pair that cannot have different counterparts: in the translation, each variable has its own counterpart.
- Supposing that denying the necessity of identity is the same as denying that identity is reflexive or that Leibniz's Law holds in the extensional language. The failure concerns modal contexts under the translation.

@practice mc
prompt: Which postulate of counterpart theory says that nothing is in two worlds?
choice: CT1: $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Ixy \to Wy)$
choice*: CT2: $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,((Ixy \wedge Ixz) \to y = z)$
choice: CT5: $\forall x\,\forall y\,\forall z\,((Ixy \wedge Czx \wedge Izy) \to z = x)$
choice: CT6: $\forall x\,\forall y\,(Ixy \to Cxx)$
hint: The postulate says that if $x$ is in $y$ and in $z$, then $y$ and $z$ are one world.
solution: CT2 says that if $x$ is in world $y$ and in world $z$ then $y = z$, so nothing is in two worlds. CT1 says only worlds contain things, CT5 forbids counterparts within one world, and CT6 says each thing is its own counterpart.

@practice num
prompt: A thing $x$ has these counterparts in five worlds: $w_1$: $p_1, p_2$; $w_2$: none; $w_3$: $q$; $w_4$: $r_1, r_2, r_3$; $w_5$: $s$. The things with property $F$ are $p_1$, $q$, $r_2$ and $r_3$. In how many worlds are all of $x$'s counterparts in that world $F$ (counting a world with no counterparts, vacuously)?
answer: 2
unit: worlds
check: sum(1 for c in [['p1', 'p2'], [], ['q'], ['r1', 'r2', 'r3'], ['s']] if all(y in ['p1', 'q', 'r2', 'r3'] for y in c))
hint: A universal claim over no counterparts is true.
solution: In $w_1$, $p_2$ is not $F$. In $w_2$ there are no counterparts, so the claim is vacuously true. In $w_3$, $q$ is $F$. In $w_4$, $r_1$ is not $F$, and in $w_5$, $s$ is not $F$. So the worlds are $w_2$ and $w_3$, which is 2.

@practice self
prompt: Prove from the postulates: if $Cxy$, then either $x = y$ or $x$ and $y$ are in different worlds. State which postulates you use.
hint: CT3 and CT4 give worlds for $x$ and for $y$. Ask what happens if they are the same world.
solution: Assume $Cxy$. By CT3 there is a world $u$ with $Ixu$, and by CT4 there is a world $v$ with $Iyv$. Suppose $u = v$, so that $x$ and $y$ are in the same world. Then CT5, applied to $y$ in that world and to $x$ as a counterpart of $y$ in the same world, gives $x = y$. So if $x \neq y$ the worlds $u$ and $v$ differ. By CT2 each of $x$ and $y$ is in only one world, so "the world of $x$" and "the world of $y$" are well defined, and the conclusion is that counterparts that are not identical are located in different worlds.

@quiz mc
prompt: In the Barcan Marcus–Kripke argument for the necessity of identity, which premise does the Lewisian counterpart theorist reject?
choice*: P1: if $x = y$ and $\Box(x = x)$, then $\Box(x = y)$
choice: P2: for all $x$, $\Box(x = x)$
choice: That the conclusion follows from the premises
choice: That identity is reflexive
hint: The counterpart theorist accepts that every counterpart is identical to itself.
solution: The translation of $\Box(x = x)$ is true, so P2 holds. The argument is valid, so that is not what is rejected, and identity remains reflexive. @quiz num
prompt: In a model, worlds contain these things: $@$: $a, b$; $w_1$: $c, d, e$; $w_2$: $f$. Besides the reflexive pairs, the counterpart relation contains $(c,a)$, $(d,a)$, $(e,b)$, $(f,a)$, $(f,b)$. In how many worlds is there a counterpart of $a$ that is not also a counterpart of $b$?
answer: 2
unit: worlds
check: (lambda C: sum(1 for w in [['a', 'b'], ['c', 'd', 'e'], ['f']] if any(((y, 'a') in C) and ((y, 'b') not in C) for y in w)))({('a', 'a'), ('b', 'b'), ('c', 'c'), ('d', 'd'), ('e', 'e'), ('f', 'f'), ('c', 'a'), ('d', 'a'), ('e', 'b'), ('f', 'a'), ('f', 'b')})
hint: List the counterparts of $a$ and of $b$ in each world, remembering that each thing is its own counterpart.
solution: The counterparts of $a$ are $a$, $c$, $d$, $f$; of $b$ they are $b$, $e$, $f$. In $@$, $a$ is a counterpart of itself and not of $b$. In $w_1$, $c$ and $d$ are counterparts of $a$ only. In $w_2$, $f$ is a counterpart of both and there is nothing else. So the answer is 2: the worlds $@$ and $w_1$. (In $w_2$ the thing $f$ is a counterpart of both $a$ and $b$, so although $a \neq b$, the translation of $\Diamond(x = y)$ holds of $a$ and $b$.)
