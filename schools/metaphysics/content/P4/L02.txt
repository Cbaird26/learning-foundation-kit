@article
Ground mereology allows a chain with two elements: a thing with exactly one proper part. Most philosophers think that is wrong. If something is composed of parts and you remove one, something must be left over. Formal mereology captures this with **supplementation** principles, and then adds a principle saying when some things have a **sum**, or **fusion**. The result is the system that, for several decades, was taken to be *the* logic of parthood.

### Supplementation

| WSP, weak supplementation | $PPxy \to \exists z\,(Pzy \wedge \neg Ozx)$ |
| SSP, strong supplementation | $\neg Pxy \to \exists z\,(Pzx \wedge \neg Ozy)$ |

WSP says that a proper part of $y$ leaves a remainder: some part of $y$ is disjoint from it. SSP says that if $x$ is not part of $y$, then some part of $x$ lies wholly outside $y$. Adding WSP to M gives what the encyclopedia entry calls minimal mereology; adding SSP gives extensional mereology.

**Theorem 1.** M + SSP proves WSP.

*Proof.* Assume $PPxy$. Then $Pxy$ and $x \neq y$, so $\neg Pyx$ (otherwise antisymmetry gives $x = y$). Apply SSP with the roles reversed: from $\neg Pyx$ it gives some $z$ with $Pzy$ and $\neg Ozx$. That is WSP. $\square$

### Fusions and unrestricted composition

Let $\varphi$ be any formula (the plural-logic version comes in Lesson 5). Say that $z$ **is a fusion of the $\varphi$s** when the things that overlap $z$ are exactly the things that overlap some $\varphi$:

$$Fu(z,\varphi) := \forall w\,\big(Owz \leftrightarrow \exists x\,(\varphi x \wedge Owx)\big).$$

The **unrestricted composition** schema (UC) says that whenever something satisfies $\varphi$, the $\varphi$s have a fusion: $\exists x\,\varphi x \to \exists z\, Fu(z,\varphi)$. **Classical extensional mereology** is M + SSP + UC. A theorem from the literature, going back to Tarski, says that when UC is taken for arbitrary sets of elements, the models are exactly the complete Boolean algebras with the zero element deleted. So a finite model with $n$ atoms (parts with no proper parts) has exactly one element for each nonempty set of atoms: $2^n - 1$ elements. The element built from $k$ atoms has $2^k - 2$ proper parts.

### Fusions are unique

**Theorem 2.** In M + SSP, if $Fu(z,\varphi)$ and $Fu(z',\varphi)$ then $z = z'$.

*Proof.* We show $Pzz'$. Suppose not. By SSP there is $w$ with $Pwz$ and $\neg Owz'$. Since $w$ is part of $w$ and of $z$, $Owz$. By $Fu(z,\varphi)$ there is $x$ with $\varphi x$ and $Owx$. Because $Fu(z',\varphi)$ and $Owx$ with $\varphi x$, we get $Owz'$, a contradiction. So $Pzz'$, and by symmetry $Pz'z$. Antisymmetry gives $z = z'$. $\square$

**Theorem 3 (extensionality of proper parts).** In M + SSP, if $x$ and $y$ have a proper part and have exactly the same proper parts, then $x = y$.

*Proof.* Suppose $\neg Pxy$. By SSP some $z$ has $Pzx$ and $\neg Ozy$. If $z = x$, then $x$ does not overlap $y$; but $x$ has a proper part $v$, which is also a proper part of $y$ by hypothesis, so $v$ is a common part and $Oxy$, a contradiction. If $z \neq x$ then $z$ is a proper part of $x$, hence of $y$, so $Pzy$ and therefore $Ozy$, a contradiction. So $Pxy$, and symmetrically $Pyx$, so $x = y$. $\square$

### The argument from vagueness

The principle most philosophers find hard to accept is UC: it says that a hat and the Eiffel Tower have a fusion. A well-known argument for it, due in outline to Lewis and developed by Sider, runs as follows.

| P1 | Composition is unrestricted, or it is restricted. |
| P2 | If composition is restricted, then in some continuous series of cases, running from cases where some things compose something to cases where they do not, there is a sharp cutoff or it is vague whether some things compose something. |
| P3 | There is no sharp cutoff in any such series. |
| P4 | It is never vague whether some things compose something. |
| C | So composition is unrestricted. |

It is valid: from P2, P3 and P4 composition is not restricted, and P1 gives the conclusion. A restrictivist denies P3 (and accepts an unknowable or arbitrary cutoff) or denies P4. Van Inwagen, who answers the special composition question by saying that composition occurs only when the xs jointly constitute a life, accepts that composition can be vague, since life is vague. The formal lesson is that UC is the one axiom here that is not a logical triviality: it is a strong existence principle.

@example
title: Sums, products and differences in a finite model
problem: Take the CEM model with atoms $a$, $b$, $c$, whose elements are the nonempty sets of atoms ordered by inclusion. How many elements does it have? What are $\{a,b\} + \{b,c\}$, the common part of $\{a,b\}$ and $\{b,c\}$, and the part of $\{a,b\}$ that is disjoint from $\{b,c\}$?
step: The elements correspond to nonempty subsets of a 3-element set, so there are $2^3 - 1 = 7$.
step: A sum of two elements is their union: $\{a,b\} \cup \{b,c\} = \{a,b,c\}$. Check it is the fusion: anything overlapping it contains an atom of $\{a,b,c\}$, hence overlaps $\{a,b\}$ or $\{b,c\}$.
step: The two elements overlap, since they share $b$. Their common parts are the elements below both, namely $\{b\}$ alone, and its fusion is $\{b\}$.
step: The parts of $\{a,b\}$ disjoint from $\{b,c\}$ are the elements below $\{a,b\}$ that avoid $b$ and $c$, namely $\{a\}$.
answer: The model has 7 elements; the sum is $\{a,b,c\}$, the common part is $\{b\}$, and the disjoint remainder is $\{a\}$.

@example
title: A model where unrestricted composition fails
problem: Let the domain be $\{a,b,c,d\}$ where $d$ has exactly the proper parts $a$ and $b$, and $c$ is an atom disjoint from the rest. Show that UC fails.
step: Take $\varphi$ to be "is $a$ or is $c$". Both exist, so UC demands a fusion $z$ of them: everything overlapping $z$ must overlap $a$ or $c$, and conversely.
step: The candidates are $a$, $b$, $c$ and $d$. Test each against the condition $Owz \leftrightarrow (Owa \vee Owc)$.
step: Candidate $a$ fails with $w = c$: $Occ$ makes the right side true, but $c$ does not overlap $a$. Candidate $c$ fails with $w = a$ in the same way. Candidate $b$ fails with $w = a$.
step: Candidate $d$ fails with $w = b$: $b$ is part of $d$, so $Obd$, but $b$ overlaps neither $a$ nor $c$, so the right side is false.
answer: There is no fusion of $a$ and $c$, so this model of M violates UC.

@mistakes
- Treating the sum of two overlapping things as their disjoint union: $\{a,b\} + \{b,c\}$ is $\{a,b,c\}$, and the shared part is not counted twice.
- Counting the fusion of $k$ atoms as having $2^k$ proper parts: the empty set is not an element of a CEM model, and the whole is not a proper part of itself, so there are $2^k - 2$.
- Saying that extensionality is a theorem of ground mereology. It follows from strong supplementation, which M lacks.
- Reading unrestricted composition as a claim about *material* objects: as an axiom it quantifies over everything in the domain, abstract or concrete.

@practice self
prompt: Prove in M + SSP that if $Fu(z,\varphi)$ and $\varphi x$, then $Pxz$: every $\varphi$ is part of its fusion.
hint: Argue by contradiction and use strong supplementation to find a part of $x$ outside $z$.
solution: Assume $Fu(z,\varphi)$, $\varphi x$ and, for contradiction, $\neg Pxz$. By SSP there is $w$ with $Pwx$ and $\neg Owz$. But $w$ is part of $w$ and of $x$, so $Owx$, and since $\varphi x$ the fusion condition gives $Owz$ (the right-to-left direction of $Fu(z,\varphi)$ with witness $x$). This contradicts $\neg Owz$. So $Pxz$.
rubric: Assumes the negation of $Pxz$ and applies strong supplementation to get a witness $w$ disjoint from $z$.
rubric: Shows that $w$ overlaps $x$ (using reflexivity) and uses the right-to-left direction of the fusion condition to get $Owz$.
rubric: Derives the contradiction and states which axiom is essential.

@quiz self
prompt: Suppose a statue and its clay have exactly the same proper parts (the same atoms, say) and each has at least one proper part. Show that classical extensional mereology entails that the statue is the clay, and say which axiom you use and which premise a friend of the statue/clay distinction should reject.
hint: Apply the theorem about proper parts to the two objects, and then ask what the distinction needs.
solution: By Theorem 3, M + SSP proves that two things with a proper part and the same proper parts are identical. So the statue is the clay. The theorem depends on strong supplementation: in the proof, SSP is what produces a part of the statue lying outside the clay. A friend of the distinction must therefore reject strong supplementation (and keep, perhaps, only weak supplementation), accepting that there are distinct things with the same proper parts, or reject that the two have the same proper parts, saying that modal or sortal properties distinguish them without changing parts.
