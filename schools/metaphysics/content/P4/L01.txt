@article
A hand is part of an arm, and the arm is part of a body. Is the hand part of the body? Is every thing part of itself? Could two distinct things each be part of the other? Formal mereology answers such questions by taking **parthood** as a primitive and writing down axioms for it. In this course the primitive is a binary predicate $P$, with $Pxy$ read "$x$ is part of $y$". Parthood here is the **improper** relation, on which everything is part of itself.

### Ground mereology

The weakest system that is standardly called a mereology is **ground mereology**, written M in the literature (following Varzi's presentation in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry). Its axioms say that $P$ is a partial order:

| Reflexivity | $\forall x\, Pxx$ |
| Antisymmetry | $\forall x\forall y\,((Pxy \wedge Pyx) \to x = y)$ |
| Transitivity | $\forall x\forall y\forall z\,((Pxy \wedge Pyz) \to Pxz)$ |

The system is formulated in first-order logic with identity. Four defined notions do most of the work:

| Proper part | $PPxy := Pxy \wedge x \neq y$ |
| Overlap | $Oxy := \exists z\,(Pzx \wedge Pzy)$ |
| Underlap | $Uxy := \exists z\,(Pxz \wedge Pyz)$ |
| Disjointness | $x$ and $y$ are disjoint iff $\neg Oxy$ |

Given antisymmetry, $PPxy$ is equivalent to $Pxy \wedge \neg Pyx$, and some presentations take that as the definition. Standard references for what follows include Simons's *Parts* (1987) and Varzi's encyclopedia entry; the first axiomatic work in this tradition goes back to Leśniewski, and Leonard and Goodman gave an independent system, the calculus of individuals, in 1940.

### Three theorems with proofs

**Theorem 1.** In M, $PP$ is irreflexive, asymmetric and transitive.

*Proof.* Irreflexive: $PPxx$ would require $x \neq x$. Asymmetric: suppose $PPxy$ and $PPyx$. Then $Pxy$ and $Pyx$, so $x = y$ by antisymmetry, contradicting $x \neq y$. Transitive: suppose $PPxy$ and $PPyz$. Then $Pxz$ by transitivity of $P$. Suppose $x = z$. Then $Pzy$ (from $Pxy$) and $Pyz$, so $y = z$ by antisymmetry, which with $x = z$ contradicts $x \neq y$. So $x \neq z$ and $PPxz$. $\square$

**Theorem 2.** In M, $Pxy \leftrightarrow \forall z\,(Pzx \to Pzy)$: $x$ is part of $y$ exactly when every part of $x$ is part of $y$.

*Proof.* ($\to$) Suppose $Pxy$ and $Pzx$. Then $Pzy$ by transitivity. ($\leftarrow$) Suppose every part of $x$ is part of $y$. By reflexivity $Pxx$, so $Pxy$. $\square$

**Corollary.** $x = y \leftrightarrow \forall z\,(Pzx \leftrightarrow Pzy)$. Right to left: two applications of Theorem 2 give $Pxy$ and $Pyx$, and antisymmetry finishes. So in M, things with the same parts are identical. That is a triviality, not the controversial principle of **extensionality**, which concerns proper parts and comes in the next lesson.

**Theorem 3.** In M, $Pxy \to \forall z\,(Ozx \to Ozy)$. (Exercise.) Whatever overlaps a part of $y$ overlaps $y$ itself: overlap is monotone along parthood.

### What the axioms leave open

Ground mereology is deliberately weak, and small models show how weak. Take the domain $\{1,2,3,6\}$ with $Pxy$ read "$x$ divides $y$". This is a partial order, so it is a model of M. But $1$ is a part of everything, so everything overlaps everything, and $2$ has exactly one proper part, $1$. Nothing in the axioms forbids this: nothing says that a proper part must leave a remainder. The supplementation principles of Lesson 2 are added exactly to rule such models out. Overlap is not transitive either: in the domain $\{a,b,c\}$ with $a$ and $b$ both parts of $c$ and no other proper parthood, $a$ overlaps $c$ and $c$ overlaps $b$, but $a$ and $b$ are disjoint.

### An objection to transitivity

The axiom most often challenged is transitivity. The challenge, set out as an argument:

| P1 | If parthood is transitive, then since the handle is part of the door and the door is part of the house, the handle is part of the house. |
| P2 | The handle is part of the door and the door is part of the house. |
| P3 | The handle is not part of the house. |
| C | So parthood is not transitive. |

The argument is valid (modus tollens after P2 is used to detach the consequent of P1). The standard replies deny P3, saying the intuition is pragmatic: the handle is a part, though one would not say so. Or they say the English "part of" expresses several relations (functional, spatial, component of a kind), each transitive, and that the argument equivocates. A formal metaphysician should notice what the reply costs: it changes the subject from the English word to a regimented relation.

@example
title: Testing a relation against the axioms
problem: Let the domain be $\{a,b,c\}$ and let $R = \{(a,a),(b,b),(c,c),(a,b),(b,c)\}$, where $(x,y) \in R$ means $Pxy$. Is this a model of ground mereology? If not, add as few pairs as possible to make it one, and list the proper-part pairs.
step: Reflexivity: $(a,a)$, $(b,b)$ and $(c,c)$ are in $R$, so it holds.
step: Antisymmetry: the only pairs of distinct elements are $(a,b)$ and $(b,c)$, and neither $(b,a)$ nor $(c,b)$ is in $R$. So it holds.
step: Transitivity: $(a,b)$ and $(b,c)$ are in $R$, so $(a,c)$ is required, but it is missing. Transitivity fails, so $R$ is not a model.
step: Add $(a,c)$. The new pair creates no new chains that need closure, and no reverse pair, so all three axioms hold. The result is the three-element chain.
step: The proper-part pairs are those with distinct coordinates: $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$ and $(a,c)$, which is 3 pairs.
answer: $R$ is not a model (transitivity fails); adding $(a,c)$ gives the chain $a < b < c$, whose proper-part pairs are $(a,b)$, $(b,c)$, $(a,c)$.

@example
title: A mixed transitivity theorem
problem: Prove in ground mereology: if $PPxy$ and $Pyz$, then $PPxz$.
step: Assume $PPxy$ and $Pyz$. By the definition of proper part, $Pxy$ and $x \neq y$.
step: From $Pxy$ and $Pyz$, transitivity gives $Pxz$. It remains to show $x \neq z$.
step: Suppose for reductio that $x = z$. Then $Pyz$ becomes $Pyx$.
step: Now $Pxy$ and $Pyx$, so $x = y$ by antisymmetry, contradicting $x \neq y$.
step: So $x \neq z$, and with $Pxz$ this gives $PPxz$.
answer: $PPxy \wedge Pyz \to PPxz$ is a theorem of M, using transitivity and antisymmetry.

@mistakes
- Reading $P$ as proper parthood and then asking why reflexivity fails: in this course $P$ is improper parthood, and the proper relation $PP$ is defined from it.
- Treating overlap as transitive. Two things can each overlap a third while being disjoint from each other.
- Thinking ground mereology says that a thing with a proper part has a second, disjoint part. That is weak supplementation, which M does not contain.
- In proofs, using the definition of $PP$ without stating the second conjunct $x \neq y$, which is what antisymmetry is used against.

@practice mc

choice: Reflexivity
choice*: Antisymmetry
choice: Transitivity
choice: Weak supplementation

solution: Antisymmetry says that if $x$ is part of $y$ and $y$ is part of $x$ then $x = y$, so mutual parthood between distinct things is impossible. Reflexivity and transitivity are consistent with such pairs, and weak supplementation is not an axiom of ground mereology.

@practice num
prompt: A binary relation on the domain $\{1,2,3\}$ is a set of ordered pairs from the domain. How many of the $2^9 = 512$ such relations satisfy reflexivity, antisymmetry and transitivity (that is, are models of ground mereology on this labeled domain)?
answer: 19
unit: relations
check: sum(1 for b in product([0, 1], repeat=9) if (lambda R: all(R[i][i] for i in range(3)) and all(not (R[i][j] and R[j][i]) for i in range(3) for j in range(3) if i != j) and all((not (R[i][j] and R[j][k])) or R[i][k] for i in range(3) for j in range(3) for k in range(3)))([b[0:3], b[3:6], b[6:9]]))
hint: Reflexivity fixes the three diagonal pairs, leaving six off-diagonal pairs to decide, and antisymmetry allows at most one of each opposite pair.
hint: Count by the shape of the order: the discrete order, the orders with one comparable pair, and so on, remembering the labels.
solution: Sort the partial orders by shape. The antichain (no proper parthood) has 1 labeling. One proper-part pair with the third point isolated has 6 labelings (choose the ordered pair). A top with two incomparable parts has 3 labelings (choose the top), and a bottom with two incomparable wholes has 3 labelings (choose the bottom). The three-element chain has 6 labelings. The total is $1 + 6 + 3 + 3 + 6 = 19$, which a brute-force count over all 512 relations confirms.

@practice self
prompt: Prove Theorem 3 of ground mereology: if $Pxy$, then for every $z$, if $z$ overlaps $x$ then $z$ overlaps $y$. State which axioms you use.
hint: Unpack $Ozx$ into a witness $w$ and ask what you know about $w$ and $y$.
solution: Assume $Pxy$ and $Ozx$. By the definition of overlap there is $w$ with $Pwz$ and $Pwx$. From $Pwx$ and $Pxy$, transitivity gives $Pwy$. So $w$ is part of both $z$ and $y$, which is what $Ozy$ says. Only transitivity (and the definition of overlap) is used.
rubric: Unpacks the overlap hypothesis into an explicit witness $w$.
rubric: Applies transitivity to $Pwx$ and $Pxy$ to get $Pwy$ and concludes $Ozy$ from the definition.
rubric: States that the proof uses only transitivity, with no appeal to antisymmetry or to any supplementation principle.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which of these is a theorem of ground mereology?
choice*: $\forall x\forall y\,(PPxy \to \neg PPyx)$
choice: $\forall x\forall y\,(PPxy \to \exists z\,(Pzy \wedge \neg Ozx))$
choice: $\forall x\forall y\,(Oxy \to Pxy \vee Pyx)$
choice: $\forall x\forall y\,\exists z\,(Pxz \wedge Pyz \wedge \forall w\,((Pxw \wedge Pyw) \to Pzw))$
hint: Three of the four need supplementation or composition principles that M does not contain.
solution: Asymmetry of proper parthood is Theorem 1. The choice that adds a disjoint remainder is weak supplementation, which fails in the two-element chain. @quiz self
prompt: Give a model of ground mereology in which overlap is not transitive, and verify the three axioms and the failure of transitivity of overlap in it.
hint: Use three elements, one of which has the other two as its only proper parts.
solution: Domain $\{a,b,c\}$ with $P$ the reflexive pairs together with $(a,c)$ and $(b,c)$. Reflexivity holds by construction. Antisymmetry holds because no pair of distinct elements is related in both directions. Transitivity holds because the only chains $x < y < z$ would need a proper part of $a$ or $b$ or a proper whole of $c$, and there are none. Overlap: $a$ and $c$ share the part $a$, and $c$ and $b$ share the part $b$, but the parts of $a$ are $\{a\}$ and the parts of $b$ are $\{b\}$, so they share none: $Oac$ and $Ocb$ but $\neg Oab$.
