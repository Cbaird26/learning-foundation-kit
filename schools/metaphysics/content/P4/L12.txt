@article
A formal result that nobody can follow, or that is advertised as stronger than it is, helps no one. Philosophers who use formal methods are read by two audiences: formalists, who will check the proofs, and metaphysicians, who will ask what the proofs are for. Writing for both is a craft, and this lesson gives its main rules.

### The shape of a formal paper

A typical paper has five parts. The **motivation** states the philosophical question informally and says what a formal treatment would settle. The **framework** gives the language, the system and its axioms, the defined notions and the semantics, in that order, with each axiom numbered and named. The **results** state each theorem precisely and prove it, or cite a published proof by name. The **interpretation** says what the results mean for the question, with the assumptions on which that reading depends. The **objections** consider the best replies, including the one that denies the formalization's dictionary.

Three conventions make this work.

1. **Define before use, and once.** If "fusion" is defined by overlap, say so on first use, and do not slide to a definition in terms of parts. The dictionary from English to formulas is stated once and kept.
2. **Say which system, and which semantics.** "Classical extensional mereology" names M with strong supplementation and unrestricted composition. "Second-order logic" must be followed by *standard* or *Henkin* semantics, because the metalogical facts of Lesson 6 differ between them. For plural logic, say whether plural variables range over nonempty pluralities.
3. **Separate the formal from the philosophical.** A theorem is a statement about a system. What it means for the world requires a bridging premise, and the bridge should be named and defended.

### Stating results with the right strength

Compare three claims. "Composition is restricted" is a philosophical thesis. "In the model of intervals of consecutive atoms, the atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion" is a fact about a model, checkable by hand. "Unrestricted composition is not a theorem of M + SSP" is a consequence of the second, and it is what the model shows. The common error is to pass from the third to the first.

The same care is needed for proofs. Say which axioms a proof uses: "this uses transitivity only" is a result in itself, since it shows the theorem holds in weaker systems. Do not write "it can easily be shown" for the key lemma. Put routine verifications in an appendix, and keep in the main text the statement and the idea of the proof, so that a reader can follow the argument without checking every line.

### Models and tools

A countermodel is part of the argument and should be stated in the text: the domain, the relation or relations, and the verification of each axiom, or of its failure at one specified instance. Python is excellent for checking small models, as in Lessons 3 and 4, and a model finder such as Mace4 or a prover such as Prover9 can search for countermodels or proofs automatically; proof assistants such as Lean and Isabelle can verify long proofs. Whatever the tool, remember what a finite check can establish: a universal claim about all models cannot be proved by testing finitely many, but a single model can refute it. Say in the paper which claims were checked by machine, and give the model so that a reader need not rerun your code.

### Abstracts and referee reports

An abstract of about 150 words should state the question, the formal result in one sentence ("We prove that, in the presence of strong supplementation, fusions are unique") and the philosophical upshot, with its dependence on any contested premise. A **referee report** on such a paper checks four things, in order: the proofs (are the steps valid, and are the side conditions met?), the dictionary (does the formalization capture what the philosophers meant?), the strength of the claims (do the results show what is said?) and the upshot (does the result bear on the question?). A good report is specific, gives the line or lemma at issue and says what would fix it.

### Common errors

- Claiming "valid" when only "valid in the system" was shown.
- Using an axiom without listing it, which hides an assumption.
- Moving from "consistent with the axioms" to "true".
- Mixing syntactic and semantic consequence: $\vdash$ and $\vDash$ are not interchangeable without a completeness theorem.
- Dropping a side condition, such as the restriction in plural comprehension that $xx$ not be free in $\varphi$.

@example
title: Rewriting an overstated claim
problem: A draft says: "I have shown by computer that composition is not universal." The check was a Python test of the interval model on four atoms, in which atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion. Rewrite the sentence to say what was established.
step: Identify what was checked: a particular finite model, and a particular set of atoms in it.
step: Identify what follows: the model satisfies M and strong supplementation but not unrestricted composition, so unrestricted composition is not a consequence of those axioms.
step: Identify what does not follow: that composition is in fact restricted in the world, since that depends on whether the model represents the intended interpretation.
step: Write the sentence with the model stated.
answer: "In the model whose elements are the intervals of consecutive atoms from $\{1,2,3,4\}$ ordered by inclusion, which satisfies ground mereology and strong supplementation, the atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion; so unrestricted composition is independent of those axioms."

@example
title: Presenting a theorem in paper style
problem: State and prove in a form suitable for a paper: strong supplementation implies weak supplementation.
step: State the claim with its system and the definitions it uses: "In ground mereology, where $PPxy := Pxy \wedge x \neq y$, strong supplementation, $\neg Pxy \to \exists z\,(Pzx \wedge \neg Ozy)$, entails weak supplementation, $PPxy \to \exists z\,(Pzy \wedge \neg Ozx)$."
step: Give the proof compactly: "Assume $PPxy$. By antisymmetry $\neg Pyx$. Strong supplementation applied to $y$ and $x$ yields $z$ with $Pzy$ and $\neg Ozx$."
step: State what the proof uses: "The proof uses antisymmetry and strong supplementation only."
answer: A one-paragraph statement with the system named, the defined notions stated, a three-line proof and an explicit list of the axioms used.

@mistakes
- Using "shows" for what a model merely permits, or "proves" for what a program tested on a finite sample.
- Stating a theorem without naming the system, or naming a system ("second-order logic") without the semantics.
- Hiding the philosophical bridge premise inside the formal development, so that readers cannot tell the theorem from its interpretation.
- Putting every routine verification in the main text, so that the argument is lost, or putting the key lemma in the appendix, so that the argument cannot be followed.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite this paragraph from a draft in five or six precise sentences: "Mereology is formalized by Leśniewski-style axioms. As is well known, sums are unique. So the statue is identical to the clay. Someone might object, but a model shows that the objection fails."
hint: Name the system, state the theorem, say which premise bridges to the statue and the clay, and say what the model shows.
solution: Take classical extensional mereology, that is, ground mereology with strong supplementation and unrestricted composition, with parthood $P$ as the one primitive. In this system fusions are unique: if $z$ and $z'$ are both fusions of the same things, then $z = z'$ (this uses strong supplementation and antisymmetry). A related theorem says that things with a proper part and the same proper parts are identical. The bridging premise is that the statue and the clay have the same proper parts, and each has at least one; given that, the system entails that they are identical. A defender of the distinction must reject strong supplementation or the bridging premise. A model in which two distinct things have the same two atoms as proper parts satisfies ground mereology and weak supplementation but not strong supplementation, which shows that the distinction is consistent with the weaker axioms, though not with the classical system.
rubric: Names the system and states the theorem precisely, with the axioms it uses.
rubric: Makes the bridging premise about the statue and the clay explicit and separate from the theorem.
rubric: Says correctly what the model shows (consistency of the distinction with weaker axioms), not that the classical theorem is false.

@quiz num
prompt: In the classical extensional mereology with $5$ atoms, whose elements are the nonempty sets of atoms ordered by inclusion, how many ordered pairs $(x,y)$ of elements satisfy $PPxy$?
answer: 180
unit: pairs
check: sum(1 for x in range(1, 32) for y in range(1, 32) if x & y == x and x != y)
hint: Count the pairs with $x \subseteq y$ and then remove the pairs with $x = y$.
solution: Each atom is in neither set, in both, or only in the larger set, so $3^5 = 243$ ordered pairs of subsets satisfy $x \subseteq y$; remove the $2^5 = 32$ with $x$ empty, leaving $211$ pairs of elements with $Pxy$. The $31$ pairs with $x = y$ are reflexive, so $211 - 31 = 180$.

@quiz self
prompt: A fictional submission claims: "Theorem. In every model of ground mereology any two things have a sum. Proof. By reflexivity. Hence composition is universal." Write a referee report paragraph of four to six sentences.
hint: Check the theorem against a two-element model with no proper parthood, then the proof, then the upshot.
solution: The theorem is false as stated. In the two-element model with domain $\{a,b\}$ and parthood only the reflexive pairs, all three axioms of ground mereology hold, but $a$ and $b$ have no sum: any fusion would have to overlap both, and neither does. The proof is also invalid: reflexivity says only that each thing is part of itself, and says nothing about sums, which require a composition principle. The conclusion that composition is universal does not follow even if the theorem were right, since it concerns every plurality, not only pairs. I would ask the author to state which system is meant, since unrestricted composition holds in classical extensional mereology but only by axiom, and to separate that axiom from the claim about the world.
rubric: Gives a correct countermodel to the theorem and verifies that the axioms hold in it.
rubric: Identifies that the proof does not establish the claim, naming what is missing.
rubric: Notes that "any two things" falls short of "any things", and asks for the system and bridging claim to be stated.
