title: Formal analysis of an argument in metaphysics
overview: Choose one argument from a recent metaphysics paper, formalize it in the logic that fits (mereology, plural or higher-order logic, counterpart theory or a logic of grounding), prove its valid steps, test its questionable premises in models built by hand or in Python, and write a 3,000-word analysis of what the formalization shows. If you have no paper yet, you may use one of the three supplied arguments as a trial run.
goal: Turn an informal argument into a regimented one with a fixed dictionary, and settle its validity by proof or countermodel.
goal: Test the contested premises for independence from the axioms of the chosen system, by explicit models that a reader can check by hand.
goal: Report precisely what the formalization shows, and what it leaves open, in the style of a formal metaphysics paper.
step: Find a recent paper through the course reading, the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries for this course and PhilPapers, and choose one argument with a clear conclusion. Write down its premises in standard form with page references, and cite the paper.
step: Write the dictionary: which expressions become primitives, which become defined notions, and which logic and semantics you use (state standard or Henkin for second-order logic, and whether plural variables are nonempty). Choose the weakest logic adequate to the argument.
step: Regiment each premise and the conclusion as formulas. Prove that the argument is valid, line by line, or exhibit a countermodel and name the hidden premise that it exposes.
step: Choose the one or two most contested premises. For each, build a small model of the axioms in which the premise holds and one in which it fails, writing the domain and relations out in the text. Check them by hand and, where useful, with a short Python program in the style of Lessons 3 to 6, 9 and 10.
step: Decide what each result shows. For each theorem and model, write one sentence that says exactly what it establishes (a theorem of a named system, a fact about a stated model, an independence result) and one that says what it does not.
step: Draft the analysis of about 3,000 words: motivation, framework, results with proofs, models, interpretation and the best objection with your reply. Put routine verifications and code in an appendix.
step: Revise with the checklist of Lesson 12: define before use, name the system and the semantics, separate theorems from interpretation, list the axioms each proof uses, and state each model so that a reader can check it without running your code.
deliverable: A written analysis of about 3,000 words, with the argument in standard form, the dictionary, the regimentation and the proofs.
deliverable: An appendix with each model written out (domain and relations), your code and its output, and the verification of any axiom you claim a model satisfies.
deliverable: A one-paragraph abstract of about 150 words stating the formal result and its philosophical upshot.
rubric: The regimentation is faithful to the paper's argument, with a stated dictionary, and its validity is established by a correct proof or an explicit countermodel.
rubric: Every theorem and proof is correct, and each proof states which axioms or principles it uses.
rubric: The models are explicit and verified, and each is correctly described as showing independence or consistency, never truth of the intended interpretation.
rubric: The claims are stated with exactly the strength the results support, and the formal results are kept separate from the philosophical interpretation.
rubric: The analysis considers the strongest objection, including one to the dictionary, and replies to it.
material: **Trial argument A (mereology).** P1: The atoms of the table, the $aa$, compose the table. P2: The $aa$ also compose the lump of matter that constitutes the table. P3: Whatever things compose, they compose at most one thing. C: So the table is the lump. Hints: define composition by overlap, regiment with plural variables, and test P3 in a model with two wholes that have the same two atoms as proper parts.
material: **Trial argument B (second-order logic).** P1: A set of axioms fixes its subject matter under a semantics if and only if it has, up to isomorphism, exactly one model under that semantics. P2: The second-order Peano axioms have, up to isomorphism, exactly one model under the standard semantics. P3: Under Henkin semantics the second-order Peano axioms have a model not isomorphic to the natural numbers. C: So the second-order Peano axioms fix their subject matter under the standard semantics but not under Henkin semantics. Hints: prove the validity, then explain why P2 is a theorem of the metatheory and what the metatheory itself assumes.
material: **Trial argument C (grounding).** P1: If ground were entailment, then $A \wedge B$ would ground $A$. P2: $A$ is a partial ground of $A \wedge B$. P3: If $A$ is a partial ground of $A \wedge B$, then $A \wedge B$ does not ground $A$. C: So ground is not entailment. Hints: use the logic of Lesson 10, say what asymmetry is assumed, and build a finite ground structure in which entailment pairs form a cycle but ground pairs do not.

@review
### Week by week

- **Weeks 1 and 2: ground and classical mereology.** $P$ is a partial order: reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive. Defined notions: $PP$, overlap $O$, underlap, disjointness. Supplementation: WSP and SSP; SSP implies WSP. Fusions defined by overlap; unrestricted composition (UC); fusions are unique given SSP; proper-part extensionality. Models of CEM: complete Boolean algebras with zero removed; a model with $n$ atoms has $2^n - 1$ elements.
- **Weeks 3 and 4: finite models, gunk and junk.** Representing $(D, P)$ in Python and checking axioms with `all` and `any`. Atoms, atomism, gunk, junk. Every finite model of ground mereology is atomistic; UC rules out junk; the dyadic intervals form a gunky model of SSP that fails UC; the statue-style model satisfies WSP and fails SSP, with two fusions of the same atoms.
- **Weeks 5 and 6: plural and second-order logic.** Plural comprehension, plural variables over nonempty subsets, the Geach–Kaplan sentence, plural composition. Second-order syntax; full versus Henkin semantics; categoricity, failure of compactness and Löwenheim–Skolem, and incompleteness for full semantics; completeness and compactness for Henkin semantics; finite domains collapse the difference.
- **Weeks 7 and 8: higher-order logic and counterpart theory.** Simple type theory, beta- and eta-conversion, sizes of full frames $\lvert D_\tau\rvert^{\lvert D_\sigma\rvert}$. The Quinean and Cantorian arguments. Counterpart theory: the eight postulates, the translation of $\Diamond$, the necessity of identity and why it fails.
- **Weeks 9 and 10: hyperintensionality and ground.** Intensional versus hyperintensional operators; possible-worlds operators are intensional; impossible worlds and belief; the logic of strict full ground: irreflexivity, factivity, cut, asymmetry; ground is hyperintensional.
- **Weeks 11 and 12: practice.** The workflow for formalizing a published argument; independence versus truth; stating results with the right strength.

### Key arguments

The argument from vagueness for UC; the argument from possible junk against UC; Boolos's argument that plural quantification is not quantification over sets; the Quinean argument for ontological commitment of higher-order quantifiers and its Cantorian counterpart; the Barcan Marcus–Kripke argument for the necessity of identity; the logical omniscience argument; the hyperintensionality of ground from $A < A \vee A$.

### Self-check

2. Prove SSP implies WSP and that fusions are unique. Give a model of WSP and not SSP.
3. Count the elements, atoms and proper-part pairs of a finite model.
4. Translate "some critics admire only one another" into plural logic. Evaluate it on a small model.
5. State what changes between full and Henkin semantics, and why finite domains do not distinguish them.
6. Type a term, beta-reduce it, and count the objects of a given type in a full frame.

### Common errors

Treating $P$ as proper parthood; calling gunk inconsistent with CEM; forgetting that plural variables are nonempty; saying "second-order logic is incomplete" without naming the semantics; swapping the exponent in $\lvert D_\tau\rvert^{\lvert D_\sigma\rvert}$; reading $\Box(x = y)$ as having one counterpart for both variables; using recursion at an impossible world; treating a model as showing a premise false rather than independent.

@exam num
prompt: In the classical extensional mereology with $4$ atoms, whose $15$ elements are the nonempty sets of atoms ordered by inclusion, how many nonempty sets $S$ of elements (pluralities) have the largest element, the whole four-atom set, as a fusion in the overlap sense?
answer: 32297
unit: pluralities
check: sum(1 for m in range(1, 2 ** 15) if all(any(((m >> (s - 1)) & 1) and (s & bit) for s in range(1, 16)) for bit in (1, 2, 4, 8)))
hint: Every element overlaps the whole, so the condition says that every atom lies in some member of $S$.
hint: Count families covering all four atoms by inclusion–exclusion over the set of atoms that are missed.
solution: The elements avoiding $k$ given atoms are the nonempty subsets of the remaining $4-k$ atoms, so number $2^{4-k} - 1$, and a family of such elements can be chosen in $2^{2^{4-k}-1}$ ways. By inclusion–exclusion the count is $2^{15} - 4 \cdot 2^{7} + 6 \cdot 2^{3} - 4 \cdot 2^{1} + 2^{0} = 32768 - 512 + 48 - 8 + 1 = 32297$.

@exam ms
prompt: Select every true statement.
choice*: On a finite domain, a Henkin model of second-order logic that satisfies comprehension with parameters has every subset of the domain in the range of a monadic variable.
choice: Full second-order logic is compact.
choice*: In a full frame over a domain of $3$ individuals, the type $e \to t$ has $8$ objects.
choice*: Under the standard semantics, $x = y$ is equivalent to $\forall X\,(Xx \leftrightarrow Xy)$.
choice: Full second-order consequence has a sound and complete effective proof system.
hint: Recall which metalogical properties belong to the standard semantics and which to Henkin semantics.
solution: Finite Henkin models with comprehension are full, because each singleton and each finite union of singletons is definable with parameters. The type $e \to t$ has $2^3 = 8$ objects. Leibniz's definition works under the standard semantics because the singleton of $x$ is in the range of $X$. Full second-order logic is not compact (finiteness is expressible), and it has no effective sound and complete proof system.

@exam self
prompt: (a) Prove in ground mereology that strong supplementation implies weak supplementation. (b) Give a finite model of ground mereology and weak supplementation in which strong supplementation fails, verifying each claim. (c) In three or four sentences, say what (a) and (b) together show about someone who holds that a statue and its clay are distinct but have the same proper parts, and what they do not show.
hint: For (b), take two atoms and two distinct wholes that each have exactly those atoms as proper parts.
solution: (a) Assume $PPxy$, so $Pxy$ and $x \neq y$. By antisymmetry $\neg Pyx$. Strong supplementation with the roles reversed gives $z$ with $Pzy$ and $\neg Ozx$, which is weak supplementation. (b) Domain $\{a,b,x,y\}$ with the reflexive pairs and $(a,x)$, $(b,x)$, $(a,y)$, $(b,y)$. Reflexivity holds by construction, antisymmetry holds because no two distinct elements are related both ways, and transitivity holds because no chain of two distinct steps exists. Weak supplementation: each proper part pair is $(a,x)$, $(b,x)$, $(a,y)$ or $(b,y)$, and the other atom is a part of the whole disjoint from the part. Strong supplementation fails for $x$ and $y$: $\neg Pxy$, but every part of $x$ ($a$, $b$, $x$) overlaps $y$. (c) The model shows that the distinction between the statue and the clay is consistent with ground mereology and weak supplementation, so a defender can keep both and reject strong supplementation, at the price of non-unique fusions. It does not show that the statue and the clay are distinct, nor that strong supplementation is false; it shows only that the axioms permit it.
rubric: Gives a correct proof of (a), using antisymmetry and strong supplementation with the roles reversed.
rubric: Gives an explicit model for (b) and verifies the axioms, weak supplementation and the failure of strong supplementation.
rubric: States correctly in (c) what the model shows (consistency with weaker axioms) and what it does not (that the distinction is true or that SSP is false).
