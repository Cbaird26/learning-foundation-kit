@article
The fact that the cup is red and round is grounded in the fact that it is red and the fact that it is round, and not the other way about. Ground is the relation of *in virtue of*, or metaphysical explanation, and the literature on it takes a **logical** approach: write down principles for a ground operator, and ask what they imply. The best-known systematic treatment is Fine's "Guide to Ground", with a pure logic of ground developed by him and, in a different style, by Schnieder in "A Logic for 'Because'". Rosen's article on metaphysical dependence is a standard source for the metaphysical background.

### The vocabulary

Write $\Gamma < A$ for "the sentences in $\Gamma$ together **strictly fully ground** $A$", with $\Gamma$ a finite list; the single-premise case is $B < A$. Several distinctions matter:

| full vs partial | $B$ is a **partial** ground of $A$ when $B$ belongs to some $\Gamma$ with $\Gamma < A$ |
| strict vs weak | strict ground rules out $B < B$; weak ground allows it |
| immediate vs mediate | $\Gamma < A$ is **immediate** when no intermediate grounding step lies between $\Gamma$ and $A$ |

The standard introduction principles for the connectives are: $A, B < A \wedge B$; $A < A \vee B$ and $B < A \vee B$; and $A < \neg\neg A$. Note the direction: a conjunction entails its conjuncts, but does not ground them.

### Core principles

| Irreflexivity | If $\Gamma < A$ then $A$ is not among the members of $\Gamma$; in particular $\neg(A < A)$ |
| Factivity | If $\Gamma < A$ then $A$ and every member of $\Gamma$ are true |
| Cut | If $\Gamma < A$ and $A, \Delta < B$ then $\Gamma, \Delta < B$ |

Cut is the transitivity principle in its general, many-premise form. Some philosophers have challenged transitivity with putative counterexamples, among them Schaffer in "Grounding, Transitivity, and Contrastivity"; most of the logical work assumes cut.

**Theorem 1 (asymmetry).** If $A < B$ then $\neg(B < A)$.

*Proof.* Suppose $A < B$ and $B < A$. Apply cut to $A < B$ (so $\Gamma = \{A\}$) and $B < A$ (so $\Delta$ is empty and the middle sentence is $B$). The result is $A < A$, which contradicts irreflexivity. $\square$

**Theorem 2 (no cycles).** There are no sentences $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ with $A_1 < A_2$, $A_2 < A_3, \ldots, A_{n-1} < A_n$ and $A_n < A_1$.

*Proof.* Chain the first $n - 1$ conditions by cut to get $A_1 < A_n$. With $A_n < A_1$ cut gives $A_1 < A_1$, contradicting irreflexivity. $\square$

So single-premise strict ground is a strict partial order. Whether it must also be **well-founded**, so that every chain of grounds ends, is a further question that the principles do not settle. In a finite structure the answer is automatic, for the reason shown in Lesson 4 for parthood.

### Ground is hyperintensional

| P1 | $A < A \vee A$ (a disjunct grounds the disjunction). |
| P2 | $A$ and $A \vee A$ are necessarily equivalent. |
| P3 | It is not the case that $A < A$ (irreflexivity). |
| C | So ground is hyperintensional: replacing $A \vee A$ by the necessarily equivalent $A$ in a true ground claim gives a false one. |

The argument is valid. Its premises are the disjunction principle, an elementary fact about classical logic, and irreflexivity. A reply denies P1, holding that a disjunction of a sentence with itself is not grounded in that sentence, or accepts the conclusion and notes that it is welcome, since ground is meant to be sensitive to the structure of facts, not only to their modal profile. Compare Lesson 9: here it is the ground relation, not belief, whose logic needs more than possible worlds. Fine's semantics for the pure logic of ground uses a truthmaker framework for that reason, and Schaffer's "Grounding in the image of causation" proposes instead to model ground by structural equations in the style of causal models.

### Finite ground structures

For computation, model the single-premise fragment: a finite set $F$ of facts and a relation $G \subseteq F \times F$ with $(x,y) \in G$ meaning "$x$ is an immediate partial ground of $y$". **Mediate partial ground** is the transitive closure $G^+$. A fact is **fundamental** if it has no ground, that is, nothing is paired with it as the second member. The structure satisfies irreflexivity and the no-cycles theorem exactly when $G^+$ is irreflexive, that is, when $G$ is **acyclic**.

```python
def closure(G):
    """Transitive closure of a set of pairs."""
    C = set(G)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for (a, b) in list(C):
            for (c, d) in list(C):
                if b == c and (a, d) not in C:
                    C.add((a, d))
                    changed = True
    return C
```

This loop adds a pair whenever two pairs chain, and stops when nothing new appears; on a finite set it terminates, because at most $\lvert F\rvert^2$ pairs exist.

@example
title: Closure and fundamental facts
problem: Facts $a, b, c, d, e$ with immediate partial grounds $G = \{(a,c), (b,c), (c,e), (d,e)\}$. Compute the closure, the fundamental facts and test for cycles.
step: Chains: $(a,c)$ and $(c,e)$ give $(a,e)$; $(b,c)$ and $(c,e)$ give $(b,e)$. No other chains: $d$ and $e$ have nothing that continues from $e$.
step: So $G^+$ has the four original pairs plus $(a,e)$ and $(b,e)$, which is $6$ pairs.
step: Fundamental facts are those never in second position: $a$, $b$, $d$. The facts $c$ and $e$ are grounded.
step: No pair has equal coordinates, so $G^+$ is irreflexive: $G$ is acyclic and the structure satisfies irreflexivity.
answer: $G^+$ has 6 pairs; the fundamental facts are $a, b, d$; there are no cycles.

@example
title: A ground structure that violates the principles
problem: Facts $x, y, z$ with $G = \{(x,y), (y,z), (z,x)\}$. Which principle fails, and what does the closure look like?
step: Chain: $(x,y)$, $(y,z)$ give $(x,z)$; $(y,z)$, $(z,x)$ give $(y,x)$; $(z,x)$, $(x,y)$ give $(z,y)$.
step: Then $(x,z)$ and $(z,x)$ give $(x,x)$, and similarly $(y,y)$ and $(z,z)$. The closure contains all $9$ pairs.
step: The closure is not irreflexive: each fact partially grounds itself, which violates irreflexivity.
step: By Theorem 2 a ground relation cannot contain such a cycle, so the structure is not a model of the logic. Nor does it have a fundamental fact: every fact is grounded.
answer: Irreflexivity fails; the closure is the full $3 \times 3$ relation and no fact is fundamental.

@mistakes
- Treating a conjunction as grounding its conjuncts. $A \wedge B$ entails $A$ but is grounded by $A, B$: ground is not entailment.
- Reading ground as causation, or as supervenience. Ground is irreflexive and hyperintensional, whereas necessitation is reflexive and intensional.
- Confusing a cycle in the closure with a cycle in $G$. They coincide: if $G^+$ has a loop, then $G$ has a cycle.
- Assuming that irreflexivity and cut give well-foundedness. They do not: an infinite descending sequence of grounds satisfies both, and finite models satisfy it automatically.

A defender of intensionality cannot deny P2, which is a fact of classical logic. They must deny P1, saying that a disjunct does not ground a disjunction of that disjunct with itself, or deny P3 and accept that some facts ground themselves, giving up irreflexivity.
```python
def is_acyclic(G):
    """No chain of pairs returns to its starting fact."""
    C = set(G)
    changed = True
    while changed:
        changed = False
        for (a, b) in list(C):
            for (c, d) in list(C):
                if b == c and (a, d) not in C:
                    C.add((a, d))
                    changed = True
    return all(a != b for (a, b) in C)
```
hint: A relation has a cycle exactly when its transitive closure contains a pair `(x, x)`.
hint: Compute the closure by repeatedly adding composite pairs, then look for a loop.
solution: Compute the transitive closure and check that no fact is paired with itself. A cycle of any length produces such a loop in the closure, and a self-loop is a loop already. The second test is a three-cycle, and the third a self-loop.
