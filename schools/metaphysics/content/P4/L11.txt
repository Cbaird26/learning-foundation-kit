@article
The previous lessons gave you tools; this one uses them in the order a referee would. The case study is the argument from vagueness for unrestricted composition, a classic of the literature rather than a recent paper, but the workflow is the one to follow for the recent paper you choose for your project.

### A workflow

1. **Isolate** the argument: premises, conclusion and the sentences that carry them, in standard form, with page references.
2. **Fix a dictionary**: which English expressions are formalized by which primitives or defined terms, written down once.
3. **Choose the weakest adequate logic**: here plural first-order logic with mereology, because the argument quantifies over arbitrary pluralities.
4. **Check validity** by a proof, or exhibit a countermodel and find the hidden premise it exposes.
5. **Test the contested premises**: build models of the axioms in which a premise holds and in which it fails, to see whether it is independent of them.
6. **Report** what the formalization shows (a validity result, an independence result) and what it leaves open (the truth of the premise).

### Regimentation

Use $P$ for parthood, $Oxy$ for overlap, and the plural variables of Lesson 5. The dictionary is:

| $Fu(z,xx)$ | $\forall w\,(Owz \leftrightarrow \exists y\,(y \prec xx \wedge Owy))$: $z$ is a fusion of the $xx$ |
| $Comp(xx)$ | $\exists z\,Fu(z,xx)$: the $xx$ compose something |
| Unrestricted composition | $\forall xx\,Comp(xx)$ |
| Restricted composition | $\exists xx\,\neg Comp(xx)$ |

The restrictivist about composition holds that some things do not compose anything. The argument of Lewis, developed by Sider, takes a **series of cases** $c_0, c_1, \ldots, c_N$: pluralities that run from one the restrictivist agrees composes something (the atoms of a table, say) to one she says does not (the same atoms scattered across the universe), where each case differs from the next in a tiny way (moving one atom a little).

| P1 | $Comp(c_0)$. |
| P2 | For every $i < N$: $Comp(c_i) \to Comp(c_{i+1})$. |
| C | $Comp(c_N)$. |

### Validity, and what the restrictivist must do

The argument is valid, for each $k \leq N$ by induction: $Comp(c_0)$ by P1; if $Comp(c_k)$ and $k < N$ then P2 gives $Comp(c_{k+1})$ by modus ponens. So $Comp(c_N)$. The restrictivist, who accepts P1 and asserts $\neg Comp(c_N)$, has therefore to deny P2: for some $i$, $Comp(c_i) \wedge \neg Comp(c_{i+1})$. That is a **sharp cutoff**. The case for P2 is not part of the formal argument. It rests on the claim that composition cannot be vague, and that a sharp cutoff would be arbitrary; Sider defended the first claim in "Against Vague Existence", arguing that the quantifiers cannot be vague. This is where the philosophy lives, and where the formalization marks it: the sentences needing support are exactly P2 and the premise about the vagueness of $Comp$.

### Testing a premise in a model

Is P2 forced by the mereological axioms themselves? No. Consider atoms $1, 2, 3, 4$ and let the elements be the **intervals** of consecutive atoms: $\{1\}, \{1,2\}, \ldots, \{1,2,3,4\}$, ten in all, with $P$ as inclusion. This is a model of M and of strong supplementation: if $x \not\subseteq y$, some atom of $x$ lies outside $y$, and that atom is a part of $x$ disjoint from $y$. Composition is restricted: the atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion, because no interval has exactly the atoms $1$ and $3$ as the atoms it overlaps. Take the series $c_0 = \{1,2\}$ and $c_1 = \{1,3\}$. Then $Comp(c_0)$, since $\{1,2\}$ is a fusion, while $\neg Comp(c_1)$. So P2 fails in a model of the axioms with $N = 1$.

What this shows: P2 is **independent** of the axioms M and SSP; the argument's conclusion cannot be reached from them. What it does not show: that P2 is false in the intended interpretation, or that composition is in fact restricted. The model is a Python-checkable object, but the claim is a hand-checkable one, and a good write-up states the domain and relation so a reader can verify it.

### What the formalization shows

Three things. First, the argument is a **sorites**, with the same logical structure and the same menu of responses: sharp cutoff, vague composition, or acceptance of the conclusion. Second, the formal content of P2 is a statement about a precisely defined predicate $Comp$, which is why the argument turns on whether a predicate defined from parthood and quantifiers can be vague. Third, the restrictivist's burden is exactly one of the two sentences $Comp(c_i) \wedge \neg Comp(c_{i+1})$, a sharp cutoff, or a rejection of bivalence for $Comp(c_i)$; supervaluationism on $Comp$ makes P2 superfalse and the existence of a cutoff supertrue, just as for "heap".

@example
title: Counting tolerance-respecting patterns
problem: A series has $6$ cases $c_0, \ldots, c_5$. How many assignments of truth values to $Comp(c_0), \ldots, Comp(c_5)$ satisfy P2? How many of those also satisfy P1, and how many also make $Comp(c_5)$ false?
step: P2 says each true value is followed by a true value. So a satisfying pattern is a run of false values followed by a run of true values (either run may be empty).
step: Such a pattern is determined by where the true run begins: at position $0, 1, \ldots, 5$, or never. That is $7$ patterns.
step: P1 requires $Comp(c_0)$ true, which forces the true run to start at position $0$: exactly $1$ pattern, the all-true one.
step: That pattern makes $Comp(c_5)$ true, so no pattern satisfies P1, P2 and $\neg Comp(c_5)$.
answer: 7 patterns satisfy P2; 1 satisfies P1 and P2; 0 satisfy P1, P2 and $\neg Comp(c_5)$, which is the validity of the argument.

@example
title: Checking independence in the interval model
problem: In the interval model on atoms $1$ to $4$, does the set of atoms $\{2,4\}$ have a fusion? Does $\{2,3\}$?
step: A fusion of a set of atoms $S$ is an interval $z$ such that every element overlapping $z$ overlaps some member of $S$ and conversely. Elements overlap exactly when they share an atom, so this says that the atoms of $z$ are exactly the members of $S$.
step: For $S = \{2,4\}$ the only candidate would contain atoms $2$ and $4$ and nothing else, but any interval containing $2$ and $4$ also contains $3$. So there is none.
step: For $S = \{2,3\}$ the interval $\{2,3\}$ has exactly those atoms.
answer: $\{2,4\}$ has no fusion; $\{2,3\}$ has the fusion $\{2,3\}$.

@mistakes
- Reporting a countermodel to a premise as a refutation of it. A model shows that the premise is not entailed by the axioms; it says nothing about the intended interpretation.
- Treating the argument as invalid because the premises are controversial. The argument is valid; the work is in P2.
- Formalizing with a quantifier over sets of atoms when the argument concerns the atoms themselves, which brings in set-theoretic assumptions the author did not make. Use plural quantification.
- Leaving the dictionary implicit, so that "compose" shifts between the overlap definition and another definition in different steps.

@practice num
prompt: A series has $8$ cases $c_0, \ldots, c_7$, and each case is assigned $1$ ($Comp$ holds) or $0$. How many assignments satisfy $Comp(c_i) \to Comp(c_{i+1})$ for every $i < 7$?
answer: 9
unit: assignments
check: sum(1 for b in product([0, 1], repeat=8) if all((not b[i]) or b[i + 1] for i in range(7)))
hint: A pattern satisfying the conditionals is a run of zeros followed by a run of ones.
solution: The pattern is fixed by the position at which the run of ones begins, which can be any of the $8$ positions or nowhere, giving $8 + 1 = 9$ patterns.

prompt: In the model whose elements are the intervals of consecutive atoms from $\{1,2,3,4\}$ ordered by inclusion, which claims are true?
choice*: Strong supplementation holds.
choice*: The atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion.
choice*: The atoms $1$ and $2$ have a fusion.
choice: Every plurality of elements has a fusion.

hint: A fusion of atoms must be an interval with exactly those atoms.
solution: If an element is not part of another, one of its atoms lies outside the other and is a disjoint part, so strong supplementation holds. The atoms $1$ and $3$ have no fusion since any interval containing both contains $2$, whereas $\{1,2\}$ is the fusion of the atoms $1$ and $2$. Unrestricted composition fails, and fusions, where they exist, are unique by strong supplementation.

@quiz num
prompt: In the interval model on atoms $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ there is a fusion of a nonempty set of atoms exactly when the set consists of consecutive atoms. How many nonempty sets of atoms have no fusion?
answer: 16
unit: sets
check: sum(1 for r in range(1, 6) for S in itertools.combinations(range(1, 6), r) if max(S) - min(S) + 1 != len(S))
hint: Count all nonempty sets of atoms, and then those made of consecutive atoms.
solution: There are $2^5 - 1 = 31$ nonempty sets of atoms. The consecutive ones are determined by their first and last atoms: $5 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 15$. So $31 - 15 = 16$ sets have no fusion.

@quiz self
prompt: Write the "what the formalization shows" paragraph (five to seven sentences) for a report on the argument from vagueness: state what you proved, what the interval model shows, and what remains philosophically open.
hint: Separate the validity result, the independence result and the open question about the vagueness of composition.
solution: The argument from $Comp(c_0)$ and the tolerance premise to $Comp(c_N)$ is valid, by induction on the series of cases, so anyone who accepts the first case and denies the last must reject a tolerance instance, that is, accept a sharp cutoff. The tolerance premise is not a consequence of ground mereology or of strong supplementation: in the model of intervals of consecutive atoms, composition is restricted and the series from $\{1,2\}$ to $\{1,3\}$ has a cutoff, so the axioms alone do not give unrestricted composition. The formalization therefore locates the argument's whole force in the claims that composition cannot be vague and that a cutoff would be arbitrary. Those claims concern the logical vocabulary used to define $Comp$ and the theory of vagueness, and the models here are silent on them. What is open is whether existence and parthood claims can be vague; if not, the restrictivist faces a sharp cutoff, and if so, the argument's premise about vagueness fails.

rubric: States the independence result from the interval model accurately, without claiming it refutes the premise.
rubric: Names the open philosophical questions: vague existence or composition, and the arbitrariness of cutoffs.
