@article
Hume said that we find no necessity in the objects when we say that one event causes another. If he is right, how can we know that every event has a cause? Kant's answer is that the principle of causality is not a generalization from experience, but a condition of the possibility of experience itself. The same move produces his account of the limits of metaphysics.

### The categories

Where sensibility supplies intuitions, the **understanding** supplies concepts, and its basic concepts are the **categories**: pure concepts of the understanding that apply a priori to the objects of experience. Kant derives twelve from the logical forms of judgment (the "metaphysical deduction"), arranged under four headings: quantity, quality, relation and modality. Under relation come substance and accident, cause and effect, and community. They are not drawn from experience, as Hume's copy principle would require, but are the forms in which objects are thought.

### The Transcendental Deduction

The Deduction asks by what right (*quid juris*) the categories apply to objects. In the B edition, the argument starts from the unity of apperception: the "I think" must be able to accompany all my representations (B131). What follows is a simplified and disputed reconstruction, not Kant's own wording:

| P1 | All my representations must be able to belong to one consciousness (the "I think" can accompany each). |
| P2 | That unity requires that the manifold of intuition be combined according to rules that make it represent a single object. |
| P3 | The categories are those rules: the basic forms of judgment about objects. |
| C | So the categories apply necessarily to every object of possible experience. |

How best to reconstruct the Deduction is one of the most disputed questions in Kant scholarship, and this is only an outline.

### The Second Analogy: Kant's answer to Hume

The Second Analogy (B232 ff.) states that all alterations take place according to the law of the connection of cause and effect. Kant's argument turns on a distinction between two kinds of succession in perception. My perceptions of the parts of a house can follow in any order, but my perceptions of a ship going downstream are in a fixed order that I cannot reverse. To represent the second as an objective event, and not merely as a succession of my states, the order must be represented as necessary, and that is to bring it under a rule of cause and effect. So the causal principle is a condition of experiencing objective change. Hume was right that necessity is not found in the impressions, says Kant, but wrong to conclude that the concept of cause is empty: it is supplied by the understanding. A standard objection is Strawson's charge, in *The Bounds of Sense* (1966), that the argument contains a non sequitur: from the premise that experiencing an objective succession requires the order of my perceptions to be fixed by how things are, and not by my whim, Kant moves to the conclusion that the events themselves must be causally necessitated, which does not follow. A related worry is that the argument seems to show only that we must take events to be rule-governed in some way, not that every event falls under a particular causal law; and Kant does not by this answer Hume's question about whether the future will resemble the past.

### The Dialectic

The Transcendental Dialectic argues that reason, seeking the **unconditioned** behind every conditioned thing, forms ideas of the soul, the world as a whole, and God, and falls into illusion when it takes them as objects of knowledge. Kant's targets are **rational psychology** (the *Paralogisms*, against the claim that the soul is a simple substance), **rational cosmology** (the *Antinomies*) and **rational theology** (the Ideal of Pure Reason, with his criticisms of the ontological, cosmological and physico-theological arguments). The ideas have a legitimate **regulative** use, guiding inquiry toward systematic unity, but not a **constitutive** use as sources of knowledge about objects.

### The antinomies

Reason is pushed into four **antinomies**, pairs of arguments with opposed conclusions. The first: the **thesis** is that the world has a beginning in time and is limited in space; the **antithesis** is that it has no beginning and no limit. Kant's proof of the thesis, in outline:

| P1 | Suppose the world has no beginning in time. |
| P2 | Then an infinite series of states has elapsed up to the present moment. |
| P3 | An infinite series cannot be completed by successive synthesis. |
| C | So the world has a beginning in time. |

The proof of the antithesis supposes that the world has a beginning and argues that there would then be an empty time before it, in which nothing could distinguish one moment as the moment at which something arises rather than another; so there is no such beginning. The other three antinomies concern whether matter is made of simple parts, whether there is freedom beside natural causality, and whether there is a necessary being.

Kant's resolution relies on transcendental idealism. The first two, the **mathematical** antinomies, both presuppose that the world is a whole of existing things in themselves. Kant says that the world of appearances is not given as a whole, so both thesis and antithesis are false. In the last two, the **dynamical** antinomies, thesis and antithesis can both be true if they concern different things: the thesis of the third (freedom) of the intelligible character of an agent, the antithesis (only natural causes) of appearances. Whether this makes room for freedom, or whether it succeeds at all, is controversial.

### The limits of metaphysics

The upshot is that metaphysics as knowledge of things in themselves, of the soul, the world as a whole and God, is impossible, while the metaphysics of experience, which analyzes the concepts and principles that make experience possible, is a science. On the standard cosmological model the universe is about 13.8 billion years old, a figure that measures the time since its hot, dense early phase, and there is no accepted theory of what, if anything, came before; physics does not obviously settle Kant's question. Philosophers continue to ask whether the antinomies are real dilemmas or are dissolved by modern mathematics, which has a well-developed theory of infinite sets.

@example
title: The first antinomy as a dilemma
problem: Show why the thesis and antithesis of the first antinomy look like contradictories, and state how Kant resolves the conflict.
step: The thesis says that the world has a beginning in time; the antithesis says that it has none. If both are about the world as a thing in itself, exactly one is true, since they are contradictories.
step: Kant argues that each side has a proof, so reason seems to establish both.
step: His diagnosis is that both sides assume that the world is a given whole existing in itself. If the world is only the totality of appearances, no such whole is given, and the question is without an answer: the series of appearances goes on indefinitely, without being either finite or infinite as a completed whole.
step: So both thesis and antithesis are false.
answer: The conflict is dissolved by denying the shared presupposition: both are false, because the world as a whole is not given as a thing in itself.

@example
title: Mathematical and dynamical antinomies
problem: Say which resolution Kant gives to each: (a) every composite substance consists of simple parts, or none does; (b) there is causality through freedom, or everything happens by natural laws.
step: (a) The second antinomy is mathematical: it concerns the composition of appearances. Kant holds that both claims are false, since appearances are not given as completed wholes.
step: (b) The third antinomy is dynamical: it concerns the relation of causes to effects. Kant holds that both can be true, if freedom belongs to the agent as intelligible and natural necessity to the agent as appearance.
answer: (a) both false; (b) both can be true, applying to different standpoints.

@mistakes
- Saying that Kant thinks that causation is merely subjective like Hume's habit: Kant holds that the causal principle is an objective condition of experience.
- Saying that Kant resolves every antinomy by saying both sides are false: that is the mathematical antinomies; in the dynamical ones both can be true.
- Saying that Kant claims to have shown that God does not exist: he argues that theoretical reason cannot know it either way.
- Treating the Deduction's outline as settled: its proper reconstruction is disputed.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about 150 words what Kant means by saying that reason's ideas have a regulative but not a constitutive use, and illustrate with the idea of God or of the world as a whole.
hint: Constitutive principles tell us what objects are like; regulative principles guide our inquiry.
solution: A constitutive principle is one that determines what objects of experience are like and is a source of knowledge about them, as the categories are. A regulative principle only directs inquiry, telling us how to proceed without claiming that its object exists as such. For Kant, the idea of the world as a complete whole, or of God as the ground of the whole of nature, can guide science to seek ever more systematic unity, by treating nature as if it were the product of a single intelligence, though we cannot know that any such object exists. The error of dogmatic metaphysics is to treat these ideas as constitutive, as if they gave knowledge of an object beyond experience.
