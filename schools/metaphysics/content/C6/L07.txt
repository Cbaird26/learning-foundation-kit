@article
A hammer strikes a nail and the nail moves. You say that the hammer *made* the nail move. What do you observe that justifies the word "made"? Hume's answer, in the *Treatise* (1739 to 1740) and the *Enquiry* (1748), is one of the most influential arguments in philosophy: you observe the hammer and the nail moving, but you do not observe any necessity connecting them. Where, then, does your idea of necessity come from?

### Three relations and a missing fourth

When Hume looks for what is in the relation of cause and effect among objects, he finds **contiguity** (cause and effect are next to each other in space and time), **priority** (the cause comes before the effect), and **constant conjunction** (objects like the cause have always been followed by objects like the effect). But we think that there is more: that the cause *necessitates* the effect. That is the idea of **necessary connection**.

### Causal inference is not a priori

In *Enquiry* section 4, Hume argues that no cause-effect relation can be known a priori. Imagine someone created fully rational but with no experience, a first man in the world. Looking at a billiard ball moving toward another, he could not know what would happen when they met: the second might move, the first might return, the first might jump over the second, and each outcome is conceivable without contradiction. The effect is a distinct event from the cause, and one distinct thing cannot be deduced from another. The same goes for the fluidity and transparency of water, which would not tell the first man that it can suffocate. We know cause and effect only by experience.

### The argument

If causal knowledge comes from experience, what do we experience that gives us the idea of necessary connection? Hume's argument is:

| P1 | Every genuine simple idea is copied from an impression (the copy principle). |
| P2 | We have an idea of necessary connection between cause and effect. |
| C1 | So there must be an impression from which the idea is copied. |
| P3 | A single case of one event followed by another gives no such impression: we see contiguity and succession, not necessity or power. |
| P4 | Repeating similar cases changes nothing in the objects, but it produces in the mind a habit, a felt determination to pass from the idea of the cause to the idea of the effect. |
| P5 | Apart from what is observed in single cases, the felt determination produced by repetition is the only candidate for the impression (neither reason nor the will supplies another). |
| C | So the idea of necessary connection is copied from this felt determination of the mind: it arises from custom, not from anything observed in the objects. |

The argument is valid. P1 is the copy principle, and P3 to P5 are claims about experience. Hume also considers whether we have an impression of power from our own **will**: when I will to raise my arm, it rises. In *Enquiry* section 7 he argues that this does not give us the impression either: we do not know how the will moves the body, the power is limited, and we learn it only by experience.

### Two definitions of cause

Hume offers two definitions of "cause", and says that both are drawn from circumstances extraneous to the cause itself. The first is in terms of objects: a cause is an object followed by another, where all objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. (He adds a gloss in counterfactual form, that if the first object had not existed, the second would never have existed, which is often seen as the ancestor of counterfactual theories of causation, such as that of David Lewis.) The second is in terms of the mind: a cause is an object followed by another, whose appearance always conveys the thought to the other.

### Interpretation

On the **regularity** reading, Hume holds that causation in the objects is nothing but constant conjunction, and that the sense of necessity is only a feeling in the observer. A more specific form is the **projectivist** reading: we project our felt determination onto the objects. A newer interpretation, the **"New Hume"** reading, holds that Hume means only to say that we have no idea of the nature of causal power, not that there is none: he is a skeptical realist who believes in real causes but denies that we can know what they are. Hume's text supports both, and the dispute is current among scholars. Evaluating the doctrine is a separate matter: critics say that regularity theories cannot distinguish genuine causal laws from accidental regularities, and that our experience of agency is richer than Hume allows.

@example
title: Applying Hume's argument to a new case
problem: A match is struck and it lights. Use Hume's analysis to say what we observe, and where our idea that the strike made the match light comes from.
step: We observe contiguity (the striking and the flame are adjacent), priority (the strike comes first), and the success of similar strikes in the past.
step: We do not observe any power or necessity in the strike, either in this single case or by looking closer at the head of the match.
step: After many instances, the mind is determined by habit to expect a flame upon seeing a strike. That felt expectation is the impression of reflection from which the idea of necessary connection is copied.
answer: We observe contiguity, priority and repetition; the idea of necessity comes from the felt determination of the mind produced by repetition, not from anything seen in the match.

@example
title: Hume's analysis of a causal claim
problem: "The kettle's heating made the water boil." Identify what each of Hume's three relations amounts to and what the further idea of necessary connection is.
step: Contiguity: the heated base of the kettle is in contact with the water.
step: Priority: the heating starts before the boiling.
step: Constant conjunction: heated water in kettles has boiled in all our experience.
step: The further idea of necessity: on Hume's account, this is the feeling of the mind being carried from the idea of the heating to the idea of the boiling.
answer: Contiguity, priority and constant conjunction are observed; the idea of necessity is the felt determination of the mind.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hume denies that causes exist: he denies that we observe necessity in the objects and gives an account of where our idea comes from; whether he also denies real powers is disputed.
- Confusing Hume's argument that causal inference is not a priori with his claim about the origin of the idea of necessary connection.
- Treating the regularity reading as the only reading: the "New Hume" reading takes Hume to be agnostic about the existence of real powers.
- Assuming Hume says that causal inference is unreasonable: that is a question about induction (next lesson).

prompt: A critic says that Hume's account of causation cannot distinguish between a genuine causal law and a merely accidental regularity, since both are constant conjunctions. In a paragraph, explain the objection, say whether it applies to both of Hume's definitions, and give one reply on Hume's behalf.
hint: Think about regularities that hold, but that we would not call causal, and about the counterfactual gloss.
solution: The objection is that if causation is no more than constant conjunction of like objects, then any exceptionless regularity counts as causal, including accidental ones, such as a regularity that holds by coincidence or one between two effects of a common cause. It applies to the first definition as stated, since it speaks only of conjunction, and the second definition, which is about the mind's habit, faces the problem that the habit arises even for accidental regularities we believe to be causal. The counterfactual gloss might help: if the first had not occurred, the second would not have. A reply on Hume's behalf is that his account is not an analysis of an objective distinction but a description of the idea we have, so what we count as causal is a matter of the habits we form; critics say that this makes the distinction unreal.
