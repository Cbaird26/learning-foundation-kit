@article
Hume ends the first book of the *Treatise* with doubts about the self, the body and reason. **Thomas Reid**, a Scottish philosopher, did not accept that these doubts must be answered one by one. In the *Inquiry into the Human Mind* (1764) and the *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), he argued that Hume's skeptical conclusions show that something is wrong with the starting point shared by Locke, Berkeley and Hume.

### The target: the theory of ideas

Reid calls the shared starting point the **theory of ideas** (or "the way of ideas"): the immediate objects of perception, memory and thought are ideas in the mind, and we know external things, if at all, only by inference from them. Reid thinks that Hume drew out the consequences of this theory more honestly than his predecessors: if we perceive only ideas, there is no way to reach bodies, other minds or even a persisting self. His argument is a *reductio*:

| P1 | If the theory of ideas were true, then the skeptical conclusions that Hume draws about bodies and the self would follow. |
| P2 | Those conclusions are absurd: they conflict with principles that no one can disbelieve in practice. |
| P3 | If a theory has absurd consequences, it is false. |
| C | So the theory of ideas is false. |

The argument is valid. Hume himself drew the skeptical conclusions while admitting that no one can live by them, so on Reid's reading the real dispute is over P2 and P3: whether a conclusion that cannot be believed in practice thereby shows that the theory behind it is false.

### Direct realism

Reid replaces the theory of ideas with a **direct realism**. In perception, a **sensation** (a feeling, such as the smell of a rose) acts as a natural sign that leads us, by the constitution of our nature, to conceive of an external object and to believe in its existence. We do not infer the object from the sensation: the belief arises immediately. Sensations are acts of the mind and are not objects that stand between us and the world.

### First principles of common sense

Reid holds that all reasoning must start from **first principles** that are not proved from anything more basic. He lists several. Among them are that the thoughts of which I am conscious are the thoughts of a being I call myself; that what I distinctly remember did happen; that the things I distinctly perceive by my senses exist and are as I perceive them; that I have some power over my actions; and that the natural faculties by which we distinguish truth from error are not fallacious. Reid's argument is that these cannot be proved: any proof would depend on the reliability of reason, and the reliability of reason can be proved only by reason. Since reason, perception and memory are all faculties given to us on the same footing, to trust one and distrust the others is arbitrary. He says that the skeptic who trusts his reason while doubting his senses has no ground for the difference. This is sometimes called the **parity argument**.

### Reid on the self

Reid agrees with Butler that memory presupposes personal identity, and adds that identity is not a matter of degree: a person is a simple, indivisible being with active powers. Against Hume, he holds that perceptions and thoughts need a thinker: a thought without a thinker is as absurd as a motion without something moving. Against Locke's memory criterion, he offers the brave officer case (lesson 3). Reid also holds, against Hume's account of causation, that our idea of power comes from our consciousness of our own agency, when we exert our active powers.

### Evaluation

The best objection is that appealing to common sense begs the question against the skeptic. Reid replies that no one can argue the skeptic out of skepticism without some starting point, and that the skeptic faces the same problem with reason. A second objection is that what counts as common sense varies, and that some principles of common sense have been shown false. Reid's reply is that his first principles are universal, are required for action, and are held by all in practice. In the twentieth century G. E. Moore defended common sense in a Reidian spirit in *A Defense of Common Sense* (1925), and the status of such appeals is still discussed in epistemology.

### Comparison of Hume and Reid on the self

| Question | Hume | Reid |
|---|---|---|
| What is the self? | A bundle of perceptions in flux | A simple, indivisible substance with active powers |
| Source of belief in the self | The imagination, from resemblance and causation | A first principle of common sense, known immediately by consciousness |
| Is there an impression of the self? | No constant impression | The self is not an object of perception; we are conscious of ourselves as thinkers |
| Role of memory | Discovers connections among perceptions | Presupposes identity; does not constitute it |

@example
title: Reconstructing Reid's parity argument
problem: A skeptic says: "I trust my reason but not my senses, since the senses sometimes deceive." State Reid's reply in standard form.
step: P1: Reason, perception and memory are faculties given to us by the same nature, and each can sometimes err.
step: P2: To justify trusting reason over the senses, one needs an argument whose premises are accepted independently of the faculties in question, and none exists.
step: C: So the preference for reason over the senses is arbitrary, and the skeptic has no ground for trusting reason alone.
step: The skeptic may reply that reason, but not the senses, can check itself; Reid says that such a check would use reason to validate reason, which is circular.
answer: Reid argues that preferring reason over the senses is arbitrary because each faculty is on the same footing and none can be validated without circularity.

@example
title: Comparing Hume and Reid on a case
problem: You remember writing a letter last week. What does each of Hume and Reid say about the status of this memory and the self who remembers?
step: Hume: the memory is a lively idea that resembles an earlier impression and, together with causal links, leads the imagination to treat the series of perceptions as one person.
step: Reid: the memory is a faculty giving direct knowledge of a past event, and it is a first principle that what I distinctly remember did happen. It presupposes that I who remember am the one who wrote.
step: The difference: for Hume the memory helps to constitute the idea of the self; for Reid it presupposes a self whose identity is not up for proof.
answer: Hume explains the idea of identity by relations among perceptions; Reid takes the identity of the self as a first principle on which memory depends.

@mistakes
- Saying that Reid thinks that common sense is infallible or that any popular belief is a first principle: he lists a small set of principles that are universal and necessary for action.
- Treating Reid's reply as a direct refutation of Hume's argument for the bundle: it is a rejection of the starting point, the theory of ideas, and an appeal to first principles.
- Confusing sensation with perception: a sensation is a feeling, and perception includes a conception of and belief in an external object.
- Assuming that Reid agrees with Locke on personal identity: he attacks Locke's memory criterion.

@practice self
prompt: Compare Hume's bundle view of the self with Reid's reply, in about 200 words. (This exercises the deliverable for weeks 9 and 10.) Say where they agree, where they disagree, and which argument you find stronger, with reasons.
hint: Use the premises of Hume's argument from lesson 9 and Reid's claim that thoughts need a thinker and that identity is a first principle.
solution: Hume argues that when he looks inward he finds only particular perceptions, never a constant impression of a self, so the self is a bundle of perceptions unified by resemblance and causation. Reid replies that perceptions and thoughts need a thinker, that the self is a simple substance with active powers, and that belief in it is a first principle, not an inference from ideas. They agree that there is no impression of the self as an object of perception, and that the idea of identity is not derived from a perception of it. They disagree about whether the lack of an impression shows that there is no simple self: Hume assumes that the theory of ideas and the copy principle fix what we can know, and Reid rejects that starting point. Hume's argument is strong given the copy principle, but that principle is empirical and had exceptions; Reid's reply is strong against the demand for an impression but relies on a first principle that Hume could regard as question-begging. A fair view is that Reid shows what Hume's argument assumes, but that the unity of the self remains a puzzle that the bundle theory and its critics address.
