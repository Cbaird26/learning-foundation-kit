@article
If the cherry is only a collection of ideas, why do the same cherries turn up on the same tree each summer? Why can you not simply imagine a bowl of cherries into being? And what becomes of physics, with its particles and forces that nobody has ever seen? These are the questions Berkeley faces after the arguments of last week. His answer to the first two is God, and his answer to the third is a view of science as the study of regularities among ideas.

### Ideas of sense and ideas of imagination

Berkeley distinguishes the ideas that we receive in perception from the ideas that we form in imagination (*Principles*, around sections 28 to 33). Ideas of sense are **involuntary**: you cannot choose what you see when you open your eyes in daylight. They are also more **vivid and distinct**, **steady** and **coherent**: they come in orderly trains that follow regular rules. Ideas of imagination are produced at will, are faint, and flit about. This is how Berkeley distinguishes real things from chimeras, and a dream from waking life, without matter.

### The argument for a divine cause

Ideas of sense need a cause that is not me. Berkeley argues that the cause must be a spirit:

| P1 | Ideas of sense are not produced by my will: I am not their cause. |
| P2 | Ideas are passive: no idea can be the cause of another idea or of anything else. |
| P3 | Only a spirit, an idea or a material substance could cause an idea of sense, and there is no material substance. |
| P4 | So ideas of sense are caused by some spirit other than me. |
| P5 | The order, regularity, variety and grandeur of these ideas show that their cause is a wise, powerful and good spirit, and a spirit of that kind is God, the infinite spirit. |
| C | So ideas of sense are caused by God, the infinite spirit. |

The step to P4 is valid given P1 to P3 (P3 includes the assumption that the only candidate causes are spirits, ideas and material things). P3 is the conclusion of the arguments of last week, and a Lockean would reject it. The step from P4 to C needs P5, which rests on an inference to the best explanation, and an objector can deny it: the same order could be produced by a committee of finite spirits or by a limited god instead of a single infinite one.

A second argument concerns continuity. We take the cherry tree to exist when nobody is in the orchard. Berkeley agrees: sensible things exist when no finite mind perceives them because they exist in the mind of God. In the Three Dialogues Philonous argues that sensible things are ideas, ideas exist only in a mind, and so there must be an infinite mind in which they exist when we do not perceive them. Critics note that this argument assumes that the ordinary view of the persistence of things must be preserved; the idealist alternative is to say that the tree is a permanent possibility of perception. That phenomenalist alternative was later developed by John Stuart Mill, who defined physical objects as permanent possibilities of sensation.

### Laws of nature

For Berkeley, the regularities that we find are the **laws of nature**: the constant ways in which God produces ideas in us. Natural things do not cause one another: fire does not cause the idea of pain, but is a **sign** of it. The ideas of sense form a **language**, the language of the Author of Nature, by which God tells us what to expect. Learning the laws is like learning to read: we discover which signs go with which. This serves Berkeley's point against materialism: the regularity that the materialist explains with matter is explained by God's constancy.

### The objection from science

Science postulates unperceived particles, forces and fields, and explains perception through a causal story about light, nerves and the brain. If all of these are ideas, which are inert, they cannot be causes; and if the particles are unperceived, they do not exist. Berkeley's reply is that natural philosophy does not discover real causes but **regularities** and **signs**: its task is to subsume phenomena under general rules, and mechanical hypotheses are useful for predicting what we will perceive. In *De Motu* (1721) he argued that terms such as "force" are mathematical devices for calculation and not names of hidden causes. This is an early **instrumentalism**. The standard objection is that this leaves unexplained why the instruments work so well, and that scientific realism, which takes the successes of theories as evidence that they describe the unobserved, is more natural. The question of scientific realism remains live in the philosophy of science.

### Other objections

If everything is God's ideas, why do we have hallucinations? Berkeley says that these lack the steadiness and coherence of sense. Does the view make God responsible for all our experiences, including evils? Berkeley holds that finite spirits are active in their own voluntary actions, but the problem remains.

@example
title: Testing the criteria for ideas of sense
problem: Apply Berkeley's criteria to two cases: (a) You see a lighthouse beam sweeping the sea at dusk. (b) You daydream of a harbor you have never visited. Which is an idea of sense, and why?
step: (a) The beam is not produced by your will, it is vivid and distinct, and it follows a regular pattern, so it counts as an idea of sense.
step: (b) The harbor is produced at will, is faint, and can be changed at once, so it is an idea of imagination.
step: A vivid hallucination is harder. Berkeley would say that it lacks steadiness and coherence with the rest of experience, and it does not fit the connected order of other ideas.
answer: (a) is an idea of sense, (b) is an idea of imagination; vivid hallucinations are a harder case for the criteria and must be handled by coherence.

@example
title: Which premise does the materialist reject?
problem: For Berkeley's argument for a divine cause, say which premise a Lockean materialist rejects and why this does not by itself save the materialist.
step: The Lockean rejects P3: material substance exists and causes our ideas of sense.
step: Berkeley's reply is that material substance cannot cause ideas because it is inert and we have no idea of how a mindless substance could cause an idea; also that the notion of matter is empty.
step: So the dispute depends on the arguments of last week against matter and the claim that only spirits are active.
answer: The materialist rejects P3, and the argument turns on whether Berkeley's case against matter succeeds.

@mistakes
- Saying that Berkeley explains the regularities of nature as caused by our own minds: P1 says that they are not produced by our will.
- Treating laws of nature as causal powers of bodies: for Berkeley bodies are not causes, and the laws are the regular ways in which God produces ideas.
- Saying that Berkeley rejects science: he accepts it as the discovery of regularities and denies only that its entities are mindless causes.
- Confusing the argument from continuity with the argument from passivity: one concerns the persistence of the tree and the other concerns the cause of ideas of sense.

@quiz self
prompt: Evaluate Berkeley's reply to the objection from science. State the objection, Berkeley's reply, and one reason to be satisfied or dissatisfied with it.
hint: Use the instrumentalist reading and the success of scientific theories.
solution: Objection: science explains our experiences by unobserved entities, and immaterialism cannot allow that these exist. Reply: science finds regularities among ideas, and the entities are tools for predicting what we will perceive; the real cause of the ideas is God. A reason for dissatisfaction is that it leaves the predictive success of theories that mention unobservables unexplained: the realist says that the best explanation of the success is that the theories are approximately true of unobserved things. A reason for satisfaction is that the instrumentalist can say that all the evidence for science is in the observed regularities, and the success is explained by the regularity of God's will. The question of whether theories describe the unobserved remains open in the philosophy of science.
