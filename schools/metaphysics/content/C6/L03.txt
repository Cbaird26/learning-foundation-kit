@article
Suppose that tomorrow you wake with the memories of someone who lived two centuries ago, and none of your own. Are you that person, or are you still you? Locke's chapter on identity (Book II, chapter 27, added to the *Essay* in its second edition of 1694) is the first systematic attempt in modern philosophy to answer such questions, and it remains the starting point for the debate on personal identity.

### Three kinds of identity

Locke argues that "same" depends on what kind of thing is in question. A mass of matter stays the same as long as no atoms are added or removed. An oak stays the same oak through a change of its matter, as long as the matter participates in one continuing life. A **man**, meaning a human animal, is likewise the same as long as it is one continuing organized living body. And a **person** is something else: for Locke, "person" is a **forensic** term, one that applies to actions and their merit, and it names a thinking, intelligent being that has reason and reflection and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing at different times and places.

### Consciousness makes the person

What makes you the same person across time? Not sameness of body, since the body changes. Not sameness of immaterial soul either, says Locke: if a soul that had once been Nestor's were now in you, with no consciousness of anything Nestor did, you would not be the same person as Nestor. Rather, personal identity consists in sameness of **consciousness**: as far as your consciousness can be extended backward to a past action or thought, so far you are the same person who did or thought it. Interpreters take "consciousness" to be a memory-like awareness of past actions as one's own, which is why this is called the **memory criterion**. Locke's argument:

| P1 | A person is a thinking being that can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, at different times and places. |
| P2 | It does this by consciousness, which accompanies all thinking and makes each thinker aware of itself as itself. |
| P3 | So a person is the same person at two times just in case the consciousness at the later time reaches back to the thoughts and actions of the earlier time. |
| C | So personal identity consists in sameness of consciousness, not in sameness of body or of substance. |

The argument is a good one for a reader who accepts P1 and P2 and the assumption, implicit in P1, that being the same person at two times is just being able to consider oneself as the same thinking thing at both: P3 then follows. C adds that this consciousness can be present with a different body or substance, as the cases below suggest. Locke's famous illustration is a prince whose soul, with the prince's memories, enters a cobbler's body. Everyone, he says, would say that this is the same *person* as the prince although he is the same *man* as the cobbler. He adds that the memory criterion explains why a person is responsible only for what he is conscious of having done, and that human courts, which cannot read consciousness, rightly punish people who may have forgotten.

### The circularity objection

Joseph Butler objected (in a dissertation on personal identity appended to *The Analogy of Religion*, 1736) that consciousness of a past experience *presupposes* personal identity: to remember doing something, you must have been the one who did it, or what you have is not a memory. So memory cannot be what constitutes identity, because the concept of memory already includes it. The standard reply, due to Sydney Shoemaker and developed by Derek Parfit, introduces **quasi-memory**: an experience is quasi-remembered when it seems to be remembered from the inside and is caused in the right way by the original experience, whether or not the rememberer was the one who had it. The memory criterion is then stated with quasi-memory, which does not presuppose identity.

### The transitivity objection

Thomas Reid (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785) described a brave officer who as a boy was flogged for robbing an orchard, who later took a standard from the enemy, and who was in old age made a general. The officer remembers the flogging; the general remembers taking the standard but has forgotten the flogging. Then:

| P1 | A later person is the same person as an earlier person if and only if the later person remembers an experience of the earlier person (the memory criterion). |
| P2 | The officer remembers the flogging, so the officer is the boy. |
| P3 | The general remembers taking the standard, so the general is the officer. |
| P4 | The general does not remember the flogging, so by the criterion the general is not the boy. |
| P5 | Identity is transitive: if the general is the officer and the officer is the boy, the general is the boy. |
| C | So the general is, and is not, the boy: a contradiction, and Locke's criterion is false. |

The best reply is to say that the criterion gives a sufficient condition only through *chains*: later neo-Lockeans define psychological continuity as overlapping chains of memory, so the general is connected to the boy through the officer. Then P1 is rejected as stated, and P4 no longer follows.

### Where things stand

The psychological approach in this tradition remains the most widely held view of personal identity in the 2020 PhilPapers Survey (about 44% of respondents, against about 19% for the biological view), though a share of philosophers holding a view is not evidence that it is true. Animalists object that you are an animal, and so the same as the man, not the person.

@example
title: Applying the transitivity objection to a new case
problem: Teo at age 70 remembers an experience of Teo at age 40. Teo at age 40 remembers an experience of Teo at age 10. Teo at age 70 remembers nothing of age 10. Show how this fits the structure of Reid's objection, and say how a chain-based neo-Lockean answers.
step: Memory links the 70-year-old to the 40-year-old and the 40-year-old to the 10-year-old, so by Locke's criterion as stated the 70-year-old is the 40-year-old and the 40-year-old is the 10-year-old.
step: No memory links the 70-year-old directly to the 10-year-old, so by the criterion as stated they are different persons.
step: Identity is transitive, so the 70-year-old would be both identical to and not identical to the 10-year-old. That is the structure of Reid's objection.
step: The neo-Lockean says that the criterion should be stated for chains: two stages are of one person when they are linked by overlapping chains of memory, so the 70-year-old and the 10-year-old are the same person even though no direct memory connects them.
answer: The case has Reid's structure. The neo-Lockean restates the criterion in terms of chains of memory, which removes the contradiction.

@example
title: Man and person in the prince-and-cobbler case
problem: In Locke's case, the soul of a prince, carrying the prince's memories, enters the cobbler's body, and the cobbler's own memories are gone. Who is the person, and who is the man?
step: Locke says that the person goes with consciousness: the one with the prince's memories is the prince, so the person is the prince.
step: The man is a human animal identified by the continuing life of one body, so the man is the cobbler.
step: Locke thinks the case shows that "person" and "man" come apart.
answer: The person is the prince; the man is the cobbler.

@mistakes
- Treating "man" and "person" as synonyms: for Locke the man is the human animal, and the person is a thinking being identified by consciousness.
- Saying that Locke thinks the soul is irrelevant because there is no soul: he leaves open whether thinking substance is material or immaterial and argues that identity of person does not depend on it either way.
- Misdescribing Reid's objection as showing that memory is unreliable: it is a logical point about the transitivity of identity.
- Confusing Butler's circularity objection (memory presupposes identity) with Reid's transitivity objection.

Then state one reason to doubt that this verdict is correct.

solution: On Locke's criterion, Dev's consciousness no longer reaches back to the earlier actions, so the person after the accident is not the same person as before; but the man, the human organism, is the same one. @quiz self
prompt: Reconstruct Butler's objection to the memory criterion in standard form, and then give the quasi-memory reply in three or four sentences, saying whether you think it works.
hint: Start from the idea that remembering an experience as one's own requires that one had it.
solution: P1: If personal identity consisted in memory, then what makes a later person the same as an earlier one would be that the later person remembers the earlier one's experience. P2: A person remembers an experience only if that person had the experience (otherwise it is only a seeming memory). Whether it works depends on whether quasi-memory can be defined without hidden reference to the person; critics ask whether the "right causal link" can be specified without it.
