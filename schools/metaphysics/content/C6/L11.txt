@article
Hume's fork seemed to leave two kinds of knowledge: relations of ideas, known by thought alone and contradicted only by contradiction, and matters of fact, known by experience. **Immanuel Kant** (*Critique of Pure Reason*, first edition 1781, second edition 1787, cited as A and B) said that Hume's challenge about causation woke him from his "dogmatic slumber" (*Prolegomena*), and he answered by showing that the fork leaves out a third kind of judgment.

### Two distinctions

A judgment is **a priori** if it is independent of experience and **a posteriori** if it depends on experience. Kant's marks of the a priori are **necessity** and **strict universality**: experience can tell us that something is so, but not that it must be so. A judgment is **analytic** if the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject, so that denying it is a contradiction ("All bodies are extended"), and **synthetic** if the predicate adds something to the concept ("All bodies are heavy"). The first distinction concerns how a judgment is known, and the second concerns what it says. Both are needed: the fork of Hume runs them together, treating relations of ideas as analytic and a priori and matters of fact as synthetic and a posteriori.

### The synthetic a priori

Kant's claim is that there are judgments that are **synthetic and a priori**: they add to the subject concept, yet are known with necessity independent of experience. His examples are the judgments of mathematics (that $7 + 5 = 12$, which he says is not contained in the concept of the sum of seven and five), of geometry (that a straight line between two points is the shortest), of the sciences of nature (that in all changes of the corporeal world the quantity of matter remains the same) and of metaphysics (that every event has a cause). Whether Kant is right about each example is disputed: Frege, for instance, argued that arithmetic is analytic. The central question of the Critique (B19) is: *how are synthetic a priori judgments possible?*

### The Copernican turn and the Aesthetic

Kant's answer is a change of viewpoint, which he compares to Copernicus (Bxvi): instead of supposing that knowledge conforms to objects, suppose that objects, as we experience them, conform to our way of knowing. The mind contributes the form of experience. In the **Transcendental Aesthetic** he distinguishes **sensibility**, our capacity to receive intuitions, from the **understanding**, our capacity for concepts, and argues that **space** and **time** are the pure forms of sensibility: space the form of outer sense, time of inner sense. They are not things, nor relations abstracted from things, but the framework in which anything appears to us.

### The argument from geometry

| P1 | Geometry consists of synthetic a priori judgments about space, which hold necessarily of all objects we can experience. |
| P2 | If space were a property of things in themselves, or a concept drawn from experience, geometry would not be both a priori and certain of all objects we can experience. |
| P3 | Space is either a property of things in themselves, a concept drawn from experience, or the pure form of our outer intuition. |
| P4 | If space is the pure form of our outer intuition, then geometry is a priori and holds necessarily for all objects of outer experience. |
| C | So space is the pure form of our outer intuition, not a property of things in themselves. |

The argument is valid and works by elimination: it claims that only the third option explains P1 (P4 shows that it does). The weakest points are P1, P2 and the assumption in P3 that there is no further option. Kant also argues that space is not an empirical concept because it must already be presupposed to represent things as outside and beside one another; that it is a single, infinite given whole; and that we can think space empty of objects but not objects without space.

### Transcendental idealism

Kant concludes that space and time have **empirical reality**, since they apply to all objects of experience, and **transcendental ideality**, since they are not features of things in themselves. This leads to the distinction between **appearances** and **things in themselves**. Interpreters disagree about what that distinction is: on a *two-world* reading, appearances and things in themselves are two sets of objects; on a *two-aspect* reading, they are the same objects considered as they appear to us and as they are independently of our cognition. The disagreement is a standing one in Kant scholarship.

### Evaluation: geometry after Kant

The argument faces a historical challenge: the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries in the nineteenth century, and Einstein's general theory of relativity, on which physical spacetime is curved and its geometry is not Euclidean (the theory is extremely well confirmed, including by the direct detection of gravitational waves in 2015). If Kant meant that Euclidean geometry is necessarily true of physical space, he seems to be mistaken. Defenders reply that Kant's claim concerns the form of our intuition and pure geometry, not the geometry of physical spacetime, which is empirical; critics reply that Kant explicitly takes geometry to apply to the objects of experience. How much of the Aesthetic survives is open.

@example
title: Classifying judgments as Kant would
problem: Classify each as analytic or synthetic and a priori or a posteriori, as Kant would: (a) "All triangles have three sides." (b) "Every event has a cause." (c) "The pond froze last night."
step: (a) The concept of a triangle contains having three sides, so denying it is a contradiction: **analytic**, and a priori.
step: (b) The concept of an event does not contain that of a cause, so the judgment adds something, and Kant takes it to be necessary and universal: **synthetic a priori**.
step: (c) It reports a contingent fact known by observation: **synthetic a posteriori**.
answer: (a) analytic a priori; (b) synthetic a priori (for Kant); (c) synthetic a posteriori.

@example
title: Why space is not an empirical concept
problem: State Kant's first argument that space is an a priori representation and not an empirical concept drawn from outer experience.
step: P1: To experience things as outside me and next to one another, I must already possess the representation of space, since to represent things as outside and beside one another is already to represent them in space.
step: P2: A representation that is derived from outer experience cannot be one that is already needed in order to have that experience.
step: C: So the representation of space is not derived from outer experience; it must underlie it.
step: A critic may reply that the argument shows only that spatial representation is needed for spatial experience, which a theory of innate capacities could accept without Kant's conclusion about things in themselves.
answer: The representation of space is presupposed by outer experience, so it cannot be abstracted from it.

@mistakes
- Treating "a priori" and "analytic" as the same distinction: the first concerns how we know, the second concerns the relation of predicate to subject.
- Saying that Kant thinks all a priori knowledge is analytic, or that the synthetic a priori is a contradiction: the point of the Critique is that it exists and needs explaining.
- Saying that Kant denies that space exists: it has empirical reality, as the form of appearances.
- Attributing to Kant the view that his examples are agreed: Frege and others dispute that arithmetic is synthetic.
