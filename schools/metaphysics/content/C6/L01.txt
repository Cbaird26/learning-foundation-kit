@article
Is anyone born knowing that whatever is, is? Or that the same thing cannot both be and not be? Many seventeenth-century thinkers, in both the scholastic and the rationalist traditions, held that the mind is furnished from the start with some ideas and first principles, and Descartes counted the ideas of God and of the self among ideas that belong to the mind by its nature. In Book I of the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689), **John Locke** argues that this is false, and in Book II he sets out an alternative: every idea we have comes from experience.

### What an idea is

Locke uses "idea" very broadly: whatever the mind is aware of when it thinks, perceives or understands. The idea of whiteness, the idea of a horse, the idea of a promise and the idea of a number all count. He divides ideas into **simple ideas**, which contain a single uniform appearance and cannot be broken down by the mind (the taste of bitterness, the color yellow, the feel of warmth), and **complex ideas**, which the mind builds out of simple ones. Complex ideas are of three kinds: **modes** (such as a dozen or a theft), **substances** (such as gold or a person) and **relations**.

### Against innate principles

The innatist's favorite argument is from **universal consent**. Locke's reconstruction runs: some principles are agreed to by all human beings; what all agree to must come from a common source; the only common source is nature itself; so those principles are innate. Locke answers by denying the first claim. His central argument is:

| P1 | If a principle were innate, it would be perceived or noticed by every human mind, including the minds of infants and of people with profound intellectual disabilities. |
| P2 | "Whatever is, is" and "it is impossible for the same thing to be and not to be" are not perceived in those minds: infants and such persons neither assent to them nor grasp them. |
| C | So those principles are not innate, and universal consent does not exist. |

The argument is valid, so the innatist must reject a premise, and the natural target is P1. The innatist can say that an innate principle is in the mind without being *noticed*, which denies that innateness requires being noticed. Locke's reply is that to be impressed on the mind without being perceived makes no sense: for something to be in the mind is for the mind to be aware of it. A second reply says that these principles are assented to as soon as a person comes to the use of reason. Locke replies that many truths nobody takes to be innate, such as the fact that sweetness is not bitterness, are also assented to as soon as the terms are understood. Quick assent on understanding shows that a truth is *self-evident*, not that it is *innate*. And since children grasp those particular truths before they ever grasp the abstract maxims, the maxims cannot be the foundation of all other knowledge.

### Experience as the source

Locke's positive thesis uses the image of the mind as "white paper" before experience. All ideas come from one of two sources. **Sensation** gives us ideas of external objects through the senses (yellow, hard, sweet). **Reflection** gives us ideas of the mind's own operations (perceiving, doubting, believing, willing). The mind is **passive** in receiving simple ideas: you cannot choose to see the lawn as anything but green in daylight. It is **active** in combining ideas, comparing them and abstracting general ideas from particular ones.

### What Locke's empiricism is not

Locke's empiricism is a thesis about the *origin of ideas*. It does not say that the mind has no capacities at birth: perceiving, remembering and reasoning are natural faculties. Nor does it say that there is no certain knowledge. In Book IV Locke holds that **intuitive** and **demonstrative** knowledge, as in mathematics and in his argument for God's existence, is certain. And his target is vaguer than it looks: Locke attacks the doctrine without naming a particular author, and later innatists, notably Leibniz in the *New Essays*, replied that innateness is a matter of dispositions and virtual presence in the mind, not of occurrent awareness, which would take the sting out of P1.

### Evaluating the argument

Locke's argument is strong against the claim that innate principles are consciously held by everyone, and weaker against the dispositional version. The debate then turns on what would distinguish an innate disposition from an ordinary capacity to learn from experience, and that is the question the innatist must answer.

@example
title: Finding the target of an innatist reply
problem: An innatist replies to Locke: "Infants do hold the principle of non-contradiction; it is in their minds, but they cannot yet notice it." Which premise of Locke's argument does this reject, and how does Locke answer?
step: Locke's argument has P1 (an innate principle would be perceived or noticed by every mind, including those of infants), P2 (it is not perceived in the minds of infants) and the conclusion that the principle is not innate. The argument is valid, so a reply must reject P1 or P2.
step: The innatist grants P2: infants do not notice the principle. The reply denies P1, the claim that being innate requires being noticed, and says the principle is in the infant's mind unnoticed.
step: Locke's answer is that the notion of a mental content that the mind has never perceived is unintelligible: to be in the mind is to be perceived by it. So the innatist's claim, he says, amounts to the empty claim that the mind has a capacity to know the principle.
step: The innatist can retreat to the dispositional reading. The dispute then concerns whether a capacity to recognize a principle on reflection differs from any other learnable truth.
answer: The reply rejects P1. Locke answers that unperceived mental content is unintelligible, and the dispositional retreat threatens to make every knowable truth innate.

@example
title: Simple or complex?
problem: Classify each as a simple or a complex idea in Locke's sense, and as an idea of sensation or reflection where that applies: (a) the taste of salt, (b) the idea of a horse, (c) the awareness that you are hesitating.
step: (a) The taste of salt is a single uniform appearance that cannot be broken down. It is a **simple idea of sensation**.
step: (b) A horse is built from many simple ideas (shape, size, color, motion, sound) combined by the mind into the idea of one kind of thing. It is a **complex idea**, a substance idea.
step: (c) Hesitating is an operation of your own mind, and awareness of it comes from reflection. The idea is a **simple idea of reflection**.
answer: (a) simple, sensation; (b) complex (substance); (c) simple, reflection.

@mistakes
- Saying that Locke thinks the mind is empty of all capacities at birth: he denies innate ideas and principles, not natural faculties of perceiving, remembering and reasoning.
- Treating Locke as denying all certain or a priori knowledge: he holds that intuitive and demonstrative knowledge is certain, and his claim is about where ideas come from.
- Confusing "assented to on first understanding" with "innate": a truth can be self-evident without being innate.
- Taking "reflection" to mean careful thought in general: for Locke it is the mind's awareness of its own operations, and it supplies ideas.
