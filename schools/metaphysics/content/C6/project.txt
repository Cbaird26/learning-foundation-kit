step: Draft the essay in four parts: Hume's account (about 600 words), the problem of induction (about 400), Kant's answer (about 600), and your evaluation with the best objection and reply (about 400). Use at least three short quotations or close paraphrases from the modernized texts, each cited by section.

material: **Hume's two problems, summarized.** Problem one (origin of the idea): we have an idea of necessary connection, yet no single case of one event following another yields an impression of necessity; the idea comes from the felt determination of the mind produced by repetition. Problem two (justification of inference): we infer unobserved cases from observed ones, but any argument for the uniformity of nature either is demonstrative, which fails since its denial is conceivable, or is probable reasoning, which is circular.
material: **Kant's reply, summarized.** Kant accepts that necessity is not given in sense impressions but holds that the concept of cause is a category, a rule of the understanding without which there could be no experience of objective events. The Second Analogy argues that to represent an objective succession (as opposed to a mere succession of my perceptions) one must bring the order of events under a rule of cause and effect.

@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2, Locke.** Ideas come from sensation and reflection, not innate principles; his argument from the minds of infants and the reply that innate means dispositional. Qualities are powers; primary qualities (solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, number) are resembled by our ideas, secondary ones are not; the three-bowls argument. Substance in general, real and nominal essence.

**Week 3.** The memory criterion; man versus person; Reid's brave officer (transitivity) and Butler's circularity; chains and quasi-memory.

**Weeks 4 and 5, Berkeley.** Esse is percipi for sensible things; the argument from ideas; the likeness principle; relativity against primary qualities; the master argument and its scope confusion. God as cause of ideas of sense, laws as a divine language, instrumentalism about science.

**Weeks 6 and 7, Hume.** Impressions and ideas; the copy principle and the missing shade; Hume's fork. Contiguity, priority and constant conjunction; the idea of necessity from custom; the two definitions; regularity and "New Hume" readings.

**Week 8.** The problem of induction in six premises; circularity; Popper, Reichenbach, Strawson, externalism, Goodman.

**Weeks 9 and 10.** The bundle theory and the Appendix; natural belief and mitigated skepticism; Reid's reductio of the theory of ideas, direct realism, first principles and the parity argument.

**Weeks 11 and 12, Kant.** Analytic and synthetic, a priori and a posteriori; the synthetic a priori; space and time as forms of intuition; the geometry argument and relativity; the categories, the Deduction, the Second Analogy; the antinomies and the limits of metaphysics.

### Key distinctions

- Idea versus quality (Locke); sensible things versus spirits (Berkeley); impression versus idea (Hume); sensation versus perception (Reid); appearance versus thing in itself (Kant).
- Origin of an idea versus justification of an inference (Hume's two problems).
- A priori versus analytic; constitutive versus regulative; mathematical versus dynamical antinomies.

### Self-check

- Can you state three arguments in standard form and say which premise each objection targets?
- Can you say, for each philosopher, what the view is and what the best objection is?
- Can you explain what the interpretive disputes are (Locke's substratum, Hume's realism, Kant's two worlds or two aspects)?

### Common errors

Saying that Berkeley denies tables exist; that Hume denies there are causes; that Locke denies natural faculties; that Kant thinks causation is only habit; running together a priori and analytic; treating a contested reading as settled.

@exam mc
prompt: Which pair correctly matches a philosopher with a thesis he held?
choice*: Berkeley: the esse of sensible things is percipi, while spirits exist by perceiving
choice: Locke: all ideas are innate in the mind of every human
choice: Hume: the self is a simple substance known by a constant impression
choice: Kant: the causal principle is learned from repeated observation
hint: Check each claim against what the philosopher says about the origin or nature of the thing in question.
solution: Berkeley holds that sensible things are collections of ideas whose esse is percipi and that spirits are active perceivers. Locke denies innate ideas, Hume denies a constant impression of a simple self, and Kant holds that the causal principle is a priori.

@exam ms
prompt: Which of these claims does Kant accept in his reply to Hume? Select all that apply.
choice*: Necessity is not given in a mere sense impression.
choice*: The causal principle is synthetic and a priori.
choice*: The concept of cause is a category of the understanding.
choice: The idea of necessary connection is only a habit of expectation.
hint: Kant agrees with Hume about what experience alone gives, but disagrees about the source of the causal principle.
solution: Kant grants that necessity is not given in sensation, and holds that the causal principle is synthetic a priori and that cause is a category. The claim that the idea is only a habit of expectation is Hume's.

@exam self
prompt: Write about 400 words on this question: "Hume reduces causation to constant conjunction and a habit of the mind; Kant makes it a condition of experience. Which account better explains why we take the world to be governed by causal laws?" Reconstruct each account in standard form, state the strongest objection to each, and defend a verdict.
hint: Use Hume's argument about the origin of the idea of necessity and the problem of induction, and the Second Analogy argument; consider what each can say about accidental regularities and about the future.
solution: Hume's argument: P1 every simple idea is copied from an impression; P2 we have an idea of necessary connection; P3 no single case gives an impression of necessity; P4 repetition produces a felt determination of the mind; C the idea comes from custom. His problem of induction adds that no argument justifies the expectation that the future will resemble the past. Kant's argument: P1 to represent an objective succession, the order of perceptions must be represented as necessary; P2 that is to bring them under a rule of cause and effect; C so the causal principle is a condition of the experience of objective events. The strongest objection to Hume is that his account cannot distinguish genuine laws from accidental regularities and omits our experience of agency; to Kant, that his argument shows at most that events must be rule-governed in some way, not that any particular law holds, and that it does not answer Hume's problem about the future. On balance, Kant better explains why the causal framework is unavoidable, and Hume better explains why we hold particular causal beliefs and why no argument secures them; a defensible verdict is that the two answer different questions, and that Kant's account explains the form of causal thinking while leaving induction open. Reasonable verdicts differ.
