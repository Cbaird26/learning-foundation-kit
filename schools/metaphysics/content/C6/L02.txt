@article
Hold a lump of beeswax in your hand and then warm it by a fire. Its color changes, its smell fades, its hardness and shape alter. What stays the same? Locke's answer in Book II of the *Essay* has two parts: an account of which of a body's qualities are really in it, and an account of what holds those qualities together.

### Qualities as powers

Locke separates an **idea** (what is in the mind) from a **quality** (the power in an object to produce that idea in us). A snowball produces ideas of white, cold and round. Locke's **primary qualities** are those that are inseparable from a body however it is altered: solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number (he also speaks of bulk). Divide a grain of wheat and every part still has them. **Secondary qualities** (colors, sounds, tastes, smells, and also sensations of warmth) are powers to produce ideas in us *by means of* the primary qualities of the body's parts, which are too small to be seen. A third group, which Locke calls powers that are "mediately" perceivable, are powers to change the primary qualities of other bodies, such as the sun's power to melt wax.

This is the **corpuscularian** picture that Locke took from the new science of Boyle and others: bodies are made of particles with size, shape and motion, and they affect our senses by impacts. The central doctrine concerns **resemblance**: the ideas of primary qualities resemble the qualities in bodies, and the ideas of secondary qualities do not. Nothing in the fire resembles the pain you feel on getting too close, and nothing in the vermilion resembles its redness.

### The argument from relativity

Locke's example uses three bowls of water. One hand is warm, the other is cold, and both are placed in the lukewarm bowl:

| P1 | The same lukewarm water at the same time gives one hand the idea of coldness and the other the idea of warmth. |
| P2 | If the ideas of warmth and coldness resembled qualities in the water, the water would at once have both qualities, being both warm and cold. |
| P3 | The same water cannot be both warm and cold in the same respect at once. |
| C | So the ideas of warmth and coldness do not resemble qualities in the water. |

The argument is valid. Notice what it proves: only that these two ideas do not resemble something in the water. Locke needs a further step to cover color, sound and taste (the quality that explains the idea is a power grounded in primary qualities, and the explanation is the same in every case) and a step to show that the ideas of primary qualities are different. His contrast is that, whatever the lukewarm water does, the same body does not at once seem both round and square. Berkeley attacks exactly this contrast in Week 4.

### Substance

Locke also thinks we must suppose something to which the qualities belong. We observe that simple ideas keep company (yellow, heavy, malleable, fusible), and since we cannot imagine them subsisting by themselves, we suppose a **substratum** that supports them. But we have no clear idea of it: it is an unknown "something", the idea of **substance in general**. Interpreters disagree about how seriously Locke takes this. On one reading, which Berkeley and Reid favor, Locke commits to a bare, qualityless substratum that is unknowable. On another, his remarks are a modest admission that we have only a relative idea (that which supports qualities) and the underlying nature is simply unknown to us, so he is not committed to a thing that is *bare*.

### Real and nominal essence

For particular kinds of substance, Locke distinguishes the **real essence**, the internal constitution of insensible parts that causes the observable qualities, from the **nominal essence**, the complex idea of observable qualities that we use to sort things into kinds (for gold: yellow, heavy, fusible, malleable). We sort by nominal essences, because we cannot know the real ones. Locke concludes that the boundaries of the species we name are drawn partly by us, which is part of his case against the scholastic doctrine of substantial forms.

### The state of the question

Locke's distinction proved influential and contested. Berkeley argued that the primary qualities are as mind-dependent as the secondary ones, and that the likeness principle fails. Defenders reply that the physical sciences do explain secondary qualities, such as color in terms of reflectance and light, by quantities that are not themselves colors, and the question is whether that supports Locke's claim that ideas of primary qualities alone resemble.

@example
title: Sorting ten qualities
problem: Sort these ten into primary qualities, secondary qualities and neither, in Locke's sense, and say why: extension, blueness, sweetness, figure, loudness, number, rest, fragrance, solidity, warmth as felt.
step: Locke's primary list contains solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number. Test: the quality stays with a body however it is divided or changed. So extension, figure, number, rest and solidity are **primary**.
step: The others are the colors, sounds, tastes, smells and felt warmth: blueness, sweetness, loudness, fragrance and warmth as felt. Each is a power in the body, grounded in the primary qualities of its insensible parts, to produce an idea in us. They are **secondary**.
step: None of the ten is a power to change another body's primary qualities (such as the power of fire to melt wax), so the third group, mediate powers, is empty here.
step: Explain: the idea of a primary quality resembles the quality; the ideas of blueness, sweetness, loudness, fragrance and felt warmth do not resemble anything in the body.
answer: Primary: extension, figure, number, rest, solidity. Secondary: blueness, sweetness, loudness, fragrance, warmth as felt. Third group: none.

@example
title: Real and nominal essence of a new kind
problem: Imagine a metal called "lumite" that we identify as pale green, light and brittle. Suppose its green color and its brittleness are produced by the arrangement of its tiny parts, which no one has yet discovered. What are its nominal and real essences in Locke's sense?
step: The nominal essence is the complex idea we use to classify it: the ideas of pale green, light and brittle.
step: The real essence is the unknown arrangement of insensible parts that produces those qualities.
step: Locke's point is that we sort lumite by its nominal essence, because we do not know the real essence, and so the line we draw around lumite as a kind is partly of our making.
answer: Nominal essence: the collection of the ideas of pale green, light and brittle. Real essence: the hidden arrangement of parts that causes them.

@mistakes
- Saying that Locke thinks secondary qualities are "only in the mind": he holds that they are real powers in bodies; what is not in bodies is anything resembling the ideas.
- Treating the idea and the quality as the same thing: the quality is the power in the object, and the idea is in the mind.
- Concluding that Locke simply denies substance: he says we have only an obscure idea of substance in general but that we must suppose it.
- Saying that Locke's argument from the three bowls proves that all secondary qualities are subjective by itself: it shows that the ideas of warmth and cold do not resemble something in the water, and he extends this to other secondary qualities by further reasoning.

@quiz self
prompt: In a paragraph, explain what Locke's contrast between primary and secondary qualities was meant to achieve in his account of how the new science relates to ordinary experience, and state one objection.
hint: Consider how the corpuscularian picture treats the colors and tastes of things, and what Berkeley says about size and shape.
solution: Locke wanted to make room for the corpuscularian science, on which bodies have only size, shape, solidity and motion, while explaining why bodies look colored and taste sweet. The distinction lets him say that colors and tastes are real powers in bodies, grounded in the arrangement of tiny parts, without being resemblances of our ideas; so the world of physics and the world of experience are connected by causal powers. An objection, due to Berkeley, is that the arguments from relativity apply equally to size and shape (a distant tower looks small, a surface looks different under magnification), so the ideas of primary qualities are no more resembling than the others; and the claim that an idea can resemble only another idea threatens the claim that any idea resembles a body.
