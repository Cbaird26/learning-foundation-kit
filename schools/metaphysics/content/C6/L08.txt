@article
The sun has risen every morning of recorded history. You expect that it will rise tomorrow. Bread has nourished you all your life, and you expect that the next loaf will too. In *Enquiry* section 4 (and in the *Treatise*, Book 1, Part 3, section 6), Hume asks what justifies such expectations. His conclusion is one of the most persistent challenges in philosophy: there is no rational justification that does not already assume what it is meant to justify.

### Deductive and inductive reasoning

A **deductive** argument is valid when its premises guarantee its conclusion. An **inductive** argument has premises that are meant to support the conclusion without guaranteeing it, as when we infer from observed cases to unobserved ones. Hume's problem is not whether we do reason this way, or whether it succeeds, but whether it can be shown to be **rational**: whether we have a good reason to think that unobserved cases will be like observed ones.

### The argument

Hume's reasoning concerns **matters of fact**, as distinguished from relations of ideas. Reconstructed:

| P1 | All reasoning about matters of fact beyond present sensation and memory is founded on the relation of cause and effect. |
| P2 | Our knowledge of cause-effect relations comes from experience, not from a priori reasoning. |
| P3 | Inferring from past experience to unobserved cases presupposes that the future will resemble the past: the **uniformity principle** (UP), that unobserved cases resemble observed ones. |
| P4 | If UP has a rational justification, it is either by demonstrative reasoning or by probable reasoning from experience. |
| P5 | There is no demonstrative justification of UP: its denial is conceivable without contradiction. |
| P6 | There is no justification of UP by probable reasoning from experience: any such reasoning would presuppose UP, and so would be circular. |
| P7 | An inference that presupposes a principle with no rational justification has no rational justification itself. |
| C | So UP has no rational justification, and neither does inference from the observed to the unobserved. |

The argument is valid. Its premises are the thing to examine. P5 is hard to resist: the sun's failing to rise tomorrow is not a contradiction. P6 is the heart of the matter: the natural defense is that induction has worked in the past, so it will work in the future, but this is itself an inductive argument and relies on UP. Hume's conclusion is not that we should stop inferring. In *Enquiry* section 5 he says that we are determined by **custom** or habit, a principle of human nature that no argument is needed to establish, and he calls custom the great guide of human life. His **mitigated skepticism** accepts that we cannot justify our beliefs by reason alone and concludes that we should be modest.

### Responses

**Popper** accepted Hume's logical point and denied that science uses induction. Science, he argued, proceeds by conjectures and attempted refutations: a theory is never confirmed, but it can survive severe tests, which he called corroboration. Critics respond that treating corroboration as a reason to rely on a theory brings induction back in.

**Reichenbach** offered a **pragmatic vindication**: if there is any method that will find the long-run frequency of an event, the straight rule of induction will find it too, so nothing is lost by using it. Critics respond that many rival rules converge on the same limit and that the vindication says nothing about finite samples.

**Strawson** argued that the demand for a justification of induction is confused: to be reasonable in empirical matters just *is* to proportion belief to inductive evidence, so asking whether induction is reasonable is like asking whether the law is legal. Critics say that this shows only that we use the word "reasonable" that way, not that induction is truth-conducive.

**Externalists** hold that an inductive belief can be justified if it is formed by a process that is in fact reliable, even if we cannot show non-circularly that it is. The cost is that this seems to concede that we cannot answer Hume's question from the inside.

**Goodman's new riddle** (in *Fact, Fiction, and Forecast*, 1955) makes the problem harder. Let "grue" apply to a thing that is green if first examined before a future time $t$, and blue otherwise. All the emeralds examined so far are both green and grue. The same evidence supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue", which conflict about emeralds first examined after $t$. So even given UP, which regularities are projectible?

### Where things stand

There is no agreed solution. Bayesian approaches model inductive support with probability, but need prior probabilities that are not themselves fixed by evidence. The problem is not a skeptical worry about everyday life, but a challenge to the epistemic status of the method that science relies on.

@example
title: Finding the hidden premise
problem: "Every sample of this new alloy we have tested conducts electricity. So the next sample will conduct electricity." Show where the uniformity principle enters, and say why defending the inference by its track record is circular.
step: The premise is about tested samples, and the conclusion is about an untested sample. The argument is not deductively valid: the premise could be true and the conclusion false.
step: To make it valid we add a premise: untested samples of the alloy resemble tested ones. This is a particular case of the uniformity principle.
step: Defense by track record: "inferences of this form have worked in the past, so this one will too." This argument moves from past cases to a new case, and so presupposes that the future will resemble the past, which is the principle in question.
step: So the defense is circular, and Hume says that no non-circular defense is available.
answer: The uniformity principle enters as the missing premise, and defending it by past success assumes it.

@example
title: Applying Goodman's riddle
problem: Define "blurple" as: blue if first examined before time $t$, and green otherwise. Every sapphire examined before $t$ has been blue. Show that the evidence supports both "all sapphires are blue" and "all sapphires are blurple", and say what they predict about a sapphire first examined after $t$.
step: Let $t$ be a future time. Each sapphire examined so far was examined before $t$ and is blue, so each is blue.
step: By the definition, a sapphire first examined before $t$ is blurple exactly when it is blue, so each sapphire examined so far is also blurple.
step: "All sapphires are blue" predicts that a sapphire first examined after $t$ is blue. "All sapphires are blurple" predicts that it is green, since after $t$ blurple means green.
step: The evidence is the same for both, yet the predictions conflict, so we need a reason to treat "blue" as projectible and "blurple" as not.
answer: Both hypotheses fit all the evidence, and they give conflicting predictions for sapphires first examined after $t$.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hume thinks that inductive inference is unreasonable and that we should stop making it: he says that we cannot justify it by reason and that custom leads us to make it anyway.
- Confusing the problem of induction with the claim that induction sometimes fails: it concerns the justification of the method as a whole.
- Thinking that probability solves the problem: a probabilistic inference from past to future still needs a premise about uniformity or a prior probability.
- Thinking that Goodman's riddle is the same as Hume's: it asks which predicates and regularities are projectible, even granting that nature is uniform.

@quiz self
prompt: In about 150 words, compare Strawson's and Popper's responses to Hume's problem. Say what each concedes and what each denies.
hint: Popper concedes the logical point and denies that science uses induction; Strawson denies that the problem is a real demand.
solution: Popper concedes that induction cannot be justified logically, and denies that science needs it: theories are conjectures that are tested and rejected if they fail, and a theory that survives is corroborated, not confirmed. Strawson concedes that there is no deductive justification of induction, but denies that this is a defect: asking whether induction is justified presupposes a standard of reasonableness, and in empirical matters that standard just is the use of inductive evidence. The two responses face different objections: critics say that Popper's corroboration quietly relies on induction to guide action, and that Strawson's reply shows only how we use "reasonable" and not that induction is likely to succeed.
