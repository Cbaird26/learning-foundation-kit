@article
You are looking at a cherry. According to Locke it is a material substance with primary qualities, and your idea of it resembles those qualities. **George Berkeley** (*Principles of Human Knowledge*, 1710; *Three Dialogues*, 1713) replies that there is no such material thing, that the cherry is a collection of ideas, and that its *esse* (being) is *percipi* (being perceived). He presents this not as a skeptical thesis but as a defense of common sense, which holds that the things we see and touch are real.

### Esse is percipi

For Berkeley the **sensible things** (houses, mountains, rivers) are the things we perceive by the senses, and what we immediately perceive are ideas. So sensible things are ideas, or collections of ideas, and ideas exist only in a mind that perceives them. Minds themselves, which he calls **spirits**, are not ideas: their being is *percipere*, perceiving, and they act. Berkeley's thesis, called **immaterialism** or idealism, is that there is no *matter*, understood as a mind-independent, unthinking substance with qualities of its own.

### The argument from ideas

| P1 | The immediate objects of human knowledge are ideas (of sense, of reflection, or of imagination). |
| P2 | The sensible things we perceive by the senses are perceived immediately, and what is perceived immediately by sense is only ideas. |
| P3 | Ideas cannot exist except in a mind that perceives them. |
| C | So sensible things exist only in minds; their esse is percipi. |

The argument is valid. Its weight falls on P2 and P3. P2 rests on Berkeley's rejection of the Lockean claim that we perceive a material object *through* an idea, and P3 on his claim that an idea is a mind-dependent item. Berkeley adds the **likeness principle** (Principles §8): an idea can be like nothing but another idea, so a color or shape can only be like another color or shape. This is used to attack the Lockean claim that our ideas resemble material qualities.

### Against primary and secondary qualities

Locke held that ideas of primary qualities resemble bodies and ideas of secondary ones do not. Berkeley replies in two ways. First, the arguments from the **relativity of perception**, which Locke used for heat, work equally for size, shape and motion: a mite and a human see the same foot as differently sized, and a body seems fast or slow to different observers. Second, primary qualities cannot be **separated** from secondary: you cannot conceive of an extended thing with no color or texture, so if colors are in the mind, so is extension.

### The master argument

Berkeley invites you to try to conceive a tree in a park with no one perceiving it. His reply to the claim that this is easy (§§22–23) is that you are conceiving it at the very moment, so it is an idea in your mind. In standard form:

| P1 | If a sensible object could exist unconceived, then it would be possible to conceive of such an object existing unconceived. |
| P2 | Whatever one conceives is thereby conceived: it is an idea in one's mind. |
| C1 | So it is impossible to conceive of a sensible object existing unconceived. |
| C | So a sensible object cannot exist unconceived. |

C follows from P1 and C1 by modus tollens. C1 is claimed to follow from P2. The standard objection is that it does not. P2 says that anything one conceives is conceived, but the content of one's thought can be of a tree *that no one is conceiving*: the thought is conceived, but the tree, as thought of, is represented as unconceived. The argument runs together **conceiving of something as unconceived** and **conceiving of something while it is conceived**, a scope confusion. Whether this diagnosis settles the matter, or whether a stronger version of the argument can be built on the claim that conceiving a thing requires having it as an idea, is debated.

### What Berkeley accepts

Berkeley does not deny that the cherry exists, nor that it is red, round and sweet; he denies only that these are qualities of a mindless substance. He relies on the claim that the notion of matter is empty, since we have no idea of a thing that is neither perceived nor perceiving. His attack on **abstract ideas** in the Introduction to the *Principles* supports it: Locke's notion of "substance in general" is an abstraction with no content.

@example
title: Applying the argument from ideas to a new object
problem: Use Berkeley's argument from ideas to state what a bell is, and say which premise a Lockean would reject.
step: P1: what we perceive of the bell by sense is a sound, a shape, a color and a feeling of cold metal. P2: we perceive these immediately, and they are ideas. P3: ideas exist only in a perceiving mind.
step: C: the bell, as a sensible thing, is a collection of ideas existing in minds; its esse is percipi.
step: A Lockean rejects P2: we perceive material objects through ideas that resemble qualities in them, so the immediate object is not the whole of what we perceive.
step: Berkeley replies with the likeness principle: an idea can be like nothing but an idea, so there is no resemblance to something in matter.
answer: The bell is a collection of ideas; the Lockean rejects P2, and Berkeley answers with the likeness principle.

@example
title: Diagnosing the master argument
problem: A student says: "I can imagine an empty cave full of crystals that nobody ever visits. So Berkeley is wrong." Another says: "No, you are imagining it, so someone is perceiving it." Who is right about the master argument?
step: The first student relies on the claim that one can conceive of something existing unconceived. The second repeats P2: whatever one conceives is conceived.
step: P2 is true in this sense: the act of imagining is an act of a mind. But the first student is claiming something different: that the content of the thought is of crystals existing when no one thinks of them.
step: So the second student's reply shows only that the thinker is conceiving the cave, not that the cave is represented as conceived.
step: Berkeley needs the stronger claim that conceiving requires the object to be an idea in the conceiver's mind, and that is what the realist rejects.
answer: The first student is not refuted by the argument as stated: the second student's point is a scope confusion, unless the further claim about conceiving can be defended.

@mistakes
- Saying that Berkeley denies that tables and trees exist: he says they exist, as collections of ideas, and denies that there is matter.
- Applying esse is percipi to minds: minds exist by perceiving (percipere) and are not ideas.
- Reading Berkeley as a skeptic: his aim was to defend common sense and refute skepticism, which he thought was a consequence of the doctrine of matter.
- Saying that the master argument proves its conclusion because I cannot picture an unperceived tree: the question is whether the content of a thought can be of something unconceived.
