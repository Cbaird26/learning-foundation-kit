@article
When Berkeley demanded that you find an idea of matter, and Locke admitted that we have only an obscure idea of substance, both were making a claim about where ideas come from. **David Hume** makes this the center of his method. In the *Treatise of Human Nature* (1739 to 1740) and the *Enquiry concerning Human Understanding* (1748), he asks of every philosophical term: from what experience is its idea derived? If there is none, the term has no clear content.

### Impressions and ideas

Hume calls the contents of the mind **perceptions** and divides them into **impressions** and **ideas**. Impressions are the more forceful and lively: sensations, passions and emotions as they first appear. Ideas are the fainter images of these in thinking and reasoning. The difference is one of **force and vivacity**, not of kind. Hume's "idea" is narrower than Locke's, for whom it covered every object of the mind, including sensations. Hume also distinguishes **simple** perceptions, which cannot be divided into parts, from **complex** ones. Some impressions are of sensation (colors, sounds) and others of reflection (passions, desires), which arise from ideas.

### The copy principle

Hume's first principle of the science of human nature is that all our *simple* ideas, in their first appearance, are derived from simple impressions that correspond to them and that they exactly represent. This is the **copy principle**. It is about simple ideas only: complex ideas can be new combinations of simple ones, as in the idea of a golden mountain or a winged horse. The imagination can recombine, but cannot create the raw materials.

His evidence is of three kinds. First, constant conjunction: whenever we examine, we find every simple idea matched by an impression that resembles it. Second, priority: the impression comes first, and the idea follows, never the reverse. Third, absence: a person who lacks a sense organ or has never had a certain experience lacks the corresponding idea. Someone blind from birth has no idea of colors, and a person who has never tasted a pineapple lacks the idea of its flavor.

### A counterexample

Hume himself raises a possible counterexample: the **missing shade of blue**. Suppose someone has seen every shade of blue except one, and the shades they have seen are arranged in order from darkest to lightest with a gap where the missing shade would be. Could they form the idea of the missing shade? Hume says that they can, and that this shows that the copy principle has an exception, but he thinks the case is so singular that it does not merit changing his general principle. Interpreters disagree about whether he regards the case as a real exception or tries to explain it away; the question is a standard one in the literature.

### The copy principle as a test

Hume applies the principle to philosophical terms:

| P1 | Every simple idea is derived from a simple impression that it copies (the copy principle). |
| P2 | There is no impression from which the supposed simple idea of X is derived. |
| C | So there is no genuine simple idea of X; the term "X" has no definite content beyond what can be traced to impressions. |

The argument is valid and is used by Hume against **substance** (we have no impression of a substance as something distinct from the collection of qualities), against the **self** (next week) and against **necessary connection** (next lesson). Its strength depends on both premises. P1 is an empirical generalization, and one may ask whether it can serve as a test of meaning if it is itself an empirical claim; the missing shade of blue, if Hume's verdict on it is accepted, is at least one exception. P2 depends on what counts as an impression: Hume's impressions of reflection may supply ideas for which there is no external impression.

### Relations of ideas and matters of fact

In the *Enquiry* Hume divides the objects of human reason into **relations of ideas** and **matters of fact**. The first, such as that the three angles of a triangle equal two right angles, are discoverable by thought alone, and their denial is a contradiction. The second concern what is the case in the world, their denial is conceivable without contradiction, and they depend on experience. This division is often called **Hume's fork**. It is not itself the copy principle, but it works with it: for any claim, either it is about relations among ideas, or it is about matters of fact and must be traceable to impressions.

### Comparing with Locke and Berkeley

Hume agrees with Berkeley that we have no idea of matter as distinct from perceptions, and endorses Berkeley's view that general ideas are particular ideas used in a general way. He does not follow Berkeley to idealism or to Berkeley's God: Hume's own project is to describe how the mind works, not to infer a divine cause of its perceptions.

@example
title: Applying the copy-principle test
problem: A philosopher says that we have an idea of "the essence of a thing, over and above all its observable qualities". Apply Hume's copy-principle test.
step: P1: every simple idea is copied from a simple impression.
step: P2: all we find when we look for an impression of an essence over and above the observable qualities is the qualities themselves; no distinct impression appears.
step: C: so there is no genuine simple idea of such an essence, and the term has no content beyond the qualities.
step: A defender may reply that the idea is complex, being built from simple ideas (such as "underlying" and "cause"), or that P1 has exceptions.
answer: By Hume's test the idea has no content beyond the observable qualities; the defender can resist by arguing that the idea is complex or that the copy principle is too strong.

@example
title: Simple or complex, impression or idea
problem: Classify: (a) the sight of a ripe plum in front of you, (b) your memory of that sight tomorrow, (c) the thought of a purple elephant, (d) the anger you feel on being cheated.
step: (a) The sight is a simple or complex impression of sensation, depending on whether you are looking at one color or the whole fruit; it is vivid.
step: (b) The memory is an idea: a fainter copy of the impression.
step: (c) The purple elephant is a complex idea made from simple ideas of purple and of elephant parts that were copied from impressions.
step: (d) The anger is an impression of reflection, a passion arising in you.
answer: (a) impression of sensation; (b) idea; (c) complex idea; (d) impression of reflection.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hume denies that we can imagine anything we have not experienced: complex ideas can be new combinations of ideas derived from experience.
- Saying that the copy principle applies to all ideas, simple and complex: it concerns simple ideas.
- Treating the difference between impression and idea as one of kind: it is a difference of force and vivacity.
- Treating Hume's fork as the copy principle: it classifies claims as relations of ideas or matters of fact.

prompt: Evaluate the claim that the copy principle can serve as a test of meaning. Give one argument for and one against.
hint: Think of the evidence Hume offers, and the status of the principle.
solution: For: if every simple idea must be derived from experience, then a term that cannot be traced to any impression has no content, and the test gives a clear way of exposing empty philosophical terms. The evidence of absent senses supports it. Against: the principle is itself an empirical generalization, so it is not clear that it can be the standard of meaningfulness; the missing shade of blue shows an exception; and some meaningful terms, such as theoretical terms in science, are not obviously traceable to impressions. A defender can reply that the principle is a working method and that it allows complex ideas.
