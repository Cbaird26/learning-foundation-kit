@article
Look inward for a moment. You notice a sound, a tightness in the shoulders, a half-formed thought about dinner. Do you also find *yourself*, the one who has these experiences? **Hume** (*Treatise*, Book 1, Part 4, section 6) says that he does not, and that this casts doubt on the common view that we each have a simple, unchanging self that persists through life.

### The argument against a simple self

| P1 | If we have an idea of a simple self that persists through life, then there is an impression from which it is copied that remains constant and invariable through life (by the copy principle). |
| P2 | There is no such impression: whenever I look into myself, I come upon a particular perception (heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure), never myself without a perception. |
| C1 | So there is no idea of a simple, persisting self. |
| P3 | Nothing other than a simple persisting self or a bundle of perceptions could be what we call the self (a premise Hume assumes rather than argues for). |
| C | So the self is nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions that follow one another with great rapidity and are in perpetual flux. |

The argument has two stages. The step to C1 is valid given P1 and P2, and it uses the copy principle. The step to C is further: it needs P3 to get from the absence of an idea of a simple self to the positive claim that the self is a bundle, and P3 is assumed more than defended. The theory is the **bundle theory**. Hume adds the image of the mind as a kind of theater in which perceptions appear, with the warning that the comparison must not mislead us, since there is no place in which they are staged and no material of which the theater is made.

### Why we believe in identity

If we are only a bundle, why do we think we are one persisting self? Hume answers that the imagination confuses a *succession of related perceptions* with a single unchanging object. The relations that do this work are **resemblance** and **causation**: our perceptions resemble one another and cause one another, and memory discovers these connections. He compares the mind to a republic or commonwealth, whose members are united by ties of government and which retains its identity through changes of members.

### Skepticism and belief in the external world

In Book 1, Part 4, Hume argues that reason, applied to itself, undermines its own conclusions, and that our belief in the continued and independent existence of bodies cannot come from the senses (they give only fleeting perceptions) nor from reason, but arises from the **imagination**, acting on the constancy and coherence of our impressions. At the end of Part 4 he reports that the skeptical arguments leave him in a kind of "melancholy", from which nature cures him, since dining, playing backgammon and talking with friends restore his ordinary beliefs. In the *Enquiry*, section 12, he distinguishes **Pyrrhonian** or excessive skepticism, which doubts everything and cannot be sustained in practice, from **mitigated skepticism**, which accepts the limits of reason, avoids dogmatism and confines itself to subjects within human capacity.

### Natural belief

A **natural belief** is a belief that we cannot help having because of the constitution of human nature, whether or not reason supports it. Belief in causal connection, in bodies, and in the persisting self are examples. The commentator Norman Kemp Smith (1941) emphasized this theme and argued that Hume's central doctrine is that belief is a matter of feeling and instinct, not reason. Hume's own text supports the idea, and later readers dispute how far the reading goes.

### The Appendix

In the Appendix to the *Treatise* (1740) Hume wrote that on reconsidering his account of personal identity he found that it was inconsistent. He says that two principles are involved that he cannot make consistent: that all our distinct perceptions are distinct existences, and that the mind never perceives any real connection among distinct existences. What exactly the problem is, and whether it is serious, is disputed; one reading is that he cannot explain how perceptions are united in one bundle without a real connection.

### Evaluation

The most common objection is that Hume's search for the self assumes a searcher: who is it that looks inward and fails to find an impression? A second objection is that the bundle theory needs an account of what makes some perceptions belong to one bundle and not another; Hume appeals to resemblance and causation, and the question is whether these can be specified without presupposing the self. The reductionist theory of persons in the twentieth century, especially that of Derek Parfit, was influenced by the bundle view.

@example
title: Applying Hume's search to a new case
problem: You try to find your self while reading a map: you notice the colored lines, a slight worry that you are late, and an urge to turn left. Use Hume's argument to state what he says about this.
step: P1: if we had an idea of a simple persisting self, there would be a constant impression from which it is copied.
step: P2: in the search you find only particular perceptions: lines, worry, urge. None is constant, since they change within seconds.
step: C1: there is no impression, and so no idea, of a simple persisting self.
step: A critic notes that the one who searches is not among the things found, and so the search presupposes a self.
answer: On Hume's argument, the search finds only a changing bundle of perceptions; the critic replies that the searcher is presupposed.

@example
title: Three attitudes to doubt
problem: Classify each: (a) "Since reason cannot prove the senses reliable, I should suspend judgment about everything and act on nothing." (b) "I cannot help believing in an external world, though I cannot prove it." (c) "Reason has limits, so I will avoid speculation beyond everyday life and science."
step: (a) This is Pyrrhonian or excessive skepticism, which Hume holds cannot be sustained.
step: (b) This expresses natural belief: a belief produced by human nature regardless of argument.
step: (c) This is mitigated skepticism, which Hume endorses.
answer: (a) Pyrrhonian skepticism; (b) natural belief; (c) mitigated skepticism.

@mistakes
- Saying that Hume denies that there are experiences or thoughts: he denies that there is a simple self distinct from them.
- Attributing to the Appendix a simple retraction of the bundle theory: Hume says only that he cannot make his principles consistent, and interpreters disagree about the problem.
- Treating natural belief as the claim that those beliefs are true: it is about why we hold the beliefs.
- Confusing Pyrrhonian with mitigated skepticism.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain in about 150 words what Hume means by natural belief, with two examples, and say whether natural belief can be a reason to think that a belief is true.
hint: Natural belief is about the source of the belief, not about its truth.
solution: A natural belief is one that human nature leads us to hold whether or not reason supports it. Hume's examples include belief in the continued existence of bodies, which arises from the imagination, and belief in causal connection, which arises from custom. That we cannot help holding a belief is a fact about its source. Whether it is a reason to think the belief true is a further question: a defender of common sense, such as Reid, argues that beliefs that are inescapable and universal have a claim on us, while a critic points out that a belief can be inescapable and false, and that the origin of a belief does not by itself tell us whether it is reliable.
