@article
Acids taste sour, bases feel slippery, and both change the color of indicators. The chemistry behind this is the transfer of hydrogen ions.

### Brønsted–Lowry definitions

An **acid** is a proton ($\text{H}^+$) donor and a **base** is a proton acceptor. When an acid HA donates a proton to water it forms its **conjugate base** $\text{A}^-$, while water becomes $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ (often written $\text{H}^+$):

$$\text{HA}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}_3\text{O}^++\text{A}^-.$$

Pairs that differ by one proton are **conjugate pairs**: $\text{NH}_4^+$ / $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{PO}_4^-$ / $\text{HPO}_4^{2-}$. A strong acid has a weak conjugate base and vice versa. Water can act as either, so it is **amphoteric**.

### Strong versus weak

**Strong acids** ionize completely in water: HCl, HBr, HI, $\text{HNO}_3$, $\text{HClO}_4$ and the first ionization of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. **Strong bases** dissociate completely: the hydroxides of Group 1 and the heavier Group 2 metals (such as $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$). Most other acids and bases are **weak** and ionize only partly, for example acetic acid and ammonia.

### Water, pH and pOH

Water self-ionizes slightly. At $25\,^\circ\text{C}$,

$$K_w=[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]=1.0\times10^{-14}.$$

The pH scale compresses the huge range of concentrations:

$$\text{pH}=-\log_{10}[\text{H}^+],\qquad \text{pOH}=-\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-],\qquad \text{pH}+\text{pOH}=14.00.$$

Neutral water has $[\text{H}^+]=1.0\times10^{-7}\ \text{M}$, so pH $=7$; acidic solutions have pH $<7$, basic ones pH $>7$. A change of one pH unit is a factor of ten in $[\text{H}^+]$. Since $[\text{H}^+]=10^{-\text{pH}}$, you can go either direction. In pH calculations, the number of decimal places in the pH equals the number of significant figures in the concentration.

### Strong acids and bases

For a strong monoprotic acid, $[\text{H}^+]$ equals the acid concentration. For a strong base, find $[\text{OH}^-]$ (count the hydroxides per formula unit), calculate pOH, then pH $=14-\text{pOH}$.

### Weak acids

For a weak acid, the **acid ionization constant** is

$$K_a=\frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}.$$

If the acid is not too dilute and $K_a$ is small, let $x=[\text{H}^+]=[\text{A}^-]$ and approximate $[\text{HA}]\approx C$, the initial concentration: $K_a\approx x^2/C$, so $x=\sqrt{K_aC}$. Check that $x$ is less than about $5\%$ of $C$.

### Buffers

A **buffer** is a mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. The **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** gives its pH:

$$\text{pH}=\text{p}K_a+\log_{10}\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}.$$

When the two concentrations are equal, $\text{pH}=\text{p}K_a$. Buffers matter in biology: blood is buffered near pH $7.4$ largely by the carbonic acid–bicarbonate system.

### Neutralization and titration

An acid and a base react to give a salt and water. In a **titration**, a solution of known concentration is added to a measured volume of the unknown until the **equivalence point**, where the moles of acid equal the moles of base for a one-to-one reaction; an indicator changes color near that point (the endpoint).

@example
title: pH of a dilute strong acid
problem: What is the pH of $2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}$ HCl?
step: HCl is a strong acid and fully ionizes, so $[\text{H}^+]=2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}$.
step: $\text{pH}=-\log_{10}(2.0\times10^{-4})=-(\log_{10}2.0-4)=-(0.301-4)$.
step: $\text{pH}=3.70$ (two decimal places for two significant figures).

@example
title: pH of a strong base with two hydroxides
problem: What is the pH of $0.020\ \text{M}$ $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$?
step: Each formula unit supplies two hydroxide ions: $[\text{OH}^-]=2(0.020)=0.040\ \text{M}$.
step: $\text{pOH}=-\log_{10}(0.040)=1.40$.
step: $\text{pH}=14.00-1.40=12.60$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that pH is a base-ten logarithm with a negative sign, so a higher $[\text{H}^+]$ gives a lower pH.
- Reporting the pH of a strong base from $[\text{OH}^-]$ directly without converting through pOH.
- Treating a weak acid as fully ionized (so $[\text{H}^+]$ equals $C$) instead of using $K_a$.
- Neglecting the number of hydroxides in a formula such as $\text{Ba(OH)}_2$.

@practice num
prompt: What is the pH of $0.10\ \text{M}$ acetic acid, for which $K_a=1.8\times10^{-5}$? Use the small-$x$ approximation and give the pH to two decimal places.
answer: 2.87
check: -log10((1.8e-5 * 0.10) ** 0.5)
tol: 0.02
hint: Set $x=[\text{H}^+]$ and assume $0.10-x\approx0.10$.
hint: $x=\sqrt{K_aC}=\sqrt{1.8\times10^{-6}}$.
solution: $x=\sqrt{(1.8\times10^{-5})(0.10)}=1.34\times10^{-3}\ \text{M}$, which is about $1.3\%$ of $0.10$, so the approximation is valid. $\text{pH}=-\log_{10}(1.34\times10^{-3})=2.87$.

@practice num
prompt: An acetate buffer contains $0.20\ \text{M}$ acetate ion and $0.10\ \text{M}$ acetic acid ($\text{p}K_a=4.76$). What is its pH?
answer: 5.06
check: 4.76 + log10(0.20 / 0.10)
tol: 0.02
hint: Use the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation.
hint: $\log_{10}(0.20/0.10)=\log_{10}2=0.301$.
solution: $\text{pH}=4.76+\log_{10}\dfrac{0.20}{0.10}=4.76+0.30=5.06$.
