@article
A gas expands to fill its container, is easily compressed and mixes completely with other gases. Four properties describe a sample: **pressure** $P$, **volume** $V$, **temperature** $T$ and **amount** $n$ (in moles).

### Units

Pressure is force per area. Equivalent units: $1\ \text{atm}=101.325\ \text{kPa}=760\ \text{mmHg (torr)}=1.01325\ \text{bar}$. Gas law calculations require **absolute temperature** in kelvin:

$$T(\text{K})=T(^\circ\text{C})+273.15.$$

Never use Celsius in a gas law: $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ is not "zero gas".

### The gas laws

For a fixed amount of gas:

- **Boyle's law** (constant $T$): $P_1V_1=P_2V_2$. Pressure and volume are inversely related.
- **Charles's law** (constant $P$): $\dfrac{V_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{V_2}{T_2}$. Volume is proportional to Kelvin temperature.
- **Gay-Lussac's law** (constant $V$): $\dfrac{P_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{P_2}{T_2}$.
- **Avogadro's law** (constant $T$, $P$): $V\propto n$.

Together they give the **combined gas law**: $\dfrac{P_1V_1}{T_1}=\dfrac{P_2V_2}{T_2}$. Use any pressure and volume units as long as both sides match, but $T$ must be in kelvin.

### The ideal gas law

$$PV=nRT.$$

With $P$ in atm, $V$ in liters, $n$ in moles and $T$ in kelvin, the gas constant is $R=0.08206\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$. In SI units, $R=8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ (or $\text{kPa L mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$). At standard temperature and pressure (STP, $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\ \text{atm}$, a definition used in many textbooks; IUPAC now uses $1$ bar) one mole of an ideal gas occupies about $22.4\ \text{L}$; at $0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $1\ \text{bar}$ it is about $22.7\ \text{L}$.

The law also gives the molar mass of a gas from a measured mass: $M=\dfrac{mRT}{PV}$, and the density $\rho=\dfrac{PM}{RT}$.

### Mixtures: Dalton's law

In a mixture, each gas exerts its **partial pressure**, and the total is the sum:

$$P_{\text{total}}=P_1+P_2+\cdots,\qquad P_i=x_iP_{\text{total}},$$

where $x_i=n_i/n_{\text{total}}$ is the mole fraction. Gases collected over water include water vapor, which you subtract from the total.

### Real gases and kinetic theory

The **kinetic-molecular theory** pictures gas particles as tiny, far apart and in random motion, with average kinetic energy proportional to $T$ (kelvin). At high pressure or low temperature, attractions and particle volume matter and real gases deviate from ideal behavior.

@example
title: Using the ideal gas law
problem: What volume does $0.250\ \text{mol}$ of an ideal gas occupy at $20.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ and $1.00\ \text{atm}$?
step: Convert the temperature to kelvin: $20.0+273.15=293.15\ \text{K}$.
step: Rearrange $PV=nRT$ to $V=\dfrac{nRT}{P}$.
step: Substitute: $V=\dfrac{(0.250)(0.08206)(293.15)}{1.00}=6.01\ \text{L}$.

@example
title: The combined gas law
problem: A gas occupies $4.0\ \text{L}$ at $2.0\ \text{atm}$ and $280\ \text{K}$. What is its volume at $1.0\ \text{atm}$ and $350\ \text{K}$?
step: Rearrange the combined gas law: $V_2=V_1\dfrac{P_1}{P_2}\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$.
step: Pressure drops by half, which doubles the volume; temperature rises by a factor of $\dfrac{350}{280}=1.25$.
step: $V_2=4.0\times\dfrac{2.0}{1.0}\times\dfrac{350}{280}=10\ \text{L}$.

@mistakes
- Using degrees Celsius instead of kelvin in a gas law.
- Using $R=8.314$ with pressure in atm, or $R=0.08206$ with pressure in kPa; the units of $R$ must match $P$ and $V$.
- Applying a gas law that holds one quantity constant when two or more properties change; use the combined law or $PV=nRT$.
- Forgetting to subtract water vapor pressure for a gas collected over water.

@quiz num
prompt: How many moles of gas are in a $10.0\ \text{L}$ container at $1.50\ \text{atm}$ and $300\ \text{K}$? Use $R=0.08206\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.
answer: 0.609
check: 1.50 * 10.0 / (0.08206 * 300)
tol: 1%
unit: mol
hint: Solve $PV=nRT$ for $n$.
hint: $n=\dfrac{PV}{RT}$.
solution: $n=\dfrac{(1.50)(10.0)}{(0.08206)(300)}=0.609\ \text{mol}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A mixture contains $2.0\ \text{mol N}_2$, $1.0\ \text{mol O}_2$ and $1.0\ \text{mol Ar}$ at a total pressure of $3.2\ \text{atm}$. What is the partial pressure of oxygen in atm?
answer: 0.80
check: 3.2 * 1.0 / (2.0 + 1.0 + 1.0)
tol: 0.01

hint: The mole fraction of oxygen is $1.0/4.0$.
