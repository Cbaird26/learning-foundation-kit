@article
If there is only one substance, then a mind and a body cannot be two substances that interact. What are they? Spinoza's answer is among the most original in the period: the mind and the body are one and the same thing, understood in two ways. The same view yields a striking account of freedom. We are not free in the sense that our choices come from nowhere, but we can be free in another sense, and the *Ethics* is written to show how.

### One thing under two attributes

In Part 2 Spinoza argues that the **order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things** (2p7). In the note to that proposition he adds that the thinking substance and the extended substance are one and the same substance, now understood under the attribute of thought and now under the attribute of extension. The human mind is the idea of the body (2p13): it is the way in which that body, a mode of extension, is expressed in thought. Mind and body are therefore not two substances but one mode of the one substance, conceived under two attributes. Later writers call this **parallelism**, a label that is not Spinoza's, and interpreters dispute whether it is better read as an identity of the mental and the physical mode or as a correlation between two distinct but corresponding series.

### No interaction

Spinoza draws a strong consequence: the body cannot cause the mind to think, nor the mind cause the body to move (3p2).

| P1 | Thought and extension are distinct attributes, each conceived through itself (1p10). |
| P2 | The modes of an attribute have God as their cause only insofar as God is considered under that attribute, and not under another (2p6). |
| C1 | So a mental event can be explained only by other mental events, and a bodily event only by bodily events. |
| C | So there is no causal interaction between mind and body, although the order and connection of their modes is the same (2p7). |

This avoids Elisabeth's problem by denying that there is interaction to explain, without Malebranche's appeal to God's action on each occasion. The cost is that the correspondence between mental and bodily series must then be accounted for, and Spinoza accounts for it by identity of substance. A critic can ask whether this makes mental properties explanatorily idle or turns every body into something with a mind: Spinoza says that all individual things are animate in different degrees (2p13, note), a view that connects to later panpsychism.

### Striving and the affects

Each thing, insofar as it can by its own power, **strives to persevere in its being** (3p6), and this striving, the **conatus**, is its actual essence (3p7). Human emotions, or **affects**, are explained as increases and decreases in the power of the body and mind to act: joy is the passage to a greater power, sadness to a lesser. This is a naturalistic psychology: Spinoza says that he will treat human actions and appetites as if he were considering lines, planes and bodies (the preface to Part 3).

### Necessity and the denial of free will

Spinoza holds that in nature there is nothing contingent: everything is determined by the necessity of the divine nature to exist and to act in a certain way (1p29). It follows that the will is not a free cause but a necessary one (1p32), and that in the mind there is no absolute or free will; each volition is determined by a cause, which is determined by another, and so on (2p48). The argument is:

| P1 | Everything that exists or happens is determined by a prior cause, and ultimately by the nature of God (1p28-29). |
| P2 | Each volition is a particular mode of thought, and so is determined by a prior cause (2p48). |
| C | So there is no absolute free will: no volition is uncaused. |

Spinoza explains why people think otherwise: they are conscious of their actions and desires but ignorant of the causes that determine them (2p35, note).

### Freedom as self-determination

Spinoza does not conclude that freedom is unreal. In 1d7, a thing is **free** when it exists and acts from the necessity of its own nature alone, and **constrained** when it is determined to act by other things. A human being is more free as more of what she does follows from her own nature, which means from **adequate ideas**, an understanding of the causes. Parts 4 and 5 describe the bondage of the person ruled by passions, whose actions come from external causes, and the way understanding the affects reduces their power and brings the highest satisfaction, which Spinoza calls the intellectual love of God. This is usually read as a form of compatibilism, in which freedom is self-determination and not the absence of causal determination, though what exactly it is to be moved by one's own nature is debated. Philosophers disagree about free will, and Spinoza's view should be assessed as one position among several: libertarians reject his premise of universal determination, and others accept it but dispute whether self-determination is enough for responsibility.

@example
title: One event under two attributes
problem: Ana decides to stand and then stands up. How does Spinoza describe this event, and how does his description differ from Descartes's?
step: On Descartes's view, a mental event (Ana's volition) causes a bodily event (her standing up). The two are different substances' modes interacting.
step: On Spinoza's view, Ana's decision is a mode of thought and her standing is a mode of extension, and these are the same thing expressed under two attributes. The decision is caused by prior ideas, and the standing by prior bodily states, with no cross-attribute causation.
step: The two series are parallel because they are one order of things expressed in two ways (2p7).
answer: For Spinoza the decision does not cause the standing: both belong to one thing under two attributes, each explained within its own attribute. For Descartes, the decision causes the standing.

@example
title: Free or constrained in Spinoza's sense
problem: Is each person free, in the sense of 1d7 and Part 5, or constrained? (a) Someone who understands why a diet is good for her and follows it because of that understanding. (b) Someone driven to overeat by an anxiety that she does not understand.
step: Spinoza calls a thing free when it acts from the necessity of its own nature, and in a human being this is acting from adequate ideas.
step: (a) She acts from understanding of the causes, which is an adequate idea, so her action follows from her own nature: she is free in his sense, even though her action is determined.
step: (b) The anxiety acts as an external cause whose operation she does not grasp, so she is moved by inadequate ideas: constrained.
answer: (a) free in Spinoza's sense; (b) constrained.

@mistakes
- Saying that Spinoza makes the mind cause the body's movements: he denies that either can cause the other (3p2).
- Calling Spinoza's view parallelism as if it were his own term: the label is later, and it may be misleading if Spinoza's view is an identity.
- Saying that Spinoza denies freedom altogether: he denies absolute free will but offers a conception of freedom as acting from one's own nature.
- Confusing necessity with fatalism: for Spinoza our desires and understanding make a real difference to what we do, but they are themselves caused.

prompt: Explain in about five sentences what Spinoza means by freedom and why it is compatible with his claim that everything is determined. State one objection.
hint: Use the contrast between being determined by one's own nature and by external causes.
solution: Spinoza means by freedom acting from the necessity of one's own nature alone, as opposed to being constrained by external causes. In a human being this means acting from adequate ideas, that is, from understanding of the causes, and not from passions that she does not understand. This is compatible with determinism, because being free does not require being uncaused: the free action is determined by the person's own nature and not by something outside her. A person who understands more is more free. An objection is that her nature is itself determined by causes outside her, so the distinction between self-determination and external determination does not give the freedom that responsibility seems to require.

solution: Descartes holds that mind and body are two distinct substances that can exist apart and that interact causally.
