@article
When Ana decides to raise her arm and it rises, we say that her decision caused the movement. When one billiard ball strikes another and the second rolls away, we say that the first ball moved it. Nicolas Malebranche (1638-1715), a Cartesian priest and philosopher, held that these ordinary statements are false. In the strict sense there is only one cause, God. Created things are merely **occasions** on which God acts.

### The doctrine

**Occasionalism** is the view that God is the only true cause: creatures have no power to produce effects, and what we call a cause is an **occasional cause**, an event on the occasion of which God brings about the effect, according to regular laws that God has established. Occasionalism has older roots: medieval Islamic theologians of the Ash'arite tradition, including al-Ghazali, defended a form of it for theological reasons. In Malebranche's case the motivation comes from Descartes's system, which has the problem of interaction of week 6, and from a strong view of divine power.

### The argument from necessary connection

Malebranche's central argument appears in *The Search after Truth* (1674-75). It says what it takes to be a true cause:

| P1 | A true cause is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect. |
| P2 | The mind perceives a necessary connection only between the will of an infinitely perfect being, that is God, and its effects. |
| C1 | So only God's will is a true cause. |
| C | So finite things, including bodies and human minds, are at most occasional causes. |

The argument is valid. Premise P2 is supported by considering the cases. Between a body's motion and the motion of the body it strikes, we see no logical necessity, and the second body might have stayed still. Between a person's will and the movement of an arm there is no necessary connection either: Ana does not even know how the nerves and animal spirits must move for her arm to rise, and cannot move them by knowing how. But if God, who is all-powerful, wills that something happen, then it is inconceivable that it fails to happen, and here we do perceive a necessary connection. Premise P1 is the heart of the view: that the idea of a cause includes a necessary connection. Later, Hume's analysis of the idea of causation pressed a related question about necessary connection, but he reached a different conclusion, and whether he was influenced by Malebranche is a matter of scholarly discussion.

### The argument from conservation

A second argument relies on a premise Descartes shared: God conserves every creature at each moment, and conservation is the same as continuous creation. Malebranche argued (for instance in the *Dialogues on Metaphysics*) that God cannot create a body without creating it somewhere in particular. So if a body is at one place at one moment and at another place at the next, God has created it in both places, and it is God's act, not the body's power, that explains the change. The argument is intended to show that even bodies do not move one another by their own power.

### Objections and replies

1. **It makes everything a miracle.** Leibniz and others objected that if God does every effect directly, then the world has no natural order of its own and every event is a miracle. Malebranche replies that God acts by **general volitions**, laws that govern all occasions, so the order is regular and not arbitrary.
2. **It undermines science.** If bodies have no powers, what is natural science about? A Malebranchean can reply that science studies the regularities among occasional causes, which are laws God keeps, and does not need hidden powers; the order of occasions is what predicts the next event.
3. **It empties creatures of activity.** Leibniz held that a substance without any active force is no substance at all. Critics added that occasionalism seems to push toward the view that only God is real, which is a direction in which Spinoza's monism, in a different form, also moves.
4. **Freedom and evil.** If God produces every motion, including the movements of human limbs in sinful acts, is God responsible? Malebranche's answer is that God acts by general laws and not by particular volitions aimed at harm, and that human beings are free in consenting to what they are inclined toward. Whether this is satisfactory is disputed.

### Where occasionalism sits among the options

| View | How mind and body are related | Who explains the correlation |
|---|---|---|
| Interaction (Descartes) | Each really acts on the other | The substances themselves |
| Occasionalism (Malebranche) | Neither acts on the other | God, on each occasion |
| Harmony (Leibniz) | Neither acts on the other | God, at the creation |

@example
title: Reconstructing the argument from necessary connection
problem: Put Malebranche's argument that finite things are not true causes in standard form, and state which premise a critic would most likely reject.
step: P1: a true cause is one such that the mind perceives a necessary connection between it and its effect. P2: the mind perceives a necessary connection only between the will of God and its effects. C: so only God's will is a true cause, and finite things are occasional causes.
step: Validity: if only God's will meets the test, and a true cause must meet the test, then nothing else is a true cause.
step: A critic could reject P1 by saying that causes need not involve a necessary connection that the mind can perceive; we might know a cause only through regular succession, or through the experience of effort in our own agency.
answer: The argument is valid, and the premise most likely to be rejected is P1.

@example
title: Describing an event under two views
problem: Ana lights a match and a flame appears. Describe the event according to (a) the ordinary view that the match's friction causes the flame, and (b) Malebranche.
step: (a) The friction between match and surface is the cause, and the flame is its effect. The power to produce the effect belongs to the friction.
step: (b) The friction is an occasional cause. On this occasion God brings about the flame in accordance with a general law, so the power belongs to God alone.
step: What is common: both views accept the regularity that friction of this kind is followed by a flame. They disagree about where the power to produce the effect lies.
answer: The ordinary view locates the power in the friction; Malebranche locates it in God, treating the friction as the occasion for God's act under a general law.

@mistakes
- Saying that Malebranche denies that regular laws hold: God acts by general laws, and the regularity among occasions is real.
- Identifying occasionalism with pre-established harmony: in harmony God arranges everything at creation, while in occasionalism God acts on each occasion.
- Saying that Malebranche denies that we move our arms: he holds that our decision is the occasion on which God moves them.
- Treating the argument as based on the claim that God is not good: it relies on a criterion for a true cause and on God's omnipotence.
