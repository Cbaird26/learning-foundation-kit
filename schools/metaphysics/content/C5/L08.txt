@article
Descartes counts three substances: God, mind and body. Spinoza (1632-1677), in the *Ethics* (published after his death in 1677), argues that there is exactly one, and that it is God, or Nature. The book is written in the **geometrical order**, with definitions, axioms and numbered propositions proved from them, so the first task is to understand the starting points. References such as 1p5 mean Part 1, proposition 5; 1d3 is a definition.

### Definitions

| Term | Spinoza's definition (paraphrased) |
|---|---|
| Substance (1d3) | What is in itself and is conceived through itself |
| Attribute (1d4) | What the intellect perceives of a substance as constituting its essence |
| Mode (1d5) | An affection of a substance: what is in another and is conceived through another |
| God (1d6) | An absolutely infinite being, a substance consisting of infinitely many attributes |

Among the axioms, the following are the ones used here: everything that is, is either in itself or in another (1a1); what cannot be conceived through another must be conceived through itself (1a2); from a given determinate cause the effect follows necessarily (1a3); knowledge of an effect depends on, and involves, knowledge of its cause (1a4); and things that have nothing in common cannot be understood through one another (1a5).

### The argument for one substance

The route from these starting points to monism passes through several propositions:

| P1 | Two substances with different attributes have nothing in common (1p2). |
| P2 | Things that have nothing in common cannot be causes of one another (1p3). |
| P3 | Two distinct substances cannot share an attribute, since distinct things differ in attributes or in affections, and substance is prior to its affections (1p4, 1p5). |
| P4 | So one substance cannot be produced by another: it could not be produced by a substance with a different attribute (P1, P2) or the same one (P3) (1p6). |
| P5 | So a substance must be its own cause, and existence belongs to its nature (1p7). |
| P6 | God, a substance with all attributes, exists (1p11). |
| P7 | Any other substance would have to be explained through some attribute, and God has all attributes, so it would share an attribute with God, contrary to P3. |
| C | So except for God, no substance can exist or be conceived (1p14). |

Spinoza draws consequences. Whatever else exists must be in something (1a1), and since there is no other substance, it is in God: everything is a **mode** of God (1p15). Extension and thought, for instance, are attributes of the one substance, and individual bodies and minds are modes of it. This is the meaning of *Deus sive Natura*, "God, or Nature" (a phrase in the preface to Part 4). Spinoza's God is not a person who makes choices, and whether to call his view pantheism, as many do, or something else is a matter of interpretation.

### How the argument is aimed at Descartes

Descartes holds that mind and body are distinct substances with nothing in common (thought versus extension), yet interact. Premise P2 (1p3) rules out causal interaction between things with nothing in common, so Spinoza's argument strikes at the interaction of Cartesian substances. His premise P3 also removes the possibility of many finite substances of the same attribute, such as many extended bodies each a substance.

### Evaluating the argument

Objections come at several points. Critics have questioned whether the definition of God as having all attributes is justified, and why a substance could not have only some attributes. Spinoza argues in 1p8 that every substance is infinite, since a finite substance would have to be limited by another substance of the same nature, which P3 forbids; and once God exists (1p11), 1p14 leaves no room for any other substance with fewer attributes. Others question P2: that things with nothing in common cannot be causally related, which Descartes's interactionism denies and which relies on axioms 1a4 and 1a5 about explanation. A third objection concerns the claim that all difference is difference of attribute or of mode: why can't there be distinct substances of the same kind that are numerically distinct? Spinoza says that they would have nothing by which to be distinguished (P3). The argument is valid as reconstructed, so the dispute is about premises.

@example
title: Reconstructing the monism argument
problem: Set out Spinoza's argument that there is only one substance, say where it is aimed at Descartes, and identify its crucial premise about causation.
step: State the key steps: different attributes mean nothing in common (1p2); nothing in common means no causation (1p3); no two substances share an attribute (1p5); so no substance is produced by another (1p6); a substance exists by its own nature (1p7); God, with all attributes, exists (1p11); so there can be no other substance (1p14).
step: The attack on Descartes: mind and body, having different attributes, have nothing in common, so by 1p3 neither can cause the other. Spinoza concludes that thought and extension are attributes of the one substance and not of two substances that act on each other.
step: The crucial premise about causation is 1p3, since without it a substance with a different attribute could be produced by another.
answer: The conclusion that there is only one substance follows from the premises, and 1p3 is the premise Cartesians such as Descartes would deny.

@example
title: Spinoza's reasoning for no shared attributes
problem: Show how Spinoza argues that two distinct substances cannot share an attribute (1p5).
step: Suppose there are two or more distinct substances. By 1p4 they are distinguished either by their attributes or by their affections (modes).
step: Suppose they are distinguished by their affections. Substance is prior to its affections (1p1), so considering the substance in itself, apart from its affections, nothing distinguishes it from the other.
step: So they cannot be distinguished by affections, and if they are distinct, it must be by a difference of attributes. So distinct substances differ in attribute, and no two share an attribute.
answer: Distinct substances can differ only in attribute, and so no two substances share an attribute.

@mistakes
- Saying that Spinoza is an atheist who denies God: he identifies God with the one substance, and his God is infinite and necessary, but not a person who chooses.
- Reading "mode" as a part of God: a mode is an affection of the substance, which is in the substance and conceived through it, not a piece cut off from it.
- Thinking that the argument starts from the existence of God: the proofs of 1p5 and 1p6 are about substances in general and precede the proof of God's existence (1p11).
- Attributing the claim that extension and thought are separate substances to Spinoza: for him they are attributes of one substance.
