@article
Descartes has a rule (what I perceive clearly and distinctly is true) and a worry (a deceiver might make even clear perceptions false). To remove the worry he needs to know that God exists and is no deceiver. That is the task of Meditations III to V, and it raises a question that his critics pressed immediately: can he prove God without already relying on the rule?

### Ideas and their causes

In Meditation III Descartes introduces some technical vocabulary. Every idea has **formal reality**, the reality it has as a mode of my mind, and **objective reality**, the reality of what it represents (its representational content). The idea of an infinite substance represents more than the idea of a stone, so it has more objective reality. He also holds a hierarchy of reality: an infinite substance has more reality than a finite one, which has more than a mode. His key principle is the **causal adequacy principle**: there must be at least as much reality in the total efficient cause as in its effect, and this applies to the objective reality of an idea, which needs a cause that contains that much reality either **formally** (actually) or **eminently** (in a higher form).

### The trademark argument

I find in myself an idea of God, a substance that is infinite, eternal, immutable, omnipotent and omniscient. The argument runs:

| P1 | I have an idea of God as an infinite and perfect substance. |
| P2 | The objective reality of this idea is that of an infinite substance. |
| P3 | Every idea has a cause, and that cause must contain, formally or eminently, at least as much reality as the idea's objective reality (causal adequacy). |
| P4 | I am a finite substance, so I do not contain, formally or eminently, the reality of an infinite substance. |
| C1 | So I am not the cause of my idea of God. |
| C | So something that contains infinite reality exists as its cause: God exists. |

The main objections target P2 and P3. Against P2, the objector says that the idea of the infinite has no more than finite content, since I could have formed it by extending or negating my ideas of finite things, in which case I could be its cause. Descartes replies that the idea of the infinite is not a mere negation of the finite: he holds that I perceive the infinite before the finite, since I understand my own doubting and lack as imperfection only by comparing myself with a perfect being, and the idea is clear and distinct. Another worry is that causal adequacy is itself a clear and distinct truth, and so rests on the rule.

### The ontological argument

In Meditation V Descartes offers a second argument. The essence of a triangle includes having three angles that sum to two right angles, and I cannot think of a triangle without this, whether or not any triangle exists. He then argues:

| P1 | I clearly and distinctly perceive that existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being, just as the angle-sum belongs to the nature of a triangle. |
| P2 | Whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive to belong to the nature of a thing does belong to it. |
| C | So existence belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being: God exists. |

His comparison is that to think of God without existence is like thinking of a mountain without a valley. This version differs from Anselm's eleventh-century argument, since it is built on the clear and distinct perception rule. Caterus, in the First Objections, replied that the argument may show only that the concept of God includes existence, not that anything meets the concept. Gassendi, in the Fifth Objections, held that existence is not a perfection or property in the way that omniscience is; Kant later developed the objection with the claim that existence is not a real predicate. Descartes replied that necessary existence belongs to the idea of God in a way it does not for other things.

### From God to the truth rule

Once God is established, Descartes argues in Meditation IV that a supremely perfect being would not deceive, since deception is a defect, and that the faculty of judgment given to me by God is reliable when used properly. Error arises because the will, which is unlimited, extends beyond the intellect's clear perception, and the remedy is to withhold judgment whenever the perception is not clear and distinct.

### The Cartesian circle

Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections, raised the objection that has become known as the **Cartesian circle**:

| P1 | To be certain that clear and distinct perceptions are true, I must first prove that God exists and is no deceiver. |
| P2 | The proof that God exists relies on premises that I accept only because I clearly and distinctly perceive them. |
| C | So the proof of God presupposes the very truth of clear and distinct perceptions that it was supposed to establish: it is circular. |

Descartes's main reply (in the Second and Fourth Replies) rejects P1 as stated. He distinguishes what I am attending to now from what I only remember. While I am actually attending to a clear and distinct perception, I cannot doubt it, and no guarantee is needed. The deceiver doubt arises only when I recall earlier conclusions without attending to their proofs. God's guarantee is therefore needed to make **memory** of conclusions, and so lasting scientific knowledge (*scientia*), secure. Interpreters disagree about whether this reply succeeds, since the doubt in Meditation III seems to arise even about simple matters while they are in view.

title: Reconstructing the trademark argument
problem: Reconstruct the trademark argument in standard form and say which premise a critic who says "my idea of God is just my ideas of finite things extended or negated" would reject.
step: State the premises in order: I have the idea of an infinite perfect substance; the idea has objective reality of an infinite substance; a cause must contain at least as much reality as the objective reality of its effect; I am finite. Conclude that I am not the cause and so God exists.
step: Check validity: from the causal adequacy principle and the content of the idea, the cause has infinite reality. From P4, I am not that cause. So there is some other cause with infinite reality. The argument is valid.
step: The critic denies P2: if the idea is only an extension or negation of ideas of finite things, its objective reality is not that of an infinite substance, and a finite thinker such as me could be its cause.
step: Descartes would reply that the idea of the infinite is not formed by negating or extending the finite, because understanding what is finite or imperfect already presupposes the idea of the infinite.
answer: The argument is valid. The critic rejects P2, and Descartes replies that the idea of the infinite is prior to the idea of the finite.

@example
title: Finding the circle
problem: A student argues: "I trust my sense of what is reliable because my sense of what is reliable says it can be trusted." Explain how this resembles the Cartesian circle, and how Descartes avoids the charge.
step: The student's reasoning uses the faculty whose reliability is in question to establish its reliability: this is the form of a circle.
step: The Cartesian version: the proof of God uses clear and distinct perception, and God is then used to guarantee clear and distinct perception.
step: Descartes's reply distinguishes what I perceive clearly now, which I cannot doubt while attending, from my memory of past conclusions. The proof of God is accepted while attending to its premises, and God's guarantee is needed afterwards, for memory.
step: This reply has a cost: if doubt can arise only about remembered conclusions, then Meditation III's doubt about simple things in view seems to be given up, and readers disagree about the right interpretation.
answer: Both reasonings use a faculty to vouch for itself. Descartes avoids the circle by saying that the guarantee is needed only for the memory of conclusions, not for present perception.

@mistakes
- Saying that the Cartesian circle means that Descartes proves God from God: the objection is that the proof of God relies on the truth rule, which is supposed to be guaranteed by God.
- Confusing objective reality with objectivity: it means the representational content of an idea.
- Confusing the causal adequacy principle with the principle of sufficient reason: Descartes's principle says that a cause must have at least as much reality as its effect, which is narrower than Leibniz's.
- Treating the ontological argument as Anselm's: Descartes's version rests on clear and distinct perception of what belongs to the nature of a supremely perfect being.
