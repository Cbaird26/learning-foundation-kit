@article
Descartes holds that the mind is an unextended thinking thing and the body an extended non-thinking thing. Yet it seems obvious that when I decide to raise my arm, my arm rises, and when someone pricks my finger, I feel pain. How can substances so unlike each other act on each other? The question was put with great force by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia in a correspondence with Descartes that began in 1643.

### Elisabeth's question

Elisabeth wrote to Descartes asking how the soul of a human being, being only a thinking substance, can determine the bodily spirits to produce voluntary actions. She observed that, as she understood it, a body is moved by being pushed by another body, which requires contact, and so depends on the extension and surface of the thing that moves it. But Descartes's soul has no extension, and the soul seems to have no contact with the body. She suggested that the account of the soul in the *Meditations* did not say enough, and asked for a more exact definition of the soul. Her argument can be put in standard form:

| P1 | For one thing to move a body, it must push it by contact, and so must have extension and a surface. |
| P2 | The soul, as Descartes defines it, is a thinking substance without extension. |
| P3 | The soul is supposed to move the body, as in voluntary action, and the body to affect the soul, as in sensation. |
| P4 | If the soul cannot move the body by the means that moving a body requires, then Descartes's account leaves interaction unintelligible unless he explains some other way in which it can. |
| C | So, unless Descartes can explain how an unextended soul moves a body without contact, his account leaves mind-body interaction unintelligible. |

The argument is valid. Its weight falls on P1, which reflects the contact mechanics of Descartes's own physics, and on whether "unintelligible" is the right conclusion, since Elisabeth's own wording makes it a challenge to explain how and not a proof that interaction is impossible.

### Descartes's replies

In his first reply (21 May 1643) Descartes says that we have three kinds of **primitive notion**, each known in its own way: of the soul (known by pure intellect), of the body (known by the intellect, with help from the imagination), and of the **union** of soul and body, which is known only obscurely by the intellect alone (even when aided by the imagination) but very clearly by the senses, in ordinary life and conversation. He added a comparison with heaviness: we are used to conceiving of heaviness as a real quality in bodies that carries them toward the earth without touching them, and Descartes says that this conception, though mistaken as an account of how bodies fall, shows how we can have a notion of something other than body moving a body, and so of the soul moving the body.

Elisabeth was not satisfied. She replied (20 June 1643) that the comparison with heaviness did not help her understand how an unextended thing could move a body, and that she found it easier to concede matter and extension to the soul than to concede that an immaterial thing could move a body and be moved by it. In his answer (28 June 1643) Descartes said that she was free to attribute matter and extension to the soul, since doing so is just a way of conceiving its union with the body, and that once she had formed this notion and felt it in herself she would find it easy to see that the matter so attributed is not thought itself. He gave no mechanism for the interaction.

In *The Passions of the Soul* (1649), a work that grew out of this exchange, Descartes proposed that the soul acts on the body at the pineal gland. This does not answer Elisabeth, since the question is how an unextended thing can move even a small gland.

### Elisabeth's objection and the modern closure objection

Do not confuse Elisabeth's objection with the modern **causal closure** argument, which holds that every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, so that an immaterial mind that moves bodies would break physical laws such as the conservation of energy. Elisabeth's objection is about **intelligibility**: given what motion is, how can an unextended thing cause it? It makes no appeal to conservation laws, which were not available in their modern form in 1643.

### Replies on behalf of the dualist

1. **Primitive notion.** The interaction is a basic fact known through the senses and lived experience, and not every causal link must be explained. The weakness is that it seems to name the problem and not solve it.
2. **Deny P1.** Contact is not required for causation. The concept of causation does not require extension, and later physics, with Newtonian gravitation, describes action at a distance. The weakness is that Descartes's own physics allows only contact, and so he cannot use this reply without revising it.
3. **Give up the interaction or the dualism.** Philosophers after Descartes took the problem as a reason to adopt occasionalism, pre-established harmony or monism, which are the subjects of the coming weeks.

title: Finding the target of a reply
problem: A defender of Descartes says: "Nothing about the concept of causation requires extension or contact; it is a brute fact that decisions cause movements." Which premise of Elisabeth's argument does this reply reject, and what does it leave open?
step: The argument has P1 (moving a body requires contact and extension), P2 (the soul is unextended) and P3 (the soul is supposed to move the body).
step: The reply says that causation does not require contact or extension. This rejects P1.
step: It leaves open the demand for an account of how the soul moves the body, since "brute fact" gives no mechanism, and Elisabeth had asked for exactly such an account.
answer: The reply rejects P1, and leaves open whether a brute connection is intelligible enough.

@example
title: Distinguishing two objections
problem: Which of these two statements is Elisabeth's objection, and which is the modern causal closure objection? (a) "An unextended thing cannot push a body, because pushing requires contact." (b) "Every physical event has a sufficient physical cause, so a mind cannot add anything to the physical world's changes."
step: Statement (a) concerns how motion is produced: pushing requires contact, which an unextended thing cannot have. It is about intelligibility given Descartes's mechanics.
step: Statement (b) is about the completeness of physical causes, and invokes the idea that physical processes are causally closed.
step: Elisabeth's letter is the first kind. She asks how an unextended soul can move a body, and does not appeal to conservation laws.
answer: (a) is Elisabeth's objection; (b) is the modern causal closure objection.

@mistakes
- Saying that Elisabeth argued that interaction violates the conservation of energy: her objection concerns the intelligibility of an unextended thing moving a body.
- Treating Descartes's reply as an explanation of how interaction works: he says that the union is a primitive notion known through ordinary life, not an account of a mechanism.
- Thinking that the pineal gland answers her question: it locates where the soul acts, not how an unextended thing can act on a body.
- Attributing to Elisabeth the conclusion that dualism is refuted: she asks for an account and says it would be easier to grant the soul extension.
