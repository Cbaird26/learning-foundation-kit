@article
Why is there something rather than nothing? Why is the world as it is, and not otherwise? Leibniz thought that these questions have answers, and that a rational enquirer is entitled to ask them about anything. His principle of sufficient reason is one of the most influential and most disputed principles in metaphysics, and it drives his arguments for God and for the best possible world.

### Two great principles

In the *Monadology* (sections 31 and 32) Leibniz says that our reasoning rests on two principles. The **principle of contradiction** says that what implies a contradiction is false. The **principle of sufficient reason** (PSR) says that no fact can be real or existing, and no statement true, unless there is a sufficient reason why it is so and not otherwise, even though in most cases we cannot know those reasons. He distinguishes **truths of reasoning**, which are necessary, and whose opposite implies a contradiction, and **truths of fact**, which are contingent and whose opposite is possible (§33). The reason for a truth of reasoning is found by analysis into identities. For a truth of fact it must be found elsewhere.

### The argument from contingency

Leibniz argues from the PSR to a necessary being (§36-39):

| P1 | Every truth of fact has a sufficient reason (the PSR). |
| P2 | Each contingent fact is explained by an earlier contingent fact, and so on in a series. |
| P3 | No contingent fact, and no series of contingent facts however long, contains within itself the sufficient reason for why that series exists rather than another. |
| P4 | So the sufficient reason for the world, the whole series of contingent things, lies outside that series. |
| P5 | What contains the sufficient reason of the series must itself have its reason in itself, that is, must be a necessary being. |
| C | So there is a necessary being outside the series of contingent things: God. |

The argument is valid, and P3 is where most of the weight lies. A familiar objection is that an explanation of each member of a series does explain the series, and to ask for more is to treat the whole as if it were one more member (an objection stated in Hume's *Dialogues concerning Natural Religion*, Part 9). Another is that the PSR itself may be false, and that the universe may be a brute fact. Philosophers disagree, and a proponent of the PSR and a defender of brute facts both need to say why the question "why is there a world?" is or is not legitimate.

### The best possible world

If there are many possible worlds, and God chose one, the PSR requires a reason for the choice (§53-55). The argument runs:

| P1 | There are infinitely many possible worlds, each a complete way things could be. |
| P2 | God, a perfectly wise, powerful and good being, actualized exactly one of them. |
| P3 | By the PSR, God had a sufficient reason for choosing the world He chose. |
| P4 | The only sufficient reason that a perfectly wise and good being could have for choosing one world over the others is that it is the best. |
| C | So the actual world is the best of all possible worlds. |

By "best", Leibniz means the world with the greatest variety of phenomena governed by the simplest laws (§58), and not the world with the most pleasure or no suffering. His *Theodicy* (1710) uses the argument to respond to the problem of evil: any evil in the world is part of the best whole. Voltaire's satire *Candide* (1759) mocks the slogan, but a satire is not itself an argument.

### Objections

1. **No best world.** For any world, perhaps there is a better one, as there is no greatest whole number. If so, P4 fails, and God could be good without creating the best. Robert Merrihew Adams has argued that a perfectly good God need not create the best world.
2. **The problem of evil.** The amount and kind of suffering in the world seem to be inconsistent with its being the best. Leibniz's reply is that we see only a small part of the whole.
3. **Modal collapse.** If the PSR is true, every truth has a sufficient reason, and God must choose the best, then the actual world could not have been otherwise, so everything is necessary. This is Spinoza's conclusion, reached by a path that uses the same principle (Ethics 1p11, 1p29, 1p33). Leibniz wished to avoid it, and he replied that the alternatives are genuinely possible, since their description involves no contradiction, and that God's choice is **morally necessary**, determined by God's goodness, but not **metaphysically necessary**. He added that contingent truths require an infinite analysis, so that no finite proof reduces them to identities. Critics ask whether the distinction between moral and metaphysical necessity does the required work, since God cannot choose otherwise.

step: State the premises: there are many possible worlds; God actualized one; the PSR requires a reason for God's choice; the only reason a wise and good being could have is that the chosen world is best; so the actual world is the best.

@example
title: Truths of reasoning and truths of fact
problem: Classify each as a truth of reasoning or a truth of fact in Leibniz's sense: (a) every square has four sides; (b) there is a lighthouse on the island of Merrow (an invented place); (c) the number 11 has no divisors other than 1 and itself.
step: Leibniz's test is whether the opposite implies a contradiction. If it does, the truth is one of reasoning; if the opposite is possible, the truth is one of fact.
step: (a) A square with a number of sides other than four is a contradiction in terms: a truth of reasoning.
step: (b) There could have been no lighthouse on Merrow: its opposite is possible. A truth of fact, so contingent.
step: (c) The number 11 having another divisor would be an arithmetical contradiction: a truth of reasoning.
answer: (a) reasoning; (b) fact; (c) reasoning.

@mistakes
- Treating the PSR as the claim that every event has a cause: Leibniz's principle covers every truth, including necessary ones and facts about why things are as they are and not otherwise.
- Saying that Leibniz holds this to be the best world because it has the least suffering: he means the greatest variety governed by the simplest laws.
- Calling Leibniz a necessitarian: he accepts the PSR but denies that everything is necessary, and rests the denial on the distinction between moral and metaphysical necessity and on the infinite analysis of contingent truths.
- Confusing the principle of contradiction with the PSR: the first governs truths of reasoning, and the second governs truths of fact as well.
