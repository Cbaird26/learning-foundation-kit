@article
Why does a dropped stone fall? A philosopher trained in the Aristotelian tradition of the universities would answer that the stone is heavy, that heavy things have the earth's center as their **natural place**, and that falling is the stone realizing its nature. A new philosopher of the seventeenth century would reject that answer as no explanation at all: it names a tendency and then explains the motion by the tendency. What is needed, she would say, is the size, shape and motion of the bodies involved, and the laws they obey. The change from the first kind of answer to the second is the background to everything you will read in this course.

### The old picture

**Scholastic Aristotelianism** was the framework taught in European universities. Its main commitments were these:

- Every natural thing is a compound of matter and a **substantial form**, which makes it the kind of thing it is and explains its characteristic behavior.
- Explanation cites four causes (material, formal, efficient and **final**), and the final cause, the end a thing is directed to, is often the most important.
- Qualities such as heat, color and sweetness are real features of bodies, resembling the sensations they cause.
- The earth is at the center of the universe, and the heavens are made of a different kind of stuff from earthly things.

### The new science

Several developments undermined this framework. Copernicus's *On the Revolutions* (1543) proposed that the earth moves around the sun. Kepler showed that planetary orbits are ellipses (1609). Galileo's telescope revealed moons around Jupiter and phases of Venus (reported in 1610), and his *Two New Sciences* (1638) described falling bodies in mathematical terms. Francis Bacon's *New Organon* (1620) argued that natural philosophy should be built on organized experiment rather than on the authority of the ancients. Newton's *Principia* (1687) united terrestrial and celestial motion under a mathematical law of universal gravitation.

### The mechanical philosophy

Out of this came the **mechanical philosophy**, held in different forms by Descartes, Gassendi, Hobbes and Boyle among others: all natural phenomena are to be explained by the size, shape, motion and arrangement of the parts of matter. For Descartes, matter is simply extension, so bodies differ only in their shapes and motions. Galileo, in *The Assayer* (1623), had already suggested that tastes, odors and colors reside in the perceiver rather than in the objects. Later, Locke would label the qualities that bodies have in themselves **primary qualities** and the sensation-dependent ones **secondary qualities**; the labels became standard through Locke's *Essay*, but the distinction was already at work.

The mechanist case for the distinction can be put as an argument:

| P1 | Everything that is needed to explain how bodies behave can be stated in terms of size, shape, motion and arrangement of parts. |
| P2 | Colors, tastes, smells and warmth, as we experience them, play no role in that explanation. |
| P3 | We should not attribute to bodies themselves qualities that do no explanatory work. |
| P4 | Our experiences of colors, tastes, smells and warmth are real, and what they are experiences of, if not qualities of bodies, is effects of bodies on perceivers. |
| C | So colors, tastes, smells and warmth, as experienced, are not qualities of bodies themselves but effects of bodies on perceivers. |

The argument is valid. Its weight falls on premise P3: an objector replies that explanatory idleness in physics does not show absence, and that color might be a physical property of surfaces (a way of reflecting light) that physics describes in other terms. The mechanist can reply that this changes the subject, since the redness we see is not a pattern of reflectance.

### New problems

The new philosophy did not only clear away the old. It left three problems that drive the next eleven weeks.

1. **Foundations.** If the authority of Aristotle is gone and the senses seem to mislead (the sun looks small), what certifies the new science? Descartes's answer is reason, starting from indubitable foundations.
2. **Mind.** If body is only matter in motion, thoughts and sensations do not seem to fit among bodies. Descartes concluded that minds are a different kind of substance, which raises the question of how the two kinds are related.
3. **God and necessity.** The laws of motion must have a source, and for Descartes it is God. Rationalists asked how far God's will could be free if the laws are mathematical, a question Spinoza and Leibniz answered very differently.

### A caution about labels

Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz are grouped as **rationalists** because each held that reason alone can establish fundamental truths about reality, and Locke, Berkeley and Hume as **empiricists**. The labels were applied by later historians. Descartes did experiments, and Locke used reason. In the 2020 PhilPapers Survey, 44% of respondents accepted or leaned toward empiricism and 34% toward rationalism about knowledge, but that share is not evidence for either view. Use the labels as a map, not as a verdict.

@example
title: Two explanations of a falling stone
problem: Give (a) the scholastic Aristotelian explanation and (b) a mechanical explanation of why a stone dropped from a tower falls, and say what the mechanist leaves out.
step: Scholastic: the stone consists of earthy matter, and its form makes it heavy. Heavy things move toward the center of the universe, their natural place, and falling is the actualization of that tendency. The explanation cites the end the motion is directed to.
step: Mechanical, Descartes's version: bodies are extension in motion, and there are no inner tendencies. The stone is pushed toward the earth by the motion of the fine matter that swirls around it, and what happens is fixed by the laws of impact.
step: What is left out: the final cause (the natural place) and the substantial form. Newton later explained falling by a gravitational force acting across distance, which some mechanists, including Leibniz, resisted because it seemed to lack a mechanism.
answer: The scholastic explains the fall by the stone's nature and end; the mechanist explains it by matter in motion and laws, and drops forms and final causes from physics.

@example
title: Assessing the mechanist argument
problem: A student says: "The mechanist argument about colors is invalid, because physics might leave out something real." Is the student right? Which premise should an objector attack?
step: Check the form. The conclusion follows from P1 to P4 (if everything needed for explanation is mechanical, experienced qualities do no explanatory work, and idle qualities should not be attributed to bodies, and the experiences are effects on perceivers if not qualities of bodies, then they are not qualities of bodies but effects). So the argument is valid.
step: The student's worry is about premise P3, not validity: that something might be real without doing explanatory work in physics.
step: An objector should attack P3, or P2 (by arguing that color does explain, say, why ripe fruit is easy to see). The best reply to P3 distinguishes the physical property that causes the experience (which bodies have) from the experienced quality (which the mechanist denies bodies have).
answer: The argument is valid; the student has located a doubt about premise P3, which is where the debate lies.

@mistakes
- Treating the mechanical philosophy as the claim that everything is made of tiny hard atoms: Descartes denied atoms and a vacuum, and what the mechanists share is explanation by size, shape and motion.
- Reading "rationalist" as "someone who does not use experience": the rationalists held that reason, not the senses, is the source of fundamental metaphysical knowledge, and several of them did experiments.
- Saying that the mechanists denied that colors exist: they denied that bodies have colors in the way we experience them and located the experience in the perceiver.
- Attributing the primary/secondary terminology to Galileo or Descartes: the terms became standard through Locke, though the distinction was drawn earlier.
