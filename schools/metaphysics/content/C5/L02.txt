@article
Suppose a builder finds one cracked brick in a wall. She could replace that brick, or, if she suspects the foundations, she could pull the wall down and start again. In the First Meditation (published 1641) Descartes takes the second course. He has found that he accepted many falsehoods in youth, and since he does not know which of his beliefs rest on them, he resolves once in his life to demolish all his opinions and to build again on firm foundations.

### The method

In the *Discourse on the Method* (1637) Descartes sets out four rules: accept nothing as true that is not evidently so; divide each difficulty into parts; proceed in order from the simplest matters to the more complex; and make enumerations complete enough to be sure nothing is omitted. The Meditations applies the first rule with extreme severity. Since he cannot examine each belief separately, Descartes attacks the **principles** on which they rest: if the sources of belief are unreliable, whatever depends on them goes too.

Two points about this **method of doubt** matter. First, the doubt is a tool, not a conclusion: Descartes is not asserting that nothing is known, but looking for something that cannot be doubted. Second, he treats a belief as *doubtful* if there is any reason, however slight, to question it, and for the sake of the inquiry treats the doubtful as if it were false. The standard is **certainty** (indubitability), which is much stricter than being reasonable or probable. That is why a possibility such as being deceived counts as a reason for doubt even if it is unlikely.

### Three stages of doubt

**Stage 1: sensory error.** The senses sometimes deceive: a distant tower looks round when it is square (Descartes uses this example in Meditation VI; in Meditation I he speaks of things very small or far away). It is prudent never to trust completely those who have deceived us even once. But this seems to cast doubt only on things that are small or far away. That I am sitting here by the fire, holding this paper, seems beyond doubt.

**Stage 2: dreaming.** Descartes recalls that in dreams he has been convinced he was sitting by the fire when he was in bed. There seem to be no certain marks that distinguish waking from dreaming. The argument can be put in standard form:

| P1 | If I cannot rule out that I am dreaming, then my present sensory experience does not guarantee that things are as they appear to me. |
| P2 | I cannot rule out that I am dreaming, because no certain mark distinguishes waking from dreaming. |
| C | So my present sensory experience does not guarantee that things are as they appear to me. |

The argument is valid (modus ponens) and concerns certainty, not probability: it does not say that you are probably dreaming. It does not yet touch everything. Descartes observes that the images of dreams, like a painter's inventions, are composed of simpler elements (color, shape, size, number). So sciences that study composite things, such as physics and astronomy, are doubtful, but **arithmetic and geometry**, which deal with the simplest and most general things, seem to be certain whether I am awake or asleep: two and three make five either way.

**Stage 3: the deceiver.** Descartes recalls the old opinion that an all-powerful God made him. Might that being have arranged that he is wrong even when he counts the sides of a square or adds two and three? This stage is sometimes called the **hyperbolic** or metaphysical doubt. He notes that a supremely good God would not allow him to be deceived, but he is sometimes deceived, so he turns the supposition around and imagines a supremely powerful and cunning being (the Latin *genium malignum*, often called the evil demon) who devotes all its effort to deceiving him. Descartes does not claim the deceiver exists. He adopts the hypothesis deliberately, because his old beliefs keep returning by habit, and he needs a counterweight. The result is that all his former beliefs, mathematics included, are suspended.

| Stage | Source of doubt | What it makes doubtful | What still seems safe |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Sensory error | Judgments about distant and small things | Ordinary judgments about nearby things |
| 2 | Dreaming | Judgments about particular bodily things, and physics | Arithmetic and geometry |
| 3 | The deceiver | Everything, including arithmetic and geometry | Nothing yet |

### Reading the First Meditation

Distinguish **exegesis** from **evaluation**. The exegetical question is what Descartes argues; the evaluative one is whether the arguments work. A common evaluation is that doubt requires a reason, and a bare possibility of error is not one; Descartes replies that his aim is certainty, so the bare possibility suffices. Another asks whether the doubt is coherent without relying on something in the very reasoning that the deceiver would corrupt. The question is open to interpretation, and it returns in week 4.

title: Placing beliefs at their stage of doubt
problem: For each belief, say which stage of the First Meditation first makes it doubtful: (a) a square tower, seen from far away, is round; (b) I am now holding a pen; (c) the three angles of a triangle sum to two right angles; (d) the stars move as astronomy says.
step: (a) The belief about the tower rests on a sensory report that is known to mislead at a distance. Stage 1.
step: (b) The belief that I am holding a pen is a judgment about a present bodily thing that seems certain, but a dreamer has equally vivid experiences. Stage 2.
step: (c) Geometry survives the dream argument, since it concerns simple and general things. It is doubtful only if a powerful deceiver could make me err even there. Stage 3.
step: (d) Astronomy studies composite things, which the dream argument already makes doubtful. Stage 2.
answer: (a) Stage 1; (b) Stage 2; (c) Stage 3; (d) Stage 2.

@example
title: Testing the dream argument
problem: A student says: "The dream argument proves that I am dreaming right now." Is that what the argument shows? State what the argument does show and what it needs in order to show it.
step: Identify the conclusion: that my present experience does not *guarantee* that things are as they appear. It is a claim about what the experience guarantees, not about what is the case.
step: The argument's second premise says only that I cannot rule out dreaming. From "I cannot rule out dreaming" it does not follow that I am dreaming.
step: To show that you are dreaming, it would need a premise that the present experience has a mark of dreaming, and Descartes denies that there are any certain marks either way.
answer: The argument shows only that sensory experience cannot guarantee how things are. It is about certainty and does not say that you are or probably are dreaming.

@mistakes
- Treating the method of doubt as skepticism: Descartes is looking for a certainty that survives doubt, and the doubt is the means.
- Reading the deceiver hypothesis as Descartes's belief that a demon exists: he adopts it as a deliberate device for suspending judgment.
- Saying that the dream argument shows that you are dreaming, or probably dreaming: it shows only that you cannot be certain you are not.
- Thinking that the dream argument already doubts arithmetic: on Descartes's account mathematics survives that stage and falls only to the deceiver.
