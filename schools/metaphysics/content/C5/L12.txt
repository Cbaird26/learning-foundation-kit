@article
Imagine that tonight, while everyone sleeps, God moves the whole universe three kilometers to the east, keeping all distances and motions exactly as they were. Would anything be different? Leibniz said no, and that the question shows there is no such thing as absolute space. The argument is a main application of the principle of sufficient reason, and it was the center of a famous correspondence between Leibniz and Samuel Clarke, who defended Newton's views.

### Two principles about identity

Keep two principles apart. **Leibniz's Law**, the **indiscernibility of identicals**, says that if x and y are identical, then they share all their properties. It is accepted by almost everyone. The **identity of indiscernibles** (PII) is its converse: if x and y share all their properties, then x and y are identical, so that no two distinct things are exactly alike. Leibniz held it for substances (for instance in *Monadology* §9: each monad must differ from every other), and it is controversial. Its force depends on what counts as a property: if identity with oneself, or being located here, count, the principle is trivial or begs the question, and the interesting version restricts it to **qualitative** properties, with or without relations to other things.

### The argument against absolute space

In his Third Paper (§5) and Fourth Paper, Leibniz argues:

| P1 | If space were an absolute thing existing independently of bodies, then the universe could have been placed elsewhere in it, or oriented differently (for instance, shifted three kilometers east), with all relations among bodies unchanged. |
| P2 | The universe so shifted would be indiscernible from the actual universe: there would be no difference in anything observable or in any relation among bodies. |
| P3 | By the principle of sufficient reason, God would have no sufficient reason to place the universe in one position rather than the other. |
| P4 | God has a sufficient reason for everything He does, so He would not have placed the universe in either position; but the universe exists. |
| C | So there is no absolute space. (Leibniz's positive view is that space is the **order of coexisting things**, and time the order of successive things.) |

On the version that uses PII, the two states would be indiscernible, so by PII they are one and the same state, and the idea of a "shifted" universe describes no further possibility. Premise P1 and the idea that absolute positions are real are what the argument is directed against.

### Clarke's replies

Clarke and the Newtonians had several replies. First, he held that the **mere will of God** can be a sufficient reason for choosing among indiscernible alternatives, so that P3 and P4 fail; Leibniz thought that this makes God's choice arbitrary, against the PSR. Second, Clarke appealed to the Newtonian view that space is real and that absolute motion can be detected by its effects. Newton's **bucket argument** (in the *Principia*) points out that water in a spinning bucket climbs the sides, although the effect does not track the relation of the water to the bucket (the surface is flat while the bucket spins and the water is still, and concave once the water has caught up and moves with the bucket), and that Newton took to show that rotation relative to absolute space has real effects. Relationalists must explain that effect, and this remained a serious problem. In current physics the question is reformulated within general relativity and is not settled: whether spacetime is a fundamental entity or emergent is open.

### Black's two spheres

A modern objection to PII comes from Max Black (1952). Imagine a universe containing only two perfectly similar iron spheres of the same size, a fixed distance apart. Each sphere has the same intrinsic properties as the other and the same relations to everything else, so the spheres seem indiscernible, yet there are two. Replies: the defender of PII may deny that the case is possible; or note that the spheres differ in relational properties (each is some distance from the other but not from itself), which some call weak discernibility, though the defender of strong PII may object that this relation does not discern them in the way that is needed; or the critic may claim that things differ in a non-qualitative way (a "haecceity"), and PII, restricted to qualitative properties, is false. The dispute shows what the principle commits one to.

title: Reconstructing the shift argument
problem: Put Leibniz's argument against absolute space in standard form, and identify what Clarke denies.
step: Premises: if space is absolute, the universe could have been placed elsewhere; the shifted universe would be indiscernible; the PSR requires a reason for placing it here and not there; there would be none; so there is no absolute space.
step: The argument is valid.
step: Clarke denies the third premise, saying that the will of God is a sufficient reason for a choice among alternatives.
step: Leibniz responds that a will without a motive is not a sufficient reason, and so Clarke's answer is not compatible with the PSR as he understands it.
answer: The argument is valid, and Clarke denies that the PSR requires a reason beyond God's will.

@example
title: Two cases for the identity of indiscernibles
problem: Two otherwise identical copper coins lie on opposite sides of an otherwise empty, uniform universe. Is each of these properties one that discerns them? (a) Being made of copper. (b) Being at a distance of ten meters from the other coin. (c) Being identical with this coin.
step: (a) Both coins are made of copper, so this does not discern them.
step: (b) Each coin is ten meters from the other, so both coins have this property, and it does not discern them.
step: (c) Only one coin is identical with this coin, so this does discern them, but only trivially: it presupposes the distinction between the coins.
answer: (a) no; (b) no; (c) only trivially, so a defender of PII excludes it.

@mistakes
- Confusing PII with Leibniz's Law: PII says that indiscernible things are identical, and Leibniz's Law says that identical things are indiscernible.
- Saying that Leibniz's argument against absolute space is only about what we can observe: it uses the PSR, saying that God would have no reason to choose.
- Attributing the claim that God's will is a sufficient reason to Leibniz: that is Clarke's reply, and Leibniz rejects it.
- Supposing that Black's spheres refute Leibniz's Law: they challenge PII, the converse principle.

@quiz self
prompt: In four or five sentences, explain how Newton's bucket argument challenges Leibniz's relationalism, and give one way a relationalist might reply.
hint: Ask what explains the climbing of the water if only relations among bodies are real.
solution: In the bucket argument the water climbs the sides of the spinning bucket, and this effect is not explained by the relation of the water to the bucket, since at the start, when the bucket spins and the water is still, there is relative motion between them and the surface is flat, while later, when the water has caught up and there is no relative motion, the surface is most concave. Newton took this to show that rotation relative to absolute space has real effects. A relationalist must say what explains the effect without absolute space. One reply is that rotation is relative to the other bodies in the universe, such as the fixed stars, so that the effect depends on relations to all matter. A critic can ask whether such a theory gives the right account of the forces.
