@article
Could you exist without your body? The thought seems coherent: people have imagined surviving death, or being a mind in a vat. Descartes thought that the answer is yes, and that the argument for it is among the most certain in philosophy. If he is right, the mind is not a part of nature as the mechanical philosophy describes it; it is a different kind of thing.

### Substance and attribute

In the *Principles of Philosophy* (Part 1) Descartes defines a **substance** as a thing that exists in such a way that it needs no other thing in order to exist. Strictly this applies only to God, but created things count as substances in a derived sense because they need only God's concurrence. Each substance has one **principal attribute** that constitutes its nature: **thought** for mind and **extension** (length, breadth and depth) for body. Everything else about a body, such as shape, motion and position, is a **mode** of extension, and everything else about a mind, such as willing or imagining, is a mode of thought. Two substances are **really distinct** when each can exist without the other, and Descartes holds that we can recognize this when we can clearly and distinctly understand one without the other (Principles 1.60).

### The argument of Meditation VI

Having proved the existence of God and the reliability of clear and distinct perception, Descartes can argue:

| P1 | I clearly and distinctly understand myself as a thinking, non-extended thing. |
| P2 | I clearly and distinctly understand body as an extended, non-thinking thing. |
| P3 | Whatever I clearly and distinctly understand as separate from another thing can be made by God to exist separately from it. |
| P4 | Things that can exist apart are really distinct. |
| C | So I (my mind) am really distinct from my body and can exist without it. |

The argument is valid, and it turns on P3, a step from what is clearly understood to what is possible. Notice that the possibility involved is metaphysical (what could exist apart), while the premises concern what I can understand, and a critic may ask whether understanding one thing without another reveals what is possible or only what I happen to know. Modern conceivability arguments in the philosophy of mind, such as the zombie argument, share this structure and face the same question.

Descartes gives a second consideration, the **divisibility argument**: a body, however small, can always be divided in thought into parts, but the mind is entirely one and has no parts, since it is the same mind that wills, understands and senses. So the mind is not a body.

### Arnauld's objection

Antoine Arnauld, in the Fourth Objections, argued that the reasoning seems to prove too much. Someone may clearly and distinctly know that a triangle inscribed in a semicircle is right-angled, while not knowing that the square on its longest side equals the sum of the squares on the other two. On Descartes's pattern, the person could conclude that the triangle could exist without that property, which is false. The problem is that understanding something incompletely may leave out features that are in fact essential to it. So in P1, when I understand myself without body, I may be understanding the mind only incompletely, and the conclusion that it can exist apart does not follow.

Descartes replied that the argument requires a **complete** understanding of each thing, one that grasps it as a whole thing that can be understood by itself, and that his understanding of mind as a thinking thing is complete: it does not include extension or any bodily attribute, and it is enough to conceive of the mind as an entire substance. Whether this reply succeeds is disputed, and it depends on how completeness is to be tested.

### The union of mind and body

The real distinction does not mean that the mind is merely in the body like a pilot in a ship. Descartes argues in Meditation VI that sensations such as hunger and pain show that he is very closely joined with his body and, as it were, intermingled with it, so that mind and body make up a single thing. In Meditation VI he calls sensations confused modes of thinking that arise from this union and intermingling of mind with body (compare *Principles* 1.48, which assigns sensations, appetites and emotions to the union). This sets the problem of week 6: if mind and body are distinct substances with nothing in common, how can they interact?

@example
title: Reconstructing the real distinction
problem: Put into standard form Descartes's argument that he is distinct from his body, find the step that is a claim about possibility, and say what Arnauld questions.
step: Write the premises: I understand myself as a thinking non-extended thing; I understand body as an extended non-thinking thing; what is clearly and distinctly understood apart can be made by God to exist apart; things that can exist apart are really distinct; so I am really distinct from body.
step: The modal step is the premise that what is understood as separate can exist separately, a claim about what God could bring about.
step: Arnauld questions the first premise: whether my understanding of the mind without body is complete, or only incomplete, as the understanding of the triangle without the angle-sum property is.
answer: The modal premise is P3, and Arnauld questions whether the understanding in P1 is complete enough to support it.

@example
title: Applying Arnauld's pattern to a new case
problem: Apply Arnauld's strategy to this argument: "I can clearly and distinctly understand a bell without any particular metal, so a bell could exist without being metal." Is the argument sound, and what does it show about the pattern?
step: Check the pattern: I understand X without Y; so X can exist without Y. Here X is a bell and Y is any particular metal.
step: The conclusion seems true: a bell can be made of glass or ceramic. So here the pattern gives a true conclusion.
step: But the pattern would also yield false conclusions, as in Arnauld's triangle case, where an incomplete understanding left out an essential property.
step: So the pattern is acceptable only if the understanding of X is complete, which is what Descartes says is true of his understanding of the mind.
answer: The conclusion is true here, but the pattern is reliable only with complete understanding, so the issue shifts to whether the understanding is complete.

@mistakes
- Saying that Descartes holds mind and body have no relation at all: he holds they are really distinct but closely united in a human being.
- Treating the real distinction argument as a proof from the cogito alone: it relies on the proof of God and on the principle that what is clearly and distinctly understood can be so made by God.
- Confusing epistemic and metaphysical possibility: the premises concern what can be clearly understood, and the conclusion concerns what can exist apart.
- Reading Arnauld as denying that the mind is distinct from body: he questioned whether Descartes's argument establishes it.
