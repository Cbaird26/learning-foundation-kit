@article
Descartes says that body is extension; Spinoza says that extension is an attribute of the one substance. Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646-1716) rejects both: extended things cannot be substances, because whatever is extended can be divided, and what is divisible is a collection of parts, not a true unity. The ultimate constituents of reality must have no parts. He called them **monads** (from the Greek for "unit"), and described them in the *Monadology* (written in 1714).

### From composites to simples

The argument opens the Monadology:

| P1 | There are composite things (§1-2). |
| P2 | A composite is an aggregate of its parts and has no unity of its own: it is real only if its parts are real. |
| P3 | If there were no simple things, the parts of every composite would themselves be composites without end, and nothing would ever have unity of its own to make a composite real. |
| C | So there are simple substances, without parts: monads. |

The argument is valid given its premises. A critic can resist P3 by saying that matter may be infinitely divisible, with no ultimate parts, so that nothing is simple; Leibniz replies that an infinite regress of aggregates never reaches anything that has real unity and so would leave no real things. A monad, being without parts, has no extension, shape or divisibility (§3), so it cannot be made, changed or destroyed by rearranging parts.

### Windowless and self-contained

Since a monad has no parts, nothing can enter or leave it, so **monads have no windows** (§7). Whatever changes in a monad comes from an **internal principle** (§11). The changes are of two kinds: **perceptions**, which are the representation of many things in the unity of a simple thing (§14), and **appetitions**, the tendencies by which the monad passes from one perception to another (§15). Each monad **mirrors the whole universe** from its own point of view (§56), and no two monads are exactly alike (§9). Monads differ in the clarity of their perceptions: bare monads have confused perceptions, animal souls have memory and feeling, and rational minds have self-awareness and reason.

Leibniz gives a thought experiment against materialism (§17). Imagine a machine that is built to think, feel and perceive, enlarged so that you can walk inside it as in a mill. You would find parts pushing on one another and never anything that explains a perception. So perception must be sought in a simple substance, not in a composite. The argument is a famous early statement of the problem of explaining consciousness in physical terms, but critics note that it shows only that the parts, as inspected, do not display perception, and does not show that the whole could not perceive.

### Pre-established harmony

If monads have no windows, how do soul and body appear to interact? Leibniz argues that no substance acts on another. Instead God created each substance so that its states unfold from its own nature in exact correspondence with the states of all others: the **pre-established harmony** (§78-81). The soul follows its own laws, the body follows its own, and they agree because of God's design at creation. He used the image of two clocks that keep exactly the same time: they might agree because each influences the other (interaction), because a skilled attendant adjusts them constantly (occasionalism) or because each was made so well that they agree without any further intervention (harmony).

| View | What explains the agreement of soul and body |
|---|---|
| Interaction (Descartes) | Each acts on the other |
| Occasionalism (Malebranche) | God acts on each occasion |
| Harmony (Leibniz) | God's design at creation, with each substance acting from its own nature |

Leibniz thought the third option more worthy of God, who need not intervene constantly, and more faithful to the active nature of substance.

### Spinoza and Leibniz compared

Both deny that mind and body interact causally, and both hold that the order of mental and bodily events correspond. They differ about almost everything else. Spinoza has one substance with two attributes, so mind and body are one thing under two descriptions; Leibniz has an infinity of substances, each a mirror of the universe, and bodies are well-founded appearances of aggregates of monads. For Spinoza the correspondence follows from the nature of the one substance, with necessity; for Leibniz it is a result of God's choice of the best possible world, a choice that is not logically forced (week 11).

### Evaluation

Objections: windowlessness seems to make the mirroring of other monads unexplained; the harmony seems to be a very large coincidence if it is not caused; a world of monads seems to leave no real place for space and bodies (Leibniz called them phenomena, so many read him as committed to a form of idealism about extension, though interpreters disagree about how far); and a charge of making each monad a world apart seems to isolate minds. Leibniz's reply is that the harmony is not a coincidence, since it is explained by God's design.

title: Reconstructing the argument for simple substances
problem: Put in standard form the argument that there must be simple substances, and say what a critic who believes that matter is infinitely divisible would reject.
step: P1: there are composites. P2: a composite is an aggregate of its parts, with no unity of its own, so it is real only if its parts are. P3: if there were no simples, every composite would have only composites as parts without end, so nothing would have unity of its own to make a composite real. C: so there are simple substances.
step: The argument is valid: given P1 to P3 the conclusion follows.
step: The critic says that matter is infinitely divisible, so every part has parts and nothing is simple. This rejects P3 (and with it the demand for a real unity at the bottom), since the critic holds that composites can be composed all the way down.
step: Leibniz replies that an unending series of aggregates never reaches something that is truly one, so aggregates would be mere appearances unless there were simples.
answer: The argument is valid. The critic rejects P3 (and challenges P2), and Leibniz replies with the point about real unity.

@example
title: Three explanations of a correlation
problem: Ana's decision to stand is followed by her standing. Explain this under (a) interaction, (b) occasionalism and (c) pre-established harmony.
step: (a) The soul's volition causes a movement of the body. The agreement between decision and movement is due to a real causal link.
step: (b) The decision is only an occasion. On this occasion God moves the body according to a general law. The agreement is due to God acting at the time.
step: (c) The soul's states unfold from its own nature, and the body's states unfold from its own, and God has so arranged them from the start that they agree. No substance causes anything in another.
answer: (a) real causal influence; (b) God acts on each occasion; (c) God set up the agreement in advance, with each substance following its own laws.

@mistakes
- Treating a monad as an atom: atoms are extended and have parts in principle, while a monad is unextended and has no parts.
- Saying that monads interact through perception: monads are windowless and perceptions unfold from within, so perception is not a causal influence from outside.
- Confusing pre-established harmony with occasionalism: in harmony no substance causes any change in another, and God does not intervene on each occasion.
- Saying that Leibniz denies that bodies exist: he treats them as well-founded phenomena grounded in monads, and interpreters differ on how far this makes him an idealist.
