@article
At the end of the First Meditation everything is doubtful. Suppose a deceiver of supreme power has arranged for you to be wrong about everything you believe. Can the deceiver deceive you if you do not exist? Descartes's answer in Meditation II is that whoever is deceived must exist, and so one belief survives the severest doubt.

### The cogito

Descartes concludes that the proposition "I am, I exist" is necessarily true whenever it is put forward by me or conceived in my mind. Whatever the deceiver does, it cannot bring it about that I am nothing while I am thinking that I am something. In the *Discourse* (Part 4) the formula appears in French, and in the *Principles of Philosophy* (Part 1, section 7) in Latin as *cogito, ergo sum*, "I think, therefore I am". The argument can be set out in standard form:

| P1 | I am thinking (doubting, being deceived and the like are ways of thinking). |
| P2 | Whatever thinks exists. |
| C | So I exist. |

The argument is valid, and P1 is the premise that survives the deceiver: the deceiver can make my thoughts false, but cannot make it false that I have them. Premise P2 is the one that is not obviously immune from doubt, and here Descartes's own account matters. In the Second Replies he says that a person who says "I am thinking, therefore I am" does not deduce existence from thought by a syllogism with the general premise P2. He sees it as a simple act of **intuition**, an immediate grasp. Other readers have taken the cogito to be a kind of **performance**: the thought "I do not exist" cannot be truly thought, so its denial undermines itself (an interpretation developed by Jaakko Hintikka in 1962). The choice between inference, intuition and performance is a question of exegesis on which interpreters differ.

### What am I?

Descartes then asks what this "I" is. He sets aside the old answers (a rational animal, a body with a soul) as unsupported at this stage, and concludes that he is a **thing that thinks**: a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, wills, refuses, imagines and senses. Moreover he is certain that he exists only as long as he thinks, since if thinking stopped, he could not be sure that he would exist. The **wax** passage that follows argues that even our knowledge of bodies is a grasp by the mind and not by the senses or imagination: a piece of wax changes in every sensible quality when melted, yet is judged to be the same wax, and this judgment is by the intellect. A consequence he draws is that the mind is better known than the body.

Two limits should be noted. First, the cogito does not prove that the self is non-bodily: that is argued later (week 5). Hobbes, in the Third Objections, replied that from the fact that there is thinking it does not follow that the thinker is not something corporeal, since acts need subjects but a subject might be a body. Descartes replied that thought and body are conceived as quite different kinds of thing. Second, a classic objection holds that all that the cogito supports is that there is thinking, not that there is an "I" that thinks. Descartes treats the existence of a subject as evident from thinking itself.

### Clear and distinct perception

In Meditation III Descartes reflects on why he is certain of the cogito. He finds that it is only that he perceives it very clearly and distinctly, and he proposes a general rule: whatever I perceive clearly and distinctly is true. In the *Principles* (1.45) he defines the terms. A perception is **clear** when it is present and accessible to an attentive mind, as objects are clear to the eye when they are present and act strongly. It is **distinct** when it is so sharply separated from every other perception that it contains within itself only what is clear. A perception can be clear without being distinct: a severe pain is vivid, but we confuse it with unclear judgments about what is in the body, so it is not distinct. What is distinct is always clear.

The rule is not yet secure. Descartes notes that it does not follow merely from the fact that the cogito is certain that the rule holds in every case. The deceiver hypothesis suggests that even clear perceptions of simple mathematical truths might be false. He therefore resolves to find out whether there is a God and, if so, whether God can be a deceiver. This is the subject of week 4, and it leads to the Cartesian circle. Since we need a guarantee for the rule, the cogito is not, for Descartes, a proof of the rule; it is a model of what clear and distinct perception is like.

@example
title: Reconstructing and testing the cogito
problem: Put the argument "I doubt everything, but I cannot doubt that I am doubting; so I exist" into standard form. Find the unstated premise and say whether the argument is valid.
step: The stated premise is that I am doubting, and the conclusion is that I exist. Write them as P1 and C.
step: To get from "I am doubting" to "I exist" we need a further premise connecting thinking with existing: whatever thinks (doubts) exists. This is P2, the suppressed premise.
step: With P1 (I am thinking, and doubting is a form of thinking), P2 (whatever thinks exists) and C (I exist), the argument is valid, since it has the form of a syllogism in which the premises guarantee the conclusion.
step: Descartes says that in practice the thinker grasps existence without the syllogism, by intuition, so the suppressed premise is not one he needs to state, though a reconstruction makes it explicit.
answer: P1: I am thinking. P2: Whatever thinks exists. C: I exist. Valid, with P2 suppressed.

@example
title: Clear, distinct, both or neither
problem: Classify each perception as clear, distinct, both or neither, using Descartes's definitions: (a) the perception that a figure has three sides if it is a triangle; (b) a dim half-memory of a face seen long ago; (c) a throbbing headache, together with the thought that the cause is something in the head.
step: (a) The perception is present to the attentive mind and contains nothing beyond what is evident, so it is both clear and distinct.
step: (b) A half-memory is neither present and accessible to the attentive mind nor separated from other perceptions, so it is neither.
step: (c) The headache is vivid, so it is clear, but the judgment about its cause mixes in unclear beliefs, so it is not distinct.
answer: (a) both; (b) neither; (c) clear but not distinct.

@mistakes
- Reading the cogito as showing that the self is immaterial: at this stage Descartes has shown only that he exists as a thinking thing, and the distinction from body is argued later.
- Saying that clear and distinct mean the same thing: distinctness requires that the perception contains only what is clear, so a vivid pain can be clear without being distinct.
- Supposing that the cogito proves the truth rule: Descartes uses the cogito as a model of clear and distinct perception, and the rule itself still awaits a guarantee.
- Thinking Descartes holds that he exists independently of thinking: he says that he is certain of existing only as long as he thinks.
