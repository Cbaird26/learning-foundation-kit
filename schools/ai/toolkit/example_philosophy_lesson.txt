@article
One grain of sand is not a heap. Adding a single grain to something that is not a heap cannot make it a heap: one grain is too small to make that difference. But then two grains are not a heap, so three grains are not a heap, and so on, until you must conclude that a million grains are not a heap either. That is plainly false. This is the **sorites paradox**, from the Greek *sōros*, "heap". It is usually credited to Eubulides of Miletus, a contemporary of Aristotle, and it arises for almost every word we use about the ordinary world: *bald*, *tall*, *red*, *child*, *old*.

### What makes a word vague

A predicate is **vague** when it has **borderline cases**: cases where it is unclear whether it applies, and where more information would not settle the question. You can count every hair on a man's head and still not know whether he is bald. Vague predicates also seem to have no sharp boundaries, and they seem **tolerant**: a tiny change never makes the difference between applying and not applying. Vagueness is different from **ambiguity** ("bank" has two meanings, each of which may be vague) and from **generality** ("someone called" is unspecific, but not vague).

### The argument

Write $H(n)$ for "$n$ grains of sand make a heap". The paradox is a short argument:

| P1 | $\neg H(1)$: one grain is not a heap. |
| P2 | $\forall n\,(\neg H(n) \to \neg H(n+1))$: adding one grain never makes a heap. |
| C | $\neg H(1{,}000{,}000)$: a million grains are not a heap. |

The argument is **valid** in classical logic. P2 yields each conditional $\neg H(1) \to \neg H(2)$, $\neg H(2) \to \neg H(3)$ and so on, and 999,999 steps of modus ponens lead from P1 to C. The base premise is true and the conclusion is false, so in classical logic P2 must be false. But the negation of P2 is

$$\exists n\,(\neg H(n) \wedge H(n+1)),$$

which says that some number of grains is not a heap while one more grain is: a sharp boundary. That seems as hard to accept as the conclusion. Each response to the paradox gives something up.

### Epistemicism: a sharp but unknowable boundary

**Epistemicists**, most prominently Timothy Williamson and Roy Sorensen, keep classical logic and **bivalence**, the principle that every statement is true or false. There really is a last number of grains that is not a heap; vagueness is our **ignorance** of where it lies. On Williamson's account, the meaning of "heap" is fixed by our whole pattern of use, which is too complex for anyone to survey, and knowledge needs a **margin for error**, so no one can know of any particular number that it is the boundary. The cost: many find it incredible that so fine-grained a fact is settled and yet beyond anyone's knowledge.

### Supervaluationism: truth on every sharpening

**Supervaluationists**, following Kit Fine's classic paper of 1975, say a vague word could be made precise in many acceptable ways, called **sharpenings** (or precisifications). A sentence is **supertrue** if it is true on every admissible sharpening, **superfalse** if it is false on every one, and neither otherwise. A borderline sentence is neither, so supervaluationism gives up bivalence. But every sharpening draws a line somewhere, so classical laws such as excluded middle ("either this is a heap or it is not") come out supertrue, P2 comes out superfalse, and the claim that there is a boundary is supertrue, although no particular number is the boundary on every sharpening. The costs: an existential statement can be true although none of its instances is, and which sharpenings count as admissible seems itself vague (**higher-order vagueness**).

### Degree theories: truth comes in degrees

**Degree theorists** give sentences degrees of truth from 0 to 1, and "$n$ grains make a heap" grows slowly truer as $n$ rises. Each instance of P2 is then almost, but not quite, completely true. With the Łukasiewicz conditional, $v(A \to B) = \min(1,\ 1 - v(A) + v(B))$, each step of modus ponens can lose a tiny amount of truth, and a million tiny losses add up: nearly true steps can lead from a true premise to a false conclusion. The costs: an exact degree such as 0.73 for "this is a heap" seems as arbitrary as a sharp boundary, and on the usual rules a borderline "it is a heap and it is not a heap" can be half true rather than simply false.

### Where things stand

There are other options. Some philosophers accept the conclusion and say that nothing is really a heap; others say that vague words shift their boundaries with the context; and some locate vagueness in the world itself rather than in our words or our knowledge. No response commands a consensus: each keeps something we want to keep, and each pays a price. To judge them, apply each one to a new sorites series and ask what it says about the boundary.

@example
title: Reconstructing a sorites argument
problem: Put this reasoning in standard form, show that it is valid, and say what each of the three main responses says about it: "Someone 1,500 millimeters tall is not tall. Growing by a single millimeter can never make someone tall. So even someone 2,100 millimeters tall is not tall."
step: Write $T(n)$ for "someone $n$ millimeters tall is tall". The base premise is P1, $\neg T(1500)$; the tolerance premise is P2, $\forall n\,(\neg T(n) \to \neg T(n+1))$; the conclusion is C, $\neg T(2100)$.
step: Validity: P2 yields the conditionals from $\neg T(1500) \to \neg T(1501)$ to $\neg T(2099) \to \neg T(2100)$, and $2100 - 1500 = 600$ applications of modus ponens take you from P1 to C.
step: P1 is true and C is false (2,100 millimeters is almost 6 feet 11 inches). So in classical logic P2 is false, and its negation says that there is some $n$ with $\neg T(n) \wedge T(n+1)$: a last height that is not tall.
step: The epistemicist accepts this: there is such a height, but no one can know which it is. The supervaluationist calls P2 superfalse and "there is a last height that is not tall" supertrue, while denying that any particular height is the boundary on every sharpening. The degree theorist says each conditional is very nearly but not fully true, so the small losses over 600 steps let the conclusion come out false.
answer: The argument is valid. All three responses reject the tolerance premise, but they give different accounts of what its failure amounts to.

@example
title: Supervaluation on a small model
problem: Suppose the admissible sharpenings of "tall" draw the line in exactly three places: 180, 181 or 182 centimeters (on each, a height counts as tall when it is at or above that sharpening's line). Is each sentence supertrue, superfalse or neither? (a) "Someone 181 cm tall is tall." (b) "Someone 181 cm tall is tall or is not tall." (c) "Some whole-centimeter height is not tall while 1 cm more is tall." (d) "179 cm is not tall while 180 cm is tall."
step: (a) With the line at 180 or 181, 181 cm is tall; with the line at 182, it is not. It is true on some sharpenings and false on another, so it is **neither**: a borderline case.
step: (b) On every sharpening, 181 cm is either at or above the line or below it, so the disjunction is true on all three: **supertrue**, although neither disjunct is supertrue.
step: (c) Every sharpening has a boundary (179 and 180 cm on the first, 180 and 181 cm on the second, 181 and 182 cm on the third), so the existential sentence is true on all three: **supertrue**.
step: (d) This is true only on the sharpening with its line at 180 and false on the other two: **neither**. Every sharpening has a boundary, but no one boundary is shared by all of them.
answer: (a) neither; (b) supertrue; (c) supertrue; (d) neither.

@mistakes
- Calling the sorites argument invalid: each step is modus ponens, so it is valid in classical logic. The responses disagree about the tolerance premise or about the logic, not about validity.
- Confusing vagueness with ambiguity ("bank") or with generality ("someone called").
- Saying that supervaluationism rejects the law of excluded middle: "A or not A" is supertrue. What it gives up is bivalence.
- Saying that the epistemicist thinks the boundary is arbitrary or stipulated: it is fixed by our use of the word, but no one can know where it is.
