@article
Plain gradient descent has two weaknesses. On a long, narrow valley it zigzags across the steep walls while crawling along the floor. And one learning rate must serve every parameter, although some parameters see gradients a thousand times larger than others. Two ideas, each a few lines of code, address these: **momentum** and **adaptive per-parameter step sizes**. Their combination is **Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014).

### Momentum

Keep a **velocity** $\mathbf{v}$, a running accumulation of past gradients, and move along it. With momentum coefficient $\beta \in [0, 1)$ and learning rate $\eta$,

$$\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \beta\,\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta} \leftarrow \boldsymbol{\theta} - \eta\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad \mathbf{v}_0 = \mathbf{0}.$$

(This is the convention of PyTorch's `SGD`; other texts put a factor $(1-\beta)$ on $\mathbf{g}$, which just rescales $\eta$.) If the gradient is a constant $\mathbf{g}$, the velocity converges to $\mathbf{g}/(1 - \beta)$: with $\beta = 0.9$ the effective step is ten times larger. If the gradient flips sign every step, as it does across the steep walls of a valley, the contributions largely cancel. So momentum speeds up along directions of consistent gradient and damps oscillation.

A one-dimensional trace on $f(w) = 2w^2$ (gradient $4w$) from $w = 1$ with $\eta = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.9$:

| step | $g$ | $v$ | $w$ after step |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4.0 | 4.0 | 0.60 |
| 2 | 2.4 | 6.0 | 0.00 |
| 3 | 0.0 | 5.4 | −0.54 |
| 4 | −2.16 | 2.7 | −0.81 |

Plain SGD with the same $\eta$ would go $1 \to 0.6 \to 0.36 \to 0.216$. Momentum reaches the minimum faster but *overshoots* it, because the velocity carries the point past $w = 0$ and takes several steps to turn around. Too much momentum causes oscillation, which is why $\beta$ is typically 0.9 or a bit above, and why it is tuned together with $\eta$.

### Adaptive step sizes and Adam

Adam keeps two exponential moving averages of the gradient $\mathbf{g}_t$ at step $t$ (all operations on vectors are elementwise):

$$\mathbf{m}_t = \beta_1 \mathbf{m}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_1)\,\mathbf{g}_t, \qquad \mathbf{s}_t = \beta_2 \mathbf{s}_{t-1} + (1 - \beta_2)\,\mathbf{g}_t^{2},$$

the first moment (a momentum-like average) and the second moment (the mean squared gradient). Both start at 0, so early on they are biased toward 0. Dividing by $1 - \beta^t$ corrects this:

$$\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{m}_t}{1 - \beta_1^t}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{s}}_t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_t}{1 - \beta_2^t}, \qquad \boldsymbol{\theta}_t = \boldsymbol{\theta}_{t-1} - \eta\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf{m}}_t}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t} + \varepsilon}.$$

The defaults are $\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$ and $\varepsilon = 10^{-8}$. Each parameter's step is divided by the typical size of its own gradient, so a parameter whose gradients are 100 and a parameter whose gradients are 0.01 both move by about $\eta$ per step. At $t = 1$, $\hat{\mathbf{m}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1$ and $\hat{\mathbf{s}}_1 = \mathbf{g}_1^2$, so the first step is $\eta\, g/(|g| + \varepsilon) \approx \eta\,\operatorname{sign}(g)$.

Two steps for a single parameter with gradients $g_1 = 2$ and $g_2 = -1$: after step 1, $m = 0.2$, $s = 0.004$, so $\hat{m} = 2$, $\hat{s} = 4$ and the step is $\eta \cdot 2/2 = \eta$. After step 2, $m = 0.08$, $s = 0.004996$, so $\hat{m} = 0.08/0.19 = 0.4211$, $\hat{s} = 0.004996/0.001999 = 2.4992$ and the step is $\eta \cdot 0.4211/1.5809 = 0.2663\,\eta$: the sign flip shrinks the step.

Adam is often a good default because it needs less learning-rate tuning, but it is not uniformly better than SGD with momentum: the best optimizer depends on the model and data, and the choice is still tuned empirically. Adam with an L2 penalty behaves differently from true weight decay; Week 8 returns to this.

### In code

```python
def momentum_step(w, v, g, lr, beta):
    v = [beta * vi + gi for vi, gi in zip(v, g)]
    w = [wi - lr * vi for wi, vi in zip(w, v)]
    return w, v

w, v = [1.0], [0.0]
for _ in range(4):
    w, v = momentum_step(w, v, [4 * w[0]], 0.1, 0.9)
    print(round(w[0], 2), round(v[0], 2))
```

This prints `0.6 4.0`, `0.0 6.0`, `-0.54 5.4`, `-0.81 2.7`, the table above. In PyTorch, `torch.optim.SGD(params, lr=0.1, momentum=0.9)` and `torch.optim.Adam(params, lr=1e-3)` keep this state for you.

@example
title: Two momentum steps
problem: Minimize $f(w) = w^2$ (gradient $2w$) from $w = 3$ with $\eta = 0.1$ and $\beta = 0.5$. Compute $w$ after two steps and compare with plain gradient descent.
step: Step 1: $g = 6$, $v = 0.5 \cdot 0 + 6 = 6$, $w = 3 - 0.1 \cdot 6 = 2.4$.
step: Step 2: $g = 4.8$, $v = 0.5 \cdot 6 + 4.8 = 7.8$, $w = 2.4 - 0.1 \cdot 7.8 = 1.62$.
step: Plain gradient descent: $3 \to 2.4 \to 1.92$.
answer: Momentum gives $w = 1.62$ after two steps, plain descent $1.92$: the velocity accumulated past gradients.

@example
title: Adam on parameters with very different gradient scales
problem: Two parameters have gradients $g = (50, 0.002)$ at the first step. With $\eta = 0.01$, how far does each move in Adam's first step, and how far would plain SGD move them?
step: At $t = 1$: $m = 0.1g$, $s = 0.001g^2$, so $\hat{m} = g$ and $\hat{s} = g^2$.
step: The Adam step is $\eta\,g/(|g| + \varepsilon)$, which is $0.01 \cdot 0.9999999998 \approx 0.01$ for the first parameter and $0.01 \cdot 0.999995 \approx 0.01$ for the second.
step: SGD with the same $\eta$ would move them by $0.01 \cdot 50 = 0.5$ and $0.01 \cdot 0.002 = 0.00002$.
answer: Adam moves both by about 0.01; SGD moves them by 0.5 and 0.00002, a factor of 25,000 apart.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the bias correction in Adam: at $t = 1$ the uncorrected $m_1 = 0.1g$ and $s_1 = 0.001g^2$ would make the first step about $0.1/0.0316 \approx 3.16$ times the intended size, with the wrong scale as well.
- Applying the momentum update with the old gradient or forgetting to carry the velocity between steps (re-initializing $\mathbf{v}$ each step turns it back into plain SGD).
- Assuming a larger momentum is always better: values near 1 let the velocity overshoot and oscillate.
- Believing Adam removes the need to tune the learning rate: it makes the scale of each step roughly $\eta$ in every parameter, so $\eta$ still matters a great deal.

model:
```python
def momentum_descent(grad, w0, lr, beta, steps):
    """SGD with momentum; returns the parameters after each step."""
    w = list(w0)
    v = [0.0] * len(w)
    path = []
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = grad(w)
        v = [beta * vi + gi for vi, gi in zip(v, g)]
        w = [wi - lr * vi for wi, vi in zip(w, v)]
        path.append(w)
    return path
```
hint: Keep the velocity list across iterations (start it at zeros), update it with the gradient at the current `w`, then move `w` by `lr` times the new velocity and record it.
solution: The velocity is carried from step to step; resetting it each step would give plain SGD, which the second and third entries of the first test would reveal. The parameter step uses the freshly updated velocity, and `w` is rebound to a new list at every step, so the recorded entries are separate lists and the starting list is untouched.

model:
```python
import math

def adam_step(w, m, v, g, t, lr, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    """One Adam step on lists of parameters; returns new (w, m, v)."""
    new_m = [beta1 * mi + (1 - beta1) * gi for mi, gi in zip(m, g)]
    new_v = [beta2 * vi + (1 - beta2) * gi * gi for vi, gi in zip(v, g)]
    c1 = 1 - beta1 ** t
    c2 = 1 - beta2 ** t
    new_w = [wi - lr * (mi / c1) / (math.sqrt(vi / c2) + eps)
             for wi, mi, vi in zip(w, new_m, new_v)]
    return new_w, new_m, new_v
```
hint: Update `m` and `v` first, divide them by `1 - beta1 ** t` and `1 - beta2 ** t`, and then compute the parameter step from the corrected values.
solution: The moments are updated before the correction and the correction uses the current step number `t`. The zero-gradient test checks that a parameter does not move (and that adding `eps` avoids division by zero), and the two-step test reproduces the sign-flip example from the article.

model:
```python
import math

def run_adam(grad, w0, lr, steps):
    """Minimize a function with Adam, starting from w0."""
    beta1, beta2, eps = 0.9, 0.999, 1e-8
    w = list(w0)
    m = [0.0] * len(w)
    v = [0.0] * len(w)
    for t in range(1, steps + 1):
        g = grad(w)
        m = [beta1 * mi + (1 - beta1) * gi for mi, gi in zip(m, g)]
        v = [beta2 * vi + (1 - beta2) * gi * gi for vi, gi in zip(v, g)]
        c1 = 1 - beta1 ** t
        c2 = 1 - beta2 ** t
        w = [wi - lr * (mi / c1) / (math.sqrt(vi / c2) + eps)
             for wi, mi, vi in zip(w, m, v)]
    return w
```
hint: Keep `m`, `v` and `w` across iterations, call `grad(w)` at the start of each step, and use the step number `t` (starting at 1) in the bias correction.
solution: This is `adam_step` placed in a loop with the moments carried over. The first test shows the sign-like first step, the second checks the carried moments, and the third checks convergence near the minimum at 0 (with a constant learning rate Adam jitters around it at a scale of roughly $\eta$).

@quiz self
prompt: Explain what the momentum velocity does when the gradient points the same way for many steps, and what it does when the gradient flips sign every step. Then explain, in two or three sentences, why Adam can use one learning rate for parameters whose gradients differ in size by a factor of 1,000.
hint: Sum the geometric series for a constant gradient; for alternating signs, add terms of opposite sign. For Adam, think about what the denominator contains.
solution: With a constant gradient $g$ the velocity follows $v_k = g(1 - \beta^k)/(1 - \beta)$ and converges to $g/(1 - \beta)$, so the effective step is $1/(1 - \beta)$ times larger (ten times for $\beta = 0.9$): consistent directions are accelerated. If the gradient alternates between $+g$ and $-g$, successive contributions mostly cancel and the velocity stays near $g/(1 + \beta)$ in magnitude, much smaller than the accumulated value in a consistent direction, so oscillations across a narrow valley are damped. Adam divides each parameter's averaged gradient by the square root of its averaged squared gradient, which has the same units and scale as the gradient itself, so the update is of order $\eta$ for every parameter regardless of whether its gradients are 0.01 or 10; this normalization is what makes one global learning rate workable.
rubric: Shows that a constant gradient yields a velocity of about $g/(1-\beta)$ and a larger effective step.
