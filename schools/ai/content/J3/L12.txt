@article
**Bivariate data** are pairs of values measured on the same individuals, such as hours studied and test score. A **scatter plot** graphs each pair as a point, with one variable on each axis. It lets you see whether the variables are related.

### Describing association

- **Direction:** positive (as $x$ rises, $y$ tends to rise), negative (as $x$ rises, $y$ tends to fall) or none.
- **Form:** linear (points follow a line) or nonlinear (they follow a curve).
- **Strength:** strong if the points hug the pattern closely, weak if they are widely scattered.
- **Outliers:** points far from the pattern. They deserve a second look.

**Association is not causation.** Two variables can move together without one causing the other.

### A line of fit

When the association is roughly linear, draw a straight **trend line** (line of fit) that passes close to as many points as possible, with about as many points above as below. It need not touch any point.

To write its equation, choose **two points on your line** (not necessarily data points), compute the slope, and find the intercept as in the linear functions lesson. For the line through $(2, 10)$ and $(8, 34)$ the slope is $\frac{34 - 10}{8 - 2} = 4$.

### Predicting

Substitute an $x$ into the line's equation to predict $y$. If the line is $y = 3.5x + 12$, then at $x = 10$ the prediction is $3.5(10) + 12 = 47$.

- **Interpolation** (predicting inside the range of the data) is usually reasonably reliable.
- **Extrapolation** (predicting far outside the range) can be badly wrong because the pattern may not continue.

### Interpreting the line

In context, the **slope** is the predicted change in $y$ for each one-unit increase in $x$. The **intercept** is the predicted $y$ when $x = 0$, which only makes sense if $x = 0$ is a sensible value. For hours studied versus score, a slope of $4.2$ means each extra hour of study goes with about $4.2$ more points.

### Residuals

The **residual** of a data point is the vertical gap, actual $y$ minus predicted $y$. A point above the line has a positive residual and a point below has a negative one. Small residuals mean the line fits well.

### Two-way tables

Bivariate data can also be **categorical**, such as grade level and preferred sport. A **two-way table** shows counts for each combination, and relative frequencies show whether the categories are associated.

@example
title: Fitting a line and predicting
problem: A trend line for plant height $h$ (cm) after $t$ weeks passes through $(1, 7)$ and $(5, 23)$. Find its equation and predict the height at week $8$.
step: Slope: $\frac{23 - 7}{5 - 1} = 4$ cm per week.
step: Intercept: $7 = 4(1) + b$, so $b = 3$. The line is $h = 4t + 3$.
step: Predict at $t = 8$: $h = 4(8) + 3 = 35$ cm.
step: Interpret: the plant grows about $4$ cm each week; $t = 8$ is a modest extension of the data, so the prediction is reasonable but not guaranteed.

@mistakes
- Forcing the trend line through the origin or through the first and last data points just because they are data.
- Treating a strong association as proof of cause and effect.
- Extrapolating far beyond the data and trusting the prediction.
- Giving the slope without units or without saying what it means.
