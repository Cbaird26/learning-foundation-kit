@review
**Weeks 1 and 2: logic.** Propositions combine with $\neg, \land, \lor, \to, \leftrightarrow$. $p \to q$ is false only when $p$ is true and $q$ false, and it is equivalent to $\neg p \lor q$ and to its contrapositive $\neg q \to \neg p$ (not to its converse). A truth table with $2^n$ rows proves equivalences. Quantifiers: $\neg\forall x\,P \equiv \exists x\,\neg P$; "every $A$ is $B$" uses $\to$, "some $A$ is $B$" uses $\land$; $\forall\exists$ differs from $\exists\forall$.

**Weeks 3 and 4: proofs.** Direct proof unfolds definitions; contrapositive proves $\neg q \to \neg p$; contradiction assumes the negation and derives something false. Induction needs a base case and a step from $P(k)$ to $P(k+1)$; strong induction assumes all earlier cases and often needs several base cases.

**Weeks 5 and 6: sets, functions, relations.** $|\mathcal{P}(A)| = 2^{|A|}$, $|A \times B| = |A||B|$. Injective: distinct inputs give distinct outputs; surjective: every codomain element is hit; bijective: both. Equivalence relations are reflexive, symmetric and transitive; their classes partition the set. Partial orders are reflexive, antisymmetric and transitive; know maximal, minimum, chains and antichains.

**Weeks 7 and 8: counting.** Product and sum rules, complement, $P(n, k)$, $\binom{n}{k}$, multinomials, stars and bars $\binom{n+k-1}{k-1}$. Pigeonhole: $N$ objects in $k$ boxes force some box to hold $\lceil N/k \rceil$. Inclusion–exclusion alternates signs, and $D_n = \sum_j (-1)^j n!/j!$.

**Weeks 9 and 10: probability and number theory.** Uniform probability is favorable over total; $\Pr[A \mid B] = \Pr[A \cap B]/\Pr[B]$; Bayes' rule needs the base rate; expectation is linear even without independence. $\gcd(a, b) = \gcd(b, a \bmod b)$; Bézout gives $ax + by = \gcd$; $a^{-1} \bmod m$ exists iff $\gcd(a, m) = 1$; Fermat: $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: graphs, trees, recurrences.** $\sum \deg = 2|E|$. Euler circuit: connected and all degrees even; Euler trail: exactly two odd vertices. A tree is connected and acyclic, has $n - 1$ edges and unique paths. Recurrences: unroll, use the characteristic equation, or the master theorem, then verify.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) write a truth table and read off equivalence, (2) negate any quantified statement, (3) choose between the three proof techniques, (4) write an induction proof with every part labeled, (5) classify a function, (6) decide whether order and repetition matter in a counting problem, (7) compute an inverse mod $m$ by hand, (8) apply Euler's theorem on a graph, and (9) solve a two-term linear recurrence?

**Common errors.** Proving the converse; treating examples as proofs; omitting a base case; counting ordered when order does not matter; adding pairs without subtracting intersections; using $ab$ instead of $\mathrm{lcm}(a, b)$; dividing in modular arithmetic without an inverse; forgetting that Euler tours need connectivity.

model:
```python
def rsa_roundtrip(p, q, e, message):
    n = p * q
    phi = (p - 1) * (q - 1)
    old_r, r = e % phi, phi
    old_s, s = 1, 0
    while r:
        k = old_r // r
        old_r, r = r, old_r - k * r
        old_s, s = s, old_s - k * s
    d = old_s % phi
    c = pow(message, e, n)
    return (n, d, c, pow(c, d, n))
```
hint: First the inverse of `e` modulo `phi` with the extended Euclidean algorithm; keep the coefficient of `e` and reduce it modulo `phi`.
hint: Then `pow(message, e, n)` encrypts and `pow(c, d, n)` decrypts. The returned decryption must equal `message` for every message below `n`.
solution: Because $ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}$, decryption returns the message for every $0 \le m < n$ when $n$ is a product of two distinct primes, by Fermat's little theorem applied modulo $p$ and modulo $q$. The extended Euclidean algorithm provides the inverse, and `pow` with three arguments performs repeated squaring.

prompt: A bit string of length 10 is chosen uniformly at random from all $2^{10}$ bit strings. What is the probability that it contains exactly three 1s and no two 1s are adjacent? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 7/128
check: Fraction(sum(1 for x in range(1024) if bin(x).count('1') == 3 and x & (x >> 1) == 0), 1024)
hint: The sample space is uniform with $1024$ outcomes, so count the favorable strings.
hint: Place the seven 0s first. The three 1s must go into three different gaps among the eight gaps (before, between and after the 0s). How many ways to choose the gaps?
solution: The seven $0$s create $8$ gaps, and choosing $3$ different gaps for the $1$s gives $\binom{8}{3} = 56$ strings with no two $1$s adjacent. The probability is $56/1024 = 7/128$.

@exam self
prompt: Prove by induction on the number of edges that every connected graph with $n$ vertices has at least $n - 1$ edges. You may use the fact that a tree with $n$ vertices has exactly $n - 1$ edges and that deleting an edge of a cycle from a connected graph leaves it connected.
solution: Let $P(m)$ be the statement: every connected graph with exactly $m$ edges, on any number $n$ of vertices, satisfies $m \ge n - 1$. We prove $P(m)$ for all $m \ge 0$ by strong induction on $m$. Let $G$ be a connected graph with $n$ vertices and $m$ edges, and assume $P(j)$ for every $j < m$. Case 1: $G$ has no cycle. Then $G$ is connected and acyclic, so it is a tree, and by the given fact $m = n - 1 \ge n - 1$. This case needs no hypothesis and includes $m = 0$: a connected graph with no edges is a single vertex, so $n = 1$ and $m = 0 = n - 1$. Case 2: $G$ has a cycle. Delete one edge of the cycle. The graph $G'$ is still connected (given fact), has the same $n$ vertices and has $m - 1 < m$ edges, so $P(m - 1)$ applies to it: $m - 1 \ge n - 1$. Hence $m \ge n > n - 1$. In both cases $m \ge n - 1$, so $P(m)$ holds. No separate base case is needed, because the case that uses the hypothesis (a cycle exists) only arises when $m \ge 3$, and the case $m = 0$ falls under Case 1.
rubric: Sets up the induction on the number of edges with an explicit statement and handles the acyclic case by the tree edge count.
rubric: In the cyclic case deletes an edge of a cycle, notes the graph stays connected with one fewer edge, and applies the hypothesis.
rubric: Combines the cases correctly to conclude $m \ge n - 1$.
hint: Split into the case where the graph has a cycle and the case where it does not.
