@article
A **greedy algorithm** builds a solution one piece at a time, always taking the choice that looks best right now and never reconsidering. Greedy algorithms are usually simple and fast, but they are correct only for problems with special structure, and a plausible rule is often wrong. So the real work is the **proof**.

### Proof technique: the exchange argument

To show that a greedy solution $G$ is optimal, take any optimal solution $O$. Show you can transform $O$ step by step into $G$ without ever making it worse: find where $O$ differs from $G$ and **exchange** that part of $O$ for the greedy choice. Typically you prove two things: (1) the **greedy choice property**: some optimal solution contains the greedy first choice, and (2) **optimal substructure**: what remains after that choice is a smaller instance of the same problem, so induction finishes the proof. The alternative, **greedy stays ahead**, shows that after each step the greedy partial solution is at least as good as any other's.

### Interval scheduling

Given jobs with start and finish times, choose the largest set of pairwise non-overlapping jobs. Use half-open intervals $[s, f)$, so a job may start exactly when another finishes. The rule "pick the job that **finishes earliest**, discard those that overlap it, repeat" is optimal; the rules "earliest start" and "shortest job" are not (a long early job can block many short ones, and a short job can overlap two jobs that fit side by side). Implementation: sort by finish time, scan once, take a job when its start is at least the last finish. Time $\Theta(n \log n)$.

*Exchange argument.* Let $g_1$ be the job with the earliest finish time and let $O$ be an optimal schedule whose first job is $o_1$. Since $f(g_1) \le f(o_1)$, replacing $o_1$ by $g_1$ cannot overlap $o_2$, so the result is still feasible and has the same size. Hence an optimal schedule containing $g_1$ exists. Removing $g_1$ and every job overlapping it leaves a smaller instance, and induction applies.

### Minimizing maximum lateness

Jobs have lengths $t_i$ and deadlines $d_i$, run one at a time with no idle time. If job $i$ finishes at $f_i$, its lateness is $\max(0, f_i - d_i)$. **Earliest deadline first** minimizes the maximum lateness. Proof idea: an **inversion** is a job $i$ scheduled right before $j$ with $d_i > d_j$. Swapping them does not increase the maximum lateness, and a schedule with no inversions is exactly the earliest-deadline order.

### Huffman codes

A **prefix code** assigns each symbol a bit string, none a prefix of another, so a bit stream decodes uniquely. For symbol frequencies $f_i$ and code lengths $\ell_i$ we minimize $\sum f_i \ell_i$. **Huffman's algorithm** repeatedly merges the two lowest-frequency nodes into a node whose frequency is their sum, until one tree remains; code lengths are the leaf depths. With a binary heap it takes $\Theta(n\log n)$ time. It is optimal because (exchange) some optimal tree has the two least frequent symbols as sibling leaves at the deepest level. The total cost equals the sum of the weights of all internal (merged) nodes.

### Fractional knapsack, and where greedy fails

If items can be taken in fractions, take them in decreasing order of value per unit weight until the capacity is full; this is optimal. But for the **0/1 knapsack**, where items are all-or-nothing, the same rule can fail (next weeks use dynamic programming instead).

Coin change is the standard cautionary tale. Greedy "take the largest coin that fits" is optimal for the coins $\{1, 5, 10, 25\}$, but not for $\{1, 3, 4\}$:

```python
def greedy_coins(coins, amount):
    """Number of coins used by the largest-coin-first rule."""
    count = 0
    for c in sorted(coins, reverse=True):
        count += amount // c
        amount %= c
    return count

print(greedy_coins([1, 3, 4], 6))  # 3, using 4 + 1 + 1; but 3 + 3 uses 2
```

One counterexample is enough to disprove a greedy rule.

@example
title: Greedy interval scheduling
problem: Choose the maximum number of non-overlapping half-open intervals from $(1,3), (2,5), (4,7), (6,8), (5,9), (8,10)$.
step: Sort by finish time: $(1,3), (2,5), (4,7), (6,8), (5,9), (8,10)$ (this list is already in that order).

step: $(4,7)$ starts at 4 ≥ 3: take it; last finish is now 7. $(6,8)$ starts at 6 < 7 and $(5,9)$ starts at 5 < 7: skip both.
step: $(8,10)$ starts at 8 ≥ 7: take it.
answer: 3 intervals: $(1,3), (4,7), (8,10)$.

@example
title: Building a Huffman tree
problem: Symbols have frequency counts 3, 5, 7, 10 and 15. Find the total cost $\sum f_i \ell_i$ of an optimal prefix code.
step: Merge the two smallest, 3 and 5, into a node of weight 8. Remaining weights: 7, 8, 10, 15.
step: Merge 7 and 8 into 15. Remaining: 10, 15, 15.
step: Merge 10 and 15 into 25. Remaining: 15, 25. Finally merge 15 and 25 into 40, the root.
step: The cost is the sum of the weights of the merged nodes: $8 + 15 + 25 + 40 = 88$. Check with depths: the symbol with count 15 is at depth 1, count 10 at depth 2, count 7 at depth 3, and counts 3 and 5 at depth 4, so $15 + 20 + 21 + 12 + 20 = 88$.
answer: 88

@mistakes
- Trusting a greedy rule because it works on the examples you tried. Look for a counterexample first, and then prove the rule.
- In interval scheduling, sorting by start time or by length instead of finish time.
- Treating $[1,3)$ and $[3,5)$ as overlapping. With half-open intervals they are compatible; decide the convention and keep it.
- Applying the largest-ratio rule to the 0/1 knapsack problem; it is optimal only for the fractional version.

```python
def fractional_knapsack(items, capacity):
    total = 0.0
    remaining = capacity
    for value, weight in sorted(items, key=lambda it: it[0] / it[1], reverse=True):
        if remaining <= 0:
            break
        take = min(weight, remaining)
        total += value * take / weight
        remaining -= take
    return total
```
hint: Rank the items by value per unit of weight, best first.
hint: Take each item whole while it fits, then a fraction of the first one that does not fit.
solution: Sort by `value / weight` in descending order, then take as much of each item as the remaining capacity allows. Taking the highest-ratio material first is optimal by an exchange argument: replacing a unit of weight of a lower-ratio item with a unit of a higher-ratio one never decreases the value.

@practice self
prompt: Jobs $1, \dots, n$ have lengths $t_i > 0$ and deadlines $d_i$, and are run one at a time starting at time 0 with no idle time. The lateness of a job is $\max(0, f_i - d_i)$ where $f_i$ is its finish time. Prove that scheduling the jobs in order of increasing deadline minimizes the maximum lateness.
solution: Call a pair of jobs $i, j$ an inversion in a schedule if $i$ runs before $j$ (not necessarily immediately before) but $d_i > d_j$. Let $S$ be any schedule with no idle time. Claim: if $S$ has an inversion, it has an adjacent one. Otherwise the deadlines along the sequence never decrease from one job to the next, so no earlier job has a later deadline than a later job, a contradiction. Swap an adjacent inverted pair $i, j$ (so $d_i > d_j$). Let $F$ be the time at which the pair finishes (the same before and after the swap). Jobs outside the pair are unaffected. After the swap, $j$ finishes earlier than before, so its lateness does not increase. Job $i$ now finishes at $F$, with lateness $\max(0, F - d_i)$; before the swap, $j$ finished at $F$ with lateness $\max(0, F - d_j) \ge \max(0, F - d_i)$ because $d_j < d_i$. So the new lateness of $i$ is at most the old lateness of $j$, and the maximum lateness over the whole schedule does not increase. Each such swap reduces the number of inversions by exactly one, so after finitely many swaps we reach a schedule with no inversions and maximum lateness no larger than before. All inversion-free schedules are orderings by non-decreasing deadline; they differ only in the order of jobs with equal deadlines, and these have the same maximum lateness (the last job of a block of equal deadlines has the largest lateness, and the block ends at the same time). So earliest-deadline-first is optimal.
rubric: Defines inversions and shows that a schedule with an inversion has an adjacent inverted pair.
rubric: Shows that swapping an adjacent inverted pair does not increase the maximum lateness, comparing the new lateness of $i$ with the old lateness of $j$.
rubric: Concludes by repeating swaps until no inversion is left, and handles ties between equal deadlines.
hint: Think of any optimal schedule and fix its inversions one adjacent pair at a time.
hint: After swapping an adjacent pair, compare the new lateness of the job moved later with the old lateness of the job moved earlier.

model:
```python
def min_rooms(meetings):
    starts = sorted(s for s, e in meetings)
    ends = sorted(e for s, e in meetings)
    rooms = 0
    j = 0
    for s in starts:
        if s >= ends[j]:
            j += 1
        else:
            rooms += 1
    return rooms
```
hint: The answer is the largest number of meetings that are in progress at the same instant.
hint: Sort the start times and the end times separately and sweep through the starts; a start at or after the earliest unmatched end can reuse that room.
solution: Sort starts and ends independently. For each start, if it is at least the earliest end that has not been reused yet, a room frees up and is reused; otherwise a new room is needed. The count of rooms equals the maximum overlap. Time $\Theta(n\log n)$.
