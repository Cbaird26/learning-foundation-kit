@article
A **magnet** is an object that attracts iron and some other metals. Every magnet has two ends, called **poles**: a north pole and a south pole. **Like poles repel and opposite poles attract.** If you cut a magnet in half, you get two smaller magnets, each with its own north and south pole. You cannot isolate a single pole.

### Magnetic fields

A magnet is surrounded by a **magnetic field**, the region where it can exert magnetic force. We draw field lines coming out of the north pole and into the south pole. Lines that are close together mean a stronger field. Earth itself acts like a giant magnet, which is why a compass needle, itself a small magnet, lines up roughly north-south.

Only a few materials, such as iron, nickel and cobalt, are strongly attracted to magnets. They are called **ferromagnetic**. Many metals, such as copper and aluminum, are not.

### Electricity and magnetism

A moving charge makes a magnetic field. So a wire carrying **electric current** has a magnetic field circling around it. If the wire is wound into a coil (a **solenoid**), the fields add up and the coil acts like a bar magnet.

An **electromagnet** is a coil of wire with a current, usually wrapped around an iron core. The iron core becomes magnetized and greatly strengthens the field. The big advantage is that you can switch it on and off, and change its strength. Electromagnets are used in cranes that lift scrap iron, doorbells, speakers and electric motors.

You can make an electromagnet **stronger** by:

- increasing the **current** (more batteries in series),
- increasing the **number of turns** of the coil,
- adding an **iron core**.

Reversing the current reverses the poles.

### Electromagnetic induction and generators

The reverse also works. Moving a magnet near a coil of wire (or a wire through a magnetic field) pushes a current through the wire. This is **electromagnetic induction**, and it is how generators make electricity in power plants.

### Series and parallel circuits to build

With one battery, two identical bulbs and wires, you can build two circuits. In **series**, the bulbs are connected one after another in one loop. Both glow dimly, and unscrewing one puts both out. In **parallel**, each bulb is connected directly across the battery with its own loop. Both glow brightly, and unscrewing one leaves the other lit. Work with an adult, use only small batteries (such as AA cells) and small bulbs, and never connect a wire directly from one end of a battery to the other because the wire gets hot.

@example
title: Strengthening an electromagnet
problem: A student has an electromagnet with a nail core and $20$ turns of wire, powered by one cell. Name two changes that would make it pick up more paper clips, and one that would change its poles.
step: More turns of wire strengthen the field, so wind more turns (for example $40$).
step: More current strengthens the field too, so add a second cell in series.
step: Reversing the connections to the battery reverses the current and swaps north and south poles, but does not change the strength.

@mistakes
- Thinking that like poles attract. Like poles repel.
- Believing that all metals are attracted to magnets. Copper and aluminum are not.
- Thinking that an electromagnet keeps its magnetism after the current is switched off. The magnetism nearly disappears.
- Connecting a wire straight across a battery. This is a short circuit and can make the wire and battery hot.

@practice num
prompt: An electromagnet has a coil with $50$ turns. A second electromagnet is identical except that it has $150$ turns. Assuming the strength is proportional to the number of turns, how many times stronger is the second electromagnet?
answer: 3
check: 150 / 50
unit: times
hint: Divide the larger number of turns by the smaller.
hint: $150 \div 50$.
solution: $150 \div 50 = 3$, so the second electromagnet is $3$ times as strong.

@practice ms

choice*: Putting an iron nail inside the coil

@practice num
prompt: Two identical bulbs, each with a resistance of $6.0\ \Omega$, are connected in parallel across a $3.0\ \mathrm{V}$ battery. What is the total current supplied by the battery, in amperes?
answer: 1
check: 2 * (3.0 / 6.0)
tol: 1%
unit: A
hint: Each branch has the full battery voltage. Find the current in one bulb, then add.
hint: One bulb carries $3.0 \div 6.0$ amperes.
solution: Each bulb carries $3.0 \div 6.0 = 0.50\ \mathrm{A}$, so the total current is $2 \times 0.50 = 1.0\ \mathrm{A}$.

@practice self
prompt: You have one battery pack, two identical small bulbs, wires and a switch. Describe how you would build a series circuit and a parallel circuit, predict the brightness of the bulbs in each, and explain what you expect to see when you unscrew one bulb.
solution: Series: connect the battery, the first bulb, the second bulb and the switch in one loop. Each bulb gets about half the voltage, so both are dim. Unscrewing one opens the only loop and both go out. Parallel: connect each bulb with its own pair of wires directly to the battery terminals, so there are two loops. Each bulb gets the full voltage, so both are bright (the same as one bulb alone). Unscrewing one leaves the other lit because its loop is still complete. The battery supplies more current in the parallel circuit and may run down faster.
hint: Count the paths. One path means series; separate paths from the battery to each bulb means parallel.
rubric: Describes a single loop for series and separate branches for parallel.
rubric: Predicts dimmer bulbs in series and full brightness in parallel.
rubric: Predicts that removing a bulb stops both in series but only that one in parallel.
rubric: Mentions a safety precaution, such as small batteries and no direct wire across the battery.

@quiz num

answer: 6
check: 2 * 3
unit: times
