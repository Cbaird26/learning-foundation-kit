@article
A city wants to know how long residents spend commuting, and asks people through a transit phone app. Of the 1,000 residents, 600 drive (average 40 minutes) and 400 bike (average 25 minutes), so the true average is $(600 \cdot 40 + 400 \cdot 25)/1000 = 34$ minutes. But the app reaches 20% of drivers and 80% of bike commuters: 120 drivers and 320 cyclists respond. The survey average is $(120 \cdot 40 + 320 \cdot 25)/440 \approx 29.1$ minutes. The survey is large, careful and wrong, by almost five minutes. More data would not fix it.

### Sampling bias

A sample is **representative** if it reflects the population on what we measure. Several things break this:

- **Selection bias**: who is in the data depends on something related to the outcome (the app reaches cyclists more than drivers).
- **Nonresponse bias**: people who answer differ from those who do not.
- **Survivorship bias**: only the cases that survived appear (analyzing only companies that still exist).
- **Measurement bias**: the instrument itself errs in a systematic way.

If the **selection probability** of each group is known, we can correct with **inverse probability weighting**: weight each response by one over its probability of being selected. Drivers get weight $1/0.2 = 5$ and cyclists $1/0.8 = 1.25$, and the weighted mean is $\dfrac{120 \cdot 5 \cdot 40 + 320 \cdot 1.25 \cdot 25}{120 \cdot 5 + 320 \cdot 1.25} = \dfrac{34{,}000}{1{,}000} = 34$, the true value. The method works only if the probabilities are known or well estimated, and no one in a group has probability zero. For a model, the same issue appears as **dataset shift**: the data you train on differ from the data the model will meet.

### Data leakage

**Data leakage** means that information unavailable at prediction time reaches the model during training or evaluation, so that measured performance is better than real performance. The model looks excellent in testing and fails in use. The main forms:

1. **Target leakage**: a feature is computed from, or affected by, the outcome. To predict whether a patient will be readmitted within 30 days, a column "days until next admission" contains the answer. A feature "refund issued" in a fraud model is recorded after fraud is found.
2. **Train-test contamination**: test rows are in the training data, directly or in disguise: exact duplicates, the same customer in both sets, near-duplicates after cleaning, or several images from one patient.
3. **Preprocessing leakage**: a scaler, imputer or feature selector is fitted on the entire data set before splitting, so statistics of the test rows influence the training. Selecting the 10 features most correlated with the label using all rows, and only then splitting, can make pure noise look predictive.
4. **Temporal leakage**: using the future to predict the past, for example shuffling rows of a time series randomly, so that training rows come after test rows.

### Splitting properly

A **train-test split** reserves test rows that the model never sees until the end. The right split depends on how the model will be used:

- A **random split** is fine when rows are independent. Make it reproducible with a seed.
- A **group split** keeps all rows of one entity (a patient, a user, a house) on the same side, when rows from the same entity are related.
- A **time split** trains on rows before a cutoff and tests on rows after it, when the model will predict the future.
- A **stratified split** keeps class proportions similar in both sets, useful for rare classes.

Do all choices (scaling, features, hyperparameters) with the training rows, or with a separate validation set cut from them, and touch the test rows once. Each time you look at test performance and change something, the test set becomes part of training in effect. A simple check for contamination is to count the key values (customer IDs, text hashes) that appear on both sides of the split; the count should be zero unless overlap is intended.

### Reading results with suspicion

Surprisingly high accuracy is a warning sign. When a model is nearly perfect on a hard problem, ask first whether a feature knows the answer, then whether the test rows were seen. Document how the data were collected, who is missing, and what the split was; week 11 turns this into a datasheet.

@example
title: Estimating the bias of a survey
problem: A population of 1,000 has 500 people in group X (mean 6 hours of screen time) and 500 in group Y (mean 2 hours). A poll reaches 300 people from X and 100 from Y. Compute the true mean, the poll mean, and the bias.
step: The true mean weights the groups by their sizes in the population: $(500 \cdot 6 + 500 \cdot 2)/1000 = 4$ hours.
step: The poll mean weights them by who answered: $(300 \cdot 6 + 100 \cdot 2)/400 = 2000/400 = 5$ hours.
step: The bias is the poll mean minus the true mean: $5 - 4 = 1$ hour. The poll overstates screen time because group X, with higher values, is overrepresented ($75\%$ of the poll against $50\%$ of the population).
step: A correction with the selection probabilities: group X was reached at rate $300/500 = 0.6$ and group Y at $100/500 = 0.2$, giving weights $1/0.6$ and $1/0.2$. The weighted mean is $\dfrac{300 \cdot (1/0.6) \cdot 6 + 100 \cdot 5 \cdot 2}{300/0.6 + 100 \cdot 5} = \dfrac{3000 + 1000}{500 + 500} = 4$ hours, the true mean.
answer: True mean 4 hours, poll mean 5 hours, bias +1 hour; weighting by the inverse selection probabilities recovers 4.

@mistakes
- Fitting a scaler, imputer or feature selector on all the data before the split, which lets test information into training.
- Randomly shuffling a time series and testing on rows that come before the training rows.
- Leaving the same customer, patient or image source on both sides of a split.
- Choosing a model by its test score again and again and then reporting that score as an honest estimate.

```python
def overlap_keys(train, test, key):
    """Sorted distinct key values present in both train and test rows."""
```

```python

```
model:
```python
import random


def train_test_split(rows, test_fraction, seed):
    """Reproducible random split into (train, test)."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    idx = list(range(len(rows)))
    rng.shuffle(idx)
    n_test = int(len(rows) * test_fraction)
    test = [rows[i] for i in idx[:n_test]]
    train = [rows[i] for i in idx[n_test:]]
    return train, test
```
hint: Shuffle a list of indices with a seeded `random.Random`, then slice it into the test part and the rest.
solution: Shuffling indices rather than the rows leaves the input untouched, and a seeded generator makes the result repeatable. The tests check the properties of a good split: right sizes, no row in both sets, no row lost, and the same result for the same seed.

tests:
```python
rows = [{"id": "a", "t": 5}, {"id": "b", "t": 1}, {"id": "c", "t": 3},
        {"id": "d", "t": 7}, {"id": "e", "t": 3}]
tr, te = time_split(rows, "t", 4)
assert [r["id"] for r in tr] == ["b", "c", "e"]
assert [r["id"] for r in te] == ["a", "d"]
tr, te = time_split(rows, "t", 3)
assert [r["id"] for r in tr] == ["b"]
assert [r["id"] for r in te] == ["c", "e", "a", "d"]
assert time_split([], "t", 0) == ([], [])

```
model:
```python
def time_split(rows, time_key, cutoff):
    """Train on rows before the cutoff, test on rows at or after it."""
    ordered = sorted(rows, key=lambda r: r[time_key])
    train = [r for r in ordered if r[time_key] < cutoff]
    test = [r for r in ordered if r[time_key] >= cutoff]
    return train, test
```
hint: Sort by the time value (Python's sort is stable), then separate the rows on either side of the cutoff.
solution: A stable sort orders the rows by time while keeping ties in their original order, and then comparing with the cutoff divides them. A row exactly at the cutoff goes to the test side, which the second test checks, so no training row is later than any test row.
