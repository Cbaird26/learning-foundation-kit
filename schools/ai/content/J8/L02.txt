@article
For most of human history, people were **hunter-gatherers**. Over the **Paleolithic** ("Old Stone") **Age**, small groups moved with the seasons, hunting animals and gathering wild plants. They made tools of stone, bone and wood, used fire, and shared knowledge through spoken language and art.

### The Neolithic Revolution

Beginning roughly 10,000 to 12,000 years ago, people in several parts of the world began to plant crops and keep animals. This change is called the **Neolithic Revolution** or the **agricultural revolution**. It happened in the Fertile Crescent of Southwest Asia (wheat, barley, sheep, goats), and independently in other places, including China (rice and millet) and Mesoamerica (maize). Farmers stayed near their fields, so permanent villages grew.

### Surplus and specialization

Farming can produce a **surplus**: more food than the farmers need. A surplus meant that not everyone had to grow food. People could become **specialists**: potters, weavers, builders, priests, soldiers, record-keepers. Some historians stress that farming also brought new problems, such as crop failure, crowding and the spread of disease.

### What is a civilization?

Historians often use **civilization** for a society with cities, a food surplus, specialized jobs, organized government, social classes, and a system of record-keeping such as writing. Not every scholar likes the word, because it can suggest that other ways of life were inferior. The term describes a pattern of organization, not a ranking of peoples.

### Why rivers?

Four early centers of city life each formed along a major river:

| River valley | Region | Early example |
|---|---|---|
| Tigris and Euphrates | Mesopotamia (today's Iraq) | Sumerian city-states such as Uruk |
| Nile | Egypt | Villages joined into one kingdom |
| Indus | South Asia (today's Pakistan and India) | Harappa and Mohenjo-daro |
| Yellow (Huang He) | China | Early Shang cities |

Rivers helped in several ways. Floods left fertile **silt** that renewed the soil. River water could be carried by **irrigation** canals to fields. Rivers worked as roads for boats and trade, and they supplied fish and drinking water. Managing floods and canals required cooperation, which may have encouraged organized government.

Rivers also brought risks: floods could be destructive, and the rivers sometimes changed course.

### Counting BCE and CE

**BCE** ("before the common era") dates count backward toward year 1; **CE** ("common era") dates count forward. There is no year 0. For two BCE dates, subtract the smaller from the larger: from 2500 BCE to 500 BCE is 2,000 years.

@example
title: From surplus to specialists
problem: In an invented village, each of 40 farming families harvests 9 sacks of grain a year and each family eats 6 sacks. How many sacks of surplus does the village have, and how many families' worth of food does that feed?
step: Surplus per family is the harvest minus what the family eats: $9 - 6 = 3$ sacks.
step: For the whole village multiply by 40 families: $40 \times 3 = 120$ sacks of surplus.
step: One family eats 6 sacks, so $120 \div 6 = 20$ families can be fed from the surplus.
step: This means 20 families' worth of people could do work other than farming, such as pottery or building. That is how specialization becomes possible.

@mistakes
- Thinking farming appeared everywhere at once; it began independently in several regions at different times.
- Treating "civilization" as meaning "better" than other ways of living.
- Counting BCE years by adding instead of subtracting (2500 BCE to 500 BCE is 2,000 years, not 3,000).
- Believing cities grew near rivers only for drinking water; silt, irrigation and transport mattered as much.
