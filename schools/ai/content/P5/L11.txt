@article
Ethical questions in research do not appear only at the end, in a statement written for a checklist. They shape what you study, what data you use, how you report, and what you release. Rules differ among institutions, funders, countries and venues, and they change; this lesson describes common practice and tells you what to look up.

### Dual use and release

Work is **dual use** when it has legitimate benefits and could also be misused. Image generation helps designers and can deceive; a tool that identifies faces helps find missing people and can enable surveillance. Researchers differ on how to weigh benefits against risks and on how much to release, and this course does not settle that. What practice does ask is that you think it through: who could benefit, who could be harmed and how severely, how likely and how easily, what the counterfactual is (could others do it without your work?), and what mitigations are available, such as staged or gated release, documenting limits, withholding the most hazardous details or components, monitoring, or engaging experts and affected communities. Some venues require a statement on broader impact or ethics and may run an ethics review; read the current requirements.

### Data: consent, license, provenance

- **Human participants.** Research that collects data from or about people, or uses identifiable private data, usually requires review by a research ethics board (called by different names in different places) *before* data collection. Whether a project is exempt is generally decided by the board, not by the researcher. Consent, privacy protections and data-handling plans are part of the review.
- **Existing data sets.** Check their license, terms and consent conditions, and whether they have been withdrawn. Public availability is not permission for every use. De-identification can fail, so treat data about people with care.
- **Web-scale data.** Whether and how copyright and consent apply to training on collected data is unsettled and is argued differently across jurisdictions; check your institution's guidance and current law, and document your sources.
- **Documentation.** Gebru and colleagues (datasheets for datasets, 2018 preprint, published 2021) and Mitchell and colleagues (model cards, 2019) give templates for describing a data set's collection, composition and intended uses, and a model's intended use, evaluation and limits.
- **Annotators.** People who label data are research participants or workers whose pay, working conditions and exposure to harmful content matter.

### Environmental cost

Strubell, Ganesh and McCallum (2019) estimated the energy and carbon costs of training and tuning language-processing models. A basic estimate: energy in kilowatt-hours equals devices times power per device (kW) times hours times the data-center overhead factor (PUE, power usage effectiveness); emissions equal energy times the carbon intensity of the electricity (kg of CO2-equivalent per kWh), which varies widely by place and time. Reporting these numbers, including tuning and failed runs, is good practice.

### Integrity, authorship, conflicts

**Research misconduct** usually means fabrication (making up results), falsification (altering results or methods) and plagiarism. Related questionable practices include selecting only favorable seeds or data sets, changing the hypothesis after seeing the results and presenting it as planned, and hiding negative results that bear on a claim. If you find an error in published or submitted work, tell the editors or chairs and correct it.

**Authorship** conventions differ by field and venue, but common principles are that authors made substantial contributions to the work and writing, approve the final version and accept accountability for it; people who did not meet these are acknowledged, not listed; and the order and roles should be agreed early, in writing if possible. **Conflicts of interest**, such as funding from or a financial stake in an organization affected by the work, should be disclosed according to the venue's and institution's rules.

### Impact statements

A good statement is specific and honest: what the work enables and who benefits; foreseeable risks and who might be harmed, including through misuse or through errors and bias; the limits of the evidence (where it was tested, where it was not); mitigations taken or recommended; and what you cannot predict. Avoid both marketing ("will solve poverty") and doom, and avoid empty boilerplate that could be pasted on any paper.

@example
title: Estimating energy and emissions
problem: A project uses 4 devices drawing 0.3 kW each for 100 hours, in a data center with a PUE of 1.1 and an electricity carbon intensity of 0.5 kg CO2e per kWh. Estimate the energy and emissions, and note what the estimate leaves out.
step: Device energy: $4 \times 0.3 \times 100 = 120$ kWh.
step: Apply the overhead factor: $120 \times 1.1 = 132$ kWh.
step: Emissions: $132 \times 0.5 = 66$ kg CO2e.
step: The estimate covers only this run. Add the tuning runs, failed runs and ablations, and note that the carbon intensity of the grid, the hardware's actual power draw and the manufacturing of the devices are uncertain or excluded. Report the assumptions, not just the total.
answer: 132 kWh and about 66 kg CO2e for this run, before counting tuning, failed runs and hardware manufacturing.

@mistakes
- Deciding yourself that a study of people's data needs no ethics review because "the data are anonymous"; the review body decides.
- Treating public availability of data as permission for any use, including redistribution.
- Writing an impact statement of generic sentences that would fit any paper, or only the benefits.
- Dropping an inconvenient seed or data set after seeing the results without reporting it.

@practice self
prompt: Write the ethics and data statement (150 to 220 words) for a project that records short voice clips from 400 volunteer adults to train and evaluate a model that detects whether a speaker has a cold. Address consent and review, privacy, data release, representativeness and limits, the intended use and the possible misuse.
solution: Participants were recruited as volunteers and gave informed consent for research use after reading a description of the study; the protocol was reviewed by our institution's research ethics board before data collection. Recordings are stored on encrypted institutional servers, linked to participants by random identifiers, and the key is held separately; voices can identify people, so we will not release raw audio. We will release derived features only for participants who agreed to sharing, and provide a documented route for qualified researchers to request access under a data-use agreement. Participants may withdraw, and we will remove their data from future releases. The sample is 400 adults recruited locally, with limited variety in accents, ages and recording devices, and the model has not been tested on children or under noisy conditions; we state this in the datasheet and model card. The intended use is research on health screening support, not diagnosis. Possible misuse includes inferring a person's health from their voice without consent, for example by an employer or insurer; we discourage such uses in the license terms and describe the model's error rates so that overreliance is less likely. Annotators were paid fairly for their time.
hint: Walk through consent and review, privacy, release, representativeness, intended use and misuse.
rubric: Addresses consent, ethics review, privacy and a data release plan appropriate for voice data.
rubric: States representativeness limits and the intended use.
rubric: Names a realistic misuse and a concrete mitigation without overclaiming.

@quiz num
prompt: A team labels 5,000 items. Each item is labeled by 3 annotators who each spend 40 seconds on it. The annotators are paid \$18 per hour. What is the total labor cost in dollars?
answer: 3000
tol: 0.001
unit: dollars
check: 5000 * 3 * 40 / 3600 * 18
hint: Count the total seconds of annotation, convert to hours, and multiply by the hourly pay.
solution: There are $5000 \times 3 = 15000$ annotations of 40 seconds, or 600,000 seconds, which is about 166.67 hours. At \$18 per hour the cost is \$3,000. Budgeting this honestly matters for paying annotators fairly.

@quiz self
prompt: You have trained a model that imitates a person's handwriting from five samples. It could help people with injuries to write personal letters, and it could be used to forge signatures. Write a balanced paragraph (120 to 180 words) on what you would consider before deciding how to release it, and name two concrete options with their trade-offs. Do not assert which decision is correct; show your reasoning.
solution: I would first ask who benefits and how: people with injuries and accessibility applications are real, concrete users. The risks are forgery of signatures and documents; severity depends on whether signatures are used as proof in the contexts reachable by the tool, and how likely misuse is depends on how easy it is, which in turn depends on whether others could build the tool without mine. Option one is a public release of code and weights with a clear statement of limits: it maximizes benefit and reproducibility, but lowers the barrier to forgery. Option two is a gated release to vetted researchers and accessibility developers under a use agreement, together with a paper describing the method and a classifier for detecting generated handwriting: it reduces misuse but excludes some legitimate users and may be less reproducible. A third element is to omit components specifically tuned for signatures. I would consult people affected, such as accessibility users and organizations that rely on signatures, and revisit the decision as evidence about misuse emerges.
hint: Weigh benefit, severity, likelihood and counterfactual, then compare a public with a gated release.
rubric: Identifies concrete benefits and risks and weighs severity, likelihood and the counterfactual.
rubric: Names two release options with honest trade-offs and does not claim a settled answer.
rubric: Mentions consulting affected people or revisiting the decision as evidence arrives.
