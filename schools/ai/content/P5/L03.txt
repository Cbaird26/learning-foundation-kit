@article
You have a hypothesis; before you run anything, find out what is known. A related-work search can save months (someone has already answered the question), reshape your question (a close paper handles most of the idea), or reveal the right baselines. It is also where a researcher's honesty is most visible: whom you cite, and how you describe them.

### Searching as a funnel

No single search finds everything. Combine several routes:

- **Keyword search** on scholarly search engines and preprint servers, with synonyms (the same idea often carries several names across subfields).
- **Citation chasing**: read the references of key papers (backward) and look at who cites them (forward). A paper that appears in the references of many of your papers is central.
- **Surveys and tutorials**, which give a map and a vocabulary.
- **Proceedings and workshop lists** of venues in the area, and conversations with people who work on it.

Treat the result as a funnel: many titles, fewer abstracts, a handful of papers read fully. Two numbers describe a search. **Precision** is the fraction of retrieved papers that are relevant; **recall** is the fraction of all relevant papers that were retrieved. If 60 papers are retrieved, 40 are relevant, and there are 50 relevant papers in all, precision is $40/60$ and recall is $40/50 = 0.8$. You never know the true total, but you can estimate recall by checking whether a set of papers you already know to be relevant shows up.

### Organizing what you find

Build a small matrix with papers as rows and properties as columns: the problem setting, the assumptions, the data, the baselines used, the main result, the limitations. Gaps in the matrix are candidate contributions; a column that all papers fill the same way shows a shared assumption you might question. Record full citation details at once, with a reference manager, to avoid hunting later.

### Writing related work

Related work is an argument, not a catalogue. Group papers by idea ("methods that prune after training", "methods that learn sparse masks from the start") rather than by date or author. For each group say what it achieves and what it leaves open, and end with the sentence that places your work: what you do that none of them does, stated precisely and without disparaging the others. Credit the original source of an idea, not only the later paper that made it popular, and cite concurrent work you know about, stating how it relates.

**Positioning your contribution.** Contributions come in kinds: a new method, an empirical finding, an analysis, a resource such as a data set or tool, a theoretical result, or a replication. State yours in a short list in the introduction. Be careful with "first": claiming to be first needs a thorough search, and a precise claim such as "to our knowledge, the first to evaluate X under condition Y" is both safer and more informative.

### Citation and integrity

- **Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words, ideas or results as your own. Paraphrasing without credit is still plagiarism; copying with a citation but without quotation marks is not acceptable either.
- **Text recycling or self-plagiarism**: reusing your own previously published text or figures without citing or disclosing it. Norms differ by field and venue (some allow limited reuse of methods descriptions with citation), so check the policy of the venue and your publisher.
- **Dual submission**: submitting substantially the same work to more than one venue for review at the same time. Most venues prohibit it, but definitions differ (for example, how workshop or preprint versions count), so read the current call for papers and policies, and ask the organizers if unclear.
- **Citing what you did not read**, or citing a result for a claim it does not make, is a quiet but real error. Read what you cite.

Authorship, credit for data, and rules on a conflict of interest are also set by institutions and venues. Common practice is that authors have contributed substantially and accept responsibility for the work; check the rules that apply to you.

@example
title: Measuring a literature search
problem: A search on a topic returns 240 titles. You keep 25% after reading titles, 40% of those after reading abstracts, and 50% of those after reading the full paper. Then, to estimate recall, you check a list of 20 papers you already knew were relevant: 14 appear among the 240 titles. How many papers do you read fully and keep, and what is the recall estimate?
step: Title screen: $240 \times 0.25 = 60$ papers remain.
step: Abstract screen: $60 \times 0.40 = 24$ papers remain.
step: Full read: $24 \times 0.50 = 12$ papers are kept.
step: Estimate recall from the known list: 14 of 20 known relevant papers were found, so recall is about $14/20 = 0.70$.
step: Interpret: about 30% of relevant work was missed by this query set. Add synonyms and citation chasing from the 12 kept papers, then check the known list again.
answer: 12 papers kept; estimated recall 0.70, so more routes are needed.

@mistakes
- Searching once with one set of keywords: the same idea has different names, so recall stays low without citation chasing.
- Describing prior work as a list of summaries without saying how yours differs, which leaves the reader to guess your contribution.
- Citing only the later, popular paper for an idea and omitting its source.
- Treating your own old text as free to reuse: undisclosed reuse can violate a venue's policy even if you wrote it.

@practice self
prompt: Write one related-work paragraph (150 to 200 words) for a paper whose contribution is "a method that learns a sparse mask for each layer during training and matches dense accuracy at 10% of the weights." Prior work you found, described generically: group A prunes weights after training and then fine-tunes; group B selects a sparse mask at initialization and trains it fixed; group C trains with a mask that changes during training but with a fixed sparsity per layer chosen by hand; group D compresses by distillation into a smaller dense model. Write as if you cited each group with a bracketed placeholder such as [A].
solution: Methods for reducing model size fall into four groups. Pruning after training removes low-magnitude weights from a converged dense network and fine-tunes the result [A]; it reaches high sparsity but still needs a full dense training run. Pruning at initialization chooses a fixed sparse mask before training, avoiding that cost [B], though the mask cannot adapt to what the data reveal. Dynamic sparse training lets the mask change during training [C] and trains sparse throughout, but the per-layer sparsity levels are set by hand, which can be suboptimal and requires tuning. Distillation instead trains a smaller dense model to imitate a large one [D]; it changes the architecture, not the sparsity pattern, and needs the large model first. Our method belongs to the dynamic family but learns the per-layer sparsity jointly with the weights, so it removes the hand-set levels of [C] while keeping the training-time savings absent in [A] and [D]. We compare against representatives of each group in Section 5.
rubric: Groups the work by idea and describes what each group achieves and what it leaves open, without disparaging.
rubric: Ends by stating precisely how the contribution differs from the closest group.
rubric: Uses citation placeholders for each group and does not make unsupported claims of priority.
