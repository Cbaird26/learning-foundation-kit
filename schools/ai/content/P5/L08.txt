@article
A benchmark is a shared test: a data set, a metric and a protocol that let many groups compare methods on equal terms. ImageNet (Deng and colleagues, 2009) and its classification challenge are the classic case: a common task that focused the field. Benchmarks create progress; they also create their own pathologies.

### What can go wrong

**Test-set reuse.** If thousands of papers evaluate on the same test set, and each chooses what to publish partly by its score, the community acts as one very long adaptive search. Sculley and colleagues (2018) discuss the winner's curse and the need for empirical rigor: the best of many tried options is selected partly for lucky noise. Recht and colleagues (2019) tested this worry directly by building new test sets for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet, following the original collection processes as closely as they could. A wide range of models scored noticeably lower on the new sets, while their relative ordering was largely preserved, which suggests that sensitivity to small shifts in the data distribution plays a part, in addition to any adaptive overfitting.

**Claims to generality.** Raji and colleagues (2021) argue that benchmarks are often presented as measuring general abilities ("understanding", "vision") when they measure performance on one data set under one protocol. A claim should be scoped to what the benchmark covers.

**Shortcuts and artifacts.** A model can score well using patterns that are incidental to the task, such as background texture, or phrases correlated with labels, and fail when they change.

**Contamination.** If test items (or near copies) are in a model's training data, which can happen with large corpora collected from the web, scores overstate ability. This is leakage (Lesson 7) at corpus scale.

**Label noise and coverage.** Test labels have errors, and a data set covers some populations, languages and conditions and not others. A ceiling below 100% may reflect noisy labels, not model weakness.

**Metric choice.** Accuracy, F1, calibration and robustness answer different questions. A single number hides trade-offs.

### How noisy is a test score?

A model's measured accuracy $\hat{p}$ on $n$ independent test items has standard error $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ and an approximate 95% interval of $\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ (the usual normal approximation, adequate when $np$ and $n(1-p)$ are not small). This covers only sampling of test items. Comparing two models on the *same* items is a paired problem, and tests such as McNemar's, studied by Dietterich (1998), use only the items on which the models disagree.

### Comparing rankings

To see whether two evaluations order models similarly, use a rank correlation. **Spearman's** $\rho$ for $m$ models with no ties is
$$ \rho = 1 - \frac{6\sum d_i^2}{m(m^2-1)}, $$
where $d_i$ is the difference between model $i$'s ranks. It is 1 for identical orderings and $-1$ for reversed ones.

### Documentation

Gebru and colleagues proposed *datasheets for datasets* (2018 preprint, published 2021): structured documentation of a data set's motivation, composition, collection, intended uses and limits. Mitchell and colleagues (2019) proposed *model cards* for models. When you introduce a benchmark, write the datasheet; when you use one, read it.

### Auditing a paper

This week's deliverable is an audit of a paper with a reproducibility checklist. For each item (claims, code, data and licenses, hyperparameters and search ranges, seeds and variability, compute, splits and leakage, baseline tuning), mark yes, partly or no, quote or point to the evidence, and say what the reader could and could not reproduce. Venues have their own checklists, which change; use the one your venue currently requires and treat this one as practice.

@example
title: Two models on one benchmark, and two rankings
problem: A model scores 85% on a test set of 2,000 independent items. What is its 95% interval? A second model scores 86% on the same set; can you tell them apart from these numbers alone? Separately, five models are ranked 1 to 5 on an original test set and 2, 1, 3, 5, 4 on a newly collected one; what is Spearman's $\rho$?
step: Standard error: $\sqrt{0.85 \times 0.15 / 2000} = \sqrt{0.00006375} \approx 0.00798$, about 0.80 points.
step: A 95% interval is $85\% \pm 1.96 \times 0.80 \approx 85\% \pm 1.56$ points, that is about $[83.4\%,\ 86.6\%]$.
step: The second model's 86% lies inside that interval, so from the aggregate numbers alone you cannot tell the models apart; a paired comparison on the same items would be more sensitive but still needs the item-level results.
step: Rank differences: $(1-2, 2-1, 3-3, 4-5, 5-4) = (-1, 1, 0, -1, 1)$, so $\sum d_i^2 = 4$.
step: $\rho = 1 - \frac{6 \times 4}{5 \times 24} = 1 - 0.2 = 0.8$. The two orderings largely agree even if the absolute scores differ.
answer: 95% interval about $85\% \pm 1.56$ points, so 85% and 86% on 2,000 items are not separable by these numbers alone; $\rho = 0.8$.

@mistakes
- Reporting a one-point gain on a few thousand test items as a clear win without noting the sampling noise of about one point.
- Concluding from a rank correlation that two evaluations measure the same thing: a high $\rho$ shows similar ordering, not equal difficulty or construct.
- Claiming a benchmark measures a broad ability ("reasoning", "vision") when it measures one data set under one protocol.
- Using a benchmark without reading its documentation, including how labels were produced and what it leaves out.

@practice num
prompt: A classifier scores 70% on 500 independent test items. Using the normal approximation, what is the half-width of a 95% interval ($1.96 \times$ standard error) in percentage points? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 4.02
tol: 0.01
unit: points
check: 1.96 * (0.7 * 0.3 / 500) ** 0.5 * 100
hint: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.7 \times 0.3 / 500}$; multiply by 1.96 and by 100.
solution: $0.7 \times 0.3 / 500 = 0.00042$ and its square root is about 0.0205. Then $1.96 \times 0.0205 \approx 0.0402$, that is 4.02 points. A difference of two points between two models on this test set is well within the noise.

@practice mc
prompt: A language model scores unusually high on a benchmark. The benchmark's items were published on the web years ago, and the model's training corpus was collected from the web. Which problem should you check first?
choice*: Contamination: the test items or near copies may appear in the training corpus.
choice: Underfitting: the model is too small for the benchmark.
choice: Label smoothing: the benchmark's labels are probably too soft.
choice: Covariate shift between the benchmark and the training corpus, which would lower the score.
hint: The facts given suggest that the test items could have been seen in training.
solution: Test items published on the web may be present in a web-scale corpus, so a high score might reflect memorization. This is the leakage of Lesson 7 at corpus scale. Underfitting and a shift between distributions would tend to lower, not raise, the score.

@practice num
prompt: Six models are ranked 1 to 6 on benchmark X. On benchmark Y their ranks are 3, 1, 2, 4, 6, 5 (in the same model order). Compute Spearman's $\rho = 1 - 6\sum d_i^2 / (m(m^2-1))$ for these rankings. Give your answer to three decimal places.
answer: 0.771
tol: 0.001
check: 1 - 6 * sum((a - b) ** 2 for a, b in zip([1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6], [3, 1, 2, 4, 6, 5])) / (6 * 35)
hint: The rank differences are $-2, 1, 1, 0, -1, 1$; square and add them.
solution: The squared differences are $4, 1, 1, 0, 1, 1$, summing to 8. Then $\rho = 1 - 6 \times 8/(6 \times 35) = 1 - 48/210 \approx 0.771$.

@practice ms
prompt: A paper says its benchmark result shows that the model "understands" a domain. Which of these are legitimate reasons to scope that claim more narrowly? Select all that apply.
choice*: The benchmark covers one data set under one protocol, so the result describes performance on that data set.
choice*: Models can sometimes score well using shortcuts that are incidental to the task.
choice*: The test labels may contain errors, and the test set may omit populations or conditions that matter.

choice: A single score has no sampling uncertainty because the test set is fixed.

solution: A benchmark result is evidence about a defined data set and protocol, can be inflated by shortcuts, and is limited by label quality and coverage. It is not worthless, and the score of a finite test set does have sampling uncertainty.

@practice self
prompt: Audit the following described paper with this checklist, marking each item yes, partly or no with a one-line reason, and then summarize in two or three sentences what a reader could and could not reproduce. Checklist: (1) claims match evidence; (2) code released; (3) data available with license; (4) hyperparameters and search ranges; (5) seeds and variability; (6) compute reported; (7) splits and leakage addressed; (8) baselines tuned equally. Paper: "We propose a fine-tuning method for a text classifier and report accuracy on a public benchmark. Our method improves accuracy over the baseline by 1.1 points on average. Code will be released upon acceptance. The data set is public, with the license cited. We report the learning rate and batch size we used; the search range is not given. We ran the method three times and report the best run; the baseline numbers are copied from another paper. The paper does not state the compute used. The data were split using the benchmark's published split; we did not check for overlap with the pretraining corpus of the base model. The abstract says the method 'consistently improves text classification'."
solution: (1) No: "consistently improves text classification" rests on one benchmark and a 1.1-point average from best-of-three runs. (2) No: code is promised only after acceptance, so nothing can be checked now. (3) Yes: the data set is public with its license cited. (4) Partly: the learning rate and batch size are given but the search range is not, so the effort behind them is unknown. (5) No: three runs, with the best reported, show no variability and bias the number upward. (6) No: compute is not stated. (7) Partly: the published split is used, but possible overlap between the benchmark and the base model's pretraining corpus was not examined. (8) No: baseline numbers are copied from another paper, so the protocols and tuning may differ. Summary: a reader can obtain the data and the split and could try to reimplement the method from its description, but cannot verify the code, the tuning or the baseline comparison, and the reported gain (best of three runs against copied baseline numbers) cannot be treated as a reliable estimate of improvement. I would ask for released code, five or more seeds with intervals, baselines rerun under the same protocol, and a contamination check.
hint: Quote the sentence that bears on each item, and mark partly when something is stated but incomplete.
rubric: Marks each of the eight items correctly with a reason tied to the text.
rubric: Notes best-of-three reporting as bias and copied baselines as an unequal comparison.
rubric: Gives a summary of what is and is not reproducible and requests specific fixes.

@quiz num
prompt: A hypothetical model scores 95.0% on an original test set and 88.0% on a freshly collected test set of the same task. By what percent does the number of errors rise from the original to the new test set?
answer: 140
tol: 0.01
unit: %
check: ((100 - 88.0) / (100 - 95.0) - 1) * 100
hint: Compare the error rates 5.0% and 12.0%, not the accuracies.
solution: The error rate rises from 5.0% to 12.0%, a factor of 2.4, which is a 140% increase in errors even though accuracy fell by only 7 points. Error ratios often express a shift more clearly than accuracy differences.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper's headline result is that its model "outperforms all previous methods on Benchmark Z by 0.7 points." Benchmark Z has 1,500 test items, the paper reports one run per method, and it tried 40 model variants, evaluating each on the test set. Write a short referee-style paragraph (100 to 150 words) on what this evidence can and cannot support, using the ideas of sampling noise, winner's curse and test-set reuse.
hint: Compute roughly the test-set noise for 1,500 items, then think about what selecting the best of 40 does.
solution: On 1,500 items, an accuracy near 85% has a standard error of about 0.9 points, so a 0.7-point gap is smaller than the sampling noise of either score, before counting training variance from the single run. Moreover, the model reported is presumably the best of 40 variants evaluated on the test set. Selecting a maximum from many noisy measurements favors lucky noise (the winner's curse; Sculley and colleagues, 2018), so the 0.7-point advantage is likely overstated even if the method is real. Using the test set for choosing among variants also removes its role as an independent measurement. The claim "outperforms all previous methods" is not supported. I would ask for variant selection on validation data, a single final test evaluation, several seeds with paired differences and intervals, and a statement scoped to this benchmark.
rubric: Estimates test-set sampling noise and compares it with the 0.7-point gap.
rubric: Explains the winner's curse from choosing the best of 40 variants on the test set.
rubric: Requests validation-based selection, multiple seeds with intervals and a scoped claim.
