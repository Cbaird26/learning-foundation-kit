@article
Peer review is how the research community checks work before it counts as part of the record. It is performed by volunteers, under time pressure, on work they did not choose. Understanding it from both sides, as reviewer and as author, makes you better at each.

### How review works

The details vary by venue, field and year, so read the current policies of any venue you submit to or review for. A common outline for conference-style review: authors submit; chairs assign each paper to several reviewers (by area, by the reviewers' stated interests, by bidding or by matching software); each reviewer writes a report; authors may respond in a rebuttal period; reviewers discuss; an area chair or editor writes a recommendation; the program committee decides. Journals typically allow several rounds of revision. Review can be single-blind (reviewers know the authors), **double-blind** (neither side knows the other) or open, with different rules about posting preprints during review.

### Norms for reviewers

- **Confidentiality.** A manuscript under review is confidential: do not share it, discuss it outside the permitted process, or use its unpublished ideas. Many venues also set rules about whether you may give the text to external software tools, including AI tools; check the current policy before you do.
- **Conflicts of interest.** Declare a conflict when you have a close relationship with an author, such as a recent collaboration, shared institution, advisor or student relationship, or financial interest; the exact definition is the venue's. If you realize you have one after assignment, tell the editor.
- **Anonymity.** In a double-blind process do not try to discover the authors. If you recognize them, say so to the chair and let them decide.
- **Fairness.** Judge the work, not the authors, the topic's fashion or whether it cites you.

### Writing a good report

A good report has: a short **summary** in your own words that shows you understood the paper's claims; **strengths**, stated honestly; **weaknesses**, each specific and tied to evidence ("the baseline in Table 2 uses default settings while the method's learning rate is searched over 20 values"); **questions** whose answers could change your view; **suggestions** for improvement; and a **recommendation** consistent with the text. Separate the problems that threaten the main claim from minor ones such as typos. Criticize the work, not the people, and say what would convince you. Do not demand experiments that are unrelated to the claims, and do not ask for citations of your own work unless they are really relevant. Reviewers differ in standards, and a score on a numerical scale means different things to different reviewers; the scale's meaning is defined by each venue.

### Summarizing scores

Given reviewer scores, the **mean** summarizes the level, the **median** resists an outlier, and the sample **standard deviation** measures disagreement. A paper scored 6, 5, 3, 7 and 4 has mean 5, median 5 and standard deviation about 1.58: a split that discussion should address, not average away. Treat review outcomes as noisy evidence about quality; strong papers are sometimes rejected and weak ones accepted, so a rejection is information about fit and clarity as much as a verdict.

### Responding to reviews

An effective rebuttal or response letter: thanks the reviewers briefly; answers each substantive point in turn, quoting the point; gives new evidence where it exists (a new experiment, a pointer to the paragraph that answers the question); concedes valid criticisms and says what you will change; and does not attack the reviewer. Prioritize the points that threaten the main claim, within the venue's length limit. For a journal revision, include a point-by-point list of changes. After rejection, read the reviews after a pause, extract what is fair, improve the paper, and resubmit to a suitable venue, remembering that rules on resubmission and on prior reviews differ.

### Why you should review

Reviewing teaches you what makes evidence convincing faster than anything else, and the field depends on it. Accept only the number of reviews you can do well and on time.

@example
title: Summarizing reviews and planning a response
problem: Four reviewers give scores 8, 6, 3 and 6 (scale defined by the venue; higher is better). Report the mean, median and sample standard deviation. The reviewer with the 3 writes that the baselines were tuned less than the method. Plan the response.
step: Mean: $(8 + 6 + 3 + 6)/4 = 5.75$. Median: the sorted scores are 3, 6, 6, 8, so the median is 6.
step: Sample standard deviation: the deviations from 5.75 are $2.25, 0.25, -2.75, 0.25$, with squares summing to $12.75$; so $s = \sqrt{12.75/3} \approx 2.06$.
step: Interpret: three of the four scores are close (median 6) and one is far lower, so the mean understates agreement; the disagreement comes from one specific concern.
step: Plan: confirm whether the criticism is correct. If baselines were tuned less, run them with the same grid and report the result; if they were tuned equally, point to the paragraph in the paper that says so and add the grid to the main text. Lead the response with this point.
answer: Mean 5.75, median 6, standard deviation about 2.06; the response should address the baseline-tuning concern first, with new results or a pointer.

@mistakes
- Reviewing the paper you wish had been written instead of the paper that was submitted.
- Vague criticism ("not novel", "weak experiments") without evidence or a pointer to what would fix it.
- Using a manuscript's unpublished ideas, or sharing it, which breaks confidentiality.
- A rebuttal that argues with tone, answers only the easy points, or promises experiments it will not run.

@practice self
prompt: Write the weaknesses-and-recommendation part of a referee report (150 to 220 words) on this short paper. "The paper proposes TinyTune, a method that fine-tunes only the bias terms of a pretrained network. It evaluates on 4 text classification data sets with a pretrained model, reports test accuracy of TinyTune and full fine-tuning (one run each), and says that TinyTune 'matches full fine-tuning at 0.1% of the trainable parameters'. TinyTune's results: 88.1, 91.0, 84.7, 79.2. Full fine-tuning: 88.6, 91.3, 85.5, 80.4. The learning rate for TinyTune was chosen from 5 values on the test sets; full fine-tuning used the default. No code is provided. A related method that trains small added modules is not discussed."
solution: The central claim, that TinyTune "matches" full fine-tuning, is not supported by the evidence as presented. TinyTune is lower on all four data sets (by 0.5, 0.3, 0.8 and 1.2 points), and with one run each the paper cannot say whether these gaps are within noise; "matches" should be replaced by a statement with intervals. More seriously, TinyTune's learning rate was selected from five values on the test sets, while full fine-tuning used a default, so the comparison is unequal and the test numbers are optimistic for TinyTune. Parameter-efficient fine-tuning with small added modules is a close alternative that is not discussed or compared, which weakens the positioning. No code is provided, so none of this can be checked. I ask for validation-based tuning with equal budgets, at least five seeds with paired differences and intervals, a comparison with the module-based alternative, and released code. The idea of tuning few parameters is worthwhile and the efficiency is interesting; if the claim is revised to match the evidence, this could be a useful contribution. Recommendation: major revision, or reject in the current form if revision is not possible at this venue.
hint: Check the claim against the four numbers, then list the design problems from most to least serious.
rubric: Tests the claim "matches" against the supplied numbers and notes that all four are lower and based on single runs.
rubric: Identifies test-set tuning with unequal baseline tuning, the missing related comparison and the missing code.
rubric: Makes specific, fair requests and gives a recommendation consistent with the text.
