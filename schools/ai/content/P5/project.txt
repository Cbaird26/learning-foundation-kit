title: A six-page research proposal, with a critique of your own proposal
overview: You will write a six-page proposal for a machine learning question that you could study with modest compute, containing related work, a falsifiable hypothesis, an experimental plan, a compute and timeline estimate, a reproducibility plan and an ethics statement. Then you will switch sides and write a referee-style critique of your own proposal, and revise it.
goal: Turn a topic into a specific, falsifiable hypothesis with a design that could show it wrong.
goal: Plan baselines, ablations, seeds and statistics, and estimate compute and time honestly.
goal: Plan for reproducibility and ethics, and calibrate every claim to the evidence you could obtain.
goal: Practice reviewing by critiquing your own work as a referee would.
step: Choose a question you could study with modest compute (for example, whether a regularizer, an augmentation, a data-selection rule or an evaluation protocol changes a measurable outcome on a small model and a public data set). Write the question, the hypothesis with a numeric threshold fixed in advance, and one result that would count against it (Lessons 2 and 4).
step: Search the literature as a funnel (Lesson 3): record how many titles you screened and kept and your estimate of recall, read the kept papers with the three passes (Lesson 1), and write a related-work section of 250 to 400 words that groups work by idea and states exactly how your proposal differs.
step: Write the experimental plan: data sets and splits, baselines (a simple strong one and the best relevant published one), a parameter-, compute- or data-matched control, ablations (one-at-a-time, with a reason if you also test combinations), the metric, the tuning protocol and equal budgets, the number of seeds with a justification from a pilot, and the statistical analysis (paired differences, intervals, effect size, a correction if you make several comparisons) (Lessons 4 and 5).
step: Estimate compute and time (Lesson 6): count runs, the cost per run, device-hours with a stated throughput and utilization or a measured time per run, tuning and failed-run allowance, a reserve of at least a quarter, and a weekly timeline over 12 weeks. If your plan involves a model with $N$ parameters trained on $D$ tokens, also give the approximation $6ND$ and label it an approximation. Write by hand, without libraries, the computations of your statistics section: a mean, sample standard deviation, standard error, t interval and a paired difference on the pilot data in the material below, or on your own pilot.
step: Write the reproducibility plan (Lesson 7): code, environment, configurations and seeds, data and licenses, evaluation scripts, what you will release and what you cannot. Include a short audit of your plan against a checklist of your choice (for example the current checklist of a venue you might submit to).
step: Write the ethics and impact statement (Lesson 11): data provenance and consent, whether ethics review applies at your institution, dual use, environmental cost estimated from your compute plan, and limits of the evidence.
step: Assemble the six pages (title and abstract, introduction, related work, hypothesis and plan, compute and timeline, reproducibility, ethics) with figures or tables where they help, and calibrate every claim (Lesson 9).
step: Write a referee-style critique of your own proposal (Lesson 10): summary, strengths, ranked weaknesses, questions and a recommendation. Then revise the proposal and write a short response saying what you changed.

@review
### Week by week

1. **Reading papers.** Three passes (Keshav, 2007): bird's-eye view, content without details, virtual re-implementation. Extract the claim, evidence, assumptions and what would falsify it.
2. **Research questions.** A specific, falsifiable hypothesis with a threshold fixed in advance; feasibility in runs, device-hours and seeds, $n \ge (\sigma/\mathrm{SE}^\ast)^2$.
3. **Related work.** Search as a funnel; precision and recall; group by idea; credit sources; plagiarism, text recycling and dual submission depend on venue policy.
4. **Design.** Equal tuning for baselines, ablations ($k+1$ one-at-a-time, $2^k$ factorial), controls (parameter-, compute- and data-matched), splits, chance wins ($1-0.5^k$, $1/k$).
5. **Statistics.** Mean, sample standard deviation, $\mathrm{SE}=s/\sqrt{n}$, t interval, bootstrap percentile interval, IQM, paired $t=\bar d/(s_d/\sqrt n)$, effect size, Bonferroni ($0.05/m$) and Benjamini-Hochberg.
6. **Compute.** $C\approx 6ND$ (approximation), device-hours $=C/(Pu\cdot 3600)$, expected maximum over a tuning budget, honest accounting including tuning and failures.
7. **Reproducibility.** Code, environment, configurations and seeds, data and licenses; leakage $a=f\,a_{\text{dup}}+(1-f)a_{\text{clean}}$; checklists.
8. **Benchmarks.** Test-set noise $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$, test-set reuse, contamination, scope of claims; Spearman's $\rho$; datasheets and model cards.
9. **Writing.** Claims first; figures that do not mislead; intervals for differences; wording calibrated to evidence.
10. **Peer review.** Confidentiality, conflicts, anonymity; specific reports; score summaries; rebuttals with evidence.
11. **Ethics.** Dual use, consent and review, licenses, honest reporting, authorship, impact statements, energy and emissions.
12. **Presenting and careers.** One message, time budget, honest answers; several career paths without rankings.

### Self-check

- Can I state my hypothesis, its threshold and a result that would refute it?
- Did every method get the same tuning budget, and did I touch the test set once?
- Do I know what each $\pm$ means, and can I compute an interval by hand?
- Have I counted all runs, including tuning, failures and a reserve?
- Could someone rerun my work from my release, and what is withheld and why?
- Do my claims match my intervals?

### Common errors

Tuning on the test set; reporting the best seed; using $n$ instead of $n-1$; comparing paired runs as if independent; treating $p<0.05$ as importance; counting only the final run; using peak instead of achieved throughput; leakage through preprocessing before splitting; claiming generality from one benchmark; rules and policies assumed instead of checked.

@exam num
prompt: A pilot gives a standard deviation of 0.75 points across seeds. You want a standard error of at most 0.25 points, so you use the smallest whole number of seeds that reaches it. You will compare 3 methods on 4 data sets with that many seeds each, and each run takes 1.5 device-hours. How many device-hours does the final comparison take, ignoring tuning?
answer: 162
tol: 0.001
unit: device-hours
check: ((0.75 / 0.25) ** 2) * 3 * 4 * 1.5
hint: First find the number of seeds with $n \ge (\sigma/\mathrm{SE}^\ast)^2$, then count runs.
solution: $n = (0.75/0.25)^2 = 9$ seeds. The runs are $9 \times 3 \times 4 = 108$, and $108 \times 1.5 = 162$ device-hours.

@exam ms
prompt: A paper claims that its new method beats a baseline on a benchmark. Which of these would a careful referee flag as threatening that claim? Select all that apply.
choice*: The new method's hyperparameters were chosen using test-set accuracy, while the baseline used defaults.
choice*: The paper reports the best of three runs for each method and no variability.
choice*: Some test items also appear in the data on which the models were trained.
choice: The authors released their code, configurations and per-seed results.
choice: The paper reports a paired difference with a 95% interval over ten shared seeds.
hint: Look for each way that the comparison could be biased or noisy.
solution: Test-set tuning with unequal baseline tuning, best-of-n reporting with no variability, and leakage between test and training data each bias or undermine the comparison. Releasing code and reporting a paired interval over shared seeds are good practices.

@exam self
prompt: Timed exam answer (about 40 minutes). A paper reports that a new regularizer improves a 9-layer image classifier on two data sets: test accuracy 90.4 versus 89.7 and 71.9 versus 71.0, one run per cell, with the regularizer strength chosen from 15 values on the test sets and the baseline at defaults. The authors' abstract says the regularizer "robustly improves generalization of deep networks", and they say the code is available on request. (a) Write a referee report paragraph (150 to 200 words) with the main threats to the claim and a recommendation. (b) Write an experimental plan (200 to 250 words) that would test the claim properly: hypothesis with threshold, baselines and controls, tuning and splits, seeds and statistics, compute estimate in runs, and the result that would count against it. (c) State in two sentences which rules you would check before publishing your own replication.
hint: Treat it as three tasks: critique first (claim, evidence, design flaws), then the design that fixes them, then the rules you must check.
solution: (a) The central claim, that the regularizer robustly improves generalization of deep networks, is not supported. The evidence is two single-run comparisons with gaps of 0.7 and 0.9 points; with one run per cell the paper cannot distinguish these from seed variation, and the word "robustly" is not supported by two data sets and one architecture. The regularizer's strength was chosen on the test sets from 15 values while the baseline used defaults, so the comparison is unequal and the test numbers are optimistically biased by selection. Code only "on request" prevents verification. I would ask for tuning of both methods on validation data with equal budgets, at least five paired seeds with intervals, a second architecture, and released code. The idea may well be useful; if the claim is scoped to the tested settings, I would recommend major revision. (b) Hypothesis: with strength chosen on validation data, the regularizer raises mean test accuracy by at least 0.5 points over a baseline with weight decay and early stopping, on each of two data sets, averaged over 10 paired seeds. Baselines and controls: the default baseline, a baseline tuned over the same number of settings (15) for its weight decay, and a control with a different regularizer of matched training cost. Tuning on a validation split with equal budgets; the test set is used once at the end. Statistics: mean and standard deviation per method, paired difference with a 95% t interval and effect size, Benjamini-Hochberg correction across the two data sets. Compute: 3 conditions x 2 data sets x 10 seeds = 60 final runs, plus tuning 3 x 2 x 15 = 90 runs, so 150 runs, plus a reserve of at least 25%. Falsifying result: an interval for the paired gain that includes values below 0.5 on either data set, or a gain that vanishes against the tuned baseline. (c) I would check the current policies of the venue or repository on replication and on posting preprints, my institution's rules on data and code release, and the license terms of the data sets and of the original code if I reuse it. I would also check how the original authors should be credited and contacted.
rubric: Referee paragraph identifies single runs, unequal tuning with test-set selection and the overreaching claim, and gives a recommendation consistent with the text.
rubric: The plan has a falsifiable threshold, tuned and matched baselines, validation-based selection, paired seeds with intervals and a multiple-comparison correction, and counts runs correctly.
rubric: Names the result that would count against the hypothesis and the rules to check before publishing, without assuming them.
