@article
Research that nobody understands changes nothing. Talks and posters are how most of your colleagues will first encounter your work, and how they will decide whether to read the paper. A short talk is mostly an exercise in deciding what to leave out.

### One message

Before you make a slide, write one sentence that you want the audience to remember: "Tuning the baseline equally erases the reported gain" or "A learned mask reaches dense accuracy with a tenth of the weights." Everything in the talk serves that sentence. If a slide does not, cut it, even if it took a week to produce.

### A structure for ten minutes

A reliable shape for a ten-minute talk to a mixed audience, with rough shares of the time:

1. **Problem and why it matters** (about 15%): a concrete example the audience can picture.
2. **Idea and method** (about 30%): the intuition first, then one clear diagram or equation.
3. **Evidence** (about 35%): one or two key results, each with uncertainty; say how many seeds and what the baselines were.
4. **Limits** (about 10%): what you did not show, and where the method failed.
5. **Takeaway** (about 10%): the one sentence again, and what comes next.

### Slides, speaking and timing

Use about one slide per minute to ninety seconds, one idea per slide, with large text and figures. A slide full of text is read instead of heard. A common speaking pace is 130 to 150 words per minute, so a nine-minute talk is about 1,200 words; many speakers plan less than that and leave a buffer. Rehearse aloud with a timer, at least three times, and once for a colleague who has not seen the work. For a recorded talk, as in this week's deliverable, check the audio first, keep the camera and slides legible, and speak to a person in your mind, not to the screen. Cite the work you build on on the slide where you use it.

### Questions

Listen to the whole question; repeat or rephrase it so everyone hears it; answer what was asked, briefly. If you do not know, say so and say how you would find out; if a question reveals a flaw, thank the person and say what you will check. Do not bluff, and do not take hostility personally: a good question is a gift and a hard one is usually a sign of interest. Credit coauthors and collaborators by name.

### Posters and written summaries

A poster is a talk that people walk up to: a title that states the finding, three to five sections, large figures, and a short pitch of two minutes you can adapt to the viewer. For a short written summary (a blog post, an abstract), the same single message and the same honesty about uncertainty apply.

### Research careers

Research in machine learning happens in several settings, and people move between them. Describing them generally and without ranking:

- **Academic research**: doctoral study, often followed by postdoctoral positions and faculty positions, with a mix of research, teaching, advising and fundraising.
- **Industry research and applied research**: laboratories and teams in companies that publish, build prototypes or develop methods for products; the balance between open publication and confidentiality varies by organization.
- **Research engineering and applied machine learning**: building the systems, experiments and tools on which research depends, or applying methods to a domain. These roles often combine strong engineering with research judgment.
- **Government, nonprofit and independent research**, and **interdisciplinary work**, for example in medicine, science or policy, where the question comes from the domain.

Entry requirements differ by path, country, employer and year; a doctorate is common for some roles and not required for others. The job market changes, so check current information from the programs and employers you are considering, and do not rely on anyone's claims, including those in this course, about salaries, rankings or outcomes. Funding, visa and employment rules also differ and change.

### Exploring wisely

Choose by the daily work you would enjoy: reading, writing, programming, experiments, teaching, working with collaborators. Skills transfer: formulating testable questions, statistics, clear writing, reproducible software practice and collaboration serve every path. Good mentors matter more than prestige; ask students of a prospective advisor how the group works. Build a record by reproducing a paper, completing a small project with a clear question, writing it up, sharing code, and presenting at a workshop or seminar. Seek feedback early and often. Research includes long stretches without results; sustaining collaboration, rest and a sense of what you value is part of doing it well.

@example
title: Budgeting a twelve-minute talk
problem: You have a twelve-minute slot. You want to reserve 10% of it as a buffer, speak at 140 words per minute, and spend 90 seconds on each slide. How many words should the script have and how many slides can you use?
step: Reserve the buffer: $12 \times 0.10 = 1.2$ minutes, leaving $12 - 1.2 = 10.8$ minutes of speaking.
step: Words: $10.8 \times 140 = 1512$ words at most.
step: Slides: $10.8 \text{ minutes} \times 60 / 90 = 7.2$, so use 7 slides.
step: Check against the structure: with about 35% for evidence, that is roughly $0.35 \times 10.8 \approx 3.8$ minutes, enough for two or three results slides at ninety seconds each.
answer: At most about 1,512 words and 7 slides.

@mistakes
- Packing the talk with every result, so that the one message is lost.
- Putting full sentences and tiny figures on slides, then reading them aloud.
- Never rehearsing aloud with a timer, which almost always leaves the talk 30% too long.
- Choosing a career path from rankings or rumors instead of from the daily work and the people involved.

@practice self
prompt: Write the outline for a recorded ten-minute talk on a paper of your choice, or on this described one: "A paper shows that when two sentiment classifiers are tuned with the same budget, a recently published method's reported 1.5-point advantage shrinks to 0.2 points (95% interval -0.4 to 0.8 over 10 seeds)." Give the one-sentence message, a time budget for each part of the talk, what each slide shows (about 8 slides), the numbers you would show and one question you expect.
solution: Message: when baselines get the same tuning budget, the reported 1.5-point advantage nearly vanishes. Time (10 minutes, 9 of speech plus 1 buffer): problem 1.5 min; method of the audit 2.5 min; evidence 3 min; limits 1 min; takeaway 1 min. Slides: (1) title and the one-sentence message; (2) why tuning budgets matter, with a concrete example of a "win" that disappears; (3) the claim: the published method's reported 1.5-point advantage; (4) our protocol: same data, same architecture, same search budget of 20 trials for both, 10 paired seeds; (5) result: bar or dot plot of mean paired difference 0.2 points with the interval -0.4 to 0.8, axis starting at zero difference; (6) tuning curve: expected best validation score against budget for both methods, showing the gap closing with budget; (7) limits: one task, two methods, one architecture; the interval does not exclude a small gain; (8) takeaway and what next, plus credit to coauthors and the original method's authors. Expected question: "Did you tune the new method as authors recommend?" Answer: we used their recommended ranges and published settings, extended with the same random search budget for both, and I can show the grids in the appendix.
hint: Write the message first, then allocate minutes by the five-part structure and assign one idea to each slide.
rubric: States one clear message and budgets the time sensibly within ten minutes.
rubric: Gives slide-by-slide content with the key numbers and the uncertainty shown honestly.
rubric: Includes limits and credit and anticipates a realistic question with an honest answer.

@quiz self
prompt: You are considering a PhD, a research engineering role, or an applied machine learning role in a domain such as medicine. Write a plan (120 to 180 words) for how you would explore which of these suits you over the next six months, naming concrete activities, what you would learn from each and how you would decide. Do not assume any path is best for everyone.
solution: I would spend the six months testing the daily work of each path. For research: reproduce one paper and then design and run a small project with a clear hypothesis, seeds and statistics, to learn whether I enjoy open-ended questions and long stretches without results; present it at a seminar and ask the audience for feedback. For applied work in medicine: spend time with clinicians or a domain lab, or use a public clinical data set, to learn what questions matter and how much of the work is data and context. I would talk to at least two people in each path about a typical week and how they entered, and read current information about the programs and employers I am considering. At the end I would write down which activities gave energy and which drained it, and decide from that, and revisit the decision later.
hint: Pick one concrete activity per path and say what you would learn about yourself from it.

rubric: Includes talking with people in each path and checking current information.
