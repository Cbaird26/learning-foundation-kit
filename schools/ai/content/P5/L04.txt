@article
An experiment is an argument whose premises are runs. It should be built so that, if your explanation is wrong, the results will show it. The design questions are: compared with what, with what removed, with what held equal, and on which data?

### Baselines

A **baseline** is the method you must beat for your claim to mean anything. Good practice:

- Include a **simple, strong baseline** (a well-tuned standard model), not only a weak or outdated one. A fancy method that beats a poor baseline demonstrates little.
- Include the **best relevant published method** you can run, or say plainly why you cannot.
- Give baselines the **same tuning budget** and the same data, compute and training length as the method. Dodge and colleagues (2019) argue that reports should show how expected validation performance changes with the number of tuning trials, since a method that looks better with a large budget may not with a small one; Lesson 6 computes this.
- Run baselines **yourself under your protocol** when you can, since numbers copied from other papers can differ in data splits, preprocessing and metrics.

### Ablations

An **ablation** removes or replaces one component of your method to see what each contributes. If your method has four ideas and the paper credits the gain to the whole bundle, you do not know which idea matters. The usual design removes one component at a time from the full system; with $k$ binary components this takes $k + 1$ configurations. A **full factorial** design tries all $2^k$ combinations and reveals interactions (two components that help only together), at exponential cost. Choose by suspicion: if you believe components interact, test their combinations.

Lipton and Steinhardt (2018) note that papers often credit a gain to one change while several things changed at once; ablations are the remedy.

### Controls

A **control** rules out an alternative explanation. If your architecture has 20% more parameters, a *parameter-matched* baseline asks whether size alone explains the gain. Other common controls: *compute-matched* (same training FLOPs), *data-matched* (same amount of training data), a *shuffled-label* or *random-feature* control that should give chance performance, and a *negative control*, a setting where your method should not help. If a control surprises you, understand it before going further.

### Data splits and decisions

Split data into **training**, **validation** and **test** sets. Make every decision (hyperparameters, early stopping, which variant to report) on validation data; touch the test set once, at the end. Repeated use of a test set for decisions leaks information into the method and inflates results. Sculley and colleagues (2018) discuss the winner's curse: the best of many tried options tends to be one whose measured score was helped by noise, so its measured advantage overstates its true advantage.

### Chance and multiplicity

Suppose you try $k$ variants of your method against a baseline and report the best. If no variant really differs from the baseline and each has an independent 50% chance of beating it on one run, the chance that at least one beats it is $1 - 0.5^{k}$, which approaches 1 quickly. Similarly, if $k$ methods are truly equal and measured with continuous scores, any particular one has probability $1/k$ of ranking first. So a win is evidence only if you counted the attempts, used enough seeds, and applied the statistics of Lesson 5. Decide the comparisons, the metric and the analysis *before* running; some venues offer registered reports for this purpose, and you should check what applies to you.

### Write the plan first

A plan names the hypothesis, the data sets, the baselines, the ablations and controls, the metric, the number of seeds, the tuning protocol and budget, the statistical analysis, and the result that would count against the hypothesis. Share it with a colleague before running anything; the objections you hear then are cheaper than reviewers' objections later.

@example
title: How many configurations does an ablation need?
problem: A method combines four binary components. How many configurations does a one-at-a-time ablation need, and how many does a full factorial need? With 5 seeds each, how many runs is that? Also, if you try 6 variants with no real effect, each with an independent 50% chance of beating the baseline on a single run, how likely is it that at least one "wins"?
step: One-at-a-time ablation: the full method plus four versions each missing one component, so $4 + 1 = 5$ configurations.
step: Full factorial: $2^4 = 16$ configurations.
step: With 5 seeds each, the runs are $5 \times 5 = 25$ and $16 \times 5 = 80$.
step: Chance of a spurious win: $1 - 0.5^6 = 1 - 0.015625 = 0.984375$.
step: Interpret: if you report the best of six variants, a "win" is almost guaranteed even with no effect. The design must count the six tries and use multiple seeds and a statistical test that accounts for them.
answer: 5 configurations versus 16 (25 versus 80 runs); the chance that at least one of six null variants appears to win is about 98.4%.

@mistakes
- Comparing against a weak or default-tuned baseline while the new method got a large search: the gain may be tuning, not the idea.
- Changing several things at once and crediting the gain to one of them, with no ablation.
- Making decisions on the test set, then reporting the same test set as if it were unseen.
- Reporting the best of many variants without saying how many were tried.

prompt: A method has 5 binary components. A full factorial ablation tries every combination, with 3 seeds per combination. How many runs does that need?
answer: 96
tol: 0.001
unit: runs
check: 2 ** 5 * 3
hint: There are $2^5$ combinations of five on/off components.
solution: $2^5 = 32$ configurations, and $32 \times 3 = 96$ runs. The one-at-a-time design would need only $(5 + 1) \times 3 = 18$ runs, but could miss interactions.

@practice num
prompt: You try 8 variants of a method against a baseline. Suppose none really differs from the baseline, and each variant independently has a 50% chance of beating it in a single run. What is the probability that at least one variant beats the baseline? Give a decimal to four places.
answer: 0.9961
tol: 0.0001
check: 1 - 0.5 ** 8
hint: Compute the probability that all eight fail, then subtract from 1.
solution: All eight fail with probability $0.5^8 = 0.00390625$, so at least one succeeds with probability $1 - 0.0039 = 0.9961$. Reporting the best of eight single-run comparisons almost guarantees an apparent win.

@practice self
prompt: Write an experiment plan (200 to 280 words) for this hypothesis: "Label smoothing with factor 0.1 lowers the expected calibration error of a small image classifier on a 10-class data set by at least 30% relative to standard cross-entropy, without lowering test accuracy by more than 0.5 points." Include baselines, controls or ablations, data splits, seeds, the statistical analysis and the result that would count against the hypothesis.
hint: List the conditions, fairness rules, splits, seeds, analysis and the falsifying result.
solution: Data and splits: use a 10-class image data set with a fixed split into training, validation and test sets; tune and select on validation only, evaluate on test once. Conditions: (1) cross-entropy, (2) label smoothing 0.1, which is the hypothesis; (3) ablation: label smoothing at 0.05 and 0.2 to see a dose response; (4) a control with post-hoc temperature scaling fitted on validation data for cross-entropy, since calibration can be fixed without changing training, and the hypothesis is only interesting if smoothing is competitive or complementary. Fairness: the same architecture, epochs and learning-rate search budget (say 6 settings per condition) for all conditions. Seeds: 10 per condition, using the same seeds across conditions so differences are paired. Metrics: expected calibration error with a fixed binning scheme chosen in advance, and accuracy. Analysis: report mean and standard deviation across seeds, the mean paired difference in calibration error with a 95% interval, and the paired difference in accuracy. Falsifying result: a relative reduction below 30% (interval including it), or an accuracy loss above 0.5 points. If temperature scaling matches the effect, report that honestly. All thresholds are fixed before running.
rubric: Specifies fair baselines with equal tuning budgets and a relevant control (such as temperature scaling) or ablation.
rubric: Keeps tuning on validation data and uses the test set once; fixes the calibration metric in advance.
rubric: Names the number of seeds, paired analysis with an interval, and the result that would count against the hypothesis.

@quiz num
prompt: A one-at-a-time ablation of a method with 6 binary components runs the full method and each version missing one component. Each configuration is run with 5 seeds on each of 3 data sets. How many runs is that in total?
answer: 105
tol: 0.001
unit: runs
check: (6 + 1) * 5 * 3
hint: Count the configurations first: the full method plus one per removed component.
solution: There are $6 + 1 = 7$ configurations, and $7 \times 5 \times 3 = 105$ runs.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper reports that its method (three new components added to a baseline) improves accuracy by 1.4 points on one data set. It has no ablations, the baseline uses default settings and the method's learning rate was tuned over 20 values. Write a referee-style paragraph (100 to 150 words) listing the design problems and the specific experiments you would request.
hint: Name the design flaws first, then the experiment you would request for each.
solution: The central claim, that the proposed components improve accuracy, is not supported by the experimental design. Three components are added together without ablations, so the gain cannot be attributed to any of them. The baseline uses default settings while the method's learning rate was searched over 20 values, so the 1.4-point gain may reflect tuning effort instead of the method. A single data set and an unstated number of runs limit the scope. I would request: a baseline tuned over the same learning-rate grid; a one-at-a-time ablation of the three components; at least five seeds per condition with paired differences and intervals; and results on at least one more data set. If the gain survives these checks, the claim would be convincing.
rubric: Identifies unequal tuning effort and the missing ablation as the main threats to the causal claim.
rubric: Requests specific experiments (equal grid, one-at-a-time ablation, multiple seeds with intervals, another data set).
rubric: Keeps a constructive referee tone.
