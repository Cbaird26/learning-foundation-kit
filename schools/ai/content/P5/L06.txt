@article
Most of a research project's cost is not the final training run: it is the dozens of small runs before it, the tuning, the ablations and the runs that crash. Budgeting those honestly decides what you can claim, and the choices you make also affect how reproducible and how costly your work is for others.

### Estimating training compute

For a dense transformer with $N$ parameters trained on $D$ tokens, a widely used **approximation** is that training takes about
$$ C \approx 6\,N\,D $$
floating-point operations (FLOPs): roughly $2ND$ for the forward pass and $4ND$ for the backward pass. It is an approximation; it ignores costs that grow with sequence length, and does not apply to sparse or otherwise different architectures. Use it for planning, not for accounting after the fact, and measure your own runs.

To convert FLOPs to time, divide by what the hardware actually delivers: peak throughput $P$ (FLOP/s) times **utilization** $u$, the fraction of peak you achieve, which is often well below one and depends on code, model size, batch size and communication. The number of device-hours is
$$ \text{device-hours} = \frac{C}{P\,u \times 3600}. $$
Spread over $g$ devices, the elapsed time is that divided by $g$, if scaling is efficient.

### How to spend a fixed budget

Kaplan and colleagues (2020) reported that language-model loss follows smooth power laws in parameters, data and compute. Hoffmann and colleagues (2022) found that, for a fixed compute budget, many models had been trained with too few tokens for their size, and that parameters and tokens should be increased in roughly equal proportion; the widely quoted rule of thumb of about 20 tokens per parameter is approximate. For your project the lesson is general: a budget buys a combination of model size and data, and the best combination is not always the biggest model. Small-scale studies can guide choices only if the effect you study is known to carry over with scale, which you must argue, not assume.

### Tuning budget, honestly

Dodge and colleagues (2019) propose reporting **expected validation performance as a function of tuning budget**: if you could afford only $k$ random hyperparameter trials, what best validation score would you expect? Given $n$ observed scores sorted $v_{(1)} \le \dots \le v_{(n)}$, and treating them as random draws with replacement, the expected maximum of $k$ draws is
$$ \mathbb{E}[\max_k] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} v_{(i)} \left[ \left(\tfrac{i}{n}\right)^{k} - \left(\tfrac{i-1}{n}\right)^{k} \right]. $$
With $k = 1$ this is the mean. A curve of this quantity against $k$ shows whether method A beats method B at every budget or only at a large one, which is the fair way to compare methods that differ in tuning sensitivity.

### Efficient experimentation

- **Ladder of experiments**: a sanity run on a tiny subset, a pilot to estimate noise, then the main runs, then ablations. Fail early and cheaply.
- **Cache** data preprocessing and pretrained features; **checkpoint** long runs so a crash does not lose everything.
- **Log everything**: configuration, seed, code version, metrics. A run you cannot reconstruct is wasted.
- **Keep a reserve** (a reasonable planning habit is about a quarter to a third of the budget) for reruns, reviewer requests and mistakes.
- **Profile** before buying more compute; a data-loading bottleneck wastes devices.

### Energy, cost and honesty

Strubell, Ganesh and McCallum (2019) estimated the energy and carbon costs of training large language-processing models, including the extra cost of hyperparameter search, and argued for reporting training time and compute. Report your totals including tuning and failed runs. Rules on compute funding, allocations and reporting differ between institutions and funders; check the ones that apply.

### This week's deliverable

Analyzing supplied multi-seed results and reporting them correctly means: state the number of seeds and what varied, give means with standard deviations, give the paired difference with an interval and an effect size, say what the interval implies, and avoid claims that go beyond it.

@example
title: From FLOPs to device-hours, and a tuning curve
problem: A model has $N = 350$ million parameters and is trained on $D = 7$ billion tokens. Each device has a peak of $2 \times 10^{14}$ FLOP/s and you achieve 40% utilization. How many FLOPs and device-hours is that? Separately, four tuning trials gave validation accuracies 0.80, 0.84, 0.82 and 0.86; what is the expected best of two random trials?
step: Compute: $C \approx 6 \times 3.5\times 10^{8} \times 7\times 10^{9} = 1.47 \times 10^{19}$ FLOPs.
step: Delivered throughput: $2\times 10^{14} \times 0.40 = 8 \times 10^{13}$ FLOP/s.
step: Time: $1.47\times 10^{19} / 8\times 10^{13} = 183{,}750$ s, which is $183{,}750/3600 \approx 51.04$ device-hours.
step: Sort the trial scores: 0.80, 0.82, 0.84, 0.86. With $n = 4$ and $k = 2$ the weights are $(i/4)^2 - ((i-1)/4)^2 = 1/16, 3/16, 5/16, 7/16$.
step: Expected best of two: $0.80/16 + 3(0.82)/16 + 5(0.84)/16 + 7(0.86)/16 = 0.8425$. For comparison the mean (budget 1) is 0.83.
answer: About $1.47 \times 10^{19}$ FLOPs, or 51.04 device-hours; expected best validation accuracy with two trials is 0.8425.

@mistakes
- Using peak throughput instead of achieved throughput: without a utilization factor the device-hours are too small.
- Applying $6ND$ as if exact, or to architectures it does not describe, and presenting the result as measured compute.
- Budgeting only the final run and forgetting tuning, ablations, failed runs and reruns.
- Comparing a heavily tuned method with a lightly tuned baseline, then reporting both as best-of-their-search without the number of trials.

answer: 317.46

solution: Delivered throughput is $3\times 10^{14} \times 0.35 = 1.05 \times 10^{14}$ FLOP/s. The time is $1.2\times 10^{20}/1.05\times 10^{14} \approx 1.143 \times 10^{6}$ s, which is about 317.46 device-hours. On 8 devices with perfect scaling the elapsed time would be about 39.7 hours.
