@article
"I want to work on robustness" is a topic, not a question. Nobody can tell you what experiment to run, what result would surprise you, or when you are done. Much of research skill is the patient narrowing of a topic into something you could be wrong about.

### Kinds of questions

Machine learning papers usually answer one of a few kinds of question:

- **Does method $M$ do better than $B$ under condition $C$?** (a method question)
- **Why does a known behavior occur?** (an analysis or diagnostic question)
- **What can be guaranteed?** (a theory question)
- **How should we measure this?** (a benchmark or metric question)
- **Does a reported result hold again?** (a replication question)

Each calls for a different kind of evidence, so naming the kind early tells you what the paper must contain.

### Properties of a good question

A good question is:

1. **Specific**: it names the models, data, metric and comparison.
2. **Falsifiable**: some outcome of the experiment would count against it.
3. **Worth answering**: for some audience, the answer changes what they believe or do. Ask: if the answer is yes, who cares, and if it is no, who cares?
4. **Feasible**: your compute, data, time and skills can settle it.
5. **Novel enough**: the literature (Lesson 3) does not already answer it. A replication or a clearly reported negative result can be worth publishing, and the norms differ among venues.

### From topic to hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a claim with a prediction attached. The chain is: topic, then question, then hypothesis, then prediction, then experiment. "Study robustness" becomes "Does training with random color jitter improve accuracy under color shift on a small image classifier?" becomes the hypothesis "jitter raises accuracy on the color-shifted test set by at least one point, averaged over seeds, without lowering clean accuracy by more than 0.5 points." The numbers make it falsifiable. Choose them *before* you run the experiment, so that you cannot adjust them to fit the results.

Good questions come from reading with a critical eye: an assumption a paper makes, a failure of a published method on a slightly different data set, a result that two papers report differently, or a combination of two ideas that no one has tried. Talk to people; a question stated aloud to a colleague usually becomes sharper within minutes.

### Feasibility in numbers

Count the runs. If a plan compares $m$ methods on $d$ data sets with $s$ seeds, the final comparison needs $m \times d \times s$ runs; tuning adds more. Multiply by the cost per run to get device-hours, and check the total against what you have.

Precision gives a second budget. The standard error of a mean over $n$ seeds is $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$ where $\sigma$ is the standard deviation across seeds. To reach a target standard error $\text{SE}^\ast$ you need $n \ge (\sigma/\text{SE}^\ast)^2$. You rarely know $\sigma$ in advance, so run a small pilot and use its standard deviation as a rough guide, aware that a few seeds estimate $\sigma$ poorly. Lesson 5 develops the statistics.

### Pilots, scooping and negative results

A pilot, a small and cheap version of the experiment, tells you whether the effect exists at all and how noisy it is. Plan for possible failure: a project whose only publishable outcome is a positive result is fragile. A well-designed experiment that finds no effect still teaches something, though how readily it can be published varies by venue and field. Another group may work on the same idea at once; this is a risk to weigh, not a reason to rush. Check any rules your institution or funder sets on how projects are proposed and approved.

### The one-page summary and critique

This week's deliverable is a one-page summary and critique of a paper. Use a fixed shape: (1) the question and why it matters; (2) the method in two or three sentences; (3) the main evidence, with numbers; (4) the strengths; (5) the weaknesses, ranked by how much they threaten the claim; (6) what you would run next. Practice this shape on every paper you read closely.

@example
title: Sharpening a topic and counting the cost
problem: You are interested in "data efficiency." You have one device and about 200 device-hours. A pilot on a small task shows a standard deviation of 0.8 accuracy points across seeds. Turn the topic into a hypothesis and decide how many seeds you need for a standard error of at most 0.25 points.
step: Narrow the topic to a question: "Does pretraining on an unlabeled corpus improve accuracy of a small classifier when only 500 labeled examples are available?"
step: State a falsifiable hypothesis with a threshold fixed in advance: "Pretraining raises mean test accuracy by at least 2 points over training from scratch with the same architecture and tuning budget."
step: Compute the seeds needed: $n \ge (0.8/0.25)^2 = 10.24$, so $n = 11$ seeds per condition.
step: Count the runs: 2 conditions $\times$ 11 seeds = 22 runs. At one device-hour per run that is 22 device-hours, comfortably inside 200 hours, leaving room for tuning.
answer: A hypothesis of at least 2 points from pretraining at 500 labels, tested with 11 seeds per condition (22 runs).

@mistakes
- Stating a hypothesis that no result could contradict ("method $M$ is helpful in some settings"): add a comparison, a metric and a threshold.
- Choosing the success threshold after seeing the results, which turns a test into a description.
- Estimating the cost of the final experiment but not of tuning, pilots, failed runs and reruns, which often cost more.
- Treating a rate of one or two seeds as enough because a pilot looked clean: few seeds estimate variability badly.

@practice num
prompt: A plan compares 4 methods on 3 data sets. The final comparison uses 5 seeds per method and data set. Tuning gives each method 8 configurations on a single seed per data set. Every run takes 1.5 device-hours. How many device-hours does the plan need in total?
answer: 234
tol: 0.001
unit: device-hours
check: (4 * 3 * 5 + 4 * 3 * 8) * 1.5
hint: Count the final runs and the tuning runs separately, add them, then multiply by the hours per run.
solution: Final runs: $4 \times 3 \times 5 = 60$. Tuning runs: $4 \times 3 \times 8 = 96$. Together 156 runs, and $156 \times 1.5 = 234$ device-hours. Tuning is the larger share.

@practice mc
prompt: Which of these is the best-formed falsifiable hypothesis for a study of label noise?
choice*: With 20% of training labels flipped at random, a loss that down-weights high-loss examples raises mean test accuracy over 5 seeds by at least one point compared with standard cross-entropy, under the same tuning budget.
choice: Label noise is an important problem and robust losses are useful in many settings.
choice: We will explore how label noise affects neural networks.
choice: Robust losses are better than cross-entropy.
hint: A falsifiable hypothesis names a comparison, a condition, a metric and a threshold.
solution: Only the first statement says what is compared, under what noise, by what measure and by how much, so a particular result could contradict it. The other statements are too vague or too general for any single experiment to refute.

@practice num
prompt: A pilot gives a standard deviation of 1.1 accuracy points across seeds. How many seeds per condition are needed so that the standard error of the mean is at most 0.4 points? Give the smallest integer that works.
answer: 8
tol: 0.001
unit: seeds
check: math.ceil((1.1 / 0.4) ** 2)
hint: Use $n \ge (\sigma/\mathrm{SE}^\ast)^2$ and round up.
solution: $(1.1/0.4)^2 = 7.5625$, so 7 seeds are not enough and 8 are. With 8 seeds the standard error is $1.1/\sqrt{8} \approx 0.389 \le 0.4$.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page summary and critique (about 250 to 300 words) of the following described paper. "A paper proposes an optimizer variant that adapts per-layer step sizes. It trains a 12-layer image classifier on two data sets for 100 epochs and reports test accuracy for the new optimizer and for plain momentum SGD: 91.8 versus 91.2 on the first data set and 74.5 versus 73.9 on the second, one run each. The step size of momentum SGD is the default; the new optimizer's three hyperparameters were searched on a grid of 27 settings using the test sets. The paper concludes that adaptive per-layer step sizes improve training of deep networks."
solution: Question and importance: Can per-layer adaptive step sizes improve the accuracy of deep classifiers over a standard optimizer? If so, it would matter to anyone training such networks. Method: the optimizer adapts a step size for each layer; the paper adds three hyperparameters. Evidence: on two data sets, one run each, test accuracy is 0.6 points higher on both (91.8 versus 91.2 and 74.5 versus 73.9). Strengths: the idea is simple, there are two data sets and the comparison is to a common baseline. Weaknesses, in order of seriousness: (1) the new optimizer's hyperparameters were chosen using the test sets by a search over 27 settings while the baseline stayed at defaults, so the comparison is unequal and the gains are biased upward; (2) one run per cell, so a 0.6 point gap cannot be separated from seed variation; (3) the conclusion is about "deep networks" but only one architecture and two data sets were tested. Next experiments: tune both optimizers with the same budget on validation data; run at least five seeds per cell and report paired differences with intervals; add an ablation of the per-layer component against a global adaptive method, and a second architecture.
rubric: Gives the question, method and numeric evidence accurately in the stated structure.
rubric: Ranks weaknesses and identifies test-set tuning with unequal baseline tuning as the most serious.
rubric: Notes the single run per cell and the overreach from two data sets to "deep networks".
rubric: Proposes specific follow-up experiments that would resolve the weaknesses.

@quiz num
prompt: A comparison uses 3 methods on 2 data sets with 10 seeds per method and data set. Each run takes 0.5 device-hours. How many device-hours does the final comparison need, ignoring tuning?
answer: 30
tol: 0.001
unit: device-hours
check: 3 * 2 * 10 * 0.5
hint: Multiply methods, data sets and seeds to get the number of runs.
solution: There are $3 \times 2 \times 10 = 60$ runs, and $60 \times 0.5 = 30$ device-hours.

@quiz mc
prompt: A well-designed experiment finds that a promising technique gives no improvement over a strong baseline. What is the most accurate description of this outcome?
choice*: It is a legitimate finding that informs the field, though how readily it can be published depends on the venue and its norms.
choice: It shows the experiment was badly designed.
choice: It should be quietly dropped, because only positive results count as research.
choice: It proves the technique can never help in any setting.
hint: Think about what a null result in one setting does and does not establish.
solution: A well-run null result is evidence about the tested setting and prevents others from repeating the attempt. It does not prove the idea fails everywhere, and it does not mean the design was poor. Publication norms differ, so check what venues accept.

@quiz self
prompt: A labmate says: "I want to study whether language models are fair." Turn this topic into a specific, falsifiable research question and hypothesis that could be tested with modest compute. State the models or data you would use, the metric, the comparison, a threshold fixed in advance, and one result that would count against your hypothesis.
solution: Topic to question: "Does a small pretrained classifier fine-tuned for sentiment give systematically different scores to otherwise identical sentences that differ only in a name associated with one group or another?" Hypothesis: on a set of 500 templated sentences where only the name changes, the mean score gap between name groups is at least 2 points on a 100-point scale, averaged over 5 fine-tuning seeds, and a reweighting method reduces that gap by at least half without losing more than 1 point of accuracy on a held-out set. Metric: mean absolute score gap per template pair and accuracy. Comparison: the same model fine-tuned with and without reweighting, with equal tuning budgets. Falsifying result: a gap below 0.5 points in the baseline, or a reweighted gap not smaller by half, or an accuracy loss above 1 point. The threshold is fixed in advance, and the claim is restricted to this template set and these models, because "fair" is not one measurable property.
rubric: Narrows the topic to a concrete model, data and measurable metric.
rubric: Fixes numeric thresholds in advance and names a result that would count against the hypothesis.
rubric: Scopes the claim to what the experiment can show and notes that fairness has several measures.
