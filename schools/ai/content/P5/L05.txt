@article
Train the same model twice with different random seeds and you will get two different accuracies. How different? And if method B beats method A by half a point, is that the method or the dice? Henderson and colleagues (2018) showed in deep reinforcement learning that different seeds, implementation details and small sample sizes can lead to opposite conclusions about which algorithm is better. Bouthillier and colleagues (2021) argue that a benchmark comparison should account for all the sources of variance: random initialization, data order, the split of the data, hyperparameter search and hardware nondeterminism.

### Summaries over seeds

Suppose a method gives scores $x_1, \dots, x_n$ over $n$ seeds.

- **Mean** $\bar{x} = \frac{1}{n}\sum x_i$.
- **Sample standard deviation** $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}$. It describes how much runs differ; it does not shrink as you add seeds.
- **Standard error** $\mathrm{SE} = s/\sqrt{n}$. It describes how uncertain the *mean* is; it shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$.
- **t-based confidence interval** $\bar{x} \pm t_{n-1,\,0.975}\, s/\sqrt{n}$ for a 95% interval, where $t_{n-1,0.975}$ is a critical value of Student's $t$ distribution (2.776 for 4 degrees of freedom, 2.365 for 7, 2.262 for 9). It assumes the seed-to-seed scores are roughly normal and independent.

Always say which of $s$, SE or an interval a "$\pm$" means. The three differ by factors of $\sqrt{n}$ and about 2.

### The bootstrap

When you doubt normality, or the statistic is not a mean, use the **bootstrap** (Efron, 1979): resample the $n$ scores with replacement many times, compute the statistic on each resample, and take the middle of those values. The **percentile interval** at 90% uses the 5th and 95th percentiles of the resampled statistics. Agarwal and colleagues (2021) recommend, for reinforcement learning results over many tasks and few runs, reporting the **interquartile mean** (IQM, the mean of the middle 50% of the scores after sorting) with *stratified bootstrap* confidence intervals, which resample runs within each task, instead of point estimates alone.

### Comparing two methods

If both methods were run on the same seeds, data splits or tasks, compare them **pairwise**: take the difference $d_i$ for each pair, then analyze $d_1, \dots, d_n$ as one sample. Pairing removes the variation shared by both methods and often gives a much more sensitive comparison. The **paired $t$ statistic** is
$$ t = \frac{\bar{d}}{s_d/\sqrt{n}}, $$
compared with a $t$ distribution with $n-1$ degrees of freedom. An **effect size** reports magnitude, not just detectability; one common choice, Cohen's $d$ for paired data, is $\bar{d}/s_d$, and another is the raw difference in the metric's own units, which is often more informative.

Choose tests to match the data. Dietterich (1998) studied approximate tests for comparing classifiers on one data set. Demšar (2006) recommended nonparametric tests (the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for two methods, the Friedman test with post-hoc comparisons for several) when comparing classifiers over multiple data sets. Dror and colleagues (2018) survey the choice of significance test in natural language processing. A $p$-value is the probability, if there were no real difference, of seeing a difference at least as large; it is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and a tiny $p$ with a tiny effect may not matter.

### Many comparisons

Test $m$ hypotheses at level 0.05 each and you expect false positives. **Bonferroni** rejects hypothesis $i$ if $p_i \le 0.05/m$, which controls the chance of *any* false rejection. **Benjamini and Hochberg (1995)** controls the *false discovery rate*, the expected fraction of rejections that are false: sort the $p$-values $p_{(1)} \le \dots \le p_{(m)}$, find the largest $i$ with $p_{(i)} \le \frac{i}{m}\,0.05$, and reject the hypotheses with the $i$ smallest $p$-values. It is less conservative than Bonferroni.

### Habits

Use at least five seeds, often more; report the number. Say what varied across seeds. Report intervals or paired differences with the raw numbers in an appendix. Do not choose the "best seed" for the table. Statistical conventions differ across venues and fields, so check what your venue expects.

@example
title: Summarizing five seeds and correcting for four tests
problem: A method scores 71.2, 72.0, 70.5, 71.8 and 72.4 over five seeds. Give the mean, the sample standard deviation, the standard error and a 95% t interval (use $t_{4,0.975} = 2.776$). Separately, four comparisons give $p$-values 0.001, 0.009, 0.020 and 0.30; which does each correction reject at level 0.05?
step: Mean: $(71.2 + 72.0 + 70.5 + 71.8 + 72.4)/5 = 71.58$.
step: Deviations from the mean are $-0.38$, $0.42$, $-1.08$, $0.22$, $0.82$; their squares sum to $2.208$, so $s = \sqrt{2.208/4} \approx 0.743$.
step: Standard error: $0.743/\sqrt{5} \approx 0.332$. Interval half-width: $2.776 \times 0.332 \approx 0.922$, so the 95% interval is about $[70.66,\ 72.50]$.
step: Bonferroni with $m = 4$: the threshold is $0.05/4 = 0.0125$, so it rejects the two smallest, 0.001 and 0.009.
step: Benjamini-Hochberg: the thresholds $\frac{i}{4}\,0.05$ are 0.0125, 0.025, 0.0375 and 0.05. The $p$-values 0.001, 0.009 and 0.020 are each below their thresholds, and 0.30 is not, so it rejects three.
answer: Mean 71.58, $s \approx 0.743$, SE $\approx 0.332$, 95% interval $[70.66, 72.50]$; Bonferroni rejects 2 hypotheses and Benjamini-Hochberg rejects 3.

@mistakes
- Writing "$\pm$" without saying whether it is $s$, the standard error or an interval, which changes its size by a factor of $\sqrt{n}$ or about 2.
- Using the standard deviation with $n$ in the denominator on a handful of seeds; use $n-1$ for a sample.
- Comparing two methods as if the runs were unrelated when the same seeds or splits were used, which discards the pairing and loses power.
- Reading $p < 0.05$ as "the effect is large" or "the hypothesis is probably true": it is neither.

@practice self
prompt: A paper's table shows, for a new method and a baseline, one number each, 83.4 and 83.0, with the new one in bold and the text "significantly outperforms". There are no seeds, intervals or tests, and the authors say "we selected the best of several runs for each". Write a critique paragraph (120 to 180 words) that explains what is wrong with the statistical reporting and states how it should be done, using the concepts of variance, selection, pairing and uncertainty.
solution: The table shows a 0.4-point gap between two single numbers and the text calls it significant, but no test, interval or number of runs supports the word. Seed-to-seed standard deviations of a few tenths of a point are common in deep learning, so a 0.4-point gap could easily arise by chance. Worse, the authors report the best of several runs for each method; selecting the maximum of a noisy quantity inflates it, and the inflation grows with the number of runs, so the reported gap is biased and cannot be reproduced by a reader running the method once. The comparison should use a fixed number of seeds (at least five) per method, shared across methods so that differences are paired; report the mean and standard deviation, the mean paired difference with a 95% interval, and an effect size in the metric's units; and state any selection made, which should be on validation data. If the interval for the difference includes zero, the honest claim is that the evidence is inconclusive.
hint: Ask what a 0.4-point gap means against the typical seed-to-seed spread, and what happens when you report the best of several runs.
rubric: Explains that single numbers and no variability cannot support "significantly".
rubric: Identifies that reporting the best run is selection bias that inflates the gap.
rubric: Prescribes multiple shared seeds, paired differences with an interval and an effect size, and honest wording.
