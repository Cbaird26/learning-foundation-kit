@article
A paper is not a diary of what you did; it is an argument that a specific claim is true, supported by specific evidence. Readers meet it in three layers: the title and abstract (everyone), the figures and tables (many), the full text (few). Each layer must stand on its own.

### Start from the claims

Before writing prose, list your claims in single sentences and, next to each, the experiment and the table or figure that supports it. A claim with no evidence is cut or weakened; evidence that supports no claim is cut or moved to an appendix. This list is your outline, and it is the same list a reviewer will make.

### Structure

A common structure, though venues and subfields differ:

- **Abstract**: the problem, the approach, the main result with a number, and the scope. About a paragraph, no citations, no claims that the paper does not support.
- **Introduction**: the problem and why it matters, what is missing in prior work, your contributions as a short list, and a pointer to the main result.
- **Related work** (Lesson 3): positioned against groups of prior ideas.
- **Method**: precisely enough that another researcher could implement it; define notation once and use it consistently.
- **Experiments**: setup (data, baselines, tuning, seeds, metrics), results, and analysis, including ablations.
- **Discussion and limitations**: what the results do not show, failure cases, and threats to validity.
- **Conclusion**: a short restatement and next steps, not new claims.

Page limits, formatting, required sections (some venues require a limitations or ethics statement) and anonymity rules all differ; read the current instructions for authors before you start, not the night before the deadline.

### Figures and tables

Each figure should make one point, which the caption states. A figure for a line chart of accuracy against training data should show variability across seeds (a shaded band or error bars, said in the caption), label axes with units, and use markers or line styles in addition to color so that it survives grayscale printing. Do not truncate the axis of a bar chart: bars encode values by length, and a truncated axis exaggerates differences. If bars for accuracies 91 and 92 start from an axis at 90, the second bar looks twice as tall as the first although the values differ by about one percent. Tables should align numbers, bold only what a stated test supports, and say what the entries mean.

### Numbers and uncertainty

Report as many digits as the precision justifies: a mean of $82.347$ with a standard error of $0.412$ is written $82.3 \pm 0.4$ (standard error), saying which uncertainty it is. When comparing methods, give the difference with its interval. For independent estimates with standard errors $a$ and $b$, the standard error of the difference is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$, and an approximate 95% interval is the difference $\pm 1.96\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ (for few seeds use $t$ critical values, and prefer paired differences when runs share seeds). State relative improvements with the baseline value, because "20% better" is ambiguous between error and accuracy.

### Calibrating the wording

Match the verb to the evidence. "Improves" requires an interval comfortably above zero over a described range of conditions. "Is competitive with" or "we observe a gain of 1.2 points, but the interval includes zero" is honest for a smaller result. Words such as "significantly" should be reserved for a stated test. Lipton and Steinhardt (2018) criticize loose language, such as using technical terms with their everyday sense, and mixing speculation with fact. Put speculation in a clearly marked place and call it speculation.

### Honesty and credit

Report negative and mixed results that bear on your claim, including seeds or data sets where the method did not help. Credit prior work precisely. Authorship, acknowledgments, and disclosure of tools used in writing or analysis follow the rules of your venue and institution, which are changing; read the current policy and disclose accordingly. Revise in layers: structure first, then paragraphs, then sentences. Ask a colleague who has not seen the work to read the abstract and tell you what the paper claims.

@example
title: Matching a claim to an interval
problem: A paper says "our method improves accuracy by 1.2 points." Method B's mean is 1.2 points above method A's. The standard errors of the two means are 0.5 and 0.6 points. What interval supports the claim, and how should the sentence be rewritten?
step: The standard error of the difference is $\sqrt{0.5^2 + 0.6^2} = \sqrt{0.61} \approx 0.781$.
step: The approximate 95% interval is $1.2 \pm 1.96 \times 0.781 = 1.2 \pm 1.53$, that is $[-0.33,\ 2.73]$ points.
step: The interval includes zero, so these data are compatible with no improvement and with an improvement of more than two points. "Improves" is stronger than the evidence.
step: Rewrite: "Method B scored 1.2 points higher on average (95% interval $-0.3$ to $2.7$ over the same setting), which is suggestive but does not rule out no difference; more seeds are needed."
answer: The interval is about $[-0.33, 2.73]$ points, which includes zero; the claim should be hedged as suggestive, not as an established improvement.

@mistakes
- Writing the abstract's claims first and then looking for evidence, instead of the reverse.
- Bar charts with truncated axes, or lines without variability, that make small differences look large.
- Bolding the best number in each column without a test, which implies significance.
- Burying limitations and negative results in an appendix or leaving them out.

@practice num
prompt: Two methods have mean accuracies that differ by 3.0 points. The standard errors of the two means are 0.8 and 0.6 points, and the estimates are independent. What is the lower end of an approximate 95% interval for the difference ($\pm 1.96$ standard errors of the difference), in points? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 1.04
tol: 0.01
unit: points
check: 3.0 - 1.96 * (0.8 ** 2 + 0.6 ** 2) ** 0.5
hint: The standard error of the difference is the square root of the sum of the squared standard errors.
solution: $\sqrt{0.8^2 + 0.6^2} = \sqrt{1.00} = 1.00$, so the interval is $3.0 \pm 1.96$, that is $[1.04, 4.96]$. The lower end is 1.04: the interval excludes zero, so "B improves on A in this setting" is supported (within the stated assumptions).

@practice mc
prompt: Over 5 seeds on one data set, the mean paired difference between a new method and a baseline is 0.4 points, with a 95% interval of $[-0.2, 1.0]$. Which sentence is best supported?
choice*: On this data set the new method scored 0.4 points higher on average, but the data are compatible with no difference and with a gain of up to about 1 point.
choice: The new method improves accuracy.
choice: The new method significantly outperforms the baseline.
choice: The new method is shown to be no better than the baseline.
hint: An interval that includes zero cannot support "improves", but it does not prove "no effect" either.
solution: The honest statement reports the observed difference and the range of values the data are compatible with. The interval includes zero, so neither "improves" nor "significantly" is justified, and since it also includes gains of up to 1 point, "no better" is not shown either.

@practice num
prompt: A bar chart of accuracy starts its vertical axis at 90 instead of 0. One bar is 91 and another is 92. What is the ratio of the visual heights of the taller bar to the shorter bar?
answer: 2
tol: 0.001
check: (92 - 90) / (91 - 90)
hint: The visible height of a bar is its value minus the axis starting value.
solution: The visible heights are $92 - 90 = 2$ and $91 - 90 = 1$, a ratio of 2, while the true ratio of the values is $92/91 \approx 1.011$. This is why bar charts should start at zero, or a dot plot with a labeled axis should be used.

choice*: State in the caption what the error bars or bands represent and over how many seeds.

@practice self
prompt: Rewrite this abstract (about 120 to 160 words) so that its claims match its evidence. Original: "We introduce FastMix, a novel augmentation that dramatically improves generalization of deep networks. FastMix achieves state-of-the-art results and consistently outperforms all existing augmentations. Experiments show it significantly improves accuracy." Evidence: a 9-layer convolutional network on one small image data set; one baseline augmentation was compared; 5 seeds each; the mean paired difference was 0.9 points with a 95% interval of [0.3, 1.5]; there was no ablation and no other data set; training time was 15% longer.
solution: We introduce FastMix, an image augmentation that mixes pairs of training images, and study it on a small image classification data set with a 9-layer convolutional network. Compared with one standard augmentation, tuned under the same budget, FastMix raised test accuracy by 0.9 points on average over 5 paired seeds (95% interval 0.3 to 1.5), at the price of 15% longer training. We did not ablate its components, and we did not test other architectures, data sets or augmentations, so we cannot say whether the gain holds more broadly or which component produces it. The result suggests that mixing-based augmentation may help in this setting, and we release code and configurations so that others can test it elsewhere.
hint: Replace each strong phrase by a number and a scope taken from the evidence, then say what was not tested.
rubric: Removes unsupported phrases ("dramatically", "state-of-the-art", "all existing") and replaces them with the measured numbers and scope.
rubric: Reports the interval, the number of seeds, the single baseline and the cost, and states what was not tested.
rubric: Keeps a clear, readable abstract that still states the contribution.

@quiz num
prompt: The standard deviation of accuracy over 9 seeds is 2.4 points. Using the critical value $t = 2.306$ (8 degrees of freedom), what is the half-width of the 95% t interval for the mean? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 1.84
tol: 0.01
unit: points
check: 2.306 * 2.4 / 9 ** 0.5
hint: The standard error is $s/\sqrt{n}$; multiply by $t$.
solution: $\mathrm{SE} = 2.4/3 = 0.8$, and the half-width is $2.306 \times 0.8 \approx 1.84$ points, so the mean is reported with an interval of about $\pm 1.84$.

@quiz mc
prompt: What is the best use of a limitations section?
choice*: To state the conditions under which the claims were tested, known failure cases and threats to validity.
choice: To leave out weaknesses that reviewers might use against the paper.
choice: To list generic statements such as "more data would help" that apply to every paper.
choice: To apologize for the work so that reviewers are lenient.
hint: A limitations section helps readers decide how far to trust and reuse the result.
solution: A useful limitations section tells readers where the result applies and where it fails, which is information they need. Omitting weaknesses, listing platitudes and apologizing all serve the author's comfort and not the reader's judgment.
