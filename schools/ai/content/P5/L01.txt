@article
You will meet far more papers than you can read: a single week can bring dozens of new preprints on one topic. Researchers cope by treating reading as a staged search, not a single act. Spending four hours on the wrong paper costs more than skimming ten right ones.

### The three passes

Keshav (2007) describes a three-pass approach that most researchers use in some form.

**First pass: a bird's-eye view (about five to ten minutes).** Read the title, abstract and introduction, the section and subsection headings, and the conclusions; glance at the references and note which you know. Then answer Keshav's "five Cs": the *category* of paper (a new method, an analysis, a benchmark, a theory result, a survey); the *context* (which earlier work it builds on); the *correctness* of its assumptions at first sight; its *contributions*; and its *clarity*. After this pass you can decide whether to stop, set the paper aside for later, or continue.

**Second pass: grasp the content, not the details (up to about an hour).** Read carefully but skip proofs and implementation details. Study every figure and table: what are the axes, are there error bars, which methods are compared? Mark references you should read next. Afterward you should be able to summarize the main thread of the paper with its supporting evidence to someone else.

**Third pass: re-create the work (hours for a beginner).** Keshav's idea is to *virtually re-implement* the paper: make the same assumptions, derive the same result, and compare your version with theirs. You will find hidden assumptions, unstated hyperparameters and steps that do not follow. Do this only for papers central to your own work, or when you must review one.

### What to extract

Whatever the pass, hold four questions:

1. **What exactly is the claim?** Write it as one sentence with a comparison and a condition: "Method $M$ reaches higher accuracy than baseline $B$ on benchmark $D$ under the same compute."
2. **What evidence supports it?** Which experiments, with which baselines, data sets, metrics and numbers of random seeds?
3. **What does the claim assume?** Data distribution, tuning budget, model size, evaluation protocol.
4. **What would falsify it?** If you cannot name a result that would count against the claim, the claim is not yet testable.

### Reading results with a skeptical eye

A results table is evidence for a narrower statement than the abstract often makes. Check the following.

- *Is the gain larger than the noise?* Results from one training run say little about how much a rerun would differ. Henderson and colleagues (2018) showed for deep reinforcement learning that seeds and implementation details can change conclusions; Lesson 5 returns to this.
- *Are the baselines strong and equally tuned?* A new method tuned for weeks against a baseline with default settings proves little.
- *Do the text and the numbers agree?* Compare the abstract's adjectives ("substantially", "consistently") with the table.
- *Are the claims scoped?* Results on two data sets support a statement about those data sets.
- *Is the test set large enough?* An accuracy $\hat{p}$ measured on $n$ test items has standard error about $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$ from the sampling of test items alone, before training variance.

Lipton and Steinhardt (2018) catalog troubling trends in machine learning scholarship, among them mixing explanation with speculation and using language loosely. Reading with these trends in mind sharpens your eye.

### Practical habits

Keep a reading log with one line per paper: the claim, the evidence and your verdict. Note whether a paper is a preprint or has been through peer review at a venue; a preprint has not necessarily been refereed. Venues, programs and fields differ in how they review and in what they expect from authors, so check the current guidelines of the venue you read or write for. Disagreement with a paper belongs in your notes first; many apparent flaws turn out to be explained in an appendix.

@example
title: Reading one row of a results table
problem: A paper's abstract says the new method "substantially improves" a benchmark. Its table lists test accuracy of 94.0% for the strongest baseline and 95.5% for the new method on a 10,000-item test set, each from a single run, with no error bars. How much did the error fall, and what should you conclude after a second pass?
step: Convert accuracy to error. The baseline error is $100 - 94.0 = 6.0\%$ and the new error is $100 - 95.5 = 4.5\%$.
step: Compute the relative error reduction: $(6.0 - 4.5)/6.0 = 0.25$, so errors fell by 25%. The same result as an absolute gain is 1.5 points; both views are true, and the table should state which is meant.
step: Estimate test-sampling noise for the new method: $\sqrt{0.955 \times 0.045 / 10000} \approx 0.0021$, that is about 0.21 points. A 1.5 point gap is several times this, so test-set sampling alone does not explain it.
step: Note what that standard error does not cover: training randomness. Each number comes from one run, so you cannot tell whether a different seed would shrink the gap. Check whether the baseline was tuned with a comparable budget.
step: Write the verdict: "On this benchmark the new method scored 1.5 points higher in one run per method; the evidence is promising but does not yet show a reliable improvement." The word "substantially" is not yet earned.
answer: A 25% relative reduction in error (1.5 points), larger than test-sampling noise of about 0.2 points, but supported by one run per method, so the claim of a substantial improvement is not yet established.

@mistakes
- Reading front to back at full depth: most papers deserve only the first pass, and starting in detail wastes the hours that matter.
- Trusting the abstract's adjectives instead of the table: always compare the words with the numbers, the baselines and the number of runs.
- Treating a preprint, a published paper or a famous author as proof of correctness: judge the evidence itself, whatever its source.
- Reporting a critique without first checking the appendix: a missing detail is often given there.

unit: %
check: (12.0 - 10.0) / 12.0 * 100
hint: The error rates are $100 - 88.0$ and $100 - 90.0$. Divide the drop in error by the original error.
solution: The error falls from 12.0% to 10.0%, a drop of 2.0 points. Relative to the original error that is $2/12 \approx 0.1667$, or 16.67%. The same change is only 2 points of accuracy, or about a 2.3% relative accuracy gain, which is why papers should say which comparison they mean.

@practice self
prompt: A paper's abstract states that its regularizer "consistently improves generalization." The paper's only evidence is a table with three image data sets and two architectures, one training run per cell. The regularizer's strength was tuned on each test set; the baselines use their default settings. Write a critique paragraph (120 to 180 words) that states the claim, names the main weaknesses in the evidence and says what additional evidence would resolve them.
hint: Start with the claim, then ask which evidence is single-run, unequal or tuned on test data.
solution: The paper claims that its regularizer consistently improves generalization. The evidence is six comparisons, each from a single training run, so the table cannot show whether the gains exceed seed-to-seed variation; "consistently" is also a strong word for six cells on three data sets. More seriously, the regularizer's strength was tuned on the test sets while the baselines used defaults, so the comparison is unequal and the test sets no longer measure generalization to unseen data. Any improvement could come from tuning on the test data, not from the regularizer. To resolve this I would ask for strength to be chosen on a validation split, for the baselines (including weight decay and early stopping) to receive a comparable tuning budget, and for at least five seeds per cell with intervals or paired differences. If gains persist, the claim could be restated for the tested settings.
rubric: States the claim precisely and notes that "consistently" outruns six single-run comparisons.
rubric: Identifies tuning on the test set as a flaw that invalidates the test measurement, and the unequal baseline tuning as a separate flaw.
rubric: Notes the missing variability across seeds.
rubric: Proposes concrete evidence (validation-based tuning, equal budgets, multiple seeds with intervals) and keeps the tone factual.

@quiz num
prompt: A model scores 80% accuracy on a test set of 400 independent items. Using $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$, what is the standard error of that accuracy, in percentage points? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 2.0
tol: 0.01
unit: points
check: (0.8 * 0.2 / 400) ** 0.5 * 100
hint: Here $\hat{p} = 0.8$ and $n = 400$; compute $\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n$ first, then take the square root, then convert to points.
solution: $0.8 \times 0.2 / 400 = 0.0004$, whose square root is $0.02$, that is 2.0 percentage points. A claimed gain of 0.5 points on such a test set is well inside this sampling noise.
