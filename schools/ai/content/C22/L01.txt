@article
A tutoring program is offered to six students. Three of them sign up, and their exam scores average 78.3; the other three average 60.0. The program looks worth 18 points. But the students who signed up may have been the more motivated ones, who would have scored higher anyway. Data alone cannot say which story is right: we need a language that separates "what we observed" from "what would have happened if things had been different."

### Association versus causation

An **associational** statement describes how the variables are related in the population we observed: "students who used tutoring scored higher on average," or "the correlation between tutoring and score is 0.7." A **causal** statement describes what would happen under an intervention: "if every student were tutored, the average score would be higher by 8 points than if none were." The first is computed from data by averaging. The second concerns a world we did not observe. The gap between them is the subject of this course.

### Potential outcomes

The **potential outcomes** framework (Rubin, 1974, building on earlier work by Neyman) gives each unit $i$ (a person, a user, a hospital) a treatment $T_i \in \{0, 1\}$ and two **potential outcomes**:

- $Y_i(1)$: the outcome unit $i$ would have if it received treatment,
- $Y_i(0)$: the outcome unit $i$ would have if it did not.

The **individual causal effect** is $Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$. Take six students, listing both potential exam scores (a table only an all-knowing observer could write):

| student | $Y(0)$ | $Y(1)$ | effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 50 | 60 | 10 |
| B | 60 | 65 | 5 |
| C | 70 | 70 | 0 |
| D | 80 | 90 | 10 |
| E | 55 | 70 | 15 |
| F | 65 | 75 | 10 |

The average of the six effects is $50/6 \approx 8.33$. Now suppose the stronger students D, E and F sign up. The treated group's observed scores are $Y(1)$ for D, E, F: 90, 70, 75, mean 78.3. The untreated group's observed scores are $Y(0)$ for A, B, C: 50, 60, 70, mean 60.0. The observed difference is $18.3$, more than double the true average effect of $8.3$, because the two groups differed before treatment.

### Linking potential outcomes to data

We only ever see one potential outcome per unit. Two assumptions connect the table to the data.

**Consistency.** If unit $i$ receives treatment $t$, its observed outcome equals the potential outcome for that treatment:

$$Y_i = T_i\, Y_i(1) + (1 - T_i)\, Y_i(0).$$

Consistency requires the treatment to be well defined: if "tutoring" can mean a weekly hour or a daily hour, there are different versions of treatment, and $Y(1)$ is not one number.

**SUTVA** (the stable unit treatment value assumption, from Rubin) has two parts: there is **no interference**, meaning unit $i$'s outcome depends only on its own treatment and not on others' treatments, and there are **no hidden versions** of treatment (the part consistency also uses). Interference is plausible for vaccines (my vaccination protects you), for marketplace discounts that shift demand among sellers, and for social-network features. When SUTVA fails, $Y_i(t)$ must be indexed by everyone's treatments, and the simple two-column table is no longer enough.

### Causal estimands

A **causal estimand** is a quantity defined in terms of potential outcomes, such as the **average treatment effect** (ATE)

$$\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)].$$

An **estimator** is a recipe applied to observed data that tries to learn it. A comparison of treated and untreated means is an estimator; whether it is a good estimator of the ATE is the question the next weeks answer. Naming the estimand before choosing the estimator keeps us honest about what we are claiming.

@example
title: Reading a table of potential outcomes
problem: Four units have $(Y(0), Y(1))$ equal to P: $(3, 5)$, Q: $(4, 4)$, R: $(2, 6)$, S: $(5, 9)$. Units P and S are treated; Q and R are not. Find the observed outcomes, the individual effects, and the difference in observed group means.
step: By consistency, treated units show $Y(1)$ and untreated units show $Y(0)$: P shows 5, Q shows 4, R shows 2, S shows 9.
step: The individual effects are $5 - 3 = 2$, $4 - 4 = 0$, $6 - 2 = 4$ and $9 - 5 = 4$, so the average treatment effect is $10/4 = 2.5$.
step: The observed treated mean is $(5 + 9)/2 = 7$ and the observed untreated mean is $(4 + 2)/2 = 3$, so the difference is $4$.
step: The difference of 4 overstates the effect of 2.5: the treated units had an average untreated outcome of $(3 + 5)/2 = 4$ while the untreated units had $(4 + 2)/2 = 3$, so the groups differed at baseline by 1. The observed difference of 4 is the mean effect among the treated units (P and S: $(2 + 4)/2 = 3$) plus this baseline gap of 1, so it mixes the effect with a pre-existing difference.
answer: Observed outcomes 5, 4, 2, 9; effects 2, 0, 4, 4; ATE 2.5; observed difference 4.

@mistakes
- Treating the observed difference between groups as the causal effect. It equals the effect only if the groups would have had the same outcomes without treatment.
- Writing $Y_i(1)$ for a unit that was untreated as if it had been measured. It is a counterfactual and is never observed for that unit.
- Forgetting that SUTVA is an assumption about the whole experiment: a coupon given to one user can change what a neighboring user buys.

@practice mc
prompt: A store gives a discount coupon to a random half of its customers. Because the store has limited stock, customers who get coupons buy items that other customers then cannot buy. Which assumption is in doubt?
choice*: No interference (part of SUTVA): one customer's outcome depends on other customers' treatments.
choice: Consistency, because coupons come in different dollar amounts for different customers.
choice: Randomization, because only half the customers got coupons.
choice: Heterogeneity, because customers differ in how much they like discounts.
hint: Ask whether a customer's purchase depends only on her own coupon.
solution: Limited stock couples customers' outcomes, so $Y_i$ depends on others' coupons: that is interference, which violates SUTVA. Differences in taste across customers are allowed, and randomizing half the customers is not an assumption failure.

@practice num
prompt: Five units have $(Y(0), Y(1), T)$ equal to $(10, 14, 1)$, $(12, 15, 1)$, $(20, 22, 0)$, $(18, 19, 0)$ and $(9, 13, 0)$. Compute the difference in observed group means minus the true ATE, to two decimal places.
answer: -3.97
tol: 0.01
check: ((14 + 15) / 2 - (20 + 18 + 9) / 3) - ((14-10) + (15-12) + (22-20) + (19-18) + (13-9)) / 5

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement is the consistency assumption?
choice*: If unit $i$ receives treatment $t$, its observed outcome equals its potential outcome $Y_i(t)$.
choice: Treatment is assigned independently of the potential outcomes.
choice: Every unit has a positive probability of receiving each treatment.
choice: Treatment effects are the same for every unit.
hint: Think of the equation $Y = T\,Y(1) + (1 - T)\,Y(0)$.
solution: Consistency ties observed outcomes to potential outcomes. Independence of assignment is exchangeability, a positive probability for each treatment is positivity, and equal effects is a much stronger homogeneity assumption that is not required.

@quiz num

answer: 400
check: 200 * 3 - 200

@quiz self
prompt: A health blog reports that people who take a daily vitamin supplement are healthier than people who do not, and concludes that supplements improve health. Using potential outcomes, explain why the conclusion does not follow and what would have to be true for it to follow.
hint: Write the observed difference in terms of $Y(0)$ for the two groups.
solution: The reported difference compares the observed health of supplement takers, who show $Y(1)$, with that of non-takers, who show $Y(0)$. It estimates the average effect only if takers would have had the same health as non-takers had they not taken supplements, that is, if the two groups have the same average $Y(0)$. People who take supplements may also exercise more, smoke less and see doctors more, so their $Y(0)$ is probably higher, and part of the gap is not caused by the supplement. For the conclusion to follow we would need the groups to be comparable in their untreated outcomes, for example through randomization, or after adjusting for all common causes, together with consistency and SUTVA.
rubric: Writes the observed difference as a comparison of $Y(1)$ among the treated with $Y(0)$ among the untreated.
rubric: Names a plausible reason the groups differ in $Y(0)$ (a common cause such as healthy habits).
rubric: States a condition under which the comparison would be causal (comparable untreated outcomes, by randomization or adjustment).
