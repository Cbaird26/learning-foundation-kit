Stratifying on one binary covariate is easy. With twenty covariates, some continuous, the number of strata explodes and most cells are empty. Rosenbaum and Rubin (1983) showed that we can summarize all covariates in a single number, the probability of treatment.

### The propensity score

The **propensity score** is $e(\mathbf{z}) = P(T = 1 \mid \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{z})$. **Balancing theorem (Rosenbaum and Rubin, 1983).** If $\mathbf{Z}$ satisfies conditional exchangeability, $Y(t) \perp T \mid \mathbf{Z}$, and positivity holds, then $Y(t) \perp T \mid e(\mathbf{Z})$. Proof sketch: given $e(\mathbf{Z})$, $P(T = 1 \mid Y(0), Y(1), e(\mathbf{Z})) = \mathbb{E}[P(T=1 \mid Y(0), Y(1), \mathbf{Z}) \mid Y(0), Y(1), e(\mathbf{Z})] = \mathbb{E}[e(\mathbf{Z}) \mid \dots] = e(\mathbf{Z})$, which does not depend on the potential outcomes. So adjusting for the scalar $e(\mathbf{Z})$ suffices whenever adjusting for $\mathbf{Z}$ does. In practice $e$ is unknown and is estimated, often by logistic regression of $T$ on $\mathbf{Z}$.

### Inverse probability weighting

The idea is to build a **pseudo-population** in which treatment is independent of $\mathbf{Z}$, by weighting each unit by the inverse of the probability of the treatment it actually received. Under consistency, exchangeability given $\mathbf{Z}$, positivity and the true propensity score $e$,

$$\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{T\,Y}{e(\mathbf{Z})}\right] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)],$$

because $\mathbb{E}\big[T Y(1)/e(\mathbf{Z}) \mid \mathbf{Z}\big] = \mathbb{E}[T \mid \mathbf{Z}]\,\mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid \mathbf{Z}]/e(\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid \mathbf{Z}]$ (the factorization uses exchangeability), and then we average over $\mathbf{Z}$. Similarly for $Y(0)$ with weight $1/(1 - e)$. The **Horvitz–Thompson IPW estimator** is

$$\hat\tau_{\text{IPW}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{T_i Y_i}{e_i} - \frac{(1 - T_i) Y_i}{1 - e_i}\right].$$

A common variant, the **Hájek** (normalized) estimator, divides each weighted sum by the sum of its weights, so it is a difference of weighted means and lies within the range of the observed outcomes:

$$\hat\tau_{\text{Hájek}} = \frac{\sum_i T_i Y_i / e_i}{\sum_i T_i / e_i} - \frac{\sum_i (1 - T_i) Y_i/(1 - e_i)}{\sum_i (1 - T_i)/(1 - e_i)}.$$

The Horvitz–Thompson estimator is unbiased when the true propensities are used. The Hájek estimator is a ratio of random quantities, so it is not unbiased in finite samples, but it is consistent, and it is often less variable when some weights are large.

Eight patients, with covariate $Z$ (binary) and known propensities $e(0) = 0.25$, $e(1) = 0.75$: in $Z = 0$ one of four is treated (outcome 7; the three untreated have 5, 4, 6); in $Z = 1$ three of four are treated (outcomes 9, 10, 8; the untreated one has 6). The stratified ATE is $0.5(7 - 5) + 0.5(9 - 6) = 2.5$. The IPW estimate: treated terms sum to $7/0.25 + (9 + 10 + 8)/0.75 = 28 + 36 = 64$, untreated terms to $(5 + 4 + 6)/0.75 + 6/0.25 = 20 + 24 = 44$, so $\hat\tau_{\text{IPW}} = (64 - 44)/8 = 2.5$, the same here.

```python
def ipw_ate(rows):
    """rows: list of (t, y, e). Horvitz-Thompson IPW estimate of the ATE."""
    total = 0.0
    for t, y, e in rows:
        total += t * y / e - (1 - t) * y / (1 - e)
    return total / len(rows)

rows = [(1, 7, .25), (0, 5, .25), (0, 4, .25), (0, 6, .25),
        (1, 9, .75), (1, 10, .75), (1, 8, .75), (0, 6, .75)]
print(ipw_ate(rows))
```

This prints `2.5`.

### Positivity, extreme weights and trimming

A unit with $e = 0.01$ who was treated has weight 100: a single observation can dominate the estimate and its variance explodes. Remedies are **trimming** (dropping units with $e$ outside, say, $[0.1, 0.9]$, which changes the estimand to the effect in the remaining population), **stabilized** or Hájek weights, and, most importantly, asking whether comparable untreated units exist at all (the overlap or positivity problem). Weights are correct only if the propensity model is correct: a misspecified $e$ biases the estimate.

### Matching

**Nearest-neighbor matching** pairs each treated unit with the untreated unit with the closest propensity score (optionally within a maximum distance, a **caliper**, and with or without replacement), and averages the outcome differences of the pairs. It estimates the ATT (the effect on the treated, for whom matches are found). Matching and weighting are two ways to make the groups comparable.

### Checking balance

A good propensity model should balance covariates in the weighted (or matched) sample. For a covariate with group means $\bar{z}_1, \bar{z}_0$ and variances $s_1^2, s_0^2$, the **standardized mean difference** is

$$\text{SMD} = \frac{\bar{z}_1 - \bar{z}_0}{\sqrt{(s_1^2 + s_0^2)/2}}.$$

A common rule of thumb treats $|\text{SMD}| < 0.1$ after adjustment as adequate. Balance can only be checked on measured covariates, and good balance does not prove there is no unmeasured confounding.

@example
title: IPW by hand
problem: Four patients have $(T, Y, e)$ equal to $(1, 10, 0.5)$, $(0, 6, 0.5)$, $(1, 8, 0.25)$ and $(0, 5, 0.75)$. Compute the Horvitz–Thompson IPW estimate of the ATE.
step: Treated terms $T Y/e$: $10/0.5 = 20$ and $8/0.25 = 32$, which sum to $52$.
step: Untreated terms $(1 - T) Y/(1 - e)$: $6/0.5 = 12$ and $5/0.25 = 20$, which sum to $32$.
step: $\hat\tau_{\text{IPW}} = (52 - 32)/4 = 5$.
step: Note that the weights of the treated sum to $2 + 4 = 6$, not 4. The Hájek version divides by the weight totals: the treated mean is $52/6 \approx 8.667$; the untreated weights are $1/0.5 = 2$ and $1/0.25 = 4$, which also sum to 6, so the untreated mean is $32/6 \approx 5.333$ and $\hat\tau_{\text{Hájek}} \approx 3.333$.
answer: $\hat\tau_{\text{IPW}} = 5$ and $\hat\tau_{\text{Hájek}} \approx 3.33$.

@example
title: Nearest-neighbor matching
problem: Two treated units have propensity scores 0.6 and 0.8 and outcomes 10 and 14. The untreated pool has propensity scores 0.3, 0.55, 0.75, 0.9 with outcomes 4, 7, 9, 12. Match each treated unit to its nearest untreated unit without replacement and estimate the ATT.
step: Treated unit with $e = 0.6$: distances to the pool are $0.3, 0.05, 0.15, 0.3$; the nearest is $0.55$ (outcome 7).
step: Treated unit with $e = 0.8$: distances are $0.5, 0.25, 0.05, 0.1$; the nearest is $0.75$ (outcome 9), which is still available.
step: The pair differences are $10 - 7 = 3$ and $14 - 9 = 5$, so the estimated ATT is $(3 + 5)/2 = 4$.
answer: The matched estimate of the ATT is $4$.

@mistakes
- Using the propensity score as a prediction target to maximize: its purpose is balance, not classification accuracy.
- Ignoring extreme weights. A few units with $e$ near 0 or 1 can drive the whole estimate.
- Believing that good balance on measured covariates removes unmeasured confounding.
- Weighting treated units by $1/(1 - e)$ or untreated units by $1/e$: each unit gets the inverse probability of the treatment it received.

```python
def hajek_ate(rows):
    """Normalized IPW estimate of the ATE from (t, y, e) rows."""
    num1 = den1 = num0 = den0 = 0.0
    for t, y, e in rows:
        if t == 1:
            num1 += y / e
            den1 += 1 / e
        else:
            num0 += y / (1 - e)
            den0 += 1 / (1 - e)
    return num1 / den1 - num0 / den0
```
hint: Accumulate, for each arm, the sum of weight times outcome and the sum of weights; the estimate is the ratio for treated minus the ratio for untreated.
solution: Each arm's estimate is a weighted mean: the weighted sum of outcomes divided by the sum of weights. The third test has weights that sum to different totals than $n$, so the Horvitz–Thompson estimator (which gives $1.0$ there) fails it.

model:
```python
def propensity_by_stratum(rows):
    """Fraction of treated units in each stratum."""
    counts = {}
    for z, t in rows:
        n, k = counts.get(z, (0, 0))
        counts[z] = (n + 1, k + t)
    return {z: k / n for z, (n, k) in counts.items()}
```
hint: Keep, for each stratum, the number of units and the number treated, then divide.
solution: One pass collects the pair (units, treated) per stratum; the propensity score is the ratio. The fourth test has a stratum with no treated units, whose propensity score of 0.0 shows a positivity violation that IPW could not handle.

@quiz self
prompt: An analyst estimates propensity scores for 5,000 users and finds that 40 treated users have scores below 0.02. The IPW estimate of the ATE changes by 30% when those 40 users are dropped. Explain what is going on, and what the analyst should do and report.
hint: Compute the weights of those users, and think about overlap.
solution: Treated users with $e < 0.02$ have weights above 50, so 40 users carry the influence of thousands of observations; the estimate is then driven by a few units and has high variance, and any error in the propensity model near 0 is amplified. This also signals a positivity problem: for such users there are almost no comparable untreated users, so the data say little about their potential outcomes. The analyst should check overlap (for example, a table of score ranges by arm), consider trimming or restricting to the region of overlap (reporting that the estimand is now the effect in that population), try normalized (Hájek) weights, and report both estimates and the sensitivity of the result to the trimming rule.
