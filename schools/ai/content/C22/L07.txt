@article
A hospital compares two procedures on 100 patients, split by whether the case was low-risk ($Z = 0$) or high-risk ($Z = 1$). The numbers below are average recovery scores (higher is better) with the number of patients in each cell.

| risk | procedure | patients | mean score |
|---|---|---|---|
| low ($Z = 0$) | new ($T = 1$) | 10 | 61 |
| low | standard ($T = 0$) | 50 | 55 |
| high ($Z = 1$) | new | 30 | 44 |
| high | standard | 10 | 40 |

Overall, the new procedure's patients average $(10 \cdot 61 + 30 \cdot 44)/40 = 48.25$ and the standard procedure's average $(50 \cdot 55 + 10 \cdot 40)/60 = 52.5$: the new procedure looks worse by 4.25. Within each risk group it is better, by 6 and by 4. The reversal is called **Simpson's paradox**. It arises because the new procedure is mostly used on high-risk patients, who score lower under either procedure.

### Standardization

Assume, for a covariate set $Z$:

- **consistency** and SUTVA (last weeks),
- **conditional exchangeability** (no unmeasured confounding): $Y(t) \perp T \mid Z$ for $t = 0, 1$, which holds when $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion,
- **positivity**: $0 < P(T = 1 \mid Z = z) < 1$ for every $z$ with $P(Z = z) > 0$.

Then $\mathbb{E}[Y(t)] = \sum_z \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = t, Z = z]\,P(Z = z)$, because within a stratum $\mathbb{E}[Y(t) \mid Z = z] = \mathbb{E}[Y(t) \mid T = t, Z = z] = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = t, Z = z]$ (exchangeability, then consistency), and we average over the population distribution of $Z$. The **stratified (standardized) estimator** of the ATE is

$$\hat\tau_{\text{strat}} = \sum_z \frac{n_z}{n}\left(\bar{Y}_{1z} - \bar{Y}_{0z}\right),$$

where $n_z$ is the size of stratum $z$ and $\bar{Y}_{tz}$ the mean outcome of units with treatment $t$ in it. For the hospital: $0.6(6) + 0.4(4) = 5.2$. Weighting by treated counts instead gives the ATT: $0.25(6) + 0.75(4) = 4.5$.

Positivity matters in practice: if a stratum has no treated units, $\bar{Y}_{1z}$ does not exist, and nothing in the data says what the treatment would do there. Any estimate must either drop that stratum (changing the estimand) or extrapolate by modeling assumptions.

### Regression adjustment

A common alternative fits the model $Y = \alpha + \tau T + \gamma Z + \varepsilon$ by least squares and reads off $\hat\tau$. If the model is correct and the effect is the same in every stratum, $\hat\tau$ estimates the ATE. If effects differ across strata, a fact about least squares (Frisch–Waugh) says $\hat\tau$ is a weighted average of the stratum effects with weights proportional to $n_z\,p_z(1 - p_z)$, where $p_z$ is the fraction treated in stratum $z$. This is not the ATE unless the weights match the stratum sizes: strata where treatment is nearly balanced count most. In the hospital data, $n_z p_z (1 - p_z)$ is $60 \cdot \tfrac16 \cdot \tfrac56 = 8.33$ for $Z=0$ and $40 \cdot 0.75 \cdot 0.25 = 7.5$ for $Z=1$, so $\hat\tau = (8.33 \cdot 6 + 7.5 \cdot 4)/15.83 = 5.05$, not 5.2. To estimate the ATE with regression, include treatment-by-covariate interactions, predict $\hat{Y}(1)$ and $\hat{Y}(0)$ for every unit, and average the difference.

### Adjustment is not free

Whether to stratify depends on the causal graph, not on the data. In the hospital data, if risk $Z$ is a *cause* of procedure choice and of recovery ($Z \to T$, $Z \to Y$), stratifying is correct: the answer is 5.2. But if the "stratifying variable" were instead an outcome of the procedure (say, "needs a second operation", a mediator: $T \to Z \to Y$), then the same table would call for the opposite conclusion, since adjusting removes the effect that flows through $Z$. Simpson's paradox is not resolved by arithmetic: the same table needs different analyses under different graphs.

Adjustment also cannot fix **unmeasured confounding**: if an important common cause $U$ is not in $Z$, conditional exchangeability fails and no amount of regression repairs it. The honest response is a sensitivity analysis (project week) or a different design.

@example
title: A stratified estimate
problem: Low-risk patients ($n = 70$): the 14 treated average 8.0 and the 56 untreated average 6.0. High-risk patients ($n = 30$): the 24 treated average 5.0 and the 6 untreated average 4.0. Compute the naive difference and the stratified ATE, assuming the risk group is a sufficient adjustment set.
step: The naive treated mean is $(14 \cdot 8 + 24 \cdot 5)/38 = 232/38 \approx 6.105$ and the naive untreated mean is $(56 \cdot 6 + 6 \cdot 4)/62 = 360/62 \approx 5.806$, so the naive difference is $\approx 0.30$.
step: The stratum effects are $8 - 6 = 2$ (low risk) and $5 - 4 = 1$ (high risk).
step: The stratum weights are $70/100 = 0.7$ and $30/100 = 0.3$, so $\hat\tau_{\text{strat}} = 0.7(2) + 0.3(1) = 1.7$.
answer: The stratified ATE is $1.7$, against a naive difference of about $0.30$.

@example
title: What a regression coefficient estimates
problem: Stratum $Z=0$ has 40 patients, half of them treated, with effect 3. Stratum $Z=1$ has 60 patients, 10% of them treated, with effect 7. Compare the ATE with the weighted average of stratum effects that the regression coefficient on $T$ estimates.
step: The ATE weights strata by size: $0.4(3) + 0.6(7) = 5.4$.
step: The regression weights are $n_z p_z (1 - p_z)$: $40 \cdot 0.5 \cdot 0.5 = 10$ for $Z=0$ and $60 \cdot 0.1 \cdot 0.9 = 5.4$ for $Z=1$.
step: Normalizing, $\hat\tau \to (10 \cdot 3 + 5.4 \cdot 7)/15.4 \approx 4.40$.
step: The coefficient is lower than the ATE because the stratum with an almost untreated population gets little weight in the regression.
answer: ATE $= 5.4$; the regression coefficient estimates about $4.40$.

@mistakes
- Treating Simpson's paradox as a rule that "the stratified answer is always right." The right analysis depends on the causal graph (confounder versus mediator or collider).
- Adjusting for a mediator or a collider to "control for more variables."
- Reading the coefficient on $T$ in a main-effects regression as the ATE when effects vary across strata.
- Stratifying on so many covariates that some strata have no treated or untreated units, which violates positivity in the data at hand.

@practice num
prompt: Stratum 0 has 50 units, 25 of them treated, and a treatment effect of 2. Stratum 1 has 50 units, 5 of them treated, and a treatment effect of 6. A regression of the outcome on $T$ and a stratum indicator (no interaction) estimates a weighted average of the stratum effects with weights proportional to $n_z p_z (1 - p_z)$. What does the coefficient on $T$ estimate? Give three decimal places.
answer: 3.059
tol: 0.001
check: (2 * 50 * 0.5 * 0.5 + 6 * 50 * 0.1 * 0.9) / (50 * 0.5 * 0.5 + 50 * 0.1 * 0.9)
hint: Compute $n_z p_z (1 - p_z)$ for each stratum, normalize to weights, and average the effects 2 and 6.
solution: The raw weights are $50 \cdot 0.25 = 12.5$ and $50 \cdot 0.09 = 4.5$. So the estimand is $(12.5 \cdot 2 + 4.5 \cdot 6)/17 = 52/17 \approx 3.059$, below the ATE of 4 because the stratum with the larger effect is rarely treated.

@practice mc
prompt: A program $X$ raises exercise $M$ ($X \to M$), and exercise lowers blood pressure $Y$ ($M \to Y$). There is also a direct effect $X \to Y$. An analyst stratifies by exercise level $M$ and compares the treated and untreated within each level. What does this estimate?
choice*: Only the part of the effect of $X$ on $Y$ that does not pass through $M$, and then only under further assumptions; it is not the total effect.
choice: The total effect of $X$ on $Y$, since $M$ is a pre-treatment variable.
choice: Nothing at all, because $M$ is a collider.
choice: The ATE, as long as every stratum has treated and untreated units.
hint: $M$ is a descendant of $X$ lying on one of the causal paths.

answer: 2.4

@quiz mc
prompt: A regression of $Y$ on $T$ and a covariate $Z$ with no interaction gives the ATE when which conditions hold?
choice*: $Z$ is a sufficient adjustment set, the outcome model is correct, and the treatment effect does not vary with $Z$.
choice: $Z$ is correlated with $Y$, whatever its relation to $T$.
choice: The treatment is positively correlated with $Y$.
choice: $Z$ includes every variable measured after treatment.
hint: Think of the three ingredients: identification, the model, and the weights.
solution: The coefficient identifies a causal quantity only if $Z$ removes confounding (backdoor), it equals the ATE when the model is correct and the effect is the same across strata (otherwise it is a weighted average of stratum effects). Post-treatment variables can block causal paths or open spurious ones.

@quiz self
prompt: A table shows that a drug has a lower recovery rate than no drug overall, but a higher rate within each of two severity groups. Explain how the same table could call for opposite conclusions depending on the causal story, and say what additional information a researcher needs.
hint: Compare severity as a common cause of drug and recovery with severity as an effect of the drug.
solution: If severity is measured before treatment and influences both who is treated and recovery, it is a confounder, and the stratified result (the drug helps) is the causal one: the aggregate is distorted because the drug goes mostly to severe patients. If instead "severity" is measured after treatment and is affected by the drug (for example a complication the drug causes or prevents), it is a mediator, and conditioning on it blocks part of the drug's effect; then the aggregate comparison, free of that conditioning, is the right total effect. The table cannot tell these cases apart; the researcher needs the time order of the variables, knowledge of the mechanism, and a causal graph defending which variables are causes of which, along with a check for unmeasured confounding.
rubric: Describes the confounder reading (pre-treatment common cause) and concludes the stratified result is causal.
rubric: Describes the mediator or collider reading (post-treatment) and concludes the aggregate is the correct total effect.
rubric: States that the data alone cannot decide, and that time order and subject-matter knowledge (a causal graph) are needed.
