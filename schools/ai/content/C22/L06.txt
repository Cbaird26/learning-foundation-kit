@article
Suppose a drug $X$ is given more often to patients with a severe condition $Z$, and severe patients also do worse: $Z \to X$, $Z \to Y$, and $X \to Y$ (the drug may help). The raw comparison of treated and untreated patients mixes the drug's effect with the effect of severity. The question is which variables must be adjusted for to separate them, and which adjustments make things worse.

### Intervening versus observing

The conditional probability $P(y \mid x)$ describes patients who happened to have $X = x$. Pearl's **do-operator** describes an intervention: $P(y \mid do(x))$ is the distribution of $Y$ if we set $X = x$ for everyone, whatever their other circumstances. In a graph, $do(x)$ deletes all edges into $X$ (the intervention overrides $X$'s usual causes) and fixes $X = x$. Writing the model's factorization and dropping the factor $P(x \mid \mathrm{pa}(x))$ gives the **truncated factorization**. In potential-outcome terms, $P(y \mid do(x)) = P(Y(x) = y)$, so the average effect is $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X=1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid do(X=0)]$.

### Confounding and the adjustment formula

For the graph $Z \to X$, $Z \to Y$, $X \to Y$ the factorization is $P(z, x, y) = P(z)\,P(x \mid z)\,P(y \mid x, z)$. Removing $P(x \mid z)$ and fixing $X = x$ gives $P(z, y \mid do(x)) = P(z)\,P(y \mid x, z)$, and summing over $z$:

$$P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z).$$

This is **standardization** (the **adjustment formula**): average the stratum-specific outcome distributions using the *population* distribution of $Z$ and not the distribution of $Z$ among those with $X = x$. The discrepancy between $P(y \mid x)$ and $P(y \mid do(x))$ is **confounding**.

Numbers: $P(Z=1) = 0.4$; $P(X=1 \mid Z=1) = 0.8$, $P(X=1 \mid Z=0) = 0.25$; $P(Y=1 \mid x, z) = 0.6, 0.3, 0.4, 0.1$ for $(x, z) = (1,1), (1,0), (0,1), (0,0)$. Then

$$P(Y=1 \mid do(X=1)) = 0.4(0.6) + 0.6(0.3) = 0.42, \quad P(Y=1 \mid do(X=0)) = 0.4(0.4) + 0.6(0.1) = 0.22,$$

a causal risk difference of $0.20$. The raw conditional probabilities are $P(Y=1 \mid X=1) = 0.504$ and $P(Y=1 \mid X=0) = 0.145$, a difference of $0.359$: the treated are disproportionately in the high-risk group $Z = 1$ (68% of the treated versus 15% of the untreated).

### The backdoor criterion

A **backdoor path** from $X$ to $Y$ is a path that starts with an arrow *into* $X$. A set $Z$ satisfies the **backdoor criterion** relative to $(X, Y)$ if

1. no node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and
2. $Z$ blocks (in the sense of d-separation) every backdoor path from $X$ to $Y$.

**Theorem (Pearl).** If $Z$ satisfies the backdoor criterion and $P$ is Markov to the graph, with $P(x, z) > 0$ wherever needed (positivity), then $P(y \mid do(x)) = \sum_z P(y \mid x, z)\,P(z)$. Equivalently, $Y(x) \perp X \mid Z$ (conditional exchangeability).

Condition 1 prevents us from blocking part of the effect we want or from opening paths; condition 2 removes the non-causal association. The criterion is sufficient, not necessary: other identification strategies exist (for instance, the front-door criterion).

### Three roles a variable can play

- A **confounder** is a common cause (or lies on a backdoor path as a fork): adjust for it, or for something that blocks the path.
- A **mediator** $X \to M \to Y$ carries part of the effect. Adjusting for it removes that part, so you estimate only the effect not through $M$, not the total effect. It is also a descendant of $X$, violating condition 1.
- A **collider** on a path is closed by default. Adjusting for it (or its descendant) opens the path. **M-bias**: with $U_1 \to X$, $U_1 \to C$, $U_2 \to C$, $U_2 \to Y$ and $X \to Y$, the variable $C$ may look like a sensible pre-treatment variable to adjust for, but the empty set is valid and $\{C\}$ is not, since it opens $X \leftarrow U_1 \to C \leftarrow U_2 \to Y$.

"Adjust for every pre-treatment variable" is therefore not a safe rule; the graph decides.

@example
title: Applying the adjustment formula
problem: $P(Z=1) = 0.5$. In the stratum $Z = 1$: $P(Y=1 \mid X=1, Z=1) = 0.7$, $P(Y=1 \mid X=0, Z=1) = 0.6$. In the stratum $Z = 0$: $P(Y=1 \mid X=1, Z=0) = 0.4$, $P(Y=1 \mid X=0, Z=0) = 0.3$. $P(X=1 \mid Z=1) = 0.9$ and $P(X=1 \mid Z=0) = 0.1$. $Z$ is a confounder. Compute the causal risk difference and the raw difference.
step: Adjustment formula: $P(Y=1 \mid do(X=1)) = 0.5(0.7) + 0.5(0.4) = 0.55$ and $P(Y=1 \mid do(X=0)) = 0.5(0.6) + 0.5(0.3) = 0.45$. The causal risk difference is $0.10$.
step: Among the treated, $P(Z=1 \mid X=1) = 0.5 \cdot 0.9/(0.5 \cdot 0.9 + 0.5 \cdot 0.1) = 0.9$, so $P(Y=1 \mid X=1) = 0.9(0.7) + 0.1(0.4) = 0.67$.
step: Among the untreated, $P(Z=1 \mid X=0) = 0.5 \cdot 0.1/0.5 = 0.1$, so $P(Y=1 \mid X=0) = 0.1(0.6) + 0.9(0.3) = 0.33$.
step: The raw difference is $0.67 - 0.33 = 0.34$, more than three times the causal effect of $0.10$.
answer: Causal risk difference $0.10$; raw difference $0.34$.

@example
title: Finding the valid adjustment sets
problem: A graph has edges $A \to X$, $A \to B$, $B \to Y$, $X \to M$, $M \to Y$, $X \to C$ and $Y \to C$. List the valid backdoor adjustment sets for the effect of $X$ on $Y$.
step: Backdoor paths start with an arrow into $X$. The only edge into $X$ is $A \to X$, giving the path $X \leftarrow A \to B \to Y$. The paths $X \to M \to Y$ and $X \to C \leftarrow Y$ start with an arrow out of $X$, so they are not backdoor paths.
step: Descendants of $X$ are $M$, $C$ and $Y$; none may be in the set. $C$ is also a collider on the non-backdoor path $X \to C \leftarrow Y$, and conditioning on it would open that path.
step: To block $X \leftarrow A \to B \to Y$, condition on $A$ (a fork) or on $B$ (a chain). The empty set leaves it open.
answer: The valid sets are $\{A\}$, $\{B\}$ and $\{A, B\}$.

@mistakes
- Adjusting for all pre-treatment variables regardless of the graph. A collider that is a pre-treatment variable (M-bias) creates bias when adjusted for.
- Adjusting for a mediator and calling the result the total effect.
- Averaging the stratum-specific outcomes with the distribution of $Z$ among the treated or untreated instead of the population distribution $P(z)$.
- Forgetting positivity: if some stratum of $Z$ contains no treated units, $P(y \mid x, z)$ is undefined there.

@practice mc
prompt: Variable $C$ is caused by both the treatment $X$ and the outcome $Y$ (edges $X \to C$ and $Y \to C$), and we want the total effect of $X$ on $Y$. What does adjusting for $C$ do?
choice*: It opens the path $X \to C \leftarrow Y$ and introduces bias, so it should not be adjusted for.
choice: It removes confounding, because $C$ is associated with both $X$ and $Y$.
choice: It has no effect, because $C$ is not on a backdoor path.
choice: It blocks the causal path from $X$ to $Y$ and gives the direct effect.
hint: $C$ is a collider and a descendant of $X$.
solution: $C$ is a collider, so conditioning on it creates an association between $X$ and $Y$ that is not causal (and $C$ is a descendant of $X$, violating the backdoor criterion's first condition). Being associated with both $X$ and $Y$ does not make a variable a confounder; a confounder is a cause of both (or lies on a backdoor path).

@practice self
prompt: For each of four graphs, list every valid backdoor adjustment set for the effect of $X$ on $Y$ and say which variables should never be adjusted for. Graph 1: edges $Z \to X$, $Z \to Y$, $X \to Y$. Graph 2: edges $X \to M$, $M \to Y$, $X \to Y$. Graph 3: edges $U_1 \to X$, $U_1 \to C$, $U_2 \to C$, $U_2 \to Y$, $X \to Y$. Graph 4: edges $W \to Z$, $Z \to X$, $W \to Y$, $Z \to Y$, $X \to Y$.
hint: For each graph, list the paths that start with an arrow into $X$ and decide which nodes block them without being descendants of $X$.
solution: Graph 1: the backdoor path is $X \leftarrow Z \to Y$; the only valid set is $\{Z\}$. Graph 2: there is no edge into $X$, hence no backdoor path; the empty set is valid and $M$ must not be adjusted for (a mediator and a descendant of $X$). Graph 3: the backdoor path $X \leftarrow U_1 \to C \leftarrow U_2 \to Y$ is blocked at the collider $C$; valid sets are $\varnothing$, $\{U_1\}$, $\{U_2\}$, $\{U_1, U_2\}$, $\{U_1, C\}$, $\{U_2, C\}$ and $\{U_1, U_2, C\}$, while $\{C\}$ alone is invalid because it opens the path. Graph 4: the backdoor paths are $X \leftarrow Z \to Y$ and $X \leftarrow Z \leftarrow W \to Y$; $\{Z\}$ blocks both (and $\{W, Z\}$ also works), but $\{W\}$ alone leaves the first open.
rubric: Graphs 1 and 2: identifies $\{Z\}$ and $\varnothing$, and flags the mediator $M$ as not to be adjusted for.
rubric: Graph 3: recognizes the collider $C$, gives $\varnothing$ as valid and $\{C\}$ alone as invalid.
rubric: Graph 4: finds both backdoor paths and gives $\{Z\}$ (and $\{W, Z\}$) while rejecting $\{W\}$.

@quiz mc
prompt: Which statement correctly gives the two conditions of the backdoor criterion for a set $Z$ relative to $(X, Y)$?
choice*: No node in $Z$ is a descendant of $X$, and $Z$ blocks every path between $X$ and $Y$ that starts with an arrow into $X$.
choice: $Z$ contains every variable that is correlated with both $X$ and $Y$, and $Z$ blocks every directed path from $X$ to $Y$.
choice: $Z$ contains all the parents of $Y$, and no node in $Z$ is a parent of $X$.
choice: $Z$ is d-separated from $X$ and from $Y$ by the empty set.
hint: Think about which paths carry non-causal association and which nodes must not be touched.
solution: The criterion has exactly those two conditions: no descendants of $X$, and blocking all backdoor paths. Blocking directed paths would destroy the effect we are estimating, and correlation with both $X$ and $Y$ does not identify confounders.
