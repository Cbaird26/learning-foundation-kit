@article
A city raises its minimum wage while a neighboring city does not. Employment in the first city falls after the change. Did the wage cause the fall, or was there a regional downturn? Comparing before and after in one city mixes the policy with time. Comparing the two cities after mixes it with permanent differences between cities. **Difference-in-differences** (DiD) uses both comparisons at once.

### Difference-in-differences

Let $G = 1$ for the group that is treated in the second period and $G = 0$ for the group that never is, observed in a pre-treatment and a post-treatment period. The DiD estimand and estimator are

$$\tau_{\text{DiD}} = \big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}} \mid G=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{pre}} \mid G=1]\big) - \big(\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}} \mid G=0] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{pre}} \mid G=0]\big).$$

Example: treated region employment index 50 (pre) and 58 (post); comparison region 48 (pre) and 52 (post). The treated region rose by 8 and the comparison region by 4, so $\hat\tau_{\text{DiD}} = 8 - 4 = 4$.

**Assumptions.** The DiD estimand equals the average effect on the treated (ATT) in the post period when:

- **parallel trends**: absent treatment, the two groups' average outcomes would have changed by the same amount: $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid G=1] = \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid G=0]$;
- **no anticipation**: treatment has no effect in the pre period, so $Y_{\text{pre}}$ of the treated group is its untreated outcome;
- consistency and SUTVA (no spillovers between the groups, and stable composition of the groups).

*Proof.* By consistency and no anticipation, $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}} \mid G=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{pre}} \mid G=1] = \text{ATT} + \mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid G=1]$. The comparison group's change is $\mathbb{E}[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid G=0]$. Subtracting and using parallel trends leaves the ATT. $\square$

Parallel trends concerns a counterfactual and cannot be tested directly. With several pre-treatment periods one can check that the groups moved in parallel before the policy (a **placebo test** or **event study**): if treated 40, 44 and control 30, 34 in two pre periods, the placebo DiD is $(44 - 40) - (34 - 30) = 0$, which supports (but does not prove) parallel trends. The regression $Y = \alpha + \beta G + \gamma\,\text{Post} + \delta\,(G \times \text{Post}) + \varepsilon$ has $\hat\delta$ equal to the DiD estimate in the two-group, two-period case. DiD is only as good as the assumption: if the treated group was already on a steeper path, the estimate is biased by that difference in trends.

```python
def did(rows):
    """rows: list of (group, period, y); group 1 treated, period 1 post."""
    def mean(g, p):
        ys = [y for gg, pp, y in rows if gg == g and pp == p]
        return sum(ys) / len(ys)
    return (mean(1, 1) - mean(1, 0)) - (mean(0, 1) - mean(0, 0))

rows = [(1, 0, 49), (1, 0, 51), (1, 1, 57), (1, 1, 59),
        (0, 0, 47), (0, 0, 49), (0, 1, 51), (0, 1, 53)]
print(did(rows))
```

This prints `4.0`.

### Regression discontinuity

Some treatments are assigned by a threshold on a **running variable** $X$: a scholarship goes to students with a test score of at least 70, a program to counties with poverty rate above a limit. In a **sharp regression discontinuity** (RD) design, $T = \mathbf{1}[X \ge c]$ for a known cutoff $c$. Units just below and just above $c$ are nearly alike in everything except treatment, as if randomized locally.

**Identification.** If $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid X = x]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid X = x]$ are continuous in $x$ at $c$ (**continuity at the cutoff**), then

$$\tau_{\text{RD}} = \lim_{x \downarrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] - \lim_{x \uparrow c} \mathbb{E}[Y \mid X = x] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = c].$$

The effect is local: it is the effect for units *at the cutoff*, and says little about units far from it. The estimate is the jump in the regression function at $c$. **Local linear regression** fits a straight line separately on each side within a **bandwidth** $h$ (using points with $c - h \le x < c$ on the left and $c \le x \le c + h$ on the right) and takes the difference of the two fitted values at $c$. A narrow bandwidth reduces bias from curvature but leaves few points (more variance); comparing the two means directly is biased whenever the outcome trends with $X$.

**Threats.** If units can precisely manipulate $X$ (students retaking a test to cross 70), those just above differ from those just below and continuity fails; a pile-up of observations just above $c$ is a warning sign. Other policies using the same cutoff would also confound the jump. In a **fuzzy** RD, crossing the cutoff only changes the probability of treatment, and the cutoff is used as an instrument, as in week 9.

@example
title: A difference-in-differences estimate and a placebo check
problem: Treated group means over three periods are 40, 44 and 52 (the policy begins in period 3); comparison group means are 30, 34 and 38. Compute the DiD estimate for the policy and a placebo DiD for periods 1 and 2.
step: Policy effect: the treated group changes by $52 - 44 = 8$ and the comparison group by $38 - 34 = 4$, so $\hat\tau = 8 - 4 = 4$.
step: Placebo: treated change $44 - 40 = 4$ and comparison change $34 - 30 = 4$ give a placebo DiD of $0$, consistent with parallel trends before the policy.
step: Interpretation: assuming the groups would have kept moving in parallel, the policy raised the outcome of the treated group by 4 relative to what it would otherwise have been.
answer: $\hat\tau_{\text{DiD}} = 4$, with a placebo estimate of $0$.

@example
title: A local linear regression discontinuity estimate
problem: A scholarship is awarded when a score is at least 70. Outcomes: $(64, 20)$, $(66, 21)$, $(68, 22)$ below the cutoff and $(70, 28)$, $(72, 29)$, $(74, 30)$ above. Estimate the effect at the cutoff with bandwidth $h = 6$.
step: Left of the cutoff, the points lie on the line $y = 20 + 0.5(x - 64)$; at $x = 70$ it predicts $23$.
step: Right of the cutoff, the points lie on $y = 28 + 0.5(x - 70)$; at $x = 70$ it predicts $28$.
step: The estimated jump is $28 - 23 = 5$.
step: Comparing group means would give $29 - 21 = 8$, which wrongly includes the upward trend of 0.5 per point over the gap between the group centers.
answer: $\hat\tau_{\text{RD}} = 5$.

@mistakes
- Comparing the outcome of treated and comparison groups after the policy only: this ignores pre-existing differences. DiD removes them only if trends would have been parallel.
- Reading parallel pre-trends as proof of the assumption. They support it, but the assumption is about the unobserved untreated post-period outcome.
- Taking a regression discontinuity effect as the average effect for everyone. It applies at the cutoff.
- Using a wide bandwidth with a straight line when the relationship with $X$ is curved, which biases the jump.

model:
```python
def fit_line(points):
    """Ordinary least squares intercept and slope."""
    n = len(points)
    mx = sum(x for x, y in points) / n
    my = sum(y for x, y in points) / n
    sxx = sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x, y in points)
    sxy = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in points)
    slope = sxy / sxx
    return my - slope * mx, slope

def rd_estimate(data, c, h):
    """Local linear regression discontinuity estimate at the cutoff c."""
    left = [(x, y) for x, y in data if c - h <= x < c]
    right = [(x, y) for x, y in data if c <= x <= c + h]
    a0, b0 = fit_line(left)
    a1, b1 = fit_line(right)
    return (a1 + b1 * c) - (a0 + b0 * c)
```
hint: Select the points on each side of the cutoff within the bandwidth, fit a least squares line to each side, and evaluate both lines at `c`.
solution: Splitting the data by the cutoff and bandwidth, fitting a line on each side, and extrapolating both lines to $x = c$ gives the jump. The second test has points outside the bandwidth that must be ignored, and the fourth test has a point exactly at the cutoff, which must be assigned to the treated side.

@quiz self
prompt: A school district gives a free tutoring program to students who score below 60 on a placement test (cutoff 60) and compares end-of-year grades just below and just above 60. Explain what causal quantity this design estimates, what assumption it needs, and give two ways it could fail and how you would check each.
hint: Think about who is near the cutoff, and about what teachers or students might do with the score.
solution: The sharp RD design estimates the average effect of tutoring on grades for students scoring right at 60, the jump in expected grades at the cutoff, provided expected untreated and treated grades are continuous in the score at 60. It says little about students far below the cutoff. It can fail if score manipulation occurs (teachers nudging borderline students to the side of the line they prefer, or students retaking the test): check for a pile-up of scores on one side of 60 with a histogram or density test, and look at the retake policy. It can also fail if other things change at 60 (a different teacher assignment or a scholarship at the same score): check whether other covariates measured before the test jump at the cutoff, and whether other programs use the same cutoff. Also check robustness to the bandwidth and to the order of the polynomial.
