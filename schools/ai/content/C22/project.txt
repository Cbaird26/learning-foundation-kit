@review
**Week 1 to 2: potential outcomes.** Each unit has $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$; only one is observed (the fundamental problem). Consistency links them to data and SUTVA rules out interference and hidden versions. ATE, ATT and ATC; naive difference $=$ ATT $+$ selection bias; $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1 - \pi)\,\text{ATC}$.

**Week 3 to 4: experiments.** Randomization gives $T \perp (Y(0), Y(1))$, so the difference in means is unbiased for the ATE; Neyman standard error $\sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_0^2/n_0}$; intention to treat. Sample size per arm: $2\sigma^2(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2/\delta^2$ for means, and $(z + z)^2[p_0 q_0 + p_1 q_1]/\delta^2$ for proportions; halving $\delta$ quadruples $n$. Pitfalls: peeking, multiple comparisons ($1 - (1 - \alpha)^m$, Bonferroni), sample ratio mismatch.

**Week 5 to 6: graphs.** Chain, fork, collider; d-separation (conditioning on a collider or its descendants opens a path); Markov factorization $\prod P(v \mid \mathrm{pa}(v))$. The backdoor criterion: no descendants of $X$ in $Z$, and $Z$ blocks every backdoor path (a path from $X$ to $Y$ that starts with an arrow into $X$). Adjustment formula $\sum_z P(y \mid x, z)P(z)$. Do not adjust for mediators or colliders.

**Week 7 to 8: adjustment and weighting.** Standardization needs consistency, conditional exchangeability and positivity. Regression coefficients are weighted averages of stratum effects with weights $\propto n_z p_z(1 - p_z)$. Propensity score $e(z)$; IPW $\frac{1}{n}\sum [TY/e - (1-T)Y/(1-e)]$; Hájek normalization; trimming; matching; balance by standardized mean differences.

**Week 9 to 10: quasi-experiments.** IV needs relevance, independence, exclusion (and monotonicity for the LATE): Wald $= (\text{ITT on } Y)/(\text{first stage})$. DiD needs parallel trends and no anticipation: $(\Delta_{\text{treated}}) - (\Delta_{\text{control}})$. Sharp RD needs continuity at the cutoff and no manipulation; local linear fits on each side.

**Week 11 to 12: heterogeneity and ML.** CATE $\tau(x)$, uplift, S- and T-learners, transformed outcome, policy value and Qini curves, subgroup pitfalls. Shortcuts are non-causal paths (forks through a common cause); covariate, label and concept shift; counterfactual fairness.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) fill the missing column of a potential-outcomes table under an assumption and say which; (2) decide d-separation in a graph; (3) list all valid adjustment sets; (4) compute IPW, DiD and Wald estimates by hand; (5) compute a sample size; (6) say which estimand each method gives (ATE, ATT, LATE, effect at a cutoff)?

**Common errors.** Adjusting for everything; adjusting for a collider or mediator; confusing a prediction model with an uplift model; reading a Wald estimate as the ATE; treating pre-trends as proof of parallel trends; rounding sample sizes down; peeking; forgetting positivity.

Estimate the propensity score as the fraction treated within each value of $Z$, and compute the Horvitz-Thompson inverse probability weighting estimate of the ATE.
answer: 3
check: float(run("rows = [(0,1,8),(0,0,5),(0,0,6),(0,0,7),(1,1,12),(1,1,11),(1,1,10),(1,0,7)]\ne = {}\nfor z in (0, 1):\n    g = [r for r in rows if r[0] == z]\n    e[z] = sum(r[1] for r in g) / len(g)\nprint(sum(t*y/e[z] - (1-t)*y/(1-e[z]) for z, t, y in rows) / len(rows))"))
hint: The propensity is $1/4$ for $Z = 0$ and $3/4$ for $Z = 1$. Sum $TY/e$ for the treated and $(1 - T)Y/(1 - e)$ for the untreated, subtract, and divide by 8.
solution: The propensity scores are $0.25$ and $0.75$. Treated terms: $8/0.25 + (12 + 11 + 10)/0.75 = 32 + 44 = 76$. Untreated terms: $(5 + 6 + 7)/0.75 + 7/0.25 = 24 + 28 = 52$. So the estimate is $(76 - 52)/8 = 3$, which equals the stratified estimate $0.5(8 - 6) + 0.5(11 - 7) = 3$.

@exam self
prompt: A subscription service wants to know whether its premium plan raises 90-day retention. It has observational data on 100,000 users with: age group, device, prior-month activity (measured before the choice), plan choice, retention, and a post-signup "feature usage" variable. Write an analysis plan: (a) a causal graph as nodes and directed edges; (b) the adjustment set and the variables you would not adjust for; (c) an estimator and the assumptions you rely on, each named; (d) a diagnostic or sensitivity check; (e) how an experiment, an instrument or a quasi-experiment could strengthen the conclusion, with its own assumptions.
hint: Decide first which variables are causes of both plan choice and retention, and which are consequences of the plan.
solution: (a) Nodes: Age, Device, Activity (prior-month), Plan, Usage, Retention. Edges: Age $\to$ Plan, Age $\to$ Retention, Device $\to$ Plan, Device $\to$ Retention, Activity $\to$ Plan, Activity $\to$ Retention, Plan $\to$ Usage, Usage $\to$ Retention, Plan $\to$ Retention. (b) The backdoor paths run through Age, Device and Activity, so adjust for $\{\text{Age}, \text{Device}, \text{Activity}\}$. Do not adjust for Usage: it is a mediator and a descendant of Plan, and adjusting would remove part of the effect. (c) Estimate propensity scores for Plan given the three covariates (for example logistic regression), then use inverse probability weighting or stratification on the scores, trimming extreme weights; this relies on consistency (a well-defined plan), SUTVA (no interference between users), conditional exchangeability (no unmeasured confounding given the three variables), and positivity (users of every covariate type are found on both plans). (d) Check positivity and covariate balance after weighting (standardized mean differences below 0.1), and run a sensitivity analysis for an unmeasured confounder, such as intent to stay long, by asking how strong it would have to be (its effect on retention and its difference in prevalence between plans) to explain the estimate away. (e) The cleanest strengthening is a randomized experiment, such as random price offers, which removes confounding by design; if only the offer is randomized (users may decline), use the offer as an instrument for plan choice, relying on relevance, independence and exclusion (the offer affects retention only through the plan) and monotonicity, and interpret the result as a complier effect (LATE).
