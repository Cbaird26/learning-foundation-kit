@article
A retailer sends a coupon to 1,000 customers and sees that 30% buy. Without the coupon, 10% would have bought. The coupon created 200 extra purchases. But the retailer also knows that some customers would buy anyway, some will never buy, and a few are annoyed by coupons. Sending coupons to everyone wastes money on the first two groups and loses sales in the last. The average effect hides who benefits.

### Conditional average treatment effects

For covariates $\mathbf{X}$, the **conditional average treatment effect** (CATE) is

$$\tau(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid \mathbf{X} = \mathbf{x}].$$

If effects differ across values of $\mathbf{x}$ the effect is **heterogeneous** (**effect modification**). Under consistency, conditional exchangeability given $\mathbf{X}$ (guaranteed by randomization) and positivity,

$$\tau(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1, \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0, \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}].$$

For a binary outcome, $\tau(\mathbf{x})$ is the **uplift**: the increase in the probability of the outcome caused by the treatment.

### Four kinds of customers

For a binary outcome and treatment, each unit has a response type determined by $(Y(0), Y(1))$:

| type | $Y(0)$ | $Y(1)$ | effect of treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| persuadable | 0 | 1 | positive |
| sure thing | 1 | 1 | none |
| lost cause | 0 | 0 | none |
| sleeping dog (do-not-disturb) | 1 | 0 | negative |

Suppose a population is split equally into four segments whose treated and untreated purchase rates are: A: $0.30$ and $0.10$ (uplift $+0.20$); B: $0.50$ and $0.50$ (uplift 0); C: $0.02$ and $0.02$ (0); D: $0.10$ and $0.15$ ($-0.05$). The ATE is $0.25(0.20 + 0 + 0 - 0.05) = 0.0375$. Targeting by uplift, treating only A, yields $0.25 \times 0.20 = 0.05$ extra purchases per customer, better than treating everybody. Targeting customers with the highest predicted purchase probability *without treatment* picks B and D, and yields $0.25(0 - 0.05) = -0.0125$: it is worse than doing nothing. A model that predicts outcomes well is not a model of uplift.

If a purchase is worth \$20 and a coupon costs \$2, treat a segment when $20\,\tau > 2$, that is, $\tau > 0.10$. Only segment A qualifies, with net value $0.25(0.20 \cdot 20 - 2) = \$0.50$ per customer; coupons for everyone have net value $0.25\big[(4 - 2) + (0 - 2) + (0 - 2) + (-1 - 2)\big] = -\$1.25$ per customer.

### Estimating the CATE

Because $\tau(\mathbf{x})$ involves two potential outcomes, we estimate it from two regression functions $\mu_t(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=t, \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}]$:

- **S-learner**: fit one model $\hat\mu(\mathbf{x}, t)$ with treatment as a feature and take $\hat\tau(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\mu(\mathbf{x}, 1) - \hat\mu(\mathbf{x}, 0)$. Regularization can shrink the effect of $T$ toward zero.
- **T-learner**: fit separate models on the treated and untreated units and take $\hat\tau(\mathbf{x}) = \hat\mu_1(\mathbf{x}) - \hat\mu_0(\mathbf{x})$.
- **Transformed outcome**: with known propensity $e(\mathbf{x})$ (a randomized experiment), define $Y^* = Y\left(\dfrac{T}{e} - \dfrac{1-T}{1-e}\right) = \dfrac{Y\,(T - e)}{e(1 - e)}$. Then $\mathbb{E}[Y^* \mid \mathbf{X}=\mathbf{x}] = \tau(\mathbf{x})$, so any regression of $Y^*$ on $\mathbf{X}$ estimates the CATE. It is unbiased but noisy.

More refined methods exist (the X-learner of Künzel and colleagues, causal forests of Wager and Athey), all built on the same identification assumptions. Without randomization, they also need no unmeasured confounding given $\mathbf{X}$.

### Evaluating a targeting policy

A **policy** $\pi(\mathbf{x}) \in \{0, 1\}$ decides who is treated. Its value relative to treating no one is $\mathbb{E}[\pi(\mathbf{X})\,\tau(\mathbf{X})]$, which can be estimated on held-out randomized data. A **Qini curve** (or uplift curve) ranks units by predicted uplift and plots the cumulative incremental outcome as the treated fraction grows. With 4,000 customers (1,000 per segment) ranked A, B, C, D, the incremental purchases are $+200, 0, 0, -50$ per segment, so the cumulative gain after each is $200, 200, 200, 150$: it rises, flattens and falls, and the best policy stops after segment A.

### Caution with subgroups

Looking for the subgroup with the largest effect is a multiple-comparisons problem: with 20 subgroups, one is likely "significant" by chance, and the winner's estimate is biased upward. A subgroup holding $1/9$ of the sample has a standard error three times as large as the full sample's. Safeguards are to pre-specify subgroups, to correct for the number of comparisons, and to estimate on one part of the data and evaluate on another (**sample splitting**, or cross-fitting).

@example
title: Choosing whom to treat
problem: A population has three segments with shares 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2 and uplifts $+0.08$, $+0.02$ and $-0.04$. A conversion is worth \$40 and treating a customer costs \$1.50. Which segments should be treated, and what is the net value per customer?
step: Break-even uplift is cost over value: $1.50/40 = 0.0375$.
step: Segment 1 ($0.08 > 0.0375$) is worth treating; segment 2 ($0.02 < 0.0375$) loses money; segment 3 has negative uplift.
step: Segment 1's net value per treated customer is $0.08 \times 40 - 1.50 = 1.70$, and it is half of the population: $0.5 \times 1.70 = 0.85$.
answer: Treat only segment 1; the net value is \$0.85 per customer.

@example
title: A transformed outcome
problem: In a randomized test with $e = 0.5$, two customers have $(T, Y) = (1, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$, and a third has $(T, Y) = (0, 0)$. Compute $Y^*$ for each and the average of $Y^*$.
step: For $T = 1$, $Y^* = Y/e = 1/0.5 = 2$.
step: For $T = 0$, $Y^* = -Y/(1 - e)$, so the second customer has $-1/0.5 = -2$ and the third has $0$.
step: The average is $(2 - 2 + 0)/3 = 0$. Each $Y^*$ is an unbiased but very noisy signal of the effect; averaging many of them (within segments) estimates the CATE.
answer: $Y^* = 2, -2, 0$, with average $0$.

@mistakes
- Targeting customers with the highest predicted outcome and calling it targeting by effect. Sure things have high outcomes and zero uplift.
- Reading the best subgroup in a post-hoc search as the true effect: it is biased upward.
- Estimating the policy's value on the same data used to build it.
- Ignoring costs: the policy that maximizes incremental conversions is not the one that maximizes profit.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about learners for the conditional average treatment effect are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: A T-learner estimates $\tau(\mathbf{x})$ as the difference between a model fit on treated units and a model fit on untreated units.
choice*: An S-learner fits a single model with treatment as a feature and predicts under $T = 1$ and $T = 0$.

solution: The T-learner and S-learner are built exactly as described. Their output is a causal quantity only under the identification assumptions. Prediction accuracy for $Y$ says little about the difference of two potential-outcome regressions, which can be dominated by small errors in either.

@quiz self
prompt: After a randomized test finds no overall effect, an analyst reports that among "customers aged 25–34 in the Midwest who visited on weekdays" the new design raised purchases ($p = 0.03$), and proposes launching it for them. Explain what is wrong with this claim and how to do the analysis properly.
hint: Count how many subgroups could have been examined, and ask how the subgroup was chosen.
solution: The subgroup was found after looking at the data, among a very large number of possible subgroups defined by age, region, day and other variables. With that many comparisons, a $p$-value of 0.03 for the best one is expected by chance, and the estimated effect in a selected subgroup is biased upward (the winner's curse). The claim should be treated as a hypothesis. To test it properly, pre-specify a small number of subgroups with a mechanism-based rationale, correct for multiple comparisons (for example Bonferroni), or use sample splitting: choose the subgroup on one part of the data and estimate the effect on a held-out part, or confirm it in a new randomized test on that subgroup, with the sample size computed for the effect and subgroup size.
rubric: Identifies the many possible subgroups (a multiple comparisons or forking-paths problem) and the upward bias of the selected estimate.
rubric: Proposes a remedy: pre-specification, correction for multiple comparisons, sample splitting or a confirmatory experiment.
rubric: Mentions the smaller sample and larger standard error in a subgroup.
