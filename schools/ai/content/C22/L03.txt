@article
An online store wants to know whether a redesigned checkout page raises conversion. It could compare the week after launch with the week before, but a holiday might intervene. Instead it assigns each visitor, by a coin flip, to the old page or the new page and compares the two groups at the same time. This is a **randomized experiment**, in industry an **A/B test**.

### Why randomization works

Let $T_i = 1$ if visitor $i$ sees the new page. **Randomization** means $T$ is assigned by a mechanism that ignores everything about the visitors, so $T$ is independent of the potential outcomes: $(Y(0), Y(1)) \perp T$ (exchangeability). Then

$$\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid T = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)],$$

where the first equality is consistency and the second is exchangeability; likewise $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0)]$. Hence the associational difference equals the causal effect: $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0] = \text{ATE}$. In the language of last week, the selection bias is zero. Randomization also supplies positivity by design, because every unit has probability strictly between 0 and 1 of each arm.

For a finite sample under **complete randomization** (exactly $n_1$ of $n$ units drawn uniformly to be treated), the **difference-in-means estimator**

$$\hat\tau = \bar{Y}_1 - \bar{Y}_0$$

is unbiased for the sample ATE: each unit is treated with probability $n_1/n$, so the expected treated mean is the average of all $Y_i(1)$, and likewise for control.

### Uncertainty

With two arms of sizes $n_1, n_0$ and sample variances $s_1^2, s_0^2$, **Neyman's variance estimator** is

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\tau) = \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{s_0^2}{n_0}}.$$

It is conservative: its expectation is at least the true variance of $\hat\tau$ (equal when the individual effects are constant). An approximate 95% confidence interval for large samples is $\hat\tau \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}$. For a binary outcome, $s^2$ is estimated by $\hat p(1 - \hat p)$.

**Example.** Control: 24 conversions of 200 ($\hat p_0 = 0.12$). Treatment: 36 of 200 ($\hat p_1 = 0.18$). Then $\hat\tau = 0.06$ and

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.18 \cdot 0.82}{200} + \frac{0.12 \cdot 0.88}{200}} \approx 0.0356,$$

so the interval is $0.06 \pm 0.070$, that is $(-0.010, 0.130)$. The $z$ statistic is $0.06/0.0356 \approx 1.69$, with a two-sided $p$-value of about $0.092$. The data are compatible with no effect and with an effect of 13 points.

### A randomization test

**Fisher's randomization test** needs no distribution for the outcomes. Under the **sharp null** hypothesis that $Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)$ for every unit, each unit's outcome would be the same under either assignment, so we can recompute $\hat\tau$ for every assignment the experiment could have produced, and ask how extreme the observed value is among them. With 4 units and 2 treated there are $\binom{4}{2} = 6$ equally likely assignments. The $p$-value is the fraction of assignments whose statistic is at least as extreme as the one observed.

### Practical points

- **Unit of randomization.** Randomize at the level where interference stops: users, not page views, if one user's later behavior depends on what they saw earlier; whole cities or sellers if units interact. This protects SUTVA.
- **Intention to treat.** If some assigned units do not comply (the feature fails to load, or users never open it), analyze units by the arm they were *assigned* to. This **intention-to-treat** (ITT) analysis keeps randomization intact and estimates the effect of assigning treatment. Dropping non-compliers, or comparing compliers with controls, reintroduces selection.
- **Pre-registration.** Fix the metric, the analysis and the stopping rule before looking at data.

```python
def diff_in_means(treated, control):
    n1, n0 = len(treated), len(control)
    m1, m0 = sum(treated) / n1, sum(control) / n0
    v1 = sum((y - m1) ** 2 for y in treated) / (n1 - 1)
    v0 = sum((y - m0) ** 2 for y in control) / (n0 - 1)
    return m1 - m0, (v1 / n1 + v0 / n0) ** 0.5

print(diff_in_means([12, 15, 11, 14], [10, 9, 13, 8, 10]))
```

title: Estimate and standard error from a small test
problem: In a test of a tutoring tool, 4 students used it and had practice times of 12, 15, 11 and 14 minutes; 5 students did not and had 10, 9, 13, 8 and 10. Compute $\hat\tau$ and its Neyman standard error.
step: The treated mean is $52/4 = 13$ and the control mean is $50/5 = 10$, so $\hat\tau = 3$.
step: Sample variances: treated deviations are $-1, 2, -2, 1$, so $s_1^2 = 10/3 \approx 3.333$; control deviations are $0, -1, 3, -2, 0$, so $s_0^2 = 14/4 = 3.5$.
step: $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} = \sqrt{3.333/4 + 3.5/5} = \sqrt{0.8333 + 0.7} \approx 1.238$.
step: The ratio $3/1.238 \approx 2.42$ is large, but with only nine students the normal approximation is poor; a $t$ reference with about 7 degrees of freedom would give a wider interval.
answer: $\hat\tau = 3$ minutes with $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}} \approx 1.24$.

@example
title: A randomization test by enumeration
problem: Four units are randomly split into two treated and two control. The outcomes are 12, 8 (treated) and 9, 6 (control). Under the sharp null, find the two-sided randomization $p$-value for the difference in means.
step: Observed $\hat\tau = (12 + 8)/2 - (9 + 6)/2 = 10 - 7.5 = 2.5$.
step: List the six assignments by which two units (outcomes 12, 9, 8, 6) are treated and compute $\hat\tau$: $\{12, 9\}$ gives $3.5$; $\{12, 8\}$ gives $2.5$; $\{12, 6\}$ gives $0.5$; $\{9, 8\}$ gives $-0.5$; $\{9, 6\}$ gives $-2.5$; $\{8, 6\}$ gives $-3.5$.
step: Count assignments with $|\hat\tau| \ge 2.5$: 3.5, 2.5, −2.5 and −3.5, four of six.
answer: $p = 4/6 \approx 0.667$.

@mistakes
- Saying randomization makes the groups identical. It makes them comparable on average: any single experiment can still show chance imbalance, which the standard error accounts for.
- Analyzing by the treatment actually received instead of the arm assigned, which breaks the randomization.
- Reading $p = 0.09$ as "there is no effect." A wide interval that includes zero says the experiment could not distinguish the effect from zero.

prompt: A site randomizes each page view (not each user) to one of two layouts and measures purchases per user over a week. Explain two problems with this design for estimating the effect of the layout, and propose a better design.
hint: Think about what happens to a user who sees both layouts, and about SUTVA.

@quiz self
prompt: Prove that if treatment is assigned by a fair coin flip for each unit, independently of everything else, then $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0]$ equals the ATE. State where each assumption is used.
hint: Use consistency to replace $Y$ by a potential outcome, then use independence.
solution: By consistency, on the event $T = 1$ we have $Y = Y(1)$, so $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid T=1]$, and similarly $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0]$. The coin flip is independent of the units' potential outcomes, so $\mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid T=1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0)]$. Subtracting, $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1)] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0)] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]$, the ATE. Both groups are nonempty with probability one here (positivity); SUTVA is needed for $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ to be well defined.
rubric: Uses consistency to replace the observed outcome by a potential outcome within each group.
rubric: Uses independence of $T$ and the potential outcomes to drop the conditioning.
rubric: Concludes with linearity of expectation and mentions SUTVA or positivity.
