Last week's table listed both potential outcomes for every student. In real data you never get that table: each unit is treated or not, so one of its two columns is always missing. This is the **fundamental problem of causal inference** (Holland, 1986): for any single unit, we can observe $Y_i(1)$ or $Y_i(0)$ but never both, so the individual effect $Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$ cannot be computed from data. Every method in this course replaces the missing column with an assumption.

### Three averages

Averages over units are different: they can be learned under assumptions. For a binary treatment $T$ there are three standard estimands:

$$\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)], \quad \text{ATT} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 1], \quad \text{ATC} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T = 0].$$

The **average treatment effect** (ATE) is over everyone; the **average treatment effect on the treated** (ATT) is over those who actually got treated; the **average effect on the controls** (ATC) is over those who did not. With a fraction $\pi = P(T = 1)$ treated, the law of total expectation gives $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1 - \pi)\,\text{ATC}$.

Here are eight units, with both potential outcomes written down (again, only we can see this):

| unit | $Y(0)$ | $Y(1)$ | $T$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 4 | 6 | 1 |
| 2 | 6 | 7 | 0 |
| 3 | 8 | 12 | 1 |
| 4 | 3 | 4 | 0 |
| 5 | 5 | 9 | 1 |
| 6 | 7 | 7 | 0 |
| 7 | 2 | 5 | 0 |
| 8 | 9 | 14 | 1 |

The effects are 2, 1, 4, 1, 4, 0, 3, 5, so $\text{ATE} = 20/8 = 2.5$. The treated units are 1, 3, 5, 8 with effects 2, 4, 4, 5, so $\text{ATT} = 3.75$; the controls have effects 1, 1, 0, 3, so $\text{ATC} = 1.25$. Indeed $0.5 \times 3.75 + 0.5 \times 1.25 = 2.5$. The treatment helped those who took it more than it would have helped the others.

### Decomposing the naive comparison

What we can compute is the **naive difference** (the difference in observed group means) $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 0]$. By consistency, $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) \mid T = 1]$ and $\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T = 0] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T = 0]$. Add and subtract $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T = 1]$, the untreated outcome of the treated group, which is a counterfactual:

$$\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y \mid T=0]}_{\text{naive difference}} = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid T=1]}_{\text{ATT}} + \underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=1] - \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0]}_{\text{selection bias}}.$$

In the eight-unit table the treated units have $Y(0)$ values 4, 8, 5, 9 (mean 6.5) and the controls have 6, 3, 7, 2 (mean 4.5), so the selection bias is $2.0$. The naive difference is $(6 + 12 + 9 + 14)/4 - (6 + 3 + 7 + 2)/4 = 10.25 - 4.5 = 5.75 = 3.75 + 2.0$. The naive difference overstates the ATE of 2.5 by 3.25.

**Selection bias** is nonzero whenever the treated and untreated groups would have had different outcomes even without treatment, and it can be of either sign. It can even make a helpful treatment look harmful: if sicker patients are the ones given a drug, the treated group's $Y(0)$ is lower than the controls'.

### What would make selection bias vanish

If treatment is assigned by a coin flip, $T$ is independent of $(Y(0), Y(1))$ (**exchangeability**, also called ignorability). Then $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0]$, the selection bias is zero, and the naive difference estimates the ATT, which also equals the ATE because the treated and untreated are exchangeable. We make this precise next week. We will also need **positivity**: every kind of unit has some chance of each treatment, $0 < P(T = 1 \mid \text{unit's covariates}) < 1$, otherwise there is no data about one of the potential outcomes for some units at all.

### In code

```python
def mean(xs):
    return sum(xs) / len(xs)

def effects(rows):
    """rows: list of (y0, y1, t). Returns (ate, att, atc, naive)."""
    treated = [(y0, y1) for y0, y1, t in rows if t == 1]
    control = [(y0, y1) for y0, y1, t in rows if t == 0]
    ate = mean([y1 - y0 for y0, y1, t in rows])
    att = mean([y1 - y0 for y0, y1 in treated])
    atc = mean([y1 - y0 for y0, y1 in control])
    naive = mean([y1 for y0, y1 in treated]) - mean([y0 for y0, y1 in control])
    return ate, att, atc, naive

rows = [(4, 6, 1), (6, 7, 0), (8, 12, 1), (3, 4, 0),
        (5, 9, 1), (7, 7, 0), (2, 5, 0), (9, 14, 1)]
print(effects(rows))
```

This prints `(2.5, 3.75, 1.25, 5.75)`. The function reads both columns, which real data never provides; it is a laboratory for checking estimators.

title: Effects and selection bias on four units
problem: Four units have $(Y(0), Y(1), T)$ equal to $(10, 14, 1)$, $(8, 8, 0)$, $(6, 10, 1)$ and $(12, 13, 0)$. Compute the ATE, ATT, ATC, the naive difference and the selection bias, and check the decomposition.
step: The individual effects are $4, 0, 4, 1$, so $\text{ATE} = 9/4 = 2.25$. The treated units (1 and 3) have effects 4 and 4, so $\text{ATT} = 4$. The controls (2 and 4) have effects 0 and 1, so $\text{ATC} = 0.5$. Check: $0.5 \times 4 + 0.5 \times 0.5 = 2.25$.
step: Observed treated outcomes are $Y(1)$: 14 and 10, with mean 12. Observed control outcomes are $Y(0)$: 8 and 12, with mean 10. The naive difference is $12 - 10 = 2$.
step: The treated units' untreated outcomes are 10 and 6 (mean 8); the controls' are 8 and 12 (mean 10). Selection bias $= 8 - 10 = -2$.
step: Check: $\text{ATT} + \text{selection bias} = 4 + (-2) = 2$, the naive difference. Here the bias is negative, so the naive difference (2) understates the ATT (4) and is close to the ATE (2.25) only by coincidence.
answer: ATE $2.25$, ATT $4$, ATC $0.5$, naive difference $2$, selection bias $-2$.

@example
title: A helpful drug that looks harmful
problem: Two sicker patients are given a drug and two healthier patients are not. Treated patients have $(Y(0), Y(1)) = (2, 5)$ and $(1, 4)$; untreated patients have $(8, 10)$ and $(9, 10)$, where higher is better. Compare the naive difference with the ATE.
step: The effects are $3, 3, 2, 1$, all positive, with $\text{ATE} = 9/4 = 2.25$.
step: Observed treated outcomes are $5$ and $4$ (mean 4.5); observed untreated outcomes are $8$ and $9$ (mean 8.5). The naive difference is $-4$.
step: The treated group's mean $Y(0)$ is $1.5$ and the controls' is $8.5$, so the selection bias is $-7$; the ATT is $3$, and $3 + (-7) = -4$.
answer: The naive difference is $-4$ although every individual effect is positive; selection bias of $-7$ outweighs the ATT of $3$.

@mistakes
- Using the treated group's observed mean minus the controls' observed mean and calling it the ATE. It equals the ATT plus selection bias, and the ATT may differ from the ATE.
- Assuming selection bias is positive. Whether treated units are better or worse off at baseline depends on how treatment was assigned.
- Believing the fundamental problem means causal effects can never be estimated. It means individual effects cannot be; averages can, under assumptions.

def selection_bias(rows):

    treated = [y0 for y0, y1, t in rows if t == 1]
    control = [y0 for y0, y1, t in rows if t == 0]

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements are true for a binary treatment, assuming consistency? Select all that apply.
choice*: The naive difference in means equals the ATT plus the selection bias.
choice*: If the ATT equals the ATC, the ATE equals the ATT.
choice*: If treatment is independent of the potential outcomes, the selection bias is zero.
choice: Selection bias is always positive, because treated units tend to be better off.
hint: Use the decomposition and the formula $\text{ATE} = \pi\,\text{ATT} + (1-\pi)\,\text{ATC}$.
solution: The decomposition is an algebraic identity under consistency. With equal ATT and ATC the weighted average equals both. Independence gives $\mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=1] = \mathbb{E}[Y(0) \mid T=0]$, so the bias is zero. The sign of selection bias depends on who gets treated: sicker patients are often treated, which makes it negative.
