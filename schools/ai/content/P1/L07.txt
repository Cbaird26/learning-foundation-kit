objective: Prove that regularized loss minimization with a $\rho$-Lipschitz convex loss is $2\rho^2/(\lambda m)$-stable and choose $\lambda$ to balance stability against bias.

@article
Uniform convergence bounds the gap for *every* hypothesis in a class, which makes them depend on the class and not on how the learner behaves inside it. **Stability** takes the opposite view: if changing one training example barely changes what the algorithm outputs, then the output cannot depend much on any single example, and so it cannot be overfit to the sample. This gives guarantees for algorithms such as regularized regression whose class (all weight vectors) is too rich for uniform convergence to say much.

### Definitions

Let $A$ map a sample $S = (z_1, \dots, z_m)$ to a parameter $A(S) \in \mathbb{R}^d$, let $\ell(\mathbf{w}, z) \ge 0$ be a loss, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E}_z \ell(\mathbf{w}, z)$ and $L_S(\mathbf{w}) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \ell(\mathbf{w}, z_i)$. Write $S^{(i)}$ for $S$ with the $i$-th example replaced by $z_i'$. The algorithm is **$\beta$-uniformly stable** (Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002) if for all $S$, all $i$, all $z_i'$ and all $z$, $|\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z) - \ell(A(S), z)| \le \beta$.

A function $f$ is **$\mu$-strongly convex** if $f(\alpha\mathbf{w} + (1-\alpha)\mathbf{u}) \le \alpha f(\mathbf{w}) + (1-\alpha)f(\mathbf{u}) - \frac{\mu}{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$ for all $\mathbf{w}, \mathbf{u}$ and $\alpha \in [0,1]$. The loss is **$\rho$-Lipschitz** if $|\ell(\mathbf{w}, z) - \ell(\mathbf{w}', z)| \le \rho\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}' \rVert$.

**Regularized loss minimization (RLM)** with Tikhonov regularization returns $A(S) = \arg\min_{\mathbf{w}} F_S(\mathbf{w})$, where $F_S(\mathbf{w}) = L_S(\mathbf{w}) + \lambda\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2$. If $\ell$ is convex in $\mathbf{w}$, $F_S$ is $2\lambda$-strongly convex, so the minimizer exists and is unique. Ridge regression is the case of the squared loss. For one parameter and data $(x_i, y_i)$ it gives $w = \sum_i x_i y_i/(\sum_i x_i^2 + \lambda m)$: the penalty shrinks the least squares solution toward zero.

### Three results

**Theorem 1 (stability implies generalization).** If $A$ is $\beta$-uniformly stable, then $\mathbb{E}_S[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) - L_S(A(S))] \le \beta$. (First example.)

**Theorem 2 (RLM is stable).** If $\ell(\cdot, z)$ is convex and $\rho$-Lipschitz for every $z$, then RLM with $\lambda\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert^2$ is $\beta$-uniformly stable with $\beta = 2\rho^2/(\lambda m)$. (Second example.)

**Theorem 3 (risk bound).** Under the hypotheses of Theorem 2, for every fixed $\mathbf{u}$,
$$\mathbb{E}_S\, L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \le L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{u}) + \lambda\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 + \frac{2\rho^2}{\lambda m}.$$
*Proof.* By Theorem 1 and 2, $\mathbb{E}L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \le \mathbb{E}L_S(A(S)) + \beta$. Since the penalty is nonnegative, $L_S(A(S)) \le F_S(A(S)) \le F_S(\mathbf{u})$ by minimality, and $\mathbb{E}F_S(\mathbf{u}) = L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{u}) + \lambda\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$ because $\mathbf{u}$ is fixed. $\square$

The three terms are the bias-complexity trade-off again: the risk of the comparator $\mathbf{u}$, the price $\lambda\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2$ of regularization (approximation) and the stability term $2\rho^2/(\lambda m)$ (estimation), which is large when $\lambda$ is small. For $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert \le B$, the choice $\lambda = (\rho/B)\sqrt{2/m}$ balances the last two terms and gives the excess risk $2\sqrt{2}\,\rho B/\sqrt{m}$. With $\rho = 1$, $B = 2$ and $m = 800$ this is $\lambda = 0.025$ and an excess of $0.2$.

Losses that fit the hypotheses include the hinge loss $\max(0, 1 - y\langle \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{x} \rangle)$ and the logistic loss $\ln(1 + e^{-y\langle \mathbf{w}, \mathbf{x} \rangle})$ for $\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert \le \rho$, which are convex and $\rho$-Lipschitz in $\mathbf{w}$. The squared loss is not Lipschitz over all of $\mathbb{R}^d$ (it needs bounded domains), and the 0-1 loss is not convex.

### Beyond expectation, and beyond RLM

Bousquet and Elisseeff also proved a high-probability bound: for a $\beta$-uniformly stable algorithm with loss bounded by $M$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \le L_S(A(S)) + 2\beta + (4m\beta + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$ (stated without proof). For RLM, $m\beta = 2\rho^2/\lambda$, so the last term is $(8\rho^2/\lambda + M)\sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$, which vanishes as $m$ grows only if $\lambda\sqrt{m} \to \infty$: the regularization weight may shrink with the sample size, but more slowly than $1/\sqrt{m}$. Stability arguments have also been applied to stochastic gradient descent on convex and some nonconvex problems (Hardt, Recht and Singer, 2016); how far they explain deep learning is a question we return to in week 11.

@example
title: Stable algorithms generalize in expectation
problem: Prove Theorem 1.
step: Let $S' = (z_1', \dots, z_m')$ be an independent copy of $S$ and $S^{(i)}$ the sample $S$ with $z_i$ replaced by $z_i'$. Since $A(S)$ is independent of a fresh example, $\mathbb{E}_S L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) = \mathbb{E}_{S, z'}\,\ell(A(S), z')$, and this is the same for every choice of which fresh example to call $z_i'$.
step: Exchange the roles of $z_i$ and $z_i'$. Since $(S, z_i')$ and $(S^{(i)}, z_i)$ have the same joint distribution, $\mathbb{E}\,\ell(A(S), z_i') = \mathbb{E}\,\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)$ for each $i$.
step: Hence $\mathbb{E}_S L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \mathbb{E}\,\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i)$, while $\mathbb{E}_S L_S(A(S)) = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \mathbb{E}\,\ell(A(S), z_i)$.
step: Subtract: $\mathbb{E}[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) - L_S(A(S))] = \frac{1}{m}\sum_i \mathbb{E}[\ell(A(S^{(i)}), z_i) - \ell(A(S), z_i)] \le \beta$ by uniform stability applied at $z = z_i$.
answer: The expected generalization gap is at most $\beta$.

@example
title: RLM is uniformly stable
problem: Prove Theorem 2.
step: Strong convexity lemma. If $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex and $\mathbf{w}^\star$ minimizes $f$, then $f(\mathbf{w}) - f(\mathbf{w}^\star) \ge \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^\star \rVert^2$. Indeed, for $\alpha \in (0, 1)$, $f(\mathbf{w}^\star) \le f(\alpha\mathbf{w} + (1-\alpha)\mathbf{w}^\star) \le \alpha f(\mathbf{w}) + (1-\alpha) f(\mathbf{w}^\star) - \frac{\mu}{2}\alpha(1-\alpha)\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^\star \rVert^2$. Rearranging and dividing by $\alpha$ gives $f(\mathbf{w}) - f(\mathbf{w}^\star) \ge \frac{\mu}{2}(1-\alpha)\lVert \mathbf{w} - \mathbf{w}^\star \rVert^2$; let $\alpha \to 0$.
step: Let $\mathbf{w} = A(S)$ and $\mathbf{w}' = A(S^{(i)})$. Since $F_S$ is $2\lambda$-strongly convex and $\mathbf{w}$ minimizes it, the lemma gives $F_S(\mathbf{w}') - F_S(\mathbf{w}) \ge \lambda\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert^2$.
step: Relate the two objectives: $F_S(\mathbf{u}) = F_{S^{(i)}}(\mathbf{u}) + \frac{1}{m}\big(\ell(\mathbf{u}, z_i) - \ell(\mathbf{u}, z_i')\big)$ for every $\mathbf{u}$. Therefore $F_S(\mathbf{w}') - F_S(\mathbf{w}) = [F_{S^{(i)}}(\mathbf{w}') - F_{S^{(i)}}(\mathbf{w})] + \frac{1}{m}[\ell(\mathbf{w}', z_i) - \ell(\mathbf{w}, z_i)] - \frac{1}{m}[\ell(\mathbf{w}', z_i') - \ell(\mathbf{w}, z_i')]$.
step: The bracket in $F_{S^{(i)}}$ is at most $0$ because $\mathbf{w}'$ minimizes $F_{S^{(i)}}$, and each of the other two brackets has absolute value at most $\rho\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert$ by the Lipschitz property. So $F_S(\mathbf{w}') - F_S(\mathbf{w}) \le \frac{2\rho}{m}\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert$.
step: Combine: $\lambda\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert^2 \le \frac{2\rho}{m}\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert$, hence $\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert \le \frac{2\rho}{\lambda m}$. For every $z$, $|\ell(\mathbf{w}', z) - \ell(\mathbf{w}, z)| \le \rho\lVert \mathbf{w}' - \mathbf{w} \rVert \le \frac{2\rho^2}{\lambda m}$.
answer: RLM is $\beta$-uniformly stable with $\beta = 2\rho^2/(\lambda m)$.

@mistakes
- Believing stability needs a small hypothesis class: it depends on the algorithm. RLM over all of $\mathbb{R}^d$ is stable, while ERM over the same class is not.
- Taking $\lambda$ as small as possible to reduce bias: the stability term $2\rho^2/(\lambda m)$ then blows up, and the optimal $\lambda$ shrinks only like $1/\sqrt{m}$.
- Applying Theorem 2 to a loss that is not Lipschitz, such as the squared loss on an unbounded domain, or to a nonconvex loss such as the 0-1 loss.
