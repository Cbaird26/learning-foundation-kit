@article
Here is a learner that cannot lose on its training data. It stores every training pair and, on a new point, outputs the stored label if it has seen the point and 0 otherwise. Its training error is zero on every sample without conflicting labels. Does it learn? To answer that we must first say precisely what learning means, which is the job of this week.

### The framework

Fix a **domain** $\mathcal{X}$, a **label set** $\mathcal{Y} = \{0, 1\}$ and an unknown probability distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}$. The learner sees a **training sample** $S = ((x_1, y_1), \dots, (x_m, y_m))$ drawn i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$, written $S \sim \mathcal{D}^m$, and outputs a **predictor** $h_S : \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$. Its quality is the **true risk** (generalization error)

$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) = P_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}\big[h(x) \neq y\big],$$

which the learner can never compute, because it does not know $\mathcal{D}$. What it can compute is the **empirical risk**

$$L_S(h) = \frac{1}{m}\,\big|\{i : h(x_i) \neq y_i\}\big| .$$

A **hypothesis class** $\mathcal{H}$ is a set of functions $\mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}$ that the learner is allowed to output. The **empirical risk minimization** (ERM) rule returns some $h_S \in \operatorname{ERM}_{\mathcal{H}}(S) = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_S(h)$; the minimizer need not be unique, and the guarantees below hold for every choice.

### Why the class matters

Let $\mathcal{X} = [0, 1]$, let $\mathcal{D}_{\mathcal{X}}$ be uniform and let the label be $y = 1$ exactly when $x < 1/2$. The memorizing predictor above has $L_S(h_S) = 0$, but with probability one it has never seen the new point $x$, so it outputs 0, and its true risk is $P[x < 1/2] = 1/2$: no better than a coin. The memorizer is ERM over the class of all functions $\mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}$, a class with no restriction at all. Restricting $\mathcal{H}$ is an **inductive bias**: prior knowledge, chosen before seeing data, that excludes hypotheses which fit the sample but are unlikely to be right. Week 6 proves that some bias is unavoidable; the rest of the course quantifies how much restriction suffices.

### Hoeffding's inequality

For a *fixed* hypothesis $h$, the variables $Z_i = \mathbb{1}[h(x_i) \neq y_i]$ are i.i.d. with values in $[0, 1]$ and mean $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)$, and $L_S(h)$ is their average. **Hoeffding's inequality** (Hoeffding, 1963) states: for i.i.d. $Z_1, \dots, Z_m \in [0, 1]$ and any $\epsilon > 0$,

$$P\Big[\Big|\tfrac{1}{m} \textstyle\sum_i Z_i - \mathbb{E} Z_1\Big| > \epsilon\Big] \le 2 e^{-2 m \epsilon^2}.$$

This is the form used throughout the course. It does not apply to $h_S$, because $h_S$ is chosen using the same sample that defines $L_S$: the terms $\mathbb{1}[h_S(x_i) \neq y_i]$ are not independent draws with mean $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S)$. The memorizer shows how badly this can fail.

### Representative samples

Call $S$ **$\epsilon$-representative** with respect to $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{D}$ if $|L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)| \le \epsilon$ for **all** $h \in \mathcal{H}$ simultaneously. Closeness for every hypothesis at once is what repairs the dependence problem, and it gives the first theorem of the course.

**Lemma 1.** If $S$ is $\epsilon/2$-representative with respect to $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{D}$, then every $h_S \in \operatorname{ERM}_{\mathcal{H}}(S)$ satisfies $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) + \epsilon$.

*Proof.* Let $h^\star \in \mathcal{H}$ be any hypothesis (the minimum may not be attained, so take any $h^\star$ and finish by taking the infimum). Then
$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le L_S(h_S) + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2} \le L_S(h^\star) + \tfrac{\epsilon}{2} \le L_{\mathcal{D}}(h^\star) + \epsilon .$$
The first and third inequalities are representativeness, the second is that $h_S$ minimizes $L_S$ over $\mathcal{H}$. $\square$

The decomposition behind it is $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) = \epsilon_{\text{app}} + \epsilon_{\text{est}}$, with the **approximation error** $\epsilon_{\text{app}} = \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)$ set by the class and the **estimation error** $\epsilon_{\text{est}} = L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) - \epsilon_{\text{app}}$ set by the sample. Lemma 1 bounds the estimation error by twice the largest gap between empirical and true risk. Week 2 bounds that gap for finite classes by a union bound; weeks 3 to 5 do it for infinite classes.

### In code

```python
def erm_index(hyps, sample):
    """Index of the first hypothesis with the smallest empirical risk."""
    def emp(h):
        return sum(h(x) != y for x, y in sample) / len(sample)
    risks = [emp(h) for h in hyps]
    return risks.index(min(risks))

hyps = [lambda x: 0, lambda x: 1, lambda x: int(x >= 2)]
sample = [(1, 0), (2, 1), (3, 1), (0, 0)]
print(erm_index(hyps, sample))
```

The program prints `2`: the threshold at 2 makes no mistakes on this sample.

@example
title: ERM over thresholds on a six-point sample
problem: The class is $\mathcal{H} = \{h_t : t \in \{1, 2, \dots, 7\}\}$ with $h_t(x) = \mathbb{1}[x \ge t]$. The sample has points $x = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$ with labels $0, 0, 1, 0, 1, 1$. Find all empirical risk minimizers and their empirical risk.
step: Each $h_t$ predicts 1 for $x \ge t$ and 0 below. Count its mistakes on the six points.
step: $t = 1$ predicts all ones and errs on the three zeros ($x = 1, 2, 4$): 3 mistakes. $t = 2$ errs at $x = 2$ and $x = 4$: 2 mistakes. $t = 3$ errs only at $x = 4$: 1 mistake.
step: $t = 4$ errs at $x = 3$ (predicts 0, label 1) and $x = 4$ (predicts 1, label 0): 2 mistakes. $t = 5$ errs only at $x = 3$: 1 mistake. $t = 6$ errs at $x = 3$ and $x = 5$: 2 mistakes. $t = 7$ predicts all zeros and errs on the three ones: 3 mistakes.
step: The minimum is 1 mistake, attained at $t = 3$ and $t = 5$, so $\operatorname{ERM}_{\mathcal{H}}(S) = \{h_3, h_5\}$ and $L_S = 1/6$. Lemma 1 holds for either one; the sample does not choose between them.
answer: Both $h_3$ and $h_5$, with empirical risk $1/6$.

@example
title: One hypothesis, one sample size
problem: For a single fixed hypothesis $h$, how many i.i.d. examples guarantee $|L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)| \le 0.1$ with probability at least $0.95$, using Hoeffding's inequality in the form above?
step: Set the failure probability to the target: $2 e^{-2 m \epsilon^2} \le \delta$ with $\epsilon = 0.1$ and $\delta = 0.05$.
step: Take logarithms: $-2 m \epsilon^2 \le \ln(\delta / 2)$, that is $m \ge \dfrac{\ln(2/\delta)}{2 \epsilon^2}$.
step: Substitute: $m \ge \ln(40) / 0.02 \approx 3.689 / 0.02 = 184.4$, so $m = 185$ suffices.
step: Note what this does not give: it holds for a hypothesis fixed before the data. It says nothing about the output of ERM, which is the subject of the next lessons.
answer: $m = 185$ examples for one fixed hypothesis.

@mistakes
- Applying Hoeffding's inequality to $L_S(h_S)$ for the learned $h_S$: the hypothesis depends on the sample, so the terms are not independent and the bound can be false.
- Reading zero training error as evidence of learning: the memorizer has it and a true risk of one half.
- Forgetting that the guarantee of Lemma 1 compares with the best hypothesis in $\mathcal{H}$, not with the best possible predictor; a poor class has a large approximation error that no sample can remove.

@practice mc
prompt: Let $h_S$ be the output of ERM over a class $\mathcal{H}$ on a sample $S$. Why can Hoeffding's inequality not be applied directly to bound $|L_S(h_S) - L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S)|$?

model:
```python
def max_deviation(hyps, true_risks, sample):
    """Largest gap between empirical and true risk over the hypotheses."""
    m = len(sample)
    worst = 0.0
    for h, true_risk in zip(hyps, true_risks):
        emp = sum(1 for x, y in sample if h(x) != y) / m
        worst = max(worst, abs(emp - true_risk))
    return worst
```
hint: For each hypothesis compute its empirical risk as a fraction of the sample it misclassifies, then take the absolute difference with its true risk and keep the maximum.
solution: The deviation is taken in absolute value, because representativeness is two-sided. Computing the maximum over all hypotheses, not the deviation at the empirical minimizer, is what makes the quantity usable for Lemma 1. The third test catches a version that forgets the absolute value: the empirical risk is 0.25 and the true risk 0.6.

prompt: Call $h_S \in \mathcal{H}$ a $\gamma$-approximate ERM if $L_S(h_S) \le \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_S(h) + \gamma$ for some $\gamma \ge 0$ (an optimizer that stops early, for instance). Prove that if $S$ is $\epsilon$-representative with respect to $\mathcal{H}$ and $\mathcal{D}$, then $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) + 2\epsilon + \gamma$. Assume the minima exist.
hint: Copy the three-step chain from the proof of Lemma 1, and track where the extra $\gamma$ enters.
solution: Let $h^\star \in \mathcal{H}$ minimize $L_{\mathcal{D}}$. By representativeness, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le L_S(h_S) + \epsilon$. By the approximate-ERM property, $L_S(h_S) \le \min_{h} L_S(h) + \gamma \le L_S(h^\star) + \gamma$. By representativeness again, $L_S(h^\star) \le L_{\mathcal{D}}(h^\star) + \epsilon$. Chaining the three inequalities gives $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le L_{\mathcal{D}}(h^\star) + 2\epsilon + \gamma$. With $\epsilon/2$ in place of $\epsilon$ and $\gamma = 0$ this is Lemma 1; the optimization error $\gamma$ simply adds to the estimation error.
