@article
Could there be one learning algorithm that, given enough data, does well on every problem? The **no-free-lunch theorem** says no, in a strong and quantified sense: for every algorithm there is a distribution on which it fails, even though a perfect predictor exists. The consequence is not pessimism but design: prior knowledge, in the form of a restricted class $\mathcal{H}$, is not optional.

### The theorem

**Theorem (no free lunch).** Let $A$ be any learning algorithm for binary classification over a domain $\mathcal{X}$ with the 0-1 loss, and let the training size $m$ satisfy $m < |\mathcal{X}|/2$. Then there is a distribution $\mathcal{D}$ over $\mathcal{X} \times \{0,1\}$ such that (1) some function $f : \mathcal{X} \to \{0,1\}$ has $L_{\mathcal{D}}(f) = 0$, and (2) with probability at least $1/7$ over $S \sim \mathcal{D}^m$, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8$.

The algorithm may be improper (it need not output a member of any class), randomized algorithms are handled by averaging, and nothing is assumed about how it works. The proof (first example) chooses $2m$ points, labels them in all $2^{2m}$ possible ways and averages: the algorithm has seen at most $m$ of the $2m$ points, and on each unseen point it is right on exactly half of the labelings. A reverse Markov inequality converts the average error $\ge 1/4$ into the probability statement.

### Consequence: infinite VC dimension

**Corollary.** If $\operatorname{VCdim}(\mathcal{H}) = \infty$ then $\mathcal{H}$ is not PAC learnable.

*Proof.* Suppose it were, and let $m' = m_{\mathcal{H}}(1/9, 1/10)$. Because the VC dimension is infinite, $\mathcal{H}$ shatters some set $C$ with $|C| > 2m'$. The distributions of the theorem are supported on $C$ and labeled by an arbitrary function on $C$; every such labeling agrees on $C$ with a hypothesis of $\mathcal{H}$, so realizability holds. By the theorem, for the learner $A$ there is such a $\mathcal{D}$ with $L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8 > 1/9$ with probability at least $1/7 > 1/10$ over $m'$ examples. This contradicts the PAC guarantee with $\epsilon = 1/9$ and $\delta = 1/10$. $\square$

This is the missing implication (4) $\Rightarrow$ (6) of the fundamental theorem. The practice problem applies the same argument to the class of all functions on an infinite domain.

### What the theorem does not say

It is a statement about the worst case over distributions. It does not say that learning from images or text is hopeless, only that success there must come from structure that the class $\mathcal{H}$, the algorithm or the optimization procedure encodes, and from natural distributions being far from the worst case. Which structure matters most for modern networks is an open question we meet in week 11.

### The trade-off, quantified

With $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) = \epsilon_{\text{app}} + \epsilon_{\text{est}}$ from week 1, the estimation error of ERM over a finite class is controlled by Theorem 2 of week 2: with probability at least $1 - \delta$,
$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(h_S) \le \min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) + \sqrt{\frac{2\ln(2|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)}{m}} .$$
Enlarge $\mathcal{H}$ and the first term falls while the second grows. Suppose a nested sequence $\mathcal{H}_1 \subset \mathcal{H}_2 \subset \cdots$ has $|\mathcal{H}_k| = 2^{3k}$ and best-in-class risks $0.30, 0.20, 0.14, 0.10, 0.08, 0.07, 0.065, 0.062$ (invented numbers). With $m = 2000$ and $\delta = 0.05$:

| $k$ | $\epsilon_{\text{app}}$ | penalty | bound |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.300 | 0.076 | 0.376 |
| 3 | 0.140 | 0.100 | 0.240 |
| 5 | 0.080 | 0.119 | 0.199 |
| 6 | 0.070 | 0.127 | 0.197 |
| 8 | 0.062 | 0.143 | 0.205 |

The bound is smallest at $k = 6$: more data would move the minimum to the right. Choosing $k$ by minimizing such a bound is **structural risk minimization**: for classes $\mathcal{H}_k$ and weights $w_k > 0$ with $\sum_k w_k \le 1$, apply the finite-class deviation bound to $\mathcal{H}_k$ at confidence $w_k \delta$ and take a union bound over $k$; then with probability at least $1 - \delta$, for all $k$ and all $h \in \mathcal{H}_k$, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) \le L_S(h) + \sqrt{\ln(2|\mathcal{H}_k|/(w_k \delta))/(2m)}$. A learner that minimizes the right-hand side over $k$ and $h$ pays only the extra term $\ln(1/w_k)$ for not knowing $k$ in advance. Which classes are learnable in this nonuniform sense is characterized in the main reading.

@example
title: Proof of the no-free-lunch theorem
problem: Prove the theorem. Take $C \subseteq \mathcal{X}$ with $|C| = 2m$, and index the $T = 2^{2m}$ functions $f_1, \dots, f_T : C \to \{0,1\}$. Let $\mathcal{D}_i$ be uniform on $C$ with labels $f_i(x)$ (and mass zero outside $C$).
step: Each $\mathcal{D}_i$ satisfies (1) with $f = f_i$. It suffices to show $\max_i \mathbb{E}_{S \sim \mathcal{D}_i^m} L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(A(S)) \ge 1/4$, and then to convert this to a probability.
step: There are $k = (2m)^m$ sequences of instances $S_1, \dots, S_k \in C^m$. Let $S_j^i$ be $S_j$ labeled by $f_i$. Then $\mathbb{E}_{S \sim \mathcal{D}_i^m} L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(A(S)) = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k} L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(A(S_j^i))$, and the maximum over $i$ is at least the average over $i$, which is at least the minimum over $j$ of $\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T} L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(A(S_j^i))$.
step: Fix $j$. Let $v_1, \dots, v_p$ be the points of $C$ not appearing in $S_j$; since $S_j$ has at most $m$ distinct points, $p \ge m$. For any $h : C \to \{0,1\}$, $L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(h) = \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{x \in C}\mathbb{1}[h(x) \ne f_i(x)] \ge \frac{1}{2m}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\mathbb{1}[h(v_r) \ne f_i(v_r)] \ge \frac{1}{2p}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\mathbb{1}[h(v_r) \ne f_i(v_r)]$.
step: Hence $\frac{1}{T}\sum_i L_{\mathcal{D}_i}(A(S_j^i)) \ge \frac{1}{2p}\sum_{r=1}^{p}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}[A(S_j^i)(v_r) \ne f_i(v_r)]$. For a fixed $r$, pair up the $T$ functions into $T/2$ pairs $(f_i, f_{i'})$ that differ only at $v_r$. They agree on every point of $S_j$, so $S_j^i = S_j^{i'}$ and $A$ returns the same predictor for both, which is wrong at $v_r$ for exactly one of the two. So the inner average is exactly $1/2$, and the whole quantity is at least $\frac{1}{2p} \cdot p \cdot \frac{1}{2} = \frac{1}{4}$.
step: So some $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{D}_i$ has $\mathbb{E}_S L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/4$. Let $Z = L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \in [0,1]$ and $a = 1/8$. Then $\mathbb{E}Z \le P[Z > a] \cdot 1 + (1 - P[Z > a])\, a$, so $P[Z > a] \ge (\mathbb{E}Z - a)/(1 - a) \ge (1/4 - 1/8)/(7/8) = 1/7$. Hence $P[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8] \ge 1/7$.
answer: For every $A$ there is a realizable $\mathcal{D}$ with $P[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8] \ge 1/7$.

@example
title: Choosing a class by the bound
problem: Use the table of the article ($|\mathcal{H}_k| = 2^{3k}$, $\delta = 0.05$) but with only $m = 500$ examples. Which $k$ among $1, \dots, 8$ minimizes the bound $\epsilon_{\text{app}}(k) + \sqrt{2\ln(2|\mathcal{H}_k|/\delta)/m}$?
step: The penalty is $\sqrt{2\ln(2 \cdot 2^{3k}/0.05)/500}$, which grows with $k$ like $\sqrt{k}$ for large $k$.
step: Evaluate: for $k = 4$ the penalty is $0.219$ and the bound is $0.100 + 0.219 = 0.319$; for $k = 5$ the penalty is $0.237$ and the bound is $0.080 + 0.237 = 0.317$; for $k = 6$ the penalty is $0.254$ and the bound is $0.070 + 0.254 = 0.324$.
step: The minimum over $k = 1, \dots, 8$ is at $k = 5$ (value $0.317$). With 2000 examples the minimum moved to $k = 6$: less data selects a smaller class.
answer: $k = 5$.

@mistakes
- Reading the theorem as "learning is impossible": it says that no algorithm is best on all distributions, so success always depends on a bias that matches the problem.
- Forgetting the condition $m < |\mathcal{X}|/2$: with a finite domain and more than half of it observed, the argument gives nothing, and indeed learning can be easy.
- Treating the bound-minimizing class as the best class: the bound is an upper bound with worst-case constants, so it picks a conservative size, and validation data usually picks a larger one.

prompt: A random variable $Z$ takes values in $[0, 1]$ and has $\mathbb{E}Z \ge 0.3$. Using the inequality $P[Z > a] \ge (\mathbb{E}Z - a)/(1 - a)$, what lower bound does it give on $P[Z > 0.1]$? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.222
tol: 0.001
check: (0.3 - 0.1) / (1 - 0.1)
hint: Substitute $\mathbb{E}Z = 0.3$ and $a = 0.1$.
solution: $(0.3 - 0.1)/(1 - 0.1) = 0.2/0.9 \approx 0.222$. The inequality comes from $\mathbb{E}Z \le P[Z > a] + a(1 - P[Z > a])$, the largest the mean can be if $Z$ equals $1$ above $a$ and $a$ below.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\mathcal{X}$ be an infinite set and let $\mathcal{H}$ be the class of all functions $\mathcal{X} \to \{0, 1\}$. Prove that $\mathcal{H}$ is not PAC learnable, using the no-free-lunch theorem as stated (constants $1/7$ and $1/8$).
hint: Suppose it is learnable with some $m_{\mathcal{H}}(\epsilon, \delta)$. Choose $\epsilon$ and $\delta$ so that the theorem's failure event contradicts the guarantee, then choose a finite subset of $\mathcal{X}$ larger than twice the sample size.
solution: Suppose $\mathcal{H}$ were PAC learnable by an algorithm $A$ with sample complexity $m_{\mathcal{H}}$. Let $\epsilon = 1/9$, $\delta = 1/10$ and $m = m_{\mathcal{H}}(\epsilon, \delta)$. Since $\mathcal{X}$ is infinite, $|\mathcal{X}| > 2m$, so the no-free-lunch theorem applies to $A$ with $m$ examples and gives a distribution $\mathcal{D}$ and a function $f$ with $L_{\mathcal{D}}(f) = 0$ such that $P_S[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8] \ge 1/7$. The function $f$ belongs to $\mathcal{H}$ because $\mathcal{H}$ contains every function, so realizability holds and the PAC guarantee must apply: with probability at least $1 - \delta = 0.9$, $L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \le 1/9$. But then $P[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) \ge 1/8] \le P[L_{\mathcal{D}}(A(S)) > 1/9] \le 0.1 < 1/7$, a contradiction. Hence no algorithm and no sample size function can PAC learn $\mathcal{H}$. The class of all functions on an infinite domain has infinite VC dimension, in line with the fundamental theorem.
rubric: Chooses $\epsilon$ below $1/8$ and $\delta$ below $1/7$ so that the theorem's failure event contradicts the guarantee.
rubric: Applies the theorem with a domain larger than twice the sample size and notes that the target $f$ lies in $\mathcal{H}$, so realizability holds.
rubric: Derives the contradiction between the probability $1/7$ of error at least $1/8$ and the probability at most $\delta$ allowed by the PAC definition.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements about the bias-complexity trade-off for ERM over finite classes are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: Enlarging the class cannot increase the approximation error $\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)$.
choice*: For fixed $\delta$ and $m$, the penalty $\sqrt{2\ln(2|\mathcal{H}|/\delta)/m}$ grows with $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$.
choice*: With more data, the class that minimizes the bound can become larger.
choice: The bound-minimizing class has the smallest true risk among the classes considered.
hint: Think of what each term depends on, and remember that the bound is only an upper bound.
solution: A larger class contains the smaller one, so its best risk is no larger. The penalty is an increasing function of $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ and decreases with $m$, so with more data the penalty for a larger class shrinks relative to the gain in approximation error. The minimizer of an upper bound need not minimize the true risk, so the last statement is false.
