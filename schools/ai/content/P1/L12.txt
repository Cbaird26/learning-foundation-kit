@article
A theory paper is a claim with a precise domain of validity. Most mistakes in reading one come from not noticing the domain: the quantifiers, the assumptions, the constants. This last lesson gives a map of the toolbox you now know, a checklist for reading, and an honest list of what remains open.

### The toolbox

| technique | bound depends on | typical strength | typical weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| union bound with Hoeffding (week 2) | $\ln|\mathcal{H}|$ | explicit constants, any finite class | useless for infinite classes |
| VC dimension (weeks 3 to 5) | $d$ only | distribution-free, tight in the worst case | ignores the algorithm and the data |
| Rademacher complexity (week 5) | the class, norms, the data (empirical version) | dimension-free for norm-bounded linear classes | needs the complexity of the whole class |
| margins (week 8) | margin and input norm | explains large-margin separators in huge spaces | fixed margin and norm in advance |
| stability (week 7) | the algorithm | regularized methods, no class needed | needs convex Lipschitz losses for strong rates |
| PAC-Bayes | divergence $\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P)$ from a prior | numerically nonvacuous for some networks | prior fixed before the data |
| online-to-batch (week 10) | regret of an algorithm | algorithmic guarantees from adversarial ones | convex losses, averaged output |
| information-theoretic (Russo and Zou, 2016; Xu and Raginsky, 2017) | mutual information between sample and output | algorithm-dependent | hard to evaluate |

### A checklist for reading a theorem

1. **Setting.** Which loss, which labels (binary, real), which assumptions on the distribution (realizable, i.i.d., bounded)? A result for the 0-1 loss need not extend to the squared loss.
2. **Quantifiers and their order.** Is the bound for every distribution, with the sample size chosen first (PAC)? For all hypotheses simultaneously, or for one fixed hypothesis, or for the output of a specific algorithm? "For all $h$ in the class" can be applied to a learned $h_S$; "for a fixed $h$" cannot.
3. **Constants.** State which version you use and evaluate the bound for realistic numbers. A bound above $1$ on an error rate says nothing.
4. **Tightness.** Is there a lower bound, or an example showing that the rate cannot be improved, in the same setting?
5. **Proof technique and assumptions.** Which assumption does the proof actually use, and does it hold in the setting you care about?

Evaluate before you believe. For instance, the PAC-Bayes bound in the form of McAllester (1999) as sharpened by Maurer (2004), here relaxed with Pinsker's inequality to a difference of risks: fix a prior $P$ over hypotheses before seeing data; for losses in $[0,1]$ and $m \ge 8$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over $S \sim \mathcal{D}^m$, simultaneously for **every** distribution $Q$ over hypotheses,
$$\mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) \le \mathbb{E}_{h \sim Q}L_S(h) + \sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P) + \ln(2\sqrt{m}/\delta)}{2m}} .$$
The posterior $Q$ may depend on the data, which is the point; the prior $P$ may not. With $\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P) = 50$ nats, $m = 10^4$ and $\delta = 0.05$ the penalty is $0.054$.

### Open problems

The list below is a map, not a ranking; check the current literature before choosing a project.

- **Generalization of deep networks** (week 11): a theory that predicts test error from architecture, data and training procedure is missing, and so is agreement on which framework will provide it.
- **Optimization and generalization together.** Which solutions do gradient methods find in nonconvex problems, and why do they generalize? Implicit bias results exist for linear models and special architectures.
- **Computational limits.** Statistical learnability (finite VC dimension) does not imply efficient learnability: Kearns and Valiant (1994) gave cryptographic evidence that some classes that are statistically learnable from polynomially many examples cannot be learned in polynomial time. Which natural problems have a gap between what is statistically and what is computationally possible is a central question.
- **Robustness and shift.** Schmidt and colleagues (2018) showed settings in which adversarially robust generalization needs more data than standard generalization; a general theory of learning under distribution shift is incomplete.
- **Private learning.** Differential privacy changes sample complexity; Alon, Livni, Malliaris and Moran (2019) showed that privately PAC learnable binary classes must have finite Littlestone dimension, a stricter condition than finite VC dimension.
- **Beyond binary classification.** Characterizations of learnability for other tasks and losses require other dimensions; extending the fundamental theorem to rich settings is an active area.

### Contributing

Good beginner contributions are modest: reproduce a bound and evaluate it on a real experiment; find the weakest assumption under which a proof still works; fix a constant; or find an example showing that an assumption is needed. State carefully what you prove, and say when a result is only an observation.

@example
title: Reading a PAC-Bayes bound
problem: A paper states the PAC-Bayes bound above and reports, for a trained network, a posterior $Q$ with expected training error $0.02$ and $\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P) = 200$ on $m = 60000$ examples, with $\delta = 0.05$. What does the bound say, and what must you check?
step: Setting and quantifiers. The bound holds with probability $1 - \delta$ over the sample, simultaneously for all $Q$, for a fixed prior $P$. So it may be applied to a data-dependent posterior, but only if $P$ did not depend on $S$.
step: Evaluate the penalty: $\ln(2\sqrt{60000}/0.05) = \ln(9798) \approx 9.19$, so the square root is $\sqrt{(200 + 9.19)/120000} \approx 0.0418$.
step: The bound on the expected risk of the randomized predictor that samples $h \sim Q$ is $0.02 + 0.0418 \approx 0.062$: informative, because it is well below $1/2$.
step: What to check: whether the prior was chosen independently of the sample, whether the guarantee concerns the randomized predictor (a sample from $Q$) and not a single deterministic network, and whether the loss was bounded in $[0,1]$.
answer: The randomized predictor has error at most about $0.062$ with probability at least $0.95$, if the prior is independent of the sample.

@example
title: Evaluating a VC bound numerically
problem: For a class of VC dimension $d = 1000$ use the one-sided gap bound $\sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m} + \sqrt{\ln(1/\delta)/(2m)}$ with $\delta = 0.05$. For which sample sizes is it informative?
step: Compute the bound for $m = 10^4, 10^5, 10^6, 10^7$.
step: The values are $0.825$, $0.339$, $0.127$ and $0.046$ (rounded).
step: At $m = 10^4$ the bound is above $1/2$, so even with zero training error it does not beat guessing; it needs roughly $1.7 \times 10^6$ examples to guarantee a gap of $0.1$, about $1700$ examples per unit of VC dimension.
step: Conclusion: the bound is correct but conservative; it is far from the sample sizes at which such classes work in practice, which is one of the reasons for the search of week 11.
answer: The bound is informative (below $0.5$) only from roughly $4 \times 10^4$ examples on, and reaches $0.1$ near $1.7 \times 10^6$.

@mistakes
- Using a bound for a fixed hypothesis on the output of a learning algorithm: the output depends on the sample, so a simultaneous guarantee over a class, a stability argument or a PAC-Bayes argument is needed.
- Quoting the form of a bound without its constants: the same result appears with different constants in different sources, and a factor of two can change a conclusion.
- Reading a vacuous bound as evidence against the method: a bound above $1$ says only that the proof technique, with worst-case constants, cannot certify the method.

@practice num
prompt: You evaluate $k = 20$ hyperparameter configurations on a held-out validation set of $m = 1000$ examples and report the best one. Using Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over the $k$ configurations, with probability at least $0.95$ every configuration's validation error is within $\epsilon = \sqrt{\ln(2k/\delta)/(2m)}$ of its true error. What is $\epsilon$? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.058
tol: 0.001
check: sqrt(log(2 * 20 / 0.05) / (2 * 1000))
hint: Here $2k/\delta = 800$.
solution: $\ln 800 \approx 6.685$, and $6.685/2000 = 0.003342$, whose square root is $0.0578 \approx 0.058$. Selecting among $20$ configurations costs only a logarithmic factor in the deviation, but reusing the validation set for thousands of choices would cost more.

@practice num
prompt: A class has VC dimension $d = 200$. Evaluate the Rademacher-based deviation bound $\sqrt{2d\ln(em/d)/m}$ at $m = 50000$ examples. Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.228
tol: 0.001
check: sqrt(2 * 200 * log(e * 50000 / 200) / 50000)
hint: $em/d = 250e$, so $\ln(em/d) = 1 + \ln 250$.
solution: $\ln(250e) = 1 + 5.5215 = 6.5215$, so the bound is $\sqrt{400 \cdot 6.5215/50000} = \sqrt{0.05217} \approx 0.228$. Even at 250 examples per unit of VC dimension, the guaranteed deviation is more than a fifth.

@practice mc
prompt: A theorem states: "for every fixed hypothesis $h$ in $\mathcal{H}$, with probability at least $1 - \delta$ over the sample $S$, $|L_S(h) - L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)| \le \epsilon$." Which use of this theorem is not justified?
choice*: Applying it to the hypothesis $h_S$ returned by ERM trained on the same sample $S$.
choice: Applying it to a hypothesis chosen before the data were drawn.
choice: Combining it with a union bound over a finite set of hypotheses fixed in advance.
choice: Applying it to a hypothesis trained on a different, independent sample and evaluated on $S$.
hint: Ask whether the hypothesis is independent of the sample on which the bound is evaluated.
solution: The statement is conditional on $h$ not depending on $S$. The learned $h_S$ is chosen using $S$, so its empirical risk is biased downward and the statement does not apply. The other uses either fix the hypothesis in advance (possibly after seeing an independent sample) or apply the union bound to a pre-specified finite set.

@practice ms
prompt: Before relying on a published generalization bound for your own problem, which of these checks are sensible? Select all that apply.
choice*: Find out whether the guarantee applies to the output of the training algorithm, or only to a fixed hypothesis.
choice*: Evaluate the bound with the sample size and complexity of your problem to see whether it is below the trivial error rate.
choice*: Check whether a lower bound or an example shows the dependence on the complexity measure cannot be improved.
choice: Check whether the paper uses the word "optimal" in the abstract.
hint: A check is sensible if it bears on what the theorem states, whether its value is informative, or whether it is tight.
solution: The domain of the guarantee (algorithm output versus fixed hypothesis), its numerical value, and its tightness are the content of the checklist. The wording of an abstract carries no mathematical information about the theorem.

@practice self
prompt: Outline a ten-minute talk presenting one learning theory result from this course (for example the fundamental theorem of PAC learning, a Rademacher bound for linear classes, or the Hedge regret bound). Give a title, the one-sentence message, the sequence of five or six slides with what each contains, the one proof idea you would show in full, and a closing slide on what the result does not explain.
hint: Choose one theorem; the audience should leave with its exact statement, why it is true in one idea, and its limits.
solution: Title: "Regret of multiplicative weights: why $\sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$ suffices." Message: a simple exponential-weights rule competes with the best of $N$ experts on every loss sequence, with regret growing only like $\sqrt{T\ln N}$. Slides: (1) the experts game and the definition of regret, with a two-expert example where a deterministic learner has regret at least $T/2$; (2) the Hedge algorithm in three lines; (3) the theorem with its exact statement: for losses in $[0,1]$, $\text{Regret}_T \le \ln N/\eta + \eta T/8$ and $\sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$ for $\eta = \sqrt{8\ln N/T}$; (4) the proof idea shown in full: the potential $W_t = \sum_i w_t(i)$, upper bounded by Hoeffding's lemma applied to $\mathbb{E}_{i \sim p_t}e^{-\eta\ell_t(i)}$ and lower bounded by the weight of the best expert; (5) a small numerical run (three experts, $T = 3$) showing regret below the bound; (6) consequences: online-to-batch conversion and the connection to AdaBoost. Closing slide: the bound is worst case and requires bounded losses and a tuned $\eta$ (or a doubling trick); it does not say which experts are good, and it gives no guarantee relative to a changing comparator. Timing: two minutes for slides 1 and 2, five for slides 3 and 4, three for the rest.
rubric: States one theorem exactly, with its conditions and constants, and a one-sentence message.
rubric: Includes a motivating example, a proof idea shown in full, and a numerical illustration.
rubric: Ends with limits of the result and fits a ten-minute schedule.

@quiz num
prompt: A PAC-Bayes bound (the Maurer form with $m \ge 8$) is evaluated with $\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P) = 120$, $m = 40000$ and $\delta = 0.05$. What is the penalty $\sqrt{(\mathrm{KL}(Q \Vert P) + \ln(2\sqrt{m}/\delta))/(2m)}$? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.040
tol: 0.001
check: sqrt((120 + log(2 * sqrt(40000) / 0.05)) / (2 * 40000))
hint: $2\sqrt{m}/\delta = 2 \cdot 200/0.05 = 8000$.
solution: $\ln 8000 \approx 8.987$, so the numerator is $128.987$ and the penalty is $\sqrt{128.987/80000} = \sqrt{0.0016123} \approx 0.040$.

@quiz mc
prompt: In the PAC-Bayes bound, which object must be fixed before the sample is seen?
choice*: The prior $P$ over hypotheses.
choice: The posterior $Q$.
choice: Neither; both may depend on the sample.
choice: The sample size $m$ must be fixed after seeing the data.
hint: The bound holds simultaneously for all $Q$.
solution: The proof uses the sample only through quantities that have a fixed reference measure, so the prior must be chosen independently of $S$. The bound holds simultaneously for all posteriors, which therefore may depend on the data arbitrarily.

@quiz self
prompt: A paper trains a network on $m = 50000$ examples, reports a training error of $0$ and a test error of $0.08$, and proves a generalization bound that evaluates to $0.97$ on the 0-1 error. Write a short referee comment on what, if anything, the bound shows and what you would ask for.
hint: Compare the bound with the trivial error rate and ask which assumptions the proof needs.
solution: A bound of $0.97$ on a classification error is vacuous: the trivial bound on an error rate is $1$, so the bound certifies almost nothing. A vacuous bound does not show the method is bad, since the test error is $0.08$; it shows that the proof technique, with its constants, cannot certify generalization here, which is the situation the field is trying to resolve. I would ask the author to (1) state precisely what the theorem proves and whether it is vacuous for the reported network, (2) report which quantity in the bound (a norm, a margin, a divergence) dominates, (3) compare with at least one other bound or a simple baseline such as a held-out estimate, which with $10^4$ held-out examples already gives a Hoeffding deviation of about $0.014$ at confidence $0.95$, and (4) avoid presenting the bound as an explanation of the observed test error.
rubric: Identifies the bound as vacuous (near the trivial error) and separates that from the quality of the method.
rubric: Asks for the dominating term, a comparison with other bounds or a held-out estimate, and the assumptions of the proof.
rubric: Does not overclaim: states what the bound can and cannot support.
