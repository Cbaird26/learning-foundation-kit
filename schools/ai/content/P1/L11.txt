@article
The classical picture of weeks 1 to 7 says: more flexible classes fit the training data better and generalize worse, and the best model balances the two. Modern networks break the picture. They have more parameters than training examples, are trained to near-zero training error, and still predict well. This week is an honest map of what is known, with one exactly solvable model in the middle.

### The puzzle

Zhang, Bengio, Hardt, Recht and Vinyals (2017) trained standard image classification architectures with the usual procedure on data in which the labels had been replaced by random ones. The networks reached (near) zero training error on random labels as well as on the true ones. So the class and the algorithm *can* fit noise, and any bound that depends only on the capacity of the class to fit arbitrary labelings (the VC dimension, the Rademacher complexity of the whole class, week 5) is vacuous for these models, which is a theorem about those bounds, not about networks. Yet the same networks generalize on the true labels. Whatever explains this must involve the data, the algorithm, or the particular solution it finds.

### Double descent

Belkin, Hsu, Ma and Mandal (2019) named and documented **double descent**: as model size grows past the **interpolation threshold**, the point at which the model can first fit the training data exactly, the test error first follows the classical U shape, peaks near the threshold, and then *decreases again* in the interpolating regime. They showed this in several model families, including random-feature models and neural networks. The phenomenon is well established empirically in many settings; how widely it holds, and what drives it in deep networks, is still being worked out.

### A solvable model: minimum-norm interpolation

Take $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ with $d > n$ and rank $n$, targets $\mathbf{y}$, and the loss $\frac{1}{2}\lVert \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} - \mathbf{y} \rVert^2$. Infinitely many $\mathbf{w}$ interpolate. Gradient descent started at $\mathbf{0}$ picks one, and the first example proves that it is the **minimum-norm interpolator** $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y}$: the algorithm carries an implicit bias.

How well does it generalize? Let the rows of $\mathbf{X}$ be i.i.d. $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}_d)$, $y = \langle \mathbf{w}^\star, \mathbf{x} \rangle + \sigma\xi$ with noise variance $\sigma^2$ and $\lVert \mathbf{w}^\star \rVert^2 = r^2$. In the asymptotic regime $n, d \to \infty$ with $d/n \to \gamma$, Hastie, Montanari, Rosset and Tibshirani (2022) showed that the excess risk $\lVert \hat{\mathbf{w}} - \mathbf{w}^\star \rVert^2$ of the minimum-norm least squares estimator converges to
$$R(\gamma) = \begin{cases} \sigma^2\,\dfrac{\gamma}{1 - \gamma} & \gamma < 1,\\[2mm] r^2\Big(1 - \dfrac{1}{\gamma}\Big) + \dfrac{\sigma^2}{\gamma - 1} & \gamma > 1.\end{cases}$$
For $\gamma < 1$ this is ordinary least squares. For $\gamma > 1$ the first term is a bias (the signal that cannot be recovered from $n$ samples in $d$ dimensions) and the second a variance. Both sides diverge as $\gamma \to 1$, which is the peak. With $\sigma^2 = 1$ and $r^2 = 4$:

| $\gamma = d/n$ | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.75 | 0.9 | 1.1 | 1.5 | 2 | 4 | 10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| excess risk | 0.33 | 1.00 | 3.00 | 9.00 | 10.36 | 3.33 | 3.00 | 3.33 | 3.71 |

The risk peaks at the threshold, then falls to a second minimum at $\gamma = 1/(1 - \sigma/r) = 2$ and rises slowly toward $r^2 = 4$, the risk of predicting zero. This model is a theorem, not a neural network: it shows that interpolation and good generalization are compatible and that a peak followed by a second descent can arise from a plain linear model; it does not show that the same mechanism operates in deep networks.

### Other proposals

**Benign overfitting.** Bartlett, Long, Lugosi and Tsigler (2020) characterized exactly when minimum-norm interpolation in linear regression predicts well: roughly, the covariance spectrum needs a few strong directions for the signal and a long tail of weak directions that can absorb the noise harmlessly. The isotropic model above has no such tail, which is why its second descent beats the null predictor only when the signal is strong enough ($\sigma < r$).

**Implicit bias.** On linearly separable data, gradient descent on the logistic loss diverges in norm but converges *in direction* to the hard-margin SVM solution (Soudry, Hoffer, Nacson, Gunasekar and Srebro, 2018). Combined with the margin bounds of week 8, this is an algorithm-dependent explanation for linear models.

**Kernel regime.** Wide networks trained in a suitable scaling behave like kernel regression with the neural tangent kernel (Jacot, Gabriel and Hongler, 2018). Whether practical networks operate in this regime, or in a feature-learning regime where it fails, is debated.

**Norm-based and compression-based bounds.** Bounds that depend on weight norms, margins or the PAC-Bayes divergence of the learned network, and bounds based on compressibility, can be numerically nonvacuous for some trained networks (Dziugaite and Roy, 2017, for a PAC-Bayes bound). Nagarajan and Kolter (2019) constructed settings where *any* uniform convergence bound over a natural norm ball must be vacuous even though the learned classifier generalizes, an argument that algorithm- and data-dependent analyses are needed.

### What is open

There is no accepted theory that predicts, from the architecture, data and training procedure, the test error of a practical deep network. Researchers disagree about how far the proposals above (and refinements of them) will go: some expect that suitable data- and algorithm-dependent bounds will close the gap within the classical framework; others expect that explaining feature learning needs new mathematics. When you read a paper in this area, ask which regime its theorem covers, what it assumes about the data, and whether it explains the experiment or only a simplified model.

@example
title: Gradient descent converges to the minimum-norm interpolator
problem: Let $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}$ have rank $n$ (so $d \ge n$) and let $\mathbf{w}_0 = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{w}_{k+1} = \mathbf{w}_k - \eta\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_k - \mathbf{y})$ with $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)$. Prove that $\mathbf{w}_k \to \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y}$, the interpolator of smallest Euclidean norm.
step: Row space. By induction every $\mathbf{w}_k$ lies in the row space $\operatorname{range}(\mathbf{X}^\top)$: $\mathbf{w}_0 = \mathbf{0}$ does, and each update adds a vector of the form $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{v}$.
step: Residuals. Let $\mathbf{r}_k = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_k - \mathbf{y}$. Then $\mathbf{r}_{k+1} = (\mathbf{I} - \eta\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)\mathbf{r}_k$. Since $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top$ is symmetric positive definite (rank $n$) with eigenvalues in $(0, \lambda_{\max}]$ and $0 < \eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}$, all eigenvalues of $\mathbf{I} - \eta\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top$ lie in $(-1, 1)$, so $\mathbf{r}_k \to \mathbf{0}$ geometrically.
step: Convergence of the iterates. $\lVert \mathbf{w}_{k+1} - \mathbf{w}_k \rVert = \eta\lVert \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{r}_k \rVert$ is bounded by a geometric sequence, so $(\mathbf{w}_k)$ is Cauchy and converges to some $\mathbf{w}_\infty$. It lies in the row space (a closed subspace) and satisfies $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_\infty = \mathbf{y}$.
step: Minimum norm. If $\mathbf{u}$ is any other interpolator then $\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{w}_\infty \in \ker\mathbf{X}$, which is orthogonal to the row space that contains $\mathbf{w}_\infty$. Hence $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert^2 = \lVert \mathbf{w}_\infty \rVert^2 + \lVert \mathbf{u} - \mathbf{w}_\infty \rVert^2 \ge \lVert \mathbf{w}_\infty \rVert^2$.
step: Formula. The vector $\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ lies in the row space and interpolates, since $\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{y}$. By the minimum norm argument an interpolator in the row space is unique, so it equals $\mathbf{w}_\infty$.
answer: $\mathbf{w}_k \to \mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^\top)^{-1}\mathbf{y}$, the interpolator with the smallest norm.

@example
title: Interpolation with one example
problem: One training example $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)$ has label $y = 5$ ($n = 1$, $d = 2$). Find the minimum-norm interpolating linear predictor and compare it with another interpolator.
step: The interpolators are the $\mathbf{w}$ with $w_1 + 2w_2 = 5$: a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
step: The formula gives $\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{x}\,y/\lVert \mathbf{x} \rVert^2 = (1, 2) \cdot 5/5 = (1, 2)$, with norm $\sqrt{5} \approx 2.236$.
step: Another interpolator is $(5, 0)$, with norm $5$. The difference $(4, -2)$ is orthogonal to $\mathbf{x}$, so $\lVert (5,0) \rVert^2 = \lVert (1,2) \rVert^2 + \lVert (4,-2) \rVert^2 = 5 + 20 = 25$, as the Pythagorean argument predicts.
answer: $\mathbf{w} = (1, 2)$, norm $\sqrt{5}$; the interpolator $(5, 0)$ has a larger norm.

@mistakes
- Treating double descent as a refutation of the bias-variance trade-off or of statistical learning theory: the theorems of weeks 1 to 8 remain true; what fails is the intuition that more parameters always means worse generalization, and some bounds become vacuous.
- Generalizing from the isotropic linear model to deep networks: the formula $R(\gamma)$ is a theorem for that model only, and its second descent can fail to beat the null predictor.
- Saying that overparameterized networks "do not overfit": they interpolate the training data, including noise, and may still generalize; the open question is why.

@practice self
prompt: A colleague writes in a draft: "Double descent shows that classical statistical learning theory is wrong about overfitting." Write a one-paragraph critique for a referee report: say which parts of the claim the evidence supports, which parts it does not, and what you would ask the author to add.
hint: Separate the empirical phenomenon, the classical theorems (which are conditional statements) and the interpretation of the bias-variance curve. Use the isotropic linear model as an example.
solution: The empirical observation, that test error can decrease again as models grow past the interpolation threshold, is well documented (Belkin and colleagues, 2019, in several model families) and a solvable linear model reproduces the peak and the second descent, so the claim that the classical U-shaped picture is not universal is supported. The claim that classical theory is wrong is not: the theorems of learning theory are conditional statements (uniform convergence bounds, stability bounds) that remain true; what the observations show is that bounds based on the capacity of the whole class are vacuous for interpolating models, so the capacity of the class is the wrong quantity to track, not that bounds as such fail. Norm-based, margin-based and PAC-Bayes analyses, and the implicit bias of the training algorithm, are all inside the classical framework, and the analysis of minimum-norm interpolation uses exactly its tools. I would ask the author to state which model family the claim concerns, to say what is proved and what is observed, to report the dependence on noise level and dataset size (the second descent need not beat the null predictor), and to replace "wrong" by a precise statement about which bound or intuition fails.
rubric: Distinguishes the supported empirical claim (double descent exists in documented settings) from the unsupported conclusion (classical theory is wrong).
rubric: Explains that theorems are conditional and that vacuity of capacity-based bounds is a statement about those bounds, mentioning alternative frameworks (norms, margins, PAC-Bayes, implicit bias).
rubric: Makes concrete requests of the author (scope, proved versus observed, dependence on noise and data size, precise wording).
