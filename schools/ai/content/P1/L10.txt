@article
Statistical learning assumes the data are drawn i.i.d. from a fixed distribution. Online learning removes that assumption entirely and still gets guarantees. The setting is a game. In each round $t = 1, \dots, T$ the learner chooses a probability vector $\mathbf{p}_t$ over $N$ **experts**; then an adversary, who may know the learner's algorithm and the whole past, reveals a loss vector $\boldsymbol{\ell}_t \in [0,1]^N$; the learner suffers the expected loss $\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle$. Its **regret** after $T$ rounds is
$$\text{Regret}_T = \sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle - \min_{i \in [N]}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ell_t(i),$$
the excess over the best single expert in hindsight. A regret that grows like $o(T)$ means that the average loss per round approaches that of the best expert, on *every* loss sequence.

Randomization is needed. With two experts and $\{0,1\}$ losses, a learner that deterministically follows one expert in each round can be given loss $1$ every round by an adversary that sets the chosen expert's loss to $1$ and the other's to $0$. The learner loses $T$, the two experts together lose $T$, so the better expert loses at most $T/2$: the regret is at least $T/2$.

### The multiplicative weights (Hedge) algorithm

Weighted majority (Littlestone and Warmuth, 1994) and Hedge (Freund and Schapire, 1997) keep a weight per expert and shrink the weights of experts that lose. With a learning rate $\eta > 0$: set $w_1(i) = 1$; in round $t$ play $p_t(i) = w_t(i)/W_t$ where $W_t = \sum_j w_t(j)$; after seeing $\boldsymbol{\ell}_t$ set $w_{t+1}(i) = w_t(i)\,e^{-\eta\ell_t(i)}$.

```python
from math import exp

def hedge_distributions(losses, eta):
    """The distributions p_1, ..., p_T that Hedge plays on a loss table."""
    n = len(losses[0])
    w = [1.0] * n
    plays = []
    for loss in losses:
        total = sum(w)
        plays.append([x / total for x in w])
        w = [x * exp(-eta * l) for x, l in zip(w, loss)]
    return plays

for p in hedge_distributions([(1, 0), (1, 0)], 1.0):
    print([round(x, 4) for x in p])
```

The program prints `[0.5, 0.5]` then `[0.2689, 0.7311]`: after expert 0 loses once, its weight is multiplied by $e^{-1}$.

### The regret bound

**Theorem (Hedge).** For any $N \ge 2$, $\eta > 0$ and any sequence of loss vectors in $[0,1]^N$ (chosen adaptively if you like),
$$\text{Regret}_T \le \frac{\ln N}{\eta} + \frac{\eta T}{8}.$$
With $\eta = \sqrt{8\ln N/T}$ this is $\text{Regret}_T \le \sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$.

The statement is deterministic: there is no probability, no assumption about the sequence and no dependence on the number of examples beyond $T$. The proof tracks the potential $W_t$. The second example gives it in full; it uses **Hoeffding's lemma**: if $X \in [0,1]$ and $s \in \mathbb{R}$, then $\ln\mathbb{E}\,e^{sX} \le s\,\mathbb{E}X + s^2/8$, which the first example proves. This is the version with constant $1/8$; weaker lemmas give bounds with larger constants.

A numerical run: three experts, $\eta = 0.5$, loss vectors $(1, 0, 0.5)$, $(1, 0, 1)$, $(0, 1, 0.5)$. Hedge plays $(0.333, 0.333, 0.333)$, then $(0.254, 0.419, 0.327)$, then $(0.200, 0.543, 0.257)$, for a total expected loss of $1.7525$. The best expert (the second) loses $1$, so the regret is $0.7525$, below the bound $\ln 3/0.5 + 0.5 \cdot 3/8 = 2.385$.

### What the bound says, and what lies around it

The dependence on the number of experts is only logarithmic, so Hedge can compete with exponentially many experts if the weights can be maintained efficiently. Lower bounds of order $\sqrt{T\ln N}$ hold for any algorithm (see Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi), so the rate is optimal up to constants. Tuning $\eta$ needs to know $T$; a doubling trick or a time-varying learning rate gives bounds of the same order without it, with larger constants. In the realizable mistake-bound model with a finite class, the **halving algorithm** (predict by majority vote of the consistent hypotheses) makes at most $\log_2|\mathcal{H}|$ mistakes, and Littlestone (1988) characterized the optimal mistake bound by a combinatorial dimension that now bears his name. AdaBoost of last week is multiplicative weights over the examples.

Online guarantees also give statistical ones. If the examples are i.i.d. and the losses are convex, averaging the iterates of an online algorithm with regret $R_T$ gives a predictor whose expected risk exceeds the best in the class by at most $R_T/T$. The practice problems ask you to prove this online-to-batch conversion, which is how stochastic gradient descent guarantees are usually derived.

@example
title: Hoeffding's lemma
problem: Prove that if $X \in [0,1]$ with mean $\mu$, then for every real $s$, $\ln\mathbb{E}\,e^{sX} \le s\mu + s^2/8$.
step: Convexity of $x \mapsto e^{sx}$ on $[0,1]$ gives $e^{sx} \le (1 - x) + x e^{s}$. Taking expectations, $\mathbb{E}\,e^{sX} \le 1 - \mu + \mu e^{s}$.
step: Define $\varphi(s) = \ln(1 - \mu + \mu e^{s}) - s\mu$. It suffices to show $\varphi(s) \le s^2/8$. We have $\varphi(0) = 0$.
step: Differentiate: $\varphi'(s) = \dfrac{\mu e^{s}}{1 - \mu + \mu e^{s}} - \mu$, so $\varphi'(0) = 0$. Let $q(s) = \dfrac{\mu e^{s}}{1 - \mu + \mu e^{s}} \in (0,1)$. Then $\varphi'(s) = q(s) - \mu$ and $q'(s) = q(s)(1 - q(s))$, hence $\varphi''(s) = q(s)(1 - q(s)) \le 1/4$.
step: By Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder, $\varphi(s) = \varphi(0) + s\varphi'(0) + \frac{s^2}{2}\varphi''(\xi) \le \frac{s^2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4} = \frac{s^2}{8}$ for some $\xi$ between $0$ and $s$. (The degenerate cases $\mu \in \{0, 1\}$ are trivial.)
answer: $\ln\mathbb{E}\,e^{sX} \le s\mu + s^2/8$.

@example
title: Proof of the Hedge regret bound
problem: Prove that Hedge satisfies $\text{Regret}_T \le \ln N/\eta + \eta T/8$ for all loss sequences in $[0,1]^N$.
step: Potential. Let $W_t = \sum_i w_t(i)$, so $W_1 = N$. Then $\dfrac{W_{t+1}}{W_t} = \sum_i \dfrac{w_t(i)}{W_t}e^{-\eta\ell_t(i)} = \mathbb{E}_{i \sim \mathbf{p}_t}\,e^{-\eta\ell_t(i)}$.
step: Apply Hoeffding's lemma to the random variable $X = \ell_t(i)$ with $i \sim \mathbf{p}_t$, which lies in $[0,1]$ and has mean $\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle$, with $s = -\eta$: $\dfrac{W_{t+1}}{W_t} \le \exp\big(-\eta\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle + \eta^2/8\big)$.
step: Multiply over $t = 1, \dots, T$ and take logarithms: $\ln\dfrac{W_{T+1}}{W_1} \le -\eta\sum_{t}\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle + \dfrac{\eta^2 T}{8}$.
step: Lower bound the potential by the weight of the best expert $i^\star$: $W_{T+1} \ge w_{T+1}(i^\star) = \exp\big(-\eta L^\star\big)$, where $L^\star = \sum_t \ell_t(i^\star)$ is the best cumulative loss. Therefore $\ln(W_{T+1}/W_1) \ge -\eta L^\star - \ln N$.
step: Combine the two bounds: $-\eta L^\star - \ln N \le -\eta L_{\text{alg}} + \eta^2 T/8$, where $L_{\text{alg}} = \sum_t\langle \mathbf{p}_t, \boldsymbol{\ell}_t \rangle$. Rearranging and dividing by $\eta$ gives $L_{\text{alg}} - L^\star \le \ln N/\eta + \eta T/8$. The choice $\eta = \sqrt{8\ln N/T}$ makes both terms equal to $\sqrt{T\ln N/8}$, whose sum is $\sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$.
answer: $\text{Regret}_T \le \ln N/\eta + \eta T/8$, and $\le \sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$ for the tuned $\eta$.

@mistakes
- Treating the regret bound as a statement about expected behavior under a distribution: it holds for every sequence of losses, including adaptive ones, because the expectation is over the learner's own randomization only.
- Forgetting that the tuned learning rate depends on $T$: with a wrong $\eta$ the bound is $\ln N/\eta + \eta T/8$, which can be linear in $T$ if $\eta$ does not shrink.
- Confusing regret with loss: a regret of $\sqrt{(T/2)\ln N}$ can accompany a very large total loss if all experts are poor.

model:
```python
from math import exp, log, sqrt

def anytime_hedge_loss(losses):
    """Total expected loss of Hedge with learning rate sqrt(8 ln N / t)."""
    n = len(losses[0])
    cum = [0.0] * n
    total = 0.0
    for t, loss in enumerate(losses, start=1):
        eta = sqrt(8 * log(n) / t)
        w = [exp(-eta * c) for c in cum]
        z = 0.0
        for x in w:
            z += x
        total += sum(x / z * l for x, l in zip(w, loss))
        cum = [c + l for c, l in zip(cum, loss)]
    return total
```
hint: Keep the cumulative losses `cum` of the experts; in round `t` compute the weights from `cum` with $\eta_t$, accumulate the expected loss, and only then add the round's losses to `cum`.
solution: Unlike fixed-rate Hedge, the weights are recomputed from the cumulative losses with the current rate, not updated multiplicatively, since the rate changes. The weights in round $t$ use losses up to round $t - 1$ only: adding the current loss before computing the play would let the learner see the adversary's move. In the first round all cumulative losses are zero, so the play is uniform. The test with one expert checks that nothing breaks when $\ln N = 0$.

@practice self
prompt: Let $\mathcal{W}$ be a convex set, $\ell(\mathbf{w}, z)$ convex in $\mathbf{w}$, and $z_1, \dots, z_T$ i.i.d. from $\mathcal{D}$, with $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbb{E}_z\ell(\mathbf{w}, z)$. An online algorithm produces $\mathbf{w}_t \in \mathcal{W}$ as a function of $z_1, \dots, z_{t-1}$ only, and satisfies $\sum_{t}\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t) - \sum_t\ell(\mathbf{u}, z_t) \le R_T$ for every sequence $z_1, \dots, z_T$ and every $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{W}$. Prove that $\bar{\mathbf{w}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_t\mathbf{w}_t$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}\,L_{\mathcal{D}}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}) \le \min_{\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{W}}L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{u}) + R_T/T$.
hint: Use convexity of $L_{\mathcal{D}}$ to compare $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\bar{\mathbf{w}})$ with the average of $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}_t)$, and use independence of $z_t$ from $\mathbf{w}_t$ to replace $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}_t)$ by $\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t)$ in expectation.
solution: Fix $\mathbf{u} \in \mathcal{W}$. Since $\ell(\cdot, z)$ is convex for each $z$, $L_{\mathcal{D}}$ is convex, so by Jensen's inequality $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}) \le \frac{1}{T}\sum_t L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}_t)$. The iterate $\mathbf{w}_t$ is a function of $z_1, \dots, z_{t-1}$ and $z_t$ is independent of them, so $\mathbb{E}[\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t) \mid z_1, \dots, z_{t-1}] = L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}_t)$ and hence $\mathbb{E}\,L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{w}_t) = \mathbb{E}\,\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t)$. Taking expectations of the regret inequality, which holds for every sequence, gives $\sum_t\mathbb{E}\,\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t) \le \sum_t\mathbb{E}\,\ell(\mathbf{u}, z_t) + R_T = T\,L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{u}) + R_T$. Combining, $\mathbb{E}\,L_{\mathcal{D}}(\bar{\mathbf{w}}) \le \frac{1}{T}\sum_t\mathbb{E}\,\ell(\mathbf{w}_t, z_t) \le L_{\mathcal{D}}(\mathbf{u}) + R_T/T$. Since $\mathbf{u}$ was arbitrary, the bound holds for the minimum over $\mathcal{W}$. An algorithm with regret $O(\sqrt{T})$ therefore gives excess risk $O(1/\sqrt{T})$.
rubric: Uses convexity (Jensen) to bound the risk of the average iterate by the average of the iterates' risks.
rubric: Uses the independence of $z_t$ from $\mathbf{w}_t$ to replace the risk by the loss on the next example in expectation.
rubric: Takes expectations of the regret inequality for a fixed comparator, divides by $T$, and concludes for the minimizer.
