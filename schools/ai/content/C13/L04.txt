@article
After 3 flips that all land heads, maximum likelihood says $\hat\theta = 1$: the coin will never land tails. Few people believe that. The data are tiny, and we had prior knowledge that coins are usually near fair. **Maximum a posteriori** (MAP) estimation writes that knowledge down and combines it with the data.

### The MAP estimate

Treat the parameter as a random variable with a **prior** density $p(\boldsymbol{\theta})$ chosen before seeing data. Bayes' rule gives the **posterior**

$$p(\boldsymbol{\theta} \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{p(\mathcal{D})} \propto p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta})\,p(\boldsymbol{\theta}).$$

The **MAP estimate** is the posterior mode. The evidence $p(\mathcal{D})$ does not depend on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and drops out, so

$$\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ \big[\log p(\mathcal{D} \mid \boldsymbol{\theta}) + \log p(\boldsymbol{\theta})\big].$$

It is the maximum-likelihood problem plus a **log-prior** term. With a flat prior (constant $p(\boldsymbol\theta)$) the MAP estimate equals the MLE.

### Bernoulli with a Beta prior

Take the $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ prior, $p(\theta) \propto \theta^{\alpha - 1}(1 - \theta)^{\beta - 1}$. With $k$ heads in $n$ flips the log-posterior is, up to a constant,
$$(k + \alpha - 1)\log\theta + (n - k + \beta - 1)\log(1 - \theta).$$
This has the same form as the Bernoulli log-likelihood with $\alpha - 1$ extra heads and $\beta - 1$ extra tails, so (for $k + \alpha > 1$ and $n - k + \beta > 1$)
$$\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{n + \alpha + \beta - 2}.$$
With the prior $\operatorname{Beta}(2, 2)$ and 7 heads in 10 flips, $\hat\theta_{\text{MAP}} = 8/12 \approx 0.667$, pulled from the MLE $0.7$ toward $0.5$. For 3 heads in 3 flips it is $4/5 = 0.8$ rather than 1. The prior acts like **pseudo-counts**: $\alpha - 1$ imaginary heads and $\beta - 1$ imaginary tails.

### Gaussian mean with a Gaussian prior

Let $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with $\sigma^2$ known and prior $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_0, \tau^2)$. The log-posterior is $-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2 - \frac{1}{2\tau^2}(\mu - \mu_0)^2$ plus a constant. Setting the derivative to zero, $\frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu) - \frac{1}{\tau^2}(\mu - \mu_0) = 0$, gives

$$\hat\mu_{\text{MAP}} = \frac{n\bar{x}/\sigma^2 + \mu_0/\tau^2}{n/\sigma^2 + 1/\tau^2}.$$

This is a **precision-weighted average** of the data mean $\bar x$ (precision $n/\sigma^2$) and the prior mean $\mu_0$ (precision $1/\tau^2$). As $n \to \infty$ the data term dominates and $\hat\mu_{\text{MAP}} \to \bar x$: with enough data the prior is washed out.

### Regularization as a prior

Consider linear regression $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ and a Gaussian prior $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2\mathbf{I})$. The negative log-posterior is

$$\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2 + \text{const}.$$

Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$ (which does not change the minimizer) gives the **ridge regression** objective $\lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert \mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2$ with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. Setting the gradient $-2\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{w}$ to zero gives

$$\hat{\mathbf{w}}_{\text{MAP}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}.$$

So **L2 regularization (weight decay) is MAP estimation under a Gaussian prior**, and the penalty strength $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ is large when the noise is large or the prior is tight (small $\tau^2$). A **Laplace** prior $p(w_j) \propto e^{-|w_j|/b}$ gives the objective $\lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$ with $\lambda = 2\sigma^2/b$: **L1 regularization** (the lasso), which tends to produce exact zeros.

### Limits of MAP

MAP is still a **point estimate**: it discards the width of the posterior. It also depends on how the parameter is written. The MLE is unchanged by reparameterization, but the posterior mode is not: a density transforms with a Jacobian factor, so the mode of $p(\theta \mid \mathcal D)$ and the mode of the induced density of $\phi = g(\theta)$ are generally different points. The next lessons keep the whole posterior instead.

### In code

```python
def map_mean(xs, sigma2, mu0, tau2):
    n = len(xs)
    xbar = sum(xs) / n
    num = n * xbar / sigma2 + mu0 / tau2
    return num / (n / sigma2 + 1 / tau2)

print(map_mean([1.0, 2.0, 3.0], 1.0, 0.0, 1.0))
```

@example
title: Ridge regression in one dimension
problem: Fit $y = w x$ to the points $(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 7)$ with noise variance $\sigma^2 = 1$ and prior $w \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)$ with $\tau^2 = 0.5$. Find the MLE and the MAP estimate of $w$.
step: The sums are $\sum x_i y_i = 2 + 6 + 21 = 29$ and $\sum x_i^2 = 1 + 4 + 9 = 14$.
step: Maximum likelihood is least squares: $\hat w_{\text{ML}} = 29/14 \approx 2.071$.
step: The penalty strength is $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2 = 1/0.5 = 2$.
step: In one dimension the ridge solution is $\hat w = \sum x_i y_i/(\sum x_i^2 + \lambda) = 29/16 = 1.8125$.
step: Check the direction: the prior is centered at 0, so the estimate is shrunk from $2.071$ toward 0.
answer: $\hat w_{\text{ML}} \approx 2.071$ and $\hat w_{\text{MAP}} = 1.8125$.

@example
title: MAP estimate of a Gaussian mean
problem: Four measurements have sample mean $\bar x = 3$ and are modeled as $\mathcal{N}(\mu, 2)$ (variance 2). The prior is $\mu \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. Find the MAP estimate and the weight placed on the prior.
step: Precision of the data: $n/\sigma^2 = 4/2 = 2$. Precision of the prior: $1/\tau^2 = 1$.
step: Numerator: $n\bar x/\sigma^2 + \mu_0/\tau^2 = 4(3)/2 + 0 = 6$. Denominator: $2 + 1 = 3$.
step: $\hat\mu_{\text{MAP}} = 6/3 = 2$.
step: The prior's weight is $1/(2 + 1) = 1/3$ and the data's weight is $2/3$, so $\hat\mu = \tfrac23(3) + \tfrac13(0) = 2$.
answer: $\hat\mu_{\text{MAP}} = 2$, with weight $1/3$ on the prior mean.

@mistakes
- Using the Beta mean instead of the mode: the MAP estimate of the Bernoulli parameter is $(k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)$, with the $-1$ and $-2$; the posterior mean (next lesson) has none.
- Mixing up the direction of the ridge strength: $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ grows when the prior variance $\tau^2$ shrinks, so a tighter prior means stronger regularization.
- Calling MAP "Bayesian inference": it is a point estimate that keeps only the posterior mode and ignores the posterior's spread.
- Penalizing the intercept or using a prior in an inconvenient parameterization without noticing: the MAP estimate changes if you reparameterize.

prompt: Show that the MAP estimate of a Bernoulli parameter with prior $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ after $k$ successes in $n$ trials is $(k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)$ (assuming $k + \alpha > 1$ and $n - k + \beta > 1$), and explain what happens to the difference between the MAP estimate and the MLE as $n$ grows.

solution: Up to a constant the log-posterior is $f(\theta) = (k + \alpha - 1)\log\theta + (n - k + \beta - 1)\log(1 - \theta)$. Its derivative is $f'(\theta) = \frac{k + \alpha - 1}{\theta} - \frac{n - k + \beta - 1}{1 - \theta}$, which is zero when $(k + \alpha - 1)(1 - \theta) = (n - k + \beta - 1)\theta$, that is, $\theta = (k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)$. Both coefficients are positive under the stated conditions, so $f$ is concave and this is the maximum. For fixed $\alpha, \beta$ the difference from $k/n$ is $O(1/n)$: the prior contributes a fixed number of pseudo-counts that become negligible relative to $n$, so MAP and MLE agree asymptotically.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about MAP estimation are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: With a flat prior (constant density), the MAP estimate equals the MLE.
choice*: For fixed prior, the MAP and ML estimates of a Bernoulli parameter converge as the number of flips grows.
choice*: A Gaussian prior on the weights of a linear model with Gaussian noise leads to the ridge penalty $\lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_2^2$.
choice: A larger prior variance $\tau^2$ leads to a larger ridge penalty $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$.
choice: The MAP estimate does not change if the parameter is reparameterized, as for the MLE.
hint: Recall $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ and how a density changes under a change of variables.
solution: With a constant prior the log-prior is a constant that does not move the maximizer. Pseudo-counts from the prior become negligible as $n$ grows. The Gaussian log-prior is the squared norm. A larger $\tau^2$ means a weaker prior and a smaller $\lambda$. The mode of a density is not preserved under nonlinear reparameterization because of the Jacobian, so MAP, unlike the MLE, depends on the parameterization.

@quiz self
prompt: Derive the ridge regression solution $\hat{\mathbf{w}} = (\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$ as the MAP estimate for $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w} + \boldsymbol\varepsilon$ with $\boldsymbol\varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ and prior $\mathbf{w} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \tau^2\mathbf{I})$, identifying $\lambda$, and explain why the matrix being inverted is always invertible when $\lambda > 0$.
hint: Write the negative log-posterior, multiply by $2\sigma^2$, differentiate with respect to $\mathbf{w}$, and think about the eigenvalues of $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$.
solution: The negative log-posterior is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \frac{1}{2\tau^2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \text{const}$. Multiplying by $2\sigma^2$ gives $J(\mathbf{w}) = \lVert\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$ with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$. Its gradient is $-2\mathbf{X}^\top(\mathbf{y} - \mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}) + 2\lambda\mathbf{w}$; setting it to zero gives $(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{w} = \mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{y}$, hence the formula. The matrix $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X}$ is symmetric positive semidefinite with eigenvalues $s_j \ge 0$, so $\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I}$ has eigenvalues $s_j + \lambda \ge \lambda > 0$ and is invertible even when $\mathbf{X}$ has dependent columns. The Hessian $2(\mathbf{X}^\top\mathbf{X} + \lambda\mathbf{I})$ is positive definite, so the stationary point is the unique minimizer.
