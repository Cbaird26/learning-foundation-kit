A new model variant has been tried on 4 users and none converted. The MLE of the conversion rate is 0, so a plug-in prediction says the next user converts with probability exactly 0. Would you bet your life savings on that? The Bayesian answer averages over what we still do not know.

### The posterior predictive distribution

Given data $\mathcal{D}$ and a posterior $p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})$, the **posterior predictive distribution** of a new observation $x^\ast$ averages the model's prediction over all plausible parameters:

$$p(x^\ast \mid \mathcal{D}) = \int p(x^\ast \mid \theta)\,p(\theta \mid \mathcal{D})\,d\theta.$$

A **plug-in** prediction instead uses a single estimate, $p(x^\ast \mid \hat\theta)$, and ignores parameter uncertainty.

**Beta-Bernoulli.** For a Bernoulli outcome with posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha', \beta')$, $P(x^\ast = 1 \mid \mathcal{D}) = \int \theta\,\operatorname{Beta}(\theta; \alpha', \beta')\,d\theta$, which is the posterior mean $\alpha'/(\alpha' + \beta')$. For the prior $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and $k$ successes in $n$ trials,

$$P(x^\ast = 1 \mid \mathcal{D}) = \frac{\alpha + k}{\alpha + \beta + n}.$$

With the uniform prior $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ this is $(k + 1)/(n + 2)$, called **Laplace's rule of succession**. With $k = 0$ and $n = 4$ it gives $1/6 \approx 0.167$, not 0: the data make a high rate unlikely but do not rule it out.

**Gaussian with known variance.** If the posterior for $\mu$ is $\mathcal{N}(\mu_n, \tau_n^2)$ and a new $x^\ast \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then $x^\ast = \mu + \varepsilon$ with $\mu$ and $\varepsilon$ independent Gaussians, so

$$x^\ast \mid \mathcal{D} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_n,\ \sigma^2 + \tau_n^2).$$

The predictive variance is the noise variance plus the posterior uncertainty about the mean; as $n \to \infty$, $\tau_n^2 \to 0$ and the predictive approaches the plug-in $\mathcal{N}(\hat\mu, \sigma^2)$.

### The marginal likelihood

How do we compare two models, say $M_1$ and $M_2$? Bayes' rule applied to models gives the **posterior odds**

$$\frac{P(M_1 \mid \mathcal{D})}{P(M_2 \mid \mathcal{D})} = \underbrace{\frac{p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_1)}{p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2)}}_{\text{Bayes factor}} \times \frac{P(M_1)}{P(M_2)}.$$

The quantity $p(\mathcal{D} \mid M) = \int p(\mathcal{D} \mid \theta, M)\,p(\theta \mid M)\,d\theta$ is the **marginal likelihood** or **evidence**: the probability the model assigned to the data, averaged over its prior. It is not the maximized likelihood; a flexible model must spread its prior probability over many parameter values, and only some predict the data well.

For the coin with a prior $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$ and one specific sequence containing $k$ successes in $n$ trials, the integral is a Beta function:

$$p(\mathcal{D}) = \int \theta^k(1 - \theta)^{n-k}\,\frac{\theta^{\alpha-1}(1-\theta)^{\beta-1}}{B(\alpha, \beta)}\,d\theta = \frac{B(\alpha + k,\ \beta + n - k)}{B(\alpha, \beta)}.$$

Compare a **fair-coin model** $M_1$ ($\theta = 0.5$, no free parameters, evidence $0.5^n$) with a **flexible model** $M_2$ ($\theta \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$). For a sequence of 10 flips with 7 heads, $p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_1) = 2^{-10} \approx 9.77 \times 10^{-4}$ and $p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2) = B(8, 4) = 7!\,3!/11! \approx 7.58 \times 10^{-4}$. The Bayes factor is $1.29$ in favor of the fair coin, even though the flexible model contains $\theta = 0.7$ and fits better. The flexible model wastes prior mass on values like $\theta = 0.1$. This is the **Occam effect**. With 9 heads in 10, $p(\mathcal{D} \mid M_2) = B(10, 2) = 1/110 \approx 9.09 \times 10^{-3}$, and the Bayes factor is about $9.3$ in favor of the flexible model: now the data are too lopsided for a fair coin.

### Cautions

Evidence is sensitive to the prior: widening a prior lowers the evidence of the model, so priors must be chosen deliberately, and improper priors (not integrating to 1) make the evidence undefined up to an arbitrary constant. Evidence ratios answer "which model predicted the observed data better, on average over its prior", which differs from "which model predicts new data best after training"; held-out likelihood and cross-validation (Lesson 12 discusses how to compare models' held-out scores) address the latter. For large $n$ the log-evidence is approximated by the maximized log-likelihood minus a complexity penalty of $\frac{d}{2}\log n$ ($d$ parameters), the idea behind the Bayesian information criterion.

### In code

```python
from math import lgamma

def log_beta(a, b):
    return lgamma(a) + lgamma(b) - lgamma(a + b)

def log_evidence(k, n, a, b):
    return log_beta(a + k, b + n - k) - log_beta(a, b)

print(round(log_evidence(7, 10, 1, 1), 4))
```

Working with logarithms avoids underflow for long data sets.

@example
title: A posterior predictive probability
problem: A prior $\operatorname{Beta}(2, 3)$ is placed on the click rate of an ad. In 5 impressions, 4 clicks are observed. Find the posterior predictive probability of a click on the next impression, and compare it with the plug-in predictions from the MLE and the MAP estimate.
step: Posterior: $\operatorname{Beta}(2 + 4,\ 3 + 1) = \operatorname{Beta}(6, 4)$.
step: Predictive: $P(x^\ast = 1 \mid \mathcal{D}) = 6/(6 + 4) = 0.6$.
step: The MLE is $4/5 = 0.8$, and the MAP estimate (the mode) is $(6 - 1)/(6 + 4 - 2) = 5/8 = 0.625$.
step: The predictive probability $0.6$ is the posterior mean and is pulled closest to the prior mean $2/5 = 0.4$, because it accounts for the uncertainty rather than selecting a single value.
answer: $P(\text{click}) = 0.6$, versus $0.8$ (MLE) and $0.625$ (MAP).

@example
title: A Bayes factor for three heads
problem: Three flips all land heads (the sequence HHH). Compare the fair-coin model $M_1$ ($\theta = 0.5$) with the model $M_2$ having $\theta \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$. If the models start with equal prior probability, what is the posterior probability of $M_2$?
step: The fair-coin evidence is $p(\mathcal D \mid M_1) = 0.5^3 = 1/8$.
step: The flexible evidence is $B(1 + 3,\ 1 + 0)/B(1, 1) = B(4, 1) = \Gamma(4)\Gamma(1)/\Gamma(5) = 6/24 = 1/4$.
step: The Bayes factor for $M_2$ over $M_1$ is $(1/4)/(1/8) = 2$.
step: With prior odds $1$, the posterior odds are $2$, so $P(M_2 \mid \mathcal{D}) = 2/(2 + 1) = 2/3$.
answer: Bayes factor $2$ in favor of $M_2$; $P(M_2 \mid \mathcal{D}) = 2/3$.

@mistakes
- Using the plug-in estimate for prediction and so assigning probability exactly 0 or 1 after a short run of identical outcomes: the predictive probability (for example Laplace's rule) never reaches 0 or 1 with a proper prior.
- Comparing maximized likelihoods and calling it model comparison: the evidence averages the likelihood over the prior, which automatically penalizes unnecessary flexibility.
- Reading a Bayes factor as the posterior probability of a model: posterior odds equal the Bayes factor times the prior odds.
- Forgetting the noise term in the Gaussian predictive: its variance is $\sigma^2 + \tau_n^2$, not just the posterior variance of the mean.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain, using the coin example (a fair-coin model against a model with $\theta \sim \operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$), why the marginal likelihood penalizes a flexible model for data that are not very surprising, and why the penalty vanishes when the data strongly contradict the simple model.
hint: Think of each model as a probability distribution over possible data sets that must sum to 1.
solution: Each model distributes one unit of probability over all possible data sets. The fair coin concentrates its probability on sequences with about half heads. The flexible model, with $\theta$ uniform, spreads the same unit over all head counts roughly evenly (every head count from 0 to $n$ has probability $1/(n + 1)$). For a data set with a typical head count, the fair coin assigned it more probability than the flexible model did, so the evidence favors the simple model even though the flexible model contains a parameter that fits at least as well. For a data set with an extreme head count the fair model assigned it exponentially small probability ($2^{-n}$ for all heads) while the flexible model assigned it $1/(n+1)$ in total, so the evidence favors the flexible model. The penalty is an automatic Occam factor rather than an added term.
