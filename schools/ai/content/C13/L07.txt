@article
How would you compute the area of a quarter-circle without calculus? Throw darts uniformly at the unit square and count the fraction that land inside the circle $x^2 + y^2 < 1$. That fraction estimates $\pi/4$. This is **Monte Carlo**: replace an integral or expectation by an average over random samples.

### The Monte Carlo estimator

To estimate $\mu = \mathbb{E}_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)p(x)\,dx$, draw independent samples $x_1, \dots, x_N \sim p$ and average:

$$\hat\mu_N = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f(x_i).$$

It is **unbiased**: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\mu_N] = \mu$. Its variance is $\sigma^2/N$ with $\sigma^2 = \operatorname{Var}_p(f(X))$, because the samples are independent. The **standard error** is therefore $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$, estimated by replacing $\sigma$ with the sample standard deviation of the $f(x_i)$. When $\mathbb{E}|f| < \infty$ the law of large numbers gives $\hat\mu_N \to \mu$ almost surely, and when $\sigma^2 < \infty$ the central limit theorem gives $\hat\mu_N \approx \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2/N)$ for large $N$. The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{N}$ whatever the dimension of $x$: to cut the error by 10 you need 100 times as many samples.

For the dart game, $f$ is the indicator of landing inside the circle, with $p = \pi/4 \approx 0.7854 $ so $\sigma^2 = p(1-p) \approx 0.1686$. The estimate $\hat\pi = 4\hat\mu_N$ has standard error $4\sqrt{0.1686/N}$, about $0.0164$ for $N = 10{,}000$.

### Importance sampling

Often we cannot sample from $p$, or sampling from $p$ rarely visits the region that matters (a rare event). Suppose we can sample from another density $q$ and evaluate both $p$ and $q$. If $q(x) > 0$ wherever $p(x)f(x) \ne 0$, then

$$\mathbb{E}_p[f(X)] = \int f(x)\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,q(x)\,dx = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[f(X)\,w(X)\Big], \qquad w(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}.$$

The **importance sampling estimator** draws $x_i \sim q$ and computes

$$\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N w(x_i)f(x_i).$$

It is unbiased, with variance $\frac1N\operatorname{Var}_q(wf)$. The weights $w$ correct for sampling from the wrong distribution: points that $q$ over-samples get weights below 1. The variance can be far lower than plain Monte Carlo if $q$ puts its mass where $|f|p$ is large (the variance-minimizing choice for nonnegative $f$ is $q \propto fp$), and infinite if $q$ has lighter tails than $|f|p$ requires.

If $p$ is known only up to a constant, $p(x) = \tilde p(x)/Z$, use the **self-normalized** estimator with $w_i = \tilde p(x_i)/q(x_i)$:

$$\hat\mu_{\text{SN}} = \frac{\sum_i w_i f(x_i)}{\sum_i w_i}.$$

It is slightly biased for finite $N$ but consistent, and does not need $Z$ (the constants cancel). A common diagnostic is the **effective sample size** $\text{ESS} = (\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$: if a few weights dominate, ESS is far below $N$.

### A rare event

Let $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ and estimate $P(X > 3) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{1}\{X > 3\}] \approx 0.001350$. Plain Monte Carlo has variance $p(1-p) \approx 0.001348$ per sample, so with $N = 10{,}000$ the standard error is $3.7 \times 10^{-4}$, about 27% of the answer; most samples never reach the tail. Take $q = \mathcal{N}(3, 1)$, centered on the event. The weight is
$$w(x) = \frac{e^{-x^2/2}}{e^{-(x-3)^2/2}} = e^{-3x + 4.5}.$$
A direct calculation gives $\mathbb{E}_q[(wf)^2] = \int_3^\infty p(x)^2/q(x)\,dx = e^{9}\,\bigl(1 - \Phi(6)\bigr) \approx 8.0 \times 10^{-6}$, so the variance per sample is about $6.2 \times 10^{-6}$: roughly 218 times smaller, and the standard error at $N = 10{,}000$ is about $2.5 \times 10^{-5}$, under 2% of the answer.

### In code

```python
import random
from math import exp

def tail_prob(n, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    total = 0.0
    for _ in range(n):
        x = rng.gauss(3.0, 1.0)
        if x > 3.0:
            total += exp(-3.0 * x + 4.5)
    return total / n

print(tail_prob(10000, 0))
```

The printed value is close to $0.00135$ (the exact value depends on the seed).

@example
title: Importance sampling by hand
problem: We want $\mathbb{E}_p[X]$ for the density $p(x) = 2x$ on $(0, 1)$, but we only have samples from the uniform distribution $q(x) = 1$ on $(0, 1)$. The samples are $0.2, 0.5, 0.9$. Compute the importance sampling estimate and the self-normalized estimate, and compare them with the true value.
step: Weights: $w(x) = p(x)/q(x) = 2x$, so $w = 0.4, 1.0, 1.8$.
step: Products $w f = 2x^2$ with $f(x) = x$: $0.08,\ 0.50,\ 1.62$.
step: Importance sampling: $\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} = (0.08 + 0.50 + 1.62)/3 = 2.2/3 \approx 0.733$.
step: Self-normalized: $\sum wf/\sum w = 2.2/(0.4 + 1.0 + 1.8) = 2.2/3.2 = 0.6875$.
step: The true value is $\int_0^1 x \cdot 2x\,dx = 2/3 \approx 0.667$; both estimates are close, as expected from only three samples.
answer: $\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} \approx 0.733$ and $\hat\mu_{\text{SN}} = 0.6875$; true value $2/3$.

@example
title: Weights for a rare-event estimate
problem: To estimate $P(X > 3)$ for $X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$, samples $3.5, 2.8, 3.2, 4.0$ are drawn from $q = \mathcal{N}(3, 1)$, with weights $w(x) = e^{-3x + 4.5}$. Compute the importance sampling estimate.
step: The function is $f(x) = \mathbf{1}\{x > 3\}$, so the sample $2.8$ contributes 0 and the others contribute their weights.
step: $w(3.5) = e^{-6} \approx 0.002479$, $w(3.2) = e^{-5.1} \approx 0.006097$, $w(4.0) = e^{-7.5} \approx 0.000553$.
step: The sum is $\approx 0.009129$, and dividing by $N = 4$ gives $\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} \approx 0.00228$.
step: The exact value is $0.00135$. With four samples the estimate is noisy, but unlike plain sampling it uses every sample that reaches the tail; plain Monte Carlo from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ would need on the order of a thousand samples to see one tail point.
answer: $\hat\mu_{\text{IS}} \approx 0.00228$ (exact value $0.00135$).

@mistakes
- Dividing by $N$ twice or forgetting the weights: the importance sampling estimate averages $w(x_i)f(x_i)$, not $f(x_i)$.
- Using the weights $q/p$ instead of $p/q$: the weight is the target density over the sampling density.
- Choosing a proposal $q$ that is zero (or has much lighter tails) where $|f|p$ is not: the estimator is then biased or has infinite variance.
- Reporting a Monte Carlo estimate without a standard error: without it you cannot tell whether the third decimal place means anything.

model:
```python
def is_estimate(f, p, q, xs, normalize=False):
    """Importance sampling estimate of E_p[f] from samples xs drawn from q."""
    weights = [p(x) / q(x) for x in xs]
    total = sum(w * f(x) for w, x in zip(weights, xs))
    if normalize:
        return total / sum(weights)
    return total / len(xs)
```
hint: Compute the list of weights $p(x)/q(x)$, then divide the weighted sum either by the number of samples or by the sum of the weights.
solution: Both estimators share the weighted sum $\sum w_i f(x_i)$; they differ only in the divisor, $N$ for the plain estimator and $\sum w_i$ for the self-normalized one. The third test uses $q = p$, where all weights are 1 and the estimate must be the plain sample mean of $f$; the last two tests use constant weights of 2, which the self-normalized version cancels.

model:
```python
import random

def mc_pi(n, seed):
    """Estimate pi from the fraction of random points inside the quarter circle."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    count = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        x, y = rng.random(), rng.random()
        if x * x + y * y < 1:
            count += 1
    return 4 * count / n
```
hint: The fraction of points inside the quarter circle estimates $\pi/4$; do not forget the factor 4.
solution: Each point lands inside with probability $\pi/4$, so the count divided by $n$ estimates $\pi/4$ and four times that estimates $\pi$. The standard error at $n = 20{,}000$ is about $0.0116$, so the tolerance of $0.1$ is far outside normal fluctuation, and a solution that forgets the factor of 4 fails.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about Monte Carlo and importance sampling are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: The standard error of a plain Monte Carlo estimate falls like $1/\sqrt{N}$.
choice*: Importance sampling can have lower variance than sampling directly from $p$ when $q$ concentrates on the region where $|f|p$ is large.
choice*: The self-normalized estimator can be used when $p$ is known only up to a normalizing constant.
choice: Importance sampling with weights $q/p$ gives an unbiased estimate of $\mathbb{E}_p[f]$.
choice: The effective sample size $(\sum w_i)^2/\sum w_i^2$ is always equal to $N$.
hint: Recall the variance $\sigma^2/N$, the formula for the weights, and what ESS measures.
solution: The standard error is $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. A well-chosen $q$ lowers the variance (the rare-event example does so by a factor of about 200). The constant in $p$ cancels in the self-normalized ratio. The correct weight is $p/q$, and ESS equals $N$ only when all weights are equal; unequal weights reduce it.

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why importance sampling with a proposal $q$ that has lighter tails than the target $p$ can have infinite variance even though the estimator is unbiased, and what a practitioner should do about it.
hint: Look at the second moment $\int f^2 p^2/q\,dx$ and think about the ratio $p/q$ far out in the tail.
solution: The variance is $\frac1N\big(\int f(x)^2\,p(x)^2/q(x)\,dx - \mu^2\big)$. If $q$ decays faster than $p$ (for example a narrow Gaussian proposal for a heavy-tailed target), the ratio $p(x)/q(x)$ grows without bound in the tail, and the integral of $f^2p^2/q$ can diverge. The estimator is still unbiased because its mean is a finite integral, but it is dominated by rare samples with enormous weights, so the standard error is meaningless and the estimates fluctuate wildly. The remedy is a proposal with tails at least as heavy as the target (a Student-$t$ or a widened Gaussian), checking the effective sample size and the largest weight, and, if needed, using the self-normalized estimator with clipped or smoothed weights while accepting some bias.
rubric: Identifies the second moment $\int f^2p^2/q$ as the variance and the growth of $p/q$ in the tails as the cause.
rubric: Distinguishes unbiasedness from finite variance.
rubric: Recommends a heavier-tailed proposal and diagnostics such as effective sample size.
