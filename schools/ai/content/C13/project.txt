@review
**Weeks 1 and 2: joint distributions and the Gaussian.** Marginals by summing or integrating out; conditionals $P(x \mid y) = P(x, y)/P(y)$; the product and chain rules; Bayes' rule; independence means $P(x, y) = P(x)P(y)$ in every cell. $\operatorname{Cov}(X, Y) = \mathbb{E}[XY] - \mathbb{E}X\,\mathbb{E}Y$; $\operatorname{Var}(aX + bY) = a^2\operatorname{Var}X + b^2\operatorname{Var}Y + 2ab\operatorname{Cov}$. Gaussian conditional: mean $\mu_1 + \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}(x_2 - \mu_2)$, covariance $\Sigma_{11} - \Sigma_{12}\Sigma_{22}^{-1}\Sigma_{21}$.

**Weeks 3 and 4: estimation.** Maximum likelihood maximizes $\sum_i\log p(x_i \mid \theta)$: Bernoulli $k/n$, Gaussian mean $\bar x$ and variance $\frac1n\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2$ (biased), exponential $n/\sum x_i$. Gaussian noise gives least squares; Bernoulli gives cross-entropy. MAP adds $\log p(\theta)$: Beta mode $(k + \alpha - 1)/(n + \alpha + \beta - 2)$; a Gaussian prior gives ridge with $\lambda = \sigma^2/\tau^2$ and a Laplace prior gives L1.

**Weeks 5 and 6: Bayesian inference.** Beta-Bernoulli posterior $\operatorname{Beta}(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)$; Gamma-Poisson posterior $\operatorname{Gamma}(a + \sum x, b + n)$; Gaussian posterior precisions add. The posterior predictive probability of success is $(\alpha + k)/(\alpha + \beta + n)$; the Gaussian predictive variance is $\sigma^2 + \tau_n^2$. Evidence $p(\mathcal{D} \mid M) = B(\alpha + k, \beta + n - k)/B(\alpha, \beta)$; posterior odds equal the Bayes factor times the prior odds.

**Weeks 7 and 8: Monte Carlo.** Standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{N}$. Importance sampling uses weights $p/q$ and needs $q > 0$ wherever $pf \ne 0$; the self-normalized version needs $p$ only up to a constant. The Metropolis algorithm accepts with probability $\min(1, \tilde p(x')/\tilde p(x))$ for a symmetric proposal; detailed balance implies stationarity; chains are correlated, so check burn-in and effective sample size.

**Weeks 9 and 10: information theory.** $H(p) = -\sum p\log p \le \log K$; $H(p, q) = H(p) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q)$; $D_{\mathrm{KL}} \ge 0$ and is asymmetric; minimizing cross-entropy is maximum likelihood; perplexity $= e^{H}$ in nats; $I(X; Y) = D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p(x, y)\,\Vert\,p(x)p(y)) = H(X) - H(X \mid Y)$; Gaussian $I = -\frac12\ln(1 - \rho^2)$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: statistics of evaluation.** Accuracy standard error $\sqrt{\hat a(1 - \hat a)/n}$; percentile bootstrap resamples with replacement and uses ranks $0.025B$ and $0.975B$; paired bootstrap resamples indices jointly. Paired $t = \bar d/(s_d/\sqrt n)$; McNemar $(b - c)^2/(b + c)$; sign-flip permutation test; Bonferroni $\alpha/m$, Holm step-down, Benjamini–Hochberg for FDR.

**Self-check.** Can you (1) turn a joint table into a conditional by dividing by the right marginal, (2) derive an MLE and a MAP estimate, (3) update a Beta prior and give the predictive probability, (4) write the importance weight and say when it fails, (5) check detailed balance, (6) compute a KL divergence in the right direction and unit, (7) say what a bootstrap interval does not capture, (8) correct p-values for several comparisons?

**Common errors.** Confusing $P(x \mid y)$ with $P(y \mid x)$; dividing the ML variance by $n - 1$; using the posterior mean where the mode is asked (and the reverse); weights $q/p$ instead of $p/q$; dropping rejected Metropolis proposals; mixing bits and nats; treating a credible interval as a confidence interval; running many comparisons without correction.

answer: 0.25
tol: 0.001
check: sum(a * log2(a / b) for a, b in zip([0.25, 0.25, 0.5], [0.5, 0.25, 0.25]))
hint: Compute the cross-entropy $H(p, q)$ and the entropy $H(p)$ in bits and subtract, or sum $p_k\log_2(p_k/q_k)$ directly.
solution: $H(p, q) = 0.25(1) + 0.25(2) + 0.5(2) = 1.75$ bits and $H(p) = 0.25(2) + 0.25(2) + 0.5(1) = 1.5$ bits, so $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\Vert\, q) = 1.75 - 1.5 = 0.25$ bits.

Create `rng = random.Random(seed)`, draw `n` pairs with `rng.betavariate(a, b)` (draw $\theta_1$ first, then $\theta_2$, in each pair), and return the fraction of pairs with $\theta_2 > \theta_1$. model:
```python
import random

def prob_better(a1, b1, a2, b2, n, seed):
    """Monte Carlo estimate of P(theta2 > theta1) for independent Beta posteriors."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    wins = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        theta1 = rng.betavariate(a1, b1)
        theta2 = rng.betavariate(a2, b2)
        if theta2 > theta1:
            wins += 1
    return wins / n
```
hint: Loop `n` times, draw one value from each Beta distribution, and count how often the second is larger.
solution: The posterior probability that parameter 2 is larger is the expectation of the indicator $\mathbf{1}\{\theta_2 > \theta_1\}$ under the independent posteriors, which the fraction over many pairs estimates with standard error $\sqrt{p(1 - p)/n}$. The first two tests use posteriors so far apart that the answer is 1 or 0, and the identical-posterior test must be near one half; a solution that compares in the wrong direction fails the last two.

@exam self
prompt: A product team runs an experiment with 80 users on each of two model variants, A and B, with 44 clicks for A and 52 for B. Using ideas from the whole course, write a short analysis plan and interpretation: (a) give the maximum-likelihood estimates and a Beta-Bernoulli posterior with a $\operatorname{Beta}(1, 1)$ prior for each variant, and say what posterior quantity answers "how likely is B better"; (b) say how you would estimate that quantity and its Monte Carlo error; (c) say how a bootstrap interval for the difference would be computed here and what it captures; and (d) say what you would tell the team about the strength of the evidence and what further information would change your advice.
hint: The estimates are $44/80$ and $52/80$; the posteriors are $\operatorname{Beta}(45, 37)$ and $\operatorname{Beta}(53, 29)$. The two groups contain different users.
solution: (a) The maximum-likelihood estimates are $\hat\theta_A = 44/80 = 0.55$ and $\hat\theta_B = 52/80 = 0.65$. With the uniform prior the posteriors are $\theta_A \sim \operatorname{Beta}(45, 37)$ (mean $0.549$) and $\theta_B \sim \operatorname{Beta}(53, 29)$ (mean $0.646$). The quantity that answers the question is $P(\theta_B > \theta_A \mid \text{data}) = \iint \mathbf{1}\{\theta_B > \theta_A\}\,p(\theta_A \mid \text{data})\,p(\theta_B \mid \text{data})\,d\theta_A\,d\theta_B$. (b) Draw $N$ independent pairs, for example $N = 100{,}000$, from the two Beta posteriors and take the fraction with $\theta_B > \theta_A$; the Monte Carlo standard error is $\sqrt{\hat p(1 - \hat p)/N}$, at most $0.0016$ for this $N$. (c) Because the groups are different users, resample each group's 0/1 outcomes separately with replacement, compute the difference of the two click rates in each of at least 10,000 replicates, and report the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles. The interval reflects the sampling variability of the users in each group for these two fixed variants; it does not reflect uncertainty from users outside the experiment's population or changes over time. (d) The point estimates favor B by 10 points, and the posterior probability that B is better is about $0.90$, high but not overwhelming (about 10% of the posterior mass remains on $\theta_A \ge \theta_B$, and the interval for the difference is wide, with 80 users per group), so I would say B looks better with moderate-to-good confidence, that the size of the improvement is uncertain, and that the prior had a small effect with this much data (it can be checked with an informative prior). Further information that would change the advice includes more users (which narrows the intervals), the cost of shipping a worse variant, evidence that users are not independent (repeat visitors, clustering), a pre-registered comparison versus many looks at the data, and whether the experiment population matches the intended audience.
