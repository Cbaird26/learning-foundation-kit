@article
You flip a coin 10 times and see 7 heads. What is the probability $\theta$ of heads? The most natural answer is $7/10$, and **maximum likelihood** explains why that answer is principled: it is the value of $\theta$ that makes the observed data most probable.

### The likelihood

Suppose the data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are independent draws from a model $p(x \mid \theta)$ with unknown parameter $\theta$. The **likelihood** is the probability (or density) of the observed data, viewed as a function of $\theta$:

$$L(\theta) = p(x_1, \dots, x_n \mid \theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} p(x_i \mid \theta).$$

It is not a probability distribution over $\theta$; it need not integrate to 1. The **maximum-likelihood estimate** (MLE) is $\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta L(\theta)$. Because the logarithm is increasing, the same $\theta$ maximizes the **log-likelihood** $\ell(\theta) = \sum_i \log p(x_i \mid \theta)$, which turns a product into a sum (easier to differentiate, and numerically stable, since products of many small probabilities underflow). Minimizing the **negative log-likelihood** is the same problem and is the form used as a loss.

### Bernoulli

For $k$ heads in $n$ flips, $L(\theta) = \theta^k (1 - \theta)^{n - k}$, so $\ell(\theta) = k \log\theta + (n - k)\log(1 - \theta)$. Setting the derivative to zero,

$$\ell'(\theta) = \frac{k}{\theta} - \frac{n - k}{1 - \theta} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \hat{\theta} = \frac{k}{n}.$$

The second derivative $-k/\theta^2 - (n-k)/(1-\theta)^2$ is negative, so this is a maximum. For 7 heads in 10 flips, $\hat\theta = 0.7$ and the maximized log-likelihood is $7\ln 0.7 + 3 \ln 0.3 \approx -6.109$. (If $k = 0$ or $k = n$ the maximum sits at the boundary, $\hat\theta = 0$ or $1$, which is a warning sign about overconfidence that the next lessons address.)

### Gaussian

For $x_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$,

$$\ell(\mu, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \mu)^2.$$

Setting $\partial\ell/\partial\mu = \frac{1}{\sigma^2}\sum_i (x_i - \mu) = 0$ gives $\hat\mu = \bar{x}$, the sample mean. Substituting and differentiating in $\sigma^2$: $\partial\ell/\partial\sigma^2 = -\frac{n}{2\sigma^2} + \frac{1}{2\sigma^4}\sum_i (x_i - \hat\mu)^2 = 0$ gives

$$\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \bar{x})^2.$$

This divides by $n$, not $n - 1$. It is **biased**: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\sigma^2_{\text{ML}}] = \frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$, because the data are closer to their own mean $\bar x$ than to the true mean. The bias vanishes as $n \to \infty$; the unbiased estimator multiplies by $n/(n-1)$.

### Why it connects to loss functions

If $y_i = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x}_i + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ and $\sigma^2$ fixed, the negative log-likelihood is $\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x}_i)^2$ plus a constant, so maximum likelihood is **least squares**. If $y_i \in \{0, 1\}$ is Bernoulli with $P(y_i = 1) = \hat{y}_i$ (for example, the output of a logistic model), the negative log-likelihood is $-\sum_i [y_i \log \hat y_i + (1 - y_i)\log(1 - \hat y_i)]$, the **binary cross-entropy** loss. Training by minimizing these standard losses is maximum likelihood in disguise.

### Properties and conditions

Under standard regularity conditions (identifiable model, independent and identically distributed data from a model in the family, and a true parameter in the interior of the parameter space), the MLE is **consistent** ($\hat\theta \to \theta_0$ as $n \to \infty$), asymptotically normal with variance $1/(n\,I(\theta_0))$ where $I$ is the Fisher information, and **equivariant**: the MLE of $g(\theta)$ is $g(\hat\theta)$. For the Bernoulli, $I(\theta) = 1/(\theta(1-\theta))$, so the standard error of $\hat\theta$ is about $\sqrt{\hat\theta(1 - \hat\theta)/n}$. Maximum likelihood is not always unbiased and, with little data, can overfit.

### In code

```python
import math

def bernoulli_loglik(theta, k, n):
    return k * math.log(theta) + (n - k) * math.log(1 - theta)

grid = [i / 100 for i in range(1, 100)]
best = max(grid, key=lambda t: bernoulli_loglik(t, 7, 10))
print(best)
```

The grid search prints `0.7`, agreeing with $k/n$; for models without a closed form, optimizers such as gradient descent maximize $\ell$ numerically.

@example
title: Gaussian maximum likelihood by hand
problem: The data $2, 4, 9$ are modeled as independent draws from $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. Find the maximum-likelihood estimates of $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$, and compare $\hat\sigma^2$ with the unbiased sample variance.
step: The estimate of the mean is the sample mean: $\hat\mu = (2 + 4 + 9)/3 = 5$.
step: Deviations from the mean: $-3, -1, 4$, with squares $9, 1, 16$ and sum $26$.
step: Maximum-likelihood variance divides by $n = 3$: $\hat\sigma^2 = 26/3 \approx 8.667$.
step: The unbiased sample variance divides by $n - 1 = 2$: $26/2 = 13$. The ratio is $(n-1)/n = 2/3$, the bias factor of the MLE.
answer: $\hat\mu = 5$, $\hat\sigma^2 = 26/3 \approx 8.667$ (unbiased version: 13).

@example
title: Maximum likelihood for an exponential model
problem: Waiting times $0.5, 1.5, 1.0, 3.0$ (in seconds) are modeled as independent with density $p(x \mid \lambda) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ for $x \ge 0$. Find $\hat\lambda$ and the maximized log-likelihood.
step: Log-likelihood: $\ell(\lambda) = \sum_i (\log\lambda - \lambda x_i) = n\log\lambda - \lambda\sum_i x_i$.
step: Differentiate and set to zero: $n/\lambda - \sum_i x_i = 0$, so $\hat\lambda = n/\sum_i x_i$. The second derivative $-n/\lambda^2$ is negative, so this is a maximum.
step: Here $n = 4$ and $\sum_i x_i = 6$, so $\hat\lambda = 4/6 = 2/3$ per second (the reciprocal of the mean waiting time $1.5$).
step: The maximized log-likelihood is $4\ln(2/3) - (2/3)(6) = -1.622 - 4 = -5.622$.
answer: $\hat\lambda = 2/3$ and $\ell(\hat\lambda) \approx -5.622$.

@mistakes
- Dividing the maximum-likelihood variance by $n - 1$: the MLE divides by $n$; $n - 1$ gives the unbiased estimator, which is a different estimator.
- Reading the likelihood as the probability of the parameter: $L(\theta)$ is the probability of the data given $\theta$ and does not sum or integrate to 1 over $\theta$ (that needs a prior, as in Bayesian inference).
- Setting the derivative to zero and stopping: check that the point is a maximum (second derivative or boundary values), and remember that the maximum can sit on the boundary, as with $k = n$ heads.
- Forgetting the independence assumption: the product form of the likelihood holds only when the examples are independent given $\theta$.

@quiz self
prompt: Counts $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are modeled as independent Poisson($\lambda$), with $P(x \mid \lambda) = e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x/x!$. Derive the maximum-likelihood estimate of $\lambda$ and verify that it is a maximum.
hint: Write the log-likelihood, drop terms that do not depend on $\lambda$, differentiate, and look at the sign of the second derivative.
solution: The log-likelihood is $\ell(\lambda) = \sum_i (-\lambda + x_i\log\lambda - \log x_i!) = -n\lambda + (\sum_i x_i)\log\lambda - \sum_i\log x_i!$. Its derivative is $-n + \sum_i x_i/\lambda$, which is zero at $\hat\lambda = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i$, the sample mean. The second derivative is $-\sum_i x_i/\lambda^2 \le 0$, so $\ell$ is concave and the stationary point is the maximum (when $\sum x_i = 0$ the maximum is at the boundary $\lambda = 0$, which is the sample mean as well).
