title: Yeast respiration experiment
overview: Baker's yeast is a single-celled fungus that breaks down sugar and releases carbon dioxide gas. You will measure gas production with balloons at three sugar amounts (or three temperatures), graph the results and explain them using what you learned about glycolysis, fermentation and enzymes. The goal is to practice experimental design, data handling and scientific explanation.
goal: Design a controlled experiment with one independent variable, one dependent variable and clearly kept-constant conditions.
goal: Collect, tabulate and graph data, including repeated trials and an average.
goal: Explain the results using respiration, fermentation and enzyme ideas, and evaluate sources of error.
step: Choose your independent variable: three sugar amounts (for example 0, 1 and 4 teaspoons) at the same warm temperature, OR three water temperatures (for example cool tap water, lukewarm water, and warm tap water that is comfortable to touch) with the same sugar amount. Write a testable hypothesis in "If ... then ... because ..." form.
step: Gather: three identical clean plastic bottles (about 500 mL), three balloons of the same size, two packets of dry yeast (about 1 teaspoon per bottle), table sugar, warm water, a measuring spoon, a soft tape measure or string and ruler, a marker and a timer. Label the bottles.
step: In each bottle mix 1 teaspoon of yeast and 150 mL of water at the chosen temperature, then add the sugar for that bottle. Swirl, stretch a balloon over each mouth and start the timer. Keep everything else the same.
step: Every 10 minutes for 60 minutes, wrap string around the widest part of each balloon, mark it and measure the circumference with the ruler. Record in a table. If possible, repeat the whole set so you have two or three trials.
step: Convert circumference to volume with $V = \dfrac{C^3}{6\pi^2}$ (sphere, $r = C/2\pi$, $V = \tfrac43\pi r^3$). Graph volume (y) against time (x) for each bottle on the same axes and compute the average rate in mL per minute.
step: Write a short report: your hypothesis, method, table, graph, and a paragraph explaining how the results fit yeast metabolism. If you cannot run the experiment, analyze the sample data below instead and write the same report.
step: Evaluate error: list at least three sources (balloon stretch, leaks, uneven temperature, dissolved $CO_2$) and one improvement.
material: Sample data (if you cannot run the experiment): balloon volume in mL after 60 minutes with 150 mL of 35 degree Celsius water and 1 teaspoon of yeast. 0 teaspoons sugar: 5 mL. 1 teaspoon: 60 mL. 4 teaspoons: 140 mL. A fourth bottle with 4 teaspoons and 70 degree Celsius water made 8 mL, because the high heat denatured the yeast enzymes.
deliverable: A labeled data table and a graph with axis titles and units.
deliverable: A one-page report (hypothesis, method, results, explanation, error analysis).
deliverable: A short explanation (written or spoken) of the carbon and energy pathway from sugar to $CO_2$ and ATP in yeast.
rubric: Experimental design controls all variables except the independent variable, and says what is measured and how.
rubric: Data are recorded with units, include repeated measurements or an average, and the graph is correct and readable.
rubric: Explanation correctly links gas production to fermentation of glucose (glycolysis, then ethanol and $CO_2$) and, where appropriate, enzyme activity versus temperature or substrate concentration.
rubric: Error analysis identifies realistic sources of error and a reasonable improvement.
rubric: Conclusion states whether the hypothesis is supported and is based on the data.
safety: Use only household materials with an adult nearby. Do not seal a bottle or balloon tightly with tape beyond the stretched balloon; do not use hot water above comfortable warm-tap temperature (never boiling water); do not drink the yeast mixture; wipe up spills and wash hands.
tutor: How can I tell which variable to keep constant, and why does that matter for my conclusion?
tutor: Why does the yeast stop producing gas faster in very hot water than in warm water?
tutor: Can you check whether my explanation of fermentation and gas production is accurate and complete?

## Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Chemistry of life:** water is polar and forms hydrogen bonds; pH is $-\log_{10}[H^+]$; four macromolecule classes (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids) with their monomers and bonds.
2. **Cell structure:** prokaryotes lack a nucleus and membrane-bound organelles; organelle functions; surface-area-to-volume ratio $6/s$ for a cube; magnification = image/actual.
3. **Membranes and transport:** fluid mosaic model; passive versus active transport; osmosis moves water toward higher solute concentration; osmolarity $= iC$.
4. **Enzymes and energy:** $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$; enzymes lower $E_A$, not $\Delta G$; $v = V_{max}[S]/(K_m+[S])$; competitive versus noncompetitive inhibition.
5. **Cellular respiration:** glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, citric acid cycle, electron transport; about 30 to 32 ATP per glucose; fermentation gives 2.
6. **Photosynthesis:** light reactions make ATP, NADPH and $O_2$ (from water); Calvin cycle fixes carbon, using 3 ATP and 2 NADPH per $CO_2$.
7. **Cell cycle and mitosis:** G1, S, G2, M; PMAT; checkpoints; tumor suppressors and oncogenes.
8. **Meiosis:** two divisions, four haploid cells; crossing over; independent assortment ($2^n$); nondisjunction.
9. **Mendelian genetics:** segregation, independent assortment, Punnett squares, product rule, X-linkage.
10. **DNA, RNA and protein synthesis:** replication, transcription (U for T), translation, mutations.
11. **Evolution:** natural selection, drift, Hardy-Weinberg $p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1$.
12. **Ecology:** mark-recapture, exponential and logistic growth, 10% energy transfer, nutrient cycles, antibiotic resistance as selection.

## Common errors

- Using $q^2$ as an allele frequency; confusing homologs with sister chromatids.
- Forgetting that enzymes lower activation energy but do not change $\Delta G$.
- Saying oxygen released in photosynthesis comes from $CO_2$.
- Counting NaCl as one particle in osmolarity.
- Writing T in mRNA, or counting the stop codon as an amino acid.

prompt: Yeast ferments 9.0 g of glucose (molar mass 180 g/mol) anaerobically by $C_6H_{12}O_6 \rightarrow 2C_2H_5OH + 2CO_2$. At room temperature one mole of gas occupies about 24.5 L. What volume of $CO_2$ in liters is produced, assuming all the glucose reacts?
answer: 2.45
check: 9.0 / 180 * 2 * 24.5
unit: L
tol: 2%
hint: Convert grams of glucose to moles.
hint: The equation gives 2 mol $CO_2$ per mole of glucose.
solution: $9.0/180 = 0.050$ mol glucose gives $0.10$ mol $CO_2$, and $0.10 \times 24.5 = 2.45$ L.

@exam num
prompt: A recessive disorder affects 1 in 400 people in a population in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium. Two unrelated people from this population, neither of whom has the disorder, are chosen at random. Assuming each unaffected person has the carrier probability $2pq/(p^2 + 2pq)$, what is the probability that such a couple's child is affected? Round to three significant figures.
answer: 0.00227
check: (2 * 0.05 * 0.95 / (1 - 0.05**2))**2 / 4
tol: 2%
hint: Find $q$ from $q^2 = 1/400$.
hint: Among unaffected people, the probability of being a carrier is $2pq/(1 - q^2)$.
hint: Multiply the two carrier probabilities by $1/4$.
solution: $q = 0.05$, $p = 0.95$, $2pq = 0.095$ and $1 - q^2 = 0.9975$, so each unaffected person is a carrier with probability $0.0952$. The child is affected with probability $0.0952^2 \times \tfrac14 = 0.00227$.

@exam self
prompt: A hospital finds that a bacterial infection is increasingly unresponsive to an antibiotic. Using what you know about mutation, natural selection, DNA and protein synthesis, and ecology, explain (a) how a resistance mutation could change a protein, (b) how the population comes to be mostly resistant, and (c) one way to slow the spread.
solution: (a) A mutation in the gene (for example a point mutation changing a codon) changes the amino acid sequence, and so the shape of the protein that the antibiotic targets or that pumps or breaks down the drug; or a plasmid supplies an enzyme that degrades the drug. The altered protein still works for the cell but the antibiotic can no longer act on it. (b) Resistant cells were present at low frequency by random mutation or gene transfer. The antibiotic kills susceptible cells and leaves resistant cells with less competition, so they reproduce and the resistance allele frequency rises in the population, which is natural selection; horizontal gene transfer can spread the gene to other bacteria. (c) Use antibiotics only when needed, finish prescribed courses, use targeted narrow-spectrum drugs, improve hygiene and infection control, and reduce agricultural overuse.
hint: Think of the central dogma from gene to protein, then of selection acting on variation.
rubric: Links a mutation in DNA to an altered protein structure or function.
rubric: Explains the population change using variation, selection and rising allele frequency, noting that the mutation arose before exposure.
rubric: Suggests a reasonable measure to reduce selection pressure or spread.
