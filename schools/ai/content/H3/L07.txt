@article
A triangle has six measurements: three sides and three angles. **Solving** a triangle means finding all of them from three well-chosen ones. We label angle $A$ opposite side $a$, angle $B$ opposite side $b$ and angle $C$ opposite side $c$. The angles always satisfy $A + B + C = 180^\circ$ (which is $\pi$ radians).

### Right triangles

For an acute angle $\theta$ in a right triangle, scale the unit-circle picture (hypotenuse $1$) up to any hypotenuse:

$$\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos\theta = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{adjacent}}.$$

These ratios, plus the Pythagorean theorem, solve every right triangle and every "angle of elevation" problem.

### Oblique triangles: two laws

For a triangle without a right angle, dropping an altitude splits it into two right triangles, and that yields two laws. The **law of sines** says each side is proportional to the sine of the opposite angle:

$$\frac{a}{\sin A} = \frac{b}{\sin B} = \frac{c}{\sin C}.$$

The **law of cosines** generalises the Pythagorean theorem:

$$c^2 = a^2 + b^2 - 2ab\cos C, \qquad\text{equivalently}\qquad \cos C = \frac{a^2 + b^2 - c^2}{2ab}.$$

When $C = 90^\circ$ the last term vanishes and you recover $c^2 = a^2 + b^2$.

### Which law to use

| Given | Plan |
|---|---|
| ASA or AAS (two angles and a side) | Find the third angle from $A + B + C = 180^\circ$, then use the law of sines |
| SAS (two sides and the included angle) | Law of cosines for the third side |
| SSS (three sides) | Law of cosines for an angle |
| SSA (two sides and a non-included angle) | Law of sines, but beware the ambiguous case |

When you must find an angle, prefer the law of cosines: $\arccos$ returns a unique angle in $[0^\circ, 180^\circ]$, while $\arcsin$ returns only acute angles and silently misses an obtuse solution.

### The ambiguous case (SSA)

Given an acute angle $A$, the side $b$ next to it and the side $a$ opposite it, let $h = b\sin A$ be the height of the triangle over the base. Side $a$ must swing down and reach the base line:

- $a < h$: no triangle, since $a$ is too short.
- $a = h$: exactly one triangle, a right triangle.
- $h < a < b$: **two** triangles (angle $B$ acute or obtuse).
- $a \ge b$: exactly one triangle.

### Area

The area is half the base times the height, and the height over side $a$ is $b\sin C$, so

$$\text{Area} = \tfrac{1}{2}ab\sin C.$$

```python
import math
a, b, C = 8.0, 11.0, math.radians(50)
c = math.sqrt(a*a + b*b - 2*a*b*math.cos(C))
print(round(c, 3))                    # about 8.478
print(round(0.5*a*b*math.sin(C), 2))  # about 33.7
```

@example
title: Law of sines with two angles and a side
problem: In triangle $ABC$, $A = 40^\circ$, $B = 65^\circ$ and $a = 10$. Find $C$, $b$ and $c$.
step: The angles sum to $180^\circ$, so $C = 180^\circ - 40^\circ - 65^\circ = 75^\circ$.
step: The law of sines gives $\dfrac{b}{\sin 65^\circ} = \dfrac{10}{\sin 40^\circ}$, so $b = \dfrac{10\sin 65^\circ}{\sin 40^\circ} \approx 14.10$.
step: Similarly $c = \dfrac{10\sin 75^\circ}{\sin 40^\circ} \approx 15.03$.
step: Check: the largest side $c$ is opposite the largest angle $C = 75^\circ$, and the smallest side $a = 10$ is opposite the smallest angle $40^\circ$.

@example
title: Law of cosines and area from two sides and the included angle
problem: Two sides of a triangle measure $7$ and $10$, and the angle between them is $120^\circ$. Find the third side and the area.
step: Let the third side be $c$ and the included angle $C = 120^\circ$. By the law of cosines, $c^2 = 7^2 + 10^2 - 2(7)(10)\cos 120^\circ$.
step: Since $\cos 120^\circ = -\frac{1}{2}$, we get $c^2 = 49 + 100 + 70 = 219$, so $c = \sqrt{219} \approx 14.80$. The obtuse angle makes $c$ longer than the hypotenuse $\sqrt{149}$ of a right triangle with those legs.
step: The area is $\frac{1}{2}(7)(10)\sin 120^\circ = 35\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = \frac{35\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 30.31$.

@mistakes
- Using the law of sines when the data are SAS or SSS. No side and opposite angle are both known, so use the law of cosines.
- Mixing up which angle is opposite which side: side $a$ is always opposite angle $A$.
- Finding an angle with $\arcsin$ and ignoring that the obtuse angle $180^\circ - B$ may also fit.
- Working in the wrong angle mode on the calculator.

@practice num
prompt: Find the area of a triangle with sides $13$, $14$ and $15$.
answer: 84
check: 0.5*13*14*sin(arccos((13**2 + 14**2 - 15**2)/(2*13*14)))
hint: Use the law of cosines to find the angle between the sides $13$ and $14$.
hint: Once you have $\cos C$, find $\sin C$ from $\sin^2 C + \cos^2 C = 1$ (it is positive), then use $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$.
solution: $\cos C = \dfrac{13^2 + 14^2 - 15^2}{2(13)(14)} = \dfrac{140}{364} = \dfrac{5}{13}$, so $\sin C = \dfrac{12}{13}$. Then the area is $\frac{1}{2}(13)(14)\cdot\frac{12}{13} = 84$. (Heron's formula agrees: with $s = 21$, $\sqrt{21\cdot 8\cdot 7\cdot 6} = 84$.)

@quiz num
prompt: Two sides of a triangle measure $9$ and $12$, and the angle between them is $60^\circ$. Find the length of the third side. Give an exact answer.
answer: 3sqrt(13)
check: sqrt(9**2 + 12**2 - 2*9*12*cos(pi/3))
hint: This is SAS, so use the law of cosines with the given angle as the included angle.
hint: $c^2 = 81 + 144 - 2(9)(12)\cos 60^\circ$. Simplify, then factor out a perfect square from the root.
solution: $c^2 = 81 + 144 - 216\cdot\frac{1}{2} = 117$, so $c = \sqrt{117} = \sqrt{9\cdot 13} = 3\sqrt{13} \approx 10.82$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Select every set of given information that determines exactly one triangle (up to congruence).
choice*: Sides $6$, $7$ and $8$
choice*: Two sides $5$ and $9$ with the included angle $40^\circ$
choice: Angles $40^\circ$, $60^\circ$ and $80^\circ$, with no side lengths
choice: Sides $3$, $4$ and $8$
hint: Every side of a triangle must be shorter than the sum of the other two. Test each side set.
hint: Congruent triangles have the same size. Do three angles alone fix the size?
solution: SSS with $6, 7, 8$ works since $6 + 7 > 8$, and SAS always fixes a triangle. Three angles fix only the shape, so there are infinitely many similar triangles. Sides $3, 4, 8$ fail the triangle inequality ($3 + 4 < 8$), so there is no triangle at all.
