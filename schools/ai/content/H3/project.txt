@project
title: Model a periodic signal and resolve two forces
overview: You will fit a sinusoidal model with amplitude, frequency and phase to a periodic signal, express it as a complex phasor, and use it to find when the signal reaches a given value. You will then resolve two forces into components, find their resultant by two independent methods, and use a dot product to check your work. The aim is to see that the unit circle, triangles, vectors and complex numbers describe one geometry.
goal: Build and test a model $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + D$ for a periodic signal, and report its parameters with units.
goal: Connect the model to the complex plane through the phasor $Z = Ae^{i\varphi}$ and explain what changing the amplitude and the phase does to the graph.
goal: Resolve two forces into components, compute their resultant in magnitude and direction in two ways, and justify the result with a dot product.
step: Choose your signal. Either use the simulated data below, or record a safe periodic motion of your own (see the safety note) with at least 10 readings per cycle. The simulated data give the displacement $x$ in cm of an imaginary oscillating marker at times $t = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75, 2.0$ s: $x = 4.47, 5.47, 5.02, 3.39, 1.53, 0.53, 0.98, 2.61, 4.47$. Plot the points.
step: Estimate the parameters. Compute $A = (\max - \min)/2$ and $D = (\max + \min)/2$, read the period $T$ from the data, compute $\omega = 2\pi/T$ and $f = 1/T$, and use the time $t_\text{peak}$ of a peak to get $\varphi = -\omega t_\text{peak}$. State units and conventions (radians, $t$ in seconds).
step: Test the model. Tabulate the model values next to the data, with the residuals. Report the largest residual, and adjust a parameter if the residuals show a pattern. Plot model and data together, by hand or with Python.
step: Write the phasor $Z = Ae^{i\varphi}$ and draw it in the complex plane. Show that $\operatorname{Re}\left(Ze^{i\omega t}\right) + D$ reproduces your model. Then change the amplitude and the phase in turn, and describe in words and with a sketch what each change does and what it leaves unchanged.
step: Use the model. Find all times in one period at which $x(t) = 4$ cm, solving the trigonometric equation exactly or to three decimals, and check each solution by substitution.
step: Resolve the forces. Two ropes pull on a ring: $\mathbf{F}_1$ is $80$ N at $20^\circ$ and $\mathbf{F}_2$ is $60$ N at $110^\circ$, both measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis. Resolve each into components, add them, and find the magnitude and direction of the resultant.
step: Verify. Recompute the magnitude of the resultant with the law of cosines. Compute $\mathbf{F}_1\cdot\mathbf{F}_2$ and explain geometrically what its value says. Find the third force that would hold the ring in equilibrium, with its direction angle in $[0^\circ, 360^\circ)$.
step: Prepare to present. Write a one-page summary and practise explaining one major result aloud, without notes, in about five minutes. Good choices are why the phase of the phasor is the time shift of the real signal, or why the resultant of perpendicular forces has magnitude $\sqrt{F_1^2 + F_2^2}$.
deliverable: A one-to-two page written report containing the data plot, the fitted model with parameters and units, the residual table, the phasor diagram and the solution of $x(t) = 4$.
deliverable: A force diagram and worked solution for the resultant and the equilibrium force, showing the components, both methods for the magnitude, and the dot-product check.
deliverable: A five-minute spoken explanation, given without notes, of one major result from the course.
rubric: Model: the parameters $A$, $D$, $T$, $\omega$ and $\varphi$ are extracted correctly with units, and the model reproduces every data point to within about $0.1$ cm.
rubric: Phasor link: states $Z = Ae^{i\varphi}$, shows that the real part of $Ze^{i\omega t}$ gives the cosine, and correctly describes how amplitude and phase changes affect the graph, including that the period is unchanged.
rubric: Equation solving: finds all solutions of $x(t) = 4$ in one period, checks them by substitution, and does not lose the second solution.
rubric: Forces: components have the correct signs for their quadrants; the resultant (magnitude $100$ N, direction about $56.9^\circ$) comes from two methods that agree; and the zero dot product is explained by perpendicularity.
rubric: Reasoning and communication: every step has units and stated conventions, there is no circular reasoning, and the oral explanation is accurate and clear without notes.
safety: If you record your own motion, use a light object, such as a small weight on a spring or on a short string, at a desk or table. Keep your face away from it and never use a heavy or fragile weight. No mains electricity is needed for any part of this project.
tutor: Here are my estimates of $A$, $D$, $T$ and $\varphi$, with my residual table. Which parameter looks wrong, and how can I tell from the pattern of the residuals?
tutor: Check my phasor diagram. Why does a negative phase delay the signal, and how do I see the time of the first peak on the circle?
tutor: I found the resultant two ways and the directions differ by a few degrees. Is this rounding or a quadrant error, and how do I decide?

@review
### Week by week

**Weeks 1 and 2: angles and the unit circle.** $\pi\text{ rad} = 180^\circ$. The formulas $s = r\theta$ and $A = \frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$ need radians. The point at angle $\theta$ on $x^2 + y^2 = 1$ is $(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$. Know the exact values at multiples of $\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $\frac{\pi}{4}$, and evaluate anywhere with a reference angle plus the quadrant signs.

**Weeks 3 and 4: graphs and inverses.** In $y = A\sin\bigl(B(x - C)\bigr) + D$ the amplitude is $|A|$, the period $2\pi/B$, the shift $C$ (factor out $B$ first) and the midline $D$. Tangent has period $\pi$ and asymptotes at $\frac{\pi}{2} + k\pi$. The ranges are $\arcsin \in [-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}]$, $\arccos \in [0, \pi]$ and $\arctan \in (-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2})$, and $\arcsin(\sin x) = x$ only on the principal range.

**Weeks 5 and 6: identities and equations.** Know the Pythagorean, sum, difference and double-angle formulas, and prove identities from one side only. To solve an equation, reduce to one function, isolate it, use symmetry (two solutions per turn for sine and cosine) and filter to the interval. For $\sin kx = c$ solve over $k$ turns.

**Weeks 7 and 8: triangles and polar coordinates.** The law of sines handles ASA, AAS and SSA; the law of cosines handles SAS and SSS; the area is $\frac{1}{2}ab\sin C$. Test the ambiguous case by comparing $a$ with $b\sin A$. For polar coordinates, $x = r\cos\theta$, $y = r\sin\theta$ and $r^2 = x^2 + y^2$, with the quadrant of $\theta$ decided by the signs.

**Weeks 9 and 10: vectors.** Components are $\langle|\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta, |\mathbf{v}|\sin\theta\rangle$ and add componentwise. The dot product $\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v} = u_1v_1 + u_2v_2 = |\mathbf{u}||\mathbf{v}|\cos\theta$ is zero exactly for perpendicular vectors, and $\text{proj}_{\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{u} = \frac{\mathbf{u}\cdot\mathbf{v}}{\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{v}}\mathbf{v}$.

**Weeks 11 and 12: complex numbers and oscillations.** Write $z = re^{i\theta}$; multiplying multiplies moduli and adds arguments, and de Moivre's theorem gives powers and $n$ roots. The oscillation $A\cos(\omega t + \varphi) + D$, with $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T$, is $D$ plus the real part of $Ze^{i\omega t}$, where $Z = Ae^{i\varphi}$ is the phasor, and sinusoids of one frequency add as phasors.

### Self-check

You are ready for the final if you can do each of these without notes:

- draw the unit circle with the standard angles in radians and degrees, and their coordinates;
- convert between degrees and radians and compute arc length and sector area;
- sketch a transformed sine or cosine and state its amplitude, period, shift and midline;
- evaluate inverse trigonometric functions and compositions using the correct range;
- prove an identity from one side and find all solutions of an equation in an interval;
- choose between the two laws for a triangle and handle the SSA case;
- convert between polar and rectangular coordinates in any quadrant;
- resolve vectors, and compute a dot product, an angle and a projection;
- write a complex number in polar form, multiply it, and find powers and roots;
- extract amplitude, frequency and phase from an oscillation and combine two sinusoids.

### The most common errors

1. Using degrees where a formula needs radians, or a calculator in the wrong mode.
2. Taking $\arctan(y/x)$ as the angle without checking the quadrant. This hits polar form, vectors and complex numbers alike.
3. Reading a shift without factoring out the coefficient of $x$ (or of $t$).
4. Treating $\arcsin(\sin x)$ as $x$ for every $x$.
5. Doing the same operation to both sides when proving an identity.
6. Dividing by a trigonometric factor, or forgetting to enlarge the interval for $\sin 2x$, and so losing solutions.
7. Adding the magnitudes of vectors or the amplitudes of sinusoids instead of adding components or phasors.

prompt: A circle has radius $10$ cm. A chord subtends a central angle of $100^\circ$ at the centre. The region between the chord and its (shorter) arc is a circular segment. Find the area of this segment in square centimetres, to two decimal places.
answer: 38.03
check: quad(lambda x: 2*sqrt(10**2 - x**2), 10*cos(math.radians(50)), 10)
tol: 0.005
unit: cm^2
hint: The segment is the sector cut out by the central angle, minus the triangle formed by the two radii and the chord.
hint: Convert $100^\circ$ to radians for the sector area $\frac{1}{2}r^2\theta$. The triangle has two sides $10$ with included angle $100^\circ$, so its area is $\frac{1}{2}(10)(10)\sin 100^\circ$.
solution: The central angle is $\theta = 100^\circ = \frac{5\pi}{9} \approx 1.7453$ rad. The sector has area $\frac{1}{2}(10)^2(1.7453) \approx 87.27$ cm^2. The triangle has area $\frac{1}{2}(10)(10)\sin 100^\circ \approx 50(0.9848) = 49.24$ cm^2. The segment has area $87.27 - 49.24 \approx 38.03$ cm^2. In general the area is $\frac{1}{2}r^2(\theta - \sin\theta)$.

@exam tuple
prompt: Two signals with the same angular frequency $\omega$ are added: $x_1(t) = 4\cos\omega t$ and $x_2(t) = 4\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{2\pi}{3}\right)$. The sum can be written $R\cos(\omega t + \varphi)$ with $R > 0$ and $-\pi < \varphi \le \pi$. Enter $(R, \varphi)$ in exact form.
answer: (4, pi/3)
check: (lambda z: (abs(z), cmath.phase(z)))(4 + 4*cmath.exp(2*I*pi/3))
hint: Represent each signal by its phasor and add the phasors in the complex plane.
hint: The phasors are $4$ and $4e^{2\pi i/3}$. Write the second as $a + bi$ using the unit circle, add, and then find the modulus and the argument of the sum (watch the quadrant).
solution: $Z = 4 + 4e^{2\pi i/3} = 4 + 4\left(-\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i\right) = 2 + 2\sqrt{3}\,i$. Then $R = |Z| = \sqrt{4 + 12} = 4$, and $\tan\varphi = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{2} = \sqrt{3}$ with $Z$ in Quadrant I, so $\varphi = \frac{\pi}{3}$. Thus $x_1 + x_2 = 4\cos\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$. Check at $t = 0$: $4 + 4\cos\frac{2\pi}{3} = 2$ and $4\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 2$.

@exam self
prompt: A radar at the origin tracks an object. At time $t = 0$ the object is at polar position $(12\text{ km}, 40^\circ)$. Ten minutes later it is at $(16\text{ km}, 100^\circ)$. Angles are measured counterclockwise from the positive $x$-axis, and you may assume constant velocity between the two fixes. (a) Convert both positions to rectangular coordinates. (b) Find the displacement vector and its length, and confirm the length with the law of cosines in the triangle formed by the radar and the two positions. (c) Find the average velocity vector in km/h, the speed, and the direction angle. (d) Using a dot product with the unit vector from the radar towards the first position, find the component of the velocity along that radial direction, and say whether the object is moving towards or away from the radar.
hint: Use $x = r\cos\theta$ and $y = r\sin\theta$ for part (a). The angle at the radar between the two fixes is $100^\circ - 40^\circ$.
hint: Ten minutes is $\frac{1}{6}$ hour, so the velocity is $6$ times the displacement. The radial unit vector is $\langle\cos 40^\circ, \sin 40^\circ\rangle$.
solution: (a) $P_1 = (12\cos 40^\circ, 12\sin 40^\circ) \approx (9.19, 7.71)$ and $P_2 = (16\cos 100^\circ, 16\sin 100^\circ) \approx (-2.78, 15.76)$. (b) The displacement is $\mathbf{d} = P_2 - P_1 \approx \langle -11.97, 8.04\rangle$, with length about $14.42$ km. The angle at the radar is $60^\circ$, so the law of cosines gives $|\mathbf{d}|^2 = 12^2 + 16^2 - 2(12)(16)\cos 60^\circ = 208$ and $|\mathbf{d}| = \sqrt{208} \approx 14.42$ km, in agreement. (c) With $\Delta t = \frac{1}{6}$ h the velocity is $6\mathbf{d} \approx \langle -71.8, 48.3\rangle$ km/h, the speed is $6\sqrt{208} \approx 86.5$ km/h, and the direction angle is $\operatorname{atan2}(48.3, -71.8) \approx 146^\circ$, in Quadrant II (not $34^\circ$). (d) With $\hat{\mathbf{r}} = \langle\cos 40^\circ, \sin 40^\circ\rangle$, $\mathbf{d}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = 16\cos 60^\circ - 12 = -4$ km, since $P_2\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = 16\cos(100^\circ - 40^\circ)$ and $P_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}} = 12$. So the radial velocity is $6(-4) = -24$ km/h. It is negative, so the object is moving towards the radar at the first fix.
rubric: Part (a) gives $P_1 \approx (9.19, 7.71)$ and $P_2 \approx (-2.78, 15.76)$, with degrees and radians used consistently.
rubric: Part (b) finds $\mathbf{d} \approx \langle -11.97, 8.04\rangle$ with length about $14.42$ km and confirms $|\mathbf{d}|^2 = 208$ by the law of cosines using the $60^\circ$ angle at the radar.
rubric: Part (c) gives a velocity of about $\langle -71.8, 48.3\rangle$ km/h, a speed of about $86.5$ km/h, and a direction of about $146^\circ$ with the quadrant checked.
rubric: Part (d) computes the radial component $-24$ km/h with a dot product and interprets the sign as motion towards the radar.
