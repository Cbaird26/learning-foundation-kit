@article
A function of several variables can change in many directions. The simplest are the coordinate directions.

### Partial derivatives

The **partial derivative** of $f(x,y)$ with respect to $x$ is

$$f_x(x,y)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h,y)-f(x,y)}{h},$$

the rate of change when only $x$ varies. In practice: **differentiate with respect to $x$, treating $y$ as a constant**. For $f=x^2y^3$, $f_x=2xy^3$ and $f_y=3x^2y^2$. Higher-order partials such as $f_{xx}$ and $f_{xy}=\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\!\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right)$ follow the same rules. If the second partials are continuous, **Clairaut's theorem** says the mixed partials are equal: $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$.

### The gradient and the tangent plane

The **gradient** collects the partials into a vector:

$$\nabla f(x,y)=\big(f_x(x,y),\,f_y(x,y)\big),\qquad\nabla f(x,y,z)=(f_x,f_y,f_z).$$

If $f_x$ and $f_y$ are continuous near $(a,b)$, the surface $z=f(x,y)$ has the **tangent plane**

$$z=f(a,b)+f_x(a,b)(x-a)+f_y(a,b)(y-b),$$

the two-variable version of the tangent line, and the **linear approximation** $f(x,y)\approx f(a,b)+\nabla f(a,b)\cdot(x-a,y-b)$ is good for $(x,y)$ near $(a,b)$.

### Directional derivatives

The rate of change of $f$ at a point in the direction of a **unit** vector $\mathbf u$ is the **directional derivative**

$$D_{\mathbf u}f=\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u=|\nabla f|\cos\varphi,$$

where $\varphi$ is the angle between $\nabla f$ and $\mathbf u$. Therefore:

- $f$ increases fastest in the direction of $\nabla f$, at rate $|\nabla f|$ (the direction of **steepest ascent**);
- $f$ decreases fastest in the direction of $-\nabla f$;
- $D_{\mathbf u}f=0$ when $\mathbf u\perp\nabla f$: the gradient is perpendicular to the level curve through the point.

The vector $\mathbf u$ must have length $1$; if you are given a direction vector $\mathbf v$, divide by $|\mathbf v|$ first.

### Critical points

At a local minimum or maximum in the interior of the domain, where $f$ is differentiable, every partial derivative is $0$, so $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$. Such points are **critical points**, and a critical point may also be a saddle point.

### Gradient descent

To minimize $f$ when equations such as $\nabla f=\mathbf 0$ cannot be solved by hand, walk downhill. Start at $\mathbf x_0$ and repeat

$$\mathbf x_{k+1}=\mathbf x_k-\alpha\,\nabla f(\mathbf x_k),$$

where $\alpha>0$ is the **step size** (learning rate). Since $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest descent, a small enough step lowers $f$.

The step size matters. For $f(x)=x^2$ we have $f'(x)=2x$, so $x_{k+1}=(1-2\alpha)x_k$. This converges to the minimum $0$ when $|1-2\alpha|<1$, that is $0<\alpha<1$; at $\alpha=\frac12$ it lands on $0$ in one step; at $\alpha>1$ the iterates alternate in sign and grow, so the method **diverges**. For $f=ax^2+by^2$ the stable step size is limited by the *steepest* direction ($\alpha<1/\max(a,b)$), while progress in the flattest direction is slowest, so elongated level curves make gradient descent slow. For a general smooth function gradient descent finds a point with small gradient, which is a local, not necessarily global, minimum. Machine learning libraries compute $\nabla f$ automatically, but the update rule is exactly this one.

@example
title: Gradient and directional derivative
problem: For $f(x,y)=x^2y^3$ find $\nabla f(1,2)$ and the directional derivative at $(1,2)$ in the direction of $\mathbf v=(3,4)$.
step: $f_x=2xy^3$ and $f_y=3x^2y^2$. At $(1,2)$: $f_x=2\cdot1\cdot8=16$ and $f_y=3\cdot1\cdot4=12$, so $\nabla f(1,2)=(16,12)$.
step: The direction vector has length $|\mathbf v|=5$, so the unit vector is $\mathbf u=\left(\frac35,\frac45\right)$.
step: $D_{\mathbf u}f=16\cdot\frac35+12\cdot\frac45=\frac{48+48}{5}=\frac{96}5=19.2$.
step: As a check, the largest possible rate is $|\nabla f|=\sqrt{256+144}=20$, and $19.2\le20$.
answer: $\nabla f(1,2)=(16,12)$ and $D_{\mathbf u}f=\frac{96}5$

@example
title: Two steps of gradient descent
problem: Minimize $f(x,y)=x^2+3y^2$ with gradient descent from $(4,2)$ and step size $\alpha=0.1$. Compute the first two iterates.
step: The gradient is $\nabla f=(2x,6y)$. At $(4,2)$ it is $(8,12)$.
step: $\mathbf x_1=(4,2)-0.1(8,12)=(3.2,\,0.8)$.
step: At $(3.2,0.8)$ the gradient is $(6.4,4.8)$, so $\mathbf x_2=(3.2-0.64,\;0.8-0.48)=(2.56,\,0.32)$.
step: The $x$-coordinate shrinks by the factor $1-2\alpha=0.8$ and the $y$-coordinate by $1-6\alpha=0.4$ each step. Both go to $0$, and $f$ decreases from $28$ to $12.16$ to about $6.86$, converging to the minimum at the origin.
answer: $\mathbf x_1=(3.2,0.8)$ and $\mathbf x_2=(2.56,0.32)$

@mistakes
- Forgetting to normalize the direction vector before computing $\nabla f\cdot\mathbf u$.
- Moving along $+\nabla f$ (uphill) when minimizing, instead of $-\nabla f$.
- Differentiating with respect to $x$ and also differentiating the factors that contain $y$: all other variables are constants.
- Using a step size that is too large and concluding that the method "does not work", when the iterates are diverging because $\alpha$ is outside the stable range.

model:
```python
def gradient_descent(grad, x0, alpha, steps):
    p = list(x0)
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = grad(p)
        p = [pi - alpha * gi for pi, gi in zip(p, g)]
    return p
```
hint: Copy `x0` into a new list before looping.
hint: In each step compute the gradient once at the current point, then build the new point from it.
solution: Copy the start, then repeat `steps` times: evaluate the gradient at the current point and subtract `alpha` times it from every coordinate. For `f = x**2 + 2*y**2` with `alpha = 0.25` the $y$-coordinate lands exactly on `0` after one step, since $1-4\alpha=0$, and the $x$-coordinate halves each step. With `alpha = 1.1` on $x^2$ the factor is $-1.2$, so the iterates grow: the method diverges.
