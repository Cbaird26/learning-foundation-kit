@article
Week 8 started from a series and found its function. Now we go the other way: given a function $f$ that is smooth near $a$, find the power series that represents it. Suppose $f(x)=\sum c_n(x-a)^n$. Differentiating $k$ times and evaluating at $x=a$ gives $f^{(k)}(a)=k!\,c_k$, which forces

$$c_k=\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}.$$

### Taylor polynomials and series

The **Taylor polynomial** of degree $n$ of $f$ at $a$ is

$$T_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}(x-a)^k.$$

It is the unique polynomial of degree at most $n$ that matches $f$ and its first $n$ derivatives at $a$. Letting $n\to\infty$ gives the **Taylor series**; when $a=0$ it is the **Maclaurin series**. The first-degree polynomial is the tangent line, so $T_n$ generalizes linear approximation.

### How good is the approximation? Taylor's theorem

The error $R_n(x)=f(x)-T_n(x)$ is described by the **Lagrange form of the remainder**: if $f^{(n)}$ is continuous on the closed interval between $a$ and $x$ and $f^{(n+1)}$ exists on its interior, then for some $c$ strictly between them

$$R_n(x)=\frac{f^{(n+1)}(c)}{(n+1)!}(x-a)^{n+1}.$$

So if $|f^{(n+1)}(t)|\le M$ for all $t$ between $a$ and $x$,

$$|R_n(x)|\le\frac{M\,|x-a|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}.$$

The Taylor series of $f$ converges to $f(x)$ exactly when $R_n(x)\to0$. For $e^x$, $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ this holds for every $x$, because $\frac{|x|^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\to0$ for every real $x$, as Week 6 showed.

Note that having a Taylor series is not enough: the function $e^{-1/x^2}$ (with value $0$ at $x=0$) has all derivatives $0$ at $0$, so its Maclaurin series is $0$, which equals the function only at $x=0$.

### The standard Maclaurin series

$$e^x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x^n}{n!}\ (R=\infty),\qquad\sin x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{(2n+1)!}\ (R=\infty),\qquad\cos x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{(2n)!}\ (R=\infty),$$

$$\frac1{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n,\qquad\ln(1+x)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{n+1}x^n}{n},\qquad\arctan x=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n+1}}{2n+1},$$

with $R=1$ for the last three. You rarely compute new series from derivatives; instead substitute into these ($e^{-x^2}=\sum\frac{(-1)^nx^{2n}}{n!}$), multiply, differentiate or integrate them.

### Uses

- **Limits.** $\frac{e^x-1-x}{x^2}=\frac12+\frac x6+\cdots\to\frac12$ as $x\to0$.
- **Integrals with no elementary antiderivative.** $\int_0^1e^{-x^2}dx=\sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{(-1)^n}{n!(2n+1)}$, an alternating series with a computable error.
- **Numerical approximation.** A calculator or library computes $\sin x$ by reducing $x$ to a small interval and evaluating a polynomial; the remainder bound says how many terms are needed.

### In code

A truncated series is a loop. For $\sin$ only odd powers occur, so the term of degree $k$ has sign $(-1)^{(k-1)/2}$. Because the factorial in the denominator grows so fast, a handful of terms is accurate for small $|x|$, but for large $|x|$ the terms first grow and rounding can ruin the result; real libraries reduce the argument first.

@example
title: A Taylor polynomial from derivatives
problem: Find the Taylor polynomial of degree 3 of $f(x)=\ln x$ centered at $a=1$.
step: Compute derivatives: $f'=\frac1x$, $f''=-\frac1{x^2}$, $f'''=\frac2{x^3}$.
step: Evaluate at $1$: $f(1)=0$, $f'(1)=1$, $f''(1)=-1$, $f'''(1)=2$.
step: The coefficients $\frac{f^{(k)}(1)}{k!}$ are $0$, $1$, $-\frac12$, $\frac26=\frac13$.
step: $T_3(x)=(x-1)-\frac{(x-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(x-1)^3}{3}$.
answer: $(x-1)-\frac{(x-1)^2}{2}+\frac{(x-1)^3}{3}$

@example
title: Choosing the degree with the remainder
problem: How large must $n$ be so that the Maclaurin polynomial $T_n$ of $e^x$ approximates $e=e^1$ with error less than $10^{-6}$?
step: On $[0,1]$ every derivative of $e^x$ is $e^x\le e<3$, so take $M=3$.
step: The bound is $|R_n(1)|\le\frac{3\cdot1^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}$. We need $\frac{3}{(n+1)!}<10^{-6}$.
step: For $n=8$: $(n+1)!=9!=362880$ and $\frac{3}{362880}\approx8.3\times10^{-6}$, too big.
step: For $n=9$: $10!=3628800$ and $\frac3{3628800}\approx8.3\times10^{-7}<10^{-6}$.
step: So $n=9$ guarantees the accuracy; $T_9(1)=\sum_{k=0}^9\frac1{k!}\approx2.7182815$.
answer: $n = 9$

@mistakes
- Forgetting the factorial: the coefficient is $\frac{f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}$, not $f^{(k)}(a)$.
- Using powers of $x$ instead of $(x-a)$ when the center is not $0$.
- Bounding $|f^{(n+1)}|$ at the point $x$ rather than the maximum on the whole interval between $a$ and $x$.
- Confusing the degree $n$ of $T_n$ with the order $n+1$ of the derivative that appears in the remainder.

assert taylor_sin(2.0, 0) == 0
assert taylor_sin(2.0, 1) == 2.0

model:
```python
import math


def taylor_sin(x, n):
    total = 0.0
    sign = 1
    for k in range(1, n + 1, 2):
        total += sign * x ** k / math.factorial(k)
        sign = -sign
    return total
```
hint: Only odd degrees $k=1,3,5,\dots$ appear, and the signs alternate $+,-,+,\dots$.
hint: Loop with `range(1, n + 1, 2)` and flip the sign after each term.
solution: Add `sign * x**k / k!` for odd `k` up to `n`, flipping the sign each time. At `x = 1` the first omitted term has degree 13, so the error is below $1/13!\approx1.6\times10^{-10}$ (the alternating series bound), which the test uses.
