@article
Calculus I gave you the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus and a short list of antiderivatives. Most integrands are not on that list, so Calculus II is largely a toolbox of techniques that turn an unfamiliar integral into a familiar one. The two most important tools each *undo* a differentiation rule: **substitution** undoes the chain rule, and **integration by parts** undoes the product rule.

### Substitution

The chain rule says $\frac{d}{dx}F(g(x)) = F'(g(x))\,g'(x)$. Read backwards, if $F' = f$ then

$$\int f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = F(g(x)) + C = \int f(u)\,du \Big|_{u=g(x)}.$$

In practice you choose $u = g(x)$ (often the "inside" of a composite function, or a part whose derivative also appears as a factor), compute $du = g'(x)\,dx$, and rewrite the whole integral in terms of $u$. Nothing of $x$ may remain.

For a **definite integral** you can change the limits along with the variable:

$$\int_a^b f(g(x))\,g'(x)\,dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\,du.$$

Then you never convert back to $x$. This requires $g'$ to be continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f$ continuous on the range of $g$.

### Integration by parts

The product rule gives $(uv)' = u'v + uv'$. Integrating both sides and rearranging yields

$$\int u\,dv = uv - \int v\,du,$$

where $du = u'(x)\,dx$ and $dv = v'(x)\,dx$. For definite integrals, $\int_a^b u\,dv = \big[uv\big]_a^b - \int_a^b v\,du$.

The art is the choice of $u$ and $dv$. You want $du$ to be *simpler* than $u$, and $v$ to be obtainable from $dv$. A common mnemonic, **LIATE**, ranks candidates for $u$: Logarithmic, Inverse trigonometric, Algebraic (polynomials), Trigonometric, Exponential. It is a guide, not a theorem.

Three patterns recur:

- **Polynomial times exponential or trig** ($x^2 e^x$, $x\sin x$): let $u$ be the polynomial. Each round of parts lowers its degree by one, so $x^n$ needs $n$ rounds.
- **A logarithm or inverse trig function alone** ($\ln x$, $\arctan x$): let $u$ be that function and $dv = dx$. Differentiating turns the awkward function into an algebraic one.
- **The cycle** ($e^x \sin x$): after two rounds the original integral reappears, and you solve for it algebraically.

### Always check

Differentiate your answer. If you get the integrand back, you are right; if you are off by a constant factor, you probably lost a $\frac12$ from $du$. A definite integral of a positive function must be positive, which catches sign slips cheaply.

@example
title: Substitution in a definite integral
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x(1+x^2)^3\,dx$.
step: The inside function $1+x^2$ has derivative $2x$, and a multiple of $x$ is present. Let $u = 1+x^2$, so $du = 2x\,dx$ and $x\,dx = \tfrac12\,du$.
step: Change the limits: when $x=0$, $u=1$; when $x=1$, $u=2$.
step: Rewrite: $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x(1+x^2)^3\,dx = \tfrac12\int_1^2 u^3\,du$.
step: Integrate: $\tfrac12\cdot\left[\tfrac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^2 = \tfrac18(16-1)$.
step: The value is $\dfrac{15}{8}$.
answer: $\frac{15}{8}$

@example
title: Parts with a polynomial and an exponential
problem: Find $\displaystyle\int x e^{3x}\,dx$.
step: A polynomial times an exponential: let $u = x$ and $dv = e^{3x}\,dx$. Then $du = dx$ and $v = \tfrac13 e^{3x}$.
step: Apply the formula: $\int x e^{3x}\,dx = \tfrac13 x e^{3x} - \int \tfrac13 e^{3x}\,dx$.
step: The remaining integral is easy: $\int \tfrac13 e^{3x}\,dx = \tfrac19 e^{3x}$.
step: So the result is $\tfrac13 x e^{3x} - \tfrac19 e^{3x} + C$.
step: Check by differentiating: $\tfrac13 e^{3x} + x e^{3x} - \tfrac13 e^{3x} = x e^{3x}$. It matches.
answer: $\frac13 x e^{3x} - \frac19 e^{3x} + C$

@mistakes
- Forgetting to change the limits (or to convert back to $x$) after a substitution in a definite integral, so the $u$-values are used as if they were $x$-values.
- Dropping the constant factor from $du$: with $u = x^2$, $x\,dx$ is $\frac12 du$, not $du$.
- Choosing $u$ and $dv$ so that $\int v\,du$ is harder than the original (for instance $u = e^x$, $dv = x\,dx$ in $\int x e^x\,dx$).
- Sign errors in parts: the second term is $-\int v\,du$, and the minus sign must multiply the whole remaining integral.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x e^{x^2}\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: (e-1)/2
check: quad(lambda x: x*exp(x*x), 0, 1)
hint: Let $u = x^2$, so $x\,dx = \frac12 du$.
hint: The limits become $u=0$ to $u=1$, and you need $\frac12\int_0^1 e^u\,du$.
solution: With $u = x^2$ the integral is $\frac12\int_0^1 e^u\,du = \frac12(e-1)$.

@practice expr
prompt: Find an antiderivative of $\dfrac{x}{x^2+4}$.
vars: x
answer: ln(x^2+4)/2
check: lambda x: 0.5*log(x*x + 4)
const: yes
hint: The derivative of the denominator is $2x$, which is almost the numerator.
hint: Let $u = x^2+4$; then $x\,dx = \frac12\,du$ and you need $\frac12\int \frac{du}{u}$.
solution: $\frac12\int\frac{du}{u} = \frac12\ln|u| + C = \frac12\ln(x^2+4) + C$. The absolute value is unnecessary because $x^2+4>0$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi} x\sin x\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi
check: quad(lambda x: x*sin(x), 0, pi)
hint: A polynomial times a trigonometric function: let $u = x$ and $dv = \sin x\,dx$.
hint: Then $v = -\cos x$, and the formula gives $\big[-x\cos x\big]_0^\pi + \int_0^\pi \cos x\,dx$.
solution: $\big[-x\cos x\big]_0^\pi = \pi$ and $\int_0^\pi\cos x\,dx = 0$, so the value is $\pi$.

@practice mc
prompt: To evaluate $\displaystyle\int x^2\ln x\,dx$ by parts, which choice of $u$ and $dv$ works well?
choice*: $u = \ln x$ and $dv = x^2\,dx$
choice: $u = x^2$ and $dv = \ln x\,dx$
choice: $u = x^2\ln x$ and $dv = dx$
choice: $u = 1$ and $dv = x^2\ln x\,dx$
hint: You need an antiderivative $v$ of $dv$ that you already know.
solution: Taking $u = \ln x$ gives $du = dx/x$, and $dv = x^2\,dx$ gives $v = x^3/3$, so $\int v\,du = \int x^2/3\,dx$ is easy. Choosing $dv = \ln x\,dx$ first requires knowing the antiderivative of $\ln x$, which is itself a parts problem.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^2 e^x\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: e-2
check: quad(lambda x: x*x*exp(x), 0, 1)
hint: Use parts twice, each time letting $u$ be the polynomial.
hint: The first round gives $x^2e^x - 2\int xe^x\,dx$, and $\int xe^x\,dx = xe^x - e^x$.
solution: An antiderivative is $x^2e^x - 2xe^x + 2e^x$. At $1$ it is $e$; at $0$ it is $2$. The value is $e-2$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{x}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx$.
answer: 1/4
check: quad(lambda x: x/(1+x*x)**2, 0, 1)
hint: Let $u = 1+x^2$ and change the limits.
hint: The integral becomes $\frac12\int_1^2 u^{-2}\,du$.
solution: $\frac12\left[-u^{-1}\right]_1^2 = \frac12\left(1-\frac12\right) = \frac14$.

@quiz num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \arctan x\,dx$. Give an exact value.
answer: pi/4 - ln(2)/2
check: quad(lambda x: math.atan(x), 0, 1)
hint: Let $u = \arctan x$ and $dv = dx$.
hint: You get $\big[x\arctan x\big]_0^1 - \int_0^1 \frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx$, and the last integral needs a substitution.
solution: $\big[x\arctan x\big]_0^1 = \pi/4$, and $\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x^2}dx = \frac12\ln 2$. The value is $\frac{\pi}{4}-\frac{\ln 2}{2}$.
