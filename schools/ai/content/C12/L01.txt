@article
You stand on a hillside in fog and can feel only the slope under your feet. In which direction should you step to climb fastest, and how quickly does the ground curve away from the slope you feel? Training a model is the same problem with millions of directions, and three objects answer it: the gradient, the Jacobian and the Hessian.

### The gradient

For a function $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$, the **partial derivative** $\partial f/\partial x_i$ is the ordinary derivative in the variable $x_i$ with all the other variables held fixed. The **gradient** stacks them into a vector:

$$\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial f/\partial x_1 \\ \vdots \\ \partial f/\partial x_n \end{pmatrix}.$$

Take $f(x,y) = x^2 y + 3y^2$ at the point $(2,1)$. Then $\partial f/\partial x = 2xy = 4$ and $\partial f/\partial y = x^2 + 6y = 10$, so $\nabla f(2,1) = (4, 10)^\top$.

If $f$ is differentiable, the **directional derivative** of $f$ at $\mathbf{x}$ in the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf{u}$ is $D_{\mathbf{u}} f(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{u}$. By the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality this is at most $\lVert \nabla f \rVert_2$, with equality when $\mathbf{u}$ points along the gradient. So the gradient is the direction of **steepest ascent**, and $-\nabla f$ is the direction of steepest descent. In the example, the direction $\mathbf{u} = (3,4)/5$ gives $(4 \cdot 3 + 10 \cdot 4)/5 = 10.4$, below the maximum $\lVert (4,10) \rVert_2 \approx 10.77$. Along a level set $\{\mathbf{x} : f(\mathbf{x}) = c\}$ the function does not change, so the gradient is perpendicular to the level set.

### The Jacobian

For a vector-valued function $\mathbf{g}:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$, the **Jacobian** is the $m \times n$ matrix with entries $J_{ij} = \partial g_i / \partial x_j$: row $i$ is the gradient (transposed) of the $i$-th output. For $\mathbf{g}(x,y) = (xy,\; x^2 + y,\; y^2)$ at $(2,3)$,

$$\mathbf{J} = \begin{pmatrix} y & x \\ 2x & 1 \\ 0 & 2y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 4 & 1 \\ 0 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The Jacobian is the best linear approximation: $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{d}) \approx \mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{J}\mathbf{d}$. For a scalar function ($m = 1$) it is the row vector $\nabla f^\top$.

### The Hessian

The **Hessian** of $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is the $n \times n$ matrix of second partial derivatives, $H_{ij} = \partial^2 f / \partial x_i \partial x_j$; it is the Jacobian of the gradient. If the second partial derivatives are continuous, mixed partials commute, so $\mathbf{H}$ is **symmetric**. For our example,

$$\mathbf{H}(x,y) = \begin{pmatrix} 2y & 2x \\ 2x & 6 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{H}(2,1) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 4 & 6 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The Hessian measures curvature: along a unit direction $\mathbf{u}$, the second derivative of $f$ is $\mathbf{u}^\top \mathbf{H} \mathbf{u}$.

### Taylor expansion

Putting the three objects together gives the second-order **Taylor expansion** of a twice continuously differentiable $f$:

$$f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{d}) \approx f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{d} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathbf{d}^\top \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\, \mathbf{d}.$$

At $(2,1)$ with $\mathbf{d} = (0.1, 0.1)$: $f = 7$, the gradient term is $0.4 + 1.0 = 1.4$, and the curvature term is $\tfrac12(2 \cdot 0.01 + 2 \cdot 4 \cdot 0.01 + 6 \cdot 0.01) = 0.08$. The estimate is $8.48$; the true value is $f(2.1, 1.1) = 8.481$.

| approximation | value at $(2.1, 1.1)$ |
|---|---|
| zeroth order: $f(2,1)$ | 7 |
| first order | 8.4 |
| second order | 8.48 |
| exact | 8.481 |

### Two formulas to remember

For $f(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{a}^\top \mathbf{w}$, the gradient is $\mathbf{a}$ and the Hessian is zero. For $f(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{A} \mathbf{w}$ with any square $\mathbf{A}$, the gradient is $(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top)\mathbf{w}$ and the Hessian is $\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top$; when $\mathbf{A}$ is symmetric these are $2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$ and $2\mathbf{A}$. Only the symmetric part of $\mathbf{A}$ matters in a quadratic form.

### In code

When you derive a gradient by hand, check it numerically with **central differences**: $\partial f / \partial x_i \approx \big(f(\mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{e}_i) - f(\mathbf{x} - h\mathbf{e}_i)\big) / 2h$ for a small $h$ such as $10^{-5}$. The error of this formula shrinks like $h^2$, much faster than the one-sided formula, whose error shrinks like $h$.

```python
def f(v):
    return v[0] ** 2 * v[1] + 3 * v[1] ** 2

h = 1e-5
print((f([2 + h, 1]) - f([2 - h, 1])) / (2 * h))   # about 4
print((f([2, 1 + h]) - f([2, 1 - h])) / (2 * h))   # about 10
```

@example
title: Gradient, Hessian and a direction
problem: Let $f(x,y) = e^{xy} + x^2$. Find $\nabla f(1,0)$ and $\mathbf{H}(1,0)$, and the derivative of $f$ at $(1,0)$ in the direction of the unit vector $(1,-1)/\sqrt{2}$.
step: First derivatives: $\partial f/\partial x = y e^{xy} + 2x$ and $\partial f/\partial y = x e^{xy}$. At $(1,0)$ these are $0 + 2 = 2$ and $1 \cdot e^0 = 1$, so $\nabla f(1,0) = (2, 1)^\top$.
step: Second derivatives: $f_{xx} = y^2 e^{xy} + 2$, $f_{xy} = e^{xy} + xy\,e^{xy}$, $f_{yy} = x^2 e^{xy}$. At $(1,0)$: $f_{xx} = 2$, $f_{xy} = 1$, $f_{yy} = 1$.
step: The Hessian is $\mathbf{H}(1,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, symmetric as expected.
step: The directional derivative is $\nabla f^\top \mathbf{u} = (2 \cdot 1 + 1 \cdot (-1))/\sqrt{2} = 1/\sqrt{2} \approx 0.707$.
answer: $\nabla f(1,0) = (2,1)^\top$, $\mathbf{H}(1,0) = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, directional derivative $\approx 0.707$.

@example
title: A quadratic form with a nonsymmetric matrix
problem: Find the gradient of $f(\mathbf{w}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$ with $\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 3 \end{pmatrix}$ at $\mathbf{w} = (1,1)^\top$.
step: Expand: $f = w_1^2 + 2 w_1 w_2 + 3 w_2^2$.
step: Differentiate: $\partial f/\partial w_1 = 2w_1 + 2w_2$ and $\partial f/\partial w_2 = 2w_1 + 6w_2$. At $(1,1)$ these are 4 and 8.
step: Check with the formula: $\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 6 \end{pmatrix}$, and multiplying by $(1,1)^\top$ gives $(4, 8)^\top$. The formula $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$ alone would give $(3, 3)^\top$, which is wrong.
answer: $\nabla f(1,1) = (4, 8)^\top$.

@mistakes
- Using $2\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w}$ for the gradient of $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{A} \mathbf{w}$ when $\mathbf{A}$ is not symmetric: the correct gradient is $(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{A}^\top)\mathbf{w}$.
- Writing the Jacobian of a map $\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^m$ as $n \times m$: it has one row per output, so it is $m \times n$.
- Saying the gradient points in the direction of steepest descent: it points uphill, and descent methods subtract it.
- Taking a directional derivative with a vector that is not of unit length: $\nabla f^\top \mathbf{u}$ is the rate of change only when $\lVert \mathbf{u} \rVert_2 = 1$.

model:
```python
def num_grad(f, x, h=1e-5):
    """Central-difference estimate of the gradient of f at x."""
    grad = []
    for i in range(len(x)):
        up = list(x)
        down = list(x)
        up[i] += h
        down[i] -= h
        grad.append((f(up) - f(down)) / (2 * h))
    return grad
```
hint: For each coordinate $i$, build two copies of `x`, one with $h$ added to entry $i$ and one with $h$ subtracted, and evaluate `f` on each.
solution: Copying the list for each coordinate keeps `x` unchanged and avoids carrying a perturbation into the next coordinate. The tests use $x^2$ terms, where the central difference is exact up to rounding but a one-sided difference is off by about $h$ and fails.

@quiz ms
prompt: Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be twice continuously differentiable and let $\mathbf{x}$ be a point where $\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \neq \mathbf{0}$. Which of these statements are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: The gradient at $\mathbf{x}$ is perpendicular to the level set of $f$ through $\mathbf{x}$.
choice*: The Hessian at $\mathbf{x}$ is a symmetric matrix.
choice*: Among all unit directions $\mathbf{u}$, the directional derivative $\nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top \mathbf{u}$ is largest for $\mathbf{u} = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) / \lVert \nabla f(\mathbf{x}) \rVert_2$.
choice: The Hessian is the Jacobian of $f$ itself, an $n \times n$ matrix of first derivatives.
hint: Recall the Cauchy–Schwarz argument for steepest ascent, the symmetry of mixed partials, and what the Hessian differentiates.
solution: The gradient is perpendicular to level sets because $f$ is constant along them, and mixed partial derivatives of a $C^2$ function commute, which makes the Hessian symmetric. Cauchy–Schwarz gives steepest ascent along the gradient. The Hessian is the Jacobian of the gradient, not of $f$, which is scalar-valued and has a $1 \times n$ Jacobian.
