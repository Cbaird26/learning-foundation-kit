@article
Suppose you must buy at least 4 units of something at the lowest cost, and a broker offers to charge you a price $\mu$ per unit of shortfall instead. If the price is too low you will happily fall short; if it is high you will comply. For each price, you can compute your best total outlay, and that number is always a lower bound on the true cost of obeying the rule. Duality studies how good the best such bound is.

### The dual problem

Take the primal problem $p^\star = \min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $g_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$ and $h_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, with Lagrangian $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = f + \sum_i\mu_i g_i + \sum_j\lambda_j h_j$. The **dual function** minimizes the Lagrangian over $\mathbf{x}$:
$$d(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) = \inf_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}),$$
and the **dual problem** is $d^\star = \max d(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$ subject to $\boldsymbol{\mu} \ge \mathbf{0}$. Two facts hold for every problem, convex or not. First, $d$ is **concave**, because it is a pointwise infimum of functions that are affine in $(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$. Second, **weak duality**: $d^\star \le p^\star$. The reason is a one-line argument: for any feasible $\mathbf{x}$ and any $\boldsymbol{\mu} \ge \mathbf{0}$, $\sum_i\mu_i g_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0$ and $h_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0$, so $d(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \le \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda}) \le f(\mathbf{x})$. The difference $p^\star - d^\star \ge 0$ is the **duality gap**.

**Example.** For $\min x^2$ subject to $x \ge 2$, write $g(x) = 2 - x \le 0$. Then $\mathcal{L} = x^2 + \mu(2 - x)$ is minimized at $x = \mu/2$, so $d(\mu) = 2\mu - \mu^2/4$. At $\mu = 3$ this gives the lower bound $3.75 \le p^\star$. Maximizing over $\mu \ge 0$: $d'(\mu) = 2 - \mu/2 = 0$ at $\mu = 4$, and $d(4) = 4$, which equals $p^\star = 4$ (at $x = 2$). The optimal dual variable is the KKT multiplier.

### Strong duality

**Strong duality** means $d^\star = p^\star$. It can fail in general, but for convex problems it holds under a mild condition. **Slater's condition**: the problem is convex ($f$ and $g_i$ convex, $h_j$ affine) and there is a point $\mathbf{x}$ that satisfies every equality constraint and has $g_i(\mathbf{x}) < 0$ strictly for every inequality constraint (strict feasibility is only required for non-affine $g_i$). **Theorem.** If the problem is convex and Slater's condition holds, then $d^\star = p^\star$, and if $p^\star$ is finite the dual optimum is attained. In that case, when the functions are differentiable, $\mathbf{x}^\star$ and $(\boldsymbol{\mu}^\star, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^\star)$ are optimal for the primal and dual problems exactly when they satisfy the KKT conditions of week 11.

The dual is useful for three reasons: it gives a certified lower bound on $p^\star$ from any $\boldsymbol{\mu} \ge \mathbf{0}$; it is always a concave maximization, even when the primal is hard; and for some problems, such as support vector machines, the dual has a structure (it depends on the data only through inner products) that enables kernels.

**Without convexity**, a gap can appear. For $\min -x^2$ subject to $-1 \le x \le 1$ the optimum is $p^\star = -1$, but the Lagrangian $-x^2 + \mu_1(x - 1) + \mu_2(-x - 1)$ is unbounded below in $x$ for every $\boldsymbol{\mu}$, so $d \equiv -\infty$ and the dual bound is useless.

### Non-convex landscapes: saddle points

A **saddle point** of a smooth function is a stationary point whose Hessian has both positive and negative eigenvalues; a **strict saddle** has at least one negative eigenvalue. Take $f(x,y) = x^2 - y^2 + y^4/2$: the gradient $(2x, -2y + 2y^3)$ vanishes at $(0,0)$ and $(0, \pm1)$. The Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(2, -2 + 6y^2)$, so $(0,0)$ is a strict saddle (eigenvalues $2$ and $-2$) and $(0,\pm1)$ are local, in fact global, minima with $f = -0.5$ (eigenvalues $2$ and $4$). Gradient descent with $\eta = 0.1$ from $(1, 0.01)$ gives:

| step | $x$ | $y$ | $f$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.107 | 0.062 | 0.0077 |
| 20 | 0.0115 | 0.363 | $-0.123$ |
| 30 | 0.0012 | 0.957 | $-0.496$ |
| 40 | 0.0001 | 0.9997 | $-0.500$ |

The iterates linger near the saddle, with $x$ shrinking and $y$ growing by a factor of about $1.2$ per step, then escape along the negative-curvature direction. Starting exactly at $y = 0$, the iterates would converge to the saddle itself. A theorem makes this precise: for a $C^2$ function with a Lipschitz gradient (constant $L$), gradient descent with a fixed $\eta < 1/L$ started from a random point (for instance with a continuous distribution) converges to a strict saddle with probability zero (Lee, Simchowitz, Jordan and Recht, 2016). (The quartic example is only locally Lipschitz; it illustrates the behavior.) Saddle points can still slow training badly, since the escape takes many steps when the negative curvature is small.

### What is known about deep networks

The loss of a neural network is non-convex, with many symmetric copies of any minimizer (permuting hidden units does not change the function). Researchers have established partial pictures, and much remains open. For deep linear networks, under mild conditions, every local minimum is a global minimum and the other stationary points are saddle points (Kawaguchi, 2016). Dauphin and colleagues (2014) argued, from analogies with random functions in physics, that in high dimensions most stationary points with high loss are saddle points rather than local minima. For realistic nonlinear networks, no complete theory explains why simple first-order methods usually find good solutions, how the landscape depends on width and depth, or how flatness of minima relates to generalization; those questions are active research and views differ. What you can rely on is the local analysis: check the gradient, then the Hessian eigenvalues, and remember that a small gradient alone does not say which kind of point you are at.

@example
title: Forming and solving a dual
problem: Find the dual function and the dual optimum for $\min x^2 + y^2$ subject to $x + y \ge 4$, and compare it with the primal optimum.
step: Write the constraint as $g = 4 - x - y \le 0$ and the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = x^2 + y^2 + \mu(4 - x - y)$.
step: Minimizing over $x$ and $y$: $2x - \mu = 0$ and $2y - \mu = 0$, so $x = y = \mu/2$.
step: Substitute: $d(\mu) = \mu^2/4 + \mu^2/4 + \mu(4 - \mu) = 4\mu - \mu^2/2$. This is concave in $\mu$.
step: Maximize over $\mu \ge 0$: $d'(\mu) = 4 - \mu = 0$ gives $\mu = 4$ and $d^\star = 16 - 8 = 8$. The primal optimum is at $x = y = 2$ with $p^\star = 8$. Slater's condition holds (the point $(3,3)$ has $g = -2 < 0$), so there is no gap, as the theorem says.
answer: $d(\mu) = 4\mu - \mu^2/2$, $d^\star = 8 = p^\star$ with optimal $\mu = 4$.

@example
title: Classifying stationary points of a non-convex function
problem: Find and classify the stationary points of $f(x,y) = x^2 - 2y^2 + y^4$.
step: The gradient is $(2x,\; -4y + 4y^3)$, which is zero when $x = 0$ and $y(y^2 - 1) = 0$, so the stationary points are $(0,0)$, $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$.
step: The Hessian is $\operatorname{diag}(2,\; -4 + 12y^2)$.
step: At $(0,0)$ the eigenvalues are $2$ and $-4$: a strict saddle with $f = 0$. At $(0,\pm 1)$ they are $2$ and $8$: strict local minima with $f = -1$.
step: Since $f \ge -1$ everywhere (write $f = x^2 + (y^2 - 1)^2 - 1$), the points $(0,\pm 1)$ are global minimizers, so this non-convex function has two global minimizers and no bad local minima.
answer: $(0,0)$ is a strict saddle point; $(0,1)$ and $(0,-1)$ are global minimizers with value $-1$.

@mistakes
- Believing the dual optimum equals the primal optimum for every problem: weak duality $d^\star \le p^\star$ always holds, but equality needs convexity and a condition such as Slater's.
- Taking the maximum of the dual function over all $\boldsymbol{\mu}$: for inequality constraints we require $\boldsymbol{\mu} \ge \mathbf{0}$.
- Concluding from a zero gradient that training has reached a minimum: the point may be a saddle, and only the Hessian's eigenvalues can tell.
- Treating "gradient descent avoids saddles" as a guarantee of fast training: the theorem is about convergence with probability one under conditions, and it says nothing about the time spent near a saddle.

@quiz self
prompt: A colleague says: "Neural network training works because the loss has no bad local minima, only saddle points, and gradient descent escapes saddle points." Evaluate this claim: say what is supported by theory or evidence, what is not, and what you would measure to test it on a particular network.
hint: Separate the theorems (deep linear networks, saddle avoidance of gradient descent with probability one) from the open questions about realistic networks, and think about Hessian eigenvalues.
solution: Parts of the claim have support. For deep linear networks under mild conditions, every local minimum is global (Kawaguchi, 2016); gradient descent with a small fixed step size almost surely avoids strict saddles (Lee and colleagues, 2016); and an influential argument (Dauphin and colleagues, 2014) says that high-loss stationary points in high dimensions tend to be saddles rather than minima. But these results do not establish the claim for realistic networks. The theorem on saddles covers only convergence in the limit, not the time spent near saddles; the linear-network result does not carry over to nonlinear architectures, and the random-function argument is an analogy that need not hold for real loss surfaces; also, non-strict saddles and wide flat regions are not covered. Whether training succeeds because of the landscape, the optimizer, the initialization or the data is still debated. To test it on a given network I would estimate the Hessian's spectrum at the end of training (for instance with Lanczos or Hessian-vector products) to see whether it has negative eigenvalues, compare the final losses of runs from different seeds, and check whether interpolating between two solutions shows a barrier, which would indicate separate basins.
