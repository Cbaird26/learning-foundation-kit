@article
Gradient descent looks at the slope and takes a cautious step because it does not know how fast the slope changes. What if we also measured the curvature and jumped straight to the bottom of the best-fitting bowl? That is Newton's method: it trades more computation per step for far fewer steps.

### The Newton step

At the current point $\mathbf{x}$ approximate $f$ by its second-order Taylor model in the step $\mathbf{d}$:
$$m(\mathbf{d}) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \nabla f(\mathbf{x})^\top\mathbf{d} + \tfrac12\mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{d}.$$
If $\mathbf{H}$ is positive definite, $m$ is a bowl whose bottom is where $\nabla m = \nabla f + \mathbf{H}\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{0}$, that is $\mathbf{d} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$. **Newton's method** is
$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}_k)^{-1}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k).$$
In practice one solves $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{d} = -\nabla f$ rather than forming the inverse. There is no step size: curvature supplies the scale. For a strictly convex quadratic, the model is exact, so **one step reaches the minimizer** from any start. For a one-variable function, the update is $x \leftarrow x - f'(x)/f''(x)$, which is the root-finding Newton iteration applied to $f'$.

### Local quadratic convergence

For $f(x) = e^x - 2x$, we have $f' = e^x - 2$ and $f'' = e^x$, so the update is $x \leftarrow x - 1 + 2e^{-x}$; the minimizer is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693147$. From $x_0 = 0$:

| step | $x_k$ | error $\lvert x_k - \ln 2\rvert$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.000000 | $3.07\times10^{-1}$ |
| 2 | 0.735759 | $4.26\times10^{-2}$ |
| 3 | 0.694042 | $8.95\times10^{-4}$ |
| 4 | 0.693148 | $4.0\times10^{-7}$ |
| 5 | 0.693147 | $8.0\times10^{-14}$ |

The number of correct digits roughly doubles at every step. This is **quadratic convergence**, and the theorem behind it is: *if $f$ is twice continuously differentiable, $\mathbf{H}$ is Lipschitz near a minimizer $\mathbf{x}^\star$, and $\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}^\star)$ is positive definite, then there is a neighborhood of $\mathbf{x}^\star$ and a constant $C$ such that starting inside it, Newton's iterates converge and $\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k+1} - \mathbf{x}^\star\rVert_2 \le C\lVert\mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{x}^\star\rVert_2^2$.* Compare with gradient descent's linear rate, where each step gains a fixed fraction of a digit.

### Costs and failure modes

- **Cost.** Forming $\mathbf{H}$ needs $O(n^2)$ memory and solving the system $O(n^3)$ operations for $n$ parameters. A network with a million parameters would need $10^{12}$ Hessian entries.
- **Only local guarantees.** Far from a minimizer, a pure Newton step can overshoot or diverge.
- **Wrong curvature.** If $\mathbf{H}$ is singular the step is undefined; if it is indefinite, the step $-\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$ may be an ascent direction or lead to a saddle point, because Newton's method seeks any stationary point.
- **Singular minimizers.** For $f(x) = x^4$ the step is $x \leftarrow x - 4x^3/(12x^2) = \tfrac23 x$: only linear convergence, because $f''(0) = 0$.

The standard remedies are **damped Newton** ($\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + t\mathbf{d}$ with $t$ from a backtracking line search) and **regularization** (solving $(\mathbf{H} + \lambda\mathbf{I})\mathbf{d} = -\nabla f$ with $\lambda$ large enough to make the matrix positive definite).

### Quasi-Newton methods

**Quasi-Newton** methods keep an approximation $\mathbf{B}_k \approx \mathbf{H}$ (or of its inverse) built only from gradients. After a step $\mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{x}_{k+1} - \mathbf{x}_k$ with gradient change $\mathbf{y}_k = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)$, the Hessian satisfies $\mathbf{y}_k \approx \mathbf{H}\mathbf{s}_k$, so we require the **secant equation** $\mathbf{B}_{k+1}\mathbf{s}_k = \mathbf{y}_k$. The **BFGS** update (Broyden, Fletcher, Goldfarb and Shanno, 1970), in terms of the inverse approximation $\mathbf{M}_k \approx \mathbf{H}^{-1}$, is
$$\mathbf{M}_{k+1} = \big(\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{y}^\top\big)\mathbf{M}_k\big(\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{y}\mathbf{s}^\top\big) + \rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^\top, \qquad \rho = \frac{1}{\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{s}},$$
and it keeps $\mathbf{M}$ symmetric positive definite as long as the curvature condition $\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{s} > 0$ holds (a line search satisfying the Wolfe conditions ensures it). The step is $\mathbf{d}_k = -\mathbf{M}_k\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)$ followed by a line search. Under standard assumptions BFGS converges superlinearly near a minimizer, faster than gradient descent without second derivatives. It still needs $O(n^2)$ memory, so **L-BFGS** (Liu and Nocedal, 1989) stores only the last $m$ pairs $(\mathbf{s}_i, \mathbf{y}_i)$ (typically $m$ between 5 and 20) and applies $\mathbf{M}_k$ implicitly, using $O(mn)$ memory. L-BFGS is a standard choice for medium-sized smooth problems such as logistic regression; for deep networks, noisy mini-batch gradients make first-order methods the norm.

### In code

```python
def newton_2d(grad, hess, x0, steps):
    x = list(x0)
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = grad(x)
        (a, b), (c, d) = hess(x)
        det = a * d - b * c
        step = [(d * g[0] - b * g[1]) / det, (-c * g[0] + a * g[1]) / det]
        x = [x[0] - step[0], x[1] - step[1]]
    return x
```

The two lines computing `step` solve the $2\times2$ system $\mathbf{H}\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{g}$ by Cramer's rule; with NumPy one writes `np.linalg.solve(H, g)`.

@example
title: A Newton step in two variables
problem: Minimize $f(x,y) = x^2 + xy + 2y^2 - 3x + y$ by one Newton step from $(5, -5)$.
step: The gradient is $\nabla f = (2x + y - 3,\; x + 4y + 1)$; at $(5,-5)$ it is $(2, -14)$.
step: The Hessian is the constant matrix $\mathbf{H} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 1 & 4 \end{pmatrix}$ with $\det\mathbf{H} = 7$, positive definite, and $\mathbf{H}^{-1} = \frac17\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: The step is $\mathbf{d} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f = -\tfrac17(4\cdot 2 + 14,\; -2 - 28) = (-22/7,\; 30/7)$.
step: The new point is $(5 - 22/7,\; -5 + 30/7) = (13/7, -5/7) \approx (1.857, -0.714)$. The gradient there is $(26/7 - 5/7 - 3,\; 13/7 - 20/7 + 1) = (0, 0)$, so we are at the minimizer after one step.
answer: $(13/7, -5/7)$, the exact minimizer, since $f$ is a convex quadratic.

@example
title: A BFGS update and the secant equation
problem: Starting with $\mathbf{M}_0 = \mathbf{I}$, a step $\mathbf{s} = (1,1)^\top$ produced a gradient change $\mathbf{y} = (3,2)^\top$. Compute the BFGS inverse update $\mathbf{M}_1$ and verify the secant equation $\mathbf{M}_1\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{s}$.
step: The curvature $\mathbf{y}^\top\mathbf{s} = 5 > 0$, so $\rho = 0.2$ and the update is allowed.
step: Compute $\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{y}^\top = \begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & -0.4 \\ -0.6 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}$, since $\mathbf{s}\mathbf{y}^\top$ has both rows equal to $(3, 2)$. The other factor $\mathbf{I} - \rho\,\mathbf{y}\mathbf{s}^\top$ is its transpose.
step: Because $\mathbf{M}_0 = \mathbf{I}$, the product is $\begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & -0.4 \\ -0.6 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & -0.6 \\ -0.4 & 0.6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.32 & -0.48 \\ -0.48 & 0.72 \end{pmatrix}$, and $\rho\,\mathbf{s}\mathbf{s}^\top$ adds $0.2$ to every entry: $\mathbf{M}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.52 & -0.28 \\ -0.28 & 0.92 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: Check the secant equation: $\mathbf{M}_1\mathbf{y} = (0.52\cdot 3 - 0.28\cdot 2,\; -0.28\cdot 3 + 0.92\cdot 2) = (1.00, 1.00) = \mathbf{s}$.
answer: $\mathbf{M}_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0.52 & -0.28 \\ -0.28 & 0.92 \end{pmatrix}$, and it satisfies $\mathbf{M}_1\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{s}$.

@mistakes
- Calling the Newton step $\mathbf{H}\nabla f$ instead of $-\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$: the inverse curvature rescales the gradient, and the minus sign makes it a descent step when $\mathbf{H}$ is positive definite.
- Assuming Newton's method always converges quickly: the guarantee is local, needs $\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}^\star)$ positive definite, and fails or needs damping far from the solution.
- Forgetting that Newton seeks a stationary point: where the Hessian is indefinite it can walk toward a saddle point or a maximum.
- Computing the matrix inverse explicitly: solving the linear system is cheaper and more accurate.

model:
```python
def regularized_newton_2d(grad, hess, x0, steps, lam):
    """Newton's method with H + lam * I, solving the 2x2 system directly."""
    x = list(x0)
    for _ in range(steps):
        g = grad(x)
        (a, b), (c, d) = hess(x)
        a += lam
        d += lam
        det = a * d - b * c
        step = [(d * g[0] - b * g[1]) / det,
                (-c * g[0] + a * g[1]) / det]
        x = [x[0] - step[0], x[1] - step[1]]
    return x
```
hint: Add `lam` to the two diagonal entries of the Hessian, then for $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{g}$ with $\mathbf{M} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$ Cramer's rule gives $d_1 = (d g_1 - b g_2)/\det$ and $d_2 = (-c g_1 + a g_2)/\det$; then subtract $\mathbf{d}$.
solution: Each iteration evaluates the gradient and Hessian at the current point, adds `lam` to the diagonal only, solves the shifted system with the explicit $2\times 2$ formula, and subtracts the step. With `lam = 0` this is Newton's method, and on a quadratic the first step lands on the minimizer; with `lam > 0` the step is shorter and no longer exact. In the last test the Hessian has eigenvalues $-1$ and $2$, and adding $3$ makes both positive. A version that adds `lam` to every entry, or adds the step instead of subtracting it, fails.

@quiz self
prompt: Derive Newton's method from the second-order Taylor model of $f$, then explain two reasons why it is rarely used directly to train large neural networks, and what a practitioner does instead.
hint: Minimize the quadratic model over the step $\mathbf{d}$, then think about the size of $\mathbf{H}$ and about what happens when $\mathbf{H}$ is not positive definite or the gradient is a noisy mini-batch estimate.
solution: Replace $f(\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{d})$ by $m(\mathbf{d}) = f + \nabla f^\top\mathbf{d} + \tfrac12\mathbf{d}^\top\mathbf{H}\mathbf{d}$. Its gradient in $\mathbf{d}$ is $\nabla f + \mathbf{H}\mathbf{d}$, which vanishes at $\mathbf{d} = -\mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$; if $\mathbf{H}$ is positive definite this is the unique minimizer of $m$, giving the update $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{H}^{-1}\nabla f$. First, scale: with $n$ parameters, $\mathbf{H}$ has $n^2$ entries and solving costs $O(n^3)$, impossible for millions of parameters. Second, the loss is non-convex, so $\mathbf{H}$ is indefinite in many places and the Newton step can go uphill or head for a saddle point; in addition, mini-batch noise in $\nabla f$ and $\mathbf{H}$ makes the exact solve of a noisy model pointless. Practitioners use first-order methods (SGD with momentum, Adam), which scale linearly in $n$; quasi-Newton methods with limited memory (L-BFGS) for smaller deterministic problems; or approximations to curvature such as damped or diagonal Hessian estimates.
