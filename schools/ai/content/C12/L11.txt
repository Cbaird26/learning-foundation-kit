@article
A hiker wants the lowest point on a trail that winds across a hillside. Along the trail the hiker is at a minimum when the trail runs along a contour line of the hill: if the trail cut across the contours, walking one way would go downhill. That tangency is the geometric heart of Lagrange multipliers.

### Equality constraints

Consider $\min f(\mathbf{x})$ subject to $h(\mathbf{x}) = 0$. Take $f(x,y) = x^2 + y^2$ and the line $h(x,y) = x + y - 2 = 0$. The level sets of $f$ are circles around the origin; the smallest circle that touches the line touches it at $(1,1)$, where the circle and the line are tangent. At a tangency, the gradients of $f$ and $h$ are parallel: $\nabla f = (2,2)$ and $\nabla h = (1,1)$, so $\nabla f = -\lambda\nabla h$ with $\lambda = -2$.

In general, define the **Lagrangian** $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x}, \lambda) = f(\mathbf{x}) + \lambda\,h(\mathbf{x})$. If $\mathbf{x}^\star$ is a local minimizer, $h$ and $f$ are continuously differentiable, and $\nabla h(\mathbf{x}^\star) \neq \mathbf{0}$ (more generally, the gradients of the constraints are linearly independent there), then there is a multiplier $\lambda^\star$ with
$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathcal{L} = \nabla f(\mathbf{x}^\star) + \lambda^\star\nabla h(\mathbf{x}^\star) = \mathbf{0}, \qquad h(\mathbf{x}^\star) = 0.$$
These are $n + 1$ equations in the $n + 1$ unknowns $(\mathbf{x}, \lambda)$. They are necessary, not sufficient: they also hold at maxima and saddle points of the constrained problem, so one compares the solutions (and uses compactness, or convexity, to know a minimum exists). With several constraints $h_j = 0$, add one multiplier each.

### Inequality constraints and the KKT conditions

Now let the problem be
$$\min_{\mathbf{x}} f(\mathbf{x}) \quad\text{subject to}\quad g_i(\mathbf{x}) \le 0\ (i = 1..m),\quad h_j(\mathbf{x}) = 0\ (j = 1..p),$$
with Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = f + \sum_i\mu_i g_i + \sum_j\lambda_j h_j$. The **Karush–Kuhn–Tucker (KKT) conditions** at $(\mathbf{x}^\star, \boldsymbol{\mu}^\star, \boldsymbol{\lambda}^\star)$ are:

1. **Stationarity:** $\nabla f + \sum_i\mu_i\nabla g_i + \sum_j\lambda_j\nabla h_j = \mathbf{0}$.
2. **Primal feasibility:** $g_i(\mathbf{x}^\star) \le 0$ and $h_j(\mathbf{x}^\star) = 0$.
3. **Dual feasibility:** $\mu_i \ge 0$ for all $i$.
4. **Complementary slackness:** $\mu_i\,g_i(\mathbf{x}^\star) = 0$ for all $i$.

Complementary slackness says that each constraint is either **active** ($g_i = 0$) or has a zero multiplier: a constraint that is not touching the solution exerts no force. The sign condition $\mu_i \ge 0$ says the constraint pushes the solution back into the feasible region, in the direction opposite to $-\nabla f$.

**Necessity.** If $\mathbf{x}^\star$ is a local minimizer and a **constraint qualification** holds, such as LICQ (the gradients of the active inequality constraints and of all equality constraints are linearly independent at $\mathbf{x}^\star$), then multipliers satisfying the KKT conditions exist. Without a qualification they may not: for $\min x$ subject to $x^2 \le 0$ the only feasible point is $0$, which is the minimizer, but $\nabla f = 1$ while $\nabla g(0) = 0$, so stationarity $1 + \mu\cdot 0 = 0$ has no solution.

**Sufficiency.** If the problem is **convex** ($f$ and every $g_i$ convex, every $h_j$ affine), any point satisfying the KKT conditions is a global minimizer. (This follows because $\mathcal{L}(\cdot, \boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\lambda})$ is convex and stationary there.) In non-convex problems KKT points need further checking.

### Solving by cases

To solve a small problem with one inequality, try the two cases of complementary slackness. **Inactive:** $\mu = 0$, solve the unconstrained problem, and check feasibility. **Active:** $g = 0$, solve the stationarity equations together with $g = 0$, and check $\mu \ge 0$. For $\min (x - 2)^2 + (y - 1)^2$ subject to $x + y \le 2$ the unconstrained minimizer $(2,1)$ has $x + y = 3 > 2$, infeasible. The active case gives $2(x - 2) + \mu = 0$ and $2(y - 1) + \mu = 0$, so $x = 2 - \mu/2$, $y = 1 - \mu/2$, and $x + y = 2$ forces $\mu = 1 \ge 0$, with $(x,y) = (1.5, 0.5)$ and $f = 0.5$.

### The multiplier as a price

Relax the constraint to $x + y \le b$. The optimal value is $p^\star(b) = (3 - b)^2/2$ for $b \le 3$, the squared distance from $(2,1)$ to the line $x + y = b$ (a line at distance $(3-b)/\sqrt2$). Its derivative at $b = 2$ is $-1 = -\mu^\star$. In general, the multiplier measures how fast the optimal value falls as the constraint is relaxed: a large multiplier marks a constraint that is expensive to keep. In machine learning, the support vector machine's margin problem is a constrained problem of this kind, and its multipliers are nonzero exactly for the support vectors.

### Checking numerically

A hand solution is easy to test: evaluate $f$ at the claimed point and at nearby feasible points (for the line $x + y = 2$, the points $(1 + t, 1 - t)$ for small $t$ of either sign) and confirm that $f$ does not decrease. Along that line $f = (1+t)^2 + (1-t)^2 = 2 + 2t^2$, which is minimal at $t = 0$.

@example
title: An equality-constrained problem
problem: Minimize $x^2 + 2y^2$ subject to $x + y = 3$.
step: Write the Lagrangian $\mathcal{L} = x^2 + 2y^2 + \lambda(x + y - 3)$.
step: Stationarity: $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial x = 2x + \lambda = 0$ and $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial y = 4y + \lambda = 0$, so $x = -\lambda/2$ and $y = -\lambda/4$.
step: Substitute into the constraint: $-\lambda/2 - \lambda/4 = 3$, so $\lambda = -4$, giving $x = 2$ and $y = 1$.
step: The value is $f = 4 + 2 = 6$. Check by substitution: along the constraint $x = 3 - y$, $f(y) = (3 - y)^2 + 2y^2 = 3y^2 - 6y + 9$, which is minimized at $y = 1$ with value $6$. The objective is convex, so this is the global minimum.
answer: The minimizer is $(2, 1)$ with value $6$ and multiplier $\lambda = -4$.

@example
title: Deciding which constraint is active
problem: Minimize $(x - 1)^2 + (y - 1)^2$ subject to $x \le 0.5$ and $y \le 3$. Find the minimizer and the multipliers.
step: Write the constraints as $g_1 = x - 0.5 \le 0$ and $g_2 = y - 3 \le 0$. The unconstrained minimizer $(1,1)$ violates $g_1$ but satisfies $g_2$ with room to spare.
step: By complementary slackness, guess $g_2$ inactive, so $\mu_2 = 0$, and $g_1$ active, so $x = 0.5$.
step: Stationarity in $x$: $2(x - 1) + \mu_1 = 0$ gives $\mu_1 = 2(1 - 0.5) = 1 \ge 0$. Stationarity in $y$: $2(y - 1) = 0$ gives $y = 1$.
step: Check all conditions: $g_1(0.5, 1) = 0$, $g_2 = -2 < 0$, $\mu_1 = 1 \ge 0$, $\mu_2 = 0$. All KKT conditions hold, and the problem is convex, so $(0.5, 1)$ is the global minimizer with value $0.25$.
answer: The minimizer is $(0.5, 1)$ with $\mu_1 = 1$, $\mu_2 = 0$ and value $0.25$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the sign condition: for $g_i \le 0$ constraints the multipliers must satisfy $\mu_i \ge 0$; a negative $\mu_i$ means the guessed active constraint is wrong.
- Treating the Lagrange conditions as sufficient: they only find candidates, which may be maxima or saddle points of the constrained problem.
- Marking a constraint active because it looks tight, without checking: use complementary slackness and test both cases.
- Applying the KKT conditions as necessary without a constraint qualification: the example $\min x$ with $x^2 \le 0$ has a minimizer but no multipliers.

@practice self
prompt: Find the minimum and maximum of $f(x,y) = x + 2y$ on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 5$. Use Lagrange multipliers, say why a minimum and a maximum exist, and check your answer by an independent method.
hint: Stationarity gives $(1, 2) + \lambda(2x, 2y) = \mathbf{0}$; express $x$ and $y$ through $\lambda$ and substitute into the constraint to get two values of $\lambda$.
solution: The circle is closed and bounded and $f$ is continuous, so $f$ attains a minimum and a maximum on it. With $\mathcal{L} = x + 2y + \lambda(x^2 + y^2 - 5)$, stationarity gives $1 + 2\lambda x = 0$ and $2 + 2\lambda y = 0$, so $x = -1/(2\lambda)$ and $y = -1/\lambda$. Then $x^2 + y^2 = 5/(4\lambda^2) = 5$ gives $\lambda = \pm\tfrac12$. For $\lambda = \tfrac12$: $(x,y) = (-1,-2)$ and $f = -5$. For $\lambda = -\tfrac12$: $(x,y) = (1,2)$ and $f = 5$. So the minimum is $-5$ at $(-1,-2)$ and the maximum is $5$ at $(1,2)$. Independent check: by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, $x + 2y \le \sqrt{1 + 4}\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} = \sqrt5\sqrt5 = 5$, with equality when $(x,y)$ is parallel to $(1,2)$; the same bound gives the minimum $-5$.
