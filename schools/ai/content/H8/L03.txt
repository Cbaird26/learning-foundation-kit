@article
The chemistry of an element is controlled by its electrons, and electrons are arranged in **shells**, **subshells** and **orbitals**. The periodic table is a map of that arrangement.

### Quantum numbers and subshells

Electrons are described by the principal quantum number $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ (the shell). Each shell contains subshells $s, p, d, f$ with $1, 3, 5, 7$ orbitals, and each orbital holds at most two electrons. So a subshell holds $2, 6, 10, 14$ electrons, and shell $n$ holds at most $2n^2$ electrons.

### Rules for filling

- **Aufbau principle:** fill the lowest-energy subshell first. The usual order is $1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, \ldots$.
- **Pauli exclusion principle:** an orbital holds at most two electrons, and they have opposite spins.
- **Hund's rule:** in a subshell with several orbitals, place one electron in each orbital (same spin) before pairing any.

Oxygen ($Z=8$) is $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^4$: the four $2p$ electrons occupy the three $p$ orbitals as one pair and two unpaired electrons. Nitrogen, $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$, has three unpaired electrons.

### Shorthand and ions

A **noble-gas configuration** replaces the inner electrons with the preceding noble gas in brackets: iron is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^6$. Two common exceptions are chromium, $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1\,3d^5$, and copper, $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1\,3d^{10}$, because half-filled and filled $d$ subshells are especially stable.

To form a cation, remove electrons from the **highest $n$ first**, even though $4s$ was filled before $3d$. Thus $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6$, not $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^4$. Anions simply add electrons: $\text{Cl}^{-}$ is $[\text{Ne}]\,3s^2\,3p^6=[\text{Ar}]$.

### Periodic table structure

Elements in a **group** (column) have the same number of **valence electrons**, the electrons in the outermost shell, and so similar chemistry. For main-group elements the group number (1, 2, 13–18) tells the valence count: $1, 2$, then $3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8$. Blocks follow the last subshell filled: $s$-block (groups 1 and 2), $p$-block (groups 13–18), $d$-block (transition metals) and $f$-block.

### Trends

**Effective nuclear charge** is the pull felt by valence electrons after core electrons shield some of the nuclear charge. Across a period it rises, while the shell number stays fixed. Down a group, new shells are added.

- **Atomic radius** decreases left to right across a period and increases down a group.
- **First ionization energy** (energy to remove one electron from a gaseous atom) generally increases across a period and decreases down a group.
- **Electronegativity** and **electron affinity** also generally increase toward the upper right, excluding the noble gases.

Small irregularities exist, for example the drop in ionization energy from nitrogen to oxygen caused by electron pairing in a $p$ orbital.

@example
title: Configuration and unpaired electrons of a transition-metal ion
problem: Write the electron configuration of $\text{Co}^{2+}$ (cobalt, $Z=27$) and state how many unpaired electrons it has.
step: Cobalt has $27$ electrons. Fill in order: $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^6\,3s^2\,3p^6\,4s^2\,3d^7$, which is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2\,3d^7$.
step: For the $2+$ cation, remove electrons from the highest shell first: both $4s$ electrons. The ion is $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^7$ with $25$ electrons.
step: Distribute seven $d$ electrons over five orbitals by Hund's rule: put five unpaired first, then pair two of them. That leaves $3$ unpaired electrons.

@mistakes
- Removing $3d$ electrons before $4s$ electrons when forming transition-metal cations.
- Writing more than two electrons in an orbital, or pairing electrons before each orbital in the subshell has one.
- Saying atoms get larger across a period because there are more electrons; the increasing nuclear charge pulls the same shell closer.
- Using group number as the valence count for transition metals.

@practice num
prompt: How many unpaired electrons are in a ground-state nitrogen atom ($Z=7$)?
answer: 3
check: 7 - 2 - 2
hint: The configuration is $1s^2\,2s^2\,2p^3$.
hint: By Hund's rule, the three $2p$ electrons go in separate orbitals.
solution: The $1s$ and $2s$ electrons are paired. The three $2p$ electrons occupy three different $p$ orbitals with parallel spins, so there are $3$ unpaired electrons.

@quiz num
prompt: What is the maximum number of electrons that can occupy the $n=4$ shell?
answer: 32
check: 2 * 4**2
hint: A shell $n$ contains $n^2$ orbitals.
hint: Each orbital holds two electrons.
solution: The $n=4$ shell contains $4^2=16$ orbitals and holds $2\times16=32$ electrons.
