@article
A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture. The **solvent** is the component present in the largest amount (water, for an **aqueous** solution), and the **solute** is dissolved in it. Concentration says how much solute is in a given amount of solution or solvent.

### Molarity

**Molarity** is the most common concentration unit in the laboratory:

$$M=\frac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{liters of solution}}.$$

Notice that the denominator is the volume of the *solution*, not the solvent. A $1.00\ \text{M}$ NaCl solution is made by dissolving $1.00\ \text{mol}$ ($58.44\ \text{g}$) of NaCl in some water and then adding water until the **total** volume is $1.00\ \text{L}$. Rearranged, $n=MV$ and $m=MVM_{\text{molar}}$ give the moles and grams of solute in any volume.

### Other units

- **Mass percent:** $\dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}}\times100$.
- **Parts per million (ppm):** $\dfrac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}}\times10^6$; in dilute water solutions, $1\ \text{ppm}\approx1\ \text{mg/L}$.
- **Molality** $m$: $\dfrac{\text{moles of solute}}{\text{kilograms of solvent}}$. It does not change with temperature, unlike molarity.
- **Mole fraction:** moles of a component divided by total moles.

### Dilution

When you add solvent to a solution, the number of moles of solute does not change. Since $n=MV$,

$$M_1V_1=M_2V_2,$$

where $1$ refers to the concentrated **stock** and $2$ to the diluted solution. The volumes may be in any unit if they match. The needed stock volume is $V_1=\dfrac{M_2V_2}{M_1}$.

### A dilution plan

A complete plan states quantities and the procedure:

1. Calculate $V_1$ of stock needed.
2. Choose a **volumetric flask** of volume $V_2$ (precise glassware for the final volume).
3. Add some water to the flask, then add the measured stock with a pipet or graduated cylinder. For strong acids, always add acid to water, never water to acid, and wear eye protection.
4. Mix, then fill to the calibration mark with solvent and mix again.

The volume of water added is $V_2-V_1$ only when volumes are additive, so you fill to the mark rather than adding a calculated volume of water. Report $V_1$ to the precision your glassware allows and note the number of significant figures.

### Solubility and electrolytes

Ionic compounds that dissolve in water **dissociate** into ions, which makes the solution conduct electricity (an **electrolyte**). One mole of $\text{CaCl}_2$ gives $1\ \text{mol Ca}^{2+}$ and $2\ \text{mol Cl}^{-}$, so $0.10\ \text{M}$ $\text{CaCl}_2$ is $0.20\ \text{M}$ in chloride. Solubility depends on temperature; most solids dissolve better when warmed, while gases dissolve less.

@example
title: Preparing a dilution
problem: How much of a $5.0\ \text{M}$ stock solution is needed to prepare $100.0\ \text{mL}$ of $0.50\ \text{M}$ solution? Describe the preparation.
step: The moles of solute are conserved, so $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$.
step: Solve for the stock volume: $V_1=\dfrac{M_2V_2}{M_1}=\dfrac{(0.50)(100.0)}{5.0}=10\ \text{mL}$.
step: Procedure: put some water in a $100.0\ \text{mL}$ volumetric flask, add $10\ \text{mL}$ of stock, then fill to the mark with water and mix.

@example
title: Molarity from mass
problem: A student dissolves $6.00\ \text{g}$ of NaOH ($M=40.00\ \text{g/mol}$) in water and makes the volume up to $500.0\ \text{mL}$. Find the molarity.
step: Convert mass to moles: $\dfrac{6.00\ \text{g}}{40.00\ \text{g/mol}}=0.150\ \text{mol}$.
step: Convert the volume to liters: $500.0\ \text{mL}=0.5000\ \text{L}$.
step: Divide: $M=\dfrac{0.150\ \text{mol}}{0.5000\ \text{L}}=0.300\ \text{M}$.

@mistakes
- Dividing by the volume of solvent instead of the volume of the final solution.
- Forgetting to convert mL to L when computing molarity.
- Using $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$ with the stock and diluted values swapped.
- Adding "the calculated volume of water" instead of filling to the mark, so the final volume is wrong.

@practice num
prompt: A solution is made by dissolving $11.7\ \text{g}$ of NaCl ($M=58.44\ \text{g/mol}$) in water to a total volume of $500.0\ \text{mL}$. What is its molarity?
answer: 0.400
check: 11.7 / 58.44 / 0.500
tol: 1%
unit: M
hint: Convert grams to moles, and milliliters to liters.
hint: $n=11.7/58.44\approx0.200\ \text{mol}$.
solution: $n=0.2002\ \text{mol}$ and $V=0.5000\ \text{L}$, so $M=0.2002/0.5000=0.400\ \text{M}$.
