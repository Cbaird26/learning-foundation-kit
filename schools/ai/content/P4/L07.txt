@article
An image of a panda is classified correctly. Add a perturbation whose every pixel changes by less than a quantization step, invisible to a person, and the same network confidently reports a different class. Szegedy and colleagues (2014) reported exactly this for image classifiers, and called such inputs **adversarial examples**. Why would a function that generalizes well be so brittle in directions nobody looks at?

### A linear explanation

Goodfellow, Shlens and Szegedy (2015) argued that high-dimensional linear behavior is enough. Take a linear score $z = \mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{x} + b$ and perturb the input by $\boldsymbol{\delta}$ with $\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_\infty \le \varepsilon$ (every coordinate moves by at most $\varepsilon$). The score changes by $\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\delta}$, which is largest when each $\delta_i = \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}(w_i)$:

$$\max_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_\infty \le \varepsilon} \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\delta} = \varepsilon \lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1 .$$

The $L_1$ norm grows with the dimension $d$: with $d = 1000$ weights of size $0.01$ and $\varepsilon = 0.1$, no coordinate moves by more than $0.1$, yet the score moves by $1000 \times 0.01 \times 0.1 = 1.0$. Many tiny aligned changes add up.

### The fast gradient sign method

The **fast gradient sign method** (FGSM) takes one step in the direction that most increases the loss under the $L_\infty$ budget: $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} + \varepsilon\,\operatorname{sign}\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}} \ell\big)$. For a linear classifier with logistic loss $\ell = \log(1 + e^{-yz})$ and label $y \in \{-1, +1\}$, the gradient is $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\ell = -y\,\sigma(-yz)\,\mathbf{w}$. The scalar $\sigma(-yz)$ is positive and does not change the sign, so $\mathbf{x}' = \mathbf{x} - \varepsilon\, y \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w})$, and the margin $yz$ falls by exactly $\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$.

```python
def sign(v):
    return (v > 0) - (v < 0)

def fgsm(w, b, x, y, eps):
    """FGSM step on a linear classifier with logistic loss, y in {-1, +1}."""
    return [xi - eps * y * sign(wi) for xi, wi in zip(x, w)]

def score(w, b, x):
    return sum(wi * xi for wi, xi in zip(w, x)) + b
```

With $\mathbf{w} = (3, -2, 1)$, $b = -1$, $\mathbf{x} = (1, 0.5, 2)$ and $y = +1$ the score is $3$ and $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1 = 6$, so after the step the score is $3 - 6\varepsilon$: $0.6$ at $\varepsilon = 0.4$ and $-0.6$ at $\varepsilon = 0.6$. The prediction flips for $\varepsilon > 0.5$. For deep networks the same step uses the gradient from backpropagation; the linear picture explains why a first-order step is effective, not why every network behaves linearly.

### Adversarial training and its limits

Madry and colleagues (2018) formulated robust training as a **min-max** problem,

$$\min_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ \mathbb{E}_{(\mathbf{x}, y)}\Big[\max_{\lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert_\infty \le \varepsilon} \ell\big(f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x} + \boldsymbol{\delta}), y\big)\Big],$$

and approximated the inner maximization with several steps of **projected gradient descent** (PGD): repeat $\boldsymbol{\delta} \leftarrow \Pi_{\varepsilon}\big(\boldsymbol{\delta} + \alpha\,\operatorname{sign}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\delta}}\ell)\big)$, projecting back into the $\varepsilon$-ball. For a linear model the inner maximum is exact: the worst case is the loss at margin $yz - \varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$, which shows that the $L_\infty$ robust training of a linear model penalizes the $L_1$ norm of the weights. Adversarial training reliably raises robustness to the attack it uses, at a cost in clean accuracy and compute. Whether the accuracy cost is inevitable or a limitation of current methods and data is debated.

### Defenses that were not defenses

Athalye, Carlini and Wagner (2018) showed that many published defenses worked by **obfuscated gradients**: they made gradients uninformative (non-differentiable preprocessing, randomness, vanishing or exploding gradients), so that gradient-based attacks failed although the model remained vulnerable. Attacks adapted to the defense (approximating the non-differentiable part on the backward pass, or averaging over the defense's randomness) broke them. The methodological lesson is general: report evaluation against **adaptive attacks** that know the defense, check that attack strength increases with budget, and treat a gradient attack's failure as weak evidence.

### Certified robustness

A **certificate** guarantees that no perturbation within a budget changes the prediction. Cohen, Rosenfeld and Kolter (2019) showed that a classifier that returns the most likely class of a base classifier under Gaussian noise $\mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2\mathbf{I})$, called **randomized smoothing**, is provably constant within an $L_2$ ball. If the top class has probability at least $p_A > 1/2$ under the noise, the smoothed prediction cannot change within radius

$$R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p_A),$$

where $\Phi^{-1}$ is the standard normal quantile function (with the runner-up probability bounded by $1 - p_A$). The probabilities are estimated by sampling, so the guarantee holds with a stated confidence. The price is a smoothed classifier that must be trained to tolerate noise, and radii that are small in high dimensions.

### Open questions

Researchers disagree on whether robustness is mainly a security problem for deployed systems with adversaries, or also a window into how models represent data. One view is that adversarial examples reflect predictive but brittle features that humans do not use; another that they reflect model limitations that more data and better architectures will remove. Threat models also matter: an $L_p$ ball is a convenient proxy, and robustness to one budget does not transfer to others. Whether robustness research is central to AI alignment or a separate problem is itself disputed.

@example
title: Smallest flip budget and a certified radius
problem: A linear classifier has $\mathbf{w} = (2, -1, 3)$, $b = -4$, and the input $\mathbf{x} = (2, 1, 1.5)$ has label $y = +1$. (a) Find the smallest $\varepsilon$ at which an $L_\infty$ FGSM step flips the prediction. (b) A smoothed classifier with $\sigma = 0.5$ has $\Phi^{-1}(p_A) = 1.2816$ (that is $p_A = 0.9$) on some input. What is its certified $L_2$ radius?
step: Score: $z = 2\cdot 2 - 1\cdot 1 + 3 \cdot 1.5 - 4 = 3.5$, so the margin is $yz = 3.5$.
step: The FGSM step lowers the margin by $\varepsilon\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1 = 6\varepsilon$, since $\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1 = 2 + 1 + 3 = 6$.
step: The margin reaches 0 at $\varepsilon = 3.5/6 \approx 0.583$, so any larger budget flips the prediction (and a smaller one does not, for this direction).
step: Radius: $R = \sigma\,\Phi^{-1}(p_A) = 0.5 \times 1.2816 = 0.641$. Note the different norms: the first is an $L_\infty$ budget on each coordinate, the second an $L_2$ ball, and the two are not comparable without converting between them.
answer: (a) $\varepsilon \approx 0.583$; (b) $R \approx 0.641$.

@mistakes
- Comparing budgets in different norms: an $L_\infty$ budget of $\varepsilon$ allows an $L_2$ perturbation of size up to $\varepsilon\sqrt{d}$, so robustness numbers are meaningful only with their threat model.
- Concluding a defense works because a gradient attack failed: obfuscated gradients can defeat gradient attacks without making the model robust, so adaptive attacks are needed.
- Getting the sign of the FGSM step wrong: the step increases the loss, which moves the input against the label, $-\varepsilon\, y \operatorname{sign}(\mathbf{w})$ for a linear model.
- Treating a randomized-smoothing radius as a deterministic guarantee for the base classifier: it certifies the smoothed classifier, with a probability estimated from samples.

model:
```python
def fgsm_clipped(w, b, x, y, eps, lo, hi):
    """FGSM step for a linear logistic classifier, clipped to [lo, hi]."""
    out = []
    for wi, xi in zip(w, x):
        s = (wi > 0) - (wi < 0)
        out.append(min(hi, max(lo, xi - eps * y * s)))
    return out
```
hint: For each coordinate compute the signed step, add it to the coordinate, then clamp with `min` and `max`; a zero weight must not move.
solution: The gradient sign of the logistic loss for a linear model is $-y\,\operatorname{sign}(w_i)$, so each coordinate moves by $-\varepsilon y\,\operatorname{sign}(w_i)$ before clipping. The tests check both labels, the zero-weight coordinate that must stay put, clipping at both ends, and that the input list is not changed.

@quiz self
prompt: Give the strongest case that adversarial robustness research is central to AI safety, and the strongest case that it is a separate problem that should not be confused with alignment. Then say which empirical finding would most change your view.
hint: Compare what each field asks: whether the objective is right versus whether the input-output map is stable.
solution: The case for centrality: systems that can be steered by small, targeted input changes cannot be trusted to follow their intended objectives in adversarial settings, and aligned behavior that can be removed by a crafted prompt or perturbation is not reliable alignment; robustness is also a precondition for oversight tools, since a reward model or a monitor that can be fooled can be exploited by the policy it trains or oversees (week 9). The case for separation: alignment concerns whether a system's objectives match human intent, which is a question about what the system tries to do; a perfectly robust classifier can still pursue a wrong objective, and a misaligned system need not be non-robust. Threat models also differ: robustness research mostly studies small perturbations under defined norms, while alignment failures involve specification and generalization, and conflating them can lead to progress on metrics that do not bear on the other problem. A fair synthesis is that they overlap where an adversary or an optimizing policy searches for inputs that exploit a learned component. The finding that would most change my view: evidence on whether robustness training of reward models or monitors measurably reduces reward hacking or successful circumvention, which would support centrality; the absence of any such effect would favor separation.
