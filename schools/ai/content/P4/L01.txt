@article
A radiology model flags a chest X-ray, and a heat map lights up the lung. A clinician is reassured. Later, an audit finds that the model also lights up the same way when the lung is covered and a hospital marker in the corner is left visible. What did the heat map tell you? This week's question is how to know whether an explanation describes the model or only flatters your expectations.

### Three kinds of question

Researchers use "interpretability" for several different goals, and the methods differ because the questions differ.

| question | example | typical methods |
|---|---|---|
| **Attribution**: which inputs does this output depend on? | Which words drove this classification? | gradients, occlusion, integrated gradients, Shapley values (week 2) |
| **Representation**: what information is present inside the model? | Can part of speech be read out of layer 6? | probing (week 3) |
| **Mechanism**: what computation turns inputs into outputs? | Which attention heads and layers implement this behavior? | circuits, activation patching, sparse dictionaries (weeks 4 to 6) |

The same behavior can be studied at all three levels, and an answer at one level does not settle the others. An input may strongly affect the output without the model computing anything you would call sensible with it, and a property may be decodable from activations that the model never uses.

### A taxonomy of methods

Methods are usually classified along a few axes, as in Molnar's book. **Intrinsic** methods restrict the model class so that the model is itself understandable (a sparse linear model, a short decision list); **post hoc** methods analyze a trained model, whatever its form. A **local** explanation concerns one prediction; a **global** one concerns the model's behavior in general. A **model-agnostic** method needs only the ability to query the model, while a **model-specific** method uses its internals, such as gradients or attention.

### Faithful versus plausible

An explanation is **faithful** if it accurately reflects what the model does, and **plausible** if it is convincing to a human. The two can come apart in either direction. A method that always highlights the most salient-looking object is plausible and may be unfaithful. A faithful explanation of a model that uses a shortcut will look implausible, and that is useful information.

Plausibility is a property of the explanation and the reader. Faithfulness is a property of the explanation and the model, so it can only be tested by acting on the model: intervening on inputs or internal activations and checking that the explanation predicted the result. Two standard tests are **deletion** (remove or replace features in order of attributed importance and watch the output fall) and **insertion** (add them back in that order and watch it recover).

### Occlusion and its limits

The simplest intervention is **occlusion**: replace feature $i$ by a baseline value $x'_i$ and record the change,

$$a_i = f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}_{i \leftarrow x'_i}).$$

For a linear model $f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$ this gives $a_i = w_i (x_i - x'_i)$, and the attributions add up to $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x}')$. For a model with interactions they do not. Take $f(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 x_2$ at $\mathbf{x} = (2, 3)$ with baseline $\mathbf{0}$. Removing either feature sends the output from 6 to 0, so each gets credit 6, and the credits sum to 12 although the output only rose by 6. The product is credited twice because each feature is necessary given the other. Any single-feature intervention has this blind spot, and later methods (integrated gradients, Shapley values) fix the accounting in different ways, each by taking a stand on what the baseline and the counterfactuals should be.

The baseline is itself a modeling choice: a black image, a mean image, a blurred copy or an average over a reference data set answer different questions, and an attribution is only meaningful relative to it.

### Why it matters, and how far it will go

Reasons to want interpretability include debugging (finding shortcuts), scientific insight, accountability in regulated decisions, and, for some researchers, safety: detecting whether a system pursues goals its developers did not intend. Researchers disagree about which of these it can deliver.

The optimistic view holds that networks, however large, are built from reusable parts that can be understood one at a time, and that early results on small models and specific behaviors point to a program that can scale with automation and better tools. The skeptical view holds that small-model circuits may not generalize, that human-readable concepts may not be the model's natural units, that explanations are hard to validate without ground truth, and that effort might be better spent on behavioral testing. A third position, associated in the public debate with Rudin (2019), argues that for high-stakes decisions one should use inherently interpretable models instead of explaining black boxes after the fact, since post hoc explanations can be unfaithful and the claimed accuracy gap often disappears on structured data. Defenders of post hoc and mechanistic work reply that for perception and language no interpretable model is known to match the accuracy of large networks, so understanding those models is the only option. Nobody currently knows which side the evidence will favor; treat each method as a tool whose reliability must be measured, and ask of every paper what intervention validated its claim.

@example
title: Occlusion on a model with an interaction
problem: A model computes $f(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 x_2 + 2 x_3$. At $\mathbf{x} = (2, 3, 1)$ with baseline $\mathbf{0}$, compute the occlusion attribution of each feature and compare their sum with $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{0})$.
step: Evaluate the model: $f(2, 3, 1) = 6 + 2 = 8$ and $f(\mathbf{0}) = 0$, so the total to explain is 8.
step: Occlude $x_1$: $f(0, 3, 1) = 0 + 2 = 2$, so $a_1 = 8 - 2 = 6$. Occlude $x_2$: $f(2, 0, 1) = 2$, so $a_2 = 6$.
step: Occlude $x_3$: $f(2, 3, 0) = 6$, so $a_3 = 8 - 6 = 2$.
step: Add them: $6 + 6 + 2 = 14$, but $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{0}) = 8$. The excess of 6 is the product term $x_1 x_2 = 6$ counted twice, once for each of the features that it needs.
answer: $(a_1, a_2, a_3) = (6, 6, 2)$; they sum to 14, not 8.

@mistakes
- Treating a plausible heat map as evidence of faithfulness: only an intervention on the model, such as a deletion test, can show that the explanation predicts the model's behavior.
- Expecting single-feature occlusion scores to sum to the output change: they do so only for additive models, and with interactions features are double-counted or undercounted.
- Reading an attribution without naming the baseline: the same model and input give different attributions under a black-image, mean or blurred baseline.
- Assuming that information decodable from activations, or an input that changes the output, shows the model uses it in the way the explanation suggests.

prompt: A model computes $f(\mathbf{x}) = \max(0, x_1 - x_2) + x_3$. At $\mathbf{x} = (4, 1, 2)$, what is the occlusion attribution of $x_1$ with baseline value 0?
answer: 3
check: (max(0, 4 - 1) + 2) - (max(0, 0 - 1) + 2)
hint: Compute $f(\mathbf{x})$, then compute $f$ with $x_1$ replaced by 0, and subtract.
solution: $f(4, 1, 2) = 3 + 2 = 5$ and $f(0, 1, 2) = \max(0, -1) + 2 = 2$, so $a_1 = 5 - 2 = 3$.

@practice num
prompt: A model computes $f(\mathbf{x}) = x_1 x_2 + x_2 x_3$. At $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)$ with baseline 0 for every feature, the occlusion attributions are computed for the three features. By how much does their sum exceed $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{0})$?
answer: 8
check: sum((1*2 + 2*3) - g for g in [0*2 + 2*3, 1*0 + 0*3, 1*2 + 2*0]) - ((1*2 + 2*3) - 0)
hint: Evaluate $f(\mathbf{x}) = 8$, then occlude each feature in turn and compute the three attributions before adding them.
solution: $f(\mathbf{x}) = 2 + 6 = 8$. Occluding $x_1$ gives 6, so $a_1 = 2$; occluding $x_2$ gives 0, so $a_2 = 8$; occluding $x_3$ gives 2, so $a_3 = 6$. The sum is 16 and $f(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{0}) = 8$, an excess of 8: the middle feature appears in both products and is credited with both.

@practice self
prompt: Make the strongest case that decisions with serious consequences for people (for example, credit or bail decisions on tabular data) should be made with inherently interpretable models rather than with a black-box model plus post hoc explanations. Then give the strongest reply, and say what evidence would move you.
solution: The case: a post hoc explanation is a second model of the first, so it can disagree with it; two different methods can give different explanations for the same prediction, and nothing guarantees either is faithful. On many structured data sets, carefully built sparse or rule-based models match black-box accuracy, so the assumed trade-off between accuracy and interpretability may not exist where the data are tabular; and an interpretable model can be audited, debugged and contested by the person it affects. The reply: for data such as images or text no interpretable model is known to reach the accuracy of large networks, and where accuracy matters for people's welfare (medical imaging) that cost is real; post hoc and mechanistic tools, validated by interventions, give partial audits that are better than none; and "interpretable" is itself not guaranteed, since a long rule list or a linear model with thousands of features is hard to read. Evidence that would move me: head-to-head comparisons of accuracy on the actual task, and user studies measuring whether decision makers detect model errors better with an interpretable model than with a black box plus explanation.

@quiz num
prompt: A linear model is $f(\mathbf{x}) = 2a - 3b + c + 4$ for features $\mathbf{x} = (a, b, c)$. At $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)$ with baseline $\mathbf{x}' = (0, 1, 1)$, what is the occlusion attribution (replacing the feature by its baseline value) of the feature $b$?
answer: -3
check: (2*1 - 3*2 + 3 + 4) - (2*1 - 3*1 + 3 + 4)
hint: For a linear model, the attribution is the weight times the difference between the feature and its baseline.
solution: Replacing $b = 2$ by 1 changes $f$ from $3$ to $6$, so $a_b = 3 - 6 = -3$, which equals $w_b (b - b') = -3 \times 1$.

@quiz self
prompt: Write a referee paragraph for a submission whose only evidence is that a saliency map for an X-ray classifier highlights the tumor, and which concludes that the classifier "has learned to detect tumors". State what the evidence does and does not show and ask for two specific additional experiments.
solution: The figure shows that the chosen method, for these images, assigns high attribution to the tumor region. This is evidence of plausibility, and the paper does not show that the map is faithful to the classifier, nor that the tumor is what the classifier uses: tumors may co-occur with other cues (scanner, annotation markers, patient positioning), and a saliency map highlights where the output is sensitive, not why. I would ask first for an intervention test: replace the tumor region by a baseline (and, separately, replace everything else) and report the change in the output across the test set, with the baseline named and compared against a random-region control. Second, I would ask for a check that the method is sensitive to the model: show that the maps change when the model's weights or training labels are randomized, and that they differ across independently trained models if the claim is general. Cherry-picked examples should be replaced by an aggregate measure over many images. The claim should be weakened to what the data support unless these tests pass.

rubric: Proposes a concrete intervention test with a named baseline and a control.
rubric: Proposes a check of the explanation method itself (randomization or aggregate evidence), not just more examples.
