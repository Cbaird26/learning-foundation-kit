@article
Learning from human feedback needs a human who can tell a good output from a bad one. A reviewer can check a short summary against a short article. Can a reviewer check a 10,000-line program for subtle vulnerabilities, or a proof, or a research claim in a field they do not know? If the system being trained will outperform its evaluators, their feedback becomes unreliable exactly where it matters. **Scalable oversight** is the research area on supervising systems on tasks too hard for the supervisor to evaluate unaided.

### Proposals

**Iterated amplification** (Christiano, Shlegeris and Amodei, 2018): a human, helped by copies of the current model that answer sub-questions, decomposes a hard task into easier ones; the combined system is stronger than the human alone, and the model is then trained to imitate it (distillation). Repeating the cycle is meant to bootstrap supervision. It works if hard tasks decompose into parts that can be checked, and if distillation preserves the properties of the amplified system.

**Recursive reward modeling** (Leike and colleagues, 2018): train agents to help the human evaluate the work of the next, more capable agent, building a chain in which each stage's assistants are trained with the previous stage's methods.

**Debate** (Irving, Christiano and Amodei, 2018): two agents take opposing positions on a question in a zero-sum game and a judge (a human) declares a winner. The hope is that pointing out a flaw is easier than hiding it, so that honest play is the equilibrium and the judge needs to inspect only a small part of a huge argument. The authors argued by analogy with interactive proofs that with optimal play, debate with a polynomial-time judge could in principle answer any question in the complexity class PSPACE, far more than the judge could check alone. Whether human judges and learned debaters behave like the idealization is an empirical question. A known concern is that a dishonest debater might construct arguments whose flaws are too diffuse for the judge to localize.

**AI feedback and Constitutional AI** (Bai and colleagues, 2022): replace some human comparisons by a model's judgments guided by written principles. In the method, a model critiques and revises its own outputs according to principles, which gives fine-tuning data, and a model labels which of two responses better follows the principles, providing preference labels for a reward model (reinforcement learning from AI feedback). This reduces human labor and makes the principles explicit. Open concerns are that AI feedback inherits and may amplify the biases and blind spots of the labeling model, and that who writes the principles, and how, is a substantive question.

**Weak-to-strong generalization** (Burns and colleagues, 2023): as an analogy for humans supervising superhuman models, fine-tune a strong pretrained model on labels produced by a much weaker model, and ask how well the strong model does. They found that strong models trained on weak labels often outperform their supervisors, but typically recover only part of the strong model's potential, and that simple techniques, such as an auxiliary confidence loss, improve recovery on some tasks. The measure is the **performance gap recovered**,

$$\mathrm{PGR} = \frac{\text{acc}_{\text{weak-to-strong}} - \text{acc}_{\text{weak}}}{\text{acc}_{\text{strong ceiling}} - \text{acc}_{\text{weak}}},$$

where the ceiling is the strong model trained on ground-truth labels. PGR $= 0$ means no gain over the supervisor and $1$ means full recovery.

### Measuring progress

Bowman and colleagues (2022) piloted an experimental design called **sandwiching**: choose a task on which a model is more capable than ordinary non-expert participants but less capable than domain experts, who provide ground truth. Test whether non-experts, using a method (an assistant, a debate, a decomposition), can reach expert-level judgments without the experts' labels. The fraction of the gap closed is $(\text{non-expert with method} - \text{non-expert alone})/(\text{expert} - \text{non-expert alone})$. The design lets researchers measure oversight methods on today's systems, before oversight of superhuman systems is needed, but it relies on the assumption that results transfer.

### Can oversight scale? The disagreement

The optimistic view: evaluating is generally easier than generating, as with checking proofs, and methods that give evaluators tools, decompositions and adversarial helpers extend the range of tasks they can judge; early experiments with weak supervisors and assisted humans show gains; and the methods can be tested incrementally. The skeptical view: each method relies on assumptions that fail in the hard cases. Decomposition may not exist for tasks that rely on holistic judgment; debaters could collude or exploit judge biases; AI feedback inherits errors; weak-to-strong experiments involve tasks where the strong model already knows the answer, so the analogy to superhuman skills may be weak; and a system that understands how it is being evaluated might behave differently when evaluated. The evidence is early on both sides, and which view is right is not known.

@example
title: PGR and the gap closed
problem: (a) A weak supervisor has accuracy $0.62$, a strong model trained on its labels reaches $0.78$, and the strong model trained on ground truth reaches $0.90$. Compute the performance gap recovered. (b) In a sandwiching study, non-experts alone score $52\%$, non-experts with an assistant score $70\%$, and experts score $88\%$. What fraction of the gap is closed?
step: (a) The gain over the supervisor is $0.78 - 0.62 = 0.16$; the available headroom is $0.90 - 0.62 = 0.28$.
step: So $\mathrm{PGR} = 0.16/0.28 = 0.571$: the strong model recovers a little over half of the possible improvement.
step: (b) The improvement from the assistant is $70 - 52 = 18$ points; the gap between non-experts and experts is $88 - 52 = 36$ points.
step: The fraction of the gap closed is $18/36 = 0.5$.
answer: PGR $\approx 0.571$; gap closed $= 0.5$.

@example
title: Reading an overoptimization table
problem: A policy is optimized against a proxy reward model. At increasing KL distance from the reference (in nats) the table below (invented numbers) gives the proxy reward, a held-out gold reward and the mean response length in tokens.

| KL | proxy | gold | length |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 120 |
| 2 | 0.9 | 0.8 | 135 |
| 5 | 1.6 | 1.3 | 170 |
| 10 | 2.3 | 1.5 | 240 |
| 20 | 3.1 | 1.2 | 380 |
| 40 | 3.9 | 0.4 | 620 |

Describe the failure, find the best stopping point, and suggest a diagnosis.
step: The proxy rises at every row, from 0.0 to 3.9. The gold reward rises to 1.5 at KL 10 and then falls to 1.2 and 0.4: the proxy and gold curves diverge after KL 10.
step: The gap (proxy minus gold) is $0.1, 0.3, 0.8, 1.9, 3.5$ at KL $2, 5, 10, 20, 40$ (and 0 at the start), growing steadily: the proxy increasingly overstates quality.
step: If gold quality is the goal, the best of these stopping points is KL $= 10$ (gold $1.5$). Stopping where the proxy is highest (KL $= 40$) would give a gold reward of only $0.4$, far below the $1.5$ available at KL $= 10$.
step: Mean length grows from 120 to 620 tokens as optimization proceeds, suggesting one exploit: the reward model may favor longer responses. This is a hypothesis to test (for example, by length-controlled comparisons or by retraining the reward model with pairs balanced for length), not a conclusion from this table alone.
answer: Gold reward peaks at KL $= 10$ and then falls while the proxy keeps rising; a length exploit is a candidate diagnosis.

@mistakes
- Reporting only the proxy's improvement: evaluate oversight methods and reward-model optimization against an independent ground truth.
- Treating weak-to-strong results as established for superhuman models: they test an analogy in which the strong model's skills are known, and the transfer is itself an open question.
- Describing debate or amplification as solved: they are proposals with theoretical arguments and early experiments, and the main objections (diffuse flaws, collusion, decomposition failure) are unresolved.
- Assuming AI feedback is neutral: a labeling model carries its own biases, which can be amplified when the labels train a similar model.

@practice self
prompt: Two reward models were trained on 1,000 and 10,000 human comparisons. A policy is optimized against each, and the table (invented numbers) shows the proxy reward of the small model and the gold reward of the policy at several KL distances.

| KL | small RM proxy | gold, optimized against small RM | gold, optimized against large RM |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.0 |
| 5 | 1.4 | 1.0 | 1.1 |
| 10 | 2.0 | 1.1 | 1.5 |
| 20 | 2.8 | 0.6 | 1.6 |
| 40 | 3.5 | -0.2 | 1.2 |

Analyze the failure of the small reward model: what the data show, two competing explanations, one experiment to distinguish them, and a mitigation with its limitation.
hint: Compare where each gold curve peaks and how the proxy-gold gap grows; then ask what would separate lack of data from a systematic exploit.
solution: The proxy of the small model rises monotonically, while the gold reward of the policy trained against it peaks near KL 10 (1.1) and then falls below the starting value by KL 40 ($-0.2$). The large reward model's gold curve peaks later (around KL 20, at 1.6) and higher, so more data pushes the overoptimization point out but does not remove it (gold falls by KL 40). Explanation one: the small model has high variance, fits noise in few comparisons, and is unreliable in regions far from its training data; more data fixes this, which the large model's result supports. Explanation two: the reward models share a systematic bias (for example a preference for length or style present in the raters' data) that more data from the same raters reproduces; this would produce the same late decline in both curves. An experiment to distinguish them: train reward models on independent subsets of the same size and compare the optimization peaks (variance predicts large differences between subsets); and measure the correlation of both proxies with features such as length, comparing the optimized outputs' features with the gold judgments (bias predicts the same exploited feature across models). Mitigations: early stopping at a KL budget chosen using held-out gold judgments, or ensembles of reward models and a larger penalty $\beta$; the limitation is that all of these treat the symptom: they cannot detect a bias shared by all models trained from the same feedback, and a budget chosen on one prompt distribution may not hold on another.
rubric: Reads the table correctly (monotone proxy, gold peaks then falls, later and higher peak for the larger model).
rubric: Gives two competing explanations (variance or data scarcity versus systematic bias) and an experiment that separates them.
rubric: Proposes a mitigation and states its limitation.

@quiz self
prompt: Design a sandwiching experiment for an assistant that helps non-expert annotators judge answers to questions in a specialized domain. Specify the participants, the ground truth, the conditions, the main metric with uncertainty, and two threats to validity.
hint: Define the three groups (non-expert alone, non-expert with assistant, expert) and the quantity "fraction of gap closed" first.
solution: Participants: non-expert annotators recruited without domain training, and a panel of domain experts who produce ground-truth labels (with agreement among experts measured, since disagreement caps the ceiling). Task: judge which of two answers to each question is correct, using a held-out set of questions with expert-verified answers, where the assistant model alone scores between the non-experts and the experts. Conditions: (1) non-experts alone, (2) non-experts with the assistant's explanations or critiques, (3) the assistant alone, (4) experts alone as the ceiling, with random assignment of questions and annotators across conditions. Metric: fraction of the gap closed, $(\text{acc}_2 - \text{acc}_1)/(\text{acc}_4 - \text{acc}_1)$, with bootstrap confidence intervals over questions and annotators. Threats: first, the assistant may leak or be tuned on the ground-truth answers or their sources, inflating accuracy (control by a contamination-free held-out set); second, the assistant may persuade annotators regardless of correctness, so gains on easy cases may hide failures on hard ones (check accuracy by question difficulty and by assistant confidence, and include cases where the assistant is wrong). A further limitation is that success on tasks where models are not superhuman need not transfer to the setting oversight is meant for.
rubric: Specifies conditions including non-expert alone, assisted, and expert ceiling, with expert-verified ground truth.
rubric: Gives the gap-closed metric with uncertainty over questions and annotators.
rubric: Identifies two real threats (leakage or contamination, persuasion unrelated to correctness) and a transfer limitation.
