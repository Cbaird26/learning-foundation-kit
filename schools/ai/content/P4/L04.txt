@article
A language model completes "When Mary and John went to the store, John gave a drink to" with " Mary". Attribution says the names matter. Probing says name identity is decodable. Neither says *how* the model gets from the sentence to the answer. Mechanistic interpretability tries to answer that question by reverse engineering the computation into parts that can be named, tested and explained, the way one reverse engineers a compiled program.

### The circuits program

Olah and colleagues (2020), in "Zoom In", framed a research program around three claims. **Features** are the fundamental units: directions in activation space that correspond to meaningful properties (an edge, a curve, a floppy ear). **Circuits** are the subgraphs of weights that connect features and compute later features from earlier ones. **Universality** is the speculation that analogous features and circuits recur across different models and tasks. The first two claims were supported by detailed case studies in vision models; universality was offered as a hypothesis and is still only partly tested. The related **linear representation hypothesis**, that features are directions, is partly supported and partly contested: it is the assumption behind probes and sparse dictionaries (week 6), and it is an empirical claim about models, not a theorem.

### Transformers as sums of paths

Elhage and colleagues (2021), "A mathematical framework for transformer circuits", analyzed small attention-only transformers in a form that makes circuits visible. The **residual stream** is a shared channel: the final representation is the token embedding plus the sum of the outputs of every attention head and MLP layer that wrote to it, $\mathbf{x}_{\text{final}} = \mathbf{x}_{\text{embed}} + \sum_{\text{heads}} \mathbf{o}_h + \sum_{\text{MLPs}} \mathbf{m}_\ell$. Each component reads from and writes to the stream, so a component's effect can be studied in isolation, up to the nonlinearities of later layers. An attention head factors into two circuits: the **QK circuit**, with matrix $\mathbf{W}_Q^\top \mathbf{W}_K$, decides where the head attends, and the **OV circuit**, with matrix $\mathbf{W}_O \mathbf{W}_V$, decides what it copies from the attended position. Heads in different layers can **compose**, one reading what another wrote, and this composition is how multi-step algorithms arise (week 5).

### Interventions

A circuit claim is causal, so it is tested by intervening. In **ablation**, a component's output is replaced by zero, its mean over a data set, or its value on a resampled input. Zero ablation can move activations far from anything seen in training, so mean and resample ablations are common. In **activation patching**, a component's activation on a *clean* run is copied into a run on a *corrupted* input (or the reverse), and the change in an output metric is measured. If patching the activation of a component restores most of the clean behavior, that component carries information the behavior depends on. A normalized effect is usually reported,

$$\text{effect} = \frac{m_{\text{patched}} - m_{\text{corrupt}}}{m_{\text{clean}} - m_{\text{corrupt}}},$$

where $m$ is the metric (a logit difference, say). Meng and colleagues (2022) used a version called **causal tracing**: corrupt the subject tokens of a factual prompt with noise, then restore the clean hidden state at one (token, layer) location and measure the recovery of the correct answer. They reported that restoring mid-layer MLP states at the last subject token recovers much of the fact. Hase, Bansal, Kim and Ghandeharioun (2023) reported that where tracing localizes an effect does not reliably predict where editing the fact works best, a reminder that localization and mechanism are distinct claims.

### A worked circuit and its criteria

Wang and colleagues (2022) studied the indirect-object-identification task in GPT-2 small and proposed a circuit of a few dozen attention heads in functional classes, including heads that identify duplicated names, heads that suppress the repeated name, and "name mover" heads that copy the remaining name to the output. They judged the circuit by three criteria. **Faithfulness**: the circuit alone, with the rest of the model ablated, reproduces the model's behavior on the task. **Completeness**: the circuit contains all components that matter, tested by checking that removing any subset of the circuit changes behavior in the same way for the circuit and for the model. **Minimality**: no component of the circuit can be removed without hurting performance. They also found backup heads that compensate when others are ablated, so ablation can understate a component's importance.

### What is open

How far this scales is the central disagreement. Optimists point to circuits found in models of moderate size, to automated circuit-discovery methods, and to the hope that features and circuits recur across models, so that understanding compounds. Skeptics reply that such studies took substantial human effort on narrow tasks; that circuits found for one prompt distribution may not describe behavior elsewhere; that backup mechanisms and distributed computation make clean subgraphs the exception; and that "understanding" lacks an agreed test. A third position holds that a bottom-up circuit view may be the wrong abstraction and that top-down study of representations and behavior will give more reliable control sooner. These are empirical questions about methods that are changing quickly, and the evidence so far does not settle them.

@example
title: Activation patching on a two-unit network
problem: A network has $h_1 = \max(0, x_1 + x_2)$, $h_2 = \max(0, x_1 - x_2)$ and output $y = 2h_1 - h_2$. The clean input is $(2, 1)$ and the corrupted input is $(0, 1)$. Compute the normalized effect of patching each hidden unit's clean activation into the corrupted run.
step: Clean run: $h_1 = 3$, $h_2 = 1$, $y = 2(3) - 1 = 5$.
step: Corrupted run: $h_1 = 1$, $h_2 = \max(0, -1) = 0$, $y = 2(1) - 0 = 2$. The total effect to explain is $5 - 2 = 3$.
step: Patch $h_1$ (set it to 3 in the corrupted run, leaving $h_2 = 0$): $y = 2(3) - 0 = 6$. The effect is $(6 - 2)/3 = 4/3$.
step: Patch $h_2$ (set it to 1, leaving $h_1 = 1$): $y = 2(1) - 1 = 1$. The effect is $(1 - 2)/3 = -1/3$.
step: The two effects sum to $1$, as they must here because the readout is linear. A normalized effect above 1 or below 0 is possible: $h_2$ pushes the output the wrong way when restored, so a component can matter in the opposite direction from the full behavior.
answer: $h_1$: $4/3$; $h_2$: $-1/3$.

@mistakes
- Equating localization with mechanism: finding where an intervention changes the output does not explain what the component computes or how downstream layers use it.
- Using zero ablation without a check: setting activations to zero can push the network off the data distribution, so a large effect may reflect damage, not importance.
- Testing a circuit on one prompt template and claiming it describes the model: faithfulness, completeness and minimality should be measured on a distribution of inputs, including ones that were not used to find the circuit.
- Ignoring compensation: backup components can take over when a head is ablated, so a small ablation effect does not show the head is unimportant.

@practice num
prompt: A network has $h_1 = \max(0, x_1 - x_2)$, $h_2 = \max(0, x_1 + x_2 - 2)$ and output $y = 3h_1 + h_2$. The clean input is $(3, 1)$ and the corrupted input is $(1, 2)$. What is the normalized effect of patching the clean value of $h_1$ into the corrupted run? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.857
tol: 0.001
check: ((3 * max(0, 3 - 1) + max(0, 1 + 2 - 2)) - (3 * max(0, 1 - 2) + max(0, 1 + 2 - 2))) / ((3 * max(0, 3 - 1) + max(0, 3 + 1 - 2)) - (3 * max(0, 1 - 2) + max(0, 1 + 2 - 2)))
hint: Compute the clean output (8) and corrupted output (1), then the output when only $h_1$ is set to its clean value while $h_2$ keeps its corrupted value.
solution: Clean: $h_1 = 2$, $h_2 = 2$, $y = 8$. Corrupted: $h_1 = 0$, $h_2 = 1$, $y = 1$. Patching $h_1 = 2$ with $h_2 = 1$ gives $y = 7$. The normalized effect is $(7 - 1)/(8 - 1) = 6/7 \approx 0.857$.

@practice mc
prompt: Why do many mechanistic studies use mean or resample ablation, or patching from a counterfactual input, instead of setting a component's output to zero?
choice*: Zero can be far outside the range of activations the downstream components saw in training, so the measured effect may reflect off-distribution damage rather than the component's role.
choice: Zero ablation cannot change the output of a network, so it measures nothing.
choice: Mean ablation is guaranteed to remove all information the component carries about the input.
choice: Patching from a counterfactual input is only defined for attention heads, not for MLPs.
hint: Think about what the next layer receives when an activation takes a value it never took during training.
solution: A zero vector may be a very unusual input to later layers, so large effects may be artifacts of that. Zero ablation certainly can change outputs. @practice num
prompt: A mean-difference probe is fit on activations: label 1 at $(2, 0)$ and $(4, 2)$; label 0 at $(0, 0)$ and $(0, 2)$. The probe's score is $\mathbf{w}^\top \mathbf{x} + b$ with $\mathbf{w} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_0$ and $b = -\mathbf{w}^\top(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 + \boldsymbol{\mu}_0)/2$. What is the score of the new activation $(2, 7)$?
answer: 1.5
tol: 0.001
check: (lambda m1, m0: sum((a - c) * x for a, c, x in zip(m1, m0, (2, 7))) - sum((a - c) * (a + c) / 2 for a, c in zip(m1, m0)))((3, 1), (0, 1))
hint: The class means are $(3, 1)$ and $(0, 1)$, so $\mathbf{w} = (3, 0)$.
solution: $\boldsymbol{\mu}_1 = (3, 1)$, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_0 = (0, 1)$, so $\mathbf{w} = (3, 0)$ and $b = -(3 \cdot 3 + 0 \cdot 2)/2 = -4.5$. The score at $(2, 7)$ is $3 \cdot 2 - 4.5 = 1.5 > 0$, so class 1.

@practice self
prompt: Give the strongest case for and the strongest case against the claim that detailed circuit analyses of small models will scale to understanding much larger networks. Then say which single experiment, if its result were known, would most change your view, and why.
hint: Give each side its best evidence, then choose an experiment whose outcomes would differ under the two views.
solution: For: circuit analyses have recovered concrete algorithms in models of moderate size (an induction mechanism, an indirect-object-identification circuit), and the same component types (heads, MLPs) recur at every scale. The human cost per circuit has dropped with better patching methods and automated search, and if components are reused across tasks and models, understanding compounds: each new behavior may reuse parts already understood. Against: analyses so far cover narrow behaviors on templated prompts; a larger model has many more components and plausibly more distributed, redundant and backup mechanisms, so the subgraph that explains a behavior may not be small; features may be superposed (week 6), so components are not clean units; there is no accepted test of understanding that would tell us when a large model is understood well enough for a purpose such as detecting deception; and automated discovery can find circuits that pass faithfulness tests without being meaningful. The experiment: take a behavior on a larger model that was not used to develop the methods, apply a fixed procedure for circuit discovery, and test the result against held-out inputs with all three criteria, while measuring the human and compute cost against the same measurement on a small model. A faithful, complete and small circuit at low marginal cost would support the scaling claim; a sharply growing size or cost, or poor held-out faithfulness, would count against it.
rubric: States the best arguments on both sides (reuse of components and falling costs versus distributed mechanisms, superposition and the lack of a test of understanding).
rubric: Proposes a specific experiment with a fixed procedure, held-out inputs and the three circuit criteria.
rubric: Explains how each possible result would change the conclusion, without simply asserting one side.

@quiz num
prompt: A network has $h_1 = \max(0, 2x_1 - x_2)$, $h_2 = \max(0, x_2)$ and output $y = h_1 - h_2$. The clean input is $(2, 1)$ and the corrupted input is $(0, 2)$. What is the normalized effect of patching the clean value of $h_1$ into the corrupted run?
answer: 0.75
tol: 0.001
check: ((max(0, 2*2 - 1) - max(0, 2)) - (max(0, 2*0 - 2) - max(0, 2))) / ((max(0, 2*2 - 1) - max(0, 1)) - (max(0, 2*0 - 2) - max(0, 2)))
hint: The clean output is 2 and the corrupted output is $-2$. Patching $h_1$ leaves $h_2$ at its corrupted value 2.
solution: Clean: $h_1 = 3$, $h_2 = 1$, $y = 2$. Corrupted: $h_1 = 0$, $h_2 = 2$, $y = -2$. Patching $h_1 = 3$ gives $y = 3 - 2 = 1$. The effect is $(1 - (-2))/(2 - (-2)) = 3/4 = 0.75$.

@quiz self
prompt: Design a causal-tracing-style experiment to test whether information about a prompt's subject flows through a particular layer at the last subject token. Specify the clean, corrupted and restored runs, the metric, the controls, and one result that would make you doubt that the layer stores the fact.
hint: Specify three runs (clean, corrupted, restored) and a metric normalized between the clean and corrupted values.
solution: Clean run: the model sees the factual prompt and we record the probability of the correct answer token and all hidden states. Corrupted run: add noise to the embeddings of the subject tokens, which should lower the probability of the correct answer. Restored runs: re-run the corrupted input but overwrite the hidden state at one chosen (token, layer) position with its clean value, for each position in turn, and measure the recovery $(p_{\text{restored}} - p_{\text{corrupt}})/(p_{\text{clean}} - p_{\text{corrupt}})$. Controls: restore at positions of other tokens (not the last subject token) and at other layers; repeat over many prompts and noise draws and report distributions; use a placebo that restores a state from a different clean prompt. A positive result at the layer indicates that information needed for the answer passes through that position and layer. It does not show that the fact is stored there: later layers may recompute it, and restoring a state may carry the information from earlier layers. A result that would make me doubt the storage reading: if overwriting that layer's weights or the MLP's output through an editing method does not change the answer, or if the best layer for changing the fact differs from the layer where restoration is strongest, as later studies have reported, localization of information flow and the location of storage come apart.
rubric: Specifies clean, corrupted and restored runs and a normalized recovery metric.
rubric: Includes controls over positions, layers, prompts or noise draws.
rubric: Distinguishes information flow from storage and names a result that would undermine the storage interpretation.
