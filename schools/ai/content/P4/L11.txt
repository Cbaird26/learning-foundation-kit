@article
A developer tests a new model on forty tasks designed to probe a worrying capability, and it passes none. Is the capability absent? Perhaps the prompts were poor, the model needed a tool, the tasks were too unlike real use, or the model was held to a standard no real actor would use. Evaluations produce evidence, and evidence is only as good as the design behind it. This lesson is about that design, at the level of methodology.

### What evaluations are for

Shevlane and colleagues (2023), "Model evaluation for extreme risks", argued that evaluations should inform decisions about training, deployment and security, and distinguished two kinds. **Dangerous-capability evaluations** ask whether a model *can* do something harmful if it tries (general categories discussed in the literature include offensive cyber operations and manipulation). **Alignment evaluations** ask whether it *tends* to apply its capabilities that way, which is a question about **propensity**. Capability asks about the ceiling; propensity asks about what is likely to happen by default.

### Designing an evaluation

1. **Threat model first.** State who the actor is, what harm pathway is of concern, and which steps of it the model would have to perform or speed up. Tasks are chosen as measurable proxies for steps on that pathway.
2. **Elicitation.** A model's best performance is rarely its first attempt. Prompt design, tools and scaffolding, many attempts, and fine-tuning can raise measured capability substantially, so a score is a **lower bound** on what a motivated actor could obtain. Report the elicitation effort, and be most skeptical of a "no capability" result when little effort was spent.
3. **Held-out, contamination-free test sets.** If test items, or their solutions, appeared in training data, a high score may reflect memory. Practices include creating tasks after the training cutoff or privately, keeping a secret portion of the test set, checking for overlap (for example, the fraction of test n-grams found in the training corpus) and limiting repeated querying that lets developers tune against the test set.
4. **Statistics.** A pass rate is an estimate. With $k$ passes in $n$ independent trials, report an interval. The **Wilson interval** at normal quantile $z$ (1.96 for 95%) is

$$\frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + z^2/n},$$

where $\hat p = k/n$; it stays inside $[0, 1]$ and behaves well for small $n$ and for $\hat p$ near 0 or 1, unlike the simple normal interval. Trials are not independent when they share tasks, so resample at the level of tasks. When a model gets $n$ samples per task and $c$ pass, the probability that at least one of $k$ randomly chosen samples passes is estimated by $\text{pass@}k = 1 - \binom{n-c}{k} / \binom{n}{k}$. With zero failures in $n$ trials, the largest failure rate not rejected at the 5% level solves $(1-p)^n = 0.05$, so $p = 1 - 0.05^{1/n}$, close to $3/n$ (the "rule of three").
5. **Decision rules fixed in advance.** Decide before testing what result triggers which action. A conservative rule treats a threshold as reached if the *upper* confidence bound of the pass rate is at least the threshold, because the costs of missing a real capability exceed the costs of extra caution, and because under-elicitation biases scores downward. Evaluate at several points during training, with a safety margin, since capability can increase between evaluations.

```python
from math import comb

def pass_at_k(n, c, k):
    """Probability that at least one of k samples drawn from n (c correct) passes."""
    if n - c < k:
        return 1.0
    return 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)
```

### Red-teaming

**Red-teaming** is adversarial testing to find failures. Perez and colleagues (2022) used language models to generate test cases for another language model, comparing zero-shot generation, few-shot generation, supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning, and finding a trade-off between the diversity and difficulty of the cases found. Ganguli and colleagues (2022) studied human red-teaming across model sizes and training methods, reported that models trained with reinforcement learning from human feedback became harder to red team as they scaled, released a data set of attacks, and described lessons learned for running red-team exercises. Red-teaming can find failures, and the coverage it gives is whatever the red team tried. It cannot certify the absence of failures.

### What evaluations cannot do, and the disagreement

An evaluation shows behavior on the inputs tested with the elicitation used. It cannot show absence of a capability under better elicitation, or behavior under inputs no one thought of. Another concern is that a system could behave differently when it recognizes that it is being tested; how serious or common this is, is open. Researchers disagree about how much weight evaluations can bear. One view holds that they are the most concrete tool available: they turn vague concern into measurements, enable "if-then" commitments with external accountability, and can improve with practice. The other view holds that thresholds are arbitrary, that tasks measure what is easy to measure, that under-elicitation and test-set contamination are hard to rule out, and that evaluation-based assurance can create false confidence and a ritual that substitutes for understanding. Most practitioners agree on the methodological points above and differ on how far evaluations can support a claim of safety.

@example
title: A Wilson interval and a zero-failure bound
problem: A model solves 18 of 20 independent tasks in an evaluation. (a) Give the 95% Wilson interval for its pass rate and compare it with the simple normal interval $\hat p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}$. (b) A second evaluation has 0 failures in 30 independent trials; give the 95% upper bound on the failure rate.
step: (a) $\hat p = 0.9$, $n = 20$, $z^2 = 3.8416$. The denominator is $1 + 3.8416/20 = 1.1921$ and the center is $(0.9 + 3.8416/40)/1.1921 = 0.8355$.
step: The half-width is $\dfrac{1.96}{1.1921}\sqrt{0.0045 + 3.8416/1600} = 1.6442 \times 0.0831 = 0.1366$. The Wilson interval is $(0.699, 0.972)$.
step: The normal interval is $0.9 \pm 1.96 \times 0.0671 = (0.769, 1.031)$, which exceeds 1, an impossible pass rate; the Wilson interval stays inside $[0, 1]$ and is not symmetric about $0.9$.
step: (b) The upper bound solves $(1-p)^{30} = 0.05$, so $p = 1 - 0.05^{1/30} = 0.095$; the rule of three gives $3/30 = 0.10$.
answer: (a) Wilson $(0.699, 0.972)$; (b) failure rate at most about $0.095$.

@mistakes
- Reporting a pass rate without an interval or without noting that tasks, not samples, are the unit of independence: a 90% pass rate on 20 tasks is compatible with a true rate near 70%.
- Reading a failure to elicit a capability as proof of its absence: report the elicitation effort, and treat the score as a lower bound.
- Tuning prompts and tasks against the test set, or not checking for overlap with training data: reuse and leakage inflate scores and shrink uncertainty falsely.
- Choosing the decision threshold after seeing the result, or comparing the point estimate, not the confidence bound, to a cautious threshold.

model:
```python
from math import sqrt


def wilson_interval(successes, n, z):
    """Wilson score interval for a binomial proportion."""
    p = successes / n
    denom = 1 + z * z / n
    center = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / denom
    half = z / denom * sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n))
    return max(0.0, center - half), min(1.0, center + half)
```
hint: Compute the denominator $1 + z^2/n$, then the center $(\hat p + z^2/2n)/\text{denominator}$ and the half-width, and finally clip.
solution: The interval is centered at a shrunk estimate that moves toward 1/2, which is why it is not centered at $\hat p$ unless $\hat p = 1/2$ (the last test). At 0 and $n$ successes the unclipped end can be off by a tiny float error, hence the clipping. The tests include both extremes, a small sample, and a larger quantile and sample.
