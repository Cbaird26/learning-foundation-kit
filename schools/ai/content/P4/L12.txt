@article
Everything in this course ends in a decision: whether to deploy a model, how much to trust an explanation, what an evaluation result should trigger. Laws and standards are one of the places where those decisions are written down. They also change faster than any textbook, so this lesson gives the position at the time of writing (late 2026) and, more importantly, the structure of the debate. **Check the current text of any law or standard before relying on it.**

### The EU AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 and is risk-based. It prohibits certain "unacceptable-risk" practices, imposes obligations on **high-risk** systems, imposes **transparency** obligations on some systems (such as chatbots and AI-generated content), leaves **minimal-risk** systems largely unregulated, and has separate rules for "general-purpose AI models". A general-purpose model trained with more than $10^{25}$ floating-point operations is presumed to pose systemic risk, which brings extra duties.

Dates of application: the prohibitions and the AI literacy duty from 2 February 2025; the obligations for general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025; the transparency obligations of Article 50 from 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, adopted in June 2026, published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, in force from 27 July 2026) moved the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 for systems listed in Annex III and to 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by Annex I. It also added two prohibitions that apply from 2 December 2026, on AI systems that generate realistic sexually explicit images, video or audio of an identifiable person without consent and on generating child sexual abuse material; gave providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to meet the marking duty of Article 50(2); and kept the AI literacy duty in revised form (providers and deployers take measures to support the AI literacy of their staff, with no specific level required).

### The United States

There is no comprehensive federal AI statute, and federal preemption of state AI laws has been actively debated in Congress since 2025, so check its current status. In January 2025 the 2023 executive order on AI (EO 14110) was revoked and EO 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," was issued; America's AI Action Plan followed in July 2025. EO 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" (11 December 2025), directs federal agencies to challenge and discourage state AI laws they judge burdensome, but an executive order does not by itself preempt state law. California's SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (signed 29 September 2025, in effect from 1 January 2026), requires developers of models trained with more than $10^{26}$ operations to publish transparency reports and, for those with annual revenue above \$500 million, a frontier AI safety framework; it requires reporting of critical safety incidents to the state's Office of Emergency Services and protects whistleblowers.

### Standards and principles

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023) organizes AI risk management into four functions: Govern, Map, Measure and Manage. ISO/IEC 42001 (2023) is a standard for AI management systems. The OECD AI Principles were adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024. These are voluntary frameworks; they matter because laws and procurement rules often refer to them.

### Compute thresholds: a worked debate

Both the EU rule and SB 53 use training compute as a trigger. Training compute for a dense transformer is approximately $6ND$ floating-point operations, with $N$ parameters and $D$ training tokens (the approximation used in the scaling-law literature, Kaplan and colleagues, 2020). A model with $N = 7 \times 10^{10}$ and $D = 1.5 \times 10^{12}$ has about $6.3 \times 10^{23}$ operations, about 6% of the $10^{25}$ line.

The case for thresholds: compute is measurable, hard to hide at scale, known before a model exists, and correlated with capability across the range studied; a bright line gives developers certainty and regulators something enforceable, and a threshold can capture the few largest systems without burdening everyone else. The case against: compute is a proxy, and algorithmic progress means that systems below any fixed threshold can reach capabilities that previously needed more; a threshold can be gamed by splitting or restructuring training; it says nothing about what a system is used for or what it can do after fine-tuning or tool use; and a number fixed in law ages quickly. Some proposals pair a compute trigger with capability evaluations (week 11), so that the evaluations, which measure what matters, decide what follows, and the compute number only decides who must evaluate. Which approach regulates better is unsettled.

### How to regulate: the larger disagreement

Informed observers disagree on whether to impose ex ante obligations on developers of the most capable systems (the precautionary view: risks may be severe and hard to reverse, so transparency, testing and incident reporting should come before harm); or to regulate uses and harms through existing law and respond as evidence accumulates (the innovation-first view: rules written before the technology is understood risk locking in errors, entrenching incumbents and slowing beneficial uses); and whether rules should be set federally or by states, or internationally. Each view has evidence on its side, and the right balance depends on empirical beliefs, discussed in week 8, about how large and how soon the risks are.

### Open research problems

The course has met these problems: validating that interpretability methods are faithful and that they scale; deciding whether sparse-dictionary features are the model's own; making reward models robust to overoptimization; testing whether oversight methods work on tasks beyond human ability; eliciting capabilities and evaluating propensities when a system may behave differently under test; understanding and predicting goal misgeneralization; making oversight components robust to adversaries; and measuring risk with calibrated uncertainty. A good proposal picks a question whose answer would change a decision under more than one of the views above, states what result would falsify its hypothesis, and uses baselines and controls that a skeptic would demand.

@example
title: Compute versus the thresholds
problem: A dense model has $N = 2 \times 10^{11}$ parameters and is trained on $D = 6 \times 10^{12}$ tokens. Estimate its training compute with $6ND$. Does it exceed the EU's $10^{25}$-operation presumption of systemic risk, or the SB 53 line of $10^{26}$?
step: Compute $6ND = 6 \times 2\times 10^{11} \times 6 \times 10^{12} = 7.2 \times 10^{24}$.
step: Compare: $7.2 \times 10^{24} < 10^{25}$, so the model is below the EU presumption, at 72% of it, and well below $10^{26}$, at 7.2%.
step: The estimate is approximate (the $6ND$ rule ignores attention costs, different architectures and any post-training compute), so a developer so close to a line would need a careful count and to check how the legal text defines and counts compute.
answer: About $7.2 \times 10^{24}$ operations, below both thresholds.

@mistakes
- Quoting dates or thresholds from memory: laws are amended (the high-risk dates of the EU AI Act were moved in 2026), so read the current text.
- Treating an executive order as law that preempts state law: an executive order directs agencies, and does not by itself preempt state statutes.
- Treating a compute threshold as a measure of risk: it is a proxy, and the debate about it is about proxies.
- Writing a proposal that argues for one conclusion: a good proposal states the result that would change the author's mind.

Choose a problem that can be studied on small models and supplied data.
hint: Pick a question where a skeptic and a supporter would both accept the evidence, then write what result would change your mind.
solution: Problem: do sparse-autoencoder latents correspond to features that a model uses, or only to patterns that a sparsity-biased dictionary finds? Why it matters: for those who think interpretability can scale, validated features are the base of circuit analysis; for skeptics, the question tests whether a popular method's output is an artifact; for evaluation-minded researchers, features that track what the model uses could support monitoring. Hypothesis: latents from SAEs trained on a small transformer's activations are causally relevant: patching activations along a latent's decoder direction changes the model's outputs in the way predicted by the latent's top-activating examples, more than random or matched directions do. Method: train a two-layer transformer on a synthetic task with known sparse latent factors (for example, tokens generated from a small set of independent features), train SAEs of several dictionary sizes on the residual stream, and test recovery of the known factors (a ground-truth check), then run the same procedure on a task where the factors are unknown and test causal relevance by patching. Controls and baselines: random directions with matched norm, directions from probes and from principal components, an SAE trained on activations of a randomly initialized model, and several seeds. Falsification: if latents recover the planted features in the synthetic task but patching along them has effects no larger than random directions on the natural task, the hypothesis fails for that setting; if SAE latents are not more causally relevant than principal components, the claim of special validity fails. Risks: synthetic factors may be unrepresentative of natural features; patching can push activations off-distribution, which I address by also testing small steps and mean-ablating; results on small models may not scale, so I would claim only what is shown.

@quiz self
prompt: Make the strongest case for using training-compute thresholds as the trigger for obligations on developers of frontier models, and the strongest case against. Then propose one design that responds to the best objection to each side.
hint: List what compute has in its favor (measurable, early, verifiable) and what it lacks (what the system can do, algorithmic progress).
solution: For: compute is measurable before and during training, difficult to conceal at the scale of interest, and has correlated with capability across a wide range; a bright line gives developers predictability and regulators an enforceable test, and confines duties to a very small set of actors, which limits the burden on research and small firms. Against: compute is only a proxy; algorithmic efficiency improves, so capability that required a given compute at one time requires less later, and a fixed number leaves growing capability uncovered; fine-tuning, tool use and inference-time compute can add capability after training; developers can restructure training to stay below the line, and the number says nothing about the use or the risk. A design responding to both: a two-part trigger in which a compute threshold, set to be updated by a regulator on a schedule, determines who must run standard capability evaluations before release, while obligations that depend on risk (mitigations, incident reporting) attach to evaluation results and to deployment context, not to compute alone, and a lower compute or revenue band requires registration so that regulators learn about systems near the line. This keeps the verifiability of compute while moving the substantive decision to measured capability; the remaining weakness is that the evaluations inherit the elicitation problems of week 11.
