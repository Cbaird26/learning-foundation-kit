## Week-by-week summary

**Week 1: probability spaces and counting.** A probability space is $(\Omega, \text{events}, P)$ with $P \ge 0$, $P(\Omega) = 1$ and additivity for disjoint events. In a uniform space $P(A) = |A|/|\Omega|$; count with the multiplication rule, permutations $n!/(n-k)!$ and combinations $\binom{n}{k}$; use complements and inclusion-exclusion.

**Week 2: conditional probability and independence.** $P(A \mid B) = P(A \cap B)/P(B)$; multiplication rule; law of total probability; independence means $P(A \cap B) = P(A)P(B)$, which is different from being disjoint.

**Week 3: Bayes' rule.** Posterior $\propto$ prior $\times$ likelihood; odds form multiplies by the likelihood ratio; beware the base rate fallacy; naive Bayes assumes conditional independence of features.

**Week 4: random variables.** PMF, CDF, functions of a random variable, joint and marginal distributions, and independence of random variables (check every pair of values).

**Week 5: expectation.** $E[X] = \sum x\,p(x)$, LOTUS, linearity without independence, indicator variables, coupon collector $nH_n$.

**Week 6: variance and distributions.** $\mathrm{Var}(X) = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$; Bernoulli, binomial $np$ and $np(1-p)$, geometric $1/p$, Poisson $\lambda$; expected costs of randomized algorithms (restarts, hashing, quicksort).

**Week 7: continuous distributions.** Densities integrate to probability; uniform, exponential (memoryless), normal and standardization.

**Week 8: LLN and CLT.** Sample mean has mean $\mu$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$; the standardized mean is approximately normal; continuity correction for binomials.

**Week 9: tail bounds.** Markov (non-negative), Chebyshev, Chernoff for sums of independent indicators, the union bound, and repeating randomized algorithms.

**Week 10: Markov chains.** Rows of $P$ sum to 1; $\pi_t = \pi_0 P^t$; stationary $\pi = \pi P$; gambler's ruin; random walks on graphs have $\pi(v) \propto \deg(v)$.

**Week 11: estimation.** Unbiasedness, sample variance with $n - 1$, standard errors, confidence intervals and their interpretation.

**Week 12: hypothesis tests.** Null and alternative, p-value, type I and II errors, power, the two-proportion z-test, permutation tests, multiple comparisons.

## Common errors

- Counting unequal outcomes as equally likely (for example the 11 sums of two dice).
- Swapping $P(A \mid B)$ and $P(B \mid A)$; forgetting the base rate.
- Using independence where it is not given, especially for variances; linearity of expectation needs none.
- Applying Markov to a variable that can be negative, or the CLT with infinite variance.
- Dividing by $n$ instead of $n - 1$; dividing by $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
- Reading a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

model:
```python
import math


def ab_test_decision(x1, n1, x2, n2, alpha=0.05):
    p1 = x1 / n1
    p2 = x2 / n2
    pooled = (x1 + x2) / (n1 + n2)
    se = math.sqrt(pooled * (1 - pooled) * (1 / n1 + 1 / n2))
    if se == 0:
        return "no difference"
    z = (p2 - p1) / se
    phi = 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(abs(z) / math.sqrt(2)))
    p_value = 2 * (1 - phi)
    if p_value < alpha:
        return "B better" if p2 > p1 else "A better"
    return "no difference"
```
hint: Compute the pooled standard error first and handle the zero case before dividing.
hint: Only after the p-value is below `alpha` does the sign of `p2 - p1` decide between the two answers.
solution: Compute `z` and the two-sided p-value as in the lesson, then compare the p-value with `alpha`. For 100/1000 versus 150/1000, $z \approx 3.38$ and $p \approx 0.0007$, so B is declared better at $\alpha = 0.05$ but not at $\alpha = 0.0001$.

@exam self
prompt: (a) State Markov's inequality, including its conditions. (b) Derive Chebyshev's inequality $P(|X - \mu| \ge a) \le \sigma^2/a^2$ from it. (c) A randomized algorithm's running time $T$ has mean 100 and variance 400. Use Chebyshev's inequality to bound the probability that $T$ is at least 160, and explain why using Chernoff-type bounds would require extra assumptions about $T$.
hint: Chebyshev's inequality is Markov's inequality applied to the non-negative variable $(X - \mu)^2$.
solution: (a) If $Y \ge 0$ and $a > 0$ then $P(Y \ge a) \le E[Y]/a$. (b) Apply it to $Y = (X - \mu)^2 \ge 0$ with threshold $a^2$: $P(|X - \mu| \ge a) = P((X - \mu)^2 \ge a^2) \le E[(X - \mu)^2]/a^2 = \sigma^2/a^2$. (c) $P(T \ge 160) \le P(|T - 100| \ge 60) \le 400/3600 = 1/9$. Chernoff bounds hold for sums of independent Bernoulli (or bounded independent) variables, and nothing says $T$ has this form; Chebyshev needs only the variance.

rubric: Derives Chebyshev by applying Markov to $(X - \mu)^2$ with threshold $a^2$.
rubric: Computes the bound $400/60^2 = 1/9$ and notes that $T \ge 160$ is contained in $|T - 100| \ge 60$.
