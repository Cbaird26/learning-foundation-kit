@article
Averages of many random values behave in a strikingly regular way. Two theorems explain how: the law of large numbers says where the average goes, and the central limit theorem says how it fluctuates around that value.

### The sample mean

Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ be independent, identically distributed (**i.i.d.**) random variables with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, and let $\bar X_n = \frac{1}{n}(X_1 + \cdots + X_n)$ be their **sample mean**. By linearity and independence,

$$E[\bar X_n] = \mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}, \qquad \mathrm{SD}(\bar X_n) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.$$

The standard deviation $\sigma/\sqrt n$ of the sample mean is called its **standard error**. Averaging shrinks fluctuations by $\sqrt n$: to halve the error you need four times as much data.

### The law of large numbers

**Weak law of large numbers.** For i.i.d. variables with finite mean $\mu$ (and, in the version we prove from Chebyshev's inequality next week, finite variance), for every $\varepsilon > 0$,

$$P\big(|\bar X_n - \mu| > \varepsilon\big) \to 0 \quad \text{as } n \to \infty.$$

The sample mean converges to the true mean. The law justifies simulation: estimating a probability by the fraction of trials in which an event occurs (the sample mean of indicators) gets closer to the truth as the number of trials grows. It says nothing about "making up" for past streaks: after ten heads in a row, the next toss of a fair coin is still heads with probability $1/2$.

### The central limit theorem

**Central limit theorem (CLT).** For i.i.d. $X_i$ with finite mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2 > 0$, the standardized sample mean converges in distribution to a standard normal:

$$P\!\left(\frac{\bar X_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n} \le z\right) \to \Phi(z) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \text{ for every } z.$$

So for large $n$, $\bar X_n$ is approximately $N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$, and the sum $X_1 + \cdots + X_n$ is approximately $N(n\mu, n\sigma^2)$, **whatever the shape of the original distribution** (as long as the variance is finite). Dice, coin flips and uniform numbers all give bell-shaped averages.

How large must $n$ be? There is no universal answer. Symmetric distributions need few terms, while very skewed or heavy-tailed ones need many. The rule of thumb $n \ge 30$ is only a rough guide. If the variance is infinite or undefined the theorem does not apply. For the Cauchy distribution, which has no mean at all, the sample mean does not even settle down.

### Normal approximation to the binomial

A Binomial$(n, p)$ variable is a sum of $n$ i.i.d. Bernoulli$(p)$ variables, so it is approximately $N(np,\, np(1-p))$. Because the binomial takes integer values, a **continuity correction** improves the approximation: $P(X \ge k) \approx 1 - \Phi\!\left(\frac{k - 0.5 - np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\right)$.

### Simulating the CLT

```python
import random
import statistics

rng = random.Random(1)
means = [statistics.fmean(rng.random() for _ in range(30))
         for _ in range(2000)]
print(statistics.fmean(means), statistics.stdev(means))
```

Each entry of `means` averages 30 uniform numbers. The theory predicts that the printed mean is close to $0.5$ and the printed standard deviation is close to $\sqrt{1/12}/\sqrt{30} \approx 0.0527$. A histogram of `means` looks like a bell curve even though a single uniform number is flat.

@example
title: The mean of 100 die rolls
problem: A fair die is rolled 100 times. Approximate the probability that the average of the rolls exceeds 3.7.
step: One roll has $\mu = 3.5$ and $\sigma^2 = 35/12$, so $\sigma \approx 1.708$.
step: The standard error of the mean of 100 rolls is $\sigma/\sqrt{100} \approx 0.1708$.
step: Standardize: $z = (3.7 - 3.5)/0.1708 \approx 1.171$.
step: By the CLT, $P(\bar X > 3.7) \approx 1 - \Phi(1.171) \approx 0.121$.
answer: about $0.121$

@example
title: Normal approximation to a binomial tail
problem: A fair coin is tossed 100 times. Approximate $P(X \ge 60)$ for the number of heads $X$.
step: $X \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(100, 1/2)$ has mean $np = 50$ and standard deviation $\sqrt{100 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12} = 5$.
step: With the continuity correction, $P(X \ge 60) \approx P(Y \ge 59.5)$ for $Y \sim N(50, 5^2)$.
step: The standardized value is $z = (59.5 - 50)/5 = 1.9$.
step: $P \approx 1 - \Phi(1.9) \approx 0.0287$. The exact binomial probability is about $0.0284$.
answer: about $0.0287$

@mistakes
- Believing the law of large numbers means a deficit "must" be made up (the gambler's fallacy).
- Using $\sigma$ instead of $\sigma/\sqrt n$ when standardizing a sample mean.
- Applying the CLT to a single observation, or to data whose variance is infinite.
- Concluding that the data themselves become normal; only the distribution of the sum or average does.

model:
```python
import random


def simulate_sample_means(n, trials, seed):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    means = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        total = 0.0
        for _ in range(n):
            total += rng.random()
        means.append(total / n)
    return means
```

```python
import math


def normal_approx_tail(n, p, k):
    mean = n * p
    sd = math.sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))
    z = (k - 0.5 - mean) / sd
    return 1 - 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))
```
hint: The mean is `n * p` and the standard deviation is `sqrt(n * p * (1 - p))`.
hint: Standardize `k - 0.5`, then take one minus the normal CDF.
solution: Compute the binomial mean and standard deviation, standardize the continuity-corrected cutoff `k - 0.5`, and return the upper tail $1 - \Phi(z)$.
