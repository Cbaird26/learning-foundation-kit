@article
You have a training budget and must choose two numbers before the first step: how many parameters and how many tokens. If you choose badly, you spend the budget and end with a worse model than you could have had. Scaling laws are the empirical tool for choosing, and they changed the field's habits twice in three years. Here we treat them as what they are: fitted curves, with a form, a range over which they were measured, and an extrapolation risk.

### Power laws

Kaplan and colleagues (2020) trained many transformer language models and reported that the test loss falls as a **power law** in the number of non-embedding parameters $N$, the number of training tokens $D$ and the training compute $C$, over several orders of magnitude, when the other factors are not the bottleneck. A power law has the form $y = a\,x^{-b}$, which is a straight line in log-log space, $\log y = \log a - b \log x$, so the exponent $b$ is the negative slope and can be fitted by linear regression:

```python
import math

def fit_power_law(xs, ys):
    """Least-squares fit of log y = log a - b log x. Returns (a, b)."""
    lx = [math.log(x) for x in xs]
    ly = [math.log(y) for y in ys]
    n = len(xs)
    mx = sum(lx) / n
    my = sum(ly) / n
    sxx = sum((u - mx) ** 2 for u in lx)
    sxy = sum((u - mx) * (v - my) for u, v in zip(lx, ly))
    slope = sxy / sxx
    return math.exp(my - slope * mx), -slope
```

Two measurements already show it: if $y = 3.0$ at $x = 10^3$ and $y = 0.9487$ at $x = 10^5$, then $b = \ln(3.0/0.9487)/\ln(100) \approx 0.25$, and the line predicts $y = 0.3$ at $x = 10^7$. Real losses have an **irreducible** part (the entropy of text), so one fits the reducible part $y = L - E$, and a power law with an offset is not a straight line until the offset is subtracted.

### Compute

Training compute is counted in floating-point operations. For a dense transformer, the forward pass costs about $2N$ FLOPs per token and the backward pass about twice that, so

$$C \approx 6\,N D .$$

This is an **approximation**: it ignores the attention-score FLOPs, which grow with context length, and the embedding parameters, and it assumes dense layers. Training $N = 2.5\times 10^{9}$ parameters on $D = 5 \times 10^{10}$ tokens costs about $7.5 \times 10^{20}$ FLOPs.

### A parametric loss and the compute-optimal split

Hoffmann and colleagues (2022) trained over four hundred models of different sizes on different amounts of data and modeled the loss as

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^{\alpha}} + \frac{B}{D^{\beta}} ,$$

an irreducible term plus a penalty for too few parameters plus a penalty for too little data. The constants below are **invented for teaching** and are not fitted values from any paper: you will always be given the constants you need.

Given a budget $C$ and the constraint $D = C/(6N)$, minimize over $N$. Substituting, $L(N) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B (C/6)^{-\beta} N^{\beta}$. Setting the derivative to zero gives $\alpha A N^{-\alpha-1} = \beta B (C/6)^{-\beta} N^{\beta-1}$, so

$$N_{\text{opt}} = \Big(\frac{\alpha A}{\beta B}\Big)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+\beta}} \Big(\frac{C}{6}\Big)^{\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}}, \qquad D_{\text{opt}} = \frac{C}{6 N_{\text{opt}}}.$$

Both grow as a power of $C$, with exponents $\beta/(\alpha+\beta)$ and $\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)$ that sum to 1. With $E=1.5$, $A=B=400$, $\alpha=\beta=0.3$ and $C = 6\times 10^{18}$, $N_{\text{opt}} = D_{\text{opt}} = 10^{9}$ and $L = 3.096$. Putting the same compute into $N = 4\times 10^9$ and $D = 2.5\times 10^{8}$ gives $L = 3.236$: worse, though the model is four times larger.

### What was found

Kaplan and colleagues reported that, at fixed compute, loss is improved most by growing the model quickly and training it on comparatively little data, and that larger models are more sample-efficient. Hoffmann and colleagues reached a different allocation: model size and tokens should grow in roughly equal proportion, and they showed that a smaller model trained on more tokens outperformed a much larger model trained with the same compute. A ratio of about 20 tokens per parameter is often quoted from their fits; it is a summary of one fit and depends on the details of data and fitting. Hoffmann and colleagues pointed to the learning-rate schedule as one reason their results differ from Kaplan and colleagues, and later analyses also pointed to how parameters are counted; treat the exact exponents as uncertain and the form of the analysis as the lasting lesson.

### Limits

First, the laws describe loss on a distribution of text, not any particular ability; what loss implies for downstream tasks is the subject of the next lesson. Second, the fits are measured over a finite range, and extrapolating several orders of magnitude beyond it assumes the form keeps holding. Third, compute-optimal for training is not optimal for use: a model that will serve many requests may deserve more training tokens than the training-only optimum, because inference cost grows with $N$. Fourth, the fits assume fresh tokens; when data must be repeated, or its quality varies, the formulas change. Whether power-law scaling continues indefinitely, and what the curves would be for other modalities and data regimes, are open questions.

@example
title: Choosing a model size for a budget
problem: Use $L(N,D) = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}$ with $E = 1.5$, $A = B = 400$, $\alpha = \beta = 0.3$ and $C = 6 N D$. For $C = 2.4 \times 10^{19}$ FLOPs, find $N_{\text{opt}}$, $D_{\text{opt}}$ and the loss.
step: Since $\alpha = \beta$ and $A = B$, $\alpha A/(\beta B) = 1$, so $N_{\text{opt}} = (C/6)^{1/2}$.
step: $C/6 = 4 \times 10^{18}$, so $N_{\text{opt}} = 2 \times 10^{9}$ and $D_{\text{opt}} = 4\times10^{18}/(2\times10^{9}) = 2\times 10^{9}$.
step: Loss: $(2\times 10^9)^{-0.3} \approx 1.6207 \times 10^{-3}$, so each of the two terms is $400 \times 0.0016207 = 0.6483$, and the sum is $1.5 + 0.6483 + 0.6483 \approx 2.797$.
step: Check against a neighbor: $N = 4 \times 10^9$, $D = 1 \times 10^9$ gives $1.5 + 400(4\times10^{9})^{-0.3} + 400(10^{9})^{-0.3} \approx 2.825$, which is higher.
answer: $N_{\text{opt}} = D_{\text{opt}} = 2\times 10^{9}$, loss about 2.797.

@example
title: Fitting an exponent from three points
problem: Reducible loss is measured at $N = 10^{6}, 10^{7}, 10^{8}$ as $2.0, 1.0, 0.5$. Fit $y = a N^{-b}$.
step: Each factor of 10 in $N$ halves $y$, so $10^{-b} = 1/2$.
step: Then $b = \log_{10} 2 \approx 0.301$.
step: Solve for $a$ at $N = 10^6$: $a = 2.0 \times (10^{6})^{0.301} = 2.0 \times 10^{1.806} \approx 128$.
answer: $b \approx 0.301$ and $a \approx 128$; the prediction at $N = 10^{9}$ is $0.25$.

@mistakes
- Fitting a power law to the total loss when the loss has an irreducible floor: subtract the offset first or fit it as a parameter.
- Treating $C = 6ND$ as exact: it is an approximation that drops attention-score FLOPs and embeddings.
- Equating compute-optimal with best for deployment: serving cost depends on $N$, so training-optimal is not use-optimal.
- Extrapolating a fit far beyond the range in which it was measured, and then reading the result as a prediction of abilities rather than of loss.

model:
```python
def best_split(C, A, alpha, B, beta, grid, E=0.0):
    """Return (N, D) from grid minimizing E + A/N**alpha + B/D**beta."""
    best = None
    for n in sorted(grid):
        d = C / (6 * n)
        loss = E + A / n ** alpha + B / d ** beta
        if best is None or loss < best[0]:
            best = (loss, n, d)
    return best[1], best[2]
```
hint: Loop over the sizes in increasing order, compute `D = C / (6 * N)` for each, and keep the first size that achieves a new minimum.
solution: Computing `D` from the budget for each candidate makes every candidate cost the same compute. Looping in increasing order and updating only on a strictly smaller loss returns the smaller size on a tie. The last test passes the grid out of order, which catches a version that scans the list as given.

model:
```python
import math


def size_for_target(xs, ys, E, target):
    """Fit y = E + a * x**-b in log space; return x where it reaches target."""
    lx = [math.log(x) for x in xs]
    ly = [math.log(y - E) for y in ys]
    n = len(xs)
    mx = sum(lx) / n
    my = sum(ly) / n
    sxx = sum((u - mx) ** 2 for u in lx)
    sxy = sum((u - mx) * (v - my) for u, v in zip(lx, ly))
    slope = sxy / sxx
    if target <= E or slope >= 0:
        return None
    intercept = my - slope * mx
    return math.exp((math.log(target - E) - intercept) / slope)
```
hint: Subtract `E` before taking logs and fit a line of log-reducible-loss against log-size; then solve $\ln(\text{target} - E) = \text{intercept} + \text{slope} \cdot \ln x$ for $x$.
solution: After subtracting the offset, the reducible loss is an exact power law in the test data, so the regression recovers its slope and intercept. Inverting the fitted line gives $\ln x = (\ln(\text{target} - E) - \text{intercept})/\text{slope}$; because the slope is negative, a smaller target loss gives a larger size. A target at or below the offset can never be reached, and a fit whose reducible loss does not fall with size has no size that reaches the target, so both return `None`.

@quiz self
prompt: A group fits a power law to the loss of models up to $10^{9}$ parameters and announces that a model of $10^{12}$ parameters "will" have a loss of a stated value, hence will "master" a particular task. Write a short critique naming at least three distinct problems with this inference.
hint: Separate the range of the data, the fitted form, and the link from loss to the task.
solution: First, the claim extrapolates three orders of magnitude beyond the measured range; a fit says what the curve did where it was measured and a different term can start to matter outside it. Second, the form is itself a choice: a power law with an offset, a different offset, or a bend would fit the measured points about equally well and diverge when extrapolated, and the stated uncertainty should come from comparing fits, not from the best one. Third, the fit is for loss on a text distribution, and a threshold for a task such as "mastering" it depends on the metric and on how loss on that task's text relates to task success, which is itself the open question of the next lesson; a loss number does not translate to a task claim without a measured link. Fourth, the loss law assumes the data, the training recipe and the tokens per parameter stay compatible with the fit, and a run at $10^{12}$ parameters may be data-limited or may use different settings. A better statement is a prediction with an interval, validated by predicting a held-out larger run before claiming the extrapolation.
