@article
A model reports a loss of 2.3 and another reports 0.95. Which is better? You cannot tell until you know the unit (nats or bits), what was divided by (tokens, characters, bytes), and which tokenizer produced the tokens. The first half of this lesson makes those units precise. The second half is about a different puzzle: why the same model and data can train smoothly or fall apart depending on a few numbers that rarely appear in a paper's abstract.

### Objectives

**Causal language modeling** (next-token prediction) minimizes the average negative log-likelihood of each token given those before it:

$$L(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t_i \mid t_{<i}).$$

This is cross-entropy between the empirical next-token distribution and the model, in nats because $\log$ is natural. Its minimum over all models is the conditional entropy of the text, which is above zero because text is genuinely uncertain: no model can drive $L$ to 0 on natural text, and the gap to that floor is unknown.

**Masked language modeling** was used by BERT (Devlin and colleagues, 2018): a fraction of tokens (15 percent in that paper) is hidden, and the model predicts the hidden tokens from context on both sides, with the loss computed only at the hidden positions. It yields bidirectional representations useful for classification, but it is not a left-to-right probability model of a whole sequence. **Denoising** and **span-corruption** objectives (for example the text-to-text transformer of Raffel and colleagues, 2019) hide spans and have an encoder-decoder model regenerate them. Which objective is best depends on the use, and the dominance of causal models for generation reflects a mix of convenience and evidence, not a theorem.

### Loss, perplexity and bits per byte

The **perplexity** is $\mathrm{PPL} = e^{L}$ with $L$ in nats. It is the inverse of the geometric mean of the probabilities given to the true tokens, and it can be read as the effective number of equally likely choices per token: a uniform model over a vocabulary of $V$ tokens has perplexity exactly $V$, and a model that is always certain and right has perplexity 1. Perplexity depends on the tokenizer, because a better tokenizer makes each token carry more text. To compare models with different tokenizers, normalize by the text itself: with $L$ nats per token and $b$ bytes per token on average,

$$\text{bits per byte} = \frac{L}{b \ln 2}.$$

For $L = 2.0$ nats and $b = 4.0$ bytes per token this is $2.0/(4.0 \times 0.6931) \approx 0.721$ bits per byte.

### The optimizer and the schedule

Models of this size are trained with **Adam** (Kingma and Ba, 2014), usually with decoupled weight decay (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2017). Three practices recur.

**Warmup and decay.** The learning rate rises linearly from 0 to $\eta_{\max}$ over $w$ steps and then follows a cosine to $\eta_{\min}$ at step $T$:

$$\eta(t) = \begin{cases} \eta_{\max}\, t/w & t < w \\[2pt] \eta_{\min} + \tfrac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\Big(1 + \cos\!\big(\pi \tfrac{t-w}{T-w}\big)\Big) & t \ge w. \end{cases}$$

With $\eta_{\max} = 3\times 10^{-4}$, $\eta_{\min} = 3\times 10^{-5}$, $w = 100$, $T = 1100$: $\eta(50) = 1.5\times 10^{-4}$ and $\eta(600) = 1.65\times 10^{-4}$, the midpoint. Common explanations for warmup are that early gradient statistics are unreliable and early updates can be destructive; the evidence for the mechanisms is partial.

**Gradient clipping.** If the global gradient norm $g = \lVert \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} L \rVert_2$ exceeds a threshold $c$, the whole gradient is multiplied by $c/g$. This shrinks its length and leaves its direction unchanged.

**Batch size.** Tokens per step are batch size times sequence length: $512 \times 2048 = 1{,}048{,}576$, so $10^{11}$ training tokens take about 95,367 steps.

### Memory of training

Per parameter, a common mixed-precision recipe stores bf16 weights (2 bytes), bf16 gradients (2), and fp32 master weights, first moment and second moment (4 + 4 + 4): **16 bytes per parameter**. This is one convention, and others (for example without a master copy, or with sharded states) differ. For $10^9$ parameters that is 16 GB (with $1\text{ GB} = 10^{9}$ bytes) before counting activations, which depend on batch size and sequence length. This is why the memory to train is many times the memory to run.

### Instabilities

Loss spikes (a sudden jump after smooth progress, sometimes recovering and sometimes not) occur at scale, and teams respond with lower learning rates, clipping, normalization changes, and restarting from a checkpoint with the offending data skipped. Their causes are not fully understood; attributions to specific mechanisms are hypotheses supported in some settings. What you can rely on is the practice: log gradient norm, learning rate and loss per step, keep checkpoints, and change one thing at a time.

@example
title: From probabilities to perplexity
problem: A model gives the true tokens of a four-token text the probabilities $0.5, 0.25, 0.125, 0.5$. Find the loss in nats, the perplexity, and the loss in bits per token.
step: Take negative natural logs: $0.693,\ 1.386,\ 2.079,\ 0.693$.
step: Average: $L = (0.693 + 1.386 + 2.079 + 0.693)/4 \approx 1.213$ nats.
step: Perplexity: $e^{1.213} \approx 3.364$. Check with the geometric mean: the product of the probabilities is $1/128$, and $128^{1/4} \approx 3.364$.
step: In bits, divide by $\ln 2$: $1.213/0.6931 \approx 1.750$ bits per token, which equals $\log_2 3.364$.
answer: $L \approx 1.213$ nats, perplexity $\approx 3.364$, about 1.75 bits per token.

@example
title: Clipping a gradient
problem: A gradient has the components $(3, 4)$. The clipping threshold is $c = 2.5$. What gradient is used for the update?
step: The norm is $g = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = 5$, which exceeds $c = 2.5$.
step: Scale by $c/g = 0.5$: the clipped gradient is $(1.5, 2)$, with norm 2.5.
answer: $(1.5, 2)$; the direction is the same and the length is halved.

@mistakes
- Comparing perplexities across tokenizers: convert to bits per byte first.
- Mixing nats and bits: a loss of 2.0 nats is 2.885 bits, and exponentiating a bits-valued loss with $e$ gives a wrong perplexity (use base 2 for bits).
- Believing clipping changes the gradient's direction: it only rescales the whole vector.
- Estimating training memory from the weights alone: gradients and optimizer state often cost several times more than the weights.

@practice num
prompt: A schedule warms up linearly for 200 steps to $\eta_{\max} = 6\times 10^{-4}$ and then decays with a cosine to $\eta_{\min} = 0$ at step $T = 2200$, following the formula in the article. What is the learning rate at step 1700? Give the answer in units of $10^{-4}$, to two decimal places.

check: 6 * 0.5 * (1 + cos(pi * (1700 - 200) / (2200 - 200)))
hint: The progress through the decay is $(1700 - 200)/(2200 - 200)$; the cosine factor is $\tfrac12(1 + \cos(\pi \cdot \text{progress}))$.
solution: Progress is $1500/2000 = 0.75$. Then $\cos(0.75\pi) = -0.7071$, so the factor is $0.5(1 - 0.7071) = 0.1464$. Multiplying by $6 \times 10^{-4}$ gives $8.79 \times 10^{-5}$, or 0.88 in units of $10^{-4}$.

@quiz self
prompt: During a long training run the loss falls smoothly and then jumps up sharply at one step. Describe how you would investigate it, what you would log beforehand so that you can, and what you would and would not conclude from a single run.
hint: Think about what to compare before and after the spike, and whether the spike is tied to the data, the optimizer, or the numerics.
solution: First check whether it is a numerical or data event. The logs that make this possible are the per-step loss, the global gradient norm before clipping, the learning rate, and the identity of the batch (so that it can be replayed). If the gradient norm rose sharply before the loss, the optimizer is implicated; if a single batch has extreme loss, the data are implicated, for example a corrupted document. I would restart from the last checkpoint before the spike with the same data order, then again with the offending batch removed, and then with a lower learning rate or tighter clipping, changing one thing at a time. If the spike recurs on the same batch, the data are the cause; if it recurs at the same step with different data, the optimizer settings are. From a single run I would conclude only what the interventions showed in that run; a mechanism such as a particular layer's instability would remain a hypothesis until it predicted the outcome of an intervention in several runs with different seeds.
