@project
title: A small language model study
overview: Train small character-level or word-level transformer language models on a public-domain text, measure how the validation loss changes with model size and with data size, fit a simple scaling curve, implement two decoding methods, and write a short paper that says what the measurements do and do not show about much larger models.
goal: Build and train a small transformer language model, and count its parameters correctly from its configuration.
goal: Measure validation loss against model size (and against data size) and fit a scaling curve with an irreducible-loss term, with honest uncertainty.
goal: Implement temperature sampling and top-p sampling from raw logits, and compare them with greedy decoding on your own samples.
goal: Write a short, honest paper: what was measured, what the fit supports, and what cannot be concluded about larger models.
step: Choose a public-domain text of at least one million characters (for example a long novel from a public-domain library). Fix a split before any experiment: the first 90 percent for training and the last 10 percent for validation. Report the character count and, for a character model, the number of distinct characters; for a word-level model, build the vocabulary from the training text only.
step: Write the parameter-count function from the configuration (width, layers, feed-forward width, vocabulary, context length, tied or untied embeddings), by hand and without a library, and state your conventions. Check it against your framework's own count on the small configuration in the material below, and report both counts.
step: Train at least five model sizes spanning a factor of at least 30 in non-embedding parameters, with identical data, context length, optimizer and learning-rate schedule (warmup then decay), the same number of tokens for each, and at least two random seeds per size. Log the validation loss in nats per token and also in bits per character (or per byte) for the final model of each size.
step: Fit the loss against non-embedding parameters with the form $L = E + A N^{-\alpha}$. Write the fit without a scientific library: for each candidate $E$ on a grid below your smallest observed loss, fit $\log(L - E)$ against $\log N$ by least squares, and keep the $E$ with the smallest residual. Check your fit code on the supplied test table first. Report $E$, $A$, $\alpha$ and a measure of uncertainty (for example, the range of fits over the two seeds or over leaving out each point in turn).
step: Repeat for data size: train one fixed model size on 10, 20, 50 and 100 percent of the training text, and describe how the validation loss changes. If the model overfits the smaller subsets, say so, and show training and validation loss together.
step: Implement greedy decoding, temperature sampling and top-p sampling from the final logits, in plain Python or NumPy, without any library sampling function. Check them against the supplied logit table. Generate at least three samples of 300 characters from each method and each of two prompts, and describe the differences you see, including repetition.
step: Write the paper (four to six pages): setup, a table of measurements, the fit with its uncertainty, the samples, and a discussion. The discussion must say what the fit supports within your range, what it cannot say about models many orders of magnitude larger (extrapolation, different data, different training budgets), and which of your choices could have changed the result.

@review
**Week 1. The transformer language model.** A decoder-only model is embeddings, $L$ blocks of causal attention plus an MLP with residual connections and layer norms, and an output matrix. Attention scores are $\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d_k}$ plus a causal mask. Count parameters per matrix and state conventions: tied embeddings, biases, layer norms.

**Week 2. Tokenization and data.** BPE counts adjacent pairs weighted by word frequency, merges the most frequent, and is encoded by replaying merges in order. Vocabulary size trades embedding parameters against sequence length. Pipelines filter and deduplicate; quality, memorization and rights are contested.

**Week 3. Objectives and dynamics.** Loss in nats, perplexity $e^L$, bits per byte $L/(b\ln2)$. Warmup then cosine decay, gradient clipping rescales without changing direction, mixed-precision training costs about 16 bytes per parameter under the stated recipe.

**Week 4. Scaling laws.** $C \approx 6ND$ is an approximation. For $L = E + A N^{-\alpha} + B D^{-\beta}$, the optimum is $N_{\text{opt}} = (\alpha A/\beta B)^{1/(\alpha+\beta)}(C/6)^{\beta/(\alpha+\beta)}$. Kaplan and colleagues (2020) versus Hoffmann and colleagues (2022). Extrapolation is a risk.

**Week 5. Emergence.** Exact match on $k$ tokens is $p^k$; metrics can create sharp curves. Whether any ability is sudden under every metric is open.

**Week 6. Fine-tuning.** Mask the prompt in the SFT loss; normalization matters; LoRA trains $2dr$ parameters per square matrix.

**Week 7. RLHF.** Bradley-Terry loss $-\log\sigma(r_w - r_l)$; KL-regularized optimum $\pi^* \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}$; reward overoptimization.

**Week 8. DPO.** Implicit reward $\beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$; the partition function cancels; loss $\ln 2$ at initialization.

**Week 9. In-context learning and retrieval.** Weights are fixed; mechanisms are debated. Cosine, BM25, recall at $k$, MRR.

**Week 10. Tools and agents.** Success $p^n$; context accumulates; prompt injection is bounded by least privilege, not removed.

**Week 11. Inference.** KV cache is exact: $2Ln_{\text{kv}}d_{\text{head}}sbq$ bytes. Quantization, top-p, speculative decoding.

**Week 12. Evaluation.** Intervals, paired tests, pass@$k$, contamination checks and their limits, hallucination, calibration, open problems.

**Self-check.** Can you count parameters from a configuration and say your conventions? Convert a loss between nats, bits and perplexity? Find the compute-optimal split for given constants? Explain why exact match can make a smooth ability look sudden? Derive the DPO loss? Compute recall at $k$? Compute a KV cache size?

**Common errors.** Counting tied embeddings twice; comparing perplexity across tokenizers; treating $6ND$ as exact; reading a loss law as a prediction of a skill; confusing reward differences with reward values; calling the KV cache an approximation; treating a clean n-gram check as proof of no contamination.

@exam num
prompt: A decoder-only transformer has $d = 512$, $L = 8$, $d_{\text{ff}} = 2048$, vocabulary $V = 32{,}000$, learned positions with $n_{\text{ctx}} = 1024$ and tied embeddings. Count weight matrices only, and count all of them, embeddings included, as the parameters $N$. It is trained on $D = 20N$ tokens. Using $C \approx 6ND$, how many FLOPs is that? Give the answer in FLOPs to three significant figures.
answer: 2.12e17
tol: 1%
unit: FLOPs
check: 120 * (32000 * 512 + 1024 * 512 + 8 * (4 * 512 ** 2 + 2 * 512 * 2048)) ** 2
hint: First count $N$: embeddings $Vd + n_{\text{ctx}}d$, plus $L$ blocks of $4d^2 + 2d\,d_{\text{ff}}$. Then $C = 6N(20N) = 120N^2$.
solution: Embeddings: $32{,}000 \cdot 512 = 16{,}384{,}000$ and $1024 \cdot 512 = 524{,}288$. One block: $4 \cdot 512^2 + 2 \cdot 512 \cdot 2048 = 1{,}048{,}576 + 2{,}097{,}152 = 3{,}145{,}728$; eight blocks give $25{,}165{,}824$. So $N = 42{,}074{,}112$. Then $C = 120 N^2 \approx 2.12 \times 10^{17}$ FLOPs; this is an approximation, and using only non-embedding parameters would give a smaller $N$ and a smaller $C$.

@exam ms
prompt: Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: In the DPO derivation the partition function $Z(x)$ cancels, because the Bradley-Terry model depends only on the difference of the rewards of two responses to the same prompt.
choice*: If a model's per-token accuracy improves smoothly while exact match on long answers jumps sharply, that is consistent with independent per-token errors, since exact match is the per-token accuracy raised to the answer length.
choice*: Byte-pair encoding applies its learned merges in the order they were learned when it encodes new text.
choice: Per-token perplexity can be compared directly across models with different tokenizers if they are evaluated on the same text.
choice: The key-value cache approximates attention by keeping only recent keys and values, which is why it saves memory.
choice: An n-gram overlap check that finds no overlap shows that a benchmark was not in the training data.
hint: Recall what each result states and what its assumptions are.
solution: The partition function depends only on the prompt, so it cancels in $r_w - r_l$. Under independent per-token errors, exact match on $k$ tokens is $p^k$, which can look sudden while $p$ is smooth. BPE replays merges in training order. Per-token perplexity depends on how much text a token carries, so it is not comparable across tokenizers (bits per byte is). The key-value cache is exact and costs memory, not saves it. A clean overlap check does not detect paraphrases or translations.

@exam self
prompt: Timed answer (about 30 minutes). A lab fine-tunes a base model on a collection of instructions and reports that its accuracy on a new reasoning benchmark rose from 12 percent to 58 percent. It states: "Instruction tuning gave the model a new reasoning ability, which emerges at this scale." Write a referee report on this claim. Give at least four distinct concerns, say which measurements or controls would address each, and state what conclusion the evidence could support if all the controls came out favorably.
hint: Consider the metric and its sharpness, the format effect of tuning, contamination of the benchmark by the tuning mixture or pretraining data, noise and prompt sensitivity, and the single scale.
solution: Concern 1, the metric. If the benchmark is scored by exact match on multi-step answers, a smooth gain in per-step accuracy produces a sharp jump in the score, so "emerges" may describe the metric; I would rescore with partial credit and with the log-probability of the correct answer and ask whether those also jump. Concern 2, format. Tuning teaches the format and habit of answering, which a strict scorer rewards, whereas the base model may have produced the right content in an unscorable form; I would score leniently with normalization and test the base model with few-shot examples of the format to see how much of the gap closes. Concern 3, contamination. The instruction mixture or the pretraining data may contain the benchmark's items or close copies; I would run n-gram overlap analyses on both, evaluate on freshly written items after the data cutoff, and report clean and flagged subsets separately, noting that paraphrases evade an overlap check. Concern 4, statistics and prompts. A single run on a benchmark of unknown size has no interval; I would report confidence intervals, use a paired comparison on shared items, and average over several prompt templates and seeds. Concern 5, scale: the claim of emergence "at this scale" rests on a model at one scale; showing emergence needs several sizes, fine-grained, with the same recipe, to see the curve. If the controls came out favorably (the jump survives continuous metrics, lenient scoring, fresh items, intervals and prompt variation), the evidence would support the narrower statement that, for this benchmark and recipe, tuning improved performance on this kind of reasoning task beyond format effects; it would not establish a general new ability, say what mechanism changed, or predict other scales.
rubric: Names at least four distinct concerns, among metric sharpness, format effects, contamination, statistics and prompt variation, and single-scale evidence.
rubric: Gives a concrete control or measurement for each concern.
rubric: States a conclusion appropriately limited to what the controlled evidence could support.
