@article
A language model never sees text. It sees integers, and the map from text to integers is chosen before training and never learned by gradient descent. That choice decides how long every sequence is, how many parameters sit in the embedding, which words are cheap and which are expensive, and which strings the model cannot even represent. Treat the tokenizer as part of the model.

### Why not words or characters

A word-level vocabulary is short per text but cannot represent a word it has not seen, so rare words, names and typos become a single "unknown" symbol. A character-level vocabulary represents everything, but sequences become long, and attention costs grow with the square of length. **Subword** tokenization sits between them: frequent strings become single tokens, rare strings are spelled out from pieces.

### Byte-pair encoding

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) was adapted to subword units for translation by Sennrich, Haddow and Birch (2016). Training on a corpus of word counts:

1. Start with each word as a sequence of single symbols (characters, or bytes).
2. Count every adjacent pair of symbols, weighting each occurrence by its word's frequency.
3. Merge the most frequent pair into a new symbol, and record the merge.
4. Repeat until the vocabulary has the wanted size.

To encode new text, apply the recorded merges **in the order they were learned**. Take the words cat (6 occurrences), cats (3), cart (4), car (6) and art (2):

| step | pair counts (top) | merge |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | (c,a) 19; (a,r) 12; (a,t) 9; (r,t) 6; (t,s) 3 | c + a → ca |
| 2 | (ca,r) 10; (ca,t) 9; (r,t) 6; (t,s) 3; (a,r) 2 | ca + r → car |
| 3 | (ca,t) 9; (car,t) 4; (t,s) 3; (a,r) 2; (r,t) 2 | ca + t → cat |

The count 19 is $6+3+4+6$: the pair (c,a) occurs once in each of those four words, weighted by frequency. In code:

```python
from collections import Counter

def count_pairs(words):
    """words maps a tuple of symbols to its corpus frequency."""
    counts = Counter()
    for symbols, freq in words.items():
        for pair in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
            counts[pair] += freq
    return counts

def merge_pair(words, pair):
    """Replace each adjacent occurrence of pair by one merged symbol."""
    merged = {}
    for symbols, freq in words.items():
        out, i = [], 0
        while i < len(symbols):
            if i + 1 < len(symbols) and (symbols[i], symbols[i + 1]) == pair:
                out.append(symbols[i] + symbols[i + 1])
                i += 2
            else:
                out.append(symbols[i])
                i += 1
        merged[tuple(out)] = freq
    return merged
```

Variants exist: WordPiece (used by BERT, Devlin and colleagues, 2018) chooses merges by a likelihood criterion; the unigram language-model tokenizer and the SentencePiece library (Kudo, 2018; Kudo and Richardson, 2018) work on raw text without pre-splitting into words. **Byte-level** BPE starts from the 256 byte values, so any string can be encoded and nothing is "unknown"; it was used by the GPT-2 line of models (Radford and colleagues, 2019).

### What a vocabulary costs

A vocabulary of size $V$ costs $Vd$ embedding parameters (twice that if the output matrix is untied) and makes the final softmax wider. A larger $V$ shortens sequences, since more strings are one token. The compression is measured in characters (or bytes) per token. If a tokenizer lifts it from 3.6 to 3.9 characters per token, the same text has $1 - 3.6/3.9 \approx 7.7\%$ fewer tokens, and attention score matrices shrink by about 15 percent, since they scale with $n^2$. Against that, going from $V = 32{,}000$ to $64{,}000$ at $d = 1024$ adds 32,768,000 parameters. There is no formula for the best size; it is an empirical trade-off that interacts with model size.

Tokenization has consequences that look like model behavior. A tokenizer trained mostly on one language splits other languages into more tokens per word, so the same context window holds less text and each word costs more to process. Numbers may split inconsistently, and leading spaces change token identity. Per-token loss is not comparable across tokenizers, a point we use in lesson 3.

### Pretraining data

A typical pipeline has these stages: collect text (web crawls, books, code, reference text), extract text from markup, identify language, filter for quality with heuristics or learned classifiers, remove personal data and unwanted content, and **deduplicate**. Exact deduplication hashes documents; near-duplicate detection, often with MinHash, finds documents that differ slightly. Lee and colleagues (2021) reported that deduplicating training data reduced verbatim memorization and improved models, and extraction of memorized training sequences from language models was shown by Carlini and colleagues (2021).

Open questions are many. What makes data "high quality" is defined by the filter, and a filter built on what a reference corpus looks like embeds that corpus's biases. How much repetition of data is harmful, how to mix sources, and how data affects abilities are studied but not settled. Whether and when training on copyrighted text is permitted is contested and differs across jurisdictions; this is a legal and policy question that research cannot answer by itself. Finally, because evaluation sets are also text on the web, pretraining data and benchmarks can overlap, which returns in lesson 12.

For a 10,000-character document, how many more embedding parameters does the larger tokenizer need, and how many fewer tokens does the document have?
step: Extra parameters: $(64{,}000 - 32{,}000) \times 1024 = 32{,}768{,}000$.
step: Tokens: $10{,}000/3.6 \approx 2778$ and $10{,}000/3.9 \approx 2564$.

@mistakes
- Counting each pair once per word type instead of weighting by the word's frequency: the most frequent pair in the corpus is not the pair in the most word types.
- Encoding new text by repeatedly merging whichever pair seems best: encoding must replay the learned merges in training order.
- Comparing perplexity across models with different tokenizers: a token of one tokenizer is not a token of another.
- Treating deduplication as one step: exact copies and near-copies are different problems with different tools.

model:
```python
def encode(word, merges):
    """Apply BPE merges, in learned order, to the characters of word."""
    symbols = list(word)
    for left, right in merges:
        out = []
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols):
            if (i + 1 < len(symbols) and symbols[i] == left
                    and symbols[i + 1] == right):
                out.append(left + right)
                i += 2
            else:
                out.append(symbols[i])
                i += 1
        symbols = out
    return symbols
```
hint: Loop over merges in the given order; inside, rebuild the symbol list with a while loop that jumps two positions after a merge.
solution: Order matters: with merges a+b then b+c, the word abc becomes ab, c, but with the order reversed it becomes a, bc. Jumping two positions after a merge gives non-overlapping merges left to right, so aaa becomes aa, a.

@quiz self
prompt: You are asked to filter a web crawl before pretraining. Propose three stages of the pipeline, and then defend one choice that involves a real trade-off, naming what could go wrong with it.
hint: Think about what a quality classifier is trained on and what it will therefore prefer.
solution: Stages: first, extract text and identify language, discarding documents too short or mostly markup; second, exact and near-duplicate removal, so that repeated pages do not dominate and verbatim memorization falls; third, a quality filter that scores documents. The quality filter has a real trade-off. A classifier trained to separate reference-like text from random crawl text raises the average polish of the data and may help downstream scores, but it prefers the style and topics of its reference set, so it can remove dialects, informal registers and subjects that the reference underrepresents, and it can push the model's coverage in one direction. One defense is to measure what each filter removes by topic and dialect and to keep a small unfiltered sample as a control. Whether the gain in measured quality is worth the narrowing is an empirical question for the intended use, and it is not settled in general.
