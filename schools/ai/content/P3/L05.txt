@article
Plot accuracy on a task against model size. At the left, accuracy sits near zero or at chance for many sizes. Then, over a narrow range, it climbs. Does the model suddenly acquire something, as water suddenly freezes, or is the sharp curve an artifact of how the plot was made? This question matters for science, because a sudden ability cannot be predicted by extrapolating from smaller models, and it matters for planning, because unpredictable abilities are harder to prepare for. It is open, and the useful skill is to know exactly what each side claims and what evidence would separate them.

### The observation and the definition

The few-shot results of GPT-3 (Brown and colleagues, 2020) showed that performance on many tasks improved with model size, and that some tasks stayed near chance for small models and rose for large ones. Wei and colleagues (2022) gathered such cases and called an ability **emergent** if it is absent in smaller models and present in larger ones, so that the improvement could not have been predicted by extrapolating the smaller models' curve. The definition has two parts: a sharp change, and unpredictability from smaller scales. Both parts are about a measured curve, and a measured curve depends on a metric.

### The metric argument

Consider a task whose answer is $k$ tokens long and is scored by **exact match**: the answer counts only if all $k$ tokens are right. Suppose the model's per-token accuracy $p$ improves smoothly with scale, and suppose, as a simplification, that tokens are independent. Then

$$\text{exact match} = p^{k}.$$

For $k = 10$ the values are $p = 0.5, 0.7, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99$ giving $0.001, 0.028, 0.349, 0.599, 0.904$. A smooth rise in $p$ from 0.5 to 0.99 produces an exact-match curve that sits near zero for a long stretch and then climbs steeply. A metric that gives partial credit, such as per-token accuracy or the log-probability of the correct answer, shows the same models improving gradually. Schaeffer and colleagues (2023) made this argument and reported that, for several tasks, switching to continuous metrics removed the apparent jump, and that they could induce apparent emergence in other settings by choosing a discontinuous metric. Multiple-choice tasks add a second effect: with four options, accuracy is $q + (1-q)/4$ if the model truly knows a fraction $q$ of the items and guesses on the rest, so accuracy is flat at 0.25 until $q$ becomes appreciable.

### The case that something real remains

The metric argument does not show that nothing is sudden, for several reasons.

- **The metric may be the point.** A user who needs the whole answer right cares about exact match, and an ability that crosses a usability threshold at a particular scale is emergent in the sense that matters, even if the underlying per-token improvement is smooth.
- **Smooth in one coordinate does not mean predictable.** Knowing that $p$ is smooth helps only if you can forecast $p$ at larger scale, and for many tasks that was not done before the fact.
- **Sudden changes during training exist.** Olsson and colleagues (2022) reported an abrupt change in training, tied to the formation of induction heads (attention circuits that complete repeated patterns), which coincided with a jump in in-context learning. That is a change over training time, not over model size, but it shows that discontinuities in capability can occur.
- **Loss may not summarize everything.** Average loss over a text distribution can be smooth while loss on the rare tokens that matter for a particular skill changes quickly.

### What is known and what is open

Known: metric choice can create or erase apparent jumps, and any claim of emergence needs the metric stated. Also known: small test sets make near-zero accuracies hard to read, since the standard error of an accuracy $\hat{p}$ on $n$ items is $\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$, so $\hat{p} = 0.02$ on 500 items is $0.020 \pm 0.006$.

Open: whether any ability has a discontinuity that survives every reasonable metric; how to forecast an ability's onset before training a larger model; and how the answers depend on training data and on evaluation prompts. Researchers disagree, and a reasonable summary is that the strongest versions of both claims, that emergence is always real and that it is always an artifact, are not established by the evidence so far.

### How to test the claim

Evaluate with several metrics (exact match, partial credit, log-likelihood of the answer), at many closely spaced scales with several seeds, on enough items that confidence intervals are narrow, with the prompt held fixed and checked for contamination. If the jump persists across all metrics and sits outside the intervals, the metric explanation fails for that task; if it disappears under a continuous metric, the sharp curve was a property of the scoring rule.

@example
title: A smooth ability, a sudden curve
problem: Per-token accuracy at model sizes $10^{8}, 10^{9}, 10^{10}, 10^{11}$ is $0.119, 0.378, 0.731, 0.924$. An answer is 5 tokens long and scored by exact match. Compute exact match at each size under the independence assumption.
step: Exact match is $p^{5}$.
step: $0.119^5 \approx 0.00002$, $0.378^5 \approx 0.0077$, $0.731^5 \approx 0.209$, $0.924^5 \approx 0.674$.
step: Per-token accuracy rises by $0.26$, $0.35$ and $0.19$ over the three decades, a smooth curve that levels off near 1. Exact match is under 1 percent for two of the four sizes, then 21 percent and 67 percent. On a plot, it looks like an ability arriving between $10^{9}$ and $10^{11}$.
answer: Exact match is about $0.00002, 0.0077, 0.209, 0.674$ while per-token accuracy rises smoothly.

@example
title: Working backward from exact match
problem: A model scores 0.20 exact match on 5-token answers. If tokens are independent, what is its per-token accuracy? What exact match would it need on 5-token answers to have per-token accuracy 0.9?
step: Per-token accuracy is the fifth root of the exact match: $0.20^{1/5} \approx 0.725$.
step: For per-token accuracy 0.9, exact match is $0.9^5 \approx 0.590$.
step: The model's exact-match score of 0.20 hides that it is already right on about 72.5 percent of tokens.
answer: About 0.725 per token; exact match 0.590 for per-token accuracy 0.9.

@mistakes
- Treating a sharp curve as evidence of a sharp change in the model without checking the metric: exact match raises a smooth quantity to a power.
- Treating the metric argument as proof that no ability is sudden: it shows that metrics can produce jumps, not that every jump is produced by them.
- Reading near-zero accuracy on a few dozen items as zero ability: the sampling error may be as large as the score.
- Confusing a change over model size with a change over training time: induction heads show a transition during training, which is a different axis.

@quiz self
prompt: Design an experiment to decide whether a particular ability, say multi-digit addition, emerges sharply with model size or only appears sharp under exact match. State the metrics, the models, the number of items, and what result would support each view.
hint: Make a prediction for each view that the data could contradict.
solution: Train or obtain models at many closely spaced sizes (at least eight over two to three decades of parameters), several seeds each, with identical training data and recipe, so that the only change is scale. Create several thousand addition problems of varying digit length, check them for overlap with training text, and fix the prompt. Score each model with exact match, with per-digit accuracy, and with the log-probability of the correct answer, and put confidence intervals on every point. The metric account predicts that per-digit accuracy and log-probability improve smoothly while exact match is a power of per-digit accuracy; I would test that by checking whether exact match is predicted by the measured per-digit accuracy raised to the answer length. The sudden-change account predicts that the per-digit or log-probability curve itself bends sharply at a scale, outside the intervals, and that this sharpness is not a function of the metric. Mixed outcomes would also be informative: for example per-digit accuracy that is smooth for easy digits and abrupt for carries would point to a specific sub-skill that changes abruptly. Whichever way it comes out, the claim is limited to this task, this training recipe and this range of sizes.
