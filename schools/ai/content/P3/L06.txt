@article
A pretrained model is a very good continuer of text. Ask it a question and it may continue with more questions, because a list of questions is a likely continuation of a question. To get a model that answers, we fine-tune it: we continue training on examples of the behavior we want. The method is a small change to the pretraining loss, and almost everything interesting is in the data, in what the changed loss does to the rest of the model, and in how we measure the result.

### Supervised fine-tuning

In **supervised fine-tuning** (SFT) each example is a prompt $x$ and a desired response $y = (y_1, \dots, y_m)$. The model is trained to maximize the likelihood of the response given the prompt:

$$\ell(x, y) = -\sum_{j=1}^{m} \log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y_j \mid x, y_{<j}).$$

The prompt tokens are inputs and are usually **masked out of the loss**: the gradient comes only from response tokens, and the prompt still serves as context in attention. Practice varies, and some pipelines also train on the prompt. How to **normalize** is a real choice. The pooled token mean divides the total over all response tokens in the batch by their number, so long responses count in proportion to their length. The per-sequence mean divides each example's loss by its own response length first and then averages, so every example counts equally. With one three-token response whose log-probabilities are $-0.4, -0.6, -0.2$ and one one-token response with log-probability $-1.4$, the pooled mean is $2.6/4 = 0.65$ and the per-sequence mean is $(0.4 + 1.4)/2 = 0.9$.

### Instruction tuning

**Instruction tuning** fine-tunes on many tasks phrased as natural-language instructions with their answers. Multi-task studies of 2021 and 2022 (Wei and colleagues, 2021; Sanh and colleagues, 2021) reported that a model tuned on a collection of such tasks followed instructions better and performed better zero-shot on held-out task types than the same pretrained model. InstructGPT (Ouyang and colleagues, 2022) began its pipeline with a supervised stage on human-written demonstrations, followed by the preference-based stages of the next two lessons. Data can also be generated by models from a small seed set, as in the Self-Instruct method (2022), which cuts cost and raises questions about quality and about what errors the generator passes on.

### What does tuning teach?

A hypothesis with substantial support, but not a settled result, is that much of the benefit of a small amount of tuning is about **form**: the style, the format and the habit of answering, with the knowledge and skill coming from pretraining. The study usually called LIMA (Zhou and colleagues, 2023) argued that about a thousand carefully curated examples could produce a strong instruction follower, which is consistent with this view. The counterview is that scale, diversity and the coverage of skills in tuning data still matter for capabilities such as reasoning and coding, and that a small set may suffice for style while larger sets matter for substance. Another open concern is that tuning on facts the model does not already know may encourage confident-sounding wrong answers; this is a hypothesis under study. The honest summary is that how much of tuning is form and how much is new capability depends on the task and is not resolved.

### Fine-tuning and forgetting

Updating all weights on a narrow data set can erode abilities the data does not exercise, which is called **catastrophic forgetting**. Mitigations include small learning rates, few epochs, mixing in pretraining-style data, and updating fewer parameters.

### Low-rank adaptation

**LoRA** (Hu and colleagues, 2021) freezes the pretrained weight $\mathbf{W} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}}$ and learns an update of rank at most $r$: $\mathbf{W}' = \mathbf{W} + \frac{\alpha}{r}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ with $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}} \times r}$ and $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{r \times d_{\text{in}}}$, where $\alpha$ is a scale hyperparameter. Training starts with $\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{0}$, so the model starts unchanged. The trainable parameters per adapted matrix are $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})$, which is $2dr$ for a square matrix, against $d^2$ for training it fully: a fraction $2r/d$. For $d = 4096$ and $r = 8$ that is $0.39$ percent. Since $\mathbf{B}\mathbf{A}$ can be merged into $\mathbf{W}$ after training, the adapted model costs nothing extra at inference. Whether the low-rank restriction limits what can be learned depends on the task; the method is a trade of capacity for memory and speed.

### Evaluating tuned models

A tuned model changes both what it knows how to do and how it formats its answers, and evaluations cannot always separate them. A score that rises after tuning can reflect better format-following under a strict scorer, as much as a better skill. We return to this in the lesson on evaluation.

@example
title: Parameters of an adapter
problem: A model has $d = 1024$ and 12 layers, each with four attention matrices of size $d \times d$. LoRA of rank $r = 4$ is applied to the query and value matrices only. How many parameters are trained, and what fraction of the attention weights is that?
step: Each adapted matrix has $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) = 4 \times 2048 = 8192$ trainable parameters.
step: There are $2 \times 12 = 24$ adapted matrices, so $24 \times 8192 = 196{,}608$ parameters.
step: The attention weights number $4 \times 12 \times 1024^2 = 50{,}331{,}648$, so the fraction is $196{,}608/50{,}331{,}648 \approx 0.39$ percent.
answer: 196,608 trainable parameters, about 0.39 percent of the attention weights.

@example
title: A LoRA forward pass
problem: $\mathbf{W} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{B} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{A} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$, $r = 1$, $\alpha = 2$. Compute the adapted layer's output for $\mathbf{x} = (1, 2, 3)^\top$.
step: Frozen part: $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{x} = (1, 2)^\top$.
step: Low-rank part: $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = 1 - 3 = -2$, then $\mathbf{B}(-2) = (-2, -4)^\top$, scaled by $\alpha/r = 2$ to give $(-4, -8)^\top$.
step: Sum: $(1, 2) + (-4, -8) = (-3, -6)$. Computing $\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}$ first costs $r(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}})$ multiplications, not $d_{\text{in}} d_{\text{out}}$.
answer: $(-3, -6)^\top$.

@mistakes
- Including prompt tokens in the loss without meaning to: the model is then also trained to generate prompts.
- Comparing losses computed with different normalizations: pooled-token and per-sequence means weight examples differently.
- Treating a rise in a benchmark score after tuning as new capability: it can be a change of format that a strict scorer rewards.
- Counting LoRA parameters as $d^2 r$: the factors $\mathbf{B}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ each have $dr$ entries, so the count is $2dr$.

@practice self
prompt: A reported result shows that an ability, "following a multi-step instruction", appears abruptly after instruction tuning: a base model scores near 0 percent by exact match, and the tuned model scores 55 percent. Explain how the choice of metric could make this ability look sudden, and describe two additional measurements that would show whether the underlying change was gradual.
hint: Think about what exact match requires, and what the base model's outputs look like when scored more leniently.
solution: Exact match gives credit only if the output matches the reference completely, including format. A base model may produce the right content in the wrong format, or the right first steps and a wrong last step, and receive 0, while the tuned model learned mostly to put its answer in the expected form and so jumps to 55. The same underlying competence would show as partial credit that is already well above zero for the base model. If a multi-step answer needs $k$ steps right and each is right with probability $p$, exact match is about $p^k$, so a moderate $p$ gives a near-zero score and a modest rise in $p$ gives a large one. Two measurements: first, score both models with a lenient metric (per-step accuracy, or accept any answer that contains the right final value after normalization), which should show whether the base model was already partly there; second, compute the log-probability each model assigns to the correct answer, which is continuous and unaffected by format. If these measures change a little where exact match changes a lot, the sudden look was due to the scoring rule. If they also jump, the change is real for this task and tuning, though one task does not settle the general question.
rubric: Explains that an all-or-nothing metric hides partial competence and format effects.
rubric: Gives the product-of-steps argument or an equivalent mechanism for how a modest gradual gain becomes a large jump.
rubric: Proposes two concrete additional measurements, such as a lenient metric and answer log-probability.

@quiz self
prompt: After instruction tuning, a model's zero-shot score on a benchmark rises from 20 to 60 percent. A colleague concludes that tuning "taught it the skill". Give at least three alternative explanations that would also produce this result, and say how you would test each.
hint: Consider the scorer, the prompt format, and the relationship between tuning data and benchmark.
solution: First, format: the tuned model may emit the answer in the form the scorer expects, while the base model gave the right content in an unscorable form; test by scoring leniently or with answer log-probabilities. Second, overlap: the tuning mixture may contain the benchmark's tasks, items or close paraphrases; test by checking tuning data for overlap with test items and by evaluating on freshly written items of the same kind. Third, prompt sensitivity: the base model may be unusually sensitive to the prompt template, and a different template or a few examples in the prompt could close much of the gap; test by evaluating both models over several templates and with few-shot examples. Fourth, evaluation noise or selection: the 60 may be the best of several runs; test with repeated evaluation and confidence intervals. If, after these controls, the tuned model still leads on fresh items under a lenient metric, the claim that tuning changed capability on this task is much better supported, though it still does not say what was learned.

rubric: Gives a concrete test for each explanation.
