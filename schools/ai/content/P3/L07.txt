@article
It is easy to ask people which of two answers they prefer and hard to ask them to write down a function that scores every possible answer. Reinforcement learning from human feedback turns the first into the second: collect comparisons, fit a model of the preferences, and optimize the language model against that learned score. The idea was shown for control tasks by Christiano and colleagues (2017), who trained reward models from human comparisons of short behavior clips, and brought to instruction following for language models by InstructGPT (Ouyang and colleagues, 2022). This lesson derives its mathematics; the next shows how to skip the explicit reinforcement learning.

### The pipeline

1. **Supervised fine-tuning.** Start from a pretrained model tuned on demonstrations (lesson 6). Call the result the **reference policy** $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.
2. **Reward model.** Collect prompts $x$ with two or more sampled responses, ask annotators which is better, and train a model $r_\phi(x, y)$ that outputs a scalar. Typically it is a transformer with a scalar head in place of the vocabulary projection.
3. **Policy optimization.** Treat the language model $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ as a policy that generates a response one token at a time, and maximize the learned reward with a policy-gradient method, in InstructGPT proximal policy optimization (PPO, Schulman and colleagues, 2017), with a penalty that keeps the policy near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$.

### The Bradley-Terry reward loss

The reward model is fitted with the **Bradley-Terry model** of paired comparisons (Bradley and Terry, 1952): the probability that response $y_w$ (the winner) is preferred to $y_l$ (the loser) is

$$P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}} .$$

Maximizing the likelihood of the observed preferences means minimizing, over a data set of triples $(x, y_w, y_l)$,

$$L(\phi) = -\mathbb{E}\big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x, y_w) - r_\phi(x, y_l)\big)\big].$$

With margin $\Delta = r_w - r_l$, one pair costs $-\log\sigma(\Delta) = \log(1 + e^{-\Delta})$, and its derivative with respect to $\Delta$ is $-\sigma(-\Delta)$, which is largest in magnitude when the model ranks the pair wrongly. Three properties matter. The loss depends only on **differences**, so rewards are identified only up to a constant per prompt, and absolute reward values mean nothing. The loss keeps decreasing as margins grow, so unregularized training pushes margins up and relies on early stopping or other control. And the model fits the preferences of the annotators it was shown, so its reward is a statistical summary of their choices, with whatever disagreement, inconsistency and bias those carry.

For the pair with $r_w = 1.2$ and $r_l = 0.4$: $\Delta = 0.8$, the model gives probability $\sigma(0.8) = 0.690$ to the observed preference, and the loss is $0.371$ nats.

### The KL-regularized objective

Optimizing a learned reward without a leash leads the policy to exploit its errors. The standard objective is

$$\max_{\pi}\ \mathbb{E}_{x,\, y \sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x, y)\big] - \beta\, \mathbb{E}_x\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\big),$$

with $\beta > 0$. In practice the per-sample reward is $r(x, y) - \beta \log \frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$. For fixed $x$ and a distribution $\pi$ over responses, the maximizer has a closed form. Adding a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for $\sum_y \pi(y) = 1$ and setting the derivative with respect to $\pi(y)$ to zero gives $r(y) - \beta(\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)} + 1) - \lambda = 0$, so

$$\pi^{*}(y \mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, \exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x, y)}{\beta}\Big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y} \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\, e^{r(x,y)/\beta}.$$

Small $\beta$ gives a policy that follows the reward closely; large $\beta$ keeps it near $\pi_{\text{ref}}$. This result is the starting point for direct preference optimization in lesson 8.

### Constitutional AI and AI feedback

Collecting human comparisons is slow and expensive. Constitutional AI (Bai and colleagues, 2022) replaced much of the human labeling of harmlessness with feedback generated by a model that was given a written list of principles: the model critiques and revises its own responses against the principles for supervised training, and a model labels which of two responses better follows the principles, giving preference data for a reward model ("reinforcement learning from AI feedback"). It shifts the question from "what did annotators prefer" to "which principles, written by whom", and it relies on the labeling model being a good judge. Neither approach removes human values from the loop; it moves where they enter.

### Failure modes and open questions

**Reward overoptimization** (Goodhart's law applied to a proxy): Gao and colleagues (2022) studied this by using a large reward model as a stand-in for true preferences, and reported that as a policy is optimized harder against a smaller proxy reward model, the proxy score keeps rising while the stand-in's score eventually falls. This is why the KL penalty and early stopping are used. Other open problems: annotators disagree, so whose preferences define the reward; preference labels can reward style, length or agreeableness over correctness, and how much that happens depends on the setup; and RL on a learned reward can be unstable and costly. These are studied questions, not settled ones.

@example
title: The optimal KL-regularized policy
problem: A prompt has three possible responses with $\pi_{\text{ref}} = (0.5, 0.3, 0.2)$ and rewards $(0, 1, 2)$. With $\beta = 1$, find $\pi^{*}$.
step: Unnormalized weights $\pi_{\text{ref}}\, e^{r/\beta}$: $0.5 \cdot 1 = 0.5$, $0.3\, e = 0.8155$, $0.2\, e^{2} = 1.4778$.
step: Their sum is $Z = 2.7933$.
step: Divide: $\pi^{*} \approx (0.179, 0.292, 0.529)$.
answer: $\pi^{*} \approx (0.179,\ 0.292,\ 0.529)$; the policy has moved probability toward the higher-reward responses but has not become deterministic.

@mistakes
- Treating reward values as meaningful on their own: Bradley-Terry fits differences, so adding a constant to all rewards of a prompt changes nothing.
- Dropping the reference policy: without the KL term the optimizer exploits errors in the learned reward.
- Reading a high pairwise accuracy of the reward model as proof that optimizing it will improve the policy: the policy's samples lie outside the distribution the reward model was trained on.
- Mixing up winner and loser in the sign: the loss uses $r_w - r_l$, so a correctly ranked pair has a positive margin and a small loss.

model:
```python
import math


def _softplus_neg(x):
    """Stable value of -log(sigmoid(x)) = log(1 + exp(-x))."""
    if x >= 0:
        return math.log1p(math.exp(-x))
    return -x + math.log1p(math.exp(x))


def rm_metrics(pairs, margin=0.0):
    """Mean Bradley-Terry loss with margin, and pairwise accuracy."""
    total = 0.0
    correct = 0.0
    for r_w, r_l in pairs:
        total += _softplus_neg(r_w - r_l - margin)
        if r_w > r_l:
            correct += 1.0
        elif r_w == r_l:
            correct += 0.5
    n = len(pairs)
    return total / n, correct / n
```
hint: Use `log1p(exp(-x))` for `x >= 0`, and `-x + log1p(exp(x))` for negative `x`, so that `exp` never receives a large positive number.
solution: A naive `log(1 + exp(-x))` overflows when `x` is very negative; rewriting it as `-x + log1p(exp(x))` is algebraically identical and safe. Accuracy ignores the margin and counts ties as half, which the last test checks.

@quiz self
prompt: A team trains a reward model with 92 percent held-out pairwise accuracy, optimizes a policy against it with a very small KL coefficient, and finds that human raters like the final policy's outputs less than those of an earlier checkpoint. Explain the likely mechanism and propose two changes to the training and one change to the monitoring.
hint: Think about the distribution on which the 92 percent was measured, and about Goodhart's law.
solution: The reward model's accuracy was measured on responses like those in its training data. As the policy is optimized, it finds outputs that score highly for reasons that do not match what raters value, which lie outside that distribution, where the reward model is unreliable. This is reward overoptimization: the proxy keeps rising while true preference falls. A very small KL coefficient removes the constraint that kept the policy near the region where the proxy was trained. Changes to training: raise the KL coefficient or stop earlier, and refresh the reward model with new comparisons made on samples from the current policy so that it is trained where it is being used; alternatives include ensembles of reward models and penalizing disagreement. Change to monitoring: track a held-out measure of true quality (fresh human or independent-model comparisons against a fixed baseline) during training, not only the proxy reward, and stop or roll back when they diverge. None of these guarantees that the learned reward is the right objective; they limit how far the optimizer can exploit its errors.
rubric: Identifies reward overoptimization or distribution shift between reward-model data and policy samples.
rubric: Proposes two plausible training changes, such as a larger KL coefficient and refreshing the reward model on policy samples.
rubric: Proposes monitoring a held-out measure of true quality alongside the proxy reward.
