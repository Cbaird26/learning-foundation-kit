@article
Four sensors measure two quantities that move almost together: the readings are $(13, 21)$, $(11, 23)$, $(7, 19)$ and $(9, 17)$. Plotted, the points lie along a diagonal band. Almost everything we need to know is *where along the band* a point lies; the position across the band is small. **Principal component analysis** (PCA) finds such directions automatically and lets us describe the data with fewer numbers.

### What PCA finds

Let $\mathbf{X}$ be the $n \times d$ data matrix (one example per row) and $\mathbf{X}_c$ the same matrix after subtracting the mean of each column. The **sample covariance matrix** is
$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n-1}\, \mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c .$$
PCA looks for a unit vector $\mathbf{v}$ such that the projections $\mathbf{X}_c \mathbf{v}$ have the largest possible variance. The variance of the projections is $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}$, and maximizing it over unit vectors gives the **eigenvector of $\mathbf{S}$ with the largest eigenvalue**; the next direction, orthogonal to the first, is the eigenvector with the second-largest eigenvalue, and so on. The eigenvectors are the **principal components** (PCs), the projections $\mathbf{X}_c \mathbf{v}_j$ are the **scores**, and the eigenvalue $\lambda_j$ is the variance of the scores along PC $j$. The total variance is $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S} = \sum_j \lambda_j$, so PC $j$ explains the fraction $\lambda_j / \sum_i \lambda_i$.

### The sensor data

The column means are $10$ and $20$, so the centered points are $(3, 1)$, $(1, 3)$, $(-3, -1)$ and $(-1, -3)$. Then $\mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c = \begin{pmatrix} 20 & 12 \\ 12 & 20 \end{pmatrix}$ and, dividing by $n - 1 = 3$,
$$\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 20/3 & 4 \\ 4 & 20/3 \end{pmatrix}.$$
Its eigenvalues are $20/3 \pm 4$, that is $32/3 \approx 10.67$ and $8/3 \approx 2.67$, with eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1 = (1, 1)/\sqrt{2}$ and $\mathbf{v}_2 = (1, -1)/\sqrt{2}$. The first PC explains $\frac{32/3}{40/3} = 80\%$ of the variance. The scores on PC 1 are $\pm 4/\sqrt{2} = \pm 2.83$, so replacing each 2-D reading by one number loses only the across-band detail.

### PCA through the SVD

Forming $\mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c$ can lose precision and is wasteful when $d$ is large. The **singular value decomposition** factors the centered data as
$$\mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \mathbf{V}^\top,$$
where $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ have orthonormal columns and $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}$ is diagonal with singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0$. Then $\mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c = \mathbf{V} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}^2 \mathbf{V}^\top$, so:

- the columns of $\mathbf{V}$ are the principal components,
- the eigenvalues are $\lambda_j = \sigma_j^2 / (n - 1)$,
- the scores are $\mathbf{X}_c \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{U} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}$.

For the sensor data the singular values are $\sqrt{32} = 5.657$ and $\sqrt{8} = 2.828$, and indeed $32/3$ and $8/3$ are their squares divided by 3. Keeping the top $k$ components gives the **rank-$k$ approximation** $\mathbf{X}_c \approx \mathbf{U}_k \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k \mathbf{V}_k^\top$, which by the Eckart–Young theorem is the best rank-$k$ approximation in the Frobenius norm. The squared error equals the sum of the discarded $\sigma_j^2$.

### Power iteration

To find the top eigenvector without a library, start from any vector $\mathbf{v}_0$ not orthogonal to it and repeat $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} / \lVert \mathbf{S}\mathbf{v} \rVert_2$. Each step multiplies the component along PC $j$ by $\lambda_j$, so the direction with the largest eigenvalue takes over; the error shrinks like $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^t$. The eigenvalue is then $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}$. A PC is defined only up to sign, so $-\mathbf{v}_1$ is equally valid.

### What PCA does not do

PCA is linear and unsupervised. The directions of largest variance need not be the directions that separate classes, and a feature measured in large units dominates the variance unless you standardize first. Centering is essential: without it the first "component" mostly points at the mean. PCA also says nothing about *why* the data vary in those directions.

### In code

```python
def covariance(X):
    n, d = len(X), len(X[0])
    mean = [sum(row[j] for row in X) / n for j in range(d)]
    return [[sum((r[i] - mean[i]) * (r[j] - mean[j]) for r in X) / (n - 1)
             for j in range(d)] for i in range(d)]

S = covariance([[13, 21], [11, 23], [7, 19], [9, 17]])
print(S)                         # [[6.67, 4.0], [4.0, 6.67]]
v = [1.0, 1.0]
for _ in range(20):
    w = [sum(S[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(2)) for i in range(2)]
    norm = sum(a * a for a in w) ** 0.5
    v = [a / norm for a in w]
print(v)                         # about [0.7071, 0.7071]
```

With NumPy, `np.linalg.svd(Xc, full_matrices=False)` returns $\mathbf{U}$, the singular values and $\mathbf{V}^\top$ in one call.

title: PCA of four points
problem: Find the first principal component of the points $(1, 3)$, $(2, 2)$, $(3, 4)$ and $(2, 5)$, the variance it explains and the scores of the points on it.
step: Column means are $2$ and $3.5$. Centered points: $(-1, -0.5)$, $(0, -1.5)$, $(1, 0.5)$, $(0, 1.5)$.
step: $\mathbf{X}_c^\top \mathbf{X}_c$ has entries $\sum x^2 = 2$, $\sum y^2 = 0.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 = 5$ and $\sum xy = 0.5 + 0 + 0.5 + 0 = 1$. Dividing by $n - 1 = 3$ gives $\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 & 1/3 \\ 1/3 & 5/3 \end{pmatrix}$.
step: Characteristic equation: $\lambda^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S})\lambda + \det \mathbf{S} = \lambda^2 - \tfrac{7}{3}\lambda + 1 = 0$, since $\det \mathbf{S} = \tfrac{10}{9} - \tfrac{1}{9} = 1$. So $\lambda = (7 \pm \sqrt{13})/6$, that is $\lambda_1 \approx 1.7676$ and $\lambda_2 \approx 0.5657$.
step: Eigenvector for $\lambda_1$: from $(2/3 - \lambda_1) v_1 + \tfrac13 v_2 = 0$ we get $v_2 \approx 3.293\, v_1$, and normalizing gives $\mathbf{v}_1 \approx (0.2898, 0.9571)$.
step: Scores $\mathbf{X}_c \mathbf{v}_1$: $-0.768$, $-1.436$, $0.768$, $1.436$. Their variance is $(0.590 + 2.062 + 0.590 + 2.062)/3 \approx 1.7676 = \lambda_1$, as it should be. The first PC explains $1.7676 / (7/3) \approx 75.8\%$ of the variance.
answer: $\mathbf{v}_1 \approx (0.290, 0.957)$ (up to sign), $\lambda_1 \approx 1.768$, $75.8\%$ of the variance.

@example
title: Explained variance from singular values
problem: Centered data with $n = 5$ examples has singular values $\sigma_1 = 6$ and $\sigma_2 = 3$. Find the variances along the two principal components and the fraction explained by the first.
step: Each variance is $\sigma_j^2 / (n - 1)$ with $n - 1 = 4$.
step: $\lambda_1 = 36/4 = 9$ and $\lambda_2 = 9/4 = 2.25$.
step: The fraction explained by PC 1 is $9 / (9 + 2.25) = 0.8$, which is also $36 / (36 + 9)$: the factor $n-1$ cancels in the ratio.
answer: $\lambda = (9, 2.25)$ and PC 1 explains $80\%$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to center: the covariance formula assumes zero-mean columns, and uncentered data makes the first component point toward the mean.
- Mixing up $\sigma_j$ and $\lambda_j$: the variance along a PC is $\sigma_j^2/(n-1)$, not $\sigma_j$.
- Running PCA on raw features in different units: the feature with the largest scale dominates the first PC, so standardize first when units differ.
- Assuming the top PC is the most useful direction for a task: it maximizes variance, which need not separate classes.

model:
```python
def power_iteration(S, iters):
    """Leading eigenvalue and unit eigenvector of a symmetric PSD matrix."""
    d = len(S)
    v = [1.0 / d ** 0.5] * d
    for _ in range(iters):
        w = [sum(S[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(d)) for i in range(d)]
        norm = sum(a * a for a in w) ** 0.5
        v = [a / norm for a in w]
    big = max(range(d), key=lambda i: abs(v[i]))
    if v[big] < 0:
        v = [-a for a in v]
    lam = sum(v[i] * S[i][j] * v[j] for i in range(d) for j in range(d))
    return lam, v
```
hint: In each step multiply the matrix by the vector and divide by the length of the result; at the end compute $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}$ for the eigenvalue.
solution: Each multiplication stretches the component along the top eigenvector most, so the direction converges to it at the rate $(\lambda_2/\lambda_1)^t$. The eigenvalue is read off with the Rayleigh quotient $\mathbf{v}^\top \mathbf{S} \mathbf{v}$, which equals $\lambda_1$ for a unit eigenvector; the third test has an eigenvector that is not along an axis.

```python
def pca_scores(X, v):
    """Projections of the centered rows of X onto the direction v."""
    n, d = len(X), len(X[0])
    mean = [sum(row[j] for row in X) / n for j in range(d)]
    return [sum((row[j] - mean[j]) * v[j] for j in range(d)) for row in X]
```
