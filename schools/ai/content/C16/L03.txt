@article
Heights of adults form two overlapping humps when men and women are measured together, but the data file has no sex column. A single bell curve fits badly, yet each hump alone is nearly a bell curve. A **Gaussian mixture model** (GMM) says the data came from several Gaussians, and that each point's component is a **latent** (hidden) variable that we never observe.

### The model

A GMM with $K$ components has parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta} = \{\pi_k, \mu_k, \sigma_k\}_{k=1}^{K}$: mixing weights $\pi_k \ge 0$ with $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$, means $\mu_k$ and standard deviations $\sigma_k$. In one dimension the density is

$$p(x) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2), \qquad \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\!\Big(-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big).$$

It is a generative process: first draw a hidden label $z \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi)$, so $P(z = k) = \pi_k$; then draw $x \mid z = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$. Summing out $z$ gives the density above. In $d$ dimensions each component has a mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ and a covariance matrix $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$.

### Responsibilities

Given a point $x$, which component probably produced it? Bayes' rule gives the **posterior** over the label, called the **responsibility** of component $k$ for $x$:

$$r_k(x) = P(z = k \mid x) = \frac{\pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K} \pi_j\, \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_j, \sigma_j^2)} .$$

The responsibilities of a point sum to 1. This is **soft clustering**: unlike k-means, a point in the overlap of two humps belongs to both, with probabilities.

Take $K = 2$ with $\pi = (0.4, 0.6)$, means $(0, 5)$ and standard deviations $(1, 2)$, and the point $x = 1$. The component densities are $\mathcal{N}(1 \mid 0, 1) = 0.2420$ and $\mathcal{N}(1 \mid 5, 4) = 0.0270$ (a distance of 2 standard deviations from the mean 5). Weighted, they are $0.4 \times 0.2420 = 0.0968$ and $0.6 \times 0.0270 = 0.0162$, which sum to $p(1) = 0.1130$. So $r_1(1) = 0.0968 / 0.1130 \approx 0.857$ and $r_2(1) \approx 0.143$.

### Likelihood

For independent data $x_1, \dots, x_n$ the **log-likelihood** is

$$\ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k\, \mathcal{N}(x_i \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2).$$

For a single Gaussian, setting the derivative to zero gives the sample mean and variance. For a mixture, the logarithm of a sum does not simplify, so there is no closed-form maximizer; the next lesson solves this with the EM algorithm. Three facts matter in practice.

- **Label switching.** Swapping the labels of two components leaves $p(x)$ unchanged, so the parameters are identifiable only up to permutation.
- **Singularities.** If a component's mean sits exactly on one data point and $\sigma_k \to 0$, the density at that point grows without bound and so does $\ell$. Practical fits add a small floor to the variances or a prior.
- **Relation to k-means.** If all components are spherical with the same fixed variance $\sigma^2$ and equal weights, and $\sigma^2 \to 0$, the responsibilities become hard 0/1 assignments to the nearest mean, which are the k-means assignments. A GMM is k-means plus component sizes, shapes (through $\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k$) and soft assignments.

### Choosing $K$

More components always raise the maximized likelihood, so likelihood on the training data cannot choose $K$. Common choices are held-out likelihood or a penalized score such as BIC (Lesson 12).

### In code

```python
from math import exp, log, pi, sqrt

def normal_pdf(x, mean, std):
    return exp(-(x - mean) ** 2 / (2 * std ** 2)) / (std * sqrt(2 * pi))

weights, means, stds = [0.4, 0.6], [0.0, 5.0], [1.0, 2.0]
x = 1.0
parts = [w * normal_pdf(x, m, s) for w, m, s in zip(weights, means, stds)]
total = sum(parts)
print(total, [p / total for p in parts])
```

With `exp` of very negative numbers, real implementations work with logarithms and the log-sum-exp trick to avoid underflow; the code above is fine for small examples.

@example
title: Responsibilities at one point
problem: A mixture has weights $(0.3, 0.7)$, means $(0, 3)$ and both standard deviations equal to 1. Compute the responsibilities of the two components for $x = 1$.
step: Component densities at $x = 1$: $\mathcal{N}(1 \mid 0, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-1/2} \approx 0.24197$ and $\mathcal{N}(1 \mid 3, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-2} \approx 0.05399$.
step: Multiply by the weights: $0.3 \times 0.24197 = 0.07259$ and $0.7 \times 0.05399 = 0.03779$.
step: Normalize by their sum $0.11038$: $r_1 = 0.07259 / 0.11038 \approx 0.658$ and $r_2 \approx 0.342$.
step: Interpretation: although the weight of component 2 is larger, the point 1 is much closer to the mean 0, so component 1 is more responsible for it.
answer: $r = (0.658, 0.342)$.

@example
title: Log-likelihood of two points
problem: For the mixture with weights $(0.5, 0.5)$, means $(0, 4)$ and standard deviations $(1, 1)$, compute the log-likelihood of the data $x = 2$ and $x = 3$.
step: At $x = 2$ both components are 2 standard deviations away, so $p(2) = 0.5(0.05399) + 0.5(0.05399) = 0.05399$.
step: At $x = 3$ the distances are 3 and 1, so $p(3) = 0.5\,\mathcal{N}(3 \mid 0, 1) + 0.5\,\mathcal{N}(3 \mid 4, 1) = 0.5(0.004432) + 0.5(0.24197) = 0.12320$.
step: Take logarithms and add: $\log 0.05399 + \log 0.12320 = -2.9189 + (-2.0940) = -5.0129$.
answer: $\ell \approx -5.013$ nats.

@mistakes
- Taking the logarithm inside the sum: $\log \sum_k \pi_k \mathcal{N}_k \ne \sum_k \pi_k \log \mathcal{N}_k$; the mixture log-likelihood has the sum inside the log.
- Mixing up variance and standard deviation: $\mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu, \sigma^2)$ divides by $\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}$ and by $2\sigma^2$ in the exponent, so passing $\sigma^2$ where $\sigma$ is expected gives wrong densities.
- Forgetting the weights $\pi_k$ in the responsibilities: the point nearest to a component's mean is not necessarily that component's, if the component is rare.
- Believing that more components is always better: the training likelihood rises with $K$ even when the extra components fit noise.

```python
from math import exp, pi, sqrt


def mixture_pdf(x, weights, means, stds):
    """Density at x of a 1-D Gaussian mixture."""
    total = 0.0
    for w, m, s in zip(weights, means, stds):
        total += w * exp(-(x - m) ** 2 / (2 * s ** 2)) / (s * sqrt(2 * pi))
    return total
```
hint: Add up, over components, the weight times the normal density with the given mean and standard deviation.
solution: Each component contributes $\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$ and the contributions are added. The fourth test has $\sigma = 2$, which a function that divides by $\sigma^2$ instead of $\sigma$ in the normalizer gets wrong.

model:
```python
from math import exp, pi, sqrt


def responsibilities(x, weights, means, stds):
    """Posterior probability of each component for the point x."""
    parts = [w * exp(-(x - m) ** 2 / (2 * s ** 2)) / (s * sqrt(2 * pi))
             for w, m, s in zip(weights, means, stds)]
    total = sum(parts)
    return [p / total for p in parts]
```
hint: Compute the weighted density of each component, then divide each by the sum of all of them.
solution: The numerators are $\pi_k \mathcal{N}(x \mid \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$ and the denominator is their sum, which is the mixture density. The fourth test has a heavy wide component and a rare narrow one: at $x = 5$ the narrow component wins despite its weight of 0.1, and a function that ignores the weights gets it wrong.

model:
```python
from math import exp, log, pi, sqrt


def log_likelihood(data, weights, means, stds):
    """Sum over points of log of the Gaussian mixture density."""
    total = 0.0
    for x in data:
        density = sum(w * exp(-(x - m) ** 2 / (2 * s ** 2)) / (s * sqrt(2 * pi))
                      for w, m, s in zip(weights, means, stds))
        total += log(density)
    return total
```
hint: For each point, sum the weighted component densities first, then take the logarithm, then add over points.
solution: The sum over components is inside the logarithm, and the sum over data points is outside. Taking the logarithm of each component separately would give a different, wrong number, which the second and third tests catch.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which of these statements about Gaussian mixture models are true? Select all that apply.
choice*: The responsibilities of one point over all components add up to 1.
choice*: Swapping the parameters of two components gives the same density, so the component labels are arbitrary.
choice*: Adding components never lowers the best achievable training log-likelihood, so the training likelihood alone cannot choose $K$.
choice: The mixing weights $\pi_k$ are the means of the components.
hint: Recall what normalizing in Bayes' rule does, and what $\pi_k$ means in the generative process.
solution: Responsibilities are a posterior distribution over the label, so they sum to 1. The density is a sum over components, so it is symmetric under relabeling. A larger model contains the smaller one (give an extra component weight near 0), so the best training likelihood cannot fall. The weights $\pi_k$ are the probabilities of the labels, not means.
