@article
Take an image and add a little Gaussian noise; then a little more; keep going for a thousand steps. At the end nothing of the image survives: what remains is pure noise. Now run the film backward. A model that can undo one small noising step at a time is a generative model, because pure noise is something we know how to sample. This is the idea of **diffusion models**, introduced by Sohl-Dickstein and colleagues (2015) and brought to high-quality image synthesis by Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020) as denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs). Today is the forward process and the structure of the reverse; next week we connect it to scores.

### The forward process

Fix a **noise schedule** $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_T\in(0,1)$. The forward process is a Markov chain that gradually corrupts a data point $\mathbf{x}_0$:
$$q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\big(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t\mathbf{I}\big),\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t,\ \ \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).$$
Shrinking by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ while adding noise of variance $\beta_t$ keeps the total variance fixed (a variance-preserving chain). Let $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t\alpha_s$.

**Closed form.** $q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\ (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\big)$, so one can write
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\qquad\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).$$
*Proof by induction.* For $t = 1$ it is the definition with $\bar\alpha_1 = \alpha_1$. Assume $\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}'$. Then
$$\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}' + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t.$$
The two noise terms are independent Gaussians, so their sum is Gaussian with variance $\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + (1-\alpha_t) = 1-\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1} = 1-\bar\alpha_t$, and $\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1} = \bar\alpha_t$. $\square$

The closed form matters for training: to corrupt $\mathbf{x}_0$ to any step $t$ you do not need to simulate $t$ steps. As $t$ grows $\bar\alpha_t\to 0$ and $\mathbf{x}_T$ is nearly $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. If $\mathbf{x}_0$ has variance $\sigma_0^2$ in some coordinate, the marginal variance is $\bar\alpha_t\sigma_0^2 + (1-\bar\alpha_t)$. (Ho and colleagues used $T = 1000$ and variances rising linearly from $10^{-4}$ to $0.02$.)

### A few steps by hand

Take $\beta = (0.1, 0.2, 0.3)$, so $\alpha = (0.9, 0.8, 0.7)$ and $\bar\alpha = (0.9, 0.72, 0.504)$. For $x_0 = 2$ the closed form gives $x_3 = \sqrt{0.504}\cdot 2 + \sqrt{0.496}\,\varepsilon = 1.4199 + 0.7043\,\varepsilon$. Simulating step by step with noises $(0.5, -1, 0.25)$: $x_1 = \sqrt{0.9}\cdot 2 + \sqrt{0.1}(0.5) = 2.0555$; $x_2 = \sqrt{0.8}(2.0555) + \sqrt{0.2}(-1) = 1.3913$; $x_3 = \sqrt{0.7}(1.3913) + \sqrt{0.3}(0.25) = 1.3009$. This is one path of the chain; it matches the closed form with the combined noise $\varepsilon = (1.3009-1.4199)/0.7043 = -0.169$, a value that depends on the three noises.

### The reverse process

The reverse conditionals $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$ are intractable, but conditioned on $\mathbf{x}_0$ they are Gaussian and available in closed form (by Bayes' rule on Gaussians):
$$q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\big(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t(\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0),\ \tilde\beta_t\mathbf{I}\big),$$
$$\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\,\beta_t}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0 + \frac{\sqrt{\alpha_t}\,(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_t,\qquad\tilde\beta_t = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\beta_t.$$
The model is a parameterized reverse chain $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\sigma_t^2\mathbf{I})$ started from $\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. Treating $\mathbf{x}_{1:T}$ as latent variables, the ELBO (week 3) decomposes into a sum of KL divergences between Gaussians $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)$ and $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$, one per step. A diffusion model is therefore a hierarchical VAE whose encoder is fixed and has no parameters.

### The training objective

Because $\mathbf{x}_0 = (\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})/\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}$, the mean $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t$ can be rewritten in terms of the noise: $\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_t = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\big)$. Ho and colleagues therefore have the network predict the noise, $\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\big(\mathbf{x}_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)\big)$, and train with the simplified loss
$$L_{\text{simple}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\Big[\big\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} - \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon},\ t\big)\big\rVert^2\Big],$$
with $t$ uniform on $\{1,\dots,T\}$. This reweights the ELBO terms, which improved sample quality in their experiments; it is a denoising regression and needs no sampling of the chain. **Sampling** starts at $\mathbf{x}_T\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ and for $t = T,\dots,1$ draws $\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t) + \sigma_t\mathbf{z}$ ($\mathbf{z}$ standard normal, with no noise at the last step). That requires $T$ sequential network evaluations; faster deterministic and few-step samplers, such as the DDIM sampler of Song, Meng and Ermon (2020), trade steps against fidelity.

### In code

```python
from math import sqrt

def alpha_bars(betas):
    out, prod = [], 1.0
    for b in betas:
        prod *= 1.0 - b
        out.append(prod)
    return out

def q_sample(x0, abar, eps):
    return sqrt(abar) * x0 + sqrt(1.0 - abar) * eps

betas = [0.1, 0.2, 0.3]
abars = alpha_bars(betas)
print([round(a, 4) for a in abars])
print([round(q_sample(2.0, a, 0.5), 4) for a in abars])
```

The closed form is one line in NumPy as well: `np.sqrt(abar) * x0 + np.sqrt(1 - abar) * eps`, applied to a whole batch with one random $t$ per example.

title: One reverse step
problem: With $\beta_1 = 0.05$ and $\beta_2 = 0.1$, find the distribution $q(x_1\mid x_2, x_0)$ for $x_0 = 1$ and $x_2 = 0.5$.
step: Compute the cumulative products: $\alpha_1 = 0.95$, $\alpha_2 = 0.9$, so $\bar\alpha_1 = 0.95$ and $\bar\alpha_2 = 0.855$.
step: Posterior variance: $\tilde\beta_2 = \frac{1-\bar\alpha_1}{1-\bar\alpha_2}\beta_2 = \frac{0.05}{0.145}(0.1) = 0.03448$.
step: Coefficient of $x_0$: $\frac{\sqrt{\bar\alpha_1}\,\beta_2}{1-\bar\alpha_2} = \frac{0.97468\times 0.1}{0.145} = 0.6722$. Coefficient of $x_2$: $\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_2}(1-\bar\alpha_1)}{1-\bar\alpha_2} = \frac{0.94868\times 0.05}{0.145} = 0.3271$.
step: Mean: $0.6722(1) + 0.3271(0.5) = 0.8358$.
step: Check by precisions: the posterior precision is $\alpha_2/\beta_2 + 1/(1-\bar\alpha_1) = 9 + 20 = 29$, and $1/29 = 0.03448$ agrees with $\tilde\beta_2$.
answer: $q(x_1\mid x_2, x_0) = \mathcal{N}(0.8358,\ 0.03448)$.

@mistakes
- Using $\beta_t$ where $\bar\alpha_t$ belongs: the closed form uses the cumulative product of $1-\beta_s$, and its variance is $1-\bar\alpha_t$, not $\sum_s\beta_s$.
- Treating the step-by-step noises and the one-shot noise $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ as the same numbers: both give the same distribution of $\mathbf{x}_t$, but a given path corresponds to a combination of the individual noises.
- Confusing the intractable reverse conditional $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)$ with the tractable $q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t,\mathbf{x}_0)$, which needs the clean data point; the network exists to replace the missing $\mathbf{x}_0$.
- Mixing up the noise-prediction target (the standard normal $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$) with the noisy input $\mathbf{x}_t$ or with the clean data.

model:
```python
from math import sqrt

def forward_path(x0, betas, noises):
    """Simulate x_t = sqrt(1 - beta_t) x_{t-1} + sqrt(beta_t) eps_t."""
    x = list(x0)
    path = []
    for beta, eps in zip(betas, noises):
        x = [sqrt(1.0 - beta) * xi + sqrt(beta) * ei for xi, ei in zip(x, eps)]
        path.append(x)
    return path
```
hint: Keep a current point `x`; in each step build a new list from the old one with a comprehension over the coordinates, and append it to the path.
solution: Each step shrinks every coordinate by $\sqrt{1-\beta_t}$ and adds $\sqrt{\beta_t}$ times that step's noise coordinate. Building a new list each step keeps `x0` unchanged and keeps earlier path entries from being overwritten, which the last two tests would catch. With zero noise the path is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0$: $0.9$ and then $0.9\times 0.8 = 0.72$.

prompt: Prove by induction that for the forward process $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_{t-1}+\sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$ with independent standard normal noises, $\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0\sim\mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0,(1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I})$ where $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$. solution: Base case: $\mathbf{x}_1 = \sqrt{\alpha_1}\mathbf{x}_0+\sqrt{1-\alpha_1}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_1$ has mean $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_1}\mathbf{x}_0$ and covariance $(1-\bar\alpha_1)\mathbf{I}$. Inductive step: suppose $\mathbf{x}_{t-1} = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}'$, with $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}'$ standard normal and independent of $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$ (it is built from $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_1,\dots,\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{t-1}$). Then $\mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1}}\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1})}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}' + \sqrt{1-\alpha_t}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_t$. The last two terms are independent zero-mean Gaussians, so their sum is zero-mean Gaussian with covariance $[\alpha_t(1-\bar\alpha_{t-1}) + 1-\alpha_t]\mathbf{I} = (1-\alpha_t\bar\alpha_{t-1})\mathbf{I} = (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}$, and the mean is $\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0$. This is the claim for $t$. When $\bar\alpha_T\to 0$, the mean vanishes and the covariance tends to $\mathbf{I}$, so $q(\mathbf{x}_T\mid\mathbf{x}_0)\to\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$ whatever $\mathbf{x}_0$ was.
