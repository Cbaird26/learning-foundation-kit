Everything below is conceptual and general: laws, standards and technical practice change quickly, so treat this lesson as a framework for analysis and check current sources for the details that matter for your work.

### What generated media are used for

Generative models help people make things: drafting and editing text, images, music and video; prototyping designs; restoring and upscaling images; dubbing and translation; and giving a synthetic voice to a person who has lost the ability to speak. In science and engineering they propose candidate molecules and materials, augment scarce training data, and simulate scenarios that are costly or dangerous to collect. **Synthetic data** can reduce the need to share raw records, but it is not automatically private: a model can memorize and reproduce training examples, so privacy claims need measurements, not assumptions (the nearest-neighbor checks of week 11, and formal tools such as differential privacy).

### Documented risks

- **Deception.** Synthetic images, audio and video can be used for impersonation, fraud, and political disinformation. Cloned voices and fabricated video have been used in scams and to impersonate public figures. The harm is both direct (a person is deceived) and systemic: when anything can be faked, genuine evidence can be dismissed as fake, a dynamic sometimes called the liar's dividend.
- **Non-consensual intimate imagery.** Generative tools have been used to produce sexual images of real people without their consent. The targets are overwhelmingly women and, in documented cases, minors; the harms (harassment, coercion, reputational and psychological damage) are serious regardless of whether viewers believe the images are real. Many jurisdictions have recently legislated on this specifically, and platforms have their own rules; check the law that applies to you.
- **Privacy and memorization.** Models trained on personal data can regenerate identifiable training examples, and a person's likeness or voice can be replicated without their involvement.
- **Bias and representation.** Outputs reproduce and can amplify patterns in the training data, including stereotypes.
- **Intellectual property and labor.** Whether training on copyrighted works is permitted, and who owns outputs, are contested legal and policy questions that courts and legislatures in different countries are still addressing; present both the creators' concerns about consent, credit and compensation and the developers' arguments about transformative, statistical use. Concerns about displacement of creative work are also debated.
- **Information ecosystem.** Large volumes of generated content change what appears on the web and can feed back into later training data; the research on training repeatedly on model output points to loss of diversity, but the extent in practice depends on how data are curated.

### Detection

A **detector** is a classifier that labels content as generated or authentic. Its usefulness depends on the base rate. Let the detector have true-positive rate $\mathrm{TPR}$ and false-positive rate $\mathrm{FPR}$, and let a fraction $\pi$ of items be generated. By Bayes' rule,
$$P(\text{generated}\mid\text{flagged}) = \frac{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi}{\mathrm{TPR}\,\pi + \mathrm{FPR}\,(1-\pi)}.$$
With $\mathrm{TPR} = 0.99$, $\mathrm{FPR} = 0.01$ and $\pi = 0.001$ this is $0.99\cdot 0.001/(0.99\cdot 0.001 + 0.01\cdot 0.999) = 0.090$: nine in ten flags are wrong. Detectors also degrade when generators change or content is edited, and an unreliable detector used to accuse individuals can cause harm of its own. Detection alone is therefore not a policy; it is one signal.

### Watermarks and provenance

**Statistical watermarking** embeds a signal at generation time. For text, one family of schemes (Kirchenbauer and colleagues, 2023) uses a secret key to split the vocabulary pseudorandomly at each step into a "green" fraction $\gamma$ and the rest, and nudges sampling toward green tokens; a detector with the key counts the green tokens $g$ among $n$ tokens and tests against the null hypothesis that each token is green with probability $\gamma$:
$$z = \frac{g - \gamma n}{\sqrt{n\gamma(1-\gamma)}}.$$
For $n = 100$, $\gamma = 0.25$ and $g = 40$, $z = 15/4.33 = 3.46$, a one-sided tail probability of about $3\times 10^{-4}$. The power grows with the number of tokens, so short texts are hard. Paraphrasing or translating can erase the signal, the watermark only covers models whose operators apply it, and a $z$ threshold sets the false-positive rate of a single test, which must be controlled when many texts are tested (a person can be wrongly accused if the detector is used carelessly). Analogous schemes embed signals in the pixels, frequencies or latent noise of images and audio; compression, cropping and re-generation can weaken them.

**Provenance metadata** takes the opposite approach: instead of detecting fakes, it records where authentic content came from. Open standards such as those of the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA) attach signed claims, called content credentials, to a file: who produced it, with what tool, and what edits followed. A valid signature shows that the claims were made by the signer and that the file has not changed since; it does not show that the content is true, and the absence of credentials does not show that content is synthetic, because metadata can be stripped by a screenshot or re-encoding and many cameras and tools do not add it. Provenance works to the extent that the signing keys are trusted, creation tools adopt it, and platforms preserve and display it.

No single measure suffices. Defenses are layered: marking at generation, provenance for authentic capture, detectors as a triage signal, platform policies and takedown processes, user education, and legal accountability for harmful uses.

### Rules and release decisions

Regulation is moving. The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) includes transparency obligations for some AI-generated content, including content that imitates real people, which under its Article 50 apply from 2 August 2026 (the Digital Omnibus on AI, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, gives providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before that date until 2 December 2026 to meet the marking duty of Article 50(2)); the text is amended from time to time, so read the current version for who is covered and the exceptions. In the United States there is no comprehensive federal AI statute, though narrower rules and state laws on synthetic media in specific settings exist and change; check their current status. Developers also choose how to release models, and the question of open versus restricted release is one on which informed people disagree: open release supports scientific scrutiny, competition and local control, while restricted access can allow monitoring, filtering and revocation, at the cost of concentrating control. Practices that cut across both positions include documenting training data and intended uses (datasheets and model cards), filtering training and output for the most serious harms, obtaining consent for likenesses and voices, red teaming before release, and monitoring after it.

```python
from math import sqrt

def watermark_z(green, n, gamma):
    return (green - gamma * n) / sqrt(n * gamma * (1 - gamma))

def p_generated_given_flag(tpr, fpr, prevalence):
    hit = tpr * prevalence
    return hit / (hit + fpr * (1 - prevalence))

print(round(watermark_z(40, 100, 0.25), 3))
print(round(p_generated_given_flag(0.99, 0.01, 0.001), 3))
```

title: A flagged item
problem: A detector catches 95% of generated images (true-positive rate $0.95$) and wrongly flags 5% of authentic images. On a platform, 2% of uploaded images are generated. An uploaded image is flagged. What is the probability that it is generated, and how does it change if the generated share is 20%?
step: Write the joint probabilities per 1,000 images at 2% prevalence: 20 generated and 980 authentic.
step: Flagged generated: $20\times 0.95 = 19$. Flagged authentic: $980\times 0.05 = 49$.
step: Probability: $19/(19+49) = 0.279$. About seven of ten flagged images are authentic.
step: At 20% prevalence there are 200 generated and 800 authentic: flagged generated $190$, flagged authentic $40$, so the probability is $190/230 = 0.826$.
step: The same detector gives very different answers because the posterior depends on the base rate, so a flag should trigger review, not punishment.
answer: $0.279$ at 2% prevalence and $0.826$ at 20%.

@mistakes
- Treating a detector's accuracy figure as the probability that a flagged item is generated: that probability depends on the base rate and on both error rates.
- Reading missing provenance metadata as evidence of fakery, or valid metadata as evidence of truth: it speaks to who signed what and to later changes only.
- Assuming a watermark applied by one developer covers all generated content: it covers the outputs of models whose operators apply it.
- Presenting one side of contested questions (release policy, copyright) as settled: state the strongest case for each position and be explicit about what the evidence shows and what it does not.

prompt: A text watermark test uses a green-list fraction $\gamma = 0.25$. In a passage of $n = 80$ tokens, $g = 30$ are green. Compute the $z$ statistic $(g-\gamma n)/\sqrt{n\gamma(1-\gamma)}$. Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 2.58
tol: 0.01
check: (30 - 0.25 * 80) / sqrt(80 * 0.25 * 0.75)
hint: Under the null hypothesis the expected number of green tokens is $\gamma n = 20$ and the variance is $n\gamma(1-\gamma)$.
solution: The expected count is $20$ and the standard deviation is $\sqrt{80\cdot 0.25\cdot 0.75} = \sqrt{15} = 3.873$. So $z = (30-20)/3.873 = 2.58$.

@practice tuple
prompt: Real one-dimensional samples are $0, 2, 4, 6$ and a model generates $1, 3, 5, 7$. Give the ordered pair (Gaussian Fréchet distance using population variances, precision with radius $r = 0.5$), where precision is the fraction of generated samples within $r$ of some real sample.
answer: (1, 0)
check: (((0 + 2 + 4 + 6) / 4 - (1 + 3 + 5 + 7) / 4) ** 2 + 0, sum(any(abs(g - r) <= 0.5 for r in [0, 2, 4, 6]) for g in [1, 3, 5, 7]) / 4)
hint: The two sets have the same spread, so only the means differ; for the precision, find the distance from each generated value to the nearest real value.
solution: The means are $3$ and $4$, and both sets have the same standard deviation, so the Fréchet distance is $(3-4)^2 = 1$. Each generated value is exactly 1 away from its nearest real values, so none lies within $0.5$ and the precision is $0$. The distance says the sets are close, while a tight neighborhood says no generated sample is near a real one: the two metrics answer different questions, and the radius changes the second (at $r = 1$ the precision would be 1).

@quiz self
prompt: A social platform wants to label AI-generated images. Propose a layered approach using provenance metadata, watermark detection and a classifier, say what each layer contributes and how it fails, and explain how the platform should handle an image that no layer flags and an image that only the classifier flags.
hint: Match each layer to what it can establish: signed origin claims, a model-operator signal, or a statistical guess. Consider base rates and the cost of mistakes to individuals.
solution: Layer one: read content credentials; a valid signature shows who claims to have produced the file and what edits were recorded, so it can support a label such as "made with an AI tool" when the signer says so. Failure: metadata is easily stripped, so its absence is uninformative, and it does not show truth. Layer two: run watermark detectors for the operators that offer them; a positive result with a controlled false-positive rate is strong evidence that an image came from a cooperating model. Failure: only covers those models, and editing or re-generation can remove the signal. Layer three: a classifier as a triage signal; it can catch unmarked content at scale. Failure: errors rise with new generators and edited content, and at low prevalence most flags can be false, as the base-rate calculation shows. An unflagged image should not be described as verified authentic, since each layer has gaps: the label should say "no provenance information", not "real". An image flagged only by the classifier should not be labeled as fake with certainty or removed automatically; at most it should be routed to review or shown with a cautious notice, with an appeals path, because accusing an authentic image has costs to the person who made it. The platform should also report error rates by audit, so users know what the labels mean.

rubric: Treats an unflagged image as unverified rather than authentic and does not overstate classifier results.
