@article
Every model so far paid a price for normalization: autoregressive models by factorizing into normalized conditionals, flows by tracking Jacobians, VAEs by working with a bound. An **energy-based model** (EBM) refuses to pay it in the model definition. It assigns each configuration a scalar energy $E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ (low energy means plausible) and defines
$$p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{-E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}},\qquad Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \int e^{-E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}\,d\mathbf{x}\ \ (\text{a sum for discrete }\mathbf{x}).$$
$E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ can be any function, such as a deep network with a scalar output, which makes the family extremely flexible. The price moves to training and sampling: $Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is an integral over all configurations, intractable except for tiny or special models. Adding a constant to $E$ changes $Z$ by the matching factor and leaves $p$ unchanged, so absolute energy values carry no meaning, only differences. Boltzmann machines and restricted Boltzmann machines are classical EBMs over binary variables, and a classifier's logits can be read as an energy over (input, label) pairs.

### A tiny example

Three states have energies $(1, 0, 2)$. Then $Z = e^{-1}+e^{0}+e^{-2} = 0.3679+1+0.1353 = 1.5032$ and $p = (0.2447, 0.6652, 0.0900)$: the lowest energy gets the most probability, and a unit of energy is a factor of $e$ in odds. Summing a three-term series is easy; for $\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^{100}$ it would need $2^{100}$ terms.

### The log-likelihood gradient

For one data point, $\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = -E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) - \log Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$. Differentiating $\log Z$,
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} = \frac{1}{Z_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\int\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}e^{-E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}')}\,d\mathbf{x}' = -\int p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}')\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}')\,d\mathbf{x}' = -\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}'\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}')\big].$$
Hence
$$\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) = -\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}'\sim p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\big[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}')\big].$$
Averaged over the data, this has two phases. The **positive phase** lowers the energy of data points; the **negative phase** raises the energy of samples from the model's own distribution. At a maximum of the likelihood the two expectations of $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}E$ agree: the model reproduces the data's statistics. The difficulty is the negative phase, an expectation under $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$, which can generally be estimated only by running a Markov chain (Gibbs sampling for binary models, Langevin dynamics for continuous ones).

### A model with a closed form: two spins

Let $\mathbf{x} = (x_1,x_2)\in\{-1,+1\}^2$ and $E_J(\mathbf{x}) = -J x_1x_2$. The four states give $Z = 2e^{J}+2e^{-J} = 4\cosh J$. The model prefers aligned spins when $J>0$: $P(x_1 = x_2) = e^{J}/(e^{J}+e^{-J}) = \sigma(2J)$, and $\mathbb{E}_{p_J}[x_1x_2] = \tanh J$. With $\partial E/\partial J = -x_1x_2$ the average log-likelihood gradient is
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial J}\,\frac1n\sum_i\log p_J(\mathbf{x}^{(i)}) = \overline{x_1x_2}_{\text{data}} - \tanh J.$$
Setting it to zero: $\tanh J^\star = \overline{x_1x_2}_{\text{data}}$, moment matching. Here the negative phase is available exactly; in a large model it is not.

### Ways to train without computing $Z$

- **Contrastive divergence** (CD-$k$; Hinton, 2002) replaces the negative phase by samples from $k$ steps of a Markov chain started at the data. It is cheap and biased; persistent variants keep chains running between updates. When the sampler is poor, regions with low energy but no data can survive, so the model puts probability mass where there are no data.
- **Score matching** (week 9) fits $-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}E$ to the data score. Since the score does not contain $Z$, no sampling is needed during training, though sampling is still needed afterward.
- **Noise-contrastive estimation** (Gutmann and Hyvärinen, 2010) turns density estimation into classification: distinguish data from samples of a known noise distribution, treating $-\log Z$ as an extra learned parameter. Its quality depends on how similar the noise is to the data.

### Where EBMs sit

An EBM can represent distributions that autoregressive or flow architectures struggle with, and it composes naturally (energies of independent factors add, a "product of experts"). But sampling requires an MCMC chain that may mix slowly, likelihoods cannot be evaluated without $Z$ (so comparisons across models need estimators such as annealed importance sampling), and training can be unstable. Diffusion and score-based models can be seen as a way to make the negative phase tractable by learning scores at many noise levels, which is one reason they largely displaced EBMs for image generation.

```python
from math import exp, tanh

def ising_gradient_ascent(data_corr, eta, steps):
    j = 0.0
    for _ in range(steps):
        grad = data_corr - tanh(j)      # positive phase minus negative phase
        j += eta * grad
        print(round(j, 4), round(grad, 4))
    return j

ising_gradient_ascent(0.75, 0.5, 4)
```

title: Fitting two coupled spins
problem: Eight observations of $(x_1, x_2)\in\{-1,+1\}^2$ contain 6 aligned pairs and 2 anti-aligned pairs, and the model is $E_J(\mathbf{x}) = -Jx_1x_2$. Find the maximum-likelihood coupling $J^\star$, and take one step of gradient ascent on the average log-likelihood from $J = 0$ with step size $0.8$.
step: The data mean of $x_1x_2$ is $(6\cdot 1 + 2\cdot(-1))/8 = 0.5$.
step: The gradient is $0.5 - \tanh J$. At the maximum, $\tanh J^\star = 0.5$, so $J^\star = \operatorname{artanh}(0.5) = \tfrac12\ln 3 = 0.5493$. Check: $P(\text{aligned}) = \sigma(2J^\star) = \sigma(1.0986) = 0.75 = 6/8$.
step: At $J = 0$ the model is uniform, so $\tanh 0 = 0$ and the gradient is $0.5$.
step: One step with size $0.8$: $J = 0 + 0.8(0.5) = 0.4$.
step: At $J = 0.4$ the model has $\tanh 0.4 = 0.3799$, so the new gradient is $0.1201$, smaller as the model's statistics approach the data's.
answer: $J^\star = 0.5493$; one step from $0$ gives $J = 0.4$.

@mistakes
- Reading $e^{-E}$ as a probability: it is an unnormalized weight, and only after dividing by $Z$ is it a probability.
- Getting the sign of the negative phase wrong: the log-likelihood gradient is data term minus model term for $-\nabla E$, so the update lowers energy at data and raises it at model samples.
- Comparing energies of different models or different parameter values as if they were likelihoods: a constant offset changes nothing in $p$, and $Z$ differs between models.
- Assuming CD-$k$ is unbiased: with few chain steps its gradient is only an approximation to the likelihood gradient.

@quiz self
prompt: An EBM trained with contrastive divergence from short chains started at the data gives low energy to some images that look nothing like the training data. Explain, using the two phases of the gradient, how this can happen, and suggest two ways to detect or reduce the problem.
hint: Ask which regions the negative phase actually pushes up when the chains never leave the neighborhood of the data.
solution: The negative phase raises the energy at samples from the model. If the chains start at data and run only a few steps, the negative samples stay near the data, so only those neighborhoods get pushed up. Regions the chains never visit receive no negative push, and if the energy network makes them low (through generalization or noise), the model puts mass there: spurious modes with no data. Detection: run long, independent chains from random starts and inspect where they settle and whether their energy is lower than that of the data; compare held-out data energies to energies of the model's long-run samples. Reduction: use persistent chains or a replay buffer of past samples so the negative phase explores more, initialize chains from noise and use more steps, add noise or regularization on the energy magnitude, or switch to a method such as denoising score matching that does not rely on a short-run sampler for training.
