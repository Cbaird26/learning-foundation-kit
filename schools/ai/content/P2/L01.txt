@article
A point estimate says "the slope is 1.4". A Bayesian says "the slope is probably near 1.4, and here is how sure I am". The second statement is what you need when a model must say "I don't know" away from its data, when you choose between models, or when a decision depends on the tails. Bayesian linear regression is a case where this is exact, and nearly every method in this course is an attempt to keep a piece of it when the exactness is lost. Read this lesson as the template.

### The model

Let $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ be a fixed feature vector (for a line, $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) = (1, x)^\top$). The model has three ingredients:

- **Prior**: $p(\mathbf{w}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$, where $\alpha > 0$ is the prior precision.
- **Likelihood**: $t_i = \mathbf{w}^\top \boldsymbol{\phi}(x_i) + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \beta^{-1})$, where $\beta$ is the noise precision, so $\sigma^2 = 1/\beta$.
- **Data**: the design matrix $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ has rows $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x_i)^\top$, and $\mathbf{t}$ stacks the targets.

### The posterior, derived

By Bayes' rule, $p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) \propto p(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{w})\, p(\mathbf{w})$. Take logarithms and drop everything that does not depend on $\mathbf{w}$:

$$\log p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) = -\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert \mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w} \rVert_2^2 - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} + \text{const}.$$

Expand the square: $\lVert \mathbf{t} - \boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w} \rVert^2 = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w} - 2\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t} + \mathbf{t}^\top\mathbf{t}$. Collecting terms,

$$\log p(\mathbf{w} \mid \mathbf{t}) = -\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\left(\alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}\right)\mathbf{w} + \beta\,\mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t} + \text{const}.$$

This is quadratic in $\mathbf{w}$, so the posterior is Gaussian. Matching with $-\frac12(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_N)^\top\mathbf{S}_N^{-1}(\mathbf{w}-\mathbf{m}_N)$ (**completing the square**) gives

$$\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}, \qquad \mathbf{m}_N = \beta\,\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t}.$$

Precisions add: the posterior precision is the prior precision plus one term of $\beta\boldsymbol{\phi}\boldsymbol{\phi}^\top$ per observation, so the posterior is never wider than the prior.

### A one-weight example

Take $y = wx$ with $\alpha = 2$, $\beta = 1$ and the data $(1, 2)$, $(2, 3)$. Then $S_N^{-1} = 2 + 1\cdot(1 + 4) = 7$ and $m_N = \frac{1}{7}(1\cdot 2 + 2\cdot 3) = 8/7 \approx 1.143$. The least-squares slope is $8/5 = 1.6$; the prior pulls the estimate toward 0.

### The predictive distribution

For a new input $x_*$ with $\boldsymbol{\phi}_* = \boldsymbol{\phi}(x_*)$, the prediction $y_* = \mathbf{w}^\top\boldsymbol{\phi}_* + \varepsilon$ is a sum of a Gaussian linear function of $\mathbf{w}$ and independent noise, hence Gaussian:

$$p(y_* \mid x_*, \mathbf{t}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(y_* \,\middle|\, \mathbf{m}_N^\top\boldsymbol{\phi}_*,\; \frac{1}{\beta} + \boldsymbol{\phi}_*^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}_*\right).$$

The first variance term is **aleatoric** (noise that more data cannot remove); the second is **epistemic** (uncertainty about $\mathbf{w}$) and shrinks as data accumulate. For the example above at $x_* = 3$: mean $24/7 \approx 3.43$, variance $1 + 9/7 = 16/7 \approx 2.29$.

### Ridge regression is the MAP estimate

The mode of the posterior minimizes $\frac{\beta}{2}\lVert\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \frac{\alpha}{2}\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$, which is ridge regression with penalty $\lambda = \alpha/\beta$ (for the loss $\lVert\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w}\rVert^2 + \lambda\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert^2$). For a Gaussian posterior the mean equals the mode, so $\mathbf{m}_N$ is the ridge solution. What Bayes adds is $\mathbf{S}_N$ and the predictive variance.

### Evidence and sequential updating

Integrating out the weights gives the **marginal likelihood** (evidence) $p(\mathbf{t} \mid \alpha, \beta) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{t} \mid \mathbf{0}, \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I} + \alpha^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top)$. Maximizing it over $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is called empirical Bayes (type-II maximum likelihood), and it will reappear in week 2 as the way to tune Gaussian process kernels. Because the posterior is Gaussian, it can serve as the prior for the next batch of data, so updating one example at a time gives the same answer as updating on all of them at once.

### In code

```python
alpha, beta = 2.0, 1.0
data = [(1.0, 2.0), (2.0, 3.0)]
precision = alpha + beta * sum(x * x for x, t in data)
mean = beta * sum(x * t for x, t in data) / precision
x_star = 3.0
variance = 1 / beta + x_star ** 2 / precision
print(round(mean, 4), round(1 / precision, 4), round(variance, 4))
```

With NumPy one writes `np.linalg.solve(alpha * np.eye(d) + beta * Phi.T @ Phi, beta * Phi.T @ t)` for the mean, never forming an explicit inverse.

### Where this leads

Everything later in the course keeps this structure: a prior, a likelihood, and a posterior that is either exact (Gaussian processes), approximated (variational inference, week 3), or replaced by a learned generative model. The limits are also visible here: the model is only as good as the features $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ and the Gaussian-noise assumption, and the posterior is only as honest as the prior. A well-calibrated posterior under a wrong model is still a wrong posterior.

@example
title: A line with a bias term
problem: Fit $y = w_0 + w_1 x$ with prior precision $\alpha = 1$ and noise precision $\beta = 1$ to the data $(x, t) = (0, 1)$ and $(1, 3)$. Find the posterior mean and covariance of $\mathbf{w} = (w_0, w_1)^\top$ and the predictive mean and variance at $x_* = 2$.
step: Build the design matrix with rows $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)^\top = (1, x)$: $\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 0\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$, so $\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{pmatrix}2 & 1\\ 1 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t} = (4, 3)^\top$.
step: The posterior precision is $\mathbf{S}_N^{-1} = \mathbf{I} + \boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi} = \begin{pmatrix}3 & 1\\ 1 & 2\end{pmatrix}$. Its determinant is $5$, so $\mathbf{S}_N = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}2 & -1\\ -1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$.
step: The posterior mean is $\mathbf{m}_N = \mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t} = \frac{1}{5}(2\cdot 4 - 3,\; -4 + 3\cdot 3)^\top = (1, 1)^\top$. The data lie exactly on $y = 1 + 2x$, but the prior shrinks the weights to $(1, 1)$.
step: For $x_* = 2$, $\boldsymbol{\phi}_* = (1, 2)^\top$. The mean is $\mathbf{m}_N^\top\boldsymbol{\phi}_* = 1 + 2 = 3$.
step: The epistemic term is $\boldsymbol{\phi}_*^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}_* = \frac{1}{5}(2 - 2\cdot 2 + 3\cdot 4) = 2$, so the predictive variance is $1/\beta + 2 = 3$.
answer: $\mathbf{m}_N = (1, 1)^\top$, $\mathbf{S}_N = \frac{1}{5}\begin{pmatrix}2 & -1\\ -1 & 3\end{pmatrix}$; at $x_* = 2$ the prediction has mean $3$ and variance $3$.

@mistakes
- Treating $\beta$ as a variance: $\beta$ is a precision, so the noise variance is $1/\beta$, and swapping them multiplies the assumed noise precision by $1/\beta^2$, so unless $\beta$ is close to 1 the posterior comes out much too wide or much too narrow.
- Dropping the noise term from the predictive variance: $\boldsymbol{\phi}_*^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}_*$ is the uncertainty about the function, but a new observation also carries noise $1/\beta$.
- Believing the predictive variance goes to zero with more data: it converges to $1/\beta$, because the aleatoric part never shrinks.
- Calling the MAP estimate "the Bayesian answer": it is the posterior's mode, and it discards the covariance that makes the method Bayesian.

@practice self
prompt: Derive the posterior distribution of $\mathbf{w}$ for the model $\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \alpha^{-1}\mathbf{I})$, $\mathbf{t}\mid\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w}, \beta^{-1}\mathbf{I})$ by completing the square, and then explain in two sentences why the posterior covariance does not depend on $\mathbf{t}$.
hint: Write the log posterior up to a constant, collect the terms quadratic and linear in $\mathbf{w}$, and compare with the expansion of a Gaussian exponent.
solution: Up to a constant, $\log p(\mathbf{w}\mid\mathbf{t}) = -\frac{\beta}{2}(\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w})^\top(\mathbf{t}-\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{w}) - \frac{\alpha}{2}\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{w} = -\frac12\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{A}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{b}^\top\mathbf{w} + \text{const}$ with $\mathbf{A} = \alpha\mathbf{I} + \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\boldsymbol{\Phi}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \beta\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t}$. A Gaussian $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}, \mathbf{S})$ has exponent $-\frac12\mathbf{w}^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{w} + \mathbf{m}^\top\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{w} + \text{const}$, so $\mathbf{S}_N = \mathbf{A}^{-1}$ and $\mathbf{m}_N = \mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \beta\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\Phi}^\top\mathbf{t}$. The posterior is therefore $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{m}_N, \mathbf{S}_N)$. The covariance depends only on $\boldsymbol{\Phi}$, $\alpha$ and $\beta$ because the targets enter the log posterior only through the term linear in $\mathbf{w}$, which moves the mean but not the curvature.

@quiz self
prompt: For the line model $\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) = (1, x)^\top$ fitted to data whose inputs lie in $[0, 1]$, explain why the predictive variance at $x_* = 5$ is much larger than at $x_* = 0.5$, using the formula for the predictive variance. State which part of the variance is responsible, and say what practical warning this gives when a model is used outside its training inputs.
hint: The predictive variance is $1/\beta + \boldsymbol{\phi}_*^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}_*$; ask how the second term grows as $x_*$ moves away from the data.
solution: The noise term $1/\beta$ is the same at every input. The epistemic term $\boldsymbol{\phi}_*^\top\mathbf{S}_N\boldsymbol{\phi}_*$ is a quadratic form in $(1, x_*)$ with a positive definite matrix, so it grows roughly like $x_*^2 S_{11}$ for large $x_*$, where $S_{11}$ is the posterior variance of the slope. Inside the data range the data constrain both intercept and slope, so the quadratic form is small; far outside, a small error in the slope is multiplied by the large lever arm $x_*$. The warning is that the model reports its own uncertainty growing under extrapolation, which is honest, but only if the linear form itself is right; a Bayesian model with a wrong functional form can be confidently wrong, so the growth in variance should be read as a lower bound on how much to distrust the prediction.
