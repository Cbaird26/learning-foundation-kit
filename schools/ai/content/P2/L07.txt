@article
Every model so far has defined a density, exactly or approximately, and was trained by maximizing a likelihood or a bound on it. A **generative adversarial network** (GAN; Goodfellow and colleagues, 2014) dispenses with the density. A **generator** $G_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ turns noise $\mathbf{z}\sim p_z$ into samples, which define an implicit distribution $p_g$ that we can sample from but cannot evaluate. A **discriminator** $D_{\boldsymbol{\psi}}(\mathbf{x})\in(0,1)$ is trained to tell real data from generated samples, and the generator is trained to fool it. The two networks play a two-player game with value function

$$V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_z}[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))],\qquad \min_G\max_D V(D, G).$$

The discriminator's inner problem is exactly the log-likelihood of a binary classifier trained to separate real examples (label 1) from generated ones (label 0).

### The optimal discriminator

Fix $G$ and write $p_g$ for its sample distribution. Since $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))] = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}\sim p_g}[\log(1-D(\mathbf{x}))]$,
$$V(D, G) = \int\big[p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})\log D(\mathbf{x}) + p_g(\mathbf{x})\log(1 - D(\mathbf{x}))\big]\,d\mathbf{x}.$$
Maximizing pointwise over $y = D(\mathbf{x})\in(0,1)$: the function $a\log y + b\log(1-y)$ with $a = p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})$ and $b = p_g(\mathbf{x})$ has derivative $a/y - b/(1-y)$, which vanishes at $y = a/(a+b)$ and is a maximum because the function is concave. Therefore
$$D^\star_G(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}) + p_g(\mathbf{x})}.$$
This is the Bayes-optimal classifier for equal class priors. Substituting back and writing $m = \frac12(p_{\text{data}} + p_g)$,
$$V(D^\star_G, G) = -\log 4 + \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\Vert m) + \mathrm{KL}(p_g\Vert m) = -\log 4 + 2\,\mathrm{JS}(p_{\text{data}}\Vert p_g),$$
where the **Jensen-Shannon divergence** $\mathrm{JS}(p\Vert q) = \frac12\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert m) + \frac12\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert m)$ is symmetric, nonnegative, zero only when $p = q$, and at most $\log 2$ (in nats). So if the generator could be optimized over all distributions against an optimal discriminator, the global minimum is $-\log 4$, attained exactly when $p_g = p_{\text{data}}$, where $D^\star\equiv\frac12$.

### A discrete check

Let three outcomes have $p_{\text{data}} = (0.6, 0.4, 0)$ and $p_g = (0.2, 0.3, 0.5)$. Then $D^\star = (0.75, 0.571, 0)$: outcome 3 is never real, so the discriminator rejects it. The mixture is $m = (0.4, 0.35, 0.25)$, $\mathrm{JS} = 0.2292$ nats, and $V(D^\star, G) = -\log 4 + 2(0.2292) = -0.928$. Computing $V$ directly as $0.6\log 0.75 + 0.4\log 0.571 + 0.2\log 0.25 + 0.3\log 0.429 + 0.5\log 1$ gives the same value.

### Why the game is hard

**Saturation and vanishing gradients.** If the supports of $p_{\text{data}}$ and $p_g$ are disjoint, then $\mathrm{JS} = \log 2$ no matter how far apart they are, and a perfect discriminator gives the generator almost no gradient through $\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))$. The original paper therefore recommends the **non-saturating** generator loss, maximizing $\mathbb{E}[\log D(G(\mathbf{z}))]$ instead; it has the same fixed point but a gradient that does not vanish when the discriminator wins. The **Wasserstein GAN** (Arjovsky, Chintala and Bottou, 2017) replaces the JS-based objective by the Wasserstein-1 distance, estimated by a critic constrained to be 1-Lipschitz, which stays informative for disjoint supports.

**Mode collapse.** A generator can map all noise to a few outputs that fool the current discriminator. The discriminator then learns to reject those, and the generator jumps to others, so the samples can cycle through modes without covering the data distribution.

**Rotational dynamics.** Training alternates gradient steps, a descent for $G$ and an ascent for $D$, on a function that is not convex-concave in the networks' parameters. A minimal caricature is the bilinear game $\min_x\max_y xy$, whose equilibrium is $(0,0)$. Simultaneous gradient steps are $x\leftarrow x-\eta y$, $y\leftarrow y+\eta x$, and
$$x'^2 + y'^2 = (x-\eta y)^2 + (y+\eta x)^2 = (1+\eta^2)(x^2+y^2),$$
so the distance to the equilibrium grows by a factor $\sqrt{1+\eta^2}$ per step: the iterates spiral outward. Practical GAN training succeeds through architecture choices, regularization of the discriminator and careful tuning, not through a guarantee, and there are no general convergence results for networks trained this way.

**Theory versus practice.** The optimality result lives in the space of densities, assuming an unlimited discriminator trained to optimality. In practice $G$ is a restricted parametric family, $D$ is a finite network trained a few steps at a time, and $p_g$ has no density. The result explains what the objective measures, not that training reaches it.

### Strengths, limits and uses

GANs produce samples in one forward pass and can be very sharp, but give no likelihood and no direct way to score held-out data (week 11). They have been used for image synthesis and translation, super-resolution and data augmentation; the same capability supports deceptive synthetic media, which is the subject of week 12.

```python
def play(eta, steps, alternating=False):
    x, y = 1.0, 1.0
    for t in range(steps):
        if alternating:
            x = x - eta * y
            y = y + eta * x
        else:
            x, y = x - eta * y, y + eta * x
        print(t + 1, round(x, 4), round(y, 4), round(x * x + y * y, 4))

play(0.1, 3)
```

@mistakes
- Stating that the GAN objective minimizes KL divergence to the data: with an optimal discriminator it is the Jensen-Shannon divergence (up to constants), and the behavior is different.
- Treating $D^\star$ as something the discriminator can reach in practice: the formula is the optimum over all functions, and a finite network trained alongside a moving generator only approximates it.
- Concluding that a good discriminator loss means a good generator: a discriminator that wins easily gives the generator little signal, so a perfectly balanced loss is not the goal and the losses are not a quality measure.
- Believing the equilibrium implies convergence of gradient play: even the bilinear game diverges under simultaneous gradient steps.

prompt: Derive the optimal discriminator $D^\star_G(\mathbf{x}) = p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})/(p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x}) + p_g(\mathbf{x}))$ for the GAN value function, explaining why the maximization can be done pointwise and why the stationary point is a maximum.

solution: By the change of variables for expectations, $\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}[\log(1-D(G(\mathbf{z})))] = \int p_g(\mathbf{x})\log(1-D(\mathbf{x}))\,d\mathbf{x}$, so $V = \int[p_{\text{data}}\log D + p_g\log(1-D)]\,d\mathbf{x}$. The discriminator is an arbitrary function and its value at one $\mathbf{x}$ does not constrain its value at another, so $V$ is maximized by maximizing the integrand for each $\mathbf{x}$ separately. With $a = p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf{x})$, $b = p_g(\mathbf{x})$ and $y = D(\mathbf{x})$, $h(y) = a\log y + b\log(1-y)$ has $h'(y) = a/y - b/(1-y)$, which is zero at $y = a/(a+b)$. The second derivative $h''(y) = -a/y^2 - b/(1-y)^2<0$, so $h$ is strictly concave and the stationary point is the maximum. This holds for each $\mathbf{x}$ with $a + b > 0$ (elsewhere the value of $D$ is irrelevant). Hence $D^\star_G = p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}}+p_g)$.
