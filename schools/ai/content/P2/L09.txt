@article
Fitting a density $p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ is hard because it must integrate to 1. The **score** sidesteps the problem. Define
$$\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p(\mathbf{x}),$$
the gradient of the log-density with respect to the data, not the parameters. If $p(\mathbf{x}) = \tilde p(\mathbf{x})/Z$ with an unnormalized $\tilde p$, then $\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log p = \nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\log\tilde p$, because $Z$ does not depend on $\mathbf{x}$. A model of the score needs no normalizing constant, and if you know the score you can sample (below). This week's route from there to diffusion is short, and it explains why the noise predictor of week 8 is secretly a score model.

### Scores of Gaussians and mixtures

For $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma})$, $\log p = -\frac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^\top\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}) + \text{const}$, so
$$\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) = -\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}),$$
which points toward the mean, with length growing with the distance. For a mixture $p = \sum_k w_k\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)$, write $\nabla p = \sum_k w_k\,\nabla\mathcal{N}_k = \sum_k w_k\mathcal{N}_k\nabla\log\mathcal{N}_k$ and divide by $p$:
$$\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_k r_k(\mathbf{x})\,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k-\mathbf{x}),\qquad r_k(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{w_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)}{\sum_j w_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x};\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j)}.$$
The **responsibilities** $r_k$ are the posterior component probabilities of the EM algorithm, so the score is a responsibility-weighted pull toward the component means. For the equal mixture of $\mathcal{N}(-2,1)$ and $\mathcal{N}(2,1)$ at $x = 1$: $r_2 = 1/(1+e^{-4}) = 0.982$, and $s(1) = 0.018(-3) + 0.982(1) = 0.928$. The point is right of center and is pulled toward the nearer mean at $+2$, though more weakly than it would be with a single component.

### Fisher divergence and explicit score matching

To fit a model score $\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$ to the data's score we minimize the **Fisher divergence** $\frac12\mathbb{E}_{p}\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla\log p(\mathbf{x})\rVert^2$, which seems to require the unknown $\nabla\log p$. Hyvärinen (2005) showed that integration by parts removes it. In one dimension, $\mathbb{E}_p[s_\theta\,(\log p)'] = \int s_\theta\,p'\,dx = -\int s_\theta'\,p\,dx$ when $p\,s_\theta\to 0$ at infinity, so up to a constant independent of $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ the objective is
$$J(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_p\Big[\tfrac12\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\rVert^2 + \operatorname{tr}\big(\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\big)\Big],$$
estimated from samples alone. The trace of the Jacobian is expensive for deep networks in high dimensions, which motivates the next idea.

### Denoising score matching

Corrupt the data with Gaussian noise: $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, so $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ and $\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = -(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2 = -\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}/\sigma$. **Denoising score matching** (Vincent, 2011) regresses a network on this known target:
$$J_{\text{DSM}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\Big\lVert\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}) + \frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\sigma}\Big\rVert^2.$$
The minimizer is the conditional mean of the target given $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}}] = \nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$, where $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \int q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})p(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$ (differentiate under the integral and divide by $q_\sigma$). So the trained network estimates the score of the **noised** distribution, not of $p$, and no Jacobian is needed.

**Tweedie's formula** relates the score to denoising: $\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{x}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}}] = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}+\sigma^2\nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$. A score model is a denoiser, and the other way round. Check with $x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$: $q_\sigma = \mathcal{N}(0,1+\sigma^2)$, whose score is $-\tilde x/(1+\sigma^2)$, and Tweedie gives $\tilde x - \sigma^2\tilde x/(1+\sigma^2) = \tilde x/(1+\sigma^2)$, the correct posterior mean.

### Langevin dynamics

Given the score, **Langevin dynamics** produces samples:
$$\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k + \frac{\eta}{2}\,\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{x}_k) + \sqrt{\eta}\,\mathbf{z}_k,\qquad\mathbf{z}_k\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).$$
Under regularity conditions the chain's distribution converges to $p$ as $\eta\to 0$ and $k\to\infty$; with a finite step size there is a bias, which can be corrected with an accept-reject step. The gradient term moves toward high density and the noise keeps the chain from collapsing to a mode.

### From scores to diffusion

Two difficulties arise in practice. Scores estimated from samples are inaccurate in low-density regions, where there are no data, and Langevin chains start in exactly such regions; also, within well-separated modes, the score carries no information about their relative weights, so chains mix slowly between modes. Song and Ermon (2019) addressed both by training one network on many noise levels and running Langevin from the largest noise level to the smallest (annealing). Song and colleagues (2021) cast the continuous-time limit of such noising processes as stochastic differential equations, and the reverse-time dynamics need exactly the score of the noised marginals. The link to week 8 is one line: since $q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0, (1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I})$,
$$\nabla_{\mathbf{x}_t}\log q(\mathbf{x}_t\mid\mathbf{x}_0) = -\frac{\mathbf{x}_t - \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf{x}_0}{1-\bar\alpha_t} = -\frac{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}},$$
so the noise predictor and the score model are related by $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t) \approx -\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\;\mathbf{s}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_t,t)$, and DDPM training is denoising score matching with a particular weighting over noise levels.

```python
def langevin(x0, score, eta, noises):
    x = x0
    path = [x]
    for z in noises:
        x = x + 0.5 * eta * score(x) + eta ** 0.5 * z
        path.append(x)
    return path

mu, var = 1.0, 0.5
score = lambda x: -(x - mu) / var
path = langevin(3.0, score, 0.1, [0.5, -1.0, 0.3, 0.0, 0.8])
print([round(v, 4) for v in path])
```

@example
title: Score of a two-component mixture
problem: The density $p(x) = 0.3\,\mathcal{N}(x;-1,1) + 0.7\,\mathcal{N}(x;2,0.25)$ is a mixture with means $-1$ and $2$ and variances $1$ and $0.25$. Compute the score $s(0.5)$.
step: Evaluate the weighted component densities at $x = 0.5$. First: $0.3\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(1.5)^2/2} = 0.3\cdot 0.3989\cdot 0.3247 = 0.03885$. Second (standard deviation $0.5$): $0.7\cdot\frac{1}{0.5\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(1.5)^2/(2\cdot 0.25)} = 0.7\cdot 0.7979\cdot 0.01111 = 0.006205$.
step: Responsibilities: the sum is $0.04506$, so $r_1 = 0.8623$ and $r_2 = 0.1377$.
step: Component pulls: $\Sigma_1^{-1}(\mu_1 - x) = (-1 - 0.5)/1 = -1.5$ and $\Sigma_2^{-1}(\mu_2-x) = (2 - 0.5)/0.25 = 6$.
step: Combine: $s(0.5) = 0.8623(-1.5) + 0.1377(6) = -1.2935 + 0.8262 = -0.4673$. The wide left component dominates, but the narrow right one pulls strongly.
answer: $s(0.5)\approx -0.467$.

@example
title: Denoising target and Tweedie's formula
problem: A data point $x = 1$ is corrupted with $\sigma = 0.5$ to $\tilde x = 1.3$. Give the denoising score-matching target. Then, if the data distribution is $\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $\tilde x = 1.5$, find the score of the noised distribution and the posterior mean $\mathbb{E}[x\mid\tilde x]$.
step: The noise realization is $\varepsilon = (\tilde x - x)/\sigma = 0.3/0.5 = 0.6$. The target is $-(\tilde x - x)/\sigma^2 = -0.3/0.25 = -1.2$, which equals $-\varepsilon/\sigma$.
step: For $x\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$, the noised variable is $\tilde x\sim\mathcal{N}(0, 1+\sigma^2) = \mathcal{N}(0, 1.25)$.
step: Its score at $\tilde x = 1.5$ is $-1.5/1.25 = -1.2$.
step: Tweedie: $\mathbb{E}[x\mid\tilde x] = 1.5 + 0.25(-1.2) = 1.2$, and indeed $\tilde x/(1+\sigma^2) = 1.5/1.25 = 1.2$.
answer: Target $-1.2$; noised score $-1.2$; $\mathbb{E}[x\mid\tilde x] = 1.2$.

@mistakes
- Taking the gradient of the log-density with respect to the parameters: the score is the gradient with respect to the data $\mathbf{x}$.
- Believing a denoising score model estimates the score of the clean data distribution: it estimates the score of the noised distribution at that noise level; the clean score is recovered only as $\sigma\to 0$.
- Dropping the noise term in Langevin dynamics: without $\sqrt{\eta}\,\mathbf{z}$ the update is gradient ascent on $\log p$ and converges to a mode instead of sampling.
- Forgetting the $\sqrt{\eta}$ (rather than $\eta$) on the noise: the noise scale must match the drift to leave $p$ stationary.

model:
```python
from math import exp, log, pi

def mixture_score(x, weights, means, variances):
    """Score of a 1-D Gaussian mixture, computed with stable responsibilities."""
    logs = [log(w) - 0.5 * log(2 * pi * v) - (x - m) ** 2 / (2 * v)
            for w, m, v in zip(weights, means, variances)]
    top = max(logs)
    unnorm = [exp(value - top) for value in logs]
    total = sum(unnorm)
    return sum(u / total * (m - x) / v
               for u, m, v in zip(unnorm, means, variances))
```
hint: Compute the log of each weighted component density, subtract the largest log before exponentiating (so at least one term is 1), normalize to get the responsibilities, then sum $r_k(\mu_k - x)/v_k$.
solution: The score is $\sum_k r_k(x)(\mu_k - x)/v_k$ with $r_k\propto w_k\mathcal{N}(x;\mu_k,v_k)$. Computing $r_k$ from the densities directly gives $0/0$ in the far tail; subtracting the maximum log density first (the log-sum-exp trick) keeps one responsibility at 1. The first test is a single Gaussian, where the formula reduces to $-(x-\mu)/v$, and the last test would crash or return NaN for a naive implementation.

prompt: Let $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. Derive the denoising score-matching target $\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})$, and show that the function minimizing $\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{s}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) - \nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\rVert^2$ over all $\mathbf{s}$ is the score $\nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ of the noised marginal.
hint: The minimizer of a squared error to a random target is a conditional expectation. Then express $\nabla q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$ as an integral over $\mathbf{x}$ and divide by $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$.
solution: The conditional is $q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}};\mathbf{x},\sigma^2\mathbf{I})$ with $\log q_\sigma = -\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^2/(2\sigma^2)+\text{const}$, so $\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf{x}}}\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x}) = -(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x})/\sigma^2 = -\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}/\sigma$. For a squared loss against a random target $\mathbf{t}$, the minimizer over functions of $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ is $\mathbf{s}^\star(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \mathbb{E}[\mathbf{t}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}}]$ (the loss differs from the loss of $\mathbf{s}^\star$ by $\mathbb{E}\lVert\mathbf{s}-\mathbf{s}^\star\rVert^2\ge 0$). Here $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}}] = \int p(\mathbf{x}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\,\nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}$, and by Bayes $p(\mathbf{x}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = p(\mathbf{x})q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})/q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$, so the integral is $\frac{1}{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\int p(\mathbf{x})\nabla q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\mid\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x} = \frac{\nabla q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}{q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})} = \nabla\log q_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})$, exchanging gradient and integral.

@quiz self
prompt: A score model trained on samples from a mixture of two well-separated Gaussian modes is used to run Langevin dynamics from random initial points. Explain two reasons the samples may be poor even if the network fits the score well where there is data, and explain how training on several noise levels with annealed Langevin dynamics helps.
hint: Think about where training data exist, and what the score says about the relative weight of two separated modes.
solution: First, scores are learned by regression on samples, so the estimate is accurate only where data are dense; random initial points lie in low-density regions where the network has seen little and its output may be wrong. Second, between well-separated modes the score of the mixture is nearly the score of whichever mode is nearer, so it carries almost no information about the mixing weights, and a Langevin chain started in one mode rarely crosses to the other in a feasible number of steps; the proportion of samples from each mode then depends on initialization instead of on $w_k$. Training at several noise levels $\sigma_1>\dots>\sigma_L$ addresses both: the noised distributions have broader support, so the score is well estimated in the regions a chain visits, and at large $\sigma$ the modes overlap, so the score does reflect their relative weights and chains can move between them. Annealed Langevin dynamics starts at the largest noise level and passes the sample to successively smaller levels, so coarse structure (which mode) is settled first and fine detail later.
rubric: Explains inaccurate scores in low-density regions where training data are absent.
rubric: Explains that the score between separated modes does not encode mode weights and mixing is slow.
rubric: Describes how noise levels broaden the support and let annealing fix coarse structure before detail.
