@article
Week 1 had a posterior in closed form, because Gaussian prior times Gaussian likelihood is Gaussian. For almost every interesting model (a mixture, a neural decoder, a topic model) the posterior $p(z\mid x) = p(x,z)/p(x)$ has an intractable normalizer $p(x) = \int p(x,z)\,dz$. There are two broad responses: sample from the posterior (Markov chain Monte Carlo), or pick a tractable family of distributions $q$ and find the member closest to the posterior. The second response is **variational inference** (VI). It turns inference into optimization, and that is why it scales to the models of the next weeks.

### The evidence lower bound

Fix an observation $x$ and any distribution $q(z)$ over the latent variable. The **evidence lower bound** (ELBO) is

$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_{q(z)}\big[\log p(x, z) - \log q(z)\big].$$

**Derivation 1 (Jensen).** Multiply and divide inside the integral by $q$ and use that $\log$ is concave:
$$\log p(x) = \log\int q(z)\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\,dz \;\ge\; \int q(z)\log\frac{p(x,z)}{q(z)}\,dz = \mathcal{L}(q).$$

**Derivation 2 (the gap).** Substitute $p(x,z) = p(z\mid x)\,p(x)$:
$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log p(z\mid x) - \log q(z)\big] + \log p(x) = \log p(x) - \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\Vert\,p(z\mid x)\big).$$
Hence the identity
$$\boxed{\log p(x) = \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\Vert\,p(z\mid x)\big)}.$$
Because $\mathrm{KL}\ge 0$ with equality only when $q = p(z\mid x)$, the ELBO is a lower bound for every $q$, and **maximizing the ELBO over $q$ is the same as minimizing the KL divergence from $q$ to the true posterior**, since $\log p(x)$ does not depend on $q$. The quantity we can compute (the ELBO) tells us how close we are to the one we cannot (the posterior), up to the unknown constant $\log p(x)$.

A second useful form splits the ELBO into a fit term and a regularizer:
$$\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] - \mathrm{KL}\big(q(z)\,\Vert\,p(z)\big).$$

### A discrete check

Let $z\in\{0,1\}$ with prior $(0.5, 0.5)$ and likelihoods $p(x\mid z) = (0.8, 0.2)$ for the observed $x$. Then $p(x,z) = (0.4, 0.1)$, $p(x) = 0.5$, and the posterior is $(0.8, 0.2)$. With $q = (0.5, 0.5)$:
$$\mathcal{L}(q) = 0.5\log\frac{0.4}{0.5} + 0.5\log\frac{0.1}{0.5} \approx -0.916,\qquad \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z\mid x)) = 0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.8} + 0.5\log\frac{0.5}{0.2} \approx 0.223.$$
Their sum is $-0.693 = \log 0.5$, as the identity requires. With $q$ equal to the posterior, $\mathcal{L} = \log p(x)$ exactly.

### Gaussian KL

You will use this closed form constantly. For univariate Gaussians
$$\mathrm{KL}\big(\mathcal{N}(\mu_1,\sigma_1^2)\,\Vert\,\mathcal{N}(\mu_2,\sigma_2^2)\big) = \log\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1} + \frac{\sigma_1^2 + (\mu_1-\mu_2)^2}{2\sigma_2^2} - \frac12,$$
and against a standard normal prior it reduces to $\frac12(\sigma_1^2 + \mu_1^2 - 1 - \log\sigma_1^2)$. For a diagonal Gaussian in $d$ dimensions, sum over coordinates.

### Mean-field VI and coordinate ascent

The simplest family is the **mean-field** family $q(\mathbf{z}) = \prod_j q_j(z_j)$. Maximizing the ELBO over one factor with the others fixed gives the update
$$\log q_j^\star(z_j) = \mathbb{E}_{q_{-j}}\big[\log p(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})\big] + \text{const},$$
and cycling through the factors is **coordinate ascent variational inference** (CAVI); each update cannot decrease the ELBO. For a Gaussian target with mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ and precision matrix $\boldsymbol{\Lambda}$, the optimal factors are $\mathcal{N}(\mu_j, \Lambda_{jj}^{-1})$. Since $\Lambda_{jj}^{-1}$ is at most the true marginal variance $\Sigma_{jj}$, with strict inequality when the coordinates are correlated, mean-field **underestimates** posterior variance in correlated Gaussians.

### What the reverse KL does

VI minimizes $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p)$, not $\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)$. The reverse KL is infinite if $q$ puts mass where $p$ has none, so $q$ avoids low-probability regions: it is **mode-seeking**. If the posterior has two well-separated modes and $q$ is a single Gaussian, a minimizer typically settles on one mode and ignores the other; minimizing the forward KL would instead stretch over both. Neither is "right": the choice trades missing probability mass against placing mass in the wrong place, and it changes what downstream uncertainty estimates mean.

### Caveats a careful reader keeps in mind

The ELBO bounds $\log p(x)$ from below with an unknown gap, so comparing ELBOs of two models compares their evidences only if the gaps are similar. The optimum is over the chosen family, so an expressive $q$ matters more than a clever optimizer. The ELBO is generally non-concave in the parameters of $q$, and CAVI finds local optima. Next week the family of $q$ will be a neural network, and the expectation will be estimated by sampling.

### In code

```python
from math import log

prior = [0.5, 0.5]
lik = [0.8, 0.2]
joint = [p * l for p, l in zip(prior, lik)]
q = [0.5, 0.5]
elbo = sum(qi * (log(ji) - log(qi)) for qi, ji in zip(q, joint))
print(round(elbo, 4), round(log(sum(joint)), 4))
```

@example
title: ELBO gap in a Gaussian model
problem: Let $z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ and $x\mid z\sim\mathcal{N}(z, 1)$, and observe $x = 2$. Compute $\log p(x)$, the ELBO for the approximation $q = \mathcal{N}(0,1)$, and check that the gap equals $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z\mid x))$.
step: The posterior is Gaussian: precision $1 + 1 = 2$ and mean $2/2 = 1$, so $p(z\mid x) = \mathcal{N}(1, 0.5)$. The marginal is $x\sim\mathcal{N}(0, 2)$, so $\log p(x) = -\frac12\log(4\pi) - \frac{2^2}{2\cdot 2} \approx -1.2655 - 1 = -2.2655$.
step: Fit term for $q = \mathcal{N}(0,1)$: $\mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x\mid z)] = -\frac12\log 2\pi - \frac12\mathbb{E}[(2 - z)^2]$ with $\mathbb{E}[(2-z)^2] = (2 - 0)^2 + 1 = 5$, so it equals $-0.9189 - 2.5 = -3.4189$.
step: Regularizer: $q$ equals the prior, so $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z)) = 0$ and $\mathcal{L}(q) = -3.4189$.
step: The gap is $\log p(x) - \mathcal{L}(q) = -2.2655 + 3.4189 = 1.1534$.
step: Direct KL: $\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{N}(0,1)\Vert\mathcal{N}(1,0.5)) = \log\frac{\sqrt{0.5}}{1} + \frac{1 + 1}{2\cdot 0.5} - \frac12 = -0.3466 + 2 - 0.5 = 1.1534$. The identity holds, and $q = \mathcal{N}(1, 0.5)$ would close the gap to zero.
answer: $\log p(x) \approx -2.266$, $\mathcal{L}(q)\approx -3.419$, and the gap $1.153$ equals $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z\mid x))$.

@mistakes
- Writing the VI objective as $\mathrm{KL}(p\Vert q)$: the ELBO identity involves $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z\mid x))$, and the direction determines the mode-seeking behavior.
- Treating the ELBO as an approximation to $\log p(x)$ with no error: it is a bound, and the gap is the posterior KL, which is unknown.
- Forgetting that $\log p(x)$ is constant in $q$: this is why optimizing the ELBO and minimizing the posterior KL are the same problem.
- Expecting mean-field to recover correlations or the true marginal variances: independence in $q$ cannot represent dependence in the posterior.

prompt: Derive the identity $\log p(x) = \mathcal{L}(q) + \mathrm{KL}(q(z)\Vert p(z\mid x))$ starting from the definition $\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(x,z) - \log q(z)]$, and conclude that $\mathcal{L}(q)\le\log p(x)$ with equality if and only if $q = p(z\mid x)$ almost everywhere. State any condition on the supports.
hint: Rewrite $p(x,z)$ using the product rule with the posterior, and recognize the KL divergence.
solution: Assume $q$ is absolutely continuous with respect to the posterior (so $p(z\mid x)>0$ wherever $q(z)>0$), which keeps the KL finite and the logarithms defined. Write $p(x,z) = p(z\mid x)\,p(x)$. Then $\mathcal{L}(q) = \mathbb{E}_q[\log p(z\mid x) + \log p(x) - \log q(z)] = \log p(x) - \mathbb{E}_q\big[\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z\mid x)}\big] = \log p(x) - \mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p(z\mid x))$, because $\log p(x)$ is constant under the expectation. Rearranging gives the identity. Gibbs' inequality gives $\mathrm{KL}\ge 0$, with equality if and only if $q = p(z\mid x)$ almost everywhere, so $\mathcal{L}(q)\le\log p(x)$ with equality exactly then.

@quiz mc
prompt: The exact posterior over a latent variable is an equal mixture of two well-separated Gaussians. You fit a single Gaussian $q$ by minimizing $\mathrm{KL}(q\Vert p)$. What behavior should you expect from the optimizer?
choice*: It tends to place $q$ on one of the two modes with a variance close to that mode's variance.
choice: It tends to place $q$ between the modes with a variance large enough to cover both.
choice: It returns exactly the posterior mean and variance of the mixture.
choice: It becomes uniform, because the reverse KL ignores the shape of $p$.
hint: The reverse KL is large when $q$ puts mass where $p$ is nearly zero. What does a broad $q$ between the modes do?
solution: A broad $q$ covering both modes puts mass in the empty region between them, where $p$ is tiny, and $\log(q/p)$ there is large and positive. A narrow $q$ on one mode only loses the other mode, which the reverse KL penalizes less. Matching the mixture's mean and variance is the behavior of the forward KL (moment matching).
