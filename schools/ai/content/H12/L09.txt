@article
After Reconstruction, Southern states enforced segregation by law (**Jim Crow**), and *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) allowed "separate but equal" facilities. In practice, facilities for Black Americans were unequal, and many were kept from voting. Discrimination in housing, jobs and violence also affected Black Americans in the North and West.

### Legal strategy

The NAACP and its lawyers challenged segregation in court, beginning with graduate and professional schools and then public schools. In *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954), the Supreme Court unanimously held that racially segregated public schools violate the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment because separate schools are inherently unequal. The Fourteenth Amendment says no state shall "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." Many Southern officials resisted, and desegregation proceeded slowly. In 1957 federal troops enforced the admission of nine Black students to Central High School in Little Rock, Arkansas.

### Direct action

Ordinary people organized. The **Montgomery bus boycott** (1955 to 1956) lasted over a year and ended segregation on city buses after a court ruling. Student sit-ins began in 1960 in Greensboro, North Carolina, and spread, leading to the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC). Freedom Riders tested bus desegregation in 1961. Leaders and groups differed in strategy and emphasis: some stressed nonviolent protest and legal change, while others, including supporters of Black Power in the later 1960s, emphasized self-defense, community control and cultural pride. Women played central roles in organizing, often with less public credit.

Protests in Birmingham in 1963 and the **March on Washington** (August 1963) increased pressure for national legislation. Violence against protesters, shown on television, also moved public opinion.

### Laws

- The **Civil Rights Act of 1964** prohibited discrimination in public accommodations, banned employment discrimination on the basis of race, color, religion, sex or national origin, and gave the government tools to desegregate schools.
- The **Twenty-fourth Amendment** (1964) banned poll taxes in federal elections.
- After the Selma marches (1965), Congress passed the **Voting Rights Act of 1965**, which outlawed discriminatory voting tests and authorized federal oversight of areas with histories of discrimination. Black voter registration in the South rose sharply. In *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013), the Court held that the formula used to decide which places needed federal approval was outdated; supporters of the decision said conditions had changed, while critics said continued protection was necessary. Congress and courts continue to address voting rules.
- The **Fair Housing Act** (1968) prohibited discrimination in housing sales and rentals.

### Other movements

The modern women's movement pushed for the Equal Pay Act (1963), Title IX (1972), which bars sex discrimination in federally funded education, and the proposed Equal Rights Amendment, which Congress sent to the states in 1972; it was not ratified by the required three-fourths within the original time limit, and its legal status is still debated. Mexican American activists, including farmworkers organized in the United Farm Workers, sought labor rights and equal education. The American Indian Movement and tribal leaders pursued treaty rights and self-government, leading to the Indian Self-Determination Act (1975). The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965 ended national-origin quotas. People with disabilities won the Rehabilitation Act (1973) and the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990). Gay and lesbian Americans organized after the Stonewall uprising of 1969; courts, legislatures and voters have since changed the law on many questions.

@example
title: Analyzing a civil rights document
problem: Analyze the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment and its use in Brown v. Board of Education (1954).
step: Read the text: no state may "deny to any person within its jurisdiction the equal protection of the laws." Note that it applies to states and to any person, not only citizens.
step: Place it in context: it was ratified in 1868 to protect the rights of freed people. In 1896 *Plessy* interpreted the Constitution to allow "separate but equal" facilities.
step: Describe the use: in *Brown*, the Court concluded that segregation in public schools stamps Black children as inferior and denies equal educational opportunity, and that separate schools are inherently unequal.
step: Evaluate the effects: the decision undermined the legal basis for segregation but required years of litigation, federal action and protest to put into practice, and it did not by itself end school segregation.

@mistakes
- Describing the civil rights movement as a single organization or a single leader; it involved many groups and strategies.
- Saying Brown immediately integrated schools; resistance and slow implementation continued for years.
- Treating the movements for women's, Latino, Native and disability rights as footnotes; they had their own histories.
