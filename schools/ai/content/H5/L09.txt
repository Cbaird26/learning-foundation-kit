@article
Differentiation takes a function and returns its slope. **Antidifferentiation** runs the process backwards: given a rate, recover the quantity. If you know a car's velocity at every moment, an antiderivative of the velocity gives its position.

### Definition

A function $F$ is an **antiderivative** of $f$ on an interval if $F'(x) = f(x)$ for every $x$ in the interval. For example, $F(x) = x^3$ is an antiderivative of $f(x) = 3x^2$.

It is not unique. The functions $x^3 + 5$ and $x^3 - 2$ also have derivative $3x^2$, because constants vanish when you differentiate. Conversely, if two functions have the same derivative on an interval, their difference has derivative $0$, so the difference is a constant: the graphs are vertical shifts of one another. Hence every antiderivative of $f$ has the form $F(x) + C$, and we write

$$\int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + C,$$

the **indefinite integral** of $f$, where $C$ is an arbitrary constant.

### Basic antiderivatives

Read the derivative table backwards.

| $f(x)$ | $\int f(x)\,dx$ |
|---|---|
| $x^n$, with $n \neq -1$ | $\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{n+1} + C$ |
| $\dfrac{1}{x}$ | $\ln\lvert x\rvert + C$ |
| $e^x$ | $e^x + C$ |
| $\cos x$ | $\sin x + C$ |
| $\sin x$ | $-\cos x + C$ |
| $\sec^2 x$ | $\tan x + C$ |

Antiderivatives are **linear**: $\int [a f + b g]\,dx = a\int f\,dx + b\int g\,dx$. As with derivatives, rewrite roots and reciprocals as powers first.

### Guess and check

You can always test an antiderivative by differentiating it. This also handles linear inside functions. Since $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x) = 3\cos(3x)$, an antiderivative of $\cos(3x)$ is $\frac{1}{3}\sin(3x)$. In general, for a constant $k \neq 0$,

$$\int \cos(kx)\,dx = \frac{1}{k}\sin(kx) + C, \qquad \int e^{kx}\,dx = \frac{1}{k}e^{kx} + C.$$

Week 12 turns this idea into the general method of substitution.

### Initial value problems

The constant $C$ is pinned down by extra information. Given $f'(x)$ and one value of $f$, integrate first and then use the value to solve for $C$.

### Motion

Position $s$, velocity $v$ and acceleration $a$ satisfy $v = s'$ and $a = v'$, so going backwards,

$$v(t) = \int a(t)\,dt, \qquad s(t) = \int v(t)\,dt.$$

Each integration introduces a constant, fixed by the initial velocity $v(0)$ and initial position $s(0)$. Near the Earth's surface, ignoring air resistance, the acceleration is constant: $a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ when upward is the positive direction.

@example
title: Integrating term by term
problem: Find $\displaystyle\int \left(4x^3 - \frac{2}{x^2} + 5\cos x\right)dx$.
step: Rewrite the reciprocal as a power: $-\dfrac{2}{x^2} = -2x^{-2}$.
step: Integrate each term. $\int 4x^3\,dx = x^4$. For the second, $\int -2x^{-2}\,dx = -2\cdot\dfrac{x^{-1}}{-1} = 2x^{-1}$. And $\int 5\cos x\,dx = 5\sin x$.
step: Combine and add one constant: $x^4 + \dfrac{2}{x} + 5\sin x + C$.
step: Check by differentiating: $4x^3 - 2x^{-2} + 5\cos x$, which matches the integrand.

@example
title: Throwing a ball upward
problem: A ball is thrown straight up from a height of $2$ m with an initial speed of $12$ m/s. Take upward as positive and $a = -9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$. Find its height $s(t)$ and its greatest height.
step: Integrate the acceleration: $v(t) = -9.8t + C_1$. The initial velocity gives $v(0) = 12 = C_1$, so $v(t) = 12 - 9.8t$.
step: Integrate the velocity: $s(t) = 12t - 4.9t^2 + C_2$. The initial height gives $s(0) = 2 = C_2$, so $s(t) = -4.9t^2 + 12t + 2$.
step: The ball is at its highest point when it stops rising, that is, when $v(t) = 0$: $t = \dfrac{12}{9.8} \approx 1.22$ s.
step: Substitute into $s$: $s(1.2245) \approx -7.347 + 14.694 + 2 \approx 9.347$ m. The greatest height is about $9.35$ m.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the constant of integration, or forgetting to use the initial condition to find it.
- Applying the power rule with $n = -1$. The antiderivative of $\frac{1}{x}$ is $\ln\lvert x\rvert + C$.
- Forgetting to divide by the inner constant: $\int \cos(3x)\,dx = \frac{1}{3}\sin(3x) + C$, not $\sin(3x) + C$.
- Not rewriting roots and reciprocals as powers before integrating.

choice*: $x^2$
choice*: $x^2 - 5$
choice: $2x^2$
choice: $\dfrac{x^2}{2}$

hint: Constants disappear when you differentiate, so adding a constant to an antiderivative gives another antiderivative.
solution: $\frac{d}{dx}x^2 = 2x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}(x^2 - 5) = 2x$, so both work. But $\frac{d}{dx}(2x^2) = 4x$ and $\frac{d}{dx}\frac{x^2}{2} = x$, so neither works.

vars: x

answer: 15
check: 4 + quad(lambda t: 6*t**2 - 2*t, 1, 2)

@practice self
prompt: A car moving at $20$ m/s brakes with constant acceleration $a = -5\ \text{m/s}^2$. Take $s(0) = 0$. Find $v(t)$ and $s(t)$ by antidifferentiation, and use them to find how far the car travels before it stops.
hint: Integrate $a(t) = -5$ once to get $v(t)$, using $v(0) = 20$ to fix the constant. Then integrate again.
hint: The car has stopped when $v(t) = 0$. Find that time first, then evaluate $s$ at that time.
solution: $v(t) = \int -5\,dt = -5t + C_1$ and $v(0) = 20$ gives $v(t) = 20 - 5t$. Then $s(t) = \int (20 - 5t)\,dt = 20t - 2.5t^2 + C_2$ and $s(0) = 0$ gives $C_2 = 0$. The car stops when $20 - 5t = 0$, at $t = 4$ s. Then $s(4) = 80 - 40 = 40$ m.
rubric: Integrates the acceleration and uses $v(0) = 20$ to fix the constant.
rubric: Integrates the velocity and uses $s(0) = 0$ to fix the constant.
rubric: Finds the stopping time from $v(t) = 0$.
rubric: Evaluates $s$ at the stopping time and gives $40$ m with units.

@quiz expr
prompt: Find an antiderivative of $f(x) = 6\cos(3x) + e^{-2x}$. Omit the constant $+C$.
vars: x
lead: $F(x) =$
const: yes
answer: 2 sin(3x) - (1/2)e^(-2x)
check: lambda x: quad(lambda t: 6*cos(3*t) + exp(-2*t), 0, x)
hint: Differentiate $\sin(3x)$ and $e^{-2x}$ with the chain rule to see which constant factors appear.
hint: $\frac{d}{dx}\sin(3x) = 3\cos(3x)$ and $\frac{d}{dx}e^{-2x} = -2e^{-2x}$. Divide by those inner constants.
solution: $\int 6\cos(3x)\,dx = 6\cdot\frac{1}{3}\sin(3x) = 2\sin(3x)$ and $\int e^{-2x}\,dx = -\frac{1}{2}e^{-2x}$. So $F(x) = 2\sin(3x) - \frac{1}{2}e^{-2x}$. Check: $F'(x) = 6\cos(3x) + e^{-2x}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle moves along a line with acceleration $a(t) = 6t - 4\ \text{m/s}^2$. At $t = 0$ its velocity is $3$ m/s and its position is $1$ m. Find its position at $t = 2$ s.
answer: 7
unit: m
check: 1 + quad(lambda t: 3 + quad(lambda u: 6*u - 4, 0, t), 0, 2)
hint: Integrate $a(t)$ to get $v(t)$, using $v(0) = 3$ to find the constant.
hint: $v(t) = 3t^2 - 4t + 3$. Integrate again and use $s(0) = 1$ before substituting $t = 2$.
solution: $v(t) = 3t^2 - 4t + C_1$ and $v(0) = 3$ gives $v(t) = 3t^2 - 4t + 3$. Then $s(t) = t^3 - 2t^2 + 3t + C_2$ and $s(0) = 1$ gives $s(t) = t^3 - 2t^2 + 3t + 1$. So $s(2) = 8 - 8 + 6 + 1 = 7$ m.
