@article
The two big ideas of calculus, slopes and areas, turn out to be inverse to each other. This is the **Fundamental Theorem of Calculus**, and it makes integrals computable without taking limits of sums.

### Accumulation functions

Let $f$ be continuous on $[a, b]$ and define

$$A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt,$$

the signed area under $f$ from $a$ to $x$. As $x$ increases, $A$ accumulates area. How fast? Increase $x$ by a small $h$. The area gained is a thin sliver of width $h$ and height about $f(x)$, so $A(x + h) - A(x) \approx f(x)\,h$, that is,

$$\frac{A(x + h) - A(x)}{h} \approx f(x).$$

In the limit $h \to 0$ the left side is the derivative of $A$.

### The theorem

**Part 1.** If $f$ is continuous on an interval, then $A(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ is differentiable and

$$A'(x) = f(x).$$

So $A$ is an antiderivative of $f$: integrate and then differentiate, and you are back where you started.

**Part 2 (evaluation).** If $F$ is any antiderivative of $f$ on $[a, b]$, that is $F' = f$, then

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a).$$

We write $F(b) - F(a)$ as $\big[F(x)\big]_a^b$. Why does Part 2 hold? Both $A$ and $F$ are antiderivatives of $f$, so they differ by a constant: $A(x) = F(x) + C$. Since $A(a) = 0$, we get $C = -F(a)$, and then $A(b) = F(b) - F(a)$.

### Net change

Because $F' = f$, Part 2 says $\int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$: **integrating a rate of change gives the total change.** Integrate velocity to get displacement, a flow rate to get the volume transferred, or a marginal cost to get the cost of extra units. Take care with signs: the total *distance* travelled needs $\int \lvert v \rvert\,dt$, so split the interval at every time $v$ changes sign.

### Variable upper limits

If the upper limit is a function of $x$, combine Part 1 with the chain rule:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f\big(g(x)\big)\,g'(x).$$

The lower limit does not matter here, because changing it only adds a constant.

### A warning

The theorem needs $f$ to be continuous on $[a, b]$. A function like $e^{-t^2}$ is continuous, so $\int_0^x e^{-t^2}\,dt$ is a perfectly good function with derivative $e^{-x^2}$, even though no elementary formula for it exists. Conversely, blindly plugging a discontinuous function into $F(b) - F(a)$ can give nonsense.

@example
title: Evaluating a definite integral
problem: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^4 \left(3\sqrt{x} + \frac{2}{x^2}\right)dx$.
step: Rewrite as powers: $3x^{1/2} + 2x^{-2}$.
step: Find an antiderivative. No constant is needed because it cancels: $F(x) = 3\cdot\dfrac{x^{3/2}}{3/2} + 2\cdot\dfrac{x^{-1}}{-1} = 2x^{3/2} - \dfrac{2}{x}$.
step: Apply Part 2: the integral equals $F(4) - F(1)$.
step: Evaluate: $F(4) = 2(8) - \tfrac{2}{4} = 15.5$ and $F(1) = 2 - 2 = 0$.
step: So the integral is $15.5 = \dfrac{31}{2}$. As a sanity check, the integrand stays between about $4.6$ and $6.1$ over an interval of length $3$, so a value between about $14$ and $18$ is expected, and $15.5$ fits.

@example
title: An accumulation function with a variable limit
problem: Let $g(x) = \displaystyle\int_0^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\,dt$. Find $g'(x)$.
step: Write $g(x) = A(x^2)$, where $A(u) = \displaystyle\int_0^{u} e^{-t^2}\,dt$. The upper limit is the inside function $u = x^2$.
step: By Part 1, $A'(u) = e^{-u^2}$.
step: By the chain rule, $g'(x) = A'(x^2)\cdot\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^2) = e^{-(x^2)^2}\cdot 2x$.
step: Simplify: $g'(x) = 2x\,e^{-x^4}$. We never needed a formula for the integral itself.

@mistakes
- Subtracting in the wrong order, $F(a) - F(b)$, or forgetting to subtract $F(a)$ at all.
- Adding $+C$ to a definite integral. The constant cancels in $F(b) - F(a)$, so leave it out.
- Forgetting the factor $g'(x)$ when the upper limit is a function of $x$ rather than $x$ itself.
- Applying the theorem when $f$ is not continuous on $[a, b]$. For example $\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{dx}{x^2}$ is not $\left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{-1}^{1} = -2$; the integrand blows up at $0$.

prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^2 (3x^2 - 2x + 1)\,dx$.
answer: 6
check: quad(lambda x: 3*x**2 - 2*x + 1, 0, 2)
hint: Find an antiderivative of $3x^2 - 2x + 1$ term by term.
hint: $F(x) = x^3 - x^2 + x$. Compute $F(2) - F(0)$.
solution: $F(x) = x^3 - x^2 + x$, so the integral is $F(2) - F(0) = (8 - 4 + 2) - 0 = 6$.

@practice num
prompt: Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} (\sin x + 2\cos x)\,dx$.
answer: 3
check: quad(lambda x: sin(x) + 2*cos(x), 0, pi/2)
hint: An antiderivative of $\sin x$ is $-\cos x$ and an antiderivative of $\cos x$ is $\sin x$.
hint: $F(x) = -\cos x + 2\sin x$. Evaluate at $\frac{\pi}{2}$ and at $0$, and subtract.
solution: $F(x) = -\cos x + 2\sin x$. Then $F\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right) = 0 + 2 = 2$ and $F(0) = -1 + 0 = -1$, so the integral is $2 - (-1) = 3$.

hint: Divide the extra area by $h$ and let $h \to 0$. Then apply the result to a rate such as velocity.
solution: Increasing $x$ to $x + h$ adds a thin strip of area under the graph. For small $h$ the strip is almost a rectangle of width $h$ and height $f(x)$, so $A(x + h) - A(x) \approx f(x)\,h$. Dividing by $h$ and letting $h \to 0$ gives $A'(x) = f(x)$: the rate at which area accumulates equals the height of the graph. So $A$ is an antiderivative of $f$. If $F' = f$ is a rate of change of $F$, then $A$ and $F$ differ by a constant, so $\int_a^b f\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$: adding up the instantaneous rates over $[a, b]$ gives the total change in the quantity, such as velocity integrating to displacement.

@quiz num
prompt: A particle moves along a line with velocity $v(t) = t^2 - 4t + 3$ m/s for $0 \le t \le 4$. Find the total distance it travels.
answer: 4
unit: m
check: quad(lambda t: abs(t**2 - 4*t + 3), 0, 4)
hint: Total distance needs $\lvert v \rvert$. Factor $v(t)$ to find when it changes sign.
hint: $v(t) = (t - 1)(t - 3)$, so split $[0, 4]$ at $t = 1$ and $t = 3$. Integrate over each piece with the antiderivative $\frac{t^3}{3} - 2t^2 + 3t$ and add the absolute values.
solution: With $F(t) = \frac{t^3}{3} - 2t^2 + 3t$ we have $F(0) = 0$, $F(1) = \frac{4}{3}$, $F(3) = 0$ and $F(4) = \frac{4}{3}$. The pieces of displacement are $+\frac{4}{3}$ on $[0, 1]$, $-\frac{4}{3}$ on $[1, 3]$ and $+\frac{4}{3}$ on $[3, 4]$. The total distance adds the sizes: $3 \cdot \frac{4}{3} = 4$ m. (The displacement is only $\frac{4}{3}$ m.)

@quiz mc
prompt: A student computes $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1} \frac{1}{x^2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{-1}^{1} = -1 - 1 = -2$. What is wrong?
choice*: The integrand is not continuous on $[-1, 1]$ because it blows up at $x = 0$, so Part 2 does not apply. The answer cannot be negative, since the integrand is positive.
choice: The antiderivative should be $\frac{1}{x}$ rather than $-\frac{1}{x}$.
choice: The student should have computed $F(-1) - F(1)$ instead of $F(1) - F(-1)$.
choice: Nothing is wrong: an integral of a positive function can be negative when the antiderivative is negative.
hint: Check the hypotheses of the theorem. Is $\frac{1}{x^2}$ defined and continuous at every point of $[-1, 1]$?
hint: What should the sign of any integral of a positive function be?
solution: The Fundamental Theorem requires $f$ to be continuous on $[a, b]$. Here $\frac{1}{x^2}$ is undefined at $0$ and unbounded near it, so the formula $F(b) - F(a)$ cannot be used. A positive integrand can never give a negative integral, which confirms that $-2$ is meaningless.
