@article
A model that is accurate, fast and monitored can still be a bad project if it costs more than it earns, if nobody knows what it was built for, or if the first failure is handled in a panic. This week covers the last three operational skills: counting cost and energy, documenting the model, and responding to incidents.

### Cost and energy, with assumptions

Costs come from device time. **Training cost** is the number of device-hours times the price per device-hour. If a run uses 64 devices for 10 days, that is $64 \times 240 = 15{,}360$ device-hours, and at an assumed price of \$2 per device-hour it costs \$30,720. **Serving cost** is usually quoted per 1,000 requests. Four replicas at an assumed \$1.50 per replica-hour cost \$6 per hour; at 200 requests per second that hour carries $200 \times 3600 = 720{,}000$ requests, so the cost is $6/720 \approx \$0.0083$ per 1,000 requests. Idle replicas cost the same as busy ones, which is why the capacity planning of week 8 is also a cost decision. All prices here are invented; use your own.

**Energy** in kilowatt-hours is

$$E = (\text{devices}) \times (\text{hours}) \times (\text{average power in kW per device}) \times \text{PUE},$$

where the **power usage effectiveness** (PUE) is the ratio of the data center's total energy to the energy used by the computing equipment, so a PUE of 1.2 means 20% overhead for cooling and power delivery. For the run above, with an assumed average draw of 0.4 kW per device and a PUE of 1.2, $E = 15{,}360 \times 0.4 \times 1.2 = 7{,}372.8$ kWh. Emissions are $E$ times the **carbon intensity** of the electricity, in kilograms of CO$_2$-equivalent per kWh, which varies by place and time of day; at an assumed 0.4 kg/kWh the run emits about 2,949 kg, or 2.9 tonnes. These are estimates built from stated assumptions, and a measurement from the actual hardware is better when you have it. Training is usually not the whole story: a model that serves millions of requests every day can use more energy over its life in serving than in training.

```python
def energy_kwh(devices, hours, kw_per_device, pue):
    return devices * hours * kw_per_device * pue

def co2_kg(kwh, kg_per_kwh):
    return kwh * kg_per_kwh

kwh = energy_kwh(64, 240, 0.4, 1.2)
print(round(kwh, 1), round(co2_kg(kwh, 0.4), 1))   # 7372.8 2949.1
```

### Documentation

A **model card**, proposed by Mitchell and colleagues in 2019, is a short document that travels with a model. A useful card states the model's **intended use** and uses it was not designed for, the **training data** (a **datasheet** for the data set, as proposed by Gebru and colleagues, describes its source and collection), the **evaluation** with metrics broken down by relevant groups or slices, known **limitations**, ethical considerations, the version and a contact. It also records the monitoring plan and the retraining policy. Documentation is a requirement as well as a courtesy: for example, the EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) requires technical documentation and logging for high-risk systems, with application dates that have been revised, so check the current text and your own obligations.

### Incident response

An **incident** is an event that harms or threatens the service's users or goals: a model starts returning bad predictions, latency explodes, a data feed breaks. The usual stages are: **detect** (an alert fires), **triage** (how severe, who is affected, who leads), **mitigate** (stop the harm quickly, most often by rolling back to the previous model or switching to a fallback, before diagnosing deeply), **resolve** (fix the root cause) and **learn** (the postmortem). Two numbers summarize responsiveness. The **mean time to detect** (MTTD) averages the time from the start of the problem to its detection, and the **mean time to recover** (MTTR), as defined here, averages the time from detection to resolution.

### The blameless postmortem

A **postmortem** is a written review after an incident. It is **blameless**: it asks what in the system and its processes made the failure possible, not who made a mistake, because people hide problems when they fear punishment. A good postmortem has: a one-paragraph **summary** with the impact (who, how long, how bad); a **timeline** with times; the **root cause** and contributing factors; **what went well and what went badly**, including how it was detected; and **action items**, each with an owner, a due date and a way to check it is done (a validation gate, an alert, a test). Actions that say "be more careful" are not actions.

title: Cost and energy of a fine-tuning job
problem: A fine-tuning job uses 16 devices for 36 hours. Assume a price of \$1.80 per device-hour, an average draw of 0.35 kW per device, a PUE of 1.1 and a carbon intensity of 0.5 kg CO$_2$e per kWh. Estimate the cost, the energy and the emissions.
step: Device-hours: $16 \times 36 = 576$.
step: Cost: $576 \times 1.80 = \$1{,}036.80$.
step: Energy: $576 \times 0.35 \times 1.1 = 221.76$ kWh.
step: Emissions: $221.76 \times 0.5 = 110.88$ kg CO$_2$e. State the assumptions with the result, since a different price, power draw, PUE or grid changes each number.
answer: About \$1,037, 222 kWh and 111 kg CO$_2$e under the stated assumptions.
@mistakes
- Quoting a cost or emissions figure without its assumptions (price, power draw, PUE, carbon intensity).
- Counting only training and ignoring the cost and energy of serving, which can dominate over a model's life.
- Writing a postmortem that names a person as the cause; ask which check, alert or process would have prevented or caught the failure.
- Action items with no owner, date or verification, so that nothing changes.

model:
```python
def cost_per_1000(requests_per_s, replicas, price_per_replica_hour):
    """Dollars per 1,000 requests at a steady request rate."""
    if requests_per_s <= 0:
        raise ValueError("request rate must be positive")
    hourly_cost = replicas * price_per_replica_hour
    requests_per_hour = requests_per_s * 3600
    return hourly_cost / requests_per_hour * 1000
```
hint: Divide the cost of one hour by the number of requests in that hour, then multiply by 1,000.
solution: The hourly cost is fixed by the number of replicas, so a higher request rate lowers the cost per request: this is why running replicas at a sensible utilization matters for cost. The error check avoids dividing by zero for an idle service.

model:
```python
def incident_metrics(incidents):
    """Mean time to detect and mean time to recover, in minutes."""
    if not incidents:
        raise ValueError("no incidents")
    detect = [i["detected"] - i["start"] for i in incidents]
    recover = [i["resolved"] - i["detected"] for i in incidents]
    return sum(detect) / len(incidents), sum(recover) / len(incidents)
```
hint: Build one list of detection delays and one of recovery times, and average each.
solution: Detection time is measured from the start of the problem and recovery time from detection, so the two add up to the total duration of each incident. A long detection time points at monitoring and alerting; a long recovery time points at runbooks, rollback tools and diagnosis.

model:
```python
def missing_card_fields(card, required):
    """Required fields that are absent or empty (None, "", [] or {})."""
    missing = []
    for field in required:
        value = card.get(field)
        if value is None or value == "" or value == [] or value == {}:
            missing.append(field)
    return missing
```
hint: Look up each required field with `card.get` and test the four kinds of empty value explicitly, so that 0 and False are not treated as empty.
solution: Truthiness would wrongly treat 0 and False as empty, so the function compares against `None`, the empty string, the empty list and the empty dictionary explicitly. Returning the fields in the order of `required` makes the report stable, and such a check can run in the release pipeline to block a model without documentation.

A recommendation model was retrained nightly. On Monday at 02:00 an upstream team renamed the column `price_cents` to `price`. The feature pipeline did not fail; it filled the missing `price_cents` with 0. The new model went live at 03:00. Click-through rate fell 18% over the next two days. The business dashboard showed the drop on Wednesday at 09:00, and an engineer rolled back to the previous model at 11:00. No data validation ran before training, and no alert watched the distribution of model scores.
hint: Use the lessons of weeks 2, 9 and 10: validation gates, monitoring of inputs and scores, and rollback.
solution: Summary: from Monday 03:00 to Wednesday 11:00 (56 hours) the recommendation model served degraded results, lowering click-through rate by up to 18%; the cause was a renamed upstream column that the pipeline silently replaced with zeros. Timeline: Mon 02:00 column renamed; Mon 03:00 retrained model deployed; Wed 09:00 the business dashboard shows the drop (detection 54 hours after start); Wed 11:00 rollback to the previous model (recovery 2 hours after detection). Root cause: a schema change upstream was not detected, and a silent default (fill with 0) let the pipeline succeed with a corrupted feature; contributing factors: no data validation gate before training, no promotion check comparing the new model with the old one, no alert on score or input distributions, and no contract with the upstream team. Went well: rollback was quick once the drop was noticed. Went badly: detection relied on a lagging business metric. Actions: (1) add a schema and range validation step that fails the training pipeline when a required column is missing or its missing-value fraction exceeds a limit (owner: data engineering, due in two weeks, verified by a test that renames a column); (2) add a pre-promotion gate that compares the new model's offline metrics and score distribution with the current model's (owner: ML engineering, verified by a failing run on corrupted data); (3) alert on the PSI of key inputs and on the mean predicted score with a persistence rule (owner: ML engineering, verified by replaying this incident); (4) agree on a change-notification process for upstream schemas (owner: platform team).

rubric: Identifies the root cause and contributing factors as process or system gaps (no validation, silent default, no monitoring) without blaming a person.
