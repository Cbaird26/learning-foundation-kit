@article
**Informative (explanatory) writing** teaches readers about a topic using facts, definitions, examples and explanation. The writer does not argue a side; the aim is to help the reader understand.

### Plan before you write

Choose a topic narrow enough to cover. "Space" is too broad; "How astronauts exercise on the International Space Station" is manageable. Gather facts from reliable sources and group them into categories. Each category can become a body paragraph.

### The structure

- **Introduction**: a hook (a surprising fact or question), brief background, and a **thesis statement** or controlling idea that names the topic and the focus. Example: "Astronauts exercise about two hours a day to protect their bones and muscles from the effects of weightlessness."
- **Body paragraphs**: each begins with a **topic sentence** that states one main point, followed by supporting details (facts, quotations, examples, numbers), and an explanation of how the details connect.
- **Conclusion**: restates the thesis in fresh words and leaves the reader with a final thought. Do not introduce new main points.

### Organization patterns

Choose the structure that fits your topic: **sequence** for processes, **compare and contrast** for two subjects, **cause and effect** for explanations of why, **description** for features and **problem and solution** for issues.

### Transitions

Transitions link ideas: *first, in addition, for example, however, as a result, in contrast, finally*. Within paragraphs they connect sentences; between paragraphs they connect ideas.

### Style

Use a **formal, objective** tone: avoid slang, opinions ("I think"), and second-person "you" unless writing instructions. Define technical terms. Use precise nouns and verbs. Vary sentence structure.

### Using sources and details

Use specific details (numbers, names, examples) rather than vague statements. Paraphrase in your own words and name the source of facts (more on citing in a later lesson). Do not copy sentences from a source.

### Revising checklist

Does the thesis match the body? Does each paragraph have one main idea? Are the details relevant and sufficient? Are transitions present? Is the conclusion more than a repeat? Have you removed opinion and slang?

@example
title: Building a thesis and topic sentences
problem: Topic: bees. Write a focused thesis and two topic sentences for an informative essay.
step: Narrow the topic: bees are broad, so focus on how honeybees help crops grow.
step: Thesis: "Honeybees support farming by pollinating crops, and their decline threatens the food supply."
step: Topic sentence 1: "First, honeybees pollinate many fruits, vegetables and nuts as they gather nectar."
step: Topic sentence 2: "In addition, losses of honeybee colonies have raised concerns among farmers and scientists." Each topic sentence supports part of the thesis and can be developed with facts.
@mistakes
- Choosing a topic so broad that the essay becomes a list of loosely connected facts.
- Writing a thesis that states only the topic, or one that argues an opinion in an informative piece.
- Ending a body paragraph without explaining how the details support the topic sentence.

choice: Volcanoes are bad.
choice: This essay is about volcanoes.
choice*: Volcanoes form when molten rock rises through cracks in Earth's crust, and they can reshape land and climate.
choice: Everyone should visit a volcano.
hint: Look for a focused statement with a specific, informative idea.
solution: The correct choice names a topic and a focus that the essay can explain with facts. The others are an opinion, an announcement or a command.

solution: A topic sentence states the paragraph's one main idea, which the details then support.
@practice mc
prompt: Which transition best fits: "Frogs breathe through their lungs. ___, they can also absorb oxygen through their skin."?
choice: In contrast
choice*: In addition
choice: Finally
choice: Therefore
hint: The second sentence adds a related fact.
solution: "In addition" signals another related fact. "In contrast" would be wrong because the idea is not opposite.
@practice ms
prompt: Which belong in a formal informative piece? Select all that apply.
choice*: Precise facts or numbers
choice*: Definitions of technical terms
choice: Slang like "super cool"
choice: Personal opinions such as "I think it's boring"
choice*: Transitions between ideas
hint: Think of an objective, clear style.
solution: Facts, definitions and transitions strengthen informative writing. Slang and personal opinions weaken the formal, objective tone.
@practice self
prompt: Choose a topic you know well (for example, how a basketball game is scored). Write a thesis statement and a body paragraph of 4-6 sentences with a topic sentence, two details and a transition.
hint: Narrow the topic first, then make the topic sentence a single clear point.
solution: Thesis: "A basketball game is scored through field goals and free throws that are worth different numbers of points." Paragraph: "Field goals are the main way teams score. A shot made inside the three-point line is worth two points. In addition, a shot made from beyond that line is worth three points. These values encourage teams to balance close shots with long ones."
rubric: The thesis names the topic and a clear focus.
rubric: The paragraph has a topic sentence, at least two specific details and a transition.
rubric: The tone is formal and objective, with no slang or opinion.
