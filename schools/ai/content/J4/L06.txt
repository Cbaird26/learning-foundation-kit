@article
**Literal language** means exactly what it says: "The wind was loud." **Figurative language** goes beyond the literal to create a picture, a feeling or an emphasis: "The wind howled down the street." Authors use it because a fresh image can say more than a plain statement.

### Common figures of speech

- **Simile**: compares two unlike things using *like* or *as*. "Her voice was like warm honey."
- **Metaphor**: says one thing *is* another. "Time is a thief."
- **Personification**: gives human qualities to a non-human thing. "The old door groaned a complaint."
- **Hyperbole**: deliberate exaggeration. "I've told you a million times."
- **Idiom**: a phrase whose meaning is not the sum of its words. "It's raining cats and dogs" (raining heavily).
- **Alliteration**: repeated beginning sounds. "Sally sells seashells."
- **Imagery**: language that appeals to the senses (sight, sound, smell, taste, touch).
- **Onomatopoeia**: words that sound like what they name: *buzz, crash, sizzle*.

### Explaining the effect

Naming a figure is only half the job. Always say **what it does**. "The poet uses a metaphor, calling the city a 'beehive,' to show that it is busy and crowded with people working together." Use this pattern: figure, quotation, meaning, effect.

### Word choice: denotation and connotation

The **denotation** is a word's dictionary meaning. The **connotation** is the emotional or cultural feeling attached to it. *Thrifty*, *cheap* and *frugal* all denote careful with money, but *thrifty* sounds positive and *cheap* sounds negative. Authors choose words precisely for their connotations and to set tone.

Compare *The man strolled into the room* with *The man slunk into the room*. The action is similar, but *strolled* suggests ease and *slunk* suggests guilt or fear.

### Reading figurative language

When you meet a figure, ask: What two things are being compared? What qualities carry over? How does it change the way I see the subject? Do not take it literally: "My heart is a stone" does not mean a stone sits in the chest, but that the speaker feels cold or heavy.

@example
title: Naming a figure and its effect
problem: Read: "The classroom was a zoo at lunchtime, and the clock dragged its feet toward the bell." Identify the figures of speech and explain the effect.
step: "The classroom was a zoo" says one thing is another without like or as, so it is a metaphor.
step: The meaning carried over is noise, chaos and energy, the qualities of a zoo.
step: "The clock dragged its feet" gives a clock a human action, so it is personification. It shows how slowly the time seems to pass.
step: Together the figures create a restless, impatient tone that helps readers feel the students' eagerness for the bell.
@mistakes
- Calling a metaphor a simile (or the reverse); check for the words like or as.
- Naming a figure of speech but not explaining its effect on meaning or tone.
- Taking a metaphor or idiom literally, or ignoring a word's connotation.
