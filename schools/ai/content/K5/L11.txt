objective: Add feelings and describing words to make the story come alive.
objective: Write a short story with a problem and a solution.

@article
A **narrative** is a story. You can tell a made-up story or a true story about something that happened to you.

### Parts of a story

- **Beginning:** tell who the characters are and where and when the story happens. "On a snowy day, Nora and her dog went outside."
- **Middle:** the problem and the events. What goes wrong? What does the character try? "Nora's sled rolled away down the hill."
- **End:** the solution, and how the character feels. "Her dog fetched the rope, and Nora laughed."

### Make it interesting

- Use **time words**: first, then, soon, after a while, at last.
- Use **describing words** that help us see, hear and feel: a *fluffy* white dog, a *loud* crash.
- Show **feelings**: "Nora's heart pounded" is better than "Nora was scared."
- Add what characters **say**, using quotation marks: "Come back!" Nora called.

### Plan first

Draw three boxes: beginning, middle, end. Put a few words in each. Then write one or two sentences for each box.

### Check your story

Read it to your grown-up. Ask: Do we know who and where? Is there a problem? Does the ending solve it? Do my sentences start with capitals and end with marks?

Great writers fix their work. That is called **revising**. Try adding one describing word to every sentence.

@example
title: Planning a story
problem: Make a plan for a story about a lost mitten.
step: Beginning: Jada has a new red mitten on a cold morning at school.
step: Middle: Her mitten falls off. She looks all over the playground.
step: End: A friend finds it in the snow. Jada says thank you and feels glad.

@mistakes
- Having no problem, so the story has nothing to solve.
- Telling the events out of order, so the reader gets mixed up.
- Ending with "and then I woke up" or just stopping without a solution.
