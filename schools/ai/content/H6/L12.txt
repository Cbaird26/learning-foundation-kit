@article
When data are counts in categories, we compare **observed** counts $O$ with the counts **expected** if a hypothesis were true. The **chi-square statistic** measures the total discrepancy:

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}.$$

Each term is large when observed and expected counts differ a lot, and the sum is always at least $0$. Under the null hypothesis, $\chi^2$ follows a **chi-square distribution** with a stated number of **degrees of freedom** (df). This distribution is right-skewed and the P-value is the upper-tail area: $P(\chi^2_{df} \ge \text{observed})$. Large values of $\chi^2$ are evidence against $H_0$; there is no one-sided/two-sided choice, since the test is built only to detect departures in any direction.

### Goodness of fit

A **goodness-of-fit test** asks whether one categorical variable follows a claimed distribution. If the claimed proportions are $p_i$ and the sample size is $n$, the expected counts are $E_i = np_i$, and $\text{df} = (\text{number of categories}) - 1$.

### Independence and homogeneity

A **test of independence** asks whether two categorical variables measured on one sample are associated. A **test of homogeneity** asks whether the distribution of one categorical variable is the same in several separate groups. Both use the same arithmetic. For a two-way table with $r$ rows and $c$ columns:

$$E = \frac{(\text{row total})(\text{column total})}{\text{grand total}}, \qquad \text{df} = (r - 1)(c - 1).$$

**Convention:** for a $2 \times 2$ table some books and software apply Yates' continuity correction, which gives a slightly smaller $\chi^2$. This course uses the uncorrected statistic above.

### Conditions

The data must come from a random sample or a randomized experiment, the observations must be independent, and all **expected** counts should be at least $5$ (some sources say at least $1$ with most above $5$). Check expected counts, not observed ones. For small expected counts, Fisher's exact test is the usual alternative for $2 \times 2$ tables.

### Interpretation and limits

A small P-value says the variables are associated, or the counts are not as claimed, but not *how* or *why*. To see where the discrepancy lies, compare observed and expected counts cell by cell. In a two-way table from an observational study, association is not causation. And as always, a large sample can make a tiny association significant.

@example
title: Goodness of fit in genetics
problem: A genetic cross predicts a $3:1$ ratio of dominant to recessive offspring. Among $400$ offspring, $290$ are dominant and $110$ are recessive. Test whether the data fit $3:1$ at $\alpha = 0.05$.
step: $H_0$: the proportions are $0.75$ and $0.25$. Expected counts are $400(0.75) = 300$ and $400(0.25) = 100$, both at least $5$.
step: $\chi^2 = \dfrac{(290 - 300)^2}{300} + \dfrac{(110 - 100)^2}{100} = 0.333 + 1.000 = 1.333$.
step: $\text{df} = 2 - 1 = 1$ and the P-value is $P(\chi^2_1 \ge 1.333) \approx 0.248$.
step: Since $0.248 > 0.05$ we fail to reject $H_0$: the data are consistent with a $3:1$ ratio. This does not prove that the ratio is exactly $3:1$.

@example
title: Independence in a two-way table
problem: In a study of $200$ adults, $30$ of $100$ smokers and $15$ of $100$ non-smokers have a chronic cough. Test whether smoking and cough are independent at $\alpha = 0.05$.
step: The column totals are $45$ with cough and $155$ without. Expected counts for smokers: $100(45)/200 = 22.5$ with cough and $77.5$ without. By symmetry the non-smokers have the same expected counts.
step: $\chi^2 = \dfrac{7.5^2}{22.5} + \dfrac{7.5^2}{77.5} + \dfrac{7.5^2}{22.5} + \dfrac{7.5^2}{77.5} = 2.5 + 0.726 + 2.5 + 0.726 \approx 6.45$.
step: $\text{df} = (2-1)(2-1) = 1$ and the P-value is about $0.011$.
step: Reject $H_0$: cough and smoking are associated in this sample. The study is observational, so this does not by itself show that smoking causes cough.

@mistakes
- Computing expected counts from the observed counts of each cell rather than from row and column totals under independence.
- Using percentages instead of counts in the chi-square formula.
- Checking that the *observed* counts, rather than the expected counts, are at least $5$.
- Concluding from a significant result that one variable causes the other.

answer: 2
check: ((30 - 25)**2 + (20 - 25)**2 + (25 - 25)**2 + (25 - 25)**2) / 25

solution: $\chi^2 = \frac{25}{25} + \frac{25}{25} + 0 + 0 = 2$.

solution: Since $7.81$ is almost exactly the $95$th percentile of the chi-square distribution with $3$ df, the P-value is about $0.050$.

@practice self
prompt: A random sample of $300$ adults is classified by whether they get at least $150$ minutes of exercise per week and whether they have hypertension. A chi-square test of independence gives $\chi^2 = 9.2$ with $1$ degree of freedom and $P = 0.002$. Write a conclusion in plain words that states the result and two limits.
hint: Say what association was found, then say what a chi-square test and an observational sample cannot show.
solution: Because $P = 0.002$ is far below $0.05$, there is strong statistical evidence that exercise level and hypertension are associated in this population. The test does not say how strong the association is or which direction it goes (compare the observed and expected counts to see this), and since the adults were only surveyed, not randomly assigned to exercise levels, we cannot conclude that exercise changes hypertension risk; other factors such as age may be involved.
rubric: States the result as an association between the two variables, with the P-value reason.
rubric: Notes that the test does not give direction or size, or tells how to find them.
rubric: States that observational data do not establish causation.

@quiz num
prompt: A random sample of $200$ patients is classified by treatment (A or B) and outcome (improved or not). Counts: A improved $40$, A not improved $60$, B improved $20$, B not improved $80$. Find the chi-square statistic (no continuity correction), rounded to three decimal places.
answer: 9.524
check: sum((o - e)**2 / e for o, e in [(40, 100 * 60 / 200), (60, 100 * 140 / 200), (20, 100 * 60 / 200), (80, 100 * 140 / 200)])
tol: 0.01
hint: Column totals are $60$ improved and $140$ not improved; each row total is $100$.
hint: The expected counts are $30, 70, 30, 70$.
solution: $\chi^2 = \frac{100}{30} + \frac{100}{70} + \frac{100}{30} + \frac{100}{70} = 3.333 + 1.429 + 3.333 + 1.429 \approx 9.524$ with $1$ df.
