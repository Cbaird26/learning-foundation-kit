@article
A qualifying exam rarely asks for the deepest result. It asks for a standard result stated exactly, with its conditions, and often a proof or a computation. Programs and departments differ in format (written or oral, closed or open book, which topics, which syllabus), so check your own department's rules and reading list. This review covers theory and optimization; Lessons 8 and 9 cover the rest. For each result, learn three things: the statement with every assumption, the idea of the proof, and a number you can plug in.

### Gradient descent on smooth convex functions

Let $f:\mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ be convex and differentiable, with an $L$-Lipschitz gradient ($L$-smooth: $\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{x}) - \nabla f(\mathbf{y})\rVert_2 \le L \lVert \mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\rVert_2$), and suppose a minimizer $\mathbf{x}^\ast$ exists, with $f^\ast = f(\mathbf{x}^\ast)$. Gradient descent with step size $1/L$ is $\mathbf{x}_{k+1} = \mathbf{x}_k - \tfrac{1}{L}\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)$. Then for every $k \ge 1$,

$$f(\mathbf{x}_k) - f^\ast \le \frac{L\,\lVert \mathbf{x}_0 - \mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert_2^2}{2k}.$$

So reaching accuracy $\varepsilon$ takes at most $L\lVert\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2/(2\varepsilon)$ steps. The proof has two ingredients: the descent lemma, which with step $1/L$ gives $f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1}) \le f(\mathbf{x}_k) - \tfrac{1}{2L}\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)\rVert^2$, and convexity, which gives $f(\mathbf{x}_k) - f^\ast \le \langle \nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k), \mathbf{x}_k - \mathbf{x}^\ast\rangle$. Combining them shows that $\lVert\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2$ decreases, and summing gives the bound.

### Strong convexity: a linear rate

If in addition $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex ($f(\mathbf{y}) \ge f(\mathbf{x}) + \langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle + \tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$ for all $\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}$, with $\mu>0$), then step size $1/L$ gives

$$f(\mathbf{x}_{k}) - f^\ast \le \left(1 - \frac{\mu}{L}\right)^{k}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_0) - f^\ast\big),$$

a **linear** (geometric) rate, with condition number $\kappa = L/\mu \ge 1$. The proof uses the inequality $\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{x})\rVert^2 \ge 2\mu\,(f(\mathbf{x}) - f^\ast)$, which follows from strong convexity, together with the descent lemma. Reaching accuracy $\varepsilon$ takes about $\kappa \ln\big((f(\mathbf{x}_0)-f^\ast)/\varepsilon\big)$ steps. Strong convexity also gives $\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2 \le f(\mathbf{x}_k)-f^\ast$, so the iterates converge too.

### Finite hypothesis classes

Let $\mathcal{H}$ be finite, the loss take values in $[0,1]$, and $S$ be $m$ independent examples drawn from the distribution $\mathcal{D}$, with $\mathcal{H}$ chosen before seeing $S$. Hoeffding's inequality for one hypothesis and a union bound over $\mathcal{H}$ give: with probability at least $1-\delta$, for **all** $h\in\mathcal{H}$ simultaneously,

$$|L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) - L_S(h)| \le \sqrt{\frac{\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(2/\delta)}{2m}}.$$

Hence the empirical risk minimizer $\hat{h}$ satisfies $L_{\mathcal{D}}(\hat{h}) \le \min_{h} L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) + 2\sqrt{\cdot}$ with the same square root. Because the bound is uniform, it holds for a hypothesis chosen after looking at the data. To make the deviation at most $\varepsilon$, it suffices that $m \ge (\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(2/\delta))/(2\varepsilon^2)$. In the **realizable** case (some $h^\ast\in\mathcal{H}$ has zero risk), a union bound over the hypotheses with risk above $\varepsilon$ shows that $m \ge (\ln|\mathcal{H}| + \ln(1/\delta))/\varepsilon$ examples suffice for every hypothesis consistent with the sample to have risk at most $\varepsilon$, with probability at least $1-\delta$.

### VC dimension

The **VC dimension** $d$ of a class of binary classifiers (Vapnik and Chervonenkis, 1971) is the size of the largest set of points that the class can label in all $2^d$ ways. For 0-1 loss, with $m \ge d$ independent examples and $\mathcal{H}$ fixed in advance, a standard form (constants differ across textbooks) says that with probability at least $1-\delta$, for all $h\in\mathcal{H}$,

$$L_{\mathcal{D}}(h) \le L_S(h) + \sqrt{\frac{d\,\big(\ln(2m/d)+1\big) + \ln(4/\delta)}{m}}.$$

It rests on the Sauer–Shelah lemma, which bounds the number of labelings of $m$ points by $(em/d)^d$ for $m\ge d$. For $d=10$, $m=1000$ and $\delta=0.05$, the added term is about $0.260$. In the agnostic case the sample complexity grows like $(d + \ln(1/\delta))/\varepsilon^2$, up to constants. These bounds are worst-case and distribution-free, and are often loose or vacuous (above 1) for modern networks with many parameters; the results hold as stated, but their usefulness for such models is a matter of active research.

### The bias-variance decomposition

Let $y = f(\mathbf{x}) + \epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}\epsilon=0$, $\operatorname{Var}\epsilon=\sigma^2$, with $\epsilon$ independent of the training set $S$ that determines the predictor $\hat{h}_S$. At a fixed $\mathbf{x}$, with expectation over $S$ and $\epsilon$ and squared loss,

$$\mathbb{E}\big[(y-\hat{h}_S(\mathbf{x}))^2\big] = \sigma^2 + \big(\mathbb{E}_S\hat{h}_S(\mathbf{x}) - f(\mathbf{x})\big)^2 + \operatorname{Var}_S\big(\hat{h}_S(\mathbf{x})\big).$$

That is noise plus squared bias plus variance. The decomposition is exact for squared loss; for other losses there are different decompositions.

@example
title: Using the smooth convex bound
problem: Gradient descent with step $1/L$ is run on a convex function with $L=10$ whose minimizer is at distance 2 from the start. What does the theorem guarantee after 50 steps? How many steps guarantee $f(\mathbf{x}_k)-f^\ast\le 0.01$?
step: The bound is $L\lVert\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2/(2k)$ with $L=10$ and squared distance 4.
step: At $k=50$: $10\times4/(2\times50) = 40/100 = 0.4$.
step: For accuracy $0.01$ we need $40/(2k)\le 0.01$, so $k \ge 40/0.02 = 2000$.
step: This is a guarantee, an upper bound: the actual error can be much smaller, and the theorem says nothing if $f$ is not convex or not $L$-smooth.
answer: At most 0.4 after 50 steps, and 2000 steps suffice for accuracy 0.01.

@example
title: Sample size for a finite class
problem: A finite class has $|\mathcal{H}|=1000$ hypotheses and a loss in $[0,1]$. How many examples guarantee, with probability at least 0.95, that every hypothesis has $|L_{\mathcal{D}}(h)-L_S(h)|\le 0.1$?
step: Set $\delta=0.05$ and $\varepsilon=0.1$ in $m\ge(\ln|\mathcal{H}|+\ln(2/\delta))/(2\varepsilon^2)$.
step: $\ln 1000 \approx 6.908$ and $\ln(2/0.05)=\ln 40\approx 3.689$, so the numerator is about $10.597$.
step: The denominator is $2\times0.01 = 0.02$, so $m\ge 529.8$, and the smallest integer is 530.
answer: 530 examples suffice.

@mistakes
- Dropping a condition: the $1/k$ bound needs convexity and an $L$-Lipschitz gradient with step $1/L$, and the linear rate additionally needs strong convexity.
- Applying a uniform-convergence bound to a hypothesis class chosen after looking at the data: the class must be fixed in advance (the hypothesis inside it may be chosen from the data).
- Writing the bias-variance decomposition for a loss other than squared loss, or forgetting the noise term $\sigma^2$.
- Misreading a bound as a prediction: worst-case guarantees hold for all distributions, but they can be far from actual performance.

@practice self
prompt: Timed (10 minutes). Derive the bias-variance decomposition of the expected squared error at a fixed point $\mathbf{x}$, stating your assumptions.
hint: Write $y-\hat{h}$ as $\epsilon + (f - \bar{h}) + (\bar{h} - \hat{h})$, where $\bar h=\mathbb{E}_S\hat h_S(\mathbf{x})$, and show the cross terms vanish.
solution: Assume $y=f(\mathbf{x})+\epsilon$ with $\mathbb{E}\epsilon=0$, $\operatorname{Var}\epsilon=\sigma^2$, and $\epsilon$ independent of the training set $S$, which determines the predictor $\hat h_S$. Write $\bar h=\mathbb{E}_S\hat h_S(\mathbf{x})$. Then $y-\hat h_S(\mathbf{x}) = \epsilon + (f(\mathbf{x})-\bar h) + (\bar h-\hat h_S(\mathbf{x}))$. Square and take expectations. The term $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon^2]=\sigma^2$. The term $(f-\bar h)^2$ is a constant, the squared bias. The term $\mathbb{E}_S(\bar h-\hat h_S)^2$ is the variance of the predictor. The cross terms: $\mathbb{E}[\epsilon(\cdot)]=0$ because $\epsilon$ is independent of $S$ with mean zero, and $\mathbb{E}_S[(\bar h-\hat h_S)]=0$ by the definition of $\bar h$, which kills the cross term with the constant $(f-\bar h)$. Hence the expected squared error equals $\sigma^2+(f-\bar h)^2+\operatorname{Var}_S\hat h_S(\mathbf{x})$. This is exact for squared loss; the expectation is over training sets and noise, at a fixed $\mathbf{x}$.
rubric: States the assumptions: additive zero-mean noise independent of the training set, squared loss, fixed $\mathbf{x}$, expectation over $S$ and $\epsilon$.
rubric: Uses the three-term split and shows that each cross term has zero expectation, with the reason.
rubric: Identifies the three terms (noise, squared bias, variance) correctly in the final formula.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed (15 minutes). Let $f$ be convex and $L$-smooth with minimizer $\mathbf{x}^\ast$. Prove that gradient descent with step $1/L$ satisfies $f(\mathbf{x}_K)-f^\ast\le L\lVert\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2/(2K)$ for $K\ge1$. You may use the descent lemma, $f(\mathbf{y})\le f(\mathbf{x})+\langle\nabla f(\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rangle+\tfrac{L}{2}\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\rVert^2$, and convexity.
hint: First show $f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\le f(\mathbf{x}_k)-\frac{1}{2L}\lVert\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)\rVert^2$, then expand $\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2$ and sum over $k$.
solution: Step 1 (descent). Put $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_k-\tfrac1L g_k$ with $g_k=\nabla f(\mathbf{x}_k)$ in the descent lemma: $f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\le f(\mathbf{x}_k)-\tfrac1L\lVert g_k\rVert^2+\tfrac1{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2=f(\mathbf{x}_k)-\tfrac1{2L}\lVert g_k\rVert^2$. So $f(\mathbf{x}_k)$ is nonincreasing. Step 2 (distance). $\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2=\lVert\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2-\tfrac2L\langle g_k,\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rangle+\tfrac1{L^2}\lVert g_k\rVert^2$. By convexity, $\langle g_k,\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rangle\ge f(\mathbf{x}_k)-f^\ast$, and by step 1, $\tfrac1{L^2}\lVert g_k\rVert^2\le\tfrac2L\big(f(\mathbf{x}_k)-f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})\big)$. Hence $\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k+1}-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2\le\lVert\mathbf{x}_k-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2-\tfrac2L\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{k+1})-f^\ast\big)$. Step 3 (sum). Summing over $k=0,\dots,K-1$ and dropping $\lVert\mathbf{x}_K-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2\ge0$: $\tfrac2L\sum_{k=1}^{K}(f(\mathbf{x}_k)-f^\ast)\le\lVert\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2$. Since $f(\mathbf{x}_k)$ is nonincreasing, each term is at least $f(\mathbf{x}_K)-f^\ast$, so $\tfrac{2K}{L}(f(\mathbf{x}_K)-f^\ast)\le\lVert\mathbf{x}_0-\mathbf{x}^\ast\rVert^2$, which is the claim.
rubric: Derives the descent inequality correctly from the descent lemma with step $1/L$.
rubric: Expands the squared distance and uses convexity and the descent inequality to get the telescoping inequality.
rubric: Sums, uses monotonicity of $f(\mathbf{x}_k)$, and reaches the stated bound with the right constant.
