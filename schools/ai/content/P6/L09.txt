Applications and responsible AI questions test whether you can be exact about definitions and honest about what a theorem does and does not say. Programs differ in what they include in this part of an exam, so check your department's list. Here are the results most often examined.

### Fairness criteria

For a binary classifier $\hat Y$, a true label $Y$ and a group attribute $A$, three common criteria are:

- **Demographic parity**: $P(\hat Y=1\mid A=a)$ is the same for all groups.
- **Equalized odds**: $P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=y,A=a)$ is the same across groups for $y=0$ and $y=1$, that is, equal false positive rates and equal true positive rates (equal false negative rates). Requiring only equal true positive rates is often called equal opportunity.
- **Predictive parity**: $P(Y=1\mid \hat Y=1,A=a)$, the positive predictive value (PPV), is the same across groups. Calibration within groups requires the analogous statement for scores.

**Incompatibility.** Let $p_a=P(Y=1\mid A=a)$ be the base rate of group $a$. Chouldechova (2017) showed that for a binary classifier,
$$\mathrm{FPR}_a=\frac{p_a}{1-p_a}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}_a}{\mathrm{PPV}_a}\cdot\mathrm{TPR}_a.$$
Hence if two groups have **different base rates**, the classifier is **imperfect**, and PPV is equal across the groups, then the false positive rates and true positive rates cannot both be equal. Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) proved an analogous result for risk scores: calibration within groups, equal average scores among true positives, and equal average scores among true negatives cannot all hold, except when prediction is perfect or the base rates are equal. These are mathematical facts about the criteria. They do not say which criterion a particular application should use: that depends on the harms, the context, the law and the values involved, and informed people disagree.

### Differential privacy

A randomized mechanism $M$ is **$\varepsilon$-differentially private** (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith, 2006) if for all pairs of neighboring data sets $D,D'$ (differing in one individual's record) and all sets $S$ of outputs, $P(M(D)\in S)\le e^{\varepsilon}P(M(D')\in S)$. Approximate $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differential privacy adds $\delta$ to the right side. What counts as neighboring (adding or removing a record, or replacing one) varies, so state it. The **L1 sensitivity** of a query $f$ is $\Delta=\max_{D\sim D'}\lVert f(D)-f(D')\rVert_1$. The **Laplace mechanism** releases $f(D)+(Z_1,\dots,Z_k)$ with $Z_j$ independent Laplace noise of scale $b=\Delta/\varepsilon$, density $\frac1{2b}e^{-|z|/b}$, variance $2b^2$ and tail $P(|Z|>t)=e^{-t/b}$. It is $\varepsilon$-differentially private. Two more facts: **sequential composition** (running mechanisms with parameters $\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_k$ on the same data is $(\sum\varepsilon_i)$-private) and **post-processing** (any function of a private output, not touching the data, is equally private). A count has $\Delta=1$; the mean of $n$ values in $[0,1]$ has $\Delta=1/n$. A small $\varepsilon$ means strong privacy and more noise; what value is acceptable is a policy choice, not a mathematical one.

### Laws and standards (check the current text)

Laws and standards change, so treat the following as facts as of the date of this course and check the current text before relying on them.

- **EU AI Act**: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, in force from 1 August 2024, risk-based: prohibited practices, high-risk systems, transparency obligations for some systems, minimal risk, and separate rules for "general-purpose AI model"s, with models trained with more than $10^{25}$ floating-point operations presumed to pose systemic risk. The prohibitions and the AI literacy duty apply from 2 February 2025; obligations for general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025; the transparency obligations of Article 50 from 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, adopted in June 2026, in force from 27 July 2026) moved the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by Annex I; it added two prohibitions from 2 December 2026 (realistic sexually explicit content of an identifiable person without consent, and child sexual abuse material), gave providers of generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 for the marking duty of Article 50(2), and kept a revised AI literacy duty.
- **United States**: no comprehensive federal AI statute may be described as enacted, and federal preemption of state AI laws has been debated in Congress since 2025, so check its status. EO 14110 was revoked in January 2025 and EO 14179 issued; America's AI Action Plan followed in July 2025; EO 14365 (11 December 2025) directs agencies to challenge and discourage state AI laws they judge burdensome, but an executive order does not by itself preempt state law. California's SB 53 (signed 29 September 2025, in effect 1 January 2026) requires developers of models trained with more than $10^{26}$ operations to publish transparency reports and, above \$500 million in annual revenue, a frontier AI safety framework; it requires critical safety incident reporting and protects whistleblowers.
- **Standards and principles**: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (January 2023) has four functions, Govern, Map, Measure and Manage; ISO/IEC 42001 (2023) is a standard for AI management systems; the OECD AI Principles (2019) were updated in 2024.

A common approximation for the training compute of a dense transformer is $6ND$ operations for $N$ parameters and $D$ training tokens; it is a rule of thumb, and legal thresholds follow the methods that the law or regulators specify.

@example
title: Equal PPV forces unequal error rates
problem: Two groups have base rates $0.3$ and $0.1$. A classifier has the same PPV $=0.8$ and the same TPR $=0.6$ in both. What are the false positive rates, and is equalized odds satisfied?
step: Group 1: $\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{0.3}{0.7}\cdot\frac{0.2}{0.8}\cdot0.6=0.4286\cdot0.25\cdot0.6=0.0643$.
step: Group 2: $\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{0.1}{0.9}\cdot0.25\cdot0.6=0.1111\cdot0.15=0.0167$.
step: The true positive rates are equal but the false positive rates differ ($0.064$ against $0.017$), so equalized odds fails even though predictive parity holds.
answer: FPRs of about $0.064$ and $0.017$; equalized odds fails because the base rates differ.

@example
title: Laplace noise for a count
problem: A counting query with sensitivity 1 is released with the Laplace mechanism at $\varepsilon=0.5$. What are the noise scale and standard deviation, and how likely is an error larger than 4?
step: The scale is $b=\Delta/\varepsilon=1/0.5=2$.
step: The variance is $2b^2=8$, so the standard deviation is $\sqrt8\approx2.83$.
step: $P(|Z|>4)=e^{-4/2}=e^{-2}\approx0.135$.
answer: $b=2$, standard deviation about 2.83, and a 13.5% chance of an error above 4.

@mistakes
- Reading an incompatibility theorem as a verdict that fairness is impossible or that one criterion is correct: it states which criteria cannot hold together, under unequal base rates and imperfect prediction.
- Forgetting the neighboring-data-set definition when stating a privacy guarantee, or computing the noise scale with the wrong sensitivity.
- Quoting law from memory: dates and thresholds change, and the current official text is the authority.
- Treating a small $\varepsilon$ as a guarantee of no harm: differential privacy bounds what the output reveals about one record under the stated definition, not every risk.

@practice self
prompt: Timed (10 minutes). Prove the identity $\mathrm{FPR}=\frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot\mathrm{TPR}$ for a binary classifier with base rate $p=P(Y=1)$, and state in two sentences what it implies for two groups with unequal base rates.
hint: Write the counts of true and false positives in terms of $p$, TPR and FPR, then use $\mathrm{PPV}=TP/(TP+FP)$.
solution: Let $\mathrm{TPR}=P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=1)$ and $\mathrm{FPR}=P(\hat Y=1\mid Y=0)$. Then the probability of a true positive is $P(\hat Y=1,Y=1)=p\,\mathrm{TPR}$ and of a false positive is $(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}$. The positive predictive value is $\mathrm{PPV}=\dfrac{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}+(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}}$. Solving: $\dfrac{1}{\mathrm{PPV}}-1=\dfrac{(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}$, that is, $\dfrac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}=\dfrac{(1-p)\mathrm{FPR}}{p\,\mathrm{TPR}}$, so $\mathrm{FPR}=\dfrac{p}{1-p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\mathrm{PPV}}{\mathrm{PPV}}\cdot\mathrm{TPR}$ (for $0<p<1$, $\mathrm{TPR}>0$, $\mathrm{PPV}>0$). Implication: if two groups share PPV and TPR but have different $p$, the factor $p/(1-p)$ differs, so their FPRs differ unless the factor $(1-\mathrm{PPV})\mathrm{TPR}$ is zero, which means perfect prediction. So predictive parity with equal error rates is impossible for an imperfect classifier under unequal base rates; which criterion to prioritize is a question of values and context, not of mathematics.
rubric: Expresses true and false positive probabilities with $p$, TPR and FPR and writes PPV correctly.
rubric: Solves algebraically for FPR without error and states the conditions needed (nonzero denominators).
rubric: States the implication correctly (unequal base rates, imperfect classifier, equal PPV and TPR force unequal FPR) without claiming which criterion is right.

@quiz self
prompt: Timed (12 minutes). Prove that the Laplace mechanism $M(D)=f(D)+(Z_1,\dots,Z_k)$ with independent $Z_j\sim\mathrm{Laplace}(b)$ and $b=\Delta/\varepsilon$, where $\Delta$ is the L1 sensitivity of $f$, is $\varepsilon$-differentially private.
hint: Compare the output densities at the same point $\mathbf{y}$ for neighboring $D$ and $D'$, and use the triangle inequality.
solution: Let $D,D'$ be neighbors. The output of $M(D)$ has density $p_D(\mathbf{y})=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac1{2b}\exp\!\big(-|y_j-f_j(D)|/b\big)$, and similarly for $D'$. The ratio is $\dfrac{p_D(\mathbf{y})}{p_{D'}(\mathbf{y})}=\exp\!\Big(\dfrac{\sum_j(|y_j-f_j(D')|-|y_j-f_j(D)|)}{b}\Big)$. By the triangle inequality, $|y_j-f_j(D')|-|y_j-f_j(D)|\le|f_j(D)-f_j(D')|$, so the sum is at most $\lVert f(D)-f(D')\rVert_1\le\Delta$. Hence the ratio is at most $\exp(\Delta/b)=e^{\varepsilon}$ for every $\mathbf{y}$. For any measurable set $S$ of outputs, integrating the density inequality gives $P(M(D)\in S)=\int_Sp_D\le e^{\varepsilon}\int_Sp_{D'}=e^{\varepsilon}P(M(D')\in S)$, which is the definition of $\varepsilon$-differential privacy.
rubric: Writes the densities of the two outputs and forms their ratio correctly.
rubric: Uses the triangle inequality and the definition of L1 sensitivity to bound the ratio by $e^{\Delta/b}$.
rubric: Concludes $e^{\varepsilon}$ for every output, and extends from densities to sets by integration.
