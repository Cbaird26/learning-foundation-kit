@article
A reviewer reads a dozen workshop submissions in an evening. She will remember the papers that say clearly what they did, show a number with its uncertainty, and admit what they could not do. A short paper cannot hide behind length: every paragraph must earn its place. This lesson shows how to write one from a reproduction and an extension, and the Week 5 and 6 deliverable is a draft of the method and results sections.

### Check the rules first

Workshops differ. Check the **call for papers** for the page limit and whether references and appendices count; the template; whether submissions are anonymous; whether the workshop is archival (the paper appears in proceedings and may count as prior publication) or non-archival; the policy on work that is also under review elsewhere; the policy on use of writing assistants and on disclosure of generated text; and what ethics or reproducibility statements are required. These rules differ and change from year to year, and breaking them can get a paper rejected without being read. Your advisor or program may have additional requirements, such as approval before submission.

### Structure of a short paper

A common structure for four to six pages is the following.

| Section | Job | Rough share |
|---|---|---|
| Abstract | Claim, evidence, caveat, in about 150 to 250 words | n/a |
| Introduction | Question, why it matters, what you did, contributions | 10 to 15% |
| Related work | The papers the reader must know, fairly described | 5 to 10% |
| Method | What you ran, exactly enough to rerun | 15 to 25% |
| Experiments and results | Setup, tables, figures, analysis | 30 to 40% |
| Limitations and conclusion | What you could not show; what follows | 5 to 10% |

The shares are guidelines, not rules. In a reproduction paper, put the reproduction target and protocol first, since the contribution is the evidence.

### Method section

Write the method so that a competent reader could rerun it. State the target claim, the model, data and splits, the training recipe, hyperparameters and how they were chosen (on validation data, with what budget), the number of seeds, the hardware and the software versions, and each deviation from the original paper. Put long tables of hyperparameters in an appendix if the venue allows, and always say where they are. Cite the original work for anything you reuse, and say which code is theirs and which is yours.

### Results section

Lead with the claim each table tests. Give **means over seeds with a labeled uncertainty**: "mean over five seeds, plus or minus one standard error" or a 95% interval, never an unlabeled plus-minus. Report all runs somewhere, including failures. A good results paragraph has four parts: what was compared; the main number and its uncertainty; what the reader should take from it; and one sentence on what it does not show. Say "we did not observe" when you mean a null result, and "consistent with" when an interval contains the reported value. Tables are usually clearer than prose for many numbers; describe each figure's content in its caption so that a reader can follow without the text.

### The abstract

Write the abstract last. Four moves: the question, what you did, the main result with its uncertainty, and the caveat. "We reproduce the central result of paper X on data set Y within 0.8 points over five seeds, fail to reproduce its second claim, and find with an ablation that component Z contributes about 0.7 points, with a wide interval." Avoid adjectives that carry no evidence.

### The limitations paragraph

Write down what a skeptical reader would object to, before she does: few seeds, one data set, tuning budgets that were small or unequal, unreported details of the original, compute limits, and what you did not test. Many venues require a limitations section or checklist; Pineau and colleagues (2021) describe a checklist used at a major conference that asks authors about code, data, hyperparameters, seeds, compute and statistical reporting, and similar checklists are now common. A frank limitations paragraph raises a paper's credibility.

### Credit and conduct

Cite everything you use, paraphrase rather than copy, and give credit to the authors of code and data. Authorship norms vary by field and group, so agree on authorship with your advisor and collaborators early, in writing, and name everyone whose contribution meets your group's criteria. Disclose conflicts of interest where the venue asks. And never submit a paper whose numbers you cannot trace to a logged run.

@example
title: Budgeting pages and days
problem: A workshop allows six pages of content, with references extra. You plan the method to take 20% of the space and the results 35%. How many pages is each? You also have 15 working days of writing tasks (method 3, results 4, introduction and related work 3, abstract and limitations 2, internal review 3) and want a 20% buffer. How many working days should you plan, and how many weeks at five working days per week?
step: Method: $0.20 \times 6 = 1.2$ pages. Results: $0.35 \times 6 = 2.1$ pages. The rest, 2.7 pages, covers the introduction, related work, limitations and conclusion, and any figures you want space for.
step: The tasks add to $3 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 3 = 15$ working days.
step: With a 20% buffer, plan $15 \times 1.2 = 18$ working days.
step: At five working days per week, that is $18 / 5 = 3.6$ weeks, so start at least four weeks before the deadline.
answer: Method 1.2 pages, results 2.1 pages; plan 18 working days, which is 3.6 weeks, so start at least four weeks ahead.

@example
title: From numbers to a results paragraph
problem: You have, for the full method, a mean accuracy of 71.1 over five seeds with standard error 0.43; for the method without component X, 70.4 with the paired mean difference 0.70 and 95% interval $[0.003, 1.397]$; the paper reports 72.1 for the full method. Write the results paragraph.
step: Say what was compared and the protocol: five seeds, paired, the paper's test split.
step: Give the main numbers with labeled uncertainty and where the reported value falls.
step: State what the reader should take away and what the data do not show.
answer: "Over five seeds, the full method reached a mean test accuracy of 71.1% (standard error 0.43), and the 95% interval, 69.9 to 72.3, contains the 72.1% reported by the original authors. Removing component X lowered mean accuracy by 0.70 points (paired 95% interval 0.003 to 1.40), which is consistent with a small benefit but, with five seeds, only weakly supports it. We did not test other data sets, so these results do not show that the benefit transfers."

@mistakes
- Submitting without reading the call for papers: page limits, anonymity and dual-submission rules are enforced, and they differ by venue and year.
- An unlabeled plus-minus in a table: readers cannot tell whether it is a standard deviation, a standard error or an interval.
- A limitations paragraph that lists only trivial limits: name the objections a skeptic would actually raise.
- Writing the abstract's claims first and the experiments afterwards: claims should be written from the evidence.

@practice self
prompt: Write the results paragraph (80 to 130 words) for these findings. Target: reproduce a paper's reported test accuracy of 88.0% for a small network on a public data set. Your five seeds gave a mean of 86.9% with a sample standard deviation of 0.80 (standard error 0.36; 95% interval 85.9 to 87.9, using $t^\ast = 2.776$). An ablation without the paper's learning-rate warmup, three seeds, gave 85.1%. You tuned only on validation data and used the paper's test split.
hint: Include the protocol, the numbers with labeled uncertainty, where the reported value falls, and a sentence on what the data do not show.
solution: Following the paper's protocol and test split, five seeds of the reproduced network reached a mean test accuracy of 86.9% (sample standard deviation 0.80, standard error 0.36). The 95% interval, 85.9 to 87.9, does not contain the reported 88.0%, so we did not reproduce the number under our protocol, although the gap of 1.1 points is small. Removing the learning-rate warmup lowered the mean to 85.1% over three seeds, suggesting that warmup matters for this recipe, but three seeds give only a rough estimate. Hyperparameters were chosen on validation data only. We did not test other data sets or model sizes, and cannot say whether unreported details of the original recipe explain the remaining gap.
rubric: Reports means over seeds with a labeled uncertainty and states where the reported value falls relative to the interval.
rubric: Uses calibrated language ("did not reproduce under our protocol", "suggesting") and does not overclaim from three seeds.
rubric: States the protocol and one thing the data do not show.

@quiz self
prompt: You reproduced the central claim of a paper on one data set with five seeds (mean within 0.8 points of the reported value), failed to reproduce a second claim, and ran one ablation with a wide interval. Write (a) an abstract of 120 to 160 words and (b) a limitations paragraph of 70 to 110 words for a four-page workshop paper. Invent no numbers beyond those given; use placeholders such as [X] where you need one.
hint: Abstract: question, what you did, result with uncertainty, caveat. Limitations: objections a skeptic would raise.
solution: (a) Abstract. Published reports of new methods are rarely accompanied by independent checks of their central claims. We reproduce the headline result of a recent paper on image classification using its released code and a small compute budget, and extend it with one ablation. Over five seeds, the mean test accuracy of our reproduction lies within 0.8 points of the reported value. A second claim of the paper, [claim], did not reproduce under our protocol: our estimate differs from the reported one by [X] points, and we could not close the gap after the checks described in the paper. An ablation suggests that one component contributes about [Y] points, but its interval is wide, so the evidence is weak. We release code, configurations and all run logs with instructions to rerun every number. (b) Limitations. Our conclusions rest on one data set, one model size and five seeds, which limits the precision of every estimate; the ablation in particular cannot distinguish a small benefit from none. Several details of the original recipe are unreported, and our choices for them may differ from the authors'. We tuned all variants with the same small budget, which may favor settings that the original authors tuned more heavily. We did not test other data sets or larger models, so our results say nothing about scale. The failure to reproduce the second claim should be read as a statement about our protocol, not about the original work.
rubric: The abstract states the question, what was done, the main result with uncertainty, and a caveat, without unsupported adjectives.
rubric: The limitations name specific skeptical objections (few seeds, one data set, unreported details, tuning budgets) and avoid blaming the original authors.
rubric: Honest handling of invented numbers: placeholders or only given numbers, and consistent with the facts stated.
