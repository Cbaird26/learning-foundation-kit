@article
A student asks, "Should I do a PhD?" The question hides three others: What kind of work do I want to do every day? Under what conditions can I do it well? And what does my life outside research require? There is no single right answer, and no route is a prerequisite for respect or a consolation prize for another. This lesson describes the common routes accurately and generally. Details differ by country, institution, employer and year, so verify the rules and conditions for each option you consider.

### The PhD route

A doctoral program trains you to pose and answer research questions independently, and ends with a thesis and a defense. In many programs in the United States the first years include coursework, a qualifying exam (Lessons 7 to 10), a thesis proposal, years of research, and a defense; in many programs elsewhere, the degree begins directly with research and has a different structure and length. The time it takes varies widely by country, field and individual. Funding arrangements also vary: some programs guarantee funding for a number of years, often through research or teaching assistantships or fellowships, and others do not. The relationship with your advisor (Lesson 11) strongly shapes the experience. A PhD suits people who want to define research questions, to write papers and teach, or to enter roles where the degree is expected, and it demands years of sustained, uncertain work.

### Research master's degrees and similar programs

A research-oriented master's degree, a research assistant or "research engineer" position, or a residency or fellowship program in a lab can give research experience and publications with a shorter commitment. Some lead into doctoral programs; others are destinations in themselves. Their structure and funding differ and are not standardized.

### Industry, government and nonprofit labs

Organizations employ researchers and research engineers in roles whose titles and expectations differ from one employer to the next. Questions that matter, and that you should ask any employer: Who chooses the research questions, and how much freedom is there? What is the **publication policy**: is there a review, do papers need approval, are some results kept internal? Who owns the **intellectual property** you produce, and what do your agreements say about it, including work done on your own time? What compute and data are available? How is success measured, and how stable are priorities? Who will mentor you? Different organizations answer these in different ways, and the answers change with the organization's priorities, so rely on written terms and conversations with current employees, not on reputation.

### Academic positions after the PhD

Postdoctoral positions, research positions and faculty jobs combine research with mentoring, teaching, writing grants and service. The balance depends on the institution type: research-intensive universities, teaching-focused colleges and other institutions weigh these duties differently. Funding and positions are competitive in many places, and norms about timelines vary by country.

### Independent research

Independent researchers work outside a formal institution, perhaps alongside another job. Resources are thinner but freedom is real: you can choose questions without permission. Practical elements include a mentor or community (reading groups, public reproduction challenges, open-source projects), preprints and workshops to share work, small grants or fellowships where they exist, and honest accounting of how much you can do: your time, not compute, is often the limit. A good independent project looks like this course's project: a modest, well-scoped question, careful methods, and honest reporting.

### How to decide

Compare options on the same dimensions: questions and freedom; mentoring; publication and IP terms; resources; stability and funding; location and visa or immigration rules, which differ for each person and country; and fit with your life, including family, health and finances, which are legitimate factors. Talk to people on each route, ask the questions above, and remember that moves between routes are common. Avoid decisions based on rankings or prestige alone, since they say little about your own fit.

### Planning an application

For doctoral programs, typical materials are a research statement or statement of purpose, letters from people who know your work, transcripts, and sometimes test scores or a writing sample; requirements, deadlines and fees differ by program and year. Letter writers need weeks of notice, a summary of your work and clear deadlines. Start early, collect feedback on drafts, and keep a buffer for technical problems.

### The research statement

A strong one-page statement has four parts: (1) the **question** you care about, in plain terms; (2) the **evidence** that you can do research: your reproduction, what you found, and what it taught you, stated with numbers and uncertainty and not adjectives; (3) the **direction** you want to pursue, with a first project; and (4) **fit**: why this program, group or role, with specific references to their work. Do not exaggerate or claim credit for work that was not yours; programs read many statements and value honesty and clarity.

@mistakes
- Treating one route as the default and the others as failures: researchers work in universities, industry, government, nonprofits and independently, and many move between them.
- Accepting a position without reading the publication and intellectual property terms: they decide what you can share, and they are hard to renegotiate later.
- Relying on rankings or reputation instead of fit with a specific advisor, team and set of questions.
- Writing a statement of adjectives ("passionate", "innovative") instead of evidence of what you did and learned.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page research statement (about 350 to 450 words) for a hypothetical applicant. Her evidence: a reproduction of a published image-classification result, five seeds, mean 0.8 points below the reported value with a 95% interval that includes it; an ablation suggesting one component contributes about 0.7 points with a wide interval; and a four-page workshop-style paper with released code. Her direction: how evaluation practices (seeds, tuning budgets) change conclusions in small-scale experiments. Include the four parts: question, evidence, direction with a first project, and fit with a hypothetical group that studies evaluation methodology.
hint: Use numbers with uncertainty, say what you could not conclude, and make the first project specific.
solution: Question. When a paper reports that a new method beats a baseline by a point or two, how often would the conclusion survive more seeds and equal tuning for the baseline? I want to understand how everyday evaluation choices change what researchers believe. Evidence. I reproduced the central result of a published image-classification paper using released code on a small budget. Over five seeds, my mean test accuracy was 0.8 points below the reported number, and my 95% interval contained it, so my data were consistent with the claim, though five seeds leave the interval wide. An ablation of one component suggested a contribution of about 0.7 points, with an interval from nearly zero to about 1.4, so I could not say whether the component matters. These results taught me how much of a one-point difference can be seed noise, and that tuning effort must be matched across variants. I wrote the work up as a four-page workshop-style paper and released the code and logs with instructions. I did not test other data sets, and I cannot say whether the effects I saw hold at larger scale. Direction. My first project would take five published method comparisons in small-scale image classification and measure how often the sign of the comparison changes with the number of seeds and the tuning budget. I would then test a simple reporting protocol (paired seeds, fixed tuning budgets, intervals) to see whether it reduces such reversals. The risks are small effects, which I would report as such, and limited compute, which I would manage with small models. Fit. Your group's work on evaluation methodology addresses the questions I care about, and I would value your guidance on statistics for comparing methods. I would bring practical experience with reproduction and a commitment to careful reporting.
rubric: Reports evidence with numbers and uncertainty, states what was not shown, and claims only what the facts support.
rubric: States a specific question and a first project with a realistic risk and a plan to handle it.
rubric: Explains fit with specific reasons, avoids exaggeration and adjectives without evidence, and stays within the length.

prompt: An independent researcher has 12 research hours per week for 40 weeks. One complete paper (reproduction, extension and writing) takes 270 hours. The researcher finishes as many complete papers as the hours allow. How many hours remain after that?
answer: 210
unit: hours
check: 12 * 40 - (12 * 40 // 270) * 270
hint: Compute the total hours, divide by 270 and round down, then subtract the hours used.
solution: The total is $12\times40=480$ hours. One paper fits ($480/270\approx1.78$, rounded down to 1), using 270 hours, so $480-270=210$ hours remain, not enough for a second complete paper without reducing its scope.

@quiz self
prompt: A hypothetical researcher has two options: a funded doctoral position in a group whose work interests her, and a research-engineer position at an industry lab on a team working on a different problem. Write a balanced decision analysis (150 to 250 words) that names the dimensions on which to compare the options, the questions she should ask about each, and how she should decide. Do not recommend either option as better in general.
hint: Use the dimensions from the article, such as questions, mentoring, publication and IP terms, stability and life circumstances.
solution: She should compare the options on the same dimensions. Research questions and freedom: who chooses the questions, and how close are they to what she wants to study in five years? Mentoring: who will supervise her day to day, how often will they meet, and what do current members say about it? Publication and intellectual property: what are the review processes, who owns her work, and can she share code and data? Resources and stability: compute access, funding for the whole doctoral program or the role's expected duration and how the organization's priorities change. Outcomes: where do people from each place go next, and what does she want that to be? Life circumstances: location, visa rules, finances and family, which matter for her and are legitimate. For the doctoral option she should ask the advisor about authorship practices and funding guarantees; for the industry option she should read the agreement on publication and intellectual property and ask how the team's questions are chosen. She can decide by writing what she wants from the next five years, scoring both options on those dimensions with the evidence she has, reading the weakest score on each, and asking whether the gaps could be repaired. Moves between routes are common, so the decision need not be permanent.
rubric: Compares both options on explicit, parallel dimensions including publication and IP terms and mentoring.
rubric: Lists specific questions to ask about each option and ties the decision to the person's goals and circumstances.
rubric: Remains balanced, with no claim that either route is better in general, and notes that the decision is reversible.
