@lesson
title: When results do not match: debugging and honest reporting
summary: Diagnose a mismatch between your reproduction and the paper in a disciplined order, and report what did and did not reproduce with its uncertainty.
objective: Triage a discrepancy: separate noise from a real gap, then test the pipeline, the data and the protocol one change at a time.
objective: Recognize data leakage and implementation errors that inflate or deflate results.
objective: Write an honest report of a partial failure to reproduce, with uncertainty and without blame.

@article
Your five seeds average 55.7% and the paper reports 58.0%. What now? The worst responses are at the two extremes: giving up after one failed run, or fiddling with settings until the number reaches the table and then reporting only that. Between them lies a method, and reporting what you found, including the failure, is itself a result.

### Step 1: Is the gap real?

Compute the mean, standard error and interval as in Lesson 3. If the reported value lies inside your interval, you may have no discrepancy, only noise. If it lies outside, measure the gap in standard errors. A gap of several standard errors is unlikely to be seed noise alone, but remember that the paper's number may itself come from one favorable run, and that your tolerance, fixed in your plan, decides whether the gap counts as a failure.

### Step 2: Check the pipeline from the data upward

Most discrepancies come from boring causes. Work in order, changing one thing at a time and logging each change.

1. **Data.** Do you have the same data version, split and label mapping? Look at a few raw examples and the labels you produce. Check normalization (the mean and standard deviation used), image size, tokenization, and augmentation.
2. **Leakage.** Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) surveyed fields that use machine learning and found that data leakage, where information from the test data influences training or model selection, is a widespread cause of results that fail to reproduce. Typical forms are duplicates across train and test, preprocessing fitted on all the data before splitting, and tuning on the test set. Leakage usually inflates a result, so a reproduction that scores lower than the paper may be revealing a leak in the original, or may have a bug of its own: check both before drawing conclusions.
3. **Implementation.** Test pieces in isolation with tiny inputs whose answers you can compute by hand: the loss function, the metric, the learning-rate schedule, the weight decay (is it applied to biases and normalization parameters, as the paper intends?).
4. **Sanity checks of training.** A classifier over $K$ classes at initialization should have a cross-entropy near $\ln K$ nats. A model should be able to overfit a tiny batch of ten examples to nearly zero training loss; if it cannot, the bug is in the model, loss or optimizer, not in the data scale. Compare with trivial baselines (majority class) and check that the loss goes down.
5. **Protocol details.** Compare your training length, batch size, optimizer settings and checkpoint choice with the paper's appendix. Papers often omit details; seeds, early stopping criteria and the hyperparameter search budget are common gaps. Dodge and colleagues (2019) argued that reporting should include the budget of tuning runs behind a result, since how much was tried affects what was achieved. Bouthillier and colleagues (2021) argued that variance from all sources should be accounted for in comparisons. Lipton and Steinhardt (2018) wrote about troubling trends in how machine learning scholarship is presented, including failing to separate explanation from speculation; their essay is a good checklist for your own writing.
6. **Ask the authors**, politely, with a specific question and your evidence. Many authors reply; some do not. Do not post accusations.

### Step 3: Report honestly

A **partial failure to reproduce** is publishable and useful when documented well. Your report should say:

- the claim and the number you tried to match;
- what you ran, with every deviation from the paper;
- results over all seeds, with uncertainty, including failed runs;
- what you tried to resolve the gap, including attempts that did not work;
- what you can and cannot conclude: "I did not obtain 58.0% under my protocol" is different from "the paper's result is wrong";
- the possible explanations, ranked by how much evidence you have for each, and what you would test next.

Avoid language that assigns motives. Write about runs and protocols, not about people. Sculley and colleagues (2018) argued that the field needs more empirical rigor, with stronger baselines and careful accounting of where gains come from; a good reproduction report contributes in that spirit. Venues and programs have their own rules for what counts as a reportable result, and you should check the ones that apply.

@example
title: How big is the gap?
problem: Five seeds give test accuracies 55.2, 56.8, 54.9, 55.7 and 56.1 (percent), with mean 55.74, sample standard deviation 0.750 and standard error 0.336. The paper reports 58.0. Is the gap larger than seed noise, using a 95% interval with $t^\ast = 2.776$?
step: The gap is $58.0 - 55.74 = 2.26$ points.
step: In standard errors, that is $2.26 / 0.336 \approx 6.7$.
step: The 95% interval is $55.74 \pm 2.776 \times 0.336$, which is about $[54.81, 56.67]$. The reported value lies far outside it.
step: So seed noise within this protocol does not explain the gap. Next, work through the pipeline checks one at a time, starting with the data and the protocol details in the paper's appendix.
answer: The gap is about 6.7 standard errors, and 58.0 lies outside the interval, so the discrepancy needs investigation.

@mistakes
- Changing several things at once while debugging: if the number moves, you do not know why, and if it does not, you do not know what was ruled out.
- Tuning on the test set until the paper's number appears: the match is then meaningless, and the report is dishonest if it hides the procedure.
- Dropping the seeds that miss the target: report all runs, or the selection rule and its reason before looking.
- Writing "the authors' result is wrong" when your evidence shows only that you did not reproduce it under your protocol.

prompt: Five seeds give 55.2, 56.8, 54.9, 55.7 and 56.1 (percent). The paper reports 58.0. By how many standard errors does the reported value exceed your mean? Use the sample standard deviation, and give your answer to one decimal place.
answer: 6.7
tol: 0.1
check: (58.0 - np.mean([55.2, 56.8, 54.9, 55.7, 56.1])) / (np.std([55.2, 56.8, 54.9, 55.7, 56.1], ddof=1) / sqrt(5))
hint: Find the mean, the sample standard deviation and the standard error; then divide the gap by the standard error.
solution: The mean is 55.74, the sample standard deviation is about 0.7503, and the standard error is $0.7503/\sqrt{5} \approx 0.3356$. The gap is $58.0 - 55.74 = 2.26$, and $2.26 / 0.3356 \approx 6.7$ standard errors.

@practice num
prompt: A classifier has 10 classes and is initialized so that it predicts roughly uniform probabilities. About what cross-entropy loss, in nats and to three decimal places, should you expect on any batch at the start of training?
answer: 2.303
unit: nats
tol: 0.001
check: log(10)
hint: With probability $1/K$ on every class, the loss on the true class is $-\ln(1/K)$.
solution: The loss for one example is $-\ln(1/10) = \ln 10 \approx 2.303$ nats, whatever the label. A much larger or smaller starting loss suggests a bug in the loss, the labels or the initialization.

@practice num
prompt: A test set has 1,000 examples, and 8% of them also appear in the training set. A model scores 100% on the duplicated examples and 70% on the others. What is its test accuracy, as a percent to one decimal place?
answer: 72.4
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * (0.08 * 1.0 + 0.92 * 0.70)
hint: Weight the two accuracies by the fraction of examples in each group.
solution: The accuracy is $0.08 \times 1.00 + 0.92 \times 0.70 = 0.08 + 0.644 = 0.724$, or 72.4%, which is 2.4 points above the 70% earned on unseen examples: the leak inflates the score.

prompt: Your reproduction reaches 55.7% over five seeds and the paper reports 58.0%, even after you checked the data and the protocol. Which statement in your report is best supported by this evidence?
choice*: Under my protocol, with five seeds, I did not obtain the reported 58.0%; here are the deviations I know of and the checks I made.
choice: The authors' result is false.
choice: The authors must have tuned on the test set.
choice: The method does not work.
hint: Which statement is a claim about your runs, and which are claims about people or about all uses of the method?
solution: Your evidence concerns your runs and protocol. It cannot show that a result is false, nor why the authors obtained it, and it does not show the method is useless. A precise statement of what you did and found is both honest and useful to others.

@practice self
prompt: Your reproduction scores about 2.3 points below the paper's number, six standard errors away. Write a debugging plan of 6 to 8 steps, in order. For each step, say what you will check, what outcome would tell you something, and how you will avoid changing more than one thing at a time.
hint: Order the checks from cheapest and most likely to most expensive; include a sanity check of training and a data check.
solution: (1) Confirm the gap is not noise: recompute the interval with all seeds, and rerun one seed twice to measure hardware nondeterminism. A tight interval far from 58.0 means a real gap. (2) Check data: print ten raw examples and their labels, and compare the split sizes, class counts and normalization constants with the paper; any difference is a candidate cause. (3) Check for leakage on my side: count exact duplicates between train and test, and confirm that preprocessing statistics are computed on training data only. (4) Unit-test the loss and the metric on hand-computed inputs, and confirm the initial loss is near $\ln K$. (5) Overfit a batch of ten examples: if the loss does not approach zero, debug the model, loss or optimizer before anything else. (6) Compare every hyperparameter with the paper's appendix (schedule, weight decay, batch size, epochs, augmentation) and list differences. (7) Change one item from that list per run, using three seeds each and recording the effect in a table, since a single run is not informative at this noise level. (8) If the gap remains, send the authors a short message with my configuration and logs, and write up the result as a partial reproduction with the checks listed. Throughout, each change is a separate commit and each run's configuration is saved, so any difference can be traced to one change.
rubric: Starts by establishing that the gap is real, then orders checks sensibly from data and sanity checks to protocol details.
rubric: Changes one thing at a time, with logging and enough seeds to see an effect against the noise.
rubric: Includes contacting the authors and an honest write-up as a fallback, without blaming anyone.

@quiz num
prompt: The standard error of your five-seed mean is 0.4 (percentage points), and the 95% critical value for four degrees of freedom is $t^\ast = 2.776$. What is the half-width of the 95% interval, to two decimal places?
answer: 1.11
tol: 0.01
check: 2.776 * 0.4
hint: The half-width is $t^\ast$ times the standard error.
solution: $2.776 \times 0.4 = 1.1104$, which rounds to 1.11 points.

@quiz ms
prompt: Your results fall short of a paper's. Which of these are appropriate next steps? Select all that apply.
choice*: Check that data splits, normalization and labels match the paper's, and test for duplicates between train and test.
choice*: Change one setting at a time and log each run with its configuration.
choice*: Send the authors a specific, polite question with your configuration and results.
choice: Evaluate on the test set after each change and keep the setting with the highest test score.
choice: Drop the seeds that score lowest, so that the mean matches the paper.
hint: Which choices keep the evidence honest, and which use the test set or the seeds to steer the answer?
solution: Checking the data, changing one thing at a time and asking the authors are all sound. Choosing settings by test score turns the test set into a tuning set, and dropping low seeds after seeing results builds in a bias.

@quiz self
prompt: Write the paragraph of a reproduction report that covers a partial failure. Facts: the paper reports 58.0% test accuracy; your five seeds gave a mean of 55.74% (standard deviation 0.75, 95% interval 54.81 to 56.67); you matched the paper's data split and hyperparameters except that the paper does not state its learning-rate warmup, for which you used 500 steps; you tried 0 and 1,000 warmup steps with three seeds each and obtained means of 54.9 and 55.9; you have not heard from the authors. Write 120 to 180 words.
hint: State what you matched, what you did not, what you tried, and what you can and cannot conclude.
solution: We tried to reproduce the paper's reported test accuracy of 58.0% for the stated model and data split. Over five seeds, with the paper's hyperparameters and a 500-step learning-rate warmup (the paper does not state its warmup), our mean test accuracy was 55.74% (standard deviation 0.75; 95% interval 54.81 to 56.67), so the reported value lies about 6.7 standard errors above our mean. To test whether the unstated warmup explains the gap, we ran three seeds each with 0 and 1,000 warmup steps and obtained means of 54.9% and 55.9%; neither approached 58.0%. We therefore did not reproduce the reported number under our protocol. We cannot say why: possible causes include other unreported details, differences in preprocessing, or variation in the original run, and we have not been able to test them; we contacted the authors and have not yet received a reply. Code, configurations and all run logs are released, and we would welcome corrections.
rubric: States the target, the protocol and every deviation or unreported detail, with the numbers and uncertainty.
rubric: Describes what was tried to explain the gap, including attempts that did not close it.
rubric: Limits the conclusion to the evidence (did not reproduce under this protocol), with no blame, and offers code and logs.
