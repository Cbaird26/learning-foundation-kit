@article
Two students plan the same reproduction. The first writes, "I will run the code and see if I get the same results." The second writes a page: the exact table entry, the number of seeds, the budget in device-hours, a tolerance, what she will do if she misses it, and a week-by-week schedule. When both get a number 0.8 points below the paper's, only the second knows whether she succeeded. A **reproduction plan** is a promise you make to your future self before the data tempt you to move the goalposts.

### Scope: the smallest experiment that tests the claim

Start from the claim you chose in Lesson 1 and cut it down. Which single table entry, one data set and one model size, would convince you? Which comparisons are needed (the method against a baseline)? Which parts you will skip (the largest model, the extra data sets)? Say what you are skipping and why, because a reader of your report must know what your result does and does not cover. A scoped plan has a **core claim** (must reproduce) and optional **stretch goals** (only if time remains).

### The compute budget

Compute is the constraint that most often breaks plans, so estimate it early. A device is characterized by a **peak throughput** (floating-point operations per second) and the **utilization** you actually achieve, the fraction of that peak your workload reaches, which is often well below one and depends on the model, batch size and software. If one training run needs $F$ floating-point operations, then

$$\text{seconds per run} = \frac{F}{\text{peak} \times \text{utilization}}, \qquad \text{device-hours per run} = \frac{\text{seconds}}{3600}.$$

Multiply by the number of runs: configurations times seeds, plus extra runs for debugging and hyperparameter tuning. A good rule is to reserve a fixed share of the budget (a quarter to a half is typical in practice, though no rule is universal) for the unexpected. Whenever possible, measure instead of estimating: run 100 steps, time them, and extrapolate. Estimates from peak throughput are for deciding whether to start, and measured step times are for scheduling.

### Success criteria, fixed in advance

A **success criterion** says what outcome counts as reproduced. Three kinds are common.

- **Point tolerance.** The reproduced mean lies within an **absolute difference** $|\hat{m} - m|\le \delta$ of the reported value $m$, or within a **relative difference** $|\hat{m} - m| / |m| \le \rho$. Absolute and relative tolerances can disagree, so state which one you use.
- **Interval criterion.** The reported value lies inside your confidence interval for the mean over seeds (Lesson 3), or your interval and the paper's overlap.
- **Qualitative criterion.** The ordering of methods, or the sign of an improvement, is preserved even if the numbers shift.

Choose tolerances from the seed-to-seed variation you expect, not from what you later observe. If the authors report a standard deviation, use it; otherwise measure your own spread in the first runs and declare the tolerance before the main runs. Reproduction rules of thumb, such as "within one percent", are conventions that differ across communities, so justify yours.

### Stopping rules and risks

Decide in advance what you will do when a run fails: how many debugging days you allow before contacting the authors, when you switch to the fallback paper, and which deviations from the paper you will document (a different library version, a smaller batch). List the main **risks** (an unreported hyperparameter, nondeterministic hardware, a data set that changed after publication) and what you will do about each.

### The plan as a document

A reproduction plan fits on one page: claim; scope; resources and licenses; budget table; seeds and tolerances; stopping rules and risks; schedule with a deliverable at the end of each week; and where code, logs and configuration will be stored. Programs and advisors may require specific formats or approvals, and you should check those requirements before you begin.

@example
title: A device-hour budget
problem: One training run needs $2 \times 10^{18}$ floating-point operations. The device has a peak throughput of $10^{14}$ operations per second, and you expect 40% utilization. You plan 3 configurations with 5 seeds each. How many device-hours does the plan need, before any reserve for debugging?
step: Effective throughput is $10^{14} \times 0.4 = 4 \times 10^{13}$ operations per second.
step: Seconds per run: $2 \times 10^{18} / (4 \times 10^{13}) = 5 \times 10^{4}$, which is $50{,}000 / 3600 \approx 13.89$ device-hours.
step: Number of runs: $3 \times 5 = 15$, so the plan needs $15 \times 13.89 \approx 208.3$ device-hours.
step: With a reserve of 30% of the total for debugging, the request is $208.3 / 0.7 \approx 297.6$ device-hours, so you should ask for about 300.
answer: About 208.3 device-hours before reserve, and about 298 with a 30% reserve.

@example
title: Absolute or relative?
problem: A paper reports 72.1% accuracy. Your five-seed mean is 71.3%. Does the reproduction meet a criterion of absolute difference at most 1.0 percentage point? Does it meet a relative difference of at most 0.5%?
step: The absolute difference is $|71.3 - 72.1| = 0.8$ points, which is within 1.0.
step: The relative difference is $0.8 / 72.1 \approx 0.0111$, or 1.11%, which exceeds 0.5%.
step: The same result passes one criterion and fails the other, which is why the plan must state the criterion, and the tolerance, before the run.
answer: It passes the absolute criterion (0.8 points) and fails the relative one (about 1.11%).

@mistakes
- Writing "we will see whether it matches" with no tolerance: any result can then be called a success.
- Choosing the tolerance after seeing the result, so the plan stops being a test.
- Forgetting debugging and tuning runs in the budget: the plan covers only the final runs, and the project stalls at half the budget.
- Quoting peak throughput as if it were achieved throughput: real utilization is lower, and it depends on the workload.

@practice num
prompt: A training run needs $6 \times 10^{17}$ floating-point operations on a device with a peak throughput of $2 \times 10^{14}$ operations per second at 30% utilization. How many device-hours does one run take? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 2.78
unit: device-hours
tol: 0.01
check: 6e17 / (2e14 * 0.3) / 3600
hint: Multiply peak throughput by utilization to get effective throughput, divide the FLOPs by it, and convert seconds to hours.
solution: Effective throughput is $2 \times 10^{14} \times 0.3 = 6 \times 10^{13}$ operations per second. Time is $6 \times 10^{17} / (6 \times 10^{13}) = 10^4$ seconds, which is $10^4 / 3600 \approx 2.78$ hours.

@practice num
prompt: A paper reports a score of 88.0. Your reproduced mean is 86.9. What is the relative difference $|\hat{m} - m| / |m|$, as a percent, to two decimal places?
answer: 1.25
unit: %
tol: 0.01
check: 100 * abs(86.9 - 88.0) / abs(88.0)
hint: The relative difference divides the absolute difference by the reported value.
solution: The absolute difference is $|86.9 - 88.0| = 1.1$. Dividing by the reported value gives $1.1 / 88.0 = 0.0125$, which is 1.25%.

answer: 84
unit: device-hours
check: 4 * 5 * 3.5 * 1.2
hint: First compute the number of runs and the hours for them, then add 20%.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-page reproduction plan for this hypothetical target: a paper reports 91.5% test accuracy for a small residual network on a public 10-class image data set, trained with a stated recipe for 30 epochs; code and data are public with permissive terms. Your budget is 100 device-hours and one run costs 4 device-hours. Include: the core claim and one stretch goal, the budget with seeds, a success criterion with a tolerance and its justification, stopping rules, two risks, and a four-week schedule.
hint: Work out how many runs the budget allows after a reserve, then split them between configurations and seeds.
solution: Core claim: the stated recipe gives a mean test accuracy over five seeds within 1.0 percentage point of 91.5%, with test accuracy measured once, at the final epoch, on the standard test split. Stretch goal: repeat with the same recipe on a second data set. Budget: each run costs 4 device-hours, so five seeds of the paper's recipe cost 20 device-hours. A baseline without the paper's key component, five seeds, costs another 20, for 40 in total. I reserve 40 device-hours for debugging and a short pilot, leaving 20 for the stretch goal, which I will run only if the core runs finish by week three. Success criterion: absolute difference at most 1.0 point between my mean and 91.5, and the reported ordering of method and baseline preserved. I justify 1.0 from a three-seed pilot in week one, whose standard deviation I will record; if the pilot spread is larger than 0.5 points, I will widen the tolerance to twice the standard error and say so before the main runs. Stopping rules: if the pilot misses by more than 3 points after two days of debugging against the paper's recipe, I contact the authors; if there is no answer by the end of week two, I switch to the fallback. Risks: an unreported detail such as the augmentation or learning-rate schedule (response: document each choice and test its effect in the pilot) and nondeterministic hardware (response: record library versions and report the spread). Schedule: week 1 environment, data checks and pilot; week 2 main runs of method and baseline; week 3 analysis, one debugging pass if needed, stretch goal; week 4 write-up and code release. All licenses were read before the start, and I will follow my program's rules on approvals.
rubric: Gives a numeric success criterion with a tolerance fixed in advance and a reason for it, not a vague "similar".

@quiz num
prompt: A budget is 150 device-hours. You reserve 20% for debugging. Each run costs 12.5 device-hours, and you compare 3 configurations with the same number of seeds each. What is the largest whole number of seeds per configuration that fits?
answer: 3
unit: seeds
check: floor(150 * 0.8 / 12.5 / 3)
hint: Find the hours left after the reserve, divide by the cost per run, then divide by the number of configurations and round down.
solution: After the reserve, $150 \times 0.8 = 120$ device-hours remain, enough for $120 / 12.5 = 9.6$ runs, so 9 whole runs. Spread over 3 configurations, that is 3 seeds each.

@quiz self
prompt: A student's plan says: "I will train the model, and if the accuracy is close to the paper's, I will say it reproduces. Budget: a few days of compute." Write a short critique (4 to 6 sentences) that names three specific weaknesses and shows how to repair each.
hint: Look for missing numbers: which table entry, how close, how many seeds, how many device-hours.
solution: First, "close" is undefined, so any result could be called a success; the plan should state a tolerance, absolute or relative, justified by expected seed-to-seed variation and fixed before running. Second, the target is unspecified: the plan should name the table entry, data set, model and evaluation protocol, so another person can check the comparison. Third, "a few days of compute" is not a budget; the plan should convert the run cost into device-hours from measured step times, multiply by seeds and configurations, and add a reserve. A fourth repair is a stopping rule and a fallback paper for a blocked target. With these changes, the plan becomes a test whose outcome is informative whichever way it falls.
rubric: Identifies the undefined tolerance and proposes a numeric one fixed in advance.
rubric: Identifies the missing target specification or missing budget arithmetic, with a concrete repair.
