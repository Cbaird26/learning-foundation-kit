@article
A first-year student tells her advisor she will "reproduce a famous paper" over the semester. Three weeks later she is stuck: the paper needs a data set she cannot download, a cluster she does not have, and a hyperparameter the authors never reported. The paper was fine. The choice was the problem. In research apprenticeship, **choosing the target is the first experiment**, and a poor choice costs more than any later mistake.

### Why reproduce at all?

Reproducing a result teaches things reading cannot: which details of a method are load-bearing, how much the numbers move from run to run, and what a paper leaves out. It is also a contribution. Several surveys and studies have asked how often published machine learning work can be reproduced. Gundersen and Kjensmo (2018) surveyed how much of the AI literature documents the method, data and experiments needed to reproduce it. Raff (2019) tried to independently reproduce 255 papers and reported that about 63.5% could be reproduced. Pineau and colleagues (2021) described a reproducibility program and checklist run at a major conference. Community reproducibility challenges, run yearly since 2018, invite students and researchers to reproduce accepted papers and publish their reports. Treat these as evidence that reproduction is common, valuable work, not as a verdict on any paper or venue.

### Words that people use differently

**Repeating** a run means the same team, code and setup. **Reproducing** usually means a different team obtains the same result from the same code or from the paper's description. **Replicating** usually means a different team tests the same claim in a different setting, such as a new data set. **Robustness** asks whether the claim survives changes in seeds, hyperparameters or data. Communities and standards bodies swap these words, so always say in your plan what you will actually do: "I will rerun the authors' code with five seeds and compare the test accuracy with Table 2."

### What makes a good target

Look for a **claim you can state as a number**: "Method A reaches 71% accuracy on data set B under protocol C." Then screen candidates on five questions.

1. **Is the claim clear?** Is there a table or figure with a number, a named data set, and a stated evaluation protocol?
2. **Is the compute modest?** Compare the reported device-hours with your budget (Lesson 2 teaches the arithmetic). Small models, small data sets and short training runs are friendly. A result that needed hundreds of devices for weeks is out of reach, though you may reproduce a small-scale version of its claim.
3. **Are code and data available?** Released code, a data set you may legally download, a stated random-seed policy, and a list of hyperparameters all raise your odds.
4. **Are you allowed to use it?** Code and data come with licenses. A paper's code with no license file does not clearly grant you permission to reuse or redistribute it; the safe course is to ask the authors or choose another target. Data sets may restrict use, redistribution or commercial use. Read the terms that apply to you, because licenses, and the rules of your institution, differ.
5. **Will you learn something?** A paper whose method touches your coursework or future thesis area repays the effort.

Weighted scores help you compare options honestly, but the weights are yours to set and should be stated before you look at the scores. A score is a thinking aid, not a measurement.

### Ask the paper's reviewers

On OpenReview, many venues publish reviews and author responses. Read them for a target: reviewers often point to missing baselines, weak variance reporting or doubts about a claim. Those concerns tell you what to check carefully. Public reviews are opinions of individual reviewers; do not treat them as rankings of papers, and follow the platform's terms when you cite them.

### Write the selection memo

Your memo should name the claim, say exactly which number you will try to match, list what you need (code, data, compute, time), flag the risks, and say what you will do if the first obstacle appears. Choose a **fallback paper** now, so that a blocked target costs days, not weeks. Check the rules that apply to you: your program may require an advisor's approval of a project, and your department or lab may have its own norms.

@example
title: Scoring two candidates
problem: You compare two candidate papers using four weighted criteria: claim clarity (weight 3), compute fit (weight 2), code and data availability (weight 2), and licensing clarity (weight 1). Each is scored 1 to 5. Paper A scores 4, 3, 5 and 2 on the four criteria. Paper B scores 5, 2, 3 and 4. Which paper has the higher weighted score, and what percent of the maximum does it earn?
step: The maximum possible total is $5 \times (3 + 2 + 2 + 1) = 40$.
step: Paper A: $3 \cdot 4 + 2 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 5 + 1 \cdot 2 = 12 + 6 + 10 + 2 = 30$, which is $30 / 40 = 75\%$.
step: Paper B: $3 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 2 + 2 \cdot 3 + 1 \cdot 4 = 15 + 4 + 6 + 4 = 29$, which is $72.5\%$.
step: The scores are close (30 against 29), so the score should not settle the choice. Look at the lowest-scoring criterion of each: Paper A's licensing is unclear (2), which you can resolve by emailing the authors; Paper B's compute fit is poor (2), which a small budget cannot fix. Paper A is the better primary choice, with Paper B as a fallback only if you can scale it down.
answer: Paper A, 30 of 40 points, which is 75%. The margin is small, so the decisive factor is whether each weak criterion can be repaired.

@example
title: A claim you can test
problem: A paper states: "our regularizer improves accuracy on small image data sets." Rewrite this as a claim a reproduction can test.
step: Name the data set, the model and the metric: for example, the paper's Table 2 reports test accuracy on one named data set with one named architecture.
step: State the comparison: regularizer against the same model without it, with the same training protocol and the same number of seeds.
step: State what would count as success before running anything: the reproduced mean test accuracy for each condition lies within a tolerance of the reported one, and the sign of the difference between conditions matches.
answer: "On the named data set, with the named architecture and protocol, the regularizer's mean test accuracy over five seeds exceeds the baseline's, and both means are within the stated tolerance of Table 2."

@mistakes
- Choosing the most famous paper instead of the most feasible one: a result you cannot afford to run teaches you nothing about reproduction.
- Assuming that released code means the result will reproduce: code can omit preprocessing, seeds, or the exact hyperparameters behind a table entry.
- Ignoring licenses and terms of use: "it was on the internet" is not a permission, and rules differ by data set, institution and country.
- Setting no fallback: one blocked download or one unreported hyperparameter can end the semester if there is no second candidate.

prompt: You score a candidate paper on four criteria with weights 3, 2, 2 and 1 (claim clarity, compute fit, code and data availability, licensing clarity). The scores, each from 1 to 5, are 3, 5, 2 and 4. What percent of the maximum possible weighted score does the paper earn? Give your answer to one decimal place.
answer: 67.5
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * sum(w * s for w, s in zip([3, 2, 2, 1], [3, 5, 2, 4])) / (5 * sum([3, 2, 2, 1]))
hint: Multiply each score by its weight and add; the maximum is 5 times the sum of the weights.
solution: The weighted total is $3 \cdot 3 + 2 \cdot 5 + 2 \cdot 2 + 1 \cdot 4 = 9 + 10 + 4 + 4 = 27$. The maximum is $5 \cdot 8 = 40$, so the paper earns $27/40 = 67.5\%$.

@practice self
prompt: You are choosing between two hypothetical papers. Paper P reports 88% test accuracy for a small convolutional network on a public image data set; code is released under a permissive license and training takes about 10 device-hours. Paper Q reports a large improvement for a new language model pretraining method; the code is partial, the data are proprietary, and training used 500 devices for several weeks. Write a selection memo of about 150 to 200 words for your advisor that chooses a primary paper and a fallback, states the exact claim you would test, and names the main risk of your choice.
hint: Compare feasibility first, then say what you could still learn from Q in a scaled-down form.
solution: I propose to reproduce Paper P as the primary target and to use a scaled-down version of Q's claim as a fallback only if my advisor thinks it worthwhile. The claim for P is: a small convolutional network with the stated training protocol reaches about 88% test accuracy on the named data set, and I will count the reproduction as successful if the mean test accuracy over five seeds lies within one percentage point of 88% (I will fix this tolerance now and justify it from the seed-to-seed spread I measure). I need the released code, the public data set (whose terms I have read), and about 70 device-hours, which covers five seeds and slack for debugging. The main risk is an unreported detail such as data augmentation or the learning-rate schedule; if the first run misses the target, I will contact the authors and document each change. Q is not reproducible at my scale: its data are proprietary, its code is partial, and its compute is far beyond my budget. A small-scale test of its central idea on public data would be a different, replication-style project, and I would label it that way.
rubric: Chooses the feasible paper and justifies the choice by data, code, license and compute, not by prestige.
rubric: States a precise, testable claim with a number, a protocol and a success tolerance fixed in advance.
rubric: Names a realistic risk (unreported details) and a concrete response, and distinguishes reproduction from a scaled-down replication.

@quiz num
prompt: A paper reports training with 8 devices for 36 hours. You have a single device and a budget of 120 device-hours. By what factor must you reduce the total device-hours of the original run to fit your budget? Give your answer to one decimal place.
answer: 2.4
tol: 0.1
check: 8 * 36 / 120
hint: First compute the original device-hours as devices times hours.
solution: The original run used $8 \times 36 = 288$ device-hours. To fit 120 device-hours you must reduce it by $288/120 = 2.4$, for example by training on a smaller data subset or for fewer steps, and then say plainly that the setup differs from the paper's.

@quiz mc
prompt: You rerun a paper's released code on its own data set, with different random seeds from the authors', and obtain the reported accuracy within normal run-to-run variation. Which conclusion does this support, whichever terminology convention you use?
choice*: The reported number is not just the result of one lucky seed, but the result has not been tested on a different data set or setting.
choice: The paper's central claim holds on every data set.
choice: The paper's method is better than every alternative method.
choice: The released code is free of errors.
hint: Ask what your experiment varied and what it held fixed.
solution: Changing the seeds tests sensitivity to randomness. Everything else was held fixed, so the experiment says nothing about other data sets, other baselines or correctness of the code. Terminology for this kind of rerun differs across communities, so describe it by what you did.

@quiz self
prompt: A classmate says, "The authors released their code, so if my run matches their table, the claim is confirmed." Write a short critique (4 to 6 sentences) that explains what such a match shows, what it does not, and what extra checks would strengthen the conclusion.
hint: Separate "my run matches" from "the claim is true", and think about what could be shared between your run and theirs.
solution: A match shows that the released code, run on the same data and protocol, yields the reported number, which is real evidence that the table is not a one-off fluke if you ran several seeds. It does not show that the claim holds beyond that setting, because your run shares the code, the data, the preprocessing and the evaluation protocol with the original, so a shared bug, a leaked test example or an unfair baseline would be reproduced too. To strengthen the conclusion, rerun the baselines under identical tuning, vary the seeds and report the spread, inspect the data split for leakage, and test one new setting, such as another data set. The classmate should say "reproduced the reported number with the released code" and reserve "confirmed the claim" for evidence beyond that.
rubric: Says what a match shows (the number is obtainable with the code and protocol), and does not overclaim.
rubric: Names a specific shared-assumption risk, such as shared bugs, leakage or weak baselines.
rubric: Proposes concrete extra checks (seeds, baselines under equal tuning, a new setting).
