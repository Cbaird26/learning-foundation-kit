@article
A second-year student says, "I want to work on robustness." That is a topic, not a direction. A direction has a question that could have an answer, a reason why the answer would matter to others, a first project that fits a year, and a sense of the next two. Choosing is partly about the science, partly about the people. This lesson addresses both, and the weeks 11 and 12 deliverable is a one-page research statement, which you will draft in Lesson 12.

### From topic to question

Start from your reproduction and extension (Lessons 1 to 6) and your exam reading (Lessons 7 to 10). Ask: where did a result surprise me? What did the authors assume that I could not check? Which experiment would I have wanted but could not run? Write the question in one sentence that a graduate student outside your area could understand, then test it:

- **Importance**: who would change what they do if the answer were known?
- **Tractability**: can a first, small piece be done with the resources you have, in months and not years?
- **Novelty**: what is known? Search the literature, including workshop papers and public reviews, and read the closest three papers carefully.
- **Fit**: does your group have the methods, data and compute?
- **Durability**: will the question still matter in three years? Fast-moving areas change; a question about a method may age quickly, and one about a phenomenon or a measurement may last longer.

### A portfolio, not a single bet

Research outcomes are uncertain. Plan a **portfolio**: one safe project (an extension you are fairly sure will yield a result), one moderately risky project, and one ambitious idea that you work on in the background. If projects succeed independently with probability $p$ each, the chance that at least one of $k$ succeeds is $1-(1-p)^k$; for $p=0.3$ and $k=3$ that is $1-0.7^3=0.657$. Real projects are not independent (they share your skills and your data) so treat the formula as an upper-end guide to why having several matters. Define in advance what counts as "enough" to publish, and when to stop.

### Advisors and groups

Advisors differ in field, working style and availability, and no ranking of advisors or programs is meaningful in general; the question is fit with **you**. Gather evidence rather than impressions.

1. **Research fit**: read recent papers from the group. Do you want to work on those questions?
2. **Mentoring style**: how often do they meet students? Do they give detailed feedback, or expect independence? What do students do when stuck?
3. **Track record with students**: where do their students go, how long do they take, and how do they describe the experience? Ask current and former students, away from the advisor.
4. **Expectations**: hours, authorship practices, who sets the project, expectations about vacations, funding responsibilities.
5. **Resources**: funding for your whole program, compute access, collaborators and data.
6. **Communication**: how do they handle disagreement? How are conflicts about credit resolved?

Programs differ in whether you apply to an advisor or to a department, whether you rotate through groups, and when you must choose; some advisors take few students; and changing advisors is possible but has costs and rules that vary. Read your program's handbook. Good questions to ask: "What would a typical first-year project look like in your group?"; "How do you decide authorship order?"; "How do your students typically get funded?"; "How would we handle it if my interests moved away from the group's?"

### Norms: authorship, credit and conflicts of interest

- **Authorship**: most fields, and many publishers' policies, base authorship on substantial contribution (to the design, the work or the writing) and agreement to be accountable for the paper, not on seniority or funding alone, and not on a gift. Orders of authors follow local conventions, which differ. Agree early, in writing, and revisit when the project changes.
- **Credit**: cite ideas and code you use. Acknowledge help that does not reach authorship.
- **Conflicts of interest**: financial or other relationships, such as an advisor's company that would benefit from your results, or a funder with a stake in the findings, should be disclosed as the institution and the venue require. Disclosure does not mean the work is invalid; hiding the relationship is the problem.
- **Power asymmetry**: advisors control funding, letters and sometimes visas, which makes frank conversation hard. Know your institution's channels (ombudsperson, graduate director, research integrity office) before you need them.

### The thesis-direction memo

A one- to two-page memo to a prospective or current advisor: the question; why it matters; what is known and the gap; a portfolio of three projects with first steps, risks and fallbacks; a twelve-month plan with milestones; and what you need from the advisor and group. Write it so that the advisor can disagree with it precisely.

@example
title: A transparent advisor-fit score
problem: You score two advisors on four criteria with weights 4 (research fit), 3 (mentoring availability), 2 (resources) and 3 (communication), each criterion from 1 to 5. Advisor X scores 4, 3, 5 and 2. What percent of the maximum does X earn, and what should you do with the number?
step: The maximum is $5\times(4+3+2+3)=60$.
step: X's score is $4\cdot4+3\cdot3+2\cdot5+3\cdot2=16+9+10+6=41$, which is $41/60\approx68.3\%$.
step: Use the number to organize your thinking, not to decide. The lowest score, communication (2), may be the one criterion that matters most to you, so look at it before the total, and gather more evidence (talk to students) on the criteria that you scored from thin evidence.
answer: 41 of 60, about 68.3%; read the weakest criterion and the evidence behind each score before deciding.

@example
title: Overlapping projects in a schedule
problem: Three papers each take 9 months of work. Paper 2 starts 3 months before Paper 1 ends, and Paper 3 starts 3 months before Paper 2 ends. How many months does the sequence take?
step: Paper 1 takes months 1 to 9.
step: Paper 2 starts at month 7 (3 months of overlap) and ends at month 15.
step: Paper 3 starts at month 13 and ends at month 21.
answer: 21 months, instead of 27 without overlap. Overlaps are optimistic: they assume you can split your attention and that no project slips.

@mistakes
- Choosing a topic because it is fashionable or because a group is famous: fit with your interests, your skills and a mentor who has time for you matters more for the next several years.
- Betting on a single project: one failed experiment can then stall a year.
- Asking only the advisor about the advisor: current and former students can tell you what the advisor cannot.
- Leaving authorship for later: disputes are far easier to prevent with an early written agreement than to resolve at submission time.

prompt: You score a group on four criteria with weights 4, 3, 2 and 3, each scored from 1 to 5. The scores are 5, 2, 3 and 4. What percent of the maximum possible score does the group earn? Give your answer to one decimal place.
answer: 73.3
unit: %
tol: 0.1
check: 100 * sum(w * s for w, s in zip([4, 3, 2, 3], [5, 2, 3, 4])) / (5 * sum([4, 3, 2, 3]))
hint: Multiply each score by its weight, add, and divide by 5 times the total weight.
solution: The weighted total is $20+6+6+12=44$, the maximum is $5\times12=60$, and $44/60\approx73.3\%$.

@practice num
prompt: A student plans 4 independent projects, each with a 20% chance of success. What is the probability that at least one succeeds? Give your answer to three decimal places.
answer: 0.590
tol: 0.001
check: 1 - 0.8 ** 4
hint: Find the probability that all four fail, then subtract from 1.
solution: All four fail with probability $0.8^4=0.4096$, so at least one succeeds with probability $1-0.4096=0.5904$, or $0.590$. Real projects are correlated, so this overstates the true chance.

@practice mc
prompt: A student ran the experiments and wrote the first draft of a paper. The advisor proposed the research question and provides the funding. Which approach to authorship is the most defensible?
choice*: Discuss and agree on authorship and order early, in writing, using the criteria of the field, the venue and the institution, which base authorship on substantial contribution and accountability.
choice: The advisor is automatically first author because the advisor funds the work.
choice: The student is automatically the only author because they ran the experiments.
choice: Authorship is decided by who submits the paper first.
hint: Which approach relies on agreed criteria rather than on seniority or on who happens to hold the files?
solution: Conventions differ, but widely used criteria tie authorship to substantive contributions (including conception and design) and accountability, and recommend early agreement. Funding alone, running the experiments alone, or being first to submit do not settle it, and the rules of the venue, program and institution take precedence.

@practice mc
prompt: Your prospective advisor co-founded a company that sells a product based on the method your project would evaluate. What is the most appropriate course?
choice*: Raise it openly, learn the institution's conflict-of-interest policy and the venue's disclosure requirements, and disclose the relationship wherever required.
choice: Say nothing, since only financial conflicts matter and this one may not be.
choice: Refuse to work with anyone who has any commercial interest.
choice: Change the experimental results so that nobody can accuse you of bias.
hint: Think about what disclosure protects and what the rules require.
solution: A relationship like this is a potential conflict of interest, not proof of wrongdoing. Open discussion and disclosure as the institution and venue require let readers weigh the work properly. Staying silent hides information, refusing all commercial links is not required, and altering results would be misconduct. The details depend on local rules.

@practice self
prompt: Write a thesis-direction memo (200 to 300 words) for this hypothetical student: she completed a reproduction showing that reported improvements from a data-augmentation scheme shrink with more seeds and equal tuning, and wants to study how evaluation practices affect conclusions in small-scale image classification. Include a question, why it matters, the gap, a three-project portfolio with risks, a twelve-month plan and what she needs from an advisor.
hint: Make the question answerable, and order the projects from safe to ambitious.
solution: Question: how much do common evaluation choices (number of seeds, tuning budgets for baselines, and choice of validation split) change which of two methods appears better in small-scale image classification, and can a cheap protocol reduce the chance of a wrong conclusion? Why it matters: published comparisons guide what others build on, and my reproduction found a claimed gain shrinking under equal tuning and more seeds. Gap: variance and tuning effects are discussed generally, but I did not find a systematic, small-scale study that quantifies them across several method pairs under a fixed budget; I will confirm this with a literature search. Projects: (1) safe: extend my reproduction to five method pairs on two public data sets, measuring how often the sign of a comparison flips with seed count and tuning budget; (2) moderate: propose and test a simple reporting protocol (paired seeds, a fixed tuning budget, intervals) by checking whether it reduces flips; (3) ambitious: build a small benchmark of method comparisons with known answers, to test any evaluation protocol. Risks: results may show little effect (still reportable as a null with intervals); compute limits (mitigate with small models). Plan: months 1 to 3 literature and a pilot; 4 to 8 project 1; 7 to 11 project 2 and a workshop paper; month 12 decide on project 3. Needs from an advisor: weekly meetings, compute access of about 2,000 device-hours, introductions to researchers working on evaluation, and agreement on authorship up front.
rubric: Poses an answerable question and justifies its importance and the gap, with the literature check stated as an assumption.
rubric: Provides a portfolio ranging from safe to ambitious, with risks and a fallback, and a twelve-month plan whose arithmetic is consistent.
rubric: States concrete needs from the advisor, including authorship agreement, and does not rank any real group or person.

prompt: A thesis plan has 3 papers of 8 months each. Paper 2 starts 2 months before Paper 1 ends, and Paper 3 starts 2 months before Paper 2 ends. How many months does the sequence take from the start of Paper 1 to the end of Paper 3?
answer: 20
unit: months
check: 8 + (8 - 2) + (8 - 2)
hint: Each later paper adds its length minus the overlap.
solution: Paper 1 ends at month 8. Paper 2 starts at month 7 and ends at month 14. Paper 3 starts at month 13 and ends at month 20. The total is $8+6+6=20$ months.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which questions help you learn whether an advisor and group are a good fit? Select all that apply.
choice*: "How do you decide authorship order, and when do you discuss it?"
choice*: "What does a typical first-year project look like in your group, and how do students get funded?"
choice*: Asking current and former students, privately, how they experienced the mentoring.
choice: "Which of the top labs in the field is yours?"
choice: "Can you promise that I will graduate in a fixed number of years?"
hint: Choose questions that gather evidence about expectations and working style.
solution: Questions about authorship, projects, funding and student experience reveal how the group works. Asking an advisor to rank their own lab invites a meaningless answer, and nobody can honestly promise a fixed completion time, since research and program rules vary.

@quiz self
prompt: Your advisor asks you to spend the next six months on a grant deliverable that is only loosely related to your thesis direction. Write a short plan (5 to 8 sentences) for how you will discuss this with your advisor, naming what you will ask, what you will offer and what you will agree in writing.
hint: Focus on understanding the need, protecting your own progress and agreeing expectations in a way that both sides can check.
solution: I will ask for a meeting and open by saying that I want to help the grant succeed and also keep my thesis moving. I will ask what exactly the deliverable requires, by when, and whether any part of it can be built from my own project's code or experiments, since a shared component would serve both. I will offer a concrete time commitment, for example 60% of my time for four months, with a milestone check-in each month, and propose that the rest of my time continue on the first project of my plan. I will ask how authorship and credit will be handled for the deliverable and for any paper that comes from it, and how the work will count toward my program's requirements and funding. I will propose that we write the agreement in a short email, covering scope, time, authorship and the date we will review it. If after the discussion the workload still threatens my progress, I will ask who else in the department I could talk to, such as a graduate director, before the conflict grows. Throughout, I will stay factual and avoid assuming that the request is unreasonable, since advisors often face funding constraints that students do not see.
rubric: Asks about scope, timing and the possible overlap with the thesis, and offers a specific compromise.
rubric: Proposes agreeing authorship, time and review dates in writing.
rubric: Uses appropriate channels and a respectful, factual tone, without assuming bad faith.
