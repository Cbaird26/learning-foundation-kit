@article
Your reproduction is done, and the paper's method works at least roughly as claimed. The extension is where you start contributing something of your own: Is the claimed benefit due to the component the authors credit, or to something else? Does it hold when the data set is smaller, noisier or different? A good extension answers one precise question and is designed so that either answer is informative.

### Ablations

An **ablation** removes or replaces one component of a method and measures what changes. If the authors say that component X causes the gain, the ablation "full method without X" tests it. The logic is that of a controlled experiment, so the same rules apply.

- **Change one factor at a time**, and keep everything else, including the training budget, the data and the evaluation protocol, identical. If removing X also changes the number of parameters, say so, or add a control that matches the parameter count.
- **Give every variant the same tuning effort.** If you tune the full method carefully and the ablated version barely at all, you have measured your tuning effort, not X. Dodge and colleagues (2019) argued for reporting the tuning budget behind results for exactly this reason.
- **Use enough seeds** that the noise (Lesson 3) is small compared with the effect you hope to see, and report intervals.
- **State the hypothesis and the decision rule in advance**: "if removing X lowers mean accuracy by at least 0.5 points, I will say X contributes."
- **Include a replacement, not only a deletion**, when a component's role is subtle: for example, replace a learned component with a fixed or random one to see whether learning matters.

With several components, a **full factorial** design tries every on-off combination: $k$ binary components give $2^k$ configurations, which grows quickly. Often a one-at-a-time ablation plus a few chosen combinations is the practical compromise, and you should say what interactions the design cannot detect.

### New settings

A **new-setting** extension asks whether the claim survives a change in the world: a smaller training set, label noise, a different data set, another model size. Choose a change that is motivated, such as the regime where the authors' argument predicts the method should matter more or less, and state the prediction first. A prediction that fails is a finding, not an embarrassment.

### Paired comparisons

When two variants are run with the same seeds, and the seed controls shared sources such as the data order and initialization, the runs are naturally **paired**. Compute the difference $d_i = a_i - b_i$ for each seed and analyze those differences: the mean $\bar{d}$, their sample standard deviation $s_d$, and $\mathrm{SE}_d = s_d / \sqrt{n}$. A 95% interval is $\bar{d} \pm t^\ast \mathrm{SE}_d$. Pairing removes the variation the two runs share and often gives a tighter interval than comparing two separate intervals, but only if the pairing is real; if the seeds do not control common sources, use an unpaired comparison instead.

### Many comparisons

Every extra comparison you make raises the chance of a false alarm. If you test six comparisons, each at a 5% error rate and independent, the chance of at least one false alarm is $1 - 0.95^6 \approx 0.265$. Two honest habits help. Declare in advance which comparisons are primary. And for the rest, either correct the threshold (the simplest correction, Bonferroni, divides the error rate by the number of comparisons, and it is conservative) or report them plainly as exploratory. Statistical software and venues differ in what they expect, so check the norms for your field.

### Negative results

Sometimes the ablation shows no effect, or the new setting breaks the method. Report it. A null result with a narrow interval is informative: it tells others what they need not try. A null result with a wide interval says only that you could not tell, and you should say that instead.

### Effect sizes and meaning

Report the size of an effect, not only whether an interval excludes zero. A difference of 0.1 points that is statistically clear may not matter; a difference of 2 points with a wide interval may matter a great deal. Say what size you would consider meaningful, and why, before you look.

@example
title: A paired comparison across five seeds
problem: Five seeds each of the full method and the method with component X removed give test accuracies of 71.2, 70.5, 72.1, 69.8 and 71.9 for the full method and 70.4, 70.1, 71.0, 69.9 and 70.6 for the ablated one, paired by seed. Does removing X lower accuracy, with a 95% interval ($t^\ast = 2.776$)?
step: The paired differences (full minus ablated) are $0.8, 0.4, 1.1, -0.1, 1.3$.
step: Their mean is $\bar{d} = 3.5/5 = 0.70$.
step: The deviations from 0.70 are $0.1, -0.3, 0.4, -0.8, 0.6$, with squares $0.01, 0.09, 0.16, 0.64, 0.36$ and sum $1.26$. So $s_d = \sqrt{1.26/4} \approx 0.561$ and $\mathrm{SE}_d = 0.561/\sqrt{5} \approx 0.251$.
step: The interval is $0.70 \pm 2.776 \times 0.251$, about $[0.003, 1.397]$. It barely excludes zero.
step: Conclusion: the data suggest that X helps by about 0.7 points on average, but with five seeds the evidence is weak, since the lower end is almost zero. More seeds, or a pre-registered larger effect, would be needed to claim more.
answer: Mean paired difference 0.70, standard error about 0.251, 95% interval about $[0.003, 1.397]$: a small benefit with weak evidence.

@mistakes
- Giving the full method more tuning than the baseline or the ablation: the difference then reflects effort, not the component.
- Changing two things in one ablation (removing a component and shrinking the model), so that the result cannot be attributed.
- Treating "not significant" as "no effect": a wide interval means the experiment could not tell.
- Running many comparisons and reporting only the one that looked good.

@practice self
prompt: A paper claims that a data-augmentation scheme is responsible for most of its method's gain on a small image data set. Design an ablation and one new-setting experiment to test this claim with a budget of 120 device-hours (one run costs 3 device-hours). State hypotheses, variants, controls, seeds, the decision rule and what you will do if the result is null.
hint: Include a variant that removes the augmentation and a control that gives the same tuning effort to every variant.
solution: Hypothesis H1: removing the augmentation lowers mean test accuracy by at least 1.0 point. Variants: (a) the full method; (b) the method without the augmentation; (c) the baseline with the augmentation but without the paper's other components, which separates the augmentation's effect from the method's. All variants use the same data split, model size, number of steps and the same tuning budget (the same three learning rates on validation data). Seeds: five per variant, paired by seed, since the seed fixes initialization and data order: 3 variants times 5 seeds is 15 runs, or 45 device-hours, plus 27 device-hours for tuning runs (three learning rates for each variant, one seed each). New setting: the claim predicts that augmentation matters more when data are scarce, so I rerun (a) and (b) with one quarter of the training set, five seeds each, which costs 30 device-hours, for a total of 102, leaving 18 as a reserve. Decision rule, fixed now: if the paired difference between (a) and (b) has a 95% interval entirely above 1.0 point, I will say that the augmentation contributes at least that much; if the interval lies within plus or minus 0.5 points, I will say that it contributes little in this setting; otherwise I will report that the experiment could not tell, and I will say how many more seeds would be needed. A null result will be reported with the interval. The paired differences, all runs and the tuning budget are reported, and the quarter-data run is labeled as a new setting, not as part of the reproduction.
rubric: Gives a precise hypothesis and a variant that removes only the augmentation, with matched tuning and training budget.
rubric: Uses paired seeds with a budget that adds up, and includes a motivated new setting with a stated prediction.
rubric: States a decision rule in advance, including what a null or inconclusive result will be called and reported.

answer: 0.239
tol: 0.001

solution: $\mathrm{SE}_d = 0.4787 / 2 \approx 0.2394$, which rounds to $0.239$.

@quiz self
prompt: Your ablation of component X gives a paired mean difference of 0.2 points with a 95% interval of $[-0.9, 1.3]$ from five seeds. A classmate wants to write "X has no effect." Write a short reply (4 to 6 sentences) that says what the interval does and does not show and what a better-designed follow-up would be.
hint: Compare the width of the interval with the effect sizes you care about.
solution: The interval contains zero, so the data do not show that X helps, but it also contains effects as large as 1.3 points in X's favor and as large as 0.9 points against it, so the experiment cannot rule out a meaningful effect either way. "No effect" is a claim that the interval would have to be narrow around zero to support. A fair sentence is "we could not detect an effect of X with five seeds (mean difference 0.2, 95% interval from -0.9 to 1.3)". The follow-up is to decide the smallest effect that matters, for example 0.5 points, and then run enough additional seeds, or a lower-noise setup, that the interval's half-width is well below it. Reporting this as a null or inconclusive result is still valuable to readers.
rubric: Explains that an interval containing zero shows only that the effect is not detected, not that it is absent.
rubric: Uses the interval's width relative to a meaningful effect size to say what is ruled out.
rubric: Proposes a concrete follow-up, with more seeds or lower noise, and a suggested honest sentence.
