@article
"Explain how attention works." A student answers for two minutes, correctly, from memory. The examiner nods, then asks, "Why divide by the square root of the key dimension?" The student has never thought about it. The oral exam is a conversation designed to find the edge of your understanding: the first question shows what you remember, and the follow-ups show whether you understand. Preparation therefore means practicing the follow-ups.

### What an oral examination is

Formats vary widely. Some programs hold an oral based on a **reading list** of papers and textbooks; some on a written **research proposal**; some combine the oral with the written qualifying exam from Lessons 7 to 9; some do not use oral exams at all. The length, the number of examiners, whether you use a whiteboard, whether the committee includes your advisor, and what happens after a weak performance all differ. **Ask your program for its written rules and, if allowed, for descriptions of past exams, and follow what your department says, not what this lesson says.**

### A skeleton for a spoken answer

A spoken answer cannot be reread, so give it a shape the listener can follow.

1. **The claim in one sentence**: "Attention computes a weighted average of values, with weights given by the similarity of a query to keys."
2. **The definition or setup**, with the formula written on the board.
3. **The intuition or the reason**: why it is built this way (the scaling by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps dot products at unit scale).
4. **A small example with numbers**, which is the best protection against confusion.
5. **Limits and assumptions**: when it does not hold, what it costs (the cost of full attention grows quadratically with sequence length).

Say aloud what you are doing: "I will state the result, then sketch why." Say when you are unsure. Examiners generally prefer a clear statement of a limit to a confident error, though committees differ in style.

### Anticipating follow-ups

Follow-ups usually climb a ladder: *what* (definition), *why* (reason), *what if* (change an assumption), *how do you know* (evidence), and *what is open* (limits of current knowledge). For each topic on your list, write the ladder. For a paper: What is the claim? What is the evidence? What would falsify it? What are the baselines and are they fair? What would you do next? For a theorem: What are the conditions, and what fails when one is dropped?

### When you do not know

It will happen. A sound response, whatever the committee's style: say what you do know that is related, reason from first principles aloud, state your best guess and flag it as a guess, and ask for a hint if the format allows. Do not bluff: a fabricated answer is worse than an honest gap, and it damages trust in your other answers. Do not stop at "I don't know" either, since the examiners want to see how you think.

### Planning the reading list

Treat the list as a project. Count the items, separate **core** papers you must know deeply from **supporting** ones you can summarize, estimate hours for each, and compare with the hours you have. Reading a paper for an oral takes more than reading it once: you should be able to state the claim, redraw the main figure from memory, name the baselines and the datasets, and give two criticisms. Active practice beats rereading: explain a paper aloud to a friend, or record yourself.

### Mock exams

Hold at least two mock orals with peers or faculty, using the real format and time limit. Ask your examiners to interrupt, to ask "why" repeatedly and to write down the questions you missed. Afterward, convert each missed question into a study item. Keep a log of questions and answers: it is also the seed of your thesis literature review.

### Practicalities

Sleep, a reasonable meal and arriving early do more for performance than a last night of reading. If you have a condition or circumstance that needs accommodation, your institution has a process; start it early. Programs also differ on whether oral exams are recorded, and on how outcomes and retakes are decided, so ask.

@example
title: A reading-list schedule
problem: Your list has 30 papers. You will read 12 core papers deeply (3 hours each, including notes and an oral summary) and 18 supporting papers lightly (1 hour each). You can study 9 hours a week. How many hours does the list need, and how many weeks?
step: Core papers: $12\times3=36$ hours.
step: Supporting papers: $18\times1=18$ hours, for a total of $36+18=54$ hours.
step: Weeks: $54/9=6$ weeks, with no time left for mock exams or review.
step: Add a 25% reserve for review and two mock orals: $54\times1.25=67.5$ hours, or $67.5/9=7.5$ weeks. Start at least eight weeks before the exam.
answer: 54 hours (6 weeks), and 7.5 weeks with a 25% reserve, so start at least eight weeks ahead.

@example
title: Building an answer in five parts
problem: An examiner asks, "What is cross-validation, and when does it fail?" Sketch the answer in five parts.
step: Claim: cross-validation estimates generalization performance by repeatedly training on part of the data and testing on the rest.
step: Setup: split into $k$ folds; for each fold, train on the other $k-1$ and evaluate on it; average the $k$ scores.
step: Reason and example: every example is used for testing exactly once; with 100 examples and $k=5$, each fit uses 80 and tests on 20.
step: Limits: the folds must be independent of how the data are related (grouped or time-ordered data need grouped or forward-chaining splits), preprocessing and model selection must happen inside each training fold to avoid leakage, and the estimate has its own variance.
answer: A claim, a setup, a numeric example and the limits (dependent data and leakage), each in a sentence or two.

@mistakes
- Memorizing answers without the follow-up ladder: the second question is where an oral is decided.
- Bluffing when you do not know: examiners usually detect it, and it casts doubt on everything else you said.
- Preparing only by rereading: speaking aloud reveals gaps that silent reading hides.
- Relying on advice about format from others: formats differ by program and change, so read your department's current rules.

prompt: As a rough model, suppose the committee picks 3 of the 40 papers on your list at random, and you know 30 of the 40 deeply. What is the probability that at least one of the 3 picked papers is one you do not know deeply? Give your answer to three decimal places.
answer: 0.589
tol: 0.001
check: 1 - comb(30, 3) / comb(40, 3)
hint: Compute the probability that all three come from the 30 you know, then subtract it from 1.
solution: The probability that all three picks are among the 30 is $\binom{30}{3}/\binom{40}{3}=4060/9880\approx0.411$, so the probability of at least one unfamiliar paper is $1-0.411=0.589$. Real committees do not pick at random, so use this only as a reminder that coverage matters.

@practice self
prompt: Mock oral (timed, 8 minutes). The examiner asks: "Explain the bias-variance trade-off, and how double descent (Belkin and colleagues, 2019) changes the picture." Write an outline of your spoken answer (the five-part skeleton) and list four likely follow-up questions with short model responses.
hint: State the decomposition for squared loss, describe the classical U-shaped test-error curve, then the second descent beyond the interpolation threshold, and the limits of what is known.
solution: Claim: for squared loss, expected error at a point is noise plus squared bias plus variance, and the classical picture says that increasing model flexibility lowers bias and raises variance, giving a U-shaped test error. Setup: $\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat h)^2]=\sigma^2+\text{bias}^2+\text{variance}$, expectation over training sets and noise, at a fixed input. Example: a degree-1 polynomial fit to curved data has high bias and low variance; a degree-9 polynomial through 10 noisy points has near-zero bias and high variance. Double descent: Belkin and colleagues (2019) described that, for some model families and training procedures, as capacity grows past the point where the model can interpolate the training data, test error can fall again after a peak, so the curve has two descents; the decomposition remains true, but variance need not increase monotonically with capacity. Limits: this has been shown in specific settings and its explanation and generality are active research topics; whether and when it matters for a given model depends on the setting. Follow-ups: (1) Does the decomposition hold for classification? It is exact for squared loss; for 0-1 loss there are several different decompositions. (2) Where does the peak occur? Near the interpolation threshold, roughly where the number of parameters matches the number of training examples in the simplest settings. (3) Does early stopping or regularization change the curve? Yes, they can smooth or remove the peak, since they limit effective capacity. (4) Is bias-variance still a useful tool? Yes, as a way to reason about errors, with the caveat that capacity is not one-dimensional.
rubric: States the decomposition correctly for squared loss, with its conditions, and uses the five-part spoken structure.
rubric: Describes double descent accurately and attributes it correctly, without overclaiming its generality.
rubric: Gives four follow-ups with accurate, concise answers that probe conditions and limits.

@quiz self
prompt: Mock oral (timed, 8 minutes). The examiner asks: "Explain the main idea of the transformer in 'Attention Is All You Need' (Vaswani and colleagues, 2017) and what its limitations are." Write the outline of your answer (claim, setup, example, limits) and four likely follow-ups with model responses.
hint: Include scaled dot-product attention, why it is scaled, multi-head attention, positional information, and the quadratic cost in sequence length.
solution: Claim: the transformer replaces recurrence with attention, so every position can use information from every other position in one layer, which allows parallel computation across a sequence. Setup: $\operatorname{Attention}(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{K},\mathbf{V})=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{K}^\top/\sqrt{d_k})\mathbf{V}$, used in several heads in parallel, with feed-forward layers, residual connections and layer normalization, and an encoder-decoder structure in the original translation model. Example: with scaled scores $(1,0,0.5)$ the weights are about $(0.51,0.19,0.31)$ and the output is that weighted average of the value vectors. Limits: the cost of full self-attention grows quadratically with sequence length in time and memory; attention alone is insensitive to order, so positional information must be added; and the paper's evidence concerned translation tasks of its time, so claims beyond them need other evidence. Follow-ups: (1) Why scale by $\sqrt{d_k}$? If query and key components have unit variance, dot products have variance $d_k$; unscaled, large scores would saturate the softmax and shrink gradients. (2) Why multiple heads? They let the model attend to different relations in different subspaces at similar cost. (3) Why are positional encodings needed? Attention treats its inputs as a set, so without positions it cannot distinguish orderings. (4) What does recurrence do better? It has constant-size state per step at inference, which can matter for very long sequences; the trade-offs depend on the task and setting.
rubric: States the main idea and formula correctly, with the correct attribution.
rubric: Gives accurate limitations (quadratic cost, need for positional information) and does not overclaim.
rubric: Provides four follow-ups with correct, concise answers (scaling, heads, positions, recurrence trade-off).
