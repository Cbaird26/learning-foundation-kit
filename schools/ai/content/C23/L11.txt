@article
Laws and standards change quickly in this field. What follows is accurate as of November 2026, with dates, but you should always check the current text before relying on any of it: for the EU, the text of the AI Act and its amendments on EUR-Lex; for the US, current federal and state law.

### Instruments of governance

**Hard law** (statutes and binding regulations) sets duties with enforcement. **Standards** set agreed practices, which may be voluntary or become obligations by reference in law. **Principles and guidelines** state shared values without binding force. **Institutions** (regulators, courts, standards bodies, testing institutes, international organizations) apply and interpret them. Existing law, such as anti-discrimination, consumer protection, privacy and product-liability law, already applies to AI systems even where no AI-specific statute exists.

### The EU AI Act

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 entered into force on 1 August 2024 and takes a **risk-based** approach with these tiers:

| tier | what it covers |
|---|---|
| prohibited ("unacceptable-risk") practices | practices judged incompatible with fundamental rights, such as certain manipulative techniques and social scoring |
| high-risk systems | systems in listed areas (Annex III, for example employment and worker management, education, access to essential services) and AI in regulated products (Annex I); they face requirements such as risk management, data governance, documentation, human oversight, accuracy and robustness |
| transparency obligations | for some systems, such as chatbots and AI-generated content (Article 50) |
| minimal risk | no specific obligations |

Separate rules cover **general-purpose AI models** ("general-purpose AI model" is the Act's term). Models trained with more than $10^{25}$ floating-point operations are presumed to pose **systemic risk**, which brings additional duties.

Application dates: the prohibitions and the AI literacy duty from 2 February 2025; obligations for general-purpose AI models from 2 August 2025; transparency obligations of Article 50 from 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted in June 2026 and published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026, moved the high-risk obligations to 2 December 2027 for systems listed in Annex III and to 2 August 2028 for AI in products covered by Annex I. It also added two prohibitions that apply from 2 December 2026 (on AI systems that generate realistic sexually explicit images, video or audio of an identifiable person without consent, and on generating child sexual abuse material), gave providers of generative AI systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 to meet the marking duty of Article 50(2), and kept the AI literacy duty in a revised form. Details of scope, exemptions and enforcement are in the legal text and may be amended.

### The United States

There is no comprehensive federal AI statute that can be described as enacted. Whether federal law should preempt state AI laws has been actively debated in Congress since 2025, so check its current status. In January 2025 the 2023 executive order on AI (EO 14110) was revoked and EO 14179, "Removing Barriers to American Leadership in Artificial Intelligence," was issued; America's AI Action Plan followed in July 2025. EO 14365, "Ensuring a National Policy Framework for Artificial Intelligence" (11 December 2025), directs federal agencies to challenge and discourage state AI laws they judge burdensome; an executive order does not by itself preempt state law. California's SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act (signed 29 September 2025, in effect from 1 January 2026), requires developers of models trained with more than $10^{26}$ operations to publish transparency reports and, for those with annual revenue above \$500 million, a frontier AI safety framework; it requires reporting of critical safety incidents to the state's Office of Emergency Services and protects whistleblowers.

### Standards and principles

The **NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0** (January 2023) is voluntary guidance organized into four functions: **Govern** (culture, accountability and policies), **Map** (understand context and identify risks), **Measure** (assess and track them) and **Manage** (prioritize and respond). **ISO/IEC 42001** (2023) is a standard for AI management systems, against which organizations can be certified. The **OECD AI Principles** were adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024; they are non-binding principles on matters such as human-centered values, transparency, robustness and accountability.

### Compute thresholds

Both the EU Act's presumption ($10^{25}$) and SB 53's threshold ($10^{26}$) use training compute. A common approximation for the training compute of a dense transformer with $N$ parameters trained on $D$ tokens is $C \approx 6ND$ floating-point operations. For $N = 7 \times 10^{10}$ and $D = 2 \times 10^{12}$, $C \approx 6 \times 7\times10^{10} \times 2\times10^{12} = 8.4 \times 10^{23}$, below $10^{25}$. Compute is easy to measure but is a proxy: capability per unit of compute changes with algorithms, so thresholds may need revising.

### How should AI be regulated?

*Comprehensive, risk-based rules* (as in the EU): predictable duties scaled to risk protect fundamental rights and give legal certainty; leaving harms to courts is slow and unequal. Objection: compliance costs and rapid change may slow beneficial uses and favor large firms, and the categories can age badly. *Sectoral rules and existing law*: regulators in health, finance and transport know their domains, and general laws already apply; a single AI law risks both overreach and gaps. Objection: gaps and inconsistency between sectors, and slow responses to general-purpose systems. *Light-touch, innovation-first*: heavy rules may block benefits and push work abroad, and voluntary standards can adapt faster. Objection: voluntary measures rely on goodwill under competitive pressure, and harms may fall on people with no recourse. *International coordination*: because models and risks cross borders, shared standards and testing are necessary. Objection: states disagree on values and interests, and agreements are slow and hard to enforce. Evidence on which approach produces better outcomes is still limited.

@example
title: Does a training run cross a threshold?
problem: A developer trains a model with $N = 4 \times 10^{11}$ parameters on $D = 6 \times 10^{12}$ tokens. Estimate the training compute with $C \approx 6ND$ and compare it with the EU presumption of systemic risk ($10^{25}$) and the SB 53 threshold ($10^{26}$).
step: Compute $C \approx 6 \times (4\times10^{11}) \times (6\times10^{12}) = 6 \times 24 \times 10^{23} = 1.44 \times 10^{25}$ operations.
step: Compare with the EU presumption: $1.44 \times 10^{25} > 10^{25}$, so the model would be presumed to pose systemic risk if the estimate is the actual training compute.
step: Compare with SB 53: $1.44\times10^{25} < 10^{26}$, so this threshold is not met.
step: Note the caveat: $6ND$ is an approximation, and the legal text defines how compute is to be counted.
answer: About $1.4 \times 10^{25}$ operations: above the EU presumption, below the SB 53 threshold.

@mistakes
- Treating a date or threshold from memory as current: laws are amended (the Digital Omnibus on AI moved the high-risk dates), so check the text.
- Saying that an executive order preempts state law: it does not by itself, though it can direct agencies to challenge such laws.
- Confusing the EU "systemic risk" presumption ($10^{25}$) with the SB 53 threshold ($10^{26}$).
- Treating voluntary frameworks such as the NIST AI RMF as legally binding, or assuming they carry no weight.

@practice self
prompt: Make the strongest case for one approach to regulating AI (comprehensive risk-based rules, sectoral rules, light-touch with voluntary standards, or international coordination), then give the strongest objection and your reply.
hint: Say what problem the approach solves, give evidence from other regulated fields, and address its main weakness.
solution: One strong case for comprehensive risk-based rules: AI is used across many sectors by many deployers who are not experts, and the people affected usually cannot detect or challenge errors, so predictable duties set in advance (documentation, testing, human oversight, transparency) scaled to the risk of the use protect rights and give firms legal certainty; relying on after-the-fact litigation is slow, costly and unequal. Product-safety regulation in other fields shows that clear ex ante requirements can reduce harm while allowing innovation within them. The strongest objection is that the categories and thresholds will age badly in a fast-moving field, and compliance costs fall hardest on smaller firms while large firms absorb them. A reply is to write duties in terms of outcomes and risk management, delegate technical detail to regulators and standards that can be updated (as the shifted application dates and the standards process show), and scale obligations to size and risk. A fair answer admits that evidence on whether such rules improve outcomes is still limited and says what evidence would change the view.
