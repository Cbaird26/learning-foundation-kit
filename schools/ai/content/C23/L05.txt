@article
A hospital publishes the number of patients with a rare condition each month: 130 in March. In April it publishes 131. If you know that exactly one patient was admitted in April and that nothing else changed, you have learned that the new patient has the condition. No name was released, yet a private fact was. This is a **differencing attack**, and it shows why "we only release aggregates" is not a privacy guarantee.

### How privacy fails

Textbooks describe three recurring failures. A **linkage attack** combines a "de-identified" data set with a public one using shared quasi-identifiers (such as birth date, ZIP code and sex) to re-identify people. **k-anonymity**, which requires each combination of quasi-identifiers to appear for at least $k$ people, helps against some linkage but fails when the other attributes of those $k$ people are the same, and it composes poorly with other releases. **Membership inference** asks whether a particular person's record was in a training set, using the model's outputs; models that fit their training data closely leak more. Privacy defenses are judged by what an attacker with arbitrary side information can learn, which is the idea behind differential privacy.

### Differential privacy

Call two data sets **neighbors** if one is obtained from the other by adding or removing one person's record. A randomized algorithm $M$ is **$\varepsilon$-differentially private** (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim and Smith, 2006) if for all neighbors $D, D'$ and all sets of outputs $S$,

$$P[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon}\, P[M(D') \in S].$$

In words: whether or not any one person's data is included changes the probability of any outcome by at most the factor $e^{\varepsilon}$. A small $\varepsilon$ means strong privacy and, in general, less accurate answers. The parameter $\varepsilon$ is called the **privacy budget** or privacy loss. What value is acceptable is a policy and risk judgment; there is no single agreed threshold.

### The Laplace mechanism

The **sensitivity** of a numerical query $f$ is the largest change one person can cause: $\Delta f = \max_{D, D'} |f(D) - f(D')|$ over neighbors. A count has sensitivity 1. A sum of values each known to lie in $[0, B]$ has sensitivity $B$.

The **Laplace distribution** with scale $b$ has density $\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|x|/b}$, mean 0, variance $2b^2$ (standard deviation $\sqrt{2}\,b$), and tail $P(|X| > t) = e^{-t/b}$. The **Laplace mechanism** releases

$$M(D) = f(D) + X, \qquad X \sim \mathrm{Laplace}(b), \quad b = \frac{\Delta f}{\varepsilon},$$

and is $\varepsilon$-differentially private. Why: for neighbors with outputs differing by at most $\Delta f$, the ratio of the two densities at any output $y$ is $e^{(|y - f(D')| - |y - f(D)|)/b} \le e^{\Delta f / b} = e^{\varepsilon}$.

For the monthly count with $\varepsilon = 0.5$: $b = 1/0.5 = 2$, so the noise has standard deviation $2\sqrt{2} \approx 2.83$ and $P(|X| > 3) = e^{-1.5} \approx 0.223$. Published values of 130 and 131 now tell you almost nothing about whether one patient has the condition.

### Composition and post-processing

Answering several queries on the same data costs privacy. Under **sequential composition**, running mechanisms with budgets $\varepsilon_1, \ldots, \varepsilon_k$ on the same data gives an $\left(\sum_i \varepsilon_i\right)$-differentially private process. Queries on **disjoint** subsets of people cost only the largest $\varepsilon_i$ (parallel composition). **Post-processing** cannot weaken the guarantee: any function of a differentially private output, computed without further access to the data, is still $\varepsilon$-differentially private. Organizations therefore set a total budget and spend it across queries.

### Local differential privacy: randomized response

In **randomized response**, each person answers a yes/no question truthfully with probability $q > 1/2$ and gives the opposite answer with probability $1-q$. The ratio of probabilities of any reported answer under the two true answers is $q/(1-q)$, so each report is $\varepsilon$-differentially private with $\varepsilon = \ln\frac{q}{1-q}$. The collector does not see true answers but can estimate the true share $\pi$ from the observed share $r$ of "yes" reports, because $r = q\pi + (1-q)(1-\pi)$, so $\hat{\pi} = \dfrac{r - (1-q)}{2q - 1}$.

### Limits

Differential privacy bounds what a release reveals about the *inclusion of one person*, not what it reveals about everyone: a study may still show that a group has a higher risk of a disease. It does not by itself address misuse of the results or the harms of data collection. Noise reduces accuracy, especially for small groups; correlated records (relatives, repeated measurements) weaken the meaning of "one person"; and a budget that is spent cannot be recovered. Always check what neighboring relation and what $\varepsilon$ a system uses.

@example
title: Noisy count and budget
problem: A clinic releases the number of patients with condition C, currently 214, using the Laplace mechanism with $\varepsilon = 0.4$. Find the noise scale, the probability that the noise exceeds 5 in absolute value, and the standard deviation of the noise.
step: The query is a count, so $\Delta f = 1$ and the scale is $b = 1/0.4 = 2.5$.
step: The tail probability is $P(|X| > 5) = e^{-5/2.5} = e^{-2} \approx 0.135$.
step: The standard deviation is $\sqrt{2}\,b = \sqrt{2} \times 2.5 \approx 3.54$.
step: Check the guarantee: a neighboring data set has count 213 or 215, a difference of 1, so the probability of any output changes by at most a factor $e^{0.4} \approx 1.49$.
answer: Scale 2.5; $P(|X|>5) \approx 0.135$; standard deviation about 3.54.

@mistakes
- Believing that removing names makes data anonymous: quasi-identifiers can re-identify people through linkage.
- Forgetting sensitivity: noise must scale with how much one person can change the query, so a sum of unbounded values has no finite noise scale.
- Treating $\varepsilon$ per query as the total: sequential releases on the same data add up.
- Thinking that a larger $\varepsilon$ means more privacy: it is the opposite, since it allows probabilities to differ by a larger factor.

@practice self
prompt: A hospital board wants to publish exact monthly counts of patients with a sensitive condition. Explain to the board why exact counts can reveal an individual's status, how adding Laplace noise addresses this, what $\varepsilon$ means for them, and what trade-off they must accept.
hint: Use a differencing example, give the noise scale for a count, and state how $\varepsilon$ relates to accuracy.
solution: Exact counts leak when an attacker has side information: if a count rises by one in a month in which one known patient was admitted, that patient's status is revealed. Adding Laplace noise with scale $1/\varepsilon$ to a count (sensitivity 1) means that the published number would be almost as likely whether or not any single patient was included: the probability of any output differs by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$. The board chooses $\varepsilon$: a small $\varepsilon$ such as 0.1 gives scale 10 and strong privacy but noisy counts that may be useless for small clinics, while a large $\varepsilon$ such as 5 gives scale 0.2 and accurate counts but a weak guarantee. Releasing monthly counts also spends budget through composition, so the board must set a total budget for the year and decide how to divide it. A strong answer adds that the guarantee covers one person's inclusion, not every inference about the population.
