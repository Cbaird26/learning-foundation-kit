@article
Two groups apply for a loan. A classifier approves 40% of group A and 25% of group B. Is that unfair? Perhaps group B's applicants are less often creditworthy. Perhaps the model makes more mistakes on group B. Perhaps neither. "Fair" is not a single quantity, and the first step is to say exactly what is being measured.

### Setup

Let $A$ be a sensitive attribute (group membership), $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ the true outcome (1 means the desirable or positive outcome, such as repaying a loan), and $\hat{Y} \in \{0, 1\}$ the classifier's decision. A classifier might also output a score $S \in [0, 1]$ and decide $\hat{Y} = 1$ when $S$ exceeds a threshold. For each group we can build a confusion table with $TP$, $FP$, $FN$ and $TN$ counts.

Four rates come from it. The **selection rate** is $P(\hat{Y} = 1)$. The **true positive rate** (TPR, recall) is $TP/(TP + FN)$. The **false positive rate** (FPR) is $FP/(FP + TN)$. The **positive predictive value** (PPV, precision) is $TP/(TP + FP)$. The **base rate** (prevalence) is $P(Y = 1)$.

### The criteria

Each criterion asks that some quantity be equal across groups.

| criterion | requires, for all groups $a, b$ |
|---|---|
| **Demographic parity** (statistical parity) | $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=a) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=b)$ |
| **Equal opportunity** (Hardt, Price and Srebro, 2016) | equal TPR: $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid Y=1, A=a)$ the same for all $a$ |
| **Equalized odds** (same paper) | equal TPR and equal FPR |
| **Predictive parity** | equal PPV: $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=1, A=a)$ the same for all $a$ |
| **Calibration within groups** | $P(Y=1 \mid S=s, A=a) = s$ for every score $s$ and group $a$ |

We report gaps: the **demographic parity difference** is the difference in selection rates; the **disparate impact ratio** is the lower selection rate divided by the higher. A common rule of thumb from US employment-selection guidance, the four-fifths rule, treats a ratio below 0.8 as a flag for further scrutiny; it is a screening heuristic, not a definition of discrimination. The **equalized odds gap** is often reported as the larger of the TPR gap and the FPR gap.

**Individual fairness** (Dwork and colleagues, 2012) is a different idea: similar individuals should be treated similarly, which requires a task-specific similarity measure. **Fairness through unawareness**, simply dropping the sensitive attribute from the features, does not ensure parity, because other features can act as proxies for it.

### A worked comparison

| | $TP$ | $FP$ | $FN$ | $TN$ | $n$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| group A | 30 | 10 | 20 | 40 | 100 |
| group B | 12 | 8 | 18 | 42 | 80 |

For group A: selection rate $= 40/100 = 0.40$; TPR $= 30/50 = 0.60$; FPR $= 10/50 = 0.20$; PPV $= 30/40 = 0.75$; base rate $= 50/100 = 0.50$. For group B: selection rate $= 20/80 = 0.25$; TPR $= 12/30 = 0.40$; FPR $= 8/50 = 0.16$; PPV $= 12/20 = 0.60$; base rate $= 30/80 = 0.375$.

The demographic parity difference is $0.40 - 0.25 = 0.15$; the disparate impact ratio is $0.25/0.40 = 0.625$; the TPR gap is $0.20$ and the FPR gap $0.04$, so the equalized odds gap is $0.20$; the PPV gap is $0.15$. All criteria are violated, in different amounts. Because the base rates differ (0.50 and 0.375), some gap in selection is expected even from a classifier that is accurate; whether the gap is unjust depends on why the base rates differ and on what the decision is for.

### What the criteria mean

Demographic parity ignores $Y$, so it can be satisfied by a useless classifier and it can force errors if base rates differ. Equal opportunity says qualified people should have the same chance of being recognized whatever their group. Equalized odds adds that unqualified people should be wrongly selected equally often. Predictive parity and calibration say that a positive decision or a score means the same thing for each group. These are different claims about what fairness requires, and choosing among them is partly a value judgment about the setting (a medical screen, a loan, a prison release decision), not only a technical matter. The next lesson shows that they cannot all hold at once in typical cases.

@example
title: Computing group metrics from counts
problem: A classifier has these counts for group P: $TP = 24$, $FP = 6$, $FN = 16$, $TN = 54$; and for group Q: $TP = 9$, $FP = 11$, $FN = 21$, $TN = 59$. Compute the selection rates, the demographic parity difference, the TPR gap and the FPR gap.
step: Group P has $n = 100$ and selects $24 + 6 = 30$, so its selection rate is $0.30$. Group Q has $n = 100$ and selects $9 + 11 = 20$, so $0.20$.
step: Demographic parity difference: $0.30 - 0.20 = 0.10$.
step: TPR for P: $24/(24+16) = 0.60$. TPR for Q: $9/(9+21) = 0.30$. The TPR gap is $0.30$.
step: FPR for P: $6/(6+54) = 0.10$. FPR for Q: $11/(11+59) \approx 0.157$. The FPR gap is about $0.057$.
step: Interpret: Q's qualified members are recognized half as often as P's (0.30 against 0.60), a much larger disparity than the selection rates alone suggest.
answer: Selection rates 0.30 and 0.20; parity difference 0.10; TPR gap 0.30; FPR gap about 0.057.

@mistakes
- Treating one fairness metric as "the" fairness measure: each encodes a different claim.
- Computing a rate over the wrong denominator (dividing TPR by all examples instead of actual positives, or PPV by all examples instead of predicted positives).
- Assuming that dropping the sensitive attribute from the features makes the model fair: proxies remain.
- Reading the four-fifths ratio as a legal test of discrimination: it is a screening rule of thumb.

@practice self
prompt: A hospital uses a model to decide which patients are referred for a costly specialist test. Choose one fairness criterion (demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalized odds, predictive parity or calibration within groups) that you would prioritize, argue for it in this setting, and give the strongest objection with your reply.
hint: Think about who is harmed by a missed referral and by an unnecessary test, and what each criterion guarantees.
solution: A defensible choice is equal opportunity: the main harm in this setting is a patient who needs the test and does not get referred, and equal opportunity says that patients who truly need it have the same chance of referral whatever their group. It directly targets the group-specific false negative harm. The strongest objection is that equal opportunity ignores false positives, so a group could be over-referred, exposed to cost and risk, and that it says nothing about whether a referral means the same thing for every group; a calibration advocate would add that clinicians rely on scores having the same meaning in each group. A reply is that false positive costs here are modest compared with missing a serious condition, and that calibration can be checked as a separate requirement, though the next lesson shows that all criteria cannot hold at once when base rates differ, so the choice reflects a judgment about which error is worse. A fair answer states that someone weighing the harms differently, for example if the test were risky, might choose equalized odds.
rubric: Chooses one criterion and ties it to specific harms in the setting.
rubric: Describes accurately what the criterion does and does not guarantee.
rubric: States the strongest objection fairly and replies to it.
