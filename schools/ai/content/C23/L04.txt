@article
A risk score is used to decide who gets a supportive program. Critics note that among people who would not have needed the program, one group is wrongly flagged much more often than the other. The developers answer that a given score means the same thing in both groups: of the people flagged, the same fraction in each group really needed the program. Both sides are reporting true numbers. Remarkably, they cannot both be made to vanish, except in special cases. This is the content of the impossibility results of Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan (2016) and Chouldechova (2017).

### An identity

Fix one group with base rate $p = P(Y = 1)$. Let TPR, FPR and PPV be its true positive rate, false positive rate and positive predictive value. Among $n$ people there are $np\,\text{TPR}$ true positives and $n(1-p)\,\text{FPR}$ false positives, so

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{p\,\text{TPR}}{p\,\text{TPR} + (1-p)\,\text{FPR}}.$$

Cross-multiplying gives $(1-p)\,\text{FPR}\cdot\text{PPV} = p\,\text{TPR}(1 - \text{PPV})$, and therefore

$$\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}.$$

This holds for every group and every classifier with $0 < p < 1$ and $\text{PPV} > 0$ (so that the division is defined).

### The theorem for classifiers (Chouldechova)

Suppose two groups have different base rates $p_a \neq p_b$, and a classifier has equal PPV, equal TPR and equal FPR in the two groups. Equal FPR means

$$\text{TPR}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\left(\frac{p_a}{1-p_a} - \frac{p_b}{1-p_b}\right) = 0.$$

The last factor is nonzero because $p/(1-p)$ is strictly increasing in $p$ and $p_a \ne p_b$. So the product vanishes only if $\text{TPR} = 0$ or $\text{PPV} = 1$: the classifier finds none of the positives, or makes no false positives. These are degenerate cases. In every other case, **if base rates differ, equal PPV and equal false negative rates (equivalently equal TPR) force unequal false positive rates.** Equal base rates remove the obstruction, as does a perfect classifier.

### The theorem for scores (Kleinberg and colleagues)

Consider a score $S$ and three conditions: **calibration within groups** ($P(Y=1 \mid S=s, A=a) = s$); **balance for the positive class** (the average score of people with $Y=1$ is the same in each group); **balance for the negative class** (the average score of people with $Y=0$ is the same in each group). The theorem says all three can hold simultaneously only if the base rates are equal or the prediction is perfect. Both results are exact mathematical statements; the first needs no assumption about how the classifier was built.

### What the results do and do not say

They do not say fairness is impossible, or that classifiers must be unfair. They say that these particular statistical criteria are incompatible in general, so a choice has to be made, and the choice is about values and context. Related trade-offs:

- Choosing **group-specific thresholds** can equalize TPR or FPR, but it changes PPV and overall accuracy, and treating people differently by group may itself be unlawful or objectionable in some settings.
- The **accuracy cost** of a fairness constraint depends on the data; it can be large, small, or zero, and it is measured, not assumed.
- **Base rates may be measured with error.** If the recorded outcome (for example, arrest) is a biased measure of the true outcome (an offense), then the "base rates" and the error rates both inherit the bias.

### Positions on which criterion to prioritize

*Error-rate balance.* Being wrongly flagged is a harm done to a particular person, and equal false positive and false negative rates mean no group bears more of the system's mistakes. Objection: when base rates differ, equal error rates make scores mean different things for different groups, so decision makers cannot interpret a score uniformly.

*Calibration and predictive parity.* A score should carry the same meaning for everyone, since decision makers act on its meaning, and miscalibration within a group is a clear defect. Objection: calibration can be met by a score that is uninformative within groups, and it permits unequal error burdens.

*Questioning the inputs.* The criteria all take labels and base rates as given; if the difference in base rates comes from unequal past treatment or measurement, the right response may be to change the process or the data rather than choose a criterion. Objection: without a criterion, the system still has to make decisions today.

@example
title: A single group's false positive rate from the identity
problem: In one group the base rate is $p = 0.3$, the classifier has TPR $= 0.7$ and PPV $= 0.8$. In another group $p = 0.5$ with the same TPR and PPV. Find each group's FPR.
step: Write the identity: $\text{FPR} = \dfrac{p}{1-p}\cdot\dfrac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$. Here $\dfrac{1 - 0.8}{0.8} = 0.25$.
step: First group: $\dfrac{0.3}{0.7} \times 0.25 \times 0.7 = 0.075$.
step: Second group: $\dfrac{0.5}{0.5} \times 0.25 \times 0.7 = 0.175$.
step: Conclude: with equal PPV and TPR, the group with the higher base rate must have the higher FPR (0.175 against 0.075).
answer: FPR $= 0.075$ for the first group and $0.175$ for the second.

@mistakes
- Reading the theorem as "no fair classifier exists": it says specific criteria conflict when base rates differ and the classifier is imperfect.
- Forgetting the conditions: equal base rates, a perfect classifier, or the degenerate cases remove the conflict.
- Treating the choice of criterion as a technical optimization: it is a judgment about which harms and which meanings matter in context.
- Assuming a fairness constraint always costs accuracy: the cost depends on the data and is measured.

@practice self
prompt: A risk score is used to allocate a supportive program, and the two groups have different base rates of need. Argue for prioritizing either calibration within groups or equal error rates, then give the strongest objection to your choice and your reply.
hint: Say who is harmed by each kind of failure, then use the impossibility result to explain why you cannot have both.
solution: One strong argument favors calibration: the score is used by caseworkers who must decide how to act on a number, and if a score of 0.7 means a 70% chance of need in one group and 50% in another, the same number leads to systematically different decisions and the tool cannot be interpreted. Calibration also keeps the score honest about uncertainty. The strongest objection is that, because base rates differ, calibration forces unequal false positive and false negative rates by the impossibility result, so one group bears more of the tool's mistakes, such as being wrongly excluded from help. A reply is that unequal error rates here follow from different base rates and can be addressed by program design (for example outreach beyond the score threshold, human review of borderline cases and low-cost resources for those flagged wrongly) rather than by distorting the meaning of the score, and that the difference in base rates is itself a reason to ask why the groups differ. An honest answer concedes that if wrongful exclusion were severe and irreversible, error-rate balance could weigh more heavily.
