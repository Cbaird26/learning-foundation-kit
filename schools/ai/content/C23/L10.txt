@article
Suppose someone tells you that a technology has a 1-in-1,000 chance of causing a catastrophe affecting billions of people. Should society spend heavily to prevent it? The answer depends on whether the probability is right, on how the harm is valued, on what else the money could do, and on who is making the claim and why. Informed researchers disagree about each of these for advanced AI. This lesson sets out the main positions as their holders would, with the evidence they give and the strongest replies. Which is right is not settled here, and not settled in the field.

### Four positions

**1. Catastrophic risk deserves priority.** The argument, developed in books by Bostrom (2014) and Russell (2019) and in many papers: (a) AI systems may come to match or exceed humans at most tasks, and progress has repeatedly been faster than many experts expected; (b) it is hard to specify objectives completely, and documented cases of specification gaming and goal misgeneralization (Lesson 8) show systems pursuing the letter of an objective; (c) capable systems that pursue almost any goal may have instrumental reasons to acquire resources and resist being switched off, and the **orthogonality thesis** holds that intelligence level and final goals can vary independently; (d) the harm could be large and irreversible, so even a modest probability justifies serious effort. *Best objections*: the scenarios extrapolate from toy cases and a few abstract arguments; capability does not imply autonomous goal-seeking; surveys of researchers show wide disagreement about timelines and probabilities, so confident numbers lack a basis; and the framing may compete with attention to concrete harms.

**2. Present harms are the priority.** Documented harms exist now: discrimination, privacy loss, labor effects, concentration of economic and political power, and environmental costs (Bender and colleagues, 2021, is a widely cited example of work on the risks of large language models). The argument: speculative future risks can divert attention, funding and regulatory capacity from those who are being harmed today, and can be used to favor the interests of large developers. *Best objections*: the two concerns are not exclusive, since methods for oversight, evaluation and robustness help with both; some present harms are early forms of problems that grow with capability; and a risk being speculative does not make it small.

**3. Skepticism about rapid progress toward systems that could pose catastrophic risk.** The argument: forecasts of human-level AI have a long history of being wrong; current methods may hit limits in reasoning, reliability or data; real-world deployment is slow; and complex technologies, from aviation to nuclear power, have been made safer by ordinary engineering and regulation. *Best objections*: forecasting has erred in both directions; the possibility that progress continues is serious enough that preparation costs little compared with the stakes; and past technologies were not general-purpose intelligence.

**4. Misuse and concentration of power.** Even without autonomous misaligned systems, powerful AI could help actors carry out large-scale harms or entrench control, and competition between firms or states can push safety down (a collective action problem). *Best objections*: the evidence for large counterfactual uplift is uncertain; restrictions needed to prevent misuse may themselves concentrate power or curb beneficial uses.

These positions overlap: someone can hold parts of several. What is unknown includes the timeline to highly capable systems, whether scaling current methods leads to general capabilities, how hard alignment is, and whether serious warnings would appear before it was too late.

### What arithmetic can and cannot do

**Expected loss** is $p \times L$ for probability $p$ of a loss $L$. A precaution that reduces the risk by a fraction $r$ at cost $C$ is worth it, in expected-value terms, when $p\,r\,L > C$, so the **break-even probability** is $p^\star = C/(rL)$. With $C = 5$, $r = 0.2$ and $L = 1{,}000$ (in the same units), $p^\star = 5/200 = 0.025$. The conclusion is proportional to $p$: a tenfold disagreement about the probability justifies a tenfold difference in spending. Expected-value reasoning faces objections: very small probabilities of huge losses are hard to estimate and can dominate calculations in ways many people find unreasonable; the valuation of $L$ is contested; and risk-averse or rights-based reasoning may weigh catastrophe differently.

When forecasters give different probabilities, they can be **pooled**. The arithmetic mean of $0.01, 0.05, 0.20$ is $0.087$. The **geometric mean of odds** converts each probability to odds $p/(1-p)$, takes the geometric mean, and converts back; here the odds are $0.0101, 0.0526, 0.25$, their geometric mean is about $0.0510$, and the pooled probability is $0.0510/1.0510 \approx 0.0485$. Pooling methods differ, and none repairs forecasts that share the same blind spots.

@example
title: Is a precaution worth its cost?
problem: A risk has probability $p = 0.02$ of causing a loss of $L = 500$ units. A precaution that costs $C = 3$ units reduces the probability by $40\%$ (a reduction fraction $r = 0.4$). Find its net expected benefit and the break-even probability.
step: The expected loss without the precaution is $0.02 \times 500 = 10$ units.
step: The precaution removes $r p L = 0.4 \times 0.02 \times 500 = 4$ units of expected loss.
step: The net expected benefit is $4 - 3 = 1$ unit.
step: The break-even probability is $p^\star = C/(rL) = 3/(0.4 \times 500) = 0.015$; since $0.02 > 0.015$, the precaution is worth it in expected terms, but someone who believes $p = 0.01$ would reach the opposite conclusion.
answer: Net expected benefit 1 unit; break-even probability 0.015.

@mistakes
- Treating one position's caricature as the whole of it: each position has a serious argument and serious objections.
- Treating expected-value arithmetic as settling the question: the answer is proportional to a probability that is itself disputed.
- Assuming that taking present harms seriously means denying long-term risk, or the reverse: many people hold both concerns.
- Reading a survey of experts as a measurement of the truth: it records opinions that vary widely and may share blind spots.

@practice self
prompt: Write the strongest case for one position on how much priority catastrophic risks from advanced AI deserve compared with present harms. Then give the strongest objection to your position and your reply.
hint: Pick a position, state its best evidence, and answer the objection a thoughtful opponent would find hardest.
solution: One strong case for giving catastrophic risk serious priority: systems built by optimizing objectives have already shown specification gaming and goal misgeneralization in small settings, capabilities have advanced quickly, and if systems become capable and goal-directed the consequences could be irreversible, so research on oversight and evaluation should start before the problem is acute; the cost of such research is small compared with the stakes even at modest probabilities. The strongest objection is that the probabilities are unknown and the scenarios rest on extrapolation from toy cases, while present harms are certain, so resources and political attention given to speculative risk are taken from people being harmed now. A reply is that the claims are compatible: much safety research (evaluations, interpretability, robustness) also reduces present harms, and the amount of resources proposed is a small share of AI investment; the position should be held with uncertainty, and would weaken if evidence showed that capable systems remain easy to control and that scaling stalls. A fair answer concedes that expected-value arguments alone cannot settle the matter because the probability is disputed.

@quiz self
prompt: Choose a position on risks from advanced AI that you disagree with or find least convincing, and write its strongest case, as its best defenders would.
hint: Present the evidence its holders find most persuasive and answer the main objection they face.
solution: A strong case for the view that present harms and structural risks deserve priority: discrimination, privacy loss, labor disruption, surveillance and the concentration of power in a few firms are happening now, are documented, and fall on identifiable people; resources for oversight and regulation are limited; and the loudest talk of distant catastrophe can shift policy attention toward the interests of the largest developers, for example by favoring rules that only they can meet.
