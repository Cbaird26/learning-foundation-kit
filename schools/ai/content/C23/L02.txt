@article
A city buys a model that flags apartment buildings for housing inspections. Inspectors say it is a good use of limited time. Tenants in flagged buildings say the inspections feel like harassment; landlords say the flags cost them money. A data scientist who built the model has to decide what to report. Who is affected, how, and who gets to decide what counts as acceptable?

### Kinds of harm

Textbooks and survey papers commonly distinguish several kinds of harm from AI systems:

- **Allocative harm**: an opportunity or resource (a loan, a job interview, a medical referral, bail) is withheld or given unfairly.
- **Representational harm**: a system reinforces stereotypes, demeans a group, or erases it, for example in image labels or generated text.
- **Quality-of-service harm**: the system works worse for some groups, such as a speech recognizer with higher error for some accents.
- **Privacy and dignity harm**: exposure of personal information, surveillance, or loss of control over one's data.
- **Safety and security harm**: physical injury, fraud, or misuse by other parties.
- **Societal and environmental harm**: effects on public discourse, labor markets, or energy use that no single person suffers alone.

A system can cause several at once, and a harm can be real even when it is hard to measure. Benefits matter too: an ethical analysis weighs harms against benefits and asks who gets which.

### Stakeholders

A **stakeholder** is anyone whose interests are affected by the system or who can affect it. A useful map separates:

| role | example in the inspection case |
|---|---|
| decision subjects | tenants and landlords of flagged buildings |
| users and operators | inspectors, city managers |
| builders | the data scientists, the vendor |
| bystanders | tenants of unflagged buildings who are never inspected |
| society | taxpayers, the public's trust in city government |

Bystanders are easy to miss: a model that sends inspectors to some buildings sends them away from others.

### Estimating expected harm

For one type of harm, a first estimate of the total expected harm is

$$\text{expected harm} = N \times p \times s,$$

where $N$ is the number of people exposed, $p$ is the probability that each suffers the harm, and $s$ is the average severity in some agreed unit (hours lost, dollars, a 1–5 score). Two risks with the same product can differ ethically: a harm that falls on the same small group every time is different from one spread randomly. A **risk matrix** scores likelihood and severity on 1–5 scales and multiplies them to prioritize; it is a rough ranking tool, not a measurement, and the scales hide judgment calls. Always ask who is in $N$ and who was left out.

### Who decides?

Someone must decide whether to build the system, what error rates are acceptable and what happens when it fails. Three main views, each with real arguments:

1. **Democratic and regulatory decision.** Decisions that shape public life should be made through accountable institutions: legislatures, regulators, courts. Evidence: developers and deployers have commercial incentives and limited knowledge of everyone affected; markets do not price harms to third parties. Objection: laws are slow and technical expertise is scarce, and rules written too early can lock in wrong choices.
2. **Developer and deployer responsibility.** The people who build and operate a system understand it best, can fix it fastest and are best placed to carry duties of care, backed by liability and professional norms. Evidence: they see failures first, and many practices (testing, documentation) came from within the field. Objection: self-interest can distort judgment, and affected people have no voice.
3. **Participatory decision.** People who bear the risks should help shape the system, through consultation, co-design or community oversight. Evidence: affected people often know failure modes that builders cannot see. Objection: participation can be costly, unrepresentative or merely symbolic, and it does not settle what happens when stakeholders' interests conflict.

These views are not mutually exclusive, and mixed arrangements are common. The practical question is usually which decisions belong at which level, and with what recourse.

@example
title: Estimating harms and who bears them
problem: A loan model is used on 10,000 applicants. Of these, 60% are truly creditworthy. The model wrongly rejects 15% of creditworthy applicants (its false negative rate). Estimate the number of creditworthy applicants wrongly rejected, and say what else the analysis would need before judging the harm.
step: Count the creditworthy applicants: $10{,}000 \times 0.60 = 6{,}000$.
step: Multiply by the false negative rate: $6{,}000 \times 0.15 = 900$ creditworthy applicants wrongly rejected.
step: Decide on severity: if each wrongful rejection costs an average of 40 hours of delay and re-application, the expected harm is $900 \times 40 = 36{,}000$ hours.
step: Ask who bears it: if the 900 are concentrated in one neighborhood or group, the harm is not just a number; the analysis must break it down by group and compare it with the harm of the alternative process.
answer: About 900 wrongly rejected applicants (36,000 hours under the stated severity), with the distribution across groups still to be examined.

@mistakes
- Listing only direct users as stakeholders and forgetting decision subjects and bystanders.
- Treating a risk-matrix score as a measurement: the 1–5 scales hide judgments and make different harms look comparable.
- Averaging a harm over everyone when it falls repeatedly on the same small group.
- Assuming participation or regulation alone solves the problem of who decides: each has costs and failure modes.

@practice self
prompt: A school district uses a model to flag students at risk of dropping out so that counselors can contact them. Analyze it with two ethical frameworks of your choice (for example consequentialist and contractualist): identify the main stakeholders and one harm to each of two groups, and say what each framework would require the district to do.
hint: Separate students, counselors and families; then ask what each framework counts, who bears the risk and what would make the practice acceptable.
solution: Stakeholders include flagged students, unflagged students, counselors, families and the district. A consequentialist analysis counts the benefit (students receive help they would not otherwise get) against harms: students wrongly flagged may be stigmatized or treated as likely to fail, and students who are not flagged but need help are missed. It would require the district to measure error rates by group, compare outcomes with and without the model, and keep the program only if the net effect is positive and mitigation (such as confidential outreach) limits stigma. A contractualist analysis asks whether the principle behind the practice could be justified to each student, including one who is flagged by mistake or never flagged: a student who did not know which they would be might accept the practice only with safeguards such as transparency about use, no automatic consequences, a way to correct errors and a say for families. Each lens yields concrete duties: error audits and net-benefit monitoring in the first, notice, correction rights and consultation in the second. A strong answer notes that the two sets of duties are compatible and say what the district should do if they conflict.
rubric: Identifies at least three stakeholder groups and a specific harm to two of them.
rubric: Applies each named framework faithfully, stating what it counts or what it requires.
rubric: Converts each analysis into concrete actions the district could take.
rubric: Acknowledges a tension or a limit in the analysis.

@quiz num
prompt: A risk register scores two risks on likelihood and severity, each from 1 to 5, and ranks by the product. Risk X has likelihood 4 and severity 2; risk Y has likelihood 2 and severity 5. What is the larger of the two scores?
answer: 10
tol: 0.001
check: max(4 * 2, 2 * 5)
hint: Multiply likelihood by severity for each risk and take the larger product.
solution: Risk X scores $4 \times 2 = 8$ and risk Y scores $2 \times 5 = 10$, so the larger score is 10. The scores rank the risks but are not measurements.

@quiz self
prompt: Argue for one of the three views on who should decide the acceptable error rate of a public-sector decision model (democratic and regulatory, developer and deployer responsibility, or participatory), and give the strongest objection to your view with your reply.
hint: Pick one view, give your best evidence, and answer the objection you find hardest.
solution: One defensible answer favors democratic and regulatory decision: acceptable error in a public system is a value judgment about how burdens are distributed, and such judgments should be made by accountable institutions, with experts informing but not replacing them. The evidence is that private deployers face incentives to underprice harms to third parties and that public bodies exercising power over citizens owe them a process they can contest. The strongest objection is speed and expertise: legislatures cannot set thresholds for every model and may write rules that become obsolete. A reply is to set outcome-based requirements (measure error by group, publish results, provide appeal) and delegate specifics to a regulator with technical staff and review, so that values are set democratically while details can adapt. A fair answer admits that this does not remove the need for participation by affected groups in design and monitoring.
rubric: Takes a clear position and supports it with an argument or evidence.
rubric: States the strongest objection fairly.
rubric: Gives a reply that engages the objection rather than restating the position.
