@article
A hospital wants to deploy a model that decides which discharged patients get a follow-up phone call. The model raises the total number of problems caught early, but it works less well for patients who speak a minority language. Should the hospital deploy it? The numbers matter, but so does the question of which numbers to care about. Ethical frameworks are organized ways of answering that question. None is universally accepted; each is a lens that makes some considerations visible and hides others.

### Four lenses

**Consequentialism** judges an action by its outcomes. Its best-known form, **utilitarianism**, says to choose the option with the greatest total welfare, counting everyone equally. If option $a$ gives each person in group $g$ (of size $n_g$) a welfare change $u_g(a)$, the utilitarian choice is

$$a^\star = \arg\max_a \sum_g n_g\, u_g(a).$$

With uncertain outcomes, replace welfare by **expected utility**: $\mathbb{E}[u] = \sum_k P(k)\, u_k$.

**Deontology** judges an action by whether it respects duties and rights, whatever the consequences. A deontological view treats some things (deceiving people, using them merely as means, ignoring their consent) as constraints that limit the set of permitted actions. Formally, it first filters the options to a permitted set, and only then, if at all, compares outcomes within it.

**Virtue ethics**, which descends from Aristotle, asks what a person of good character (honest, fair, courageous, compassionate, prudent) would do, and what habits an institution or profession cultivates. It shifts attention from single decisions to the character of designers and organizations.

**Contractualism** asks which principles people could justify to one another. In Rawls's *A Theory of Justice* (1971), principles are chosen behind a **veil of ignorance**, without knowing your own position in society. One much-discussed result is the **difference principle**: arrange inequalities so that the worst-off group is as well off as possible. As a decision rule this is **maximin**:

$$a^\star = \arg\max_a \; \min_g\, u_g(a).$$

Other approaches, such as the ethics of care, stress relationships, dependence and responsiveness to particular people's needs.

### A small case

Suppose a policy helps two large groups and slightly harms a small one. A utilitarian adds up benefits weighted by group size; a maximin reasoner looks only at the group that does worst. They can disagree, and neither is making an arithmetic mistake: they differ about what matters.

| policy | group sizes | welfare change per person | total | worst group |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| deploy the model | 500, 300, 200 | +3, +2, −2 | 1,700 | −2 |
| use manual review | 500, 300, 200 | +1, +1, +1 | 1,000 | +1 |

Utilitarianism favors deploying (1,700 against 1,000); maximin favors manual review (a worst-off welfare of +1 against −2).

### Objections

Each lens has well-known objections. Utilitarianism can license sacrificing a minority for a larger total, and it requires comparing welfare across people, which is hard to measure. Deontology can seem rigid when two duties conflict or when following a rule produces a disaster, and it does not say how to rank duties. Virtue ethics gives less concrete guidance for a particular decision. Maximin can seem to give too much weight to a tiny group's loss when the gain elsewhere is enormous, and defenders of Rawls reply that it is a principle for the basic structure of society, not for every decision. Reasonable people weigh these objections differently.

### Using the frameworks well

In practice, people use several lenses and look for convergence. Where all of them point the same way (do not deploy a system that discriminates on a protected trait without any justification), a decision is easy. Where they split, the split is informative: it shows which value a decision trades away, which is what you should document and discuss with stakeholders. A common method is **reflective equilibrium**: adjust principles and judgments about cases against each other until they cohere. Another is to take **moral uncertainty** seriously by asking which option is acceptable under several frameworks, not only the one you favor.

Two cautions. First, a framework does not remove the need for facts: a utilitarian analysis is only as good as its estimates of benefits, harms and who bears them. Second, formalizing an ethical question as an optimization problem hides choices (whose welfare counts, how it is measured, what is excluded) that are themselves ethical.

@example
title: Total welfare against the worst-off group
problem: A clinic can use policy P or policy Q for 1,000 patients in three groups of sizes 600, 300 and 100. Policy P changes welfare per person by $+2$, $+2$ and $-1$ for the three groups; policy Q changes it by $+1$, $+1$ and $+1$. Which policy does a utilitarian choose, and which does a maximin reasoner choose?
step: Compute the utilitarian total for P: $600(2) + 300(2) + 100(-1) = 1200 + 600 - 100 = 1700$.
step: Compute the total for Q: $600(1) + 300(1) + 100(1) = 1000$. Since $1700 > 1000$, the utilitarian chooses P.
step: Find the worst-off group under each policy: for P it is $-1$ (the smallest group), for Q it is $+1$. Since $+1 > -1$, the maximin reasoner chooses Q.
step: State the lesson: the two rules disagree because they weigh the small group differently, not because either is miscalculated.
answer: Utilitarian: P (total 1,700 against 1,000). Maximin: Q (worst-off welfare +1 against −1).

@example
title: A constraint as a filter
problem: Three options for using patient records have total expected welfare 80 (option A), 95 (option B) and 120 (option C). Option C uses records without consent, which a deontological view treats as a forbidden constraint violation. What do a pure utilitarian and a constraint-respecting reasoner choose?
step: The pure utilitarian compares totals only: $120 > 95 > 80$, so option C.
step: The deontological reasoner first removes option C because it violates the consent constraint, leaving A and B.
step: Among the permitted options, compare outcomes: $95 > 80$, so option B.
answer: The utilitarian chooses C; the constraint-respecting reasoner chooses B.

@mistakes
- Treating one framework as the "scientific" answer: each has serious, well-known objections, and informed people disagree about how to weigh them.
- Confusing a utilitarian total with fairness: a large total can hide a group that is made worse off.
- Reading deontology as "ignore consequences entirely": it makes some constraints strong, but most versions still care about outcomes within the permitted set.
- Assuming the numbers in a cost-benefit table are neutral facts: someone chose whose welfare counts and how it is measured.

@practice self
prompt: A company uses a model to screen job applications. Analyze it with one consequentialist and one deontological lens: name one consideration each lens makes visible, state what each would recommend or ask for, and say where they might conflict.
hint: Name who is helped and harmed, and whose rights or consent are involved.
solution: A consequentialist lens counts the benefits (faster hiring, lower cost, possibly more consistent decisions) against the harms (qualified applicants rejected by error, effects on groups the model handles poorly) and recommends deploying only if the expected total, with an honest estimate of the error costs, is better than the alternative; it asks for measured error rates. A deontological lens asks whether applicants are treated as persons with rights: whether they know an automated system is used, can contest a rejection, and are not judged on protected traits or on irrelevant information. The lenses can conflict: a system that raises total hiring quality but cannot explain or allow challenge to rejections may pass the first test and fail the second. A strong answer notes that the second lens could be answered by process changes (notice, appeal) rather than by abandoning the system, and says which consideration it would put first and why.

@quiz self
prompt: A model raises average accuracy for all patients but lowers it for a small group. Argue for either a utilitarian or a maximin position on deploying it, and then give the strongest reply from the other side.
hint: State what you count, give a reason for it, then answer the best objection to your own view.
solution: A utilitarian argument: if the gain for the many is large and the loss to the small group is modest, withholding the model harms far more people than deploying it, and refusing a net-beneficial tool because it is imperfectly distributed treats the status quo as if it had no victims; the loss to the small group should be mitigated, for example with human review, not used to block the benefit. The strongest reply from maximin: the small group did not choose to bear the cost, and a policy would be unacceptable if a person who did not know which group they were in could not accept it; aggregate gains cannot justify making the worst-off worse off, especially when the group is already disadvantaged. The utilitarian can respond that maximin, taken literally, would forbid any improvement that costs one person a little. The disagreement turns on whether there is a threshold of harm to a minority that total gains cannot outweigh, and on whether mitigation is realistic.
rubric: Commits to one position and gives a reason that appeals to the stated framework.
rubric: States the strongest objection fairly, without caricature.
rubric: Notes what facts (size of loss, availability of mitigation) would change the conclusion.
