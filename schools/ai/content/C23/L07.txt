@article
When an automated system harms someone, who answers for it? If the vendor says the customer chose the settings, the customer says the vendor built the model, and nobody wrote down what the model was tested on, then no one is accountable in practice. **Accountability** means that identifiable parties can be asked to explain and justify their decisions about a system and can be made to fix problems. It rests on mechanisms: documentation that records what was done, audits that check it, reporting of incidents, and consequences, such as liability or regulatory action, when things go wrong.

### Documentation

A **model card** (Mitchell and colleagues, 2019) is a short document that accompanies a trained model. Typical sections: model details (who built it, when, what type); intended use and out-of-scope uses; relevant factors (groups, environments, instruments); metrics; evaluation data; training data; **quantitative analyses**, ideally *disaggregated* by group and by combinations of factors; ethical considerations; and caveats and recommendations. The central practice is to report performance for subgroups, not only an overall number, and to say where the model should not be used. **Datasheets for datasets** (Gebru and colleagues, 2018) do the same for data: why it was collected, who is represented, how it was labeled, what it should not be used for. Documentation is useful only if it is accurate, kept current and read.

### Audits

An **audit** is a systematic examination of a system against stated criteria. An *internal* audit is done by the organization's own staff; an *external* audit by an independent party. The auditor's **access** matters: black-box (only inputs and outputs), grey-box (some documentation or data) or white-box (model and training data). Arguments for external audits: independence reduces conflicts of interest. Arguments for internal audits: they can happen earlier and with full access, and an organization can learn from its own findings. Objections to each: external audits may lack access and can be limited to a snapshot, and internal audits can be shaped by the audited organization's interests. Many proposals combine them.

### The statistics of an audit

Suppose an audit samples $n$ decisions and finds a proportion $\hat{p}$ with some property (an error, a selection). With a normal approximation, a $95\%$ confidence interval is

$$\hat{p} \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}},$$

and the half-width is the **margin of error** $m$. The sample size needed for a margin $m$ is $n = z^2\,p(1-p)/m^2$, where $z = 1.96$; using $p = 0.5$ gives the largest, conservative value. Margin shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$: quadrupling the sample halves it.

To test whether two groups' selection rates differ, with $x_1$ of $n_1$ and $x_2$ of $n_2$ selected, let $\hat{p}_1 = x_1/n_1$, $\hat{p}_2 = x_2/n_2$ and the pooled rate $\hat{p} = (x_1 + x_2)/(n_1 + n_2)$. The **two-proportion z statistic** is

$$z = \frac{\hat{p}_1 - \hat{p}_2}{\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})\left(\frac{1}{n_1} + \frac{1}{n_2}\right)}}.$$

A value with $|z| > 1.96$ is significant at the 5% level. Remember three cautions. Statistical significance is not practical importance: a tiny gap can be significant in a huge sample. A non-significant result in a small subgroup shows little, since the test may lack power. And when many groups or metrics are tested, some will look significant by chance unless the analysis is corrected for multiple comparisons.

### Limits of accountability mechanisms

Audits capture a system at a moment; models and data change. Auditors need access and expertise. Documentation can become a box-ticking exercise, and disclosure alone does not give anyone power to demand change. Accountability also needs someone with authority to act on findings.

@example
title: Margin of error from an audit sample
problem: An audit samples 400 automated decisions and finds that 120 were wrong. Give a 95% confidence interval for the error rate.
step: The sample proportion is $\hat{p} = 120/400 = 0.30$.
step: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.30 \times 0.70 / 400} = \sqrt{0.000525} \approx 0.0229$.
step: The margin of error is $1.96 \times 0.0229 \approx 0.0449$.
step: The interval is $0.30 \pm 0.045$, that is about $0.255$ to $0.345$.
answer: About 0.255 to 0.345.

@example
title: Comparing two selection rates
problem: In a sample of 200 applicants from each of two groups, 90 of group 1 and 60 of group 2 were selected. Compute the two-proportion z statistic.
step: The rates are $\hat{p}_1 = 0.45$ and $\hat{p}_2 = 0.30$, a difference of $0.15$.
step: The pooled rate is $(90 + 60)/400 = 0.375$.
step: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.375 \times 0.625 \times (1/200 + 1/200)} = \sqrt{0.00234375} \approx 0.0484$.
step: Then $z = 0.15/0.0484 \approx 3.10$, which exceeds 1.96, so the difference is statistically significant at the 5% level. Whether it is unjust depends on why it arises and on the qualifications of the applicants.
answer: $z \approx 3.10$.

@mistakes
- Reporting only an overall accuracy: disaggregated results are the point of a model card.
- Reading "not significant" as "no difference": a small sample may be unable to detect a real gap.
- Testing many subgroups and reporting only the significant ones: multiple comparisons make chance findings likely.
- Treating documentation as accountability: someone must be answerable and able to act on what the documentation shows.

@practice num
prompt: In a sample, 48 of 120 applicants from group 1 and 30 of 100 applicants from group 2 were selected. What is the two-proportion z statistic for the difference in selection rates, using the pooled rate? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 1.54
tol: 0.01
check: (48 / 120 - 30 / 100) / sqrt((78 / 220) * (1 - 78 / 220) * (1 / 120 + 1 / 100))
hint: Pool the two samples: $\hat{p} = (48 + 30)/(120 + 100)$, then divide the difference by the standard error.
solution: The rates are 0.40 and 0.30. The pooled rate is $78/220 \approx 0.3545$, and the standard error is $\sqrt{0.3545 \times 0.6455 \times (1/120 + 1/100)} \approx 0.0648$. So $z \approx 0.10 / 0.0648 \approx 1.54$, below 1.96, so the gap is not significant at the 5% level (the sample may simply be small).

@practice self
prompt: A city plans to audit an automated benefits-eligibility system. Argue whether the audit should be led by an internal team or an external body, and describe what access, metrics and sample size you would require. Give the strongest objection to your choice and your reply.
hint: Weigh independence against access and speed; say which statistics you would report and why.
solution: One strong position favors an external audit with legal access to the model, decision logs and data: a public system that decides who gets benefits affects people's livelihoods, and an independent body avoids the conflict of interest of an agency judging its own system. The auditor should have grey- or white-box access, report error and selection rates disaggregated by group with confidence intervals, test differences with an appropriate multiple-comparison correction, and choose samples large enough for small groups (for a margin of 0.05 on a rate near one half, about 385 decisions per group). The strongest objection is that external auditors lack context, can only provide a snapshot and may be slow, whereas an internal team can monitor continuously and fix problems quickly. A reply is to require both: continuous internal monitoring reported to an external auditor who verifies it periodically and has authority to publish findings. A fair answer notes that audit findings change nothing unless someone is obliged to respond.
rubric: Takes a position on who audits and justifies it with independence and access.
rubric: Specifies concrete audit measures (disaggregated metrics, intervals, sample sizes).
rubric: States and answers the strongest objection.

prompt: Which statements about audit statistics are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Quadrupling the sample size halves the margin of error of a proportion, other things equal.
choice*: Testing many subgroups without correction makes at least one chance "significant" difference likely.
choice*: A statistically significant difference can be too small to matter in practice when the sample is very large.
choice: A non-significant result in a small subgroup proves that the groups are treated identically.

solution: The margin scales like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so quadrupling $n$ halves it; multiple comparisons inflate false positives; and significance is different from importance. A non-significant result in a small group may reflect low power, and the margin does not shrink in proportion to $n$.
