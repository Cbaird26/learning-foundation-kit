step: Write a model card (model details, intended use and out-of-scope uses, factors, metrics, evaluation data, quantitative analyses by group, ethical considerations, caveats).
step: Map the system's obligations under the EU AI Act: roles, tier, obligations by role and application dates as taught in Lesson 11 and Lesson 12, and say what you checked or would check in the current text. Then write a recommendation (deploy, deploy with conditions, or do not deploy), state the strongest objection to it and your reply.

### Week-by-week key ideas

- **Week 1, ethical frameworks.** Utilitarian choice maximizes $\sum_g n_g u_g$ (use expected utility under uncertainty); maximin maximizes the worst group's welfare; deontology filters options by duties and rights; virtue ethics asks about character; contractualism asks what could be justified behind a veil of ignorance.
- **Week 2, harms and stakeholders.** Allocative, representational and quality-of-service harms; decision subjects, operators, builders, bystanders; expected harm $N p s$; views on who decides.
- **Week 3, fairness.** Selection rate, TPR, FPR, PPV; demographic parity, equal opportunity, equalized odds, predictive parity, calibration within groups; the four-fifths rule of thumb.
- **Week 4, impossibility.** $\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$; with different base rates, equal PPV and TPR force different FPR except in degenerate cases; the Kleinberg et al. conditions.
- **Week 5, privacy.** $\varepsilon$-differential privacy; sensitivity; Laplace scale $b = \Delta f/\varepsilon$ and $P(|X|>t) = e^{-t/b}$; sequential composition adds; post-processing is safe; randomized response $\varepsilon = \ln\frac{q}{1-q}$.
- **Week 6, explanation.** Shapley values and efficiency; linear attributions $w_i(x_i - \bar{x}_i)$; counterfactuals $\Delta = (\tau - s)/w_j$; interpretable versus post-hoc debate.
- **Week 7, accountability.** Model cards, datasheets, audits; margin of error $1.96\sqrt{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})/n}$; two-proportion z; multiple comparisons.
- **Week 8, safety.** Specification gaming and regret; goal misgeneralization; adversarial change $\epsilon\lVert\mathbf{w}\rVert_1$; distribution shift; misuse; open versus closed release.
- **Week 9, society.** Displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects; detector PPV under a low base rate; $E = N P t\,\text{PUE}$ and emissions; copyright views.
- **Week 10, advanced AI risk.** Four positions with best objections; break-even $p^\star = C/(rL)$; forecast pooling.
- **Week 11, governance.** EU AI Act tiers and dates; US measures; NIST AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage); ISO/IEC 42001; OECD principles; $C \approx 6ND$.
- **Week 12, professional responsibility.** Codes, practices, reviewers $1 - (1-q)^k$, Poisson incidents, mapping obligations.

### Common errors

Using the wrong denominator for TPR, FPR or PPV; claiming that a fairness impossibility result means fairness cannot be achieved at all; thinking larger $\varepsilon$ means more privacy; treating an attribution as a cause; calling an executive order preemptive of state law; quoting application dates from memory instead of checking the text; and treating one side of a contested debate as settled.

@exam num
prompt: In one group the base rate is 0.25, and a classifier has true positive rate 0.8 and positive predictive value 0.8. Using the identity relating these quantities, what is its false positive rate? Give your answer to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0667
tol: 0.0001
check: (0.25 / 0.75) * ((1 - 0.8) / 0.8) * 0.8
hint: Use $\text{FPR} = \frac{p}{1-p}\cdot\frac{1-\text{PPV}}{\text{PPV}}\cdot\text{TPR}$.
solution: $\frac{0.25}{0.75} = \frac13$, $\frac{1-0.8}{0.8} = 0.25$, so $\text{FPR} = \frac13 \times 0.25 \times 0.8 = 0.0667$. If another group with a different base rate had the same TPR and PPV, its FPR would differ, by the impossibility result.

@exam self
prompt: A hospital proposes to use a model to rank patients for a scarce follow-up program. Write a short recommendation memo (about 300 words) that (1) names the stakeholders and two harms, (2) states which fairness criterion you would prioritize and why, given that base rates differ across groups, (3) proposes how to publish group counts privately, (4) notes one legal or standards framework to check, and (5) gives the strongest objection to your recommendation and your reply.
hint: Connect weeks 2, 3 to 5, 7, 11 and the debates of weeks 1 and 10; commit to a recommendation.
solution: Recommendation: deploy as a decision aid with conditions. Stakeholders: patients ranked by the model, patients not enrolled because slots are scarce, clinicians, the hospital and its community. Harms: a qualified patient wrongly ranked low is denied care (allocative); lower accuracy for one group is a quality-of-service harm. Because base rates of need differ, the impossibility results mean calibration and equal error rates cannot both hold, so I would prioritize equal opportunity (equal true positive rates), since the main harm is a patient who needs the program missing it, while reporting calibration and PPV by group so clinicians know what a score means. Group counts for public reports would use the Laplace mechanism with sensitivity 1 and a stated $\varepsilon$ for each release, with a total annual budget set in advance, since repeated exact releases can leak individuals' status; small groups would be merged or suppressed when noise would swamp the count. The framework to check is the EU AI Act where applicable (healthcare triage and medical uses can bring high-risk obligations; check the current text, roles and dates) together with the NIST AI RMF for organizing risk management and the hospital's duties of documentation and a model card. The strongest objection: equal opportunity ignores false positives, so one group could be over-enrolled at the expense of scarce slots, and a utilitarian would say the program should simply maximize total benefit. Reply: audit false positive rates and total benefit and adjust the threshold if the cost is large; a justified departure from equal opportunity should be documented and reviewed, and clinician oversight with an appeal route handles individual errors. I would revisit the choice if measured outcomes showed that equalizing true positive rates reduced total benefit substantially.
