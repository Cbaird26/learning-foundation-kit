@article
Imagine you built the evaluation pipeline for a hiring tool. Two days before launch you notice that the tool's error rate for applicants with employment gaps is double the average. The schedule is tight, your manager says the average is what the contract specifies, and nobody outside the team knows. What do you owe, and to whom? Professional responsibility is the answer a field gives to such moments before they happen.

### Professional codes

The **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** (revised in 2018) is a widely used code for computing professionals. Its general principles include contributing to society and to human well-being, avoiding harm, being honest and trustworthy, being fair and not discriminating, respecting privacy and confidentiality, and respecting the work required to produce new ideas, inventions and works; its professional responsibilities include competent work, honest disclosure of limits, and giving comprehensive evaluations of systems and their risks. Other organizations have similar codes. Codes are not law: they state expectations that members accept, and they guide choices where the law is silent. They conflict at times, for example when loyalty to an employer pulls against the public interest, and they do not say how to resolve every conflict.

### Practices that turn duties into routines

- **Impact assessment** before deployment: who is affected, what could go wrong, and what alternatives exist.
- **Documentation**: model cards, datasheets, records of decisions and testing (Lesson 7).
- **Testing and red teaming** on disaggregated groups and foreseeable misuse.
- **Human oversight** with real authority and information, so that review is not a rubber stamp.
- **Monitoring and incident handling**: track performance after deployment, with a channel for reports and the ability to roll back.
- **Escalation paths** and protection for people who raise concerns. Some laws now protect such reporters; for instance, California's SB 53 protects whistleblowers (Lesson 11).

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework's four functions (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) organize these routines at the level of an organization.

### Responsibility is shared, and limits are real

Harms from AI usually involve many hands: data collectors, model builders, product teams, deployers, users and regulators. This makes responsibility diffuse, which is why it needs explicit assignment. Individuals have duties, but an individual practitioner cannot fix a structure that rewards cutting corners, and organizations should not rely on individual heroism. When a practitioner finds a serious problem there are options: raise it internally, document it, propose mitigations, decline to proceed, or report externally where the law provides protection. *One view* holds that the duty to the public comes first when the risk of serious harm is credible, and that internal channels are worth trying but not at the price of silence. *Another view* stresses loyalty to the organization, proper channels and the risk of acting on incomplete information, and holds that external disclosure is for rare cases after internal remedies fail. Both agree on documenting concerns and on the importance of evidence.

### Reliability arithmetic for review processes

If each of $k$ reviewers independently catches a problem with probability $q$, the chance that at least one catches it is

$$1 - (1-q)^k.$$

Independence is a strong assumption: reviewers who share training, tools or incentives miss the same problems, so real gains are smaller. If incidents arrive at an average rate $\lambda$ per year, as a Poisson process, the probability of at least one incident in a period of $t$ years is $1 - e^{-\lambda t}$. For $\lambda = 2$ per year over half a year, $1 - e^{-1} \approx 0.632$.

### Mapping a system's obligations under the EU AI Act

To map obligations under a law, work in steps: identify the **role** (the Act distinguishes, among others, providers who develop a system and put it on the market and deployers who use it); decide whether the system is **in scope** and which **tier** it falls into; list the **obligations** for that tier and role; and note the **dates** on which they apply. As of this writing, a recruitment or candidate-screening tool is an example of a use listed in Annex III (employment and worker management), so it is a **high-risk** system. Its provider must, among other things, maintain a risk management system, govern its data, keep technical documentation and logs, give deployers instructions, design for human oversight and ensure accuracy and robustness. The deployer must use it as instructed, assign trained human oversight, monitor it, and inform affected workers or candidates as the Act requires. After the Digital Omnibus on AI, the high-risk obligations for Annex III systems apply from 2 December 2027; the AI literacy duty already applies from 2 February 2025, and the prohibitions (which include some uses of emotion inference at work, with narrow exceptions) from the same date. If the tool includes a chatbot, the Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 August 2026. The text, annexes and amendments change, so check the current version on EUR-Lex.

@example
title: Several independent reviewers
problem: Each of three independent reviewers catches a given flaw with probability 0.3. What is the probability that at least one catches it, and how many reviewers would you need for at least 0.9?
step: The probability that one reviewer misses it is $0.7$, so all three miss it with probability $0.7^3 = 0.343$.
step: The probability that at least one catches it is $1 - 0.343 = 0.657$.
step: For at least 0.9 we need $0.7^k \le 0.1$, that is $k \ge \ln 0.1/\ln 0.7 \approx 6.46$, so $k = 7$.
step: Caution: this assumes independence; if the reviewers share blind spots, more reviewers add less.
answer: 0.657 with three reviewers; seven reviewers would be needed for 0.9 under independence.

@mistakes
- Treating a professional code as law, or as irrelevant because it is not law.
- Assuming that more reviewers always multiplies detection: shared blind spots break independence.
- Assuming the high-risk obligations apply on the original date: check the current dates, since the Digital Omnibus on AI moved them.
- Mapping obligations without naming the role: providers and deployers have different duties under the same law.

@quiz self
prompt: Two days before launch you find that a hiring tool's error rate for applicants with employment gaps is double the average, and management wants to ship because the contract specifies only the average. Argue what you should do, and give the strongest objection to your course of action and your reply.
hint: Consider documenting, escalating, mitigations, and when external reporting might be justified.
solution: A defensible course: document the finding with the data, report it in writing to the responsible manager and the safety or ethics contact, propose mitigations (human review of rejections for this group, a delayed launch for this feature, a retraining plan), and ask for a written decision; if the company ships a system with a known, serious and unmitigated harm and no internal route resolves it, consider external reporting through any protected channel. The reasons: a doubled error rate for an identifiable group is a foreseeable harm, the code of ethics asks practitioners to avoid harm and report risks honestly, and an average-only contract does not change the harm. The strongest objection is that the finding is from a small test, that internal escalation first respects the organization's right to fix it, and that premature disclosure could damage the company and the practitioner on incomplete evidence. A reply is that escalation in writing is the first step the plan takes, the data should be confirmed (sample sizes and confidence intervals), and external action is reserved for serious, credible, unresolved harm. A fair answer notes that people reasonably differ about where the line between loyalty and public duty falls.
