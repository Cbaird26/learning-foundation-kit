@article
Three questions arrive together whenever a new technology spreads: what happens to jobs, what happens to what people can trust, and what does it cost the planet? AI raises all three. On the first two the evidence is still developing and informed people disagree; on the third there is arithmetic that you can do yourself.

### Work

The **task-based** view (associated with Acemoglu and Restrepo) treats a job as a bundle of tasks. Technology affects labor demand through several effects. The **displacement effect**: machines take over tasks that workers did, reducing labor demand for those tasks. The **productivity effect**: cheaper production raises demand for output and for tasks still done by people. The **reinstatement effect**: new tasks appear in which people have an advantage. The net effect depends on the balance, and it varies across occupations and over time.

A toy calculation: an industry uses 40 million hours of labor. Automation takes over tasks that were $35\%$ of the hours. If output stays the same, labor falls to $40 \times 0.65 = 26$ million hours. If cheaper output raises demand so that output grows by $25\%$, and the remaining tasks scale with it, labor is $26 \times 1.25 = 32.5$ million hours: still lower than before, but the productivity effect has offset part of the displacement.

*View 1: technological change has historically created more work than it destroyed.* Past waves of automation shifted employment toward new occupations and raised living standards, and nothing guarantees that this time differs. Objection: adjustment can take decades, harm particular workers and regions severely, and a technology that automates cognitive tasks broadly may not follow past patterns. *View 2: AI could displace labor faster than new tasks arise.* Evidence cited includes the substantial wage declines of workers in routine tasks after past automation, and the breadth of tasks to which AI methods apply. Objection: exposure of a task is not the same as replacement of a job, adoption is slow and costly, and predictions of mass unemployment have failed before. What is known is limited: forecasts of net effects vary widely, and the distribution of gains, by wages, ownership and policy, matters as much as the total.

### Information

AI makes synthetic text, images, audio and video cheap, which affects what can be trusted. Responses include **provenance** (signed metadata that records how content was made), **watermarking** (marks embedded in generated content) and **detection** classifiers. Each has limits: metadata can be stripped, watermarks may be removed or only work for cooperating generators, and detectors err. A detector's usefulness depends on the **base rate**. Suppose it has sensitivity (true positive rate) 0.95, a false positive rate 0.05, and 1% of items are synthetic. In 10,000 items, 100 are synthetic and 95 are caught; of 9,900 genuine items, 495 are flagged. Of 590 flags, only 95 are right, so the positive predictive value is $95/590 \approx 0.161$. A seemingly accurate detector produces mostly false alarms when the thing it seeks is rare.

### Copyright and training data

Whether training on copyrighted works without permission is lawful and fair is contested, and courts and legislatures in different countries are addressing it, so check the current status. *The case that training is permissible*: a model learns statistical patterns, as a person who reads many books does, and does not store copies; the use is transformative and the resulting tools have broad public benefit; requiring licenses for all training data would favor the largest firms. *The case against*: works are copied at scale, without consent or payment, to build products that compete with the creators; models can sometimes reproduce training material; and individual creators lack bargaining power. *Replies*: the first side must address market substitution and memorization; the second must explain why learning from works differs from what people do and how licensing could work in practice. Both sides agree that how training data is documented (see Lesson 7) affects what can be decided.

### Environment

The energy of a training run is approximately

$$E = N \cdot P \cdot t \cdot \text{PUE},$$

with $N$ accelerators of average power $P$ (in kW) running for $t$ hours, and **power usage effectiveness** $\text{PUE} \ge 1$, the ratio of a data center's total energy to the energy used by computing equipment (1.2 means 20% overhead). Emissions are $E$ times the **carbon intensity** of the electricity (kg of CO$_2$-equivalent per kWh), which varies widely by place and time. For 256 accelerators at 0.5 kW for 300 hours with PUE 1.2: $E = 256 \times 0.5 \times 300 \times 1.2 = 46{,}080$ kWh, and at 0.4 kg/kWh, about 18,432 kg, or 18.4 tonnes. Training is only part of the footprint: serving a model to many users, manufacturing hardware and cooling water also count, and estimates are uncertain. Whether AI will reduce emissions elsewhere by improving efficiency, or increase total demand for energy as use grows, is an open empirical question.

@example
title: Energy and emissions of a training run
problem: A model is trained on 64 accelerators drawing an average of 0.3 kW each for 500 hours in a data center with PUE 1.15, on a grid with carbon intensity 0.5 kg of CO$_2$-equivalent per kWh. Find the energy used and the emissions.
step: Multiply the compute power and time: $64 \times 0.3 \times 500 = 9600$ kWh.
step: Apply the PUE overhead: $9600 \times 1.15 = 11{,}040$ kWh in total.
step: Multiply by the carbon intensity: $11{,}040 \times 0.5 = 5{,}520$ kg, or 5.52 tonnes of CO$_2$-equivalent.
answer: 11,040 kWh and 5,520 kg CO$_2$-equivalent.

@example
title: A detector under a low base rate
problem: A detector for synthetic images has sensitivity 0.8 and false positive rate 0.1. In a collection, 10% of images are synthetic. What is the probability that an image flagged by the detector is synthetic?
step: Take 1,000 images: 100 synthetic and 900 genuine.
step: True positives: $0.8 \times 100 = 80$. False positives: $0.1 \times 900 = 90$.
step: Positive predictive value: $80/(80 + 90) = 80/170 \approx 0.471$.
step: Interpret: more than half of the flags are false, although the detector is right 80% of the time on synthetic images.
answer: About 0.47.

@mistakes
- Equating a task's exposure to AI with the loss of the job: jobs bundle many tasks, and adoption and demand effects matter.
- Judging a detector by its sensitivity alone: with a low base rate most flags can be false.
- Omitting PUE or the grid's carbon intensity when estimating emissions.
- Presenting either side of the copyright and training-data debate as settled: the law differs between countries and is changing.

prompt: A detector for AI-generated text has sensitivity 0.9 and false positive rate 0.02. In a set of documents, 5% are AI-generated. What is the probability that a flagged document is AI-generated? Give your answer to three decimal places.
answer: 0.703
tol: 0.001
check: (0.9 * 0.05) / (0.9 * 0.05 + 0.02 * 0.95)
hint: Use Bayes' rule, or imagine 1,000 documents and count true and false flags.
solution: Of 1,000 documents, 50 are AI-generated and 45 are flagged; of 950 others, 19 are flagged. So the probability is $45 / (45 + 19) = 45/64 \approx 0.703$.

@practice self
prompt: Make the strongest case for one position on whether it is lawful and fair to train AI models on copyrighted works without permission, then give the strongest objection to your position and your reply. (The law differs between countries and is changing; argue the ethical and policy case.)
hint: Say what is copied, who benefits and who is harmed, and address market substitution or memorization.
solution: One strong case for permitting training: a model extracts statistical patterns from many works and does not, in ordinary operation, store or reproduce them, which resembles how people learn from reading; the use is transformative, and the resulting tools serve a wide public. Requiring licenses for every work would make training feasible only for the largest firms, concentrating power. The strongest objection is that the creators' work is copied at scale and used to build products that can substitute for them in the market, without consent or payment, and that models sometimes reproduce training works closely. A reply is to separate the cases: allow training in general, but treat verbatim reproduction as infringement, require opt-out mechanisms or collective licensing for commercial products that compete directly with creators, and require transparency about training data so that disputes can be settled. A fair answer concedes that if substitution harms are large, a compensation mechanism has strong ethical force, and that the facts about substitution and memorization are empirical questions.

@quiz self
prompt: Present the strongest case for one view on whether AI is likely to reduce overall employment or mostly shift it, then give the strongest objection and your reply.
hint: Use the displacement, productivity and reinstatement effects, and say what evidence would distinguish the views.
solution: One strong case for the shifting view: automation has repeatedly lowered the labor needed for particular tasks while raising incomes and demand and creating new tasks, so employment has moved among occupations without a lasting decline; the productivity and reinstatement effects have offset displacement. The strongest objection is that AI is a general-purpose technology that may automate cognitive tasks across many occupations at once, leaving fewer areas for workers to move to, and that transitions can be long and costly for those displaced.
