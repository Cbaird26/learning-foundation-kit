@article
This lesson **describes** religions as historians do: it reports what believers teach and how communities developed, without judging which beliefs are true. Historians also study how religions moved, because belief traveled with merchants, pilgrims, teachers, armies and rulers.

### Major traditions in brief

**Judaism** teaches belief in one God who made a covenant with the people of Israel. Its sacred texts include the Hebrew Bible (Tanakh) and later rabbinic writings. Jewish communities spread widely across the Mediterranean and Middle East, often as minorities.

**Christianity** began in first-century Judea among followers of Jesus of Nazareth, whom Christians believe to be the Messiah and Son of God. Its scripture is the Bible, including the New Testament. Early Christianity spread along Roman roads and sea lanes. The Edict of Milan (313 CE) granted toleration in the Roman Empire, and in 380 CE a decree made a form of Nicene Christianity the empire's favored religion. Later divisions produced Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and (after the 1500s) Protestant branches.

**Islam** began in the Arabian Peninsula in the early 600s CE. Muslims believe that God (Allah) revealed the Quran to the Prophet Muhammad. The **Five Pillars** are the declaration of faith, prayer, almsgiving, fasting in Ramadan and pilgrimage (hajj) to Mecca. The Islamic calendar begins with the **Hijra**, the move to Medina in 622 CE. Islam later split into Sunni and Shia communities over questions of leadership after Muhammad.

**Hinduism** has no single founder or creed and developed in South Asia over thousands of years. Common ideas include dharma (duty), karma (action and consequence), samsara (cycles of rebirth) and moksha (liberation). Texts include the Vedas, Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita.

**Buddhism** arose in northern India around the 400s to 500s BCE. Its teachings include the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path as a way to end suffering. Buddhism split into major traditions such as Theravada and Mahayana.

### How religions spread

Spread had several channels:
- **Trade.** Merchants on the **Silk Road** carried Buddhism from India through Central Asia to China (first centuries CE), where monks translated texts and built monasteries along oases. Muslim merchants brought Islam along **Indian Ocean** sea routes to East Africa, India and Southeast Asia, and along **trans-Saharan** caravan routes to West Africa.
- **Rulers.** The Mauryan emperor Ashoka supported Buddhism in the 200s BCE; Roman emperors shaped Christianity's growth; Muslim-ruled states expanded after the 630s.
- **Missionaries and pilgrims.** Teachers and pilgrims carried texts and ideas. Hajj routes connected distant Muslim lands.
- **Conquest and conversion.** Some conversions were voluntary and gradual; others were influenced by taxation, law or force. Historians study each region separately.

### Mapping a religion

To map a religion's spread, give the **origin**, the **route** (Silk Road, Indian Ocean, trans-Saharan caravan, Roman roads), the **carriers** (merchants, monks, rulers) and an approximate **date**.

### A calendar detail

The Islamic calendar has 12 lunar months of 29 or 30 days, about 354 days a year, so it shifts earlier by about 11 days each solar year relative to a 365-day calendar.

title: Mapping the spread of Buddhism

step: Origin: northern India, around the 400s to 500s BCE.
step: Route: overland Silk Road through Central Asia, with oasis towns and monasteries as stops.
step: Carriers: merchants, monks and pilgrims who translated and copied texts; some rulers supported monasteries.

@mistakes
- Mixing up description and endorsement: "Muslims believe the Quran was revealed to Muhammad" is a description; "the Quran was revealed to Muhammad" asserts a belief as fact.
- Treating a religion as a single unified group; Christianity, Islam and Buddhism each contain several traditions.
- Saying a religion spread only by conquest, or only by peaceful trade; the pattern varies by region and period.
- Confusing the Hijra (622 CE, the start of the Islamic calendar) with the date of Muhammad's death or of the first revelation.
