@article
A sample gives one value of $\hat{p}$ or $\bar{x}$, but we want to say something about the unknown parameter. A **confidence interval** gives a range of plausible values for the parameter, built from the sampling distribution of week 9:

$$\text{statistic} \pm \text{margin of error}, \qquad \text{margin of error} = (\text{critical value}) \times (\text{standard error}).$$

### Interpreting the confidence level

A $95\%$ confidence interval comes from a method that captures the true parameter in $95\%$ of all random samples. After the interval is computed, the parameter either is or is not inside it; the $95\%$ describes the reliability of the method, not the probability for this one interval. A correct sentence is: "We are $95\%$ confident that the true proportion lies between $0.22$ and $0.34$." The interval is about the **parameter**, not about where $95\%$ of individual patients fall.

### Interval for a proportion

For a random sample with $x$ successes in $n$ trials, $\hat{p} = x/n$ and

$$\hat{p} \pm z^*\sqrt{\frac{\hat{p}(1-\hat{p})}{n}}.$$

The critical value $z^*$ leaves the stated confidence in the middle of a standard normal curve: $z^* = 1.645$ for $90\%$, $1.96$ for $95\%$ and $2.576$ for $99\%$. This is the simple (Wald) interval; some software uses alternatives such as the Wilson interval, especially for small $n$ or $\hat{p}$ near $0$ or $1$. **Conditions:** random sample, fewer than $10\%$ of the population, and at least $10$ successes and $10$ failures in the sample.

### Interval for a mean

When $\sigma$ is unknown (the usual case) replace it with the sample standard deviation $s$ and use the **Student's $t$ distribution** with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom:

$$\bar{x} \pm t^*\,\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

The $t$ curves are bell-shaped like the normal but with heavier tails; $t^*$ is larger than $z^*$, especially for small $n$ (for $95\%$ confidence, $t^* = 2.131$ with $15$ degrees of freedom, versus $1.96$ for $z^*$). Conditions: random sample, and either a roughly normal population or a large enough $n$ (for a small sample check for strong skew and outliers).

### What controls the width

Margin of error shrinks as $n$ grows (like $1/\sqrt{n}$) and grows with the confidence level: to be more confident you need a wider interval. It does *not* correct for bias; a convenience sample gives a meaningless interval no matter how narrow.

### Choosing a sample size

To get margin of error at most $m$ for a proportion at confidence level with critical value $z^*$:

$$n \ge \left(\frac{z^*}{m}\right)^2 p^*(1-p^*),$$

where $p^*$ is a prior estimate, or $0.5$ if none exists (the most conservative choice). Always round $n$ **up**.

@example
title: A 95% interval for a proportion
problem: In a random sample of $200$ patients, $56$ developed a rash. Construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for the true proportion.
step: $\hat{p} = 56/200 = 0.28$. Check conditions: $56$ successes and $144$ failures, both at least $10$.
step: $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.28(0.72)/200} = \sqrt{0.001008} \approx 0.0317$.
step: Margin of error $= 1.96(0.0317) \approx 0.0622$. The interval is $0.28 \pm 0.062$, or about $(0.218,\ 0.342)$.
step: We are $95\%$ confident that between about $21.8\%$ and $34.2\%$ of all such patients would develop a rash.

@example
title: How many patients are needed?
problem: A researcher wants a $95\%$ confidence interval for a proportion with margin of error at most $0.03$ and no prior estimate. How large a sample is needed?
step: With no prior estimate use $p^* = 0.5$ and $z^* = 1.96$.
step: $n \ge (1.96/0.03)^2(0.25) = 4268.44 \times 0.25 \approx 1067.1$.
step: Round up: $n = 1068$ patients.

@mistakes
- Saying there is a $95\%$ probability that the true parameter is in this particular interval, or that $95\%$ of patients lie in it.
- Using $z^*$ instead of $t^*$ for a mean when $\sigma$ is estimated by $s$.
- Rounding a required sample size to the nearest integer instead of up.
- Thinking a larger sample can repair a biased sampling method.

@practice num
prompt: A sample of $n = 400$ has $\hat{p} = 0.5$. Find the margin of error of a $95\%$ confidence interval ($z^* = 1.96$), rounded to three decimal places.
answer: 0.049
check: 1.96 * sqrt(0.5 * 0.5 / 400)
tol: 0.001
hint: The standard error is $\sqrt{0.25/400}$.
solution: $\text{SE} = 0.025$ and the margin of error is $1.96(0.025) = 0.049$.

answer: 0.248
check: 90 / 300 - 1.96 * sqrt((90 / 300) * (1 - 90 / 300) / 300)

@quiz num
prompt: A random sample of $25$ patients has mean cholesterol reduction $\bar{x} = 50$ mg/dL and standard deviation $s = 10$ mg/dL. Find the lower limit of a $95\%$ $t$ interval for the population mean (use $t^* = 2.064$ for $24$ degrees of freedom), rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 45.87
check: 50 - special.stdtrit(24, 0.975) * 10 / sqrt(25)
tol: 0.02
unit: mg/dL
hint: The standard error is $s/\sqrt{n} = 10/5$.
solution: $\text{SE} = 2$, margin $= 2.064(2) = 4.128$, and the lower limit is $50 - 4.128 \approx 45.87$ mg/dL.

@quiz mc
prompt: A researcher keeps the same data but changes from a $90\%$ to a $99\%$ confidence interval for a proportion. What happens to the interval?
choice*: It becomes wider, because a larger critical value is needed for greater confidence.
choice: It becomes narrower, because the data are the same.
choice: It stays the same width, because only the sample size changes the margin of error.
choice: It becomes more likely to be biased.
hint: Compare $z^* = 1.645$ with $z^* = 2.576$.
solution: The margin of error is $z^*\times\text{SE}$, so raising the confidence level raises $z^*$ and widens the interval. Confidence level does not affect bias.
