@article
A **hypothesis test** asks whether sample data are consistent with a claim about a parameter, or so unlikely under the claim that we doubt it.

### The structure

1. **Hypotheses.** The **null hypothesis** $H_0$ is the claim being tested, a statement of "no effect" or a specific value, written with equality: $H_0: p = 0.40$. The **alternative hypothesis** $H_a$ is what the data may support: $p > 0.40$ (**one-sided**), $p < 0.40$ (one-sided) or $p \ne 0.40$ (**two-sided**).
2. **Conditions** for the model (random sample, size, normal approximation).
3. **Test statistic.** For a proportion, $z = \dfrac{\hat{p} - p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}$ with the *null* value $p_0$ in the standard error. For a mean with $\sigma$ unknown, $t = \dfrac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}$ with $n - 1$ degrees of freedom.
4. **P-value.** The probability, assuming $H_0$ is true, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. Extreme means in the direction of $H_a$: for $H_a: p > p_0$ the P-value is $P(Z \ge z)$; for $H_a: p < p_0$ it is $P(Z \le z)$; for the two-sided $H_a: p \ne p_0$ it is $2\,P(Z \ge |z|)$.
5. **Decision and conclusion.** Compare the P-value with the **significance level** $\alpha$ (commonly $0.05$, a convention rather than a law of nature). If $P \le \alpha$ reject $H_0$; otherwise **fail to reject** it. We never "accept" $H_0$ or "prove" it.

**One-sided or two-sided?** Choose before looking at the data. A two-sided test is the default in most medical research because a treatment could also be harmful; a one-sided test needs a justification stated in advance. This course states the choice in each problem.

### What the P-value is, and is not

A small P-value means the data would be surprising if $H_0$ were true. It is **not** the probability that $H_0$ is true, not the probability the result is due to chance, and it says nothing about the size or importance of an effect. A huge study can give a tiny P-value for a trivial difference; a small study can miss an important one.

### Errors and power

A **Type I error** is rejecting a true $H_0$ (a false alarm); its probability is $\alpha$. A **Type II error** is failing to reject a false $H_0$ (a miss). **Power** $= 1 - P(\text{Type II})$ is the chance of detecting a real effect; it grows with larger samples, larger true effects and larger $\alpha$.

### Writing the conclusion

State, in context: the evidence level, what it suggests, and the limits. For example: "Because $P = 0.02 < 0.05$, there is statistically significant evidence that more than $40\%$ of patients improve with the new therapy. This was a single sample from one clinic; it does not show the size of the benefit, and without randomization with a control group it does not show that the therapy caused the improvement."

@example
title: A one-sided test of a proportion
problem: A standard therapy helps $40\%$ of patients. A new therapy is tried on a random sample of $100$ patients and $50$ improve. Test $H_0: p = 0.40$ against $H_a: p > 0.40$ (one-sided, chosen in advance) at $\alpha = 0.05$.
step: Conditions: $np_0 = 40$ and $n(1-p_0) = 60$, both at least $10$.
step: $\hat{p} = 0.50$ and $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.4(0.6)/100} \approx 0.0490$, so $z = (0.50 - 0.40)/0.0490 \approx 2.04$.
step: $P(Z \ge 2.04) \approx 0.0206$.
step: Since $0.0206 < 0.05$, reject $H_0$: there is evidence that more than $40\%$ of patients improve with the new therapy, though nothing here shows it caused the improvement.

@example
title: The same data, two-sided
problem: Repeat the test above with $H_a: p \ne 0.40$.
step: The test statistic is unchanged: $z \approx 2.04$.
step: The P-value doubles: $2 \times 0.0206 \approx 0.0412$.
step: At $\alpha = 0.05$ we still reject, but the evidence is weaker. A result with a one-sided P-value just under $0.05$ would not be significant two-sided, which is why the choice must be made before seeing the data.

@mistakes
- Saying the P-value is the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
- Concluding "the null is true" or "accepting $H_0$" when the P-value is large; the correct phrase is "fail to reject".
- Using $\hat{p}$ instead of $p_0$ in the standard error of a test of a proportion.
- Treating statistical significance as proof of an important or causal effect.

@practice num
prompt: A hospital claims that $25\%$ of its patients are readmitted. In a random sample of $200$ patients, $60$ were readmitted. Find the $z$ statistic for testing $H_0: p = 0.25$, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 1.63
check: (60 / 200 - 0.25) / sqrt(0.25 * 0.75 / 200)
tol: 0.01
hint: $\hat{p} = 0.30$. Use $p_0 = 0.25$ in the standard error.
solution: $\text{SE} = \sqrt{0.25(0.75)/200} \approx 0.0306$ and $z = (0.30 - 0.25)/0.0306 \approx 1.63$.

@practice num
prompt: For a one-sided test with $H_a: p > p_0$, the test statistic is $z = 2.33$. Find the P-value, rounded to four decimal places.
answer: 0.0099
check: 1 - 0.5 * (1 + erf(2.33 / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.0005
hint: The P-value is the upper-tail area beyond $z$.
solution: $P(Z \ge 2.33) = 1 - \Phi(2.33) \approx 1 - 0.9901 = 0.0099$.

@practice num
prompt: For a two-sided test, the test statistic is $z = -1.80$. Find the P-value, rounded to three decimal places.
answer: 0.072
check: 2 * 0.5 * (1 + erf(-1.80 / sqrt(2)))
tol: 0.001
hint: Find the area in one tail and double it.
solution: $P(Z \le -1.80) \approx 0.0359$, so the two-sided P-value is $2(0.0359) \approx 0.072$.

@practice self
prompt: A random sample of $100$ patients on a new therapy gives a one-sided test of $H_0: p = 0.40$ versus $H_a: p > 0.40$ with $P = 0.02$ (patients were not randomized and there was no control group). Write a conclusion in plain words at $\alpha = 0.05$ that states the decision, what it suggests about the therapy, and two limits of the analysis.
hint: Link the decision to the P-value, say what it means in context, and say what the test cannot show.
solution: Because $P = 0.02$ is below $0.05$, we reject $H_0$: there is statistically significant evidence that more than $40\%$ of patients improve on the new therapy. Limits: the test does not show how large the improvement is (a confidence interval would), and with no control group and no randomization we cannot conclude the therapy caused the improvement, since other factors or natural recovery may explain it. The result also comes from a single sample and could be a Type I error.
rubric: States the decision (reject $H_0$) and links it to $P < \alpha$.
rubric: Says what is suggested in context, not that $H_0$ is "proven false".
rubric: Gives at least two limits, such as no causal claim without randomization, effect size unknown, or possibility of error.

@quiz num
prompt: A sample of $64$ patients has mean change $\bar{x} = 102$ and standard deviation $s = 8$. Find the $t$ statistic for testing $H_0: \mu = 100$.
answer: 2
check: (102 - 100) / (8 / sqrt(64))
tol: 0.01
hint: The standard error is $s/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: $\text{SE} = 8/\sqrt{64} = 1$, so $t = (102 - 100)/1 = 2$ with $63$ degrees of freedom.
