@article
**Preterm birth** is delivery before 37 0/7 weeks. It is the leading cause of neonatal death outside congenital anomalies, and the risk of respiratory distress, intraventricular hemorrhage, necrotizing enterocolitis and cerebral palsy rises steeply with earlier gestational age. The interventions you must know either prevent preterm birth or improve outcomes when it cannot be prevented.

### Risk factors and prediction

The strongest risk factor is a **previous spontaneous preterm birth**. Others are a short cervix, multiple gestation, polyhydramnios, uterine anomalies, infection (including bacteriuria, periodontal disease and bacterial vaginosis in some studies), smoking, cocaine use, short interpregnancy interval and vaginal bleeding. Two tests help assess a symptomatic patient between 24 and 34 weeks: **cervical length** by transvaginal ultrasound (30 mm or more is reassuring) and **fetal fibronectin** from cervicovaginal fluid, which has high negative predictive value (a negative result makes birth within 7-14 days unlikely).

### Diagnosis of preterm labor

Regular contractions (for example 4 in 20 minutes or 8 in 60 minutes) **with cervical change**: dilation of 3 cm or more, effacement of 80% or more, or progressive change on serial examinations. Contractions without cervical change are not preterm labor.

### Management

1. **Antenatal corticosteroids:** betamethasone 12 mg IM, two doses 24 hours apart (typical adult dose for study), for those at risk of delivery between 24 0/7 and 33 6/7 weeks. They accelerate lung maturation and reduce respiratory distress, intraventricular hemorrhage and neonatal death. A single course may also be considered between 34 0/7 and 36 6/7 weeks in some patients not previously treated.
2. **Magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection** when delivery is anticipated before 32 weeks (reduces cerebral palsy).
3. **Tocolysis** for 48 hours only, to allow steroids to act and transfer to a center with a NICU, in patients between about 24 and 34 weeks. Options: **nifedipine** (calcium channel blocker), **indomethacin** (a prostaglandin inhibitor, generally before 32 weeks; it can cause premature closure of the ductus arteriosus and oligohydramnios), and terbutaline (rarely used; maternal tachycardia and hypokalemia, and a boxed warning). Contraindications to tocolysis are chorioamnionitis, abruption, severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, fetal demise, a nonreassuring fetal status and any other reason to deliver. Maintenance tocolysis does not improve outcomes.
4. **GBS prophylaxis** while labor remains possible if the culture result is unknown or positive.

### Prevention

For a singleton pregnancy with a **short cervix** (about 20-25 mm or less at 16-24 weeks) and no prior preterm birth, **vaginal progesterone** reduces preterm birth. **Cerclage** is considered for a prior spontaneous preterm birth before 34 weeks with a cervix shorter than 25 mm before 24 weeks, or for painless cervical dilation. The injectable 17-hydroxyprogesterone caproate was withdrawn from the U.S. market in 2023 after a large trial found it ineffective.

### Premature rupture of membranes

**PROM** is rupture before the onset of labor; **PPROM** is PROM before 37 weeks. Diagnosis: pooling of fluid in the posterior vaginal fornix, a **nitrazine** test that turns blue (amniotic fluid pH is 7.0-7.3 versus vaginal pH of 4.5 or less), a **ferning** pattern on microscopy, and oligohydramnios on ultrasound; immunoassays (for example for placental alpha microglobulin-1) can help. Blood, semen and bacterial vaginosis cause false-positive nitrazine tests. Avoid digital examinations unless labor is imminent, because they raise infection risk.

Management of PPROM: admit, confirm gestational age and fetal well-being, give antenatal steroids and magnesium (before 32 weeks), start **latency antibiotics** (ampicillin plus erythromycin for 7 days; amoxicillin-clavulanate is avoided because of necrotizing enterocolitis) and GBS prophylaxis if labor begins. Expectant management continues to 34 0/7 weeks, when delivery is recommended. At or beyond 37 weeks, PROM is managed by induction of labor. Delivery is indicated earlier for chorioamnionitis, abruption, fetal distress or labor.

**Chorioamnionitis (intraamniotic infection):** maternal fever of 39.0 degrees C or more, or 38.0-38.9 with maternal tachycardia, fetal tachycardia, a white blood cell count above 15,000 (without steroids), purulent cervical drainage or a tender uterus. Treat with ampicillin and gentamicin and **deliver** regardless of gestational age; cesarean is performed only for obstetric indications. **Umbilical cord prolapse** (risk after rupture with a high presenting part or malpresentation) causes sudden variable decelerations or bradycardia. The examiner manually elevates the presenting part, the patient adopts knee-chest or steep Trendelenburg position, and emergency cesarean is performed.

### Distinguishing previa from abruption

Painless bright red bleeding with a soft uterus points to previa (confirm by ultrasound before any digital exam). Painful dark bleeding with a hard, tender uterus and risk factors (hypertension, cocaine, trauma) points to abruption, a clinical diagnosis, with possible DIC.

@example
title: Managing PPROM at 30 weeks
problem: A 26-year-old G2P1 at 30 0/7 weeks has a gush of clear fluid. Speculum examination shows pooling, the nitrazine test is positive, and the fetal tracing is reassuring. She has no contractions, fever or uterine tenderness. What do you do?
step: Pooling, a positive nitrazine test and a clear history confirm PPROM. She is not in labor and has no signs of infection or abruption.
step: Admit and monitor for infection, labor and fetal well-being. Avoid digital cervical exams.
step: Give betamethasone, magnesium sulfate for neuroprotection (below 32 weeks) and latency antibiotics (ampicillin plus erythromycin).
step: Continue expectant management until 34 0/7 weeks unless chorioamnionitis, abruption, labor or fetal compromise develops.

@example
title: Chorioamnionitis during PPROM
problem: A woman at 32 weeks with PPROM develops a temperature of 38.9 degrees C, uterine tenderness, maternal heart rate 112/min and fetal heart rate of 175/min. What is the management?
step: Fever plus maternal and fetal tachycardia and a tender uterus indicate chorioamnionitis.
step: Start ampicillin and gentamicin immediately and give antipyretics.
step: Deliver, regardless of gestational age, by induction or augmentation; do not wait for steroids to complete. Cesarean is reserved for obstetric indications.

@mistakes
- Giving tocolytics to a patient with chorioamnionitis, abruption or severe preeclampsia.
- Using long-term maintenance tocolysis or bed rest, neither of which improves outcomes.
- Performing digital vaginal exams on a patient with PPROM before labor begins.
- Choosing cesarean automatically for chorioamnionitis rather than delivering by the route that is obstetrically appropriate.
