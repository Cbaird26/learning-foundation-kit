@article
The puerperium is the 6 weeks after delivery during which the reproductive tract returns toward its nonpregnant state. Most mortality in this period results from hemorrhage, hypertensive disease, infection, thromboembolism and cardiomyopathy, so the postpartum period is an active period of surveillance, not a time to relax.

### Normal puerperium

- **Uterine involution:** the fundus is at about the umbilicus just after delivery and descends about 1 cm per day, becoming nonpalpable by about 2 weeks.
- **Lochia:** lochia rubra (red, days 1-3), lochia serosa (pink-brown, about days 4-10) and lochia alba (yellow-white, up to 4-6 weeks). Foul-smelling lochia suggests infection.
- **Lactation:** falling estrogen and progesterone after delivery of the placenta release prolactin's action, producing milk; suckling releases oxytocin (let-down). Breastfeeding has traditionally been contraindicated with HIV infection in the United States (current guidance is moving toward shared decision-making when viral suppression is sustained), infant galactosemia, active untreated tuberculosis, HTLV-1 infection and a few drugs (for example chemotherapy). Most other drugs are compatible.
- **Care schedule:** contact within 3 weeks and a comprehensive visit by 12 weeks (ACOG), with blood pressure checks within 72 hours for severe hypertension and within 7-10 days for other hypertensive disorders, depression screening, contraception, and vaccines (Tdap if not given, MMR or varicella if nonimmune, Rh immune globulin within 72 hours if Rh-negative and the infant is Rh-positive).

### Postpartum hemorrhage

**Postpartum hemorrhage (PPH)** is cumulative blood loss of 1,000 mL or more, or blood loss with signs of hypovolemia, within 24 hours of birth, regardless of delivery route. (The older definition was over 500 mL for vaginal and over 1,000 mL for cesarean delivery.) Blood loss is underestimated visually, so use **quantitative blood loss** (weighing materials; 1 g is about 1 mL). The **shock index** (heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure) above about 0.9-1.0 is an early warning.

Remember the four Ts:
- **Tone** (uterine atony, about 80%): risk factors are a prolonged or augmented labor, macrosomia, polyhydramnios, multiple gestation, chorioamnionitis and magnesium use.
- **Trauma:** lacerations, uterine rupture, inversion, hematoma. Clue: bleeding with a firm uterus.
- **Tissue:** retained placenta or products, placenta accreta.
- **Thrombin:** coagulopathy such as DIC (from abruption, sepsis, amniotic fluid embolism) or von Willebrand disease.

**Stepwise management** (typical adult doses for study, check current references):
1. Call for help and start the hemorrhage protocol; establish two large-bore IV lines; send CBC, type and cross-match, fibrinogen and coagulation studies; empty the bladder.
2. **Uterine massage** and bimanual compression; give **oxytocin** (10-40 units in 500-1,000 mL crystalloid IV, or 10 units IM).
3. If bleeding continues: **methylergonovine** 0.2 mg IM (contraindicated with hypertension or preeclampsia), **carboprost** 250 micrograms IM every 15 minutes up to 8 doses (contraindicated with asthma), or **misoprostol** 800-1,000 micrograms rectally or sublingually.
4. Give **tranexamic acid** 1 g IV over 10 minutes, ideally within 3 hours of birth.
5. Inspect the cervix, vagina and perineum for lacerations; explore the uterus for retained tissue.
6. If atony persists: intrauterine **balloon tamponade**, then uterine artery embolization or surgery (compression sutures such as the B-Lynch, vessel ligation, hysterectomy).
7. Transfuse early with a massive transfusion protocol (balanced red cells, plasma and platelets) and replace fibrinogen.

**Uterine inversion** presents with shock out of proportion to blood loss, a mass in the vagina and a fundus that cannot be palpated; stop uterotonics, replace the uterus immediately (Johnson maneuver), use a relaxing agent such as nitroglycerin if needed, and then give oxytocin. A **retained placenta** (not delivered within 30 minutes) requires manual removal; suspect accreta if the plane cannot be found.

### Infection and mood

**Endometritis** presents 2-3 days after delivery with fever, uterine tenderness and foul lochia; cesarean delivery is the major risk factor. It is polymicrobial and treated with IV clindamycin plus gentamicin until the patient is afebrile for 24-48 hours; fever that persists despite antibiotics suggests an abscess or septic pelvic thrombophlebitis. **Mastitis** (usually Staphylococcus aureus) causes a tender, erythematous breast with fever; continue breastfeeding and give dicloxacillin or cephalexin (MRSA coverage if risk), and drain an abscess.

**Postpartum blues** affect up to 80% of women, begin about days 2-5 and resolve within 2 weeks with support only. **Postpartum depression** lasts longer than 2 weeks with depressed mood, anhedonia and impaired functioning; screen with the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale; treat with psychotherapy and an SSRI such as sertraline. **Postpartum psychosis** is a psychiatric emergency (delusions, hallucinations, risk of infanticide or suicide), usually within the first 2 weeks, and strongly associated with bipolar disorder; hospitalization and antipsychotic or mood-stabilizing therapy are required.

@example
title: Postpartum hemorrhage from atony
problem: A 30-year-old G3P2 had a 26-hour augmented labor and delivered a 4,300 g infant. Thirty minutes later she has heavy bleeding, a boggy fundus, heart rate 118/min and blood pressure 94/58 mmHg. She has no asthma or hypertension. What are the next steps?
step: Prolonged augmented labor and macrosomia point to tone. Call for help, place two large-bore IV lines, send labs and empty the bladder.
step: Perform fundal massage and bimanual compression and give oxytocin.
step: If bleeding continues, add methylergonovine or carboprost (neither contraindicated here) or misoprostol, and give tranexamic acid IV.
step: Check for lacerations and retained tissue; if atony persists, use balloon tamponade, then surgery or embolization, and transfuse as needed.

@example
title: Fever after cesarean delivery
problem: A 28-year-old on postoperative day 2 after cesarean delivery for failure to progress after prolonged rupture of membranes has a fever of 38.8 degrees C, a tender uterus and foul-smelling lochia. The wound and lungs are normal. What is the diagnosis and management?
step: Fever with uterine tenderness and foul lochia after cesarean delivery and prolonged labor is postpartum endometritis.
step: It is polymicrobial, so give IV clindamycin plus gentamicin.
step: Continue until she is afebrile for 24-48 hours; no oral antibiotic course is needed afterward.
step: If fever persists beyond 48-72 hours, image for abscess or consider septic pelvic thrombophlebitis.

@mistakes
- Relying on visual estimates of blood loss instead of quantitative measurement.
- Giving methylergonovine to a patient with hypertension or preeclampsia, or carboprost to a patient with asthma.
- Missing lacerations or retained tissue when bleeding continues despite a firm uterus.
- Stopping breastfeeding for mastitis; emptying the breast helps recovery.
