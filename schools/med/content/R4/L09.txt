@article
Contraceptive counseling begins with the patient's goals, then matches method to medical history. The first principle is that **effectiveness depends on whether the method relies on the user**. Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC) has near-identical typical and perfect-use failure rates, whereas pills, patches, rings and condoms perform worse in typical use than in perfect use.

### Effectiveness (typical-use first-year pregnancy rate, approximate)

| Method | Typical-use failure |
|---|---|
| Implant, levonorgestrel IUD, copper IUD | under 1% |
| Depot medroxyprogesterone (DMPA) | about 4% |
| Pill, patch, ring | about 7% |
| Male condom | about 13% |
| Withdrawal | about 20% |
| Spermicide | about 21% |
| No method | about 85% |

### Methods

- **Combined hormonal contraceptives** (pill, patch, ring): estrogen suppresses FSH and the progestin suppresses the LH surge, thickens cervical mucus and thins the endometrium. Non-contraceptive benefits: regular, lighter menses, less dysmenorrhea and acne, and lower risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer and of ectopic pregnancy. Risks: venous thromboembolism, and stroke and myocardial infarction in smokers. Enzyme inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, topiramate, St John's wort) lower efficacy.
- **Progestin-only methods:** pill (norethindrone, drospirenone), **etonogestrel implant** (3 years), **DMPA** 150 mg IM every 13 weeks (bone density loss, delayed return of fertility, weight gain, unpredictable bleeding then amenorrhea), and the **levonorgestrel IUD** (labeled for 3-8 years depending on the device; reduces menstrual bleeding and treats heavy menstrual bleeding).
- **Copper IUD:** effective for at least 10 years, non-hormonal, but may increase menstrual bleeding and cramping. Risks of any IUD: expulsion, perforation (about 1 per 1,000) and a small short-term infection risk.
- **Barrier methods:** condoms are the only method that also protects against most sexually transmitted infections.
- **Lactational amenorrhea method:** effective (about 98%) only if the infant is under 6 months, the mother is amenorrheic and breastfeeds exclusively.

### Medical eligibility (CDC categories 1-4)

Category 4 is an unacceptable health risk. **Estrogen-containing methods are category 4** for: less than 3 weeks postpartum, smoking at age 35 or older (15 or more cigarettes a day), systolic pressure of 160 or higher or diastolic of 100 or higher, acute venous thromboembolism or a past one with a higher risk of recurrence (category 3 if the risk is lower), a thrombogenic mutation, ischemic heart disease or stroke, **migraine with aura**, current breast cancer, severe cirrhosis or liver tumor, and **SLE with positive or unknown antiphospholipid antibodies**. Multiple cardiovascular risk factors, and diabetes with nephropathy, retinopathy, neuropathy or other vascular disease, are category 3 or 4 depending on severity (estrogen is avoided). Controlled or mild hypertension (140-159/90-99) is category 3. In such patients, a **progestin-only method or an IUD** is typically chosen; the copper IUD has no hormonal contraindications at all. Obesity, depression and well-controlled epilepsy do not exclude combined methods (but enzyme-inducing antiseizure drugs reduce efficacy).

### Emergency contraception

The **copper IUD** inserted within 5 days is the most effective (over 99%) and also provides ongoing contraception. **Ulipristal** 30 mg (a selective progesterone receptor modulator) works up to 5 days and is more effective than levonorgestrel in the later days. **Levonorgestrel** 1.5 mg is most effective within 72 hours, and its efficacy falls in people with a BMI of 30 or more. These delay ovulation and do not interrupt an established pregnancy.

### Sterilization and abortion

Tubal sterilization (bilateral salpingectomy is increasingly performed because it also lowers ovarian cancer risk) and **vasectomy** (safer and simpler; confirm azoospermia with a semen analysis after about 3 months) are permanent. **Medication abortion** up to 70 days (10 weeks) uses **mifepristone** 200 mg orally (a progesterone receptor antagonist) followed in 24-48 hours by **misoprostol** 800 micrograms buccally; surgical options are suction aspiration and, later, dilation and evacuation. Counseling is nondirective, ectopic pregnancy must be excluded when appropriate, and Rh status is checked.

### Calculation: the Pearl Index

**Pearl Index** = (number of pregnancies / total woman-years of use) x 100. A woman-year is 12 months of exposure. It estimates pregnancies per 100 women per year.

@example
title: Choosing a method in a patient with hypertension
problem: A 29-year-old with chronic hypertension (blood pressure 148/96 mmHg on treatment) requests highly effective contraception and is not interested in becoming pregnant for 5 years. She does not smoke. Which method is best?
step: Check eligibility: controlled hypertension of 140-159/90-99 is category 3 for estrogen-containing methods (risk usually outweighs benefit), so avoid the pill, patch and ring.
step: She wants a highly effective, long-term option, so recommend LARC: a levonorgestrel or copper IUD or the implant.
step: Each is category 1 or 2 for hypertension; counsel about bleeding patterns (lighter with levonorgestrel, heavier with copper, unpredictable with the implant).
step: Counsel that condoms are still needed for protection from sexually transmitted infections if she is at risk.

@mistakes
- Prescribing an estrogen-containing method to a patient with migraine with aura or a smoker aged 35 or older.
- Using levonorgestrel emergency contraception as the most effective option, when a copper IUD is more effective, particularly in patients with obesity.
- Assuming DMPA does not carry bone density risk or that fertility returns promptly after stopping it.
- Forgetting that only condoms protect against sexually transmitted infections.
