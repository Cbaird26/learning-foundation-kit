@article
Pregnancy changes maternal physiology so much that many chronic conditions behave differently and many drugs become unsafe. Diabetes is the most testable example.

### Physiology of glucose in pregnancy

Fasting glucose falls because the fetus continuously consumes glucose, but after meals glucose rises because of **insulin resistance** caused by human placental lactogen (hPL), progesterone, cortisol and prolactin. Resistance peaks in the third trimester, so a patient who cannot raise insulin secretion enough develops **gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM)**. The placenta delivers, and hPL disappears, so insulin requirements drop sharply after delivery.

### Pregestational diabetes

Poor glycemic control during organogenesis causes major congenital anomalies (neural tube defects, congenital heart disease, and **caudal regression syndrome**, which is characteristic). Later complications include macrosomia with shoulder dystocia, polyhydramnios, preeclampsia, stillbirth and neonatal hypoglycemia, respiratory distress and polycythemia (fetal hyperinsulinism). Counsel before conception: hemoglobin A1c as close to normal as is safe (ADA suggests below 6.0-6.5%), folic acid, stopping teratogens (ACE inhibitors, statins) and screening for retinopathy and nephropathy. Insulin is the standard drug. Insulin needs usually rise through pregnancy and fall abruptly after delivery.

### Gestational diabetes

Screen all patients at 24-28 weeks, and earlier if obese or otherwise at high risk.
- **Two-step approach:** a nonfasting 50 g glucose challenge; if the 1-hour glucose reaches the chosen threshold (130, 135 or 140 mg/dL), do a fasting 100 g, 3-hour test. GDM is diagnosed when two or more values meet or exceed the Carpenter-Coustan thresholds: fasting 95, 1 hour 180, 2 hours 155 and 3 hours 140 mg/dL.
- **One-step approach:** a fasting 75 g, 2-hour test; GDM if any one value is at or above fasting 92, 1 hour 180 or 2 hours 153 mg/dL.

**Management:** nutrition therapy, exercise and self-monitoring of glucose. Targets: fasting below 95 mg/dL, 1-hour postprandial below 140 or 2-hour postprandial below 120 mg/dL. Add **insulin** when targets are missed (metformin is an acceptable alternative though it crosses the placenta; glyburide is less favored). Class A1 (diet-controlled) GDM is delivered at 39 0/7 to 40 6/7 weeks; class A2 (medication-controlled) and uncomplicated pregestational diabetes at 39 0/7 to 39 6/7 weeks. Offer cesarean if estimated fetal weight is 4,500 g or more in diabetes (5,000 g or more without diabetes). Postpartum, screen with a 75 g OGTT at 4-12 weeks, then every 1-3 years because of the high risk of type 2 diabetes.

### Other medical problems

- **Hypothyroidism:** TSH rises in pregnancy; increase the levothyroxine dose by about 25-30% as soon as pregnancy is confirmed and recheck TSH every 4 weeks in the first half.
- **Hyperthyroidism (Graves):** propylthiouracil in the first trimester (methimazole is associated with aplasia cutis and choanal atresia), then often switch to methimazole because of PTU hepatotoxicity. Radioactive iodine is contraindicated. Maternal TRAb can cross the placenta and cause fetal or neonatal thyrotoxicosis.
- **Anemia:** defined as hemoglobin below 11 g/dL in the first and third trimesters and below 10.5 in the second. Iron deficiency is the commonest cause and is treated with oral iron.
- **Venous thromboembolism:** pregnancy is hypercoagulable. Treat with low-molecular-weight heparin, which does not cross the placenta. **Warfarin is teratogenic** (embryopathy in weeks 6-12, central nervous system defects later) and is avoided.
- **Bacteriuria:** screen at the first visit with a urine culture and treat asymptomatic bacteriuria to prevent pyelonephritis. Pyelonephritis is treated with admission and IV antibiotics such as ceftriaxone.
- **Intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy:** third-trimester pruritus of the palms and soles without rash and total bile acids of 10 micromol/L or more. Ursodeoxycholic acid is used and delivery is timed at 36-39 weeks by bile acid level, because of the risk of stillbirth.
- **Lupus and antiphospholipid syndrome:** anti-Ro/SSA antibodies cause neonatal lupus and congenital heart block; hydroxychloroquine is continued; antiphospholipid syndrome with recurrent loss is treated with low-dose aspirin and heparin.
- **Antiseizure drugs:** valproate carries the highest risk of neural tube defects and should be avoided where possible; give folic acid.

@example
title: Diagnosing gestational diabetes with the two-step approach
problem: A 31-year-old at 26 weeks has a 1-hour, 50 g nonfasting glucose of 152 mg/dL. A fasting 100 g, 3-hour test gives fasting 98, 1 hour 172, 2 hours 160 and 3 hours 128 mg/dL. Does she have GDM?
step: The screening value of 152 mg/dL exceeds the 130-140 mg/dL threshold, so a diagnostic test is needed.
step: Compare each value with the Carpenter-Coustan thresholds: fasting 98 is at least 95 (abnormal), 1 hour 172 is below 180 (normal), 2 hours 160 is at least 155 (abnormal), 3 hours 128 is below 140 (normal).
step: Two values are abnormal, which is the criterion, so she has GDM.
step: Start nutrition therapy and glucose monitoring, and add insulin if fasting and postprandial targets are not met.

@mistakes
- Using the abnormality of a single value on the 3-hour test to diagnose GDM (two are required in the two-step method).
- Continuing the antepartum insulin dose after delivery; requirements fall abruptly when the placenta is delivered.
- Prescribing warfarin for a pregnant patient with deep vein thrombosis.
- Forgetting to treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in pregnancy.
