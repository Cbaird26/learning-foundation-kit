@article
Hypertensive disorders complicate roughly 5-10% of pregnancies and remain a leading cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity. Start with the definitions, because management follows from the category.

### Classification

- **Chronic hypertension:** blood pressure of 140/90 mmHg or higher before pregnancy or before 20 weeks.
- **Gestational hypertension:** new blood pressure of 140/90 or higher on two occasions at least 4 hours apart after 20 weeks, with no proteinuria and no severe features. Blood pressure normalizes within 12 weeks postpartum.
- **Preeclampsia:** new hypertension after 20 weeks **plus** proteinuria (at least 300 mg/24 h, a protein:creatinine ratio of 0.3 or more, or dipstick 2+ if quantification is unavailable) **or**, without proteinuria, any severe feature.
- **Preeclampsia with severe features:** any of the following: blood pressure of at least 160 systolic or 110 diastolic; platelets under 100,000/microliter; liver transaminases at least twice the upper limit of normal; serum creatinine above 1.1 mg/dL or doubled; pulmonary edema; new-onset headache unresponsive to medication; visual disturbances; or severe persistent right upper quadrant or epigastric pain. The amount of proteinuria is no longer used to define severity.
- **Superimposed preeclampsia:** chronic hypertension with new proteinuria or severe features after 20 weeks.
- **Eclampsia:** new-onset generalized tonic-clonic seizure in a patient with preeclampsia, not explained by another cause.
- **HELLP syndrome:** Hemolysis, Elevated Liver enzymes, Low Platelets; a severe variant that can occur with little hypertension.

### Pathophysiology

Normally, trophoblast invades the maternal spiral arteries and converts them into low-resistance, high-capacitance vessels. In preeclampsia, this remodeling is shallow, producing placental underperfusion. The stressed placenta releases anti-angiogenic factors (soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1, which binds VEGF and placental growth factor) into the maternal circulation, causing diffuse **endothelial dysfunction**: vasoconstriction, increased permeability, glomerular endotheliosis (proteinuria), hepatic injury and microangiopathic hemolysis. The only cure is delivery of the placenta.

**Risk factors:** nulliparity, prior preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, diabetes, renal disease, autoimmune disease, multifetal gestation, obesity, age 35 or older, and conditions with a large placenta such as a hydatidiform mole. **Low-dose aspirin 81 mg daily** started at 12-28 weeks (ideally before 16) is recommended for those with at least one high-risk factor (for example prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, diabetes, renal or autoimmune disease) or two or more moderate-risk factors.

### Management

**Delivery timing**
- Gestational hypertension or preeclampsia without severe features: delivery at 37 0/7 weeks (expectant management with surveillance before that).
- Preeclampsia with severe features: deliver after maternal stabilization at 34 0/7 weeks or later. Before 34 weeks, expectant management in a tertiary center is possible if mother and fetus are stable, with antenatal corticosteroids. Delivery is required at any gestational age for uncontrolled severe hypertension, eclampsia, pulmonary edema, DIC, abruption, HELLP or a nonreassuring fetal status.
- Chronic hypertension: treat to a goal below 140/90; delivery at 38-39 weeks if uncomplicated.

**Magnesium sulfate** is the drug of choice to **prevent and treat eclamptic seizures**. A typical adult regimen for study is a 4-6 g IV loading dose over 20-30 minutes followed by 1-2 g/h, continued for 24 hours after delivery. Magnesium is cleared by the kidney, so reduce the dose and check levels in renal impairment. Monitor deep tendon reflexes, respiratory rate and urine output. Toxicity progresses from loss of patellar reflexes (about 8-10 mEq/L) to respiratory depression and then cardiac arrest; the antidote is **calcium gluconate** (typically 1 g IV).

**Severe-range blood pressure** (160/110 or more, persisting 15 minutes) is treated urgently to prevent stroke: IV labetalol (20 mg, then 40 mg, then 80 mg), IV hydralazine (5-10 mg), or immediate-release oral nifedipine (10-20 mg). These are typical adult doses for study, to be checked against current references. For long-term therapy, labetalol, nifedipine and methyldopa are standard; **ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and direct renin inhibitors are contraindicated** (fetal renal injury and oligohydramnios).

Seizure risk persists postpartum. Blood pressure often peaks 3-6 days after delivery, and postpartum preeclampsia or eclampsia can appear up to 6 weeks after birth, so sustained postpartum hypertension warrants evaluation and treatment.

@example
title: Preeclampsia without severe features at term
problem: A 33-year-old G1 at 37 0/7 weeks has blood pressures of 152/96 and 148/94 four hours apart. Her protein:creatinine ratio is 0.5. Platelets, creatinine and liver enzymes are normal, and she has no headache or visual symptoms. Her blood pressure was 112/70 at 12 weeks. What is the diagnosis and plan?
step: Blood pressure is normal at 12 weeks, so this is new hypertension after 20 weeks, and two readings 4 hours apart exceed 140/90.
step: A protein:creatinine ratio of 0.5 is at least 0.3, which defines proteinuria, so this is preeclampsia.
step: No reading reaches 160/110 and laboratory values and symptoms are normal, so there are no severe features.
step: At 37 0/7 weeks preeclampsia without severe features is delivered, so offer induction of labor. Magnesium is often given intrapartum and postpartum for seizure prophylaxis, and blood pressure is monitored after delivery.

@example
title: Magnesium toxicity
problem: A patient with severe preeclampsia receiving magnesium at 2 g/h after cesarean has a respiratory rate of 8/min, absent patellar reflexes and a urine output of 15 mL/h. What do you do?
step: Absent reflexes and respiratory depression indicate magnesium toxicity, and oliguria explains it: magnesium is renally cleared and is accumulating.
step: Stop the infusion immediately.
step: Give calcium gluconate IV as the antidote, support the airway and breathing, and check a serum magnesium level and creatinine.
step: Restart at a reduced rate only when reflexes return and urine output has recovered.

@mistakes
- Using the degree of proteinuria to classify severity; severe features are defined by blood pressure, laboratory values and symptoms.
- Withholding delivery because the blood pressure was controlled with medication, despite HELLP, pulmonary edema or other indications for delivery.
- Prescribing lisinopril or losartan for chronic hypertension in a pregnant patient.
- Stopping monitoring at delivery; eclampsia and severe hypertension can occur postpartum.
