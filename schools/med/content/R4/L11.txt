@article
Gynecologic oncology questions are mostly about three skills: **screening intervals, matching a presentation to a cancer, and matching a tumor to its marker**.

### Cervical cancer: etiology and prevention

Persistent infection with high-risk **human papillomavirus** (HPV types 16 and 18 cause about 70% of cervical cancers) drives dysplasia (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, CIN 1-3) and then squamous cell carcinoma; adenocarcinoma arises from endocervical glands. Risk factors are early intercourse, multiple partners, smoking, HIV and immunosuppression. HPV oncoproteins E6 and E7 inactivate p53 and Rb. **Vaccination** with the 9-valent vaccine is routine at ages 11-12 (can start at 9); two doses 6-12 months apart if started before age 15, three doses if started at 15 or later or if immunocompromised. Catch-up vaccination is recommended through age 26, and age 27-45 is by shared decision-making.

### Screening intervals (USPSTF 2018; other bodies differ slightly)

| Age | Screening |
|---|---|
| Under 21 | none |
| 21-29 | cytology every 3 years (HPV testing alone is not used in this group by USPSTF) |
| 30-65 | cytology every 3 years, or high-risk HPV testing alone every 5 years, or cotesting (cytology plus HPV) every 5 years |
| Over 65 | stop if adequate negative prior screening (3 consecutive negative cytology results or 2 consecutive negative cotests within 10 years, the most recent within 5 years) and no CIN 2 or worse in the last 20 years |
| After hysterectomy with cervix removed for benign disease | none, if no history of CIN 2 or worse |

The American Cancer Society (2020) prefers primary HPV testing every 5 years starting at 25. Screening continues for 25 years after treatment of CIN 2 or worse. Patients with HIV are screened more often (annually for 3 years, then every 3 years if age under 30; follow HIV guidelines thereafter). Screening is performed regardless of HPV vaccination status.

### Abnormal results (ASCCP risk-based management, simplified)

- **ASC-US:** reflex HPV testing; HPV-positive goes to **colposcopy**; HPV-negative returns to routine screening (follow-up in about 3 years). Ages 21-24 repeat cytology in 12 months.
- **LSIL:** colposcopy (age 21-24: repeat cytology in 12 months).
- **HSIL:** colposcopy, with excisional treatment (LEEP) permitted without biopsy confirmation in some adults.
- **HPV 16 or 18 positive** with normal cytology: colposcopy. **Other high-risk HPV** with normal cytology: repeat testing in 12 months.
- **Atypical glandular cells:** colposcopy with endocervical sampling, plus endometrial biopsy at age 35 or older (or with risk factors).
- **CIN 2-3:** excision (loop electrosurgical excision or cold-knife cone). CIN 2 in a patient under 25 may be observed.

Invasive cervical cancer presents with postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding and discharge; small lesions are treated with conization or hysterectomy and locally advanced disease with chemoradiation (cisplatin).

### Endometrial cancer

The most common gynecologic cancer in the United States. **Type I** (endometrioid) is estrogen-driven: obesity, chronic anovulation (PCOS), nulliparity, late menopause, diabetes, unopposed estrogen, tamoxifen and Lynch syndrome. It arises from complex hyperplasia with atypia. **Type II** (serous, clear cell) is unrelated to estrogen, appears in thin older women, and has a worse prognosis (TP53 mutation). Protective factors: combined oral contraceptives, levonorgestrel IUD and parity.

**Postmenopausal bleeding** is cancer until proven otherwise: evaluate with transvaginal ultrasound (an endometrial thickness of 4 mm or less is reassuring) or endometrial biopsy; biopsy if the lining is thicker or bleeding persists. Treatment is hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and staging; adjuvant therapy follows stage and grade. Hyperplasia without atypia is treated with progestins; atypical hyperplasia usually with hysterectomy because of a coexisting cancer risk.

### Ovarian cancer

About 90% are epithelial (serous the commonest). Risk: age, BRCA1/BRCA2, Lynch syndrome, nulliparity, endometriosis; protective: oral contraceptives, pregnancy, breastfeeding, tubal ligation or salpingectomy. Symptoms are vague (bloating, early satiety, pelvic pain) so most present at stage III; there is **no effective screening** for average-risk women. CA-125 is a marker of epithelial tumors for monitoring, but it is elevated in endometriosis, menstruation and other conditions. Treatment is cytoreductive surgery plus carboplatin and paclitaxel. BRCA carriers should be offered risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy (around 35-40 for BRCA1, 40-45 for BRCA2).

| Tumor | Marker or feature |
|---|---|
| Dysgerminoma | LDH; sheets of cells, radiosensitive |
| Yolk sac (endodermal sinus) | alpha-fetoprotein; Schiller-Duval bodies |
| Choriocarcinoma | hCG |
| Mature cystic teratoma | benign; torsion risk; struma ovarii may cause hyperthyroidism |
| Granulosa cell | inhibin, estrogen; Call-Exner bodies; endometrial hyperplasia |
| Sertoli-Leydig | androgens, virilization |
| Fibroma | Meigs syndrome (ascites and pleural effusion) |
| Krukenberg | metastatic signet-ring (usually gastric) tumor |

The **Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI)** = U x M x CA-125 (U/mL), where U is the ultrasound score (0 for no features, 1 for one feature, 3 for two or more of multilocular cyst, solid areas, bilateral lesions, ascites, metastases) and M is 1 if premenopausal or 3 if postmenopausal. An RMI above 200 is high risk and warrants referral to a gynecologic oncologist.

### Vulvar disease

Lichen sclerosus (white, atrophic, pruritic, with a slightly higher cancer risk) and HPV-related vulvar intraepithelial neoplasia precede most vulvar squamous cancers; biopsy any persistent ulcer or white lesion.

@example
title: Managing an abnormal Pap
problem: A 34-year-old woman has a Pap showing ASC-US. Reflex HPV testing is positive. What is next?
step: ASC-US with a positive HPV test carries a higher risk of CIN 2 or worse.
step: Refer for colposcopy with biopsy of any abnormal area.
step: If biopsy shows CIN 2-3, treat by excision; if normal or CIN 1, follow with repeat testing at the interval the risk-based guidelines specify.

@example
title: Postmenopausal bleeding
problem: A 62-year-old obese woman has had vaginal bleeding for 2 weeks. Transvaginal ultrasound shows an endometrial thickness of 11 mm. What is the next step?
step: Postmenopausal bleeding requires exclusion of endometrial cancer, and she has risk factors (obesity).
step: An endometrium above 4 mm is abnormal, so perform an endometrial biopsy.
step: If cancer is confirmed, the treatment is hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and staging.

@mistakes
- Screening women under 21, or screening annually, or ignoring HPV status after a normal cytology result at age 30 or older.
- Using HPV-only screening as USPSTF-endorsed in the 21-29 group.
- Attributing postmenopausal bleeding to atrophy without an endometrial evaluation.
- Treating CA-125 as a screening test for ovarian cancer.
