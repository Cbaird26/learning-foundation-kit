@article
**Infertility** is failure to conceive after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse (6 months if the woman is 35 or older; evaluate promptly at 40 or with a known risk factor such as amenorrhea or a known tubal or male factor). Causes are shared: about one-third female, one-third male and one-third combined or unexplained. The strongest single determinant of female fertility is **age**, because oocyte number and quality fall.

### Basic evaluation

**Male:** a **semen analysis** after 2-7 days of abstinence. Lower reference limits (WHO 2021) are approximately a volume of 1.4 mL, a concentration of 16 million per mL, total motility of 42% and 4% normal forms. Causes of abnormal results include varicocele, hypogonadism, Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY, small firm testes, high FSH and LH, low testosterone), medications and heat, and **obstructive azoospermia** (congenital absence of the vas deferens in cystic fibrosis carriers; normal FSH).

**Female:**
- **Ovulation:** regular cycles suggest ovulation; confirm with a mid-luteal progesterone above 3 ng/mL. Check TSH and prolactin, and evaluate for PCOS.
- **Ovarian reserve:** antral follicle count, AMH or cycle-day-3 FSH in women of advanced age.
- **Tubes and uterus:** **hysterosalpingography (HSG)** with contrast, performed in the follicular phase after menses (before ovulation), shows tubal patency and the uterine cavity; saline-infusion sonography evaluates polyps and fibroids; laparoscopy if endometriosis or adhesions are suspected.

### Treatment

- **Ovulatory dysfunction (PCOS):** lifestyle and weight loss; **letrozole** (an aromatase inhibitor; first-line) or clomiphene (a selective estrogen receptor modulator that blocks hypothalamic feedback, raises FSH, and can cause hot flashes, visual symptoms and multiple gestation); metformin as adjunct; gonadotropins with monitoring.
- **Mild male factor or unexplained infertility:** ovarian stimulation with intrauterine insemination (IUI), then IVF.
- **Tubal disease, severe male factor, advanced age, or failure of other therapy:** **in vitro fertilization (IVF)**: ovarian stimulation, hCG trigger, oocyte retrieval, fertilization (by **intracytoplasmic sperm injection**, ICSI, for severe male factor), and embryo transfer. Hydrosalpinx is removed or occluded before IVF.
- **Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS)** follows gonadotropin stimulation and an hCG trigger: VEGF-mediated vascular leak causes ovarian enlargement, ascites, pleural effusion, hemoconcentration and thrombosis. Treatment is supportive (fluids, paracentesis, thromboprophylaxis).

### Menopause

**Menopause** is 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea; the average age is 51. Follicle depletion lowers inhibin B (so FSH rises first), then estradiol. After menopause estrone from peripheral conversion of androstenedione predominates. **Perimenopause** has irregular cycles and fluctuating hormones. Diagnosis in women over 45 is clinical; check hCG, TSH and prolactin if there is amenorrhea at a younger age.

**Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)** is loss of ovarian function before age 40, with two FSH levels in the menopausal range at least 4 weeks apart; evaluate with a karyotype (Turner mosaicism), fragile X premutation testing and autoimmune screening. Hormone therapy is given until the average age of natural menopause (about 51).

Symptoms: **vasomotor symptoms** (hot flashes, night sweats), sleep disruption, mood change, **genitourinary syndrome of menopause** (vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency and infections) and accelerated bone loss (screen with DXA at 65, earlier with risk factors).

### Treatment of menopausal symptoms

**Systemic hormone therapy** is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms. Women with a uterus need **estrogen plus a progestogen** (progestogen prevents endometrial hyperplasia and cancer); women without a uterus take estrogen alone. The Women's Health Initiative showed that combined therapy increased breast cancer, stroke, venous thromboembolism and coronary events, but absolute risks are low and the balance is favorable for symptomatic women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause, using the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time. **Contraindications:** breast cancer, coronary disease, stroke, prior venous thromboembolism, active liver disease and undiagnosed vaginal bleeding. **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** treats genitourinary symptoms with minimal systemic absorption. **Nonhormonal options:** SSRIs and SNRIs (paroxetine, venlafaxine), gabapentin and fezolinetant (a neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist; monitor liver tests).

@example
title: Evaluating a couple who cannot conceive
problem: A 33-year-old woman and her 35-year-old partner have not conceived after 14 months of unprotected intercourse. She has regular 29-day cycles, and she has no pain. What initial workup do you order?
step: She meets the definition (12 months, age under 35). Evaluate both partners.
step: Order a semen analysis for the partner.
step: Confirm ovulation with a mid-luteal progesterone, and check TSH and prolactin.
step: Perform an HSG in the follicular phase to check tubal patency and the uterine cavity.
step: Treat the cause found; if no cause is found (unexplained infertility), stimulate ovulation with IUI, then IVF.

@example
title: Hot flashes in a woman with a uterus
problem: A 52-year-old woman 1 year after her last menstrual period has severe hot flashes that disturb her sleep. She has no history of cancer, thrombosis or heart disease. She has an intact uterus. What is the best treatment?
step: Severe vasomotor symptoms in a woman under 60 and within 10 years of menopause are best treated with systemic hormone therapy.
step: She has an intact uterus, so give estrogen plus a progestogen to prevent endometrial hyperplasia.
step: Counsel on risks (breast cancer, stroke, thromboembolism), use the lowest effective dose, and reassess regularly.
step: If hormones are contraindicated or declined, offer an SSRI or SNRI, gabapentin or fezolinetant.

@mistakes
- Prescribing estrogen alone to a menopausal woman who still has a uterus.
- Waiting 12 months before evaluating a woman over 35 who has been trying to conceive.
- Ordering an HSG during pregnancy, with active pelvic infection, or after ovulation.
- Using vaginal estrogen for hot flashes; it is effective for genitourinary symptoms only.
