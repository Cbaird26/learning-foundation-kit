@article
Children dehydrate quickly because they have higher body-water fractions, higher insensible losses per kilogram and less ability to concentrate urine. Most pediatric dehydration comes from **acute gastroenteritis**, usually viral (rotavirus has declined with vaccination, and norovirus is now more common).

### Estimating dehydration

Percent dehydration is the fluid deficit as a percentage of body weight. The best measure is **weight change**:
$$\text{dehydration}\% = \frac{\text{pre-illness weight} - \text{current weight}}{\text{pre-illness weight}} \times 100.$$
Usually the pre-illness weight is unknown, so estimate from signs:

| Degree | Infant (percent) | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 3–5% | thirsty, slightly dry mucosa, normal vitals and perfusion |
| Moderate | 6–9% | dry mucosa, sunken fontanelle and eyes, decreased tears and urine, delayed capillary refill (more than 2 seconds), tachycardia, irritability |
| Severe | 10% or more | lethargy, very sunken eyes, cool mottled extremities, capillary refill more than 3 seconds, tachycardia, hypotension (a late sign) |

Capillary refill, abnormal skin turgor and abnormal respiratory pattern (deep acidotic breathing) are the most reliable single signs. Hypotension is a late sign of shock in children.

### Treatment

**Oral rehydration solution (ORS)** is first line for mild-to-moderate dehydration: about **50 mL/kg over 4 hours for mild**, **100 mL/kg over 4 hours for moderate**, given in small frequent volumes (a teaspoon every 1–2 minutes, by syringe or spoon if the child vomits), plus replacement of ongoing losses (about 10 mL/kg per loose stool). Early refeeding with a regular diet follows rehydration. **Ondansetron** (a single dose orally; a typical study dose is 0.15 mg/kg, or about 2 mg for 8–15 kg, 4 mg for 15–30 kg and 8 mg above 30 kg; study values, to be verified against current references before any clinical use) may reduce vomiting and the need for IV fluids. Antidiarrheals are not recommended, and antibiotics are not routine for watery diarrhea (they are considered for specific pathogens, severe illness or special hosts). Probiotics have limited, inconsistent evidence.

**Intravenous fluids** are for severe dehydration, shock or failure of oral therapy. Give an **isotonic bolus of 20 mL/kg** (normal saline or lactated Ringer's), reassess, and repeat until perfusion improves.

### Fluid calculations

**Maintenance** by the Holliday–Segar (4-2-1) rule, in mL/h:

- 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg
- 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10 kg
- 1 mL/kg/h for each kilogram above 20 kg

(The equivalent daily rule is 100 mL/kg for the first 10 kg, 50 mL/kg for the next 10 kg and 20 mL/kg above 20 kg. The two give slightly different totals, for example 14 kg gives 48 mL/h, or 1152 mL/day, by the hourly rule but 1200 mL/day by the daily rule, so use the rule a problem names.) The **deficit** in mL is the percent dehydration multiplied by the weight in kg and by 10, because 1 kg of body water is about 1000 mL:
$$\text{deficit (mL)} = \% \times \text{weight (kg)} \times 10.$$
For isonatremic dehydration, replace the deficit over about 24 hours plus maintenance, often giving half in the first 8 hours, after subtracting any fluid already given in boluses. Current AAP guidance recommends **isotonic maintenance fluid** (for example dextrose 5% in normal saline, with potassium once urine output is established) in hospitalized children to lower the risk of hyponatremia.

**Hypernatremic dehydration** (sodium above about 150 mEq/L) must be corrected slowly, with a fall in sodium of no more than about 10–12 mEq/L per day, over 48 hours or more, to avoid **cerebral edema** and seizures.

### The vomiting child: surgical causes

- **Intussusception** (age 3 months to 3 years, peak 5–9 months): intermittent severe colicky pain with drawing up of the legs, vomiting, lethargy, "currant jelly" stool, and a sausage-shaped mass. Ultrasound shows a **target sign**; air or contrast enema is both diagnostic and therapeutic.
- **Malrotation with midgut volvulus:** **bilious vomiting** in a neonate or infant is a surgical emergency; an upper GI series shows the abnormal duodenojejunal junction.
- **Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis:** projectile nonbilious vomiting at 3–6 weeks of age in an infant who stays hungry, with an olive-shaped mass and hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis; correct fluids and electrolytes before surgery.
- **Appendicitis:** periumbilical pain migrating to the right lower quadrant, anorexia, low fever; ultrasound or CT confirms.
- **Hirschsprung disease:** failure to pass meconium within 48 hours or chronic constipation; diagnosed by rectal suction biopsy showing absent ganglion cells.

title: Maintenance rate for a 14 kg child
problem: A 14 kg child who is not eating needs maintenance intravenous fluids. What is the hourly rate by the 4-2-1 rule?
step: First 10 kg: $10 \times 4 = 40$ mL/h.
step: Next 4 kg: $4 \times 2 = 8$ mL/h.
step: Total: $40 + 8 = 48$ mL/h.

@example
title: Deficit and oral rehydration
problem: An 8 kg infant with gastroenteritis has moderate dehydration of about 7%. Estimate the deficit and the ORS plan.
step: Deficit: $7 \times 8 \times 10 = 560$ mL.
step: For moderate dehydration give ORS at about 100 mL/kg over 4 hours: $100 \times 8 = 800$ mL, or about 200 mL/h.
step: Give in small frequent volumes, add replacement of ongoing stool losses, and reassess hydration after 4 hours.

@mistakes
- Using hypotension as a requirement for diagnosing shock in a child; it is a late sign.
- Correcting hypernatremic dehydration quickly, which risks cerebral edema.
- Giving antidiarrheal drugs to children with acute gastroenteritis.
- Treating bilious emesis in a neonate as simple reflux instead of an emergency.
