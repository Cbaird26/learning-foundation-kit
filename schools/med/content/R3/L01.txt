@article
The first days of life are a physiologic transition: the lungs fill with air, pulmonary vascular resistance falls, the foramen ovale and ductus arteriosus begin to close, and the liver and gut take over functions the placenta provided. Most newborns make this transition without help, so newborn care is mostly **assessment, prevention and screening**, with a low threshold to act when the pattern is wrong.

### Apgar score

The Apgar score is assigned at **1 and 5 minutes** (and every 5 minutes up to 20 minutes if the 5-minute score is below 7). Five items are each scored 0, 1 or 2:

| Item | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | absent | below 100/min | 100/min or more |
| Respiratory effort | absent | weak, irregular | good, crying |
| Muscle tone | limp | some flexion | active motion |
| Reflex irritability | none | grimace | cry, cough or sneeze |
| Color | blue or pale | pink body, blue extremities | completely pink |

The total is 0–10. It describes the baby's condition and response to resuscitation; it does **not** decide whether to start resuscitation, which is based on breathing, heart rate and tone in the first seconds.

### Routine interventions

- **Vitamin K** (phytonadione) intramuscularly, once, prevents vitamin K deficiency bleeding: newborns have low stores, a sterile gut that makes little vitamin K, and breast milk is low in it. The factors affected are II, VII, IX and X. A typical study dose for a term infant is 1 mg IM (study value; verify against current references before any clinical use).
- **Erythromycin ophthalmic ointment** prevents gonococcal conjunctivitis.
- **Hepatitis B vaccine** within 24 hours for medically stable infants of at least 2 kg; infants of HBsAg-positive mothers also receive hepatitis B immune globulin within 12 hours.
- **Screening:** newborn blood-spot screening (for example phenylketonuria, congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, hemoglobinopathies), hearing screening, and **critical congenital heart disease (CCHD) screening** by pulse oximetry at 24 hours or later. CCHD screening is passed with saturation of at least 95% in the right hand or foot and a difference of 3 points or less between them; below 90% at either site is an immediate fail, and values in between are repeated. A fail leads to echocardiography.
- **Hip examination** (Ortolani and Barlow maneuvers) for developmental dysplasia; ultrasound at 4–6 weeks for risk factors such as breech presentation.

Typical normal newborn values are heart rate 120–160/min and respiratory rate 40–60/min. Meconium should pass within 48 hours and urine within 24 hours; delayed meconium suggests Hirschsprung disease or cystic fibrosis.

### Weight loss

Term breastfed newborns lose weight in the first days and regain birth weight by about 10–14 days. Loss of up to about 7–10% is expected. Percent loss is
$$\text{loss}\% = \frac{\text{birth weight} - \text{current weight}}{\text{birth weight}} \times 100.$$
Greater loss prompts a feeding assessment, a look for dehydration and hypernatremia, and a bilirubin check.

### Jaundice

**Physiologic jaundice** appears after 24 hours, peaks around day 3–5 in term infants, and resolves within 1–2 weeks. It reflects high red cell turnover and immature hepatic conjugation. **Jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathologic until proven otherwise**, usually hemolysis (ABO or Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency); check total and direct bilirubin, blood types and a direct Coombs test. Jaundice that persists beyond 2 weeks, or any elevated **conjugated** (direct) bilirubin, needs evaluation for biliary atresia and other cholestasis (pale stools are a clue; early surgery improves outcome). Phototherapy and exchange transfusion thresholds depend on gestational age, age in hours and neurotoxicity risk factors, and are read from current AAP nomograms. Untreated severe indirect hyperbilirubinemia can cause kernicterus.

### Early-onset sepsis

Group B Streptococcus is the leading cause of early-onset sepsis in term infants. Intrapartum prophylaxis with penicillin, ampicillin or cefazolin given at least 4 hours before delivery is considered adequate. A well-appearing term infant whose mother received adequate prophylaxis needs routine care with observation; an ill-appearing infant needs cultures and empiric ampicillin plus gentamicin.

@example
title: Percent weight loss on day 2
problem: A term newborn weighed 3400 g at birth and 3162 g on day 2 of life. Calculate the percent weight loss and decide whether it is within the expected range.
step: Find the loss in grams: $3400 - 3162 = 238$ g.
step: Divide by the birth weight and convert to a percent: $238/3400 = 0.07$, so the loss is 7%.
step: Interpret: up to about 7–10% is expected in the first days for a term newborn, so 7% is acceptable if feeding is going well. Keep following weights; regain of birth weight is expected by 10–14 days.

@example
title: Scoring an Apgar
problem: At 1 minute a newborn has a heart rate of 90/min, a weak irregular cry, some flexion of the limbs, a grimace when the foot is flicked, and a blue body. What is the Apgar score?
step: Heart rate below 100/min scores 1.
step: Weak, irregular breathing scores 1; some flexion scores 1; grimace scores 1.
step: A blue or pale body scores 0.
step: Total: $1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 = 4$. A low score reflects the baby's condition and calls for support such as stimulation and ventilation per resuscitation guidelines.

@mistakes
- Treating jaundice at 18 hours of age as physiologic; jaundice in the first 24 hours is pathologic until proven otherwise.
- Using the Apgar score to decide whether to begin resuscitation instead of assessing breathing, heart rate and tone immediately.
- Calling a weight loss of more than 10% "normal" without checking feeding, hydration, sodium and bilirubin.
- Forgetting to fractionate bilirubin in prolonged jaundice, and so missing biliary atresia.
