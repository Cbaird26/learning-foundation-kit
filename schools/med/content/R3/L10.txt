@article
Adolescence is the transition from child to adult, and the leading causes of death are **injuries (motor vehicle crashes, firearms), suicide and homicide**, so health supervision focuses on behavior as much as on disease. The core skill is a **confidential, nonjudgmental conversation**.

### Confidential care

Interview the adolescent alone for part of every visit. Explain at the start that what is said is private **except** when there is a risk of serious harm to self or others, or abuse; state laws govern what minors can consent to (many allow contraception, STI testing and mental health care without parental consent), so know your local rules. Use a framework such as **HEEADSSS**: Home, Education and employment, Eating, peer-related Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide and depression, Safety. The **CRAFFT** questionnaire screens for substance use (Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble); **two or more yes answers indicate a high risk** that needs further assessment. Screen annually for depression (PHQ-A) and suicidal thinking, and ask about firearm access.

### Puberty

Girls: breast budding (thelarche) at about 8–13 years, then pubic hair, growth spurt, and menarche about 2–2.5 years after thelarche (average 12.5 years). Boys: testicular enlargement to at least 4 mL (age 9–14) is first, and the growth spurt comes late in puberty. Use Tanner stages 1–5. **Precocious puberty:** onset before age 8 in girls or 9 in boys. **Delayed puberty:** no breast development by 13 in girls, or no testicular enlargement by 14 in boys. **Primary amenorrhea:** no menses by age 15, or 3 years after thelarche. Consider pregnancy first for any amenorrhea, then hypothalamic causes (low energy availability, athletes, eating disorders), polycystic ovary syndrome, Turner syndrome, and Müllerian agenesis or androgen insensitivity. Heavy menstrual bleeding in an adolescent suggests anovulation or a bleeding disorder, most commonly von Willebrand disease.

### Contraception and infections

**Long-acting reversible contraception** (IUD and implant) is first line for adolescents. Combined hormonal methods are used with attention to contraindications (for example migraine with aura). **Emergency contraception** options include a copper IUD (most effective), ulipristal (up to 120 hours) and levonorgestrel (best within 72 hours). Screen sexually active females under 25 for chlamydia and gonorrhea annually (urine NAAT), test for HIV at least once in adolescence, and offer HPV vaccination (see the immunization lesson).

### Eating disorders

**Anorexia nervosa:** restriction of intake, intense fear of weight gain and disturbed body image, with low weight for the individual. Indications for hospitalization (medical instability) include awake **heart rate under 50 bpm**, blood pressure under 90/45 mmHg, orthostatic changes, electrolyte disturbance (hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia), arrhythmia, acute food refusal and weight below 75% of median BMI. Beware **refeeding syndrome** (low phosphate, potassium and magnesium) when nutrition is restarted. **Bulimia nervosa:** binge eating with compensatory behaviors; look for Russell sign, parotid swelling, dental erosion and hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis. First-line treatment for adolescents with anorexia is family-based therapy.

### Orthopedic and other problems

- **Slipped capital femoral epiphysis:** an obese early adolescent with hip or knee pain, a limp and limited internal rotation; diagnose with radiographs (frog-leg lateral view); make non-weight bearing and operate (pinning).
- **Scoliosis:** screen with the forward bend test; Cobb angle of 10 degrees or more defines scoliosis; bracing for moderate curves in the growing child; surgery for large curves.
- **Osgood–Schlatter disease:** anterior knee pain at the tibial tuberosity in active adolescents; treat symptomatically.
- **Testicular torsion:** acute scrotal pain with a high-riding testis and absent cremasteric reflex is a surgical emergency (best within about 6 hours).

### Respiratory distress in adolescents

Common causes: asthma exacerbation, pneumonia, and **primary spontaneous pneumothorax** in tall thin young males, from rupture of apical subpleural blebs: sudden pleuritic chest pain and dyspnea, decreased breath sounds and hyperresonance on one side. A small stable pneumothorax may be observed; a large one is drained. A **tension pneumothorax** (tracheal deviation, hypotension, distended neck veins) is a clinical diagnosis requiring immediate needle decompression. E-cigarette or vaping-associated lung injury (EVALI) is another cause of respiratory distress in adolescents.

### Body mass index

$$\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)}}{\text{height (m)}^2}.$$
In children and adolescents, interpret BMI using age- and sex-specific percentiles: 85th to below 95th is overweight and the 95th or above is obesity.

@example
title: Computing BMI
problem: An adolescent weighs 54 kg and is 1.58 m tall. Calculate the BMI.
step: Square the height: $1.58^2 = 2.4964$ m².
step: Divide: $54 / 2.4964 \approx 21.6$ kg/m².
step: Plot on the age- and sex-specific chart; a value of 21.6 is in the healthy range for most 15- to 16-year-old girls, but the percentile depends on age and sex.

@example
title: Primary amenorrhea
problem: A 15-year-old girl has never had a menstrual period. She has breast development (Tanner 4) and normal pubic hair. What should be considered?
step: Normal breast development shows estrogen production, so the ovaries are working. Check pregnancy first.
step: Consider anatomical causes, such as Müllerian agenesis or an imperforate hymen with hematocolpos (look for a bulging blue membrane or cyclic pain), and obtain a pelvic ultrasound.
step: If anatomy is normal, consider hypothalamic suppression, polycystic ovary syndrome and thyroid or prolactin disorders, with hormone tests.

@mistakes
- Promising absolute confidentiality when abuse or serious risk of harm requires disclosure.
- Missing pregnancy in an amenorrheic teenager.
- Relying on a near-normal BMI percentile to exclude medical instability in anorexia nervosa; check vital signs, orthostatics and electrolytes.
- Treating knee pain in an obese adolescent without examining the hip.
