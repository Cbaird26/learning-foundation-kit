@article
Child maltreatment is common, underrecognized and treatable by early detection. As a clinician you do not need to prove that abuse occurred and you are not an investigator. Your role is to **recognize concerning patterns, protect the child, document carefully and report**.

### Forms of maltreatment

**Neglect** (failure to provide food, shelter, supervision, medical or educational care) is the most common form. Others are **physical abuse**, **sexual abuse**, **emotional (psychological) abuse** and **medical child abuse** (caregiver-driven unnecessary medical care). Risk factors include caregiver stress, substance use, intimate partner violence, social isolation, poverty and a child with special needs, but maltreatment occurs in every community, so avoid stereotypes.

### General red flags

No single finding diagnoses abuse. The concern arises from a **mismatch** or a **pattern**:

- The history is **inconsistent with the injury** or with the child's developmental ability (for example, a serious injury attributed to a minor fall, or an injury to an infant who cannot yet roll or crawl).
- The explanation **changes** or differs between caregivers, or there is **no explanation** for a significant injury.
- **Delay in seeking care** that is not otherwise explained.
- **Injuries in locations or patterns** that are unusual for accidental injury, or injuries of different ages.
- Bruising in a **young infant** (the saying is "those who don't cruise rarely bruise"). A useful decision aid for children under 4 is **TEN-4-FACESp**: bruising on the torso, ears or neck, any bruising in an infant 4 months of age or younger, or bruising of the frenulum, angle of the jaw, cheeks, eyelids or subconjunctivae, or patterned bruising, should raise concern.
- Fractures in a child who is not walking, or multiple fractures at different stages of healing.
- An infant with unexplained **lethargy, vomiting, irritability, seizures or apnea**, which may reflect head injury even when the history gives no trauma; evaluate with neuroimaging and a dilated retinal examination.
- Findings that suggest sexual abuse, a disclosure by the child, or sexually transmitted infection in a prepubertal child.
- Poor growth, missed immunizations or medical care, or serious unmet needs, which suggest neglect.

### Evaluation

- Treat medical emergencies first. Obtain a **detailed history from the caregivers and child separately**, in open-ended, non-leading questions, and record exact quotes.
- Perform a complete skin-to-toe examination, with photographs if hospital policy allows. For children under 2 years with a concerning injury, perform a **skeletal survey**; consider laboratory studies for bleeding disorders and bone health when the bruising or fracture pattern raises an alternative explanation.
- Involve **social work** and, where available, a **child abuse pediatrician**. The goal is the child's safety, so consider whether it is safe for the child to go home, and arrange admission or an alternative safe plan when it is not.
- For concerns about sexual abuse, avoid repeated interviews; arrange a **forensic interview by a trained interviewer** and a specialized examination; remember that most children with a history of abuse have a normal examination.

### Mimics

Evaluate alternatives honestly: congenital dermal melanocytosis (Mongolian spots), cultural practices such as coining or cupping, immune thrombocytopenia, hemophilia and von Willebrand disease, Henoch–Schönlein purpura, vitamin K deficiency bleeding, osteogenesis imperfecta, rickets, and accidental injuries that fit the history. A bleeding or bone disorder can coexist with abuse, so a diagnosis does not erase a concerning mismatch.

### Duty to report

Every U.S. state requires clinicians to report **reasonable suspicion** of maltreatment to child protective services or law enforcement. You report **suspicion, not proof**. Reporting does not require parental consent; reporters acting in **good faith are legally immune**; and failure to report can result in penalties and licensing action. Follow your institution's process, and tell the family about the report when this will not place the child at risk. A report is a request for a protective investigation, not an accusation. In emergencies, hospital security or law enforcement can place a hold according to local law.

### Documentation

Medical records are legal documents. Write **objective, specific, non-judgmental** entries: the source and time of each statement with direct quotes, a precise description of the findings (location, size, color, shape, number), the studies ordered and their results, the consultations made, and the report made (date, time, agency, name of the person you spoke with). Avoid conclusions you cannot support (for example, "mother is lying"); instead write "the history provided does not explain the injury" and give your medical opinion about consistency.

@example
title: Recognizing a mismatch
problem: A 5-month-old is brought in by his father, who says he rolled off a sofa the previous day. The infant has a fractured femur. He has not yet started rolling over consistently. Which features raise concern, and what should be done?
step: The mechanism (short fall) is not usually enough to cause a femur fracture in a young infant, and an infant who does not yet roll cannot move far enough to fall. The history is inconsistent with the injury and the developmental stage, and there was a delay of one day.
step: Treat the fracture and pain, perform a complete examination, and obtain a skeletal survey and further studies such as neuroimaging if indicated.
step: Involve social work and a child abuse pediatrician, make a report to child protective services based on reasonable suspicion, and document history, findings and the report objectively.

@example
title: Writing an objective entry
problem: Which of the entries "The father is clearly lying about the fall" and "The father states the infant 'rolled off the sofa yesterday'. The explanation provided is not consistent with the infant's developmental stage or with the fracture pattern." belongs in the chart, and why?
step: A medical record should record observations and direct statements.
step: The second entry quotes the history and states a medical judgment about consistency, which is within the clinician's role.
step: The first entry assigns intent and is unsupported opinion. It does not belong in the record.

@mistakes
- Waiting for certainty before reporting; the legal threshold is reasonable suspicion.
- Asking leading questions or repeating interviews of a child who has disclosed.
- Assuming that a normal examination excludes sexual abuse, or that an alternative medical explanation for one finding excludes concern about a mismatch.
- Documenting opinions about caregivers' character instead of objective findings and quotations.
