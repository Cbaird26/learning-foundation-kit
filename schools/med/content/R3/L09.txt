@article
Pediatric emergencies are usually **respiratory or circulatory failure** first, and cardiac arrest second. The typical arrest in a child is **asphyxial**: hypoxia and acidosis lead to bradycardia and then asystole. So the most valuable skill is to recognize deterioration early and treat it.

### Pediatric assessment triangle

In seconds, from the doorway, assess three things: **appearance** (tone, interactiveness, consolability, look or gaze, speech or cry), **work of breathing** (positioning, retractions, noises) and **circulation to skin** (pallor, mottling, cyanosis). Together they tell you whether the child is stable, in respiratory distress or failure, or in shock, and prompt the ABCDE approach: airway, breathing, circulation, disability, exposure.

### Shock

Children **compensate** for shock by raising heart rate and vascular tone, so **blood pressure stays normal until late**. Compensated shock: tachycardia, delayed capillary refill (more than 2 seconds), weak peripheral pulses, cool extremities. **Hypotension** signals decompensation. Minimum systolic blood pressure by age: neonates 60 mmHg; infants (1–12 months) 70 mmHg; children aged 1–10 years $70 + 2 \times \text{age (years)}$; age 10 and older 90 mmHg.

Types of shock: hypovolemic (most common worldwide; gastroenteritis, hemorrhage), distributive (septic, anaphylactic, neurogenic), cardiogenic (myocarditis, congenital heart disease) and obstructive (tension pneumothorax, tamponade). Initial treatment of hypovolemic or septic shock: oxygen, vascular access (intraosseous if intravenous fails), and **isotonic crystalloid bolus 10–20 mL/kg**, reassessing after each (for septic shock the guidelines say 10–20 mL/kg with frequent reassessment, with early epinephrine or norepinephrine for fluid-refractory shock). Give antibiotics within the first hour for suspected sepsis. Cardiogenic shock requires cautious fluid.

### Resuscitation

- Check responsiveness and breathing; call for help.
- **Compressions** at a rate of 100–120/min, to a depth of at least one-third of the chest's anteroposterior diameter (about 4 cm in infants and 5 cm in children), with full recoil and minimal interruptions.
- Compression-to-ventilation ratio **30:2 for a single rescuer and 15:2 for two rescuers**, until an advanced airway is placed.
- Shockable rhythms (ventricular fibrillation, pulseless ventricular tachycardia): **defibrillate at 2 J/kg**, then 4 J/kg for later shocks.
- **Epinephrine** for non-shockable rhythms as early as possible, and for shockable rhythms after the second shock: **0.01 mg/kg IV or IO** (0.1 mL/kg of the 0.1 mg/mL concentration, also written 1:10,000), every 3–5 minutes (typical study dose).

### Anaphylaxis

Rapidly progressive urticaria, angioedema, wheeze, stridor, vomiting or hypotension after an allergen exposure. First-line treatment is **intramuscular epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg of the 1 mg/mL (1:1000) concentration, maximum 0.3 mg in children (0.5 mg in adolescents and adults)**, in the anterolateral thigh; repeat in 5–15 minutes if needed (typical study dose; verify against current references before any clinical use). Auto-injectors deliver 0.1 mg (about 7.5–15 kg), 0.15 mg (15–30 kg) or 0.3 mg (30 kg or more). Antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts, not substitutes. Observe for biphasic reactions and prescribe an epinephrine auto-injector with an action plan.

### Seizures

A **simple febrile seizure** is generalized, lasts under 15 minutes, does not recur within 24 hours, and occurs in a child aged 6 months to 5 years with fever and no CNS infection or neurologic abnormality. Management: reassure, find the fever source; no lumbar puncture, EEG or neuroimaging is needed in a well-appearing, fully vaccinated child back to baseline. Antipyretics do not prevent recurrence. **Status epilepticus** (seizure lasting 5 minutes or more) is treated with a **benzodiazepine** first (intramuscular midazolam is effective without IV access; intravenous lorazepam is typically given at about 0.1 mg/kg, maximum 4 mg per dose), followed by a second-line antiseizure medication if seizures persist.

### Other emergencies

- **Choking:** infants get back blows and chest thrusts; children over 1 year get abdominal thrusts.
- **Button battery or magnet ingestion:** a button battery lodged in the esophagus requires **emergent removal** because of rapid tissue injury.
- **Acetaminophen overdose:** assess the level at 4 hours after ingestion on the Rumack–Matthew nomogram; treat with N-acetylcysteine.
- **Hypoglycemia:** treat with dextrose (a typical study dose is 0.5–1 g/kg, for example 5–10 mL/kg of 10% dextrose, which contains 0.1 g/mL).
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis:** cautious isotonic fluids (not rapid large boluses unless in shock) and insulin infusion after initial fluid; cerebral edema is the major complication.

@example
title: Epinephrine for anaphylaxis
problem: A 22 kg child has hives, wheeze and vomiting after a bee sting. What is the intramuscular epinephrine dose and volume of the 1 mg/mL solution (typical study dose 0.01 mg/kg, maximum 0.3 mg in children)?
step: Dose: $22 \times 0.01 = 0.22$ mg.
step: Compare with the maximum 0.3 mg for children: 0.22 mg is below it.
step: Volume of 1 mg/mL: $0.22 \text{ mg} / 1 \text{ mg/mL} = 0.22$ mL, given in the anterolateral thigh.
step: Repeat in 5–15 minutes if symptoms persist, and transport for observation.

@example
title: Is this child hypotensive?
problem: A 6-year-old has a heart rate of 150/min, capillary refill of 4 seconds, cool hands and a blood pressure of 88/52 mmHg during a febrile illness. Is she in compensated or decompensated shock?
step: Minimum systolic pressure for age 6: $70 + 2 \times 6 = 82$ mmHg.
step: Her systolic pressure of 88 mmHg is above 82, so she is not hypotensive.
step: With tachycardia, delayed capillary refill and cool extremities, she is in compensated septic shock. Treat now with fluid bolus and antibiotics; do not wait for hypotension.

@mistakes
- Waiting for hypotension before treating shock in a child.
- Withholding epinephrine in anaphylaxis in favor of antihistamines.
- Using the wrong epinephrine concentration: 1 mg/mL for IM anaphylaxis, 0.1 mg/mL for IV cardiac arrest.
- Performing a lumbar puncture, EEG and imaging routinely after a simple febrile seizure.
