@article
Children are not small adults: they have narrower airways (resistance is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the radius, so a little swelling causes a large rise), a compliant chest wall and fewer respiratory reserves, so they decompensate quickly. The **respiratory rate**, **work of breathing** and **appearance** are your best early signs.

### Recognizing distress

Signs of increased work of breathing: tachypnea, nasal flaring, retractions (intercostal, subcostal, suprasternal), accessory muscle use, **grunting** (an attempt to keep alveoli open), head bobbing, and tripod positioning. **Warning signs of failure:** poor air movement, fatigue, cyanosis, bradycardia, and altered mental status. WHO tachypnea thresholds: at least 60/min under 2 months, 50/min at 2–12 months, 40/min at 1–5 years. **Stridor** (usually inspiratory) implies upper-airway (extrathoracic) obstruction, **wheeze** implies lower-airway obstruction, and **grunting** implies alveolar disease.

### Upper airway

**Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis)** is caused most often by parainfluenza virus in children 6 months to 3 years: barking "seal" cough, hoarseness, inspiratory stridor, preceded by a cold; neck radiograph shows the **steeple sign** but is rarely needed. Treat with **dexamethasone, about 0.6 mg/kg once** (typical study dose; maximum about 16 mg) for any severity, humidified air has no proven benefit, and add **nebulized racemic epinephrine** for stridor at rest with observation for several hours afterward.

**Epiglottitis** is rare now because of Hib vaccination: toxic appearance, drooling, tripod position, muffled voice, no cough. Do not agitate the child or examine the throat; secure the airway in the operating room, then treat with ceftriaxone. A radiograph shows the **thumb sign**. **Bacterial tracheitis** follows a viral prodrome with high fever, toxic appearance and thick purulent secretions.

### Lower airway

**Bronchiolitis** is a viral infection (most often RSV) of infants under 2 years: rhinorrhea for 1–3 days, then cough, tachypnea, wheezing, crackles and retractions, peaking at days 3–5. Management is **supportive**: nasal suction, hydration and supplemental oxygen when the saturation is persistently below about 90% (thresholds vary); the AAP advises **against routine bronchodilators, corticosteroids, antibiotics and chest physiotherapy**. High-flow nasal cannula is used for increasing work of breathing. Risk factors for severe disease: prematurity, congenital heart disease, chronic lung disease and age under 12 weeks.

**Asthma** causes recurrent wheeze, cough and dyspnea triggered by viral infections, allergens, exercise and cold air; spirometry in children over 5 years shows reversible obstruction. An **exacerbation** is treated with inhaled short-acting beta agonist (albuterol) repeated, a **systemic corticosteroid** (for example prednisolone or prednisone about 1–2 mg/kg/day, typical study dose, maximum about 40–60 mg), ipratropium for severe disease and oxygen for hypoxemia. Long-term control uses inhaled corticosteroids.

**Community-acquired pneumonia:** viruses are the most common cause in preschoolers; *Streptococcus pneumoniae* is the most common bacterial cause; *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* is common in school-age children. Outpatient treatment for presumed bacterial pneumonia is **high-dose amoxicillin (about 90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily)**; a macrolide such as azithromycin is used for suspected atypical infection. Hospitalize for hypoxemia, severe distress, dehydration or age under about 6 months. Chest radiography is not required for every outpatient.

**Foreign body aspiration** typically involves a toddler who suddenly coughs and chokes while eating (nuts, small toys, button batteries in the esophagus). Examination: unilateral wheeze or decreased breath sounds. Chest radiography may show **hyperinflation on the affected side** (air trapping) or be normal. Treatment: bronchoscopy.

**Pertussis:** catarrhal phase, paroxysmal coughing with an inspiratory whoop and post-tussive emesis, and marked lymphocytosis. Young infants may have apnea rather than whoop. Treat with azithromycin and provide isolation and prophylaxis for contacts.

### Febrile infants by age

The deliverable for this block is to evaluate fever in an infant by age group. Up to 7 days or ill appearing: full sepsis evaluation (outside the AAP 2021 guideline). At 8–21 days: blood, urine and CSF testing, admission and empiric parenteral antibiotics. At 22–28 days: urinalysis, cultures and inflammatory markers, with lumbar puncture when markers are abnormal, and admission when markers or urinalysis are abnormal. At 29–60 days: risk-stratify with urinalysis, cultures and inflammatory markers. Older infants: identify a source; consider urinary tract infection and pneumonia.

@example
title: Dexamethasone for croup
problem: A 15 kg toddler with a barking cough and inspiratory stridor at rest is treated with dexamethasone at a typical study dose of 0.6 mg/kg. What is the dose?
step: Multiply weight by dose: $15 \times 0.6 = 9$ mg.
step: Compare with the maximum of about 16 mg: 9 mg is below it.
step: Give the single dose orally or by another route, and add nebulized racemic epinephrine because stridor is present at rest, then observe.

@example
title: Bronchiolitis in a 5-month-old
problem: A 5-month-old has had a runny nose for 3 days, and now has wheezing, a respiratory rate of 62/min, mild subcostal retractions and an oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. He is feeding adequately. What is the management?
step: Recognize bronchiolitis: viral prodrome in an infant followed by wheeze, crackles and tachypnea.
step: He is not hypoxemic (94%) and is feeding, so he can be managed supportively at home with nasal suction and fluids and return precautions.
step: Do not give albuterol, steroids or antibiotics routinely. Admit if he cannot feed, is hypoxemic or has worsening work of breathing.

@mistakes
- Giving routine albuterol, steroids or antibiotics for typical bronchiolitis.
- Examining the throat of a drooling, toxic child with suspected epiglottitis.
- Equating stridor with wheeze; they point to different sites of obstruction.
- Withholding dexamethasone for mild croup, or discharging a child after racemic epinephrine without observation.

Describe the evaluation and initial management for (a) a well-appearing 10-day-old with a rectal temperature of 38.4 °C, (b) a well-appearing 45-day-old with a rectal temperature of 38.2 °C, and (c) a well-appearing 5-month-old who is fully vaccinated, has a temperature of 39.0 °C and has no focus of infection.
hint: Age determines how extensive the evaluation is; think about which tests each age group needs.
solution: (a) At 10 days (8–21 day band), perform a full sepsis evaluation: blood culture, catheterized urinalysis and culture, lumbar puncture, inflammatory markers; hospitalize and start empiric parenteral antibiotics (ampicillin plus gentamicin or cefotaxime), adding acyclovir if herpes simplex virus is a concern. (b) At 45 days, obtain urinalysis, urine and blood cultures and inflammatory markers (procalcitonin, CRP, ANC) to risk-stratify; lumbar puncture and admission are more likely when markers are abnormal or the infant is ill; discuss follow-up carefully if low risk.
