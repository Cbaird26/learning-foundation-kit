@article
A **well-child visit** has the same skeleton at every age: interval history, growth plotting, physical examination, developmental surveillance and screening, immunizations, and **anticipatory guidance**, which means counseling about what is coming next (sleep, feeding, safety, behavior). Visits are recommended in the first week, at 1 month, at 2, 4, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 24 and 30 months, and then yearly from age 3.

### Screening highlights

Screen vision and hearing at appropriate ages; measure hemoglobin at about 12 months; screen for lead at 12 and 24 months where risk or local rules require; screen for developmental delay at 9, 18 and 30 months and for autism at 18 and 24 months; start fluoride varnish when the first tooth erupts; measure blood pressure yearly from age 3; screen for depression from age 12. Hearing screening is done in the newborn period, and dyslipidemia screening is recommended once at ages 9–11 and again at 17–21.

### Safety counseling by age

- **Safe sleep:** place infants on their back, on a firm flat surface with no soft bedding; room-share without bed-sharing; no sleep position devices. Pacifier use at nap and bedtime is acceptable after breastfeeding is established.
- **Car seats:** rear-facing as long as the seat's weight and height limits allow, then forward-facing with a harness, then a booster until the vehicle belt fits.
- **Toddlers and preschoolers:** water safety, poison control, safe storage of medications and any firearms (locked, unloaded, ammunition separate), window guards, helmets.
- **School age and adolescents:** seatbelts, helmets, substance use, screens and sleep.

### Routine immunizations (general terms)

Always check the current CDC/AAP schedule; the following is the general pattern.

| Vaccine | Usual schedule |
|---|---|
| Hepatitis B | birth, 1–2 months, 6–18 months (3 doses) |
| Rotavirus | 2, 4 and sometimes 6 months (oral; first dose by 15 weeks) |
| DTaP | 2, 4, 6, 15–18 months, 4–6 years (5 doses) |
| Hib | 2, 4, sometimes 6 months, and booster at 12–15 months |
| Pneumococcal conjugate | 2, 4, 6 months, booster at 12–15 months |
| Inactivated poliovirus | 2, 4, 6–18 months, 4–6 years (4 doses) |
| Influenza (inactivated) | yearly from 6 months; two doses 4 weeks apart in the first season for children under 9 years |
| MMR | 12–15 months and 4–6 years |
| Varicella | 12–15 months and 4–6 years |
| Hepatitis A | two doses starting at 12–23 months, at least 6 months apart |
| Tdap | once at 11–12 years (and in each pregnancy) |
| HPV | start at 9–12 years; 2 doses if started before age 15, 3 doses if started at 15 or later or if immunocompromised |
| MenACWY | 11–12 years, booster at 16 |
| MenB | 16–18 years by shared decision-making |

RSV prevention for infants under 8 months entering their first RSV season is either maternal RSV vaccination (in late pregnancy) or a long-acting monoclonal antibody (nirsevimab) given to the infant. COVID-19 vaccine recommendations have changed repeatedly, so rely on the current schedule.

### Contraindications and precautions

- **Contraindications** (do not give): a **severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)** to a previous dose or a vaccine component; for live vaccines (MMR, varicella, rotavirus, live intranasal influenza), **severe immunocompromise** such as SCID, and pregnancy for MMR and varicella; for rotavirus, a history of intussusception; for pertussis-containing vaccines, encephalopathy within 7 days of a previous dose.
- **Precautions** (weigh benefit and risk): moderate or severe acute illness, Guillain–Barré syndrome within 6 weeks of a prior dose of some vaccines.
- **Not contraindications:** mild illness with or without low-grade fever, antibiotic therapy, prematurity (give at chronologic age), breastfeeding, egg allergy (MMR and influenza vaccines can be given), and family history of adverse events.

Multiple vaccines can be given at the same visit in different sites. Live vaccines not given on the same day should be separated by at least 4 weeks.

@example
title: Vaccines at the 2-month visit
problem: A healthy term infant is seen at 2 months. She received hepatitis B vaccine at birth. Which vaccines are due?
step: Hepatitis B: dose 2 is due at 1–2 months, so give it now if it was not given at 1 month.
step: Give the first doses of DTaP, Hib, pneumococcal conjugate, inactivated poliovirus and rotavirus (oral, with the first dose before 15 weeks).
step: Ask about RSV protection: if the mother did not receive RSV vaccine in late pregnancy and the infant is entering her first RSV season, offer nirsevimab.
step: Counsel that fever and fussiness for 1–2 days are common, and acetaminophen can be used for comfort at a weight-based dose.

@example
title: Mild illness before MMR
problem: A 12-month-old has a runny nose and temperature of 37.9 °C (100.2 °F) and is otherwise well. Should the MMR vaccine be given today?
step: Identify contraindications: no history of anaphylaxis to a vaccine component, and no immunocompromise.
step: Identify precautions: a moderate or severe acute illness is a precaution; this is a mild illness.
step: Conclude that the vaccine should be given. Deferring causes missed opportunities.

@mistakes
- Deferring vaccines for a cold, low-grade fever or antibiotic course; these are not contraindications.
- Delaying vaccines for a premature infant instead of giving them at chronologic age.
- Giving live vaccines to a child with a severe immunodeficiency or to a pregnant patient.
- Recalling the schedule from memory without checking the current CDC/AAP table, which is revised annually.
