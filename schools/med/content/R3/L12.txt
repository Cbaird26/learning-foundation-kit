@article
Most pediatric drug errors are **dosing errors**, and the commonest is a **tenfold error** from a misplaced decimal. Doses are calculated from weight (or sometimes from body surface area), checked against a **maximum dose**, and verified by a second person. All doses in these lessons are typical study doses; always check a current reference before any clinical use.

### The basic calculation

1. **Use a current, measured weight in kilograms** (not pounds).
2. Choose the dose in mg/kg and determine whether it is **per dose** or **per day**. A per-day dose is divided by the number of doses:
$$\text{dose per administration (mg)} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)} \times \text{dose (mg/kg/day)}}{\text{doses per day}}.$$
3. **Compare with the maximum dose** (per dose and per day). If the calculated dose exceeds the maximum, give the maximum; children who weigh as much as adults receive adult doses, not more.
4. **Convert to a volume** for a liquid:
$$\text{volume (mL)} = \frac{\text{dose (mg)}}{\text{concentration (mg/mL)}}.$$
For example, a suspension labeled 160 mg per 5 mL contains 32 mg/mL.
5. State the **dose in mg and in mL** on the prescription, and sanity check: does it make sense for the child's size?

### Examples of typical study doses

| Drug | Typical dose |
|---|---|
| Acetaminophen | 15 mg/kg per dose every 4–6 h; maximum 75 mg/kg/day and not more than 4 g/day |
| Ibuprofen (age 6 months and older) | 10 mg/kg per dose every 6–8 h; maximum 40 mg/kg/day |
| Amoxicillin, high dose (otitis media, pneumonia) | 90 mg/kg/day divided every 12 h; maximum about 4 g/day |
| Ceftriaxone | 50 mg/kg IV or IM once daily; maximum 2 g (meningitis: 100 mg/kg/day, maximum 4 g) |
| Azithromycin | 10 mg/kg on day 1 (maximum 500 mg), then 5 mg/kg on days 2–5 (maximum 250 mg) |
| Dexamethasone for croup | 0.6 mg/kg once; maximum about 16 mg |

### Body surface area

For some drugs (for example chemotherapy) the dose is based on **body surface area (BSA)**. The Mosteller formula is
$$\text{BSA (m}^2) = \sqrt{\frac{\text{height (cm)} \times \text{weight (kg)}}{3600}}.$$

### Why children differ

- **Neonates and young infants:** reduced glomerular filtration and tubular secretion (dose intervals are longer for renally cleared drugs such as gentamicin), immature hepatic metabolism (glucuronidation), higher total body water (larger volume of distribution for water-soluble drugs), lower plasma protein binding, and a more permeable blood–brain barrier.
- **Toddlers and young children:** faster hepatic metabolism and renal clearance per kilogram, so some drugs need higher mg/kg doses than adults.
- **Obesity:** dose by weight can overshoot. For many drugs dose by ideal or adjusted weight, and **cap at the adult maximum**.

### Drugs to avoid or use with caution

- **Ceftriaxone** in neonates with hyperbilirubinemia (it displaces bilirubin from albumin) or receiving calcium-containing intravenous fluids (precipitation); cefotaxime is the usual alternative in neonates.
- **Chloramphenicol** in neonates: gray baby syndrome from impaired glucuronidation.
- **Sulfonamides** in neonates: kernicterus risk from bilirubin displacement; also in G6PD deficiency (hemolysis).
- **Aspirin** in children with viral illness (Reye syndrome), except in specific indications such as Kawasaki disease.
- **Codeine and tramadol** in children: variable CYP2D6 metabolism can produce life-threatening respiratory depression; avoid in young children and after tonsillectomy.
- **Tetracyclines** (tooth discoloration) in children under 8 years, with exceptions such as short doxycycline courses for serious rickettsial disease.
- **Fluoroquinolones:** used selectively because of cartilage and tendon concerns.
- **Honey** and **ipecac** are not medications for infants.

### Safe prescribing

Write the **dose, the weight used and the dose in both mg and mL**. Use a **leading zero** (0.5 mg, never .5 mg) and **never a trailing zero** (5 mg, never 5.0 mg), because an overlooked decimal point produces a tenfold overdose. Avoid abbreviations such as "U" for units. Use oral syringes for liquid medications, not kitchen spoons. For high-risk drugs (insulin, opioids, heparin, chemotherapy), use independent double checks.

@example
title: Acetaminophen dose and volume
problem: A 14 kg child needs acetaminophen at a typical study dose of 15 mg/kg per dose. The suspension is 160 mg/5 mL. What are the dose and the volume?
step: Dose: $14 \times 15 = 210$ mg.
step: Concentration: $160/5 = 32$ mg/mL.
step: Volume: $210/32 \approx 6.6$ mL (about 6.5 mL measured with an oral syringe).
step: Check the limit: 5 doses of 210 mg in a day is 1050 mg, which is 75 mg/kg/day, so at most 5 doses a day.

@example
title: Capping at the maximum dose
problem: A 60 kg adolescent needs high-dose amoxicillin at a typical study dose of 90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily, with a maximum of 4000 mg/day. What is the dose per administration?
step: Calculated daily dose: $60 \times 90 = 5400$ mg/day.
step: This exceeds the maximum of 4000 mg/day, so cap the daily dose at 4000 mg.
step: Divide by two doses: $4000/2 = 2000$ mg every 12 hours.

@mistakes
- Using pounds instead of kilograms, or forgetting to convert.
- Confusing mg/kg/day with mg/kg/dose, which gives an error equal to the number of daily doses.
- Failing to cap the dose at the maximum in a large child.
- Writing "5.0 mg" or ".5 mg", which can be misread as 50 mg or 5 mg.
