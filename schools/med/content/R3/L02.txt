@article
Growth and development are the pediatric vital signs of long-term health. Growth is measured; development is observed across **four domains**: gross and fine motor, language, social-emotional, and cognitive. At every visit you compare the child with norms, but you also compare the child with their own trajectory.

### Growth

Use **WHO growth charts from birth to 2 years** and **CDC charts from 2 to 20 years**. Plot weight, length (supine until age 2, then standing height) and head circumference (to age 3), and for older children, BMI. Typical landmarks:

- Birth weight roughly **doubles by 4–5 months** and **triples by 12 months**.
- Length increases about **50% by 12 months** (about 50 cm to 75 cm) and doubles by about 4 years.
- Head circumference is about 35 cm at birth and about 47 cm at 12 months; it grows fastest in the first 3 months.
- Typical weight gain is about 20–30 g/day in the first 3 months, slowing later in the first year.
- Weight loss up to about 7–10% of birth weight in the first days is expected and is regained by 10–14 days.

**Failure to thrive** (faltering growth) is suggested by weight-for-age below the 5th percentile (some definitions use the 2nd percentile, or a z-score below -2) or by crossing two or more major percentile lines downward. Organic causes are less common than inadequate caloric intake; measure intake before ordering extensive tests. Children with constitutional delay or familial short stature track below the mean along a curve; they usually grow along their own percentile. For BMI in children 2 years and older, 85th to below the 95th percentile is overweight and at or above the 95th percentile is obesity.

### Milestones

The CDC revised its milestones to those that most children (75% or more) reach at each age. Typical examples:

| Age | Examples |
|---|---|
| 2 months | smiles at people; calms when spoken to; coos; looks at your face; holds head up briefly on the tummy |
| 4 months | laughs; holds head steady; brings hands to mouth; pushes up on elbows on the tummy |
| 6 months | takes turns making sounds; reaches for toys; rolls from tummy to back; begins sitting with support |
| 9 months | sits without support; babbles "mamama"; plays peek-a-boo; transfers toys hand to hand; looks for a dropped object |
| 12 months | pulls to stand; may cruise; waves bye-bye; says "mama" or "dada"; uses a pincer grasp |
| 18 months | walks without holding on; says three or more words; points to show something; scribbles |
| 24 months | kicks a ball; says at least two words together; points to named pictures; follows simple directions |
| 3 years | copies a circle; talks in conversation; alternates feet on stairs; strangers understand most speech |
| 4 years | draws a person with three or more body parts; speaks in four-word sentences; hops; copies a cross |
| 5 years | hops on one foot; counts to 10; draws a person with six body parts; tells a story |

Shapes are copied in a typical order: circle at about 3 years, cross at 4, square at about 4–5 and triangle at about 5.

### Surveillance, screening and red flags

Developmental **surveillance** happens at every visit; formal standardized **screening** (for example the Ages and Stages Questionnaire) is recommended at 9, 18 and 30 months, and **autism-specific screening** (for example the M-CHAT-R/F) at 18 and 24 months. Hearing and vision should be checked at appropriate ages. Red flags include **loss of any previously acquired skill at any age**, no social smile by 2–3 months, no babbling by 9 months, no single words by 16 months, no two-word phrases by 24 months, not walking by 18 months, and any asymmetry in motor function. Delay in one domain prompts testing for its likely causes (audiology for language delay) and referral to early intervention (birth to age 3) or school-based services.

### Primitive reflexes

Primitive reflexes are present at birth and fade as cortical control grows. The **Moro reflex** fades by about 4–6 months; the **rooting reflex** by about 4 months; the **palmar grasp** by about 5–6 months; the **asymmetric tonic neck reflex** by about 6 months. Persistence beyond these ages, or asymmetry, suggests central nervous system dysfunction such as cerebral palsy.

@example
title: Rate of weight gain
problem: A term infant weighed 3300 g at birth and 4500 g at the 2-month visit (60 days later). Calculate the average daily weight gain and judge it.
step: Weight gained: $4500 - 3300 = 1200$ g.
step: Divide by the days elapsed: $1200/60 = 20$ g/day.
step: Compare with the typical 20–30 g/day in early infancy. This infant is at the lower end of the usual range; plot the weight to check the percentile trajectory and review feeding, but this rate alone does not indicate failure to thrive.

@example
title: Assessing a 15-month-old
problem: A 15-month-old takes a few steps alone, says "mama", "dada" and two other words, waves, and uses a pincer grasp. Her parents ask whether her development is on track.
step: Gross motor: walking independently is typically expected by 18 months, and a few steps at 15 months is on track.
step: Language: three to four words at 15 months is within the expected range, and waving and pincer grasp are 12-month milestones already achieved.
step: Conclude that development is on track in all domains. Reinforce surveillance at the next visit and use a standardized screen at 18 months.

@mistakes
- Treating a child with delayed milestones in one domain as "just a late talker" without checking hearing.
- Using a chronologic age for a preterm infant instead of correcting for prematurity (correct through about age 2 for development and growth plotting).
- Plotting a child over 2 years on the WHO chart or a younger child on the CDC chart.
- Waiting for formal screening when a parent reports regression; loss of skills needs prompt evaluation.
