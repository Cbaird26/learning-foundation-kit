@article
Congenital conditions are present at birth; genetic conditions may appear at any time. About 3% of newborns have a major structural anomaly. Think in a stepwise way: the **pattern of features**, the **likely mechanism** (chromosomal, single gene, imprinting, deletion, teratogen) and the **associated defects to screen for**.

### Chromosomal disorders

- **Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome):** the most common chromosomal disorder, usually from meiotic nondisjunction (risk rises with maternal age). Features: hypotonia, flat facial profile, upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, single palmar crease, a gap between the first and second toes. Associations: **atrioventricular septal defect**, **duodenal atresia** (double bubble sign), Hirschsprung disease, hypothyroidism, atlantoaxial instability, celiac disease, hearing loss, leukemia and early Alzheimer disease. Screen with cell-free DNA or quad screen, and confirm with karyotype or amniocentesis.
- **Trisomy 18 (Edwards):** clenched fists with overlapping fingers, rocker-bottom feet, micrognathia, ventricular septal defect, severe prognosis.
- **Trisomy 13 (Patau):** holoprosencephaly, cleft lip and palate, polydactyly, scalp defects, severe prognosis.
- **Turner syndrome (45,X):** short stature, webbed neck, shield chest, lymphedema, **coarctation of the aorta** and bicuspid aortic valve, streak ovaries with primary amenorrhea, horseshoe kidney. Treat with growth hormone and estrogen replacement.
- **Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY):** tall stature, small firm testes, gynecomastia, infertility, learning difficulties; testosterone replacement.
- **22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge):** conotruncal heart defects, abnormal facies, thymic aplasia (T-cell deficiency), cleft palate and **hypocalcemia** from parathyroid hypoplasia.
- **Williams syndrome (7q11.23 deletion):** elfin facies, supravalvular aortic stenosis, hypercalcemia, friendly personality.

### Imprinting and repeat expansions

**Prader–Willi** (loss of the paternal 15q11–q13 contribution): neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding, then hyperphagia and obesity. **Angelman** (loss of the maternal contribution): ataxia, seizures, inappropriate laughter. **Fragile X** (CGG repeat expansion in *FMR1*, X-linked): intellectual disability, a long face, large ears, macroorchidism and autism features.

### Single-gene disorders

**Cystic fibrosis (CF)** is autosomal recessive (*CFTR* on chromosome 7; the ΔF508 variant is most common). Features: meconium ileus, recurrent pulmonary infections with *Pseudomonas*, pancreatic insufficiency with malabsorption, nasal polyps, salt-depleting sweat, and male infertility. Newborn screening uses immunoreactive trypsinogen, confirmed by **sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more**. Treatment is airway clearance, pancreatic enzymes, nutrition and CFTR modulators.

**Phenylketonuria (PKU)** is autosomal recessive deficiency of phenylalanine hydroxylase; untreated, it causes intellectual disability, a musty odor, and light pigmentation. Newborn screening allows early dietary phenylalanine restriction. Other screened conditions include congenital hypothyroidism, galactosemia and congenital adrenal hyperplasia (21-hydroxylase deficiency).

### Congenital heart disease

**Acyanotic** (left-to-right shunts): ventricular septal defect (most common), atrial septal defect, patent ductus arteriosus, atrioventricular septal defect. **Cyanotic:** tetralogy of Fallot (ventricular septal defect, overriding aorta, right ventricular outflow obstruction, right ventricular hypertrophy; boot-shaped heart; cyanotic "tet" spells relieved by squatting), **transposition of the great arteries** (cyanosis from birth, "egg on a string" heart, requires mixing), truncus arteriosus, tricuspid atresia and total anomalous pulmonary venous return. Lesions that depend on the ductus arteriosus for blood flow are kept open with **prostaglandin E1**; a patent ductus arteriosus in a preterm infant is closed with indomethacin or ibuprofen.

### Inheritance calculations

If both parents are carriers of an autosomal recessive condition, each child has a **1 in 4** chance of being affected, 1 in 2 of being an unaffected carrier, and 1 in 4 of being unaffected non-carrier; an unaffected sibling is a carrier with probability **2/3**. By the Hardy–Weinberg principle, if the disease incidence is $q^2$, the carrier frequency is $2pq \approx 2q$.

### Pyloric stenosis and fluids

Infantile hypertrophic pyloric stenosis causes projectile nonbilious vomiting and **hypochloremic, hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis**. Surgery (pyloromyotomy) is delayed until fluids and electrolytes are corrected with isotonic saline, with potassium once he urinates, and maintenance by weight.

@example
title: Carrier frequency from incidence
problem: Cystic fibrosis affects about 1 in 2500 newborns of European ancestry. Estimate the carrier frequency using the Hardy–Weinberg principle.
step: Incidence $q^2 = 1/2500 = 0.0004$, so $q = 0.02$.
step: Then $p = 1 - q = 0.98$.
step: Carrier frequency $2pq = 2 \times 0.98 \times 0.02 \approx 0.039$, or about 1 in 25.

@example
title: Risk for an unaffected sibling
problem: A couple has a child with an autosomal recessive condition. Their healthy older child asks about being a carrier. What is the probability that the healthy child is a carrier?
step: The parents are both obligate carriers; the four equally likely outcomes are unaffected non-carrier, two kinds of carrier, and affected.
step: Exclude the affected outcome because the sibling is healthy: three outcomes remain.
step: Two of three are carriers, so the probability is $2/3$.

@mistakes
- Using 1/2 instead of 2/3 for an unaffected sibling's carrier risk.
- Confusing Prader–Willi (paternal deletion) with Angelman syndrome (maternal deletion).
- Missing an associated cardiac or gastrointestinal defect in a child with trisomy 21.
- Giving oxygen alone to a ductal-dependent cyanotic neonate instead of prostaglandin E1.

prompt: Two healthy carriers of an autosomal recessive condition plan to have a child. What is the probability that the child is affected? Enter as a fraction.
answer: 1/4
check: 1/2 * 1/2
hint: Each parent passes the mutant allele with probability 1/2.
solution: The child is affected only if both parents pass the mutant allele: $1/2 \times 1/2 = 1/4$.

@quiz num
prompt: An autosomal recessive condition has an incidence of 1 in 10,000 newborns. Using the Hardy–Weinberg principle, what is the approximate carrier frequency (as a decimal)?
answer: 0.0198
check: 2 * 0.01 * (1 - 0.01)
tol: 0.002
hint: Incidence equals $q^2$, so first find $q$.
solution: $q = \sqrt{1/10000} = 0.01$, $p = 0.99$, and $2pq = 2 \times 0.99 \times 0.01 \approx 0.0198$, about 1 in 50.
