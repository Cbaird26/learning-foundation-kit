@article
Fever is the most common reason children are brought for care. Definition: temperature of **38.0 °C (100.4 °F) or higher**, measured rectally in young infants. The question is never just the number; it is the child's **age, appearance, immunization status and focus of infection**.

### Fever by age

- **Birth to 7 days, and any ill-appearing infant of any age:** outside the AAP 2021 febrile-infant guideline, which covers well-appearing infants aged 8 to 60 days. Manage as suspected sepsis: full evaluation, hospitalization and empiric parenteral antibiotics. Risk of invasive bacterial infection is highest in the youngest infants (group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, Listeria, herpes simplex virus).
- **8 to 21 days (well appearing):** blood culture, urinalysis and catheterized urine culture, inflammatory markers (procalcitonin, C-reactive protein, absolute neutrophil count) and **lumbar puncture** for every infant; hospitalize and start empiric parenteral antibiotics (typically ampicillin plus gentamicin, or ampicillin plus cefotaxime; ceftriaxone is avoided in the first weeks because of bilirubin displacement and calcium interactions). Add acyclovir if herpes is suspected (vesicles, seizures, mother with genital lesions, elevated transaminases, CSF pleocytosis).
- **22 to 28 days:** obtain urinalysis, urine and blood cultures and inflammatory markers. Lumbar puncture is recommended when the markers are abnormal and is otherwise at the clinician's discretion; infants with a positive urinalysis or abnormal markers are hospitalized and given parenteral antibiotics. Guidance for the normal-marker case is individualized, so check current references.
- **29 to 60 days:** urinalysis, blood culture and inflammatory markers risk-stratify the infant. Lumbar puncture is optional (considered when markers are abnormal); abnormal markers lead to hospitalization with parenteral antibiotics, a positive urinalysis with normal markers can be treated as a UTI with oral antibiotics and close follow-up, and a fully normal evaluation allows outpatient follow-up. Tools differ among guidelines and are updated, so check current references.
- **3 months to 3 years:** look for a source. Check for otitis media, pneumonia and especially **urinary tract infection**, which is the most common serious bacterial infection in febrile girls under 2 years and uncircumcised boys under 1 year. Vaccination against *Haemophilus influenzae* type b and pneumococcus has made occult bacteremia uncommon.

An ill-appearing child of any age needs a full evaluation and, if toxic or in shock, immediate empiric treatment.

### Common infections

**Acute otitis media (AOM):** a bulging tympanic membrane with middle ear effusion, or otorrhea, with acute onset. Treat with **high-dose amoxicillin (about 80–90 mg/kg/day divided twice daily; typical study dose)** for 10 days if under 2 years or severe; observation or a shorter course may be used in older children with mild disease. If amoxicillin was used in the last 30 days or there is concurrent purulent conjunctivitis, use amoxicillin-clavulanate.

**Streptococcal pharyngitis:** sudden sore throat, fever, tonsillar exudate, tender anterior cervical nodes and **no cough or rhinorrhea** (cough and coryza suggest viral infection). Test children over 3 years with a **rapid antigen detection test**; treat positives with penicillin V or amoxicillin for 10 days (amoxicillin 50 mg/kg once daily, maximum 1000 mg, is a typical study dose). Treatment prevents **acute rheumatic fever** but not post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.

**Urinary tract infection:** diagnose with urinalysis (pyuria, nitrites) and a culture obtained by catheterization or suprapubic aspiration in children not toilet trained. Treat for 7–10 days (oral cephalosporin or amoxicillin-clavulanate if well; IV if ill or under about 2 months). After a first febrile UTI in a child aged 2–24 months, obtain a **renal and bladder ultrasound**; voiding cystourethrography is reserved for abnormal imaging or recurrent infection.

**Meningitis:** fever, irritability, vomiting, bulging fontanelle (infants), neck stiffness and photophobia (older children). Obtain blood cultures and lumbar puncture; give dexamethasone and empiric ceftriaxone plus vancomycin without delaying treatment in suspected bacterial meningitis beyond 1 month of age.

**Kawasaki disease:** fever for **at least 5 days** plus at least four of five features: **bilateral nonexudative conjunctivitis**, **oral changes** (red cracked lips, strawberry tongue), **polymorphous rash**, **extremity changes** (swelling, erythema, later peeling), and **cervical lymphadenopathy** (at least 1.5 cm, usually unilateral). It is a medium-vessel vasculitis; the danger is **coronary artery aneurysm**. Treat with **intravenous immunoglobulin 2 g/kg** and aspirin and obtain echocardiography. Incomplete forms occur in infants.

**Exanthems:** measles (cough, coryza, conjunctivitis, Koplik spots, rash from head downward); roseola (3–5 days of high fever, then a rash as fever resolves; human herpesvirus 6); erythema infectiosum (parvovirus B19, "slapped cheek"); varicella (vesicles in crops); hand-foot-and-mouth disease (coxsackievirus).

### Antipyretics

Fever is not harmful in itself, and antipyretics treat comfort, not infection. Typical study doses: **acetaminophen 15 mg/kg per dose every 4–6 hours** (maximum 75 mg/kg/day and not more than 4 g/day); **ibuprofen 10 mg/kg per dose every 6–8 hours** (for infants 6 months and older). Aspirin is avoided in children with viral illness (Reye syndrome).

@example
title: Acetaminophen dose and volume
problem: A 16 kg child with fever is to receive acetaminophen at a typical study dose of 15 mg/kg. The liquid contains 160 mg per 5 mL. What dose in mg and in mL?
step: Dose in mg: $16 \times 15 = 240$ mg.
step: Concentration: $160 \text{ mg}/5 \text{ mL} = 32$ mg/mL.
step: Volume: $240/32 = 7.5$ mL.
step: Check the daily limit: the maximum is 75 mg/kg/day, which is 5 doses of 15 mg/kg, so give no more than 5 doses in 24 hours (and never more than 4 g/day in a large child).

@example
title: Febrile neonate
problem: A 3-week-old term boy has a rectal temperature of 38.2 °C and is feeding well and well appearing. What should be done?
step: At 21 days of age he is in the 8–21 day band of the AAP 2021 guideline, where every infant needs a lumbar puncture and admission regardless of appearance.
step: Obtain blood, urine (catheterized) and CSF for culture and cell counts, and send inflammatory markers.
step: Hospitalize and start parenteral antibiotics (ampicillin plus gentamicin or cefotaxime) after cultures, adding acyclovir if herpes is suspected.

@mistakes
- Sending a febrile 3-week-old home because he looks well.
- Treating a sore throat with cough and rhinorrhea as streptococcal pharyngitis.
- Using ceftriaxone in a hyperbilirubinemic neonate.
- Diagnosing UTI from a bag specimen; use catheterization or suprapubic aspiration for culture.
