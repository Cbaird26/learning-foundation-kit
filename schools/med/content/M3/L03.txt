@article
Cells are separated from their surroundings by a lipid bilayer that is permeable to small nonpolar molecules and water (partly through aquaporins), but not to ions and large polar molecules. Transport proteins let the cell control exactly what crosses.

### Passive transport

**Simple diffusion** needs no carrier. Net flux down a concentration gradient follows **Fick's law**:

$$J = -D\,A\,\frac{\Delta C}{\Delta x} = P\,A\,\Delta C,$$

where $D$ is the diffusion coefficient, $A$ the surface area, $\Delta x$ the membrane thickness and $P$ the permeability. Flux rises with a steeper gradient, a larger area and higher lipid solubility, and falls with thickness. This explains why pulmonary edema (thicker barrier) or emphysema (less area) impairs gas exchange.

**Facilitated diffusion** uses a channel or carrier but still moves solute down its gradient, with no energy. It shows **saturation** (a maximum rate, $V_{max}$) and specificity. GLUT4 in muscle and fat is insulin-dependent; GLUT1 and GLUT3 are not.

### Active transport

**Primary active transport** uses ATP directly to move solute against a gradient. The $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$-ATPase moves **3 Na$^+$ out and 2 K$^+$ in** per ATP, so it is electrogenic and creates the gradients that power nearly everything else. It is inhibited by ouabain and digoxin. Other primary pumps: $\text{Ca}^{2+}$-ATPases (SERCA, PMCA) and the gastric $\text{H}^+/\text{K}^+$-ATPase (blocked by proton pump inhibitors).

**Secondary active transport** uses the sodium gradient built by the pump. **Cotransport** (symport) moves solutes in the same direction, for example SGLT1 and SGLT2 (sodium-glucose), and the NKCC2 loop of Henle transporter (blocked by loop diuretics). **Countertransport** (antiport) moves them in opposite directions, for example the $\text{Na}^+/\text{Ca}^{2+}$ exchanger and the $\text{Na}^+/\text{H}^+$ exchanger.

### Osmosis, osmolarity and tonicity

**Osmosis** is net water movement across a semipermeable membrane toward higher solute concentration. The **osmotic pressure** needed to stop it is given by the van't Hoff equation:

$$\pi = i\,C\,R\,T,$$

with $C$ the molar concentration, $R = 0.08206$ L$\cdot$atm/(mol$\cdot$K), $T$ in kelvin (310 K at body temperature), and $i$ the number of particles per formula unit (NaCl is about 2 for osmolarity purposes). The concentration of particles is the **osmolarity** (mOsm/L); $C\cdot i$ gives it directly.

**Tonicity** is different: it is the effect of a solution on cell volume, and it depends only on **effective osmoles**, those that cannot cross the membrane. Urea is an *ineffective* osmole in most cells because it enters freely.

- **Isotonic**: no net water movement, cell volume unchanged.
- **Hypotonic**: water enters, cell swells.
- **Hypertonic**: water leaves, cell shrinks.

A solution can be isosmotic yet hypotonic. Red cells in 300 mOsm/L urea would swell and lyse, since urea enters and water follows.

### Clinical connections

Digoxin inhibits the $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$-ATPase, raising intracellular sodium, reducing $\text{Na}^+/\text{Ca}^{2+}$ exchanger extrusion of calcium, and increasing contractility (and, at toxic levels, causing hyperkalemia). SGLT2 inhibitors lower plasma glucose by blocking proximal tubular glucose reabsorption. Oral rehydration solution works because SGLT1 couples glucose and sodium uptake, which pulls water in.

@example
title: Osmolarity of 3% saline and its effect on cells
problem: 3% NaCl contains $30$ g/L. The molar mass of NaCl is $58.5$ g/mol. Calculate its osmolarity and state what it does to red blood cells.
step: Moles of NaCl per liter: $30/58.5 = 0.513$ mol/L.
step: Each NaCl gives two particles, so osmolarity $= 2 \times 0.513 = 1.026$ osmol/L $\approx 1{,}026$ mOsm/L.
step: This is far above the roughly 290 mOsm/L inside cells, and NaCl is an effective osmole, so the solution is hypertonic.
step: Water leaves the red cells and they shrink (crenate).

@mistakes
- Equating osmolarity with tonicity; only effective (non-penetrating) solutes determine tonicity.
- Saying the pump is electroneutral: it exports 3 positive charges and imports 2, so it is electrogenic.
- Forgetting that facilitated diffusion is passive and saturable, so it cannot move solute uphill.
