@article
A **chemical synapse** transmits a signal by released neurotransmitter, trading speed for flexibility: signals can be amplified, inhibited and modulated.

### Steps of transmission

1. An action potential depolarizes the presynaptic terminal.
2. **Voltage-gated Ca$^{2+}$ channels** (P/Q-type at the neuromuscular junction) open and Ca$^{2+}$ enters.
3. Ca$^{2+}$ binds **synaptotagmin** and triggers vesicle fusion through the **SNARE** proteins (synaptobrevin, syntaxin, SNAP-25), releasing transmitter by exocytosis.
4. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.
5. The signal is terminated by enzymatic breakdown, reuptake or diffusion.

Release is **quantal**: each vesicle holds a fixed amount of transmitter (a *quantum*), and the synaptic response is the sum of many quanta. The **quantal content** is

$$m = \frac{\text{mean end-plate potential (EPP)}}{\text{mean miniature end-plate potential (mEPP)}}.$$

### Excitatory and inhibitory synapses in the CNS

Glutamate acts on AMPA, NMDA and kainate receptors (ligand-gated cation channels), producing **EPSPs**. GABA (GABA$_A$ chloride channels) and glycine produce **IPSPs**, typically hyperpolarizing by increasing Cl$^-$ conductance. Postsynaptic potentials are graded and undergo **spatial and temporal summation** at the axon hillock; the neuron fires if the sum reaches threshold. Tetanus toxin blocks release of GABA and glycine from inhibitory interneurons, producing spastic paralysis; botulinum toxin blocks acetylcholine release, producing flaccid paralysis.

### The neuromuscular junction

The alpha motor neuron releases **acetylcholine (ACh)**. ACh binds the muscle **nicotinic receptor** (two $\alpha$ subunits plus $\beta$, $\delta$ and $\epsilon$ in the adult), a ligand-gated cation channel permeable to Na$^+$ and K$^+$ with a reversal potential near 0 mV. Net Na$^+$ entry depolarizes the end plate, producing the **end-plate potential**. Normally the EPP far exceeds the depolarization needed to reach threshold, so every motor neuron spike triggers a muscle action potential. The ratio is the **safety factor** (about 3 or more). **Acetylcholinesterase** in the cleft rapidly hydrolyzes ACh to acetate and choline, and choline is taken back up to make new ACh with choline acetyltransferase.

### Disorders and drugs

- **Myasthenia gravis:** autoantibodies against the postsynaptic nicotinic receptor reduce receptor number, so the EPP falls and the safety factor approaches 1. Presentation: fatigable weakness worse with use and late in the day, ptosis, diplopia, bulbar symptoms; association with thymic hyperplasia or thymoma. Repetitive nerve stimulation shows a **decremental** response because each stimulus releases a little less ACh. Treatment: acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (pyridostigmine), immunosuppression, thymectomy; myasthenic crisis is respiratory failure.
- **Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome:** autoantibodies against presynaptic P/Q Ca$^{2+}$ channels reduce release. Proximal weakness improves briefly with exertion, hyporeflexia, autonomic dryness; often paraneoplastic (small-cell lung cancer). High-frequency stimulation produces an **incremental** response.
- **Botulism:** toxin cleaves SNARE proteins, so there is no ACh release (descending flaccid paralysis, dilated pupils, dry mouth).
- **Organophosphate poisoning:** irreversible acetylcholinesterase inhibition, so ACh accumulates (miosis, salivation, lacrimation, bronchorrhea, bradycardia, diarrhea, fasciculations). Treat with atropine for muscarinic effects and pralidoxime to reactivate the enzyme.
- **Neuromuscular blockers:** *nondepolarizing* agents (rocuronium, vecuronium) compete with ACh and are reversed by neostigmine or sugammadex. *Depolarizing* succinylcholine opens the channel and keeps the end plate depolarized (fasciculations, then paralysis); it can cause hyperkalemia in patients with burns, denervation or prolonged immobilization and can trigger malignant hyperthermia.

@example
title: Quantal content
problem: At a neuromuscular junction, the mean miniature end-plate potential is $0.4$ mV and the mean end-plate potential evoked by a nerve impulse is $24$ mV. Estimate the quantal content.
step: The mEPP is the response to one vesicle, the quantum: $0.4$ mV.
step: Divide the evoked EPP by the quantum: $24/0.4 = 60$.
step: About 60 vesicles are released per impulse, which gives a large safety factor.

@example
title: Why myasthenia causes fatigable weakness
problem: A normal EPP is $36$ mV and a depolarization of $12$ mV is needed to reach threshold. In myasthenia gravis, only $30\%$ of receptors remain. Does the muscle fire?
step: The safety factor normally is $36/12 = 3$.
step: With $30\%$ of receptors, the EPP falls to about $0.30 \times 36 = 10.8$ mV.
step: $10.8$ mV is less than $12$ mV, so the muscle fiber fails to fire. With repeated use, ACh release falls a little, so more fibers drop below threshold and strength declines: fatigable weakness.

@mistakes
- Confusing myasthenia gravis (postsynaptic, decremental response) with Lambert-Eaton (presynaptic, incremental response).
- Treating organophosphate poisoning as an anticholinergic syndrome, when it is excessive cholinergic stimulation.
- Giving succinylcholine to a patient with extensive burns or denervation without considering hyperkalemia.
