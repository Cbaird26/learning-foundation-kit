@article
Skeletal muscle is organized into fascicles of long, multinucleated fibers. Each fiber is packed with **myofibrils**, which are chains of **sarcomeres**, the contractile unit. Contraction is a sliding of filaments, not a shortening of them.

### The sarcomere

- **Thick filaments** are myosin; **thin filaments** are actin with **tropomyosin** and the **troponin** complex (troponin T binds tropomyosin, troponin I inhibits, troponin C binds Ca$^{2+}$).
- The **Z disc** bounds a sarcomere. The **A band** is the length of the thick filaments and stays constant. The **I band** (thin filaments only) and the **H zone** (thick filaments only, at the center) both shrink in contraction, and the sarcomere shortens.

### The cross-bridge cycle

1. **Attached (rigor):** myosin head is bound to actin with no nucleotide.
2. **ATP binds** myosin, which releases actin.
3. **ATP hydrolysis** to ADP and P$_i$ re-cocks the head into a high-energy position.
4. The head binds actin again (only if Ca$^{2+}$ has exposed the binding site).
5. **P$_i$ release** drives the **power stroke**, then ADP is released.

Because ATP is needed to detach, ATP depletion after death leaves the heads locked: **rigor mortis**.

### Excitation-contraction (EC) coupling

1. The motor neuron's ACh produces an end-plate potential and a muscle action potential.
2. The action potential travels down the **T-tubules**.
3. The voltage sensor **DHPR** (L-type Ca$^{2+}$ channel, Cav1.1) changes conformation and, through direct mechanical coupling, opens the **ryanodine receptor RyR1** in the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR).
4. Ca$^{2+}$ floods the cytosol and binds **troponin C**; tropomyosin moves off the myosin binding sites.
5. Cross-bridges cycle and the muscle shortens.
6. **SERCA** pumps Ca$^{2+}$ back into the SR (using ATP), troponin C releases Ca$^{2+}$, and the muscle relaxes.

Skeletal muscle EC coupling does **not** require extracellular Ca$^{2+}$. **Malignant hyperthermia** (an inherited *RYR1* variant) causes uncontrolled Ca$^{2+}$ release after volatile anesthetics or succinylcholine: hypermetabolism, rigidity, hyperthermia, hypercarbia, rhabdomyolysis. Treatment is **dantrolene** (blocks RyR1), cooling and stopping the trigger.

### Whole-muscle mechanics

- **Length-tension:** active force is maximal at an optimal sarcomere length of about $2.0$-$2.2\ \mu\text{m}$, where overlap of thick and thin filaments is greatest. At long lengths (about $3.6\ \mu\text{m}$) overlap is minimal and force approaches zero. Passive tension from titin rises when stretched.
- **Force-velocity:** the faster a muscle shortens, the less force it produces; power $= \text{force} \times \text{velocity}$ peaks at intermediate loads.
- **Motor unit:** one motor neuron and all the fibers it innervates. Small units are recruited first (**size principle**), allowing graded force.
- **Summation and tetanus:** a single action potential causes a twitch. Rapid repeated stimulation summates because Ca$^{2+}$ remains elevated; at high frequencies the twitches fuse into **tetanus**. Skeletal muscle can tetanize because its action potential is brief relative to the twitch.
- **Fiber types:** type I (slow oxidative, many mitochondria, fatigue-resistant, myoglobin-rich), type IIa (fast oxidative-glycolytic), type IIx (fast glycolytic, high force, fatigues quickly).

### Energy

Resting ATP stores last only seconds. Phosphocreatine regenerates ATP (about 10 seconds of maximal effort), anaerobic glycolysis supports 1-2 minutes of intense work, and oxidative phosphorylation sustains prolonged effort.

@example
title: Total shortening of a fiber
problem: A muscle fiber has $10{,}000$ sarcomeres in series. Each sarcomere shortens from $2.2\ \mu\text{m}$ to $1.8\ \mu\text{m}$. How much does the fiber shorten, in mm?
step: Each sarcomere shortens by $2.2 - 1.8 = 0.4\ \mu\text{m}$.
step: Sarcomeres in series add their shortening: $10{,}000 \times 0.4\ \mu\text{m} = 4{,}000\ \mu\text{m}$.
step: $4{,}000\ \mu\text{m} = 4$ mm.
step: Note that the A band length was unchanged; only the overlap changed.

@mistakes
- Saying that the thick or thin filaments shorten; they slide and the A band stays constant.
- Believing that ATP is only needed for the power stroke: ATP binding releases the head from actin, which is why rigor mortis occurs.
- Thinking skeletal muscle needs extracellular Ca$^{2+}$ for contraction (cardiac muscle does, to a degree).

choice*: A band

solution: The A band is the length of the thick filaments, which do not shorten. The I band, H zone and Z-to-Z distance all decrease as filaments slide.

@quiz mc
prompt: In an isolated muscle experiment, a fiber is stretched until its sarcomeres reach $3.6\ \mu\text{m}$, and active tension on stimulation is almost zero. What is the best explanation?
choice: The sarcomere has run out of ATP.
choice: Ca$^{2+}$ is no longer released by the sarcoplasmic reticulum.
choice*: There is minimal overlap between thick and thin filaments, so few cross-bridges can form.
choice: The A band has lengthened beyond the thick filaments.
hint: Active force depends on the number of cross-bridges.
solution: According to the length-tension relationship, active force is proportional to the overlap of thick and thin filaments. At very long sarcomere lengths overlap is nearly absent, so few cross-bridges can form. The A band length does not change.
