@article
At rest, the inside of a neuron or muscle cell is about $-70$ to $-90$ mV relative to the outside. The cause is the combination of **ion concentration gradients** (set up by the $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$-ATPase) and **selective permeability** (the membrane at rest is far more permeable to $\text{K}^+$ than to $\text{Na}^+$ through leak channels).

### Typical concentrations

| Ion | Extracellular (mM) | Intracellular (mM) |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{Na}^+$ | 145 | 12 |
| $\text{K}^+$ | 4 | 140 |
| $\text{Cl}^-$ | 110 | 10 |
| $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ | 2 (free about 1.2) | about 0.0001 |

### The Nernst equation

The **equilibrium potential** of one ion is the membrane potential at which the electrical force exactly balances the chemical gradient, so net flux is zero:

$$E_X = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i} \approx \frac{61.5\ \text{mV}}{z}\log_{10}\frac{[X]_o}{[X]_i}\quad (37\,^{\circ}\text{C}).$$

Remember to include the valence $z$ with its sign. For K$^+$ with 4 mM outside and 140 mM inside: $E_K = 61.5\log(4/140) \approx -95$ mV. For Na$^+$: $E_{Na} \approx +66$ mV. For Cl$^-$ ($z=-1$): $E_{Cl} = -61.5\log(110/10) \approx -64$ mV (Cl$^-$ is close to the resting potential in many neurons and is regulated by transporters such as KCC2).

### The Goldman-Hodgkin-Katz equation

The membrane potential with several permeant ions is a **permeability-weighted** average:

$$V_m = 61.5\log\frac{P_K[K]_o + P_{Na}[Na]_o + P_{Cl}[Cl]_i}{P_K[K]_i + P_{Na}[Na]_i + P_{Cl}[Cl]_o}.$$

If $P_{Na}/P_K = 0.04$ and chloride is ignored, $V_m \approx -71$ mV. The resting potential is close to $E_K$ because $P_K$ dominates, yet slightly less negative because of a small sodium leak. During the action potential, $P_{Na}$ transiently exceeds $P_K$ and $V_m$ moves toward $E_{Na}$. Notice that the membrane potential is **not** an equilibrium: ions leak continuously and the pump restores the gradients. The pump's direct electrogenic contribution is only a few millivolts.

### Extracellular potassium and excitability

Because $E_K$ depends on the ratio $[K]_o/[K]_i$, changes in plasma potassium have an outsized effect on the resting potential:

- **Hyperkalemia** (for example $[K]_o$ rising from 4 to 7 mM) makes $E_K$ less negative (to about $-80$ mV), so $V_m$ **depolarizes**. Initially the cell is closer to threshold and excitability rises. With sustained depolarization, voltage-gated sodium channels remain **inactivated** (the inactivation gate stays closed), so cells cannot fire and excitability falls. Clinically: muscle weakness, peaked T waves, widened QRS and eventually arrhythmias.
- **Hypokalemia** makes $E_K$ more negative and hyperpolarizes the cell, which moves it away from threshold, causing weakness and ileus (although cardiac repolarization is also impaired, with arrhythmia risk).
- **Calcium** has a different mechanism: extracellular $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ screens surface charge and shifts the threshold. Hypocalcemia lowers threshold, causing hyperexcitability (tetany, perioral paresthesias, Chvostek and Trousseau signs). IV calcium in hyperkalemia does not lower potassium; it **stabilizes the membrane** by raising the threshold, which is why it is given first for ECG changes (insulin with glucose, beta-agonists, diuretics, binders and dialysis then lower potassium).

@example
title: Nernst potential for potassium in hyperkalemia
problem: A cell has $[K]_i = 140$ mM. Calculate $E_K$ at a plasma $[K]_o$ of $4$ mM and of $7$ mM, and state the effect on resting potential.
step: At $4$ mM: $E_K = 61.5\log(4/140) = 61.5 \times (-1.544) \approx -95$ mV.
step: At $7$ mM: $E_K = 61.5\log(7/140) = 61.5 \times (-1.301) \approx -80$ mV.
step: $E_K$ becomes about $15$ mV less negative, and since $V_m$ lies near $E_K$, the resting potential depolarizes by a similar amount (a bit less, because of the sodium leak).
step: This brings the cell nearer threshold initially, but prolonged depolarization inactivates Na$^+$ channels and reduces excitability.

@mistakes
- Dropping the sign of $z$ for chloride, which flips the answer's sign.
- Thinking the resting potential equals $E_K$ exactly; the small $P_{Na}$ leak makes it somewhat less negative.
- Saying hyperkalemia "hyperpolarizes" the cell because potassium is "high": the gradient shrinks, so the cell depolarizes.
