@article
Exercise can raise whole-body oxygen consumption more than tenfold. The cardiovascular, respiratory and muscle systems cooperate to deliver oxygen and remove carbon dioxide, heat and metabolites.

### Oxygen uptake and the Fick principle

$$\dot V_{O_2} = \text{CO} \times (C_aO_2 - C_vO_2).$$

Oxygen uptake equals cardiac output times the arteriovenous oxygen content difference. At rest, $\text{CO} \approx 5$ L/min and the difference is about $50$ mL O$_2$ per liter of blood (5 mL/dL), giving about $250$ mL/min. In maximal exercise, both terms rise: cardiac output to 20-25 L/min in untrained adults (35 L/min or more in elite endurance athletes) and the arteriovenous difference to about $150$ mL/L (15 mL/dL) as muscle extracts more oxygen. **$\dot V_{O_2\max}$** is the maximal oxygen uptake, about 35-45 mL/kg/min in young sedentary adults and over 70 in elite endurance athletes.

### Cardiovascular response

- **Heart rate** rises through vagal withdrawal at first and sympathetic activation at higher intensities, in rough proportion to work and oxygen uptake. A common estimate of maximum is $220 - \text{age}$ (beats/min), with substantial individual variation.
- **Stroke volume** rises (more venous return from the muscle pump and sympathetic venoconstriction, plus greater contractility), typically plateauing at about 40-60% of $\dot V_{O_2\max}$ in untrained people.
- **Cardiac output** therefore rises by about four to five times in untrained people.
- **Regional flow:** blood flow to working muscle rises from about 20% of cardiac output at rest to as much as 80% in heavy exercise. **Local metabolites** (adenosine, K$^+$, H$^+$, CO$_2$, lactate, NO, osmolarity) cause arteriolar vasodilation (**active hyperemia**), overriding sympathetic vasoconstriction. Flow to the splanchnic and renal beds falls by sympathetic vasoconstriction, and skin flow rises for heat loss, then may fall at maximal effort.
- **Blood pressure:** systolic pressure rises with cardiac output; diastolic pressure stays about the same or falls slightly, because total peripheral resistance falls with muscle vasodilation. The pulse pressure widens, and mean arterial pressure rises moderately.

### Respiratory response

Minute ventilation rises almost in proportion to $\dot V_{CO_2}$ and oxygen uptake, from about 5-8 L/min to over 100 L/min. It is driven by central command from the motor cortex, input from muscle and joint proprioceptors, and chemoreceptors. In **moderate** exercise, $P_aO_2$, $P_aCO_2$ and pH remain nearly constant, so the signal is not simply blood gas changes. Above the **ventilatory (lactate) threshold**, lactic acid accumulates, buffered by bicarbonate to produce extra CO$_2$, and ventilation rises disproportionately, with a fall in $P_aCO_2$ (respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis). Oxygen diffusion capacity increases because more capillaries are recruited, and ventilation-perfusion matching improves.

### Metabolic response

At the start, ATP comes from stored ATP and phosphocreatine, then from anaerobic glycolysis (producing lactate), and from aerobic metabolism of muscle glycogen, blood glucose and fatty acids. With longer exercise, fat use rises and glycogen depletion produces fatigue. Epinephrine, glucagon and cortisol rise, and insulin falls, while muscle contraction increases GLUT4-mediated glucose uptake independent of insulin. Core temperature rises and sweating increases.

### Training adaptations

Endurance training increases mitochondrial density, capillary density, stroke volume and blood volume (and so $\dot V_{O_2\max}$), lowers resting and submaximal heart rate, and increases reliance on fat. Strength training causes muscle fiber hypertrophy.

### Clinical connections

**McArdle disease** (myophosphorylase deficiency) causes exercise intolerance, early fatigue, cramps and myoglobinuria, with a flat lactate response to ischemic exercise and a "second wind" after a rest, as blood-borne glucose and fatty acids are mobilized. **Rhabdomyolysis** after intense exercise causes pigmenturia, elevated creatine kinase and risk of acute kidney injury.

@example
title: Oxygen uptake at rest and at relative intensity
problem: At rest, cardiac output is $5$ L/min and the arteriovenous O$_2$ difference is $50$ mL/L. Calculate $\dot V_{O_2}$. A runner's maximal uptake is $3{,}500$ mL/min and she exercises at $2{,}100$ mL/min. What percentage of $\dot V_{O_2\max}$ is that?
step: By the Fick principle, $\dot V_{O_2} = 5 \times 50 = 250$ mL/min.
step: Relative intensity $= 2{,}100/3{,}500 = 0.60$.
step: She is exercising at $60\%$ of $\dot V_{O_2\max}$.

@mistakes
- Saying that diastolic pressure rises greatly in dynamic exercise; muscle vasodilation keeps it flat or lower, and a large diastolic rise should prompt concern.
- Believing that exercise hyperpnea is driven mainly by falling $P_aO_2$ or rising $P_aCO_2$; in moderate exercise those are unchanged.
- Mixing units in the Fick equation: use mL O$_2$ per liter with L/min, or mL per dL with dL/min.
