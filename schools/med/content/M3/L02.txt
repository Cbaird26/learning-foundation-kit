@article
Roughly 60% of an adult man's body weight is water (about 50% in an adult woman, who has proportionally more fat; infants about 70-75%, older adults less). This **total body water (TBW)** is divided by the cell membrane and by the capillary wall.

### The compartments

For a 70 kg man (TBW $= 0.6 \times 70 = 42$ L):

| Compartment | Fraction of body weight | Fraction of TBW | Volume |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intracellular fluid (ICF) | 40% | 2/3 | 28 L |
| Extracellular fluid (ECF) | 20% | 1/3 | 14 L |
| Interstitial fluid | 15% | 3/4 of ECF | 10.5 L |
| Plasma | 5% | 1/4 of ECF | 3.5 L |

"60-40-20" and "rule of thirds, then quarters" are the mnemonics.

### Measuring volumes: indicator dilution

A marker is injected, allowed to mix, and its concentration measured:

$$V = \frac{\text{amount injected} - \text{amount lost}}{\text{concentration}}.$$

Radiolabeled albumin or Evans blue measure plasma volume; inulin, mannitol or sulfate measure ECF; deuterium oxide or antipyrine measure TBW. Interstitial volume $=$ ECF $-$ plasma, and ICF $=$ TBW $-$ ECF; these two cannot be measured directly.

### Osmolarity and what moves water

Water moves freely across cell membranes and capillary walls to equalize **osmolality**. Because ECF and ICF are in osmotic equilibrium (about 285-295 mOsm/kg), a change in the total solute of one compartment moves water between them. Plasma osmolarity is estimated by

$$\text{Osm} \approx 2[\text{Na}^+] + \frac{\text{glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8}$$

with sodium in mEq/L and glucose and BUN in mg/dL; the factor 2 accounts for accompanying anions. Sodium is the dominant extracellular solute, and **effective osmoles** such as sodium and glucose (in the absence of insulin) hold water in a compartment, while urea crosses membranes freely.

### Predicting shifts after an infusion

Follow two rules: (1) the added solute stays where it cannot cross, and (2) water then equilibrates until osmolalities match.

- **Isotonic saline (0.9% NaCl, about 154 mEq/L Na and Cl):** sodium is confined to the ECF, so the ECF expands by the full volume, with no change in osmolality and no change in ICF. About 3/4 of it enters interstitium and 1/4 stays in plasma.
- **Free water (including 5% dextrose in water, D5W):** the dextrose is metabolized, leaving free water that distributes over the whole TBW, 2/3 into ICF and 1/3 into ECF. Only $\tfrac{1}{3}\times\tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{12}$ of the volume stays in plasma, and osmolality falls in all compartments. D5W is thus a poor resuscitation fluid.
- **Hypertonic saline (3% NaCl):** sodium stays extracellular, raising ECF osmolality, so water leaves the cells. ECF expands by more than the volume infused and ICF shrinks.

These simple predictions assume no urinary loss and full equilibration. In real patients, Starling forces, kidney function and capillary permeability (as in sepsis) modify them.

@example
title: One liter of saline versus one liter of D5W
problem: A 70 kg man (TBW $42$ L, ICF $28$ L, ECF $14$ L) receives $1$ L of 0.9% NaCl, and on another day $1$ L of D5W. Compare the changes in ICF and plasma volume. Assume equilibration, no urine output, and that the dextrose is fully metabolized.
step: Saline is isotonic and its sodium stays extracellular. ICF change is $0$, ECF increases by $1$ L.
step: Plasma gets one quarter of the ECF gain: $1 \times \tfrac{1}{4} = 0.25$ L.
step: D5W leaves $1$ L of free water that spreads over TBW. ICF gains $\tfrac{2}{3}$ L $= 0.67$ L and ECF gains $\tfrac{1}{3}$ L $= 0.33$ L.
step: Plasma gains $\tfrac{1}{4}\times 0.33 = 0.083$ L, about 83 mL, a third of saline's effect.

@mistakes
- Assuming that isotonic saline raises ICF; because sodium is confined to the ECF, only the ECF expands.
- Forgetting that dextrose is metabolized, so D5W behaves as free water despite being roughly isotonic in the bag.
- Using the wrong fraction: plasma is 1/4 of ECF (5% of body weight), not 1/4 of TBW.

@quiz mc
prompt: A hypovolemic 45-year-old man is given only 2 L of 5% dextrose in water. Why is this a poor choice for expanding intravascular volume?
choice: Dextrose is not metabolized, so it remains in the vascular space.
choice: Water cannot cross the capillary wall.
choice*: The metabolized dextrose leaves free water that distributes across TBW, so only about one twelfth remains in plasma.
choice: Free water is confined to the interstitial space.
hint: What happens to the dextrose after it enters the cells?
solution: Free water spreads over TBW, with about two thirds into cells and one third into ECF, of which one quarter is plasma. Only about $\tfrac{1}{12}$ of the volume stays intravascular, which is why isotonic crystalloid is used for resuscitation.
