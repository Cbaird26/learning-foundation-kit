@article
Core temperature (about $37\,^{\circ}\text{C}$, normal range roughly $36.5$-$37.5\,^{\circ}\text{C}$) is held within narrow limits because enzymes, membrane function and neuron excitability are temperature-sensitive. Skin and shell temperatures vary much more.

### The control system

- **Sensors:** peripheral thermoreceptors in skin and deep tissues, and central thermoreceptors in the **preoptic area of the anterior hypothalamus**, which is the most important sensor.
- **Integrator:** the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
- **Effectors:** vessels, sweat glands, skeletal muscle, brown fat, behavior.

**Heat loss mechanisms.** Warmth triggers **cutaneous vasodilation** (reduced sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone) and **sweating** (sympathetic cholinergic fibers acting on muscarinic receptors). Heat leaves the body by:

- **radiation** (largest share at rest in a comfortable room),
- **conduction and convection** (to air or water in contact with skin; water conducts far faster than air),
- **evaporation** (the only mechanism that works when the environment is warmer than the skin; about $0.58$ kcal per mL of water evaporated).

**Heat gain and production.** Cold triggers **cutaneous vasoconstriction** (sympathetic), **shivering** (involuntary rhythmic muscle contraction, controlled by the posterior hypothalamus), **non-shivering thermogenesis** (norepinephrine, thyroid hormone, and **brown fat**, where **UCP1** uncouples oxidative phosphorylation and releases energy as heat, important in infants), and behavior.

### Fever versus hyperthermia

In **fever**, pyrogens (exogenous such as bacterial lipopolysaccharide; endogenous cytokines IL-1, IL-6, TNF-$\alpha$) act on the hypothalamus through **prostaglandin E$_2$** (via COX-2) to **raise the set point**. The patient feels cold and shivers until the new set point is reached. Antipyretics (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) lower the set point by inhibiting prostaglandin synthesis. In **hyperthermia**, the set point is unchanged, but heat production or heat loss fails: **heat stroke** (core temperature above $40\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ with CNS dysfunction), malignant hyperthermia, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, serotonin syndrome, thyrotoxicosis, anticholinergic toxicity. Antipyretics do not work in hyperthermia; the treatment is rapid external cooling (such as cold-water immersion in exertional heat stroke) plus management of the cause.

### Metabolism and energy

The **basal metabolic rate (BMR)** is about 1 kcal per kg per hour in a resting adult (about 1,700 kcal/day for a 70 kg person; thyroid hormone is the major determinant). Energy values: carbohydrate and protein about $4$ kcal/g, fat about $9$ kcal/g, ethanol about $7$ kcal/g. Oxygen consumption maps to energy: about **$4.8$ kcal per liter of O$_2$** for a mixed diet.

The **respiratory quotient (RQ)** is $\dot V_{CO_2}/\dot V_{O_2}$ at tissue level:

| Fuel | RQ |
|---|---|
| carbohydrate | 1.0 |
| protein | about 0.8 |
| fat | about 0.7 |
| typical mixed diet | about 0.8-0.85 |

An RQ above 1.0 suggests lipogenesis (overfeeding); an RQ near $0.7$ suggests fat oxidation (fasting).

### Heat balance calculations

The body behaves as if its specific heat were about $0.83$ kcal/kg/$^{\circ}$C, so storing heat $Q$ in a body of mass $m$ raises temperature by

$$\Delta T = \frac{Q}{0.83\,m}.$$

Evaporation of water removes about $0.58$ kcal/mL. **Q$_{10}$** describes how reaction rate changes with a $10\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ rise (typically 2-3), which is why metabolic rate rises about 10-13% for every $1\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ of fever.

@example
title: Interpreting a respiratory quotient
problem: A fasting patient has an oxygen consumption of $250$ mL/min and a CO$_2$ production of $175$ mL/min. Calculate RQ and interpret it.
step: $\text{RQ} = 175/250 = 0.70$.
step: This is the RQ of fat oxidation.
step: In prolonged fasting, glycogen is depleted and the body relies mainly on fat (and ketones), so a low RQ is expected.

@example
title: Heat stored and temperature rise
problem: A $70$ kg person stores $100$ kcal of heat, with no heat loss. Estimate the temperature rise.
step: Use $\Delta T = Q/(0.83\,m)$.
step: $\Delta T = 100/(0.83 \times 70) = 100/58.1 = 1.72\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.
step: Small imbalances therefore add up quickly if heat loss mechanisms are blocked, such as in an exercising person in a hot, humid environment where sweat cannot evaporate.

@mistakes
- Treating a fever as heat stroke: in fever the set point rises and antipyretics help, while in hyperthermia the set point is normal and antipyretics do not.
- Forgetting that evaporation is the only route of heat loss when ambient temperature exceeds skin temperature, and that humidity limits it.
- Reading an RQ of $1.0$ as fat oxidation; fat gives $0.7$ and carbohydrate gives $1.0$.
