@article
**Homeostasis** is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, the fluid that bathes your cells, despite constant disturbances from outside and from the cells themselves. Variables such as arterial pressure, core temperature, plasma $[\text{Na}^+]$, $\text{pH}$, $P_{a}\text{CO}_2$ and glucose are held near a **set point**. Homeostasis is not a fixed value: it is a *dynamic* steady state in which a disturbance is detected and opposed.

### The parts of a control loop

Every regulated variable uses the same architecture:

- A **sensor** (receptor) measures the variable: baroreceptors in the carotid sinus and aortic arch, central chemoreceptors in the medulla, thermoreceptors, osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus.
- An **integrator** (control center) compares the signal with the set point: the nucleus tractus solitarius and medullary centers, the hypothalamus, an endocrine gland.
- An **effector** changes the variable: heart, vessels, kidney, sweat glands, skeletal muscle.

The difference between the measured value and the set point is the **error signal**.

### Negative feedback

In **negative feedback** the response opposes the original change. If arterial pressure rises, baroreceptor firing rises (via cranial nerves IX and X), the medulla increases vagal output and decreases sympathetic output, and heart rate, contractility and vascular tone fall, so pressure returns toward the set point. Most homeostatic loops are negative: plasma osmolality and ADH, calcium and parathyroid hormone, and thyroid hormones acting back on TSH.

Negative feedback never corrects the error completely, because a nonzero error is what drives the correction. The strength of a loop is its **gain**:

$$\text{gain} = \frac{\text{correction}}{\text{remaining error}} .$$

If a disturbance would shift a variable by $D$ with no control, a loop with gain $G$ leaves a residual error

$$E = \frac{D}{1 + G}.$$

A gain of $-4$ is often written for the baroreflex-type loop: the sign shows the opposition, the magnitude shows strength. A high gain means a tightly regulated variable.

### Positive feedback

In **positive feedback** the response amplifies the change. It is rare, usually explosive, and ends with a terminating event. Examples: the upstroke of the action potential (depolarization opens more $\text{Na}^+$ channels), the LH surge produced by high estradiol before ovulation, oxytocin release as the cervix stretches in labor (ended by delivery), and platelet plug and clotting cascade amplification. Positive feedback in disease, such as the cycle of decompensated shock, is a vicious circle.

### Feedforward and set point changes

**Feedforward** control anticipates a disturbance: the cephalic phase of gastric secretion before food arrives, or the rise in heart rate and ventilation at the start of exercise before metabolites accumulate. A **set point can be reset**: in fever, pyrogens raise the hypothalamic set point so the body defends a higher temperature; in chronic hypertension, baroreceptors reset to a higher pressure.

### Clinical meaning

Disease often represents failure of a loop. If the sensor, integrator or effector fails, or the disturbance overwhelms a finite compensatory capacity, the variable drifts. Early compensation can mask a serious problem: a patient losing blood may keep a normal pressure for a time because the baroreflex raises heart rate and vascular tone, so tachycardia comes first.

@example
title: Gain of the baroreflex-type loop
problem: An uncontrolled blood loss would lower mean arterial pressure by $36$ mmHg. With the baroreflex intact, pressure falls by only $9$ mmHg. Calculate the gain of the loop.
step: The remaining error is the fall that is still present: $9$ mmHg.
step: The correction is the part of the disturbance that the reflex removed: $36 - 9 = 27$ mmHg.
step: Gain = correction divided by remaining error: $27/9 = 3$.
step: Check with $E = D/(1+G)$: $36/(1+3) = 9$ mmHg, matching the observed fall.

@example
title: Residual error with a known gain
problem: A heat load would raise core temperature by $2.4\,^{\circ}\text{C}$ without thermoregulation. The loop has a gain of $5$. By how much does temperature actually rise?
step: Use $E = D/(1+G)$ with $D = 2.4$ and $G = 5$.
step: $E = 2.4/6 = 0.4\,^{\circ}\text{C}$.
step: A higher gain would leave an even smaller residual error, but never exactly zero.

@mistakes
- Calling any response that restores normal "positive" because it is helpful: the sign of feedback refers to opposing (negative) or amplifying (positive) the change, not to whether the result is good.
- Thinking negative feedback returns the variable exactly to the set point; a residual error always remains.
- Treating a fever as failed homeostasis: it is a deliberate rise in the set point, not a failure to defend the old one.

choice*: Increased vagal and decreased sympathetic outflow from the medulla
choice: Decreased vagal and increased sympathetic outflow from the medulla

hint: Negative feedback must oppose the rise in pressure.

solution: To lower pressure, the medulla increases parasympathetic (vagal) output to the heart and withdraws sympathetic tone, lowering heart rate, contractility and vascular resistance. The opposite pattern would raise pressure further.

prompt: A hemorrhage would lower mean arterial pressure by $40$ mmHg if there were no reflexes. With reflexes intact the fall is only $8$ mmHg. What is the feedback gain (use the magnitude)?
answer: 4
check: (40 - 8) / 8
tol: 0.01
hint: Gain is correction divided by remaining error.
hint: The correction is the part of the fall that the reflexes prevented.
solution: Correction $= 40 - 8 = 32$ mmHg; remaining error $= 8$ mmHg; gain $= 32/8 = 4$.
