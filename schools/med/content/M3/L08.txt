@article
Smooth and cardiac muscle share with skeletal muscle the sliding of actin and myosin, but they differ in how they raise Ca$^{2+}$, how the signal is sensed, and how the muscle is excited.

### Smooth muscle

Smooth muscle lines vessels, airways, the gut, bladder and uterus. It has no sarcomeres or troponin; actin and myosin are arranged obliquely, anchored to dense bodies.

**Excitation-contraction coupling:**

1. Ca$^{2+}$ rises from entry through L-type channels and from the SR via **IP$_3$ receptors** (Gq pathways: for example $\alpha_1$, $M_3$ and angiotensin II receptors).
2. Ca$^{2+}$ binds **calmodulin**.
3. Ca$^{2+}$-calmodulin activates **myosin light chain kinase (MLCK)**, which phosphorylates the myosin regulatory light chain, allowing cross-bridge cycling.
4. **Myosin light chain phosphatase (MLCP)** dephosphorylates myosin, ending contraction. Rho kinase inhibits MLCP (calcium sensitization).

**Relaxation:** NO activates guanylyl cyclase, raising cGMP and PKG, which lowers Ca$^{2+}$ and activates MLCP. Beta$_2$ stimulation raises cAMP, which inhibits MLCK. Sildenafil inhibits PDE5 and so increases cGMP. Smooth muscle can sustain force economically in the **latch state** and responds to stretch, hormones, local factors and autonomic nerves. **Single-unit** (visceral) smooth muscle is coupled by gap junctions and shows pacemaker slow waves; **multi-unit** smooth muscle (iris, ciliary muscle, vas deferens) is controlled fiber by fiber.

### Cardiac muscle

Cardiac myocytes are striated with sarcomeres and troponin, connected by intercalated discs and gap junctions so they function as a syncytium.

**Calcium-induced calcium release:** during the plateau (phase 2), Ca$^{2+}$ enters via L-type channels and triggers the **RyR2** receptor in the SR to release much more Ca$^{2+}$. The calcium binds troponin C. For relaxation, Ca$^{2+}$ is taken up by **SERCA** (regulated by phospholamban) and extruded by the $\text{Na}^+/\text{Ca}^{2+}$ exchanger. Contraction strength depends on Ca$^{2+}$ availability, which is why extracellular Ca$^{2+}$ and the Na$^+$ gradient matter.

**Beta$_1$-adrenergic stimulation** raises cAMP and PKA, which phosphorylates L-type channels (more Ca$^{2+}$ entry) and phospholamban (faster SERCA uptake). The result is greater contractility (**inotropy**) and faster relaxation (**lusitropy**).

**The long plateau** (about 200-300 ms in ventricular muscle) gives a long refractory period, so cardiac muscle cannot undergo tetanus: it must relax and refill. The sinoatrial node fires spontaneously because of the "funny" current $I_f$ (HCN channels) and slow depolarization in phase 4.

### Comparing the three

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Striated | yes | yes | no |
| Ca$^{2+}$ sensor | troponin C | troponin C | calmodulin (MLCK) |
| Trigger for SR release | DHPR mechanically coupled to RyR1 | extracellular Ca$^{2+}$ entry opens RyR2 (CICR) | IP$_3$ (and some Ca$^{2+}$ entry) |
| Extracellular Ca$^{2+}$ needed | no | yes | yes |
| Initiation | motor neuron | pacemaker cells, gap junctions | nerves, hormones, stretch, pacemaker slow waves |
| Tetanus | yes | no | sustained, latch |

### Useful calculations

- **Stroke volume** $= \text{EDV} - \text{ESV}$; **ejection fraction** $= \text{SV}/\text{EDV}$ (normal $\approx 55$-$70\%$).
- **Cardiac output** $= \text{HR} \times \text{SV}$.
- **Laplace's law** (thin-walled sphere): wall stress $\sigma = Pr/(2h)$. A dilated ventricle (larger $r$) has more wall stress at the same pressure; hypertrophy (larger $h$) reduces it.

**Digoxin** inhibits the Na$^+$/K$^+$-ATPase, raising intracellular Na$^+$ and Ca$^{2+}$ and thus contractility.

@example
title: Ejection fraction and cardiac output
problem: A patient has an end-diastolic volume of $130$ mL, an end-systolic volume of $55$ mL and a heart rate of $72$ beats/min. Calculate the ejection fraction and cardiac output.
step: Stroke volume $= 130 - 55 = 75$ mL.
step: Ejection fraction $= 75/130 = 0.577$, or about $58\%$.
step: Cardiac output $= 72 \times 75 = 5{,}400$ mL/min $= 5.4$ L/min.
step: Both values are within the normal range.

@mistakes
- Saying that smooth muscle contains troponin; its Ca$^{2+}$ sensor is calmodulin, which activates MLCK.
- Saying that cardiac contraction does not need extracellular calcium: L-type channel entry triggers calcium-induced release.
- Forgetting that the long cardiac refractory period is the reason the heart cannot be tetanized.

@quiz mc
prompt: Why can the ventricle not be tetanized like skeletal muscle?
choice: Cardiac muscle has no troponin.
