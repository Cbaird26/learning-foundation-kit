@article
Constants used here: speed of light in vacuum $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, Planck constant $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$ (so $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$), $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$.

### What an electromagnetic wave is

Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field makes an electric field. Maxwell's insight was that a changing electric field also makes a magnetic field. Together they allow a self-sustaining **transverse wave** in which $\vec E$ and $\vec B$ oscillate perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. No medium is needed. In vacuum all electromagnetic waves travel at $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and obey

$$c = f\lambda.$$

The ratio of field amplitudes is $E_0/B_0 = c$. The wave carries energy and momentum. Polarization describes the direction of the $\vec E$ oscillation; polarizing sunglasses block light polarized in one direction, which reduces glare.

### Speed in a medium

In a transparent material light travels more slowly. The **refractive index** is $n = c/v \ge 1$. The frequency stays the same when the wave enters the material, so the wavelength shrinks: $\lambda_n = \lambda_{vac}/n$. Typical values: air $1.000$, water $1.33$, glass $1.50$.

### The spectrum

In order of increasing frequency: radio waves, microwaves, infrared, visible light ($\approx 400$ to $700\ \mathrm{nm}$), ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays. All are the same kind of wave; they differ in frequency, wavelength and how they interact with matter.

### Photons and ionization

At the atomic level the energy arrives in quanta, **photons**, each with energy

$$E = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda}.$$

Higher frequency means higher photon energy. Photons with energies of roughly $10\ \mathrm{eV}$ or more (far ultraviolet, X-rays, gamma rays) can remove electrons from atoms and break chemical bonds: this is **ionizing radiation**, the source of the cell damage and mutation risk discussed in the imaging lesson. Radio waves, microwaves, infrared and visible light photons have much too little energy to ionize, though they can heat tissue if the intensity is high enough. The distinction is photon energy, not total power: a very intense microwave beam does not become ionizing.

### Intensity

The **intensity** $I$ is power per unit area, in $\mathrm{W/m^2}$. A source of power $P$ radiating equally in all directions has $I = P/(4\pi r^2)$ at distance $r$, so intensity falls as $1/r^2$. For a plane wave $I = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$, where $E_0$ is the electric-field amplitude. Safety limits on radio-frequency exposure in MRI and in cell-phone use are expressed as absorbed power per unit mass, because heating is the established effect.

@example
title: A microwave oven photon
problem: A microwave oven operates at $2.45\ \mathrm{GHz}$. Find the wavelength in air and the energy of one photon in electron-volts, and say whether it is ionizing. Use $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$.
step: $\lambda = c/f = (3.00\times10^{8})/(2.45\times10^{9}) = 0.122\ \mathrm{m}$.
step: $E = hf = (6.626\times10^{-34})(2.45\times10^{9}) = 1.62\times10^{-24}\ \mathrm{J}$.
step: Convert: $E = 1.62\times10^{-24}/1.602\times10^{-19} \approx 1.0\times10^{-5}\ \mathrm{eV}$.
step: This is millions of times smaller than the roughly $10\ \mathrm{eV}$ needed to ionize atoms, so the radiation is non-ionizing; it heats by making water molecules rotate.

@mistakes
- Thinking wavelength stays the same when light enters water or glass; it is the frequency that stays the same.
- Believing a more intense low-frequency source (such as a radio transmitter) can ionize atoms; ionization depends on photon energy.
- Mixing nanometers with meters in $c = f\lambda$ and $E = hc/\lambda$.
- Treating the visible spectrum as separate from the rest of the electromagnetic spectrum; it is only a narrow band.

@practice num
prompt: An FM radio station broadcasts at $100\ \mathrm{MHz}$. Using $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, find the wavelength in meters.
answer: 3

check: 3.00e8 / 100e6
hint: Use $\lambda = c/f$ with $f = 1.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{Hz}$.
solution: $\lambda = 3.00\times10^{8}/1.00\times10^{8} = 3.0\ \mathrm{m}$.

@practice num
prompt: Green light has wavelength $550\ \mathrm{nm}$ in vacuum. Using $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$, find its frequency in hertz.
answer: 5.4545e14
tol: 1%
unit: Hz
check: 3.00e8 / 550e-9
hint: Convert $550\ \mathrm{nm}$ to meters.
solution: $f = c/\lambda = 3.00\times10^{8}/(550\times10^{-9}) = 5.45\times10^{14}\ \mathrm{Hz}$.

@practice num
prompt: Use $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}\ \mathrm{J\,s}$, $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $1\ \mathrm{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{J}$. Find the energy of a photon of wavelength $550\ \mathrm{nm}$, in electron-volts.
answer: 2.256

@practice num
prompt: A plane electromagnetic wave in air has electric-field amplitude $E_0 = 100\ \mathrm{V/m}$. Using $I = \tfrac12c\varepsilon_0E_0^2$ with $c = 3.00\times10^{8}\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$, find the intensity in $\mathrm{W/m^2}$.
answer: 13.28
tol: 1%
unit: W/m²
check: 0.5 * 3.00e8 * 8.854e-12 * 100**2

solution: $I = \tfrac12(3.00\times10^{8})(8.854\times10^{-12})(100)^2 = 13.3\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A small lamp radiates $100\ \mathrm{W}$ of light equally in all directions. Find the intensity at a distance of $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ from it, in $\mathrm{W/m^2}$.
answer: 1.989
tol: 1%
unit: W/m²
check: 100 / (4 * math.pi * 2.0**2)
hint: The power spreads over a sphere of area $4\pi r^2$.
solution: $I = 100/(4\pi \times 4.0) = 100/50.27 = 1.99\ \mathrm{W/m^2}$.
