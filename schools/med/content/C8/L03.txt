@article
Constants used here: elementary charge $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$. Values of resistivity are stated inside each problem.

### Current and resistance

**Current** is the rate at which charge crosses a surface: $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$, measured in amperes ($1\ \mathrm{A} = 1\ \mathrm{C/s}$). **Sign convention:** conventional current is the direction positive charge would move, from high potential toward low potential through a resistor, even when the real carriers are electrons. In body fluids the carriers are ions, and positive and negative ions moving opposite ways both contribute to conventional current in the same direction.

Many materials obey **Ohm's law**:

$$V = IR,$$

where $R$ is resistance in ohms ($1\ \Omega = 1\ \mathrm{V/A}$). For a uniform wire of length $L$ and cross-sectional area $A$, $R = \rho L/A$, where $\rho$ is the **resistivity** of the material. Thinner or longer conductors resist more. Body tissues are electrolytes, and their resistivities differ widely, which is why current takes paths of low resistance such as blood and nerve rather than fat or dry skin.

### Power

An element carrying current $I$ with voltage $V$ across it dissipates (or delivers) power

$$P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}, \qquad 1\ \mathrm{W} = 1\ \mathrm{J/s}.$$

Because $P = I^2R$, doubling the current through a fixed resistance quadruples the heating.

### Series and parallel

Resistors in **series** carry the same current and their resistances add: $R_{eq} = R_1 + R_2 + \cdots$. Resistors in **parallel** share the same voltage and their conductances add: $1/R_{eq} = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2 + \cdots$. A parallel combination is always smaller than its smallest member. The pattern is reversed for capacitors.

### Kirchhoff's rules

Two conservation laws handle any circuit.

- **Junction rule** (charge conservation): the sum of currents into a junction equals the sum out of it.
- **Loop rule** (energy conservation): the sum of potential changes around any closed loop is zero.

Sign convention for the loop rule: pick a direction of travel around the loop. Crossing a resistor **in the direction of the assumed current** is a change of $-IR$ (a drop); crossing against the current is $+IR$. Crossing a battery from its negative to its positive terminal is a rise of $+\mathcal E$; the other way is $-\mathcal E$. If your solved current is negative, the real current runs opposite to your assumed direction.

### Real batteries

A real source of emf $\mathcal E$ has internal resistance $r$. With current $I$ flowing, the terminal voltage is $V_{term} = \mathcal E - Ir$. Connected to a load $R$ the current is $I = \mathcal E/(R + r)$. This is the reason a heavily loaded battery, or a defibrillator pad on high-resistance skin, delivers less than its nominal voltage.

### Electrical safety

The physiological effect depends on current, not voltage alone, and on the path through the body. Currents of a few milliamperes across the skin are felt; larger currents through the chest can disturb the heart rhythm. Dry skin has far higher resistance than wet skin, so the same voltage can drive a much larger current when the skin is wet.

@example
title: Two resistors and a battery
problem: A $12\ \mathrm{V}$ ideal battery drives a $4.0\ \Omega$ resistor in series with an $8.0\ \Omega$ resistor. Find the current, the voltage across the $8.0\ \Omega$ resistor and the power dissipated in it.
step: Series resistances add: $R_{eq} = 4.0 + 8.0 = 12\ \Omega$.
step: By Ohm's law the current is $I = V/R_{eq} = 12/12 = 1.0\ \mathrm{A}$, the same through both resistors.
step: The voltage across the $8.0\ \Omega$ resistor is $V = IR = (1.0)(8.0) = 8.0\ \mathrm{V}$; the other $4.0\ \mathrm{V}$ is across the $4.0\ \Omega$ resistor, so the loop rule holds: $12 - 4 - 8 = 0$.
step: Power in the $8.0\ \Omega$ resistor: $P = I^2R = (1.0)^2(8.0) = 8.0\ \mathrm{W}$.

@mistakes
- Adding parallel resistances directly instead of adding their reciprocals.
- Using diameter instead of area in $R = \rho L/A$, or forgetting $A = \pi r^2$ with the radius in meters.
- Getting the sign of a battery or resistor wrong in the loop rule; decide the direction of travel and the assumed current first and apply the convention consistently.
- Treating terminal voltage as equal to emf when the battery has internal resistance and current flows.

@practice num
prompt: A copper wire has resistivity $\rho = 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\mathrm{m}$, length $2.0\ \mathrm{m}$ and diameter $1.0\ \mathrm{mm}$. Find its resistance in ohms.
answer: 0.04329
tol: 1%
unit: Ω
check: 1.7e-8 * 2.0 / (math.pi * (0.5e-3)**2)
hint: The radius is half the diameter; the area is $\pi r^2$ in square meters.
hint: Use $R = \rho L/A$ with $A = \pi(0.5\times10^{-3})^2$.
solution: $A = \pi(5.0\times10^{-4})^2 = 7.85\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, so $R = (1.7\times10^{-8})(2.0)/(7.85\times10^{-7}) \approx 0.043\ \Omega$.

@practice num
prompt: A $24\ \mathrm{V}$ ideal battery is connected to a $6.0\ \Omega$ resistor in series with a parallel pair made of a $3.0\ \Omega$ resistor and a $6.0\ \Omega$ resistor. Find the current supplied by the battery, in amperes.
answer: 3
tol: 1%
unit: A
check: 24 / (6.0 + 1/(1/3.0 + 1/6.0))
hint: First replace the parallel pair by its equivalent resistance.
hint: The parallel pair is $1/(1/3 + 1/6) = 2.0\ \Omega$; add it to the series $6.0\ \Omega$.
solution: The parallel pair is $2.0\ \Omega$, so $R_{eq} = 8.0\ \Omega$ and $I = 24/8.0 = 3.0\ \mathrm{A}$.

@practice num
prompt: A battery has emf $9.0\ \mathrm{V}$ and internal resistance $0.50\ \Omega$. It is connected to a load of $4.5\ \Omega$. Find the terminal voltage across the battery, in volts.
answer: 8.1
tol: 1%
unit: V
check: 9.0 - (9.0/(4.5 + 0.50)) * 0.50
hint: First find the current through the whole loop: $I = \mathcal E/(R + r)$.
hint: Then $V_{term} = \mathcal E - Ir$.
solution: $I = 9.0/(4.5 + 0.50) = 1.8\ \mathrm{A}$. $V_{term} = 9.0 - (1.8)(0.50) = 8.1\ \mathrm{V}$, which equals $IR = (1.8)(4.5)$.

@quiz num
prompt: A steady current of $2.0\ \mathrm{mA}$ flows for $5.0\ \mathrm{s}$ through a saline-filled tube. Treating the carriers as single-charged ions with charge magnitude $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, how many elementary charges pass a cross-section in that time? Give the answer as a number.
answer: 6.242e16
tol: 1%
check: 2.0e-3 * 5.0 / 1.602e-19
hint: Total charge is $Q = It$.
hint: Divide the total charge by $e$.
solution: $Q = (2.0\times10^{-3})(5.0) = 1.0\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{C}$, and $N = Q/e = 1.0\times10^{-2}/1.602\times10^{-19} \approx 6.2\times10^{16}$.

@quiz num
