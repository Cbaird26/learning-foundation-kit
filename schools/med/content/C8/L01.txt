@article
Constants used in this course: Coulomb constant $k = 8.99\times10^{9}\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$, elementary charge $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$, permittivity of free space $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C^2/(N\,m^2)}$ (so $k = 1/(4\pi\varepsilon_0)$). Charge is measured in coulombs (C); a microcoulomb is $1\ \mu\mathrm{C} = 10^{-6}\ \mathrm{C}$ and a nanocoulomb is $1\ \mathrm{nC} = 10^{-9}\ \mathrm{C}$.

### Charge

Matter contains positive protons and negative electrons. Two facts organize everything: charge is **conserved** (the net charge of an isolated system never changes) and **quantized** (any charge is an integer multiple of $e$). Like charges repel; unlike charges attract. In a living body the charge carriers are mostly ions such as $\mathrm{Na^+}$, $\mathrm{K^+}$, $\mathrm{Cl^-}$ and $\mathrm{Ca^{2+}}$, each carrying a whole number of elementary charges.

### Coulomb's law

The force between two point charges $q_1$ and $q_2$ separated by $r$ has magnitude

$$F = k\,\frac{|q_1 q_2|}{r^2}.$$

It acts along the line joining the charges: repulsive if the charges have the same sign, attractive if opposite. Doubling the distance cuts the force to one quarter (an inverse-square law). For a sign convention, treat the direction separately: use magnitudes in the formula and decide the direction from the signs.

### The electric field

Rather than think of charges pushing each other directly, say that a charge $Q$ creates a **field** in the space around it. The electric field at a point is the force per unit positive test charge:

$$\vec E = \frac{\vec F}{q}, \qquad \text{units } \mathrm{N/C} = \mathrm{V/m}.$$

By convention $\vec E$ points in the direction of the force on a **positive** test charge. A point charge $Q$ has field magnitude $E = k|Q|/r^2$, pointing away from $Q$ if $Q>0$ and toward it if $Q<0$. A charge $q$ placed in a field feels $\vec F = q\vec E$, so a negative charge is pushed opposite to the field.

### Superposition

When several charges are present, the net field is the **vector sum** of the individual fields. Along a line, assign a sign to each contribution (for example positive to the right) and add. At the midpoint between $+Q$ and $-Q$, both fields point from the positive toward the negative charge, so they add. At the midpoint between two equal positive charges, they point in opposite directions and cancel.

### Field lines and conductors

Field lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges; they never cross, and where they crowd together the field is strong. In electrostatic equilibrium the free charges in a conductor rearrange until the field inside the conductor is zero and any excess charge sits on its surface. This is why a hollow metal enclosure shields its interior from external static fields, and it is the idea behind the shielding of sensitive recording equipment in physiology labs.

@example
title: Field and force near a proton
problem: What is the electric field magnitude at a distance $r = 5.29\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{m}$ from a proton, and what force does it exert on an electron there? Use $k = 8.99\times10^9\ \mathrm{N\,m^2/C^2}$ and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$.
step: Use the point-charge field $E = ke/r^2$ with $Q = e$.
step: Compute $r^2 = (5.29\times10^{-11})^2 = 2.80\times10^{-21}\ \mathrm{m^2}$.
step: $E = \dfrac{(8.99\times10^{9})(1.602\times10^{-19})}{2.80\times10^{-21}} \approx 5.15\times10^{11}\ \mathrm{N/C}$, directed away from the proton.
step: The force on the electron is $F = eE = (1.602\times10^{-19})(5.15\times10^{11}) \approx 8.2\times10^{-8}\ \mathrm{N}$, directed toward the proton because the electron is negative.

@mistakes
- Using the charge in microcoulombs or nanocoulombs without converting to coulombs.
- Forgetting to square the distance, or using a distance in centimeters while $k$ is in SI units.
- Adding field magnitudes without checking directions; fields are vectors and may cancel.
- Believing the force on a negative charge points along the field; it points opposite to the field.

answer: 7192
tol: 1%

@quiz mc
prompt: A hollow metal box is placed in a strong static external electric field and left until electrostatic equilibrium is reached. What is the electric field in the empty cavity inside the metal box?
choice: Equal to the external field, because field lines pass through metal.
choice: Equal to half the external field.
choice*: Zero, because free charges rearrange on the outer surface to cancel the field inside.
choice: Directed opposite to the external field with the same magnitude as the external field outside.
hint: In equilibrium the free charges in a conductor stop moving.
solution: If a field existed inside the conductor, free charges would keep moving. They rearrange on the surface until the field in the conductor and in the enclosed cavity is zero.
