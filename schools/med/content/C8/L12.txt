@article
Constants used here: speed of sound in soft tissue $1540\ \mathrm{m/s}$, in air $343\ \mathrm{m/s}$; $1\ \mathrm{Gy} = 1\ \mathrm{J/kg}$; radiation weighting factor $w_R = 1$ for X-rays, gamma rays and beta particles and $w_R = 20$ for alpha particles. The typical doses below are approximate, rounded values that vary with equipment, protocol and patient size.

### X-ray and CT: attenuation

An X-ray beam passing through tissue is attenuated exponentially:

$$I = I_0\,e^{-\mu x},$$

where $\mu$ is the linear attenuation coefficient and $x$ is thickness. Bone and contrast agents attenuate strongly (high atomic number, absorption by the photoelectric effect), soft tissue less, air least, so a radiograph is a shadow image. The **half-value layer** is the thickness that halves the intensity, $x_{1/2} = \ln 2/\mu$. **CT** rotates an X-ray source around the patient, records many projections, and reconstructs cross-sectional images by computer; it gives excellent anatomical detail at a higher dose than a single radiograph.

### Nuclear imaging

In **SPECT** and planar scintigraphy a gamma emitter such as technetium-99m ($140\ \mathrm{keV}$) is tagged to a molecule that localizes in an organ, and a gamma camera maps where photons come from. In **PET** a positron emitter such as fluorine-18 (in fluorodeoxyglucose) produces two opposite $511\ \mathrm{keV}$ photons from annihilation, detected in coincidence to locate metabolic activity. The radiation source is inside the patient.

### Ultrasound

A transducer emits pulses of sound at several MHz and listens for echoes. The depth of a reflector is $d = vt/2$ where $t$ is the round-trip time; the factor $2$ accounts for going and returning. At a boundary between media of **acoustic impedance** $Z = \rho v$, the fraction of intensity reflected is

$$R = \left(\frac{Z_2 - Z_1}{Z_2 + Z_1}\right)^2.$$

A large impedance mismatch (soft tissue to air or bone) reflects almost everything, which is why a coupling gel is used and why ultrasound cannot image through bone or gas. **Doppler ultrasound** measures blood velocity from the frequency shift $\Delta f = 2 f v\cos\theta / c_s$, where $f$ is the transmitted frequency, $v$ the blood speed, $\theta$ the angle between beam and flow and $c_s$ the speed of sound. Ultrasound uses no ionizing radiation.

### MRI

MRI (previous lessons) uses a static field, radiofrequency pulses and gradients; it gives excellent soft-tissue contrast without ionizing radiation. Its limitations are cost, time, noise, claustrophobia, and hazards from ferromagnetic objects and some implants.

### Dose quantities

- **Absorbed dose** $D$ = energy absorbed per unit mass, in gray (Gy, J/kg).
- **Equivalent dose** $H = w_R\,D$, in sievert (Sv), accounting for the biological effectiveness of the radiation type.
- **Effective dose** $E = \sum_T w_T H_T$ sums the equivalent doses in organs, weighted by tissue sensitivity $w_T$, to express whole-body stochastic risk in sieverts. It is used to compare studies, not to predict an individual's risk exactly.

Approximate typical effective doses: chest radiograph about $0.1\ \mathrm{mSv}$; mammogram about $0.4\ \mathrm{mSv}$; CT of the head about $2\ \mathrm{mSv}$; CT of the abdomen and pelvis about $8$ to $10\ \mathrm{mSv}$; MRI and ultrasound $0$. Natural background radiation averages about $3\ \mathrm{mSv}$ per year in the United States (more when medical sources are included).

### Safety principles

The risk at diagnostic doses is mainly a small increase in cancer probability (stochastic effect, assumed to rise with dose without a threshold), while very high doses cause deterministic tissue damage above a threshold. Radiology follows **justification** (the study must have a net benefit), **optimization** (**ALARA**, as low as reasonably achievable, using the lowest dose that answers the question) and dose limits for workers. Time, distance and shielding reduce exposure; the dose from a point source falls as $1/r^2$. Children are more sensitive, and pregnancy requires special consideration, often favoring ultrasound or MRI when they answer the question.

@example
title: Half-value layers and transmission
problem: A shield attenuates a beam with $\mu = 0.35\ \mathrm{cm^{-1}}$. Find its half-value layer and the fraction of intensity transmitted by $3$ half-value layers.
step: $x_{1/2} = \ln 2/\mu = 0.693/0.35 = 1.98\ \mathrm{cm}$.
step: Three half-value layers halve the intensity three times: $(1/2)^3 = 1/8$.
step: Check with the exponential: $e^{-\mu x}$ with $x = 3(1.98) = 5.94\ \mathrm{cm}$ gives $e^{-2.08} = 0.125$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting the factor of $1/2$ in $d = vt/2$ for ultrasound echoes.
- Mixing up absorbed dose (Gy) with effective dose (Sv): they measure different things.
- Thinking MRI and ultrasound carry ionizing-radiation risk; their risks are different (heating, implants and magnet safety for MRI).
- Taking effective-dose comparisons as exact predictions of cancer for any one patient.

prompt: Compare the radiation doses and the main benefits and risks of a chest radiograph, a CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis, an MRI of the abdomen and an abdominal ultrasound. Rank them by effective dose using approximate typical values, and say which principle guides the choice of study.

solution: Approximate effective doses: ultrasound $0$ and MRI $0$ (no ionizing radiation); chest radiograph about $0.1\ \mathrm{mSv}$; CT abdomen and pelvis about $8$ to $10\ \mathrm{mSv}$, roughly $80$ to $100$ chest radiographs and about three years of natural background. Ultrasound is portable, cheap and real-time but limited by bone, gas and operator skill. MRI gives superb soft-tissue contrast without radiation but is slow, expensive and unsafe with some implants or ferromagnetic objects. CT is fast and shows detail of bone, bowel and bleeding but carries the highest dose. A radiograph is quick and low dose with limited soft-tissue detail. @quiz num
prompt: A Doppler probe transmits at $5.0\ \mathrm{MHz}$. Blood moves at $0.50\ \mathrm{m/s}$ at an angle of $60^\circ$ to the beam. Use $c_s = 1540\ \mathrm{m/s}$ and $\Delta f = 2fv\cos\theta/c_s$ to find the Doppler shift in hertz.
answer: 1623
tol: 1%
unit: Hz
check: 2 * 5.0e6 * 0.50 * math.cos(math.radians(60)) / 1540
