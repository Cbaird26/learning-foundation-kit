goal: Recommend, for each of three short scenarios, which modality is most appropriate and justify the choice using risk, benefit and the ALARA principle.

Use at least one equation per modality, for example $I = I_0e^{-\mu x}$, $f = (\gamma/2\pi)B$ with $\gamma/2\pi = 42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T}$, $d = vt/2$ with $v = 1540\ \mathrm{m/s}$ in soft tissue, and $R = [(Z_2 - Z_1)/(Z_2 + Z_1)]^2$.

Approximate typical effective doses (they vary with equipment and protocol): chest radiograph 0.1 mSv; mammogram 0.4 mSv; CT of the head 2 mSv; CT of the abdomen and pelvis 8 mSv; MRI 0 (no ionizing radiation); ultrasound 0 (no ionizing radiation). step: Build a table with the columns study, effective dose in mSv, equivalent number of chest radiographs, and equivalent days of natural background. Show one worked calculation for each column type.

For each, name the first-choice study, a reasonable alternative, and the principle (justification and ALARA) that supports your choice. This is an educational exercise and not medical advice.

### Week-by-week key ideas

1. **Charge and fields.** Coulomb's law $F = k|q_1q_2|/r^2$; $E = F/q$; fields add as vectors; field is zero inside a conductor in equilibrium.
2. **Potential and capacitance.** $\Delta U = q\Delta V$; $C = Q/V = \kappa\varepsilon_0A/d$; $U = \tfrac12CV^2$; membrane as a capacitor with $E = V/d \approx 10^7\ \mathrm{V/m}$.
3. **Circuits.** $V = IR$, $R = \rho L/A$, $P = I^2R$; series and parallel; Kirchhoff's junction and loop rules; internal resistance.
4. **Bioelectricity.** Nernst potential $E = (26.7\ \mathrm{mV}/z)\ln([out]/[in])$; RC time constant; length constant $\lambda = \sqrt{r_m/r_i}$; myelin and saltatory conduction.
5. **Magnetism.** $F = qvB\sin\theta$; $r = mv/(qB)$; $F = ILB\sin\theta$; field of a wire $\mu_0I/(2\pi r)$.
6. **Induction.** $\mathcal E = -N\Delta\Phi/\Delta t$; Lenz's law; MRI uses a static field, RF pulses at the Larmor frequency and gradients.
7. **Electromagnetic waves.** $c = f\lambda$; $E = hf$; ionizing versus non-ionizing; $n = c/v$; inverse-square intensity.
8. **Geometric optics.** Snell's law; $1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i$ with real-positive sign convention; $m = -d_i/d_o$; three principal rays.
9. **The eye.** Myopia needs a diverging lens, $P = -1/d_{far}$; hyperopia needs a converging lens; magnifier $M = 25/f$.
10. **Wave optics.** $\Delta y = \lambda L/d$; single-slit minima $a\sin\theta = m\lambda$; $\theta = 1.22\lambda/D$; Malus's law.
11. **Atomic and nuclear.** Bohr levels; binding energy $\Delta m\times931.5\ \mathrm{MeV}$; $N = N_0(1/2)^{t/T}$; $A = \lambda N$.
12. **Imaging and safety.** $I = I_0e^{-\mu x}$; $d = vt/2$; impedance reflection; Gy, Sv and effective dose; justification and ALARA.

### Most common errors

- Forgetting to square $r$, or leaving distances in centimeters.
- Wrong sign for a virtual image or a diverging lens.
- Adding capacitors and resistors with the wrong rule.
- Forgetting the factor $1/2$ in echo depth, the sign of $z$ in the Nernst equation, or the negative sign in Lenz's law.
- Confusing absorbed dose, equivalent dose and effective dose.

prompt: A myopic patient has a far point of $50\ \mathrm{cm}$. Her correcting lens is chosen so that distant objects appear sharp, using $P = -1/d_{far}$. She then uses the same thin lens to look at a small object $25\ \mathrm{cm}$ in front of it. Using $1/f = 1/d_o + 1/d_i$ with a negative image distance for a virtual image, find the image distance in centimeters.
answer: -16.67
tol: 1%
unit: cm
check: 100 / (-1/0.50 - 1/0.25)
hint: First find $P$ and so $f = 1/P$ (negative).
hint: Then solve $1/d_i = 1/f - 1/d_o$.
solution: $P = -1/0.50 = -2.0\ \mathrm{D}$, so $f = -0.50\ \mathrm{m}$. Then $1/d_i = -2.0 - 4.0 = -6.0\ \mathrm{m^{-1}}$, so $d_i = -0.1667\ \mathrm{m} = -16.7\ \mathrm{cm}$: a virtual, reduced, upright image.

prompt: A membrane patch of area $2.0\times10^{-9}\ \mathrm{m^2}$ is modeled as a parallel-plate capacitor with thickness $7.5\ \mathrm{nm}$ and dielectric constant $7.0$. Use $\varepsilon_0 = 8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$ and $e = 1.602\times10^{-19}\ \mathrm{C}$. How many singly charged ions (each with charge magnitude $e$) must sit on one face of the membrane to produce a potential difference of $70\ \mathrm{mV}$? Give the number.
answer: 7.222e6
tol: 1%
check: 7.0 * 8.854e-12 * 2.0e-9 / 7.5e-9 * 0.070 / 1.602e-19
hint: Find the capacitance first, then the charge $Q = CV$.
hint: Divide the charge by $e$.
solution: $C = (7.0)(8.854\times10^{-12})(2.0\times10^{-9})/(7.5\times10^{-9}) = 1.65\times10^{-11}\ \mathrm{F}$. $Q = CV = (1.65\times10^{-11})(0.070) = 1.157\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{C}$. $N = Q/e \approx 7.2\times10^{6}$ ions, tiny compared with the roughly $10^{11}$ ions or more in the cell.

@exam self
prompt: A student says: "MRI is safer than CT because it uses weaker radiation, and ultrasound is the same as X-ray except with a different wavelength." Correct the errors. In your answer (a) state what kind of wave or field each of X-ray, MRI and ultrasound uses, (b) explain photon energy and ionization, (c) explain how position is encoded in MRI, and (d) give approximate effective doses for a chest radiograph and an abdominal CT.
hint: Ultrasound is a mechanical wave, not electromagnetic. MRI uses radio-frequency photons that are non-ionizing.
solution: (a) X-rays are high-frequency electromagnetic waves; MRI uses a strong static magnetic field plus radio-frequency electromagnetic pulses; ultrasound is a mechanical pressure wave in tissue at several MHz traveling at about $1540\ \mathrm{m/s}$, not an electromagnetic wave. (b) Photon energy is $E = hf$. X-ray photons (tens of keV) exceed the roughly $10\ \mathrm{eV}$ needed to ionize atoms and can damage DNA; the radio photons in MRI ($\approx 64\ \mathrm{MHz}$ at $1.5\ \mathrm{T}$, energy about $10^{-7}\ \mathrm{eV}$) cannot. MRI is not "weaker radiation": it is non-ionizing, with different hazards (strong magnet, implants, RF heating). (c) Gradient fields make the local field, and so the Larmor frequency $f = 42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T}\times B$, vary linearly with position, so frequency and phase encode location. (d) Chest radiograph about $0.1\ \mathrm{mSv}$; CT abdomen and pelvis about $8\ \mathrm{mSv}$ (about $80$ chest radiographs).
rubric: Identifies ultrasound as a mechanical wave and X-ray and MRI radiofrequency radiation as electromagnetic waves.
rubric: Explains ionization in terms of photon energy $hf$ versus the energy needed to ionize atoms.
rubric: Explains encoding of position through gradient fields and the Larmor frequency.
rubric: Gives approximately correct effective doses for a chest radiograph and an abdominal CT.
