@article
Constants used here: for hydrogen nuclei (protons) in water and fat, the resonance frequency per tesla is $\gamma/2\pi = 42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T}$. Sign convention: magnetic flux is positive when the field has a component along the loop's chosen normal direction.

### Magnetic flux

The **magnetic flux** through a flat loop of area $A$ in a uniform field is

$$\Phi = BA\cos\theta,$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec B$ and the normal to the loop. Its unit is the weber ($1\ \mathrm{Wb} = 1\ \mathrm{T\,m^2}$). Flux is largest when the field is perpendicular to the plane of the loop.

### Faraday's law and Lenz's law

A changing flux through a loop induces an emf. For a coil of $N$ turns,

$$\mathcal E = -N\,\frac{\Delta\Phi}{\Delta t}.$$

The minus sign is **Lenz's law**: the induced current flows in the direction that creates a magnetic field opposing the *change* in flux. If the flux through a loop is increasing, the induced current produces a field opposite to the original; if it is decreasing, the induced field points the same way as the original. Lenz's law is energy conservation in disguise: if the induced current aided the change, energy would be created from nothing.

### Motional emf and transformers

A conducting rod of length $L$ moving at speed $v$ perpendicular to a field $B$ (with the rod perpendicular to both) has a motional emf $\mathcal E = BLv$. Moving blood in a magnetic field is the basis for electromagnetic flowmeters.

A **transformer** uses a changing flux in a shared iron core to change AC voltages: $V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p$. A step-down transformer has fewer secondary turns. Only changing currents work; a transformer does not operate on steady DC.

### Magnetic fields in MRI

Magnetic resonance imaging combines three kinds of field, all consequences of the physics of this unit.

1. **The static main field $B_0$** (usually $1.5\ \mathrm{T}$ or $3\ \mathrm{T}$), made by a superconducting solenoid. It aligns the magnetic moments of hydrogen nuclei slightly along the field. Such nuclei precess at the **Larmor frequency** $f = (\gamma/2\pi)B$, so $f = 42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T} \times B$; at $1.5\ \mathrm{T}$ it is about $63.9\ \mathrm{MHz}$, a radio frequency.
2. **Radiofrequency (RF) pulses** at the Larmor frequency, produced by coils, tip the net magnetization away from $\vec B_0$. When the pulse ends, the precessing magnetization **induces an emf in a receiver coil** (Faraday's law); that signal is the raw MRI data. Relaxation times (T1 and T2) differ among tissues and give contrast.
3. **Gradient fields**, small additional fields (typically around $10$ to $40\ \mathrm{mT/m}$) that vary linearly with position. Because $f$ depends on the local field, protons at different positions precess at different frequencies, which encodes position into frequency. Rapid switching of the gradients can induce weak currents in the body, and the magnetic forces on the gradient coils produce the loud knocking noise.

No ionizing radiation is used, but the strong static field makes ferromagnetic objects a hazard, and some implanted devices are unsafe.

@example
title: EMF induced by a changing field
problem: A single circular loop of radius $0.050\ \mathrm{m}$ lies perpendicular to a field that increases uniformly from $0.20\ \mathrm{T}$ to $0.80\ \mathrm{T}$ in $0.30\ \mathrm{s}$. Find the magnitude of the induced emf and describe the direction of the induced current.
step: The area is $A = \pi r^2 = \pi (0.050)^2 = 7.85\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{m^2}$, and $\cos\theta = 1$.
step: The flux changes by $\Delta\Phi = (0.80 - 0.20)(7.85\times10^{-3}) = 4.71\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{Wb}$.
step: $|\mathcal E| = N\,\Delta\Phi/\Delta t = (1)(4.71\times10^{-3})/0.30 \approx 1.57\times10^{-2}\ \mathrm{V} = 15.7\ \mathrm{mV}$.
step: Flux into the loop is increasing, so by Lenz's law the induced current makes a field opposing the original one, i.e. in the opposite direction through the loop.

@mistakes
- Using the angle between $\vec B$ and the plane of the loop instead of between $\vec B$ and the normal in $\Phi = BA\cos\theta$.
- Believing that a large flux produces a large emf; only the **rate of change** of flux matters.
- Reading Lenz's law as "the induced field opposes $\vec B$" rather than "opposes the change in flux".
- Thinking MRI uses ionizing radiation, or that the frequency of the RF pulse is arbitrary; it must match the Larmor frequency.

@quiz num
prompt: Use $\gamma/2\pi = 42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T}$. A linear gradient field of $12\ \mathrm{mT/m}$ is applied along one axis. Find the difference in proton Larmor frequency between two points $0.050\ \mathrm{m}$ apart along that axis, in kilohertz.
answer: 25.55
tol: 1%
unit: kHz
check: 42.58e6 * 12e-3 * 0.050 / 1e3
hint: The field difference between the points is gradient times separation.
hint: Multiply that field difference by $42.58\ \mathrm{MHz/T}$ and convert units.
solution: $\Delta B = (12\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{T/m})(0.050\ \mathrm{m}) = 6.0\times10^{-4}\ \mathrm{T}$. $\Delta f = (42.58\times10^{6})(6.0\times10^{-4}) = 2.555\times10^{4}\ \mathrm{Hz} = 25.55\ \mathrm{kHz}$.
