@article
A **colligative property** depends on the *number* of dissolved solute particles, not on what those particles are. Dissolve a mole of glucose or a mole of urea in a kilogram of water and the freezing point drops by nearly the same amount. These properties matter in medicine because cells sit in a solution (plasma) whose particle concentration must stay within a narrow window.

### Concentration units you need

The **molality** of a solution is moles of solute per kilogram of *solvent*:

$$m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{\text{kg solvent}}.$$

Molality does not change with temperature (mass does not expand), which is why it is used for freezing and boiling problems. **Molarity** ($M$, mol per liter of solution) is used for osmotic pressure. The **mole fraction** of component $A$ is $X_A = n_A / n_{\text{total}}$.

### Vapor-pressure lowering (Raoult's law)

Solute particles occupy part of the surface, so fewer solvent molecules escape. For an ideal solution with a nonvolatile solute,

$$P_{\text{solution}} = X_{\text{solvent}}\,P^\circ_{\text{solvent}}.$$

The vapor pressure falls in proportion to the mole fraction of solute.

### Boiling-point elevation and freezing-point depression

Lower vapor pressure means the solution must be heated more to boil and cooled more to freeze:

$$\Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m, \qquad \Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m.$$

For water, $K_b = 0.512\ \text{°C/m}$ and $K_f = 1.86\ \text{°C/m}$. The **van 't Hoff factor** $i$ is the number of particles produced per formula unit: $i \approx 1$ for glucose, about $2$ for NaCl, about $3$ for CaCl$_2$. Real solutions give slightly lower values than the ideal integer because oppositely charged ions pair up, especially at high concentration.

### Osmosis and osmotic pressure

A **semipermeable membrane** lets water pass but not solute. Water moves toward the side with the higher solute concentration. The pressure needed to stop the net flow is the **osmotic pressure**:

$$\Pi = i\,M\,R\,T,$$

with $R = 0.08206\ \text{L·atm/(mol·K)}$ and $T$ in kelvin. The form matches the ideal gas law ($P = (n/V)RT$) for a reason: both come from particle number density.

### Clinical connection

The osmolarity of plasma is about $285$–$295\ \text{mOsm/L}$. Normal saline (0.9% NaCl, about $0.154\ \text{M}$ NaCl, $\approx 308\ \text{mOsm/L}$) is **isotonic** with red blood cells: no net water movement. Pure water is **hypotonic**, so red cells swell and can lyse; a solution with much more solute is **hypertonic**, so cells shrink (crenate). Osmotic pressure is also the standard way to find the molar mass of a large molecule such as a protein, because the effect is large even when the molarity is tiny.

@example
title: Freezing-point depression of a glucose solution
problem: A solution contains $18.0\ \text{g}$ of glucose (molar mass $180\ \text{g/mol}$) dissolved in $200\ \text{g}$ of water. Find the freezing point. Use $K_f = 1.86\ \text{°C/m}$.
step: Convert mass to moles: $18.0/180 = 0.100\ \text{mol}$ glucose. The solvent mass is $0.200\ \text{kg}$.
step: Molality: $m = 0.100/0.200 = 0.500\ \text{m}$. Glucose does not dissociate, so $i = 1$.
step: $\Delta T_f = (1)(1.86)(0.500) = 0.93\ \text{°C}$.
step: The solution freezes at $0.00 - 0.93 = -0.93\ \text{°C}$.

@example
title: Osmotic pressure of normal saline at body temperature
problem: Find the osmotic pressure of $0.15\ \text{M}$ NaCl at $310\ \text{K}$, taking $i = 2$.
step: NaCl gives two particles per formula unit, so the particle concentration is $iM = 0.30\ \text{mol/L}$.
step: Substitute: $\Pi = (2)(0.15)(0.08206)(310)$.
step: $(0.08206)(310) = 25.44$, and $25.44 \times 0.30 = 7.63$. So $\Pi \approx 7.6\ \text{atm}$.

@mistakes
- Using kilograms of solution (or liters) instead of kilograms of **solvent** when computing molality.
- Forgetting the van 't Hoff factor for ionic solutes, which halves or thirds the predicted effect.
- Reporting $\Delta T_f$ as the new freezing point; the freezing point is the pure-solvent value minus $\Delta T_f$.
- Using Celsius temperature in $\Pi = iMRT$.

prompt: A solution is made by dissolving $0.40\ \text{mol}$ of sucrose in $800\ \text{g}$ of water. What is its molality?
answer: 0.5
check: 0.40 / 0.800
tol: 1%
unit: m
hint: Molality is moles of solute per kilogram of solvent. Convert $800\ \text{g}$ to kilograms.
solution: $800\ \text{g} = 0.800\ \text{kg}$, so $m = 0.40/0.800 = 0.50\ \text{m}$.

check: 1 * 0.512 * 0.50

@practice num
prompt: Calculate the freezing-point depression $\Delta T_f$ (a positive number) of a $0.20\ \text{m}$ aqueous CaCl$_2$ solution. Assume complete dissociation and $K_f = 1.86\ \text{°C/m}$.
answer: 1.116
check: 3 * 1.86 * 0.20
tol: 0.01
unit: °C
hint: Count the ions that one formula unit of CaCl$_2$ produces.
hint: $i = 3$ (one Ca$^{2+}$ and two Cl$^-$). Then use $\Delta T_f = iK_fm$.
solution: $\Delta T_f = (3)(1.86)(0.20) = 1.116\ \text{°C}$.

@practice num
prompt: Pure water has a vapor pressure of $23.8\ \text{torr}$ at $25\ \text{°C}$. A solution with a nonvolatile solute has a water mole fraction of $0.980$. What is its vapor pressure in torr?
answer: 23.3
check: 0.980 * 23.8
tol: 0.1
unit: torr
hint: Apply Raoult's law: multiply the solvent mole fraction by the pure-solvent vapor pressure.
solution: $P = (0.980)(23.8) = 23.3\ \text{torr}$, a drop of about $0.5\ \text{torr}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A protein sample of $1.00\ \text{g}$ is dissolved in water to make $0.100\ \text{L}$ of solution. The osmotic pressure at $298\ \text{K}$ is $0.0120\ \text{atm}$. Estimate the molar mass of the protein in g/mol ($R = 0.08206\ \text{L·atm/(mol·K)}$, $i = 1$).
answer: 20378
check: 1.00 / ((0.0120 / (0.08206 * 298)) * 0.100)
tol: 1%
unit: g/mol
hint: First find the molarity from $\Pi = MRT$.
hint: Multiply the molarity by the volume to get moles, then divide the mass by moles.
solution: $M = 0.0120/(0.08206 \times 298) = 4.91\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}$. Moles $= 4.91\times10^{-4} \times 0.100 = 4.91\times10^{-5}$. Molar mass $= 1.00/4.91\times10^{-5} \approx 2.04\times10^{4}\ \text{g/mol}$.
