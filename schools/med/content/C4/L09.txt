@article
Why does ice melt at room temperature, yet a puddle never freezes spontaneously at $25\ \text{°C}$? Energy alone ($\Delta H$) does not decide. The second ingredient is **entropy**, $S$, a measure of how many microscopic arrangements ($W$) correspond to a state ($S = k_B\ln W$). Entropy is a state function with units J/(mol·K).

### Predicting the sign of $\Delta S$

Entropy increases with:

- phase change toward greater freedom: solid $\to$ liquid $\to$ gas;
- more moles of gas produced;
- dissolving a solid into a liquid (usually) and higher temperature;
- more complex molecules, and more particles from one.

The **second law** says that the entropy of the universe increases in any spontaneous process: $\Delta S_{\text{univ}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0$. The **third law** sets the entropy of a perfect crystal at $0\ \text{K}$ to zero, so absolute standard entropies $S^\circ$ can be tabulated. Then

$$\Delta S^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum n\,S^\circ(\text{products}) - \sum m\,S^\circ(\text{reactants}).$$

### Gibbs free energy

Tracking the surroundings via $\Delta H$ leads to one criterion for the system alone:

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S.$$

A process at constant $T$ and $P$ is **spontaneous** if $\Delta G < 0$, at equilibrium if $\Delta G = 0$, and nonspontaneous if $\Delta G > 0$. Spontaneous does not mean fast; kinetics is separate. The signs of $\Delta H$ and $\Delta S$ give four cases:

| $\Delta H$ | $\Delta S$ | Result |
|---|---|---|
| $-$ | $+$ | spontaneous at all $T$ |
| $+$ | $-$ | nonspontaneous at all $T$ |
| $-$ | $-$ | spontaneous at low $T$ |
| $+$ | $+$ | spontaneous at high $T$ |

When the signs match, the sign of $\Delta G$ flips at the **crossover temperature** $T = \Delta H/\Delta S$ (watch the units: $\Delta H$ in J, $\Delta S$ in J/K).

### Free energy and the equilibrium constant

Under standard conditions, and for non-standard conditions respectively:

$$\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K, \qquad \Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q.$$

Use $R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$ and $T$ in kelvin. If $\Delta G^\circ < 0$ then $K > 1$; if $\Delta G^\circ = 0$ then $K = 1$; if $\Delta G^\circ > 0$ then $K < 1$. A reaction proceeds spontaneously whenever $\Delta G < 0$, which depends on $Q$ and not only on $\Delta G^\circ$: a reaction with positive $\Delta G^\circ$ can still run forward if $Q$ is kept small enough.

### Biochemical coupling

Cells keep ATP and ADP far from equilibrium, so hydrolysis of ATP has a very negative $\Delta G$ in the cell (about $-50$ to $-65\ \text{kJ/mol}$, more negative than the standard biochemical value of about $-30.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$). **Coupling** a nonspontaneous reaction to ATP hydrolysis makes the sum have $\Delta G < 0$. Because $\Delta G$ values add, reactions with positive $\Delta G$ in a pathway can be driven by a later highly favorable step.

@example
title: Spontaneity and crossover temperature
problem: The Haber synthesis $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \to 2\text{NH}_3$ has $\Delta H^\circ = -92.2\ \text{kJ}$ and $\Delta S^\circ = -198.7\ \text{J/K}$. Find $\Delta G^\circ$ at $298\ \text{K}$ and the temperature at which it changes sign.
step: Convert to the same units: $\Delta H^\circ = -92\,200\ \text{J}$.
step: $\Delta G^\circ = -92\,200 - (298)(-198.7) = -92\,200 + 59\,213 = -32\,987\ \text{J}$, about $-33.0\ \text{kJ}$. It is spontaneous at $298\ \text{K}$.
step: Both signs are negative, so it is favored only at low $T$. Crossover: $T = \Delta H/\Delta S = (-92\,200)/(-198.7) = 464\ \text{K}$.

@example
title: From $\Delta G^\circ$ to $K$
problem: A reaction has $\Delta G^\circ = -20.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$ at $298\ \text{K}$. Find $K$.
step: Rearrange $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ to $\ln K = -\Delta G^\circ/(RT)$.
step: $\ln K = 20\,000/(8.314 \times 298) = 20\,000/2477.6 = 8.073$.
step: $K = e^{8.073} = 3.2\times10^{3}$. A $\Delta G^\circ$ of $-20\ \text{kJ/mol}$ gives a product-favored equilibrium.

@mistakes
- Mixing kJ for $\Delta H$ with J/K for $\Delta S$ in $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S$.
- Using Celsius temperature in $\Delta G$ or $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$.
- Equating "spontaneous" with "fast", or $\Delta G^\circ$ with $\Delta G$ under non-standard conditions.
- Subtracting entropies in the wrong order (reactants minus products) and using coefficients incorrectly.

@practice num
prompt: A process has $\Delta H = +40.0\ \text{kJ}$ and $\Delta S = +110\ \text{J/K}$. Find $\Delta G$ at $298\ \text{K}$ in kJ.
answer: 7.22
check: (40000 - 298 * 110) / 1000
tol: 1%
unit: kJ
hint: Convert $\Delta H$ to joules (or $\Delta S$ to kJ/K) before combining.
solution: $\Delta G = 40\,000 - (298)(110) = 40\,000 - 32\,780 = 7220\ \text{J} = 7.22\ \text{kJ}$. It is positive, so the process is nonspontaneous at $298\ \text{K}$.

@practice num
prompt: For $\Delta H = +40.0\ \text{kJ}$ and $\Delta S = +110\ \text{J/K}$, above what temperature (in K) does the process become spontaneous?
answer: 363.6
check: 40000 / 110
tol: 1%
unit: K
hint: At the crossover, $\Delta G = 0$, so $T = \Delta H/\Delta S$.
solution: $T = 40\,000\ \text{J}/110\ \text{J/K} = 364\ \text{K}$ (about $90\ \text{°C}$). Above this temperature the entropy term wins.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction has $K = 1.0\times10^{-3}$ at $298\ \text{K}$. Calculate $\Delta G^\circ$ in kJ/mol ($R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$).
answer: 17.1
check: -8.314 * 298 * log(1.0e-3) / 1000
tol: 1%
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Use $\Delta G^\circ = -RT\ln K$ and note that $\ln K$ is negative.
solution: $\Delta G^\circ = -(8.314)(298)\ln(1.0\times10^{-3}) = -(2477.6)(-6.908) = 17\,114\ \text{J/mol} = 17.1\ \text{kJ/mol}$.

@practice num
prompt: ATP hydrolysis has $\Delta G^\circ{}' = -30.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$. In a cell at $310\ \text{K}$, the reaction quotient is $Q = 2.0\times10^{-3}$. Find $\Delta G$ in kJ/mol ($R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$).
answer: -46.5
check: (-30500 + 8.314 * 310 * log(2.0e-3)) / 1000
tol: 1%
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Use $\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q$ with consistent units.
hint: $RT = 2577\ \text{J/mol}$ and $\ln(2.0\times10^{-3}) = -6.215$.
solution: $\Delta G = -30\,500 + (2577)(-6.215) = -30\,500 - 16\,017 = -46\,517\ \text{J/mol} = -46.5\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Keeping $Q$ small makes the hydrolysis more favorable than the standard value.

@quiz num
prompt: Using $S^\circ$ values ($\text{SO}_2(g)$: $248.2$, $\text{O}_2(g)$: $205.1$, $\text{SO}_3(g)$: $256.8\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$), find $\Delta S^\circ$ in J/K for $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{SO}_3$.
answer: -187.9
check: 2 * 256.8 - 2 * 248.2 - 205.1
tol: 1%
unit: J/K
hint: Sum the products times their coefficients and subtract the sum for the reactants.
solution: $\Delta S^\circ = 2(256.8) - [2(248.2) + 205.1] = 513.6 - 701.5 = -187.9\ \text{J/K}$. It is negative because three moles of gas become two.
