@article
Few ionic compounds are completely insoluble; they dissolve a little until the solution is **saturated**, with dissolution and precipitation in equilibrium. For $\text{M}_x\text{A}_y(s) \rightleftharpoons x\text{M}^{y+} + y\text{A}^{x-}$ the **solubility product** is

$$K_{sp} = [\text{M}^{y+}]^x[\text{A}^{x-}]^y.$$

The solid does not appear in the expression. A smaller $K_{sp}$ means lower solubility *for salts of the same formula type* (compare AgCl with AgBr, not AgCl with $\text{PbI}_2$).

### From $K_{sp}$ to molar solubility

Let $s$ be the **molar solubility** (mol of solid that dissolves per liter). For each salt type:

- $\text{AB}$ (such as AgCl): $K_{sp} = s^2$.
- $\text{AB}_2$ (such as $\text{CaF}_2$, $\text{PbI}_2$): $K_{sp} = (s)(2s)^2 = 4s^3$.
- $\text{A}_2\text{B}$ (such as $\text{Ag}_2\text{CrO}_4$): $K_{sp} = (2s)^2(s) = 4s^3$.

Solve for $s$ by taking the appropriate root. The reverse calculation (finding $K_{sp}$ from a measured solubility) is the same relationship used backward.

### The common-ion effect

If the solution already contains one of the ions, the solubility drops (Le Chatelier). In $0.10\ \text{M}$ NaCl, the chloride concentration is set at about $0.10\ \text{M}$, so AgCl dissolves only until $[\text{Ag}^+] = K_{sp}/0.10$. Neglect the tiny $s$ added to $0.10$.

### Predicting precipitation

Compute the reaction quotient $Q = [\text{M}]^x[\text{A}]^y$ using the **concentrations after mixing** (account for dilution). Then:

- $Q < K_{sp}$: unsaturated; no precipitate.
- $Q = K_{sp}$: saturated.
- $Q > K_{sp}$: a precipitate forms until $Q$ drops to $K_{sp}$.

When two ions can precipitate with the same reagent, the one with the smaller $K_{sp}$ (for the same formula type) precipitates first, which allows **selective precipitation**.

### The effect of pH

If the anion is the conjugate base of a weak acid, adding acid removes it ($\text{F}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{HF}$), pulling the dissolution forward. So $\text{CaF}_2$, $\text{CaCO}_3$ and $\text{Mg(OH)}_2$ dissolve better in acid. A salt whose anion is the conjugate base of a strong acid (Cl$^-$, Br$^-$, I$^-$) has solubility that is independent of pH, and adding $\text{OH}^-$ *lowers* the solubility of a hydroxide because of the common-ion effect.

### Clinical connection

Calcium oxalate is the most common component of kidney stones. Urine is often near saturation; a higher $[\text{Ca}^{2+}]$ or $[\text{oxalate}]$ pushes $Q$ above $K_{sp}$, so stones form. Dilute urine (high fluid intake) lowers both concentrations. Barium sulfate, extremely insoluble, is used as a radiocontrast agent precisely because so little barium dissolves.

@example
title: Molar solubility of a 1:1 salt and the common-ion effect
problem: For AgCl, $K_{sp} = 1.8\times10^{-10}$. Find the molar solubility in pure water and in $0.10\ \text{M}$ NaCl.
step: In pure water, $K_{sp} = s^2$, so $s = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}} = 1.3\times10^{-5}\ \text{M}$.
step: In $0.10\ \text{M}$ NaCl, $[\text{Cl}^-] \approx 0.10\ \text{M}$ and $[\text{Ag}^+] = s$, so $K_{sp} = s(0.10)$.
step: $s = 1.8\times10^{-10}/0.10 = 1.8\times10^{-9}\ \text{M}$, about $7000$ times lower than in pure water.

@example
title: Molar solubility of an AB2 salt
problem: For $\text{PbI}_2$, $K_{sp} = 9.8\times10^{-9}$. Find the molar solubility.
step: $\text{PbI}_2(s) \rightleftharpoons \text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{I}^-$, so $[\text{Pb}^{2+}] = s$ and $[\text{I}^-] = 2s$.
step: $K_{sp} = s(2s)^2 = 4s^3 = 9.8\times10^{-9}$.
step: $s^3 = 2.45\times10^{-9}$, so $s = 1.35\times10^{-3}\ \text{M}$.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to square the anion concentration (or to double it) when the formula has a subscript of 2.
- Using concentrations before mixing instead of after dilution when computing $Q$.
- Comparing $K_{sp}$ values of salts with different formula types to rank solubility.
- Claiming that the solubility of any salt increases in acid; this is true only when the anion is basic.
