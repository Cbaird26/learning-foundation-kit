@article
Most balanced equations hide the real story. A **mechanism** is the sequence of **elementary steps** (single molecular events) that add up to the overall reaction. The rate law measured in the lab is a clue to which mechanism is correct.

### Elementary steps and molecularity

The **molecularity** of an elementary step is the number of particles that collide in it: unimolecular (1), bimolecular (2), termolecular (3, rare). For an *elementary step only*, the rate law can be read from the coefficients: $A + B \to \text{products}$ has rate $=k[A][B]$, and $2A \to \text{products}$ has rate $=k[A]^2$.

Species that are formed in one step and consumed in a later one are **intermediates**. They do not appear in the overall equation and should not appear in the final rate law.

### The rate-determining step

In a sequence of steps, the slowest step limits the overall rate, like the narrowest point of a pipe. The overall rate law is the rate law of the **rate-determining step** (RDS), rewritten so that no intermediate appears. If the RDS follows a fast reversible step, solve the fast equilibrium for the intermediate: for $2\text{NO} \rightleftharpoons \text{N}_2\text{O}_2$ (fast) the intermediate satisfies $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_2] = K[\text{NO}]^2$.

### Temperature and activation energy

Reactions need an **activation energy** $E_a$, the energy barrier for the collision. The **Arrhenius equation** is

$$k = A\,e^{-E_a/RT}, \qquad \ln\frac{k_2}{k_1} = \frac{E_a}{R}\left(\frac{1}{T_1} - \frac{1}{T_2}\right),$$

with $R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$ and $T$ in kelvin. The second form lets you find $E_a$ from two rate constants, or a new $k$ from $E_a$. A rough rule is that many reactions about double in rate for a $10\ \text{°C}$ rise, which is why fever and hypothermia change biochemical rates.

### Catalysis

A **catalyst** speeds a reaction by providing a different mechanism with a lower activation energy. It is regenerated, so it is not consumed overall. It does **not** change $\Delta G$, the equilibrium constant or the equilibrium position; it speeds the forward and reverse reactions equally and so only shortens the time to reach equilibrium. A catalyst in the same phase as the reactants is **homogeneous**; one in a different phase (a metal surface) is **heterogeneous**.

### Enzymes

**Enzymes** are biological catalysts. The substrate $S$ binds the enzyme $E$ to give a complex $ES$, which forms product $P$:

$$E + S \rightleftharpoons ES \to E + P.$$

The **Michaelis-Menten equation** gives the initial velocity:

$$v = \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}.$$

At low $[S]$ the rate is first order in $S$; at high $[S]$ the enzyme is saturated and the rate approaches $V_{\max}$ (zero order). $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which $v = V_{\max}/2$. A smaller $K_m$ usually means tighter substrate binding. Enzymes lower $E_a$ but obey the same thermodynamics as any other catalyst.

@example
title: Mechanism to rate law
problem: A proposed mechanism for $\text{NO}_2 + \text{CO} \to \text{NO} + \text{CO}_2$ is: step 1, $\text{NO}_2 + \text{NO}_2 \to \text{NO}_3 + \text{NO}$ (slow); step 2, $\text{NO}_3 + \text{CO} \to \text{NO}_2 + \text{CO}_2$ (fast). Identify the intermediate and the rate law.
step: Add the steps and cancel: $\text{NO}_3$ appears as a product in step 1 and a reactant in step 2, so it is an intermediate. The overall equation is $\text{NO}_2 + \text{CO} \to \text{NO} + \text{CO}_2$.
step: Step 1 is slow, so it is the rate-determining step.
step: Step 1 is elementary and bimolecular in $\text{NO}_2$, so $\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}_2]^2$. CO does not appear because it reacts only after the slow step.

@example
title: Effect of temperature on a rate constant
problem: A reaction has $E_a = 50.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$. By what factor does $k$ increase when the temperature goes from $298\ \text{K}$ to $308\ \text{K}$?
step: Use $\ln(k_2/k_1) = (E_a/R)(1/T_1 - 1/T_2)$ with $E_a = 50\,000\ \text{J/mol}$.
step: $1/298 - 1/308 = 1.0895\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$, and $E_a/R = 50\,000/8.314 = 6014\ \text{K}$.
step: $\ln(k_2/k_1) = 6014 \times 1.0895\times10^{-4} = 0.655$.
step: $k_2/k_1 = e^{0.655} = 1.93$. The rate nearly doubles for a $10\ \text{K}$ rise.

@mistakes
- Writing the rate law from the overall equation's coefficients instead of from the slow elementary step.
- Leaving an intermediate in the final rate law.
- Saying a catalyst changes $K$ or the yield at equilibrium.
- Using Celsius temperatures or $E_a$ in kJ with $R$ in J in the Arrhenius equation.

prompt: An elementary step is $2\text{A} + \text{B} \to \text{products}$. What is its rate law?
choice: $\text{rate} = k[\text{A}][\text{B}]$
choice*: $\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^2[\text{B}]$

choice: $\text{rate} = k[2\text{A}][\text{B}]$

solution: In an elementary step the coefficient of each reactant is its order, giving $k[\text{A}]^2[\text{B}]$.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction has $E_a = 75.0\ \text{kJ/mol}$. By what factor does its rate constant increase when the temperature rises from $300\ \text{K}$ to $310\ \text{K}$? ($R = 8.314\ \text{J/(mol·K)}$)
answer: 2.64
check: exp(75000 / 8.314 * (1/300 - 1/310))
tol: 1%
hint: Use $\ln(k_2/k_1) = (E_a/R)(1/T_1 - 1/T_2)$ and convert $E_a$ to joules.
hint: $E_a/R = 9021\ \text{K}$ and $1/300 - 1/310 = 1.075\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$.
solution: $\ln(k_2/k_1) = 9021 \times 1.075\times10^{-4} = 0.970$, so $k_2/k_1 = e^{0.970} = 2.64$.

@practice ms
prompt: Which statements about a catalyst are true?
choice*: It lowers the activation energy of the reaction by providing an alternative pathway.
choice*: It is regenerated, so it is not consumed in the overall reaction.
choice: It shifts the equilibrium position toward the products.
choice: It makes the overall $\Delta G$ more negative.
hint: A catalyst changes the route, not the starting and ending energies.
solution: A catalyst offers a lower-barrier pathway and is regenerated. It does not change the free energies of reactants and products, so $\Delta G$, $K$ and the equilibrium position are unchanged.

@practice num
prompt: An enzyme has $V_{\max} = 100\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 2.0\ \text{mM}$. Find the initial velocity at $[S] = 6.0\ \text{mM}$.
answer: 75
check: 100 * 6.0 / (2.0 + 6.0)
tol: 1%

@quiz num

@quiz mc
prompt: A mechanism is: step 1, $2\text{NO} \rightleftharpoons \text{N}_2\text{O}_2$ (fast equilibrium); step 2, $\text{N}_2\text{O}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{NO}_2$ (slow). Which rate law does it predict for the overall reaction $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \to 2\text{NO}_2$?
choice: $\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}][\text{O}_2]$
choice: $\text{rate} = k[\text{N}_2\text{O}_2][\text{O}_2]$
choice*: $\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]$
choice: $\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}]^2$
hint: The slow step sets the rate, but an intermediate cannot appear in the final rate law.
hint: Use the fast equilibrium to replace $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_2]$ with an expression in $[\text{NO}]$.
solution: The slow step gives $k_2[\text{N}_2\text{O}_2][\text{O}_2]$. The fast equilibrium gives $[\text{N}_2\text{O}_2] = K[\text{NO}]^2$, so the overall rate is $k[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]$.
