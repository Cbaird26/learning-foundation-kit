@article
Chemistry changes electrons; **nuclear reactions** change the nucleus. A nuclide is written $^{A}_{Z}\text{X}$ with mass number $A$ (protons plus neutrons) and atomic number $Z$ (protons). Different nuclides of one element are **isotopes**. Nuclear reactions release millions of times more energy per atom than chemical reactions.

### Types of decay

A nuclear equation is balanced when the sums of $A$ and of $Z$ are equal on both sides.

| Decay | Particle | Change in nucleus |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha ($\alpha$) | $^4_2\text{He}$ | $A$ down $4$, $Z$ down $2$ |
| Beta-minus ($\beta^-$) | electron, $^{\ 0}_{-1}e$ | $A$ unchanged, $Z$ up $1$ (a neutron becomes a proton) |
| Positron ($\beta^+$) | $^{0}_{+1}e$ | $A$ unchanged, $Z$ down $1$ |
| Electron capture | inner electron absorbed | $A$ unchanged, $Z$ down $1$ |
| Gamma ($\gamma$) | high-energy photon | no change in $A$ or $Z$ |

Alpha particles are heavy and stopped by paper or skin but harmful if the emitter is ingested; beta particles penetrate a few millimeters of tissue; gamma rays are the most penetrating and need dense shielding such as lead.

### Rate of decay and half-life

Radioactive decay is a first-order process. The **activity** $A$ (decays per second; becquerel, Bq $= 1$ decay/s, or the older curie, Ci) is proportional to the number of nuclei $N$, so

$$N = N_0\left(\tfrac12\right)^{t/t_{1/2}} = N_0e^{-\lambda t}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}.$$

The same equation applies to activity, and so to the dose of a radiopharmaceutical remaining in a sample. To find the time to reach a given fraction $f$: $t = t_{1/2}\log_2(1/f)$.

### Medical isotopes

Technetium-99m ($t_{1/2} \approx 6\ \text{h}$) is a gamma emitter used in many imaging studies; fluorine-18 ($t_{1/2} \approx 110\ \text{min}$) is a positron emitter used in PET scans; iodine-131 ($t_{1/2} \approx 8\ \text{d}$) is used for thyroid treatment; cobalt-60 has been used for external-beam therapy. A short half-life gives high activity (many decays per second) from a small amount of tracer and a low long-term dose, but requires prompt preparation. Exposure is also measured in absorbed dose (gray, $1\ \text{Gy} = 1\ \text{J/kg}$) and equivalent dose (sievert), which weights the type of radiation.

### Binding energy

The mass of a nucleus is less than the sum of its separate nucleons. This **mass defect** $\Delta m$ appears as the **binding energy**:

$$E = \Delta m\,c^2, \qquad 1\ \text{u} \leftrightarrow 931.5\ \text{MeV}.$$

Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron-56. **Fission** of very heavy nuclei and **fusion** of very light nuclei both move toward the peak and release energy.

@example
title: Dose remaining after several half-lives
problem: A patient is given $20.0\ \text{mCi}$ of a radiotracer with $t_{1/2} = 6.0\ \text{h}$. How much activity remains after $24\ \text{h}$ (ignoring biological clearance)?
step: Count half-lives: $24/6.0 = 4$.
step: Fraction remaining: $(1/2)^4 = 1/16$.
step: Activity: $20.0/16 = 1.25\ \text{mCi}$.

@example
title: Balancing a decay equation
problem: Write the alpha decay of radium-226 ($Z = 88$).
step: An alpha particle is $^4_2\text{He}$, so the daughter has $A = 226 - 4 = 222$ and $Z = 88 - 2 = 86$.
step: Element $86$ is radon: $^{226}_{88}\text{Ra} \to {}^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He}$.
step: Check: $226 = 222 + 4$ and $88 = 86 + 2$.

@mistakes
- Changing $A$ in beta decay (only $Z$ changes) or forgetting that alpha decay lowers $A$ by $4$.
- Using a fraction of the half-life as if it were a whole number of half-lives.
- Confusing activity decay (a physical half-life) with biological elimination, which gives an effective half-life.
- Computing mass defect with atomic masses and nuclear masses mixed.

@practice num
prompt: Iodine-131 ($Z = 53$) decays by beta-minus emission. What is the atomic number of the daughter nuclide?
answer: 54
check: 53 + 1
hint: In beta-minus decay a neutron becomes a proton.
solution: $^{131}_{53}\text{I} \to {}^{131}_{54}\text{Xe} + {}^{0}_{-1}e$. The charge balances: $53 = 54 + (-1)$.

@practice num
prompt: Iodine-131 has $t_{1/2} = 8.02\ \text{d}$. What fraction of a sample remains after $24.06\ \text{d}$?
answer: 0.125
check: 0.5 ** (24.06 / 8.02)
tol: 1%
hint: Divide the elapsed time by the half-life.
solution: $24.06/8.02 = 3.00$ half-lives, so the fraction is $(1/2)^3 = 0.125$.

@practice num
prompt: A PET dose of fluorine-18 ($t_{1/2} = 110\ \text{min}$) has an activity of $370\ \text{MBq}$ at preparation. What is the activity after $330\ \text{min}$?
answer: 46.25
check: 370 * 0.5 ** (330 / 110)
tol: 1%
unit: MBq
hint: Find the number of half-lives, then halve that many times.
solution: $330/110 = 3$ half-lives, so $370 \times (1/2)^3 = 370/8 = 46.3\ \text{MBq}$.

@practice mc
prompt: A nuclide emits radiation and its daughter has an atomic number lower by $2$ and a mass number lower by $4$. What was emitted?
choice: A beta particle
choice: A positron
choice*: An alpha particle
choice: A gamma ray
hint: Which particle consists of two protons and two neutrons?
solution: Loss of $2$ in $Z$ and $4$ in $A$ is exactly an alpha particle, $^4_2\text{He}$. Beta decay and positron emission leave $A$ unchanged, and gamma emission changes neither.

@quiz num
prompt: Iodine-123 ($t_{1/2} = 13.2\ \text{h}$) is used for thyroid imaging. How long, in hours, until its activity falls to $10\%$ of the initial value?
answer: 43.85
check: 13.2 * log2(1 / 0.10)
tol: 1%
unit: h
hint: Solve $0.10 = (1/2)^{t/t_{1/2}}$ using logarithms.
hint: $t = t_{1/2}\log_2(10)$ and $\log_2(10) = 3.32$.
solution: $t = 13.2 \times \log_2(10) = 13.2 \times 3.322 = 43.9\ \text{h}$, between three and four half-lives.

@quiz num
prompt: Helium-4 has a nuclear mass of $4.001506\ \text{u}$. A proton has mass $1.007276\ \text{u}$ and a neutron $1.008665\ \text{u}$. Find the binding energy in MeV ($1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}/c^2$).
answer: 28.3
check: (2 * 1.007276 + 2 * 1.008665 - 4.001506) * 931.5
tol: 1%
unit: MeV
hint: The mass defect is the mass of the separated nucleons minus the nuclear mass.
solution: $\Delta m = 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) - 4.001506 = 0.030376\ \text{u}$. $E = 0.030376 \times 931.5 = 28.3\ \text{MeV}$.
