### Anatomy and benign disease

Milk is made in lobules and carried by ducts to the nipple; most cancers arise in the terminal duct lobular unit. Lymph drains mainly to **axillary nodes** (levels I to III relative to the pectoralis minor) and also to internal mammary nodes. Hormonal fluctuation causes cyclic pain and nodularity.

- **Fibrocystic changes**: very common, cyclic, bilateral pain and lumpy breasts; cysts that aspirate clear fluid and collapse. Nonproliferative changes carry no added risk; **atypical hyperplasia** raises risk about fourfold.
- **Fibroadenoma**: the most common benign tumor in women under 30; firm, rubbery, mobile, painless "breast mouse"; estrogen-responsive (may grow in pregnancy); biphasic epithelial and stromal histology.
- **Phyllodes tumor**: large, rapidly growing, leaf-like architecture, usually age 40 to 50; may be benign, borderline or malignant; wide local excision.
- **Intraductal papilloma**: single-duct, often bloody nipple discharge, subareolar.
- **Mammary duct ectasia**: periareolar, perimenopausal, thick green-brown discharge.
- **Fat necrosis**: after trauma or surgery; mimics cancer on imaging.
- **Lactational mastitis**: *Staphylococcus aureus*; continue breastfeeding, give dicloxacillin or cephalexin (consider MRSA coverage); drain abscess.
- **Galactorrhea**: bilateral milky discharge from prolactin excess (prolactinoma, hypothyroidism, dopamine antagonists such as antipsychotics and metoclopramide).
- **Gynecomastia** in men: puberty, drugs (spironolactone), cirrhosis, Klinefelter syndrome.

### Breast cancer

**Risk factors**: older age, female sex, prior atypical hyperplasia or LCIS, early menarche and late menopause, nulliparity or late first birth, combined hormone therapy, obesity after menopause, alcohol, chest irradiation, dense breasts, and family history, especially **BRCA1 and BRCA2** (lifetime risk of breast cancer roughly 45 to 70%, plus ovarian cancer risk; BRCA1 tumors are often triple-negative). Screening of carriers includes MRI and consideration of risk-reducing surgery.

**Histology**:
- **Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS)**: confined within ducts; often found as calcifications on mammography; treated with excision with or without radiation.
- **Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)**: incidental, often bilateral; a risk marker.
- **Invasive ductal carcinoma (no special type)**: most common (about 70 to 80%).
- **Invasive lobular carcinoma**: loss of E-cadherin, single-file infiltration, often bilateral and diffusely infiltrative, may metastasize to unusual sites (GI tract, ovary).
- **Inflammatory breast cancer**: erythema, edema and **peau d'orange** from dermal lymphatic invasion, without a discrete mass; aggressive.
- **Paget disease of the nipple**: eczematous nipple lesion; underlying DCIS or invasive carcinoma; biopsy shows large clear Paget cells.

**Receptors guide therapy**: **ER and PR-positive** disease receives endocrine therapy (**tamoxifen**, or **aromatase inhibitors** such as anastrozole and letrozole in postmenopausal women); **HER2-positive** disease receives trastuzumab (a monoclonal antibody; cardiotoxicity); **triple-negative** disease relies on chemotherapy and, in selected cases, immunotherapy. Tamoxifen acts as an estrogen antagonist in the breast and agonist in the uterus and bone: raises endometrial cancer and thromboembolism risk. Aromatase inhibitors cause bone loss and arthralgia. Staging uses tumor size, nodes and metastasis (bone, lung, liver, brain). Surgery is lumpectomy plus radiation (equal survival to mastectomy for most) or mastectomy, with sentinel lymph node biopsy.

### Screening

Guidelines differ: the USPSTF recommends biennial mammography from age 40 to 74; the American Cancer Society offers annual screening from 40 to 44 as an option, annual from 45 to 54, then every 1 to 2 years; ACOG supports offering screening from 40. Higher-risk women (for example, BRCA carriers or chest radiation in youth) start earlier with MRI.

### Workup of a breast mass

Take the history (duration, cyclic change, family history, nipple discharge) and perform a clinical breast examination (size, mobility, skin, nipple, axillary nodes). Then use **triple assessment**: clinical exam, imaging and tissue sampling; the results must agree. In women **under 30**, begin with **ultrasound** (dense breast tissue); in women **30 and older**, perform **diagnostic mammography plus ultrasound**. A simple cyst is aspirated if symptomatic. A solid mass is sampled by **image-guided core needle biopsy** (preferred over fine-needle aspiration because it gives architecture and receptors). Discordant results mean repeat biopsy or excision. Excisional biopsy is not the initial test.

**BI-RADS**: 0, incomplete (needs additional imaging); 1, negative; 2, benign; 3, probably benign (under 2% risk; short-interval follow-up); 4, suspicious (biopsy); 5, highly suggestive of malignancy (95% or more; biopsy); 6, known biopsy-proven cancer.

@example
title: A young woman with a breast lump
problem: A 24-year-old woman notices a painless, firm, mobile 2-cm right breast lump. What is the evaluation?
step: Age under 30, a mobile, discrete mass: most likely a fibroadenoma. Mammography is less sensitive in dense young breasts and gives radiation, so ultrasound is the first imaging test.
step: Ultrasound shows a well-circumscribed oval hypoechoic mass, parallel to the skin.
step: A core needle biopsy confirms fibroadenoma if the lesion is palpable or enlarging (triple assessment: concordant findings).
step: Management is observation or excision if growing or symptomatic; reassure about the low malignant risk.

@example
title: A suspicious mass in a middle-aged woman
problem: A 52-year-old woman has a hard, fixed, irregular 2.5-cm mass in the upper outer quadrant of the left breast with a palpable axillary node. Mammography shows a spiculated mass (BI-RADS 5). What are the next steps?
step: Clinical findings and imaging both suggest malignancy: concordant high suspicion.
step: Proceed to image-guided core needle biopsy (not excisional biopsy) with a marker clip.
step: Pathology: grade, histology, ER, PR and HER2 status. Stage with exam and imaging as indicated by stage.
step: Treatment is planned multidisciplinary: surgery (with sentinel or axillary node assessment), radiation, and systemic therapy guided by receptors.

@mistakes
- Relying on mammography alone in a young woman with a palpable mass; a negative mammogram does not exclude cancer in a palpable mass, so ultrasound and tissue are required.
- Using fine-needle aspiration as the standard instead of core needle biopsy.
- Calling LCIS cancer needing mastectomy; it is a risk marker.
- Forgetting that tamoxifen increases endometrial cancer risk, and aromatase inhibitors are for postmenopausal women.

@practice self
prompt: A 52-year-old postmenopausal woman presents with a new 2-cm hard, irregular, nontender left breast mass. Outline the workup (history, examination, imaging, tissue diagnosis) and name the pathology results that guide treatment.
hint: Follow triple assessment: clinical, imaging, pathology; then receptors.
solution: History: duration, family history of breast or ovarian cancer, hormone use, nipple discharge, prior biopsies. Examination: mass size, mobility, skin or nipple changes, both axillae and supraclavicular nodes. Imaging: diagnostic bilateral mammography with targeted ultrasound of the mass and axilla. Tissue: image-guided core needle biopsy with a marker clip (not excisional); biopsy of any suspicious node. Pathology: histologic type, grade, ER, PR, HER2 (and Ki-67 as indicated) to guide endocrine, anti-HER2 or chemotherapy. Confirm concordance of clinical, imaging and pathology findings; if discordant, repeat sampling or excise. Further staging imaging for advanced stage or symptoms.
