### Cervical cancer

Persistent infection with high-risk **HPV (types 16 and 18)** drives cervical carcinogenesis: viral **E6** degrades p53 and **E7** inactivates Rb. Squamous cell carcinoma (about 70%) arises from the transformation zone through cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN 1 to 3); adenocarcinoma is next. Risk factors: HPV, early intercourse, multiple partners, smoking, HIV, immunosuppression. Presentation: postcoital bleeding, discharge. Locally advanced disease obstructs the ureters and **renal failure** is a leading cause of death.

**Screening** (US guidelines differ in details, so know the principle): ages 21 to 29, cytology (Pap) every 3 years; ages 30 to 65, cytology every 3 years, **primary high-risk HPV testing** every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. Screening stops after 65 with adequate negative history, and is not needed after hysterectomy with cervix removal for benign disease. The American Cancer Society favors primary HPV testing every 5 years from age 25. Abnormal results: high-grade lesions (**HSIL**, ASC-H) or HPV 16 or 18 positivity go to **colposcopy with biopsy**; low-risk results are repeated at an interval set by risk. CIN 2 to 3 is treated by excision (**LEEP** or cold-knife conization). Treatment of invasive cancer: early stage by surgery; stages IB3 and above by **chemoradiation with cisplatin**.

### Endometrium and myometrium

**Abnormal uterine bleeding** is classified by PALM-COEIN (Polyp, Adenomyosis, Leiomyoma, Malignancy and hyperplasia; Coagulopathy, Ovulatory dysfunction, Endometrial, Iatrogenic, Not otherwise classified). Start with a pregnancy test.

- **Leiomyoma (fibroid)**: most common benign pelvic tumor; estrogen-dependent, grows in pregnancy and shrinks after menopause. Presents with heavy menses, pelvic pressure, enlarged irregular firm uterus. Treatment: observation, hormonal methods, GnRH antagonists, uterine artery embolization, myomectomy, hysterectomy. Leiomyosarcoma is rare and does not usually arise from fibroids.
- **Adenomyosis**: endometrial glands within the myometrium; diffusely enlarged, globular, tender uterus, with dysmenorrhea and heavy bleeding; diagnosed with ultrasound or MRI; definitive therapy is hysterectomy.
- **Endometriosis**: functioning endometrial tissue outside the uterus (ovary, peritoneum), causing cyclic pelvic pain (dysmenorrhea, dyspareunia, dyschezia), infertility, "chocolate cysts" (endometriomas) and fixed retroverted uterus with nodularity. Laparoscopy with biopsy is the definitive diagnosis, although empiric treatment is common: NSAIDs, combined hormonal contraception, progestins or GnRH analogs; surgery for severe disease.
- **Endometrial cancer**: **unopposed estrogen** (obesity, PCOS and anovulation, nulliparity, early menarche or late menopause, tamoxifen, estrogen-secreting tumors) promotes hyperplasia; Lynch syndrome is a hereditary risk. **Any postmenopausal bleeding is cancer until proven otherwise.** Evaluate with transvaginal ultrasound (endometrial thickness of 4 mm or less makes cancer unlikely) or endometrial biopsy. Endometrioid adenocarcinoma (type I) is most common; serous carcinoma (type II, p53 mutations) is aggressive. Treatment is total hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and staging. Progestins protect the endometrium.

### Ovarian disease

Functional cysts (follicular, corpus luteum) are common and usually resolve. **Ovarian torsion** causes sudden unilateral pain with nausea and a mass; urgent laparoscopy. **Epithelial tumors** (serous cystadenocarcinoma is the most common ovarian malignancy; CA-125 is nonspecific and used for monitoring) arise in older women; risk is increased by nulliparity and **BRCA1 and BRCA2** mutations, and decreased by oral contraceptives, pregnancy and tubal ligation. Treatment is surgical staging and debulking with carboplatin and paclitaxel; screening is not recommended in average-risk women.

### Choosing tests and treatment for vaginitis (review of week 9)

Measure vaginal pH, then add microscopy: pH above 4.5 with clue cells or a positive whiff test is bacterial vaginosis (metronidazole); pH above 4.5 with motile flagellates or strawberry cervix is trichomoniasis (NAAT, metronidazole, treat partners); normal pH with pseudohyphae and itch is candidiasis (fluconazole or a topical azole). Atrophic vaginitis in a postmenopausal woman (thin pale mucosa, pH above 4.7) is treated with vaginal estrogen. Test for gonorrhea and chlamydia when cervicitis or risk is present.

@example
title: Abnormal screening result
problem: A 41-year-old woman has primary HPV screening that is positive for HPV 16. Cytology is normal. What is the next step?
step: HPV 16 has the highest risk of progression to CIN 3 or worse among HPV types.
step: HPV 16 or 18 positivity carries a risk high enough that colposcopy with biopsy is indicated even with normal cytology.
step: Perform colposcopy with directed biopsy and endocervical sampling as appropriate.
step: If CIN 2 to 3 is found, treat with excision (LEEP); invasive cancer is staged and treated by stage.

@example
title: Postmenopausal bleeding
problem: A 62-year-old obese woman has had vaginal bleeding for 2 weeks, 11 years after menopause. Transvaginal ultrasound shows an endometrial stripe of 12 mm. What is the next step?
step: Postmenopausal bleeding requires exclusion of endometrial cancer; obesity increases risk through peripheral aromatization of androgens to estrone.
step: A stripe above 4 mm is abnormal.
step: Perform endometrial biopsy (office sampling), proceeding to hysteroscopy with dilation and curettage if the biopsy is inadequate or the bleeding persists.
step: If endometrioid adenocarcinoma is found, treatment is hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy and surgical staging.

@mistakes
- Treating postmenopausal bleeding as atrophy without excluding endometrial cancer.
- Ordering HPV testing as routine screening in a 22-year-old; most US guidelines use cytology alone from 21 to 29 because HPV infection is common and usually transient at that age.
- Equating a high CA-125 with ovarian cancer; it is raised in endometriosis, PID, menses and other conditions.
- Confusing leiomyoma (focal, firm, irregular uterus) with adenomyosis (globular, tender uterus).

@practice self
prompt: Choose tests and treatment for each patient. (1) A 27-year-old with thin gray discharge, fishy odor, vaginal pH 5.4 and no itching. (2) A 30-year-old with intense vulvar itching, thick white discharge and pH 4.2. (3) A 22-year-old with frothy yellow-green discharge, dysuria, and a new partner. State the test that distinguishes each condition and the first-line treatment.
hint: Start with vaginal pH; then decide which microscopy finding and which test will confirm.
solution: (1) Bacterial vaginosis: pH above 4.5, positive whiff test, clue cells on wet mount (Amsel criteria); metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, or vaginal metronidazole or clindamycin. (2) Vulvovaginal candidiasis: normal pH, budding yeast and pseudohyphae on KOH preparation; fluconazole 150 mg once or a topical azole. (3) Trichomoniasis: pH above 4.5, motile flagellated protozoa on wet mount or a NAAT; metronidazole 500 mg twice daily for 7 days, treat the partner, and test for gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis and HIV.
