### Infertility

**Infertility** is failure to conceive after **12 months** of regular unprotected intercourse (**6 months** if the woman is 35 or older). Evaluate sooner if there is amenorrhea, known tubal disease, endometriosis, or a history of cancer therapy. Causes split roughly into male factor, ovulatory dysfunction, tubal or uterine factor, and unexplained, and a male factor contributes in about 40 to 50% of couples, so both partners are tested together.

| Question | Test |
|---|---|
| Does she ovulate? | Regular cycles; midluteal progesterone (above about 3 ng/mL); ovulation predictor kits |
| Is the ovarian reserve adequate? | Anti-Müllerian hormone, antral follicle count, early follicular FSH and estradiol |
| Are the tubes and uterus normal? | Hysterosalpingogram (tubal patency, uterine cavity), saline sonography, hysteroscopy |
| Is semen normal? | Semen analysis after 2 to 7 days of abstinence |

Also check TSH and prolactin if cycles are irregular. Treatment follows the cause: **letrozole** (first-line for ovulation induction in PCOS) or **clomiphene** (a selective estrogen receptor modulator that blocks hypothalamic estrogen feedback and raises FSH and LH), gonadotropins, intrauterine insemination, **in vitro fertilization (IVF)**, or tubal or varicocele surgery in selected cases. Risks include multiple gestation and **ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome** (ovarian enlargement, ascites, hemoconcentration, thromboembolism, occurring with hCG trigger).

### Contraception: how effective and why

Effectiveness is reported as the percentage of women with an unintended pregnancy in the first year of use, for **perfect use** and **typical use**. The **Pearl Index** is the number of pregnancies per 100 woman-years of exposure:

$$\text{Pearl Index} = \frac{\text{pregnancies} \times 1200}{\text{woman-months of exposure}}$$

Approximate first-year typical-use failure rates (US sources): implant about 0.1%, levonorgestrel IUD 0.1 to 0.4%, copper IUD about 0.8%, injectable DMPA about 4%, pill, patch and ring about 7%, male condom about 13%, withdrawal about 20%, no method about 85%. Methods that need no action after placement (**long-acting reversible contraception, LARC**: IUDs and implant) have typical-use rates close to perfect-use rates; methods that depend on correct use perform much worse in practice.

### Mechanisms

- **Combined hormonal contraceptives** (pill, patch, ring; ethinyl estradiol plus a progestin): suppress GnRH, LH and the LH surge, so no ovulation; thicken cervical mucus; thin the endometrium.
- **Progestin-only methods** (norethindrone pill, DMPA injection every 3 months, etonogestrel implant): the injection and implant suppress ovulation; all thicken mucus. DMPA reduces bone density and delays return of fertility (months).
- **Levonorgestrel IUD**: mainly local progestin effects (thick mucus, thin endometrium; ovulation usually continues); reduces menstrual bleeding and is used for heavy menstrual bleeding and endometrial protection.
- **Copper IUD**: copper ions are toxic to sperm and cause a sterile inflammatory reaction; no hormones; may increase bleeding and cramping. It is also the most effective emergency contraception.
- **Barrier methods**: condoms (the only method that also reduces STI transmission), diaphragm, spermicide.
- **Lactational amenorrhea**: over 98% effective when the infant is under 6 months, fully breastfeeding and the mother is amenorrheic.
- **Sterilization**: vasectomy (confirm azoospermia by semen analysis after about 3 months) and tubal occlusion (permanent; failures can be ectopic).

### Safety: US Medical Eligibility Criteria

Estrogen-containing methods carry risk of venous thromboembolism, stroke and myocardial infarction. They are generally contraindicated (category 4, unacceptable risk) in: **migraine with aura**, age 35 or older and smoking 15 or more cigarettes per day, history of venous thromboembolism or known thrombophilia, current breast cancer, systemic lupus erythematosus with positive antiphospholipid antibodies, **hypertension of 160/100 or greater**, complicated valvular heart disease, ischemic heart disease or stroke, severe cirrhosis or liver tumors, diabetes with vascular complications, and under 21 days postpartum. The copper IUD, and for most of these conditions progestin-only methods or the levonorgestrel IUD, are alternatives; check the U.S. MEC category for each condition (for example, current breast cancer makes all hormonal methods category 4).

### Emergency contraception

**Copper IUD** (within 5 days; more than 99% effective), **ulipristal 30 mg** (selective progesterone receptor modulator, within 5 days), and **levonorgestrel 1.5 mg** (best within 72 hours; efficacy falls with obesity, BMI above about 30). Typical adult doses for study; confirm with current references. Emergency contraception does not terminate an established pregnancy.

@example
title: Calculating a Pearl Index
problem: In a study, a new contraceptive is used by women for a total of 8000 woman-months and 10 pregnancies occur. What is the Pearl Index?
step: Pearl Index is pregnancies per 100 woman-years. One year is 12 months, so 100 woman-years equals 1200 woman-months.
step: Compute: $\frac{10 \times 1200}{8000} = 1.5$.
step: Interpretation: about 1.5 pregnancies per 100 women using the method for a year. Pearl Index assumes constant risk over time and ignores dropouts, so life-table analysis is also used.

@example
title: Contraception for a woman with migraine with aura
problem: A 28-year-old woman has migraine with aura and asks about contraception. Which methods are appropriate?
step: Migraine with aura increases the risk of ischemic stroke, and estrogen adds to that risk. Estrogen-containing methods (pill, patch, ring) are contraindicated.
step: Remaining options: levonorgestrel IUD, copper IUD, etonogestrel implant, DMPA, progestin-only pill, and barrier methods.
step: If she wants the highest effectiveness with no daily action, offer the implant or an IUD, which carry typical-use failure rates under 1%.
step: Counsel on bleeding changes with the implant, and add condoms if STI protection is needed.

@mistakes
- Using perfect-use rates when counseling; typical use is what patients experience.
- Prescribing a combined hormonal method to a woman with migraine with aura, uncontrolled hypertension, or active VTE.
- Forgetting that only condoms reduce STI risk among common methods.
- Telling a woman with obesity that levonorgestrel emergency contraception is as reliable as ulipristal or the copper IUD.

@practice self
prompt: A 19-year-old woman with regular cycles asks whether she should use a daily pill or a long-acting method. Write a brief counseling script that covers effectiveness (typical use), mechanism, common side effects and STI protection.
hint: Lead with the typical-use difference, then explain each method in plain language.
solution: "Methods you do not have to remember, the implant and IUDs, work best in real life: fewer than 1 in 100 women get pregnant in a year, compared with about 7 in 100 with the pill because pills are sometimes missed. The pill contains estrogen and progestin, stops ovulation and thickens mucus; side effects include nausea, breast tenderness and spotting, and rarely blood clots. The implant releases a progestin that stops ovulation and often causes irregular bleeding; the hormonal IUD thins the lining and usually lightens periods; the copper IUD has no hormones but can make periods heavier. None of these protects against infections, so condoms are still advised if you or a partner might have an STI. All can be removed and fertility returns quickly, except that the injection can delay it. What matters most to you?"
rubric: Compares typical-use effectiveness correctly (LARC below 1% versus about 7% for pills).
rubric: Explains mechanisms accurately in plain language.
rubric: Mentions side effects and the need for condoms for STI protection.
rubric: Invites the patient's preferences.
