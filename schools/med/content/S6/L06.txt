### From fertilization to implantation

Fertilization occurs in the **ampulla** of the fallopian tube. The sperm completes the **acrosome reaction**; fusion triggers the **cortical reaction**, which blocks polyspermy and lets the secondary oocyte complete meiosis II. The zygote divides to a **morula** and **blastocyst**; the blastocyst implants in the secretory endometrium about **6 to 10 days** after ovulation. The outer trophoblast differentiates into **cytotrophoblast** (proliferative cells) and **syncytiotrophoblast** (invasive, multinucleate, hormone-secreting). The syncytiotrophoblast makes **hCG**, whose alpha subunit is shared with LH, FSH and TSH; the beta subunit is specific, so assays detect beta-hCG.

### hCG and the corpus luteum

hCG acts on the LH receptor and rescues the corpus luteum, maintaining progesterone until the placenta takes over (the luteal-placental shift, around 8 to 10 weeks). Serum hCG is detectable about 8 to 11 days after conception and in early viable pregnancy it roughly **doubles every 48 hours** (a minimum rise of about 49% in 48 hours is typical when the initial value is below 1500 mIU/mL). It peaks around 8 to 11 weeks then declines. Very high hCG occurs in molar pregnancy and multiple gestation; slow rise suggests ectopic or failing pregnancy. hCG can stimulate the TSH receptor, causing a transient fall in TSH in the first trimester, and drives nausea and vomiting.

### The placenta

The placenta provides gas and nutrient exchange. It makes **progesterone** (maintains the endometrium, relaxes smooth muscle, raises ventilatory drive), **estrogens** (with the fetal adrenal and liver: estriol), **human placental lactogen (hPL)** (an insulin antagonist that mobilizes fat and shifts maternal fuel use to the fetus, predisposing to gestational diabetes) and **relaxin**. Fetal circulation: oxygenated blood travels in the single **umbilical vein**, passes the **ductus venosus** into the inferior vena cava, crosses the **foramen ovale** to the left atrium, and the **ductus arteriosus** diverts pulmonary artery blood to the aorta. Two **umbilical arteries** return deoxygenated blood to the placenta.

### Maternal adaptations

| System | Change |
|---|---|
| Cardiovascular | Cardiac output up 30 to 50%; heart rate up; systemic vascular resistance down, so BP is lowest in the second trimester; flow murmurs |
| Blood volume | Plasma volume up about 40 to 50%, red cell mass up less (about 20 to 30%): **physiologic dilutional anemia** |
| Renal | GFR up about 50%; serum creatinine falls (about 0.4 to 0.8 mg/dL is normal); mild hydronephrosis |
| Respiratory | Tidal volume up; functional residual capacity down; PaCO2 about 28 to 32 mmHg with compensated respiratory alkalosis; respiratory rate unchanged |
| Coagulation | Hypercoagulable: higher fibrinogen and factors VII, VIII and X; lower protein S |
| GI | Lower esophageal sphincter relaxation (heartburn), slower motility |
| Endocrine | Higher total thyroid hormone (TBG rises) with normal free T4; insulin resistance from hPL |

A serum creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL or a PaCO2 of 40 mmHg is therefore not normal in pregnancy and may signal renal disease or impending respiratory failure.

### Dating and routine prenatal care

**Gestational age** is counted from the last menstrual period (LMP); a full-term pregnancy is about 40 weeks (280 days). **Naegele rule**: expected date of delivery = LMP plus 7 days, minus 3 months, plus 1 year. Dating is confirmed or revised by first-trimester ultrasound (crown-rump length).

- **First visit**: blood type and Rh, antibody screen, CBC, rubella and varicella immunity, hepatitis B surface antigen, syphilis, HIV, urine culture, chlamydia and gonorrhea screening, and cervical cancer screening if due. Start **folic acid 400 mcg** daily before conception (4 mg for a previous neural tube defect). Offer aneuploidy screening.
- **Aneuploidy screening**: first trimester (10 to 13 weeks): nuchal translucency plus PAPP-A and free beta-hCG, or **cell-free DNA** (highest sensitivity for trisomy 21, from 10 weeks; a screening test, so positives need diagnostic testing by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis). **Quad screen** (15 to 22 weeks): AFP, hCG, unconjugated estriol, inhibin A. Trisomy 21: low AFP, low estriol, high hCG, high inhibin A. Trisomy 18: all four low (hCG, AFP, estriol low; inhibin A normal or low). **High AFP**: neural tube defect, abdominal wall defect, multiple gestation or incorrect dates; confirm dating, then detailed ultrasound.
- **Anatomy ultrasound** at 18 to 22 weeks.
- **Gestational diabetes screening** at 24 to 28 weeks: a 50-g glucose challenge (positive threshold 130, 135 or 140 mg/dL at 1 hour, depending on the practice), then a diagnostic 100-g 3-hour OGTT (two abnormal values using Carpenter and Coustan limits of 95, 180, 155 and 140 mg/dL), or a one-step 75-g 2-hour OGTT (one abnormal value: fasting at least 92, 1 hour at least 180, 2 hours at least 153 mg/dL).
- **Rh-negative mothers** with a negative antibody screen receive **Rh immune globulin** (anti-D) at 28 weeks and within 72 hours of delivery of an Rh-positive baby, and after bleeding or invasive procedures.
- **Group B streptococcus** rectovaginal culture at 36 0/7 to 37 6/7 weeks; positive cultures get intrapartum penicillin G.
- **Vaccines**: inactivated influenza, Tdap at 27 to 36 weeks of each pregnancy, COVID-19, and RSV vaccine at 32 to 36 weeks in season. Live vaccines (MMR, varicella) are avoided.

@example
title: Dating a pregnancy with Naegele's rule
problem: A woman's last menstrual period began on June 12. What is her expected date of delivery?
step: Add 7 days: June 12 becomes June 19.
step: Subtract 3 months: June 19 becomes March 19.
step: Add 1 year: the expected date of delivery is March 19 of the following year.
step: Check against an early ultrasound; if the crown-rump length dates differ from the LMP dates by more than the accepted threshold for that gestational age (for example, more than 7 days at 9 to 13 6/7 weeks), the ultrasound dating is used.

@example
title: Reading a quad screen
problem: At 17 weeks, a woman's quad screen shows low AFP, low unconjugated estriol, high hCG and high inhibin A. What is the interpretation and next step?
step: This pattern (high hCG and inhibin A, low AFP and estriol) is characteristic of trisomy 21.
step: The quad screen is a screening test: a positive result gives risk, not diagnosis.
step: Confirm dating by ultrasound, offer detailed ultrasound and counsel about diagnostic testing with amniocentesis, or cell-free DNA as a further screen.
step: Amniocentesis gives a karyotype and is done after 15 weeks, with a procedure-related loss risk that is small and is now estimated below 1 in 500.

@mistakes
- Treating a creatinine of 1.0 mg/dL as normal in pregnancy. GFR is up, so values fall below the nonpregnant range.
- Calling a normal PaCO2 of 40 mmHg reassuring at term; it suggests inadequate ventilation.
- Treating cell-free DNA as diagnostic. Positive results need CVS or amniocentesis.
- Forgetting that hPL is anti-insulin and drives gestational diabetes.

@practice self
prompt: Compare the combined oral contraceptive, the levonorgestrel IUD, the copper IUD and the male condom by mechanism, typical-use effectiveness, one important adverse effect or contraindication and what each does for STI risk. Present your answer as a table or ordered list.
hint: For each method write mechanism, typical-use failure, and one safety point, in the same order.
solution: Combined oral contraceptive: suppresses LH surge and ovulation, thickens mucus, thins endometrium; typical failure about 7% per year; contraindicated with migraine with aura, VTE history, smoking at 35 or older (15 or more cigarettes per day) and severe hypertension; no STI protection. Levonorgestrel IUD: local progestin thickens mucus and thins endometrium (ovulation often continues); typical failure about 0.1 to 0.4%; lighter periods, rare uterine perforation, expulsion; no STI protection. Copper IUD: copper is spermicidal and causes sterile inflammation; failure about 0.8%; heavier, crampier periods; no hormone and no STI protection; also most effective emergency contraception. Male condom: physical barrier; typical failure about 13%; latex allergy; reduces risk of many STIs.
rubric: Gives mechanisms accurately for all four methods.
rubric: Orders effectiveness correctly: IUDs best, pill intermediate, condom lowest of the four.
rubric: Gives a valid safety point for each.
rubric: States that only the condom reduces STI transmission.
