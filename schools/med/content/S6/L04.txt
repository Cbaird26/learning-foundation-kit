### The ovarian cycle in two cells

The ovary builds estrogen with a cooperative two-cell system. **Theca cells** respond to **LH** by making androgens (androstenedione, testosterone) from cholesterol. **Granulosa cells** respond to **FSH** by expressing **aromatase**, which converts those androgens to estradiol. Remember "LH to theca, FSH to granulosa" and "aromatase is FSH dependent". Granulosa cells also make **inhibin B**, which inhibits FSH. After ovulation the follicle becomes the **corpus luteum**, which makes progesterone (and estradiol and inhibin A) in response to LH.

### The cycle (28-day model)

- **Early follicular phase (days 1 to 5).** Falling progesterone and estrogen at the end of the previous cycle release FSH from negative feedback; FSH recruits a cohort of antral follicles, and menses occur.
- **Mid-to-late follicular phase.** One dominant follicle emerges. Rising estradiol exerts **negative feedback** on FSH, which declines and causes the other follicles to undergo atresia. The endometrium enters the **proliferative phase** (estrogen), with straight glands and growing stroma; cervical mucus becomes thin and stretchy (spinnbarkeit, ferning).
- **Ovulation (about day 14).** Sustained high estradiol (roughly 200 pg/mL for about 2 days) switches to **positive feedback**, producing the **LH surge** (with an FSH surge). Ovulation follows about 34 to 36 hours after the LH surge begins (10 to 12 hours after the peak). The primary oocyte, arrested in prophase I since fetal life, resumes meiosis I and is released as a **secondary oocyte arrested in metaphase II**; meiosis II completes only if fertilization occurs.
- **Luteal phase (days 15 to 28).** The corpus luteum secretes progesterone, which raises basal body temperature by about 0.3 to 0.5 degrees C, makes cervical mucus thick, and converts the endometrium to the **secretory phase** (tortuous glands, vacuoles, edematous stroma) for implantation. The luteal phase is a nearly constant **14 days**; variation in cycle length comes from the follicular phase.
- **Menstruation.** Without hCG rescue, the corpus luteum degenerates in about 14 days into the corpus albicans; progesterone drops and the functional endometrium sheds (prostaglandin-mediated spiral artery vasoconstriction).

**Dating ovulation:** ovulation day equals the cycle length minus 14. The **fertile window** is the 5 days before and the day of ovulation (sperm survive up to 5 days; the oocyte survives about a day).

To draw the cycle: FSH has a small early peak and then a mid-cycle surge; LH is flat until a large surge at ovulation; estradiol rises gradually through the follicular phase, peaks just before the LH surge, dips, then has a second smaller luteal peak; progesterone is near zero until ovulation, then rises to a luteal peak and falls; inhibin B peaks in the follicular phase, inhibin A in the luteal phase.

### Amenorrhea

**Primary amenorrhea**: no menses by age 15 with normal secondary sexual development (or by 13 with none). Causes include Turner syndrome, Müllerian agenesis, androgen insensitivity, hypothalamic or pituitary disease, and an imperforate hymen. **Secondary amenorrhea**: absence of menses for 3 months in a woman who was previously regular (6 months if irregular). **First step is a pregnancy test**, then TSH, prolactin and FSH. Interpretation:

- **High FSH** (primary ovarian insufficiency, menopause).
- **High prolactin** (prolactinoma, dopamine antagonists, hypothyroidism); treat the cause, cabergoline for prolactinoma.
- **Low or normal FSH and LH** with low estrogen (functional hypothalamic amenorrhea from weight loss, exercise or stress; pituitary disease).
- **Normal hormones but a history of dilation and curettage or infection**: Asherman syndrome (intrauterine adhesions); confirm by hysteroscopy or sonohysterography.

**Primary ovarian insufficiency (POI)**: loss of ovarian function before age 40 with FSH in the menopausal range on two occasions about a month apart. Workup includes karyotype and FMR1 premutation testing.

### PCOS

Polycystic ovary syndrome is diagnosed (Rotterdam criteria) when two of three are present after excluding mimics (thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, nonclassic CAH): **oligo- or anovulation**, **clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism** (hirsutism, acne, raised free testosterone), and **polycystic ovarian morphology**. Insulin resistance, obesity and the elevated LH to FSH ratio are common but not diagnostic. Management is individualized: weight loss, combined hormonal contraceptive for menstrual regularity and androgen effects, metformin, spironolactone for hirsutism, and letrozole for ovulation induction. Unopposed estrogen from anovulation raises the risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.

### Menopause

Menopause is 12 months of amenorrhea; the median age is about 51. Declining follicle numbers cut inhibin B first, so **FSH rises first**, followed by falling estradiol. Consequences include vasomotor symptoms, vaginal atrophy, bone loss and an adverse lipid profile. Estrone (from peripheral aromatization in fat) becomes the main estrogen.

@example
title: Dating ovulation in a long cycle
problem: A woman has regular 35-day cycles and wants to time intercourse to conceive. When is ovulation expected, and what is her fertile window?
step: The luteal phase is about 14 days, so ovulation occurs on cycle length minus 14: $35 - 14 = 21$.
step: The fertile window is the five days before ovulation and the day of ovulation: days 16 through 21.
step: Counting from the first day of menses (day 1), her ovulation falls a week later than in the textbook 28-day cycle because the follicular phase is longer.
step: Midluteal progesterone (about 7 days before expected menses) above about 3 ng/mL would confirm that she ovulated.

@example
title: Secondary amenorrhea in a young woman
problem: A 31-year-old woman has had no periods for 8 months and has hot flashes. hCG is negative, TSH and prolactin are normal and FSH is 62 mIU/mL. What is the diagnosis and what comes next?
step: Pregnancy, thyroid disease and hyperprolactinemia are excluded by the first three tests.
step: A high FSH with low estrogen means the ovary is not responding: hypergonadotropic hypogonadism. Under age 40, this is primary ovarian insufficiency.
step: Repeat FSH and estradiol in about a month to confirm; send a karyotype (X chromosome abnormalities) and FMR1 premutation testing, with consideration of autoimmune causes.
step: Offer estrogen-progestin replacement until about the age of natural menopause for bone and cardiovascular protection, and counsel about fertility options such as donor oocytes.

@mistakes
- Saying estrogen only exerts negative feedback; sustained high estradiol is the trigger for the positive feedback LH surge.
- Assuming ovulation is always on day 14. The luteal phase is fixed at about 14 days, so the follicular phase absorbs variation.
- Skipping the pregnancy test in amenorrhea.
- Forgetting that the oocyte resumes meiosis II only after fertilization.
