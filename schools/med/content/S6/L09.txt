### Vaginitis

Normal vaginal flora is dominated by lactobacilli that make lactic acid and keep **pH at or below 4.5**. Evaluate with pH, wet mount (saline), 10% KOH preparation (whiff test) and NAAT when appropriate.

| | Bacterial vaginosis | Candida vulvovaginitis | Trichomoniasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cause | Loss of lactobacilli; overgrowth of *Gardnerella* and anaerobes | *Candida albicans* | *Trichomonas vaginalis* (STI) |
| Discharge | Thin, gray-white, fishy odor | Thick, white, "cottage cheese" | Frothy, yellow-green, malodorous |
| Symptoms | Often few; no inflammation | Itching, burning, vulvar erythema | Itching, dysuria, "strawberry cervix" |
| pH | Above 4.5 | 4.5 or below (normal) | Above 4.5 |
| Microscopy | **Clue cells**; positive whiff test | Budding yeast, pseudohyphae (KOH) | Motile flagellated protozoa |
| Treatment | Metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days (or metronidazole gel or clindamycin cream) | Fluconazole 150 mg once or a topical azole | Metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days; treat partners |

All doses in this lesson are typical adult regimens for study; verify them against current references before any clinical use. **Amsel criteria** diagnose BV when three of four are present: homogeneous discharge, pH above 4.5, positive whiff test, clue cells. BV is associated with preterm birth and acquisition of STIs. Candida is more common with diabetes, antibiotics and pregnancy (use topical azoles in pregnancy). Patients were traditionally told to avoid alcohol with metronidazole; the CDC 2021 guidelines no longer advise this, because a disulfiram-like reaction has not been convincingly shown.

### Cervicitis, chlamydia and gonorrhea

*Chlamydia trachomatis* (obligate intracellular) and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (gram-negative diplococci within neutrophils) cause mucopurulent cervicitis, urethritis and, if ascending, PID. Many infections are asymptomatic. Screen all sexually active women under 25 and older women at risk annually with a **nucleic acid amplification test (NAAT)** on a vaginal swab or urine. Treatment: **chlamydia**: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days (azithromycin 1 g once in pregnancy); **gonorrhea**: ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once (1 g if 150 kg or more). Treat for chlamydia too if it has not been excluded. Treat partners (expedited partner therapy where permitted), abstain until treatment is complete, and retest at about 3 months. Untreated infection causes tubal scarring, ectopic pregnancy and infertility; neonates exposed during delivery develop conjunctivitis (prophylaxis with erythromycin ointment), and chlamydia can cause neonatal pneumonia.

### Pelvic inflammatory disease

PID is ascending infection of the upper genital tract. Treat empirically when a sexually active young woman has pelvic or lower abdominal pain **and** cervical motion, uterine or adnexal tenderness, with no other cause. Outpatient regimen: **ceftriaxone 500 mg IM once plus doxycycline 100 mg twice daily plus metronidazole 500 mg twice daily, both for 14 days**. Hospitalize for pregnancy, tubo-ovarian abscess, severe illness or failure of outpatient therapy (IV cefoxitin or cefotetan plus doxycycline). Complications: tubo-ovarian abscess, infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, and **Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome** (perihepatitis with right upper quadrant pain and "violin string" adhesions).

### Syphilis

*Treponema pallidum* (a spirochete). **Primary**: painless indurated chancre. **Secondary** (weeks later): diffuse maculopapular rash including palms and soles, mucous patches, **condylomata lata**, fever, lymphadenopathy. **Latent**: positive serology without symptoms. **Tertiary**: gummas, aortitis, and neurosyphilis (tabes dorsalis, Argyll Robertson pupils). Congenital syphilis is preventable by screening and treating pregnant women. Test with a nontreponemal test (RPR or VDRL, titers track activity) confirmed by a treponemal test (TP-PA, FTA-ABS), or in the reverse algorithm a treponemal immunoassay first. Treat with **benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM**: one dose for primary, secondary and early latent syphilis, and three weekly doses for late latent or latent of unknown duration. Penicillin-allergic pregnant women must be desensitized. A **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** (fever, myalgia within 24 hours of treatment) is expected and not an allergy.

### Genital ulcers and HPV

- **Herpes simplex** (HSV-2 most often): painful grouped vesicles that ulcerate; diagnose with PCR; treat with acyclovir or valacyclovir. Cesarean delivery is offered if active lesions or prodrome at labor.
- **Chancroid** (*Haemophilus ducreyi*): painful ulcer with suppurative inguinal nodes. **Lymphogranuloma venereum** (*C. trachomatis* L1 to L3): transient painless ulcer, then painful inguinal nodes ("groove sign"); doxycycline for 21 days. **Granuloma inguinale** (*Klebsiella granulomatis*): painless, beefy-red ulcers with Donovan bodies.
- **HPV**: types 6 and 11 cause genital warts; **types 16 and 18** cause cervical, anal and oropharyngeal cancers. The 9-valent vaccine is recommended routinely at ages 11 to 12 (can start at 9): **two doses** if started before age 15, three doses at age 15 or older or if immunocompromised (US schedule; some countries use fewer doses); catch-up through age 26, and shared clinical decision-making for ages 27 to 45.

### Test performance

**Positive predictive value** depends on prevalence. For a test with sensitivity $s$, specificity $p$ and prevalence $\pi$:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{s\,\pi}{s\,\pi + (1-p)(1-\pi)}$$

A screening test with excellent specificity still produces many false positives when prevalence is low, which is why confirmatory testing matters.

@example
title: Diagnosing and treating bacterial vaginosis
problem: A 26-year-old woman has a thin gray vaginal discharge with an odor after intercourse. Vaginal pH is 5.2, whiff test is positive and wet mount shows epithelial cells studded with bacteria. What is the diagnosis and treatment?
step: Discharge, pH above 4.5, positive whiff test and clue cells: all four Amsel criteria are present (three are enough).
step: Diagnosis: bacterial vaginosis, a polymicrobial imbalance and not primarily an STI.
step: Treat with metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 7 days (or intravaginal metronidazole or clindamycin cream).
step: Routine partner treatment is not recommended; recurrence is common, and pregnancy-associated BV is treated because of preterm birth risk.

@example
title: Predictive value of a screening test
problem: A chlamydia screening assay has sensitivity 95% and specificity 99%. In a population with prevalence 2%, what fraction of positive results are true positives?
step: Per 10,000 people: 200 are infected and 9,800 are not.
step: True positives: $0.95 \times 200 = 190$. False positives: $0.01 \times 9800 = 98$.
step: PPV equals true positives divided by all positives: $\frac{190}{190 + 98} \approx 0.66$.
step: Even with 99% specificity about one third of positives are false at this prevalence, so positives are confirmed or interpreted in context.

@mistakes
- Treating a vaginal discharge without checking pH and microscopy.
- Treating gonorrhea without also covering chlamydia unless chlamydia has been excluded.
- Using azithromycin 1 g instead of doxycycline as first-line therapy for uncomplicated chlamydia in a nonpregnant patient.
- Mistaking the Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction for penicillin allergy.
