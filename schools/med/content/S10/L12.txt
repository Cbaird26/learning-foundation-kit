@article
Psychotherapy is treatment through a structured relationship. Common factors (a **therapeutic alliance**, empathy, shared goals, hope) account for much of its benefit, and specific techniques add to it. Choice of therapy depends on diagnosis, severity, patient preference and availability. Confidentiality is the rule, with limited exceptions: imminent risk to self or others, suspected abuse of a child or vulnerable adult, and legal requirements.

### Major therapies

- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):** present-focused, structured and skills-based; identifies **cognitive distortions** (catastrophizing, all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralization, mind reading), uses thought records, **behavioral activation** and graded **exposure**. First-line for depression, anxiety disorders, OCD (exposure and response prevention), panic disorder, PTSD (trauma-focused forms) and insomnia (CBT-I).
- **Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT):** acceptance plus change; mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness. Evidence-based for borderline personality disorder and self-harm.
- **Interpersonal therapy (IPT):** time-limited, targets grief, role disputes, role transitions and interpersonal deficits; effective for depression.
- **Psychodynamic psychotherapy:** explores unconscious conflict and defense mechanisms; concepts include **transference** (patient's feelings about important people redirected onto the therapist) and **countertransference** (the therapist's emotional reaction to the patient).
- **Supportive therapy**, **family therapy** (including family-based treatment for adolescent anorexia nervosa), **group therapy**, and **contingency management** (reward-based, for stimulant use).
- **Somatic treatments:** **ECT** (induced seizure under anesthesia and muscle relaxation) is highly effective for severe depression, catatonia, mania and psychotic depression; adverse effects are transient confusion, headache and retrograde memory loss. **Transcranial magnetic stimulation** is an outpatient option for medication-resistant depression.

### Learning theory

**Classical conditioning:** a neutral stimulus paired with an unconditioned stimulus becomes a conditioned stimulus eliciting a conditioned response; underlies phobias and is treated by **exposure** and **systematic desensitization** (relaxation with graded exposure). **Operant conditioning:** behavior is shaped by consequences. **Positive reinforcement** adds a pleasant stimulus; **negative reinforcement** removes an aversive stimulus; both increase behavior. **Punishment** decreases behavior. **Extinction** is the removal of reinforcement. Schedules: **variable ratio** (reinforcement after an unpredictable number of responses, as in gambling) yields high rates of responding and the **greatest resistance to extinction**; fixed ratio gives high rates with pauses; variable interval gives steady responses; fixed interval gives a scalloped pattern. Applications: token economies, parent management training, habit reversal.

### Behavior change

The **transtheoretical (stages of change) model**: **precontemplation** (not considering change), **contemplation** (ambivalent), **preparation** (planning), **action**, **maintenance**, with **relapse** a common part of the process. Match your approach to the stage: raise awareness in precontemplation, explore ambivalence in contemplation, plan in preparation, support in action.

**Motivational interviewing (MI)** is a collaborative, patient-centered style for resolving ambivalence. Core skills (**OARS**): **O**pen questions, **A**ffirmations, **R**eflective listening, **S**ummaries. Elicit "change talk" (desire, ability, reasons, need, commitment), roll with resistance rather than argue, and support autonomy. Avoid the **righting reflex**.

### Effect sizes

Cohen's $d = \frac{\text{difference in means}}{\text{pooled standard deviation}}$; about 0.2 is small, 0.5 moderate and 0.8 large.

@example
title: Reflective listening in a motivational interview
problem: A 45-year-old man with hypertension drinks heavily and says, "I know I drink too much, but it's the only way I relax, and I'm not ready to quit." Which stage and which response fit?
step: He recognizes the problem but is ambivalent and unready to act: contemplation.
step: A MI-consistent response is a reflection that includes both sides: "Drinking helps you unwind, and part of you is also worried about it."
step: Follow with an open question that elicits change talk: "What concerns you most about your drinking?"
step: Avoid arguing or lecturing; affirm the strength it took to say that, and offer information with permission.

@mistakes
- Confusing negative reinforcement (removing something aversive to increase behavior) with punishment.
- Arguing for change in the contemplation stage, which increases resistance.
- Confusing transference with countertransference.
- Treating ECT as a last resort of no benefit or as contraindicated by age alone.

@practice num
prompt: In a trial, mean PHQ-9 improvement was 6 points with therapy and 3 points with control. The pooled standard deviation was 6. What is Cohen's d?
answer: 0.5
check: (6 - 3) / 6
tol: 0.01
hint: Divide the difference in means by the pooled standard deviation.
solution: d = (6 - 3) / 6 = 0.5, a moderate effect.
