@article
**Bipolar disorders** are defined by episodes of abnormally elevated or irritable mood with increased energy or activity, usually alternating with depressive episodes. Heritability is high (about 60 to 80 percent in twin studies), and first-degree relatives of a person with bipolar I carry a much higher risk than the general population. Typical onset is late adolescence to early adulthood. Many patients first present with depression, so screen every depressed patient for past mania or hypomania, and ask family members.

### Mania versus hypomania

A **manic episode** requires a distinct period of abnormally and persistently elevated, expansive or irritable mood **and** increased energy or activity lasting at least **1 week** (any duration if hospitalization is needed). During the period, **three or more** of the following are present (four if the mood is only irritable): **D**istractibility, **I**ndiscretion (risky behavior such as spending sprees), **G**randiosity, **F**light of ideas or racing thoughts, increased goal-directed **A**ctivity or agitation, decreased need for **S**leep, pressured **T**alkativeness (DIGFAST). The episode causes marked impairment, requires hospitalization, or includes psychosis.

A **hypomanic episode** has the same symptoms but lasts at least **4 consecutive days**, represents an observable change noticed by others, and is **not severe enough to cause marked impairment or require hospitalization, and has no psychotic features**. Psychosis or hospitalization makes the episode manic by definition.

| Diagnosis | Required episodes |
|---|---|
| Bipolar I | At least one manic episode (depression common but not required) |
| Bipolar II | At least one hypomanic and one major depressive episode; never a manic episode |
| Cyclothymic disorder | Two years (1 year in children) of numerous hypomanic and depressive symptoms that never meet full episode criteria |

Specifiers include **with mixed features**, **with rapid cycling** (four or more episodes in 12 months), anxious distress, psychotic features and seasonal pattern. Mania can also be triggered by antidepressants, corticosteroids, stimulants and cocaine, and by hyperthyroidism, multiple sclerosis or frontal lesions; these cause a substance/medication-induced or medical-condition-related diagnosis.

### Treatment

**Acute mania:** lithium, valproate, or a second-generation antipsychotic (quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone, aripiprazole) is first-line; combination of lithium or valproate with an antipsychotic is often used for severe mania. Stop antidepressants. Benzodiazepines can help agitation and sleep short term. ECT is effective for severe or refractory mania.

**Bipolar depression:** quetiapine, lurasidone, cariprazine, lumateperone, or lamotrigine are options; **antidepressant monotherapy is avoided** because of the risk of switching to mania.

**Maintenance:** lithium is the classic choice and reduces suicide risk; valproate, lamotrigine (better for preventing depression than mania) and several antipsychotics are alternatives. Adherence is the largest practical issue; long-acting injectable antipsychotics may help.

### Mood stabilizers: monitoring and serious adverse effects

- **Lithium:** narrow therapeutic index; typical maintenance serum levels are about 0.6 to 1.0 mEq/L (somewhat higher, up to about 1.2, during acute mania), drawn about 12 hours after the last dose at steady state, roughly 5 days after a dose change. Renal clearance; levels rise with dehydration, **NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers and thiazide diuretics**. Early toxicity: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, coarse tremor; later ataxia, dysarthria, confusion, seizures. Chronic effects: **nephrogenic diabetes insipidus**, hypothyroidism, hyperparathyroidism and hypercalcemia, weight gain, benign leukocytosis, and teratogenicity (Ebstein anomaly with first-trimester exposure). Check renal function, TSH and calcium at baseline and periodically; pregnancy test before starting.
- **Valproate:** hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, weight gain, tremor, polycystic ovary syndrome, and **neural tube defects**; avoid in people who may become pregnant unless essential. Monitor LFTs, platelets and levels.
- **Carbamazepine:** **Stevens–Johnson syndrome** (higher risk with HLA-B*1502, more common in people of Asian ancestry), agranulocytosis and aplastic anemia, hyponatremia, and strong CYP3A4 induction (reduces levels of many drugs, including oral contraceptives).
- **Lamotrigine:** benign rash can progress to **Stevens–Johnson syndrome**; risk is lowered by slow titration, and the dose is cut roughly in half if valproate is co-prescribed (valproate raises lamotrigine levels). Stop for any rash with fever or mucosal involvement.

title: Mania or hypomania?
problem: A 27-year-old woman reports 5 days of feeling "unstoppable." She sleeps 3 hours per night without fatigue, talks faster than usual, and starts three new projects. Coworkers say she is not herself, but she continues working and has no hallucinations or delusions. She has had two major depressive episodes and never needed hospitalization. What is the most likely diagnosis?
step: Duration is 5 days, at least the 4 days required for hypomania, but less than the 7 days needed for mania unless hospitalized.
step: Symptoms include decreased need for sleep, talkativeness and increased goal-directed activity (three symptoms), with an observable change.
step: There is no marked impairment, hospitalization or psychosis, so the episode is hypomanic, not manic.
step: A hypomanic episode plus past major depressive episodes with no mania is bipolar II disorder. Treatment of the depressive phase avoids antidepressant monotherapy.

@mistakes
- Calling any hospitalized or psychotic elevated episode "hypomania"; those make it mania.
- Starting an antidepressant alone in bipolar depression.
- Checking a lithium level before steady state or at a random time after a dose, and forgetting to hold or adjust lithium when starting an NSAID or thiazide.
- Using full-dose lamotrigine at once, or forgetting that valproate raises lamotrigine levels.
