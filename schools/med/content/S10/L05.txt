@article
**Psychosis** is a loss of contact with shared reality, shown by delusions, hallucinations or disorganized thought. **Schizophrenia** affects about 1 percent of people over a lifetime. Onset is typically in the late teens to mid-20s in men and slightly later in women. Risk comes from genes (about 10 percent risk with an affected parent or sibling, roughly 40 to 50 percent concordance in identical twins; 22q11.2 deletion carries a markedly increased risk) and environmental factors (obstetric complications, urban upbringing, cannabis use in adolescence, migration).

### Mechanism

The **dopamine hypothesis**: excess dopamine activity in the **mesolimbic pathway** produces positive symptoms (delusions, hallucinations), while reduced dopamine in the **mesocortical pathway** is linked to negative and cognitive symptoms. Hypofunction of **NMDA glutamate receptors** probably contributes, which explains why ketamine and phencyclidine can mimic psychosis, including negative symptoms. All antipsychotics block **D2 receptors**; blockade in the **nigrostriatal pathway** causes parkinsonism and dystonia, in the **tuberoinfundibular pathway** raises prolactin, and in the mesolimbic pathway reduces positive symptoms.

### Diagnosis by pattern and duration

Schizophrenia requires **two or more** of: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior, and negative symptoms (diminished emotional expression, avolition, alogia, anhedonia, asociality), **with at least one of the first three**, for a significant portion of one month, and continuous signs of disturbance for **at least 6 months** (including prodromal or residual periods), with functional decline.

| Disorder | Key feature |
|---|---|
| Brief psychotic disorder | 1 day to less than 1 month, then full return to baseline |
| Schizophreniform disorder | 1 month to less than 6 months |
| Schizophrenia | 6 months or more |
| Schizoaffective disorder | Mood episode concurrent with active psychosis, plus at least 2 weeks of delusions or hallucinations without a major mood episode; mood symptoms present for the majority of the illness |
| Delusional disorder | One or more delusions for at least 1 month, functioning not markedly impaired, no prominent hallucinations |
| Substance- or medication-induced; due to another medical condition | Psychosis caused by drugs (stimulants, cannabis, phencyclidine, steroids), delirium, seizure, autoimmune encephalitis, brain tumor, HIV, B12 deficiency or thyroid disease |

Mood disorders with psychotic features differ because psychosis occurs only during mood episodes. In a first episode, obtain a urine drug screen, basic labs and TSH, and consider neuroimaging and, if there are seizures, catatonia, autonomic instability or rapid decline, evaluation for **anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis**.

### Treatment

**Second-generation antipsychotics** (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole, paliperidone, lurasidone, ziprasidone) are generally first-line; first-generation agents (haloperidol, chlorpromazine) are equally effective for positive symptoms but cause more movement disorders. Positive symptoms respond in weeks; negative symptoms respond poorly. **Clozapine** is for treatment-resistant illness (failure of two adequate trials) and reduces suicide risk; it carries a risk of severe neutropenia and agranulocytosis, so absolute neutrophil count (ANC) monitoring remains recommended per labeling and practice (the FDA eliminated the clozapine REMS program in 2025); it also causes myocarditis, seizures, sialorrhea, constipation and metabolic effects. **Long-acting injectable** formulations improve adherence. Psychosocial treatment (coordinated specialty care, family psychoeducation, supported employment, cognitive remediation) is essential.

### Adverse effects to know

- **Acute dystonia** (hours to days, especially young men on high-potency agents): treat with benztropine or diphenhydramine.
- **Akathisia** (restlessness): lower the dose; propranolol is first line, and a benzodiazepine or mirtazapine can help (anticholinergics such as benztropine help less).
- **Drug-induced parkinsonism** (days to weeks): switch or lower the dose; anticholinergic.
- **Tardive dyskinesia** (months to years): VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine); consider switching.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:** fever, lead-pipe rigidity, altered mental status, autonomic instability, elevated creatine kinase; stop the drug, supportive care, dantrolene or bromocriptine.
- **Hyperprolactinemia** (risperidone, paliperidone, haloperidol).
- **Metabolic syndrome**, greatest with olanzapine and clozapine; monitor weight, waist, blood pressure, fasting glucose or A1c and lipids.
- **QT prolongation** (ziprasidone, haloperidol IV, thioridazine). **Bazett correction:** $QTc = \frac{QT}{\sqrt{RR}}$ with RR in seconds ($RR = 60/\text{heart rate}$); prolongation above about 450 ms in men and 460 ms in women is abnormal, and a QTc above about 500 ms, or an increase of more than 60 ms from baseline, carries high risk for torsades de pointes.

@example
title: Duration decides the diagnosis
problem: A 21-year-old man has had hallucinations and delusions with disorganized speech for 3 months, preceded by 2 months of social withdrawal and declining grades. He has no mood episode and a negative drug screen. What is the diagnosis?
step: Active psychotic symptoms plus disorganized speech: at least two criteria, including delusions or hallucinations.
step: Total duration of illness including the prodrome is 5 months, which is more than 1 month but less than 6.
step: With no mood episode and no substance cause, the diagnosis is schizophreniform disorder. If symptoms continue to 6 months it becomes schizophrenia.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing schizophrenia before 6 months of illness or missing drug-induced psychosis.
- Calling a mood disorder with psychotic features schizoaffective disorder; schizoaffective requires 2 weeks of psychosis without mood episode.
- Treating dystonia with more antipsychotic, or missing neuroleptic malignant syndrome because fever was attributed to infection.
- Not measuring baseline weight, glucose, lipids and QTc before and during treatment.

@practice num
prompt: A 45-year-old man has an ECG with a QT interval of 440 ms and a heart rate of 75 beats per minute. What is the QTc by the Bazett formula, in ms, rounded to the nearest whole number?
answer: 492
check: 440 / math.sqrt(60 / 75)
tol: 1%
unit: ms
hint: RR interval in seconds is 60 divided by the heart rate.
hint: RR = 0.8 s. Divide the QT by the square root of RR.
solution: RR = 60/75 = 0.8 s. QTc = 440 / sqrt(0.8) = 440 / 0.894 = about 492 ms, which is prolonged.

@practice self
prompt: A 24-year-old man has been prescribed olanzapine for newly diagnosed schizophrenia. Write a brief monitoring plan with the baseline tests and reasons for each.
hint: Think of metabolic, cardiac, endocrine and movement-disorder risks.
solution: Baseline: weight/BMI and waist circumference; blood pressure; fasting glucose or A1c; fasting lipid panel; family history of diabetes and cardiac disease; ECG if risk factors for QT prolongation. Follow-up: weight at each visit for the first months, then metabolic labs at about 3 months and at least annually; abnormal involuntary movements examination (AIMS) at baseline and periodically for tardive dyskinesia; ask about prolactin-related symptoms with agents that raise prolactin. Reasons: olanzapine causes weight gain, insulin resistance and dyslipidemia, so early intervention (diet, activity, switching or adding metformin) lowers cardiovascular risk.
rubric: Includes weight/BMI, blood pressure, glucose or A1c, and lipids with a reason tied to metabolic syndrome.
rubric: Mentions movement-disorder screening and ECG/QT when indicated.
rubric: States follow-up timing and action when abnormalities appear.
