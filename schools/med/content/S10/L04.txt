@article
Fear is a response to an immediate threat; anxiety is anticipation of a future threat. Both involve the amygdala, which signals danger, and a prefrontal cortex that normally restrains it. In anxiety disorders the amygdala is overactive and prefrontal control is reduced; noradrenergic hyperactivity (locus coeruleus) drives the autonomic symptoms and GABA signaling is relatively insufficient. Always exclude medical causes first: hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, arrhythmias, hypoglycemia, asthma, caffeine, stimulants and withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives.

### The anxiety disorders

- **Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD):** excessive, hard-to-control worry about multiple domains on more days than not for at least **6 months**, with at least three of: restlessness, fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance (one symptom in children). The **GAD-7** scores 0 to 21; 5, 10 and 15 mark mild, moderate and severe anxiety, and 10 is a common cutoff.
- **Panic disorder:** recurrent **unexpected panic attacks** (abrupt surge peaking within minutes, with at least four of 13 symptoms such as palpitations, sweating, trembling, dyspnea, chest pain, nausea, derealization, fear of dying) followed by at least **1 month** of persistent worry about more attacks or maladaptive behavior change. **Agoraphobia** (fear of situations where escape is difficult) is diagnosed separately and often accompanies it.
- **Social anxiety disorder:** marked fear of scrutiny in social or performance situations, lasting at least 6 months; the "performance only" specifier covers public speaking alone.
- **Specific phobia:** marked fear of a particular object or situation, almost always provoking immediate anxiety, lasting 6 months or more.
- **Separation anxiety disorder** and **selective mutism** begin in childhood (Lesson 9).

**Treatment.** **SSRIs and SNRIs** (for example sertraline, escitalopram, venlafaxine) are first-line medications. **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** with **exposure** is first-line psychotherapy and is as effective as medication with lower relapse. Buspirone (a 5-HT1A partial agonist) can treat GAD but takes weeks. **Benzodiazepines** act quickly but cause sedation, falls, cognitive impairment, dependence, withdrawal seizures with abrupt discontinuation and misuse risk; limit them to short courses. Beta-blockers such as propranolol can blunt autonomic symptoms of performance anxiety. Hydroxyzine is a nonaddictive sedating option. Start SSRIs at low doses; early jitteriness can worsen panic symptoms.

### Obsessive-compulsive and related disorders

**Obsessions** are recurrent intrusive, unwanted thoughts causing anxiety (contamination, harm, symmetry); **compulsions** are repetitive behaviors or mental acts performed to reduce distress or according to rigid rules. In **OCD** they are time-consuming (more than 1 hour per day) or cause distress or impairment, and insight is often good (specifiers: good, poor, absent). The most effective treatment is **exposure and response prevention (ERP)** and/or an SSRI at higher doses than for depression, with response taking 8 to 12 weeks; **clomipramine** is an effective serotonergic tricyclic; antipsychotic augmentation is used for partial response. Related disorders include body dysmorphic disorder, hoarding disorder, trichotillomania and excoriation disorder (habit reversal training). OCD is **ego-dystonic**, unlike obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (Lesson 7).

### Trauma- and stressor-related disorders

**PTSD** follows exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury or sexual violence, and lasts **more than 1 month**. It has four symptom clusters: **intrusion** (flashbacks, nightmares), **avoidance**, **negative alterations in cognition and mood** (guilt, detachment, anhedonia, amnesia for aspects of the event), and **arousal and reactivity** (hypervigilance, exaggerated startle, irritability, poor sleep). **Acute stress disorder** has similar symptoms lasting from **3 days to 1 month**. An **adjustment disorder** is distress out of proportion to a stressor, starting within 3 months of it and not meeting criteria for another disorder; it resolves within 6 months after the stressor ends.

**Treatment of PTSD:** trauma-focused psychotherapies (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, EMDR) are first-line; sertraline, paroxetine or venlafaxine are recommended medications. **Benzodiazepines should be avoided** because they do not improve and may worsen outcomes. Prazosin is often used for trauma nightmares, though guidelines differ about its strength of recommendation.

@example
title: Panic disorder versus a medical cause
problem: A 26-year-old woman has had four episodes of sudden chest tightness, palpitations, sweating and fear of dying, each peaking within 10 minutes, with no clear trigger. For two months she has avoided driving for fear of another attack. ECG, TSH and electrolytes are normal. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?
step: The attacks are abrupt, peak in minutes, and have at least four typical symptoms, and they are unexpected (no consistent trigger).
step: The consequent worry and behavioral change (avoiding driving) have lasted more than 1 month, so the diagnosis is panic disorder (with agoraphobia if the fear includes situations that are hard to escape).
step: Normal ECG and TSH reduce the likelihood of cardiac or thyroid disease. Also ask about caffeine, stimulants and alcohol.
step: First-line care is CBT with interoceptive exposure and/or an SSRI or SNRI started at a low dose. Benzodiazepines are not first-line.

@mistakes
- Prescribing long-term benzodiazepines as first-line therapy for anxiety, or for PTSD.
- Diagnosing PTSD at 2 weeks (it is acute stress disorder before 1 month) or calling it an adjustment disorder when full criteria are met.
- Treating OCD with the same low SSRI dose and short trial used for depression.
- Missing medical mimics: hyperthyroidism, pheochromocytoma, arrhythmia, stimulant use.

@practice self
prompt: Mania and hypomania differ, and so do their treatments. A 24-year-old man has had 6 days of euphoria, little sleep, pressured speech and impulsive spending, and he continues to attend work with no psychosis. A second 24-year-old man has had 10 days of similar symptoms, delusions of special powers, and needed admission. Explain which has mania and which has hypomania, and name first-line treatments for the manic episode, plus maintenance treatment and the monitoring it needs.
hint: Compare duration, psychosis, impairment and hospitalization against DSM-5-TR criteria.
solution: The first man has a hypomanic episode: at least 4 days, observable change, no marked impairment, no hospitalization, no psychosis. The second has a manic episode: psychosis and hospitalization make the episode manic (and duration is at least 1 week). First-line treatment of acute mania is lithium, valproate or a second-generation antipsychotic (such as quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone or aripiprazole), often an antipsychotic combined with lithium or valproate in severe mania; stop antidepressants. Maintenance with lithium or another mood stabilizer follows.
rubric: Uses duration, psychosis, impairment and hospitalization to classify each episode correctly.
rubric: Names at least two first-line options for acute mania and states that antidepressants should be stopped.
rubric: Mentions maintenance treatment and monitoring needs (for example lithium levels, renal function and thyroid tests).
