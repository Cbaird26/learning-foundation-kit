@article
**Major depressive disorder (MDD)** is among the most common causes of disability worldwide. Its biology is multifactorial: reduced monoaminergic signaling (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine), dysregulated hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, reduced neuroplasticity in the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, inflammation and strong genetic contribution. Monoamine reuptake blockers raise synaptic transmitter within hours, but clinical benefit takes weeks, which tells us that downstream adaptation matters.

### DSM-5-TR criteria for a major depressive episode

At least **five** of nine symptoms present during the same **2-week** period, nearly every day, representing a change from previous functioning, with **at least one** being **depressed mood** or **loss of interest or pleasure (anhedonia)**. The mnemonic **SIG E CAPS** covers: **S**leep change, **I**nterest loss, **G**uilt or worthlessness, **E**nergy loss, **C**oncentration difficulty, **A**ppetite or weight change, **P**sychomotor agitation or retardation, **S**uicidal ideation. Symptoms cause clinically significant distress or impairment and are not attributable to a substance or another medical condition. **MDD requires that there has never been a manic or hypomanic episode**; otherwise the diagnosis is bipolar disorder.

### Related diagnoses

- **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia):** depressed mood most of the day, more days than not, for at least **2 years** in adults (1 year in children and adolescents), with at least two additional symptoms.
- **Premenstrual dysphoric disorder:** mood symptoms in the final week before menses that remit shortly after menses begin.
- **Disruptive mood dysregulation disorder:** chronic irritability and severe outbursts in children (Lesson 9).
- **Grief:** sadness after loss is expected. DSM-5-TR includes **prolonged grief disorder** when intense yearning and preoccupation persist beyond 12 months in adults (6 months in children). MDD can be diagnosed in the setting of bereavement when criteria are met; pervasive worthlessness, marked psychomotor retardation and prolonged functional impairment favor MDD over normal grief.

### Specifiers

**Melancholic** (early-morning awakening, worse mood in the morning, weight loss, guilt), **atypical** (mood reactivity, hypersomnia, hyperphagia, leaden paralysis, interpersonal rejection sensitivity), **with psychotic features**, **catatonia**, **peripartum onset**, **seasonal pattern**, **anxious distress**.

### Medical mimics and workup

Check TSH, CBC, metabolic panel, B12 and, as indicated, vitamin D, HIV, and drug screening. Hypothyroidism, anemia, obstructive sleep apnea, Cushing syndrome, Parkinson disease, stroke, and drugs such as corticosteroids and interferon can all cause depressive symptoms.

### Screening: the PHQ-9

Nine items scored 0 to 3 (not at all to nearly every day), total 0–27. Bands: 5–9 mild, 10–14 moderate, 15–19 moderately severe, 20–27 severe. A score of 10 or more is a common screening threshold. Item 9 asks about thoughts of death or self-harm, and any positive response requires direct suicide risk assessment that day.

### Treatment

For mild to moderate depression, **psychotherapy (cognitive behavioral therapy, interpersonal therapy)** or an antidepressant are both reasonable; for moderate to severe depression, combination treatment is more effective than either alone. **SSRIs** such as sertraline and escitalopram are first-line because of safety in overdose and tolerability. Expect to wait 4–6 weeks for a full trial at an adequate dose; if there is partial response, optimize the dose before switching. After remission, continue treatment for at least 6–12 months; patients with recurrent episodes may need longer or indefinite maintenance. **Treatment-resistant depression** is lack of response to at least two adequate trials. Options include switching class, augmenting (for example with an atypical antipsychotic, lithium or bupropion), **intranasal esketamine**, **transcranial magnetic stimulation**, and **electroconvulsive therapy (ECT)**, the most effective option for severe, psychotic, catatonic, or acutely suicidal depression.

**SSRI adverse effects:** nausea, sexual dysfunction, insomnia or somnolence, headache, **hyponatremia from SIADH** (older adults), increased bleeding risk (especially with NSAIDs), QT prolongation with higher-dose citalopram, **discontinuation syndrome** (shorter half-life agents such as paroxetine), and **serotonin syndrome** with serotonergic combinations. All antidepressants carry a boxed warning for increased suicidal thoughts and behaviors in patients under 25; monitor closely early in treatment, especially in adolescents and young adults.

@example
title: Scoring a PHQ-9
problem: A 29-year-old man has PHQ-9 item scores of 3, 2, 2, 1, 2, 1, 2, 1, 0. What is his total and severity band, and what must you do next?
step: Add the item scores: $3+2+2+1+2+1+2+1+0 = 14$.
step: A score of 14 falls in the 10–14 band: moderate depression. Item 9 is scored 0, but a positive screen still calls for a clinical interview to confirm the diagnosis.
step: Confirm with DSM-5-TR criteria: at least five symptoms for two weeks, including depressed mood or anhedonia, and exclude mania and medical causes.
step: Suicide risk is still asked about directly: a score of 0 on item 9 does not replace clinical inquiry.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing MDD without asking about past manic or hypomanic episodes, which can lead to antidepressant monotherapy in bipolar disorder.
- Declaring an SSRI failure before 4–6 weeks at an adequate dose.
- Stopping the antidepressant right at remission, which has a high relapse rate.
- Treating the PHQ-9 total as a diagnosis, or not asking about suicidal thoughts when item 9 is scored 0.

hint: DSM-5-TR removed the old exclusion for bereavement.
solution: DSM-5-TR permits an MDD diagnosis in bereavement when criteria are met (six symptoms for more than two weeks here). Persistent depressive disorder requires two years. An adjustment disorder is diagnosed only if criteria for another disorder are not met.

prompt: Read this excerpt and write a mental status examination. A 33-year-old woman arrives on time, in clean clothes, and sits with arms crossed. She answers questions after a pause and quietly. "I just feel nothing. I used to love painting. I don't now." Her face remains still. "Everything takes so much effort." She says she wakes at 4 a.m. and cannot return to sleep. Her thoughts are organized. When asked, she says, "Sometimes I think it would be easier not to wake up. I don't have a plan. I'd never do it, I have my kids." She denies voices or odd experiences. She is oriented, repeats seven digits forward, and remembers three of three words. "I know this is depression. I made the appointment myself."
hint: Go domain by domain; quote the mood and describe affect separately.
solution: Appearance: well groomed, clean clothing, guarded posture with crossed arms. Behavior: cooperative, mild psychomotor slowing. Speech: soft, decreased rate and volume, delayed latency. Mood: "I just feel nothing." Affect: restricted to flat, mood-congruent. Thought process: linear and goal-directed. Thought content: anhedonia and fatigue, passive death wish without plan or intent and with protective factor of her children; no delusions or obsessions reported. Perception: denies hallucinations. Cognition: alert, oriented, attention (digit span) and short-term recall intact. Insight: good. Judgment: intact (self-referred). Impression: probable major depressive episode with melancholic features (early-morning awakening); needs a structured suicide risk assessment and a safety plan.
rubric: Writes mood in quotes and affect separately, including range and congruence.
rubric: Describes passive suicidal ideation precisely (no plan, no intent) and names a protective factor, without method details.
