@article
Suicide is a leading cause of death among young adults in the United States. Most people who die by suicide had contact with a health professional in the preceding year, which makes assessment and prevention a core skill for every physician. Asking directly about suicide **does not increase risk** and is therapeutic. This lesson stays at the level of clinical assessment and management.

### Risk factors and protective factors

**Static (historical) factors:** a prior suicide attempt (the strongest single predictor), psychiatric diagnosis (depression, bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use, borderline personality disorder, eating disorders), family history of suicide, history of trauma or abuse, male sex (men die more often; women attempt more often), older age, and certain groups with higher rates (American Indian and Alaska Native people, veterans, LGBTQ youth). **Dynamic (modifiable) factors:** hopelessness, agitation or insomnia, recent discharge from psychiatric hospital, intoxication, acute stressors (loss, legal, financial), new serious medical diagnosis, psychosis (command hallucinations) and **access to lethal means**. **Protective factors:** social connection, responsibility to children, engagement in treatment, reasons for living, future orientation, problem-solving skills, and restricted access to lethal means.

### Structured assessment

1. **Screen:** Ask Suicide Questions (ASQ) in medical settings, PHQ-9 item 9, or the **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** screener.
2. **Characterize ideation:** passive (wishing not to wake up) versus active; frequency, duration, controllability; plan, intent, and preparatory behavior; prior attempts and their medical severity.
3. **Assess dynamic risk, protective factors, access to means, and mental status** (including hopelessness, psychosis, intoxication, impulsivity).
4. **Formulate risk** as acute (immediate) and chronic (baseline), low, moderate or high, with reasoning; do not rely on a checklist alone or on a patient's promise.
5. **Choose disposition:** outpatient care with a **safety plan** and close follow-up for lower acute risk; voluntary psychiatric admission or emergency evaluation for high risk; involuntary hold when a person is a danger to self or others, or gravely disabled, as defined by state law, after assessment of less restrictive alternatives. Treat underlying illness: lithium reduces suicide in mood disorders, clozapine in schizophrenia, and CBT and DBT reduce attempts.

### The safety plan (Stanley–Brown Safety Planning Intervention)

A safety plan is a short, written, prioritized list created **with** the patient in their own words:
1. Warning signs (thoughts, moods, situations) that a crisis may develop.
2. Internal coping strategies the person can do alone.
3. People and social settings that provide distraction.
4. People the person can ask for help.
5. Professionals and agencies to contact, including the **988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline** (call or text 988, or chat; available 24 hours in the United States) and emergency services (911).
6. **Making the environment safer**, called lethal-means counseling: working with the patient and family to put distance between the person and dangerous means, for example securing or temporarily removing firearms and limiting stockpiles of medications.

### Violence risk and duty to protect

Predictors include prior violence, active substance use, psychosis with persecutory or command content, access to weapons, and expressed intent against an identifiable person. Under the **Tarasoff** line of cases and similar state laws, clinicians may have a duty to warn or protect an identifiable victim; the details vary by state.

### Emergencies

- **Agitation:** assess for medical causes (delirium, intoxication, hypoglycemia, hypoxia, pain). De-escalate verbally first, offer oral medication (for example lorazepam, olanzapine), then IM medication; restraint or seclusion is a last resort with continuous observation and documentation.
- **Catatonia:** immobility, mutism, posturing, waxy flexibility, negativism, sometimes excitement; occurs in mood disorders, schizophrenia and medical illnesses. A **lorazepam challenge** is diagnostic and therapeutic; **ECT** treats refractory catatonia; avoid antipsychotics when possible.
- **Serotonin syndrome:** rapid onset (hours) after serotonergic drug combinations; **clonus, hyperreflexia, agitation, diaphoresis, diarrhea, tremor, fever**. Stop the drugs, give benzodiazepines and fluids; cyproheptadine if moderate.
- **Neuroleptic malignant syndrome:** onset over days; **lead-pipe rigidity**, hyporeflexia, fever, altered mental status, elevated creatine kinase; stop antipsychotic; dantrolene or bromocriptine.
- **Lithium toxicity**, **alcohol withdrawal delirium** and **anticholinergic toxicity** are also psychiatric emergencies with medical causes.

### Decision-making capacity

Capacity is decision-specific. A patient must be able to **communicate a choice**, **understand** relevant information, **appreciate** its application to their own situation, and **reason** about options. A psychiatric diagnosis alone does not remove capacity, and capacity can fluctuate.

@example
title: Formulating risk and disposition
problem: A 46-year-old man with depression and heavy drinking is seen after losing his job. He has thoughts of ending his life nearly daily, a specific plan, intent to act this week, and keeps firearms at home. He has two children he says he would not want to leave. How would you formulate risk and plan?
step: Risk factors: depression, alcohol use, recent job loss, male sex, active ideation with plan and intent, access to firearms. Protective factor: concern for his children.
step: Acute risk is high because of plan, intent, timeline and access to means; the protective factor does not outweigh these.
step: Disposition: emergency psychiatric evaluation with a recommendation for inpatient admission, preferably voluntary; involuntary if he refuses and meets criteria. Do not leave him alone.
step: Address means (arrange for secure storage with family), treat depression and alcohol use, and develop a safety plan for the transition, including 988 and follow-up within days of discharge.

@mistakes
- Believing that asking about suicide will cause it, or accepting a "no-suicide contract" in place of assessment.
- Documenting "low risk" without describing the reasoning.
- Treating a safety plan as a generic handout rather than a collaborative, personalized list.
- Confusing serotonin syndrome (clonus, rapid onset, hyperreflexia) with NMS (rigidity, slow onset, bradyreflexia).

@practice self
prompt: A 22-year-old college student with depression tells you she has had thoughts of wishing she were dead, with no plan or intent, for two weeks. She has supportive roommates, a dog and a counselor. Structure your risk assessment and write her safety plan outline (use your own examples, no method details).
hint: Cover screening, ideation details, risk and protective factors, formulation and disposition, then the six plan steps.
solution: Assessment: use the C-SSRS screener; characterize passive ideation (frequency, controllability); ask about plan, intent, preparatory behavior, prior attempts, substance use, psychosis and access to lethal means; assess hopelessness, sleep and stressors; protective factors: roommates, her dog, counseling. rubric: Describes ideation, plan, intent, history, risk and protective factors and gives reasoned acute and chronic risk.
rubric: Plan contains all six components in the patient's own words, including 988.
rubric: Includes follow-up timing and means safety without method detail.
