@article
Eating disorders are serious psychiatric illnesses with significant medical morbidity, and anorexia nervosa has one of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric disorders. They typically begin in adolescence or young adulthood, occur in all genders, body sizes and backgrounds, and often coexist with depression, anxiety, OCD or substance use. Assessment focuses on diagnosis, medical stability and safety.

### Anorexia nervosa

Criteria: (1) restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight for age, sex, development and health; (2) intense fear of gaining weight or persistent behavior that prevents weight gain; (3) disturbance in body image, undue influence of weight on self-evaluation, or lack of recognition of the seriousness of low weight. Subtypes: **restricting** and **binge-eating/purging**. Severity in adults is graded by BMI: **mild at least 17, moderate 16 to 16.99, severe 15 to 15.99, extreme below 15 kg/m²**.

Medical findings: bradycardia, hypotension and orthostasis, hypothermia, lanugo, dry skin, amenorrhea, osteopenia and osteoporosis, hypercarotenemia, leukopenia, mildly elevated liver enzymes, and electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis with purging; hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia). QT prolongation and arrhythmia are important causes of death along with suicide. Admission is considered for hemodynamic instability, severe bradycardia, significant electrolyte disturbance, arrhythmia, acute food refusal, severe underweight or failure of outpatient care.

**Refeeding syndrome** occurs when nutrition restarts in a malnourished person: insulin surges drive phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells, producing **hypophosphatemia** (the hallmark), hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, fluid retention and heart failure; thiamine deficiency can cause Wernicke encephalopathy. Prevention: start feeding cautiously, replace thiamine, and monitor electrolytes closely.

**Treatment:** nutritional rehabilitation and weight restoration is the foundation. **Family-based treatment** (Maudsley approach) is first-line for adolescents; **CBT-enhanced (CBT-E)** and other specialist psychotherapies are used in adults. No medication reliably restores weight; olanzapine has modest evidence as an adjunct, and antidepressants do not treat the core illness in underweight patients.

### Bulimia nervosa and binge-eating disorder

**Bulimia nervosa:** recurrent binge eating (a discrete period in which one eats an amount definitely larger than most people would, with a sense of lack of control) plus recurrent inappropriate compensatory behavior (self-induced vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse, fasting, excessive exercise), both occurring **at least once a week for 3 months**, with self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape. Weight is often normal or above normal; if the behaviors occur only during episodes of anorexia nervosa, that diagnosis applies instead. Signs: **Russell sign** (knuckle calluses), parotid enlargement, dental enamel erosion, hypokalemic hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis, elevated amylase. Treatment: **CBT-E** is first-line; the SSRI **fluoxetine** (the labeled dose is about 60 mg daily, a typical adult dose given for study only; verify against current references before any clinical use) helps; **bupropion is contraindicated** because of seizure risk.

**Binge-eating disorder:** recurrent binge eating at least once weekly for 3 months with marked distress and **no regular compensatory behavior**; commonly associated with obesity. Treatment: CBT; **lisdexamfetamine** is FDA approved; SSRIs have modest benefit.

**Avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder (ARFID)** involves avoidance of food from sensory features, lack of interest or fear of aversive consequences, without body-image disturbance.

### Sleep disorders

- **Insomnia disorder:** difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep or early awakening, at least 3 nights per week for at least 3 months, with daytime impairment. **CBT for insomnia (CBT-I)** (stimulus control, sleep restriction, relaxation, cognitive restructuring) is first-line. Medications are second-line and short term: low-dose doxepin, orexin receptor antagonists (suvorexant, lemborexant), melatonin-receptor agonist (ramelteon), and "Z-drugs" (zolpidem; complex sleep behaviors boxed warning). Avoid chronic benzodiazepines, particularly in older adults.
- **Obstructive sleep apnea:** snoring, witnessed apneas, daytime sleepiness, obesity, hypertension; diagnosed by polysomnography (apnea-hypopnea index of 5 or more with symptoms, or 15 or more regardless); CPAP is first-line and weight loss helps.
- **Narcolepsy:** excessive daytime sleepiness for at least 3 months with cataplexy (type 1) from loss of orexin (hypocretin) neurons, **hypnagogic hallucinations** and **sleep paralysis**; the multiple sleep latency test shows mean sleep latency of 8 minutes or less and at least two sleep-onset REM periods. Treatment: scheduled naps, modafinil or other stimulants, sodium oxybate; antidepressants for cataplexy.
- **Restless legs syndrome:** urge to move the legs at rest, worse at night, relieved by movement; check ferritin (treat iron deficiency), then dopamine agonists or alpha-2-delta ligands (augmentation with dopaminergic agents).
- **REM sleep behavior disorder:** loss of REM atonia with dream enactment; strongly predicts later synucleinopathy (Parkinson disease, Lewy body dementia).
- **NREM parasomnias** (sleepwalking, night terrors): arise from slow-wave sleep in the first third of the night; the person is hard to wake and has no memory.

**Sleep efficiency** is $\frac{\text{time asleep}}{\text{time in bed}} \times 100\%$; a value above 85 percent is considered normal.

@example
title: BMI and severity
problem: A 19-year-old woman is 1.60 m tall and weighs 41.0 kg. She restricts intake, fears weight gain and says she is "fine." What is her BMI, and how is the severity graded?
step: $BMI = \frac{41.0}{1.60^2} = \frac{41.0}{2.56} = 16.0$ kg/m².
step: For anorexia nervosa in adults, BMI 16 to 16.99 is moderate severity.
step: Evaluate vital signs, orthostasis, ECG, electrolytes (potassium, magnesium, phosphate), and bone health, and decide the level of care.
step: Lack of recognition of the seriousness of low weight satisfies the third criterion; engage family and start nutritional rehabilitation.

@mistakes
- Excluding an eating disorder because the patient has normal weight (bulimia) or is not "thin enough."
- Starting refeeding rapidly without monitoring phosphate, potassium, magnesium and thiamine.
- Prescribing bupropion in bulimia nervosa, or relying on sedatives as first-line therapy for chronic insomnia.
- Giving only pills for insomnia without CBT-I.
