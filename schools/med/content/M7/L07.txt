@article
**Mycobacteria** are aerobic rods with a waxy cell wall rich in **mycolic acids**. The wall makes them **acid-fast** (they resist decolorization by acid-alcohol after staining with carbolfuchsin: Ziehl-Neelsen or Kinyoun stain), resistant to many disinfectants, and slow growing. *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* has a generation time of roughly 15 to 20 hours, so culture on Löwenstein-Jensen medium takes weeks (liquid systems such as MGIT are faster). **Cord factor** (trehalose dimycolate) is associated with virulent strains.

### Pathogenesis of tuberculosis

Infection begins when droplet nuclei reach the alveoli, where macrophages ingest the bacilli but fail to kill them because the organism inhibits phagosome-lysosome fusion. Bacilli spread to hilar nodes. Within 2 to 8 weeks, a **cell-mediated (type IV) immune response** forms:

1. Infected macrophages secrete **IL-12**, which drives CD4 T-helper type 1 cells.
2. Th1 cells secrete **interferon-gamma**, which activates macrophages.
3. Activated macrophages and **TNF-alpha** assemble a **granuloma** with epithelioid cells, Langhans giant cells and **caseous necrosis**.

The primary lesion (**Ghon focus**) plus hilar adenopathy is the **Ghon complex**; healing leaves calcification. In most people the infection becomes **latent**. Patients with TNF-alpha inhibitors, HIV, transplant immunosuppression, silicosis, diabetes or kidney failure have a higher risk of reactivation, and defects in the IL-12/interferon-gamma pathway predispose to mycobacterial disease. Roughly 5 to 10% of immunocompetent people with latent infection progress in their lifetimes, about half within two years; HIV makes this risk far higher.

**Reactivation** classically involves the **apical and posterior upper lobes** (high oxygen tension), with cavities, cough, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats and weight loss. Progression of primary disease or immune failure causes **miliary tuberculosis** (millet-seed nodules on chest radiograph). Extrapulmonary sites: lymph nodes (scrofula), pleura, **spine (Pott disease)**, meninges (basilar meningitis with lymphocytic CSF and low glucose), kidneys (sterile pyuria), adrenals, and pericardium.

### Diagnosis

- **Active disease**: chest radiograph; three sputum specimens for **AFB smear, culture and a nucleic acid amplification test** (for example Xpert MTB/RIF, which also detects *rpoB* mutations that cause rifampin resistance). Patients are placed in **airborne isolation** (negative-pressure room, N95 respirator for staff) until active disease is excluded or treatment has made them non-infectious.
- **Latent infection** is detected by immune sensitization, which cannot distinguish latent from active disease:
  - **Tuberculin skin test (TST)**: 0.1 mL (5 tuberculin units) of purified protein derivative injected intradermally; read the **induration** (not the redness) at 48 to 72 hours. Cutoffs: **5 mm or more** in HIV infection, recent close contacts, transplant or other immunosuppression (including prolonged prednisone 15 mg/day or more), and fibrotic changes on chest radiograph; **10 mm or more** for recent immigrants from high-prevalence countries, people who inject drugs, health care workers, residents of congregate settings, children under 4, and those with diabetes or kidney failure; **15 mm or more** for people with no risk factors.
  - **Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA)** such as QuantiFERON-TB Gold: blood T cells are stimulated with *M. tuberculosis*-specific antigens (ESAT-6, CFP-10) and interferon-gamma is measured. They need one visit and are **not affected by BCG vaccination** (BCG, a live attenuated *M. bovis*, can cause a false-positive TST). Both tests can be falsely negative with anergy (advanced HIV, overwhelming disease, malnutrition).

A positive test with a normal chest radiograph and no symptoms indicates latent infection.

### Treatment

Active tuberculosis is treated with multiple drugs to prevent resistance: **rifampin, isoniazid, pyrazinamide and ethambutol for 2 months** (intensive phase), then **isoniazid and rifampin for 4 months** (6 months total for drug-susceptible pulmonary disease).

| Drug | Notable toxicity or point |
|---|---|
| Rifampin | Orange-red body fluids; strong CYP450 inducer (many drug interactions); hepatitis; inhibits RNA polymerase |
| Isoniazid | Hepatitis; peripheral neuropathy (give **pyridoxine, vitamin B6**); drug-induced lupus; acetylator status matters |
| Pyrazinamide | Hyperuricemia and gout; hepatitis |
| Ethambutol | **Optic neuritis** (reduced acuity, red-green color loss) |

**Multidrug-resistant tuberculosis** is resistance to at least isoniazid and rifampin. Latent infection is treated to prevent reactivation after active disease is excluded; options include 4 months of daily rifampin (4R), 3 months of weekly isoniazid plus rifapentine (3HP), 3 months of daily isoniazid plus rifampin (3HR), or 6 to 9 months of isoniazid (6H or 9H).

### Other mycobacteria

- ***M. leprae*** (leprosy, Hansen disease): cannot be grown on artificial media; grows in cooler tissues (skin, peripheral nerves) and infects armadillos. **Tuberculoid** disease (strong Th1 response, few bacilli, one or few anesthetic hypopigmented plaques) versus **lepromatous** disease (weak Th1 response with a Th2-dominant pattern, many bacilli, leonine facies, widespread nodules and nerve damage). Treat with dapsone and rifampin, adding clofazimine for multibacillary disease.
- ***M. avium* complex (MAC)**: disseminated disease in advanced HIV (CD4 below 50), and chronic lung disease in bronchiectasis. Treat with a macrolide plus ethambutol (with or without a rifamycin).
- ***M. kansasii***: cavitary lung disease resembling tuberculosis. ***M. marinum***: "fish tank" skin granulomas (grows at 30 degrees C). ***M. abscessus***: infections in cystic fibrosis and after surgery; intrinsically drug-resistant.

@example
title: Interpreting a skin test
problem: A 33-year-old nurse with no symptoms and a normal examination has a TST with 11 mm induration at 48 hours. She is HIV negative and has no other medical problems. Is the test positive, and what is the next step?
step: Health care workers fall in the intermediate-risk group, for whom 10 mm or more is positive. Her result is 11 mm, so it is positive.
step: A positive test cannot separate latent from active disease. She has no symptoms, but a chest radiograph is the next step.
step: If the radiograph is normal, she has latent infection and is offered treatment (for example 4 months of rifampin) after baseline liver evaluation as indicated.

@example
title: Why predictive value matters in screening
problem: In 10,000 people, 2% have latent tuberculosis. A test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 95%. How many are true positives and how many are false positives?
step: Infected: $0.02 \times 10{,}000 = 200$. True positives: $0.80 \times 200 = 160$.
step: Not infected: $9{,}800$. False positives: $0.05 \times 9{,}800 = 490$.
step: The positive predictive value is $160/(160 + 490) \approx 24.6\%$. Even a good test gives mostly false positives when prevalence is low, which is why screening is targeted to higher-risk groups.

@mistakes
- Measuring the redness of a TST rather than the induration, or reading it at the wrong time (48 to 72 hours).
- Treating a positive TST or IGRA as proof of active disease; neither distinguishes latent from active infection.
- Forgetting pyridoxine with isoniazid, or forgetting that rifampin induces drug metabolism.
- Starting a single drug for active tuberculosis; combinations prevent resistance.
