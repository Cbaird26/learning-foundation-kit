This lesson covers organisms that hide: those poisoned by oxygen (**anaerobes**), those too thin for the Gram stain (**spirochetes**), and those that live only inside host cells.

### Anaerobes

Anaerobes lack superoxide dismutase and catalase, so they grow where tissue is poorly perfused. They outnumber aerobes in the gut and mouth. Aminoglycosides do not work against anaerobes because uptake requires oxygen-dependent transport. Key features: foul odor, abscess and gas.

***Bacteroides fragilis*** is a gram-negative anaerobe with a polysaccharide capsule that causes intra-abdominal abscesses after bowel disruption; treat with metronidazole (or a beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor).

***Clostridium*** species are gram-positive, spore-forming anaerobes:

- ***C. perfringens***: **alpha toxin (lecithinase)** destroys cell membranes, causing **gas gangrene** (myonecrosis with crepitus after trauma) and a double zone of hemolysis. Also causes food poisoning from reheated meats. Treat with surgical debridement and penicillin plus clindamycin.
- ***C. tetani***: spores enter wounds; **tetanospasmin** travels retrogradely in neurons and blocks release of GABA and glycine from inhibitory interneurons (Renshaw cells), producing spastic paralysis, trismus (lockjaw), risus sardonicus and opisthotonos. Management: wound care, **tetanus immune globulin**, vaccine toxoid, muscle relaxants and metronidazole.
- ***C. botulinum***: **botulinum toxin** cleaves SNARE proteins, blocking acetylcholine release: **descending symmetric flaccid paralysis** with intact sensation and no fever (diplopia, ptosis, dysphagia, then respiratory failure). Sources: home-canned foods, wounds, and infants (honey; "floppy baby"). Treat with antitoxin (heptavalent for adults; BabyBIG for infants) and ventilatory support.
- ***C. difficile***: toxins A and B damage the colon, causing pseudomembranous colitis after antibiotics (especially clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins). Spores resist alcohol hand rubs, so contact precautions with soap-and-water handwashing. Diagnosis uses stool PCR or toxin assay (with testing only for patients with diarrhea). First-line therapy is oral fidaxomicin (or oral vancomycin); recurrence may need fecal microbiota transplantation.

### Spirochetes

***Treponema pallidum*** causes **syphilis**.

| Stage | Features |
|---|---|
| Primary (about 3 weeks) | Painless indurated chancre |
| Secondary (weeks to months) | Diffuse rash including palms and soles, condylomata lata, mucous patches, lymphadenopathy |
| Latent | Positive serology, no symptoms |
| Tertiary | Gummas, aortitis, tabes dorsalis, Argyll Robertson pupil |
| Congenital | Stillbirth, snuffles, Hutchinson teeth, saddle nose |

It cannot be seen by Gram stain; use **darkfield microscopy** of chancre fluid or serology. **Nontreponemal tests (VDRL, RPR)** detect antibody to cardiolipin, are used for screening and **titer follow-up**, and can be falsely positive (pregnancy, lupus, viral infections). **Treponemal tests (FTA-ABS, TP-PA, EIA)** detect specific antibodies and usually stay positive for life. Penicillin G is the treatment (benzathine penicillin for early syphilis); a transient fever and rash after the first dose is the **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction**. A **fourfold decline (two dilutions)** in RPR titer indicates response, and a fourfold rise suggests relapse or reinfection. Some laboratories use a **reverse-sequence algorithm**: a treponemal immunoassay first, then a quantitative RPR if it is reactive; when the treponemal test is reactive but the RPR is nonreactive, a second, different treponemal test (such as TP-PA) decides, and a reactive result supports past treated, very early or late latent infection rather than a false positive.

***Borrelia burgdorferi*** (Lyme disease): *Ixodes* tick (needs about 36 to 48 hours of attachment), Northeast, upper Midwest. **Erythema migrans** (expanding annular rash), then facial nerve palsy, meningitis and **AV block**, then monoarticular large-joint arthritis. Treat early disease with doxycycline (or amoxicillin); use ceftriaxone for meningitis or heart block. ***Leptospira*** (floodwater, rodent urine) causes fever, conjunctival suffusion and, severely, jaundice with kidney failure (Weil disease); penicillin or doxycycline.

### Obligate intracellular and cell wall-deficient bacteria

- ***Rickettsia rickettsii*** (Rocky Mountain spotted fever): *Dermacentor* ticks; fever, headache and a rash starting on wrists and ankles and spreading centrally with palm and sole involvement. It infects endothelium. Treat promptly with **doxycycline**, even before confirmation, in children too.
- ***Ehrlichia*** and ***Anaplasma***: tick-borne; leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases; morulae in leukocytes; doxycycline.
- ***Coxiella burnetii*** (Q fever): aerosols from livestock; pneumonia or hepatitis without rash; can cause culture-negative endocarditis; doxycycline.
- ***Chlamydia trachomatis***: infectious **elementary body** enters the cell, becomes the replicating **reticulate body**; no peptidoglycan wall of standard type. Serovars A to C cause trachoma, D to K cause urethritis, cervicitis and neonatal conjunctivitis, L1 to L3 cause lymphogranuloma venereum. Diagnose by nucleic acid amplification; treat with doxycycline (azithromycin is an alternative). *C. pneumoniae* and *C. psittaci* (birds, psittacosis) cause atypical pneumonia.
- ***Mycoplasma pneumoniae***: no cell wall (so no Gram stain; resistant to beta-lactams) and cholesterol in the membrane. "Walking pneumonia" in young adults, with **cold agglutinins** (IgM against red cells) and sometimes hemolysis; treat with a macrolide, doxycycline or a fluoroquinolone.
- ***Bartonella henselae***: cat scratch disease; regional lymphadenopathy; bacillary angiomatosis in AIDS.

@example
title: Reading syphilis titers after treatment
problem: A man with secondary syphilis has an RPR titer of 1:32 before benzathine penicillin and 1:4 at 6 months. By how many dilutions and by what fold did the titer fall, and is this a serologic response?
step: Serial two-fold dilutions: 1:32, 1:16, 1:8, 1:4. That is three dilutions down.
step: The fold decrease is $32/4 = 8$, which is $2^3$.
step: A fourfold (two-dilution) or greater decline is the accepted response, so this is an adequate serologic response; the treponemal test will probably remain positive for life.

@example
title: Matching a toxin to its neuromuscular syndrome
problem: Compare tetanus and botulism: both toxins block neurotransmitter release, but one causes spasm and the other paralysis. Explain why.
step: Both toxins cleave SNARE proteins and block vesicle fusion.
step: Tetanospasmin travels retrogradely to the spinal cord and blocks release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters GABA and glycine, so motor neurons fire unopposed: spastic paralysis.
step: Botulinum toxin acts at the peripheral neuromuscular junction and blocks acetylcholine release: flaccid paralysis.

@mistakes
- Treating suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever only after serology returns; doxycycline should start on suspicion.
- Using a beta-lactam for *Mycoplasma* (no cell wall) or relying on a Gram stain for it.
- Using alcohol hand rubs alone against *C. difficile*; spores need soap-and-water handwashing plus contact precautions.
- Interpreting a positive treponemal test as active infection when the RPR is negative without considering past treated disease or a false positive.
