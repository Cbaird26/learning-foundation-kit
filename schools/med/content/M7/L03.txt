@article
Gram-positive cocci split first on one test: **catalase**. Staphylococci make catalase (they break hydrogen peroxide into oxygen bubbles); streptococci and enterococci do not.

### Staphylococci (catalase positive, clusters)

***Staphylococcus aureus*** is **coagulase positive** (it clots plasma by converting fibrinogen to fibrin), golden on culture, and salt tolerant. Virulence factors: **protein A** (binds the Fc portion of IgG and blocks opsonization), a fibrin-forming coagulase, hemolysins, leukocidins (Panton-Valentine leukocidin in many community-associated MRSA strains) and toxins:

- **Preformed enterotoxin**: food poisoning with vomiting 1 to 6 hours after eating contaminated food (custards, salads, meats); heat stable and self-limited.
- **TSST-1**: toxic shock syndrome, with fever, diffuse macular rash, hypotension and multi-organ failure (tampons, wound packing). It is a **superantigen**: it cross-links MHC class II outside the peptide groove to the T-cell receptor V-beta region, activating up to 20 to 30% of T cells and causing a cytokine storm.
- **Exfoliative toxin**: cleaves desmoglein 1, causing staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome in infants (Nikolsky sign positive, blisters in the stratum granulosum).

Clinical syndromes also include impetigo, folliculitis, abscess and cellulitis, acute osteomyelitis and septic arthritis, pneumonia after influenza, and **bacteremia with endocarditis** (right-sided tricuspid valve disease in people who inject drugs). *S. aureus* bacteremia is never treated as a contaminant.

**MRSA** carries *mecA*, encoding PBP2a, and resists nearly all beta-lactams (except ceftaroline). Typical agents: vancomycin, linezolid or daptomycin (daptomycin is inactivated by lung surfactant, so not for pneumonia) for serious infection; trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline for mild skin infection. Methicillin-susceptible isolates are treated with an anti-staphylococcal beta-lactam (nafcillin, oxacillin, or cefazolin).

***S. epidermidis*** (coagulase negative, novobiocin sensitive) forms **biofilms** on catheters, shunts and prosthetic valves or joints. ***S. saprophyticus*** (coagulase negative, **novobiocin resistant**) causes urinary tract infection in sexually active young women.

### Streptococci (catalase negative, chains or pairs)

Classify by **hemolysis** on blood agar: **beta** (complete clearing), **alpha** (partial, green), gamma (none). Beta-hemolytic species are divided by **Lancefield** cell-wall carbohydrate groups.

***S. pyogenes*** (group A, beta-hemolytic, **bacitracin sensitive**, **PYR positive**) causes pharyngitis, impetigo, erysipelas, cellulitis, necrotizing fasciitis and toxic shock. Virulence factors: **M protein** (antiphagocytic; its type defines strains), streptolysin O (basis of the **ASO titer**), streptolysin S, hyaluronidase and **pyrogenic exotoxins** (scarlet fever: sandpaper rash, strawberry tongue, circumoral pallor). Penicillin remains the drug of choice. Two non-suppurative sequelae follow infection:

| | Acute rheumatic fever | Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis |
|---|---|---|
| Follows | Pharyngitis only | Pharyngitis (1 to 3 weeks) or skin infection (2 to 6 weeks) |
| Mechanism | Molecular mimicry of M protein with host tissue (type II) | Immune-complex deposition (type III) |
| Features | Jones criteria: carditis, migratory polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, nodules | Hematuria (cola-colored urine), edema, hypertension, low C3 |
| Prevented by treating the infection? | Yes | No |

***S. agalactiae*** (group B, bacitracin resistant, hippurate positive, **CAMP positive**) colonizes the vagina and rectum and causes neonatal sepsis, pneumonia and meningitis. U.S. guidelines screen pregnant women by vaginal-rectal culture at 36 to 37 weeks (36 0/7 to 37 6/7) and give intrapartum penicillin G to carriers (or others with risk factors); this lowers early-onset disease.

***S. pneumoniae*** (alpha-hemolytic, lancet-shaped diplococci, encapsulated, **optochin sensitive**, **bile soluble**) is the leading cause of community-acquired pneumonia (lobar, rust-colored sputum), otitis media, sinusitis and adult bacterial meningitis; it produces IgA protease. Pneumococcal conjugate and polysaccharide vaccines are given to children, older adults and asplenic patients.

**Viridans streptococci** (alpha-hemolytic, optochin resistant, bile insoluble) are oral flora: *S. mutans* causes dental caries, and *S. sanguinis* causes subacute endocarditis on damaged valves after dental procedures. *S. gallolyticus* (formerly *S. bovis*) bacteremia or endocarditis should prompt colonoscopy for colon neoplasia.

### Enterococci

*Enterococcus faecalis* and *E. faecium* (group D) are **bile-esculin positive** and grow in 6.5% NaCl. They cause urinary tract infection, biliary and intra-abdominal infection, and endocarditis. Intrinsic resistance to cephalosporins; **VRE** acquire *vanA* (D-Ala-D-lactate). Ampicillin is first line for susceptible isolates; linezolid or daptomycin for VRE.

@example
title: A staph-or-strep decision path
problem: A wound culture grows gram-positive cocci in clusters. Catalase is positive, and a tube coagulase test is positive. Name the organism and its likely treatment depends on which gene?
step: Catalase positive means *Staphylococcus*, not *Streptococcus* or *Enterococcus*.
step: Coagulase positive identifies *S. aureus* among staphylococci.
step: Whether to use an anti-staphylococcal beta-lactam or vancomycin depends on *mecA* (MRSA). Susceptibility testing, with cefoxitin or a *mecA*/PBP2a assay, decides it.

@example
title: Lancefield group from a lab sheet
problem: A swab from a newborn's blood culture shows beta-hemolytic, catalase-negative gram-positive cocci that are bacitracin resistant, hippurate positive and CAMP positive. Identify the organism and name the prevention strategy.
step: Beta-hemolytic and catalase negative narrows to a beta-hemolytic streptococcus.
step: Bacitracin resistant excludes group A (which is bacitracin sensitive); hippurate and CAMP positive identify group B *S. agalactiae*.
step: Prevention is vaginal-rectal screening at 36 to 37 weeks of gestation and intrapartum penicillin G for carriers.

@mistakes
- Treating catalase as a species test; it only separates staphylococci from streptococci and enterococci.
- Assuming MRSA means resistance to vancomycin; it means resistance to methicillin and other beta-lactams via PBP2a.
- Expecting treatment of pharyngitis to prevent post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis; it prevents rheumatic fever only.
- Calling viridans streptococci optochin sensitive (that is *S. pneumoniae*) or *S. pyogenes* bacitracin resistant (that is group B).

prompt: A rapid antigen test for group A streptococcus is evaluated in 200 children. Throat culture is positive in 60 and negative in 140. The rapid test is falsely positive in 14 of the 140 culture-negative children. What is the specificity of the rapid test, as a percentage?
answer: 90
check: (140 - 14) / 140 * 100
hint: Specificity is the proportion of culture-negative children whose rapid test is negative.
hint: True negatives equal 140 minus the false positives.
solution: True negatives $= 140 - 14 = 126$. Specificity $= 126/140 = 0.90 = 90\%$.
tol: 0.5
unit: %

@quiz num
prompt: In a hypothetical trial of intrapartum prophylaxis in group B streptococcal carriers, early-onset neonatal disease occurs in 4% of untreated pregnancies and in 1% of treated pregnancies. What is the number needed to treat to prevent one case? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 33.3
check: 1 / (0.04 - 0.01)
hint: NNT is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction.
hint: The absolute risk reduction is $0.04 - 0.01$.
solution: ARR $= 0.04 - 0.01 = 0.03$; NNT $= 1/0.03 = 33.3$.
tol: 0.1
