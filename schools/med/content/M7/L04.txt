Gram-positive rods are classified by two questions: **do they form spores**, and **are they aerobic or anaerobic**? This week covers the aerobic and facultative ones; the anaerobic *Clostridium* species follow in week 6.

### *Bacillus anthracis*

A large, nonmotile, spore-forming rod that causes **anthrax**, acquired from contact with infected herbivores or their products (hides, wool) or from deliberate release. It has a **capsule of poly-D-glutamic acid** (plasmid pXO2) and a three-part toxin (plasmid pXO1): **protective antigen (PA)** binds host receptors and forms the pore that lets the other two enter; **edema factor (EF)** is a calmodulin-dependent adenylate cyclase that raises cAMP and causes edema; **lethal factor (LF)** is a zinc metalloprotease that cleaves MAP kinase kinases and kills cells.

- **Cutaneous** (most common): a painless papule becoming a vesicle and then a black eschar with surrounding edema; usually self-limited but can disseminate.
- **Inhalational**: biphasic illness with a flu-like prodrome then abrupt dyspnea, shock, **hemorrhagic mediastinitis (widened mediastinum on chest radiograph)** and often hemorrhagic meningitis; the lungs show little parenchymal pneumonia. Mortality is high.
- **Gastrointestinal** disease is rare.

Gram stain of blood or tissue shows large gram-positive rods in chains ("boxcars"). Treatment combines bactericidal antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin with a second agent, with antitoxin for systemic disease; vaccine and post-exposure prophylaxis exist.

### *Bacillus cereus*

Food poisoning, classically from reheated **fried rice**. It has two syndromes: an **emetic** type (preformed heat-stable toxin, onset 1 to 6 hours, vomiting) and a **diarrheal** type (enterotoxin made in the gut, onset 8 to 16 hours, watery diarrhea). Treatment is supportive.

### *Corynebacterium diphtheriae*

Club-shaped gram-positive rods in "Chinese-character" arrangements with metachromatic granules. Culture uses Loeffler medium or tellurite agar (black colonies). Toxigenic strains carry a **beta-prophage** with the *tox* gene. **Diphtheria toxin** is an A-B toxin: the B subunit binds heparin-binding EGF-like receptors and the A subunit **ADP-ribosylates elongation factor 2 (EF-2)**, halting protein synthesis. The result is a tough gray **pseudomembrane** over the tonsils and pharynx (bleeds if scraped), "bull neck" lymphadenopathy, airway obstruction, **myocarditis** and neuropathies. The **Elek test** confirms toxin production. Treat with **diphtheria antitoxin** (neutralizes unbound toxin) plus penicillin or erythromycin; vaccinate with diphtheria toxoid (DTaP in children, Tdap or Td in adults).

### *Listeria monocytogenes*

A short, beta-hemolytic, catalase-positive rod with **tumbling motility at room temperature**; it grows at refrigerator temperatures ("cold enrichment"). It is a **facultative intracellular** pathogen: listeriolysin O lets it escape the phagosome, and ActA polymerizes host actin into "rockets" that propel it from cell to cell, so **cell-mediated immunity** is required for control. Sources: unpasteurized soft cheese, deli meats, hot dogs and other ready-to-eat refrigerated foods. Groups at risk: **pregnant women** (febrile flu-like illness, miscarriage, stillbirth), **neonates** (sepsis, meningitis), older adults and patients with impaired T-cell function (transplant, corticosteroids, lymphoma). Treatment is **ampicillin** (with gentamicin for severe disease); cephalosporins are inactive, so empiric meningitis regimens in at-risk patients add ampicillin.

### Branching rods: *Nocardia* and *Actinomyces*

| | *Nocardia* | *Actinomyces israelii* |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Strictly aerobic | Anaerobic |
| Acid-fast | Weakly (modified acid-fast) | No |
| Host | Immunocompromised (steroids, transplant) | Normal flora after trauma or dental work |
| Disease | Pulmonary nodules or cavities; brain abscess; skin | Cervicofacial (jaw) abscess with draining sinuses; pelvic infection with IUD |
| Clue | Soil exposure | **Sulfur granules** |
| Treatment | Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole | Penicillin G or ampicillin |

### Staph or strep? The test algorithm

For gram-positive cocci that you meet in weeks 3 and 4: **catalase** positive and clusters means *Staphylococcus*; then **coagulase** positive is *S. aureus*, negative is a coagulase-negative species (novobiocin resistant: *S. saprophyticus*). Catalase negative means *Streptococcus* or *Enterococcus*: then check **hemolysis**; for beta-hemolytic organisms, **bacitracin sensitive and PYR positive** is group A, **bacitracin resistant, CAMP and hippurate positive** is group B; for alpha-hemolytic organisms, **optochin sensitive** is *S. pneumoniae* and optochin resistant is viridans streptococci; **bile esculin positive** with growth in 6.5% NaCl indicates *Enterococcus*.

@example
title: Food-poisoning outbreak arithmetic
problem: Of 40 people at a banquet, 12 develop vomiting 3 hours after eating, and rice served warm from a pot left out all day is implicated. What is the attack rate, and which organism does the timing suggest?
step: Attack rate = number ill / number exposed $= 12/40 = 0.30 = 30\%$.
step: Vomiting within 1 to 6 hours of eating points to a preformed toxin. With rice, the emetic form of *Bacillus cereus* is the classic cause (*S. aureus* enterotoxin fits the timing too, but the rice is the clue).
step: Treatment is supportive with hydration; antibiotics do not help because the toxin is already formed.

@example
title: Choosing therapy for a high-risk host
problem: A 31-year-old pregnant woman at 33 weeks has fever and myalgia after eating soft cheese. Blood culture grows gram-positive rods with tumbling motility. Which antibiotic is appropriate and which common class would fail?
step: Tumbling motility at 25 degrees C and the food exposure identify *Listeria monocytogenes*.
step: The drug of choice is ampicillin (for pregnancy, penicillin-class agents are standard).
step: Cephalosporins lack activity against *Listeria*, so ceftriaxone alone would fail.

@mistakes
- Forgetting ampicillin when treating meningitis in pregnant, older or immunocompromised patients; cephalosporins miss *Listeria*.
- Mixing up toxin targets: diphtheria toxin inactivates EF-2; anthrax toxin edema factor raises cAMP.
- Calling *Nocardia* and *Actinomyces* the same organism; *Nocardia* is aerobic and weakly acid-fast, *Actinomyces* is anaerobic with sulfur granules.
- Expecting inhalational anthrax to look like lobar pneumonia; the hallmark is a widened mediastinum.

@quiz num
prompt: At a church supper, 30 of 40 people who ate the potato salad became ill, and 4 of 40 people who did not eat it became ill. What is the relative risk of illness associated with eating the potato salad?
answer: 7.5
check: (30 / 40) / (4 / 40)
hint: Relative risk is the attack rate in the exposed divided by the attack rate in the unexposed.
solution: Exposed attack rate $= 30/40 = 0.75$; unexposed $= 4/40 = 0.10$; RR $= 0.75/0.10 = 7.5$.
