### HIV: a retrovirus that attacks CD4 cells

HIV-1 is an enveloped **retrovirus** (lentivirus) with two copies of positive-sense single-stranded RNA. Its genome has three main genes: **gag** (capsid protein **p24**, matrix), **pol** (reverse transcriptase, integrase, protease) and **env** (**gp120** and **gp41**). The envelope protein **gp120** binds **CD4** and a co-receptor, **CCR5** (macrophage-tropic strains; early infection) or **CXCR4** (T-cell-tropic strains; later); **gp41** mediates fusion. Inside the cell, **reverse transcriptase** (error-prone, generating diversity) makes double-stranded DNA from the RNA, **integrase** inserts it into the host genome as a provirus, and **protease** cleaves viral polyproteins so virions mature.

Transmission is by sex, blood and perinatal or breastfeeding exposure. About 2 to 4 weeks after infection, many patients have an **acute retroviral syndrome** (mononucleosis-like illness with very high viral load). Without treatment, CD4 T cells fall over years; **AIDS** is defined by a CD4 count below 200 cells/microliter or an AIDS-defining illness.

**Diagnosis**: a **fourth-generation antigen/antibody combination immunoassay** (detects p24 antigen plus HIV-1 and HIV-2 antibodies), followed if reactive by an HIV-1/2 antibody differentiation assay and, if discordant, an HIV-1 RNA test (nucleic acid test). In infants of infected mothers, maternal IgG crosses the placenta, so diagnosis uses **virologic testing (RNA or DNA PCR)**. Therapy is monitored by plasma HIV RNA (viral load) and CD4 count.

**Treatment** is lifelong **antiretroviral therapy (ART)**, started at diagnosis. A typical initial regimen combines an **integrase strand transfer inhibitor** (dolutegravir, bictegravir) with two **nucleoside/nucleotide reverse transcriptase inhibitors** (tenofovir plus emtricitabine, or other pairs). Other classes: **NNRTIs**, **protease inhibitors** (boosted with ritonavir or cobicistat), **entry inhibitors** (maraviroc blocks CCR5; enfuvirtide blocks gp41). Test for HLA-B*57:01 before abacavir (hypersensitivity). Preexposure prophylaxis (tenofovir-based) and prompt postexposure prophylaxis reduce acquisition; maternal ART lowers perinatal transmission to under 1%.

**Opportunistic infections by CD4 count**:

| CD4 (cells/microliter) | Typical problems | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|---|
| Below 200 | *Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia | Trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole |
| Below 100 | *Toxoplasma* encephalitis, cryptococcal meningitis, esophageal candidiasis | TMP-SMX (toxoplasma IgG positive) |
| Below 50 | Disseminated *M. avium* complex, CMV retinitis | Treat with ART; routine MAC prophylaxis is not needed if ART is started |

### Hepatitis viruses

**Hepatitis A (HAV)**: naked positive-sense RNA picornavirus; fecal-oral (contaminated food, water, shellfish, day care); acute self-limited illness, never chronic; diagnose with **IgM anti-HAV**; inactivated vaccine.

**Hepatitis B (HBV)**: enveloped **partially double-stranded DNA** hepadnavirus that replicates through an RNA intermediate using reverse transcriptase. Spread by blood, sex and perinatal exposure. Chronic infection occurs in about 90% of infected infants and under 5% of immunocompetent adults, and can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **HBsAg** | Surface antigen: current infection (acute or chronic); present for over 6 months defines chronic infection |
| **Anti-HBs** | Immunity (from vaccine or recovery) |
| **IgM anti-HBc** | Acute or recent infection; often the only marker during the **window period** |
| **IgG anti-HBc** | Past or chronic infection; **not** produced by vaccine |
| **HBeAg** | High viral replication and infectivity |
| **HBV DNA** | Viral load |

Typical patterns:

| HBsAg | Anti-HBs | Anti-HBc | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Negative | Positive | Negative | Immune by vaccination |
| Negative | Positive | IgG positive | Resolved infection (immune) |
| Positive | Negative | IgM positive | Acute infection |
| Negative | Negative | IgM positive | Window period of acute infection |
| Positive | Negative | IgG positive (IgM negative) | Chronic infection |

Chronic HBV is treated with tenofovir or entecavir (suppressive); immunosuppression, especially rituximab, can reactivate resolved infection. Newborns of HBsAg-positive mothers receive HBV vaccine plus **hepatitis B immune globulin** within 12 hours of birth.

**Hepatitis C (HCV)**: enveloped positive-sense RNA **flavivirus** spread by blood (injection drug use, historic transfusions). About 75 to 85% develop chronic infection, with cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma and cryoglobulinemia. Screen with **anti-HCV antibody, then HCV RNA** to confirm active infection. **Direct-acting antivirals** (for example sofosbuvir-velpatasvir) given for 8 to 12 weeks cure over 95% of cases. There is no vaccine.

**Hepatitis D (HDV)**: defective RNA virus requiring HBsAg to form virions; **coinfection** with HBV or **superinfection** of a chronic HBV carrier (worse prognosis).

**Hepatitis E (HEV)**: fecal-oral RNA virus, usually self-limited, with high mortality in pregnancy.

@example
title: Interpreting a hepatitis B panel
problem: A 26-year-old nurse has HBsAg negative, anti-HBs positive and total anti-HBc negative. What does this mean and does she need more vaccine?
step: Anti-HBs positive means she has antibody to the surface antigen.
step: Anti-HBc is negative, so she has not been infected; vaccination produces anti-HBs but never anti-HBc.
step: Interpretation: immunity from vaccination. If anti-HBs is adequate (at least 10 mIU/mL) she needs no further doses.

@example
title: The window period
problem: A man with acute hepatitis has HBsAg negative, anti-HBs negative and IgM anti-HBc positive. Explain how that can be acute hepatitis B.
step: During acute infection, HBsAg disappears before anti-HBs appears.
step: In that gap (the window period) neither surface marker is detectable.
step: IgM anti-HBc rises early and persists through the gap, so it identifies acute infection. HBV DNA would also be detectable.

@mistakes
- Reading anti-HBs alone as proof of vaccination; recovered infection also gives anti-HBs, so check anti-HBc.
- Calling a negative HBsAg "no infection" in someone with IgM anti-HBc during the window period.
- Forgetting that a positive HIV screening immunoassay needs confirmatory testing and that infants need PCR rather than antibody testing.
- Overlooking HBV reactivation during rituximab or chemotherapy in patients with resolved infection (anti-HBc positive).

prompt: Suppose a fourth-generation HIV screening test has sensitivity 99% and specificity 99.5% and is used in 100,000 people with a true prevalence of 0.1%. What is the positive predictive value of a reactive screen, as a percentage rounded to one decimal place?
answer: 16.5
check: (100000 * 0.001 * 0.99) / (100000 * 0.001 * 0.99 + 100000 * 0.999 * 0.005) * 100
hint: Infected people number 100 and uninfected 99,900.
hint: True positives are 99; false positives are $0.005 \times 99{,}900$.
solution: True positives $= 99$; false positives $= 0.005 \times 99{,}900 \approx 499.5$; PPV $= 99/598.5 = 16.5\%$. A reactive screen at low prevalence needs confirmatory testing.
tol: 0.2
unit: %
