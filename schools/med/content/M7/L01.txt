@article
Bacteria are **prokaryotes**: no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles, a single circular chromosome in a region called the nucleoid, and 70S ribosomes (30S + 50S subunits). Those 70S ribosomes, the peptidoglycan cell wall and the folate pathway are why so many antibiotics are selectively toxic: they hit structures that human cells lack.

### The envelope decides the stain and the disease

**Peptidoglycan** is a mesh of alternating N-acetylglucosamine and N-acetylmuramic acid chains cross-linked by short peptides ending in D-alanine-D-alanine. Transpeptidases, also called **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)**, form the cross-links. Beta-lactams mimic D-Ala-D-Ala and block transpeptidation; vancomycin binds D-Ala-D-Ala itself.

| Feature | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
|---|---|---|
| Peptidoglycan | Thick | Thin, in the periplasm |
| Outer membrane | Absent | Present, with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) |
| Teichoic and lipoteichoic acids | Present | Absent |
| Gram stain | Purple (retains crystal violet) | Pink-red (counterstain safranin) |
| Endotoxin | No | Yes: lipid A of LPS |

**Lipid A** is the toxic part of LPS (endotoxin). It binds CD14/TLR4 on macrophages and drives release of TNF-alpha, IL-1 and IL-6, causing fever, vasodilation, hypotension, DIC and septic shock. Antibiotic-induced lysis releases extra endotoxin. Gram-positive organisms provoke similar cytokine release through lipoteichoic acid and peptidoglycan fragments.

Other structures worth knowing:

- **Capsule** (usually polysaccharide) resists phagocytosis; the main encapsulated pathogens are *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, *Neisseria meningitidis*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *Salmonella* Typhi (Vi antigen) and *Cryptococcus* (a fungus). Asplenic patients are prone to these. Capsular polysaccharide vaccines are made more immunogenic by conjugating them to a protein, which recruits T-cell help.
- **Pili/fimbriae** mediate adherence; the **F (sex) pilus** mediates conjugation.
- **Flagella** give motility; **biofilms** protect organisms on catheters and prosthetic material.
- **Spores** (*Bacillus*, *Clostridium*) form when nutrients run out. They contain dipicolinic acid with calcium, are metabolically dormant and survive boiling and alcohol; autoclaving at 121 degrees C kills them.

### Growth

Bacteria divide by binary fission, so numbers double each generation: $N = N_0 \cdot 2^{n}$ where $n = t / t_d$ is the number of doublings and $t_d$ is the doubling time. A growth curve has four phases: **lag** (adapting, no net growth), **log/exponential** (maximal growth and antibiotic susceptibility), **stationary** (nutrient limitation; growth equals death; secondary metabolites and spores) and **death**. Doubling time varies widely: *E. coli* about 20 minutes in rich medium, *M. tuberculosis* many hours, which is why cultures take weeks.

### Genetics and resistance

Bacteria evolve fast mainly through **horizontal gene transfer**:

- **Transformation**: uptake of naked DNA by a competent cell (*S. pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus*, *Neisseria*).
- **Conjugation**: plasmid transfer through the F pilus. A cell carrying the F plasmid (F+) donates to F-; if the plasmid integrates into the chromosome (Hfr), chromosomal genes can transfer. R plasmids carry resistance genes.
- **Transduction**: a bacteriophage carries bacterial DNA. In generalized transduction a lytic phage packages random host DNA; in specialized transduction a prophage excising imprecisely carries adjacent genes.
- **Lysogenic conversion**: a temperate phage integrates as a prophage and its own gene confers a new trait. Toxins encoded this way include diphtheria toxin, cholera toxin, Shiga toxin and the erythrogenic (pyrogenic exotoxin) toxins of *S. pyogenes*.
- **Transposons** are mobile elements that hop between plasmid and chromosome.

Resistance mechanisms fall into a few types: enzymatic destruction (beta-lactamases), **altered target** (*mecA* encodes PBP2a with low beta-lactam affinity in MRSA; *vanA* changes D-Ala-D-Ala to D-Ala-D-lactate in vancomycin-resistant enterococci), reduced uptake or **efflux**, and drug modification (aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes).

@example
title: Exponential growth and doubling time
problem: A broth culture contains 500 bacteria per mL and has a doubling time of 30 minutes in log phase. How many bacteria per mL are present after 2.5 hours, assuming no lag and no nutrient limit?
step: Convert the time to doublings: $n = t/t_d = 150\text{ min} / 30\text{ min} = 5$.
step: Apply $N = N_0 \cdot 2^n$: $N = 500 \times 2^5$.
step: $2^5 = 32$, so $N = 500 \times 32 = 16{,}000$ bacteria per mL.
step: Sanity check: five doublings multiply the population by 32, not by 5; that is the signature of exponential growth.

@example
title: Which gene-transfer mechanism?
problem: A nontoxigenic strain of a gram-positive rod becomes toxigenic after exposure to a temperate bacteriophage. Name the process and explain why the toxin gene persists.
step: The phage is temperate, so it can integrate into the chromosome as a prophage rather than lyse the cell.
step: The toxin gene belongs to the phage genome, not to random host DNA, so this is lysogenic conversion (distinct from generalized transduction, where the phage carries host DNA).
step: The prophage replicates with the chromosome, so every daughter cell is toxigenic. Diphtheria toxin in *Corynebacterium diphtheriae* is the classic example.

@mistakes
- Saying gram-negative bacteria have a thicker peptidoglycan layer; they have a thin layer inside an outer membrane, and the outer membrane is what makes them resist many drugs.
- Confusing endotoxin (lipid A of LPS, in gram-negative cell walls) with exotoxins (secreted proteins, from either group).
- Treating doubling as addition: after $n$ doublings the count is multiplied by $2^n$, not by $n$.
- Calling all phage-mediated transfer "transduction": lysogenic conversion is a phage gene conferring a trait, while transduction moves bacterial DNA.

@quiz num
prompt: In log phase a culture grows from $2.0 \times 10^4$ to $6.4 \times 10^5$ cells/mL in 100 minutes. What is the doubling time in minutes?
answer: 20
check: 100 / math.log2(6.4e5 / 2e4)
hint: First find the fold increase, then how many doublings produce it.
hint: The fold increase is 32, which equals $2^n$ for some $n$.
solution: Fold increase $= 6.4\times10^5 / 2\times10^4 = 32 = 2^5$, so there were 5 doublings in 100 minutes: $t_d = 100/5 = 20$ minutes.
tol: 1%
