@article
The MCAT is a computer-based, standardized exam that tests whether you can **use** science knowledge and reasoning to solve problems, not whether you can recite facts. Almost every question is tied to a passage, a figure or a stated scenario, so this course is built around passage-based practice.

### The four sections

| Section | Questions | Time | Main content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical and Physical Foundations of Biological Systems | 59 | 95 min | General chemistry, organic chemistry, physics, some biochemistry |
| Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS) | 53 | 90 min | Humanities and social science passages; no outside knowledge |
| Biological and Biochemical Foundations of Living Systems | 59 | 95 min | Biology, biochemistry, some chemistry |
| Psychological, Social, and Biological Foundations of Behavior | 59 | 95 min | Psychology, sociology, related biology |

That is 230 questions and 375 minutes of testing, or 6 hours 15 minutes. With the tutorial, breaks and check-in, the test day lasts roughly seven and a half hours. Three of the sections are mostly **passage-based** (a passage of about 5 to 7 questions) plus a smaller number of **discrete** questions that stand alone.

### Scoring

Each section is scored on a scaled range of **118 to 132**, with a midpoint of 125. The four section scores add to a total of **472 to 528**, with a midpoint of 500. Scores are scaled to account for differences in difficulty between test forms, so a raw count of correct answers does not map to one fixed score. There is no penalty for a wrong answer, so you should never leave a question blank.

### Pacing

Divide time by questions to get a baseline. In a 95-minute section with 59 questions you have about $95/59 \approx 1.6$ minutes per question. Discrete questions usually take less than that, which banks time for dense passages. A passage with 5 questions at that pace earns about 8 minutes in total, including reading.

### Planning backwards from the test date

1. **Take a full-length diagnostic** under real timing to get section scores and, more importantly, a list of why you missed questions.
2. **Set a target** from the programs you are considering, and compute the gap in points.
3. **Count the weeks and weekly hours** you can truly protect, then multiply for total hours.
4. **Allocate hours** by need: weak sections and weak topics get more, but no section drops to zero.
5. **Mix three activities**: content review, active recall (flashcards, blank-page summaries), and timed passage practice. Practice is where most score gains come from.
6. **Schedule checkpoints**: a timed block each week and a full-length exam every few weeks, each followed by an error log (Week 12).

A plan is a hypothesis. After each checkpoint, move hours toward whatever the data show is still costing you points.

@example
title: Pacing a Chemical and Physical section
problem: A section has 59 questions in 95 minutes: 44 are in 10 passages and 15 are discrete. You decide to spend 1 minute per discrete question. How many minutes can each passage (reading plus its questions) take on average?
step: Time for discrete questions: $15 \times 1 = 15$ minutes.
step: Time left for passages: $95 - 15 = 80$ minutes.
step: Spread over 10 passages: $80/10 = 8$ minutes per passage, which is about 1.8 minutes per passage question.
step: In practice you will run faster on easy passages and slower on hard ones; the 8 minutes is a checkpoint, not a limit. If a passage is eating well over 10 minutes, mark its hardest questions and move on.

@example
title: Turning a diagnostic into a gap
problem: A diagnostic gives section scores 124, 126, 121 and 125. Your target total is 508. How many points do you need to gain, and which section should get the most study hours first?
step: Total now: $124 + 126 + 121 + 125 = 496$.
step: Gap to the target: $508 - 496 = 12$ points, or 3 per section on average.
step: The lowest section (121) has the most room to rise, so it gets the largest share of hours, while the others still receive maintenance practice.
step: Next, look at the error pattern inside that section: content gaps need review, while misreads and timing problems need timed practice.

@mistakes
- Studying only the content you already like instead of the sections and topics that cost you the most points.
- Doing untimed practice only; the exam rewards accuracy at speed.
- Reading answer explanations without writing down why you missed the question, so the same error repeats.
- Planning more weekly hours than you can actually protect.
