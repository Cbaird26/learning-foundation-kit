@article
Real MCAT passages rarely stay inside one subject. A single passage on blood gas analysis may require acid-base chemistry (buffers), physics (gas laws and diffusion), physiology (ventilation and renal compensation) and statistics (interpreting a table of patients). The key is to **find the model hiding behind each question** and to supply constants only when the passage does.

### A method for integrated passages

1. **Skim the passage for the system** (what is being studied?) and the **question stems** (what is being asked?). Do this before reading closely.
2. **Name the model** for each stem: "Henderson-Hasselbalch," "Nernst equation," "continuity equation," "Hardy-Weinberg," "confounding."
3. **Collect the data**: write the numbers with units next to the model.
4. **Compute or reason**, and perform a sanity check: units, order of magnitude and direction of change.
5. **Mark and move** if a stem needs more than about 2 minutes. Come back after finishing the rest of the passage.

### Models that combine well

- **Bicarbonate buffer**: $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log\dfrac{[\text{HCO}_3^-]}{0.03\,P_{\text{CO}_2}}$ with $[\text{HCO}_3^-]$ in mEq/L and $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ in mmHg. Ventilation changes $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ (respiratory component) and the kidney changes bicarbonate (metabolic component).
- **Nernst equation** at 37 degrees C: $E_{ion} = \dfrac{61.5\ \text{mV}}{z}\log_{10}\dfrac{[\text{ion}]_{out}}{[\text{ion}]_{in}}$. Membrane potential lies between the equilibrium potentials of the ions, weighted by their conductances.
- **Oxygen content**: $C_{\text{O}_2} = 1.34 \times [\text{Hb}] \times S_{\text{O}_2} + 0.003 \times P_{\text{O}_2}$ in mL O$_2$/dL, with $[\text{Hb}]$ in g/dL and $S_{\text{O}_2}$ as a fraction. Almost all oxygen is carried on hemoglobin.
- **Flow and resistance**: $Q = \Delta P / R$ and $R \propto 1/r^4$ for laminar flow.
- **Henderson-Hasselbalch for drugs**: a weak acid is mostly uncharged (and crosses membranes) when $\text{pH} < \text{p}K_a$; a weak base when $\text{pH} > \text{p}K_a$.
- **Diffusion (Fick)**: rate is proportional to surface area and concentration or partial pressure gradient, and inversely proportional to thickness.

### Where students lose points

Students lose points not because the content is hard, but because they apply a rule to the wrong quantity, forget that a passage may redefine a symbol, or mishandle units. A passage may give a nonstandard value on purpose; use the passage's value. Check whether a concentration is in the right units before taking a logarithm, and whether a question asks for a *change* or a *final value*.

### Timed practice

A full block of mixed passages is the closest rehearsal for test day. Use a timer, apply the five steps, mark and move, and log every error by cause (Week 12). Do not look up facts during a block: the point is to measure what you can do under time.

@example
title: Nernst potential for potassium
problem: A neuron has $[\text{K}^+]_{out} = 5$ mM and $[\text{K}^+]_{in} = 140$ mM at 37 degrees C. Use $E = (61.5/z)\log_{10}([\text{out}]/[\text{in}])$ mV. What is the equilibrium potential for K$^+$?
step: For K$^+$, $z = +1$, and the ratio is $5/140 = 0.0357$.
step: $\log_{10}(0.0357) = -1.447$.
step: $E_K = 61.5 \times (-1.447) = -89$ mV. Potassium would flow out until the interior is about 89 mV more negative than the outside.

@example
title: Fick's law reasoning
problem: In a lung disease, the alveolar membrane thickens to twice its normal thickness and the surface area available for diffusion falls to half of normal. By what factor does the diffusion rate of oxygen change if the pressure gradient is unchanged?
step: Fick's law gives rate $\propto A\,\Delta P / d$.
step: Area is multiplied by $1/2$ and thickness by 2, so the rate is multiplied by $(1/2)/2 = 1/4$.
step: The diffusion rate falls to one quarter of normal, so a hypoxemia that worsens with exercise is expected.

@mistakes
- Using the Nernst equation with the ratio inverted ([in]/[out]) and getting the wrong sign.
- Taking the logarithm of the bicarbonate concentration alone rather than the bicarbonate to dissolved-CO$_2$ ratio.
- Ignoring a nonstandard constant supplied in the passage.
- Spending a long time on one stem in an integrated passage instead of marking it and returning.

@practice num
prompt: An arterial blood sample has $[\text{HCO}_3^-] = 24$ mEq/L and $P_{\text{CO}_2} = 40$ mmHg. Use $\text{pH} = 6.1 + \log_{10}\big([\text{HCO}_3^-]/(0.03\,P_{\text{CO}_2})\big)$. What is the pH, to two decimal places?
answer: 7.40
check: 6.1 + math.log10(24 / (0.03 * 40))
tol: 0.01
hint: First find the dissolved CO$_2$ term, $0.03 \times 40$.
hint: The ratio inside the logarithm is $24/1.2$.
solution: Dissolved CO$_2$ $= 0.03 \times 40 = 1.2$. The ratio is $24/1.2 = 20$, and $\log_{10}20 = 1.30$, so $\text{pH} = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40$.

@practice num
prompt: Atherosclerosis narrows a coronary artery so that its radius falls to half of its original value. If the vessel remains cylindrical and flow is laminar, by what factor does the resistance to flow increase?
answer: 16
check: 1 / 0.5 ** 4
hint: In laminar flow, resistance varies as $1/r^4$.
hint: Compute $(1/0.5)^4$.
solution: $R \propto 1/r^4$, so halving the radius multiplies resistance by $2^4 = 16$.

@practice mc
prompt: A patient anxious before surgery hyperventilates, and arterial $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ falls from 40 to 25 mmHg while bicarbonate is initially unchanged at 24 mEq/L. Using the bicarbonate buffer equation, what happens to the arterial pH?
choice: It falls, because lowering $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ makes the blood more acidic.
choice*: It rises, because the ratio of bicarbonate to dissolved CO$_2$ increases.
choice: It is unchanged, because bicarbonate is unchanged.
choice: It falls, because the kidney immediately excretes bicarbonate.
hint: Look at what happens to the denominator inside the logarithm.
solution: The denominator $0.03\,P_{\text{CO}_2}$ falls from 1.2 to 0.75, so the ratio rises from 20 to 32 and the log increases, giving a higher pH (respiratory alkalosis). Renal compensation takes hours to days and is not immediate.

@practice ms
prompt: A neuron has resting potential -65 mV. The equilibrium potentials are $E_K = -90$ mV, $E_{Cl} = -70$ mV and $E_{Na} = +60$ mV. Which changes would move the membrane potential in the negative (hyperpolarizing) direction? Select all that apply.
choice*: Increasing the conductance of K$^+$ channels.
choice*: Opening more Cl$^-$ channels.
choice: Increasing the conductance of Na$^+$ channels.
choice: Raising the extracellular K$^+$ concentration.
hint: The membrane potential is pulled toward the equilibrium potential of the ion whose conductance rises.
solution: Raising K$^+$ conductance pulls the potential toward -90 mV, and raising Cl$^-$ conductance pulls it toward -70 mV, both more negative than -65 mV. Increasing Na$^+$ conductance pulls it toward +60 mV, and raising extracellular K$^+$ makes $E_K$ less negative, which depolarizes.

@practice self
prompt: Aspirin (acetylsalicylic acid) is a weak acid with $\text{p}K_a = 3.5$. The stomach lumen has pH about 2 and the small intestine about pH 7. Using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, calculate the ratio of ionized to un-ionized aspirin at each pH, and explain which compartment favors passive membrane crossing of the drug and why absorption still occurs largely in the small intestine.
hint: For a weak acid, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log([\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}])$.
hint: Only the uncharged form crosses lipid membranes readily.
hint: Think about surface area, as well as ionization.
solution: At pH 2, $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 10^{2 - 3.5} = 10^{-1.5} \approx 0.03$, so about 97% is uncharged. At pH 7, the ratio is $10^{3.5} \approx 3000$, so almost all is ionized. The fraction un-ionized therefore favors crossing in the stomach. Absorption nevertheless occurs largely in the small intestine because its villi and microvilli give an enormous surface area and blood flow, and because the small fraction that is un-ionized is replenished continuously as the drug equilibrates, so the very large area outweighs the lower un-ionized fraction.
rubric: Computes the ionized-to-unionized ratios (about 0.03 and about 3000).
rubric: States that the uncharged form crosses membranes, favoring the stomach per unit area.
rubric: Explains intestinal absorption in terms of surface area and continuing equilibration.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient has hemoglobin 15 g/dL, arterial oxygen saturation 98% and arterial $P_{\text{O}_2}$ 100 mmHg. Use $C_{\text{O}_2} = 1.34 \times [\text{Hb}] \times S_{\text{O}_2} + 0.003 \times P_{\text{O}_2}$, with $[\text{Hb}]$ in g/dL and $S_{\text{O}_2}$ as a fraction. What is the oxygen content in mL O$_2$/dL, to one decimal place?
answer: 20.0
check: 1.34 * 15 * 0.98 + 0.003 * 100
tol: 0.1
unit: mL O₂/dL
hint: Compute the hemoglobin-bound term and the dissolved term separately.
hint: The bound term is about 19.7 and the dissolved term is 0.3.
solution: Bound: $1.34 \times 15 \times 0.98 = 19.70$. Dissolved: $0.003 \times 100 = 0.30$. Total $= 20.0$ mL O$_2$/dL, of which 98.5% is on hemoglobin.

@quiz mc
prompt: Acetazolamide inhibits carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, reducing reabsorption of filtered bicarbonate so that more bicarbonate is excreted in the urine. Which change in arterial blood is expected with prolonged use?
choice: Increased bicarbonate and a rise in pH.
choice*: Decreased bicarbonate and a fall in pH (metabolic acidosis).
choice: Decreased $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ with no change in bicarbonate.
choice: No change, since the lungs compensate immediately and completely.
hint: Which component of the bicarbonate buffer is being lost?
solution: Excreting more bicarbonate lowers the numerator of the buffer ratio, so pH falls: a metabolic acidosis. The lungs compensate partially by lowering $P_{\text{CO}_2}$, but not immediately or completely.
