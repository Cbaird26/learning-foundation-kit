@article
By now you have reviewed content and practiced passages. Your score on test day depends on two things you can still train: **how you manage the exam** and **how well you have learned from earlier mistakes**.

### Pacing and flagging

Each section has a fixed time limit (95, 90, 95 and 95 minutes) with a break between some sections, and the clock does not stop for a hard question. Decide your rules in advance:

- **Checkpoints**: for a 59-question, 95-minute section, aim to be at about question 15 at 25 minutes, about question 30 at 50 minutes, and about question 45 at 75 minutes, leaving a margin at the end.
- **Flag and return**: if you do not know how to begin a question within about 30 seconds, make your best guess, flag it and move on. Return if time remains.
- **Answer every question**: there is no penalty for wrong answers. A blind guess among four choices earns, on average, $1/4$ of a question; eliminating even one choice raises it to $1/3$.
- **Passage order**: you may take passages in any order. Many students start with their strongest passages and flag long, dense ones for later, but never skip the entire section tail without guessing.

### Test-day logistics

Sleep well, eat a familiar meal, arrive early with valid identification, and use the breaks to eat, drink and stretch. Do not review notes between sections; you cannot learn anything useful in ten minutes, and stress hurts recall. Expect an on-screen tutorial and a long day. Simulating it with at least two full-length practice exams under the same timing and break schedule reduces surprises.

### The error log

An error log turns a practice set into a diagnosis. For every missed or guessed question, record:

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Source and topic | Practice set 3, enzyme kinetics |
| What I chose and why | Chose noncompetitive because $V_{max}$ looked lower |
| What was correct and why | Competitive: $K_m$ rose, $V_{max}$ unchanged |
| **Cause** | Content gap / reasoning error / misread / timing / careless |
| Fix | Redo 5 inhibitor problems; make a one-page comparison table |

Count the causes. A **content gap** needs review and recall; a **reasoning error** needs more worked examples of that question type; a **misread** needs slower stem reading and underlining qualifiers; **timing** needs pacing drills; a **careless** error (sign, unit, arithmetic) needs a checking habit. Fix the largest category that is cheapest to fix first.

### The repair plan

For a two-week repair plan, choose the two or three largest error causes, give each a specific daily activity (for example, 20 minutes of recall on a weak topic, plus 12 timed questions), and set a re-test: a new timed set at the end of week 1 and again at the end of week 2. If a category does not shrink after a week of focused work, change the method and not just the amount of time.

### Final week

In the last week, reduce volume, keep one or two timed sets, review your error log and formula sheet (not new material), and protect sleep. Confidence comes from having a practiced routine for each section.

@example
title: Reading an error log
problem: A student logs 40 errors from a 120-question mixed set: 14 content gaps, 10 misreads, 8 timing errors, 6 careless errors and 2 reasoning errors. What percentage are content gaps, and which category would you work on first if misreads are cheap to fix?
step: Content gaps: $14/40 = 0.35$, or 35%.
step: Misreads: $10/40 = 25\%$; timing: $20\%$; careless: $15\%$; reasoning: $5\%$.
step: Content is the largest category, but misreads (25%) are cheap to reduce with a stem-reading routine (underline qualifiers, restate the question), so start there while scheduling content review for the highest-yield topics.

@example
title: Value of eliminating choices
problem: You are unsure of 8 questions, each with 4 choices. For half of them you can eliminate one choice. What is the expected number correct from guessing?
step: For the 4 questions with no elimination, each has a $1/4$ chance: $4 \times 1/4 = 1$.
step: For the 4 with one choice eliminated, each has a $1/3$ chance: $4 \times 1/3 = 1.33$.
step: Expected correct $= 1 + 1.33 = 2.33$ questions, better than the 2 you would get by guessing blindly on all 8.

@mistakes
- Logging only the right answer and not why you chose the wrong one, so the thinking error is never found.
- Spending the final week on new content instead of rehearsing routines and reviewing the error log.
- Leaving questions blank when time is short, even though there is no penalty for wrong answers.
- Fixing every error category at once instead of choosing the largest, cheapest one.

@quiz num
prompt: A section has 59 questions in 95 minutes. You plan to spend 18 minutes on the 15 discrete questions and want a 12-minute margin at the end for review. How many minutes per question can you spend on the 44 passage-based questions, to two decimal places?
answer: 1.48
check: (95 - 18 - 12) / 44
tol: 0.01
unit: minutes
hint: Subtract the discrete time and the margin from the total time.
hint: Divide the remaining minutes by 44.
solution: Time left $= 95 - 18 - 12 = 65$ minutes, and $65/44 = 1.48$ minutes per passage question (which includes reading time).
