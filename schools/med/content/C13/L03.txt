@article
Biochemistry is tested in two sections: it is a major part of the Biological and Biochemical section and appears in Chemical and Physical passages. The best way to review it is to organize it around **a few quantities that control everything**: charge, binding and rate, free energy, and regulation.

### Amino acids and proteins

At low pH an amino acid is fully protonated and carries a net positive charge; as pH rises, the carboxyl group loses a proton first (pKa near 2), then an acidic side chain if there is one, then the amino group (pKa near 9 to 10), and finally a basic side chain if there is one (pKa near 10.5 to 12.5). The **isoelectric point** (pI) is the pH at which the net charge is zero. For an amino acid with a nonionizable side chain, the pI is the average of the two backbone pKa values; with an ionizable side chain, average the two pKa values that bracket the neutral form (the two acidic ones for an acidic residue, the two basic ones for a basic residue). Protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (alpha helices and beta sheets, stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds), tertiary (side-chain interactions) and quaternary (subunit arrangement).

### Enzymes

Enzymes lower the activation energy and do not change the equilibrium constant or $\Delta G$ of the reaction. The Michaelis-Menten equation gives the initial velocity:

$$v = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}.$$

$K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which $v = V_{max}/2$; a lower $K_m$ usually means tighter binding. The Lineweaver-Burk plot graphs $1/v$ against $1/[S]$: the y-intercept is $1/V_{max}$ and the x-intercept is $-1/K_m$.

| Inhibitor | Binds | Effect on $V_{max}$ | Effect on $K_m$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competitive | Active site (free enzyme) | Unchanged | Increases |
| Noncompetitive | Site other than active site (E or ES) | Decreases | Unchanged |
| Uncompetitive | ES complex only | Decreases | Decreases |

A competitive inhibitor can be overcome by raising $[S]$; the others cannot.

### Bioenergetics and metabolism

The actual free-energy change depends on concentrations:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ\prime} + RT \ln\frac{[\text{products}]}{[\text{reactants}]},$$

where $R = 8.314\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$. A reaction with a positive $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ can proceed if the product-to-reactant ratio is kept low by the next step.

Glycolysis converts glucose to two pyruvate with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH; **phosphofructokinase-1** is the key control point, inhibited by ATP and citrate and activated by AMP and fructose-2,6-bisphosphate. **Gluconeogenesis** is not a simple reversal: it bypasses three irreversible steps using pyruvate carboxylase, PEP carboxykinase, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase and glucose-6-phosphatase. In the fed state, insulin promotes storage (glycogen synthesis, lipogenesis); in fasting, glucagon and epinephrine promote glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis and beta-oxidation. In humans, even-chain fatty acids cannot make net glucose, because the two carbons of acetyl-CoA are lost as CO$_2$ in the citric acid cycle.

### Reading a passage

First locate what is being measured, then ask which of the four quantities (charge, binding, free energy, regulation) the experiment manipulates.

@example
title: Michaelis-Menten velocity
problem: An enzyme has $V_{max} = 50\ \mu\text{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 4.0$ mM. What is the initial velocity at $[S] = 1.0$ mM?
step: Write the equation: $v = V_{max}[S]/(K_m + [S])$.
step: Substitute: $v = 50(1.0)/(4.0 + 1.0) = 50/5.0$.
step: $v = 10\ \mu\text{mol/min}$, which is 20% of $V_{max}$. This is below half-maximal because $[S] < K_m$.

@example
title: Free energy at cellular concentrations
problem: A reaction A $\to$ B has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = +7.5$ kJ/mol. At 310 K the cell keeps $[B]/[A] = 0.01$. Use $R = 8.314\times10^{-3}$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$. What is $\Delta G$?
step: $RT = (8.314\times10^{-3})(310) = 2.577$ kJ/mol.
step: $\ln(0.01) = -4.605$, so $RT\ln Q = 2.577 \times (-4.605) = -11.87$ kJ/mol.
step: $\Delta G = 7.5 - 11.87 = -4.37$ kJ/mol, so the reaction is spontaneous in the cell although $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ is positive.

@mistakes
- Saying that an enzyme changes the equilibrium constant or $\Delta G$; it changes only the rate at which equilibrium is reached.
- Mixing up the effects of competitive inhibitors (apparent $K_m$ up, $V_{max}$ unchanged) and noncompetitive inhibitors (apparent $V_{max}$ down, $K_m$ unchanged).
- Using $\Delta G^{\circ\prime}$ instead of $\Delta G$ to decide whether a reaction goes in the cell.
- Averaging the wrong pKa values for pI; average the two pKa values on either side of the neutral form.

answer: 90
check: 120 * 6.0 / (2.0 + 6.0)

@practice num
prompt: Glycine has pKa values of 2.34 (carboxyl) and 9.60 (amino) and a nonionizable side chain. What is its isoelectric point, to two decimal places?
answer: 5.97
check: (2.34 + 9.60) / 2
tol: 0.01
hint: The neutral zwitterion lies between the two ionizations.
hint: Average the two pKa values.
solution: $\text{pI} = (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 5.97$.

@practice mc
prompt: In an assay, adding compound X increases the apparent $K_m$ of an enzyme for its substrate but leaves $V_{max}$ unchanged. Raising the substrate concentration restores the activity. Which interpretation is best?
choice*: X is a competitive inhibitor that binds the free enzyme at the active site.
choice: X is a noncompetitive inhibitor that binds the enzyme-substrate complex and the free enzyme equally.
choice: X is an uncompetitive inhibitor that binds only the enzyme-substrate complex.
choice: X is an irreversible inhibitor that permanently removes active enzyme.
hint: Which type of inhibition can be overcome by more substrate?
solution: Higher apparent $K_m$ with unchanged $V_{max}$, reversible by more substrate, is the signature of competitive inhibition. Noncompetitive and uncompetitive inhibitors lower $V_{max}$, and irreversible inactivation also lowers $V_{max}$ because active enzyme is lost.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction has $\Delta G^{\circ\prime} = +10.0$ kJ/mol. At 310 K the product-to-reactant ratio is held at 0.001. Using $R = 8.314\times10^{-3}$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ K$^{-1}$, calculate $\Delta G$ in kJ/mol to one decimal place.
answer: -7.8
check: 10.0 + 8.314e-3 * 310 * math.log(0.001)
tol: 0.1
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Use $\Delta G = \Delta G^{\circ\prime} + RT\ln Q$.
hint: $RT \approx 2.58$ kJ/mol and $\ln(0.001) \approx -6.91$.
solution: $RT\ln Q = (2.577)(-6.908) = -17.8$ kJ/mol, so $\Delta G = 10.0 - 17.8 = -7.8$ kJ/mol.

@practice self
prompt: A passage reports that after an overnight fast, a person's liver releases glucose even though no carbohydrate was eaten, while blood ketone concentrations rise. Explain the hormonal signal and the main pathways responsible for each observation, and say why fatty acids cannot supply the net carbon for the glucose.
hint: Name the hormone that is high in fasting and the hormone that is low.
hint: Separate glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis for glucose, and beta-oxidation for ketones.
hint: Follow what happens to the carbons of acetyl-CoA in the citric acid cycle.
solution: Fasting lowers insulin and raises glucagon (with epinephrine), which activates glycogenolysis and, as glycogen runs out, gluconeogenesis from lactate, glycerol and amino acids, with glucose-6-phosphatase releasing free glucose. Adipose lipolysis raises fatty acids, whose beta-oxidation in the liver produces acetyl-CoA that exceeds the citric acid cycle capacity (oxaloacetate is drawn into gluconeogenesis), so acetyl-CoA is made into ketone bodies. Even-chain fatty acids give only acetyl-CoA, and the two carbons entering the cycle leave as CO$_2$ before oxaloacetate is regenerated, so there is no net conversion to glucose. Glycerol from triglycerides is the exception that can supply glucose.
rubric: States low insulin and high glucagon as the signal.
rubric: Names glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis for glucose release and beta-oxidation for ketone production.
rubric: Explains why acetyl-CoA cannot be converted to net glucose in humans.

@quiz num
prompt: Lysine has pKa values of 2.18 (carboxyl), 8.95 (alpha amino) and 10.53 (side-chain amino). What is its isoelectric point, to two decimal places?
answer: 9.74
check: (8.95 + 10.53) / 2
tol: 0.01
hint: Lysine is a basic amino acid; the neutral form lies between the two basic pKa values.
hint: Average the two highest pKa values.
solution: The neutral form exists between the loss of the alpha-amino proton and the loss of the side-chain proton, so $\text{pI} = (8.95 + 10.53)/2 = 9.74$.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which statements about glycolysis and gluconeogenesis are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: Glycolysis yields a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH per glucose.
choice*: Fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase allows gluconeogenesis to bypass the phosphofructokinase-1 step.
choice*: High ATP and high citrate inhibit phosphofructokinase-1.
choice: Gluconeogenesis uses the same three irreversible kinase reactions of glycolysis, run in reverse.
choice: In humans, even-chain fatty acids give net glucose through acetyl-CoA.
hint: Remember that gluconeogenesis uses different enzymes at the three irreversible steps.
solution: The net yield of glycolysis is 2 ATP and 2 NADH, fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase bypasses phosphofructokinase-1, and high ATP and citrate signal energy sufficiency. The irreversible kinase steps are bypassed with different enzymes, and acetyl-CoA from even-chain fatty acids cannot be converted to net glucose.
