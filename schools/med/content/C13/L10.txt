@article
Many science questions give you a graph, table or diagram you have never seen. The skill being tested is not recall; it is **disciplined reading**. A reliable routine has five steps.

### The five-step routine

1. **Read the title and legend**: what is plotted, in what units, and which groups are shown?
2. **Read the axes**: linear or logarithmic? Does the axis start at zero? Are the units consistent between panels?
3. **Describe the pattern in words** before looking at the answer choices: increasing, plateau, peak, threshold, no change.
4. **Compare to the control**: values mean something only relative to the right baseline.
5. **Match the claim to the evidence**: ask whether the data show that claim, or only something consistent with it.

### Calculations you will need

- **Percent change** $= \dfrac{\text{new} - \text{old}}{\text{old}} \times 100\%$.
- **Fold change** $= \dfrac{\text{treated}}{\text{control}}$. For a western blot, first divide each band by its loading control, then compare.
- **Mean of replicates** $= \dfrac{\sum x_i}{n}$.
- **Linear interpolation** between $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$: $x = x_1 + (y - y_1)\dfrac{x_2 - x_1}{y_2 - y_1}$.
- **Slope** $= \Delta y / \Delta x$; on a graph of a linear transformation the slope or intercept gives a parameter. On a Lineweaver-Burk plot of $1/v$ against $1/[S]$ the y-intercept is $1/V_{max}$, the slope is $K_m/V_{max}$, and the x-intercept is $-1/K_m$.
- **Logarithmic axes**: each gridline step is a constant factor, not a constant difference. On a $\log_{10}$ axis a rise from $10^2$ to $10^4$ is a 100-fold increase.

### Error bars and variability

Error bars may show the standard deviation (spread of individuals), the standard error of the mean (precision of the mean) or a confidence interval. **Always check the legend.** Standard error bars that overlap suggest, but do not prove, no significant difference; a confidence interval for a difference that includes zero indicates no significant difference at that level. A graph with no error bars gives you no idea whether a difference is meaningful.

### Common traps

- **Truncated axes** exaggerate small differences.
- **Correlation in a figure** does not mean causation.
- **Extrapolation** beyond the measured range is a guess: the data say nothing about conditions that were not tested.
- **Unit changes** between figure and text (mg versus $\mu$g, minutes versus hours).
- **Dual-axis graphs** where two curves seem to track each other only because of the chosen scales.

### Handling an unfamiliar figure

Do not worry that you have never seen the technique. The passage explains it in a paragraph, and the question typically asks about the pattern. Find one data point you can verify to be sure you are reading the right axis.

@example
title: Percent change from a table
problem: A table reports mean enzyme activity (units per mg protein): untreated cells 40, cells treated with inhibitor 26. What is the percent change caused by the inhibitor?
step: Change $= 26 - 40 = -14$ units.
step: Divide by the baseline (untreated): $-14/40 = -0.35$.
step: The activity decreased by 35%. Using the treated value as the denominator is a common error; the control is always the baseline.

@example
title: Interpolating a half-maximal response
problem: A dose-response table shows responses of 30 at 2 $\mu$M and 50 at 4 $\mu$M, with a plateau at 80. At about what concentration is the response half of the plateau?
step: Half of the plateau is $80/2 = 40$.
step: The value 40 lies between the points (2, 30) and (4, 50), where the response rises 20 over 2 $\mu$M.
step: Interpolate: $x = 2 + (40 - 30)(4 - 2)/(50 - 30) = 3$ $\mu$M. This is an approximation because the curve is not truly a straight line between points.

@mistakes
- Computing percent change with the wrong baseline.
- Reading a log axis as if the gridlines were evenly spaced in value.
- Forgetting to normalize to a loading control before computing fold change.
- Claiming a conclusion about conditions outside the range of the data.

@practice mc
prompt: A researcher measures enzyme activity (units) at five pH values, all other conditions held constant: pH 5, 12; pH 6, 48; pH 7, 95; pH 8, 60; pH 9, 18. Which conclusion is best supported?
choice*: Among the pH values tested, activity is highest at pH 7, so the optimum lies near pH 7.
choice: The enzyme is irreversibly denatured at pH 9.
choice: Activity doubles for every one unit increase in pH.
choice: The enzyme has no activity at pH 4.
hint: Look for what was actually measured, not what you expect from theory.
solution: The data show a peak at pH 7 among the five values, so the optimum is near 7. Irreversible denaturation was not tested (activity after returning to a neutral pH was not measured), the data are not a constant doubling, and pH 4 was not measured.

answer: 12.2
check: (12.1 + 11.8 + 12.4 + 12.5) / 4

@practice num
prompt: A control group has a mean of 40 units and a treated group has a mean of 52 units. What is the percent increase in the treated group relative to control?
answer: 30
check: (52 - 40) / 40 * 100
tol: 0.5
unit: %
hint: The control is the baseline.
hint: Divide the difference by 40 and multiply by 100.
solution: $(52 - 40)/40 = 0.30$, a 30% increase.

@practice num
prompt: On a Lineweaver-Burk plot, two points on the line are $(1/[S], 1/v) = (0.10, 0.30)$ and $(0.50, 0.70)$, with $[S]$ in mM and $v$ in $\mu$mol/min. What is $V_{max}$ in $\mu$mol/min?
answer: 5
check: 1 / (0.30 - ((0.70 - 0.30) / (0.50 - 0.10)) * 0.10)
tol: 1%
unit: µmol/min
hint: Find the slope of the line, then extend it to $1/[S] = 0$ to find the y-intercept.
hint: The slope is 1.0, so the intercept is $0.30 - 1.0(0.10) = 0.20$. What does it equal?
solution: Slope $= (0.70 - 0.30)/(0.50 - 0.10) = 1.0$. The y-intercept is $0.30 - (1.0)(0.10) = 0.20$, and it equals $1/V_{max}$, so $V_{max} = 1/0.20 = 5\ \mu\text{mol/min}$.

@practice self
prompt: Cells were treated for 24 hours with inhibitor I at several concentrations (n = 3 wells each, means shown). Concentration ($\mu$M): 0, 10, 20, 40. Cell viability (% of untreated): 100, 92, 61, 35. Apoptosis marker (fold change over untreated): 1.0, 1.3, 2.9, 5.2. (a) Describe the relationship between concentration, viability and the marker. (b) Estimate by linear interpolation the concentration giving 50% viability. (c) State one conclusion the data do not support.
hint: Describe the trend of each measurement in words first.
hint: Between 20 and 40 $\mu$M, viability goes from 61 to 35; find where it equals 50.
hint: Consider what controls or extra measurements would be needed to claim a mechanism.
solution: (a) Viability falls as concentration rises, with little change from 0 to 10 $\mu$M and a steep decline from 10 to 40 $\mu$M, while the apoptosis marker rises in parallel from 1.0 to 5.2-fold. (b) Between (20, 61) and (40, 35), $x = 20 + (61 - 50)(20)/(61 - 35) = 20 + 8.5 \approx 28.5\ \mu$M. (c) The data do not show that apoptosis causes the loss of viability, or that the effect would be the same in other cell types, in vivo, or beyond 40 $\mu$M; a marker rising with concentration is consistent with apoptosis but does not prove the mechanism (cell death assays and controls would be needed).
rubric: Describes the dose-dependent decrease in viability and increase in the marker.
rubric: Gets an interpolated value near 28 to 29 $\mu$M.
rubric: Identifies an unsupported claim such as causation or extrapolation beyond tested range.

@quiz num
prompt: A western blot shows band intensities (arbitrary units) for a target protein of 4800 in treated cells and 1200 in control cells. The loading-control protein bands are 1000 in treated cells and 1200 in control cells. What is the fold change in the target protein in treated versus control cells after normalizing to the loading control?
answer: 4.8
check: (4800 / 1000) / (1200 / 1200)
tol: 0.01
hint: Normalize each lane by dividing the target by its own loading control.
hint: The normalized values are 4.8 and 1.0.
solution: Treated normalized $= 4800/1000 = 4.8$; control normalized $= 1200/1200 = 1.0$. Fold change $= 4.8/1.0 = 4.8$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A bar graph shows the mean reaction time (ms) for two groups: 310 for group A and 340 for group B. The y-axis runs from 300 to 350, and the error bars (standard error of the mean, n = 12 per group) overlap. Which statement is best supported?
choice: Group B is more than twice as slow as group A, as the bars suggest.
choice: Group A is faster, with strong evidence, because its bar is lower.
choice*: The visual gap is exaggerated by the truncated axis, and the overlapping error bars suggest the difference may not be reliable.
choice: The error bars show that both groups have a standard deviation of zero.
hint: Check where the y-axis starts and what the overlapping bars imply.
solution: The axis starts at 300, not zero, so the bar heights exaggerate a difference of about 10% (340 versus 310). Overlapping standard error bars suggest the difference may not be statistically reliable, though a formal test would be needed. Error bars indicate variability, not a standard deviation of zero.
