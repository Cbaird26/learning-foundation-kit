@article
The Critical Analysis and Reasoning Skills (CARS) section has 53 questions in 90 minutes, built from about nine passages from the humanities and social sciences. No outside knowledge is required, and outside knowledge can hurt you: **the passage is the only evidence**. The section tests whether you can follow an argument, not whether you have read the topic before.

### Read for structure

For every passage, ask four things as you read, and note them in a few words:

1. **Main idea**: what is the author trying to get you to accept or understand?
2. **Tone**: neutral, critical, admiring, skeptical, qualified?
3. **Structure**: what job does each paragraph do (claim, example, objection, reply, qualification)?
4. **Whose voice**: when a view is described, is it the author's own or someone else's that the author reports or rejects?

Transition words signal the structure: *however*, *yet* and *but* introduce a turn; *for example* introduces support; *thus* introduces a conclusion. Strong language such as *clearly* or *misguided* reveals attitude.

### The three question families

- **Foundations of comprehension**: main idea, detail, definition of a term in context, tone. Answer from the text itself and go back to the lines.
- **Reasoning within the text**: inference, the function of a sentence, the relationship between two ideas, identifying an assumption. The correct answer must follow from what is written.
- **Reasoning beyond the text**: apply a principle to a new case, decide which new evidence would strengthen or weaken the argument, or predict how the author would respond.

### Judging answer choices

- **Scope**: wrong answers often discuss something the passage mentions but not what the question asks.
- **Extremeness**: words like *always*, *never*, *only*, *all* are rarely supported unless the author uses them.
- **Direction**: for strengthen and weaken questions, check which way the choice pushes the conclusion, not just whether it is related.
- **Reverse and half-right traps**: a choice may begin correctly and end by contradicting the passage.

Test each remaining choice against the text: if you cannot point to the support, do not choose it. Eliminate the two worst choices first, then compare the remaining ones for the narrowest defensible claim.

### Timing

A passage with 5 to 7 questions deserves about 9 to 10 minutes. Spend about 3 minutes on a first read that produces a passage map, then answer. If a passage is too dense, work the questions you can answer from the map and return to the rest. Because there is no penalty for guessing, answer every question, and avoid spending more than 2 minutes on one.

@example
title: Mapping a short passage
problem: Read: "Critics call nostalgia a cheap emotion. Yet longing for a vanished street or season is often a form of memory work: it makes us measure what we have against what we valued. Nostalgia turns toxic only when it replaces the measuring with mere mourning." Build a passage map.
step: Main idea: nostalgia is not merely cheap; it can be useful memory work, though it can go wrong.
step: Structure: sentence 1 reports a critics' view; sentence 2 ("Yet") turns against it and gives the author's claim; sentence 3 qualifies the claim with the condition under which nostalgia fails.
step: Tone: measured defense. The author is not an unqualified fan, because of the closing qualification, so a choice that says nostalgia is "always beneficial" is too extreme.

@mistakes
- Importing outside knowledge to justify an answer that the passage does not support.
- Choosing an answer that is true or reasonable but does not answer the question that was asked.
- Missing a qualifier (*some*, *often*, *only when*) and choosing an extreme restatement.
- Spending several minutes on one hard question at the cost of an easy passage later.

@practice mc
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "Many critics treat the footnote as an apology, a place where a scholar admits what the main text could not hold. This view misses what footnotes do. A well-made footnote is a small act of honesty: it shows the reader where an argument leans on someone else's work and invites the reader to check the weight it bears. Without notes, a reader must trust the author's confidence; with them, the reader can test it. Footnotes can be abused, piled up to impress rather than inform. But the abuse of a tool is poor evidence against the tool." Which best states the main idea?
choice: Footnotes are most often used to impress readers and should be reduced.
choice*: Footnotes are valuable because they let readers examine the support for an argument, despite their occasional misuse.
choice: Scholars use footnotes mainly to admit the limitations of their main text.
choice: Readers should trust confident authors who do not use footnotes.
hint: Find the sentence where the author rejects the critics' view and states a positive claim.
solution: The author rejects the view of footnotes as apologies and argues they allow readers to test an argument, conceding only that they can be misused. The impress-readers choice makes the concession the main point, the apology choice is the view the author rejects, and the trust choice reverses the passage.

@practice mc
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "Planners often defend the public park on the grounds that it raises nearby property values, as though the shade of an oak were a line on a ledger. The argument is effective, and that is the trouble with it. A park justified by revenue will be judged by revenue, and when a developer offers more, the park has no answer. The better case rests on something that resists pricing: a place where strangers share space without a transaction." Which best describes the author's attitude toward the property-value argument?
choice: Enthusiastic approval, since it is persuasive to planners.
choice*: Critical, conceding that it works but arguing that it weakens the case for parks.
choice: Indifferent, treating it as one of several equal arguments.
choice: Hostile, dismissing it as dishonest.
hint: Look at the sentence "The argument is effective, and that is the trouble with it."
solution: The author grants that the argument is effective and then explains why this effectiveness is a liability, which is criticism with a concession. The author gives a reasoned objection and does not call the argument dishonest, and prefers a different case, so the attitude is not indifferent.

@practice ms
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "A translator who renders every idiom word for word produces a text that is faithful to the dictionary and foreign to the reader. A translator who replaces every idiom with a local equivalent produces a text that reads well but may carry meanings the original never intended. Most working translators therefore move between the two methods, deciding case by case what a given phrase must preserve." Which statements can be inferred from the passage? Select all that apply.
choice*: Word-for-word translation of idioms can leave a reader with a text that feels unfamiliar.
choice*: Replacing idioms with local equivalents risks adding meanings that were not in the original.
choice: Translators should never translate idioms word for word.
choice: Most translators prefer local equivalents to preserving the original wording.
hint: Check each statement against a specific sentence and reject those with extreme words.
solution: The statements about unfamiliar-feeling word-for-word translation and about added meanings from local equivalents restate the costs the passage describes. The passage says working translators combine the methods, so "never" is too extreme and the claim of a general preference for local equivalents is unsupported.

@practice mc
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "Researchers argue that neighborhoods with many shared community gardens have fewer reported disputes between neighbors because working side by side builds trust." Which new finding would most weaken this argument?
choice: Residents of garden neighborhoods say they enjoy gardening.
choice: Neighborhoods with gardens have more residents who own their homes.
choice*: Neighborhoods that already had few disputes were the ones in which residents chose to build gardens.
choice: Disputes in garden neighborhoods are reported at the same rate as in neighborhoods with no gardens where trust is rated high.
hint: Ask whether the finding could reverse the direction of the claimed cause.
solution: If low-dispute neighborhoods were the ones that built gardens, the causal direction runs from low conflict to gardens, and gardens would not explain the lower number of disputes. Enjoyment is irrelevant, home ownership suggests a confounder only weakly and is not tied to the argument as clearly, and the choice about equal dispute rates where trust is rated high does not compare gardens with trust.

@practice self
prompt: Timed task: allow 9 minutes. Read the passage and write (a) a passage map of at most four short lines (main idea, tone, paragraph or sentence roles) and (b) a one-sentence answer to "Why does the author mention the lighthouse?" Passage: "Museums of technology are often organized as processions of progress, each machine a step toward ours. The arrangement flatters the present. Consider the lighthouse keeper's lamp, which was not a failed attempt at electric light but a finished solution to a different problem: a ship's survival in fog, at night, far from the grid. To see it so is to admit that old tools were not waiting for us to improve them."
hint: Find the author's thesis and the sentence that introduces the counterexample.
hint: Ask what claim the lighthouse supports.
solution: (a) Main idea: arranging old technology as steps toward the present misleads. Tone: critical and reflective. Roles: sentence 1 describes the common practice; sentence 2 states the objection; sentence 3 gives the lighthouse lamp as an example; sentence 4 states the broader conclusion. (b) The lighthouse lamp is an example showing that an old device can be a complete solution to its own problem and not merely an incomplete step toward modern technology.
rubric: The map states the main idea and a critical tone.
rubric: The map assigns roles to the sentences (practice, objection, example, conclusion).
rubric: The answer says the lighthouse is an example supporting the claim that old tools were complete solutions to different problems.

@quiz mc
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "A common complaint holds that the novel's rise made readers solitary, since silent reading replaced shared recitation. But the complaint assumes that solitude and attention are the same thing. A person reading alone may be attending to another mind more closely than someone in a crowded hall is attending to the speaker. What silent reading changed was not whether readers met an author, but who else was present when they did." Which best captures the author's central point?
choice: Silent reading is superior to shared recitation in every respect.
choice: The novel made readers solitary and reduced their attention.
choice*: Silent reading changed the setting of reading but did not make readers less engaged with the author.
choice: Crowded halls produce inattentive audiences.
hint: Find what the author says silent reading did and did not change.
solution: The author concedes that readers are alone more often but denies that this reduces engagement with the author. The superiority claim and the crowded-hall claim are too extreme, and the claim that the novel made readers solitary and less attentive is the complaint being rejected.

@quiz mc
prompt: Read the passage and answer. "A rule that is easy to follow but hard to enforce teaches people that rules are optional; a rule that is hard to follow but easy to enforce teaches people to hide. Good rules are modest in what they demand and clear in how they are checked." Which situation best illustrates the second kind of rule (hard to follow but easy to enforce)?
choice: A speed limit on a remote road that no one ever patrols.
choice*: A requirement that employees submit forty-page weekly reports, checked automatically for length.
choice: A request to recycle that is posted on a bulletin board.
choice: A rule that students should be kind, with no procedure for noticing violations.
hint: Match "hard to follow" with the burden and "easy to enforce" with the checking.
solution: A forty-page weekly report is a heavy burden but a length check is automatic, so it is hard to follow and easy to enforce; this would encourage people to hide shortcuts. The other situations are easy to follow but hard to check, or vague.
