@article
Every science section, and the Psychological and Social section in particular, asks you to act as a reviewer of someone else's study. The skills transfer across fields: **what was manipulated, what was measured, what was controlled, and what can be concluded?**

### Study designs

- **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)**: participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control. Randomization balances confounders, on average, and supports causal inference. Blinding reduces bias in measurement and reporting.
- **Cohort study**: follow exposed and unexposed groups forward in time and compare outcomes (relative risk).
- **Case-control study**: begin with people who have the outcome and matched controls, then look back at exposures (odds ratio). It suits rare diseases.
- **Cross-sectional study**: measures exposure and outcome at one time (prevalence); it cannot show temporal order.

The **independent variable** is manipulated or compared; the **dependent variable** is measured. A **confounder** is related to both exposure and outcome and is not on the causal pathway; it can create or hide an association. Threats to internal validity include selection bias, attrition, history, instrumentation and the placebo effect. Threats to external validity include unrepresentative samples and artificial settings.

### Screening and diagnostic tests

From the 2 by 2 table of results (TP, FP, FN, TN):

$$\text{sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \quad \text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}, \quad \text{PPV} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}.$$

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test; predictive values also depend on prevalence, and PPV falls as prevalence falls.

### Descriptive and inferential statistics

The mean, median and mode summarize the center; standard deviation (SD) describes spread. The standard error of the mean is $SD/\sqrt{n}$. For a large sample, an approximate 95% confidence interval for a mean is

$$\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\frac{SD}{\sqrt{n}}.$$

A **p-value** is the probability of data at least as extreme as those observed if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true, and a small p-value does not guarantee that the effect is large or important. A **Type I error** is rejecting a true null hypothesis (probability $\alpha$); a **Type II error** is failing to reject a false null hypothesis (probability $\beta$), and power is $1 - \beta$. Use a t-test for two means, ANOVA for three or more groups, chi-square for categorical counts, and correlation for the strength of a linear relationship. The correlation coefficient $r$ ranges from -1 to 1; $r^2$ is the fraction of variance in one variable explained by the linear relation with the other. Correlation does not show causation.

### Reading an experiment passage

Identify groups and controls first. A positive control shows the assay can detect an effect; a negative control shows the effect is absent when it should be. Check whether the figure plots means with error bars (what do they represent?), whether a sample size is stated, and whether the conclusion goes beyond the design.

@example
title: Screening test statistics
problem: A test is applied to 200 people. Results: 90 true positives, 10 false negatives, 30 false positives and 70 true negatives. Find the sensitivity, specificity and PPV.
step: Sensitivity $= 90/(90 + 10) = 0.90$.
step: Specificity $= 70/(70 + 30) = 0.70$.
step: $\text{PPV} = 90/(90 + 30) = 0.75$. A positive result is correct in three out of four cases in this sample.

@example
title: 95% confidence interval
problem: A sample of 64 patients has a mean systolic pressure of 132 mmHg and SD of 16 mmHg. Using 1.96 for the 95% critical value, what is the interval?
step: Standard error: $16/\sqrt{64} = 16/8 = 2$ mmHg.
step: Margin of error: $1.96 \times 2 = 3.92$ mmHg.
step: The interval is $132 \pm 3.92$, or about 128.1 to 135.9 mmHg.

@mistakes
- Interpreting a p-value of 0.03 as a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true.
- Concluding causation from a cross-sectional association or a correlation.
- Computing sensitivity with the wrong denominator; use all people who truly have the condition.
- Confusing the SD (spread of individuals) with the standard error (precision of the mean) when reading error bars.

prompt: A test is applied to a group with these results: 45 true positives, 20 false positives, 5 false negatives and 130 true negatives. What is the positive predictive value, to three decimal places?
answer: 0.692
check: 45 / (45 + 20)
tol: 0.002
hint: PPV asks: among positive tests, how many are true?
hint: Divide true positives by all positive tests.
solution: There are $45 + 20 = 65$ positive tests, so $\text{PPV} = 45/65 = 0.692$.

prompt: A cohort study finds that people who drink coffee have a higher rate of lung cancer than people who do not. Researchers later learn that coffee drinkers in the cohort smoke much more. Smoking is best described as:
choice: A mediator on the causal path from coffee to lung cancer.
choice*: A confounder related to both coffee drinking and lung cancer.
choice: A dependent variable, since it was measured.
choice: A placebo effect that biased the measurement of cancer.
hint: Smoking is associated with the exposure and independently causes the outcome.
solution: Smoking is related to coffee drinking and independently causes lung cancer, and it is not an effect of coffee, so it is a confounder. It could create a spurious association between coffee and lung cancer.

@practice num
prompt: A sample of 100 participants has a mean resting heart rate of 70 beats/min and a standard deviation of 15 beats/min. Using 1.96 as the critical value for a 95% confidence interval, what is the margin of error (the half-width), to two decimal places?
answer: 2.94
check: 1.96 * 15 / math.sqrt(100)
tol: 0.01
unit: beats/min
hint: First compute the standard error, $SD/\sqrt{n}$.
hint: The standard error is 1.5; multiply by 1.96.
solution: $SE = 15/\sqrt{100} = 1.5$, and the margin is $1.96 \times 1.5 = 2.94$ beats/min.

@practice self
prompt: Experiment passage. Sixteen mice with implanted tumors were randomly assigned to receive daily injections of Drug X (n = 8) or saline vehicle (n = 8) for 14 days. The technician measuring tumor volume knew which mice received which treatment. Mean final tumor volume was 310 mm$^3$ (SEM 40) with Drug X and 520 mm$^3$ (SEM 55) with vehicle, p = 0.008. The authors conclude that "Drug X cures cancer." Identify the independent and dependent variables and the control, state what the data support, and give two limitations of the design or the conclusion.
hint: The control group receives everything except the active ingredient.
hint: Consider who knew the group assignments and how large the sample is.
hint: Compare "reduced tumor growth in mice" with "cures cancer".
solution: The independent variable is the treatment (Drug X versus vehicle); the dependent variable is final tumor volume; the saline vehicle group is the negative control. Random assignment supports a causal claim that Drug X reduced tumor volume in these mice, and p = 0.008 means a difference this large would be uncommon if there were no true effect. Limitations include an unblinded measurer (measurement bias), a small sample (n = 8), a single model and time point, no dose range, and an overreaching conclusion: reduced tumor size in mice does not show a cure or efficacy in people.
rubric: Names the independent variable, dependent variable and negative control correctly.
rubric: States that the data support reduced tumor volume in this mouse model but not a cure.
rubric: Gives at least two valid limitations such as lack of blinding, small n, or limited generalizability.

@quiz num
prompt: The correlation coefficient between hours of sleep and reaction time score in a sample is $r = -0.60$. What fraction of the variance in reaction time is explained by its linear relationship with sleep?
answer: 0.36
check: (-0.60) ** 2
tol: 0.005
hint: The fraction of variance explained uses the square of the correlation.
hint: Squaring removes the negative sign.
solution: $r^2 = (-0.60)^2 = 0.36$, so 36% of the variance is explained.

@quiz ms
prompt: A trial reports a difference in blood pressure between treatments with p = 0.03. Which statements are correct? Select all that apply.
choice*: If there were truly no difference, data this extreme or more extreme would occur about 3% of the time.
choice*: The p-value alone does not tell you whether the effect size is clinically important.
choice*: A 95% confidence interval for the difference that excludes zero is consistent with this result.
choice: There is a 3% probability that the null hypothesis is true.
choice: There is a 97% probability that the finding will replicate.
hint: A p-value is conditional on the null hypothesis being true.
solution: The p-value is the probability of data at least this extreme if the null hypothesis were true. It says nothing about the importance of the effect, and a result significant at the 0.05 level corresponds to a 95% confidence interval that excludes the null value. It is neither the probability that the null hypothesis is true nor a replication probability.
