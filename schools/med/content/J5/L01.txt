@article
Look at your hand. It seems smooth and solid, but it is built from trillions of tiny units called **cells**. A cell is the smallest unit that can carry out the activities of life: taking in materials, using energy, growing and responding to its surroundings.

### The cell theory

Scientists gathered evidence for more than two centuries before they agreed on three big ideas. Together they are called the **cell theory**:

1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in living things.
3. All cells come from other living cells.

Some organisms, like bacteria and amoebas, are **unicellular** (one cell). Others, like trees, dogs and people, are **multicellular** (many cells that work together).

### Seeing cells

Most cells are far too small to see with your eyes alone. A typical human cell is about 10 to 30 micrometers wide. A **micrometer** (µm) is one thousandth of a millimeter, so

$$1\ \text{mm} = 1000\ \mu\text{m}.$$

A **light microscope** uses lenses and light to magnify. Its main parts are the **eyepiece** (the lens you look through), the **objective lenses** (several lenses on a rotating nosepiece), the **stage** where the slide sits, a **light source**, and the **coarse** and **fine focus knobs**.

The two lenses magnify one after the other, so the powers multiply:

$$\text{total magnification} = \text{eyepiece power} \times \text{objective power}.$$

A $10\times$ eyepiece with a $40\times$ objective gives $400\times$.

### Field of view

The circle you see is the **field of view**. When magnification goes up, the field of view gets smaller in the same proportion. A field 2 mm wide at $100\times$ is only 0.5 mm wide at $400\times$, because the magnification is four times greater. Always find your sample at low power first, then move to higher power.

### Actual size from a drawing

If a cell looks 36 mm long in a drawing made at $400\times$, the real cell is

$$\text{actual size} = \frac{\text{image size}}{\text{magnification}}.$$

### Safe observation

Look at a thin piece of onion skin in a drop of tap water with a hand lens or a school microscope, with a grown-up nearby. Never use a mirror to aim sunlight into a microscope, because it can damage your eyes. Always carry a microscope with two hands.

@example
title: Finding the real size of a cell
problem: A student draws a cheek-skin cell seen at $400\times$. The drawing is 36 mm wide. How wide is the real cell in micrometers?
step: Use $\text{actual size} = \text{image size} \div \text{magnification}$.
step: Divide: $36\ \text{mm} \div 400 = 0.09\ \text{mm}$.
step: Convert to micrometers by multiplying by $1000$: $0.09 \times 1000 = 90\ \mu\text{m}$.
step: Check that the answer is sensible: 90 µm is just under a tenth of a millimeter, which is tiny but near the upper end for a flat skin cell.

@mistakes
- Adding the eyepiece and objective powers instead of multiplying them.
- Forgetting to convert millimeters to micrometers (multiply by $1000$) after dividing.
- Thinking the field of view gets larger at higher power; it gets smaller.

unit: ×

@practice num
prompt: At $40\times$ the field of view is 5 mm across. Switching to $100\times$, how wide is the field of view in millimeters?
answer: 2
check: 5 * 40 / 100
unit: mm
tol: 1%
hint: The field of view shrinks in the same proportion that magnification grows.
hint: Magnification becomes $100 \div 40 = 2.5$ times larger.
solution: The width is $5 \div 2.5 = 2$ mm, which is also $5 \times 40 \div 100$.

@quiz num
prompt: At $50\times$ the field of view of a microscope is 3.6 mm across. What is its width in millimeters at $150\times$?
answer: 1.2
check: 3.6 * 50 / 150
unit: mm
tol: 1%
hint: Find how many times larger the magnification became.
hint: The magnification became $3$ times larger, so the field becomes $3$ times narrower.
solution: $3.6 \div 3 = 1.2$ mm.
