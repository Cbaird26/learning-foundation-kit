@article
Abnormal white counts are either a normal response to something (infection, steroids, stress) or a clonal disease. The skill is separating reactive from neoplastic, then naming the leukemia.

### Reactive changes and cytopenias

Always convert percentages into **absolute counts**: $\text{ANC} = \text{WBC} \times (\text{segmented neutrophils} + \text{bands})\%$. Neutropenia is ANC below 1,500/µL; severe below 500/µL (high risk of bacterial infection). Common reactive patterns: neutrophilia with left shift in bacterial infection; eosinophilia in allergy, parasites, drug reactions and Hodgkin lymphoma; lymphocytosis with atypical (reactive) lymphocytes in infectious mononucleosis (EBV) and other viral infections. A **leukemoid reaction** (WBC above 50,000/µL from infection or inflammation) differs from CML by a high leukocyte alkaline phosphatase score (LAP), toxic granulation, and absent BCR-ABL.

### Acute versus chronic

**Acute leukemias** are arrests at an early stage with at least **20 percent blasts** in marrow or blood (some genetically defined AML types are diagnosed at lower counts: WHO 2022 drops the blast requirement for most of them, whereas ICC 2022 requires at least 10 percent, so classifications differ). Patients present with marrow failure: anemia, bleeding from thrombocytopenia, infections from neutropenia, bone pain. **Chronic leukemias** have mature cells, indolent course and often incidental discovery.

### Acute myeloid leukemia (AML)

Adults, median age in the 60s. Risk factors: prior chemotherapy (alkylating agents, topoisomerase II inhibitors), radiation, benzene, myelodysplastic syndromes, Down syndrome. Myeloblasts are **myeloperoxidase positive**, often with **Auer rods** (needle-like azurophilic granule aggregates), and express CD13, CD33 and CD34 by flow cytometry. Monocytic types cause gum infiltration and leukemia cutis. Treatment: intensive induction ("7+3": cytarabine for 7 days plus an anthracycline for 3 days) in fit patients, targeted drugs for mutations (FLT3, IDH1/2) and venetoclax with azacitidine for older or unfit patients; allogeneic transplantation for higher-risk disease. Prognosis depends on cytogenetics and mutations.

**Acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL, t(15;17), PML-RARA):** heavily granulated promyelocytes with many Auer rods ("faggot cells") and **DIC** with severe bleeding. It is a medical emergency: start **all-trans retinoic acid (ATRA)** as soon as suspected (before confirmation), plus arsenic trioxide; ATRA causes differentiation (differentiation syndrome: fever, dyspnea, edema; treat with dexamethasone). With this treatment, APL has become highly curable.

### Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL)

The most common childhood cancer, with a peak at 2 to 5 years and a second smaller peak in adults. Lymphoblasts are **TdT positive** with B-cell (CD10, CD19) or T-cell markers. T-ALL often presents as a mediastinal mass in adolescents. Features: bone pain, hepatosplenomegaly, lymphadenopathy, CNS and testicular involvement. Good prognostic features: age 2 to 10, hyperdiploidy, t(12;21). Poor: t(9;22) (Philadelphia chromosome), infant and adult age, very high WBC count. Treatment is multi-agent chemotherapy in phases (induction, consolidation, maintenance of about 2 years) with CNS prophylaxis (intrathecal methotrexate); add a tyrosine kinase inhibitor for Philadelphia-positive disease; blinatumomab and CAR-T cells for relapse.

### Chronic myeloid leukemia (CML)

Adults 40s to 60s, with marked leukocytosis with the full spectrum of granulocyte maturation, **basophilia**, splenomegaly, **low LAP score**. The **Philadelphia chromosome, t(9;22), creates the BCR-ABL fusion**, a constitutively active tyrosine kinase. Phases: chronic, accelerated, blast crisis (myeloid or lymphoid). Treatment is a tyrosine kinase inhibitor: **imatinib** (first generation), dasatinib, nilotinib, bosutinib. Monitor BCR-ABL transcripts by PCR.

### Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL)

The most common adult leukemia in Western countries, typically older than 60, often found incidentally as sustained lymphocytosis (clonal B cells above 5,000/µL) with **smudge cells** on smear. Flow cytometry: **CD5+, CD19+, CD20 (dim), CD23+**. Complications: infections (hypogammaglobulinemia), autoimmune hemolytic anemia and immune thrombocytopenia, and Richter transformation to diffuse large B-cell lymphoma. Early disease is observed; treat for symptoms or progressive marrow failure with BTK inhibitors (ibrutinib, acalabrutinib) or venetoclax-based regimens. **Hairy cell leukemia** (older men, pancytopenia, splenomegaly, dry tap, BRAF V600E, TRAP positive) responds to cladribine.

### Myeloproliferative neoplasms and MDS

| Disease | Features | Key facts |
|---|---|---|
| Polycythemia vera | High hemoglobin, pruritus after bathing, thrombosis, splenomegaly | JAK2 V617F in nearly all; low EPO; phlebotomy to hematocrit below 45 percent plus aspirin |
| Essential thrombocythemia | Persistently high platelet count, thrombosis and bleeding, erythromelalgia | JAK2, CALR or MPL; aspirin, hydroxyurea in high-risk |
| Primary myelofibrosis | Massive splenomegaly, teardrop cells, leukoerythroblastic smear, dry tap | Marrow fibrosis; ruxolitinib |
| Myelodysplastic syndromes | Cytopenias with dysplasia, often macrocytosis | Risk of AML; del(5q) responds to lenalidomide |

CML, PV, ET and myelofibrosis can all transform to acute leukemia. Differentiate secondary causes of polycythemia (hypoxia, EPO-producing tumors) by EPO level: **low EPO with high hemoglobin suggests PV**.

@example
title: Distinguishing leukemias by features
problem: A 67-year-old man has WBC 48,000/µL, 82 percent small mature lymphocytes with smudge cells, Hb 12.8 g/dL and platelets 160,000/µL. He feels well. What is the diagnosis and what should you do?
step: Mature small lymphocytes with smudge cells in an older adult point to CLL rather than an acute leukemia (no blasts).
step: Confirm with flow cytometry for CD5, CD19, CD20 dim and CD23 coexpression.
step: Compute the absolute lymphocyte count: $48000 \times 0.82 = 39360$/µL, well above 5,000/µL.
step: With normal hemoglobin and platelets and no symptoms, treatment is not needed; observe, with periodic counts, and watch for autoimmune cytopenias, infection or progression.

@mistakes
- Treating a high WBC count in an asymptomatic patient with CLL: early-stage disease is observed.
- Waiting for confirmation before starting ATRA in a patient with suspected APL and bleeding.
- Calling a patient with many mature lymphocytes and smudge cells acute lymphoblastic leukemia.
- Using the raw percentage of neutrophils instead of the absolute count to judge neutropenia.

@practice self
prompt: Compare a leukemoid reaction with CML in a patient with WBC 55,000/µL: list the smear features, LAP score, genetic test and clinical clues that separate them, and name the single confirmatory test.
hint: Think about maturation, basophils, LAP, splenomegaly, and the fusion gene.
solution: A leukemoid reaction occurs in infection or inflammation: neutrophilia with left shift, toxic granulation and Dohle bodies, high LAP score, no basophilia, an identifiable trigger. CML shows the full range of granulocyte maturation with basophilia and eosinophilia, low LAP score, splenomegaly and a slow onset. The confirmatory test is detection of BCR-ABL (t(9;22)) by PCR or FISH.
rubric: Gives toxic granulation and high LAP for leukemoid reaction.
rubric: Gives basophilia, splenomegaly and low LAP for CML.
rubric: Names BCR-ABL detection (PCR or FISH) as confirmatory.
rubric: Mentions an inflammatory or infectious trigger in leukemoid reaction.
