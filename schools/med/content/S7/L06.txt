@article
Thrombosis is clotting in the wrong place. Venous clots are fibrin- and red-cell-rich ("red thrombi") and cause deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE); arterial clots are platelet-rich ("white thrombi") and cause myocardial infarction and stroke. This lesson concentrates on venous thromboembolism (VTE) and the drugs that prevent and treat it.

### Virchow triad

Thrombosis follows **stasis** (immobility, long travel, heart failure, atrial fibrillation), **endothelial injury** (surgery, trauma, catheters) and **hypercoagulability**. Acquired hypercoagulable states include malignancy, pregnancy, estrogen therapy (oral contraceptives), nephrotic syndrome (urinary loss of antithrombin), PNH and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

### Inherited and acquired thrombophilias

- **Factor V Leiden** is the most common inherited thrombophilia (about 5 percent of people of European ancestry): a mutation makes factor V resistant to cleavage by **activated protein C** (APC resistance).
- **Prothrombin G20210A** raises prothrombin levels.
- **Protein C or S deficiency** (vitamin K-dependent natural anticoagulants): initiating warfarin without heparin can cause **warfarin-induced skin necrosis**, because protein C has the shortest half-life and falls first.
- **Antithrombin deficiency** causes heparin resistance.
- **Antiphospholipid syndrome (APS):** antibodies (lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I) cause venous and arterial thrombosis, recurrent pregnancy loss and thrombocytopenia. The lupus anticoagulant prolongs the aPTT in vitro without correcting on mixing, yet the patient clots. Long-term anticoagulation is with **warfarin** (DOACs are less effective in high-risk "triple-positive" APS).

### Diagnosis of VTE

Assess pretest probability (Wells score). In low or intermediate probability, a **D-dimer** is useful because a negative result excludes VTE (high sensitivity, low specificity: elevated in surgery, pregnancy, cancer, infection). Otherwise, compression ultrasonography for DVT and CT pulmonary angiography for PE (V/Q scan if contrast is contraindicated). Massive PE with shock is treated with systemic thrombolysis (alteplase) or catheter or surgical embolectomy.

### Anticoagulant drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Monitoring | Key hazard | Reversal |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Unfractionated heparin (UFH) | Binds antithrombin, inhibiting thrombin and Xa | aPTT (or anti-Xa) | Bleeding, HIT | Protamine |
| Enoxaparin (LMWH) | Antithrombin, mainly anti-Xa | Usually none (anti-Xa in renal failure, pregnancy, obesity) | Bleeding, less HIT, renal accumulation | Protamine (partial) |
| Fondaparinux | Indirect Xa inhibitor via antithrombin | None | Renal accumulation | None specific |
| Warfarin | Inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKORC1): II, VII, IX, X, proteins C and S | PT/INR (target 2 to 3 for most indications) | Bleeding, teratogenicity, skin necrosis | Vitamin K, 4-factor PCC, plasma |
| Apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban | Direct factor Xa inhibitors | None routine (PT/aPTT unreliable) | Bleeding | 4-factor PCC (andexanet alfa was withdrawn in the U.S. in December 2025) |
| Dabigatran | Direct thrombin inhibitor | None routine (thrombin time sensitive) | Bleeding | Idarucizumab |

**Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT):** antibodies against platelet factor 4-heparin complexes activate platelets 5 to 10 days after exposure (sooner if prior exposure). The platelet count falls by 30 to 50 percent or more, paradoxically with **thrombosis** rather than bleeding. Use the 4Ts score (thrombocytopenia, timing, thrombosis, other causes) and confirm with PF4 antibody ELISA and a functional assay. **Stop all heparin** (including flushes) and start a non-heparin anticoagulant (argatroban, bivalirudin, fondaparinux or a DOAC); avoid platelet transfusion and delay warfarin until platelets recover.

**Warfarin** starts with overlapping parenteral anticoagulation for several days because factor II has a long half-life and early protein C depletion is prothrombotic. Many drugs change the INR: metronidazole, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, fluconazole and amiodarone raise it; rifampin and carbamazepine lower it. It is contraindicated in pregnancy (embryopathy). Warfarin is preferred over DOACs for mechanical heart valves and antiphospholipid syndrome.

**DOACs** are first-line for most VTE and non-valvular atrial fibrillation: predictable dosing, few interactions, no INR monitoring. Doses depend on renal function, age and weight.

### Treatment duration and stroke risk

VTE provoked by a major transient risk factor is treated for 3 months; unprovoked VTE is treated at least 3 months, then extension is considered by balancing recurrence and bleeding; cancer-associated VTE is treated while the cancer is active. For atrial fibrillation, the **CHA2DS2-VASc** score guides anticoagulation: congestive heart failure 1, hypertension 1, age 75 or older 2, diabetes 1, prior stroke or TIA 2, vascular disease 1, age 65 to 74 1, female sex 1.

### Which test for which drug?

Heparin: aPTT. Warfarin: INR. DOAC: no routine test. A new bleeding patient on any of these: stop the drug, give the specific reversal and consider PCC for life-threatening bleeding.

@example
title: Calculating a heparin infusion rate
problem: A 70 kg adult with a PE is started on unfractionated heparin at 18 units/kg/hour (study example; verify against current institutional protocols). The bag contains 25,000 units in 250 mL. What infusion rate in mL/h is needed?
step: Calculate the hourly dose: $18 \times 70 = 1260$ units/h.
step: Calculate the bag concentration: $25000/250 = 100$ units/mL.
step: Divide dose by concentration: $1260/100 = 12.6$ mL/h.
step: Monitor the aPTT (or anti-Xa) at 6 hours and adjust the rate per protocol; check platelets for HIT.

@mistakes
- Using INR to monitor heparin or a DOAC; INR is for warfarin.
- Continuing heparin when platelets fall 5 to 10 days after starting, or giving platelets for HIT.
- Starting warfarin alone in acute VTE without a parenteral overlap.
- Giving a DOAC to a patient with a mechanical valve or triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome.
