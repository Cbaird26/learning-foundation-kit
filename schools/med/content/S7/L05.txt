@article
Hemostasis stops bleeding without clotting the whole circulation. Think in three layers: **primary hemostasis** (platelet plug), **secondary hemostasis** (fibrin formation by coagulation factors) and **fibrinolysis** (plasmin removes the clot). The skill of this lesson is to interpret the PT, aPTT and platelet count as a pattern.

### Primary hemostasis

Endothelial injury exposes collagen. **von Willebrand factor (vWF)** bridges collagen to platelet **GPIb**; platelets activate, release ADP and thromboxane A2, and aggregate via **GPIIb/IIIa** bound to fibrinogen. Disorders cause **mucocutaneous bleeding**: epistaxis, gum bleeding, menorrhagia, petechiae, easy bruising, and immediate bleeding after cuts or dental work. Platelet counts below about 50,000/µL raise bleeding risk with procedures, and below 10,000 to 20,000/µL carry risk of spontaneous bleeding.

### Secondary hemostasis

The coagulation cascade ends in thrombin converting fibrinogen to fibrin, and factor XIIIa cross-links it. For testing:

- **PT** measures the **extrinsic** (factor VII) and common pathways (X, V, II, fibrinogen). It is reported as the **INR**, which corrects for reagent sensitivity:

$$\text{INR} = \left(\frac{\text{patient PT}}{\text{mean normal PT}}\right)^{\text{ISI}},$$

where ISI is the reagent's International Sensitivity Index. The INR monitors warfarin.
- **aPTT** measures the **intrinsic** (XII, XI, IX, VIII) and common pathways. It monitors unfractionated heparin.
- **Factors II, VII, IX, X** (and proteins C and S) require **vitamin K** for gamma-carboxylation. Factor VII has the shortest half-life (about 6 hours), so the **PT prolongs first** with vitamin K deficiency or warfarin.
- Disorders of secondary hemostasis cause **deep bleeding**: hemarthroses, muscle hematomas, and delayed bleeding after surgery.

### The mixing study

When PT or aPTT is prolonged, mix patient plasma 1:1 with normal plasma. **Correction** means a factor deficiency (normal plasma supplies the missing factor). **Failure to correct** means an inhibitor: a lupus anticoagulant (which paradoxically promotes thrombosis), a specific factor inhibitor such as acquired factor VIII antibodies, or heparin.

### Pattern interpretation

| PT | aPTT | Platelets | Think of |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prolonged | Normal | Normal | Factor VII deficiency, early warfarin or vitamin K deficiency, early liver disease |
| Normal | Prolonged | Normal | Hemophilia A or B, factor XI deficiency, von Willebrand disease (low VIII), heparin, lupus anticoagulant |
| Prolonged | Prolonged | Normal | Common pathway (X, V, II, fibrinogen), warfarin overdose, severe liver disease, vitamin K deficiency |
| Prolonged | Prolonged | Low | DIC, severe liver disease with hypersplenism |
| Normal | Normal | Low | ITP, TTP/HUS, HIT, marrow failure, hypersplenism |
| Normal | Normal | Normal | Platelet dysfunction (aspirin, uremia, vWD), factor XIII deficiency, vascular disorders |

### Key disorders

**Hemophilia A (factor VIII) and B (factor IX):** X-linked recessive, males, hemarthroses and deep hematomas, prolonged aPTT that corrects on mixing, normal PT, platelet count and bleeding time. Treatment is factor replacement (recombinant factor; emicizumab, a bispecific antibody mimicking factor VIII, as prophylaxis in hemophilia A). Mild hemophilia A may respond to desmopressin.

**von Willebrand disease:** the most common inherited bleeding disorder. Type 1 (partial quantitative deficiency, autosomal dominant) is most common. Because vWF carries factor VIII, the aPTT may be mildly prolonged. Diagnose with vWF antigen and **ristocetin cofactor (activity)**. Treat with desmopressin (releases stored vWF) or vWF concentrate.

**Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP):** autoantibodies to platelet glycoproteins cause isolated thrombocytopenia in an otherwise well patient; peripheral smear shows large platelets. Treat if bleeding or platelets below about 30,000/µL: glucocorticoids, IVIG (rapid), thrombopoietin receptor agonists (romiplostim, eltrombopag) or rituximab; splenectomy later.

**Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP):** severe ADAMTS13 deficiency (autoantibody) leaves ultralarge vWF multimers; microthrombi cause thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolysis, fever, renal dysfunction and neurologic signs, with **normal PT and aPTT**. Treat urgently with **plasma exchange** plus glucocorticoids, rituximab and caplacizumab; **platelet transfusion is avoided** unless life-threatening bleeding. **Hemolytic uremic syndrome** follows Shiga-toxin-producing *E. coli* (children, bloody diarrhea) and is managed supportively without antibiotics.

**DIC:** a consumptive state triggered by sepsis, trauma, obstetric catastrophes, malignancy or promyelocytic leukemia: thrombocytopenia, prolonged PT and aPTT, **low fibrinogen**, **high D-dimer** and schistocytes. Treat the cause and replace components for bleeding.

**Acquired factor deficiency:** vitamin K deficiency (antibiotics, cholestasis, malabsorption; newborns receive prophylactic vitamin K) and liver disease (factor VII falls first; factor VIII is preserved as it is also made by endothelium).

@example
title: Reading a coagulation panel
problem: A 7-year-old boy has a swollen, painful knee after minor trauma. Platelets are 280,000/µL, PT is 12 seconds (normal), aPTT is 68 seconds (normal 25 to 35) and corrects fully on 1:1 mixing. What is the likely diagnosis and next test?
step: Joint bleeding is a deep bleed, pointing to a secondary hemostasis (factor) disorder rather than a platelet disorder.
step: Normal platelets and PT with a prolonged aPTT localize the defect to the intrinsic pathway: factors VIII, IX, XI or XII.
step: Correction on mixing means a factor deficiency rather than an inhibitor.
step: In a boy, X-linked hemophilia A or B is most likely. The next test is specific factor VIII and IX assays (factor VIII is deficient in hemophilia A, which is more common).

@mistakes
- Assuming a prolonged aPTT always means bleeding risk: lupus anticoagulant and factor XII deficiency prolong the aPTT without bleeding.
- Transfusing platelets for TTP, which can worsen microvascular thrombosis.
- Using the PT alone to judge liver synthetic function in a vitamin K-deficient patient: give vitamin K and see whether the PT corrects.
- Forgetting that von Willebrand disease can mimic hemophilia A with a prolonged aPTT, but presents with mucocutaneous bleeding in both sexes.

@practice self
prompt: Interpret each panel and name the next test. Panel A: platelets 8,000/µL, PT and aPTT normal, hemoglobin normal, otherwise well adult. Panel B: platelets 250,000/µL, PT 11 seconds, aPTT 70 seconds, does not correct on mixing, no bleeding, history of two miscarriages. Panel C: platelets 90,000/µL, PT 22 seconds, aPTT 55 seconds, fibrinogen 80 mg/dL, D-dimer markedly high, septic patient.
hint: Classify each panel by pattern: isolated thrombocytopenia, isolated prolonged aPTT with an inhibitor, or consumption with both tests prolonged.
solution: Panel A: isolated severe thrombocytopenia, most consistent with ITP after excluding pseudothrombocytopenia (check a smear, and exclude HIV, hepatitis C, drugs); treat if bleeding or very low. Panel B: prolonged aPTT that fails to correct is an inhibitor; with miscarriages, a lupus anticoagulant (antiphospholipid syndrome), confirmed with dilute Russell viper venom time and anticardiolipin and anti-beta-2 glycoprotein I antibodies. Panel C: DIC from sepsis; treat the cause, support with plasma, cryoprecipitate and platelets if bleeding.
rubric: Panel A is isolated thrombocytopenia (ITP) with a smear to exclude artifact.
rubric: Panel B identifies an inhibitor (lupus anticoagulant) and names confirmatory antiphospholipid testing.
rubric: Panel C identifies DIC from the low fibrinogen, high D-dimer and prolonged PT and aPTT.
rubric: Each panel gets a sensible next step, not just a label.
