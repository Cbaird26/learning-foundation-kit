@article
Oncologic emergencies arise from the tumor mass, tumor products or treatment effects. Speed matters: the outcome is often set by the first hours. This block's deliverable is to recognize **tumor lysis syndrome** and **spinal cord compression**.

### Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS)

Rapid death of tumor cells releases intracellular contents. **Potassium** causes hyperkalemia and arrhythmias; **phosphate** binds calcium (causing **hypocalcemia**, tetany, seizures, and calcium-phosphate precipitation in the kidney); nucleic acids are catabolized to **uric acid** (hyperuricemia, urate crystal nephropathy). The result is **acute kidney injury**. TLS usually appears 12 to 72 hours after starting chemotherapy (sometimes spontaneously, or after venetoclax or rituximab).

The Cairo-Bishop definition: **laboratory TLS** is two or more of uric acid 8 mg/dL or more, potassium 6 mEq/L or more, phosphate 4.5 mg/dL or more, and calcium 7 mg/dL or less (or a 25 percent change from baseline) within 3 days before to 7 days after therapy; **clinical TLS** adds creatinine elevation, arrhythmia or seizure.

**High risk:** Burkitt lymphoma, ALL, high-grade lymphomas, AML with a high WBC count, bulky disease, elevated LDH and preexisting renal impairment.

**Prevention:** vigorous IV hydration (about 2 to 3 L/m$^2$/day with urine output goals), **allopurinol** (xanthine oxidase inhibitor that prevents new uric acid formation) for low and intermediate risk, and **rasburicase** (recombinant urate oxidase that converts uric acid to soluble allantoin) for high risk or established hyperuricemia. **Screen for G6PD deficiency before rasburicase**, because it generates hydrogen peroxide and causes hemolysis and methemoglobinemia. Alkalinization is not recommended because it promotes calcium-phosphate precipitation. **Treatment:** monitor labs every 4 to 6 hours with cardiac monitoring; treat hyperkalemia (calcium gluconate for ECG changes, insulin with glucose, albuterol), avoid treating asymptomatic hypocalcemia (precipitation risk), and use dialysis for refractory hyperkalemia, volume overload, hyperphosphatemia or oliguria.

### Malignant spinal cord compression

Epidural metastasis or collapse of a vertebral body compresses the cord or cauda equina. Common primaries: **prostate, breast, lung** and myeloma, lymphoma and kidney cancer; the thoracic spine is most often involved. The first symptom is **back pain** (worse lying down, with percussion tenderness), followed by leg weakness, sensory level, and late bladder or bowel dysfunction. Lesions above L1 to L2 give upper motor neuron signs (spasticity, hyperreflexia, upgoing toes); cauda equina compression gives lower motor neuron signs, saddle anesthesia and areflexia. Pretreatment neurologic function is the strongest predictor of outcome, so treat while ambulatory.

**Management:** suspect it in any patient with cancer and new back pain plus neurologic findings; give **high-dose dexamethasone** immediately; obtain **emergent MRI of the entire spine** (skip-lesions are common); then definitive treatment with **radiation** (radiosensitive tumors) or **surgical decompression** (single-level compression, instability, radioresistant tumors, or good prognosis) followed by radiation.

### Febrile neutropenia

Fever of 38.3 C once (or 38.0 C sustained for an hour) with ANC below 500/µL (or expected to fall below 500 within 48 hours). It is a medical emergency, since signs of infection are muted without neutrophils. Obtain cultures (including from central lines), then give an **empiric anti-pseudomonal beta-lactam** (cefepime, piperacillin-tazobactam or a carbapenem) **within 60 minutes**. Add vancomycin only for specific indications (hemodynamic instability, catheter infection, skin or soft tissue infection, pneumonia, known MRSA). If fever persists for 4 to 7 days, evaluate for fungal infection and add antifungal therapy. Low-risk patients (MASCC score) may be treated as outpatients with oral antibiotics.

### Hypercalcemia of malignancy

The most common paraneoplastic syndrome. Causes: **PTHrP** from solid tumors (squamous lung, head and neck, renal, breast), osteolytic bone metastases and myeloma, and **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D** from lymphoma. PTH is suppressed. Symptoms include polyuria, volume depletion, constipation, confusion and arrhythmias. Calculate **corrected calcium** $= \text{Ca} + 0.8(4 - \text{albumin})$. Treatment: **IV isotonic saline** first, then **zoledronic acid** (effect in 2 to 4 days) or denosumab (for renal impairment or refractory disease); **calcitonin** acts within hours but wears off; glucocorticoids help lymphoma (vitamin D mediated); loop diuretics only after volume repletion if overloaded; dialysis for severe cases.

### SVC syndrome and leukostasis

**SVC syndrome:** obstruction by mass or thrombus produces facial swelling, plethora, dilated chest wall veins, dyspnea and a positive Pemberton sign (facial congestion on raising the arms). Causes: lung cancer (small cell), lymphoma, thymoma, and central venous catheters. Obtain CT with contrast, raise the head, give radiation or chemotherapy based on histology (chemotherapy for small cell lung cancer and lymphoma), and consider a stent for severe symptoms. **Leukostasis:** WBC above 100,000/µL (usually AML), with hypoxia and neurologic symptoms from blast sludging; treat with hydroxyurea, leukapheresis and prompt induction chemotherapy, and avoid red-cell transfusion that raises viscosity unless necessary.

### Supportive and palliative care

Prophylactic antiemetics, G-CSF when febrile neutropenia risk is high, transfusion support, mucositis care and vaccination matter. Palliative care is not limited to the end of life: pain control (opioids with a bowel regimen), symptom management and goals-of-care discussions improve quality of life and sometimes survival when started with active treatment.

@example
title: Recognizing TLS
problem: A 9-year-old boy with Burkitt lymphoma received his first chemotherapy 36 hours ago. Now: K 6.8 mEq/L, phosphate 7.5 mg/dL, calcium 6.7 mg/dL, uric acid 13 mg/dL, creatinine rising from 0.6 to 1.4 mg/dL, urine output falling. What is the diagnosis and the immediate plan?
step: Hyperkalemia, hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia and hyperuricemia after treatment of a high-risk tumor, with acute kidney injury, constitute clinical tumor lysis syndrome.
step: Start an ECG and cardiac monitoring; treat hyperkalemia (IV calcium if there are ECG changes, insulin with glucose, albuterol).
step: Give aggressive IV fluids and rasburicase for the uric acid (after a G6PD result or a rapid G6PD screen), and avoid giving calcium for asymptomatic hypocalcemia.
step: Consult nephrology early: refractory hyperkalemia, oliguria or severe hyperphosphatemia are indications for dialysis. Recheck labs every 4 to 6 hours.

@mistakes
- Obtaining an MRI before giving dexamethasone to a patient with suspected cord compression, or imaging only the symptomatic level.
- Delaying antibiotics in febrile neutropenia to await culture results or the ANC.
- Giving rasburicase without checking G6PD, or alkalinizing urine in TLS.
- Starting zoledronic acid before volume resuscitation in hypercalcemia of malignancy.
