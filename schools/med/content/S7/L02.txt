@article
Microcytosis (MCV below 80 fL) means red cells are made with too little hemoglobin, so they divide an extra time and come out small. The mnemonic **TAILS** lists the causes: **T**halassemia, **A**nemia of chronic disease (inflammation), **I**ron deficiency, **L**ead poisoning, **S**ideroblastic anemia. The deliverable of this block is to tell the first three apart.

### Iron physiology

Dietary iron is absorbed in the duodenum and proximal jejunum, as heme iron (from meat, absorbed efficiently) or non-heme ferrous iron (reduced from ferric by duodenal cytochrome B; vitamin C helps, phytates and tea hinder). **Ferroportin** exports iron from enterocytes and macrophages into plasma, where **transferrin** carries it. Stores are held as **ferritin** (and hemosiderin). The body has no regulated excretion of iron, only loss through bleeding and shedding of cells, so balance is set at absorption by **hepcidin**, a liver peptide that degrades ferroportin. Iron deficiency suppresses hepcidin; inflammation (IL-6) raises it.

### Iron deficiency anemia (IDA)

Causes: chronic blood loss (menstruation, gastrointestinal bleeding), increased need (pregnancy, growth), poor intake, malabsorption (celiac disease, gastrectomy, H. pylori gastritis). In **men and postmenopausal women, IDA is gastrointestinal cancer until proven otherwise**, so endoscopic evaluation (colonoscopy and upper endoscopy) is the next step. Findings: fatigue, pallor, pica (ice, pagophagia), koilonychia, angular cheilitis; rarely dysphagia with an esophageal web (Plummer-Vinson syndrome).

Labs: **low ferritin** (below about 30 ng/mL is highly specific; ferritin is an acute-phase reactant, so a higher cutoff such as 100 may be used in inflammation), **low serum iron, high TIBC** (transferrin rises), **low transferrin saturation**, **high RDW** and often a high platelet count. Smear shows hypochromic microcytes and pencil cells.

$$\text{Transferrin saturation} = \frac{\text{serum iron}}{\text{TIBC}} \times 100.$$

Treat the cause plus iron replacement. Oral ferrous salts (once-daily or alternate-day dosing; the latter often absorbs better with fewer GI effects) raise the reticulocyte count in about a week and hemoglobin in 2 to 3 weeks; continue for months to refill stores. Intravenous iron is used with intolerance, malabsorption, ongoing losses or CKD.

### Anemia of chronic disease (inflammation)

IL-6 raises hepcidin, which traps iron in macrophages and blocks absorption: iron is plentiful but unavailable (functional deficiency). Labs: **normal or high ferritin, low serum iron, low TIBC**, low transferrin saturation. The anemia is usually normocytic and may become mildly microcytic. Treat the underlying disease; in CKD, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents help.

### Thalassemias

Thalassemias are quantitative globin-chain defects causing imbalanced chains, ineffective erythropoiesis and hemolysis. Iron stores are normal or high, so **do not give iron** without proof of deficiency.

- **Alpha thalassemia**: gene deletions on chromosome 16 (four alpha genes). One deletion is silent; two (trait) gives mild microcytosis with a normal-appearing electrophoresis; three is **HbH disease** (beta chains form $\beta_4$ tetramers) with moderate hemolytic anemia; four is **hemoglobin Barts** ($\gamma_4$), hydrops fetalis and death in utero or at birth. Two-gene deletion in cis (--/alpha alpha) is common in Southeast Asian populations and carries risk of Barts or HbH in offspring.
- **Beta thalassemia**: mutations of the beta gene on chromosome 11. **Minor (trait)**: mild microcytic anemia, elevated **HbA2** ($\alpha_2\delta_2$) above 3.5 percent on electrophoresis. **Major**: severe transfusion-dependent anemia presenting after 6 months as HbF falls, with extramedullary hematopoiesis, frontal bossing and "crew-cut" skull, hepatosplenomegaly and iron overload; treated with transfusion, chelation (deferasirox), and potentially stem cell transplantation or gene therapy.

The **Mentzer index** is MCV divided by RBC count (millions per microliter). A value **below 13** favors thalassemia trait (high RBC count despite small cells) and above 13 favors IDA. It is a screening heuristic, not a diagnosis.

### Other microcytoses

**Sideroblastic anemia**: impaired heme synthesis (ALAS2 requires pyridoxine, vitamin B6) leaves iron trapped in mitochondria around the nucleus, seen as **ringed sideroblasts** with Prussian blue stain. Causes: X-linked ALAS2 mutations, isoniazid (B6 antagonism), alcohol, copper deficiency, myelodysplastic syndromes. Iron is high. **Lead** inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, giving basophilic stippling, abdominal pain, wrist drop and, in children, developmental effects; confirm with blood lead level and chelate if indicated.

@example
title: Three microcytic patients
problem: Patient A: MCV 68 fL, RBC $3.9 \times 10^6/\mu$L, RDW 18 percent, ferritin 8 ng/mL, TIBC high. Patient B: MCV 62 fL, RBC $5.8 \times 10^6/\mu$L, RDW 14 percent, ferritin 120 ng/mL, HbA2 5.5 percent. Patient C: MCV 78 fL, RDW 15 percent, ferritin 300 ng/mL, TIBC low, active rheumatoid arthritis. Name each diagnosis.
step: Compute the Mentzer index. Patient A: $68/3.9 \approx 17.4$, above 13. Patient B: $62/5.8 \approx 10.7$, below 13.
step: Patient A has a high RDW, very low ferritin and high TIBC: iron deficiency anemia. Next step: find the source of loss and replace iron.
step: Patient B has normal ferritin, a high RBC count and elevated HbA2: beta-thalassemia trait. Iron is not indicated and no further workup is needed except genetic counseling.
step: Patient C has high ferritin and low TIBC with chronic inflammation: anemia of chronic disease (inflammation), driven by hepcidin. Treat the arthritis.

@mistakes
- Giving iron to every microcytic patient: thalassemia trait and inflammation do not respond and iron can overload.
- Interpreting ferritin in isolation during inflammation, when it can be falsely normal despite deficiency.
- Treating the iron deficiency without looking for the source in a man or postmenopausal woman.
- Reading a normal HbA2 as excluding all thalassemia: alpha thalassemia trait and coexisting iron deficiency can give normal HbA2.
