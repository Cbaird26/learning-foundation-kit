@article
Proteins do most of the work in your cells: they catalyze reactions, carry oxygen, build collagen and signal between organs. Every protein is a chain of the same twenty building blocks, so understanding biochemistry starts with those building blocks.

### The amino acid

Each standard amino acid has a central **alpha carbon** bonded to an amino group, a carboxyl group, a hydrogen and a variable **side chain (R group)**. At physiologic pH the amino group is protonated ($-NH_3^+$) and the carboxyl group is deprotonated ($-COO^-$), so the molecule is a **zwitterion** (zero net charge when the side chain is uncharged). Every standard amino acid except glycine (R = H) has a chiral alpha carbon, and the amino acids in human proteins are the **L** enantiomers.

Side chains determine behavior. Group them by chemistry:

| Class | Examples | Key property |
|---|---|---|
| Nonpolar (hydrophobic) | Gly, Ala, Val, Leu, Ile, Met, Pro, Phe, Trp | Buried in a protein's core |
| Polar uncharged | Ser, Thr, Cys, Tyr, Asn, Gln | Hydrogen bond; Cys forms disulfides |
| Acidic (negative at pH 7.4) | Asp, Glu | Carboxylate side chains |
| Basic (positive at pH 7.4) | Lys, Arg, His | Amine, guanidinium, imidazole |

Histidine has a side-chain pKa near 6, so it is the only side chain that buffers near physiologic pH; this is why it matters in hemoglobin and in enzyme active sites.

### Ionization and the isoelectric point

The **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation** relates pH and pKa to the ratio of the deprotonated form (base, $A^-$) to the protonated form (acid, $HA$):

$$\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a + \log\frac{[A^-]}{[HA]}.$$

When $\mathrm{pH} = \mathrm{p}K_a$ the two forms are equal. Each unit of pH away from the pKa changes the ratio tenfold.

The **isoelectric point (pI)** is the pH at which the molecule has zero net charge. For an amino acid with no ionizable side chain, it is the average of the two pKa values: $\mathrm{pI} = \frac{1}{2}(\mathrm{p}K_{a1} + \mathrm{p}K_{a2})$. For one with an ionizable side chain, average the two pKa values that surround the neutral species: for an acidic amino acid, the two lowest; for a basic one, the two highest. A protein migrates toward the electrode of opposite charge when pH differs from its pI, which is the basis of electrophoresis and isoelectric focusing.

### Peptide bonds and four levels of structure

A **peptide bond** is an amide bond between the carboxyl carbon of one residue and the amino nitrogen of the next. It has partial double-bond character, so it is planar and rigid; rotation occurs about the phi and psi angles on either side of each alpha carbon.

- **Primary structure** is the amino acid sequence, read from the N-terminus to the C-terminus. Covalent peptide bonds hold it.
- **Secondary structure** is local folding stabilized by backbone hydrogen bonds. In an **alpha helix** the C=O of residue $i$ bonds to the N-H of residue $i+4$; in a **beta sheet** strands bond side by side. Proline breaks helices because its ring fixes the backbone angle and lacks an N-H.
- **Tertiary structure** is the overall 3D fold, driven by the hydrophobic effect and stabilized by ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds and **disulfide bonds** between cysteines.
- **Quaternary structure** is the assembly of two or more polypeptide subunits, as in hemoglobin ($\alpha_2\beta_2$).

Chaperones help proteins fold; misfolding underlies prion disease and contributes to Alzheimer disease. Collagen shows structure and medicine together: its repeating Gly-X-Y triple helix needs proline hydroxylation, which requires vitamin C, and its absence causes scurvy.

@example
title: Isoelectric point of aspartate
problem: Aspartic acid has pKa values of $1.99$ (alpha-carboxyl), $3.90$ (side-chain carboxyl) and $9.90$ (alpha-amino). Find its pI.
step: Aspartate is acidic, so its neutral form is the one that has lost only the alpha-carboxyl proton and still carries the protonated side-chain carboxyl.
step: Order the ionizations: $1.99$, then $3.90$, then $9.90$. The neutral species exists between the first and second deprotonations.
step: Average the two surrounding pKa values: $\mathrm{pI} = \frac{1.99 + 3.90}{2}$.
step: $\mathrm{pI} = 2.945 \approx 2.95$. At physiologic pH, far above this, aspartate is negatively charged.

@mistakes
- Averaging the same two pKa values (for example the two lowest) for every amino acid; only the two flanking the neutral species count, and the side chain matters for acidic and basic residues.
- Assuming glycine is chiral; its side chain is hydrogen, so it has no stereocenter.
- Saying disulfide bonds hold secondary structure; they are covalent cross-links of tertiary (or quaternary) structure, and the peptide backbone hydrogen bonds define secondary structure.
- Confusing the N- and C-terminus: sequences are written, and proteins are made, from the N-terminus toward the C-terminus.

@practice num
prompt: The imidazole side chain of histidine has a pKa of about $6.0$. What fraction of histidine side chains is protonated at pH $7.4$? Round to three decimals.
answer: 0.038
check: 1 / (1 + 10 ** (7.4 - 6.0))
tol: 0.002
hint: Find the ratio $[A^-]/[HA]$ from Henderson-Hasselbalch first.
hint: The ratio is $10^{1.4} \approx 25$. The protonated fraction is $1/(1 + \text{ratio})$.
solution: $[A^-]/[HA] = 10^{1.4} = 25.1$, so the protonated fraction is $1/(1 + 25.1) = 0.038$. Most histidine side chains are neutral at pH 7.4.
