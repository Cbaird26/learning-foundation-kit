@article
Amino acids are building blocks, but excess ones are catabolized: the carbon skeletons enter glucose, ketone or energy metabolism, and the nitrogen must be disposed of. Free ammonia is neurotoxic, so the liver converts it to urea.

### Removing the nitrogen

**Transamination** moves an amino group from an amino acid to $\alpha$-ketoglutarate, giving glutamate and the corresponding $\alpha$-keto acid. Aminotransferases such as **ALT** (alanine aminotransferase) and **AST** (aspartate aminotransferase) require **pyridoxal phosphate (vitamin B$_6$)**; they are reversible and serve both catabolism and synthesis. Glutamate then undergoes **oxidative deamination** by glutamate dehydrogenase in the liver mitochondria, releasing NH$_4^+$ and regenerating $\alpha$-ketoglutarate.

Ammonia travels to the liver in non-toxic forms. In most tissues, **glutamine synthetase** adds NH$_4^+$ to glutamate to form **glutamine**; in the liver, glutaminase releases NH$_4^+$ again. In muscle, nitrogen goes to pyruvate to form **alanine**, which the liver converts back to pyruvate for gluconeogenesis (the **glucose-alanine cycle**). In the brain, ammonia excess depletes $\alpha$-ketoglutarate and glutamate and raises astrocyte glutamine, contributing to cerebral edema and hepatic encephalopathy.

### The urea cycle

The cycle occurs in liver, partly in mitochondria and partly in cytosol:

1. **Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I (CPS I)**, mitochondrial: NH$_4^+$ + CO$_2$ + 2 ATP $\to$ carbamoyl phosphate. This is the rate-limiting step; it is **activated by N-acetylglutamate**, whose synthesis is stimulated by arginine and by high glutamate.
2. **Ornithine transcarbamylase (OTC)**, mitochondrial: carbamoyl phosphate + ornithine $\to$ citrulline.
3. **Argininosuccinate synthetase**, cytosol: citrulline + aspartate + ATP $\to$ argininosuccinate (ATP to AMP + PP$_i$).
4. **Argininosuccinate lyase:** argininosuccinate $\to$ arginine + **fumarate**.
5. **Arginase:** arginine $\to$ urea + ornithine.

The two nitrogens of urea come from NH$_4^+$ and aspartate. Fumarate links the cycle to the citric acid cycle. The energy cost is **4 high-energy phosphate bonds** per urea (2 ATP at CPS I and 2 equivalents at argininosuccinate synthetase). CPS I (urea cycle, uses ammonia) differs from CPS II (pyrimidine synthesis, cytosolic, uses glutamine).

### Carbon skeletons

Glucogenic amino acids give pyruvate or citric acid cycle intermediates and can supply glucose. Only **leucine and lysine** are purely **ketogenic**. Isoleucine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and tyrosine are both.

### Inherited disorders

| Disorder | Defect | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **OTC deficiency** (X-linked) | Ornithine transcarbamylase | Hyperammonemia, vomiting, lethargy after protein; **high orotic acid**, low BUN, low citrulline |
| **PKU** | Phenylalanine hydroxylase (or BH$_4$ synthesis) | Intellectual disability, fair skin, musty odor; tyrosine becomes essential; treat with low-phenylalanine diet |
| **Maple syrup urine disease** | Branched-chain $\alpha$-ketoacid dehydrogenase | Sweet-smelling urine, poor feeding, encephalopathy; limit Leu, Ile, Val; thiamine may help |
| **Homocystinuria** | Cystathionine synthase (commonly) | Lens subluxation, thrombosis, intellectual disability |
| **Alkaptonuria** | Homogentisate oxidase | Dark urine on standing, ochronosis, arthritis |

Acute hyperammonemia is managed by stopping protein intake, providing dextrose, and using nitrogen scavengers (sodium benzoate, phenylacetate) or dialysis. In liver disease, lactulose lowers colonic ammonia absorption.

**Nitrogen balance** is nitrogen intake minus nitrogen losses. Protein is about 16% nitrogen by mass. Positive balance occurs in growth and pregnancy; negative balance with starvation or catabolic illness.

@example
title: Nitrogen balance
problem: A patient receives 60 g of protein per day (16% nitrogen) and loses 8 g of nitrogen in urine and 2 g by other routes daily. What is the nitrogen balance?
step: Nitrogen intake $= 60 \times 0.16 = 9.6$ g/day.
step: Total loss $= 8 + 2 = 10$ g/day.
step: Balance $= 9.6 - 10 = -0.4$ g/day.
step: The slightly negative balance means the patient is losing body protein.

@mistakes
- Saying the urea cycle makes ammonia; it consumes it.
- Forgetting that one urea nitrogen comes from aspartate, not ammonia.
- Placing the entire cycle in the mitochondria; the first two steps are mitochondrial, the next three cytosolic.
- Confusing CPS I (liver mitochondria, urea cycle) with CPS II (cytosol, pyrimidines).
