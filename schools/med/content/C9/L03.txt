@article
Enzymes are mostly proteins (a few are RNA) that speed reactions by factors of $10^6$ or more. They do so by stabilizing the **transition state**, lowering the **activation energy**. An enzyme does not change the free-energy difference between substrate and product, so it does not change $\Delta G$ or the equilibrium constant; it only gets the system there faster. The substrate binds in the **active site**, and the **induced fit** model says the site reshapes around the substrate. Many enzymes need **cofactors** (metal ions) or **coenzymes** (organic molecules such as NAD$^+$ or the vitamin-derived thiamine pyrophosphate).

### The Michaelis-Menten model

The simplest mechanism is

$$E + S \;\underset{k_{-1}}{\overset{k_1}{\rightleftharpoons}}\; ES \;\xrightarrow{k_{cat}}\; E + P.$$

At steady state the rate is described by the **Michaelis-Menten equation**:

$$v = \frac{V_{max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}.$$

Two parameters summarize the curve:

- $V_{max}$ is the maximum velocity, reached when all enzyme is saturated with substrate. It equals $k_{cat}[E]_T$, where $[E]_T$ is total enzyme concentration, so it depends on how much enzyme is present.
- $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which $v = \tfrac{1}{2}V_{max}$. It is a property of the enzyme-substrate pair, and it is independent of enzyme concentration. Within the usual assumptions, a **low Km** means the enzyme reaches half-maximal velocity at low substrate, which we read as high apparent affinity.

### Limiting behavior

- When $[S] \ll K_m$, $v \approx (V_{max}/K_m)[S]$: first-order in substrate, so velocity is proportional to $[S]$.
- When $[S] \gg K_m$, $v \approx V_{max}$: **zero-order** in substrate; the enzyme is saturated, and doubling $[S]$ does nothing. Alcohol dehydrogenase is a clinical example: at intoxicating blood ethanol levels (many mM, well above the enzyme's Km of about 1 mM or less) it is near saturation, which is why blood ethanol then falls at a roughly constant hourly rate (at low concentrations the rate becomes concentration-dependent again).

A useful fraction: to reach a fraction $f$ of $V_{max}$, you need $[S] = K_m \cdot f/(1 - f)$. For 80% of Vmax, $[S] = 4K_m$; for 90%, $[S] = 9K_m$.

### Turnover and efficiency

The **turnover number** $k_{cat} = V_{max}/[E]_T$ is the number of substrate molecules converted per active site per second. **Catalytic efficiency** is $k_{cat}/K_m$; it is the best single number for comparing how well an enzyme handles different substrates, with an upper limit near the diffusion rate ($10^8$ to $10^9\ \mathrm{M^{-1}s^{-1}}$). Enzymes near this limit are called "catalytically perfect."

### Why this matters in the body

Isozymes with different Km values suit different jobs. Hexokinase (Km about 0.1 mM glucose) works near saturation in most tissues, whereas liver glucokinase (half-saturation concentration $S_{0.5}$ about 7 to 10 mM; it is cooperative, so this is an approximate half-saturation value rather than a true Km) has a velocity that tracks blood glucose after a meal and is not inhibited by glucose 6-phosphate. Many drugs are designed around Km and Vmax: a competitive inhibitor raises the apparent Km, and a pharmacologist reads the effect off kinetic plots (next lesson).

Not all enzymes obey this model. Allosteric enzymes, which are multi-subunit and cooperative, give **sigmoidal** curves, and the model also does not describe enzymes with substrate inhibition or with several substrates in complex sequences without extension.

@example
title: Velocity from Km and Vmax
problem: An enzyme has $V_{max} = 80\ \mu\mathrm{mol/min}$ and $K_m = 3\ \mathrm{mM}$. Find the velocity at $[S] = 9\ \mathrm{mM}$, and the substrate concentration at which $v = 40\ \mu\mathrm{mol/min}$.
step: Substitute into the equation: $v = 80 \times 9 / (3 + 9)$.
step: $v = 720/12 = 60\ \mu\mathrm{mol/min}$, which is 75% of Vmax.
step: For $v = 40 = \tfrac{1}{2}V_{max}$, the definition of Km gives $[S] = K_m$.
step: So half-maximal velocity needs $[S] = 3$ mM. Check: $80 \times 3/6 = 40$.

@mistakes
- Saying an enzyme shifts the equilibrium; it changes only the rate at which equilibrium is approached.
- Reading Km as "the substrate concentration at Vmax"; it is the concentration at half of Vmax.
- Believing Km changes when more enzyme is added; only Vmax scales with $[E]_T$.
- Doubling $[S]$ well above Km and expecting double velocity; the enzyme is already saturated.

@practice num
prompt: An enzyme has $K_m = 5$ mM. What substrate concentration (in mM) gives $80\%$ of $V_{max}$?
answer: 20
check: 5 * 0.8 / (1 - 0.8)
tol: 1%
unit: mM
hint: Set $v/V_{max} = 0.8$ in the Michaelis-Menten equation.
hint: $0.8 = [S]/(5 + [S])$. Solve for $[S]$.
solution: $0.8(5 + [S]) = [S]$, so $4 = 0.2[S]$ and $[S] = 20$ mM, which is $4K_m$.

@practice num
prompt: At saturating substrate, a reaction proceeds at $V_{max} = 120\ \mu\mathrm{M/s}$ with a total enzyme concentration of $0.40\ \mu\mathrm{M}$. What is $k_{cat}$ in $\mathrm{s^{-1}}$?
answer: 300
check: 120 / 0.40
tol: 1%
unit: s^-1
hint: $k_{cat}$ is turnover per active site (one site per enzyme here).
hint: $V_{max} = k_{cat}[E]_T$.
solution: $k_{cat} = V_{max}/[E]_T = 120/0.40 = 300\ \mathrm{s^{-1}}$.

@practice mc
prompt: Enzyme A has $K_m = 0.1$ mM and enzyme B has $K_m = 10$ mM for the same substrate (both obey Michaelis-Menten kinetics). At a substrate concentration of 1 mM, what is the best description?
choice: Enzyme B is nearly saturated and enzyme A is nearly inactive
choice*: Enzyme A is nearly saturated and its velocity changes little if substrate rises further; enzyme B is far from saturation and responds strongly to changes
choice: Both enzymes have the same velocity because they catalyze the same reaction
choice: Enzyme B has the higher Vmax because its Km is higher
hint: Compare $[S]$ with each Km.
solution: At 1 mM, enzyme A is at about 91% of Vmax ($1/1.1$) and enzyme B is at about 9% ($1/11$). Km says nothing about Vmax. This is the logic of hexokinase versus glucokinase.

@quiz num
prompt: An enzyme with $K_m = 4$ mM has a velocity of $7.5\ \mu\mathrm{mol/min}$ at $[S] = 12$ mM. What is $V_{max}$ in $\mu\mathrm{mol/min}$?
answer: 10
check: 7.5 * (4 + 12) / 12
tol: 1%
unit: µmol/min
hint: Rearrange the Michaelis-Menten equation to solve for Vmax.

solution: $V_{max} = 7.5 \times 16/12 = 10\ \mu\mathrm{mol/min}$. Check: $10 \times 12/16 = 7.5$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A 40-year-old man's blood ethanol falls by roughly the same amount each hour over a wide range of high starting levels. Which kinetic description best explains this?
choice: Alcohol dehydrogenase is working at low substrate, where velocity is proportional to ethanol concentration
choice*: At the high ethanol concentrations, the enzyme is saturated and behaves as zero-order in substrate
choice: Ethanol raises the Km of the enzyme so that velocity falls with time
choice: The enzyme is allosterically activated by its product
hint: Constant rate means velocity does not depend on $[S]$.
solution: When $[S] \gg K_m$, $v \approx V_{max}$, so velocity is constant (zero-order) and independent of substrate concentration.
