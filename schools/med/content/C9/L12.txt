@article
No pathway runs in isolation. Hormones coordinate which tissues store or release fuel, and the switches are the enzymes you have met in the course. The main idea is simple: **the fed state stores, the fasting state releases**, and the brain and red cells must always be fed.

### The hormones

- **Insulin** (beta cells; high after a meal) promotes storage: glucose uptake via GLUT4 in muscle and fat, glycogen synthesis, glycolysis, fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis, protein synthesis, and it inhibits lipolysis, gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis. It signals through a receptor tyrosine kinase, activating phosphatases such as protein phosphatase 1.
- **Glucagon** (alpha cells; high in fasting) acts mainly on the liver through cAMP and PKA: glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis and ketogenesis. Lipolysis in adipose tissue during fasting is driven mainly by low insulin, with catecholamines adding to it; glucagon's direct effect on human fat cells is small.
- **Epinephrine** mobilizes glycogen and fat in exercise and stress; **cortisol** supports gluconeogenesis and protein catabolism in prolonged stress.

### The fed (absorptive) state

Insulin is high and glucagon low. The liver takes up glucose (GLUT2), stores glycogen, converts excess to fatty acids and exports triglycerides in **VLDL**; chylomicrons carry dietary fat, and **lipoprotein lipase** delivers fatty acids to adipose tissue and muscle. Muscle takes up glucose and builds glycogen and protein. The brain uses glucose.

### Fasting

- **Early fasting (about 4 to 16 hours):** insulin falls and glucagon rises. The liver breaks down glycogen and releases glucose; liver glycogen (about 100 g) lasts roughly a day at most.
- **Fasting to several days:** gluconeogenesis from lactate, glycerol and alanine becomes the major source; lipolysis in adipose tissue releases fatty acids that fuel liver, muscle and heart, sparing glucose. The liver makes ketone bodies.
- **Prolonged fasting (more than about 3 days):** ketone bodies rise to several mM, and the brain derives much of its energy from them, which **reduces the glucose needed and spares muscle protein**. Insulin is low, and glucagon and cortisol are high. Red cells still depend on glucose.

| State | Insulin | Glucagon | Blood glucose | Fatty acids | Ketones |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fed | High | Low | High to normal | Low | Low |
| Early fast | Falling | Rising | Normal | Rising | Low |
| Prolonged fast | Low | High | Low-normal | High | High |

### Exercise

During exercise, muscle first uses **phosphocreatine** and its own glycogen (broken down by phosphorylase activated by Ca$^{2+}$, AMP and epinephrine), then blood glucose and fatty acids. **AMPK** senses AMP and stimulates glucose uptake and fatty acid oxidation. Lactate formed by glycolysis returns to the liver via the Cori cycle. In longer exercise, fatty acid oxidation increases and liver glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis maintain blood glucose.

### Diabetes

In **type 1 diabetes** with insufficient insulin, the body behaves as if it were fasting while glucose is plentiful: glucagon dominates, hepatic glucose output rises, lipolysis is unrestrained, and acetyl-CoA is converted to ketones faster than tissues use them, causing **diabetic ketoacidosis** (hyperglycemia, an anion gap acidosis, osmotic diuresis). Insulin is the treatment. In type 2 diabetes, tissue resistance to insulin and relative insulin deficiency cause hyperglycemia, usually without marked ketosis, since some insulin still restrains lipolysis.

@example
title: How long does liver glycogen last for the brain?
problem: The liver holds about $100$ g of glycogen. If the brain alone used $120$ g of glucose per day, how many hours would the glycogen last?
step: Convert to an hourly rate: $120\ \mathrm{g/day} \div 24 = 5$ g/h.
step: Divide the store by the rate: $100/5 = 20$ h.
step: Interpretation: even in this simplified case the store lasts about a day, so after an overnight fast and into the next day, gluconeogenesis is essential.
step: In reality red cells and other tissues also use glucose, so glycogen lasts less time.

@mistakes
- Saying glucagon acts on muscle; muscle lacks glucagon receptors, and epinephrine mobilizes muscle glycogen.
- Predicting the brain stops using glucose in prolonged fasting; it still needs some, but ketones supply a large share.
- Expecting fatty acids to be converted into glucose in the fed or fasting state; essentially only glycerol (and propionyl-CoA from odd-chain fatty acids) contributes.
- Forgetting that type 1 diabetes is a "starvation in the midst of plenty" state with high glucagon and ketones.
