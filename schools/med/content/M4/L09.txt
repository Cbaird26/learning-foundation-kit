@article
**Heme** is the iron-containing porphyrin of hemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes and catalase. About 85 percent of heme is made in developing red cells (for hemoglobin) and most of the rest in the liver (for cytochrome P450 enzymes). Defects of the pathway are the **porphyrias**.

### The pathway

1. **Mitochondrion:** glycine and succinyl-CoA form **delta-aminolevulinic acid (ALA)** by **ALA synthase** (rate-limiting; cofactor **pyridoxal phosphate**).
2. **Cytosol:** **ALA dehydratase** joins two ALA to **porphobilinogen (PBG)**; **PBG deaminase** (hydroxymethylbilane synthase) makes hydroxymethylbilane; **uroporphyrinogen III synthase** forms uroporphyrinogen III; **uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase** makes coproporphyrinogen III.
3. **Mitochondrion:** **coproporphyrinogen oxidase** makes protoporphyrinogen IX; **protoporphyrinogen oxidase** makes protoporphyrin IX; **ferrochelatase** inserts Fe$^{2+}$ to form **heme**.

A memory path: ALA, PBG, hydroxymethylbilane, uroporphyrinogen, coproporphyrinogen, protoporphyrinogen, protoporphyrin, heme.

### Regulation

- **ALAS1** (liver, housekeeping) is repressed by heme. Anything that uses heme (inducing cytochrome P450 enzymes with drugs such as barbiturates, rifampin or carbamazepine; or fasting, which induces ALAS1 via PGC-1alpha) removes this feedback and raises ALAS1, pushing precursors forward. Glucose represses ALAS1.
- **ALAS2** (erythroid) is regulated by iron through an iron-responsive element in its mRNA. Mutations of *ALAS2* cause **X-linked sideroblastic anemia**, often pyridoxine-responsive.

### Porphyrias

Porphyrias are classified as **acute (neurovisceral)** when the block is early and ALA and PBG accumulate, **cutaneous** when porphyrins accumulate and are photoactive, or both.

| Disorder | Enzyme | Inheritance | Key features and labs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acute intermittent porphyria (AIP)** | PBG deaminase | Autosomal dominant, low penetrance | Neurovisceral attacks only; **urine ALA and PBG elevated**; no photosensitivity |
| Variegate porphyria | Protoporphyrinogen oxidase | AD | Attacks plus blistering photosensitivity |
| Hereditary coproporphyria | Coproporphyrinogen oxidase | AD | Attacks plus photosensitivity |
| **Porphyria cutanea tarda (PCT)** | Uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase | Acquired (most) or familial | Most common porphyria; **blistering of sun-exposed skin** (hands), fragility, hypertrichosis; **urine uroporphyrin** (pink-orange fluorescence under Wood lamp); associated with hepatitis C, alcohol, iron overload, estrogens, HIV |
| Congenital erythropoietic porphyria | Uroporphyrinogen III synthase | AR | Severe photosensitivity with mutilation, red urine, fluorescent red teeth, hemolytic anemia |
| **Erythropoietic protoporphyria (EPP)** | Ferrochelatase | AR (usually) | **Painful burning photosensitivity without blisters**; elevated **free erythrocyte protoporphyrin**; may cause liver disease |

### Acute intermittent porphyria

The **"five Ps"**: **P**ainful abdomen, **P**olyneuropathy (motor, often proximal), **P**sychological disturbances, **P**ort-wine-colored urine (PBG darkens on standing in light and air), and **P**recipitated by drugs. Other findings: tachycardia, hypertension, constipation, hyponatremia (SIADH), seizures. Attacks often start in women after puberty, linked to luteal phase hormones, fasting or dieting, alcohol, infections and drugs (cytochrome P450 inducers). There is **no rash**. Diagnosis during an attack: **spot urine PBG** (normalized to creatinine), with ALA.

Treatment: remove triggers; **carbohydrate loading** (intravenous dextrose); **intravenous hemin**, typically 3 to 4 mg/kg once daily for 4 days (a typical adult regimen for study; verify against current references), which represses ALAS1; **givosiran** (siRNA against ALAS1) for recurrent attacks; opioids for pain; and avoidance of unsafe drugs.

### Lead poisoning

Lead inhibits **ALA dehydratase** and **ferrochelatase** (and **pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase**). Results:

- **Urine and blood ALA rise**; **free erythrocyte or zinc protoporphyrin rises** (zinc substitutes for iron in the unfinished ring).
- **Microcytic, hypochromic anemia** with **basophilic stippling** (ribosomal RNA fragments not degraded because of nucleotidase inhibition) and ringed sideroblasts in marrow.
- **Abdominal colic and constipation**; **peripheral neuropathy** (wrist or foot drop, mainly motor); **encephalopathy** in children (irritability, ataxia, seizures, cerebral edema); renal tubular injury (Fanconi syndrome); hypertension; gout. Gingival **lead lines** (Burton) and dense **metaphyseal lines** on radiographs of children's long bones.
- Exposure sources: old lead-based paint dust in homes built before 1978, contaminated water or soil, imported ceramics, certain traditional remedies and cosmetics, occupations (battery, renovation).
- Management: remove the source; **succimer** (oral) is commonly used for children with blood lead of 45 micrograms/dL or more; **dimercaprol and calcium disodium EDTA** are used for encephalopathy or very high levels. Blood lead of 3.5 micrograms/dL or more is the CDC reference value for the highest 2.5 percent of children; no level is considered safe.

Compare: both AIP and lead raise ALA, and both can produce abdominal pain and neuropathy, but AIP has elevated **PBG** and no anemia, whereas lead raises protoporphyrin and causes anemia with basophilic stippling.

@example
title: Why lead causes anemia
problem: A 3-year-old child living in an old house has pallor, constipation and irritability. Hemoglobin is 8 g/dL with MCV low and basophilic stippling on smear. Explain the anemia.
step: Heme requires ALA dehydratase (making PBG) and ferrochelatase (inserting iron). Lead inhibits both enzymes.
step: Blocking ALA dehydratase raises ALA in blood and urine; blocking ferrochelatase leaves protoporphyrin IX without iron, so free erythrocyte or zinc protoporphyrin rises.
step: Hemoglobin synthesis falls, giving a microcytic, hypochromic anemia.
step: Lead also inhibits pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase, so ribosomal RNA fragments persist as basophilic stippling.

@example
title: Recognizing acute intermittent porphyria
problem: A 26-year-old woman is admitted with severe abdominal pain, vomiting and constipation after two weeks of crash dieting and starting an anticonvulsant. Her sodium is 126 mEq/L and her urine darkens in the sunlight. There is no rash. Which test confirms the diagnosis and what is the mechanism?
step: Abdominal pain, neuropsychiatric signs, hyponatremia and dark urine after fasting and a drug trigger suggest AIP.
step: The test is a spot urine PBG (and ALA); both are elevated during an attack.
step: PBG deaminase is partially deficient (about half activity). A trigger that raises ALAS1 (fasting, cytochrome P450 inducers) overloads the blocked step, so ALA and PBG accumulate.
step: Treat with dextrose, hemin, trigger removal and supportive care.

@mistakes
- Looking for photosensitive skin lesions in acute intermittent porphyria; they are absent because the accumulating precursors are not photoactive.
- Treating lead poisoning in a child with iron or phlebotomy alone: correct any iron deficiency, but remove the lead source and chelate when the level calls for it.
- Forgetting that lead inhibits two heme enzymes (ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase), not one.
- Giving cytochrome P450 inducers (barbiturates) to a patient with an acute porphyria.
