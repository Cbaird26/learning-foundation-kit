@article
**Collagen** is the most abundant protein in the body. Its basic unit is a **triple helix** of three alpha chains with the repeating sequence **Gly-X-Y** (X is often proline and Y is often hydroxyproline), so **glycine at every third position** is essential: it is small enough to fit inside the helix.

### Synthesis

1. **Ribosome and rough ER:** pre-pro-alpha chains are made and the signal peptide is removed.
2. **Hydroxylation** of proline and lysine residues by prolyl and lysyl hydroxylase, requiring **vitamin C** and Fe$^{2+}$. Deficiency produces scurvy.
3. **Glycosylation** of hydroxylysine residues, then **triple helix formation** (procollagen) starting at the C-terminal propeptides.
4. **Golgi and secretion** by vesicles.
5. **Extracellular:** procollagen peptidases cleave N- and C-terminal propeptides, giving tropocollagen, which assembles into fibrils.
6. **Cross-linking** by **lysyl oxidase** (a **copper**-dependent enzyme) joins lysine and hydroxylysine residues, giving tensile strength.

### Collagen types

| Type | Tissue | Disease when defective |
|---|---|---|
| I | Bone, skin, tendon, sclera, late wound repair | Osteogenesis imperfecta |
| II | Cartilage, vitreous, nucleus pulposus | Chondrodysplasias |
| III | Reticulin, blood vessels, skin, uterus, early wound repair | Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome |
| IV | Basement membranes (glomerulus, cochlea, lens) | Alport syndrome; Goodpasture (autoantibody to its alpha-3 chain) |
| V | Skin, placenta | Classical Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (*COL5A1*, *COL5A2*) |

A helpful memory aid: type I is bone, II is cartilage, III is reticulin (vessels), IV is basement membrane ("under the floor").

### Osteogenesis imperfecta

Usually autosomal dominant mutations of *COL1A1* or *COL1A2*. Glycine substitutions disrupt the helix and act in a **dominant-negative** manner; haploinsufficiency gives a milder phenotype. Features: **multiple fractures with minimal trauma**, **blue sclerae** (thin sclera shows the choroid), **hearing loss**, **dentinogenesis imperfecta** and joint laxity. Severity ranges from mild (type I, most common) to perinatally lethal (type II). Bone density is low, and treatment includes bisphosphonates, orthopedic care and fall prevention. Consider it in the differential of child abuse when fractures are unexplained, but remember that normal sclerae do not exclude mild disease.

### Ehlers-Danlos syndromes

Group of connective tissue disorders with **skin hyperextensibility, joint hypermobility and tissue fragility**.

- **Classical** (*COL5A1*, *COL5A2*): velvety hyperextensible skin, wide atrophic ("cigarette-paper") scars, joint hypermobility.
- **Hypermobile**: most common, mainly joint hypermobility and pain; the genetic cause is usually unknown.
- **Vascular** (*COL3A1*, autosomal dominant): thin translucent skin with visible veins, easy bruising, characteristic facial appearance and **rupture of arteries, bowel or the gravid uterus**. The most dangerous type.
- **Kyphoscoliotic** (*PLOD1*, lysyl hydroxylase deficiency): scoliosis, hypotonia, ocular fragility.

### Marfan syndrome

Autosomal dominant mutation of ***FBN1*** (fibrillin-1), a component of elastic microfibrils that also sequesters **TGF-beta**; abnormal signaling contributes to aortic wall damage. Features: tall stature, **arachnodactyly**, arm span greater than height (ratio above about 1.05), pectus deformity, scoliosis, high-arched palate, **upward (superotemporal) lens dislocation (ectopia lentis)**, **mitral valve prolapse** and **aortic root dilation with dissection** (the main cause of death). Management: **echocardiographic surveillance**, beta-blockers or angiotensin receptor blockers, avoidance of contact sports and surgical root replacement at a diameter of roughly 5 cm (lower with a family history of dissection or rapid growth). **Loeys-Dietz syndrome** (*TGFBR1* or *TGFBR2*) has similar features with hypertelorism, bifid uvula and arterial tortuosity.

### Alport syndrome

Mostly **X-linked** (*COL4A5*), also autosomal forms. Defective type IV collagen causes **hematuria, progressive kidney failure, sensorineural hearing loss and anterior lenticonus**. Electron microscopy of the glomerular basement membrane shows thinning and splitting (**basket-weave** appearance).

### Putting it together: lead poisoning versus acute intermittent porphyria

Both involve heme synthesis (last week). **Lead**: inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase, so **ALA** and **protoporphyrin** rise; anemia with basophilic stippling; lead lines; neuropathy (wrist drop). **AIP**: PBG deaminase deficiency, so **ALA and PBG** rise; **no anemia or skin findings**; attacks of abdominal pain, neuropathy, psychiatric symptoms and port-wine urine, triggered by fasting and cytochrome P450 inducers.

title: Classifying a connective tissue disorder
problem: A 22-year-old tall man has an arm span of 188 cm and a height of 175 cm, a pectus excavatum, and upward-dislocated lenses. An echocardiogram shows an aortic root dilated to 4.4 cm. Which gene is affected and what is the major risk?
step: Compute the arm span to height ratio: $188/175 \approx 1.07$, above 1.05, supporting a marfanoid habitus.
step: Upward lens dislocation with aortic root dilation points to Marfan syndrome (*FBN1*), not homocystinuria, which has downward lens dislocation and thrombosis.
step: The main risk is aortic dissection. Management includes beta-blocker or ARB therapy, annual imaging and restriction from contact sports.

@example
title: Fractures and blue sclerae
problem: A 3-year-old girl has had four fractures after minor falls. She has blue sclerae, her father is deaf with several childhood fractures, and radiographs show osteopenia. What is the defect and the inheritance?
step: Fragility fractures with blue sclerae and hearing loss suggest osteogenesis imperfecta.
step: The defect is in type I collagen (*COL1A1* or *COL1A2*): glycine substitutions disrupt the triple helix.
step: Inheritance is usually autosomal dominant, consistent with her father; she is at risk for further fractures, so bisphosphonates and orthopedic care are considered.

@mistakes
- Mixing up Marfan (upward lens dislocation, aortic root dilation) with homocystinuria (downward lens dislocation, thrombosis).
- Forgetting that lysyl oxidase needs copper; Menkes disease therefore affects collagen cross-linking.
- Confusing type III collagen (vascular EDS) with type IV collagen (Alport and basement membranes).
- Labeling every case of unexplained pediatric fractures as osteogenesis imperfecta or as abuse without evaluating both.

@practice self
prompt: Compare lead poisoning and acute intermittent porphyria. For each, name the enzyme or enzymes affected, the main laboratory abnormalities, and how the clinical picture differs.
solution: Lead inhibits ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase (and pyrimidine 5'-nucleotidase), so ALA and free erythrocyte protoporphyrin rise and heme synthesis falls. It causes microcytic anemia with basophilic stippling, abdominal colic, wrist or foot drop, encephalopathy in children, and lead lines. AIP is caused by partial PBG deaminase deficiency, so ALA and PBG accumulate in urine, especially when fasting or P450-inducing drugs raise ALA synthase activity. It causes episodic abdominal pain, neuropathy, psychiatric symptoms, hyponatremia and port-wine urine, with no anemia and no photosensitive rash. Both raise ALA and cause abdominal pain and neuropathy; PBG and absence of anemia point to AIP, and protoporphyrin with stippled microcytic anemia points to lead.
