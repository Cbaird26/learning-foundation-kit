@article
Minerals are inorganic elements needed in the diet. They act as enzyme cofactors, structural components and electrolytes. As with vitamins, each deficiency or excess has a signature syndrome.

### Iron

Dietary iron enters enterocytes (heme iron as heme, non-heme iron as Fe$^{2+}$ through DMT1 after reduction by vitamin C and duodenal cytochrome b). Ferroportin exports iron to plasma, where it is carried by **transferrin** and stored as **ferritin**. **Hepcidin**, made by the liver, degrades ferroportin and so lowers iron absorption and release; inflammation raises hepcidin.

| Condition | Serum iron | TIBC | Ferritin | Transferrin saturation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iron deficiency | low | high | low | low |
| Anemia of chronic disease | low | low | normal or high | low or normal |
| Iron overload | high | low or normal | high | high |

**Transferrin saturation (TSAT)** is serum iron divided by TIBC, times 100. **Hereditary hemochromatosis** is usually autosomal recessive, with the *HFE* C282Y variant causing inappropriately low hepcidin. Features: fatigue, arthralgia of the second and third metacarpophalangeal joints, bronze skin, diabetes, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma, cardiomyopathy and hypogonadism. TSAT above about 45 percent with a high ferritin prompts *HFE* testing. First-line treatment is **phlebotomy**; chelation (deferoxamine) is reserved for those who cannot be phlebotomized.

### Calcium, phosphate and magnesium

About half of serum calcium is free (ionized) and physiologically active; most of the rest binds albumin. The corrected calcium for hypoalbuminemia is

$$\text{Ca}_{\text{corrected}} = \text{Ca}_{\text{measured}} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin})$$

with both in g/dL or mg/dL as appropriate (calcium in mg/dL, albumin in g/dL). Direct measurement of ionized calcium is better when the correction is unreliable. **Magnesium** deficiency causes hypocalcemia (impaired PTH release and action) and hypokalemia that does not respond until magnesium is repleted; it also causes tremor, tetany and arrhythmias such as torsades de pointes.

### Copper

Copper is a cofactor for lysyl oxidase (collagen cross-linking), cytochrome c oxidase, superoxide dismutase, dopamine beta-hydroxylase, tyrosinase and ceruloplasmin (a ferroxidase).

- **Wilson disease**: autosomal recessive mutation of *ATP7B*, a hepatic copper-transporting ATPase. Copper cannot be excreted in bile or loaded onto ceruloplasmin. Findings: liver disease (hepatitis, cirrhosis), neuropsychiatric signs (tremor, dysarthria, dystonia, behavior change), **Kayser-Fleischer rings**, Coombs-negative hemolysis. Labs: **low serum ceruloplasmin, high 24-hour urinary copper**, high hepatic copper. Treatment: chelation (penicillamine or trientine) or zinc, which blocks intestinal copper absorption.
- **Menkes disease**: X-linked recessive mutation of *ATP7A*, an intestinal copper transporter. Copper cannot be absorbed or delivered across the blood-brain barrier, so copper enzymes (including lysyl oxidase) are deficient. Findings in infant boys: kinky, brittle hair, hypotonia, seizures, developmental regression, vascular tortuosity and connective-tissue defects. Serum copper and ceruloplasmin are low. Early parenteral copper histidinate helps only when started very early.

### Zinc and other trace elements

- **Zinc**: cofactor for many enzymes and zinc-finger transcription factors. Deficiency: dermatitis around the mouth and extremities, poor wound healing, loss of taste and smell, hair loss, diarrhea, hypogonadism, immune dysfunction. *Acrodermatitis enteropathica* is an autosomal recessive defect of intestinal zinc uptake (*SLC39A4*).
- **Selenium**: part of glutathione peroxidase and iodothyronine deiodinases. Deficiency: Keshan cardiomyopathy, muscle weakness.
- **Iodine**: thyroid hormone synthesis. Deficiency: goiter, hypothyroidism, and congenital hypothyroidism with cretinism if maternal deficiency is severe.
- **Fluoride**: strengthens enamel; excess causes dental fluorosis.
- **Molybdenum**: cofactor for sulfite oxidase and xanthine oxidase.
- **Manganese, chromium, cobalt**: manganese in superoxide dismutase; cobalt as the metal center of cobalamin; chromium may modulate insulin action.

|---|---|

| Thiamine | Wernicke encephalopathy, wet or dry beriberi |
| Niacin | Pellagra (diarrhea, dermatitis, dementia) |

@example
title: Interpreting an iron panel
problem: A 48-year-old man with arthralgia and bronze skin has serum iron 200 mcg/dL and TIBC 250 mcg/dL. Calculate transferrin saturation and say what it suggests.
step: TSAT is serum iron divided by TIBC, times 100.
step: $200 / 250 = 0.80$, so TSAT is 80 percent.
step: Normal TSAT is roughly 20 to 45 percent, so this is markedly elevated, consistent with iron overload from hemochromatosis.
step: The next steps are a ferritin level and *HFE* genotyping; treatment is phlebotomy if confirmed.

@example
title: Correcting calcium for low albumin
problem: A malnourished patient has total calcium 7.6 mg/dL and albumin 2.0 g/dL. What is the corrected calcium?
step: Use $\text{Ca}_{\text{corrected}} = \text{Ca}_{\text{measured}} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin})$.
step: The albumin deficit is $4.0 - 2.0 = 2.0$ g/dL, so the adjustment is $0.8 \times 2.0 = 1.6$ mg/dL.
step: Corrected calcium is $7.6 + 1.6 = 9.2$ mg/dL, which is normal. The low total value reflects low albumin, not true hypocalcemia.

@mistakes
- Reporting hypocalcemia from a total calcium without correcting for albumin (or measuring ionized calcium).
- Mixing up Wilson disease (*ATP7B*, copper overload, low ceruloplasmin) with Menkes disease (*ATP7A*, copper deficiency).
- Treating hypokalemia with potassium alone while ignoring a low magnesium.
- Using chelation first in hemochromatosis when phlebotomy is the standard initial therapy.
