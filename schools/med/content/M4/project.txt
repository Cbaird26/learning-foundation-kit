title: Metabolic disease casebook
overview: Write a casebook of six short, original clinical cases, each linking a defective enzyme or transporter to its laboratory findings, presentation and treatment. The aim is to practice reasoning from the biochemical block to the bedside and back. Choose cases from different weeks of the course so that the casebook covers carbohydrate, lipid, amino acid, nucleotide, heme and connective-tissue or lysosomal metabolism.
goal: Explain, for each case, how one defective protein produces its accumulated substrate, deficient product and clinical findings.
goal: Choose appropriate confirmatory tests and treatment, and state the reason for each.
goal: Distinguish each disease from at least two look-alike diagnoses.
goal: Communicate clearly in a standard case-report format with accurate units and invented patient details.
step: From the list below, pick six defects, at least five of them from different lessons. Candidates: glucose-6-phosphatase (von Gierke); muscle glycogen phosphorylase (McArdle); galactose-1-phosphate uridyltransferase (classic galactosemia); aldolase B (hereditary fructose intolerance); lipoprotein lipase or LDL receptor; hexosaminidase A (Tay-Sachs); glucocerebrosidase (Gaucher); alpha-galactosidase A (Fabry); phenylalanine hydroxylase (PKU); cystathionine beta-synthase (homocystinuria); ornithine transcarbamylase; HGPRT (Lesch-Nyhan); UMP synthase; PBG deaminase (AIP); uroporphyrinogen decarboxylase (PCT); ATP7B (Wilson); FBN1 (Marfan); COL1A1 (osteogenesis imperfecta).
step: For each case, first draw a short pathway sketch showing the blocked step, the accumulating substrate, the deficient product and any alternative pathway that produces abnormal metabolites.
step: Write the vignette (about 100 to 150 words): an invented patient with age, sex, setting, symptoms, examination findings and relevant history including family history and triggers. Do not name the diagnosis in the vignette.
step: List the laboratory and imaging findings with units and, where useful, a calculation (for example anion gap, LDL by the Friedewald equation, fractional excretion of uric acid, corrected calcium, glucose infusion rate or nitrogen balance). Show the arithmetic.
step: Write a mechanism paragraph that explains each finding from the enzyme defect, then name the inheritance pattern and the confirmatory test (enzyme assay, genetic testing or a characteristic metabolite).
step: Write the management section: acute measures, long-term treatment and what to avoid (dietary, drug or exercise triggers). Give doses only as typical values for study, labeled as such.
step: Add a "look-alikes" line naming two other diagnoses and the single finding that separates each from the case.
step: Review all six cases for accuracy against the lessons and your reading, check every calculation twice, and write a one-paragraph reflection on which mechanism you found hardest and how you resolved it.
deliverable: A casebook with six complete cases in the format above (vignette, laboratory data with calculations, mechanism, management, look-alikes).
deliverable: A one-page pathway map showing where each of the six enzymes sits in metabolism.
deliverable: A short reflection (about 150 words) identifying one error you corrected and why.
rubric: Each case names a defective protein that truly causes the findings described, and the mechanism links substrate accumulation or product deficiency to every major finding.
rubric: Laboratory values and calculations are internally consistent, correctly computed and use appropriate units.
rubric: Management is correct, specific and distinguishes acute from long-term care; doses, if given, are labeled as typical study doses.
rubric: Look-alike diagnoses are plausible and separated by a specific, correct feature.
rubric: Cases cover at least five different areas of metabolism and use person-first, respectful language and invented patients only.
safety: All patients in the casebook must be invented. This is a study exercise and not medical advice; check any dose or treatment against current references before clinical use.
tutor: Ask the tutor to quiz you by giving a metabolite pattern (for example high orotic acid with high ammonia) and asking you to name the enzyme and the look-alikes.
tutor: Ask the tutor to check the mechanism paragraph of one case and point out any finding that your enzyme defect does not explain.
tutor: Ask the tutor to give you a calculation (such as a glucose infusion rate or nitrogen balance) and mark each step of your working.

### Key ideas, week by week

1. **Vitamins:** fat-soluble A, D, E, K; B1 (TPP, Wernicke, beriberi), B3 (pellagra), B6, B12 and folate (MMA and homocysteine), C (collagen hydroxylation), K (carboxylation of II, VII, IX, X).
2. **Minerals:** transferrin saturation, hemochromatosis, corrected calcium, Wilson (*ATP7B*) versus Menkes (*ATP7A*), zinc, selenium, iodine.
3. **Glycogen storage diseases:** I (G6Pase: hypoglycemia, lactic acidosis, high urate and triglycerides), II (lysosomal Pompe), III (debranching), V (McArdle), glucose infusion rate.
4. **Carbohydrate disorders:** galactosemia (GALT, galactitol cataracts), fructose intolerance (aldolase B, trapped phosphate), lactose intolerance, G6PD deficiency, stool osmotic gap, why G6Pase deficiency causes hypoglycemia.
5. **Lipoproteins:** apo B-48, B-100, C-II, E, A-I; types I, IIa, III, IV; abetalipoproteinemia; Friedewald LDL.
6. **Lysosomal and peroxisomal:** Tay-Sachs, Niemann-Pick, Gaucher, Fabry, Hurler and Hunter, I-cell disease, Zellweger, X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy, Refsum; the dyslipidemia comparison.
7. **Amino acids:** PKU (PAH, BH4, newborn screen), alkaptonuria, homocystinuria, MSUD, urea cycle (OTC and orotic acid), cystinuria.
8. **Purines and pyrimidines:** HGPRT, gout and allopurinol, ADA deficiency, orotic aciduria, nucleotide drug targets, PKU revisited.
9. **Heme and porphyrias:** ALA synthase, AIP (PBG deaminase), PCT, EPP, lead (ALA dehydratase and ferrochelatase).
10. **Collagen:** synthesis steps, osteogenesis imperfecta, Ehlers-Danlos, Marfan, Alport; lead versus AIP.
11. **Nutrition:** kcal per gram, nitrogen balance, fasting states, kwashiorkor versus marasmus, refeeding syndrome.
12. **Obesity and diabetes:** insulin signaling, DKA, A1c and eAG, HOMA-IR, metabolic syndrome criteria, appetite hormones.

### Self-check

- Can you draw the pathway for each disease and mark the block?
- Can you state which substrate accumulates and which product is missing?
- Can you give the inheritance pattern and the confirmatory test?
- Can you do the calculations (anion gap, corrected calcium, TSAT, Friedewald LDL, GIR, nitrogen balance, eAG, FEUA) without a formula sheet?
- Can you name two look-alikes for each disorder and the separating finding?

### Most common errors

- Confusing Wilson with Menkes, Marfan with homocystinuria, Hurler with Hunter, kwashiorkor with marasmus.
- Forgetting that Fabry, Hunter, OTC deficiency, G6PD deficiency, Lesch-Nyhan and X-linked ALD are X-linked.
- Using Friedewald LDL when triglycerides exceed 400 mg/dL.
- Giving folate without B12, glucose without thiamine, or starting nutrition without monitoring phosphate.
- Forgetting that lead inhibits two heme enzymes and that AIP has no rash.

@exam mc
prompt: A 3-day-old boy is lethargic with ammonia 520 micromol/L and respiratory alkalosis; urine orotic acid is markedly elevated. A different infant has megaloblastic anemia unresponsive to B12 and folate, high urine orotic acid and normal ammonia. Which statement correctly explains the difference between the two disorders?
choice: Both are due to deficiency of dihydrofolate reductase
choice*: In the first infant, carbamoyl phosphate from the blocked urea cycle spills into pyrimidine synthesis; in the second, UMP synthase deficiency blocks pyrimidine synthesis without affecting the urea cycle
choice: In the first infant UMP synthase is deficient, and in the second ornithine transcarbamylase is deficient
choice: Both cause hyperammonemia because orotic acid inhibits carbamoyl phosphate synthetase I
choice: The second infant needs sodium benzoate and the first needs uridine
hint: Where does carbamoyl phosphate go when ornithine transcarbamylase is blocked?
hint: Where does orotic acid come from when UMP synthase is deficient?
solution: Ornithine transcarbamylase deficiency causes hyperammonemia, and the excess mitochondrial carbamoyl phosphate enters pyrimidine synthesis to form orotic acid. In hereditary orotic aciduria UMP synthase is deficient, so orotic acid accumulates and pyrimidines are low (megaloblastic anemia) while the urea cycle and ammonia are normal. Uridine treats the second disorder; nitrogen scavengers treat the first.

@exam self
prompt: Write a short casebook entry (vignette, key labs, mechanism, treatment and two look-alikes) for acute intermittent porphyria, and then explain why the same patient should not be given a barbiturate.
solution: Vignette: a 27-year-old woman has 3 days of severe abdominal pain, vomiting and constipation after two weeks of crash dieting and starting an anticonvulsant. She is tachycardic and anxious, sodium is 127 mEq/L, urine darkens on standing, and there is no rash. Labs: spot urine porphobilinogen and ALA markedly elevated; hyponatremia from SIADH. Mechanism: partial deficiency of porphobilinogen deaminase (autosomal dominant, low penetrance). Fasting and cytochrome P450 inducers lower hepatic heme and so derepress ALA synthase 1, pushing ALA and PBG into the blocked step, so they accumulate and cause neurovisceral symptoms; accumulated PBG polymerizes to dark pigments. Treatment: stop the trigger, give intravenous dextrose, intravenous hemin (for example 3 to 4 mg/kg daily for 4 days as a study dose), analgesia, and consider givosiran for recurrent attacks. Look-alikes: lead poisoning (microcytic anemia with basophilic stippling, high protoporphyrin and blood lead, normal PBG) and variegate porphyria (also has blistering photosensitivity). Barbiturates induce cytochrome P450 enzymes, which consume heme, release feedback repression of ALA synthase 1 and worsen precursor accumulation, so they can precipitate or worsen an attack.
rubric: Names PBG deaminase deficiency and elevated urinary PBG and ALA, with a vignette that has no photosensitive rash.
rubric: Explains the trigger mechanism: lowered heme or increased heme demand raises ALA synthase 1 activity.
rubric: Lists correct treatments (remove triggers, carbohydrate loading, hemin, givosiran as appropriate).
rubric: Gives two sensible look-alikes with a separating finding, and explains why barbiturates (cytochrome P450 inducers) are harmful.
hint: Start from the pathway and ask which step is partly missing, then what raises the flux into it.
hint: Barbiturates induce cytochrome P450 enzymes, which use heme.
