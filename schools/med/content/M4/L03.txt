@article
**Glycogen** is a branched polymer of glucose: chains joined by **alpha-1,4 glycosidic bonds**, with **alpha-1,6 branches** about every 8 to 10 residues. It is stored mainly in **liver** (to maintain blood glucose between meals) and **skeletal muscle** (as a fuel for the muscle itself). The key difference: liver expresses **glucose-6-phosphatase**, which releases free glucose; muscle does not, so muscle glycogen can never supply blood glucose.

### Synthesis and breakdown

- **Synthesis:** glucose-6-phosphate to glucose-1-phosphate to UDP-glucose, then **glycogen synthase** extends alpha-1,4 chains (primed by glycogenin) and the **branching enzyme** creates alpha-1,6 branches.
- **Breakdown:** **glycogen phosphorylase** (cofactor pyridoxal phosphate) cleaves alpha-1,4 bonds, releasing glucose-1-phosphate, until four residues from a branch point, leaving a **limit dextrin**. The **debranching enzyme** (4:4 transferase moves three residues; alpha-1,6-glucosidase releases the branch glucose as *free glucose*) then lets phosphorylase continue. Glucose-1-phosphate becomes glucose-6-phosphate; in liver, **glucose-6-phosphatase** (on the endoplasmic reticulum) produces free glucose. Lysosomal **acid alpha-glucosidase** also degrades a small amount of glycogen.
- **Regulation:** glucagon and epinephrine activate phosphorylase (via cAMP and protein kinase A) in liver; epinephrine and calcium do so in muscle. Insulin favors synthesis.

### The diseases

Most are autosomal recessive. A useful memory aid for the common four is "Very Poor Carbohydrate Metabolism": von Gierke, Pompe, Cori, McArdle.

| Type | Enzyme | Main findings |
|---|---|---|
| I, von Gierke | Glucose-6-phosphatase (Ia); G6P translocase (Ib) | Severe fasting hypoglycemia, hepatomegaly, **lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia, hypertriglyceridemia**; Ib adds neutropenia |
| II, Pompe | Lysosomal acid alpha-glucosidase | Cardiomegaly, hypotonia, early death in infants; later-onset muscle weakness; enzyme replacement therapy |
| III, Cori or Forbes | Debranching enzyme | Hepatomegaly, milder hypoglycemia with **ketosis**, normal lactate and uric acid, myopathy; limit dextrin accumulates; gluconeogenesis intact |
| IV, Andersen | Branching enzyme | Abnormal long unbranched glycogen, hepatosplenomegaly, cirrhosis, failure to thrive |
| V, McArdle | Muscle glycogen phosphorylase | Exercise intolerance, cramps, **myoglobinuria**, "second wind" phenomenon; no rise in lactate with ischemic exercise; normal glycogen structure |
| VI, Hers | Liver glycogen phosphorylase | Mild hypoglycemia and hepatomegaly; milder than type I |
| VII, Tarui | Muscle phosphofructokinase-1 | McArdle-like plus hemolysis |

### Von Gierke disease in detail

Glucose-6-phosphatase performs the **final step of both glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis**. When it is absent, neither pathway can release free glucose, so the infant becomes hypoglycemic within a few hours of fasting and does not raise glucose in response to glucagon. The accumulating glucose-6-phosphate has consequences: it is shunted into glycolysis (lactate rises), into the pentose phosphate pathway (ribose-5-phosphate drives purine overproduction and hyperuricemia; lactate also competes with urate for renal excretion), and into fatty acid and triglyceride synthesis (hypertriglyceridemia, fatty liver). Glycogen in liver is structurally normal but excessive, giving a large liver; kidneys also enlarge. Children have doll-like faces, short stature and later risk hepatic adenomas.

Treatment centers on avoiding fasting: frequent daytime feeds, **uncooked cornstarch** and overnight continuous gastric dextrose feeding, with restriction of fructose and galactose (which enter the pathway as glucose-6-phosphate and cannot help). Allopurinol and lipid therapy are used as needed.

### Glucose infusion rate

When intravenous dextrose is used to keep a child normoglycemic, the **glucose infusion rate (GIR)** in mg/kg/min is

$$\text{GIR} = \frac{\text{rate (mL/h)} \times \text{dextrose (\%)} \times 10}{\text{weight (kg)} \times 60}.$$

A 10 percent dextrose solution contains 100 mg of glucose per mL.

@example
title: Telling type I from type III
problem: Two infants have hepatomegaly and fasting hypoglycemia. Infant 1 has lactate 9 mmol/L, uric acid elevated and triglycerides 800 mg/dL. Infant 2 has normal lactate and uric acid, ketones in the urine, elevated creatine kinase and a biopsy showing glycogen with short outer branches. Name each diagnosis.
step: Infant 1: hypoglycemia with lactic acidosis, hyperuricemia and hypertriglyceridemia points to a block at glucose-6-phosphatase, which causes accumulation of glucose-6-phosphate. This is von Gierke disease (type I).
step: Infant 2: gluconeogenesis is intact, so alanine and lactate can still make glucose; lactate stays normal. Ketones rise because the liver breaks down fat when glucose is short.
step: Short outer branches (limit dextrin) plus muscle involvement (raised creatine kinase) indicates a defective debranching enzyme. This is Cori disease (type III).

@example
title: Computing a glucose infusion rate
problem: A 6-kg infant receives 10 percent dextrose at 24 mL/h. What is the GIR in mg/kg/min?
step: Convert to mg of glucose per hour: 10 percent is 100 mg/mL, so $24 \times 100 = 2400$ mg/h.
step: Convert to per minute: $2400 / 60 = 40$ mg/min.
step: Divide by weight: $40 / 6 \approx 6.7$ mg/kg/min.

@mistakes
- Assuming muscle glycogen can raise blood glucose; muscle lacks glucose-6-phosphatase.
- Calling every glycogen storage disease with hypoglycemia "von Gierke"; check lactate, uric acid and ketones.
- Forgetting that the second wind phenomenon (McArdle) occurs because blood-borne glucose and fatty acids become available.
- Forgetting that Pompe disease is a lysosomal disorder and its glycogen accumulates in lysosomes.
