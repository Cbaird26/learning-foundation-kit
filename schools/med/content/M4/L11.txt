@article
Nutrition supplies **energy**, **building blocks** (essential amino acids and fatty acids) and **micronutrients**. Understanding how the body adapts to feeding and fasting explains the clinical picture of malnutrition.

### Energy and macronutrients

| Substrate | Energy (kcal/g) | Respiratory quotient (RQ) |
|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | 4 | 1.0 |
| Protein | 4 | about 0.8 |
| Fat | 9 | about 0.7 |
| Ethanol | 7 | about 0.67 |

The **RQ** is CO$_2$ produced divided by O$_2$ consumed; mixed diets give about 0.8 to 0.85. Overfeeding with carbohydrate raises RQ above 1.0 because of lipogenesis, which increases CO$_2$ production and can make weaning a ventilator difficult.

**Essential amino acids** (cannot be synthesized): phenylalanine, valine, threonine, tryptophan, isoleucine, methionine, histidine, leucine and lysine. Arginine is conditionally essential in growth. **Essential fatty acids**: linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3).

### Nitrogen balance

Protein is about 16 percent nitrogen, so grams of nitrogen equal grams of protein divided by 6.25. A common estimate in clinical practice:

$$\text{Nitrogen balance} = \frac{\text{protein intake (g)}}{6.25} - \left(\text{UUN (g)} + 4\right),$$

where UUN is 24-hour urinary urea nitrogen and 4 g accounts for non-urinary losses (stool, skin) and non-urea urinary nitrogen. A negative balance means net protein catabolism; positive balance occurs in growth, pregnancy and recovery.

### Feeding and fasting

- **Fed state:** insulin high; glucose is stored as glycogen and fat; amino acids build protein.
- **Early fasting (hours):** insulin falls and glucagon rises; **liver glycogenolysis** supplies glucose; glycogen lasts roughly 24 hours.
- **Fasting (days):** **gluconeogenesis** from lactate, glycerol and amino acids (especially alanine from muscle); lipolysis releases fatty acids; the liver makes **ketone bodies** (acetoacetate and beta-hydroxybutyrate).
- **Prolonged starvation:** the brain uses ketone bodies for a large fraction of its energy, which spares glucose and slows muscle protein breakdown; fat is the dominant fuel; resting metabolic rate falls.

### Protein-energy malnutrition

Severe acute malnutrition in children is defined by the WHO as weight-for-height below $-3$ standard deviations, mid-upper arm circumference (MUAC) below 11.5 cm (age 6 to 59 months), or **bilateral pitting edema**.

| Feature | Marasmus | Kwashiorkor |
|---|---|---|
| Deficit | Total energy (calories and protein) | Relatively greater protein deficit; diet often mostly starch |
| Edema | **None** | **Bilateral pitting edema**, ascites |
| Body weight | Severely low, wasted | May be masked by edema |
| Fat and muscle | Severe loss of subcutaneous fat and muscle ("skin and bones") | Muscle wasting, some fat preserved |
| Face | Wizened, "old man" | Moon face |
| Liver | Small | **Enlarged fatty liver** (decreased apolipoprotein B and lipoprotein export) |
| Skin and hair | Dry, thin | Flaky-paint dermatitis, depigmented sparse hair ("flag sign") |
| Behavior | Alert, irritable | Apathetic |
| Albumin | Normal or mildly low | **Low**, reducing oncotic pressure; oxidative stress and infection contribute |

Mixed forms (marasmic kwashiorkor) occur. Both increase susceptibility to infection, hypoglycemia, hypothermia and electrolyte problems. Treatment of severe acute malnutrition: treat infection and hypoglycemia, **cautious** rehydration (oral rather than IV when possible, low-sodium ORS) because the heart is weak, correct micronutrient deficiencies (vitamin A, zinc, potassium, magnesium), start with a low-protein therapeutic formula and progress to catch-up feeding, using ready-to-use therapeutic food in community programs.

### Refeeding syndrome

Reintroducing carbohydrate after prolonged undernutrition causes an insulin surge: **phosphate, potassium and magnesium** move into cells, producing **hypophosphatemia** (the hallmark), hypokalemia and hypomagnesemia; sodium and fluid retention cause edema and heart failure; thiamine is consumed (risk of Wernicke encephalopathy). Presents days after feeding starts with weakness, arrhythmias, respiratory failure and seizures. Prevent it by identifying at-risk patients (little intake for days, low BMI, alcohol use disorder), starting nutrition slowly, giving **thiamine before and during feeding**, and monitoring and replacing phosphate, potassium and magnesium.

### Nutritional assessment

BMI is weight (kg) divided by height (m) squared: below 18.5 underweight, 18.5 to 24.9 normal, 25 to 29.9 overweight, 30 or more obesity (U.S. adult categories). Albumin and prealbumin reflect inflammation as well as nutrition, so they are poor stand-alone markers.

@example
title: Nitrogen balance in a surgical patient
problem: A patient receives 75 g of protein per day. The 24-hour urinary urea nitrogen is 10 g. What is the nitrogen balance?
step: Convert protein intake to nitrogen: $75 / 6.25 = 12$ g.
step: Estimate losses: UUN plus 4 g gives $10 + 4 = 14$ g.
step: Balance $= 12 - 14 = -2$ g/day, a mild net catabolic state. Increasing protein or treating the stressor would help.

@example
title: Distinguishing the two forms of malnutrition
problem: A 2-year-old has swollen legs, an enlarged liver, flaky skin and sparse, light-colored hair. He is apathetic. Mid-upper arm circumference is 12 cm. Which diagnosis fits, and why is the abdomen swollen?
step: Pitting edema with an enlarged liver and skin and hair changes indicates kwashiorkor, not marasmus, which has no edema.
step: Low albumin lowers plasma oncotic pressure, and increased capillary leak with infection and oxidative stress allow fluid into the interstitium and peritoneum (ascites).
step: The liver is large because reduced apolipoprotein B production impairs export of triglycerides as VLDL.
step: MUAC alone (12 cm) misses him; edema meets the WHO definition of severe acute malnutrition.

@mistakes
- Treating severe acute malnutrition with large IV fluid boluses or high-protein feeds at first, which can precipitate heart failure or refeeding complications.
- Forgetting thiamine when feeding a malnourished patient.
- Using albumin as a reliable marker of nutritional status in acute illness.
- Calling any edematous child with poor intake "marasmus".
