@article
Cells die in two broad ways. **Necrosis** is accidental death after severe injury: the membrane fails, contents leak out, and the leaked contents provoke inflammation. **Apoptosis** is programmed, energy-requiring, tightly regulated death in which the cell dismantles itself and is cleared without inflammation. The first lesson ended at the point of no return; this lesson is about what comes after.

### Necrosis: the patterns

On the slide, necrotic cells show **hypereosinophilic** cytoplasm (loss of RNA and denatured proteins bind eosin) and nuclear changes: **pyknosis** (shrunken, dark nucleus), **karyorrhexis** (fragmentation) and **karyolysis** (fading). The overall tissue pattern depends on the cause.

| Pattern | Typical cause and setting | Key description |
|---|---|---|
| **Coagulative** | Ischemia in solid organs (heart, kidney, spleen); not brain | Cell outlines and tissue architecture preserved for days as pale "ghosts"; firm, pale, wedge-shaped infarct |
| **Liquefactive** | Bacterial or fungal infection (abscess); hypoxic death in the brain | Enzymatic digestion turns tissue into a liquid mass; in an abscess, pus consists of neutrophils and debris |
| **Caseous** | Tuberculosis, some fungi (histoplasmosis) | Soft, white, "cheese-like" debris surrounded by granulomatous inflammation; no tissue architecture left |
| **Fat necrosis** | Acute pancreatitis, trauma to breast or fatty tissue | Lipases split triglycerides; fatty acids combine with calcium to form chalky white **saponification** deposits |
| **Fibrinoid** | Immune-mediated vasculitis, malignant hypertension | Immune complexes and fibrin deposit in vessel walls, giving a bright pink, amorphous appearance |

**Gangrene** is a clinical term for necrosis of a limb or segment, usually from ischemia. **Dry gangrene** is coagulative necrosis that looks shriveled and black. **Wet gangrene** has superimposed bacterial infection, so liquefactive necrosis dominates and the tissue is swollen, foul and may spread. **Gas gangrene** is caused by *Clostridium* species that produce gas in tissue.

Why does the brain differ? Brain tissue is rich in lipid and in lysosomal enzymes of microglia, and has little structural connective tissue, so after ischemic infarction it liquefies and eventually leaves a cystic cavity.

### Apoptosis

Apoptosis occurs physiologically (embryogenesis, removal of self-reactive lymphocytes, turnover of intestinal epithelium) and pathologically (DNA damage, misfolded proteins, some viral infections, cytotoxic T cell killing). Morphology: the cell **shrinks**, cytoplasm becomes dense and eosinophilic, chromatin condenses and fragments, the membrane **blebs**, and the cell breaks into **apoptotic bodies** that are phagocytosed. The plasma membrane stays intact and **there is no inflammation**. DNA is cleaved at internucleosomal sites, which gives a "ladder" pattern on gel electrophoresis.

The execution machinery is the **caspases**, cysteine proteases that cleave after aspartate. Initiator caspases (8, 9) activate executioner caspases (3, 6, 7).

**Intrinsic (mitochondrial) pathway.** Stresses such as DNA damage (sensed by p53), growth factor withdrawal and misfolded proteins tilt the balance of BCL-2 family proteins. Anti-apoptotic members (**BCL-2**, BCL-XL) hold the mitochondrial membrane intact; pro-apoptotic members (**BAX**, **BAK**) form pores. Cytochrome c leaks into the cytosol, binds APAF-1 to form the apoptosome and activates **caspase-9**.

**Extrinsic (death receptor) pathway.** Ligands such as **Fas ligand** or TNF bind death receptors (Fas, TNF receptor type 1). The adaptor FADD activates **caspase-8**. Cytotoxic T cells and NK cells also kill by delivering granzymes through perforin pores, which activates caspases directly.

Clinical correlations: overexpression of BCL-2 prevents apoptosis in **follicular lymphoma**, caused by the translocation t(14;18). Loss of p53 allows cells with DNA damage to survive and is common in cancer. **Councilman bodies** are apoptotic hepatocytes in viral hepatitis. Other regulated forms include **necroptosis** (inflammatory, RIPK-dependent) and **pyroptosis** (inflammasome and caspase-1).

### Necrosis versus apoptosis at a glance

Necrosis: cell swells, membrane lysed, enzymatic digestion, nuclei fade, inflammation, pathologic, passive. Apoptosis: cell shrinks, membrane intact, caspase-driven, nuclear fragments, no inflammation, physiologic or pathologic, active (needs ATP).

@example
title: Matching the pattern of necrosis to the setting
problem: A 64-year-old man with a three-day history of fever and cough has a lung abscess that was drained. A different patient, a 70-year-old woman, has an acute myocardial infarction. Name the pattern of necrosis expected in each, and say how you would tell them apart in tissue.
step: The abscess is a localized bacterial infection. Neutrophils release hydrolytic enzymes that digest the tissue, producing liquefactive necrosis, seen as a cavity of pus containing neutrophils and cellular debris without recognizable architecture.
step: Myocardial infarction is ischemic necrosis of a solid organ. This gives coagulative necrosis: for the first days the dead myocytes keep their outlines, with hypereosinophilic cytoplasm and lost nuclei, and the tissue is firm.
step: The distinguishing feature is architecture. Coagulative necrosis preserves it temporarily; liquefactive necrosis destroys it. A third pattern, caseous necrosis, would show amorphous pink granular debris surrounded by epithelioid histiocytes and giant cells (granuloma), as in tuberculosis.

@mistakes
- Assuming all ischemic necrosis is coagulative: ischemic brain infarcts undergo liquefactive necrosis.
- Describing apoptosis as always pathologic or always inflammatory. It is often physiologic and does not provoke inflammation.
- Linking caseous necrosis to any infection. It is characteristic of tuberculosis and some fungi, with granulomas.
- Confusing fat necrosis (enzymatic, in pancreatitis) with steatosis (fat accumulation inside living cells).
