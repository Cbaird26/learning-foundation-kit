@article
A laboratory result is a measurement with a reference interval, not a verdict. Reference intervals typically cover the central 95% of healthy people, so about 1 in 20 healthy people is flagged abnormal on any single test. Interpret every result in clinical context, compare to prior values, and ask what would change management.

### The basic metabolic panel

The **BMP** reports sodium, potassium, chloride, bicarbonate (total CO$_2$), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine, glucose and usually calcium. Approximate adult reference ranges (they vary by laboratory): sodium 135 to 145 mEq/L, potassium 3.5 to 5.0 mEq/L, chloride 98 to 106 mEq/L, bicarbonate 22 to 28 mEq/L, BUN 7 to 20 mg/dL, creatinine about 0.6 to 1.3 mg/dL, glucose (fasting) 70 to 99 mg/dL, calcium 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL.

**Anion gap** estimates unmeasured anions:

$$\text{AG} = \text{Na}^+ - (\text{Cl}^- + \text{HCO}_3^-),$$

normally about 8 to 12 mEq/L (potassium is not included). A **high anion gap metabolic acidosis** is seen with lactate, ketoacids, uremia, toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), salicylate and others (mnemonics such as MUDPILES or GOLDMARK). A normal-gap (hyperchloremic) acidosis is caused by diarrhea or renal tubular acidosis. Low albumin lowers the expected gap by about 2.5 mEq/L for each 1 g/dL drop in albumin.

**BUN to creatinine ratio** above about 20 suggests prerenal azotemia (volume depletion, heart failure) or gastrointestinal bleeding, and a ratio of 10 to 15 is typical of intrinsic renal disease. A creatinine that doubles from baseline indicates a large fall in glomerular filtration rate, because creatinine rises in inverse proportion to GFR.

**Corrected calcium** accounts for albumin binding:

$$\text{Ca}_{\text{corr}} = \text{Ca}_{\text{measured}} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin in g/dL}).$$

**Calculated osmolality** is $2\,\text{Na}^+ + \text{glucose}/18 + \text{BUN}/2.8$ in mOsm/kg. A measured osmolality well above the calculated value (an osmolar gap) suggests toxic alcohols.

Hyperkalemia: peaked T waves, widened QRS; common causes are renal failure, acidosis, hemolyzed specimen (spurious), potassium-sparing drugs. Hyponatremia is classified by serum osmolality and volume status. Hyperglycemia lowers measured sodium by drawing water out of cells (about 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL of glucose above normal, a convention that varies by source; some use 2.4).

### The complete blood count

The **CBC** gives hemoglobin, hematocrit, red cell indices, white cell count and differential, and platelet count. Approximate adult reference values: hemoglobin 13.5 to 17.5 g/dL in men and 12.0 to 15.5 g/dL in women; MCV 80 to 100 fL; white cells 4,500 to 11,000 per microliter; platelets 150,000 to 400,000 per microliter. The **MCV** is mean corpuscular volume, equal to hematocrit (%) times 10 divided by red cell count (millions per microliter). **RDW** measures variation in cell size.

**Anemia by MCV:**

- **Microcytic (MCV below 80):** iron deficiency (low ferritin, high TIBC, low iron, high RDW), thalassemia (normal or high ferritin, normal RDW, high red cell count), anemia of chronic disease (high ferritin, low TIBC; hepcidin) and sideroblastic anemia (including lead poisoning).
- **Normocytic (80 to 100):** acute blood loss, hemolysis, chronic kidney disease, aplastic anemia, early iron deficiency.
- **Macrocytic (above 100):** megaloblastic (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, with hypersegmented neutrophils) and nonmegaloblastic (alcohol, liver disease, hypothyroidism, some drugs).

The **reticulocyte count** separates underproduction from destruction or loss. A corrected count is reticulocyte percent times patient hematocrit divided by normal hematocrit (about 45). A corrected value above 2 to 3% indicates an appropriate marrow response (hemolysis or bleeding); a low value indicates a production problem. The **Mentzer index** (MCV divided by red cell count in millions) is above 13 in iron deficiency and below 13 in thalassemia trait.

### Test performance

For a test applied to a population: **sensitivity** is the proportion of diseased people who test positive; **specificity** is the proportion of non-diseased who test negative. **Positive predictive value** and **negative predictive value** depend on prevalence. **Likelihood ratios** do not:

$$LR^+ = \frac{\text{sensitivity}}{1-\text{specificity}},\qquad LR^- = \frac{1-\text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}.$$

Pretest odds times the likelihood ratio equals posttest odds. A highly sensitive test, when negative, helps rule out disease (SnNout); a highly specific test, when positive, helps rule in (SpPin).

@example
title: Reading a metabolic panel in a sick patient
problem: A 24-year-old man with vomiting and polyuria has: Na 132 mEq/L, K 5.4, Cl 95, HCO3 8, BUN 30 mg/dL, creatinine 1.6 mg/dL, glucose 620 mg/dL. Calculate the anion gap and corrected sodium-based interpretation, and give the most likely diagnosis.
step: Anion gap = 132 - (95 + 8) = 29 mEq/L, markedly elevated.
step: A very low bicarbonate with high anion gap indicates a high anion gap metabolic acidosis. Glucose of 620 and the history point to diabetic ketoacidosis (ketoacids are the unmeasured anions).
step: BUN to creatinine ratio is 30 / 1.6, about 19, with volume depletion from osmotic diuresis. The measured sodium is low in part because hyperglycemia pulls water out of cells; corrected sodium is about 132 + 1.6 x 5.2, about 140 mEq/L.
step: Potassium appears high because acidosis shifts it out of cells, but total body potassium is depleted, which matters when insulin is given.

@mistakes
- Including potassium in the anion gap, or forgetting that low albumin lowers the baseline gap.
- Reading an elevated BUN to creatinine ratio as proof of renal failure; it usually points to prerenal causes.
- Judging a test only by sensitivity and ignoring prevalence and predictive value.
- Calling every microcytic anemia iron deficiency without ferritin and the clinical context.
