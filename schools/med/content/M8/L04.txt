@article
**Chronic inflammation** lasts weeks to years. It features mononuclear cells (macrophages, lymphocytes, plasma cells), tissue destruction by the inflammatory process itself, and attempts at repair by angiogenesis and fibrosis. It follows persistent infection (tuberculosis, viruses), prolonged exposure to toxic agents (silica, cholesterol in atherosclerosis) and autoimmune disease (rheumatoid arthritis). It may also arise after unresolved acute inflammation.

### The macrophage is the central cell

Monocytes from the blood become tissue macrophages. Activation takes two main paths. **Classical (M1)** activation, by microbial products and **IFN-$\gamma$** from Th1 cells, makes macrophages kill microbes and produce inflammatory mediators. **Alternative (M2)** activation, by IL-4 and IL-13, promotes tissue repair and fibrosis. Macrophages secrete TNF, IL-1 and growth factors. T cells and macrophages amplify each other: Th1 cells make IFN-$\gamma$, Th17 cells recruit neutrophils and monocytes, and **eosinophils** are prominent in parasitic infection and allergy. **Plasma cells** (eccentric nucleus, clock-face chromatin) make antibody.

### Granulomatous inflammation

A **granuloma** is a collection of activated macrophages called **epithelioid histiocytes**, usually with **multinucleated giant cells** (Langhans type has nuclei in a horseshoe arrangement) and a rim of lymphocytes. Th1 cells secrete IFN-$\gamma$, which activates macrophages; **TNF** maintains the granuloma. Treatment with anti-TNF drugs can therefore reactivate latent tuberculosis.

- **Caseating** granulomas (central caseous necrosis): tuberculosis and some fungi (histoplasmosis).
- **Noncaseating** granulomas: sarcoidosis, Crohn disease and berylliosis (cat-scratch disease instead forms stellate granulomas with central neutrophilic necrosis).
- **Foreign-body** granulomas surround indigestible material, such as suture.

### Tissue repair

Repair occurs by **regeneration** (replacement by the same cell type) or by **scar** (connective tissue). Which one depends on cell type:

- **Labile cells** divide continuously: epidermis, gut and bone marrow stem cells. They regenerate well.
- **Stable cells** are quiescent but can re-enter the cell cycle: hepatocytes, renal tubular cells, endothelium.
- **Permanent cells** cannot divide effectively: neurons and cardiac myocytes. Injury heals with scar (glial scar in the brain, fibrous scar in the heart).

Scar repair proceeds as follows:

1. **Hemostasis and inflammation (day 0 to 2):** a platelet plug and fibrin clot; neutrophils at about 24 hours, macrophages by days 2 to 3.
2. **Proliferation (days 3 to 5 onward):** **granulation tissue**, pink and soft with a granular surface, composed of new capillaries (angiogenesis driven by **VEGF**), fibroblasts and loose matrix. Fibroblasts are stimulated by **TGF-$\beta$**, FGF and PDGF and deposit **type III collagen**.
3. **Remodeling (week 2 onward, months):** type III collagen is replaced by stronger **type I collagen** by matrix metalloproteinases (zinc-dependent collagenases). Wound strength is about 10% of normal at 1 week and reaches about 70 to 80% at 3 months; it never reaches 100%.

**Primary intention** describes a clean, closely apposed incision with little tissue loss. **Secondary intention** describes a large wound with a gap, which needs more granulation tissue and shows **wound contraction** by **myofibroblasts**.

### What impairs healing

Infection, diabetes (poor perfusion, impaired leukocyte function), **vitamin C deficiency** (needed for hydroxylation of proline and lysine in collagen), zinc deficiency, glucocorticoids, ischemia, foreign bodies, and mechanical stress. Excess healing also causes disease: **hypertrophic scars** remain within the wound border and may regress; **keloids** grow beyond the border with thick, glassy eosinophilic collagen bundles, and are more common in people with darker skin. **Exuberant granulation tissue** ("proud flesh") protrudes above the surface and blocks re-epithelialization. Excessive fibrosis of organs causes **cirrhosis**, pulmonary fibrosis and strictures.

### Putting the cardinal signs together

In a clinical scenario you should be able to link every sign to its mechanism: redness and warmth to vasodilation, swelling to permeability and exudate, pain to bradykinin and prostaglandins, and the leukocyte infiltrate to selectin-mediated rolling, integrin-mediated adhesion and chemotaxis. Chronic inflammation replaces neutrophils with macrophages and lymphocytes and adds fibrosis.

@example
title: Following a surgical incision through the weeks
problem: A 40-year-old man has an uncomplicated abdominal incision closed with sutures. Describe what the histology would show at day 1, day 4 and week 6, and why the scar is weaker than the original skin.
step: Day 1: a fibrin clot fills the incision. Neutrophils migrate to the wound margins, having rolled, adhered and crossed venules in response to chemoattractants. The epithelium begins to migrate across the gap.
step: Day 4: macrophages have replaced neutrophils. Granulation tissue (new capillaries and fibroblasts) fills the incision, laying down delicate type III collagen.
step: Week 6: the scar is a largely acellular collagen-rich band. Type III collagen has been replaced by type I and cross-linked, but disorganized fibres give a strength of at most about 70 to 80% of unwounded skin.

@mistakes
- Thinking all granulomas are caseating or tuberculous: sarcoidosis and Crohn disease are noncaseating.
- Confusing hypertrophic scars (stay within the wound) with keloids (extend beyond the wound).
- Saying type I collagen forms first. Type III collagen is deposited first and is later replaced by type I.
- Treating cardiac myocytes as stable cells; they are permanent, so infarcts scar.
