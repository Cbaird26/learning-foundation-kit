@article
Normal blood flow depends on fluid staying in vessels, blood staying fluid inside them, and clotting promptly when a vessel is injured. This lesson covers failure in each direction: too much fluid in tissue (edema), clot where it should not form (thrombosis), and the consequences when a clot or other mass travels (embolism and infarction).

### Edema

**Edema** is accumulation of fluid in the interstitium. Fluid movement across the capillary wall follows **Starling forces**:

$$\text{Net filtration} = (P_c - P_i) - \sigma(\pi_c - \pi_i),$$

where $P_c$ and $P_i$ are capillary and interstitial hydrostatic pressures, $\pi_c$ and $\pi_i$ are the oncotic pressures, and $\sigma$ is the reflection coefficient (near 1 for albumin). Edema develops when net filtration rises or lymph drainage fails. Mechanisms:

- **Increased hydrostatic pressure:** heart failure (generalized), deep venous thrombosis (local, one leg), venous obstruction; sodium and water retention in renal disease.
- **Reduced plasma oncotic pressure** (hypoalbuminemia): nephrotic syndrome, cirrhosis, protein malnutrition.
- **Lymphatic obstruction:** filariasis, after axillary node dissection or radiation, and breast carcinoma with a "peau d'orange" skin look.
- **Increased vascular permeability:** inflammation.

**Transudate** (low protein, specific gravity under 1.012) results from hydrostatic or oncotic imbalance; **exudate** (protein-rich, above 1.020) results from permeability change. Dependent pitting edema of the legs is classic for heart failure; periorbital edema in the morning suggests nephrotic or nephritic disease. **Anasarca** is severe generalized edema. In the lung, edema fluid fills alveoli (heart failure, ARDS); in the brain it causes herniation risk.

### Hemostasis and thrombosis

After injury, vasoconstriction is followed by **primary hemostasis**: platelets adhere to exposed collagen through **von Willebrand factor** binding to **GPIb**, are activated, and aggregate by **GPIIb/IIIa** binding fibrinogen. **Secondary hemostasis**: **tissue factor** triggers the coagulation cascade, producing **thrombin**, which converts fibrinogen to fibrin. Counterbalancing it, normal endothelium makes prostacyclin (PGI$_2$), nitric oxide, tissue plasminogen activator, thrombomodulin (activating protein C) and heparin-like molecules that enhance antithrombin.

**Thrombosis** is inappropriate clotting within an intact vessel. **Virchow's triad** lists its causes:

1. **Endothelial injury** (atherosclerotic plaque, hypertension, vasculitis, catheters, cigarette smoke).
2. **Abnormal blood flow:** stasis (immobility, atrial fibrillation, aneurysm, varicose veins) or turbulence.
3. **Hypercoagulability.**

Hypercoagulable states: **factor V Leiden** (the commonest inherited cause; factor V resists inactivation by activated protein C), **prothrombin G20210A** mutation, antithrombin deficiency, protein C or S deficiency (warfarin-induced skin necrosis can be an early sign), **antiphospholipid syndrome** (venous or arterial thrombosis, recurrent miscarriage, prolonged aPTT that does not correct on mixing study, false-positive syphilis test), pregnancy, oral contraceptives, malignancy (Trousseau syndrome, especially pancreatic), nephrotic syndrome (loss of antithrombin), and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.

Arterial and cardiac thrombi form under high flow and are platelet-rich, with **lines of Zahn** (alternating pale layers of platelets and fibrin and darker layers of red cells) that distinguish a true thrombus from a postmortem clot. Venous thrombi form in stasis and are red, rich in fibrin and erythrocytes. A thrombus can **propagate**, **embolize**, **dissolve**, or be **organized** and **recanalized**.

### Embolism

An **embolus** is a detached intravascular mass carried to a distant site. Over 95% are thromboemboli from **deep leg veins** (iliofemoral and popliteal). **Pulmonary embolism** causes dyspnea, pleuritic pain, tachycardia and hypoxemia; a large **saddle embolus** at the bifurcation can cause sudden death, and small ones are often silent because the bronchial arteries also supply lung. Pulmonary infarction is uncommon for that reason. Other emboli:

- **Paradoxical:** venous thrombus crossing a patent foramen ovale into the systemic circulation (stroke).
- **Fat:** after long-bone fracture or orthopedic surgery; 1 to 3 days later, hypoxemia, confusion and a petechial rash.
- **Amniotic fluid:** during labor; dyspnea, shock and DIC; fetal squames and lanugo in maternal pulmonary vessels.
- **Air:** more than very small volumes can obstruct; decompression sickness (nitrogen bubbles) in divers.
- Tumor, septic and atheroembolic (cholesterol clefts) emboli.

### Infarction

An **infarct** is ischemic necrosis from occlusion of arterial supply or venous drainage. **White (pale) infarcts** follow arterial occlusion in solid organs with a single blood supply (heart, spleen, kidney) and are wedge-shaped. **Red (hemorrhagic) infarcts** occur with venous occlusion (testicular torsion, ovarian torsion), in loose tissues (lung), with dual circulation (liver, lung, small bowel) or after reperfusion. Brain infarcts become liquefactive.

@example
title: Applying Virchow's triad to a post-operative leg swelling
problem: A 66-year-old woman with metastatic pancreatic cancer, three days after hip surgery, has a swollen painful left calf. Identify the triad elements and the most feared complication.
step: Stasis: immobilization after surgery slows venous flow in the deep leg veins.
step: Hypercoagulability: malignancy (Trousseau syndrome) and the post-surgical state raise procoagulant activity.
step: Endothelial injury: the surgery itself and venous trauma during the hip operation damage the endothelium. All three limbs of the triad are present.
step: The feared complication is pulmonary embolism, from detachment of the deep-vein thrombus. Typical findings are sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia and hypoxemia.

@mistakes
- Expecting lines of Zahn in a postmortem clot. They mark a thrombus that formed in flowing blood before death, and they are most prominent in arterial and cardiac thrombi.
- Believing all pulmonary emboli cause infarcts; most do not because of the bronchial circulation.
- Calling heart failure edema an exudate: it is a transudate from raised hydrostatic pressure.
- Mixing up the parts of Virchow's triad: stasis is a change in blood flow, while hypercoagulability is a change in the blood itself.

@practice num
prompt: A patient with cirrhosis has capillary hydrostatic pressure of 30 mm Hg, interstitial hydrostatic pressure of 0 mm Hg, capillary oncotic pressure of 15 mm Hg, interstitial oncotic pressure of 5 mm Hg, and reflection coefficient 1. What is the net filtration pressure in mm Hg?
answer: 20
check: (30 - 0) - 1 * (15 - 5)
tol: 0.1
unit: mm Hg
hint: Use net filtration = (Pc - Pi) - sigma (pic - pii).
solution: (30 - 0) - (15 - 5) = 30 - 10 = 20 mm Hg, a large outward force explained by low albumin.
