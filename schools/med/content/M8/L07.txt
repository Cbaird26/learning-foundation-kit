@article
Genetic disease arises from mutations in the genome. Some are inherited through germ cells; others arise de novo or in somatic cells. The pattern of inheritance often tells you the type of protein affected. Review from your genetics course: each person carries two copies of each autosomal gene, one from each parent.

### Mendelian patterns

**Autosomal dominant** disease appears in every generation and affects males and females equally, and an affected parent transmits to about half of children. It often involves structural proteins or receptors: **Marfan syndrome** (fibrillin-1, *FBN1*; tall stature, arachnodactyly, lens subluxation upward, aortic root dilation and dissection), **familial hypercholesterolemia** (LDL receptor), **neurofibromatosis type 1** (*NF1* on chromosome 17; café-au-lait spots, Lisch nodules), **autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease**, **Huntington disease**. Penetrance may be incomplete and expressivity variable.

**Autosomal recessive** disease typically skips generations; affected children have unaffected carrier parents, and each pregnancy has a 25% risk. It often involves enzymes: **cystic fibrosis** (*CFTR* on chromosome 7, most commonly deletion F508 causing misfolding; thick secretions, pancreatic insufficiency, recurrent pneumonia, meconium ileus, elevated sweat chloride), **phenylketonuria** (phenylalanine hydroxylase deficiency; screened at birth), **sickle cell disease**, **Tay–Sachs disease**, **Gaucher disease** (glucocerebrosidase).

**X-linked recessive** disease affects mostly males and is never transmitted father to son. Examples: **Duchenne muscular dystrophy** (dystrophin; calf pseudohypertrophy, Gowers sign), hemophilia A and B, G6PD deficiency, Lesch–Nyhan syndrome. A carrier mother has a 50% chance of an affected son.

**Mitochondrial** inheritance is exclusively maternal: all children of an affected mother can be affected; an affected father transmits to none. Variable expression comes from **heteroplasmy**. Examples: MELAS and Leber hereditary optic neuropathy.

### Trinucleotide repeat disorders and anticipation

Expansion of repeats causes disease, and repeats often expand across generations, so symptoms appear earlier and more severely: **anticipation**. **Fragile X syndrome** (CGG repeat in *FMR1*, more than 200 repeats leads to methylation and silencing; intellectual disability, long face, large ears, macroorchidism); **Huntington disease** (CAG, chromosome 4, striatal atrophy; paternal transmission favors anticipation); **myotonic dystrophy** (CTG); **Friedreich ataxia** (GAA, autosomal recessive).

### Imprinting

For a few genes only the maternal or paternal copy is active. A deletion of the paternal 15q11-q13 causes **Prader–Willi syndrome** (hypotonia, hyperphagia and obesity); deletion of the maternal copy causes **Angelman syndrome** (ataxia, seizures, inappropriate laughter).

### Cytogenetic disorders

- **Down syndrome (trisomy 21):** about 95% from meiotic nondisjunction, risk rising with maternal age; about 4% from Robertsonian translocation (recurrence risk higher); about 1% mosaic. Findings: flat facies, epicanthal folds, single palmar crease, hypotonia, **endocardial cushion (atrioventricular septal) defects**, duodenal atresia, Hirschsprung disease, increased risk of acute leukemia and early Alzheimer disease.
- **Edwards syndrome (trisomy 18):** clenched fists with overlapping fingers, rocker-bottom feet, severe cardiac defects; death in infancy.
- **Patau syndrome (trisomy 13):** holoprosencephaly, cleft lip and palate, polydactyly.
- **Turner syndrome (45,X):** short stature, webbed neck, cystic hygroma, **coarctation of the aorta** or bicuspid aortic valve, streak ovaries and primary amenorrhea; no Barr body.
- **Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY):** tall, small firm testes, gynecomastia, infertility, one Barr body.
- **22q11.2 deletion (DiGeorge):** thymic and parathyroid hypoplasia (T-cell deficiency, hypocalcemia), conotruncal heart defects.

### Hardy–Weinberg and risk

If $p$ and $q$ are allele frequencies, genotype frequencies are $p^2$, $2pq$ and $q^2$. For an autosomal recessive disease, the incidence is $q^2$, and the carrier frequency is $2pq$ (approximately $2q$). Assumptions: random mating, no selection, no migration, no new mutation, large population.

### Neonatal pathology

**Respiratory distress syndrome** of the premature infant is caused by surfactant deficiency (type II pneumocytes mature around 35 weeks) with hyaline membranes on histology; a lecithin-to-sphingomyelin ratio above 2 in amniotic fluid suggests maturity. Antenatal corticosteroids (for example betamethasone) accelerate maturation. Other prematurity complications: necrotizing enterocolitis, intraventricular hemorrhage, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, retinopathy of prematurity.

@example
title: Carrier frequency from disease incidence
problem: Cystic fibrosis is autosomal recessive. Suppose the incidence in a population is 1 in 2,500 births. What is the frequency of the CF allele, and what fraction of people are carriers?
step: Incidence equals $q^2 = 1/2500 = 0.0004$.
step: Take the square root: $q = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$. Then $p = 1 - q = 0.98$.
step: Carrier frequency is $2pq = 2(0.98)(0.02) = 0.0392$, about 4%, or roughly 1 in 25.

@mistakes
- Using $2pq$ as the disease incidence; for a recessive disease the incidence is $q^2$.
- Assuming that an affected father cannot transmit an X-linked recessive trait to daughters; daughters become carriers (father to son transmission is the thing that never occurs).
- Mixing Prader–Willi (paternal deletion) with Angelman (maternal deletion).
- Forgetting that mitochondrial disease passes from mothers to all offspring.
