title: Pathology case conference
overview: You will present three supplied cases to an imaginary case conference. For each case you link the gross and microscopic findings to the mechanism of disease and to the patient's clinical course. The cases draw on injury and death, inflammation and repair, hemodynamics, and neoplasia.
goal: Explain each case as a chain: cause, mechanism, morphology, clinical consequence.
goal: Describe gross and microscopic findings accurately in words, as you would dictate them to a pathologist's report.
goal: Compare the cases to show how the same principles (hypoxia, inflammation, repair, growth control) recur in different organs.
step: Read the three cases below slowly and underline every clue (age, risk factor, symptom, finding, timing).
 Case A. A 68-year-old woman, five days after a hip fracture repair, collapses with sudden dyspnea and chest pain. Heart rate is 124 beats per minute, blood pressure 82/50 mm Hg, oxygen saturation 84%, and the neck veins are distended. Her left calf was swollen for two days. She dies despite resuscitation. At autopsy a large, dark, coiled, rubbery mass straddles the bifurcation of the main pulmonary artery. The left femoral vein contains a similar mass that is attached to the wall. Microscopy of the vein thrombus shows alternating pale layers of platelets and fibrin and darker layers of red cells. The right ventricle is dilated. The liver shows centrilobular congestion with focal necrosis.
 Case B. A 63-year-old man with a 40 pack-year smoking history has a crushing chest pain for 3 hours. Troponin is elevated and the ECG shows ST elevation in the anterior leads. He is treated, but on day 5 he suddenly develops hypotension (blood pressure 74/48 mm Hg), muffled heart sounds and jugular venous distension and dies. Autopsy shows a tear in the anterior wall of the left ventricle with 400 mL of blood in the pericardial sac. The infarct area is soft, yellow-tan and surrounded by a hyperemic border. Microscopy shows coagulative necrosis, loss of nuclei, and a dense infiltrate of macrophages removing dead myocytes, with early granulation tissue at the edge. A coronary artery shows an eccentric atherosclerotic plaque with a recent occlusive thrombus.
 Case C. A 58-year-old man, with a 45 pack-year smoking history, presents with cough, hemoptysis, weight loss and confusion. Serum calcium is 13.4 mg/dL, albumin 4.0 g/dL, and parathyroid hormone is low. A chest CT shows a 5 cm central mass in the left main bronchus with enlarged hilar nodes, and the liver contains several nodules. Bronchoscopic biopsy shows nests of atypical cells with intercellular bridges and keratin pearls. The adjacent bronchial epithelium shows squamous metaplasia and areas of dysplasia. Staging shows lymph node and liver metastases.
step: For each case, write a one-paragraph problem representation naming the key finding and the likely diagnosis.

@review
### Week-by-week key ideas

- **Week 1, injury and adaptation.** Hypertrophy, hyperplasia, atrophy, metaplasia; reversible injury (swelling, fatty change) vs irreversible (mitochondrial damage, membrane rupture); ischemia-reperfusion and reactive oxygen species.
- **Week 2, cell death.** Coagulative, liquefactive, caseous, fat and fibrinoid necrosis with their settings; apoptosis, intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, BCL-2, caspases.
- **Week 3, acute inflammation.** Vasodilation and permeability; selectins, integrins, chemotaxis; mediators; leukocyte defects.
- **Week 4, chronic inflammation and repair.** Macrophages, granulomas, labile, stable and permanent cells, granulation tissue, collagen III to I, keloid versus hypertrophic scar.
- **Week 5, hemodynamics.** Starling forces, Virchow's triad, hypercoagulable states, emboli, red versus white infarcts.
- **Week 6, shock.** Four types by CO, SVR and filling pressures; MAP and SVR; septic shock; stages and organ injury.
- **Week 7, genetic and pediatric disease.** Inheritance patterns, repeat expansions, imprinting, chromosomal syndromes, Hardy–Weinberg.
- **Week 8, immunopathology and amyloid.** Hypersensitivity types I to IV, autoantibodies, rejection; AL, AA, ATTR, beta-2 microglobulin, A-beta.
- **Week 9, neoplasia biology.** Nomenclature, invasion and metastasis, oncogenes, tumor suppressors, two-hit hypothesis, viruses, carcinogens.
- **Week 10, grading, staging, markers.** Grade is cell appearance; stage is spread (TNM); markers monitor, they do not screen; PPV depends on prevalence.
- **Week 11, environmental and nutritional.** Tobacco, alcohol, acetaminophen, lead, carbon monoxide, marasmus vs kwashiorkor, vitamins.
- **Week 12, laboratory medicine.** BMP, anion gap, corrected calcium, CBC by MCV, Mentzer index, likelihood ratios.

### Common errors

Mixing hyperplasia with hypertrophy; calling every ischemic infarct coagulative (brain is liquefactive); confusing rolling with adhesion; forgetting that cardiac myocytes are permanent cells; mixing grade with stage; treating tumor markers as screening tests; trusting a pulse oximeter in carbon monoxide poisoning; using prevalence incorrectly in predictive values.

prompt: A 58-year-old woman with septic shock from pneumonia has a blood pressure of 84/48 mm Hg, a central venous pressure of 6 mm Hg and a cardiac output of 7.5 L/min. Using MAP = diastolic pressure plus one third of the pulse pressure, calculate her systemic vascular resistance in dyn.s/cm^5.
answer: 576
check: ((48 + (84 - 48) / 3) - 6) / 7.5 * 80
tol: 1%
unit: dyn·s/cm^5
hint: Find MAP first.
hint: SVR = (MAP - CVP) / CO times 80.
solution: Pulse pressure is 36, one third is 12, so MAP is 60 mm Hg. (60 - 6) / 7.5 = 7.2; 7.2 x 80 = 576 dyn.s/cm^5, which is low, consistent with distributive shock.

prompt: A 66-year-old man with metastatic pancreatic cancer develops sudden dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia and an oxygen saturation of 86% one week after abdominal surgery. His left calf is swollen. Which combination best explains the sequence of events?
choice*: Hypercoagulability and stasis lead to deep venous thrombosis, then pulmonary embolism that obstructs flow and causes hypoxemia
choice: Endothelial injury in the lung produces a thrombus that causes cardiogenic shock
choice: Fat droplets from marrow produce a petechial rash and brain emboli
choice: Antiphospholipid antibodies cause arterial thrombosis of the leg and a paradoxical embolus
choice: Pulmonary edema from raised left atrial pressure causes the hypoxemia
hint: Use Virchow's triad for the leg, then follow the clot.
solution: Malignancy (Trousseau syndrome) and postoperative immobilization cause hypercoagulability and stasis. A deep leg vein thrombus embolizes to the pulmonary artery, causing a ventilation-perfusion mismatch and hypoxemia; massive embolism can cause obstructive shock.

prompt: A 5-year-old girl has anemia with hemoglobin 9.5 g/dL, a red cell count of 5.6 million per microliter and an MCV of 62 fL. Her serum ferritin is normal. Calculate the Mentzer index (MCV divided by red cell count, with the count in millions per microliter) to two decimal places.
answer: 11.07
check: 62 / 5.6
tol: 0.05
hint: Divide MCV by the red cell count in millions.
solution: 62 / 5.6 = 11.07, below 13, which favors thalassemia trait over iron deficiency, consistent with a high red cell count and normal ferritin.

@exam self
prompt: A 70-year-old man has a myocardial infarction. On day 6 he becomes hypotensive with jugular venous distension and muffled heart sounds. Explain, in sequence, the histologic stage of his infarct on day 6, why this was a time of increased risk, the type of shock that follows, and the underlying principle of healing that explains the vulnerable period.
solution: By day 6 the infarct is in the phase of macrophage removal of necrotic myocardium; neutrophils are fading and early granulation tissue is only beginning at the edges. The dead muscle has been partly digested and not yet replaced by collagen, so the wall is at its weakest between about days 3 and 14. Rupture of the free wall causes blood to fill the pericardial sac, producing cardiac tamponade, which is obstructive shock (high central venous pressure, low cardiac output, high systemic vascular resistance). The principle is that repair of tissue that cannot regenerate (permanent cardiac myocytes) is by granulation and scar, and the replacement of necrotic tissue passes through a weak intermediate stage before type I collagen strengthens the scar.
rubric: Identifies macrophage predominance with early granulation tissue on day 6.
rubric: Explains the weak wall because necrotic tissue is removed before collagen replaces it.
rubric: Names tamponade as obstructive shock and gives its hemodynamics.
rubric: Links the healing sequence to cardiac myocytes being permanent cells.
hint: Recall the histologic changes at 1 to 3 days, 3 to 7 days and 1 to 2 weeks.
hint: Think about which structure fills the pericardial sac and what it does to ventricular filling.
