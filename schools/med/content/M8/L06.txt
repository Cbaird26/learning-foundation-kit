@article
**Shock** is a state of systemic hypoperfusion: oxygen delivery fails to meet tissue demand. Cells switch to anaerobic metabolism, lactate accumulates, and the injury cascade from Lesson 1 begins. Hypotension is common but not required for the diagnosis, especially early.

### Hemodynamic vocabulary

$$\text{CO} = \text{HR}\times\text{SV},\qquad \text{MAP} = \text{DBP} + \tfrac13(\text{SBP}-\text{DBP}),\qquad \text{SVR} = \frac{\text{MAP}-\text{CVP}}{\text{CO}}\times 80.$$

With CO in L/min and pressures in mm Hg, SVR comes out in dyn·s/cm$^5$ (normal about 800 to 1200). MAP also equals CO times SVR (neglecting CVP). Organs need a MAP of roughly 65 mm Hg or more for perfusion. A heart rate divided by systolic pressure (the **shock index**) above about 0.9 is concerning; the normal range is about 0.5 to 0.7.

### Four categories

| Type | Examples | CO | SVR | Filling pressures (CVP, PCWP) | Skin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Hypovolemic** | Hemorrhage, severe vomiting or diarrhea, burns | Low | High | Low | Cold, clammy |
| **Cardiogenic** | Myocardial infarction, arrhythmia, acute valvular failure | Low | High | High | Cold, clammy |
| **Obstructive** | Cardiac tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive pulmonary embolism | Low | High | CVP high; PCWP low in PE, high or equalized in tamponade | Cold |
| **Distributive** | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic (spinal cord injury) | Often high early (low in neurogenic) | Low | Low or normal | Warm early |

**Neurogenic shock** (cord injury above about T6) causes loss of sympathetic tone: hypotension with **bradycardia** and warm skin. **Anaphylactic shock** is IgE and mast cell-mediated release of histamine and other mediators causing vasodilation and permeability; the first-line treatment is intramuscular epinephrine (typical adult dose about 0.3 to 0.5 mg of the 1 mg/mL solution in the anterolateral thigh; a dose for study, check current references).

### Septic shock

**Sepsis** (Sepsis-3 definition) is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized as an increase of 2 or more points in the SOFA score. **Septic shock** is sepsis in which, despite adequate fluid resuscitation, vasopressors are needed to keep MAP at 65 mm Hg or higher and serum lactate remains above 2 mmol/L. Pathogenesis: bacterial products, such as lipopolysaccharide of Gram-negative bacteria (through TLR4) and components of Gram-positive bacteria, activate macrophages to release TNF, IL-1, IL-6 and other mediators. Effects: widespread vasodilation (nitric oxide), endothelial activation and leak, activation of coagulation with possible **disseminated intravascular coagulation**, and myocardial depression with metabolic derangement. Norepinephrine is the usual first-line vasopressor. Meningococcemia can cause bilateral adrenal hemorrhage (Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome).

### Stages

1. **Nonprogressive (compensated):** baroreceptor-driven sympathetic activation, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone and ADH release raise heart rate, vasoconstrict, and conserve volume; organs are perfused.
2. **Progressive:** tissue hypoxia, lactic acidosis, worsening perfusion, arteriolar failure, organ dysfunction.
3. **Irreversible:** cell injury beyond repair; even restoring pressure cannot reverse it.

### Morphology of shock

Hypoxia affects the most vulnerable cells: **acute tubular necrosis** of the kidney, ischemic encephalopathy (watershed infarcts of the brain), **centrilobular** necrosis of the liver, ischemic mucosal injury of the gut, and diffuse alveolar damage (ARDS, "shock lung"). The heart shows subendocardial hemorrhage and necrosis.

@example
title: Comparing two patients in shock
problem: Patient A is a 24-year-old man with a stab wound, cold clammy skin, HR 130, BP 80/50, low CVP. Patient B is a 58-year-old woman with fever, warm flushed skin, HR 120, BP 80/50 and low CVP. Calculate MAP for both and state each shock type.
step: MAP = diastolic + one third of pulse pressure = 50 + (80 - 50)/3 = 50 + 10 = 60 mm Hg for each, below the 65 target.
step: Patient A has low filling pressures, cold skin and a bleeding source: hypovolemic shock, with compensatory vasoconstriction (high SVR).
step: Patient B has warm skin, tachycardia and fever with low filling pressures: distributive (septic) shock, with vasodilation (low SVR) and often an early high cardiac output.
step: The comparison shows why skin temperature and SVR separate the types when blood pressure is identical.

@mistakes
- Assuming all shock has cold skin; early distributive shock is warm.
- Expecting high CVP in hypovolemia; it is low, whereas cardiogenic and obstructive shock have high CVP.
- Stating that hypotension is required for the diagnosis; it is a late sign.
- Forgetting bradycardia in neurogenic shock, where unopposed vagal tone dominates.

@practice num
prompt: A patient has a blood pressure of 80/50 mm Hg. What is the mean arterial pressure in mm Hg?
answer: 60
check: 50 + (80 - 50) / 3
tol: 0.5
unit: mm Hg
hint: MAP = DBP + one third of the pulse pressure.
solution: Pulse pressure is 30, one third is 10, so MAP = 50 + 10 = 60 mm Hg.

@practice num
prompt: A patient in septic shock has a MAP of 55 mm Hg, a central venous pressure of 7 mm Hg and a cardiac output of 6 L/min. Calculate the systemic vascular resistance in dyn.s/cm^5.
answer: 640
check: (55 - 7) / 6 * 80
tol: 1
unit: dyn·s/cm^5
hint: SVR = (MAP - CVP) / CO times 80.
solution: (55 - 7)/6 = 8; 8 x 80 = 640 dyn.s/cm^5, which is low, consistent with vasodilation.

choice: Warm, flushed skin
choice: High pulmonary capillary wedge pressure
hint: The heart is underfilled; the body vasoconstricts to compensate.
