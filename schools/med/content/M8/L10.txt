@article
Once a cancer is diagnosed, two questions follow: how abnormal do the cells look, and how far has the disease spread? The first is **grade**; the second is **stage**. Together with tumor markers and molecular tests, they determine prognosis and treatment.

### Grading

**Grade** is a pathologist's estimate of aggressiveness based on the microscopic appearance: the degree of **differentiation**, nuclear atypia and the number of mitoses. It is usually reported as well differentiated (grade 1), moderately differentiated (grade 2) or poorly differentiated (grade 3), with undifferentiated or anaplastic tumors the worst. Grading is tumor specific:

- **Breast (Nottingham/Elston–Ellis):** score 1 to 3 each for tubule formation, nuclear pleomorphism and mitotic count; the total of 3 to 9 gives grade 1 (3 to 5), grade 2 (6 to 7) or grade 3 (8 to 9).
- **Prostate (Gleason, now reported as Grade Groups 1 to 5):** the two most prevalent architectural patterns are added (for example 3 + 4 = 7). Score 6 is Grade Group 1; 3 + 4 is Grade Group 2; 4 + 3 is Grade Group 3; 8 is Grade Group 4; 9 to 10 is Grade Group 5.
- **Soft tissue sarcomas and others** use their own systems combining differentiation, mitoses and necrosis.

### Staging

**Stage** describes the **anatomic extent** of disease. The **TNM** system (American Joint Committee on Cancer, 8th edition) records:

- **T**: size or local extent of the primary tumor (T1 to T4; Tis for in situ).
- **N**: regional lymph node involvement (N0 none; N1 to N3 increasing).
- **M**: distant metastasis (M0 none; M1 present).

TNM categories are grouped into stages I to IV; stage IV usually means distant metastasis (M1). Pathologic stage (pTNM) comes from the resected specimen; clinical stage (cTNM) comes from examination and imaging. Some cancers (breast, thyroid, prostate) add biomarkers or age to stage groupings.

**Stage is generally the more powerful predictor of survival and the main determinant of treatment, but grade adds independent information within a stage.** Grading depends on the pathologist, staging on the extent of disease. Examples: a small, well-differentiated carcinoma with no nodal spread (low grade, early stage) carries an excellent prognosis; a poorly differentiated tumor can still be curable if it is localized.

### Tumor markers

Tumor markers are molecules (proteins, hormones, enzymes, antigens) made by or in response to cancer. Because most are also produced in benign states and are absent in many early cancers, **most lack the sensitivity and specificity to screen or diagnose**. Their proper uses are **monitoring response to therapy, detecting recurrence, staging or prognosis, and, for some, supporting a diagnosis of a specific tumor type**.

| Marker | Associated tumor | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **PSA** | Prostate adenocarcinoma | Also raised in BPH, prostatitis; screening is debated and individualized by shared decision-making; used to monitor after treatment |
| **AFP** | Hepatocellular carcinoma, yolk sac tumor | Also raised in pregnancy and open neural tube defects |
| **CEA** | Colorectal and other adenocarcinomas | Monitor recurrence; raised in smokers |
| **CA-125** | Epithelial ovarian cancer | Monitor response; nonspecific (endometriosis, menstruation) |
| **CA 19-9** | Pancreatic adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma | Monitoring, not screening |
| **Beta-hCG** | Gestational trophoblastic disease, choriocarcinoma, germ cell tumors | Very sensitive for response |
| **Calcitonin** | Medullary thyroid carcinoma | MEN2 screening in carriers |
| **Thyroglobulin** | Differentiated thyroid cancer | After thyroidectomy, recurrence |
| **LDH** | Germ cell tumors, lymphoma | Nonspecific; prognostic |
| **S-100** | Melanoma, nerve sheath tumors | Immunohistochemical marker |
| **Chromogranin** | Neuroendocrine tumors | |

Immunohistochemistry helps identify the tissue of origin: cytokeratin (epithelial), vimentin (mesenchymal), desmin (muscle), GFAP (glia), CD45 (leukocytes). Predictive markers guide therapy: estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor and HER2 in breast cancer; *BRAF* in melanoma.

### Paraneoplastic syndromes

Tumors may cause distant effects by ectopic hormones or immune cross-reactivity: **small cell lung cancer** (ACTH causing Cushing syndrome, ADH causing SIADH, Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome); **squamous cell carcinoma of the lung** (PTH-related peptide causing hypercalcemia); **renal cell carcinoma** (erythropoietin causing polycythemia); **hepatocellular carcinoma** (erythropoietin); Trousseau syndrome (migratory thrombophlebitis with pancreatic cancer); cachexia (TNF).

### Using test performance

The **positive predictive value** of a marker depends on prevalence:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens}\times\text{prev}}{\text{sens}\times\text{prev} + (1-\text{spec})(1-\text{prev})}.$$

In low-prevalence populations most positive results are false positives, which is one reason markers are poor screening tools.

@example
title: Grade, stage and marker in one patient
problem: A 52-year-old woman has a 1.8 cm invasive breast carcinoma. Tubule formation score 3, nuclear pleomorphism score 2 and mitotic count score 2. One axillary node has a 4 mm metastasis, and no distant disease is found. Report the Nottingham grade and give the TNM categories.
step: Add the three scores: 3 + 2 + 2 = 7, which is grade 2 (6 to 7), a moderately differentiated tumor.
step: A tumor larger than 1 cm but not more than 2 cm is T1c.
step: One to three involved axillary nodes with metastasis larger than 2 mm is N1. No distant disease means M0. So the tumor is T1c N1 M0.
step: The grade describes the cells; the stage describes spread. Stage rather than grade mainly guides systemic therapy and prognosis, along with receptors and HER2.

@mistakes
- Using "grade" and "stage" interchangeably. Grade is microscopic appearance, stage is anatomic extent.
- Using a tumor marker to screen the general population or to establish a diagnosis by itself.
- Assuming a normal marker excludes cancer, or that an elevated PSA proves prostate cancer.
- Forgetting AFP rises in hepatocellular carcinoma and in germ cell tumors, but also in pregnancy.

@practice num
prompt: A tumor marker has a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 95%. The cancer prevalence in the tested population is 2%. What is the positive predictive value, as a percentage, to the nearest whole number?
answer: 27
check: (0.90 * 0.02) / (0.90 * 0.02 + 0.05 * 0.98) * 100
tol: 1
unit: %
hint: Use the PPV formula with sensitivity times prevalence in the numerator.
hint: False positives = (1 - specificity) times (1 - prevalence).
solution: True positives 0.018; false positives 0.05 x 0.98 = 0.049; PPV = 0.018 / 0.067 = 0.269, about 27%.
