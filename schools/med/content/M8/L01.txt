@article
Pathology asks four questions about any disease: what is the **cause** (etiology), how does it develop (**pathogenesis**), what structural changes result (**morphology**), and what are the clinical consequences. Cell injury is where all four begin. Recall from your cell biology course that a cell keeps its internal environment constant using ATP-driven pumps, an intact membrane and functioning mitochondria. Disease disturbs one or more of these.

### Adapt, injure, die

A cell faced with stress has three possible fates. If the stress is mild or physiologic, the cell **adapts**: it changes its size, number or type so that it can survive in a new steady state. If the stress is too severe, or the adaptive capacity is exceeded, the cell is **injured**. Injury is **reversible** if the stressor is removed in time and the cell returns to normal. If not, the cell passes a point of no return and dies (next lesson).

### Adaptations

- **Hypertrophy** is an increase in cell size, and therefore organ size, from more structural proteins and organelles. It occurs in cells that cannot divide efficiently, such as cardiac and skeletal muscle. Example: the left ventricle thickens in response to systemic hypertension or aortic stenosis.
- **Hyperplasia** is an increase in cell number, requiring cells able to divide. Physiologic examples are the proliferating breast epithelium at puberty and the endometrium under estrogen. Pathologic examples are endometrial hyperplasia from excess estrogen and benign prostatic hyperplasia. Hyperplasia is controlled and stops when the stimulus stops, which is why it is not itself cancer, although pathologic hyperplasia is a fertile soil for it. The gravid uterus shows both hypertrophy and hyperplasia of smooth muscle.
- **Atrophy** is a decrease in cell size and number from reduced workload, denervation, loss of blood supply, inadequate nutrition or loss of endocrine stimulation. Mechanisms include decreased protein synthesis and increased degradation by the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway and autophagy.
- **Metaplasia** is the reversible replacement of one differentiated cell type by another that is better suited to the stress. Chronic gastroesophageal reflux changes the lower esophageal squamous epithelium into intestinal-type columnar epithelium with goblet cells (**Barrett esophagus**), which predisposes to adenocarcinoma. Cigarette smoke turns bronchial columnar epithelium into squamous epithelium. Vitamin A deficiency also causes squamous metaplasia. Metaplasia arises by reprogramming of stem cells, not by transformation of mature cells.
- **Dysplasia** is disordered growth with loss of uniformity and architecture. It is not a true adaptation; it is a preneoplastic change.

### Causes of injury

Hypoxia and ischemia are the most common. Others include toxins, infections, immune reactions, genetic defects, nutritional imbalance, physical agents (trauma, temperature, radiation) and aging. **Hypoxia** is low oxygen; **ischemia** is reduced blood supply and also deprives the tissue of nutrients and removal of waste, so it injures faster than pure hypoxia.

### Sequence of ischemic injury

1. Reduced oxidative phosphorylation lowers ATP. The Na$^+$/K$^+$-ATPase fails, so Na$^+$ and water enter and the cell swells (**cellular swelling**, the earliest morphologic change of reversible injury).
2. Anaerobic glycolysis raises lactate and lowers intracellular pH, which clumps nuclear chromatin.
3. Failure of the Ca$^{2+}$ pump lets calcium accumulate in the cytosol, which activates phospholipases, proteases, endonucleases and ATPases.
4. Ribosomes detach from the rough endoplasmic reticulum and protein synthesis falls. Lipid accumulates, producing **fatty change** in hepatocytes.
5. The point of no return is marked by irreversible **mitochondrial damage** (permeability transition and loss of membrane potential) and by profound loss of membrane function. Lysosomal enzymes leak, and proteins such as troponin and creatine kinase-MB escape into the blood.

Light microscopy of reversible injury shows swollen, pale cells with vacuoles; electron microscopy shows blebs and swollen mitochondria. Irreversible injury shows large flocculent densities in mitochondria, membrane defects, and nuclear changes (pyknosis, karyorrhexis, karyolysis).

### Reperfusion and free radicals

Restoring blood flow can paradoxically worsen injury because it delivers oxygen that generates **reactive oxygen species** (ROS): superoxide, hydrogen peroxide and the hydroxyl radical. ROS cause lipid peroxidation of membranes, protein oxidation and DNA damage. Defenses include **superoxide dismutase**, **catalase**, **glutathione peroxidase**, and antioxidants such as vitamins E and C. In carbon tetrachloride poisoning, the CCl$_3$ free radical produced by cytochrome P-450 causes fatty change and necrosis of the liver.

### Intracellular accumulations

Cells can accumulate normal or abnormal substances. **Steatosis** is fat in parenchymal cells (alcohol, obesity, diabetes). **Lipofuscin** is a yellow-brown "wear-and-tear" pigment of lipid peroxidation seen in atrophic aging organs. **Hemosiderin** is iron stored in ferritin aggregates and is golden-brown on H&E and blue with Prussian blue stain; heart failure cells are hemosiderin-laden macrophages in the lung.

@example
title: Barrett esophagus in a patient with reflux
problem: A 58-year-old man with 15 years of untreated heartburn has an endoscopic biopsy of the distal esophagus. Normal esophagus is lined by stratified squamous epithelium. The biopsy shows columnar epithelium with goblet cells and no nuclear atypia. Name the adaptation, state its mechanism and name the main risk.
step: Identify what changed. One mature, differentiated epithelium (squamous) has been replaced by another (intestinal-type columnar). Replacement of one differentiated cell type by another is metaplasia, not hyperplasia (more cells of the same type) or dysplasia (atypia, which is absent here).
step: State the stimulus and mechanism. Repeated exposure to refluxed gastric acid injures squamous cells. Columnar epithelium resists acid better, so reprogramming of stem cells produces it, as an adaptation.
step: Name the risk. The metaplastic epithelium can acquire dysplasia and progress to esophageal adenocarcinoma, so the patient needs surveillance endoscopy. The change is potentially reversible only if the stimulus is removed, and in practice it often persists.

@mistakes
- Calling any increase in organ size "hyperplasia": decide whether the cells are bigger (hypertrophy) or more numerous (hyperplasia), or both.
- Thinking metaplasia is malignant. It is a reversible adaptation, although it increases cancer risk if the stimulus continues.
- Naming nuclear changes (pyknosis, karyolysis) as signs of reversible injury; swelling and fatty change are reversible, nuclear dissolution is not.

@practice self
prompt: A 55-year-old man has long-standing uncontrolled hypertension. Echocardiography shows concentric thickening of the left ventricular wall. Explain why this occurs by hypertrophy and not by hyperplasia, and say what can happen if the stress continues.
hint: Ask whether cardiac muscle cells can divide to add new cells.
hint: Then describe what the existing cells do to cope with extra work.
solution: Cardiac myocytes are terminally differentiated and have very limited capacity to divide, so the heart cannot meet increased afterload by adding cells. Instead each myocyte synthesizes more contractile proteins and organelles and gets bigger, thickening the wall. If the pressure overload persists, the hypertrophied muscle outgrows its blood supply and its capacity for adaptation; ischemia and cell injury develop, and the ventricle can fail.
rubric: States that cardiac myocytes are permanent, nondividing (or minimally dividing) cells.
rubric: States that hypertrophy means larger cells with more proteins and organelles.
rubric: Names progression to ischemia, injury or heart failure if the stress persists.
