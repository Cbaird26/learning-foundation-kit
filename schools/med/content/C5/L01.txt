@article
Organic chemistry is the chemistry of carbon compounds, and nearly every drug you will meet in medicine is one. Carbon sits in group 14, has four valence electrons, and almost always forms **four bonds**. In C4 you saw the octet rule and electronegativity; here we use them to predict the shape and reactivity of molecules.

### Lewis structures and formal charge

A Lewis structure shows bonds and lone pairs. To keep track of where electrons really are, assign each atom a **formal charge**:

$$\text{FC} = (\text{valence electrons}) - (\text{nonbonding electrons}) - (\text{number of bonds}).$$

Neutral atoms in their usual bonding patterns are easy to remember: carbon has 4 bonds, nitrogen 3 bonds plus 1 lone pair, oxygen 2 bonds plus 2 lone pairs, hydrogen 1 bond, and halogens 1 bond plus 3 lone pairs. Deviations produce charges: an oxygen with 3 bonds and 1 lone pair is $+1$; a nitrogen with 4 bonds is $+1$; a carbon with 3 bonds and a lone pair is $-1$ (a carbanion), while a carbon with 3 bonds and no lone pair is $+1$ (a carbocation).

### Resonance

When electrons can be drawn in more than one valid way without moving atoms, the real molecule is a **resonance hybrid**, more stable than any single contributor. The carboxylate ion $\text{RCO}_2^-$ has the negative charge shared equally over two oxygens. Resonance forms differ only in electron positions, never in atom positions, and the best contributors have full octets and the fewest charges.

### Shape and hybridization

Valence-shell electron-pair repulsion (VSEPR) counts **electron domains** (bonds, treating a double or triple bond as one domain, plus lone pairs) around an atom. Hybridization is the orbital description of the same count:

| Domains | Hybridization | Geometry | Angle | % s character |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4 | $sp^3$ | tetrahedral | about $109.5^\circ$ | 25% |
| 3 | $sp^2$ | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ | 33% |
| 2 | $sp$ | linear | $180^\circ$ | 50% |

Ammonia's nitrogen has three bonds and one lone pair, so four domains: $sp^3$, trigonal pyramidal. Water's oxygen is $sp^3$ and bent.

### Sigma and pi bonds

A **sigma ($\sigma$) bond** is formed by head-on overlap along the bond axis; every single bond is a $\sigma$ bond. A **pi ($\pi$) bond** forms by side-on overlap of unhybridized $p$ orbitals above and below the axis. A double bond is one $\sigma$ plus one $\pi$; a triple bond is one $\sigma$ plus two $\pi$. Because $\pi$ overlap is weaker, $\pi$ electrons are more exposed and more reactive, which is why alkenes and alkynes undergo addition reactions. Higher bond order and higher s character both shorten and strengthen the bond: $\text{C}\equiv\text{C} < \text{C}=\text{C} < \text{C}-\text{C}$ in length.

### Degrees of unsaturation

A saturated acyclic hydrocarbon is $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$. Each ring or $\pi$ bond removes two hydrogens. For $\text{C}_c\text{H}_h\text{N}_n\text{X}_x$ (oxygen ignored):

$$\text{DoU} = \frac{2c + 2 + n - h - x}{2}.$$

One degree is one ring or one $\pi$ bond; a benzene ring counts as four.

@example
title: Formal charge, shape and hybridization of hydronium
problem: Hydronium, $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$, has three O-H bonds and one lone pair on oxygen. Find the formal charge on oxygen, its hybridization and its geometry.
step: Oxygen has 6 valence electrons, 2 nonbonding electrons (one lone pair) and 3 bonds.
step: $\text{FC} = 6 - 2 - 3 = +1$. The positive charge sits on oxygen.
step: Count domains: three bonds plus one lone pair is 4 domains, so oxygen is $sp^3$.
step: Four domains with one lone pair give a trigonal pyramidal geometry, like ammonia, with bond angles slightly under $109.5^\circ$.

@example
title: Degrees of unsaturation of a drug-like formula
problem: Nicotinamide has the formula $\text{C}_6\text{H}_6\text{N}_2\text{O}$. How many degrees of unsaturation does it have, and what could they be?
step: Ignore oxygen. Apply the formula with $c = 6$, $n = 2$, $h = 6$, $x = 0$.
step: $\text{DoU} = (12 + 2 + 2 - 6)/2 = 5$.
step: A pyridine ring accounts for four (one ring plus three $\pi$ bonds) and the amide C=O accounts for the fifth.

@mistakes
- Counting a double bond as two electron domains when predicting geometry; a multiple bond is one domain.
- Moving atoms (not just electrons) between resonance forms, or treating resonance forms as rapidly interconverting molecules.
- Including oxygen in the degrees-of-unsaturation formula, or forgetting to add nitrogens and subtract halogens.
- Forgetting that a double bond contains one $\sigma$ bond as well as one $\pi$ bond when counting $\sigma$ bonds.

@practice num
prompt: Caffeine has the molecular formula $\text{C}_8\text{H}_{10}\text{N}_4\text{O}_2$. How many degrees of unsaturation does it have?
answer: 6
check: (2*8 + 2 + 4 - 10) / 2
hint: Oxygen is ignored; each nitrogen adds one to the numerator.
hint: $(16 + 2 + 4 - 10)/2$.
solution: $\text{DoU} = (2\cdot 8 + 2 + 4 - 10)/2 = 6$: two rings plus four $\pi$ bonds (two C=O, one C=C, one C=N).
