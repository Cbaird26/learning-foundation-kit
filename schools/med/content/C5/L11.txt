@article
Alcohols (R-OH) and ethers (R-O-R') are everywhere in medicine: ethanol, glycerol, the sugar hydroxyls, and ether-containing drugs. Their chemistry follows from two facts: the O-H bond is polar and can hydrogen bond, and hydroxide is a poor leaving group until it is activated.

### Physical properties

Alcohols hydrogen bond to one another, so they boil far higher than ethers or alkanes of similar mass (ethanol, $78\ ^\circ\text{C}$; dimethyl ether, $-24\ ^\circ\text{C}$). Ethers accept hydrogen bonds but cannot donate them. Small alcohols mix with water; solubility drops as the nonpolar chain grows. Alcohol pKa is about 16 (similar to water, 15.7), phenols about 10.

### Making alcohols

Hydration of alkenes (Markovnikov via acid, anti-Markovnikov via hydroboration), SN2 substitution of an alkyl halide by hydroxide, reduction of carbonyl compounds ($\text{NaBH}_4$ reduces aldehydes to primary and ketones to secondary alcohols) and addition of Grignard reagents to carbonyls.

### Reactions of alcohols

**Substitution.** The OH must be converted to a good leaving group.
- With HBr or HCl, **tertiary** alcohols react by SN1 via protonation of OH to give water as the leaving group and then the carbocation; primary alcohols cannot form a stable carbocation and react only slowly with HBr (by SN2, with heating), so $\text{SOCl}_2$ or $\text{PBr}_3$ is usually used.
- $\text{SOCl}_2$ (with pyridine) gives the chloride and $\text{PBr}_3$ gives the bromide, both by SN2 with **inversion** at a stereocenter, and without rearrangement.
- **Tosyl chloride** (TsCl, pyridine) converts $\text{R-OH}$ into $\text{R-OTs}$, an excellent leaving group, with **retention** (the C-O bond is not broken). A tosylate can then undergo SN2 with inversion.

**Dehydration.** Concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ and heat give an E1 elimination (tertiary > secondary >> primary); Zaitsev's alkene predominates and rearrangements may occur. $\text{POCl}_3$ in pyridine dehydrates by E2 without a carbocation.

**Oxidation.**
- Primary alcohol, **PCC** (or Dess-Martin): aldehyde.
- Primary alcohol, **chromic acid** ($\text{CrO}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$; Jones reagent): carboxylic acid.
- Secondary alcohol, either: ketone.
- Tertiary alcohol: no oxidation (no hydrogen on the carbinol carbon).

### Ethers

Ethers are unreactive, which is why diethyl ether and THF serve as solvents. They form by the **Williamson ether synthesis**: an alkoxide displaces a leaving group on an alkyl halide by SN2. **Use the less hindered halide:** make *tert*-butyl methyl ether from sodium *tert*-butoxide and methyl iodide, not from methoxide and *tert*-butyl bromide, which would give elimination. Ethers are cleaved by strong acid (HI or HBr): protonation, then bromide or iodide attacks the less hindered alkyl group in SN2 (or the tertiary group by SN1).

### Epoxides

An **epoxide** is a strained three-membered cyclic ether (about 110 kJ/mol of strain), so it opens readily.
- **Basic or nucleophilic conditions** (alkoxide, $\text{HO}^-$, Grignard, $\text{LiAlH}_4$): SN2 attack at the **less substituted** carbon, with inversion; protonation of the alkoxide follows.
- **Acidic conditions** (HX, or ROH with acid): protonation of the oxygen first, then attack at the **more substituted** carbon, where partial positive charge builds, again from the back side.

Both give *trans* (anti) products in rings.

@example
title: Choosing an oxidant
problem: Convert 1-pentanol into (a) pentanal and (b) pentanoic acid.
step: Primary alcohols can stop at the aldehyde only if water is absent; use PCC in dichloromethane.
step: For (a): PCC gives pentanal.
step: For (b): chromic acid (Jones reagent, aqueous) oxidizes through the aldehydic hydrate to the carboxylic acid, pentanoic acid.

@example
title: Regiochemistry of epoxide opening
problem: Give the product of 2,2-dimethyloxirane with (a) sodium methoxide in methanol and (b) methanol with a catalytic amount of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
step: (a) Under basic conditions methoxide attacks the less hindered CH$_2$ carbon by SN2, giving 1-methoxy-2-methylpropan-2-ol after protonation.
step: (b) Under acidic conditions the protonated epoxide opens at the tertiary carbon, which holds more positive charge.
step: Methanol attacks the tertiary carbon, giving 2-methoxy-2-methylpropan-1-ol after loss of a proton.

@mistakes
- Oxidizing a primary alcohol to an aldehyde with chromic acid in water; the reaction goes on to the carboxylic acid.
- Proposing a Williamson synthesis with a secondary or tertiary halide, which gives elimination.
- Attacking the more substituted carbon of an epoxide under basic conditions (or the less substituted under acidic conditions).
- Expecting retention or inversion mixed up: tosylation retains configuration at carbon, while SN2 on the tosylate or with $\text{SOCl}_2/\text{PBr}_3$ inverts it.

@practice self
prompt: Draw the mechanism, with curved arrows, for the reaction of 2-methylbutan-2-ol with concentrated HBr to give 2-bromo-2-methylbutane. Explain why the OH needs to be protonated first, and why this method would not suit a primary alcohol such as butan-1-ol.
solution: Step 1: a lone pair on the OH oxygen takes a proton from HBr, giving an oxonium ion (water as the leaving group) and bromide. Step 2 (slow): the C-O bond breaks, both electrons going to oxygen, giving a tertiary carbocation and water. Step 3: bromide attacks the carbocation. Hydroxide is a strong base and a poor leaving group; protonation converts it into water, a weak base and good leaving group. Primary alcohols cannot form a stable carbocation, and SN2 with bromide on the protonated primary alcohol is slow, so $\text{PBr}_3$ (SN2) is preferred.
hint: Number the steps and start each arrow at an electron pair.
hint: Ask what each leaving group is.
rubric: Protonation, ionization to the tertiary cation and bromide capture are all shown with arrows.
rubric: The answer explains that water is a better leaving group than hydroxide.
rubric: The answer notes that primary alcohols do not ionize and need PBr3 or SOCl2.
