@article
In a **nucleophilic substitution** a nucleophile replaces a leaving group on an $sp^3$ carbon:

$$\text{Nu}^- + \text{R-LG} \to \text{R-Nu} + \text{LG}^-.$$

There are two limiting mechanisms. Which one operates depends on the substrate, nucleophile, leaving group and solvent.

### The SN2 reaction

**SN2** means substitution, nucleophilic, bimolecular. It occurs in one concerted step: the nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group (**backside attack**) while the leaving group departs. The rate depends on both species:

$$\text{rate} = k[\text{R-LG}][\text{Nu}].$$

Consequences:
- **Inversion of configuration** at a stereocenter (a Walden inversion): the three other groups flip like an umbrella in the wind. An (S) substrate gives the (R) product if the CIP priorities are maintained.
- **Steric hindrance** slows the reaction: methyl > primary > secondary $\gg$ tertiary (tertiary does not react by SN2). Neopentyl halides are primary but hindered by the adjacent quaternary carbon.
- **Strong, unhindered nucleophiles** ($\text{I}^-$, $\text{CN}^-$, $\text{N}_3^-$, $\text{RS}^-$, $\text{HO}^-$) favor SN2.
- **Polar aprotic solvents** (DMSO, DMF, acetone, acetonitrile) raise nucleophilicity because they solvate cations but leave the anion unencumbered.

### The SN1 reaction

**SN1** means substitution, nucleophilic, unimolecular. It has two or three steps: (1) slow ionization of the leaving group to give a **carbocation intermediate**, (2) fast attack of the nucleophile on the carbocation, and (3) a proton transfer if the nucleophile was neutral (water, alcohol). Only the first step is rate-determining, so

$$\text{rate} = k[\text{R-LG}].$$

Consequences:
- The carbocation is planar and $sp^2$, so the nucleophile can attack from either face and a chiral substrate gives largely **racemization** (often with a slight excess of inversion because of ion-pair effects).
- Reactivity follows carbocation stability: tertiary > secondary $\gg$ primary, and allylic or benzylic carbocations are especially stable by resonance. Methyl and primary substrates do not ionize.
- **Polar protic solvents** (water, alcohols, carboxylic acids) stabilize both the ion and the leaving group through hydrogen bonding and favor SN1. A weak nucleophile, often the solvent itself (**solvolysis**), is common.
- **Carbocation rearrangements** (hydride or methyl shifts that give a more stable cation) can occur.

### Leaving groups

A good leaving group is a weak base. The order for halides is $\text{I}^- > \text{Br}^- > \text{Cl}^- \gg \text{F}^-$; sulfonates such as tosylate and mesylate are excellent. Hydroxide is a poor leaving group, but protonation makes water the leaving group (as with HBr and alcohols).

### Choosing the pathway

| Feature | SN2 | SN1 |
|---|---|---|
| Substrate | methyl, 1$^\circ$ (2$^\circ$ possible) | 3$^\circ$, allylic, benzylic (2$^\circ$ possible) |
| Nucleophile | strong | weak or neutral fine |
| Solvent | polar aprotic | polar protic |
| Kinetics | second order | first order |
| Stereochemistry | inversion | racemization |

Secondary substrates sit on the borderline; the nucleophile and solvent decide.

@example
title: Stereochemistry of an SN2 reaction
problem: (S)-2-Bromobutane reacts with sodium cyanide in DMSO. Give the product and its configuration.
step: A secondary halide with a strong nucleophile in a polar aprotic solvent reacts by SN2, with backside attack.
step: The product is 2-methylbutanenitrile (cyano replaces bromine). The nucleophile enters from the face opposite bromine, so the configuration is inverted spatially.
step: The CIP priorities remain in the same order (cyano takes the rank of bromine: first, then ethyl, then methyl, then H), so spatial inversion changes the label: the product is **(R)-2-methylbutanenitrile**.

@example
title: Choosing a mechanism
problem: tert-Butyl bromide is dissolved in warm ethanol. What substitution mechanism is expected, and what is the rate law?
step: The substrate is tertiary, so backside attack is blocked and SN2 is not possible, but the tertiary cation is stable.
step: Ethanol is a polar protic solvent and a weak nucleophile, which suits SN1.
step: Slow loss of bromide gives the tert-butyl cation; ethanol attacks, and loss of a proton gives tert-butyl ethyl ether.
step: The rate law is first order: $\text{rate} = k[(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}]$.

@mistakes
- Predicting SN2 on a tertiary substrate, or SN1 on a methyl or primary substrate.
- Thinking SN2 gives racemization or SN1 gives clean inversion; the stereochemical outcome is a signature of the mechanism.
- Using a polar protic solvent to maximize the rate of an SN2 reaction with an anionic nucleophile; it hydrogen bonds to and weakens the nucleophile.
- Labeling the product R or S by inversion of the label without checking whether the priorities changed.

@practice self
prompt: Draw the full SN1 mechanism for the reaction of 2-bromo-2-methylbutane with water, with curved arrows. Identify the rate-determining step, the intermediate and the product, and explain why adding more water does not change the rate.
solution: Step 1 (slow): the C-Br bond breaks, with both electrons going to bromine, giving the tertiary 2-methylbutan-2-yl cation and bromide. Step 2 (fast): a lone pair on the oxygen of water attacks the cation, giving an oxonium ion. Step 3 (fast): another water removes a proton from the oxonium, giving 2-methylbutan-2-ol and hydronium. The rate-determining step is ionization, so rate = k[RBr]. Water is not involved in that step, so changing its concentration does not alter the rate (it is the solvent in large excess).
hint: Number the steps and put a curved arrow on each one.
hint: The slowest step controls the rate law.
rubric: Three steps are shown with arrows starting at electron pairs.
rubric: Ionization is named as the rate-determining step and the carbocation as the intermediate.
rubric: The answer notes that the rate law is first order in the substrate only.
