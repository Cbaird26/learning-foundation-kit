@article
An **alkyne** contains a C$\equiv$C triple bond: two $sp$ carbons joined by one $\sigma$ and two $\pi$ bonds. The geometry about each $sp$ carbon is linear, so a triple bond cannot fit in a small ring (cyclooctyne is the smallest isolable cycloalkyne). Name them like alkenes with the suffix **-yne** (hex-2-yne, propyne). A **terminal alkyne** has the triple bond at the end of the chain ($\text{R-C}\equiv\text{C-H}$); an **internal alkyne** has it within the chain.

### Acidity and acetylide alkylation

The C-H of a terminal alkyne has pKa about 25, much more acidic than alkene (44) or alkane (50) C-H because the carbanion is in an $sp$ orbital (50% s character). A very strong base such as sodium amide ($\text{NaNH}_2$, conjugate acid pKa 38) deprotonates it completely to the **acetylide ion** $\text{R-C}\equiv\text{C}^-$. Hydroxide and alkoxides are too weak.

The acetylide is a strong carbon nucleophile. It reacts with **methyl or primary** alkyl halides by SN2 to make a new C-C bond:

$$\text{R-C}\equiv\text{C}^- + \text{R'-CH}_2\text{Br} \to \text{R-C}\equiv\text{C-CH}_2\text{R'}.$$

Secondary and tertiary halides give E2 elimination, because the acetylide is also a strong base.

### Reduction

- $\text{H}_2$, Pd/C (or Pt): complete reduction to the alkane.
- $\text{H}_2$ over **Lindlar's catalyst** (poisoned palladium): stops at the alkene, **cis (Z)**, by syn addition.
- **Na or Li in liquid $\text{NH}_3$**: stops at the **trans (E)** alkene by anti addition through a radical anion.

### Hydration

Aqueous $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ with $\text{HgSO}_4$ adds water in the Markovnikov sense to give an enol, which tautomerizes to a **ketone**. A terminal alkyne gives a methyl ketone, and a symmetric internal alkyne gives one ketone. **Hydroboration-oxidation** (using a hindered borane such as disiamylborane or 9-BBN) is anti-Markovnikov: a terminal alkyne gives an **aldehyde**.

### Addition of HX and halogens

Two equivalents of HX add to give a geminal dihalide (Markovnikov; both halogens on the same carbon). One equivalent of $\text{X}_2$ gives a trans dihaloalkene, and two equivalents give a tetrahalide.

### Planning a synthesis

Work **backward** (retrosynthesis). Ask what bond or group must be made, then which reagent makes it:
1. Need more carbons? Alkylate an acetylide with a primary halide.
2. Need a $Z$-alkene? Lindlar. An $E$-alkene? Na/$\text{NH}_3$. An alkane? Pd/C.
3. Need a ketone? Alkyne hydration. An aldehyde? Hydroboration-oxidation of a terminal alkyne.
Check each step for competing reactions, and make sure each reagent tolerates the groups already present.

@example
title: A two-step synthesis of a Z-alkene
problem: Design a two-step synthesis of (Z)-oct-2-ene from propyne and any halide.
step: Retrosynthesis: a Z-alkene comes from an internal alkyne by Lindlar reduction, so the target comes from oct-2-yne.
step: Oct-2-yne is propyne (3 C) plus a five-carbon piece; build it by alkylating propynide with a primary halide, 1-bromopentane.
step: Forward: (1) $\text{NaNH}_2$, then 1-bromopentane gives oct-2-yne. (2) $\text{H}_2$, Lindlar catalyst gives (Z)-oct-2-ene.

@example
title: Hydration regiochemistry
problem: What is formed when but-1-yne is treated with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ and $\text{HgSO}_4$? What if it is treated with disiamylborane and then $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2/\text{NaOH}$?
step: Mercury-catalyzed hydration is Markovnikov: OH adds to the internal carbon (C2) and gives an enol that tautomerizes to the ketone, butan-2-one.
step: Hydroboration-oxidation is anti-Markovnikov: OH adds to the terminal carbon, giving an enol that tautomerizes to butanal.

@mistakes
- Alkylating an acetylide with a secondary or tertiary halide; E2 elimination dominates.
- Using hydroxide or an alkoxide to make an acetylide; the base is not strong enough.
- Mixing up the reducing agents: Lindlar gives cis, sodium in ammonia gives trans.
- Forgetting that hydration goes through an enol that tautomerizes to the carbonyl.

@quiz num
prompt: The terminal alkyne C-H has pKa 25 and ethanol has pKa 16. Calculate $\log_{10} K_{eq}$ for the reaction of an alkyne with ethoxide to give the acetylide and ethanol.
answer: -9
check: 16 - 25
tol: 0.005
hint: $\log K_{eq} = \text{p}K_a(\text{product acid}) - \text{p}K_a(\text{reactant acid})$.
solution: $\log K_{eq} = 16 - 25 = -9$, so the equilibrium lies far on the reactant side and ethoxide cannot form acetylide effectively.
