@article
Single bonds rotate. The different three-dimensional arrangements that result from rotation about single bonds are **conformations** (or conformers); they interconvert rapidly at room temperature, so they are not different compounds. Their energies decide which shape a molecule prefers, and that shape often controls reactivity and drug binding.

### Newman projections

Look down a C-C bond: the front carbon is a point where three bonds meet, the back carbon is a circle. In **staggered** conformations the front and back substituents are $60^\circ$ apart; in **eclipsed** conformations they are aligned ($0^\circ$). Ethane's eclipsed form is about $12\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (about $3\ \text{kcal/mol}$) higher in energy than staggered because of **torsional strain** (repulsion between bonding electrons on adjacent bonds).

### Butane

Rotating about C2-C3 of butane gives four distinct arrangements:
- **Anti** ($180^\circ$ between the methyls): the most stable.
- **Gauche** ($60^\circ$): about $3.8\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (0.9 kcal/mol) above anti, owing to **steric strain** between the methyls.
- **Eclipsed** forms, with a methyl eclipsing a hydrogen, are higher.
- **Totally eclipsed** (methyls at $0^\circ$): the highest, about $19\ \text{kJ/mol}$ above anti.

### Ring strain

Small rings are strained: cyclopropane has severe **angle strain** (the $60^\circ$ C-C-C angle versus $109.5^\circ$) plus eclipsing. Cyclobutane and cyclopentane puckering reduces torsional strain. Cyclohexane has essentially none.

### The chair conformation of cyclohexane

The **chair** has all C-C-C angles near $109.5^\circ$ and all bonds staggered, so it is virtually strain free. Each carbon has two positions: **axial** (parallel to the ring axis, alternating up and down) and **equatorial** (roughly in the plane of the ring, pointing outward). Rules for drawing:
1. Draw the chair with parallel pairs of lines.
2. Axial bonds point straight up or straight down, alternating from carbon to carbon.
3. Equatorial bonds are parallel to ring bonds once removed.

A **ring flip** converts one chair into the other and swaps every axial and equatorial position. A substituent that is equatorial becomes axial and vice versa; cis or trans relationships do not change.

### Substituent preferences

An axial substituent suffers **1,3-diaxial interactions** with the two other axial hydrogens on the same face. Larger groups prefer the equatorial position. The preference is measured by the **A-value** (the free-energy cost of axial versus equatorial): methyl 7.3 kJ/mol (1.74 kcal/mol), ethyl about 7.5 kJ/mol, isopropyl about 9.2 kJ/mol and tert-butyl about 20 kJ/mol or more, which essentially locks the ring.

For a disubstituted ring, put the larger group equatorial. In 1,2-trans and 1,4-trans arrangements the diequatorial chair is possible; in a cis-1,4 arrangement one group is always axial. A useful rule: **1,2-trans, 1,3-cis and 1,4-trans can be diequatorial**. The ratio of conformers follows

$$\Delta G = -RT\ln K, \qquad K = e^{-\Delta G/RT}.$$

At $298\ \text{K}$, $RT \approx 2.48\ \text{kJ/mol}$. Here $\Delta G$ is the free-energy cost of the less stable conformer (for example, axial minus equatorial), $K$ is the ratio of less stable to more stable conformer, and the ratio of more stable to less stable is $e^{\Delta G/RT}$.

@example
title: Most stable chair of trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane
problem: Draw and compare the two chair conformations of trans-1,2-dimethylcyclohexane.
step: In one chair both methyls can occupy equatorial positions on adjacent carbons; trans on adjacent carbons means one up and one down, which is possible with both equatorial.
step: Ring flip gives the other chair, where both methyls are axial (one up, one down, anti to each other).
step: Diaxial has four 1,3-diaxial interactions (two for each methyl), about $2 \times 7.3 = 14.6\ \text{kJ/mol}$ in total; the diequatorial chair has only a gauche interaction between the methyls.
step: The diequatorial chair is more stable by roughly $11\ \text{kJ/mol}$ (an A-value estimate), so it dominates overwhelmingly.

@example
title: Chair of a cis-1,4 compound
problem: Which chair of cis-1-tert-butyl-4-methylcyclohexane is more stable?
step: Cis on carbons 1 and 4 means one substituent is axial and the other equatorial in either chair.
step: The two chairs are (tert-butyl equatorial, methyl axial) and (tert-butyl axial, methyl equatorial).
step: tert-Butyl has an A-value of about 20 kJ/mol versus 7.3 kJ/mol for methyl, so the chair with tert-butyl equatorial is far more stable.

@mistakes
- Drawing axial bonds that do not alternate up and down around the ring.
- Thinking a ring flip converts a cis isomer into a trans isomer; ring flip only swaps axial and equatorial, so cis and trans do not change.
- Calling a gauche butane conformation "eclipsed"; gauche is staggered with methyls $60^\circ$ apart.
- Putting the larger substituent axial when computing which chair is more stable.

answer: 19
check: math.exp(7.3 / 2.478)
tol: 5%
hint: $K = e^{\Delta G/RT}$ for the ratio of the more stable conformer to the less stable one.
hint: Compute $7.3/2.478 \approx 2.95$.
solution: $K = e^{7.3/2.478} = e^{2.946} \approx 19$, so about 95% of molecules have the methyl equatorial.
