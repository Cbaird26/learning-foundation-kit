@article
Week 3 asked what must be true given the text. **Reasoning beyond the text** asks what else would be true, or what should be done, if the author's reasoning is right. You must now bring the author's principle into a new context, and the new context may differ from the original in ways that matter.

### Find the principle

An argument about one case usually rests on a more general **principle**. A passage says, "The clinic should offer weekend appointments because working patients otherwise skip care." The principle: a service should be arranged so that the people who need it can actually use it. To apply this to a pharmacy that closes at 5 p.m., you restate the principle without the original details, then check whether the same conditions hold in the new case (are there patients who need the pharmacy and work until 6?).

### Applying a principle

1. Strip the argument to a general rule.
2. List the **conditions** under which the rule is meant to apply.
3. Check whether the new case meets them.
4. State the conclusion, with any qualification.

A frequent error is applying the rule in a case with the same surface features but different relevant conditions. Another is refusing to apply it at all because the new case "is different" in some irrelevant way.

### Strengthen and weaken

To **strengthen** an argument, add a fact that makes the conclusion more likely or supports a needed assumption. To **weaken** it, add a fact that makes the conclusion less likely, undermines an assumption, or offers a better alternative explanation. A fact that leaves the link between the reasons and the conclusion untouched is **irrelevant**, however interesting. The strongest way to weaken is to attack the assumption on which the reasoning depends. If an author argues that a new sign-in system caused shorter waits, a finding that waits were already falling for six months before the system started weakens the argument.

### Analogy

An **analogy** argues that because two things are alike in some respects, they are alike in another. "A hospital's infection-control policy is like a fire code: you do not wait for a fire to enforce it." Evaluate analogies by asking: Are the **relevant** similarities strong? Are there relevant **differences** that break the comparison? Analogies persuade, but they do not prove; their job is to suggest where to look.

### Parallel reasoning

Some questions ask you to find an argument with the **same structure** as the original. Abstract the original ("Most A are B; this is an A; so this is B") and match the pattern, ignoring subject matter. Keep the strength of the claims aligned too: a "probably" argument should not be matched with a "certainly" one.

### Limits of extrapolation

Extending a trend beyond the range of data is risky. A dose-response relationship seen between 0 and 20 mg says little about 200 mg. State clearly when you are extrapolating.

@example
title: Applying a principle to a new case
problem: (Invented passage.) "A screening test is worth offering only if it finds at least one true case per 200 people screened, since below that rate the harm from false alarms and follow-up procedures outweighs the benefit." A clinic screened 900 people with a new test and found 4 true cases. Does the author's principle support offering the test?
step: State the principle in general form: offer the test only if cases found per person screened is at least 1/200.
step: Compute the clinic's rate: 4 cases in 900 people is 1 in 225, that is 4/900.
step: Compare: 1/225 is smaller than 1/200 (225 is greater than 200). The rate falls below the threshold.
step: Conclusion: by the passage's own rule, the evidence does not support offering the test. A further qualification: the rule is stated as "only if," a necessary condition, so meeting the threshold would not alone prove the test worthwhile.

@mistakes
- Applying a rule to a new case without checking that the conditions behind the rule still hold.
- Calling a fact "irrelevant" because it does not repeat the original reasons, when it actually attacks an assumption.
- Treating an analogy as proof instead of checking the relevant differences.
- Reading "only if" (necessary) as if it meant "if" (sufficient).

prompt: (Invented passage.) "The hospital should publish surgeons' complication rates, because patients cannot choose wisely without information." Which new fact most weakens the argument?
choice: Many hospitals publish other quality measures.
choice*: Studies show patients rarely read published surgeon data and do not change their choices.
choice: Surgeons are well trained.
choice: The hospital is in a large city.
hint: The argument depends on patients using the information.
solution: If patients do not use the data, the stated benefit (wiser choice) does not materialize. The other facts do not bear on that link.

@practice mc
prompt: (Invented passage.) "Treating medical records like bank records, we should let patients see every entry, since account holders see every transaction." Which is the most serious relevant difference that weakens this analogy?
choice: Banks keep records longer than hospitals do.
choice*: Clinical notes may contain a clinician's tentative impressions that could be misread or could cause harm without context, unlike transaction lists.
choice: Banks employ more staff than clinics.
choice: Both kinds of records can be stored electronically.
hint: Ask which difference touches the reason the author gave for letting people see the record.
solution: The author's reason is that account holders see what is theirs; the key difference is that clinical notes involve judgment and context. The other differences are not relevant to the point of the analogy.

@practice num
prompt: A passage argues that a screening test is worthwhile only if it finds at least 1 true case per 200 people screened. A new test finds 7 true cases in 1,000 people. By how many cases per 1,000 people does this test exceed the minimum rate the passage requires?
answer: 2
check: 7 - 1000 / 200
hint: First turn "1 per 200" into a number per 1,000 people.
solution: 1 per 200 is 5 per 1,000. The test finds 7 per 1,000, which is 7 - 5 = 2 more than the minimum.

@practice ms
prompt: (Invented passage.) "The ward's new bed alarms reduced falls: falls dropped from 15 per month to 6 per month after the alarms were installed." Select every additional fact that would strengthen the claim that the alarms caused the drop.
choice*: Falls on a comparable ward without alarms stayed near 15 per month over the same period.
choice*: Staffing, patient mix and fall-risk scores were unchanged between the two periods.
choice: The alarms are made by a well-known manufacturer.
choice: A comparable ward that did not install alarms also saw falls drop from 15 to 6 per month over the same period.
hint: Strengthening means ruling out other explanations or showing a matching pattern.
solution: A comparison ward whose falls did not change, and stable staffing and patient mix, both rule out other explanations. The manufacturer's reputation is irrelevant. A comparison ward without alarms that also saw falls drop would weaken the claim, since something other than the alarms may explain the drop.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented passage.) "A good clinical handbook, like a good map, leaves out nearly everything. Its value lies in what it chooses to omit, because a reader in a hurry needs the route, not the terrain." Apply the idea of this passage to a new situation: the design of a one-page patient discharge instruction sheet. In four or five sentences, say what the sheet should omit, what it should include, and one way the map analogy might mislead.
hint: State the passage's principle first (usefulness depends on selective omission for a hurried reader).
hint: For the limit of the analogy, ask what a map reader can do that a patient might not (check the terrain, ask questions).
solution: The principle is that a tool for a hurried user is useful because it omits detail, keeping only what guides action. A discharge sheet should omit the full pathophysiology and rare complications and include the medicine schedule, warning signs that need a call or return, and a phone number. The sheet should be tested with the reader's health literacy in mind. The map analogy may mislead because a traveler can see the terrain and correct course, whereas a patient cannot see the omitted risk; so omissions should be chosen conservatively and warning signs must never be left out.
rubric: States the passage's underlying principle before applying it.
rubric: Applies it to the discharge sheet with specific content to omit and include.
rubric: Names a relevant limit or difference in the analogy.
rubric: Stays within about five sentences and avoids copying the passage.

@quiz mc
prompt: (Invented passage.) "Since most residents who attend the writing workshop later write clearer notes, the program should require all residents to attend." Which assumption is the argument most clearly depending on?
choice: Residents enjoy writing.
choice*: The clearer notes result from the workshop rather than from the kinds of residents who choose to attend.
choice: The workshop is held on weekdays.
choice: Notes are written only by residents.
hint: Think of an alternative explanation for the improvement.
solution: The argument moves from an association among attendees to a requirement for everyone, so it assumes the workshop produces the improvement, not that motivated residents both attend and improve.

@quiz num
prompt: A passage argues that a clinic should add a second nurse whenever the average wait exceeds 30 minutes. Over 5 days, the average waits were 24, 36, 33, 27 and 45 minutes. On how many of those days did the passage's rule call for a second nurse?
answer: 3
check: sum(1 for w in [24, 36, 33, 27, 45] if w > 30)
hint: Compare each day's wait with the threshold; equality would not count because the rule says "exceeds."
solution: The waits above 30 minutes are 36, 33 and 45, so the rule applied on 3 days.
