@article
An ethics essay in medicine does not ask what you feel. It asks you to give a defensible answer to a practical question, supported by reasons the reader can examine. The method is as important as the conclusion.

### The four principles

A widely taught framework (principlism) uses four prima facie principles, meaning each binds unless it conflicts with a stronger one:

- **Respect for autonomy**: honor the informed, voluntary choices of patients who have decision-making capacity. This grounds informed consent, informed refusal and confidentiality.
- **Beneficence**: act for the patient's benefit.
- **Nonmaleficence**: avoid causing harm ("first, do no harm"), weighed against benefit.
- **Justice**: distribute benefits, burdens and resources fairly, and treat like cases alike.

Other considerations often accompany them: honesty, fidelity (keeping promises), and professional integrity. The principles do not rank themselves. Reasoned judgment is needed when they pull apart.

### Capacity and consent

An adult is presumed to have **capacity** unless shown otherwise. Capacity is decision-specific and is usually assessed by whether the patient can communicate a choice, understand the relevant information, appreciate how it applies to them, and reason about options. A patient with capacity may refuse a recommended treatment even when refusal is likely to lead to harm. When a patient lacks capacity, a surrogate decides using the patient's known wishes (substituted judgment), or the patient's best interests if wishes are unknown.

### A structure for the essay

1. **Facts**: summarize the case in two or three sentences, including what is not known.
2. **The question**: state precisely what must be decided, and by whom.
3. **Principles engaged**: name the relevant principles and say how each bears on the case.
4. **Weighing**: explain which principle should weigh more here, and why. This is the heart of the essay: cite features of the case (capacity, severity, reversibility, alternatives) rather than just asserting.
5. **Decision**: state a clear position and the practical steps that follow.
6. **Objection and response**: state the strongest objection and answer it. Acknowledge any remaining cost of your choice.

### Writing tips

Use **neutral, respectful, person-first language**. Distinguish what you assert as fact from what you assume. Avoid fallacies such as the slippery slope, appeal to authority and false dilemma, and look for options beyond the two obvious ones (a time-limited trial, a mediated conversation, an ethics consultation). Where law or institutional policy applies, say so, but note that what is legal is not automatically what is ethical.

@example
title: Weighing autonomy and beneficence
problem: (Invented case.) A 62-year-old woman with a painful, infected foot ulcer and diabetes refuses the recommended amputation of her toe. She understands that the infection may spread and could be fatal, and says she wants to try antibiotics and wound care at home. Outline the analysis.
step: Facts and question: an adult with a serious but treatable infection refuses surgery after understanding the risk. The question is whether the team may override her refusal or must respect it.
step: Principles: autonomy supports honoring her informed refusal; beneficence and nonmaleficence favor surgery because the infection threatens her life and limb.
step: Weighing: she has capacity, she was informed of the risks, the choice concerns her own body, and the alternatives (antibiotics and close follow-up) are not unreasonable for a trial. Autonomy outweighs beneficence here, and overriding a capacitated refusal would be a serious wrong.
step: Decision: respect her choice, continue to offer the surgery, arrange close follow-up and a clear plan for when to return, explore her reasons (cost, fear, family duties), and document the discussion. Objection: that respect abandons her to harm; response: respecting a choice is not abandonment, since the team keeps treating and revisits the recommendation.

@mistakes
- Naming the principles without explaining how they apply to the particular facts.
- Treating one principle (usually beneficence) as always overriding the others.
- Ignoring capacity, which determines whether autonomy can ground a refusal.
- Presenting only two options when a middle course (a time-limited trial, an ethics consultation) exists.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented case.) A 17-year-old girl asks her physician to keep confidential that she is seeking contraception. Her mother has called the clinic asking whether her daughter has been seen. The jurisdiction's law allows minors to consent to contraception without parental notification. Write a response of 150 to 200 words that weighs respect for autonomy (including confidentiality) against the parents' interest and the physician's beneficence, and states what the physician should say to the mother.
hint: Begin by naming the two principles you will weigh and the key facts (the law, the minor's maturity, the harm of disclosure).
hint: Consider an option beyond disclosing or refusing: encouraging the patient to involve her mother while keeping her confidence.
solution: The question is whether the physician may confirm the visit to the mother. Respect for autonomy and confidentiality support protecting the patient's information; she is an adolescent who can plausibly understand the decision, and the law allows her to consent. Beneficence supports her health, which is better served if she trusts the clinic and returns for care; disclosure would risk deterring her and other adolescents from seeking care. The mother's concern is real, but without a risk to safety that the law requires be disclosed, her interest does not outweigh the patient's confidentiality. The physician should tell the mother that clinic policy does not allow confirming or discussing any patient's visits without that patient's permission, and should invite her to speak with her daughter. The physician should encourage the patient to involve her mother or another trusted adult when she is ready. An objection is that parents have a duty to care for minors; the response is that confidentiality in this narrow area serves the minor's health, and the physician can still support family communication.
rubric: Names autonomy or confidentiality and beneficence as the principles in tension.
rubric: Uses specific facts (the law, age, the risk that disclosure would deter care) in the weighing.
rubric: States what the physician should say to the mother.
rubric: Considers an objection and responds, or proposes a middle course.
