@article
Physicians read all day: notes, guidelines, journal articles, letters from patients, and editorials that try to change practice. Most of what you read is not a list of facts; it is an **argument**, a set of statements offered to convince you that something is true or that you should do something. Close reading means seeing how the argument is built before you decide whether to accept it.

### The parts of an argument

An argument has a **claim** (also called the conclusion or thesis), the statement the author wants you to accept. It is supported by **reasons** (premises), the smaller statements that are supposed to make the claim believable, and by **evidence**, the data, examples, testimony or citations behind each reason. Finally, almost every argument rests on **assumptions**, premises the author never states because they seem obvious to the author. A reader who cannot see the assumptions cannot test them.

Keep the three layers apart. In "Clinic wait times should be published, because patients choose better when they have information, and a survey of 400 patients found that most wanted the numbers," the claim is that wait times should be published. One reason is that information improves choice. The survey is evidence for a different point (that patients want the numbers). An unstated assumption is that patients can actually switch clinics.

### Signposts

Authors leave markers that tell you what each sentence is doing.

| Function | Typical signposts |
|---|---|
| Conclusion | therefore, thus, hence, so, it follows that, in short |
| Reason | because, since, for, given that, the reason is |
| Concession | although, admittedly, it is true that, granted |
| Turn or objection | but, yet, however, nevertheless, still |
| Example | for instance, consider, such as |

A concession followed by a turn ("Admittedly the trial was small, but its effect was large") usually places the author's real position **after** the turn. The sentence you most need to read slowly is often the one that follows "but."

### Annotating and mapping

Read the passage twice. On the first pass, read for the gist without a pen. On the second pass, underline the claim, number each reason, circle signposts, bracket the evidence, and write a margin note of five words or fewer beside each paragraph ("concedes critics," "key example"). Then write a one-line **map**: Claim, because Reason 1, and Reason 2, supported by Evidence, assuming Assumption.

### Questions to ask

- What exactly is being claimed, and how strong is the wording (always, often, may)?
- Which sentences are doing argument work, and which are background?
- Who is the author arguing against?
- What must be true for the reasons to lead to the claim?

@example
title: Mapping a short argument
problem: (Invented passage.) "Critics often say that the case report is the weakest form of medical writing, since a single patient cannot show that a treatment works. Yet the case report does a job no trial can do. It is where a rare reaction is first noticed. Trials answer the questions we already know to ask; case reports prompt the questions we have not yet thought of. We should therefore judge the case report by its own task rather than ranking it against the trial." Map the argument.
step: Find the signposts. "Critics often say" and "since" open a concession; "Yet" is the turn; "therefore" introduces the conclusion.
step: The claim is the last sentence: the case report should be judged by its own task, not ranked against the trial.
step: Reason 1: the case report does a job no trial can do, namely noticing rare reactions first. Reason 2: trials answer known questions while case reports prompt new ones. The sentences about critics state the opposing view, not the author's.
step: Unstated assumption: ranking a form of writing by one standard (proof of treatment effect) is unfair if the form serves a different purpose.
step: Map: Claim: judge case reports by their own task, because they detect the unexpected and trials cannot, assuming that fair evaluation matches a form to its purpose.

@mistakes
- Treating the concession ("critics say...") as the author's own position.
- Confusing evidence (a survey, an example) with the claim it is meant to support.
- Underlining nearly everything, so the structure of the argument disappears.
- Ignoring the strength of the wording: "may reduce" and "reduces" are different claims.

@practice ms
prompt: (Invented passage.) "Hospitals should stop scheduling routine procedures on Friday afternoons, since staffing is thinnest then. Administrators say Friday slots are the easiest to fill, but empty weekends do not justify thin cover." Select every statement that is an assumption the argument relies on without stating it.
choice*: Thin staffing makes procedures less safe or less well supported.
choice: Administrators say Friday slots are easy to fill.
choice*: Procedures could be moved to other days without serious harm to patients.
choice: Hospitals currently schedule procedures on Friday afternoons.
hint: An assumption is not written in the passage; it must be true for the reason to support the claim. Statements the passage actually makes do not count.
solution: The argument needs thin staffing to matter (safety or quality) and needs rescheduling to be workable. The administrators' view and current Friday scheduling are stated outright in the passage.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented passage.) "Some argue that premedical students should avoid taking humanities courses, as these courses lower grade-point averages. This is true of some classes. Yet physicians spend their working lives listening to stories and writing about people. A student who has practiced close reading will be better prepared for that work. Premedical advisers should therefore encourage humanities study." Write a one-line map (claim, reasons, concession, one assumption) and mark the signposts you used.
hint: Find "therefore" first, then work backward to the reasons.
hint: Decide who the opposing "some" are and where the turn occurs.
solution: Claim: advisers should encourage humanities study. Reasons: medicine is built on listening and writing, and close reading prepares a student for it. Concession: humanities courses may lower GPA (signposts "some argue," "this is true"). Turn: "Yet." Conclusion marker: "therefore." Assumption: the skills gained in humanities courses transfer to clinical work and are worth a possible GPA cost.
rubric: Identifies the final sentence as the claim.
rubric: Lists at least one reason and separates it from the concession.
rubric: Names the signposts (yet, therefore, some argue).
rubric: States an assumption that the passage does not say outright.

prompt: (Invented passage.) "The clinic's text reminders did not eliminate missed appointments. Nevertheless, missed visits fell from 18% to 12%, and reminders cost almost nothing. The clinic should keep them." Which statement best describes the role of the sentence beginning "Nevertheless"?
choice: It states a concession against the author's position.
choice*: It begins the author's reasons after admitting a limitation.
choice: It restates the conclusion in different words.
choice: It introduces an unrelated example.
hint: "Nevertheless" is a turn that follows an admitted limit.
solution: The first sentence concedes that reminders did not eliminate no-shows. "Nevertheless" turns to the evidence supporting the claim that the clinic should keep them.

@quiz ms
prompt: (Invented passage.) "Patients should be told their own test results immediately, because delay breeds anxiety, and because the results belong to them. Some clinicians fear patients will misread the numbers; but patients can ask questions." Select every sentence part that functions as a reason for the claim.
