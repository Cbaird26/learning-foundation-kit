@article
Medicine is a communication profession. Admissions interviews test how you think and speak under mild pressure, and the emails, notes and messages you write in training are read by busy people who need to act quickly. The skills are the same ones you have practiced: a clear claim, relevant evidence and an audience in mind.

### Interview formats

A **traditional interview** is a conversation with one or two interviewers about your motivation, experiences and views. A **multiple mini interview (MMI)** uses a circuit of short stations, each with a scenario (ethical dilemma, role-play, a policy question), scored on communication, judgment and professionalism. Some programs use **behavioral** questions ("Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate"). In all formats, interviewers can only evaluate what you say, so make your reasoning audible.

### Structuring answers

For behavioral questions use **STAR**: **S**ituation (one or two sentences of context), **T**ask (what you were responsible for), **A**ction (what you personally did, in the first person), **R**esult (what happened and what you learned, including when it did not go well). Aim for about 90 seconds to two minutes. A comfortable speaking rate is roughly 130 words per minute, so a 90-second answer is only about 195 words: choose one story and tell it well.

For ethical or policy questions, use the essay structure from Week 10 aloud: clarify facts, name the principles in tension, weigh them, give a position, and acknowledge the best objection. Say "I would want to know more about..." when information is missing. In role-plays, listen first, name the other person's concern, and avoid giving a lecture.

### Professional emails

Write for a reader with 30 seconds.

- **Subject line**: specific ("Request for letter of recommendation, due March 1"), not vague ("Question").
- **Greeting**: use the person's correct title ("Dear Dr. Alvarez,") unless invited otherwise.
- **First sentence**: who you are and what you are asking, in one or two lines.
- **Body**: the minimum context needed, with dates and deadlines; make the request easy to grant.
- **Close**: thank the reader without groveling, and sign with your full name and role.
- **Check**: names spelled correctly, attachments included, nothing confidential in the message.

Keep the tone respectful and direct. Avoid slang, excessive exclamation points and apologies for existing. Reply promptly, and send a short thank-you note after interviews or favors.

### Clinical communication: SBAR

SBAR organizes urgent messages: **S**ituation (who you are and what is happening now), **B**ackground (relevant history), **A**ssessment (what you think the problem is, and how severe), **R**ecommendation (what you are asking the listener to do, and by when). Example: "I'm calling about Mr. Reyes in bed 12. His oxygen saturation has fallen from 95% to 88% over the past hour. He was admitted with pneumonia yesterday. I think he is getting worse. I'd like you to see him within 15 minutes." Never put identifiable patient information in ordinary email or text; use approved secure channels.

### Revising your statement

Your final personal statement should open with a specific moment, show growth, and end with a forward-looking point. In your last pass, check each paragraph for one idea, delete clichés, replace adjectives with actions, vary sentence length, and match your tone to the page: confident but not boastful.

@example
title: From vague email to clear request
problem: Revise this email to a professor: "Subject: Question. hey prof, i was wondering if maybe you could possibly write me a letter for med school or something? let me know!!"
step: Subject line: make it specific and include the deadline: "Request for letter of recommendation for medical school applications (due June 15)."
step: Greeting and identification: "Dear Professor Okoye, I was a student in your Biochemistry course (Fall 2025, section 2), where I earned an A and presented the enzyme kinetics project."
step: The clear ask and context: "I am applying to medical school this cycle and would be grateful if you would write a letter of recommendation. The deadline is June 15. I have attached my résumé and draft statement, and I am glad to meet to discuss."
step: Close with thanks and full name: "Thank you for considering this. Sincerely, Maya Torres, Class of 2026." The email now states the request in the first two sentences and gives the reader what is needed to act.

@mistakes
- Telling a team story with "we" throughout, so the interviewer cannot tell what you did.
- Writing a long email that buries the request in the last paragraph.
- Omitting the recommendation in an SBAR, so the listener does not know what you want.
- Including identifiable patient information in unsecured email or text.
