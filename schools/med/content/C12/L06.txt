@article
A **fallacy** is a pattern of reasoning that looks persuasive but does not support its conclusion. Fallacies are not just forms of rudeness; they are errors in the link between reasons and claim. Naming them helps you say exactly what is wrong. Naming them is also a tool to be used carefully: a fallacy label shows that an argument fails, not that its conclusion is false. A conclusion can be true even when it was argued badly.

### Common fallacies

| Fallacy | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ad hominem | Attacks the person instead of the argument | "Dr. Lee's dosing study is worthless; she once worked for a drug company." |
| Straw man | Distorts an opponent's view, then refutes the distortion | "He wants fewer antibiotics, so he wants infections left untreated." |
| False dilemma | Presents two options as if no others exist | "Either we ban all screening or we accept overdiagnosis." |
| Slippery slope | Claims one step will inevitably lead to extreme results without support | "If we allow this form, soon no records will be private." |
| Appeal to authority | Treats an authority's say-so as proof, especially outside their expertise | "A famous actor says vaccines are unsafe." |
| Appeal to popularity | Argues something is true because many believe it | "Millions use this remedy, so it works." |
| Appeal to nature | Argues that natural means safe or good | "It is herbal, so it cannot harm you." |
| Post hoc | Assumes that because B followed A, A caused B | "I took the tonic and my cold ended, so the tonic cured it." |
| Hasty generalization | Draws a broad conclusion from too few cases | "Two of my patients refused, so patients today distrust doctors." |
| Circular reasoning | The conclusion is assumed in the premise | "This treatment is effective because it works." |
| Equivocation | Uses a word with two meanings as if it had one | "Natural selection is selection, and selection implies a selector." |
| Anecdotal evidence | Uses one story in place of data | "My uncle smoked and lived to 95." |

### Fallacy or weak argument?

Not every flaw is a fallacy. Citing an expert on a question in the expert's own field is a legitimate, if fallible, kind of evidence; the **fallacy** is citing someone with no relevant expertise or treating authority as final. Pointing out that a source has a financial conflict is relevant to how closely you check the methods, but it becomes an **ad hominem** if used to dismiss the data without examining them.

### Unsupported claims

Besides fallacies, editorials often contain claims that are simply not supported: statistics with no source ("studies show..."), certainty beyond the evidence ("everyone knows"), loaded wording that assumes the point ("the so-called experts"), and sweeping words (always, never, all). To mark an editorial, number each claim, mark each as *supported* (with source or data), *anecdotal*, or *unsupported*, and note any fallacy.

### Repairing an argument

To repair a fallacious argument, identify the real claim, state the reasons that could support it, and look for evidence. "The tonic cured my cold" can become "Colds resolve in about a week with or without treatment, so my recovery cannot show that the tonic worked; a controlled comparison would."

@example
title: Marking an editorial
problem: (Invented editorial paragraph.) "Dr. Ruiz says hospitals should cut routine blood tests, but she has never worked a night shift. Surely she wants patients to go undiagnosed. Everyone knows more testing means better care." Identify the fallacies and unsupported claims.
step: "She has never worked a night shift" attacks the speaker rather than her argument about test use: ad hominem.
step: "Surely she wants patients to go undiagnosed" distorts her position (cutting routine tests is not the same as wanting undiagnosed patients): straw man.
step: "Everyone knows more testing means better care" is a sweeping, unsourced assertion that also appeals to popularity; it is an unsupported claim.
step: None of the three sentences addresses her actual reasons or evidence. A repaired response would state her argument fairly and cite data on outcomes with and without routine testing.

@mistakes
- Concluding that a claim is false because the argument for it contains a fallacy.
- Calling any appeal to an expert a fallacy, even when the expert has relevant expertise and the claim is checkable.
- Using the label as a substitute for explanation, rather than saying how the reasoning fails.
- Missing a straw man because the distortion is only slight.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented editorial.) "Hospital administrators claim that mandatory flu shots for staff protect patients. But these are the same people who cut our break rooms. If we accept this, next they will require every vaccine ever made, and then our medical choices will be gone. Last winter my cousin got the shot and caught the flu anyway. Obviously the shots don't work. Either we trust nurses to decide or we live under a dictatorship of the pharmacy." Mark each unsupported claim and fallacy. Name the fallacy for at least four sentences and explain in one clause why each fails.
hint: Number the sentences, then go through them one at a time.
hint: Look for an attack on persons, a chain of consequences without support, a single anecdote, and a two-option framing.
solution: (1) Ad hominem: the break-room cuts say nothing about whether vaccination protects patients. (2) Slippery slope: no evidence that one requirement leads to all vaccines. (3) Anecdote and hasty generalization: one cousin's influenza says nothing about vaccine effectiveness, which is partial, not absolute, and measured in populations. (4) "Obviously the shots don't work" is an unsupported claim built on that anecdote. (5) False dilemma: other options exist, such as targeted requirements, exemptions or masking policies. The first sentence's claim about patient protection is stated without data on both sides and would need evidence to evaluate.
rubric: Names at least four fallacies correctly (ad hominem, slippery slope, anecdote or hasty generalization, false dilemma).
rubric: Gives a one-clause reason for each failure.
rubric: Marks at least one unsupported claim.
rubric: Does not treat the editorial's conclusion as false merely because the arguments are bad.
