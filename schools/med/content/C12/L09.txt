@article
After two weeks of reading arguments, you now build one. Good argumentative writing is not louder than weak writing; it is clearer about what it claims and why.

### The thesis

A **thesis** is the claim your whole piece defends, in one or two sentences. A strong thesis is **specific** (it names what, for whom, and under what conditions), **debatable** (a reasonable person could disagree), and **provable** in the space you have. Compare:

- Weak (a topic): "This essay is about electronic health records."
- Weak (a fact): "Electronic health records are used in most U.S. hospitals."
- Weak (vague): "Electronic health records have good and bad points."
- Stronger: "Hospitals should give patients real-time access to clinic notes, because transparency improves safety and trust more than it increases anxiety."

The stronger thesis commits to a position and previews the reasons.

### Claim, evidence, reasoning

Each body paragraph can follow the **CER** pattern, extended with a link:

1. **Topic sentence**: the paragraph's claim, in service of the thesis.
2. **Evidence**: data, a study, a documented example or an authoritative source. Cite it.
3. **Reasoning (analysis)**: explain *how* the evidence supports the claim. Evidence never speaks for itself.
4. **Link**: connect back to the thesis or forward to the next paragraph.

Use **transitions** (first, moreover, however, as a result) to show the logic between ideas.

### Counterargument and rebuttal

Strong writers state the best objection to their view, in terms its defenders would accept, and then respond. A rebuttal may show the objection is mistaken, show it is true but outweighed, or modify the thesis to absorb it. Do this once, in a dedicated paragraph or within a paragraph, introduced with phrases such as "Critics argue that..." and "This concern is real, but...". Attacking a weaker version of the objection (a straw man) damages your credibility.

### Clarity and concision

- Prefer **active voice** and concrete subjects: "The committee approved the protocol," not "The protocol was approved by the committee."
- Replace **nominalizations** with verbs: "make a decision" becomes "decide."
- Cut filler: "due to the fact that" becomes "because," "it is important to note that" is usually deleted.
- Put the main point at the start of a sentence and keep subject and verb close.
- Use one term for one idea; do not vary words for elegance when precision matters.
- Define terms that the reader may not know.

### Structure and revision

A short argumentative essay often has: an introduction ending in the thesis; two or three body paragraphs; a counterargument and rebuttal; and a conclusion that states what follows from the argument rather than repeating it. Revise in passes: first for argument (is every paragraph needed for the thesis?), then for organization, then sentences, then proofreading. Read your work aloud, and cut every sentence that does not support a claim.

@example
title: Strengthening a thesis and a paragraph
problem: A student writes the thesis "Sleep is important for residents." Revise it into a strong thesis and write a topic sentence for the first body paragraph.
step: Diagnose: the claim is true but uncontroversial and vague. Nobody would argue against it, and it does not say what to do or why.
step: Decide on a specific position: for example, that duty-hour limits for residents should keep a protected sleep period.
step: Draft thesis: "Residency programs should guarantee a protected five-hour sleep period on overnight call, because fatigue raises the risk of errors more than the continuity of care gained by longer shifts."
step: Topic sentence for the first paragraph: "First, sleep deprivation measurably impairs the attention and judgment that safe prescribing requires." The next sentences would give evidence, explain how it supports the claim, and link to the thesis.

@mistakes
- Writing a thesis that is a topic ("This essay discusses..."), a fact, or a claim no one would dispute.
- Piling up quotations and statistics without explaining what they show.
- Refuting a weak, invented version of the opposing view.
- Ending with a restatement of the introduction instead of the argument's consequences.

@practice self
prompt: Write an argumentative paragraph of 120 to 160 words on this thesis: "Medical schools should teach students to read clinical notes aloud to patients." Include a topic sentence, one piece of evidence (you may invent a plausible example and label it as invented), an explanation, and one fair counterargument with a rebuttal.
hint: Start with the benefit you will defend, then choose evidence that shows that benefit.
hint: State the strongest objection (time, privacy or confusion) in terms its holders would accept before you rebut it.
solution: Reading notes aloud helps students write notes patients can accept. In an invented example, a student who read a draft note aloud to a patient learned that the phrase "non-compliant" sounded blaming and rewrote it as "had difficulty obtaining the medication." This shows that listening exposes tone and errors a silent reader misses, and that patients can correct facts in real time. Critics argue that reading aloud takes time clinicians do not have and may distress patients. That concern is real, but a brief review of the plan and results takes only minutes, can be offered rather than required, and the time saved from corrected errors and misunderstandings offsets part of the cost. Teaching the habit therefore improves both accuracy and respect.
rubric: Opens with a topic sentence that supports the thesis.
rubric: Includes evidence (labeled invented if made up) and explains how it supports the claim.
rubric: States a fair counterargument and responds to it.
rubric: Is 120 to 160 words, with clear, concise sentences.
