@article
In medicine you rarely copy a source; you condense it for colleagues, patients and committees. Doing this well depends on three skills: quoting, paraphrasing and summarizing. Each must be accurate and fairly attributed.

### Three tools

- A **quotation** reproduces the source's exact words in quotation marks with a citation. Use it when the wording itself matters (a definition, a striking phrase, a legal or ethical text). Medical writing uses quotation sparingly.
- A **paraphrase** restates a specific passage in your own words and sentence structure, at about the same length. It conveys the same detail and still needs a citation.
- A **summary** condenses a whole passage or paper to its main points, in much less space, and also needs a citation.

### How to paraphrase

1. Read until you understand the passage; then look away from it.
2. Write the idea from memory, in your own words and structure.
3. Compare with the original: check that the meaning, numbers, and degree of certainty are the same, and that you have not kept its distinctive phrases.
4. Cite the source.

Changing a few words (**patchwriting**) is not paraphrase. If your sentence follows the original's order and swaps synonyms, you have not rewritten it, and it can count as plagiarism even if you cite the source.

### How to summarize an abstract

A good summary of a study abstract answers: who was studied, what was done, what was found (with the key number), and what the authors concluded, in order and in proportion. Keep:

- **Numbers** and units (do not round differently or omit the comparison group).
- **Hedges**: "may be associated with" is not "causes"; "suggests" is not "shows."
- **Scope**: do not turn "in 48 students" into "in people."

Leave out: background the reader does not need, secondary results, and your opinions. A summary should contain nothing that is not in the source. If you want to add a criticism, put it in a separate sentence that is clearly marked as yours.

### Accuracy errors to avoid

- **Overstating**: changing "associated with" to "caused."
- **Understating**: dropping a major finding or a significant caveat.
- **Altering scope**: generalizing beyond the participants studied.
- **Dropping the comparison**: "Patients improved" without saying compared with what.
- **Introducing new claims** that the source does not make.

### Attribution

Say whose claim it is: "The authors report that...," "According to a 2020 trial...". Follow the citation style your field uses (the AMA style is common in medicine, with numbered references). When in doubt, cite.

@example
title: Paraphrasing and summarizing accurately
problem: (Invented source sentence.) "In this single-center pilot study of 60 adults with mild hypertension, a 12-week walking program was associated with a mean fall in systolic pressure of 6 mmHg compared with no change in the control group, although the study was not powered to detect differences in cardiovascular events." Write a one-sentence summary.
step: Identify the essentials: population (60 adults with mild hypertension, one center), intervention (12-week walking program), comparison (control group), result (6 mmHg fall in systolic pressure versus no change), hedge ("associated with"), and limitation (not powered for cardiovascular events).
step: Check what to leave out: nothing here can be dropped without changing the meaning, but the sentence can be shortened by dropping "single-center" if the scope is kept elsewhere.
step: Draft: "In a small pilot of 60 adults with mild hypertension, a 12-week walking program was associated with a 6 mmHg lower systolic pressure than in controls, but the study could not assess effects on cardiovascular events."
step: Check against the source: the number, the comparison and the hedge are preserved, and no causal claim has been added.

@mistakes
- Replacing a few words with synonyms and calling it a paraphrase.
- Turning "associated with" into "causes," or "may" into "does."
- Leaving out the comparison group or the sample size.
- Adding your own judgment inside the summary without marking it as yours.

prompt: (Invented abstract.) "Background: Hand hygiene prompts may improve compliance. Methods: In a before-and-after study on two surgical wards (total 85 staff) over 6 months, we installed reminder signs at sinks. Results: Observed hand-hygiene compliance rose from 52% to 67% (difference 15 percentage points, 95% CI 8 to 22). Compliance on a third ward with no signs changed from 55% to 57%. Conclusions: Reminder signs were associated with improved compliance. Because the design was not randomized, other changes may have contributed." Write a summary of 45 words or fewer that is accurate, keeps the hedges and the key numbers, and adds nothing new.
hint: Use the order: who and what was done, what was found, how sure the authors are.
hint: Include the comparison ward; it is part of what makes the result believable.
solution: In a non-randomized before-and-after study on two surgical wards, reminder signs at sinks were associated with a rise in observed hand-hygiene compliance from 52% to 67%, versus 55% to 57% on a ward without signs; the authors caution that other changes may have contributed.
rubric: Keeps the design (non-randomized, before and after) and the hedge "associated with."
rubric: Includes the main numbers (52% to 67%) and the comparison ward.
rubric: Notes the authors' caution about other contributing changes.
rubric: Is 45 words or fewer and adds no claims absent from the abstract.
