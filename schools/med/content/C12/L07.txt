@article
Research papers look forbidding because they are dense, but most follow a predictable structure called **IMRaD**: Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion. Knowing what each part is for lets you read selectively and critically.

### The sections

- **Abstract.** A summary of the whole paper, usually structured as background, methods, results and conclusions. It is the easiest part to read and the easiest to over-trust: authors present their best case there.
- **Introduction.** What is known, what gap remains, and the specific question or hypothesis.
- **Methods.** Who was studied and how (design, setting, inclusion and exclusion criteria), what was measured (the **primary outcome** named in advance), and how data were analyzed. A reader should be able to judge whether the study could answer its question.
- **Results.** What was found, with tables and figures. Results should be reported without interpretation.
- **Discussion.** What the authors think the results mean, how they compare with earlier work, the **limitations**, and the conclusion. Speculation belongs here, and you should label it as such in your own notes.
- **Funding and conflicts of interest**, often at the end, and the **references**.

### A reading order

Many experienced readers do not read front to back. A practical order is: (1) title and abstract to decide whether the paper is relevant; (2) the stated question in the introduction; (3) the methods, asking whether the design fits the question; (4) tables and figures in the results; (5) the limitations in the discussion; (6) the abstract again, to check whether it matches what you found. Write down the PICO as you go: **P**opulation, **I**ntervention (or exposure), **C**omparison, **O**utcome.

### Reading the numbers

A **relative risk (RR)** is the risk in the treated (or exposed) group divided by the risk in the comparison group. RR = 1 means no difference; RR below 1 means lower risk in the treated group. A **95% confidence interval (CI)** gives a range of values compatible with the data, at that confidence level; if the CI for an RR includes 1, the data are compatible with no effect. A **p-value** is the probability of seeing a result at least as extreme as the one observed if the null hypothesis were true. It is not the probability that the hypothesis is true, and it says nothing about the size or importance of an effect. A statistically significant result (conventionally p below 0.05) can be clinically trivial, and a nonsignificant result from a small study does not prove there is no effect.

### Common design points

Check for **randomization** and **blinding**, for the proportion lost to follow-up, and whether analysis followed **intention to treat** (everyone analyzed in the group to which they were assigned) or only participants who completed the protocol. Check whether the primary outcome is **clinically meaningful** or a surrogate (such as a lab value). Be wary of subgroup analyses that were not planned.

@example
title: Reading a result
problem: (Invented trial.) In a randomized trial of 2,000 adults, 150 of 1,000 given drug Q and 200 of 1,000 given placebo were hospitalized. The authors report RR 0.75 (95% CI 0.62 to 0.91). Interpret the result.
step: Compute the risks: 150/1,000 = 15% with drug Q and 200/1,000 = 20% with placebo.
step: RR = 0.15 / 0.20 = 0.75. Drug Q was associated with a 25% lower relative risk of hospitalization.
step: The 95% CI runs from 0.62 to 0.91, entirely below 1, so the data are not compatible with no effect at that level, and the finding is statistically significant.
step: Absolute difference: 20% - 15% = 5 percentage points, so about 20 patients must be treated to prevent one hospitalization. Whether this matters depends on side effects, cost and the population studied.

@mistakes
- Reading only the abstract and treating the authors' conclusions as the findings.
- Interpreting a p-value as the probability that the hypothesis is true.
- Assuming a nonsignificant result shows that there is no effect.
- Confusing the results (what was found) with the discussion (what the authors think it means).

@practice num
prompt: In an invented trial, 30 of 200 patients in the treatment arm and 50 of 200 patients in the control arm had the outcome. What is the relative risk? Give an exact fraction or decimal.
answer: 3/5
check: (30 / 200) / (50 / 200)
hint: Compute each group's risk before dividing.
solution: Treatment risk = 0.15 and control risk = 0.25, so RR = 0.15 / 0.25 = 0.6 (3/5).

answer: 364
check: 400 - 36
hint: A per-protocol analysis leaves out participants who did not complete the protocol (an intention-to-treat analysis would keep everyone randomized).

@practice self
prompt: (Invented abstract.) "Background: Sleep loss may impair memory in students. Methods: We recruited 48 volunteer first-year students at one university and randomly assigned them to normal sleep or to a night of restricted sleep, then tested recall of 40 word pairs. Results: Mean recall was 28 pairs (normal sleep) and 24 pairs (restricted), difference 4 (95% CI 1.6 to 6.4). Conclusions: Sleep restriction impairs memory, and students should avoid all-night study." List, in short phrases, the PICO, the main result, two limitations, and one point where the conclusion goes beyond the data.
hint: Population, intervention, comparison and outcome can be taken from the Methods sentence.
hint: Ask what the study did not test (long-term memory, real exams, other populations).
solution: P: 48 volunteer first-year students at one university. I: one night of restricted sleep. C: normal sleep. O: recall of 40 word pairs. Result: 4 more pairs recalled with normal sleep (95% CI 1.6 to 6.4), so the interval excludes zero. Limitations: small sample of volunteers from one university; a word-pair test may not reflect real study or exams; only one night tested. The conclusion about avoiding "all-night study" goes beyond the data because the study tested one night of restriction, not all-night study as a strategy or exam performance.
rubric: Identifies population, intervention, comparison and outcome correctly.
rubric: States the main result with the confidence interval and its meaning.
rubric: Gives two plausible limitations.
rubric: Identifies one overreach in the conclusion.

@quiz mc
prompt: A paper's abstract states that a supplement "significantly improved" mood. In the Results, the improvement was 0.5 points on a 100-point scale (p = 0.03) in a study of 6,000 people. What is the best reading?
choice: The effect is large and important for every patient.
choice*: The effect is statistically detectable but very small and probably not clinically meaningful.
choice: The p-value shows the supplement has a 97% chance of working.
choice: The result is meaningless because p is above 0.01.
hint: Distinguish statistical significance from clinical importance.
solution: With a large sample even tiny differences can reach p below 0.05. A half-point change on a 100-point scale is unlikely to matter to patients. The p-value is not the probability that the supplement works.
