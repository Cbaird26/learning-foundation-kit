@article
A claim is only as good as the evidence behind it. Evaluating evidence is not the same as deciding whether you like the conclusion. It means asking how the evidence was gathered, what it can show, and whether the claim goes further than the evidence does.

### Kinds of evidence

Evidence varies in strength. A rough ladder for claims about treatments, from weaker to stronger, is: expert opinion or anecdote; case reports and case series; case-control and cohort studies (observational); randomized controlled trials (RCTs); and systematic reviews of RCTs. Higher is not always better for every question (an RCT cannot ethically test whether smoking harms), but for the claim "this treatment causes improvement," randomization is powerful because it balances known and unknown differences between groups.

### Source and sample

Ask who produced the evidence and what they gain. Funding by a manufacturer does not make a study false, but it is a reason to read the methods closely. Then ask about the **sample**: how many people, chosen how, and do they resemble the people the claim is about? A small sample gives imprecise estimates. A sample of volunteers or of patients at one specialist center may differ from the general population (**selection bias**). A survey that only 25% of recipients answer may reflect those who feel strongly (**non-response bias**).

### Correlation and causation

Two things that occur together are **correlated**, but one need not cause the other. Alternatives to "A causes B" include: B causes A (**reverse causation**); a third factor causes both (**confounding**); and chance. Patients who see more doctors die sooner, yet the visits did not cause the deaths: illness drives both. Look for a mechanism, a dose-response pattern, a correct time order (the cause comes first), and consistency across studies.

### Presenting numbers

The same result can be dressed differently. Suppose 10% of untreated patients and 6% of treated patients have a stroke in five years.

- **Absolute risk reduction (ARR)** = control risk - treatment risk = 10% - 6% = 4 percentage points.
- **Relative risk reduction (RRR)** = ARR / control risk = 4 / 10 = 40%.
- **Number needed to treat (NNT)** = 1 / ARR = 1 / 0.04 = 25.

"Cuts stroke risk by 40%" and "25 people must be treated for five years to prevent one stroke" describe the same finding, but the first sounds much larger. When a relative figure is quoted without the baseline risk, ask for the absolute one. A 50% reduction of a 2-in-100,000 risk helps almost no one.

### Warning signs of an unsupported claim

- Words such as "proves," "everyone," "always" without data.
- A cited source that is not named or cannot be found.
- Anecdote presented as typical.
- A causal verb ("causes," "prevents") attached to correlational data.
- A result from animals, cells, or a narrow group applied to all patients.

@example
title: Absolute, relative and number needed to treat
problem: In an invented trial, 15% of patients taking placebo and 9% of patients taking drug X were hospitalized within a year. Calculate the ARR, the RRR and the NNT.
step: ARR is the difference in risk: 15% - 9% = 6 percentage points, or 0.06.
step: RRR is ARR divided by the control risk: 0.06 / 0.15 = 0.40, or 40%.
step: NNT is the reciprocal of ARR: 1 / 0.06 = 16.67, which is rounded up to 17 when reported as a count of people.
step: Interpretation: drug X cuts the hospitalization risk by 40% in relative terms; about 17 patients must be treated for a year to prevent one hospitalization.

@mistakes
- Quoting the relative risk reduction with no baseline, which makes small effects sound large.
- Concluding causation from an association between two measurements.
- Treating a large sample as proof of good design when the sample was chosen in a biased way.
- Confusing percentage points (an absolute difference) with percent (a relative change).

prompt: A news story reports: "People who take vitamin supplements live longer." Which is the most plausible confounding explanation?
choice: Vitamins are expensive.
choice*: People who take supplements may also exercise more, smoke less and see doctors regularly.
choice: Supplements are sold in pharmacies.
choice: Living longer causes people to buy vitamins.
hint: A confounder is a third factor linked to both the exposure and the outcome.
solution: Health-conscious habits may lead to both supplement use and longer life, so the association need not mean supplements cause longer life.

choice: A clinician's report of three patients who improved.
choice: A survey in which patients say the drug works.
choice*: A well-conducted randomized controlled trial comparing the drug with usual care.

@practice num
prompt: In an invented trial, 20% of control patients and 15% of treated patients had a relapse in one year. What is the number needed to treat to prevent one relapse? Give a whole number.
answer: 20
check: 1 / (Fraction(20, 100) - Fraction(15, 100))
hint: Find the absolute risk reduction first.
solution: ARR = 0.20 - 0.15 = 0.05. NNT = 1 / 0.05 = 20.

@practice num
prompt: In an invented study, a drug lowers the risk of an event from 8% in the control group to 6% in the treated group. What is the relative risk reduction, in percent?
answer: 25
check: (8 - 6) / 8 * 100
hint: Divide the absolute difference by the control risk.
solution: ARR = 2 percentage points. RRR = 2 / 8 = 0.25, or 25%.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented news item.) "A new study proves that coffee prevents heart disease. Researchers surveyed 60 office workers at one company, and those who drank four or more cups a day reported fewer heart problems. The lead author, who consults for a coffee brand, called the result 'life-changing.'" In four to six sentences, list the problems with the evidence and say what you would need to see before accepting the claim.
hint: Check the verb ("proves," "prevents"), the sample, how the outcome was measured and who benefits.
hint: Think of at least two alternative explanations for the association.
solution: The story uses causal and absolute language ("proves," "prevents") for a survey, which can show only association. The sample is 60 workers from one company, too small and not representative. Heart problems were self-reported, which is unreliable. Healthy people may drink more coffee, and healthier lifestyles are a plausible confounder. The author has a financial tie to the industry, which calls for scrutiny of methods. To accept the claim I would want larger prospective studies with measured outcomes, adjustment for confounders and ideally a randomized trial or a systematic review with consistent results.
rubric: Notes that the design supports association, not causation, and flags the causal wording.
rubric: Criticizes the sample (size, selection) and the self-reported outcome.
rubric: Suggests at least one confounder or alternative explanation.
rubric: Names the stronger evidence that would be needed.

@quiz ms
prompt: A press release says: "Our herbal tonic reduced colds by 50%." It cites a trial with 30 volunteers who chose to take the tonic and compared them with 30 who declined. Select every problem with the evidence.
choice*: Participants chose their own group, so the groups may differ in other ways.
choice*: The sample is small, so the estimate is imprecise.
choice*: The statement gives a relative reduction without the baseline number of colds.
choice: The trial included volunteers, which makes the results always more reliable.
hint: Think about randomization, size and how numbers are reported.
solution: Self-selection into groups invites confounding, 60 people give imprecise results, and "50%" without baseline rates hides the absolute change. Volunteering does not increase reliability.

@quiz num
prompt: A survey about patient satisfaction was mailed to 500 patients, and 125 patients returned it. What was the response rate, in percent?
answer: 25
check: 125 / 500 * 100
hint: Divide the returned surveys by the number mailed.
solution: 125 / 500 = 0.25, so the response rate was 25%. A low rate raises the possibility of non-response bias.
