@review
## Week-by-week review

**Week 1: Reading arguments closely.** Identify the claim, reasons, evidence and assumptions. Use signposts (therefore, because, although, but) to find the structure, and remember that a concession followed by "but" usually puts the author's real view after the turn.

**Week 2: Main idea, tone and purpose.** The topic is the subject; the main idea is a full sentence about it. Tone comes from diction, syntax and emphasis; purpose is what the author wants the reader to understand or do. Map a dense passage in four lines.

**Week 3: Reasoning within the text.** Only what must be true given the text counts. Watch "some," "most" and "may." The contrapositive of "if P then Q" is "if not Q then not P"; the converse and inverse do not follow.

**Week 4: Reasoning beyond the text.** Extract the principle, check the conditions in the new case, and apply it. Strengthen by ruling out alternatives; weaken by attacking an assumption. Check analogies for relevant differences.

**Week 5: Evaluating evidence.** Consider design, source, sample and numbers. ARR = control risk - treated risk; RRR = ARR / control risk; NNT = 1 / ARR. Association is not causation.

**Week 6: Logical fallacies.** Name the error and say how the reasoning fails: ad hominem, straw man, false dilemma, slippery slope, post hoc, hasty generalization, circularity and others. A fallacy does not prove a conclusion false.

**Week 7: Reading scientific papers.** Use IMRaD. Extract PICO. Interpret relative risk, confidence intervals and p-values without overstating, and separate results from discussion.

**Week 8: Summarizing and paraphrasing.** Keep numbers, hedges and scope. Restructure, do not patchwrite, and cite.

**Week 9: Writing clear arguments.** A specific, debatable thesis; paragraphs with claim, evidence, reasoning and link; a fair counterargument; active, concise sentences.

**Week 10: Ethics essays.** Four principles, capacity, the six-part structure, and honest weighing using features of the case.

**Week 11: Personal statements.** Scenes and reflection; no clichés, overclaiming or identifiable details.

**Week 12: Interviews and professional writing.** STAR, SBAR, clear subject lines and a request in the first lines.

### Self-check

- Can I map the argument of a passage in one sentence per part?
- Can I tell what a passage states, implies and does not support?
- Can I compute ARR, RRR, NNT and relative risk, and explain a confidence interval?
- Can I summarize an abstract without changing a number or a hedge?
- Can I write a thesis that someone could dispute?
- Can I weigh two principles using features of a case?

### Common errors

Treating the concession as the author's view; confusing topic with main idea; using outside knowledge on an inference question; quoting relative risk without baseline; calling an argument false because it is fallacious; turning "associated with" into "caused"; writing a thesis that is a topic; listing principles without weighing them; telling instead of showing in a statement.

@exam mc
prompt: (Invented editorial excerpt.) "A large survey found that residents who drink coffee during night shifts make fewer documentation errors. The hospital should therefore provide free coffee to all residents. Critics who oppose the plan simply do not care about patient safety." Which best describes the editorial's reasoning?
choice: It is sound, because a large survey always shows causation.
choice*: It moves from an association to a causal policy claim without ruling out other explanations, and it dismisses critics with an ad hominem attack.
choice: It is circular, because it restates its conclusion as its premise.
choice: It is a false dilemma between coffee and no coffee, and nothing else.
choice: It is a fair analogy between coffee and safety checklists.
hint: Look at the survey's design, the policy conclusion, and the last sentence.
hint: Two separate errors are present: one concerns evidence, the other concerns how critics are treated.
solution: The survey shows association only (residents who choose coffee may differ in experience, sleep or workload). The conclusion that coffee should be provided assumes it caused the effect. The last sentence attacks critics' motives instead of answering their reasons, which is ad hominem.

@exam num
prompt: (Invented trial.) In a randomized trial with 800 patients in each arm, 12% of patients receiving usual care and 9% of patients receiving drug M had a stroke within two years. If 10,000 similar patients were treated with drug M instead of usual care, how many strokes would be expected to be prevented?
answer: 300
check: 10000 * (0.12 - 0.09)
hint: Find the absolute risk reduction first, then apply it to the larger group.
hint: The sample sizes in the trial are not needed once you have the risks.
solution: ARR = 12% - 9% = 3 percentage points = 0.03. For 10,000 patients, 0.03 * 10,000 = 300 strokes prevented. (The NNT is about 33, and 10,000 / 33.3 is also 300.) This assumes the trial results apply to the larger group, which depends on how similar the patients are.

@exam self
prompt: (Invented abstract.) "Background: Mindfulness training may reduce burnout in medical students. Methods: In a randomized trial at one school, 90 first-year students were assigned to an eight-week mindfulness course or to a waitlist. Burnout was measured by a self-report scale (0 to 100). Results: At eight weeks, mean burnout was 41 in the mindfulness group and 48 in the waitlist group (difference 7; 95% CI 1 to 13). Conclusions: Mindfulness training reduced burnout. Limitations: single school; self-reported outcome; no long-term follow-up." Write (a) a summary of 40 words or fewer that keeps the design, the key numbers and the hedges, and (b) three to four sentences evaluating how far the conclusion is supported, and what a thoughtful student would still ask.
hint: For the summary, include the design, the sample, the result with the comparison and the main limitation.
hint: For the evaluation, consider randomization, the confidence interval, the outcome measure and the generalizability.
solution: (a) In a randomized trial of 90 first-year students at one school, an eight-week mindfulness course lowered self-reported burnout by 7 points versus a waitlist (95% CI 1 to 13); the authors note one school and no long-term follow-up. (b) Randomization supports a causal interpretation, and the interval excludes zero, so the effect is statistically significant. However, the interval is wide (from barely any effect to a substantial one), the outcome is self-reported and participants knew which group they were in, so expectation could influence reports. The study is from one school with no follow-up, so it is unclear whether the effect lasts or applies to other students. I would ask whether a 7-point difference matters to students, how it compares with other interventions, and whether results were replicated.
rubric: The summary preserves design, sample size, the key result with comparison, and a limitation, in 40 words or fewer.
rubric: The evaluation notes the strength of randomization and the meaning of the confidence interval.
rubric: The evaluation raises at least two limits (self-report, unblinded, single site, follow-up, size of effect).
rubric: Does not overstate or add claims beyond the abstract.
