@article
In Week 1 you took arguments apart into claim, reasons and evidence. This week you step back and ask three larger questions: what is the passage mainly saying, how does the author feel about it, and why was it written?

### Topic versus main idea

The **topic** is what a passage is about, in a few words: "pain scales." The **main idea** is what the author says about the topic, as a full sentence: "Numeric pain scales give clinicians a shared language but flatten the patient's experience." A good main idea covers the whole passage. A statement that matches only one paragraph is a **detail**; a statement broader than the passage (such as "Medicine is complicated") is **too general**. To test a candidate, ask whether removing it would leave the passage without its point.

### Tone

**Tone** is the author's attitude toward the subject, carried by **diction** (word choice), **syntax** (sentence structure), and what is emphasized or left out. Compare:

- "The committee's report offers a thorough and balanced account." (approving)
- "The committee's report offers an account, of a sort." (skeptical, close to sarcastic)
- "The committee's report omits three of the five relevant studies." (critical, flat and factual)

Words carry **connotation** beyond their dictionary meaning: "thrifty," "frugal" and "cheap" all describe low spending, but they are respectively admiring, neutral and insulting. Scientific writing often aims at a **neutral** or **objective** tone, but even neutral prose shows attitude through hedges ("may," "appears") and intensifiers ("clearly," "merely"). Name tone with a precise adjective rather than "good" or "bad": appreciative, wary, ironic, urgent, dismissive, measured, reverent, indignant.

### Author's purpose

The **purpose** is what the author wants the reader to do or understand. Common purposes are to **inform** (explain a method), **persuade** (urge a policy), **reflect** (make sense of an experience), **evaluate** (judge a work or a claim), and **entertain**. Purpose is shaped by audience: the same facts about antibiotic resistance appear one way in a journal for infectious disease specialists, another in a letter to a patient, and another in an op-ed for the public. Ask who the intended reader is, and what the author hopes will change.

### Mapping a dense passage

Dense humanities prose packs a claim, a qualification and an example into a single sentence. Work sentence by sentence: cut each into its clauses, mark the main clause, and write a plain-language paraphrase in the margin. Then assemble a map with four lines: **Topic; Main idea; Tone and purpose; Argument chain** (reason leading to reason leading to claim).

@example
title: Topic, main idea, tone and purpose
problem: (Invented passage.) "We praise the doctor who 'never loses a patient's name,' as though memory were the whole of regard. Yet a name recalled by rote is a sort of clerical courtesy; the regard that matters is shown in the question that follows it. If the profession wants kinder physicians, it should teach attention, not mnemonic tricks." State the topic, main idea, tone and purpose.
step: Topic: remembering patients' names, and what it shows about regard. This is only the subject.
step: Main idea (a full sentence about the topic): remembering names is a weak sign of real regard, so medical teaching should cultivate attention rather than memory tricks.
step: Tone: gently skeptical and ironic. The scare quotes around "never loses a patient's name" and the phrases "a sort of clerical courtesy" and "mnemonic tricks" signal mild disdain for the praised habit.
step: Purpose: to persuade, since the final sentence recommends a change in what the profession should teach.

@mistakes
- Giving the topic ("patient names") when asked for the main idea, which must say something about the topic.
- Choosing a detail from one paragraph as the main idea.
- Naming tone with "negative" or "positive" instead of a precise word, or reading sarcasm as sincerity.
- Assuming that formal or scientific prose has no tone and no purpose beyond informing.

@practice ms
prompt: (Invented passage.) "The editorial announces, with the confidence of a man who has never staffed a night shift, that overtime is 'a solved problem.' One admires the serenity." Select every feature that contributes to the author's ironic tone.
choice*: The phrase "with the confidence of a man who has never staffed a night shift"
choice*: The scare quotes around "a solved problem"
choice*: The closing remark "One admires the serenity."
choice: The use of the word "editorial"
hint: Irony says one thing while meaning another; look for praise that is really criticism.
solution: The aside mocks the editor's lack of experience, the scare quotes distance the author from the claim, and the final sentence praises where it means to criticize. The word "editorial" only names the genre.

@practice self
prompt: (Invented passage, a dense humanities argument.) "It is commonly held that the stethoscope made the physician a better listener. The opposite may be nearer the truth: by lending the ear a mediating object, it licensed the physician to stop attending to the patient's voice and to attend instead to the chest. Instruments do not simply extend our senses; they redirect our attention, and what they redirect it from is rarely counted." Write a four-line map: topic, main idea (full sentence), tone and purpose, and the argument chain.
hint: Begin by finding the sentence that sounds like a conclusion, then the sentence it contradicts.
hint: The chain should read like: common belief rejected, because the instrument redirects attention, so instruments have an uncounted cost.
solution: Topic: the stethoscope and listening. Main idea: instruments such as the stethoscope redirect attention away from the patient's voice, so they may make physicians worse rather than better listeners. Tone and purpose: measured, mildly contrarian, and reflective; the purpose is to persuade readers to reconsider a common belief. Chain: common belief that the stethoscope improves listening is questioned; the instrument mediates hearing and shifts attention to the chest; therefore instruments' costs in attention are unrecorded.
rubric: States a main idea as a full sentence, not a topic phrase.
rubric: Names a precise tone adjective and a purpose.
rubric: Shows the chain of reasoning from the rejected belief to the conclusion.
rubric: Does not simply copy sentences from the passage.
