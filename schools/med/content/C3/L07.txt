@article
The periodic table is organized so that elements with similar valence electron configurations fall in the same column. Because chemistry is done by valence electrons, properties vary in regular, predictable ways, called **periodic trends**. Nearly all of them follow from two ideas.

### Two controlling ideas

**Effective nuclear charge** $Z_{\text{eff}}$ is the net positive charge felt by a valence electron: the nuclear charge $Z$ minus the shielding by inner electrons. As a rough estimate, $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - (\text{number of core electrons})$. Electrons in the same shell shield each other only weakly, so across a period $Z_{\text{eff}}$ rises steadily.

**Shell number** $n$ sets how far the valence electrons are from the nucleus. Moving down a group adds a shell, so the valence electrons sit farther out and are shielded by a larger core.

### Atomic radius

Across a period (left to right), $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases at nearly constant $n$, pulling electrons inward: **atomic radius decreases**. Down a group, the added shells dominate: **atomic radius increases**. Sodium is therefore large and chlorine is small.

### Ionic radius

A cation is smaller than its parent atom (fewer electrons, less repulsion, often a lost shell). An anion is larger than its parent atom (more electron-electron repulsion for the same nuclear charge). For **isoelectronic** species, which have the same number of electrons, the one with the larger nuclear charge is smaller: $\text{O}^{2-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Na}^+ > \text{Mg}^{2+}$.

### Ionization energy

The **first ionization energy** $\text{IE}_1$ is the energy needed to remove one electron from a gaseous atom: $\text{X}(g) \to \text{X}^+(g) + e^-$. It increases across a period and decreases down a group, because a more tightly held, closer electron is harder to remove. Two familiar dips occur: Be to B (the $2p$ electron is higher in energy and better shielded than a $2s$ electron) and N to O (the paired $2p$ electron in oxygen feels repulsion from its partner).

**Successive ionization energies** always increase, because each electron is removed from a more positive ion. A huge jump signals the start of the core. For an element with IE values (kJ/mol) of $738, 1451, 7733$, the jump after the second electron shows two valence electrons, so it is in group 2 (magnesium).

### Electron affinity

**Electron affinity** is the energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron. Large negative values mean the atom readily accepts an electron; halogens have the most negative values. The trend is less regular than for ionization energy: chlorine has a slightly more negative electron affinity than fluorine because adding an electron to the compact $2p$ subshell of fluorine causes extra repulsion.

### Electronegativity

**Electronegativity** is the ability of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electrons. On the Pauling scale it increases across a period and decreases down a group, so fluorine ($3.98$) is the most electronegative element and cesium is among the least. The difference in electronegativity between bonded atoms predicts bond polarity, a theme in the next lessons.

### Metallic character

Metallic character (tendency to lose electrons) follows the opposite pattern of ionization energy: it decreases across a period and increases down a group. This is why the periodic table's metals gather at the left and bottom.

@example
title: Ranking atomic radii and ionization energies
problem: Rank Na, Mg, Al and Cl from largest to smallest atomic radius, and decide which has the highest first ionization energy.
step: All are in period 3, so the shell number is constant and $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases from Na to Cl.
step: Greater $Z_{\text{eff}}$ means smaller radius, so the radius order is $\text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Al} > \text{Cl}$.
step: Ionization energy generally increases with $Z_{\text{eff}}$, so chlorine has the highest first ionization energy of the four.
step: The one subtlety is Mg versus Al: $\text{IE}_1(\text{Al}) = 578$ kJ/mol is lower than $\text{IE}_1(\text{Mg}) = 738$ kJ/mol because Al's $3p$ electron is higher in energy and shielded by the $3s$ pair.

@example
title: Identifying a group from ionization energies
problem: An element has successive ionization energies (kJ/mol) of $496, 4562, 6910, 9543$. Which group is it in?
step: Compare consecutive ratios: $4562/496 \approx 9.2$, $6910/4562 \approx 1.5$, $9543/6910 \approx 1.4$.
step: The only huge jump comes after the first electron.
step: One easily removed valence electron means group 1 (this pattern matches sodium).

@mistakes
- Assuming ionization energy always increases smoothly across a period, ignoring the dips at Be to B and N to O.
- Saying fluorine has the most negative electron affinity (chlorine does) while correctly noting fluorine is the most electronegative.
- Thinking an anion is smaller than its atom because it "gained" electrons that were pulled in.
- Comparing isoelectronic ions by their charge magnitude instead of nuclear charge.

choice*: $\text{O}^{2-}$
choice: $\text{F}^-$
choice: $\text{Na}^+$
choice: $\text{Mg}^{2+}$

solution: With the same electron count, the species with the fewest protons ($8$ for oxygen) holds its electrons least tightly, so it is the largest.

answer: 7
check: 17 - (2 + 2 + 6)

solution: $Z_{\text{eff}} \approx Z - \text{core} = 17 - 10 = 7$.

prompt: Why is the first ionization energy of aluminum ($578$ kJ/mol) lower than that of magnesium ($738$ kJ/mol)?
choice: Aluminum has a larger nuclear charge, which weakens attraction to its electrons.
choice*: Aluminum's outermost electron is in a $3p$ orbital, which is higher in energy and shielded by the $3s$ electrons.
choice: Aluminum has a half-filled $p$ subshell, which is unusually stable.
choice: Aluminum's outermost electron is in a lower shell than magnesium's.
hint: Compare the subshells that hold the outermost electron in each atom.
solution: Mg loses a $3s$ electron, while Al loses a $3p$ electron, which lies slightly higher in energy and is partly shielded by the filled $3s$ subshell, so it is easier to remove.

@quiz ms

choice*: Effective nuclear charge increases.
choice*: Atomic radius decreases.
choice*: Electronegativity increases.
choice: Metallic character increases.
choice: Atomic radius increases.

solution: $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and electronegativity rise, and radius falls, as electrons are pulled in more tightly. Metallic character decreases from Na to Cl.

@quiz mc
