### Phases and phase diagrams

A **phase diagram** plots the stable phase of a pure substance as a function of pressure and temperature. Lines separate solid, liquid and gas, and the **triple point** is where all three coexist. The **critical point** is the end of the liquid-gas line: beyond it, no distinct liquid forms. For water, the solid-liquid line slopes slightly to the left, because ice is less dense than liquid water, so pressure can melt ice. For most substances the line slopes to the right.

### Types of solids

- **Ionic solids** (NaCl): ions in a lattice; hard, brittle, high melting point, conduct when molten or dissolved.
- **Molecular solids** (ice, $\text{I}_2$, glucose): molecules held by IMFs; soft, low melting point, nonconducting.
- **Covalent network solids** (diamond, quartz): atoms joined by covalent bonds throughout; very hard and very high melting point.
- **Metallic solids** (Cu, Fe): cations in a sea of delocalized electrons; malleable, conductive.

### Ways to express concentration

For a solute dissolved in a solvent:

$$M = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{V_{\text{solution}}\ (\text{L})}, \qquad m = \frac{n_{\text{solute}}}{m_{\text{solvent}}\ (\text{kg})}, \qquad \chi_A = \frac{n_A}{n_{\text{total}}}.$$

**Molarity** ($M$) depends on temperature because volumes expand; **molality** ($m$) does not, because it uses mass of solvent. Mole fractions of all components sum to one. For dilute aqueous solutions, molarity and molality are nearly equal. Clinicians also use **mass percent (w/v)**: $0.9\%$ w/v saline contains $0.9$ g of NaCl per $100$ mL, which is $9.0$ g per liter.

### Osmolarity

Colligative properties depend on the number of dissolved particles, not their identity. **Osmolarity** counts particles:
$$\text{osmolarity} = i \times M,$$
where $i$ is the number of particles per formula unit ($i = 2$ for NaCl, $3$ for $\text{CaCl}_2$, $1$ for glucose). The osmolarity of blood plasma is about $285$ to $295$ mOsm/L. A solution with the same osmolarity as plasma is **isotonic**. A $0.9\%$ saline solution is therefore approximately isotonic because NaCl at $9.0$ g/L is $0.154$ M with $i = 2$, giving about $308$ mOsm/L. Cells placed in hypotonic solutions swell (water enters), and in hypertonic solutions they shrink.

### Colligative properties

Dissolving a nonvolatile solute lowers the vapor pressure, raises the boiling point, lowers the freezing point and creates **osmotic pressure**:
$$\Delta T_f = i\,K_f\,m, \qquad \Delta T_b = i\,K_b\,m, \qquad \Pi = iMRT.$$
For water, $K_f = 1.86\ ^\circ\text{C kg/mol}$ and $K_b = 0.512\ ^\circ\text{C kg/mol}$. Osmotic pressure is the pressure needed to stop water from flowing across a semipermeable membrane into the more concentrated solution.

### Gas solubility: Henry's law

The concentration of a dissolved gas is proportional to its partial pressure above the liquid:
$$C = k_H\,P_{\text{gas}}.$$
This explains why a carbonated drink fizzes when opened, and why oxygen dissolved in blood plasma rises with alveolar $P_{\text{O}_2}$ (although most oxygen is carried by hemoglobin).

@example
title: Preparing and checking 0.9% saline
problem: How much NaCl is needed for $500.$ mL of $0.9\%$ (w/v) saline, and what are its molarity and osmolarity? ($M(\text{NaCl}) = 58.44$ g/mol.)
step: Mass: $0.9\%$ w/v is $0.9$ g per $100$ mL, so $500.$ mL needs $4.5$ g of NaCl.
step: Moles: $4.5/58.44 = 0.0770$ mol in $0.500$ L, so $M = 0.154$ M.
step: NaCl dissociates into $2$ ions per formula unit, so $i = 2$.
step: Osmolarity $= 2 \times 0.154 = 0.308$ Osm/L $= 308$ mOsm/L, close to plasma and therefore nearly isotonic.

@example
title: Molality and freezing-point depression
problem: A solution contains $18.0$ g of glucose ($M = 180.16$ g/mol) in $250.$ g of water. Find its molality and freezing point.
step: Moles of glucose: $18.0/180.16 = 0.0999$ mol.
step: Molality: $0.0999\ \text{mol}/0.250\ \text{kg} = 0.400$ mol/kg.
step: Glucose does not dissociate, so $i = 1$: $\Delta T_f = (1)(1.86)(0.400) = 0.74\,^\circ\text{C}$.
step: Freezing point: $0 - 0.74 = -0.74\,^\circ\text{C}$.

@mistakes
- Using the volume of solvent instead of the volume of solution for molarity, or the mass of solution for molality.
- Forgetting the van't Hoff factor $i$ when computing osmolarity or $\Delta T_f$.
- Subtracting a freezing-point depression from the wrong baseline or adding it instead of subtracting.
- Confusing percent w/v (g per $100$ mL) with percent w/w.

@practice num
prompt: A solution is prepared by dissolving $5.85$ g of NaCl ($M = 58.44$ g/mol) in $500.$ g of water. What is the molality, in mol/kg?
answer: 0.200
check: 5.85 / 58.44 / 0.500
tol: 1%
unit: mol/kg
hint: Convert grams of NaCl to moles, and grams of water to kilograms.
hint: $n = 0.100$ mol and the solvent mass is $0.500$ kg.
solution: $n = 5.85/58.44 = 0.1001$ mol. Molality $= 0.1001/0.500 = 0.200$ mol/kg.

@practice num
prompt: A mixture contains $1.00$ mol of ethanol and $4.00$ mol of water. What is the mole fraction of ethanol?
answer: 0.200
check: 1.00 / (1.00 + 4.00)
tol: 0.001
hint: Mole fraction is the moles of one component divided by the total moles.
hint: Total moles $= 5.00$.
solution: $\chi_{\text{ethanol}} = 1.00/5.00 = 0.200$.

prompt: A 5% (w/v) dextrose solution contains $50.0$ g of glucose per liter. Treating dextrose as glucose ($M = 180.16$ g/mol, $i = 1$), what is its osmolarity in mOsm/L?
answer: 278
check: 50.0 / 180.16 * 1000
tol: 1%
unit: mOsm/L

hint: $50.0/180.16$ mol/L, then multiply by $1000$ to get milliosmoles.
solution: $50.0/180.16 = 0.2775$ mol/L. With $i = 1$, osmolarity $= 0.2775$ Osm/L $= 278$ mOsm/L.

@practice num
prompt: What is the freezing-point depression, in $^\circ$C, of a $0.250$ mol/kg aqueous solution of $\text{CaCl}_2$? Assume complete dissociation and $K_f = 1.86\ ^\circ\text{C kg/mol}$.
answer: 1.40
check: 1.86 * 3 * 0.250
tol: 2%
unit: °C
hint: Find the van't Hoff factor for $\text{CaCl}_2$.
hint: $\text{CaCl}_2 \to \text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\,\text{Cl}^-$ gives $i = 3$.
solution: $\Delta T_f = iK_f m = (3)(1.86)(0.250) = 1.40\,^\circ\text{C}$.

@quiz num
prompt: One liter of an intravenous solution contains $4.68$ g of NaCl ($M = 58.44$ g/mol) and $9.00$ g of glucose ($M = 180.16$ g/mol). Assuming NaCl dissociates completely, what is the total osmolarity in mOsm/L?
answer: 210
check: (4.68 / 58.44 * 2 + 9.00 / 180.16) * 1000
tol: 1%
unit: mOsm/L
hint: Compute the osmoles from each solute separately.
hint: NaCl contributes $2 \times (4.68/58.44)$ Osm; glucose contributes $9.00/180.16$ Osm.
solution: NaCl: $4.68/58.44 = 0.0801$ mol, times $2$ gives $0.1602$ Osm. Glucose: $9.00/180.16 = 0.0500$ Osm. Total $0.2102$ Osm/L $= 210$ mOsm/L.
