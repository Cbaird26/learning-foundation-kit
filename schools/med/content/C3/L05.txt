@article
**Thermochemistry** is the study of heat flow in chemical and physical changes. We separate the universe into the **system** (the reaction mixture) and the **surroundings** (everything else). Energy that leaves the system enters the surroundings, and the total is conserved.

### Heat, temperature and heat capacity

**Heat** $q$ is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. By convention, $q > 0$ when the system absorbs heat (**endothermic**) and $q < 0$ when it releases heat (**exothermic**). The heat needed to change a sample's temperature is
$$q = mc\,\Delta T,$$
where $c$ is the **specific heat capacity** (for liquid water, $4.184\ \text{J g}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$) and $\Delta T = T_{\text{final}} - T_{\text{initial}}$. Water's large heat capacity is one reason it buffers body temperature.

### Calorimetry

A **calorimeter** isolates a reaction so that its heat can be measured. In a coffee-cup calorimeter at constant pressure, heat released by the reaction is absorbed by the solution:
$$q_{\text{rxn}} = -q_{\text{solution}} = -m c\,\Delta T.$$
A temperature rise means an exothermic reaction. Divide by the moles that reacted to obtain the molar enthalpy change.

### Enthalpy

At constant pressure, the heat exchanged equals the **enthalpy change** $\Delta H$. For a thermochemical equation, the sign and size of $\Delta H$ go with the amounts as written:
$$\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\,\text{O}_2(g) \to \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \qquad \Delta H^\circ = -890\ \text{kJ}.$$
Because $H$ is a **state function**, $\Delta H$ depends only on initial and final states, not on the path. It follows that reversing a reaction changes the sign of $\Delta H$, and multiplying a reaction by a factor multiplies $\Delta H$ by the same factor.

### Hess's law

**Hess's law**: if a reaction can be written as a sum of steps, its $\Delta H$ is the sum of the steps' $\Delta H$ values. To use it:

1. Arrange the given equations so that the species you want appear on the correct sides.
2. Reverse or scale equations as needed, changing $\Delta H$ accordingly.
3. Add the equations. Species that appear on both sides cancel, and the sum must equal the target equation.

### Standard enthalpies of formation

The **standard enthalpy of formation** $\Delta H^\circ_f$ is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states ($1$ bar); it is zero for an element in its standard state. Hess's law then gives, for any reaction,
$$\Delta H^\circ_{\text{rxn}} = \sum n\,\Delta H^\circ_f(\text{products}) - \sum n\,\Delta H^\circ_f(\text{reactants}),$$
where $n$ is each substance's coefficient. This is how most reaction enthalpies are found in practice, including the energy yield of metabolizing foods.

@example
title: Hess's law with two combustions
problem: Given $\text{C(graphite)} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$ with $\Delta H = -393.5$ kJ and $\text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2$ with $\Delta H = -283.0$ kJ, find $\Delta H$ for $\text{C(graphite)} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}$.
step: Keep the first equation as written, since it contains carbon on the left: $\Delta H = -393.5$ kJ.
step: Reverse the second equation so that CO is a product: $\text{CO}_2 \to \text{CO} + \tfrac12\text{O}_2$, $\Delta H = +283.0$ kJ.
step: Add the two. The $\text{CO}_2$ cancels, and $\text{O}_2$ leaves $\tfrac12\text{O}_2$ on the left, matching the target.
step: $\Delta H = -393.5 + 283.0 = -110.5$ kJ.

@example
title: Calorimetry of a dissolving salt
problem: A $50.0$ g sample of water at $22.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ absorbs the heat of a process and rises to $25.5\,^\circ\text{C}$. How much heat did the water absorb?
step: Temperature change: $\Delta T = 25.5 - 22.0 = 3.5\,^\circ\text{C}$.
step: Substitute: $q = (50.0\ \text{g})(4.184\ \text{J g}^{-1}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1})(3.5\,^\circ\text{C})$.
step: $q = 732$ J absorbed by the water, so the process released $732$ J.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to reverse the sign of $\Delta H$ when reversing an equation, or to scale $\Delta H$ when scaling coefficients.
- Using $\Delta T = T_{\text{initial}} - T_{\text{final}}$ and getting the wrong sign for $q$.
- Omitting the stoichiometric coefficients when summing formation enthalpies.
- Confusing the sign convention: the reaction's $q$ is the negative of the solution's $q$.

@practice num
prompt: Use $\Delta H^\circ_f$ values (in kJ/mol): $\text{CH}_4(g) = -74.8$, $\text{CO}_2(g) = -393.5$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) = -285.8$, and $\text{O}_2(g) = 0$. Calculate $\Delta H^\circ$ for $\text{CH}_4 + 2\,\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$ in kJ.
answer: -890.3
check: (-393.5 + 2*(-285.8)) - (-74.8 + 2*0)
tol: 0.1
unit: kJ
hint: Products minus reactants, each multiplied by its coefficient.
hint: Products: $-393.5 + 2(-285.8)$. Reactants: $-74.8 + 0$.
solution: $\Delta H^\circ = [-393.5 + 2(-285.8)] - [-74.8] = -965.1 + 74.8 = -890.3$ kJ.

@practice mc
prompt: A reaction mixture in a coffee-cup calorimeter warms from $21.0\,^\circ\text{C}$ to $27.5\,^\circ\text{C}$. What can you conclude about the reaction?
choice: It is endothermic, because the surroundings gained heat.
choice*: It is exothermic, because the system released heat to the solution.
choice: Its $\Delta H$ is positive, because the temperature increased.
choice: It does not exchange heat, because the calorimeter is insulated.
hint: The solution is the surroundings. Where did its extra energy come from?
solution: The solution gained energy, which must have come from the reaction. The reaction released heat, so $q_{\text{rxn}} < 0$ and $\Delta H < 0$.

@quiz num
prompt: In a coffee-cup calorimeter, $50.0$ mL of $1.00$ M HCl is mixed with $50.0$ mL of $1.00$ M NaOH and the temperature rises by $6.80\,^\circ\text{C}$. Assume a solution density of $1.00$ g/mL and $c = 4.184$ J g$^{-1}\,^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$. Calculate $\Delta H$ per mole of water formed, in kJ/mol.
answer: -56.9
check: -(100.0 * 4.184 * 6.80) / 1000 / 0.0500
tol: 1%
unit: kJ/mol
hint: Find the heat absorbed by $100.0$ g of solution.
hint: The reaction made $0.0500$ mol of water. Remember the reaction's heat is the negative of the solution's heat.
solution: $q_{\text{soln}} = (100.0)(4.184)(6.80) = 2845$ J. $q_{\text{rxn}} = -2845$ J, and $\Delta H = -2.845\ \text{kJ}/0.0500\ \text{mol} = -56.9$ kJ/mol.

@quiz num

tol: 0.5
unit: kJ
hint: Keep the first and third equations and reverse the second so that $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ is a product.
