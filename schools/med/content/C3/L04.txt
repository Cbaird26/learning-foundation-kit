@article
A gas has no fixed shape or volume: its particles are far apart and move rapidly and randomly. Four variables describe it: **pressure** $P$, **volume** $V$, **temperature** $T$ and amount $n$.

### Pressure units

Pressure is force per area. Common units are the atmosphere (atm), millimeters of mercury (mmHg, equal to the torr) and the pascal:
$$1\ \text{atm} = 760\ \text{mmHg} = 101.325\ \text{kPa}.$$

### The simple gas laws

At fixed $n$ and $T$, volume is inversely proportional to pressure (**Boyle's law**): $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$. At fixed $n$ and $P$, volume is proportional to **absolute** temperature (**Charles's law**): $V_1/T_1 = V_2/T_2$. At fixed $T$ and $P$, volume is proportional to amount (**Avogadro's law**): equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules. Temperatures must always be in kelvin, $T(\text{K}) = T(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$.

### The ideal gas law

Combining these laws gives
$$PV = nRT,$$
where $R = 0.082057\ \text{L atm mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ when pressure is in atm and volume in liters. Check that your units match $R$ before substituting. Real gases behave ideally at low pressure and high temperature, where particles rarely interact.

Rearranging gives useful forms. Since $n = m/M$, the molar mass is
$$M = \frac{dRT}{P},$$
where $d$ is the density in g/L. **Standard temperature and pressure** (STP, $273.15$ K and $1$ atm in this course) corresponds to a molar volume of about $22.4$ L/mol.

### Mixtures and Dalton's law

In a mixture of gases, each gas contributes a **partial pressure** equal to the pressure it would exert alone. **Dalton's law** says total pressure is the sum of partials, and each partial is the mole fraction times the total:
$$P_{\text{total}} = \sum_i P_i, \qquad P_i = x_i P_{\text{total}}.$$

### Gases in the lungs and blood

Dry air at sea level ($760$ mmHg) is about $20.9\%$ oxygen, so $P_{\text{O}_2} = 0.209 \times 760 \approx 159$ mmHg. Air inhaled into the airways is saturated with water vapor, which at body temperature ($37\,^\circ\text{C}$) exerts $47$ mmHg and takes up part of the total. The inspired oxygen partial pressure is therefore
$$P_{I\text{O}_2} = F_{I\text{O}_2}\,(P_{\text{atm}} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}).$$
In the alveoli, oxygen is removed by blood and $\text{CO}_2$ is added. The **alveolar gas equation** estimates the resulting alveolar oxygen pressure:
$$P_{A\text{O}_2} = F_{I\text{O}_2}\,(P_{\text{atm}} - P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}}) - \frac{P_{a\text{CO}_2}}{R_Q},$$
with $R_Q \approx 0.8$ the respiratory quotient. Gas partial pressures also decide how much dissolves in liquids, a point we return to with solutions. At altitude, $F_{I\text{O}_2}$ is unchanged but $P_{\text{atm}}$ falls, so every partial pressure falls with it, which is why high altitude causes hypoxia.

@example
title: Moles of gas from the ideal gas law
problem: How many moles of gas are in a $2.50$ L container at $1.00$ atm and $25\,^\circ\text{C}$?
step: Convert temperature: $T = 25 + 273.15 = 298.15$ K.
step: Solve $PV = nRT$ for $n$: $n = \dfrac{PV}{RT}$.
step: Substitute: $n = \dfrac{(1.00)(2.50)}{(0.082057)(298.15)} = 0.102$ mol.

@example
title: Alveolar oxygen at sea level
problem: A healthy person breathes room air ($F_{I\text{O}_2} = 0.21$) at $P_{\text{atm}} = 760$ mmHg. Alveolar $P_{\text{CO}_2}$ is $40$ mmHg, $P_{\text{H}_2\text{O}} = 47$ mmHg and $R_Q = 0.8$. Find the inspired and alveolar $P_{\text{O}_2}$.
step: Subtract water vapor from the barometric pressure: $760 - 47 = 713$ mmHg of dry gas.
step: Inspired oxygen: $P_{I\text{O}_2} = 0.21 \times 713 = 149.7 \approx 150$ mmHg.
step: Correct for carbon dioxide: $P_{\text{CO}_2}/R_Q = 40/0.8 = 50$ mmHg.
step: Alveolar oxygen: $P_{A\text{O}_2} = 149.7 - 50 \approx 100$ mmHg, close to the textbook value.

@mistakes
- Using Celsius temperatures in gas-law calculations instead of kelvin.
- Mixing units of pressure and $R$ (for example mmHg with $R = 0.082057$ L atm/mol K).
- Forgetting to subtract water vapor pressure before multiplying by the oxygen fraction.
- Treating $0.209$ as a percentage when substituting (use the fraction).

@practice num
prompt: A gas occupies $2.0$ L at $1.0$ atm. The temperature stays constant while it is compressed to $0.50$ L. What is the new pressure in atm?
answer: 4.0
check: 1.0 * 2.0 / 0.50
tol: 0.01
unit: atm
hint: Constant $n$ and $T$ means Boyle's law applies.
hint: $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.
solution: $P_2 = P_1V_1/V_2 = (1.0)(2.0)/0.50 = 4.0$ atm.
