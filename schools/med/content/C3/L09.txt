@article
The three-dimensional shape of a molecule decides how it fits a receptor, whether it dissolves in water, and how strongly it attracts its neighbors. **Valence shell electron pair repulsion (VSEPR) theory** predicts shape with one simple idea: electron groups around a central atom repel each other, so they spread as far apart as possible.

### Counting electron groups

An **electron group** is a region of electron density around the central atom: a single bond, a double bond, a triple bond, a lone pair or a lone electron each counts as **one** group. Draw the Lewis structure first, then count the groups. The number of groups sets the **electron-group geometry**, and the **molecular geometry** is the arrangement of atoms only, ignoring lone pairs.

### The common geometries

| Groups | Electron geometry | Lone pairs | Molecular geometry | Ideal angle(s) | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | 0 | linear | $180^\circ$ | $\text{CO}_2$ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 0 | trigonal planar | $120^\circ$ | $\text{BF}_3$ |
| 3 | trigonal planar | 1 | bent | $<120^\circ$ | $\text{SO}_2$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 0 | tetrahedral | $109.5^\circ$ | $\text{CH}_4$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | $<109.5^\circ$ | $\text{NH}_3$ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | 2 | bent | $<109.5^\circ$ | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | $90^\circ, 120^\circ$ | $\text{PCl}_5$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 1 | seesaw | $<90^\circ, <120^\circ$ | $\text{SF}_4$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 2 | T-shaped | $<90^\circ$ | $\text{ClF}_3$ |
| 5 | trigonal bipyramidal | 3 | linear | $180^\circ$ | $\text{XeF}_2$ |
| 6 | octahedral | 0 | octahedral | $90^\circ$ | $\text{SF}_6$ |
| 6 | octahedral | 1 | square pyramidal | $<90^\circ$ | $\text{BrF}_5$ |
| 6 | octahedral | 2 | square planar | $90^\circ$ | $\text{XeF}_4$ |

Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, so they squeeze bond angles: the $\text{H–O–H}$ angle in water is about $104.5^\circ$ and the $\text{H–N–H}$ angle in ammonia is about $107^\circ$. In trigonal bipyramidal arrangements, lone pairs prefer the **equatorial** positions, which have more room.

### Bond dipoles and molecular polarity

A **polar bond** has a **bond dipole** pointing toward the more electronegative atom. A molecule is **polar** if its bond dipoles do not cancel, so that it has a net **dipole moment** $\mu$. To decide:

1. Find the molecular geometry.
2. Draw the bond dipoles as vectors.
3. If the vectors cancel by symmetry, the molecule is nonpolar. If not, it is polar.

$\text{CO}_2$ has two polar C=O bonds pointing in opposite directions, so it is nonpolar overall. $\text{CCl}_4$ is symmetric tetrahedral and nonpolar. Water is bent, so its dipoles add to a net dipole and water is polar, as is ammonia. Lone pairs also contribute to the dipole. A molecule with polar bonds but a symmetric shape ($\text{BF}_3$, $\text{SF}_6$, $\text{XeF}_4$) is nonpolar, while the same bonds in an asymmetric shape, such as $\text{SF}_4$, give a polar molecule.

Polarity governs solubility ("like dissolves like") and is the basis for the intermolecular forces in lesson 11.

@example
title: Shape and polarity of $\text{XeF}_4$
problem: Determine the electron-group geometry, molecular geometry and polarity of $\text{XeF}_4$.
step: Valence electrons: $8 + 4(7) = 36$. Four F atoms bond to Xe using $8$ electrons, and each F takes three lone pairs ($24$ electrons), leaving $4$ electrons, or two lone pairs, on Xe.
step: Xe has $4 + 2 = 6$ electron groups, so the electron-group geometry is octahedral.
step: Two lone pairs go on opposite sides of the octahedron to minimize repulsion, so the molecular geometry is square planar with $90^\circ$ F–Xe–F angles.
step: Each Xe–F bond is polar, but the four dipoles are arranged symmetrically and cancel. $\text{XeF}_4$ is nonpolar.

@example
title: Comparing $\text{NH}_3$ and $\text{BF}_3$
problem: Both molecules have three bonded atoms. Compare their shapes and polarities.
step: Boron in $\text{BF}_3$ has three electron groups and no lone pair: trigonal planar, $120^\circ$.
step: Nitrogen in $\text{NH}_3$ has three bonds and one lone pair, so four groups: trigonal pyramidal.
step: The $\text{BF}_3$ dipoles cancel by symmetry, so it is nonpolar. In $\text{NH}_3$ the dipoles and the lone pair do not cancel, so it is polar.

@mistakes
- Counting a double or triple bond as more than one electron group.
- Naming the electron geometry when asked for the molecular geometry (for water, "tetrahedral" instead of bent).
- Assuming that any molecule with polar bonds is polar, without checking whether the dipoles cancel.
- Placing lone pairs in axial positions of a trigonal bipyramid, where repulsion is greater.
