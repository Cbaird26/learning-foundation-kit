@article
Chemical behavior is controlled by the arrangement of electrons around the nucleus. Understanding that arrangement began with the study of light.

### Light as a wave and as particles

Light is an electromagnetic wave with wavelength $\lambda$ and frequency $\nu$ related by $c = \lambda\nu$, with $c = 2.998\times10^8$ m/s. But light also comes in packets, **photons**, each with energy
$$E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda},$$
where $h = 6.626\times10^{-34}$ J s is **Planck's constant**. Shorter wavelength means higher energy per photon, which is why ultraviolet light damages skin while visible light does not.

### Bohr model and line spectra

Heated hydrogen emits only certain wavelengths: a **line spectrum**. The Bohr model explains this by allowing the electron only discrete energies:
$$E_n = -\frac{2.178\times10^{-18}\ \text{J}}{n^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$$
When an electron falls from level $n_i$ to $n_f$, it emits a photon with energy $\Delta E = 2.178\times10^{-18}\left(\frac{1}{n_f^2} - \frac{1}{n_i^2}\right)$ J. The Bohr model works quantitatively only for hydrogen-like species, but it established that energy is **quantized**.

### Orbitals and quantum numbers

In quantum mechanics, an electron is described by a wavefunction, and an **orbital** is a region where the electron is likely to be found. Each electron has four quantum numbers:

| Symbol | Name | Allowed values | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | principal | $1, 2, 3, \dots$ | shell, energy and size |
| $\ell$ | angular momentum | $0$ to $n-1$ | subshell shape ($s,p,d,f$ for $0,1,2,3$) |
| $m_\ell$ | magnetic | $-\ell$ to $+\ell$ | orbital orientation |
| $m_s$ | spin | $\pm\tfrac12$ | electron spin |

A subshell holds $2\ell + 1$ orbitals, and each orbital holds at most two electrons.

### Filling the orbitals

Three rules determine the **ground-state configuration**:

- **Aufbau principle:** fill from lowest energy upward, in the order $1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, \dots$
- **Pauli exclusion principle:** no two electrons in an atom share all four quantum numbers, so an orbital holds two electrons of opposite spin.
- **Hund's rule:** electrons fill degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing.

Carbon is $1s^2 2s^2 2p^2$ with two unpaired electrons in separate $2p$ orbitals. The shorthand uses the preceding noble gas: iron is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2 3d^6$.

### Exceptions and ions

Half-filled and completely filled $d$ subshells have extra stability, so chromium is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1 3d^5$ and copper is $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^1 3d^{10}$, not the predicted $4s^2$ configurations. When forming cations from transition metals, electrons leave the **outermost shell** first: remove the $4s$ electrons before the $3d$. So $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^6$. Anions add electrons to the next available orbital, giving $\text{Cl}^- = [\text{Ne}]\,3s^2 3p^6 = [\text{Ar}]$.

### Magnetism

Species with unpaired electrons are **paramagnetic** (attracted to a magnetic field). Those with all electrons paired are **diamagnetic**.

@example
title: Photon energy from wavelength
problem: Calculate the energy of one photon of green light with $\lambda = 500.$ nm.
step: Convert the wavelength to meters: $500.\ \text{nm} = 5.00\times10^{-7}$ m.
step: Use $E = hc/\lambda$: $E = \dfrac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.998\times10^{8})}{5.00\times10^{-7}}$.
step: $E = 3.97\times10^{-19}$ J per photon.

@example
title: Configuration of iron and its ions
problem: Write the ground-state electron configurations of Fe ($Z = 26$) and $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, and give the number of unpaired electrons in each.
step: Fill in order: $1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 4s^2 3d^6$, or $[\text{Ar}]\,4s^2 3d^6$ ($18 + 2 + 6 = 26$ electrons).
step: The $3d$ subshell has five orbitals holding six electrons: one pair and four singles, so Fe has $4$ unpaired electrons.
step: For $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, remove the two $4s$ electrons first, then one $3d$ electron: $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5$.
step: Five electrons in five orbitals are all unpaired by Hund's rule, giving $5$ unpaired electrons.

@mistakes
- Removing $3d$ electrons before $4s$ electrons when forming transition-metal cations.
- Using nanometers directly in $E = hc/\lambda$ without converting to meters.
- Pairing electrons in a subshell before each orbital has one, violating Hund's rule.
- Applying the predicted rather than the actual configuration for Cr and Cu.
