@article
Lewis structures and VSEPR describe where electrons are and what shapes result, but not why bonds form. Two quantum-mechanical models fill the gap: **valence bond theory** and **molecular orbital theory**.

### Valence bond theory: sigma and pi bonds

In valence bond theory, a covalent bond forms when atomic orbitals **overlap**, sharing a pair of electrons. Head-on overlap along the bond axis gives a **sigma ($\sigma$) bond**. Side-by-side overlap of $p$ orbitals gives a **pi ($\pi$) bond**, with electron density above and below the bond axis. A single bond is one $\sigma$ bond; a double bond is one $\sigma$ plus one $\pi$; a triple bond is one $\sigma$ plus two $\pi$. Pi bonds prevent rotation about the bond axis, which is why double bonds create cis and trans isomers.

### Hybridization

Carbon has configuration $2s^2 2p^2$, yet it forms four equivalent bonds in methane. Valence bond theory resolves this by mixing atomic orbitals into **hybrid orbitals** with the geometry that VSEPR predicts. The number of hybrid orbitals equals the number of atomic orbitals mixed, and equals the number of electron groups (bonds plus lone pairs, counting a multiple bond once).

| Electron groups | Hybridization | Geometry | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | $sp$ | linear | $\text{CO}_2$, ethyne |
| 3 | $sp^2$ | trigonal planar | $\text{BF}_3$, ethene |
| 4 | $sp^3$ | tetrahedral | $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| 5 | $sp^3d$ | trigonal bipyramidal | $\text{PCl}_5$ |
| 6 | $sp^3d^2$ | octahedral | $\text{SF}_6$ |

The unhybridized $p$ orbitals left over form the $\pi$ bonds. In ethene, each carbon is $sp^2$ with three $\sigma$ bonds and one unhybridized $p$ orbital that forms the $\pi$ bond. In ethyne, each carbon is $sp$, with two $\sigma$ bonds and two $\pi$ bonds. Note that lone pairs count too: nitrogen in $\text{NH}_3$ is $sp^3$.

### Molecular orbital theory

Molecular orbital (MO) theory treats electrons as delocalized over the whole molecule. Atomic orbitals combine to form the same number of molecular orbitals: a lower-energy **bonding** MO (constructive overlap) and a higher-energy **antibonding** MO, marked with an asterisk (destructive overlap). Electrons fill MOs from the lowest energy upward following the Pauli and Hund rules.

$$\text{bond order} = \frac{(\text{electrons in bonding MOs}) - (\text{electrons in antibonding MOs})}{2}.$$

A higher bond order means a stronger, shorter bond. A bond order of zero means no stable bond, which is why $\text{He}_2$ does not exist.

### Second-period diatomics

The valence MOs in order of increasing energy are:

- For $\text{B}_2$, $\text{C}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$: $\sigma_{2s}, \sigma^*_{2s}, \pi_{2p}$ (two orbitals), $\sigma_{2p}, \pi^*_{2p}$ (two orbitals), $\sigma^*_{2p}$.
- For $\text{O}_2$, $\text{F}_2$ and $\text{Ne}_2$: the $\sigma_{2p}$ drops below the $\pi_{2p}$ orbitals.

Nitrogen ($10$ valence electrons) has bonding electrons $2 + 4 + 2 = 8$ and antibonding electrons $2$, so its bond order is $3$, in line with the triple bond in its Lewis structure. Oxygen ($12$ valence electrons) places its last two electrons in the two degenerate $\pi^*_{2p}$ orbitals, one in each by Hund's rule. Its bond order is $(8-4)/2 = 2$, and the two unpaired electrons make $\text{O}_2$ **paramagnetic**, a fact Lewis structures cannot explain and a triumph of MO theory. Species with all electrons paired are diamagnetic.

When an electron is removed from an antibonding orbital, the bond order rises: $\text{O}_2^+$ has a bond order of $2.5$ and a shorter bond than $\text{O}_2$.

@example
title: Hybridization and bonds in ethyne
problem: Describe the hybridization of carbon in $\text{HC}\!\equiv\!\text{CH}$ and count the $\sigma$ and $\pi$ bonds.
step: Each carbon has one bond to hydrogen and one bond (a triple bond counts once) to the other carbon: $2$ electron groups.
step: Two groups means $sp$ hybridization, with a linear geometry and $180^\circ$ H–C–C angles.
step: The molecule has two C–H $\sigma$ bonds and one C–C $\sigma$ bond: $3$ $\sigma$ bonds.
step: The triple bond contributes two $\pi$ bonds, formed by overlap of the two unhybridized $p$ orbitals on each carbon: $2$ $\pi$ bonds.

@example
title: Bond order and magnetism of oxygen
problem: Use MO theory to find the bond order of $\text{O}_2$ and decide whether it is paramagnetic.
step: Count valence electrons: $2 \times 6 = 12$.
step: Fill in order $\sigma_{2s}^2\,\sigma_{2s}^{*2}\,\sigma_{2p}^2\,\pi_{2p}^4\,\pi_{2p}^{*2}$, placing the last two electrons in separate $\pi^*$ orbitals.
step: Bonding electrons: $2 + 2 + 4 = 8$. Antibonding electrons: $2 + 2 = 4$. Bond order $= (8 - 4)/2 = 2$.
step: Two unpaired electrons in the $\pi^*$ orbitals make $\text{O}_2$ paramagnetic, as observed.

@mistakes
- Counting lone pairs as nothing when determining hybridization (they count as electron groups).
- Saying a double bond contains two $\sigma$ bonds (it has one $\sigma$ and one $\pi$).
- Subtracting the wrong way when computing bond order, or forgetting to divide by two.
- Using the same MO energy order for $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$.

@quiz num
prompt: The $\text{C}_2$ molecule has $8$ valence electrons. In its ground state, $6$ occupy bonding MOs (the $\sigma_{2s}$ and both $\pi_{2p}$ orbitals) and $2$ occupy antibonding MOs. What is its bond order?

check: (6 - 2) / 2
