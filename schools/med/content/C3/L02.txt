@article
A balanced chemical equation is a recipe written in moles. In
$$\text{N}_2 + 3\,\text{H}_2 \longrightarrow 2\,\text{NH}_3$$
the coefficients say that $1$ mol of $\text{N}_2$ reacts with $3$ mol of $\text{H}_2$ to give $2$ mol of $\text{NH}_3$. Atoms are conserved, so each element must have the same count on both sides. Balance an equation by changing coefficients only, never subscripts, because changing a subscript changes the substance.

### The stoichiometric pathway

Balances measure grams, but equations speak in moles, so every stoichiometry problem follows the same route:

$$\text{grams A} \xrightarrow{\ \div M_A\ } \text{mol A} \xrightarrow{\ \text{coefficient ratio}\ } \text{mol B} \xrightarrow{\ \times M_B\ } \text{grams B}.$$

The **mole ratio** is the only step that uses the balanced equation. For example, from the ammonia equation, the factor converting moles of $\text{H}_2$ to moles of $\text{NH}_3$ is $\frac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{3\ \text{mol H}_2}$.

### Limiting reagent

Reactants are rarely mixed in exactly the ratio the equation demands. The **limiting reagent** is the reactant that runs out first and therefore caps how much product can form. The others are **in excess**. To identify it:

1. Convert the mass of every reactant to moles.
2. Divide each by its coefficient. The smallest quotient belongs to the limiting reagent.
3. Base all product calculations on the limiting reagent only.

Notice that the limiting reagent is not simply the one with the smaller mass or smaller mole count. It is the one that supports the least reaction once the coefficients are taken into account. This is like making sandwiches: with $10$ slices of bread and $8$ slices of cheese, a sandwich needing $2$ breads and $1$ cheese is limited by bread, because $10/2 = 5 < 8/1$.

### Excess reagent left over

The amount of excess reagent consumed is found from the limiting reagent by the mole ratio. Subtract that from the starting amount to find what remains.

### Theoretical and percent yield

The **theoretical yield** is the maximum product mass predicted by stoichiometry from the limiting reagent. The **actual yield** is what you really collect, usually less because of incomplete reactions, side reactions and losses during purification. The **percent yield** is

$$\%\text{ yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100\%.$$

A percent yield above $100\%$ in practice signals an error, often a product still wet with solvent or contaminated with another substance.

### Multistep problems

Real syntheses chain reactions together. If the product of reaction 1 is the reactant for reaction 2, convert through moles at each stage, and when yields are given for each step, multiply the fractional yields. A $90\%$ step followed by an $80\%$ step gives an overall yield of $0.90 \times 0.80 = 72\%$.

@example
title: Ammonia synthesis with a limiting reagent
problem: A mixture of $28.0$ g of $\text{N}_2$ and $8.00$ g of $\text{H}_2$ reacts according to $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \to 2\text{NH}_3$. Which reagent is limiting, and what mass of $\text{NH}_3$ can form? Use $M(\text{N}_2) = 28.014$, $M(\text{H}_2) = 2.016$, $M(\text{NH}_3) = 17.031$ g/mol.
step: Convert to moles: $n(\text{N}_2) = 28.0/28.014 = 0.9995$ mol and $n(\text{H}_2) = 8.00/2.016 = 3.968$ mol.
step: Divide by coefficients: $\text{N}_2$ gives $0.9995/1 = 0.9995$ and $\text{H}_2$ gives $3.968/3 = 1.323$. The smaller value is $\text{N}_2$, so $\text{N}_2$ is limiting.
step: Mole ratio: $0.9995\ \text{mol N}_2 \times \frac{2\ \text{mol NH}_3}{1\ \text{mol N}_2} = 1.999$ mol $\text{NH}_3$.
step: Convert to mass: $1.999 \times 17.031 = 34.0$ g of $\text{NH}_3$.
step: Hydrogen consumed is $3 \times 0.9995 = 2.999$ mol, so $3.968 - 2.999 = 0.969$ mol of $\text{H}_2$ ($1.95$ g) is left over.

@mistakes
- Comparing grams of reactants instead of moles divided by coefficients when picking the limiting reagent.
- Using the coefficient of the wrong species in the mole ratio, or forgetting the ratio altogether.
- Calculating product from the excess reagent, which gives an impossible, too-large yield.
- Computing percent yield upside down (theoretical over actual).

prompt: Methane burns by $\text{CH}_4 + 2\,\text{O}_2 \to \text{CO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. How many moles of $\text{O}_2$ are needed to burn $3.0$ mol of $\text{CH}_4$ completely?
answer: 6.0
check: 3.0 * 2
tol: 0.01
unit: mol
hint: Read the coefficients as a mole ratio between $\text{O}_2$ and $\text{CH}_4$.
hint: Every $1$ mol of $\text{CH}_4$ requires $2$ mol of $\text{O}_2$.
solution: $3.0\ \text{mol CH}_4 \times \frac{2\ \text{mol O}_2}{1\ \text{mol CH}_4} = 6.0$ mol $\text{O}_2$.

@practice num
prompt: Glucose is oxidized by $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\,\text{O}_2 \to 6\,\text{CO}_2 + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. What mass of $\text{CO}_2$ is produced from $18.0$ g of glucose with unlimited oxygen? Use $M(\text{glucose}) = 180.156$ and $M(\text{CO}_2) = 44.009$ g/mol.
answer: 26.4
check: 18.0 / 180.156 * 6 * 44.009
tol: 1%
unit: g
hint: Convert grams of glucose to moles first.
hint: The mole ratio of $\text{CO}_2$ to glucose is $6:1$. Then convert moles of $\text{CO}_2$ to grams.
solution: $18.0/180.156 = 0.09991$ mol glucose. Times $6$ gives $0.5995$ mol $\text{CO}_2$. Times $44.009$ g/mol gives $26.4$ g.

@practice num
prompt: A reaction mixture contains $4.00$ mol of $\text{H}_2$ and $3.00$ mol of $\text{O}_2$ for $2\,\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. How many moles of water can form at most?
answer: 4.00
check: min(4.00/2, 3.00/1) * 2
tol: 0.01
unit: mol
hint: Divide each reactant's moles by its coefficient. The smaller number identifies the limiting reagent.
hint: $4.00/2 = 2.00$ and $3.00/1 = 3.00$, so hydrogen limits the reaction.
solution: $\text{H}_2$ is limiting. $4.00\ \text{mol H}_2 \times \frac{2\ \text{mol H}_2\text{O}}{2\ \text{mol H}_2} = 4.00$ mol of water.

@practice mc
prompt: In a reaction between two reactants, how is the limiting reagent correctly identified?
choice: It is the reactant with the smaller mass in grams.
choice: It is the reactant with the smaller molar mass.
choice*: It is the reactant that would produce the smaller amount of product, found from moles divided by coefficient.
choice: It is the reactant with the larger coefficient in the balanced equation.
hint: Think about which reactant runs out first, taking the equation's ratio into account.
solution: Only the mole-to-coefficient comparison tells you which reactant supports less reaction. Mass alone ignores both molar mass and stoichiometric ratio.

@practice num
prompt: Iron is made by $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 + 3\,\text{CO} \to 2\,\text{Fe} + 3\,\text{CO}_2$. From $160.$ g of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ with excess CO, a furnace yields $98.0$ g of iron. What is the percent yield? Use $M(\text{Fe}) = 55.845$ and $M(\text{O}) = 15.999$ g/mol. Give your answer to one decimal place.
answer: 87.6
check: 98.0 / (160. / (2*55.845 + 3*15.999) * 2 * 55.845) * 100
tol: 0.5
unit: %
hint: Find the theoretical yield of iron from $160.$ g of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ first.
hint: $M(\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3) = 159.69$ g/mol, and the mole ratio of Fe to $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ is $2:1$.
solution: $160./159.69 = 1.002$ mol $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$, giving $2.004$ mol Fe $= 111.9$ g theoretical. Percent yield $= 98.0/111.9 \times 100 = 87.6\%$.

@quiz num
prompt: Ammonia is oxidized by $4\,\text{NH}_3 + 5\,\text{O}_2 \to 4\,\text{NO} + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$. A vessel holds $6.80$ g of $\text{NH}_3$ and $12.0$ g of $\text{O}_2$. What mass of NO can form? Use $M(\text{NH}_3) = 17.031$, $M(\text{O}_2) = 31.998$, $M(\text{NO}) = 30.006$ g/mol.
answer: 9.00
check: min(6.80/17.031/4, 12.0/31.998/5) * 4 * 30.006
tol: 1%
unit: g
hint: Convert both masses to moles and divide each by its coefficient.
hint: Oxygen gives the smaller quotient ($0.0750$), so it limits. Use the $4:5$ ratio of NO to $\text{O}_2$.
solution: $n(\text{NH}_3) = 0.3993$ mol ($/4 = 0.0998$), $n(\text{O}_2) = 0.3750$ mol ($/5 = 0.0750$). $\text{O}_2$ is limiting. $0.3750 \times \frac{4}{5} = 0.3000$ mol NO, which is $9.00$ g.

@quiz num
prompt: For $2\,\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \to 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$, a mixture of $10.0$ g of $\text{H}_2$ and $40.0$ g of $\text{O}_2$ reacts completely until the limiting reagent is used up. What mass of the excess reagent remains? Use $M(\text{H}_2) = 2.016$ and $M(\text{O}_2) = 31.998$ g/mol.
answer: 4.96
check: 10.0 - 2 * (40.0/31.998) * 2.016
tol: 1%
unit: g
hint: Find moles of each reactant and decide which one limits.
hint: $\text{O}_2$ is limiting ($1.250$ mol). It consumes $2 \times 1.250$ mol of $\text{H}_2$.
solution: $n(\text{H}_2) = 4.960$ mol and $n(\text{O}_2) = 1.250$ mol; $\text{O}_2$ limits. Hydrogen used: $2.500$ mol. Left: $4.960 - 2.500 = 2.460$ mol, or $4.96$ g of $\text{H}_2$.
