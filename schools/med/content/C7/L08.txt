@article
Blood moves because the heart creates a pressure difference. Describing the flow requires three ideas: continuity, Bernoulli's equation and Poiseuille's law.

### Flow rate and continuity

The **volume flow rate** is $Q = Av$ (m$^3$/s), where $A$ is the cross-sectional area and $v$ is the average speed. Cardiac output of about $5\ \text{L/min}$ is a flow rate. For an incompressible fluid with no leaks, the same volume per second passes every point of a single tube:

$$A_1 v_1 = A_2 v_2.$$

Where the tube narrows, the fluid speeds up. In the circulation, the total cross-sectional area of all capillaries is far larger than that of the aorta, so blood in capillaries moves slowly, which gives time for exchange. Note that a single capillary is tiny; it is the sum over the whole bed that matters.

### Bernoulli's equation

For steady, incompressible, nonviscous flow along a streamline,

$$P + \tfrac{1}{2}\rho v^2 + \rho g h = \text{constant}.$$

Where speed is higher, pressure is lower (at the same height). A tight narrowing (a stenosis) therefore shows a drop in pressure at the narrow throat. This ideal equation neglects viscosity, so it predicts changes in pressure from speed and height but not the steady pressure loss along a long vessel. For blood use $\rho = 1060\ \text{kg/m}^3$ and $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

### Viscosity and Poiseuille's law

A real fluid has **viscosity** $\eta$ (Pa$\cdot$s); blood is about $3.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$. For steady **laminar** flow in a long cylindrical tube of radius $r$ and length $L$ with pressure difference $\Delta P$,

$$Q = \frac{\pi r^4\,\Delta P}{8\eta L}.$$

Resistance to flow is $R = \Delta P/Q = 8\eta L/(\pi r^4)$. The key feature is the fourth power: halving the radius cuts the flow by a factor of $2^4 = 16$ at the same pressure difference, and a $19\%$ reduction in radius reduces flow by about $57\%$ ($0.81^4 \approx 0.43$). This is why a modest narrowing from atherosclerosis, or the constriction and dilation of arterioles, strongly changes flow. Arterioles regulate blood pressure and organ perfusion mainly by changing $r$.

### Turbulence

Flow stays laminar at low speed. The **Reynolds number**

$$Re = \frac{\rho v D}{\eta}$$

(with $D$ the tube diameter) predicts the transition: values below roughly $2000$ are laminar, above roughly $3000$ turbulent, with an unstable range between. Turbulence makes a sound; this is the basis of a bruit heard over a stenosed artery and of the Korotkoff sounds heard while measuring blood pressure.

@example
title: Flow and speed in the aorta
problem: The aorta has radius $1.2\ \text{cm}$ and blood moves through it at an average $0.30\ \text{m/s}$. Find the volume flow rate in L/min.
step: Cross-sectional area: $A = \pi r^2 = \pi(0.012)^2 = 4.52 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$.
step: Flow rate: $Q = Av = (4.52 \times 10^{-4})(0.30) = 1.36 \times 10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$.
step: Convert: $1\ \text{m}^3 = 1000\ \text{L}$ and $1\ \text{min} = 60$ s, so $Q = 1.36 \times 10^{-4} \times 1000 \times 60 = 8.1\ \text{L/min}$. This exceeds resting cardiac output because $0.30$ m/s is closer to the peak speed than to the average over a heartbeat.

@mistakes
- Using the diameter in place of the radius in $A = \pi r^2$ or in Poiseuille's law (it is $r^4$).
- Forgetting to convert cm and L/min to SI units before using the formulas.
- Applying Bernoulli's equation to explain the steady pressure drop along a long vessel; that comes from viscosity.
- Thinking narrowing a tube increases the flow rate; it increases the speed, but $Q$ is fixed by continuity.

@practice num
prompt: Blood moves at $30\ \text{cm/s}$ through an artery of cross-sectional area $2.0\ \text{cm}^2$. The artery narrows to $0.50\ \text{cm}^2$ at a stenosis. What is the blood speed at the stenosis, in cm/s?
answer: 120
check: 30*2.0/0.50
tol: 1
unit: cm/s
hint: Flow rate $Av$ is the same at both locations.
solution: $v_2 = v_1A_1/A_2 = (30)(2.0)/0.50 = 120\ \text{cm/s}$.

prompt: Blood of viscosity $3.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ flows through a vessel of radius $1.0\ \text{mm}$ and length $0.10\ \text{m}$ under a pressure difference of $400\ \text{Pa}$. @quiz num
prompt: At the same pressure difference and vessel length, an artery narrows to $70\%$ of its original radius. What fraction of the original laminar flow rate remains? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.240
check: 0.70**4
tol: 0.002
hint: Flow is proportional to a power of the radius.
solution: $Q_2/Q_1 = (0.70)^4 = 0.240$, so flow falls by about $76\%$.
