@article
Many things in the body repeat: the heartbeat, the swing of a leg during walking, the vibration of the eardrum. The simplest model is **simple harmonic motion (SHM)**.

### Hooke's law and SHM

A spring exerts a restoring force proportional to its displacement from equilibrium, **Hooke's law**: $F = -kx$, with spring constant $k$ in N/m. The minus sign says the force points back toward equilibrium. A mass attached to such a spring oscillates in SHM. SHM arises whenever the restoring force is proportional to displacement.

### Describing oscillations

- **Amplitude** $A$: the maximum displacement from equilibrium.
- **Period** $T$: time for one full cycle, in seconds.
- **Frequency** $f = 1/T$, in hertz (Hz, cycles per second).
- **Angular frequency** $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi/T$, in rad/s.

The displacement can be written $x(t) = A\cos(\omega t)$ if the motion starts at maximum displacement. The speed is greatest at equilibrium, where $v_{\max} = A\omega$, and zero at the turning points $x = \pm A$. The acceleration is greatest at the turning points, $a_{\max} = A\omega^2$.

### Period of a spring and a pendulum

For a mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}.$$

For a simple pendulum of length $L$ swinging through small angles (less than about $15^\circ$), with $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$:

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}}.$$

The period of a spring depends on mass and $k$, but **not on amplitude**. The period of a pendulum depends on length and $g$ but not on mass or amplitude. A stiffer spring or a lighter mass gives a shorter period. A leg swinging while walking behaves approximately like a pendulum, which is why the natural walking pace depends on leg length.

### Energy

The total mechanical energy of the oscillator stays constant when there is no friction:

$$E = \tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2.$$

At the turning points all of it is potential (spring) energy; at equilibrium all of it is kinetic. Solving for speed at displacement $x$ gives

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}\,(A^2 - x^2)}.$$

### Damping and resonance

Real oscillators lose energy to friction and air resistance; the amplitude decays and the motion is **damped**. Shock absorbers and the viscoelastic tissues around joints are examples of designed or natural damping. If a periodic driving force acts at a frequency near the oscillator's natural frequency, the amplitude becomes large: **resonance**. Resonance explains why a child's swing is pushed in time with its motion, why a glass can shatter at the right pitch, and how the inner ear (basilar membrane) separates sound frequencies. In magnetic resonance imaging, hydrogen nuclei respond resonantly to a radio-frequency pulse.

@example
title: A mass on a spring
problem: A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ mass is attached to a spring with $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$. Find the period and frequency, and the maximum speed if the amplitude is $0.040\ \text{m}$.
step: Period: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k} = 2\pi\sqrt{0.50/200} = 2\pi(0.05) = 0.314\ \text{s}$.
step: Frequency: $f = 1/T = 3.18\ \text{Hz}$, and $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$.
step: Maximum speed occurs at equilibrium: $v_{\max} = A\omega = (0.040)(20) = 0.80\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Check by energy: $\tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}(200)(0.040)^2 = 0.16$ J, and $\tfrac{1}{2}(0.50)(0.80)^2 = 0.16$ J.

@mistakes
- Using amplitude to compute the period; for SHM the period does not depend on it.
- Mixing up frequency $f$ (Hz) and angular frequency $\omega$ (rad/s), forgetting the $2\pi$.
- Thinking a heavier pendulum bob has a shorter period; the mass cancels.
- Using the pendulum formula for large angles or a spring formula when the force is not proportional to displacement.

@practice num
prompt: A heart beats with a period of $0.80\ \text{s}$. What is its frequency in hertz?
answer: 1.25
check: 1/0.80
tol: 0.01
unit: Hz
hint: Frequency is the reciprocal of period.
solution: $f = 1/T = 1/0.80 = 1.25\ \text{Hz}$ (75 beats per minute).

prompt: A $0.80\ \text{kg}$ mass hangs from a spring with $k = 50\ \text{N/m}$ and oscillates vertically. What is its period in seconds? Give three decimal places.
answer: 0.795
check: 2*math.pi*(0.80/50)**0.5
tol: 0.002
unit: s
hint: Use $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$.
solution: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.016} = 2\pi(0.1265) = 0.795\ \text{s}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.50\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 200\ \text{N/m}$ oscillates with amplitude $0.050\ \text{m}$. What is its maximum speed in m/s?
answer: 1.0
check: 0.050*(200/0.50)**0.5
tol: 0.01
unit: m/s
hint: Use $v_{\max} = A\omega$ with $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, or energy conservation.
solution: $\omega = \sqrt{400} = 20\ \text{rad/s}$, so $v_{\max} = (0.050)(20) = 1.0\ \text{m/s}$.

@quiz num
prompt: What length of simple pendulum has a period of $2.0\ \text{s}$? Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$ and give metres to three decimal places.
answer: 0.993
check: 9.8*2.0**2/(4*math.pi**2)
tol: 0.005
unit: m
hint: Solve $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ for $L$.
solution: $L = gT^2/(4\pi^2) = (9.8)(4.0)/39.48 = 0.993\ \text{m}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A $0.30\ \text{kg}$ mass on a spring with $k = 120\ \text{N/m}$ oscillates with amplitude $0.10\ \text{m}$. What is its speed when it is $0.050\ \text{m}$ from equilibrium, in m/s? Give three decimal places.
answer: 1.732
check: ((120/0.30)*(0.10**2 - 0.050**2))**0.5
tol: 0.005
unit: m/s
hint: Use conservation of energy: $\tfrac{1}{2}kA^2 = \tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}kx^2$.
solution: $v = \sqrt{(120/0.30)(0.0100 - 0.0025)} = \sqrt{400 \times 0.0075} = \sqrt{3.0} = 1.73\ \text{m/s}$.
