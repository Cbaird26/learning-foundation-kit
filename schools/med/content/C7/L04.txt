@article
**Linear momentum** is the product of mass and velocity, $\vec p = m\vec v$, in kg$\cdot$m/s. It is a vector, so direction (sign) matters. Newton's second law can be written in its original form,

$$\sum \vec F = \frac{\Delta \vec p}{\Delta t}.$$

### Impulse

Multiplying by $\Delta t$ gives the **impulse-momentum theorem**:

$$\vec J = \vec F_{\text{avg}}\,\Delta t = \Delta \vec p = m\vec v_f - m\vec v_i.$$

For a fixed change in momentum, a longer stopping time means a smaller average force. This is why airbags, bent knees on landing and padded helmets reduce injury: they extend $\Delta t$ and so reduce $F_{\text{avg}}$.

### Conservation of momentum

If the net external force on a system is zero (or negligible during a short collision), the total momentum of the system stays constant:

$$m_1\vec v_{1i} + m_2\vec v_{2i} = m_1\vec v_{1f} + m_2\vec v_{2f}.$$

The internal forces between the bodies are equal and opposite (Newton's third law), so they cannot change the total. Choose a positive direction and give each velocity a sign.

### Types of collisions

- **Perfectly inelastic:** the bodies stick together, $(m_1 + m_2)v_f = m_1v_{1i} + m_2v_{2i}$. Kinetic energy is lost the most.
- **Elastic:** both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. In one dimension the relative speed of approach equals the relative speed of separation.
- **Inelastic (general):** momentum is conserved, kinetic energy decreases, and the bodies may or may not stick.

Momentum is conserved in all of these when external forces are negligible; kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions. This is the most common source of errors.

### Combining the laws

Many problems use both principles in sequence. For a **ballistic pendulum**, a bullet embeds in a block (momentum conservation, with kinetic energy lost), and then the block swings up (energy conservation, with the kinetic energy of the pair becoming potential energy $(m_1 + m_2)gh$). Treat each stage separately and use the speed right after the collision as the link. A person landing from a fall is similar: energy gives the speed at impact, and impulse gives the force while stopping. Use $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$.

### Recoil

When a system starts at rest, the total momentum is zero, so parts must move in opposite directions with equal momentum magnitudes. A rifle recoils because the bullet gains forward momentum; a lighter object gains the greater speed.

@example
title: A sticking collision
problem: A $2.0\ \text{kg}$ clay ball moving horizontally at $3.0\ \text{m/s}$ hits and sticks to a stationary $4.0\ \text{kg}$ cart on a frictionless track. Find the final speed and the kinetic energy lost.
step: Horizontal momentum is conserved because no net horizontal external force acts on the ball-cart system.
step: Before: $p = (2.0)(3.0) + (4.0)(0) = 6.0\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$.
step: After: $(2.0 + 4.0)v_f = 6.0$, so $v_f = 1.0\ \text{m/s}$.
step: Kinetic energy before is $\tfrac{1}{2}(2.0)(3.0)^2 = 9.0$ J and after is $\tfrac{1}{2}(6.0)(1.0)^2 = 3.0$ J. Six joules became thermal energy and deformation, so the collision is inelastic but momentum is conserved.

@mistakes
- Treating kinetic energy as conserved in a collision where objects stick together.
- Dropping the sign of velocity, so that objects moving in opposite directions are added instead of subtracted.
- Using conservation of momentum across a stage with a large external force, such as the swing of a pendulum or a ground impact.
- Forgetting that a longer collision time lowers the average force for the same change in momentum.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.15\ \text{kg}$ ball moving at $20\ \text{m/s}$ is brought to rest by a hand in $0.050\ \text{s}$. What is the magnitude of the average force on the ball, in newtons?
answer: 60
check: 0.15*20/0.050
unit: N
hint: Impulse equals change in momentum.
solution: $F = \Delta p/\Delta t = (0.15)(20)/0.050 = 60\ \text{N}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $1000\ \text{kg}$ car moving at $15\ \text{m/s}$ collides with and sticks to a stationary $500\ \text{kg}$ car. What is the speed of the combined cars in m/s?
answer: 10
check: 1000*15/(1000 + 500)
unit: m/s
hint: The cars stick together, so they share one final velocity.
solution: $(1000 + 500)v_f = (1000)(15)$, so $v_f = 10\ \text{m/s}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $60\ \text{kg}$ person stands at rest on frictionless ice and throws a $2.0\ \text{kg}$ ball horizontally at $12\ \text{m/s}$. What is the recoil speed of the person in m/s?
answer: 0.4
check: 2.0*12/60
unit: m/s
hint: Total momentum starts at zero.
solution: $0 = (2.0)(12) - (60)v$, so $v = 24/60 = 0.40\ \text{m/s}$.

@practice num
prompt: A $0.010\ \text{kg}$ bullet moving at $400\ \text{m/s}$ embeds in a $0.990\ \text{kg}$ block hanging at rest from strings. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, how high does the block-bullet system rise, in metres? Give your answer to two decimal places.
answer: 0.82
check: ((0.010*400/(0.010 + 0.990))**2)/(2*9.8)
tol: 0.01
unit: m
hint: First use momentum conservation for the embedding. Then use energy conservation for the swing.
hint: The speed right after impact is $4.0$ m/s; set $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 = gh$.
solution: $v = (0.010)(400)/1.000 = 4.0\ \text{m/s}$. Then $h = v^2/(2g) = 16/19.6 = 0.82\ \text{m}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A $5.0\ \text{kg}$ cart moving right at $4.0\ \text{m/s}$ collides with and sticks to a $3.0\ \text{kg}$ cart moving left at $2.0\ \text{m/s}$ on a frictionless track. What is the velocity of the combined carts in m/s, taking right as positive?
answer: 1.75
check: (5.0*4.0 - 3.0*2.0)/(5.0 + 3.0)
tol: 0.01
unit: m/s
hint: Give the leftward velocity a negative sign.
solution: $(8.0)v = (5.0)(4.0) + (3.0)(-2.0) = 14$, so $v = 1.75\ \text{m/s}$ to the right.

@quiz num
prompt: A $70\ \text{kg}$ person jumps from rest from a height of $0.50\ \text{m}$ and lands, coming to rest in $0.20\ \text{s}$ with stiff legs. Using $g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^2$, what is the average upward force on the person during the stop, in newtons? Include the effect of gravity and give your answer to the nearest newton.
answer: 1782
check: 70*(2*9.8*0.50)**0.5/0.20 + 70*9.8
tol: 20
unit: N
hint: Find the speed at impact from energy, then the impulse needed to stop.
hint: The net force is the average ground force minus the weight.
solution: Impact speed $v = \sqrt{2(9.8)(0.50)} = 3.13$ m/s. Net force $= (70)(3.13)/0.20 = 1096$ N upward, so the ground force is $1096 + 686 = 1782$ N, about $1782$ N.
