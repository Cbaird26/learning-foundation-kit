@article
Every human life begins when two haploid gametes fuse to restore a diploid zygote. Gametogenesis is the process that makes those gametes, and it relies on **meiosis**: one round of DNA replication followed by two divisions, which halves the chromosome number. Two notation conventions help you keep track. **n** counts chromosome sets (a diploid cell is 2n = 46 chromosomes) and **C** counts DNA content (a diploid cell in G1 has 2C; after S phase it has 4C).

### Spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis occurs in the **seminiferous tubules**, beginning at puberty and continuing throughout adult life. A stem-cell **spermatogonium** (2n, 2C) divides by mitosis to renew itself and to produce a **primary spermatocyte**. After S phase the primary spermatocyte is 2n, 4C. **Meiosis I** produces two **secondary spermatocytes** (n, 2C), each still holding duplicated chromosomes. **Meiosis II** separates sister chromatids to give four **spermatids** (n, 1C). In **spermiogenesis** each round spermatid sheds cytoplasm and becomes a spermatozoon with a head (condensed nucleus capped by the **acrosome**, a Golgi-derived vesicle of hydrolytic enzymes), a midpiece packed with mitochondria, and a flagellum built on a 9+2 microtubule axoneme with dynein arms.

Two supporting cells matter clinically. **Sertoli cells** line the tubule, form the **blood-testis barrier** with tight junctions, nourish germ cells, and secrete inhibin and androgen-binding protein under FSH. **Leydig cells** sit in the interstitium and secrete testosterone under LH. The whole process takes roughly two to three months, so a drug or fever can affect semen quality long after exposure. Sperm gain motility in the epididymis but are not yet able to fertilize until **capacitation** in the female tract.

### Oogenesis

Oogenesis is lopsided in time and in cytoplasm. During fetal life, oogonia divide by mitosis, and the resulting **primary oocytes** enter meiosis I but arrest in **prophase I** (the dictyotene stage), surrounded by a single layer of flat granulosa cells to form primordial follicles. Roughly one to two million exist at birth; a few hundred thousand remain at puberty, and only a few hundred ovulate.

Just before ovulation, the LH surge triggers completion of meiosis I. Unequal cytokinesis yields a large **secondary oocyte** and a tiny **first polar body**. The secondary oocyte immediately begins meiosis II and arrests in **metaphase II**. It is this cell that is ovulated. Meiosis II finishes only if a sperm penetrates, producing a mature **ovum** and a **second polar body**. Because a primary oocyte may wait decades in prophase I, advanced maternal age raises the risk of **nondisjunction**, the classic cause of trisomy 21.

### Fertilization

Fertilization usually occurs in the **ampulla** of the uterine tube within about a day of ovulation; sperm may remain viable for several days. The steps are:

1. **Capacitation** of sperm in the female tract.
2. Passage through the **corona radiata**, helped by hyaluronidase.
3. Binding to the **zona pellucida** glycoprotein ZP3, which triggers the **acrosome reaction** (release of acrosin and other enzymes) so the sperm digests a path through the zona.
4. **Fusion** of the sperm and oocyte membranes.
5. The **cortical reaction**: a calcium wave causes cortical granules to release enzymes that modify the zona so that other sperm cannot bind. This is the block to polyspermy.
6. Completion of meiosis II; the male and female **pronuclei** replicate their DNA and combine at **syngamy** to make a diploid zygote.

### The first week

The zygote undergoes **cleavage**, mitotic divisions without growth, producing smaller blastomeres. At about day 3 to 4 it is a solid ball of 16 or so cells, the **morula**. Fluid enters, forming a **blastocyst** by about day 5 with an **inner cell mass** (embryoblast, which becomes the embryo) and an outer **trophoblast** (which forms the placenta). The blastocyst hatches from the zona pellucida before implantation.

### When it goes wrong

If an empty egg is fertilized and the paternal genome duplicates, the result is a **complete hydatidiform mole** (46,XX, entirely paternal): swollen villi like a bunch of grapes, no embryo, and very high hCG. **Dispermy** (two sperm fertilizing one egg) produces a triploid (69 chromosome) **partial mole**, which may contain fetal tissue. Primary ciliary dyskinesia, with defective dynein arms, causes immotile sperm and cilia, linking male infertility with ectopic pregnancy risk and recurrent sinopulmonary infection.

@example
title: Following DNA content through meiosis
problem: A primary spermatocyte has 46 chromosomes and 4C DNA content. State the chromosome number and DNA content of the secondary spermatocyte and of the spermatid that it produces, and the number of spermatids from one primary spermatocyte.
step: Meiosis I separates homologous chromosomes, not sister chromatids. Each daughter receives 23 chromosomes, but each chromosome still has two chromatids. The secondary spermatocyte is therefore n, 2C.
step: Meiosis II separates sister chromatids, as in mitosis. Each daughter again has 23 chromosomes, now with one chromatid each, so the DNA content is halved again: the spermatid is n, 1C.
step: Count the products: one primary spermatocyte makes 2 secondary spermatocytes, each making 2 spermatids, so $2 \times 2 = 4$ spermatids.
step: By contrast, one primary oocyte yields only one ovum, plus polar bodies, because cytokinesis in both divisions is unequal.

@mistakes
- Saying the secondary oocyte is arrested in prophase I; prophase I arrest belongs to the primary oocyte, and the ovulated cell is arrested in metaphase II.
- Believing meiosis I separates sister chromatids; it separates homologs, and that is why the cell becomes haploid at that step.
- Placing the acrosome reaction after membrane fusion; it occurs on zona binding, before fusion, and the cortical reaction is what follows fusion.
