@project
title: Development-to-defect atlas
overview: You will build a six-organ atlas that links one step of embryologic development to one congenital anomaly and to the histological appearance of the normal and affected tissue. The atlas is a study tool: each page lets you start from a defect and explain it by development and histology, or start from a slide and predict the defect.
goal: Connect an embryologic mechanism (the step that failed or was disrupted) to the resulting structural anomaly for six organs.
goal: Describe in words the normal histology of each organ and the histologic change that accompanies the anomaly or its consequence.
goal: Practice explaining the same chain aloud, from germ layer to cell type to clinical presentation.
step: Choose six organs from this supplied list, or substitute your own with the same structure. Heart (septation, ventricular septal defect); nervous system (neural tube closure, myelomeningocele); gut (neural crest migration to the colon, Hirschsprung disease); kidney and lung (ureteric bud and metanephric mesenchyme, renal agenesis with Potter sequence and pulmonary hypoplasia); limb and bone (endochondral ossification, achondroplasia); skin (melanocytes from neural crest, or epidermal adhesion and the blistering diseases); thymus and parathyroids (third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, DiGeorge syndrome).
step: For each organ write the germ layer or layers it comes from, the week or stage when the key developmental step occurs, and the normal sequence of events in two or three sentences.
step: Name one congenital anomaly for each organ, state which developmental step failed and the mechanism (failure to close, to migrate, to induce, to septate, to recanalize), and give one teratogen, gene or infection that can cause it when one is known.
step: Describe the normal histology of the organ in words, as if explaining it to a colleague who cannot see the slide: epithelium or cell types, layers, staining (basophilic or eosinophilic structures), and landmarks.
step: Describe the histologic or microscopic change in the defect or its consequence: for example absent ganglion cells in the Meissner and Auerbach plexuses in Hirschsprung disease, foot process effacement in minimal change disease, immature type II pneumocytes and surfactant deficiency in prematurity, or an absent thymus with no Hassall corpuscles.
step: Add a clinical page for each organ: one realistic vignette (an invented patient), the key finding, and one confirmatory test.
step: Finish with a one-page cross-index linking the six organs by shared mechanisms (for example, neural crest, induction, folding, apoptosis or recanalization) and a self-quiz of six questions.
deliverable: A six-page atlas, one page per organ, each with development, defect, normal histology, pathologic histology and a vignette.
deliverable: A one-page cross-index grouping the six defects by mechanism, with the self-quiz and an answer key.
deliverable: A two-minute spoken explanation, recorded or given to a friend, of one chain from germ layer to defect to histology, without notes.
rubric: Each organ has a correct germ layer origin and a correctly timed developmental step.
rubric: Each defect is tied to a specific mechanism and not just named, and any teratogen or gene is correctly matched.
rubric: Normal histology is described precisely enough to identify the tissue: epithelium type, layers, key cells and staining.
rubric: Histologic change is accurate and clearly linked to the clinical picture.
rubric: Vignettes are original, medically plausible, and contain enough findings to reach the diagnosis.
safety: Vignettes must be invented, and the atlas is for study; it is not medical advice for any real person or pregnancy.

@review
### Week by week

**Week 1: gametogenesis and fertilization.** Meiosis and the notation n and C. Primary oocytes are arrested in prophase I until the LH surge; the secondary oocyte is arrested in metaphase II until fertilization. Capacitation, zona binding and acrosome reaction, fusion, cortical reaction, syngamy. Complete mole is 46,XX and paternal; partial mole is triploid.

**Week 2: implantation and gastrulation.** Syncytiotrophoblast makes hCG; epiblast and hypoblast; the primitive streak creates endoderm, mesoderm and ectoderm. Ectopic pregnancy: ampulla, risk factors, hCG and ultrasound, methotrexate versus surgery.

**Week 3: folding and organogenesis.** Neurulation, neuropores (about days 25 and 27 to 28), neural crest, somites, lateral folding, omphalocele versus gastroschisis, pharyngeal pouches 3 and 4.

**Week 4: anomalies and teratogens.** Malformation, disruption, deformation, sequence. Weeks 3 to 8 are critical. Match teratogens to effects, and TORCH infections to findings.

**Week 5: the cell and epithelium.** H&E, junctions, classification (layers then shape) and locations; metaplasia.

**Week 6: connective tissue.** Cells, collagen types I to IV, elastic fibers, collagen synthesis steps and diseases.

**Week 7: cartilage and bone.** Three cartilages, osteoblast, osteocyte and osteoclast, osteon, growth plate zones, ossification.

**Week 8: muscle.** Skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle, the sarcomere, excitation-contraction coupling, dystrophin.

**Week 9: nervous tissue.** Neuron, glia, myelin, nerve layers, MS versus Guillain-Barre syndrome.

**Week 10: blood and lymphoid tissue.** Identify white cells, ANC, thymus, lymph node, spleen.

**Week 11: skin, gut and liver.** Epidermal layers, the four gut layers, villi and crypts, the lobule and zones.

**Week 12: lung, kidney, glands.** Airway lining, type I and II pneumocytes, nephron segments, thyroid, adrenal, pancreas.

### Self-check

- I can draw meiosis for the oocyte and the sperm with chromosome and DNA counts.
- I can list the derivatives of each germ layer and place neural crest.
- I can match five teratogens and four congenital infections to their effects.
- I can name an epithelium from a description and give a location.
- I can describe each nephron segment and each white blood cell in words.

### Common errors

- Mixing up meiotic arrest points, or saying meiosis I separates sister chromatids.
- Assigning kidneys to paraxial instead of intermediate mesoderm.
- Calling omphalocele "without a sac" and gastroschisis "with a sac".
- Reversing blood flow and bile flow in the liver lobule.
- Giving the DCT a brush border, or assigning surfactant to type I pneumocytes.

prompt: A woman with regular cycles has a last menstrual period 56 days ago. Fertilization occurred 14 days after the last menstrual period, and the posterior neuropore of the embryo closes on embryonic day 28 (counted from fertilization). How many days ago did the neuropore close?
answer: 14
check: (56 - 14) - 28
hint: Subtract the 14 days from the last menstrual period to fertilization to get the embryonic age in days.
hint: Compare the embryonic age with the day of neuropore closure.
solution: Embryonic age is $56 - 14 = 42$ days. Closure occurred at day 28, so it happened $42 - 28 = 14$ days ago.
unit: days
tol: 0.01

@exam mc
prompt: A newborn dies of respiratory failure shortly after birth. The pregnancy was complicated by oligohydramnios, and ultrasound at 20 weeks showed no kidneys. At autopsy the lungs are small, with few alveoli and few airway generations. Which sequence best explains the findings?
choice: Failure of neural crest migration, causing absent ganglion cells and lung hypoplasia
choice*: Failure of the ureteric bud to induce the metanephric mesenchyme, causing no urine and oligohydramnios, which restricts fetal lung growth
choice: A teratogen in the first two weeks causing an all-or-none effect on only the lungs
choice: Excess surfactant from type II pneumocytes causing alveolar overdistension
choice: Failure of lateral folding causing a ventral wall defect
hint: Fetal urine is the main source of amniotic fluid in the second half of pregnancy.
solution: Renal agenesis results from failure of the ureteric bud to induce the metanephric mesenchyme. Absent fetal urine causes oligohydramnios, which compresses the fetus and prevents normal lung growth (pulmonary hypoplasia): Potter sequence.

@exam self
prompt: Choose one organ and explain, from germ layer to clinical presentation, a chain that links a developmental step, a congenital anomaly and the histological change that you would see. For example, use neural crest migration, Hirschsprung disease and the biopsy findings.
hint: Write the chain as four links: germ layer or cell origin, failed step, anomaly, microscopic finding.
hint: Finish with one sentence on how the patient presents and how you would confirm the diagnosis.
solution: Neural crest cells arise from the edges of the neural folds in the ectoderm in week 3 to 4 and migrate to the gut, where they become the ganglion cells of the Meissner and Auerbach plexuses, colonizing the bowel from the esophagus to the rectum by about week 7. If migration stops short, the distal colon has no ganglion cells (Hirschsprung disease); the aganglionic segment cannot relax and remains contracted while the proximal bowel dilates. The newborn fails to pass meconium within 48 hours with abdominal distension. Rectal biopsy shows absent ganglion cells in the submucosal and myenteric plexuses with hypertrophied nerve fibers, confirming the diagnosis.
rubric: Names the germ layer or cell origin and the timing of the key developmental step.
rubric: States the specific mechanism of failure and the resulting anomaly.
rubric: Describes the histologic finding accurately and ties it to the clinical presentation.
