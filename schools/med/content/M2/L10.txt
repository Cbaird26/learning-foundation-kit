@article
Blood is a connective tissue whose matrix, **plasma**, is fluid. On a Wright- or Giemsa-stained peripheral smear you can identify the formed elements by size, nuclear shape and granules.

### Formed elements

- **Erythrocytes (red blood cells)**: anucleate **biconcave discs**, about 7 to 8 µm, pink with a central pallor of about a third of the diameter. Lifespan is about 120 days.
- **Platelets**: small (2 to 4 µm), anucleate, purple-granular fragments of **megakaryocyte** cytoplasm. Normal count is about 150,000 to 400,000 per µL.
- **Neutrophil** (about 40 to 70 percent of white cells): 10 to 12 µm, nucleus with **3 to 5 lobes** joined by thin strands, pale pink cytoplasm with fine granules. The most abundant WBC; first responder to bacterial infection. A **band** neutrophil has an unsegmented, U-shaped nucleus. A rise in bands is a "left shift."
- **Eosinophil** (1 to 4 percent): **bilobed nucleus** (a pair of spectacles) with large **bright orange-red** granules. It fights parasites and participates in allergic reactions; major basic protein is in the granules.
- **Basophil** (under 1 percent): the rarest. A bilobed nucleus is **obscured by large, dark blue-purple** granules containing histamine and heparin.
- **Lymphocyte** (20 to 40 percent): a small cell (7 to 10 µm) with a **round, dark nucleus** filling most of the cell and a thin rim of pale blue cytoplasm. T cells, B cells and NK cells look alike by light microscopy. Large granular lymphocytes (NK cells) have more cytoplasm with granules.
- **Monocyte** (2 to 8 percent): the largest WBC (12 to 20 µm), with a **kidney-bean or horseshoe nucleus**, fine chromatin and abundant gray-blue cytoplasm that may be vacuolated. In tissues it becomes a **macrophage**.

Normal total WBC is about 4,500 to 11,000 per µL. The **absolute neutrophil count (ANC)** equals the total WBC multiplied by the fraction of neutrophils (segmented neutrophils plus bands). An ANC under 1,500 per µL is neutropenia, and under 500 per µL carries a high risk of serious infection. **Hypersegmented neutrophils** (six or more lobes) suggest megaloblastic anemia (vitamin B12 or folate deficiency).

### Hematopoiesis

Blood formation moves between sites during development: the **yolk sac**, then the **liver** (and spleen), and finally the **bone marrow** from late gestation. In adults it occurs in the red marrow of the axial skeleton and proximal long bones. A **hematopoietic stem cell** gives a **common myeloid progenitor** (red cells, platelets via megakaryocytes, granulocytes and monocytes) and a **common lymphoid progenitor** (lymphocytes). **Erythropoietin** from peritubular interstitial cells of the kidney stimulates red cell production, and other growth factors direct white cell lines (G-CSF, GM-CSF) and platelets (thrombopoietin).

### Lymphoid organs

**Primary** lymphoid organs (bone marrow, thymus) produce lymphocytes; **secondary** organs (lymph nodes, spleen, tonsils, mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue such as Peyer patches) activate them. These are framed by a **reticular** network of type III collagen.

**Thymus**: a lobulated organ in the anterior mediastinum, derived from the third pharyngeal pouch. The **cortex** is dark, densely packed with immature T cells undergoing positive selection; the **medulla** is paler, with mature T cells and **Hassall corpuscles**, whorls of keratinizing epithelial reticular cells. The thymus involutes after puberty, with fat replacing tissue. Absent thymus leads to T cell deficiency (DiGeorge syndrome).

**Lymph node**: lymph enters through afferent lymphatics, passes the subcapsular sinus, and exits via the efferent lymphatic at the hilum. The outer **cortex** has **follicles** of B cells; secondary follicles have a pale **germinal center** where B cells proliferate after antigen contact. The **paracortex** holds T cells and **high endothelial venules** through which lymphocytes enter from blood. The **medulla** has cords with plasma cells and macrophages. Lymph node enlargement is common in infection; hard, fixed nodes raise concern for malignancy.

**Spleen**: surrounded by a capsule, with no cortex and medulla. **White pulp** is lymphoid tissue around a central arteriole: the **periarteriolar lymphoid sheath (PALS)** holds T cells and the follicles hold B cells. **Red pulp** consists of **splenic cords (of Billroth)** and venous sinusoids, where macrophages remove old or abnormal red cells and organisms. Asplenia results in **Howell-Jolly bodies** (nuclear remnants within red cells) and a high risk of sepsis with encapsulated bacteria (*Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Neisseria meningitidis*), so patients need vaccination.

@example
title: Calculating an absolute neutrophil count
problem: A patient receiving chemotherapy has a total WBC of 2,400 per µL, with 20 percent segmented neutrophils and 5 percent bands. Calculate the ANC and classify it.
step: Neutrophils include segmented cells and bands: $20\% + 5\% = 25\%$.
step: ANC $= 2400 \times 0.25 = 600$ per µL.
step: This is below 1,500 (neutropenia) but above 500, so it is moderate neutropenia, and fever would require urgent evaluation.

@mistakes
- Identifying a cell with a bilobed nucleus only as an eosinophil; a basophil is also bilobed but has dark granules that hide the nucleus.
- Forgetting to include band forms when calculating the ANC.
- Placing T cells in lymph node follicles; follicles are B-cell zones and the paracortex is the T-cell zone.
