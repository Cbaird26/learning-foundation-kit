@article
About 3 percent of newborns have a major structural anomaly. Causes are genetic (chromosomal and single-gene), environmental (teratogens), or multifactorial, and in many cases the cause is never found.

### Vocabulary

- **Malformation**: an intrinsically abnormal developmental process (cleft lip, neural tube defect, congenital heart defect).
- **Disruption**: breakdown of originally normal tissue by an outside force, for example **amniotic band** amputation of digits.
- **Deformation**: abnormal mechanical force on normal tissue, usually late in gestation; for example clubfoot from **oligohydramnios**.
- **Sequence**: one initiating anomaly causes a cascade; **Potter sequence** begins with bilateral renal agenesis, leading to oligohydramnios, pulmonary hypoplasia, flattened facies and limb deformities.
- **Syndrome**: multiple anomalies with one cause (such as trisomy 21).
- **Association**: non-random co-occurrence without a known single cause, such as **VACTERL** (vertebral, anal atresia, cardiac, tracheoesophageal fistula, renal, limb).

### Principles of teratology

1. **Timing matters most.** During the first 2 weeks after fertilization the effect is **all-or-none**: the embryo either dies or compensates completely. The **critical period** for major structural anomalies is weeks 3 to 8 (organogenesis), when each organ is most vulnerable at its own moment: neural tube about week 4, heart weeks 3 to 6, limbs weeks 4 to 8. In the **fetal period** (week 9 onward), teratogens more often reduce growth or cause functional impairment, and the brain stays vulnerable throughout.
2. **Dose and duration** matter.
3. **Genotype** of mother and fetus influences susceptibility.
4. Each teratogen has **specific** manifestations.

### Drugs and toxins to know

| Agent | Characteristic effect |
|---|---|
| Thalidomide | Limb reduction (phocomelia, amelia) |
| Isotretinoin and other retinoids | Craniofacial, ear, cardiac and CNS defects; absolute contraindication in pregnancy |
| Warfarin | Nasal hypoplasia and stippled epiphyses; fetal bleeding |
| ACE inhibitors and ARBs (2nd and 3rd trimester) | Fetal renal dysgenesis and anuria, oligohydramnios, pulmonary hypoplasia, skull hypoplasia |
| Valproate and carbamazepine | Neural tube defects |
| Phenytoin | Fetal hydantoin syndrome: cleft lip and palate, hypoplastic nails and distal phalanges, growth deficiency |
| Lithium | Cardiac defects including Ebstein anomaly (absolute risk is low) |
| Alcohol | Fetal alcohol spectrum: short palpebral fissures, smooth philtrum, thin upper lip, microcephaly, intellectual disability |
| Tetracyclines | Tooth discoloration and enamel hypoplasia, bone growth effects |
| Aminoglycosides | Eighth nerve toxicity (hearing loss) |
| Diethylstilbestrol (in utero) | Clear cell adenocarcinoma of the vagina and uterine structural abnormalities |
| Cocaine | Placental abruption, growth restriction |
| Maternal diabetes | Caudal regression, transposition of the great arteries, neural tube defects, macrosomia |
| Tobacco | Low birth weight, preterm birth |

### Congenital infections (TORCH and others)

- **Toxoplasma gondii** (cat feces, undercooked meat): **chorioretinitis, hydrocephalus and diffuse intracranial calcifications**.
- **Rubella**: **cataracts, sensorineural deafness, patent ductus arteriosus** (also pulmonary artery stenosis), and "blueberry muffin" skin lesions. Preventable by vaccination before pregnancy; live vaccines are not given in pregnancy.
- **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**: the **most common congenital infection**; **periventricular calcifications**, microcephaly and sensorineural hearing loss.
- **Herpes simplex**: mostly acquired at delivery, causing skin, eye and mouth vesicles, or encephalitis.
- **Syphilis**: stillbirth, hepatosplenomegaly, saddle nose and Hutchinson teeth, treated with penicillin.
- **Varicella**: limb hypoplasia, skin scarring and cortical atrophy.
- **Zika virus**: microcephaly with brain calcifications.

Diagnosis combines history of exposure, timing, physical findings and imaging; counseling should be nondirective, factual and respect the patient's decisions. Where an exposure is avoidable, prevention is key: preconception folic acid, medication review, vaccination, glycemic control and alcohol avoidance.

@example
title: Classifying an anomaly by mechanism
problem: Classify each as malformation, disruption, deformation or sequence: (a) cleft lip in an otherwise normal infant; (b) fused fingers and a constriction ring caused by strands of torn amnion; (c) clubfoot in an infant whose mother had prolonged leakage of amniotic fluid; (d) pulmonary hypoplasia and limb contractures that follow bilateral renal agenesis.
step: (a) is an intrinsic developmental error in fusion of the facial processes: a malformation.
step: (b) normal tissue was damaged by an external force, amniotic bands: a disruption.
step: (c) normal bones were compressed by abnormal mechanical forces in oligohydramnios: a deformation.
step: (d) the first event, absence of fetal urine, set off a cascade of oligohydramnios and its consequences: a sequence (Potter).

@mistakes
- Treating all drug exposures in the first two weeks as causing malformations; that window is all-or-none.
- Confusing the findings of CMV (periventricular calcifications) with Toxoplasma (diffuse calcifications and chorioretinitis).
- Prescribing an ACE inhibitor in pregnancy because "the first trimester is safest"; fetal renal toxicity is greatest in the second and third trimesters.
