@article
Muscle cells (fibers) are specialized to contract. All three types contain **actin** and **myosin**, but they differ in appearance, control and calcium handling.

### Skeletal muscle

Skeletal muscle consists of long, cylindrical, **multinucleated** fibers with **peripheral nuclei** pressed against the sarcolemma, formed by fusion of myoblasts. Under the microscope a longitudinal section shows regular **cross-striations**, and a cross-section shows polygonal fibers of similar size, each ringed by thin connective tissue. Connective tissue wraps the muscle at three levels: **endomysium** (around each fiber), **perimysium** (around fascicles) and **epimysium** (around the whole muscle). **Satellite cells** lie beneath the basal lamina and repair injured fibers. Contraction is voluntary and controlled by somatic motor neurons at the **neuromuscular junction**, where acetylcholine acts on nicotinic receptors. Antibodies against these receptors cause **myasthenia gravis**, with fatigable weakness that worsens with use.

### The sarcomere

A **sarcomere** runs from one **Z line** to the next. Thin filaments (actin, with troponin and tropomyosin) are anchored at the Z line; thick filaments (myosin) are anchored at the **M line** in the center.

- **I band**: thin filaments only; it **shortens** during contraction.
- **A band**: the full length of the thick filaments; it **stays constant**.
- **H zone**: thick filaments only, in the middle of the A band; it **shortens**.
- **Sarcomere length** shortens as the Z lines come closer.

This is the **sliding filament** mechanism: myosin heads bind actin and pull the thin filaments inward, using ATP, with no change in filament length. Excitation-contraction coupling in skeletal muscle: an action potential spreads along the sarcolemma and down **T-tubules**; the voltage sensor (dihydropyridine receptor) mechanically opens the **ryanodine receptor (RyR1)** of the sarcoplasmic reticulum; calcium binds **troponin C**, moves tropomyosin, and exposes the myosin-binding sites. At the junction of the A and I bands, a T-tubule is flanked by two terminal cisternae in a **triad**.

**Fiber types**: Type I fibers are slow-twitch, oxidative, rich in mitochondria and myoglobin (red), for endurance. Type II fibers are fast-twitch, and type IIx are more glycolytic (paler), for short, powerful efforts.

Dystrophin links the actin cytoskeleton to the sarcolemma. A frameshift deletion in the X-linked **dystrophin** gene causes **Duchenne muscular dystrophy** (boys with proximal weakness, calf pseudohypertrophy, Gowers sign, elevated creatine kinase, with fiber necrosis, regeneration and replacement by fat and fibrous tissue). **Becker** dystrophy is a milder in-frame defect.

### Cardiac muscle

Cardiac fibers are shorter, **branched**, with a **single central nucleus**, and are striated. They connect end-to-end through **intercalated discs**, which contain **fascia adherens** (anchoring actin), **desmosomes** (resisting stress) and **gap junctions** (allowing the electrical wave to spread, so the myocardium acts as a functional syncytium). Mitochondria are abundant. T-tubules are larger and sit at the Z line, forming **diads**. Contraction uses **calcium-induced calcium release** through RyR2, depends on extracellular calcium entering through L-type channels, and is involuntary and rhythmic. Cardiac muscle regenerates poorly; infarcted myocardium is replaced by scar. Cardiac fibers are often described as having one nucleus, though some contain two.

### Smooth muscle

Smooth muscle cells are **spindle-shaped**, each with a **single central nucleus** and no striations, because the actin and myosin are not arranged in sarcomeres. Thin filaments attach to **dense bodies** (equivalent to Z lines) and to the membrane. There are no T-tubules; **caveolae** and a sparse sarcoplasmic reticulum provide calcium. Calcium binds **calmodulin**, which activates **myosin light-chain kinase** and so phosphorylates myosin; troponin is absent. Smooth muscle is involuntary, is regulated by the autonomic nervous system, hormones and local factors, and forms the walls of vessels and hollow organs. Smooth muscle can divide and hypertrophy, as in the pregnant uterus.

### Comparison

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | Long cylinders | Short, branched | Spindle |
| Nuclei | Many, peripheral | One, central | One, central |
| Striations | Yes | Yes | No |
| Special junctions | Neuromuscular junction | Intercalated discs | Gap junctions |
| Calcium regulator | Troponin | Troponin | Calmodulin and MLCK |
| Control | Voluntary | Involuntary | Involuntary |

@example
title: Predicting band changes in contraction
problem: A skeletal muscle sarcomere contracts from 2.2 µm to a shorter length. What happens to the A band, I band and H zone, and why?
step: Thick filaments (myosin) keep their length, so the A band is unchanged.
step: Thin filaments slide over the thick filaments toward the center, so the I band (thin filaments only) shortens as the Z lines approach.
step: The ends of the thin filaments move toward the M line, shrinking the H zone, where only thick filaments are seen.

@mistakes
- Saying the A band shortens in contraction; filament lengths do not change.
- Giving smooth muscle troponin; it uses calmodulin and myosin light-chain kinase.
- Placing the nuclei of skeletal muscle in the center; they are peripheral (central nuclei suggest regeneration or a myopathy).
