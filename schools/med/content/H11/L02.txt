@article
British literature spans more than a thousand years, but in grades 11 and 12 you usually meet three stretches: the **Romantic** period (about 1798 to 1832), the **Victorian** era (1837 to 1901) and **modernism** in the early twentieth century. World literature adds voices from every continent, often read in translation. The goal is the same as last week: connect a text to the conditions that produced it.

### Romantic and Victorian Britain

Romantic writers reacted to the French Revolution and the first stages of industrialization. They prized feeling, imagination and nature. Keats ends an ode with the line "Beauty is truth, truth beauty", a claim about how perception and knowledge relate that makes more sense against Romantic trust in imagination.

The Victorian period was shaped by factories, crowded cities, a vast empire, expanding literacy and new debates about science and religion. Novels often appeared in monthly or weekly installments, which is why many have cliffhangers and large casts. Dickens opens *A Tale of Two Cities* (1859) with "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times", a balanced pair that sets up the novel's theme of opposites and extremes in revolutionary Paris and London. Austen, writing a little earlier, opens *Pride and Prejudice* (1813) with an ironic claim: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." The sentence sounds like a law of nature but actually exposes how marriage and money were tied together for the women in her world.

### Satire and irony

**Irony** says one thing and means another. **Satire** uses irony, exaggeration or ridicule to criticize people, institutions or ideas. Jonathan Swift's *A Modest Proposal* (1729) pretends to be a calm economic plan to relieve Irish poverty. The calm tone, set against an unthinkable proposal, is the weapon. Readers who miss the irony misread the essay completely. To interpret satire, ask: who is the speaker pretending to be, what target is being criticized, and what is the gap between what is said and what is meant?

### World literature and translation

World texts often arrive through a translator. Translation is interpretation: word choice, rhythm and cultural references change from one language to another. When you cite a translated work, note the translation you read and avoid making claims about individual word choices unless you know the original. Context matters even more because a reader may not share the culture the text assumes. A novel about colonial rule, a classical epic and a folktale each ask you to learn a little about their world before judging.

When comparing works from different traditions, find a shared question (what does a society owe the individual? what does it cost to speak out?) and compare how each text answers, citing details from both.

### Writing the context connection

Use the same formula from last week: detail, context, interpretation. Name the work, give the date or period, cite a specific condition, and say what it changes about the reading. Keep the paragraph short and avoid summarizing plot.

@example
title: A contextual claim about irony
problem: Write a context-based claim about the opening sentence of Pride and Prejudice: "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife."
step: Notice the device. The sentence uses the grand voice of a general law ("a truth universally acknowledged"), but the content is about marriage and money.
step: Add context. In the Regency England of 1813, many women of the gentry could not inherit property easily and depended on marriage for security.
step: Interpret. The mismatch between grand tone and small subject is ironic. Because marriage mattered so much to women's security, the "truth" is really about what families and daughters needed, not what a single man wants.
step: Write it. "By stating a social anxiety as a universal law, Austen's opening sentence shows that marriage in her world was an economic arrangement, and she invites the reader to watch for the gap between social talk and real motives."

@mistakes
- Treating irony as a literal statement of the author's belief.
- Making claims about a translated work's exact wording without knowing the original.
- Using a period label ("Victorian") with no specific event, institution or debate behind it.
- Summarizing plot in the place where a contextual claim belongs.

@practice self
prompt: Choose a text you have read (British or from another country). In four to five sentences, write a context connection: name the text, state a specific historical condition or event from its time, and explain what that condition helps a reader notice about a particular detail in the text. If you have no text in mind, use Swift's A Modest Proposal (1729) and the poverty and English rule that Irish readers faced.
hint: Start with the text's title and date, then name one concrete condition of that time (a law, a hardship, a publishing practice or a debate).
hint: Pick one detail from the text, such as the narrator's tone or a key image, and finish by saying what the condition lets a reader notice about it.
solution: In A Modest Proposal (1729), Swift writes as a calm projector offering a plan to deal with Irish poverty. At the time, Ireland was under English political and economic control, and many families lived in severe hardship while pamphlets offered detached schemes for improvement. Knowing this, a reader can see that the speaker's cold, statistical manner imitates the way officials discussed the poor. The monstrous proposal exposes how such language turns people into numbers, so the satire targets a style of thinking as well as a policy.
rubric: Names the text and gives a date or period.
rubric: States a specific, accurate condition or event, not a vague generality.
rubric: Connects the context to a particular detail of the text.
rubric: Explains what the context reveals rather than summarizing plot.
