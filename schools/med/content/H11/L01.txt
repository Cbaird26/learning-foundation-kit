@article
Every text is written by someone, at some time, for someone. **Context** is the set of historical, social and literary conditions around a text: the events that prompted it, the audience it addressed, the movement it joined or resisted. Reading in context does not replace close reading. It tells you which details matter and why a writer chose them.

### A map of the periods

American literature is usually taught in overlapping movements:

| Period (approximate) | Concerns | Example texts |
|---|---|---|
| Colonial and Puritan (1600s to 1700s) | Faith, community, providence | Sermons, journals, early poetry |
| Revolutionary and early national (1760s to 1800s) | Liberty, reason, self-government | Declaration of Independence (1776), pamphlets |
| Romanticism and Transcendentalism (1820s to 1860s) | Individual intuition, nature | Emerson, *Walden* (1854), Whitman, Dickinson |
| Slavery, abolition and the Civil War (1830s to 1860s) | Freedom, citizenship, union | Douglass's *Narrative* (1845), Lincoln's speeches |
| Realism and regionalism (1860s to 1900) | Ordinary life, dialect, social criticism | *Adventures of Huckleberry Finn* (1885) |
| Modernism and the Harlem Renaissance (1910s to 1930s) | Fragmentation, new forms, Black cultural flowering | *The Great Gatsby* (1925), Hughes's early poems |

The dates are fuzzy and the categories overlap. Treat them as handles for thinking, not boxes.

### Three kinds of context

1. **Historical.** What events surrounded the writing? The Gettysburg Address (November 1863) was delivered months after a battle that killed and wounded tens of thousands, at the dedication of a cemetery. That setting explains why a very short speech is about the meaning of the war.
2. **Audience and occasion.** Who was meant to read or hear it? A sermon, a pamphlet, a private poem and a novel in installments all shape their prose differently.
3. **Literary.** What conventions is the writer following or breaking? Realist novelists rejected the idealized plots of earlier romance and recorded speech as people actually used it.

### Using context as evidence

Weak context reads like a history lesson stapled to a book report: "The novel was written in 1885. In 1885 there were railroads." Strong context is **specific and tied to a detail of the text**: it names an event, date or debate, and shows how it changes what a line means.

A useful formula is *detail, context, interpretation*. Quote or point to a detail. State the relevant fact. Say what the fact reveals that you would not see without it. Lincoln's phrase "Four score and seven years ago" counts back to 1776 and so frames the Civil War as a test of the Declaration's promise. Without the date, the phrase is just old-fashioned counting.

### Cautions

Do not assume an author's biography settles a text's meaning. Do not treat a text as only a symptom of its time: good writing also argues with its time. And verify your facts, since a confident but wrong date undermines an otherwise sound reading.

@example
title: Turning a detail into contextual evidence
problem: A student wants to write about this sentence from Emerson's essay "Self-Reliance" (1841): "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines." Build one body-paragraph claim that uses context.
step: Identify the detail. The sentence mocks consistency and lists three kinds of authority: statesmen, philosophers and divines (clergy).
step: Find specific context. In the 1830s and 1840s, Transcendentalists such as Emerson argued that individuals should trust their own intuition rather than inherited institutions and doctrines.
step: Connect them. The three named groups are the institutions Transcendentalism asked readers to question, so the insult is aimed at deference to authority, not at honesty about one's views.
step: Write the claim. "Emerson's list of statesmen, philosophers and divines targets the public institutions that, in the Transcendentalist view, pressure individuals to defend old opinions rather than trust present insight."

@mistakes
- Writing a biography or a century-wide overview instead of one fact that changes the reading of a particular line.
- Treating the period label as the whole argument ("This is Romantic, so it is about nature") without pointing to a detail in the text.
- Stating a date or event from memory without checking it.
- Assuming the author's life explains the text completely.

prompt: A student claims that Emerson's call to trust one's own intuition fits the Transcendentalist movement of the 1830s and 1840s. Which statement best supports the claim as context?
choice: Emerson wrote many essays, and essays are a form of nonfiction.
choice*: Transcendentalists held that individuals could perceive truth directly and should question inherited institutions and doctrine.
choice: Many Americans in the 1840s lived on farms.
choice: Emerson's essay is longer than most speeches of his time.
hint: Good context links a specific idea of the time to a specific idea in the text.
hint: Look for the choice that names a belief of the movement rather than a general fact.
solution: The belief that individuals can know truth directly and should question institutions explains the essay's argument. The other choices are true or plausible but do not connect to the claim.

@practice num
prompt: The Gettysburg Address begins "Four score and seven years ago". A score is 20 years. In what year does the speech say the nation began, if the speech was delivered in 1863? Give the year.
answer: 1776
check: 1863 - (4 * 20 + 7)
hint: Convert the phrase to a number of years: four scores plus seven.
hint: Subtract that number from 1863.
solution: Four score and seven is $4 \times 20 + 7 = 87$ years. $1863 - 87 = 1776$, the year of the Declaration of Independence.

@practice self
prompt: Read this invented passage from a speech given in 1862 by a fictional town clerk to departing volunteers: "You carry more than a rifle. You carry the question of whether a country can ask this of its sons and still call itself free." Write three to four sentences that connect the passage to a specific piece of Civil War context (an event, date or debate) and say what the context helps a reader notice.
hint: Choose one dated fact about the Civil War in 1862, such as the reliance on volunteers and the move toward conscription, and name it in your first sentence.
hint: Quote a phrase such as "still call itself free" and say what the context lets a reader notice about it.
solution: The speech comes from 1862, when the Civil War was well underway and both armies depended on volunteers and, later, on conscription. The clerk's question about whether a country can demand sacrifice and "still call itself free" echoes the wartime debate over what duties free citizens owe a government. Knowing that debate lets a reader see the line as a worry about liberty, not just a send-off. It also suggests the speaker is addressing doubt among listeners, not only encouraging them.
rubric: Names at least one specific, accurate piece of context (an event, date or debate).
rubric: Quotes or points to a detail of the passage.
rubric: Explains what the context reveals rather than only stating a fact.
