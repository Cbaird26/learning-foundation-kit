@article
Research is not collecting quotations. It is a process of asking a question, finding evidence, judging its quality and building an answer that is yours. A planned process saves hours and protects you from weak sources.

### From topic to question

A **topic** ("teen sleep") is too broad. A **research question** is specific, open to inquiry and arguable or at least answerable with evidence: "How does moving high school start times after 8:30 a.m. affect attendance and academic performance?" Narrow by population, place, time and aspect. A good question cannot be answered with yes or no or a single fact, and it should be neither too narrow to find sources nor so broad that you would drown. Expect to revise it as you read.

### Searching smartly

Turn the question into **keywords** and synonyms: "school start time," "later school hours," "adolescent sleep," "academic performance." Combine them with **Boolean operators**:

- **AND** narrows (both terms must appear).
- **OR** broadens (either term; useful for synonyms).
- **NOT** excludes a term.
- **Quotation marks** keep a phrase together ("school start time").
- Parentheses group terms: ("start time" OR "school hours") AND sleep.

Use library databases and scholarly search engines, and apply filters for date, peer review and subject. For medical topics, searchable databases of biomedical literature are standard. If a search gives thousands of results, add terms. If it gives none, remove terms or try synonyms. Mine the reference lists of good sources to find more.

### Kinds of sources

- **Primary sources** give direct evidence: original data, an interview, a law, a letter, a study reporting its own experiment.
- **Secondary sources** analyze or interpret primary sources: a review article, a textbook, a biography.
- **Scholarly (peer-reviewed)** sources are reviewed by experts before publication; **popular** sources (news, magazines, blogs) are written for general readers and are usually less detailed.
- **Tertiary sources** (encyclopedias, indexes) are good for background and for finding other sources, but rarely cited as main evidence.

### Evaluating a source

Ask five questions (sometimes called the CRAAP test: currency, relevance, authority, accuracy, purpose):

1. **Currency**: When was it published or updated, and does your topic change quickly?
2. **Relevance**: Does it answer your question at the right depth?
3. **Authority**: Who wrote it and what qualifies them? Who published it?
4. **Accuracy**: Is it supported by evidence you can check? Is it reviewed?
5. **Purpose**: Is it informing, selling, persuading or entertaining? Who benefits?

For websites, try **lateral reading**: leave the page and see what other reliable sources say about the author or organization, rather than judging it only by how it looks.

### Taking notes

Keep three kinds of notes separate and labeled: **direct quotations** (in quotation marks, with page number), **paraphrases** (your own words, with source and page) and **your own thoughts** (marked as yours). Record full citation details when you find the source, so you do not hunt for them later. Mixing these categories is the main cause of accidental plagiarism.

@example
title: Narrowing a topic and building a search
problem: Narrow the topic "vaping" into a research question and write a Boolean search string.
step: Narrow by population: high school students.
step: Narrow by aspect: the effect of school policies on student use.
step: Write the question: "Do school policies that offer counseling instead of suspension reduce vaping among high school students?"
step: List keywords and synonyms: vaping, e-cigarette, "electronic cigarette," adolescent, "high school," suspension, counseling, policy.
step: Build the search: (vaping OR e-cigarette OR "electronic cigarette") AND (adolescent* OR "high school") AND (policy OR suspension OR counseling). The asterisk (where a database supports it) catches endings such as adolescent and adolescents.

@mistakes
- Starting with a topic so broad that no search can be focused.
- Using only one keyword and giving up when the results are poor.
- Judging a source by its appearance rather than by authorship, evidence and purpose.
- Copying text into notes without quotation marks, then forgetting it was not your wording.

@practice ms
prompt: Which are signs that a source is trustworthy for a research paper? Select all that apply.
choice*: The author's credentials and affiliation are listed and relevant.
choice*: Claims are supported by citations or data you can check.
choice*: Other reliable sources agree with its main findings (lateral reading).
choice: The page looks professional and has no ads.
hint: Appearance is easy to fake.
solution: Credentials, checkable evidence and corroboration show reliability. A professional appearance can be copied by anyone.

answer: 720
check: 2400 * 0.30
hint: Convert 30% to a decimal and multiply.
solution: $2400 \times 0.30 = 720$ results.

@practice self
prompt: You are researching whether free breakfast programs affect school attendance. Write (a) a one-sentence research question, (b) a Boolean search string with at least two sets of synonyms, and (c) two criteria you would use to evaluate whether a result is a good source.
hint: For (b), list synonyms for "breakfast program" and for "attendance" and join each set with OR, then join the sets with AND.
hint: For (c), pick two of authority, accuracy, currency, relevance and purpose, and say what you would look for on the page to judge each.
solution: (a) Do school breakfast programs that serve all students for free improve attendance in public elementary schools? (b) ("school breakfast" OR "free breakfast") AND (attendance OR absenteeism) AND (elementary OR "primary school"). (c) Authority: the author is a researcher or an agency with relevant expertise and the work is peer reviewed. Accuracy: the study reports its method, sample and data so claims can be checked, and it is recent enough to reflect current programs.
rubric: The question is specific and cannot be answered with yes or no alone.
rubric: The search string uses OR for synonyms and AND to combine ideas, with quotation marks for phrases.
rubric: The criteria come from authority, accuracy, currency, relevance or purpose, and are explained.

@quiz ms
prompt: Which are primary sources for a paper about a hospital's handwashing policy? Select all that apply.
choice*: The hospital's published handwashing policy document.
choice*: A study reporting the hospital's own infection rates before and after the policy.
choice: A textbook chapter summarizing handwashing research.
choice: An encyclopedia entry on hand hygiene.
hint: Primary sources give direct evidence.
solution: The policy itself and a study reporting original data are primary. Textbooks and encyclopedias are secondary or tertiary.
