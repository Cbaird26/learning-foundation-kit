@article
**Pharmacodynamics** describes what a drug does to the body: drug–receptor binding and the resulting effect. Most drugs act on receptors (G-protein-coupled receptors, ion channels, enzyme-linked receptors, nuclear receptors); others inhibit enzymes (statins, ACE inhibitors), block transporters (loop diuretics, SSRIs) or act by chemistry (antacids).

### Binding and occupancy

If a drug binds reversibly to a receptor, the fraction of receptors occupied depends on the concentration $C$ and the **dissociation constant** $K_d$ (the concentration at which half of the receptors are occupied):

$$\text{occupancy} = \frac{C}{C + K_d}.$$

A low $K_d$ means high affinity. Occupancy is hyperbolic in $C$: 50% at $C = K_d$, 90% at $C = 9K_d$.

### Dose–response curves

A graded dose–response curve plots effect against the logarithm of dose and is sigmoidal. Two numbers describe it:

- **$E_{max}$ (efficacy)**: the maximal effect the drug can produce.
- **$EC_{50}$ (potency)**: the concentration giving half of $E_{max}$.

With a simple hyperbolic model, $E = E_{max}\,C/(C + EC_{50})$. A **more potent** drug has its curve to the left (smaller $EC_{50}$), but potency says nothing about how large the maximal effect is. Efficacy is usually more clinically important than potency: a high-potency drug that cannot produce the needed effect is not useful, whereas a low potency simply means a larger dose. A low-efficacy drug cannot match a high-efficacy drug at any dose (for example, a weak opioid cannot match the maximal analgesia of a strong opioid).

**Spare receptors** exist when a maximal response occurs at less than full occupancy, so the $EC_{50}$ is lower than the $K_d$.

### Agonists and antagonists

- **Full agonist**: produces the maximal response.
- **Partial agonist**: lower $E_{max}$ even at full occupancy (buprenorphine at the $\mu$-opioid receptor, aripiprazole at dopamine $D_2$). In the presence of a full agonist a partial agonist acts as an antagonist, and buprenorphine can precipitate withdrawal in a patient with a high level of full-agonist opioids.
- **Competitive antagonist**: binds the same site reversibly, shifts the curve **to the right in parallel** with no change in $E_{max}$ (surmountable by more agonist); increases the apparent $EC_{50}$. Example: naloxone, atropine, propranolol.
- **Non-competitive (or irreversible) antagonist**: reduces $E_{max}$; the curve flattens and cannot be overcome by more agonist. Example: phenoxybenzamine binds $\alpha$ receptors covalently.
- **Inverse agonist**: reduces constitutive receptor activity below baseline.

For a competitive antagonist at concentration $[B]$ with dissociation constant $K_B$, the agonist **dose ratio** (how many times more agonist is needed for the same effect) is

$$\text{dose ratio} = 1 + \frac{[B]}{K_B}.$$

**Physiologic antagonism** uses opposing pathways (for example, epinephrine reverses histamine-mediated bronchospasm). **Chemical antagonism** binds the drug itself (protamine binds heparin).

### Quantal curves and therapeutic index

A quantal dose–response curve plots the fraction of subjects with a defined all-or-none effect. $ED_{50}$ is the dose effective in 50% of subjects, $TD_{50}$ the dose toxic in 50%, and $LD_{50}$ (animals only) the lethal dose in 50%:

$$\text{therapeutic index} = \frac{TD_{50}}{ED_{50}}.$$

A narrow index means little room between effect and toxicity (warfarin, digoxin, lithium, phenytoin, aminoglycosides); these require monitoring. The **certain safety factor**, $TD_1/ED_{99}$, is a stricter measure.

### Changes in response over time

**Tachyphylaxis** is a rapid loss of response with repeated doses (nitroglycerin patches, pseudoephedrine). **Tolerance** develops over a longer time (opioids). **Desensitization** and receptor down-regulation underlie both; up-regulation can occur after chronic blockade (rebound hypertension after abrupt $\beta$-blocker withdrawal).

@example
title: Occupancy, response and therapeutic index
problem: A drug has $K_d = 4$ nM and $E_{max} = 80\%$ of maximal tissue response, with $EC_{50} = 4$ nM. At a concentration of 12 nM, find the occupancy and the response. Separately, a drug has $ED_{50} = 20$ mg and $TD_{50} = 200$ mg; find its therapeutic index.
step: Occupancy $= C/(C + K_d) = 12/(12 + 4) = 0.75$, or 75%.
step: Response $= E_{max} \times C/(C + EC_{50}) = 80\% \times 12/16 = 60\%$.
step: Therapeutic index $= TD_{50}/ED_{50} = 200/20 = 10$.
@mistakes
- Equating potency with efficacy; a drug can be more potent and have lower $E_{max}$.
- Expecting more agonist to overcome a non-competitive antagonist; only competitive blockade is surmountable.
- Forgetting that a competitive antagonist leaves $E_{max}$ unchanged and shifts only the curve to the right.
- Using $TD_{50}/ED_{50}$ with doses from different animals or endpoints; both must be measured on the same quantal scale.
@practice num
prompt: A drug binds its receptor with $K_d = 6$ nM. At a concentration of 2 nM, what fraction of receptors is occupied? Give an exact fraction.
answer: 1/4
check: 2 / (2 + 6)
hint: Occupancy $= C/(C + K_d)$.
solution: $2/(2 + 6) = 2/8 = 1/4$.
