@article
Standard doses are derived from healthy young adults. Physiology changes in other groups, so the same dose can fail or poison.

### Pregnancy and lactation

Most drugs cross the placenta by passive diffusion, especially lipophilic, low-molecular-weight, non-ionized drugs. Heparins and insulin do not cross appreciably. Risk depends on the **timing**: the first 2 weeks after conception are all-or-none; organogenesis (weeks 3 to 8 after conception) carries the highest risk of structural malformations; later exposure affects growth and function. In 2015 the FDA replaced the old A, B, C, D, X letter categories with narrative labeling (Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule), though the letters are still widely quoted.

Important teratogens and fetal toxins:

- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs**: fetal renal dysfunction, oligohydramnios, pulmonary hypoplasia, skull hypoplasia, especially in the second and third trimesters.
- **Warfarin**: embryopathy (nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses) and fetal bleeding.
- **Valproate**: neural-tube defects and neurodevelopmental effects; **phenytoin**: fetal hydantoin syndrome; **carbamazepine**: neural-tube defects.
- **Isotretinoin** (and other retinoids): craniofacial, cardiac and CNS defects (requires a pregnancy-prevention program). **Methotrexate, misoprostol, mycophenolate, thalidomide** (limb defects, phocomelia).
- **Tetracyclines**: staining of teeth, bone effects. **Aminoglycosides**: ototoxicity (streptomycin). **Lithium**: cardiac malformations (small increase in Ebstein anomaly risk). **Alcohol**: fetal alcohol spectrum disorder. **Testosterone-like agents** (danazol): virilization. **Diethylstilbestrol**: clear-cell vaginal adenocarcinoma in exposed daughters. **NSAIDs** after about 30 weeks: premature ductus closure.
- Folic acid supplementation lowers the risk of neural-tube defects.

Preferred drugs: labetalol, nifedipine and methyldopa for hypertension; insulin for diabetes; levothyroxine for hypothyroidism; heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin for anticoagulation; acetaminophen for pain. During **lactation**, drugs transfer into milk by diffusion; weakly acidic, highly protein-bound, large drugs transfer least, and maternal CYP2D6 ultrarapid status makes codeine hazardous.

### Children

Neonates have immature glucuronidation (chloramphenicol, "gray baby syndrome"), reduced renal clearance, higher body-water fraction (larger $V_d$ for water-soluble drugs), lower plasma proteins and a more permeable blood–brain barrier. Sulfonamides and ceftriaxone can displace bilirubin and are avoided in neonates (kernicterus; ceftriaxone also precipitates with calcium). Avoid tetracyclines in children under 8 years (teeth) and aspirin in viral illness (Reye syndrome). Children generally have fast hepatic clearance, so they may need higher mg/kg doses than adults. Doses are **weight-based** (mg/kg) up to the adult maximum, and dosing errors from decimal mistakes are a major hazard.

### Older adults

With age, lean mass and total body water decrease and fat increases, so **lipophilic drugs have larger $V_d$** (diazepam, longer half-life) and water-soluble drugs have smaller $V_d$ (higher levels from the same dose, such as digoxin and gentamicin). **GFR declines** about 1 mL/min/year after the third decade, often with a normal serum creatinine because muscle mass is low. Hepatic blood flow and phase I metabolism decline; glucuronidation is relatively preserved. Sensitivity to CNS drugs, anticholinergics and antihypertensives increases. **Polypharmacy** drives interactions, falls and delirium. The **Beers criteria** list potentially inappropriate drugs (long-acting benzodiazepines, first-generation antihistamines, strongly anticholinergic drugs, sliding-scale insulin alone, NSAIDs with kidney disease). The guiding principle is "start low and go slow", with deprescribing review.

### Kidney disease

Renally cleared drugs accumulate: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, digoxin, lithium, enoxaparin, gabapentin, many antibiotics and metformin (avoid when eGFR is below 30). Avoid nephrotoxins, and note that meperidine's metabolite accumulates. Estimate creatinine clearance with the **Cockcroft–Gault equation**:

$$\text{CrCl (mL/min)} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight (kg)}}{72 \times \text{SCr (mg/dL)}} \quad (\times\, 0.85 \text{ for women}).$$

For a drug eliminated partly unchanged by the kidney, with fraction excreted unchanged $f_e$ (about 1 for a drug eliminated almost entirely by the kidney), the **dosing factor** is

$$Q = 1 - f_e\left(1 - \frac{\text{CrCl}}{120}\right),$$

where 120 mL/min is the assumed normal clearance (this is the Giusti–Hayton convention; some references use 100 or 125 mL/min as normal, and others adjust by lengthening the interval from a CrCl table, so always follow the stated method), and the adjusted maintenance dose is $Q \times$ the usual dose (or the usual dose given at an interval lengthened by $1/Q$). A **loading dose** is generally **not** reduced, because it depends on $V_d$ rather than clearance.

@example
title: Estimating CrCl and adjusting a dose
problem: A 75-year-old man weighing 70 kg has SCr 1.5 mg/dL. A renally cleared drug has $f_e = 0.9$ and a usual maintenance dose of 300 mg. Estimate CrCl and the adjusted dose (study exercise).
step: $\text{CrCl} = (140 - 75)(70)/(72 \times 1.5) = 4550/108 = 42.1$ mL/min.
step: $Q = 1 - 0.9(1 - 42.1/120) = 1 - 0.9(0.649) = 0.416$.
step: Adjusted maintenance dose $= 0.416 \times 300 \approx 125$ mg.
step: A loading dose, if needed, would not be reduced.
@mistakes
- Reducing a loading dose for kidney disease; it depends on $V_d$ not clearance.
- Forgetting the 0.85 factor for women in Cockcroft–Gault.
- Trusting a normal creatinine in an older, frail patient; low muscle mass overestimates function.
- Assuming that all drugs in neonates are handled like small adults.

prompt: A 70-year-old man weighs 80 kg and has a serum creatinine of 1.4 mg/dL. Estimate his creatinine clearance by the Cockcroft–Gault equation, in mL/min to one decimal.
answer: 55.6
check: (140 - 70) * 80 / (72 * 1.4)
unit: mL/min
tol: 0.2
hint: Use $(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight}/(72 \times \text{SCr})$.
solution: $70 \times 80 = 5600$; $72 \times 1.4 = 100.8$; $5600/100.8 = 55.6$ mL/min.
