@article
Antimicrobials are chosen by matching the likely or proven organism, the site of infection, host factors (allergy, kidney and liver function, pregnancy) and local resistance patterns. **Selective toxicity** exploits targets present in microbes but not in human cells.

### MIC and killing patterns

The **minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)** is the lowest drug concentration that prevents visible growth in broth after incubation. The **minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)** is the lowest concentration that kills 99.9% of the inoculum. **Bacteriostatic** agents (tetracyclines, macrolides, clindamycin, linezolid, trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) stop growth and depend on host defenses, while **bactericidal** agents (beta-lactams, aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, vancomycin, daptomycin, metronidazole) kill. The distinction matters in endocarditis, meningitis and neutropenia, where bactericidal drugs are preferred.

Killing patterns determine dosing:

- **Time-dependent** (beta-lactams and most macrolides; vancomycin is usually grouped here too, but its target is AUC/MIC): efficacy depends on the time the free concentration exceeds the MIC ($fT>\text{MIC}$). Give more frequent doses or extended infusions.
- **Concentration-dependent** (aminoglycosides, fluoroquinolones, daptomycin, metronidazole): efficacy depends on the peak concentration relative to the MIC ($C_{max}/\text{MIC}$ about 10 or more for aminoglycosides). Give larger, less frequent doses; aminoglycosides also have a **post-antibiotic effect** that supports once-daily dosing.

### Classes: mechanism, spectrum and key toxicities

| Class | Mechanism | Key spectrum | Signature adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Penicillins | Bind PBPs; inhibit cell-wall cross-linking (transpeptidation) | Streptococci, *Treponema*; broader with ampicillin/amoxicillin; piperacillin adds *Pseudomonas* | Hypersensitivity, rash, C. difficile |
| Cephalosporins | Same as penicillins; later generations cover more Gram-negatives | Cefazolin Gram-positive; ceftriaxone Gram-negative and CNS; cefepime *Pseudomonas* | Hypersensitivity; cross-reactivity low |
| Carbapenems | Same | Very broad; including anaerobes, ESBL organisms | Seizures (imipenem) |
| Vancomycin | Binds D-Ala-D-Ala; blocks cell-wall synthesis | Gram-positives incl. MRSA | Infusion reaction, nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity |
| Aminoglycosides | Bind 30S subunit, misread mRNA (bactericidal) | Aerobic Gram-negatives; not anaerobes (need oxygen-dependent uptake) | Nephrotoxicity, ototoxicity, neuromuscular blockade |
| Tetracyclines | Block aminoacyl-tRNA binding at 30S | Atypicals, rickettsiae, *Borrelia* | Teeth and bone effects in children, photosensitivity |
| Macrolides | Block translocation at 50S | Atypicals, streptococci | QT prolongation, GI upset, CYP3A4 inhibition (not azithromycin) |
| Clindamycin | 50S | Anaerobes above the diaphragm, Gram-positive | *C. difficile* |
| Fluoroquinolones | Inhibit DNA gyrase and topoisomerase IV | Gram-negatives, atypicals | Tendinopathy, QT, cartilage effects in children, CNS effects, dysglycemia |
| TMP-SMX | Sequential block of folate synthesis | UTI, *Pneumocystis* | Hyperkalemia, rash, marrow suppression, sulfonamide allergy |
| Metronidazole | Forms DNA-damaging radicals in anaerobes | Anaerobes, protozoa | Metallic taste, neuropathy |
| Linezolid | 50S initiation | Gram-positive including MRSA and VRE | Marrow suppression, serotonin syndrome |
| Daptomycin | Membrane depolarization | Gram-positive including MRSA | Myopathy; inactivated by lung surfactant |

### Resistance

Main mechanisms: **beta-lactamases** (inactivated by clavulanate, tazobactam or avibactam), **altered targets** (PBP2a encoded by *mecA* in MRSA; altered D-Ala-D-Lac in VRE; ribosomal methylation for macrolides; gyrase mutations), **efflux pumps** and **reduced permeability**, and drug-modifying enzymes (aminoglycoside acetyltransferases). Resistance spreads by plasmids, transposons and conjugation, transformation or transduction.

### Principles of use

Take cultures before starting antibiotics when feasible. Start **empiric** therapy broad when the patient is unstable, then narrow (**de-escalate**) when culture and susceptibility data are available. Choose the shortest effective duration. Combination therapy is used for synergy (beta-lactam with aminoglycoside), to prevent resistance (tuberculosis) and for polymicrobial infection. Check allergy history carefully; many patients labeled penicillin-allergic are not truly allergic, and allergy delabeling is encouraged.

@example
title: Reading an MIC and a pharmacodynamic target
problem: In a broth dilution, tubes contain 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16 mcg/mL of an antibiotic. Growth is visible at 0.5, 1 and 2 mcg/mL and absent at 4, 8 and 16. Another patient has a peak gentamicin concentration of 24 mcg/mL against an isolate with an MIC of 2 mcg/mL. Give the MIC and the $C_{max}/\text{MIC}$ ratio.
step: The MIC is the lowest concentration with no visible growth: 4 mcg/mL.
step: For concentration-dependent killing, compute $C_{max}/\text{MIC} = 24/2 = 12$.
step: A ratio of about 10 or more is the usual aminoglycoside target, so this peak is adequate.
@mistakes
- Confusing the MIC with the concentration that kills; MIC measures inhibition of growth.
- Using an aminoglycoside for anaerobic infection; uptake requires oxygen.
- Treating concentration-dependent drugs like time-dependent drugs (and the reverse).
- Assuming a bacteriostatic drug is always inferior; for many infections it works well.
