@article
**Metabolism** converts drugs into more polar compounds that are easier to excrete. The liver is the main site; the gut wall, kidney and lung contribute.

### Phase I and phase II

**Phase I reactions** (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) are mostly carried out by the **cytochrome P450 (CYP)** enzymes and add or expose a functional group. They often produce a metabolite that is more or less active than the parent. A **prodrug** needs metabolic activation: codeine (to morphine by CYP2D6), clopidogrel (CYP2C19), enalapril (esterases) and azathioprine (to mercaptopurine). **Phase II reactions** (glucuronidation, sulfation, acetylation, glutathione conjugation) attach a polar group and usually inactivate the drug. Glucuronidation (UGT) is relatively preserved in older age and liver disease (lorazepam, oxazepam and temazepam are glucuronidated), but is immature in neonates.

The CYP enzymes most relevant to drug interactions are CYP3A4 (about half of drugs, also in the gut wall), CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP1A2 and CYP2E1.

### Induction

An **inducer** increases transcription of CYP enzymes. Clearance rises, so substrate levels fall. Induction takes **days to weeks** to develop (synthesis of new enzyme) and fades over weeks after the inducer stops. Common inducers: rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, St John's wort, chronic alcohol and griseofulvin (remember "Rifampin, Carbamazepine, Phenytoin, Phenobarbital, St John's wort, Chronic alcohol, Griseofulvin"). Consequences: failure of oral contraceptives, reduced warfarin effect (lower INR), reduced cyclosporine and tacrolimus levels with graft rejection risk. For prodrugs, induction can increase the active metabolite.

### Inhibition

An **inhibitor** blocks enzyme activity, usually immediately, so substrate levels rise. Inhibition is usually reversible and acts within hours to days. Common inhibitors: azole antifungals (ketoconazole, itraconazole, fluconazole), macrolides except azithromycin (clarithromycin, erythromycin), protease inhibitors (ritonavir), cimetidine, amiodarone, ciprofloxacin, isoniazid, valproate, acute alcohol and **grapefruit juice** (inhibits intestinal CYP3A4). Consequences: toxicity from statins (simvastatin and rhabdomyolysis), tacrolimus, calcium channel blockers or benzodiazepines; and lower activation of prodrugs (for example, fluoxetine or paroxetine are CYP2D6 inhibitors that reduce conversion of codeine to morphine).

### Genetic polymorphism

Genes for some enzymes vary. CYP2D6 can be absent (poor metabolizers: codeine ineffective) or duplicated (ultrarapid metabolizers: dangerous morphine levels from codeine, including in breastfed infants of mothers who are ultrarapid). CYP2C19 poor metabolizers get little benefit from clopidogrel. **NAT2** slow acetylators have more isoniazid toxicity (neuropathy, lupus-like reaction). **TPMT** deficiency causes mercaptopurine and azathioprine myelosuppression; **G6PD** deficiency is a pharmacodynamic/biochemical susceptibility to oxidant drugs (primaquine, dapsone, rasburicase); **HLA-B\*57:01** predicts abacavir hypersensitivity.

### Predicting a change in drug level

At steady state $C_{ss} = \text{rate}/CL$. A drug taken at a fixed rate therefore changes inversely with clearance:

$$\frac{C_{ss,\text{new}}}{C_{ss,\text{old}}} = \frac{CL_{\text{old}}}{CL_{\text{new}}}.$$

Halving clearance doubles the steady-state concentration. The change takes about 4 to 5 *new* half-lives to appear, and since halving clearance also doubles the half-life, the delay is longer than usual. When an inducer is stopped, enzyme activity returns to baseline over weeks and the substrate level rises again, so doses may need to be lowered.

### Other interaction types

Not all interactions are metabolic. **Absorption** interactions (calcium or iron chelating tetracyclines and fluoroquinolones), **renal** interactions (probenecid blocks tubular secretion of penicillin; NSAIDs raise lithium levels), **P-glycoprotein** effects and **pharmacodynamic** interactions (two QT-prolonging drugs, an opioid with a benzodiazepine, an ACE inhibitor with a potassium-sparing diuretic) are all common.

### Hepatic disease and the first-pass effect

Cirrhosis reduces first-pass extraction (higher bioavailability of propranolol and others) and reduces clearance of low-extraction drugs; dose reduction and avoiding drugs with active hepatotoxicity are needed.

@example
title: Predicting a CYP3A4 interaction
problem: A 66-year-old man on simvastatin (CYP3A4 substrate) is started on clarithromycin. Predict the effect on simvastatin exposure, and calculate the new steady-state level if clarithromycin lowers its clearance to 25% of baseline and the baseline steady-state concentration is 4 ng/mL.
step: Clarithromycin is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor, so simvastatin clearance falls.
step: At steady state with a fixed dose, $C_{ss}$ is inversely proportional to clearance: new $C_{ss} = 4/0.25 = 16$ ng/mL.
step: A fourfold rise raises the risk of myopathy and rhabdomyolysis, so the statin is held (or an alternative antibiotic such as azithromycin is chosen).
step: The inhibition acts quickly, but the new steady state still takes about 4 to 5 of the longer half-lives.
@mistakes
- Assuming induction acts as quickly as inhibition; it takes days to weeks.
- Forgetting that inducers lower levels of drugs but can raise levels of active metabolites of prodrugs.
- Assuming grapefruit juice acts only in the liver; it mainly inhibits intestinal CYP3A4.
- Predicting the direction of a prodrug interaction backwards; inhibiting activation lowers the effect.

prompt: A drug's steady-state concentration is 5 mg/L on a fixed dosing rate. A new medication reduces its clearance to 40% of baseline. What is the new steady-state concentration in mg/L if the dose rate is unchanged?
answer: 12.5
check: 5 / 0.4
unit: mg/L
tol: 0.01
hint: $C_{ss} = \text{rate}/CL$, so concentration varies inversely with clearance.
solution: $5/0.4 = 12.5$ mg/L.
