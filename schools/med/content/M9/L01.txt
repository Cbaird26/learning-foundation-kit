@article
**Pharmacokinetics** is what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME). **Pharmacodynamics**, covered in week 3, is what the drug does to the body. This week covers the first two steps of ADME; week 2 covers elimination.

### Crossing membranes

Most drugs reach their target by passive diffusion across lipid membranes. Small, **lipophilic, non-ionized** molecules cross easily. Charged (ionized) molecules cross poorly. A few drugs use carriers (for example, levodopa uses an amino-acid transporter) and many are pushed back out by efflux pumps such as P-glycoprotein.

Most drugs are weak acids or weak bases, so their ionization depends on pH. The **Henderson–Hasselbalch equation** gives the ratio of ionized to non-ionized drug:

$$\text{weak acid: } \log\frac{[\text{ionized}]}{[\text{non-ionized}]} = \text{pH} - \text{p}K_a \qquad \text{weak base: } \log\frac{[\text{ionized}]}{[\text{non-ionized}]} = \text{p}K_a - \text{pH}$$

A weak acid is mostly ionized when the surrounding pH is **above** its $\text{p}K_a$; a weak base is mostly ionized when the pH is **below** its $\text{p}K_a$. The practical consequence is **ion trapping**: a drug diffuses across a membrane in its non-ionized form, then becomes ionized and stays where the pH favors ionization. Alkalinizing the urine with sodium bicarbonate traps salicylate (a weak acid) in the tubular lumen and increases its excretion; acidifying the urine does the same for weak bases, although this is rarely done.

### Routes and bioavailability

**Bioavailability** ($F$) is the fraction of an administered dose that reaches the systemic circulation unchanged. By definition an intravenous dose has $F = 1$. For an oral dose,

$$F = \frac{\text{AUC}_{\text{oral}}}{\text{AUC}_{\text{IV}}} \quad (\text{same dose}),$$

where AUC is the area under the plasma concentration–time curve. Oral bioavailability is reduced by incomplete absorption, gut-wall metabolism (CYP3A4), efflux by P-glycoprotein, and **first-pass metabolism**: drug absorbed from the gut travels in the portal vein through the liver before reaching the systemic circulation. Drugs with high first-pass extraction, such as nitroglycerin, are given **sublingually** or transdermally because those routes drain into the systemic veins and bypass the liver. Rectal administration partly bypasses first pass. Inhaled, intramuscular and subcutaneous routes avoid it as well.

### Distribution and volume of distribution

After absorption, drug distributes between plasma and tissues. The **apparent volume of distribution** is the volume that would be needed to contain the whole dose at the measured plasma concentration:

$$V_d = \frac{\text{amount of drug in the body}}{C_p} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V_d = \frac{\text{dose}}{C_0} \text{ after an IV bolus,}$$

where $C_0$ is the concentration extrapolated to time zero. $V_d$ is not a real space. A drug confined to plasma has $V_d \approx 3$ L; extracellular fluid is about 14 L; total body water is about 42 L in a 70 kg adult. Drugs that bind tissue (digoxin, amiodarone, chloroquine, many antidepressants) have enormous values, hundreds to thousands of liters, because very little remains in plasma. Highly plasma-protein-bound drugs and large molecules (heparin) have small values.

**Plasma protein binding** matters because only unbound drug is active, filtered and metabolized. Acidic drugs bind mainly albumin (warfarin, phenytoin); basic drugs bind $\alpha_1$-acid glycoprotein. Hypoalbuminemia or displacement raises the free fraction, but the effect on the free concentration at steady state is usually transient, so the interaction rarely matters except for narrow-therapeutic-index drugs.

### Loading dose

If you want to reach a target plasma concentration quickly, give a **loading dose** that fills the volume of distribution:

$$\text{loading dose} = \frac{V_d \times C_{\text{target}}}{F}.$$

Notice that the loading dose depends on $V_d$, not on clearance. The maintenance dose, which depends on clearance, is the subject of week 2.

### Barriers

The **blood–brain barrier** (tight junctions, astrocyte end-feet, efflux pumps) admits lipophilic drugs and excludes polar ones; inflamed meninges are more permeable. The placenta behaves similarly and allows most lipophilic drugs through (week 11).

@example
title: Volume of distribution and a loading dose
problem: A 400 mg intravenous bolus of a drug gives an extrapolated plasma concentration $C_0 = 5$ mg/L. (a) What is $V_d$? (b) What oral loading dose would give a target concentration of 8 mg/L if oral $F = 0.8$?
step: After an IV bolus, $V_d = \text{dose}/C_0 = 400 \text{ mg} / 5 \text{ mg/L} = 80$ L.
step: This is more than total body water (about 42 L), so the drug must be binding to tissues rather than staying in plasma.
step: The amount needed in the body is $V_d \times C_{\text{target}} = 80 \times 8 = 640$ mg.
step: Only 80% of an oral dose reaches the circulation, so the oral loading dose is $640 / 0.8 = 800$ mg.
@mistakes
- Treating $V_d$ as a real anatomic volume; a value larger than body water means tissue binding, not an error.
- Forgetting to divide by $F$ when converting a loading dose to an oral dose.
- Reversing the acid/base rule: a weak acid is more ionized in alkaline surroundings, a weak base in acidic ones.
- Believing a loading dose depends on clearance; it depends on $V_d$ and the target concentration.
