@article
Bringing a drug to market takes about a decade or more, and most candidates fail.

### Stages of development

**Preclinical:** in vitro and animal studies for pharmacology, pharmacokinetics and toxicology, including genotoxicity and reproductive toxicity. The sponsor then files an **Investigational New Drug (IND)** application.

- **Phase 0** (optional): microdosing in few subjects to check PK.
- **Phase I:** small numbers of healthy volunteers (patients for cytotoxic drugs); tests safety, tolerability, dose escalation and PK. Establishes maximum tolerated dose.
- **Phase II:** hundreds of patients; first evidence of efficacy, dose-finding and more safety data.
- **Phase III:** large randomized controlled trials (hundreds to thousands) comparing the drug with placebo or standard care; confirm efficacy and detect common adverse effects. Basis for a **New Drug Application (NDA)** or biologics license application.
- **Phase IV (post-marketing surveillance):** detects rare and long-term adverse effects in the broader population (for example, via the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System); can lead to warnings or withdrawal.

Expedited programs include **fast track, breakthrough therapy, priority review** and **accelerated approval** (based on a surrogate endpoint with confirmatory trials), and the **Orphan Drug Act** gives incentives for rare diseases. **Generic drugs** require bioequivalence to the reference product (the 90% confidence intervals of the ratios of AUC and $C_{max}$ lie within 80 to 125%). **Biosimilars** must show high similarity to a biologic.

### Trial design and bias

- **Randomization** balances known and unknown confounders. **Allocation concealment** prevents selection bias.
- **Blinding** (single, double) reduces observer and participant bias; a **placebo** control addresses placebo effects.
- **Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis** analyzes patients in the group they were randomized to, regardless of adherence; it preserves randomization and avoids attrition bias. **Per-protocol analysis** includes only adherent patients and can overestimate effect.
- **Non-inferiority trials** show that a new drug is not worse than a comparator by more than a predefined margin; **superiority trials** show it is better.
- **Crossover trials** let each participant receive both treatments, with a washout period.
- Hard clinical endpoints (death, stroke) are more persuasive than **surrogate** endpoints (LDL, blood pressure), and composite endpoints can mislead if one component dominates.

### Statistics

The **null hypothesis** is that there is no difference. A **type I error** ($\alpha$, false positive; usually set at 0.05) is rejecting a true null; a **type II error** ($\beta$, false negative) is failing to detect a real difference. **Power** $= 1 - \beta$ (often 80% or more) depends on sample size, effect size and variance. A **p-value** is the probability of results at least as extreme as observed if the null is true, and a **95% confidence interval** that excludes the null value (0 for differences, 1 for ratios) corresponds to $p < 0.05$. Statistical significance is not clinical significance.

With control event rate $CER$ and experimental event rate $EER$:

$$\text{ARR} = CER - EER, \quad \text{RRR} = \frac{\text{ARR}}{CER}, \quad \text{NNT} = \frac{1}{\text{ARR}}.$$

### Ethics

Research requires **informed consent**, **institutional review board (IRB)** approval, and a **data and safety monitoring board** for large trials. The Belmont principles are respect for persons, beneficence and justice. Declaration of Helsinki and the Common Rule underpin the oversight system. Equipoise (genuine uncertainty about which arm is better) justifies randomization.

### Dose adjustment for reduced kidney function

The same dosing factor as in week 11 applies to new drugs: $Q = 1 - f_e(1 - \text{CrCl}/120)$, with the adjusted maintenance dose $= Q \times$ the usual dose.

@example
title: ARR, RRR and NNT
problem: In a trial of a new antiplatelet drug, 20% of control patients and 15% of treated patients have a stroke within 2 years. Calculate ARR, RRR and NNT.
step: $\text{ARR} = 20\% - 15\% = 5\% = 0.05$.
step: $\text{RRR} = 0.05/0.20 = 25\%$.
step: $\text{NNT} = 1/0.05 = 20$ patients treated over 2 years to prevent one stroke.
step: The RRR sounds impressive, but the NNT communicates the absolute benefit, which also depends on baseline risk.
@mistakes
- Quoting relative risk reduction without the baseline risk; it exaggerates small absolute benefits.
- Confusing type I error (false positive) with type II error (false negative).
- Excluding non-adherent patients from the primary analysis of a superiority trial and calling it ITT.
- Assuming a phase III trial can detect rare adverse effects; post-marketing surveillance does.
@practice num
prompt: In a trial, 12% of control patients and 8% of treated patients have an event. What is the number needed to treat?
answer: 25
check: 1 / (0.12 - 0.08)
tol: 0.01
hint: NNT is the reciprocal of the absolute risk reduction.
solution: ARR $= 0.04$; NNT $= 1/0.04 = 25$.

@quiz num
prompt: A new renally cleared drug has $f_e = 0.6$ and a usual maintenance dose of 400 mg. A patient's creatinine clearance is 60 mL/min. Using $Q = 1 - f_e(1 - \text{CrCl}/120)$, what is the adjusted maintenance dose in mg?
answer: 280
check: 400 * (1 - 0.6 * (1 - 60 / 120))
unit: mg
tol: 0.01
hint: CrCl/120 is one half.
solution: $Q = 1 - 0.6 \times 0.5 = 0.7$; $0.7 \times 400 = 280$ mg.
