@article
Poisoning is managed by stabilizing the patient first, then identifying the toxin, preventing absorption, enhancing elimination and giving a specific antidote when one exists. In most patients **supportive care** (airway, breathing, circulation, glucose, temperature) is the main treatment.

### General approach

1. **ABCs**, bedside glucose, ECG, and a history of what, how much and when (including co-ingestants).
2. **Decontamination.** Activated charcoal binds many drugs if given within about 1 hour and the airway is protected; it does **not** bind metals (lithium, iron), alcohols or strong acids and bases. It is contraindicated with an unprotected airway or GI obstruction. Gastric lavage is rarely used. Whole-bowel irrigation is considered for sustained-release products and iron.
3. **Enhanced elimination.** Urinary alkalinization (salicylates), multiple-dose charcoal (some drugs), and **hemodialysis** for toxic alcohols, salicylates, lithium, metformin-associated lactic acidosis, valproate and theophylline.
4. **Antidotes**, below.

### Toxidromes

| Toxidrome | Features | Examples | Antidote or key treatment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Opioid | Coma, respiratory depression, miosis | Heroin, fentanyl | Naloxone |
| Sedative–hypnotic | Sedation, respiratory depression, normal pupils | Benzodiazepines | Supportive; flumazenil rarely (seizure risk) |
| Anticholinergic | Hot, dry, flushed, mydriasis, delirium, urinary retention, tachycardia | Diphenhydramine, atropine, TCAs | Supportive, benzodiazepines; physostigmine in selected cases |
| Cholinergic | SLUDGE, miosis, bradycardia, fasciculations | Organophosphates | Atropine plus pralidoxime |
| Sympathomimetic | Agitation, hypertension, tachycardia, mydriasis, diaphoresis | Cocaine, amphetamine | Benzodiazepines |
| Serotonin syndrome | Clonus, hyperreflexia, hyperthermia, diarrhea | SSRIs with MAOIs, linezolid | Stop drugs, benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine |
| Salicylate | Tinnitus, tachypnea, mixed acid–base | Aspirin | Sodium bicarbonate, dialysis |

### Specific antidotes

- **Acetaminophen**: N-acetylcysteine (interpret the serum level on the Rumack–Matthew nomogram from 4 hours after a single acute ingestion; toxic acute ingestion is usually taught as more than 150 mg/kg).
- **Methanol and ethylene glycol**: **fomepizole** (blocks alcohol dehydrogenase) plus dialysis; ethylene glycol causes renal failure with calcium-oxalate crystals; methanol causes visual loss.
- **Carbon monoxide**: 100% oxygen (hyperbaric oxygen in selected cases). **Cyanide**: hydroxocobalamin. **Methemoglobinemia**: methylene blue.
- **Digoxin**: digoxin-specific Fab fragments. **Beta-blocker**: glucagon. **Calcium-channel blocker**: calcium, insulin with glucose.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants** with QRS widening: **sodium bicarbonate**.
- **Heparin**: protamine. **Warfarin**: vitamin K, with prothrombin complex concentrate when bleeding is severe. **Dabigatran**: idarucizumab. **Factor Xa inhibitors**: four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (andexanet alfa was withdrawn from the U.S. market in December 2025).
- **Iron**: deferoxamine. **Lead**: succimer or EDTA. **Arsenic and mercury**: dimercaprol or succimer.
- **Isoniazid** seizures: pyridoxine. **Sulfonylurea**: octreotide. **Local anesthetic systemic toxicity**: lipid emulsion. **Malignant hyperthermia**: dantrolene. **Benzodiazepines**: flumazenil is not routine. **Opioids**: naloxone.

### Calculations

**Anion gap**: $\text{AG} = \text{Na}^+ - \text{Cl}^- - \text{HCO}_3^-$ (normal about 8 to 12 mEq/L; albumin-dependent). Causes of a raised gap include methanol, uremia, diabetic ketoacidosis, propylene glycol, isoniazid or iron, lactic acidosis, ethylene glycol and salicylates ("MUDPILES").

**Osmolar gap**: measured osmolality minus calculated osmolality, where
$$\text{calculated} = 2\,\text{Na}^+ + \frac{\text{glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8}$$
with glucose and BUN in mg/dL. A gap above about 10 mOsm/kg suggests unmeasured osmoles such as toxic alcohols (ethanol, which contributes $\text{ethanol}/3.7$, should be subtracted when the level is known). The gap can fall to normal late as the parent alcohol is metabolized to acids, while the anion gap rises.

**Dose per kilogram**: $\text{mg/kg} = \text{total mg}/\text{weight}$; compare it with the toxic threshold for that agent.

@example
title: Estimating an acetaminophen ingestion and an anion gap
problem: A 70 kg adult says she took thirty 500 mg acetaminophen tablets about 2 hours ago. Her labs show Na 140, Cl 100, HCO3 20 mEq/L. Estimate the ingested dose per kilogram and the anion gap.
step: Total ingested: $30 \times 500 = 15{,}000$ mg.
step: Dose per kilogram: $15{,}000 / 70 = 214$ mg/kg, above the 150 mg/kg threshold.
step: Gap: $140 - 100 - 20 = 20$ mEq/L, which is elevated.
step: Management: activated charcoal is mainly considered within about 1 hour of ingestion with a protected airway, so at 2 hours its benefit is uncertain; start N-acetylcysteine by the level on the nomogram at 4 hours, or empirically if timing or level is uncertain.
@mistakes
- Giving activated charcoal for lithium, iron or alcohols, which it does not bind.
- Using flumazenil for benzodiazepine overdose in a chronic user or a mixed overdose; seizures can occur.
- Waiting for the acetaminophen level before starting treatment when the ingestion time is unknown.
- Forgetting that the osmolar gap can be normal late in toxic-alcohol poisoning.

unit: mOsm/kg
tol: 0.5
