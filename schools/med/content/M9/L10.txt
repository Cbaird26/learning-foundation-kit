@article
An **adverse drug reaction (ADR)** is a harmful, unintended response to a drug at normal doses. Medication errors are a separate and preventable category.

### Classification

- **Type A (augmented)**: predictable, dose-dependent extensions of the pharmacology: bleeding with warfarin, hypoglycemia with insulin, sedation with benzodiazepines. Common and often manageable by dose adjustment.
- **Type B (bizarre)**: unpredictable, not dose-related: immune-mediated reactions, idiosyncratic reactions (halothane hepatitis), pharmacogenomic reactions. Less common but severe.
- Other types describe chronic effects (adrenal suppression with steroids), delayed effects (teratogenesis, carcinogenesis) and withdrawal.

### Hypersensitivity (Gell and Coombs)

| Type | Mechanism | Drug examples |
|---|---|---|
| I | IgE, mast-cell degranulation (minutes) | Penicillin anaphylaxis, urticaria |
| II | Antibody to cell-bound antigen | Hemolytic anemia (penicillin, methyldopa), heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (platelet factor 4 antibodies) |
| III | Immune complexes | Serum sickness (including with antithymocyte globulin) |
| IV | T-cell mediated (days) | Contact dermatitis, Stevens–Johnson syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis, DRESS |

Severe cutaneous reactions are associated with specific alleles: **HLA-B\*15:02** (carbamazepine, in people of Asian ancestry), **HLA-B\*58:01** (allopurinol) and **HLA-B\*57:01** (abacavir hypersensitivity). Screening before starting is standard for abacavir. **Drug-induced lupus** (hydralazine, procainamide, isoniazid) features anti-histone antibodies, and typically resolves after withdrawal. **Pseudoallergic** reactions occur without IgE: vancomycin infusion reaction (rapid infusion, histamine release, flushing; slow the infusion and give an antihistamine), opioids, and radiocontrast.

### Organ-specific toxicities to know

- **Heart**: doxorubicin (dilated cardiomyopathy, dose-related), QT-prolonging drugs (macrolides, fluoroquinolones, antipsychotics, methadone, ondansetron), trastuzumab.
- **Lung**: amiodarone, bleomycin and nitrofurantoin (pulmonary fibrosis), methotrexate.
- **Liver**: acetaminophen, isoniazid, valproate, amiodarone, statins (mild).
- **Kidney**: aminoglycosides, vancomycin, NSAIDs, amphotericin B, cisplatin, contrast, ACE inhibitors (reduced GFR in renal artery stenosis).
- **Ear**: aminoglycosides, loop diuretics, cisplatin.
- **Nerve and muscle**: vincristine (neuropathy), statins (myopathy, rhabdomyolysis), isoniazid (pyridoxine-responsive neuropathy).
- **Endocrine**: glucocorticoids (adrenal suppression, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis), amiodarone (thyroid).
- **Signature reactions**: **ACE inhibitor cough and angioedema** (bradykinin accumulation); **serotonin syndrome**; **neuroleptic malignant syndrome** (dopamine antagonists; rigidity, fever, elevated CK); **malignant hyperthermia** (volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine; dantrolene); **tardive dyskinesia** with chronic antipsychotics.

### Medication safety

Errors occur at prescribing, transcription, dispensing, administration and monitoring. Strategies: the **five rights** (right patient, drug, dose, route, time; many add documentation), medication reconciliation at transitions of care, **barcode scanning**, computerized order entry with decision support, independent double checks for **high-alert medications** (insulin, anticoagulants, opioids, concentrated electrolytes, chemotherapy), tall-man lettering for look-alike sound-alike names, and a **just culture** that encourages reporting of errors and near misses, supported by systems (Swiss-cheese model) rather than individual blame. The Joint Commission "do not use" list includes **U** for units, trailing zeros (write 5 mg, never 5.0 mg), a missing leading zero (write 0.5 mg, never .5 mg) and **QD**. Teach-back confirms patient understanding. Report suspected ADRs to the FDA MedWatch system. Older adults benefit from review against the **Beers criteria** (week 11).

### Number needed to harm

If a drug raises the absolute risk of an adverse event by $\text{ARI}$ (absolute risk increase), then
$$\text{NNH} = \frac{1}{\text{ARI}}.$$

@example
title: Number needed to harm and toxidrome matching
problem: In a trial, 9% of patients on drug A have a bleeding event versus 5% on control. Calculate the NNH. Then match: pinpoint pupils with respiratory depression; QRS widening from amitriptyline; ethylene glycol; organophosphate poisoning.
step: Absolute risk increase $= 9\% - 5\% = 4\% = 0.04$.
step: $\text{NNH} = 1/0.04 = 25$: treating 25 patients causes one additional bleeding event.
step: Matches: opioid, naloxone; amitriptyline, sodium bicarbonate; ethylene glycol, fomepizole; organophosphate, atropine plus pralidoxime.
@mistakes
- Calling any rash an allergy; ADR history should be classified before recording an allergy.
- Writing "5.0 mg" or ".5 mg", which invites a tenfold error.
- Treating a vancomycin infusion reaction as a type I allergy and stopping all therapy.
- Computing NNH from relative risk instead of absolute risk difference.
@practice num
prompt: In a trial, 6% of patients on a new drug have an adverse event versus 2% on placebo. What is the number needed to harm?
answer: 25
check: 1 / (0.06 - 0.02)
tol: 0.01
hint: NNH is the reciprocal of the absolute risk increase.
solution: ARI $= 0.04$; NNH $= 1/0.04 = 25$.
