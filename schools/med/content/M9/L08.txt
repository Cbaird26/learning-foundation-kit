### NSAIDs and aspirin

**Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs** inhibit **cyclooxygenase (COX)**, which converts arachidonic acid to prostaglandins and thromboxane. COX-1 is constitutive (gastric mucosal protection, platelet thromboxane A2, renal blood flow); COX-2 is induced at inflammation. Inhibition gives analgesic, anti-inflammatory and antipyretic effects.

**Aspirin** acetylates COX **irreversibly**, so platelets (which lack a nucleus) cannot make new enzyme and the antiplatelet effect lasts for the platelet lifespan, about 7 to 10 days. Low doses (typically 81 mg daily) are used for secondary prevention of atherosclerotic events. Other NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, ketorolac, indomethacin) are **reversible** inhibitors. **Celecoxib** is COX-2 selective: less gastric toxicity, but no platelet inhibition, so it does not protect against thrombosis, and cardiovascular risk is a concern with the class.

Adverse effects: **gastric ulcer and bleeding** (reduced mucosal prostaglandins; gastroprotection with a proton pump inhibitor or misoprostol), **acute kidney injury** (prostaglandins dilate the afferent arteriole, so blocking them with volume depletion or an ACE inhibitor plus a diuretic can reduce GFR, the "triple whammy"), hypertension and fluid retention, platelet dysfunction, and **aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease** (asthma, nasal polyps and NSAID sensitivity). Children with viral illness should not receive aspirin because of **Reye syndrome**. Salicylate toxicity causes tinnitus, hyperventilation with mixed respiratory alkalosis and anion-gap metabolic acidosis, fever and altered mental status (week 9). NSAIDs in the third trimester can close the ductus arteriosus prematurely; indomethacin and ibuprofen are used deliberately to close a patent ductus in preterm infants.

### Acetaminophen

Acetaminophen (paracetamol) is analgesic and antipyretic with little anti-inflammatory effect; its mechanism is mainly central. It is conjugated by glucuronidation and sulfation; a small fraction is oxidized by CYP2E1 and CYP3A4 to the toxic metabolite **NAPQI**, normally detoxified by **glutathione**. In overdose, glutathione is depleted and NAPQI injures hepatocytes (centrilobular necrosis). The antidote is **N-acetylcysteine**, which replenishes glutathione and is most effective within 8 to 10 hours of ingestion. Chronic alcohol use and fasting increase risk. The maximum adult daily dose is commonly taught as 4 g, with many clinicians using 3 g or less for chronic use.

### Opioids

Opioids act on $\mu$, $\kappa$ and $\delta$ receptors, which are $G_i$-coupled: they reduce cAMP, close presynaptic calcium channels and open potassium channels, hyperpolarizing neurons and inhibiting transmitter release. The $\mu$ receptor mediates analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, miosis, constipation and dependence.

- **Morphine, hydromorphone, oxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl**: full $\mu$ agonists.
- **Codeine**: prodrug (CYP2D6 converts it to morphine); **tramadol** acts as a weak opioid agonist plus serotonin and norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor (lowers seizure threshold, serotonin syndrome risk).
- **Meperidine**: active metabolite normeperidine accumulates in renal impairment and causes seizures; serotonergic.
- **Methadone**: long half-life, NMDA antagonism, QT prolongation.
- **Buprenorphine**: partial agonist, high affinity, ceiling on respiratory depression; used for opioid use disorder.
- **Naloxone** is a competitive antagonist (short-acting; may need repeat doses or infusion because it wears off before many opioids). **Naltrexone** is longer acting.

Adverse effects: respiratory depression, sedation, nausea, **constipation**, urinary retention, pruritus (histamine release with morphine), biliary spasm, **miosis**. **Tolerance** develops to analgesia, euphoria, sedation, nausea and respiratory depression but **not to miosis and constipation**. Opioid withdrawal is unpleasant but rarely dangerous: lacrimation, rhinorrhea, yawning, mydriasis, piloerection, diarrhea, abdominal cramps, anxiety. Overdose presents with the triad of coma, respiratory depression and pinpoint pupils.

### Morphine milligram equivalents

To compare opioid regimens, convert each daily dose to **oral morphine milligram equivalents (MME)**: $\text{MME/day} = \text{daily dose (mg)} \times \text{conversion factor}$. The CDC 2022 factors include oral oxycodone 1.5 and oral hydrocodone 1, while morphine is 1. Conversion factors are approximations and differ by source; clinicians reduce the dose when switching because of incomplete cross-tolerance.

@example
title: Calculating daily MME
problem: A patient takes oral oxycodone 10 mg every 6 hours and oral morphine 15 mg twice daily. Using factors of 1.5 for oxycodone and 1 for morphine, find the total daily MME.
step: Oxycodone: $10\ \text{mg} \times 4 = 40$ mg per day.
step: Oxycodone MME $= 40 \times 1.5 = 60$.
step: Morphine: $15 \times 2 = 30$ mg per day, factor 1, so 30 MME.
step: Total $= 60 + 30 = 90$ MME/day.
@mistakes
- Believing aspirin's antiplatelet effect is reversible within hours; it persists for the platelet's lifespan.
- Assuming tolerance develops to opioid-induced constipation and miosis.
- Giving only naloxone once for a long-acting opioid overdose; observe for renarcotization.
- Combining NSAIDs, an ACE inhibitor and a diuretic without monitoring renal function.
