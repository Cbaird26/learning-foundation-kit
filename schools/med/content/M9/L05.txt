@article
The **parasympathetic** nervous system uses acetylcholine (ACh). ACh is made from choline and acetyl-CoA by choline acetyltransferase, released at the terminals, and broken down in the synapse by **acetylcholinesterase (AChE)**. Receptors are of two kinds:

- **Muscarinic** (G-protein-coupled): M1 (CNS, gastric parietal cells), M2 (heart; $G_i$ slows the sinoatrial node and AV node), M3 ($G_q$: smooth-muscle contraction in the gut, bladder and bronchi, pupillary sphincter and ciliary muscle contraction, gland secretion, and endothelial nitric-oxide release causing vasodilation).
- **Nicotinic**: ligand-gated sodium channels at autonomic ganglia (both sympathetic and parasympathetic) and at the neuromuscular junction (skeletal muscle). Sweat glands are sympathetic but use muscarinic receptors.

### Predicting effects

A cholinergic (muscarinic) drug causes the picture often remembered as SLUDGE or DUMBELS: salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI cramps, emesis, plus **miosis**, **bradycardia**, bronchospasm and bronchorrhea. A muscarinic **antagonist** gives the reverse: **mydriasis**, cycloplegia (loss of accommodation), **tachycardia**, dry mouth, dry skin, urinary retention, constipation, reduced bronchial secretions.

**Heart rate and pupil rule of thumb.** Parasympathetic stimulation: slow heart (M2), small pupil (M3 sphincter). Muscarinic blockade: fast heart, big pupil. (Sympathetic effects are in week 6.)

### Direct cholinergic agonists

- **Bethanechol**: muscarinic agonist, resistant to AChE, used for non-obstructive urinary retention and neurogenic bladder (and postoperative ileus).
- **Pilocarpine**: muscarinic agonist used in glaucoma (miosis opens trabecular outflow) and dry mouth (Sjögren syndrome, post-radiation).
- **Methacholine**: used in the bronchial challenge test for asthma.
- **Carbachol**: also acts on nicotinic receptors; used topically in the eye.

### Cholinesterase inhibitors

These raise ACh levels at all sites with cholinergic transmission.

- **Neostigmine**: quaternary amine (does not enter CNS); used for myasthenia gravis, to reverse non-depolarizing neuromuscular blockade, and for ileus and urinary retention. It is usually given with an antimuscarinic (glycopyrrolate) to prevent bradycardia.
- **Pyridostigmine**: long-acting oral therapy for myasthenia gravis.
- **Edrophonium**: very short-acting (used for diagnostic testing in the past).
- **Physostigmine**: tertiary amine that **crosses the blood–brain barrier**; used for anticholinergic toxicity.
- **Donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine**: CNS-acting for Alzheimer disease; adverse effects are nausea, bradycardia and syncope.

Overdose of a cholinesterase inhibitor in myasthenia gravis causes a **cholinergic crisis** (weakness plus SLUDGE and miosis); under-treatment causes a **myasthenic crisis**.

### Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphates (insecticides such as parathion; nerve agents) **irreversibly phosphorylate AChE**. The result is muscarinic overstimulation (miosis, bradycardia, bronchorrhea, diarrhea), nicotinic effects (fasciculations, weakness, paralysis, tachycardia) and CNS effects (confusion, seizures). Treatment is decontamination plus **atropine** (reverses muscarinic effects, titrated to drying of bronchial secretions) and **pralidoxime**, which reactivates AChE by breaking the phosphate bond. Pralidoxime must be given early, before the enzyme–inhibitor complex "ages" and becomes unreactivatable. Benzodiazepines control seizures.

### Muscarinic antagonists

- **Atropine**: increases heart rate in symptomatic bradycardia; reverses organophosphate toxicity; mydriasis and cycloplegia. Adverse effects: "hot as a hare, dry as a bone, red as a beet, blind as a bat, mad as a hatter".
- **Ipratropium, tiotropium**: inhaled for COPD and asthma.
- **Oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin**: overactive bladder.
- **Scopolamine**: motion sickness (patch).
- **Benztropine, trihexyphenidyl**: parkinsonism and drug-induced extrapyramidal symptoms.
- **Glycopyrrolate**: reduces secretions and treats drooling.
Anticholinergic drugs are to be avoided in older adults (delirium) and in closed-angle glaucoma and in benign prostatic hyperplasia (urinary retention).

### Nicotinic blockers

**Succinylcholine** is a depolarizing neuromuscular blocker (rapid, short-acting; hyperkalemia and malignant hyperthermia risk). **Rocuronium, vecuronium and cisatracurium** are non-depolarizing competitive blockers reversed by neostigmine or sugammadex.

@example
title: Predicting heart rate and pupil effects
problem: A 35-year-old man is given pilocarpine eye drops for glaucoma, and another man is given atropine eye drops for an eye examination. Predict the pupil size in each, and predict the heart rate effect if a large systemic dose of each were given.
step: Pilocarpine is a muscarinic agonist: it contracts the pupillary sphincter (miosis) and, in large systemic doses, slows the heart via M2 receptors (bradycardia, along with sweating and salivation).
step: Atropine is a muscarinic antagonist: it relaxes the sphincter (mydriasis, with cycloplegia) and blocks vagal tone on the sinoatrial node, giving tachycardia.
step: Summary: parasympathetic agonist gives small pupil and slow heart; antagonist gives large pupil and fast heart.
@mistakes
- Predicting tachycardia from a cholinergic drug; muscarinic stimulation slows the heart.
- Forgetting that sweat glands are sympathetic yet muscarinic, so cholinergic overdose causes sweating.
- Giving pralidoxime late; it works only before aging and does not replace atropine.
- Using physostigmine for poisoning with tricyclic antidepressants, where it can cause seizures and asystole.
