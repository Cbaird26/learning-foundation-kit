@review
### Weeks 1 and 2: pharmacokinetics
Absorption depends on route, ionization and first-pass metabolism; $F = \text{AUC}_{\text{oral}}/\text{AUC}_{\text{IV}}$. $V_d = \text{dose}/C_0$. Loading dose $= V_d C_{target}/F$. Clearance relates to half-life by $t_{1/2} = 0.693 V_d/CL$. Maintenance rate $= CL \cdot C_{ss}/F$. Steady state needs 4 to 5 half-lives. Phenytoin, ethanol and high-dose aspirin show zero-order kinetics.

### Weeks 3 and 4: pharmacodynamics and metabolism
Occupancy $= C/(C + K_d)$; potency is $EC_{50}$, efficacy is $E_{max}$; competitive antagonists shift the curve right, non-competitive ones lower $E_{max}$; therapeutic index $= TD_{50}/ED_{50}$. Inducers lower and inhibitors raise substrate levels; prodrugs behave in reverse. $C_{ss} \propto 1/CL$.

### Weeks 5 and 6: autonomic drugs
Muscarinic agonists slow the heart and constrict pupils; antagonists do the opposite. Organophosphates: atropine plus pralidoxime. $\alpha_1$ agonists raise BP and cause reflex bradycardia; $\beta_1$ stimulation raises heart rate; $\beta$-blockers should follow $\alpha$ blockade in pheochromocytoma. Infusion rate (mL/h) $=$ mcg/min $\times 60$ / (mcg/mL).

### Weeks 7 and 8: antimicrobials and analgesics
MIC, time-dependent versus concentration-dependent killing, class mechanisms and toxicities (aminoglycosides need oxygen; fluoroquinolones: tendons and QT). NSAIDs inhibit COX; aspirin is irreversible; NSAIDs plus ACE inhibitor plus diuretic injure the kidney. Acetaminophen: NAPQI and N-acetylcysteine. Opioids: miosis and constipation without tolerance; MME conversion.

### Weeks 9 and 10: toxicology and safety
Antidotes: naloxone, atropine plus pralidoxime, fomepizole, N-acetylcysteine, bicarbonate for tricyclics, hydroxocobalamin, digoxin Fab, protamine. Anion gap and osmolar gap. Gell and Coombs types, HLA screening, NNH $= 1/\text{ARI}$, safe-writing rules.

### Weeks 11 and 12: special populations and trials
ACE inhibitors and ARBs, warfarin, valproate and retinoids are teratogenic. Neonates: immature glucuronidation. Older adults: Beers criteria. Cockcroft–Gault and $Q = 1 - f_e(1 - \text{CrCl}/120)$; do not reduce loading doses. Phases I to IV, ITT, type I and II errors, ARR, RRR, NNT.

### Self-check
- Can you derive loading and maintenance doses from $V_d$, $CL$ and $F$?
- Can you predict an interaction's direction and timing?
- Can you predict heart rate and pupil size for any autonomic drug?
- Can you name an antidote for each toxidrome?
- Can you adjust a dose for kidney function?

### Common errors
Forgetting $F$ in oral doses; thinking dose changes the time to steady state; confusing potency with efficacy; predicting the wrong direction for prodrug interactions; mixing mg and mcg; reducing a loading dose for kidney disease.
@exam tuple
prompt: An 80 kg adult is to receive an oral drug with $V_d = 0.5$ L/kg, clearance 4 L/h, oral bioavailability 0.8 and a target average steady-state concentration of 5 mg/L. Give (oral loading dose in mg, oral dose in mg to give every 12 hours).
answer: (250, 300)
check: (0.5 * 80 * 5 / 0.8, 4 * 5 / 0.8 * 12)
hint: Loading dose $= V_d C/F$, where $V_d = 0.5 \times 80$ L.
hint: Maintenance rate $= CL \cdot C_{ss}/F$ in mg/h; multiply by 12.
solution: $V_d = 40$ L; loading $= 40 \times 5/0.8 = 250$ mg. Rate $= 4 \times 5/0.8 = 25$ mg/h; per 12 h $= 300$ mg.
@exam num
prompt: A 72-year-old man weighs 75 kg and has a serum creatinine of 1.8 mg/dL. A renally cleared drug has $f_e = 0.7$ and a usual maintenance dose of 600 mg. Estimate CrCl by Cockcroft–Gault, compute $Q = 1 - f_e(1 - \text{CrCl}/120)$ and give the adjusted maintenance dose in mg to one decimal.
answer: 317.7
check: 600 * (1 - 0.7 * (1 - ((140 - 72) * 75 / (72 * 1.8)) / 120))
unit: mg
tol: 1%
hint: First CrCl $= (140 - 72)(75)/(72 \times 1.8)$.
hint: Then $Q = 1 - 0.7(1 - \text{CrCl}/120)$ and multiply by 600.
solution: CrCl $= 5100/129.6 = 39.4$ mL/min; $Q = 1 - 0.7(1 - 0.328) \approx 0.5295$; dose $= 0.5295 \times 600 \approx 317.7$ mg.

prompt: A 48-year-old man with epilepsy is stable on carbamazepine. He is given clarithromycin for pneumonia and 4 days later develops diplopia, ataxia and drowsiness. What best explains these symptoms?
choice: Clarithromycin induces CYP3A4 and lowers the carbamazepine level, causing breakthrough seizures.
choice*: Clarithromycin inhibits CYP3A4, raising the carbamazepine level and causing toxicity.
choice: Clarithromycin displaces carbamazepine from plasma proteins permanently.
choice: Carbamazepine induces its own metabolism, causing a high level of its active metabolite.
choice: The infection has caused a type I hypersensitivity reaction.
hint: Which enzyme metabolizes carbamazepine, and what does clarithromycin do to it?
solution: Clarithromycin is a CYP3A4 inhibitor, so carbamazepine clearance falls and its level rises, producing diplopia, ataxia and sedation; an alternative such as azithromycin avoids this interaction.
