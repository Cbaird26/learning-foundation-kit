@article
**Elimination** removes drug from the body by metabolism (mostly liver) and excretion (mostly kidney, also bile, lungs and breast milk). Everything in this lesson follows from one idea: at a constant rate of administration, the drug level rises until the rate in equals the rate out.

### Clearance

**Clearance** ($CL$) is the volume of plasma completely cleared of drug per unit time (L/h). Rate of elimination $= CL \times C_p$. Clearances of organs add: $CL_{\text{total}} = CL_{\text{renal}} + CL_{\text{hepatic}} + \dots$. For a drug with **first-order** kinetics, a constant *fraction* is eliminated per unit time, and clearance is constant regardless of concentration.

### Half-life and the elimination rate constant

The elimination rate constant is $k = CL/V_d$, and the **half-life** is the time for the plasma concentration to fall by half:

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693\, V_d}{CL} = \frac{\ln 2}{k}.$$

Half-life depends on both $V_d$ and $CL$. A large $V_d$ prolongs half-life (the drug hides in tissue), and a lower clearance prolongs half-life too. After $n$ half-lives, the fraction remaining is $(1/2)^n$: 50%, 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%, 3.1%. A drug is considered essentially eliminated after about 4 to 5 half-lives.

### Steady state

With repeated or continuous dosing, the concentration accumulates until input equals output. This steady state is reached after about **4 to 5 half-lives** (about 94 to 97%), regardless of dose size or interval. The time to steady state depends **only** on the half-life. Changing the dose changes the steady-state concentration in proportion but not the time needed to reach it. The same rule applies when clearance changes: after a dose adjustment or an interaction, the new steady state again takes about 4 to 5 new half-lives.

At steady state, the dosing rate equals the elimination rate:

$$\text{maintenance dosing rate} = \frac{CL \times C_{ss}}{F}.$$

The **dose per interval** is the rate multiplied by the dosing interval $\tau$. Shortening the interval smooths the peaks and troughs without changing the average concentration. A **loading dose** $= V_d \times C_{\text{target}}/F$ reaches the target at once, whereas without it the level climbs to the target only over about 4 to 5 half-lives; the maintenance dose then keeps the level there.

### Zero-order (saturable) elimination

When the enzymes that eliminate a drug are saturated, a constant *amount* rather than a constant fraction is removed per hour. This is **zero-order** kinetics. Clearance falls as concentration rises, so half-life lengthens with dose. Classic examples are **ethanol** at ordinary intoxicating levels, **phenytoin** (Michaelis–Menten kinetics within the therapeutic range) and high-dose **aspirin**. The clinical consequence is non-linear dosing: a small dose increase of phenytoin can produce a disproportionate rise in the level and toxicity (nystagmus, ataxia).

### Extraction and clearance

Hepatic clearance of high-extraction drugs (propranolol, lidocaine, morphine) depends on liver blood flow; clearance of low-extraction drugs (warfarin, diazepam, theophylline) depends on enzyme activity and, for binding, the free fraction. Renal clearance combines filtration, secretion and reabsorption: $CL_{\text{renal}} = \text{filtration} + \text{secretion} - \text{reabsorption}$.

### Putting it together

To design a regimen: choose a $C_{ss}$ target from the therapeutic range; compute the loading dose from $V_d$; compute the maintenance rate from $CL$; and predict steady state from $t_{1/2}$. Doses and values in this course are for study and must be checked against current references before any clinical use.

@example
title: Half-life, maintenance dose and time to steady state
problem: A drug has $V_d = 70$ L and $CL = 7$ L/h, with $F = 1$ (IV infusion). The target steady-state concentration is 10 mg/L. Find the half-life, the infusion rate, and the time to steady state.
step: Half-life: $t_{1/2} = 0.693 \times 70 / 7 = 6.93$ h, about 7 h.
step: Infusion rate $= CL \times C_{ss}/F = 7 \times 10 = 70$ mg/h.
step: Steady state is reached after 4 to 5 half-lives: $5 \times 6.93 \approx 35$ h (about 1.5 days).
step: A loading dose would be $V_d \times C_{ss} = 70 \times 10 = 700$ mg, which would put the patient at the target immediately while the 70 mg/h infusion keeps the level there.
@mistakes
- Using clearance alone to estimate half-life; $V_d$ matters equally.
- Thinking a larger dose reaches steady state sooner; only the half-life sets the time.
- Forgetting $F$ in the maintenance dose for an oral drug.
- Applying first-order half-life rules to phenytoin, ethanol or high-dose aspirin, which show saturable kinetics.
