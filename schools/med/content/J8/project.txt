@project
title: Three sources, one argument: the industrial era
overview: You will analyze three short primary sources about working life in a mill town in the 1890s and write a short historical argument that uses them as evidence. The sources below were written for this course in the style of documents from the period, so you practice the same skills you would use on real documents.
goal: Analyze each source by asking who made it, when, why, and for whom.
goal: Write a claim that can be supported with evidence from at least two of the three sources.
goal: Acknowledge a limit or disagreement among the sources.
step: Read the three sources below twice. On the first read, say what each source is about in one sentence.
step: For each source, write down the author, date, audience, purpose and point of view. Note what each source can tell you and what it cannot.
step: Compare the sources. Where do they agree? Where do they disagree? Which statements could be checked against other evidence?
step: Write a one-sentence claim that answers this question: "How did industrial work shape life in the mill town in the 1890s?"
step: Draft a 3-paragraph argument (about 250 to 350 words): a paragraph that states your claim, a paragraph that uses two quotations as evidence and explains each one, and a paragraph that addresses a disagreement or limit.
step: Revise. Make sure every quotation is copied exactly, every claim is supported, and you did not present one source as the whole truth.
material: Source 1 (invented newspaper letter from a mill owner, 1893): "The mill gives steady work to four hundred families. Wages are paid every Saturday, and the company store sells goods at fair prices. Those who complain of long hours forget that idle hands earn nothing."
material: Source 2 (invented letter from a mill worker to a sister, 1893): "We rise before dawn and the whistle calls us to the looms at six. We are paid by the week, but the store charges more than the shops in town, and by Saturday much of my pay is already owed. My youngest brother works beside me; he is eleven."
material: Source 3 (invented report by a town doctor, 1894): "Of the sixty mill children I examined this winter, many were thin and tired, and several had injuries from the machines. The families told me they need the children's wages to pay rent. The mill owners told me the work is light."
deliverable: A source-analysis table with one row per source (author, date, audience, purpose, point of view, one limit).
deliverable: A 3-paragraph historical argument that quotes at least two of the sources exactly.
deliverable: A two-sentence note naming one other kind of evidence you would look for next.
rubric: Each source is correctly analyzed for author, date, audience, purpose and point of view.
rubric: The claim is clear and can be argued from the evidence.
rubric: At least two quotations are exact and each is explained, not just inserted.
rubric: The argument notices a disagreement or limit instead of treating one source as complete.
rubric: Writing is organized and in the learner's own words, with neutral, fair descriptions of the people involved.
safety: This project uses only the invented sources written here. If you choose to add a real source, use one published before 1929 and copy it exactly.
tutor: How can I tell the difference between what a source says and what I infer from it?
tutor: Can you help me check whether my claim is supported by both sources I quoted?
tutor: What other evidence would a historian look for to test what the mill owner says?

### Week by week

1. **Geography skills.** Latitude measures north-south from the equator; longitude measures east-west from the prime meridian. Scale converts map distance to real distance. Earth turns 15° per hour. Flat maps distort.
2. **Early humans and the first civilizations.** Farming created surplus, specialization and cities. River valleys offered silt, water and transport.
3. **Egypt, India and China.** Pharaoh and ma'at; Indus cities and the undeciphered script; Qin and Han, Mandate of Heaven, paper and silk.
4. **Greece and Rome.** Athenian direct democracy (limited citizenship), Sparta, Roman Republic (consuls, Senate, tribunes), empire in 27 BCE, Western fall in 476 CE.
5. **World religions and empires.** Attribute beliefs ("Buddhists teach..."). Buddhism's Four Noble Truths, Christianity's spread through the Roman Empire, Islam's Five Pillars.
6. **The medieval world.** Feudalism, Byzantine and Islamic learning, Mali, Mongols, the Crusades from several perspectives, the Black Death.
7. **Renaissance, Reformation, exploration.** Humanism, printing, the Ninety-five Theses, Council of Trent, voyages and their effects.
8. **Colonial America.** Jamestown, Plymouth, three regions, slavery, and the Columbian Exchange with its disease effects.
9. **Revolution and Constitution.** Causes, Declaration of Independence, Articles of Confederation, the Great Compromise, Three-Fifths Compromise, slave trade compromise, Bill of Rights.
10. **Early republic and expansion.** Parties, judicial review, Louisiana Purchase, removal, Manifest Destiny, Mexican-American War, Missouri Compromise.
11. **Civil War and Reconstruction.** Slavery as central cause, Emancipation Proclamation, 13th, 14th and 15th Amendments, Jim Crow after 1877.
12. **Industrialization and immigration.** Railroads, trusts, unions, Ellis Island, Angel Island, Chinese Exclusion Act.

### Self-check

- I can give latitude and longitude with N/S and E/W and convert a map scale.
- I can count years across BCE and CE dates.
- I can compare two governments using the same questions for both.
- I can describe a religion in neutral language by attributing beliefs.
- I can explain events from more than one perspective.
- I can tell primary from secondary sources and use a quotation as evidence.

### Common errors

- Mixing up latitude and longitude, or subtracting BCE years the wrong way.
- Writing beliefs as flat facts instead of attributing them.
- Saying "discovered" for lands where millions already lived.
- Counting the Three-Fifths Compromise as giving enslaved people a vote.
- Using a quotation without explaining how it supports the claim.

prompt: A map has the scale 1 cm = 25 km. A trade route between two cities in the Roman Empire is 7.2 cm long on the map. A traveler walks 20 km each day. How many days does the trip take?
answer: 9
check: 7.2 * 25 / 20
hint: First convert the map distance to kilometers.
hint: Then divide the total distance by the distance walked per day.
solution: $7.2 \times 25 = 180$ km, and $180 \div 20 = 9$ days.

@exam self
prompt: Choose two of these three: Athens, the Roman Republic, or the United States under the Constitution. In 6 to 8 sentences, compare how each chose its leaders or made laws, who was left out, and one compromise or limit on power. Use neutral language.
solution: Athens used direct democracy: free adult men of Athenian birth voted in an assembly, while women, enslaved people and foreigners were excluded. The United States under the Constitution uses elected representatives in a two-house Congress created by the Great Compromise, with federalism, separation of powers and checks and balances limiting power. In 1787 enslaved people, most women and Native Americans were excluded from political participation, and the Three-Fifths Compromise counted part of the enslaved population for representation without giving them a vote. Both systems limited political rights to a part of the population, though in the United States later amendments expanded voting rights. Athens relied on direct voting, while the United States used representatives and written constitutional limits.
hint: Use the same three questions for both: who decides, who is excluded, what limits power.
rubric: Compares both systems using the same questions rather than describing them separately.
rubric: Correctly identifies who was excluded in each system.
rubric: Names at least one compromise or limit on power with accurate details.
rubric: Uses neutral, factual language.
