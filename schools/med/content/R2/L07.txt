@article
Trauma care follows one idea: **treat what kills first**. The structured approach of Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) is a primary survey that looks for and treats life threats in order of lethality, then a secondary survey for everything else.

### Primary survey

Modern ATLS places control of **catastrophic external hemorrhage** (direct pressure, tourniquet) first when it is present, then proceeds through **ABCDE**:

- **A, Airway with cervical spine protection.** Talking patients have an airway. Use a jaw thrust, suction, and a definitive airway (rapid sequence intubation) if the patient cannot protect it or has a **GCS of 8 or less**. If intubation fails and oxygenation is impossible, perform a surgical cricothyroidotomy.
- **B, Breathing.** Inspect, palpate and auscultate the chest. Identify and treat immediately life-threatening chest injuries.
- **C, Circulation with hemorrhage control.** Pulses, perfusion, two large-bore IVs, FAST (focused assessment with sonography for trauma), and chest and pelvic radiographs. Assume hypotension is hemorrhage until proven otherwise.
- **D, Disability.** GCS, pupils, glucose, gross motor and sensory function.
- **E, Exposure and environment.** Undress the patient completely, then warm them. The "lethal triad" of **hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy** accelerates death.

### Chest life threats

- **Tension pneumothorax:** hypotension, tracheal deviation (late), distended neck veins, absent breath sounds on one side. It is a **clinical diagnosis**: decompress immediately with a needle or finger thoracostomy (fifth intercostal space, anterior axillary line, or second space midclavicular) followed by chest tube.
- **Open pneumothorax:** a "sucking" chest wound. Cover with a dressing taped on three sides, then place a chest tube.
- **Massive hemothorax:** dullness to percussion, shock. Place a chest tube and resuscitate. Immediate output above about **1,500 mL** or ongoing drainage above 200 mL/h over 2 to 4 hours calls for thoracotomy.
- **Cardiac tamponade:** Beck triad (hypotension, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds), usually from penetrating injury. FAST shows pericardial fluid. Definitive treatment is surgical.
- **Flail chest:** three or more adjacent ribs each fractured in two places, with paradoxical movement; treat with analgesia and ventilatory support if needed.

### Hemorrhagic shock

For a 70-kg adult (blood volume about 70 mL/kg, roughly 5 L):

| Class | Blood loss | Findings |
|---|---|---|
| I | under 15% | normal vitals |
| II | 15 to 30% | tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure |
| III | 30 to 40% | hypotension, tachycardia, confusion |
| IV | above 40% | profound shock, obtunded |

Give blood products early and in a balanced ratio (massive transfusion protocol), limit crystalloid to about 1 liter initially, and give **tranexamic acid within 3 hours** of injury. Accept a low blood pressure (**permissive hypotension**) until bleeding is controlled, except in brain injury, where perfusion pressure is protected. Stop the bleeding: tourniquet, pelvic binder, chest tube, or operation or angioembolization. Do not delay the operating room for imaging in an unstable patient.

### Disability

The **Glasgow Coma Scale** sums eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6); the total ranges from 3 to 15. Motor 6 obeys, 5 localizes, 4 withdraws, 3 flexion (decorticate), 2 extension (decerebrate), 1 none.

### Abdomen and pelvis

A **hypotensive blunt trauma patient with positive FAST** goes to laparotomy. Stable patients undergo CT. Penetrating wounds with hypotension, peritonitis or evisceration need laparotomy. Splenic and liver injuries in stable patients are often managed nonoperatively. A suspected **pelvic fracture** with hypotension is managed with a **pelvic binder**, transfusion and angioembolization or preperitoneal packing. Blood at the urethral meatus or a high-riding prostate means no urinary catheter until a retrograde urethrogram is done.

### Secondary survey

Once the primary survey is complete and the patient is resuscitated, perform a head-to-toe examination (including log roll and back, perineum and rectum), take an **AMPLE history** (Allergies, Medications, Past illness, Last meal, Events) and obtain targeted imaging. Reassess repeatedly. A tertiary survey within 24 hours finds missed injuries.

@example
title: Tension pneumothorax in the primary survey
problem: A 30-year-old man is stabbed in the left chest. He is anxious and dyspneic with a heart rate of 132/min, blood pressure 78/50 mmHg and SpO2 85 percent. The left chest is hyperresonant with absent breath sounds, and his neck veins are distended. What do you do?
step: Airway is patent because he is speaking. Breathing is abnormal: unilateral absent breath sounds and hyperresonance.
step: Hypotension with distended neck veins and absent breath sounds on one side is a tension pneumothorax, a clinical diagnosis. Do not wait for a chest radiograph.
step: Perform immediate needle or finger decompression in the fifth intercostal space at the anterior axillary line.
step: Follow with a chest tube and give oxygen. Continue with circulation, FAST and the rest of the survey.

@mistakes
- Obtaining a chest radiograph before decompressing a clinically obvious tension pneumothorax.
- Giving large volumes of crystalloid instead of blood products to a patient in hemorrhagic shock.
- Sending an unstable patient to CT instead of the operating room.
- Placing a urinary catheter before considering urethral injury, or forgetting to protect the cervical spine.
