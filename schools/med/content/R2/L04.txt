@article
### Appendicitis

**Mechanism.** Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen by a fecalith or lymphoid hyperplasia raises intraluminal pressure. Venous outflow is blocked, bacteria multiply, the wall becomes ischemic, and without treatment the appendix perforates, often within 24 to 72 hours.

**Presentation.** Dull periumbilical (visceral) pain, anorexia, then nausea. Within hours the pain migrates to the right lower quadrant (somatic pain from the parietal peritoneum), with low-grade fever. Exam: tenderness at **McBurney point**; **Rovsing sign** (palpation of the left lower quadrant causes right-sided pain); **psoas sign** (pain on hip extension, retrocecal appendix); **obturator sign** (pain on internal rotation of the flexed hip, pelvic appendix). Laboratory data show leukocytosis with neutrophilia; the urinalysis may show mild pyuria.

**Alvarado score** (MANTRELS), out of 10: **M**igration of pain (1), **A**norexia (1), **N**ausea or vomiting (1), **T**enderness in the right lower quadrant (2), **R**ebound (1), **E**levated temperature at least 37.3 C (1), **L**eukocytosis above 10,000 (2), and **S**hift to the left, neutrophils above 75 percent (1). Low scores make appendicitis unlikely; high scores (7 or more) favor surgery. Scores guide imaging; they do not replace judgment.

**Imaging.** CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast is the usual test in adults. In children and pregnant women, start with **ultrasound** (noncompressible tubular structure over 6 mm), then MRI if the ultrasound is nondiagnostic. In pregnancy the appendix is displaced upward, so pain may be higher on the right.

**Treatment.** IV fluids, antibiotics covering enteric gram-negatives and anaerobes (for example cefazolin plus metronidazole), and **laparoscopic appendectomy**. Postoperative antibiotics are stopped within about 24 hours for uncomplicated disease and given for several days after perforation. For selected patients with uncomplicated appendicitis, a course of antibiotics alone is an accepted alternative, though roughly a quarter to a third need appendectomy within a few months and the failure rate is higher with an appendicolith. A well-defined **abscess** is drained percutaneously with antibiotics; interval appendectomy is then individualized. Other causes of right lower quadrant pain include Meckel diverticulitis, Crohn ileitis, mesenteric adenitis, ovarian torsion and ectopic pregnancy.

### Bowel obstruction

**Small bowel obstruction (SBO).** The most common cause in adults in developed countries is **postoperative adhesions**, followed by hernias and then malignancy and Crohn disease. Symptoms: colicky pain, vomiting (earlier and bilious in proximal obstruction), abdominal distension and obstipation. Examine the abdominal scars and all hernia orifices. Plain films show dilated loops above 3 cm with air-fluid levels, but **CT** shows the transition point, a **closed loop** (a segment obstructed at both ends), and signs of ischemia such as bowel-wall thickening, mesenteric edema and lack of mucosal enhancement.

**Strangulation** means compromised blood supply. Suspect it with constant pain, fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis, rising lactate or peritonitis.

**Initial management** is the same for all: NPO, **nasogastric decompression**, IV isotonic fluids with electrolyte replacement, and monitoring of urine output. A partial SBO without signs of strangulation can be observed for 24 to 72 hours. Oral water-soluble contrast (diatrizoate) is both diagnostic and therapeutic: if it reaches the colon in 24 hours, resolution without surgery is likely. **Operate** for peritonitis, strangulation, closed-loop obstruction, complete obstruction that does not resolve, or a hernia that cannot be reduced.

**Large bowel obstruction (LBO).** The commonest causes are **colon cancer**, **volvulus** and diverticular stricture. A cecal diameter above about 12 cm raises the risk of perforation. **Sigmoid volvulus** occurs in older, often institutionalized, constipated patients; the abdominal film shows a "coffee bean" loop and a contrast enema shows a "bird's beak." Treat with endoscopic detorsion and a rectal tube if the bowel is viable, and plan elective resection because recurrence is common; peritonitis or ischemia needs emergency surgery. **Cecal volvulus** generally needs surgery. **Ileus** and **Ogilvie syndrome** (colonic pseudo-obstruction) are functional; they are managed with supportive care and correction of electrolytes, and neostigmine for Ogilvie when there is no contraindication. **Gallstone ileus** is an SBO from a stone eroded into the bowel, with pneumobilia on imaging.

@example
title: Scoring a patient with possible appendicitis
problem: A 22-year-old man has 18 hours of periumbilical pain that moved to the right lower quadrant, anorexia and vomiting. He is tender in the right lower quadrant with rebound. Temperature is 37.9 C, WBC is 15,000/microliter with 85 percent neutrophils. What is his Alvarado score and what is the next step?
step: Migration (1), anorexia (1), nausea or vomiting (1).
step: Right lower quadrant tenderness (2) and rebound (1).
step: Temperature of at least 37.3 C (1), leukocytosis (2), left shift (1).
step: The total is 10 out of 10, so appendicitis is very likely. Start IV fluids and antibiotics, make him NPO, obtain imaging if the diagnosis is uncertain, and arrange laparoscopic appendectomy.

@mistakes
- Imaging a pregnant patient with CT first instead of ultrasound followed by MRI.
- Giving a trial of observation to an SBO patient who has peritonitis, fever with tachycardia, or a closed loop.
- Forgetting to examine hernia orifices in a patient with obstruction.
- Calling Ogilvie syndrome an obstruction that requires surgery; it is a functional dilation managed supportively.
