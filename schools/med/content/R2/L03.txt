@article
The **acute abdomen** is sudden, severe abdominal pain that may need an operation. Your first job is not to name the diagnosis; it is to decide whether this patient needs an operation now, soon or not at all, and to resuscitate while you decide.

### Types of pain

**Visceral pain** comes from stretch or ischemia of hollow organs. It is dull, crampy and poorly localized, felt in the midline according to embryologic origin: epigastric for the foregut (stomach, duodenum, liver, pancreas), periumbilical for the midgut (small bowel, appendix, right colon) and suprapubic for the hindgut. **Somatic pain** arises when inflamed tissue irritates the parietal peritoneum, which is supplied by spinal nerves. It is sharp, constant and exactly localized, and it is worse with movement or coughing. **Referred pain** is felt at a distant site that shares spinal segments: gallbladder pain to the right scapula, diaphragmatic irritation to the shoulder, pancreatitis to the back, ureteral stones to the groin.

This explains the classic appendicitis story: periumbilical visceral pain moves to the right lower quadrant when the inflamed appendix touches the parietal peritoneum.

### Peritonitis

**Peritonitis** is inflammation of the peritoneum. Look for rigidity ("board-like" abdomen), **involuntary guarding**, **rebound tenderness**, and pain with percussion or with the jolt of a bump or heel strike. The patient lies still. These signs generally mean an operation is likely: perforated viscus, ischemic or gangrenous bowel, or leaking pus or bile. Elderly, immunosuppressed and steroid-treated patients can have a catastrophe with a soft abdomen.

### Location-based differential

| Region | Common causes |
|---|---|
| Right upper quadrant | biliary colic, cholecystitis, cholangitis, hepatitis, duodenal ulcer, pneumonia |
| Epigastric | peptic ulcer or perforation, pancreatitis, gastritis, myocardial infarction, aortic aneurysm |
| Left upper quadrant | splenic injury or infarct, gastric ulcer, pancreatitis, pyelonephritis |
| Periumbilical | early appendicitis, small bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischemia, gastroenteritis |
| Right lower quadrant | appendicitis, Crohn disease, ovarian torsion or cyst, ectopic pregnancy, ureteral stone, hernia |
| Left lower quadrant | diverticulitis, ovarian pathology, ectopic pregnancy, ureteral stone, sigmoid volvulus |
| Diffuse | peritonitis from perforation, bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischemia, ruptured aneurysm, diabetic ketoacidosis |

**Mesenteric ischemia** (often an embolus from atrial fibrillation to the superior mesenteric artery) presents with **pain out of proportion to the exam**. A ruptured **abdominal aortic aneurysm** classically gives abdominal or back pain, hypotension and a pulsatile mass. In any woman of childbearing age, get a **pregnancy test**: ectopic pregnancy is a surgical emergency.

### Initial workup

Take vital signs and consider sepsis criteria (SIRS: temperature above 38 or below 36 C, heart rate above 90, respiratory rate above 20, WBC above 12,000 or below 4,000/microliter; **qSOFA**: respiratory rate 22 or higher, altered mentation, systolic BP 100 or lower). Order CBC, basic metabolic panel, liver tests, lipase, **lactate**, urinalysis, **hCG** and type and screen. An ECG excludes inferior MI. Start IV access, isotonic fluids, antiemetics and analgesia; **giving opioids does not mask the diagnosis and is appropriate**. Start broad-spectrum antibiotics when peritonitis or sepsis is suspected.

**CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast** is the workhorse for undifferentiated pain in adults. **Ultrasound** is first for right upper quadrant pain, for pelvic pain, and in children and pregnant patients (MRI if ultrasound is inconclusive). An upright chest radiograph shows **free air under the diaphragm** in perforated viscus, but a negative film does not exclude it. An unstable patient with peritonitis or a known source does not go to the scanner; they go to the operating room.

### Indications for urgent operation

Hemodynamic instability from intra-abdominal hemorrhage, generalized peritonitis, free perforation, ischemic or strangulated bowel, and abscess or necrosis not amenable to drainage.

### Mimics

Lower lobe pneumonia, inferior myocardial infarction, diabetic ketoacidosis, sickle cell crisis, herpes zoster and testicular torsion can all present as abdominal pain.

@example
title: A patient with peritonitis
problem: A 68-year-old man on ibuprofen for arthritis develops sudden severe epigastric pain that becomes diffuse over two hours. He lies motionless. Temperature is 38.2 C, heart rate 118/min, respiratory rate 24/min and blood pressure 104/62 mmHg. His abdomen is rigid with rebound tenderness. What is the likely diagnosis and next step?
step: Sudden severe epigastric pain with a rigid abdomen and NSAID use points to a perforated peptic ulcer with chemical peritonitis.
step: He meets SIRS (fever, tachycardia, tachypnea) and is at risk of septic shock. Begin IV fluids, antiemetics, analgesia and broad-spectrum antibiotics.
step: An upright chest film may show free subdiaphragmatic air; a CT confirms the site if the patient is stable.
step: Generalized peritonitis from perforation requires prompt surgical repair (omental patch). Notify the surgical team and anesthesia, and send a type and screen.

@mistakes
- Withholding analgesia until the surgeon has examined the patient.
- Relying on a normal chest radiograph to exclude free air.
- Forgetting a pregnancy test in a woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain.
- Expecting a rigid abdomen in elderly or immunosuppressed patients; their findings are blunted.
