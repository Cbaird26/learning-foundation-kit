@article
The goal of a preoperative evaluation is not to "clear" a patient. It is to estimate the risk of the planned operation for this particular patient, to find problems that can be fixed or optimized, and to make sure the patient understands the plan and has agreed to it (informed consent). Two questions drive everything: **how risky is the operation** and **how much physiologic reserve does the patient have**.

### Start with the history

Ask about cardiac, pulmonary, renal, hepatic, endocrine and bleeding problems; prior anesthetic reactions (personal or family history of malignant hyperthermia); alcohol, tobacco and drug use; allergies; and every medication, including anticoagulants, supplements and the newer diabetes drugs. Examine the airway, heart, lungs and volume status. Order tests selectively: a pregnancy test in anyone who could be pregnant, a type and screen when significant blood loss is possible, and an ECG, creatinine or hemoglobin only when the history or the surgery's risk justifies it. Routine panels in healthy patients undergoing low-risk surgery do not improve outcomes.

### ASA physical status

The American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) class is a quick global score of comorbidity: **I** healthy; **II** mild systemic disease without functional limitation (well-controlled diabetes or hypertension, current smoker, obesity with BMI 30 to 40); **III** severe systemic disease (poorly controlled diabetes or hypertension, COPD, BMI at least 40, dialysis, a stent older than 3 months); **IV** severe disease that is a constant threat to life (recent myocardial infarction, sepsis, ongoing ischemia); **V** moribund, not expected to survive without the operation; **VI** brain-dead organ donor. An **E** is added for an emergency.

### Cardiac risk: RCRI and functional capacity

The **Revised Cardiac Risk Index** gives one point for each of six predictors of major cardiac events after noncardiac surgery:

- high-risk surgery (intraperitoneal, intrathoracic or suprainguinal vascular);
- ischemic heart disease;
- history of heart failure;
- history of cerebrovascular disease (stroke or TIA);
- diabetes treated with insulin;
- preoperative creatinine above 2.0 mg/dL (177 micromol/L).

Risk rises with the score (0, 1, 2, and 3 or more points). A score of 2 or more is generally considered elevated risk, which prompts a look at functional capacity.

**Functional capacity** is measured in metabolic equivalents (**METs**); 1 MET is resting oxygen use. Walking slowly on level ground is about 2 METs. **Climbing a flight of stairs or walking up a hill is about 4 METs**, the threshold that matters. A patient who can reach 4 METs without symptoms can generally proceed to surgery without further testing, even with elevated RCRI. If capacity is below 4 METs or unknown, the 2024 ACC/AHA approach is to test only when the result would change management (for example, a stress test that might lead to revascularization), and biomarkers such as BNP or troponin may refine risk.

Before an elective operation, active cardiac conditions are addressed first: acute coronary syndrome, decompensated heart failure, significant arrhythmia and severe symptomatic aortic stenosis all justify delaying elective surgery.

### Timing after a coronary stent

Elective noncardiac surgery should be delayed at least 6 months after a drug-eluting stent (ideally 12 months after an acute coronary syndrome), and **aspirin is continued** through the operation when possible, because stopping antiplatelet therapy early risks stent thrombosis. If surgery cannot wait, decisions are made with cardiology.

### Medications

- **Continue:** beta-blockers taken chronically (never start a high dose on the day of surgery), statins, inhaled bronchodilators, most antihypertensives other than ACE inhibitors and ARBs, and antiepileptics.
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs:** commonly held 24 hours before surgery to reduce intraoperative hypotension; practice varies.
- **SGLT2 inhibitors:** hold 3 days beforehand (4 for ertugliflozin) because of the risk of euglycemic ketoacidosis.
- **Warfarin:** hold about 5 days; bridging is reserved for the highest-risk patients, such as a mechanical mitral valve.
- **DOACs:** hold 1 to 3 days depending on the drug, renal function and bleeding risk.
- **Insulin:** give a reduced basal dose; hold oral hypoglycemics the morning of surgery.
- **Chronic glucocorticoids:** patients taking prednisone-equivalent 20 mg or more daily for over 3 weeks may need stress-dose steroids.
- **GLP-1 receptor agonists:** these slow gastric emptying and may raise aspiration risk. Older advice was to hold them (a week before for weekly agents); the 2024 multisociety guidance changed this and advises continuing them in most patients, with attention to GI symptoms (nausea, vomiting, bloating, abdominal pain), which, if present, may prompt a delay, a liquid diet the day before or a full-stomach airway plan. Check local protocol, as practice is still evolving.

### Fasting

For elective anesthesia, fast **2 hours** from clear liquids, **6 hours** from a light meal, and **8 hours** from a fatty or fried meal.

### Other risk domains

Pulmonary risk rises with smoking, COPD, OSA (screen with STOP-BANG), upper abdominal or thoracic surgery and low albumin. Smoking cessation helps even a few weeks before surgery. Frailty, malnutrition and anemia predict poor outcomes. Assess venous thromboembolism risk (for example, the Caprini score) and plan prophylaxis.

@example
title: Calculating RCRI and choosing a plan
problem: A 72-year-old man with a prior myocardial infarction, insulin-treated type 2 diabetes and a creatinine of 1.2 mg/dL is scheduled for an elective open sigmoid colectomy. He becomes short of breath climbing one flight of stairs. What is his RCRI and what should happen next?
step: Count the predictors. Open colectomy is intraperitoneal surgery (high-risk), 1 point. Prior myocardial infarction is ischemic heart disease, 1 point. Insulin-treated diabetes, 1 point. Creatinine 1.2 mg/dL is not above 2.0, 0 points. Heart failure and cerebrovascular disease are absent.
step: RCRI is 3, an elevated risk of major cardiac events.
step: Functional capacity is below 4 METs because stairs provoke dyspnea. Capacity below 4 METs with elevated risk means testing is reasonable if the result would change management.
step: Next steps: assess for active cardiac conditions, consider an ECG and biomarkers, and obtain a stress test or cardiology input only if revascularization would be considered. Continue his beta-blocker and statin, continue aspirin, and reduce his basal insulin dose.

@mistakes
- Treating creatinine of 2.0 mg/dL or less as a point; the RCRI threshold is above 2.0 mg/dL, and diabetes counts only when treated with insulin.
- Stopping aspirin in a patient with a recent drug-eluting stent, or stopping chronic beta-blockers abruptly.
- Ordering routine stress tests or panels for every patient instead of asking whether the result would change management.
- Confusing the ASA class (a comorbidity score) with operative risk; the type of surgery matters too.

@quiz mc
prompt: A 58-year-old man has taken prednisone 20 mg daily for 6 months for polymyalgia rheumatica. He is scheduled for elective colectomy. Which is the best perioperative steroid plan?
choice: Stop prednisone 1 week before surgery to improve wound healing.
