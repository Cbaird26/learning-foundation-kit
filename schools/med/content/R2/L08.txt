@article
### Burn assessment

Stop the burning process: remove clothing and jewelry, and cool small burns with cool running water for about 20 minutes (not ice, which causes further injury). Then run the **ABCDE** survey, because burn patients are often trauma patients too. **Airway** comes first.

**Inhalation injury** is suggested by closed-space fire, facial burns, singed nasal hairs, soot in the mouth or sputum, hoarseness, stridor or wheeze. Edema progresses over hours, so intubate early when it is likely. **Carbon monoxide poisoning** causes headache, confusion and coma; pulse oximetry reads falsely normal because it cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, so measure co-oximetry. Treat with **100 percent oxygen** (it shortens the half-life from about 5 hours on room air to under 90 minutes); consider hyperbaric oxygen for severe cases. Suspect **cyanide** with lactic acidosis and shock in a smoke inhalation victim; give hydroxocobalamin.

### Depth

| Depth | Appearance | Pain | Healing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial (first degree) | red, dry, no blisters | painful | days, no scar |
| Superficial partial thickness | blisters, moist, pink, blanches | very painful | 2 to 3 weeks |
| Deep partial thickness | mottled red or white, may not blanch | decreased | weeks, scars, may need grafting |
| Full thickness (third degree) | white, brown or leathery, dry | painless | needs excision and grafting |

### Area

In adults the **rule of nines** assigns: head and neck 9 percent; each arm 9; anterior trunk 18; posterior trunk 18; each leg 18 (9 front, 9 back); perineum 1. In children the head is proportionally larger and legs smaller (Lund-Browder chart). The patient's **palm including fingers is about 1 percent** and can map scattered burns. **Do not count superficial (first-degree) burns.**

### Fluid resuscitation

Burn shock comes from capillary leak. The **Parkland formula** gives lactated Ringer's:

$$\text{total over 24 h} = 4 \text{ mL} \times \text{weight (kg)} \times \%\text{TBSA burned}.$$

Give **half in the first 8 hours from the time of injury** and the other half over the next 16 hours. The formula is a starting estimate: adjust hourly to **urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/h** (30 to 50 mL/h) in adults, because over-resuscitation ("fluid creep") causes compartment syndromes and pulmonary edema. Some burn centers start at 2 mL/kg/%TBSA. Fluid is given for burns of about 20 percent TBSA or more in adults, or about 15 percent in children.

### Other injuries

- **Circumferential full-thickness burns** of limbs or chest can restrict perfusion or ventilation; **escharotomy** is needed.
- **Electrical injuries:** check ECG, expect hidden deep tissue necrosis, myoglobinuria and compartment syndrome; give fluids to maintain brisk urine output.
- **Chemical burns:** remove contaminated clothing and irrigate copiously; alkalis penetrate deeply. Hydrofluoric acid needs calcium gluconate.
- **Burn center referral:** partial-thickness burns over about 10 percent TBSA, any full-thickness burn, burns of the face, hands, feet, genitalia or major joints, electrical, chemical and inhalation injuries, and burns in children or high-risk patients.

Give tetanus prophylaxis, analgesia and early enteral nutrition (burns are hypermetabolic). Systemic prophylactic antibiotics are not used. Topical agents include silver sulfadiazine (not on the face; avoid in sulfa allergy) and mafenide (penetrates eschar). Deep burns are excised and grafted early.

### Wound healing

1. **Hemostasis** (minutes): platelets, fibrin clot.
2. **Inflammation** (days 1 to 4): neutrophils, then macrophages, which clear debris and release growth factors.
3. **Proliferation** (about day 4 to week 3): fibroblasts deposit **type III collagen**, angiogenesis creates granulation tissue, epithelial cells migrate, and myofibroblasts contract the wound.
4. **Remodeling** (weeks to a year): type III collagen is replaced by **type I** collagen and cross-linked; tensile strength reaches about 80 percent of normal.

**Primary intention** closes a clean wound by sutures; **secondary intention** lets a wound heal by granulation (contaminated or infected wounds); **delayed primary closure** leaves the wound open for several days and then sutures it. **Impaired healing:** infection, ischemia, diabetes, smoking, malnutrition, glucocorticoids, radiation, vitamin C or zinc deficiency, and tension. Vitamin C is required to hydroxylate proline and lysine in collagen. **Hypertrophic scars** stay within the wound; **keloids** extend beyond it.

@example
title: Parkland calculation
problem: A 90-kg man has partial- and full-thickness burns over 25 percent of his body surface after a house fire 1 hour ago. Calculate his Parkland fluid requirement and the rate for the first 8 hours.
step: Total over 24 hours: $4 \times 90 \times 25 = 9000$ mL of lactated Ringer's.
step: Half, 4500 mL, goes in the first 8 hours from the time of the burn, not from arrival.
step: Because 1 hour has already passed, 7 hours remain: $4500/7 \approx 643$ mL/h to start (starting the clock at arrival and using $4500/8 = 563$ mL/h would under-infuse him).
step: Titrate hourly to a urine output of about 45 mL/h (0.5 mL/kg/h); reduce the rate if output is above target and increase it if below.

@mistakes
- Including superficial (first-degree) burns when calculating total body surface area.
- Starting the 8-hour clock at arrival instead of the time of injury.
- Trusting a normal pulse oximeter reading in suspected carbon monoxide poisoning.
- Delaying intubation in a patient with signs of inhalation injury and progressive edema.
