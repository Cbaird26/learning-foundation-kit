@article
### Acute limb ischemia

Acute limb ischemia (ALI) is a sudden drop in limb perfusion that threatens the limb within hours. The classic findings are the six Ps: **pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis and poikilothermia** (cool limb). The cause is either an **embolus** (usually from the heart, as in atrial fibrillation or a recent myocardial infarction; abrupt onset, no history of claudication, normal pulses in the other leg, lodges at an arterial bifurcation such as the common femoral) or **thrombosis in situ** of a chronically diseased artery (history of claudication, gradual worsening, abnormal pulses bilaterally).

**Severity (Rutherford):** I viable (no sensory loss or weakness); IIa marginally threatened (minimal sensory loss, no weakness); **IIb immediately threatened** (more than minimal sensory loss, rest pain, mild to moderate weakness); III irreversible (profound sensory loss, paralysis, rigor; amputation). Handheld Doppler shows whether arterial signals are audible.

**Management.** Give **IV unfractionated heparin immediately**, make the diagnosis clinically, and call vascular surgery. Imaging (CT angiography) is obtained if it does not delay care. Revascularization: **surgical embolectomy** (Fogarty catheter) for embolic occlusion, **catheter-directed thrombolysis** for viable or marginally threatened limbs, or bypass for thrombosis on chronic disease. Irreversible ischemia requires amputation. After reperfusion, watch for **reperfusion injury**: **compartment syndrome** (treat with fasciotomy), **hyperkalemia**, rhabdomyolysis with myoglobinuria and acute kidney injury, and metabolic acidosis. Find the source of embolus and start long-term anticoagulation.

### Chronic limb ischemia

**Intermittent claudication** is exertional calf, thigh or buttock pain relieved by rest. **Critical limb-threatening ischemia** shows rest pain, nonhealing ulcers or gangrene. The **ankle-brachial index (ABI)** is the higher ankle systolic pressure (dorsalis pedis or posterior tibial) divided by the higher brachial pressure. **Normal is 1.0 to 1.4**; **0.9 or less** diagnoses peripheral artery disease; 0.4 or less indicates severe disease; above 1.4 means noncompressible calcified arteries (use toe-brachial index). Treatment of claudication: **supervised exercise**, smoking cessation, a high-intensity statin, an antiplatelet agent, blood pressure and glucose control, and cilostazol (contraindicated in heart failure). Critical ischemia needs revascularization (endovascular or bypass). Arterial ulcers are painful, pale, punched-out and at the toes or pressure points; venous ulcers are shallow, wet and at the medial malleolus with edema; neuropathic ulcers are painless on pressure points of the foot.

### Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)

An AAA is an aortic diameter of 3 cm or more. Risk factors: age, male sex, smoking, hypertension, family history. A **one-time ultrasound screening** is recommended for men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked. Repair is indicated at **5.5 cm or more in men (5.0 to 5.4 cm in women)**, growth above 0.5 cm in 6 months, or symptoms. Options are open repair or **endovascular repair (EVAR)** when the anatomy allows. **Rupture** classically presents with abdominal or back pain, hypotension and a pulsatile mass; the patient goes straight to the operating room, with limited fluid resuscitation (**permissive hypotension**) and no delay for imaging if unstable.

### Aortic dissection

A tear in the intima lets blood enter the media, causing sudden tearing chest or back pain, a blood pressure difference between the arms and sometimes a new aortic regurgitation murmur or stroke. Hypertension is the leading risk factor, with connective tissue disease (Marfan syndrome) and bicuspid aortic valve. CT angiography confirms the diagnosis. **Stanford type A** involves the ascending aorta and is an **emergency for surgical repair**; **type B** (distal to the left subclavian) is treated medically unless complicated by malperfusion, rupture or refractory pain. Medical therapy lowers shear stress: a **beta blocker first (for example esmolol)** to a heart rate under about 60, then a vasodilator to a systolic pressure of 100 to 120 mmHg. A vasodilator alone causes reflex tachycardia and worsens shear.

### Carotid disease

A symptomatic carotid stenosis of 70 to 99 percent (and selected 50 to 69 percent) benefits from **carotid endarterectomy** within about 2 weeks of the event. Asymptomatic stenosis is managed with best medical therapy, and intervention is considered for selected patients with high-grade stenosis.

@example
title: Embolus or thrombosis?
problem: A 74-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation who stopped taking her anticoagulant has a sudden, painful, cold, pale left leg for 3 hours. No femoral pulse in the left leg, normal pulses on the right, foot sensation reduced beyond the toes, mild weakness of the foot. What is the diagnosis and the first action?
step: Sudden onset in a patient with atrial fibrillation and normal contralateral pulses indicates an embolus to the common femoral artery.
step: Sensory loss beyond the toes and mild weakness make this Rutherford class IIb, immediately threatened.
step: Start IV unfractionated heparin immediately and call vascular surgery. Do not delay for imaging.
step: She needs urgent revascularization, usually surgical embolectomy. Monitor potassium, creatinine and compartment pressures afterward for reperfusion injury.

@mistakes
- Waiting for angiography before starting heparin in acute limb ischemia.
- Using a vasodilator alone, without a beta blocker, in aortic dissection.
- Misreading a noncompressible ABI above 1.4 as normal.
- Forgetting reperfusion injury, including compartment syndrome and hyperkalemia, after revascularization.

prompt: A 69-year-old man with a history of claudication and diabetes presents with 8 hours of a painful, cold, mottled right foot with decreased sensation of the toes and ankle and mild foot weakness. solution: The six Ps (pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia) indicate acute limb ischemia. The history of claudication suggests thrombosis on chronic atherosclerosis, though an embolus must be considered (check for atrial fibrillation). Sensory loss above the toes and mild weakness with absent arterial signals make this Rutherford IIb, immediately threatened. Start IV unfractionated heparin at once and call vascular surgery for emergency revascularization (bypass or endovascular thrombectomy or thrombolysis; embolectomy if embolic). Obtain CT angiography if it does not delay treatment. Check potassium, creatinine, CK and ECG. After reperfusion, monitor for compartment syndrome (fasciotomy), hyperkalemia, rhabdomyolysis and kidney injury. Treat the cause: statin, antiplatelet therapy, and anticoagulation if cardioembolic.
