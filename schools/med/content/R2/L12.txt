@article
Anesthesia has four goals: unconsciousness (or sedation), analgesia, muscle relaxation when needed, and physiologic stability. Typical adult doses below are for study; check current references before any clinical use.

### Types of anesthesia

- **General anesthesia:** induction (IV agent), airway control, maintenance (inhaled volatile agent such as sevoflurane, or IV propofol) and emergence.
- **Neuraxial:** **spinal** (a single injection into cerebrospinal fluid; rapid, dense block; hypotension from sympathetic blockade) and **epidural** (catheter; titratable; labor and postoperative analgesia). Contraindications: patient refusal, infection at the site, coagulopathy or recent anticoagulant dosing (risk of **epidural hematoma**), severe hypovolemia and raised intracranial pressure. **Post-dural puncture headache** is worse when upright and better when supine; treat with fluids, caffeine and, if it persists, an **epidural blood patch**.
- **Peripheral nerve blocks** and **local infiltration** for limb and superficial surgery; **monitored anesthesia care** is sedation with monitoring.

### Airway

Assess mouth opening, Mallampati class, neck mobility, thyromental distance and dentition. A patient with a full stomach (emergency, bowel obstruction, pregnancy, trauma; a patient taking a GLP-1 agonist with active GI symptoms is treated as possibly having retained gastric contents) needs **rapid sequence induction** with preoxygenation, a rapidly acting induction agent and neuromuscular blocker, and no mask ventilation. If you cannot intubate or oxygenate, perform a surgical airway.

### Induction agents and muscle relaxants

- **Propofol:** fast onset, antiemetic; causes hypotension and respiratory depression.
- **Etomidate:** hemodynamically stable (good in shock), but suppresses adrenal steroid synthesis and causes myoclonus.
- **Ketamine:** NMDA antagonist; sympathomimetic (raises blood pressure and heart rate), bronchodilating, maintains airway reflexes; useful in hypovolemia and asthma; can cause emergence reactions.
- **Succinylcholine:** depolarizing, fastest and shortest-acting. Dangers: **hyperkalemia** in burns (after about 24 to 48 hours), crush injury, denervation and prolonged immobilization; **malignant hyperthermia**; prolonged paralysis in pseudocholinesterase deficiency.
- **Rocuronium:** nondepolarizing; reversed by **sugammadex** (or neostigmine with glycopyrrolate).
- **Opioid** overdose is reversed with naloxone.

### Malignant hyperthermia (MH)

MH is a genetic (RYR1) disorder triggered by **volatile anesthetics and succinylcholine**, causing uncontrolled calcium release in skeletal muscle. The earliest sign is a **rising end-tidal CO2** with tachycardia; then rigidity (including masseter), hyperthermia, acidosis, hyperkalemia and rhabdomyolysis. Treatment: **stop the triggers, hyperventilate with 100 percent oxygen, give dantrolene 2.5 mg/kg IV (repeat as needed)**, cool the patient and treat hyperkalemia and acidosis.

### Local anesthetics

Amides (lidocaine, bupivacaine) block sodium channels. Maximum doses: lidocaine **4.5 mg/kg plain** (7 mg/kg with epinephrine). A 1 percent solution contains 10 mg/mL. **Local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST)** begins with perioral numbness, metallic taste, tinnitus and confusion, then seizures and cardiovascular collapse; bupivacaine is the most cardiotoxic. Treatment: stop injection, support airway and circulation, give benzodiazepines for seizures and **20 percent lipid emulsion**.

### Perioperative anaphylaxis and nausea

The commonest triggers are neuromuscular blockers, antibiotics, chlorhexidine and latex; treat with IM epinephrine (typical adult dose 0.3 to 0.5 mg of 1 mg/mL), fluids and airway support. **Postoperative nausea and vomiting** risk is estimated with the **Apfel score** (female sex, nonsmoker, history of PONV or motion sickness, postoperative opioids; each is 1 point; 0 to 4 points corresponds to roughly 10, 20, 40, 60 and 80 percent risk). Prophylax with ondansetron, dexamethasone and, in high-risk patients, propofol-based anesthesia or additional agents.

### Postoperative analgesia and ERAS

Use **multimodal analgesia**: acetaminophen, NSAIDs when appropriate, regional blocks, and the lowest effective dose of opioids. **Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS)** pathways include preoperative counseling and optimization, avoidance of prolonged fasting, carbohydrate loading, no routine bowel preparation or nasogastric tubes, opioid-sparing analgesia, goal-directed fluids, normothermia, VTE prophylaxis, early oral intake and early ambulation. They shorten hospital stays and reduce complications.

### Fever after surgery: work it up by timing

Combine the timing from the previous lesson with the exam. **POD 0 to 1:** malignant hyperthermia, transfusion reaction, aspiration, necrotizing infection. **POD 1 to 2:** atelectasis or aspiration pneumonia. **POD 3 to 5:** urinary tract infection, line infection, pneumonia. **POD 5 to 7:** wound infection, DVT or PE, anastomotic leak. **POD 7 to 14:** abscess. **Anytime:** drug fever, *C. difficile*. Examine the incision, lungs, calves, catheter site, IV sites and abdomen, and test in proportion.

@example
title: Malignant hyperthermia dose
problem: During an anesthetic with sevoflurane and succinylcholine, an 80-kg patient develops a heart rate of 140/min, jaw rigidity, a temperature of 39.5 C and an end-tidal CO2 that has risen from 38 to 75 mmHg. What is the diagnosis and the first dantrolene dose?
step: A rising end-tidal CO2, tachycardia, rigidity and hyperthermia after a volatile agent and succinylcholine indicate malignant hyperthermia.
step: Stop the volatile agent and succinylcholine, hyperventilate with 100 percent oxygen and call for help and for the MH cart.
step: Dantrolene is $2.5 \text{ mg/kg} \times 80 \text{ kg} = 200$ mg IV, repeated as needed.
step: Cool the patient, treat hyperkalemia and acidosis, monitor CK and urine for myoglobin, and continue in an intensive care unit.

@mistakes
- Giving succinylcholine to patients with burns older than 24 to 48 hours or with crush injuries or denervation.
- Calling a fever after surgery "atelectasis" without examining the wound, lungs, legs and lines.
- Giving a neuraxial block to a patient who recently received therapeutic anticoagulation.
- Forgetting that a 1 percent solution contains 10 mg of drug per milliliter.

A urinary catheter was removed on postoperative day 2. Explain how you would work up his fever by timing, naming the most likely causes for this postoperative day, the focused examination and the first tests.
solution: Postoperative day 5 sits in the range of wound infection, DVT or PE, and anastomotic leak, and urinary tract infection, pneumonia and line infection are still possible. Exam: inspect and palpate the incision for erythema, drainage and fluctuance; auscultate the lungs; examine the calves for swelling and tenderness; check IV sites and the catheter history; and examine the abdomen for distension, guarding and rebound. First tests: CBC, basic metabolic panel, lactate, urinalysis and culture, blood cultures if toxic, and chest radiograph if respiratory signs. Because this is a colon anastomosis with fever, tachycardia and abdominal pain, obtain a CT of the abdomen and pelvis with contrast early to look for a leak or abscess. Duplex ultrasound or CT pulmonary angiography if the exam or Wells score suggests DVT or PE. Treat according to the source: open a wound infection, drain an abscess, anticoagulate for VTE, or reoperate for a leak with peritonitis.
