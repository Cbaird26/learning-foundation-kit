@article
### Gallstones

Most gallstones are **cholesterol stones** (supersaturated bile, reduced gallbladder motility); the rest are pigment stones from hemolysis or chronic biliary infection. Risk factors: female sex, pregnancy, obesity, rapid weight loss, age, and hemolytic disease for pigment stones. Most stones are silent and are not removed.

**Biliary colic** is episodic right upper quadrant or epigastric pain, often after a fatty meal, lasting 30 minutes to a few hours (under about 6 hours) as a stone transiently blocks the cystic duct. Exam and labs are normal. Ultrasound shows stones. Offer elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy to prevent recurrence and complications.

### Acute cholecystitis

When the cystic duct stays blocked, the gallbladder becomes inflamed and secondarily infected. Pain lasts longer than 6 hours, with fever, nausea, leukocytosis and a positive **Murphy sign** (inspiratory arrest on palpation of the right upper quadrant). **Right upper quadrant ultrasound** is the first test: stones, **wall thickening above 3 mm, pericholecystic fluid** and a sonographic Murphy sign. If ultrasound is equivocal, a **HIDA scan** that fails to fill the gallbladder confirms cystic duct obstruction. Treatment: NPO, IV fluids, analgesia, antibiotics (for example ceftriaxone plus metronidazole) and **early laparoscopic cholecystectomy**, ideally within about 72 hours of presentation and certainly within the same admission. Patients too ill for surgery can receive a **percutaneous cholecystostomy tube**. Complications include gangrene, perforation, empyema, emphysematous cholecystitis (gas in the wall, seen in diabetics) and Mirizzi syndrome. **Acalculous cholecystitis** occurs in critically ill patients (ischemia, stasis) and carries high mortality.

### Common bile duct stones and cholangitis

A stone in the common bile duct (**choledocholithiasis**) causes jaundice, pain and elevated alkaline phosphatase, GGT and conjugated bilirubin. Stratify the likelihood using the ASGE approach:

- **High:** stone seen on imaging, clinical ascending cholangitis, or total bilirubin above 4 mg/dL with a dilated common bile duct. Go directly to **ERCP** with sphincterotomy and stone extraction.
- **Intermediate:** abnormal liver tests, age above 55 or a dilated duct without the above. Confirm first with **MRCP** or endoscopic ultrasound (or intraoperative cholangiography).
- **Low:** none of these; proceed to cholecystectomy.

**Ascending cholangitis** is infection of an obstructed biliary tree. **Charcot triad** is fever, jaundice and right upper quadrant pain; **Reynolds pentad** adds hypotension and altered mental status. Treat with IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics and **biliary decompression** (ERCP, or percutaneous drainage if ERCP fails), urgently in severe cases. Antibiotics alone are not enough.

### Acute pancreatitis

Causes: gallstones and alcohol (most common), hypertriglyceridemia (usually above 1000 mg/dL), hypercalcemia, medications, ERCP, trauma, and autoimmune disease. Diagnosis requires **two of three**: characteristic epigastric pain radiating to the back; **lipase or amylase more than 3 times the upper limit of normal**; and imaging findings. Ultrasound looks for gallstones and a dilated duct; CT is not needed at presentation unless the diagnosis is uncertain.

Management is supportive: **goal-directed IV fluids with lactated Ringer's** (avoid both under- and over-resuscitation), analgesia, and **early oral or enteral feeding** as tolerated (enteral is preferred over parenteral). **Prophylactic antibiotics are not indicated.** ERCP is used only for gallstone pancreatitis with cholangitis or persistent obstruction. **Mild gallstone pancreatitis: cholecystectomy during the same admission** to prevent recurrence. Severe disease is defined by persistent organ failure beyond 48 hours. Pancreatic necrosis that becomes infected is treated with antibiotics and, when possible, delayed drainage or debridement at about 4 weeks (a minimally invasive "step-up" approach). A **pseudocyst** is a fluid collection with a wall that forms after more than 4 weeks; it is drained if symptomatic.

### Pancreatic cancer

Most are ductal adenocarcinomas of the head. Presentation: **painless jaundice**, weight loss, back pain, new-onset diabetes, and a palpable nontender gallbladder (**Courvoisier sign**). Trousseau sign (migratory thrombophlebitis) can occur. Get a pancreas-protocol CT; CA 19-9 is used to follow disease, not to screen. Resectable tumors without vascular involvement go to **pancreaticoduodenectomy (Whipple procedure)** followed by adjuvant chemotherapy; borderline resectable tumors usually receive neoadjuvant therapy. Most patients present with unresectable disease.

@example
title: Cholecystitis or cholangitis?
problem: A 52-year-old woman has 36 hours of constant right upper quadrant pain, fever of 38.4 C, a positive Murphy sign, WBC 15,500/microliter and normal bilirubin. She is not jaundiced and her ALT and alkaline phosphatase are normal. Ultrasound shows stones, a 5-mm wall and pericholecystic fluid. What is the diagnosis and plan?
step: Constant pain over 6 hours with fever, leukocytosis, a Murphy sign and ultrasound findings of wall thickening above 3 mm and pericholecystic fluid are acute calculous cholecystitis.
step: Charcot triad is incomplete (no jaundice) and her bilirubin and alkaline phosphatase are normal, so a common bile duct stone is unlikely (low risk).
step: Make her NPO, give IV fluids, analgesia and antibiotics.
step: Perform early laparoscopic cholecystectomy within the admission, ideally within 72 hours; ERCP is not needed.

@mistakes
- Using CA 19-9 as a screening test for pancreatic cancer.
- Giving antibiotics to every patient with acute pancreatitis; prophylaxis does not help.
- Treating cholangitis with antibiotics alone and not decompressing the duct.
- Withholding feeding for days in mild pancreatitis; early oral feeding is better.
