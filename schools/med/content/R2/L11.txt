@article
Complications follow patterns. Asking **when after surgery** the problem arose is the best way to narrow the differential diagnosis, and a systematic exam of the wound, lungs, legs, urine, lines and abdomen finds most causes.

### Fever by timing

Fever in the first 48 hours is very common and is usually an inflammatory response to surgery; it needs an examination but rarely extensive testing. The classic teaching uses five Ws:

| Timing | Cause |
|---|---|
| POD 1 to 2 | **Wind**: atelectasis or aspiration pneumonitis (the link between atelectasis and fever is weaker than once taught, so examine for true pneumonia) |
| POD 3 to 5 | **Water**: urinary tract infection (catheter-associated); also IV line phlebitis |
| POD 5 to 7 | **Wound**: surgical site infection; **Walking**: deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism |
| POD 5 to 7 and later | anastomotic leak, intra-abdominal abscess (often POD 7 to 14), *C. difficile* colitis |
| Any time | **Wonder drugs**: drug fever, transfusion reactions |

Some causes are **early and dangerous**: **malignant hyperthermia** (intraoperative or in the recovery room), necrotizing soft-tissue infection (fever, pain out of proportion and rapidly spreading erythema in the first 24 to 72 hours, often group A streptococcus or *Clostridium*), and transfusion reactions.

### Surgical site infection

Surgical wounds are classified as clean, clean-contaminated, contaminated and dirty, with infection risk rising across the classes. Prevent infection with **a single dose of cefazolin (or an appropriate agent) within 60 minutes before incision** (120 minutes for vancomycin or fluoroquinolones), redosed for long cases and stopped within 24 hours; clipping (not razor shaving) of hair; chlorhexidine-alcohol skin preparation; normothermia; and glucose control. Wound infection usually presents at days 5 to 7 with erythema, warmth, drainage and fever. Treatment is to **open the wound, drain it and pack it**; add antibiotics only for spreading cellulitis or systemic signs.

### Venous thromboembolism

Surgery causes stasis, endothelial injury and a hypercoagulable state (Virchow triad). Use mechanical prophylaxis and pharmacologic prophylaxis (heparin or low-molecular-weight heparin) according to the **Caprini score**. DVT causes unilateral leg swelling and pain; PE causes dyspnea, pleuritic chest pain, tachycardia and hypoxia. Estimate probability with the **Wells score** for PE: clinical signs of DVT 3, PE as likely or more likely than another diagnosis 3, heart rate above 100 1.5, surgery or immobilization in the previous 4 weeks 1.5, previous DVT or PE 1.5, hemoptysis 1, malignancy 1. Probable PE is above 4. Confirm with CT pulmonary angiography (V/Q scan if contrast is contraindicated) and treat with anticoagulation.

### Anastomotic leak and abscess

A leak occurs typically at POD 5 to 7 with fever, tachycardia, ileus, abdominal pain and leukocytosis; the patient may progress to peritonitis. CT with contrast shows extraluminal fluid or contrast. Treatment: resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and drainage or reoperation depending on stability. A contained abscess is drained percutaneously.

### Wound dehiscence

Fascial dehiscence classically appears at POD 5 to 10 with a sudden gush of **serosanguineous ("salmon-colored") fluid**, often after straining. **Evisceration** is bowel exposed through the wound: cover it with saline-moistened sterile gauze, keep the patient flat and NPO, give antibiotics and return to the operating room.

### Renal and urinary

**Oliguria** (urine below 0.5 mL/kg/h) is common. Look for hypovolemia (prerenal), obstruction (a blocked or kinked catheter, urinary retention) and acute tubular necrosis. A fluid challenge helps prerenal states. **FENa** is $(U_{Na} \times P_{Cr})/(P_{Na} \times U_{Cr}) \times 100$: below 1 percent suggests prerenal azotemia and above 2 percent suggests ATN (diuretics invalidate it). Retention in elderly men is common after surgery and anesthesia.

### Other

**Ileus** resolves within 2 to 3 days in the small bowel and a little longer in the colon; manage with electrolytes, minimal opioids and early mobilization. **Delirium** is frequent in older adults: treat the cause (pain, infection, hypoxia, drugs, urinary retention), avoid benzodiazepines and anticholinergics, and keep sleep and orientation routines. **Perioperative myocardial infarction** is often silent and occurs in the first 48 hours; check troponin and ECG. **Postoperative hemorrhage** presents with tachycardia, hypotension, falling hemoglobin and bloody drain output; resuscitate and reoperate if unstable.

@example
title: Fever on postoperative day 6
problem: A 62-year-old man has temperature 38.9 C on POD 6 after an open sigmoid colectomy. His lungs are clear, his calves are soft and he has a catheter-free urinary stream. His midline incision is red, warm and has purulent drainage. What is the likely diagnosis and treatment?
step: Timing at POD 5 to 7 with a red, warm incision and pus points to surgical site infection (wound).
step: The lungs, legs and urine are unremarkable, which argues against wind, walking and water.
step: Open the wound at the bedside, drain the pus and pack it. Antibiotics are added only if cellulitis is spreading or he has systemic signs such as hypotension.
step: Because he had a colon resection, also keep a leak or abscess in mind if fever persists after the wound is drained; obtain CT if so.

@mistakes
- Ordering extensive cultures and imaging for a fever in the first 48 hours without a focused examination.
- Starting antibiotics for a superficial wound infection without opening the wound.
- Treating oliguria with diuretics before assessing volume status and catheter patency.
- Missing a leak because fever and tachycardia were attributed to atelectasis.

Using the Wells score (clinical signs of DVT 3, PE most likely 3, heart rate above 100 1.5, surgery within 4 weeks 1.5, prior DVT/PE 1.5, hemoptysis 1, cancer 1), what is her score?

@practice num
prompt: A postoperative patient has a urine sodium of 10 mEq/L, plasma sodium of 140 mEq/L, urine creatinine of 80 mg/dL and plasma creatinine of 2.0 mg/dL. What is the fractional excretion of sodium in percent? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.18
check: (10 * 2.0) / (140 * 80) * 100
tol: 0.02
unit: %
hint: FENa equals urine sodium times plasma creatinine, divided by plasma sodium times urine creatinine, times 100.
solution: $(10 \times 2.0)/(140 \times 80) \times 100 = 0.18$ percent, which suggests prerenal azotemia.

@quiz num
prompt: An 80-kg man has had 100 mL of urine over the past 4 hours. What is his urine output in mL/kg/h? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.31
check: 100 / 4 / 80
tol: 0.01
unit: mL/kg/h
hint: Divide the volume by the hours and by the body weight.
solution: $100/4 = 25$ mL/h, and $25/80 = 0.31$ mL/kg/h, which is below the 0.5 mL/kg/h threshold for oliguria.
