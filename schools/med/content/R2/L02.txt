@article
Surgical patients lose fluid before, during and after operations: fasting, bowel preparation, vomiting, blood loss, evaporation from open abdomens and **third spacing** (shift of fluid into injured tissue and bowel wall). Good fluid management means knowing what the patient has lost, what the body still needs, and what the kidneys are doing.

### Body water

Total body water is about 60 percent of body weight in men and 50 percent in women. Two thirds is intracellular; one third is extracellular, and of that, three quarters is interstitial and one quarter is plasma. Isotonic saline distributes across the whole extracellular space, so only about a quarter of an infused liter stays intravascular after equilibration.

### Maintenance fluids: the 4-2-1 rule

Hourly maintenance volume is **4 mL/kg for the first 10 kg, 2 mL/kg for the next 10 kg, and 1 mL/kg for each kilogram above 20 kg**. For 70 kg: $40 + 20 + 50 = 110$ mL/h. Adults in a hospital are often given a little less. Urine output is the simplest bedside target: at least **0.5 mL/kg/h** in adults.

### Choosing a crystalloid

| Fluid | Na (mEq/L) | Cl (mEq/L) | Other |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.9% saline | 154 | 154 | none |
| Lactated Ringer's | 130 | 109 | K 4, Ca 3, lactate 28 |
| 5% dextrose in water | 0 | 0 | free water, no electrolytes |

Large volumes of normal saline cause a **hyperchloremic, non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis**. Lactated Ringer's (a "balanced" crystalloid) avoids this and is favored in most resuscitation, though it is hypotonic relative to plasma and is not the first choice in severe hyponatremia or brain injury. D5W provides free water; once the dextrose is metabolized, it behaves as free water and is not used for volume resuscitation.

### GI losses and electrolyte patterns

- **Vomiting or NG suction** (gastric acid): hypochloremic, hypokalemic **metabolic alkalosis** with volume depletion. In gastric outlet obstruction, the kidney retains sodium and bicarbonate and excretes H+ ("paradoxical aciduria"). Treat with normal saline plus potassium.
- **Diarrhea, small bowel, pancreatic or biliary fistula output** (bicarbonate-rich): **non-anion-gap metabolic acidosis**. Replace with lactated Ringer's.
- **Replace like with like:** measure ongoing losses and match them with a fluid of similar composition.

### Key electrolytes

**Hyperkalemia** with ECG changes: give IV calcium (gluconate) to stabilize the membrane, then insulin with dextrose, nebulized albuterol and, as needed, loop diuretics, binders or dialysis. **Hypokalemia:** replace potassium, usually at no more than 10 mEq/h peripherally; check magnesium. **Hypernatremia** is corrected with free water: the **free water deficit** is $\text{TBW} \times (\text{Na}/140 - 1)$, where TBW is estimated from weight (0.6 for men). Lower sodium slowly (about 10 mEq/L per day or less in chronic cases). **Chronic hyponatremia** must not be corrected faster than about 8 mEq/L per 24 hours, to avoid osmotic demyelination.

### Nutrition

Indications for support: expected inability to eat for 5 to 7 days, or sooner in a malnourished patient. **If the gut works, use it** (enteral nutrition preserves the mucosal barrier, costs less and has fewer infections than parenteral). Start enteral feeding within about 24 to 48 hours in patients who cannot eat. Reserve **parenteral nutrition (PN)** for patients whose gut cannot be used, such as high-output fistula, prolonged ileus or obstruction.

Typical targets: **25 to 30 kcal/kg/day** and **1.2 to 2.0 g/kg/day of protein** in surgical and critical illness. Energy density: dextrose 3.4 kcal/g, protein 4 kcal/g, fat about 9 kcal/g. Serum albumin is a marker of inflammation and illness severity, not a good marker of nutritional status.

**Complications of PN:** catheter-related bloodstream infection, hyperglycemia, cholestasis and gallstones, and **refeeding syndrome**. In refeeding, carbohydrate raises insulin, which drives phosphate, potassium and magnesium into cells: **hypophosphatemia** (weakness, respiratory failure, arrhythmia), hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, fluid overload and thiamine deficiency. Prevent it by starting slowly in malnourished patients, giving thiamine and monitoring phosphate, potassium and magnesium daily.

@example
title: Maintenance fluids and a free water deficit
problem: A 70-kg man (TBW 60 percent) has a serum sodium of 154 mEq/L after a week of fever and poor oral intake. Calculate his maintenance rate by the 4-2-1 rule and his free water deficit.
step: Maintenance: 4 mL/kg/h for the first 10 kg is 40 mL/h; 2 mL/kg/h for the next 10 kg is 20 mL/h; 1 mL/kg/h for the remaining 50 kg is 50 mL/h. The total is 110 mL/h.
step: TBW is $0.6 \times 70 = 42$ L.
step: Free water deficit $= 42 \times (154/140 - 1) = 42 \times 0.1 = 4.2$ L.
step: Replace the deficit over 48 to 72 hours rather than rapidly, so sodium falls by no more than about 10 mEq/L per day, and add maintenance and ongoing losses.

@mistakes
- Using normal saline in large volumes without watching for hyperchloremic acidosis.
- Correcting chronic hyponatremia or hypernatremia too quickly.
- Starting full feeding at once in a starved patient and missing refeeding hypophosphatemia.
- Using serum albumin to diagnose malnutrition.
