@article
A **hernia** is a protrusion of a viscus or tissue through a defect in the wall that normally contains it. It has a **neck** (the defect), a **sac** (peritoneum) and **contents** (omentum, bowel, rarely bladder or appendix). Weakness, high intra-abdominal pressure (straining, cough, obesity, ascites) and poor tissue quality (age, smoking, steroids) combine to produce it.

### Inguinal anatomy

The inguinal canal runs from the **deep ring** (an opening in the transversalis fascia, lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels) to the **superficial ring** (an opening in the external oblique aponeurosis). In men it contains the spermatic cord (vas deferens, testicular artery, pampiniform plexus, cremaster muscle) and the ilioinguinal nerve; in women it holds the round ligament. **Hesselbach triangle** is bounded by the inferior epigastric vessels laterally, the lateral border of the rectus sheath medially and the inguinal ligament below.

- **Indirect inguinal hernia:** enters through the deep ring, **lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels**, and follows the cord. It results from a **patent processus vaginalis** and is the most common hernia in both sexes, including children.
- **Direct inguinal hernia:** bulges through weakened transversalis fascia **medial to the inferior epigastric vessels**, in Hesselbach triangle. It is acquired, occurs in older men and rarely enters the scrotum.
- **Femoral hernia:** below the inguinal ligament, **medial to the femoral vein**, through the femoral canal. It is more common in women, appears as a firm bulge in the upper medial thigh, and has the **highest risk of strangulation**.

Direct and indirect hernias are managed alike (repair when symptomatic or in women; see below); a femoral hernia is repaired promptly even when asymptomatic.

### Descriptive terms

A **reducible** hernia returns to the abdomen. An **incarcerated** hernia is irreducible; it may obstruct the bowel. A **strangulated** hernia has compromised blood supply: constant severe pain, a tender, firm, erythematous bulge, fever, leukocytosis and elevated lactate. A **Richter hernia** traps only part of the bowel wall and may strangulate without causing obstruction. Strangulation is a surgical emergency: do not wait for imaging if the picture is clear, and expect to resect nonviable bowel.

### Diagnosis and repair

Diagnosis is clinical: examine standing and with Valsalva, and palpate through the scrotum. Use ultrasound (or CT or MRI) when the exam is unclear. For men, **watchful waiting is safe for minimally symptomatic or asymptomatic inguinal hernias**; repair is offered when symptoms interfere with life. In women, repair is generally recommended because femoral hernias are common and are easy to misdiagnose. Children with an indirect hernia are repaired soon after diagnosis because of the incarceration risk, especially in infants.

Repair uses **mesh** to lower recurrence (open Lichtenstein tension-free repair, or laparoscopic TEP or TAPP approaches). Complications: seroma, hematoma, urinary retention, chronic groin pain, ilioinguinal nerve injury, testicular ischemia and recurrence.

### Other hernias

- **Umbilical:** common in infants (most close by age 4 to 5 years) and in adults with ascites or obesity.
- **Epigastric:** midline defect between the xiphoid and umbilicus.
- **Spigelian:** through the linea semilunaris at the lateral border of the rectus; often intraparietal and hard to feel.
- **Incisional:** occurs in about 10 to 15 percent of laparotomies; risk factors are obesity, wound infection, smoking, diabetes and steroids. Elective repair is usually deferred until smoking stops, glucose is controlled and BMI is lowered (many programs use a BMI under 35 to 40).
- **Obturator:** thin elderly women with obstruction; **Howship-Romberg sign** (pain down the medial thigh).
- **Littre hernia** contains a Meckel diverticulum; **Amyand hernia** contains the appendix.
- **Hiatal hernia:** type I sliding hernias (about 95 percent) cause reflux; paraesophageal (type II to IV) hernias can obstruct or strangulate and are repaired when symptomatic.

@example
title: A tender bulge below the inguinal ligament
problem: A 78-year-old woman has 2 days of vomiting and abdominal distension. She has a firm, tender, irreducible bulge below the inguinal ligament, medial to the femoral pulse. Temperature is 38.1 C. What is it, and what is the plan?
step: A bulge below the inguinal ligament and medial to the femoral vessels in an older woman is a femoral hernia.
step: Irreducibility, tenderness, fever and obstructive symptoms indicate incarceration with possible strangulation.
step: Resuscitate with IV fluids, make her NPO, place a nasogastric tube if she is vomiting, and give antibiotics.
step: Take her to the operating room urgently for repair and assessment of bowel viability; resect any nonviable bowel.

@mistakes
- Mixing up direct and indirect hernias; indirect is lateral to the inferior epigastric vessels, direct is medial.
- Observing a femoral hernia because it is small; the strangulation risk is high regardless of size.
- Attempting forceful reduction of a hernia with signs of strangulation.
- Treating any irreducible hernia as an emergency without checking for signs of ischemia.
