@article
An **argumentative essay** tries to convince a reader to accept a position by reasoning from evidence. Unlike a quarrel, it is calm, organized and honest about the other side.

### Kinds of claim

- **Fact**: can be verified ("Teen sleep has declined").
- **Value**: judges something good or bad ("Cruelty to animals is wrong").
- **Policy**: says what should be done ("Schools should start later").

Policy and value claims are common thesis types. A claim is arguable only if reasonable people could disagree.

### Building the argument

1. **Claim (thesis)**: your position in one sentence, often with a "because" preview: "Schools should start after 8:30 a.m. because later start times improve sleep, health and attendance."
2. **Reasons**: the main points supporting the claim; each becomes a body paragraph.
3. **Evidence**: facts, statistics, expert testimony, examples. Cite the source.
4. **Warrant**: the reasoning that explains *why* the evidence supports the reason. Often it is unstated; stating it makes the argument stronger.
5. **Counterargument and rebuttal**: state the best opposing view fairly, then respond with evidence or reasoning.

### The appeals again

Use ethos (fair, informed tone; credible sources), logos (evidence and logic) and a measured pathos (a true example). Avoid fallacies such as straw man (distorting the other side), ad hominem and false dilemma.

### Counterargument

Present the strongest objection, not the weakest. Use a pattern: "Some argue that... This view has merit because... However, ..." A **concession** shows fairness and builds ethos. The rebuttal must answer the objection itself, not change the subject.

### Structure

- **Introduction**: context, hook, thesis.
- **Body**: reasons in a logical order (often strongest last), each with evidence and explanation; one paragraph for the counterargument.
- **Conclusion**: restate the claim, summarize the reasoning and say why it matters or what should happen next.

### Style

Use precise, formal language, transitions ("furthermore," "in contrast," "as a result"), and avoid first-person opinion phrases such as "I think." State claims directly.

@example
title: Rebutting a counterargument
problem: Thesis: "Schools should start after 8:30 a.m." Opposing view: "Later starts would interfere with after-school sports." Write a concession and rebuttal.
step: State the counterargument fairly: "Some argue that later starts would shorten the time available for after-school sports."
step: Concede what is true: "That concern is real, since schedules would shift."
step: Rebut with reasoning: "However, studies link adequate sleep in teenagers to better attention and a lower risk of sports injuries, and practices can be adjusted with lighting or schedule changes."
step: Link back to the thesis: "The benefit of improved sleep outweighs the inconvenience of rescheduling."

@mistakes
- Stating a thesis that is a fact or a personal preference.
- Ignoring the opposing view or presenting a weak version of it.
- Listing evidence without explaining why it supports the claim.
- Relying only on emotion or only on statistics.

@practice self
prompt: Deliverable practice: Write a thesis-driven essay outline: thesis, three reasons with one piece of evidence each, one counterargument with rebuttal, and a one-sentence conclusion, on the claim "Schools should teach financial literacy."
hint: Order the reasons, strongest last, and state the evidence you would cite.
solution: Thesis: Schools should require a financial-literacy course because it prepares students for real decisions, reduces debt problems and builds equity. Reason 1: Students face financial choices soon after graduation (evidence: surveys of young adults on credit and loans). Reason 2: Early education reduces costly mistakes (evidence: studies comparing adults who took courses). Reason 3: It narrows gaps between families that discuss money and those that do not (evidence: research on household financial knowledge). Counterargument: the curriculum is already crowded; rebuttal: a short, integrated math unit fits existing requirements. Conclusion: A financial-literacy requirement is a practical step toward informed, independent adults.
rubric: Thesis is arguable and previews reasons.
rubric: Three distinct reasons each name a type of evidence.
rubric: Includes a fair counterargument and a rebuttal.
