@article
Pathology tells you what happened to the tissue; pharmacology tells you what a drug will do to the patient. Both reward the same habit: connect the **mechanism** to the **consequence**.

### Cell injury

Injury begins with a stressor such as hypoxia, toxins, infection or immune attack. **Reversible injury** is marked by cell swelling, loss of microvilli, membrane blebs and ribosome detachment from the endoplasmic reticulum, mostly because ATP falls and the sodium pump fails. **Irreversible injury** adds severe membrane damage, lysosomal rupture and nuclear change: **pyknosis** (shrinkage), **karyorrhexis** (fragmentation) and **karyolysis** (dissolution).

**Necrosis** is uncontrolled and triggers inflammation. Patterns:

| Pattern | Typical setting |
|---|---|
| Coagulative | Ischemia in solid organs (heart, kidney, spleen) |
| Liquefactive | Brain infarct, abscess |
| Caseous | Tuberculosis, some fungal infections |
| Fat necrosis | Pancreatitis, breast trauma |
| Fibrinoid | Immune vasculitis, malignant hypertension |
| Gangrenous | Ischemia of a limb, with superimposed infection (wet) or without (dry) |

**Apoptosis** is programmed, ATP-dependent and does not cause inflammation. The **intrinsic** pathway is regulated by the BCL-2 family: loss of BCL-2 or BAX activation releases cytochrome c and activates caspase 9. The **extrinsic** pathway starts when Fas ligand binds Fas and activates caspase 8. Both converge on executioner caspases such as caspase 3.

### Pharmacokinetics

Volume of distribution $V_d = \dfrac{\text{amount in body}}{C_0}$, where $C_0$ is the extrapolated initial concentration. Clearance $CL = V_d \times k$, and half-life is

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693\, V_d}{CL}.$$

**Loading dose** $= \dfrac{C_p \times V_d}{F}$ and the **maintenance dose rate** $= \dfrac{CL \times C_{ss}}{F}$, where $F$ is bioavailability. A drug reaches steady state after about **4 to 5 half-lives** regardless of dose. Most drugs show **first-order** elimination (a constant fraction per unit time); ethanol, and phenytoin and aspirin at high doses, show **zero-order** (constant amount) elimination, so small dose increases can cause large concentration increases.

### Metabolism and interactions

Phase I reactions (oxidation by cytochrome P450, reduction, hydrolysis) are lost first in elderly or liver-diseased patients; phase II conjugation (glucuronidation, acetylation, sulfation) is relatively preserved. **Inducers** increase metabolism of co-administered drugs and lower their effect: rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital, St John's wort and chronic alcohol use. **Inhibitors** raise levels of co-administered drugs: ciprofloxacin, clarithromycin and erythromycin, azole antifungals, ritonavir, cimetidine, amiodarone and grapefruit juice. Warfarin is the classic victim: induction lowers the INR, inhibition raises it. Allopurinol (a xanthine oxidase inhibitor) raises levels of azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine.

Weak acids such as aspirin and phenobarbital are trapped in the urine and excreted faster when the urine is alkalinized with bicarbonate; weak bases are excreted faster in acidic urine.

### Pharmacodynamics

**Potency** is the dose needed for a given effect (lower EC50 is more potent); **efficacy** is the maximum effect ($E_{max}$). A **competitive antagonist** shifts the agonist curve to the right (higher EC50) with the same $E_{max}$, and can be overcome by more agonist. A **noncompetitive antagonist** lowers $E_{max}$. A **partial agonist** has a lower $E_{max}$ than a full agonist. The therapeutic index is $TD_{50}/ED_{50}$; a narrow index (warfarin, digoxin, lithium, phenytoin) calls for monitoring.

@example
title: Half-life from volume and clearance
problem: A 600 mg intravenous bolus gives an extrapolated initial concentration of 12 mg/L. Clearance is 5 L/h. Find the volume of distribution and the half-life.
step: $V_d = 600 \text{ mg} / 12 \text{ mg/L} = 50$ L.
step: Elimination constant $k = CL / V_d = 5/50 = 0.1$ per hour.
step: $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k = 0.693/0.1 = 6.93$ hours. Steady state would take about 4 to 5 half-lives, roughly 28 to 35 hours.

@example
title: Loading and maintenance doses
problem: A drug with $V_d = 50$ L and $CL = 4$ L/h is given intravenously ($F = 1$). The target concentration is 8 mg/L. Find the loading dose and the maintenance infusion rate.
step: Loading dose $= C_p \times V_d / F = 8 \times 50 / 1 = 400$ mg.
step: Maintenance rate $= CL \times C_{ss} / F = 4 \times 8 / 1 = 32$ mg/h.
step: The loading dose fills the volume quickly. The maintenance rate replaces what clearance removes. Doses are for study; always check references before clinical use.

@mistakes
- Using clearance instead of volume of distribution to calculate a loading dose.
- Forgetting to divide by bioavailability $F$ for oral dosing.
- Thinking a loading dose shortens the time to steady state; it only gets the concentration there faster, while the half-life still governs washout.
- Calling a competitive antagonist "less efficacious"; it reduces potency, not maximal effect.
