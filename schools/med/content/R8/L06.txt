@article
The kidney regulates volume, osmolality, potassium and pH. The gut digests, absorbs and detoxifies. In both systems the exam tests **patterns**: a lab panel that implies a diagnosis, a biopsy finding that names a glomerular disease, or a symptom that localizes a lesion.

### Acid-base

Start with the pH and then ask which of bicarbonate and $P_aCO_2$ moved to explain it. For a metabolic acidosis, calculate the **anion gap**:

$$AG = Na^+ - (Cl^- + HCO_3^-),$$

normally about 8 to 12 mEq/L. A high gap suggests added unmeasured acid: methanol, uremia, diabetic ketoacidosis, propylene glycol, isoniazid or iron, lactic acidosis, ethylene glycol and salicylates (MUDPILES). A normal gap (hyperchloremic) acidosis suggests diarrhea, renal tubular acidosis or large-volume saline. Check compensation with **Winter formula**: expected $P_aCO_2 = 1.5 \times HCO_3^- + 8 \pm 2$. A measured value above the range signals an added respiratory acidosis; below it, an added respiratory alkalosis.

**Ethylene glycol** poisoning gives a high-anion-gap acidosis, an osmolar gap, acute kidney injury and calcium oxalate crystals in the urine; the antidote is **fomepizole**, which inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase. **Salicylate** toxicity classically mixes a respiratory alkalosis and a metabolic acidosis; alkalinize the urine.

### Acute kidney injury

Fractional excretion of sodium

$$FE_{Na} = \frac{U_{Na} \times P_{Cr}}{P_{Na} \times U_{Cr}} \times 100.$$

A value below 1 percent suggests **prerenal** azotemia (intact tubules reabsorbing sodium); above 2 percent suggests **acute tubular necrosis**. Prerenal azotemia also has a BUN:creatinine ratio above 20 and a bland sediment; ATN has muddy brown granular casts.

### Glomerular disease

| Syndrome | Features | Examples and biopsy |
|---|---|---|
| Nephrotic | Proteinuria above 3.5 g/day, hypoalbuminemia, edema, hyperlipidemia | Minimal change (children; normal light microscopy; effacement of podocyte foot processes on EM); FSGS; membranous (adults; subepithelial spike-and-dome; anti-PLA2R antibody) |
| Nephritic | Hematuria with red cell casts, hypertension, oliguria | Post-streptococcal (1 to 3 weeks after pharyngitis; low C3; subepithelial humps); IgA nephropathy (days after infection; mesangial IgA); Alport (COL4A5; basket-weave basement membrane, hearing loss) |

### Diuretics and electrolytes

Loop diuretics inhibit NKCC2 (hypokalemia, ototoxicity, hypocalcemia); thiazides inhibit the NaCl cotransporter (hypercalcemia, hyponatremia); spironolactone blocks the mineralocorticoid receptor (hyperkalemia). Hyperkalemia with ECG changes (peaked T waves, wide QRS) is treated first with IV calcium to stabilize the myocardium, then with insulin plus glucose and other shifting or removing therapies.

### Gastrointestinal physiology

Gastrin (G cells) increases acid; CCK causes gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme release; secretin raises pancreatic bicarbonate. **Duodenal ulcers**, mostly from *H. pylori*, often hurt less after eating; gastric ulcers often hurt more. A Zollinger-Ellison gastrinoma causes multiple, refractory ulcers.

### Liver and bowel

Unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia fits hemolysis or Gilbert syndrome; conjugated fits obstruction or hepatocellular disease. **Hereditary hemochromatosis** (HFE C282Y) causes cirrhosis, diabetes, arthropathy and skin pigmentation, with high ferritin and transferrin saturation; phlebotomy treats it. **Wilson disease** is ATP7B deficiency with low ceruloplasmin and Kayser-Fleischer rings.

| Feature | Crohn disease | Ulcerative colitis |
|---|---|---|
| Distribution | Any site, skip lesions, terminal ileum | Rectum upward, continuous |
| Depth | Transmural; fistulas, strictures | Mucosa and submucosa |
| Histology | Noncaseating granulomas | Crypt abscesses |
| Association | Calcium oxalate stones, B12 deficiency | Primary sclerosing cholangitis, pANCA |

Celiac disease features anti-tissue transglutaminase antibodies, HLA-DQ2 or DQ8 and villous atrophy.

@example
title: Anion gap and compensation
problem: A patient has Na 140, Cl 98 and HCO3 8 mEq/L. Calculate the anion gap and the expected arterial $P_aCO_2$ using the Winter formula.
step: $AG = 140 - (98 + 8) = 34$ mEq/L, which is high.
step: Expected $P_aCO_2 = 1.5 \times 8 + 8 = 20$ mm Hg, with a range of 18 to 22.
step: A measured $P_aCO_2$ of 20 would mean appropriate compensation. A value of 35 would mean a second, respiratory acidosis.

@example
title: Prerenal azotemia or ATN
problem: A patient with vomiting and low blood pressure has urine sodium 10 mEq/L, plasma sodium 140 mEq/L, urine creatinine 80 mg/dL and plasma creatinine 2.0 mg/dL. Calculate the fractional excretion of sodium and interpret it.
step: $FE_{Na} = (10 \times 2.0)/(140 \times 80) \times 100$.
step: $= 20/11200 \times 100 = 0.18$ percent.
step: Below 1 percent, so prerenal azotemia from volume depletion is most likely.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to check compensation, so a mixed acid-base disorder goes unnoticed.
- Using the fractional excretion of sodium in a patient on diuretics, where sodium excretion is raised for pharmacologic reasons (fractional excretion of urea is an alternative).
- Mistaking minimal change disease for glomerulonephritis because it follows an infection.
- Reversing Crohn disease (transmural, granulomas) and ulcerative colitis (continuous, mucosal).
