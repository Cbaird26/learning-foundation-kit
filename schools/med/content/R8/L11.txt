This part of Step 1 is dependable points: the formulas are short, and the ethics rules follow a small number of principles (autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice). Practice until you can set up a 2 by 2 table in seconds.

### The 2 by 2 table

Let TP be true positives, FP false positives, FN false negatives and TN true negatives.

$$\text{Sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}, \qquad \text{Specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}$$

$$PPV = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad NPV = \frac{TN}{TN + FN}$$

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test. **PPV rises and NPV falls as prevalence rises**, because the same test is applied to a population with more true disease. The likelihood ratios are $LR^+ = \dfrac{\text{sensitivity}}{1 - \text{specificity}}$ and $LR^- = \dfrac{1 - \text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}$. A high-sensitivity test, when negative, rules out disease (SNOUT); a high-specificity test, when positive, rules it in (SPIN).

### Risk and effect measures

- **Relative risk** $RR = \dfrac{\text{risk in exposed}}{\text{risk in unexposed}}$ (cohort studies and trials).
- **Odds ratio** $OR = \dfrac{ad}{bc}$ (case-control studies), approximating RR when the disease is rare.
- **Absolute risk reduction** $ARR = \text{control event rate} - \text{treatment event rate}$; **number needed to treat** $NNT = 1/ARR$. The reciprocal of an absolute risk increase is the number needed to harm.
- **Prevalence** $\approx$ incidence $\times$ mean duration of disease (when stable).

### Inference

The **standard error of the mean** $SEM = SD/\sqrt{n}$. A 95 percent confidence interval for a mean is approximately $\bar{x} \pm 1.96 \times SEM$. If the 95 percent CI for a **difference** includes 0, or the CI for a **ratio** includes 1, the result is not statistically significant at $\alpha = 0.05$. A **type I error** ($\alpha$) rejects a true null hypothesis; a **type II error** ($\beta$) fails to reject a false one; **power** $= 1 - \beta$ and rises with larger sample size and larger effect size.

**Bias** examples: *selection bias* (nonrepresentative groups), *recall bias* (case-control), *lead-time bias* (earlier detection looks like longer survival without altering the course), *length-time bias* (screening finds slow-growing disease preferentially), *confounding* (a third variable linked to both exposure and outcome, addressed by randomization, matching or adjustment).

### Ethics

**Informed consent** requires disclosure of the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the proposed treatment, risks, benefits, alternatives (including no treatment), and the patient's voluntary agreement. **Capacity** is decision-specific and requires that the patient understand the information, appreciate its relevance to themselves, reason about options and express a consistent choice. A patient with capacity may refuse treatment, even life-saving treatment. Exceptions to consent include emergencies when the patient cannot consent and no surrogate is available.

Minors generally need a parent or guardian to consent, but in most states a minor may consent without a parent to care related to contraception, pregnancy, sexually transmitted infections and substance use (laws vary by state), and emancipated minors consent for themselves. If a patient lacks capacity and has no advance directive, a **surrogate** decides by substituted judgment, generally in the order of spouse, adult children, parents and siblings (the hierarchy varies by state).

**Confidentiality** can be broken to protect others when there is a serious and imminent threat to an identifiable person (Tarasoff duty), for reportable infectious diseases, and for suspected child or elder abuse. After a medical error, honest disclosure to the patient is expected.

### Development and grief

Typical milestones are social smile at about 2 months, sitting without support at about 6 months, walking at about 12 months and two-word phrases at about 2 years. Grief is not a fixed sequence; stages such as denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance can occur in any order.

@example
title: Test characteristics from a 2 by 2 table
problem: A screening test is evaluated in 300 patients. 90 patients with disease test positive and 10 with disease test negative. Of 200 patients without disease, 30 test positive and 170 test negative. Calculate sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV and the positive likelihood ratio.
step: Sensitivity $= 90/(90 + 10) = 90$ percent. Specificity $= 170/(170 + 30) = 85$ percent.
step: PPV $= 90/(90 + 30) = 75$ percent. NPV $= 170/(170 + 10) = 94.4$ percent.
step: $LR^+ = 0.90/(1 - 0.85) = 6$. A positive result raises the odds of disease sixfold.

@example
title: How prevalence changes PPV
problem: A test with 90 percent sensitivity and 90 percent specificity is applied to 1000 people with a disease prevalence of 10 percent. What is the PPV?
step: 100 have disease and 900 do not. True positives $= 0.9 \times 100 = 90$.
step: False positives $= 0.1 \times 900 = 90$.
step: $PPV = 90/(90 + 90) = 50$ percent. At a prevalence of 1 percent the PPV would be much lower, which is why screening low-risk populations produces many false positives.

@mistakes
- Using the column totals for PPV instead of the row of positive tests (PPV depends on the positives).
- Saying sensitivity and specificity change with prevalence; PPV and NPV change.
- Interpreting a nonsignificant result as proof of no effect when the study may have been underpowered.
- Breaking confidentiality for an adult patient's relatives without a legal or safety reason.

@practice num
prompt: In a trial, 12 percent of control patients and 8 percent of treated patients have the outcome. What is the number needed to treat to prevent one outcome?
answer: 25
check: 1 / (0.12 - 0.08)
tol: 0.5
hint: Find the absolute risk reduction first.
solution: $ARR = 0.12 - 0.08 = 0.04$, and $NNT = 1/0.04 = 25$.

@practice num
prompt: A test has a sensitivity of 80 percent and a specificity of 90 percent. What is the positive likelihood ratio?
answer: 8
check: 0.8 / (1 - 0.9)
tol: 0.05
hint: Positive likelihood ratio equals sensitivity divided by the false-positive rate.
solution: $LR^+ = 0.80/(1 - 0.90) = 0.80/0.10 = 8$.

@quiz num
prompt: A test has 95 percent sensitivity and 90 percent specificity. In a population with a disease prevalence of 2 percent, what is the positive predictive value? Give a decimal rounded to three places.
answer: 0.162
check: (0.95 * 0.02) / (0.95 * 0.02 + (1 - 0.90) * (1 - 0.02))
tol: 0.005
hint: Imagine 1000 people: how many have the disease, and how many without the disease test positive?
solution: Among 1000 people, 20 have disease and 19 test positive; of 980 without disease, 98 test positive. $PPV = 19/(19 + 98) = 0.162$.
