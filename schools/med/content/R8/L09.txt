@article
In neurology, **where is the lesion?** comes before **what is the lesion?** Sort the deficit into upper versus lower motor neuron, then cortical, brainstem, cord or peripheral, and name the vessel or structure. In psychiatry, anchor on **duration, function and exclusions**, then match the drug to the diagnosis and its characteristic toxicity.

### Stroke syndromes

- **Middle cerebral artery**: contralateral face and arm weakness and sensory loss; aphasia if the dominant hemisphere is involved, neglect if not.
- **Anterior cerebral artery**: contralateral leg weakness, abulia.
- **Posterior cerebral artery**: contralateral homonymous hemianopia with macular sparing.
- **Lateral medullary (Wallenberg) syndrome**, usually from the posterior inferior cerebellar or vertebral artery: ipsilateral facial pain and temperature loss, contralateral body pain and temperature loss, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, dysphagia and hoarseness, vertigo with ataxia.
- **Lacunar infarcts** from hypertensive small-vessel disease cause pure motor or pure sensory stroke.

### Spinal cord and peripheral nerve

**Brown-Sequard syndrome** (hemisection) causes ipsilateral weakness and loss of vibration and proprioception below the lesion, and contralateral loss of pain and temperature beginning one to two levels below. **Guillain-Barre syndrome** is ascending symmetric weakness with areflexia, often 1 to 3 weeks after infection (classically *Campylobacter jejuni*); cerebrospinal fluid shows high protein with a normal cell count (albuminocytologic dissociation). **Myasthenia gravis** has antibodies to the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptor, with fatigable ptosis and weakness and a possible thymoma; treat with pyridostigmine. **Lambert-Eaton** has antibodies to presynaptic calcium channels, associated with small cell lung cancer, and improves with repeated use.

### Neurodegeneration and demyelination

**Alzheimer disease**: amyloid plaques and tau tangles, hippocampal atrophy, reduced acetylcholine; cholinesterase inhibitors such as donepezil help symptoms. **Parkinson disease**: loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, with Lewy bodies containing alpha-synuclein; resting tremor, rigidity and bradykinesia; treat with levodopa and carbidopa. **Huntington disease**: CAG repeat expansion on chromosome 4 with caudate atrophy. **Multiple sclerosis**: autoimmune demyelination of CNS myelin, relapsing course, oligoclonal bands, periventricular plaques and internuclear ophthalmoplegia from a lesion of the medial longitudinal fasciculus.

### Intracranial pressure

$$CPP = MAP - ICP.$$

Normal ICP is about 5 to 15 mm Hg and CPP is generally kept at 60 mm Hg or more. Rising ICP produces headache, vomiting, papilledema and, late, the **Cushing triad** of hypertension, bradycardia and irregular respirations.

The **Glasgow Coma Scale** is the sum of eye opening (1 to 4), verbal response (1 to 5) and motor response (1 to 6), for a total of 3 to 15. A score of 8 or lower generally prompts airway protection.

### Seizure drugs and cerebrospinal fluid

First-line treatment of status epilepticus is a benzodiazepine. Phenytoin causes gingival hyperplasia and zero-order kinetics at high doses; carbamazepine causes hyponatremia and Stevens-Johnson syndrome and treats trigeminal neuralgia; valproate is a teratogen (neural tube defects) and hepatotoxic; ethosuximide treats absence seizures. In bacterial meningitis, CSF shows neutrophils, high protein and low glucose; in viral meningitis, lymphocytes, normal glucose and mildly high protein.

### Psychiatry

**Major depression** requires at least 2 weeks of depressed mood or anhedonia plus other symptoms; first-line therapy is an SSRI and psychotherapy. **Bipolar I** requires a manic episode lasting at least 1 week (or any duration if hospitalization is needed). **Lithium** has a narrow therapeutic index; adverse effects are tremor, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, hypothyroidism and teratogenicity (Ebstein anomaly). **Schizophrenia** requires 6 months of illness including at least 1 month of active symptoms. Clozapine can cause agranulocytosis and metabolic effects.

| Feature | Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Serotonin syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Trigger | Dopamine antagonist | Serotonergic drugs |
| Onset | Days | Within hours |
| Tone and reflexes | Lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia | Clonus, hyperreflexia |
| Other | Very high CK | Diarrhea, mydriasis, agitation |
| Treatment | Stop drug, supportive care, dantrolene or bromocriptine | Stop drug, benzodiazepines, cyproheptadine |

Alcohol withdrawal begins with tremor at 6 to 24 hours, seizures at 12 to 48 hours and delirium tremens at 48 to 96 hours; treat with benzodiazepines and give thiamine.

@example
title: Perfusion pressure after head injury
problem: A patient with a head injury has a blood pressure of 110/60 mm Hg and an intracranial pressure of 20 mm Hg. Calculate the cerebral perfusion pressure.
step: MAP $= 60 + (110 - 60)/3 = 60 + 16.7 = 76.7$ mm Hg.
step: $CPP = MAP - ICP = 76.7 - 20 = 56.7$ mm Hg.
step: This is below the usual goal of 60 or more, so treatment would target raising the MAP or lowering the ICP.

@example
title: Total Glasgow Coma Scale
problem: A patient opens his eyes only to pain, makes incomprehensible sounds and withdraws from pain. Find the GCS and decide whether airway protection is needed.
step: Eye opening to pain scores 2.
step: Incomprehensible sounds score 2, and withdrawal from pain scores 4.
step: Total $= 2 + 2 + 4 = 8$. A score of 8 or lower generally indicates the need for airway protection.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that facial pain and temperature loss in Wallenberg syndrome is ipsilateral while the body loss is contralateral.
- Reversing Brown-Sequard sensory modalities: vibration on the same side as the lesion, pain and temperature on the opposite side.
- Calling serotonin syndrome rigidity "lead-pipe"; it features clonus and hyperreflexia.
- Calculating cerebral perfusion pressure with systolic pressure instead of mean arterial pressure.
