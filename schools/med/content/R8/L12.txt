@article
Real Step 1 blocks are **mixed**. One item is renal, the next is pharmacology, then a biostatistics calculation, then a neurology vignette. Switching systems is part of the difficulty, so practice must also be mixed. This week you combine everything and learn to turn every block into a plan.

### A routine for every vignette

1. **Read the last line first.** Is the task a diagnosis, a mechanism, a next step, a drug adverse effect, a lab finding or a calculation?
2. **Read the stem with that task in mind.** Underline age, sex, time course, key vitals and the one or two decisive findings. Ignore filler.
3. **Predict the answer before reading the options.** A prediction protects you from attractive distractors.
4. **Match, then eliminate.** Cross out options that contradict a finding or answer a different question (for example a diagnosis when a next step was asked).
5. **Flag and move.** If you cannot decide in about 90 seconds, choose the best remaining option, flag it and continue.

### Integration across disciplines

Most vignettes require two or three linked facts. A confused man with alcohol use disorder and ophthalmoplegia needs thiamine (nutrition, neurology), and the missing cofactor explains reduced transketolase activity (biochemistry). A man with arthritis after urethritis has reactive arthritis, classically after *Chlamydia trachomatis* (or an enteric infection) (microbiology), associated with HLA-B27 (immunology). A patient started on rifampin who loses control of anticoagulation illustrates induction (pharmacology, hematology). When a question looks like it belongs to one system, ask what the mechanism links it to.

### Reviewing a block

Review **every** item, including the ones you got right by guessing. For each error, log the system, the discipline, the cause (knowledge gap, application or reasoning error, misread, timing) and a one-sentence rule. Then compute:

- Accuracy by block, to see trend: $\text{accuracy} = \dfrac{\text{correct}}{\text{total}} \times 100$.
- Pace: seconds per item overall and in each half of the block.
- Error share by system or cause: $\dfrac{\text{errors in a category}}{\text{total errors}} \times 100$.

### Repairing the weakest system

Rank systems by the number of errors, weighted by how heavily the content outline tests the area. Then match the remedy to the cause:

| Cause | Remedy |
|---|---|
| Knowledge gap | Rewrite as flash cards; retrieval practice with spaced intervals |
| Application error | Mixed vignettes that require the same decision; teach yourself the discriminating feature |
| Misread | Last-line-first routine; underline age, time course and the question word |
| Timing | Timed sets of 10 to 20 items with a pace target |

A repair week might follow this cycle: day 1 review of the missed items and flash cards for the weakest system, days 2 to 4 short mixed question sets in that system under time, day 5 a mixed block containing that system, day 6 error-log update, day 7 rest or light review. After the week, re-measure. If accuracy in that system has not improved, change the method.

### The final stretch

In the last weeks, take full-length timed practice, keep the same breakfast and sleep schedule you will use on exam day, and shift from learning new facts to consolidating weak points. Treat each block as data, not a verdict.

@example
title: Finding the weakest system
problem: Over three 40-item blocks, a student gets 24, 27 and 30 correct. Her 39 errors split as renal 11, cardiovascular 6, pharmacology 8, biochemistry 7 and other 7. Find her mean accuracy and the system with the largest error share, and say what to do first.
step: Total correct $= 24 + 27 + 30 = 81$ out of 120, so accuracy is $81/120 = 67.5$ percent.
step: Errors $= 120 - 81 = 39$, which matches the categories: $11 + 6 + 8 + 7 + 7 = 39$. Every error should carry a category.
step: Renal has the largest share, $11/39 = 28.2$ percent. Repair renal first, using the cause column of the log to choose the remedy.

@example
title: Adjusting pace mid-block
problem: A student has finished 25 of 40 items after 40 minutes in a 60-minute block. How many seconds can she spend on each remaining item?
step: Time left is $60 - 40 = 20$ minutes $= 1200$ seconds.
step: Items left are $40 - 25 = 15$.
step: $1200/15 = 80$ seconds per item. She is slightly under the 90-second average, so she should skip any long calculation and flag it.

@mistakes
- Reading the stem before the question, then answering a different question than the one asked.
- Changing correct answers on flagged items without a specific reason.
- Reviewing only incorrect items and missing guessed correct ones.
- Studying new content in the final days instead of repairing logged weak areas.

@practice mc
prompt: A 52-year-old man with chronic alcohol use disorder is brought in confused with an unsteady gait and horizontal nystagmus. Before giving intravenous dextrose, a clinician gives a vitamin. Which laboratory assay is a classic functional test for that vitamin's deficiency?
choice: Erythrocyte glutathione reductase activity
choice*: Erythrocyte transketolase activity
choice: Serum methylmalonic acid
choice: Erythrocyte aminolevulinate synthase activity
choice: Serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D
hint: The vitamin is thiamine. Which pentose phosphate pathway enzyme depends on it?
solution: Wernicke encephalopathy is treated with thiamine before glucose. Thiamine pyrophosphate is a cofactor for transketolase, so erythrocyte transketolase activity (with and without added cofactor) is a functional test. Glutathione reductase reflects riboflavin, and methylmalonic acid reflects B12.

@practice mc
prompt: A 45-year-old woman with rheumatoid arthritis taking weekly methotrexate develops mouth ulcers, macrocytic anemia and leukopenia. Which agent is most appropriate to reverse the toxicity?
choice: Mesna
choice: N-acetylcysteine
choice*: Leucovorin
choice: Dexrazoxane
choice: Vitamin K
hint: Methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase.
solution: Methotrexate blocks dihydrofolate reductase, depleting tetrahydrofolate. Leucovorin (folinic acid) bypasses the block. Mesna protects against cyclophosphamide cystitis, N-acetylcysteine treats acetaminophen overdose and dexrazoxane reduces anthracycline cardiotoxicity.

@practice self
prompt: A student completes a 40-item mixed block and logs 14 errors: renal and gastrointestinal 5 (3 knowledge gaps, 2 calculation mistakes), biochemistry and genetics 3 (all knowledge gaps), pharmacology 3 (all application errors), biostatistics 2 (both misreads of the question) and cardiovascular 1 (timing). Write a one-week repair plan that names the top two priorities and gives a specific remedy for each error cause.
hint: Rank categories by error count, then match each cause to its remedy.
solution: Top priorities are renal and gastrointestinal (5 errors, the largest share) and then biochemistry/genetics together with pharmacology (3 each). Knowledge gaps (6 of 14, in renal/GI and biochemistry) are fixed with flash cards for the missed facts and spaced retrieval on days 1, 3 and 7. Renal calculation mistakes are fixed by writing formulas on a sheet and doing five anion gap, Winter and fractional excretion problems per day. Pharmacology application errors need mixed vignettes that ask for the mechanism or adverse effect, with a written discriminating rule for each miss. Biostatistics misreads need a last-line-first routine and underlining the question word. The single timing error needs only a pace check, so it receives the lowest priority. End the week with a mixed block and re-measure each category.

prompt: A 25-year-old man has painful swelling of the right knee and left ankle 3 weeks after an episode of urethritis. He also has conjunctivitis. Which allele is most strongly associated with this condition?
choice: HLA-DR4
choice: HLA-DR3
choice*: HLA-B27
choice: HLA-DQ2
choice: HLA-DR2

solution: Reactive arthritis follows genitourinary (often Chlamydia trachomatis) or gastrointestinal infection and is associated with HLA-B27, like ankylosing spondylitis. HLA-DR4 is associated with rheumatoid arthritis and HLA-DQ2 with celiac disease.
