@article
The heart is a pump with valves, the lungs are an exchange surface. Most Step 1 items in these two systems ask you to reason about pressure, volume and flow, and then name the pathology that disturbs them.

### Hemodynamics

Cardiac output $CO = HR \times SV$. Stroke volume is $EDV - ESV$, and **ejection fraction** $EF = SV/EDV$ (normal is 55 percent or more). **Mean arterial pressure** is approximately

$$MAP = DBP + \tfrac{1}{3}(SBP - DBP),$$

because diastole occupies about two thirds of the cardiac cycle. Preload rises with venous return (Frank-Starling: more stretch gives more stroke volume, until failure). Afterload is the resistance the ventricle ejects against, such as aortic stenosis or hypertension.

### Coronary disease and myocardial infarction

The **left anterior descending** artery supplies the anterior wall and septum. The **right coronary** artery (in most people) supplies the inferior wall, the sinoatrial node and atrioventricular node, so inferior infarcts cause bradycardia and heart block. ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF indicates an inferior infarct. A right ventricular infarct (JVD, clear lungs, hypotension) is preload dependent: give fluids and avoid nitrates.

Complications follow a typical timeline: arrhythmias and heart failure in the first days; fibrinous pericarditis at 1 to 3 days; **papillary muscle rupture** (acute mitral regurgitation, large v waves) at 3 to 7 days; interventricular septal rupture and free wall rupture (tamponade) at 3 to 14 days; ventricular aneurysm from weeks; **Dressler syndrome** (autoimmune pericarditis) at weeks. The posteromedial papillary muscle is the one most prone to rupture because it has a single blood supply.

### Murmurs and maneuvers

- **Aortic stenosis**: crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border radiating to the carotids; angina, syncope and heart failure.
- **Mitral regurgitation**: holosystolic apical murmur radiating to the axilla.
- **Mitral stenosis**: opening snap and diastolic rumble; usually from rheumatic disease.
- **Aortic regurgitation**: early diastolic decrescendo murmur with a wide pulse pressure.

Maneuvers that **decrease** venous return (Valsalva strain, standing) quieten most murmurs. Squatting and leg raising increase venous return and make most murmurs louder. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and mitral valve prolapse are the main exceptions: they get louder with Valsalva and standing.

### Pulmonary physiology

**Obstructive** disease (asthma, COPD) reduces FEV1/FVC below 0.70, with air trapping. **Restrictive** disease (fibrosis, obesity, neuromuscular weakness) reduces total lung capacity with a normal or increased ratio. Emphysema also reduces the diffusing capacity (DLCO) because alveolar surface is destroyed.

Alveolar gas and gradients (at sea level on room air, water vapor pressure 47 mm Hg):

$$P_AO_2 = F_iO_2(P_{atm} - 47) - \frac{P_aCO_2}{0.8}, \qquad \text{A-a gradient} = P_AO_2 - P_aO_2.$$

Hypoventilation lowers $P_aO_2$ with a **normal** A-a gradient. V/Q mismatch, shunt and diffusion limitation widen it. Oxygen content is

$$C_aO_2 = 1.34 \times Hb \times S_aO_2 + 0.003 \times P_aO_2,$$

so hemoglobin and saturation, not dissolved oxygen, carry nearly all the oxygen. A pulmonary embolus classically causes dyspnea, pleuritic pain and tachycardia, with a widened A-a gradient and a V/Q mismatch (ventilation without perfusion).

@example
title: Mean arterial pressure and ejection fraction
problem: A patient has a blood pressure of 126/78 mm Hg, an end-diastolic volume of 140 mL and an end-systolic volume of 60 mL. Calculate the MAP and the ejection fraction.
step: MAP $= 78 + (126 - 78)/3 = 78 + 16 = 94$ mm Hg.
step: Stroke volume $= 140 - 60 = 80$ mL.
step: $EF = 80/140 = 0.571$, or about 57 percent, which is normal.

@example
title: Calculating an A-a gradient
problem: A patient breathing room air at sea level has $P_aO_2 = 60$ mm Hg and $P_aCO_2 = 40$ mm Hg. Calculate the A-a gradient.
step: Inspired $P_{O_2} =0.21 \times (760 - 47) = 149.7$ mm Hg.
step: $P_AO_2 = 149.7 - 40/0.8 = 149.7 - 50 = 99.7$ mm Hg.
step: Gradient $= 99.7 - 60 = 39.7$ mm Hg, which is wide and points to V/Q mismatch, shunt or diffusion defect rather than pure hypoventilation.

@mistakes
- Forgetting that the A-a gradient is normal in pure hypoventilation.
- Using $(SBP + DBP)/2$ for MAP; the diastolic weighting matters.
- Giving nitroglycerin to a patient with a right ventricular infarct.
- Memorizing that all murmurs decrease with Valsalva; hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the exception.

@practice num
prompt: A patient has a blood pressure of 138/72 mm Hg. Estimate the mean arterial pressure in mm Hg.
answer: 94
check: 72 + (138 - 72) / 3
tol: 0.5
hint: Add one third of the pulse pressure to the diastolic pressure.
solution: Pulse pressure is 66, one third is 22, and $72 + 22 = 94$ mm Hg.
unit: mm Hg

@practice num
prompt: A patient has an end-diastolic volume of 120 mL and an end-systolic volume of 70 mL. Calculate the ejection fraction as a percent, rounded to the nearest tenth.
answer: 41.7
check: (120 - 70) / 120 * 100
tol: 0.1
hint: Stroke volume is the difference of the two volumes.
hint: Divide stroke volume by end-diastolic volume.
solution: $SV = 50$ mL, $EF = 50/120 = 41.7$ percent, which is reduced.
unit: percent

@practice num
prompt: A patient breathing room air at sea level ($F_iO_2 = 0.21$, barometric pressure 760 mm Hg, water vapor pressure 47 mm Hg) has $P_aO_2 = 70$ mm Hg and $P_aCO_2 = 56$ mm Hg. Calculate the A-a gradient to the nearest tenth.
answer: 9.7
check: 0.21 * (760 - 47) - 56 / 0.8 - 70
tol: 0.5
hint: First find the alveolar oxygen pressure using the alveolar gas equation with a respiratory quotient of 0.8.
solution: $P_AO_2 = 149.7 - 56/0.8 = 149.7 - 70 = 79.7$. Gradient $= 79.7 - 70 = 9.7$ mm Hg, a normal-range value consistent with hypoventilation.
unit: mm Hg

@practice mc
prompt: A 63-year-old man has chest pain for 2 hours. His ECG shows ST elevation in leads II, III and aVF and in the right-sided leads. He is hypotensive with distended neck veins and clear lungs. Which of the following is most important to avoid?
choice*: Sublingual nitroglycerin
choice: Aspirin
choice: Intravenous isotonic saline bolus
choice: Unfractionated heparin
choice: Primary percutaneous coronary intervention
hint: This is a right ventricular infarct, which depends on preload.
solution: Nitrates lower preload and can cause profound hypotension in right ventricular infarction. Fluids, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation and reperfusion are appropriate.

@practice mc
prompt: A 70-year-old man has exertional syncope, angina and a crescendo-decrescendo systolic murmur at the right upper sternal border that radiates to the carotid arteries. Which maneuver would decrease the intensity of the murmur?
choice*: The strain phase of the Valsalva maneuver
choice: Squatting from a standing position
choice: Passive leg raise
choice: Rapid intravenous fluid bolus
hint: Think about what each maneuver does to venous return.
solution: Valsalva strain decreases venous return and the volume across the stenotic valve, so the murmur softens. Squatting, leg raise and fluid load increase venous return and make the murmur louder.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient has hemoglobin 14 g/dL, arterial oxygen saturation 0.97 and $P_aO_2$ 95 mm Hg. Calculate the arterial oxygen content in mL O2 per dL of blood, using 1.34 mL per gram of hemoglobin. Round to one decimal place.
answer: 18.5
check: 1.34 * 14 * 0.97 + 0.003 * 95
tol: 0.1
hint: Add hemoglobin-bound and dissolved oxygen.
solution: Bound $= 1.34 \times 14 \times 0.97 = 18.2$; dissolved $= 0.003 \times 95 = 0.29$; total about 18.5 mL/dL.
unit: mL/dL

@quiz mc
prompt: Five days after an inferior myocardial infarction, a 66-year-old woman develops acute dyspnea and pulmonary edema. A new loud holosystolic murmur is heard at the apex radiating to the axilla. Pulmonary capillary wedge tracing shows giant v waves. Which complication has most likely occurred?
choice: Rupture of the free wall of the left ventricle
choice: Rupture of the interventricular septum
choice*: Rupture of the posteromedial papillary muscle
choice: Dressler syndrome
choice: Left ventricular aneurysm
hint: Giant v waves reflect regurgitant flow into the left atrium.
solution: Papillary muscle rupture causes acute mitral regurgitation 3 to 7 days after infarction. The posteromedial papillary muscle has a single supply, usually from the right coronary artery. Free wall rupture causes tamponade, and septal rupture would show a step-up in right ventricular oxygen saturation.
