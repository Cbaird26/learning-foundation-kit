@article
USMLE Step 1 asks one question: can you apply foundational science to a patient in front of you? It is not a list-recall test. A typical item is a short clinical vignette followed by a question about mechanism, pathology, pharmacology, a calculation or the next best step. This course reviews the content, but its main job is to train the **cycle** that gets you through the exam: test, log, diagnose, repair, retest.

### Format and reporting

Step 1 is a computer-based exam given in one 8-hour testing day (including breaks) with up to 280 single-best-answer items, delivered in seven blocks of up to 40 items. Each block is timed at 60 minutes. Since January 2022 the result is reported as **pass/fail only**; no three-digit score is given. Pass/fail does not make the exam easier to take. It means there is no reward for being far above the standard, and no number to hide a weak area behind. Standards and details are revised from time to time, so confirm the current rules in the official content outline and sample questions before you plan.

Within a block you may flag items and move back and forth. When you end the block, you cannot return to it. Because 60 minutes divided by 40 items is 90 seconds per item, your target pace is about **90 seconds** a question, with time banked on fast items to spend on dense ones.

### How the content outline is organized

The outline is built on two axes. One axis is **general principles** that cut across organs: biochemistry and molecular biology, genetics, nutrition, immunology, microbiology, pathology, pharmacology and behavioral science including biostatistics and ethics. The other is the **organ systems**: blood and lymphoreticular, nervous system, cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, renal and urinary, endocrine, reproductive, musculoskeletal, skin, and multisystem disorders. Every vignette sits on both axes. A patient with hemolysis after an antimalarial is a hematology item and also a pathway item (the pentose phosphate pathway) and a pharmacology item.

### Why a diagnostic block comes first

Reading content you already know feels productive and is not. A **diagnostic block** (a timed, random, mixed set of about 40 items) tells you where you actually lose points. Take it under exam conditions: 60 minutes, no notes, no pausing.

### The error log

For every missed item, and for correct guesses, record:

1. **System** (cardiovascular, renal, and so on) and **discipline** (pharmacology, micro and so on).
2. **Cause of error**, chosen from a short list:
   - *Knowledge gap*: you did not know the fact.
   - *Reasoning or application error*: you knew the fact but could not apply it, for example you chose the diagnosis when the question asked for the next step.
   - *Misread*: you missed a keyword such as "most likely", "initial", "except" or the patient's age.
   - *Timing or fatigue*: you rushed or ran out of time.
3. **The rule** in one sentence you would write on a flash card.

Causes matter because they need different treatments. Knowledge gaps need retrieval practice on the missing facts. Reasoning errors need more vignettes that force the same decision. Misreads need a reading routine (read the last line first, circle the age and time course). Timing errors need pacing drills.

### Turning the log into a plan

Count errors per system and per cause, then rank. Spend the most time where errors are most frequent **and** the topic is heavily represented in the outline. Use **active recall** (answering questions, blank-page recall) rather than rereading, and **spaced repetition** (revisiting a missed fact after 1 day, 3 days, 1 week and so on). Interleave systems rather than blocking one subject for days, because mixed practice mirrors the exam. Schedule full-length timed practice late in the plan to build stamina, and protect sleep.

Always re-test. A plan is a hypothesis: if a system's accuracy does not rise after a week of work, change the method, not only the hours.

@example
title: Reading a diagnostic block
problem: On a 40-item timed diagnostic block you answer 28 correctly. Your 12 errors are: 5 knowledge gaps, 4 reasoning errors, 2 misreads and 1 timing error. Find your percent correct and the share of errors that are knowledge gaps, then decide what to do first.
step: Percent correct is $28/40 = 0.70$, or 70%.
step: Errors total $5 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 12$, which matches $40 - 28$. Knowledge gaps are $5/12 \approx 41.7\%$ of errors.
step: Reasoning plus misread errors are $4 + 2 = 6$ of 12, half of all errors. Those are not fixed by reading more content.
step: Plan: fix the 5 knowledge gaps with flash-card retrieval, and add vignette practice that asks for the next best step plus a last-line-first reading routine to address the 6 process errors.

@example
title: Checking pace mid-block
problem: After 36 minutes of a 40-item, 60-minute block you have finished 20 items. How many seconds per item have you used so far, and how many seconds per item remain for the other 20 items?
step: Time used per item so far: $36 \times 60 / 20 = 108$ seconds, which is slower than the 90-second target.
step: Time left is $60 - 36 = 24$ minutes $= 1440$ seconds for 20 items, or $1440/20 = 72$ seconds each.
step: You must now average 72 seconds per item, about a third less time per item than you have been using. Answer easy items at once, flag long calculations, and return to flagged items only if time remains.

@mistakes
- Using a diagnostic block as a content review and checking notes during it, which hides the real pace and accuracy.
- Logging only what the right answer was, not why you missed it, so every error looks like a knowledge gap.
- Spending most of the plan on favorite subjects because they feel productive, instead of on the highest error counts.
- Rereading explanations passively instead of writing a one-sentence rule and retesting after a delay.
