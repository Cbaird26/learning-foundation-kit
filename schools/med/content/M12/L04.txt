@article
The HEENT examination is a series of small, focused examinations linked by an inspect-then-test routine. The head and neck are rich in clinical information: the eye is the only place where nerves and small vessels can be seen directly, and the ear, nose and throat are where infection and obstruction most often present.

### The eye

**Visual acuity** is tested with a Snellen chart at 20 feet (6 m), each eye separately with correction. "20/40" means the patient sees at 20 feet what a person with normal vision sees at 40 feet; normal is 20/20. Use a near card or finger counting if the chart cannot be read. **Visual fields** are tested by confrontation: face the patient and compare their fields with yours, testing each quadrant. A bitemporal hemianopia suggests a chiasmal lesion such as a pituitary tumor; a homonymous hemianopia suggests a lesion behind the chiasm.

**Pupils**: look at size, symmetry and reaction to light (direct and consensual) and accommodation. In the **swinging flashlight test**, move the light from one eye to the other every 2 to 3 seconds. In a **relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)** the pupils constrict less (and so appear to dilate) when the light swings to the affected eye, because the afferent input from that optic nerve is weaker than from the other side. It indicates optic nerve or severe retinal disease, for example optic neuritis.

**Extraocular movements** are tested by following a finger in an "H" pattern. Remember **LR6 SO4 and the rest are 3**: the lateral rectus is supplied by cranial nerve (CN) VI, the superior oblique by CN IV, and all others by CN III. A CN III palsy gives an eye that is "down and out" with ptosis, and a dilated pupil when parasympathetic fibers on the surface of the nerve are compressed (for example, by a posterior communicating artery aneurysm). A microvascular palsy (diabetes, hypertension) often spares the pupil. CN VI palsy causes failure to abduct the eye and horizontal diplopia.

**Fundoscopy**: look for the red reflex, then the optic disc (sharp margins, cup-to-disc ratio), vessels and retina. **Papilledema** (bilateral disc swelling from raised intracranial pressure) shows blurred margins, an elevated disc and loss of spontaneous venous pulsations.

### The ear

Use an otoscope, pulling the pinna up and back in adults to straighten the canal. The normal tympanic membrane (TM) is pearly gray and translucent, with the malleus handle, umbo and a cone of light. A **bulging, red, opaque TM with reduced mobility** on pneumatic otoscopy suggests acute otitis media.

Use a 512 Hz tuning fork for hearing:

| Result | Conductive loss | Sensorineural loss |
|---|---|---|
| Weber | Lateralizes to the **affected** (worse) ear | Lateralizes to the **better** ear |
| Rinne | Bone conduction louder than air conduction on the affected side | Air conduction louder than bone, though both reduced |

Causes of conductive loss include wax, otitis media and otosclerosis; sensorineural loss arises from presbycusis, noise or cochlear/nerve disease.

### Nose, mouth and throat

Inspect the nasal septum and turbinates. Examine the lips, buccal mucosa, gums, teeth and tongue, then the oropharynx using a tongue depressor: look at tonsil size (graded 0 to 4+), exudates, uvula position and palatal movement (CN X). The **Mallampati** class describes how much of the pharynx is visible, which helps predict a difficult airway.

### The neck

Palpate lymph nodes (preauricular to supraclavicular), assess the trachea (midline?) and examine the **thyroid** from behind while the patient swallows water: note size, nodules and tenderness. The carotid arteries are auscultated for bruits only where indicated, and the jugular venous pressure is assessed in the cardiovascular examination.

### Integrating the vital signs

A single abnormal vital sign matters less than the pattern. A patient with fever, tachycardia, tachypnea and low BP has a systemic inflammatory response and possibly sepsis. For the quick bedside **qSOFA** score, one point each is given for respiratory rate 22 or above, systolic BP 100 or below, and altered mentation; a score of 2 or more identifies higher risk of poor outcome and should prompt urgent assessment (qSOFA is a prompt, not a diagnosis).

@example
title: Interpreting a swinging flashlight test
problem: When a light shines in a man's right eye both pupils constrict briskly. When the light swings to his left eye both pupils dilate slightly. Which side has the defect, and what is the nature of it?
step: A pupil that dilates when the light arrives means the eye receiving the light delivers less afferent signal than the eye that was just stimulated.
step: The paradoxical dilation occurs on swinging to the left eye, so the left afferent pathway (optic nerve or severe retinal disease) is abnormal.
step: This is a left RAPD. The efferent limb is intact because both pupils react, so the problem is not CN III.

@example
title: Weber and Rinne
problem: A man with hearing loss in the right ear has a Weber test that lateralizes to the right ear. On Rinne testing in the right ear, bone conduction is heard longer than air conduction. What type of loss is this?
step: Weber localizes to the right (the affected side): that fits a conductive loss in the right ear, or a sensorineural loss in the left ear.
step: Rinne in the right ear is abnormal (bone louder than air), which means the conduction of sound through the ear canal and middle ear is impaired on the right.
step: Together: right conductive hearing loss. Look for wax, effusion or perforation with the otoscope.

@mistakes
- Describing RAPD as "the affected pupil is dilated"; the baseline pupil sizes are equal and the defect is only revealed by the swing.
- Saying Weber lateralizes to the "good" ear in conductive loss; it lateralizes to the ear with the conductive loss.
- Pulling the pinna down in an adult, which does not straighten the canal.
- Palpating the thyroid without asking the patient to swallow.

solution: Model answer: Vitals: BP 88/54 mmHg, HR 124/min, RR 26/min, T 39.1 °C (102.4 °F), SpO2 90% on room air. MAP = $54 + (88 - 54)/3 = 65.3$ mmHg (borderline). Interpretation: fever, tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension, hypoxemia and altered mentation indicate a systemic inflammatory response with probable sepsis and shock physiology, likely from pneumonia; this needs immediate escalation, oxygen, cultures, fluids and antibiotics according to local protocol.
rubric: The vital signs are written with numbers and units, and the temperature is identified as a fever.
rubric: MAP (about 65 mmHg), shock index (about 1.4) and qSOFA (3) are calculated correctly.
