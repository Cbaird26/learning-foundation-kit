@article
A medical case presentation is a short, structured story that lets a listener understand the patient, share your reasoning and help decide what to do next. The note is a permanent record that the entire team, the patient and often the courts will read. Both use the same skeleton, tuned for different audiences: the oral presentation is selective, and the note is complete.

### The oral presentation

A standard presentation, which a student can deliver in about **three minutes**, runs in this order:

1. **Identifying statement (one-liner)**: age, sex, relevant background, the main problem and its time course. "Mr. K is a 58-year-old man with hypertension and a 30 pack-year smoking history who presents with 3 weeks of exertional chest pressure."
2. **Chief complaint** (when not in the one-liner) and **HPI**: a chronological story using OLDCARTS, including pertinent positives and negatives, prior episodes and treatments.
3. **Pertinent** past history, medications, allergies, family and social history, and review of systems. "Pertinent" means relevant to the problem you are presenting.
4. **Vital signs and examination**: begin with vitals and general appearance, then pertinent systems. Give numbers.
5. **Data**: laboratory values, ECG, imaging, with trends.
6. **Assessment**: a **summary statement** (a one- to two-sentence problem representation), then a **prioritized differential diagnosis** with reasons for and against each.
7. **Plan**: by problem, with tests, treatments, consultations, patient education and disposition.

Practical advice: speak rather than read; use past tense for history and present tense for current findings; give the most important items first; say "no" rather than long lists of negatives; if you do not know something, say so. Expect interruptions and questions; they are part of teaching. For follow-up (daily) presentations, give a one-liner and the **interval history** (events overnight), vitals, examination, new data and an updated plan by problem.

### Problem representation

The **summary statement** replaces a list of facts with a pattern: it uses **semantic qualifiers**, which are paired descriptors such as acute versus chronic, constant versus intermittent, localized versus diffuse, febrile versus afebrile. "A 24-year-old man with 18 hours of migrating periumbilical-to-right-lower-quadrant pain, anorexia and low-grade fever with focal peritoneal signs" invites the diagnosis of appendicitis. A good statement is brief, specific and includes the features that discriminate between diagnoses.

### The SOAP note

| Section | Contents | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ubjective | What the patient reports: CC, HPI, ROS, PMH, medications, allergies, social and family history | "Exertional chest pressure, relieved by rest in 5 minutes" |
| **O**bjective | What you measure or observe: vitals, examination, labs, imaging, ECG | "BP 142/88, HR 78; no murmurs" |
| **A**ssessment | Your synthesis: summary statement and differential, ranked | "Stable angina most likely" |
| **P**lan | Actions by problem | "Stress test, aspirin, follow-up in 1 week" |

A common error is to put an interpretation in the wrong section: a report of "chest pain" is subjective, while "tender to palpation of the chest wall" is objective. Distinguish a **symptom** (reported) from a **sign** (observed).

### Documentation safety and professionalism

Notes should be timely, legible, signed, dated and honest. Do not copy forward old findings you did not re-examine. Avoid the abbreviations on the Joint Commission "Do Not Use" list: "U" for units (mistaken for 0 or 4), "IU", "Q.D." and "Q.O.D.", a **trailing zero** ("5.0 mg" can be misread as 50 mg), a missing **leading zero** (".5 mg" can be misread as 5 mg), and "MS", "MSO4", "MgSO4". Keep patient information private and share it only with those who need it.

@example
title: Writing a one-liner
problem: A 34-year-old woman with asthma comes to the emergency department with 2 days of fever and productive cough, and today she has right-sided pleuritic chest pain. Her temperature is 38.9 °C. Write a one-liner.
step: Start with the identifying data and the relevant background: "34-year-old woman with asthma."
step: Add the main problem and the time course, with the most discriminating features: "presenting with 2 days of fever and productive cough and new right pleuritic chest pain."
step: Final: "A 34-year-old woman with asthma presents with 2 days of fever, productive cough and right pleuritic chest pain." Keep it to a single sentence.

@example
title: Sorting data into SOAP
problem: Classify each as S, O, A or P: (1) "I've felt dizzy when I stand." (2) BP 104/62 supine and 82/50 standing. (3) Orthostatic hypotension, likely from volume depletion. (4) Give 1 L of normal saline and stop the diuretic.
step: The patient's own statement of dizziness is a symptom, so it is Subjective (S).
step: The measured blood pressures are an examination finding, so they are Objective (O).
step: The diagnosis and its cause are the clinician's interpretation, so they are Assessment (A); the fluids and stopping the drug are actions, so they belong in the Plan (P).

@mistakes
- Reading the entire note aloud instead of giving a three-minute, selective presentation.
- Putting the diagnosis in the HPI ("the patient presented with a heart attack") instead of describing the symptoms.
- Writing "5.0 mg" or ".5 mg", which invites tenfold dosing errors.
- Listing a differential diagnosis with no reasoning for or against each item.

@practice self
prompt: Write a one-line summary and a brief SOAP note for this case. A 45-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes reports 4 days of burning on urination, urinary frequency and suprapubic discomfort without fever, flank pain or vaginal discharge. Temperature is 37.1 °C, HR 82 per minute, BP 126/78 mmHg. She is tender over the suprapubic area, with no costovertebral angle tenderness. Urinalysis shows positive leukocyte esterase and nitrite, and she has no known drug allergies.
solution: Model answer: One-liner: A 45-year-old woman with type 2 diabetes presents with 4 days of dysuria, frequency and suprapubic discomfort without fever or flank pain. S: 4 days of dysuria, frequency, suprapubic discomfort; no fever, flank pain or vaginal discharge; type 2 diabetes; NKDA. O: T 37.1 °C, HR 82, BP 126/78; suprapubic tenderness, no CVA tenderness; urinalysis positive for leukocyte esterase and nitrite. A: Acute uncomplicated (or, because of diabetes, possibly complicated) cystitis most likely; pyelonephritis unlikely without fever or flank pain; vaginitis or STI less likely without discharge. P: Send urine culture; start empiric antibiotic chosen from current local guidelines and her allergy status; hydrate; counsel on symptoms of pyelonephritis; review glycemic control; follow up if no improvement in 48 to 72 hours.
rubric: The one-liner includes age, sex, relevant background, main problem and time course.
rubric: Subjective and objective data are separated correctly and each contains the relevant facts.
rubric: The assessment ranks cystitis first with reasons and names at least one alternative; the plan is by problem.
hint: Subjective is what she tells you; objective is what you measure.
hint: Rank the diagnosis, then give one reason for and one against alternatives.

@quiz mc
prompt: During a presentation of a 52-year-old man with 2 days of fever and productive cough, who has focal crackles at the right base and no pleuritic pain, edema or orthopnea, the attending asks, "What is your differential?" Which response is best?
choice: "He has pneumonia."
choice: "I don't have a differential until the chest radiograph returns."
