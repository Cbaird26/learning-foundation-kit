@article
Musculoskeletal complaints account for a large share of primary care visits. The examination tests structure (bone, joint, soft tissue), function (range of motion, strength) and the nerves that supply them. The sequence for each joint is the same: **look, feel, move**, followed by **special tests** and an assessment of the joints above and below.

### Look, feel, move

- **Look**: deformity, swelling, erythema, scars, muscle wasting, skin changes and how the patient moves.
- **Feel**: temperature (warmth indicates inflammation or infection), tenderness along the joint line, effusion, and whether swelling is bony or soft tissue. Always check the unaffected side first and watch the patient's face.
- **Move**: **active** range of motion (the patient moves), then **passive** (you move). Pain on active but not passive movement suggests a tendon or muscle problem; restriction on both suggests a joint problem. Record motion in degrees with a goniometer; full extension is $0^\circ$ (the neutral zero method). Test power with the MRC scale.

The **GALS** screen (Gait, Arms, Legs, Spine) takes about two minutes and asks three screening questions about pain, stiffness and function, then observes gait, spine, arms, hands and legs.

### Inflammatory versus mechanical (degenerative) pain

| Feature | Inflammatory (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis) | Mechanical (osteoarthritis) |
|---|---|---|
| Morning stiffness | More than 1 hour | Under 30 minutes |
| Effect of activity | Improves | Worsens, relieved by rest |
| Joints | MCP, PIP, wrists, symmetric; spares DIP | DIP (Heberden nodes), PIP (Bouchard nodes), first CMC, knees, hips |
| Systemic features | Fatigue, weight loss, elevated inflammatory markers | None |

An acutely red, hot, swollen, painful single joint with fever is **septic arthritis** until proved otherwise and requires urgent arthrocentesis; gout (needle-shaped, negatively birefringent urate crystals) and pseudogout (rhomboid, positively birefringent calcium pyrophosphate crystals) are the main crystal causes.

### Special tests

**Knee.** Check for an effusion (patellar tap, bulge sign). **Lachman** (best test for the anterior cruciate ligament, ACL: anterior translation of the tibia on the femur at 20 to 30 degrees of flexion), anterior drawer (ACL), posterior drawer (PCL), **valgus stress** (medial collateral ligament), varus stress (lateral collateral ligament) and **McMurray** (meniscal tear: click or pain as the flexed knee is rotated and extended).

**Shoulder.** **Neer** and **Hawkins-Kennedy** (subacromial impingement), the **empty-can (Jobe) test** (supraspinatus), drop-arm test (large rotator cuff tear) and the apprehension test (anterior instability).

**Wrist and hand.** **Phalen** (flexed wrists for 60 seconds) and **Tinel** (tapping over the median nerve) reproduce median nerve symptoms in **carpal tunnel syndrome** (tingling in the thumb, index, middle and radial half of the ring finger, with thenar wasting late). **Finkelstein** test: de Quervain tenosynovitis.

**Hip.** **Thomas test** (fixed flexion deformity), **FABER** (hip or sacroiliac pain), and **Trendelenburg** (weak gluteus medius: the pelvis drops on the side opposite the weak stance leg). **Spine and nerve roots.** The **straight leg raise (SLR)** reproduces sciatic pain radiating below the knee between about 30 and 70 degrees and indicates L5 or S1 radiculopathy (commonly disc herniation); the femoral stretch test indicates upper lumbar roots.

**Ankle.** The **Ottawa ankle rules** reduce unnecessary radiographs: obtain an ankle series if there is pain in the malleolar region and either bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of either malleolus, or inability to bear weight for four steps both immediately and in the clinic. The **Thompson test** (squeezing the calf produces no plantar flexion) indicates Achilles tendon rupture.

### Red flags in back pain

New back pain with **saddle anesthesia, urinary retention or incontinence, or bilateral leg weakness** suggests cauda equina syndrome, which is a surgical emergency requiring urgent MRI. Other red flags: history of cancer, fever, major trauma, age over 50 with new pain, and progressive neurological deficit.

@example
title: Range of motion as an arc
problem: A man has a knee that can be fully straightened to $0^\circ$ on the right. On the left, the knee lacks $10^\circ$ of full extension (a flexion contracture) and can bend to $120^\circ$. What are the arcs of motion for each knee?
step: Using the neutral zero method, the right knee moves from $0^\circ$ to its maximum flexion; the left from $10^\circ$ (its limit of extension) to $120^\circ$.
step: Left arc $= 120 - 10 = 110^\circ$.
step: If the right knee flexes to $135^\circ$, its arc is $135^\circ$, so the left is restricted by $25^\circ$. Record the extension lag as well as the arc, because the lag affects walking.

@example
title: Choosing a test for a twisting knee injury
problem: A 22-year-old footballer felt a "pop" while pivoting and the knee swelled within an hour. Which ligament is likely injured and which test is most sensitive?
step: A pop with pivoting and rapid hemarthrosis (swelling within hours) suggests ACL rupture.
step: The Lachman test is more sensitive than the anterior drawer test because it is less limited by hamstring spasm and pain.
step: A positive Lachman test (increased anterior translation with a soft end-point) supports ACL injury, and MRI confirms it.

@mistakes
- Testing only the painful joint and not the joints above and below (hip pain may present as knee pain).
- Interpreting any swelling as arthritis without looking for bony versus soft tissue swelling.
- Reading morning stiffness of 20 minutes as inflammatory arthritis.
- Missing cauda equina syndrome by not asking about bladder, bowel and saddle sensation in back pain.

prompt: Describe how you would examine the knee of a 30-year-old with a swollen, painful knee after a twisting injury. Use look, feel, move, then name the special tests for the ACL, the medial collateral ligament and the menisci, and describe what a positive test looks like.
solution: Model answer: Look: compare both knees standing and lying for swelling, bruising, deformity and wasting of the quadriceps. Feel: warmth, joint-line tenderness, effusion (patellar tap or bulge sign). Move: active then passive flexion and extension, recording the arc and any extension lag; check the hip above and the ankle below. Special tests: Lachman (ACL): with the knee flexed 20 to 30 degrees, pull the tibia forward while stabilizing the femur; excess forward translation with a soft end-point is positive. Valgus stress (MCL): apply a valgus force at 0 and 30 degrees; excessive opening of the medial joint line indicates MCL injury. McMurray (meniscus): flex the knee and rotate the tibia while extending; a click or pain at the joint line suggests a meniscal tear.
