@article
Communication is a clinical skill with measurable effects: better adherence, fewer complaints, better recall, and greater patient and clinician satisfaction. Good communication is learned deliberately, in the same way as a physical examination maneuver.

### Empathy

**Empathy** is understanding a patient's experience and feelings and communicating that understanding. It differs from **sympathy** (feeling sorry for someone) and from reassurance. Patients often give "empathic opportunities": indirect emotional cues ("I just can't stop thinking about it"). The **NURSE** mnemonic lists responses:

- **N**ame the emotion ("It sounds like you are frightened.")
- **U**nderstand ("Anyone in your position would be worried.")
- **R**espect ("You have been managing this on your own for a long time.")
- **S**upport ("I will be with you through this.")
- **E**xplore ("Tell me more about what worries you most.")

Common blocks to empathy: premature reassurance ("Don't worry, it will be fine"), changing the subject, giving advice before exploring, and writing in the chart while the patient is speaking.

### Plain language, health literacy and teach-back

Many adults have limited health literacy. Use short words ("high blood pressure" rather than "hypertension"), chunk information into two or three points, and then use **teach-back**: "I want to be sure I explained clearly. Can you tell me in your own words how you will take this medicine?" Teach-back tests the clinician's explanation, not the patient's intelligence.

### Interpreters

When a patient has limited proficiency in the language of the clinic, use a **qualified medical interpreter** (in person, video or phone), not a family member, especially a child, and not an untrained bilingual staff member. Brief the interpreter, sit facing the patient, speak directly to the patient ("What brings you in?"), use short sentences, and ask the interpreter to translate everything.

### Breaking bad news: SPIKES

Bad news is information that seriously and adversely alters a patient's view of the future. **SPIKES** is a six-step protocol:

1. **S**etting: private, seated, no interruptions, phone silenced, support person invited if the patient wishes.
2. **P**erception: "What is your understanding of why we did the scan?"
3. **I**nvitation: "How much would you like me to tell you about the results?"
4. **K**nowledge: give a **warning shot** ("I am afraid the news is not good"), then share information in small chunks without jargon, and pause.
5. **E**motions with empathy: observe the reaction, name it, respond (NURSE), and allow silence.
6. **S**trategy and **S**ummary: once the patient is ready, outline the next steps, check understanding, and arrange follow-up.

Order matters. If you give the plan (step 6) before the emotion has been acknowledged (step 5), the patient usually cannot absorb the information.

### Cultural humility and other situations

Ask about preferences rather than assuming: in some families, patients prefer that relatives receive information first. Check, in line with local law and the patient's wishes. After an error or unexpected harm, disclosure also follows a protocol of honest explanation, apology and plan. Looking after yourself after hard conversations is part of professional practice.

@example
title: Responding to an emotional cue
problem: A 50-year-old woman says, after you explain that her biopsy shows cancer, "I knew it. My sister died from this." Give an empathic response that uses the NURSE structure.
step: Name the emotion: "You sound frightened, and it sounds like this is bringing back memories of your sister."
step: Understand and respect: "That is a very hard thing to carry, and it makes sense that you are worried."
step: Support: "I want you to know you will not go through this alone. We will plan every step together."
step: Explore: "Can you tell me what worries you most right now?" Then stop and listen, rather than moving straight to the treatment plan.

@mistakes
- Responding to a patient's distress with immediate reassurance or information ("Don't worry, we have good treatments") instead of acknowledging the emotion first.
- Using a family member, especially a child, as interpreter.
- Delivering a long monologue of results with jargon and no pauses, so the patient cannot take in the information.
- Asking "Do you understand?" instead of using teach-back; patients nearly always say yes.
