@article
Clinical reasoning is the thinking that turns a story, examination and tests into a diagnosis and plan. Experienced clinicians use two modes. **System 1** is fast pattern recognition ("I have seen this before") and **System 2** is slow, analytic reasoning. System 1 is efficient but can fail when a case is atypical; System 2 is reliable but effortful. Good clinicians use both, and slow down when a pattern does not fit.

### From data to a differential

1. **Collect and cluster** the important features: the most discriminating symptoms, signs and results.
2. **Create a problem representation** with semantic qualifiers: "a 34-year-old woman on estrogen-containing contraception with sudden-onset pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea and tachycardia after a long flight."
3. **Generate hypotheses** by matching the representation to **illness scripts**, mental models of a disease's typical epidemiology, pathophysiology and presentation. Use a framework to avoid missing categories, for example anatomy (organs in the region), physiology, or the VINDICATE mnemonic (Vascular, Infectious, Neoplastic, Degenerative, Iatrogenic/intoxication, Congenital, Autoimmune, Trauma, Endocrine/metabolic).
4. **Prioritize**: list the **most likely** diagnosis, the **most dangerous** ("can't-miss") diagnoses that must be excluded, and the **most treatable**. For acute chest pain, for example, can't-miss diagnoses include acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, tension pneumothorax and esophageal rupture.
5. **Test** hypotheses with targeted history, examination and investigations, and **revise**.

Occam's razor (prefer a single diagnosis) is helpful in young patients; in older patients with multiple conditions, Hickam's dictum ("a patient can have as many diseases as they please") is a useful reminder.

### Probability and Bayes

The **pretest probability** is how likely the disease is before the test, based on prevalence and the clinical picture. A test result changes it to the **post-test probability**.

$$\text{LR}^+ = \frac{\text{sensitivity}}{1 - \text{specificity}}, \qquad \text{LR}^- = \frac{1 - \text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}$$

$$\text{pretest odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad \text{post-test odds} = \text{pretest odds} \times \text{LR}, \qquad p_{\text{post}} = \frac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}$$

Rough guide to the impact of a positive result: LR$^+$ above 10 gives large, often conclusive shifts; 5 to 10 moderate; 2 to 5 small; 1 to 2 minimal. A test with an LR of 1 gives no information. A highly **sensitive** test, when negative, helps rule out disease (**SnNout**); a highly **specific** test, when positive, helps rule it in (**SpPin**). The **positive predictive value** depends on prevalence:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sensitivity} \times \text{prevalence}}{\text{sensitivity} \times \text{prevalence} + (1 - \text{specificity})(1 - \text{prevalence})}.$$

The **test threshold** and **treatment threshold** describe the probabilities below which no further testing is needed and above which treatment is warranted; testing is most useful between them.

### Cognitive biases

Diagnostic errors are more often errors of thinking than of knowledge.

- **Anchoring**: clinging to the first impression despite new data.
- **Premature closure**: accepting a diagnosis before it is verified and stopping the search.
- **Confirmation bias**: seeking only data that support the favored diagnosis.
- **Availability bias**: judging a diagnosis as likely because recent or memorable cases come to mind.
- **Diagnostic momentum**: accepting a label that previous clinicians attached.
- **Framing effect**: being swayed by how the case is presented (for example, "frequent flier" or "drug seeker").

Strategies: a **diagnostic time-out** ("What else could this be? What doesn't fit? What is the worst thing it could be?"), considering the opposite, using checklists and decision rules, reflecting on feedback, and asking for a second opinion.

### Defending a differential

When you present, give your summary statement, then a ranked differential, each with evidence for and against, and state your next step. Be willing to say what finding would change your mind.

@example
title: Updating a probability with a likelihood ratio
problem: A patient has a pretest probability of 30% for a disease. A test has LR$^+$ = 5 and the result is positive. What is the post-test probability?
step: Convert to odds: $0.30/0.70 = 0.4286$.
step: Multiply by the likelihood ratio: $0.4286 \times 5 = 2.143$.
step: Convert back: $2.143/(1 + 2.143) = 0.682$, or about 68%.

@example
title: Ranking a can't-miss diagnosis
problem: A 66-year-old man with hypertension has sudden, severe tearing chest pain radiating to his back. Blood pressure is 186/104 mmHg in the right arm and 148/90 mmHg in the left arm. What is the leading diagnosis, and why does it outrank more common causes of chest pain?
step: The sudden maximal onset, tearing character, radiation to the back, hypertension and the blood pressure difference between the arms form the illness script of acute aortic dissection.
step: Acute coronary syndrome is more common, but it does not explain the inter-arm pressure difference or the tearing pain.
step: Dissection is rapidly fatal and treatable, so it must be pursued immediately (CT angiography) while avoiding anticoagulants and thrombolytics until it is excluded.

@mistakes
- Treating the pretest probability as unimportant: a positive test for a rare disease is often a false positive.
- Calculating LR$^+$ as sensitivity divided by specificity instead of by (1 minus specificity).
- Anchoring on the first diagnosis and explaining away the findings that do not fit.
- Listing a differential without ranking or reasons.

@practice num
prompt: The pretest probability of pulmonary embolism in a patient is 20%. A test with a positive likelihood ratio of 6 returns positive. What is the post-test probability, as a percentage?
answer: 60
check: (0.20/0.80 * 6) / (1 + 0.20/0.80 * 6) * 100
tol: 0.5
unit: %
hint: Convert the probability to odds, multiply by the likelihood ratio, then convert back.
hint: Pretest odds are 0.2/0.8 = 0.25.
solution: Pretest odds $= 0.25$; post-test odds $= 0.25 \times 6 = 1.5$; probability $= 1.5/2.5 = 0.60$, or 60%.

Her HR is 112 per minute, BP 118/74 mmHg, RR 24 per minute, temperature 37.4 °C and SpO2 93% on room air. Her right calf is swollen and tender. Lungs are clear and heart sounds are normal. Write a one-line summary statement, a ranked differential of four diagnoses with one supporting and one opposing point for each, and your next steps.
solution: Model answer: Summary: A 34-year-old woman on a combined oral contraceptive with sudden pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, tachycardia, tachypnea and hypoxemia after prolonged travel, with a swollen tender calf. Differential: (1) Pulmonary embolism: sudden onset, pleurisy, hypoxemia, risk factors (estrogen, immobility) and probable DVT; against: nothing major; the clear lungs are typical. (2) Pneumothorax: sudden pleuritic pain and dyspnea; against: breath sounds are normal and the calf swelling is unexplained. (3) Pneumonia: pleuritic pain; against: no fever, cough or crackles and the onset was abrupt. (4) Acute coronary syndrome or pericarditis: chest pain; against: age, pleuritic pattern, and the calf findings; ECG and troponin would help. Next steps: oxygen as needed, assess pretest probability (Wells score is high), CT pulmonary angiography (D-dimer is not appropriate for high probability), start anticoagulation without delay if probability is high and imaging is delayed, bilateral leg ultrasound, ECG, chest radiograph, and stop estrogen therapy.
rubric: The summary statement is one sentence with risk factors, time course and key positive findings.
rubric: The differential is ranked, with pulmonary embolism first and at least one for/against point for each diagnosis.
rubric: The next steps include appropriate imaging and treatment and note that a D-dimer is not suitable for a high pretest probability.
hint: Which diagnosis explains the risk factors, the pleurisy, the hypoxemia and the swollen calf together?

@quiz num
prompt: A screening test has a sensitivity of 90% and a specificity of 95%. In a population with a disease prevalence of 10%, what is the positive predictive value, as a percentage rounded to one decimal place?
answer: 66.7
check: (0.90 * 0.10) / (0.90 * 0.10 + (1 - 0.95) * (1 - 0.10)) * 100
tol: 0.2
unit: %
hint: Imagine 1000 people: how many truly have the disease and test positive, and how many do not have it but test positive?
hint: With 1000 people, 100 have the disease and 900 do not.
solution: True positives: $0.90 \times 100 = 90$. False positives: $0.05 \times 900 = 45$. PPV $= 90/(90 + 45) = 0.667$, or 66.7%.
