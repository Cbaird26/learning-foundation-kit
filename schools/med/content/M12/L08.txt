@article
The neurological examination asks two questions: **is there a deficit**, and **where is the lesion**? Localization uses the anatomy you already know: cortex, subcortical white matter, brainstem, cerebellum, spinal cord, nerve roots, peripheral nerves, neuromuscular junction and muscle. Findings are combined into a single, anatomically coherent lesion.

### Components

1. **Mental status**: level of consciousness, orientation, attention, memory, language and mood. Screening tools include the MoCA and MMSE. The **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** sums three scores: eye opening (4 spontaneous, 3 to voice, 2 to pain, 1 none), verbal response (5 oriented, 4 confused, 3 inappropriate words, 2 incomprehensible sounds, 1 none) and motor response (6 obeys, 5 localizes, 4 withdraws, 3 abnormal flexion, 2 extension, 1 none). The total ranges from 3 to 15, and 8 or less is a conventional threshold for considering airway protection.
2. **Cranial nerves**: I smell; II acuity, fields, fundi, pupillary light reflex (afferent limb); III, IV, VI eye movements; V facial sensation and jaw muscles; VII facial expression; VIII hearing; IX, X palate and gag; XI trapezius and sternocleidomastoid; XII tongue. In an **upper motor neuron (UMN)** facial palsy the forehead is spared (bilateral cortical input), whereas in a **lower motor neuron (LMN)** palsy such as Bell palsy the whole face is weak. A protruded tongue deviates **toward** the side of a hypoglossal (LMN) lesion.
3. **Motor**: inspect for atrophy and fasciculations, test tone, power and reflexes. Power uses the MRC scale: 0 none, 1 flicker, 2 movement with gravity eliminated, 3 against gravity, 4 against some resistance, 5 normal.
4. **Reflexes**: biceps C5–6, brachioradialis C6, triceps C7, patellar L3–4, Achilles S1; graded 0 to 4+. A plantar response with an upgoing great toe (**Babinski sign**) is abnormal after infancy.
5. **Sensation**: pain and temperature (spinothalamic tract), vibration, proprioception and fine touch (dorsal columns). **Romberg test**: loss of balance with eyes closed but not open indicates impaired proprioception (sensory ataxia).
6. **Coordination**: finger-to-nose, heel-to-shin, rapid alternating movements (dysdiadochokinesia), nystagmus and gait. **Cerebellar signs are ipsilateral to the cerebellar hemisphere lesion.**
7. **Gait**: spastic (stiff, circumduction), ataxic (broad-based), parkinsonian (shuffling, small steps), steppage (foot drop), waddling (proximal weakness).

### UMN versus LMN

| Feature | Upper motor neuron lesion | Lower motor neuron lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Increased (spasticity, clasp-knife) | Decreased (flaccid) |
| Reflexes | Increased | Decreased or absent |
| Atrophy and fasciculations | Absent or mild late disuse atrophy | Marked |
| Plantar response | Upgoing (Babinski) | Normal or absent |
| Pattern of weakness | Pyramidal distribution | Muscle, root or nerve distribution |

An acute UMN lesion (stroke, spinal cord injury) may initially cause flaccid weakness with hyporeflexia (shock) before spasticity develops.

### Localizing

- **Cortex or internal capsule**: contralateral face and limb weakness with UMN signs; cortical findings include aphasia (dominant hemisphere) and neglect (non-dominant).
- **Brainstem**: crossed signs (cranial nerve deficit on one side and long tract signs on the other).
- **Spinal cord**: a sensory level, UMN signs below it, and bladder dysfunction. **Brown-Séquard syndrome** (hemisection): ipsilateral weakness and loss of vibration and position sense below the lesion, with contralateral loss of pain and temperature (the spinothalamic tract crosses within the cord).
- **Peripheral nerve or root**: LMN signs and sensory loss in the nerve or dermatome distribution; a symmetric stocking pattern suggests polyneuropathy (diabetes is the commonest cause).
- **Neuromuscular junction**: fatigable weakness (myasthenia gravis).

@example
title: Adding up a Glasgow Coma Scale
problem: A man found after a fall opens his eyes only in response to a sternal rub, speaks in confused sentences, and pushes the examiner's hand away from the painful stimulus. What is his GCS?
step: Eye opening to pain scores 2.
step: Confused conversation scores verbal 4; localizing (reaching to the stimulus) scores motor 5.
step: Total $= 2 + 4 + 5 = 11$, which is written GCS 11 (E2 V4 M5).

@example
title: Localizing weakness with a spared forehead
problem: A 67-year-old woman has sudden weakness of the lower left face (the forehead wrinkles normally) and left arm, with brisk left arm reflexes and an upgoing left plantar response. Where is the lesion?
step: Forehead sparing means a UMN pattern of facial weakness, since the upper face has bilateral cortical input.
step: Hyperreflexia and a Babinski sign confirm UMN signs.
step: The corticobulbar and corticospinal tracts cross, so left-sided UMN signs localize to the right cerebral hemisphere or internal capsule above the facial nucleus; a right middle cerebral artery stroke is a likely cause.

@mistakes
- Calling a facial palsy that spares the forehead a Bell palsy; Bell palsy is an LMN lesion that weakens the forehead too.
- Assuming that cerebellar signs appear on the opposite side to the lesion.
- Testing reflexes with the patient's limb tense; relaxation and reinforcement (Jendrassik) are needed.
- Summing GCS components incorrectly: the minimum is 3 (not 0).

@practice self
prompt: Localize the findings from a neurological examination. For each patient, name the likely location of the lesion and one reason: (a) A 60-year-old with diabetes has loss of vibration and proprioception in both feet, an absent ankle reflex and a positive Romberg test. (b) A man stabbed in the back has right leg weakness and loss of position sense in the right leg, and loss of pain and temperature in the left leg. (c) A woman has an intention tremor and dysmetria of the right arm, past-pointing on the right and a wide-based gait with falls to the right.
solution: Model answer: (a) Peripheral sensory neuropathy (large fiber), possibly with dorsal column involvement; absent ankle reflex and distal loss of vibration and proprioception in a stocking pattern fit diabetic polyneuropathy, and Romberg is positive because of loss of proprioceptive input. (b) Right hemisection of the spinal cord (Brown-Séquard): the corticospinal tract and dorsal column (both uncrossed in the cord) are affected on the right, and the spinothalamic fibers, which cross within one or two segments, are affected on the left. (c) Right cerebellar hemisphere, because the signs are ipsilateral to the lesion and include intention tremor, dysmetria and a wide-based gait.

rubric: The Brown-Séquard pattern identifies ipsilateral motor and proprioceptive loss with contralateral pain and temperature loss.
rubric: The learner states that cerebellar signs are ipsilateral.
hint: Ask which tract carries each modality and where it crosses.
hint: Cerebellar hemisphere signs appear on the same side as the lesion.
