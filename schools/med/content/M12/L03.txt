@article
Vital signs are the quickest objective summary of physiology: **blood pressure (BP), heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), temperature (T)** and **oxygen saturation (SpO2)**. Many services also record pain score, weight and height. Abnormal vital signs often detect deterioration before a patient looks ill, so every set must be interpreted, not simply recorded.

### Typical adult values

| Sign | Typical resting adult range | Abnormal |
|---|---|---|
| Heart rate | 60 to 100 per minute | Bradycardia below 60; tachycardia above 100 |
| Respiratory rate | 12 to 20 per minute | Tachypnea above 20 |
| Temperature | about 36.5 to 37.5 °C (97.7 to 99.5 °F) | Fever 38.0 °C (100.4 °F) or higher; hypothermia below 35 °C (95 °F) |
| SpO2 on room air | 95% or above | Below 94 to 95% needs explanation |
| Blood pressure | below 120/80 mmHg | See categories below |

Convert temperatures with $°F = \tfrac{9}{5}\,°C + 32$.

### Measuring blood pressure

Correct technique matters because small errors misclassify patients. The patient should sit quietly for about five minutes, back supported, feet flat, legs uncrossed, bare arm supported at heart level, with no caffeine or smoking in the previous 30 minutes. The **cuff bladder should encircle at least 80% of the arm**. A cuff that is too small gives falsely **high** readings and one that is too large gives falsely low ones. Take at least two readings a minute apart and average them; at the first visit measure both arms and use the arm with the higher reading.

The 2017 ACC/AHA categories (other bodies, for example some European guidelines, use somewhat different thresholds):

- **Normal**: systolic below 120 and diastolic below 80
- **Elevated**: systolic 120 to 129 and diastolic below 80
- **Stage 1 hypertension**: systolic 130 to 139 or diastolic 80 to 89
- **Stage 2 hypertension**: systolic 140 or higher, or diastolic 90 or higher

Diagnosis requires elevated averaged readings on at least two occasions (or confirmation with out-of-office monitoring). **Orthostatic hypotension** is a fall of at least 20 mmHg in systolic or at least 10 mmHg in diastolic pressure within 3 minutes of standing. **Pulsus paradoxus** is an inspiratory fall in systolic pressure greater than 10 mmHg, classically seen in cardiac tamponade and severe asthma.

### Derived values

$$\text{pulse pressure} = \text{SBP} - \text{DBP}$$

$$\text{MAP} \approx \text{DBP} + \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{SBP} - \text{DBP})$$

MAP estimates the average driving pressure for organ perfusion; a MAP of at least 65 mmHg is a common minimum target in shock. Pulse pressure widens in aortic regurgitation and narrows in tamponade and severe aortic stenosis.

$$\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)}}{\text{height (m)}^2}$$

with adult categories underweight below 18.5, normal 18.5 to 24.9, overweight 25 to 29.9, obesity 30 or above (BMI has limits: it does not distinguish muscle from fat). The **shock index** is $\text{HR}/\text{SBP}$; normal is roughly 0.5 to 0.7, and values above about 0.9 to 1 suggest hemodynamic compromise.

### Pulse, respirations, temperature, oximetry

Palpate the radial pulse for 15 to 30 seconds if it is regular (then multiply) and a full minute if irregular, and note rate, rhythm and volume. Count respirations for a full 30 to 60 seconds while appearing to still take the pulse, because patients change breathing when they know it is counted. Core temperature is best reflected by rectal, tympanic or temporal measures depending on setting. Pulse oximetry can overestimate saturation in patients with darker skin pigmentation and is falsely normal in carbon monoxide poisoning.

### The general survey

The general survey is your first impression: apparent age versus stated age, level of consciousness, **distress** (respiratory, pain), **nutrition and body habitus**, hygiene and dress, posture and gait, speech, odors (ketones, alcohol), skin color (pallor, jaundice, cyanosis) and affect. State it as descriptive sentences: "A thin man who appears older than his stated age, sitting upright and speaking in short phrases, mildly short of breath at rest."

@example
title: Pulse pressure and mean arterial pressure
problem: A patient's BP is 148/92 mmHg. Calculate the pulse pressure and MAP, and classify the reading.
step: Pulse pressure: $148 - 92 = 56$ mmHg.
step: MAP: $92 + \tfrac{1}{3}(56) = 92 + 18.7 = 110.7$ mmHg.
step: Classify: systolic 148 is at least 140 and diastolic 92 is at least 90, so a single reading in the stage 2 range. It is confirmed only with repeated readings.

@example
title: Converting a temperature
problem: A child's thermometer reads 101.3 °F. What is the temperature in degrees Celsius, and is it a fever?
step: Rearrange the formula: $°C = (°F - 32) \times \tfrac{5}{9}$.
step: Compute: $(101.3 - 32) \times \tfrac{5}{9} = 69.3 \times 0.5556 = 38.5$ °C.
step: This is above 38.0 °C, so it meets the usual definition of fever.

@mistakes
- Using a standard cuff on a large arm, which overestimates blood pressure.
- Diagnosing hypertension from a single reading taken while the patient is anxious, talking or has a full bladder.
- Counting respirations in an obvious way so that the patient alters the rate.
- Calculating MAP as the simple average of SBP and DBP instead of weighting diastole twice as much as systole.

@practice num
prompt: A trauma patient has HR 118 per minute and BP 92/58 mmHg. Calculate the shock index, rounded to two decimal places.
answer: 1.28
check: 118 / 92
tol: 0.01
hint: The shock index is heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure.
hint: Use the systolic value of 92.
solution: $118/92 = 1.28$. This is well above 0.9 to 1, suggesting hemodynamic compromise from blood loss.
