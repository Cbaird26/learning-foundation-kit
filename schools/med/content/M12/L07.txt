@article
The abdomen contains the gastrointestinal tract, liver, biliary tree, pancreas, spleen, kidneys, bladder, great vessels and, in women, the reproductive organs, so a pain or mass can come from many systems. Examination is organized by **quadrant** (right upper, left upper, right lower, left lower) or the nine regions.

### Preparation

Ask the patient to empty the bladder, lie flat with one pillow and arms by the sides, and expose from the xiphoid to the pubic symphysis (maintain dignity with drapes). Warm your hands, keep your face on the patient's face for signs of pain, and ask the patient to point to the **painful area, which you examine last**.

### Order: look, listen, percuss, feel

Unlike other systems, **auscultation comes before percussion and palpation** because touching may change bowel activity.

**Inspection.** Note contour, scars, distension, visible peristalsis, hernias, striae, jaundice and veins. Distension is classically caused by the "five Fs": fat, fluid, flatus, feces, fetus. In chronic liver disease look for spider angiomata, palmar erythema, caput medusae, gynecomastia, ascites and asterixis. Periumbilical (Cullen) and flank (Grey Turner) bruising suggest retroperitoneal hemorrhage, as in severe pancreatitis.

**Auscultation.** Listen for 1 to 2 minutes. Normal bowel sounds are commonly quoted as about 5 to 34 per minute (sources vary). High-pitched, tinkling or rushes of sound suggest early mechanical obstruction; silence after careful listening suggests ileus or peritonitis. Listen for aortic and renal artery bruits.

**Percussion.** Tympany over gas-filled bowel and dullness over solid organs, fluid or a full bladder. Percuss the **liver span** (normally roughly 6 to 12 cm in the midclavicular line). **Shifting dullness** (flank dullness that moves when the patient rolls onto their side) indicates free fluid.

**Palpation.** Begin superficially away from the painful area, then palpate deeply. Look for tenderness, **guarding** (voluntary or involuntary contraction of abdominal muscles), **rigidity** (a board-like abdomen that signals peritonitis) and **rebound tenderness** (pain on sudden release of pressure). Palpate the liver edge from the right iliac fossa upward with inspiration, and the spleen from the right iliac fossa toward the left costal margin; a palpable spleen usually means enlargement of at least about two to three times. Test for a pulsatile, expansile aortic mass (abdominal aortic aneurysm). Check for hernias with a cough.

### Classic signs

| Sign | Technique | Suggests |
|---|---|---|
| **Murphy sign** | Palpate right upper quadrant while the patient inhales; inspiration stops because of pain | Acute cholecystitis |
| **McBurney point tenderness** | Pressure one-third of the way from the anterior superior iliac spine to the umbilicus | Appendicitis |
| **Rovsing sign** | Palpating the left lower quadrant causes right lower quadrant pain | Appendicitis |
| **Psoas sign** | Pain on extension of the right hip | Retrocecal appendicitis |
| **Courvoisier law** | A palpable, non-tender gallbladder with painless jaundice | Malignant biliary obstruction (pancreatic head cancer) rather than gallstones |
| **Costovertebral angle tenderness** | Percuss the flank | Pyelonephritis |

### Ascites and SAAG

Fluid in the peritoneal cavity (ascites) causes bulging flanks, shifting dullness and a fluid wave. Paracentesis fluid is characterized by the **serum-ascites albumin gradient**:

$$\text{SAAG} = \text{serum albumin} - \text{ascitic fluid albumin}.$$

A SAAG of **at least 1.1 g/dL** indicates portal hypertension (cirrhosis, heart failure, Budd-Chiari syndrome); a SAAG below 1.1 g/dL points to causes such as peritoneal carcinomatosis, tuberculosis or nephrotic syndrome. An ascitic fluid neutrophil count (absolute) of **250 cells/mm$^3$ or more** diagnoses spontaneous bacterial peritonitis.

### Putting it together

Ask where pain began and how it moved: appendicitis typically starts as periumbilical pain with anorexia and migrates to the right lower quadrant. A rigid abdomen, absent bowel sounds or an expansile pulsatile mass with hypotension are emergencies.

@example
title: Serum-ascites albumin gradient
problem: A 55-year-old man with alcohol-related cirrhosis has a paracentesis. Serum albumin is 2.8 g/dL and ascitic fluid albumin is 1.1 g/dL. Calculate the SAAG and interpret it.
step: Subtract: $2.8 - 1.1 = 1.7$ g/dL.
step: A SAAG of at least 1.1 g/dL indicates portal hypertension.
step: This fits cirrhosis, the most common cause. A low-albumin, low-gradient ascites would have prompted a search for peritoneal disease.

@example
title: An evening with right upper quadrant pain
problem: A 40-year-old woman has 12 hours of right upper quadrant pain after a fatty meal, with fever. During right subcostal palpation she stops inhaling abruptly. What is the sign, and what is the likely diagnosis?
step: Inspiration moves the inflamed gallbladder onto the examining hand, causing sharp pain that makes her stop breathing in.
step: This is Murphy sign.
step: With fever and postprandial right upper quadrant pain it suggests acute cholecystitis, usually from a gallstone obstructing the cystic duct; ultrasound is the first imaging test.

@mistakes
- Palpating before auscultating, which may alter bowel sounds, and examining the painful area first.
- Reading a palpable gallbladder with painless jaundice as gallstone disease; Courvoisier law points to malignancy.
- Mistaking voluntary guarding for peritonitis; involuntary rigidity persists when the patient is distracted.
- Calculating SAAG with ascitic albumin minus serum albumin, which gives a negative number.
