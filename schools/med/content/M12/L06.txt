@article
The respiratory examination tests how well air moves into the lungs and how the lung and pleural space transmit sound. The same four steps are used for every system: **inspection, palpation, percussion, auscultation**. Compare left and right at every level, and examine the back as well as the front, because the lung fields lie largely posteriorly.

### Inspection

Look from the end of the bed. Note respiratory rate, effort, the use of accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoids), pursed-lip breathing, ability to speak full sentences, and chest shape: a **barrel chest** is seen in emphysema. Look at the hands for **clubbing** (loss of the angle between nail and nail fold), tar staining, cyanosis and a flapping tremor of CO$_2$ retention. Clubbing occurs in lung cancer, bronchiectasis, interstitial lung disease, cyanotic congenital heart disease, endocarditis, inflammatory bowel disease and cirrhosis, but **not in uncomplicated COPD**.

### Palpation

Check that the **trachea** is central. It is pushed away from a tension pneumothorax or large effusion and pulled toward a collapsed lung or fibrosis. Assess **chest expansion** (the examiner's thumbs move apart by about 3 to 5 cm with deep inspiration; reduced expansion on one side localizes disease). **Tactile fremitus** (vibration felt when the patient says "ninety-nine") is increased over consolidation, which conducts sound well, and decreased by air or fluid in the pleural space.

### Percussion

Place the middle finger of the non-dominant hand flat on the chest and strike its middle phalanx with the tip of the middle finger of the other hand. Normal lung is **resonant**. **Dullness** indicates a solid or fluid-filled structure beneath (consolidation, effusion, mass), and **stony dullness** a large pleural effusion. **Hyperresonance** indicates excess air (pneumothorax, emphysema). Diaphragmatic excursion normally spans about 3 to 5 cm.

### Auscultation

Use the diaphragm, with the patient breathing deeply through the mouth.

- **Vesicular** breath sounds are soft and heard throughout the peripheral lung, with inspiration longer than expiration.
- **Bronchial** breath sounds are loud and high-pitched with a pause between phases and expiration as long as inspiration; abnormal over peripheral lung, where they indicate consolidation.
- **Crackles** (discontinuous, brief). Fine late-inspiratory crackles ("Velcro") occur in pulmonary fibrosis; fine bibasal crackles occur in pulmonary edema; coarse crackles occur with secretions (pneumonia, bronchiectasis).
- **Wheeze** (continuous, high-pitched, typically expiratory) indicates narrowed lower airways (asthma, COPD).
- **Stridor** is a harsh inspiratory sound over the upper airway and signals a potentially life-threatening obstruction.
- A **pleural friction rub** (creaking, in both phases) indicates pleural inflammation.

**Vocal resonance**: over consolidation, speech is louder and clearer (bronchophony, whispered pectoriloquy) and a spoken "ee" sounds like "ay" (**egophony**).

### Patterns of disease

| Condition | Trachea | Fremitus | Percussion | Breath sounds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lobar consolidation | Central | Increased | Dull | Bronchial, crackles, egophony |
| Large pleural effusion | Away from effusion | Decreased | Stony dull | Decreased or absent |
| Pneumothorax | Central (away if tension) | Decreased | Hyperresonant | Decreased or absent |
| Lobar collapse (atelectasis) | Toward collapse | Decreased | Dull | Decreased |
| Asthma/COPD | Central | Normal or decreased | Normal or hyperresonant | Prolonged expiration with wheeze |

### What changes breath sounds?

Sound is transmitted from the airways to the chest wall through lung tissue. Consolidation (fluid-filled alveoli) transmits high frequencies better, so sounds are louder and bronchial. Air or fluid in the pleural space and hyperinflation separate lung from stethoscope and reduce sound. Airway narrowing causes wheeze, and a blocked bronchus causes absent sounds over collapsed lung.

### Bedside measurements

Count the respiratory rate for 30 seconds and double it (or count a full minute). Spirometry gives the **FEV$_1$/FVC ratio**; a ratio below 0.70 after bronchodilator indicates airflow obstruction.

@example
title: Calculating the respiratory rate
problem: You count 13 breaths in 30 seconds. What is the respiratory rate per minute, and is it normal?
step: Multiply the count by 2 to convert 30 seconds to 60 seconds: $13 \times 2 = 26$ breaths per minute.
step: The usual adult range is 12 to 20 per minute, so 26 is tachypnea.
step: Look for a cause: fever, hypoxemia, pain, anxiety, metabolic acidosis, pulmonary embolism, or heart failure.

@example
title: Putting the findings together
problem: A 66-year-old man with 3 days of fever and cough has dullness to percussion, increased tactile fremitus, bronchial breath sounds and crackles over the right lower lobe posteriorly. The trachea is central. What is the diagnosis?
step: Dullness and increased fremitus mean the lung tissue is solid and conducts sound well.
step: Bronchial breath sounds and egophony support air-filled bronchi surrounded by fluid-filled alveoli, and the central trachea shows no volume change in the chest.
step: This pattern is lobar consolidation, most commonly bacterial pneumonia.

@mistakes
- Examining only the front of the chest; most of the lower lobes are posterior.
- Confusing consolidation (increased fremitus, dull) with effusion (decreased fremitus, stony dull).
- Saying the trachea deviates toward a pneumothorax; with tension it is pushed away.
- Assuming clubbing is a sign of COPD.

@practice self
prompt: Describe breath sounds and what changes them. For each of the following patients, state the expected tactile fremitus, percussion note and breath sounds, and explain in one sentence why: (a) a 60-year-old with right lower lobe pneumonia; (b) a 25-year-old tall, thin man with a spontaneous pneumothorax; (c) a 30-year-old with an acute asthma exacerbation.
solution: Model answer: (a) Pneumonia: increased tactile fremitus, dull percussion, bronchial breath sounds with crackles and egophony, because consolidated lung conducts sound better than air-filled lung. (b) Pneumothorax: decreased or absent tactile fremitus, hyperresonant percussion, and decreased or absent breath sounds, because air in the pleural space separates the lung from the chest wall and transmits sound poorly. (c) Asthma: normal or slightly decreased fremitus, normal or hyperresonant percussion, and diffuse expiratory wheeze with prolonged expiration, because narrowed airways make turbulent flow during exhalation.
rubric: Each of the three scenarios has fremitus, percussion and breath sounds stated correctly.
rubric: Each explanation names the physical reason: solid lung, air in the pleural space, or narrowed airways.
rubric: The learner distinguishes continuous (wheeze) from discontinuous (crackles) added sounds.
hint: Ask whether the tissue between the airway and the stethoscope conducts sound better or worse than normal.
hint: Wheeze comes from airway narrowing.
