@article
The history is the single most productive part of the encounter. In many outpatient studies and in classic teaching, the history alone (or the history plus examination) points to the final diagnosis in the majority of patients, and tests mostly confirm or refine what the history suggested. Your job in the interview is twofold: build a **relationship** and gather **accurate data**. The two reinforce each other, because patients disclose more to clinicians who listen.

### Opening the encounter

Introduce yourself with your name and role, confirm the patient's name and how they wish to be addressed, explain what you are going to do, wash your hands, and make sure the patient is comfortable and the room is private. Then ask an open-ended opener such as "What brings you in today?" This elicits the **chief complaint (CC)**: the reason for the visit, ideally in the patient's own words. Let the patient speak. Studies of clinic visits find that clinicians interrupt after roughly 20 seconds on average, and that patients who are allowed to finish usually do so within about two minutes. Then ask "Anything else?" until the patient's agenda is complete, and negotiate priorities if there are many concerns.

### The funnel

Start broad and narrow down.

- **Open-ended questions** ("Tell me about the pain") invite narrative and surface the unexpected.
- **Focused questions** ("Where exactly does it hurt?") narrow to a topic.
- **Closed questions** ("Does it radiate to your jaw?") confirm or exclude a specific feature and have yes/no answers.
- **Facilitation** (nodding, "go on", silence), **clarification** ("When you say dizzy, do you mean spinning or about to faint?") and **summarizing** keep the story accurate.

Avoid **leading questions** ("The pain goes down your left arm, doesn't it?"), which suggest the expected answer, and avoid **multiple questions at once** and medical jargon.

### History of the present illness (HPI)

The HPI is a chronological story of the chief complaint. Two common mnemonics characterize a symptom, especially pain:

- **OLDCARTS**: Onset, Location, Duration, Character, Aggravating/Alleviating factors, Radiation, Timing, Severity.
- **SOCRATES**: Site, Onset, Character, Radiation, Associations, Time course, Exacerbating/relieving factors, Severity.

Always add associated symptoms, pertinent negatives (symptoms whose absence helps rule out a diagnosis), prior episodes, treatments tried, and the patient's own ideas, concerns and expectations.

### The rest of the history

| Section | Contents |
|---|---|
| Past medical and surgical history (PMH/PSH) | Diagnoses, hospitalizations, operations, immunizations |
| Medications | Prescription, over-the-counter, supplements, doses, adherence |
| Allergies | Drug and the **reaction** (rash vs anaphylaxis vs intolerance) |
| Family history | Illnesses in first-degree relatives, with ages at onset |
| Social history | Occupation, living situation, tobacco, alcohol, other substances, sexual history, travel, diet, exercise |
| Review of systems (ROS) | Brief head-to-toe screen of symptoms not yet covered |

### Quantifying tobacco

**Pack-years** estimates cumulative smoking exposure:

$$\text{pack-years} = \frac{\text{cigarettes per day}}{20} \times \text{years smoked}.$$

One pack contains 20 cigarettes. Pack-years matter because they drive screening decisions (for example, eligibility for lung cancer screening with low-dose CT is based partly on pack-years and years since quitting) and risk estimates for COPD and cardiovascular disease. Ask also about alcohol in a non-judgmental way; a "standard drink" in the United States contains about 14 g of ethanol.

### Close the interview

Summarize back what you heard, ask "Is there anything I missed?", explain the next steps, and thank the patient.

@example
title: Calculating pack-years from a smoking history
problem: A 60-year-old woman says she smoked 10 cigarettes a day from age 25 to age 55 and then quit. How many pack-years is that?
step: Convert cigarettes to packs: 10 cigarettes per day is $10/20 = 0.5$ pack per day.
step: Find the years smoked: age 25 to age 55 is $30$ years.
step: Multiply: $0.5 \times 30 = 15$ pack-years.
step: Record it with the quit date, for example "15 pack-years, quit 5 years ago", because time since quitting also matters for risk.

@example
title: Filing information in the right section
problem: During an interview a man says: "My father had a heart attack at 52." Where does this belong, and what follow-up would you ask?
step: A heart attack in a parent is a statement about a relative, so it belongs in the **family history**, not the PMH.
step: Premature coronary disease in a male first-degree relative is defined as onset before age 55 (before 65 in a female relative), so the age of 52 is the key detail to record.
step: Follow up with open then closed questions: "Tell me about other heart problems in your family", then ask about siblings, other parents' relatives, and any sudden deaths.

@mistakes
- Interrupting the opening statement or asking a closed question first, which anchors the interview to your own hypothesis and hides the patient's agenda.
- Recording "NKDA" without asking about the reaction, or mixing an intolerance (nausea) with an allergy (hives, angioedema).
- Calculating pack-years from cigarettes per day without dividing by 20, or counting years since quitting as years smoked.
- Putting the family history or social history inside the HPI rather than in its own section.

@practice self
prompt: A 55-year-old man reports chest pain that began an hour ago. Write eight focused questions, in a sensible order, that characterize the pain using OLDCARTS and then screen for important associated symptoms.
solution: Model answer: (1) "When did the pain start, and what were you doing?" (onset) (2) "Can you point to where it hurts?" (location) (3) "How would you describe the pain: pressure, sharp, burning, tearing?" (character) (4) "Does it spread anywhere, such as your arm, jaw or back?" (radiation) (5) "Is it constant or does it come and go, and how long does each episode last?" (duration, timing) (6) "On a scale of 0 to 10, how bad is it?" (severity) (7) "Does anything make it better or worse: exertion, rest, breathing, position, meals?" (aggravating and alleviating factors) (8) "Do you also have shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, palpitations or fainting?" (associated symptoms).
rubric: Questions are open or focused rather than leading and cover all OLDCARTS elements.
rubric: Associated symptoms include dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea or syncope.
rubric: The order moves from broad to specific and each question asks only one thing.
hint: Go through the letters of OLDCARTS one at a time.
hint: Then add pertinent associated symptoms such as dyspnea and sweating.

@quiz num
prompt: A man smoked 2 packs per day from age 18 to age 30, then 1 pack per day from age 30 to age 55. How many total pack-years did he smoke?
answer: 49
check: 2 * (30 - 18) + 1 * (55 - 30)
tol: 0.01
unit: pack-years
hint: Calculate each smoking period separately.
hint: The first period is 12 years at 2 packs per day; the second is 25 years at 1 pack per day.
solution: $2 \times 12 + 1 \times 25 = 24 + 25 = 49$ pack-years.
