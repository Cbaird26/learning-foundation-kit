@article
The skin is the largest organ, and it is the easiest to examine. Many systemic diseases show in the skin and nails, and the lymph nodes drain it. A precise description is the key skill: it lets you communicate with colleagues, build a differential diagnosis and decide on a biopsy.

### Examining the skin

Use good light, expose the whole skin when possible (with consent and a chaperone offered), and include the scalp, nails, mucosa and between the toes. **Inspect first, then palpate** (texture, temperature, tenderness, depth, and whether the lesion **blanches** with pressure).

### Primary lesions

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Macule** | Flat color change, under 1 cm |
| **Patch** | Flat color change, 1 cm or larger |
| **Papule** | Raised solid lesion under 1 cm |
| **Plaque** | Raised, flat-topped lesion, 1 cm or larger |
| **Nodule** | Raised, deeper solid lesion, 1 cm or larger, in dermis or subcutis |
| **Vesicle** | Fluid-filled blister under 1 cm |
| **Bulla** | Fluid-filled blister, 1 cm or larger |
| **Pustule** | Pus-filled lesion |
| **Wheal** | Transient, edematous plaque (urticaria) |

Non-blanching red or purple spots are **petechiae** (about 3 mm or less) or **purpura** (larger), indicating bleeding into the skin.

### Secondary changes

**Scale** (flaking stratum corneum), **crust** (dried serum or blood), **erosion** (loss of epidermis, heals without scarring), **ulcer** (loss through the dermis, heals with scarring), **fissure**, **excoriation** (scratch marks), **lichenification** (thickened skin with accentuated markings from chronic rubbing), **atrophy** and **scar**.

### How to describe a lesion

Give the **distribution** (where, symmetric or not, sun-exposed, flexural, dermatomal), **primary morphology** and size in millimetres or centimetres, **color**, **border** (sharp or ill-defined), **surface** (smooth, scaly, crusted), **configuration** (linear, annular or ring-shaped, grouped, reticulated, dermatomal), **number** and **associated symptoms** (itch, pain, bleeding). A model phrase: "a solitary 8 mm raised, asymmetric, dark brown to black papule with irregular borders on the left upper back."

### Skin cancer

- **Basal cell carcinoma**: pearly papule or nodule with telangiectasia and rolled borders, often on the face; locally invasive, rarely metastasizes.
- **Squamous cell carcinoma**: scaly, crusted or ulcerated papule, plaque or nodule on sun-exposed skin; can metastasize.
- **Melanoma**: use the **ABCDE** criteria: **A**symmetry, **B**order irregularity, **C**olor variegation, **D**iameter over 6 mm, **E**volving (change in size, shape or color). Also use the "ugly duckling" sign, a lesion that looks different from the patient's other moles. Suspicious lesions need **excisional biopsy with a narrow margin**; the Breslow thickness, which is measured in millimetres, is the most important prognostic factor.

### Burn surface area: the rule of nines (adult)

Head and neck 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%. The patient's palm (including fingers) is about 1% of the body surface.

### Lymph nodes

Examine the groups in order: preauricular, postauricular, occipital, tonsillar, submandibular, submental, anterior and posterior cervical chains, supraclavicular, axillary, epitrochlear and inguinal. Use the pads of two or three fingers in small circular motions. Describe **size** (normal is usually under 1 cm; inguinal nodes up to 1.5 cm can be normal), **consistency**, **tenderness**, **mobility** and whether nodes are matted, and the area they drain.

- Tender, soft, mobile nodes suggest reactive or infectious causes.
- **Rubbery, non-tender nodes** raise concern for lymphoma.
- **Hard, fixed, non-tender nodes** suggest metastatic cancer.
- A **left supraclavicular node (Virchow node)** suggests intra-abdominal malignancy such as gastric cancer. Any supraclavicular node is considered abnormal.

**Localized** lymphadenopathy involves one region; **generalized** lymphadenopathy involves two or more non-contiguous regions and suggests systemic disease (infectious mononucleosis, HIV, lymphoma, sarcoidosis). Always examine the area drained by an enlarged node and also palpate the liver and spleen.

@example
title: Estimating a burn with the rule of nines
problem: An adult has burns of the entire right arm and the entire anterior trunk. What percentage of body surface area is burned?
step: Look up the adult values: one arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%.
step: Add the regions: $9 + 18 = 27\%$.
step: Report as 27% total body surface area; second-degree and third-degree areas are used for resuscitation calculations, and the rule of nines is only an estimate.

@example
title: Applying ABCDE
problem: A 55-year-old man has a 9 mm brown and black patch-like plaque on his back. One half is darker than the other, the border is notched, and his wife says it was smaller last year. Which ABCDE criteria are met, and what is the next step?
step: Asymmetry (halves differ), Border irregularity (notched), Color variegation (brown and black), Diameter over 6 mm (9 mm), and Evolving (growth) are all present.
step: Together these make melanoma a serious concern.
step: The next step is excisional biopsy with narrow margins (not a shave biopsy), so histology can give the Breslow thickness for staging.

@mistakes
- Using the word "rash" instead of describing the lesion type, distribution and configuration.
- Doing a shave biopsy of a suspected melanoma, which can destroy the depth measurement.
- Confusing a macule with a patch, or a papule with a plaque; the cut-off is 1 cm.
- Ignoring a supraclavicular lymph node because it is small or painless.
