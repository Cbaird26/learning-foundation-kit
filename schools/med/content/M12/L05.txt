@article
The cardiovascular examination links the physical signs to physiology: every sound or pulse feature reflects flow, pressure and valve behavior. Follow a fixed sequence: general inspection, hands, pulse and blood pressure, face, JVP, precordium (inspection, palpation, auscultation), then the back, lungs, abdomen and legs for signs of failure.

### Pulses and peripheral signs

Note the rate, rhythm and **character** of the pulse. A **slow-rising, low-volume pulse** suggests aortic stenosis. A **collapsing (water-hammer) pulse** with wide pulse pressure suggests aortic regurgitation. **Radio-femoral delay** suggests coarctation of the aorta. Inspect for cyanosis, clubbing, xanthelasma, splinter hemorrhages (endocarditis) and peripheral edema.

### The jugular venous pressure

Position the patient at 45 degrees with the head turned slightly away. Identify the internal jugular pulsation (a double waveform that falls with inspiration and on pressure, is not palpable, and fills from above). Measure the **vertical** height of the top of the pulsation above the **sternal angle**. The right atrium lies about 5 cm below the sternal angle, so:

$$\text{estimated RA pressure (cm H}_2\text{O)} = \text{height above sternal angle (cm)} + 5.$$

A height above 3 cm above the sternal angle (about 8 cm H$_2$O or more) is elevated, as in right heart failure, tricuspid regurgitation, pulmonary hypertension and tamponade. **Kussmaul sign** (a paradoxical rise in JVP on inspiration) occurs in constrictive pericarditis.

### Palpation

The **apex beat** is normally in the fifth intercostal space at the midclavicular line. It is displaced laterally in left ventricular dilation. A **heave** reflects right ventricular hypertrophy, and a **thrill** is a palpable murmur (grade 4 or louder).

### Auscultation

Listen with the diaphragm (high-pitched sounds: S1, S2, regurgitant murmurs) and the bell (low-pitched: S3, S4, mitral stenosis rumble) at four main areas: aortic (right second intercostal space), pulmonic (left second intercostal space), tricuspid (left lower sternal border) and mitral (apex). Palpate the carotid pulse at the same time to time systole.

**Heart sounds**

- **S1**: closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves, marking the start of systole; loudest at the apex.
- **S2**: closure of the aortic (A2) and pulmonic (P2) valves. A2 normally precedes P2. In **physiologic splitting**, the gap widens during inspiration because increased venous return delays right ventricular ejection and P2. A **fixed split** (does not vary with breathing) suggests atrial septal defect. **Paradoxical (reversed) splitting**, which is wider in expiration, occurs when A2 is delayed (left bundle branch block, severe aortic stenosis).
- **S3**: early diastolic sound from rapid ventricular filling; normal in young adults and pregnancy but pathologic in older adults with volume overload and heart failure.
- **S4**: late diastolic sound just before S1 from atrial contraction against a stiff, hypertrophied ventricle; absent in atrial fibrillation.

### Murmurs

Describe each murmur by **timing**, **location**, **radiation**, **intensity** (grades 1 to 6; palpable thrill at 4), **pitch**, **quality** and response to **inspiration** or maneuvers.

| Lesion | Murmur | Clues |
|---|---|---|
| Aortic stenosis | Crescendo-decrescendo systolic, right upper sternal border, radiates to carotids | Slow-rising pulse, syncope, angina, dyspnea |
| Mitral regurgitation | Holosystolic, apex, radiates to axilla | Soft S1, displaced apex |
| Mitral stenosis | Opening snap then low-pitched mid-diastolic rumble at apex (bell, left lateral position) | Rheumatic heart disease, atrial fibrillation |
| Aortic regurgitation | Early diastolic decrescendo, left sternal edge, patient leaning forward in expiration | Wide pulse pressure, bounding pulses |
| Mitral valve prolapse | Midsystolic click, late systolic murmur | Murmur earlier and longer on standing |
| Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy | Systolic murmur, left lower sternal border | Louder with Valsalva and standing; syncope in young athletes |

**Maneuvers.** Inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, so right-sided murmurs and sounds become louder (Carvallo sign for tricuspid regurgitation). Standing or the strain phase of Valsalva reduces venous return and preload, which makes most murmurs softer but hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (and mitral valve prolapse) louder. Squatting increases preload and afterload, which makes aortic stenosis louder and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy softer. Sustained handgrip raises afterload, which increases the murmurs of mitral regurgitation, aortic regurgitation and ventricular septal defect and decreases that of aortic stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

@example
title: Estimating right atrial pressure from the JVP
problem: A patient reclining at 45 degrees has the top of the internal jugular pulsation 7 cm vertically above the sternal angle. Estimate the right atrial pressure and say whether the JVP is elevated.
step: The right atrium is about 5 cm below the sternal angle, so add 5 cm to the measured height.
step: Estimated RA pressure = $7 + 5 = 12$ cm H$_2$O.
step: A height greater than 3 cm above the sternal angle (RA pressure above about 8 cm H$_2$O) is elevated, so the JVP is raised. Possible causes include right or biventricular heart failure and pericardial tamponade.

@example
title: Using a maneuver to separate two systolic murmurs
problem: A 19-year-old athlete has a systolic ejection murmur that becomes louder when he stands from squatting. Which diagnosis is favored over aortic stenosis?
step: Moving from squatting to standing decreases venous return and preload, so the left ventricle is smaller.
step: In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy a smaller ventricular cavity increases outflow tract obstruction, so the murmur gets louder; in fixed valvular aortic stenosis, lower flow makes the murmur softer.
step: A louder murmur with standing or Valsalva favors hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

@mistakes
- Measuring the JVP along the neck instead of vertically from the sternal angle, or using the external jugular vein.
- Using the bell for high-pitched murmurs and the diaphragm for the low-pitched mitral rumble.
- Forgetting that inspiration accentuates right-sided and expiration accentuates left-sided murmurs.
- Calling any systolic murmur "pathologic" without considering a flow murmur in a young, healthy person.

@practice self
prompt: Describe the heart sounds. A normal 25-year-old listens with her stethoscope at the left second intercostal space while breathing slowly. Describe what S1 and S2 are, what S2 does during inspiration versus expiration, explain why, and name one condition in which the splitting of S2 behaves differently and what it looks like.
solution: Model answer: S1 is closure of the mitral and tricuspid valves at the start of systole; S2 is closure of the aortic then pulmonic valves at the end of systole. During inspiration, negative intrathoracic pressure increases venous return to the right heart; the larger right ventricular stroke volume takes slightly longer to eject, so P2 is delayed and S2 splits into audible A2 and P2. During expiration the two components merge into a single S2. In atrial septal defect the split is fixed (it does not change with respiration) because shunting equalizes right and left filling; in left bundle branch block or severe aortic stenosis A2 is delayed and the split is paradoxical (narrower on inspiration, wider on expiration).
