@article
A proof is a chain of statements, each justified by a given fact, a definition, a postulate or an earlier theorem. For triangle proofs there is a reliable plan.

### A four-step plan

1. **Mark the givens** on a sketch.
2. **List the free facts**: shared sides (reflexive property), vertical angles, midpoints and bisectors (which create equal parts), and parallel lines (which create equal angles).
3. **Choose a criterion** (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS) and make sure you have three matching parts in the right arrangement.
4. **Conclude** with the congruence statement, then use **CPCTC** to get the part you were asked about.

### Key theorems

**Triangle angle-sum theorem.** The three angles of a triangle add to $180^\circ$. (Proof idea: draw a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side; the alternate interior angles match two of the triangle's angles and the three angles along the line make $180^\circ$.)

**Exterior angle theorem.** An exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two **remote interior angles**. If you extend side $BC$ beyond $C$, the exterior angle at $C$ equals $m\angle A + m\angle B$. This holds because the exterior angle and $\angle C$ form a linear pair, so both equal $180^\circ - m\angle C$.

**Isosceles triangle theorem.** If two sides of a triangle are equal, the angles opposite them are equal. Its converse is also true: equal angles mean equal opposite sides. So a triangle is isosceles exactly when it has two equal angles, and an equilateral triangle has three $60^\circ$ angles.

**Perpendicular bisector theorem.** Any point on the perpendicular bisector of a segment is equally far from the two endpoints. Its converse says a point equally far from both endpoints lies on the perpendicular bisector.

### A model proof

*Given:* $AB = AC$, and $M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{BC}$. *Prove:* $\angle B \cong \angle C$.

Picture a triangle with apex $A$ above the base $BC$ and $M$ at the middle of the base; draw $\overline{AM}$. Then $AB = AC$ (given), $BM = CM$ (definition of midpoint) and $AM = AM$ (reflexive), so $\triangle ABM \cong \triangle ACM$ by SSS. By CPCTC, $\angle B \cong \angle C$. This is the proof of the isosceles triangle theorem, and the same triangles also show $AM$ is perpendicular to $BC$.

### Choosing the right criterion

If your givens are about **sides**, aim for SSS or SAS. If they include **parallel lines or bisected angles**, the angle facts push you toward ASA or AAS. A proof that is stuck usually needs one extra free fact: a shared side, a vertical angle, or an auxiliary segment.

@example
title: Isosceles triangle from an angle bisector
problem: In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = AC$. Point $D$ lies on $\overline{BC}$ and ray $AD$ bisects $\angle BAC$. Prove $\angle B \cong \angle C$.
step: Mark the givens: $AB = AC$, and $\angle BAD \cong \angle CAD$ because $AD$ is an angle bisector.
step: The triangles $ABD$ and $ACD$ share side $AD$, so $AD = AD$ by the reflexive property.
step: Two sides ($AB = AC$ and $AD = AD$) and the included angle ($\angle BAD \cong \angle CAD$) are equal, so $\triangle ABD \cong \triangle ACD$ by SAS.
step: By CPCTC, $\angle B \cong \angle C$.

@mistakes
- Using CPCTC before the triangles have been proved congruent.
- Marking parts as equal because they look equal in the sketch rather than because of a given or a theorem.
- Writing the congruence statement with the vertices in the wrong order.
- Forgetting that the exterior angle equals the sum of the two **remote** interior angles, not the adjacent one.
