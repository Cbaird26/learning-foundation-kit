@article
Statistical tests answer a narrow question: if there were really no effect, how surprising would our data be? This lesson builds that logic step by step.

### The logic of a test

1. State the **null hypothesis** ($H_0$): no difference or no association (for example, the two treatments have equal mean effect). The **alternative hypothesis** ($H_1$) says there is a difference. A **two-sided** test allows a difference in either direction and is the default.
2. Choose the **significance level** $\alpha$ in advance, conventionally 0.05.
3. Compute a **test statistic** from the data and the **p-value**.
4. If $p < \alpha$, reject $H_0$ ("statistically significant"); otherwise fail to reject it.

### The p-value

The **p-value** is the probability, assuming the null hypothesis is true, of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is **not**:

- the probability that the null hypothesis is true,
- the probability that the result occurred by chance alone,
- the size or importance of the effect.

A small p-value says the data are hard to reconcile with the null. A large p-value does not prove the null; it means the study did not find convincing evidence against it (absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, especially in a small study).

### Errors and power

|  | $H_0$ actually true | $H_0$ actually false |
|---|---|---|
| Reject $H_0$ | **Type I error** (false positive), probability $\alpha$ | Correct, probability $1-\beta$ (**power**) |
| Fail to reject | Correct | **Type II error** (false negative), probability $\beta$ |

Type I error means concluding there is an effect when there is none. Type II error means missing a real effect, usually because the sample was too small. Power $= 1 - \beta$ is typically set at 80% to 90%.

### Statistical versus clinical significance

A very large study can give $p < 0.001$ for a difference of 1 mmHg in blood pressure that matters to no patient. A small trial can show a large, important difference with $p = 0.08$. Always look at the **size of the effect** and its confidence interval, not the p-value alone.

### Choosing a test

| Data and design | Test |
|---|---|
| Compare means of two independent groups (approximately normal) | Unpaired (two-sample) **t-test** |
| Compare means before and after in the same people, or matched pairs | **Paired t-test** |
| Compare means of three or more groups | **ANOVA** |
| Compare proportions or association between two categorical variables | **Chi-square test** |
| Categorical data with small expected counts (below 5 in a cell) | **Fisher's exact test** |
| Skewed or ordinal data, two independent groups | **Mann-Whitney U** (Wilcoxon rank-sum) |
| Skewed or ordinal data, paired | **Wilcoxon signed-rank** |
| Linear relationship between two continuous variables | **Pearson correlation** (Spearman for ranks) |
| Time to event between groups | **Log-rank test** (Kaplan-Meier curves) |

### Chi-square for a 2x2 table

Under $H_0$, the expected count in a cell is $E = \dfrac{(\text{row total})(\text{column total})}{N}$. Then

$$\chi^2 = \sum \frac{(O - E)^2}{E}.$$

A 2x2 table has 1 degree of freedom, and the critical value for $\alpha = 0.05$ is 3.84. A $\chi^2$ above 3.84 gives $p < 0.05$.

### z-statistic for a mean

When the population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known (or the sample is large), compare a sample mean $\bar{x}$ with a hypothesized value $\mu_0$:

$$z = \frac{\bar{x} - \mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}.$$

For a two-sided test at $\alpha = 0.05$, $|z| > 1.96$ is significant.

Remember: a test result tells you about chance, not about bias or confounding. A significant result from a biased study is still biased.
@example
title: Chi-square test on a 2x2 table
problem: In a trial, 40 of 100 patients given treatment A improve, compared with 25 of 100 given treatment B. Test whether improvement is associated with treatment at $\alpha = 0.05$ (critical value 3.84).
step: Totals: 65 improved and 135 did not improve out of $N = 200$; each group has 100.
step: Expected improved in each group $= 100 \times 65/200 = 32.5$. Expected not improved $= 100 \times 135/200 = 67.5$.
step: Each cell differs from its expected value by 7.5, so $(O-E)^2 = 56.25$. $\chi^2 = 2(56.25/32.5) + 2(56.25/67.5) = 3.46 + 1.67 = 5.13$.
step: Since $5.13 > 3.84$, $p < 0.05$ (about 0.024). Reject $H_0$: improvement differs between treatments. This says nothing yet about the size of the benefit or about bias.
@mistakes
- Interpreting $p = 0.03$ as a 3% chance that the null hypothesis is true.
- Concluding that a non-significant result proves there is no difference.
- Equating statistical significance with clinical importance.
- Using an unpaired test on paired data, or repeated t-tests instead of ANOVA for several groups.

hint: The p-value is calculated assuming that the null hypothesis is true.

@practice num
prompt: In a trial, 30 of 100 patients on treatment X and 50 of 100 patients on treatment Y have the outcome. Calculate the chi-square statistic for the 2x2 table (without continuity correction). Round to two decimal places.
answer: 8.33
check: sum((o - e)**2 / e for o, e in [(30, 40), (70, 60), (50, 40), (50, 60)])
tol: 0.01
hint: Total with the outcome is 80 and without is 120, out of 200.
hint: Expected counts are 40 and 60 in the treatment X row, and the same in the treatment Y row.
solution: Expected: 40 with the outcome and 60 without in each group. $\chi^2 = 2(100/40) + 2(100/60) = 5 + 3.33 = 8.33$. This exceeds 3.84, so $p < 0.05$.

@practice num
prompt: A population has a mean systolic blood pressure of 100 mmHg under the null hypothesis, with standard deviation 20 mmHg. A sample of 100 patients has a mean of 104 mmHg. What is the z statistic?
answer: 2
check: (104 - 100) / (20 / math.sqrt(100))
tol: 0.01
hint: Standard error $= \sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
hint: Standard error is 2 mmHg; the observed difference is 4 mmHg.
solution: $z = (104 - 100)/(20/10) = 2$. Since $|z| > 1.96$, the result is significant at the 5% level (two-sided).
@quiz num
prompt: In a trial, 20 of 100 patients on drug P and 35 of 100 patients on drug Q have an adverse outcome. Calculate the chi-square statistic for the 2x2 table (without continuity correction). Round to two decimal places.
answer: 5.64
check: sum((o - e)**2 / e for o, e in [(20, 27.5), (80, 72.5), (35, 27.5), (65, 72.5)])
tol: 0.01
hint: Total with the outcome is 55 and without is 145, out of 200. Expected counts per group are 27.5 and 72.5.
solution: Each cell deviates by 7.5, so $(O-E)^2 = 56.25$. $\chi^2 = 2(56.25/27.5) + 2(56.25/72.5) = 4.09 + 1.55 = 5.64$, which exceeds 3.84.
@quiz mc
prompt: A small trial of a new drug finds a 30% relative reduction in a clinical outcome with p = 0.12. The investigators write: "The drug has no effect." What is the best criticism of this conclusion?
choice: A p-value above 0.05 proves the null hypothesis is false.
choice*: A non-significant result in a small trial may reflect low power; absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.
choice: The result must be due to confounding.
choice: A type I error has certainly occurred.
hint: What does a p-value above alpha tell you about the true effect?
solution: Failing to reject the null may simply reflect insufficient sample size (type II error). The confidence interval would show which effect sizes remain compatible with the data.
