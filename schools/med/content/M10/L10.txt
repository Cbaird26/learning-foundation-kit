@article
Medicine is more than treating established disease. **Prevention** aims to stop illness from occurring, to catch it early, or to limit its consequences. Screening is a form of secondary prevention and it needs particularly careful evaluation, because it is offered to people who feel well.

### Levels of prevention

| Level | Aim | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Prevent disease before it starts | Vaccination, seat belts, smoking-cessation counseling, folic acid in pregnancy, sanitation |
| **Secondary** | Detect disease early in asymptomatic people, so treatment can change the course | Mammography, colonoscopy, Pap smear and HPV testing, newborn screening |
| **Tertiary** | Reduce complications and disability in established disease | Cardiac rehabilitation after myocardial infarction, foot care in diabetes, stroke rehabilitation |
| **Quaternary** | Protect patients from overmedicalization and unnecessary intervention | Avoiding low-value tests and treatments |

Screening is applied to people without symptoms; **case finding** and diagnostic testing apply to people with symptoms or risk factors.

### Principles for a screening program

The classic **Wilson and Jungner criteria** (from the World Health Organization) are summarized as:

- The condition is an important health problem with a recognizable **latent (preclinical) phase**.
- The **natural history** is understood.
- There is an accepted **effective treatment**, and treatment at the early stage gives **better outcomes** than treatment after symptoms appear.
- A suitable test exists that is **acceptable, safe**, and sufficiently sensitive and specific.
- Facilities for diagnosis and treatment are available, and the **cost** is balanced against the benefit.
- A screening interval and policy on whom to treat are agreed.

Screening should improve a patient-important outcome (usually disease-specific or all-cause mortality), and its harms (false positives, anxiety, further invasive testing, overdiagnosis and overtreatment) must be weighed against the benefit.

### Biases that make screening look better than it is

**Lead-time bias.** Screening finds disease earlier. Survival measured **from diagnosis** then appears longer even if the date of death is unchanged, because the clock started earlier. The **lead time** is the interval between detection by screening and the time at which the disease would have been diagnosed from symptoms. Example: a cancer found at 60 would have caused symptoms at 63 and death at 66 regardless; survival from diagnosis is 6 years with screening and 3 years without, but no one lives longer.

**Length-time bias.** Slow-growing, indolent disease spends longer in a detectable preclinical phase, so a periodic test preferentially catches it. Screened cases therefore seem to have better prognosis because they include more benign disease.

**Overdiagnosis.** Screening detects true disease that would never have caused symptoms or death in the person's lifetime (for example, some low-risk prostate and thyroid cancers). These patients cannot benefit and can only be harmed by treatment. It is an extreme form of length-time bias.

**Selection (healthy volunteer) bias**: people who choose screening are healthier and more health-conscious.

### How to evaluate screening properly

Because these biases inflate **survival from diagnosis** and **stage shift**, the valid evidence for screening is a **randomized trial** comparing **mortality** (disease-specific, and ideally all-cause) between screened and unscreened groups. A related measure is the **number needed to screen** $= 1/\text{ARR in mortality}$ over the follow-up period.

### Examples of recommendations

Recommendations vary by organization and change with new evidence, so check current guidance. In the U.S., the Preventive Services Task Force currently recommends, among others: colorectal cancer screening for adults aged 45 to 75; cervical cancer screening for ages 21 to 65 (cytology every 3 years from 21 to 29); biennial mammography for women aged 40 to 74; annual low-dose CT for adults aged 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years; and one-time abdominal ultrasound for aortic aneurysm in men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked. Use these as study examples rather than clinical rules.
@example
title: Lead-time bias with numbers
problem: With screening, a cancer is detected at age 58. Without screening, it would have been diagnosed at age 63 when symptoms appeared. In either case, the patient dies at age 70. Compare survival from diagnosis and identify the lead time.
step: With screening, survival from diagnosis is $70 - 58 = 12$ years.
step: Without screening, survival from diagnosis is $70 - 63 = 7$ years.
step: The lead time is $63 - 58 = 5$ years, which is exactly the apparent gain of $12 - 7 = 5$ years.
step: The age at death did not change, so the patient did not live longer. Survival from diagnosis rose only because the clock started earlier. Only a reduction in mortality in a randomized comparison shows that screening helps.
@mistakes
- Judging a screening test by survival from diagnosis or by earlier stage at diagnosis, both of which are inflated by lead-time bias.
- Calling a diagnostic test for a symptomatic patient "screening."
- Assuming that any early detection is beneficial; overdiagnosis causes harm without benefit.
- Mixing up levels: treating established disease to prevent complications is tertiary, not secondary, prevention.

prompt: In a screening trial, the 10-year disease-specific mortality is 0.7% in the unscreened group and 0.5% in the screened group. What is the number needed to screen to prevent one death over 10 years?
answer: 500
check: 1 / (0.007 - 0.005)
hint: Compute the absolute difference in mortality as a proportion.
solution: $\text{ARR} = 0.007 - 0.005 = 0.002$; $\text{NNS} = 1/0.002 = 500$.
