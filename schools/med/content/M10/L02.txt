@article
A study design is a plan for collecting and comparing data. The choice decides which questions the study can answer and which biases threaten it. The first split is by whether the investigator assigns the exposure.

- **Experimental studies**: the investigator assigns the intervention. The randomized controlled trial (RCT) is the standard example.
- **Observational studies**: the investigator watches what happens. These are cohort, case-control, cross-sectional and descriptive studies.

### Randomized controlled trial

Participants are randomly allocated to an intervention or a control group and followed for outcomes. Randomization distributes known and **unknown** confounders equally between the groups on average, so a difference in outcome can be attributed to the intervention. RCTs give the strongest evidence for efficacy of treatments. Limitations: expense, limited generalizability to real-world patients, and ethical limits (you cannot randomize people to a harmful exposure).

### Cohort study

Groups defined by **exposure** (exposed and unexposed) are followed forward in time to compare **outcome** occurrence. A **prospective** cohort enrolls people now and follows them; a **retrospective** (historical) cohort uses past records to define exposure and outcome that have already happened.

- Measures: incidence, **relative risk (risk ratio)**, rate ratio, hazard ratio.
- Strengths: clear temporal sequence (exposure precedes outcome); can study **rare exposures**; many outcomes per exposure; gives incidence directly.
- Weaknesses: slow and expensive; inefficient for **rare diseases** and for diseases with long latency; loss to follow-up causes bias; confounding.

### Case-control study

Groups defined by **outcome** (cases with the disease and controls without) are compared for **past exposure**. Controls should come from the same source population that produced the cases.

- Measure: **odds ratio**.
- Strengths: fast and cheap; ideal for **rare diseases** and diseases with long latency; can examine many exposures for one disease.
- Weaknesses: cannot calculate incidence or risk directly (the investigator chose how many cases and controls to include); susceptible to **recall bias** and **selection bias** (especially in choosing controls); poor for rare exposures; temporal sequence can be unclear.

A **nested case-control study** draws cases and controls from within a defined cohort, which reduces both biases.

### Cross-sectional study

Exposure and outcome are measured **at the same time** in a defined population, like a snapshot. It estimates **prevalence** and can show association (prevalence ratio or odds ratio), but it cannot establish temporality: you may not know whether the exposure came before the disease. It is cheap and useful for planning health services and generating hypotheses. A survey of everyone in a community is a typical example.

### Descriptive designs

- **Case report and case series**: detailed description of one patient or several patients with no comparison group. Good for describing new diseases or unexpected adverse effects and for generating hypotheses; they cannot test them.
- **Ecological study**: the unit of analysis is a population or group (for example, countries), not individuals. It is vulnerable to the **ecological fallacy**: concluding that an association seen at the group level applies to individuals.

### Hierarchy of evidence

For questions about treatment, the usual ranking from strongest to weakest is: systematic reviews and meta-analyses of RCTs, individual RCTs, cohort studies, case-control studies, cross-sectional studies, case series and reports, expert opinion. The rank is a starting point; a poorly done RCT can be weaker than a well-done cohort study.

### Matching the design to the question

| Question | Best feasible design |
|---|---|
| Does a new drug reduce mortality? | RCT |
| Does an exposure that is unethical to assign (smoking) cause disease? | Cohort |
| What exposures are linked to a very rare cancer? | Case-control |
| How common is a condition in a community now? | Cross-sectional |
| What is the prognosis (natural history) of a disease? | Cohort |
| What is an unusual new adverse event? | Case report or series, then analytic study |

Always ask three things: (1) Can I assign the exposure? (2) Is the outcome rare or common? (3) Is the exposure rare or common?
@example
title: Picking a design and computing its main measure
problem: A neurologist wants to know which environmental exposures are associated with a very rare neurodegenerative disease that has a long latency. Which design is best, and which measure does it provide? A separate screening survey of 2500 adults in a village finds 325 with hypertension. What is the prevalence?
step: The disease is rare with a long latency, so following a cohort would take decades and accrue few cases. Start from the outcome instead.
step: Choose a **case-control** design: collect patients with the disease and compare past exposures with a suitable control group. The measure is the **odds ratio**; incidence cannot be calculated directly.
step: The village survey measured exposure and outcome at one point in time, so it is **cross-sectional** and gives prevalence: $325 / 2500 = 0.13$.
step: The prevalence of hypertension is 13%. The same survey could not tell us whether any exposure came before the disease.
@mistakes
- Using a case-control study to estimate incidence or relative risk; only the odds ratio is available.
- Calling a retrospective cohort a case-control study. A cohort is defined by exposure, whatever the time direction of data collection.
- Treating an association between group averages (an ecological study) as proof about individuals.
- Assuming a cross-sectional study shows that exposure came before disease.
