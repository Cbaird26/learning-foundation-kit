@article
A **measure of association** compares disease frequency between an exposed group and an unexposed group. Every measure in this lesson comes from the same four numbers arranged in a 2x2 table.

|  | Disease | No disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Exposed** | $a$ | $b$ |
| **Unexposed** | $c$ | $d$ |

### Risk ratio (relative risk)

In a cohort study (or RCT), risk in each group is the incidence proportion:

$$\text{risk}_{\text{exposed}} = \frac{a}{a+b}, \qquad \text{risk}_{\text{unexposed}} = \frac{c}{c+d}.$$

$$\text{RR} = \frac{a/(a+b)}{c/(c+d)}$$

- $\text{RR} = 1$: no association.
- $\text{RR} > 1$: exposure increases risk (a risk factor).
- $\text{RR} < 1$: exposure decreases risk (protective).

An RR of 3 means the exposed group has three times the risk. An RR of 0.6 means a 40% **relative** reduction in risk. The rate ratio and hazard ratio are read the same way but use person-time or time-to-event data.

### Odds ratio

**Odds** are the probability of an event divided by the probability of no event: $\text{odds} = p/(1-p)$. The odds ratio compares the odds of **exposure** among cases to the odds of exposure among controls (case-control study), or equivalently the odds of disease in exposed versus unexposed:

$$\text{OR} = \frac{a/c}{b/d} = \frac{ad}{bc}.$$

The OR is the only measure a case-control study can provide, because the investigator fixes the numbers of cases and controls, so risks cannot be computed. When the disease is **rare** (roughly under 10% in both groups), $b \approx a+b$ and $d \approx c+d$, so **OR approximates RR**. When disease is common, the OR overstates the RR in magnitude (further from 1). Logistic regression reports adjusted odds ratios.

### Differences: absolute measures

- **Risk difference (attributable risk)** $= \text{risk}_{\text{exposed}} - \text{risk}_{\text{unexposed}}$. It is the excess risk attributable to the exposure among the exposed, and it matters for public health impact and for number needed to harm.
- **Attributable risk percent (among the exposed)** $= \dfrac{\text{RR} - 1}{\text{RR}} \times 100$. It is the percent of disease in exposed people that is due to exposure, assuming the association is causal.
- **Number needed to harm** $= 1/\text{risk difference}$ (see the trials lesson).
- **Population attributable risk** is the excess risk in the entire population attributable to the exposure; it depends on how common the exposure is as well as on RR. A rare exposure with a high RR can account for little disease in a whole population.

### Relative versus absolute

A relative measure shows strength of association (useful for causal inference); an absolute measure shows impact. A doubling of risk from 1 in 100,000 to 2 in 100,000 is an RR of 2 but a risk difference of only 1 in 100,000. Always ask for both when a headline reports only a relative figure.

### Interpreting results

State the measure, direction and magnitude, and the population. For example: "Smokers had 3 times the risk of lung cancer over 20 years compared with non-smokers." Then look at the confidence interval and consider bias and confounding (next lesson) before inferring causation.
@example
title: Risk ratio, risk difference and odds ratio from a cohort
problem: In a cohort, 30 of 200 workers exposed to a solvent develop dermatitis, compared with 20 of 400 unexposed workers. Calculate the RR, the risk difference and the attributable risk percent among the exposed.
step: Risk in the exposed $= 30/200 = 0.15$. Risk in the unexposed $= 20/400 = 0.05$.
step: $\text{RR} = 0.15/0.05 = 3$. The exposed workers have 3 times the risk.
step: Risk difference $= 0.15 - 0.05 = 0.10$, or 10 extra cases per 100 exposed workers.
step: Attributable risk percent $= (3-1)/3 = 0.667$, so about 67% of dermatitis among exposed workers is attributable to the solvent if the association is causal.
step: For comparison, the odds ratio is $(30/170)/(20/380) = 3.35$. It exceeds the RR because dermatitis is not rare here.
@mistakes
- Computing odds ratio from a cohort and calling it relative risk without noting that disease is common (the OR then exaggerates the association).
- Dividing by the total sample instead of by the exposed (or unexposed) group when calculating risk.
- Interpreting RR = 0.6 as "60% lower risk"; it is a 40% lower risk.
- Reading the relative risk as the absolute chance of disease.
@practice num
prompt: In a cohort study, 24 of 120 participants who take a certain medication develop a rash, compared with 12 of 240 who do not. What is the risk ratio?
answer: 4
check: (24/120) / (12/240)
hint: First compute the risk in each group (cases divided by group size).
hint: Risk in the exposed is $0.20$ and in the unexposed is $0.05$.
solution: $\text{RR} = (24/120)/(12/240) = 0.20/0.05 = 4$.
@practice num
prompt: In a case-control study of a rare tumor, 45 of 100 cases and 20 of 100 controls had been exposed to a chemical. What is the odds ratio? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 3.27
check: (45*80) / (55*20)
tol: 0.01
hint: Build the table: exposed cases 45, unexposed cases 55, exposed controls 20, unexposed controls 80.
hint: $\text{OR} = ad/bc$.
solution: $\text{OR} = (45 \times 80)/(55 \times 20) = 3600/1100 = 3.27$.
@practice num
prompt: The annual risk of a fracture is 9 per 1000 in long-term users of a drug and 3 per 1000 in non-users. What is the risk difference (attributable risk) in cases per 1000 per year?
answer: 6
check: 9 - 3
unit: per 1000 per year
hint: The risk difference is a subtraction of two risks, not a ratio.
solution: $9 - 3 = 6$ extra fractures per 1000 persons per year among users.
@practice num
prompt: Smoking has a relative risk of 5 for a particular disease. Assuming the association is causal, what percent of disease among smokers is attributable to smoking?
answer: 80
check: (5 - 1) / 5 * 100
unit: %
hint: Use attributable risk percent among the exposed: $(\text{RR} - 1)/\text{RR}$.
solution: $(5-1)/5 = 0.80$, so 80%.

choice*: The risk is 40% lower in the group with the dietary pattern, compared with the usual diet.

@quiz num
prompt: In a cohort study, 28 of 200 people with high sodium intake develop hypertension over five years, compared with 16 of 400 people with low sodium intake. What is the risk ratio?
answer: 3.5
check: (28/200) / (16/400)
hint: Compute each group's five-year risk.
hint: $0.14$ and $0.04$.
solution: $\text{RR} = 0.14/0.04 = 3.5$.
@quiz num
prompt: A case-control study finds that 62 of 100 cases of a rare cancer and 35 of 100 controls reported a past exposure. Calculate the odds ratio. Round to two decimal places.
answer: 3.03
check: (62*65) / (38*35)
tol: 0.01
hint: Exposed cases 62, unexposed cases 38, exposed controls 35, unexposed controls 65.
hint: $\text{OR} = ad/bc$.
solution: $\text{OR} = (62 \times 65)/(38 \times 35) = 4030/1330 = 3.03$.
