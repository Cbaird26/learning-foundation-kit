@article
Epidemiology is the study of the distribution and determinants of disease in populations. Before you can ask what causes a disease, you have to count it properly. Two questions drive almost every measure of frequency: **who is counted in the numerator** (new cases or all cases?) and **who is counted in the denominator** (everyone, or only people who could develop the disease?).

### Incidence: new cases

**Incidence** counts new events in a population at risk over a period of time. People who already have the disease are not at risk of getting it for the first time, so they are excluded from the denominator.

The **incidence proportion** (also called cumulative incidence or **risk**) is

$$\text{incidence proportion} = \frac{\text{new cases during the period}}{\text{population at risk at the start}}$$

It is a probability, so it lies between 0 and 1, and it requires a stated time period ("2.5% over one year"). It assumes everyone is followed for the whole period.

When people are followed for different lengths of time (they move away, die of other causes, or enter the study late) use the **incidence rate**, also called incidence density:

$$\text{incidence rate} = \frac{\text{new cases}}{\text{total person-time at risk}}$$

Person-time is the sum of the time each person contributed while at risk, for example person-years. Ten people followed for 5 years each contribute 50 person-years. A rate has units of 1/time and can exceed 1 in principle. Person-time stops accumulating for a person once they develop the outcome (for a first event), are lost to follow-up, or die.

### Prevalence: existing cases

**Prevalence** is the proportion of a population that has the disease at a point in time (**point prevalence**) or at any time during a period (**period prevalence**). The numerator includes both new and old cases:

$$\text{prevalence} = \frac{\text{all existing cases}}{\text{total population}}$$

Prevalence is a proportion with no time units. It is what a cross-sectional survey measures, and it is the number that tells a health system how much care a disease needs right now.

### How incidence, duration and prevalence relate

Think of a bathtub. Incidence is the tap filling the tub, and recovery or death is the drain. Prevalence is the water level. When the disease is stable and fairly uncommon:

$$P \approx I \times D$$

where $P$ is prevalence, $I$ is incidence rate and $D$ is the mean duration of disease. Three consequences are tested constantly:

- A treatment that **prolongs life without curing** (for example insulin in type 1 diabetes, or antiretroviral therapy in HIV infection) increases duration, so prevalence rises even if incidence is unchanged.
- A **highly lethal, rapidly fatal** disease can have high incidence but low prevalence because duration is short.
- A **vaccine** lowers incidence, and prevalence falls after a lag.

### Other rates you should know

- **Attack rate**: incidence proportion during an outbreak, among those exposed (for example, ill attendees divided by all attendees at a meal).
- **Mortality rate**: deaths in a population divided by the population (often per 100,000 per year). The denominator is everyone, not only people with the disease.
- **Case-fatality rate**: deaths from a disease divided by the number of people with that disease. It measures lethality.
- **Crude versus specific rates**: crude rates ignore age structure; age-specific rates describe one stratum; **direct or indirect standardization** adjusts for age so that populations can be compared fairly.

### Choosing the right measure

Use incidence to study causes and risk (it tells you how quickly people become ill). Use prevalence to plan services. Remember that a cross-sectional prevalence study mixes risk factors for getting a disease with factors for surviving it, which can distort associations (prevalence-incidence bias).
@example
title: Incidence proportion, rate and prevalence from one cohort
problem: A cohort study enrols 2000 adults free of asthma. Over the next year 50 develop asthma. A separate survey of 8000 adults finds 640 currently have asthma. Calculate the one-year incidence proportion per 1000 and the prevalence as a percent.
step: Incidence counts only new cases among those at risk. The 2000 enrolled adults are all at risk at baseline, so the denominator is 2000.
step: Incidence proportion $= 50 / 2000 = 0.025$, which is 25 per 1000 over one year.
step: Prevalence counts all existing cases over the whole surveyed population. Prevalence $= 640 / 8000 = 0.08$.
step: As a percent, prevalence is 8%. Notice the two numbers answer different questions: 2.5% of at-risk adults develop asthma each year, while 8% of all adults currently have it. The gap reflects the long duration of asthma.
@mistakes
- Including people who already have the disease in the denominator of an incidence calculation.
- Reporting an incidence proportion without a time period, or using population size instead of person-time when follow-up differs between people.
- Concluding that a rising prevalence always means more new cases; longer survival also raises prevalence.
- Using the number of people with the disease as the denominator of a mortality rate (that is the case-fatality rate).

answer: 9.52
