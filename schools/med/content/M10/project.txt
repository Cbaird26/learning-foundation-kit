title: Journal club: appraise a trial and a diagnostic study
overview: You will run a journal club on two supplied (invented) studies, a randomized trial of a heart-failure drug and a diagnostic accuracy study of a rapid blood test. You will appraise validity, calculate the number needed to treat and number needed to harm, compute likelihood ratios and post-test probabilities, and finish with a one-page recommendation for a clinician and patient.
goal: Appraise a trial and a diagnostic study with a structured checklist (validity, results, applicability).
goal: Calculate absolute and relative effect measures, NNT and NNH, and interpret a confidence interval.
goal: Calculate sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and post-test probabilities for patients with different pre-test probabilities.
goal: Communicate a balanced, evidence-based recommendation in plain language.
step: Read the supplied trial abstract carefully.
 TRIAL ABSTRACT (invented for teaching). Background: Drug R is a new oral agent for chronic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. Methods: In this multicenter, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, 2400 adults were randomly assigned by a central computer system with concealed allocation, 1200 to Drug R and 1200 to placebo, in addition to usual care. The pre-registered primary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular death or first hospitalization for heart failure over a median follow-up of 24 months. Analysis was by intention-to-treat. Loss to follow-up was 6% in each arm. The trial was funded by the manufacturer. Results: The primary outcome occurred in 168 of 1200 patients (14.0%) in the Drug R group and 216 of 1200 (18.0%) in the placebo group (hazard ratio 0.76; 95% CI 0.62 to 0.93; p = 0.008). All-cause death occurred in 8.0% versus 9.5% (hazard ratio 0.84; 95% CI 0.65 to 1.09). Symptomatic hypotension occurred in 8.0% of the Drug R group and 5.0% of the placebo group. Conclusions: Drug R reduced the composite of cardiovascular death or heart-failure hospitalization.
step: Appraise the trial with a checklist: randomization and concealment, blinding, baseline comparability (not reported here, so note it as missing), follow-up and intention-to-treat analysis, primary outcome, funding, and applicability. Record each item as a strength, a weakness or unknown, with one sentence of justification.
step: Calculate the effect measures for the primary outcome: control event rate, experimental event rate, absolute risk reduction, relative risk and relative risk reduction, and the number needed to treat for the median 24 months. Then calculate the absolute risk increase and number needed to harm for symptomatic hypotension. Interpret the confidence intervals for the primary outcome and for all-cause death (does each exclude 1?).
step: Read the supplied diagnostic study.
 DIAGNOSTIC STUDY (invented for teaching). Test T is a rapid point-of-care blood test for acute disease D. Consecutive adults presenting to an emergency department with suspected D were enrolled (n = 1000). All patients received the reference standard imaging, which was interpreted by radiologists blinded to Test T. Disease D was confirmed in 200 patients. Test T was positive in 170 of the 200 patients with D, and positive in 80 of the 800 patients without D.
step: Appraise the diagnostic study (consecutive enrollment, reference standard applied to all, blinding, spectrum of patients, applicability to your setting). Build the 2x2 table and calculate sensitivity, specificity, the positive and negative likelihood ratios, and the positive and negative predictive values at the study prevalence.
step: Use likelihood ratios to update the probability for three patients with pre-test probability of 5%, 20% and 60% of disease D. For each, give the post-test probability after a positive result and after a negative result, using odds. Comment on which patients would still need further testing after each result.
step: Write a one-page recommendation: summarize the question (PICO), the validity of each study, the key numbers (NNT with time frame, NNH, post-test probabilities), the uncertainties, and the decisions you would discuss with a patient. Include one sentence on what further evidence you would want.

@review
### Review guide for Epidemiology, biostatistics and evidence-based medicine

**Week by week**

1. **Disease frequency.** Incidence proportion (new cases over population at risk), incidence rate (cases per person-time), prevalence (all cases over population). $P \approx I \times D$. Attack rate and case-fatality rate have specific denominators.
2. **Study designs.** RCT, cohort (exposure to outcome, gives RR and incidence), case-control (outcome to exposure, gives OR, good for rare disease), cross-sectional (prevalence), case series, ecological (ecological fallacy).
3. **Association.** 2x2 table; $\text{RR} = [a/(a+b)]/[c/(c+d)]$, $\text{OR} = ad/bc$, risk difference, attributable risk percent $(\text{RR}-1)/\text{RR}$.
4. **Bias and confounding.** Selection bias, information bias, lead-time bias; confounder criteria; stratification, restriction, matching, regression, and randomization (the only method that handles unmeasured confounders); effect modification versus confounding.
5. **Sensitivity and specificity.** Sensitivity $TP/(TP+FN)$, specificity $TN/(TN+FP)$; SnNout and SpPin; cutoffs and ROC curves.
6. **PPV, NPV and likelihood ratios.** Predictive values depend on prevalence; $LR^+ = \text{sens}/(1-\text{spec})$, $LR^- = (1-\text{sens})/\text{spec}$; update odds, not probabilities.
7. **Hypothesis tests.** Null hypothesis, p-value, type I and II errors, choice of test, chi-square.
8. **Confidence intervals and power.** $\text{estimate} \pm 1.96\,\text{SE}$; null value 0 for differences and 1 for ratios; power rises with sample size and effect size.
9. **Trials and NNT.** Randomization, concealment, blinding, intention-to-treat; ARR, RRR, $\text{NNT} = 1/\text{ARR}$, NNH.
10. **Screening and prevention.** Levels of prevention, Wilson and Jungner criteria, lead-time, length-time and overdiagnosis; judge screening by mortality.
11. **Critical appraisal.** PICO; validity, results, applicability; systematic reviews, heterogeneity, publication bias; multiple comparisons, surrogate and composite endpoints.
12. **Quality improvement and safety.** PDSA, process, outcome and balancing measures, control charts, Swiss cheese model, strength of actions, just culture.

**Self-check**

- Can I build any 2x2 table from a description and compute each measure without a formula sheet?
- Can I turn a pre-test probability and a likelihood ratio into a post-test probability?
- Can I explain a p-value and a confidence interval in a sentence each, in plain words?
- Can I state which design and which bias apply to a vignette?

**Common errors**

- Using the wrong denominator (sensitivity versus PPV, incidence versus prevalence).
- Multiplying probabilities, not odds, by a likelihood ratio.
- Using ARR in percent when taking the reciprocal for NNT.
- Interpreting a p-value as the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
- Reading survival from diagnosis as proof that screening works.
@exam num
prompt: Test T has a sensitivity of 85% and a specificity of 90%. A patient with minimal symptoms has a pre-test probability of 5% of disease D, and the test is positive. What is the post-test probability of disease, as a percent? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 30.9
check: (0.85 * 0.05) / (0.85 * 0.05 + 0.10 * 0.95) * 100
tol: 0.1
unit: %
hint: Work out the positive likelihood ratio, then update the odds.
hint: $LR^+ = 0.85/0.10 = 8.5$. Pre-test odds $= 0.05/0.95$.
solution: $LR^+ = 8.5$. Pre-test odds $= 0.05/0.95 = 0.0526$. Post-test odds $= 0.0526 \times 8.5 = 0.447$. Probability $= 0.447/1.447 = 0.309$, or 30.9%. Even with a good test, a positive result in a low-risk patient leaves most of the probability on the side of no disease.
@exam num
prompt: In a double-blind trial, 52 of 400 patients assigned to a new drug and 76 of 400 patients assigned to placebo have the primary event over 3 years. What is the number needed to treat over 3 years? Round to one decimal place.
answer: 16.7
check: 1 / (76/400 - 52/400)
tol: 0.1
hint: Compute the event rate in each arm, then the ARR.
hint: Event rates are 13% and 19%, so the ARR is 6 percentage points.
solution: $\text{CER} = 76/400 = 0.19$; $\text{EER} = 52/400 = 0.13$; $\text{ARR} = 0.06$; $\text{NNT} = 1/0.06 = 16.7$, usually rounded up to 17 over 3 years.
@exam self
prompt: A colleague shows you the following summary and asks whether to adopt a new screening test for a cancer. "In a registry, 5-year survival after diagnosis was 62% before the test was introduced and 84% after, and the test detects 90% of cancers (sensitivity) with a 4% false-positive rate. We recommend routine screening of all adults." Write a short critique that names at least three problems with the argument and states what evidence would justify the recommendation.
hint: Ask what survival from diagnosis can and cannot show, and what the accuracy figures leave out.
solution: Survival measured from diagnosis is vulnerable to lead-time bias: earlier diagnosis lengthens survival without delaying death, and length-time bias and overdiagnosis add indolent cases, so the registry comparison does not show benefit. A before-and-after registry comparison also has no control group, so secular trends and changes in treatment could contribute. Sensitivity and false-positive rate describe test accuracy, but they say nothing about benefit; with low disease prevalence in all adults, even a 4% false-positive rate yields many false positives and a low positive predictive value, causing anxiety, further testing and overtreatment. To justify screening, I would want a randomized trial (or strong evidence) showing lower disease-specific mortality in screened versus unscreened people, an estimate of the number needed to screen, quantified harms including overdiagnosis, and a favorable balance with costs.
rubric: Identifies lead-time bias (and ideally length-time bias or overdiagnosis) as the flaw in using survival from diagnosis
rubric: Notes that sensitivity and specificity do not show patient benefit and that low prevalence leads to a low PPV and many false positives
rubric: Names the lack of a control group or randomization in a before-and-after registry comparison
rubric: States that mortality reduction in a randomized comparison, with harms and number needed to screen, would justify the recommendation
