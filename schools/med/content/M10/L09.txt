@article
A well-run **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** is the best design for testing whether a treatment works. Its strength comes from specific design features, each of which blocks a particular bias.

### Features that protect validity

- **Randomization**: assigns participants by chance, balancing measured and unmeasured confounders between arms.
- **Allocation concealment**: the people enrolling participants cannot know or predict the next assignment (for example, central telephone or web allocation). It prevents selection bias at entry. This is different from blinding.
- **Blinding (masking)**: participants (single), also clinicians (double), also outcome assessors and analysts (triple) do not know the assignment. It prevents performance and detection bias. A **placebo** makes blinding possible.
- **Intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis**: participants are analyzed in the group to which they were **randomized**, regardless of adherence or crossover. It preserves the benefit of randomization and gives a realistic estimate of the effect of a treatment **policy**. **Per-protocol** analysis includes only adherent participants and can reintroduce bias, but it can be useful as a secondary analysis.
- **Pre-specified primary outcome** and registration of the trial protocol prevent selective outcome reporting.
- **Adequate follow-up** with few dropouts.

Other designs: **crossover** (each participant receives both treatments in sequence, after washout), **cluster randomized** (groups, such as clinics, are randomized), **non-inferiority** (tests whether a new treatment is not worse than the standard by more than a pre-specified margin), and **factorial** designs. Trials may stop early for harm or overwhelming benefit, which tends to overestimate effects.

### Phases of drug development

Phase I: small numbers, usually healthy volunteers, safety, dosing and pharmacokinetics. Phase II: small groups of patients, preliminary efficacy and further safety. Phase III: large RCTs comparing the drug with placebo or standard care, which usually support approval. Phase IV: **post-marketing surveillance** for rare or long-term adverse effects.

### Measuring treatment effect

Define the proportion of patients with the bad outcome in each arm:

- **Control event rate (CER)** and **experimental event rate (EER)**.
- **Absolute risk reduction (ARR)** $= \text{CER} - \text{EER}$.
- **Relative risk (RR)** $= \text{EER}/\text{CER}$.
- **Relative risk reduction (RRR)** $= \text{ARR}/\text{CER} = 1 - \text{RR}$.
- **Number needed to treat (NNT)** $= 1/\text{ARR}$.

The NNT is the number of patients who must receive the treatment, for the trial's duration, for **one additional patient** to benefit (avoid the outcome). It is usually rounded up to the next whole number. A smaller NNT means a more effective treatment. The ARR must be written as a proportion (0.05), not a percent, before taking the reciprocal; 5 percentage points means $\text{NNT} = 1/0.05 = 20$.

### Why absolute measures matter

An RRR of 50% sounds impressive, but it depends on baseline risk. If CER is 2%, EER is 1%, so ARR is 1% and NNT is 100. If CER is 40%, a 50% RRR gives EER 20%, ARR 20% and NNT 5. Relative reductions often look similar across populations while absolute benefit differs greatly, so **NNT applies to a specific baseline risk and time period**. For patients at a different baseline risk, assume RRR is roughly constant and recalculate: $\text{NNT} = 1/(\text{CER}_{\text{patient}} \times \text{RRR})$.

### Harm: number needed to harm

For adverse events, the **absolute risk increase (ARI)** $= \text{risk}_{\text{treated}} - \text{risk}_{\text{control}}$ and the **number needed to harm (NNH)** $= 1/\text{ARI}$. Compare NNT with NNH, and consider the severity of the outcomes. A **likelihood of being helped versus harmed** ratio is $\text{LHH} = (1/\text{NNT})/(1/\text{NNH})$.

### Time-to-event outcomes

Survival trials use **Kaplan-Meier curves**, the **log-rank test** and the **hazard ratio (HR)**, the instantaneous relative risk of the event, read like a relative risk (HR 0.75 means a 25% lower rate of the event at any time). Always give the confidence interval and the follow-up period.
@example
title: ARR, RRR and NNT from trial results
problem: In a 5-year trial, 20% of patients taking placebo and 15% of patients taking a drug have a stroke. Calculate ARR, RRR and NNT.
step: CER $= 0.20$ and EER $= 0.15$.
step: $\text{ARR} = 0.20 - 0.15 = 0.05$ (5 percentage points).
step: $\text{RRR} = 0.05/0.20 = 0.25$, a 25% relative reduction. Equivalently $\text{RR} = 0.15/0.20 = 0.75$.
step: $\text{NNT} = 1/0.05 = 20$. Twenty patients must be treated for 5 years to prevent one stroke. The same RRR in patients with a 4% baseline risk would give an ARR of 1% and an NNT of 100.
@mistakes
- Using the percent, not the proportion, of the ARR in the reciprocal (1/5 instead of 1/0.05).
- Quoting the RRR without the baseline risk; it can hide a very small absolute benefit.
- Confusing allocation concealment (before assignment) with blinding (after assignment).
- Excluding non-adherent patients from the primary analysis, which breaks intention-to-treat.

@quiz num
prompt: A trial compares a new drug with standard care. The 3-year rate of the primary outcome is 8% with standard care and 5.5% with the new drug. What is the number needed to treat?
answer: 40
check: 1 / (0.08 - 0.055)
hint: Compute the ARR as a proportion.
solution: $\text{ARR} = 0.08 - 0.055 = 0.025$; $\text{NNT} = 1/0.025 = 40$ over 3 years.
