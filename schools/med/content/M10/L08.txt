@article
A p-value gives a yes-or-no verdict on chance. A **confidence interval (CI)** shows what the data say about the size of an effect. For that reason most journals now ask for CIs alongside, or instead of, p-values.

### Standard error

Each sample gives a slightly different estimate. The **standard error (SE)** measures how much the estimate would vary from sample to sample.

$$\text{SE of a mean} = \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \text{SE of a proportion} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}.$$

Do not confuse the **standard deviation** (spread of individual values) with the standard error (precision of the estimate). SE shrinks as $n$ grows, but only with $\sqrt{n}$: quadrupling the sample size halves the SE.

### The 95% confidence interval

For a large sample,

$$95\%\ \text{CI} = \text{estimate} \pm 1.96 \times \text{SE}.$$

The correct interpretation: if the study were repeated many times, 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true value. For this one interval, we say the data are compatible with true values in that range. It is not correct to say that there is a 95% probability that the true value lies in this particular interval (although this reading is common in practice).

Features:

- **Width** reflects precision. A narrow interval means a precise estimate. Larger $n$ and lower variability narrow it.
- A 99% CI is wider than a 95% CI; a 90% CI is narrower.
- The interval gives both significance and magnitude.

### CI and the null value

The null value is **0** for differences (difference in means, risk difference) and **1** for ratios (RR, OR, HR). A 95% CI that **excludes** the null value corresponds to a two-sided $p < 0.05$. If the interval includes the null value, the result is not statistically significant at the 5% level.

Example: an RR of 0.80 with a 95% CI of 0.55 to 1.15 is not significant, since 1 lies inside. But the interval is also compatible with a 45% reduction and a 15% increase, so the study is **inconclusive**, not proof of no effect. Ratio CIs are calculated on the log scale and are asymmetric on the natural scale.

### Reading a CI clinically

Ask whether the whole interval is above or below the smallest effect that would matter to patients (the minimal clinically important difference). An interval that excludes the null value but includes only trivial effects is statistically but not clinically convincing. An interval that includes both the null and a clinically important benefit calls for a larger study.

### Power and sample size

**Power** is the probability of rejecting $H_0$ when it is false: $1 - \beta$. Power rises when:

- the **sample size** is larger,
- the **true effect size** is larger,
- the **variability** (standard deviation) is smaller,
- $\alpha$ is **larger** (less stringent; this raises the type I error risk),
- a one-sided test is used or a more efficient design (paired data) is chosen.

Sample size is calculated before the study from the chosen $\alpha$, power (commonly 80% or 90%), the smallest effect worth detecting and the variability. Underpowered studies often fail to detect real effects, and the "significant" findings they do report tend to overestimate the effect. A **post hoc** power calculation after a negative study is not informative; the confidence interval is the better guide.

To halve the width of a CI, you need about **four times** the sample size, because width scales with $1/\sqrt{n}$.
@example
title: Calculating and interpreting a CI for a treatment effect
problem: In a trial of 100 patients, a drug lowers systolic blood pressure by a mean of 8 mmHg relative to placebo. The standard deviation of the difference is 20 mmHg. Calculate the 95% CI and interpret it.
step: $\text{SE} = 20/\sqrt{100} = 2$ mmHg.
step: $95\%\ \text{CI} = 8 \pm 1.96 \times 2 = 8 \pm 3.92$, which gives 4.08 to 11.92 mmHg.
step: The interval excludes 0 (the null value for a difference), so $p < 0.05$ and the reduction is statistically significant.
step: Clinically, the data are compatible with a true reduction anywhere from about 4 to 12 mmHg. Whether that matters depends on the smallest reduction patients and clinicians would consider worthwhile.
@mistakes
- Saying there is a 95% probability that the true value lies in a specific computed interval, or interpreting a 95% CI as containing 95% of individual patients' values.
- Confusing standard deviation with standard error.
- Concluding "no effect" because a CI includes the null value when the interval is wide and also includes a clinically important benefit.
- Using 0 as the null for a ratio or 1 as the null for a difference.

check: 15 / math.sqrt(100)
tol: 0.05
unit: mmHg

@practice num
prompt: A sample of 144 patients has a mean LDL cholesterol of 120 mg/dL and a standard deviation of 30 mg/dL. What is the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval for the mean, in mg/dL?
answer: 124.9
check: 120 + 1.96 * 30 / math.sqrt(144)
tol: 0.05
unit: mg/dL
hint: First compute the standard error.
hint: $\text{SE} = 30/12 = 2.5$. Then add $1.96 \times \text{SE}$ to the mean.
solution: $\text{SE} = 2.5$; $120 + 1.96 \times 2.5 = 124.9$ mg/dL.
@practice num
prompt: In a sample of 400 people, the proportion with a condition is 0.20. What is the standard error of the proportion?
answer: 0.02
check: math.sqrt(0.2 * 0.8 / 400)
tol: 0.0005
hint: Use $\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}$.
solution: $\sqrt{0.2 \times 0.8/400} = \sqrt{0.0004} = 0.02$.

@quiz num
prompt: A study of 200 participants gives a 95% confidence interval for a mean difference with a certain width. By what factor must the sample size be multiplied to halve the width? Give the factor.
answer: 4
check: 2 ** 2
hint: Width is proportional to $1/\sqrt{n}$.
solution: Halving the width requires $\sqrt{n}$ to double, so $n$ must be multiplied by 4 (to 800).
@quiz num
prompt: A trial finds a mean reduction in HbA1c of 0.5 percentage points with a standard error of 0.2. What is the lower limit of the 95% confidence interval for the reduction, in percentage points? Round to two decimal places.
answer: 0.11
check: 0.5 - 1.96 * 0.2
tol: 0.01
unit: percentage points
hint: The CI is estimate $\pm 1.96 \times \text{SE}$.
solution: $0.5 - 1.96(0.2) = 0.5 - 0.392 = 0.108$, about 0.11. The upper limit is 0.89. Because 0 is excluded, $p < 0.05$, but the lower limit is a small effect.
