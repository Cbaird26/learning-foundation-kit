@article
**Evidence-based medicine** combines the best available research evidence with clinical expertise and the patient's values and circumstances. The research part is a skill: ask a precise question, find the evidence, appraise it, apply it and evaluate the result. This lesson covers framing the question and appraising the paper.

### Framing the question: PICO

- **P**atient or population (adults with type 2 diabetes and chronic kidney disease)
- **I**ntervention or exposure (a specific drug, test or risk factor)
- **C**omparison (placebo, standard care, another test)
- **O**utcome (patient-important: mortality, stroke, quality of life)

The question type suggests the design: therapy (RCT), diagnosis (cross-sectional study against a reference standard), prognosis (cohort), harm (cohort or case-control).

### Three questions to ask of any study

1. **Is it valid?** Are the results likely to be free of bias?
2. **What are the results and are they important?** How large is the effect and how precise is it?
3. **Can I apply them to my patient?** Is my patient similar to the participants, and are benefits worth harms and costs?

### Appraisal checklist for a randomized trial

- Were participants **randomized**, with **concealed allocation**?
- Were the groups similar at baseline (Table 1)?
- Were participants, clinicians and assessors **blinded**?
- Was follow-up complete and was analysis by **intention-to-treat**?
- Was the **primary outcome** pre-specified and patient-important, or only a **surrogate** (for example LDL cholesterol instead of cardiovascular events)?
- Was the sample size adequate and the CI narrow enough to be useful?
- Was the trial stopped early? Who funded it?
- Are the results reported in absolute terms (ARR, NNT), not only relative?

For **cohort** studies ask about representative sampling, accurate measurement of exposure and outcome, adequate length and completeness of follow-up, and adjustment for confounders. For **case-control** studies ask whether controls came from the same source population as cases and whether exposure was measured the same way in both. For **diagnostic** studies ask whether there was an independent, blinded comparison with a **reference standard** applied to everyone, and whether the spectrum of patients resembles your practice. Reporting guidelines exist for each design (CONSORT for trials, STROBE for observational studies, PRISMA for systematic reviews, STARD for diagnostic accuracy).

### Systematic reviews and meta-analysis

A **systematic review** uses a pre-specified, reproducible search and appraisal of all relevant studies. A **meta-analysis** statistically pools their results into a weighted summary (larger, more precise studies get more weight). A **forest plot** shows each study as a square (point estimate) with a horizontal line (CI), and the pooled estimate as a diamond. A line crossing the vertical line of no effect (1 for ratios, 0 for differences) is not individually significant.

- **Heterogeneity**: variation between studies beyond chance, assessed by visual overlap, Cochran's Q and the **$I^2$** statistic (roughly 25%, 50% and 75% are often described as low, moderate and high). When heterogeneity is high, a **random-effects** model and a search for reasons (subgroups, meta-regression) are appropriate rather than a single pooled number.
- **Publication bias**: positive studies are published more often. A **funnel plot** (effect against precision) is asymmetric when small negative studies are missing.
- The certainty of a body of evidence can be rated with **GRADE** (high, moderate, low, very low), considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias.

### Common traps

- **Multiple comparisons**: testing many outcomes or subgroups inflates the chance of a false positive. With $k$ independent tests at $\alpha = 0.05$, $P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - 0.95^k$. The **Bonferroni correction** uses $\alpha/k$ for each test.
- **Subgroup analyses**: post hoc subgroups are hypothesis-generating; trust them only if pre-specified, few, and supported by a significant interaction test.
- **Composite endpoints**: components may differ greatly in importance (death versus a lab change), and the effect may be driven by the least important one.
- **Surrogate endpoints**: they may not predict patient benefit.
- **Relative risk without baseline risk**, selective reporting and conflicts of interest.
- **Statistical versus clinical significance**: always compare the CI with the minimal important difference.
@example
title: Appraising an abstract quickly
problem: An abstract states: "In an open-label, industry-funded trial, 300 patients with mild hypertension were assigned to Drug Z or usual care by clinicians' choice. The primary outcome (stroke) did not differ, so a post hoc analysis in women aged 50 to 59 was performed and showed a 60% relative reduction in a composite outcome (p = 0.04)." Identify the main problems.
step: Allocation by clinicians' choice is not randomization and cannot be concealed, so confounding by indication and selection bias are likely. Open-label design adds performance and detection bias.
step: The pre-specified primary outcome was negative. The positive result comes from a post hoc subgroup, which is vulnerable to multiple comparisons: one in 20 such analyses would be "significant" by chance at $\alpha = 0.05$.
step: A composite outcome in a subgroup may be driven by a minor component, and "60% relative reduction" gives no absolute risk or NNT.
step: Industry funding is not itself invalidating, but it increases the need for pre-registration and independent analysis. Conclusion: the finding is hypothesis-generating at best and should not change practice.
@mistakes
- Treating all published studies as equally trustworthy because they passed peer review.
- Taking a "significant" post hoc subgroup result as proof of benefit.
- Averaging very heterogeneous studies into one pooled result without exploring why they differ.
- Accepting a surrogate endpoint (a lab value) as proof of patient benefit.
