@article
Every test result is a piece of evidence, not a verdict. To judge a test we compare it against a **reference (gold) standard**, such as biopsy, angiography or long-term follow-up, in people who are known to have or not have the disease. The results fall into four cells.

|  | Disease present | Disease absent |
|---|---|---|
| **Test positive** | True positive (TP) | False positive (FP) |
| **Test negative** | False negative (FN) | True negative (TN) |

### Sensitivity

**Sensitivity** is the proportion of people **with** disease who test positive:

$$\text{sensitivity} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}.$$

It answers "if the patient has the disease, how likely is a positive test?" Its complement is the **false-negative rate**: $FN/(TP+FN) = 1 - \text{sensitivity}$.

### Specificity

**Specificity** is the proportion of people **without** disease who test negative:

$$\text{specificity} = \frac{TN}{TN + FP}.$$

It answers "if the patient does not have the disease, how likely is a negative test?" The **false-positive rate** is $FP/(FP+TN) = 1 - \text{specificity}$.

Both measures use **columns** of the table (disease status as the denominator), so they are properties of the test and do **not** change with disease prevalence in the usual teaching model. (In practice they can vary between populations because spectrum of disease differs, a point appraisal checklists ask about.) Predictive values, covered next lesson, use the **rows** and do depend on prevalence.

### Using sensitivity and specificity

- **SnNout**: a highly **Sen**sitive test, when **N**egative, rules **out** disease. Use it for screening and for serious diseases you cannot afford to miss. Few false negatives mean a negative result is reassuring.
- **SpPin**: a highly **Sp**ecific test, when **P**ositive, rules **in** disease. Use it as a confirmatory test, especially when a false positive leads to risky treatment. Few false positives mean a positive result is meaningful.

Typical pattern: a sensitive screening test first (for example a fourth-generation HIV antigen/antibody immunoassay), then a highly specific confirmatory test on positives (for HIV, an antibody differentiation assay, with an RNA test if results disagree).

### The cutoff trade-off

For a test with a continuous result (troponin, glucose, PSA), the choice of cutoff sets the balance.

- **Lowering** the threshold for a positive result catches more diseased people (sensitivity up) but also labels more healthy people positive (specificity down).
- **Raising** the threshold does the opposite.

Choose the cutoff according to the cost of a missed case versus the cost of a false alarm.

### ROC curves

A **receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve** plots sensitivity (true-positive rate) on the vertical axis against $1 -$ specificity (false-positive rate) on the horizontal axis, with each point corresponding to a cutoff. The diagonal line represents a useless test. The **area under the curve (AUC)** summarizes discrimination: 0.5 means no better than chance, 1.0 is perfect. A curve that hugs the upper left corner is best, and the point closest to that corner is a common compromise cutoff.

### Accuracy and a caution

$$\text{accuracy} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN}.$$

Accuracy mixes the two kinds of error and depends on prevalence, so sensitivity and specificity are more informative. A test that calls everyone negative has high accuracy in a low-prevalence population and zero sensitivity.
@example
title: Sensitivity and specificity from a 2x2 table
problem: A rapid test is compared with culture in 1000 patients. Of 100 culture-positive patients, 80 test positive. Of 900 culture-negative patients, 90 test positive. Find sensitivity, specificity and the false-positive rate.
step: Fill the table: $TP = 80$; $FN = 100 - 80 = 20$; $FP = 90$; $TN = 900 - 90 = 810$.
step: Sensitivity $= TP/(TP+FN) = 80/100 = 0.80$.
step: Specificity $= TN/(TN+FP) = 810/900 = 0.90$.
step: False-positive rate $= 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$. Because sensitivity is only 80%, a negative result cannot safely rule out disease, while the specificity of 90% means a positive result is reasonably informative.
@mistakes
- Using the wrong denominator: sensitivity divides by everyone **with** the disease, not by everyone who tests positive.
- Confusing SnNout with SpPin; a negative result from a sensitive test rules out, and a positive from a specific test rules in.
- Believing sensitivity and specificity depend directly on disease prevalence in the textbook model (predictive values do).
- Reading a high accuracy as proof that a test is good when prevalence is very low.

prompt: A test has a specificity of 92%. What is its false-positive rate, as a percent?
answer: 8
check: 100 - 92
unit: %
hint: False positives and true negatives together make up all people without disease.
solution: False-positive rate $= 1 - \text{specificity} = 1 - 0.92 = 0.08$, or 8%.

prompt: A new test is positive in 68 of 85 patients with the disease and positive in 60 of 400 patients without it. What is the specificity, as a percent?
answer: 85
check: (400 - 60) / 400 * 100
unit: %
hint: Count true negatives among those without disease: those who are not positive.
solution: $TN = 400 - 60 = 340$; $340/400 = 0.85 = 85\%$.
