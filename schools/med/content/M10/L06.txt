@article
Sensitivity and specificity describe a test given the disease status. At the bedside the question is reversed: given this result, what is the chance my patient has the disease? That is answered by the **predictive values**.

### Predictive values

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}, \qquad \text{NPV} = \frac{TN}{TN + FN}.$$

- **PPV** is the probability of disease given a positive test.
- **NPV** is the probability of no disease given a negative test.

They use the **rows** of the 2x2 table and therefore depend on **prevalence** (pre-test probability) as well as on test quality.

### Prevalence changes predictive values

Hold sensitivity and specificity fixed. As prevalence **rises**, there are more diseased people, so more of the positives are true: **PPV increases and NPV decreases**. As prevalence **falls**, PPV drops and NPV rises. This is why a screening test in a low-risk population has many false positives, even if its specificity is 95%, and why the same test is far more useful in a symptomatic patient with high pre-test probability.

The reliable method is to build a table for a hypothetical population. For prevalence 1%, sensitivity 95%, specificity 95%, take 10,000 people: 100 diseased (95 TP, 5 FN) and 9,900 healthy (495 FP, 9,405 TN). PPV $= 95/(95+495) = 16.1\%$, even though the test looks excellent.

### Likelihood ratios

A **likelihood ratio** (LR) tells you how much a result changes the odds of disease, without needing prevalence:

$$LR^{+} = \frac{\text{sensitivity}}{1 - \text{specificity}}, \qquad LR^{-} = \frac{1 - \text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}.$$

- $LR^+$ is how much more likely a positive result is in diseased than healthy people. Larger is better: above 10 gives a large (often conclusive) increase in probability; 5 to 10 moderate; 2 to 5 small; near 1 almost none.
- $LR^-$ is how much more likely a negative result is in diseased than healthy people. Smaller is better: below 0.1 gives a large decrease; 0.1 to 0.2 moderate; 0.2 to 0.5 small.
- An LR of 1 leaves the probability unchanged.

LRs can also be calculated for multilevel or continuous results (an LR for each range of values), which uses more information than a single cutoff.

### Updating probability with Bayes' theorem

Likelihood ratios multiply **odds**, not probabilities. The steps:

1. Convert the pre-test probability $p$ to odds: $\text{odds} = p/(1-p)$.
2. Multiply by the LR: $\text{post-test odds} = \text{pre-test odds} \times LR$.
3. Convert back: $p_{\text{post}} = \dfrac{\text{odds}}{1 + \text{odds}}$.

The **Fagan nomogram** performs the same steps graphically. Pre-test probability is your estimate from prevalence, history and examination (the clinician's gestalt, or a validated score).

### Why it matters

A positive result in a patient with a low pre-test probability usually remains likely to be a false positive; a negative result in a patient with a high pre-test probability usually should not be believed without more testing. Test results should always be interpreted in context.

Remember the pairing: sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test; PPV and NPV are properties of the test **in a population with a given prevalence**; LRs are properties of the test and can be applied to any pre-test probability.
@example
title: PPV, NPV and a likelihood-ratio update
problem: A test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%. A patient has a pre-test probability of 10%. Find the PPV by building a table for 1000 people, then confirm it with likelihood ratios.
step: With prevalence 10%, 1000 people have 100 diseased and 900 healthy. Diseased: $TP = 80$, $FN = 20$. Healthy: $TN = 810$, $FP = 90$.
step: $\text{PPV} = 80/(80+90) = 0.471$ and $\text{NPV} = 810/(810+20) = 0.976$.
step: $LR^+ = 0.80/(1-0.90) = 8$. Pre-test odds $= 0.10/0.90 = 0.111$.
step: Post-test odds $= 0.111 \times 8 = 0.889$. Probability $= 0.889/(1+0.889) = 0.471$, matching the PPV: a positive result raises the probability from 10% to 47%.
@mistakes
- Multiplying probability (rather than odds) by the likelihood ratio.
- Quoting a PPV from one population as if it applied to a population with a different prevalence.
- Calculating the likelihood ratio as sensitivity divided by specificity, or using $1 - \text{sensitivity}$ in the numerator of $LR^+$.
- Treating a negative test as ruling out disease regardless of the pre-test probability.
