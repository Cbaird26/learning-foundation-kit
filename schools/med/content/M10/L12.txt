@article
Evidence tells us what works; **quality improvement (QI)** is the discipline of making it happen reliably in a real system. **Patient safety** is the part of quality concerned with avoiding harm from care itself. Both rest on a systems view: most errors come from poorly designed processes rather than from careless individuals.

### The Model for Improvement

Three questions come first:

1. **What are we trying to accomplish?** (a specific, measurable aim with a time frame)
2. **How will we know a change is an improvement?** (measures)
3. **What change can we make that will result in improvement?**

The changes are tested in **PDSA cycles**: **Plan** (the change, prediction and data to collect), **Do** (run a small test, one patient or one shift), **Study** (compare results with the prediction), **Act** (adopt, adapt or abandon, then scale up). Small, rapid cycles let a team learn cheaply and refine the change before a wide rollout.

### Types of measures

- **Outcome measures**: the result patients care about (infection rate, mortality, readmission).
- **Process measures**: whether the steps are done (hand-hygiene compliance, antibiotic given within 1 hour).
- **Balancing measures**: possible unintended harms elsewhere in the system (for example, readmission rates when you shorten length of stay; staff overtime when you add a checklist).

Rates should use a meaningful denominator. For example, a central-line infection rate is infections per **1000 central-line days**, and a fall rate is falls per **1000 patient-days**, not a raw count, so that changes in volume do not mislead.

### Run charts and control charts

A **run chart** plots a measure over time with the median as the center line. A **control chart** adds an upper and lower **control limit**, usually the mean $\pm$ 3 standard deviations (sigma). The pattern distinguishes:

- **Common-cause variation**: the random scatter inherent in a stable process. Do not react to individual points.
- **Special-cause variation**: signals such as a point beyond a control limit, a run of 6 to 8 consecutive points on one side of the center line, or a sustained trend. These suggest a real change in the process and should be investigated.

Reacting to common-cause variation as if it were special wastes effort and can make the process worse.

### Understanding error

**Reason's Swiss cheese model**: every defence layer (policies, checklists, alarms, double-checks) has holes; harm occurs when holes in several layers line up. Layers include **active failures** (errors at the sharp end) and **latent conditions** (design, staffing, culture) that make them likely.

- **Near miss**: an error that did not reach the patient. Reporting near misses is a key source of learning.
- **Adverse event**: harm from care. **Sentinel event**: an unexpected event involving death or serious harm that triggers a thorough review. Some **never events** (wrong-site surgery) are considered preventable by definition.
- **Root cause analysis (RCA)** is a structured review after serious events to identify system causes ("five whys", fishbone diagram), and **failure mode and effects analysis (FMEA)** looks ahead to anticipate how a process could fail.
- **Just culture** distinguishes human error (console), at-risk behavior (coach) and reckless behavior (discipline), and encourages reporting.

### Strength of interventions

Strong actions change the system (**forcing functions** such as connectors that prevent wrong-route administration, removing concentrated electrolytes from ward stock, automation, standardization). Intermediate actions include checklists, double-checks and software alerts. **Weak actions** depend on memory and vigilance: education, new policies, warnings. Strong actions are more reliable, so favor them.

### Communication and disclosure

Standardized handoffs (**I-PASS**) and **SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) reduce communication failures, a leading contributor to harm. When an error reaches a patient, clinicians have an ethical duty to **disclose** it promptly and honestly, express regret, explain what is known, and describe steps to prevent recurrence.

### Appraising a QI report

A before-and-after study without a comparison group is vulnerable to **secular trends**, **regression to the mean** (a bad period tends to be followed by a better one) and other simultaneous changes. Look for a time series with enough points, a comparison group, balancing measures and sustained results.
@example
title: Reading a control chart signal
problem: A ward's monthly falls average 3 per 1000 patient-days with a standard deviation of 0.5. This month, after a new sensor-mat rollout, the rate is 1.2 per 1000 patient-days. Is this a special-cause signal?
step: Control limits are the mean $\pm 3$ standard deviations: upper $= 3 + 3(0.5) = 4.5$, lower $= 3 - 3(0.5) = 1.5$ per 1000 patient-days.
step: This month's value of 1.2 is below the lower control limit of 1.5, so it is a special-cause signal and not ordinary scatter.
step: A single point is suggestive. The team should confirm it persists (a run of consecutive points below the center line), check that the measurement did not change, and examine a balancing measure such as patient restraint use or staff workload.
step: If the lower rate is sustained, the PDSA test can be adopted and spread; if not, the point may be a fluke.
@mistakes
- Reacting to every month-to-month fluctuation as if it were a real change (treating common-cause variation as special).
- Using raw counts instead of rates with a suitable denominator such as patient-days.
- Choosing weak interventions (a memo or retraining) for problems that need system redesign.
- Declaring success from a simple before-and-after comparison without considering secular trends and regression to the mean.

answer: 1.5
check: 6 / 4000 * 1000
unit: per 1000 central-line days
hint: Divide infections by line-days, then scale to 1000.

@practice num
prompt: A process has a mean of 10 events per week and a standard deviation of 2. What is the upper control limit (mean plus 3 standard deviations)?
answer: 16
check: 10 + 3 * 2
hint: Control limits are the mean plus or minus three sigma.
solution: $10 + 3(2) = 16$ events per week. A point above 16 suggests special-cause variation.
@practice mc
prompt: A hospital program aims to shorten emergency department length of stay. Which is the best balancing measure?
choice: Median length of stay
choice: Percentage of patients seen by a physician within 30 minutes
choice*: Return visits within 72 hours and rates of admission after discharge
choice: Number of staff trained in the new process
hint: A balancing measure detects unintended harm elsewhere.
solution: Faster throughput could cause premature discharge, which shows up as returns and admissions. Median length of stay is the outcome measure, physician time is a process measure and training counts are a process measure.
@practice self
prompt: Read this abstract and appraise it with a checklist. "A hospital introduced a sepsis bundle in its emergency department. In the 3 months before introduction, 24 of 120 patients with sepsis died (20%). In the 3 months after, 15 of 125 died (12%). We conclude that the bundle reduced sepsis mortality (p = 0.09)." List at least four strengths or weaknesses, state whether the conclusion is justified, and describe what additional data you would want.
hint: Ask what comparison group, time frame, and measures the report has, and whether the p-value supports the conclusion.
solution: This is an uncontrolled before-and-after study. The mortality fell from 20% to 12% (ARR 8 percentage points, NNT about 12.5), but the difference is not statistically significant (p = 0.09), so the conclusion that the bundle reduced mortality is not justified. Weaknesses: no comparison group; only 3 months before and after, so secular trends and regression to the mean (a bad period followed by a better one) are plausible; changes in case mix or sepsis definition, or other simultaneous interventions, could explain the change; small numbers give a wide confidence interval. No balancing measures (for example, antibiotic overuse or fluid overload) and no process measures (bundle compliance) are reported. A strength is a patient-important outcome (death) with a clear denominator. I would want a longer run or control chart, a comparison unit, adjustment for severity, process compliance data and balancing measures.
rubric: Notes that there is no control group and that secular trends and regression to the mean could explain the change
rubric: Notes that p = 0.09 is not statistically significant and that the sample and time frame are small
rubric: Asks for process (compliance) and balancing measures, case-mix adjustment or a time series
rubric: Reaches a conclusion that matches the evidence (suggestive but unproven) rather than accepting or dismissing it outright

@quiz mc
prompt: After a patient is harmed by an accidental injection of concentrated potassium chloride that was stocked on a ward, a root cause analysis lists four possible actions. Which is the strongest (most reliable) action?
choice: Retrain all nurses on the safe handling of concentrated electrolytes
choice: Issue a new policy memo reminding staff to double-check labels
choice*: Remove concentrated potassium chloride from ward stock and supply premixed solutions from pharmacy
choice: Add a warning sticker to the vial
hint: Strong actions change the system so that the error cannot easily happen.
solution: Removing the hazard from the environment (a forcing function or standardization) does not depend on memory or vigilance. Training, memos and stickers are weak actions.
