@article
An observed association can arise from four sources: **truth**, **chance**, **bias** and **confounding**. Chance is handled by statistics (later lessons). This lesson covers the other two. **Bias** is a systematic error in the design, conduct or analysis that pushes the result away from the truth. Unlike chance, it does not shrink as the sample size grows.

### Selection bias

Selection bias arises when the people included in the analysis differ systematically from the target population, or when the groups differ in ways other than the exposure.

- **Berkson bias**: hospital-based controls and cases are both more likely to have had exposures leading to admission, creating spurious associations.
- **Healthy worker effect**: workers are healthier than the general population, so occupational cohorts look deceptively safe when compared with the general population.
- **Volunteer (self-selection) bias**: people who volunteer differ from those who do not.
- **Loss to follow-up (attrition bias)**: if people leave a cohort or trial for reasons related to exposure and outcome, the remaining groups are no longer comparable.
- **Non-response bias**: responders differ from non-responders.

### Information (measurement) bias

Information bias occurs when exposure or outcome is measured with systematic error.

- **Recall bias**: cases remember or report past exposures differently from controls (typical of case-control studies; mothers of children with a birth defect search their memory harder).
- **Interviewer (observer) bias**: knowing who is a case changes how the interviewer asks or records.
- **Misclassification**: **non-differential** misclassification (the same error in both groups) usually biases the association toward the null; **differential** misclassification can push it in either direction.
- **Reporting bias and social desirability bias**: people under-report stigmatized behaviors.
- **Hawthorne effect**: people change behavior because they know they are observed.

Blinding of participants and assessors, standardized protocols and objective measures reduce information bias.

### Biases of screening

**Lead-time bias** makes survival after diagnosis appear longer simply because the disease is found earlier, without delaying death. **Length-time bias** occurs because screening preferentially detects slow-growing disease. **Overdiagnosis** detects disease that would never have caused harm. These are treated further in the screening lesson.

### Confounding

A **confounder** is a third variable that distorts the true exposure-outcome relationship. It must satisfy all three criteria:

1. It is associated with the **exposure**.
2. It is an independent **risk factor for the outcome** (among the unexposed).
3. It is **not on the causal pathway** between exposure and outcome (not a mediator).

Classic example: coffee drinking appears associated with lung cancer, but coffee drinkers are more likely to smoke, and smoking causes lung cancer. Smoking confounds the apparent link.

**Detecting confounding**: compare the **crude** estimate with the **adjusted** estimate (stratified or from a regression model). If they differ appreciably (a common rule of thumb is more than about 10% difference), confounding is present. Stratified estimates that are similar to each other but different from the crude one indicate confounding.

### Effect modification

**Effect modification** (interaction) is a different thing. The true effect of the exposure differs across levels of a third variable. Here the stratum-specific estimates **differ from each other** (for example, a drug halves risk in women but has no effect in men). Effect modification is a real finding to be **reported**, not a nuisance to be removed, whereas confounding is a bias to be removed.

### Controlling confounding

| Stage | Method | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Design | **Randomization** | Controls known and unknown confounders |
| Design | **Restriction** | Include only one level (for example, non-smokers); limits generalizability |
| Design | **Matching** | Match on age and sex; must be accounted for in analysis |
| Analysis | **Stratification** | Mantel-Haenszel pooled estimate |
| Analysis | **Multivariable regression** | Adjusts for many measured confounders |
| Analysis | **Standardization** | Age-adjusted rates |

Only randomization controls for **unmeasured** confounders; other methods control only the ones you measured. Residual confounding remains in all observational studies.
@example
title: Detecting confounding by stratification
problem: A study of 400 people finds that coffee drinkers (200) have more lung cancer than non-drinkers (200): 34 cases among drinkers and 16 among non-drinkers. Stratifying by smoking gives: smokers, 32 of 160 drinkers and 8 of 40 non-drinkers with cancer; non-smokers, 2 of 40 drinkers and 8 of 160 non-drinkers with cancer. Is smoking a confounder?
step: Crude RR $= (34/200)/(16/200) = 0.17/0.08 = 2.125$. Coffee appears to more than double the risk.
step: Among smokers, risks are $32/160 = 0.20$ in drinkers and $8/40 = 0.20$ in non-drinkers, so $\text{RR} = 1$.
step: Among non-smokers, risks are $2/40 = 0.05$ and $8/160 = 0.05$, so $\text{RR} = 1$.
step: Within each stratum there is no association, but the crude RR is 2.1. Coffee drinkers are mostly smokers (160 of 200 against 40 of 200 among non-drinkers) and smoking raises cancer risk, so smoking is a confounder. The stratum-specific RRs are equal to each other, so there is no effect modification.
@mistakes
- Calling any third variable a confounder; a variable on the causal pathway (a mediator) should not be adjusted for.
- Believing that a larger sample size reduces bias; it reduces random error only.
- Confusing confounding (a bias to remove) with effect modification (a real difference in effect to report).
- Assuming matching or regression removes all confounding; unmeasured and residual confounding remain except with randomization.

choice: Berkson bias
choice*: Recall bias
choice: Lead-time bias
choice: Healthy worker effect
hint: Think about differences in the accuracy of remembering past exposure between cases and controls.
solution: Cases (or their mothers) search their memory more thoroughly than controls, which is recall bias, a differential information bias common in case-control studies.
@practice mc
prompt: A cohort study of the health effects of a chemical compares exposed factory workers with the general population and finds lower overall mortality in workers. Which is the most likely explanation?
choice: Recall bias
choice: Confounding by a mediator
choice*: Healthy worker effect
choice: Lead-time bias
hint: Who is well enough to hold a job?
solution: People who are employed are healthier on average than the general population, which includes the sick and disabled; this is the healthy worker effect, a selection bias.

@practice ms
prompt: Which properties must a variable have to be a confounder of an exposure-outcome association? Select all that apply.
choice*: It is associated with the exposure
choice*: It is an independent risk factor for the outcome
choice*: It is not an intermediate step on the causal pathway from exposure to outcome
choice: It is caused by the outcome
choice: It has the same prevalence in exposed and unexposed groups
hint: Confounding requires imbalance between exposure groups and an effect on the outcome.
solution: A confounder is associated with exposure, independently predicts outcome and is not a mediator. A variable that is equally distributed between exposure groups cannot confound, and one caused by the outcome is not a confounder.
@practice mc
prompt: A drug lowers the risk of an outcome by half in women (relative risk 0.5) but has no effect in men (relative risk 1.0). The crude relative risk is 0.75. Which term best describes the difference between men and women?
choice: Confounding
choice: Selection bias
choice*: Effect modification
choice: Recall bias
hint: Do the stratum-specific estimates agree with one another?
solution: The effect of the drug truly differs between strata, so sex is an effect modifier. This should be reported as a finding rather than adjusted away.
@quiz num
prompt: A study reports: exposed older adults, 24 of 120 with the outcome; unexposed older adults, 16 of 80; exposed younger adults, 2 of 80; unexposed younger adults, 8 of 320. Pooling both age groups, what is the crude relative risk for the exposed versus the unexposed? Round to two decimals.
answer: 2.17
check: ((24+2)/(120+80)) / ((16+8)/(80+320))
tol: 0.01
hint: Add the exposed groups together and the unexposed groups together.
hint: Exposed: 26 cases among 200. Unexposed: 24 among 400.
solution: Exposed risk $= 26/200 = 0.13$; unexposed risk $= 24/400 = 0.06$; $\text{RR} = 2.17$. Within each age group the RR is 1, so age confounds the crude result.
@quiz mc
prompt: A trial of an exercise program loses 30% of the intervention arm to follow-up, mostly participants who felt unwell, but only 5% of the control arm. The analysis includes only completers. Which bias is the main threat to validity?
choice: Recall bias
choice: Lead-time bias
choice*: Selection (attrition) bias
choice: Hawthorne effect
hint: Who remains in the groups being compared, and is it related to the outcome?
solution: Differential loss of participants related to outcome makes the remaining groups non-comparable, which is attrition bias, a form of selection bias.
