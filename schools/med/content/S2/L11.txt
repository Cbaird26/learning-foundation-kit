@article
The **pleural space** between the visceral and parietal pleura normally holds a few milliliters of fluid. Fluid accumulates when hydrostatic or oncotic pressures change, or when inflammation, malignancy or lymphatic obstruction raises capillary permeability or impairs drainage.

### Recognizing an effusion
Symptoms are dyspnea and pleuritic pain, or none. Examination: **dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds and decreased tactile fremitus** over the fluid, with egophony and bronchial breathing at its upper margin. An upright PA chest film shows blunting of the costophrenic angle once about 200 mL or more is present; a lateral view detects about 50 mL; ultrasound and CT are more sensitive. A lateral decubitus film shows that fluid layers if free-flowing. A large effusion shifts the mediastinum away; if the mediastinum shifts **toward** the opaque hemithorax, suspect atelectasis from an obstructing bronchial lesion. Ultrasound guides **thoracentesis**, with the needle inserted just above the rib to avoid the neurovascular bundle running under the lower border. Complications: pneumothorax, bleeding, re-expansion pulmonary edema if too much is removed (stop at about 1.5 L or with cough or chest discomfort).

### Light's criteria
An effusion is an **exudate** if it meets **at least one** of:
1. pleural fluid protein / serum protein above **0.5**,
2. pleural fluid LDH / serum LDH above **0.6**,
3. pleural fluid LDH above **two-thirds of the upper limit of normal** for serum LDH.

If none are met, it is a **transudate**. Light's criteria are sensitive for exudates but can misclassify patients who have been diuresed (a transudate from heart failure appears exudative); a serum-minus-pleural albumin gradient above 1.2 g/dL supports a transudate in that case.

**Transudates** (systemic imbalance, not pleural disease): heart failure (the commonest), cirrhosis (hepatic hydrothorax), nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminemia, peritoneal dialysis. **Exudates**: parapneumonic effusion and empyema, malignancy, pulmonary embolism, tuberculosis, pancreatitis, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, esophageal rupture, post-cardiac injury syndromes.

### Further fluid analysis
- **pH below 7.20, glucose below 60 mg/dL, LDH above 1000 U/L or gross pus or positive Gram stain:** complicated parapneumonic effusion or **empyema**, requiring **drainage** (chest tube) plus antibiotics; fibrinolytics or surgery (VATS) for loculations.
- **Low glucose** also in rheumatoid pleurisy, tuberculosis and malignancy.
- **Lymphocyte-predominant exudate**: tuberculosis (high adenosine deaminase) and malignancy; cytology for malignant cells; pleural biopsy if needed.
- **Triglycerides above 110 mg/dL** with milky fluid: **chylothorax** (thoracic duct injury or obstruction from lymphoma or surgery).
- **High amylase**: pancreatitis or esophageal rupture (low pH).
- **Bloody fluid** with pleural hematocrit above half the blood hematocrit: **hemothorax**.
- Eosinophilia: blood or air, drugs, parasites, asbestos.

**Malignant effusions** often recur: options include repeated thoracentesis, an **indwelling pleural catheter**, or **pleurodesis** (talc). Therapeutic drainage is also the treatment for large symptomatic effusions.

### Pneumothorax
Air in the pleural space. **Primary spontaneous** occurs in tall, thin young men and smokers from rupture of apical subpleural blebs. **Secondary** occurs with underlying lung disease (COPD, cystic fibrosis, Pneumocystis pneumonia, Marfan syndrome, lymphangioleiomyomatosis). **Traumatic** and **iatrogenic** (central line, transthoracic biopsy, ventilator barotrauma) causes are also common. Presentation: sudden pleuritic pain and dyspnea, with hyperresonance and decreased breath sounds and fremitus on the affected side. Chest radiograph shows a visceral pleural line with no lung markings beyond.

**Management:** a small, minimally symptomatic primary spontaneous pneumothorax can be observed with oxygen and follow-up imaging; a larger or symptomatic one needs needle aspiration or a small-bore chest tube. Secondary pneumothorax and recurrent episodes usually need chest tube drainage and a definitive plan (VATS with pleurodesis or bullectomy).

**Tension pneumothorax:** a one-way valve traps air, raising intrapleural pressure, collapsing the lung, shifting the mediastinum **away**, kinking the vena cavae and causing obstructive shock: severe dyspnea, hypotension, tachycardia, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, tracheal deviation. It is a **clinical diagnosis**; do not wait for imaging. Treat at once with **needle decompression** (in adults, ATLS 10th edition prefers the fourth or fifth intercostal space just anterior to the midaxillary line; the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line is the traditional alternative), then place a chest tube. Chest tubes are inserted in the "triangle of safety" (anterior to the midaxillary line, above the nipple line and behind the lateral border of the pectoralis major), above a rib.

@example
title: Applying Light's criteria
problem: A 70-year-old woman has a right pleural effusion. Pleural fluid protein is 4.2 g/dL and serum protein is 6.4 g/dL. Pleural LDH is 310 U/L and serum LDH is 380 U/L (laboratory upper limit of normal 250 U/L). Classify the effusion.
step: Protein ratio: $4.2/6.4 = 0.66$, which is above 0.5. One criterion alone makes it an exudate.
step: Check the others: LDH ratio $310/380 = 0.82$ (above 0.6), and $310$ exceeds two-thirds of 250, which is 167 U/L.
step: All three criteria are met: an exudate. Look for pleural causes (pneumonia, malignancy, PE, TB) with cell count, pH, glucose and cytology.

@example
title: When to drain a parapneumonic effusion
problem: A 45-year-old man has pneumonia and a right pleural effusion. Thoracentesis returns cloudy fluid with pH 7.05, glucose 30 mg/dL and a Gram stain showing gram-positive cocci. What is the next step?
step: A pH below 7.20 with low glucose and a positive Gram stain indicates a complicated parapneumonic effusion or empyema.
step: Antibiotics alone fail because the thick, loculated fluid is hard for drugs to penetrate and the fibrin forms a peel.
step: Insert a chest tube for drainage, continue antibiotics, and consider intrapleural fibrinolytics or VATS if drainage fails.

@mistakes
- Treating a tension pneumothorax with a chest radiograph first.
- Applying Light's criteria to decide the cause rather than the category: they only separate exudates from transudates.
- Inserting a needle or tube below the rib instead of above it.
- Calling a diuresed heart-failure effusion malignant because it meets Light's LDH criterion.
