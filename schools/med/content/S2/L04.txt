@article
Blood is the delivery truck. How much oxygen reaches tissue depends mostly on hemoglobin, much less on the pressure of dissolved oxygen.

### Oxygen content and delivery
Each gram of fully saturated hemoglobin binds 1.34 mL of O$_2$, and dissolved O$_2$ contributes 0.003 mL per dL per mmHg:
$$CaO_2 = 1.34 \times Hb \times SaO_2 + 0.003 \times PaO_2 \quad (\text{mL O}_2/\text{dL}).$$
For Hb 15 g/dL, SaO$_2$ 0.98 and PaO$_2$ 100: $1.34 \times 15 \times 0.98 + 0.3 \approx 20$ mL/dL. Dissolved oxygen is only about 1.5 percent of the total. **Oxygen delivery** is
$$DO_2 = CO \times CaO_2 \times 10 \quad (\text{mL/min}),$$
with cardiac output in L/min. Normal DO$_2$ is about 1000 mL/min. Anemia lowers $CaO_2$ without changing PaO$_2$ or SaO$_2$, so a patient with severe anemia can have a normal ABG and still be hypoxic at the tissue level. Mixed venous saturation is about 75 percent at rest.

### The oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve
The curve is sigmoid because of cooperative binding: loading in the lung is easy at high PO$_2$ (the flat upper portion protects loading when PO$_2$ falls moderately), and unloading is easy in tissue (the steep lower portion). **P50**, the PO$_2$ at 50 percent saturation, is about 27 mmHg.

- **Right shift** (higher P50, lower affinity, more unloading): increased CO$_2$ and H$^+$ (the **Bohr effect**), increased temperature, increased 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG, raised in chronic hypoxemia, high altitude and anemia), exercise.
- **Left shift** (lower P50, higher affinity, less unloading): decreased CO$_2$, alkalosis, cold, decreased 2,3-BPG, **fetal hemoglobin** (HbF binds 2,3-BPG poorly), carbon monoxide and methemoglobin.

### Carbon monoxide and methemoglobin
**Carbon monoxide** binds hemoglobin with about 200-fold the affinity of O$_2$, reducing O$_2$-carrying capacity and left-shifting the curve. Presentation: headache, nausea, confusion, syncope, lactic acidosis; after fires or faulty heaters. Routine **pulse oximetry reads falsely normal** because it cannot distinguish carboxyhemoglobin from oxyhemoglobin, and PaO$_2$ is also normal; diagnosis needs **co-oximetry** (COHb level). Treatment is 100% oxygen (shortens the half-life of COHb from about 4 to 6 hours to about 1 hour); hyperbaric oxygen is considered for severe cases (loss of consciousness, high COHb, pregnancy, ischemic ECG changes, neurological deficits). Delayed neurological sequelae can occur.

**Methemoglobinemia** occurs when iron is oxidized from Fe$^{2+}$ to Fe$^{3+}$ (causes: dapsone, benzocaine and other local anesthetics, nitrites, G6PD deficiency stress). Patients are cyanotic with **chocolate-brown blood**, a pulse oximeter stuck near 85 percent that does not improve with oxygen, and a normal PaO$_2$. Treatment is **methylene blue** (avoid in G6PD deficiency, where it is ineffective and may cause hemolysis; ascorbic acid or exchange transfusion are alternatives). **Cyanide** poisoning, from smoke inhalation, blocks the electron transport chain; give hydroxocobalamin.

### CO$_2$ transport
About 70 percent of CO$_2$ is carried as **bicarbonate**: in the red cell carbonic anhydrase converts CO$_2$ + H$_2$O to H$_2$CO$_3$, which dissociates; HCO$_3^-$ leaves in exchange for Cl$^-$ (the **chloride shift**). About 20 to 25 percent is bound to hemoglobin as **carbaminohemoglobin**, and 5 to 10 percent is dissolved. In the lung, oxygenated hemoglobin binds CO$_2$ less well (**Haldane effect**), releasing CO$_2$ for exhalation.

### Reading a blood gas
The **Henderson-Hasselbalch equation** is
$$pH = 6.1 + \log_{10}\frac{[HCO_3^-]}{0.03 \times PaCO_2}.$$
Normal pH is 7.35 to 7.45, PaCO$_2$ 35 to 45 mmHg, HCO$_3^-$ 22 to 28 mEq/L. Compensation rules (study values; textbooks differ slightly):
- Acute respiratory acidosis: HCO$_3^-$ rises about 1 per 10 mmHg rise in PaCO$_2$; chronic: about 3.5 to 4.
- Acute respiratory alkalosis: HCO$_3^-$ falls about 2 per 10 mmHg fall; chronic: about 4 to 5.
- Metabolic acidosis: **Winters formula**, expected PaCO$_2 = 1.5 \times HCO_3^- + 8 \pm 2$.
- Anion gap $= Na - (Cl + HCO_3)$, normal about 8 to 12.

Compare the measured compensation with the expected one; a mismatch means a second disorder.

@example
title: Oxygen content and delivery
problem: A patient has Hb 15 g/dL, SaO$_2$ 98 percent, PaO$_2$ 100 mmHg and a cardiac output of 5 L/min. Calculate the arterial O$_2$ content and oxygen delivery.
step: Bound oxygen: $1.34 \times 15 \times 0.98 = 19.7$ mL/dL.
step: Dissolved oxygen: $0.003 \times 100 = 0.3$ mL/dL, so $CaO_2 = 20.0$ mL/dL.
step: Delivery: $DO_2 = 5 \times 20.0 \times 10 = 1000$ mL/min. The factor of 10 converts mL/dL to mL/L.

@example
title: Anemia with a normal blood gas
problem: A woman with a gastrointestinal bleed has Hb 7.5 g/dL, SaO$_2$ 98 percent and PaO$_2$ 95 mmHg. Estimate $CaO_2$ and compare with a patient with Hb 15 g/dL, SaO$_2$ 98 percent and PaO$_2$ 100 mmHg (CaO$_2$ 20.0 mL/dL).
step: Bound: $1.34 \times 7.5 \times 0.98 = 9.85$ mL/dL.
step: Dissolved: $0.003 \times 95 = 0.29$ mL/dL, total about 10.1 mL/dL.
step: This is half the content of a patient with Hb 15, though the PaO$_2$ and SaO$_2$ are normal. Oxygen content tracks hemoglobin: the remedy is transfusion and treating the bleed, not supplemental oxygen alone.

@mistakes
- Believing PaO$_2$ measures oxygen content; it reflects only dissolved oxygen.
- Trusting the pulse oximeter in carbon monoxide poisoning and methemoglobinemia.
- Forgetting the factor of 10 when computing delivery from cardiac output in L/min and content in mL/dL.
- Mixing up the direction of the shift: increased CO$_2$, H$^+$, temperature and 2,3-BPG shift the curve to the **right**.
