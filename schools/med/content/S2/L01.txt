@article
You move about 500 mL of air in and out roughly 12 to 16 times a minute without thinking about it. The anatomy decides where the air can go, and the mechanics decide how much work it takes to get it there.

### The airway tree
The **conducting zone** (trachea to terminal bronchioles, about generations 0 to 16) only carries air. It has no gas exchange and forms the **anatomic dead space**, roughly 150 mL in an adult (about 2 mL per kg of ideal body weight). Large airways have cartilage, ciliated pseudostratified epithelium and goblet cells. **Bronchioles** have no cartilage and no glands, so they stay open only because of radial traction from surrounding lung tissue; this is why they collapse in emphysema. **Club cells** line the terminal bronchioles, secrete part of the surfactant-associated and protective proteins, detoxify inhaled agents and act as progenitor cells.

The **respiratory zone** (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts, alveoli) does the gas exchange. The right lung has 3 lobes (upper, middle, lower) and the left has 2 (upper with the lingula, and lower). The right main bronchus is shorter, wider and more vertical than the left, so aspirated objects usually enter the right side: the right lower lobe when upright, and the superior segment of the right lower lobe or the posterior segment of the right upper lobe when supine.

### The blood-gas barrier
Oxygen crosses a barrier under 1 micrometer thick: type I pneumocyte, fused basement membrane, capillary endothelium. **Type I pneumocytes** cover about 95 percent of the alveolar surface. **Type II pneumocytes** are cuboidal, make **surfactant** (mostly dipalmitoylphosphatidylcholine, DPPC), and divide to replace injured type I cells.

### Muscles and nerves
The **diaphragm** (phrenic nerve, roots C3, C4, C5: "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive") does most of the work of quiet inspiration, aided by the external intercostals. Accessory muscles (sternocleidomastoid, scalenes) are recruited in distress. Quiet expiration is passive elastic recoil; forced expiration uses the abdominal muscles and internal intercostals. The diaphragm is pierced by the IVC (T8), esophagus (T10) and aorta (T12).

### Pressures and functional residual capacity
At the end of a quiet expiration, no muscle is working and the system is at **functional residual capacity (FRC)**. The lung wants to collapse inward, and the chest wall wants to spring outward. FRC is the volume where these two recoils balance. The pleural space sits between them at about $-5$ cmH$_2$O. Transpulmonary pressure is alveolar pressure minus pleural pressure.

- **Emphysema** destroys elastic tissue: less inward recoil, so FRC rises and the chest becomes barrel-shaped.
- **Pulmonary fibrosis** makes the lung stiff: more inward recoil, so FRC falls.

### Compliance
**Compliance** is the volume change per unit pressure change:
$$C = \frac{\Delta V}{\Delta P}.$$
Normal total respiratory-system compliance is on the order of 100 mL/cmH$_2$O in a healthy adult; on a ventilator, **static compliance** is $V_T/(P_{plat} - PEEP)$. Compliance is high in emphysema and aging, and low in fibrosis, pulmonary edema, ARDS and atelectasis.

### Surfactant and Laplace
For a sphere, $P = \dfrac{2T}{r}$ (Laplace). Small alveoli would generate higher pressures than large ones and empty into them. Surfactant lowers surface tension more in small alveoli, stabilizing them, increasing compliance and reducing the work of breathing. Premature infants lack it: **neonatal respiratory distress syndrome** presents with grunting, retractions and cyanosis; chest film shows diffuse ground-glass ("reticulogranular") opacities with air bronchograms; a lecithin-sphingomyelin ratio of 2 or more in amniotic fluid predicts lung maturity. Antenatal corticosteroids (betamethasone) accelerate maturation; treatment is exogenous surfactant plus CPAP or ventilation.

### Airway resistance
In laminar flow (Poiseuille), $R \propto \dfrac{\eta L}{r^4}$. Halving the radius multiplies resistance by 16. In a healthy lung most of the resistance is in the medium-sized bronchi, because the huge combined cross-section of the small airways keeps their total resistance low; this is why small-airway disease can hide before spirometry changes. Lung volume matters too: at high volumes the airways are tethered open and resistance falls.

@example
title: Static compliance on the ventilator
problem: A sedated patient receives a tidal volume of 480 mL. The plateau pressure is 24 cmH$_2$O and the PEEP is 8 cmH$_2$O. Calculate the static compliance of the respiratory system.
step: Static compliance uses the pressure that actually distends the lung during the pause: the driving pressure, $P_{plat} - PEEP = 24 - 8 = 16$ cmH$_2$O.
step: Divide the delivered volume by that pressure change: $C = 480/16$.
step: $C = 30$ mL/cmH$_2$O, well below normal, consistent with a stiff lung such as ARDS or pulmonary edema.

@example
title: Why small alveoli collapse without surfactant
problem: Two connected alveoli have radii of 100 micrometers and 50 micrometers and identical surface tension. Compare their distending pressures and predict what happens.
step: Laplace's law gives $P = 2T/r$. For the same $T$, pressure is inversely proportional to radius.
step: The smaller alveolus has half the radius, so its pressure is twice that of the larger one.
step: Air flows from the high-pressure small alveolus into the large one, so the small one collapses (atelectasis). Surfactant lowers $T$ more in the small alveolus, equalizing pressures.

@mistakes
- Saying that compliance is the same as elastance; they are reciprocals (stiff lungs have low compliance and high elastance).
- Claiming the small airways are the main site of resistance in a healthy lung; the medium bronchi are.
- Using peak inspiratory pressure instead of plateau pressure to compute static compliance, which mixes in airway resistance.
- Confusing the cause of RDS (too little surfactant from immaturity) with the cause of adult ARDS (inflammatory injury and surfactant inactivation).
