@article
Gas exchange needs two things to meet at the same place: air (ventilation, $\dot V$) and blood (perfusion, $\dot Q$). Almost every respiratory disease can be understood as a disturbance of one, the other, or the match between them.

### Ventilation
**Minute ventilation** is $\dot V_E = RR \times V_T$. Not all of it reaches gas-exchanging alveoli, because part fills dead space ($V_D$). **Alveolar ventilation** is
$$\dot V_A = RR \times (V_T - V_D).$$
Alveolar ventilation, not minute ventilation, sets the arterial CO$_2$:
$$PaCO_2 \propto \frac{\dot V_{CO_2}}{\dot V_A}.$$
Halve alveolar ventilation (at constant CO$_2$ production) and PaCO$_2$ roughly doubles. **Physiologic dead space** includes anatomic dead space plus alveolar dead space (ventilated but not perfused alveoli, as in pulmonary embolism). The **Bohr equation** estimates it:
$$\frac{V_D}{V_T} = \frac{PaCO_2 - P_{\bar E}CO_2}{PaCO_2},$$
where $P_{\bar E}CO_2$ is the mixed expired CO$_2$. Normal is about 0.2 to 0.35.

### The alveolar gas equation
To judge whether the lung is moving oxygen into blood efficiently, compare the oxygen the alveolus should contain with what the artery contains:
$$P_AO_2 = F_iO_2\,(P_{atm} - P_{H_2O}) - \frac{PaCO_2}{R}.$$
At sea level $P_{atm} = 760$ mmHg, water vapor pressure is 47 mmHg at 37 degrees C, and the respiratory quotient $R$ is taken as 0.8. On room air ($F_iO_2 = 0.21$):
$$P_AO_2 \approx 0.21(713) - \frac{PaCO_2}{0.8} \approx 150 - 1.25\,PaCO_2.$$
The **A-a gradient** is $P_AO_2 - PaO_2$. A normal value rises with age, estimated as about $\text{age}/4 + 4$ mmHg on room air (for example, about 19 at age 60). A gradient above that is abnormal and points to a problem at the alveolar-capillary level.

### Causes of hypoxemia
| Cause | A-a gradient | Response to 100% O$_2$ | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hypoventilation | Normal | Corrects | Opioid overdose, neuromuscular weakness, obesity hypoventilation |
| Low inspired PO$_2$ | Normal | Corrects | High altitude |
| V/Q mismatch | Increased | Corrects | Asthma, COPD, pneumonia (partly), PE |
| Diffusion limitation | Increased | Corrects | Interstitial lung disease, especially on exertion |
| Shunt | Increased | **Little or none** | ARDS, lobar consolidation, atelectasis, right-to-left cardiac shunt |

With a shunt, blood passes unventilated alveoli and never meets oxygen, so raising $F_iO_2$ barely helps. Hypoventilation raises PaCO$_2$, which displaces alveolar oxygen, so the A-a gradient stays normal.

### V/Q matching
The ideal ratio overall is about 0.8. Ventilation and perfusion both increase from apex to base, but perfusion increases more, so the apex has a high V/Q (about 3) and the base a low V/Q (about 0.6). The apex has the highest PO$_2$ and lowest PCO$_2$, which is why secondary tuberculosis favors the apices. At the extremes, V/Q of zero is shunt and V/Q approaching infinity is dead space.

**Hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction** diverts blood from poorly ventilated segments, protecting oxygenation. Globally low alveolar PO$_2$ (altitude, chronic hypoxemia) constricts all vessels and raises pulmonary artery pressure.

### Diffusion
Fick's law says gas flux is proportional to surface area, diffusion coefficient and pressure difference, and inversely proportional to barrier thickness. Normally oxygen equilibrates within about a third of the capillary transit time, so it is **perfusion-limited**; carbon monoxide is **diffusion-limited**, which is why it is used to measure diffusing capacity (DLCO). A diffusion defect matters mainly when transit time shortens, as in exercise.

@example
title: Computing the A-a gradient
problem: A 34-year-old woman breathing room air at sea level has PaO$_2$ 68 mmHg and PaCO$_2$ 32 mmHg. Calculate the A-a gradient.
step: Alveolar PO$_2$: $0.21 \times (760 - 47) - 32/0.8 = 149.7 - 40$.
step: $P_AO_2 \approx 109.7$ mmHg.
step: A-a gradient $= 109.7 - 68 = 41.7$ mmHg.
step: The expected value at age 34 is about $34/4 + 4 \approx 12.5$ mmHg, so the gradient is clearly elevated: the hypoxemia is not explained by hypoventilation (her low PaCO$_2$ shows she is hyperventilating). Consider V/Q mismatch, such as pulmonary embolism.

@example
title: Shallow versus deep breathing
problem: Two patients have the same minute ventilation of 6 L/min and a dead space of 150 mL. Patient A breathes 20 times a minute at 300 mL, patient B 10 times a minute at 600 mL. Compare their alveolar ventilation.
step: $\dot V_A = RR \times (V_T - V_D)$.
step: Patient A: $20 \times (300 - 150) = 3000$ mL/min.
step: Patient B: $10 \times (600 - 150) = 4500$ mL/min.
step: Rapid shallow breathing wastes ventilation on dead space; patient A will have a higher PaCO$_2$ despite identical minute ventilation.

@mistakes
- Forgetting to subtract water vapor pressure (47 mmHg) before multiplying by $F_iO_2$.
- Calling a hypoxemic patient with a normal A-a gradient "V/Q mismatch"; a normal gradient points to hypoventilation or low $F_iO_2$.
- Expecting 100% oxygen to correct hypoxemia from a shunt.
- Using minute ventilation rather than alveolar ventilation to predict PaCO$_2$.

answer: 3600

solution: $12 \times (450 - 150) = 12 \times 300 = 3600$ mL/min.
