@article
Pulmonary function tests (PFTs) turn a symptom, "I can't breathe", into numbers that localize the problem. They answer three questions: can you blow air out quickly (flow), how big are your lungs (volume), and does gas cross into the blood (diffusion)?

### Volumes and capacities
A spirometer measures anything you can breathe in and out: tidal volume (TV), inspiratory reserve volume (IRV), expiratory reserve volume (ERV), and the capacities built from them. It **cannot** measure **residual volume (RV)**, the air left after maximal exhalation, so it also cannot give **FRC** ($= ERV + RV$) or **total lung capacity** ($TLC = FRC + IC$, where the inspiratory capacity $IC = TV + IRV$). These need **body plethysmography** or gas dilution (helium or nitrogen washout). **Vital capacity** is $IRV + TV + ERV$. Typical adult values are TLC about 6 L, FRC about 2.5 to 3 L, RV about 1.2 L.

### The two key spirometry numbers
After a full inspiration the patient blows out as hard and long as possible. **FVC** is the total exhaled volume and **FEV1** is the volume exhaled in the first second. The ratio $\text{FEV1/FVC}$ is normally about 0.75 to 0.8 or higher (it declines with age).

- **Obstructive pattern:** FEV1/FVC is reduced. The GOLD guidelines use a fixed post-bronchodilator cutoff of less than 0.70; ATS/ERS recommend the lower limit of normal, which avoids over-diagnosis in older adults. FEV1 falls more than FVC. TLC is normal or high, with raised RV (air trapping).
- **Restrictive pattern:** FEV1/FVC is normal or high (0.80 or more), and both FEV1 and FVC are reduced proportionally. Restriction is **confirmed only by a reduced TLC** (below 80 percent of predicted, or below the lower limit of normal), because a low FVC alone can also occur in obstruction with air trapping.
- **Mixed:** both a low ratio and a low TLC.

Severity in COPD (GOLD, post-bronchodilator FEV1 percent predicted): 80 or more is GOLD 1, 50 to 79 GOLD 2, 30 to 49 GOLD 3, below 30 GOLD 4.

### Causes
**Obstructive:** asthma, COPD (emphysema, chronic bronchitis), bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, bronchiolitis obliterans.
**Restrictive** is divided into **intrinsic** (lung parenchyma: interstitial lung disease, pneumonitis, pulmonary edema), where DLCO is low, and **extrinsic** (chest wall, pleura, neuromuscular: obesity, kyphoscoliosis, myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barre syndrome, ALS, large pleural effusion), where DLCO is normal or only mildly reduced when corrected for volume.

### Diffusing capacity (DLCO)
The patient inhales a small, safe amount of carbon monoxide; its uptake reflects surface area, capillary blood volume and membrane thickness. DLCO is **reduced** in emphysema (lost surface), interstitial lung disease, pulmonary vascular disease (PE, pulmonary hypertension) and anemia (correct for hemoglobin). DLCO is **increased** in polycythemia, pulmonary hemorrhage (blood in alveoli binds CO), left-to-right shunts and often in asthma. So in an obstructive pattern: low DLCO suggests emphysema; normal or high DLCO suggests asthma or chronic bronchitis.

### Bronchodilator responsiveness and challenge tests
After inhaled albuterol, an increase in FEV1 or FVC of at least **12 percent and at least 200 mL** from baseline is a significant response, supporting asthma. A **methacholine challenge** is used when baseline spirometry is normal but asthma is suspected: a fall in FEV1 of 20 percent or more at a low dose (PC20) indicates airway hyperresponsiveness. It has a high negative predictive value. It is not done with severe baseline obstruction.

### Flow-volume loops
Plot flow against volume. **Obstructive disease** gives a scooped (concave) expiratory limb. **Restriction** gives a small but normally shaped, tall-and-narrow loop. Upper airway obstruction flattens the loop:
- **Fixed** (tracheal stenosis): both limbs flat.
- **Variable extrathoracic** (vocal cord paralysis, goiter at the thoracic inlet): the inspiratory limb flat.
- **Variable intrathoracic** (tracheal tumor in the chest): the expiratory limb flat.

@example
title: Classifying an obstructive pattern
problem: A 62-year-old smoker has a post-bronchodilator FEV1 of 1.6 L (predicted 2.9 L), FVC 3.2 L, TLC 7.4 L (predicted 6.0 L) and DLCO 45% of predicted. Classify the pattern and give the GOLD grade.
step: Compute the ratio: $1.6/3.2 = 0.50$, below 0.70, so the pattern is obstructive.
step: TLC is above predicted, which fits air trapping and hyperinflation and excludes a restrictive component.
step: FEV1 percent predicted: $1.6/2.9 \approx 55\%$, which is in the 50 to 79 percent range, so GOLD grade 2 (moderate).
step: The low DLCO indicates loss of alveolar surface area: emphysema is likely.

@example
title: Recognizing intrinsic restriction
problem: A 58-year-old woman with exertional dyspnea and bibasilar crackles has FEV1 2.1 L, FVC 2.5 L, TLC 55% of predicted and DLCO 40% of predicted. Interpret.
step: The ratio is $2.1/2.5 = 0.84$, so there is no obstruction.
step: TLC of 55 percent of predicted is below 80 percent, confirming restriction.
step: The markedly low DLCO places the process in the lung parenchyma (intrinsic restriction), for example interstitial lung disease, rather than the chest wall.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing restriction from a low FVC alone without a measured TLC.
- Forgetting that FEV1/FVC may be high (not just normal) in restriction because recoil is increased.
- Using pre-bronchodilator values for the GOLD severity grade.
- Interpreting DLCO without checking hemoglobin; anemia lowers it.
