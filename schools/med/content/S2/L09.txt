### Pulmonary embolism (PE)
Most pulmonary emboli are thrombi that travel from deep veins of the legs and pelvis. **Virchow's triad** explains the risk: venous stasis (immobility, long flights, surgery), endothelial injury (trauma, surgery) and hypercoagulability (cancer, pregnancy, estrogen therapy, factor V Leiden, prothrombin gene mutation, antiphospholipid syndrome, nephrotic syndrome). An obstructed pulmonary artery produces dead space and, through inflammation and redistribution of flow, hypoxemia with a widened A-a gradient; hyperventilation lowers PaCO$_2$. A large embolus abruptly raises pulmonary vascular resistance, causing **right ventricular strain**, falling cardiac output, syncope and shock.

**Presentation:** dyspnea (most common), pleuritic chest pain, tachypnea (the most common sign), tachycardia, cough, hemoptysis (infarction), syncope, unilateral leg swelling. Hypoxemia is common but not universal. The ECG usually shows sinus tachycardia; the classic S1Q3T3 pattern and right-heart strain are less common. Radiograph is often normal (Hampton hump and Westermark sign are rare). Troponin and BNP elevations and RV enlargement on CT or echocardiography (including McConnell sign, free-wall hypokinesis with apical sparing) indicate right ventricular strain.

### Estimating probability
**Wells criteria for PE** (clinical signs of DVT 3; PE the most likely diagnosis, or equally likely 3; heart rate above 100: 1.5; immobilization of 3 days or more or surgery in the previous 4 weeks: 1.5; previous PE or DVT: 1.5; hemoptysis: 1; active malignancy, treated within 6 months or palliative: 1). A score above 4 is "PE likely", and 4 or below "PE unlikely" (two-tier). In the three-tier version, below 2 is low, 2 to 6 moderate and above 6 high.

**PERC** (pulmonary embolism rule-out criteria) applies only to **low-pretest-probability** patients (below about 15 percent): if all 8 are met (age below 50; heart rate below 100; SaO$_2$ 95 percent or more; no hemoptysis; no estrogen use; no prior VTE; no unilateral leg swelling; no surgery or trauma needing hospitalization in the last 4 weeks), PE is excluded without further testing.

**Algorithm.**
- **PE unlikely:** measure a **D-dimer** (highly sensitive, poorly specific; raised in pregnancy, cancer, infection, surgery and age). A negative result excludes PE. For patients over 50, use an **age-adjusted cutoff** (age $\times$ 10 micrograms/L).
- **PE likely**, or a positive D-dimer: **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)**, which shows filling defects. D-dimer is skipped when probability is high.
- **V/Q scan** when contrast is contraindicated (renal impairment, contrast allergy) or in pregnancy, or when the chest film is normal; a normal V/Q excludes PE and a high-probability scan (multiple segmental mismatches) confirms it. Lower-extremity compression ultrasound can confirm DVT and justify treatment.

### Treatment
Initial anticoagulation for hemodynamically stable patients: **direct oral anticoagulants** (apixaban, rivaroxaban) are the usual first choice; in cancer-associated clots, apixaban, rivaroxaban or edoxaban and low-molecular-weight heparin are all accepted options, with heparin often chosen when gastrointestinal or genitourinary bleeding risk is high; low-molecular-weight heparin (enoxaparin) is the choice in pregnancy (DOACs are avoided in pregnancy); **unfractionated heparin** is used when thrombolysis may be needed or with severe renal failure; warfarin requires overlap with a parenteral drug until the INR is 2 to 3. Treat at least 3 months; provoked events by a major transient factor generally need 3 months, and unprovoked or cancer-associated events often justify extended therapy. **Massive (high-risk) PE** with hypotension (systolic below 90 mmHg) or shock: **systemic thrombolysis with alteplase** if no contraindication (bleeding risk: recent surgery, intracranial disease, active bleeding), otherwise catheter-directed therapy or surgical embolectomy. An **IVC filter** is for patients with acute VTE who cannot receive anticoagulation. Supportive care: oxygen, cautious fluids (to avoid overdistending the RV) and vasopressors for shock.

### Pulmonary hypertension (PH)
Defined by right heart catheterization: **mean pulmonary artery pressure above 20 mmHg at rest** (2022 ESC/ERS). Precapillary PH (pulmonary arterial hypertension, PAH) also has pulmonary artery wedge pressure of 15 mmHg or less and PVR above 2 Wood units, where
$$PVR = \frac{mPAP - PAWP}{CO}.$$
Symptoms: exertional dyspnea, fatigue, syncope, chest pain, edema; signs: loud P2, RV heave, right-sided S4, JVD and tricuspid regurgitation. Echocardiography screens; catheterization confirms. **WHO groups:**
1. **PAH**: idiopathic, heritable (BMPR2), connective tissue disease (especially systemic sclerosis), HIV, portal hypertension, drugs (anorexigens such as fenfluramine, methamphetamine), congenital shunts.
2. **Left heart disease** (the most common cause overall; wedge pressure is high).
3. **Lung disease or hypoxia** (COPD, ILD, sleep apnea, high altitude).
4. **Chronic thromboembolic** (CTEPH).
5. Multifactorial or unclear mechanisms (sarcoidosis, hematologic disease).

**Group 1 treatment** targets three pathways: endothelin receptor antagonists (bosentan, ambrisentan, macitentan; teratogenic, so pregnancy must be excluded), **PDE-5 inhibitors** (sildenafil, tadalafil) and the soluble guanylate cyclase stimulator riociguat (all raise nitric oxide-cGMP signaling), and **prostacyclin pathway** agents (epoprostenol, treprostinil, selexipag). **Calcium channel blockers** only for the small minority with a positive vasoreactivity test. Combine drugs early in moderate to severe disease. Group 2 and 3 disease is treated by managing the underlying condition and oxygen. **CTEPH** is screened for with a **V/Q scan** after unexplained dyspnea following PE; treat with lifelong anticoagulation, **pulmonary endarterectomy** if operable, riociguat or balloon angioplasty. Chronic PH leads to **cor pulmonale** (right heart failure from lung disease).

@example
title: Wells score and test choice
problem: A 55-year-old man 3 weeks after knee replacement presents with dyspnea, a heart rate of 112/min, a swollen, tender left calf and no better alternative diagnosis. What is his Wells score and what is the next test?
step: Clinical signs of DVT: 3. PE the most likely diagnosis: 3. Heart rate above 100: 1.5. Surgery within 4 weeks: 1.5.
step: Total $= 3 + 3 + 1.5 + 1.5 = 9$, which is above 4 (PE likely) and above 6 (high probability).
step: A D-dimer is not needed (a negative would not reliably exclude PE at high probability). Start anticoagulation if CT will be delayed and perform CTPA.

@example
title: Age-adjusted D-dimer
problem: A 72-year-old woman with a Wells score of 2 (PE unlikely) has a D-dimer of 650 micrograms/L (FEU). Does she need CT angiography?
step: Wells score of 2 means PE unlikely, so D-dimer is the right first test.
step: The age-adjusted cutoff is $72 \times 10 = 720$ micrograms/L.
step: 650 is below 720, so the test is negative, PE is effectively excluded and CTPA is not needed.

@mistakes
- Using the PERC rule in a patient who is not low pretest probability.
- Ordering a D-dimer in a high-probability patient or in pregnancy or post-operative states where it is nearly always raised.
- Treating hypotension from massive PE with large fluid boluses, which can distend the failing right ventricle.
- Starting prostacyclins or PAH drugs in group 2 or group 3 pulmonary hypertension without addressing the cause.
