@article
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death. **Smoking** causes about 85 percent of cases; other risks are **radon** (the second most common cause), asbestos, other occupational carcinogens (arsenic, chromium, nickel), secondhand smoke, and prior chest radiation. About 10 to 15 percent of cases occur in never-smokers, mostly adenocarcinoma.

### Screening
The USPSTF (2021) recommends **annual low-dose CT (LDCT)** for adults **aged 50 to 80 with at least a 20 pack-year history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years**; stop if the person has not smoked for 15 years or has a health problem that limits life expectancy or the ability to have curative surgery. Screening lowers lung cancer mortality (by about 20 percent in the National Lung Screening Trial) at the cost of false positives. Chest radiography is not a screening test. Shared decision-making and cessation counseling are part of every screening visit.

### Histologic types
| Type | Location and risk | Pathology and markers | Notable features |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Adenocarcinoma** (most common overall; most common in nonsmokers and women) | Peripheral | Glandular; TTF-1 and napsin A positive; lepidic growth in the in situ and minimally invasive forms | Driver mutations: **EGFR, ALK, ROS1, BRAF, KRAS, MET, RET**; hypertrophic osteoarthropathy |
| **Squamous cell carcinoma** | Central, near the hilum, smokers | Keratin pearls, intercellular bridges; p40 and p63 positive | Cavitation; **PTHrP hypercalcemia** |
| **Small cell carcinoma** | Central, strongly linked to smoking | Small blue cells, scant cytoplasm, nuclear molding, crush artifact; neuroendocrine markers (chromogranin, synaptophysin, CD56); very high proliferation | **SIADH** (ectopic ADH), ectopic **ACTH** (Cushing), **Lambert-Eaton** (anti-presynaptic calcium channel antibodies), anti-Hu neuropathy and encephalitis; early metastasis; responds initially to chemotherapy |
| **Large cell carcinoma** | Peripheral | Undifferentiated large cells | Poor prognosis |
| **Bronchial carcinoid** | Central, younger patients | Neuroendocrine nests; low grade | Rarely carcinoid syndrome |

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) includes adenocarcinoma, squamous and large cell.

### Regional effects
- **Pancoast tumor** (superior sulcus): shoulder and arm pain in the C8 to T2 distribution (brachial plexus, with hand weakness), and **Horner syndrome** (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis) from invasion of the cervical sympathetic chain.
- **Superior vena cava syndrome:** facial swelling, plethora, dilated chest wall veins; commonly caused by lung cancer or lymphoma.
- Hoarseness (recurrent laryngeal nerve invasion), dysphagia, phrenic nerve palsy, pleural effusion, and pericardial effusion.
- Metastasis: brain, bone, liver, adrenals.
- **Mesothelioma** is a pleural tumor caused by asbestos exposure: pleural thickening or effusion, epithelioid cells that stain with calretinin; poor prognosis.

### Evaluation and staging
A new nodule or mass prompts comparison with prior imaging, chest CT, and then PET-CT. **Benign features:** stable size over 2 years for a solid nodule, or dense central, laminated or popcorn calcification (hamartoma); concerning features are spiculation, upper-lobe location, growth and larger size (Fleischner Society guidelines set follow-up by nodule size and risk). Tissue diagnosis is by bronchoscopy, EBUS for mediastinal nodes, or CT-guided biopsy. Staging (TNM) uses PET-CT, brain MRI, and mediastinal node sampling. Small cell disease is staged as **limited** (one radiation field) or **extensive**.

### Treatment principles
- **Stage I and II NSCLC:** surgical resection (lobectomy) for fit patients; stereotactic body radiotherapy if not.
- **Stage III:** concurrent chemoradiation, followed by consolidation immunotherapy (durvalumab) if no progression; selected surgical cases.
- **Stage IV NSCLC:** test for biomarkers. **EGFR** mutation: osimertinib; **ALK** rearrangement: alectinib; **ROS1**: crizotinib or entrectinib; **PD-L1 expression of at least 50 percent** without a driver mutation: pembrolizumab; otherwise, chemotherapy with immunotherapy. Palliative care is started early.
- **Small cell:** limited stage, chemoradiation (cisplatin and etoposide) plus thoracic radiotherapy; extensive stage, platinum-etoposide plus an immune checkpoint inhibitor (atezolizumab or durvalumab). Prophylactic cranial irradiation or MRI surveillance is considered.

Cancer is itself a hypercoagulable state: **venous thromboembolism** is common in lung cancer, so keep PE high on the differential, and add the malignancy point to the Wells score.

@example
title: Is this patient eligible for screening?
problem: A 62-year-old woman smoked 1 pack per day for 25 years and quit 8 years ago. She has no symptoms. Is annual LDCT recommended?
step: Age 62 is within 50 to 80.
step: Pack-years $= 1 \times 25 = 25$, which meets the 20 pack-year requirement.
step: She quit 8 years ago, within the last 15 years. All three conditions are met: offer annual LDCT with shared decision-making.

@example
title: Matching a tumor to a syndrome
problem: A 64-year-old smoker has a central, cavitating hilar mass. Serum calcium is 13.2 mg/dL, with low parathyroid hormone and an elevated PTHrP. Biopsy shows keratin pearls. What is the diagnosis?
step: The central location in a smoker and the cavity suggest squamous cell carcinoma.
step: Keratin pearls confirm it, and PTHrP-mediated hypercalcemia is the classic paraneoplastic syndrome.
step: Squamous cell carcinoma is a non-small cell cancer, so staging and biomarker testing guide treatment; hydrate and give a bisphosphonate for hypercalcemia.

@mistakes
- Using chest radiography for lung cancer screening.
- Linking SIADH and Lambert-Eaton to squamous carcinoma instead of small cell carcinoma.
- Treating a central, small-cell tumor with surgery; most patients are not surgical candidates.
- Forgetting to send an adenocarcinoma biopsy for EGFR, ALK and other biomarkers and PD-L1.

prompt: In a hypothetical screening trial, lung cancer mortality is 1.6 percent in the control group and 1.28 percent in the screened group over the study period. What is the number needed to screen to prevent one lung cancer death (rounded to the nearest whole number)?
answer: 313
check: 1 / (0.016 - 0.0128)
hint: First find the absolute risk reduction, then take its reciprocal.
solution: Absolute risk reduction $= 1.6\% - 1.28\% = 0.32\% = 0.0032$. Number needed to screen $= 1/0.0032 = 312.5$, about 313.
tol: 1
