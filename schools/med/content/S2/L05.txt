@article
**Asthma** is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable, usually reversible airflow obstruction, airway hyperresponsiveness and symptoms (wheeze, cough, chest tightness, dyspnea) that are typically worse at night or early morning and triggered by allergens, exercise, cold air, viral infection or irritants.

### Mechanism
In allergic asthma, allergen exposure activates Th2 cells that release **IL-4, IL-5 and IL-13**. IL-4 and IL-13 drive B-cell class switching to IgE and mucus production; IL-5 recruits and sustains **eosinophils**. IgE on mast cells triggers degranulation on re-exposure (early phase: histamine, leukotrienes C4, D4 and E4, prostaglandin D2) followed by a late phase of eosinophilic and lymphocytic inflammation. Chronic disease causes airway remodeling: smooth-muscle hypertrophy, goblet-cell hyperplasia, basement membrane thickening and mucus plugging. Pathology classically shows **Curschmann spirals** (mucus casts) and **Charcot-Leyden crystals** (eosinophil protein) in sputum. Not all asthma is allergic: non-type-2 (neutrophilic) asthma exists and responds less well to corticosteroids.

Variants to know: **exercise-induced bronchoconstriction**; **aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease** (asthma, nasal polyps and sensitivity to aspirin and other COX-1 inhibitors; COX blockade shunts arachidonic acid toward leukotrienes, so leukotriene modifiers help); **allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis** (central bronchiectasis, very high IgE, eosinophilia, fleeting infiltrates).

### Diagnosis
Take a history of variable symptoms, then document variable expiratory airflow limitation: FEV1/FVC reduced, with **bronchodilator reversibility** (FEV1 increase of at least 12 percent and 200 mL), or a positive methacholine challenge when spirometry is normal, or fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) as a marker of type 2 inflammation. Between attacks, examination and spirometry may be normal. **Peak expiratory flow (PEF)** is used for home monitoring in zones relative to the patient's personal best: green 80 percent or more, yellow 50 to 79 percent, red below 50 percent. Chest radiograph is usually normal or hyperinflated and is obtained to exclude alternatives (pneumonia, pneumothorax).

### Acute exacerbation
Severity signs: inability to speak full sentences, accessory-muscle use, pulsus paradoxus, silent chest, PEF below 50 percent of best, and SpO$_2$ below 90 percent. Because hyperventilation lowers PaCO$_2$ early, a **normal or rising PaCO$_2$ in a dyspneic asthmatic signals impending respiratory failure** from fatigue. Management:
- Inhaled short-acting beta-2 agonist (albuterol), repeated or continuous, with inhaled **ipratropium** in moderate to severe attacks.
- **Systemic corticosteroids** early (typical adult dose prednisone 40 to 60 mg daily for 5 to 7 days; for study, check current references before clinical use).
- Oxygen titrated to SpO$_2$ of about 93 to 95 percent.
- **IV magnesium sulfate** for severe attacks not responding to initial treatment.
- Noninvasive ventilation or intubation for fatigue; if intubated, use low respiratory rate and long expiratory time to avoid auto-PEEP. Avoid beta-blockers.

### Long-term, stepwise therapy
The principle is to control inflammation with **inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)** and avoid reliance on short-acting beta-agonists alone, because SABA-only treatment leaves inflammation untreated and raises exacerbation risk. Current GINA guidance (2024) prefers, for adolescents and adults, **low-dose ICS-formoterol as the reliever** (a rapid-onset LABA combined with ICS) at every step, with regular ICS-formoterol maintenance added at higher steps (maintenance-and-reliever therapy):
1. Steps 1 and 2: as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol (alternative: daily low-dose ICS with SABA as needed).
2. Step 3: low-dose ICS-formoterol as maintenance and reliever.
3. Step 4: medium-dose ICS-formoterol maintenance and reliever.
4. Step 5: add a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA; tiotropium) and refer for phenotyping and **biologics**.

The 2020 NAEPP focused update takes a slightly different route for step 1 and 2 (for example, concomitant ICS with SABA as needed) but agrees on single maintenance and reliever therapy with ICS-formoterol at steps 3 and 4. **LABAs must never be used without ICS.** Leukotriene receptor antagonist (montelukast) is an alternative controller with a boxed warning for neuropsychiatric events. Check inhaler technique, adherence, triggers and comorbidities (rhinitis, GERD, obesity) before stepping up. **Biologics** for severe asthma: omalizumab (anti-IgE, allergic), mepolizumab and reslizumab (anti-IL-5), benralizumab (anti-IL-5 receptor), dupilumab (anti-IL-4 receptor alpha) and tezepelumab (anti-TSLP). Allergen immunotherapy suits selected patients with allergic asthma.

@example
title: Peak flow zones
problem: A 17-year-old with asthma has a personal best PEF of 600 L/min. During a cold, he measures 270 L/min. Which zone is he in, and what should he do?
step: Percent of personal best $= 270/600 = 0.45$, or 45 percent.
step: Below 50 percent is the **red zone**, a medical alert.
step: He should use his rescue inhaler as directed, and if there is no rapid improvement, seek emergency care (an exacerbation needing systemic corticosteroids).

@example
title: Choosing the controller step
problem: A 22-year-old has daytime symptoms three days a week and one nocturnal awakening a month, uses albuterol about three times a week and has had no exacerbations. Spirometry is normal between attacks. Outline therapy.
step: Symptoms more than twice a week mean he is not at step 1 by symptom frequency, and mild persistent disease needs anti-inflammatory therapy.
step: A GINA-preferred step-2 regimen is as-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol (an alternative is daily low-dose ICS plus SABA as needed).
step: Review inhaler technique and triggers, and plan to reassess control in 2 to 3 months; step up if control is inadequate and step down only after 3 months of good control.

@mistakes
- Treating asthma with SABA alone, or a LABA alone, which leaves inflammation uncontrolled.
- Reassured by a "normal" PaCO$_2$ during a severe attack; it means fatigue.
- Giving a beta-blocker, or aspirin or NSAIDs in AERD.
- Stepping up the dose before checking technique and adherence.
