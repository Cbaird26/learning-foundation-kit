@article
Fiction has themes; nonfiction has a **central idea**: the most important point the author makes about the topic. Everything else in the text should help explain, prove or illustrate it.

### Topic, central idea, details

The **topic** is what the text is about, such as honeybees. The **central idea** is what the author says about it: "Honeybees depend on cooperation to survive winter." **Supporting details** are facts, examples, statistics, quotations and explanations that back up that idea.

### Finding the central idea

Use these clues:

- **Title and headings** often name the topic and hint at the focus.
- **Introduction and conclusion** commonly state or restate the main point.
- **Repetition**: if an idea or key word returns in many paragraphs, it is probably central.
- **Topic sentences**: the first sentence of a paragraph often states that paragraph's point. The central idea is what these points add up to.

Try this check: if you remove a detail, does the central idea still stand? Then the detail was supporting, not central. If removing an idea collapses the text, that idea is central.

### Key versus minor details

A **key detail** directly proves or explains the central idea. A **minor detail** is interesting but could be removed without changing the main point. In an article arguing that sleep helps memory, a study showing students who slept recalled more is key; the name of the town where the study took place is minor.

### Objective summaries

A **summary** restates the central idea and the most important details in your own words, in a few sentences. An **objective** summary contains no opinions, no judgments, and no outside facts. It does not include phrases like "I think" or "this is boring." Good summaries are much shorter than the original, use your own wording, and keep the original order of ideas.

A simple formula: **This text explains that** (central idea). **It supports this by** (key detail 1) **and** (key detail 2).

### A caution

Do not confuse the central idea with the first detail you meet, or with a detail you found surprising. Read to the end before deciding.

@example
title: Choosing the central idea
problem: Read: "Sea otters wrap themselves in kelp while they sleep so they do not drift away. Otters also keep kelp forests healthy by eating sea urchins, which would otherwise devour the kelp. Where otters were hunted nearly to extinction, urchins multiplied and kelp forests shrank. Today, protecting otters helps protect the whole coastal ecosystem." Which is the central idea: (a) otters sleep in kelp, (b) otters help keep coastal ecosystems healthy, or (c) urchins eat kelp?
step: List the main points: sleeping in kelp, eating urchins to protect kelp, kelp loss after otters were hunted, and protecting otters helps the ecosystem.
step: The last sentence states a main point, and three of four sentences connect to otters protecting kelp forests.
step: The sleeping detail is interesting but minor; removing it leaves the point intact. The urchin fact is a supporting detail, not the whole message.
step: Central idea: Otters help keep coastal ecosystems healthy, so protecting them matters. Choice (b).
@mistakes
- Choosing a fascinating detail, such as the sleeping otters, as the central idea.
- Writing a central idea so broad ("Animals are important") that it fits any article, or so narrow that it covers only one paragraph.
- Adding opinions or outside information to a summary that should be objective.
