@article
Good writing is built on good sources. **Research** means asking a question, finding information, judging it, and giving credit.

### Start with a research question

A good question is focused and cannot be answered with yes or no. "How does school start time affect teenagers' sleep and attention?" is better than "Is sleep good?" Break it into subquestions and note keywords to search.

### Evaluating sources

Use the **CRAAP**-style checks:

- **Currency**: is it recent enough for your topic?
- **Relevance**: does it answer your question?
- **Authority**: who wrote it, and what are their qualifications?
- **Accuracy**: is it supported by evidence and can you confirm it elsewhere?
- **Purpose**: is it to inform, persuade, or sell? Look for bias.

Websites ending in .gov and .edu, peer-reviewed journals and established news organizations are often reliable, but still check. Use at least **three** sources and compare them.

### Quote, paraphrase, summarize

- **Quote**: exact words in quotation marks, used sparingly for strong or technical phrases.
- **Paraphrase**: restate an idea in your own words and sentence structure, with a citation. Changing a few words is not paraphrasing.
- **Summarize**: condense a longer text into its main points, with a citation.

### Plagiarism and citation

**Plagiarism** is presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own. Avoid it by taking notes in your own words, marking quotations, and citing every source. A **citation** tells readers where the information came from. In-text citations (for example, the author's last name in parentheses) point to a **works cited** list with the author, title, publisher or website, and date. Follow the format your teacher assigns (MLA is common in English classes) and keep source details as you go.

### Presenting an argument

For a three-minute talk, aim for about **130 words per minute**, so roughly **390 words** in all. Plan three parts: an **opening** with a hook and claim, a **middle** with two or three reasons and evidence, and a **close** that restates the claim and ends memorably.

Delivery tips: speak slowly and clearly, make eye contact, use brief notes rather than a script, and use gestures and pauses for emphasis. Visuals should be simple. Practice aloud with a timer.

### Listening and responding

Good audiences listen for the claim, reasons and evidence, and ask questions that begin with "What evidence...?" or "How does that connect to...?"

@example
title: Paraphrasing and citing
problem: Original: "Studies show that teenagers' body clocks shift later during puberty, making it harder for them to fall asleep before 11 p.m." (Garcia, Sleep Research Review, 2022). Write an acceptable paraphrase with an in-text citation.
step: Identify the idea: teen body clocks shift later, delaying sleep.
step: Rewrite with new wording and structure, without copying phrases: "During puberty, young people tend to get sleepy later at night, so falling asleep early is difficult (Garcia)."
step: Check: the idea is accurate, the wording is original, and the source is credited.
step: Add the source to the works cited list with author, title and year.
@mistakes
- Copying a sentence and changing only a few words, then calling it a paraphrase.
- Trusting the first search result without checking who wrote it and why.
- Citing only direct quotations and forgetting to credit paraphrased ideas.
