@article
Hemodynamics is the physics of the circulation, and it is the language of every later week: heart failure is a loop that has shifted, shock is a resistance or output problem, and valve disease is a pressure gradient.

### The cardiac cycle

Take the left side. **Diastole** includes isovolumetric relaxation, rapid filling, diastasis (slow filling) and atrial systole. **Systole** includes isovolumetric contraction, rapid ejection and reduced ejection.

1. **Atrial systole** adds the last 10 to 20% of filling (the "atrial kick," more important when the ventricle is stiff) and produces the **a wave** on the jugular venous pulse. S4 is a sound from atrial contraction against a stiff ventricle.
2. **Isovolumetric contraction.** Ventricular pressure exceeds atrial pressure, the mitral valve closes (**S1**), and pressure rises with no volume change.
3. **Rapid ejection.** Pressure exceeds aortic pressure, the aortic valve opens, and most of the stroke volume leaves.
4. **Reduced ejection.** Ventricular pressure peaks and begins to fall.
5. **Isovolumetric relaxation.** The aortic valve closes (**S2**) with the **dicrotic notch**, then pressure falls at constant volume.
6. **Rapid filling.** The mitral valve opens when ventricular pressure falls below atrial pressure. **S3** is a vibration from rapid filling of a volume-overloaded ventricle (normal in children and pregnancy; pathologic in older adults, a sign of heart failure).

Physiologic splitting of S2: inspiration increases venous return to the right heart, delaying pulmonic closure (P2), so the split widens in inspiration and narrows in expiration.

### The pressure-volume loop

Plot left ventricular pressure (y) against volume (x). One beat is a counterclockwise rectangle-like loop with four corners: **end-diastolic volume (EDV)** at the lower right, then isovolumetric contraction up to the aortic valve opening, ejection leftward to **end-systolic volume (ESV)**, and isovolumetric relaxation back down to the mitral valve opening. The width of the loop is the **stroke volume (SV = EDV - ESV)**.

- **Preload** is the ventricular wall stretch at end-diastole, represented by EDV (clinically, end-diastolic pressure or volume). By the **Frank-Starling mechanism**, more stretch gives a stronger contraction up to a limit. Raising preload shifts the right edge of the loop rightward, widening the loop and raising SV. Venodilators (nitroglycerin) and diuretics lower preload.
- **Afterload** is the wall stress the ventricle must generate during ejection, approximated by aortic pressure (and, by Laplace, wall stress $\propto \frac{P r}{h}$, so dilation raises it and hypertrophy lowers it). Raising afterload makes the loop taller and narrower: the aortic valve opens later, ESV rises and SV falls.
- **Contractility** is the force at a given preload and afterload. Greater contractility (catecholamines, increased intracellular calcium, digoxin) steepens the **end-systolic pressure-volume relationship**: ESV falls and SV rises. Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction lowers it.

### Key formulas

Use these constantly:

- $SV = EDV - ESV$ and $EF = SV/EDV$ (normal about 55 to 70%).
- $CO = SV \times HR$ (normal about 5 L/min at rest).
- $MAP \approx DBP + \tfrac13 (SBP - DBP)$.
- $MAP - CVP = CO \times SVR$, so $SVR = 80 \times \frac{MAP - CVP}{CO}$ in dyn$\cdot$s/cm$^5$ (or in Wood units without the 80).
- $PVR = \frac{mPAP - PCWP}{CO}$ in Wood units.
- Fick: $CO = \frac{\dot V_{O_2}}{C_aO_2 - C_vO_2}$, where oxygen content is $1.34 \times Hb \times S\,O_2$ (plus a negligible dissolved term).
- Resistance from Poiseuille: $R \propto \frac{\eta L}{r^4}$, so radius dominates: halving the radius raises resistance 16-fold.

@example
title: Cardiac output and resistance from bedside data
problem: A patient has HR 80/min, EDV 120 mL, ESV 50 mL, BP 120/80 mm Hg and central venous pressure 4 mm Hg. Find SV, EF, CO, MAP and SVR.
step: $SV = 120 - 50 = 70$ mL and $EF = 70/120 \approx 58\%$, which is normal.
step: $CO = 70 \times 80 = 5600$ mL/min $= 5.6$ L/min.
step: $MAP = 80 + \frac{120 - 80}{3} = 93.3$ mm Hg.
step: $SVR = 80 \times \frac{93.3 - 4}{5.6} \approx 1276$ dyn$\cdot$s/cm$^5$, slightly above the normal range of about 800 to 1200; read it with the clinical picture.

@example
title: Reading a loop after a drug
problem: A hypertensive patient's loop is taller and narrower after a sudden rise in aortic pressure. Explain the change in SV and ESV.
step: Higher aortic pressure means the ventricle must reach a higher pressure before the aortic valve opens, so the vertical limb is taller.
step: Because the ventricle works against a higher load, it ejects less before relaxing, so ESV rises (left edge moves right).
step: With the same EDV, SV = EDV - ESV falls, so the loop is narrower.
step: Compensation in later beats: the extra end-systolic volume adds to filling, raising EDV and restoring SV by Frank-Starling.

@mistakes
- Treating afterload as "pressure in the ventricle at the end of filling"; that is preload-related. Afterload is the load during ejection (wall stress, aortic pressure or SVR).
- Forgetting the factor of 80 when converting Wood units to dyn$\cdot$s/cm$^5$, or using systolic pressure instead of MAP.
- Using CO in mL/min with the Wood-unit formulas, which expect L/min.

@practice self
prompt: Draw (in words or on paper) a normal left ventricular pressure-volume loop with its four corners labeled, then explain how the loop changes if preload rises and, separately, if afterload rises. Say what happens to stroke volume in each case.
hint: Label the corners by valve events: mitral closes, aortic opens, aortic closes, mitral opens.
solution: The loop starts at end-diastolic volume at the lower right; isovolumetric contraction rises vertically to the point where the aortic valve opens; ejection moves left along the top to end-systolic volume (on the end-systolic pressure-volume line); isovolumetric relaxation falls vertically to where the mitral valve opens; filling moves back right to the start. Increased preload moves the right edge further right (greater EDV), widening the loop so SV rises (Frank-Starling), with ESV about unchanged. Increased afterload makes the loop taller and shifts ESV to the right, narrowing the loop so SV falls until compensatory increased filling restores it.

rubric: States preload as EDV and shows the right edge moving right with larger SV.
rubric: States afterload as ejection wall stress or aortic pressure, with taller loop, higher ESV and smaller SV.
rubric: Uses stroke volume as EDV minus ESV (loop width).
