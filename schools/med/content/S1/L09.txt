@article
Valves fail in two ways: they do not open fully (**stenosis**, a pressure overload behind the valve) or they do not close fully (**regurgitation**, a volume overload). The chamber behind the lesion adapts: pressure overload gives concentric hypertrophy, volume overload gives dilation and eccentric hypertrophy. Murmurs are named by their **timing** (systolic or diastolic), **shape**, **location** and **radiation**.

### Aortic stenosis (AS)

Causes: calcific degeneration (older adults), a **bicuspid aortic valve** (earlier, in the 50s to 60s) and rheumatic disease. The murmur is a harsh **crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur** at the right upper sternal border that radiates to the carotids; it peaks later as severity increases, with a soft or single S2, an S4 and a **slow, small pulse (pulsus parvus et tardus)**. Classic symptoms are **angina, syncope and heart failure** (average survival after onset is about 5, 3 and 2 years respectively). Severe AS: peak velocity 4 m/s or higher, mean gradient 40 mm Hg or more, valve area 1.0 cm$^2$ or less. The only effective therapy is **valve replacement** (surgical or transcatheter, TAVR) once severe AS is symptomatic (or with reduced EF). Avoid vasodilators and hypovolemia. Heyde syndrome is AS with gastrointestinal angiodysplasia bleeding. The murmur gets louder with squatting and leg raising (more preload) and softer with Valsalva.

### Aortic regurgitation (AR)

Causes: aortic root dilation (hypertension, Marfan syndrome, syphilis, aortitis), bicuspid valve, endocarditis and dissection. The murmur is a high-pitched **early diastolic decrescendo murmur** at the left sternal border, heard best sitting forward in expiration. The pulse pressure is wide (for example 160/50) with **bounding pulses**: water-hammer pulse, de Musset sign (head bobbing), Quincke pulse (nail bed pulsations), and Corrigan pulse. An **Austin Flint murmur** is a mid-to-late diastolic rumble at the apex from regurgitant jet impinging on the mitral valve. Chronic AR: vasodilators (ACE inhibitors, dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers) for hypertension; surgery for symptoms, or for EF 55% or below or marked LV dilation. **Acute AR** (dissection, endocarditis) gives pulmonary edema with a short soft murmur and is a surgical emergency; avoid beta blockers. The murmur increases with handgrip (afterload).

### Mitral stenosis (MS)

Almost always **rheumatic** (group A streptococcal rheumatic fever; molecular mimicry). An **opening snap** follows S2 and then a low-pitched **mid-diastolic rumble** at the apex, best heard in left lateral decubitus with the bell; the shorter the S2 to opening snap interval, the worse the stenosis. Left atrial pressure rises: dyspnea, **atrial fibrillation** (with thrombus risk), hemoptysis, and a dilated left atrium that can cause dysphagia and hoarseness. Treatment: rate control with beta blockers or non-dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (a longer diastole helps filling), diuretics, **warfarin anticoagulation** for AF (direct oral anticoagulants are not used in moderate-to-severe rheumatic MS), and **percutaneous balloon mitral valvotomy** for symptomatic severe MS (area 1.5 cm$^2$ or less; normal is 4 to 6 cm$^2$).

### Mitral regurgitation (MR) and prolapse

MR gives a **holosystolic, high-pitched murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla**, with an S3 in volume overload. Causes: myxomatous degeneration or prolapse, ischemic papillary dysfunction, rheumatic disease, endocarditis, a dilated LV (functional MR). Acute MR (papillary rupture, endocarditis) causes pulmonary edema; chronic MR leads to LV dilation and AF. The murmur increases with handgrip and squatting (afterload). Repair is preferred over replacement; indications include symptoms or LV dysfunction (EF 60% or less, or LV end-systolic diameter over 40 mm).

**Mitral valve prolapse (MVP)** (myxomatous degeneration, associated with Marfan and Ehlers-Danlos syndromes) causes a **mid-systolic click** followed by a late systolic murmur; the click moves earlier and the murmur lengthens with standing and Valsalva (a smaller ventricle). Most patients are asymptomatic.

### Maneuvers

Most murmurs soften with Valsalva and standing (reduced venous return). **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and MVP are the exceptions** and get louder. Squatting increases venous return and afterload: most murmurs louder, HCM and MVP softer. Handgrip raises afterload: AR and MR louder, AS and HCM softer. **Inspiration** increases right-sided venous return, so murmurs of the tricuspid and pulmonic valves (**Carvallo sign** for tricuspid regurgitation) get louder in inspiration.

### Quantifying severity

- **Simplified Bernoulli:** pressure gradient across a valve $\Delta P \approx 4v^2$ (mm Hg, with $v$ in m/s).
- **Continuity equation** for aortic valve area: $AVA = \frac{A_{LVOT}\times VTI_{LVOT}}{VTI_{AV}}$, with $A_{LVOT} = \pi (d/2)^2$.
- **Pressure half-time** for mitral valve area: $MVA \approx \frac{220}{T_{1/2}}$ (cm$^2$, with half-time in ms).

@example
title: Grading aortic stenosis from echo data
problem: An echocardiogram shows peak aortic jet velocity 4.5 m/s, LVOT diameter 2.0 cm, LVOT velocity-time integral (VTI) 20 cm and aortic valve VTI 80 cm. The patient has exertional syncope. What is the severity and plan?
step: Gradient: $4v^2 = 4 \times 4.5^2 = 81$ mm Hg (peak); this exceeds the severe threshold (a peak velocity over 4 m/s).
step: LVOT area $= \pi (1.0)^2 = 3.14$ cm$^2$.
step: $AVA = 3.14 \times 20/80 = 0.79$ cm$^2$, below 1.0 cm$^2$: severe stenosis.
step: Symptomatic severe AS: valve replacement (TAVR or surgical, depending on age and risk), not medical therapy.

@example
title: Using maneuvers to separate two systolic murmurs
problem: Two patients have systolic murmurs: one is a harsh murmur at the left sternal border that gets louder when the patient stands from squatting, and the other is a harsh murmur at the right upper sternal border that gets softer on standing. Which is which?
step: Standing and Valsalva reduce preload and ventricular size.
step: A smaller ventricle narrows the outflow tract in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, so its murmur gets louder.
step: In aortic stenosis the flow across the fixed valve falls, so the murmur gets softer, and it is heard at the right upper border radiating to the carotids.
step: The first is HCM and the second is AS.

@mistakes
- Treating AS and HCM as identical because both are crescendo-decrescendo; their responses to Valsalva and squatting are opposite.
- Forgetting that the diastolic murmur of AR is high pitched and early, whereas MS is low pitched, mid-diastolic with an opening snap.
- Using a direct oral anticoagulant for AF with moderate-to-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis.
- Using vasodilators in severe AS with hypotension.
