@article
Arrhythmias arise from abnormal **automaticity**, **triggered activity** (early or delayed afterdepolarizations) or **reentry** around an anatomic or functional circuit. Your first question is always about stability: hypotension, altered mentation, ischemic chest pain, acute heart failure or shock in a patient with an arrhythmia means act now.

### Bradyarrhythmias and AV block

- **First degree:** PR over 200 ms with every P conducted. Usually benign.
- **Second degree, Mobitz I (Wenckebach):** the PR lengthens progressively until a beat is dropped; the block is in the AV node; grouped beating; often benign (increased vagal tone, inferior MI).
- **Second degree, Mobitz II:** PR is constant, then a P wave suddenly fails to conduct; the block is below the node (His-Purkinje), the QRS is often wide, and it can progress suddenly to complete block, so it requires a **permanent pacemaker**.
- **Third degree (complete):** atria and ventricles beat independently (AV dissociation). Escape rhythm is narrow if junctional and wide if ventricular. Treat symptomatic bradycardia with atropine, then transcutaneous pacing or dopamine or epinephrine infusion, then a pacemaker. Lyme disease can cause reversible block.

### Supraventricular tachycardias

**Atrial fibrillation (AF):** an irregularly irregular narrow-complex rhythm with no distinct P waves. Management has three parts. (1) If unstable, **synchronized cardioversion**. (2) Rate control in stable patients: beta blocker or a nondihydropyridine calcium channel blocker (diltiazem, verapamil; avoid these in reduced ejection fraction). (3) **Stroke prevention** by CHA2DS2-VASc: Congestive heart failure 1, Hypertension 1, Age 75 or over 2, Diabetes 1, prior Stroke/TIA 2, Vascular disease 1, Age 65 to 74 1, female Sex 1. Anticoagulation is recommended for a score of 2 or more in men and 3 or more in women (guidelines also support it at lower scores in some cases). Direct oral anticoagulants are preferred; **warfarin** is used for moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis and mechanical valves. Duration of AF over 48 hours without anticoagulation requires TEE or weeks of anticoagulation before elective cardioversion.

**Atrial flutter:** regular atrial rate near 300 per minute with "sawtooth" waves, typically 2:1 block giving a ventricular rate near 150; typical flutter circles the tricuspid annulus and is cured by catheter ablation of the cavotricuspid isthmus.

**AVNRT:** a regular, narrow-complex tachycardia at 150 to 250 per minute from a reentry circuit in the AV node. Treat with vagal maneuvers (modified Valsalva), then **adenosine** (typical adult dosing for study, to be checked against current references before any clinical use: 6 mg rapid IV push, then 12 mg), which transiently blocks the AV node. Unstable: synchronized cardioversion.

**Wolff-Parkinson-White (WPW):** an accessory pathway (bundle of Kent) causes a short PR and a **delta wave** (slurred QRS upstroke). Orthodromic reentrant tachycardia is narrow and regular. In **AF with pre-excitation** (irregular, wide, very fast), AV nodal blockers (beta blockers, calcium channel blockers, adenosine, digoxin) can push all conduction down the pathway and trigger ventricular fibrillation; use procainamide or cardioversion, and ablate the pathway.

### Ventricular arrhythmias

**Ventricular tachycardia (VT):** at least three beats at over 100 per minute, wide QRS; sustained if over 30 seconds or causing instability. **A wide-complex tachycardia is VT until proven otherwise.** Unstable VT with a pulse: synchronized cardioversion. Pulseless VT and **ventricular fibrillation (VF):** CPR and unsynchronized defibrillation, epinephrine 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes, amiodarone for refractory VF/VT (typical adult doses for study, to be checked against current references before any clinical use). Stable monomorphic VT: procainamide, amiodarone or cardioversion. Long-term: treat ischemia and electrolytes and consider an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) for secondary prevention or for ejection fraction 35% or less despite therapy.

**Torsades de pointes:** polymorphic VT with twisting QRS amplitude in the setting of a long QT interval (congenital channelopathies; drugs such as macrolides, fluoroquinolones, antipsychotics, ondansetron, class IA and III antiarrhythmics; hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia). Treat with **IV magnesium sulfate**, correct potassium, stop offending drugs and defibrillate if pulseless.

### Antiarrhythmic drugs (Vaughan Williams)

- **IA** (procainamide, quinidine): sodium channel block plus potassium channel block; prolong QT; procainamide causes drug-induced lupus.
- **IB** (lidocaine, mexiletine): shorten action potential; useful in ischemic ventricular arrhythmia.
- **IC** (flecainide, propafenone): marked sodium block; contraindicated in structural heart disease or after myocardial infarction (increased mortality).
- **II** (beta blockers): slow the AV node.
- **III** (amiodarone, sotalol, dofetilide, ibutilide): potassium channel block, prolonging QT. Amiodarone toxicity: pulmonary fibrosis, hypo- or hyperthyroidism, hepatotoxicity, corneal deposits, blue-gray skin, and bradycardia.
- **IV** (verapamil, diltiazem): block AV nodal calcium channels.

title: Interpreting a described tracing
problem: A rhythm strip shows regular R-R intervals of 4 large boxes, a P wave before every QRS, a PR interval of 280 ms and a QRS of 90 ms, with a normal axis. Interpret it.
step: Rate $= 300/4 = 75$ per minute, which is normal.
step: Rhythm is regular and sinus (a P before every QRS, one QRS per P).
step: Axis is normal and the QRS (90 ms) is narrow.
step: PR 280 ms is above 200 ms: first-degree AV block, which needs no specific treatment unless caused by a drug (beta blocker, calcium channel blocker, digoxin) or with symptoms.

@example
title: Regular narrow tachycardia in a stable patient
problem: A stable 28-year-old has a regular narrow-complex tachycardia at 180 per minute with no visible P waves. What is the plan?
step: A regular narrow tachycardia at about 180 without P waves suggests AVNRT (or AV reentry).
step: First, vagal maneuvers (modified Valsalva).
step: If these fail, give adenosine as a rapid IV push followed by a saline flush; it will briefly produce AV block.
step: If adenosine fails or recurs, a calcium channel blocker or beta blocker can be used; if unstable, synchronized cardioversion.
step: Atrial flutter with 2:1 block can be unmasked by adenosine because the sawtooth waves appear during the block.

@mistakes
- Using AV nodal blockers in AF with pre-excitation (WPW).
- Using unsynchronized shocks for a rhythm with a pulse (synchronized) or synchronized shocks for VF (unsynchronized).
- Treating torsades with amiodarone or another QT-prolonging drug instead of magnesium and correction of the cause.

Supplied ECG description: the rhythm is regular at a ventricular rate of 38 per minute; P waves are regular at 90 per minute with no consistent relation to the QRS complexes; the QRS is narrow at 100 ms; the axis is normal. hint: Compare the atrial rate with the ventricular rate and ask whether they are related.
