@article
Ischemia is supply failing to meet demand. Myocardial oxygen demand rises with heart rate, wall stress (afterload, preload) and contractility; supply depends on coronary flow, which occurs mostly in diastole, and on oxygen content. At rest the myocardium already extracts most of the oxygen it receives, so the only way to give it more is more flow.

### Syndromes

- **Stable angina:** exertional substernal pressure relieved by rest or nitroglycerin; fixed atherosclerotic stenosis, usually above 70%. ECG at rest is often normal; during an episode there is ST depression. Management: aspirin, high-intensity statin, a beta blocker, a calcium channel blocker or a long-acting nitrate as first-line antianginal therapy (current guidelines treat these as options chosen by patient factors, with a beta blocker preferred after myocardial infarction or with reduced ejection fraction), others added as needed, and revascularization for refractory symptoms or left main/multivessel disease.
- **Vasospastic (Prinzmetal) angina:** rest pain, often at night or morning, with transient **ST elevation**; caused by coronary spasm (smoking, cocaine, triptans). Treat with calcium channel blockers and nitrates; avoid nonselective beta blockers in cocaine toxicity.
- **Unstable angina:** new-onset, crescendo or rest angina, with no troponin elevation.
- **NSTEMI:** troponin elevated, ST depression or T inversion (or normal ECG), partial or transient occlusion.
- **STEMI:** transmural ischemia from complete occlusion; ST elevation of 1 mm or more in two contiguous limb leads or precordial leads (in V2 and V3 the thresholds are higher, about 2 mm in men aged 40 or older, 2.5 mm in men under 40 and 1.5 mm in women).

Notes: **posterior MI** shows ST depression and tall R waves in V1 to V3, confirmed with posterior leads V7 to V9. **Right ventricular infarction** (proximal RCA) accompanies inferior STEMI and presents with hypotension, elevated jugular venous pressure and clear lungs; confirm with right-sided lead V4R and treat with IV fluids; **avoid nitrates and diuretics** (preload dependent).

### Biomarkers and histology

**Troponin I or T** rises 3 to 4 hours after injury, peaks at 24 to 48 hours and remains elevated for 7 to 14 days; it is the preferred test (high-sensitivity assays are now standard). **CK-MB** rises at 3 to 6 hours and normalizes within 48 to 72 hours, so it helps detect early reinfarction. Troponin also rises in myocarditis, PE, sepsis and renal failure.

Histologic timeline of infarction: first 4 hours, no gross or microscopic change (but electron microscopy shows mitochondrial changes); 4 to 24 hours, coagulative necrosis with wavy fibers; **1 to 3 days, neutrophil infiltration**; **3 to 7 days, macrophages** removing debris (the myocardium is softest, with peak risk of rupture); 1 to 3 weeks, granulation tissue; after 2 months, a collagenous scar.

### Complications

- Within hours: **ventricular arrhythmias** (VF is the main cause of death before hospitalization), then heart block, particularly with inferior MI.
- 3 to 7 days: **free wall rupture** (tamponade, pulseless electrical activity), **interventricular septal rupture** (new holosystolic murmur with a step-up in oxygen saturation between the right atrium and ventricle), and **papillary muscle rupture** (acute mitral regurgitation with pulmonary edema; the posteromedial papillary muscle is most vulnerable, with a single blood supply from the posterior descending artery).
- Weeks: **ventricular aneurysm** (persistent ST elevation, mural thrombus), pseudoaneurysm, and **Dressler syndrome** (pericarditis, 2 to 10 weeks later, autoimmune). Early pericarditis occurs in 1 to 3 days.

### Acute management

Initial steps: ECG within 10 minutes, IV access, monitor, and defibrillator available. **Aspirin** (162 to 325 mg chewed, typical adult dose for study), a **P2Y12 inhibitor** (ticagrelor, prasugrel or clopidogrel), parenteral anticoagulation (unfractionated heparin or enoxaparin, bivalirudin with PCI), **sublingual nitroglycerin** for pain (avoid within 24 to 48 hours of a phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitor, in hypotension or in RV infarction), an early oral **beta blocker** (unless heart failure, shock, bradycardia or heart block), a **high-intensity statin**, and an ACE inhibitor if there is LV dysfunction, hypertension, diabetes or anterior MI. Supplemental oxygen only if oxygen saturation is below 90%.

**Reperfusion in STEMI:** **primary PCI** is preferred, with a goal of first medical contact to device time of 90 minutes or less (120 minutes if transfer is needed). If PCI cannot be performed within 120 minutes of first contact and symptoms began less than 12 hours ago, give **fibrinolysis** (door-to-needle 30 minutes or less). **Contraindications to fibrinolysis** include prior intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke within 3 months, active internal bleeding, suspected aortic dissection, intracranial neoplasm or vascular malformation, and significant head trauma within 3 months. Cardiogenic shock or heart failure, or a failure of fibrinolysis, mandates transfer for PCI. NSTEMI risk stratification (such as GRACE or TIMI scores) guides an invasive strategy, immediate if unstable.

@example
title: Choosing reperfusion
problem: A 58-year-old man arrives at a rural hospital 3 hours after chest pain onset with ST elevation in leads V1 to V4. The nearest PCI center is 2.5 hours away by transport, and he has no bleeding contraindications. What do you do?
step: Anterior STEMI is a diagnosis for emergent reperfusion.
step: Transfer would give a first-contact-to-device time of more than 150 minutes, exceeding the 120-minute threshold.
step: Symptom onset is under 12 hours and there are no contraindications, so give fibrinolysis (door-to-needle goal 30 minutes) along with aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor and an anticoagulant.
step: Then transfer to a PCI-capable center for angiography (routine within 2 to 24 hours of lysis, immediate if lysis fails or he develops shock).

@example
title: Classifying chest pain from data
problem: A 64-year-old woman has 30 minutes of rest chest pressure. The ECG shows 1.5 mm of ST depression in V4 to V6, and high-sensitivity troponin is above the 99th percentile and rising. Classify the syndrome.
step: Rest pain with ST depression suggests an acute coronary syndrome without ST elevation.
step: Troponin elevation with a rising pattern signifies myocardial necrosis.
step: Diagnosis: NSTEMI (if troponin were normal on serial testing, it would be unstable angina).
step: Treatment: dual antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, beta blocker and statin, with an early invasive strategy (immediate if refractory pain, hemodynamic instability or arrhythmia).

@mistakes
- Using nitroglycerin in a patient with an inferior MI and hypotension without checking for right ventricular infarction.
- Waiting for troponin before treating or activating the cath lab when the ECG already shows STEMI.
- Giving a beta blocker in acute decompensated heart failure or cardiogenic shock.
