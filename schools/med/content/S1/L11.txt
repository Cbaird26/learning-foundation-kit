@article
Congenital heart disease occurs in about 1% of live births. A useful way to organize it is by **physiology**: does blood bypass the lungs (cyanosis), is there a shunt from the high-pressure left side to the right side, or is there a fixed obstruction?

### Left-to-right shunts (acyanotic)

Blood flows from systemic to pulmonary circulation, causing pulmonary overcirculation and, over years, pulmonary vascular disease.

- **Ventricular septal defect (VSD):** the most common congenital defect; usually membranous. A **holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border**; small defects are louder (high-velocity jet) and often close spontaneously, large ones cause heart failure and failure to thrive at 1 to 2 months as pulmonary resistance falls.
- **Atrial septal defect (ASD):** **secundum** (in the fossa ovalis) is the commonest; primum (with AV canal defects, Down syndrome) and sinus venosus (near the SVC, with anomalous pulmonary veins) are others. A soft systolic ejection murmur at the left upper sternal border from increased flow across the pulmonic valve and a **wide, fixed split S2** because right ventricular volume is constant across respiration. Complications: right-heart dilation, atrial arrhythmias and **paradoxical embolism**. Closure (percutaneous for secundum) for significant shunts.
- **Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA):** continuous machine-like murmur at the left infraclavicular area; bounding pulses and wide pulse pressure; associated with prematurity and maternal rubella. Close with indomethacin or ibuprofen in premature infants, or by device or surgery. A PDA must be **kept open** (alprostadil, prostaglandin E1) in lesions that depend on it.
- **Atrioventricular septal defect:** strongly associated with Down syndrome (trisomy 21).

### Eisenmenger syndrome

A large, uncorrected left-to-right shunt causes progressive pulmonary vascular remodeling and pulmonary hypertension. When pulmonary resistance exceeds systemic resistance, the shunt **reverses to right-to-left**, causing late cyanosis, clubbing and polycythemia; in a PDA, cyanosis and clubbing affect only the lower extremities (**differential cyanosis**). The reversed state is not repairable by closing the defect (closure is contraindicated); treat pulmonary hypertension and consider transplantation.

### Cyanotic lesions

- **Tetralogy of Fallot:** the most common cyanotic lesion beyond infancy. Four features: **VSD, overriding aorta, right ventricular outflow obstruction (pulmonary stenosis), right ventricular hypertrophy**, all from anterosuperior displacement of the infundibular septum. The degree of pulmonary stenosis sets the cyanosis. Chest x-ray: boot-shaped heart. **Tet spells** (hypercyanotic episodes) occur with crying or feeding; **squatting or knee-to-chest** raises systemic vascular resistance and reduces the right-to-left shunt. Treat spells with knee-chest positioning, oxygen, morphine, IV fluids, beta blocker or phenylephrine. Definitive treatment is surgical repair. Association: 22q11.2 deletion.
- **Transposition of the great arteries (d-TGA):** the aorta arises from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left, creating two parallel circuits; survival depends on mixing through a PDA, ASD or VSD. A cyanotic newborn on the first days with an "egg on a string" cardiac silhouette; maternal diabetes is a risk factor. Treat with **prostaglandin E1** to keep the ductus open, balloon atrial septostomy, and the **arterial switch operation**.
- **Truncus arteriosus:** one arterial trunk with a VSD.
- **Total anomalous pulmonary venous return (TAPVR):** pulmonary veins drain to the right atrium or systemic veins; "snowman" sign (supracardiac type).
- **Tricuspid atresia:** needs an ASD and VSD for survival.
- **Ebstein anomaly:** apical displacement of the tricuspid valve with atrialization of the right ventricle; associated with maternal lithium use.

### Obstructive lesions and others

**Coarctation of the aorta:** narrowing near the ligamentum arteriosum. Infantile (preductal) forms present in the first days with shock when the ductus closes; juxtaductal or postductal forms present in older children or adults with **hypertension in the arms, weak delayed femoral pulses** (arm-leg gradient), a systolic murmur over the back and **rib notching** on x-ray. Associations: **Turner syndrome** and bicuspid aortic valve. **Hypoplastic left heart** and critical aortic stenosis are **ductal-dependent systemic** lesions; **critical pulmonary stenosis** and pulmonary atresia are ductal-dependent pulmonary lesions. **Williams syndrome** causes supravalvular aortic stenosis; **Noonan syndrome** causes pulmonary stenosis and HCM; congenital rubella causes PDA and pulmonary artery stenosis.

### Shunt calculation

Shunt size is measured as the ratio of pulmonary to systemic blood flow:
$$\frac{Q_p}{Q_s} = \frac{S_aO_2 - S_{mv}O_2}{S_{pv}O_2 - S_{pa}O_2}$$
where $S_aO_2$ is the systemic arterial saturation, $S_{mv}O_2$ the mixed venous saturation, $S_{pv}O_2$ the pulmonary vein saturation and $S_{pa}O_2$ the pulmonary artery saturation. A ratio above about 1.5 typically indicates a hemodynamically significant shunt that merits closure. An oxygen **step-up** of about 7% or more between the right atrium and the right ventricle (or ventricle and pulmonary artery) locates the shunt.

@example
title: Calculating a shunt ratio
problem: At catheterization of a child with an ASD, systemic arterial saturation is 97%, mixed venous saturation 70%, pulmonary vein saturation 98% and pulmonary artery saturation 85%. Calculate the $Q_p/Q_s$ ratio.
step: Numerator (systemic extraction): $97 - 70 = 27$.
step: Denominator (pulmonary extraction): $98 - 85 = 13$.
step: $Q_p/Q_s = 27/13 = 2.08$.
step: A ratio above 1.5 indicates a significant left-to-right shunt (here, about twice as much blood goes through the lungs as through the body); closure is indicated.

@example
title: A cyanotic newborn
problem: A 2-day-old term infant of a mother with poorly controlled diabetes is cyanotic, with oxygen saturation of 78% that does not improve with 100% oxygen, a single loud S2 and no murmur. What is the diagnosis and first step?
step: Persistent cyanosis unresponsive to oxygen (a hyperoxia test) suggests a cardiac cause.
step: Maternal diabetes and a single S2 with no murmur suggest transposition of the great arteries.
step: Start IV prostaglandin E1 to keep the ductus open for mixing.
step: Confirm with echocardiography; balloon atrial septostomy as a bridge; arterial switch within the first weeks.

@mistakes
- Closing a defect after Eisenmenger reversal.
- Treating a tet spell with beta agonists or by agitating the child; calm, position and give oxygen.
- Forgetting prostaglandin E1 for a cyanotic newborn with a suspected ductal-dependent lesion, and giving indomethacin to a ductal-dependent neonate.
- Confusing a fixed split S2 (ASD) with the physiologic splitting of respiration.

@practice self
prompt: Explain Eisenmenger syndrome: describe the sequence from a large VSD to cyanosis, the signs a physician would find, and why closing the defect at that stage is contraindicated.
hint: Follow what happens to pulmonary vascular resistance and shunt direction over years.
hint: Think about what the shunt does for the right ventricle once resistance is high.
solution: A large VSD shunts blood left to right, overloading the pulmonary circulation. Chronic high flow and pressure cause pulmonary arteriolar remodeling and rising pulmonary vascular resistance. Eventually resistance equals or exceeds systemic resistance and the shunt reverses to right-to-left, causing central cyanosis, clubbing, polycythemia, a loud P2 and right ventricular heave. Closing the defect at this stage removes the pop-off route for the right ventricle, causing right heart failure and death, so the established disease is managed with pulmonary vasodilators, and transplantation is considered.
rubric: Describes the left-to-right shunt leading to pulmonary vascular disease.
rubric: States that shunt reversal produces cyanosis, clubbing and polycythemia.
rubric: Explains that closure is contraindicated because the right ventricle needs the shunt as a pressure relief.
