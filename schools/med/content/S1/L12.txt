### Aneurysm and dissection

**Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)** is a focal dilation to 3 cm or more, usually infrarenal, linked to atherosclerosis, smoking and male sex. Most are asymptomatic and found by ultrasonography; **one-time ultrasound screening is recommended for men 65 to 75 who have ever smoked.** Rupture presents with abdominal or back pain, hypotension and a pulsatile mass. Elective repair is indicated at 5.5 cm or more in men (about 5.0 cm in women), growth over 0.5 cm in 6 months, or symptoms; ruptured AAA is a surgical emergency. **Thoracic aneurysm** is associated with cystic medial necrosis (Marfan syndrome), hypertension and tertiary syphilis.

**Aortic dissection:** an intimal tear lets blood split the media. Abrupt **tearing chest or back pain**, a blood pressure difference between arms, a widened mediastinum, and new aortic regurgitation are clues. Risk: hypertension, Marfan syndrome, bicuspid valve, cocaine, pregnancy. Diagnose with CT angiography (or transesophageal echocardiography if unstable). **Stanford type A** (involves the ascending aorta) needs emergency surgery; **type B** (descending only) is managed medically: IV **beta blocker first** (esmolol, labetalol) to lower heart rate below about 60 and shear force, then a vasodilator if needed, with a systolic target around 100 to 120 mm Hg. Vasodilator alone causes reflex tachycardia and increased shear. Complications of type A: tamponade, aortic regurgitation, coronary occlusion (usually the RCA), stroke.

### Peripheral arterial disease and vasculitis

**Peripheral arterial disease (PAD):** intermittent claudication (calf pain with walking, relieved by rest), diminished pulses and rest pain or ulcers in critical ischemia. The **ankle-brachial index (ABI)** is ankle systolic pressure divided by brachial systolic pressure: normal 1.0 to 1.4; 0.90 or below is PAD; below about 0.4 suggests severe disease; above 1.4 suggests noncompressible calcified vessels. Treatment: smoking cessation, **supervised exercise**, statin and antiplatelet therapy, and cilostazol for claudication (contraindicated in heart failure); revascularization for lifestyle-limiting or critical ischemia. Acute limb ischemia (pain, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis, poikilothermia) needs heparin and urgent revascularization.

**Vasculitides:** **giant cell arteritis** (age over 50, new headache, jaw claudication, scalp tenderness, elevated ESR, polymyalgia rheumatica; risk of **permanent vision loss**): start **high-dose glucocorticoids immediately**, before temporal artery biopsy. **Takayasu arteritis** (young Asian women, pulseless disease, aortic arch). **Kawasaki disease** (children under 5: fever at least 5 days, conjunctivitis, rash, cervical lymphadenopathy, red cracked lips and strawberry tongue, swollen extremities; **coronary aneurysm** risk): IVIG plus aspirin. **Polyarteritis nodosa** (medium vessels; hepatitis B; spares the lungs). **Buerger disease** (young male smokers; digital ischemia).

### Cardiovascular pharmacology

- **Beta blockers:** selective (metoprolol, atenolol, bisoprolol) or nonselective (propranolol), plus carvedilol and labetalol (alpha and beta). Lower heart rate and contractility, reduce renin. Adverse effects: bradycardia, AV block, bronchospasm, fatigue, masking of hypoglycemia. Avoid in decompensated HF, severe asthma and high-grade block.
- **Calcium channel blockers:** dihydropyridines (amlodipine, nifedipine) are vasodilators (edema, flushing, reflex tachycardia); verapamil and diltiazem act on the heart (bradycardia, AV block; verapamil causes constipation).
- **Nitrates:** release nitric oxide and cause **venodilation**, lowering preload; headache; tolerance (needs a nitrate-free interval); contraindicated with phosphodiesterase-5 inhibitors. **Hydralazine** dilates arterioles (reflex tachycardia, drug-induced lupus).
- **Diuretics:** loop (furosemide; inhibit NKCC2; hypokalemia, ototoxicity, hypocalcemia), thiazide (inhibit NCC; hypercalcemia), and potassium-sparing (spironolactone blocks the aldosterone receptor; amiloride blocks ENaC; hyperkalemia).
- **Digoxin:** inhibits the Na-K ATPase, raising intracellular calcium (positive inotropy) and increasing vagal tone. Narrow therapeutic index. **Toxicity**: nausea, visual changes (yellow-green halos), confusion, and arrhythmias (atrial tachycardia with block, ventricular ectopy); precipitated by hypokalemia, renal failure, and drugs such as amiodarone, verapamil and quinidine. Treat with **digoxin-specific antibody (Fab) fragments** and correct electrolytes.
- **Antiplatelets:** aspirin irreversibly inhibits COX-1 (reducing thromboxane A2); clopidogrel and prasugrel irreversibly block the P2Y12 receptor, and ticagrelor reversibly.
- **Anticoagulants:** **unfractionated heparin** (activates antithrombin; monitored by aPTT; HIT risk; reversed by protamine); LMWH (anti-Xa; renal clearance); **warfarin** (inhibits vitamin K epoxide reductase; monitored by INR, target usually 2 to 3; teratogenic; reversed by vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrate); **DOACs** (apixaban and rivaroxaban inhibit factor Xa, dabigatran inhibits thrombin; reversed by four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate and idarucizumab, respectively; andexanet alfa was withdrawn from the U.S. market in December 2025).

### Comparing the heart failure drug classes

For HFrEF, ARNI/ACE inhibitor/ARB reduce afterload and block remodeling (risks: hyperkalemia, angioedema, creatinine rise, teratogenicity); beta blockers reduce sympathetic toxicity (start low, avoid in acute decompensation); MRAs block aldosterone (hyperkalemia, gynecomastia with spironolactone); SGLT2 inhibitors reduce hospitalization (genital infection, volume depletion); loop diuretics relieve congestion only; hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate adds benefit in selected patients; ivabradine lowers sinus rate; digoxin reduces hospitalization but not mortality.

@example
title: Ankle-brachial index
problem: A 68-year-old smoker has calf pain after walking two blocks, relieved by rest. The ankle systolic pressure is 72 mm Hg and the brachial systolic is 130 mm Hg. Calculate the ABI and decide on management.
step: $ABI = 72/130 = 0.55$.
step: An ABI of 0.90 or lower confirms PAD; 0.55 indicates moderate to severe disease.
step: Start smoking cessation, supervised exercise therapy, a statin and an antiplatelet drug; consider cilostazol if there is no heart failure.
step: Refer for revascularization if symptoms limit lifestyle despite therapy or if rest pain or tissue loss develops.

@example
title: Medical management of a type B dissection
problem: A 60-year-old hypertensive man has tearing interscapular pain. CT angiography shows a dissection of the descending aorta only. His blood pressure is 176/102 mm Hg and heart rate 94/min. What do you give first?
step: Stanford type B (descending aorta only) is treated medically in the absence of malperfusion or rupture.
step: Start an IV beta blocker (esmolol or labetalol) to lower heart rate (below about 60) and contractility.
step: If systolic pressure remains above target, add a vasodilator such as nicardipine or nitroprusside, only after beta blockade.
step: Target a systolic pressure of about 100 to 120 mm Hg, give analgesia, and monitor in the ICU.

@mistakes
- Starting a vasodilator before a beta blocker in aortic dissection.
- Waiting for a temporal artery biopsy before giving steroids in suspected giant cell arteritis.
- Giving cilostazol to a patient with heart failure.
- Ignoring hypokalemia or renal impairment in a patient on digoxin.

For each give the main mechanism, whether it reduces mortality, and one key adverse effect or precaution.
hint: Start by listing which classes improve survival.
hint: Remember the loop diuretic is the one class used for symptoms rather than mortality.
solution: ARNI (sacubitril/valsartan): blocks the angiotensin receptor and inhibits neprilysin to raise natriuretic peptides; reduces mortality; angioedema (never with an ACE inhibitor), hyperkalemia and hypotension. Beta blocker (carvedilol, metoprolol succinate, bisoprolol): blocks sympathetic toxicity and remodeling; reduces mortality; bradycardia, and do not start in acute decompensation. MRA (spironolactone, eplerenone): blocks aldosterone; reduces mortality; hyperkalemia, gynecomastia (spironolactone). SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin): osmotic diuresis and metabolic effects; reduces hospitalization and mortality; genital infections, volume depletion, euglycemic ketoacidosis. Loop diuretic (furosemide): blocks NKCC2 in the thick ascending limb; relieves congestion with no proven mortality benefit; hypokalemia, ototoxicity, volume depletion.
rubric: States a plausible mechanism for each class.
rubric: Correctly identifies that the loop diuretic does not reduce mortality while the other four do.
rubric: Gives one valid adverse effect or precaution per class, including angioedema with ARNI plus ACE inhibitor.
