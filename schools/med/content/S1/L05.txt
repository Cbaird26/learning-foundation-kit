@article
Blood pressure follows from $MAP = CO \times SVR$ (plus a small venous term). Chronic hypertension damages vessels and organs silently: stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, aortic dissection and retinopathy. Treating it is among the most effective preventive interventions in medicine.

### Definitions

The ACC/AHA categories (introduced in 2017 and kept in the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline) define, from the average of two or more careful readings on at least two occasions:

| Category | Systolic | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|
| Normal | under 120 | and under 80 |
| Elevated | 120 to 129 | and under 80 |
| Stage 1 | 130 to 139 | or 80 to 89 |
| Stage 2 | 140 or higher | or 90 or higher |

European guidelines use 140/90 as the diagnostic threshold, so organizations differ. Home and ambulatory monitoring identify **white coat** and **masked** hypertension. Correct cuff size matters: a cuff that is too small gives falsely high readings.

### Primary and secondary hypertension

About 90% of cases are primary (essential): a mix of genetics, sodium intake, obesity, alcohol, inactivity and aging. Suspect **secondary** causes when hypertension is severe, resistant to three drugs including a diuretic, of early or abrupt onset, or accompanied by clues:

- **Primary aldosteronism (Conn syndrome):** hypertension with hypokalemia, mild metabolic alkalosis and low renin; screen with the **plasma aldosterone to renin ratio**; adenoma or bilateral hyperplasia; treat with surgery or a mineralocorticoid antagonist (spironolactone, eplerenone).
- **Renal artery stenosis:** atherosclerotic in older patients, **fibromuscular dysplasia** (a "string of beads" on angiography) in young women; flank or abdominal bruit; worsening creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB.
- **Renal parenchymal disease:** the most common secondary cause; look for abnormal urinalysis and creatinine.
- **Pheochromocytoma:** episodic headache, sweating and palpitations with hypertension; test plasma free metanephrines; give an **alpha blocker first** and only then a beta blocker, to avoid unopposed alpha stimulation.
- **Cushing syndrome, coarctation of the aorta** (arm-leg blood pressure gradient, delayed femoral pulses), **obstructive sleep apnea**, hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, and drugs (NSAIDs, oral contraceptives, decongestants, glucocorticoids, cocaine).

### Treatment

Lifestyle changes (DASH diet, sodium reduction, weight loss, exercise, limiting alcohol) lower systolic pressure by several mm Hg each. In the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline, drug therapy starts at 130/80 or higher for adults with established cardiovascular disease, diabetes, chronic kidney disease or an elevated estimated 10-year cardiovascular risk (about 7.5% or more by the PREVENT equations), and after 3 to 6 months of lifestyle change for lower-risk stage 1; at 140/90 or higher (stage 2) drug therapy starts for everyone. Most goals are **under 130/80**. For stage 2 or when blood pressure is more than 20/10 mm Hg above goal, start two agents, preferably as a single pill.

**First-line classes:** thiazide-type diuretics (chlorthalidone, hydrochlorothiazide), ACE inhibitors or ARBs, and dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers (amlodipine). Compelling indications modify the choice: diabetes with albuminuria or chronic kidney disease favors an ACE inhibitor or ARB; heart failure with reduced ejection fraction favors an ACE inhibitor, ARB or ARNI plus beta blocker and a mineralocorticoid antagonist; Black patients without heart failure or CKD usually start with a thiazide-type diuretic or calcium channel blocker; pregnancy uses labetalol, nifedipine or methyldopa; **ACE inhibitors and ARBs are teratogenic** and contraindicated.

Characteristic adverse effects: ACE inhibitors cause cough and angioedema (bradykinin), hyperkalemia and a creatinine rise in bilateral renal artery stenosis; ARBs share all but cough and have less angioedema; thiazides cause hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hyperuricemia and hyperglycemia; amlodipine causes peripheral edema.

### Hypertensive crises

**Hypertensive emergency** is severe elevation (generally above 180/120) with acute target organ damage: encephalopathy, stroke, acute coronary syndrome, acute pulmonary edema, aortic dissection, acute kidney injury or eclampsia. Give a titratable IV drug (nicardipine, clevidipine, labetalol, nitroprusside) in an ICU. Lower MAP by no more than about **25% in the first hour**, then to about 160/100 over the next 2 to 6 hours, because brain, heart and kidneys autoregulate. Exceptions: aortic dissection (rapid target systolic under 120 mm Hg, beta blocker such as esmolol first) and ischemic stroke (permissive hypertension unless thrombolysis is planned, when the threshold is 185/110). **Hypertensive urgency** has no acute organ damage and is managed with oral agents and follow-up; rapid lowering is harmful.

@example
title: Choosing a first step from a clinic reading
problem: A 52-year-old woman without diabetes or kidney disease has an average clinic blood pressure of 152/94 mm Hg and a 10-year ASCVD risk of 12%. What is the plan?
step: Category: systolic at least 140, so stage 2 hypertension; drug therapy is indicated at this stage whatever her risk estimate.
step: Goal under 130/80; she is $152 - 130 = 22$ mm Hg above the systolic goal, which is over 20 (and she has stage 2 disease), so start two agents.
step: Reasonable combination: an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a calcium channel blocker or thiazide-type diuretic, in a single pill, with lifestyle changes.
step: Recheck in about a month and titrate; check potassium and creatinine after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB or a diuretic.

@example
title: Urgency or emergency?
problem: A 60-year-old man has a blood pressure of 210/125 mm Hg and a headache. He has papilledema and new confusion. Is this urgency or emergency, and how fast should pressure fall?
step: Papilledema and confusion indicate acute target organ damage (hypertensive encephalopathy), so this is an emergency.
step: Admit to the ICU and start an IV titratable agent such as nicardipine or clevidipine.
step: MAP $= 125 + \frac{210 - 125}{3} = 153$ mm Hg; reduce by no more than 25% in the first hour, to about 115.
step: Then lower gradually to roughly 160/100 over 2 to 6 hours. Overshoot can cause cerebral, myocardial or renal ischemia.

@mistakes
- Treating an asymptomatic severe elevation as an emergency and dropping the pressure rapidly; only elevation with acute organ damage is an emergency.
- Starting a beta blocker alone in suspected pheochromocytoma; start the alpha blocker first.
- Prescribing an ACE inhibitor or ARB to a woman who is pregnant or planning pregnancy.
