### Cardiomyopathies

**Dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)** is the commonest: a dilated, poorly contracting ventricle (systolic failure, eccentric hypertrophy). Causes: alcohol, **doxorubicin** (dose-dependent), viral myocarditis (coxsackievirus B), **Chagas disease** (*Trypanosoma cruzi*), cocaine, hemochromatosis, peripartum, thiamine deficiency, tachycardia-induced, and genetic variants (titin truncation). Treat as HFrEF (week 8); stop the toxin.

**Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM):** autosomal dominant sarcomere mutations (beta-myosin heavy chain, myosin-binding protein C) cause asymmetric septal hypertrophy with myocyte disarray. Systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve causes **dynamic left ventricular outflow obstruction** and a harsh systolic murmur that **increases with Valsalva and standing and decreases with squatting and handgrip**. Presentations: exertional dyspnea, syncope, angina, and **sudden cardiac death in young athletes** (ventricular arrhythmia). Treatment: **beta blockers** or **verapamil** (longer diastolic filling), disopyramide, and the cardiac myosin inhibitor **mavacamten**; septal myectomy or alcohol septal ablation for refractory obstruction; an ICD for high-risk features. **Avoid** dehydration, diuretics, vasodilators (nitrates) and inotropes such as digoxin, which worsen obstruction.

**Restrictive cardiomyopathy:** a stiff ventricle with normal systolic function and diastolic failure. Causes: **amyloidosis** (apple-green birefringence with Congo red; low-voltage ECG despite thick walls; AL or transthyretin types), sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis, radiation fibrosis, eosinophilic (Loeffler) endomyocarditis. It resembles **constrictive pericarditis** (a rigid, thickened or calcified pericardium after tuberculosis, radiation or surgery): both have Kussmaul sign (JVP rises with inspiration) and a dip-and-plateau pressure tracing; constriction has a **pericardial knock** and calcification, a marked respiratory variation of ventricular interdependence and a normal or modest BNP; treatment of constriction is **pericardiectomy**.

**Takotsubo (stress) cardiomyopathy:** emotional or physical stress in a typically postmenopausal woman; chest pain with ST elevation and troponin rise, **apical ballooning** with basal hyperkinesis, no culprit coronary lesion; catecholamine mediated; usually recovers within weeks.

### Pericardium

**Acute pericarditis** (viral most often; also uremic, post-MI, tuberculous, autoimmune, malignant): sharp pleuritic chest pain relieved by sitting forward, a **pericardial friction rub** and an ECG with **diffuse concave ST elevation and PR depression** (with PR elevation in aVR). Treat with an **NSAID (ibuprofen or aspirin) plus colchicine**, with restriction of activity; corticosteroids are reserved for refractory or specific cases because they raise the risk of recurrence.

**Pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade:** fluid in the pericardium raises intrapericardial pressure and impairs filling. **Beck triad:** hypotension, jugular venous distension and muffled heart sounds. **Pulsus paradoxus** is a drop in systolic pressure of more than 10 mm Hg with inspiration. The ECG shows low voltage and sometimes electrical alternans; echocardiography shows right atrial and ventricular diastolic collapse. Treat with urgent **pericardiocentesis** (or surgical drainage) and IV fluids as a temporizing measure.

### Infective endocarditis

Infection of the endocardium, usually valves, by bacteria forming vegetations. Organisms: **Staphylococcus aureus** (most common overall; acute, in injection drug use with tricuspid valve involvement, and in health care-associated infection), **viridans streptococci** (subacute, damaged native valves after dental procedures), coagulase-negative staphylococci (prosthetic valves), **Enterococcus**, **Streptococcus gallolyticus** (bovis; associated with colon cancer), and HACEK organisms or *Bartonella* and *Coxiella* in culture-negative disease. Findings: fever, a new murmur, **Janeway lesions** (painless macules on palms and soles), **Osler nodes** (painful nodules on finger pads), splinter hemorrhages, **Roth spots** (retinal hemorrhages with pale centers), glomerulonephritis and septic emboli.

**Modified Duke criteria:** there are two kinds of major criterion: (1) blood cultures positive for typical organisms (persistently positive for other organisms), and (2) evidence of endocardial involvement on imaging (vegetation, abscess, new prosthetic dehiscence) or new valvular regurgitation; minor criteria include a predisposing heart condition or injection drug use, fever of 38 C or higher, vascular phenomena, immunologic phenomena and microbiologic evidence not meeting a major criterion. Diagnosis: at least 3 sets of blood cultures before antibiotics, then echocardiography (TTE, then TEE if suspicion remains or a prosthetic valve). Treatment: prolonged IV antibiotics (4 to 6 weeks) after cultures, empiric vancomycin plus ceftriaxone in native-valve disease; **surgery** for heart failure from valve destruction, perivalvular abscess, persistent bacteremia, recurrent emboli with a large vegetation or fungal disease.

**Prophylaxis** before dental procedures involving gingival manipulation is recommended only for the highest-risk patients: prosthetic valves or prosthetic repair material, prior endocarditis, certain congenital heart disease (unrepaired cyanotic disease, repaired disease with a residual defect, or within 6 months of repair with prosthetic material), and cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy. The usual regimen is oral amoxicillin 2 g (adults) or 50 mg/kg (children, maximum 2 g) 30 to 60 minutes before the procedure (typical doses for study, to be checked against current references before any clinical use).

### Matching murmurs to lesions

| Murmur | Lesion | Maneuver |
|---|---|---|
| Crescendo-decrescendo systolic, right upper sternal border, to carotids | Aortic stenosis | Softer with Valsalva; louder with squatting |
| Harsh systolic, left sternal border | HCM | Louder with Valsalva and standing; softer with squatting and handgrip |
| Holosystolic at apex to axilla | Mitral regurgitation | Louder with handgrip |
| Early diastolic decrescendo at left sternal border | Aortic regurgitation | Louder with handgrip, sitting forward |
| Opening snap and mid-diastolic rumble at apex | Mitral stenosis | Louder in left lateral decubitus |
| Mid-systolic click and late systolic murmur | Mitral valve prolapse | Click earlier with standing |
| Holosystolic at left lower sternal border | Tricuspid regurgitation | Louder with inspiration |

@example
title: Separating restrictive from constrictive physiology
problem: A 55-year-old man has Kussmaul sign, ascites and edema. Echocardiography shows normal systolic function and calcification of the pericardium; BNP is only mildly elevated. Which diagnosis, and what is the definitive treatment?
step: Elevated venous pressure with a paradoxical rise in JVP on inspiration (Kussmaul) and normal EF with ascites suggests restrictive or constrictive physiology.
step: A calcified pericardium points to constriction rather than a myocardial restrictive process such as amyloidosis.
step: A near-normal BNP also favors constrictive pericarditis (it is high in restrictive cardiomyopathy).
step: Definitive treatment is pericardiectomy.

@example
title: A tamponade bedside check
problem: A 48-year-old with metastatic lung cancer has a blood pressure of 98/70 mm Hg in expiration, distended neck veins and distant heart sounds. During inspiration the systolic pressure falls from 98 to 82 mm Hg. Is this pulsus paradoxus, and what is the next step?
step: Pulsus paradoxus is an inspiratory fall in systolic pressure greater than 10 mm Hg.
step: The fall is $98 - 82 = 16$ mm Hg, so it is present.
step: With Beck triad this is tamponade; confirm with echocardiography (diastolic collapse of the right chambers).
step: Treat with urgent pericardiocentesis; fluids are temporizing.

@mistakes
- Starting diuretics or vasodilators for symptoms in HCM with obstruction.
- Giving glucocorticoids first for routine viral pericarditis instead of an NSAID with colchicine.
- Treating a painful finger nodule as a Janeway lesion; Janeway lesions are painless and Osler nodes are painful.
- Giving endocarditis prophylaxis to patients with only a bicuspid aortic valve or mitral valve prolapse.
