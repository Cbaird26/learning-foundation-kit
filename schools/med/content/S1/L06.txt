@article
**Atherosclerosis** is chronic, lipid-driven inflammation of the arterial intima. It underlies most coronary, cerebrovascular and peripheral arterial disease.

### Pathogenesis

1. **Endothelial dysfunction** (hypertension, smoking, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, shear stress at branch points).
2. **LDL particles enter the intima** and are oxidized; the endothelium expresses adhesion molecules and monocytes migrate in.
3. Monocytes become **macrophages** that ingest oxidized LDL and become **foam cells**: the **fatty streak**, the earliest lesion, seen even in young people.
4. Smooth muscle cells migrate from the media, proliferate and secrete collagen, forming a **fibrous cap** over a lipid core: the **fibrous plaque**. Calcification, necrosis and neovascularization follow.
5. **Complications:** a stable plaque with a thick cap causes fixed stenosis and **stable angina** at demand above supply. A **vulnerable plaque** has a thin cap, a large lipid core and inflammation; it **ruptures**, exposing thrombogenic material, and platelet and fibrin thrombus forms: **acute coronary syndrome**, stroke or limb ischemia. Plaque often causes a less than 50% stenosis before rupture, which is why many infarcts come without prior angina.

Sites in decreasing order of involvement: abdominal aorta, coronary arteries, popliteal arteries, carotid bifurcation, circle of Willis. Major risk factors are age, male sex, family history of premature disease, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, high LDL and low HDL, and chronic kidney disease.

### The lipid panel

Total cholesterol, HDL and triglycerides are measured; LDL is usually estimated by the **Friedewald equation**
$$LDL = TC - HDL - \frac{TG}{5}$$
(all in mg/dL; valid when triglycerides are under about 400 mg/dL). **Non-HDL cholesterol** is $TC - HDL$ and captures all atherogenic lipoproteins; it is useful when triglycerides are high or the patient is not fasting.

**Familial hypercholesterolemia** (autosomal dominant, most commonly an LDL receptor mutation) presents with LDL of 190 mg/dL or higher, tendon xanthomas (Achilles), corneal arcus at a young age and premature coronary disease. **Severe hypertriglyceridemia** (above 500, especially above 1000 mg/dL) carries a risk of acute pancreatitis and eruptive xanthomas.

### Lipid-lowering drugs

| Class | Mechanism | Main effect | Key adverse effects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Statins (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin) | Inhibit HMG-CoA reductase; upregulate hepatic LDL receptors | LDL falls 30 to 55%+ (high intensity at least 50%); proven to reduce events | Myopathy, rhabdomyolysis, transaminase rise; avoid in pregnancy; simvastatin and lovastatin interact with CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, azole antifungals) |
| Ezetimibe | Blocks NPC1L1 intestinal cholesterol absorption | LDL falls about 15 to 20% | Diarrhea; rare liver effects |
| PCSK9 inhibitors (evolocumab, alirocumab) | Monoclonal antibodies preventing LDL receptor degradation | LDL falls about 50 to 60% | Injection site reactions |
| Bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine) | Bind bile acids | LDL falls; triglycerides can rise | GI upset, reduced absorption of other drugs |
| Fibrates (fenofibrate, gemfibrozil) | PPAR-alpha agonists | Triglycerides fall markedly | Myopathy (especially gemfibrozil with statins), gallstones |
| Niacin | Reduces hepatic VLDL | HDL rises, TG falls | Flushing, hyperuricemia, hyperglycemia |

### Who gets a statin (2018 ACC/AHA summary)

Clinical ASCVD (prior MI, stroke or symptomatic peripheral disease): **high-intensity statin**; add ezetimibe, then a PCSK9 inhibitor, if LDL remains above the threshold (about 70 mg/dL in very high-risk patients). LDL 190 mg/dL or higher: high-intensity statin. Diabetes at age 40 to 75: at least moderate intensity. Others aged 40 to 75: use the pooled cohort equation for 10-year risk (about 7.5% or higher favors a statin after risk discussion); a coronary artery calcium score of zero can support deferring therapy, and a score of 100 or more favors it. Targets versus fixed-percent reduction vary by organization. (The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline instead uses PREVENT-ASCVD risk with lower categories of about 3% to under 5%, 5% to under 10% and 10% or more, LDL-C goals of below 100, 70 or 55 mg/dL, a once-in-adulthood lipoprotein(a) measurement, and ezetimibe, bempedoic acid or a PCSK9 antibody as add-ons.)

### Choosing antihypertensives for different patients

Revisit week 5: diabetes with albuminuria uses an ACE inhibitor or ARB; Black patients without heart failure or CKD use a thiazide-type diuretic or calcium channel blocker; heart failure with reduced EF uses ACE inhibitor/ARB/ARNI, beta blocker, MRA; pregnancy uses labetalol, nifedipine or methyldopa; recent MI uses a beta blocker plus ACE inhibitor; BPH can add an alpha blocker; thiazides are avoided in gout and hypokalemia.

@example
title: Calculating LDL and non-HDL
problem: Fasting results: total cholesterol 240 mg/dL, HDL 40 mg/dL, triglycerides 150 mg/dL. Find LDL and non-HDL cholesterol.
step: $LDL = 240 - 40 - 150/5$.
step: $150/5 = 30$, so $LDL = 240 - 40 - 30 = 170$ mg/dL.
step: Non-HDL $= TC - HDL = 240 - 40 = 200$ mg/dL.
step: Interpretation: LDL of 170 is elevated; a statin decision depends on risk estimate and risk enhancers rather than the value alone.

@example
title: Number needed to treat
problem: In a trial, 12% of control patients and 9% of statin patients have a cardiovascular event over 5 years. Calculate the absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat.
step: $ARR = 12\% - 9\% = 3\% = 0.03$.
step: $NNT = 1/ARR = 1/0.03 = 33.3$.
step: Interpretation: about 34 patients treated for 5 years prevent one event. The relative risk reduction is $3/12 = 25\%$, which sounds bigger than the absolute benefit.

@mistakes
- Using Friedewald when triglycerides exceed about 400 mg/dL.
- Calling a statin a "cholesterol absorption blocker"; that is ezetimibe. Statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase.
- Combining a statin with gemfibrozil or a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor without considering myopathy risk.
- Reporting relative risk reduction as if it were absolute benefit.
