@article
The ECG is a surface recording of the heart's summed electrical activity. Every pattern you see is the action potentials of millions of cells projected onto a lead, so start with the cell.

### Ventricular myocyte action potential

Resting potential is about -90 mV, set by the inward-rectifier potassium current (IK1).

- **Phase 0:** rapid depolarization by fast sodium channels (INa). Class I antiarrhythmics block this phase.
- **Phase 1:** brief repolarization by transient potassium efflux.
- **Phase 2:** the **plateau**: inward calcium current through L-type channels balances outward potassium current. Calcium entry triggers calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum (excitation-contraction coupling). Phase 2 is why the ventricle has a long refractory period.
- **Phase 3:** repolarization by potassium efflux (delayed rectifiers). Class III drugs block these channels and prolong repolarization (so QT lengthens).
- **Phase 4:** resting potential.

### Pacemaker cells

SA node and AV node cells have an unstable phase 4 caused by the **funny current** (If, slow inward sodium) and calcium entry; **phase 0 is carried by L-type calcium channels**, not sodium, so these nodal tissues are slower and are suppressed by calcium channel blockers (verapamil, diltiazem). Intrinsic rates: SA node 60 to 100, AV junction 40 to 60, Purkinje ventricular escape 20 to 40 (Purkinje fibers have fast sodium-channel upstrokes and conduct quickly). The fastest pacemaker wins (**overdrive suppression**). Sympathetic stimulation steepens phase 4 (beta-1 receptors, cAMP); acetylcholine (M2) flattens it.

### Conduction and the ECG waves

Order: SA node, atrial myocardium, **AV node** (slowest conduction, which gives time for ventricular filling and protects the ventricle from rapid atrial rates), bundle of His, bundle branches, Purkinje fibers, ventricles.

- **P wave:** atrial depolarization.
- **PR interval** (normal 120 to 200 ms): atrial depolarization plus AV nodal delay.
- **QRS** (normal under 120 ms): ventricular depolarization; wide if conduction is slow (bundle branch block, hyperkalemia, ventricular origin).
- **ST segment:** the plateau; elevation or depression indicates injury currents.
- **T wave:** ventricular repolarization.
- **QT interval:** ventricular depolarization plus repolarization. Correct for rate: Bazett $QTc = \frac{QT}{\sqrt{RR}}$ with RR in seconds. QTc over about 450 ms (men) or 460 ms (women) is prolonged, and over 500 ms carries high torsades risk.

### Systematic reading

At the standard 25 mm/s: one small box is 0.04 s, one large box 0.2 s, and 10 mm is 1 mV. **Rate** for regular rhythms is $300 \div$ (large boxes between R waves) or $1500 \div$ small boxes; for irregular rhythms count QRS complexes in a 6-second strip and multiply by 10. Then **rhythm** (is there a P before every QRS, and a QRS after every P?), **axis**, **intervals**, and finally morphology (hypertrophy, ischemia, blocks).

**Axis:** the mean frontal-plane QRS direction. Normal is about -30 to +90 degrees. Use leads I and aVF: both positive is normal; I positive with aVF negative is **left axis deviation** (e.g., inferior MI, left anterior fascicular block); I negative with aVF positive is **right axis deviation** (right ventricular hypertrophy, left posterior fascicular block, lateral MI). Lead I lies at 0 degrees and aVF at +90, so from net QRS deflections, $\theta = \arctan\!\left(\frac{aVF}{I}\right)$ (taking the quadrant from the signs).

### Electrolytes on the ECG

**Hyperkalemia:** peaked narrow T waves, then PR prolongation, P wave flattening, QRS widening and a sine-wave pattern. **Hypokalemia:** flattened T waves, ST depression and prominent U waves. **Hypocalcemia** lengthens the ST segment and the QT; **hypercalcemia** shortens the QT.

@example
title: Rate and axis from a described tracing
problem: A regular rhythm has R waves 4 large boxes apart. The net QRS deflection is $+5$ mm in lead I and $+5$ mm in aVF. Give the heart rate and axis.
step: Rate $= 300/4 = 75$ per minute.
step: Both leads are positive, so the axis is in the normal quadrant.
step: Angle $= \arctan(5/5) = 45$ degrees, which lies within the normal range of $-30$ to $+90$.

@example
title: Correcting the QT interval
problem: A patient on a new antibiotic has a heart rate of 100/min and a measured QT interval of 380 ms. Is the QTc prolonged?
step: RR interval $= 60/100 = 0.6$ s.
step: $\sqrt{0.6} = 0.775$.
step: $QTc = 380/0.775 \approx 490$ ms.
step: This exceeds 450 to 460 ms, so it is prolonged, and the drug should be reviewed and electrolytes checked; the uncorrected QT looks normal only because the heart rate is fast.

@mistakes
- Calling phase 0 of the SA node a sodium spike; it is an L-type calcium current.
- Reading the rate as $300/\text{small boxes}$ or $1500/\text{large boxes}$.
- Using QT without correcting for rate, or inserting RR in milliseconds into the Bazett formula.
