@article
A **nucleoside** is a nitrogenous base joined to a five-carbon sugar by an N-glycosidic bond (to N9 of purines, N1 of pyrimidines). A **nucleotide** adds a phosphate on the sugar's 5$'$ hydroxyl. The sugar is **ribose** in RNA and **2$'$-deoxyribose** in DNA. The **purines** are adenine (A) and guanine (G); the **pyrimidines** are cytosine (C), thymine (T, found in DNA, 5-methyluracil) and uracil (U, found in RNA). All are aromatic heterocycles (10 or 6 $\pi$ electrons) that absorb UV light near 260 nm.

### Backbone and double helix

Nucleotides join by **phosphodiester** bonds from the 3$'$-OH of one sugar to the 5$'$-phosphate of the next. Strands run 5$'$ to 3$'$ and are written that way. At physiological pH, phosphate groups (pK$_a$ about 1) are ionized, so DNA is a polyanion. DNA forms an **antiparallel double helix** in which A pairs with T through **two** hydrogen bonds and G pairs with C through **three**. **Chargaff's rules** follow: in double-stranded DNA, $[\text{A}] = [\text{T}]$ and $[\text{G}] = [\text{C}]$, so purines equal pyrimidines. A GC-rich duplex has a higher melting temperature. RNA is usually single stranded, and its 2$'$-OH group lets it hydrolyze under mild base (through a 2$'$,3$'$-cyclic phosphate), which is why DNA is the stable genetic store.

Some drugs are nucleoside analogs: acyclovir (a guanosine analog) and zidovudine (3$'$-azido, so no 3$'$-OH) terminate chain growth; 5-fluorouracil inhibits thymidylate synthase.

### Drug molecules: functional groups and absorption

Oral drugs must dissolve in aqueous gut fluid yet cross lipid membranes. Two ideas from this course explain most of it.

1. **Ionization (Henderson-Hasselbalch).** Only the neutral form crosses a membrane readily. For an acid, $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 10^{\text{pH} - \text{pK}_a}$; for an amine, $[\text{BH}^+]/[\text{B}] = 10^{\text{pK}_{aH} - \text{pH}}$. Aspirin and other carboxylic acids are largely neutral in the stomach (pH 1 to 2) and ionized in blood; amines are protonated in both. Acetaminophen's phenol (pK$_a$ about 9.5) is neutral at physiological pH.
2. **Polarity and size.** **Lipinski's rule of five** predicts poor oral absorption if a compound has more than one violation of: molecular weight $\le$ 500, log P $\le$ 5, hydrogen-bond donors $\le$ 5, hydrogen-bond acceptors $\le$ 10. Polar groups (OH, NH, COOH) increase solubility and reduce permeability; lipophilic groups do the opposite.

**Prodrugs** mask polar groups to improve absorption: esters of carboxylic acids (enalapril, oseltamivir) are hydrolyzed by esterases after absorption to release the active acid.

### Metabolism

The liver converts drugs to more polar, excretable products.

- **Phase I** (functionalization): cytochrome P450 oxidation (aromatic and aliphatic hydroxylation, N- and O-dealkylation), ester and amide hydrolysis, reduction.
- **Phase II** (conjugation): attachment of glucuronic acid (glucuronidation), sulfate, glycine or glutathione to -OH, -NH$_2$, or -COOH groups.

For example, aspirin is hydrolyzed to salicylate, which is conjugated with glycine or glucuronic acid. Acetaminophen is mainly glucuronidated and sulfated; a small fraction is oxidized by CYP2E1 to the reactive quinone imine NAPQI, which glutathione normally detoxifies. Overdose depletes glutathione and N-acetylcysteine is the antidote.
@example
title: Predicting absorption from structure
problem: A drug has a carboxylic acid (pK$_a$ 4.4), molecular weight 206 g/mol, log P 3.5, 1 hydrogen-bond donor and 2 acceptors. Predict its charge state in the stomach (pH 1.5) and blood (pH 7.4) and its likely oral absorption.
step: For the acid, $[\text{A}^-]/[\text{HA}] = 10^{\text{pH} - \text{pK}_a}$. At pH 1.5, this is $10^{-2.9}$ (about 0.001), so the drug is more than 99.8% neutral in the stomach.
step: At pH 7.4, the ratio is $10^{3.0} = 1000$, so about 99.9% is ionized in blood.
step: Check Lipinski: MW 206 ($\le$ 500), log P 3.5 ($\le$ 5), 1 donor ($\le$ 5), 2 acceptors ($\le$ 10). No violations.
step: The drug is neutral and lipophilic in the stomach and gut (good for crossing membranes), meets Lipinski's rule and is predicted to be well absorbed orally; once in blood the anion is more soluble and distributes in plasma, largely bound to albumin.
@mistakes
- Assuming that an ionized drug cannot be absorbed at all; ionization lowers, but does not eliminate, membrane permeability, and the neutral fraction re-equilibrates.
- Counting the 2$'$-OH when asked about the DNA backbone; DNA lacks it.
- Reversing the base-pair hydrogen-bond counts: G-C has three, A-T has two.
- Believing a prodrug is active as given; it is converted to the active drug.

@practice self
prompt: A drug contains a carboxylic acid (pK$_a$ 4.5) and a tertiary amine (pK$_{aH}$ 9.5), has molecular weight 380 and log P 2.5. Describe its dominant charge state in the stomach (pH 1.5) and in blood (pH 7.4), and predict how those groups affect absorption.
solution: In the stomach (pH 1.5) the acid is neutral (pH well below 4.5) and the amine is protonated (pH well below 9.5), so the net charge is $+1$. At pH 7.4 the acid is ionized (CO$_2^-$) and the amine is protonated (about 99% as pH is about 2 units below 9.5), so the drug is mostly a zwitterion with net charge near zero but with two charged groups. Charged groups reduce passive membrane permeability, but the drug meets Lipinski's rules (MW 380, log P 2.5) and its neutral microspecies, though present in small amount, permit some absorption. Absorption is therefore moderate and could be improved by a prodrug that masks the acid as an ester.
