@article
An **amine** has a nitrogen with a lone pair bonded to alkyl or aryl groups: primary (RNH$_2$), secondary (R$_2$NH), tertiary (R$_3$N). Substituted ammonium ions with four groups are **quaternary**. This nitrogen lone pair makes amines both **bases** and **nucleophiles**. Many drugs contain an amine, and the ionization state of that nitrogen at physiological pH shapes absorption, distribution and receptor binding.

### Basicity

Amine basicity is conventionally reported as the pK$_a$ of the conjugate acid (pK$_{aH}$). The larger the pK$_{aH}$, the stronger the base, and pK$_{aH} + $ pK$_b = 14$ at 25$^{\circ}$C. Typical values:

| Base | pK$_{aH}$ (conjugate acid) |
|---|---|
| Ammonia | 9.25 |
| Methylamine | 10.64 |
| Diethylamine | 11.0 |
| Triethylamine | 10.75 |
| Pyridine | 5.2 |
| Aniline | 4.6 |
| Imidazole | 7.0 |
| Acetamide | about $-0.5$ |

Why? Alkyl groups donate electron density and stabilize the ammonium cation, so alkylamines are stronger bases than ammonia (the order among primary, secondary and tertiary is complicated by solvation). In **aniline** the lone pair is delocalized into the ring, so it is less available and aniline is about $10^6$ times less basic than cyclohexylamine. **Pyridine's** lone pair lies in an sp$^2$ orbital in the plane of the ring and is less basic than an sp$^3$ amine, because sp$^2$ orbitals hold electrons closer to the nucleus. In an **amide** the nitrogen lone pair is delocalized onto the carbonyl oxygen, so amides are essentially not basic (protonation occurs on oxygen, if at all). Electron-withdrawing groups lower basicity and electron-donating groups raise it.

At a given pH, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives $[\text{RNH}_3^+]/[\text{RNH}_2] = 10^{\text{pK}_{aH} - \text{pH}}$. An alkylamine with pK$_{aH}$ about 10 is more than 99% protonated at blood pH 7.4, which makes the drug water soluble as a hydrochloride salt but reduces passive membrane crossing.

### Preparation

- **Direct alkylation** of ammonia with RX gives mixtures (polyalkylation), so it is a poor method.
- **Gabriel synthesis**: potassium phthalimide + RX (S$_N$2), then hydrazine or hydroxide gives a primary amine only, free of over-alkylation.
- **Reduction**: nitriles (LiAlH$_4$) give primary amines (one extra carbon); amides (LiAlH$_4$) give amines of the same carbon count; nitro compounds (H$_2$/Pd, or Fe or Sn with HCl) give anilines.
- **Reductive amination**: an aldehyde or ketone plus NH$_3$ or an amine and NaBH$_3$CN (or NaBH(OAc)$_3$) gives primary, secondary or tertiary amines through an imine or iminium ion. It is the most widely used method.

### Reactions

- Amines are acylated by acid chlorides or anhydrides to amides (this is how acetaminophen is formed from 4-aminophenol).
- **Hofmann elimination**: a quaternary ammonium hydroxide, heated, gives the least substituted alkene.
- **Diazonium salts**: ArNH$_2$ + NaNO$_2$, HCl at 0 to 5$^{\circ}$C gives ArN$_2^+$, which reacts with CuCl, CuBr, CuCN (**Sandmeyer**), KI, H$_2$O (to phenols), H$_3$PO$_2$ (to Ar-H), or couples with activated rings to form **azo dyes**.
- Secondary amines with nitrous acid give N-nitrosamines, which are carcinogenic.

Spectroscopy: primary amines show two N-H stretches near 3300 to 3500 cm$^{-1}$, secondary amines one; by the nitrogen rule, a compound with an odd number of nitrogens has an odd molecular mass.
@example
title: Amine basicity at blood pH
problem: An amine drug has pK$_{aH}$ 9.4. What fraction is protonated at pH 7.4?
step: Use $\log([\text{BH}^+]/[\text{B}]) = \text{pK}_{aH} - \text{pH} = 9.4 - 7.4 = 2.0$.
step: The ratio $[\text{BH}^+]/[\text{B}] = 10^2 = 100$.
step: The fraction protonated is $100/(100+1) = 0.990$, or about 99%.
step: Only about 1% is the neutral base, which is the form that crosses lipid membranes most easily, though equilibrium between forms is fast and so absorption still proceeds.
@mistakes
- Quoting the pK$_a$ of the amine N-H (about 35) when the question is about basicity; use the conjugate acid's pK$_a$.
- Predicting that an amide nitrogen is as basic as an amine nitrogen.
- Using direct alkylation with ammonia to make a clean primary amine.
- Confusing nitrile reduction with amide reduction: a nitrile gives a primary amine with one extra carbon relative to the alkyl halide it came from, while an amide gives an amine with the same carbon count.

@practice self
prompt: Explain why aniline is a much weaker base than cyclohexylamine and why acetamide is not basic at all. Then predict which of the two, aniline or cyclohexylamine, is mostly protonated at pH 7.4.
solution: In aniline the nitrogen lone pair overlaps the ring $\pi$ system, so it is delocalized and less available for bonding to H$^+$; protonation also breaks that conjugation. In cyclohexylamine the lone pair is localized on nitrogen and enhanced by the alkyl group. In acetamide the lone pair is delocalized into the carbonyl by amide resonance, so the nitrogen is essentially non-basic. With pK$_{aH}$ about 10.6, cyclohexylamine is mostly protonated at pH 7.4 (ratio about 1600:1), while aniline (pK$_{aH}$ 4.6) is almost entirely neutral.
