@article
Nuclei with an odd mass number or odd atomic number, such as $^1$H and $^{13}$C, behave like tiny magnets. In a strong external field $B_0$ they line up with or against the field, and radio-frequency radiation of the right frequency flips them between the two states. That absorption is **nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)**. Electrons around a nucleus shield it slightly from $B_0$, so each chemically distinct hydrogen resonates at a slightly different frequency. A spectrum is a map of those environments.

### Chemical shift

Because absolute frequencies depend on the magnet, positions are reported as the **chemical shift** $\delta$ in parts per million, measured from tetramethylsilane (TMS, defined as $0$):

$$\delta = \frac{\text{frequency offset from TMS (Hz)}}{\text{spectrometer frequency (MHz)}}.$$

Electronegative atoms and $\pi$ systems pull electron density away from a proton (**deshielding**) and move its signal **downfield** (larger $\delta$). Rough guide:

| Proton type | $\delta$ (ppm) |
|---|---|
| Alkyl C-H | 0.9 to 1.8 |
| C-H next to C=O or C=C (allylic) | 2.0 to 2.7 |
| C-H next to O or halogen | 3.3 to 4.5 |
| Vinyl C=C-H | 4.5 to 6.5 |
| Aromatic H | 6.5 to 8.0 |
| Aldehyde H | 9 to 10 |
| Carboxylic acid O-H | 10 to 12 (broad) |

O-H and N-H signals are variable and broad, and they exchange with D$_2$O.

### Integration

The area under each signal is proportional to the number of hydrogens producing it. Only ratios are measured, so scale them so the total equals the number of hydrogens in the molecular formula.

### Splitting (spin-spin coupling)

A proton with $n$ equivalent neighboring protons on adjacent carbons splits into $n+1$ lines (the **$n+1$ rule**), with Pascal-triangle intensities: singlet (0 neighbors), doublet (1), triplet (2, intensities 1:2:1), quartet (3, 1:3:3:1), and so on. Equivalent protons do not split each other. The classic fingerprints are the **ethyl group** (a 2H quartet near 3.5 to 4.5 if attached to O, plus a 3H triplet near 1.2) and the **isopropyl group** (a 1H septet and a 6H doublet).

### Degrees of unsaturation and carbon-13

Before interpreting a spectrum, compute the **degrees of unsaturation** (rings plus $\pi$ bonds) for $\text{C}_c\text{H}_h\text{N}_n\text{X}_x$:

$$\text{DoU} = \frac{2c + 2 + n - h - x}{2}.$$

Oxygen does not change the count. A benzene ring alone contributes 4. In $^{13}$C NMR each distinct carbon gives one line, so the number of lines reveals symmetry, and a carbonyl carbon appears far downfield (160 to 220 ppm).

### A working strategy

1. Compute DoU. 2. Count signals (the number of distinct hydrogen environments). 3. Use integration and shift to name fragments. 4. Use splitting to say which fragments are adjacent. 5. Assemble and check that every signal is accounted for.
@example
title: Solving C4H8O2 from its proton spectrum
problem: A compound C$_4$H$_8$O$_2$ shows three signals: $\delta$ 1.26 (3H, triplet), $\delta$ 2.04 (3H, singlet) and $\delta$ 4.12 (2H, quartet). Propose a structure.
step: DoU $= (2\cdot 4 + 2 - 8)/2 = 1$, so there is one ring or one $\pi$ bond. With two oxygens and no signal above 9 ppm, a C=O (ester) is likely.
step: The 2H quartet and 3H triplet are an ethyl group. The quartet at 4.12 ppm means the CH$_2$ is bonded to oxygen: an O-CH$_2$CH$_3$ unit.
step: The 3H singlet at 2.04 ppm has no neighbors and sits next to a carbonyl: a CH$_3$-C=O unit.
step: Joining CH$_3$C(=O)- to -OCH$_2$CH$_3$ uses all four carbons, eight hydrogens and both oxygens. The compound is ethyl acetate, CH$_3$CO$_2$CH$_2$CH$_3$.
@mistakes
- Treating O-H or N-H protons as if they split their neighbors; they usually exchange and appear as broad singlets.
- Using the $n+1$ rule with the total number of hydrogens on the molecule instead of only the equivalent hydrogens on adjacent carbons.
- Forgetting that halogens count as hydrogen replacements in the DoU formula, and that oxygen does not count at all.
- Reading integrals as absolute hydrogen counts instead of a ratio that must be scaled to the formula.
@practice num
prompt: On a 300 MHz spectrometer a proton resonates 2100 Hz downfield from TMS. What is its chemical shift in ppm?
answer: 7
check: 2100 / 300
tol: 0.01
unit: ppm
hint: Divide the offset in Hz by the spectrometer frequency in MHz.
hint: $\delta = 2100/300$.
solution: $\delta = 2100\ \text{Hz} / 300\ \text{MHz} = 7.0$ ppm, in the aromatic region.

@practice num
prompt: How many degrees of unsaturation does C$_8$H$_8$O$_2$ have?
answer: 5
check: (2*8 + 2 - 8) / 2
hint: Use $(2c + 2 + n - h - x)/2$ and ignore oxygen.
hint: $(16 + 2 - 8)/2$.
solution: $(2\cdot8 + 2 - 8)/2 = 5$. A benzene ring (4) plus one C=O (1) fits, for example.

@practice num
prompt: Consider a CH$_2$ group in a propyl fragment whose neighbors are a CH$_3$ group on one side and a CH$_2$ group on the other, with all other couplings ignored. How many lines does its signal show?
answer: 6
check: 3 + 2 + 1
hint: Count all equivalent hydrogens on the adjacent carbons.
hint: Three plus two gives five neighbors; apply $n+1$.
solution: Five neighboring hydrogens give $5 + 1 = 6$ lines (a sextet).

@quiz mc
