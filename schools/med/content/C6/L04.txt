@article
The carbonyl group C=O is polarized: carbon is partially positive (electrophilic), oxygen partially negative. Aldehydes (RCHO) and ketones (RCOR') react mainly by **nucleophilic addition**: the nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, the C=O $\pi$ electrons move onto oxygen, and the carbon changes from sp$^2$ (trigonal planar) to sp$^3$ (tetrahedral). Aldehydes react faster than ketones for two reasons: sterics (one small H instead of a second alkyl group) and electronics (a second alkyl group donates electron density and stabilizes the carbonyl).

### Key reactions

| Reagent | Product | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| NaBH$_4$ (then H$_2$O) or LiAlH$_4$ (then H$_3$O$^+$) | Alcohol: aldehyde $\to$ 1°, ketone $\to$ 2° | Hydride is the nucleophile; NaBH$_4$ is milder |
| R-MgX (then H$_3$O$^+$) | Alcohol: formaldehyde $\to$ 1°, other aldehydes $\to$ 2°, ketones $\to$ 3° | New C-C bond |
| HCN / cyanide | Cyanohydrin | Adds one carbon |
| H$_2$O | Hydrate (gem-diol) | Favored for formaldehyde and for electron-poor carbonyls |
| 2 R-OH, H$^+$ | Acetal (ketal for ketones) | Reversible; protecting group for carbonyls, removed by aqueous acid |
| R-NH$_2$, mild acid | Imine (Schiff base) | Loses H$_2$O |
| R$_2$NH, mild acid | Enamine | Secondary amine |
| Ph$_3$P=CHR | Alkene (**Wittig**) | Replaces C=O by C=C |

An acetal is stable to base and nucleophiles, so a ketone can be protected while a different group, such as an ester, is attacked by a Grignard or hydride reagent, and later regenerated with aqueous acid. Aldehydes are oxidized easily (Tollens' reagent, Cr(VI)) to carboxylic acids, and ketones are not.

Spectroscopy: C=O stretch near 1715 cm$^{-1}$ (ketone) or 1725 cm$^{-1}$ (aldehyde, with C-H stretches near 2720 cm$^{-1}$); aldehyde H at 9 to 10 ppm; carbonyl carbon at 190 to 220 ppm.

### Directing effects in substituted benzenes

A substituent already on a ring controls both **rate** and **position** of the next EAS. Substituents are classified by the stability of the arenium ion they allow:

- **Activating, ortho/para directors**: -OH, -OR, -NH$_2$, -NHR, -NHCOR (strong to moderate; lone pair donation), and alkyl groups (weak; inductive and hyperconjugation). They stabilize the arenium ion when the charge sits on the carbon bearing the group (ortho or para attack).
- **Deactivating, ortho/para directors**: the halogens (F, Cl, Br, I). Inductively withdrawing, so the ring is less reactive, but lone pair resonance still prefers ortho/para.
- **Deactivating, meta directors**: -NO$_2$, -CN, -SO$_3$H, -CF$_3$, and carbonyl groups (-CHO, -COR, -CO$_2$H, -CO$_2$R). They destabilize the arenium ion most for ortho and para attack, leaving meta as the least bad option.

When two groups compete, the **stronger activator** controls the position, and substitution rarely occurs between two meta-related groups because of sterics.
@example
title: Nitration of acetophenone
problem: Predict the major product of mononitration (HNO$_3$, H$_2$SO$_4$) of acetophenone, C$_6$H$_5$COCH$_3$, and explain the rate relative to benzene.
step: The acetyl group has a carbonyl carbon attached directly to the ring, with a partially positive carbon. It withdraws electron density by induction and resonance.
step: In ortho or para attack, one resonance form of the arenium ion places positive charge on the ring carbon bonded to the carbonyl carbon, putting two positive charges adjacent. This form is strongly destabilized.
step: In meta attack, positive charge never lands on that carbon, so the arenium ion is less destabilized. Meta is favored.
step: The ring is less reactive than benzene, so harsher conditions are needed. The major product is 3-nitroacetophenone.
@mistakes
- Treating all deactivating groups as meta directors; halogens are deactivating but ortho/para directing.
- Forgetting that a Grignard reagent adds twice to esters but only once to aldehydes and ketones.
- Writing an acetal as the product of 1 mol of alcohol; a hemiacetal has one OR, an acetal has two and requires acid and removal of water.
- Using NaBH$_4$ to reduce an ester or carboxylic acid (it does not, under ordinary conditions).
