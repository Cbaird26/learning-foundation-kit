@article
Acid derivatives have the general form R-C(=O)-Y, where Y is Cl (acid chloride), OCOR (anhydride), SR (thioester), OR' (ester), or NR$_2$ (amide). Unlike aldehydes and ketones, they have a leaving group on the carbonyl carbon, so they undergo **nucleophilic acyl substitution**, not simple addition.

### Mechanism: addition then elimination

1. The nucleophile adds to C=O, forming a **tetrahedral intermediate** with a negative oxygen.
2. The C=O reforms and the **best leaving group** departs.

The overall change is replacement of Y by the nucleophile. A reaction proceeds usefully only if the incoming group is a poorer leaving group than the departing one, that is, if the product is more stable (a weaker-base group) than the starting derivative.

### Reactivity order

$$\text{acid chloride} > \text{anhydride} > \text{thioester} > \text{ester} \approx \text{carboxylic acid} > \text{amide} > \text{carboxylate}.$$

Two factors explain this: (a) **leaving-group ability**, which tracks the acidity of H-Y (HCl, pK$_a$ about $-7$; carboxylic acid about 5; ROH about 16; NH$_3$ about 38); and (b) **resonance donation** from Y into the carbonyl, which is strong for N (amides, very stable, with partial C-N double bond character and planar geometry) and weak for Cl. A more reactive derivative can be converted into a less reactive one, but not the reverse without activation.

### Typical conversions

- Acid chloride + R'OH $\to$ ester; + NH$_3$ (2 equiv.) or R'NH$_2$ $\to$ amide; + H$_2$O $\to$ acid; + carboxylate $\to$ anhydride.
- Anhydride + R'OH $\to$ ester + acid (this is how aspirin is made from salicylic acid and acetic anhydride).
- Ester + R'NH$_2$ $\to$ amide (slow, heat).
- Amides are hydrolyzed only by prolonged heating in strong acid or base.

### Ester hydrolysis

**Acid-catalyzed:** protonation of C=O makes it more electrophilic, water adds, a proton transfer converts OR' into a good leaving group (R'OH), and elimination followed by deprotonation gives the carboxylic acid. Every step is reversible, so this is the reverse of Fischer esterification and the position of equilibrium is controlled by excess water or removal of products. For example, with $K = 4$ and equimolar acid and alcohol, the ester fraction at equilibrium is $2/3$.

**Base-promoted (saponification):** hydroxide adds to the carbonyl, the tetrahedral intermediate expels alkoxide, and the resulting carboxylic acid is immediately deprotonated by the alkoxide (or hydroxide) to give the carboxylate. This last step is essentially irreversible, so one full equivalent of base is consumed and the reaction goes to completion. Acidifying afterward gives the carboxylic acid. Isotopic labeling shows the C-OR' bond (acyl-oxygen cleavage) breaks.

### Reductions and Grignard reactions

- LiAlH$_4$ reduces esters to two alcohols (the acyl portion gives a primary alcohol) and amides to amines.
- DIBAL-H at $-78^{\circ}$C stops at the aldehyde stage with esters; acid chlorides give aldehydes with LiAlH(Ot-Bu)$_3$.
- Esters and acid chlorides react with **two** equivalents of a Grignard reagent to give tertiary alcohols, since the intermediate ketone is more reactive than the starting ester.

Biological relevance: acetyl-CoA is a thioester (an activated acyl donor); peptide bonds are amides; triglycerides and phospholipids are esters.
@example
title: Hydrolysis of methyl benzoate in base
problem: Describe the course and result of heating methyl benzoate with excess NaOH(aq), and explain why the reaction is irreversible.
step: Hydroxide attacks the carbonyl carbon, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate.
step: The C=O reforms and methoxide (CH$_3$O$^-$) is expelled, giving benzoic acid.
step: Benzoic acid (pK$_a$ 4.2) is a far stronger acid than methanol (pK$_a$ about 15.5). Methoxide or hydroxide immediately removes its proton to give sodium benzoate. This step has an enormous equilibrium constant and drives the process to completion.
step: Acidification with HCl later converts the benzoate salt to benzoic acid; methanol is a by-product.
@mistakes
- Calling saponification catalytic in hydroxide; one full equivalent of base is consumed.
- Stating that an ester can be turned into an acid chloride directly by simple treatment with HCl; going down the reactivity ladder is easy but going up is not.
- Forgetting that Grignard reagents add twice to esters and acid chlorides.
- Predicting that LiAlH$_4$ converts an amide to an alcohol; the product is an amine.

@practice self
prompt: Rank these derivatives from most to least reactive toward water and explain the order with leaving-group and resonance arguments: acetyl chloride, acetic anhydride, ethyl acetate, N-methylacetamide.
solution: Acetyl chloride > acetic anhydride > ethyl acetate > N-methylacetamide. Chloride (conjugate acid pK$_a$ about $-7$) is the best leaving group and donates almost no resonance electron density into C=O. The anhydride's leaving group is carboxylate (conjugate acid pK$_a$ about 5) and the carbonyl is shared with a second acyl group. The ester has alkoxide as leaving group (pK$_a$ about 16) and moderate oxygen lone pair donation. The amide has the poorest leaving group (amide anion, conjugate acid pK$_a$ about 38) and strong nitrogen lone pair donation, so it is the least reactive.

rubric: Mentions resonance donation from Y (strongest for nitrogen) stabilizing the amide.
hint: Order by how weak a base the leaving group is, then check which group donates electrons into the carbonyl most.
