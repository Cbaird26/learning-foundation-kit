@article
When $p$ orbitals on adjacent atoms overlap continuously, the $\pi$ electrons are shared over all of them. This is **conjugation**, and it appears in 1,3-butadiene, in enones such as CH$_2$=CH-C(=O)CH$_3$, and in benzene. Delocalization lowers energy: the heat of hydrogenation of 1,3-butadiene is less than twice that of a simple alkene, and the central C-C bond is shorter than a normal single bond because it has partial double-bond character.

### Resonance

When one Lewis structure cannot show delocalization, draw several resonance forms that differ only in electron placement (never atom positions). The true structure is a **resonance hybrid**, more stable than any single form. More significant contributors have full octets, more bonds, and charges on the appropriate atoms. Allyl cations, allyl anions, enolates, carboxylates and amides all owe their stability to resonance.

### UV-visible spectroscopy

Conjugation shrinks the energy gap between the highest occupied and lowest unoccupied $\pi$ orbitals, so $\lambda_{\max}$ moves to longer wavelength with each additional conjugated double bond (ethene about 170 nm, 1,3-butadiene 217 nm, and highly conjugated pigments such as $\beta$-carotene absorb visible light). The Beer-Lambert law $A = \varepsilon c \ell$ relates absorbance to concentration.

### Aromaticity

An aromatic compound is **cyclic, planar, fully conjugated** (every ring atom has a $p$ orbital), and has $4n+2$ $\pi$ electrons ($n = 0, 1, 2, \dots$), **Hückel's rule**. Benzene has 6. Aromatic compounds have special stability. A cyclic, planar, conjugated ring with $4n$ $\pi$ electrons (such as cyclobutadiene, 4) is **antiaromatic** and unusually unstable. Cyclooctatetraene (8) avoids this by puckering into a tub shape, so it is nonaromatic.

Common aromatic ions and heterocycles:

- **Cyclopentadienyl anion** (6 $\pi$ electrons) is aromatic, which is why cyclopentadiene is unusually acidic (pK$_a \approx 16$) compared with an ordinary alkane. The cation (4 electrons) is antiaromatic.
- **Pyridine**: the nitrogen lone pair lies in the ring plane, not in the $\pi$ system, so the ring has 6 $\pi$ electrons and the lone pair is available for protonation (weak base).
- **Pyrrole**: the nitrogen lone pair is part of the $\pi$ system (4 from two C=C plus 2 from N = 6), so pyrrole is aromatic and a very weak base. Furan and thiophene are similar.
- **Naphthalene** (10 $\pi$ electrons, $n = 2$) and the purines and pyrimidines of DNA are aromatic.

### NMR of aromatic compounds

The circulating $\pi$ electrons produce a **ring current** that deshields protons on the outside of the ring, giving signals at 6.5 to 8.0 ppm. A monosubstituted benzene often shows a 5H multiplet, while a para-disubstituted benzene with two different groups shows two 2H doublets. Benzylic protons (on the carbon attached to the ring) appear around 2.3 to 2.7 ppm. In $^{13}$C NMR, aromatic carbons appear at 120 to 150 ppm.
@example
title: A ketone from its spectrum
problem: A compound C$_9$H$_{10}$O shows $\delta$ 1.22 (3H, triplet), $\delta$ 2.98 (2H, quartet), and $\delta$ 7.4 to 7.95 (5H, multiplet). Propose a structure.
step: DoU $= (20 - 10)/2 = 5$. A benzene ring accounts for 4, leaving one more, probably a C=O.
step: The 5H multiplet at 7.4 to 7.95 ppm is a monosubstituted benzene ring (C$_6$H$_5$-), so only three carbons and five hydrogens remain.
step: The 2H quartet and 3H triplet form an ethyl group. A CH$_2$ at 2.98 ppm is deshielded by a carbonyl (not by oxygen, which would put it above 3.5 ppm).
step: Connect C$_6$H$_5$-C(=O)-CH$_2$CH$_3$: this is propiophenone (1-phenylpropan-1-one), and all C$_9$H$_{10}$O atoms are used.
@mistakes
- Counting a pyridine nitrogen lone pair as part of the $\pi$ system; it lies in the plane of the ring.
- Applying Hückel's rule to a ring that is not planar or not fully conjugated (for example, cyclooctatetraene).
- Moving atoms rather than electrons when drawing resonance structures.
- Assuming that any compound with $4n$ $\pi$ electrons is antiaromatic even if it can escape planarity.
