@article
A hydrogen on the carbon next to a carbonyl (the **alpha hydrogen**) is unusually acidic, with pK$_a$ about 19 to 20 for ketones and aldehydes, versus about 50 for an alkane. Removing it gives an **enolate**, whose negative charge is delocalized between the alpha carbon and oxygen. Enolates are the key nucleophiles in carbonyl chemistry, and every step in biosynthesis that builds a carbon chain (fatty acids, polyketides, sugars) is an enolate reaction in disguise.

### Enols and enolates

Carbonyl compounds are in equilibrium with their **enol** tautomers (C=C-OH). For simple ketones the keto form is greatly favored (the enol fraction of acetone is about $10^{-8}$), but the enol is a nucleophile at carbon, and acid-catalyzed alpha-halogenation (Br$_2$, H$^+$) proceeds through it.

| Compound type | Approximate pK$_a$ of alpha H |
|---|---|
| Aldehyde or ketone | 17 to 20 |
| Ester | 25 |
| Amide | 30 |
| 1,3-Diketone or 1,3-ketoester | 9 to 13 |
| Diethyl malonate | 13 |

A base such as ethoxide (conjugate acid pKa about 16) forms only a small equilibrium amount of the enolate of a simple ketone, $K = 10^{(\text{pK}_a^{\text{EtOH}} - \text{pK}_a^{\text{ketone}})}$. The strong, bulky base **LDA** (diisopropylamide, conjugate acid pK$_a$ about 36) forms the enolate completely and rapidly at $-78^{\circ}$C, which allows controlled alkylation with alkyl halides by S$_N$2.

### Aldol reaction

In the **aldol addition** an enolate adds to the carbonyl group of a second molecule (nucleophilic addition), giving a **$\beta$-hydroxy aldehyde or ketone**. Two molecules of acetaldehyde with base give 3-hydroxybutanal. Heating, especially with base or acid, causes **dehydration** (E1cB, via the enolate) to an **$\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated carbonyl**, which is conjugated and therefore favored (for acetaldehyde: but-2-enal). The overall transformation is the **aldol condensation**. Crossed aldol reactions work best when one partner has no alpha hydrogens (benzaldehyde, formaldehyde). Biologically, the retro-aldol reaction splits fructose 1,6-bisphosphate in glycolysis.

### Claisen condensation

Esters with alpha hydrogens condense with one equivalent of alkoxide base (matching the ester's alkoxy group to avoid transesterification) to give **$\beta$-keto esters**: two molecules of ethyl acetate give ethyl acetoacetate. The reaction needs a full equivalent of base because the product's alpha H (pK$_a$ about 11) is deprotonated, which pulls the equilibrium forward. Fatty acid synthesis uses the same logic with malonyl-CoA.

### Alkylation and decarboxylation

**Malonic ester synthesis:** diethyl malonate is deprotonated, alkylated (S$_N$2), hydrolyzed and heated: the resulting $\beta$-diacid loses CO$_2$ to give a substituted acetic acid RCH$_2$CO$_2$H. **Acetoacetic ester synthesis** gives methyl ketones in the same way. Any $\beta$-keto acid or malonic acid decarboxylates on gentle heating through a cyclic six-membered transition state that yields an enol.

### Michael addition

Stabilized enolates (from malonate or 1,3-diketones) add in a conjugate (1,4) manner to $\alpha,\beta$-unsaturated carbonyls. Combined with an intramolecular aldol this is the **Robinson annulation**, which builds six-membered rings in steroid synthesis.
@example
title: Aldol condensation of acetaldehyde
problem: Show how acetaldehyde, CH$_3$CHO, is converted by NaOH into but-2-enal on heating.
step: Hydroxide removes an alpha hydrogen of one acetaldehyde molecule, giving the enolate $^-$CH$_2$CHO $\leftrightarrow$ CH$_2$=CH-O$^-$.
step: The enolate carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second acetaldehyde, forming a C-C bond and an alkoxide, which is protonated by water to give 3-hydroxybutanal, CH$_3$CH(OH)CH$_2$CHO.
step: Heating with base removes the new alpha hydrogen to form another enolate, which expels hydroxide (E1cB).
step: The product is CH$_3$CH=CHCHO (but-2-enal), a conjugated enal. Its formation drives the equilibrium forward.
@mistakes
- Writing the aldol bond between two carbonyl carbons; the new bond is from the alpha carbon of one molecule to the carbonyl carbon of the other.
- Using NaOH in a Claisen condensation, which hydrolyzes the ester; use the matching alkoxide.
- Forgetting that decarboxylation requires a carbonyl beta to the carboxylic acid.
- Thinking that any alpha-hydrogen-containing ketone is deprotonated completely by ethoxide; the equilibrium amount is small.

@practice self
prompt: Explain, with a mechanism in words, how benzaldehyde and acetone with NaOH give benzalacetone (4-phenylbut-3-en-2-one) and why benzaldehyde does not self-condense.
solution: Hydroxide removes an alpha hydrogen from acetone to give an enolate. Its carbon attacks the carbonyl carbon of benzaldehyde, giving a $\beta$-hydroxy ketone after protonation. Loss of water by E1cB, driven by conjugation with both the carbonyl and the benzene ring, gives PhCH=CHCOCH$_3$. Benzaldehyde has no alpha hydrogens, so it cannot form an enolate and can act only as the electrophile, so the crossed reaction dominates.
