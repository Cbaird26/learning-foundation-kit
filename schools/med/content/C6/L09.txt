@article
**Carbohydrates** are polyhydroxy aldehydes and ketones (and compounds that give them on hydrolysis), with general formula near C$_n$(H$_2$O)$_n$. A simple sugar is a **monosaccharide**; **aldoses** have an aldehyde at C1 and **ketoses** have a ketone (usually at C2). They are named by chain length: triose, tetrose, pentose, hexose. Glucose is an aldohexose; fructose is a ketohexose.

### Fischer projections and D/L

In a Fischer projection the carbon chain is vertical, with the most oxidized carbon (C1) at the top; horizontal bonds point toward the viewer and vertical bonds point away. A sugar is **D** if the OH on the highest-numbered stereocenter (the one farthest from C1) is on the right, and **L** if on the left. Nearly all natural sugars are D. D-Glucose has four stereocenters (C2 to C5), arranged right, left, right, right (OH on C2, C3, C4, C5). An aldohexose therefore has $2^4 = 16$ stereoisomers (8 D, 8 L). Diastereomers that differ at exactly one stereocenter are **epimers**: D-mannose is the C2 epimer of D-glucose, and D-galactose is the C4 epimer.

### Ring forms

In solution, glucose exists almost entirely as cyclic **hemiacetals**: the C5 hydroxyl attacks the C1 aldehyde carbon, forming a six-membered **pyranose** ring (a five-membered ring is a **furanose**, common for fructose and ribose). The new stereocenter at C1 is the **anomeric carbon**, giving two **anomers**:

- **$\alpha$-D-glucopyranose**: the C1 OH is on the opposite face from the CH$_2$OH at C5 (down in the Haworth projection; axial in the chair).
- **$\beta$-D-glucopyranose**: the C1 OH is on the same face as the CH$_2$OH (up in the Haworth; equatorial).

To go from Fischer to Haworth: groups on the right in the Fischer projection point **down**, those on the left point **up**, and the CH$_2$OH of a D-sugar points up. In $\beta$-D-glucopyranose every substituent can be equatorial in the chair, which makes it the most stable anomer (about 64% at equilibrium).

**Mutarotation.** A pure anomer dissolved in water slowly changes its optical rotation as the ring opens to the aldehyde and recloses. The specific rotation of pure $\alpha$-D-glucose is $+112^{\circ}$, pure $\beta$ is $+18.7^{\circ}$, and the equilibrium mixture is $+52.7^{\circ}$. For a two-component mixture, $[\alpha]_{\text{mix}} = x_\alpha[\alpha]_\alpha + x_\beta[\alpha]_\beta$.

### Reactions

- **Reducing sugars** have a free hemiacetal (or can open to an aldehyde, or tautomerize through an enediol), so they react with Tollens' or Benedict's reagent. Glucose, fructose, maltose and lactose are reducing.
- **Glycosides:** the anomeric OH reacts with an alcohol (acid catalyst) to give an acetal, a glycoside, which is stable to base and does not mutarotate. This glycosidic bond links sugars and bonds sugars to nucleobases (N-glycosides) and drug aglycones.
- **Reduction** of the aldehyde (NaBH$_4$) gives an alditol (glucose gives sorbitol); **oxidation** with Br$_2$ water gives an aldonic acid, and with nitric acid, an aldaric acid.
- Hydroxyl groups form esters and ethers like any alcohol.

### Disaccharides and polysaccharides

- **Maltose**: two glucoses joined $\alpha$(1$\to$4); reducing.
- **Lactose**: galactose $\beta$(1$\to$4) glucose; reducing.
- **Sucrose**: glucose $\alpha$(1$\to$2)$\beta$ fructose, joined through both anomeric carbons, so it is **non-reducing**.
- **Starch (amylose and amylopectin) and glycogen**: glucose polymers with $\alpha$(1$\to$4) links (branches $\alpha$(1$\to$6)); **cellulose**: $\beta$(1$\to$4), which humans cannot digest.
@example
title: From Fischer projection to ring
problem: Draw $\beta$-D-glucopyranose starting from the Fischer projection of open-chain D-glucose.
step: Fischer projection of D-glucose (top to bottom): C1 CHO; C2 OH right; C3 OH left; C4 OH right; C5 OH right; C6 CH$_2$OH. The OH on C5 is on the right, so the sugar is D.
step: Number the ring clockwise in the Haworth projection with ring oxygen at the back right and C1 at the right. Right-hand substituents go down and left-hand ones go up: C2 OH down, C3 OH up, C4 OH down. The CH$_2$OH on C5 points up (D-sugar).
step: Closing the ring by attack of the C5 hydroxyl oxygen on C1 creates the anomeric OH. For the $\beta$ anomer, place it up, on the same side as the CH$_2$OH.
step: In the chair, all substituents (C1 OH, C2 OH, C3 OH, C4 OH and CH$_2$OH) are equatorial, so $\beta$-D-glucopyranose is the most stable form.
@mistakes
- Assigning D or L from the OH on C2; the label uses the highest-numbered stereocenter (C5 for hexoses).
- Confusing anomers with epimers; anomers differ at C1 of the ring form only.
- Calling sucrose a reducing sugar; its anomeric carbons are both tied up in the glycosidic bond.
- Putting right-hand OH groups up in the Haworth projection; right means down.
