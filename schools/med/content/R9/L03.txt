@article
### Upper GI bleeding and varices

Hematemesis or melena: resuscitate first (two large-bore IVs, crystalloid, blood if hemoglobin is below about 7 g/dL, or higher with active cardiovascular disease), start an IV **proton pump inhibitor**, and perform **endoscopy within 24 hours**. If cirrhosis is present, suspect varices: add **octreotide** (splanchnic vasoconstriction) and **ceftriaxone** prophylaxis, and perform endoscopy within 12 hours with band ligation. Avoid over-transfusion, which raises portal pressure. Rebleeding despite therapy leads to TIPS. Long-term prophylaxis uses nonselective beta-blockers (propranolol, nadolol, carvedilol) plus band ligation.

### Cirrhosis complications

- **Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP):** fever, abdominal pain or unexplained decline in a patient with ascites. Diagnostic paracentesis shows ascitic PMN of 250/mm3 or more. Treat with ceftriaxone (or cefotaxime) and IV albumin to lower the risk of hepatorenal syndrome.
- **Hepatic encephalopathy:** find the trigger (infection, bleeding, constipation, sedatives, hypokalemia); treat with lactulose, adding rifaximin to prevent recurrence.
- **Hepatorenal syndrome:** AKI in cirrhosis unresponsive to volume; stop diuretics, give albumin, then vasoconstrictors such as terlipressin or norepinephrine.

### Biliary disease and pancreatitis

**Cholangitis** (Charcot triad: fever, jaundice, right upper quadrant pain; Reynolds adds hypotension and confusion) is treated with antibiotics plus biliary drainage (ERCP). **Acute pancreatitis** needs two of three: typical pain, lipase above three times normal, characteristic imaging. Gallstones and alcohol are the common causes; give isotonic fluid (lactated Ringer's is reasonable), analgesia and early oral feeding. ERCP is for cholangitis or persistent obstruction, not for gallstone pancreatitis alone. Cholecystectomy follows during the same admission for mild gallstone pancreatitis.

### Acute kidney injury and electrolytes

Prerenal: BUN/Cr above 20, urine sodium low, $FE_{Na}<1\%$ (use $FE_{urea}<35\%$ if on diuretics). Acute tubular necrosis: $FE_{Na}>2\%$, muddy-brown casts. Postrenal: obstruction on ultrasonography or a bladder scan.

$$FE_{Na}=\frac{U_{Na}\times P_{Cr}}{P_{Na}\times U_{Cr}}\times 100.$$

**Hyperkalemia** with ECG changes (peaked T waves, wide QRS) is treated in this order: **IV calcium** (membrane stabilization, onset within minutes), then shift (regular insulin with glucose, albuterol), then removal (loop diuretic, potassium binder, dialysis). Anion gap $=\text{Na}-(\text{Cl}+\text{HCO}_3)$, normal about 8 to 12 mEq/L; modern mnemonics for a high gap include ketoacidosis, uremia, lactate, toxic alcohols, salicylates and 5-oxoproline. Corrected calcium $=\text{Ca}+0.8\times(4.0-\text{albumin})$.

### Endocrine emergencies

**DKA:** fluids, then insulin infusion once potassium is at least 3.5 mEq/L (replace potassium first if below that), potassium replacement to keep 4 to 5 mEq/L, dextrose added as glucose falls, and a search for the trigger (infection, missed insulin, myocardial infarction, SGLT2 inhibitor). The 2024 international hyperglycemic-crisis consensus defines DKA as glucose 200 mg/dL or more (or known diabetes), beta-hydroxybutyrate 3.0 mmol/L or more, and pH below 7.3 and/or bicarbonate below 18 mEq/L; it emphasizes ketone measurement, recognizes DKA at lower glucose levels including euglycemic cases, and defines resolution by ketones and acidosis rather than glucose alone. **HHS** shows extreme hyperglycemia and hyperosmolality with little ketosis and requires larger volume repletion. **Adrenal crisis** (hypotension, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia after steroid withdrawal or infection) is treated immediately with IV hydrocortisone and saline; do not wait for stimulation testing. **Thyroid storm**: a thionamide, then iodine at least an hour later, beta-blocker, steroids. **Severe hypercalcemia**: isotonic saline, calcitonin for rapid effect, then a bisphosphonate.

@example
title: Anion gap and the cause of the acidosis
problem: A patient has Na 138 mEq/L, Cl 100 mEq/L and HCO3 12 mEq/L. What is the anion gap, and what does it imply?
step: Anion gap $=138-(100+12)$.
step: $100+12=112$, so the gap is $138-112=26$ mEq/L.
step: A gap well above 12 means unmeasured anions: ketoacids, lactate, uremia, or toxic alcohols or salicylates. A normal-gap acidosis would instead suggest diarrhea or a renal tubular acidosis.

@example
title: Correcting calcium for albumin
problem: A malnourished patient has total calcium 7.2 mg/dL and albumin 2.0 g/dL. What is the corrected calcium?
step: Corrected Ca $=7.2+0.8\times(4.0-2.0)$.
step: $0.8\times2.0=1.6$, so corrected Ca $=7.2+1.6=8.8$ mg/dL.
step: The corrected value is within the normal range (about 8.5 to 10.5 mg/dL), so the low total value is explained by hypoalbuminemia and does not need calcium replacement.

@mistakes
- Starting insulin in DKA before checking potassium; a potassium below 3.5 mEq/L needs repletion first because insulin drives potassium into cells.
- Choosing insulin and glucose over calcium for hyperkalemia with ECG changes; calcium acts first by stabilizing the myocardium.
- Performing a diagnostic paracentesis too late, or treating suspected SBP with antibiotics without sampling ascitic fluid.
- Large-volume saline resuscitation in a cirrhotic bleeding patient or giving beta-blockers during acute hypotensive bleeding.

answer: 0.18
check: (10 * 2.0) / (140 * 80) * 100
tol: 0.02
unit: %

solution: $(10\times2.0)/(140\times80)\times100=20/11200\times100=0.18\%$. A value below 1% indicates prerenal azotemia.

@practice self
prompt: A 22-year-old man with type 1 diabetes has 2 days of vomiting and abdominal pain. RR is 30/min with deep breathing, glucose is 520 mg/dL, pH is 7.08, bicarbonate is 7 mEq/L, potassium is 3.2 mEq/L and beta-hydroxybutyrate is elevated. Write your management plan in order and state what you would do about insulin and why.
hint: The potassium is low. What happens to serum potassium when insulin is started?
hint: List fluids, potassium, insulin, glucose monitoring and the search for a trigger.
solution: This is diabetic ketoacidosis. Start isotonic IV fluids, obtain access and monitor. Because potassium is below 3.5 mEq/L, hold insulin and replace potassium first; starting insulin would drive potassium into cells and risk arrhythmia and respiratory muscle weakness. Once potassium is at least 3.5 mEq/L, start a continuous regular insulin infusion (typical adult dose about 0.1 unit/kg/h, checked against a current protocol), continue potassium to maintain 4 to 5 mEq/L, add dextrose when glucose falls to roughly 200 to 250 mg/dL so insulin can continue until ketosis resolves, check electrolytes and ketones frequently, and look for the precipitant (infection, missed insulin, ischemia, or an SGLT2 inhibitor). Resolution is defined by ketone and acid-base criteria, not by glucose alone, as emphasized in the 2024 consensus.

prompt: A patient has a total calcium of 8.0 mg/dL and an albumin of 2.5 g/dL. What is the corrected calcium in mg/dL?
answer: 9.2
check: 8.0 + 0.8 * (4.0 - 2.5)
tol: 0.05
unit: mg/dL
hint: Use the correction of $0.8$ mg/dL for each $1$ g/dL that albumin is below $4.0$.
