### Levels of prevention

**Primary** prevention stops disease before it starts (vaccination, statins for risk reduction, smoking cessation counseling). **Secondary** prevention detects disease early (mammography, colonoscopy). **Tertiary** prevention limits complications of established disease (rehabilitation, glycemic control). **Quaternary** prevention avoids unnecessary medicalization.

### Screening: the main recommendations

These reflect U.S. Preventive Services Task Force positions in 2025; guidelines differ among organizations and change, so check current sources.

- **Colorectal cancer:** ages 45 to 75, using colonoscopy every 10 years, annual FIT, or stool DNA testing at set intervals; earlier or more frequent for family history or inflammatory bowel disease.
- **Breast cancer:** biennial mammography for ages 40 to 74 (the 2024 update lowered the start age to 40).
- **Cervical cancer:** ages 21 to 65 as described in the obstetrics lesson.
- **Lung cancer:** annual low-dose CT for ages 50 to 80 with a smoking history of at least 20 pack-years who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years.
- **Abdominal aortic aneurysm:** one-time ultrasonography for men 65 to 75 who ever smoked.
- **Hepatitis C:** once for all adults 18 to 79; **HIV:** at least once for ages 15 to 65; **hypertension:** screen all adults 18 and older.
- **Osteoporosis:** bone density testing in women 65 and older, and younger postmenopausal women at increased risk.
- **Statins:** moderate-intensity statin for adults 40 to 75 with at least one cardiovascular risk factor and a 10-year risk of at least 10%. This is the USPSTF 2022 threshold (the 2018 ACC/AHA guideline used pooled cohort equations with 7.5% and 20% cutoffs). The March 2026 ACC/AHA multisociety dyslipidemia guideline instead uses the PREVENT-ASCVD equations with lower risk categories (about 3 to under 5% borderline, 5 to under 10% intermediate, 10% or more high), LDL-C goals below 100, 70 or 55 mg/dL by risk, and one-time lipoprotein(a) testing; confirm current wording, but the principle of risk-based statin therapy is unchanged.

### Adult immunizations

Annual influenza vaccine for all adults. Tdap once as an adult (and in each pregnancy at 27 to 36 weeks), then Td or Tdap every 10 years. Recombinant zoster vaccine (two doses) at age 50 and older. Pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV20 or PCV21) for adults 50 and older, and for younger adults with risk conditions. Hepatitis B for all adults 19 to 59 and older adults with risk factors. HPV vaccination through age 26. RSV vaccine (ACIP, 2025) for all adults 75 and older and for adults 50 to 74 at increased risk; confirm the current schedule. **Live vaccines** (MMR, varicella) are contraindicated in pregnancy and severe immunocompromise; egg allergy is no longer a contraindication to influenza vaccine, and mild illness is not a reason to delay.

### Capacity, consent and confidentiality

A patient has **decision-making capacity** if they can communicate a choice, understand the relevant information, appreciate its application to themselves, and reason about options. Capacity is decision-specific and is assessed by any physician; disagreement with advice or an unusual choice does not by itself show incapacity. A competent adult may refuse any treatment, including life-saving transfusion. **Informed consent** requires discussion of the diagnosis, the proposed intervention, alternatives and risks, with an opportunity for questions. Exceptions: emergencies when the patient cannot consent, incapacity (use a surrogate), voluntary waiver, and rarely therapeutic privilege.

When a patient lacks capacity, follow the **advance directive** or documented wishes; otherwise use the designated health care proxy, then the legal surrogate hierarchy (spouse, adult children, parents, siblings), using **substituted judgment** (what the patient would want) and then best interests. **Minors** may consent to contraception, treatment of sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy care and substance use treatment in most states and receive confidential care; emergencies are treated without parental consent. **Confidentiality** is breached only for legally required situations: reportable infectious diseases, suspected child or elder abuse, certain injuries, and a credible serious threat to an identifiable person. **Error disclosure:** tell the patient promptly and honestly about an error that reached them, apologize, explain what is known, describe corrective steps, and report through the safety system.

title: Pack-years and lung cancer screening
problem: A 58-year-old man smoked 1.5 packs per day for 30 years and quit 8 years ago. Does he qualify for annual low-dose CT screening?
step: Pack-years $=1.5\times30=45$.
step: He is within ages 50 to 80, has at least 20 pack-years, and quit within the past 15 years.
step: He qualifies for annual low-dose CT until he has quit for 15 years or becomes unable or unwilling to undergo curative surgery.

@example
title: Refusal of transfusion
problem: A 40-year-old Jehovah's Witness with a hemoglobin of 5.8 g/dL after postpartum hemorrhage is alert and clearly refuses blood products. What do you do?
step: She is awake and can communicate; assess capacity, which requires understanding, appreciation, reasoning and a stable choice.
step: If she has capacity, a competent adult may refuse blood even when death is possible; respect the decision and document the discussion.
step: Offer all acceptable alternatives (cell salvage, erythropoietin, iron, tranexamic acid, surgical hemostasis) and involve the ethics service if there is uncertainty.

@mistakes
- Treating an unusual or risky decision as evidence of incapacity, or obtaining a psychiatric consult without first assessing the four capacity elements.
- Seeking parental consent or notification when a minor requests confidential contraception or STI care.
- Counting pack-years incorrectly (packs per day times years of smoking) or applying the screening cutoffs after the patient has quit for more than 15 years.
- Breaching confidentiality for a spouse's curiosity or an employer, which is not legally required.

solution: "Mr. Lee, I need to tell you about something that happened. You received a much larger dose of insulin than intended because of an error in how the order was entered. We will check your blood sugar closely for the next day, and we will keep you informed. I will report the error through our safety system so that we can find out how it happened and prevent it from happening to others. Please ask me any questions, and I will update you as we learn more." Afterward: document the event factually in the chart, file an incident report, involve risk management and the care team, and support a root-cause analysis.
