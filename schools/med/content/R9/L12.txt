@article
### What the exam asks

Step 2 CK tests the application of medical knowledge to patient care across all systems and the physician tasks of diagnosis, management, health maintenance and professionalism. Questions are single-best-answer vignettes. The exam is delivered in blocks of up to 40 questions with 60 minutes allotted per block, and the outcome is reported as a three-digit score with a pass/fail determination; confirm the current structure and rules in the official content outline before your test. Treat every block as a mixed block, because the real exam does not announce the specialty.

### Pacing

At 40 questions in 60 minutes you have 90 seconds per question on average. Do not spend 3 minutes on one question and then rush ten others. Check pace at fixed points (for example, at questions 10, 20 and 30): the time remaining divided by the questions remaining should stay near 90 seconds. If you are behind, answer faster on the easy questions rather than skipping the reading. Mark and move on when you have not narrowed to two choices by about two minutes; come back with leftover time. Answer every question, because there is no penalty for wrong answers.

### Decision under uncertainty

If you can eliminate wrong choices with reasons, guessing is no longer random. With five choices, the chance of a correct guess is $1/5$ at baseline, $1/4$ after eliminating one, $1/3$ after eliminating two, and $1/2$ after eliminating three. Change an answer only when you find a specific mistake in your reading or reasoning, not because of anxiety. Common traps: choosing a drug that is correct for a related diagnosis, answering the question you expected rather than the one asked, missing a "most likely" versus "initial" qualifier, and overlooking age, pregnancy, allergy or renal function in the stem.

### The error log

After each block, review every question, including correct ones you guessed. For each, record the topic, the question type, why you missed it, and the corrected principle in one sentence. Classify the cause:

- **Knowledge gap:** you did not know the fact (repair with focused review and spaced recall).
- **Reasoning error:** you knew the facts but chose the wrong step (repair by practicing the six-step method).
- **Reading error:** you missed a qualifier, unit or the last line (repair with slower first reads and underlining mental flags).
- **Test-taking error:** timing, anxiety, changing a correct answer (repair with pacing checkpoints).

Count errors by category and by specialty. If one specialty accounts for much of your error volume, schedule a study session on that area first. Rework missed concepts after 1 to 2 days and again after a week.

### Planning the final weeks and test day

Take full-length timed practice sets, alternating them with review days. In the last week, taper: review the error log, high-yield tables and calculations, and sleep on a regular schedule. On the day: eat, arrive early, take scheduled breaks, and use the time between blocks to reset rather than reviewing the last block. If you hit a run of hard questions, remember that blocks are not graded individually and the exam deliberately includes difficult items.

title: A mixed vignette, solved with the method
problem: A 62-year-old man on postoperative day 2 after hip fracture repair has sudden dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain. HR is 118/min, BP is 124/78 mm Hg, SpO2 is 89% on room air, and the lungs are clear. Creatinine is normal. What is the best next step?
step: The last line asks for the next step; he is hypoxic but hemodynamically stable.
step: Surgery and immobility with sudden hypoxia and clear lungs indicate pulmonary embolism with high pretest probability.
step: Give oxygen and start empiric anticoagulation if imaging will be delayed, and obtain CT pulmonary angiography; a D-dimer is inappropriate at high probability.

@mistakes
- Spending too long on one hard question and then rushing the rest of the block.
- Changing a correct answer without a specific reason.
- Logging only the questions you got wrong and ignoring lucky guesses.
- Studying only your favorite specialty instead of the categories where errors cluster.

hint: Calculate the percentage in each category first.
solution: Categories: knowledge gaps 6/12 (50%), reading errors 3/12 (25%), reasoning errors 2/12 (17%), test-taking error 1/12 (8%). The dominant problem is knowledge, so list the six topics and review them with active recall (write the principle in one sentence, then complete 10 to 15 related questions) within 1 to 2 days and again at a week. For reading errors, circle the last line first and underline qualifiers such as initial, most likely and next best step in the first read. For reasoning errors, write the six-step method on each missed question and note the step where it went wrong. For the changed answer, only change when you can state a specific mistake. Repeat a mixed timed block at the end of the week and compare the category counts.

@quiz mc
prompt: A 25-year-old man is brought in after an intentional ingestion of an unknown pill. He has tinnitus, tachypnea, vomiting and a temperature of 38.5 C. ABG shows pH 7.35, PaCO2 22 mm Hg, HCO3 12 mEq/L, and the anion gap is elevated. What is the best next step in management?
choice: Intravenous flumazenil
choice: Observation with no treatment until a level returns
choice*: Intravenous sodium bicarbonate
choice: Intubation and hyperventilation to correct the respiratory alkalosis
hint: Tinnitus plus mixed respiratory alkalosis and anion gap acidosis suggests a particular drug.
solution: Salicylate toxicity causes tinnitus, tachypnea and a mixed acid-base disorder. Sodium bicarbonate alkalinizes the serum and urine to trap salicylate in the urine and reduce central nervous system penetration; hemodialysis is used for severe toxicity. Intubation can worsen acidosis by reducing minute ventilation.
