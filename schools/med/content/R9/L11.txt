@article
### The 2x2 table

For a diagnostic test against a reference standard:

| | Disease present | Disease absent |
|---|---|---|
| Test positive | TP | FP |
| Test negative | FN | TN |

$$\text{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad \text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\quad PPV=\frac{TP}{TP+FP},\quad NPV=\frac{TN}{TN+FN}.$$

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test and do not depend on prevalence. **Predictive values** depend strongly on prevalence: as prevalence falls, PPV falls and NPV rises. Likelihood ratios are $LR^+=\frac{\text{sens}}{1-\text{spec}}$ and $LR^-=\frac{1-\text{sens}}{\text{spec}}$. Using Bayes theorem with prevalence $\pi$:

$$PPV=\frac{\text{sens}\cdot\pi}{\text{sens}\cdot\pi+(1-\text{spec})(1-\pi)}.$$

Changing the cutoff of a test moves along an ROC curve: lowering the threshold raises sensitivity and lowers specificity. The area under the curve summarizes discrimination.

### Effect measures

In a trial with event rates $CER$ (control) and $EER$ (experimental): relative risk $RR=EER/CER$; relative risk reduction $RRR=1-RR$; absolute risk reduction $ARR=CER-EER$; **number needed to treat** $NNT=1/ARR$ (round up); number needed to harm $=1/ARI$. The **odds ratio** $=\frac{ad}{bc}$ from a case-control table approximates the relative risk when the disease is rare. The **hazard ratio** compares instantaneous event rates in survival analysis.

### Study designs

| Design | Direction | Measure | Typical weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| Randomized controlled trial | Assign exposure, follow | RR, ARR, NNT | Cost, generalizability |
| Cohort | Exposure then outcome | RR, incidence | Loss to follow-up, confounding |
| Case-control | Outcome then exposure | Odds ratio | Recall bias, selection of controls |
| Cross-sectional | One time point | Prevalence | No temporal sequence |
| Case series / report | Descriptive | None | No comparison group |

### Bias, confounding and inference

**Selection bias** (nonrepresentative groups, differential loss to follow-up), **recall bias** (cases remember exposure differently), **lead-time bias** (screening finds disease earlier so survival appears longer without postponing death), **length-time bias** (screening preferentially finds slow-growing disease), **observer/measurement bias**, and **confounding** (a third variable associated with both exposure and outcome; handled by randomization, restriction, matching or adjustment). A p-value below the chosen alpha (usually 0.05) means the observed difference would be unlikely if the null hypothesis were true; it is not the probability that the hypothesis is true. **Type I error** ($\alpha$) rejects a true null; **type II error** ($\beta$) misses a real difference; **power** is $1-\beta$. A 95% confidence interval for a ratio that includes 1 is not statistically significant. In a normal distribution about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations of the mean.

### Patient safety

Most harm comes from system failures, not single bad actors. The **Swiss cheese model** describes harm when holes in several defensive layers line up. A **root cause analysis** investigates a sentinel event (unexpected death or serious harm) to find system causes; **failure mode and effects analysis** looks for weaknesses proactively. Strong fixes are forcing functions and standardization (barcode scanning, checklists, removing look-alike products); weak fixes are education and warnings alone. A **just culture** distinguishes human error (console), at-risk behavior (coach) and reckless behavior (discipline). Use structured handoffs (I-PASS), a surgical **time-out** with site marking, and reporting of near misses. Disclosure of errors follows the principles in the previous lesson.

@example
title: Likelihood ratio from a 2x2 table
problem: A test is evaluated in 1,000 people: TP = 80, FP = 40, FN = 20, TN = 860. Calculate the sensitivity, specificity and positive likelihood ratio.
step: Sensitivity $=80/(80+20)=0.80$.
step: Specificity $=860/(860+40)=860/900\approx0.956$, so $1-\text{specificity}=40/900\approx0.0444$.
step: $LR^+=0.80/0.0444=18$, a result that moves probability substantially.

@example
title: Number needed to treat
problem: In a trial, 12% of control patients and 8% of treated patients have a stroke over 2 years. Find the relative risk, absolute risk reduction and NNT.
step: $RR=0.08/0.12\approx0.67$.
step: $ARR=0.12-0.08=0.04$.
step: $NNT=1/0.04=25$: treat 25 patients for 2 years to prevent one stroke.

@mistakes
- Quoting PPV as a fixed property of a test; it changes with prevalence.
- Computing NNT from relative risk reduction, or forgetting to take the reciprocal of the absolute (not relative) risk reduction.
- Interpreting a nonsignificant p-value as proof of no effect, when the study may be underpowered.
- Blaming the individual in a sentinel event instead of examining system failures.

prompt: A screening test correctly identifies 45 of 60 people with a disease. What is its sensitivity as a decimal?
answer: 0.75
check: 45 / 60
hint: Sensitivity is true positives divided by everyone with the disease.
solution: Among 60 diseased patients, 45 are true positives (15 false negatives): $45/60=0.75$.

@practice num
prompt: A test has sensitivity 90% and specificity 95%. The disease prevalence is 2%. What is the positive predictive value, as a decimal rounded to three places?
answer: 0.269
check: (0.9*0.02) / (0.9*0.02 + (1-0.95)*(1-0.02))
tol: 0.005
hint: In 1,000 people, how many have the disease and how many test positive among those with and without it?
hint: 20 have disease and 18 test positive; of the 980 without it, 5% (49) test positive.
solution: True positives $=0.9\times20=18$. False positives $=0.05\times980=49$. $PPV=18/(18+49)=0.269$. The low prevalence makes most positives false.

@practice mc
prompt: In a case-control study of birth defects, mothers of affected infants report more medication use during pregnancy than mothers of healthy infants, partly because they search their memories more carefully. Which bias is most likely?
choice: Lead-time bias
choice: Length-time bias
choice*: Recall bias
choice: Berkson bias in a randomized trial
hint: The difference lies in how exposure is remembered.
solution: Cases tend to recall exposures more completely than controls, which inflates the apparent association. Lead-time and length-time bias apply to screening studies.

@practice self
prompt: A surgeon operates on the left knee of a patient whose consent form and diagram show the right knee. Using the Swiss cheese model, describe how this could happen, and list three system changes that would be stronger than a reminder to be more careful.
hint: Think of the layers of defense before incision.
solution: The harm results from several defenses failing at once: unclear scheduling documentation, no site marking by the surgeon, inconsistent consent review, a rushed or perfunctory time-out in which the team does not stop and verify against the consent, and a hierarchy that discourages staff from speaking up. Stronger fixes: (1) standardize site marking by the operating surgeon with the patient awake and participating, (2) require a hard-stop time-out in which all team members verify patient, procedure and site against the consent and imaging before incision, with authority for any member to stop the procedure, and (3) build verification into the electronic scheduling and consent record so that discrepancies trigger an alert. Hold a root cause analysis rather than blaming one person, and disclose to the patient.
rubric: Explains the event as multiple aligned failures, not a single individual's mistake.
rubric: Names at least three concrete system-level interventions (site marking, hard-stop time-out, standardized verification).
rubric: Distinguishes strong from weak fixes.
rubric: Mentions root cause analysis and disclosure.

@quiz num
prompt: In a trial, 20% of control patients and 15% of treated patients die within a year. What is the number needed to treat to prevent one death?
answer: 20
check: 1 / (0.20 - 0.15)
hint: Compute the absolute risk reduction first.
solution: $ARR=0.20-0.15=0.05$, so $NNT=1/0.05=20$.

@quiz mc
prompt: A new screening test detects a cancer 3 years earlier than clinical symptoms would. Five-year survival after diagnosis rises from 50% to 80%, but age at death does not change. Which bias explains the apparent benefit?
choice: Recall bias
choice: Selection bias
choice*: Lead-time bias
choice: Observer bias
hint: Survival is measured from diagnosis, which has been moved earlier.
solution: Earlier detection lengthens the interval from diagnosis to death without changing the time of death. Better evidence uses disease-specific mortality in a randomized trial rather than survival from diagnosis.
