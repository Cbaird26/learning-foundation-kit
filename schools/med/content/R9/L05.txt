### The primary survey

Trauma is managed with a fixed sequence, finding and treating the most rapidly lethal problem first: **A**irway with cervical spine protection, **B**reathing, **C**irculation with hemorrhage control, **D**isability (GCS, pupils, glucose), **E**xposure. Life-threatening findings are treated as they are found.

- **Tension pneumothorax:** needle or finger decompression, then chest tube; it is a clinical diagnosis.
- **Open pneumothorax:** occlusive dressing taped on three sides, then chest tube.
- **Massive hemothorax:** large-bore chest tube plus blood; operative thoracotomy for 1,500 mL initial output or more than about 200 mL/h.
- **Cardiac tamponade:** Beck triad (hypotension, distended neck veins, muffled heart sounds); pericardiocentesis or surgical exploration.
- **Pelvic fracture with shock:** pelvic binder, then angioembolization or preperitoneal packing.

### Hemorrhagic shock

Estimated blood volume is about 70 mL/kg in adults. ATLS classes: **class I** loss up to 15%, **class II** 15 to 30% (tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure), **class III** 31 to 40% (hypotension, confusion), **class IV** above 40% (obtunded, imminent arrest). Resuscitate with blood products (balanced massive transfusion protocol), not large crystalloid volumes; give **tranexamic acid** within 3 hours of injury.

### Abdominal trauma

Use the **FAST** examination. A hemodynamically unstable patient with positive FAST goes to **laparotomy**; a stable blunt-trauma patient goes to **CT**. Penetrating abdominal injury with peritonitis, evisceration or hypotension also goes to laparotomy. Isolated gunshot wounds to the abdomen are generally explored; stable stab wounds without peritonitis may be observed or evaluated with CT. A retroperitoneal injury such as a stable duodenal hematoma is managed with CT and observation.

### Burns

Resuscitate partial- and full-thickness burns of at least about 20% total body surface area (TBSA) with lactated Ringer's using the **Parkland formula**:

$$\text{total volume in 24 h}=4\ \text{mL}\times\text{weight (kg)}\times\%\text{TBSA},$$

with half given in the first 8 hours from the time of the burn and half over the next 16. Titrate to a urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/h in adults rather than following the formula blindly. Circumferential burns need escharotomy; inhalation injury (soot, singed hair, hoarseness) needs early intubation.

### The acute abdomen

- **Appendicitis:** CT (or ultrasonography in children and pregnancy, with MRI if nondiagnostic), antibiotics, appendectomy.
- **Cholecystitis:** right upper quadrant ultrasonography (stones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy sign), antibiotics, early laparoscopic cholecystectomy; HIDA scan if equivocal.
- **Small bowel obstruction:** NPO, nasogastric decompression, fluids; operate for peritonitis, strangulation or a closed loop; most partial adhesive obstructions resolve without surgery.
- **Perforated viscus:** free air under the diaphragm, peritonitis; fluids, antibiotics, laparotomy.
- **Acute mesenteric ischemia:** atrial fibrillation or vasculopathy with pain out of proportion to the examination; **CT angiography**, heparin, revascularization, resection of nonviable bowel.
- **Abdominal aortic aneurysm:** unstable rupture goes straight to repair; elective repair at 5.5 cm or more in men or rapid growth; a one-time screening ultrasound is recommended for men 65 to 75 who ever smoked.
- **Compartment syndrome:** pain on passive stretch, tense compartment; emergent fasciotomy.

### Postoperative complications by timing

Fever in the first 48 hours is usually from the inflammatory response or atelectasis; days 3 to 5 suggest urinary infection, C. difficile, or venous thromboembolism; days 5 to 7 raise wound infection or anastomotic leak; always consider drugs and deep abscess later.

@example
title: Parkland formula for a burn
problem: An 80-kg man has partial- and full-thickness burns over 30% of his body surface area. Calculate the total 24-hour fluid requirement and the infusion rate for the first 8 hours.
step: Total 24-hour volume $=4\times80\times30=9600$ mL of lactated Ringer's.
step: Half is given in the first 8 hours: $9600/2=4800$ mL.
step: Rate for the first 8 hours $=4800/8=600$ mL/h, then titrate to urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/h.

@example
title: Positive FAST in an unstable patient
problem: A 30-year-old driver in a high-speed crash has BP 74/40 mm Hg, HR 138/min and a distended, tender abdomen. The FAST examination shows free fluid in Morison pouch. What is the best next step?
step: The question asks for the next step, and the patient is in class III to IV shock, so he is unstable.
step: Start the massive transfusion protocol and give tranexamic acid; avoid large crystalloid volumes.
step: With unstable blunt abdominal trauma and positive FAST, intra-abdominal hemorrhage is the source. The answer is emergency laparotomy, not CT.

@mistakes
- Obtaining CT in a hypotensive trauma patient with a positive FAST.
- Giving liters of crystalloid to a bleeding patient instead of blood products and early hemorrhage control.
- Using the Parkland formula with the wrong time reference: the first 8 hours run from the time of injury, not from arrival.
- Getting a chest radiograph before decompressing a clinically obvious tension pneumothorax, or ordering a barium study for suspected perforation.

prompt: A 32-year-old man is brought in after a stab wound to the left chest. BP is 72/40 mm Hg, HR is 136/min, breath sounds are absent on the left, percussion is dull, and the neck veins are flat. Write the primary survey in order, naming the likely injury and the interventions.
hint: Flat neck veins with dullness suggest blood, not air.
hint: Walk through A, B, C, D, E and say what you do at each step.
solution: Airway: assess patency, give oxygen, protect the cervical spine. Breathing: absent breath sounds with dullness and flat neck veins indicate a massive left hemothorax, not tension pneumothorax. Place a large-bore left chest tube. Circulation: two large-bore IVs, draw type and cross, start blood products (massive transfusion protocol) rather than large crystalloid volumes, and control external bleeding; give tranexamic acid. If chest-tube output is 1,500 mL or more initially, or more than about 200 mL/h, proceed to thoracotomy. Disability: GCS, pupils and glucose. Exposure: undress completely to search for other wounds while preventing hypothermia, and perform an extended FAST including the pericardium.
