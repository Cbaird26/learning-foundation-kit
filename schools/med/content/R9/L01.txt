@article
Most Step 2 CK questions are not asking "what is the diagnosis?" They ask **what you would do next**. The diagnosis is usually a stepping stone: you must identify it, place the patient in the right stage, and then pick the action that belongs at that moment. A fixed method keeps you from being pulled toward choices that are true but premature, late or aimed at the wrong problem.

### The six-step method

1. **Read the last line first.** Is the stem asking for the most likely diagnosis, the best initial test, the best confirmatory test, the next step in management, the most appropriate pharmacotherapy, or the most likely mechanism? These are different questions with different answers for the same patient.
2. **Triage stability.** Look at vital signs, mental status and airway. An unstable patient gets an intervention (airway, decompression, fluids and blood, defibrillation, cardioversion, the operating room) before imaging or consultation. A stable patient gets the least invasive test that changes management.
3. **Name the syndrome.** Collect the two or three discriminating clues: age, sex, pregnancy status, timing, risk factors, one pathognomonic sign.
4. **Locate the step in the pathway.** Typical order: stabilize, confirm with a safe test, treat, then prevent or follow up.
5. **Predict before you look.** Cover the choices and say the answer. Predicting protects you from distractors that merely sound sophisticated.
6. **Eliminate with reasons.** A wrong choice is usually wrong in one of four ways: wrong patient, wrong time (too early or too late), wrong invasiveness (a procedure where a test would do), or wrong question (a correct treatment for a different diagnosis).

### Stable versus unstable

Two patients can share a diagnosis and still have different answers. A hypotensive patient with a ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm goes to the operating room; a stable patient with an incidental aneurysm gets surveillance. A patient with a tension pneumothorax is decompressed with a needle or finger thoracostomy **before** any chest radiograph. Hemodynamic instability moves a question from "which test" to "which intervention".

### Bayesian reasoning in one page

Every test result updates a **pretest probability**. Convert to odds, multiply by the likelihood ratio (LR), and convert back:

$$\text{pretest odds}=\frac{p}{1-p},\qquad \text{post-test odds}=\text{pretest odds}\times LR,\qquad p_{\text{post}}=\frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}.$$

The positive likelihood ratio is $LR^+=\frac{\text{sensitivity}}{1-\text{specificity}}$ and the negative is $LR^-=\frac{1-\text{sensitivity}}{\text{specificity}}$. Two thresholds organize decisions. Below the **test threshold** the disease is so unlikely that you do not test; between the thresholds you test; above the **treatment threshold** you treat without further testing (as when you give anticoagulation for a high-probability pulmonary embolism while arranging imaging). A highly **sensitive** test with a negative result rules disease out (SnNout) and is the right tool at low pretest probability; a highly **specific** test with a positive result rules disease in (SpPin).

### Reflexes worth memorizing

- Reproductive-age woman with abdominal pain or bleeding: **pregnancy test first**.
- Suspected life-threatening infection: **cultures, then antibiotics without waiting for imaging**.
- Pulmonary embolism: low probability uses a D-dimer; moderate or high probability goes to CT pulmonary angiography.
- When two choices are both correct actions, the answer is the one that comes **earlier** in the pathway or addresses the **most dangerous** possibility.

@example
title: Decompress first, image later
problem: A 24-year-old man is brought in after a motorcycle crash. BP is 78/42 mm Hg, HR 134/min, RR 36/min, SpO2 86% on a non-rebreather mask. The right chest is hyperresonant with absent breath sounds, neck veins are distended and the trachea is deviated to the left. What is the best next step?
step: The last line asks for the next step in management, so the question is about action, not diagnosis.
step: Triage: hypotension, tachycardia and hypoxemia mean the patient is unstable. Interventions come before tests.
step: Syndrome: absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, distended neck veins and tracheal deviation after chest trauma indicate a tension pneumothorax, a clinical diagnosis.
step: The best step is immediate needle decompression (or finger thoracostomy) of the affected side, followed by a chest tube. A chest radiograph is wrong because it is the wrong time, and CT is wrong because the patient is unstable.

@example
title: Updating a probability with a likelihood ratio
problem: A patient has a pretest probability of pulmonary embolism of 30%. A test with sensitivity 80% and specificity 84% is positive. What is the post-test probability?
step: Find the likelihood ratio: $LR^+=\frac{0.80}{1-0.84}=\frac{0.80}{0.16}=5$.
step: Convert the pretest probability to odds: $\frac{0.30}{0.70}=\frac{3}{7}\approx 0.4286$.
step: Multiply: post-test odds $=\frac{3}{7}\times 5=\frac{15}{7}\approx 2.143$.
step: Convert back: $p=\frac{15/7}{1+15/7}=\frac{15}{22}\approx 0.68$, so about 68%.

@mistakes
- Answering the diagnosis when the stem asks for the next step, or choosing a definitive test for an unstable patient.
- Picking the most impressive or most invasive option (angiography, surgery) when a simple test or bedside action comes first.
- Adding probabilities instead of multiplying odds by the likelihood ratio, or forgetting that the same LR gives different post-test probabilities at different pretest probabilities.
- Treating a "correct treatment for the wrong diagnosis" choice as attractive because it is true in general.

prompt: A patient has a pretest probability of 20% for a disease. A test with a positive likelihood ratio of 6 is positive. What is the post-test probability, as a decimal?
answer: 0.6
check: (0.2/0.8*6)/(1 + 0.2/0.8*6)
tol: 0.005
hint: Convert the probability to odds: $0.20/0.80$.
hint: Multiply the odds by $6$, then convert back with $p = \text{odds}/(1+\text{odds})$.
solution: Pretest odds $=0.25$. Post-test odds $=0.25\times 6=1.5$. Probability $=1.5/2.5=0.6$.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient has a pretest probability of 10% for a disease. A test with a positive likelihood ratio of 9 is positive. What is the post-test probability, as a decimal?
answer: 0.5
check: (0.1/0.9*9)/(1 + 0.1/0.9*9)
tol: 0.005
hint: Pretest odds are $0.10/0.90 = 1/9$.
hint: Multiply by $9$ to get the post-test odds, then convert to a probability.
solution: Pretest odds $=1/9$. Post-test odds $=1/9\times 9=1$. Probability $=1/(1+1)=0.5$.
