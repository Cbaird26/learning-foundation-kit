### Week by week

1. **Innate immunity:** PAMPs and PRRs (TLR4 for LPS, TLR5 flagellin, TLR3/7/9 nucleic acids), NF-kB and cytokines, type I interferons, NK cells ("missing self"), respiratory burst (NADPH oxidase, myeloperoxidase); CGD with catalase-positive organisms; ANC.
2. **Inflammation and complement:** selectin rolling, integrin-ICAM-1 adhesion, chemotaxis (C5a, IL-8); acute-phase response and fever; classical, lectin and alternative pathways, C3 and C5 convertases, MAC; C1-INH deficiency, PNH, C3 and terminal deficiencies.
3. **Antigen presentation and MHC:** class I (all nucleated cells, proteasome, TAP, CD8) versus class II (APCs, invariant chain, CLIP, HLA-DM, CD4); haplotypes; HLA associations.
4. **T cells:** positive and negative selection, AIRE, three signals, CD28, CTLA-4, PD-1; Th1, Th2, Th17, Tfh, Treg; CTLs; superantigens.
5. **B cells and antibodies:** structure, V(D)J, AID, hypermutation, class switching, T-dependent versus T-independent responses; IgM, IgG, IgA, IgE, IgD; XLA and hyper-IgM; IgG half-life.
6. **Cytokines and regulation:** cytokine table, anti-cytokine drugs, tolerance mechanisms, Tregs and FOXP3, Fas, CRS.
7. **Hypersensitivity:** types I to IV, anaphylaxis and epinephrine, Coombs tests, transfusion reactions.
8. **Autoimmunity:** mimicry, autoantibody table, SLE, PPV of ANA.
9. **Immunodeficiencies:** infection patterns, SCID, WAS, CVID, IgA deficiency, Job, IVIG dosing.
10. **Transplant:** direct and indirect allorecognition, rejection types, GVHD, immunosuppressants.
11. **Vaccines:** live versus inactivated, conjugate vaccines, herd immunity, vaccine efficacy, contraindications.
12. **Immunotherapy and tests:** checkpoint inhibitors, CAR T cells, monoclonal antibodies, ELISA, flow cytometry, predictive value.

### Most common errors

- Mixing up the CD4 and CD8 coreceptors and MHC classes, or the loading sites.
- Using antihistamines before epinephrine for anaphylaxis.
- Calling a positive screening test diagnostic without considering prevalence.
- Giving live vaccines to pregnant or severely immunocompromised patients.
- Saying class switching changes the antigen specificity of an antibody.

answer: 0.93
check: (1 - 1/6) / 0.90
tol: 0.005
hint: First find the herd-immunity threshold as the fraction that must be immune.
hint: Divide that fraction by the vaccine effectiveness to find the coverage needed.
solution: HIT $= 1 - 1/6 = 5/6 \approx 0.833$. Required coverage $= 0.833/0.90 \approx 0.926$, which rounds to $0.93$.

prompt: A 24-year-old receives a first dose of a recombinant protein vaccine. Serum drawn on day 5 shows no specific antibody. On day 12 specific IgM is positive with low-level IgG. On day 30 she receives a second dose, and by day 37 specific IgG is 20 times higher than before with little additional IgM. Which interpretation is best?
choice: Day 12 reflects a secondary response, and day 37 reflects a primary response
choice*: Day 12 reflects a primary response dominated by IgM, and day 37 reflects a booster response from memory B cells with class-switched, higher-affinity IgG
choice: The vaccine is a T-independent antigen, since IgG appears late
choice: The delay shows that she has a defect in complement
choice: IgE class switching explains the rapid rise on day 37
hint: A primary response has a lag, IgM first and modest IgG; a secondary response is faster, larger and IgG-dominant.
solution: A naive first exposure has a lag of several days, with IgM then low IgG. A second exposure restimulates memory B cells and Tfh help, leading to rapid, higher-titer IgG with higher affinity. T-independent antigens give mostly IgM without a booster effect.

@exam self
prompt: A 58-year-old man with pneumococcal pneumonia has fever, WBC 18,500/microL with 80% neutrophils and 14% bands, CRP 180 mg/L and blood cultures growing Gram-positive diplococci. Calculate his absolute neutrophil count and explain, step by step, the innate and adaptive events that led to these findings and how complement and antibody help to clear the organism.
hint: Add segmented neutrophils and bands before multiplying by the white count.
hint: Then walk from PAMP recognition to cytokines, acute-phase proteins, opsonization and antibody.
solution: ANC $= 18{,}500 \times (80 + 14)/100 = 17{,}390/\mu\text{L}$, a marked neutrophilia with a left shift. Alveolar macrophages detect pneumococcal lipoproteins and peptidoglycan through TLR2 and NOD receptors, activating NF-kB and releasing TNF-alpha, IL-1 and IL-6. These cytokines cause fever (PGE2), activate endothelium (selectins, ICAM-1) and recruit neutrophils with chemokines such as IL-8 and C5a, and IL-6 drives hepatic CRP production. Complement, activated by the alternative and classical pathways, deposits C3b on the polysaccharide capsule, and phagocytes with C3b receptors and Fc receptors ingest the organism. Over about a week, anticapsular IgM and IgG (T-independent and T-dependent) opsonize the bacteria, improving clearance. Antibiotics and these defenses together lower the neutrophil count and CRP.
rubric: Calculates the ANC as about 17,390 per microL with the correct method.
rubric: Describes PRR recognition and the cytokines TNF-alpha, IL-1 and IL-6 leading to fever and acute-phase proteins.
rubric: Describes neutrophil recruitment and the role of complement (C3b opsonization, C5a).
rubric: Describes anticapsular antibody providing opsonization in the adaptive response.
