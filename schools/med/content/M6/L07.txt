@article
**Hypersensitivity** is a harmful, exaggerated immune response to an antigen. The **Gell and Coombs classification** sorts reactions by the effector mechanism. Many diseases involve more than one type.

### Type I: immediate (IgE-mediated)

On first exposure, an allergen drives Th2 cells (IL-4, IL-5, IL-13) and class switching to **IgE**, which coats **mast cells and basophils** through FcepsilonRI (**sensitization**). On re-exposure, allergen cross-links IgE and triggers degranulation.

- **Early phase** (minutes): histamine, tryptase and prostaglandins cause vasodilation, increased permeability, bronchoconstriction and itching.
- **Late phase** (4 to 8 hours): leukotrienes, cytokines and recruited eosinophils and Th2 cells cause sustained inflammation.

Examples: allergic rhinitis, atopic asthma, urticaria, food and drug allergy, and **anaphylaxis**, a systemic reaction with skin (hives, flushing, swelling), respiratory (wheeze, stridor), cardiovascular (hypotension) or gastrointestinal involvement within minutes of exposure. Diagnosis rests on history, skin-prick testing or allergen-specific IgE; **serum tryptase** may be elevated shortly after anaphylaxis.

**First-line treatment of anaphylaxis is intramuscular epinephrine** into the anterolateral thigh. A typical dose for study is 0.01 mg/kg of the 1 mg/mL (1:1000) solution, to a usual maximum of 0.5 mg in adults and 0.3 mg in children, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes if needed; check current references before clinical use. Antihistamines and glucocorticoids are adjuncts and do not replace epinephrine. Allergen immunotherapy shifts the response toward IgG4 and regulatory T cells.

### Type II: antibody against cell-surface or matrix antigens

IgG or IgM binds antigen on a cell or in tissue and causes injury by three mechanisms: **opsonization** and phagocytosis (including splenic destruction); **complement activation** and inflammation (C5a recruits neutrophils, MAC lyses); and **cellular dysfunction** without destruction (antibody blocks or stimulates a receptor).

- Destruction: autoimmune hemolytic anemia, immune thrombocytopenia, acute hemolytic transfusion reaction (ABO mismatch), hemolytic disease of the fetus and newborn (Rh), Goodpasture disease (anti-GBM), pemphigus vulgaris (anti-desmoglein), rheumatic fever (cross-reactive antibody to heart tissue).
- Dysfunction: myasthenia gravis (anti-acetylcholine receptor blocks signaling), Graves disease (stimulating antibody to the TSH receptor).

The **direct Coombs test** detects antibody or complement already on a patient's red cells (autoimmune hemolysis, hemolytic disease of the newborn). The **indirect Coombs test** detects antibodies in the patient's serum (antibody screening before transfusion, prenatal Rh screening).

### Type III: immune-complex disease

Soluble antigen-antibody complexes deposit in vessel walls, joints and glomeruli, activating complement and recruiting neutrophils. **Serum sickness** appears 1 to 2 weeks after exposure to a foreign protein (antivenom, antithymocyte globulin) or drug: fever, rash, arthralgia, and low C3 and C4. The **Arthus reaction** is local (swelling at an injection site on repeated vaccination). Other examples: systemic lupus erythematosus, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis, polyarteritis nodosa and cryoglobulinemia.

### Type IV: delayed (T-cell-mediated)

There is no antibody. Sensitized T cells cause injury 24 to 72 hours after exposure: **Th1** cells activate macrophages with IFN-gamma (the tuberculin skin test, granulomas), and **CD8+** cells kill cells directly (contact dermatitis from nickel or poison ivy, graft rejection, drug rashes). Type 1 diabetes and multiple sclerosis involve T cell-mediated injury. Patch testing identifies contact allergens.

### Transfusion reactions

| Reaction | Mechanism and timing |
|---|---|
| Acute hemolytic | preformed ABO antibodies, type II; minutes to hours; fever, hypotension, hemoglobinuria |
| Febrile nonhemolytic | recipient cytokines or antibodies to donor leukocytes; fever only |
| Allergic or urticarial | IgE to donor plasma proteins; hives |
| Anaphylactic | IgG anti-IgA in IgA-deficient recipient |
| TRALI | donor anti-HLA or anti-neutrophil antibody; noncardiogenic pulmonary edema within 6 hours |

@example
title: Weight-based epinephrine for anaphylaxis
problem: A 20 kg child has anaphylaxis after eating peanut. Using 0.01 mg/kg of a 1 mg/mL solution IM, how many milliliters should be drawn up? (Study example only.)
step: Compute the dose in milligrams: $0.01\ \text{mg/kg} \times 20\ \text{kg} = 0.2\ \text{mg}$.
step: Compare with the usual pediatric maximum of 0.3 mg: 0.2 mg is below it, so no cap applies.
step: The solution has 1 mg per mL, so $0.2\ \text{mg} \div 1\ \text{mg/mL} = 0.2\ \text{mL}$ intramuscularly into the anterolateral thigh.

@example
title: Classify the reaction
problem: A 9-year-old with a snakebite receives equine antivenom. Nine days later he has fever, urticarial rash and knee pain; C3 and C4 are low. What type of hypersensitivity is this and what is the mechanism?
step: The delay of about 1 to 2 weeks after a foreign protein and low complement suggest immune complexes.
step: IgG antibodies against equine proteins form soluble complexes that deposit in joints and skin and activate complement.
step: This is type III hypersensitivity (serum sickness).

@mistakes
- Treating anaphylaxis with antihistamines or glucocorticoids first rather than IM epinephrine.
- Calling the tuberculin skin test an antibody reaction; it is type IV, read at 48 to 72 hours.
- Classifying Graves disease and myasthenia gravis as type III because they are autoimmune; both are type II (antibody against a receptor).
- Confusing direct Coombs (antibody on the patient's cells) with indirect Coombs (antibody in serum).

@quiz self
prompt: Explain why the early and late phases of an allergic reaction occur, and why epinephrine rather than an antihistamine is first-line treatment for anaphylaxis.
hint: Separate the mediators of the early phase from those of the late phase, then think about which drug acts on the receptors that reverse shock.
solution: In the early phase, allergen cross-links IgE on sensitized mast cells and releases preformed histamine and tryptase, causing vasodilation, leak, bronchoconstriction and itching within minutes. The late phase 4 to 8 hours later comes from newly made leukotrienes and cytokines recruiting eosinophils and Th2 cells. Antihistamines block only H1 receptors and do not counter the other mediators or reverse shock. Epinephrine acts on alpha-1 receptors (vasoconstriction, less edema), beta-1 (increased cardiac output) and beta-2 (bronchodilation, and reduced mast-cell mediator release), so it treats hypotension and airway obstruction and is given first.
