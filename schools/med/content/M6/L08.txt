@article
**Autoimmunity** is an adaptive response against self antigens. It arises when tolerance fails and is often chronic. Remember that low-level autoantibodies exist in healthy people, so a positive test must be interpreted with the clinical picture.

### Why tolerance fails

- **Genetics.** HLA alleles (lesson 3) are the strongest single risk factor, but many other genes contribute: *PTPN22* variants (rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, lupus), complement deficiency (C1q, C2, C4 in lupus), and single-gene syndromes such as AIRE and FOXP3 mutations.
- **Sex.** Most autoimmune diseases are more common in women, with sex hormones and X-linked genes thought to contribute.
- **Infection and molecular mimicry.** A microbial antigen resembling a self antigen can activate cross-reactive lymphocytes: group A streptococcal M protein and cardiac myosin in **rheumatic fever**, and *Campylobacter jejuni* lipooligosaccharide and peripheral nerve gangliosides in **Guillain-Barre syndrome**. Inflammation can also expose hidden antigens and spread the response to new epitopes (**epitope spreading**), and activate bystander cells.
- **Sequestered antigens** such as lens protein or sperm may become exposed after injury.
- **Drugs** can induce autoimmunity (for example, hydralazine, procainamide and isoniazid cause lupus-like disease).

**Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)** is the prototype multisystem disease: malar rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, arthritis, serositis, glomerulonephritis, cytopenias and neuropsychiatric disease. It involves type III (immune-complex) injury and type II injury to blood cells. Active disease consumes complement, so **C3 and C4 are low**. Anti-dsDNA and anti-Smith are diagnostic clues; a negative ANA makes SLE very unlikely.

**Antiphospholipid syndrome** causes arterial and venous thrombosis and pregnancy loss, with a prolonged aPTT that does not correct on mixing study (lupus anticoagulant) despite clotting in vivo.

### Classifying autoimmune disease by mechanism

- **Type II (antibody to cells or receptors):** autoimmune hemolytic anemia, ITP, Graves, myasthenia gravis, Goodpasture disease, pemphigus, pernicious anemia, rheumatic fever.
- **Type III (immune complexes):** SLE, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis, polyarteritis nodosa, serum sickness, cryoglobulinemia.
- **Type IV (T-cell-mediated):** type 1 diabetes, multiple sclerosis, Hashimoto thyroiditis (also antibody), contact dermatitis, celiac disease (with antibodies as markers), Crohn disease.

### Interpreting a screening test

A test that is very sensitive but not specific gives many false positives when the disease is uncommon. The **positive predictive value (PPV)** is
$$\text{PPV} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}.$$

@example
title: PPV of ANA testing
problem: Among 1000 patients with joint pain, 50 have SLE and 48 of them are ANA positive. Of the 950 without SLE, 190 are ANA positive. What is the PPV of ANA?
step: True positives are $48$ and false positives are $190$.
step: PPV is $48 / (48 + 190) = 48/238$.
step: That equals about $0.20$: most positive ANA results in this population are false positives, so confirm with anti-dsDNA or anti-Smith and the clinical picture.

@example
title: Autoantibodies in a neonate's heart block
problem: A newborn has complete heart block. The mother has dry eyes and mouth and has never had a lupus diagnosis. Which maternal antibody is likely, and why does it affect the fetus?
step: Congenital heart block in a structurally normal heart is typical of neonatal lupus.
step: Maternal IgG anti-Ro/SSA (and anti-La/SSB) crosses the placenta and injures the fetal conduction system.
step: The mother's symptoms fit Sjogren syndrome, which has the same autoantibodies.

@mistakes
- Relying on a positive ANA to diagnose SLE; it is sensitive but not specific, and anti-dsDNA or anti-Smith confirm.
- Assigning all autoimmune diseases to type III.
- Mixing up anti-centromere (limited scleroderma, CREST) with anti-topoisomerase I (diffuse).
- Attributing neonatal lupus to anti-dsDNA; anti-Ro/SSA is the usual antibody.
