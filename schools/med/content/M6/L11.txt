@article
A vaccine gives **active immunity**: the recipient's own B and T cells make a primary response and memory without the cost of infection. **Passive immunity** is the transfer of preformed antibody (maternal IgG, immune globulin, monoclonal antibody); it is immediate but short-lived and produces no memory.

### Vaccine types

| Type | Examples | Immune features |
|---|---|---|
| **Live attenuated** | MMR, varicella, rotavirus (oral), yellow fever, intranasal influenza, oral typhoid, BCG | replicate in host; humoral and cellular response; durable immunity after one or two doses; can cause disease in severely immunocompromised patients; rarely revert to virulence |
| **Inactivated (killed) whole** | inactivated polio, hepatitis A, rabies, injected influenza (split or subunit forms) | cannot replicate; need adjuvant and repeated doses or boosters; mainly humoral response |
| **Toxoid** | tetanus, diphtheria | inactivated toxin; protects against toxin effect |
| **Subunit or recombinant** | hepatitis B, human papillomavirus, recombinant zoster, acellular pertussis, RSV vaccines | purified or recombinant antigens; safe in immunocompromise |
| **Conjugate** | *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, pneumococcal conjugate, meningococcal ACWY | polysaccharide linked to a protein carrier |
| **Polysaccharide** | pneumococcal polysaccharide (PPSV23) | T-independent response; IgM-dominant and little memory |
| **mRNA** | COVID-19 vaccines, one RSV vaccine | host cells make the spike protein; humoral and T-cell responses |

**Why conjugate vaccines work in infants.** Polysaccharides are T-independent antigens, so children under about 2 years respond poorly. Attaching the polysaccharide to a protein carrier lets the B cell present carrier peptide to helper T cells, giving T-dependent responses with class switching, affinity maturation and memory.

**Adjuvants** (such as aluminum salts) enhance innate activation and thereby the adaptive response.

### Passive immunization

Immune globulins (hepatitis B, rabies, tetanus, varicella-zoster) are used for post-exposure protection, often together with the vaccine (for example rabies vaccine plus rabies immune globulin; hepatitis B vaccine plus HBIG for an exposed newborn of an infected mother). Monoclonal antibodies against RSV are used in infants. Botulism antitoxin is another example.

### The schedule in general terms

Schedules are updated by the CDC's Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices, so check the current version. In general terms for a U.S. child: hepatitis B starting at birth; at 2, 4 and 6 months DTaP, Hib, pneumococcal conjugate, inactivated polio, and rotavirus (2 or 3 doses by 8 months); influenza vaccination yearly from 6 months; at 12 to 15 months MMR, varicella and a booster of Hib and pneumococcal conjugate vaccines, with hepatitis A at 12 to 23 months; boosters of DTaP, IPV, MMR and varicella at 4 to 6 years; Tdap, HPV (two doses if started before age 15) and meningococcal ACWY at 11 to 12 years. Adults need a tetanus-diphtheria booster every 10 years (one with Tdap), yearly influenza vaccine, pneumococcal vaccination from late middle age or earlier with risk conditions, recombinant zoster vaccine from age 50, and RSV vaccine for older adults. Tdap is recommended in each pregnancy (usually at 27 to 36 weeks). Patients with **asplenia or complement deficiency or taking eculizumab** need pneumococcal, Hib and meningococcal vaccines.

### Contraindications and precautions

- **Contraindication for any vaccine:** a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to a prior dose or a vaccine component.
- **Live vaccines are contraindicated in pregnancy, severe cellular immunodeficiency (SCID, advanced HIV, high-dose immunosuppression, chemotherapy)**. Two live injectable vaccines not given on the same day should be separated by at least 4 weeks. Live vaccine is given after pregnancy if a woman is non-immune (for example rubella).
- **Not contraindications:** mild illness with or without low-grade fever, current antibiotic use, and egg allergy for influenza vaccine.
- Moderate or severe acute illness is a precaution (delay until recovered).

### Herd immunity and vaccine efficacy

If each case infects $R_0$ others in a fully susceptible population, transmission falls below 1 once the immune fraction exceeds the **herd-immunity threshold**:
$$\text{HIT} = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}.$$
If the vaccine is imperfect, the vaccination coverage needed is HIT divided by the vaccine effectiveness. **Vaccine efficacy** compares attack rates in unvaccinated (ARU) and vaccinated (ARV) groups:
$$\text{VE} = \frac{\text{ARU} - \text{ARV}}{\text{ARU}} = 1 - \text{relative risk}.$$

@example
title: Herd-immunity threshold for measles
problem: Measles has $R_0$ of about 15. What fraction of the population must be immune to interrupt sustained transmission?
step: Use $\text{HIT} = 1 - 1/R_0$.
step: $1 - 1/15 = 14/15$.
step: This is about $0.93$, so roughly 93% of the population must be immune; that is why measles outbreaks follow small drops in coverage.

@example
title: Vaccine efficacy in a trial
problem: In a trial, 40 of 2000 unvaccinated people and 4 of 2000 vaccinated people develop the disease. Calculate vaccine efficacy.
step: Attack rate in the unvaccinated: $40/2000 = 0.020$; in the vaccinated: $4/2000 = 0.002$.
step: Relative risk is $0.002/0.020 = 0.10$.
step: $\text{VE} = 1 - 0.10 = 0.90$, or 90%.

@mistakes
- Giving live vaccines (MMR, varicella) to pregnant or severely immunocompromised patients.
- Treating a mild cold, antibiotic use or egg allergy as a reason to withhold vaccines.
- Using PPSV23 alone for infants; protein conjugates are required for a T-dependent response.
- Confusing efficacy (trial attack-rate reduction) with herd immunity (population-level threshold).

@quiz self
prompt: A parent asks why their child with an inherited severe T-cell deficiency cannot receive the MMR vaccine, although the child can receive tetanus and hepatitis B vaccines. Explain the difference between live attenuated and inactivated or subunit vaccines and the reasoning for this advice.
hint: Think about replication of the vaccine organism and what a missing T-cell response allows.
solution: MMR contains live attenuated viruses that replicate in the host. Normally, T cells and antibody contain them, but in a child with severe T-cell deficiency the virus can replicate unchecked and cause disseminated disease. Tetanus toxoid and hepatitis B (recombinant surface antigen) cannot replicate, so they pose no infection risk; they may produce a weaker response but are safe and may still help. The family can also protect the child through household vaccination (herd protection) and immune globulin after exposure.

rubric: States that toxoid and recombinant subunit vaccines cannot replicate and are safe.
rubric: Notes that responses to non-live vaccines may be reduced and that other measures (household immunization, immune globulin) help.
