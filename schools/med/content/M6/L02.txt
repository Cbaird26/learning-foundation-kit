### Acute inflammation

Inflammation is the local response that delivers plasma proteins and leukocytes to a site of infection or injury. Its cardinal signs are **rubor** (redness), **calor** (heat), **tumor** (swelling), **dolor** (pain) and **functio laesa** (loss of function).

Resident macrophages and mast cells release histamine, TNF-alpha, IL-1, prostaglandins and leukotrienes. These cause **vasodilation** (redness, heat) and increased **vascular permeability** (swelling from protein-rich exudate). Bradykinin and prostaglandins sensitize nerve endings, producing pain.

### Getting leukocytes out of the vessel

Neutrophils reach the tissue in four steps:

1. **Margination and rolling.** Cytokines make endothelium express **E-selectin and P-selectin**, which bind sialyl Lewis X on leukocytes. These weak, transient bonds make the cell roll.
2. **Activation and firm adhesion.** Chemokines (such as IL-8, CXCL8) on the endothelial surface activate leukocyte **integrins** (LFA-1, Mac-1), which bind **ICAM-1** on endothelium and stop the cell.
3. **Transmigration (diapedesis).** The leukocyte squeezes between endothelial cells, assisted by PECAM-1 (CD31).
4. **Chemotaxis.** It follows a gradient toward the injury: C5a, IL-8, leukotriene B4 and bacterial formylated peptides.

In **leukocyte adhesion deficiency type 1 (LAD-1)**, the CD18 (beta-2 integrin) subunit is absent. Infants show delayed separation of the umbilical cord, recurrent skin and mucosal infections without pus, and a striking blood neutrophilia, because cells cannot leave the vessel.

### The systemic response

IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-alpha act on the liver to raise **acute-phase proteins**: C-reactive protein (CRP), fibrinogen, serum amyloid A, ferritin and hepcidin (which lowers iron availability and contributes to anemia of chronic disease). Fibrinogen coats red cells and speeds their settling, which raises the **erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR)**. Albumin falls (a negative acute-phase reactant). **Fever** occurs when IL-1, IL-6 and TNF-alpha induce **PGE2** synthesis in the hypothalamus; NSAIDs and acetaminophen reduce fever by inhibiting cyclooxygenase.

### Complement

Complement is a set of plasma proteins, mostly made in the liver, activated in a cascade. It has three outcomes: **opsonization** (C3b), **inflammation and chemotaxis** (C3a, C5a), and **lysis** (the membrane attack complex).

| Pathway | Trigger | Early components |
|---|---|---|
| Classical | IgG or IgM bound to antigen (immune complexes) | C1q, C1r, C1s, C4, C2 |
| Lectin | mannose-binding lectin (MBL) on microbial sugars | MBL, MASPs, C4, C2 |
| Alternative | spontaneous C3 hydrolysis, microbial surfaces | C3, factor B, factor D, properdin |

The classical and lectin pathways both cleave C4 and C2 to form **C3 convertase (C4b2a)**. The alternative pathway forms its own C3 convertase (C3bBb), stabilized by properdin. C3 convertase cleaves C3 into **C3b** (opsonin) and **C3a** (anaphylatoxin). Adding C3b to the convertase creates **C5 convertase**, which cleaves C5 into **C5a** (the most potent complement chemoattractant and an anaphylatoxin) and **C5b**. C5b recruits C6, C7, C8 and multiple C9 molecules to form the **membrane attack complex (MAC)**, a pore that lyses cells, and is especially important against *Neisseria*.

Host cells are protected by regulators: **C1 inhibitor (C1-INH)** blocks C1r/C1s and MASPs, and also inhibits kallikrein and factor XIIa of the contact system; **decay-accelerating factor (DAF, CD55)** and **CD59** (which blocks MAC assembly) are anchored by glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI).

### Diseases of complement

- **C1q, C1r, C1s, C2, C4 deficiency (early classical pathway):** impaired clearance of immune complexes and apoptotic debris gives a lupus-like illness, plus infections with encapsulated bacteria. C2 deficiency is the most common in Europeans.
- **C3 deficiency:** severe, recurrent pyogenic infections with encapsulated bacteria and immune-complex glomerulonephritis. CH50 and AH50 are both low.
- **C5-C9 (terminal) deficiency:** recurrent **Neisseria** infections (meningococcal meningitis or bacteremia, disseminated gonococcal infection) because lysis is the key defense. Properdin deficiency is X-linked and also predisposes to Neisseria.
- **MBL deficiency:** common, mostly silent; may increase infections in infancy or with other immune defects.
- **Hereditary angioedema (HAE):** C1-INH deficiency, autosomal dominant. Unopposed kallikrein produces **bradykinin**, causing episodes of nonpitting, non-itchy swelling of the face, airway and bowel (colicky abdominal pain), often triggered by trauma, procedures or ACE inhibitors or estrogens. **C4 is low** (also between attacks), C3 is normal. It does not respond to antihistamines, glucocorticoids or epinephrine; treat with C1-INH concentrate, the bradykinin B2 antagonist icatibant, or ecallantide, and prevent with lanadelumab or C1-INH.
- **Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH):** acquired PIGA mutation in a stem cell, so cells lack GPI-anchored DAF and CD59 and are lysed by complement: hemolytic anemia with hemoglobinuria, thrombosis and cytopenias. Flow cytometry shows loss of CD55 and CD59. The anti-C5 antibody **eculizumab** (and ravulizumab) treats it; blocking C5 raises the risk of meningococcal disease, so patients are vaccinated against *Neisseria meningitidis*.

The **CH50** assay screens the classical and terminal pathways, and the AH50 screens the alternative and terminal pathways.

@example
title: Choosing the deficiency from the infection
problem: A 17-year-old has had two episodes of meningococcal meningitis, with different serogroups. Antibody levels and neutrophil function are normal. What deficiency is likely and what test screens for it?
step: Recurrent infection with *Neisseria* in a person with normal antibodies and phagocytes points to the one mechanism most needed for Neisseria: lysis by the membrane attack complex.
step: The defect is therefore in a terminal complement component (C5, C6, C7, C8 or C9), or occasionally in properdin or factor D.
step: CH50 (classical pathway through the MAC) will be markedly low or absent, and the AH50 will also be low in a terminal defect. Individual component assays then identify which one.

@example
title: Hereditary angioedema versus allergy
problem: A 28-year-old woman has episodes of lip and facial swelling and severe abdominal cramping with no hives or itching. Antihistamines and epinephrine do not help. Her C4 level is low and C3 is normal. What is the mechanism and best first-line acute therapy?
step: Absence of urticaria and itch and failure of epinephrine and antihistamines argue against histamine-mediated allergy.
step: Low C4 with normal C3 points to C1 inhibitor deficiency. Unregulated kallikrein generates bradykinin, which increases vascular permeability.
step: Therapy targets the bradykinin pathway: C1-INH concentrate or icatibant (a B2-receptor antagonist) or ecallantide.

@mistakes
- Treating hereditary angioedema as an allergic reaction and giving epinephrine or antihistamines expecting benefit.
- Linking terminal complement deficiency to pyogenic sinopulmonary infections; its signature is Neisseria. C3 deficiency is the one that causes severe pyogenic infections.
- Forgetting that C5a is the key chemoattractant while C3b is the opsonin and C3a/C5a are anaphylatoxins.
- Assuming selectins mediate firm adhesion; selectins mediate rolling, integrins and ICAM-1 mediate firm adhesion.
