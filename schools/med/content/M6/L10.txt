### Types of graft and allorecognition

An **autograft** comes from the same person, an **isograft** (syngeneic) from an identical twin, an **allograft** from a genetically different member of the same species, and a **xenograft** from another species. Only autografts and isografts avoid rejection without drugs.

Recipient T cells recognize donor tissue in two ways:

- **Direct allorecognition:** recipient T cells recognize intact donor MHC molecules on donor antigen-presenting cells in the graft. A large fraction of T cells cross-react with foreign MHC, which is why this pathway drives vigorous **acute rejection**.
- **Indirect allorecognition:** recipient antigen-presenting cells process donor proteins (including donor MHC) and present peptides on recipient MHC; this pathway drives **chronic rejection** and antibody responses.

### Matching and testing

Matching of **HLA-A, -B and -DR** (and now -C and -DQ) improves long-term outcomes, with HLA-DR being especially important in kidney transplant. Also essential: **ABO compatibility**, a **crossmatch** (recipient serum against donor cells, to detect preformed donor-specific antibodies), and the **panel-reactive antibody (PRA, or calculated PRA)** level, which expresses the fraction of donors to whom a recipient has antibodies. Sensitization follows previous transfusion, pregnancy or transplant.

### Patterns of rejection

| Type | Timing | Mechanism | Features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hyperacute | minutes to hours | preformed antibodies (ABO or anti-HLA), type II; complement | thrombosis, graft necrosis; irreversible, graft removed; prevented by crossmatch and ABO matching |
| Acute cellular | days to months (often first weeks to months) | recipient T cells, mainly direct pathway | rising creatinine in kidney graft; lymphocytic infiltrate and tubulitis; usually reversible with glucocorticoids or antithymocyte globulin |
| Acute antibody-mediated | days to months | new donor-specific antibodies; C4d deposition | vasculitis, graft dysfunction; plasmapheresis, IVIG, rituximab |
| Chronic | months to years | T cells and antibodies; indirect pathway | fibrosis; graft arteriosclerosis; bronchiolitis obliterans (lung); vanishing bile ducts (liver); responds poorly to treatment |

### Graft-versus-host disease (GVHD)

GVHD occurs when **donor T cells** in a graft attack host tissue. It requires an immunocompromised host, immunocompetent donor cells and host antigens foreign to the donor. It is most common after **hematopoietic stem cell transplantation** and also occurs after liver or small-bowel transplant and with nonirradiated blood products. **Acute GVHD** (classically in the first 100 days) affects the **skin (rash), liver (cholestatic hepatitis) and gut (diarrhea)**; chronic GVHD resembles autoimmune sclerosing disease. The same donor T cells also give a beneficial **graft-versus-leukemia effect**. Prevention uses HLA matching, calcineurin inhibitors with methotrexate or other agents and irradiation of blood products; treatment includes glucocorticoids.

### Immunosuppressive drugs

| Drug | Mechanism | Notable toxicity |
|---|---|---|
| Cyclosporine, tacrolimus | calcineurin inhibitors; block NFAT and IL-2 transcription | nephrotoxicity, hypertension, hyperkalemia; tacrolimus: tremor, hyperglycemia; cyclosporine: hirsutism, gingival hyperplasia |
| Sirolimus, everolimus | mTOR inhibitors (block response to IL-2) | hyperlipidemia, impaired wound healing, cytopenias, mouth ulcers |
| Mycophenolate mofetil | inhibits inosine monophosphate dehydrogenase, blocking purine synthesis in lymphocytes | GI toxicity, leukopenia, teratogenic |
| Azathioprine | purine analog (converted to 6-mercaptopurine) | bone marrow suppression; check TPMT; interaction with allopurinol |
| Glucocorticoids | reduce cytokine gene transcription, lymphocyte numbers | hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, infection |
| Antithymocyte globulin, basiliximab | deplete T cells; anti-CD25 | cytokine release; infections |
| Belatacept | CTLA-4-Ig; blocks CD28-B7 costimulation | post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (avoid in EBV-seronegative recipients) |

Immunosuppressed patients have infections typical of T-cell deficiency: **CMV**, *Pneumocystis* (prophylaxis with trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole), fungal infections and EBV-related lymphoproliferative disorder.

@example
title: Counting HLA mismatches
problem: A donor has HLA-A 1, 2; HLA-B 7, 8; HLA-DR 3, 4. The recipient has HLA-A 1, 3; HLA-B 7, 44; HLA-DR 3, 4. How many of the six donor antigens are mismatched with the recipient?
step: For each locus, list the donor antigens that the recipient lacks. At HLA-A the donor's 2 is absent in the recipient (1 mismatch).
step: At HLA-B the donor's 8 is absent in the recipient (1 mismatch); at HLA-DR both antigens match (0).
step: The total is $1 + 1 + 0 = 2$ mismatches (a 4 of 6 match).

@example
title: Rejection timing
problem: A 45-year-old receives a kidney transplant. Six weeks later his creatinine rises from 1.2 to 2.1 mg/dL. Biopsy shows lymphocytic interstitial infiltrate with tubulitis; C4d is negative. Name the process and first-line treatment.
step: Timing (weeks) and lymphocytes invading tubules suggest T-cell-mediated injury, not antibody.
step: Negative C4d argues against antibody-mediated rejection.
step: This is acute cellular rejection; initial treatment is high-dose glucocorticoids (antithymocyte globulin if steroid-resistant).

@mistakes
- Treating hyperacute rejection as reversible; it is caused by preformed antibody and requires graft removal.
- Confusing graft rejection (host versus graft) with GVHD (donor T cells attacking the host).
- Forgetting the glucocorticoid hyperglycemia and calcineurin-inhibitor nephrotoxicity.
- Attributing acute rejection to indirect allorecognition; direct recognition drives early acute rejection.
