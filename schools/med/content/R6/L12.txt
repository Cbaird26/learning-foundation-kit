@article
Primary care serves a defined panel of patients and a community. Population thinking asks two questions: whom should we offer a test or intervention, and are we reaching everyone who would benefit?

### Levels of prevention

**Primary** prevention avoids disease (immunization, tobacco cessation). **Secondary** prevention detects disease early (screening). **Tertiary** prevention reduces complications. **Quaternary** prevention protects patients from overmedicalization (unnecessary tests, overdiagnosis, overtreatment).

### What makes a good screening program

A condition suitable for screening is common and serious, has a detectable preclinical phase, and has a test that is acceptable, accurate and safe, with treatment at the early stage that improves outcomes over treatment later. The proof is a **reduction in disease-specific or all-cause mortality in randomized trials**, not just earlier detection.

### Test performance depends on prevalence

Sensitivity and specificity are properties of the test; **predictive values** also depend on **prevalence**. For sensitivity Se, specificity Sp and prevalence p:

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{Se \cdot p}{Se \cdot p + (1 - Sp)(1 - p)}.$$

A highly specific test still produces mostly false positives when the disease is rare, which is why screening is aimed at defined age or risk groups and why positive screens need confirmatory testing. As prevalence falls, PPV falls and negative predictive value rises.

### Biases that make screening look better

- **Lead-time bias:** earlier diagnosis lengthens measured survival from diagnosis even if the date of death does not change.
- **Length-time bias:** screening preferentially detects slow-growing, indolent disease, which has better survival.
- **Overdiagnosis:** detection of disease that would never have caused symptoms or death (common in prostate, thyroid and some breast cancers).
- **Selection (healthy volunteer) bias:** people who attend screening are healthier.

Mortality from the disease (or all-cause mortality) in a randomized trial avoids all of these.

### Effect measures

Let the event rates be $CER$ (control) and $EER$ (treated). **Absolute risk reduction** $ARR = CER - EER$; **relative risk reduction** $RRR = ARR / CER$; **number needed to treat** $NNT = 1/ARR$. Identical RRR gives very different NNT at different baseline risk, so baseline risk determines whether a preventive treatment is worthwhile for an individual.

### USPSTF grades and equity

A and B recommendations carry a net benefit and must be covered by most insurers; C is selective; D is discouraged; I means more evidence is needed. The Task Force increasingly examines evidence for differences by race, ethnicity and income, and notes that recommendations may under-serve groups at higher risk (for example, Black women have higher breast cancer mortality, and Black men have higher prostate cancer incidence and mortality).

### Social determinants of health

Health is shaped by conditions where people live, work and learn: income, education, housing stability, food security, neighborhood safety, transportation, health insurance, language, and experience of discrimination. These explain a large share of outcomes and disparities. Practical tools: brief screening questions (for example the two-item Hunger Vital Sign for food insecurity), referral to community health workers and social workers, and addressing cost barriers by prescribing lower-cost drugs, 90-day supplies, and patient assistance programs.

### Communication and behavior change

- **Interpreters:** use a qualified medical interpreter, not a family member (especially children), for patients with limited English proficiency.
- **Health literacy:** use plain language, limit to a few key points, and confirm understanding with **teach-back** ("So that I know I explained it well, how will you take this medicine?").
- **Shared decision-making:** explain options, benefits and harms, and elicit values, especially for grade C recommendations.
- **Motivational interviewing:** open questions, affirmations, reflections and summaries (OARS), to resolve ambivalence. The **stages of change** are precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance and relapse; match the intervention to the stage.

### Using a questionnaire in practice

For depression, give the PHQ-9 to everyone at risk, record the total, and act on it: 0 to 4 minimal, 5 to 9 mild (watchful waiting or brief counseling), 10 to 14 moderate, 15 to 19 moderately severe and 20 or more severe (treatment, usually psychotherapy and medication for the higher bands), always reviewing item 9. Repeat at 4 to 6 weeks: a 50% fall indicates response, a score below 5 remission. A registry or care manager helps ensure follow-up for the whole panel.

@example
title: Positive predictive value of a screening test
problem: A screening test has sensitivity 80% and specificity 90%, and is used in a population where disease prevalence is 1%. What is the probability that a person with a positive result has the disease?
step: Per 10,000 people, 100 have the disease and 9,900 do not.
step: True positives: $0.80 \times 100 = 80$. False positives: $0.10 \times 9{,}900 = 990$.
step: PPV is $80 / (80 + 990) = 80/1070 = 7.5\%$.
step: Most positives are false even though the test is decent, so confirmatory testing is needed and prevalence matters.

@example
title: Tracking response with the PHQ-9
problem: A patient begins treatment with a PHQ-9 score of 18. At 6 weeks the score is 8. Is this a response or remission?
step: Compute the change: $18 - 8 = 10$ points, a reduction of $10/18 = 56\%$.
step: Response requires at least a 50% reduction, so this is a response.
step: Remission requires a score below 5, and 8 does not meet that.
step: Continue the same treatment (or optimize the dose) and reassess in a few weeks, aiming for remission.

@mistakes
- Quoting 5-year survival after screening as proof of benefit, ignoring lead-time bias.
- Assuming a test with good sensitivity and specificity has a high PPV in a low-prevalence population.
- Using a family member or child as the interpreter.
- Reporting only relative risk reduction without the baseline risk or NNT.

answer: 25
check: 1 / (0.10 - 0.06)
tol: 0.01
hint: Calculate the absolute risk reduction first.
solution: $ARR = 0.10 - 0.06 = 0.04$ and $NNT = 1/0.04 = 25$. The relative risk reduction is 40%.

@quiz num
prompt: A screening test has sensitivity 90% and specificity 95%. It is used in a population with 2% prevalence. What is the positive predictive value as a percentage, rounded to one decimal place?
answer: 26.9
check: (0.90 * 0.02) / (0.90 * 0.02 + (1 - 0.95) * (1 - 0.02)) * 100
tol: 0.2
hint: Count true and false positives per 10,000 people.
solution: Per 10,000: 200 have disease, 180 test positive; 9,800 do not, and 5% (490) test positive. $PPV = 180/(180 + 490) = 26.9\%$.
