@article
Primary care clinicians provide most contraception counseling, menopause care and men's urologic evaluation. The mechanisms (hormones, estrogen deficiency, androgen effects on the prostate) predict the drug effects and contraindications.

### Contraception

Effectiveness ranks as follows. **Long-acting reversible methods** (copper or levonorgestrel intrauterine device, etonogestrel implant) fail in under 1% of users per year, independent of adherence. Injectable depot medroxyprogesterone, combined pills, patch and ring have typical-use failure rates of about 4% to 9%, and condoms about 13%. Offer LARC as a first option to people who want highly effective contraception.

**Combined hormonal contraception** (estrogen plus progestin) is contraindicated (U.S. MEC category 4) in: migraine **with aura** (stroke risk), smoking at age 35 or older (at least 15 cigarettes per day), history or high risk of venous thromboembolism or a known thrombogenic mutation, uncontrolled hypertension (systolic 160 or higher or diastolic 100 or higher), ischemic heart disease or stroke, breast cancer, decompensated cirrhosis, systemic lupus erythematosus with antiphospholipid antibodies, and fewer than 21 days postpartum. In these patients choose progestin-only methods, an intrauterine device or the implant. Benefits of combined methods include lighter periods, less dysmenorrhea and lower ovarian and endometrial cancer risk.

**Emergency contraception:** the copper IUD (within 5 days) is the most effective. **Ulipristal** 30 mg works within 120 hours and is more effective than **levonorgestrel** 1.5 mg, which is best within 72 hours and is less effective with BMI of 30 or more. These are typical adult doses for study; check current references. Preconception counseling includes **folic acid 0.4 to 0.8 mg daily** to prevent neural tube defects, and review of teratogenic drugs (warfarin, isotretinoin, ACE inhibitors, valproate, statins).

### Abnormal uterine bleeding and menopause

Use the **PALM-COEIN** framework: structural (polyp, adenomyosis, leiomyoma, malignancy) and nonstructural (coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial, iatrogenic, not otherwise classified). Check a pregnancy test first, then a CBC, TSH and, in anovulation, prolactin. Obtain endometrial sampling at age 45 or older, or younger with obesity, unopposed estrogen exposure, polycystic ovary syndrome or persistent bleeding. Any **postmenopausal bleeding** requires transvaginal ultrasound (endometrial thickness of 4 mm or less is reassuring) or biopsy. **Polycystic ovary syndrome** requires two of three: oligo-ovulation, clinical or biochemical hyperandrogenism, polycystic ovaries; treat with lifestyle change, combined hormonal pills, and metformin or spironolactone as needed, and screen for diabetes and sleep apnea.

**Menopause** is 12 months without menses (median age near 51). For moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms, **hormone therapy** is the most effective treatment, safest when started before age 60 or within 10 years of menopause and without contraindications. Women with a uterus need a progestogen with estrogen to protect against endometrial cancer; those without a uterus may use estrogen alone. Contraindications: history of breast cancer, coronary disease, stroke, venous thromboembolism, unexplained vaginal bleeding, active liver disease. Alternatives: SSRIs or SNRIs (paroxetine, venlafaxine), gabapentin, and fezolinetant (neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist). Genitourinary symptoms respond to low-dose vaginal estrogen.

### Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is a DXA **T-score of -2.5 or lower**, or a fragility fracture of the hip or spine regardless of density.

$$T = \frac{\text{BMD}_{\text{patient}} - \text{BMD}_{\text{young adult mean}}}{\text{SD}_{\text{young adult}}}.$$

The USPSTF recommends DXA for women 65 and older, and for younger postmenopausal women at increased risk. Treat after a hip or vertebral fragility fracture, with a T-score of -2.5 or lower, or with low bone mass (T-score between -1 and -2.5) and a **FRAX** 10-year probability of at least 20% for major osteoporotic fracture or 3% for hip fracture. First-line is a **bisphosphonate** (alendronate: take fasting with water, remain upright for 30 minutes; avoid with esophageal disorders or severe renal impairment). Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, and fall prevention. Denosumab requires continuation or a transition to avoid rebound fractures. Rare effects of long-term antiresorptives include atypical femoral fractures and osteonecrosis of the jaw.

### Men's health

**Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH):** lower urinary tract symptoms (hesitancy, weak stream, nocturia). Evaluate with the International Prostate Symptom Score, urinalysis, creatinine and digital rectal exam; review medications that worsen retention (anticholinergics, decongestants). **Alpha blockers** (tamsulosin) work quickly (orthostasis, floppy iris syndrome in cataract surgery); **5-alpha reductase inhibitors** (finasteride) shrink glands over months, helpful when the prostate is large, lowering PSA by about half and causing sexual side effects.

**Erectile dysfunction** is a marker of vascular disease: assess cardiovascular risk, diabetes, hypogonadism and medications. **PDE5 inhibitors** (sildenafil, tadalafil) are first-line and are **contraindicated with nitrates** because of severe hypotension.

**Testosterone deficiency** requires symptoms (low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue) and two early-morning total testosterone levels below about 300 ng/dL. Check LH and FSH to distinguish primary from secondary causes. Testosterone therapy is contraindicated with prostate cancer and calls for caution with polycythemia, untreated sleep apnea and desire for fertility (it suppresses spermatogenesis).

**PSA screening:** ages 55 to 69, individual decision after discussing a small chance of avoiding prostate cancer death versus false positives, overdiagnosis, and treatment harms (incontinence, erectile dysfunction); ages 70 and over, not recommended.

title: Contraception for a patient with migraine
problem: A 28-year-old woman who smokes 5 cigarettes a day has migraine with visual aura 2 times per month and requests a contraceptive pill. What do you recommend?
step: Identify the key contraindication: migraine with aura is U.S. MEC category 4 for combined hormonal contraception because of ischemic stroke risk.
step: Smoking at age 28 is not by itself a contraindication, but it adds to cardiovascular risk and warrants cessation counseling.
step: Offer estrogen-free options: levonorgestrel or copper intrauterine device, etonogestrel implant, or a progestin-only pill.
step: Counsel on effectiveness (LARC under 1% failure) and on tobacco cessation.

@example
title: A man with urinary symptoms
problem: A 66-year-old man has weak stream and nocturia for 1 year. The prostate is smooth and enlarged, PSA is 2.8 ng/mL, and he takes pseudoephedrine for congestion. He also has erectile dysfunction and uses sublingual nitroglycerin for angina. What should you consider?
step: Stop the decongestant: pseudoephedrine is a sympathomimetic that worsens retention.
step: Start tamsulosin for symptom relief, and consider adding finasteride if the gland is large.
step: Because he uses nitroglycerin, do not prescribe a PDE5 inhibitor: the combination can cause profound hypotension.
step: Address the angina and cardiovascular risk, and review PSA screening as an individual decision.

@mistakes
- Prescribing estrogen-containing contraception to a patient with migraine with aura, active smoking at 35 or older, or a history of venous thromboembolism.
- Giving a PDE5 inhibitor to a man taking nitrates.
- Using hormone therapy in a woman more than 10 years after menopause or with a history of breast cancer or coronary disease without weighing risks.
- Forgetting that finasteride roughly halves the PSA, which can mask a rising value.
