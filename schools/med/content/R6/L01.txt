@article
Primary care is where most prevention happens. **Primary prevention** stops disease before it starts (vaccination, tobacco cessation, folic acid before pregnancy). **Secondary prevention** detects asymptomatic disease early (mammography, colonoscopy, blood pressure checks). **Tertiary prevention** limits complications of established disease (foot exams in diabetes, statins after myocardial infarction).

### How the USPSTF grades evidence

The USPSTF is an independent panel that rates preventive services by certainty and net benefit.

| Grade | Meaning | Practical message |
|---|---|---|
| A | High certainty of substantial net benefit | Offer or provide |
| B | High certainty of moderate net benefit, or moderate certainty of moderate to substantial benefit | Offer or provide |
| C | Small net benefit | Offer selectively, shared decision-making |
| D | No benefit or harms outweigh benefits | Discourage |
| I | Insufficient evidence | Balance cannot be determined |

Under U.S. law, A and B services must be covered without cost sharing by most insurers. Other organizations (the American Cancer Society, specialty societies) sometimes differ on ages and intervals; board questions usually follow the USPSTF.

### Cancer screening in average-risk adults

- **Breast:** mammography every 2 years, ages 40 to 74 (2024 update). Women with a first-degree relative with breast cancer or a known BRCA variant need a separate, higher-risk plan.
- **Cervical:** ages 21 to 29, cytology every 3 years. Ages 30 to 65, any of cytology every 3 years, high-risk HPV (hrHPV) testing alone every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. Stop at 65 after adequate prior negative screening, or after hysterectomy with cervical removal for a benign indication.
- **Colorectal:** ages 45 to 75 (grade B at 45 to 49, grade A at 50 to 75); selective offering at 76 to 85 based on health and prior screening. Options: colonoscopy every 10 years, annual fecal immunochemical test (FIT), stool DNA-FIT every 1 to 3 years, CT colonography every 5 years, or flexible sigmoidoscopy every 5 years. The best test is the one the patient will complete.
- **Lung:** annual low-dose CT for ages 50 to 80 with at least a **20 pack-year** history who currently smoke or quit within the past 15 years. Stop if the patient has not smoked for 15 years or has a health problem that limits life expectancy or the ability to have curative surgery.

$$\text{pack-years} = \frac{\text{cigarettes per day}}{20} \times \text{years smoked}.$$

- **Prostate:** ages 55 to 69, individual decision after discussing PSA benefits and harms (grade C). Ages 70 and over, recommended against (grade D).

### Other screens worth knowing

- **Abdominal aortic aneurysm:** one-time ultrasound in men aged 65 to 75 who have ever smoked. For women who have ever smoked, evidence is insufficient (I); for women who never smoked, recommended against.
- **Hepatitis C:** once for all adults 18 to 79. **HIV:** all aged 15 to 65, and others at increased risk. **Hepatitis B:** adults at increased risk (the CDC recommends once-in-lifetime screening for all adults, a point on which guidance differs).
- **Osteoporosis:** DXA for women 65 and older, and for younger postmenopausal women at increased risk by a formal tool.
- **Type 2 diabetes:** ages 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity, repeated about every 3 years if normal.
- **Blood pressure:** all adults 18 and older, with confirmation outside the office before diagnosis.
- **Depression** (including pregnancy and postpartum), **anxiety** (adults 64 and younger), **unhealthy alcohol use** and **tobacco use**: screen every adult, then counsel or treat.
- **Statin use for primary prevention:** ages 40 to 75 with at least one cardiovascular risk factor and 10-year risk of 10% or more (grade B), or 7.5% to under 10% (grade C). The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline instead uses PREVENT-ASCVD risk with lower categories, so organizations differ.

### Counseling and medications

Aspirin for primary prevention is no longer routine: an individual decision for ages 40 to 59 with 10-year risk of 10% or more (grade C), and recommended against starting at 60 and older. Women who could become pregnant should take 0.4 to 0.8 mg of folic acid daily. Adults 65 and older at increased fall risk benefit from exercise interventions.

### A planning habit

For any patient, ask in order: age and sex, tobacco history, family history, chronic conditions, then walk through cancer screens, one-time screens, risk-factor screens and counseling.

@example
title: Is this patient eligible for lung cancer screening?
problem: A 62-year-old man smoked 1.5 packs per day for 30 years and quit 12 years ago. He is otherwise healthy and would accept surgery if a nodule were found. Should he have annual low-dose CT?
step: Compute pack-years: $1.5 \text{ packs/day} \times 30 \text{ years} = 45$ pack-years, which is at least 20.
step: Check the age window: 62 is within 50 to 80.
step: Check time since quitting: 12 years is within the 15-year limit.
step: Check the stopping rules: he is healthy and surgical candidacy is intact, so none apply. He is eligible for annual low-dose CT, with shared decision-making about harms such as false positives and incidental findings.

@example
title: Plan for a healthy 52-year-old woman
problem: A 52-year-old woman who has never smoked has normal cytology and a negative hrHPV test 2 years ago, a normal mammogram 1 year ago, and has never had colorectal screening. Which screens are due?
step: Colorectal screening is due now: she is within 45 to 75. Offer colonoscopy, FIT or another accepted test.
step: Mammography is every 2 years, so she is not due for another year.
step: A negative hrHPV test covers 5 years, so cervical screening is not due for 3 more years.
step: Add hepatitis C (once), HIV (age 15 to 65, if never tested), depression, alcohol and blood pressure screening, and discuss the statin decision if she has risk factors.

@mistakes
- Applying annual mammography to all women, instead of the USPSTF's every 2 years (some groups differ, and the learner should know both).
- Counting only cigarettes per day, or using years alone, when calculating lung screening eligibility instead of pack-years.
- Screening men for AAA who have never smoked, or forgetting that the AAA screen is one-time.
- Continuing cervical screening after 65 regardless of prior results, or screening after hysterectomy for benign disease.

@practice mc
prompt: A 47-year-old asymptomatic woman with no family history of colorectal cancer has never been screened. Which is the most appropriate recommendation?
choice: Wait until age 50, because screening before 50 is not recommended
choice*: Begin screening now with an option such as annual FIT or colonoscopy every 10 years
choice: Order a serum CEA level annually
choice: Order a single stool occult blood test at the next office visit and repeat only if positive
hint: The USPSTF lowered the starting age for average-risk adults.
solution: Colorectal screening begins at 45. Any accepted modality is appropriate; FIT is annual and colonoscopy every 10 years. Serum CEA is not a screening test, and a one-time stool test performed in the office is not an accepted strategy.
