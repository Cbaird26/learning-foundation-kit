@article
Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease in which energy intake, genetics, sleep, medications and the environment shape body weight, and in which hormones such as GLP-1, leptin and ghrelin alter appetite. Even modest weight loss of **5% to 10%** improves blood pressure, glucose, lipids and sleep apnea.

### Measuring and classifying

$$\text{BMI} = \frac{\text{weight (kg)}}{\text{height (m)}^2}.$$

BMI categories: underweight below 18.5; normal 18.5 to 24.9; overweight 25 to 29.9; **obesity class 1** 30 to 34.9, **class 2** 35 to 39.9, **class 3** 40 or more (kg/m²). BMI is a screening tool with limits: it overestimates fat in muscular people and underestimates it in some Asian populations, where lower cutoffs are used. Waist circumference (above 102 cm in men or 88 cm in women) indicates central adiposity.

**Metabolic syndrome** requires at least three of five: waist above 102 cm (men) or 88 cm (women); triglycerides 150 mg/dL or more; HDL below 40 mg/dL (men) or 50 mg/dL (women); blood pressure 130/85 or higher or on treatment; fasting glucose 100 mg/dL or more or on treatment.

### Evaluation

Take a weight history, diet and sleep history, and review medications. **Weight-promoting drugs** include atypical antipsychotics (olanzapine, clozapine), mirtazapine, paroxetine, valproate, glucocorticoids, insulin, sulfonylureas and some beta blockers; lower-weight alternatives exist (bupropion, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 receptor agonists). Screen for obstructive sleep apnea, depression, binge eating, and look for secondary causes when clinically indicated: hypothyroidism (TSH), Cushing syndrome (central obesity, striae, proximal weakness), polycystic ovary syndrome. Order fasting glucose or A1c, lipids and liver enzymes for fatty liver disease.

### Treatment pillars

1. **Behavioral and lifestyle therapy.** The USPSTF recommends offering or referring adults with a BMI of 30 or more to **intensive multicomponent behavioral interventions** (at least 12 sessions in the first year, with dietary change, activity, goal setting and self-monitoring). Physical activity targets are 150 to 300 minutes per week of moderate aerobic exercise plus muscle strengthening on 2 days per week. No single diet is best: Mediterranean, DASH, low-carbohydrate and calorie-restricted plans all work if the patient can sustain them; adherence predicts results. Use **motivational interviewing** and set specific, measurable goals.
2. **Medications**, offered as an adjunct when BMI is 30 or more, or 27 or more with a weight-related condition:
 - **Semaglutide** (2.4 mg weekly) and **tirzepatide** (a GIP and GLP-1 agonist): the most effective, producing roughly 15% and up to roughly 20% average loss, respectively, in trials. Adverse effects are nausea, vomiting, constipation, gallstones and pancreatitis; contraindicated with personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2. Stopping usually leads to weight regain.
 - **Liraglutide** (3 mg daily), **phentermine-topiramate** (teratogenic: avoid in pregnancy; also avoid in glaucoma), **naltrexone-bupropion** (avoid with opioid use, seizure disorder, uncontrolled hypertension), and **orlistat** (lipase inhibitor; steatorrhea and fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption).
3. **Bariatric surgery** for BMI of 35 or more regardless of comorbidity (and consider at 30 to 34.9 with metabolic disease), after multidisciplinary evaluation. Roux-en-Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy cause the largest and most durable weight loss and often remit type 2 diabetes. Bypass risks include dumping syndrome and deficiencies of iron, B12, calcium and vitamin D; sleeve gastrectomy can worsen reflux. Patients need lifelong vitamin supplementation and follow-up.

### Adjusting other treatments

When weight falls, reassess antihypertensives, insulin and sulfonylureas, because needs fall and hypoglycemia or hypotension can follow. Treat comorbidities in parallel: blood pressure goal below 130/80, statin by risk (Lesson 5), diabetes screening, sleep apnea evaluation.

### Communication

Use person-first, nonjudgmental language ("a person with obesity"), ask permission before discussing weight, and emphasize health gains instead of appearance. Weight bias leads patients to avoid care.

@example
title: Metabolic syndrome count
problem: A 49-year-old man has a waist circumference of 106 cm, triglycerides 180 mg/dL, HDL 38 mg/dL, blood pressure 128/78 mm Hg without treatment, and fasting glucose 104 mg/dL. Does he meet the criteria for metabolic syndrome?
step: Waist above 102 cm in a man: yes (1).
step: Triglycerides 150 mg/dL or more: yes (2). HDL below 40 mg/dL in a man: yes (3).
step: Blood pressure 128/78 is below 130/85 and he takes no drugs: no. Fasting glucose 104 mg/dL is 100 or more: yes (4).
step: Four of five criteria are present, so he meets the criteria (at least 3 required). Counsel on lifestyle, screen for diabetes and fatty liver disease, and assess cardiovascular risk.

@example
title: Choosing treatment by BMI and comorbidity
problem: A 44-year-old woman has BMI 33 kg/m², prediabetes, and hypertension. She has completed a 6-month behavioral program and lost 3%. She has no personal or family history of thyroid cancer and is not planning pregnancy. What is the next step?
step: She qualifies for pharmacotherapy: BMI is 30 or more, and she has had inadequate response to lifestyle therapy.
step: Check contraindications: no medullary thyroid cancer or MEN2 history, no pregnancy plans, which are relevant for a GLP-1-based agent and for phentermine-topiramate.
step: Offer semaglutide or tirzepatide with continued lifestyle therapy, titrating slowly to limit nausea.
step: Reassess weight, blood pressure and glucose at 3 months; bariatric surgery would require BMI of 35 or more.

@mistakes
- Using BMI alone without considering muscle mass, ethnicity or waist circumference.
- Stopping lifestyle counseling when medication begins, or expecting sustained weight loss after stopping a GLP-1 receptor agonist.
- Prescribing phentermine-topiramate to a woman who could become pregnant without effective contraception.
- Forgetting to reduce insulin or sulfonylurea doses after substantial weight loss.
