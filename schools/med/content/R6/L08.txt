@article
Musculoskeletal complaints account for a large share of primary care visits. The skill is to find the rare serious condition among many benign ones and then to avoid low-value care (early imaging, opioids, bed rest).

### Low back pain: the first question

Over 85% of low back pain is nonspecific, mechanical and improves substantially within 4 to 6 weeks. The first step is to screen for red flags.

| Concern | Red flags |
|---|---|
| Cancer | History of cancer, unexplained weight loss, age over 50, pain at night or unrelieved by rest |
| Infection | Fever, injection drug use, immunosuppression, recent bacteremia or procedure |
| Fracture | Major trauma, or minor trauma in an older adult with osteoporosis or glucocorticoid use |
| Cauda equina syndrome | Urinary retention or overflow incontinence, saddle anesthesia, bilateral leg weakness or sciatica, fecal incontinence |
| Progressive neurologic deficit | Worsening weakness, foot drop |

**Cauda equina syndrome** and **spinal epidural abscess** are emergencies requiring urgent MRI and neurosurgical evaluation. Without red flags, do **not** order imaging in the first 6 weeks: findings such as disc bulges are common in asymptomatic adults and imaging does not change outcomes.

### Radiculopathy

A herniated disc most often affects L4-L5 or L5-S1. A posterolateral herniation compresses the nerve root that exits one level below the disc (L4-L5 disc: L5 root).

| Root | Motor | Sensation | Reflex |
|---|---|---|---|
| L4 | Knee extension (quadriceps), ankle dorsiflexion | Medial leg | Patellar reduced |
| L5 | Great toe dorsiflexion, heel walking, hip abduction | Dorsum of foot, lateral leg | None reliably |
| S1 | Plantar flexion, toe walking | Lateral foot, sole | Achilles reduced |

The **straight leg raise** (pain reproduced between 30 and 70 degrees) is sensitive for radiculopathy from L5 or S1; the **crossed straight leg raise** is specific. Radiculopathy without progressive deficit improves in most patients within 6 to 12 weeks. **Lumbar spinal stenosis** causes neurogenic claudication in older adults: leg pain and weakness on standing or walking, relieved by sitting or leaning forward (shopping-cart sign), with preserved pulses.

### Management of acute low back pain without red flags

1. Reassure the patient about the favorable course and advise staying active, avoiding bed rest.
2. Use superficial heat. If medication is needed, start with an **NSAID** (or a skeletal muscle relaxant for short-term use, with sedation warnings). Check cardiovascular, renal and bleeding risk for NSAIDs. Opioids are not first-line.
3. Nonpharmacologic options with some evidence: exercise, physical therapy, spinal manipulation, massage and acupuncture. For chronic back pain add yoga, tai chi, cognitive behavioral therapy and mindfulness.
4. Reassess in 2 to 4 weeks. Obtain imaging if red flags appear, if neurologic deficits progress, or if radiculopathy persists after 6 weeks and the patient is a candidate for surgery or injections.

### Other common problems

- **Knee osteoarthritis:** pain with activity, short morning stiffness (under 30 minutes), crepitus, bony enlargement. Treat with exercise, weight loss, topical or oral NSAIDs and intra-articular glucocorticoid injections; acetaminophen has little benefit; glucosamine and chondroitin are not recommended. Radiographs show joint-space narrowing, osteophytes and subchondral sclerosis. Consider joint replacement for severe symptoms.
- **Gout:** acute monoarthritis (often the first metatarsophalangeal joint) with needle-shaped, negatively birefringent urate crystals. Treat flares with an NSAID, colchicine or glucocorticoids. Begin urate-lowering therapy (allopurinol, with a target uric acid below 6 mg/dL) after recurrent flares, tophi or kidney stones; flare prophylaxis for the first months. A hot swollen joint with fever needs arthrocentesis to exclude septic arthritis (synovial white cell count above 50,000 per microliter). Pseudogout shows rhomboid, positively birefringent crystals.
- **Carpal tunnel syndrome:** median nerve compression with pain and paresthesias in the thumb, index, middle and radial half of the ring finger, worse at night; Tinel and Phalen signs; treat with night wrist splints and glucocorticoid injection, surgery for persistent or severe disease. Screen for hypothyroidism, diabetes and pregnancy.
- **Rotator cuff disease:** painful arc, weakness of abduction and external rotation, positive empty-can test; supraspinatus is most often affected. Treat with exercise and physical therapy.
- **Plantar fasciitis:** heel pain with the first steps in the morning; stretching, supportive footwear.
- **Polymyalgia rheumatica:** age over 50 with morning stiffness of shoulders and hips for over 45 minutes, high ESR and CRP, and rapid response to low-dose prednisone; ask about headache, jaw claudication and vision, which suggest giant cell arteritis. An age-adjusted ESR upper limit estimates normal: **men age/2; women (age + 10)/2**.

title: Acute back pain with and without red flags
problem: A 38-year-old office worker has had 5 days of low back pain after lifting boxes. There is no leg pain, fever, weight loss, trauma, bladder or bowel symptom, and the neurologic exam is normal. What should you do?
step: Screen for red flags: none are present, so this is nonspecific mechanical low back pain.
step: Do not order imaging; explain that most pain resolves within 4 to 6 weeks.
step: Advise activity as tolerated, heat, and a short course of an NSAID if there are no contraindications.
step: Return precautions: leg weakness, saddle numbness, urinary retention, fever, or worsening pain. Review in 2 to 4 weeks.

@example
title: Localizing radiculopathy
problem: A 45-year-old man has sciatica with weakness of great toe dorsiflexion, trouble walking on his heels, and numbness over the dorsum of his foot. His Achilles reflex is normal. Which root is affected and what is the likely level of disc herniation?
step: Great toe dorsiflexion weakness, heel-walking difficulty and dorsal foot numbness map to the L5 root.
step: The Achilles reflex (S1) is normal, supporting an L5 rather than S1 lesion.
step: A posterolateral L4-L5 disc compresses the L5 root, so L4-L5 is the likely level.
step: Without red flags, manage conservatively and reassess in 4 to 6 weeks; consider MRI if deficits progress or symptoms persist beyond 6 weeks and intervention is contemplated.

@mistakes
- Ordering MRI in the first weeks of low back pain without red flags.
- Prescribing bed rest or long courses of opioids and muscle relaxants for acute back pain.
- Missing urinary retention or saddle numbness as signs of cauda equina syndrome.
- Starting urate-lowering therapy without flare prophylaxis, or treating a hot swollen joint without excluding infection.
