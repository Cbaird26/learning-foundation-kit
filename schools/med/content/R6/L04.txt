@article
In type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance in muscle, liver and fat combines with progressive beta-cell failure. Chronic hyperglycemia damages small vessels (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and, with other risk factors, large vessels (myocardial infarction, stroke, peripheral artery disease). Primary care therapy therefore targets glucose, blood pressure, lipids and weight together.

### Diagnosis

| Test | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---|---|
| Hemoglobin A1c | 5.7% to 6.4% | 6.5% or higher |
| Fasting plasma glucose (8 hours without calories) | 100 to 125 mg/dL | 126 mg/dL or higher |
| 2-hour glucose in a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test | 140 to 199 mg/dL | 200 mg/dL or higher |
| Random glucose with classic symptoms | not applicable | 200 mg/dL or higher |

In the absence of unequivocal hyperglycemia, the diagnosis needs two abnormal results, either from two separate samples or two different tests on the same sample. A1c is unreliable with hemoglobinopathies, anemia, recent transfusion or pregnancy; use glucose measures instead. Prediabetes is managed with an intensive lifestyle program (the Diabetes Prevention Program approach: about 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of activity weekly) and, for selected patients, metformin.

The USPSTF screens adults aged 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity, and the American Diabetes Association recommends screening from age 35 for all adults and earlier with risk factors.

### Glycemic targets

For most nonpregnant adults the target is **A1c below 7%**, with fasting glucose 80 to 130 mg/dL and peak postprandial glucose below 180 mg/dL. Less stringent goals (for example below 8%) fit patients with limited life expectancy, advanced complications, hypoglycemia risk or extensive comorbidities. Estimated average glucose follows:

$$\text{eAG (mg/dL)} = 28.7 \times \text{A1c} - 46.7.$$

### Choosing medications

**Metformin** (reduces hepatic glucose output through AMP-activated protein kinase) remains a common first agent: weight neutral, low hypoglycemia risk, inexpensive. Gastrointestinal effects are common (extended-release helps); long-term use lowers vitamin B12. Do not start it if estimated GFR is below 45 mL/min per 1.73 m² (reduce dose between 30 and 45), and stop it below 30 because of lactic acidosis risk; hold it around iodinated contrast in selected patients.

Current guidance is to **choose by comorbidity, not only by A1c**:
- **Established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, heart failure or CKD:** an SGLT2 inhibitor (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) or a GLP-1 receptor agonist (semaglutide, liraglutide, dulaglutide) with proven benefit, independent of A1c. SGLT2 inhibitors reduce heart failure hospitalization and CKD progression; adverse effects are genital mycotic infections, volume depletion and euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis.
- **Need for weight loss:** GLP-1 receptor agonists (and tirzepatide). Adverse effects are nausea, vomiting, pancreatitis, gallbladder disease; they are contraindicated with a personal or family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2.
- **Cost or limited alternatives:** sulfonylureas (hypoglycemia, weight gain) or pioglitazone (edema, heart failure, fractures, weight gain). Dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors are weight neutral and well tolerated but have limited benefit.

### Insulin

Start basal insulin when A1c is very high (above about 10%) with symptoms or catabolism, or when other agents fail. A common starting dose is **10 units daily or 0.1 to 0.2 units per kilogram**; titrate every 3 days by about 2 units (or 10 to 15%) until fasting glucose is at target, and reduce the dose if there is hypoglycemia. Consider adding a GLP-1 receptor agonist before prandial insulin.

### Annual checks and risk reduction

- **Kidney:** urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) and estimated GFR annually. Albuminuria is ACR of 30 mg/g or more.
- **Eyes:** dilated retinal exam at diagnosis for type 2 diabetes, then every 1 to 2 years if normal.
- **Feet:** inspection and a 10 g monofilament test yearly; refer high-risk feet to podiatry.
- **Blood pressure** below 130/80; **statin** for ages 40 to 75 (the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline starts therapy for adults 40 or older with diabetes); consider **ACE inhibitor or ARB** with albuminuria.
- **Vaccines:** influenza, pneumococcal, hepatitis B.
- **Hypoglycemia:** below 70 mg/dL is level 1; treat with 15 g of fast carbohydrate and recheck in 15 minutes.

@example
title: Choosing the next drug
problem: A 61-year-old man with type 2 diabetes has an A1c of 8.1% on metformin 1,000 mg twice daily. He has had a myocardial infarction and has an estimated GFR of 72 mL/min per 1.73 m². What should be added?
step: Establish the target: A1c below 7% for this patient.
step: He has established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, so add an agent with proven cardiovascular benefit regardless of A1c: a GLP-1 receptor agonist (for example semaglutide) or an SGLT2 inhibitor (for example empagliflozin).
step: Counsel on adverse effects: nausea for the GLP-1 receptor agonist; genital infections and volume depletion for the SGLT2 inhibitor.
step: Continue metformin, repeat A1c in about 3 months and recheck estimated GFR.

@example
title: Titrating basal insulin
problem: A 55-year-old woman starts glargine 10 units at bedtime. Her fasting glucose log over 3 days is 188, 176 and 182 mg/dL. The target is 80 to 130 mg/dL, and she has had no hypoglycemia. How do you adjust the dose?
step: Average the fasting values: $(188 + 176 + 182)/3 = 182$ mg/dL, which is above target.
step: Use the titration rule: increase by 2 units every 3 days while fasting glucose is above target and there is no hypoglycemia.
step: New dose: $10 + 2 = 12$ units at bedtime; check again in 3 days.
step: Reduce the dose by 10 to 20% if any glucose is below 70 mg/dL, and reassess the overall plan if the total dose exceeds about 0.5 units/kg/day.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing diabetes on a single abnormal A1c in an asymptomatic patient, or using A1c in a patient with sickle cell trait or recent transfusion.
- Continuing to escalate on A1c alone and neglecting an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 receptor agonist in a patient with cardiovascular disease, heart failure or CKD.
- Prescribing metformin with estimated GFR below 30 mL/min per 1.73 m², or forgetting B12 monitoring.
- Titrating insulin without reviewing glucose logs, or ignoring overnight hypoglycemia.

hint: eGFR is between 30 and 45 for metformin dosing.
solution: SGLT2 inhibitors slow CKD progression; an ACE inhibitor or ARB slows albuminuric nephropathy; GLP-1 receptor agonists with proven benefit are also supported.
