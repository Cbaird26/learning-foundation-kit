@article
Most people with depression, anxiety and substance use disorders are treated in primary care. Screening works best when systems exist to confirm the diagnosis, treat and follow up, as in the **collaborative care model** (primary care clinician, behavioral care manager and psychiatric consultant), which improves outcomes.

### Depression

The USPSTF recommends screening all adults, including pregnant and postpartum women, for depression. The **PHQ-2** (little interest or pleasure; feeling down) is positive at a score of 3 or more out of 6, and is followed by the **PHQ-9**, which sums nine items scored 0 to 3 (maximum 27): 0 to 4 minimal, 5 to 9 mild, 10 to 14 moderate, 15 to 19 moderately severe, 20 to 27 severe. A score of 10 or more has about 88% sensitivity and specificity for major depression. Item 9 asks about thoughts of being better off dead or self-harm; any positive response requires a suicide risk assessment during that visit. A screen is not a diagnosis.

**DSM-5 major depressive disorder** requires at least 5 of 9 symptoms for at least 2 weeks, including depressed mood or loss of interest (the others are sleep change, appetite or weight change, psychomotor change, fatigue, guilt or worthlessness, poor concentration, and suicidal thoughts), causing impairment. Before treating, exclude: **bipolar disorder** (history of mania; screen with questions about decreased need for sleep, elevated mood, racing thoughts, or use the Mood Disorder Questionnaire), **medical causes** (hypothyroidism, anemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, sleep apnea), **substances and medications** (alcohol, glucocorticoids, beta blockers in some patients), **bereavement** and **persistent depressive disorder** (symptoms for at least 2 years).

**Treatment.** Mild to moderate depression: psychotherapy (cognitive behavioral therapy, interpersonal therapy), an antidepressant, or both; moderate to severe: antidepressant, ideally with psychotherapy. First-line drugs are **SSRIs** (sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine), then SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), **bupropion** and **mirtazapine**. Choose by side effects and comorbidity: bupropion has few sexual effects and aids smoking cessation but lowers the seizure threshold (avoid with seizures or bulimia); mirtazapine helps sleep and appetite and causes weight gain; SNRIs may help comorbid pain. Common SSRI effects: nausea, sexual dysfunction, insomnia or sedation, headache. Specific risks: **hyponatremia (SIADH)** especially in older adults; **bleeding** risk with NSAIDs or anticoagulants; **QT prolongation** with citalopram (maximum 40 mg daily, and 20 mg for adults over 60); and **serotonin syndrome** (agitation, hyperthermia, clonus, hyperreflexia, diarrhea) when combined with monoamine oxidase inhibitors, linezolid, tramadol, triptans or St John's wort. Abrupt discontinuation causes a discontinuation syndrome (dizziness, "electric shock" sensations, irritability), most with paroxetine and venlafaxine. A boxed warning describes increased suicidal thinking in patients under 25, so follow closely after starting.

**Monitoring.** Reassess within 1 to 2 weeks for safety and tolerability, and at 4 to 6 weeks for response using the PHQ-9. A **response** is a drop of at least 50% from baseline; **remission** is a PHQ-9 score below 5. If response is inadequate at adequate dose and adherence, increase the dose, switch or augment. After remission, continue treatment for at least 6 to 12 months; longer for recurrent episodes.

### Anxiety

The USPSTF recommends screening adults 64 and younger for anxiety. The **GAD-7** has seven items scored 0 to 3 (maximum 21): 5, 10 and 15 mark mild, moderate and severe; a score of 10 or more has good sensitivity and specificity for generalized anxiety disorder. First-line treatment is CBT and an SSRI or SNRI; buspirone and hydroxyzine are second-line options. Avoid long-term benzodiazepines because of dependence, falls and cognitive effects. Screen for PTSD (the PC-PTSD-5) and panic disorder when appropriate.

### Suicide risk

Ask directly: asking does not increase risk. Use a structured tool (for example the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale), assess plan, intent, access to means and protective factors, and create a **safety plan** with means restriction (firearm and medication storage). Imminent risk (plan and intent) requires emergency psychiatric evaluation.

### Substance use

Screen for alcohol with the **AUDIT-C** (three items, scored 0 to 12; positive at 4 or more for men and 3 or more for women). Provide brief intervention and referral (SBIRT). For alcohol use disorder, offer **naltrexone** (avoid with opioid use; use with caution in liver disease) or **acamprosate** (renal dosing). **Tobacco:** use the 5 A's (Ask, Advise, Assess, Assist, Arrange); **varenicline** is the most effective single drug, and combination nicotine replacement (patch plus short-acting form) is also effective. **Opioid use disorder:** **buprenorphine** or methadone reduces mortality; give naloxone to patients at risk of overdose. Insomnia is treated first with CBT for insomnia.

@example
title: Scoring a PHQ-9
problem: A 44-year-old man completes the PHQ-9 with item scores of 3, 2, 3, 1, 2, 2, 1, 2 and 0. How do you interpret the result?
step: Add the item scores: $3 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 0 = 16$.
step: Interpret: 15 to 19 is moderately severe depression symptoms.
step: Item 9 is 0, but still ask directly about suicidal thoughts. Confirm the diagnosis with a clinical interview (5 or more symptoms for at least 2 weeks, exclude bipolar disorder and medical causes).
step: Offer an SSRI plus psychotherapy, repeat the PHQ-9 in 4 to 6 weeks, and aim for a 50% reduction (response) and then a score below 5 (remission).

@example
title: SSRI adverse effect in an older adult
problem: A 74-year-old woman starts sertraline for depression. Two weeks later she is confused and weak with sodium 123 mEq/L, normal volume status, low serum osmolality and a urine osmolality of 420 mOsm/kg. What happened and what should you do?
step: The picture (hyponatremia, euvolemia, urine inappropriately concentrated) fits SIADH, a known SSRI effect, especially in older adults and in the first weeks.
step: Stop sertraline and evaluate for severity: confusion indicates symptomatic hyponatremia and needs monitored correction.
step: Correct sodium cautiously to avoid osmotic demyelination.
step: After recovery, consider a different class such as bupropion or mirtazapine, with sodium monitoring.

@mistakes
- Treating a positive screen as a diagnosis without clinical interview, or starting an antidepressant without asking about prior mania.
- Not asking about suicide directly, or failing to address item 9 of the PHQ-9.
- Stopping antidepressant therapy right after improvement (relapse risk) or abruptly (discontinuation syndrome).
- Combining serotonergic drugs (SSRI plus linezolid, tramadol or MAOIs) without recognizing serotonin syndrome risk.
