@article
Older adults have lower renal clearance, less lean mass, altered hepatic metabolism and more medications, so the same dose gives higher exposure and more adverse effects. **Polypharmacy** is commonly defined as five or more regular medications; it increases falls, delirium, hospitalizations and adverse drug events. The goal of care is function and what matters most to the patient, not just disease control.

### The multidimensional assessment

The "5 Ms" organize the visit: **Mind** (cognition, mood, delirium), **Mobility** (gait, falls), **Medications**, **Multicomplexity** (multiple conditions, social context), and **What Matters most** (goals, advance care planning). Assess **activities of daily living** (bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding) and **instrumental activities** (medications, finances, shopping, transport). Cognitive screens: Mini-Cog (three-word recall plus clock draw) or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (a score below 26 out of 30 suggests impairment). Screening for dementia in asymptomatic adults has insufficient evidence for the USPSTF, but evaluate when the patient or a family member notices a decline. Delirium is an acute fluctuating disturbance of attention, often triggered by infection, medications, or surgery, and is screened with the Confusion Assessment Method.

### Falls

One in four adults over 65 falls each year. Screen annually. A **Timed Up and Go** of 12 seconds or longer suggests risk. Look for orthostatic hypotension (a drop of at least 20 mm Hg systolic or 10 mm Hg diastolic within 3 minutes of standing), vision problems, foot disease, neuropathy, home hazards, and medications. The USPSTF recommends **exercise interventions** for adults 65 and older at increased fall risk (grade B), suggests individualized multifactorial interventions (grade C), and recommends against vitamin D supplementation to prevent falls in community-dwelling adults 60 and older (grade D). Review sedatives, opioids, antihypertensives, diuretics, antidepressants and antipsychotics.

### Potentially inappropriate medications

The **American Geriatrics Society Beers Criteria** list drugs to avoid or use with caution. Examples to know:
- **First-generation antihistamines** (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine) and other strongly anticholinergic agents: confusion, constipation, urinary retention, dry mouth.
- **Benzodiazepines and nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics ("Z-drugs")**: falls, fractures, delirium, cognitive impairment.
- **Antipsychotics** for behavioral symptoms of dementia: increased mortality and stroke; use only when nonpharmacologic measures fail and there is danger.
- **Sulfonylureas** (avoid as first or second-line, especially glyburide, because of hypoglycemia), **sliding-scale insulin alone**.
- **Chronic NSAIDs** (gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney injury, hypertension, heart failure).
- **Proton pump inhibitors** beyond 8 weeks without a clear indication (C. difficile, fractures, B12 and magnesium deficiency).
- **Skeletal muscle relaxants**, **tricyclic antidepressants**, **meperidine**, and **aspirin for primary prevention** in older adults.
- **Nitrofurantoin** if creatinine clearance is below 30 mL/min.

STOPP/START criteria are a companion tool, listing medications to stop and beneficial ones that are omitted (for example, a statin after stroke or an anticoagulant in atrial fibrillation).

A **prescribing cascade** occurs when an adverse effect is misread as a new condition and treated with another drug: amlodipine causes ankle edema and a diuretic is added; metoclopramide causes parkinsonism and levodopa is started; an anticholinergic for urge incontinence causes confusion and a cholinesterase inhibitor is started. Always ask whether a new symptom followed a new drug.

### Kidney function and dosing

Estimate creatinine clearance with the **Cockcroft-Gault** equation for drug dosing:

$$\text{CrCl (mL/min)} = \frac{(140 - \text{age}) \times \text{weight (kg)}}{72 \times \text{serum creatinine (mg/dL)}} \times 0.85 \text{ (if female).}$$

Serum creatinine alone overestimates kidney function in frail older adults with low muscle mass.

### Deprescribing

**Deprescribing** is the planned, supervised reduction or stopping of medications that no longer help or cause harm. Steps: (1) list all medications including over-the-counter and supplements; (2) identify the indication and whether it is still present; (3) weigh benefit and harm in light of life expectancy and goals; (4) prioritize drugs with the greatest harm and least benefit; (5) taper or stop one drug at a time; (6) monitor for withdrawal or disease recurrence; (7) document and communicate. Some drugs need gradual tapers (benzodiazepines, beta blockers, antidepressants, gabapentinoids, clonidine, and glucocorticoids after long use) because abrupt discontinuation risks seizures, rebound hypertension or withdrawal. Reduce antihypertensives and glucose-lowering drugs when targets are overshot in frail patients, and adopt less intensive targets (for example A1c of 8% or lower with limited life expectancy). Involve patients and caregivers, and support non-drug alternatives such as cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia.

@example
title: Drug dosing with Cockcroft-Gault
problem: A 75-year-old man weighing 80 kg has a serum creatinine of 1.2 mg/dL. Estimate his creatinine clearance.
step: Compute $140 - 75 = 65$.
step: Multiply by weight: $65 \times 80 = 5200$.
step: Divide by $72 \times 1.2 = 86.4$: $5200/86.4 = 60.2$ mL/min. He is male, so no 0.85 factor.
step: A value near 60 mL/min indicates mild to moderate impairment; check drug-specific cutoffs before dosing renally cleared drugs.

@example
title: A safe deprescribing plan
problem: An 82-year-old woman with mild dementia takes lorazepam 1 mg nightly for 6 years, diphenhydramine for sleep, omeprazole for 4 years (original esophagitis resolved), and glyburide for type 2 diabetes with A1c of 6.4%. She has had two falls. What do you change first?
step: Rank harms: diphenhydramine (anticholinergic, worsens cognition) and lorazepam (falls, cognition) are high-priority; glyburide with an A1c of 6.4% risks hypoglycemia and falls; omeprazole is lower priority.
step: Stop diphenhydramine now; substitute sleep hygiene and cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia.
step: Taper lorazepam gradually (small dose reductions every 2 or more weeks) rather than abruptly, since stopping suddenly after long use can cause withdrawal and seizures.
step: Replace glyburide with a safer drug (or stop it and monitor, targeting an A1c of 8% or lower), then trial a PPI step-down or on-demand use; change one drug at a time and review in 2 to 4 weeks.

@mistakes
- Stopping a long-term benzodiazepine or beta blocker abruptly.
- Adding a drug to treat a symptom caused by another medication (prescribing cascade).
- Estimating kidney function from serum creatinine alone in a small, frail older adult.
- Making multiple medication changes at once, which makes the cause of any problem impossible to identify.

answer: 40
check: (140 - 82) * 70 / (72 * 1.4)
tol: 0.6

solution: $(140 - 82) \times 70 = 4060$; $72 \times 1.4 = 100.8$; $4060/100.8 = 40.3$ mL/min. He is male, so no correction factor is applied.
