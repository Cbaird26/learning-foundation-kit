@article
Vaccines are the highest-yield primary prevention in adult care. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) and the CDC publish a schedule each year; details change, so on the wards and in practice you look up the current table. For exams, learn the stable logic below.

### Inactivated versus live vaccines

**Live attenuated** vaccines replicate in the host: MMR, varicella, live intranasal influenza, oral typhoid, yellow fever. They are **contraindicated in pregnancy and in severe immunocompromise** (for example advanced HIV with a low CD4 count, high-dose steroids, chemotherapy). Two live vaccines not given on the same day must be separated by at least 28 days. Everything else on the adult schedule is inactivated, recombinant or mRNA and is safe in pregnancy unless a specific note says otherwise.

### Routine adult vaccines

- **Influenza:** annually, everyone 6 months and older. Egg allergy is not a contraindication.
- **COVID-19:** follow the current CDC schedule, which has changed repeatedly and has moved toward risk-based and shared decision-making recommendations.
- **Tdap/Td:** one dose of Tdap for any adult who has not had it, then Td or Tdap every 10 years. Give **Tdap in every pregnancy at 27 to 36 weeks** to protect the newborn from pertussis. For a deep, dirty wound, a booster is given if more than 5 years since the last dose.
- **Zoster (recombinant, RZV, Shingrix):** two doses 2 to 6 months apart for adults 50 and older, including those who previously had shingles or the older live vaccine, and for immunocompromised adults 19 and older (second dose may follow after 1 to 2 months).
- **Pneumococcal:** adults 50 and older (and younger adults with risk conditions such as diabetes, chronic heart, lung or liver disease, smoking, alcoholism) who have not had a conjugate vaccine receive one dose of PCV20 or PCV21. An alternative is PCV15 followed by PPSV23 at least 1 year later (8 weeks for immunocompromise, cerebrospinal fluid leak or cochlear implant).
- **Hepatitis B:** universal for ages 19 to 59; adults 60 and older with risk factors should be vaccinated, and others may choose it.
- **Hepatitis A:** risk-based: chronic liver disease, men who have sex with men, injection drug use, homelessness, travel to endemic areas.
- **HPV:** through age 26 if not adequately vaccinated; ages 27 to 45 by shared decision-making. Two doses if the series began at 9 to 14 years, otherwise three doses (0, 1 to 2, 6 months), and three doses for immunocompromised patients.
- **RSV:** a single dose for all adults 75 and older and for adults 50 to 74 at increased risk (2025 ACIP) (chronic heart or lung disease, diabetes with complications, frailty, nursing home residence); recommendations have been changing, so check the current CDC schedule.
- **MMR:** adults born in 1957 or later without evidence of immunity need at least one dose; two doses for health care personnel, students in college and international travelers. Women should avoid pregnancy for 4 weeks after MMR or varicella.
- **Varicella:** two doses, 4 to 8 weeks apart, if there is no evidence of immunity.
- **Meningococcal ACWY and B:** for asplenia, complement deficiency (including complement inhibitor therapy), HIV for ACWY, and certain outbreak or travel settings.

### Special situations

- **Asplenia (anatomic or functional, including sickle cell disease):** pneumococcal, meningococcal ACWY and B, and *Haemophilus influenzae* type b vaccines.
- **Pregnancy:** influenza (inactivated), Tdap, and RSV vaccine in the appropriate season (32 to 36 weeks); no live vaccines.
- **Chronic kidney disease, dialysis, diabetes:** hepatitis B and pneumococcal are prominent; dialysis patients need higher or additional hepatitis B doses.
- **Immunocompromised hosts:** no live vaccines in severe immunocompromise; recombinant zoster is preferred.

### Contraindications versus precautions

A **contraindication** means do not give: a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to a previous dose or a vaccine component; for live vaccines, pregnancy and severe immunocompromise. A **precaution** means weigh risk and benefit, often by delaying: moderate or severe acute illness, with or without fever; a history of Guillain-Barré syndrome within 6 weeks of a prior influenza vaccine. **Mild illness, a low-grade fever, antibiotic therapy, breastfeeding and egg allergy are not reasons to withhold vaccines.**

### Putting a plan together

For each patient write age, sex, conditions and pregnancy status, then list: screening tests due (Week 1), vaccines due, vaccines deferred and why, and counseling. Most inactivated vaccines can be given at the same visit at different sites.

@example
title: Vaccines for a 67-year-old man with diabetes
problem: A 67-year-old man with type 2 diabetes and COPD has had an influenza shot this fall and one Tdap 6 years ago. He has never had pneumococcal, zoster or RSV vaccines and has no known immunity to hepatitis B. Which vaccines does he need?
step: Pneumococcal: he is over 50 and has diabetes and COPD, so give one dose of PCV20 or PCV21 (no PPSV23 needed).
step: Zoster: recombinant zoster vaccine, 2 doses 2 to 6 months apart, because he is 50 or older.
step: RSV: he is 50 to 74 with chronic lung disease and diabetes, which are increased-risk conditions, so he qualifies for a single dose.
step: Hepatitis B: he is 60 or older with diabetes, a risk factor, so vaccinate. Tdap is already current; a Td or Tdap booster is due at the 10-year mark.

@example
title: A pregnant patient asks about vaccines
problem: A healthy woman at 30 weeks of gestation is nonimmune to rubella and has not received Tdap during this pregnancy. What should you do?
step: Give Tdap now: it is recommended at 27 to 36 weeks of every pregnancy, so this is the correct window.
step: Give inactivated influenza vaccine if it is flu season and she has not had one.
step: Do not give MMR now: it is live and contraindicated in pregnancy.
step: Plan MMR postpartum before discharge; breastfeeding does not prevent this.

@mistakes
- Withholding vaccines for a mild cold or because the patient is on antibiotics.
- Giving live vaccines to pregnant or severely immunocompromised patients, or forgetting the 28-day spacing between separate live vaccines.
- Treating egg allergy as a contraindication to influenza vaccine.
- Assuming a prior case of shingles or the older live zoster vaccine means recombinant zoster vaccine is unnecessary.

@quiz ms
prompt: A 45-year-old man has had a splenectomy after trauma. Which vaccines are specifically indicated because of asplenia?
choice*: Pneumococcal vaccine
choice*: Meningococcal ACWY and B vaccines
choice*: Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccine
choice: Live oral typhoid vaccine
choice: Varicella vaccine, as a replacement for splenic function
hint: These patients are at risk for infection by encapsulated organisms.
solution: Encapsulated organisms (Streptococcus pneumoniae, Neisseria meningitidis, Haemophilus influenzae) cause overwhelming sepsis in asplenia, so pneumococcal, meningococcal and Hib vaccines are indicated. Live oral typhoid and varicella have no role as splenic-function replacements.
