@article
Acute complaints are the most common reason for primary care visits, and most are self-limited. The skill is to find the few patients who need an antibiotic, a test or urgent referral, and to give the rest accurate expectations and symptom relief. Unnecessary antibiotics cause adverse effects, *Clostridioides difficile* infection and resistance.

### Sore throat

Most pharyngitis is viral. **Group A streptococcus** matters because untreated infection can cause acute rheumatic fever (not glomerulonephritis, which is not prevented by antibiotics). Score the patient with the **Centor criteria**: tonsillar exudates, tender anterior cervical adenopathy, fever by history, and absence of cough, one point each. The **McIsaac modification** adds 1 point for ages 3 to 14 and subtracts 1 point for age 45 or older. A low score (0 to 1) needs no test; a score of 2 to 3 needs a rapid antigen test; a score of 4 or more warrants testing and some clinicians treat empirically. In adults a negative rapid test does not need culture backup. Treatment for a positive test is **penicillin V or amoxicillin for 10 days** (cephalexin or azithromycin if allergic, depending on the type of reaction). Consider infectious mononucleosis with posterior nodes, splenomegaly and fatigue, and peritonsillar abscess with trismus, uvular deviation and a muffled voice.

### Rhinosinusitis

Viral rhinosinusitis resolves in 7 to 10 days. Suspect **acute bacterial** disease if symptoms last more than 10 days without improvement, begin with severe features (fever of at least 39 C with purulent discharge or facial pain for 3 to 4 days), or worsen after initial improvement ("double worsening"). Even then, watchful waiting is reasonable; the usual first antibiotic is amoxicillin-clavulanate. Imaging is not required. Consider complications (periorbital swelling, visual changes, severe headache) as red flags.

### Cough and pneumonia

Acute bronchitis (a cough under 3 weeks, often with purulent sputum) does not need antibiotics; sputum color does not indicate bacterial infection. Subacute cough lasts 3 to 8 weeks (often postinfectious) and chronic cough over 8 weeks; common causes are upper airway cough syndrome, asthma, gastroesophageal reflux disease and ACE inhibitors. Suspect pneumonia with fever, tachycardia, tachypnea and focal chest findings; confirm with a chest radiograph. Severity guides disposition with **CURB-65**, one point each for: **C**onfusion, **U**rea (BUN above 19 mg/dL), **R**espiratory rate 30 or higher, **B**lood pressure (systolic below 90 or diastolic 60 or lower), and age **65** or older. A score of 0 to 1 favors outpatient care, 2 favors short admission or close observation, and 3 or more indicates severe pneumonia, possibly intensive care. Outpatient therapy for community-acquired pneumonia: in healthy adults, amoxicillin or doxycycline (macrolide only if local resistance is low); with comorbidities, amoxicillin-clavulanate or a cephalosporin plus a macrolide or doxycycline, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone. Treat for at least 5 days and until clinically stable.

### Urinary tract infection

**Uncomplicated cystitis** in a nonpregnant woman (dysuria, frequency, urgency, no fever, no flank pain or vaginal discharge) can be diagnosed on symptoms and a urinalysis, with culture reserved for treatment failure, recurrence or pregnancy. Typical adult regimens (for study, to be checked against local resistance and current references): **nitrofurantoin** 100 mg twice daily for 5 days, **trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole** double strength twice daily for 3 days when local resistance is below 20%, or **fosfomycin** 3 g once. Fluoroquinolones are reserved for other situations because of tendon and neuropathy risks. Pyelonephritis (fever, flank pain, costovertebral angle tenderness) needs culture and a longer course. **Asymptomatic bacteriuria** is treated only in pregnancy and before urologic procedures that breach the mucosa.

### Headache

Primary headaches: **migraine** (unilateral, pulsatile, 4 to 72 hours, nausea, photophobia and phonophobia, sometimes aura); **tension-type** (bilateral, pressing, mild to moderate, no nausea); **cluster** (severe unilateral orbital pain, autonomic signs, 15 to 180 minutes, treated with high-flow oxygen or subcutaneous sumatriptan). Treat migraine early with an NSAID or a triptan (avoid triptans with coronary disease, uncontrolled hypertension or hemiplegic migraine); consider prophylaxis (propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, CGRP antagonists) for frequent attacks. Daily or near-daily use of analgesics causes medication-overuse headache. **Red flags** (the mnemonic SNOOP): **S**ystemic symptoms or illness, **N**eurologic deficits, **O**nset sudden (thunderclap), **O**lder age at onset (over 50), and **P**attern change, positional or papilledema. New headache over age 50 with jaw claudication, scalp tenderness and elevated ESR suggests **giant cell arteritis**, which requires immediate glucocorticoids to prevent blindness.

step: Score: fever 1, exudates 1, tender anterior nodes 1, no cough 1 equals 4; age 45 or older subtracts 1, so the McIsaac score is 3.
step: A score of 2 to 3 calls for a rapid antigen test.
step: If positive, treat with penicillin V or amoxicillin for 10 days to prevent rheumatic fever and shorten symptoms.
step: If negative in an adult, no culture backup is needed; offer symptomatic care (analgesics, fluids, throat lozenges).

@example
title: Cystitis versus complicated infection
problem: A 29-year-old nonpregnant woman has 2 days of dysuria and frequency with no fever, flank pain or discharge. Urinalysis shows pyuria and nitrites. What is your treatment?
step: This is uncomplicated cystitis: the presentation is typical and she is not pregnant.
step: Choose a short course such as nitrofurantoin for 5 days, or fosfomycin as a single dose.
step: Avoid fluoroquinolones as first choice, and culture only if symptoms persist or recur.
step: Advise return for fever, flank pain or vomiting, which suggest pyelonephritis.

@mistakes
- Prescribing antibiotics for acute bronchitis or a cold because the sputum is yellow or green.
- Treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in a nonpregnant adult.
- Forgetting the red flags for headache: sudden onset, age over 50, neurologic deficit, systemic illness.
- Giving a triptan to a patient with coronary artery disease, or treating pharyngitis with 5 days of penicillin.

@practice self
prompt: A 35-year-old woman describes recurrent severe unilateral throbbing headaches lasting about 1 day, each with nausea and light sensitivity, 3 times per month. Her neurologic exam is normal and she takes ibuprofen with partial relief. Write an assessment and plan.
hint: Name the headache type, then give acute treatment, trigger advice, and when prophylaxis is considered.
solution: Migraine without aura. Screen for red flags (none present). Treat attacks early with an NSAID at an adequate dose, adding a triptan if inadequate (no contraindications such as coronary disease). Add an antiemetic if needed. Advise a headache diary, regular sleep and meals, trigger avoidance, and limiting acute medicines to under 10 days per month to avoid medication-overuse headache. Consider prophylaxis if attacks are frequent or disabling (propranolol, topiramate or amitriptyline), and discuss estrogen-containing contraception if she has aura.
