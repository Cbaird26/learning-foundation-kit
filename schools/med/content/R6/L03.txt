@article
Blood pressure (BP) depends on cardiac output and systemic vascular resistance, and its regulation includes the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), sympathetic tone, sodium handling and vessel compliance. Each drug class acts on one of these levers, which is why you can predict their side effects.

$$\text{MAP} = \text{DBP} + \tfrac{1}{3}(\text{SBP} - \text{DBP}).$$

### Categories and diagnosis

The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline classifies adult BP as: **normal** below 120/80; **elevated** 120 to 129 systolic and below 80 diastolic; **stage 1** 130 to 139 or 80 to 89; **stage 2** 140/90 or higher. Several European guidelines still define hypertension as 140/90 or above, and some U.S. primary care societies use a higher threshold for adults 60 and older, so state which guideline you are using.

Diagnose on at least two readings on at least two occasions, using proper technique (seated and rested 5 minutes, back supported, feet flat, correct cuff size, arm at heart level). Confirm with **out-of-office** measurement: home BP monitoring (average of readings; home 135/85 corresponds to office 140/90) or 24-hour ambulatory monitoring (the reference standard). **White-coat hypertension** is raised office BP with normal out-of-office BP; **masked hypertension** is the reverse and is associated with higher cardiovascular risk.

A **hypertensive emergency** is severe elevation (usually above 180/120) with acute target-organ damage (encephalopathy, aortic dissection, pulmonary edema, acute kidney injury, MI). Severe asymptomatic elevation without organ damage is "urgency" and is managed by restarting or adding oral therapy and early follow-up.

### Goals and lifestyle

For most adults the ACC/AHA goal is **below 130/80**, including those with diabetes, chronic kidney disease (CKD) and established cardiovascular disease. Lifestyle therapy applies to everyone: DASH-style diet, sodium reduction (under 2,300 mg daily, ideally nearer 1,500 mg), potassium-rich foods when kidney function allows, weight loss (about 1 mm Hg per kilogram lost), aerobic activity, and limiting alcohol.

### Starting medications

Begin drug therapy when BP is at or above 140/90, or at 130/80 with clinical cardiovascular disease or a 10-year ASCVD risk of 10% or more (the 2025 AHA/ACC guideline keeps these categories and the 130/80 goal but estimates risk with the PREVENT equations, using a 10-year total cardiovascular risk threshold of 7.5%); for stage 1 with lower risk, try lifestyle therapy for 3 to 6 months. Start **two first-line agents of different classes**, preferably as a single pill, when BP is more than 20/10 above goal.

First-line classes:
- **Thiazide-like diuretics** (chlorthalidone, indapamide; hydrochlorothiazide is shorter acting): hypokalemia, hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, hyperuricemia and gout, mild hyperglycemia.
- **ACE inhibitors and ARBs**: cough and angioedema (ACE inhibitors only; bradykinin), hyperkalemia, a modest creatinine rise. A creatinine rise of up to about 30% is acceptable; a larger rise, especially with hyperkalemia, suggests volume depletion or bilateral renal artery stenosis. Both are contraindicated in pregnancy. Do not combine an ACE inhibitor with an ARB.
- **Dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers** (amlodipine): peripheral edema, flushing, headache. Nondihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) cause bradycardia and constipation and are avoided in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

**Beta blockers** are not first-line for uncomplicated hypertension but are used for compelling indications (recent MI, heart failure with reduced EF, atrial fibrillation rate control).

Selection by context:
- Black adults without heart failure or CKD: thiazide-like diuretic or calcium channel blocker.
- Diabetes with albuminuria or CKD with albuminuria: ACE inhibitor or ARB.
- Pregnancy: labetalol, nifedipine or methyldopa.
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia: alpha blockers help urinary symptoms but are not first-line for BP.

### Follow-up and titration

Reassess about every month until at goal, then every 3 to 6 months. If BP is above goal: check adherence, technique, interfering drugs (NSAIDs, decongestants, oral contraceptives, glucocorticoids, alcohol), then increase the dose or add a second or third class (ACE inhibitor or ARB plus calcium channel blocker plus thiazide-like diuretic).

### Resistant and secondary hypertension

**Resistant hypertension** means BP above goal on three agents of different classes at maximum tolerated doses, one of which is a diuretic, or controlled only on four or more agents. The next steps are confirming with ambulatory or home BP, addressing adherence, and screening for **primary aldosteronism** (the most common secondary cause; hypertension plus spontaneous or diuretic-induced hypokalemia, or incidental adrenal mass; use the aldosterone-to-renin ratio) and obstructive sleep apnea. Spironolactone is the most effective fourth-line drug. Consider other secondary causes with these clues: young age or abrupt onset (renal artery stenosis from fibromuscular dysplasia in young women, atherosclerotic disease in older smokers), episodic headache, sweating and palpitations (pheochromocytoma), a creatinine rise after starting an ACE inhibitor, and Cushing syndrome features.

@example
title: Interpreting readings and choosing initial therapy
problem: A 52-year-old Black man without diabetes or kidney disease has office BP of 158/96 and 154/98 on two visits. His home average is 148/92. Estimated GFR is 88 mL/min per 1.73 m² and potassium is 4.2 mEq/L. How do you classify and treat him?
step: Confirm: home readings are above 135/85, so hypertension is real (not white-coat), and 158/96 is stage 2.
step: The BP is more than 20/10 above goal 130/80, so start two agents from different first-line classes.
step: For a Black patient without heart failure or CKD, choose a calcium channel blocker plus a thiazide-like diuretic, for example amlodipine with chlorthalidone, and emphasize lifestyle changes.
step: Check basic metabolic panel in 1 to 2 weeks for potassium and sodium, and reassess BP monthly.

@example
title: Titration when BP is not at goal
problem: A 60-year-old woman has been taking lisinopril 10 mg daily for 6 weeks. Her home BP averages 146/88 and she takes the pill every morning. She uses ibuprofen daily for knee pain. Her creatinine is 0.9 mg/dL and potassium 4.3 mEq/L. What is the next step?
step: Confirm adherence and look for interfering drugs: daily NSAID use raises BP and blunts ACE inhibitors, so address it (use acetaminophen or topical therapy).
step: Increase lisinopril to a higher dose or, better, add a second class such as amlodipine, because she is above goal 130/80.
step: Recheck a metabolic panel 1 to 2 weeks after any ACE inhibitor dose change to watch creatinine and potassium.
step: Reassess in 4 weeks; if above goal on two agents at full dose, add a thiazide-like diuretic.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing hypertension from a single office reading, without out-of-office confirmation.
- Combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB (more hyperkalemia and kidney injury, no added benefit).
- Starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB in a woman who is pregnant or planning pregnancy.
- Adding a third drug before confirming adherence, correct technique, and removing interfering drugs such as NSAIDs.
