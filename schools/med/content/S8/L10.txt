@article
Most inflammatory skin disease can be recognized by describing the lesion correctly (primary lesion, surface, distribution) and then asking about triggers, distribution and associated findings.

### Psoriasis

Psoriasis is an immune-mediated disease driven by the **IL-23/Th17** axis and TNF, causing keratinocyte hyperproliferation. **Plaque psoriasis** presents as well-demarcated erythematous plaques with **silvery scale** on **extensor surfaces** (elbows, knees), scalp and lower back, with **nail pitting and onycholysis**, the **Auspitz sign** and the **Koebner phenomenon**. Histology: **parakeratosis, acanthosis, loss of the granular layer and Munro microabscesses**. Guttate psoriasis follows streptococcal pharyngitis; pustular and erythrodermic forms can be severe. About a third develop psoriatic arthritis. Triggers include infection, stress, trauma, and drugs (beta blockers, lithium, antimalarials, abrupt withdrawal of systemic glucocorticoids).

**Treatment** by severity (the palm with fingers is about 1% of BSA): topical corticosteroids and **vitamin D analogs** (calcipotriene); **narrowband UVB phototherapy**; methotrexate; and biologics (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors such as secukinumab, IL-23 inhibitors, IL-12/23 inhibitor ustekinumab) for moderate to severe disease.

### Atopic dermatitis

Pruritic, relapsing eczema from impaired barrier (loss-of-function **filaggrin** variants) and a Th2-skewed immune response, with the **atopic triad** of eczema, allergic rhinitis and asthma. **Infants** have facial and extensor involvement; **children and adults** have flexural (antecubital and popliteal fossae) involvement with **lichenification** from scratching. Complications: bacterial superinfection (S. aureus) and **eczema herpeticum** (disseminated HSV; vesicles with punched-out erosions and fever, treat with acyclovir). Treatment: **emollients**, trigger avoidance, **topical corticosteroids** (low potency on the face and folds), topical calcineurin inhibitors, and for severe disease **dupilumab** (blocks IL-4 receptor alpha).

### Contact dermatitis and others

**Allergic contact dermatitis** is a **type IV** (T-cell) hypersensitivity (poison ivy, nickel, topical neomycin) with vesicles in linear streaks; diagnose by patch testing. **Irritant** contact dermatitis is non-immune and dose dependent. **Seborrheic dermatitis** (greasy scale in the scalp, nasolabial folds, brows; Malassezia) responds to antifungal shampoos and mild topical steroids. **Acne vulgaris**: follicular plugging, sebum, **Cutibacterium acnes** and inflammation. Treat mild disease with topical retinoids and benzoyl peroxide, add topical or oral antibiotics (never alone, to limit resistance) for moderate disease, and **isotretinoin** for severe nodular scarring acne. **Isotretinoin is teratogenic** and requires pregnancy testing and a risk-management program; it also raises triglycerides and liver enzymes and dries mucosa. **Rosacea** causes facial flushing, papules and pustules without comedones. **Urticaria** (wheals, IgE-mediated mast-cell degranulation) is treated with second-generation antihistamines. **Lichen planus**: pruritic, purple, polygonal, planar papules and plaques with **Wickham striae**, oral lacy white lesions, associated with hepatitis C.

### Blistering diseases

| | Pemphigus vulgaris | Bullous pemphigoid | Dermatitis herpetiformis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Target | **Desmoglein 3** (desmosome) | **BP180/BP230** (hemidesmosome) | IgA to tissue transglutaminase |
| Level | **Intraepidermal** (suprabasal) | **Subepidermal** | Subepidermal |
| Blisters | Flaccid, easily rupture to erosions; painful oral erosions | Tense, on flexures of elderly; pruritic | Intensely itchy grouped vesicles on extensor surfaces |
| Nikolsky | **Positive** | Negative | Negative |
| Immunofluorescence | **Intercellular** IgG ("fishnet") | **Linear** IgG and C3 at the junction | **Granular** IgA in dermal papillae |
| Treatment | Glucocorticoids, rituximab | Topical clobetasol, glucocorticoids | Gluten-free diet, dapsone |

### Severe cutaneous drug reactions

**Erythema multiforme** presents with target lesions after HSV or Mycoplasma. **Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS)** and **toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN)** are life-threatening reactions with mucosal erosions, painful dusky macules, epidermal detachment and a positive Nikolsky sign, usually 1-3 weeks after starting a drug (**allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, sulfonamides, nevirapine, beta-lactams**). Classification by epidermal detachment of BSA: **under 10% SJS, 10-30% overlap, over 30% TEN**. Stop the culprit drug immediately, give burn-unit-type supportive care (fluids, wound care, ophthalmology, nutrition) and monitor for sepsis. Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome affects young children and is toxin-mediated, with a superficial split (see next week).

@example
title: Classifying a blistering disease
problem: A 76-year-old woman has tense blisters on the flexor forearms and groin, intense itching and no oral lesions. Nikolsky sign is negative. Direct immunofluorescence shows linear IgG and C3 along the basement membrane. What is the diagnosis and first-line treatment?
step: Tense blisters, an elderly patient, no mucosal disease and a negative Nikolsky sign point to a subepidermal blister.
step: Linear deposition of IgG and C3 at the dermoepidermal junction identifies bullous pemphigoid (antibodies to BP180/BP230).
step: Treat with high-potency topical corticosteroids (for example clobetasol) or systemic prednisone if widespread; review any recently started drugs.

@mistakes
- Treating pemphigus and pemphigoid as the same: pemphigus is intraepidermal with a positive Nikolsky sign and intercellular IgG.
- Giving systemic glucocorticoids to a patient with plaque psoriasis without a plan; withdrawal can cause a pustular flare.
- Using oral antibiotics alone as monotherapy for acne rather than combining them with benzoyl peroxide or retinoids.
- Missing pregnancy testing and contraception counseling when prescribing isotretinoin.

@practice self
prompt: Describe each of these five lesions in standard dermatologic terms (primary lesion, size, color, surface or secondary change, arrangement or distribution): (1) A 3 cm well-demarcated raised scaly red area on the extensor elbow with silvery scale. (2) Multiple 2-4 mm grouped clear fluid-filled lesions in a dermatomal band on the right chest. (3) A 0.5 cm flat brown spot on the cheek. (4) Intensely itchy, thickened, scratched skin with accentuated lines in the antecubital fossa. (5) A 4 cm flat hypopigmented area on the forearm.
solution: (1) Erythematous plaque with silvery scale on an extensor surface. (2) Grouped vesicles in a dermatomal arrangement (herpes zoster pattern). (3) Brown macule under 1 cm. (4) Lichenified erythematous plaques with excoriations in a flexural distribution. (5) Hypopigmented patch, 4 cm, flat.
hint: Name the primary lesion first; use 1 cm as the cutoff between small and large for flat and raised lesions.
rubric: Plaque used for the 3 cm raised lesion and patch for the 4 cm flat area.
rubric: Macule used for the 0.5 cm flat spot and vesicles for the small fluid-filled lesions.
rubric: Dermatomal arrangement and flexural distribution are stated.
rubric: Secondary changes (scale, lichenification, excoriation) are named.
