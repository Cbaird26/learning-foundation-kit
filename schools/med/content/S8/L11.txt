@article
Skin infections are common, and most are treated in the clinic. The skill is to match the pathogen to the pattern, and to recognize the few that threaten life.

### Bacterial infections

**Impetigo** is a superficial infection by *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Streptococcus pyogenes* with **honey-colored crusts** around the nose and mouth in children. **Bullous impetigo** is caused by S. aureus exfoliative toxin, which cleaves **desmoglein 1** in the granular layer. Treat limited disease with topical **mupirocin**; extensive disease with an oral agent active against S. aureus. **Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome** (SSSS) is the widespread toxin-mediated form in young children: fever, tender erythema, a positive Nikolsky sign, and superficial peeling with sparing of the mucosa; treat with IV anti-staphylococcal antibiotics.

**Folliculitis, furuncles and carbuncles** are infections of hair follicles by S. aureus; "hot tub folliculitis" is due to Pseudomonas. **Abscess** is a collection of pus: **incision and drainage** is the primary treatment; add antibiotics for large size, systemic illness, extensive surrounding cellulitis, immunosuppression or difficult sites. **Cellulitis** is a deep dermal and subcutaneous infection with warm, tender, spreading erythema without a sharp border. **Erysipelas** is more superficial, bright red, raised and sharply demarcated, often on the face or leg, and due to group A streptococcus. For **nonpurulent cellulitis**, cover streptococci and methicillin-susceptible S. aureus (for example cephalexin or dicloxacillin); for **purulent** cellulitis, risk factors for MRSA or failure to respond, add MRSA coverage (for example trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole or doxycycline, or vancomycin IV for severe disease). Look for a portal of entry such as tinea pedis and for lymphedema.

**Necrotizing fasciitis** is a surgical emergency. Suspect it when pain is out of proportion, erythema progresses quickly, the skin is dusky with bullae, there is anesthesia over the area, crepitus, fever and hypotension. Type I is polymicrobial (diabetes, post-surgery); type II is group A streptococcus or S. aureus, often after minor trauma. Management: **early surgical exploration and debridement**, broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (including MRSA coverage and **clindamycin** to suppress toxin production), and resuscitation. Imaging should not delay surgery.

### Fungal infections

**Dermatophytes** (Trichophyton, Microsporum, Epidermophyton) infect keratin: tinea corporis (annular scaly plaque with central clearing and an active edge), tinea pedis, cruris and capitis (scalp scaling and hair loss, requiring **oral** therapy such as terbinafine or griseofulvin) and onychomycosis (oral terbinafine; check liver enzymes). Diagnose with **KOH preparation** showing septate hyphae. Treat skin disease with topical terbinafine or azoles. **Tinea versicolor** (Malassezia): hypo- or hyperpigmented scaly macules on the trunk, "spaghetti and meatballs" on KOH; treat with selenium sulfide or topical azoles. **Candidiasis** causes beefy-red intertriginous patches with satellite papules and pustules; KOH shows pseudohyphae.

### Viral infections

**Warts** (HPV) are treated with salicylic acid or cryotherapy. **Molluscum contagiosum** (poxvirus) gives dome-shaped umbilicated papules, self-limited in children. **Herpes simplex** causes grouped vesicles on an erythematous base; Tzanck smear shows multinucleated giant cells (PCR is preferred). **Herpes zoster** is reactivation of varicella-zoster virus in a sensory ganglion, with a painful dermatomal vesicular rash; start an oral antiviral (valacyclovir or acyclovir) ideally within 72 hours; complications include **postherpetic neuralgia** and herpes zoster ophthalmicus (V1 distribution, with nose-tip involvement, needing urgent ophthalmology evaluation). The recombinant zoster vaccine is recommended for adults 50 and older.

### Parasites and arthropods

**Scabies** (*Sarcoptes scabiei*) causes intense, nocturnal itch with burrows in finger webs, wrists, waist and genitalia. Treat with **permethrin 5% cream** applied from the neck down and washed off after 8-14 hours, repeated in a week, or oral ivermectin; treat close contacts at the same time and launder bedding and clothing in hot water. Itch persists for weeks after treatment. **Crusted (Norwegian) scabies** in the immunocompromised is highly contagious. **Pediculosis** is treated with topical pediculicides.

### Tick-borne rashes

**Lyme disease** (Borrelia) causes **erythema migrans**, an expanding annular rash with central clearing; treat with doxycycline. **Rocky Mountain spotted fever** (Rickettsia rickettsii) causes fever, headache and a rash that starts on the wrists and ankles and spreads centrally including palms and soles; start **doxycycline immediately** on clinical suspicion, in children too, without waiting for results.

### Number needed to treat

If a preventive intervention lowers event risk from $p_c$ to $p_t$, the **absolute risk reduction** is $p_c - p_t$ and

$$\text{NNT} = \frac{1}{\text{ARR}}.$$

@example
title: Calculating NNT
problem: In a trial, herpes zoster occurred in 2.0% of unvaccinated older adults and 0.5% of vaccinated adults over a follow-up period. What is the NNT to prevent one case?
step: ARR is $0.020 - 0.005 = 0.015$.
step: $\text{NNT} = 1 / 0.015 = 66.7$.
step: Round up conventionally: about 67 people must be vaccinated to prevent one case during that follow-up.

@mistakes
- Treating necrotizing fasciitis with antibiotics alone or waiting for imaging before surgery.
- Using topical antifungals alone for tinea capitis or onychomycosis; these need oral therapy.
- Applying permethrin only to the affected area or failing to treat household contacts for scabies.
- Delaying doxycycline for suspected Rocky Mountain spotted fever until serology is positive.

@quiz num
prompt: A trial of a preventive therapy finds a skin infection in 4.0% of the control group and 1.0% of the treated group. What is the NNT, rounded to one decimal?
answer: 33.3
check: 1 / (0.04 - 0.01)
tol: 0.2
hint: Subtract the treated risk from the control risk.
solution: $ARR = 0.04 - 0.01 = 0.03$, so $NNT = 1/0.03 = 33.3$.
