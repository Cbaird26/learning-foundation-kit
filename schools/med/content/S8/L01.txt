@article
Bone looks inert, but it is a living, vascular tissue that is rebuilt throughout life. It gives mechanical support, protects organs, houses marrow, and stores calcium and phosphate. You will see that almost every disease in this course comes back to one of two questions: what went wrong with bone or joint **turnover**, and what went wrong with the **signals** that control it.

### Matrix and structure

About 90% of the organic matrix is **type I collagen**; the mineral phase is **hydroxyapatite**, a calcium phosphate crystal. Collagen gives tensile strength and the mineral gives compressive stiffness. Unmineralized new matrix is called **osteoid**. Defective collagen produces brittle bone (osteogenesis imperfecta, a type I collagen defect); defective mineralization of osteoid produces soft bone (rickets in children, osteomalacia in adults).

**Cortical (compact) bone** forms the outer shell and about 80% of skeletal mass. **Trabecular (cancellous) bone** is a spongy lattice with a much larger surface area, so it turns over faster and is lost first in osteoporosis; vertebral bodies and the femoral neck are rich in it. Immature **woven bone** has disorganized collagen and is laid down rapidly in fracture callus; it is remodeled into strong **lamellar bone**. **Wolff's law**: bone remodels along the lines of mechanical load.

### The three bone cells

- **Osteoblasts** (mesenchymal lineage) make osteoid and then promote its mineralization. They release alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and osteocalcin, which rise with high bone formation.
- **Osteocytes** are former osteoblasts walled into the matrix. They sense mechanical load and secrete **sclerostin**, which inhibits Wnt signaling and so suppresses bone formation.
- **Osteoclasts** are multinucleated cells derived from the monocyte-macrophage lineage. A ruffled border pumps protons (H+-ATPase, with carbonic anhydrase II) to dissolve mineral, and **cathepsin K** digests collagen.

### Controlling remodeling

Osteoblast-lineage cells express **RANKL**, which binds **RANK** on osteoclast precursors; together with **M-CSF** this drives osteoclast formation and activity. Osteoblasts also secrete **osteoprotegerin (OPG)**, a decoy receptor that binds RANKL and blocks it. The RANKL:OPG ratio therefore sets resorption. Estrogen raises OPG and restrains osteoclasts, which is why estrogen loss at menopause speeds bone loss. **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** given continuously raises RANKL and increases resorption, but given intermittently it is anabolic (the basis of teriparatide). Calcitonin inhibits osteoclasts. Vitamin D (as 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D) increases intestinal calcium and phosphate absorption so that osteoid can mineralize. Drug targets map directly onto these pathways: denosumab binds RANKL, bisphosphonates poison osteoclasts, and romosozumab binds sclerostin.

### How bone forms and grows

**Intramembranous ossification** makes flat bones (skull vault, mandible, most of the clavicle) directly from mesenchyme with no cartilage template. **Endochondral ossification** makes long bones: a cartilage model is replaced by bone. Longitudinal growth happens at the **growth plate (physis)**, which has zones from the epiphysis toward the metaphysis: **reserve (resting), proliferative, hypertrophic, and calcification**. Chondrocytes stack in columns, enlarge, and die as matrix calcifies. The hypertrophic zone is the weakest, so the physis tends to fail there (slipped capital femoral epiphysis in an obese adolescent). In **achondroplasia**, a gain-of-function mutation in **FGFR3** impairs chondrocyte proliferation: limbs are short, but intramembranous skull bones grow normally and intelligence is normal.

### Joints

Fibrous joints (skull sutures) and cartilaginous joints (pubic symphysis) move little. **Synovial joints** (knee, hip, shoulder) have articular cartilage, a capsule, and a synovial membrane. **Articular cartilage** is hyaline: chondrocytes embedded in **type II collagen** and proteoglycan (aggrecan) that holds water. It has no blood supply or nerves, so it repairs poorly. Menisci are fibrocartilage rich in type I collagen. The **synovium** has macrophage-like type A cells and fibroblast-like type B cells that secrete hyaluronic acid. Normal synovial fluid is viscous, clear and has few cells (under 200 WBC/microL). Tendons connect muscle to bone and ligaments connect bone to bone; both are mainly type I collagen.

### Calcium and albumin

About 40% of serum calcium is bound to albumin, so low albumin lowers total calcium without changing ionized (active) calcium. The usual estimate is

$$\text{corrected Ca} = \text{measured Ca} + 0.8\,(4.0 - \text{albumin}),$$

with calcium in mg/dL and albumin in g/dL. If in doubt, measure ionized calcium directly.

@example
title: Correcting calcium in a patient with low albumin
problem: A malnourished woman has a total serum calcium of 7.2 mg/dL and an albumin of 2.5 g/dL. Is she truly hypocalcemic?
step: Albumin is below the reference albumin of 4.0 g/dL, so total calcium underestimates the active calcium.
step: Apply the correction: $7.2 + 0.8(4.0 - 2.5) = 7.2 + 1.2$.
step: Corrected calcium is $8.4$ mg/dL, which is just below the normal range (about 8.5-10.5 mg/dL). She has at most mild true hypocalcemia, so measure ionized calcium if there is any doubt.

@mistakes
- Saying osteoclasts come from the osteoblast (mesenchymal) lineage; they come from monocyte-macrophage precursors.
- Treating OPG as a RANKL activator; it is a decoy receptor that blocks RANKL.
- Forgetting that intermittent PTH is anabolic while continuous PTH excess causes bone loss.
- Using hyaline cartilage's collagen as type I; articular cartilage is type II, and type I belongs to bone, tendon and menisci.
