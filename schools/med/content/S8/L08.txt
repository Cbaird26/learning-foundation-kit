@article
A persistent, progressive bone pain, especially at night or at rest, is a tumor or infection until proven otherwise. The best clue to the diagnosis is the combination of **age, bone and position in the bone, and radiographic pattern**.

### Metastases and myeloma

In adults, **metastasis is far more common than a primary bone tumor**. Common sources: breast, prostate, lung, kidney and thyroid. Prostate metastases are typically osteoblastic (sclerotic); most others are lytic. **Multiple myeloma** produces "punched-out" lytic lesions, anemia, hypercalcemia, renal injury and an M-spike; skeletal surveys and MRI detect lesions that bone scans miss (CRAB: calcium, renal, anemia, bone). Order serum protein electrophoresis with immunofixation and serum free light chains.

### Primary bone tumors

| Tumor | Typical patient and site | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Osteosarcoma** | Age 10-20 (second peak in older adults with Paget disease or prior radiation); metaphysis of distal femur or proximal tibia | **Codman triangle**, **sunburst** periosteal reaction; high ALP; RB1 and TP53 mutations; chemotherapy plus limb-sparing surgery |
| **Ewing sarcoma** | Age 5-20; **diaphysis** of long bones, pelvis | **Onion-skin** periosteal reaction; **t(11;22) EWSR1-FLI1**; small round blue cells, CD99 positive, PAS-positive glycogen; fever and high ESR can mimic osteomyelitis |
| **Chondrosarcoma** | Age over 40; pelvis, proximal femur | Hyaline cartilage matrix with calcifications; resistant to chemotherapy; wide excision |
| **Giant cell tumor** | Age 20-40; **epiphysis** of long bone near the knee | Eccentric "soap-bubble" lytic lesion; multinucleated giant cells with RANKL-expressing stromal cells; locally aggressive; denosumab |
| **Osteochondroma** | Adolescent; metaphysis | **Most common benign bone tumor**; cartilage-capped exostosis; hereditary multiple exostoses (EXT1/EXT2) |
| **Osteoid osteoma** | Age 10-25; cortex of long bone | Small nidus (under 2 cm) with sclerosis; **night pain relieved by NSAIDs**; radiofrequency ablation |

### Soft-tissue sarcomas

A deep, enlarging, usually painless mass over 5 cm should be imaged (MRI) and biopsied after consultation with the surgeon who will resect it, with the biopsy tract placed to be excised. Examples: **liposarcoma** (MDM2 amplification), **leiomyosarcoma**, **rhabdomyosarcoma** (children, desmin and myogenin positive; botryoid form in the vagina or bladder), **synovial sarcoma** (**t(X;18) SYT-SSX**, near joints in young adults), and undifferentiated pleomorphic sarcoma. Lung metastases are common; staging includes chest CT.

### Osteomyelitis

**Hematogenous** spread in children usually seeds the **metaphysis** of long bones; in adults, **vertebral** osteomyelitis follows bacteremia or IV drug use, and **contiguous** spread occurs from diabetic foot ulcers, open fractures and surgery. **Staphylococcus aureus** is the most common organism overall. **Salmonella** is classically associated with sickle cell disease, **Pseudomonas** with puncture wounds through the sole of a shoe and with injection drug use, **Pasteurella** with cat and dog bites, and **Mycobacterium tuberculosis** with vertebral disease (Pott disease).

Fever may be absent. **ESR and CRP are elevated**; get blood cultures. Plain radiographs are normal for the first 10-14 days; **MRI** is the best imaging test. A **bone biopsy culture** guides therapy when blood cultures are negative. Treat with prolonged antibiotics (typically 4-6 weeks, often starting IV) targeted to the organism with MRSA coverage (for example vancomycin) in the empiric regimen, and surgical debridement of abscess or necrotic bone (sequestrum, with new bone called involucrum in chronic disease). Check for epidural abscess in vertebral osteomyelitis with any neurologic deficit.

### Septic arthritis

An acutely hot, painful joint with limited range of motion and fever. The most common organism is **S. aureus**; **Neisseria gonorrhoeae** is common in sexually active young adults. Perform arthrocentesis (Gram stain, culture, cell count, crystals) before antibiotics when possible, but do not delay treatment in an unstable patient. Treat with IV antibiotics and joint drainage; hips in children and prosthetic joints often need surgical washout. In a child with a hip effusion, the **Kocher criteria** (non-weight-bearing, ESR over 40 mm/h, fever over 38.5 C, WBC over 12,000/microL) estimate the probability of septic arthritis: each criterion present raises the likelihood sharply, and three or four make septic arthritis likely.

### Comparing osteoporosis and osteomalacia

Both produce fractures and low bone density on imaging, but osteoporosis has **less bone of normal quality** while osteomalacia has **unmineralized osteoid**. In osteoporosis, calcium, phosphate, PTH and ALP are normal and the patient has fragility fractures; in osteomalacia, 25-hydroxyvitamin D is low, phosphate is low, PTH and ALP are high, bone pain and proximal weakness occur, and Looser zones are seen.

@example
title: Age and site point to a tumor
problem: A 13-year-old boy has 6 weeks of pain above the knee, worse at night, and a firm warm swelling of the distal femur. Radiograph: a lytic and sclerotic metaphyseal lesion with a sunburst periosteal reaction and a Codman triangle. ALP is elevated. What is the diagnosis, and what is the next step?
step: The adolescent age, distal femoral metaphysis, sunburst pattern, Codman triangle and elevated ALP indicate osteosarcoma.
step: Obtain MRI of the entire femur, chest CT for lung metastases and a bone scan or PET; do the biopsy at the treating center, planning the tract so it can be resected.
step: Treatment is neoadjuvant chemotherapy, limb-sparing resection and postoperative chemotherapy.

@mistakes
- Calling every lytic bone lesion in an adult a primary tumor; metastasis and myeloma are far more common.
- Using plain radiographs to exclude early osteomyelitis; they stay normal for 10-14 days, so use MRI.
- Giving antibiotics for septic arthritis before any joint fluid is obtained when the patient is stable, or injecting steroids into a possibly infected joint.
- Mixing Ewing sarcoma (diaphysis, onion skin, t(11;22)) with osteosarcoma (metaphysis, sunburst).

@practice self
prompt: Compare osteoporosis and osteomalacia: for each, state the defect, calcium, phosphate, PTH and alkaline phosphatase, the typical symptoms and radiographic features, and the first-line management.
solution: Osteoporosis: reduced bone mass with normal mineralization; calcium, phosphate, PTH and ALP normal; asymptomatic until fragility fractures (vertebral compression, hip, wrist); DXA T-score at or below -2.5; treat with calcium, vitamin D, exercise and a bisphosphonate (or denosumab or anabolic agent for high risk). Osteomalacia: impaired mineralization of osteoid (rickets in children), usually from vitamin D deficiency; calcium low or low-normal, phosphate low, PTH high, ALP high, 25-hydroxyvitamin D low; diffuse bone pain, proximal weakness; Looser zones and decreased density; treat with vitamin D and calcium and address the cause.

rubric: Gives the correct laboratory pattern for each (normal in osteoporosis; low phosphate, high PTH and ALP in osteomalacia).
rubric: Names Looser zones for osteomalacia and fragility fractures for osteoporosis.
rubric: Gives vitamin D and calcium for osteomalacia and bisphosphonate-based therapy for osteoporosis.
