@article
A fracture is a break in bone continuity. The skill for this week is a systematic description that another clinician could draw from your words, followed by a hunt for the complications that threaten limb or life.

### Describing a fracture

State, in order: **which bone and which part** (proximal, shaft or diaphysis, distal; intra-articular or not); **the pattern** (transverse, oblique, spiral, comminuted, segmental, greenstick or buckle in children); **displacement** (translation, angulation, shortening, rotation); **open or closed**; and **neurovascular status** before and after any manipulation. Pathologic fractures occur through weakened bone (osteoporosis, metastasis); stress fractures follow repetitive load. A spiral pattern implies a twisting force; in a non-ambulatory infant it should raise concern for abuse.

### Pediatric physeal injury: Salter-Harris

The physis is weaker than the surrounding ligaments, so children often fracture it rather than tear a ligament. Remember **SALTR**: type I **S**traight across (slipped), type II **A**bove (fracture exits toward the metaphysis, the most common), type III **L**ower (through the epiphysis into the joint), type IV **T**hrough (metaphysis, physis and epiphysis), type V **R**ammed (crush). Higher types carry more risk of growth arrest and limb deformity, and types III and IV need anatomic reduction because they enter the joint.

### Urgent complications

**Compartment syndrome.** Pressure in a closed fascial space rises until perfusion falls. It follows tibial or forearm fractures, crush injury, tight casts and reperfusion. The key symptom is **pain out of proportion** to the injury and **pain with passive stretch** of the muscles in the compartment; tense swelling, paresthesias and, late, pallor, paralysis and pulselessness appear. A normal pulse does not exclude it. The diagnosis is clinical; if uncertain, measure compartment pressure. A **delta pressure** (diastolic pressure minus compartment pressure) below 30 mmHg, or an absolute pressure above about 30 mmHg, is concerning. Treatment is removing constrictive dressings, keeping the limb at heart level, and emergent **fasciotomy**. Delay causes muscle necrosis, myoglobinuria and kidney injury.

**Open fractures** (bone communicates with the environment) are surgical urgencies: give IV antibiotics as early as possible (a first-generation cephalosporin; add gram-negative coverage for severe contamination), update tetanus, irrigate, debride and stabilize. The Gustilo-Anderson grade reflects wound size and contamination.

**Neurovascular injury.** Always document pulses, capillary refill and sensation and motor function first. Classic pairings: **supracondylar humerus** fracture, with brachial artery and anterior interosseous nerve injury; **humeral shaft**, with radial nerve; **anterior shoulder dislocation**, with axillary nerve (loss of deltoid sensation); **posterior hip dislocation**, with sciatic nerve; **knee dislocation**, with popliteal artery; **fibular neck**, with common peroneal nerve (foot drop). A pulseless limb after fracture needs emergent reduction and vascular evaluation.

**Fat embolism.** After long bone or pelvic fractures, usually 24-72 hours later, hypoxemia, confusion and a petechial rash on the chest, axilla and conjunctivae appear. Care is supportive; early fracture fixation lowers risk.

**Avascular necrosis (AVN).** Fractures that disrupt retrograde blood supply risk bone death: **femoral neck**, **scaphoid** (proximal pole), talus. Suspect a **scaphoid fracture** after a fall on an outstretched hand with snuffbox tenderness even when the first radiograph is normal: immobilize in a thumb spica splint and repeat imaging or obtain MRI.

### Hip fracture

Older adults with osteoporosis who fall present with a shortened, externally rotated leg. Treat with surgery within about 24-48 hours, analgesia, and then evaluation for osteoporosis. Complications include venous thromboembolism, so prophylaxis is standard.

### Soft-tissue injuries

- **ACL tear:** pivoting injury with a pop and rapid swelling (hemarthrosis); **Lachman** test is the most sensitive; anterior drawer is less so. The **"unhappy triad"** is ACL, medial collateral ligament and medial meniscus.
- **Meniscal tear:** joint-line pain, locking, positive McMurray test.
- **Rotator cuff:** the **supraspinatus** is injured most often; weakness of abduction in the first 15 degrees and a positive empty-can test. **Subscapularis** is tested by lift-off.
- **Ankle sprain:** the **Ottawa ankle rules** limit radiographs: image if there is malleolar-zone pain with bone tenderness at the posterior edge or tip of the malleolus, or inability to bear weight for four steps.

@example
title: Interpreting a compartment pressure
problem: A 24-year-old man has severe pain 8 hours after a tibial shaft fracture. Passive toe extension is excruciating and the calf is tense. His diastolic blood pressure is 70 mmHg and the anterior compartment pressure is 48 mmHg. What is the delta pressure and what do you do?
step: Delta pressure is diastolic blood pressure minus compartment pressure: $70 - 48 = 22$ mmHg.
step: A value below 30 mmHg indicates inadequate perfusion; combined with the clinical picture, the diagnosis is acute compartment syndrome.
step: Remove circumferential dressings, keep the limb at heart level (not elevated), notify orthopedics and proceed to emergent fasciotomy. Do not wait for pulselessness.

@mistakes
- Reassuring the patient because distal pulses are present; pulses are often normal until very late in compartment syndrome.
- Elevating the limb above heart level in suspected compartment syndrome, which lowers arterial inflow.
- Dismissing a normal first radiograph when snuffbox tenderness is present.
- Memorizing Salter-Harris types without remembering that type II is the most common and types III and IV cross the joint surface.

@practice self
prompt: A 27-year-old man is hit by a car and has an obvious left tibial shaft deformity and a 4 cm wound over the medial tibia with exposed bone. Write a one-paragraph description of the fracture in standard terms and list your first four actions, including the urgent complications you must look for.
hint: Use the order: bone and site, pattern, displacement, open or closed, neurovascular status; then ask what could threaten the limb in the first hours.
solution: Describe: open (compound) left tibial shaft fracture, specify the pattern (for example transverse or comminuted), displacement and angulation, and neurovascular status distal to the injury. Actions: assess ABCs and the rest of the trauma survey; document and monitor distal pulses, capillary refill, sensation and motor function; cover the wound with a sterile dressing, splint the leg, and give IV antibiotics as soon as possible with tetanus prophylaxis; obtain radiographs of the whole tibia including the joints above and below and call orthopedics for irrigation, debridement and stabilization. Watch for compartment syndrome (pain out of proportion, pain with passive stretch, tense calf), vascular injury, infection and fat embolism.
rubric: The fracture description includes bone, site, pattern, displacement and open versus closed status.
rubric: Neurovascular examination is documented before and after splinting.
rubric: Early IV antibiotics, tetanus update and surgical debridement are named.
rubric: Compartment syndrome is listed with its early signs.
