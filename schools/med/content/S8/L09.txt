@article
The skin is the largest organ: it is a barrier to water loss and microbes, regulates temperature, senses touch and pain, synthesizes vitamin D, and provides immune surveillance. Describing a lesion in standard words is the first diagnostic step, because the same words carry the same meaning to every clinician.

### Layers and cells

The **epidermis** is a stratified squamous epithelium of keratinocytes. From deep to superficial: **stratum basale** (a single layer of dividing basal cells; **melanocytes** from the neural crest and **Merkel cells**, touch receptors, sit here), **stratum spinosum** (keratinocytes joined by **desmosomes**, which give the "spiny" look; Langerhans cells are antigen-presenting cells), **stratum granulosum** (keratohyalin granules, with filaggrin helping aggregate keratin; lamellar bodies release barrier lipids), **stratum lucidum** (only in thick skin of palms and soles) and **stratum corneum** (anucleate flattened keratinocytes). Epidermal turnover takes roughly a month.

The **dermoepidermal junction** anchors the epidermis through **hemidesmosomes** (collagen XVII or BP180, BP230, integrins) to the basement membrane. Antibodies against desmosomes cause **intraepidermal** blisters (pemphigus); antibodies against hemidesmosomes cause **subepidermal** blisters (pemphigoid).

The **dermis** has a thin **papillary** layer (loose collagen, capillaries, nerve endings) and a thick **reticular** layer (dense type I collagen and elastin). The **hypodermis** (subcutis) is fat and connective tissue. Adnexa: hair follicles, **sebaceous glands** (sebum; acne), **eccrine glands** (sweat, cholinergic sympathetic supply, widespread) and **apocrine glands** (axilla and groin; hidradenitis suppurativa).

**Wound healing:** hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation (granulation tissue with type III collagen), and remodeling (type I collagen replaces type III). Keloids grow beyond the original wound; hypertrophic scars do not.

### Describing a lesion

Name the **primary lesion**, then add **size, shape, color, surface changes, borders, arrangement and distribution**.

| Primary lesion | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Macule** | Flat color change, under 1 cm |
| **Patch** | Flat color change, 1 cm or more |
| **Papule** | Raised solid lesion under 1 cm |
| **Plaque** | Raised, flat-topped lesion 1 cm or more |
| **Nodule** | Solid, palpable lesion 1 cm or more, extending deeper into dermis or fat |
| **Vesicle / bulla** | Fluid-filled blister under / over 1 cm |
| **Pustule** | Pus-filled lesion |
| **Wheal** | Transient, pruritic edematous plaque (urticaria) |
| **Petechia / purpura / ecchymosis** | Non-blanching red-purple macules from bleeding: about 3 mm or less / larger / over 1 cm |

**Secondary changes** follow primary lesions: **scale**, **crust** (dried exudate), **erosion** (loss of epidermis, heals without scar), **ulcer** (loss through the dermis, heals with scar), **excoriation**, **lichenification** (thickened skin with accentuated lines from chronic rubbing), fissure, atrophy and scar.

**Arrangement:** linear, annular (ring), grouped (herpetiform), target, reticular, and **dermatomal** (herpes zoster). **Distribution:** sun-exposed, flexural versus extensor, intertriginous, acral, symmetric or asymmetric. **Special signs:** **Nikolsky sign** (epidermis shears off with lateral pressure; positive in pemphigus, staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome and toxic epidermal necrolysis), **Koebner phenomenon** (lesions appear at sites of trauma, as in psoriasis, lichen planus and vitiligo), **Auspitz sign** (pinpoint bleeding after scale removal, psoriasis), and dermographism.

### Burns

The **rule of nines** (adult): head and neck 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%. The patient's palm with fingers is about 1% of body surface. Only partial- and full-thickness burns count toward total body surface area (TBSA).

- **Superficial (first degree)**: painful, erythematous, dry, no blisters.
- **Superficial partial thickness**: painful, blistered, blanching, heals in 2-3 weeks.
- **Deep partial thickness**: mottled, less sensitive, may need grafting.
- **Full thickness (third degree)**: white or leathery, painless, does not blanch; needs excision and grafting.

The **Parkland formula** for adults gives lactated Ringer's in the first 24 hours:

$$V = 4\ \text{mL} \times \text{weight (kg)} \times \%\text{TBSA},$$

half in the first 8 hours from the time of injury and half over the next 16 hours. Titrate to urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/h. Secure the airway early in inhalation injury.

@example
title: Parkland fluid calculation
problem: A 70 kg man has a burn covering 30% TBSA. How much lactated Ringer's is given in the first 8 hours, and at what hourly rate?
step: Total volume in 24 hours is $4 \times 70 \times 30 = 8400$ mL.
step: Half of that, $4200$ mL, is given over the first 8 hours from the time of the burn.
step: The rate is $4200 / 8 = 525$ mL/h, then adjusted to a urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/h (35 mL/h for this man).

@mistakes
- Using the size cutoff incorrectly: a flat lesion at or over 1 cm is a patch, and a raised flat-topped lesion at or over 1 cm is a plaque.
- Counting first-degree (superficial) burns in the TBSA used for fluid calculation.
- Starting the 8-hour clock when the patient reaches the hospital rather than at the time of injury.
- Confusing erosion (epidermis only, no scar) with ulcer (into dermis, scars).
