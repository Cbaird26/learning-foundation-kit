@article
Metabolic bone diseases change the quantity, mineralization or turnover of bone without a local tumor or infection. Ask first whether the problem is **too little bone** (osteoporosis), **too little mineral in the bone** (osteomalacia), or **disordered turnover** (Paget disease, hyperparathyroidism, renal disease).

### Osteoporosis

Osteoporosis is reduced bone mass and microarchitectural deterioration with normal mineralization, so bone is fragile. Peak bone mass is reached in the late twenties to thirties; loss accelerates with **estrogen deficiency** at menopause, and trabecular bone (vertebrae, distal radius, femoral neck) is affected first. Risk factors: age, female sex, low body weight, smoking, alcohol, prior fragility fracture, family history, **glucocorticoids**, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, hypogonadism, celiac disease, inadequate calcium or vitamin D, and long-term use of some drugs (PPIs, anticonvulsants, aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation).

It is silent until a **fragility fracture** (vertebral compression with loss of height and kyphosis, hip, wrist). **Serum calcium, phosphate, PTH and alkaline phosphatase are normal**, and ALP rises only transiently after a fracture.

**Diagnosis.** Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA) reports a **T-score**, the number of standard deviations from the mean for a healthy young adult:

$$T = \frac{\text{BMD}_{\text{patient}} - \text{BMD}_{\text{young adult mean}}}{\text{SD}_{\text{young adult}}}.$$

A T-score at or below **-2.5** is osteoporosis; between -1.0 and -2.5 is osteopenia (low bone mass). A **hip or vertebral fragility fracture** establishes osteoporosis regardless of T-score. Use T-scores in postmenopausal women and men over 50; use Z-scores in younger patients. FRAX estimates 10-year fracture risk to guide treatment of osteopenia. The USPSTF recommends screening women 65 and older, and younger postmenopausal women at increased risk.

**Treatment.** Calcium and vitamin D adequacy, weight-bearing exercise, stopping smoking, fall prevention and treating secondary causes. Drug options:

- **Bisphosphonates** (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid) are first line. They bind hydroxyapatite and inhibit osteoclasts (nitrogen-containing ones inhibit farnesyl pyrophosphate synthase). Oral alendronate: take fasting with a full glass of water and stay upright for 30 minutes (esophagitis). Rare harms: osteonecrosis of the jaw, atypical femoral fracture, and hypocalcemia; avoid in severe kidney impairment and esophageal disorders. Zoledronic acid can cause an acute-phase reaction.
- **Denosumab** (anti-RANKL) twice yearly; stopping it abruptly causes rebound bone loss and vertebral fractures, so transition to another agent.
- **Anabolic agents**: teriparatide and abaloparatide (PTH analogs, given intermittently) and **romosozumab** (anti-sclerostin; boxed warning for cardiovascular events) for very high risk.
- **Raloxifene** (a SERM) lowers vertebral fracture risk and breast cancer risk but raises venous thromboembolism risk.

### Vitamin D, osteomalacia and rickets

Skin UVB converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to vitamin D3; the liver makes 25-hydroxyvitamin D (the storage form that is measured); the kidney **1-alpha-hydroxylase** makes active **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D**, stimulated by PTH and low phosphate. Deficiency (little sun, dark skin, malabsorption, liver or kidney disease, anticonvulsants) lowers calcium absorption, so PTH rises (secondary hyperparathyroidism), which wastes phosphate in urine. Osteoid cannot mineralize: this is **rickets** in children (growth plate widening, bowed legs, rachitic rosary, delayed fontanelle closure) and **osteomalacia** in adults (diffuse bone pain, proximal muscle weakness, **Looser zones**, or pseudofractures, on radiographs).

**Laboratory pattern:** low or low-normal calcium, **low phosphate, high PTH, high alkaline phosphatase, low 25-hydroxyvitamin D**. Treat with vitamin D and calcium. Phosphate-losing disorders (X-linked hypophosphatemic rickets with excess FGF23) cause low phosphate with normal PTH and need phosphate replacement and burosumab or calcitriol.

| | Osteoporosis | Osteomalacia |
|---|---|---|
| Defect | Less bone, normal mineralization | Normal bone amount, poor mineralization |
| Calcium, phosphate | Normal | Low or low-normal |
| PTH | Normal | High |
| ALP | Normal | High |
| Features | Fragility fractures, painless | Bone pain, proximal weakness, Looser zones |

### Other metabolic bone diseases

- **Paget disease of bone:** an older adult with elevated ALP but **normal calcium and phosphate**, excessive osteoclast activity followed by disorganized bone with a mosaic pattern; skull enlargement, hearing loss, bowing, fractures, high-output heart failure, and risk of osteosarcoma. Treat with a bisphosphonate if symptomatic.
- **Osteopetrosis:** osteoclast failure (carbonic anhydrase II or other defects); dense brittle bone, fractures, cranial nerve compression and pancytopenia from marrow crowding; "bone-in-bone" appearance.
- **Chronic kidney disease-mineral bone disorder:** low 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, hyperphosphatemia and hypocalcemia raise PTH, causing osteitis fibrosa cystica (subperiosteal resorption, brown tumors). Treat with phosphate binders, vitamin D analogs and calcimimetics.
- **Osteogenesis imperfecta:** type I collagen defect; fractures, blue sclerae, hearing loss, dentinogenesis imperfecta.

@example
title: Calculating a T-score
problem: A 66-year-old woman has a femoral neck bone mineral density of 0.80 g/cm2. The young adult reference mean is 1.00 g/cm2 with an SD of 0.10. Classify her bone density.
step: Subtract the mean from the patient value: $0.80 - 1.00 = -0.20$.
step: Divide by the SD: $-0.20 / 0.10 = -2.0$.
step: A T-score of $-2.0$ lies between $-1.0$ and $-2.5$, so she has osteopenia by DXA alone. Combine with her FRAX risk and fracture history, since a fragility fracture would make the diagnosis osteoporosis regardless.

@mistakes
- Expecting abnormal calcium, phosphate or ALP in osteoporosis; they are normal.
- Reading the T-score as a percentage; it is a count of standard deviations.
- Using a T-score in a premenopausal woman or a child instead of a Z-score.
- Stopping denosumab without transition therapy, or giving bisphosphonates to a patient who cannot sit upright or has an esophageal stricture.
