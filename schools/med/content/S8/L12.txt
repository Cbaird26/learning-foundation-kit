@article
Skin cancer is the most common cancer. Ultraviolet (UV) radiation is the dominant cause, producing DNA photoproducts (pyrimidine dimers). People with fair skin, many sunburns, tanning-bed use, immunosuppression (organ transplant), prior skin cancer, or defective nucleotide excision repair (xeroderma pigmentosum) are at higher risk.

### Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)

The most common skin cancer. A **pearly papule with telangiectasias and a rolled border**, often with central ulceration ("rodent ulcer"), on sun-exposed skin of the face, especially the nose. Driven by hedgehog pathway activation (**PTCH1** mutations; vismodegib for advanced disease). It grows slowly and **rarely metastasizes** but is locally destructive. Histology: nests of basaloid cells with **peripheral palisading**. Treat by surgical excision, or **Mohs micrographic surgery** for the face and other high-risk sites; superficial lesions can be treated with topical imiquimod or curettage.

### Actinic keratosis and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC)

**Actinic keratoses** are rough, scaly, erythematous papules on sun-damaged skin (precancerous; treat with cryotherapy or topical 5-fluorouracil). **SCC** appears as a firm, scaly or crusted nodule or ulcer, often on the lower lip, ear, dorsal hands, or in chronic wounds and scars (Marjolin ulcer); the incidence is much higher in transplant recipients. It arises from keratinocytes (keratin pearls, intercellular bridges), can metastasize to lymph nodes, and is treated by excision or Mohs surgery, with radiation or PD-1 inhibitors for advanced disease. **Keratoacanthoma** is a rapidly growing crateriform lesion regarded as a low-grade SCC.

### Melanoma

A malignancy of melanocytes with the highest mortality among skin cancers. Risk factors: intermittent intense sun exposure and blistering sunburns, many or atypical nevi, family history (CDKN2A), light skin. Subtypes: **superficial spreading** (most common), **nodular** (rapid vertical growth), **lentigo maligna** (older adults, chronic sun damage, face) and **acral lentiginous** (palms, soles, nail beds; the most common type in dark-skinned patients).

The **ABCDE** features that make a pigmented lesion suspicious:

- **A**symmetry
- **B**order irregularity
- **C**olor variegation (tan, brown, black, red, white, blue)
- **D**iameter over 6 mm (melanomas can be smaller)
- **E**volving: change in size, shape, color, symptoms (itching, bleeding)

The "ugly duckling" sign (a lesion that differs from the patient's other nevi) also helps.

**Diagnosis.** Perform a **full-thickness excisional biopsy with narrow (1-3 mm) margins**; punch or incisional biopsy is acceptable for large or cosmetically sensitive lesions. Avoid shave biopsy of a lesion suspected of melanoma because it can transect the base. Immunostains: S100, HMB-45, Melan-A (SOX10). **Breslow thickness** (depth in mm from the granular layer) is the **most important prognostic factor**, together with ulceration and nodal status. Treat with wide local excision with margins set by thickness (about 0.5-1 cm for in situ, 1 cm for up to 1 mm, 1-2 cm for 1-2 mm, 2 cm for over 2 mm). **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** is recommended for tumors thicker than 1 mm and considered for T1b tumors (0.8 to 1 mm thick, or thinner than 0.8 mm with ulceration); it is not routine for thinner, non-ulcerated tumors. Advanced disease: **BRAF V600E** mutations are targeted with BRAF inhibitors (vemurafenib, dabrafenib) with MEK inhibitors, and checkpoint inhibitors (anti-PD-1 nivolumab and pembrolizumab; anti-CTLA-4 ipilimumab) have transformed outcomes.

Other cancers: **Merkel cell carcinoma** (neuroendocrine, polyomavirus, violaceous nodule on sun-exposed skin of older adults, perinuclear dot CK20), **Kaposi sarcoma** (HHV-8, violaceous plaques in immunosuppression), and **mycosis fungoides** (cutaneous T-cell lymphoma, patches and plaques that mimic eczema).

**Prevention.** Broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming; protective clothing; avoid tanning beds; skin self-examination and clinical skin checks for high-risk patients.

### Dermatologic pharmacology

**Topical corticosteroids** are classed by potency from low (hydrocortisone 1%) to medium (triamcinolone 0.1%) to super-potent (clobetasol 0.05%). Ointments are more potent than creams. Use **low-potency** agents on the face, skin folds and in infants, and higher potency on thick skin (palms, soles). Adverse effects: skin atrophy, striae, telangiectasia, perioral dermatitis, acne, tachyphylaxis, and systemic absorption with HPA axis suppression; super-potent agents are limited to about two weeks of continuous use.

The **fingertip unit (FTU)** is the amount of cream squeezed from a tube along the distal phalanx of an adult index finger, about **0.5 g**, and covers an area of about two adult palms with fingers (about 2% of BSA).

Other agents: **topical retinoids** (tretinoin, adapalene) and **systemic retinoids** (isotretinoin, acitretin) are teratogenic; **methotrexate** requires folate and monitoring; **calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus) are steroid-sparing topicals; **dapsone** causes hemolysis in G6PD deficiency and methemoglobinemia; **doxycycline** causes photosensitivity; **terbinafine** can cause hepatotoxicity; TNF, IL-17 and IL-23 inhibitors require screening for latent TB (and hepatitis B screening before TNF inhibitors); dupilumab (IL-4/13 pathway) does not require TB screening.

@example
title: Quantity of topical steroid
problem: A patient needs a medium-potency steroid applied twice daily to eczema covering 8% of BSA for 7 days. How many grams of cream are needed (one FTU = 0.5 g covers 2% BSA)?
step: Number of FTUs per application is $8 / 2 = 4$ FTUs.
step: Grams per application is $4 \times 0.5 = 2$ g, so per day, $2 \times 2 = 4$ g.
step: Over 7 days the patient needs $4 \times 7 = 28$ g.

@mistakes
- Using a super-potent steroid on the face or in skin folds, or continuing it for more than a couple of weeks.
- Doing a shave biopsy on a lesion suspected of melanoma, which can destroy the Breslow depth.
- Dismissing a lesion because it is under 6 mm; amelanotic and small melanomas exist, and evolution matters most.
- Treating BCC as metastasis-prone; it is locally invasive, while SCC and melanoma metastasize.
