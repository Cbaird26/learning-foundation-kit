@article
Osteoarthritis (OA) and back pain are the two commonest reasons people see a clinician for musculoskeletal pain. Most cases are benign and managed conservatively. The skill is to recognize the small fraction that is not: infection, cancer, fracture and neurologic compression.

### Osteoarthritis

OA is a disease of the whole joint driven by cartilage breakdown and abnormal remodeling, not simple "wear." Mechanical load, age, obesity, prior injury and genetics tip chondrocytes toward degrading the type II collagen and aggrecan matrix, so cartilage thins and bone responds. Inflammation is mild and local; it is not primarily autoimmune.

**Presentation.** Gradual pain that worsens with use and improves with rest, **morning stiffness under 30 minutes**, bony enlargement, crepitus and reduced range of motion. Typical sites are the knees, hips, **first carpometacarpal joint**, spine, and the **DIP** (Heberden nodes) and **PIP** (Bouchard nodes) joints. OA spares the MCP joints and wrists unless there is another cause (hemochromatosis arthropathy or CPPD).

**Imaging.** Radiographs show **asymmetric joint space narrowing, osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis and subchondral cysts**. Erosions and periarticular osteopenia point to inflammatory arthritis instead. Labs (ESR, CRP) are normal; synovial fluid is non-inflammatory (WBC under 2,000/microL).

**Management.** Start with **exercise, weight loss** (even modest loss reduces knee load) and physical therapy. Pharmacologic options: **topical NSAIDs** (especially for the knee), oral NSAIDs at the lowest effective dose and shortest duration (with attention to GI, renal and cardiovascular risk), duloxetine for chronic pain, and intra-articular glucocorticoid injection for flares. Acetaminophen has modest benefit and recommendations differ between guidelines. **Joint replacement** is reserved for severe pain and disability after conservative treatment. Opioids and hyaluronic acid injections are not recommended routinely.

### Low back pain

Most acute low back pain is **nonspecific mechanical** pain from muscles, ligaments and discs, and 90% improves within weeks. Advise staying active, heat, NSAIDs, and avoidance of bed rest. **Imaging is not needed in the first six weeks** unless red flags are present, because incidental findings are common and do not change management.

### Red flags

These findings raise the chance of a serious cause and justify earlier imaging and labs:

- **Cancer:** history of malignancy, unexplained weight loss, age over 50 with new pain, pain at night or at rest.
- **Infection:** fever, IV drug use, recent bacteremia or spinal procedure, immunosuppression (vertebral osteomyelitis or epidural abscess).
- **Fracture:** significant trauma, osteoporosis, prolonged glucocorticoid use, age over 70.
- **Neurologic compromise:** progressive or severe motor weakness, **saddle anesthesia, urinary retention or overflow incontinence, fecal incontinence, bilateral leg symptoms** (cauda equina syndrome).
- Age under 20 with persistent pain, and an inflammatory pattern (age under 40, insidious onset, morning stiffness, improvement with exercise, nocturnal pain; see ankylosing spondylitis next week).

**Cauda equina syndrome** is a surgical emergency, usually from a massive central disc herniation at L4-L5 or L5-S1: low back pain, bilateral sciatica or weakness, perineal sensory loss, **urinary retention** (confirm with a bladder scan; a high post-void residual is key), and reduced anal tone. Order **emergent MRI** and neurosurgical decompression.

### Radiculopathy

A herniated disc, most commonly posterolateral at L4-L5 or L5-S1, compresses the traversing root. A positive **straight leg raise** (pain below the knee at 30-70 degrees) is sensitive; the **crossed** straight leg raise is specific.

| Root | Weakness | Sensory loss | Reflex |
|---|---|---|---|
| L4 | Knee extension (quadriceps) | Medial leg | Patellar reduced |
| L5 | Great toe and foot dorsiflexion, hip abduction (foot drop) | Dorsum of foot | None reliably reduced |
| S1 | Plantarflexion, toe walking | Lateral foot and sole | Achilles reduced |

### Spinal stenosis and others

Lumbar **spinal stenosis** in older adults causes **neurogenic claudication**: bilateral buttock and leg pain on walking that eases by **sitting or leaning forward** (the "shopping cart sign"), with normal pulses. **Vascular claudication** is relieved by standing still and comes with diminished pulses. **Spondylolysis** (a pars defect) in a young gymnast and **spondylolisthesis** (forward slippage, commonly L5 on S1) cause pain with extension.

### Likelihood ratios

A positive result's weight is the **positive likelihood ratio**: $LR+ = \dfrac{\text{sensitivity}}{1 - \text{specificity}}$. A higher LR+ shifts probability further toward disease.

@example
title: Localizing a radiculopathy
problem: A 45-year-old warehouse worker has 10 days of low back pain radiating down the lateral leg to the dorsum of the foot. He trips on his toes. Examination shows weak great toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion, with a normal Achilles reflex. Which root is affected and what is the first-line management?
step: Weakness of ankle and great toe dorsiflexion with a foot drop is the L5 pattern.
step: The dorsum of the foot is the L5 dermatome, and a normal Achilles reflex argues against S1.
step: No red flags: no bilateral symptoms, saddle anesthesia, retention, fever or cancer history. Without imaging, treat conservatively with staying active, NSAIDs and physical therapy and review for progression; MRI is indicated if severe or progressive weakness develops or symptoms persist beyond about six weeks.

@example
title: Computing a positive likelihood ratio
problem: A physical examination maneuver has a sensitivity of 80% and a specificity of 60% for a herniated disc. What is its positive likelihood ratio?
step: Convert to proportions: sensitivity $0.80$, specificity $0.60$.
step: False-positive rate is $1 - 0.60 = 0.40$.
step: $LR+ = 0.80 / 0.40 = 2$. A positive result only modestly raises the probability of disease.

@mistakes
- Ordering MRI for every patient with acute low back pain; it is not indicated without red flags in the first six weeks.
- Missing cauda equina syndrome because strength is preserved; retention and saddle anesthesia may come first.
- Calling OA an inflammatory arthritis, or attributing MCP and wrist involvement to OA without looking for hemochromatosis or CPPD.
- Reversing the claudication patterns: neurogenic claudication improves with forward flexion, vascular claudication with standing rest.
