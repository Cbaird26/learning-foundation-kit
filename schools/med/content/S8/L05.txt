@article
A hot swollen joint in one place is an emergency until you have excluded infection. Arthrocentesis is the key test, and learning to read the fluid is the core skill of this week.

### Uric acid and gout

Purines are metabolized to hypoxanthine, xanthine and then **uric acid** by **xanthine oxidase**. Humans lack uricase, so urate is the end product, and it is excreted mainly by the kidney. When serum urate exceeds its solubility (about 6.8 mg/dL), **monosodium urate (MSU)** crystals can deposit. Crystals trigger the NLRP3 inflammasome and IL-1 release, which recruits neutrophils and produces the flare.

**Underexcretion** causes about 90% of cases: chronic kidney disease, thiazide and loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, cyclosporine, alcohol, and lactic acidosis. **Overproduction** occurs with a purine-rich diet, fructose, high cell turnover (tumor lysis, hemolysis, psoriasis, myeloproliferative disease), and enzyme defects: **Lesch-Nyhan syndrome** (X-linked HGPRT deficiency: gout, intellectual disability, dystonia, self-mutilation) and PRPP synthetase overactivity.

**Presentation.** A sudden, very painful, red, warm monoarthritis, often at night, classically of the **first metatarsophalangeal joint (podagra)**, but also ankle, knee and wrist. Untreated disease leads to **tophi** (chalky deposits in the ear helix, tendons and joints), erosions with overhanging edges, and uric acid kidney stones. Serum urate can be **normal or low during a flare**, so it does not exclude gout, and a high urate alone does not prove it.

### Calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD, pseudogout)

CPPD affects older adults, usually the knee or wrist, with **chondrocalcinosis** (linear calcification of menisci or the triangular fibrocartilage) on radiographs. Screen for associated conditions: **hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia**. Basic calcium phosphate crystals cause Milwaukee shoulder (they are not birefringent; they stain with alizarin red).

### Crystal identification

Under compensated polarized light:

| Crystal | Shape | Birefringence | Color when parallel to compensator axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| MSU (gout) | Needle | Strongly negative | Yellow (blue when perpendicular) |
| CPP (pseudogout) | Rhomboid | Weakly positive | Blue (yellow when perpendicular) |

### Reading synovial fluid

| Category | Appearance | WBC (per microL) | PMN % | Typical causes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Normal | Clear, viscous | under 200 | under 25 | none |
| Noninflammatory | Clear to yellow | 200-2,000 | under 25 | OA, trauma, SLE |
| Inflammatory | Yellow, turbid | 2,000-50,000 | over 50 | RA, crystals, spondyloarthropathy |
| Septic | Turbid, purulent | over 50,000 (often over 100,000) | over 90 | bacterial infection |
| Hemorrhagic | Bloody | variable | variable | trauma, hemophilia, anticoagulation |

Always send **Gram stain and culture** when infection is possible. Counts overlap: gout can reach 50,000-100,000 and septic arthritis can be lower in the immunosuppressed or partially treated. **Crystals and infection can coexist**, so finding crystals does not exclude infection. Leukocyte count and differential (cells per microL) are the main numbers; the absolute neutrophil count is WBC multiplied by the neutrophil fraction.

### Treatment of acute gout

Start within 24 hours. Options: **NSAIDs** (if no CKD, ulcer or heart failure), **colchicine** (low-dose regimen; diarrhea is the main side effect; dose reductions are needed for kidney or liver impairment and with CYP3A4 or P-glycoprotein inhibitors such as clarithromycin), or **glucocorticoids** (oral prednisone or an intra-articular injection when infection is excluded; ideal when NSAIDs and colchicine are unsuitable). Do not stop an established urate-lowering drug during a flare.

### Long-term urate lowering

Indications: recurrent flares (two or more per year), tophi, erosions, CKD stage 3 or worse, or urate stones. **Allopurinol** (a xanthine oxidase inhibitor) is first line; start low (for example 100 mg daily, lower in CKD; a typical adult study value to be verified against current references before any clinical use) and titrate to a serum urate **below 6 mg/dL**. Severe hypersensitivity is associated with **HLA-B*58:01** in some ancestries, so testing is recommended for those at higher risk. Febuxostat is an alternative with a boxed warning for cardiovascular death. **Probenecid** (uricosuric) fails in poor renal function and is contraindicated with urate stones. **Pegloticase** (recombinant uricase) is for refractory tophaceous gout; screen for G6PD deficiency. Starting urate-lowering therapy can provoke flares, so give anti-inflammatory prophylaxis (low-dose colchicine, NSAID or prednisone) for the first 3-6 months. Counsel on limiting alcohol (especially beer), sugary drinks and organ meats, and review diuretics.

@example
title: Interpreting an acute knee effusion
problem: A 67-year-old woman with hypertension on hydrochlorothiazide has a hot swollen knee. Aspirate: cloudy yellow, 28,000 WBC/microL, 86% neutrophils, rhomboid crystals with weakly positive birefringence, Gram stain negative. What is the diagnosis and the next step?
step: A count of 28,000 with mostly neutrophils is an inflammatory effusion, not noninflammatory; it is below the usual septic threshold but does not exclude infection.
step: Rhomboid, weakly positive birefringent crystals are calcium pyrophosphate: acute CPP crystal arthritis (pseudogout).
step: Send culture to be sure infection is absent; treat with intra-articular glucocorticoid, NSAID or colchicine as appropriate, and screen for hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism and hypomagnesemia.

@mistakes
- Excluding gout because serum urate is normal during a flare.
- Reversing crystal birefringence: needles are negative (yellow parallel), rhomboids positive (blue parallel).
- Stopping suspicion of infection once crystals are seen; they can coexist.
- Starting allopurinol at a full dose without flare prophylaxis, or giving an NSAID to a patient with advanced CKD.

prompt: A 24-year-old sexually active woman has 3 days of migrating joint pain, a painful swollen right wrist with tenosynovitis, and several pustules on the hands. Fever is 38.4 C. Wrist fluid has 45,000 WBC/microL, 88% neutrophils and a negative Gram stain. Which is the best next step?
