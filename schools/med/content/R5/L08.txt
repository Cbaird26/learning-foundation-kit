@article
Two situations take most psychiatric consults on the medical floors: the patient who is agitated and the patient who refuses care. The approach to each is structured.

### Acute agitation

**Find the cause while you protect safety.** Agitation can come from delirium, hypoxia, hypoglycemia, pain, urinary retention, intoxication or withdrawal, a medical illness, medication effects, psychosis, mania, or an anxiety or personality disorder. Check vital signs and glucose first.

1. **Verbal de-escalation and environment:** a calm voice, short statements, offering choices, asking what would help, a low-stimulation room, enough personnel and distance, and responding to pain, hunger and toileting needs. This works more often than medication.
2. **Oral medication** if the patient will accept it: a second-generation antipsychotic (for example olanzapine or risperidone), with or without a benzodiazepine (lorazepam). Benzodiazepines are preferred when intoxication or withdrawal from alcohol or sedatives is the cause and avoided in delirium otherwise.
3. **Parenteral medication** if there is imminent danger. Typical adult doses for study (check current references before clinical use): haloperidol 5 mg IM often with lorazepam 2 mg IM (and sometimes diphenhydramine or benztropine to prevent dystonia), olanzapine 10 mg IM, or ketamine in some emergency departments. **Avoid giving IM olanzapine together with a parenteral benzodiazepine**, because of additive sedation and possible cardiorespiratory depression. Check the ECG when possible because haloperidol and other antipsychotics prolong the QTc.
4. **Restraint** is a last resort for imminent danger when less restrictive measures have failed. It requires a physician order, face-to-face evaluation, continuous observation, regular reassessment of the need and release as soon as safe, according to hospital policy and regulation.

The corrected QT by the Bazett formula is
$$\text{QTc} = \frac{\text{QT}}{\sqrt{\text{RR}}},$$
with the QT in milliseconds and RR in seconds (RR = 60/heart rate). A QTc above about 500 ms or an increase of more than 60 ms raises the risk of torsades de pointes; correct potassium and magnesium and review other QT-prolonging drugs.

### Decision-making capacity

Capacity is the ability to make a particular decision. It is **decision-specific and time-specific**, presumed present in adults until shown otherwise, and can be assessed by any treating clinician. A psychiatry consult helps in complex or disputed cases. *Competence* is a legal determination made by a court.

The four abilities (Appelbaum) are:

1. **Expressing a choice** that is consistent.
2. **Understanding** the relevant information (diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives, risks and benefits, and the consequences of refusing).
3. **Appreciating** that the information applies to oneself.
4. **Reasoning** by weighing options in line with one's values.

The more severe the consequences of the decision, the more confidence you need in the assessment (the "sliding scale"). The presence of a psychiatric diagnosis, an unwise decision, disagreement with the team or refusal of a recommendation does not alone mean incapacity. Treat reversible contributors (delirium, pain, sedatives) and reassess. Document the specific decision, the information given, the patient's answers on each ability and your conclusion.

### If the patient lacks capacity

Find a **surrogate**: the health care proxy named in an advance directive, otherwise by state law usually the spouse or partner, adult children, parents, then siblings. The surrogate decides according to the patient's known wishes (substituted judgment) and otherwise their best interests. In a true emergency without a surrogate and without time, treat to prevent serious harm under the **emergency exception** (implied consent). A patient who wants to leave against medical advice but has capacity may leave after you discuss risks, and the discharge paperwork is not a substitute for that conversation.

@example
title: Calculating the QTc before a haloperidol dose
problem: An agitated patient has a QT of 380 ms at a heart rate of 90/min. What is the QTc by Bazett, and what do you conclude?
step: RR interval = 60/90 = 0.667 s, and its square root is about 0.816.
step: QTc = 380/0.816, about 465 ms.
step: A QTc of 465 ms is mildly prolonged in a man (above about 450 ms) but well under 500 ms. Haloperidol is not contraindicated, but use the lowest effective dose, correct electrolytes, and repeat the ECG.

@mistakes
- Calling a patient "incapacitated" because of a psychiatric diagnosis or an unwise choice, without testing the four abilities.
- Giving IM olanzapine within an hour of parenteral lorazepam.
- Using benzodiazepines to sedate delirium that is not from sedative or alcohol withdrawal.
- Continuing restraints without reassessment or documentation.

Assess capacity, document the four abilities, and state what to do.
solution: She has capacity for this decision. She expresses a clear, stable choice (refusal). She understands the diagnosis, the proposed surgery, the alternatives and the consequences. She appreciates that these apply to her, and she reasons from long-standing values about her death and quality of life. The daughter's disagreement and the fact that the choice seems unwise to the team do not negate capacity. Document the specific decision, the information given, her answers on each ability and the conclusion. Respect the refusal, offer palliative options (pain control by nerve block or analgesia, hospice or palliative care involvement), revisit the decision if her condition changes, and involve the daughter in a supportive way without making her the decision maker.
