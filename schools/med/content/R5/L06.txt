@article
Delirium is an acute disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days and fluctuates. It is common in hospitalized older adults, raises mortality and risk of dementia, and is often missed. Dementia is a chronic, progressive decline in more than one cognitive domain that interferes with independence. They often coexist, and dementia is the biggest risk factor for delirium.

### Delirium

The **Confusion Assessment Method** is positive when there is (1) acute onset and fluctuating course **and** (2) inattention (for example, cannot say the months backward or lose the thread of a conversation) **and** either (3) disorganized thinking **or** (4) altered level of consciousness. Subtypes are hyperactive, hypoactive (frequently missed) and mixed.

Search for the cause with a structured list: infection (urinary, pneumonia), medications (anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, opioids, steroids, **diphenhydramine**), metabolic (sodium, glucose, calcium, uremia, hepatic), hypoxia, withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines), pain, urinary retention and constipation, sleep deprivation, stroke, seizures, and intracranial causes. Check vital signs, glucose, electrolytes, a complete blood count, urinalysis when indicated, oxygenation and a medication review.

Management is **treating the cause plus nonpharmacologic care:** reorientation, glasses and hearing aids, daytime light and normal sleep, early mobilization, hydration, family presence, and avoiding restraints, catheters and tethers. Medications are reserved for severe distress or danger: a low-dose antipsychotic such as haloperidol briefly, with an ECG for QTc. **Avoid benzodiazepines** unless the delirium is from alcohol or sedative withdrawal. No medication prevents delirium reliably, and nonpharmacologic bundles do.

### Dementia syndromes

| Condition | Key features | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Alzheimer disease | Insidious amnestic decline, then language and visuospatial loss | Medial temporal and parietal atrophy; amyloid and tau |
| Vascular dementia | Stepwise decline, focal signs, vascular risk factors | Control blood pressure and risk factors |
| Lewy body dementia | **Fluctuating cognition, well-formed visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behavior disorder** | Severe sensitivity to antipsychotics; dementia within 1 year of parkinsonism |
| Frontotemporal dementia | Behavior change, disinhibition, apathy or language loss; relatively preserved memory | Younger onset, often 50s to 60s |
| Normal pressure hydrocephalus | **Gait disturbance first, then urinary incontinence, then dementia** | Ventriculomegaly; improvement after a large-volume lumbar puncture predicts shunt response |
| Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease | Rapid decline over weeks to months with myoclonus | RT-QuIC, MRI diffusion restriction, periodic sharp waves |

Screen with the **Montreal Cognitive Assessment** (score below 26 out of 30 is abnormal; add 1 point for 12 or fewer years of education) or the Mini-Mental State Examination (score below 24 out of 30 suggests impairment). Evaluate reversible contributors: depression, hypothyroidism, vitamin B12 deficiency, medications, alcohol, sleep apnea, hearing and vision loss, and structural lesions with MRI or CT. **Depression** can mimic dementia (patients complain more than they perform poorly).

Treatment of Alzheimer disease: **cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine; side effects include nausea, bradycardia, syncope and vivid dreams) for mild to moderate disease, and **memantine** (NMDA antagonist) for moderate to severe disease. Antiamyloid antibody therapies exist for early disease with risks of amyloid-related imaging abnormalities and require MRI monitoring. For behavioral symptoms use nonpharmacologic strategies first; antipsychotics carry a boxed warning for increased mortality in older adults with dementia. Address safety (driving, firearms, medications), caregiver support, advance care planning and capacity.

### Guillain–Barré syndrome versus myasthenic crisis

Both cause generalized weakness with respiratory failure, and both need serial forced vital capacity and inspiratory pressure. The bedside distinction:

| Feature | Guillain–Barré syndrome | Myasthenic crisis |
|---|---|---|
| Reflexes | Absent or reduced | Normal |
| Sensory symptoms | Common (paresthesias, pain) | None |
| Pattern | Ascending, symmetric; autonomic dysfunction | Fatigable; ptosis, diplopia, bulbar weakness |
| Precipitant | Preceding infection | Infection, new drug, surgery, steroid start |
| Key tests | Albuminocytologic dissociation; demyelination | Acetylcholine receptor antibodies; decrement on repetitive stimulation |

Both are treated with IV immunoglobulin or plasma exchange; glucocorticoids help myasthenia (with caution because of transient worsening) but not GBS.

@example
title: Applying the Confusion Assessment Method
problem: An 82-year-old woman, 2 days after hip surgery, is calm in the morning and agitated at night. She cannot recite the days of the week backward. Her speech is rambling. Is this delirium, and what do you do?
step: Acute onset and a fluctuating course: present. Inattention (cannot do the days backward): present.
step: Disorganized thinking (rambling speech): present. All required CAM features are met, so this is delirium.
step: Look for causes: ask about new medications (opioids, diphenhydramine), urinary retention, infection, hypoxia, pain, sodium and glucose.
step: Treat the cause and use nonpharmacologic care. Avoid benzodiazepines and restraints; use low-dose haloperidol only if she is in severe distress or danger.

@mistakes
- Calling an acute change "baseline dementia" without searching for a precipitant.
- Using benzodiazepines or diphenhydramine for sleep or agitation in an older hospitalized patient.
- Treating behavioral symptoms of Lewy body dementia with a typical antipsychotic.
- Missing hypoactive delirium because the patient is quiet.

@practice self
prompt: A 45-year-old woman with myasthenia gravis, recently started on a fluoroquinolone for a urinary infection, has progressive generalized weakness, slurred speech and shallow breathing; her reflexes are normal and there is no sensory loss. A second patient has ascending weakness, absent reflexes and paresthesias 2 weeks after gastroenteritis. Compare the two presentations, state what to monitor in both, and state what treatment is shared and what is not.
solution: The first patient has myasthenic crisis (fatigable bulbar and respiratory weakness, normal reflexes, no sensory loss, a precipitating drug, known myasthenia); the second has Guillain–Barré syndrome (ascending, areflexic, sensory symptoms, post-infectious). Both are respiratory emergencies: monitor forced vital capacity and maximal inspiratory pressure every few hours in a monitored bed, with intubation for vital capacity below 20 mL/kg, inspiratory pressure weaker than -30 cm H2O or expiratory pressure below 40 cm H2O, bulbar failure or a falling trend. Both respond to plasma exchange or IV immunoglobulin. Stop the fluoroquinolone for the first patient and avoid other offending drugs; glucocorticoids are used cautiously in myasthenia but not in GBS. Confirm with antibodies and repetitive stimulation for myasthenia, or cerebrospinal fluid protein and nerve conduction for GBS.
rubric: Uses reflexes, sensory involvement, fatigability and precipitant to separate the two.
