@article
This lesson combines two skills on the psychiatric consult service: keeping a suicidal patient safe and knowing when the law allows treatment without consent. It ends with an alcohol-withdrawal plan, because medical and psychiatric care overlap.

### Safety planning

After you have assessed risk (see the psychiatric consult lesson), a patient who is not being admitted needs a plan for the next days. The best-studied tool is the **Stanley–Brown Safety Planning Intervention**, written collaboratively in the patient's own words:

1. **Warning signs:** thoughts, moods or situations that start a crisis.
2. **Internal coping strategies:** things the patient can do alone (walking, music, breathing exercises).
3. **People and social settings** that provide distraction.
4. **People the patient can ask for help.**
5. **Professionals and agencies** to contact, including the clinic, emergency services and **988**, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988 in the United States).
6. **Making the environment safer:** reducing access to lethal means, including securing or temporarily removing firearms and limiting stockpiled medications, with the patient and family.

A "no-suicide contract" is not an effective substitute. Caring follow-up contacts after emergency visits, outpatient therapy targeting suicide (for example, cognitive behavioral therapy for suicide prevention or dialectical behavior therapy) and treating the underlying disorder reduce risk. Lithium and clozapine have evidence for lowering suicide risk in specific illnesses. Use person-first, nonjudgmental language, ask about reasons for living, and involve the patient's supports with permission.

### Voluntary and involuntary admission

Most psychiatric hospitalization should be voluntary. Involuntary treatment is governed by state law, so details differ. The usual criteria for an emergency hold (often 72 hours, sometimes shorter or longer by state) are a **mental disorder** together with one of: **danger to self**, **danger to others**, or **grave disability** (being unable to meet basic needs such as food, shelter or safety because of the illness). Longer commitment requires a court hearing, the right to counsel and review. Many states also have outpatient commitment.

Key points:

- **Capacity and commitment are different questions.** A hold lets the system detain a patient for evaluation and treatment of mental illness; whether a particular patient can refuse a particular treatment is a separate matter in many states, and non-emergency medication over objection often needs a further process. Emergency treatment of imminent danger is allowed.
- A medical student never holds or restrains a patient alone: bring in the supervising physician and follow hospital policy and local law.
- **Medical emergencies come first:** screen for delirium, intoxication and injury; do not "medically clear" by assumption.
- The federal law EMTALA requires a medical screening and stabilization of emergency conditions.
- A patient who is intoxicated may need observation until sober before a psychiatric assessment is reliable.

### Confidentiality and duty to protect

Confidentiality is broad but has limits. When a patient makes a serious threat against an identifiable person, many states recognize a duty to **protect** (Tarasoff principle), which may mean notifying the person, the police or both, or hospitalizing the patient; some states make it permissive or require it. Mandatory reporting also applies to child and elder abuse. Share the minimum information needed and document reasoning, and consult your supervisor and the hospital's legal office.

### Alcohol withdrawal on a symptom scale

A typical symptom-triggered protocol (see the withdrawal lesson): assess with the CIWA-Ar every 1 to 4 hours, give a benzodiazepine for scores above the protocol threshold (often 8 to 10), reassess an hour after each dose, give thiamine and folate, and use lorazepam if there is liver disease. A rising score despite large cumulative doses, a first seizure, hallucinations with unstable vital signs, or an inability to take oral medication means escalation to ICU-level care and possibly phenobarbital. Plan treatment of alcohol use disorder and a screen for self-harm risk: alcohol use disorder increases suicide risk.

@example
title: Disposition for suicidal ideation
problem: A 32-year-old man tells his primary care physician he has been thinking of ending his life and describes a specific plan and intent to act tonight. He refuses to go to the hospital. What now?
step: He has a serious mental disorder concern with high acute risk: a plan and imminent intent. A safety plan alone is insufficient.
step: Do not leave him alone. Call your supervising physician, security and emergency services, and arrange transfer to an emergency psychiatric evaluation.
step: If he will not go voluntarily and meets the criteria in your state (danger to self due to a mental disorder), an emergency hold is appropriate, initiated by authorized personnel under local law.
step: Reduce access to means with his supports, communicate the situation with the receiving team, and document your assessment and reasoning.

@mistakes
- Relying on a "no-suicide contract" in place of risk assessment and a collaborative safety plan.
- Confusing an emergency hold with a finding of incapacity for every decision.
- Initiating a hold or restraint as a student rather than escalating to the supervising physician.
- Overlooking withdrawal or intoxication as the cause of a "psychiatric" presentation.

@practice self
prompt: A 52-year-old man with cirrhosis is admitted for an abdominal infection and is 36 hours from his last drink. He has tremor, sweating and anxiety, a CIWA-Ar of 14, a heart rate of 108/min, and he is oriented. He had a withdrawal seizure two years ago. Write a plan for his withdrawal management.
solution: He has moderate withdrawal and high risk for complications because of the previous seizure. Use symptom-triggered benzodiazepines with the CIWA-Ar every 1 to 2 hours: choose lorazepam (or oxazepam) because of cirrhosis, with dosing per protocol for scores of about 8 to 10 or more, and reassess an hour after each dose. Give thiamine (high dose if Wernicke is suspected) before or with glucose, folate and a multivitamin, and replace magnesium and potassium. Monitor vitals, mental status and glucose; keep seizure precautions. Escalate to the ICU or add phenobarbital for escalating requirements, seizures, delirium or unstable vitals. Treat the infection and avoid hepatotoxic drugs. Before discharge offer treatment for alcohol use disorder, screen for depression and suicidal thoughts, and plan follow-up.
