@article
Intoxication and withdrawal fill emergency departments and medical wards. The consult skills are recognizing the syndrome, using a structured scale to dose treatment, and not missing coexisting conditions such as head injury, infection and suicidal intent.

### Alcohol withdrawal

Withdrawal follows a fall in blood alcohol after chronic heavy use, through reduced GABA activity and glutamate overactivity.

| Time after last drink | Syndrome |
|---|---|
| 6 to 24 hours | Tremor, anxiety, sweating, nausea, insomnia, tachycardia |
| 12 to 48 hours | Withdrawal seizures; alcoholic hallucinosis (hallucinations with a clear sensorium) |
| 48 to 96 hours | **Delirium tremens**: confusion, hallucinations, fever, marked autonomic hyperactivity; mortality without treatment is substantial |

Risk factors for severe withdrawal: prior seizures or delirium tremens, very high daily intake, acute medical illness, abnormal electrolytes, older age and a high initial score. The **Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol, revised (CIWA-Ar)** scores ten items: nausea and vomiting, tremor, paroxysmal sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile disturbances, auditory disturbances, visual disturbances and headache (each 0 to 7) plus orientation (0 to 4), for a maximum of 67. Total below 8 to 10 is mild or absent, roughly 8 to 15 moderate, and above 15 severe; protocols dose a benzodiazepine for scores of about 8 to 10 or more. The scale needs a patient who can communicate, so it is unreliable in delirium or intubated patients.

**Treatment:** a benzodiazepine, given symptom-triggered by scale in most patients (fixed-schedule dosing for high-risk patients or those who cannot be scored). Long-acting drugs (diazepam, chlordiazepoxide) give smooth coverage; **lorazepam or oxazepam** are preferred with cirrhosis, advanced age or respiratory disease because they do not rely on hepatic oxidation. Phenobarbital is an alternative or adjunct in some centers. Give **thiamine before or with glucose** (patients with a risk of deficiency): high doses (commonly 500 mg IV three times daily) for suspected **Wernicke encephalopathy** (confusion, ophthalmoplegia and nystagmus, ataxia), plus folate, multivitamin, magnesium and potassium repletion. Treat seizures with benzodiazepines (phenytoin is not effective for withdrawal seizures). Evaluate for pancreatitis, hepatitis, GI bleeding, and head trauma. Many patients benefit afterwards from medication for alcohol use disorder: naltrexone (avoid with opioid use or acute hepatitis), acamprosate (adjust for renal impairment), gabapentin or topiramate, disulfiram.

### Opioids

**Overdose:** respiratory depression, miosis, coma. Support ventilation first, then give **naloxone** (typical adult starting dose for study 0.4 to 2 mg IV, IM or intranasal 4 mg, repeating as needed). Naloxone lasts shorter than many opioids, so observe for re-sedation, and use an infusion for long-acting agents or large doses. Provide naloxone and overdose education at discharge.

**Withdrawal:** yawning, rhinorrhea, piloerection, myalgias, diarrhea, dilated pupils, agitation; unpleasant but rarely life-threatening. The **Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS)** guides treatment. **Buprenorphine** (partial agonist) for opioid use disorder should be started only after objective withdrawal is evident (commonly COWS 8 to 12 or higher) because it can **precipitate withdrawal** if full agonists occupy the receptors (low-dose "micro" induction is an alternative that avoids waiting for withdrawal); methadone is the alternative; clonidine eases autonomic symptoms. Naltrexone requires a period of opioid abstinence.

### Other sedatives and toxins

- **Benzodiazepine withdrawal** is dangerous (seizures, delirium); taper slowly. Flumazenil is generally avoided because it can precipitate seizures in habitual users.
- **Acetaminophen:** the most common serious overdose; early symptoms are minimal. Measure a level at least 4 hours after ingestion and plot it on the **Rumack–Matthew nomogram** (treatment line: 150 µg/mL at 4 hours); give **N-acetylcysteine** (typical IV load 150 mg/kg over 1 hour in the traditional 21-hour three-bag regimen of 150, 50 and 100 mg/kg; newer two-bag regimens exist) when the level is above the treatment line, or when the time of ingestion is unknown with a detectable level or elevated transaminases.
- **Salicylate:** tinnitus, tachypnea (respiratory alkalosis with anion gap acidosis), fever; treat with IV sodium bicarbonate and dialysis in severe cases.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants:** anticholinergic features, seizures, wide QRS; treat with **sodium bicarbonate**.
- **Toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol):** inebriation with a high anion gap acidosis and **osmolar gap**; treat with fomepizole and dialysis.

Serum osmolality is estimated as $2\,[\text{Na}] + \text{glucose}/18 + \text{BUN}/2.8$ (mg/dL units). The osmolar gap is measured minus calculated osmolality, and a gap above about 10 raises concern for an unmeasured osmole such as a toxic alcohol (or ethanol).

@example
title: Using the CIWA-Ar to dose
problem: A 56-year-old man, 30 hours after his last drink, has the following CIWA-Ar item scores: nausea 2, tremor 4, sweats 3, anxiety 3, agitation 2, tactile 0, auditory 0, visual 1, headache 2, orientation 0. Total, and what do you do?
step: Total = 2 + 4 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 0 + 0 + 1 + 2 + 0 = 17.
step: A total above 15 is severe; give a benzodiazepine per protocol (for example lorazepam in a patient with liver disease), and reassess in an hour.
step: Give thiamine and folate, correct magnesium and potassium, and monitor for seizures and delirium.
step: Continue symptom-triggered dosing until scores stay low, then stop; consider ICU consultation if large cumulative doses are needed.

@mistakes
- Giving glucose-containing fluids before thiamine in a malnourished patient.
- Using the CIWA-Ar in a confused patient who cannot report symptoms.
- Starting buprenorphine in a patient without objective withdrawal, causing precipitated withdrawal.
- Giving flumazenil to a patient who may be dependent on benzodiazepines.
