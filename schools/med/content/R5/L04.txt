@article
Most headache and dizziness is benign; the skill is finding the minority that is not. Start with the history and let the pattern decide how much testing is needed.

### Red flags for secondary headache

The mnemonic **SNOOP** helps: **S**ystemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, cancer, HIV), **N**eurologic signs (confusion, papilledema, focal deficit), **O**nset sudden (thunderclap), **O**lder age at onset (over 50), **P**attern change (new, progressive, positional, or triggered by Valsalva or exertion). Pregnancy, anticoagulation and trauma add concern.

- **Thunderclap headache** (maximal in about 1 minute) is subarachnoid hemorrhage until proven otherwise. A non-contrast head CT within 6 hours of onset in a neurologically intact patient is nearly 100% sensitive on modern scanners. If it is negative and suspicion persists (or it is later than 6 hours), perform a lumbar puncture for **xanthochromia** and red cells, or obtain CT angiography. Other causes: reversible cerebral vasoconstriction, cervical artery dissection, pituitary apoplexy, venous sinus thrombosis.
- **Giant cell arteritis:** age over 50 with new headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication and often polymyalgia rheumatica. Check ESR and CRP; **start high-dose glucocorticoids immediately** if suspicion is high (to protect vision), then arrange temporal artery biopsy. A rule of thumb for the upper limit of normal ESR is age/2 for men and (age + 10)/2 for women.
- **Meningitis or encephalitis:** fever, neck stiffness, altered mental status; give empiric antibiotics without waiting for imaging or lumbar puncture.
- **Idiopathic intracranial hypertension:** an obese young woman with daily headache, transient visual obscurations and papilledema; normal imaging, opening pressure above 25 cm H2O; treat with weight loss and acetazolamide, and protect vision.

### Primary headaches

**Migraine:** unilateral, throbbing, nausea, photophobia and phonophobia, 4 to 72 hours, sometimes with aura. Acute treatment: NSAIDs or acetaminophen with an antiemetic, early; **triptans** (serotonin 5-HT1B/1D agonists) for moderate to severe attacks, contraindicated in coronary disease, uncontrolled hypertension and hemiplegic or brainstem aura. Avoid opioids. Consider prevention with four or more headache days a month or disabling attacks: propranolol, topiramate, amitriptyline, or CGRP-pathway monoclonal antibodies. Limit acute medication to under 10 to 15 days a month to avoid **medication overuse headache**.

**Cluster headache:** severe unilateral orbital pain lasting 15 to 180 minutes, up to eight times a day, with ipsilateral tearing, nasal congestion and Horner features, restlessness, often at night, more often in men. Treat attacks with **100% oxygen at 12 to 15 L/min** by non-rebreather mask or subcutaneous sumatriptan; verapamil is first-line prevention (check ECG).

**Tension-type headache:** bilateral, pressing, mild to moderate, without nausea; simple analgesics.

### Dizziness: from symptom to syndrome

Ask about the timing and triggers rather than the word "dizzy".

- **Brief, positional vertigo** (seconds, with head turns): **benign paroxysmal positional vertigo**, confirmed by the **Dix–Hallpike maneuver** (torsional upbeat nystagmus with latency, fatigable) and treated with the **Epley canalith repositioning maneuver**.
- **Acute vestibular syndrome** (continuous vertigo for days, nausea, nystagmus, gait unsteadiness): vestibular neuritis versus posterior circulation stroke. Use **HINTS**: a **normal head impulse** test (no corrective saccade), **direction-changing nystagmus** or **skew deviation** suggests a central cause, and any one of them means imaging and stroke work-up (MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging; CT misses most posterior fossa infarcts). A unidirectional nystagmus with an abnormal (positive) head impulse and no skew suggests vestibular neuritis, treated with short-term vestibular suppressants and early rehabilitation. HINTS applies only to patients who truly have continuous vertigo with nystagmus.
- **Episodic vertigo lasting minutes to hours with tinnitus and low-frequency hearing loss:** Ménière disease.
- **Presyncope** (near-faint): orthostasis, arrhythmia, anemia, medication.

### Seizures as a complication

A patient on the ward who seizes for over 5 minutes is in status epilepticus: airway and glucose, then a benzodiazepine, then a second-line loading drug, then a cause search (see the seizure lesson).

@example
title: Thunderclap headache
problem: A 44-year-old woman has a sudden severe headache while lifting a box, 3 hours ago. Neurologic exam is normal. How do you proceed?
step: This is thunderclap onset: subarachnoid hemorrhage must be excluded.
step: Order a non-contrast head CT now. At under 6 hours from onset, in a neurologically intact patient on a modern scanner, a negative scan effectively rules out hemorrhage.
step: If the CT shows blood, obtain CT angiography, control blood pressure, and call neurosurgery. If it is negative, consider other dangerous causes (vasoconstriction, dissection) and CT angiography if suspicion remains.
step: If imaging is equivocal or delayed beyond 6 hours, perform lumbar puncture looking for xanthochromia.

@example
title: HINTS in acute vertigo
problem: A 62-year-old man with hypertension and diabetes has had 2 days of continuous vertigo and vomiting. Nystagmus is present. The head impulse test is normal on both sides, and the nystagmus changes direction on lateral gaze. What is the interpretation?
step: First confirm the patient has acute vestibular syndrome with ongoing nystagmus, so HINTS is valid.
step: A normal head impulse test means the vestibulo-ocular reflex is intact, which is unexpected if the labyrinth or nerve were damaged.
step: Direction-changing nystagmus is a central sign.
step: Conclusion: central vertigo, such as a cerebellar or brainstem infarct. Obtain MRI with diffusion-weighted imaging and CT angiography of the head and neck, and admit to a stroke unit.

@mistakes
- Using HINTS in a patient with brief or positional vertigo, or after the nystagmus has stopped.
- Relying on a head CT to exclude posterior fossa infarction.
- Delaying glucocorticoids in suspected giant cell arteritis until the biopsy result.
- Prescribing a triptan to a patient with coronary disease or hemiplegic migraine.
