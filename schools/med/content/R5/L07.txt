@article
A psychiatric consult answers a question such as "Is this patient depressed?", "Is he psychotic?", "Can she go home?" The process is the same as in any consult: gather the history, examine, form a differential, and make specific recommendations. Two features are distinctive: the **mental status examination** (MSE) is the physical examination of psychiatry, and **safety assessment** comes first.

### The consult interview

Clarify the question and who is asking. Review the chart, medications, laboratory results and collateral information from family, the outpatient team or police reports. Interview in a private, safe setting (with security available if needed). Cover the history of present illness, past psychiatric history (diagnoses, hospitalizations, attempts, medications and responses), substance use, medical history, family history, and social context including housing, relationships, trauma and access to means of harm.

### The mental status examination

| Domain | What to describe | Examples of terms |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance and behavior | Grooming, eye contact, motor activity | Psychomotor retardation or agitation, catatonia |
| Speech | Rate, volume, amount | Pressured, poverty of speech |
| Mood and affect | Mood = patient's words; affect = observed | Euthymic, dysphoric, flat, blunted, labile, congruent |
| Thought process | How ideas connect | Linear; **circumstantial** (detours but returns to the point); **tangential** (never returns); flight of ideas; loose associations; thought blocking; word salad |
| Thought content | What the ideas are | Delusions, obsessions, suicidal or homicidal ideation, phobias |
| Perception | Hallucinations, illusions | A hallucination has no external stimulus; an illusion misperceives a real one |
| Cognition | Orientation, attention, memory | Months backward, three-word recall |
| Insight and judgment | Awareness of illness; decisions | Good, limited, poor |

### Medical and substance causes first

New psychiatric symptoms at any age, especially after 40 or with abnormal vital signs, disorientation, visual hallucinations or neurologic findings, may be **delirium** or another medical condition. Think of hypo- and hyperthyroidism, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypercalcemia, B12 deficiency, infection, stroke, temporal lobe seizures, autoimmune encephalitis, Cushing syndrome, medications (corticosteroids, levodopa, isoniazid, interferon) and substances (stimulants, cannabis, alcohol, benzodiazepines, synthetic cannabinoids, hallucinogens). Check vital signs, glucose, electrolytes, renal and liver tests, thyroid-stimulating hormone, a toxicology screen, pregnancy status when relevant and an ECG if QT-prolonging drugs are used. A primary psychiatric diagnosis is made when substances and medical illness do not explain the symptoms.

### Suicide risk assessment

Every consult asks directly about suicidal thoughts; asking does not cause suicide. Brief screens include the **Ask Suicide-Screening Questions (ASQ)**, the **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale** and item 9 of the PHQ-9; any positive answer leads to a fuller assessment of ideation, plan, intent, preparatory behavior, prior attempts and available means.

- **Risk factors:** prior attempt (strongest predictor), mood disorder, psychosis, substance use disorder, hopelessness, impulsivity, recent discharge from psychiatric hospitalization, chronic or terminal illness, social isolation, recent loss, male sex, older age, and access to lethal means.
- **Protective factors:** social connection, reasons for living, engagement in treatment, future orientation, limited access to means.

Risk is estimated with a structured formulation, not a numerical score: combine static factors, acute stressors, current intent and plan, the patient's ability to engage in a safety plan, and supports. Document your reasoning and disposition (discharge with a safety plan, observation, or admission). Tools predict risk poorly, so they never replace clinical judgment. Crisis resources in the United States include calling or texting **988** (Suicide and Crisis Lifeline), and emergency services for imminent danger.

### Screening test arithmetic

For a screening test, sensitivity is the fraction of people with the condition who test positive, specificity the fraction without it who test negative, and

$$\text{PPV} = \frac{\text{sens} \times \text{prev}}{\text{sens} \times \text{prev} + (1-\text{spec})(1-\text{prev})}, \qquad \text{LR}^+ = \frac{\text{sens}}{1-\text{spec}}.$$

The lower the prevalence, the lower the positive predictive value of the same test.

@example
title: Predictive value of a depression screen
problem: A screening questionnaire has sensitivity 85% and specificity 90%. In a clinic where 20% of patients have major depression, what is the probability that a positive screen is a true case?
step: Take 1,000 patients: 200 have depression, 800 do not.
step: True positives: 0.85 x 200 = 170. False positives: 0.10 x 800 = 80.
step: PPV = 170/(170 + 80) = 0.68, or 68%.
step: Interpretation: about one in three positive screens is false, so a positive screen leads to a clinical interview, not a diagnosis.

@mistakes
- Attributing new psychosis or mood change to a primary psychiatric disorder before checking for delirium, intoxication and medical causes.
- Avoiding the suicide question for fear of "planting the idea".
- Using a risk checklist score as a substitute for formulation, or relying on a promise (a "no-harm contract") for safety.
- Mixing up circumstantiality (returns to the point) and tangentiality (does not).
