@article
A neurology consult is a question from another team: "new left-sided weakness," "seizure in a patient on the ward," "altered after surgery." Your job is to answer that question, not to describe the whole nervous system. The classic sequence still works best: **where is the lesion, and what is it?** Localize first, then use the time course and risk factors to choose the cause, then state how urgent it is.

### Time course suggests the cause

- Sudden onset, maximal at once: vascular (ischemic stroke, hemorrhage) or a seizure.
- Hours to days, with fever or systemic illness: infection or inflammation.
- Weeks to months, progressive: tumor, chronic infection, neurodegeneration.
- Episodic with full recovery: seizure, migraine, transient ischemic attack, syncope.

### Upper versus lower motor neuron

The **upper motor neuron** (UMN) runs from motor cortex through the internal capsule, brainstem and corticospinal tract to the anterior horn. The **lower motor neuron** (LMN) runs from the anterior horn cell through root, plexus and nerve to the muscle.

| Feature | UMN lesion | LMN lesion |
|---|---|---|
| Tone | Increased (spasticity) | Decreased |
| Reflexes | Brisk | Reduced or absent |
| Plantar response | Extensor (Babinski) | Flexor |
| Atrophy and fasciculations | Absent early | Present |

Facial weakness illustrates the rule. A UMN lesion above the facial nucleus spares the forehead, because the upper face receives bilateral cortical input. An LMN lesion (Bell palsy, facial nerve injury) weakens the whole half of the face, forehead included.

### Localizing along the neuraxis

- **Cortex:** aphasia (dominant hemisphere), neglect (non-dominant), seizures, cortical sensory loss, contralateral hemianopia.
- **Internal capsule:** dense contralateral weakness of face, arm and leg together, often without cortical signs.
- **Brainstem:** **crossed findings**, meaning cranial nerve signs on the side of the lesion with motor or sensory signs on the opposite body. The cranial nerve involved gives the level (midbrain III and IV, pons V to VIII, medulla IX to XII).
- **Cerebellum:** ipsilateral dysmetria, intention tremor and gait ataxia; vermis lesions cause truncal ataxia.
- **Spinal cord:** a sensory level, bilateral leg signs, bladder and bowel dysfunction.
- **Root, nerve, neuromuscular junction, muscle:** LMN pattern, with fatigability pointing to the junction and proximal weakness with normal sensation to muscle.

**Visual fields** localize precisely: monocular loss means the eye or optic nerve, bitemporal hemianopia the chiasm, and a homonymous hemianopia a lesion behind the chiasm on the opposite side (optic tract, radiations or occipital cortex). Macular sparing suggests the occipital cortex (posterior cerebral artery territory).

### Two numbers every consult uses

The **Glasgow Coma Scale** adds Eye opening (1 to 4), Verbal response (1 to 5) and Motor response (1 to 6), for a range of 3 to 15. Motor 6 obeys commands, 5 localizes pain, 4 withdraws, 3 flexor posturing, 2 extensor posturing, 1 none.

**Cerebral perfusion pressure** is the pressure driving blood into the brain:

$$\text{CPP} = \text{MAP} - \text{ICP}, \qquad \text{MAP} = \frac{\text{SBP} + 2\,\text{DBP}}{3}.$$

Normal intracranial pressure is below about 15 mm Hg; a CPP of at least 60 mm Hg is the usual target in raised intracranial pressure.

### Emergencies to recognize on any consult

Acute stroke inside the treatment window, status epilepticus, spinal cord compression, a blown pupil with falling consciousness (herniation), Guillain–Barré syndrome with failing breathing, and bacterial meningitis (give antibiotics without waiting for imaging). Write the note so the recommendation that matters most comes first.

@example
title: Localizing crossed brainstem findings
problem: A 60-year-old man has a complete right facial droop including the forehead, an inability to abduct the right eye, and weakness of the left arm and leg. Localize the lesion.
step: Group the findings by side. Right face and right eye are cranial nerve signs; left arm and leg are long-tract motor signs. Cranial nerve signs on one side with body signs on the other is a crossed pattern, so the lesion is in the brainstem.
step: The whole right face including the forehead is an LMN pattern, so the right facial nerve or its nucleus is involved. The right abducens (VI) palsy puts the lesion where VI and VII lie together: the pons.
step: The corticospinal fibers have not yet crossed at the pons, so left hemiparesis means the lesion is on the right. Localization: right ventral pons (Millard–Gubler pattern), usually a basilar artery branch infarct in an older patient with vascular risk factors.
step: Recommendation: stroke pathway with urgent MRI and vascular imaging of the posterior circulation, because the basilar artery can occlude progressively.

@mistakes
- Naming a diagnosis before localizing, so the exam findings are forced to fit.
- Calling any facial droop a "stroke" without checking whether the forehead is spared (UMN) or involved (LMN).
- Ordering imaging of the wrong region, for example a head CT for a sensory level that points to the spinal cord.
- Calculating CPP with the systolic pressure instead of the mean arterial pressure.

@practice num
prompt: A patient with a severe head injury has a blood pressure of 130/70 mm Hg and an intracranial pressure of 20 mm Hg. What is the cerebral perfusion pressure in mm Hg?
answer: 70
check: (130 + 2*70)/3 - 20
tol: 0.5
unit: mm Hg
hint: Compute the mean arterial pressure first: one third of the systolic plus two thirds of the diastolic.
hint: MAP is 90 mm Hg. Now subtract the intracranial pressure.
solution: MAP = (130 + 140)/3 = 90 mm Hg. CPP = 90 - 20 = 70 mm Hg.
