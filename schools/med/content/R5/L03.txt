@article
A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal, excessive or synchronous neuronal activity. The consult asks three questions: **Was this a seizure? Was it provoked? Is it still going?**

### Classification

- **Focal seizures** start in one hemisphere. Awareness may be preserved or impaired (a temporal lobe seizure often begins with a rising epigastric sensation, then staring, lip smacking and postictal confusion). A focal seizure can spread to a bilateral tonic-clonic seizure.
- **Generalized seizures** involve both hemispheres from the start: tonic-clonic, absence (brief staring, 3-Hz spike-and-wave on EEG, provoked by hyperventilation), myoclonic, atonic.

A long postictal state, tongue-side laceration, incontinence and a raised lactate or creatine kinase support a seizure over syncope; syncope usually has a brief jerk phase and quick recovery. **Psychogenic nonepileptic events** often have long duration, side-to-side head movements, forced eye closure and rapid recovery, and the diagnosis rests on video EEG.

### Provoked versus unprovoked

Provoked (acute symptomatic) seizures come from a reversible cause: hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hypocalcemia, uremia, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, stimulant or other drug toxicity, central nervous system infection, head trauma, stroke, and eclampsia (treated with magnesium sulfate). Treat the cause; long-term medication is usually not needed. Check glucose, sodium, calcium, magnesium, renal function, a toxicology screen when indicated and a pregnancy test, and obtain neuroimaging (CT in the emergency setting, MRI as the definitive study) and an EEG.

After a **first unprovoked seizure**, recurrence risk over 2 years is roughly 20 to 45%, higher with an abnormal MRI or EEG or a prior brain injury. Treatment after a single seizure is a shared decision; after a second unprovoked seizure it is usually offered. Epilepsy is diagnosed after two unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or one with a high recurrence risk.

### Medication selection

| Seizure type | Common first-line choices |
|---|---|
| Focal | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam, carbamazepine or oxcarbazepine |
| Generalized tonic-clonic or myoclonic | Valproate, lamotrigine, levetiracetam |
| Absence only | Ethosuximide (valproate if there are other seizure types) |

**Valproate** is teratogenic (neural tube defects and neurodevelopmental effects) and should be avoided in people who may become pregnant if alternatives work; give folic acid to all women of childbearing potential on antiseizure medication. **Carbamazepine** can worsen absence and myoclonic seizures, causes hyponatremia and agranulocytosis, and requires HLA-B*1502 testing in people of some Asian ancestries because of Stevens–Johnson syndrome risk. Enzyme-inducing drugs (phenytoin, carbamazepine) lower the effect of oral contraceptives and many other drugs. **Levetiracetam** may cause irritability and mood change.

Counsel about driving (laws vary by state), heights, swimming and bathing alone, medication adherence, sleep, and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy.

### Status epilepticus

Status epilepticus is a seizure lasting **5 minutes or longer**, or recurrent seizures without recovery between them. It is a medical emergency with a mortality that rises with duration.

1. **0 to 5 minutes:** airway, breathing, circulation, oxygen, IV access, glucose (give dextrose if low; give thiamine to patients at risk for alcohol-related deficiency), and monitoring.
2. **First-line (from 5 minutes):** a benzodiazepine. Typical adult doses for study (verify against current references before clinical use): **IM midazolam 10 mg** for adults over 40 kg (5 mg for 13 to 40 kg), or **IV lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg per dose, maximum 4 mg**, may be repeated once, or IV diazepam 0.15 to 0.2 mg/kg per dose, maximum 10 mg. Do not wait for IV access to give IM midazolam.
3. **Second-line (5 to 20 minutes, if seizures persist):** one of **levetiracetam 60 mg/kg** (maximum 4,500 mg), **fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg** (maximum 1,500 mg PE; infuse no faster than 150 mg PE/min, with blood pressure and rhythm monitoring) or **valproate 40 mg/kg** (maximum 3,000 mg). The ESETT trial found these three about equally effective. PE means phenytoin equivalents.
4. **Refractory (after 20 to 40 minutes):** intubate, start continuous EEG, and use an anesthetic infusion (midazolam, propofol or pentobarbital) while a cause is sought.

Look for the cause throughout: infection, withdrawal, metabolic derangement, a structural lesion, medication nonadherence, toxins (isoniazid toxicity needs pyridoxine).

@example
title: Status epilepticus in a 70 kg adult
problem: A 70 kg man has been seizing continuously for 6 minutes. Plan the first two steps with doses.
step: Secure the airway, apply oxygen, check glucose (it is 104 mg/dL) and obtain IV access.
step: First-line: lorazepam at 0.1 mg/kg is 7 mg, which exceeds the 4 mg per-dose maximum, so give 4 mg IV; repeat once after 5 minutes if seizures continue.
step: If still seizing, load levetiracetam at 60 mg/kg: 60 x 70 = 4,200 mg, below the 4,500 mg cap, infused over 10 minutes.
step: Seek the cause: sodium, calcium, toxicology, anticonvulsant adherence and CT head; arrange EEG monitoring and ICU level of care if seizures persist.

@mistakes
- Waiting for IV access or the next lab result before giving a first-line benzodiazepine.
- Using phenytoin or fosphenytoin faster than 150 mg PE/min, which risks hypotension and arrhythmia.
- Using carbamazepine for generalized absence or myoclonic epilepsy, where it can worsen seizures.
- Forgetting to look for and treat a precipitating cause such as hypoglycemia or withdrawal.
