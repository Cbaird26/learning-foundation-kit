@article
"Weak" can mean the brain, spinal cord, nerve, junction or muscle, and a good consult starts by asking which. The decisive questions are **the distribution** (proximal, distal, bulbar, ocular), **the tempo**, **reflexes**, **sensation**, and **whether weakness fatigues**.

### Localization table

| Site | Pattern | Reflexes | Sensation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upper motor neuron | Pyramidal weakness, spasticity | Brisk | Variable |
| Anterior horn (ALS has UMN signs too) | Asymmetric, atrophy, fasciculations | Reduced (brisk if UMN co-involved) | Normal |
| Peripheral nerve | Distal, often with sensory loss | Reduced or absent | Impaired |
| Neuromuscular junction | Fatigable, ocular and bulbar | Normal (reduced in Lambert–Eaton) | Normal |
| Muscle | Proximal, symmetric | Normal or mildly reduced | Normal |

### Spinal cord compression: the emergency not to miss

Back pain, bilateral leg weakness, a sensory level and urinary retention in a patient with cancer, trauma, infection (epidural abscess) or anticoagulation is cord compression until proven otherwise. Obtain **emergent MRI of the whole spine**, give dexamethasone if due to tumor, and call spine surgery or radiation oncology. Saddle anesthesia and bladder dysfunction suggest cauda equina syndrome, which also needs emergent MRI and decompression.

### Guillain–Barré syndrome

Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS) is an immune-mediated acute inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy, often 1 to 3 weeks after infection (**Campylobacter jejuni** diarrhea, cytomegalovirus, Zika, or vaccination rarely). It causes **ascending, symmetric weakness with areflexia**, paresthesias, back pain and, in a third of cases or more, facial weakness; **autonomic instability** (arrhythmia, blood pressure swings) is common. The Miller Fisher variant has ophthalmoplegia, ataxia and areflexia.

- **Diagnosis:** clinical. Cerebrospinal fluid shows **albuminocytologic dissociation** (high protein, normal white cells), which may be normal in the first week. Nerve conduction studies show demyelination.
- **Treatment:** IV immunoglobulin (typical adult dose for study, 0.4 g/kg per day for 5 days) or plasma exchange, started within 4 weeks of onset and best within 2. Corticosteroids do not help. Give DVT prophylaxis, and monitor on telemetry.
- **Respiratory monitoring** is the key consult recommendation. Measure **forced vital capacity** and **maximal inspiratory pressure** at least every 4 to 6 hours at the bedside and consider intubation for a vital capacity below **20 mL/kg**, maximal inspiratory pressure weaker than **-30 cm H2O**, maximal expiratory pressure below **40 cm H2O** (the "20/30/40 rule"), a falling trend, bulbar failure or a rising CO2. Do not wait for hypoxia or hypercapnia.

### Myasthenia gravis

Antibodies to the **acetylcholine receptor** (85% of generalized cases; MuSK antibodies in some of the rest) cause fatigable weakness: ptosis and diplopia worse at day's end, bulbar symptoms, proximal weakness, normal reflexes, no sensory loss. Check for **thymoma** with chest CT. Diagnosis rests on antibodies, repetitive nerve stimulation (a decrement of more than 10% at low-frequency stimulation) or single-fiber EMG; the ice pack test on ptosis is a quick bedside aid.

Treatment: **pyridostigmine** for symptoms, immunosuppression (glucocorticoids, azathioprine and others), thymectomy for most patients with acetylcholine receptor antibodies who are under 65 and for any thymoma, and newer complement and FcRn-targeted agents for refractory disease. In **myasthenic crisis** (respiratory failure from weakness), monitor as in GBS with forced vital capacity and inspiratory pressure, treat with plasma exchange or IV immunoglobulin, and intubate early. **Drugs that worsen myasthenia:** fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides, IV magnesium, beta-blockers and neuromuscular blockers (also check before adding any new drug).

### Other junction and muscle disorders

- **Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome:** antibodies to presynaptic P/Q-type calcium channels; proximal leg weakness, dry mouth, reduced reflexes that improve after exercise; **incremental response with high-frequency stimulation**; most often paraneoplastic with small cell lung cancer. Screen for cancer; treat with amifampridine.
- **Botulism:** toxin blocks acetylcholine release; **descending** paralysis with dilated pupils, dry mouth, preceded by food or wound exposure (honey in infants); treat with antitoxin and supportive care, and monitor respiration.
- **Inflammatory myopathy and statin myopathy:** proximal weakness with high creatine kinase; periodic paralysis: episodic weakness with abnormal potassium.

@example
title: Respiratory threshold in Guillain–Barré syndrome
problem: A 70 kg man with GBS has an FVC of 1.5 L. Does he meet the vital capacity criterion for intubation, and what do you recommend?
step: The criterion is 20 mL/kg, so the threshold is 20 x 70 = 1,400 mL.
step: His FVC is 1,500 mL, or about 21 mL/kg, which is just above the threshold. He does not yet meet the criterion.
step: But he is borderline, so the trend matters. Recommend FVC and maximal inspiratory pressure every 4 hours, ICU or step-down monitoring, and a low threshold to intubate if he drops or has bulbar failure or a rising CO2.
step: Start IV immunoglobulin 0.4 g/kg per day for 5 days.

@mistakes
- Waiting for hypoxemia or hypercapnia before intubating a patient with GBS or myasthenic crisis; these are late signs.
- Treating GBS with corticosteroids.
- Giving IV magnesium or a fluoroquinolone to a patient with myasthenia gravis.
- Assuming areflexia excludes a neuromuscular junction disorder; reflexes are normal in myasthenia but reduced in Lambert–Eaton syndrome.
