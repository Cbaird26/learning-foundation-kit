@article
Psychosis is a loss of contact with shared reality: delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech or behavior. Mania is a distinct episode of elevated or irritable mood with increased energy. Both appear in emergency departments, on medical floors and in the clinic.

### Psychosis: medical first, then primary

Rule out delirium, intoxication or withdrawal (stimulants, cannabis, hallucinogens, synthetic cannabinoids, alcohol), medications (corticosteroids, dopamine agonists), and organic causes: temporal lobe seizures, tumors, HIV, syphilis, thyroid disease, B12 deficiency, and autoimmune encephalitis. **Anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis** typically affects a young woman who develops agitation, psychosis, seizures, orofacial dyskinesias and autonomic instability, sometimes with an ovarian teratoma; the diagnosis uses CSF antibodies and the treatment is immunotherapy and tumor removal.

**Primary psychotic disorders by duration:** brief psychotic disorder (1 day to 1 month), schizophreniform disorder (1 to 6 months), schizophrenia (6 months or longer with at least 1 month of active symptoms). Schizophrenia needs at least two of delusions, hallucinations, disorganized speech, grossly disorganized or catatonic behavior, and negative symptoms, with at least one being one of the first three. Schizoaffective disorder includes a major mood episode concurrent with psychosis and psychosis lasting at least 2 weeks without mood symptoms.

### Antipsychotic treatment and its adverse effects

Second-generation antipsychotics (risperidone, olanzapine, quetiapine, aripiprazole) are used for acute psychosis and maintenance, and long-acting injectables help when adherence is poor. **Clozapine** is for treatment-resistant schizophrenia or persistent suicidal behavior; it needs absolute neutrophil count monitoring (severe neutropenia risk), and can cause myocarditis, seizures, sialorrhea and constipation. The **absolute neutrophil count** equals the white cell count times the percentage of neutrophils plus bands.

| Syndrome | Timing and features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| Acute dystonia | Hours to days; muscle spasm of neck, jaw or eyes | IM benztropine or diphenhydramine |
| Akathisia | Days to weeks; inner restlessness | Lower dose; propranolol; benztropine is less effective |
| Parkinsonism | Days to weeks; tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia | Lower dose, switch, anticholinergic |
| Tardive dyskinesia | Months to years; orofacial and limb movements | Switch to a lower-risk drug; VMAT2 inhibitors (valbenazine, deutetrabenazine) |
| Neuroleptic malignant syndrome | Fever, **lead-pipe rigidity**, altered mental status, autonomic instability, high creatine kinase | Stop drug, cool and hydrate, benzodiazepines; dantrolene or bromocriptine in severe cases |

Monitor weight, waist, blood pressure, fasting glucose or hemoglobin A1c and lipids at baseline, around 12 weeks and yearly; olanzapine and clozapine carry the greatest metabolic risk, and risperidone raises prolactin most. Obtain an ECG for drugs that prolong the QT interval.

### Mania

DSM criteria for a manic episode: elevated, expansive or irritable mood with increased energy lasting at least **1 week** (any duration if hospitalization is needed), plus at least three of: grandiosity, decreased need for sleep, pressured speech, flight of ideas, distractibility, increased goal-directed activity and risky behavior. **Bipolar I** requires a manic episode; **bipolar II** requires hypomania (at least 4 days, no psychosis or hospitalization) plus major depression.

Acute treatment: **lithium**, **valproate** or a second-generation antipsychotic (often combined), with benzodiazepines for agitation. Stop antidepressants.

- **Lithium:** therapeutic level about 0.6 to 1.2 mEq/L. Toxicity: coarse tremor, vomiting, ataxia, confusion, seizures. Levels rise with dehydration, **thiazides, ACE inhibitors, NSAIDs** and renal impairment. Long-term effects: hypothyroidism, nephrogenic diabetes insipidus, kidney disease; teratogenic (Ebstein anomaly). Check renal function, thyroid tests and calcium.
- **Valproate:** hepatotoxicity, pancreatitis, thrombocytopenia, weight gain, neural tube defects.
- **Carbamazepine:** hyponatremia, agranulocytosis, Stevens–Johnson syndrome (HLA-B*1502 in people of some Asian ancestries), enzyme induction.
- **Lamotrigine:** prevents bipolar depression; titrate slowly to limit rash and Stevens–Johnson syndrome.

### Choosing and monitoring a first antidepressant

Choose by patient factors and screen first for bipolar disorder. Start with an SSRI for most people. Choose bupropion if sexual dysfunction or weight gain is a concern and there is no seizure disorder or eating disorder. Choose mirtazapine when insomnia and poor appetite dominate. Avoid tricyclics if overdose risk is a concern. Set the baseline PHQ-9, review interacting drugs (for example tramadol, linezolid, warfarin), check sodium in older adults, and follow up within 1 to 2 weeks, with response assessed at 4 to 6 weeks.

@example
title: Absolute neutrophil count before clozapine
problem: A patient has a white cell count of 5.0 x 10^3/uL with 48% neutrophils and 2% bands. What is the absolute neutrophil count?
step: Neutrophils plus bands = 48% + 2% = 50%.
step: ANC = 5,000/uL x 0.50 = 2,500/uL.
step: Typical clozapine registry rules for the general population allow starting treatment with an ANC of at least 1,500/uL. This patient qualifies, and the count is monitored on a fixed schedule.

@mistakes
- Diagnosing schizophrenia before 6 months of illness or before excluding substances and medical causes.
- Starting an antidepressant alone in a patient with unrecognized bipolar disorder.
- Starting an NSAID or thiazide in a patient taking lithium without checking levels.
- Treating neuroleptic malignant syndrome with another antipsychotic.

@practice self
prompt: A 34-year-old man has a first episode of major depression with a PHQ-9 of 18. He sleeps 11 hours, has gained weight, and has had sexual dysfunction on a prior SSRI. He has no history of mania, seizures or eating disorder, drinks little alcohol, and takes no other medications. Choose a first antidepressant and describe how you would start it and monitor it.
solution: Confirm the diagnosis and screen again for mania, substance use and medical causes (TSH). Reasonable choice: bupropion extended-release, typically 150 mg each morning, increased to 300 mg after about a week if tolerated (typical adult dose for study), because it has a low rate of sexual dysfunction and weight gain and he has none of its contraindications (seizure disorder, eating disorder, abrupt alcohol withdrawal). Counsel on insomnia, anxiety, headache and the small seizure risk. Combine with psychotherapy. Record the baseline PHQ-9, ask about suicidal thoughts at each visit, review in 1 to 2 weeks and reassess at 4 to 6 weeks; if there is no adequate response adjust the dose, switch or augment. Continue 6 to 12 months after remission.
