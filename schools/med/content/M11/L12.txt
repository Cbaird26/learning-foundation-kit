@article
Law sets minimum requirements for conduct; ethics sets higher aspirations. Clinicians need a working knowledge of the law of malpractice and reporting. U.S. law is mostly state law, so details vary, but the principles below are the ones tested on the USMLE.

### Medical malpractice: negligence

Malpractice is **professional negligence**, a civil wrong (a tort) in which the patient seeks money damages. The plaintiff must prove four elements by a **preponderance of the evidence** (more likely than not):

1. **Duty:** a physician-patient relationship existed. It starts when a physician agrees to treat, expressly or implicitly, for example by accepting a patient into care or giving specific advice.
2. **Breach:** the physician failed to meet the **standard of care**, meaning the care a reasonably prudent physician of the same specialty would provide under similar circumstances. Expert testimony usually establishes it. Bad outcomes alone are not breach.
3. **Causation:** the breach caused the harm. **Actual cause** ("but for" the breach, the harm would not have occurred) and **proximate cause** (the harm was a foreseeable result).
4. **Damages:** actual harm, such as medical costs, lost income, and pain and suffering. Without harm, there is no negligence claim even if care was poor.

A mnemonic is the four D's: **duty, dereliction, direct causation, damages**.

### Res ipsa loquitur

"The thing speaks for itself": in some cases negligence is inferred without expert proof when (1) the event ordinarily does not occur without negligence, (2) the instrumentality was under the defendant's control, and (3) the plaintiff did not contribute. A **retained surgical sponge** is the classic example.

### Defenses and related concepts

- **Comparative negligence:** the plaintiff's own fault (for example, ignoring instructions) reduces damages in proportion in most states (state law varies: some states bar recovery if the plaintiff is more than 50% or 51% at fault, and a few follow contributory negligence, which bars recovery entirely).
- **Assumption of risk** (consent to a known risk) and **statute of limitations** (the claim must be filed within a time limit, which differs by state and may be extended for minors).
- **Vicarious liability (respondeat superior):** employers, such as hospitals, may be liable for the negligence of employees acting in the scope of employment.
- **Damages:** compensatory damages (economic and non-economic) and, rarely, punitive damages for reckless conduct. Some states cap non-economic damages.
- **Insurance:** claims-made policies cover claims filed while the policy is active, so "tail" coverage is needed after leaving; occurrence policies cover events during the policy period.
- **Malpractice payments** (settlements and judgments) made on a physician's behalf, and certain licensing and privileging actions, are reported to the **National Practitioner Data Bank**, which hospitals and state boards query.

### Intentional torts and consent

Treatment without consent can be **battery**, an intentional tort; inadequate disclosure is usually **negligence** (week 2). Defamation and breach of confidentiality are other claims. Criminal liability is separate and rare, requiring intent, recklessness or gross negligence. Licensing boards can discipline physicians independently of civil cases.

### Abandonment and duties

**Abandonment** is ending the relationship without reasonable notice and without time to find another physician, when the patient still needs care. To terminate properly, give written notice, a reasonable period (often 30 days), emergency coverage and transfer of records. **Good Samaritan laws** protect volunteers who give emergency care outside of the hospital from liability for ordinary negligence, although not for gross negligence; there is no general legal duty to stop and help in most states, although ethics encourages it. Under **EMTALA**, hospitals must screen and stabilize emergency patients (week 10).

### Mandatory reporting

Physicians must report to government agencies despite confidentiality (see week 4):

- **Child abuse and neglect** on reasonable suspicion (all states), with immunity for good-faith reports.
- **Elder and dependent adult abuse** (most states).
- **Intimate partner violence** against competent adults is mandatory to report in only a few states (many require reporting of injuries from weapons); state law varies and the patient's safety is a priority.
- **Communicable diseases** designated by state (for example, tuberculosis, syphilis, measles, hepatitis A, and many others).
- **Gunshot and stab wounds** (most states require reporting).
- **Deaths:** suspicious, unattended, or unexplained deaths to the medical examiner or coroner.
- **Impaired or incompetent colleagues** and certain driver-related conditions (varies by state).
- **Vaccine adverse events:** certain events after vaccination must be reported to VAERS, and any suspected event may be. Other **drug adverse events** go to the FDA's MedWatch program, which is voluntary for clinicians.

@example
title: Does this case meet the elements of negligence?
problem: A 55-year-old man calls his primary care physician's office with chest pain and is told by the physician on the phone to "take an antacid and come in next week." The physician has treated him for years. The next morning he has a myocardial infarction with permanent heart damage. A cardiology expert testifies that a reasonable physician would have sent him to the emergency department. Does it meet the elements of negligence?
step: Duty: the physician-patient relationship exists, and the physician gave advice, so duty is established.
step: Breach: expert testimony shows the standard of care required urgent evaluation; telling him to wait departed from it.
step: Causation: if the delay allowed the infarction to progress to permanent damage that timely care would have prevented, the delay is both the actual cause and a foreseeable (proximate) cause. Damages: permanent heart damage, costs and suffering.
step: Conclusion: the four elements can be proved, so the physician is likely liable if the plaintiff proves that earlier treatment would have changed the outcome. Note that duty, breach, causation and damages must all be present; missing any one defeats the claim.

@mistakes
- Equating a bad outcome with malpractice; breach of the standard of care is required.
- Forgetting that harm (damages) and causation must be proved, even if the care was poor.
- Thinking a signed consent form protects against negligent care.
- Confusing battery (no consent) with negligence (careless care or inadequate disclosure).

@practice self
prompt: A 30-year-old woman has a delayed diagnosis of appendicitis after an emergency physician discharged her with "gas pain" without examining her abdomen. Her appendix ruptured the next day, causing sepsis and a prolonged ICU stay. Analyze the four elements of negligence and identify what the plaintiff must prove for each.
solution: Duty: she presented to the emergency department and the physician evaluated her, creating a physician-patient relationship. Breach: the plaintiff must show through expert testimony that a reasonable emergency physician would have examined her abdomen and considered appendicitis, and that the discharge without examination departed from the standard. Causation: the failure to examine (actual cause) led to delayed diagnosis and rupture, which is a foreseeable result (proximate cause); the plaintiff must show that timely diagnosis would likely have prevented the rupture. Damages: sepsis, ICU costs, lost income and pain and suffering. All four are proved by a preponderance of the evidence.
