@article
Medicine is a profession: society grants physicians authority and autonomy in exchange for competence, integrity and putting patients' interests first. **Professionalism** includes honesty, accountability, respect, confidentiality, altruism, commitment to excellence and self-regulation.

### Conflict of interest

A **conflict of interest** exists when a **secondary interest** (financial gain, career advancement, personal relationship) creates a risk of undue influence on judgment about a **primary interest** (the patient's welfare, the integrity of research, the quality of education). A conflict can exist without any wrongdoing; the risk is what matters. Evidence shows that even small gifts influence prescribing, often without the physician being aware of it.

There are three responses: **avoid** the conflict, **disclose** it, and **manage** it (for example, by recusal or independent review). Disclosure is necessary but is often not enough on its own.

Common situations:

- **Industry gifts and meals:** many institutions bar gifts from industry. Educational items of minimal value that benefit patients may be allowed by some policies. Pharmaceutical representatives should not influence formularies or prescribing.
- **Industry payments:** the **Physician Payments Sunshine Act** (part of the Affordable Care Act) requires manufacturers to report payments to physicians, which are published in a federal public database called Open Payments.
- **Speakers' bureaus, consulting and research funding:** disclose in presentations and publications; accept no payment for prescribing.
- **Self-referral and kickbacks:** the federal **Anti-Kickback Statute** prohibits paying or receiving anything of value to induce referrals of items or services paid for by federal programs. The **Stark law** prohibits physicians from referring Medicare patients for certain designated health services to entities with which they or immediate family have a financial relationship, with exceptions.
- **Gifts from patients:** small tokens are generally acceptable; large gifts or bequests create conflicts and are generally declined or reported to the institution.

### Boundaries

Physicians hold power and have access to intimate information. Maintaining boundaries protects patients.

- **Sexual or romantic relationships with current patients** are unethical and can lead to loss of license, regardless of the patient's apparent consent. Relationships with former patients are generally discouraged if the physician could exploit the earlier relationship (especially in psychiatry).
- **Treating oneself or family:** discouraged because objectivity and confidentiality suffer, except in emergencies or minor problems when no other physician is available.
- **Social media:** do not post patient information, even without names, if the patient could be identified; do not friend patients; keep professional presentation.
- **Dual relationships** (employer, friend, business partner) should be avoided or managed.

### Honesty and conduct

Professional honesty includes accurate documentation (do not copy forward findings that you did not examine, and do not alter records), truthful reporting of data in research, and honesty about what you did or did not do as a student (never report an examination you did not perform). Respectful behavior toward patients and colleagues, including recognition of bias, is expected, and so is accountability for mistakes.

### Impaired and incompetent colleagues

If you suspect a colleague is impaired (substance use, illness, cognitive decline) or incompetent in a way that endangers patients, you have an **ethical duty to act**. First ensure patient safety, then report to the appropriate supervisor, department chair or hospital program (often a physician health program), and when required, to the state medical board. Many states mandate reporting. A student or resident should report to a supervisor or the institution's designated office. Do not ignore the problem, confront the colleague alone and leave it there, or cover for the colleague. Physician health programs offer confidential monitoring and treatment, and many physicians return to practice.

### Duty hours and fatigue

Fatigue increases errors. The ACGME limits resident duty hours to **80 hours per week averaged over 4 weeks**, with one day off in seven (averaged), and limits on continuous duty (up to 24 hours plus up to 4 hours for transitions). Trainees should speak up about unsafe supervision or fatigue.

### Learner mistreatment and supervision

Students and residents should be supervised at a level that matches their skill. Report mistreatment through institutional channels; retaliation is prohibited. A student must not perform procedures beyond their competence without supervision.

@example
title: An intoxicated colleague
problem: A third-year student sees a surgical attending smelling of alcohol, unsteady and slurring, about to scrub for an elective operation. Others have joked about it before. What should the student do?
step: Recognize the duty: suspicion of impairment that threatens patient safety creates an ethical duty to act. The risk is immediate because the attending is about to operate.
step: Ensure patient safety first: tell the senior resident, charge nurse or chief of surgery (or the anesthesiologist) right away so the operation can be stopped or reassigned. The student should not scrub in or take part in an unsafe procedure.
step: Report through the institution's channel, such as the department chair or the medical staff office or program for impaired physicians, and describe observations factually without diagnosing.
step: Understand the aim: patient safety and getting the colleague help. Avoid gossip and avoid confronting the attending alone or ignoring the matter.

@mistakes
- Believing disclosure alone eliminates a conflict of interest.
- Thinking that a patient's consent makes a sexual relationship with a current patient acceptable.
- Staying silent about an impaired colleague because "everyone knows" or because reporting feels disloyal.
- Posting a case with identifying details on social media because the patient's name is omitted.

@practice self
prompt: A hospital's cardiologist owns part of an imaging center and refers patients there. Explain why this is a conflict of interest, which federal laws may apply, and how the conflict could be managed.
solution: The cardiologist's financial gain from referrals may influence judgment about whether tests are needed and where they should be done, so the secondary interest (profit) may compromise the primary interest (the patient's welfare and appropriate use of resources). The Stark law restricts referrals of Medicare patients for designated health services to entities in which the physician has a financial interest unless an exception applies, and the Anti-Kickback Statute prohibits payments to induce referrals. Management: avoid the arrangement or ensure it fits an exception, disclose it to patients, offer alternatives, base orders on guidelines, and have compliance review.
