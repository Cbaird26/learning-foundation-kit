@article
Ethics is the disciplined study of what we ought to do. **Medical ethics** applies that question to patient care, and it is tested on the USMLE mostly through vignettes in which a clinician must choose the best next step. You will not memorize a formula for "the right answer"; you will learn a vocabulary and a method that make your reasoning explicit and defensible.

### The four principles

The most widely taught framework (Beauchamp and Childress) uses four prima facie principles. "Prima facie" means each binds you unless it is outweighed by a stronger, competing duty in the case at hand.

| Principle | Core meaning | Typical clinical expression |
|---|---|---|
| **Respect for autonomy** | Honor the self-governing choices of a capable person | Informed consent, informed refusal, truth-telling, confidentiality |
| **Beneficence** | Act to benefit the patient | Recommending effective treatment, preventive care |
| **Nonmaleficence** | Do not inflict harm ("first, do no harm") | Avoiding treatments whose burdens outweigh benefits; stopping a harmful drug |
| **Justice** | Fairness in distributing benefits, burdens and resources | Equal access, triage criteria, fair research selection |

Nonmaleficence and beneficence are not the same. Beneficence is a positive duty to help; nonmaleficence is a duty to refrain from harm. Most treatments carry some harm, so clinicians weigh benefit against burden.

### Related duties

Several duties follow from the principles. **Veracity** is truthfulness. **Fidelity** is keeping promises and staying loyal to the patient's interests. **Confidentiality** is protecting private information (week 4). **Respect for persons** also includes privacy and dignity.

### When principles conflict

Conflict is the usual reason a case is hard. The classic conflict is **autonomy versus beneficence**: a capable adult may refuse a recommended, even life-saving, treatment. When a capable patient makes an informed refusal, the right answer on exams is almost always to respect it, while continuing to explore the reasons and to offer other care. Overriding a capable patient "for their own good" is **paternalism**, which is justified only in narrow circumstances, such as lack of decision-making capacity (week 3).

### Ethics, law and professionalism

These overlap but are not identical. A law can permit what ethics discourages, and an ethical duty can exceed the law. **Professionalism** is the set of behaviors and commitments (competence, honesty, accountability, putting patients' interests first) that the profession expects of you. When you are unsure, consult an ethics committee or ethics consultation service, which advises but does not decide or order care.

### A four-topics method

A common bedside method (Jonsen, Siegler and Winslade) organizes any case under four headings:

1. **Medical indications:** diagnosis, prognosis, goals of treatment, benefits and risks (beneficence and nonmaleficence).
2. **Patient preferences:** capacity, informed choices, who speaks for the patient (autonomy).
3. **Quality of life:** from the patient's perspective, and what the patient would consider acceptable.
4. **Contextual features:** family, finances, law, institutional policy, resource allocation (justice).

Write the facts under each heading first, then name the conflict, then decide and justify. A structured analysis also tells you which facts are missing, such as whether capacity was ever assessed.

### Double effect

The **principle of double effect** distinguishes foreseen from intended effects. An action with a good intended effect (relief of pain with an opioid) and a foreseen but unintended bad effect (respiratory depression) can be ethically acceptable when the good effect is the intention, the bad effect is not the means to the good, and the good outweighs the harm. It underlies the acceptability of opioid titration for comfort at the end of life.

@example
title: A capable patient declines a recommended transfusion
problem: A 52-year-old woman with a bleeding gastric ulcer has a hemoglobin of 6.2 g/dL and is hemodynamically stable. She is alert, understands that she could die without transfusion, and declines red cell transfusion because of her religious beliefs. She accepts endoscopic therapy and iron. Her sister urges the team to transfuse anyway. What is the best action, and which principles are in conflict?
step: Identify the conflict. Beneficence (transfusion would benefit her) and nonmaleficence (avoiding death) pull toward transfusion; respect for autonomy pulls toward honoring her refusal.
step: Check the key facts under the four-topics method. Preferences: she is alert, understands the risk and consequences, and her refusal is consistent with long-held beliefs, so she appears to have capacity. Medical indications: transfusion is effective but not the only treatment; endoscopy and iron are acceptable to her.
step: Apply the rule. A capable adult may refuse any treatment, including life-saving treatment. Her sister is not the decision-maker while the patient has capacity.
step: Decide. Respect the informed refusal, document the discussion, continue all acceptable treatment, and offer blood-sparing strategies. If she later loses capacity, her known wishes still guide care.

@mistakes
- Treating beneficence as automatically stronger than autonomy; a capable patient's informed refusal generally prevails.
- Calling any disagreement with a patient "paternalism"; paternalism means overriding a patient's choice for the patient's benefit.
- Letting a relative's wishes override a capable patient's own decision.
- Assuming that what is legal is therefore ethically sufficient, or that an ethics consultant gives binding orders.

@practice self
prompt: A 45-year-old man with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes is told by his physician to start insulin. He has capacity and refuses because he fears needles. He asks the physician not to tell his employer. Analyze the case using the four principles: name which principles are involved, how they interact, and what the physician should do.
solution: Autonomy: he has capacity and may refuse insulin; the physician should explore the fear, explain risks of poor control, and offer alternatives such as non-injectable agents, pen devices or a nurse-led teaching visit. Beneficence and nonmaleficence: insulin may help, but oral or other agents can be reasonable if he declines, and the physician should keep recommending effective care without coercion. Confidentiality (a duty arising from autonomy and fidelity): his health information must not be disclosed to the employer without his authorization. Justice is not central here. The physician respects the informed refusal, documents the discussion, offers follow-up, and protects his privacy.
rubric: Identifies autonomy, beneficence and nonmaleficence and states that capacity makes his refusal binding.
rubric: Proposes exploring the fear and offering alternatives rather than coercion or abandonment.
rubric: Addresses confidentiality regarding the employer.
hint: Use the four-topics method: indications, preferences, quality of life, context.
