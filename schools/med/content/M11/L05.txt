@article
In law a **minor** is a person under the age of majority, which is 18 in most U.S. states. The default rule is that **a parent or legal guardian gives consent** for a minor's medical care. Ethically, children are not simply property of their parents: the parents' authority exists to serve the child's interests, and the child's developing autonomy deserves respect. States differ in details, so learn the general framework that exam questions test.

### Assent

**Assent** is a child's agreement to care, appropriate to developmental level. Children roughly 7 years and older should be told what will happen and asked for their agreement, but assent is not legally binding. The child's strong objection to a non-urgent intervention should be taken seriously, while the parent's consent is still required.

### Exceptions: when a minor can consent

1. **Emergency.** Treatment of an emergency proceeds without parental consent when delay would risk life or health.
2. **Emancipated minors.** A minor who is emancipated has the legal rights of an adult. Typical routes are marriage, active military service, a court order of emancipation, or (in some states) financial independence with living apart from parents. Having a child or being self-supporting alone does not always qualify.
3. **Minor-consent statutes for specific services.** Every state lets minors consent without parents to some services, because fear of parental involvement would deter care. These usually include **testing and treatment of sexually transmitted infections**, **contraception**, **prenatal care** and care related to pregnancy, **substance use treatment** and often **outpatient mental health care** (the age limit varies). Laws on abortion for minors vary widely by state, including parental notification or consent requirements and judicial bypass.
4. **Mature minor doctrine.** In some states, courts or statutes allow a minor who shows adult-level understanding to consent to a particular treatment, especially for lower-risk decisions. It is applied case by case and varies by state; it is not a general right.

Notice what these exceptions share: a minor who may consent to a service also controls its confidentiality, in most cases. A minor who consents to contraception does not need a parent's authorization for that service, and clinicians should not call the parent.

### Confidentiality for adolescents

Adolescent care is best done with part of the visit alone with the patient. The clinician explains at the start the limits of confidentiality: confidentiality will be broken for risk of serious harm to self or others and for abuse. A structured psychosocial interview (such as HEADSS: home, education, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide and depression) helps screen for risk. Beware of billing and insurance statements (explanations of benefits) that can inadvertently reveal confidential services to parents; discuss this with the patient.

### Parental refusal of treatment

Parents have broad discretion in how to raise their children, but not unlimited discretion. The state has an interest in protecting children (the doctrine of **parens patriae**). The usual approach:

- **Life-threatening condition and effective treatment** (for example, antibiotics for bacterial meningitis, chemotherapy for curable leukemia, or a blood transfusion for life-threatening anemia in a child of Jehovah's Witness parents): treat in an emergency and seek a court order if time allows. Religious beliefs do not give parents the right to deny a child needed life-saving care.
- **Serious but not life-threatening, or uncertain benefit:** explore the parents' concerns, give time and offer alternatives; courts are more reluctant to intervene.
- **Refusal of routine care such as vaccination:** counsel repeatedly and document; refusing vaccines is not by itself reportable neglect, though state laws on school exemptions vary.

Child protective services may be involved when refusal of necessary care amounts to medical neglect. Involve the ethics committee and risk management early, and keep communicating with the family.

### Reporting

The abuse and neglect reporting duty (week 4) overrides confidentiality and parental authority. Sexual activity of a young adolescent with an older partner may trigger mandatory reporting depending on state age-of-consent laws.

@example
title: A 15-year-old asks for confidential contraception
problem: A 15-year-old girl comes alone to a clinic requesting oral contraceptives. She is sexually active with a boyfriend of the same age, says her parents would be angry, and asks that they not be told. She is on her parents' insurance. What should the physician do?
step: Identify the rule. Every state allows minors to consent to some contraceptive services. This is a minor-consent statute exception, so the parents' consent is not required and no parental notice is needed for this service.
step: Confirm she understands the options, risks and how to use the pill, and screen for coercion or abuse, STIs and pregnancy, since an older or coercive partner would change reporting duties.
step: Protect confidentiality: explain its limits (serious harm, abuse), and talk about insurance statements that might reveal the visit. Encourage, but do not require, involving a parent.
step: Provide the service, document, and arrange follow-up.

@mistakes
- Requiring parental consent for services that minors may legally consent to, such as STI treatment and contraception.
- Assuming 18 is the cutoff for every service, or that all states have the same mature-minor rules; laws vary.
- Calling the parents about a minor's confidential visit without a legal exception.
- Deferring to a parent's refusal of life-saving treatment for a child on religious grounds instead of treating and seeking a court order.

solution: The general rule is that a parent or guardian consents for a minor, with the child's assent when developmentally appropriate.
