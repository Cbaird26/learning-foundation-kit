@article
Medicine can sustain organ function long after a patient would have chosen to stop. End-of-life ethics asks how to honor patients' values when they are dying or cannot speak for themselves.

### The right to refuse

A capable patient may refuse any treatment, including mechanical ventilation, dialysis, antibiotics, and artificial nutrition and hydration, which the law and professional bodies treat as medical treatments rather than basic care. The U.S. Supreme Court in Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health (1990) recognized the right of a competent person to refuse treatment (it assumed this right for the case), and held that a state may require **clear and convincing evidence** of an incapacitated patient's prior wishes before surrogates withdraw life support. This is why clear documentation of wishes matters.

**Withholding and withdrawing** life-sustaining treatment are **ethically and legally equivalent**: if a treatment is no longer wanted or beneficial, stopping it is as acceptable as never starting it. A trial of treatment can be time-limited, and stopping it is allowed if goals are not met. Death after withdrawal results from the underlying disease, not from the clinician.

### Advance directives

An **advance directive** records wishes in advance, completed by a capable adult and revocable at any time.

- **Living will:** instructions about particular treatments in specified situations (for example, no mechanical ventilation if permanently unconscious).
- **Durable power of attorney for health care (healthcare proxy):** names an agent to decide if the patient loses capacity. Many experts consider it more flexible than a living will, because no document can anticipate every situation.
- **Do-not-resuscitate (DNR) order:** a physician order not to attempt CPR. **A DNR order is not "do not treat"**; all other indicated care continues.
- **POLST (Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment, also called MOLST in some states):** a portable medical order for seriously ill or frail patients, for whom death within a year would not be surprising. It converts preferences into orders that emergency medical services can follow.

The **Patient Self-Determination Act (1990)** requires hospitals and other facilities that receive Medicare or Medicaid funds to ask patients about advance directives and inform them of their rights. When a valid directive conflicts with the family's wishes, the **directive prevails**, because it expresses the patient's own voice.

### Talking about goals of care

Use a structured approach to difficult conversations. Ask what the patient understands, share information in clear language, respond to emotion, explore goals, and then recommend a plan that fits them (a "recommendation" is more helpful than listing options without guidance). A code status discussion should not be a menu: explain what CPR involves and its likely outcome in the patient's situation.

### Futility and disagreement

Clinicians are not obliged to provide treatments that cannot achieve the patient's goals or that offer no physiologic benefit. Because "futility" is hard to define, most hospitals use a fair process: communicate, consult ethics, seek second opinions, attempt transfer to another willing clinician, and then, if needed, follow the policy for refusal of requested treatment.

### Comfort care and double effect

Opioids and sedatives titrated to relieve suffering are ethically accepted even when they might hasten death, provided the intent is relief of symptoms (double effect, week 1) and doses are proportionate. **Palliative sedation** for refractory suffering at the end of life is distinct from euthanasia. **Hospice** provides comfort-focused care for patients whose life expectancy is about 6 months or less if the illness follows its usual course (the Medicare hospice criterion); a patient may leave hospice at any time.

### Assisted dying

In **euthanasia** a physician directly administers a lethal agent; it is illegal throughout the United States. In **physician-assisted death (physician aid in dying)** the physician prescribes a lethal medication that the patient self-administers. In Washington v. Glucksberg and Vacco v. Quill (1997) the Supreme Court held that there is no constitutional right to assisted suicide, and that states may prohibit it, and that the distinction between withdrawing treatment and assisted suicide is legitimate. Several states, beginning with Oregon, permit it by statute under safeguards (details vary by state): an adult resident with decision-making capacity, a terminal illness with a prognosis of 6 months or less, repeated requests, confirmation by two physicians, and self-administration. Clinicians may decline to participate. The AMA considers physician-assisted suicide incompatible with the physician's role, while the question remains contested.

### Death by neurologic criteria (brain death)

Death is declared by circulatory or neurologic criteria. The Uniform Determination of Death Act defines it as irreversible cessation of circulatory and respiratory functions or irreversible cessation of all functions of the **entire brain, including the brainstem**. The evaluation requires a known cause of catastrophic brain injury, exclusion of confounders (hypothermia, sedatives, severe metabolic derangement), coma, absent brainstem reflexes, and an **apnea test** showing no respiratory effort when the arterial carbon dioxide is at least 60 mm Hg and at least 20 mm Hg above baseline (the 2023 guideline requires both; older criteria accepted either). Ancillary tests (such as cerebral blood flow studies) are used if the apnea test cannot be completed. Brain death is **death**: consent is not required to stop ventilation, although families need time and support. A patient in a **persistent vegetative state (unresponsive wakefulness)** is alive, with preserved brainstem function.

@example
title: A directive conflicts with the family's wishes
problem: A 79-year-old man with end-stage COPD completed a living will and named his daughter as healthcare proxy. His directive says no intubation. He now has respiratory failure and is confused. His son demands intubation. What should the team do?
step: Establish who speaks for the patient: the daughter is the named agent, and she ranks above the son. The directive is valid and applies to this situation.
step: Recognize that a valid directive and the agent's interpretation of it take precedence over the son's wishes, because they express the patient's own choice.
step: Do not intubate; provide all other indicated care, including noninvasive support if consistent with his wishes and treatment of symptoms. Clarify with the daughter what he would accept.
step: Support the son, arrange a family meeting, and involve palliative care and the ethics service if conflict persists.

@mistakes
- Thinking that withdrawing a ventilator is ethically worse than never starting it.
- Reading a DNR order as a decision to stop all treatment.
- Following the family's wishes over a valid advance directive and an appointed proxy.
- Confusing brain death with a persistent vegetative state, or requiring family consent to declare brain death.
