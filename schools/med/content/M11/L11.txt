@article
Research aims to produce generalizable knowledge. Its participants may bear risk without personal benefit, so ethics requires protections that clinical care does not. Modern research ethics grew out of abuses.

### History

- The **Nuremberg Code** (1947), written after the trials of physicians who conducted atrocities in concentration camps, states that the **voluntary consent of the human subject is absolutely essential** and that subjects may withdraw at any time.
- The **Declaration of Helsinki** (World Medical Association, 1964, revised many times) states the ethical principles of medical research, including independent committee review, consent, and priority of subjects' welfare over science.
- The **Tuskegee Study of Untreated Syphilis** (1932 to 1972) followed Black men with syphilis without informing them of the diagnosis and withheld penicillin after it became standard treatment. It involved deception, no informed consent and exploitation of a vulnerable group.
- The **Belmont Report** (1979) set three principles: **respect for persons** (informed consent, protection of those with diminished autonomy), **beneficence** (maximize benefits and minimize harms, a favorable risk-benefit ratio) and **justice** (fair selection of subjects, so no group bears the burdens while another reaps the benefits).
- The **Common Rule** (45 CFR 46) is the U.S. federal regulation for research funded by federal agencies, now adopted by many institutions for all human research. The FDA has parallel regulations for products it regulates.

### Institutional Review Boards

An **institutional review board (IRB)** reviews and approves human subjects research before it begins and monitors it. It must have at least five members of varied backgrounds, including at least one scientist, one non-scientist and one member not affiliated with the institution. Review categories: **exempt** (certain low-risk categories, such as anonymous surveys or analysis of existing de-identified data, which should still be determined by the IRB or its designee), **expedited** (no more than minimal risk in listed categories) and **full board review**. **Minimal risk** means that the probability and magnitude of harm are not greater than those ordinarily encountered in daily life or during routine physical or psychological examinations.

### Consent in research

Research consent must be voluntary and cover: the purpose and procedures, duration, risks and benefits, alternatives (including not participating), confidentiality, any compensation or medical care for injury, whom to contact, and that participation is voluntary and subjects can withdraw without penalty or loss of benefits. Payment should not be so large as to be an undue inducement. Waivers of consent are possible only with IRB approval, for minimal-risk research for which consent is impracticable. Emergency research exceptions exist under strict conditions.

### Vulnerable populations

Additional protections apply to **children** (parental permission plus the child's assent, with limits on risk without direct benefit), **prisoners** (coercion concerns), **pregnant women and fetuses**, and **adults with cognitive impairment**. Students and employees of the investigator may feel pressured. Vulnerability should not be an excuse for exclusion either: children and pregnant people deserve evidence about treatments, so justice requires inclusion with safeguards.

### Equipoise, placebos and monitoring

A randomized trial is ethical when there is **clinical equipoise**: genuine uncertainty in the expert community about which arm is better. A placebo control is acceptable when no proven effective treatment exists or when withholding treatment poses only minor, temporary risks. A **data safety monitoring board (DSMB)**, independent of the investigators, reviews interim data and can stop a trial early for harm, clear benefit or futility. Serious adverse events must be reported to the IRB.

### Research versus clinical care

The **therapeutic misconception** is the mistaken belief that research participation is designed to benefit them personally, as treatment would. The consent process should correct it. Clinicians who recruit their own patients have dual roles, so patients must understand that declining will not affect their care. A single innovative treatment of one patient is not research, but systematic investigation to produce generalizable knowledge is.

### Integrity and publication

**Research misconduct** is **fabrication** (making up data), **falsification** (manipulating or omitting data) and **plagiarism** (using others' ideas or words without credit). It does not include honest error or differences of opinion. Authorship (ICMJE criteria) requires substantial contribution to the work, drafting or revising it, final approval, and accountability; gift authorship and ghost authorship are unacceptable. Investigators disclose conflicts of interest, and results should be reported honestly, including negative results.

@example
title: Evaluating a proposed study
problem: An investigator proposes a trial of a new oral drug versus placebo for moderate hypertension in adults, with a 12-week placebo arm and monitoring of blood pressure every 2 weeks. Hypertension has well-established effective treatments, and some participants have blood pressures near 170/105 mm Hg. Evaluate it.
step: Apply the Belmont principles. Respect for persons: consent must state clearly that effective treatments exist. Beneficence: risk-benefit must be favorable; participants with very high blood pressure face a risk from an inactive placebo.
step: Check equipoise and placebo use. Because effective treatment exists, a placebo-only arm for patients with markedly elevated pressure is hard to justify. An active-control or add-on design, or exclusion of high-risk patients, is more ethical.
step: Consider safeguards: a DSMB, predefined blood pressure thresholds for rescue therapy and withdrawal, and independent IRB approval.
step: Conclusion: revise the design (active control or add-on, rescue criteria) before approval.

@mistakes
- Believing that consent from a child's parent alone is sufficient; the child's assent is also required when feasible.
- Calling research "exempt" without IRB or designee determination.
- Confusing the therapeutic misconception with deception by the investigator; it is a participant's mistaken belief.
- Thinking misconduct includes honest error.

@practice self
prompt: A physician-investigator invites her own patient with advanced cancer to join a phase 1 trial. Explain the ethical concerns about consent, including the therapeutic misconception, and how the consent discussion should address them.
solution: Phase 1 trials mainly test safety and dosing; benefit is uncertain and unlikely. The patient may believe the trial is treatment designed to help him (therapeutic misconception), and he may feel unable to refuse his own physician. The consent process should state the primary purpose is knowledge, describe risks, the low chance of direct benefit, alternatives (standard care, palliative care), voluntariness, and that declining or withdrawing will not affect his care. Having a different member of the team or an independent person assist, and allowing time to decide, helps.
