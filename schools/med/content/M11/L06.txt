@article
Reproductive decisions touch the deepest personal values, so they are governed above all by respect for autonomy: capable patients decide whether and when to have children. The clinician's role is to inform, to avoid coercion, and to keep personal views from distorting counseling.

### Contraception and sterilization

Patients may choose any contraceptive method after counseling. A patient's consent is the only consent required; a spouse's or partner's permission is not needed, and clinicians should not refuse a method on grounds of age or marital status alone. Counsel about effectiveness, side effects and alternatives, with a bias toward the patient's goals.

**Sterilization** (tubal ligation, vasectomy) requires full informed consent from a capable adult, with discussion of permanence and reversible alternatives. Do not sterilize a patient who lacks capacity merely for convenience or "for their own good"; courts require a rigorous process, and the history of coerced sterilization is a reason for strict safeguards. Federal rules for sterilization paid for by Medicaid (state law may add requirements) require that the patient be at least 21 and mentally competent, give voluntary written consent at least 30 days, and no more than 180 days, before the procedure, and not be consented during labor or childbirth, while seeking or obtaining an abortion, or under duress. The narrow exceptions are premature delivery and emergency abdominal surgery, for which the 30-day wait is shortened (at least 72 hours between consent and the procedure; for premature delivery, consent must also have been given at least 30 days before the expected delivery date). Offering sterilization immediately after delivery to a patient who has not previously discussed it raises concern about coercion.

### Abortion

In Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022), the Supreme Court held that the federal constitution does not protect a right to abortion and returned regulation to states. Laws on abortion therefore vary widely: gestational limits, exceptions, waiting periods and rules for minors differ by state. Whatever the local law, ethical practice means giving accurate information, respecting the patient's decision and referring or transferring when allowed. Under the federal **EMTALA** law, hospitals with emergency departments must screen and stabilize emergency medical conditions, including pregnancy complications that threaten the patient's life or health, and how it interacts with state abortion laws is legally contested and changing: state laws differ, and federal guidance and litigation have changed since 2022, so check current law and hospital policy. Know the general principle that emergency conditions require a screening examination and stabilizing care or an appropriate transfer.

### Prenatal testing and nondirective counseling

Prenatal screening (cell-free DNA, ultrasonography) and diagnostic testing (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling) are offered with **nondirective counseling**: explain what the test can and cannot show, what results would mean, and that the choice is the patient's. Screening tests estimate risk; they do not diagnose. A positive screen has a **positive predictive value** that depends on prevalence, so for rare conditions most positives can be false.

### Assisted reproduction

In vitro fertilization, gamete donation and gestational surrogacy raise questions of consent, embryo disposition (agreements made in advance usually guide disputes, though courts differ), the welfare of the child, and justice (cost limits access). Sex selection for non-medical reasons is controversial, and professional bodies generally discourage it.

### Maternal-fetal conflict

A pregnant patient with capacity keeps the same right to refuse treatment as any patient, including surgery or cesarean delivery. Courts have only rarely ordered treatment, and professional ethics bodies oppose coercion of pregnant patients. The best approach is patient-centered communication, exploring the reasons for refusal, offering alternatives, and involving ethics early. Pregnant people who lack capacity are treated through surrogates, with respect for their known wishes.

### Conscientious objection

Clinicians may have moral objections to some services, such as abortion or contraception. Professional guidance generally holds that a clinician may decline to provide a service, but must: inform the patient honestly of all options, not impose personal beliefs, ensure the patient can obtain care (through referral or transfer, as the law and policy allow), and **never abandon a patient or refuse emergency care**. Institutional policies and state laws differ on the duty to refer. The objection is based on the clinician's integrity, not on the patient's characteristics or convenience.

### Minors in reproductive care

The rules in week 5 apply: minors can consent to contraception and prenatal care in most states, and confidentiality is protected; parental involvement laws for abortion differ by state, often with a judicial bypass. Suspected abuse or statutory rape triggers mandatory reporting.

@example
title: A positive screening test with low prevalence
problem: A cell-free DNA screening test for a chromosomal condition has a sensitivity of 90% and a false positive rate of 5% in a population in which the condition has a prevalence of 1 in 500. A patient in this population tests positive. What is the positive predictive value, and how should she be counseled?
step: Convert to probabilities: prevalence $0.002$, sensitivity $0.90$, false positive rate $0.05$.
step: True positives per person tested: $0.90 \times 0.002 = 0.0018$. False positives: $0.05 \times 0.998 = 0.0499$.
step: $\text{PPV} = \frac{0.0018}{0.0018 + 0.0499} \approx 0.035$, or about 3.5%. Most positive screens in this population are false positives.
step: Counseling: explain that the test is a screen, not a diagnosis; offer diagnostic testing (CVS or amniocentesis) without pressure, discuss the small procedure risk, and support her choice nondirectively.

@mistakes
- Requiring spousal consent for contraception or sterilization of a capable adult.
- Treating a positive prenatal screening result as a diagnosis, or counseling toward a particular choice.
- Believing conscientious objection allows refusing emergency care, or hiding options from the patient.
- Forgetting that a capable pregnant patient may refuse treatment recommended for fetal benefit.
