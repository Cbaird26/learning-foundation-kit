@article
Medicine is complex, and errors happen. Ethics and professionalism ask two things of you: to learn from errors systematically and to be honest with patients when an error affects them. This lesson combines patient safety science and the ethics of disclosure.

### Vocabulary

- **Error:** a failure to carry out a planned action as intended, or use of a wrong plan. Errors can be of commission or omission.
- **Adverse event:** harm caused by medical care rather than the underlying disease. Not all adverse events involve error (a known drug reaction in a correctly prescribed drug is an adverse event without error).
- **Preventable adverse event:** harm caused by error.
- **Near miss:** an error that did not reach the patient or did not cause harm. Near misses are valuable for learning.
- **Sentinel event:** an unexpected event causing death or serious physical or psychological injury (or risk thereof), for which the Joint Commission expects review and a root cause analysis.
- **Never event:** a serious, preventable event that should never occur, such as surgery on the wrong site or patient, or retained surgical objects.

### Why errors happen: systems thinking

The **Swiss cheese model** (Reason) describes layers of defense, each with holes. An error reaches the patient when holes in several layers line up: for example, a look-alike drug label, a rushed shift, no pharmacy verification and an alert fatigued by too many alarms. Individual blame fixes little; improving the system does. Common contributors include handoff failures, fatigue, poor communication, illegible or ambiguous orders, look-alike or sound-alike drugs, and cognitive errors such as anchoring and premature closure.

A **just culture** distinguishes human error (console and fix the system), at-risk behavior (coach), and reckless behavior (discipline), so staff are willing to report. **Root cause analysis (RCA)** reviews a serious event to find system causes, and tools such as checklists, time-outs, read-backs, standardized handoffs (for example I-PASS) and computerized order entry with decision support reduce errors.

### The duty to disclose

Patients are entitled to know about events that affect their care. Professional organizations (AMA, Joint Commission and others) and most hospitals hold that physicians must **disclose errors that cause harm**, and that disclosure is also appropriate for errors that did not harm but that the patient would want to know about when they reach the patient. The ethical basis is veracity, respect for autonomy (the patient needs information to make decisions and seek care) and fidelity. Concealment destroys trust and also increases the chance of litigation, as patients who feel deceived are more likely to sue.

### How to disclose

Prepare with risk management and the attending, but do not delay needed disclosure. Disclose promptly, in a private setting, with the person or surrogate. Include:

1. **What happened**, in plain facts, without speculation or blaming colleagues.
2. **The consequences** now and possible later effects, and the plan for care and monitoring.
3. **An apology and expression of responsibility.** Many states have **apology laws** that make expressions of sympathy (and in some states, admissions of fault) inadmissible in court; the scope varies by state. An apology is an ethical duty, not an admission of liability by itself.
4. **What is being done to find out why** it happened and to prevent recurrence.
5. **Support:** a named contact, follow-up meetings, and help with costs when institutional policy provides.

Avoid minimizing, defensive language and false certainty ("it was nobody's fault"). Do not alter the medical record; document the facts of the event and the disclosure conversation objectively, but do not refer to incident reports in the chart.

### Reporting

Report events and near misses through the institution's safety reporting system. Incident reports are for quality improvement and are usually protected from discovery (patient safety work product); they are not part of the chart. State laws require reporting of certain serious events to state agencies. The first victim is the patient, but clinicians involved are the "second victims": they need peer support, and should not be left to cope alone.

@example
title: Planning a disclosure after a wrong-dose error
problem: A 6-year-old receives a tenfold overdose of morphine because a decimal point was misplaced in the order. She becomes lethargic with slow respirations, is given naloxone and recovers in the pediatric intensive care unit without lasting harm. The resident who wrote the order asks whether to tell the parents. What should happen?
step: Classify the event: a preventable adverse event (a medication error that reached the patient and caused harm), likely meeting criteria for review.
step: Decide: disclosure is required. The parents are the child's surrogates and are entitled to know what happened and its effect.
step: Plan the conversation with the attending and risk management: private room, a clear statement of the facts (an incorrect dose due to a decimal error), the effects and the reversal treatment, a sincere apology, the monitoring plan, and what will be done to prevent recurrence (for example, review of order entry safeguards).
step: Afterward, document the event objectively in the chart, file a safety report separately, support the resident, and follow up with the family.

@mistakes
- Equating every adverse event with error; some harm occurs despite correct care.
- Blaming the individual instead of analyzing the system.
- Hiding or delaying disclosure to protect the clinician from liability, which erodes trust and increases risk.
- Documenting the incident report or blame in the medical record, or altering the record.
