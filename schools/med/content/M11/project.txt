@project
title: Ethics consultation: three cases, one structured method
overview: You are a member of a hospital ethics consultation service. Using a structured method, you will analyze three supplied cases and write a consultation note for each with a clear recommendation that answers the strongest counterargument. The aim is to show that you can reason from principles, facts and law to a defensible action.
goal: Apply a structured case method (medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life, contextual features) and the four principles to real-style cases.
goal: Identify the legal rules that constrain each case and say where state law varies.
goal: Write a recommendation that states the best action, the reasoning and a fair response to the opposing view.
step: Case 1 (consent, capacity and surrogates). Mr. A is a 74-year-old man with chronic kidney disease stage 5, admitted with volume overload, hyperkalemia and confusion. His nephrologist recommends starting dialysis tonight. Mr. A is intermittently drowsy and, when awake, says "No machines, I want to go home." His wife says he told her last year that he would never want dialysis. His daughter says he is confused from uremia and that dialysis may restore him to normal. He has no advance directive or proxy. His potassium is 6.8 mEq/L with peaked T waves. His confusion fluctuates through the day.
step: Case 2 (confidentiality and a minor). Ms. B is a 15-year-old girl who comes alone to an adolescent clinic. She tests positive for chlamydia and also tells the physician that she has been sexually active for a year with a 20-year-old partner and that she is afraid her father, who is strict, will find out. She asks that nothing be shared with her parents. She is covered by her father's insurance, which sends explanation-of-benefits statements to the home. She has also had passing thoughts of "not wanting to be here" but denies a plan or intent.
step: Case 3 (error disclosure and allocation). Dr. C, a night-float resident, realizes that a patient, Mr. D, received a double dose of a heparin infusion for 6 hours because of a pump programming error. Mr. D has a small gastrointestinal bleed and needs a transfusion of one unit of red cells but is stable. Dr. C is afraid to report it and says the attending has a bad temper. In the same unit, there is one remaining monitored ICU bed, and Mr. D's needs for close monitoring conflict with another patient's need for the bed (a 40-year-old woman with sepsis who is deteriorating). Mr. D's risk is moderate and monitored care could also be provided in a step-down unit with additional nursing.
step: Read the three cases twice. For each, list the facts under the four headings: medical indications, patient preferences, quality of life and contextual features. Mark missing facts you would need to ask.
step: State the ethical question in one sentence for each case, and name the principles in conflict.
step: Identify the relevant concepts and rules: capacity, surrogate hierarchy and standards, disclosure, confidentiality exceptions, minor consent rules, advance directives, error disclosure, or allocation criteria. Note any rule that varies by state.
step: List at least two options for each case and the strongest argument for the option you will not recommend.
step: Write a consultation note for each case of 250 to 400 words, with this structure: question, key facts, principles in conflict, analysis, recommendation (specific and actionable), response to the main counterargument, and follow-up (who speaks to whom, what is documented, when to reassess).
step: Review your three notes for the most common errors: deciding on diagnosis instead of capacity, using family wishes over a valid proxy, forgetting an exception to confidentiality, or leaving a recommendation vague. Revise.
step: Write a one-paragraph reflection on which case was hardest and what additional information might change your recommendation.
deliverable: Three consultation notes (250 to 400 words each) following the structure above.
deliverable: A one-paragraph reflection naming what additional information would change each recommendation.
rubric: Facts are organized under the four headings, and missing facts are identified.
rubric: The principles in conflict are named correctly and the analysis shows how they are weighed, not just listed.
rubric: Legal rules (capacity, surrogates, confidentiality exceptions, minor consent, reporting, EMTALA) are used correctly, and state variation is acknowledged.
rubric: Each recommendation is specific and actionable, and addresses a strong counterargument fairly.
rubric: Notes use respectful, person-first language and do not blame individuals.
safety: All cases are invented. This project is a teaching exercise and is not legal advice or advice about any real person's care.
tutor: Which facts in this case would change my assessment of capacity, and how would I test them?
tutor: What is the strongest argument against my recommendation, and how would I answer it in front of the family?
tutor: Which part of this case depends on state law, and what general principle applies if I do not know the local rule?

### Week-by-week key ideas

- **Week 1, principles:** autonomy, beneficence, nonmaleficence and justice; conflicts; paternalism; the four-topics method; double effect.
- **Week 2, informed consent:** elements (capacity, voluntariness, disclosure, understanding, decision); reasonable-patient versus professional standards; exceptions (emergency, waiver, therapeutic privilege); battery versus negligence.
- **Week 3, capacity and surrogates:** four abilities; capacity is decision-specific and clinician-assessed, competence is legal; surrogate hierarchy; expressed wishes, substituted judgment, best interest.
- **Week 4, confidentiality:** HIPAA treatment, payment and operations; minimum necessary; exceptions (duty to protect, abuse, reportable diseases, court order).
- **Week 5, minors:** parental consent, assent, emancipated and mature minors, minor-consent statutes, emergency; parental refusal of life-saving care.
- **Week 6, reproductive ethics:** consent for contraception and sterilization; nondirective counseling; conscientious objection and EMTALA; maternal-fetal conflict.
- **Week 7, end of life:** withholding equals withdrawing; living will, proxy, DNR, POLST; Cruzan, Glucksberg; assisted death versus euthanasia; brain death.
- **Week 8, errors:** error types, Swiss cheese model, just culture, root cause analysis; disclosure and apology.
- **Week 9, conflicts of interest and professionalism:** primary versus secondary interests; Sunshine Act, Anti-Kickback, Stark; boundaries; impaired colleagues; duty hours.
- **Week 10, justice:** theories of justice; EMTALA; organ allocation; crisis triage; QALY and ICER.
- **Week 11, research ethics:** Nuremberg, Helsinki, Belmont, Common Rule; IRBs; vulnerable subjects; equipoise; misconduct.
- **Week 12, law:** negligence elements; res ipsa loquitur; defenses; abandonment; Good Samaritan laws; mandatory reporting.

### Self-check

- Can I distinguish capacity from competence, and state the four abilities?
- Can I name the surrogate and the standard for any scenario?
- Can I list four exceptions to confidentiality and one example of each?
- Can I explain three ways in which a minor can consent alone?
- Can I explain why stopping a treatment equals never starting it, and what a DNR order does not mean?
- Can I describe a disclosure conversation in order?
- Can I compute a QALY, an ICER and a number needed to treat?
- Can I list the four elements of negligence and apply them?

### Common errors

- Deciding by diagnosis or by disagreement instead of testing capacity.
- Following the loudest relative rather than the legal surrogate hierarchy and the patient's own wishes.
- Thinking confidentiality is absolute or that HIPAA blocks sharing for treatment.
- Calling a bad outcome malpractice, or forgetting causation and damages.
- Allocating by social worth, or treating an ethics consultant as a decision-maker.
- Forgetting that many rules (minor consent, abortion, duty to protect, reporting) vary by state.

@exam mc
prompt: A 16-year-old girl is brought to the emergency department by a friend, with right lower quadrant pain, a positive pregnancy test and a hemoperitoneum, with hypotension. She is conscious and alert and says, "My parents can't know I'm pregnant." Her parents cannot be reached. A surgeon recommends emergency laparoscopy for suspected ruptured ectopic pregnancy. Which is the best course of action?
choice: Wait until her parents are reached before operating
choice: Obtain a court order before surgery
choice*: Proceed with surgery under the emergency exception and her own consent, and keep the pregnancy confidential from her parents unless she agrees to share it
choice: Refuse surgery and arrange outpatient follow-up
choice: Ask the friend to sign consent
hint: Combine the emergency exception, minor-consent rules for pregnancy-related care, and confidentiality.
hint: Delay would risk life, and pregnancy-related care is a service minors may consent to in most states.
solution: This is a life-threatening emergency, so treatment proceeds without parental consent (emergency exception), and pregnancy-related care is a service minors can usually consent to. She is alert, so explain the plan and obtain her agreement. Confidentiality continues, and she is encouraged but not required to involve her parents. A friend cannot consent, and delay for a court order would risk her life.

prompt: A trial reports that a new drug lowers the event rate from 12% in the control group to 8% in the treatment group. The drug costs \$2,000 per patient for the treatment period. What is the drug cost, in dollars, of preventing one event (the number needed to treat multiplied by the cost per patient)?
answer: 50000
check: (1 / (0.12 - 0.08)) * 2000
tol: 1%
unit: dollars
hint: Compute the number needed to treat first.
hint: $\text{NNT} = 1/(0.12 - 0.08) = 25$. Multiply by the cost per patient.
solution: $\text{ARR} = 0.04$, so $\text{NNT} = 25$. The cost per event prevented is $25 \times \$2{,}000 = \$50{,}000$. This links research results (week 11) to cost-effectiveness reasoning (week 10); the decision also involves equity and patients' values.

@exam self
prompt: A 68-year-old woman with metastatic ovarian cancer has a healthcare proxy document naming her son. She is now delirious from sepsis and unable to participate. Her daughter insists on full resuscitation and intubation; her son says that she told him she would not want a ventilator but would accept antibiotics and short-term fluids and vasopressors. A resident, rushing, tells the daughter that she can decide because she is "the closest relative." Write a consultation note that identifies the surrogate and standard, evaluates the resident's statement, recommends a plan, addresses the daughter's counterargument, and states what to document.
solution: The surrogate is the son, as the named healthcare agent, who outranks other relatives under the default hierarchy; the resident was mistaken. The standard is substituted judgment based on the patient's expressed wishes: no ventilator, but acceptance of antibiotics, fluids and vasopressors. Recommend treating sepsis with the accepted measures, entering a limitation on intubation (DNR/DNI) as the agent decides with the team after confirming that the wishes apply to this illness, with palliative care involved. Reassess capacity as delirium resolves, since she may then decide herself. The daughter's counterargument, that everything possible should be done and that she knows her mother, is understandable, but the proxy and expressed wishes govern; address her grief, hold a family meeting, offer palliative support and ethics follow-up. Document the proxy document, the discussion, the plan and the capacity assessment, and the correction of the earlier statement.
rubric: Identifies the named agent as surrogate and corrects the "closest relative" error.
rubric: Applies substituted judgment and explains what care continues.
rubric: Addresses the daughter's perspective respectfully and proposes support or a family meeting.
rubric: Includes reassessment of capacity and documentation.
hint: A named agent outranks the default hierarchy.
hint: Substituted judgment asks what the patient would choose.
