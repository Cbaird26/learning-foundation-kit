@article
Respect for autonomy applies only to patients who can make the decision at hand. **Decision-making capacity** is a clinical judgment about whether a particular patient can make a particular decision at a particular time. When capacity is lacking, someone else decides for the patient.

### Capacity versus competence

**Capacity** is determined by the treating clinician at the bedside. **Competence** is a legal status determined by a judge. In everyday speech they are blurred, but on exams the distinction matters: physicians assess capacity; courts declare incompetence and appoint guardians. Any physician can assess capacity; a psychiatry consultation helps in difficult cases but is not required, and psychiatrists do not "give" capacity.

### The four abilities

A patient has capacity for a decision if they can:

1. **Communicate a choice** that is stable enough to act on.
2. **Understand** the relevant information (diagnosis, proposed treatment, alternatives, consequences).
3. **Appreciate** how that information applies to their own situation (the patient with cancer who says "I'm sure this doesn't apply to me" does not appreciate).
4. **Reason** with the information, comparing options in light of their own values.

Test each ability by open-ended questions ("Tell me in your own words what is wrong and what will happen if we do not treat it"), not yes or no questions, and not by orientation questions alone. Orientation and the Mini-Mental State Examination do not by themselves decide capacity.

### Features of capacity

- **Decision-specific.** A patient may be able to choose a diet but not consent to a complex surgery.
- **Variable over time.** Delirium, sedation, hypoglycemia and acute psychosis can cause temporary incapacity. Treat reversible causes, and reassess.
- **A sliding scale.** The riskier the decision (refusing a life-saving drug), the more certainty you want that the patient understands and appreciates it. Accepting a clearly beneficial low-risk treatment requires less.
- **Not equal to a diagnosis.** Dementia, schizophrenia or depression do not automatically remove capacity. Age and an "unwise" choice are also not enough; the process of deciding, not the content, is assessed. A sudden unexplained change in a long-held choice does warrant a closer look.

### When capacity is lacking: surrogates

First look for the patient's own instructions: a **healthcare proxy** (a durable power of attorney for health care) names an agent, and a living will may state wishes (week 7). If none exist, state law sets a default **surrogate hierarchy**. The usual order is the court-appointed guardian or the person named as agent, then the spouse or domestic partner, then adult children, then parents, then adult siblings, and then other relatives or close friends; the exact list varies by state. Where nobody is available (an "unbefriended" patient), hospital policy and ethics committees, sometimes courts, guide decisions.

### Standards for surrogates

Surrogates use a hierarchy of standards:

1. **Expressed wishes** (advance directive or clear prior statements).
2. **Substituted judgment:** what would this patient choose, given their values? The surrogate speaks for the patient.
3. **Best interest:** what a reasonable person would want, used when the patient's wishes are unknown (for example, never-capable patients or infants).

The surrogate is a decision-maker for the patient, not a free agent: a surrogate who asks for care that would clearly harm the patient or who contradicts the patient's known wishes can be challenged, and the ethics committee or a court may be involved. A surrogate cannot demand treatment that is medically ineffective.

### Approach to the "refusing patient"

A 72-year-old man with delirium pulls out an IV and says he "wants to go home." The first step is to assess capacity for this decision and to look for the reversible cause of delirium. If capacity is lacking, treat the delirium, use the surrogate for major decisions, and use the least restrictive measures to keep him safe.

@example
title: Who decides, and by what standard?
problem: A 78-year-old woman with advanced dementia and aspiration pneumonia is admitted. She has no advance directive and cannot communicate. She has a husband of 50 years, two adult children, and a sister. In the past she told her family she "never wanted tubes or machines if I couldn't recognize them." The children disagree about whether to place a feeding tube. Who is the surrogate, and what standard applies?
step: Establish incapacity: the patient cannot communicate a choice or understand her situation, so a surrogate is needed. No proxy or directive exists, so the state default hierarchy applies.
step: Identify the surrogate: the spouse ranks above adult children and siblings in most states, so the husband is the surrogate (check local law).
step: Apply the standard. She has stated wishes, though informal. Her husband should use substituted judgment: what would she choose, given her values, rather than what he or her children would choose for themselves.
step: Recommend: the husband decides in light of her prior statements; the team explains the limited benefit of tube feeding in advanced dementia and supports the family. The ethics committee can help if the family conflict persists.

@mistakes
- Equating a psychiatric diagnosis, an unusual choice or disagreement with the physician with lack of capacity.
- Saying a patient "lacks capacity" in general rather than for a specific decision.
- Choosing the surrogate by who is most vocal or lives nearest instead of by the legal hierarchy and the patient's own designation.
- Letting the surrogate decide by personal preference instead of substituted judgment.
