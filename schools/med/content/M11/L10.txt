@article
Justice asks how benefits, burdens and resources should be fairly distributed. Health care is never unlimited: organs, ICU beds, vaccines, clinic hours and money are limited. Ethics offers criteria for deciding fairly.

### Theories of distributive justice

| Theory | Core idea | Implication for health care |
|---|---|---|
| **Egalitarian** | Equals should be treated equally; allocate equally or by need | Universal access, equal opportunity for care |
| **Utilitarian** | Maximize overall benefit (greatest good for the greatest number) | Allocate where outcomes are best per resource |
| **Libertarian** | Respect free exchange and property; limited role for the state | Care as a market good; no right to care |
| **Rawlsian** | Choose rules behind a "veil of ignorance," protecting the worst off | Arrangements that benefit the least advantaged |

Common material principles for allocation include need, equal share, merit, effort, contribution and ability to pay. In medicine, **medical need and likelihood of benefit** are the preferred criteria. **Social worth** (wealth, status, occupation) is not accepted for allocating treatment; the main exception is giving priority to health care workers in a crisis when this is needed to keep care running, which is debated.

### Access and the U.S. system

- **EMTALA** (1986) requires hospitals with emergency departments that take part in Medicare to give a medical screening examination and stabilizing treatment to anyone who comes in with an emergency condition (including active labor), regardless of insurance or ability to pay, and to transfer unstable patients only when appropriate. It does not guarantee primary care.
- **Medicare** covers people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities. **Medicaid** (jointly funded by federal and state governments) covers some low-income people. The **Affordable Care Act** expanded coverage through Medicaid expansion (varying by state), marketplaces and protections for pre-existing conditions.
- Uninsured and underinsured people face barriers to access. Language, transportation, immigration status, rural location and discrimination also limit care. **Title VI** of the Civil Rights Act requires language assistance for patients with limited English proficiency in programs that receive federal funding.

### Disparities and bias

Health disparities (differences in outcomes by race, ethnicity, income, geography and other factors) arise from social determinants of health, structural factors and differences in care quality, including implicit bias. Clinicians have a duty to recognize and reduce them, using standardized protocols, interpreters, and attention to patients' circumstances.

### Allocating scarce organs

In the United States the **Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN)** sets national allocation rules. Criteria are medical (urgency, expected benefit, blood and size match, distance) and fairness (waiting time), not social worth or ability to pay. For liver allocation, severity of illness is quantified by the MELD score. Alcohol-related liver disease and substance use do not automatically exclude someone, but programs may require demonstrated engagement in treatment as a predictor of benefit. Organ donation requires consent, and the donation team is separate from the clinicians who declare death.

### Triage in a crisis

When demand overwhelms resources (a pandemic or mass casualty event), hospitals use **crisis standards of care**. Principles: maximize the number of lives saved (or life-years), use transparent criteria, apply them consistently, avoid discrimination based on disability, race, religion or age by itself, reassess regularly, and support those who do not receive scarce treatment. Treating clinicians should not be the ones to allocate: a triage team applies the criteria. Allocation by lottery is used among patients with similar prognoses.

### Cost and stewardship

Physicians should be good stewards without becoming bedside rationers who deny beneficial care to an individual because of cost. Use evidence-based guidelines and avoid low-value care (the "Choosing Wisely" campaign).

**Cost-effectiveness analysis** compares the cost and benefit of interventions. A **quality-adjusted life year (QALY)** equals years of life times a utility between 0 (death) and 1 (perfect health):

$$\text{QALY} = \text{years} \times \text{utility}.$$

The **incremental cost-effectiveness ratio** is

$$\text{ICER} = \frac{C_{\text{new}} - C_{\text{old}}}{\text{QALY}_{\text{new}} - \text{QALY}_{\text{old}}}.$$

QALYs are criticized for possibly disadvantaging people with disabilities or chronic illness, and thresholds for acceptable cost per QALY differ between countries and are debated.

@example
title: Comparing two treatments with an ICER
problem: A new therapy costs \$80,000 per patient and yields 6.0 QALYs. The standard therapy costs \$30,000 and yields 4.5 QALYs. What is the ICER, and what question does it answer?
step: Compute the extra cost: $\$80{,}000 - \$30{,}000 = \$50{,}000$.
step: Compute the extra benefit: $6.0 - 4.5 = 1.5$ QALYs.
step: Divide: $\text{ICER} = \frac{\$50{,}000}{1.5 \text{ QALY}} \approx \$33{,}333$ per QALY gained.
step: Interpretation: it is the extra cost of each additional QALY. Whether it is "worth it" depends on a society's willingness to pay, so the number informs but does not decide allocation, and equity concerns remain.

@mistakes
- Allocating scarce treatment by social worth (wealth, fame, occupation).
- Believing EMTALA guarantees coverage of all care, rather than emergency screening and stabilization.
- Assuming the ICER alone decides what to fund, or that QALYs raise no ethical issues (they can disadvantage people with disabilities).
- Letting the bedside clinician alone ration a crisis resource, rather than a transparent triage process.
