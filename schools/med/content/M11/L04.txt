@article
Patients disclose sensitive information because they trust that it will be protected. **Confidentiality** is the duty not to disclose patient information without permission. **Privacy** is the patient's right to control access to personal information and to be free from unwanted observation. **Privilege** is a legal rule that protects certain communications from being forced in court (for example, physician-patient, attorney-client). Confidentiality supports autonomy and fidelity, and it also serves beneficence: patients who trust you disclose more and are more likely to seek care, for example for sexually transmitted infections or substance use.

### HIPAA in brief

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule protects **protected health information (PHI)**: identifiable health information held by covered entities (clinicians, hospitals, insurers). Key points:

- **No authorization is needed** to use or disclose PHI for **treatment, payment and health care operations**, including sharing with another clinician caring for the patient.
- **Minimum necessary:** disclose only the amount needed. This standard does not apply to disclosures for treatment.
- **Family and friends:** you may share relevant information with family involved in the patient's care if the patient does not object or, when the patient is incapacitated, if you judge it in the patient's interest.
- **Patient rights:** access to one's own records, requests for amendments, an accounting of certain disclosures, and requests for confidential communications.
- **Psychotherapy notes** and **substance use disorder treatment records** (federal rule 42 CFR Part 2) have additional protections.
- **Authorization** (written) is required for most other disclosures, such as sending information to an employer, a life insurer or a lawyer, and for marketing.
- **Breach** of PHI (for example, snooping in a record of a patient you are not treating, or sharing a case on social media with identifiers) can bring sanctions. Curiosity is not a treatment purpose.

### When confidentiality may or must be broken

Confidentiality is strong but not absolute. Exceptions are recognized when:

1. **There is a serious, imminent threat to an identifiable person (duty to protect).** The landmark case is Tarasoff v. Regents of the University of California, which established a duty to protect a person threatened by a patient. States differ: some mandate a duty to warn or protect, some permit disclosure, and a few impose no duty. Actions include warning the person, notifying police or hospitalizing the patient.
2. **Mandatory reporting laws apply:** suspected child abuse and neglect (universal), elder or dependent adult abuse (most states), and certain communicable diseases to the health department (tuberculosis, syphilis, HIV, measles and others, as listed by each state). Most states require reporting of gunshot wounds (and many also of stab wounds), while only a few require reporting of intimate partner violence against competent adults; state law varies.
3. **A court order or valid subpoena with a court order** compels disclosure. A subpoena from an attorney alone is not enough without patient authorization or a court order in many circumstances; consult the hospital's legal department.
4. **Public safety:** some states require reporting of seizures or other conditions that impair driving, while others leave it to physician discretion; the law varies by state.
5. **Third parties at risk of infection:** a patient with HIV who refuses to disclose to a partner should first be counseled; many states permit but do not require disclosure to an identifiable partner at risk, usually after counseling and efforts to get the patient to disclose.

When you must break confidentiality, disclose only what is needed, to the proper party, and (when safe) tell the patient.

### Special practical situations

- **Adolescents** may receive confidential care for certain services under state law (week 5).
- **Electronic records:** access only the records you need for your role.
- **Public settings:** do not discuss patients in elevators or cafeterias.
- **Patient asks that information be kept from family:** honor it unless an exception applies.
- **Emails and texts:** use secure, approved channels.

@example
title: A threat against an identifiable person
problem: A 34-year-old man in outpatient psychiatric treatment tells his therapist that he plans to kill his ex-girlfriend, names her, says he knows where she works, and says he has bought a gun. He declines hospitalization. What should the clinician do?
step: Identify the exception. A serious, specific threat against a named person is the situation that triggers a duty to protect (Tarasoff and state statutes). Confidentiality is not absolute here.
step: Assess seriousness: specific plan, identified victim, means (a gun) and intent suggest imminent danger.
step: Act in proportion. Options include notifying the potential victim and law enforcement, pursuing involuntary evaluation and hospitalization if he meets criteria, and removing access to the weapon where possible. State law determines the exact duty; document the reasoning.
step: Disclose only the information necessary to protect the victim, and consult the risk manager or legal counsel without delaying protective action.

@mistakes
- Believing that confidentiality is absolute, or that a vague statement of anger always justifies breaking it.
- Believing HIPAA requires written authorization to share information with other clinicians involved in treatment.
- Disclosing the whole record when only limited information is needed.
- Treating a lawyer's subpoena as automatically requiring disclosure without checking for patient authorization or a court order.

@practice self
prompt: A 23-year-old woman with a new HIV diagnosis tells you she will not tell her partner of 2 years and plans unprotected sex. Explain the ethical reasoning and what you would do, including when disclosure to the partner may be justified.
solution: Confidentiality is strong, but there is a foreseeable risk of serious harm to an identifiable person. First, counsel the patient: explain that antiretroviral therapy reduces transmission risk and that undetectable viral load prevents sexual transmission, offer help to disclose with the health department's partner services, and discuss condoms and PrEP for the partner. Report the diagnosis to the health department as required. If she still refuses and the partner remains at risk, many states permit (a few require) the physician to notify the partner, after consultation with the risk manager or public health officials; the law varies. Disclosure should be limited to what is needed.
