@article
Informed consent is the ethical and legal process by which a capable patient authorizes a treatment or procedure after understanding what it involves. It operationalizes respect for autonomy. It is a conversation, not a signature: a signed form is evidence of consent, but a form signed without understanding is not valid consent.

### Elements

Valid informed consent requires:

1. **Decision-making capacity** (week 3).
2. **Voluntariness:** the choice is free of coercion or undue pressure.
3. **Disclosure** of the relevant information: the diagnosis, the nature and purpose of the proposed intervention, its risks and benefits, **reasonable alternatives (including no treatment)**, and their risks and benefits.
4. **Understanding:** the patient can restate the information in their own words ("teach-back").
5. **Decision:** the patient agrees to or declines the intervention.

The clinician who will perform the procedure (or a member of the team who can answer questions about it) should obtain consent. Delegating the conversation to a clerk who only collects signatures is a classic exam error.

### How much to disclose

Two standards exist, and states differ on which they use. The **reasonable-patient standard** (the majority standard in U.S. states, following Canterbury v. Spence) asks what a typical patient would want to know to decide. The **professional (reasonable-physician) standard** asks what a typical physician would disclose. Under either, you disclose material risks: those that are common or those that are rare but severe (paralysis from spinal surgery, for example). You do not need to list every conceivable risk.

### Exceptions

Consent may be unnecessary or modified when:

- **Emergency:** a patient lacks capacity, immediate treatment is needed to prevent death or serious harm, and no surrogate is available. The law presumes that a reasonable person would consent (**implied consent**). Treat only what the emergency requires. Known refusals, such as a valid advance directive or a card refusing transfusion, are honored.
- **Waiver:** a capable patient may knowingly decline to receive information and ask someone else to decide. Explore the request and offer the information again later.
- **Therapeutic privilege:** withholding information because disclosure would seriously harm the patient. It is very narrow and rarely accepted; discomfort or anxiety does not qualify.
- **Incapacity:** consent comes from a surrogate (week 3).
- **Public health or court orders** for certain situations, such as isolation for dangerous infectious disease.

### Refusal, language and context

Capable patients may refuse. **Informed refusal** requires the same disclosure, including the consequences of refusing; document it. If the patient does not speak English fluently, use a qualified medical interpreter, not a family member or untrained staff. Consent should be obtained without pressure and with enough time to ask questions. Consent for one procedure does not cover another unrelated procedure, and consent can be withdrawn at any time. Patients should be told when trainees will take part in their care.

### Legal consequences

Treatment without any consent (outside an exception) can be **battery**, an unauthorized touching. Treatment performed with consent that was inadequately informed is usually pursued as **negligence** for failure to disclose; the plaintiff must show that a reasonable patient would have refused had the risk been disclosed and that harm resulted from the undisclosed risk.

### Risk communication

Present risks in absolute numbers when possible. A statement that a drug "increases risk by 50%" is relative; if baseline risk goes from $2\%$ to $3\%$, the **absolute risk increase** is $1$ percentage point. Patients understand "1 more person in 100" better than relative percentages.

@example
title: Consent for surgery with an unmentioned risk
problem: A 60-year-old man is scheduled for elective carotid endarterectomy. His surgeon's resident has him sign a form at the bedside before transport, without discussion, and the patient says he "just does what the doctor says." The surgeon never mentions a stroke risk of about 3%. Evaluate the consent and say what to do.
step: Check the elements. Capacity and voluntariness seem fine; disclosure and understanding are missing. Nobody has described the alternatives (medical therapy, stenting) or the material risks (stroke, death).
step: Recognize that the signature is not informed consent. A stroke risk of about 3% is material to a reasonable patient, so under either standard it must be disclosed.
step: Note the statement "I just do what the doctor says": it may be a request for the doctor to decide (waiver), but it should be explored rather than assumed, and the basic nature and risks should still be offered.
step: Plan: postpone the procedure if needed, have the operating surgeon explain the diagnosis, the procedure, the 3% stroke risk and its alternatives, check understanding with teach-back, and document.

@mistakes
- Equating the signed form with consent.
- Using therapeutic privilege because a diagnosis is frightening; it applies only when disclosure itself would cause serious harm.
- Using a family member as interpreter instead of a qualified medical interpreter.
- Treating incapacity or an emergency as permission to ignore a known prior refusal.

@practice self
prompt: A 38-year-old woman has stage II breast cancer. Her surgeon recommends lumpectomy with radiation or mastectomy; survival is similar. She says, "You pick, doctor." Using the four principles, write how the surgeon should respond, including whether this is a valid waiver.
solution: Autonomy: a capable patient may delegate a decision, but a statement made in the moment may reflect anxiety, not a settled waiver. The surgeon should ask what makes the choice hard and offer information in digestible amounts. Beneficence and nonmaleficence: both options have similar survival, so the choice depends on values (breast conservation versus avoiding radiation and recurrence risk). Justice: not central. The surgeon should describe both options, risks, benefits and alternatives, explore the patient's values, offer a recommendation that reflects them, and confirm her choice. If she truly prefers the surgeon to decide after hearing the options, that is a knowing waiver, which should be documented.
rubric: Identifies that autonomy permits delegation but the preference should be explored.
rubric: Describes both options and the patient's values as the deciding factor.
rubric: Includes teach-back or confirmation and documentation.
