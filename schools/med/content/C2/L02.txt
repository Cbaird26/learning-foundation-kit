@article
A **gene pool** is all the alleles in a population. **Population genetics** asks how allele frequencies change. Its null model is the **Hardy–Weinberg principle**: if no evolutionary forces act, allele and genotype frequencies stay constant from generation to generation.

### The equation

For a gene with two alleles, let the dominant allele $A$ have frequency $p$ and the recessive allele $a$ have frequency $q$, with
$$p + q = 1.$$
With random mating, the genotype frequencies in the next generation are
$$p^2 \;(AA) \;+\; 2pq \;(Aa) \;+\; q^2 \;(aa) = 1.$$
The middle term is the **carrier frequency** for a recessive condition: heterozygotes are unaffected but can pass the allele on.

### Assumptions

Equilibrium requires (1) no mutation, (2) no gene flow, (3) a very large population so that drift is negligible, (4) random mating, and (5) no natural selection on the locus. Real populations violate these, which is the point: deviation from Hardy–Weinberg proportions is evidence that something is acting. Note that the equation is also used as an approximation in medicine, for example for carrier screening, when the assumptions hold well enough.

### From incidence to carrier frequency

For an autosomal recessive disease the only genotype you can count directly is the affected one, $q^2$. So you work backward:

1. Take the incidence as $q^2$.
2. Compute $q = \sqrt{q^2}$ and $p = 1 - q$.
3. Carrier frequency is $2pq$.

Because $q$ is small, $p \approx 1$ and $2pq \approx 2q$. This explains why carriers are far more common than affected individuals: if $q = 0.02$, then $q^2 = 0.0004$ but $2pq \approx 0.039$, about one hundred times larger. Most copies of a rare recessive allele sit in carriers, where selection cannot act on them. For an **X-linked recessive** condition, males have one X chromosome, so the male incidence equals $q$, and affected females occur at $q^2$.

### Forces that change frequencies

**Genetic drift** is random sampling error and is strongest in small populations. A **bottleneck** (a sharp reduction in size) and the **founder effect** (a few individuals establishing a new population) both change frequencies by chance, which is why some rare conditions are common in particular isolated populations. **Gene flow** makes populations more similar. **Selection** changes frequencies according to fitness. **Heterozygote advantage**, as in sickle cell trait and malaria resistance, can maintain a harmful allele.

### Speciation

The **biological species concept** defines a species as a group of populations that interbreed and produce fertile offspring in nature. **Reproductive isolation** keeps species separate. **Prezygotic barriers** act before fertilization: habitat, temporal or behavioral isolation, mechanical incompatibility, and gametic incompatibility. **Postzygotic barriers** act after: hybrid inviability, sterility (the mule) or breakdown. In **allopatric speciation**, a geographic barrier stops gene flow and the populations diverge. In **sympatric speciation**, new species arise within the same area, often through polyploidy in plants.

@example
title: Carrier frequency from incidence
problem: An autosomal recessive condition affects $1$ in $1600$ births. Estimate the carrier frequency, assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium.
step: The incidence is $q^2 = 1/1600$, so $q = \sqrt{1/1600} = 1/40$.
step: Then $p = 1 - q = 39/40$.
step: Carrier frequency is $2pq = 2 \cdot \dfrac{39}{40} \cdot \dfrac{1}{40} = \dfrac{78}{1600} \approx 0.0488$.
step: About $1$ in $20$ people carries the allele, even though only $1$ in $1600$ is affected.

@mistakes
- Using the incidence of the disease as $q$ instead of $q^2$.
- Forgetting the factor $2$ in $2pq$, or calculating carriers as $p \cdot q$.
- Applying $p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1$ to a population that is not in equilibrium (for example, one with strong selection or non-random mating) without noting the assumption.
- Assuming that the recessive phenotype is rare because the recessive allele is rare in the same proportion: $q$ is far larger than $q^2$.

@practice num
prompt: In a sample of $100$ people, $50$ are $AA$, $40$ are $Aa$ and $10$ are $aa$. Find the frequency $p$ of allele $A$ as an exact fraction.
answer: 7/10
check: (2*50 + 40) / (2*100)
hint: Each person carries two alleles, so there are $200$ alleles in total.
hint: Count the $A$ alleles: two from each $AA$ person and one from each $Aa$ person.
solution: $A$ alleles: $2(50) + 40 = 140$ of $200$, so $p = 140/200 = 7/10$.

@practice num
prompt: A cystic-fibrosis-like autosomal recessive condition affects $1$ in $2500$ births. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, estimate the carrier frequency using $2pq$ with $p = 1 - q$, rounded to $4$ decimal places.
answer: 0.0392
check: 2 * (1/2500)**0.5 * (1 - (1/2500)**0.5)
tol: 0.0005
hint: The incidence equals $q^2$.
hint: $q = 1/50$, so $p = 49/50$.
solution: $q = \sqrt{1/2500} = 0.02$, $p = 0.98$, and $2pq = 2(0.98)(0.02) = 0.0392$, about $1$ in $25$.

@practice num
prompt: In a screening population of $10{,}000$ people, the frequency of a recessive allele is $q = 0.02$. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, how many carriers do you expect?
answer: 392
check: 2 * 0.02 * 0.98 * 10000
tol: 0.5
hint: Carrier frequency is $2pq$.
hint: $p = 0.98$. Multiply $2pq$ by $10{,}000$.
solution: $2pq = 2(0.98)(0.02) = 0.0392$, and $0.0392 \times 10{,}000 = 392$.

@practice mc
prompt: A very small island population was founded by six individuals, and a rare recessive allele is now much more common there than in the mainland source population. Which mechanism best explains this?
choice: Natural selection acting on a new environment
choice*: The founder effect, a form of genetic drift
choice: Gene flow from a large neighboring population
choice: Random mating in a very large population
hint: Think about which assumption of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is violated.
solution: A few founders carry a random subset of the source alleles, so frequencies shift by chance. This is the founder effect. Large random-mating populations and gene flow would tend to keep frequencies stable or similar.

@quiz mc
prompt: Two populations of mice were separated by a newly formed river for many generations. When individuals were later brought together, they mated but all the hybrid offspring were sterile. Which statement is correct?
choice: This is a prezygotic barrier, because mating occurred.
choice*: This is a postzygotic barrier (hybrid sterility), and the earlier river separation is consistent with allopatric speciation.
choice: This is sympatric speciation, because the mice are now in one place.
choice: The two groups are the same biological species because they can mate.
hint: Ask whether the barrier acts before or after a zygote forms.
solution: The zygotes formed, so the barrier acts afterward: hybrid sterility is postzygotic. The geographic split that stopped gene flow is allopatric. The biological species concept requires fertile offspring, not just mating.
