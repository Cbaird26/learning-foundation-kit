@article
Plants and animals are multicellular eukaryotes with very different lifestyles. Understanding both groups, and how they differ from bacteria and fungi, gives the comparative background for pharmacology: a drug works well when it targets something the pathogen has and the host lacks.

### Plants

Plants are photosynthetic eukaryotes with cellulose cell walls. Land plants arose from green algae and are grouped by key adaptations:

- **Nonvascular plants** (bryophytes, such as mosses) lack true vascular tissue and stay small.
- **Seedless vascular plants** (ferns) have vascular tissue, but need water for sperm to swim.
- **Gymnosperms** (conifers) have seeds without fruit.
- **Angiosperms** (flowering plants) have flowers and fruit, and are the most diverse group.

All plants alternate between a haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte (**alternation of generations**). **Xylem** carries water and minerals upward from roots in dead, hollow cells; **phloem** carries sugars from sources to sinks in living sieve-tube cells. Water moves by **water potential** $\Psi$, in megapascals (MPa), always from higher to lower values:
$$\Psi = \Psi_s + \Psi_p.$$
Here $\Psi_s$ is the solute potential (zero for pure water, negative with solutes) and $\Psi_p$ is the pressure potential (positive in a turgid cell). Many drugs and medicines trace to plants, but plant physiology itself is less often tested in medicine than animal biology.

### Animals

Animals are multicellular heterotrophs whose cells lack walls. Their body plans are classified by:

- **Symmetry**: radial or bilateral. Bilateral animals show cephalization (a head).
- **Germ layers**: ectoderm and endoderm in diploblasts; ectoderm, mesoderm and endoderm in triploblasts, which include humans.
- **Body cavity**: a true **coelom** is lined with mesoderm; **acoelomates** lack a cavity.
- **Development**: in **protostomes**, the blastopore becomes the mouth; in **deuterostomes**, including echinoderms and chordates, it becomes the anus.

**Chordates** have at some stage a notochord, a dorsal hollow nerve cord, pharyngeal slits and a post-anal tail. Vertebrates add a vertebral column and a skull. Humans are deuterostome, triploblastic, coelomate chordates.

### Cells as drug targets

The principle of **selective toxicity** is to harm the pathogen without harming the patient. The comparison below shows why:

| Feature | Bacterial cell | Fungal cell | Human cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Peptidoglycan | Chitin and glucans | None |
| Membrane sterol | None (mostly) | Ergosterol | Cholesterol |
| Ribosomes | $70$S | $80$S | $80$S |
| Folate | Synthesizes it | Varies | Obtains from diet |

Bacteria are the easiest targets: beta-lactams such as penicillin block peptidoglycan cross-linking, which humans lack; tetracyclines and aminoglycosides bind the $30$S subunit and macrolides the $50$S subunit of the bacterial ribosome; trimethoprim and sulfonamides block bacterial folate synthesis. Fungi are eukaryotes and share more machinery with humans, so fewer targets exist: azoles such as fluconazole inhibit ergosterol synthesis, amphotericin B binds ergosterol and forms pores, and echinocandins inhibit glucan synthesis in the wall. Because human cells make cholesterol, amphotericin B also has toxicity. Viruses, which have no cell structure, require different targets.

@example
title: Direction of water movement in a cell
problem: A plant cell has $\Psi_s = -0.8$ MPa and $\Psi_p = +0.3$ MPa. It is placed in a solution with $\Psi = -0.2$ MPa. Which way does water move?
step: Find the cell's water potential: $\Psi_{cell} = -0.8 + 0.3 = -0.5$ MPa.
step: Compare: the solution is $-0.2$ MPa, which is higher than $-0.5$ MPa.
step: Water moves from higher to lower water potential, so water moves into the cell.
step: As water enters, $\Psi_p$ rises until the cell's potential equals that of the solution or the wall resists further expansion.

@example
title: Choosing a drug target
problem: A new compound inhibits an enzyme that builds the cross-links in peptidoglycan. Is it likely to be selectively toxic to bacteria, and would it work against a yeast infection?
step: Peptidoglycan is found in bacterial cell walls and not in human cells, so the target is absent from the host.
step: That predicts selective toxicity: the drug should harm bacteria and spare human cells.
step: Yeast cell walls are made of chitin and glucans, not peptidoglycan, so the compound has no target in fungi.
step: Conclusion: likely selective for bacteria, and not expected to treat a fungal infection.

@mistakes
- Saying water moves toward the more negative solute potential without including pressure; compare total $\Psi$.
- Thinking fungi are plants; they are heterotrophs with chitin walls and are closer to animals.
- Assuming antibacterial drugs work on fungi, or that antifungals are as selective as antibacterials; fungi are eukaryotes.
- Mixing up protostome and deuterostome; the question is which opening becomes the mouth.
