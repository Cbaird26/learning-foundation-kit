@article
The emergency department (ED) inverts the clinic workflow. In the clinic you collect a story and then decide how sick the patient is. In the ED you decide **how sick the patient is first**, treat what is killing them, and only then refine the diagnosis. The patient arrives undifferentiated: you know a symptom and a set of vital signs, not a disease.

### Sick or not sick

The first few seconds are a gestalt: color, work of breathing, responsiveness, ability to speak a full sentence. A patient who speaks clearly has a patent airway, enough breathing to phonate and enough cerebral perfusion to think. Abnormal vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, temperature, oxygen saturation) confirm the impression. Never "clear" a patient with abnormal vital signs without explaining them.

### The primary survey: ABCDE

Work in order, fixing each threat before moving on, because the earlier letters kill faster.

- **Airway** with cervical spine protection if trauma is possible: Is the patient talking? Stridor, gurgling, snoring respirations or a tongue falling back mean obstruction. Fixes: jaw thrust, suction, airway adjuncts, definitive airway.
- **Breathing**: respiratory rate, work of breathing, SpO$_2$, breath sounds, tracheal position. Fixes: oxygen, bag-mask ventilation, decompression of a tension pneumothorax.
- **Circulation**: pulses, skin perfusion, capillary refill, blood pressure, two large-bore IVs, ECG monitor, control of external bleeding.
- **Disability**: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), pupils, focal weakness and a **bedside glucose**. Hypoglycemia mimics stroke and is cheap to check and fix.
- **Exposure**: undress fully, look for rashes, wounds, track marks and medication patches, but prevent hypothermia.

Parallel processing is normal: a nurse places monitors while you examine.

### Numbers you will calculate constantly

The **GCS** is the sum of eye opening (E, 1-4), verbal response (V, 1-5) and motor response (M, 1-6); the range is 3 to 15 and 8 or below suggests a patient who may not protect the airway.

**Mean arterial pressure** (MAP) estimates organ perfusion pressure:
$$\text{MAP} = \frac{\text{SBP} + 2\,\text{DBP}}{3}$$
A MAP of at least 65 mm Hg is the usual minimum goal.

The **shock index** is heart rate divided by systolic blood pressure. Normal is about 0.5 to 0.7; a value above about 0.9 to 1.0 suggests occult hemodynamic compromise, even when the blood pressure looks acceptable. Beta-blockers can blunt it.

### Can't-miss thinking

For every complaint, build the list of diagnoses that kill or disable quickly if missed, then use history, examination and tests to rule them in or out. A typical short list:

| Complaint | Can't-miss causes |
|---|---|
| Chest pain | Acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, aortic dissection, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, esophageal rupture |
| Dyspnea | Pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, heart failure, asthma or COPD exacerbation, anaphylaxis |
| Altered mental status | Hypoglycemia, hypoxia, stroke, intracranial hemorrhage, meningitis or encephalitis, sepsis, overdose |
| Abdominal pain | Ruptured aortic aneurysm, mesenteric ischemia, ectopic pregnancy, perforation, torsion |
| Headache | Subarachnoid hemorrhage, meningitis, mass lesion, stroke |

Rank by **danger and treatability**, not only by probability: a 1% chance of a treatable lethal disease deserves a test when the test is safe.

### Probability and testing

Tests change probability; they do not give yes or no. Convert a pretest probability to odds, multiply by the likelihood ratio (LR), and convert back:
$$\text{odds} = \frac{p}{1-p}, \qquad \text{posttest odds} = \text{pretest odds} \times \text{LR}, \qquad p = \frac{\text{odds}}{1+\text{odds}}.$$
A highly sensitive test with a low negative LR is best for ruling out; a highly specific test with a high positive LR is best for ruling in. Use clinical decision rules to set the pretest probability before ordering.

### History in a hurry

Use **SAMPLE**: Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medications (anticoagulants, insulin, beta-blockers), Past history, Last meal or last oral intake, and Events leading up. Collateral history from paramedics, family and pill bottles matters most when the patient cannot speak.

@example
title: Triage a hypotensive patient
problem: A 66-year-old man arrives with weakness and black stools. Blood pressure is 84/50 mm Hg, heart rate 118/min, respiratory rate 24/min, SpO$_2$ 96% on room air. He is awake but pale and cool. Calculate his MAP and shock index and state the first actions.
step: MAP = $(84 + 2\times 50)/3 = 184/3 \approx 61$ mm Hg, below the usual goal of 65.
step: Shock index = $118/84 \approx 1.4$, well above 0.9. This is a patient in shock until proven otherwise, even though he is awake.
step: Airway is patent (he talks) and breathing is adequate, so circulation is the problem: place two large-bore IVs, connect a monitor, send type and crossmatch, hemoglobin, lactate and glucose, and give blood products for suspected gastrointestinal hemorrhage.
step: Disability and exposure: check glucose and look for the source (rectal examination, medication list for anticoagulants and NSAIDs). Then call for endoscopy and plan ICU-level care.

@mistakes
- Anchoring on the first diagnosis and not asking what else could kill the patient in the next hour.
- Skipping the bedside glucose in any patient with altered mental status or focal neurologic deficit.
- Trusting a normal blood pressure while ignoring a high shock index, tachycardia or poor skin perfusion.
- Interpreting a negative test as "disease excluded" without considering pretest probability.

@practice mc
prompt: A 72-year-old man with diabetes is brought in with sudden confusion and right-sided weakness that began an hour ago. Vital signs are normal. Fingerstick glucose is 38 mg/dL. Which is the best next step?
choice: Obtain an urgent CT of the head before any other intervention.
choice*: Give intravenous dextrose and reassess his neurologic deficits.
choice: Start intravenous alteplase for presumed ischemic stroke.
choice: Perform a lumbar puncture for suspected meningitis.
hint: A glucose this low can cause focal deficits that resolve with treatment.
solution: Hypoglycemia can mimic stroke. It is rapidly treatable and itself harmful, so give dextrose first, then reassess. If deficits persist after the glucose is normal, pursue stroke imaging. Thrombolysis is contraindicated when glucose is below 50 mg/dL.

@practice self
prompt: A 34-year-old woman is brought in by a friend, drowsy and difficult to arouse, with no other history. Outline your first five minutes: the primary survey steps, the bedside tests, and the first six diagnoses on your can't-miss list.
hint: Follow A, B, C, D, E, then list diagnoses by danger.
solution: Airway: assess patency, position, suction, adjuncts. Breathing: respiratory rate, SpO$_2$, oxygen or bag-mask ventilation. Circulation: pulse, blood pressure, monitor, IV access. Disability: GCS, pupils, focal signs, fingerstick glucose. Exposure: full examination for trauma, track marks, patches and rash; check temperature. Tests: glucose, ECG, basic labs, acetaminophen and salicylate levels, pregnancy test, blood gas, then CT head if focal signs or trauma. Can't-miss list: hypoglycemia, opioid or other overdose, hypoxia or hypercapnia, intracranial hemorrhage or stroke, meningitis or encephalitis or sepsis, and a metabolic cause such as diabetic ketoacidosis or hyponatremia.
rubric: Follows A, B, C, D, E in order and treats threats as found.
rubric: Includes a bedside glucose and an ECG.
rubric: Lists at least six dangerous diagnoses including overdose and intracranial pathology.
rubric: Orders targeted tests and uses collateral history.

@quiz num
prompt: A patient has blood pressure 98/58 mm Hg. Calculate the mean arterial pressure, rounded to the nearest whole number.
answer: 71
check: (98 + 2 * 58) / 3
tol: 0.6
hint: MAP = (systolic + 2 times diastolic) / 3.

solution: (98 + 116) / 3 = 71.3, so about 71 mm Hg.

@quiz num
prompt: A clinical rule places a patient at a 10% pretest probability of a disease. A test with a positive likelihood ratio of 9 returns positive. What is the posttest probability, as a decimal?
answer: 0.5
check: (0.1/0.9*9) / (1 + 0.1/0.9*9)
tol: 0.01
hint: Convert 10% to odds, multiply by 9, convert back.
hint: Pretest odds are 0.1/0.9, about 0.111.
solution: Pretest odds 0.111, times 9 gives posttest odds 1.0, so probability is 1/(1+1) = 0.5.
