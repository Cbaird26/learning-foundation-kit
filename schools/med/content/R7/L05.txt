@article
Abdominal pain is common and the cause is often benign, but the ED job is to find the few patients who need an operation, a vascular intervention or urgent antibiotics. Start from mechanism.

### Mechanism and location

**Visceral pain** arises from stretch of hollow organs and is dull, poorly localized, and felt in the midline: epigastric (foregut), periumbilical (midgut), hypogastric (hindgut). **Somatic (parietal) pain** arises when inflamed organ touches the peritoneum; it is sharp, localized and worse with movement, with guarding and rebound. **Referred pain** is perceived in a distant dermatome, for example diaphragmatic irritation referred to the shoulder. Appendicitis classically starts periumbilical (visceral) and migrates to the right lower quadrant (somatic).

| Region | Common causes | Can't-miss |
|---|---|---|
| Right upper | Biliary colic, cholecystitis, hepatitis | Ascending cholangitis, right lower lobe pneumonia, MI |
| Epigastric | Gastritis, ulcer, pancreatitis | Inferior MI, perforated ulcer, aortic dissection |
| Right lower | Appendicitis, ovarian cyst | Ectopic pregnancy, ovarian or testicular torsion |
| Left lower | Diverticulitis, constipation | Ectopic, sigmoid volvulus |
| Diffuse | Gastroenteritis | Mesenteric ischemia, obstruction, ruptured AAA, perforation, DKA |

### The can't-miss list

- **Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA):** older man with sudden abdominal, flank or back pain, hypotension and a pulsatile mass; surgical emergency. Do not delay for CT in unstable patients; bedside ultrasound shows the aneurysm. Resuscitate with permissive hypotension and blood products and take to the operating room.
- **Acute mesenteric ischemia:** pain out of proportion to a soft abdomen in a patient with atrial fibrillation, vascular disease or low-flow state. Diagnosis: CT angiography; lactate can be normal early. Treat with heparin, resuscitation and revascularization or resection.
- **Ectopic pregnancy:** in any woman of reproductive age with pain, get a pregnancy test. Positive test plus no intrauterine pregnancy on transvaginal ultrasound, especially with free fluid and hypotension, means ruptured ectopic until proven otherwise; unstable patients go to surgery, stable ones may receive methotrexate.
- **Perforated viscus:** sudden severe pain, rigid abdomen; free air under the diaphragm on an upright chest radiograph or on CT; surgery and antibiotics.
- **Appendicitis:** CT with contrast in adults (ultrasound first in children and pregnancy, with MRI if equivocal); antibiotics and appendectomy.
- **Bowel obstruction:** colicky pain, distension, vomiting, obstipation; adhesions and hernias are the commonest causes. CT; nasogastric decompression, fluids; surgery for strangulation (fever, severe pain, peritonitis, lactate) or closed loop.
- **Testicular torsion:** acute scrotal pain and a high-riding testis with absent cremasteric reflex in a boy or young man; Doppler ultrasound, but do not delay surgery; detorsion within about 6 hours salvages the testis. Abdominal pain can be the presenting complaint.
- **Biliary and pancreatic disease:** see below.
- **Non-abdominal mimics:** inferior MI, lower lobe pneumonia, diabetic ketoacidosis (anion gap acidosis), and adrenal crisis.

### Biliary tract and pancreas

**Acute cholecystitis:** persistent RUQ pain, fever, Murphy sign; ultrasound shows stones, wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid and sonographic Murphy sign; HIDA scan if equivocal. Give fluids, analgesia, antibiotics and early cholecystectomy.

**Ascending cholangitis:** infection above an obstruction. **Charcot triad** is fever, jaundice and RUQ pain; **Reynolds pentad** adds hypotension and altered mental status. Give broad-spectrum antibiotics and fluids and decompress the biliary tree urgently with ERCP.

**Acute pancreatitis:** diagnosis requires two of three: epigastric pain radiating to the back, serum lipase (or amylase) more than 3 times the upper limit of normal, and characteristic imaging. Causes: gallstones, alcohol, hypertriglyceridemia (above 1000 mg/dL), hypercalcemia, drugs. Treat with goal-directed fluids (balanced crystalloid preferred), analgesia and early oral feeding; ERCP only for coexisting cholangitis or persistent obstruction.

### Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP)

Cirrhotic patients with ascites and abdominal pain, fever or encephalopathy need diagnostic paracentesis. The diagnosis is an ascitic fluid neutrophil (polymorphonuclear) count of at least 250 cells/mm$^3$: absolute count = WBC $\times$ percent neutrophils. Treat with third-generation cephalosporin and IV albumin.

### Special populations

Older adults, diabetic and immunocompromised patients have blunted pain and fever and higher mortality, so lower the threshold for CT. Pregnant patients need ultrasound or MRI before CT when possible, but never withhold necessary imaging. Always check pregnancy status in women of reproductive age, and always examine the groin and genitalia.

### Testing

Initial tests: pregnancy test, CBC, electrolytes with anion gap, renal function, liver tests, lipase, lactate, urinalysis, ECG for epigastric pain in at-risk patients. Analgesia (for example, IV opioid) does not mask the diagnosis and should be given early.

@example
title: A diagnosis in an older patient
problem: A 74-year-old man with atrial fibrillation who stopped his anticoagulant presents with 6 hours of severe diffuse abdominal pain. The abdomen is soft with minimal tenderness. Lactate is 3.8 mmol/L. What is the most likely diagnosis and next step?
step: Pain out of proportion to the examination in a patient with atrial fibrillation suggests embolic acute mesenteric ischemia from the superior mesenteric artery.
step: Obtain CT angiography of the abdomen without waiting for lactate to rise further or for peritonitis to develop; do not rely on a normal lactate.
step: Start heparin, give fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and call vascular surgery for revascularization; bowel resection if necrosis is found.
step: Avoid vasopressors that cause mesenteric vasoconstriction when possible, and make the diagnosis early because mortality rises quickly with delay.

@mistakes
- Dismissing severe pain in an older patient because the abdomen is soft.
- Forgetting the pregnancy test in a woman of reproductive age.
- Withholding analgesia until a surgeon examines the patient.
- Delaying surgery in an unstable patient for CT in suspected ruptured AAA or ruptured ectopic pregnancy.

@practice self
prompt: A 68-year-old man with hypertension has sudden severe left flank and abdominal pain and then faints. His blood pressure is 82/50 mm Hg and heart rate is 118/min. List the can't-miss diagnoses and explain your bedside steps and disposition.
hint: Think vascular first, then consider the other causes of hemodynamic collapse with abdominal pain.
solution: The leading diagnosis is ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm. Others: aortic dissection, mesenteric ischemia, perforated viscus, ruptured spleen, and gastrointestinal hemorrhage. Immediately activate the vascular surgery and operating room teams, place two large-bore IVs, send type and crossmatch, start blood products with permissive hypotension (do not overshoot pressure), and perform bedside ultrasound of the aorta and a FAST examination. If unstable, go to the operating room without CT; if stable enough, CT angiography. Disposition is the operating room and ICU.
rubric: Names ruptured AAA first with other vascular and hemorrhagic causes.
