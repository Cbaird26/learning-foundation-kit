@article
**Shock** is circulatory failure that delivers too little oxygen to meet tissue needs. Hypotension is common but not required: early shock may have a normal pressure with tachycardia, cool skin, low urine output, altered mentation and rising lactate. Lactate above 2 mmol/L signals hypoperfusion or impaired clearance.

### The physiology

Blood pressure follows a simple relationship:
$$\text{MAP} = \text{CO}\times \text{SVR}, \qquad \text{CO} = \text{HR}\times \text{SV}.$$
Cardiac output (CO) depends on heart rate and stroke volume, which depends on preload, contractility and afterload. Systemic vascular resistance (SVR) can be calculated, in dyn$\cdot$s/cm$^5$, as
$$\text{SVR} = \frac{\text{MAP} - \text{CVP}}{\text{CO}}\times 80,$$
with CO in L/min and pressures in mm Hg. Normal SVR is roughly 800 to 1200. The four shock categories are defined by which term fails.

| Type | Cause | Preload (CVP, wedge) | Cardiac output | SVR | Skin |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hypovolemic | Hemorrhage, vomiting, diarrhea, burns | Low | Low | High | Cold, clammy |
| Cardiogenic | MI, arrhythmia, acute valve failure, myocarditis | High | Low | High | Cold, clammy |
| Obstructive | Tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE | High CVP | Low | High | Cold |
| Distributive | Sepsis, anaphylaxis, neurogenic, adrenal crisis | Low or normal | Often high early | Low | Warm early |

### Classifying at the bedside

Ask three questions. Is the neck flat or distended? Are the lungs wet? Are the extremities warm or cold? **Flat neck veins** suggest volume loss or distributive shock. **Distended veins with clear lungs** suggest obstruction (tamponade, tension pneumothorax, PE). **Distended veins with crackles** suggest cardiogenic shock. **Warm skin with a wide pulse pressure** suggests distributive shock. Bedside echocardiography (inferior vena cava, ventricular function, effusion, right ventricular size) answers most of these questions in minutes.

### Sepsis

**Sepsis-3** defines sepsis as life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, operationalized as an acute rise in the SOFA score of at least 2. **Septic shock** is sepsis in which vasopressors are needed to maintain MAP at least 65 mm Hg and lactate remains above 2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. The **qSOFA** screen (respiratory rate at least 22/min, systolic pressure at most 100 mm Hg, altered mentation) flags patients at higher risk of death; a score of 2 or more is concerning, but it is a prognostic prompt, not a diagnostic test.

Priorities from the Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 guidelines:

1. Recognize and measure lactate; obtain blood cultures (two sets) before antibiotics if this causes no substantial delay.
2. Give **broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour** for possible septic shock or high likelihood of sepsis; within 3 hours if sepsis is possible without shock after rapid evaluation.
3. For sepsis-induced hypoperfusion (hypotension or lactate at least 4 mmol/L), suggest at least **30 mL/kg of IV crystalloid within the first 3 hours**, preferably balanced crystalloid (for example lactated Ringer's) over saline, and guide further fluid by dynamic measures such as passive leg raise, capillary refill and ultrasound.
4. Source control (drain abscess, relieve obstruction, remove infected device) as soon as practical.
5. **Norepinephrine is first-line** vasopressor, started peripherally if needed rather than waiting for central access; target MAP 65 mm Hg. Add vasopressin if norepinephrine requirements are rising (commonly around 0.25 to 0.5 microgram/kg/min). Add hydrocortisone (typical adult dose 200 mg/day) for persistent vasopressor need.

### Other distributive shock

**Anaphylaxis** is a systemic allergic reaction with skin or mucosal involvement plus respiratory or circulatory compromise. The first and most important drug is **intramuscular epinephrine**, typical adult dose 0.3 to 0.5 mg (1 mg/mL concentration) in the anterolateral thigh, repeated every 5 to 15 minutes as needed. Add airway management, IV fluids and oxygen; antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts and never replace epinephrine.

**Neurogenic shock** follows high spinal cord injury (above about T6): loss of sympathetic tone gives hypotension with relative bradycardia and warm skin. Exclude hemorrhage first in trauma, give fluids cautiously and use a vasopressor such as norepinephrine.

**Adrenal crisis** gives refractory hypotension in a patient on chronic steroids or with adrenal disease; treat with hydrocortisone (typical adult dose 100 mg IV) and fluids.

@example
title: Septic shock numbers
problem: A 72-kg woman with a urinary tract infection has blood pressure 78/40 mm Hg, heart rate 124/min, temperature 39.1 degrees C and lactate 4.8 mmol/L. Describe the first hour and the initial fluid volume.
step: Classify: warm skin, wide pulse pressure and fever suggest distributive (septic) shock; confirm flat neck veins and look at the inferior vena cava with ultrasound.
step: Draw lactate and two sets of blood cultures, plus urine culture, then give broad-spectrum antibiotics within the hour; do not delay antibiotics to obtain a CT.
step: Fluid: 30 mL/kg x 72 kg = 2160 mL of balanced crystalloid within 3 hours, then reassess with dynamic measures.
step: MAP = (78 + 2 x 40)/3 = 52.7 mm Hg. If MAP stays below 65 despite fluids, start norepinephrine; consider source control for obstruction (renal ultrasound for hydronephrosis).

@mistakes
- Waiting for blood-culture results or imaging before giving antibiotics in septic shock.
- Giving antihistamines or steroids before epinephrine in anaphylaxis, or giving epinephrine intravenously as the routine first route.
- Using dopamine as first-line pressor, or phenylephrine in a patient who needs inotropy.
- Treating cold, clammy shock with large fluid volumes without checking for pulmonary edema or obstruction.

@practice self
prompt: A 30-year-old woman develops hives, wheezing, a swollen tongue and a blood pressure of 76/40 mm Hg ten minutes after a bee sting. Write the order of your treatments and your plan for observation and discharge.
hint: Epinephrine goes first; everything else is an adjunct.
solution: Remove the trigger, lay her flat with legs raised, and give epinephrine 0.3 to 0.5 mg IM (1 mg/mL) in the anterolateral thigh, repeating every 5 to 15 minutes if needed. Give high-flow oxygen, place two IVs and give a rapid crystalloid bolus, and prepare for early intubation for stridor or tongue swelling. If refractory, start an epinephrine infusion. Adjuncts after epinephrine: H1 antihistamine, albuterol for bronchospasm, and corticosteroid, which do not replace epinephrine. Observe for several hours (longer for severe reactions because of biphasic reactions), and at discharge prescribe epinephrine auto-injectors, teach their use, and refer to allergy.
