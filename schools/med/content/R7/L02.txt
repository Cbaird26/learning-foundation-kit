@article
Cardiac arrest is the loss of effective circulation. Survival depends on a chain: immediate recognition, early high-quality CPR, early defibrillation for shockable rhythms, advanced life support, and post-arrest care. The details below follow the American Heart Association (AHA) adult guidelines as commonly taught in 2025; doses are typical adult doses for study and must be checked against current references before any clinical use.

### Recognize and start

An unresponsive adult who is not breathing or only gasping and has no definite pulse (checked for no more than 10 seconds) needs CPR now. Call for help and a defibrillator, and start compressions. Compressions come first because coronary perfusion pressure builds slowly and falls within seconds of a pause.

### High-quality CPR

- Rate **100 to 120/min**.
- Depth at least **2 inches (5 cm)** and not more than 2.4 inches (6 cm) in an adult, with full chest recoil.
- Minimize interruptions; a high chest compression fraction (time spent compressing) is the goal.
- Before an advanced airway: **30 compressions : 2 breaths**. After an advanced airway: continuous compressions with **1 breath every 6 seconds** (10 breaths/min).
- Switch compressors about every 2 minutes. Use waveform capnography when intubated; an end-tidal CO$_2$ persistently below 10 mm Hg during CPR predicts a poor outcome and prompts improving CPR quality.

### The two branches

Attach the monitor or defibrillator and read the rhythm.

**Shockable: ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT).** Deliver a shock immediately and resume CPR for 2 minutes. Use the manufacturer's recommended biphasic energy; if unknown, use the maximum available (typically 200 J). Recheck the rhythm every 2 minutes. Give **epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3 to 5 minutes**, typically after initial shocks have failed. For VF/pVT that persists after shocks, give **amiodarone 300 mg** IV/IO bolus, then 150 mg for a second dose; lidocaine (1 to 1.5 mg/kg, then 0.5 to 0.75 mg/kg) is an acceptable alternative.

**Nonshockable: pulseless electrical activity (PEA) or asystole.** No shock. Continue CPR and give **epinephrine 1 mg every 3 to 5 minutes, as soon as possible** because earlier epinephrine is associated with better outcomes in nonshockable rhythms. Atropine and vasopressin no longer have a role. Confirm asystole in more than one lead. Spend the 2-minute cycles looking for causes.

### Reversible causes: Hs and Ts

| H | T |
|---|---|
| Hypovolemia | Tension pneumothorax |
| Hypoxia | Tamponade (cardiac) |
| Hydrogen ion (acidosis) | Toxins |
| Hypo- or hyperkalemia | Thrombosis, pulmonary (PE) |
| Hypothermia | Thrombosis, coronary (MI) |

Point-of-care ultrasound can find tamponade, a hyperdynamic empty ventricle in hypovolemia, or a dilated right ventricle in massive PE without prolonging pauses beyond 10 seconds. Hyperkalemia is treated with calcium (for example, calcium chloride 1 g IV) plus insulin with glucose and bicarbonate as indicated.

### Return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) and post-arrest care

After ROSC, obtain a 12-lead ECG and send a STEMI patient to the catheterization laboratory. Avoid hypoxia and hyperoxia (target SpO$_2$ about 92 to 98%), avoid hypotension (systolic pressure at least 90 mm Hg, MAP at least 65 mm Hg), and correct the cause. Patients who remain comatose receive **targeted temperature management**: choose a constant target between 32 and 37.5 degrees C, maintain it for at least 36 hours (the 2025 AHA recommendation; earlier AHA guidance said 24 hours), then keep preventing fever (European guidelines advise fever prevention for at least 72 hours after ROSC). Avoid early prognostication; wait at least 72 hours after normothermia and exclude confounders such as sedatives.

### Peri-arrest rhythms

For a patient with a pulse, treat by stability. **Unstable tachycardia** (hypotension, altered mental status, shock, ischemic chest pain or acute heart failure) gets **synchronized cardioversion**. **Symptomatic bradycardia** with poor perfusion gets atropine (typical adult dose 1 mg IV, repeated every 3 to 5 minutes up to 3 mg), then dopamine or epinephrine infusion or transcutaneous pacing. Atropine is unlikely to work in high-degree block below the AV node, so pace early.

@example
title: Running a VF arrest
problem: A 58-year-old man collapses in the waiting room. He is unresponsive and not breathing. The monitor shows ventricular fibrillation. Walk through the first six minutes.
step: Start CPR at 100 to 120/min, call the code team and bring the defibrillator. Place pads while compressions continue.
step: Rhythm check shows VF. Charge while compressions continue, clear, shock, and resume compressions immediately for 2 minutes (30:2 until an advanced airway is placed).
step: At the next rhythm check VF persists. Shock again, resume CPR, and give epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO (repeat every 3 to 5 minutes). Place an advanced airway without interrupting compressions for more than a few seconds and confirm with waveform capnography.
step: At the following check VF persists. Shock, resume CPR and give amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO. Consider the Hs and Ts, with hypoxia, hyperkalemia and acute coronary occlusion at the top of the list.
step: If a pulse returns, obtain an ECG, avoid hypotension and hyperoxia, and begin post-arrest care and temperature control.

@mistakes
- Pausing compressions for long periods to intubate or to search for IV access.
- Shocking asystole or PEA, or giving atropine in an arrest.
- Hyperventilating after intubation, which raises intrathoracic pressure and lowers venous return; ventilate at 10 breaths/min.
- Forgetting to search for reversible causes during each 2-minute cycle.
