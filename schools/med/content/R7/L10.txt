@article
Vasoactive drugs push blood pressure and cardiac output toward a goal; they treat the physiology, not the cause. First restore volume if it is low and fix the underlying cause; then choose the drug by the defect: low vascular tone, low contractility, or both. Doses are typical adult doses for study.

### The drugs

| Drug | Receptors | Main effect | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Norepinephrine** | $\alpha_1$ strong, $\beta_1$ | Vasoconstriction, modest inotropy | First-line pressor in most shock |
| **Epinephrine** | $\alpha$, $\beta_1$, $\beta_2$ | Inotropy and vasoconstriction | Anaphylaxis, refractory shock, arrest; raises lactate |
| **Vasopressin** | $V_1$ (non-adrenergic) | Vasoconstriction | Add to norepinephrine; fixed dose about 0.03 units/min; may reduce arrhythmias |
| **Phenylephrine** | $\alpha_1$ only | Vasoconstriction | Reflex bradycardia; can lower cardiac output; useful with tachyarrhythmia |
| **Dopamine** | Dose-dependent D, $\beta_1$, $\alpha$ | Variable | More arrhythmias than norepinephrine; rarely preferred |
| **Dobutamine** | $\beta_1 > \beta_2$ | Inotropy and mild vasodilation | Low-output states; may drop blood pressure |
| **Milrinone** | PDE-3 inhibitor | Inotropy and vasodilation | Renally cleared; hypotension; long half-life |

### Choosing by shock type

- **Septic (distributive):** norepinephrine first; add vasopressin if requirements rise; add epinephrine for persistent hypotension; hydrocortisone for refractory shock. Dobutamine only if there is persistent hypoperfusion with documented low output.
- **Cardiogenic:** norepinephrine to maintain MAP; add dobutamine (or milrinone) for low cardiac output once pressure allows. Treat the cause: revascularization, mechanical support, and avoid fluid overload.
- **Hypovolemic and hemorrhagic:** volume and blood first; vasopressors are only a bridge and may worsen organ ischemia.
- **Obstructive:** relieve the obstruction (decompression, drainage, thrombolysis); fluid cautiously for right-sided failure from PE; norepinephrine bridges.
- **Anaphylactic:** IM epinephrine, then an epinephrine infusion if refractory.
- **Neurogenic:** norepinephrine (phenylephrine is an option); atropine for symptomatic bradycardia.
- **Adrenal crisis and other:** hydrocortisone.

Peripheral administration of norepinephrine is acceptable in the short term through a good proximal vein, with vigilance for extravasation (treat with phentolamine). Titrate to a MAP target of about 65 mm Hg and to markers of perfusion: mentation, capillary refill, lactate and urine output of at least 0.5 mL/kg/hour.

### Infusion arithmetic

$$\text{rate (mL/h)} = \frac{\text{dose (mcg/kg/min)} \times \text{weight (kg)} \times 60}{\text{concentration (mcg/mL)}}.$$
For norepinephrine, 8 mg in 250 mL is 32 mcg/mL.

### Analgesia, sedation and delirium

The Society of Critical Care Medicine PADIS guidelines favor **analgesia first** (for example fentanyl), then the lightest sedation that is safe, with a target Richmond Agitation-Sedation Scale (RASS) of 0 to $-1$. Prefer **propofol or dexmedetomidine** over benzodiazepines, because benzodiazepines increase delirium and ventilator days. Propofol gives rapid onset but causes hypotension, hypertriglyceridemia and, with prolonged high-dose use, **propofol infusion syndrome** (metabolic acidosis, rhabdomyolysis, cardiac failure). Dexmedetomidine ($\alpha_2$ agonist) spares respiratory drive but causes bradycardia and hypotension. Ketamine gives analgesia and preserves blood pressure. Screen daily for delirium with the CAM-ICU. Use a **daily sedation interruption** with a spontaneous breathing trial, early mobilization, sleep protection and family presence (the ABCDEF bundle).

### Monitoring

- **Arterial line:** continuous blood pressure and frequent blood gases; suggested for patients on vasopressors as soon as practical.
- **Central venous catheter:** for vasopressors and venous sampling; a central venous pressure alone poorly predicts fluid responsiveness. Use **dynamic measures**: passive leg raise, pulse-pressure variation (in a mechanically ventilated, sinus-rhythm patient on adequate tidal volumes), and ultrasound.
- **Central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO$_2$)** and **lactate trends** give perfusion information.
- **Capnography** and pulse oximetry; urine output; intracranial pressure monitoring in severe brain injury.
- **Daily care:** head of bed at 30 to 45 degrees, venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, stress ulcer prophylaxis in high-risk ventilated patients, glucose control (about 140 to 180 mg/dL), restrictive transfusion (hemoglobin threshold about 7 g/dL).

@example
title: Norepinephrine infusion rate
problem: A 70-kg patient in septic shock needs norepinephrine at 0.2 mcg/kg/min. The bag contains 8 mg in 250 mL. What pump rate should be set?
step: Concentration = 8000 mcg / 250 mL = 32 mcg/mL.
step: Dose per minute = 0.2 x 70 = 14 mcg/min, or 840 mcg/hour.
step: Rate = 840 / 32 = 26.25 mL/h.
step: Reassess MAP and lactate; if requirements rise above about 0.25 to 0.5 mcg/kg/min, add vasopressin.

@mistakes
- Using dopamine as a routine first-line drug in septic shock.
- Using phenylephrine in cardiogenic shock with low output, increasing afterload on a failing ventricle.
- Giving vasopressors to a hypovolemic patient without fluid or blood.
- Defaulting to benzodiazepine infusions for sedation rather than analgesia-first, light sedation.

answer: 15
check: 0.1 * 80 * 60 / (8000 / 250)
tol: 0.1
unit: mL/h
hint: Convert the dose to mcg/min, then to mcg/h, then divide by the concentration.
solution: 0.1 x 80 = 8 mcg/min = 480 mcg/h; 480/32 = 15 mL/h.
