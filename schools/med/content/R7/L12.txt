@article
Emergency care ends not with a diagnosis but with a **safe disposition**: someone, somewhere, knows what to do next. The commonest preventable harms in emergency medicine arise at transitions: triage, consultation, admission, transfer, and shift change.

### Triage: the Emergency Severity Index (ESI)

ESI sorts patients into five levels with a simple algorithm:

1. **Level 1:** needs an immediate life-saving intervention (cardiac arrest, intubation, shock requiring immediate resuscitation).
2. **Level 2:** high-risk situation, confusion, lethargy or disorientation, or severe pain or distress (chest pain suspicious for ACS, stroke symptoms, suspected sepsis).
3. **Level 3, 4, 5:** stable patients sorted by expected **resources** (labs, imaging, IV fluids or medication, procedures, consultation): two or more resources is level 3, one is level 4, none is level 5. Vital signs in the danger zone may upgrade a level 3 patient.

### Disposition

Options: **discharge** with follow-up, **observation** (short stay to complete a risk assessment or treatment), **admission** to the floor, telemetry, step-down or ICU according to the level of monitoring and organ support needed, **transfer** to another facility with greater capability, **left against medical advice (AMA)**, or **death**. Admit to the ICU if the patient needs vasopressors, mechanical ventilation, titration of vasoactive infusions or invasive monitoring. Boarding in the ED does not lower the required level of care.

Safe discharge requires stable vital signs, a diagnosis or well-defined symptom plan, understanding by the patient, medications they can obtain, follow-up arranged within a defined time, and **return precautions**. Use **teach-back** so the patient repeats the key points in their own words, in their preferred language with an interpreter when needed.

### EMTALA

The Emergency Medical Treatment and Labor Act requires any hospital with an emergency department to provide, to anyone who comes in, a **medical screening examination** to determine whether an emergency medical condition exists, and **stabilizing treatment** within its capability, regardless of ability to pay. An unstable patient may be transferred only if the benefits outweigh the risks and the following are in place: physician certification of that benefit, the **receiving facility has accepted** and has capacity and specialty capability, medical records and imaging accompany the patient, and transport is by qualified personnel with appropriate equipment. Insurance status does not determine transfer. Labor is an emergency condition until delivery of the placenta.

### Capacity, consent and refusal

The patient with decision-making capacity can consent to or refuse care. Capacity is task-specific and has four elements: the ability to **understand** the information, **appreciate** its application to oneself, **reason** about options, and **express a choice**. In an emergency in which the patient cannot consent, and no surrogate is available, implied consent applies for necessary treatment. For a patient leaving AMA, document the capacity assessment, the risks explained (including death), alternatives offered, the patient's understanding, and an invitation to return. Mandatory reporting laws vary by state but commonly cover suspected child abuse and neglect, elder abuse, certain communicable diseases and some firearm injuries.

### Documentation

A good emergency record reads as a reasoning trail:

- History and examination with pertinent negatives.
- **Medical decision making:** the differential diagnosis considered, tests ordered and their interpretation, and reasoning for the working diagnosis.
- **ED course:** treatments given, times, reassessments and response.
- Consultations (who, when, recommendation), discussion with the patient or family, disposition and instructions.
- **Critical care time:** time spent personally providing or directing care for a patient with a high probability of imminent life-threatening deterioration. The first 30 to 74 minutes is billed as 99291, and each additional block of up to 30 minutes as 99292 (under CPT rules from 75 minutes; Medicare requires a full 30 minutes beyond 74, that is 104 minutes in total). Time spent on separately billed procedures is excluded.

Quality metrics frequently tracked include door-to-ECG within 10 minutes for chest pain, door-to-balloon of 90 minutes or less for STEMI, door-to-needle for thrombolysis (goal of 60 minutes or less, many centers aim for 45), and time to antibiotics in sepsis.

### Handoffs

Handoffs fail from omission and assumptions. **I-PASS** is a validated structure:
- **I**llness severity: stable, "watcher," or unstable.
- **P**atient summary: events, history, assessment and plan.
- **A**ction list: to-do items with owner and deadline.
- **S**ituation awareness and contingency planning: "if X happens, do Y."
- **S**ynthesis by receiver: the receiver restates and asks questions.

**SBAR** (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) is a concise format for urgent calls and consultations. State clearly what you want: "I am requesting admission to the ICU."

@example
title: An I-PASS handoff
problem: At shift change you hold a 71-year-old woman with pneumonia. She is on 4 L oxygen, has a blood pressure of 108/62 mm Hg after 1 L fluid, a lactate of 2.6 mmol/L, antibiotics were given at 14:10, and a repeat lactate is pending. The ICU has been consulted and has agreed to evaluate her. Give the handoff.
step: **Illness severity:** "Watcher," at risk of becoming unstable.
step: **Patient summary:** 71-year-old woman with community-acquired pneumonia, mild hypoxemia on 4 L oxygen, early sepsis; fluids and antibiotics at 14:10 given; blood pressure improved.
step: **Action list:** follow up repeat lactate at 16:30; reassess blood pressure and oxygenation hourly; confirm ICU evaluation by 17:00; follow blood cultures.
step: **Situation awareness and contingency:** if MAP is below 65 despite another liter of fluid, start norepinephrine and call the ICU; if oxygen requirement rises above 6 L, consider high-flow oxygen or intubation. **Synthesis:** the receiver reads back the plan and asks questions.

@mistakes
- Documenting the final diagnosis only, without the differential and reasoning that supported it.
- Discharging a patient who cannot explain the plan, without teach-back or return precautions.
- Transferring an unstable patient without confirming that the receiving hospital has accepted.
- Giving a handoff with no contingency plan and no read-back from the receiver.
