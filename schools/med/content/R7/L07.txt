@article
In an overdose the history is often unreliable, so you rely on examination: vital signs, pupils, skin, bowel sounds, mental status and reflexes define a **toxidrome**, a syndrome that points to a class of drug and its antidote. Doses below are typical adult doses for study; check current references before any clinical use.

### General approach

Treat the patient, not the pill bottle. Airway, breathing and circulation first. Check glucose and obtain an ECG (QRS and QTc) in every undifferentiated overdose, plus acetaminophen and salicylate levels, because both are often asymptomatic early. Consider **activated charcoal** (about 1 g/kg) for a recent ingestion (within about 1 hour) of a charcoal-binding substance in a patient with a protected airway; it does not bind metals (lithium, iron) or alcohols. Do not induce emesis. Whole-bowel irrigation helps for sustained-release products and iron. Contact the regional poison center.

### Toxidromes

| Toxidrome | Vitals and pupils | Skin, other | Examples | Antidote or treatment |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sympathomimetic | High HR, BP, T; large pupils | Diaphoresis, agitation | Cocaine, amphetamines | Benzodiazepines; cooling |
| Anticholinergic | High HR, T; large pupils | Dry, flushed skin, urinary retention, delirium ("mad as a hatter") | Diphenhydramine, atropine, tricyclics | Supportive, benzodiazepines; physostigmine in selected cases |
| Cholinergic | Slow HR, small pupils | Salivation, lacrimation, urination, diarrhea, bronchorrhea, bronchospasm | Organophosphates, carbamates | Atropine, then pralidoxime |
| Opioid | Slow RR, small pupils | Sedation, hypoactive bowel | Heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone | Naloxone |
| Sedative-hypnotic | Variable, normal pupils | Slurred speech, ataxia, coma | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, ethanol | Supportive; flumazenil only in selected cases |
| Serotonin syndrome | High HR, T | Clonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis | SSRIs plus MAOIs or linezolid | Benzodiazepines, cooling, cyproheptadine |

**Naloxone** (typical adult starting dose 0.4 mg IV or IM, titrated to respiratory rate; 4 mg intranasally) reverses opioid respiratory depression but may precipitate withdrawal and lasts less than many opioids, so observe for recurrent sedation. **Flumazenil** can precipitate seizures in benzodiazepine-dependent patients and in mixed overdoses with proconvulsants; it is not routine.

### Specific poisonings and antidotes

- **Acetaminophen:** early patients have no symptoms; liver injury appears at 24 to 72 hours. Plot a level drawn at least 4 hours after a single acute ingestion on the **Rumack-Matthew nomogram**; a level above the treatment line (150 microgram/mL at 4 hours) indicates **N-acetylcysteine** (NAC), which replenishes glutathione. A typical 21-hour IV regimen begins with 150 mg/kg over 1 hour. NAC is most effective within 8 hours but is still given later and for unknown ingestion time with detectable level or elevated transaminases.
- **Salicylates:** tinnitus, tachypnea, fever, confusion. Mixed anion-gap metabolic acidosis and respiratory alkalosis. Treat with IV sodium bicarbonate (urine alkalinization, keep serum pH from falling), potassium repletion and early hemodialysis for altered mental status, pulmonary edema, renal failure or very high levels. Do not intubate casually, because loss of respiratory compensation can precipitate collapse.
- **Toxic alcohols:** methanol (visual disturbance, optic neuropathy) and ethylene glycol (calcium oxalate crystals, renal failure) cause an anion-gap acidosis and an elevated osmolar gap early. Give **fomepizole** (blocks alcohol dehydrogenase), correct acidosis, and use hemodialysis for severe disease.
- **Tricyclic antidepressants:** anticholinergic effects, seizures, wide QRS and ventricular arrhythmias from sodium-channel blockade. Treat QRS above 100 ms or arrhythmias with **sodium bicarbonate**; benzodiazepines for seizures.
- **Organophosphates:** cholinergic toxidrome; **atropine** titrated to drying bronchial secretions, then **pralidoxime**.
- **Beta-blocker and calcium-channel blocker:** bradycardia and hypotension; give calcium, glucagon (beta-blockers), and high-dose insulin with glucose, plus vasopressors.
- **Digoxin:** arrhythmias, hyperkalemia; **digoxin-specific Fab fragments**.
- **Others:** carbon monoxide: 100% oxygen (pulse oximetry is falsely normal); cyanide: hydroxocobalamin; methemoglobinemia: methylene blue; isoniazid seizures: pyridoxine; iron: deferoxamine; sulfonylurea hypoglycemia: octreotide; warfarin: vitamin K and prothrombin complex concentrate; heparin: protamine; dabigatran: idarucizumab.

### Calculations

$$\text{Osmolar gap} = \text{measured osmolality} - \left(2\,\text{Na} + \frac{\text{glucose}}{18} + \frac{\text{BUN}}{2.8}\right), \qquad \text{Anion gap} = \text{Na} - (\text{Cl} + \text{HCO}_3).$$
A gap above about 10 mOsm/kg suggests unmeasured osmoles such as toxic alcohols.

@example
title: A possible acetaminophen ingestion
problem: A 19-year-old woman reports taking many acetaminophen tablets 5 hours ago. She feels well. Her 5-hour level is above the treatment line. What do you do?
step: She is asymptomatic, which is typical in the first 24 hours; absence of symptoms does not exclude toxicity.
step: Her 4-hour-or-later level is above the line on the Rumack-Matthew nomogram, so give N-acetylcysteine now, without waiting for transaminases.
step: Send AST, ALT, INR, creatinine, and a repeat acetaminophen level; continue NAC until the level is undetectable and liver tests are improving.
step: Provide psychiatric evaluation and safe disposition after medical clearance; this may be intentional self-harm.

@mistakes
- Reassuring an asymptomatic patient after an acetaminophen or salicylate ingestion and discharging without a level.
- Using flumazenil routinely for a possible benzodiazepine overdose.
- Forgetting that opioid effects can outlast naloxone, so the patient may become sedated again.
- Intubating a salicylate-poisoned patient without a plan to maintain the high minute ventilation.

@practice self
prompt: A 24-year-old woman presents 8 hours after intentionally taking a large number of aspirin tablets. She has tinnitus, vomiting, respiratory rate 32/min, temperature 38.4 degrees C and confusion. Describe the expected acid-base findings and your treatment plan, including the role of intubation.
hint: Think of the two simultaneous acid-base processes and how to trap salicylate in the urine.
solution: Expect respiratory alkalosis from direct stimulation of the respiratory center plus anion-gap metabolic acidosis from uncoupled oxidative phosphorylation. Send a salicylate level (repeat serially), blood gas, electrolytes, glucose and creatinine. Give IV fluids, sodium bicarbonate to alkalinize the urine (replace potassium), and consider activated charcoal if early. Because of her confusion, she meets criteria for emergent hemodialysis. Avoid intubation if possible; if needed, maintain a high minute ventilation to avoid precipitating acidemia and CNS salicylate entry, and arrange dialysis immediately.
