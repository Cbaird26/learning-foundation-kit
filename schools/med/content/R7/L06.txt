@article
Trauma is the leading cause of death in young adults. The Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) approach is a systematic primary survey that finds and fixes life threats in order of how fast they kill, followed by a secondary survey and definitive care.

### Primary survey: xABCDE

- **x: exsanguinating hemorrhage** is controlled first: direct pressure, tourniquet for limb bleeding, pelvic binder, hemostatic dressing.
- **A: airway** with cervical spine motion restriction. A patient who speaks has a patent airway. Intubate for airway compromise or a GCS of 8 or below.
- **B: breathing:** inspect, palpate, auscultate. Life threats: tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, flail chest.
- **C: circulation:** two large-bore IVs, blood pressure, FAST. Shock in trauma is hemorrhage until proven otherwise.
- **D: disability:** GCS, pupils, glucose, motor examination.
- **E: exposure and environment:** remove clothes, log-roll, keep warm.

### Chest injuries

**Tension pneumothorax**: clinical diagnosis (dyspnea, hypotension, distended neck veins, absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation as a late sign). Needle decompression in adults in the fourth or fifth intercostal space, anterior to the midaxillary line (ATLS 10); the second intercostal space at the midclavicular line remains an option and is used in children, or finger thoracostomy, followed by chest tube.

**Open pneumothorax** ("sucking chest wound"): cover with an occlusive dressing taped on three sides, then chest tube at a separate site.

**Massive hemothorax:** more than 1500 mL of blood on initial chest tube placement, or more than 200 mL/hour for 2 to 4 hours, indicates thoracotomy. Resuscitate with blood.

**Cardiac tamponade:** Beck triad, positive FAST pericardial view; pericardiocentesis or thoracotomy.

**Flail chest** (three or more adjacent ribs each fractured in two places) causes paradoxical movement and underlying pulmonary contusion; treat with analgesia, oxygen and ventilation as needed.

### Hemorrhagic shock

Blood volume is approximately 70 mL/kg in an adult. The ATLS classes are:

| Class | Blood loss | Typical findings |
|---|---|---|
| I | Under 15% (under about 750 mL) | Normal vitals |
| II | 15 to 30% (750 to 1500 mL) | Tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure, anxious |
| III | 30 to 40% (1500 to 2000 mL) | Hypotension, marked tachycardia, confusion |
| IV | Over 40% (over 2000 mL) | Profound shock, obtunded, minimal urine |

Young patients compensate until late, so a normal blood pressure does not exclude severe hemorrhage; the shock index and tachycardia matter. **Damage control resuscitation:** early blood products in a balanced ratio (approximately 1:1:1 packed red cells, plasma and platelets) rather than large volumes of crystalloid; permissive hypotension (systolic pressure about 80 to 90 mm Hg) until bleeding is controlled, except in traumatic brain injury; **tranexamic acid** (typical adult dose 1 g IV over 10 minutes within 3 hours of injury, then 1 g over 8 hours); warming; calcium replacement; and early surgical or interventional hemostasis. The "lethal triad" is hypothermia, acidosis and coagulopathy.

### Abdominal and pelvic trauma

**FAST** (focused assessment with sonography in trauma) looks for free fluid in the pericardium, right upper quadrant (hepatorenal space), left upper quadrant, and pelvis. It detects fluid, not organ injury, and a negative study does not exclude retroperitoneal or hollow-viscus injury. Decision logic:

- **Unstable blunt trauma with positive FAST:** laparotomy.
- **Stable blunt trauma:** CT of the abdomen and pelvis with IV contrast; solid-organ injuries (spleen is the most commonly injured in blunt trauma, then liver) are often managed non-operatively.
- **Penetrating abdominal trauma with instability, peritonitis or evisceration** (and gunshot wounds crossing the peritoneum): laparotomy.
- **Pelvic fracture with shock:** pelvic binder, blood, then preperitoneal packing or angioembolization. Blood at the urethral meatus or a high-riding prostate calls for a retrograde urethrogram before a Foley catheter.

### Head and spine

**Cervical spine:** the NEXUS criteria allow clearance without imaging if the patient has no midline tenderness, no focal neurologic deficit, normal alertness, no intoxication and no painful distracting injury; otherwise use the Canadian C-spine rule or CT. **Head injury:** the GCS stratifies severity (13 to 15 mild, 9 to 12 moderate, 8 or below severe). CT head without contrast for moderate or severe injury and for mild injury meeting decision rules (for example Canadian CT Head Rule). Signs of herniation (Cushing reflex of hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respiration, blown pupil) call for hyperosmolar therapy, head elevation and neurosurgical consultation. Avoid hypotension and hypoxia, the two most important secondary insults.

title: Estimating volume loss
problem: A 70-kg man has an estimated 25% blood volume loss. Estimate the loss in milliliters and classify the shock.
step: Estimated blood volume is 70 mL/kg x 70 kg = 4900 mL.
step: Loss is 0.25 x 4900 = 1225 mL.
step: A loss of 15% to 30% is class II: expect tachycardia, narrowed pulse pressure and a normal or minimally lower blood pressure.
step: Because he is at risk of ongoing bleeding, activate blood products and look for the source with FAST; do not rely on crystalloid.

@mistakes
- Waiting for a chest radiograph before decompressing a clinically obvious tension pneumothorax.
- Relying on a normal blood pressure to exclude major hemorrhage in a young patient.
- Placing a Foley catheter before considering a urethral injury.
- Giving large crystalloid volumes in hemorrhagic shock, causing dilution, acidosis and hypothermia.
