@article
Environmental emergencies are failures of thermoregulation or oxygen delivery caused by the external setting. Many are diagnosed by context and a temperature measured with a core (rectal or esophageal) thermometer.

### Heat illness

**Heat exhaustion** produces weakness, headache, nausea, tachycardia and a core temperature below 40 degrees C with intact mentation. Treat with rest, cooling and oral or IV fluids. **Heat stroke** is core temperature above about 40 degrees C **with central nervous system dysfunction** (confusion, seizures, coma). It may be exertional (young athletes, laborers, soldiers) or classic (older adults in heat waves). Complications include rhabdomyolysis, acute kidney injury, liver injury, DIC and shock. The most important treatment is **immediate whole-body cooling**: cold-water immersion (about 1 to 15 degrees C water) is best and cools fastest; alternatives are ice-water-soaked sheets, evaporative cooling and cold IV fluids. The target is to reach about 39 degrees C within 30 minutes of recognition; stop active cooling at about 38 to 39 degrees C to avoid overshoot. Antipyretics such as acetaminophen are ineffective because the set point is normal. Dantrolene is not helpful in heat stroke. Think also of other hyperthermia syndromes: sympathomimetic toxicity, serotonin syndrome, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, malignant hyperthermia and thyroid storm, each with its own treatment.

### Hypothermia

Core temperature below 35 degrees C: **mild** 32 to 35 (shivering, tachycardia), **moderate** 28 to 32 (shivering stops, confusion, bradycardia, J or Osborn waves on ECG), **severe** below 28 (coma, arrhythmias, apparent death). Handle the patient gently, because rough movement can trigger ventricular fibrillation in a cold myocardium. Remove wet clothing and insulate. Rewarming is **passive** (blankets) for mild hypothermia, **active external** (forced warm air, heating blankets) for moderate, and **active core** (warmed IV fluids at 40 to 42 degrees C, warm humidified oxygen, warm body-cavity lavage, and extracorporeal rewarming with ECMO or bypass for arrest) for severe. In cardiac arrest with severe hypothermia, "no one is dead until warm and dead": continue resuscitation and rewarm, preferably with ECMO, rather than terminating early; medications and defibrillation are less effective below about 30 degrees C. Correct associated problems such as hypoglycemia and look for the trigger (sepsis, intoxication, hypothyroidism, adrenal crisis, trauma).

### Drowning

The key injury is hypoxia. Start with airway and **ventilation**; rescue breaths with compressions form the resuscitation, because the arrest is hypoxic. Give oxygen, consider noninvasive or invasive ventilation for pulmonary edema, and treat hypothermia and cervical spine injury if relevant. Observe symptomatic patients; asymptomatic patients with normal oxygenation after a mild event may be observed for several hours.

### Burns and smoke inhalation

Estimate burn size using the **rule of nines** for adults: head and neck 9%, each arm 9%, anterior trunk 18%, posterior trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%. Only partial-thickness and full-thickness burns are counted. The **Parkland formula** estimates 24-hour fluid need:
$$\text{Volume} = 4\ \text{mL}\times \text{weight (kg)}\times \%\text{TBSA},$$
with half given in the first 8 hours after the injury and half over the next 16 hours; titrate to urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/hour. Use lactated Ringer's. Early intubation is indicated for stridor, hoarseness, facial burns with soot in the airway, or deep neck burns before edema distorts the airway. Circumferential burns may need escharotomy. **Carbon monoxide** poisoning causes headache and confusion with normal pulse oximetry; treat with 100% oxygen and hyperbaric oxygen in selected cases. **Cyanide** toxicity from enclosed-space fires is suspected with coma, hypotension and a very high lactate; give **hydroxocobalamin**.

### Electrical injury and lightning

Low-voltage alternating current can cause tetany and ventricular fibrillation; high voltage causes deep tissue damage, rhabdomyolysis, and compartment syndrome. Obtain an ECG and creatine kinase and give IV fluids for rhabdomyolysis. In multiple lightning victims, use **reverse triage**: treat the patients in cardiac arrest first, because many can be resuscitated.

### Altitude illness

Above about 2500 m, **acute mountain sickness** (headache, nausea, fatigue) is treated with rest, stopping ascent and acetazolamide; typical adult prophylaxis is 125 mg twice daily. **High-altitude cerebral edema** adds ataxia and altered mentation: descend, give oxygen and dexamethasone. **High-altitude pulmonary edema** causes dyspnea at rest, cough, and hypoxemia: descend, give oxygen, and use nifedipine. Descent is the definitive treatment for both.

### Envenomation

For pit viper bites, local swelling and coagulopathy indicate envenomation; give antivenom, mark swelling, elevate and monitor labs. Do not use tourniquets, cutting or suction. Black widow bites cause severe muscle spasm and are treated with analgesia, benzodiazepines and sometimes antivenom.

@example
title: Parkland fluid calculation
problem: A 60-kg man has 40% total body surface area partial-thickness burns. Calculate the fluid for the first 8 hours and the hourly rate.
step: Total 24-hour volume = 4 mL x 60 kg x 40 = 9600 mL.
step: Half is given in the first 8 hours: 4800 mL.
step: Hourly rate = 4800/8 = 600 mL/h, adjusted to maintain urine output of about 0.5 mL/kg/hour (30 mL/h).
step: Time begins at the injury, not at arrival. Subtract fluids already given. Consider early intubation if there is airway involvement.

@mistakes
- Giving acetaminophen or ice-pack cooling alone to a heat stroke patient instead of cold-water immersion.
- Stopping resuscitation in a severely hypothermic patient before rewarming.
- Counting superficial (first-degree) burns when estimating burn size.
- Relying on a normal pulse oximeter reading to exclude carbon monoxide poisoning.
