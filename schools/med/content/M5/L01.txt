@article
Every autosomal gene is present in two copies, one from each parent, and a person's **genotype** at a locus is the pair of **alleles** they carry. Medical genetics begins with Mendel's rules, but clinical families rarely behave like textbook diagrams. Your job is to recognize the underlying pattern and then explain the exceptions.

### Autosomal dominant (AD)

One altered allele is enough to cause the phenotype. Features of an AD pedigree: the trait appears in every generation (**vertical transmission**), affected people have an affected parent (unless the variant is new), and **male-to-male transmission** occurs, which excludes X-linked inheritance. An affected heterozygote has a **50% risk** for each child, regardless of the child's sex. Many AD disorders involve structural proteins (collagen, fibrillin) or **gain-of-function** variants (achondroplasia, Huntington disease), and many involve haploinsufficiency of a regulatory or tumor-suppressor gene (neurofibromatosis type 1).

### Autosomal recessive (AR)

Two altered alleles are needed, so the trait often appears in siblings but not in parents, who are unaffected **carriers**. It is said to be **horizontal** across a generation. Two carrier parents have a **25%** risk of an affected child, a 50% chance of a carrier child, and a 25% chance of a child with neither variant, with each pregnancy independent. Consanguinity raises the chance that both parents carry the same rare allele inherited from a common ancestor. Many AR disorders are enzyme deficiencies (phenylketonuria, Tay–Sachs disease) because one working copy usually makes enough enzyme.

### X-linked recessive (XLR)

Males have one X chromosome, so a single variant allele on it is expressed (**hemizygous**). Affected people are mostly males; the trait is passed from carrier mothers to half their sons, and **there is no male-to-male transmission**. All daughters of an affected father are obligate carriers. A carrier mother has a 50% risk of an affected son and a 50% risk of a carrier daughter with each pregnancy. Examples: Duchenne muscular dystrophy, hemophilia A and B, G6PD deficiency. Because of **X-inactivation** (random in each cell early in female development), a carrier female can occasionally show mild features if inactivation is skewed.

### X-linked dominant and Y-linked

In **X-linked dominant** disorders, affected fathers pass the trait to all daughters and no sons, while affected mothers pass it to half of all children (examples: X-linked hypophosphatemia, Rett syndrome, which is usually lethal in males). **Y-linked** traits go from father to all sons.

### Why real pedigrees are messy

- **Penetrance** is the proportion of people with a genotype who show any phenotype. A person with a pathogenic variant and no signs is **non-penetrant** (reduced or age-dependent penetrance).
- **Variable expressivity** is variation in the severity of the phenotype among people who all have it, even within one family (neurofibromatosis type 1).
- **Pleiotropy** means one gene affects multiple organ systems (Marfan syndrome: eye, skeleton, aorta).
- **Locus heterogeneity**: variants in different genes cause the same phenotype. **Allelic heterogeneity**: different variants in one gene.
- **De novo variants** arise in the gamete or early embryo, so an affected child can have unaffected parents. **Germline mosaicism** means a parent carries the variant in some of their gametes but not in blood; that parent has a small but real recurrence risk (a few percent), as seen in osteogenesis imperfecta and Duchenne muscular dystrophy.
- **Codominance** shows both alleles in the heterozygote (ABO, HbA/HbS on electrophoresis).

When pedigree ratios seem off, check for incomplete penetrance, a new variant, a misattributed parent, or a small sample size before abandoning the model.

@example
title: Carrier parents with an affected child
problem: A couple are both carriers of the same pathogenic phenylalanine hydroxylase variant (autosomal recessive phenylketonuria). Find the probability that a child is affected, and the probability that an unaffected child is a carrier.
step: Write each parent as $Aa$. The Punnett square gives $AA$, $Aa$, $aA$, $aa$, each with probability $\frac{1}{4}$.
step: The child is affected only if the genotype is $aa$: probability $\frac{1}{4}$.
step: Among all children, the carrier genotypes $Aa$ and $aA$ together have probability $\frac{1}{2}$.
step: For an unaffected child, restrict to the three genotypes that are not $aa$. Two of those three are carriers, so the conditional probability is $\frac{2}{3}$.

@example
title: A carrier mother in an X-linked recessive family
problem: A woman is a known carrier of a Duchenne muscular dystrophy variant. Her partner is unaffected. Give the risk for each type of child.
step: Mother is $X^{D}X^{d}$ and father is $X^{D}Y$. Each pregnancy independently receives one maternal X (either with equal chance) and either the father's X or Y.
step: Sons receive the father's Y and one maternal X. So $\frac{1}{2}$ of sons are affected ($X^{d}Y$) and $\frac{1}{2}$ are unaffected.
step: Daughters receive the father's normal X, so none is affected, and $\frac{1}{2}$ are carriers.
step: For any one pregnancy, the chance of an affected son is $\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}$ (half the children are male, half of those affected).

@mistakes
- Treating a "recessive" pedigree as proof of consanguinity; most carrier couples are unrelated.
- Forgetting that an unaffected sibling of an affected child has a $\frac{2}{3}$, not $\frac{1}{2}$, chance of being a carrier.
- Reporting an XLR risk as 50% "for the child" without separating sons from daughters.
- Confusing penetrance (is any phenotype present?) with expressivity (how severe is it?).

@practice num
prompt: A woman is heterozygous for an autosomal dominant variant with 80% penetrance. Her partner does not carry the variant. What is the probability that a given child both inherits the variant and shows the phenotype? Give a fraction or decimal.
answer: 2/5
check: 0.5 * 0.8
hint: Break the event into two steps: inheriting the allele, then expressing it.
hint: Multiply the transmission probability by the penetrance.
solution: Transmission is $\frac{1}{2}$ and penetrance is $0.8$, so the probability is $0.5\times0.8=0.4=\frac{2}{5}$.
