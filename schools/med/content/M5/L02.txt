@article
A **pedigree** is a standardized family tree. Squares are males, circles are females, filled symbols are affected, a half-filled or dotted symbol marks a carrier, a diagonal line means deceased, and a double line joins a consanguineous couple. The **proband** (or index case) is the first affected person who brought the family to attention; the **consultand** is the person seeking counseling, who may be unaffected. An arrow marks the proband. Record at least three generations, ancestry, age at diagnosis or death, and pregnancy losses.

### Mendelian risk

Risk calculations multiply probabilities along a path and add the probabilities of alternative paths. For an autosomal recessive condition:

$$P(\text{affected child}) = P(\text{mother carrier}) \times P(\text{father carrier}) \times \tfrac{1}{4}.$$

A healthy sibling of someone with an AR disease has a **2/3** carrier probability. If the partner is a random member of a population with carrier frequency $c$, the risk to the couple's child is $\frac{2}{3}\times c \times \frac{1}{4}$. An **obligate carrier** is someone who must carry the variant given the pedigree, such as the mother of an affected son who also has an affected brother, or the daughter of a man with an X-linked recessive disease.

### Bayesian analysis

A **prior probability** comes from Mendelian inheritance. **Conditional information** then changes it: unaffected children of a woman at risk for an XLR disease, a normal examination at an age when a dominant disease would usually have appeared, or a negative test with imperfect sensitivity. Bayesian analysis uses a table:

| | Carrier | Not a carrier |
|---|---|---|
| Prior | $p$ | $1-p$ |
| Conditional | $c_1$ | $c_2$ |
| Joint | $p\,c_1$ | $(1-p)\,c_2$ |

$$\text{Posterior (carrier)}=\frac{p\,c_1}{p\,c_1+(1-p)\,c_2}.$$

Always check that the conditional events are what you actually observed: three unaffected sons give a conditional probability of $(\frac{1}{2})^3$ for a carrier, because each son has a 50% chance of being unaffected, and exactly 1 for a non-carrier. Notice that the posterior does not depend on the order of the sons.

For a late-onset dominant disease, the conditional probability for a carrier who is still well at age $a$ is $1-$ (penetrance by age $a$). The prior of 1/2 is reduced accordingly.

### Consanguinity

The **coefficient of relationship** is the fraction of genes shared by descent: 1/2 for parent–child and full siblings, 1/4 for grandparent–grandchild, aunt–niece and half siblings, 1/8 for first cousins. The **inbreeding coefficient** $F$ of a child is the probability that two alleles at a locus are identical by descent; for first cousins $F=\frac{1}{16}$. Children of first cousins have about a 3–5% risk of major congenital anomalies or severe recessive disease, roughly double the background risk.

### Practical points

- Draw first, calculate second. Mistakes come from misreading the chart.
- Clearly state your assumptions (penetrance, mutation rate, carrier frequency in this ancestry).
- Report a final number and an interpretation; "1 in 9" is more useful than 0.111.

@example
title: Autosomal recessive risk with a random partner
problem: A woman's brother has cystic fibrosis, and she is unaffected. Her partner has no family history, and the carrier frequency in his ancestry is $\frac{1}{25}$. What is the risk that their child will have cystic fibrosis?
step: Her parents must both be carriers, so she has a $\frac{2}{3}$ chance of being a carrier (she is unaffected, so there are three possible genotypes and two are carriers).
step: Her partner is a random member of the population, so $P(\text{carrier})=\frac{1}{25}$.
step: If both are carriers, the child is affected with probability $\frac{1}{4}$.
step: Multiply: $\frac{2}{3}\times\frac{1}{25}\times\frac{1}{4}=\frac{2}{300}=\frac{1}{150}$.

@example
title: Bayesian analysis for an X-linked recessive disease
problem: A woman's mother is an obligate carrier of Duchenne muscular dystrophy. The woman has three unaffected sons and no daughters. What is the probability that she is a carrier?
step: Prior: she received one of her carrier mother's two X chromosomes, so $P(\text{carrier})=\frac{1}{2}$ and $P(\text{not carrier})=\frac{1}{2}$.
step: Conditional: three unaffected sons. If she is a carrier, each son is unaffected with probability $\frac{1}{2}$, so $(\frac{1}{2})^3=\frac{1}{8}$. If not a carrier, the probability is $1$.
step: Joint: carrier $\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{8}=\frac{1}{16}$; non-carrier $\frac{1}{2}\times 1=\frac{8}{16}$.
step: Posterior: $\frac{1/16}{1/16+8/16}=\frac{1}{9}$, about 11%.

@mistakes
- Using $\frac{1}{2}$ instead of $\frac{2}{3}$ for an unaffected sibling of a person with an AR disease.
- Forgetting the conditional step, so that unaffected children do not lower the carrier risk.
- Adding probabilities that should be multiplied (independent events along a path).
- Applying the Bayesian table to the wrong event, such as using the probability of an affected child when the observation was an unaffected one.

@practice num
prompt: The unaffected sister of a child with Tay–Sachs disease is planning a pregnancy. Her partner is a randomly chosen person with a carrier frequency of $\frac{1}{40}$. What is the probability that their child will be affected? Give a fraction.
answer: 1/240
check: (2/3) * (1/40) * (1/4)
hint: Combine three probabilities: she is a carrier, he is a carrier, and the child inherits both.
hint: The sister's carrier probability is $\frac{2}{3}$ since she is unaffected.
solution: $\frac{2}{3}\times\frac{1}{40}\times\frac{1}{4}=\frac{2}{480}=\frac{1}{240}$.

@practice num
prompt: A woman's mother is an obligate carrier of an X-linked recessive disease. The woman has two unaffected sons. What is the probability that she is a carrier? Give a fraction.
answer: 1/5
check: (0.5 * 0.5**2) / (0.5 * 0.5**2 + 0.5 * 1)
hint: Prior $\frac{1}{2}$. Each unaffected son has conditional probability $\frac{1}{2}$ if she is a carrier.
hint: Joint carrier $=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{4}$; joint non-carrier $=\frac{1}{2}\cdot 1$.
solution: Carrier joint $\frac{1}{8}$, non-carrier joint $\frac{1}{2}=\frac{4}{8}$. Posterior $=\frac{1/8}{5/8}=\frac{1}{5}$.

@practice num
prompt: A 45-year-old woman is the daughter of a man with a late-onset autosomal dominant disorder. Assume that 60% of carriers are symptomatic by age 45 and she is currently unaffected. What is her probability of carrying the variant? Give a fraction.
answer: 2/7
check: (0.5 * 0.4) / (0.5 * 0.4 + 0.5 * 1)
hint: Prior $\frac{1}{2}$. What fraction of carriers would still be well at 45?
hint: Conditional: carriers $0.4$, non-carriers $1$.
solution: Joint carrier $0.5\times0.4=0.2$; joint non-carrier $0.5\times1=0.5$. Posterior $=\frac{0.2}{0.7}=\frac{2}{7}\approx 0.29$.

@practice num
prompt: A couple are first cousins. What is the inbreeding coefficient of their child? Give a fraction. (First cousins share $\frac{1}{8}$ of their genes by descent.)
answer: 1/16
check: (1/8) / 2
hint: The inbreeding coefficient is the chance the child receives identical alleles by descent from the two parents. It is half the coefficient of relationship of the parents.
hint: Half of $\frac{1}{8}$.
solution: $F=\frac{1}{2}\times\frac{1}{8}=\frac{1}{16}$.

@practice mc
prompt: A woman whose brother has Duchenne muscular dystrophy (X-linked recessive) has had four sons, all unaffected. Her mother is an obligate carrier. Her genetic counselor revises her carrier risk to less than the prior 50%. Which principle explains the revision?
choice: Independent assortment alters the carrier frequency in sisters.
choice*: Unaffected sons are more likely if she is not a carrier, so Bayesian analysis lowers her risk.
choice: Because the sons are healthy, she cannot be a carrier.
choice: X-inactivation protects sons of carriers from developing the disease.
hint: Compare the chance of four unaffected sons in a carrier and in a non-carrier.
solution: A carrier has a $(\frac{1}{2})^4=\frac{1}{16}$ chance of four unaffected sons, whereas a non-carrier has 1. The unaffected sons are therefore evidence against, but not proof against, carrier status; her risk falls to $\frac{1}{17}$.

@quiz num
prompt: A woman's sister has an autosomal recessive disorder, and the woman is unaffected. Her partner has no family history and the carrier frequency in his ancestry is $\frac{1}{30}$, but he tests negative on a panel that detects 80% of carrier variants. What is the probability that their child has cystic fibrosis? Give a fraction.
answer: 1/876
check: (2/3) * ((1/30 * 0.2) / (1/30 * 0.2 + 29/30 * 1)) * (1/4)
hint: First find the partner's residual carrier probability after a negative test, using Bayes with a conditional of $0.2$ for carriers and $1$ for non-carriers.
hint: Residual risk $=\frac{(1/30)(0.2)}{(1/30)(0.2)+(29/30)(1)}=\frac{1}{146}$. Then multiply by her $\frac{2}{3}$ and by $\frac{1}{4}$.
solution: Partner's residual risk is $\frac{0.2}{0.2+29}=\frac{1}{146}$. Child's risk is $\frac{2}{3}\times\frac{1}{146}\times\frac{1}{4}=\frac{1}{876}$.
