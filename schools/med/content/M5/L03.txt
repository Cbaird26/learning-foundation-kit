@article
Humans normally have 46 chromosomes: 22 pairs of autosomes and two sex chromosomes. Chromosome disorders are found in about half of first-trimester pregnancy losses and in roughly 0.5% of live births.

### Numerical abnormalities

**Euploidy** means a multiple of 23 chromosomes (triploidy, 69, is usually lost early or causes a partial mole when two sets are paternal). **Aneuploidy** is a gain or loss of single chromosomes. It arises mainly from **nondisjunction**, failure of homologs to separate in meiosis I or of sister chromatids in meiosis II (or in mitosis, producing **mosaicism**). The risk of autosomal trisomy rises steeply with maternal age because oocytes remain arrested in meiosis I for decades and cohesion between chromosomes weakens.

- **Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome)**: intellectual disability, hypotonia, upslanting palpebral fissures, epicanthal folds, single transverse palmar crease, duodenal atresia, atrioventricular septal defect, Hirschsprung disease, hypothyroidism, early-onset Alzheimer disease, increased risk of acute leukemia. About 95% are trisomy from maternal meiotic nondisjunction, about 4% are due to unbalanced Robertsonian translocation, and about 1% are mosaic.
- **Trisomy 18 (Edwards)**: clenched fists with overlapping fingers, rocker-bottom feet, small jaw, ventricular septal defect, severe intellectual disability; most die in the first year.
- **Trisomy 13 (Patau)**: cleft lip and palate, holoprosencephaly, polydactyly, cutis aplasia, microphthalmia; most die in the first year.
- **Monosomy X (Turner, 45,X)**: short stature, webbed neck, shield chest, streak ovaries with primary amenorrhea and elevated FSH, lymphedema, bicuspid aortic valve and coarctation, horseshoe kidney. The only viable monosomy. Management includes echocardiography or cardiac MRI, renal ultrasound, growth hormone and estrogen replacement. Intelligence is typically normal.
- **Klinefelter (47,XXY)**: tall, long limbs, small firm testes, gynecomastia, infertility (azoospermia), low testosterone with high FSH and LH. One Barr body. Testosterone replacement at puberty.

### Structural abnormalities

Deletions, duplications, inversions, translocations, rings and isochromosomes. In a **reciprocal translocation** two nonhomologous chromosomes exchange segments; carriers are usually healthy (balanced) but have increased miscarriage and risk of a child with an unbalanced karyotype. In a **Robertsonian translocation** the long arms of two acrocentric chromosomes (13, 14, 15, 21, 22) fuse at the centromere and the short arms are lost; carriers have 45 chromosomes and a normal phenotype. A 14;21 translocation carrier has a risk of Down syndrome in offspring of about 10–15% if the mother is the carrier and about 1–3% if the father is. A carrier of a 21;21 translocation can produce only trisomic or monosomic zygotes, so Down syndrome risk is essentially 100% for live births.

### Microdeletions

Often detected by FISH or microarray. **22q11.2 deletion** (DiGeorge/velocardiofacial): conotruncal heart defects, hypocalcemia from parathyroid hypoplasia, thymic hypoplasia with T-cell deficiency, cleft palate, and later psychiatric illness. **5p deletion (cri-du-chat)**: high-pitched cry. **7q11.23 deletion (Williams syndrome)**: supravalvular aortic stenosis, elfin facies, hypercalcemia, outgoing personality. **4p deletion (Wolf–Hirschhorn)**: "Greek warrior helmet" face.

### Karyotype nomenclature

Written as total number, sex chromosomes, then abnormalities: 47,XY,+21 (male with trisomy 21); 45,X (Turner); 46,XX,del(5)(p15) (cri-du-chat); 45,XX,der(14;21)(q10;q10) (balanced Robertsonian carrier). Count a fused derivative chromosome as one chromosome. In sex chromosome disorders, the number of Barr bodies (inactivated X chromosomes) is the number of X chromosomes minus one.

@example
title: Counting chromosomes in a translocation Down syndrome karyotype
problem: A child with Down syndrome has the karyotype 46,XY,der(14;21)(q10;q10),+21. Explain what the notation means and say what the parent's karyotype probably was.
step: The derivative chromosome der(14;21) is the fusion of the long arms of one chromosome 14 and one chromosome 21, written as a single chromosome.
step: The child has one normal 14, one normal 21, one fusion chromosome carrying 14q and 21q, plus one additional normal 21 (from +21), which gives three copies of 21q material in total.
step: The child's total count is 46 because the fusion is counted as one chromosome.
step: A parent with 45,XX,der(14;21)(q10;q10) or 45,XY,der(14;21)(q10;q10) is the likely source; parental karyotypes are needed because recurrence risk is then much higher than for sporadic trisomy 21.

@mistakes
- Believing all Down syndrome is due to maternal age; translocation Down syndrome is unrelated to maternal age.
- Counting a Robertsonian translocation carrier as having 46 chromosomes (the count is 45).
- Forgetting that the number of Barr bodies is one fewer than the number of X chromosomes.
- Mixing up the DiGeorge (22q11.2) and Williams (7q11.23) deletion features.

@quiz num
prompt: A child has the karyotype 46,XX,der(13;14)(q10;q10),+13. How many copies of the long arm of chromosome 13 (13q) are present in each cell?
answer: 3
check: 1 + 1 + 1
hint: List the chromosomes that carry 13q: any normal 13, the derivative chromosome, and any added 13.
hint: The derivative der(13;14) carries one copy of 13q, the single normal 13 carries one, and +13 adds one.
solution: One normal 13, the 13q carried on der(13;14), and the extra 13 give $1+1+1=3$ copies, so the child is trisomic for 13q (Patau syndrome) although the chromosome count is 46.
