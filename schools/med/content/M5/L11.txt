@article
Most of these syndromes are tested on board examinations as pattern recognition: a few unusual signs point to a gene, the gene points to a complication, and the complication tells you what to screen for.

### Connective tissue disorders

**Marfan syndrome** (*FBN1*, 15q21; autosomal dominant, about 25% de novo). Fibrillin-1 forms microfibrils and regulates TGF-β signaling. Features: tall stature with long limbs and arachnodactyly, arm span greater than height, pectus deformity, scoliosis, **ectopia lentis (usually upward)**, mitral valve prolapse, and **aortic root dilation with risk of dissection**, dural ectasia and spontaneous pneumothorax. Diagnosis uses the revised Ghent criteria (aortic root enlargement and ectopia lentis, or an *FBN1* variant with aortic enlargement, and a systemic score). Management: serial echocardiography, a beta-blocker or an angiotensin receptor blocker, avoidance of competitive contact sports, and elective aortic root repair when the root reaches about 5 cm (lower thresholds with rapid growth or a family history of dissection). Homocystinuria also causes lens dislocation (usually downward), with thrombosis and intellectual disability.

**Ehlers–Danlos syndromes**: classical (*COL5A1*, *COL5A2*; hyperextensible velvety skin, atrophic scars, joint hypermobility), hypermobile (the commonest; no gene identified), and **vascular** (*COL3A1*; thin translucent skin, easy bruising, arterial, uterine and bowel rupture). These three types are autosomal dominant (some rarer EDS types are recessive). **Osteogenesis imperfecta** (*COL1A1*, *COL1A2*; autosomal dominant) causes fractures with minimal trauma, blue sclerae, dentinogenesis imperfecta and hearing loss; bisphosphonates are used.

### Neurocutaneous disorders

**Neurofibromatosis type 1** (*NF1*, 17q11.2, neurofibromin, a negative regulator of RAS; autosomal dominant, about half de novo, essentially complete penetrance with variable expressivity). Diagnosis requires two or more of: **six or more café-au-lait macules** (over 5 mm before puberty, over 15 mm after), **two or more neurofibromas or one plexiform neurofibroma**, **axillary or inguinal freckling**, **optic pathway glioma**, **two or more Lisch nodules** (iris hamartomas), a distinctive bony lesion (sphenoid dysplasia, tibial pseudarthrosis), or a first-degree relative with NF1 (a pathogenic *NF1* variant also counts in current criteria). Complications: learning difficulties, scoliosis, hypertension (renal artery stenosis, pheochromocytoma), and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor.

**Neurofibromatosis type 2** (*NF2*, 22q12, merlin): **bilateral vestibular schwannomas** (hearing loss, tinnitus), meningiomas and ependymomas.

**Tuberous sclerosis complex** (*TSC1* on 9q34 encoding hamartin, *TSC2* on 16p13.3 encoding tuberin; autosomal dominant, about two-thirds de novo). Loss of the hamartin–tuberin complex activates **mTOR**. Features: hypomelanotic ("ash-leaf") macules, facial angiofibromas, shagreen patches, ungual fibromas, **cardiac rhabdomyomas** (often detected prenatally and regress), cortical tubers with **infantile spasms** and other seizures, subependymal giant cell astrocytoma, renal angiomyolipomas, and lymphangioleiomyomatosis. Treatment: vigabatrin is first line for infantile spasms; the mTOR inhibitor everolimus shrinks subependymal giant cell astrocytomas and angiomyolipomas.

### Developmental syndromes

**Williams syndrome** (7q11.23 microdeletion including *ELN*, elastin; usually sporadic): **supravalvular aortic stenosis**, elfin facies, **infantile hypercalcemia**, intellectual disability with relative strength in verbal and social skills ("cocktail party" personality). Test: microarray or FISH.

**Noonan syndrome** (*PTPN11* in about half; RAS–MAPK pathway; autosomal dominant): short stature, webbed neck, ptosis and low-set ears, **pulmonary valve stenosis and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy**, bleeding diathesis, and a **normal karyotype** in both sexes (unlike Turner syndrome with its left-sided heart lesions and ovarian failure).

**Achondroplasia** (*FGFR3* gain-of-function variant, nearly always p.Gly380Arg; autosomal dominant, about 80% de novo, associated with advanced paternal age). FGFR3 normally restrains growth-plate chondrocytes, so the activating variant shortens bones formed by endochondral ossification: **rhizomelic short limbs**, macrocephaly, frontal bossing, midface hypoplasia, trident hands and normal intelligence and lifespan, with risk of foramen magnum stenosis and hydrocephalus. **Homozygous achondroplasia is lethal**, so two affected parents have a 1/4 risk of a lethal homozygote, 1/2 of an affected child and 1/4 of an average-stature child.

### Cystic fibrosis

**Cystic fibrosis** (*CFTR*, 7q31.2, a cAMP-regulated chloride channel; autosomal recessive; p.Phe508del (ΔF508) is the commonest variant and impairs protein folding and trafficking). Thick secretions cause chronic sinopulmonary infection (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa*), bronchiectasis, pancreatic insufficiency with fat malabsorption, meconium ileus, biliary disease, diabetes, and **congenital bilateral absence of the vas deferens** in men. Diagnosis: positive newborn screen (immunoreactive trypsinogen), a **sweat chloride of 60 mmol/L or more** (30–59 intermediate) and *CFTR* genotyping. Modern treatment uses **CFTR modulators**, for example elexacaftor–tezacaftor–ivacaftor for people with at least one F508del allele, with airway clearance, pancreatic enzymes and nutrition.

@example
title: Risk for two parents with achondroplasia
problem: Two adults with achondroplasia (both heterozygous) plan a pregnancy. What is the probability that a liveborn child has achondroplasia?
step: Each parent is $Aa$ ($A$ for the activating allele). The four genotypes are $aa$, $Aa$, $aA$ and $AA$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{4}$, $\frac{1}{2}$ (for $Aa$ and $aA$ together) and $\frac{1}{4}$.
step: $AA$ is homozygous achondroplasia, which is lethal, so those fetuses are not liveborn.
step: Among liveborn children, remove the $\frac{1}{4}$: the remaining probability mass is $\frac{3}{4}$.
step: Affected heterozygotes are $\frac{1/2}{3/4}=\frac{2}{3}$ of liveborn children, and $\frac{1}{3}$ have average stature.

@example
title: Applying the NF1 criteria
problem: An 8-year-old girl has seven café-au-lait macules, each larger than 5 mm, and freckling in both axillae. Neither parent has similar findings. Does she meet the diagnostic criteria for NF1?
step: Count criteria: six or more café-au-lait macules over 5 mm before puberty. She has seven macules, all above 5 mm, so this criterion is met.
step: Axillary freckling is a second criterion.
step: Two criteria are met, so the diagnosis of NF1 is made, even with no family history (about half of cases are de novo).
step: Follow-up: ophthalmology for optic pathway glioma and Lisch nodules, annual blood pressure, and developmental and scoliosis monitoring.

@mistakes
- Confusing Noonan syndrome (normal karyotype, pulmonary stenosis) with Turner syndrome (45,X, coarctation).
- Calling lens dislocation in Marfan syndrome "downward" (it is typically upward; downward suggests homocystinuria).
- Forgetting that the lethal homozygote changes the ratios among liveborn children in achondroplasia.
- Diagnosing NF1 on a single feature, or requiring a family history (half of cases are de novo).
