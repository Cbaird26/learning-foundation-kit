@article
Mendel's rules assume that an allele works the same whichever parent contributed it. Two important exceptions are genomic imprinting and mitochondrial DNA.

### Genomic imprinting

In **imprinting**, certain genes are expressed from only one parental allele, because the other copy is silenced by DNA methylation and histone marks established in the germline. The silenced copy is the imprinted one. Imprinted genes cluster in regions with an **imprinting control region**. The consequence is that deletion or mutation of the *active* allele has an effect that the same change on the silenced allele does not, so the disease depends on the parent of origin.

**Uniparental disomy (UPD)** is inheritance of both copies of a chromosome from one parent. **Heterodisomy** means both different homologs of that parent; **isodisomy** means two copies of the same homolog (which can unmask a recessive variant). A common route is **trisomy rescue**: a trisomic zygote loses one chromosome, and if the lost one is the other parent's the child is left with UPD; this is more frequent with advanced maternal age.

### Chromosome 15q11-q13

- **Prader–Willi syndrome (PWS)** is loss of the paternally expressed genes (including *SNRPN* and a cluster of small nucleolar RNAs). About 70% have a **paternal deletion**, about 25–30% **maternal UPD 15**, and a few percent an imprinting center defect. Features: severe neonatal hypotonia and poor feeding, then **hyperphagia** and obesity from early childhood, hypogonadism, short stature, almond-shaped eyes, mild intellectual disability, and behavioral problems. Growth hormone treatment helps body composition.
- **Angelman syndrome (AS)** is loss of the maternally expressed *UBE3A* gene in neurons. About 70% have a **maternal deletion**, a few percent **paternal UPD 15**, a few percent an imprinting center defect, and about 10% a **pathogenic variant in maternal UBE3A**. Features: severe intellectual disability, absent speech, ataxic gait ("puppet-like"), seizures, microcephaly, and a happy demeanor with frequent laughter.

The first diagnostic test for both is **DNA methylation analysis of the 15q11-q13 region**, which detects deletion, UPD and imprinting defects (all give an abnormal pattern). Microarray or FISH then identifies a deletion, and UPD testing uses parental samples. A *UBE3A* variant has normal methylation, so sequencing is needed if AS is strongly suspected.

### Other imprinting disorders

**Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome** (chromosome 11p15.5, with overgrowth from excess *IGF2* expression, for example from loss of methylation at one imprinting center or paternal UPD of 11p15): macroglossia, omphalocele, hemihyperplasia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and increased risk of Wilms tumor and hepatoblastoma, so surveillance includes serial abdominal ultrasonography and serum alpha-fetoprotein in early childhood.

### Mitochondrial inheritance

Mitochondria carry their own circular genome (about 16.6 kb; 37 genes, including 13 oxidative phosphorylation subunits, 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs). The oocyte contributes essentially all of the mitochondria of the zygote, so mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) variants show **maternal inheritance**: an affected mother can pass the variant to all her children (sons and daughters), whereas an affected father passes it to none.

Cells hold many copies of mtDNA, so the proportion of mutant copies matters. **Heteroplasmy** means a mixture of mutant and normal mtDNA in a cell; **homoplasmy** means one type only. Because of **replicative segregation**, the mutant fraction drifts as cells divide, and a germline **bottleneck** makes the fraction in one child very different from the fraction in the mother. A disease appears when the mutant fraction in a tissue exceeds a **threshold** (the threshold effect), and tissues with high energy demand (brain, muscle, heart, retina) fail first.

Clinical examples:

- **MELAS**: mitochondrial encephalopathy, lactic acidosis and stroke-like episodes (commonly m.3243A>G in *MT-TL1*).
- **MERRF**: myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibers (m.8344A>G in *MT-TK*). Ragged red fibers on Gomori trichrome stain are subsarcolemmal mitochondrial accumulations.
- **Leber hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON)**: subacute painless central vision loss in young adults, mostly men (for example m.11778G>A); often homoplasmic with incomplete penetrance.
- **Kearns–Sayre syndrome**: onset before 20 with progressive external ophthalmoplegia, pigmentary retinopathy and cardiac conduction block; usually a sporadic single large mtDNA deletion.

Many mitochondrial diseases are caused by nuclear genes (Leigh syndrome can be either), and these follow ordinary Mendelian rules. Elevated lactate, often with a raised lactate-to-pyruvate ratio, is a common screening finding.

@example
title: Choosing the test for a hypotonic infant
problem: A 3-month-old boy has marked hypotonia, a weak suck and requires tube feeding. He has almond-shaped eyes and hypogonadism (small genitalia). What is the diagnosis, what test comes first, and which molecular classes does it detect?
step: Neonatal hypotonia with poor feeding followed by hypogonadism and later hyperphagia is typical of Prader–Willi syndrome, a loss of paternally expressed genes on 15q11-q13.
step: The first-line test is methylation analysis of 15q11-q13. The normal pattern shows a methylated maternal and an unmethylated paternal allele at the *SNRPN* locus; PWS lacks the paternal pattern.
step: An abnormal methylation result is found in all three molecular classes: paternal deletion, maternal UPD, and imprinting center defect.
step: Follow-up microarray or FISH shows a deletion; if there is none, parental samples are used to test for UPD. The class matters because recurrence risk is very low for deletion and UPD but can be up to 50% for some imprinting center defects.

@example
title: Risk for children in a mitochondrial pedigree
problem: A woman with a heteroplasmic MELAS variant has two children. Her husband is unaffected. What is the chance each child inherits the variant, and would children of an affected man be at risk?
step: mtDNA comes from the oocyte, so every child of an affected mother can inherit mutant mtDNA, but the percentage differs from child to child because of the bottleneck.
step: Whether a child is affected depends on the mutant fraction crossing the threshold in vulnerable tissues; the mother's blood level only roughly predicts it.
step: If the affected parent were the father, none of his children would inherit his mtDNA, so the risk would be negligible, and a pedigree with father-to-child transmission excludes a primary mtDNA variant.

@mistakes
- Saying that a mother with a mitochondrial disease has a 50% chance of transmitting it; the correct statement is that she may pass it to all children, at variable mutant loads.
- Testing for PWS or Angelman syndrome with a karyotype alone, or only with FISH, which misses UPD and imprinting defects.
- Mixing up the parent of origin: PWS loses the paternal copy, AS loses the maternal copy.
- Assuming homoplasmy in all mtDNA diseases; most pathogenic mtDNA variants are heteroplasmic.
