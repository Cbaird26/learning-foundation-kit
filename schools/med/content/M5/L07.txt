@article
A genetic test is only as good as the question it is chosen to answer. The best test depends on the **size and type of the suspected change** (a whole chromosome, a deletion of kilobases, a single base), the **clinical hypothesis** (one specific disorder versus an unexplained phenotype) and the **sample** available.

### The main tests

| Test | What it detects | Approximate resolution | Misses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Karyotype** (G-banding) | Aneuploidy, large deletions and duplications, balanced translocations and inversions, low-level mosaicism | About 5–10 Mb | Small deletions, point variants; needs dividing cells |
| **FISH** (fluorescent probes) | A specific known region, such as 22q11.2, or rapid aneuploidy of chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X, Y | Probe-size (tens to hundreds of kb) | Anything outside the probe |
| **Chromosomal microarray (CMA)** | Copy-number variants (deletions and duplications); SNP arrays also show triploidy and regions of homozygosity or uniparental disomy | Tens of kb to about 100 kb | Balanced rearrangements, point variants, repeat expansions, low-level mosaicism |
| **Sanger sequencing / single gene** | Point variants and small insertions or deletions in one gene | Single base | Large deletions, repeat expansions |
| **MLPA / targeted deletion-duplication** | Exon-level deletions and duplications | One exon | Point variants |
| **Gene panel** | Sequencing of dozens to hundreds of genes for one phenotype | Single base | Genes not on the panel |
| **Exome sequencing** | Protein-coding exons (about 1–2% of the genome) | Single base | Most noncoding variants, repeat expansions, many structural variants |
| **Genome sequencing** | Nearly the entire genome including noncoding and many structural variants | Single base | Some repeat expansions and complex regions |
| **Repeat-expansion analysis** (PCR, Southern or long-read) | Repeat length in FMR1, HTT, DMPK | Repeat count | Variants elsewhere |
| **Methylation analysis** | Imprinting disorders (15q11-q13), fragile X silencing | Locus-specific | Sequence variants |

Rules of thumb: **chromosomal microarray is the first-tier test for unexplained developmental delay, intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder and multiple congenital anomalies** (diagnostic yield about 15–20%), usually together with fragile X testing in boys; **exome or genome sequencing** is used for these patients if the microarray is negative or when a monogenic disease is likely (diagnostic yield about 25–40%, higher when the patient and both parents are sequenced together as a **trio**); a **panel or single-gene test** fits a phenotype with a recognizable and limited gene list; **karyotype** is still the test for suspected aneuploidy in a child (such as Turner syndrome) and for parents with recurrent pregnancy loss, because it detects balanced translocations that microarray cannot.

### Variant classification

The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology classify germline variants into five categories: **pathogenic**, **likely pathogenic** (at least 90% certainty of being disease-causing), **variant of uncertain significance (VUS)**, **likely benign**, and **benign**. Evidence includes population frequency (a disease variant is rarer than the disease would allow), the type of change (nonsense, frameshift and canonical splice-site variants usually cause loss of function; missense variants need more evidence), segregation with disease in families, de novo occurrence in a proband with a matching phenotype, functional assays, computational predictions and prior reports in databases such as ClinVar. A **VUS** must not be used to make clinical decisions or to test relatives for predictive purposes; management follows the personal and family history, and the variant may be reclassified (usually to benign) as data accumulate. Variants are named by the HGVS convention, for example *c.68_69del* for the coding DNA change and *p.Glu23ValfsTer17* for the protein change.

### Types of testing and cascade testing

Tests are used for **diagnosis** (symptomatic person), **predictive or presymptomatic testing** (healthy person at risk for a later-onset disease), **carrier testing**, **prenatal and newborn screening** and **pharmacogenomic** use. Always test an **affected** relative first when possible. If a pathogenic variant is found, relatives are offered targeted testing for that one variant (**cascade testing**). A negative result in a relative who is tested for a known familial variant is a **true negative**; a negative result in the first person tested does not rule out a genetic cause (it may be an **uninformative negative**) because the variant may lie in an untested gene or region.

### Test performance

For a test applied to people with and without the target condition:

$$\text{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN},\quad \text{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP},\quad \text{PPV}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP}.$$

The predictive values depend strongly on **prevalence**: a highly accurate test applied to a rare condition gives many false positives, which is why screening results (week 8) need diagnostic confirmation.

@example
title: Choosing a first test for unexplained delay
problem: A 5-year-old boy has global developmental delay, no dysmorphic features and no family history. Normal metabolic screening. Choose a testing strategy.
step: The phenotype is nonspecific, so no single-gene test is obvious; a large copy-number change is the commonest detectable cause at this resolution.
step: First tier: chromosomal microarray, plus *FMR1* CGG repeat analysis because he is a boy with intellectual disability.
step: If both are negative, move to exome sequencing (ideally a trio), or genome sequencing, because most remaining causes are monogenic.
step: Karyotype is no longer first line here because it has lower yield and resolution than microarray.

@example
title: Positive predictive value in a rare condition
problem: A test has sensitivity 95% and specificity 98% for a condition with prevalence 1 in 500. What fraction of positive results is a true positive?
step: Per person tested: true positives $=0.95\times0.002=0.0019$.
step: False positives $=(1-0.98)\times0.998=0.01996$.
step: PPV $=\frac{0.0019}{0.0019+0.01996}=0.0869$.
step: Only about 9% of positive results are true positives, despite good sensitivity and specificity, because the condition is rare.

@mistakes
- Ordering a karyotype to look for a small deletion, or microarray to look for a balanced translocation.
- Acting on a VUS as if it were pathogenic, for example by recommending surgery or testing relatives.
- Thinking a negative panel excludes a genetic cause.
- Quoting sensitivity and specificity but ignoring prevalence when asked how likely a positive result is correct.

check: 10000 * (1 - 0.02) * (1 - 0.95)
hint: How many people do not have the condition?
hint: $9800$ unaffected people, each with a $5\%$ chance of a false positive.
solution: Unaffected people $=10000\times0.98=9800$. False positives $=9800\times0.05=490$.
