@article
Some genes contain runs of a repeated three-nucleotide unit. During DNA replication and repair, the polymerase can slip on the template and add units, and longer repeats slip more often, so repeats tend to expand once they pass a threshold. The result is a family of **trinucleotide repeat expansion disorders**, nearly all neurological or neuromuscular.

### Anticipation

**Anticipation** means that a disease appears earlier or more severely in successive generations because the repeat grows when transmitted. Historically it was an observation that skeptics called ascertainment bias, until the molecular basis was found. The direction of expansion depends on the sex of the transmitting parent: for Huntington disease expansion is more likely in **paternal** transmission; for fragile X and congenital myotonic dystrophy, large expansion occurs through **maternal** transmission.

### Huntington disease (HD)

Cause: a **CAG** repeat in exon 1 of *HTT* (4p16.3), translated into a **polyglutamine** tract; toxic gain of function with aggregation of mutant huntingtin and atrophy of the caudate and putamen. Inheritance is autosomal dominant. Alleles are classified by CAG count: **26 or fewer** normal, **27–35** intermediate (not disease-causing but may expand in a sperm to the affected range), **36–39** reduced penetrance, **40 or more** full penetrance. Longer repeats predict earlier onset. Typical onset is in the fourth to fifth decade with chorea, behavioral change (depression, irritability) and dementia, and death about 15–20 years later. Expansions are larger in paternal transmission, so juvenile HD (onset before 20, with rigidity rather than chorea; the Westphal variant) is usually inherited from a father. Treatment is symptomatic (for chorea, a VMAT2 inhibitor such as tetrabenazine).

### Fragile X syndrome (FXS)

Cause: a **CGG** repeat in the 5′ untranslated region of *FMR1* (Xq27.3). Alleles: **normal** about 5–44, **intermediate (gray zone)** 45–54, **premutation** 55–200, **full mutation** more than 200. A full mutation triggers **hypermethylation** of the CGG repeat and promoter, silencing *FMR1* and abolishing the protein **FMRP**, an RNA-binding protein regulating translation at synapses. Males have moderate intellectual disability, autism spectrum features, a long face with large ears, and **macroorchidism** after puberty; hyperextensible joints and mitral valve prolapse can occur. Females with a full mutation are variably affected because of X-inactivation. Diagnosis: PCR and methylation analysis (with Southern blot or other methods for large repeats); chromosome analysis alone is insufficient. FXS is the most common inherited cause of intellectual disability.

Expansion from premutation to full mutation occurs **only in maternal meiosis**, and the chance rises with the size of the maternal premutation (small premutations of 55–59 repeats seldom expand; those above about 100 repeats nearly always do). A male premutation carrier gives it to his daughters without expansion to full mutation. Premutation carriers do not have FXS but may have **FXTAS** (tremor, ataxia, older adults, mostly male) or **FXPOI** (primary ovarian insufficiency in women).

### Other repeat disorders

- **Myotonic dystrophy type 1**: CTG in the 3′ UTR of *DMPK*; autosomal dominant myotonia, muscle weakness, frontal balding, cataracts, cardiac conduction defects, insulin resistance. The congenital form comes from a maternal transmission. The toxic effect is RNA gain of function, sequestering splicing factors.
- **Friedreich ataxia**: **GAA** in intron 1 of *FXN*; autosomal recessive; frataxin deficiency impairs iron–sulfur cluster formation and mitochondrial function. Gait ataxia with loss of position and vibration sense, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, diabetes, kyphoscoliosis, and pes cavus.
- **Spinocerebellar ataxias and Kennedy disease**: CAG repeats encoding polyglutamine.

CAG disorders are usually protein gain-of-function, while some noncoding repeat disorders (CGG in FMR1, GAA in FXN) cause loss of the gene product through silencing; myotonic dystrophy is an RNA gain-of-function exception.

@example
title: Classifying an HTT result
problem: A 42-year-old man with mild chorea has a father who developed similar symptoms at 60. Genetic testing shows 43 CAG repeats in one *HTT* allele. Classify the result, and predict what a child might inherit if he passes on the allele.
step: Compare 43 with the thresholds: 40 or more is a full-penetrance allele, so the result is diagnostic of Huntington disease in a symptomatic patient.
step: Autosomal dominant: each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the expanded allele.
step: Paternal transmission carries the risk of further expansion, so a child who inherits the allele may carry more than 43 repeats and have earlier onset (anticipation).
step: The exact size cannot be predicted, and predictive testing of children at risk should follow adult-onset guidelines (see week 12).

@mistakes
- Reversing the parent-of-origin rule: HD expands mainly through the father, fragile X full mutation only through the mother.
- Calling a fragile X premutation carrier "affected by fragile X syndrome" when the syndrome requires the full mutation with silencing.
- Ordering a karyotype to diagnose fragile X syndrome.
- Describing all repeat disorders as gain of function; fragile X is loss of function.
