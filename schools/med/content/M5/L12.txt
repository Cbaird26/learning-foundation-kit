@article
**Genetic counseling** is the process of helping people understand and adapt to the medical, psychological and familial implications of the genetic contributions to disease. It includes interpreting family and medical history to assess the chance of disease occurrence or recurrence, educating about inheritance, testing, management and prevention, and **counseling** to promote informed choices and adaptation to the risk or condition.

### Ethical principles

- **Autonomy**: the person decides, after understanding the information (informed consent). This includes a **right not to know**.
- **Nondirectiveness**: the counselor gives balanced information and supports the person's own values rather than steering a decision (although the counselor does give clear medical recommendations when there are effective interventions).
- **Beneficence and nonmaleficence**: maximize benefit, avoid harm (psychological harm, stigma, false reassurance).
- **Justice**: fair access, attention to ancestry-related gaps (reference databases underrepresent many populations, so non-European patients have more variants of uncertain significance).
- **Confidentiality and privacy**: genetic information is also information about relatives.

### Pretest and posttest counseling

**Pretest**: purpose of the test; possible results (positive, negative, uncertain) and their meaning; limits and false-negative rate; what the result would change in management; implications for relatives; reproductive options; costs; possible **secondary findings** (exome and genome sequencing can reveal pathogenic variants in about 80 genes on the ACMG secondary findings list, such as *BRCA1/2*, *MLH1* and *FBN1*, which have actionable management); privacy and insurance issues (GINA); and consent. **Posttest**: deliver the result in plain language, explore feelings, address recurrence risk and family notification, and arrange follow-up and support.

### Communicating risk

- Use **absolute** risk and natural frequencies ("about 6 in 100") rather than only relative risk. $\text{Absolute risk}=\text{baseline risk}\times\text{relative risk}$, so a relative risk of 3 on a 2% baseline is a 6% absolute risk, an increase of 4 percentage points.
- State risk **per pregnancy**: a 1-in-4 recurrence risk applies fresh to each pregnancy; the "coin has no memory."
- Give the reference group, the time frame (by age 70, lifetime) and the uncertainty. Use simple visual aids.

### Disclosing a genetic result

A useful framework adapts the SPIKES protocol for delivering news: **S**etting (private, time, tissues, support person); **P**erception (what does the patient understand and expect?); **I**nvitation (how much does the patient want to know?); **K**nowledge (a "warning shot", then short plain statements, one idea at a time, avoiding jargon); **E**motions (name and respond to feelings with empathy); **S**trategy and **S**ummary (a clear plan, written summary, follow-up and resources). Allow silence, check understanding by asking the person to say it back, and never leave a patient with a positive result and no plan.

### Testing minors and adult-onset conditions

Test children when the result changes management in childhood (for example familial adenomatous polyposis, MEN2, Li–Fraumeni syndrome, or a treatable metabolic disease). **Defer predictive testing for adult-onset conditions** (Huntington disease, *BRCA1/2*) and for carrier status until the person can decide for themselves, because the child's future autonomy and the right not to know should be preserved. A diagnostic test for a symptomatic child is appropriate. Predictive testing for Huntington disease in adults follows a protocol with pretest counseling, a support person, psychological and neurological assessment, and a follow-up visit, because of the risk of depression and suicide.

### Family communication and confidentiality

The result belongs to the patient. Counselors encourage patients to inform at-risk relatives and provide a **family letter**. Disclosure to relatives **without the patient's consent** is an exceptional measure, justified only if harm to relatives is serious, likely and preventable, and the patient has been counseled and refuses; local law and institutional policy vary. Counseling can uncover **misattributed paternity** and consanguinity, so decide before testing how such incidental information will be handled.

### GINA and other protections

The U.S. **Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (GINA, 2008)** prohibits **health insurers** from using genetic information in coverage or premium decisions, and prohibits **employers** with 15 or more employees from using it in employment decisions. GINA **does not cover life insurance, disability insurance or long-term care insurance**, nor the military and some federal health programs. The Affordable Care Act separately forbids denial of coverage for pre-existing conditions. Direct-to-consumer tests have limited validity for medical use; results should be confirmed in a clinical laboratory.

### Reproductive options

Options include prenatal diagnosis (CVS, amniocentesis), **preimplantation genetic testing** with in vitro fertilization, donor gametes, adoption, or accepting the risk without testing. The counselor presents all options neutrally and supports the couple's choice.

@example
title: Converting relative to absolute risk
problem: A news report says a variant "triples the risk" of a disease. The lifetime baseline risk is 2%. What is the absolute risk for a carrier, and how would you explain it?
step: Absolute risk $=\text{baseline}\times\text{relative risk}=2\%\times3=6\%$.
step: The absolute increase is $6\%-2\%=4$ percentage points.
step: Use natural frequencies: "Out of 100 people without the variant, about 2 will develop the disease; out of 100 with the variant, about 6."
step: Add that 94 of 100 carriers will not develop the disease, to avoid fatalism.

@example
title: Disclosing a positive result
problem: A 34-year-old woman returns for her result: a pathogenic *BRCA1* variant, found after her mother's breast cancer. Outline the disclosure.
step: Setting and perception: private room, support person present; ask what she has understood and what she expects.
step: Warning shot and knowledge: "I'm sorry, the result is not the one we hoped for. The test found a change in the BRCA1 gene that raises the risk of breast and ovarian cancer." Pause.
step: Emotions: acknowledge her reaction ("This is a lot to take in"), allow time; then explain that it is a risk, not a diagnosis.
step: Strategy: surveillance, risk-reducing options and their timing, and informing her sister and relatives, who each have a 50% chance of carrying it, with a family letter and a follow-up visit.

@mistakes
- Quoting only relative risk ("three times higher") without giving the baseline.
- Giving a recommendation as a directive instead of presenting options, or withholding clear advice when an effective intervention exists.
- Testing children for adult-onset conditions at a parent's request without a childhood benefit.
- Telling patients that GINA protects against any insurer; it does not cover life, disability or long-term care insurance.
