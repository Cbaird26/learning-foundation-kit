@article
Cancer is a genetic disease of somatic cells, but about **5–10%** of cancers arise in people with an inherited pathogenic variant that raises risk. Finding these people allows surveillance, risk-reducing surgery, targeted therapy and testing of relatives.

### Oncogenes and tumor suppressors

**Proto-oncogenes** promote cell growth; a **gain-of-function** change (point variant, amplification, translocation) makes an **oncogene** and acts dominantly at the cell level, so one altered allele is enough (examples: *RET*, *MYC*, *KRAS*). **Tumor suppressor genes** restrain growth, repair DNA or trigger apoptosis; they act recessively at the cell level, requiring loss of **both** alleles. Most hereditary cancer syndromes involve tumor suppressors and show autosomal dominant inheritance with reduced penetrance.

**Knudson's two-hit hypothesis** (from retinoblastoma): a tumor suppressor needs two inactivating hits. In **sporadic** disease both hits occur somatically in one cell, which is unlikely, so tumors are late, single, unilateral. In **hereditary** disease the first hit is in the germline (every cell has it), so only one somatic hit is needed, giving earlier onset, **multiple or bilateral tumors** and a family history. The second hit is often loss of heterozygosity.

### Clues to hereditary cancer

Early age at diagnosis, multiple primary cancers in one person, bilateral or multifocal disease, rare tumors (male breast cancer, adrenocortical carcinoma, medullary thyroid carcinoma), several relatives on one side with related cancers, a known familial variant, and ancestry with founder variants (about 1 in 40 people of Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry carry one of three *BRCA1/2* founder variants).

### Hereditary breast and ovarian cancer: BRCA1 and BRCA2

*BRCA1* (17q21) and *BRCA2* (13q13) are tumor suppressors required for **homologous recombination repair** of double-strand DNA breaks. Autosomal dominant. Approximate lifetime risks to about age 80: breast cancer about 55–72% for BRCA1 (tumors are often triple-negative) and 45–69% for BRCA2, and ovarian, fallopian tube or peritoneal cancer about 39–44% for BRCA1 and about 11–17% for BRCA2 (estimates vary by study). *BRCA2* also raises risk of male breast cancer, prostate cancer, pancreatic cancer and melanoma. Management (check current guidelines): breast MRI and mammography surveillance from the mid-20s to 30, risk-reducing mastectomy as an option, and **risk-reducing salpingo-oophorectomy** after childbearing (around 35–40 for BRCA1, 40–45 for BRCA2), which also lowers breast cancer risk when done premenopausally. Test an affected relative first; relatives then receive single-variant testing. A person who tests negative for a known familial variant has population-level risk.

Tumors with BRCA deficiency cannot repair double-strand breaks accurately and depend on the backup pathway of base-excision repair, which requires **PARP**. A **PARP inhibitor** (for example olaparib) therefore kills these cells selectively, an example of **synthetic lethality**. PARP inhibitors are used in BRCA-associated ovarian, breast, prostate and pancreatic cancers.

### Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer)

Autosomal dominant germline variants in the **DNA mismatch repair** genes *MLH1*, *MSH2*, *MSH6*, *PMS2* (and deletions in *EPCAM* that silence *MSH2*). Mismatch repair deficiency produces **microsatellite instability (MSI)**. Cancers: colorectal (often right-sided, early onset), endometrial (as common a first cancer as colon in women), and ovarian, gastric, urinary tract, small bowel, brain and sebaceous tumors (Muir–Torre variant). Surveillance includes **colonoscopy every 1–2 years** starting at age 20–25 (or earlier by family history) and gynecologic risk management; low-dose aspirin may lower risk.

**Tumor-based testing** is now applied to all colorectal and endometrial cancers: immunohistochemistry for mismatch repair proteins or MSI testing. If **MLH1 (with PMS2) is lost**, test a colorectal tumor for **BRAF V600E** or *MLH1* promoter hypermethylation (an endometrial tumor for methylation only, since BRAF V600E is rare there): either one points to a sporadic cancer with somatic MLH1 silencing. If neither is found, or another protein pair is lost (MSH2 with MSH6, isolated MSH6 or PMS2), germline testing follows. MSI-high tumors respond to checkpoint inhibitor immunotherapy.

### Other syndromes

- **Familial adenomatous polyposis (FAP)**: *APC* (5q22) variants; hundreds to thousands of colonic adenomas from adolescence, colorectal cancer almost certain without colectomy (average age about 40), duodenal and periampullary adenomas, desmoid tumors, osteomas, congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigment epithelium, hepatoblastoma. *MUTYH*-associated polyposis is autosomal recessive.
- **Li–Fraumeni syndrome**: *TP53* (17p13); sarcomas, premenopausal breast cancer, brain tumors, adrenocortical carcinoma, leukemia; avoid unnecessary radiation.
- **Hereditary retinoblastoma**: *RB1* (13q14); leukocoria; bilateral tumors; later osteosarcoma.
- **MEN2** (*RET*): medullary thyroid carcinoma, pheochromocytoma, parathyroid disease; prophylactic thyroidectomy; MEN1 (menin): parathyroid, pituitary, pancreatic tumors.
- **VHL** (3p25): hemangioblastomas, clear cell renal carcinoma, pheochromocytoma. **Peutz–Jeghers** (*STK11*): hamartomatous polyps and mucocutaneous pigmentation. **Hereditary diffuse gastric cancer** (*CDH1*).

@example
title: Tumor testing for Lynch syndrome
problem: A 44-year-old man has a right-sided colon adenocarcinoma. Immunohistochemistry shows loss of MLH1 and PMS2; MSH2 and MSH6 are retained. What is the next step?
step: Loss of MLH1 and PMS2 means MLH1 is lost (PMS2 is unstable without its partner), so the tumor is mismatch repair deficient.
step: MLH1 can be silenced by somatic promoter hypermethylation in sporadic cancers (often older patients with BRAF V600E), so test the tumor for BRAF V600E or MLH1 promoter methylation.
step: If BRAF V600E or methylation is present, Lynch syndrome is unlikely and no germline test is needed.
step: If neither is found, offer germline testing of the mismatch repair genes, then cascade testing of relatives if a variant is identified.

@mistakes
- Assuming a BRCA carrier's cancer risk is 100%; penetrance is incomplete and varies.
- Testing a healthy relative before the affected relative, when an uninformative negative could result.
- Treating loss of MLH1/PMS2 on tumor staining as proof of Lynch syndrome without BRAF or methylation testing.
- Forgetting that risk-reducing surgery in BRCA carriers should be timed after childbearing.
