@article
**Population genetics** asks how common alleles and genotypes are and why. In medicine its main job is to give the **prior probability** that an unrelated person carries a variant, which feeds into the risk calculations of week 2.

### The Hardy–Weinberg principle

For a locus with two alleles, let $p$ be the frequency of the normal allele and $q$ the frequency of the disease allele, so

$$p+q=1.$$

In a population that meets the assumptions below, genotype frequencies after one generation of mating are

$$p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1,$$

where $p^2$ is homozygous normal, $2pq$ is heterozygous (carriers) and $q^2$ is homozygous affected. The assumptions: **random mating** (no assortative mating or consanguinity), **no selection**, **no new mutation**, **no migration** (gene flow), and a **large population** (no genetic drift). Allele frequencies need not be equal ($p=q$ is not required). Real populations violate every one of these a little, but the equation is a very good approximation for medical use.

### Autosomal recessive disease: from incidence to carrier frequency

The incidence of an AR disease is $q^2$. Take the square root to find $q$, then $p=1-q$, then the carrier frequency $2pq$. For a rare disease $p\approx1$, so

$$\text{carrier frequency}\approx 2q.$$

For cystic fibrosis in people of European ancestry the incidence is about 1 in 2500, so $q=\frac{1}{50}$ and the carrier frequency is $2\times\frac{1}{50}\times\frac{49}{50}\approx\frac{1}{25}$. Carriers are much more common than patients: **a disease that affects 1 in 2500 is carried by about 1 in 25 people.**

### X-linked recessive disease

Males are hemizygous, so the male incidence equals $q$. Affected females are $q^2$ and carrier females are $2pq\approx2q$. If hemophilia A affects 1 in 5000 males, then $q=\frac{1}{5000}$, about 1 in 2500 females is a carrier, and about 1 in 25,000,000 females is affected. X-linked disease is therefore far more common in males.

### Screening and residual risk

A negative carrier screening test does not remove the risk, because panels do not detect every variant. With carrier frequency $c$ and detection rate (sensitivity) $s$, the **residual carrier risk** after a negative test is, by Bayes,

$$\frac{c\,(1-s)}{c\,(1-s)+(1-c)}.$$

Residual risk depends on ancestry because carrier frequencies and panel sensitivity both differ by ancestry. For a couple, the chance of an affected child is (probability mother carries) × (probability father carries) × $\frac{1}{4}$.

### Forces that change allele frequencies

- **Founder effect and genetic drift**: a small founding group (or a population bottleneck) carries a few alleles to high frequency (Tay–Sachs and several other recessive disorders in Ashkenazi Jewish populations, and Ellis–van Creveld syndrome in the Old Order Amish).
- **Selection**: harmful dominant alleles are removed quickly; recessive alleles persist in carriers. Under **mutation–selection balance**, the frequency reflects the mutation rate against negative selection.
- **Heterozygote advantage** (balanced polymorphism): sickle cell trait protects against severe falciparum malaria, maintaining the HbS allele in malaria-endemic regions (about 8% of African Americans have sickle cell trait). Cystic fibrosis carriers are hypothesized to have had advantage against infectious diarrhea, but this is less certain.
- **Gene flow** (migration) changes allele frequencies by bringing in new alleles, whereas **non-random mating** (consanguinity, assortative mating) changes genotype frequencies without changing allele frequencies.
- **Linkage disequilibrium** is the non-random association of alleles at nearby loci, the basis of genome-wide association studies.

@example
title: Carrier frequency from disease incidence
problem: A recessive disease has an incidence of 1 in 10,000. What is the carrier frequency?
step: The incidence is $q^2=\frac{1}{10000}$, so $q=\frac{1}{100}$.
step: Then $p=1-q=\frac{99}{100}$.
step: Carrier frequency $2pq=2\times\frac{99}{100}\times\frac{1}{100}=\frac{198}{10000}=\frac{99}{5000}$, about 2%.
step: The approximation $2q=\frac{1}{50}=0.02$ is close, so about 1 in 50 people carry the variant.

@example
title: Combining pedigree and population data
problem: A healthy woman's brother has a recessive disorder of incidence 1 in 2500, and her partner has no family history. Estimate the risk that their child is affected.
step: She is a carrier with probability $\frac{2}{3}$.
step: Population carrier frequency: $q=\frac{1}{50}$, so $2pq=2\times\frac{1}{50}\times\frac{49}{50}=\frac{98}{2500}=0.0392$.
step: Child's risk: $\frac{2}{3}\times0.0392\times\frac{1}{4}=0.00653$.
step: That is about 1 in 153, roughly 0.65%.

@mistakes
- Reporting $q^2$ (the disease incidence) as the carrier frequency, or taking $q$ as the carrier frequency.
- Using the AR formula for X-linked disease; in males the incidence is $q$, not $q^2$.
- Assuming a negative screen means zero risk instead of calculating the residual risk.
- Believing the equation requires equal allele frequencies, or that Hardy–Weinberg proves a population is "healthy".

@practice num
prompt: A recessive disease has an incidence of 1 in 1600 in a population in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium. What is the carrier frequency $2pq$? Give a fraction.
answer: 39/800
check: 2 * (1/40) * (39/40)
hint: Take the square root of the incidence to get $q$.
hint: $q=\frac{1}{40}$ and $p=\frac{39}{40}$.
solution: $q=\sqrt{1/1600}=\frac{1}{40}$, $p=\frac{39}{40}$, so $2pq=2\times\frac{39}{40}\times\frac{1}{40}=\frac{78}{1600}=\frac{39}{800}\approx0.049$.

@practice num
prompt: In a population, the carrier frequency for Tay–Sachs disease is $\frac{1}{30}$ and a carrier panel has a detection rate of 98%. What is a person's residual carrier probability after a negative panel result? Give a decimal.
answer: 0.00069
check: (1/30 * 0.02) / (1/30 * 0.02 + 29/30 * 1)
hint: Use Bayes: carriers test negative with probability $0.02$, non-carriers with probability $1$.
hint: Prior carrier $\frac{1}{30}$, prior non-carrier $\frac{29}{30}$.
solution: Joint carrier $\frac{1}{30}\times0.02$; joint non-carrier $\frac{29}{30}\times1$. Ratio $=\frac{0.02}{0.02+29}\approx0.00069$, about 1 in 1450.
tol: 2%
