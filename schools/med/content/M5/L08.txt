@article
A **screening test** is offered to everyone in a group to identify those at increased risk; it does not give a diagnosis. A **diagnostic test** answers the question for the individual. Screening results are expressed as a risk, and a "screen-positive" result must be followed by a diagnostic test before any irreversible decision.

### First-trimester screening

At 11–14 weeks, **nuchal translucency** (ultrasound measurement of fluid behind the fetal neck) is combined with maternal serum **PAPP-A** and **free β-hCG** and maternal age. A fetus with Down syndrome typically has **increased nuchal translucency, low PAPP-A and high β-hCG**. Increased nuchal translucency also occurs with other aneuploidies, congenital heart disease and many genetic syndromes, so it merits a detailed anatomy scan and fetal echocardiography even if aneuploidy testing is normal.

### Second-trimester serum screening

The **quadruple screen** (15–22 weeks) measures AFP, hCG, unconjugated estriol and inhibin A.

| Condition | AFP | hCG | Estriol | Inhibin A |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Down syndrome | Low | High | Low | High |
| Trisomy 18 | Low | Low | Low | Normal or low |

Results are reported as **multiples of the median (MoM)**, the measured value divided by the median for gestational age.

**Elevated maternal serum AFP** has several causes: **neural tube defect** (anencephaly, spina bifida), **ventral wall defects** (gastroschisis, omphalocele), underestimated gestational age, multiple gestation, and fetal demise. Evaluate with ultrasonography to confirm dates and look for anomalies. Folic acid (a daily supplement of 400 micrograms for all people who may become pregnant, and a much higher dose for those with a previous child with a neural tube defect, per current obstetric guidance) lowers neural tube defect risk.

### Cell-free DNA screening

Maternal blood contains small DNA fragments, a fraction of which originates from the **placenta** (trophoblast cells). Counting fragments from each chromosome detects excess from chromosome 21, 18 or 13. It can be done from about 10 weeks and has high sensitivity (about 99% for trisomy 21) and very high specificity, but it is a **screening test**. Its **positive predictive value depends on prevalence**: it is highest in older pregnancies, lower in young people, and lower for sex chromosome conditions and microdeletions. False positives result from **confined placental mosaicism**, a vanished co-twin, or maternal chromosome abnormalities and, rarely, maternal cancer. A low fetal fraction gives a failed result and needs follow-up. A positive result should be confirmed by diagnostic testing.

### Diagnostic prenatal testing

- **Chorionic villus sampling (CVS)**, at 10–13 weeks: placental tissue; earlier results but a risk of confined placental mosaicism; sampling before 9 weeks has been linked to limb defects.
- **Amniocentesis**, from 15 weeks: amniotic fluid cells; the procedure-related pregnancy loss risk is roughly 0.1–0.3% in recent studies (older teaching quoted about 0.5%).

Both can be analyzed by karyotype, rapid aneuploidy FISH or PCR, chromosomal microarray, or sequencing. **Karyotype** detects aneuploidy, large structural changes and balanced rearrangements; **chromosomal microarray** adds submicroscopic deletions and duplications (it adds a diagnosis in about 1–2% of fetuses with a normal karyotype and normal ultrasound, and about 6% when ultrasound shows an anomaly); **exome sequencing** may provide an additional diagnosis in a fetus with structural anomalies and a normal microarray. Results with uncertain significance should be anticipated during counseling.

### Newborn screening

A **dried blood spot** is collected by heel stick at 24–48 hours of age and analyzed, largely by tandem mass spectrometry, for dozens of conditions on the national panel, including:

- **Phenylketonuria** (elevated phenylalanine; treated with a phenylalanine-restricted diet; early treatment prevents intellectual disability);
- **Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency** (elevated octanoylcarnitine; prevent fasting and hypoglycemia);
- **Galactosemia**, **congenital hypothyroidism** (TSH or T4), **congenital adrenal hyperplasia** (17-hydroxyprogesterone), **cystic fibrosis** (immunoreactive trypsinogen then DNA), **sickle cell disease and other hemoglobinopathies**, **severe combined immunodeficiency** (T-cell receptor excision circles, TRECs), and **spinal muscular atrophy** (SMN1 deletion).

Pulse oximetry screens for critical congenital heart disease, and hearing screening is also done. Principles for including a condition (Wilson–Jungner): important health problem, a recognized early phase, **an effective treatment that works better early**, a reliable and acceptable test, and a net benefit. A positive newborn screen is **not a diagnosis**; it prompts urgent confirmatory testing (for example, sweat chloride after a positive cystic fibrosis screen).

@example
title: Predictive value of cell-free DNA
problem: Cell-free DNA screening for trisomy 21 has sensitivity 99% and specificity 99.9%. In a group of pregnant patients the prevalence of trisomy 21 is 1 in 500. What is the positive predictive value?
step: Per pregnancy: true positives $=0.99\times0.002=0.00198$.
step: False positives $=(1-0.999)\times0.998=0.000998$.
step: PPV $=\frac{0.00198}{0.00198+0.000998}=0.665$.
step: About two of three positive results are true positives; confirmation by CVS or amniocentesis is required before any decision.

@example
title: Interpreting a quadruple screen
problem: A 29-year-old woman's quadruple screen at 17 weeks reports AFP 0.6 MoM, hCG 2.4 MoM, unconjugated estriol 0.6 MoM and inhibin A 2.3 MoM. What does this pattern suggest, and what is the next step?
step: Low AFP, high hCG, low estriol and high inhibin A is the classic pattern for fetal trisomy 21.
step: This is a screen-positive result (increased risk), not a diagnosis.
step: Offer genetic counseling, a detailed ultrasound, and diagnostic testing with amniocentesis (cell-free DNA is an option as a further screen, but diagnostic testing is the only way to confirm).
step: The fetal sample would be tested by karyotype or chromosomal microarray.

@mistakes
- Treating a positive cell-free DNA screen as a diagnosis.
- Ignoring prevalence: a positive screen in a young person has a lower predictive value than in an older one.
- Forgetting that a normal AFP does not exclude all open defects, and that high AFP has several benign causes (wrong dates, twins).
- Believing newborn screening results are diagnostic; they require confirmation.

@practice self
prompt: A pregnant patient's 12-week ultrasound shows a nuchal translucency of 4.5 mm. At 16 weeks, rapid aneuploidy testing (FISH) on an amniocentesis sample is normal for chromosomes 13, 18, 21, X and Y. Compare karyotype, chromosomal microarray and sequencing as next steps, saying what each can and cannot detect, and recommend a plan.
solution: Karyotype shows all chromosomes at about 5–10 Mb resolution, so it detects aneuploidy beyond the FISH panel, large structural changes and balanced translocations, but misses smaller deletions and duplications and all point variants. Chromosomal microarray detects copy-number changes down to tens of kilobases, such as 22q11.2 deletion, and is recommended for a fetus with increased nuchal translucency or structural anomalies after the rapid test; it misses balanced rearrangements, repeat expansions and point variants, and may report variants of uncertain significance. Exome (or genome) sequencing detects single-nucleotide variants and small insertions or deletions across genes (Noonan syndrome and skeletal dysplasias are examples that cause increased nuchal translucency) but takes longer, has lower resolution for copy number in exome form, and gives uncertain findings. The reasonable plan is chromosomal microarray on the existing amniotic fluid sample, a detailed anatomy scan and fetal echocardiography, and exome sequencing if structural anomalies appear and the microarray is normal, with pre-test counseling about uncertain results.
rubric: Correctly states what karyotype, microarray and sequencing each detect and miss.
rubric: Recommends microarray as the next test after normal aneuploidy testing and increased nuchal translucency.
rubric: Mentions sequencing for a monogenic cause (for example Noonan syndrome) if microarray is normal and anomalies are present.
rubric: Mentions detailed ultrasound and fetal echocardiography, and counseling about uncertain results.
hint: Think about the size of the change each test can see, and whether copy number or sequence is being measured.
