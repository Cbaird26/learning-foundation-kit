@article
World War II began in Europe in September 1939 when Germany invaded Poland. Germany conquered much of Western Europe in 1940. The United States stayed officially neutral, but public opinion was divided between those who wanted to stay out and those who wanted to aid Britain. Congress passed the **Lend-Lease Act** (1941), which allowed the United States to supply Allied nations.

### Entry into the war

On December 7, 1941, Japan attacked the U.S. naval base at **Pearl Harbor**, Hawaii. Congress declared war on Japan the next day, and Germany and Italy declared war on the United States a few days later. The Allies, chiefly the United States, Britain and the Soviet Union, adopted a "Europe first" strategy. Key turning points included the Battle of Midway (June 1942) in the Pacific, the Soviet victory at Stalingrad, and the Allied landings in Normandy on **D-Day**, June 6, 1944. Germany surrendered in May 1945. After intense fighting in the Pacific, the United States dropped atomic bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945, and Japan surrendered. Historians still discuss the decision: supporters argue it shortened the war and saved lives, while critics argue that alternatives were available and the human cost was severe.

### The home front

The war ended the Depression. Factories turned out planes, ships and tanks, and unemployment nearly disappeared. The government rationed items such as gasoline and sugar, sold war bonds, and raised taxes. Millions of women took jobs in war industries and elsewhere; most were laid off or left when the war ended, but the wartime experience changed expectations for many. Black Americans pressed for equality under the slogan "Double V," meaning victory over fascism abroad and discrimination at home. After organizers threatened a mass march, **Executive Order 8802** (1941) banned discrimination in defense industries. About a million Black Americans served in segregated military units. Mexican Americans served in large numbers, and the Bracero Program brought Mexican workers to U.S. farms. Native Americans served at high rates, and the Navajo code talkers developed an unbroken code used in the Pacific.

### Japanese American incarceration

In February 1942 **Executive Order 9066** authorized the military to exclude people from designated zones. Roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, about two-thirds of them U.S. citizens, were forced from the West Coast and held in camps. Officials justified it as a military necessity; later investigations found it resulted from racial prejudice, wartime hysteria and failed political leadership rather than from evidence of disloyalty. In *Korematsu v. United States* (1944), the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion order, but in *Ex parte Endo* (1944) it ruled that loyal citizens could not be held. Japanese American soldiers in the 442nd Regimental Combat Team became one of the most decorated units. In 1988 Congress formally apologized and authorized payments to survivors.

### The Holocaust and refugees

Nazi Germany murdered six million Jews and millions of others. The United States had immigration quotas, and its response to refugees before and during the war has been widely criticized. U.S. forces liberated camps in 1945.

### Looking ahead

The **GI Bill** (1944) provided education and home-loan benefits to veterans, though discrimination limited access for many Black veterans. The war made the United States an economic and military superpower and helped create the postwar order studied in week 8.

@example
title: Evaluating a government justification
problem: Evaluate the claim "Executive Order 9066 was justified by military necessity" using evidence from the war years and later findings.
step: State the claim as officials made it in 1942: a Japanese attack on the West Coast seemed possible after Pearl Harbor, and officials said they could not quickly tell loyal from disloyal people.
step: Gather evidence against it: German and Italian Americans were not removed as a group, Hawaii (with a larger Japanese American population) did not carry out mass removal, and later investigations found no evidence of widespread disloyalty.
step: Consider the legal outcomes: the Supreme Court upheld the order in *Korematsu* but limited detention in *Endo*; Congress later apologized.
step: State a conclusion supported by evidence: historians generally conclude the incarceration was driven by prejudice and fear, not demonstrated military need.

@mistakes
- Saying the United States entered the war in 1939; it entered in December 1941.
- Treating the home front experience as the same for everyone; race, sex and ancestry affected jobs and treatment.
- Calling the camps simply "relocation" without noting that the people were citizens and noncitizens held without trial.
