@article
### Cushing syndrome: confirm, then localize

**Cushing syndrome** is chronic glucocorticoid excess. By far the most common cause is **exogenous glucocorticoid** therapy. Endogenous causes divide by ACTH:

- **ACTH-dependent**: pituitary adenoma (**Cushing disease**, most common endogenous cause) or **ectopic ACTH** (small cell lung cancer, bronchial carcinoid).
- **ACTH-independent**: adrenal adenoma, adrenal carcinoma, or bilateral nodular hyperplasia (ACTH is suppressed).

Features: central obesity with thin limbs, moon face, dorsocervical fat pad, wide purple striae, easy bruising, proximal myopathy, hypertension, glucose intolerance, osteoporosis, mood changes, hirsutism and menstrual disruption. Ectopic ACTH produces rapid onset with marked hypokalemia and hyperpigmentation.

**Step 1: exclude exogenous glucocorticoids, then perform one or two screening tests**: **late-night salivary cortisol** (two samples), **24-hour urinary free cortisol** (at least two collections; a value above the upper reference limit supports the diagnosis, and more than about three times the limit is strongly supportive), or the **1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test** (give 1 mg at 11 PM; an 8 AM cortisol of **at least 1.8 µg/dL (50 nmol/L)** fails to suppress). Oral estrogen raises CBG and total cortisol and can cause a false-positive dexamethasone test; use urine or salivary tests instead. Alcohol use, depression, obesity and severe illness can cause pseudo-Cushing states.

**Step 2: plasma ACTH.** Low or suppressed (below about 5 to 10 pg/mL) means ACTH-independent disease: obtain an adrenal CT. Normal or high ACTH means ACTH-dependent disease: obtain pituitary MRI; if the adenoma is small or MRI is equivocal, use **inferior petrosal sinus sampling** (central-to-peripheral ACTH gradient indicates a pituitary source). The **high-dose (8 mg) dexamethasone test** suppresses cortisol by more than 50% in most pituitary adenomas but not in most ectopic sources. Treatment is surgical removal of the source (**transsphenoidal surgery** for Cushing disease), and after surgery a transient adrenal insufficiency is expected. After bilateral adrenalectomy, loss of feedback can cause growth of a pituitary corticotroph adenoma (**Nelson syndrome**) with hyperpigmentation.

### Adrenal insufficiency: suspect, test, treat

**Primary** insufficiency (Addison disease): destruction of the cortex, most commonly autoimmune (21-hydroxylase antibodies) in developed countries; also tuberculosis, adrenal hemorrhage (meningococcemia: Waterhouse–Friderichsen syndrome), metastases and adrenoleukodystrophy. All three zones fail: low cortisol and low aldosterone. ACTH and its co-product melanocyte-stimulating hormone rise, giving **hyperpigmentation**. Features: fatigue, weight loss, orthostasis, salt craving, hyponatremia, **hyperkalemia**, hypoglycemia and non-anion gap metabolic acidosis.

**Secondary/tertiary** insufficiency: lack of ACTH or CRH (pituitary disease, or suppression from exogenous glucocorticoids, the commonest cause). Aldosterone is preserved, so there is **no hyperkalemia and no hyperpigmentation**.

**Choosing the test.** A morning cortisol below about 3 µg/dL strongly suggests insufficiency; above about 15 to 18 µg/dL makes it unlikely (assay-dependent). Between those values, perform the **cosyntropin (ACTH 1–24) stimulation test**: give 250 µg and measure cortisol at 30 and 60 minutes; a peak below about 18 µg/dL (500 nmol/L) is a failed response (cutoffs of about 14 to 15 µg/dL are used with newer assays). A plasma ACTH distinguishes primary (high) from secondary (low or normal); plasma renin is high in primary disease. Recent pituitary injury may give a falsely normal stimulation test because the adrenal has not yet atrophied.

**Adrenal crisis** (hypotension, vomiting, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypoglycemia) is a medical emergency: **do not delay treatment for testing**; draw cortisol and ACTH if possible, then give **hydrocortisone 100 mg IV** (typical adult dose for study) and isotonic saline with dextrose, then about 200 mg/24 hours. Chronic replacement is hydrocortisone in divided doses (typical total 15 to 25 mg/day), with fludrocortisone for primary insufficiency; patients double or triple the dose during febrile illness ("sick day rules") and receive stress dosing for surgery.

### Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)

An enzyme block reduces cortisol, so ACTH rises and the cortex hypertrophies, with accumulation of the precursors upstream.

| Enzyme | Cortisol | Aldosterone pathway | Sex steroids | Findings |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **21-hydroxylase** (about 95%) | low | low (classic) | high androgens | Female genital virilization; salt-wasting crisis with hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension; **high 17-hydroxyprogesterone** |
| **11β-hydroxylase** | low | low aldosterone, high 11-deoxycorticosterone | high androgens | Virilization **with hypertension** and low renin |
| **17α-hydroxylase** | low | high mineralocorticoids | low | Hypertension, hypokalemia; XY individuals undervirilized, XX no puberty |

Treat with glucocorticoid replacement (and fludrocortisone in salt wasters). Nonclassic 21-hydroxylase deficiency presents later with hirsutism and oligomenorrhea.

### Primary aldosteronism

Autonomous aldosterone production (aldosterone-producing adenoma, **Conn syndrome**, or bilateral hyperplasia) causes hypertension (including resistant hypertension), hypokalemia (not always), mild metabolic alkalosis and **low renin**. Screen with the ARR (high aldosterone, suppressed renin), confirm with a saline infusion or oral sodium loading suppression test, image with CT, and when surgery is considered use **adrenal vein sampling** to lateralize. Treat unilateral disease with adrenalectomy and bilateral disease with a mineralocorticoid antagonist (spironolactone or eplerenone).

@example
title: Choosing tests in suspected Cushing syndrome
problem: A 36-year-old woman has weight gain, purple abdominal striae, hypertension and easy bruising. She takes an estrogen-containing oral contraceptive and no glucocorticoids. Her 24-hour urinary free cortisol is 3.5 times the upper limit on two collections, and plasma ACTH is 3 pg/mL (suppressed). What is the next step?
step: Exogenous glucocorticoids are excluded, and urinary free cortisol is clearly elevated on two collections, which confirms hypercortisolism. A dexamethasone test would have been unreliable because estrogen raises CBG.
step: ACTH is suppressed, so the cortisol excess does not depend on the pituitary: ACTH-independent Cushing syndrome.
step: Next, image the adrenal glands with CT. The likely finding is an adrenal adenoma (or carcinoma, or bilateral nodular hyperplasia).
step: Treat with adrenalectomy for a unilateral lesion, and give perioperative glucocorticoids because the contralateral gland is suppressed.

@mistakes
- Starting with pituitary MRI or high-dose dexamethasone before confirming hypercortisolism and measuring ACTH.
- Using the dexamethasone suppression test in a patient taking oral estrogen.
- Delaying hydrocortisone in suspected adrenal crisis to complete testing.
- Expecting hyperkalemia or hyperpigmentation in secondary adrenal insufficiency.
